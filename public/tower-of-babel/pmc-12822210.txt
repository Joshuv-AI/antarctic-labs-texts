The relationships of renal function markers with disease activities and neurosyphilis in HIV-negative patients with syphilis
Authors: Fanghua He, Ming Wang, Yuxuan Xu, Ming Jin, Jiaqing Liu, Yating Xu, Jingjing Li, Chongke Zhong, Shanshan Li, Jun Dai, Minzhi Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo investigate the associations between renal function markers and disease activities and neurosyphilis in patients with syphilis.MethodsA total of 390 HIV-negative syphilis patients were included in the study. Serum blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine (CR), and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) were used as markers of renal function. Binary logistic regression models and restricted cubic splines were performed to investigate the associations of renal function markers with neurosyphilis.ResultsBUN and CR were positively correlated with serum TRUST titer and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) protein, whereas eGFR was inversely correlated with serum TRUST titer, CSF-protein and erythrocyte sedimentation rate. Renal function markers were associated with neurosyphilis, and the adjusted odds ratios (95% CIs) for the highest versus lowest tertiles of BUN and CR were 1.81 (1.04–3.13) and 2.27 (1.22–4.22). Multivariable-adjusted restricted cubic spline analyses suggested there were dose–response relationships of BUN and CR with neurosyphilis (P for linearity < 0.05). Furthermore, the addition of BUN or CR to the conventional model improved risk reclassification of neurosyphilis.ConclusionsRenal function markers were associated with disease activity of syphilis and the likelihood of neurosyphilis in HIV-negative patients with syphilis. These findings highlight the critical role of renal function monitoring in syphilis management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03679-w.

Introduction

Syphilis ranks third among category A and B notifiable infectious diseases in China, according to the data of the national notifiable infectious diseases report in China. Neurosyphilis is a group of clinical syndromes caused by Treponema pallidum (T.p) invading the nervous system and causing meningeal, cerebral, vascular, or spinal cord damage. Neurosyphilis can develop at any stage of syphilis infection, frequently due to incomplete treatment during early syphilis, and serves as an important manifestation of systemic damage in late syphilis. Neurosyphilis can cause severe clinical outcomes, such as hemiplegia, paraplegia, and mental disorders, which may impose negative impacts on the patients, their families and society.

Although syphilis is treatable, the high burden of severe complications observed in recent years emphasizes the importance of disease activity indices in syphilis research [1]. Several indicators have been reported to be associated with disease activity during syphilis infection. Serum non-treponemal specific tests such as toluidine red unheated serum test (TRUST) titers and rapid plasma reagin (RPR) titers can be used as indicators of syphilis activity and can also be used to assess treatment response. White blood cell count (WBC) and protein concentration in cerebrospinal fluid are diagnostic markers of neurosyphilis and surrogate markers to assess treatment response [2]. Exploring the factors related to disease activities in neurosyphilis is of great significance for the early detection and treatment of neurosyphilis.

Syphilis can cause damage to multiple organs, including the kidneys and nervous system [3]. Previous studies have suggested a potential association between syphilis, along with some other infectious diseases, and impaired kidney function. A case report described two patients with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) diagnosed with secondary syphilis, and presenting acute renal failure and nephrotic-range proteinuria. Renal biopsy revealed membranous nephropathy in both cases, which is a rare complication of secondary syphilis [4]. There is evidence suggesting that patients with syphilis and some other infectious diseases have abnormal levels of kidney function indicators. Previous studies have found that estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) levels are significantly higher in treated individuals with HIV than in untreated individuals [5]. Furthermore, eGFR was inversely associated with the risk of tuberculosis infection among patients with chronic kidney disease who were not undergoing dialysis [6]. Aoufi Rabih S et al. [7] found that renal insufficiency was a risk factor for HCV infection by comparing HCV-negative and HCV-positive patients. However, little is known about the relationships between renal function markers and neurosyphilis. Serum creatinine (CR), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and eGFR were commonly used clinical markers of renal function. Therefore, we aimed to investigate the association between renal function markers, disease activity, and neurosyphilis in HIV-negative patients with syphilis.

Materials and methods

Participants

Syphilis patients who underwent lumbar puncture in the Fifth People’s Hospital of Suzhou from November 2018 to May 2024 were included in the study. The eligible participants were those diagnosed with syphilis according to the diagnostic criteria of syphilis in 2020 by the Sexually Transmitted Diseases Association, China Centers for Disease Control (CDC) [8]. Patients without renal function laboratory data, including BUN and CR, were excluded from the analysis. Patients with other central nervous system disorders and HIV infection were excluded. Finally, a total of 390 HIV-negative syphilis patients were included in this study.

The Ethics Committee of the Fifth People’s Hospital of Suzhou approved this study. All participants provided written informed consent.

Data collection

The basic information of the patients was collected, including gender, age, education level, occupation, cigarette smoking and drinking status, medical histories, etc. The clinical data encompassed symptoms and signs of the patients, including neurological and psychiatric symptoms. Neurological symptoms mainly covered the main symptoms of meningeal neurosyphilis, meningeal vascular syphilis, and tabes in brain parenchymal syphilis. Psychiatric symptoms mainly included the symptoms of general paresis type in brain parenchymal syphilis. The auxiliary examinations included blood routine, urine routine, liver and kidney function, infection index, infectious disease examination, cerebrospinal fluid examination, etc.

In this study, renal function markers included BUN, CR, and eGFR. BUN and CR were measured enzymatically using Roche Cobas analyzers. eGFR was commonly calculated using the Modification of Diet in Renal Disease (MDRD) Study Equation, which was expressed as: eGFR (mL/min/1.73 m2) = 175 × (Scr)−1.154 × (Age)−0.203 × (0.742 if female) [Scr: serum creatinine concentration (mg/dL)].

Outcome assessment

The study outcome was neurosyphilis. The diagnostic criteria for neurosyphilis meet the 2020 diagnostic criteria of neurosyphilis issued by the Sexually Transmitted Diseases Association, CDC. The eligible participants were recruited if the following three conditions were met at the same time: (1) positive serum TPPA and TRUST; (2) positive TPPA and/or TRUST in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF); (3) abnormal cerebrospinal fluid (WBC ≥ 5 × 106/L and/or protein > 500 mg/L). All patients were diagnosed by experienced dermatologists.

Statistical analysis

All participants were divided into two groups according to the diagnostic criteria for neurosyphilis. The differences of baseline characteristics between the neurosyphilis and non-neurosyphilis groups were tested using t tests or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and percentages were assessed using χ2 tests.

Spearman correlation analyses were performed to investigate the correlations between renal function markers, disease activity, and diagnostic indicators of neurosyphilis, including serum TRUST titer and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), and CSF TRUST titer, protein, and WBC. Binary logistic regression analyses were performed to investigate the association between the level of renal function markers and neurosyphilis. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the higher tertiles compared with the lowest tertile and for each SD increment of logarithm-transformed renal function markers were calculated. Based on prior knowledge, we built 2 logistic regression models. Model 1 was an unadjusted model. Model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, neurological symptoms, psychiatric symptoms, smoking, drinking, hypertension, fixed sex partner, and ever received treatment. Tests for linear trends in ORs across renal function markers’ tertiles were performed using the median within each tertile as the predictor. We used restricted cubic splines to provide more precise estimates and to explore the shapes of the associations between renal function markers’ levels and neurosyphilis with 4 knots (at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles), and the 2.5% highest and lowest observations of markers (BUN, CR, and eGFR) were trimmed [9].

We examined the joint effects of renal function markers on the odds of neurosyphilis. We constructed a weighted renal function score by adding concentrations of BUN, CR, and eGFR, weighted by the multivariable-adjusted risk estimates (β coefficients), for the outcome of neurosyphilis: renal function score = (β1 × BUN + β2 × CR + β3 × eGFR) × (3/sum of the β coefficients). Here, β1, β2, and β3 denote the estimates of the β coefficients for BUN, CR, and eGFR, respectively, and were obtained by fitting the multivariable logistic regression model for neurosyphilis [10, 11].

We further calculated integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) and category-free net reclassification improvement (NRI) to assess the improvements in risk reclassification by adding renal function markers to the model with conventional risk factors. The Hosmer–Lemeshow χ2 statistic was used to evaluate the calibration of models with renal function markers [12]. All P values were 2-tailed, and a significance level of 0.05 was used. Data analyses were performed using SAS, version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc, Cary, NC).

Results

Baseline characteristics

Among the included 390 participants, the mean age was 48.8 ± 24.5 years and 53.2% of participants were men. There were 163 patients with neurosyphilis and 227 patients without neurosyphilis. We presented baseline characteristics of neurosyphilis and non-neurosyphilis participants in Table 1. We found that the patients in the neurosyphilis group were more likely to be older and men, to have higher rates of smoking, drinking, neurological and psychiatric symptoms, and history of hypertension, and to have significantly higher levels of CSF-WBC, protein, and TRUST titer; while the proportion of previous treatment and having a fixed sex partner was significantly lower than that in the non-neurosyphilis group (P < 0.05). Other baseline characteristics we collected did not differ significantly between the two groups (all P > 0.05). Association of renal function markers and neurosyphilis

The relationship between renal function markers and neurosyphilis is shown in Table 2. Patients with higher levels of BUN and CR had higher odds of neurosyphilis than those with lower levels of BUN and CR. After adjusting for age, sex, neurological symptoms, psychiatric symptoms, smoking and drinking status, hypertension, fixed sex partner, and ever received treatment, the multivariable-adjusted ORs for the highest tertiles of BUN and CR were 1.81 (95% CI 1.04–3.13; P = 0.04) and 2.27 (95% CI 1.22–4.22; P = 0.01), respectively, for neurosyphilis compared with the lowest tertiles. Each 1-SD increase in BUN and CR was associated with a 43% (95% CI 13%–80%) and 30% (95% CI 1%–68%) higher risk of neurosyphilis, respectively. Multivariable-adjusted restricted cubic spline analyses suggested dose–response associations of BUN and CR with neurosyphilis (P = 0.03 and P = 0.04 for linearity, respectively; Fig. 2). Furthermore, patients with lower eGFR had higher odds of neurosyphilis than those with higher eGFR (Table 2). After multivariable adjustment, there is a borderline relationship between eGFR and neurosyphilis across tertiles. Compared with participants in the lowest tertile of eGFR, the OR for the highest tertile of eGFR was 0.55 (95% CI 0.29–1.04; P = 0.06).

Renal function markers were integrated to construct a renal function score. The higher the renal function score, the more severe the degree of renal function damage. Participants were then divided into 3 groups according to the tertile of calculated renal function scores. The association of renal function score with the odds of neurosyphilis is presented in Supplementary Figure S1. Compared to the lowest tertile, the multivariable-adjusted OR for the highest tertile of renal function score was 2.22 (95% CI 1.20–4.12), which was consistent with the results of previous analyses.

Moreover, adding BUN, CR, and eGFR simultaneously to the conventional model, the reclassification ability of the model for neurosyphilis [NRI: 26.5% (95% CI 6.3%–46.7%; P = 0.01); IDI: 1.4% (95% CI 0.3%–2.6%; P = 0.01)] was improved. Similar incremental prognostic value was observed when renal function score was included in the conventional model, shown by an increase in NRI of 33.9% (95% CI 14.1%–53.7%; P = 0.001) and IDI of 1.0% (95% CI 0.1%–2.0%; P = 0.04). Hosmer–Lemeshow test indicated that model calibration was adequate after the addition of renal function markers for neurosyphilis.

Discussion

The present study explored the relationships of renal function markers with disease activities and neurosyphilis in patients with syphilis. We firstly found that BUN and CR were positively correlated with serum TRUST titer and CSF-protein, while eGFR was inversely correlated with serum TRUST titer, CSF-protein, and ESR. In addition, our findings further demonstrated that renal function markers were associated with neurosyphilis. Elevated levels of BUN and CR are associated with increased odds of neurosyphilis. Therefore, our study emphasized the critical importance of monitoring renal function markers in patients with neurosyphilis.

Syphilis is a sexually transmitted disease caused by the T.p, and the incidence of syphilis is increasing worldwide. The clinical manifestations of syphilis are highly heterogeneous, with renal involvement representing one of the features of secondary syphilis. Syphilis can cause pathological changes across various renal structures, including the glomeruli, tubules, and renal vasculature, with membranous nephropathy identified as the most common glomerular lesion in syphilis [13]. Given the intricate interplay between the nervous system and kidney function, coupled with the fact that chronic kidney disease is recognized as an independent risk factor for cerebrovascular disease [14] and cognitive impairment [15], it has been hypothesized that renal function may be associated with the development or progression of neurosyphilis. However, direct evidence supporting this hypothesis remains notably lacking.

Previous studies have established a link between renal function and various infectious diseases. For instance, a cross-sectional study reported that the prevalence of impaired renal function was higher in antiretroviral therapy-naive HIV‑infected patients than in those HIV-negative individuals (P = 0.045) [5]. In a prospective cohort study of 7221 patients with chronic kidney disease not receiving dialysis, the risk of tuberculosis was negatively correlated with the renal function determined by eGFR [6]. Our study further suggested that renal function was correlated with disease activities and neurosyphilis in HIV-negative patients with syphilis. In addition, renal function markers offered significant improvement in risk reclassification accuracy for neurosyphilis diagnosis. Taken together, these findings demonstrated that alterations in renal functional markers exhibit robust correlations with established indicators of syphilitic disease progression and provide new insight into renal function monitoring in syphilis management and treatment. Therefore, our study had important clinical implications. It is of clinical interest to see whether interventions targeting renal function would improve neurosyphilis outcomes.

The pathogenesis of neurosyphilis involves T.p causing direct vascular damage and tissue injury, as well as immune-mediated pathology. Cytokines like IL-17 play a dual role by both mediating immune responses and compromising the blood–brain barrier [16], thereby facilitating T.p invasion into the cerebrospinal fluid. Notably, clinical studies have reported that neurosyphilis patients exhibit elevated Th17 cell frequencies and IL-17 levels, suggesting a potential pathogenic association between the Th17/IL-17 axis and the development of central nervous system injury and clinical symptoms in neurosyphilis. In addition, IL-17 levels were significantly elevated in patients with primary nephrotic syndrome compared with healthy controls, which indicated that IL-17 may be involved in the pathogenesis of primary membranous nephropathy. It appears that IL-17 may serve as a shared pathogenic mediator, potentially explaining the observed renal complications in neurosyphilis patients. Furthermore, syphilitic nephropathy, a rare complication of T.p infection, involves immune complex-mediated glomerular injury. Previous studies [13] have documented cases presenting with acute nephrotic syndrome and biopsy-confirmed membranous glomerulonephritis, in conjunction with positive syphilis serology. These findings highlight the potential link between syphilis infection and renal pathology. And the arterial vessels of the kidney and brain have similar anatomical features, characterized by short arterioles branching from larger arteries that autonomously regulate tissue perfusion [17]. Therefore, it is speculated that neurosyphilis and renal impairment may involve similar mechanisms of cellular immunity and vascular damage. However, our study had some limitations. Firstly, this study was a cross-sectional observation; a causal relationship between measures of renal function and neurosyphilis cannot be inferred. Therefore, longitudinal studies are needed to establish temporal and causal relationships, as well as to explore the mechanisms underlying the observed associations. Secondly, we did not collect renal pathology and renal tubular function indicators, which can also be used to evaluate the degree of renal damage. Thirdly, the participants were exclusively Chinese patients from a single hospital in Suzhou; the findings should be extrapolated cautiously to other geographic regions and populations. In addition, the absence of continuously collected time-series data on renal function markers precludes evaluation of correlations between renal function changes, neurosyphilis, and disease activity.

Conclusions

The present study found that the renal function markers were associated with disease activity of syphilis, as well as the likelihood of neurosyphilis in HIV-negative patients with syphilis. Our findings highlight the pivotal role of these renal function markers’ monitoring in syphilis management, particularly for early identification and risk stratification of neurosyphilis.
