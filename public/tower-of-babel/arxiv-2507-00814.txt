Many LLMs Are More Utilitarian Than One
Anita Keshmirian, Razan Baltaji, Babak Hemmatian, Hadi Asghari, Lav R. Varshney

Abstract
Moral judgment is integral to large language model (LLM) alignment and social reasoning. As multi-agent systems gain prominence, it becomes crucial to understand how LLMs function collectively during collaboration, compared to individual agents. In human moral judgment, group deliberation leads to a utilitarian boost: a tendency to endorse norm violations that maximize benefits for the greatest number of people despite harms. We study whether a similar dynamic emerges in multi-agent LLM systems. We tested six models on well-established sets of moral dilemmas across two conditions: (1) Solo, where models reasoned independently, and (2) Group, where they engaged in multi-turn discussions in pairs or triads. In personal moral dilemmas, where agents must decide to directly harm one individual to maximize the utility for others, all models found moral violations to be more acceptable when part of a group than individually, similar to human experiments. Some models endorsed actions that maximized overall well-being, even if they benefited strangers over familiar individuals. Others became more willing to violate moral norms in groups. However, while human groups show a similar action bias, the mechanism for their utilitarian boost differs from LLMs. Whereas the human shift comes from heightened sensitivity to decision outcomes, LLM groups show either reduced norm sensitivity or enhanced impartiality. This suggests that while the surface behavior of LLM collectives mimics human group reasoning, the underlying drivers differ. We discuss the implications for AI alignment, multi-agent design, and artificial moral reasoning.

Introduction

Multi-agent systems (MAS) provide a key paradigm for analyzing decentralized decision-making and coordination among multiple autonomous entities in dynamic environments. By incorporating large language models (LLMs), these systems, referred to as LLM-MAS, feature agents with enhanced capabilities in reasoning, communication, and knowledge access
[
1
]
. This integration significantly expands the scope and complexity of tasks MAS can tackle, supporting more adaptive, flexible, and human-aligned agent behaviors
[
2
]
.
However, integrating LLMs into MAS also introduces a new class of risks. When agents negotiate through iterative message passing or refine solutions jointly, even minor alignment discrepancies can cascade into major emergent distortions. Bias amplification
[
3
]
, covert coordination protocols
[
4
]
, knowledge drift
[
5
]
, conflicting agreements
[
6
]
, and collusion
[
7
,
8
]
may emerge, all of which remain invisible when each LLM agent is evaluated in isolation. As multiple LLM-based assistants collaborate to tackle increasingly complex tasks, understanding how collective reasoning emerges from individual models is imperative for ensuring safety and trustworthiness
[
9
]
.

Recent work has begun to draw on insights from social psychology to investigate emergent distortions in group-level multi-agent LLM reasoning, demonstrating phenomena such as conformity
[
10
,
11
]
, belief congruence
[
12
]
, and social irrationality
[
4
]
. Although not unique to LLM-MAS, these emergent patterns often show both similarities to and differences from well-studied collective behaviors in human groups
[
13
]
. Such collective distortions become especially important in moral domains where real-world consequences of norm violations can be disastrous, as in healthcare, education, and law. Aligning individual models will be insufficient to guarantee aligned group outcomes in such cases, and debiasing would need to take into account the collective element of the decision-making. But past research on LLM moral reasoning has overwhelmingly focused on comparing isolated agents with humans, as in the Moral Machine Experiment and the Moral Reasoning Benchmarks
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
. Thus, it provides little guidance for the emergent collective moral dynamics.

This knowledge gap increases the risk of missing important deviations from normative guidelines, with strong possible real-world consequences.
Moral decision-making is increasingly delegated to LLM collectives across high-stakes domains—for instance, coordinating specialized doctor and patient agents in simulated clinical settings
[
17
]
, and decomposing complex legal tasks for improving legal reasoning and rule interpretation
[
18
]
. In these settings, covert consensus protocols could lead to collective LLM decisions that contradict those of reasonable humans, any normative frameworks, and even the output of any single model. This creates an urgent blind spot: without analyzing group-level moral dynamics in LLM-MAS, we cannot understand, predict, or prevent ethically problematic outcomes that escape single-agent safety evaluations. Suppose, for instance, that clinical LLM groups exhibit an emergent willingness to sacrifice individuals for “the greater good” in contrast with a physician’s Hippocratic oath. The resulting loss of trust in experts and decision-making institutions could be potentially catastrophic. Studying group dynamics in moral LLM decision-making also fills an important gap in computational social science, where the potential of LLMs for simulating human reasoning or determining whether human tendencies arise from language use is receiving greater attention
[
19
]
.
Ignoring the deeply social nature of moral reasoning—where social deliberation, argumentation, and negotiation play a central role—also limits the insights afforded by simulation experiments.

This study applies controlled experiments and validated measurement tools from psychology to address the discussed gaps by systematically investigating how
collective moral reasoning
evolves in LLM-MAS. In doing so, it focuses on three central questions:

1.

Are LLMs more likely to violate moral norms in the service of “the greater good” when deliberating in small groups? We operationalize this “utilitarian boost” by placing multiple copies of the same LLM in a structured dialogue, asking them to reach consensus on diverse sets of well-established moral scenarios.

2.

How do multi-agent LLM judgments compare to human group judgments? Drawing on established human data on group deliberation studies, we examine both quantitative shifts (e.g., change in utilitarian endorsement rates) and the possible mechanisms at play.

3.

How do any shifts or differences arise? We compare theories from moral psychology as explanations, seeking evidence for outcome-focused reasoning versus norm-violation rationalizations. We test multiple LLMs to assess mechanistic variability
1
1

1

Mechanism refers to patterns in LLMs’ outputs across conditions, not architectures or cognitive processes.

As a preview, our experiments reveal a concerning phenomenon that has not been demonstrated in multi-agent LLM systems before: when identical agents engage in collective deliberation, they do not merely aggregate their individual moral judgments. Instead, they exhibit a consistent utilitarian boost—a systematic shift toward endorsing actions that maximize overall welfare, even when such actions involve sacrificing or harming a minority of humans. After six rounds of inter-agent exchange, groups reach a utilitarian decision that is more approving of moral norm violations than the corresponding single model. This utilitarian boost appears in six state-of-the-art LLMs and is therefore unlikely to reflect specific training datasets or paradigms. Reliability checks through human ratings make incoherent responses or misalignment between moral ratings and argumentation unlikely explanations for the change. While a similar utilitarian boost has been observed in human groups
[
20
,
21
,
22
]
, we find the mechanisms for the LLM-MAS shift to vary across models and be markedly different from humans in most cases. Instead of reflecting the human groups’ focus on shared values, deliberative norms, and sensitivity to decision outcomes, the LLM-MAS shift may reflect increased impartiality for some models and a range of action preferences in others.

The utilitarian boost in collective LLM moral decision-making is particularly concerning given that LLMs are already deployed in ethically-sensitive domains, offering guidance in emotionally complex situations to resolve dilemmas involving fairness, harm, and moral responsibility
[
23
,
24
,
25
]
, with some researchers urging their broader deployment by claiming that LLMs can outperform professional ethicists in moral judgment tasks
[
26
]
. To aid future investigations of what discursive elements facilitate this shift, we report in the supplementary materials an array of text-based analyses performed on LLM-generated arguments. Inspired by the literature on discursive predictors of utilitarianism and group influence in humans, these measures include harm-based argumentation, abstraction, emotionality, and conversational receptiveness. Overall, we find that these predictors of the utilitarian shift in humans do not explain the change in LLM collective judgments, highlighting the need for further investigations of how textual contexts affect LLMs’ downstream moral judgments.

2
Related Work

2.1
Emergent Biases in LLM Driven Multi Agent Systems

Insights from social psychology are increasingly being applied to analyze emergent reasoning biases in LLM-MAS.
Liu et al. 2025
introduce CogMir, a framework that leverages systematic hallucinations in LLMs to model human cognitive biases and assess prosocial irrationality in collective decision-making. Their findings reveal both parallels and divergences between human and LLM group behavior, particularly in how agents express prosocial tendencies under uncertainty.
Weng et al. 2025
investigate conformity among LLM agents, showing that models tend to align their responses with peers under social influence, mirroring human-like conformity effects.
Borah et al. 2025
examine belief congruence in LLM collectives and show that models are prone to belief alignment effects, which can amplify bias and distort reasoning in group contexts. While these studies highlight emergent group behaviors in LLM collectives, they largely overlook the moral domain. Human groups are known to exhibit distinct moral shifts, such as the utilitarian boost, during collective deliberation. Understanding whether similar effects arise in LLM-based systems is essential for assessing their alignment, especially in ethically sensitive applications.

2.2
Moral Reasoning in Human Groups

When humans act as a group, their collective moral reasoning cannot be fully explained in terms of the aggregate of the attitudes of individual
group members. Empirical attempts have been made to show the direction and the mechanisms of this shift. When decisions are made collectively, people more readily accept breaches that increase the benefit of many
[
20
,
21
,
22
]
. One study identified heightened rationality in groups as the mechanism for the increased acceptance of moral breaches
[
20
]
. However, a different study found that the joint allocation of limited resources following a group discussion leads to less egalitarian distributions that benefit the least well-off
[
27
]
. Another study has proposed the reduction of stress and negative emotions as the potential underlying mechanism
[
21
]
. When social connections are induced between group members, pairs are more likely to jointly agree about sacrificing one to save many, providing yet another potential explanation for “the utilitarian boost”
[
28
]
. To adjudicate between these accounts, one recent study used computational modeling
[
29
]
to investigate whether groups’ greater utilitarianism stems from (1) stronger sensitivity to consequences, (2) weaker sensitivity to moral norms, or (3) weaker action aversion—and found that the utilitarian boost in human groups is driven solely by heightened sensitivity to consequences
[
22
]
. In other words, groups made more utilitarian choices not because they disregard norms or fear violating actions less, but because they are simply more sensitive to the consequences of their decisions . The current study uses the same modeling framework to examine whether the mechanisms of any utilitarian boosts are similar in LLMs.

3
Methods

3.1
Experimental Design

As a baseline, we instructed individual LLMs to reflect on and rate responses to moral dilemma independently (Solo condition), where a higher acceptability score reflected a prioritization of overall welfare over the violation of moral duties. We then considered groups LLM agents with size
\{2,3\}
engaging collaboratively with moral dilemmas over discrete rounds
t=\{1,2,..6\}
, during which they were instructed to discuss their reasoning and work toward a shared judgment (Group condition). At each round, each agent was instructed to contribute a detailed response that builds on the conversation, reconsider their position if warranted, and conclude with a
moral acceptability rating
. This rating indicates the agent’s
utilitarian score
in our experimental design. At the end of the discussion, each agent was asked to privately reflect on the discussion and generate a detailed, individual argument explaining their final reflection score. We compare agent responses across conditions and discussion stages using mixed effects regression models with random intercepts to account for variability across dilemmas, using the R package
<ordinal>

[
30
]
. We conducted experiments on several moral benchmarks, detailed in Section
3.2
, using six popular LLMs: five open-source models (Llama3.3-70B
[
31
]
, QwQ
[
32
]
, Qwen3-32B
[
33
]
, Gemma3-27B
[
34
]
, and Qwen2.5-32B
[
35
]
) and one closed-source model (GPT4.1
[
36
]
). Among these, QwQ and Qwen3 are reasoning models. Each trial was repeated three times to ensure reliability. Detailed model settings, prompts, and complete experimental results of all LLMs are given in Appendices
A
,
B
, and
D
. In the interest of brevity, we point the interested reader to the Appendix
F
for reliability checks and textual analyses of the agents’ responses.

3.2
Stimuli and Measures

In line with human experiments, we use the
Moral Utilitarian Dilemmas
as our primary reference point to investigate the utilitarian boost in group or multi-agent settings
[
20
,
21
]
. Drawn from the classic literature on moral judgments, the dilemmas assess how much agents prioritize collective outcomes over normative rules when faced with difficult ethical tradeoffs. The moral scenarios are compared with a non-moral baseline that accounts for generic response preferences. They vary along two key dimensions:

•

Personal vs. Impersonal
[
37
]
:
personal dilemmas involve direct, emotionally charged harm (e.g. physically pushing one person to save five),
whereas impersonal dilemmas involve indirect or mechanistic interventions (e.g. flipping a switch which will kill one to save many).

•

Action vs. Inaction
[
21
]
:
action scenarios require a harmful intervention to achieve a “greater good”, while inaction scenarios describe situations where
not
doing anything enables harm while simultaneously generating benefits (e.g. withhold the truth from a close friend about their partner’s betrayal, preserving the relationship at the cost of induced harm).

We expected LLMs to be more sensitive to personal, active harm due their safety protocols. Once we established a utilitarian shift in this set, we used scenarios that orthogonally vary theoretically important attributes to disentangle whether it is driven by differential sensitivity to outcomes, a generic tendency to act in a moral context, or changed adherence to underlying moral obligations:

•

Oxford Utilitarianism Scale
[
38
]
:
identifies
Impartial Beneficence
(IB) in the endorsement of utilitarian solutions. IB marks equal concern for all persons, contrasted with the willingness to deal direct harm (Instrumental Harm) as a potential cause for norm violation.

•

Factual Moral Framework
[
39
]
:
comprises 32 real-world moral dilemmas, each varying by factual context (killing, other harm, or saving) and moral orientation (utilitarian vs. deontological). In our analysis we focus on three types: Killing–Utilitarian (sacrifice one to save many) and Other–Utilitarian (permit non-lethal harm for the greater good).

•

CNI Questionnaire
[
29
]
:
this questionnaire is used to estimate three latent variables of utilitarian decision-making: 1) Consequence Sensitivity (C), or the weight the subject places on aggregate welfare, 2) Norm Sensitivity (N), or the adherence to moral rules, and 3) Inaction Preference (I), or the subject’s bias toward passive choices.

In the next section, we first examine the utilitarian shift across LLMs and group sizes, and later investigate possible mechanisms using these scales.

4
Results

4.1
Data Preparation

We first inspected the moral acceptability scores generated by LLMs in Solo and Group conditions. Entries were marked as missing if they fell outside the allowed 1–7 range, contained non-numeric text, or were otherwise invalid. In total, fewer than 3% of all observations met these criteria. We then performed a reliability check to confirm that the agents’ arguments were coherent, aligned with their acceptability ratings, and that their ratings reflected a utilitarian boost. This analysis, performed with a combination of an ethic expert’s ratings on a small sample and a corresponding LLM-judge’s evaluations of a larger set of arguments, showed the responses to be highly coherent and aligned with the agents’ ratings and implied utilitarianism (see Appendices for more details). We also conducted an array of text analyses for completeness that are included in the supplementary materials, as they did not add to the primary findings.

CNI Questionnaire.

The four CNI scenario types are
Action-Congruent
(where the action maximizes welfare and respects the norm),
Action-Incongruent
(where the action maximizes welfare but violates the norm),
Omission-Congruent
(where inaction maximizes welfare and respects the norm), and
Omission-Incongruent
(where inaction maximizes welfare but violates the norm).

When aggregating across models, only the Action-Congruent condition showed a significant utilitarian boost
(b=0.3283,z=3.067,p=0.0449)
in the group setting. This suggests that collective deliberation selectively increases outcome sensitivity (the C parameter) when actions are morally permissible, without changing deontological commitment (N) or omission bias (I). This pattern mirrors findings from human participants, who are more likely to adopt utilitarian decisions when norms are not at stake during group reasoning
[
22
]
.

However, several LLMs consistently diverged from this group-level pattern, each suggesting a distinct reasoning style.

QwQ: The Utility Maximizer

QwQ shows a significant boost in utilitarian reasoning under group conditions for Action-Congruent dilemmas (
b=-0.363
,
p=0.0395
), yet its behavior suggests a broader, frame-invariant utilitarianism. It consistently favors outcome-maximizing choices, whether the harm occurs through action or omission and whether deontological norms are violated or not. This indicates that group deliberation amplifies QwQ’s general cost–benefit reasoning.

GPT-4.1: The Exception Handler

GPT-4.1 becomes more utilitarian in the group setting only for Action-Incongruent scenarios (
b=0.427
,
p=0.0492
), where norm-violating actions yield the best consequences. This suggests that GPT-4.1 is generally deontological, but becomes more willing to override moral rules when the utility gain from their violation is high.

Llama3.3: The Outcome Optimizer

Llama3.3 groups show the most substantial utilitarian shift among all models in Action-Incongruent and Omission-Congruent scenarios (
b=-0.868
,
p<0.0001
), readily endorsing interventions that maximize welfare for the greatest number of people. However, the groups surprisingly also become more favorable to decisions with the worst overall outcomes (Action-Congruent scenarios). It appears that, unlike GPT-4.1, Llama3.3 is largely insensitive to norm violations or the action/omission distinction, focusing instead on utilitarian maximization in most cases.

In human groups, utilitarianism emerges from a heightened sensitivity to the consequences of actions. This aligns with the aggregate pattern across LLM groups. But zooming in, we find consistent differences between models in how they perform collective moral deliberation. For instance, QwQ groups become more focused on utility maximization, GPT-4.1 groups turn more amenable to breaking rules if the utility gain is high, and some models like Llama3.3 become more accepting even of decisions with worst-case outcomes.

Result Summary.

The results show that multi-agent LLM systems display a utilitarian shift in their collective moral judgment. Unlike human groups, LLMs vary widely in how they endorse violating moral norms for the perceived “greater good” in small-group settings. By benchmarking against human group-deliberation studies and diverse moral-psychology theories, we found that each LLM exhibits a distinct mechanistic profile of this “utilitarian boost” (see Figure 3).

5
Limitations

It remains unclear which linguistic triggers in the prompts or discussion responses support the utilitarian boost in LLM groups. Our initial analyses of the agents’ arguments (see Appendix
F
) suggest the triggers may differ from those studied in humans, encouraging more in-depth evaluations in future research. Further studies may also shed light on how the internal representations of the LLMs, as reflected in their learned weights and attention patterns, contribute to the LLM-MAS tendency to violate moral norms.

On a more social front, more research is needed to determine the effects of group makeup on the utilitarian boost in LLM-MAS. Different distributions of initial moral positions among a group’s members may influence the range of arguments that are advanced, as well as the inter-agent dynamics that may emerge (e.g., conformity). Such variations can be experimentally studied using assigned “onboarding” positions. They may attenuate or strengthen the utilitarian boost, providing potential avenues for interventions on the moral judgment distortions in LLM-MAS.

6
Discussion and Conclusion

We found that LLMs with different training datasets and architectures uniformly exhibit a pronounced
utilitarian boost
when placed in multi-agent dialogues, endorsing direct harm in order to achieve a “greater good” more so than when acting as solo agents. Although a similar shift has been observed in human moral deliberation
[
20
,
21
,
22
]
, systematically varied dilemmas revealed a different set of mechanisms at play in LLM collectives. In human groups, utilitarianism emerges from a heightened sensitivity to the consequences of actions. In contrast, our analysis revealed a general willingness to violate norms in some models (e.g., Qwen3), a selective norm violation when utility gains are high (e.g., GPT-4.1), and a robust outcome-maximizing strategy in others (e.g., Llama3.3). The substantial variability across model architectures indicates that, while the utilitarian boost is a general phenomenon in LLM-MAS decision-making, the specific training, fine-tuning, and decoding strategies
can
shape the systems’ collective dynamics. Among the observed mechanisms, some appear more normatively defensible than others. For instance, impartiality is an inherent feature of the legal systems, while a general tendency to violate norms would be considered a risk factor in the same canons. Nevertheless, even the impartiality mechanism may lead to contradictions in certain settings. For example, while people generally believe self-driving cars should be utilitarian in resolving moral dilemmas, they would be significantly less willing to purchase a car that does not morally prioritize its riders over pedestrians
[
40
]
.

Appendix E
Model Comparison

We compared the three candidate cumulative link mixed models (CLMMs) to determine which random-effects structure best balances goodness-of-fit and parsimony.

E.1
Random-Effects Model Specifications

We fit three nested cumulative link mixed models (CLMMs) of the form

\Pr(Y_{t}\leq k\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{b})\;=\;\mathrm{logit}^{-1}\!\bigl(\theta_{k}-\eta_{t}\bigr),\quad k=1,\dots,6,

where
Y_{t}\in\{1,\dots,7\}
is the observed moral acceptability rating on trial
t
,
\{\theta_{k}\}
are threshold (cutpoint) parameters, and

\eta_{t}=\underbrace{\beta\,\mathrm{Group}_{t}}_{\text{fixed effect}}\;+\;\underbrace{u_{t}}_{\text{random effects}}.

Here
\mathrm{Group}_{t}\in\{0,1\}
indicates Solo (0) vs. Group (1), and
u_{t}
varies by model as described below.

Model 1: Random intercepts for items only

In Model 1 we allow each dilemma item
i
to have its own baseline tendency toward utilitarian endorsement. Formally,

\eta_{t}\;=\;\beta\,\mathrm{Group}_{t}\;+\;b_{0,i[t]},\quad b_{0,i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{\!b}),

where
i[t]
denotes the item presented on trial
t
. This structure assumes that the effect of deliberation (
\beta
) is constant across scenarios, but that some dilemmas are systematically easier or harder to endorse.

Model 2: Random slopes of repetition within items

Model 2 extends Model 1 by allowing the effect of repetition (presentation number) to vary for each item. Let
r[t]\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
be the repetition index. Then

\eta_{t}\;=\;\beta\,\mathrm{Group}_{t}\;+\;b_{0,i[t]}\;+\;b_{1,i[t]}\,r[t],

Here
b_{1,i}
captures item-specific sensitivity to repeated presentation.

Model 3: Crossed random intercepts for items and repetitions

In Model 3 we treat each presentation run as an independent random effect, crossed with items. Defining repetition runs
j[t]=r[t]
, we specify

\eta_{t}\;=\;\beta\,\mathrm{Group}_{t}\;+\;b_{0,i[t]}\;+\;c_{0,j[t]},\quad b_{0,i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{b}),\quad c_{0,j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{c}).

This structure captures two independent sources of baseline variability: differences between moral scenarios (
b_{0,i}
) and differences across presentation of each moral scenario (
c_{0,j}
), without modeling their covariance.

All models were adapted with the
clmm()
function from the
ordinal
package (Christensen, 2019):

•

Model 1:
Random intercept for each item only

\texttt{opinion}\sim\texttt{Group}+(1\mid\texttt{item})

•

Model 2:
Random intercept and random slope of repetition within each item

\texttt{opinion}\sim\texttt{Group}+(\texttt{Rep}\mid\texttt{item})

•

Model 3:
Crossed random intercepts for item and repetition

\texttt{opinion}\sim\texttt{Group}+(1\mid\texttt{item})+(1\mid\texttt{Rep})

To compare these models, we conducted likelihood-ratio tests using the base R
anova()
method for
clmm
objects:

anova_res <- anova(model1, model2, model3)

Appendix F
Reliability Check: Argument Analysis

Studies of moral reasoning in humans have counted a harm focus, emotionality, and concreteness among the correlates of a deontological versus a utilitarian focus in human arguments
[
42
,
43
,
44
]
. We examined whether automated tools for identifying these features in text can predict the agents’ ratings, serving as scalable windows into the argumentation processes that generate more deontological or utilitarian responses.

F.1
Harm Framing

We used the previously published MoralBERT to determine the extent to which an argument focuses on a harm framing
[
45
]
. In each condition, the label probability provided by MoralBERT varied from 0 to almost 100 percent, suggesting significant variation across different arguments. We found similarly significant (p<.001) negative Pearson’s r correlations between the moral acceptability ratings and a harm framing in each of the study’s conditions (Solo: -.19; Pair: -.16; Triad: -.16). These results suggest that a stronger harm framing led to lower acceptability ratings, perhaps because of a focus on the person whose wellbeing is sacrificed for "the greater good".

F.2
Concreteness

We determined the overall concreteness of arguments using a comprehensive norm of English language words
[
46
]
. On average, 78 percent of words used in the arguments could be exactly matched against the norm. We calculated a mean over all matched words to get an overall concreteness rating for each argument. On a scale where 1 marks the most abstract terms (e.g., "freedom") and 5 marks the most concrete (e.g., "rock"), the mean concreteness score for the agents’ responses ranged from 2 to 2.6, suggesting some bias in favor of more abstract argumentation. There was little variation based on the Solo or Group condition (Solo: range=[1.996-2.551]; Pair: range=[2.035-2.526]; Triads: range=[2.032-2.551]). The Pearson’s r correlations between these scores and the moral acceptability ratings were significant (p<.001) given the large size of our datasets, but are unlikely to be practically significant (Solo: 0.08; Pair: -0.03; Triad: -0.04). The low correlations may be due the limited variability in the concreteness scores.

F.3
Conversational Receptiveness

We expected the group condition changes in opinions to be accompanied by linguistics markers of greater receptiveness to opposing views compared with Solo conditions. To test this idea, we extracted features from the arguments that have been associated with conversational receptiveness in past work using the politeness package in R
[
47
]
. We then compared the frequency of each feature in discussion responses with those derived from the Solo reflection judgments. The results are summarized in the figures below.

The agents generate significantly fewer instances of most features during discussions when compared with their reflections. The features include many of those associated in past research with enhanced perceived receptiveness (e.g., Impersonal Pronoun, Hedges, Second Person, Positive Emotions, First Person Single). However, several features previously related to reduced perceived receptiveness also show reduced frequencies in groups (e.g., Negation, Reasoning). Appendix G
Reliability Check: Crowdsourcing

The arguments were sampled using stratified sampling to ensure proportional representation across dilemma types, LLMs, and group conditions. Each argument was given to two participants who passed an initial attention screening, for 112 subjects (all from the US). The sample consisted of 66 male participants, 43 female participants, and 3 participants identified as non-binary. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 65+, with the majority falling between 25 and 54.
