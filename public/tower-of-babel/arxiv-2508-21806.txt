Suppression of errors in collectively coded information
Martin Falk*, Leon Zhou*, Yoshiya J. Matsubara, Kabir Husain, Jack W. Szostak, Arvind Murugan

Abstract
Modern life largely transmits genetic information from mother to daughter through the duplication of single physically intact molecules that encode information. However, copying an extended molecule requires highly processive copying machinery and high fidelity that scales with the genome size to avoid the error catastrophe. Here, we explore these fidelity requirements in an alternative architecture, the virtual circular genome, in which no one physical molecule encodes the full genetic information. Instead, information is encoded and transmitted in a collective of overlapping and interacting segments. Using a model experimental system of a complex mixture of DNA oligos that can partly anneal and extend off each other, we find that mutant oligomers are suppressed relative to a model without collective encoding. Through simulations and theory, we show that this suppression of mutants can be explained by competition for productive binding partners. As a consequence, information can be propagated robustly in a virtual circular genome even if the mutation rate is above the error catastrophe for a physically intact genome.



Modern life transmits genetic information using long, physically continuous polymers such as chromosomes and plasmids. This mode of storage imposes several stringent demands on the copying process. First, replication must be highly processive: subsequent bases of the coding polymer must be copied in sequence without dissociation. Second, the strands must be physically separated during copying, requiring mechanisms capable of strand displacement. Third, fidelity must scale and increase with genome length to avoid the so-called error catastrophe, where errors accumulate faster than they can be purged by natural selection
eigen1971selforganization
.

All of these requirements are met by complex life today but at the cost of enormous complexity in the copying machinery. For example, the requirements of strand displacement and processivity are are handled by the coordinated action of helicases, single-strand binding proteins and polymerases. Fidelity is enforced through multiple layers of error correction, from nucleotide selectivity
echols1991fidelity
and exonucleolytic proofreading
brutlag1972enzymatic
to post-replicative mismatch repair
modrich1996mismatch
. But such complexity likely arose gradually and would have been unavailable during the earliest stages of evolution of life on earth, raising the question of how genomic information could be copied at early stages.
Notably, even efforts to engineer minimal cells today are constrained by these same requirements: currently, these ‘minimal’ cells must still encode genomes large enough to code for complex proteins required for reliable replication of long polymers
hutchison2016design
.

As a consequence, several prior studies have explored how informational patterns can emerge and persist in pools of short oligonucleotides, often using random sequence mixtures subjected to cycles of annealing, extension, or ligation to mimic simple or primitive replication environments
prywes2016nonenzymatic
;
mutschler2018random
;
toyabe2019cooperative
;
kudella2021structured
;
rosenberger2021self
;
mizuuchi2023minimal
;
calacca2024replication
. These studies showed that even in the absence of long templates or sophisticated enzymes, simple chemical processes can give rise to heritable molecular features. Building on this foundation, we examine a more structured scenario in which the genome is deliberately encoded across a designed set of overlapping sequences that form a virtual circular genome (VCG)
zhou2021virtual
;
ding2023experimental
. In this architecture, no single molecule encodes the full sequence; instead, information is distributed redundantly across a population of short oligonucleotides, each of which can act as both primer and template. This compositional, collectively encoded system enables replication through thermally-driven melt-anneal-extend cycles.

Here, we explore the propagation of errors in this alternative distributed architecture, the Virtual Circular Genome, for storing and replicating genetic information. We show that cooperative interactions inherent to the VCG architecture naturally suppress the propagation of replication errors. Using an experimental DNA system combined with simulations and theoretical analysis, we demonstrate that mutant alleles, particularly when they arise at 3
′
-ends of strands, are at a competitive disadvantage (compared to wildtype alleles) due to reduced access to productive extension partners. This fidelity arises from ecological interactions among molecules, rather than explicit proofreading, and is enhanced by stalling at mismatches
huang1992extension
;
rajamani2010effect
. These results suggest that the architecture of genome encoding can play a central role in enabling robust information transmission, with implications for early life and the design of minimal or synthetic self-replicating systems.

Although our ultimate interest lies in RNA-based virtual circular genomes, we employ here a DNA-based experimental model as done in prior related work
kudella2021structured
;
calacca2024replication
;
luther1998surface
;
sievers1994oligomers
. This choice allows precise control over sequence composition, strand concentrations, and reaction conditions, while enabling direct quantification of wildtype and mutant abundances. Importantly, despite the chemical differences between DNA and RNA, the critical features relevant to mutation suppression are preserved: both systems involve complex mixtures of overlapping oligomers that anneal to multiple partners, and relax slowly toward equilibrium through numerous competing interactions (including strand displacement).
As such, the DNA system can faithfully test whether collective encoding suppresses mutation propagation, while deferring RNA-specific differences, such as persistence length and the mechanism of template copying, to later discussion.

We designed a DNA-based system composed of short, overlapping oligonucleotides that form a redundant circular architecture. The full VCG comprises 12 distinct 25-nt strands and their reverse complements. Each of the 12 strands in one direction overlaps with its downstream neighbor (i.e., the next strand in the 5
′
–3
′
direction) by 20 bases, encoding a 60 bp circular genome (Fig.
2
A, Supp. Table I). Thus, each strand initially has four
binding partners
that productively allow it to be extended or part of it to be copied, i.e., reverse-complementary strands that can hybridize and serve as templates and/or as primers, with partial complementarity ranging from 5 to 20 bp. Each strand also has an additional five binding partners which anneal in unproductive configurations that do not allow for primer extension.

To evaluate how mutations propagate within this architecture, we introduced a 25-nt mutant strand that differs from one VCG strand by a single 4 bp substitution near the center of the sequence (Fig.
2
B, Supp. Sec. I). Mutant strands were spiked into VCG mixtures at defined low proportions (2.5%, 5%, or 25% relative to the wildtype) to mimic rare variant emergence.

Extension of VCG strands was driven by thermal cycling with Bst DNA Polymerase (Large Fragment, NEB M0275), using 10–30 cycles of alternating denaturation (
80~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}
) and annealing/extension (
35~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}
), followed by a final enzyme deactivation step at
90~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}
for 10 minutes. Notably, no new oligomers are supplied to this system; extension is initiated by overlap-driven annealing of partially complementary VCG strands (Supp. Sec. III).
Gel electrophoresis confirmed that initial 25-nt VCG strands were extended incrementally over cycles, with products reaching a length of 45-nt by cycle 10, and further reaching 60-nt by cycle 20, with gel profiles remaining unchanged through cycle 30 (Fig.
2
E). Duplexes up to 45 bp (
T_{\mathrm{m}}=80~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}
) likely remain partially meltable during cycling, allowing strand reshuffling and continued extension, whereas 60 bp duplexes (
T_{\mathrm{m}}=82~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}
) are too stable to denature, halting further growth.

To quantify the relative amplification of wildtype and mutant strands, we used sequence-specific qPCR with forward primers that selectively bind either the wildtype or mutant sequence, combined with a shared reverse primer (Fig.
2
C, Supp. Sec. IV). Two aliquots from each thermocycled sample were analyzed in parallel using both primer sets, allowing independent quantification of wildtype and mutant strand concentrations (Fig.
2
D–E). Ct values were determined from the qPCR amplification curves and converted into absolute concentrations using standard curves generated from known DNA concentrations included in the same qPCR run. Control experiments confirmed that the qPCR assay reliably quantifies each strand with high specificity and within the relevant concentration range (Supp. Fig. 2).

qPCR analysis revealed that the wildtype strand was consistently amplified more than the mutant strand over 30 cycles of VCG extension, supporting the hypothesis that competitive strand interactions favor wildtype proliferation and effectively suppress the mutant (Fig.
2
E, Supp. Sec. VII).

To investigate the role of VCG architecture in mutation suppression, we varied the system’s ‘virtualness’—defined as the number of overlapping strands encoding the genome—by constructing VCGs with 12, 6, or 3 strand pairs (Fig. 3C, Supp. Table II).
All of these sets of strands encode the same 60 bp genome but differ in the number of distinct potential reverse complement binding partners available to each strand.
Lower virtualness (e.g., the 3-strand VCG) more closely resembles a real physical circular genome in that it has low redundancy measured by how many different strands cover a given subsequence of the genome. We observed stronger suppression of mutant amplification in higher-virtualness VCGs across all mutant input levels we tested, including 2.5% (Fig.
3
A–B), 5% (Supp. Fig. S6), and 25% (Supp. Fig. S7).
In the 12-strand VCG, the wildtype outcompeted the mutant by over 10-fold, while in the 3-strand VCG, the amplification advantage for the wildtype was nearly absent (Fig.
3
D).

We next examined how the position of the 4 bp mutation within the mutant strand affects its suppression. Using additional mutant variants with mutations placed near the 3
′
or 5
′
ends, we found that mutations near the 3
′
end were most strongly suppressed during extension (Fig.
3
A–B). In contrast, the 5
′
-end mutant exhibited amplification levels nearly indistinguishable from the wildtype. This trend held consistently across all VCG virtualness levels and initial mutant proportions (Fig. 3D, Supp. Fig. 5, 6, 7), suggesting that suppression is more effective when the mutation is closer to the 3
′
end—the site where strand extension initiates. This positional bias aligns with the expectation that errors or mismatches near the 3
′
end would exhibit a decreased likelihood of propagation.

These results were consistent across all initial mutant concentrations tested (2.5%, 5%, and 25%; Supp. Fig. 5, 6, 7).
Together, these findings demonstrate that both VCG virtualness and mutation position govern selective replication within this strand-extension-based system.
The results suggest that VCG architectures can impose intrinsic fidelity constraints, naturally suppressing the propagation of mutant alleles relative to the wildtype allele during thermal cycles.

To understand the source of position- and virtualness-dependent mutation fates in the VCG, we build a simplified model of VCG replication (Fig.
4
A). In this model, we consider an initial pool of oligos which grows through repeated thermal cycles.
This initial oligo pool is based on the experimental oligo pools studied in Sec.
I
. It contains 12 oligo sequences of length 25 bp which form a consensus VCG sequence of length 60 bp with an offset of 5 bp between each consecutive oligo. In addition, the pool also contains all the reverse complements of the original 12 oligos, plus a small concentration of an additional oligo that is identical to the underlying VCG sequence except at a single position, where it contains a mutant allele.

The initial pool undergoes thermal cycles which consist of the following:

1.

First, oligos anneal with any possible complementary partner to form duplexes, following irreversible second-order reaction kinetics until no further annealing reactions are possible. Only oligos which have exact contiguous overlaps above a threshold
o_{min}
amount are allowed to bind.

2.

Second, if an oligo has an annealed 3
′
end, it acts as a primer and is extended using its annealed duplex partner as a template. This extension continues without any errors until the extending oligo reaches either the 5
′
end of its template or a maximal length
l_{max}
equal to the length of the consensus VCG sequence (60 bp).

3.

Finally, during the melting stage, duplexes dissociate into single-stranded oligonucleotides unless their overlap exceeds a specified threshold length
o_{max}
.

In most simulations, we take
o_{min}=2
and
o_{max}=55
, meaning that oligos must overlap by at least 2 bp in order to anneal and duplexes with more than 55 bp overlap do not melt (as approximately observed in our experimental conditions).
We note that annealing in any single thermal cycle is random in that the annealed duplexes are kinetically determined and thus only a small fraction of an oligo may be bound to its perfect complementary pair.
For instance, in the initial oligo pool with where oligos are of length 25 bp and are separated by offsets of 5 bp, then any oligo has
9
potential binding partners, only one of which is its perfect complementary pair. Furthermore, if all oligos are initially present at equal concentration, then only
1/9
of annealed duplexes will be bound in perfect complementary pairs which cannot extend any further.

Thus, because this system anneals through irreversible kinetics, not all pairs bind with perfect complementarity and hence extension can occur through the formation of duplexes with overhangs.
The formation of duplexes with perfect complementarity is further disrupted by the melting phase of thermal cycles on a timescale
\tau_{cycle}
.
For more details on how our model is defined and implemented, please see Supp. Sec. VIII.

II.2
Mutant suppression in the simplified model

Following simulation of repeated thermal melt-anneal-extend cycles, we first observe that oligos extend on each other (Fig.
4
B, top); the distribution of oligo lengths shifts from their initial values (25 bp) to a maximal length set by a combination of
o_{max}
(the maximal duplex overlap length for unbinding during the melt phase) and
l_{max}
(the maximal oligo length). As a consequence of their extension, oligos become longer and more likely to be trapped in duplexes that overlap too much to unbind during the melting step.
In particular, while oligos at the end of thermal cycle 0 are still all single-stranded, virtually all oligos are bound in duplexes of length 55-60 bp at the end of cycle 10 (Fig.
4
B, bottom). We refer to this state where all oligos are duplex-bound as ‘pool stasis’, since the VCG cannot replicate anymore.
We note that introducing a source of short oligos (e.g. from external sources or from templated nucleation of activated nucleotides) would instead allow for a continued steady state of pool replication.

Extension also allows for an increase in the concentration of mut and wt alleles, despite the fact that no new oligos are created in our model. As oligos extend off each other as templates, new strands bind downstream of the mut and wt alleles (i.e., between the allele on an oligo and the 3
′
end of that oligo) and extend past those alleles, thereby increasing the concentration of oligos which contain the two alleles.

To model the qPCR readouts of the DNA model experiments, we track the relative amplification of the mut and wt alleles by measuring the total concentration of oligos that contain the allele along with a flanking region around the allele. This flanking region is set by the oligo that the mut allele initially appears on. After normalizing these concentrations to the initial concentration of flanking region oligos, we can define a ‘wt advantage’ statistic as the ratio between the wt region amplification at pool stasis and the mut region amplification at pool stasis. We find that the wt advantage increases with the virtualness
V
of the initial oligo pool (Fig.
4
C), where
V
here can be defined as the number of unique oligos that cover the consensus VCG sequence in the oligo pool. We also find that the wt advantage measured at pool stasis is stronger as the initial mutant position shifts from the 5
′
end to the 3
′
of the oligo it starts on (Fig.
4
D).

In order to find an explanation for the observation of mutant suppression both in our experimental DNA system as well as our simplified simulation model, we focus on the case where the mutant allele is initially at the 3
′
end of the oligo on which it occurs. This is both the situation where the mutant suppression is the most dramatic (Fig.
4
D), as well as the most relevant for mutant alleles that arise through mutations during extension.

We propose that the wt advantage comes from a binding partner effect. In the VCG pool, each oligonucleotide has multiple potential reverse complement binding partners, with the number of potential partners increasing with the virtualness
V=3,\ldots 12
of the initial VCG oligo pool. These binding partners all compete for annealing with one another on the template oligo, on equal footing with the perfect reverse complement partner.

Crucially, only binding partners whose 3
′
ends anneal downstream of the allele (i.e., between the allele and the 3
′
end of the template), can increase that allele’s reverse complement concentration via extension.
In contrast, binding partners whose 3
′
ends are upstream of the allele may extend but do not result in copying of the allele’s reverse complement, precluding an increase in concentration of the allele itself upon further rounds of extension.

Therefore, a mutant allele located near the 3
′
end of an oligo has fewer binding partners that can bind downstream of that allele and make its reverse complement during extension. In comparison, the same allele, when located towards the 5
′
end of an oligo, can be copied off of by almost all binding partners that anneal to that template oligo (see Fig.
5
A).

The binding partner effect implies that the instantaneous
per-cycle rate at which an allele on a given oligo can produce more copies of its reverse complement is set by two quantities: 1. the concentration of ‘productive’ binding partner oligos which can bind and extend past the allele and are thus productive in copying that allele, and; 2. the concentration of all binding partner oligos that compete with (1), regardless of whether they can pick up the allele upon extension.
An allele is therefore more productive if it exists further from the 3
′
oligo end and hence a greater fraction of the oligo’s binding partners can form pairings that allow the allele to be copied.
Correspondingly, an allele is also more productive if it sits closer to the 5
′
oligo end since there are fewer binding partners that do not pick up the allele upon extension.

F^{A}_{i}
for an allele
A
on a single-stranded oligo
i
as:

\displaystyle F^{A}_{i}\equiv\frac{\sum_{j\in D^{A}_{i}}c_{ss,j}}{\sum_{j\in B_{i}}c_{ss,j}},

(1)

where

is the concentration of single-stranded oligo

i

and per-oligo, we take a concentration-weighted average of the individual oligo productivity factors
F^{A}_{i}

f^{A}

\displaystyle f^{A}=\frac{1}{c^{A}_{tot}}\sum_{i\in O_{A}}c_{ss,i}~F^{A}_{i},

(2)

This definition captures the intuition of how the position of an allele on an oligo influences the allele’s productivity. For instance, when oligos are shorter than the oligo length maximum

and as a potential evolutionary precursor to more elaborate error-correcting proofreading systems
ravasio2024minimal
. Here, we investigate how stalling influences the propagation of mutant alleles within the VCG framework.

We consider annealed configurations where one of the oligos in the duplex is not correctly matched to its template at its 3
′
end. This can either happen when an oligo with a mutant allele at its 3
′
end is annealed to an oligo with a wt allele at that position, or it is an oligo with a wt allele at its 3
′
end annealed to an oligo with a mutant allele there.

For such duplexes where one of the oligos has a mismatch at its 3
′
end, stalling delays extension by a time
\tau_{stall}
. Whether or not the duplex gets to extend therefore depends crucially on whether thermal cycling (with timescale
\tau_{cycle}
) drives the duplex apart before the stalling delay can be overcome. For primers with no 3
′
mismatch, there is no barrier to extension and such primers will fully extend during the current thermal cycle. To model the effect of stalling, we posit that primers with a mismatched 3
′
end get to extend through an exponential decay process with timescale
\tau_{stall}
. Hence by the end of a melt-anneal-extend cycle of length
\tau_{cycle}
, only a fraction
1-e^{-\frac{\tau_{cycle}}{\tau_{stall}}}
of primers with a mismatched 3
′
end will have extended (Fig.
6
A). Please see Supp Sec. VIII for further discussion and implementation details on stalled extension.

Stalling can have a dramatic effect on the suppression of mutant alleles in a VCG scenario depending on the relative balance of
\tau_{stall}
and
\tau_{cycle}
(Fig.
6
B, top). When
\tau_{stall}\ll\tau_{cycle}
, we find the expected mutant suppression described earlier, which is due to the transient replicative ability reduction arising from the binding partner effect.
For intermediate
\tau_{stall}\sim\tau_{cycle}
, that transient period is greatly increased for the mutant because only a fraction of mismatched primers extends in any cycle; as a consequence, the mutant allele stays encoded at the 3
′
end for many more cycles and has a reduction in replicative ability. When
\tau_{stall}\gg\tau_{cycle}
, the mutation remains confined to the 3
′
end and there is no increase in mutant allele concentration at all.
Thus stalling provides a suppression of mutant alleles by lengthening the transient period of reduced replicative ability for the mutant. We calculated the amplification of mutant alleles at the end of 10 thermal cycles as a function of the ratio
\frac{\tau_{stall}}{\tau_{cycle}}
(Fig.
6
B, bottom). At
\tau_{stall}=0
, stalling is not present, but mutant amplification is still suppressed due to collective VCG effects. Mutant amplification drops rapidly as a function of increasing
\tau_{stall}
.

IV
Discussion

Our results show that collective replication in a virtual circular genome (VCG) architecture intrinsically suppresses the propagation of mutant alleles, even in the absence of high-fidelity copying or kinetic proofreading. Suppression is strongest when mutations are 3
′
-proximal and increases with the VCG’s ‘virtualness’ (redundancy of overlapping oligos). Experiments, simulations, and a reduced theory agree that mutant alleles are selectively under-amplified relative to wildtype alleles due to competitive binding for productive extension partners.

Mechanistically, the suppression follows from a binding partner effect: an allele’s chance of being copied depends on how many annealed partners place their 3
′
end
downstream
of that allele so that extension traverses it. 3
′
-proximal mutants have fewer such productive partners and thus are at a disadvantage compared to wildtype alleles that are coded at varying distances from the 3
′
end. Polymerase stalling at mismatched 3
′
ends prolongs this period of lowered replicative ability by delaying extension past the mismatch. Consequently, the combined effect of partner limitation and stalling leads to strong suppression that strengthens with virtualness and with increasing ratio
\tau_{stall}/\tau_{cycle}
of stalling time to thermal melt-anneal-extend cycle time. Unlike mispriming suppression in PCR, which relies on reduced binding affinity from mismatches, our effect persists even when binding between wildtype and mutant is allowed.

Prior classic theories of distributed replication (e.g., hypercycles
eigen1971selforganization
) posit cooperative networks that can stabilize information, but they have been difficult to realize and manipulate at the molecular level
vaidya2012spontaneous
;
szathmary2013propagation
. Although the VCG can perhaps be cast, in retrospect, as a cooperative network in the spirit of hypercycle theory, our work begins from a concrete molecular specification: here, a set of overlapping strands and their allowed anneal–extend interactions. That molecular starting point, rather than an abstract catalytic graph, is what makes the system experimentally tractable and tunable, allowing controlled variation of virtualness, mutation position, and kinetic parameters. In this aspect, our system shares features with molecularly-defined experimental models
ashkenasy2004design
;
vaidya2012spontaneous
;
lincoln2009self
;
li2013dynamic
;
jeancolas2021rna
. While the focus of these prior works was often the emergence of catalytic cycles from random sequence pools, we focused on the maintenance of information in a more structured pool. This assumed structure let us quantify an architecture-driven fidelity mechanism and map how virtualness and 3
′
proximity govern the fate of mutants.

Our experiments emphasize primer extension in a fixed pool of oligomers that eventually ‘die’ by forming long, unmeltable duplexes; sustained replication will require continual regeneration of short oligos (e.g.,
de novo
synthesis from activated nucleotides).
Moving to driven steady states could strengthen mutant suppression, especially under strong stalling, because wildtype alleles can continue to amplify while 3
′
-proximal mutants remain suppressed. While we focused on templated extension, templated ligation is natural in distributed architectures
toyabe2019cooperative
;
kudella2021structured
;
rosenberger2021self
;
burger2024toward
;
matsubara2018kinetic
and enables growth from both 5
′
and 3
′
ends. The binding partner effect will have to be studied and quantified in the context of ligation to understand biases intrinsic to that mode of replication.
We expect the core ecological mechanism revealed by our experimental DNA model to carry over to RNA; however, backbone-specific chemistry (persistence length, hydrolysis, non-enzymatic extension kinetics) will shift quantitative outcomes. Additional instabilities, most notably mismatched annealing
todisco2022hybridization
and sequence repeats
chamanian2022computer
, remain to be mapped to determine parameter regimes in which VCGs are stable. In particular, some mutations could create repeats which will allow oligomers to anneal and extend off novel regions of the genome, generating novel chimeric oligomers. Finally, our complete set of results on mutations at varying distances from the
′
end also explain the fate of mutations that arise due to processes like deamination
duncan1980mutagenic
which are not confined to the 3
′
end.

Despite these limitations and potential extensions, our work shows that genomic architecture can act as an intrinsic bias for how stored information evolves. That is, by filtering which novelties are propagated versus rejected, the architecture shapes evolvability and effectively tunes the error threshold without explicit proofreading. Comparable architecture-level filters might be operating in extant biology, for example how RNA viruses with segmented genomes can undergo genetic reassortment
mcdonald2016reassortment
.
Other non-standard genome architectures include those of multipartite bacteria (e.g.,
Borrelia burgdorferi
fraser1997genomic
;
ren2023organization
), which partition essential genes across a chromosome plus several plasmids; and trypanosomatids
borst1991kinetoplast
;
lukeš2002kinetoplast
, which maintain kinetoplast DNA as a topologically linked network of maxi- and minicircles.
In all these cases, information is distributed across many molecules with heterogeneous maintenance and inheritance, rather than on one continuous polymer. We lack a general framework for how such architectures influence mutation fate, dosage control, recombination, assortment noise, and long-term evolvability or why particular architectures arise and persist. While we do not address architectural features specific to these extant systems, our work highlights open questions about the trade-offs of distributing genomic information across many versus few molecules.
