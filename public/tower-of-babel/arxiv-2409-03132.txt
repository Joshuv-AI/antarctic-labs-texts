Abstract
. We describe a mechanism for transport of energy in a mechanical system consisting of a pendulum and a rotator subject to a random perturbation. The perturbation that we consider is the product of a Hamiltonian vector field and a scalar, continuous, stationary Gaussian process with Hölder continuous realizations, scaled by a smallness parameter. We show that for almost every realization of the stochastic process, there is a distinguished set of times for which there exists a random normally hyperbolic invariant manifold with associated stable and unstable manifolds that intersect transversally, for all sufficiently small values of the smallness parameter. We derive the existence of orbits along which the energy changes over time by an amount proportional to the smallness parameter. This result is related to the Arnold diffusion problem for Hamiltonian systems, which we treat here in the random setting.

1.
Introduction

The main idea of the present work is using randomness to overcome geometric obstacles in
dynamical systems coming from classical mechanics, and, in particular, to generate energy transfer.
We consider a
2
-degrees of freedom, uncoupled pendulum-rotator system, which is described by an
integrable Hamiltonian. The energies of the rotator and of the pendulum are first integrals of the system.
Hence, there are no trajectories that cross the level sets of the energies, so these are geometric obstacles for the dynamics.
We add a small, random perturbation to the system; we assume that this perturbation is of a special type.
More precisely, the perturbation is given by a Hamiltonian vector field multiplied by a scalar, continuous, stationary Gaussian process with Hölder continuous paths. Additionally, the Hamiltonian vector field is assumed to vanish at the hyperbolic fixed point of the pendulum.
The energies of the rotator and of the pendulum are no longer conserved. We show that, in particular, the energy of the rotator can change by an amount proportional to the size of the perturbation.

Our treatment of the underlying random dynamics is path-wise, in the sense that we derive results for fixed realizations of the stochastic process. Each such realization is given by an unbounded, continuous curve. Nevertheless, we cannot reduce the problem to the case of a non-autonomous perturbation by regarding each realization as a time-dependent parameter, since we need to use the ergodicity of the process and make use of the Birkhoff ergodic theorem.

Considering the effect of random perturbations of mechanical system is very natural in applications.
There are inherently many sources of noise that affect mechanical systems, or, more generally engineering systems.
Some concrete examples can be found in, e.g.,
[
SHS07
,
Bel08
,
CH17
]
.
One particular application that we plan to study in the future concerns piezoelectric energy harvesting devices, where one wants to exploit external vibrations
to generate electrical output; see
[
EHI09
,
Gra17
,
AGMS23
]
. Many of the existing models assume that the external vibrations are periodic, but it would be more realistic to consider noisy perturbations. Such systems also include dissipation effects, yielding random attractors
[
Wan12
]
and stochastic resonance
[
CLRS17
]
.
For applications, the path-wise approach is suitable when we want to analyze the output of a single experiment at a time, rather than study the statistics of multiple experiments.

Our approach is based on geometric methods. The unperturbed system possesses a normally hyperbolic invariant manifold (NHIM) whose stable and unstable manifolds coincide. To understand the effect of the time-dependent perturbation, we work in the extended space, where time is viewed as an additional coordinate.
We show that for a distinguished set of times, there is a
random normally hyperbolic invariant manifold
(RNHIM) and corresponding stable and unstable manifolds that survive the perturbation.
The main difference from the standard normal hyperbolicity theory is that our normally hyperbolic invariant manifold is time-dependent, and, moreover
we cannot guarantee its existence for all times, but only for a certain set of times.
These manifolds are reminiscent of Pesin sets in non-uniform hyperbolicity theory
[
BP07
]
.
The reason for why the RNHIM’s may break up is that the underlying stochastic process is unbounded, and large spikes in the noise can destroy the relations among the hyperbolic rates that are needed for normal hyperbolicity.

Results on the persistence of stable and unstable manifolds of hyperbolic fixed points under random perturbations have been obtained in
[
LW10
,
LW11
,
Yag18
]
.
To obtain a similar result for normally hyperbolic invariant manifolds, we apply some general results from
[
LLB13
,
LLB14
]
; see also
[
LLB15
]
. One difficulty in applying these results to our case is that they assume that the perturbed flow is close to the unperturbed flow at all times.
This is not the case when the noise driving the perturbation is unbounded. To deal with unbounded noise, we modify the system by multiplying the Hamiltonian vector field that appears in the perturbation by a
random bump function
defined in the extended space. This bump function depends on the noise parameter, and has the effect of cutting off the noise when it spikes too much. This makes the modified flow stay close to the unperturbed flow, so we can apply the general theory. For the aforementioned distinguished set of times (where time is viewed as an additional coordinate), we show that the modified flow coincides with the original flow. This is how we obtain the persistence of the NHIM and of its stable and unstable manifolds for the distinguished set of times.

We also show that, for the distinguished set of times, the stable and unstable manifolds intersect transversally provided that certain non-degeneracy conditions are satisfied. The transverse intersections of the manifolds correspond to non-degenerate zeroes of a certain
Melnikov process
.
A key ingredient to show the existence of zeroes for the Melnikov process is
Rice’s formula
, concerning the number of times the process crosses a predetermined level. Melnikov theory for random perturbations has been developed in
[
LW10
,
LW10
]
, and the idea of using Rice’s formula to obtain intersections of the invariant manifolds comes from
[
Yag18
]
.

Once the existence of transverse intersections of the stable and unstable manifolds is established, the dynamics along the corresponding homoclinic orbits can be described via a
random scattering map
. This is an analogue of the (deterministic) scattering map developed in
[
DdlLS08
]
, and its version for time-dependent system developed in
[
BdlL11
]
. This is a map defined on the RNHIM, and gives the future asymptotic of a homoclinic orbit as a function of its the past asymptotic. We show that the random scattering map changes the energy of the rotator by an amount proportional to the size of the perturbation, provided that some non-degeneracy conditions are satisfied.
These non-degeneracy conditions rely again on Rice’s formula.

The change in energy owed to the small perturbation is reminiscent of the
Arnold diffusion problem
for Hamiltonian systems
[
Arn64
]
. Arnold conjectured that integrable Hamiltonian systems of general type, of more than two degrees of freedom, subjected to small, Hamiltonian perturbations of generic type have trajectories along which the energy changes by an amount that is independent of the perturbation parameter. A survey on some recent results can be found in
[
GdlLS20
]
.
Much of the existing work considers deterministic perturbations.
Diffusion in randomly perturbed integrable Hamiltonian systems has been studied in
[
BST94
,
BB98
]
,
where they derive the Fokker-Planck equation for the distribution function of the action angle-variables.
A model for diffusion for random compositions of cylinder maps was considered in
[
CGK17
]
.
Another paper of related interest is
[
DDG23
]
.

The upshot of our work is that, we can extend the geometric methods for Arnold diffusion developed in
[
DdlLS00
,
DdlLS00
,
DdlLS06
,
GT17
,
GdlLS20
]
to the case in which the perturbations are random (rather than deterministic).
The main difficulty in applying the geometric method is that the perturbation is driven by unbounded noise, so the spikes in the noise may destroy the geometric structures.
Our current results yield only a small change in energy, of the order of the perturbation (that is, we obtain micro-diffusion – a term coined in
[
BK16
]
), rather than of order one.
The identification of orbits with diffusion of order one is the object of future investigation.

2.
Set-up

2.1.
Unperturbed system

The unperturbed system is a rotator-pendulum system described by an autonomous Hamiltonian
H_{0}
of the form:

(2.1)

\begin{split}H_{0}(I,\phi,p,q)&=h_{0}(I)+h_{1}(p,q)\\
&=h_{0}(I)+\left(\frac{1}{2}p^{2}+V(q)\right),\end{split}

with
z=(I,\phi,p,q)
in
M:=\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{T}
.

The phase space
M
is endowed with the symplectic form

dI\wedge d\phi+dp\wedge dq.

We denote by

\nu(I):=\frac{\partial h_{0}}{\partial I}(I)

the frequency of the rotator.

We assume the following:

(P-i)

The potential
V
is periodic of period
1
in
q
;

(P-ii)

The potential
V
has a non-degenerate local maximum, which, without loss of generality,
we set at
0
; that is,
V^{\prime}(0)=0
and
V^{\prime\prime}(0)<0
. We additionally assume that
q=0
is non-degenerate in the sense of Morse, i.e.,
0
is the only critical point in the level set
\{V(q)=V(0)\}
.

Condition (P-ii) implies that the
pendulum has a homoclinic orbit to
(0,0)
, the hyperbolic fixed point of the pendulum. We consider that the homoclinic orbit is parametrized by
(p_{0}(t),q_{0}(t))
for
t\in\mathbb{R}
, where
(p_{0}(t),q_{0}(t))\to(0,0)
as
t\to\pm\infty
.

The Hamilton equation associated to (
2.1
) is

(2.2)

\dot{z}=X^{0}(z)=J\nabla H_{0}(z),

where
J
is the symplectic matrix

J=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}J_{2}&0\\
0&J_{2}\\
\end{array}\right)\textrm{ with }J_{2}=\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}0&-1\\
1&0\\
\end{array}\right).

We denote by
\Phi^{t}_{0}
the flow of (
2.2
).

Since for
H_{0}
the pendulum and the rotator are decoupled, the action variable
I
is preserved along the trajectories of (
2.2
).
Similarly, the energy
P=\left(\frac{1}{2}p^{2}+V(q)\right)
of the pendulum is a conserved quantity.

In the sequel, we will show that if we add a small, random perturbation to the pendulum-rotator system, there are trajectories of the perturbed system along which
I
changes over time. If
\nu(I)=\frac{\partial h_{0}}{\partial I}(I)\neq 0
for all
I
within some range, the fact that
I
changes along a trajectory implies that the energy of the rotator
h_{0}(I)
also changes along that trajectory.

2.2.
Perturbed system

To the system (
2.2
) we add a random perturbation, so that the perturbed system is of the form

(2.3)

\begin{split}\dot{z}=&X_{\varepsilon}(z,\eta(t))=X^{0}(z)+\varepsilon X^{1}(z,\eta(t))\\
=&J\nabla H_{0}(z)+\varepsilon J\nabla H_{1}(z)\eta(t)\end{split}

for
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}
, where
\eta(t)
is a scalar, continuous, stationary Gaussian process satisfying the properties (
R1
), (
R2
), (
R3
) below.

In the above, we assume that
H_{1}(z)
is a Hamiltonian function, uniformly
\mathcal{C}^{2}
in
z
, satisfying the following condition

(H1)

\begin{split}H_{1}(I,\phi,0,0)&=0,\,DH_{1}(I,\phi,0,0)=0.\end{split}

The perturbation is chosen so does not affect the
inner dynamics, given by the restriction to the phase space of
the rotator. The dynamics of the rotator is integrable, hence
I

is preserved by the inner dynamics.

The level sets of
I
(which are invariant circles) constitute geometric obstacles for the inner dynamics. We will show that we can use the outer dynamics, along the homoclinic orbits of the pendulum, to overcome these geometric obstacles.

The system (
2.3
) is non-autonomous.
We denote by
\Phi^{t_{0},t}_{\varepsilon}(\omega)
the corresponding flow, which depends on the initial time
t_{0}
and on the realization
\omega
of the stochastic process
\eta(t)
. For every fixed realization
\omega
of
\eta(t)
we have a sample path given by
\eta(t)(\omega)=\omega(t)
. See Section
4.2
.

Remark 2.1
.

It may be possible to remove condition (
H1
).
In fact, we will not use this condition for two of the main results (Theorem
3.1
and Theorem
3.2
.
However, without (
H1
), the inner dynamics will be affected by the random perturbation, and the resulting inner dynamics may overcome on its own the geometric obstacles.

2.3.
Noise

The time-dependent function
\eta(t)
is a scalar stationary Gaussian process with mean
0
, i.e.,

(R1)

E[\eta(t)]=0.

Stationarity means that for any
n
,
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}
, and
h>0
, the random vectors

(\eta(t_{1}),\ldots,\eta(t_{n}))\textrm{ and }\textrm{(}\eta(t_{1}+h),\ldots,\eta(t_{n}+h))

have the same (Gaussian) distribution.

The autocorrelation
function
r(h)
is

(2.4)

r(h):=E[\eta(t)\eta(t+h)].

By stationarity, the right-hand side of (
2.4
) does
not depend on
t
.

We assume that the autocorrelation function satisfies the following conditions

(R2)

r(h)\textrm{ is continuous and absolutely integrable on }\mathbb{R},

and

(R3)

\begin{split}r(h)=&1-C|h|^{a}+o(|h|^{a})\textrm{ as $h\to 0$,}\\
\textrm{for some $C$ with }&\textrm{$0<C<\infty$, and some $a$ with $1<a\leq 2$}.\end{split}

We use the notation
f(x)=o(g(x))
to signify
\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=0
.

For a Gaussian process, if
r(h)
satisfies (
R3
) for
0<a\leq 2
, then the sample paths
\omega(t)
are continuous, and if
1<a\leq 2
,
then the sample paths
\omega(t)
are
\alpha
-Hölder continuous for any
0<\alpha<\frac{a-1}{2}
. See
[
Bel61
,
Lin12
]
.
That is,
there exists
C_{H}>0
, independent of
\omega
, such that

(2.5)

|\omega(t_{1})-\omega(t_{2})|<C_{H}|t_{1}-t_{2}|^{\alpha}\textrm{ for all }t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{R}.

Intuitively, the above conditions say that the lesser the loss of memory of the process is, the more regular the sample paths of the process are.

To summarize, by assumption (
R3
), the sample paths of
\eta(t)
are continuous and
\alpha
-Hölder continuous with probability
1
.

Condition (
R3
) yields
r(0)=1
, and therefore

(2.6)

E[\eta(t)^{2}]=1,

which means that the Gaussian process has variance equal to
1
.

Also, (
R3
) implies, via the Maruyama Theorem
[
Mar49
]
, that
\eta(t)
is ergodic

(2.7)

\lim_{T\to\pm\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\phi(\eta(t))dt=E[\phi(\eta(t))],\,\forall\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\textrm{ measurable function}.

Remark 2.2
.

Gaussian stationary processes that are not continuous are necessarily very irregular.
More precisely, one of the following alternatives holds: either with probability one the sample paths
\omega(t)
are continuous, or with probability one they are unbounded on every finite interval
[
Bel61
]
.
Hence, considering Gaussian processes with continuous sample paths as in (
R3
) is a reasonable assumption.

Theorem 3.3
.

Assume that the system (
2.3
) satisfies (P-i), (P-ii), (R-i), (R-ii), (R-iii) and (H1).
Also assume the condition (

) on the spectral moments of

, for every

4.
Background

4.1.
Random dynamical system

We consider a probability space
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P})
, where
\Omega
is the sample space of
outcomes,
\mathscr{F}
is the
\sigma
-algebra of events, and
\mathbb{P}
is a probability measure
that assigns probabilities to the events in
\mathscr{F}
.

A stochastic process
\eta
is a mapping
t\in{\mathbb{R}}\mapsto\eta(t):\Omega\to R
, where
each
\eta(t)
is a random variable, i.e., a measurable function from
\Omega
to
{\mathbb{R}}
.
For a fixed realization
\omega\in\Omega
, the function
\omega(t):=\eta(t)(\omega)
is referred to as a sample path.
(We note that, in this paper, under (
R3
), we assume that a.e. sample path is Hölder continuous.)

On
\Omega
we consider the
\mathbb{P}
-preserving measurable flow
\theta^{t}:\Omega\to\Omega
, given by

(4.1)

\theta^{t}\omega(s)=\omega(t+s).

It satisfies the following conditions for all
\omega\in\Omega
:

(i)

\theta^{0}\omega=\omega
,

(ii)

\theta^{t_{1}+t_{2}}\omega=\theta^{t_{1}}(\theta^{t_{2}}\omega)
, for
t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
,

for each
\omega\in\Omega
.
The dynamical system
(\Omega,\theta^{t})
is referred to as a
metric dynamical system
.

Let
(M,\mathscr{B})
be a measurable space.
A measurable mapping

\Phi:\mathbb{R}\times\Omega\times M\to M

is a
random dynamical system (RDS) over
\theta
, if it satisfies the following cocycle conditions for all
\omega\in\Omega
:

(i)

\Phi(0,\omega)(z)=(z)
, for all
z\in M
,

(ii)

\Phi(t_{1}+t_{2},\omega)(z)=\Phi(t_{1},\theta^{t_{2}}\omega)(\Phi(t_{2},\omega)(z))
, for all
z\in M
,
t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
.

We will often write
\Phi(t,\omega)=\Phi^{t}(\omega)
.

4.2.
Random differential equations

Let
M
be a smooth manifold and
X:M\times\Omega\to TM
,
(z,\omega)\in M\times\Omega\mapsto X(z,\omega)\in T_{z}M
, be a random vector field that is
\mathcal{C}^{r}
,
r\geq 1
, in the
z
-component, and measurable in the
\omega
-component.

A solution (in the sense of Carathéodory) of a system

(4.2)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\dot{z}&=X(z,\theta^{t}\omega)\\
z(t_{0})&=z_{0}\end{array}\right.

is a function
z(t;z_{0},t_{0},\omega):M\to M
satisfying

(4.3)

z(t;z_{0},t_{0},\omega)=z_{0}+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}X\left(z(s;z_{0},t_{0},\omega),\theta^{s}\omega\right)ds,

where we fix a realization
\omega
of the process
\eta(t)
.
In the above notation
\omega
refers to the choice of realization at time
t=0
.

If for all
\omega\in\Omega
,
X_{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{C}^{r}
,
r\geq 1
, then (
2.3
) yields a unique solution
t\mapsto z(t;z_{0},t_{0},\omega)
which is
\mathcal{C}^{r}
in
z_{0}
.
See
[
Arn98
,
Dua15
]
.

If we denote
z(t)=z(t;z_{0},t_{0},\omega)
and
\bar{z}(t)=z(t_{0}+t)
, and make a change of variable
s\mapsto t_{0}+s^{\prime}
in (
4.3
),
we have

(4.4)

\begin{split}z(t)=&z_{0}+\int_{0}^{t-t_{0}}X\left(z(t_{0}+s^{\prime}),\theta^{t_{0}+s^{\prime}}\omega\right)ds^{\prime}\\
=&z_{0}+\int_{0}^{t-t_{0}}X\left(\bar{z}(s^{\prime}),\theta^{s^{\prime}}(\theta^{t_{0}}\omega)\right)ds^{\prime}.\end{split}

Noting that
\bar{z}(0)=z(t_{0})=z_{0}
, we see that the right hand side of (
4.4
) is the solution of

(4.5)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\dot{\bar{z}}&=X(\bar{z},\theta^{t}(\theta^{t_{0}}\omega))\\
\bar{z}(0)&=z_{0}\end{array}\right.

evaluated at time
t-t_{0}
, therefore, it coincides with
z(t-t_{0};z_{0},0,\theta^{t_{0}}\omega)
.

We obtained the following invariance relation:

(4.6)

z(t;z_{0},t_{0},\omega)=z(t-t_{0};z_{0},0,\theta^{t_{0}}\omega).

That is, the solution only depends on the elapsed time
t-t_{0}
and on the random parameter
\theta^{t_{0}}\omega
at time
t_{0}
.

Therefore,

(4.7)

\Phi(t,\omega)(z_{0})=z(t;z_{0},0,\omega)

determines a
random dynamical system (RDS) over
\theta^{t}
.

From (
4.6
) and (
4.7
) we have that, for any
t_{0}
,

(4.8)

\Phi(t-t_{0},\theta^{t_{0}}\omega)(z_{0})=z(t-t_{0};z_{0},0,\theta^{t_{0}}\omega)=z(t;z_{0},t_{0},\omega).

In the case when the sample paths
\omega(t)
are continuous, for each realization
\omega
the equation (
4.5
) is a classical non-autonomous differential equation,
and its solutions (
4.3
) are in classical sense.

4.3.
Normally hyperbolic invariant manifolds for random dynamical systems

In the sequel we follow
[
LLB13
,
LLB14
]
.

Let
\Phi(t,\omega)
be a random dynamical system.

A random set is a mapping

\omega\in\Omega\mapsto\mathcal{M}(\omega)\subseteq M

assigning to each path
\omega\in\Omega
a closed subset
\mathcal{M}(\omega)\subseteq M
,
such that

\omega\to\inf_{y\in\mathcal{M}(\omega)}\|y-x\|\textrm{ is measurable for each }x\in M.

A random invariant manifold is a random manifold
\mathcal{M}(\omega)\subseteq M
such that

\Phi(t,\omega)(\mathcal{M}(\omega))=\mathcal{M}(\theta^{t}\omega),\textrm{ for all }t\in\mathbb{R},\omega\in\Omega.

A random variable
C(t)
is said to be
tempered
if

(4.9)

\lim_{t\to\pm\infty}\frac{\log C(\theta^{t}\omega)}{t}=0\textrm{ for a.e. }\omega\in\Omega.

Definition 4.1
.

A random invariant manifold
\Lambda(\omega)
is normally hyperbolic
if for a.e.
\omega\in\Omega
and all
x\in\Lambda(\omega)
there exists an invariant splitting of
T_{x}M
, which is
\mathcal{C}^{0}
in
x
and measurable in
\omega
,

T_{x}M=T_{x}\Lambda(\omega)\oplus E^{{\rm u}}_{x}(\omega)\oplus E^{{\rm s}}_{x}(\omega),

whose bundles are invariant in the sense

\begin{split}D_{x}\Phi(t,\omega)(T_{x}\Lambda(\omega))=&T_{\Phi(t,\omega)(x)}\Lambda(\theta^{t}\omega),\\
D_{x}\Phi(t,\omega)(E^{{\rm u}}_{x}(\omega))=&E^{{\rm u}}_{\Phi(t,\omega)(x)}(\theta^{t}\omega),\\
D_{x}\Phi(t,\omega)(E^{{\rm s}}_{x}(\omega))=&E^{{\rm s}}_{\Phi(t,\omega)(x)}(\theta^{t}\omega),\end{split}

and there exist a tempered random variable
C(x,\omega)>0
and
(\theta,\Phi)
-invariant random variables (rates)

0<\alpha(x,\omega)<\beta(x,\omega)

such that for all
x\in\Lambda(\omega)
we have

(4.10)

\begin{split}v\in E^{{\rm s}}_{x}(\omega)\Rightarrow\|D_{x}\Phi(t,\omega)v\|<&C(x,\omega)e^{-\beta(x,\omega)t}\|v\|,\textrm{ for }t\geq 0,\\
v\in E^{{\rm u}}_{x}(\omega)\Rightarrow\|D_{x}\Phi(t,\omega)v\|<&C(x,\omega)e^{\beta(x,\omega)t}\|v\|,\textrm{ for }t\leq 0,\\
v\in T_{x}\Lambda(\omega)\Rightarrow\|D_{x}\Phi(t,\omega)v\|<&C(x,\omega)e^{\alpha(x,\omega)|t|}\|v\|,\textrm{ for all }t.\end{split}

When the objects in Definition
4.1
do not depend on the random parameter
\omega
, we obtain the classical definition of a NHIM as in
[
HPS77
]
; a brief summary of the normal hyperbolicity theory can be found in
[
DdlLS00
]
.

We will use the following results from
[
LLB13
,
LLB14
]
on the persistence of the NHIM under random perturbations in Section
7.1
.

Theorem 4.2

(Persistence of NHIM)
.

Assume that
\Phi_{0}(t)
is a (deterministic)
\mathcal{C}^{r}
flow on

,

. Assume that
\Phi_{0}(t)
has a compact, connected

. Let the positive exponents
related to the normal hyperbolicity of

be

, which are
constant and deterministic.

Then there exists
\bar{\varepsilon}>0
such that for any random

\Phi(t,\omega)
on

which is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
in

, if

\|\Phi(t,\omega)-\Phi_{0}(t)\|_{\mathcal{C}^{1}}<\bar{\varepsilon},\textrm{ for }t\in[0,1],\,\omega\in\Omega

(i)

Persistence:

\Phi(t,\omega)
has a normally hyperbolic random invariant manifold

which is
\mathcal{C}^{1}

(ii)

\omega
.

Condition (

) implies that the perturbed flow and the unperturbed flow stay

-close for all time, since we can

.
Also note that we can replace the time domain

The perturbed NHIM, and its stable and unstable manifolds, can be described as graphs over the unperturbed ones, respectively.

Sub-linearity of the noise

We assume that the stochastic process

satisfies (

), (

), (

Lemma 5.1
.

For almost every realization
\omega\in\Omega
of

(5.1)

\lim_{s\to+\infty}\frac{|\omega(s)|}{s}=0,

that is,
\omega(s)=o(s)
.
Therefore, there exist
A_{\omega}>0
depending on
\omega
and
B>0
that can be chosen independent of

, such that

(5.2)

|\omega(s)|\leq A_{\omega}+Bs\textrm{ for all }s\geq 0.

See Fig.

Proof.

Since

is a continuous stationary Gaussian process with

, it follows from
[

, Theorem 1.4]
that for every realization

we have

\limsup_{s\to+\infty}\frac{|\omega(s)|}{\sqrt{2\log(s)}}\leq 1.

This implies that

\lim_{s\to+\infty}\frac{|\omega(s)|}{s}=0.

Since for almost every
\omega\in\Omega
,
\omega
is continuous in
s
, then for any given
B>0
there exists
T_{\omega}>0
such that
\frac{|\omega(s)|}{s}<B
for all
s\geq T_{\omega}
.
Let
A_{\omega}>\sup\{|\omega(s)|\,|\,s\in[0,T_{\omega}]\}
. Then
|\omega(s)|\leq A_{\omega}+Bs
for all
s\geq 0
.
Note that
A_{\omega}
depends on
\omega
, while
B
does not; moreover,
B>0
can be chosen arbitrarily small.
∎

The meaning of Lemma

is that the graph of
|\omega(s)|
for

is below the line

, where

is a fixed slope independent of the path

, and

is the vertical intercept of the line and depends on the path. The slope

can be chosen arbitrarily close to

We define some relevant sets and describe relations among them.

A consequence of Lemma

is that for a.e.
\omega\in\Omega
, there exist

and

|\omega(s)|\leq A_{\omega}+B|s|\textrm{ for all }s\in\mathbb{R}.

Without any loss of generality, by disregarding a measure zero set of paths, we can assume that this property is true for all
\omega\in\Omega
.

For any
A>0
fixed, define the set:

(5.4)

\Omega_{A}=\{\omega\in\Omega\,|\,|\omega(s)|\leq A+B|s|,\forall s\in\mathbb{R}\}.

Since

A_{1}\leq A_{2}\implies\Omega_{A_{1}}\subseteq\Omega_{A_{2}}.

Since

, we have that
\lim_{A\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{A})=\mathbb{P}(\Omega)=1

\forall\delta>0,\,\exists A_{\delta}>0\textrm{ s.t. }\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{A})>1-\delta.

Note that if

On the other hand, given any

\theta^{t_{n}}\omega\in\Omega_{A},\textrm{ for all }n\in\mathbb{Z}.

See

, and
A_{\delta}

Lemma 5.2

For any

.

Using the ergodicity and stationarity of

A similar result holds when we take the limit as

5.2.
Construction of a random bump function

For each
t\in\mathbb{R}
, and
\omega\in\Omega
and
A>0
we define the following sets

(5.11)

C_{A}(\omega)=\{s\in\mathbb{R}\,|\,|\omega(s)|\leq A+B|s|\}

which is a closed set in
\mathbb{R}
, and for
\rho>0
small,

(5.12)

U_{A,\rho}(\omega)=\bigcup_{s\in C_{A}(\omega)}(s-\rho,s+\rho)

which is a
\rho
-neighborhood of
C_{A}(\omega)
.

Let

(5.13)

F_{A,\rho}(\omega)=\mathbb{R}\setminus U_{A,\rho}(\omega)

which is a closed set in
\mathbb{R}
. See Fig.
4
.

Figure 4.
Sets involved in the construction of a random bump function.

It is clear that:

(5.14)

C_{A}(\omega)\subseteq U_{A,\rho}(\omega)\textrm{ and }d_{H}(C_{A}(\omega),F_{A,\rho}(\omega))=\rho,

where
d_{H}
refers to the Hausdorff distance.

If
s^{\prime}\in\textrm{Cl}(U_{A,\rho}(\omega))
, then there exists
s\in C_{A}(\omega)
such that
|s-s^{\prime}|\leq\rho
, therefore,
by the Hölder property (
2.5
) of
\omega(t)
, we have

(5.15)

|\omega(s)-\omega(s^{\prime})|\leq C_{H}|s-s^{\prime}|^{\alpha}\leq C_{H}\rho^{\alpha},

hence

(5.16)

\begin{split}|\omega(s^{\prime})|\leq&|\omega(s^{\prime})-\omega(s)|+|\omega(s)|\leq C_{H}\cdot\rho^{\alpha}+A+B|s|\\
\leq&A^{\prime}_{\rho}+B|s|,\end{split}

where
A^{\prime}_{\rho}:=C_{H}\cdot\rho^{\alpha}+A
.

Note that for
t\in\mathbb{R}
we obviously have

(5.17)

\begin{split}C_{A}(\theta^{t}\omega)=&\{s\in\mathbb{R}\,|\,|\theta^{t}\omega(s)|\leq A+B|s|\}\\
=&\{s\in\mathbb{R}\,|\,|\omega(t+s)|\leq A+Bs\},\\
\textrm{Cl}(U_{A,\rho}(\omega))\subseteq&\{s\in\mathbb{R}\,|\,|\theta^{t}\omega(s)|\leq A^{\prime}_{\rho}+B|s|\}\\
\subseteq&\{s\in\mathbb{R}\,|\,|\omega(t+s)|\leq A^{\prime}_{\rho}+B|s|\}.\end{split}

We construct
random bump function

\psi_{A}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\psi_{A}(s,\omega)
being a measurable function in
\omega
for each
s
fixed, and a
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
function in
s
for each
\omega
fixed, such that

(5.18)

\begin{split}\psi_{A,\rho}(\cdot,\omega):\mathbb{R}\to[0,1],\\
\psi_{A,\rho}(s,\omega)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&\hbox{for $s\in C_{A}(\omega)$,}\\
0,&\hbox{for $s\in F_{A,\rho}(\omega)$,}\end{array}\right.\\
\|D_{s}\psi_{A,\rho}(s,\omega)\|_{C^{0}}\leq\frac{1}{\rho},\end{split}

where
D_{s}
denotes the derivative with respect to
s
.

The latter condition comes from the fact that a slope of a line that changes from
0
to
1
within an interval of at least
\rho
is at most

\frac{1}{\rho}
. Therefore the upper bound on the first derivative of a bump function that is equal to
1
on
C_{A}(\omega)
and is supported on the closure of the
\rho
-neighborhood
U_{A,\omega}
of
C_{A}(\omega)
is at most
\frac{1}{\rho}
. Recipes to construct such bump functions can be found in
[
Nes03
]
.

Therefore,

(5.19)

\begin{split}\psi_{A,\rho}(s,\omega)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&\hbox{if $|\omega(s)|\leq A+B|s|$,}\\
0,&\hbox{if $|\omega(s)|\geq A^{\prime}_{\rho}+B|s|$,}\end{array}\right.\end{split}

Consequently, for
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

(5.20)

\begin{split}\psi_{A,\rho}(s,\theta^{t}\omega)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&\hbox{if $|\omega(t+s)|\leq A+B|s|$,}\\
0,&\hbox{if $|\omega(t+s)|\geq A^{\prime}_{\rho}+B|s|$.}\end{array}\right.\end{split}

Note that if
t\in Q_{A}(\omega)
then
|\omega(t+s)|\leq A+B|s|
for all
s
, hence
\psi_{A,\rho}(\cdot,\theta^{t}\omega)\equiv 1
.

6.
Geometric structures of the unperturbed system

6.1.
Coordinate system for the unperturbed rotator-pendulum system

We introduce a new coordinates system defined in a neighborhood of the homoclinic orbit
(p_{0}(t),q_{0}(t))
of the pendulum
in the
(p,q)
-phase space, as we describe below.

Choose some fixed reference value
q_{*}\neq 0
of the position coordinate
q
of the pendulum.
The first coordinate of a point
(p,q)
is
the pendulum energy level
P(p,q)=\frac{1}{2}p^{2}+V(q)
on which the point lies.
The second coordinate represents the time it takes for the solution
(p(\tau),q(\tau))
to go from
q_{*}
to
(p,q)
along the energy level
P
corresponding to
(p,q)
. Note that for a given initial position
q_{*}
the corresponding initial momentum
p_{*}
is uniquely determined, up to sign, by the energy condition
P(p_{*},q_{*})=P(p,q)
.
In order for the coordinate change
(p,q)\to(P,\tau)
to be well defined, we restrict to a neighborhood
\mathcal{N}
of the homoclinic orbit
\{p_{0}(t),q_{0}(t)\}
that does not contain any critical point of
P
, of the form

\mathcal{N}=\{(p,q)\,|\,P_{1}<P<P_{2},q_{1}<q<q_{2}\}

for some
P_{1}<0<P_{2}
and
0<q_{1}<q_{*}<q_{2}<1
.

The coordinate change
(p,q)\mapsto(P,\tau)
is canonical, i.e.,
dp\wedge dq=dP\wedge d\tau
for
(p,q)\in\mathcal{N}
; see
[
GdlL18
]
, also
[
GdlLM21
]
.
While the above coordinate change is only defined on
\mathcal{N}
, the energy of the pendulum
P
as a function of
(p,q)
is well defined at all points
(p,q)
.

For the rotator-pendulum system we have the canonical coordinates
(I,\phi,P,\tau)
for
(p,q)\in\mathcal{N}
.
In these coordinates the Hamiltonian
H_{0}
is given by

(6.1)

H_{0}(I,\phi,P,\tau)=h_{0}(I)+P.

In Section
6.3
below, we will consider the extended phase space, by considering time
\tilde{t}
as an additional phase-space coordinate.
In this case we have the system of coordinates
(I,\phi,P,\tau,\tilde{t})
for
(p,q)\in\mathcal{N}
.

6.2.
Normally hyperbolic invariant manifold for the unperturbed rotator-pendulum system

Consider the unperturbed rotator-pendulum system given by
H_{0}
.

The point
(0,0)
is a hyperbolic fixed point for the pendulum, the characteristic
exponents are
\beta=(-V^{\prime\prime}(0))^{1/2}>0
,

,
and the corresponding unstable/stable eigenspaces are

,

Also, define

(6.2)

\begin{split}E^{\rm u}_{z}=&\{z\}\times\textrm{Span}(v^{\rm u}),\\

It immediately follows that for each closed interval
[a,b]\subseteq\mathbb{R}

and

at

), respectively, and

for

,

for

,
and

for

The stable and unstable manifolds of

are denoted by

and

,
respectively. They are

-dimensional manifolds, and

Relative to the

[

7.1.

The result below will be used to prove Theorem

The second part of the result says that for the distinguished set of times, the modified perturbed flow coincides with the original perturbed flow.

.

To simplify notation, we substitute

We write the solution

of the unperturbed system and the solution

of

(7.11)

\begin{split}z_{0}(t)=&z_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}J\nabla H_{0}(z_{0}(s))ds\\

By subtraction we obtain

\begin{split}\|\hat{z}_{\varepsilon}(t)-z_{0}(t)\|\leq&\int_{0}^{t}|J\nabla H_{0}(\hat{z}_{\varepsilon}(s))-J\nabla H_{0}(z_{0}(s))|ds\\

Restricting
z
to some suitable, compact domain, we let

be the Lipschitz constant for

,
and
K_{2}
be the supremum of

From (

Hence (

\begin{split}\|\hat{z}_{\varepsilon}(t)-z_{0}(t)\|\leq&K_{1}\int_{0}^{t}\|\hat{z}_{\varepsilon}(s)-z_{0}(s)\|ds+\varepsilon K_{2}\int_{0}^{t}(A^{\prime}_{\delta,\rho}+Bs)ds\\

A.4

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon^{k^{\prime}}\ln(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})=0\textrm{ and }\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon^{k^{\prime}}\left(\ln(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})\right)^{2}=0.

We have only showed that

-close. Now we make a similar argument to show that

\tilde{z}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon}\in W^{\rm s}(\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(\theta^{t_{0}}\omega))
given by
P^{\rm s}(\tilde{z}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon})=P^{\rm s}(I,\phi,\tau,t_{0})
, and a point

\tilde{z}^{\rm u}_{\varepsilon}\in W^{\rm u}(\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(\theta^{t_{0}}\omega))
given by
P^{\rm u}(\tilde{z}^{\rm u}_{\varepsilon})=P^{\rm u}(I,\phi,\tau,t_{0})
, for the same coordinates
(I,\phi,\tau,t_{0})
.

Then

(8.1)

\begin{split}P&(\tilde{z}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon})-P(\tilde{z}^{\rm u}_{\varepsilon})\\
&=-\varepsilon\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\{P,H_{1}\}(I,\phi+\nu(I)s,p_{0}(\tau+s),q_{0}(\tau+s),t_{0}+s)\omega(t_{0}+s)\,ds\\
&\quad+O(\varepsilon^{1+\rho})\end{split}

for some
\rho\in(0,1)
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\tilde{z}_{0}=(I,\phi,p_{0}(\tau),q_{0}(\tau),t_{0})
is a homoclinic point for
\tilde{\Phi}^{t}_{0}
.
Then the stable and unstable foot-points are both given by
\tilde{z}_{0}^{\pm}=(I,\phi,0,0,t_{0})
, as
the stable foot-point and the unstable foot-point coincide in the unperturbed case.
Hence, in (
8.1
),
\left(I,\phi+\nu(I)s,p_{0}(\tau+s),q_{0}(\tau+t),t_{0}+s\right)

represents the effect of the unperturbed flow
{\tilde{\Phi}}^{s}_{0}
on the homoclinic point
\tilde{z}_{0}
, and

\left(I,\phi+\nu(I)s,0,0,t_{0}+s\right)
represents the effect of the unperturbed flow
{\tilde{\Phi}}^{s}_{0}
on the foot-point
\tilde{z}_{0}^{\pm}
.

Since
d(\tilde{\Phi}^{s}(\tilde{z}_{0}),\tilde{\Phi}^{s}(\tilde{z}^{\pm}_{0})\to 0
exponentially fast as
s\to\pm\infty
,

\begin{split}\{P,H_{1}\}&\left(I,\phi+\nu(I)s,p(\tau+t),q(\tau+s),t_{0}+s\right)\\
&\,-\{P,H_{1}\}\left(I,\phi+\nu(I)s,0,0,t_{0}+s\right)\to 0\end{split}

exponentially fast as
t\to\infty
.
Note that
\{P,H_{1}\}=V^{\prime}(q)\frac{\partial H_{1}}{\partial p}-p\frac{\partial H_{1}}{\partial q}
vanishes at
p=q=0
.
Since
t_{0}\in Q_{A_{\delta},T_{\delta}}
,
|\omega(t_{0}+s)|<A_{\delta}+B|s|
for all
s
, so we obtain

\{P,H_{1}\}\left(I,\phi+\nu(I)t,p(\tau+s),q(\tau+s)\right)\omega(t_{0}+s)\to 0

exponentially fast as
s\to\infty
.
Thus, the improper integral in (
8.1
) is absolutely convergent.

For
\tilde{z}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon}\in W^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon}(\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(\theta^{t_{0}}\omega))
let
\Omega^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon}({\tilde{z}}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon})
be the foot-point of the unique stable fiber of
W^{\rm s}(\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(\theta^{t_{0}}\omega))
through
{\tilde{z}}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon}
.
Using the fundamental theorem of calculus and

(8.2)

\begin{split}P({\tilde{z}}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon})-&P({\Omega}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon}({\tilde{z}}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon}))=P({\tilde{\Phi}}^{T}_{\varepsilon}({\tilde{z}}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon}))-P({\tilde{\Phi}}^{T}_{\varepsilon}{\Omega}_{\varepsilon}^{\rm s}({\tilde{z}}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon}))\\
&-\int_{0}^{T}\frac{d}{ds}\big[P({\tilde{\Phi}}^{s}_{\varepsilon}({\tilde{z}}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon}))-P({\tilde{\Phi}}^{s}_{\varepsilon}{\Omega}_{\varepsilon}^{\rm s}({\tilde{z}}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon}))\big]\,ds\\
=&P({\tilde{\Phi}}^{T}_{\varepsilon}({\tilde{z}}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon}))-P({\tilde{\Phi}}^{T}_{\varepsilon}{\Omega}_{\varepsilon}^{\rm s}({\tilde{z}}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon}))\\
&-\varepsilon\int_{0}^{T}\big[(J\nabla H_{1})P({\tilde{\Phi}}^{s}_{\varepsilon}({\tilde{z}}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon}))\ -(J\nabla H_{1})P({\tilde{\Phi}}^{s}_{\varepsilon}{\omega}_{\varepsilon}^{\rm s}({\tilde{z}}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon}))\big]\omega(t_{0}+s)\,ds\\
\end{split}

The vector field
(J\nabla H_{1})
is thought of as derivation, and so
(J\nabla H_{1})P
is the corresponding directional derivative of
P
.
Hence

\begin{split}(J\nabla H_{1})P({\tilde{\Phi}}^{s}_{\varepsilon}({\tilde{z}}))=&-\varepsilon\left[\frac{\partial P}{\partial p}\frac{\partial H_{1}}{\partial q}+\frac{\partial P}{\partial q}\frac{\partial H_{1}}{\partial p}\right]({\tilde{\Phi}}^{s}_{\varepsilon}({\tilde{z}}))\omega(t_{0}+s)\\
=&\varepsilon\{P,H_{1}\}({\tilde{\Phi}}^{s}_{\varepsilon}({\tilde{z}}))\omega(t_{0}+s),\end{split}

where
\{\cdot,\cdot\}
denotes the Poisson bracket.

Letting
T\to+\infty
, since
\left[P({\tilde{\Phi}}^{T}_{\varepsilon}({\tilde{z}}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon}))-P({\tilde{\Phi}}^{T}_{\varepsilon}{\Omega}_{\varepsilon}^{\rm s}({\tilde{z}}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon}))\right]\omega(t_{0}+s)\to 0
, we obtain

(8.3)

\begin{split}P({\tilde{z}}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon})-P({\Omega}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon}({\tilde{z}}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon}))=&-\varepsilon\int_{0}^{+\infty}\big[\{P,H_{1}\}({\tilde{\Phi}}^{s}_{\varepsilon}({\tilde{z}}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon}))\ -\{P,H_{1}\}({\tilde{\Phi}}^{s}_{\varepsilon}{\Omega}_{\varepsilon}^{\rm s}({\tilde{z}}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon}))\big]\omega(t_{0}+s)\,ds.\end{split}

We split the integral on the right-hand side of the above into two:

(8.4)

\begin{split}I=&-\varepsilon\int_{0}^{k\log(1/\varepsilon)}\big(\{P,H_{1}\}({\tilde{\Phi}}^{s}_{\varepsilon}({\tilde{z}}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon}))-\{P,H_{1}\}({\tilde{\Phi}}^{s}_{\varepsilon}{\Omega}_{\varepsilon}^{\rm s}({\tilde{z}}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon}))\big)\omega(t_{0}+s)\,ds,\\
II=&-\varepsilon\int_{k\log(1/\varepsilon)}^{\infty}\big(\{P,H_{1}\}({\tilde{\Phi}}^{s}_{\varepsilon}({\tilde{z}}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon}))-\{P,H_{1}\}({\tilde{\Phi}}^{s}_{\varepsilon}{\Omega}_{\varepsilon}^{\rm s}({\tilde{z}}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon}))\big)\omega(t_{0}+s)\,ds,\end{split}

for some
k>0
.

For the second integral, since

\big[\{P,H_{1}\}({\tilde{\Phi}}^{s}_{\varepsilon}({\tilde{z}}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon}))-\{P,H_{1}\}({\tilde{\Phi}}^{s}_{\varepsilon}{\Omega}_{\varepsilon}^{\rm s}({\tilde{z}}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon}))\big]\omega(t_{0}+s)\to 0

exponentially fast, for any
k>0
we have that
II=O_{C^{1}}(\varepsilon^{\varrho_{2}})
for some
\varrho_{2}>0
.

For the first integral, by applying the Gronwall Inequality – III
A.4
and choosing
k>0
sufficiently small, we can replace the terms depending on the perturbed flow by corresponding terms depending on the unperturbed flow, while making an error of order
O_{C^{1}}(\varepsilon^{\varrho_{1}})
, for some
\varrho_{1}\in(0,1)
, obtaining

\begin{split}I=&-\varepsilon\int_{0}^{k\log(1/\varepsilon)}\big[\{P,H_{1}\}({\tilde{\Phi}}^{s}_{0}({\tilde{z}}^{\rm s}_{0}))-\{P,H_{1}\}({\tilde{\Phi}}^{s}_{0}{\Omega}_{0}^{\rm s}({\tilde{z}}^{\rm s}_{0}))\big]\omega(t_{0}+s)\,ds\\
&+O_{C^{1}}(\varepsilon^{1+\varrho_{1}}).\end{split}

Since also

\big[\{P,H_{1}\}({\tilde{\Phi}}^{s}_{0}({\tilde{z}}^{\rm s}_{0}))-\{P,H_{1}\}]({\tilde{\Phi}}^{s}_{0}{\Omega}_{0}^{\rm s}({\tilde{z}}^{\rm s}_{0}))\big]\omega(t_{0}+s)\to 0

exponentially fast, we
can replace the above integral from
0
to
{k\log(1/\varepsilon)}
by the improper integral from
0
to
+\infty
, by making an error of order
O(\varepsilon^{1+\rho_{2}})
.

Thus,

\begin{split}I=&-\varepsilon\int_{0}^{+\infty}\big[\{P,H_{1}\}({\tilde{\Phi}}^{s}_{0}({\tilde{z}}^{\rm s}_{0}))-\{P,H_{1}\}({\tilde{\Phi}}^{s}_{0}{\Omega}_{0}^{\rm s}({\tilde{z}}^{\rm s}_{0})\big]\omega(t_{0}+s)\,ds\\
&+O_{C^{1}}(\varepsilon^{1+\varrho}),\end{split}

where
\varrho=\min\{\varrho_{1},\varrho_{2}\}
.

Combining
I
and
II
we obtain

(8.5)

\begin{split}P(\tilde{z}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon})-P({\Omega}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{z}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon}))=&-\varepsilon\int_{0}^{+\infty}\big[\{P,H_{1}\}({\tilde{\Phi}}^{s}_{0}(\tilde{z}^{\rm s}_{0}))-\{P,H_{1}\}](\tilde{\Phi}^{s}_{0}{\Omega}_{0}^{\rm s}(\tilde{z}^{\rm s}_{0}))\big]\omega(t_{0}+s)\,ds\\
&+O_{C^{1}}(\varepsilon^{1+\rho}).\end{split}

Using that
\tilde{z}_{0}=(I,\phi,p_{0}(\tau),q_{0}(\tau),t_{0})
and
\tilde{z}_{0}^{\pm}=(I,\phi,0,0,t_{0})
, we have

(8.6)

\begin{split}P({\tilde{z}}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon})-P({\Omega}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon}({\tilde{z}}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon}))=&-\varepsilon\int_{0}^{+\infty}\{P,H_{1}\}(I,\phi+\nu(I)s,p(\tau+s),q(\tau+s))\omega(t_{0}+s)\,ds\\
&+O_{C^{1}}(\varepsilon^{1+\rho}).\end{split}

A similar computation for a point
{\tilde{z}}^{\rm u}_{\varepsilon}
in
W^{\rm u}(\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(\theta^{t_{0}}\omega))
,
yields

(8.7)

\begin{split}P({\tilde{z}}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon})-P({\Omega}^{\rm u}_{\varepsilon}({\tilde{z}}^{\rm u}_{\varepsilon}))=&-\varepsilon\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\{P,H_{1}\}(I,\phi+\nu(I)s,p(\tau+s),q(\tau+s))\omega(t_{0}+s)\,ds\\
&+O_{C^{1}}(\varepsilon^{1+\rho}),\end{split}

where
\Omega^{\rm u}_{\varepsilon}({\tilde{z}}^{\rm u}_{\varepsilon})
be the foot-point of the unique unstable fiber of
W^{\rm u}_{\varepsilon}(\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(\theta^{t_{0}}\omega))
through
{\tilde{z}}^{\rm u}_{\varepsilon}
.

Subtracting (
8.6
) from (
8.7
) yields

(8.8)

\begin{split}P({\tilde{z}}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon})-P({\tilde{z}}^{\rm u}_{\varepsilon})=&P({\Omega}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon}({\tilde{z}}^{\rm u}_{\varepsilon}))-P({\Omega}^{\rm u}_{\varepsilon}({\tilde{z}}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon}))\\
&-\varepsilon\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\{P,H_{1}\}(I,\phi+\nu(I)s,p(\tau+s),q(\tau+s))\omega(t_{0}+s)\,ds\\
&+O(\varepsilon^{1+\rho}).\end{split}

The points
{\Omega}^{\rm u}_{\varepsilon}({\tilde{z}}^{\rm u}_{\varepsilon})
and
{\Omega}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon}({\tilde{z}}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon})
are in

\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(\theta^{t_{0}}\omega)
.
Since
P
has a critical point at
(0,0)
and
P({\Omega}^{{\rm s}}_{0}({\tilde{z}}_{0}))=P({\Omega}^{{\rm u}}_{0}({\tilde{z}}_{0}))

it follows that

\|P({\Omega}^{{\rm s},{\rm u}}_{\varepsilon}({\tilde{z}}^{{\rm s},{\rm u}}_{\varepsilon}))-P({\Omega}^{{\rm s},{\rm u}}_{0}({\tilde{z}}_{0}))\|_{C^{1}}\leq C\varepsilon^{2},

and therefore

\|P({\Omega}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon}({\tilde{z}}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon}))-P({\Omega}^{\rm u}_{\varepsilon}({\tilde{z}}^{\rm u}_{\varepsilon}))\|_{C^{1}}=O(\varepsilon^{2}).

Therefore, (
8.8
) immediately implies (
8.1
), which concludes the proof.
∎

8.2.
Time invariance

Consider the
Melnikov function
that appears on the right-hand side of Proposition
8.1
.

(8.9)

\begin{split}(I,\phi,\tau,t_{0})\mapsto&M^{P}(I,\phi,\tau,t_{0})\\
=&\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\{P,H_{1}\}(I,\phi+\nu(I)s,p_{0}(\tau+s),q_{0}(\tau+s))\omega(t_{0}+s)\,ds\end{split}

The following is immediate:

Lemma 8.2
.

Let
t_{0}\in Q_{A_{\delta},T_{\delta}}
.
If the mapping

\tau\mapsto M^{P}(I,\phi,\tau,t_{0})

has a non-degenerate zero
\tau^{*}=\tau^{*}(I,\phi,t_{0})
, then there exists
0<\varepsilon_{1}<\varepsilon_{0}
such that

\tau\mapsto P(\tilde{z}^{\rm s}_{\varepsilon})-P(\tilde{z}^{\rm u}_{\varepsilon})

has a non-degenerate zero
\tau^{*}_{\varepsilon}=\tau^{*}(I,\phi,t_{0})+O_{C^{1}}(\varepsilon^{1+\varrho})
for all
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{1}
, which in turn implies the existence of a transverse homoclinic intersection of
W^{\rm s}(\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(\theta^{t_{0}}\omega))
and
W^{\rm u}(\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(\theta^{t_{0}}\omega))
for all
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{1}
.

We will show that
W^{\rm s}(\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(\theta^{t_{0}}\omega))
and
W^{\rm u}(\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(\theta^{t_{0}}\omega))
intersect transversally in Section
8.3
.

Towards this goal, we start by making the following key observation:

Remark 8.3
.

The argument in the proof of Proposition
8.1
shows that the mapping

(I,\phi,\tau,\tilde{t})\mapsto M^{P}(I,\phi,\tau,\tilde{t})

is well-defined for all
\tilde{t}\in\mathbb{R}
, and
not only for
\tilde{t}\in Q_{A_{\delta},T_{\delta}}(\omega)
.
Indeed, for any
\tilde{t}\in\mathbb{R}
, by Lemma
5.1
we have

|\omega(\tilde{t}+s)|\leq A_{\theta^{\tilde{t}}\omega}+B|s|
for all
s
.
Therefore, for
\omega
and
\tilde{t}
fixed, the integrand in (
8.9
) is exponentially convergent to
0
as
s\mapsto\pm\infty
.

Proposition 8.4

(Time invariance of
M^{P}
)
.

(i)

For any
\varsigma\in\mathbb{R}
we have

(8.10)

M^{P}(I,\phi,\tau,\tilde{t})=M^{P}(I,\phi+\nu(I)\varsigma,\tau+\varsigma,\tilde{t}+\varsigma).

(ii)

If the mapping

\tau\mapsto M^{P}(I,\phi,\tau,\tilde{t}),

has a non-degenerate zero at
\tau^{*}(I,\phi,\tilde{t})
,
then for any
\varsigma\in\mathbb{R}
, the mapping

\tau\mapsto M^{P}(I,\phi+\nu(I)\varsigma,\tau,\tilde{t}+\varsigma)

has a non-degenerate zero
\tau^{*}(I,\phi+\nu(I)\varsigma,\tilde{t}+\varsigma)
, and

(8.11)

\tau^{*}(I,\phi+\nu(I)\varsigma,\tilde{t}+\varsigma)=\tau^{*}(I,\phi,\tilde{t})+\varsigma.

(iii)

If the mapping

\tilde{t}\mapsto M^{P}(I,\phi,\tau,\tilde{t}),

has a non-degenerate zero at
t^{*}(I,\phi,\tau)
,
then for any
\varsigma\in\mathbb{R}
, the mapping

\tilde{t}\mapsto M^{P}(I,\phi+\nu(I)\varsigma,\tau+\varsigma,\tilde{t})

has a non-degenerate zero
\tilde{t}^{*}(I,\phi+\nu(I)\varsigma,\tau+\varsigma)
, and

(8.12)

\tilde{t}^{*}(I,\phi+\nu(I)\varsigma,\tau+\varsigma)=t^{*}(I,\phi,\tau)+\varsigma.

Proof.

In (
8.9
) make the change of variable
t\mapsto\varsigma+t
, obtaining

(8.13)

\begin{split}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}&\{P,H_{1}\}(I,\phi+\nu(I)s,p_{0}(\tau+s),q_{0}(\tau+s))\omega(\tilde{t}+s)\,ds\\
=&\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\{P,H_{1}\}(I,\phi+\nu(I)\varsigma+\nu(I)s,p_{0}(\tau+\varsigma+s),q_{0}(\tau+\varsigma+s))\omega(\tilde{t}+\varsigma+s)\,ds.\end{split}

Thus, if
\tau^{*}(I,\phi,\tilde{t})
is a non-degenerate zero of the first integral, then
\tau^{*}(I,\phi,\tilde{t})+\varsigma
is a non-degenerate zero for the second integral,
and hence
\tau^{*}(I,\phi,\tilde{t})+\varsigma=\tau^{*}(I,\phi+\nu(I)\varsigma,\tilde{t}+\varsigma)
.

Similarly, if
\tilde{t}^{*}(I,\phi,\tau)
is a non-degenerate zero for the first integral, then
\tilde{t}^{*}+\varsigma=\tilde{t}^{*}(I,\phi,\tau)+\varsigma
is non-degenerate zero for the second integral.
∎

8.3.
Existence of transverse homoclinic intersections

Let
\mathscr{F}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
a
C^{1}
-function with the property that
\mathscr{F}(\tilde{t})\to 0
as
\tilde{t}\to+\infty
and
D{\mathscr{F}}(\tilde{t})\to 0
as
\tilde{t}\to+\infty
exponentially fast, where
D({\cdot})
denotes the derivative of a function.

Lemma 8.5
.

Let

M(\tilde{t})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathscr{F}(s)\theta^{\tilde{t}}\omega(s)ds.

Then the process
M(\tilde{t})
is a stationary Gaussian process with expectation

(8.14)

E[M(\tilde{t})]=0

and autocorrelation function

(8.15)

\begin{split}\rho(h)=&E[M(\tilde{t})M(\tilde{t}+h)]\\
=&\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathscr{F}(s_{1})\mathscr{F}(s_{2})r(s_{2}-s_{1}+h)ds_{1}ds_{2},\end{split}

where
r
is the autocorrelation function of
\eta
.
The integral is independent of
\tilde{t}
as the process is stationary.

Proof.

The proof is similar to
[
Yag18
, Lemma 4.4]
.
Since
\eta(\tilde{t})
is a stationary Gaussian process with mean
0
,
M(\tilde{t})
is also a stationary Gaussian process with mean
0
.

The variance (zeroth spectral moment) of
M(\tilde{t})
is given by

\chi_{0}=\rho(0)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathscr{F}(s_{1})\mathscr{F}(s_{2})r(s_{2}-s_{1})ds_{1}ds_{2}.

Performing a change of variable
(s_{1},s_{2}-s_{1})\mapsto(\tilde{t},s)
we can write

\chi_{0}=\rho(0)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathscr{F}(\tilde{t})\mathscr{F}(\tilde{t}+s)r(s)dtds.

By definition, the autocorrelation function of
M(\tilde{t})
is given by

\rho(h):=E\left[M(\tilde{t}+h)M(\tilde{t})\right].

Taking the second derivative with respect to
\tilde{t}
of

\rho(h)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathscr{F}(s_{1})\mathscr{F}(s_{2})r(s_{2}-s_{1}+h)ds_{1}ds_{2}

and applying integration by parts twice we obtain

\frac{d^{2}}{dh^{2}}\rho(h)=-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}D{\mathscr{F}}(s_{1})D{\mathscr{F}}(s_{2})r(s_{2}-s_{1}+h)ds_{1}ds_{2},

where
D{\mathscr{F}}(s_{1})=\frac{\partial\mathscr{F}}{\partial s_{1}}
and
D{\mathscr{F}}(s_{2})=\frac{\partial\mathscr{F}}{\partial s_{2}}
.
For integration by parts we have used that
\lim_{s\to\pm\infty}\mathscr{F}(s)=0
exponentially fast together with its derivative, and that
r
is a bounded function together with its derivative.

Performing a change of variable
(s_{1},s_{2}-s_{1})\mapsto(\tilde{t},s)
and setting
h=0
we obtain that the second spectral moment of
M
is given by

\chi_{2}=-\frac{d^{2}}{dh^{2}}\rho(h)_{\mid h=0}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}D{\mathscr{F}}(\tilde{t})D{\mathscr{F}}(\tilde{t}+s)r(s)dtds.

∎

Denote

(8.16)

\begin{split}\mathscr{P}(I,\phi,\tau,s)=&\{P,H_{1}\}\left(I,\phi+\nu(I)s,p_{0}(\tau+s),q_{0}(\tau+s)\right).\end{split}

When
(I,\phi,\tau)
are fixed and we only want to emphasize the dependence on
s
we denote the above function by
\mathscr{P}(s)
.
Note that
\mathscr{P}(s)
converges to
0
exponentially fast together with its derivative as
s\to\pm\infty
.

Define the
Melnikov stochastic process

(8.17)

\tilde{t}\in\mathbb{R}\mapsto M^{P}(\tilde{t}):=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\mathscr{P}(s)\theta^{\tilde{t}}\omega(s)ds.

Proposition 8.6
.

The Melnikov process (
8.17
) is a stationary Gaussian process with expectation

(8.18)

E[M^{P}(\tilde{t})]=0

and autocorrelation function

(8.19)

\begin{split}\rho(h)=&E[M^{P}(\tilde{t})M^{P}(\tilde{t}+h)]\\
=&\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathscr{P}(s_{1})\mathscr{P}(s_{2})r(s_{2}-s_{1}+h)ds_{1}ds_{2},\end{split}

with the right-side independent of
\tilde{t}
as the process is stationary.
The zeroth spectral moments is:

\chi^{P}_{0}=\rho(0)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathscr{P}(s_{1})\mathscr{P}(s_{1}+s)r(s)ds_{1}ds

and the second spectral moment is:

\chi^{P}_{2}=-\left(\frac{d^{2}}{dh^{2}}\rho(h)\right)_{\mid h=0}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}D{\mathscr{P}}(s_{1})D{\mathscr{P}}(s_{1}+s)r(s)ds_{1}ds.

Proof.

It follows immediately from Lemma
8.5
.
∎

We will make the following assumption on the spectral moments of
M^{P}
:

(SMP)

\chi^{P}_{0}>0\textrm{ and }\chi^{P}_{2}>0.

Proposition 8.7
.

Assume condition (
SMP
).
Fix
(I^{*},\phi^{*},\tau^{*})
. Then the mapping

\tilde{t}\in\mathbb{R}\mapsto M^{P}(I^{*},\phi^{*},\tau^{*},\tilde{t})

has a non-degenerate zero
\tilde{t}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Applying the Rice’s Formula
[
Ric39
,
Lin12
]
to the Melnikov process

\tilde{t}\mapsto M^{P}(\tilde{t}),

the number
N_{T}
of zeros of
M^{P}(\tilde{t})
on the interval
[0,T]
has expectation

(8.20)

E[N_{T}]=\frac{T}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{\chi^{P}_{2}}{\chi^{P}_{0}}}e^{-\frac{E[M^{P}(\tilde{t})]^{2}}{2\chi^{P}_{0}}}=\frac{T}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{\chi^{P}_{2}}{\chi^{P}_{0}}},

and
M^{P}(\tilde{t})
has almost surely no tangential zeroes. This yields the desired conclusion.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.2
.

Let
\tilde{t}^{*}=\tilde{t}^{*}(I^{*},\phi^{*},\tau^{*})
be a non-degenerate zero from Proposition
8.7
, for some
I^{*},\phi^{*},\tau^{*}
fixed.
Then
\tau^{*}
is a zero of

\tau\mapsto M^{P}(I^{*},\phi^{*},\tau,\tilde{t}^{*}).

Moreover, from Proposition
8.4
, (
8.11
) and (
8.12
), it follows that
\tau^{*}
is a non-degenerate zero.
Indeed, the fact that
\tilde{t}^{*}
is a non-degenerate zero implies that there are
\varsigma>0
arbitrarily close to

such that one of the following holds:

\begin{split}M(I,\phi+\nu(I)(-\varsigma),\tau^{*}-\varsigma,t^{*}_{0}-\varsigma)<0<M(I,\phi+\nu(I)\varsigma,\tau^{*}+\varsigma,t^{*}_{0}+\varsigma),\\
M(I,\phi+\nu(I)(-\varsigma),\tau^{*}-\varsigma,t^{*}_{0}-\varsigma)>0>M(I,\phi+\nu(I)\varsigma,\tau^{*}+\varsigma,t^{*}_{0}+\varsigma).\end{split}

This implies that
\tau^{*}
is a non-degenerate zero of

\tau\mapsto M^{P}(I^{*},\phi^{*},\tau,\tilde{t}^{*}).

Therefore,
\tau^{*}
is (locally) uniquely defined by
I^{*},\phi^{*},\tilde{t}^{*}
, so we can write
\tau^{*}=\tau^{*}(I^{*},\phi^{*},\tilde{t}^{*})
.

If
t_{0}:=\tilde{t}^{*}\in Q_{A_{\delta},T_{\delta}}(\omega)
, then

W^{\rm u}(\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(\theta^{t_{0}}\omega))
and
W^{\rm s}(\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(\theta^{t_{0}}\omega))
are well defined. By Lemma
8.2
, it follows that they intersect transversally for
\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{1}
.

If
\tilde{t}^{*}\not\in Q_{A_{\delta},T_{\delta}}(\omega)
then take
\varsigma^{*}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

. Such a
\varsigma^{*}
always exists due to Proposition
5.2
. By Proposition
8.4
we have that

\tau^{*}(I^{*},\phi^{*}+\nu(I^{*})\varsigma,t^{*}_{0}+\varsigma^{*})=\tau^{*}+\varsigma^{*}
is a non-degenerate zero of

\tau\mapsto M^{P}(I^{*},\phi^{*}+\nu(I^{*})\varsigma,\tau^{*},\tilde{t}^{*}+\varsigma^{*}).

This implies that
W^{\rm u}(\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(\theta^{\tilde{t}^{*}+\varsigma^{*}}\omega))
and
W^{\rm s}(\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(\theta^{\tilde{t}^{*}+\varsigma^{*}}\omega))
intersect transversally for all
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{1}
for some
\varepsilon_{1}<\varepsilon_{0}
sufficiently small.
∎

Remark 8.8
.

Recall that
\tau
corresponds to a ‘position’ along the homoclinic orbit of the unperturbed system.
The typical way to use Melnikov theory to show existence of transverse intersection of the perturbed stable and unstable manifolds is to vary the position
\tau
until we reach a place where the distance between the manifolds is

9.1.
Random scattering map

In this section we adapt the theory of the scattering map developed in
[

]
for the case of random perturbations.
Our construction is very similar with the time-dependent scattering theory for general vector fields developed in
[

The most significant difference is that in our case the scattering map depends on the realization of the stochastic process in a measurable fashion.

Let

For a point

10.
Conclusions and future work

To summarize, in this paper we considered a rotator-pendulum system with a random perturbation of special type,
and we proved the persistence of the NHIM and of the stable and unstable manifolds, the existence of transverse homoclinic orbits,
and the existence of orbits that exhibit micro-diffusion in the action.
The perturbation is given by some Hamiltonian vector field that vanishes on the phase space of the rotator, multiplied by unbounded noise.
We work with path-wise solutions under the assumption that the sample paths are Hölder continuous.
The persistence of the NHIM is proved not for all times, but only for a distinguished set of times within some arbitrarily large time interval.
It seems possible that the RNHIMs and their stable and unstable manifolds exist for all times, but may not be of uniform size, or of a size necessary to guarantee the crossing of the stable and unstable manifolds. Further investigation is planned on this regard, including also a generalization to less regular sample paths for the noise.

We also plan to show the existence of Arnold diffusion (rather than micro-diffusion); a foreseeable way is by developing random versions of the lambda lemma and shadowing lemma, and showing that we can find
O(1/\varepsilon)
pseudo-orbits of the scattering map that we can join together.

\begin{split}\|z_{\varepsilon}(t)-z_{0}(t)\|\leq&\varepsilon^{\varrho}\left[C_{0}+C_{1}k\ln(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})+\frac{C_{2}}{2}k^{2}\left(\ln(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})\right)^{2}\right]\\
=&\varepsilon^{\varrho_{1}}\varepsilon^{\varrho-\varrho_{1}}\left[C_{0}+C_{1}k\ln(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})+\frac{C_{2}}{2}k^{2}\left(\ln(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})\right)^{2}\right]\end{split}

for
0<\varrho_{1}<\varrho
. Note that
\varrho_{1}
can be chosen arbitrary.

There exists
\varepsilon_{0}
and constants
A,B>0
such that, for
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
, we have

\varepsilon^{\varrho-\varrho_{1}}\ln(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})<A\textrm{ and }\varepsilon^{\varrho-\varrho_{1}}\left(\ln(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})\right)^{2}<B.

Therefore from (
A.17
) we obtain that, for the constant

K=[C_{0}+C_{1}kA+\frac{C_{2}}{2}k^{2}B]

we have

\|z_{\varepsilon}(t)-z_{0}(t)\|\leq K\varepsilon^{\varrho_{1}}.

∎
