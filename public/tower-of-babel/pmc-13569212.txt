Comparison of four different in vitro assays for prediction of potential RBC antibody clinical significance
Authors: Yeniley Ruiz Noa, Clémence Salou, Myriam Beshai, Selena Y Cen, Lindsay K Hillis, Katrina Billingsley, Donald R Branch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe monocyte monolayer assay (MMA) uses monocytes, but it is known that antibody‐mediated hemolysis occurs in macrophages. Whether the use of macrophages would improve the predictive capacity of the MMA is unclear. Also, it is unclear if RBC alloantibodies contribute to hemolysis via antibody‐dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC). We have compared the MMA to an assay that utilizes monocytes cultured to two different macrophages, called the monocyte–macrophage assay (MOMA), and to ADCC using a 51chromium‐release assay with natural killer cells.Study Design and MethodsMMA was performed using peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) to adhere monocytes to chamber slides. MOMA was done using purified peripheral blood monocytes cultured in the presence of GM‐CSF (M1 macrophages) or M‐CSF (M2 macrophages) and then polarized using IL‐4 (M2) or LPS and IFNγ (M1). NK cells were purified and incubated with alloantibody‐opsonized RBCs loaded with 51‐chromium. We examined 38 antibodies of different specificities, including anti‐D, ‐E, ‐K, ‐Jka, ‐M, ‐S, ‐k, ‐Fya, ‐P, ‐Ge2, ‐Dib, ‐Yta.ResultsMost weakly reactive antibodies were negative in MMA but positive in MOMA and ADCC. M2 was generally more positive than M1. All anti‐D, ‐K, ‐E gave strong IATs and showed reactivity in all four assays. Anti‐P (2+ IAT) was positive in the MMA and MOMA but negative in ADCC. Twelve anti‐Ytas had very weak IATs and all were negative in the MMA and 3/12 positive in MOMA and none were positive in ADCC.DiscussionWe show that both MOMA and ADCC are more sensitive than MMA for indicating potential for antibody clinical significance. However, validation with antibodies having clinical data as to their actual significance is necessary.Keywords: ADCC, macrophages, MMA, MOMA, monocyte monolayer assay, monocyte–macrophage assay, monocytes, NK cells

INTRODUCTION

Up until the early 1980s, the potential clinical significance of alloantibodies was based solely on the specificity of a given antibody. However, in 1980/81 three independent groups published on using an in vitro functional phagocytosis assay as a means for distinguishing clinically significant from insignificant antibodies to red blood cells (RBCs). 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 This assay became known as the monocyte monolayer assay (MMA). 6 , 7 The MMA has been in use now for more than 55 years without many changes in how it is performed. 7 , 8 , 9 It has been shown to be a good predictor of antibody potential for inducing or not inducing a transfusion reaction, 10 and has been used to select incompatible blood for transfusion with high success. 7 , 11 However, it is not always predictive of an outcome of transfusion; 12 therefore, we were interested in examining other potential assays that may be an improvement over the MMA.

Herein, we compare four different assays that have the potential to predict the outcome of an incompatible transfusion: (1) MMA, (2) an assay that utilizes monocytes cultured to two different macrophage populations, inflammatory (M1) and anti‐inflammatory (M2) macrophages, we call the monocyte–macrophage assay (MOMA), and (3) the antibody‐dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) assay using purified natural killer (NK) cells. Fc receptor expression in the MMA and MOMA was also examined to help explain any differences in outcomes. We also provide preliminary results showing that M1 and M2 macrophages can be frozen and thawed for use, making it easier for reference laboratories to incorporate the MOMA into their testing, if desired.

METHODS

Antibodies and RBCs

Alloantibodies considered clinically significant based on their specificity were provided by LifeShare, Shreveport, LA. Antibodies that had been previously stored frozen were sent to the laboratory in Toronto, thawed and aliquoted, and re‐frozen to be thawed when required for individual assays. Polyclonal anti‐D (WinRho, human Rh immune globulin SDF) was from Saol Therapeutics, Dublin, Ireland. Anti‐human globulin (AHG) for the indirect antiglobulin test (IAT) was from Immucor/Werfen, Norcross, GA (Novaclone, a mixture of two murine monoclonal antibodies reactive with IgG1, IgG2, IgG3 and IgG4). R2R2 RBCs used for control anti‐D were from Canadian Blood Services. Appropriate phenotyped RBCs used in the analyses were from antibody identification panel cells (Immucor‐Werfen, Norcross, GA).

MMA

The MMA was performed as previously described. 8 , 9 , 13 , 14 Briefly, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) are isolated from whole blood by density gradient (Ficoll‐Paque, Cytiva, Marlborough, MA) and distributed in complete medium (RPMI‐1640 + 10% Fetal Bovine Serum, 10 mM HEPES and 100 IU Penicillin, 100 μg/mL Streptomycin, all from Wisent Bioproducts, Saint‐Jean‐Baptiste, QC) to chamber slides (Nunc Lab‐Tek II, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Ottawa, ON) wells. Cells are incubated for 1–1.5 h at 37°C with 5% CO2 to allow for adherence. Chamber slides are removed, washed with phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and RBCs opsonized with the desired antibody are added to the chamber slide wells and then incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 2 h. Chamber slides are removed and processed for visualization of phagocytosis using a phase contrast microscope. A phagocytic index (PI) is determined by counting the number of phagocytosed RBCs in 100 monocytes. A PI > 5 was used to indicate potential for clinical significance which has been previously validated. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 12 , 13 , 14

MOMA

The MOMA was performed as previously described. 15 Briefly, PBMCs were obtained from whole blood and plated into two T25 flasks (Corning Inc., Corning, NY) precoated with 0.1 mg/mL poly‐D‐lysine (Sigma‐Aldrich, P7886, St. Louis, MO) in PBS (Wisent Bioproducts, Saint‐Jean‐Baptiste, QC). To one flask for M1 macrophages was added 2.5 ng/mL GM‐CSF (StemCell Technologies, Vancouver, BC) and the other flask for M2 macrophages received 50 ng/mL M‐CSF (StemCell Technologies). Cells were cultured for 5–6 days at 37°C with 5% CO2. Following this culture period, the different macrophages were polarized by adding 50 ng/mL IFNγ (StemCell Technologies), 10 ng/mL LPS (StemCell Technologies), and 2.5 ng/mL GM‐CSF to the M1 flask and 20 ng/mL IL‐4 (StemCell Technologies) and 50 ng/mL M‐CSF to the M2 flask, with additional culturing of the two populations for 2 days at 37°C with 5% CO2. Polarized cells were harvested by scraping and 400,000 cells of M1 and M2 added to the wells of chamber slides and processed in an identical manner as for the MMA (see above). Significant PI values were calculated for each macrophage assay by calculating the means ± standard deviation for PI results with unopsonized RBCs. For M1 macrophages, a mean of 8.26 ± 7.22 (n = 20) was found and for M2 macrophages, a mean of 6.97 ± 5.85 (n = 29). Therefore, to be conservative, we used the mean + 3SD as the cut‐off. Thus, a PI > 30 for M1 macrophages and a PI > 25 for M2 macrophages was considered a significant phagocytosis result.

ADCC

ADCC was performed as previously described. 9 Briefly, PBMCs were isolated from buffy coat collections by density gradient centrifugation using Ficoll‐Paque and used for isolation of NK cells (human NK enrichment kit; StemCell Technologies). NK cells were cultured overnight in complete media at a density of 1–2 million/mL. RBCs were washed with PBS, then opsonized with anti‐D (1 μg/mL) as positive control, PBS alone, or the alloantibody opsonizing with appropriate phenotyped RBCs. Opsonized RBCs were washed, viable cells counted using a Luna cell counter (Logos Biosystems, Anyang‐si, Gyeonggi‐do, South Korea) with a portion reserved for IAT analysis and 10 million viable cells were labeled for 1 h at 37°C with 50 μCi 51Cr (51Cr; cat. # NEZ030001MC, Revvity Biotechnology, Waltham, MA). After washing 3x with complete media, 51Cr‐labeled RBC were incubated in triplicate with NK cells (NK; 10:1 effector: target ratio), complete media only (S; for spontaneous lysis determination) or 1 M HCl (T; total lysis determination) in 200 μL complete media for 4 h at 37°C, 5% CO2. Fifty microliters portions of supernatant were then transferred to LumaPlate Microplate (Revvity Biotechnology, Waltham, MA) wells which were air‐dried overnight then counted using a MicroBeta2 (PerkinElmer, Shelton, CT). The percent lysis was calculated as 100 × (NK − S)/(T − S). The formula means 100 times (lysis count in the wells containing NK minus lysis count in the wells containing RPMI) divided by (lysis count in the wells containing 1 mM HCl minus lysis count in the wells containing RPMI).

Any percent lysis was considered a significant result as unopsonized RBCs never showed any lysis (n = 20).

Fc receptor expression

PBMCs were isolated from fresh whole blood and differentiated into M1 and M2 macrophages as described in Section 2.3. Differentiated cells were harvested using a cell scraper (VWR International, Radnor, PA) and washed with PBS. Harvested cells were blocked for 15 min on ice with PBS + 2% human serum, avoiding use of CD16/CD32 Fc blockers as it will impact Fc receptor staining downstream. Live cell staining was performed. CD16‐FITC (BioLegend, San Diego, CA), CD32‐PE (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN), and CD64‐APC (BioLegend) antibodies were used to detect FcγRIII, FcγRII, and FcγRI, respectively. FACS data were collected using FACS Calibur (BD BioScience, San Jose, CA). FMO controls and isotype controls were used in combination for FACS gating.

Freezing and thawing M1 and M2 macrophages

This protocol was designed by the Canadian Blood Services under the direction of Dr. Jason Acker and outlines a standardized approach for achieving a freezing rate of −10 to −15°C/min, suitable for cryopreserving cells under defined conditions. A freezing device was pre‐prepared by filling a non‐insulated container (such as a pipette tip box) with packed ice. Wells were formed in the container by firmly placing 1.5 mL freezing tubes in the ice. The container was placed in the −80°C until the ice was frozen. When a mold was formed, the freezing tubes were removed, and the device was placed back in the freezer for future use.

Freezing M1 and M2 macrophages

Following the culture and polarization of monocytes to M1 and M2 macrophages (see Section 2.3), the cells were collected into 15 mL Falcon tubes (Corning Inc., Corning, NY), washed in PBS and resuspended in complete medium and counted. After cells were centrifuged and the remaining supernatant was discarded, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Fisher BioReagents, Pittsburg, PA) freezing media (5% DMSO, 10% FBS and 85% RPMI‐1640) was added to the tube to achieve 2 × 106 cells/mL concentration. The solution was then transferred to a 1.5 mL freezing vial, transferred into the pre‐prepared wells of the freezing device and placed into a −80°C freezer for up to 4 days.

Thawing M1 and M2 macrophages

The vials are removed from the freezer and immersed immediately into a 37°C water bath to the level of the cap, swirling gently, checking to see if the solution has thawed. Once thawed, vials are removed from the water bath and the cell content transferred to 15 mL Falcon tubes containing prewarmed to 37°C complete medium, viable cells counted using a Luna cell counter (Logos Biosystems), centrifuged, and the supernatant removed. The cells are resuspended in complete medium and counted. 2 × 105 cells, either M1 or M2, were added to wells of a chamber slide and placed in a 37°C incubator with 5% CO2 for 1.5 h for adherence of the M1 and M2 cells. Opsonization of R2R2 RBCs with anti‐D and subsequent steps were as with the MMA (see above).

Table 2 shows the number of different antibodies where the results of the assay indicated a potential for clinical significance. When the IATs were 3–4+, such as with anti‐D, ‐E, ‐K, ‐P, and testing of DAT+ RBCs from a patient with wAIHA, all four assay results were in concordance. In contrast, when testing antibodies with a weak IAT, significant differences were observed between the MMA and the other assays. With anti‐Jka antibodies, MMA indicated that none of the 3 antibodies tested would be clinically significant; however, the M1 assay indicated that 1/3 would be clinically significant while the M2 and ADCC assays indicated that all three anti‐Jka would be potentially clinically significant. With anti‐Fya, having a weak to 2+ IAT, the MMA and ADCC result indicated potential for clinical significance while the MOMA indicated a non‐clinically significant antibody; however, when the MOMA was performed, the IAT was weak while for the MMA and ADCC the IAT was 2+. Anti‐S and anti‐M having 1+ IATs showed nonsignificant phagocytosis using MMA but MOMA and ADCC indicated potential for a clinically significant antibody. Anti‐Ge2, despite a weak IAT showed to be a potentially clinically significant antibody only using ADCC. Finally, testing of 12 anti‐Yta antibodies indicated that using MMA, none would be clinically significant; however, 3/12 were predicted to be potentially clinically significant by MOMA. Using ADCC, due to limited sample, only 6 anti‐Yta could be tested and all 6 gave a negative ADCC test, despite two having a positive IAT (1+/2+).

Fc receptor expression

To begin to explain the differences between MMA and MOMA, we surmised that the different PIs we were seeing may be due to differential expression of FcγRI, FcγRII, or FcγRIII. We therefore used flow cytometry to examine the receptor levels on monocytes and M1 and M2 macrophages (Figure 1). Indeed, we found that although FcγRI and FcγRIII remain at similar expression levels in the three different mononuclear phagocytes, the expression of FcγRII is highly increased on M2 macrophages. An increase in FcγRIIa, an activating Fc receptor, 16 could explain the increased phagocytosis using M2 macrophages.

Freezing/thawing M1/M2 macrophages

The MOMA assay requires a week to culture the monocytes to the point of a 2‐day polarization step. Approximately 8 days to prepare these cells is not an option for transfusion services or reference laboratories to use these cells. We thought we could freeze the M1 and M2 macrophages after polarization and when thawed, these cells would maintain reasonable viability and function. Figure 2 shows our preliminary results. We found that using a freezing protocol, developed specifically for us, we could freeze M1 and M2 macrophages that had approximately 55% viability on thawing for M1 macrophages and ~30% for M2 macrophages (Figure 2A). Despite a significant loss in viable cells, M1 and M2 macrophages were able to phagocytose antibody‐opsonized RBCs (Figure 2A,B). However, the phagocytosis seen using the frozen/thawed cells was approximately 25% less than when tested with freshly prepared M1 cells and about 50% less phagocytosis using M2 cells.

DISCUSSION

Although the MMA has been in existence since the early 1980s and is successfully used to predict the clinical outcome of transfusion of antigen‐positive blood into people with the corresponding antibody, 7 , 11 it remains a question as to whether or not the MMA is the best predictor of antibody clinical significance. 7 , 12 Indeed, the MMA uses circulating blood monocytes in the assay while macrophages in the spleen and/or liver are the effector mononuclear phagocytes that cause extravascular hemolysis in transfusion reactions and warm antibody autoimmune hemolytic anemia. 17 , 18 Therefore, we asked whether using macrophages instead of monocytes in an assay would better predict a given RBC antibody's potential clinical significance.

It has been suggested but never clearly shown that ADCC is also a mechanism of RBC destruction in patients with hemolytic anemias. 19 , 20 , 21 Garratty in 1981 19 was the first to suggest that NK cells could be involved in immune hemolytic anemias, specifically DAT‐negative autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA) and hemolytic transfusion reactions (HTRs), however he was “not able to prove it”. 19 , 21 In 1990, Engelfriet and Ouwehand reported that ADCC may be a good predictor of hemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn (HDFN). 22 Other investigators also investigated use of ADCC for prediction of HDFN. 23 , 24 , 25 All of these reports used PBMCs, thinking monocytes were the effector cell in their assay. However, measuring ADCC using PBMCs doesn't distinguish between the monocyte or NK effector cell causing the ADCC. Indeed, more recent use of ADCC also uses a pool of PBMCs to predict outcomes in HDFN and is used routinely with anti‐D and anti‐K1, but not with other alloantibodies. 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 The report herein is the first to use purified NK cells to show that ADCC by specific NK killing may be an important mechanism contributing to the RBC destruction in all immune‐mediated hemolytic anemias, such as AIHA or HTRs, especially if hemoglobinemia/hemoglobinuria with an IgG antibody without complement activation is detected.

As previous work has only examined the use of ADCC as a predictor of HDFN, with some investigators showing ADCC a better predictor than use of MMA, 23 , 24 , 27 , 29 our results suggest that ADCC may also be a better predictor than MMA for potential alloantibody clinical significance in other antibody‐mediated hemolytic anemias, although this would require validation with antibodies having known clinical significance. However, it is unlikely that ADCC would ever be a routine assay in transfusion services or reference laboratories due to its use of radioactive chromium and requirement for specialized equipment. Our findings, however, do indicate a potential role of ADCC in antibody‐mediated hemolytic anemias. Thus, in cases of robust hemolysis, such as fulminant AIHA or when Rh antibodies cause apparent intravascular hemolysis, consideration should be given to treatments that target NK cell activity as well as antibody production and inhibition of phagocytosis.

Our results reveal that the MMA and M1 MOMA assays are the least sensitive tests for phagocytosis of antibody‐opsonized RBCs. In total, of the 38 antibodies we tested, MMA predicted 17/38 (45%) would be clinically significant, but includes 3 anti‐Jka, 2 anti‐S, 2 anti‐M, and 12 anti‐Yta, predicted to be clinically insignificant, despite some anti‐Yta having 1–2+ IAT. M1 macrophages predicted 23/38 (61%) would be potentially clinically significant including 1 each anti‐Jka, anti‐S, and anti‐M, and 3 anti‐Yta. M2 macrophages predicted 26/38 (68%) would be deemed to be potentially clinically significant. ADCC, surprisingly, predicted the most potentially clinically significant antibodies 25/32 (78%). It is interesting that M2 MOMA and ADCC predicted all three anti‐Jka tested as having the potential to be clinically significant. These results were seen even with weak to 1+ saline IATs. Transfusion of antigen‐positive blood into someone with anti‐Jka should be avoided at all costs, if possible, and our results with MOMA and ADCC reinforce this thinking.

In this study, we tested 12 anti‐Yta using MMA and MOMA and, due to sample exhaustion, only half of these antibodies were also tested using ADCC. These antibodies are notorious for their variable clinical impact, ranging from clinically insignificant to causing significant transfusion reactions when antigen‐positive blood is administered. 11 , 12 , 13 , 30 We found that only the MOMA, either M1 or M2, gave PI values indicating potential for clinical significance of 3/12 anti‐Yta, while these samples were insignificant in the MMA and negative for ADCC. This result is consistent with the known clinical significance of this antibody; some are clinically significant and some not. Thus, results with MOMA may be a better predictor of the clinical significance. However, validation using anti‐Yta with clinical data is required to know if MOMA is better than MMA to predict the clinical outcome of Yta‐positive blood transfusion where MMA indicates clinically insignificant, and MOMA indicates the opposite.

The differential outcomes using MMA, MOMA, or ADCC may be related to effector cell expression of Fc receptors. We found that expression of FcγRII (CD32) was higher on M2 than on M1 or monocytes. Perhaps this differential expression can explain why M2 phagocytosis was always higher than M1 or monocyte phagocytosis. Also, the results with anti‐P in particular stand out as this antibody gives significant PIs in the MMA (PI = 52) and MOMA (M1 PI = 66; M2 PI = 113) yet ADCC is negative. NK cells only express FcγRIIIa 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 ; so perhaps these negative results represent alloantibodies that do not interact with FcγRIIIa. This hypothesis may also apply to the results with the anti‐Yta that gave positive potential clinical significance with M2 MOMA but negative with ADCC.

Our findings suggest that using the MOMA assay, at least with M2 macrophages, may be a better assay than MMA for prediction of antibody clinical significance; however, this assay requires validation with antibodies having known clinical significance. Even if the MOMA is shown to be a more reliable predictor than MMA of potential antibody clinical significance, the fact that it requires at least a week to prepare the cells precludes its use in clinical laboratories. That is why we examined whether M1/M2 macrophages could be frozen for future use. In preliminary studies, we found this may be possible, as frozen/thawed M1 and M2 macrophages, although their viability on thaw is only around 50%, can still phagocytose, though with a lower PI than freshly prepared macrophages. Although frozen–thawed M1/M2 macrophages showed reduced phagocytosis, the results demonstrate that optimized cryopreservation and clinical antibody testing can enable the use of stored macrophages, offering a practical solution for clinical and transfusion medicine reference labs.

This study has some limitations. First, the four assays were not tested simultaneously. This was not feasible given the time‐intensive nature for the various assays. Ideally, in all four assays, the same antibody should have been used to opsonize the same RBCs. Our approach of using frozen aliquots of each antibody so that each was thawed only once helped mitigate this limitation; however, it was virtually impossible to use the same RBCs. Thus, we used RBCs from panel cells based on their phenotype. Use of the same RBCs with the appropriate phenotype would have been desirable but not possible for all 4 assays. Second, MMA, MOMA, and ADCC testing was done on different days and in some cases by different individuals. IAT scores varied slightly in some cases; although the individuals were skilled in performing the particular assay, their technique at preparing and reading a manual IAT likely varied. It would have been desirable to use the gel assay to obtain more objective IAT results. Third, we did not compare the assay outcomes with antibodies having validated clinical significance. It is difficult to obtain antibodies having documented clinical significance, particularly those that do not cause problems when antigen‐positive blood is transfused. Future validation should include such antibodies.

In summary, we compared four different assay methods to determine their capability to predict the clinical relevance of individual antibody specificities. The MMA is currently used for such predictions and is considered the “gold standard” 7 . the M2 MOMA and ADCC assays predicted more antibodies to have potential clinical significance than did the MMA (or M1 MOMA). What our results do establish is the potential role for NK cell‐mediated ADCC in alloantibody‐mediated hemolysis, confirming Garratty's previous hypothesis. 19 However, the classical chromium‐release ADCC is a labor‐intensive assay that requires purification of NK cells and a radionuclide work area. It is unlikely to become a standard clinical assay. Although M1/M2 macrophage polarization is labor‐intensive and unsuitable for immediate transfusion lab application, our preliminary data indicates that freezing/thawing is feasible. This approach enables the translation of these polarized macrophages into the clinical laboratory setting. The question now as to the best predictor of alloantibody potential for clinical significance rests with future validation testing with allo‐ or autoantibodies having documented clinical significance.
