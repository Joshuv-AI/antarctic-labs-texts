Infinite-dimensional Lipschitz stability in the Calderón problem and general Zernike bases
Henrik Garde, Markus Hirvensalo, Nuutti Hyvönen

Abstract
. Calderón’s inverse conductivity problem has, so far, only been subject to conditional logarithmic stability for infinite-dimensional classes of conductivities and to Lipschitz stability when restricted to finite-dimensional classes. Focusing our attention on the unit ball domain in any spatial dimension d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 , we give an elementary proof that there are (infinitely many) infinite-dimensional classes of conductivities for which there is Lipschitz stability. In particular, Lipschitz stability holds for general expansions of conductivities, allowing all angular frequencies but with limited freedom in the radial direction, if the basis coefficients decay fast enough to overcome the growth of the basis functions near the domain boundary. We construct general d d -dimensional Zernike bases and prove that they provide examples of infinite-dimensional Lipschitz stability.



Keywords: Calderón problem, inverse conductivity problem, Lipschitz stability, Zernike basis.

2020 Mathematics Subject Classification: 35R30, 35R25, 33C45.

1.
Introduction

Let
B
be the Euclidean unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for a spatial dimension
d\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\}
. For a conductivity coefficient
\gamma\in L^{\infty}(B;\mathbb{R})
, with
\mathop{\textup{ess\,inf}}\gamma>0
, the conductivity equation is

-\nabla\cdot(\gamma\nabla u)=0\text{ in }B.

(1.1)

One can consider boundary measurements in the form of the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map

\Lambda(\gamma)\colon H^{1/2}(\partial B)\to H^{-1/2}(\partial B),

given as

u|_{\partial B}\mapsto\nu\cdot(\gamma\nabla u)|_{\partial B}

for the solutions (electric potentials)
u\in H^{1}(B)
to (
1.1
). Calderón’s inverse conductivity problem is to determine
\gamma
from
\Lambda(\gamma)
; see, e.g., Calderón’s original paper
[
20
]
or the survey papers
[
18
,
38
]
for more information.

In this paper we assume enough regularity for Lipschitz stability to hold for boundary determination, i.e. we consider conductivity coefficients in

C_{+}(\overline{B})=\{\,\gamma\in C(\overline{B};\mathbb{R})\mid\min_{x\in\overline{B}}\gamma(x)>0\,\}.

There is the following classical result on stability at
\partial B
.

Theorem 1.1

(Sylvester–Uhlmann
[
37
]
)
.

There exists
C_{\partial B}>0
such that for any
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in C_{+}(\overline{B})
,

\lVert\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\partial B)}\leq C_{\partial B}\lVert\Lambda(\gamma_{1})-\Lambda(\gamma_{2})\rVert_{\mathscr{L}(H^{1/2}(\partial B),H^{-1/2}(\partial B))}.

In particular,
C_{\partial B}
does not depend on the minima/maxima of
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
.

We will give infinite-dimensional examples where
\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}
can be bounded by its restriction to the boundary, which leads to Lipschitz stability for the Calderón problem through the use of Theorem
1.1
.

1.1.
Infinite-dimensional Lipschitz stability

The first result is a fairly simple observation that follows directly from Theorem
1.1
under the weak maximum principle.

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in C_{+}(\overline{B})
. If
(\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2})\in C^{2}(B)\cap C^{1}(\overline{B})
and is a solution to an elliptic equation (with nonnegative zero-order term) satisfying a weak maximum principle, then there is Lipschitz stability in
B
,

\lVert\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(B)}\leq C_{\partial B}\lVert\Lambda(\gamma_{1})-\Lambda(\gamma_{2})\rVert_{\mathscr{L}(H^{1/2}(\partial B),H^{-1/2}(\partial B))},

where
C_{\partial B}
is the constant from Theorem
1.1
.

Remark 1.3
.

Although we focus on
B
in this paper, Corollary
1.2
obviously generalizes to other bounded smooth domains, as this is also the case for Theorem
1.1
.

Since the condition in Corollary
1.2
is for the difference
\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}
, the two conductivities can also share a common component not related to a partial differential equation:

\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{0}+\widetilde{\gamma}_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad\gamma_{2}=\gamma_{0}+\widetilde{\gamma}_{2},

where
\gamma_{0}
could, e.g., be a large enough
C_{+}(\overline{B})
function to ensure that
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
are positive, while
\widetilde{\gamma}_{1}
and
\widetilde{\gamma}_{2}
could be solutions to the same equation satisfying a weak maximum principle. A canonical example is that
\widetilde{\gamma}_{1}
and
\widetilde{\gamma}_{2}
are harmonic functions bounded in
\overline{B}
.

Next we will look for other infinite-dimensional classes for which there is interior Lipschitz stability. For
x\in B\setminus\{0\}
consider polar coordinates
r=\lvert x\rvert
and
\theta=\frac{x}{\lvert x\rvert}\in\partial B
such that
x=r\theta
. The following are the core assumptions on the basis functions employed in this work:

Assumption 1.4
.

(i)

Let
\{f_{n}\}_{n}
be an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}(\partial B)
.

(ii)

For each
n
, let
\{g_{n,k}\}_{k}
be an orthonormal set in the weighted space

L^{2}_{r^{d-1}}([0,1])=\bigl\{\,g\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{C}\text{ measurable}\bigm|\int_{0}^{1}\lvert g(r)\rvert^{2}r^{d-1}\,\mathrm{d}r<\infty\,\bigr\}.

(iii)

Assume that
g_{n,k}(r)
is defined and continuous at
r=1
.

(iv)

Assume that
\inf_{n,k}\lvert g_{n,k}(1)\rvert>0
.

(v)

Define
\psi_{n,k}(r\theta)=g_{n,k}(r)f_{n}(\theta)
, and assume there are scalars
a_{n,k}
such that

\lVert\psi_{n,k}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(U)}\leq a_{n,k}

in a spherical shell
U=\{\,x\mid 1-\eta\leq\lvert x\rvert\leq 1\,\}
for some arbitrarily small
\eta>0
.

Remark 1.5
.

As a special case, the functions
g_{n,k}
can be constant in the index
n
, which corresponds to independently finding orthonormal bases
\{f_{n}\}_{n}
and
\{g_{k}\}_{k}
in the variables
\theta
and
r
, respectively. We allow the dependence of
g_{n,k}
on
n
because it is natural when the functions
\psi_{n,k}
originate from an eigenvalue problem, as is the case for the Zernike basis in Section
2.2
.

Due to Assumptions
1.4
(i) and
1.4
(ii),
\{\psi_{n,k}\}_{n,k}
is an orthonormal set in
L^{2}(B)
. Let

W=\overline{\mathop{\textup{span}}\{\psi_{n,k}\}_{n,k}},

with the closure taken in
L^{2}(B)
, so that
\{\psi_{n,k}\}_{n,k}
becomes an orthonormal basis for
W
. Expanding elements of
W
in the basis
\{\psi_{n,k}\}_{n,k}
naturally involves coefficients
(c_{n,k})\in\ell^{2}
. However, to ensure that a boundary trace can be taken inside such an expansion (Lemma
3.1
), we need to impose an additional decay condition on
(c_{n,k})
. To this end, we say that a sequence
(c_{n,k})
belongs to the weighted
\ell_{a_{n,k}}^{1}
space if

\sum_{n,k}\lvert c_{n,k}\rvert a_{n,k}<\infty,

and we further define

\mathcal{A}=\bigl\{\,f\in W\mid(c_{n,k})\in\ell^{1}_{a_{n,k}}\text{ where }c_{n,k}=\langle f,\psi_{n,k}\rangle_{L^{2}(B)}\,\bigr\}.

This leads to our main result on stability.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in C_{+}(\overline{B})
. Assume
(\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2})\in\mathcal{A}
and for
c_{n,k}=\langle\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2},\psi_{n,k}\rangle_{L^{2}(B)}
assume there exists
\epsilon>0
such that

\sum_{k}\lvert c_{n,k}\rvert^{2}\leq\frac{\inf_{k}\lvert g_{n,k}(1)\rvert^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\Bigl\lvert\sum_{k}c_{n,k}\Bigr\rvert^{2}.

(1.2)

Then there is Lipschitz stability in
B
,

\lVert\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}(B)}\leq\epsilon^{-1}\lvert\partial B\rvert^{1/2}C_{\partial B}\lVert\Lambda(\gamma_{1})-\Lambda(\gamma_{2})\rVert_{\mathscr{L}(H^{1/2}(\partial B),H^{-1/2}(\partial B))},

with
\lvert\partial B\rvert
being the surface measure of
\partial B
and
C_{\partial B}
the constant from Theorem
1.1
.

Proof.

The proof is given in Section
3
.
∎

The condition (
1.2
) is equivalent to

\sum_{k,k^{\prime}\text{ with }k\neq k^{\prime}}\mathop{\textup{Re}}(c_{n,k}\overline{c_{n,k^{\prime}}})\geq\Bigl(\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{\inf_{k}\lvert g_{n,k}(1)\rvert^{2}}-1\Bigr)\sum_{k}\lvert c_{n,k}\rvert^{2},

and is a peculiar condition: for any fixed
n
, not too much cancellation is allowed for the mixed
k
-indices, indicating a limited freedom in the behavior of
\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}
in the radial direction. As an example, if the coefficients
c_{n,k}
are real and their signs only depend on
n
(ignoring vanishing coefficients), then the condition is guaranteed to hold with

\epsilon=\inf_{n,k}\lvert g_{n,k}(1)\rvert.

Moreover, in
d=2
spatial dimensions such examples of Lipschitz stability can be transferred to a general bounded simply-connected
C^{1,\alpha}
domain by using the Kellogg–Warschawski theorem (see, e.g.,
[
25
]
for details in a linearized setting).

Condition (
1.2
) simplifies when nonzero coefficients are only allowed for a fixed
k
. Let

W_{k}=\overline{\mathop{\textup{span}}\{\psi_{n,k}\}_{n}}

and define

\mathcal{A}_{k}=\bigl\{\,f\in W_{k}\mid(c_{n})\in\ell^{1}_{a_{n,k}}\text{ where }c_{n}=\langle f,\psi_{n,k}\rangle_{L^{2}(B)}\,\bigr\}.

If
(\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2})\in\mathcal{A}_{k}
then (
1.2
) is replaced with

\lvert g_{n,k}(1)\rvert\geq\epsilon,

which holds with
\epsilon=\inf_{n}\lvert g_{n,k}(1)\rvert
. This is summarized in the following corollary that provides Lipschitz stability for conductivity changes in infinite-dimensional subspaces of
L^{2}(B)
.

Corollary 1.7
.

Let
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in C_{+}(\overline{B})
. If
(\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2})\in\mathcal{A}_{k}
there is Lipschitz stability in
B
,

\lVert\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}(B)}\leq\frac{\lvert\partial B\rvert^{1/2}}{\inf_{n}\lvert g_{n,k}(1)\rvert}C_{\partial B}\lVert\Lambda(\gamma_{1})-\Lambda(\gamma_{2})\rVert_{\mathscr{L}(H^{1/2}(\partial B),H^{-1/2}(\partial B))}.

In particular, if
(\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2})\in\cup_{k}\mathcal{A}_{k}
, one can use the common stability constant

\frac{\lvert\partial B\rvert^{1/2}}{\inf_{n,k}\lvert g_{n,k}(1)\rvert}C_{\partial B}.

Once again, we could have

\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{0}+\widetilde{\gamma}_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad\gamma_{2}=\gamma_{0}+\widetilde{\gamma}_{2},

where, e.g.,
\widetilde{\gamma}_{1},\widetilde{\gamma}_{2}\in\mathcal{A}_{k}\cap C(\overline{B})
for some
k
, and
\gamma_{0}
is a large enough
C_{+}(\overline{B})
function to ensure that the conductivities are bounded away from zero.

Concluding that Theorem
1.6
and Corollary
1.7
are actually applicable requires concrete examples where Assumption
1.4
is satisfied. This is tackled in Section
2
where we first derive an orthonormal Zernike basis for
L^{2}(B)
in any spatial dimension
d\geq 2
, and subsequently verify that such a basis satisfies Assumption
1.4
. Sections
2.4
and
2.5
are dedicated to the Zernike basis functions in two and three spatial dimensions, which are the most relevant settings for the Calderón problem. We moreover give several fundamental properties of the Zernike basis in Theorem
2.2
.

1.2.
Earlier stability results

For very general infinite-dimensional classes of conductivities the Calderón problem exhibits conditional logarithmic stability
[
5
]
, and this is optimal for such general settings
[
34
,
31
]
. For measurements on a subset of the domain boundary, only double-logarithmic stability has been proved
[
29
]
, unless the conductivities are known near the domain boundary, in which case it is possible to again obtain logarithmic stability
[
7
]
. However, we emphasize that for smaller classes of conductivities the stability can be improved and there is no restriction why such classes cannot still be infinite-dimensional, as observed in this paper. To our knowledge, Lipschitz stability has not previously been documented for infinite-dimensional classes of conductivities for the nonlinear Calderón problem. Note that Lipschitz stability, instead of, e.g., logarithmic stability, is also important for convergence guarantees in reconstruction using iterative methods (see, e.g.,
[
30
]
).

In a linearized problem for the spatial dimension
d=2
, we previously proved that there are infinite-dimensional function spaces of perturbations (also expanded in a Zernike basis) for which there is Lipschitz stability
[
25
]
. However, this stability was with respect to a Hilbert–Schmidt norm for the linearized forward map (based on a Neumann-to-Dirichlet map), which prevented generalizations to
d>2
. It was at that time unclear if such results could be carried over to the nonlinear problem, in particular for the operator norm and any spatial dimension
d\geq 2
. On the other hand, the stability results for the linearized problem do not require the additional decay condition for the basis coefficients.

For finite-dimensional classes of conductivities there have been many results on Lipschitz stability. A non-exhaustive list of such papers include
[
27
,
28
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
1
,
8
,
6
,
13
]
. We emphasize that in many such cases it is also possible to reduce the number of measurements from a Dirichlet-to-Neumann or Neumann-to-Dirichlet map to just a finite set of boundary measurements.

For the related easier problem of inclusion detection, where the support of a perturbation is reconstructed rather than the conductivity values, Lipschitz stability has been proved in quite general settings of polygonal, polyhedral, and small volume fraction inclusions
[
26
,
10
,
14
,
15
,
23
]
. We emphasize, in particular, the recent paper
[
26
]
for Lipschitz stability in the determination of a polygonal inclusion from just two boundary measurements.

2.
Zernike basis

2.1.
Spherical harmonics

As a preparation to the introduction of the Zernike basis, we recall some facts about spherical harmonics; see
[
12
, Chapter 5]
and
[
22
]
for additional insights.

A homogeneous polynomial
p\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
of degree
\ell
satisfies
p(\lambda x)=\lambda^{\ell}p(x)
for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, or equivalently

p(x)=\sum_{|\alpha|=\ell}c_{\alpha}x^{\alpha},

for coefficients
c_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{C}
with
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
. We use multi-index notation, i.e. denote
x^{\alpha}=\prod_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}
and
\lvert\alpha\rvert=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}
for
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
. Let

\mathcal{P}_{\ell}=\Bigl\{\,p\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}\mid p(x)=\sum_{|\alpha|=\ell}c_{\alpha}x^{\alpha},\;c_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{C},\;\Delta p=0\,\Bigr\}

be the space of
harmonic
homogeneous polynomials of degree
\ell
.

The space of spherical harmonics of degree
\ell
is defined as the restriction of the harmonic homogeneous polynomials of degree
\ell
to the unit sphere:

\mathcal{H}_{\ell}=\{\,p|_{\partial B}\mid p\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}\,\}.

We have

\dim\mathcal{H}_{\ell}=\dim\mathcal{P}_{\ell}=\binom{\ell+d-1}{d-1}-\binom{\ell+d-3}{d-1},

(2.1)

with the convention that
\binom{m}{k}=0
for nonnegative integers
m<k
.

The
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
-spaces are the eigenspaces for the Laplace–Beltrami operator
\Delta_{\partial B}
, with

\Delta_{\partial B}f=-\ell(\ell+d-2)f,\quad f\in\mathcal{H}_{\ell}.

(2.2)

Hence, there is the following orthogonal decomposition:

L^{2}(\partial B)=\bigoplus_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}_{\ell}.

Let
\{f_{\ell,m}\}_{m\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}}
be an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
; in particular, the index set
\mathcal{I}_{\ell}
has the cardinality
\dim\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
. Thereby,
\{f_{\ell,m}\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0},m\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}}
is an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}(\partial B)
.

We will need the following bound in
L^{\infty}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

For
f\in\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
, there is the sharp bound

\lVert f\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\partial B)}\leq\Bigl(\frac{\dim\mathcal{H}_{\ell}}{\lvert\partial B\rvert}\Bigr)^{1/2}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial B)}.

Proof.

The proof is given in Appendix
A
.
∎

2.2.
Constructing the Zernike basis

Consider the
d
-dimensional Zernike operator

Z=-\Delta+(x\cdot\nabla)^{2}+d(x\cdot\nabla)

in the unit ball domain
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, generalized from the two-dimensional case as originally considered in
[
39
, Section 6]
. Here
x\cdot\nabla
denotes the differential operator

x\cdot\nabla=\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}.

The
d
-dimensional Zernike basis for
L^{2}(B)
is comprised of normalized eigenfunctions for
Z
, i.e. we consider the eigenvalue problem

Z\psi=\mu\psi.

Despite the fact that Zernike bases can be introduced for any
d\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\}
, they are mostly unexplored for
d>2
. For
d=3
see, e.g.,
[
24
,
35
]
. We construct the Zernike basis for a general spatial dimension
d\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\}
.

In polar coordinates the eigenvalue problem takes the form

\Bigl(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r^{2}}+\frac{d-1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}+\frac{1}{r^{2}}\Delta_{\partial B}\Bigr)\psi-\Bigl(r\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\Bigr)^{2}\psi-dr\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\psi=-\mu\psi.

(2.3)

Using separation of variables with
\psi(r,\theta)=R(r)\Theta(\theta)
, we may expand
\Theta
in the orthonormal spherical harmonics basis
\{f_{\ell,m}\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0},m\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}}
for
L^{2}(\partial B)
. Since
f_{\ell,m}
is an eigenfunction for
\Delta_{\partial B}
with the eigenvalue

\lambda_{\ell}=-\ell(\ell+d-2),

this approach reduces (
2.3
) to an
\ell
-dependent radial problem on the interval
(0,1
). After multiplying with
r^{d-1}
, one ends up with the equation

r^{d-1}(1-r^{2})\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}r^{2}}R_{\ell}+\bigl[(d-1)r^{d-2}-(d+1)r^{d}\bigr]\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}R_{\ell}+\lambda_{\ell}r^{d-3}R_{\ell}=-\mu r^{d-1}R_{\ell}.

(2.4)

Rearranging the terms leads to a singular (at
r=0
and
r=1
) Sturm–Liouville eigenvalue problem

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\Bigl[r^{d-1}(1-r^{2})\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}R_{\ell}\Bigr]+\lambda_{\ell}r^{d-3}R_{\ell}=-\mu r^{d-1}R_{\ell},

(2.5)

which is self-adjoint, with eigenvalue
\mu
and weight-function
r^{d-1}
. By virtue of Sturm–Liouville theory, this entails that for each
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, there are discrete real eigenvalues
\{\mu_{\ell,k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, and one can construct an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}_{r^{d-1}}([0,1])
from the corresponding eigenfunctions for (
2.5
).

We will first determine certain bounded orthonormal eigenfunctions
\{R_{\ell,k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
for (
2.5
), without worrying about whether we find all the orthogonal eigenfunctions. Subsequently, we will prove that the constructed
\{R_{\ell,k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
indeed forms an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}_{r^{d-1}}([0,1])
. As
\{f_{\ell,m}\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0},m\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}}
is an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}(\partial B)
, then

\psi_{\ell,m,k}(r\theta)=R_{\ell,k}(r)f_{\ell,m}(\theta),\quad\ell,k\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\enskip m\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}

becomes an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}(B)
.

We may solve (
2.4
), and thus (
2.5
), with the following strategy. Consider the hypergeometric differential equation, with parameters
a
,
b
, and
c
, on the interval
(0,1)
,

z(1-z)\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}z^{2}}w+[c-(a+b+1)z]\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}z}w-abw=0,

(2.6)

which admits a solution given by the Gauss hypergeometric function

w(z)=\mathop{{}_{2}F_{1}}(a,b;c;z),

assuming
c
is not a nonpositive integer. It is known that linear second order ordinary differential equations with three regular singularities, e.g., at
0
,
1
, and
\infty
, can be transformed to a hypergeometric differential equation. By making the change of variable
z=r^{2}
, and
h(r)=w(r^{2})
, we transform (
2.6
) into

(1-r^{2})\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}r^{2}}h+\Bigl[\frac{2c-1}{r}-2\Bigl(a+b+\frac{1}{2}\Bigr)r\Bigr]\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}h-4abh=0.

(2.7)

By inserting

R(r)=r^{\ell}h(r)=r^{\ell}w(r^{2})

(2.8)

into (
2.7
) and multiplying the equation with
r^{\ell+d-1}
, we arrive at

\displaystyle r^{d-1}(1-r^{2})\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}r^{2}}R+[(2c-2\ell-1)r^{d-2}

\displaystyle-(2a+2b-2\ell+1)r^{d}]\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}R

\displaystyle-\ell(2c-\ell-2)r^{d-3}R=-(\ell-2a)(2b-\ell)r^{d-1}R.

(2.9)

For the coefficients of (
2.4
) and (
2.9
) to match, we must have

\displaystyle 2c-2\ell

\displaystyle=d,

\displaystyle 2a+2b-2\ell

\displaystyle=d,

\displaystyle\ell(2c-\ell)

\displaystyle=\ell(\ell+d),

\displaystyle(\ell-2a)(2b-\ell)

\displaystyle=\mu,

where the first condition makes the third one redundant. In consequence,

c=a+b=\ell+\tfrac{d}{2}

and

\mu=-4ab+2(a+b)\ell-\ell^{2}=-4ab+\ell^{2}+d\ell.

Since
c=a+b
, it follows from
[
33
, §15.4(ii) Eq. 15.4.21 and Eq. 15.4.24]
that we must insist that

a=-k,\quad k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

as we aim for bounded eigenfunctions and thus want to avoid a logarithmic singularity in the hypergeometric function at
z=1
. Thus, we have

b=c-a=\ell+k+\tfrac{d}{2},

and the eigenvalues take the form

\mu_{\ell,k}=-4(-k)(\ell+k+\tfrac{d}{2})+\ell^{2}+d\ell=(\ell+2k)(\ell+2k+d).

Altogether, with a normalization constant
C_{\ell,k}
, we have constructed the orthonormal eigenfunctions

R_{\ell,k}(r)=C_{\ell,k}r^{\ell}\mathop{{}_{2}F_{1}}(-k,\ell+k+\tfrac{d}{2};\ell+\tfrac{d}{2};r^{2}),\quad k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

(2.10)

in
L^{2}_{r^{d-1}}([0,1])
for the Sturm–Liouville problem (
2.5
), as the eigenfunctions correspond to different eigenvalues. It follows from
[
33
, §15.2(ii) Eq. 15.2.4]
that
R_{\ell,k}
is a polynomial of degree
\ell+2k
, which we call a
radial Zernike polynomial
,

R_{\ell,k}(r)=C_{\ell,k}\sum_{q=0}^{k}(-1)^{q}\binom{k}{q}\frac{(\ell+k+\tfrac{d}{2})_{q}}{(\ell+\tfrac{d}{2})_{q}}r^{\ell+2q},

(2.11)

written in terms of Pochhammer symbols (rising factorials).

We have collected a number of fundamental properties of the radial Zernike polynomials below.

Theorem 2.2
.

(i)

For each
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
\{R_{\ell,k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}_{r^{d-1}}([0,1])
.

(ii)

The normalizing constant is

C_{\ell,k}=(-1)^{k}\sqrt{2\ell+4k+d}\binom{\ell+k+\tfrac{d-2}{2}}{k},

where
(-1)^{k}
ensures that
R_{\ell,k}(1)
is positive, with the function value

R_{\ell,k}(1)=\sqrt{2\ell+4k+d}.

This choice of sign is assumed for the properties
(iii)
and

(\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2})|_{\partial B}=\sum_{n}\Bigl(\sum_{k}c_{n,k}g_{n,k}(1)\Bigr)f_{n}.

Proof of Theorem
2.2

(ii)

For a fixed

\displaystyle F_{k}(r)

(b-1)\mathop{{}_{2}F_{1}}(a+1,b;c;z)=(b-a-2)\mathop{{}_{2}F_{1}}(a+1,b-1;c;z)+(a+1)\mathop{{}_{2}F_{1}}(a+2,b-1;c;z),

Appendix A
Bounds on spherical harmonics

We prove Proposition
2.1
, that for
f\in\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
there is the sharp bound

\lVert f\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\partial B)}\leq\Bigl(\frac{\dim\mathcal{H}_{\ell}}{\lvert\partial B\rvert}\Bigr)^{1/2}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial B)}.

The result can also be found in lecture notes by Paul Garrett, University of Minnesota. However since we have no reference to the result in a permanent archive, we give our adaptation of the proof here for the sake of completion.

Let
\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle
be the
L^{2}(\partial B)
inner product. Since
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
is finite-dimensional and comprised of bounded continuous functions, the norms
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\partial B)}
and
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial B)}
are equivalent on
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
. Hence, for a fixed
x\in\partial B
, the mapping
f\mapsto f(x)
is a linear and bounded functional on
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
in the topology of
L^{2}(\partial B)
. By Riesz’ representation theorem, for each
x\in\partial B
there exists a unique
v_{x}\in\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
such that

f(x)=\langle f,v_{x}\rangle,\quad f\in\mathcal{H}_{\ell}.

Note that
\Delta_{\partial B}
commutes with orthogonal coordinate transformations. So for any orthogonal matrix
U\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, the mapping
f\mapsto f\circ U
is unitary on
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
with adjoint
f\mapsto f\circ U^{-1}
. Hence,

\langle f,v_{Ux}\rangle=f\circ U(x)=\langle f\circ U,v_{x}\rangle=\langle f,v_{x}\circ U^{-1}\rangle,\quad f\in\mathcal{H}_{\ell},

which gives the identification

v_{Ux}=v_{x}\circ U^{-1}.

(A.1)

In particular,

v_{x}(z)=\langle v_{x},v_{z}\rangle=\langle v_{x}\circ U^{-1},v_{z}\circ U^{-1}\rangle=\langle v_{Ux},v_{Uz}\rangle=v_{Ux}(Uz),

(A.2)

which demonstrates that
x\mapsto v_{x}(x)
is a constant function that we denote as

V=v_{x}(x),\quad x\in\partial B.

Indeed, for any
x,y\in\partial B
, choosing in (
A.2
)
z=x
and an orthogonal matrix
U
such that
y=Ux
gives
v_{x}(x)=v_{y}(y)
. An example of such a matrix is

U=\begin{pmatrix}y&\widetilde{y}_{2}&\cdots&\widetilde{y}_{d}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x&\widetilde{x}_{2}&\cdots&\widetilde{x}_{d}\end{pmatrix}^{\textup{T}},

where
\{\widetilde{y}_{i}\}_{i=2}^{d}
is an orthonormal basis for
\mathop{\textup{span}}\{y\}^{\perp}
, and
\{\widetilde{x}_{i}\}_{i=2}^{d}
is an orthonormal basis for
\mathop{\textup{span}}\{x\}^{\perp}
.

Next we will show that

V=\lVert v_{x}\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial B)}^{2}=\lVert v_{x}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\partial B)}

(A.3)

for any
x\in\partial B
. The first equality follows from the definition of
v_{x}
by setting
f=v_{x}
. To deduce the second equality, we write

\displaystyle\lvert v_{x}(y)\rvert

\displaystyle=\lvert\langle v_{x},v_{y}\rangle\rvert\leq\lVert v_{x}\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial B)}\lVert v_{y}\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial B)}

\displaystyle=\lVert v_{x}\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial B)}\lVert v_{x}\circ U^{-1}\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial B)}=\lVert v_{x}\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial B)}^{2}=V,

(A.4)

where we again used an orthogonal matrix
U
satisfying
y=Ux
together with (
A.1
). As the upper bound in (
A.4
) is attained when
y=x
, we have proved (
A.3
).

Finally, we expand
v_{x}
in the finite orthonormal basis
\{f_{\ell,m}\}_{m\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}}
,

v_{x}=\sum_{m\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}}\langle v_{x},f_{\ell,m}\rangle f_{\ell,m}=\sum_{m\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}}\overline{f_{\ell,m}(x)}f_{\ell,m},

which evaluated at
x
gives

V=\sum_{m\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}}\lvert f_{\ell,m}(x)\rvert^{2}

for any
x\in\partial B
. Integrating this expression results in

V\lvert\partial B\rvert=\sum_{m\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}}\lVert f_{\ell,m}\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial B)}^{2}=\dim\mathcal{H}_{\ell}.

(A.5)

Using (
A.3
) and (
A.5
) proves the proposition statement,

\displaystyle\lVert f\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\partial B)}

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in\partial B}\lvert\langle f,v_{x}\rangle\rvert\leq\sup_{x\in\partial B}\lVert v_{x}\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial B)}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial B)}

\displaystyle=V^{1/2}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial B)}=\Bigl(\frac{\dim\mathcal{H}_{\ell}}{\lvert\partial B\rvert}\Bigr)^{1/2}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial B)}.

Moreover, (
A.3
) shows that the bound is sharp by choosing
f=v_{x}
for any
x\in\partial B
.
