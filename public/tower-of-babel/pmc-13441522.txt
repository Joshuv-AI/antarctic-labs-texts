The Role of Indocyanine Green Videoangiography in Intramedullary Spinal Cord Tumor Surgery: Focus on Lesion Localization Accuracy
Authors: Jungbo SIM, Chang-Hyun LEE, Ayoung YOO, Yunhee CHOI, Woojin KIM, Hosung MYEONG, Hangeul PARK, Jun-Hoe KIM, Chi Heon KIM, Chun Kee CHUNG
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Intramedullary spinal cord tumors/lesions are rare, but they present significant surgical challenges owing to the high risk of spinal cord injury during myelotomy. Conventional techniques are often limited by positional discrepancies and anatomical variations. Indocyanine green videoangiography provides real-time fluorescence imaging of the lesion vasculature. This study aimed to evaluate the safety and efficacy of intraoperative indocyanine green videoangiography and to determine which pathological subtypes are most amenable to this technique. We enrolled patients with intramedullary spinal cord tumors, excluding those with hemangioblastoma. A 2-step approach was used for lesion localization: initial delineation using conventional methods, followed by indocyanine green videoangiography, in which indocyanine green was administered intravenously, and lesion boundaries were re-marked using fluorescence imaging. The outcome measure was the prediction error distance between conventional localization and indocyanine green videoangiography localization, and the impact of lesion characteristics on localization accuracy. This study included 15 patients with intramedullary spinal cord tumors. The mean prediction error distance was 3.63 mm for the conventional method and 2.77 mm for indocyanine green videoangiography. The mean prediction error distance difference between the 2 methods was 0.87 mm (p = 0.047). Tumor size correlated with greater reductions in prediction error distance using indocyanine green videoangiography (height, p = 0.005). Larger tumors (23.4 mm in height) tended to demonstrate a mean prediction error distance reduction of 2.0 mm (p = 0.085). Minor adverse events that could be related to indocyanine green videoangiography were observed in 3 cases, all of which resolved spontaneously. Intraoperative indocyanine green videoangiography performed before myelotomy may improve the accuracy of tumor localization with minimal risk in patients with intramedullary spinal cord tumors/lesions. It might be particularly effective at minimizing errors in large intramedullary spinal cord tumors. By reducing unnecessary myelotomy, indocyanine green videoangiography may improve surgical outcomes.

Introduction

Intramedullary spinal cord tumors/lesions (IMSCTs) are rare, but they present a critical neurosurgical challenge because of high surgical risks.1-3) Despite advances in surgical techniques and instruments, imaging, and intraoperative neuromonitoring, there remains a significant risk of postoperative functional deterioration and residual tumor manifestation.4) Precise identification of lesion boundaries is essential for achieving gross total resection while avoiding spinal cord injury.2,5) Given that the length of myelotomy directly influences postoperative outcomes, even minor errors in lesion localization may result in unnecessary neurological deficits.6,7) The most widely used approach for lesion localization is based on preoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). This method estimates the lesion's location during surgery by identifying its relative position to the vertebral bodies based on preoperative MRI. This approach is prone to inaccuracies from surgeon error or positional discrepancy between imaging and surgery when identifying vertebral levels during surgery. Preoperative MRI is performed with the patient in the supine position, whereas surgery is conducted in the prone position, potentially resulting in misalignments of up to one-third of a vertebral body.8,9) These challenges become more pronounced for smaller lesions, where precise localization is critical to minimizing unnecessary myelotomy.4)

Near-infrared indocyanine green videoangiography (ICG-VA) has emerged as a promising intraoperative tool that enhances surgical outcomes by providing real-time fluorescence imaging of the target anatomy.2,10,11) Visualization of vascular anatomy using ICG-VA facilitates effective management of vascular pathologies such as arteriovenous fistulas or spinal hemangioblastomas.12-18) However, for solid tumors such as ependymoma, cavernous angioma, and astrocytoma, the utility and safety of ICG-VA remain insufficiently supported by evidence.2,10,15,19) There is a paucity of research identifying which tumor characteristics influence the utility of ICG-VA.15) The application of ICG-VA in solid IMSCT lesions remains underexplored, and its role in reducing complications and improving surgical outcomes warrants further investigation.

This study primarily aimed to assess whether ICG-VA enhances the accuracy of lesion localization and, secondarily, to identify tumor characteristics that influence delineation precision in IMSCTs.

Materials and Methods

This prospective cohort study was conducted at a single institution from 2021 to 2024. Participants were recruited based on predefined inclusion criteria as follows: (i) age ≥19 years, (ii) need for surgical removal of an IMSCT based on MRI findings, and (iii) informed consent for both the surgery and the use of ICG fluorescence imaging. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) refusal of surgery, (ii) diagnosis of hemangioblastoma, (iii) no lesion visualization using ICG-VA, (iv) presence of comorbidities such as neuromuscular diseases or spinal myelopathy, and (v) history of adverse reactions to ICG. In this study, we utilized an established surgical technique for IMSCT removal. After laminotomy and dural and arachnoid opening, the dura was retracted with 4-0 black silk sutures. Before performing the myelotomy, tumor margins were marked on the spinal cord surface using a sterile paper ruler, guided by anatomical landmarks identified from preoperative MRI. Subsequently, 12.5 mg of ICG was administered intravenously, and intraoperative ICG-VA was used to visualize vascular structures associated with the lesion. We used operating microscopes, including the Pentero 900 and Kinevo (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany), equipped with the INFRARED 800 indocyanine green fluorescence module and FLOW 800 hemodynamic analysis software. Fluorescence-enhanced lesion boundaries were then precisely marked using surgical instruments for subsequent comparison. In most cases, a posterior midline myelotomy was performed through the posterior median sulcus. After complete resection of the IMSCTs, the true tumor extent was documented. Photographic records taken at each step (conventional marking, fluorescence-based marking, and actual lesion boundary post-resection) were aligned using dura-anchoring suture knots as consistent anatomical landmarks to calculate prediction error distances (PEDs) between the actual lesion location and 2 predicted positions in both the cranial and caudal directions. The primary outcome was the mean difference in PEDs (MD-PEDs) between the conventional (MRI-based) and ICG-VA-based measurements. A paired sample t-test was used to compare MD-PED between ICG-VA and the conventional method for the whole dataset. Additionally, subgroup analyses were categorized according to lesion characteristics known to affect ICG visualization in prior studies.15,20) Correlation analysis was performed to identify statistically significant associations between MD-PED and presumed variables. For variables with statistically significant correlations, patients were divided into 2 groups based on each independent variable, and MD-PED results were compared. The cutoff values for continuous variables were estimated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. An independent t-test was used to analyze MD-PEDs. We monitored and documented any adverse events (AEs), including skin reactions, angioedema, anaphylaxis, hemodynamic instability, hyperthermia, and other unforeseen complications. All data were analyzed using appropriate statistical methods to compare primary and secondary outcomes. Continuous variables are presented as mean values with standard deviations (SDs), and categorical variables are presented as frequencies.

Effects were considered statistically significant if the 2-tailed p-value was <0.05. Results are presented as mean differences with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics version 21 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of our hospital (H-19 05-031-1034). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal representatives. This study adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

A total of 15 patients (9 males and 6 females) were enrolled during the study period, with a mean age of 47.87 ± 13.79 years (mean ± SD; Table 1). Tumor metrics demonstrated a mean height of 20.84 ± 18.30 mm and a mean volume of 1162.71 ± 1513.38 mm3. The mean lesion depth from the posterior median sulcus was 0.75 ± 0.65 mm. Pathological diagnoses included cavernous angiomas (46.7%), ependymomas (33.3%), astrocytomas (13.3%), and intramedullary schwannoma (6.7%). Lesion locations were evenly distributed between the cervical (53.3%) and thoracic (46.7%) regions. The mean PED values for the conventional and ICG-VA methods were 3.63 ± 2.66 mm and 2.77 ± 2.35 mm, respectively (Table 2). The MD-PED between these methods for the same patient was 0.87 ± 1.54 mm, and the difference was statistically significant (p = 0.047). This indicates that the conventional method produced a mean PED of 3.63 mm, which could be reduced by 0.87 mm by integrating ICG-VA results.

To identify factors that may affect the accuracy of tumor localization with ICG-VA, additional subgroup analyses were conducted based on factors such as lesion vascularity (pathological characteristics), lesion size, and depth from the spinal cord surface. Lesion metrics included width, thickness, height, and volume. Width was measured as the transverse (left-to-right) diameter, thickness as the anteroposterior diameter, and height was explicitly defined as the maximal craniocaudal length of the lesion measured on sagittal MRI sequences. For the nominal variable of pathological diagnosis, an independent t-test showed that MD-PED was 0.43 ± 1.72 mm for cavernous angiomas and 1.10 ± 1.24 mm for ependymomas, which did not show a significant difference (p = 0.475) (Table 2). For the remaining continuous variables, size (width, thickness, height, and volume) and depth from the posterior median sulcus, correlation analyses were carried out to examine their association with MD-PED. Lesion height significantly correlated with MD-PED (r = 0.561, p = 0.030), indicating that larger lesions demonstrated greater PED reductions with ICG-VA. The other variables, including lesion width (r = −0.085, p = 0.764), lesion thickness (r = −0.204, p = 0.465), lesion volume (r = 0.442, p = 0.099), and depth from the posterior median sulcus (r = −0.130, p = 0.644), did not significantly correlate with MD-PED.

A ROC curve analysis was conducted to determine the cutoff values for lesion height at which ICG-VA achieved effective PED reduction (Figure 3). To stratify patient groups based on lesion height, which showed a significant correlation with MD-PED, an ROC analysis was conducted to determine the optimal threshold value. Among patients with a PED ≥2 mm using the conventional method, those achieving a ≥2 mm reduction with ICG-VA were compared with those who did not. The area under the ROC curve for lesion height was 0.771 (95% CI, 0.67-1.00). Optimal cutoff values were determined to be a lesion height of 23.4 mm, yielding 60.0% sensitivity, 85.7% specificity, 80.8% positive predictive value, and 68.2% negative predictive value. These results indicate that IMSCTs surpassing these cutoff values were more likely to benefit from ICG-VA in reducing PEDs. Using the determined cutoff values, we compared MD-PED values between small and large IMSCTs using the independent samples t-test. MD-PEDs values for small 23.4 mm lesions were 0.45 ± 1.42 mm and 2.00 ± 1.41 mm, respectively (Table 2).

AEs associated with ICG administration were systematically monitored throughout the study. Among the 15 patients, 3 patients reported minor AEs, including dyspnea (grade 1), an itching sensation in the right upper chest, and diarrhea. These AEs resolved with either medications or observation. No major AEs, such as anaphylaxis or substantial hemodynamic changes, were recorded. Postoperative clinical outcomes, including motor power, pain, allodynia, and myelopathy, were evaluated, and no significant deterioration was observed during the 6-month follow-up period.

Discussion

The implementation of ICG-VA yielded a statistically significant decrease in PED by an average of 0.87 mm during IMSCT surgeries. Among lesion characteristics such as vascularity, size, and depth beneath the surface, lesion height significantly correlated with MD-PED between MRI-based localization and ICG-VA. Specifically, large tumors (≥23.4 mm in height) had a 77.1% likelihood of achieving a PED reduction of at least 2 mm with ICG-VA compared with that achieved by conventional techniques. The binary classification based on a lesion height cutoff of 23.4 mm did not yield a statistically significant difference in MD-PED between the 2 resulting groups. No substantial AEs related to ICG use were observed.

Our study demonstrated that the application of ICG-VA reduced the PED for tumor localization by an average of 0.87 mm compared with the conventional MRI-based method. The improvement in PED may appear numerically small, and its clinical significance could be questioned. However, given the eloquent and compact nature of the spinal cord, even submillimeter improvements in localization can be meaningful for preserving neural function. Furthermore, this benefit was more pronounced in large tumors (23.4 mm in height). In addition, a mathematically shorter myelotomy may not guarantee better functional outcomes, as sufficient exposure is often necessary to safely dissect an IMSCT to prevent spinal cord damage. However, our study showed that the value of ICG-VA lies in providing reliable tumor localization for the myelotomy. Even if the surgeon chooses to extend the opening for safety, knowing the precise location of the underlying lesion prevents starting the myelotomy too far cranially or caudally (mis-targeting). The precise localization provided by ICG-VA holds significant clinical value by eliminating the risk of unnecessary exploratory myelotomy, thereby allowing the surgeon to confidently initiate the opening at the optimal starting point and safely extend the exposure as planned.

Besides ICG-VA, several advanced techniques―including intraoperative ultrasound, neuronavigation, and fluorescein dye with polarization filters in surgical microscopes―have been introduced to improve the localization of IMSCTs. Ultrasonography is a widely accessible real-time imaging tool; however, its use in IMSCT surgery is limited by the deep and narrow surgical corridor and the relatively large size of the probe.21) Inserting additional marking tools is often impractical due to interference with the probe; thus, ultrasound is primarily used to confirm the presence or absence of a lesion rather than to guide precise localization.21) Five-aminolevulinic acid (5-ALA) has demonstrated strong fluorescence in spinal ependymomas; however, its effectiveness varies depending on tumor type.22) A recent meta-analysis involving 12 studies reported consistently high fluorescence rates in intramedullary ependymomas, whereas intramedullary astrocytomas showed more variable uptake.23) Neuronavigation based on preoperative MRI or computed tomography imaging, sometimes integrated with augmented reality, can assist in localizing the tumor level and planning dural opening. However, because these systems rely on static preoperative imaging, they cannot account for intraoperative spinal cord shift, and registration errors may increase after dural opening.24) ICG-VA provides real-time imaging without the registration errors often encountered with navigation systems, as it is performed directly under the surgical microscope and does not obstruct the surgeon's view, unlike ultrasound probes. In contrast to 5-ALA, which requires perioperative light protection (typically for 48 hours after oral administration) to prevent phototoxic reactions and is expensive, ICG requires no pretreatment and is considered safe and cost-effective.2)

It has not been directly established whether the ICG fluorescence boundary strictly corresponds to the actual tumor parenchyma. Nevertheless, given the well-known hypervascular nature of the peritumoral environment and the tendency for neovascularization to develop along the tumor surface, ICG fluorescence serves as a valuable indirect surrogate marker. This concept is further supported by previous literature demonstrating successful tumor delineation based on these vascular patterns.11,15,25) These studies also reported that because ICG-VA enables indirect delineation of tumor margins by visualizing the surrounding vasculature, IMSCTs that are hypervascular, large, and superficially located are more likely to be effectively visualized.2,15) In this study, lesions were categorized according to size, depth, and pathology. Regarding pathology, prior studies have shown that spinal ependymomas are well visualized with ICG-VA.2,15) In our study, the difference in PED reduction according to tumor pathology did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.475). This result may be partly attributed to the limited sample size (n = 15), which likely restricted the statistical power to detect subtle differences. However, prior studies have suggested that ICG visualization patterns vary significantly depending on vascular architecture. Endo et al.25) reported that in intramedullary cavernous malformations, the lesion margins could be identified as avascular areas surrounded by the cord vasculature. Similarly, Takami et al.15) observed that while highly vascularized tumors exhibited well-demarcated tumor stains, cavernous malformations were recognized as avascular areas on the dorsal spinal cord surface. These findings suggest that although the mechanism of visualization differs, positive staining in hypervascular tumors versus negative filling defects in cavernous angiomas, ICG-VA may provide localization cues for both pathologies. This dual utility may explain why the quantitative benefit in localization (PED reduction) was observed across different tumor types in our series, resulting in a lack of statistical disparity despite their distinct vascular features. With regard to the effect of tumor depth from the spinal cord surface, there is some discrepancy in the literature. One study demonstrated that ICG fluorescence can penetrate 10-20 mm into human tissue,20) and given that the spinal cord typically ranges from 6 to 13 mm in thickness, this suggests that IMSCTs may be visualized regardless of depth. However, another study reported that deep-seated or ventrally located tumors are difficult to visualize effectively.15) In our study, although lesion depth did not significantly affect MD-PED, it was subjectively more difficult to delineate the tumor margin in deeper lesions. One possible explanation is that bright superficial vessels on the spinal cord surface may obscure the delineation of deeper IMSCTs. Further large-scale studies are warranted to clarify this issue.

The size of IMSCTs can be evaluated using several parameters, including height, thickness, and calculated volume. Among these, lesion height was the only factor that demonstrated a significant association with MD-PED. The lack of correlation between volume and PED reduction may be due to the lesion's unique morphology and limitations in volume estimation. Another possible explanation for the lack of correlation is the unique shape of IMSCTs and the limitations of the formula used for volume calculation. Given the cylindrical shape of the spinal cord and IMSCTs, lesion thickness and width are often similar, making height the most variable and influential factor. Thus, lesion height may be the most significant factor affecting the accurate estimation of lesion volume. We calculated lesion volume using the simplified formula “(thickness × width × height) / 2,” which is a modified version of the standard 4/3πR3 formula. This approximation may reduce accuracy, particularly for irregularly shaped lesions. This could explain why volume may not correlate as strongly with PED reduction, as lesion height may be a more reliable measure in such anatomical contexts.

This study has several limitations that need to be considered. First, the small sample size (n = 15) may have limited our ability to detect significant differences in some analyses, particularly when comparing lesion pathologies or assessing minor clinical outcomes. With a limited number of participants, the statistical power to detect subtle but clinically meaningful differences is reduced, increasing the risk of a Type II error. Variability in patient characteristics and tumor behavior can have a disproportionate impact in small samples, potentially introducing selection bias or skewing the interpretation of outcomes. However, IMSCTs are a rare pathology, and the observed trends are consistent with prior literature. Further validation in larger, multicenter prospective studies is necessary to confirm our present findings and to better understand the factors influencing the efficacy of intraoperative visualization techniques. Second, lesion boundaries were initially marked using conventional methods, followed by ICG-VA-based marking, which does not allow for a completely independent comparison between the 2 approaches. However, in clinical practice, surgical decisions are rarely based on a single modality; instead, multimodal strategies are routinely employed to achieve clear tumor demarcation. Therefore, this approach may be more appropriately interpreted as a combination of conventional techniques and ICG-VA, reflecting real-world surgical conditions. We also recognize the possibility of measurement errors arising from morphological changes of the spinal cord, such as shrinkage or shift of the removal cavity. Although we made every effort to minimize measurement errors by utilizing multiple reference points, including dura-anchoring sutures, hemoclips, and surface vessels, measurement of length may still be affected by tissue deformation. Third, PED may not be directly correlated with clinical outcomes. A mean PED of 3.63 mm, as measured using conventional methods, does not mean that 3.63 mm of unnecessary myelotomy was performed. In clinical practice, surgeons usually initiate the myelotomy at the central 60%-70% of the predicted tumor location to account for potential localization errors. The incision is then gradually extended until the tumor margins become clearly visible. Therefore, the actual extent of unnecessary myelotomy may be smaller than the measured PED. Moreover, although the 0.87 mm reduction was statistically significant, the degree to which this could influence clinical outcomes remains undetermined. However, accurate tumor localization and minimization of unnecessary myelotomy are fundamental principles of spinal cord surgery. Given that ICG-VA adds minimal operative time and poses low risk, its use may be considered as a reasonable adjunct to enhance surgical precision. Fourth, although no substantial AEs were observed during the short-term postoperative period, the absence of long-term follow-up in this study limits the ability to evaluate sustained safety and broader clinical efficacy. However, ICG has a well-established safety profile, with a plasma half-life of approximately 3-4 minutes and exclusive hepatic excretion. Given these pharmacokinetic properties and the lack of ICG-related complications reported in prior literature, extended follow-up specifically for ICG-related AEs may not be necessary. Despite suggesting potential advantages for larger lesions, our study shows that future research is needed to determine the broader impact of ICG-VA on surgical decisions and long-term patient outcomes. Finally, regarding the limited representation of astrocytomas, we clarify that this was not due to intentional exclusion. Several eligible cases, including some astrocytomas, had to be excluded from the final analysis because tumor measurements were not recorded due to procedural oversight or technical problems during surgery. This unintentional loss of data resulted in a smaller sample size for this specific pathological subtype than initially anticipated.

Conclusions

Intraoperative ICG-VA prior to myelotomy may enable real-time visualization of IMSCTs beneath the spinal cord and improve the accuracy of lesion localization with minimal risk. It might be particularly effective at minimizing errors in large IMSCTs. By reducing unnecessary myelotomy, ICG-VA may improve surgical outcomes, supporting further investigation into its broader application in IMSCT treatment.
