Association of edentulism with incident non-communicable diseases among middle-aged and older adults: a population-based cohort study
Authors: Chengwei Duan, Weiping Wan, Lufei Wang, Haiqing Liao, Zeni Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEdentulism has been associated with multiple non-communicable diseases (NCDs). However, existing evidence from China is largely limited to specific high-risk regions or lacks national representativeness, and large-scale population-based prospective studies remain scarce. This study investigated the association between edentulism and the risk of developing NCDs in middle and older adults from China.MethodsWe examined the relationship between edentulism and NCDs in the CHARLS study, a large prospective cohort of 17,708 adults in China. Follow-up started from the baseline survey in 2011 and ended at the 2018 survey, and the primary endpoint was the first diagnosis of any NCD during follow-up. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) with adjustment for potential confounders.ResultsA total of 6,585 participants were included, with a mean follow-up of 6.46 years. Participants with edentulism had a higher risk of NCDs (HR = 1.17, 95% CI: 1.03–1.33), asthma (HR = 2.83, 95% CI: 1.38–5.79), hypertension (HR = 1.21, 95% CI: 1.01–1.45), and cancer (HR = 2.66, 95% CI: 1.46–4.84) than those who had teeth. No statistically significant associations between edentulism and other diseases were found. Stratified analyses showed effect modification by age for asthma and by sex for dyslipidemia.ConclusionsThe study from China found that edentulism was associated with NCDs, asthma, hypertension, and cancer, and also with the other cancers category, although this finding was unstable. No significant associations were found between edentulism and other individual NCDs or specific cancer types including breast, reproductive, and gastrointestinal cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09412-1.

Introduction

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) have emerged as the predominant health challenge in China, with cardiovascular disorders, malignancies, chronic respiratory conditions, and type 2 diabetes collectively accounting for 91% of total mortality in 2021 [1]. Characterized by multifactorial pathogenesis involving complex interactions between genetic predisposition, environmental exposures, and modifiable lifestyle factors, these conditions present substantial etiological challenges. Current evidence suggests that NCDs typically have unclear etiologies, and their pathogenesis involves complex factors such as genetics, environment, and lifestyle, which remain not fully understood to date [2].

Edentulism, the complete loss of permanent dentition, represents the terminal sequela of oral health deterioration with significant systemic implications. This irreversible condition arises from compounded biological and behavioral determinants, predominantly attributable to advanced dental caries progression and severe periodontal disease [3]. Recent epidemiological surveillance by the WHO reveals the global prevalence of edentulism among individuals aged 20 years and older was approximately 7% [4]. Furthermore, the prevalence increased notably with age, reaching an estimated 23% among individuals aged 60 years and older.

During recent decades, edentulism has been found to be associated with increased risk and higher mortality of numerous NCDs, including heart failure, stroke, cancer, and cardiovascular disease [5–13]. A cohort of 29,584 healthy adults from rural Linxian, China, provided evidence linking tooth loss to the incidence of several NCDs. Using data from this cohort, studies found that after nearly 10 years of follow-up, tooth loss substantially elevated the risk of all-cause mortality, as well as mortality from upper gastrointestinal cancer, cardiovascular disease, and stroke. Furthermore, individuals with the greatest degree of age-specific tooth loss had a higher risk of developing liver cancer after 30 years of follow-up [14–16].

However, previous Chinese studies have largely been conducted in Linxian, a high-risk region for esophageal cancer. Evidence of nationwide scale in China is scarce. Oral health deterioration, culminating in edentulism, may serve as a marker of systemic vulnerability in the aging process. A recent longitudinal study from the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing demonstrated that poor oral health, particularly tooth loss, was significantly associated with increased frailty risk among middle-aged and older adults, suggesting that the impact of poor oral health extends beyond local inflammatory conditions to systemic aging-related outcomes [17]. The current study aims to assess these associations using the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a longitudinal cohort drawn from approximately 450 villages or urban communities across 150 counties in 28 provinces, including 17,708 individuals from about 10,000 households.

Methods

Study population

The CHARLS is a nationally representative, population-based cohort encompassing community-dwelling residents aged 45 years and older, employing a multi-stage stratified probability sampling strategy. Detailed descriptions of the study design, methodology, and response rates have been previously published [18]. Multiple studies have confirmed the data stability of this database [19, 20]. In brief, the baseline survey recruited 17,708 participants. Trained interviewers collected data on participants’ demographic characteristics, health status, functional abilities, healthcare access and insurance, as well as work and retirement status [18]. This analysis utilized baseline data from 2011, along with data from three subsequent follow-up surveys conducted in 2013, 2015, and 2018. Participants were excluded based on the following criteria: (1) individuals aged < 45 years (N = 508), (2) missing baseline data on age or edentulism status (N = 261), (3) presence of NCDs at baseline (N = 9,751), and (4) missing NCDs data at the end of follow-up (N = 603). Ethical approval was granted by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of Peking University, and all participants provided written informed consent prior to study enrollment. This study was conducted in strict accordance with the ethical principles and standards for medical research involving human subjects as outlined in the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki.

Exposure and covariates

At baseline, information regarding edentulism was collected through a self-reported question by asking “Have you lost all of your teeth?” with possible responses: No (dentition) or Yes (edentulism).

The following baseline characteristics were selected as covariates for this analysis: sex was categorized as male or female. Age was grouped into middle-aged (45–64 years) and older (≥ 65 years). Place of residence was classified as urban or rural. Income level was dichotomized by the median annual household income in the analytical sample (¥23,710). Individuals below the median were categorized as low income, and those at or above the median as high income. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated from measured height and weight collected during the baseline survey. BMI was then categorized into four groups based on the World Health Organization classification for Asian populations: underweight (BMI < 18.5), normal weight (BMI 18.5–24.9), overweight (BMI 25.0–29.9), and obese (BMI ≥ 30.0) [21]. Categorization was used instead of continuous BMI to account for potential non-linear associations with NCDs and to enhance clinical interpretability. Smoking status was categorized as ever smoker (including current and former smokers) or never smoker. Alcohol consumption status was categorized as ever drinker (including current and former drinkers) or never drinker.

Study end point

The primary endpoint was the first occurrence of any NCD during follow-up, analyzed in the baseline healthy cohort, i.e., participants free of any NCD at baseline. Disease-specific outcomes were examined as secondary endpoints. The follow-up period started from the baseline survey in 2011 and ended at the completion of the 2018 wave of data collection. The time to NCDs onset was defined as the interval from baseline to the first documented diagnosis of an NCD. All participants entered the cohort at baseline with an entry time of zero, and no delayed entry was applied. Because NCDs status was assessed at discrete follow-up waves, the exact date of onset was not available. For participants who reported a new NCD at a given follow-up wave, the time to event was defined as the number of years from the baseline survey to that follow-up wave. This approach is consistent with standard practice in cohort studies with intermittent follow-up, where the precise time of an event is unknown and the time-to-event is thus estimated based on the follow-up wave at which it was first reported [22]. Participants who did not report any NCD by the end of the 2018 follow-up were censored. NCDs data were gathered using two parallel questions. The first question regarding NCDs in general, “Have you been diagnosed with any of the following conditions by a doctor?” Options for answer were hypertension, dyslipidemia, type 2 diabetes, cancer or malignant tumor, lung disease, heart disease, stroke, stomach or other digestive disease, asthma. The second question was only answered if the answer in the first question included “cancer”. The second question focused specifically on cancer: “In which organ or part of your body do you have cancer, including origins and metastasis of the tumor?” Cancers were classified according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10), and the options for answer included: 1. brain (C71), 2. oral cavity (C04), 3. larynx (C32), 4. pharynx (C10), 5. thyroid (C73), 6. lung (C34), 7. breast (C50), 8. esophagus (C15), 9. stomach (C16), 10. liver (C22), 11. pancreas (C25), 12. kidney (C64), 13. prostate (C61), 14. testicle (C62), 15. ovary (C56), 16. cervix (C53), 17. endometrium (C54), 18. colon or rectum (C18-C20), 19. bladder (C67), 20. skin (C44), 21. non-Hodgkin lymphoma (C82-C85), 22. leukemia (C91), 23. other organs. Considering the low number of cases, cancers were summarized into four groups: breast cancer (C50); reproductive cancer included cervical cancer (C53), ovarian cancer (C56), endometrial cancer (C54), and prostate cancer (C61); gastrointestinal cancer included colorectal cancer (C18-C20), gastric cancer (C16), liver cancer (C22), and pancreatic cancer (C25); other cancers category included lung (C34), kidney (C64), bladder (C67), thyroid (C73), brain (C71), larynx (C32), pharynx (C10), oral cavity (C04), skin (C44), non-Hodgkin lymphoma (C82-C85), and leukemia (C91). This aggregated category was created for statistical signal detection due to limited case numbers and should not be interpreted as a biologically homogeneous group.

Statistical analysis

The categorical data were analyzed using frequencies and percentages. Chi-square tests were performed to evaluate the balance of baseline characteristics between study groups. For categorical baseline variables, we calculated Cohen’s h as a measure of standardized difference, with values of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 considered small, medium, and large effects, respectively [23]. The time scale for all Cox proportional hazards models was years of follow-up from the baseline survey in 2011 to the first self-reported NCDs during any follow-up wave (2013, 2015, or 2018), or the last follow. Cox proportional hazards regression model was applied to estimate the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for edentulism and the incidence of NCDs, with adjustment for potential confounders, including age, sex, residence, smoking status, alcohol consumption status, education, BMI and income. The proportional hazards assumption was tested by Schoenfeld residuals test for each Cox model. All global tests and individual covariate tests yielded P > 0.05, indicating no violation of the proportional hazards assumption. Kaplan–Meier cumulative incidence curves were generated to visualize the time-to-event distributions for the primary and secondary outcomes that reached statistical significance. Differences in cumulative incidence between the edentulous and dentate groups were assessed using the two-sided log-rank test.

Missing values were handled using multiple imputation with 10 imputed datasets. Each dataset was analyzed independently, and the results were subsequently combined using Rubin’s rules to yield final estimates. To assess the robustness of our findings to potential unmeasured confounding, we calculated E-values for the main associations of interest. The E-value represents the minimum strength of association that an unmeasured confounder would need to have with both the exposure and the outcome, conditional on the measured covariates, to fully explain the observed association [24].

Stratified analyses by baseline age, income, sex, smoking status, and alcohol consumption status were conducted. Heterogeneity across strata were tested by interaction effect testing. Three sensitivity analyses were performed to strengthen the validity of the results. (1) We performed a sensitivity analysis using disease-specific risk datasets. For each outcome, only participants who reported having the specific disease at baseline were excluded, while all other participants, including those with other baseline NCDs, were retained. We then estimated the HR for the association between edentulism and each outcome using the same multivariable Cox proportional hazards models. (2) We conducted an analysis treating missing values of covariates as a separate category, to compare the results with those obtained after multiple imputation. (3) We performed analyses excluding subjects with follow-up times of ≤ 3 years to minimize reverse causation bias, ensuring that the observed associations reflected more stable, long-term patterns rather than short-term influences.

The number of events and incidence rates per 1,000 person-years for each outcome are shown in Table 2, stratified by edentulism status. In multivariable analyses adjusting for potential confounders, participants with edentulism had a higher risk of NCDs (HR = 1.17, 95% CI: 1.03–1.33), a 2.83 times higher risk of asthma (HR = 2.83, 95% CI: 1.38–5.79), and a significant association was also observed for hypertension (HR = 1.21, 95% CI: 1.01–1.45). Additionally, a marginally higher association was observed between edentulism and cancer incidence (HR = 2.66, 95% CI: 1.46–4.84). For the aggregated other cancers category, participants with edentulism had a 4.51 times higher risk (HR = 4.51, 95% CI: 1.72–11.81). This estimate was based on only six events in the edentulous group and 25 in the dentate group, and the wide confidence interval indicates substantial statistical uncertainty. Participants with edentulism also had slightly higher risks of stroke (HR = 1.96, 95% CI: 0.97–3.95), type 2 diabetes (HR = 1.19, 95% CI: 0.83–1.72), dyslipidemia (HR = 1.01, 95% CI: 0.88–1.37), and gastropathy (HR = 1.11, 95% CI: 0.88–1.37), but these associations were not statistically significant. For the association between edentulism and asthma, the E-value for the point estimate was 5.11, and the E-value for the lower confidence limit was 2.10. For the association between edentulism and cancer, the E-values were 4.76 for the point estimate and 2.28 for the lower confidence limit. Discussion

The present study identified significant associations between edentulism and NCDs, asthma, hypertension, and cancer within a nationally representative cohort of middle-aged and older Chinese adults. A notable but unstable association was also observed for the aggregated other cancers category. Given the limitations inherent in self-reported data and the single-time assessment of edentulism, these findings reflect statistical correlations rather than definitive causal relationships.

In our analysis, edentulism was significantly associated with cancer. This point estimate is higher than those reported in many previous studies [25, 26], a difference that may reflect the older age and predominantly rural composition of our study population, though direct comparisons should be made with caution due to heterogeneity in study designs and outcome definitions. Given the modest total number of cancer cases, this overall finding should be interpreted cautiously.

A notable association was observed for the other cancers category, which included malignancies of the lung, kidney, bladder, thyroid, brain, larynx, pharynx, oral cavity, skin, non-Hodgkin lymphoma, and leukemia. However, this category contained only 31 events, with wide confidence intervals indicating substantial statistical uncertainty. The association became non-significant in the sensitivity analysis excluding short follow-up times. This reflects data constraints inherent to this database, where cancer ascertainment relies on self-reported diagnosis and may be subject to underreporting. Moreover, this category was constructed to explore the statistical signal detection and does not represent a biologically meaningful grouping, therefore, no mechanistic interpretation could be drawn from this estimate. Additionally, the other cancers category includes malignancies of the respiratory tract and oral cavity, which have been most consistently linked to poor oral health in previous studies due to direct anatomical continuity and shared exposure to tobacco and alcohol.

Previous epidemiological studies have consistently reported associations between poor oral health and increased cancer risk. A recent meta-analysis of 26 cohort studies found that periodontal disease was associated with elevated risks of pancreatic, bladder, esophageal, colorectal, and other cancers category [27]. A U.S. nationally representative study reported a 1.50 times increased risk of total cancer mortality associated with edentulism [25]. Our findings are broadly consistent with these reports.

Several mechanisms have been proposed in the literature to explain similar findings, including chronic inflammation, microbial dysbiosis, and immune dysregulation. Tooth loss frequently stems from longstanding periodontitis and dental caries, which trigger persistent low-grade systemic inflammation and promote carcinogenesis [28, 29]. Periodontal pathogens may reach distant organs via the bloodstream or oral-gastrointestinal axis [30, 31], and immune dysregulation may foster an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [32]. Our data cannot verify any of these pathways. Given substantial residual confounding from unmeasured inflammation, nutrition, and comorbidities, these mechanistic discussions remain highly speculative and should not be interpreted as causal evidence. The observed associations may equally reflect systemic health deterioration and socioeconomic disadvantage rather than a direct pathogenic effect of edentulism.

The present findings are broadly consistent with previous studies linking edentulism to asthma [33–35], although effect sizes differ due to methodological heterogeneity. For example, a cross-sectional study reported a weaker association (OR = 1.1) [30], likely due to differences in study design and outcome ascertainment. From a pathophysiological perspective, edentulism could contribute to asthma pathogenesis through oral microbiota dysbiosis, respiratory pathogen translocation, and low-grade chronic inflammation [36–38]. However, reverse causation is also possible, as corticosteroid use in asthmatic patients has been associated with oral pathologies and subsequent tooth loss [34]. Thus, the association may be bidirectional, though further longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the temporal direction.

This study adjusted for several basic confounders including age, sex, income, residence, smoking, alcohol consumption, education, and BMI. However, residual confounding from unmeasured variables cannot be fully ruled out. Factors such as dietary patterns, physical activity, early-life socioeconomic status, and access to dental care were not measured and could potentially influence the observed associations. To quantify the robustness of our main findings to potential unmeasured confounding, we calculated E-values. For the association between edentulism and asthma, the E-value was 5.11, with a lower confidence limit of 2.10. For the association between edentulism and cancer, the E-values were 4.76 and 2.28. These E-values quantify the minimum strength of association that an unmeasured confounder would need to exhibit with both edentulism and the outcomes to fully explain the observed associations. However, E-value analysis cannot substitute for direct measurement of important confounders. Multiple moderate confounders acting jointly could still explain these results. Edentulism is closely associated with aging, frailty, poor nutritional status, chronic inflammation, limited healthcare access, and socioeconomic disadvantage. Dietary quality, oral hygiene behaviors, physical activity, dental care utilization, inflammatory biomarkers, medication use, and comorbidity burden were not measured in this study and could each contribute residual confounding. These factors are likely to cluster within individuals and act synergistically, making it difficult to isolate the independent effect of edentulism. Residual confounding remains a substantial possibility. Regarding the direction of bias from self-reported outcomes, edentulism is closely associated with advanced age, and older edentulous individuals may differ from dentate counterparts in health awareness, access to diagnostic services, and disease detection rates [35, 36]. This difference may introduce differential misclassification bias. On one hand, older edentulous individuals may have reduced healthcare seeking behavior and underdiagnosis of chronic conditions, biasing the observed association toward the null. On the other hand, edentulous individuals may have more frequent healthcare contact due to dental problems or prosthodontic treatment, leading to more opportunities for diagnosing other diseases and potentially overestimating the true association. In our study, the edentulism group had significantly higher proportions of low-income and rural residents, populations that typically have lower health service accessibility and health literacy, which may systematically lower their disease diagnosis rates. Therefore, our effect estimates may still be biased toward the null. Nevertheless, because we cannot directly measure individual-level healthcare seeking frequency or health awareness, the potential for bias in either direction cannot be fully excluded. In addition, edentulism, defined as complete loss of all natural teeth, is an extreme and readily observable oral condition, making recall bias and misclassification unlikely. Any remaining non-differential misclassification would bias hazard ratios toward the null, making our effect estimates conservative.

Stratified analyses revealed evidence of effect modification for asthma by age and for dyslipidemia by sex. The association between edentulism and asthma was stronger in middle-aged than in older adults, indicating that age is an important effect modifier for this outcome. One possible explanation is that age-related changes in immune function or oral microbiome composition may influence the inflammatory response to tooth loss [37, 38], though this hypothesis requires direct testing. For dyslipidemia, the association was positive in men and negative in women, suggesting a sex-specific effect. Sex differences in lipid metabolism, hormonal regulation, or health behaviors could contribute to this pattern [39, 40], but the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. No other interactions reached statistical significance. Given the multiple subgroup comparisons, chance findings were possible. The underlying reasons for these differences require further investigation in independent cohorts.

In the disease-specific risk dataset sensitivity analysis, the asthma association remained significant, whereas the cancer association was attenuated to non-significance. This attenuation may reflect the inclusion of participants with other baseline NCDs, conditions that are themselves linked to cancer risk and could partly account for the observed edentulism-cancer relationship. The primary analysis, confined to a healthier baseline cohort free of any NCD, likely provides a less confounded estimate of the association. Both analytical approaches yielded directionally consistent results. This pattern indicates that the asthma finding was more stable across different analytical scenarios, whereas the cancer association was sensitive to the composition of the study population and the exclusion of short follow-up times. Accordingly, the cancer findings should be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than robust evidence.

This study benefits from several strengths, including its nationwide representativeness, prospective design, and extended follow-up duration. Rigorous quality control protocols including photographic validation, geolocation confirmation, audio consistency checks, telephone-based verification, and real-time coding during data collection, were implemented to guarantee data integrity. However, several limitations warrant consideration. First, both exposure and outcomes were self-reported. No formal validation of the edentulism question has been published for CHARLS, but a Norwegian study among older adults reported excellent agreement [41]. Second, edentulism was assessed only once at baseline. This single assessment does not capture the timing, duration, or cause of tooth loss (e.g., periodontal disease versus dental caries), nor does it account for denture use. Consequently, we cannot determine whether the observed associations reflect long-term cumulative exposure or acute effects, nor can we examine potential effect modification by the underlying cause of tooth loss. Third, outcome ascertainment relied on self-reported physician diagnosis, which may vary in accuracy across different NCDs. For example, self-reported hypertension and diabetes have high specificity but modest sensitivity in CHARLS [20], whereas self-reported cancer may be more accurate but still subject to recall bias. Furthermore, undiagnosed early-stage cancers are not captured by self-report, meaning that our cancer cases likely represent only clinically diagnosed, more advanced malignancies. This may lead to underestimation of cancer incidence and potentially bias the association between edentulism and cancer if the relationship differs by cancer stage. Fourth, the small number of cancer cases limited statistical power. The association for the other cancers category was not robust, being based on few events and losing significance after excluding short follow-up times. Thus, it should be considered hypothesis-generating only. Fifth, although CHARLS adopted a complex sampling design, we did not apply sampling weights in the primary analysis. This may limit the generalizability of our descriptive estimates to the national population, though it is unlikely to bias the etiologic associations.

Conclusions

This study identified significant associations between edentulism and NCDs, asthma, hypertension, and cancer within a nationally representative cohort of middle-aged and older Chinese adults. The observed associations may partly reflect shared risk factors and general health deterioration rather than a direct biological effect of edentulism. Future investigations incorporating objective, longitudinal measurements of tooth loss and disease status are essential to validate these associations and elucidate the underlying biological mechanisms.
