Abstract
. The homological shift algebra and the projective dimension function of complementary edge ideals are investigated. Let G G be a connected graph, and let I I be its complementary edge ideal. For bipartite graphs G G , we show that the projective dimension of I s I^{s} increases strictly with s s until reaching its maximum value. For trees and cycles, explicit expressions for the projective dimension of I s I^{s} are provided, along with precise formulas of their homological shift ideals. In particular, it is shown that the i i -th homological shift algebra of such ideals is generated in degree at most i i . We prove that if G G is a tree, then the homological shift ideal HS i ​ ( I i ) \mathrm{HS}_{i}(I^{i}) , when divided by a suitable monomial, becomes a Veronese-type ideal. Moreover, every Veronese-type ideal arises in this manner.



Introduction

Let

n

{\mathbb{K}}

For

[

that this equivalence extends to a characterization involving powers: for squarefree monomial ideals of degree 2,
I
has a linear resolution if and only if every

A monomial ideal

is the complementary edge ideal of a finite simple graph

Let

As noted earlier, the complementary edge ideal of a graph was independently introduced and studied by two research groups in 2025: Ficarra and Moradi
[
13
,
14

15

[

In addition to characterizing the conditions under which such ideals admit a linear resolution, the two groups independently established the sequentially Cohen-Macaulay, Cohen-Macaulay, and Gorenstein properties of these ideals. Furthermore, Ficarra and Moradi extended these findings by characterizing the nearly Gorenstein and matroidal classes of complementary edge ideals. Beyond structural characterizations, they computed key homological invariants of powers of these ideals including regularity, asymptotic depth, and the index of depth stability—partly by analyzing the Rees algebras of related ideals.

To study the syzygies and resolutions of monomial ideals, the notion of

Theorem 0.3

Let

1.
Preliminary

This section provides the definitions and basic facts used throughout the paper.
For a vector
{\mathbf{a}}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
, we set
{\mathbf{x}}^{{\mathbf{a}}}=x_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{a_{n}}
. For a subset
F\subseteq[n]
, we set
{\mathbf{x}}_{F}=\prod_{j\in F}x_{j}
. Given positive integers
j,k
with
j\leq k
, let
[j,k]
denote the set
\{j,j+1,\ldots,k\}
. In particular, if
j=1
,
[j,k]
is denoted by
[k]
.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
I\subset S
be a monomial ideal with minimal multigraded free
S
-resolution

\mathbb{F}\ \ :\ \ 0\rightarrow F_{p}\rightarrow F_{p-1}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow F_{0}\rightarrow I\rightarrow 0,

where
F_{i}=\bigoplus\limits_{j=1}^{\beta_{i}(I)}S(-{\mathbf{a}}_{i,j})
. The vectors
{\mathbf{a}}_{i,j}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
are called the
i
-th
multigraded shifts
of the resolution
\mathbb{F}
. The monomial ideal

\mbox{HS}_{i}(I)=\ ({\mathbf{x}}^{{\mathbf{a}}_{i,j}}:\ j=1,\dots,\beta_{i}(I))

is called the
i
-th
homological shift ideal
of
I
.

Note that
\mbox{HS}_{0}(I)=I
and
\mbox{HS}_{i}(I)=(0)
for
i<0
or
i>\operatorname{pd}(I)
. By definition, a monomial ideal has a
d
-linear resolution
if
\operatorname{HS}_{i}(I)
is either 0 or generated in degree
i+d
for all
i
.

Let
I
be a monomial ideal, and let
\mathcal{G}(I)
denote its unique minimal set of monomial generators. We say that
I
has
linear quotients
if there exists an ordering
u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}
of
\mathcal{G}(I)
such that the colon ideal
(u_{1},\ldots,u_{i-1}):(u_{i})
is generated by variables for all
i=2,\ldots,m
. By
[
29
, Lemma 2.1]
, we may assume without loss of generality that
\deg(u_{1})\leq\cdots\leq\deg(u_{m})
.

When
I
admits linear quotients, we define
\operatorname{set}(u_{1})=\emptyset
and

\operatorname{set}(u_{i})=\left\{\ell\ :\ x_{\ell}\in(u_{1},\ldots,u_{i-1}):(u_{i})\right\},\quad\text{for }i=2,\ldots,m.

By
[
27
, Lemma 1.5]
, it follows that

(1)

\operatorname{HS}_{i}(I)=\left({\mathbf{x}}_{F}u:\ u\in\mathcal{G}(I),\ F\subseteq\operatorname{set}(u),\ |F|=i\right),

where
{\mathbf{x}}_{F}=\prod_{j\in F}x_{j}
if
F\neq\emptyset
, and
{\mathbf{x}}_{\emptyset}=1
otherwise. From this formula, we deduce that the projective dimension of
I
satisfies

(2)

\mathrm{pd}(I)=\max\left\{|\operatorname{set}(u)|:\ u\in\mathcal{G}(I)\right\}.

Let
G
be a finite simple graph with vertex set
V(G)=\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
edge set
E(G)
. The
complementary edge ideal

I_{c}(G)
of
G
is defined as the following monomial ideal

I_{c}(G)=\left(\frac{{\mathbf{x}}_{[n]}}{x_{i}x_{j}}:\ \{i,j\}\in E(G)\right).

Then
I_{c}(G)
has a linear resolution if and only if
G
is connected if and only if
I_{c}(G)^{s}
has linear quotients for all
s\geq 1
.

The following Lemma can be proved in the same way as
[
22
, Lemma 10.3.5]
.

Lemma 1.2
.

Let
I,J\subset S
be monomial ideals with
d
-linear resolution and suppose that
J\subset I
.
Then, for all
{\mathbf{a}}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
and all
i\geq 0
, we have

\beta_{i,{\mathbf{a}}}(J)\leq\beta_{i,{\mathbf{a}}}(I).

In particular, for all
i\geq 0
, we have
\operatorname{HS}_{i}(J)\subseteq\operatorname{HS}_{i}(I)
.

This result has two immediate consequences, both of which are relevant to our work.

Lemma 1.3
.

Let
G
be a connected graph and
H
be a connected subgraph of
G
. Let
u=\prod_{i\in V(G)\setminus V(H)}x_{i}
. Then
u^{s}\cdot\operatorname{HS}_{i}(I_{c}(H)^{s})\subseteq\operatorname{HS}_{i}(I_{c}(G)^{s})
for all
i,s\geq 1
.
In particular,
\mathrm{pd}(I_{c}(H)^{s})\leq\mathrm{pd}(I_{c}(G)^{s})
.

Proof.

Note that
u^{s}I_{c}(H)^{s}\subseteq I_{c}(G)^{s}
and both ideals have linear resolutions, the result follows from Lemma
1.2
.

∎

Lemma 1.4
.

Let
G
be a connected graph. Then
\beta_{i}(I_{c}(G)^{s})\leq\beta_{i}(I_{c}(G)^{s+1})
for all
s\geq 1
. In particular, the projective dimension function
\mathrm{pd}(I_{c}(G)^{s})
of
I_{c}(G)
is a non-decreasing function.

Proof.

Fix a minimal monomial generator
u
of
I_{c}(G)
. Then
uI_{c}(G)^{s}\subseteq I_{c}(G)^{s+1}
and both have
(n-2)(s+1)
-linear resolution. It follows that
\beta_{i}(I_{c}(G)^{s})=\beta_{i}(uI_{c}(G)^{s})\leq\beta_{i}(I_{c}(G)^{s+1})
by Lemma
1.2
.

∎

2.
A Theorem on the homological shift ideals of monomial ideals with linear resolutions

In this section, we present a result regarding the homological shift ideal of a monomial ideal with a linear resolution. Although this result is not directly related to other parts of this paper, it is interesting and applies to general monomial ideals. We therefore include it here.

Let
\mathfrak{m}
be the maximal graded ideal of
S=\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
, and let

\mathfrak{m}^{[i]}
denote the
i
-th squarefree power of
\mathfrak{m}
, i.e.,
\mathfrak{m}^{[i]}=({\bf x}_{F}:\ F\subseteq[n],|F|=i).

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
I\subseteq S=\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
be a monomial ideal with a
d
-linear resolution. Then for all
i\in[n]
, we have

\mathrm{HS}_{i}(I)+\mathrm{HS}_{i-1}(\mathfrak{m}I)=\mathfrak{m}^{[i]}I.

Proof.

Consider the short exact sequence of multi-graded
S
-modules:

0\rightarrow\mathfrak{m}I\rightarrow I\rightarrow T\rightarrow 0,

where
T=\bigoplus\limits_{{\bf x}^{\alpha}\in\mathcal{G}(I)}\mathbb{K}[-\alpha]
.

Fix an integer
i\in[n]
and a multi-degree
\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
with
|\beta|=i+d
. Since
I
has a
d
-linear resolution, its
k
-th syzygy module is concentrated in degree
d+k
for all
k\geq 0
, so
\mathrm{Tor}_{i-1}(I,\mathbb{K})_{\beta}=0
. For
\mathfrak{m}I
, note that multiplying an ideal with a
d
-linear resolution by
\mathfrak{m}
yields an ideal with a
(d+1)
-linear resolution, so
\mathrm{Tor}_{i}(\mathfrak{m}I,\mathbb{K})_{\beta}=0
.

Applying the
\mathbb{K}\otimes_{S}-
functor to the short exact sequence above, we obtain the following long exact sequence in multi-graded
\mathbb{K}
-vector spaces (truncated at the relevant degrees):

\rightarrow\mathrm{Tor}_{i}(\mathfrak{m}I,\mathbb{K})_{\beta}\rightarrow\mathrm{Tor}_{i}(I,\mathbb{K})_{\beta}\rightarrow\mathrm{Tor}_{i}(T,\mathbb{K})_{\beta}\rightarrow\\
\mathrm{Tor}_{i-1}(\mathfrak{m}I,\mathbb{K})_{\beta}\rightarrow\mathrm{Tor}_{i-1}(I,\mathbb{K})_{\beta}\rightarrow.

Since
\mathrm{Tor}_{i-1}(I,\mathbb{K})_{\beta}=\mathrm{Tor}_{i}(\mathfrak{m}I,\mathbb{K})_{\beta}=0
, this reduces to a short exact sequence:

0\rightarrow\mathrm{Tor}_{i}(I,\mathbb{K})_{\beta}\rightarrow\mathrm{Tor}_{i}(T,\mathbb{K})_{\beta}\rightarrow\mathrm{Tor}_{i-1}(\mathfrak{m}I,\mathbb{K})_{\beta}\rightarrow 0.

Thus,
\mathrm{Tor}_{i}(T,\mathbb{K})_{\beta}\neq 0
if and only if either
\mathrm{Tor}_{i}(I,\mathbb{K})_{\beta}\neq 0
or
\mathrm{Tor}_{i-1}(\mathfrak{m}I,\mathbb{K})_{\beta}\neq 0
. Since all minimal generators of
\mathrm{HS}_{i}(I)
,
\mathrm{HS}_{i-1}(\mathfrak{m}I)
, and
\mathrm{HS}_{i}(T)
have degree
i+d
(matching
|\beta|
), we conclude that

\mathrm{HS}_{i}(I)+\mathrm{HS}_{i-1}(\mathfrak{m}I)=\mathrm{HS}_{i}(T).

On the other hand, the Koszul complex of
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
provides a minimal free resolution of
\mathbb{K}
. It follows that for each
{\bf x}^{\alpha}\in\mathcal{G}(I)
,
\mathrm{Tor}_{i}(\mathbb{K}[-\alpha],\mathbb{K})_{\beta}\neq 0
if and only if
\beta=\alpha+\gamma
for some
\gamma\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
with
{\bf x}^{\gamma}\in\mathcal{G}(\mathfrak{m}^{[i]})
. Since
T=\bigoplus_{{\bf x}^{\alpha}\in\mathcal{G}(I)}\mathbb{K}[-\alpha]
,
\mathrm{HS}_{i}(T)
is generated by all monomials
{\bf x}^{\alpha+\gamma}={\bf x}^{\alpha}{\bf x}^{\gamma}
where
{\bf x}^{\alpha}\in\mathcal{G}(I)
and
{\bf x}^{\gamma}\in\mathcal{G}(\mathfrak{m}^{[i]})
, which is exactly
\mathfrak{m}^{[i]}I
.

Combining the two equalities, we get
\mathrm{HS}_{i}(I)+\mathrm{HS}_{i-1}(\mathfrak{m}I)=\mathfrak{m}^{[i]}I
.

∎

This result indicates that every minimal generator of
\mathrm{HS}_{i}(I)
has the form
{\bf x}_{F}\cdot u
, where
u\in\mathcal{G}(I)
and
F
is a subset of cardinality
i
in
[n]
. This is consistent with the case when
I
has linear quotients. In
[
12
]
, the authors expected that if
I
has linear resolutions then
\mathrm{HS}_{1}(I)
would have linear quotients (see
[
12
, the third paragraph, p134]
).
This result lends support to this expectation.

Another consequence of Theorem
2.1
is as follows:

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
I\subset S
be a monomial ideal with a
d
-linear resolution. Then

\mathrm{HS}_{n-1}(\mathfrak{m}I)=\mathfrak{m}^{[n]}I
.
In particular, if
I\neq 0
, then
\mathrm{pd}(\mathfrak{m}I)=n-1
.

Proof.

Note that
\mathrm{HS}_{n}(I)=0
. Specializing
i
to
n
in Theorem
2.1
yields the desired formula.
∎

3.
Projective dimension functions

In this section, we determine the projective dimension of powers of complementary edge ideals for several classes of connected graphs, including trees and cycles.

First, we will review the concept of
even-connectedness
and a related result. By abuse of notation, an edge of
G
refers to both a two-element subset
\{i,j\}
of
V(G)
and a monomial
x_{i}x_{j}
. This abuse of notation should not cause any confusion.

Definition 3.1
.

(
[
1
, Definition 6.2]
) Two vertices
j
and
k
(
j
may be equal to
k
) of
G
are said to be even-connected with respect to the
s
-fold product
e_{1}\cdots e_{s}
of edges if there exists a walk
j_{1}-j_{2}-\cdots-j_{2\ell+2}
(
\ell\geq 2
) in
G
such that

(1)

j_{1}=j
and
j_{2\ell+2}=k
;

(2)

For all
t\in[\ell]
,
j_{2t}j_{2t+1}=e_{i}
for some
i
;

(3)

For all
i\in[s]
,
|\{t\in[\ell]:\ j_{2t}j_{2t+1}=e_{i}\}|\leq|\{t\in[s]:\ e_{t}=e_{i}\}|
.

Theorem 3.2
.

(
[
1
, Theorems 6.1 and 6.7]
)
Let
G
be a graph and let
e_{1}\cdots e_{s}
be an
s
-fold product of edges in
G
, where
s\geq 1
and
e_{i}
may be equal to
e_{j}
for
i\neq j
. Then
(I(G)^{s+1}:e_{1}\cdots e_{s})
is generated by monomials of degree two. Moreover, each generator
x_{j}x_{k}
(
x_{j}
may be equal to
x_{k}
) of
(I(G)^{s+1}:e_{1}\cdots e_{s})

is either an edge of
G
or even-connected with respect to
e_{1}\cdots e_{s}
.

Let
G
be a connected graph with vertex set
[n]
. As noted in
[
13
, Remark 3.3]
, if the vertices of
G
are labeled with integers from
[n]
such that for all
i\in[n-1]
, the induced subgraph of
G
on
V(G)\setminus[i]
(i.e., on vertex set
\{i+1,\ldots,n\}
) is connected, then
I_{c}(G)^{k}
has linear quotients for all

with respect to the lexicographical order on the minimal generators of

induced by
x_{1}>x_{2}>\cdots>x_{n}
.
For the rest of this section, any order involved refers to this lexicographical order.

The following result plays a key role in this section and next section. For convenience, we always denote
\prod_{i\in V(G)}x_{i}
by
\alpha(G)
or simply
\alpha
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\beta\in\mathcal{G}(I_{c}(G)^{s})
, and write
\beta=\frac{\alpha^{s}}{u_{1}\cdots u_{s}}
, where each
u_{i}\in\mathcal{G}(I(G))
, and
I(G)
is the edge ideal of

. Then

\mathrm{set}(\beta)\subseteq\{i\mid x_{i}\mbox{ divides }u_{1}\cdots u_{s}\}.

Furthermore, if
u_{1}=x_{i}x_{j}
, then
i\in\mathrm{set}(\beta)
if and only if there is some
k\in[n]
with
k>i
such that either
\{j,k\}\in E(G)
or
j
is even-connected to
k
with respect to the
(s-1)
-fold product
u_{2}\cdots u_{s}
.

Proof.

For any monomials
U_{1},\ldots,U_{k},U
that divides
\alpha^{s}
, one has
(\frac{\alpha^{s}}{U_{1}},\cdots,\frac{\alpha^{s}}{U_{k}}):\frac{\alpha^{s}}{U}=(U:U_{1})+\cdots+(U:U_{k})
. The first assertion follows from this observation. Next, we prove the second assertion.

Let
u=u_{1}\cdots u_{s}
. If
i\in\mathrm{set}(\beta)
, then there exists some
\gamma>\beta
such that
\gamma=\alpha^{s}/v
and
\gamma:\beta=u:v=(x_{i})
, where
v\in\mathcal{G}(I(G)^{s})
.

Since
u=x_{i}x_{j}u_{2}\cdots u_{s}
, we deduce
v=x_{k}x_{j}u_{2}\cdots u_{s}
for some
k\in[n]
. As
\gamma>\beta
, it follows that
u>v
, which in turn implies
i<k
. It follows directly that
x_{k}x_{j}\in(I(G)^{s}:u_{2}\cdots u_{s})
. By Theorem
3.2
, either
\{j,k\}\in E(G)
or
j
is even-connected to
k
with respect to the
(s-1)
-fold product
u_{2}\cdots u_{s}
.

Conversely, suppose there exists
k\in[n]
such that
k>i
, and either
\{j,k\}\in E(G)
or
j
is even-connected to
k
with respect to the
(s-1)
-fold product
u_{2}\cdots u_{s}
. Then
x_{k}x_{j}\in(I(G)^{s}:u_{2}\cdots u_{s})
. Let
v=x_{k}x_{j}u_{2}\cdots u_{s}
and
\gamma=\alpha^{s}/v
. It follows that
\gamma>\beta
and
\gamma\in\mathcal{G}(I_{c}(G)^{s})
. Moreover,
\gamma:\beta=(x_{i})
, which implies

.

∎

By
[
15

, if
G
is a connected graph, then the maximal value of
\mathrm{pd}(I_{c}(G)^{s})
for

Theorem 3.4
.

(1) Let
G
be an even cycle with
2m
vertices. Then

\mathrm{pd}(I_{c}(G)^{s})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}2s,&\hbox{if }s\in[m-1];\\
2m-2,&\hbox{if }s\geq m-1.\end{array}\right.

(2) Let
G
be an odd cycle with

vertices. Then

\mathrm{pd}(I_{c}(G)^{s})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}2s,&\hbox{if }s\in[m];\\
2m,&\hbox{if }s>m.\end{array}\right.

Proof.

(1) Suppose the vertices of
G
are labeled as
1-2-3-\cdots-(2m-1)-(2m)-1

\beta=\frac{\alpha^{s}}{u_{1}\cdots u_{s}}\in\mathcal{G}(I_{c}(G)^{s})
where each

Conversely, we choose

for any

, and set

,
(2i-1)\in\mathrm{set}(\beta)
for any

Given this fact and since

The proof of (2) is similar to that of (1), so we omit the proof.

Remark 3.5
.

Let
G
be a tree with
n
vertices. We fix a degree-one vertex of
G
and label it
n
. There exists at least one labeling of the remaining vertices in
V(G)
using the elements of
[n-1]
such that
\text{dist}(i,n)\geq\text{dist}(j,n)
for all
1\leq i<j\leq n-1
. Here,
\text{dist}(i,n)
is the length of the shortest path between
i
and
n
(commonly referred to as “distance” in graph theory). In such a labeling, a key property holds: for each
j\in[n-1]
, there exists a unique vertex
k
with
j<k\leq n
such that
j
and
k
are adjacent. We call this vertex
k
the “parent” of
j
(relative to the root
n
). This kind of labeling is not unique in general, and is unique precisely when
G
is a path. In the rest of this section, all trees will be labeled in this manner.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
G
be a tree and let
i-y_{1}-y_{2}-\cdots-y_{p}-k
be a walk in
G
with
i<k
. If
y_{1}<i
, then there exists
2\leq q\leq p
such that
y_{q}=i
.

Proof.

First, define a function
\phi:[n-1]\rightarrow[n]
where
\phi(j)
denotes the unique parent of
j
(as defined in Remark
3.5
): for each
j\in[n-1]
,
\phi(j)
is the unique vertex
k>j
that is adjacent to
j
.
A fundamental observation about
\phi
is that for any walk in
G
starting at a vertex
j\in[n-1]
and ending at a vertex
\ell>j
, the parent
\phi(j)
must lie on this walk. This follows from the tree structure: the unique path from
j
to
\ell
(and hence any walk from
j
to
\ell
, which reduces to this path) must pass through
\phi(j)
—the only neighbor of
j
with a larger label.

Applied this observation to the walk
y_{1}-y_{2}-\cdots-y_{p}-k
and note that
\phi(y_{1})=i
, the result follows.

∎

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
G
be a tree and
\beta\in\mathcal{G}(I_{c}(G)^{s})
. Write
\beta=\frac{\alpha^{s}}{u_{1}\cdots u_{s}}
, where each
u_{i}\in\mathcal{G}(I(G))
,
Assume that
u_{1}=x_{i}x_{j}
with
1\leq i<j\leq n
. Then

(1)

i\in\mathrm{set}(\beta)
if and only if
j\neq n
. In particular,
\mathrm{set}(\beta)\subseteq[n-2]
.

(2)

If
j\in[n-2]
, then
j\in\mathrm{set}(\beta)
if and only if there is some
2\leq t\leq s
such that
u_{t}=x_{j}x_{k}
and
k>j
.

Proof.

(1) If
j\neq n
, then there is some
k\in[n]
such that
k>j
and
k
is adjacent to
j
. Since
j>i
, we have
k>i
. By Lemma
3.3
,
i\in\mathrm{set}(\beta)
.

If
j=n
, then
i=n-1
. If
(n-1)\in\mathrm{set}(\beta)
, then by Lemma
3.3
,
n
is even-connected to
n
with respect to the
(s-1)
-fold product
u_{2}\cdots u_{s}
. Thus there exists a closed walk
y_{1}-y_{2}-\cdots-y_{2\ell+1}-y_{2\ell+2}
such that
y_{1}=y_{2\ell+2}=n
. However, since
G
is a tree, every edge of
G
appears an even number of times in this walk. Therefore, the walk is of even length. However, this walk has length
2\ell+1
, which is a contradiction. This implies
i=n-1\notin\mathrm{set}(\beta)
.

(2) If
j\in\mathrm{set}(\beta)
, then by Lemma
3.3
, there exists some
k^{\prime}\in[n]
such that
k^{\prime}>j
and either
\{i,k^{\prime}\}\in E(G)
or
i
is even-connected to
k^{\prime}
with respect to the
(s-1)
-fold product
u_{2}\cdots u_{s}
. Since
G
is a tree, and according to the vertex labeling of
G
described in Remark
3.5
,
j
is the unique vertex such that
\{j,i\}\in E(G)
and
j>i
. Thus,
i
must be even-connected to
k^{\prime}
with respect to
u_{2}\cdots u_{s}
, say via the walk
y_{1}-y_{2}-\cdots-y_{2\ell+1}-y_{2\ell+2}
, where
y_{1}=i
and
y_{2\ell+2}=k^{\prime}
. Let
p
be the largest integer for which
y_{p}=i
.

If
p\neq 1
, then
y_{1}-y_{2}-\cdots-y_{p-1}-y_{p}
is a closed walk. This implies
p
is odd, and so
x_{y_{p+1}}x_{y_{p+2}}\in\{u_{2},\ldots,u_{s}\}
. By Lemma
3.6
, we have
j=y_{p+1}
.

- If
y_{p+2}>j
, set
k=y_{p+2}
and the proof is complete.

- If
y_{p+2}<j
, let
q
be the largest integer such that
y_{q}=j
and
q\geq p+1
. Then
q-(p+1)
is even, and
y_{q+1}>j
. Since
q-(p+1)
is even,
x_{y_{q}}x_{y_{q+1}}\in\{u_{2},\ldots,u_{s}\}
. Setting
k=y_{q+1}
, we obtain the desired result.

The converse conclusion follows from (1).

∎

Let
G
be a graph and
e=\{i,j\}
be an edge of
G
, we set
\min(e)=\min\{i,j\}
.

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
G
be a tree with
n
vertices. Then

\mathrm{pd}(I_{c}(G)^{s})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}s,&\hbox{ \text{if\ } $s\in[n-2]$;}\\
n-2,&\hbox{ \text{if\ } $s\geq n-2$.}\end{array}\right.

Proof.

Let
e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n-2},e_{n-1}
be all the edges of
G
, where
e_{n-1}=\{n-1,n\}=x_{n-1}x_{n}
. Then
\min(e_{1}),\ldots,\min(e_{n-2})
are pairwise distinct.

For any
s\in[n-2]
, we choose
\beta_{s}=\frac{\alpha^{s}}{e_{1}\cdots e_{s}}
. Then
\beta_{s}\in\mathcal{G}(I_{c}(G)^{s})
and, by Lemma
3.7
.(1), we can deduce that

\{\min(e_{1}),\ldots,\min(e_{s})\}\subseteq\mathrm{set}(\beta_{s}).

This implies that
\mathrm{pd}(I_{c}(G)^{s})\geq s
.

For any
\beta\in\mathcal{G}(I_{c}(G)^{s})
, we can write
\beta
as
\beta=\frac{\alpha^{s}}{e_{i_{1}}\cdots e_{i_{s}}}
, where each
i_{j}\in[n-1]
.
If
i\in\mathrm{set}(\beta)
, then by Lemma
3.7
(2), there is a
t\in[s]
such that

.
This implies

(3)

\mathrm{set}(\beta)=\{\min(e_{i_{j}})\mid j\in[s]\}\setminus\{n-1\}.

Therefore,
|\mathrm{set}(\beta)|\leq s
. It follows from the arbitrariness of

that
\mathrm{pd}(I_{c}(G)^{s})=\max\{|\operatorname{set}(\beta)|:\ \beta\in\mathcal{G}(I_{c}(G)^{s})\}\leq s
.

Suppose that

. Then

n-2=\mathrm{pd}(I_{c}(G)^{n-2})\leq\mathrm{pd}(I_{c}(G)^{s})\leq n-2.

This implies
\mathrm{pd}(I_{c}(G)^{s})=n-2
, as desired.

∎

This result generalizes
[
15
, Proposition 4.6]
, which proves that the same formula holds for paths, a specific type of trees.

We next determine the projective dimension of
I_{c}(G)
for all connected graphs

.

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
G
be a connected graph. Then
\mathrm{pd}(I_{c}(G))
is either
1
or
2
. Moreover,
\mathrm{pd}(I_{c}(G))=1
if and only if
G
is a tree.

Proof.

It is straightforward to see that

by the first assertion of Lemma
3.3
.

If
G
is a tree, then
\mathrm{pd}(I_{c}(G))=1
by Theorem
3.8
. If
G
is not a tree, there exists a cycle
C
that is a subgraph of

. By Theorem
3.4
and Theorem

, we have
\mathrm{pd}(I_{c}(G))\geq\mathrm{pd}(I_{c}(C))=2
.

∎

For any connected graph
G
, it is known that
\mathrm{pd}(I_{c}(G)^{s})
is non-decreasing by Lemma
1.3
. This can be improved upon when
G
is bipartite. A subgraph
H
of
G
is called a
spanning subgraph
if they share the same vertex set, that is,
V(H)=V(G)
.

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
G
be a connected bipartite graph with
n
vertices. Then
\mathrm{pd}(I_{c}(G)^{s})
strictly increases until it reaches its maximum value
n-2
.

Proof.

By
[
15
, Proposition 4.3]
and the Auslander-Buchsbaum formula, we know that
\mathrm{pd}(I_{c}(G)^{s})=n-2
for all
s\gg 0
.
Let
T
be a spanning tree of
G
, and label its vertices as described in Remark
3.5
. Note that this also defines a labeling on
V(G)
that satisfies the requirement specified in
[
13
, Remark 3.3]
. Suppose that
\mathrm{pd}(I_{c}(G)^{s})=k\leq n-3
. We can choose
\beta=\frac{\alpha^{s}}{u_{1}u_{2}\cdots u_{s}}\in\mathcal{G}(I_{c}(G)^{s})
such that
|\operatorname{set}(\beta)|=k
.
Let
\ell
be the minimal element of
[n]\setminus\operatorname{set}(\beta)
. Since
k\leq n-3
, it follows that
\ell\leq n-2
. Thus, there exists an integer
m
with
\ell<m\leq n-1
such that
\{\ell,m\}\in E(T)
.
Set
u_{s+1}=x_{\ell}x_{m}
and
\gamma=\beta\cdot\frac{\alpha}{u_{s+1}}
. By Lemma
3.3
,
\ell\in\operatorname{set}(\gamma)
. Therefore,
|\operatorname{set}(\gamma)|\geq k+1
. It follows that
\mathrm{pd}(I_{c}(G)^{s+1})\geq k+1
, which completes the proof.

∎

If
G
is a connected non-bipartite graph with
n
vertices, then the maximal value of
\mathrm{pd}(I_{c}(G)^{s})
is
n-1
. Using the same method as in Lemma
3.10
, we can prove that
\mathrm{pd}(I_{c}(G)^{s})
is strictly increasing in
s
until it reaches
n-2
. However, it is difficult to prove that it continues to increase strictly when it reaches
n-2
.

By
[
4
]
, we know that the limit

\lim\limits_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mathrm{depth}(S/I^{k})
exists for any ideal
I\subset S
, that is,

\mathrm{depth}(S/I^{k})=\mathrm{depth}(S/I^{k+1})
for all
k\gg 0
. The least integer
k_{0}>0
for which

\mathrm{depth}(S/I^{k})=\mathrm{depth}(S/I^{k_{0}})

for all
k\geq k_{0}
is called the
index of depth stability
of

and is denoted by
\mathrm{dstab}(I)
.

Corollary 3.11
.

Let
G
be a connected bipartite graph with
n
vertices. Then

\mathrm{dstab}(I_{c}(G))\leq n-2.

The equality holds if and only if
G
is a tree.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.8
together with the Auslander-Buchsbaum formula, we can show that if
G
is a tree, then
\mathrm{dstab}(I_{c}(G))=n-2
.

Suppose that
G
contains at least one cycle, denoted
C
. It suffices to show that
\mathrm{dstab}(I_{c}(G))<n-2
. By Lemma
3.4
,
\mathrm{pd}(I_{c}(C))=2
. By Theorem
1.3
, it follows that
\mathrm{pd}(I_{c}(G))\geq\mathrm{pd}(I_{c}(C))=2
. Assume, on the contrary, that
\mathrm{dstab}(I_{c}(G))\geq n-2
. Then, according to Proposition
3.10
, we have

\mathrm{pd}(I_{c}(G)^{n-2})\geq\mathrm{pd}(I_{c}(G)^{n-3})+1\geq\cdots\geq\mathrm{pd}(I_{c}(G))+(n-3)\geq 2+(n-3)=n-1.

This yields a contradiction, completing the proof.

∎

4.
Homological shifts

In this section, we examine the following conjecture and prove its validity for graphs that are either trees or cycles. Along the way, we derive explicit formulas for
\mathrm{HS}_{i}(I^{s})
in the case where
I
is the complementary edge ideal associated with a tree or a cycle.
We use
\alpha
to denote
{\bf x}_{[n]}
in this section.

Conjecture 4.1
.

Let
G
be a connected graph, and let
I=I_{c}(G)
be its complementary edge ideal. Then
\mathrm{HS}_{i}(\mathcal{R}(I))
is generated in degree
\leq i
.

4.1.
The case when
i=1

As proven in
[
11
]
, for polymatroidal ideals
I
,
\mathrm{HS}_{1}(\mathcal{R}(I))
is generated in degree
1
. The case
i=1
for complementary edge ideals yields a similar outcome and admits an elementary proof.

Recall from
[
16
, Lemma 2.3]
that if
I
is a monomial ideal (regardless of whether it has linear quotients), then
\mathrm{HS}_{1}(I)
is generated by all
\mathrm{lcm}(u,v)
, where
u,v\in\mathcal{G}(I)
and
u\neq v
. Here,
\mathrm{lcm}(u,v)
denotes the least common multiple of
u
and
v
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
G
be a connected graph on the set
[n]
and let
I=I_{c}(G)
be its complementary edge ideal. Then
\mathrm{HS}_{1}(\mathcal{R}(I))
is generated in degree
1
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove that
\mathrm{HS}_{1}(I^{s+1})=I^{s}\cdot\mathrm{HS}_{1}(I)
for all
s\geq 0
.

First, we recall two definitions for clarity:

- For any vertex
i\in V(G)
,
\deg_{G}(i)
denotes the number of edges incident to
i
.

- For two monomials
u,v\in S
,
\gcd(u,v)
stands for the greatest common divisor of
u
and
v
.

By
[
16
, Lemma 2.3]
and the fact that
\mathrm{HS}_{1}(I)
is generated in degree
n-1
, it is straightforward to verify that
\mathrm{HS}_{1}(I)=\left(\frac{\alpha}{x_{i}}\mid\deg_{G}(i)\geq 2\right)
. We now proceed by induction on
s
. Note that
I^{0}=S
, so the case when
s=0
is trivial.

Suppose
s\geq 1
. Let
\beta\in\mathcal{G}(\mathrm{HS}_{1}(I^{s+1}))
. By
[
16
, Lemma 2.3]
, there exist elements
u,v\in\mathcal{G}(I(G)^{s+1})
such that
\beta=\frac{\alpha^{s+1}}{\gcd(u,v)}
, where
\deg(\gcd(u,v))=2s+1
. It follows that
u=y\cdot\gcd(u,v)
for some
y\in\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
.

Express
u
as
u=\prod_{j=1}^{s+1}u_{j}
, where each
u_{i}\in\mathcal{G}(I(G))
. Without loss of generality, assume
y
divides
u_{1}
, so
u_{1}=x_{i}y
for some
i\in[n]
. Thus,
\gcd(u,v)=x_{i}\cdot\prod_{j=2}^{s+1}u_{j}
. We consider two cases:

(1) If
\deg_{G}(i)\geq 2
, then:

\beta=\frac{\alpha^{s}}{u_{2}\cdots u_{s+1}}\cdot\frac{\alpha}{x_{i}}\in I^{s}\cdot\mathrm{HS}_{1}(I),

and the conclusion holds.

(2) If
\deg_{G}(i)=1
: Express
v
as
v=\prod_{j=1}^{s+1}v_{j}
(where each
v_{j}\in\mathcal{G}(I(G))
). Since
x_{i}\mid\gcd(u,v)
, we may assume
x_{i}
divides
v_{1}
, which implies
u_{1}=v_{1}
(as both are generators of
I(G)
containing
x_{i}
). Let
u_{1}=v_{1}=w
for simplicity. Set
f=\prod_{j=2}^{s+1}u_{j}
and
g=\prod_{j=2}^{s+1}v_{j}
. Then
\gcd(u,v)=w\cdot\gcd(f,g)
, and
\frac{\alpha^{s}}{\gcd(f,g)}\in\mathcal{G}(\mathrm{HS}_{1}(I^{s}))
. By the induction, it follows that
\frac{\alpha^{s}}{\gcd(f,g)}\in I^{s-1}\cdot\mathrm{HS}_{1}(I)
. Therefore:

\beta=\frac{\alpha}{w}\cdot\frac{\alpha^{s}}{\gcd(f,g)}\in I^{s}\cdot\mathrm{HS}_{1}(I),

as required.

∎

For
i\geq 2
, there is no uniform method to determine the generating degree of the
i
-th homological shift algebra of the complementary edge ideal of a connected graph.

4.2.
Trees

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
G
be a tree with
n
vertices, labelled as in Remark
3.5
, and let
I=I_{c}(G)
denote its complementary edge ideal. Define a map
\phi:[n-1]\rightarrow[n]
such that
\phi(k)
is the unique
j\in[n]
, with
j>k
for which
\{k,j\}\in E(G)
. Then, for any
1\leq i\leq s
, we have

\mathrm{HS}_{i}(I^{s})=I^{s-i}\cdot\left(\frac{\alpha^{i}}{\prod_{k\in F}x_{\phi(k)}}:\ F\subseteq[n-2]\text{\ and\ }|F|=i\right).

Proof.

Let
e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n-2},e_{n-1}
be all the edges of
G
. Without loss of generality, we can assume that
\min(e_{i})=i
for all
i\in[n-1]
.
Thus,
e_{i}=x_{i}x_{\phi(i)}
. It is well-known that the monomial algebra
\mathbb{K}[e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}]
(i.e., the edge ring of
G
) has a trivial defining ideal, see for instance
[
20
, Lemmas 5.9-5.11]
. This means that the edges
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n-1}
are algebraically independent over
\mathbb{K}
. Hence, every element of
\mathcal{G}(I(G)^{s})
is uniquely written as

e_{1}^{s_{1}}e_{2}^{s_{2}}\cdots e_{n-1}^{s_{n-1}},

where
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n-1}s_{i}=s
and each
s_{i}\geq 0
.

Take any
\beta=\frac{\alpha^{s-i}}{e_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots e_{n-1}^{a_{n-1}}}\in\mathcal{G}(I^{s-i})
, where
e_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots e_{n-1}^{a_{n-1}}\in\mathcal{G}(I(G)^{s-i})
(so
\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}a_{i}=s-i
). For any subset
F\subseteq[n-2]
with
|F|=i
, define

\gamma=\frac{\alpha^{s}}{e_{1}^{a_{1}+b_{1}}\cdots e_{n-1}^{a_{n-1}+b_{n-1}}},

where
b_{i}=1
if
i\in F
, and
b_{i}=0
otherwise. In particular,
b_{n-1}=0
. Note that
\gamma\in\mathcal{G}(I^{s})
, and
F\subseteq\operatorname{set}(\gamma)
by the equation (
3
). This implies that
{\bf x}_{F}\gamma\in\mathrm{HS}_{i}(I^{s})
. Direct computation gives

\beta\cdot\frac{\alpha^{i}}{\prod_{k\in F}x_{\phi(k)}}=\mathbf{x}_{F}\gamma\in\mathrm{HS}_{i}(I^{s}).

Thus, the inclusion
\supseteq
holds.

Conversely, take any
U\in\mathcal{G}(\mathrm{HS}_{i}(I^{s}))
. By definition of
\mathrm{HS}_{i}(I^{s})
, there exists a subset
F\subseteq[n]
with
|F|=i
and
V=\frac{\alpha^{s}}{e_{1}^{s_{1}}\cdots e_{n-1}^{s_{n-1}}}\in\mathcal{G}(I^{s})
such that
U=\mathbf{x}_{F}V
and
F\subseteq\mathrm{set}(V)
. By Equation (
3
),
F\subseteq[n-2]
and
s_{k}>0
for all
k\in F
. Define
c_{k}=s_{k}-1
if
k\in F
and
c_{k}=s_{k}
otherwise; then
\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}c_{k}=s-i
, so
W=\frac{\alpha^{s-i}}{e_{1}^{c_{1}}\cdots e_{n-1}^{c_{n-1}}}\in\mathcal{G}(I^{s-i})
. Direct computation verifies

W\cdot\frac{\alpha^{i}}{\prod_{k\in F}x_{\phi(k)}}=\mathbf{x}_{F}V=U,

so
U\in I^{s-i}\cdot\left(\frac{\alpha^{i}}{\prod_{k\in F}x_{\phi(k)}}\,:\,F\subseteq[n-2],|F|=i\right)
. Thus, the inclusion
\subseteq
holds.

Combining both inclusions completes the proof.

∎

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
G
be a tree and let
I=I_{c}(G)
be its complementary edge ideal. For
1\leq i\leq n-2
, the
i
-th homological shift algebra
\mathrm{HS}_{i}(\mathcal{R}(I))
is generated exactly in degree
i
.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.3
, we have
\mathrm{HS}_{i}(I^{s})=I^{s-i}\cdot\mathrm{HS}_{i}(I^{i})
for all
s\geq i
. This inclusion implies
\mathrm{HS}_{i}(\mathcal{R}(I))
is generated in degrees at most
i
. By Theorem
3.8
, it follows that
\mathrm{HS}_{i}(I^{s})=0
for all
s<i
and no generators of
\mathrm{HS}_{i}(\mathcal{R}(I))
can exist in degrees less than
i
. Combining these two observations, the algebra is generated exactly in degree
i
.

∎

The ideal
\left(\frac{\alpha^{i}}{\prod_{k\in F}x_{\phi(k)}}:F\subseteq[n-2]\text{ and }|F|=i\right)
is uniquely determined by
G
and
i
up to variable permutation, as it coincides with
\mathrm{HS}_{i}(I^{i})
. This implies the ideal is independent of the labeling of
G
, and we denote it by
J_{i}(G)
.

To study the properties of
J_{i}(G)
, we recall foundational theory on polymatroidal ideals, following the notation from
[
6
]
and
[
22
]
. For a monomial
u=x_{1}^{c_{1}}x_{2}^{c_{2}}\cdots x_{n}^{c_{n}}\in S
where
c_{i}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
, we denote by
\deg_{x_{i}}(u)=c_{i}
, the the
x_{i}
-degree of
u
.

A monomial ideal
I\subset S
generated in a single degree is called
polymatroidal
if it satisfies the
exchange property
. The exchange property requires that for all
u,v\in\mathcal{G}(I)
and all indices
i
such that
\deg_{x_{i}}(u)>\deg_{x_{i}}(v)
, there exists an index
j
with
\deg_{x_{j}}(u)<\deg_{x_{j}}(v)
such that
x_{j}(u/x_{i})\in I
. A stronger variant, the
strong exchange property
, demands that for all
u,v\in\mathcal{G}(I)
, all
i
with
\deg_{x_{i}}(u)>\deg_{x_{i}}(v)
, and
all

j
with
\deg_{x_{j}}(u)<\deg_{x_{j}}(v)
, the monomial
x_{j}(u/x_{i})\in I
.

It is straightforward that the strong exchange property implies the exchange property and thus any ideal with the strong exchange property is polymatroidal. A key class of polymatroidal ideals satisfying the strong exchange property are
ideals of Veronese type
: given a vector
{\bf a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{n}
and an integer
d
with
|{\bf a}|=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\geq d
, the ideal of Veronese type determined by
{\bf a}
and
d
is defined as

I_{{\bf a},d}=\left({\bf x}^{b}\in S:|{\bf b}|=d,\,{\bf b}\leq{\bf a}\right),

where
{\bf b}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{n}
,
|{\bf b}|=\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}
(the total degree of
{\bf x}^{b}
), and
{\bf b}\leq{\bf a}
means
b_{i}\leq a_{i}
for all
i
. Moreover, by
[
23
]
, ideals with the strong exchange property are essentially Veronese-type ideals: if
I
satisfies the strong exchange property, then
I=(u)I_{{\bf a},d}
for some monomial
u\in S
,
{\bf a}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{n}
, and
d>0
.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
G
be a tree with
n
vertices. Then

is a polymatroidal ideal with the strong exchange property for all

.

Proof.

Let

. For any

if and only if

. Moreover,

such that

for any
k\in[n-1]
. Then

for all

\subseteq

Conversely, take any monomial

u_{2}u_{3}\cdots u_{s}
. We may assume the following is a shortest walk by which
2m+1
is even-connected to itself with respect to
u_{2}u_{3}\cdots u_{s}
:

2m+1=y_{0}-y_{1}-y_{2}-\cdots-y_{2t-1}-y_{2t}-y_{2t+1}=2m+1,({\dagger})

It is clear that
y_{1}\in\{1,2m\}
. We only consider the case when
y_{1}=1
, as the proof for the other case is dual. Since
y_{1}=1
, we have
y_{2}\in\{2,2m+1\}
. However, if
y_{2}=2m+1
, then the sequence
2m+1-y_{3}-y_{4}-\cdots-(2m-1)-(2m)-(2m+1)
also forms a walk by which
2m+1
is even-connected to itself. This contradicts the assumption that walk (
{\dagger}
) is shortest. Thus,
y_{2}=2
. This implies
y_{3}=3
or
y_{3}=1
. If
y_{3}=1
, walk (
{\dagger}
) can be shortened, which is another contradiction. Therefore,
y_{3}=3
. By analogy, we conclude
y_{4}=4,y_{5}=5,\ldots
. Finally, we have
t=m
and
y_{2m}=2m
, which implies
\{\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{3},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{2m-1}\}\subseteq\{u_{2},u_{3},\ldots,u_{s}\}
. In particular,
\{\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{3},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{2m-1}\}\subseteq\{u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{s}\}
, as required.

(ii)
u_{1}=x_{2m-1}x_{2m}={\bf e}_{2m-1}
: By Lemma
3.3
,
2m+1
is even-connected to
2m-1
with respect to
u_{2}u_{3}\cdots u_{s}
. We may assume the following walk is a shortest walk by which
2m-1
is even-connected to
2m+1
with respect to
u_{2}u_{3}\cdots u_{s}
:

2m-1=y_{0}-y_{1}-y_{2}-\cdots-y_{2t-1}-y_{2t}-y_{2t+1}=2m+1.

If
y_{1}=2m
, then
y_{2}=2m+1
. It follows that
2m+1-y_{3}-y_{4}-y_{5}-y_{6}-\cdots-y_{2t-1}-y_{2t}-y_{2t+1}=2m+1
is a shortest walk that
even-connects
2m+1
to itself with respect to
u_{2}\cdots u_{2}
. By the conclusion of (i), it follows that
\{{\bf e}_{1},{\bf e}_{3},\cdots{\bf e}_{2m-1}\}\subseteq\{u_{2},u_{3},\ldots,u_{s}\}
.

It remains to consider the case when
y_{1}=2m-2
. In this case, we can conclude, as in case (i), that
y_{2}=2m-3,y_{3}=2m-4,\cdots,
and that
y_{2m-3}=2
and
y_{2m-2}=1
. Hence, we have
\{{\bf e}_{1},{\bf e}_{3},\cdots{\bf e}_{2m-1}\}\subseteq\{u_{2},u_{3},\ldots,u_{s}\}
again.

∎

Remark 4.9
.

(1) By the above lemma, every vertex in an odd cycle is even-connected to itself via some product of edges. Since any connected non-bipartite graph contains an odd cycle, this property extends to all vertices of any connected non-bipartite graph. This implies that if
G
is a connected non-bipartite graph, there exists
\beta\in\mathcal{G}(I_{c}(G)^{s})
for some
s\geq 1
with
\mathrm{set}(\beta)=[n-1]
, providing an alternative proof for
[
15
, Proposition 3.5]
.

(2) In the case when
G
is bipartite, every closed walk has even length. From this it follows that that no vertex is even-connected to itself with respect to any product of edges. This implies that the maximal set
\mathrm{set}(\beta)
for
\beta\in\mathcal{G}(I_{c}(G)^{s})
(where
s\geq 1
) is equal to
[n-2]
, giving an alternative proof for
[
15
, Proposition 3.3]
.

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
G
be the cycle of length
n
, whose vertices are labelled
1,2,\ldots,n
successively. Let
{\bf e}_{j}
denote the edge
x_{j}x_{j+1}
for
j=1,\ldots,n-1
and set
{\bf e}_{0}={\bf e}_{n}=x_{n}x_{1}
. Take
\beta=\frac{\alpha^{s}}{u_{1}u_{2}\cdots u_{s}}\in I^{s}
. Let
T=\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{s}\}
. Define

N_{1}(\beta)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\max\{[2\ell]:\ \{{\bf e}_{1},{\bf e}_{3},\ldots,{\bf e}_{2\ell-1}\}\subseteq T,1\leq\ell\leq m-1\},&\hbox{ $n=2m$;}\\
\max\{[2\ell]:\ \{{\bf e}_{1},{\bf e}_{3},\ldots,{\bf e}_{2\ell-1}\}\subseteq T,1\leq\ell\leq m\},&\hbox{ $n=2m+1$.}\end{array}\right.

N_{2}(\beta)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\max\{[2\ell+1]:\ \{{\bf e}_{0},{\bf e}_{2},\ldots,{\bf e}_{2\ell}\}\subseteq T,\ 1\leq\ell\leq m-2\},&\hbox{$n=2m$;}\\[2.0pt]
\max\{[2\ell+1]:\ \{{\bf e}_{0},{\bf e}_{2},\ldots,{\bf e}_{2\ell}\}\subseteq T,\ 1\leq\ell\leq m-1\},&\hbox{$n=2m+1$.}\end{array}\right.

By convention, if
{\bf e}_{1}\notin\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{s}\}
then
N_{1}(\beta)=\emptyset
and if
{\bf e}_{0}\notin\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{s}\}
then
N_{2}(\beta)=\emptyset
.
Then

\operatorname{set}(\beta)=N_{1}(\beta)\cup N_{2}(\beta)\cup\left(\{\min({u_{j}}):\ 1\leq j\leq s\}\setminus\{n-1\}\right).

Before the proof, we made some remarks. If
n=2m+1
is an odd number, any
\beta\in\mathcal{G}(I^{s})
is expressed as
\frac{\alpha^{s}}{u_{1}u_{2}\cdots u_{s}}
uniquely. Hence, there is no ambiguity in the definitions of
N_{1}(\beta)
and
N_{2}(\beta)
. However, if
n
is even, we should note that the elements of
N_{1}(\beta)
and
N_{2}(\beta)
depend on the way
\beta
is written as
\frac{\alpha^{s}}{u_{1}u_{2}\cdots u_{s}}
. For example, let
\beta_{1}=\frac{\alpha^{m}}{{\bf e}_{1}{\bf e}_{3}\cdots{\bf e}_{2m-1}}
and
\beta_{2}=\frac{\alpha^{m}}{{\bf e}_{2}{\bf e}_{4}\cdots{\bf e}_{2m}}
. Then
\beta_{1}=\beta_{2}
. However,
N_{1}(\beta_{1})=[2m-2]
,
N_{1}(\beta_{2})=\emptyset
,
N_{2}(\beta_{1})=\emptyset
and
N_{2}(\beta_{2})=[2m-3]
.
Of course,
\operatorname{set}(\beta)
is independent of the expression of
\beta
. In the above example, note that
\min({\bf e}_{2m-2})=2m-2
. we have
\operatorname{set}(\beta_{1})=\operatorname{set}(\beta_{2})=[2m-2]
. Since the only real relation that occurs among
{\bf e}_{1},\ldots,{\bf e}_{n}
is
{\bf e}_{1}{\bf e}_{3}\cdots{\bf e}_{2m-1}={\bf e}_{2}\cdots{\bf e}_{2m-2}{\bf e}_{2m}
, the set
\operatorname{set}(\beta)
given in Lemma
4.10
is actually independent of the expression of
\beta
.

Proof.

We only consider the case when
n=2m+1
(an odd number), as the proof for the other case is analogous and even simpler. Let
j\in\mathrm{set}(\beta)
. Then
j\in[n-1]
and
j\in u_{k}
for some
1\leq k\leq s
. If
j=n-1=2m
, then by Lemma
4.8
.(3),
\{{\bf e}_{1},{\bf e}_{3},\ldots,{\bf e}_{2m-3},{\bf e}_{2m-1}\}\subseteq\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{s}\}
, which implies
j\in N_{1}(\beta)
. Thus, we may assume
j\leq 2m-1
.

If
j=\min(u_{k})
for some
k\in[s]
, then
j\in\{\min(u_{j}):\ 1\leq j\leq s\}\setminus\{n-1\}
. Suppose now that
j\neq\min(u_{k})
for any
k=1,\ldots,s
. By Lemma
4.8
, we conclude:

- If
j=2\ell
for some
\ell
, then
\{{\bf e}_{1},{\bf e}_{3},\ldots,{\bf e}_{2\ell-1}\}\subseteq\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{s}\}
;

- If
j=2\ell+1
for some
\ell
, then
\{{\bf e}_{n},{\bf e}_{2},\ldots,{\bf e}_{2\ell}\}\subseteq\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{s}\}
.

Since
j\leq 2m-1
, we have:
If
j=2\ell+1
, then
\ell\leq m-1
;
If
j=2\ell
, then
\ell\leq m-1
.
This implies
j\in N_{1}(\beta)\cup N_{2}(\beta)
, so the inclusion
\subseteq
holds. The converse inclusion follows directly from Lemma
4.8
.

As an immediate consequence of Lemma
4.10
, we obtain:

.

Keep the notation as before. Let
\beta=\frac{\alpha^{s}}{{\bf e}_{1}^{s_{1}}{\bf e}_{2}^{s_{2}}\cdots{\bf e}_{n}^{s_{n}}}\in\mathcal{G}(I^{s})

if

and

By the preceding corollary, it is straightforward to verify that

Hereafter, we focus on computing the homological shift ideals of powers of the complementary edge ideal associated with a cycle graph.

Lemma 4.12
.

Let
G
be a cycle of length
n
and
I
be its complementary edge ideal. For
1\leq i\leq n-1
and
s\geq\frac{i}{2}
, the following inclusion holds:

(4)

\mathrm{HS}_{i}(I^{s})\subseteq\sum_{0\leq k\leq\frac{i}{2}}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\subseteq[n]\\
|F|=i-2k\end{subarray}}\frac{\alpha^{i-k}}{{\bf x}_{F}}\cdot I^{s-i+k}\right).

Here,
I^{t}=0
if
t<0
.

Proof.

We only prove the case where
n=2m
(even cycle), as the argument for
n=2m+1
(odd cycle) is analogous. Fix
i\in[2m-2]
and
s\geq\frac{i}{2}
. Let
\beta\in\mathcal{G}(I^{s})
with
i\leq|\mathrm{set}(\beta)|
, and take a subset
Q\subseteq\mathrm{set}(\beta)
with
|Q|=i
. It suffices to show that
{\bf x}_{Q}\beta
lies in the right-hand side of (
4
).

Write
\beta=\frac{\alpha^{s}}{u_{1}\cdots u_{s}}
with
u_{j}\in I(G)
for all
j=1,\ldots,s
. Let
T=\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{s}\}
. Denote

\ell_{1}:=\max\left\{1\leq\ell\leq m-1:\ \{{\bf e}_{1},{\bf e}_{3},\ldots{\bf e}_{2\ell-1}\}\subseteq T\right\}

and

\ell_{2}:=\max\{1\leq\ell\leq m-2:\ \{{\bf e}_{0},{\bf e}_{2},\ldots{\bf e}_{2\ell}\}\subseteq T\}.

By convention,
\max\emptyset=-\infty
. It is clear that
N_{1}(\beta)\cup N_{2}(\beta)=[2\ell_{1}]\cup[2\ell_{2}+1]
. We consider two cases:

(1)
Q\cap\left([2\ell_{1}]\cup[2\ell_{2}+1]\right)=\emptyset
: By Lemma
4.10
, there exist distinct edges
v_{1},\ldots,v_{i}\in T
such that
Q=\{\min(v_{1}),\ldots,\min(v_{i})\}
. We may write
u_{1}u_{2}\cdots u_{s}=(v_{1}\cdots v_{i})(u_{1}\cdots u_{s-i})
(after reindexing if necessary). Note that
\max(v_{1}),\ldots,\max(v_{i})
are pairwise distinct; let
F
denote the
i
-element set
\{\max(v_{1}),\ldots,\max(v_{i})\}
.

Then

{\bf x}_{Q}\beta=\frac{\alpha^{s}}{{\bf x}_{F}\cdot u_{1}\cdots u_{s-i}}=\frac{\alpha^{i}}{{\bf x}_{F}}\cdot\frac{\alpha^{s-i}}{u_{1}\cdots u_{s-i}}.

Hence,
{\bf x}_{Q}\beta
belongs to the
k=0
component of the right-hand side of (
4
).

(2)
Q\cap\left([2\ell_{1}]\cup[2\ell_{2}+1]\right)=Q_{1}\neq\emptyset
: In this case, we can decompose
Q
as

Q=Q_{1}\sqcup\{j_{p+1}<\cdots<j_{i}\},

where
|Q_{1}|=p
,
j_{p+1}>\max Q_{1}
, and for all
k=p+1,\ldots,i
,

{\bf e}_{j_{k}}\in\{u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{s}\}\setminus\{{\bf e}_{1},{\bf e}_{3},\ldots,{\bf e}_{2\ell_{1}-1},{\bf e}_{0},{\bf e}_{2},\ldots,{\bf e}_{2\ell_{2}}\}.

Note that if
p=i
, then
\{j_{p+1}<\cdots<j_{i}\}
is an empty set.

First, consider the subcase
\ell_{2}\geq\ell_{1}
. Here,
Q_{1}\subseteq{\bf e}_{0}\cup{\bf e}_{2}\cup\cdots\cup{\bf e}_{2\ell_{2}}
. Let
\Omega:=\{{\bf e}_{0},\ldots,{\bf e}_{2\ell_{2}}\}
. There exist edges
v_{1},\ldots,v_{q}\in\Omega\setminus\{{\bf e}_{0}\}
and edges
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}\in\Omega
such that

{\bf x}_{Q_{1}}=v_{1}v_{2}\cdots v_{q}\cdot x_{j_{1}}\cdots x_{j_{r}},

where
j_{t}\in f_{t}
for
t=1,\ldots,r
. These edges
v_{1},\ldots,v_{q},f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}
are pairwise disjoint, with
2q+r=p
and
q+r\leq\ell_{2}
. For each
t=1,\ldots,r
, let
i_{t}\in[2\ell_{2}+1]
be such that
f_{t}=x_{j_{t}}x_{i_{t}}
.

Since
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{q},f_{1},\ldots,f_{r},{\bf e}_{j_{p+1}},\ldots,{\bf e}_{j_{i}}\}\subseteq\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{s}\}
, we may assume (after reindexing) that

u_{1}\cdots u_{s}=\left(v_{1}\cdots v_{q}f_{1}\cdots f_{r}{\bf e}_{j_{p+1}}\cdots{\bf e}_{j_{i}}\right)u_{1}\cdots u_{s-i+q}.

Then

{\bf x}_{Q}\beta=\frac{\alpha^{s}}{u_{1}\cdots u_{s-i+q}\cdot(x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{r}})\cdot(x_{j_{p+1}+1}\cdots x_{j_{i}+1})}.

Let
F=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{r},j_{p+1}+1,\ldots,j_{i}+1\}
, which is an
(i-q)
-subset of
[n]
. Thus,
{\bf x}_{Q}\beta\in\frac{\alpha^{i-q}}{{\bf x}_{F}}I^{s-i+q}
, meaning
{\bf x}_{Q}\beta
belongs to the
k=q
component of the right-hand side of (
4
).

The proof for
\ell_{1}>\ell_{2}
is analogous, so the argument is complete.

∎

In general, the converse of the inclusion (
4
) fails to hold. For the converse inclusion to hold, it is necessary to remove certain components from the right-hand side of (
4
) when
i
is sufficiently large. This is accomplished by proving that any subset
F\subseteq\operatorname{set}{\left(\frac{\alpha^{s}}{u_{1}\ldots u_{s}}\right)}
with
|F|=\left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil+k-1
contains at least
k
pairwise disjoint edges among
u_{1},\ldots,u_{s}
.

Proposition 4.13
.

Let
G
be a cycle of length
n
and
I
be its complementary edge ideal. For all integers
1\leq i<n
and
s\geq\left\lfloor\frac{i}{2}\right\rfloor
, the following inclusion holds:

(5)

\mathrm{HS}_{i}(I^{s})\subseteq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\max\left\{i-\left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil+1,0\right\}\leq k\leq\left\lfloor\frac{i}{2}\right\rfloor\end{subarray}}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\subseteq[n]\\
|F|=i-2k\end{subarray}}\frac{\alpha^{i-k}}{{\bf x}_{F}}\cdot I^{s-i+k}\right).

Here,
I^{t}=0
for any integer
t<0
(by convention).

Proof.

If
i\leq\left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil-1
, then the right-hand side of (
5
) coincides with the left-hand side of (
4
). Thus, the result follows from Lemma
4.12
. Suppose now that
i>\left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil-1
. We only consider the case where
n=2m
, as the argument for
n=2m+1
is analogous. We may write
i=m-1+q
. Note that
q=\max\left\{i-\left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil+1,0\right\}
. Let
\beta\in\mathcal{G}(I^{s})
with
i\leq|\mathrm{set}(\beta)|
, and take a subset
Q\subseteq\mathrm{set}(\beta)
with
|Q|=i
. It suffices to show that
{\bf x}_{Q}\beta
lies in the right-hand side of (
5
). Write
\beta=\frac{\alpha^{s}}{u_{1}\cdots u_{s}}
with
u_{j}\in I(G)
for all
j=1,\ldots,s
. Let
T=\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{s}\}
.

Denote

\ell_{1}:=\max\left\{1\leq\ell\leq m-1:\ \{{\bf e}_{1},{\bf e}_{3},\ldots,{\bf e}_{2\ell-1}\}\subseteq T\right\}

and

\ell_{2}:=\max\left\{1\leq\ell\leq m-2:\ \{{\bf e}_{0},{\bf e}_{2},\ldots,{\bf e}_{2\ell}\}\subseteq T\right\}.

By convention,
\max\emptyset=-\infty
. Suppose first that
\ell_{1}>\ell_{2}
. By Lemma
4.10
, it follows that

\mathrm{set}(\beta)=[2\ell_{1}]\cup\left(\{\min(u_{j}):\ j=1,\ldots,s\}\setminus\{n-1\}\right).

Note that the edges
{\bf e}_{1},{\bf e}_{3},\ldots,{\bf e}_{2m-3}
are pairwise disjoint, and their union is the set
[2m-2]
. Since
Q\subseteq\mathrm{set}(\beta)\subseteq[2m]
and
|Q|=m-1+q
, by the pigeonhole principle, there are
k\geq q
edges among
{\bf e}_{1},{\bf e}_{3},\ldots,{\bf e}_{2m-3}

Q

k

{\bf e}_{2j_{1}-1},{\bf e}_{2j_{2}-1},\ldots,{\bf e}_{2j_{k}-1}
with
j_{1}<j_{2}<\cdots<j_{k}
.

There are three possible scenarios for the indices
j_{t}
relative to
\ell_{1}
:
1.
\ell_{1}<j_{1}
,
2.
j_{k}\leq\ell_{1}
,
3. There exists some
1\leq r\leq k-1
such that
j_{r}\leq\ell_{1}<j_{r+1}
(indices straddle
\ell_{1}
).

We only prove the third scenario, as the first two follow by analogous reasoning. For
r+1\leq t\leq k
, since
j_{t}>\ell_{1}
, we have
2j_{t}-1>2\ell_{1}
. Given that
2j_{t}-1\in Q\subseteq\mathrm{set}(\beta)
, Lemma
4.10
implies
{\bf e}_{2j_{t}-1}\in T
. For
1\leq t\leq r
, since
j_{t}\leq\ell_{1}
, the edge
{\bf e}_{2j_{t}-1}
is in
\{{\bf e}_{1},{\bf e}_{3},\ldots,{\bf e}_{2\ell_{1}-1}\}
, which is contained in
T
by the definition of
\ell_{1}
. Thus,
{\bf e}_{2j_{1}-1},\ldots,{\bf e}_{2j_{k}-1}
are pairwise disjoint edges in
T
whose union is contained in
Q
. Let
Q_{1}=Q\setminus({\bf e}_{2j_{1}-1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup{\bf e}_{2j_{k}-1})
. Then
|Q_{1}|=i-2k
; write
Q_{1}=\{\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{p}\}
where
p=i-2k
. For each
\theta_{j}\in Q_{1}
:

- If
\theta_{j}\leq 2\ell_{1}
, choose
v_{j}\in\{{\bf e}_{1},{\bf e}_{3},\ldots,{\bf e}_{2\ell_{1}-1}\}
such that
\theta_{j}\in v_{j}
;

- If
\theta_{j}>2\ell_{1}
, set
v_{j}=\{\theta_{j},\theta_{j}+1\}
.

It is straightforward to verify that each
v_{j}\in T
. Let
F=\bigsqcup_{j=1}^{p}\left(v_{j}\setminus\{\theta_{j}\}\right)
. After reindexing, we may write

u_{1}\cdots u_{s}=\left({\bf e}_{2j_{1}-1}\cdots{\bf e}_{2j_{k}-1}\right)\left(v_{1}\cdots v_{p}\right)\left(u_{1}\cdots u_{s-i+k}\right).

Then

{\bf x}_{Q}\beta=\frac{\alpha^{s}}{(u_{1}\cdots u_{s-i+k})\cdot{\bf x}_{F}}\in\frac{\alpha^{i-k}}{{\bf x}_{F}}\cdot I^{s-i+k}.

Hence,
{\bf x}_{Q}\beta
belongs to the right-hand side of (
5
).

Next, we consider the case when
\ell_{1}\leq\ell_{2}
, which is much more complicated than the first case. By Lemma
4.10
, we have

\mathrm{set}(\beta)=[2\ell_{2}+1]\cup\left(\{\min(u_{j}):\ j=1,\ldots,s\}\setminus\{n-1\}\right).

Consider the
m-2

{\bf e}_{2},{\bf e}_{4},\ldots,{\bf e}_{2m-4}
. Note that
\mathrm{set}(\beta)\subseteq({\bf e}_{2}\sqcup{\bf e}_{4}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup{\bf e}_{2m-4})\cup\{1,2m-2\}
, and since
Q\subseteq\mathrm{set}(\beta)
, it follows that

Q\subseteq({\bf e}_{2}\sqcup{\bf e}_{4}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup{\bf e}_{2m-4})\cup\{1,2m-2\}.

This inclusion implies

|Q\cap({\bf e}_{2}\sqcup{\bf e}_{4}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup{\bf e}_{2m-4})|\geq|Q|-2.

Since
|Q|=m-1+q
, substituting this in gives

|Q\cap({\bf e}_{2}\sqcup{\bf e}_{4}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup{\bf e}_{2m-4})|\geq(m-1+q)-2=m-3+q.

Equality holds if and only if
Q\cap\{1,2m-2\}=\{1,2m-2\}
, i.e.,
\{1,2m-2\}\subseteq Q
.

If
|Q\cap({\bf e}_{2}\sqcup{\bf e}_{4}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup{\bf e}_{2m-4})|\geq m-2+q

q
edges among
{\bf e}_{2},{\bf e}_{4},\ldots,{\bf e}_{2m-4}
that are entirely contained in
Q
. The proof then proceeds analogously to the case where
\ell_{1}>\ell_{2}
.

Assume now that
|Q\cap({\bf e}_{2}\sqcup{\bf e}_{4}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup{\bf e}_{2m-4})|=m-3+q
, which implies
\{1,2m-2\}\subseteq Q
. By the pigeonhole principle, there are at least
q-1
edges among
{\bf e}_{2},{\bf e}_{4},\ldots,{\bf e}_{2m-4}
that are entirely contained in
Q
. If there are more than
q-1
such edges, we are done (as we would have at least
q
edges). Thus, we may assume there are exactly
q-1
such edges, denoted
{\bf e}_{2j_{1}},\ldots,{\bf e}_{2j_{q-1}}
where
j_{1}<j_{2}<\cdots<j_{q-1}
. For the remaining edges
{\bf e}_{2t}
with
t\in[m-2]\setminus\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{q-1}\}
, it follows that
|Q\cap{\bf e}_{2t}|=1
.

The indices
j_{1},\ldots,j_{q-1}
satisfy one of three scenarios relative to
\ell_{2}
:
1.
\ell_{2}<j_{1}
(all indices are greater than
\ell_{2}
),
2.
j_{q-1}\leq\ell_{2}
(all indices are less than or equal to
\ell_{2}
),
3. There exists some
1\leq r\leq q-2
such that
j_{r}\leq\ell_{2}<j_{r+1}
(indices straddle
\ell_{2}
).

We focus on the third scenario (the others follow similarly, with minor adjustments). First, we claim
2\ell_{2}+2\notin Q
: if
2\ell_{2}+2\in Q
, then
2\ell_{2}+2\in\mathrm{set}(\beta)
, so Lemma
4.10
implies
{\bf e}_{2\ell_{2}+2}\in T
, contradicting the maximality of
\ell_{2}
.

Set
p=q-1-r
. Since
2m-2\in Q
, we calculate the size of
Q\cap[2\ell_{2}+3,2m-2]
:

|Q\cap[2\ell_{2}+3,2m-2]|=(m-1+q)-|Q\cap[1,2\ell_{2}+2]|.

By construction,
|Q\cap[1,2\ell_{2}+2]|=|Q\cap[1,2\ell_{2}+1]|=\ell_{2}+r+1
(including
1
, plus
2r
elements from the
r
edges
{\bf e}_{2j_{1}},\ldots,{\bf e}_{2j_{r}}\subseteq[2\ell_{2}+2]
, and
\ell_{2}-r
elements from
\ell_{2}-r
edges
{\bf e}_{2t}
with
1\leq t\leq\ell_{2}
and
t\notin\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{r}\}
). Substituting this gives:

|Q\cap[2\ell_{2}+3,2m-2]|=(m-1+q)-(\ell_{2}+r+1)=m-\ell_{2}-1+p,

(using
p=q-1-r
and simplifying).

Next, consider the
m-2-\ell_{2}
edges
{\bf e}_{2\ell_{2}+3},{\bf e}_{2\ell_{2}+5},\ldots,{\bf e}_{2m-3}
, whose disjoint union is exactly
[2\ell_{2}+3,2m-2]
. Since
|Q\cap[2\ell_{2}+3,2m-2]|=(m-2-\ell_{2})+(p+1)
, the pigeonhole principle guarantees at least
p+1
of these edges are fully contained in
Q
. These
p+1
edges, together with the
r
edges
{\bf e}_{2j_{1}},\ldots,{\bf e}_{2j_{r}}
, form
(p+1)+r=q
pairwise disjoint edges in
T
whose union is contained in
Q
. We denote such
q
edges by
f_{1},\ldots,f_{q}
. Let
Q_{1}
be the remainder of
Q
after removing all vertices contained in these
q
edges.

Write
Q_{1}=\{\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{p}\}
where
p=i-2q
. For each
\theta_{j}\in Q_{1}
:

- If
\theta_{j}\leq 2\ell_{1}
, choose
v_{j}\in\{{\bf e}_{0},{\bf e}_{2},\ldots,{\bf e}_{2\ell_{2}}\}
such that
\theta_{j}\in v_{j}
;

- If
\theta_{j}>2\ell_{2}+1
, set
v_{j}=\{\theta_{j},\theta_{j}+1\}
.

It is straightforward to verify that each
v_{j}\in T
. Let
F=\bigsqcup_{j=1}^{p}\left(v_{j}\setminus\{\theta_{j}\}\right)
. Then
|F|=p=i-2q
. After reindexing, we may write

u_{1}\cdots u_{s}=\left(f_{1}\cdots f_{q}\right)\left(v_{1}\cdots v_{p}\right)\left(u_{1}\cdots u_{s-i+q}\right).

Then

{\bf x}_{Q}\beta=\frac{\alpha^{s}}{(u_{1}\cdots u_{s-i+q})\cdot{\bf x}_{F}}\in\frac{\alpha^{i-q}}{{\bf x}_{F}}\cdot I^{s-i+q}.

Hence,
{\bf x}_{Q}\beta
belongs to
k=q
component of the right-hand side of (
5
). This completes the proof.

∎

To obtain the converse inclusion, we need the following lemma.

Lemma 4.14
.

Let
G
be a cycle of length
n
and
I
be its complementary edge ideal. Assume
n\in\{2m,2m+1\}
. Given a subset
F\subseteq[n]
with
|F|=p
and an integer
k\geq 0
such that
k+p<\frac{n}{2}
(i.e.,
k+p\leq m-1
if
n=2m
, and
k+p\leq m
if
n=2m+1
), set
i=2k+p
. Then there exist pairwise distinct edges
u_{1},\ldots,u_{k},u_{k+1},\ldots,u_{k+p}
and a subset
E\subseteq\mathrm{set}\left(\frac{\alpha^{k+p}}{u_{1}\cdots u_{k+p}}\right)
with
|E|=i
such that
\frac{\alpha^{k+p}}{{\bf x}_{F}}={\bf x}_{E}\cdot\frac{\alpha^{k+p}}{u_{1}\cdots u_{k+p}}\in\mathrm{HS}_{i}(I^{k+p})
.

Proof.

We only consider the case
n=2m
; the proof for
n=2m+1
is analogous. Let
F=\{j_{1}<j_{2}<\cdots<j_{p}\}
. We analyze two cases:

(1)
n\notin F
(i.e.,
j_{p}<n
): Define
t=\max\{a:j_{1}\leq 2k+2,\ j_{2}\leq 2k+4,\ \ldots,\ j_{a}\leq 2k+2a\}
. By convention,
t=0
if
j_{1}>2k+2
. Then
1\leq j_{1}<\cdots<j_{t}\leq 2k+2t\leq 2m-2
and
j_{p}>\cdots>j_{t+1}>2k+2t+2
. Set

\beta=\frac{\alpha^{k+p}}{({\bf e}_{1}{\bf e}_{3}\cdots{\bf e}_{2k+2t-1})({\bf e}_{j_{t+1}-1}\cdots{\bf e}_{j_{p}-1})}.

Then
E=([2k+2t]\setminus\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{t}\})\cup\{j_{t+1}-1,\ldots,j_{p}-1\}
is a subset of
\mathrm{set}(\beta)
. Moreover,
|E|=2k+p=i
and
{\bf x}_{E}\cdot\beta=\frac{\alpha^{k+p}}{{\bf x}_{F}}\in\mathrm{HS}_{i}(I^{k+p})
, as required.

(2)
n\in F
(i.e.,
j_{p}=n
): As in case (1), there exists
t
with
0\leq t\leq p-1
such that
\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{t}\}\subseteq[2k+2t+1]\subseteq[2m-1]
but
j_{t+1}>2k+2t+3
. Define

\beta=\frac{\alpha^{k+p}}{({\bf e}_{0}{\bf e}_{2}\cdots{\bf e}_{2k+2t})({\bf e}_{j_{t+1}-1}\cdots{\bf e}_{j_{p-1}-1})}

and

E=\left([2k+2t+1]\setminus\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{t}\}\right)\cup\{j_{t+1}-1,\ldots,j_{p-1}-1\}.

Then
|E|=2k+p
and
E\subseteq\mathrm{set}(\beta)
. Furthermore,
\frac{\alpha^{k+p}}{{\bf x}_{F}}={\bf x}_{E}\cdot\beta\in\mathrm{HS}_{i}(I^{k+p})
, as required.

∎

We now are ready to present the main result of this subsection.

Theorem 4.15
.

Let
G
be a cycle of length
n
and
I
be its complementary edge ideal. Then, for all integers
1\leq i<n
and
s\geq\left\lfloor\frac{i}{2}\right\rfloor
, the following equality holds:

(6)

\mathrm{HS}_{i}(I^{s})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\max\left\{i-\left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil+1,0\right\}\leq k\leq\left\lfloor\frac{i}{2}\right\rfloor\end{subarray}}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\subseteq[n]\\
|F|=i-2k\end{subarray}}\frac{\alpha^{i-k}}{{\bf x}_{F}}\cdot I^{s-i+k}\right)

Proof.

For any
k
satisfying

\max\left\{i-\left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil+1,0\right\}\leq k\leq\left\lfloor\frac{i}{2}\right\rfloor,

let
p=i-2k
. Then
k+p\leq\left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil-1
and
k+p=i-k
. By Lemma
4.14
, for any
F\subseteq[n]
with
|F|=p
, we have
\frac{\alpha^{i-k}}{{\bf x}_{F}}\in\mathrm{HS}_{i}(I^{i-k})
. It follows from
[
16
, Proposition 1.3]
that

\frac{\alpha^{i-k}}{{\bf x}_{F}}\cdot I^{s-i+k}\subseteq\mathrm{HS}_{i}(I^{s}).

Thus, all terms in the right-hand side are contained in
\mathrm{HS}_{i}(I^{s})
, so
\supseteq
holds. The converse inclusion follows from Proposition
4.12
. This completes the proof.

∎

As an immediate consequence of Theorem
4.15
, we obtain the generating degree of
\operatorname{HS}_{i}(\mathcal{R}(I))
.

Corollary 4.16
.

Let
G
be a cycle of length
n
and
I
be its complementary edge ideal. For all integers
1\leq i<\frac{n}{2}
, the
i
-th homological shift algebra
\operatorname{HS}_{i}(\mathcal{R}(I))
is generated in degree
\leq i-q
. Here,
q=\max\left\{i-\left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil+1,0\right\}
.

Let
L_{j}
denote the path with vertex set
[n]
and edge set
E(G)\setminus\{j-1,j\}
for
j=1,\ldots,n
. As before,
0
refers to the vertex
n
. Note that
L_{1},\ldots,L_{n}
are all the spanning trees of
G
.

Let
I_{j}
denote the complementary edge ideal of
L_{j}
for all
j
. By Lemma
1.3
, we have
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{HS}_{i}(I_{j}^{s})\subseteq\mathrm{HS}_{i}(I^{s})
. It is therefore natural to ask which part of the right-hand of Formula (
6
) corresponds to
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{HS}_{i}(I_{j}^{s})
. Recall that a
vertex cover
of a graph
G
is a subset
C
of
V(G)

such that every edge of
G
is incident to at least one vertex of
C
.

Remark 4.17
.

If
i<\frac{n}{2}
, then

\mathrm{HS}_{i}(I^{s})=\sum_{0\leq k\leq\left\lfloor\frac{i}{2}\right\rfloor}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\subseteq[n]\\
|F|=i-2k\end{subarray}}\frac{\alpha^{i-k}}{{\bf x}_{F}}\cdot I^{s-i+k}\right).

We claim that
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{HS}_{i}(I_{j}^{s})
corresponds to the
k=0
component of the expression above. More precisely,

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{HS}_{i}(I_{j}^{s})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\subseteq[n]\\
|F|=i\end{subarray}}\frac{\alpha^{i}}{{\bf x}_{F}}\cdot I^{s-i}\quad\text{for all }s\geq i.

In fact, by Theorem
4.3
,

\mathrm{HS}_{i}(I_{1}^{s})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\subseteq[n]\setminus\{1,n\}\\
|F|=i\end{subarray}}\frac{\alpha^{i}}{\mathbf{x}_{F}}I_{1}^{s-i},

and for
j=2,\ldots,n
,

\mathrm{HS}_{i}(I_{j}^{s})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\subseteq[n]\setminus\{j-1,j\}\\
|F|=i\end{subarray}}\frac{\alpha^{i}}{\mathbf{x}_{F}}I_{j}^{s-i}.

This establishes the inclusion
\subseteq
in the desired equality. For the converse, take any
F\subseteq[n]
with
|F|=i
. Since every vertex cover of
G
contains at least
\left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil
vertices,
F
cannot be a vertex cover of
G
. Thus, there exists some
j_{0}\in[n]
such that
F\cap\{j_{0}-1,j_{0}\}=\emptyset
, which implies
\frac{\alpha^{i}}{{\bf x}_{F}}\cdot I^{s-i}\subseteq\mathrm{HS}_{i}(I_{j_{0}}^{s})
. This proves the claim.

For the case where
i\geq\frac{n}{2}
, the same question can be posed. However, the answer in this scenario is not as elegant as that for
i<\frac{n}{2}
.

For the largest
i
satisfying
\mathrm{HS}_{i}(\mathcal{R}(I))\neq 0
,
\mathrm{HS}_{i}(I^{s})
admits a simple expression.

Proposition 4.18
.

Let
G
be a cycle of length
n
and
I
be its complementary edge ideal.

(1) If
n=2m+1
, then
\mathrm{HS}_{2m}(I^{s})=\alpha^{m}\cdot I^{s-m}
for
s\geq m
. In particular,
\mathrm{HS}_{2m}(\mathcal{R}(I))
is generated in degree
m
.

(2) If
n=2m
, then
\mathrm{HS}_{2m-2}(I^{s})=\alpha^{m-1}\cdot I^{s-m+1}
for
s\geq m-1
. In particular,
\mathrm{HS}_{2m-2}(\mathcal{R}(I))
is generated in degree
m-1
.

Proof.

(1) In Formula (
6
), the range of
k
is given by

\max\left\{i-\left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil+1,\ 0\right\}\leq k\leq\left\lfloor\frac{i}{2}\right\rfloor.

For
n=2m+1
and
i=2m
, substituting these values gives
\left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil=m+1
, so the left bound simplifies to
\max\{2m-(m+1)+1,\ 0\}=\max\{m,\ 0\}=m
. The right bound is
\left\lfloor\frac{2m}{2}\right\rfloor=m
, hence
k=m
is the only possible value. Thus, the result follows from Formula (
6
).

The proof of (2) is analogous.

∎
