New resistance threat in difficult-to-treat resistance Pseudomonas aeruginosa co-producing AFM and KPC carbapenemases: plasmid dynamic transfer and global phylogeography perspective
Authors: Haiyang Liu, Jiayao Yao, Chongmei Tian, Pengpeng Min, Longjie Zhou, Wenhao Wu, Minhua Chen, Robert A. Moran, Yunsong Yu, Xi Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMetallo-β-lactamase (MBL) production is one of the primary carbapenem resistance mechanisms in carbapenem-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa (CRPA). The emergence of the novel MBL gene blaAFM poses a significant threat to global public health. Concerningly, we have identified clinical CRPA strains co-producing AFM and the widely-disseminated carbapenemase KPC-2. Here, we describe AFM-producing, KPC-2-producing, and AFM/KPC-2 co-producing clinical CRPA isolates that were collected from three patients in two different hospital buildings in China. Comparative genomics suggested horizontal transfer of a blaAFM-2-harboring plasmid may have contributed to the spread of the AFM carbapenemase between different hospital areas, and to the emergence of dual carbapenemase-producing CRPA. Further epidemiological source tracing revealed the likely involvement of cross-patient nursing care and cross-area patient transfer in carbapenemase transmission. Experimental data confirmed the transfer ability of clinical blaAFM-2-bearing plasmids into P. aeruginosa PAO1. As the global epidemiology of blaAFM has not been systematically evaluated, we further examined 30,800 publicly available P. aeruginosa genome sequences. Including those generated in this study, blaAFM genes were detected in 36 isolates in total, which were derived from China (35/36) or Australia (1/36). AFM-containing genomes were sourced from six Chinese provinces, with 63.9% (23/36) isolated in Zhejiang between 2020 and 2024. The most prominent AFM-associated P. aeruginosa clone was ST463 (17/36 genomes). Our study highlights the concerning challenge presented by blaAFM-harboring CRPA in clinical settings. Horizontal transfer of blaAFM-bearing plasmids can contribute to difficult-to-treat resistance (DTR) phenotypes. Surveillance should be strengthened to prevent the further spread of these plasmids, particularly into and within ICUs.

Introduction

Pseudomonas aeruginosa (PA) is a major global healthcare-associated pathogen [1, 2] that causes ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP), bloodstream infections, and catheter-related urinary tract infections, especially in immunocompromised patients in intensive care units (ICUs) [3]. Infections caused by antibiotic resistant P. aeruginosa are usually associated with significant morbidity and mortality [4]. In particular, carbapenem-resistant (CRPA) and difficult-to-treat resistance (DTR-PA) P. aeruginosa pose an enormous challenge by severely limiting clinical treatment options [5, 6].

One of the primary carbapenem resistance mechanisms in CRPA is the production of carbapenemases, particularly metallo-β-lactamases (MBLs). Imipenemases (IMPs), Verona Integron-encoded metallo-β-lactamases (VIMs) and New Delhi metallo-β-lactamases (NDMs), are the most clinically relevant MBLs in CRPA isolates globally [7]. The prevalence of MBL-producing P. aeruginosa has been increasing in clinical settings worldwide [8]. For example, a multi-centre study covering 14 countries in Europe revealed a stark increase in the prevalence of MBL-producing P. aeruginosa, rising from 12.3% in 2010 to 30.6% in 2011 [9]. The presence of MBL-producing P. aeruginosa in hospitals can be associated with prolonged outbreaks of infections, as seen in a hospital in Upper Austria between 2017 and 2023 [10].

The novel MBL gene blaAFM-1, which encodes Alcaligenes faecalis metallo-β-lactamase (AFM), was first recognized in an Alcaligenes faecalis strain (A. faecalis AN70) in China in 2018 [11]. AFM confers resistance to all β-lactams apart from monobactams, and has been increasingly identified in clinical P. aeruginosa isolates. We have characterized the variant enzymes AFM-2 and AFM-3 from clinical CRPA strains, and AFM-4 from a hospital sink isolate, including modelling their structure and catalytic abilities [3, 12]. The blaAFM genes have been associated with translocatable genetic elements and plasmids [3], highlighting their potential for horizontal dissemination in bacterial populations.

To date, there have been few reports of AFM-producing clinical strains and their transmission in hospitals, and the global epidemiology of AFM-positive CRPA has not been systematically evaluated. To address these knowledge gaps, we aimed to explore the transmission of a blaAFM-harboring plasmid between CRPA strains and different ICU units in Hangzhou, China, and to further clarify our understanding of the global distribution of blaAFM genes in P. aeruginosa.

Materials and methods

Bacteria isolation and identification

P. aeruginosa isolates were collected during routine diagnostic analysis from 2021 to 2023 in a tertiary teaching hospital in Hangzhou, China. In total, 725 nonduplicated CRPA isolates were included. Isolates identification was performed by matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization-time of flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) technology (bioMérieux, Marcy l'Etoile, France) and the species was further confirmed via fIDBAC platform (http://fbac.dmicrobe.cn/submitTask/pipline) based on genome sequencing data [13].

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Susceptibility of eleven antimicrobial agents, including cefepime (FEP), ceftazidime (CAZ), imipenem (IPM), meropenem (MEM), amikacin (AMK), ciprofloxacin (CIP), levofloxacin (LEV), piperacillin-tazobactam (TZP), aztreonam (ATM), colistin (COL) and ceftazidime-avibactam (CZA), was determined using broth microdilution method and cefoperazone-sulbactam (CSL) was measured using Kirby-Bauer (KB) disk diffusion method. Results were interpreted according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) 2021 guidelines and European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) 2021 breakpoint tables (http://www.eucast.org/clinical_breakpoints/) for colistin. P. aeruginosa ATCC 27,853 isolate was used as a quality control.

Plasmid conjugation experiments and plasmid stability assays

The blaKPC-2 and/or blaAFM-2 positive P. aeruginosa was used as the donor strain, the rifampin-resistant P. aeruginosa PAO1 (for PA1590, PA7219 and PA8483) and tetracycline-resistant P. aeruginosa PAO1 (for PA3860) were used as the recipient strains [14]. The bacterial solution of the donor and recipient bacteria in the logarithmic growth phase was mixed at 1:1 ratio and dropped onto filter membranes for overnight culture. Screening of overnight cultures with corresponding selection plates (for blaKPC-2-harbouring plasmid (PA8483): 800 μg/mL rifampin, 4 μg/mL meropenem, 50 μg/mL aztreonam; for blaAFM-2-harbouring plasmid (PA1590 and PA7219): 800 μg/mL rifampin, 4 μg/mL meropenem, 16 μg/mL ceftazidime-avibactam); for blaAFM-2-harbouring plasmid (PA3860): 400 μg/mL tetracycline and 16 μg/mL ceftazidime-avibactam. Moreover, conjugation experiments were further performed using the clinical isolates directly. PA3860 was used as the donor strain, PA8483 clinical strain was used as the recipient strain and screened on the selection plates (4 μg/mL meropenem, 50 μg/mL aztreonam and 16 μg/mL ceftazidime-avibactam). Identification of transconjugants by antimicrobial susceptibility testing test and PCR with specific primers detecting [blaAFM-2, blaKPC-2, and a segment of the PAO1 chromosome]. Independent experiments were performed in triplicate.

To determine the stability of plasmids in the clinical isolates and transconjugants, stability experiments for blaKPC-2 and blaAFM-2 carrying plasmids were performed as described previously [15]. Briefly, a single colony was inoculated into 2 mL of LB broth without antibiotics and grown at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm. Overnight cultures were diluted 1:1000 each day using fresh antibiotic-free LB broth. After five or ten days, the bacterial solution was spread on antibiotic-free MHA plates for overnight incubation. Then, 150 colonies were randomly selected, inoculated on MHA plates containing 4 μg/mL meropenem and the presence of blaKPC-2 and blaAFM-2 was confirmed by PCR.

Relative growth rate determination

To evaluate the fitness of different P. aeruginosa strains with or without blaKPC-2/blaAFM-2 plasmids, growth ability was measured as previously reported [16, 17]. Briefly, P. aeruginosa strains were streaked onto MHA plates, followed by overnight culture at 37°C. Four independent cultures for each strain were grown overnight, diluted 1:1000 using MH broth and added into a flat-bottom honeycomb 100-well plate (Saue vald, Estonia) in three replicates. The plate was incubated at 37°C with agitation. The optical density of each culture was determined every 5 min for 24 h using a Bioscreen C MBR machine (Oy Growth Curves Ab Ltd., Finland). Growth curve, area under the growth curve and relative growth rate were calculated using R script and GraphPad Prism 8.0.2, respectively.

Virulence assessment by Galleria mellonella infection model

Galleria mellonella infection model was used to assess the virulence level of the strains [18,19]. Briefly, log-phase bacterial cultures were centrifuged and resuspended using phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and underwent serial dilutions to 1 × 105 CFU/mL. 10 μL volume (1 × 103 CFU) was injected into G. mellonella larvae (n = 10) using the Hamilton syringe (Hamilton, USA). G. mellonella larvae were incubated at 37°C and survival was recorded every 12 h and monitored for 72 h. P. aeruginosa PA14 was used as a positive control [7], PBS was served as negative control. Experiments were carried out in triplicate independently.

Short-read and long-read whole-genome sequencing

To explore the plasmid dissemination and dynamic evolution of DTR P. aeruginosa co-producing AFM and KPC carbapenemases, four related P. aeruginosa isolates (PA3860, PA1590, PA7219 and PA8483) were subjected to short- and long-read whole-genome sequencing. For Illumina sequencing, DNA was isolated using the Qiagen Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Libraries were prepared and paired-end sequencing (2 × 150-bp reads) was performed on the Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). For long-read sequencing, genomic DNA was extracted using the Gentra® Puregene® Yeast/Bact. Kit (Qiagen, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Libraries were prepared using the SQU-LSK109 Ligation Sequencing Kit (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, UK) in conjunction with the PCR-Free ONT EXP-NBD104 Native Barcode Expansion Kit (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, UK) according to the native barcoding genomic DNA protocol and sequenced on the MinION Oxford Nanopore platform (MinION system, Nanopore, Oxford, UK).

Bioinformatic analyses

Hybrid assemblies of filtered Nanopore and Illumina reads were assembled using Raven v1.1.10 with error correction (Pilon v1.24) [20,21]. Prokka 1.11 was used for gene annotation [22]. Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) of strains and plasmid replicon types of plasmids were performed via the Center for Genomic Epidemiology (CGE) server (http://www.genomicepidemiology.org/). Antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) were identified using the ResFinder database with ABRicate v0.8.13. Insertion sequences (ISs) elements were identified using ISFinder (https://www-is.biotoul.fr/) [23]. Sequence comparisons were performed using BLASTn v2.4.0 [24]. Plasmid comparisons were conducted using BLAST Ring Image Generator (BRIG). Transmission diagrams of strains and plasmids was drawn via BioRender (https://www.biorender.com/).

Global analyses for AFM-positive isolates

To explore the global distribution of AFM-positive isolates, 30,800 P. aeruginosa genome sequences were obtained from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database (search conducted on 4.4.2024). All P. aeruginosa genome sequences were analysed with mlst v2.19.0 and ABRicate v1.0.1 with the Resfinder database [25], aiming to identify sequence types (STs) and antibiotic resistance genes. Quality was assessed using FastANI v1.33 and QUAST v5.0.2 [26]. Snippy v4.4.5 (https://github.com/tseemann/snippy) was used to align Illumina reads against the complete genome of P. aeruginosa PA1590 to generate a core genome alignment. Polymorphic sites were extracted with Gubbins v2.4.0 excluding those that were predicted to occur via recombination. Phylogenies were constructed from these polymorphic sites using FastTree [27]. The resulting tree was annotated and visualized using ChiPlot (https://www.chiplot.online/). Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms (SNPs) were calculated using SNP-dists V0.6.3 (https://github.com/tseemann/snp-dists) and the matrix was further visualized with ChiPlot using the MaxAbsScaler algorithm standardization.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 8.0.2 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego California USA) was used to evaluate the statistical significance using Student's t-test for relative growth rate and area under the growth curve. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

CRPA were derived from three patients from two distinct hospital areas

CRPA isolates described in this study were obtained from three different patients who were treated in ICUs located in two different hospital buildings in the same city. The ZH and WJS areas are both part of Zhejiang Provincial People's Hospital in Hangzhou (ZPPH), but the buildings that house them are 20 km apart. The WJS area primarily accepts aged care patients, many of whom are transferred from the ZH area.

Patient A, a 97-year-old female, was admitted to the rehabilitation ward (RW, 5–12 unit) in ZH area on 04.04.2021 due to osteoporosis with pathological fractures (Figure 1(A)). During hospitalization, Patient A received piperacillin-tazobactam (PTZ), levofloxacin (LEV), polymyxin (POL), cefperazone/sulbactam (CFZ/SBT), meropenem (MEM) and ceftazidime (CAZ). During 04.06.2021–05.08.2021 and in 2022, Patient A was transferred to the ICU ward (6-5 unit) twice in the same area (ZH area). P. aeruginosa PA3860 was collected from Patient A’s sputum on 02.08.2021 in the ICU ward in ZH area. Patient B, an 84-year-old male, was admitted to the 6–5 ICU unit in ZH area on 03.12.2022 due to unstable angina (Figure 1(B)) and was treated with imipenem (IPM) and POL. Patient B was transferred from ZH area to WJS area on 16.01.2023. After a course of antimicrobial agents that included CFZ/SBT, POL, latamoxef (MOX), PTZ, MEM, IPM, and LEV, two CRPA isolates were obtained from Patient B in two locations in the WJS area. PA8483 was isolated from sputum on 01.08.2023 in rehabilitation ward 2 (RW2, 5–2 unit) and PA1590 was collected from bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) in another ICU (ICU3, 11–2 unit) on 08.11.2023.

Patient C, a 70-year-old male, was admitted to the RW2 (5-2 unit) in WJS area on 03.08.2023 owing to coma and cerebral infarction (Figure 1(C)). Patient C received CAZ, CFZ/SBT, LEV and MEM over the course of his treatment. PA7219 was isolated from a sputum sample on 17.11.2023.

As for all three patients, a number of risk factors for P. aeruginosa nosocomial infection have been identified: including admission to ICU, exposure history to broad-spectrum antibiotics, mechanical ventilation, and usage of multiple invasive devices. Moreover, Patient A and Patient B were also found to be colonized or infected with other carbapenem-resistant organisms (CRO). In terms of the outcomes of the three patients, clinical data showed the Patient A was died at discharge, whereas patients B and C were survival when discharged.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing showed that PA3860, PA8483 and PA1590 were resistant to all tested antimicrobial agents, except for colistin, to which they displayed an intermediate phenotype (Figure 1(D)). PA7219 was also found to be intermediate to LEV (Figure 1(D)). Based on the difficult-to-treat resistance (DTR) phenotypic definition, all four isolates were classified as DTR-PA.

Genome characteristics of the blaAFM-2 or blaKPC-2-positive CRPA clinical isolates

Complete genome sequences for all four CRPA isolates were generated from Illumina and Nanopore data. The isolates were of three sequence types: PA3860 from Patient A was ST262, PA7219 from Patient C was ST207, and PA8483 and PA1590 from Patient B were both ST1212. Whole-genome comparison revealed that PA8483 and PA1590 differed by just one core genome single nucleotide polymorphism (cgSNP), suggesting that they are the same strain.

Isolates PA3860 and PA7219 carried blaAFM-2, PA8483 carried blaKPC-2, and PA1590 carried blaAFM-2 and blaKPC-2 (Table S1). Consistent with their close relationship, PA8483 and PA1590 carry blaKPC-2 in the same 52 kb p10547A-like plasmid, which was first identified in a P. aeruginosa isolated in Nanjing, China [28]. Across the AFM-producing isolates, blaAFM-2 was found in IncP-2 pBT2101-like plasmids that ranged in size from 437.5 to 498.4 kb.

The repA genes of the three blaAFM-2-bearing plasmids sequenced here were identical to one another and to that of the blaAFM-2-bearing plasmid pNDTH10366 (GenBank accession CP064402). In all three plasmids, the blaAFM-2 gene was located in the same ISCR29-associated unit found in pNDTH10366, which we described in our previous study [3]. These traits suggest that the blaAFM-2-bearing plasmids in ZPPH belong to the same lineage that has previously been associated with carbapenem resistance in Eastern and Central Chinese cities [3].

pBT2101-like plasmids are known to have dynamic structures that can be subject to extensive recombination events [29]. Comparing the structures of the blaAFM-2-bearing plasmids here revealed that they were generally well conserved in terms of content and nucleotide identity, but featured some striking structural differences (Figure 2). These differences were clearly apparent in the total sizes of the plasmids, which differed by as much as 60 kb. Conserved sequences (99.99% nucleotide identity) accounted for 92% and 89% of pPA3860-AFM relative to pPA7219-AFM and pPA1590, while 97% of pPA7219-AFM was accounted for by pPA1590-AFM with 100% nucleotide identity (Figure 3). Closer examination of the structural variation between these plasmids revealed one instance where a significant change in size appeared to be associated with a mobile genetic element (MGE), namely ISPpu29 and the loss of a 14.8 kb segment by pPA1590-AFM relative to pPA3860-AFM. Other significant acquisitions or losses of plasmid segments were not associated with MGEs and might have been the products of recombination events. Plasmid transfer among patients and dynamic transmission model construction

To explain the dissemination of the blaAFM-2 gene observed here, we used isolate metadata and patient movement data to construct a timeline and putative transmission diagram. The first AFM-positive CRPA described here was isolated from patient A in August 2021, four months after Patient A was admitted to ZH area. Patient A remained in ZH area and was present when Patient B was admitted in December 2022. That month, one nurse provided care to both Patient A and Patient B. In January 2023, Patient B was transferred from ZH area to WJS area, where both PA8483 and PA1590 were isolated in August and November, respectively. The blaAFM-2-bearing plasmid of PA3860 from Patient A may have transferred to PA8483 of patient B, generating PA1590 (Figure 3). Patient C was admitted to WJS area in August 2023, and his stay overlapped that of Patient B. PA7219 was isolated from Patient C in November 2023, nine days after PA1590 was isolated from Patient B. It is possible that the blaAFM-2 – bearing plasmid of PA7219 was acquired from PA1590.

An alternative explanation for the transmission of blaAFM-2 in these settings is that the pBT2101-like plasmid lineage persisted in a population of hosts in the nosocomial environment, accumulating variations, before being acquired by the strains responsible for patient infections that were sequenced here.

Transfer, stability and the influence of blaKPC-2 or blaAFM-2-carrying plasmids on host fitness

In conjugation experiments, the blaAFM-2-bearing plasmid of PA3860 did not transfer to clinical isolate PA8483. However, transconjugants were obtained at a conjugation frequency of 2.38 × 10−8 when PA3860 was mated with P. aeruginosa PAO1. Moreover, pPA1590_KPC-2 plasmid could transfer to P. aeruginosa PAO1 with a conjugation frequency of 5.12 × 10−3. However, no transconjugants were obtained for pPA1590_AFM-2 plasmid. Likewise, pPA8483_KPC-2 could also transfer to P. aeruginosa PAO1 with a conjugation frequency of 3.00 × 10−3. Of note, the pPA7219_1 (AFM-2) plasmid in PA7219 failed to transfer to P. aeruginosa PAO1 after three attempts.

Stability assays were conducted to evaluate the stability of blaAFM-2 and blaKPC-2-bearing plasmids in clinical isolates and PAO1. These revealed that blaAFM-2-carrying plasmids were lost from transconjugant populations over time, with a decrease in plasmid carriage over 10 days from 100% to 0-26% of tested colonies for the blaAFM-2-harboring plasmid and 100% to 15-33% for the blaKPC-2-harboring plasmid except PAO1/pPA8483_KPC-2 (Figure 4(A)). To further evaluate the effect of the blaAFM-2 or blaKPC-2 plasmid on fitness, growth ability was measured and further analysed using three parameters (growth curve, area under the growth curve [AUC] and the relative growth rate). Experimental results revealed transconjugants containing the blaKPC-2 plasmid exhibited decreased fitness compared to plasmid-free PAO1 (P < 0.01) (Figure 4(B–D)). A reduced relative growth rate was also observed for the transconjugant containing the blaAFM-2 plasmid (P < 0.01). However, no significant difference was shown in AUC between P. aeruginosa PAO1 and corresponding transconjugants. Virulence assessment

Ninety percent of the larvae died after 12 h post-injection in the case of the virulent strain P. auerginosa PA14 strain (Figure 4(E)). Based on the survival curve, PA8483 and PA7219 both have a relatively high virulence but slightly lower than P. auerginosa PA14. Moreover, no significant differences were detected for PA3860 and PA1590 in the first 24 h. However, all larvae died after 48 h for PA3860 and PA1590 still possesses a 30% survival rate (Figure 4(E)).

Global spatiotemporal distribution of blaAFM-positive P. aeruginosa

We sought to assess the global spatiotemporal distribution of blaAFM-containing P. auerginosa isolates (Figure 5(A)). In a collection of 30,800 publicly available whole genome sequences and the genomes generated this study (apart from PA8483), we found 36 that contained blaAFM genes. Apart from one isolated in Australia in 2018, all blaAFM-containing genomes were derived from China (Table S2). Within China, blaAFM-containing genomes were isolated between 2013 and 2024 from Zhejiang, Jiangsu, Shanghai, Beijing, Hubei and Guangdong regions (Figure 5(B,C)). The largest number of blaAFM-positive isolates, 23, were collected in Zhejiang between 2020 and 2024 (Figure 5(B)). Features of blaAFM-containing genomes

The 36 blaAFM-containing P. aeruginosa genomes were of 15 different STs, with the largest proportion ST463 (47.2%, 17/36), followed by ST262 (13.9%, 5/36) and ST316 (5.6%, 2/36). The main AFM type is AFM-2 (18/36), followed by AFM-1 (16/36). The AFM-3 and AFM-4 are rare. Of the 17 ST463 P. aeruginosa isolates, 10 carried blaAFM-1 and 7 blaAFM-2 (Figure 6(A)). A phylogenetic tree and similarity matrix were generated for the 36 blaAFM-containing genomes (Figure 6(B,C)). These showed that isolates from the adjacent Jiangsu and Zhejiang provinces were closely related (Figure 6(B)). The genome from Australia was ST357, but this ST was not represented amongst the genomes from China (Figure 6(A)).

To clarify the association between blaAFM genes and plasmids related to pNDTH10366, all 36 genomes were screened for the presence of pNDTH1036 repA gene (repApNDTH10366), which was found in the blaAFM-2-bearing plasmids from the Hangzhou hospital reported in this study. The repApNDTH10366 gene was found in 22/36 blaAFM-containing genomes (61.1%), including genomes from multiple provinces, confirming its strong association with AFM determinants.

Discussion

CRPA in hospitals represent a significant healthcare burden, increasing mortality rates, lengths of hospital stay, and overall patient treatment costs [30]. Clinical CRPA isolates have been reported to produce a wide variety of carbapenemases globally [1], such as KPC, GES, IMP, VIM, NDM, and SPM in Latin America [31], KPC, NDM, VIM, and IMP in the United States [9], and KPC, IMP, and AFM in China [32, 33]. Given the widely-recognized global impact of carbapenemases, it is crucial to study newly emerging enzymes while they remain geographically restricted. To this end, we conducted this study to further characterize the local and international impact of blaAFM genes, which have recently emerged in China. Our findings emphasize the transmission potential of blaAFM genes in hospital settings, as well as their important role in the generation of highly clinically relevant DTR-PA.

We found that, to date, the detection rate of AFM-producing CRPA isolates has been highest in Zhejiang Province, followed by Jiangsu and Shanghai. These locations in Eastern China are in close geographical proximity, and the prevalence of AFM-producing CRPA in this region is consistent with significant local spread. AFM-producing isolates have also been found in the more distant Hubei, Guangdong, and Beijing. The blaAFM-containing genome derived from Australia is a concerning indication of the potential for this gene to disseminate internationally. Crucially, 15 different STs are represented amongst just 36 AFM-producing CRPA isolates. This highlights the capacity for horizontal transmission of AFM genes in P. aeruginosa, indicating that the spread of AFM will not be dependent on the individual success of CRPA clones.

Plasmid transfer is a key factor in the spread of antimicrobial resistance amongst clinically relevant bacteria worldwide [25,34–37]. pBT2101-like plasmids related to pNDTH10366, which we have described previously [3], were strongly associated with blaAFM-containing P. aeruginosa (co-occurring in 22/36 genomes), and in this study we observed their apparent spread between clinical CRPA clones and hospital buildings. Taken together, the evidence suggests that plasmids related to pNDTH10366 are playing an important and ongoing role in the dissemination of AFM determinants in P. aeruginosa. However, 14/36 genomes examined here did not contain repApNDTH10366. This suggests that the blaAFM genes in these 14 isolates are present in different plasmids, in cointegrate plasmids that have lost repApNDTH10366, or in chromosomal locations. Further work will be required to assess these alternative contexts and the intermolecular mobility of the blaAFM-ISCR29 unit.

A limitation of this study is the lack of direct links regarding the transmission of blaAFM-2-bearing between the CRPA strains isolated here. We reason that, because the patients involved had stayed in the same area of the hospital and were cared for by the same nurse, it is most likely that they were exposed to a common source in the ICU from which they acquired their AFM-producing CRPA. However, variation in the structures of blaAFM-bearing plasmids carried by these CRPA obfuscates attempts to trace plasmid transmission. It is possible that this blaAFM-bearing plasmid lineage has persisted in the hospital for a prolonged period, and has passed through multiple hosts as it diversified. Unfortunately, no such possible intermediate hosts were sequenced here, given the focus on isolates causing clinical infections. Further work is needed to understand the evolutionary dynamics of pNDTH10366-like plasmids in P. aeruginosa, and the ability of these plasmids to persistent in nosocomial populations. While contact with the same medical staff may have contributed to plasmid dissemination here, we did not find direct evidence to support this hypothesis. Despite this, we recommend that hand hygiene should be further strengthened, particularly for the nurses who provide direct care to multiple patients in the ICU.

In conclusion, we observed the impact of blaAFM-2-bearing pNDTH10366-like plasmids in two distinct hospital buildings, and in two patients who spent time in the same unit and were cared for by the same nurse. Whether plasmid transmission was associated with the hospital environment, staff, or other factors, this work emphasizes the importance of thorough infection prevention and control strategies in curtailing the spread of resistance plasmids in nosocomial settings. From a global perspective, it will be important to continue to monitor the spread of blaAFM genes and the plasmids that carry them. We conclude that AFM-producing CRPA strains are an emerging high-risk pathogen, and effective targeted IPC strategies for ICU staff, patients and environment should be reinforced to avoid further global dissemination.
