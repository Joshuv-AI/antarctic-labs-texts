Maximal determinants of matrices over the roots of unity
Guillermo Nuñez Ponasso

Abstract
We study the maximum absolute value of the determinant of matrices with entries in the set of ℓ \ell -th roots of unity — this is a generalization of D D -optimal designs and Hadamard’s maximal determinant problem, which involves ± 1 \pm 1 matrices. For general values of ℓ \ell , we give sharpened determinantal upper bounds and constructions of matrices of large determinant. The maximal determinant problem in the cases ℓ = 3 \ell=3 , ℓ = 4 \ell=4 is similar to the classical Hadamard maximal determinant problem for matrices with entries ± 1 \pm 1 , and many techniques can be generalized. For ℓ = 3 \ell=3 we give and additional construction of matrices with large determinant, and calculate the value of the maximal determinant over μ 3 \mu_{3} for all orders n < 14 n<14 . Additionally, we survey the case ℓ = 4 \ell=4 and exhibit an infinite family of maximal determinant matrices over the fourth roots of unity.

If

Hadamard’s maximal determinant problem is simple to state: If

In this paper, we extend Hadamard’s maximal determinant problem to matrices over the roots of unity: Letting
\mathcal{M}_{\ell}(n)=\{M\in\Mat_{n}(\C):M_{ij}\in\mu_{\ell},\text{ for }1\leq i,j\leq n\}

\gamma_{\ell}(n)=\max\{|\det M|:M\in\mathcal{M}_{\ell}(n)\}.

(4)

This problem depends heavily on the behavior of the sums of
\ell
-th roots of unity, particularly on their minimal absolute value. In the cases

In the case

The case

2
General upper and lower bounds

For matrices with entries in
\mu_{2}=\{\pm 1\}
, Guido Barba established the following strengthening of Hadamard’s bound:

Theorem 2

(Barba, 1931
[
5
]
)
.

Let
M
be a
n\times n
matrix with entries
\pm 1
, with
n
odd. Then

|\det M|\leq\sqrt{2n-1}(n-1)^{(n-1)/2}.

(5)

Furthermore,
M
meets the bound with equality if and only if
M
is monomially equivalent to a matrix
B
satisfying

BB^{*}=(n-1)I_{n}+J_{n}.

(6)

In
[
15
]
, Cohn showed that this result also holds for matrices with entries in
\mu_{4}
. Later we will show that Theorem
2
is also true for matrices with entries in
\mu_{3}
. For other values of
\ell
, we show a more general form of Theorem
2
which depends on the minimal modulus of sums of
\ell
-th roots “of length
n
”.

2.1
A generalization of Barba’s bound

We begin with two auxiliary results from which Barba’s determinant bound
[
5
]
, and Cohn’s extension
[
15
]
, can be obtained. Both results appeared in Wojtas’ paper
[
51
]
for symmetric positive-definite matrices. Their proofs can be adapted to the show the equivalent results for Hermitian positive-definite matrices. Recall the following result:

Theorem 3

(Muir-Kelvin bound; cf., Theorem 7.8.1.
[
27
]
)
.

Let
G
be an
n\times n
positive-definite matrix. Then

|\det G|\leq\prod_{i=1}^{n}g_{ii},

(7)

and equality holds above if and only if
G
is diagonal.

Lemma 1
.

Let
B
be a
k\times k
Hermitian positive-definite matrix, written in block form as:

B=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}G_{k-1}&\beta\\
\hline\cr\beta^{*}&b\end{array}\right],

(8)

where the diagonal entries of
G_{k-1}=(g_{ij})_{ij}
are all equal to
m
. If
0<b\leq|\beta_{i}|
for all
1\leq i\leq k
, then

\det B\leq b(m-b)^{k},

(9)

and
B
achieves this bound with equality of and only if
|\beta_{i}|=b
, and
g_{ij}=\beta_{i}\beta_{j}^{*}/b
for all
i\neq j
.

Proof.

A series of (Hermitian) elementary row and column operations shows that

\det B=\det\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}G_{k-1}-\beta\beta^{*}/b&0\\
\hline\cr 0&b\end{array}\right]=b\det(G_{k-1}-\beta\beta^{*}/b).

(10)

Sylvester’s criterion implies that the Hermitian matrix
D=(G_{k-1}-\beta\beta^{*}/b)
is positive-definite, so we can apply the Muir-Kelvin bound (Theorem
3
) to it, and obtain:

\det(G_{k-1}-\beta\beta^{*}/b)\leq\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(m-\frac{|\beta_{i}|^{2}}{b}\right)\leq\prod_{i=1}^{k}(m-|\beta_{i}|)\leq(m-b)^{k}.

(11)

The first inequality above is an equality if and only if
D
is diagonal, which is equivalent to
g_{ij}=\beta_{i}\beta_{j}^{*}/b
for all
i\neq j
. The remaining inequalities are equalities if and only if
|\beta_{i}|=b
for all
i
.∎

Proposition 1
.

Let
G
be an
n\times n
Hermitian positive-definite matrix, with diagonal entries equal to
m
. If
b
is a positive real number such that
b\leq|G_{ij}|
for all
i\neq j
, then

\det G\leq(m+(n-1)b)(m-b)^{n-1}.

Additionally,
G
meets this bound with equality if and only if there is a (necessarily unique) diagonal matrix
\Delta=\diag(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
, with
|d_{i}|=1
for all
i
, such that

\Delta^{*}G\Delta=(n-b)I_{n}+bJ_{n}.

(12)

Proof.

Proceeding by induction on
n
, we first show that the result holds for
n=2
:

\det\begin{bmatrix}m&g_{12}\\
g_{12}^{*}&m\end{bmatrix}=m^{2}-|g_{12}|^{2}\leq m^{2}-b^{2}=(m+b)(m-b).

(13)

Now, suppose the result holds for all matrices of order
n-1
and let
G
be an
n\times n
matrix satisfying the hypotheses. Write,

G=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}G_{n-1}&\gamma\\
\hline\cr\gamma^{*}&m\end{array}\right],

(14)

where
\gamma=(g_{1n},g_{2n},\dots,g_{n-1,n})^{\intercal}
. By linearity of the determinant on rows:

\det G=\det\underbrace{\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}G_{n-1}&\gamma\\
\hline\cr\mathbf{0}_{n-1}^{\intercal}&m-b\end{array}\right]}_{\textstyle A}+\det\underbrace{\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}G_{n-1}&\gamma\\
\hline\cr\gamma^{*}&b\end{array}\right]}_{\textstyle B}

(15)

If
\det(B)>0
, then by Sylvester’s criterion, the matrix
B
is positive-definite, so we can apply Lemma
1
to it, and obtain

\det G=(m-b)\det(G_{n-1})+\det(B)\leq(m-b)\det(G_{n-1})+b(m-b)^{n-1}.

(16)

If
\det(B)\leq 0
, then trivially
\det G\leq(m-b)\det(G_{n-1})\leq(m-b)\det(G_{n-1})+b(m-b)^{n-1}.
Now, use the induction hypothesis on
G_{n-1}
to obtain:

\begin{split}\det G&\leq(m-b)(m+(n-2)b)(m-b)^{n-2}+b(m-b)^{n-1}\\
&=(m+(n-1)b)(m-b)^{n-1}.\end{split}

(17)

The matrix
G
meets this bound with equality if and only if
\det(B)=b(m-b)^{n-1}
. By Lemma
1
, this is characterized by
|\gamma_{i}|=|g_{in}|=b
for all
i
, and
g_{ij}=\gamma_{i}\gamma_{j}^{*}/b=g_{in}g_{jn}^{*}/b
. Let
\Delta=\diag(g_{1n}/b,\dots,g_{n-1,n}/b,1)
, and let
G^{\prime}=\Delta^{*}G\Delta
. Then,
g^{\prime}_{in}=b
for
1\leq i\leq n-1
, and since the diagonal entries of
\Delta
have modulus
1
, it follows that
\det G^{\prime}=\det G
. Decomposing
G^{\prime}=A^{\prime}+B^{\prime}
as in Equation
15
, we have
\det B^{\prime}=b(m-b)^{n-1}
, and by Lemma
1

g^{\prime}_{ij}=g^{\prime}_{in}g^{\prime}_{jn}/b=b.
This implies that

G^{\prime}=(n-b)I_{n}+bJ_{n},

(18)

from which we conclude the result.∎

To apply Proposition
1
to matrices with entries in
\mu_{\ell}
we first introduce some terminology and notation: An
n
-sum of
\ell
-th roots
is a sum of
n
elements taken from the set
\mu_{\ell}
. Denote by
\sigma_{\ell}(n)
the minimum absolute value of an
n
-sum of
\ell
-th roots, i.e.

\sigma_{\ell}(n)=\min\left\{|a_{0}+a_{1}\zeta_{\ell}+\dots+a_{\ell-1}\zeta^{\ell-1}|:a_{i}\in[n],\ \text{ and }\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}a_{i}=n\right\},

(19)

where
[n]:=\{0,1,\dots,n\}
. Equivalently,
\sigma_{\ell}(n)
is equal to the minimal absolute value of the inner product of two vectors of length
n
with entries in
\mu_{\ell}
.

Theorem 4

(Generalized Barba bound. Theorem 5.2.3
[
39
]
)
.

Let
M
be a matrix of order
n
with entries in
\mu_{\ell}
. Suppose that
\sigma_{\ell}(n)>0
. Then,

|\det M|\leq\sqrt{(n+(n-1)\sigma_{\ell}(n)}(n-\sigma_{\ell}(n))^{(n-1)/2}.

(20)

Furthermore,
M
achieves the bound with equality if and only if there exists a diagonal matrix
\Delta=\diag(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
, with
|d_{i}|=1
for all
i
, such that the matrix
B=\Delta^{*}M
satisfies

BB^{*}=(n-\sigma_{\ell}(n))I_{n}+\sigma_{\ell}(n)J_{n}

(21)

Proof.

Apply Proposition
1
to the Gram matrix
G=MM^{*}
, noticing that
|G_{ij}|\geq\sigma_{\ell}(n)
.∎

In general, the matrix
B
of Theorem
4
may be inequivalent to
M
in the sense of Definition
1
, since the entries of
\Delta=\diag(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
need not belong to
\mu_{\ell}
. However, for
\ell=3
we have:

Corollary 1
.

Let
M
be an
n\times n
matrix with entries in
\mu_{3}=\{1,\omega,\omega^{2}\}
. Then, if
n>2
and
n\equiv 1,2\pmod{3}
,

|\det M|\leq\sqrt{2n-1}(n-1)^{(n-1)/2}.

(22)

Furthermore,
M
meets this bound with equality if and only if
M
is monomially equivalent to a matrix
B
satisfying

BB^{*}=(n-1)I_{n}+J_{n}.

(23)

Proof.

The ring
\Z[\omega]
is the hexagonal lattice in
\C
, and from this, it is easy to see that
n\equiv 1,2\pmod{3}
implies
\sigma_{3}(n)=1
. Using Theorem
4
, we find that Inequality
22
holds in this case. One can also show (see Lemma 5.2.1. in
[
39
]
) that if
\sigma
is an
n
-sum of third-roots with
|\sigma|=1
, then

\begin{split}&\text{if }n\equiv 1\pmod{3},\ \sigma\in\{1,\omega,\omega^{2}\},\text{ and }\\
&\text{if }n\equiv 2\pmod{3},\ \sigma\in\{-1,-\omega,-\omega^{2}\}.\end{split}

(24)

Suppose that
M
achieves equality in Inequality
22
. Let
G=MM^{*}
and
\Delta=\diag(g_{1n},\dots,g_{n-1,n},1)
, the the matrix
B=\Delta^{*}M
satisfies
BB^{*}=(n-1)I_{n}+J_{n}
. To conclude that
B
is monomially equivalent to
M
we only need to show that the diagonal entries of
\Delta
are third roots of unity: By the proof of Proposition
1
, the identity
g_{ij}=g_{in}g_{jn}^{*}
holds for
i\neq j
. Since
n>2
, we have in particular that
g_{12}=g_{13}g_{23}^{*}
; considering this equation modulo
(1-\omega)
, we find

n\equiv g_{12}=g_{13}g_{23}^{*}\equiv n^{2}\equiv 1\pmod{(1-\omega)}

(25)

This implies
n\equiv 1\pmod{3}
, and in particular all elements
g_{in}
are third roots of unity. So the entries of
B
also belong to
\mu_{3}
.
∎

Definition 2
.

We say that an
n\times n
matrix
B
with entries of modulus
1
is a
Barba matrix
if
BB^{*}=(n-1)I_{n}+J_{n}
.

Remark 1
.

The proof of Corollary
1
shows that Barba matrices over the third roots can only exist at orders
n\equiv 1\pmod{3}

Theorem 5
.

Let
B
be a Barba matrix with entries in
\mu_{\ell}
,
\ell=2,3,4
. Then there is a normal Barba matrix with entries in
\mu_{\ell}
and constant row and column sum.

Proof.

By definition,
BB^{*}=(n-1)I_{n}+J_{n}
, and
|\det(B)|=\sqrt{2n-1}(n-1)^{(n-1)/2}
. Since
|\det(B)|=|\det(B^{*})|
, then
B^{*}
also meets the Barba bound with equality and there is a diagonal matrix
\Delta
with diagonal entries in
\mu_{\ell}
such that

\Delta^{*}B^{*}B\Delta=(n-1)I_{n}+J_{n}.

(26)

Let
N=B\Delta^{*}
, then
NN^{*}=N^{*}N=(n-1)I_{n}+J_{n}
. Furthermore, associativity implies
(NN^{*})N=N(N^{*}N)
, so
N
commutes with
J_{n}
, i.e.,
NJ_{n}=J_{n}N
, so
N
has a constant row sum and column sum.
∎

Remark 2
.

The assumption that
\ell=2,3,4
is only used here to guarantee that the matrix
N
will have its entries in
\mu_{\ell}
. We showed this fact here for
\ell=3
; the case
\ell=2
is found in
[
5
,
51
]
, and the case
\ell=4
in
[
15
]
.

Corollary 2

(cf. Neubauer-Radcliffe; Brouwer
[
8
,
36
]
)
.

There is a Barba matrix with entries
\pm 1
of order
(q+1)^{2}+q^{2}
for every odd prime power
q
.

Proof.

Let
B
be a
\pm 1
Barba matrix of order
n
; by Theorem
5
, we may assume that
B
is normal and has constant row and column sum
\alpha
. Let
N=(J_{n}-B)/2
be the
(0,1)
-matrix obtained by replacing the entries equal to
+1
in
B
with
0
, and
-1
with
+1
. Since
B
has constant row sum
\alpha
, we have that
\alpha J_{n}=BJ_{n}
and
NJ_{n}=J_{n}N=kJ_{n}
, where
k:=(n-\alpha)/2\in\Z
. Since
B=J_{n}-2N
, we find

(n-1)I_{n}+J_{n}=BB^{\intercal}=4NN^{\intercal}+(n-4k)J_{n}.

(27)

From where we conclude,

NN^{\intercal}=\frac{n-1}{4}I_{n}+(k-\frac{n-1}{4})J_{n}.

(28)

This implies that
N
is the incidence matrix of a symmetric
2
-design with parameters
(v,k,\lambda)=(n,k,k-(n-1)/4)
. Re-writing these parameters, we can see that there exists an infinite family of designs satisfying this condition: Since
k>0
, there is a positive real number
t\in\R
such that
k=t^{2}
. We then have that
4\lambda=4k-(n-1)=4t^{2}-x
, where
x=n-1
. Since
(v,k,\lambda)
are the parameters of a
2
-design, we have
(v-1)\lambda=k(k-1)
. Therefore,

4x\lambda=4k(k-1)=4t^{2}(t^{2}-1).

(29)

Now, substituting
4\lambda=4t^{2}-x
we find

x^{2}-4t^{2}x+4t^{2}(t^{2}-1)=0.

(30)

The solutions to this quadratic equation on
x
are
x=2t^{2}\pm 2t
, which implies that
v=x+1=t^{2}+(t\pm 1)^{2}
. Since
2\lambda=2k-\frac{x}{2}=t^{2}\mp t
, we have that
\lambda={t\choose 2}
or
\lambda={t+1\choose 2}
. Finally, since
t^{2}=k
is an integer, and
\lambda=t^{2}\mp t\in\Z
, it follows that
t\in\Z
. So the parameters
(v,k,\lambda)
can be rewritten as

(v,k,\lambda)=(t^{2}+(t+1)^{2},t^{2},{t\choose 2}),\text{ or }(t^{2}+(t-1)^{2},t^{2},{t+1\choose 2}).

(31)

The existence of symmetric
2
-
(t^{2}+(t+1)^{2},t^{2},{t\choose 2})
designs for
t=q
an odd prime power was established by Brouwer in
[
8
]
. Therefore, there is a Barba matrix of order
q^{2}+(q+1)^{2}
for each odd prime power
q
.

∎

2.2
A general determinantal lower bound

Proposition 2
.

Let
H
be an Hadamard matrix of order
n
with constant row sum. Let
M
be the bordered matrix,

M=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1&\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\intercal}\\
\mathbf{1}_{n}&H\end{array}\right].

(32)

Then

|\det M|\geq(\sqrt{n}+1)n^{n/2}.

(33)

Proof.

Since
H
has a constant row sum, there exists a complex number
s
such that
HJ_{n}=sJ_{n}
. Using the fact that
H
is Hadamard, it is easy to show that
H
has a constant column sum
s
, and that
ss^{*}=n
. We can compute the Gram matrix of
M
as follows,

MM^{*}=\begin{bmatrix}n+1&(1+s^{*})\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\intercal}\\
(1+s)\mathbf{1}_{n}&nI_{n}+J_{n}\end{bmatrix}.

(34)

Let
\alpha=1+s
, a series of elementary row and column operations give the following similarity of matrices,

\begin{bmatrix}n+1&\alpha^{*}\mathbf{1}\\
\alpha\mathbf{1}&nI_{n}+J_{n}\end{bmatrix}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc|c}n+1&\alpha^{*}&\alpha^{*}\mathbf{1}_{n-1}^{\intercal}\\
\alpha&n+1&\mathbf{1}_{n-1}^{\intercal}\\
\hline\cr\alpha\mathbf{1}_{n-1}&\mathbf{1}_{n-1}&nI_{n-1}+J_{n-1}\end{array}\right]\simeq\left[\begin{array}[]{cc|c}n+1&\alpha^{*}&\mathbf{0}_{n-1}^{\intercal}\\
n\alpha&2n&\mathbf{0}_{n-1}^{\intercal}\\
\hline\cr\alpha\mathbf{1}_{n-1}&\mathbf{1}_{n-1}&nI_{n-1}\end{array}\right].

(35)

Taking determinants, and using the fact that
|\alpha|^{2}=\alpha\alpha^{*}=n+1+2\re(s)
, we find

|\det M|^{2}=(2(n+1)-|\alpha|^{2})n^{n}=(n+1-2\re(s))n^{n}.

(36)

From the fact that
\re(s)\leq\sqrt{n}
, it follows that

|\det M|^{2}\geq(n+1-2\sqrt{n})n^{n}=(\sqrt{n}+1)^{2}n^{n},

(37)

and taking square roots the result follows. ∎

In particular, Proposition
2
shows that the existence of a
\BH(n,\ell)
matrix with constant row-sum, then there is a large-determinant matrix of order
n+1
with entries in
\mu_{\ell}
. A
Bush-type
Hadamard matrix is an Hadamard matrix of order
n^{2}
consisting of
n^{2}
blocks
E_{ij}
of order
n
, such that
E_{ij}J_{n}=\delta_{ij}J_{n}
. In other words, a Bush-type Hadamard matrix has diagonal blocks equal to
J_{n}
, and all other blocks have constant row-sum equal to
0
. The following construction of Bush-type matrices is well-known, see for example Kharaghani’s paper
[
28
]
.

Proposition 3

(cf.
[
28
]
)
.

Let
H
be a dephased
\BH(n,\ell)
. Let
r_{i}
be the
i
-th row of
H
, and let
E_{i}=r_{i}^{*}r_{i}
be the rank-
1
projection matrix onto the subspace spanned by
r_{i}
. Then the block-circulant matrix
M=[E_{i-j}]_{ij}
, i.e.

M=\begin{bmatrix}E_{0}&E_{1}&E_{2}&\dots&E_{n-1}\\
E_{n-1}&E_{0}&E_{1}&\dots&E_{n-2}\\
E_{n-2}&E_{n-1}&E_{0}&\dots&E_{n-3}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
E_{1}&E_{2}&E_{3}&\dots&E_{0}\end{bmatrix},

(38)

is a Bush-type
\BH(n^{2},\ell)
matrix.

Combining the results above we obtain the following determinantal lower bound:

Theorem 6
.

If there is a
\BH(n,\ell)
, then there is a matrix
M
of order
n^{2}+1
with entries in the
\ell
-th roots of unity such that

|\det(M)|\geq(n+1)n^{n^{2}}.

(39)

Proof.

Let
H_{0}
be a
\BH(n,\ell)
, Theorem
3
gives the existence of a Bush-type
\BH(n^{2},\ell)
, say
H
. Apply Proposition
2
to
H
to obtain a matrix
M
of order
n^{2}+1
satisfying the lower bound in the statement.∎

Corollary 3
.

For all integers
\ell
,
t\geq 1
,

\gamma_{\ell}(\ell^{2t}+1)\geq(\ell^{t}+1)\ell^{t\ell^{2t}}.

(40)

Proof.

The Fourier matrix
F_{\ell}
is a
\BH(\ell,\ell)
matrix. Taking the
t
-fold tensor product of
F_{\ell}
with itself we obtain a
\BH(\ell^{t},\ell)
for all
t\geq 1
. Then, Theorem
6
with
n=\ell^{t}
yields the result.∎

Notice that

\lim_{t}\frac{\gamma_{\ell}(\ell^{2t}+1)}{h(\ell^{2t}+1)}\geq\lim_{t}\frac{(\ell^{t}+1)\ell^{t\ell^{2t}}}{(\ell^{2t}+1)^{(\ell^{2t}+1)/2}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{e}},

(41)

so our construction achieves asymptotically at least 60% of the Hadamard bound. In the cases
\ell=2,3,4
, the ratio of our determinantal lower bound to the Barba bound in Corollary
1
is
1/\sqrt{2}
, which is a fraction of approximately 70%.

3
Lower bounds for matrices over the third roots

The existence of a
\BH(n,3)
implies that
3\mid n
. So, over the third roots of unity, the lower bound in Theorem
6
applies only at orders
\equiv 1\pmod{3}
. In this section, we give an additional family of large determinant matrices over
\mu_{3}
at orders
q+1\equiv 2\pmod{3}
, where
q
is a prime power.

Definition 3
.

Let
\ell>1
be an integer, and
q
be a prime power. Suppose that
q\equiv 1\pmod{\ell}
, and let
\chi
be a character of
(\F_{q}^{\times},\cdot)
of order
\ell
. Extend
\chi
to
\F_{q}
by letting
\chi(0)=0
. We define the
generalized Paley core

Q_{q}
as

(Q_{q})_{ij}=\chi(i-j),

(42)

for every
i,j\in\F_{q}

The entries of
Q_{q}
are
\ell
-th roots of unity, and
Q_{q}
satisfies the following matrix identities:

(i)

Q_{q}Q_{q}^{*}=qI_{q}-J_{q},
and

(ii)

Q_{q}J_{q}=\mathbf{0}
.

Notice that the usual Paley core
[
26
]
, corresponds to the case
\ell=2
. The extended matrix

W_{q}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\mathbf{1}_{q}^{\intercal}\\
\mathbf{1}_{q}^{\intercal}&Q_{q}\end{bmatrix},

(43)

satisfies
W_{q}W_{q}^{*}=qI_{q+1}
, i.e. it is a
generalized weighing matrix

\GW_{\ell}(q+1,q)
(see Theorem 5.2.13.
[
39
]
). The diagonal of
W_{q}
is equal to the zero vector, so it cannot directly yield a lower bound for the determinant of a matrix over the
\ell
-th roots. We can, however, compute the determinant of
W_{q}+\alpha I_{q}
, where
\alpha\in\mu_{\ell}
, using the theory of
cyclotomy
,
[
23
,
43
]
. This will, in turn, give us a determinantal lower bound at orders
q+1\equiv 2\pmod{3}.

Definition 4
.

Let
q
be a prime power, and let
\ell
and
f
be integers such that
q=\ell f+1
. The
\ell
-th cyclotomic classes
are the cosets of the subgroup
H_{0}
of
\ell
-th powers in
\F_{q}^{\times}
. In other words, the cyclotomic classes are the sets

H_{i}=\{\gamma^{\ell a+i}:a\in\{0,1\dots,f-1\}\},

(44)

for
0\leq i\leq\ell-1
, where
\gamma
is a primitive element of
\F_{q}
.

Definition 5
.

Over the field
\F_{q}
, for
q
a prime power, the
\ell
-th cyclotomic number

(i,j)
is defined as the number of elements
x_{i}\in H_{i}
such that

x_{i}+1\in H_{j}.

(45)

Equivalently, the cyclotomic number
(i,j)
is the number of solutions
(x,y)
to the equation

\gamma^{\ell x+i}+1=\gamma^{\ell y+j}.

(46)

The cyclotomic numbers satisfy the following elementary relations:

Lemma 2

(cf. Part 1, Lemma 3
[
43
]
)
.

Let
(i,j)
be the
\ell
-th cyclotomic number, where
q=\ell f+1
. Then

1.

(i+a\ell,j+b\ell)=(i,j)
for any integers
a,b
,

2.

(i,j)=(\ell-i,j-i)
,

3.

(i,j)=(j,i)
if
f
is even, and
(i,j)=(j+\ell/2,i+\ell/2)
if
f
is odd,

4.

\sum_{j=0}^{\ell-1}(i,j)=\begin{cases}f-1&\text{ if }f\text{ is even, and }i=0\\
f-1&\text{ if }f\text{ is odd, and }i=\ell/2\\
f&\text{ otherwise }\end{cases}

5.

\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}(i,j)=\begin{cases}f-1&\text{ if }j=0\\
f&\text{ otherwise }\end{cases}

The following Lemma characterizes
cubic cyclotomy
, i.e. the case
\ell=3
, in terms of
q
.

Theorem 7

(Cubic cyclotomic numbers, cf. Part 1, Lemma 7
[
43
]
)
.

The cyclotomic numbers are given by

\begin{bmatrix}(0,0)&(0,1)&(0,2)\\
(1,0)&(1,1)&(1,2)\\
(2,0)&(2,1)&(2,2)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}A&B&C\\
B&C&D\\
C&D&B\end{bmatrix},

(47)

where

\begin{split}9A&=q-8+c\\
18B&=2q-4-c-9d\\
18C&=2q-4-c+9d\\
9D&=q+1+c,\end{split}

(48)

and
c
is uniquely determined by the conditions
4q=c^{2}+27d^{2}
, and
c\equiv 1\pmod{3}
.

Remark 3
.

The cubic cyclotomic numbers
A=(0,0)
and
D=(1,2)=(2,1)
are uniquely determined, but
B
and
C
are interchanged according to the sign of
d
. We do not need to resolve this indeterminacy, since our results will be independent of the value of
d
.

Lemma 3

(cf. Part 1, Section 3
[
43
]
)
.

Let
q=3f+1
be a prime power, and
H_{i}
be the cubic cyclotomic classes in
\F_{q}
. Let
N
be the number of solutions to the equation

1+x_{0}+x_{1}+x_{2}=0,

(49)

where
x_{i}\in H_{i}
; for
i=0,1,2
. Then,
N
satisfies

N=AD+B^{2}+C^{2}=BC+BD+CD=\frac{1}{27}(q^{2}-3q-c).

(50)

The relationship between the generalized Paley core, its eigenvalues, and cyclotomy, can be established from a representation-theoretic point of view. For this we recall the following:

Definition 6
.

Let
\Gamma=(\F_{q},+)
, and
\C[\Gamma]
be the group algebra of
\Gamma
, i.e. the
\C
-algebra generated by formal sums
\sum_{x\in\Gamma}\alpha_{x}[x]
where each
\alpha_{x}\in\C
. For each
x,y\in\Gamma
, the product of
[x]
and
[y]
in
G
is defined as
[x]\cdot[y]:=[x+y]
. An element
\alpha\in\F_{q}^{\times}
induces an automorphism of the group
\Gamma=(\F_{q},+)
by left multiplication:
x\mapsto\alpha x
, which induces an automorphism of
\C[\Gamma]
written as
[x]^{\alpha}:=[\alpha x]
.

Define

K_{i}:=\sum_{x\in H_{i}}[x]=\sum_{a=0}^{f-1}[\gamma^{a\ell+i}]\in\C[\Gamma],

(51)

for
0\leq i\leq\ell-1
. Let
\varrho
be the regular representation of
\Gamma
, i.e.
\varrho(z)
for
z\in\F_{q}
is the
q\times q
matrix indexed by elements
x,y
in
\F_{q}
such that

(\varrho(z))_{xy}=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }x+z=y\\
0&\text{ otherwise }\end{cases}.

(52)

Extending
\varrho
to
\C[\Gamma]
by linearity, the generalized Paley core can be written as

Q_{q}=\varrho\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\zeta_{q}^{i}K_{i}\right).

(53)

Linear relationships for the product of matrices
\varrho(K_{i})
and
\varrho(K_{j})
can be computed using the cyclotomic numbers:

Proposition 4

(cf.
[
3
]
)
.

Let
H_{r}
be the unique
\ell
-th cyclotomic class containing the element
-1
. The product in
\C[\Gamma]
of
K_{i}
and
K_{j}
is

K_{i}K_{j}=f\delta_{i^{\prime},j}[0]+\sum_{k=0}^{\ell-1}(j-i,k-i)K_{k},

(54)

where
i^{\prime}=i+r
.

This proposition shows that the matrices
A_{i}:=\varrho(K_{i})
are the adjacency matrices of the
Bose-Mesner algebra
of an
\ell
-class
association scheme

[
4
]
, called the
cyclotomic association scheme
.

The following is a straightforward fact, see also
[
2
]
:

Lemma 4
.

Let
\Gamma=\{g_{0}=1_{G},g_{1},\dots,g_{n-1}\}
be a finite abelian group of order
n
. Let
\{\chi_{0},\chi_{1},\dots,\chi_{n-1}\}
be the irreducible characters of
\Gamma
, where
\chi_{i}:=\hat{g_{i}}
is the image of
g_{i}
under the isomorphism
\hat{(\cdot)}:\Gamma\rightarrow\hat{\Gamma}
between
\Gamma
and its dual. Let
X
be the character table of
\Gamma
, indexed as
X_{ij}=\chi_{i}(g_{j})
. Then, for every
g\in G
,

\varrho(g)X=X\diag(\chi_{0}(g),\chi_{1}(g),\dots,\chi_{n-1}(g)).

In other words, the character table of
\Gamma
diagonalizes the regular representation of
\Gamma
, and the eigenvalues of
\varrho(g)
are given by evaluating
g
at every irreducible character of
\Gamma
. Since
Q_{q}+\alpha I_{q}
is a linear combination of the matrices
\varrho(K_{i})
, Lemma
4
gives us the following:

Corollary 4
.

Let
Q_{q}
be a generalized Paley matrix of order
q
. Then,

\det(W_{q}+\alpha I_{q+1})=\det\begin{bmatrix}\alpha&\mathbf{1}_{q}^{\intercal}\\
\mathbf{1}_{q}&Q_{q}+\alpha I_{q}\end{bmatrix}=\frac{\alpha^{2}-q}{\alpha}\det(Q_{q}+\alpha I_{q}).

(55)

Proof.

To prove this, we find the rational normal form of the matrix
W_{q}+\alpha I_{q+1}
. The weighing matrix
W_{q}
can be decomposed as
W_{q}=A+B
, where

A=\begin{bmatrix}0&\mathbf{1}_{q}^{\intercal}\\
\mathbf{1}_{q}&\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix},\text{ and }B=\begin{bmatrix}0&\mathbf{0}_{q}^{\intercal}\\
\mathbf{0}_{q}&Q_{q}\end{bmatrix}.

(56)

Let
X
be the character table of
\Gamma=(\F_{q},+)
, and let

F=\begin{bmatrix}1&\mathbf{0}_{q}^{\intercal}\\
\mathbf{0}_{q}&X\end{bmatrix}.

(57)

Since
X^{-1}=\frac{1}{q}X^{*}
, we have:

F^{-1}AF=\begin{bmatrix}0&\mathbf{1}_{q}^{\intercal}X\\
X^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{q}&\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc|c}0&q&\mathbf{0}_{q-1}^{\intercal}\\
1&0&\mathbf{0}_{q-1}^{\intercal}\\
\hline\cr\mathbf{0}_{q-1}&\mathbf{0}_{q-1}&\mathbf{0}\end{array}\right].

(58)

Lemma
4
implies that
X^{-1}Q_{q}X=\begin{bmatrix}0&\mathbf{0}_{q-1}^{\intercal}\\
\mathbf{0}_{q}&\Delta\end{bmatrix}
, where
\Delta
is the diagonal matrix given by the non-zero eigenvalues of
Q_{q}
. Hence,

F^{-1}BF=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc|c}0&0&\mathbf{0}_{q-1}^{\intercal}\\
0&0&\mathbf{0}_{q-1}^{\intercal}\\
\hline\cr\mathbf{0}_{q-1}&\mathbf{0}_{q-1}&\Delta\end{array}\right].

(59)

From the above, we conclude that

\det\begin{bmatrix}\alpha&\mathbf{1}_{q}^{\intercal}\\
\mathbf{1}_{q}&Q_{q}+\alpha I_{q}\end{bmatrix}=\det(W_{q}+\alpha I_{q+1})=\det\left[\begin{array}[]{cc|c}\alpha&q&\mathbf{0}_{q-1}^{\intercal}\\
1&\alpha&\mathbf{0}_{q-1}^{\intercal}\\
\hline\cr\mathbf{0}_{q-1}&\mathbf{0}_{q-1}&\Delta+\alpha I_{q-1}\end{array}\right].

(60)

Computing the determinant by blocks, and noticing that
\det(\Delta+\alpha I_{q-1})=\frac{1}{\alpha}\det(Q_{q}+\alpha I_{q})
, we obtain the result.∎

Corollary
4
allows us to reduce the computation of
\det(W_{q}+\alpha I_{q+1})
to the computation of
\det(Q_{q}+\alpha I_{q})
. The following is an auxiliary result to compute this latter determinant:

Proposition 5
.

Let
q=3f+1
be a prime power, and
\alpha\in\C
. The Gram matrix
G=(Q_{q}+\alpha I_{q})(Q_{q}+\alpha I_{q})^{*}
, is expressed as the image under
\varrho
of an element of
\C[\Gamma]
as follows:

G=\varrho\left((|\alpha|^{2}+q-1)[0]+(2\re(\alpha)-1)K_{0}+(2\re(\alpha\omega^{2})-1)K_{1}+(2\re(\alpha\omega)-1)K_{2}\right).

(61)

Proof.

The matrix
Q_{q}+\alpha I_{q}
can be written as:

Q_{q}+\alpha I_{q}=\varrho(\alpha[0]+K_{0}+\omega K_{1}+\omega^{2}K_{2}).

(62)

Notice that
\rho(g)^{\intercal}=\rho(-g)
. Since
-1\in H_{0}
, we have that
-H_{i}=H_{i}
, and the matrix
(Q_{q}+\alpha I_{q})^{*}
is given by the element
\overline{\alpha}[0]+K_{0}+\omega^{2}K_{1}+\omega K_{2}\in\C[\Gamma]
. Computing the product in
\C[\Gamma]
we find:

\begin{split}&(\alpha[0]+K_{0}+\omega K_{1}+\omega^{2}K_{2})(\overline{\alpha}[0]+K_{0}+\omega^{2}K_{1}+\omega K_{2})\\
=&|\alpha|^{2}[0]+\alpha K_{0}+\alpha\omega^{2}K_{1}+\alpha\omega K_{2}\\
&\overline{\alpha}K_{0}+K_{0}^{2}+\omega^{2}K_{0}K_{1}+\omega K_{0}K_{2}\\
&\overline{\alpha}\omega K_{1}+\omega K_{1}K_{0}+K_{1}^{2}+\omega^{2}K_{1}K_{2}\\
&\overline{\alpha}\omega^{2}K_{2}+\omega^{2}K_{2}K_{0}+\omega K_{2}K_{1}+K_{2}^{2}.\end{split}

(63)

We evaluate this expression: First we find by Proposition
4
and Lemma
2
, that

K_{0}^{2}+K_{1}^{2}+K_{2}^{2}=3f[0]+\sum_{k}\left(\sum_{i}(0,k-i)\right)K_{k}=(q-1)f[0]+(f-1)(K_{0}+K_{1}+K_{2}).

(64)

Next we evaluate
\omega(K_{0}K_{2}+K_{1}K_{0}+K_{2}K_{1})
and
\omega^{2}(K_{0}K_{1}+K_{1}K_{2}+K_{2}K_{0})
. It is easy to check that

\begin{split}K_{0}K_{2}+K_{1}K_{0}+K_{2}K_{1}&=f(K_{0}+K_{1}+K_{2}),\text{ and}\\
K_{0}K_{1}+K_{1}K_{2}+K_{2}K_{0}&=f(K_{0}+K_{1}+K_{2}).\end{split}

(65)

This implies that
\omega(K_{0}K_{2}+K_{1}K_{0}+K_{2}K_{1})+\omega^{2}(K_{0}K_{1}+K_{1}K_{2}+K_{2}K_{0})=f(\omega+\omega^{2})(K_{0}+K_{1}+K_{2})=-f(K_{0}+K_{1}+K_{2}).
Therefore the Gram matrix is given by the image of
\varrho
at the element

(|\alpha|^{2}+(q-1))[0]+(2\re(\alpha)-1)K_{0}+(2\re(\alpha\omega^{2})-1)K_{1}+(2\re(\alpha\omega)-1)K_{2},

(66)

as we wanted to show.∎

Now, Lemma
4
will allow us to compute the eigenvalues of the Gram matrix of
Q_{q}+\alpha I_{q}
. These eigenvalues can be expressed using the following:

Definition 7
.

Let
q=\ell f+1
be a prime power. The
\ell
-th
Gaussian periods
are defined as

\eta_{i}:=\sum_{x\in H_{i}}\zeta_{q}^{x},\ \ \ 0\leq i\leq\ell-1.

Corollary 5
.

Let
q=3f+1
be a prime power, and let
Q_{q}
be the generalised Paley core of order
q
over the third roots. Then, the eigenvalues of the Gram matrix of
Q_{q}+\alpha I_{q}
with
\alpha\in\{1,\omega,\omega^{2}\}
are:

(i)

1=q-3f
, occurring with multiplicity
1
, and

(ii)

(q+2)+3\eta_{i}
, each occurring with multiplicity
f
, for
i=0,1,2
.

Proof.

By Proposition
5
, and using the fact that
\alpha\in\{1,\omega,\omega^{2}\}
, we have that
M=(Q_{q}+\alpha I_{q})(Q_{q}+\alpha I_{q})^{*}
is given by the group algebra element

\begin{split}&(|\alpha|^{2}+(q-1))[0]+(2\re(\alpha)-1)K_{0}+(2\re(\alpha\omega^{2})-1)K_{1}+(2\re(\alpha\omega^{2})-1)K_{2}\\
&=q[0]+K_{i}-2K_{i+1}-2K_{i+2},\end{split}

(67)

for some
i=0,1,2
. Lemma
4
implies that the eigenvalues of
M
are given by the evaluation of
q[0]+K_{i}-2K_{i+1}-2K_{i+2}
at each linear character of the additive group
\Gamma=(\F_{q},+)
. For the trivial character
\chi_{0}
, we find

\chi_{0}(q[0]+K_{i}-2K_{i+1}-2K_{i+2})=q-3|K_{0}|=q-3f=1.

(68)

All non-trivial linear characters are of the type
\chi_{j}(x)
,
1\leq j\leq q-1
, where
\chi_{j}(1)=\zeta_{q}^{\gamma^{j}}
, and
\gamma
is a primitive element of
\F_{q}^{\times}
. We have that

\begin{split}\chi_{j}(q[0]+K_{i}-2K_{i+1}-2K_{i+2})&=q+\chi_{j}(K_{i})-2\chi_{j}(K_{i+1})-2\chi_{j}(K_{i+1})\\
&=q+\eta_{i+j}-2\eta_{i+j+1}-2\eta_{i+j+2}\\
&=q+3\eta_{k}-2(\eta_{0}+\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}),\end{split}

(69)

for some
k\in\{0,1,2\}
. Since
\sum_{x\in\F_{q}}\zeta_{q}^{x}=0
, we have that
\eta_{0}+\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}=-1
, and the eigenvalues are
q+2+3\eta_{i}
, each with multiplicity
f=(q-1)/3
, for
i=0,1,2
.∎

Theorem 8
.

Let
q=3f+1
be a prime power, and
\alpha\in\{1,\omega,\omega^{2}\}
. Then, the absolute value of the determinant of
Q_{q}+\alpha I_{q}
is

\begin{split}|\det(Q_{q}+\alpha I_{q})|&=\left(\prod_{i=0}^{2}((q+2)+3\eta_{i})\right)^{f/2}\\
&=\left[(q+2)^{3}-3(q+2)^{2}-3(q-1)(q+2)+(3+c)q-1\right]^{(q-1)/6},\end{split}

(70)

where
c
is uniquely determined by
4q=c^{2}+27d^{2}
, and
c\equiv 1\pmod{3}
.

Proof.

By Corollary
5
, the determinant of
(Q_{q}+\alpha I_{q})(Q_{q}+\alpha I_{q})^{*}
is

[(q+2+3\eta_{0})(q+2+3\eta_{1})(q+2+3\eta_{2})]^{f}.

(71)

By Lemma
4
, instead of directly computing the product above, we can equivalently compute it using the images of the elements
K_{i}
in the quotient ring
\C[\Gamma]/(S_{\Gamma})
, where
S_{\Gamma}:=\sum_{x\in\Gamma}[x]
. The product
\prod_{i}((q+2)[0]+3K_{i})
expands as

\begin{split}(q+2)^{3}[0]&+3(q+2)^{2}(K_{0}+K_{1}+K_{2})\\
&+3^{2}(q+2)(K_{0}K_{1}+K_{0}K_{2}+K_{1}K_{2})\\
&+3^{3}K_{0}K_{1}K_{2}.\end{split}

(72)

We have that
K_{0}K_{1}+K_{0}K_{2}+K_{1}K_{2}=f(K_{0}+K_{1}+K_{2})\equiv-f[0]\pmod{S_{\Gamma}}
, so

\prod_{i=0}^{2}((q+2)[0]+3K_{i})\equiv((q+2)^{3}-3(q+2)^{2}-3^{2}(q+2)f)[0]+3^{3}K_{0}K_{1}K_{2}\pmod{S_{\Gamma}}.

(73)

It remains to compute the term
K_{0}K_{1}K_{2}
. Using the notation of Theorem
7
, we have

\begin{split}K_{0}K_{1}K_{2}&=K_{0}(DK_{0}+BK_{1}+CK_{2})\\
&=fD\cdot[0]+ADK_{0}+BDK_{1}+CDK_{2}\\
&\phantom{=fD\cdot[0]\ }+B^{2}K_{0}+BCK_{1}+BDK_{2}\\
&\phantom{=fD\cdot[0]\ }+C^{2}K_{0}+CDK_{1}+CBK_{2}.\end{split}

(74)

By Lemma
3
, we have that
AD+B^{2}+C^{2}=BC+BD+CD=N=(q^{2}-3q-c)/3^{3}
.
Therefore,

\begin{split}K_{0}K_{1}K_{2}&=fD[0]+N(K_{0}+K_{1}+K_{2})\\
&\equiv(fD-N)[0]\pmod{S_{\Gamma}}.\end{split}

(75)

Substituting
N=(q^{2}-3q-c)/3^{3}
, and using the fact that
f=(q-1)/3
and
3^{2}D=(q+1+c)
, we have that
3^{2}(q+2)f=3(q-1)(q+2)
and
3^{3}(fD-N)=3q-1+qc
. Therefore,

\prod_{i=0}^{2}((q+2)[0]+3K_{i})\equiv((q+2)^{3}-3(q+2)^{2}-3(q-1)(q+2)+(3+c)q-1)[0]\pmod{S_{\Gamma}}.

(76)

Evaluating this expression at a non-trivial character of
\F_{q}
, the result follows.∎

Corollary 6
.

For every prime power
q\equiv 1\pmod{3}
, there is a matrix
M
of order
q+1\equiv 2\pmod{3}
over the third roots of unity such that

|\det M|=\sqrt{q^{2}+q+1}\cdot\left[(q+2)^{3}-3(q+2)^{2}-3(q-1)(q+2)+(3+c)q-1\right]^{(q-1)/6},

(77)

where
c
is uniquely determined by
4q=c^{2}+27d^{2}
, and
c\equiv 1\pmod{3}
.

Proof.

By Corollary
4
, the determinant of
W+\alpha I_{q+1}
is

|\det(W+\alpha I_{q+1})|=\left|\frac{\alpha^{2}-q}{\alpha}\right||\det(Q_{q}+\alpha I_{q})|.

(78)

If
\alpha=1
, then
|\alpha^{2}-q|/|\alpha|=q-1
. On the other hand, if

then

or

In particular, this gives an infinite family of matrices of order

(79)

of the Barba bound in the limit.

Butson matrices over the third roots

1.

The tensor product

\BH(nm,3)

[

If there is a
\BH(n,3)

\BH((n-1)n,3)

[

[

[

. In particular it shows the existence of a
\BH(6,3)
matrix, recovering the result of Butson
[
11
]
on the existence of
\BH(2p,p)
matrices for all primes
p
in the case

1
at orders

4.2
Barba matrices over the third roots

Barba matrices over the third roots only exist at orders
n\equiv 1\pmod{3}
. Recall that for an element
\alpha=a+b\omega\in\Z[\omega]
the
norm
of
\alpha
in the quadratic ring extension
\Z\subset\Z[\omega]
is given by
N(\alpha):=\alpha\overline{\alpha}=a^{2}-ab+b^{2}
. The proposition below imposes restrictions on the prime factors of
N(\alpha)
, and can be used to obtain additional non-existence conditions for Barba matrices:

Proposition 6
.

Let
\alpha=a+b\omega\in\Z[\omega]
, and let
N(\alpha):=\alpha\overline{\alpha}=a^{2}-ab+b^{2}\in\Z
be the norm of
\alpha
. Suppose that
p\neq 3
is a prime number dividing the square-free part of
N(\alpha)
. Then,
p\equiv 1\pmod{3}
.

Proof.

Recall from algebraic number theory, e.g. Theorem 25 in
[
31
]
, that, in the quadratic ring extension
\Z\subset\Z[\omega]
, the assumption
p\neq 3
implies two possibilities for the prime ideal decomposition of the ideal
(p)
in
\Z[\omega]
:

1.

(p)
is prime and
self-conjugate
, in the sense that
\overline{(p)}=(p)
—the
inert
case; or

2.

(p)=\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{q}
, where
\mathfrak{p}
and
\mathfrak{q}
are
conjugate
prime ideals in
\Z[\omega]
, i.e.
\mathfrak{q}=\overline{\mathfrak{p}}
—the
split
case.

Since
\Z[\omega]
is a Dedekind domain, the prime ideal factorization of
(\alpha\overline{\alpha})=(\alpha)\overline{(\alpha)}
is unique. Suppose
(p)
divides
(\alpha)
, then
\overline{(p)}
divides
\overline{(\alpha)}
and
(p)
must have the decomposition
(p)=\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{q}
with
\mathfrak{p}=\overline{\mathfrak{q}}
; otherwise,
p
cannot divide the square-free part of
N(\alpha)
—the multiplicity with which
p
divides
\alpha\overline{\alpha}
is even. The prime
(p)
splits as
(p)=\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{q}
in
\Z[\omega]
if and only if
-3
is a square residue modulo
p
, i.e. if and only if
\genfrac{(}{)}{}{}{-3}{p}=+1
; see Theorem 25
[
31
]
. By quadratic reciprocity
\genfrac{(}{)}{}{}{-3}{p}=\genfrac{(}{)}{}{}{p}{3}
and
(p)
splits if and only if
p\equiv 1\pmod{3}
.
∎

Theorem 9
.

Let
n\equiv 1\pmod{3}
be an integer, and write
(2n-1)(n-1)^{n-1}=s^{2}r
with
r
square-free. If
p\equiv 2\pmod{3}
is a prime number dividing
r
, then there is no Barba matrix of order
n
over the third roots.

Proof.

Suppose that a Barba matrix
B
of order
n
exists, then
\det(M)\in\Z[\omega]
and,

N(\det(M))=\det(M)\overline{\det(M)}=(2n-1)(n-1)^{n-1}=s^{2}r.

(84)

By Proposition
6
, if
p\equiv 2\pmod{3}
is a prime number dividing
r
, we have a contradiction; so a Barba matrix of order
n
over the third roots cannot exist.
∎

By Theorem

, the first few orders

at which a Barba matrix over the third roots cannot exist are:

4.3
Maximal determinant matrices at orders
n\equiv 2\pmod{3}

In this subsection, we develop tools to give certificates of maximality of putative maximal determinant matrices of order
n\equiv 2\pmod{3}
. At orders
n\equiv 2\pmod{3}
, the bounds of Theorem
1
and Corollary
1
cannot be met with equality, so computational approachs similar to the ones developed in
[
35
]
and
[
37
]
are needed.

Let
\tilde{X}
be a candidate maximal determinant matrix over the third roots, and let
\tilde{G}=\tilde{X}\tilde{X}^{*}
be its Gram matrix.
The algorithm we will describe is a backtracking search for the set of all putative Gram matrices
G=XX^{*}
of matrices
X
with entries in the set
\{1,\omega,\omega^{2}\}
satisfying
\det(G)\geq\det(\tilde{G})
. In particular, we will begin with a set
\mathcal{G}_{1}=\{[n]\}
of
1\times 1
principal submatrices of Gram matrices, and at the
r
-th step of the algorithm we will construct a set
\mathcal{G}_{r}
of principal
r\times r
submatrices from the set
\mathcal{G}_{r-1}
: this is done by considering all feasible extensions of a matrix
G\in\mathcal{G}_{r-1}
of the type

G_{f}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}G&f\\
\hline\cr f^{*}&n\end{array}\right],

(88)

where
f
is a vector of length
r-1
. If the final set of candidate matrices
\mathcal{G}:=\mathcal{G}_{n}
contains no matrices
G
with determinant strictly greater than that of
\tilde{G}
, then
\tilde{X}
is of maximal determinant.

In order to reduce the size of the backtracking search tree, we carry some pruning steps. First, we can “normalize” matrices over the third-roots, to reduce the number of possible off-diagonal entries to consider.

Definition 8
.

Let
v
be a vector of length
n
with entries in
\{1,\omega,\omega^{2}\}
, and let
v_{1}
,
v_{\omega}
and
v_{\omega^{2}}
the number of entries in
v
equal to
1
,
\omega
, and
\omega^{2}
, respectively. We say that
v
is
balanced
if
v_{\omega}\equiv v_{\omega^{2}}\pmod{3}
. We say that a square matrix of order
n
with entries in
\{1,\omega,\omega^{2}\}
is
balanced
if all of its row vectors and column vectors are balanced.

Lemma 5
.

Let
v
be a balanced vector, and let
\Delta=\diag(\omega^{e_{1}},\dots,\omega^{e_{n}})
where
\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\equiv 0\pmod{3}
. Then, the vector
\Delta v
is also balanced.

Proof.

Let
u=(\omega^{e_{1}},\dots,\omega^{e_{n}})^{\intercal}
, then the product
\Delta v
is equal to the entry-wise product
u\circ v
of
u
and
v
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
u
and
v
have the following form:

\begin{split}v&=[\mathbf{1}_{v_{1}}|\omega\mathbf{1}_{v_{\omega}}|\omega^{2}\mathbf{1}_{v_{\omega}^{2}}];\text{ and }\\
u&=[\mathbf{1}_{a_{1}}|\omega\mathbf{1}_{a_{\omega}}|\omega^{2}\mathbf{1}_{a_{\omega^{2}}}|\mathbf{1}_{b_{1}}|\omega\mathbf{1}_{b_{\omega}}|\omega^{2}\mathbf{1}_{b_{\omega^{2}}}|\mathbf{1}_{c_{1}}|\omega\mathbf{1}_{c_{\omega}}|\omega^{2}\mathbf{1}_{c_{\omega^{2}}}],\end{split}

(89)

where
a_{1}+a_{\omega}+a_{\omega^{2}}=v_{1}
,
b_{1}+b_{\omega}+b_{\omega^{2}}=v_{\omega}
, and
c_{1}+c_{\omega}+c_{\omega^{2}}=v_{\omega^{2}}
. Since
v
is balanced, we have that

b_{1}+b_{\omega}+b_{\omega^{2}}\equiv c_{1}+c_{\omega}+c_{\omega^{2}}\pmod{3},

(90)

and from the hypothesis
\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\equiv 0\pmod{3}
, we find

0(a_{1}+b_{1}+c_{1})+1(a_{\omega}+b_{\omega}+c_{\omega})+2(a_{\omega^{2}}+b_{\omega^{2}}+c_{\omega^{2}})\equiv 0\pmod{3}.

(91)

From this, it follows that

a_{\omega}+b_{\omega}+c_{\omega}\equiv a_{\omega^{2}}+b_{\omega^{2}}+c_{\omega^{2}}\pmod{3}.

(92)

Now, combining Equation
90
with Equation
92
, we find that

\begin{split}(\Delta v)_{\omega}&=a_{\omega}+b_{1}+c_{\omega^{2}}\\
&\equiv a_{\omega}+(c_{1}+c_{\omega}+c_{\omega^{2}}-b_{\omega}-b_{\omega^{2}})+c_{\omega^{2}}\\
&\equiv a_{\omega}-b_{\omega}-b_{\omega^{2}}+c_{1}+c_{\omega}-c_{\omega^{2}}\\
&\equiv(a_{\omega^{2}}+b_{\omega^{2}}+c_{\omega^{2}}-b_{\omega}-c_{\omega})-b_{\omega}-b_{\omega^{2}}+c_{1}+c_{\omega}-c_{\omega^{2}}\\
&\equiv a_{\omega^{2}}+b_{\omega}+c_{1}\pmod{3}.\end{split}

(93)

Since
(\Delta_{v})_{\omega^{2}}=a_{\omega^{2}}+b_{\omega}+c_{1}
, this shows that
(\Delta_{v})_{\omega}\equiv(\Delta_{v})_{\omega^{2}}\pmod{3}
, i.e.
\Delta v
is balanced.∎

Lemma 6
.

Let
M
be a matrix of order
n\equiv 1,2\pmod{3}
with entries in the set
\{1,\omega,\omega^{2}\}
. Then, there exists a unique pair of diagonal matrices
(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2})
with diagonal entries in
\{1,\omega,\omega^{2}\}
such that
\Delta_{1}M\Delta_{2}
is balanced.

Proof.

We begin with the following observation: Let
(v_{1},v_{\omega},v_{\omega^{2}})\in\Z^{3}
be a triple, then its unordered list of residue classes modulo
3
,
[v_{1}\pmod{3},v_{\omega}\pmod{3},v_{\omega^{2}}\pmod{3}]
, is one of the following:

\begin{array}[]{ccc}000&001&002\\
012&022&011\\
111&112&122\\
222\end{array}

Under the assumption that
v_{1}+v_{\omega}+v_{\omega^{2}}=n\equiv 1,2\pmod{3}
, the list of residue classes belongs to the second or third columns above. Furthermore, we see that every list in the second and third column has exactly two identical elements. This implies that for an arbitrary vector
v
of length
n\equiv 1,2\pmod{3}
, there exists a unique
i
such that the vector
\omega^{i}v
is balanced.

Let
M
be a matrix of order
n
; by the observation above, there is a unique diagonal matrix
\Delta_{1}
such that every row of
\Delta_{1}M
is balanced. Likewise, there is a unique diagonal matrix
\Delta_{2}
such that all the columns of
\Delta_{1}M\Delta_{2}
are balanced. It suffices to show that all the rows of
\Delta_{1}M\Delta_{2}
are balanced: To see this, let
N:=\Delta_{1}M
and let
N_{1}
,
N_{\omega}
, and
N_{\omega^{2}}
be the total number of entries of
N
equal to
1
,
\omega
, and
\omega^{2}
, respectively. Since all rows of
N
are balanced,
N_{\omega}\equiv N_{\omega^{2}}\pmod{3}
. Let
c_{1},\dots,c_{n}
be the columns of
N
, and define
e_{i}\in\{0,1,2\}
by
e_{i}\equiv(c_{i})_{\omega}-(c_{i})_{\omega^{2}}\pmod{3}
, then

\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[(c_{i})_{\omega}-(c_{i})_{\omega^{2}}\right]=N_{\omega}-N_{\omega^{2}}\equiv 0\pmod{3}.

(94)

Since
\Delta_{2}
is unique we must have
\Delta_{2}=\diag(\omega^{e_{1}},\dots,\omega^{e_{n}})
, and by Lemma
5
we find that every row of
N\Delta_{2}=\Delta_{1}M\Delta_{2}
is balanced.∎

Corollary 7
.

Let
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
be two balanced matrices of order
n
. Then,
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are equivalent under the action of the group of
n\times n
monomial matrices over the third roots if and only if
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are permutation-equivalent.

Proof.

Suppose that there exists a pair of permutation matrices
(P,Q)
and a pair of diagonal matrices
(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2})
with diagonal entries in
\{1,\omega,\omega^{2}\}
such that

X_{1}=\Delta_{1}PX_{2}Q\Delta_{2}.

(95)

Then, since the matrix
PX_{2}Q
is balanced, Lemma

\Delta_{1}=\Delta_{2}=I_{n}
. Therefore
X_{1}=PX_{2}Q
, i.e.

and
X_{2}
are permutation-equivalent. ∎

Remark 4

The main pruning technique we employ is an adaptation of the Moyssiadis-Kounias bound
[

,
12
]
, and Orrick’s sharpening in
[

. The following are extensions of these bounds to hermitian matrices; the proofs are analogous but we reproduce them here for completeness:

Theorem 10

(cf. Moyssiadis-Kounias
[
35
]
)
.

Let
D
be an
r\times r
Hermitian matrix, and
M
be an
m\times m
Hermitian positive-definite matrix extending
D
as follows:

M=\begin{bmatrix}D&B\\
B^{*}&A\end{bmatrix},

(103)

Assume furthermore that
M_{ii}=n
, and that the entries of
M
are taken from a set
\Phi\subseteq\C
such that
|\alpha|\geq c
for all
\alpha\in\Phi
, and let

\hat{d}:=\max_{\gamma\in\Phi^{r}}\det\begin{bmatrix}D&\gamma\\
\gamma^{*}&c\end{bmatrix}.

(104)

Then

\det M\leq(n-c)^{m-r-1}\left[(n-c)\det D+(m-r)\max(0,\hat{d})\right].

(105)

Proof.

We prove this by induction on
m-r
. When
m-r=1
, any matrix
M
of order
m
extending
D
is of the shape

M=\begin{bmatrix}D&\gamma\\
\gamma^{*}&n\end{bmatrix},

(106)

where
\gamma\in\Phi^{r}
. By the linearity of the determinant on rows:

\begin{split}\det M&=(n-c)\det D+\det\begin{bmatrix}D&\gamma\\
\gamma^{*}&c\end{bmatrix}\\
&\leq(n-c)\det D+\max(0,\hat{d}).\end{split}

(107)

Suppose the claim is true for
k=m-r
, we show that the claim is true for all Hermitian, positive-definite matrices of order
m=k+r+1
extending
D
. Such a matrix
M
has the shape

M=\begin{bmatrix}D&B_{1}&\gamma\\
B_{1}^{*}&A_{1}&\delta\\
\gamma^{*}&\delta^{*}&n\end{bmatrix}.

(108)

By linearity of the determinant,

\begin{split}\det M&=(n-c)\begin{bmatrix}D&B_{1}\\
B_{1}^{*}&A_{1}\end{bmatrix}+\det\begin{bmatrix}D&B_{1}&\gamma\\
B_{1}^{*}&A_{1}&\delta\\
\gamma^{*}&\delta^{*}&c\end{bmatrix}\\
&=(n-c)\begin{bmatrix}D&B_{1}\\
B_{1}^{*}&A_{1}\end{bmatrix}+c\det\begin{bmatrix}D-\gamma\gamma^{*}/c&B_{1}-\gamma\delta^{*}/c\\
B_{1}^{*}-\delta\gamma^{*}/c&A_{1}-\delta\delta^{*}/c\end{bmatrix}\end{split}

(109)

Let

M^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}D&B_{1}&\gamma\\
B_{1}^{*}&A_{1}&\delta\\
\gamma^{*}&\delta^{*}&c\end{bmatrix}

(110)

If
\det M^{\prime}\leq 0
, then the claim is trivially true. Otherwise,
M^{\prime}
is positive-definite, which implies that
A_{1}-\delta\delta^{*}/c
is also positive-definite. By Fischer’s inequality

c\det\begin{bmatrix}D-\gamma\gamma^{*}/c&B_{1}-\gamma\delta^{*}/c\\
B_{1}^{*}-\delta\gamma^{*}/c&A_{1}-\delta\delta^{*}/c\end{bmatrix}\leq c\det(D-\gamma\gamma^{*}/c)\det(A_{1}-\delta\delta^{*}/c).

(111)

By definition,
c\det(D-\gamma\gamma^{*}/c)\leq\hat{d}
. Since
A-\delta\delta^{*}/c
is positive-definite and has diagonal terms bounded above by
(n-c)
,

\det(A_{1}-\delta\delta^{*}/c)\leq(n-c)^{m-r}.

(112)

Now, applying the induction hypothesis we have

\begin{split}\det M&\leq(n-c)^{m-r}\left[(n-c)\det D+(m-r)\max(0,\hat{d})\right]+\max(0,\hat{d})(n-c)^{m-r}\\
&=(n-c)^{m-r}\left[(n-c)\det D+(m-r+1)\max(0,\hat{d})\right],\end{split}

(113)

which is the claim we wanted to prove. ∎

Corollary 8

(cf. Orrick
[
37
]
)
.

Let
M
be a Hermitian positive-definite matrix of order
m
with
M_{ii}\leq n
, and
|M_{ij}|\geq c
. Then,

\det M\leq(n-c)^{m}+cm(n-c)^{m-1}.

(114)

Proof.

Apply the Moyssiadis-Kounias bound with
D=[n]
, and
r=1
. We have that
\hat{d}=c(n-c)
, since

\det\begin{bmatrix}n&\alpha\\
\overline{\alpha}&c\end{bmatrix}=nc-|\alpha|^{2}\leq c(n-c).

(115)

Substituting the values of
D
,
r
, and
\hat{d}
in Equation
105
yields the result.
∎

The following result can be proved in analogy to Corollary 3.2 in
[

Corollary 9
.

Let
M=\begin{bmatrix}D&B\\
B^{*}&A\end{bmatrix}
be an
n\times n
matrix satisfying the conditions of Theorem
10
, and suppose furthermore that the entries of
M
are in
\Z[\omega]
, and that
M_{ij}\equiv n\equiv 2\pmod{(1-\omega)}
. Then,

\det M\leq(n-1)^{n-r}\det D+\max(0,\hat{d})\left[2(n-1)^{n-r}-2(n-2)^{n-r}-(n-r)(n-2)^{n-r-1}\right].

(116)

Proof.

In the proof of Theorem
10
we used the fact that if
\det\overline{M}>0
, then
A_{1}-\delta\delta^{*}/c
is positive-definite and used Fischer’s inequality to conclude

\det(A_{1}-\delta\delta^{*}/c)\leq(n-c)^{m-r-1}.

(117)

Corollary
8
gives us a stronger upper bound for the determinant of
A_{1}-\delta\delta^{*}/c
: First, notice that, under our assumptions,
c=1
and that
\delta_{i}\equiv 2\pmod{(1-\omega)}
for all
i
. This implies

(A-\delta\delta^{*}/c)_{ij}=A_{ij}-\delta_{i}\overline{\delta_{j}}\equiv 2-2\cdot 2\equiv 1\pmod{(1-\omega)}.

(118)

Also, since
A_{ii}=n
, we have that
(A-\delta\delta^{*})_{ii}\leq n-1
. Applying Inequality
114
, we find

\det(A-\delta\delta^{*})\leq(n-2)^{m-r-1}+(m-r-1)(n-2)^{m-r-2}.

(119)

Using this inequality in the proof of Theorem
10
, we can prove by induction that

\det M\leq(n-1)^{m-r}\det(D)+\max(0,\hat{d})S_{m-r},

(120)

where
S_{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(n-1)^{k-1-j}(n-2)^{j}+\sum_{j=0}^{k-2}(n-1)^{k-2-j}(j+1)(n-2)^{j}
. Using the formula for the partial sum of a geometric series, and its derivative, we can prove that

S_{k}=2(n-1)^{k}-2(n-2)^{k}-k(n-2)^{k-1}.

(121)

Using Equation

\det M\leq(n-1)^{m-r}\det D+\max(0,\hat{d})\left[2(n-1)^{m-r}-2(n-2)^{m-r}-(m-r)(n-2)^{m-r-1}\right].

(122)

Letting

∎

be —by means of Lemma

1.

Start with the set

of putative

.

by each vector

5
Maximal determinants of matrices over the fourth roots

We conclude with an account of results for matrices over the fourth roots; this case was first studied by J.H.E. Cohn
[
15
]
. This maximal determinant problem is much better behaved that one over the third roots mainly because of two reasons 1) we have an inclusion
\mu_{2}\subset\mu_{4}
, which implies that all Hadamard and Barba matrices with entries
\pm 1
are also maximal determinant over the fourth roots; and 2) there is a mapping from
\mu_{4}=\{\pm 1,\pm i\}
into
2\times 2
matrices over
\mu_{2}
, called the
Turyn morphism

\pm 1\mapsto\pm\begin{bmatrix}1&-\\
1&1\end{bmatrix};\penalty\ \pm i\mapsto\pm\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
-&1\end{bmatrix}

(137)

which sends
\BH(n,4)
matrices into
\BH(2n,2)
matrices, when it is applied entrywise. This morphism not only maps Hadamard matrices into Hadamard matrices of twice the size; more generally it relates the maximal determinant problem over the fourth roots to the maximal determinant problem over
\pm 1
for matrices of twice the size.

Definition 10
.

A matrix
M
of order
2n
is a
skew block matrix
if and only if
M
has the following block-structure

M=\begin{bmatrix}A&B\\
-B&A\end{bmatrix},

(138)

where
A
and
B
are of order
n
.

The following theorem can be obtained by analyzing the value of the determinant of a
\pm 1
matrix obtained by applying the Turyn morphism to a matrix over the fourth roots.

Theorem 11

(Cohn; Theorem 1
[
15
]
)
.

Let
\gamma_{m}(n)
be the maximum absolute value of a matrix of order
n
over the
\ell
-th roots. Then,
\gamma_{2}(2n)\geq 2^{n}\gamma_{4}(n)^{2}
. Furthermore, equality holds if and only if there exists a skew
\pm 1
block matrix
M
with
|\det(M)|=\gamma_{2}(2n)
.

Theorem 12

(Cohn; Theorem 3
[
15
]
)
.

Suppose that
2^{n}\gamma_{4}(n)^{2}=\gamma_{2}(2n)
, then there exists a Barba matrix of order
n
over the fourth roots.

There is a construction due to Koukovinos, Kounias, and Seberry
[
29
]
, for maximal determinant matrices with entries
\pm 1
at orders
2(q^{2}+q+1)\equiv 2\pmod{4}
with the block structure

\begin{bmatrix}R&S\\
-S^{\intercal}&R^{\intercal}\end{bmatrix},

(139)

where
R
and
S
are circulant matrices of order
q^{2}+q+1
. In
[
14
]
it is shown that if
R
and
S
are circulant matrices of order
n
, then letting
P
be the back-diagonal matrix of order
n

\begin{bmatrix}P&0\\
0&I_{n}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}R&S\\
-S^{\intercal}&R^{\intercal}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}P&0\\
0&I_{n}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}PRP&PS\\
-PS^{\intercal}&R^{\intercal}\end{bmatrix},

(140)

is a block skew matrix.

Theorem 13
.

Let
q
be a prime power. Then, there exists a Barba matrix over the fourth roots of order
q^{2}+q+1
.

Proof.

The matrix
P
in Equation
140
is a permutation matrix. Hence, for every prime power
q
there is a
\pm 1
skew block matrix
M
of order
2(q^{2}+q+1)
with
|\det(M)|=\gamma_{2}(2n)
. The result now follows from Theorem
11
and Theorem
12
.
∎
