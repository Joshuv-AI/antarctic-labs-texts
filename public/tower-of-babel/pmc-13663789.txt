Vaccine data suggest immunity could mitigate dementia. What’s the biology behind these apparent effects?
Authors: Jyoti Madhusoodanan
Published: 2026

In 2013, a vaccine policy inadvertently launched a massive natural experiment that would offer new clues about the roots of dementia. At the time, the National Health Service in Wales started a shingles vaccination program with cutoffs based strictly on a person’s age: People who were 80 years of age or older on September 1, 2013, were not eligible to receive the vaccine, which protects against the painful rash caused by herpes zoster virus.

Because of the arbitrary date cutoff, the two groups—one that received the vaccine and one that did not—were unlikely to differ significantly in their diet or exercise, health habits, or other chronic health conditions, and few would not have carried the herpes virus responsible for chickenpox. The only clear-cut difference was their age on the designated September 1 date, explains Pascal Geldsetzer, a population health researcher at Stanford University in California. “What we had now, all of a sudden, is essentially a control group and an intervention group—just like in a clinical trial,” Geldsetzer says.

In their 2025 study, Geldsetzer and his colleagues used anonymized data from more than 280,000 individuals between the ages of 70 and 88 to examine whether receiving a shingles vaccine affected a person’s risk of dementia. It did. Vaccination was associated with a 20% reduction in new diagnoses of dementia over a seven-year period after getting the shot. Receiving the vaccine was correlated with a slower progression of the disease amongst those already living with dementia, based on a decrease in deaths due to the disease (1). “We just see these large protective effects,” Geldsetzer says.

The 2025 research, which received plenty of press attention, is the latest of multiple observational studies suggesting that late-in-life chronic diseases like dementia may be triggered by viruses that linger and lie dormant in our bodies—and perhaps in the nervous system in particular.

It’s not only the shingles vaccine. Routine adult vaccinations for RSV, diphtheria, tetanus, and other infections seem to have some link to lower dementia risk as well, based on observational studies (2, 3). These results are leading researchers to explore how infections that lead to chronic inflammation—including brain inflammation—may be the roots of dementia later in life.

Thus far, the data do not establish a cause–effect relationship: Infections do not cause dementia, and the shingles vaccine does not prevent it. But these studies are allowing researchers to see dementia and Alzheimer’s disease through a different lens. Eschewing a purely brain-centric view, the work has led researchers to examine how inflammation and immunity in the body as a whole can influence the brain over time. Such revelations could also lead to a clearer picture of how infections and immunity shape lifelong health. “It was a very niche area a few decades ago,” Geldsetzer says. “Now it’s really come to the forefront because of our increasing realization of the chronic consequences of infectious disease.”

An Elusive Trigger

Approximately 57.4 million people were living with dementia in 2019, a number that could rise to 152.8 million by the year 2050, based on the most recent estimates from the Global Burden of Disease study (4). It’s an incredible public health burden that has, for decades, spurred numerous investigations into both causes and potential therapies. Typically, the focus has been the brain and, in particular, the role of amyloid plaques. The most recent wave of treatments approved—antibodies against amyloid—sparked controversy about efficacy and safety, with one particularly controversial drug discontinued by its manufacturer (5). Data on the protective effects of vaccines could open up other, potentially fruitful avenues of inquiry.

Two growing bodies of evidence led Geldsetzer and his colleagues, among others, to probe the connection between the shingles vaccine and dementia. First, a swell of evidence started to link viral infections—particularly those that target the nervous system—with dementia risk (6). Shingles is one such disease. The virus that causes shingles, varicella zoster or herpes zoster, infects most people in childhood, causing chickenpox. It then hides out in the body for decades and can reactivate to cause shingles, a condition that affects nerve cells, in late adulthood. The chickenpox vaccine was only introduced in the mid-1990s, so the vast majority of people over the age of 30 carry the virus and are at risk of developing shingles (7). The shingles vaccine prevents reactivation of this virus.

Secondly, many studies have reported that vaccines can confer an immune boost that extends far beyond the single target pathogen. Vaccination can, for example, improve chronic diseases and reduce the risk of stroke or heart attack (8, 9), protect against infections by other pathogens, and possibly slow age-related declines in immunity.

Unbiased Evidence

Evaluating whether a vaccine works—even for its intended target—is surprisingly complicated. On average, people who choose to receive vaccines tend to be healthier than those who do not. Researchers can have a hard time overcoming this “healthy vaccinee bias,” even when data are parsed to remove confounding factors such as chronic health conditions or socioeconomic drivers of health.

—Pascal Geldsetzer

The September 1 birthdate cutoff criterion in Wales allowed Geldsetzer and his colleagues to avoid this bias, as people on either side of September 1 were equally likely to have chronic health conditions or other factors that can affect a person’s choice to receive a vaccine. Studies they conducted in other regions that used a birthdate cutoff corroborated their results (10, 11).

And the vaccine appears to do more than just help prevent dementia. In a study last year of people who were already diagnosed with the condition, the researchers found that vaccination could reduce the likelihood of dying from dementia, thus indicating that the vaccine slows down the progression of the disease. “This is not a fluke finding,” Geldsetzer says. “We keep seeing these same effects” (1). It’s a strong signal, but only establishes a correlation—a causal connection is impossible to find, since that would require withholding a beneficial vaccine from people.

There is an important caveat: The shingles vaccine used in the trio of study cohorts was an older version, named Zostavax, that used a live attenuated version of the virus to confer immunity. Today, Shingrix, which uses a recombinant form of the virus and an adjuvant to further boost the immune response, is much more common.

But recent work suggests that Shingrix also protects against dementia. In a February 2026 study (12), infectious disease researcher Emily Rayens at Kaiser Permanente’s Department of Research and Evaluation in Pasadena, California, and her colleagues analyzed retrospective data from a cohort of more than 300,000 people, with an average age of 73 across the group.

They compared two groups of individuals: The first group included 65,800 people over the age of 65 who had received two doses of Shingrix within a six-month period between April 2018 and December 2020. The second group included 263,200 people within the same age range who did not receive the shots.

The team followed the participants for one to three years and tracked new diagnoses of dementia. The follow-up period varied, as some unvaccinated individuals received the shot during that time and were then removed from further analyses. Overall, the team found that receiving two doses of the vaccine was associated with a 51% lower risk of being diagnosed with dementia during that follow-up period. The results held across different age groups and racial and ethnic groups; the benefits of the vaccine appeared slightly greater for women than for men, just as in Geldsetzer’s studies. Rayens speculates that because Shingrix is more protective against shingles, it confers even greater benefits for protecting against dementia. The precise mechanism remains unclear, however.

To gauge the role of healthy vaccine bias, the researchers also analyzed dementia diagnosis rates amongst those who had received a Tdap booster shot. They found that the Tdap vaccine, which confers immunity to the bacterial infections of tetanus, diphtheria, and pertussis, also had some protective effect. But that protection—which could be attributed to healthy lifestyles or the vaccine’s protection—was not as strong as that from the shingles shots.

Several other efforts have correlated Tdap, RSV, and other vaccines to a lower risk of dementia, though none have shown as strong a correlation as the two shingles vaccines. Like Shingrix, some of these vaccines include an adjuvant that prods the immune system toward a stronger response. The boost from adjuvants may offer additional protection against dementia risk because it strengthens the innate immune system even further, Rayens says, although this correlation requires further investigation.

While compelling, none of the studies demonstrate that the connection between receiving the shingles or other vaccines and a lower risk of dementia diagnosis is a cause–effect relationship. “We don’t have enough evidence,” Rayens says. Establishing that causal connection remains a big methodological challenge, she says. The gold standard, a double-blind, placebo-controlled, randomized clinical trial, would be difficult to achieve for any vaccine, including the shingles shot. Because the disease-preventing health benefits of the vaccine are clear, withholding the shot from a control group would be unethical.

Beyond the Brain

Further clues about the role of the immune system come from decades of work focused on how infections affect dementia risk. In addition to studies of vaccines, researchers have found that other infections are associated with a greater risk of dementia. Observational studies and analyses of thousands of individuals in national databases suggest that dementia rates are higher in older adults who had periodontal disease or COVID-19 infections than in those who did not get infected (13–15).

Together, these studies point to a key role for the immune system—specifically, systemic immunity. “Now, we’re shifting from looking at inflammation only in the brain in Alzheimer’s to looking beyond the brain to see what’s happening systemically,” Jaime Ramos-Cejudo of NYU Langone Health in New York, NY.

Early studies have shed light on the immune system mechanisms at work. In a 2026 study, aging researcher Svetlana Ukraintseva of Duke University in North Carolina and her colleagues zeroed in on an important target for the shingles vaccine’s protective effects—at the very point of infection. They found that one version of the nectin-2 protein, a target that herpes zoster and related viruses use to infect human cells, was likely to be important: Women who carried the rs6859 (A) variant in the NECTIN2 gene appeared to be more protected by the shingles vaccine than men and women who did not have this variant (16). Precisely how the nectin-2 protein mediates the connection between shingles and dementia is unknown. But the variant is an established risk factor for Alzheimer’s disease, Ukraintseva notes, and data such as these could help identify which individuals might be most likely to benefit from the vaccine’s protective effects on dementia.

And in a recent study, Ramos-Cejudo and his colleagues analyzed data on immune cells in circulation from more than 370,000 individuals in the NYU Langone Health and Veterans Affairs health systems. They found that the ratio of two immune cell types, neutrophils and lymphocytes, appeared to be a strong risk predictor for Alzheimer’s and related dementias, especially in women and Hispanic participants (17). “We confirmed for the first time that there’s a clear relationship between neutrophil metrics and risk of dementia,” Ramos-Cejudo says.

Neutrophils are key regulators of the innate immune system, mediating systemic inflammation and clearing away cellular debris and toxic proteins. Lymphocytes, as part of the adaptive immune system, are trained to guard against infections and marshalled in response to viruses or other pathogens. A lower ratio of these cell types indicates a balanced immune response; vaccines may act to reduce the ratio by enhancing adaptive immunity.

Ramos-Cejudo speculates that the innate immune system, which marshals neutrophils and other cells to sites of injury and infection, is involved in a series of steps that culminates in the accumulation of toxic proteins such as amyloid in the brain. By reducing systemic inflammation, vaccines against shingles and other infections appear to lower the risk of developing dementia. Within the next decade, “we might be able to eventually identify what are the pathways that specifically link the immune system with Alzheimer’s disease progression,” Ramos-Cejudo says. “And we’ll be able to potentially even start treating people very early with some immune modulators.”

It’s still far from clear how all the pieces, from the immune system to the brain, fit together. But these new pieces are both compelling and promising. “What we’re seeing is that increasing systemic inflammation is directly linked to the risk of developing dementia in the future,” he says. “Conceptually, the whole framework makes sense.”
