Impact of black garlic and its encapsulated form on the physicochemical properties, antioxidant potential, and aroma-active profile of wheat bread
Authors: Hasim Kelebek, Hatice Kubra Sasmaz, Gamze Nil Yazici, Onur Sevindik, Gamze Guclu, Turkan Uzlasir, Özge Aksay, Mehmet Sertac Ozer, Serkan Selli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the impact of black garlic (BG) and encapsulated black garlic (EBG) additions (2–8%) on the physicochemical properties, antioxidant potential, aroma profile, and sensory characteristics of bread. Antioxidant capacity, measured by DPPH and ABTS assays, was highest in bread with 8% BG, while total phenolic content increased from 48.27 mg/100 g in the control to 86.56 mg/100 g. LC-ESI-MS/MS revealed significant increases in citric, malic, succinic, and pyroglutamic acids with BG addition. Texture profile analysis revealed that hardness significantly increased with BG addition, reaching 1214.67 g in 8% BG bread compared to 237.39 g in the control, while chewiness also followed an upward trend. In contrast, resilience decreased from 0.57 to 0.29 with 8% BG enrichment. HS-SPME-GC-MS and GC-O identified sulfur-containing compounds, especially diallyl disulfide and allyl methyl trisulfide, as key contributors to the distinctive aroma of BG- and EBG-enriched breads. Sensory evaluation showed overall acceptability scores between 3.78 and 4.10, with 8% BG bread rated highest for flavor and aroma. These findings indicate that BG and EBG can enhance both the functional and sensory qualities of bread, supporting their potential as innovative ingredients in commercial bakery products.Keywords: Allium sativum L., Black garlic, BreadSubject terms: Biochemistry, Chemistry, Plant sciences

Introduction

Bread is one of the most fundamental components of human diets and is considered among the oldest known food production technologies1. While bread is a primary source of carbohydrates and proteins, refined white wheat bread often possesses a limited profile of dietary fiber, essential vitamins, and minerals due to the loss of the bran and germ layers during milling2. Therefore, fortifying white bread with functional ingredients to compensate for these nutritional deficiencies is crucial for supporting human health and meeting consumer demand for healthier food options3, 4. In recent years, the growing consumer demand for functional foods has stimulated research aimed at enriching traditional staple products, such as bread, with health-promoting ingredients4.

Garlic (Allium sativum L.), a member of the Liliaceae family, is a widely consumed vegetable containing diverse bioactive compounds, including organosulfur compounds (e.g., allicin, alliin), phenolic compounds, vitamins, minerals, and polysaccharides5, 6, 7. Numerous studies have documented its antioxidant, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, antidiabetic, and anticancer properties [5,7,8,9]. BG is a functional product obtained by fermenting fresh garlic under controlled conditions (60–90 °C and 60–80% relative humidity) for approximately 10–80 days8, 9. This thermal aging process modifies the color, texture, and flavor of garlic, resulting in a softer texture and a sweet, caramel-like taste7. Compared with fresh garlic, BG contains higher levels of phenolic compounds and flavonoids and exhibits stronger antioxidant activity[7,9]. Moreover, BG is less irritating to the digestive system and has been associated with protective effects against metabolic disorders [5,12].

The documented health-promoting properties of BG have stimulated its incorporation into diverse food matrices, including meat products such as pork ham 10 and specialty bakery products like bagels11, to improve their nutritional value and functional characteristics. Nevertheless, to the best of our knowledge, only a limited number of studies have explored the application of BG or EBG in bread formulations. For instance, Lasekan et al.,11 investigated the use of BG in specialty bakery products like bagels, focusing on the characterization of key aroma compounds and their impact on buttery and roasted aroma-active compounds. While these studies primarily addressed sensory and volatile profiles, research on the simultaneous impact of BG and EBG on physicochemical properties, HMF safety limits, and organic acid transformations in wheat bread remains scarce. Encapsulation is an emerging technique for protecting sensitive bioactive compounds, controlling their release, and improving their stability during processing and storage9. Incorporating BG in its encapsulated form (EBG) may further enhance the functional and sensory properties of bread by preserving its bioactive constituents and modulating its flavor profile5.

The novelty of the present study lies in its multidimensional approach, simultaneously evaluating the impact of BG and EBG on the physicochemical, functional, and aroma-active profiles of wheat bread. To address the current research gap, this investigation provides the first comprehensive data on 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF) content, a critical food safety parameter for functional bakery products, thereby ensuring a robust scientific safety assessment. Furthermore, the integration of LC-ESI-MS/MS for organic acid profiling and Aroma Extract Dilution Analysis (AEDA) to determine the flavor dilution (FD) factors of aroma-active compounds enables the precise identification of how sulfur-containing compounds contribute to the distinctive aroma of the final product.

Accordingly, this study aimed to evaluate the effects of BG and EBG addition at various concentrations (2-8%) on the textural properties, antioxidant potential, and sensory characteristics of bread. Parameters defining bread quality, including specific volume, baking loss, color, texture profile, and crumb porosity, were analyzed through advanced instrumental techniques, while consumer acceptability and overall liking were determined via sensory evaluation. Finally, to verify bioactive enrichment and functional potential, total phenolic content, antioxidant capacity, HMF levels, and organic acid profiles were characterized, with the volatile profile being detailed using headspace solid-phase microextraction (HS-SPME) combined with gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC-MS).

Materials and methods

Samples

Fresh white garlic bulbs were purchased from local markets in Taşköprü, Kastamonu, Türkiye (41°30′ N, 34°12′ E). BG was produced by applying controlled thermal processing using a climate-controlled chamber (Model HCP105, Memmert GmbH + Co. KG, Germany). The processing conditions-temperature, relative humidity, and time (65 °C, 85% RH, 24 days) were selected based on our previous optimization study5. Before being incorporated into the bread formulation, the BG cloves were peeled and ground into a uniform, smooth paste (wet form) using a high-speed blender to facilitate homogenous distribution during the kneading process. EBG was prepared using the complex coacervation technique9. Briefly, black garlic extract was encapsulated through the electrostatic interaction between lentil protein isolate and pectin at a specific pH, followed by cross-linking and drying to produce a stable, functional powder. The resulting microcapsules, consisting of a black garlic core within a gelatin-gum arabic matrix, were characterized by an encapsulation efficiency of 73.86% and a mean particle size of 35,3 μm, ensuring a stable delivery system for the bioactive constituents.

Bread production

The bread formulation was based on the flour weight and consisted of 100% wheat flour, 4% yeast, 1.5% salt, 60% water, and 0.004% L-ascorbic acid. To create the functional groups, BG or EBG was incorporated at levels of 2%, 4%, 6%, and 8% based on the total flour weight as an additional ingredient, while the control bread contained no garlic. The dough was prepared using a spiral kneading machine (Günsa Makine Sanayi A.Ş., İzmir, Türkiye) with a total kneading time of 5 min at 160 rpm. Following kneading, the dough was divided into 100 g portions, manually rounded, and flattened to a uniform thickness of 2 cm using a rotary dough press12. The shaped dough pieces were placed into baking pans (AACC Method 10-10B; top internal dimensions: 143 × 79 mm, bottom internal dimensions: 129 × 64 mm, depth: 57 mm) and proofed at 30 ± 5 °C and 75 ± 5% relative humidity for 75 min. The loaves were baked in a deck oven (Fimak Makine A.Ş., Konya, Türkiye) at 235 ± 10 °C for 15 min with a 3-second automatic steam injection. After baking, the loaves were cooled on wire racks at room temperature for 1 h. Physical quality analyses (height, specific volume, baking loss, and color) were performed after 2 h of cooling, while moisture content, texture profile, and crumb porosity were assessed after 24 h. All analyses were performed in at least three replicates. Representative images of the bread samples are presented in Fig. 1.

Determination of water activity, pH, and titratable acidity

The water activity (aw) of the bread crumb was measured at 25 °C using a calibrated water activity meter (METER Aqualab 4TE; METER Group, Pullman, WA, USA). The titratable acidity (%) and pH were analyzed according to the method described by Cemeroglu et al.[17].

Specific volume

The volume of the bread samples was determined using the rapeseed displacement method (AACC Method 10-05.0112). The specific volume (cm3/g) was then calculated as the ratio of bread volume to its weight.

Height measurement

The height (mm) of each bread sample was measured at its central point using a digital caliper (AEK-Tech Electronic Caliper; AEK-Tech Elektronik, İstanbul, Türkiye).

Crumb porosity analysis

Bread samples were sliced to a uniform thickness of 10 ± 0.1 mm and imaged under a fixed height of 9 cm using a white LED light source (Skygo). The images were transferred to a computer, and a standardized region of interest (35 × 25 mm) was selected from the central crumb area. Each image was acquired in RGB format and converted to 8-bit grayscale using ImageJ software (version 1.34; National Institutes of Health, USA). For each bread sample, two slices were analyzed, and images were taken from both cut surfaces. The Otsu threshold algorithm was applied to the grayscale images to quantify crumb porosity (expressed as the percentage of the total area occupied by gas cells within the region of interest), pore density, and circularity parameters[18].

Texture profile analysis (TPA)

The textural properties of bread samples (cut to standard dimensions of 25 mm3) were evaluated according to AACC Method 74-09.01 using a TA.XT-2-Plus Texture Analyzer (Stable Micro Systems, UK) equipped with a 36 mm diameter probe and a 5 kg load cell, operating at a test speed of 1.7 mm/s (pre-test 1 mm/s, post-test 10 mm/s), 5 g trigger force, and 40% deformation rate. Through TPA, the sensory-related and textural properties of the breads, including hardness, cohesiveness, springiness, and chewiness, were determined. All measurements were performed in triplicate using a double-compression cycle at room temperature.

Color analysis

Color measurements were carried out using a chromameter (Model CM-5, Konica Minolta, Inc., Japan), focusing on three key color parameters: L*, a*, and b*. The L* value indicates lightness, ranging from 0 (dark) to 100 (light). Positive a* values denote red hues, while negative a* values correspond to green hues. Similarly, positive b* values reflect yellow tones, and negative b* values represent blue tones [19]. In addition, chroma (C*), which represents color saturation, and Hue angle (h°), indicating the color tone, were calculated from the a* and b* values to provide a more detailed characterization of color.

Proximate composition and sugar analysis

Total crude protein and dry matter were quantified according to the method of the AOAC[20]. For sugar analysis, 2 g of the bread sample was mixed with 20 mL of distilled water and stirred on a magnetic stirrer for 30 min. The resulting extract was centrifuged at 5500 rpm and 4 °C (Hettich Universal 320R, Germany), and the clear supernatant was collected and filtered through 0.45 μm membrane filters. The filtrate was then injected into a Shimadzu Prominence-i LC-2030 C HPLC system (Shimadzu Corp., Kyoto, Japan) for quantification of sugars7. Chromatographic separation was performed using an Aminex HPX-87 H column (300 × 7.8 mm; Bio-Rad, California, USA) with 5 mM sulfuric acid as the mobile phase under isocratic conditions at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min. A refractive index detector (RID) was used, and sugar concentrations were determined using an external calibration method. For this purpose, calibration solutions were prepared from analytically pure sucrose, glucose, and fructose standards at five concentration levels ranging from 31.25 to 500 ppm. Quantification of sugars in the bread extracts was performed using external calibration curves constructed from these standard solutions, ensuring reliable linearity, sensitivity, and accuracy across the expected concentration range.

5-Hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF) analysis

The method used by Sasmaz et al.5 was applied to determine the amount of HMF. The analysis was performed on whole bread samples; specifically, the entire loaves were cooled for 2 h, dried, and finely ground to obtain a representative homogeneous mixture of both crust and crumb before extraction. In the analysis, an Agilent 6430 Triple Quadrupole MS coupled with HPLC (LC-DAD-ESI-MS/MS) and a Phenomenex Luna C18 column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm) (Torrance, California, USA) was used. Electrospray ionization mass spectrometry detection was performed in Multiple Reaction Monitoring (MRM) mode. For HMF analysis, the ions monitored were m/z 109 and m/z 127. Data were recorded using chromatograms obtained at a wavelength of 285 nm.

Antioxidant capacity and total phenolic compounds (TPC) analysis

The antioxidant capacity was evaluated using two distinct methods: DPPH (2,2′-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl hydrate) and ABTS (2,2′-azino-bis 3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) assays. The DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging activities of the garlic samples were determined following the procedures outlined by Sasmaz et al.7. Total phenolic content (TPC) was measured using the Folin–Ciocalteu method, as described in Sasmaz et al.7.

Determination of organic acids by LC-ESI-MS/MS

The method was conducted following Sasmaz et al.7. Chromatographic analyses utilized an Aminex HPX-87 H column (300 × 7.8 mm, Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA) with isocratic elution (water/formic acid; 99.9%:0.1%; v/v). The column temperature was set to 40 °C, the flow rate was maintained at 0.4 mL/min, and 10 µL injection volume was applied. Organic acids were identified by comparing their UV spectra with reference standards, followed by confirmation using LC-MS/MS (Agilent 6430). An electrosprey ionization (ESI) source in negative ion mode was employed, with operational conditions set to 12 L/min N2 as the drying gas, 200 °C drying gas temperature, 3000 V capillary voltage, and 50 psi nebulizer pressure. Data acquisition was performed using multiple reaction monitoring (MRM), detecting specific mass transitions in MS-MS negative ion mode at set retention times. Quantification was achieved through calibration curves of standard organic acids.

Aroma compounds analysis

Analysis of aroma compounds was performed using a compact system integrating solid-phase microextraction (SPME) with gas chromatography (GC, Agilent 7890B) and mass spectrometry (MS, Agilent 7010B), based on a dynamic headspace (HS) approach. For analysis, 3 g of sample was placed into a 20 mL glass vial, and an internal standard solution (IS: 4-nonanol, 40 µg) was added. The vial was sealed with a screw cap containing a PTFE–silicone septum. Using an autosampler (Gerstel Inc., Linthicum, MD, USA), the sample was transferred to the preconditioning unit and incubated at 50 °C for 15 min. Subsequently, a 2 cm fiber (Supelco, Bellefonte, PA, USA) was inserted 33 mm into the vial and exposed to the headspace for 30 min at 50 °C to adsorb volatile aroma compounds. The adsorbed volatiles were then thermally desorbed at 250 °C and injected into a DB-Wax capillary column (60 m × 0.25 mm i.d. × 0.25 μm film thickness; J&W Scientific, USA). The oven temperature was programmed from 90 °C to 140 °C, then ramped to 240 °C. Helium was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1 mL/min. In the GC–MS system, the interface temperature was set at 250 °C and the ion source temperature at 180 °C. Aroma compounds were identified by comparing their retention indices (RI), determined using a series of C8–C32 n-alkanes, with mass spectra from the Wiley 11.0 and NIST 14 libraries. Quantification of the identified volatile compounds was conducted by the internal standard method based on GC-MS data, and the concentrations were calculated and expressed as µg/kg6.

Aroma extract dilution analysis (AEDA)

Aroma extract dilution analysis (AEDA) was applied to identify the key aroma-active compounds in the samples. Initially, a liquid extract representing the original aromatic profile of the sample was obtained using a purge and trap system. This system consisted of a nitrogen gas source regulated by a flow meter, a manifold allowing the gas stream to be split into multiple directions, and the capability to process three samples simultaneously. Solid samples were placed into 20 mL crimp-top vials sealed with septa. Nitrogen gas was passed through the vials, and volatile aromatic compounds were trapped on an adsorbent cartridge connected through the septum. Detailed extraction parameters have been described in our previous study6. For AEDA, the obtained extracts were stepwise diluted with dichloromethane (starting at 1:1, then 1:2, 1:4, 1:8, 1:16, etc.). Three trained panelists evaluated the diluted extracts using an olfactometric port attached to the GC–MS–O system. At each dilution level, perceived odors were expressed as flavor dilution (FD) factors (e.g., 4, 8, 12, etc.). A higher FD factor indicated a stronger contribution of the compound to the overall aroma profile6.

Sensory analysis

Bread samples deemed acceptable for consumption were subjected to sensory evaluation 24 h after baking. Three types of bread control, 8% BG, and 8% EBG, were prepared and assigned random three-digit codes before testing. A total of 14 trained panelists (6 female and 8 male, aged between 25 and 54) evaluated the sensory attributes of the breads. Six quality parameters volume, crumb porosity, color, texture, taste, aroma, and overall acceptability were assessed using a five-point hedonic scale (1 = very poor, 5 = very good). Statistical data analysis

Data were statistically examined by using the analysis of variance in the SPSS package program at 95% confidence interval (p˂0.05) (Version: 20, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Duncan’s multiple comparison test was also applied to decide the significance of the differences between the means. Heatmap and clustering analysis were performed using the ClustVis program by Metsalu & Vilo, 2015. Principal component analysis was carried out by XLSTAT 2025 trial version.

The water activity (aw) of the control bread and the BG-enriched breads was determined to be 0.95, whereas the aw of the EBG-enriched bread was slightly lower at 0.94 (Table 3). Although these differences appear subtle, statistical analysis confirmed that the variation among the samples was significant (p < 0.05), indicating that the addition of BG and EBG may slightly alter moisture distribution and water-binding capacity within the bread matrix. The pH of the control bread was 7.15, while that of BG-enriched breads ranged from 6.30 to 6.42 and EBG-enriched breads from 6.17 to 6.45. Increasing the proportion of BG and EBG resulted in a gradual decrease in pH, accompanied by a significant increase in titratable acidity, a trend likely related to the inherent organic acid content of garlic and its influence on the buffering capacity of the dough. This acidification may further affect protein network formation and crumb structure characteristics, thereby influencing the textural and sensory attributes of the final bread. Overall, the combined changes in hardness, chewiness, and resilience suggest that enrichment affects crumb structure rather than simply increasing firmness.

Moisture content plays a critical role in the textural properties of bakery products, as it directly affects crumb softness, cohesiveness, and staling behavior, and a strong negative correlation has been reported between moisture content and hardness[22]. In the present study, the moisture content of the control bread was 33.52%, whereas that of the garlic-enriched breads ranged from 31.82% to 38.86% (Table 3). In the present study, breads enriched with black garlic (BG) exhibited lower baking loss, particularly at higher concentrations, suggesting that the phenolic compounds, Maillard reaction products, and polysaccharides present in BG may contribute to improved water retention and reduced evaporative loss during baking [23,24,25]. These constituents, particularly polysaccharides such as fructans, which are released through the thermal depolymerization of garlic cell walls during BG production, contain abundant hydrophilic hydroxyl groups that facilitate hydrogen bonding with water molecules. This interaction enhances the hydration of the dough matrix and hinders moisture migration[24]. Furthermore, phenolic compounds, which are significantly elevated in BG compared to fresh garlic, and Maillard reaction products formed during thermal aging, further contribute to stabilizing the water-binding capacity within the gluten-starch network, thereby minimizing evaporative loss[25]. Additionally, the physical form of the added ingredient may also have contributed to these differences, since BG was incorporated as a paste while EBG was added as a dry powder, which can influence dough hydration behavior and consequently affect crumb texture.

Statistical analysis revealed that these differences were significant (p < 0.05). An increase in the concentration of BG and EBG generally led to a higher moisture content in the bread samples. Compared with the control bread, BG enriched breads exhibited a higher moisture content, whereas EBG enriched breads showed lower moisture values. The incorporation of BG appears to enhance water retention within the dough matrix, which may support cohesiveness and preserve a fresh texture; however, TPA results indicate that this moisture increase did not necessarily translate into improved crumb softness.On the contrary, BG addition led to a significant increase in hardness (Table 3), likely due to the nature of the added material; garlic-derived organosulfur compounds may have interfered with gluten network development, while the high sugar content and the wet-paste form of BG contributed to a more compact and denser crumb structure. In contrast, the use of EBG resulted in reduced moisture content compared to the control, which contributed to a firmer structure and potentially extended shelf life by slowing microbial spoilage.

The baking loss of the control bread was determined to be 15.56%, whereas the breads formulated with different concentrations of BG and EBG exhibited baking loss values ranging from 12.33% (8% BG) to 17.18% (2% EBG) (Table 3). The highest baking loss was observed in the bread containing 2% EBG, while the lowest value was recorded in the bread with 8% BG. Statistical analysis revealed that these differences in baking loss among the samples were significant (p < 0.05). Increasing the concentration of BG and EBG tended to reduce the overall baking loss. During bread baking, moisture and volatile organic compounds are typically lost at levels of 8-12% , and such losses are strongly influenced by dough composition and the presence of water-retaining compounds [26]. In the present study, breads enriched with BG exhibited lower baking loss, particularly at higher concentrations, suggesting that the phenolic compounds, Maillard reaction products, and polysaccharides present in BG may contribute to improved water retention and reduced evaporative loss during baking.

The height values of the breads are presented in Table 3. Statistically significant differences were observed among the samples in terms of loaf height (p < 0.05). The control bread exhibited the highest loaf height (54.82 mm), while the lowest height was observed in the sample with 8% BG (45.42 mm). Increasing the concentration of BG and EBG generally resulted in a gradual reduction in loaf height. This decrease is likely associated with the increased levels of sulfur-containing compounds naturally present in garlic, particularly garlic-derived thiosulfinates and other organosulfur compounds such as allicin and its derivatives. These compounds can interfere with gluten network development by modifying the disulfide bond formation between glutenin and gliadin proteins, potentially leading to a weaker gluten matrix and reduced gas-holding capacity of the dough[27,28].

The loaf height and specific volume values of the bread samples, which are closely related indicators of bread expansion and quality, are presented in Table 3. Statistically significant differences (p < 0.05) were observed among the samples for both parameters. The control bread exhibited the highest height (54.82 mm) and a specific volume of 4.60 cm³/g, whereas increasing concentrations of BG and EBG led to a progressive reduction in these values, reaching minimums of 45.42 mm and 3.44 cm³/g in the 8% BG sample, respectively.

This decline is primarily attributed to the interference of garlic-derived organosulfur compounds, such as thiosulfinates and allicin derivatives, which disrupt the formation of disulfide bonds between glutenin and gliadin proteins. As a result, the gluten matrix is weakened, significantly impairing the dough’s gas-holding capacity during proofing and baking. Furthermore, as previously discussed, the increased acidity introduced by black garlic further destabilizes the protein network through electrostatic repulsion. The increased acidity may also influence gluten protein interactions and modify the structural properties of the dough matrix, as organic acids have been reported to affect gluten network behavior and textural characteristics[29]. The reduced expansion is also linked to impaired fermentation performance, as these bioactive compounds may exert mild antimicrobial effects, thereby inhibiting yeast activity. In the case of EBG, while the encapsulation matrix may partially mitigate these effects at lower concentrations, the addition of the encapsulating materials themselves likely contributed to gluten dilution at higher supplementation levels.

Pore size distribution

Porosity refers to the volume fraction of gas cells within a loaf of bread and is a key indicator of crumb structure and product quality. The porous network develops through the incorporation, expansion, and coalescence of gas bubbles during mixing, fermentation, and baking[30]. During baking, heat and mass transfer occur simultaneously, including water evaporation, starch gelatinisation, and protein denaturation, which cause physical and structural changes that determine the final crumb porosity and cell morphology[31].

Circularity measures how much a pore’s shape deviates from a perfect circle and is an important descriptor of crumb cell geometry. Evaluating crumb pore distribution is widely used to assess bread staling and overall quality, since both the number and structural characteristics of pores affect loaf volume, crumb softness, and consumer acceptance[32]. A decrease in porosity values was observed with increasing concentrations of BG and EBG (Table 3). Compared with the control bread, both porosity and circularity values were reduced across all enriched samples, while pore density generally increased with higher BG and EBG concentrations, indicating a more compact crumb structure with a greater number of smaller gas cells. The porosity of the control bread was 75.31%, whereas breads containing BG exhibited values ranging from 58.31% to 64.43%, and those containing EBG ranged from 70.91% to 74.41%. This reduction in porosity may be attributed to the disruption of gluten network formation by garlic-derived sulfur compounds, which can interfere with disulfide bond formation and gas retention during fermentation and baking, ultimately resulting in a denser crumb structure.

Color characteristics

Color development in bread occurs mainly during the final stages of baking as a result of complex biochemical reactions and the formation of colored compounds [33]. Another factor influencing crust and crumb color is the composition of the dough (water content, pH, reducing sugars, and amino acids) as well as processing conditions [34]. The color changes observed in the breads are presented in Table 1. Significant differences (p < 0.05) were observed in all color parameters (L*, a*, b*) for both the crust and crumb of the breads.

In the control bread, the L* value was 67.85 for the crust and 74.41 for the crumb. In breads containing BG, the crust L* ranged from 51.69 to 59.73 and the crumb L* from 55.80 to 74.03. Increasing the levels of BG and EBG led to noticeable changes in color compared with the control: the crust and crumb became darker and less bright as L* values decreased, while the overall color intensity increased. This darkening is attributed to the high protein and sugar content of BG, which enhances Maillard reactions, as well as the inherently dark pigments present in BG. The Chroma values of the crust decreased significantly (p < 0.05), whereas those of the crumb increased with BG and EBG addition. Similarly, the Hue angle values for the crumb decreased from 95.94 in the control to 75.63 in the 8% BG sample, reflecting a shift from a yellowish tone toward a more reddish-brown hue, consistent with the visual impact of black garlic enrichment. In light of these significant changes in physical and color characteristics, the influence of BG and EBG supplementation on the textural attributes of the bread was further investigated through Texture Profile Analysis (TPA).

Texture profile analysis (TPA)

The structural properties of bread are commonly assessed through sensory analysis and TPA, with softness and hardness regarded as key indicators of freshness. Hardness is described as the reduction in crumb softness, usually caused by moisture loss and starch retrogradation during storage[35]. Hardness values are expressed in grams (g), and the results of the texture analysis are shown in Table 2. The hardness of the control bread was 237.39 g, while breads containing BG ranged from 458.26 g (2% BG) to 1214.67 g (8% BG). The highest hardness was observed in the 8% BG sample (1214.67 g), whereas the lowest was noted in the 2% BG bread (458.26 g) (p < 0.05). Breads enriched with EBG exhibited hardness values between 508.63 g and 704.61 g, with the highest in 8% EBG (704.61 g) and the lowest in 2% EBG (508.63 g). Overall, increasing levels of BG and EBG tended to raise bread hardness.

The springiness of the control bread was 0.97, while BG-enriched breads ranged from 0.92 to 0.97. The highest springiness was found in the control and 4% BG breads (p < 0.05). Breads with EBG showed springiness values between 0.96 and 0.97. Previous studies have reported that fiber enrichment generally reduces bread elasticity, whereas protein- or hydrogel-based additives tend to increase it[36,37].

The cohesiveness values of the bread samples are presented in Table 2 and showed significant differences among samples (p < 0.05). The control bread exhibited a cohesiveness of 0.70, while BG-enriched breads ranged from 0.62 (8% BG) to 0.72 (2% BG). EBG-enriched breads ranged from 0.68 to 0.70, with the highest cohesiveness observed in 2% EBG and the lowest in 4% and 8% EBG samples.

Chewiness, which is generally considered undesirable at high levels, also varied significantly (p < 0.05). The control bread had a chewiness of 157.88 g, whereas BG breads ranged from 315.99 g (2% BG) to 699.62 g (8% BG). EBG breads showed chewiness values between 353.10 g and 485.74 g. The resilience values of the breads are presented in Table 2. The control bread had a resilience of 0.57, while BG breads ranged from 0.29 (8% BG) to 0.36 (4% BG). Similarly, EBG-enriched breads exhibited significantly lower resilience values than the control, ranging from 0.32 (8% EBG) to 0.35 (6% EBG). These findings indicate that both forms of black garlic supplementation reduce the crumb’s ability to regain its original shape after compression, a change likely linked to the weakened gluten network and the more compact crumb structure observed in the enriched samples.

Protein content

Bread is a valuable food source providing carbohydrates, proteins, essential vitamins (thiamine, riboflavin, niacin, pyridoxine), as well as minerals, antioxidants, and phytochemicals. About 80% of its protein consists of gluten, a complex of gliadins and glutenins that forms a viscoelastic dough with extensibility and gas-holding capacity, crucial for gas cell stabilization and the development of a porous crumb structure[38].

The protein contents (%) of breads produced with different concentrations of BG and EBG are presented in Table 4. The protein content of the control bread was 9.34%, whereas the values of the BG- and EBG-enriched breads ranged from 9.14% (8% EBG) to 9.99% (4% BG). These differences were found to be statistically significant (p < 0.05). The observed variations in crude protein content can be attributed to the nitrogenous composition of BG and EBG and their potential interactions with the gluten matrix. While statistical analysis indicated significant differences (p < 0.05), it should be noted that the measured nitrogen-based protein levels remained within a narrow range (9.14% to 9.99%). These minor fluctuations likely reflect the contribution of non-protein nitrogenous compounds from black garlic and the dilution effect of adding non-wheat ingredients, rather than a substantial change in the total protein content of the bread. Therefore, while statistically valid, these differences may have a limited practical impact on the overall nutritional profile of the loaves.

Sugar content

The sucrose, glucose and fructose contents of the bread samples were determined using HPLC-RID. The total sugar content of the breads ranged from 34.87 to 66.37 g/kg, with sucrose being the predominant sugar, followed by fructose and glucose. The control bread had a total sugar content of 34.87 g/kg, whereas breads enriched with BG contained 40.79-59.25 g/kg, and those with EBG contained 42.78-66.37 g/kg.

During the production of BG, the depolymerization of garlic polysaccharides significantly increases the content of reducing sugars. Under high-temperature conditions, cell wall polysaccharides break down, contributing to the release of fructose and glucose [39]. In this study, breads containing BG exhibited significantly higher fructose levels (19.62 g/kg for 8% BG) compared to EBG samples (4.03 g/kg for 8% EBG) (Table 4). This difference is attributed to the presence of the encapsulation matrix in EBG; since EBG is composed of both the black garlic and a wall material (lentil protein isolate and pectin), the actual amount of black garlic incorporated into the EBG breads is lower than in the BG-enriched breads at the same substitution level. Consequently, the fructose released through the thermal depolymerization of garlic fructans is more concentrated in the BG-enriched samples. Conversely, the higher total sugar content in EBG-enriched breads (up to 66.37 g/kg) is primarily due to a substantial increase in sucrose levels. As illustrated in the PCA biplot (Fig. 2), 8% EBG is strongly associated with sucrose content, suggesting that the encapsulation process or the materials used in the complex coacervation facilitated this increase, thereby defining the distinct sugar profile of EBG bread. In addition, fresh garlic contains about 23% fructans, which are unstable in acidic environments and at high temperatures; during BG processing, these fructans hydrolyze into fructose and glucose [40]. The thermal treatment applied during BG production thus promotes fructan degradation and conversion into fructose and glucose, explaining the elevated levels of these sugars in breads containing BG and EBG.

5-Hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF) content

HMF content in breads made with varying levels of BG and EBG was measured using LC-MS/MS. The analysis was conducted in positive ion mode based on the 127→109 multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) transition. The HMF levels detected are shown in Table 4. The HMF content ranged from 0.38 to 8.77 mg/kg across the samples. The control bread contained 0.38 mg/kg, while breads enriched with BG ranged from 2.20 to 8.77 mg/kg, and those with EBG ranged from 3.57 to 5.84 mg/kg. These differences were statistically significant (p < 0.05).

The rise in HMF levels in breads containing BG and EBG is probably linked to the increased Maillard reaction rate, caused by higher concentrations of reducing sugars and amino acids introduced by BG. Both the amount of reducing sugars, their ratio to amino acids and proteins, and the dough pH are viewed as key factors affecting HMF formation.Ramírez-Jiménez et al. [41], reported that HMF levels in bread crumb ranged from 0.60 to 2.20 mg/kg dry matter, while crust levels ranged from 18.30 to 176.10 mg/kg dry matter. Since no previous study has documented the HMF content of breads enriched with BG, direct comparisons with existing literature are not possible; however, the HMF value of the control bread in this study aligns with published data.

Antioxidant capacity and total phenolic content

The antioxidant capacity of the bread samples was determined using two different assays: DPPH and ABTS (Table 5). According to the DPPH assay, breads enriched with BG showed antioxidant capacities ranging from 78.94 to 134.94 µmol Trolox/100 g dry matter (DM), while those enriched with EBG ranged from 71.15 to 99.83 µmol Trolox/100 g DM. The highest antioxidant capacity was observed in the bread containing 8% BG. According to the ABTS assay, the antioxidant capacity ranged from 179.40 to 294.51 µmol Trolox/100 g DM in BG-enriched breads and from 176.72 to 213.54 µmol Trolox/100 g DM in EBG-enriched breads. As shown in Table 5, the addition of BG and EBG significantly enhanced the antioxidant capacity of the breads compared to the control . However, breads enriched with BG exhibited consistently higher antioxidant activities and total phenolic content than those with EBG at equivalent substitution levels. This difference is primarily attributed to the composition of EBG, as it contains both the black garlic and an encapsulating wall material; the total amount of active garlic constituents in EBG breads is effectively lower than in BG-enriched breads. Consequently, while the encapsulation matrix is designed to protect bioactive compounds during the thermal process, the dilution effect of the wall materials results in a lower net contribution to the bread’s overall antioxidant potential compared to the direct incorporation of black garlic paste. Yu et al., [42] reported that breads made from whole wheat flour had DPPH antioxidant capacities ranging from 2.79 to 4.05 µmol Trolox equivalents (TE)/g (mean 3.36 µmol TE/g), while breads made from refined wheat flour ranged between 2.83 and 3.90 µmol TE/g (mean 3.07 µmol TE/g). Phenolic compounds are important aromatic secondary metabolites in plants, influencing the sensory attributes of foods (color, taste, and flavor). Many phenolics exhibit antioxidant, anticancer, antimicrobial, antiallergic, antimutagenic, and anti-inflammatory activities. Garlic is known to be rich in phenolic compounds [7,43], and the total phenolic content of BG has been reported to be up to ten times higher than that of fresh garlic13.

Organic acid content

In the bread samples, citric acid (m/z 191), pyroglutamic acid (m/z 128), pyruvic acid (m/z 87), malic acid (m/z 133), and succinic acid (m/z 117) were identified and quantified using LC–MS/MS (Table 6). Succinic acid was not detected in the control bread but was found at the highest level in the bread enriched with 8% BG. In this sample, the levels of citric, malic, succinic, and pyroglutamic acids were all significantly higher compared with the control (p < 0.05). These findings indicate that increasing the concentration of BG and EBG markedly enhances the organic acid content of bread. A comparative analysis of the two enrichment forms reveals that BG-enriched breads consistently exhibited significantly higher organic acid concentrations than EBG-enriched breads at equivalent substitution levels (Table 6). The influence of BG and EBG on bread quality parameters was further evaluated using principal component analysis (PCA), and the results are presented in Fig. 2. The biplot explained 85.15% of the total variance (F1: 67.36%, F2: 17.80%) and revealed how chemical and physical properties clustered among the samples. Aroma profiles of breads enriched with BG and EBG

Among the aroma compound groups formed during baking, aldehydes, ketones and alcohols are mainly produced through lipid oxidation, while certain characteristic aroma compounds can also be generated in yeast cells via the Ehrlich pathway from amino acid degradation [44]. In this study, during conventional bread production, fresh garlic, BG and EBG were incorporated at selected concentrations, and preliminary sensory evaluations were performed by a panel of researchers experienced in bread analysis. Based on these initial sensory tests, optimal addition levels of garlic were determined. Three bread samples were subsequently analyzed for their aroma profiles: a control bread without garlic, bread with 8% BG, and bread with 8% EBG.

Volatile compounds were extracted using headspace solid-phase microextraction (HS-SPME) and analyzed by GC–MS. The identified compounds and their relative quantities are presented in Table 7. The control bread contained eight volatile compounds, whereas the 8% BG sample contained 21 compounds and the 8% EBG sample contained 18 compounds. Across all samples, a total of 21 distinct volatile compounds were identified and quantified, including four sulfur compounds, four aldehydes, two ketones, two furans, three alcohols, four volatile acids, one ester, and one terpene. Sulfur compounds were notably more abundant in the BG-enriched breads, likely contributing to their characteristic roasted and sweet garlic aroma.

Organosulfur compounds According to the results presented in Table 7, adding garlic had a significant effect on the total amount of volatile aroma compounds in the breads. The breads enriched with 8% BG and 8% EBG contained 1559.50 µg/kg and 958.00 µg/kg of total volatile compounds, respectively. In contrast, the control bread, made with a conventional formulation without garlic, showed the lowest volatile concentration (819.10 µg/kg) and the fewest identified compounds. As expected, no sulfur-containing compounds were detected in the control bread. However, both the BG- and EBG-enriched breads contained a total of four organosulfur compounds, including two disulfides and two trisulfides.

The organosulfur compounds, which are the predominant aroma constituents of BG, also played a crucial role in shaping the characteristic aroma of the garlic-enriched breads. Previous studies have reported that garlic contains more than thirty organosulfur compounds, which act as key bioactive components of the Allium genus, contribute to its distinctive aroma, and are involved in plant defence against abiotic stress and pests[45]. In the present study, the total disulfide concentration was determined to be 92.30 µg/kg in the BG-enriched bread and 35.10 µg/kg in the EBG-enriched bread, highlighting the contribution of these compounds to the overall volatile profile. These disulfides are likely formed through the thermal degradation of sulfur-containing precursors such as alliin and allicin, which enhance the roasted and sweet garlic notes characteristic of BG-enriched breads.

The S–CH₃ bond present in organosulfur compounds is known to be thermally labile and prone to cleavage during heat treatment [46]. In this study, the concentrations of organosulfur compounds detected in the breads were found to be substantially lower than those reported in the literature for BG [47]. Moreover, when compared with our previous work6, it became evident that the levels of key organosulfur compounds, responsible for the distinctive garlic aroma, were markedly reduced during fermentation and encapsulation, with some sulfur compounds disappearing entirely. Literature reports indicate that the alliinase enzyme degrades cysteine sulfoxides, such as alliin, upon heat exposure, leading to the formation of thiosulfinates and volatile sulfur compounds, including diallyl sulfide and diallyl disulfide [48]. This thermal and process-induced degradation likely explains the lower sensory intensity of garlic aroma in bread compared with raw or BG.

Aldehydes: Another prominent aroma group identified in both the BG-enriched breads and the control bread without garlic was aldehydes. In bread, aldehydes and their related alcohols are mainly produced through yeast-driven breakdown of amino acids present in flour[49]. These compounds are crucial for shaping bread aroma due to their low odour thresholds and unique sensory properties[50]. In this study, furfural, likely formed from the thermal degradation of precursor amino acids such as cysteine and glutathione during BG fermentation, was found in breads containing 8% BG and 8% EBG at concentrations of 175.90 µg/kg and 20.80 µg/kg, respectively. Previous research has also indicated that furfural arises from the Maillard reaction and the thermal breakdown of sugars, adding sweet and roasted notes to bread and other baked goods[49]. The significantly higher furfural level in BG-enriched bread suggests that the thermal process involved in BG promotes the formation of Maillard-derived aldehydes.

Benzaldehyde, known to impart almond-like and caramel notes and typically present in both the crust and crumb of bread, was identified as one of the key aldehydes in the samples. Another important aldehyde, (E, E)-2,4-decadienal, which contributes a fried, fatty aroma, and nonanal, associated with citrus-like and soapy notes, were also detected in all bread samples[50]. All of these compounds have been reported in various types of bread in earlier studies[49,50,51]. Their presence in both BG- and EBG-enriched breads suggests that the addition of BG does not suppress the formation of key bread-related aldehydes.

Ketones: According to the findings of this study, 2(5H)-furanone and (E)-furfurylideneacetone were detected in all breads enriched with BG but were absent in the control bread. Previous studies have identified 2(5H)-furanone in BG and reported that it imparts burnt and caramel-like notes[47]. This compound is typically formed as part of the furan derivatives generated during Maillard reactions and the thermal degradation of sugars. Due to its low odor threshold, 2(5H)-furanone can significantly contribute to the overall aroma profile of food products[6,50,52].

(E)-Furfurylideneacetone has been identified in a variety of food matrices, including coffee, cocoa, soy sauce and baked goods, and is characterized by its woody, spicy and roasted aroma notes[53]. This compound is typically formed through reactions such as the aldol condensation of carbonyl compounds and unsaturated aldehydes and is associated with high-temperature baking or roasting processes, where it becomes part of the volatile aroma profile.

Furans Another aroma group found in bread samples and typically linked with thermal processing is furans. In this study, acetylfuran was found only in breads enriched with BG and EBG, while 2-methylbenzofuran was present in all bread samples. Acetylfuran is formed during BG fermentation through the Maillard reaction involving sulfur-containing amino acids such as S-alk(en)yl-L-cysteine under heat, and it imparts a distinctive sweet and caramel-like aroma to BG [54]. Furan derivatives generally form through the thermal dehydration of carbohydrates, especially pentoses, and from the breakdown of Maillard reaction intermediates[55]. Due to their low odour detection thresholds, these compounds significantly influence the overall aroma profile of foods. The exclusive presence of acetylfuran in BG- and EBG-enriched breads suggests that BG serves as a precursor for thermally produced furan derivatives, amplifying sweet and caramel-like aroma notes.

Alcohols Among the alcohols identified in the bread samples, furfuryl alcohol, 2-phenylethanol, and maltol were the most prominent. Furfuryl alcohol and maltol were detected only in breads enriched with BG and EBG and were absent in the control bread. Furfuryl alcohol is a furan derivative typically formed through the thermal degradation of pentoses and the reduction of Maillard reaction intermediates[50]. It has previously been reported in black onion [56] and BG[54], where it contributes caramel-like, sweet, and slightly burnt notes. Maltol is a heterocyclic aroma compound generated during sugar degradation and Maillard reactions in baking. Owing to its low odor threshold, it is responsible for caramel, cotton candy, and roasted sugar notes in various foods[57].

In contrast, 2-phenylethanol was detected in all bread samples. This compound is a key product of yeast metabolism, formed via the Ehrlich pathway from the amino acid phenylalanine during fermentation. With its floral, honey-like, and rose-like aroma, 2-phenylethanol plays an essential role in shaping the sensory profile of bread and other fermented foods[50]. The presence of furfuryl alcohol and maltol exclusively in BG- and EBG-enriched breads suggests that BG contributes thermally derived sweet and caramel-like notes, while yeast-driven 2-phenylethanol remains a fundamental contributor to the overall bread aroma.

Volatile Acids According to the data presented in Table 7, volatile acids are one of the main aroma groups in the bread samples based on total aroma concentration. Among these compounds, hexanoic acid, octanoic acid, and nonanoic acid were found in all breads, while acetic acid was only present in the bread enriched with 8% BG. Medium-chain fatty acids such as hexanoic, octanoic, and nonanoic acids are usually produced through lipid oxidation, yeast metabolism, and the fermentative activity of lactic acid bacteria[50,51]. Because of their low odour thresholds, these acids give fatty, slightly cheesy, and creamy notes to bread and other fermented foods[49]. Acetic acid, on the other hand, is a key volatile organic acid mainly produced during the metabolism of Saccharomyces cerevisiae and heterofermentative lactic acid bacteria, contributing mild sourness and sharp, vinegary notes to bread[58]. Its detection only in the BG-enriched sample is probably related to the organic acid content produced during BG fermentation and to thermal reactions occurring during baking[59].

The variation in the aroma composition of bread samples as influenced by the addition of BG and EBG was further visualized using a heat map and hierarchical cluster analysis (Fig. 3). In the heat map, the color scale indicates that red tones represent compound concentrations above the mean, while blue tones indicate concentrations below the mean; lighter colors (white and pale shades) correspond to values close to the average.

Heatmap and cluster analysis of bread samples.

Hierarchical clustering identified two main sample groups along the horizontal axis: the 8% BG and 8% EBG breads clustered together, while the control bread formed a separate group. This separation emphasises that the addition of BG caused a similar and significant shift in the volatile profile, whereas the control sample displayed a distinctly different aroma composition. On the vertical axis, volatile compounds were grouped based on their chemical similarity. For instance, allyl methyl trisulfide, di-2-propenyl trisulfide, and diallyl disulfide clustered together and exhibited high abundance (red tones) in both BG and EBG breads. Conversely, compounds such as octanoic acid, 2-methylbenzofuran, and nonanoic acid were more prevalent in the control bread and appeared at lower levels in the BG- and EBG-enriched samples. These clustering patterns clearly demonstrate that BG enrichment substantially remodels the bread’s volatile profile by enhancing sulfur-containing compounds while reducing certain lipid-derived and furan-based volatiles.

Aroma-active compounds of BG and BG-enriched breads

The aroma-active compounds in breads enriched with BG, along with their related odour descriptions and flavour dilution (FD) factors, are shown in Table 8, including the control bread made without any garlic addition. A total of 19 aroma-active compounds were identified across all bread samples, with FD factors ranging from 8 to 128. Of these, 16 compounds were successfully identified, while three remained unknown (LRI: 1110, 1195, and 1302). The unknown compounds were detected by the sensory panel during olfactometric analysis but could not be identified by GC–MS, likely due to their trace concentrations being below the MS detection limit or the lack of matching spectra in available mass spectral libraries. Among the identified compounds, five were alcohols, four were sulphur-containing compounds, four were acids, two were aldehydes, and one was a ketone. This distribution emphasises the important role of alcohols and sulphur-containing compounds in defining the distinctive aroma profile of BG enriched breads.

The most dominant aroma-active compounds identified in the breads enriched with BG were sulfur-containing compounds. This group, consisting of (1Z)-1-propen-1-yl propyl disulfide, allyl methyl trisulfide, di-2-propenyl trisulfide, and diallyl disulfide, was absent in the control bread but contributed garlic-like, burnt, and sulfurous notes in the BG-enriched samples. The flavor dilution (FD) factors of these compounds ranged from 8 to 128, with the highest FD value (FD = 128) observed for allyl methyl trisulfide in the bread containing 8% BG. Previous studies have reported the presence of allyl methyl trisulfide in BG and described it as a key contributor to cooked garlic, sulfurous, and egg-like aromas[47,60]. Moreover, such trisulfide compounds have been reported to form during BG processing under high temperature and humidity[47].

Allyl methyl trisulfide, one of the major sulfur compounds in garlic essential oils and a derivative of allicin, is not only responsible for the distinctive flavor and aroma of garlic but also exhibits antioxidant properties and potential health benefits[61]. Sulfur-containing compounds derived from the degradation of alliin and its derivatives have been recognized as crucial contributors to the aroma of both fresh and BG[47,60]. Another sulfur compound with high FD values in the garlic breads was diallyl disulfide, which was perceived more intensely in breads containing 8% BG. Literature reports indicate that diallyl disulfide, a key compound responsible for the characteristic garlic odor, is present in high concentrations in both fresh and fermented garlic and possesses notable anticancer potential [62]. In addition to garlic and its products, these sulfur-containing aroma compounds have also been detected as aroma-active components in various foods such as red radish, lychee, cheddar cheese, and blue cheeses, where they contribute to garlic-like notes[12,61,63] . These findings emphasize that sulfur-derived compounds, particularly allyl methyl trisulfide and diallyl disulfide, play a pivotal role in defining the characteristic aroma of BG-enriched breads.

Several aroma-active compounds identified in this study have not previously been reported in bread varieties and appear to be specifically associated with the incorporation of BG and EBG. Among the alcohols, 2-methyl-1-propanol, 3-penten-2-ol, 3-methyl-1-butanol, furfuryl alcohol, and 2-phenylethanol were detected, with flavor dilution (FD) factors ranging from 8 to 64. Notably, 2-phenylethanol, an aromatic alcohol known for imparting pleasant floral and honey-like notes, was absent in the control bread but contributed a distinct honey and floral character to breads enriched with BG and EBG.

These alcohols have also been previously documented in bread. Xu et al. [64] identified 2-methyl-1-propanol, 3-methyl-1-butanol, furfuryl alcohol, and 2-phenylethanol as aroma-active compounds in sourdough breads produced under low-temperature fermentation, where they were associated with sweet, wine-like, floral, caramel, burnt, and honey-like notes. Such higher alcohols are typically formed via the Ehrlich pathway, in which yeast metabolizes amino acids to produce fusel alcohols during fermentation. Among this group, 2-furanmethanol was particularly noteworthy. It contributed burnt and caramel-like notes to both the control and garlic-enriched breads, with the highest FD value (FD = 64) observed in the bread containing 8% BG. 2-Furanmethanol, a furan alcohol, is primarily generated through the thermal degradation of sugars and Maillard reaction intermediates, and it is widely reported as a key contributor to the aroma of baked products [65]. Importantly, 2-furanmethanol was also identified as the most abundant aroma-active compound in the BG samples produced in this study, and its highest FD values were again observed in BG-enriched breads, indicating a direct carryover from the garlic ingredient.

Regarding aroma-active acids, four compounds were identified: acetic acid, hexanoic acid, octanoic acid, and nonanoic acid, with FD factors ranging from 8 to 64. Previous studies have shown that acetic acid contributes pleasant sour and slightly sharp notes to baked goods and can positively influence bread aroma when present in moderate concentrations [50,66]. Among the acids, hexanoic acid (FD = 8-64) emerged as a particularly important contributor. It is typically produced via yeast metabolism and has been reported as a major aroma-active compound in products such as Xinjiang flatbread, sponge cakes, rye breads, whole-wheat and white breads[67,68] . Our findings further indicate that the addition of black garlic (BG) and encapsulated black garlic (EBG) altered the perception and intensity of these acid-derived notes, likely by modifying substrate availability and fermentation pathways.

Two aroma-active aldehydes were also identified: furfural (burnt, sweet) and benzaldehyde (bitter almond), with FD factors ranging from 8 to 64 for benzaldehyde and 8 to 32 for furfural. Benzaldehyde, a key aromatic aldehyde, is formed during thermal degradation of the amino acid phenylalanine [69] and has been widely reported as an important contributor to the aroma of sourdough breads [64] , as well as wheat bread crust and crumb[50]. Furfural, formed through sugar degradation and Maillard reactions, imparts sweet, caramel-like, and roasted notes and has been recognized as a major aroma-active compound in pumpernickel bread[70]. Furfural is also a characteristic volatile of BG[47], consistent with our observation of higher FD values in BG-enriched breads compared to the control.

The only aroma-active ketone identified was 3-hydroxy-2-butanone, which was detected in all samples with flavor dilution (FD) factors ranging from 8 to 32 (Table 8). The highest intensity (FD = 32) was recorded in the control bread, while the addition of BG and EBG led to a reduction in its perceived intensity, with FD values falling to 16 and 8, respectively. Lasekan et al.11 reported that 3-hydroxy-2-butanone contributes buttery notes to baked goods such as simit, and omission tests showed that its removal significantly altered the characteristic aroma profile. This compound has also been positively correlated with the aroma quality of bread in earlier studies[50].

Overall, these results demonstrate that enriching bread with BG profoundly modifies its aroma-active profile by introducing novel alcohols and enhancing furan- and acid-derived notes while simultaneously reducing ketone-related buttery attributes. These shifts are expected to influence the sensory complexity and consumer appeal of BG enriched breads, highlighting their potential for the development of differentiated, functional bakery products.

Sensory evaluation

Sensory analysis is an essential tool in the food industry for understanding consumer perception, guiding product development, and supporting marketing decisions [71]. In this study, breads made with 8% BG and 8% EBG were compared with a control bread to assess consumer acceptability. Six quality attributes were evaluated: loaf volume, crumb porosity, colour, texture, taste, aroma, and overall acceptability. Each attribute was rated on a five-point hedonic scale, where 1 indicated “very poor” and 5 indicated “very good.”

The control bread received the highest score for volume (4.57), while BG- and EBG-enriched breads scored 3.78 and 3.71, respectively (Fig. 4). In terms of crumb structure, the control and EBG breads scored 4.07, while BG bread scored 3.96. For colour, the control sample was rated highest (4.32), followed by BG (4.28) and EBG (4.07). Texture scores were slightly lower in the enriched samples (4.03–4.07) compared to the control (4.28), yet they remained well above the acceptability threshold. It is noteworthy that despite the substantial increase in instrumental hardness observed in Table 2, the sensory perception of texture was not negatively impacted to the same degree. This can be attributed to the significantly higher moisture content in the BG-enriched breads, which likely improved the perceived crumb softness and mouthfeel, thereby masking the objective increase in firmness. Furthermore, since a hedonic scale was employed, the results indicate that the panelists found the slightly firmer texture of the enriched loaves to be highly acceptable, rather than a quality defect. Regarding taste, BG bread achieved the highest score (4.10), slightly exceeding the control (4.07) and EBG (4.01). Aroma scores were highest in BG bread (4.25), followed by EBG (4.03) and the control (3.75).

Spider web diagram of the sensory analysis of the bread samples.

Overall acceptability scores ranged from 3.78 to 4.10, with all breads receiving ratings well above the mid-point of the scale. Based on these scores, the Acceptability Index (AI) was calculated to be between 75.6% and 82.0% for all samples. An AI value exceeding 70% is generally considered to indicate good sensory acceptance[21], further confirming the commercial potential of both BG- and EBG-enriched breads. BG bread achieved higher scores than the control in both aroma and overall liking, showing that BG addition can enhance the sensory profile of bread even when loaf volume and crumb uniformity decrease slightly. The improvement in aroma perception aligns with the enriched volatile and aroma-active compound profiles observed in BG-enriched samples.

These findings suggest that BG is a promising functional ingredient for bakery products. Its addition improves flavour complexity and consumer appeal without compromising acceptability. Although EBG enhanced sensory characteristics compared with the control, its effect was less pronounced than BG, suggesting that the encapsulation matrix may have partially hindered the release of certain aroma intensity during the short baking period. It should be noted, however, that the specific release kinetics of the bioactive compounds and volatiles under the high-temperature conditions of bread baking were not directly monitored in this study. Therefore, the protective and modulatory effects of EBG are interpreted based on the final product quality, and future research focusing on the real-time release behavior during thermal processing is warranted to further validate these findings.

Conclusion

This study comprehensively assessed the effects of BG and EBG at various concentrations on the technological quality, chemical composition, antioxidant capacity, and aroma profile of bread. Compared with the control, breads enriched with EBG exhibited lower water activity (aw) and decreased pH values. Baking loss, which was 15.56% in the control, ranged from 12.33% to 17.18% in breads enriched with BG and EBG, showing a declining trend as the level of supplementation increased. Colour analysis indicated that incorporating BG and EBG darkened both the crust and crumb and lowered lightness values. Protein content ranged from 9.14% to 9.99% in enriched breads, compared to 9.34% in the control. Addition of BG significantly increased the total sugar content, especially by elevating fructose levels due to the thermal depolymerisation of garlic polysaccharides during BG production.

The results showed that BG and EBG significantly improved the antioxidant capacity and total phenolic content of the breads. The highest levels were found in breads containing 8% BG, confirming the strong contribution of the phenolic-rich BG matrix to the functional properties of bakery products. Volatile analysis indicated that BG and EBG supplementation significantly increased both the total amount and diversity of aroma compounds compared to the control. Specifically, organosulfur compounds such as allyl methyl trisulfide and diallyl disulfide contributed to the characteristic garlic-like aroma while also offering bioactive benefits associated with health. Additionally, compounds like furfural, furfuryl alcohol, benzaldehyde, and 2-phenylethanol were identified as key aroma-active substances, imparting sweet, roasted, floral, and almond-like notes, thereby boosting the sensory appeal of the breads.

Overall, the findings show that BG and EBG can act as promising functional ingredients in bread making, enhancing both bioactive compound levels and flavour complexity without reducing consumer acceptance. The high sensory acceptability index and the maintenance of essential technological parameters (such as volume and texture) using standard commercial baking processes imply that black garlic-enriched breads could be successfully adapted to industrial scales. These products provide both improved nutritional profiles (through enriched organic acids) and enhanced functional properties (via increased phenolic content and antioxidant capacity) that may appeal to health-conscious consumers.
