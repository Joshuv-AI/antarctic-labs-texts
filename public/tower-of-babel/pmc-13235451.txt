Are shoulder and elbow surgeons well represented? A national analysis of shoulder and elbow faculty numbers in orthopedic residency programs
Authors: Stephen Cho, Kallan Richards, Youssef Galal, Vahe Yacoubian, Scott Steinmann, Wesley P Phipatanakul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOrthopedic surgery residents commonly rotate through various subspecialty services to receive a well-rounded training experience. In programs without dedicated shoulder and elbow faculty, the training experience for residents is generally supplemented through other rotations such as sports and hand surgery. Orthopedic surgery is a highly sought-after specialty for medical school graduates, with many studies available analyzing applicant demographics and trends in the application process.7,10,12 In contrast, relatively little attention has been provided for postresidency trends with regards to fellowship and job selection for graduating orthopedic surgeons.3,6,13 It is well known that there has been a noticeable decline in generalist orthopedic surgeons, with the vast majority of orthopedic surgery residents opting to complete a fellowship postresidency.4,9,11 Exposure to different orthopedic subspecialties is formative for many residents in the process of selecting a subspecialty that is right for them. However, subspecialty rotations differ between residency programs not only in terms of length of rotation, the year of residency said rotation is conducted, and the type of education that is provided while on the service but also in orthopedic subspecialty attending faculty representation at the program.

Anecdotally, some orthopedic surgery residents state that they have felt dissuaded by the prospect of applying for a shoulder and elbow position due to a perceived risk of less availability of jobs. Other obstacles cited include the overlap in shoulder cases between shoulder and elbow–trained and hand/sports-trained orthopedic surgeons.1,6,8 Despite these concerns, a recent survey study of graduates from American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES)-accredited fellowships demonstrates a 100% employment rate postfellowship.2 The purpose of this study is to characterize national trends in the faculty representation of fellowship-trained shoulder and elbow surgeons within orthopedic residency programs. To this end, a cross sectional study was designed to analyze the faculty registry of accredited orthopedic surgery residency programs in the United States (US). We hypothesize that ASES members are currently under-represented as faculty in residency training programs.

Methods and materials

This was a cross-sectional descriptive study with 2 phases. The first phase involved compiling data from the “Public Program Search” function on the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) website to list all ACGME-accredited orthopedic surgery residency programs in the US for the 2024–2025 academic year. A total of 209 programs were identified. For each program, the name, city, and state were logged in a standardized spreadsheet. Each ACGME-accredited orthopedic surgery residency was queried for its faculty directory. Of the 209 programs that are ACGME accredited, 13 were excluded due to inaccessible or unclear faculty information, leaving 196 programs that met our inclusion criteria.

All listed orthopedic surgery faculty were reviewed, and their fellowship training was recorded if it included fellowship training in shoulder and elbow, hand, or sports medicine surgery. When fellowship information was not available on the official program website, supplemental publicly available sources such as LinkedIn, Doximity, and US News Health were used to verify fellowship background. Faculty was categorized based on fellowship training in shoulder and elbow, sports medicine, or hand/upper extremity surgery. A shoulder and elbow–trained faculty member was defined as a surgeon who completed an accredited shoulder and elbow fellowship.

In the second phase of data collection, each identified faculty member was searched in the ASES membership directory from May to June 2025. For faculty with ASES membership, we recorded their membership category (active, associate, or candidate). To categorize residency programs geographically, we used the Association of American Medical Colleges Geographic Preferences Map, which divides the US into 9 distinct regions. These include New England (Northeastern coastal states), Middle Atlantic (Mid-Atlantic corridor), East North Central (Great Lakes region), West North Central (Upper Midwest and Great Plains), South Atlantic (Southeastern Atlantic states), East South Central (Appalachian and Mississippi River region), West South Central (Gulf states and central plains), Mountain (Rocky Mountain states and high desert), and Pacific (West Coast and Pacific states). This framework was used to stratify our data and examine regional variation in ASES representation across residency programs.

Results

Of the 196 ACGME-accredited orthopedic surgery residency programs evaluated, 67% (131/196) had at least 1 ASES member on faculty, meaning 33% of those programs did not have an ASES member on staff (65/196). Of the 65 programs with no ASES member, 28% (18/65) of those programs were located in the Middle Atlantic. Of the 196 programs, 40% (79/196) had 2 or more faculty who were ASES members. Discussion

Our results demonstrate that one-third of ACGME-accredited orthopedic surgery residency programs in the US have no ASES member on their faculty list. Moreover, more than one-third of residency programs currently do not have a shoulder and elbow fellowship–trained physician on their faculty list. The findings of this study suggest not only an under-representation in the presence of ASES members in residency training programs overall but more specifically an under-representation of faculty members with shoulder and elbow fellowship training. This raises questions about the level of mentorship residents receive from ASES members and shoulder and elbow fellowship–trained faculty. Based on our study results, we hypothesize that the majority of shoulder training at institutions without a shoulder and elbow–trained faculty member is likely conducted by fellowship-trained sports surgeons followed by fellowship-trained hand surgeons. Prior literature has emphasized the importance of faculty mentorship in influencing subspecialty choice and research productivity during training.5,14 The current results may explain recent trends in the perception of shoulder and elbow fellowship among residents.

Among all ASES members who are faculty at a residency program, 150 of 330 (45%) are candidate members. This could suggest that a substantial proportion of ASES members are still in the earlier stages of their career and less likely to hold senior academic appointments. Likewise, about 36% of shoulder and elbow fellowship–trained ASES members are candidate members. This distribution may translate into fewer individuals in leadership or mentorship roles within residency programs. The relatively high proportion of candidate members reflects a development and growth of future ASES involvement but also could indicate a smaller current base of members engaged in direct resident education and national society activities. More specifically, this study found that only 55% of residency programs had an associate/active ASES member listed as faculty. While shoulder and elbow fellowship is the most common fellowship training experience among ASES members who are residency program faculty, there is also a substantial number (32%) who are sports fellowship–trained ASES members, at 32% of the total number of faculty members who are ASES members.

The findings of this study also revealed notable regional disparities. ASES membership was most concentrated in the South Atlantic and Middle Atlantic regions, both of which contain large urban academic centers and are home to the majority of the orthopedic surgery residency programs. These regions featured 7 programs each with more than 4 ASES members each on the faculty list. This suggests an increased amount of faculty positions in these regions, increased shoulder and elbow case volumes, or less reliance on other orthopedic subspecialty surgeons to perform shoulder and elbow cases. Regions such as the West South Central, East South Central, and Mountain areas demonstrated relatively sparse ASES members and shoulder and elbow fellowship–trained in those programs' faculty list. These geographical variations may influence the likelihood of exposure to shoulder and elbow surgery during training in these areas, as well as play a role in further solidifying regional distribution of future fellowship applicants and subspecialty-trained surgeons. However, the presence of a higher number of ASES members in a given program or region does not necessarily equate to proportionally greater clinical exposure. Regional variation in case mix is influenced by local population demographics and workforce patterns. For example, areas with a high proportion of manual laborers, young athletes, or elderly populations may have a substantial shoulder and elbow surgical volume despite fewer formally affiliated ASES faculty. Thus, while the Middle and South Atlantic demonstrates a high concentration of ASES representation, the true extent of operative training in shoulder and elbow surgery may also depend on regional patient populations and local practice patterns.

There are several limitations of this study. Most importantly, the study relies on publicly collected data, which may be outdated or incomplete depending on the website information provided by different programs for their faculty rosters and ASES membership. Faculty hires, departures, or changes in membership status may not be immediately reflected on the residency program or ASES websites. Additionally, surgeons without clearly documented fellowship training in shoulder, sports, or hand/upper extremity surgery were excluded from subspecialty classification. While these individuals were still counted as ASES members where applicable, their exclusion from fellowship-specific categories may have led to an underestimation of subspecialty exposure in certain programs. Additionally, this study could not assess the extent of each individual involvement in their respective residency programs. Finally, this study is cross-sectional in nature, and while it can provide data regarding membership and faculty standing, it is unable to assess the reasons why these trends exist.

Despite these limitations, this study is the first to comprehensively map ASES membership across orthopedic surgery residency programs in the US and subsequently has elucidated national patterns of resident exposure to faculty with shoulder and elbow fellowship training. The findings reveal that a significant number of residency training programs do not have any ASES members, and more than a third of programs do not have a shoulder and elbow fellowship–trained surgeon listed as program faculty. This study highlights opportunities for ASES to expand its mentorship and educational outreach. This can potentially contribute to improving resident education and altering future fellowship trends in orthopedic surgery.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that one-third of ACGME-accredited orthopedic surgery residency programs in the US have no ASES member on their faculty list. Additionally, more than a third of programs do not have a shoulder and elbow fellowship–trained surgeon. Finally, there are large regional disparities in terms of ASES and shoulder and elbow fellowship–trained surgeon representation in residency programs. This study is the first to comprehensively map ASES membership across orthopedic surgery residency programs in the US and subsequently has elucidated national patterns of resident exposure to faculty with shoulder and elbow fellowship training. The findings highlight opportunities for ASES to expand its mentorship and educational outreach. This can contribute to improving resident education and altering future fellowship trends in orthopedic surgery.
