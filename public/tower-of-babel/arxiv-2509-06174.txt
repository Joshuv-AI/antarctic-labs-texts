From Long to Short: LLMs Excel at Trimming Own Reasoning Chains
Wei Han, Geng Zhan, Sicheng Yu, Chenyu Wang, Bryan Hooi

Abstract
O1/R1-style large reasoning models (LRMs) signal a substantial leap forward over conventional instruction-following LLMs. By applying test-time scaling to generate extended reasoning paths, they establish many SOTAs across a wide range of complex reasoning tasks. However, recent studies show that LRMs are prone to suffer from overthinking —the tendency to overcomplicate simple problems, leading to excessive strategy switching and long, convoluted reasoning traces that hinder their interpretability. To mitigate this issue, we conduct a systematic investigation into the reasoning efficiency of a broad set of LRMs and uncover a common dilemma: the difficulty in balancing multiple generation objectives such as correctness and brevity. Based on this discovery, we propose a test-time scaling method, EDIT ( E fficient D ynamic I nference T rimming), which efficiently guides LRMs to identify the shortest correct reasoning paths at test time. EDIT employs constraint-guided generation while jointly tracking length and answer distributions under varying constraints, allowing it to select responses that strike an optimal balance between conciseness and correctness. Extensive experiments across diverse models and datasets show that EDIT substantially enhance the reasoning efficiency, producing compact yet informative outputs that improve readability and user experience.

Methods

In this section, we first formalize the task definition of efficient reasoning mathematically.
Next, we present key observations derived from our benchmark results.
Finally, based on these observations, we propose our efficient search algorithm that navigates toward the optimal solution.

3.1
Problem Formulation

EDIT
is a test-time adaptation method, and therefore we hold a target model
\mathcal{M}
with constant parameters during the entire inference period.
Given a reasoning dataset
\mathcal{D}
, for each question
q_{i}\in\mathcal{D}
with the ground truth
a_{i}
,
we prompt
\mathcal{M}
multiple times to sample a collection
\mathcal{R}=\{\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2},...,\mathbf{r}_{N}\}
, where
The final answer
\hat{a}_{i}=\phi(\mathbf{r}_{i})
can be further extracted by a format matching function
\phi
. Our goal is to maximize the accuracy, which is based on an identity-checking function:

\mathrm{acc}=\frac{\sum_{\mathbf{r}_{i}\in\mathcal{R}}\mathbf{1}(\phi(\mathbf{r}_{i})=a_{i})}{|D|}

(1)

where
\mathbf{1(\cdot)}
is the indicator function and
|D|
is the dataset size. Meanwhile, we aim to minimize the average reasoning lengths
\bar{l}
calculated as the average number of tokens from the correct solution path for each question (at most one for each question) across the datasets.

\bar{l}=\frac{\sum_{i,\phi(\vec{r}_{i}=a_{i})}l_{i}}{|D_{corr}|}

(2)

The goal is to seek a generation strategy
\Gamma
, which can produce accurate answers and minimize the length of reasoning paths.
Formally, this problem can be rewritten in a constrained optimization form, where
\alpha
is the length constraint.

\Gamma^{*}=\arg\max_{\Gamma}\mathrm{acc}(\Gamma),s.t.\;\bar{l}=\bar{l}(\Gamma)\leq\alpha

(3)

Empirically, as
\alpha
diminishes, accuracy usually degrades because more solutions are truncated ahead of figuring out the answer. However, we can leverage this property for efficiency evaluation.

3.3
Dual Goal Search

Overview

EDIT
is inspired by the achievements of
principle-guided generation

(
sun2023principle
;
pmlr-v235-beurer-kellner24a
;
pmlr-v202-zhou23g
)
on LLMs and LRMs’ ultimate goals of response on queries
wu2025more
. In
EDIT
, beyond the ultimate goal of solving the given problem, an additional parameterizable length constraint is included in the prompt templates.
In normal scenarios, LRMs endeavor to satisfy both goals. However, when LRMs forecast that they are incapable of fulfilling both goals, they tend to sacrifice one objective in favor of the other.
Take
Figure

2(a)
as an example—when the constraint is loose, LRMs automatically enhance the reasoning outcome by creating a long reasoning path, as developers expect.
At the beginning of tightening the length constraint, LRMs successfully follow our guidance to shorten their output length while keeping the final answer correct (step 1 to step 2). However, when we further limit the output length, the LRMs cannot simultaneously satisfy both objectives forever.
They tend to prioritize the goal they —typically the length during generation, and output the wrong answer (from step 2 to step 3).
To address this,
EDIT
monitors the dynamics of two key statistics—answer confidence and length distribution—and adjusts the constraint strength accordingly, either relaxing or tightening it as needed.

Algorithm

We describe the core algorithm in
EDIT

Dual-Goal Search
in
Algorithm

This algorithm is essentially a binary search with patience.
The search process begins with the loosest constraint and full patience (line 3).
At the start of every following iteration, the algorithm checks the consistency of the predicted answers and length distributions by comparing the answer and length metrics. Consistency is not broken only if 1) Answers from the two iterations are same; 2) The length metric adapts in response to changes in the applied constraints.

If the consistency checking passes, the algorithm proceeds with a stronger constraint by updating the current constraint parameter as the new upper bound (line 9-10).
Otherwise, if there is patience remaining, the algorithm continues this procedure with a tighter constraint and reduced patience (line 19-20). If patience is exhausted (line 11-18), the algorithm compares the current answer with the most confident answer recorded in previous iterations from
H
. The search boundary is updated according to the comparison result: if the current answer is considered consistent with the previously confident answer, the upper bound is decreased to further strengthen the constraint; otherwise, the lower bound is increased to make room for LRMs to think more freely and find the correct answer.

The hyperparameter patience is introduced to prevent the misdirect of search and the return of suboptimal solutions.
For example, as illustrated in
Figure

2(b)
, when patience
\beta=0
, cases like
Figure

2(a)
may arise, where the algorithm encounters an inconsistency and exits the process immediately. With the patience
\beta_{0}

The criteria of confidence can be implemented as majority voting or aggregation of logarithm probabilities. We find the results of the two implementations are quite close, and thus opt to use voting for simplicity. As for the statistical indicators that represent the length distribution, we select the average value of the minimum, lower quartile, and median, based on the findings from

3.2

lstat=\mathtt{Ans\_Stat}(\mathcal{C},\hat{a})=\frac{\min(\mathcal{L})+Q_{1}(\mathcal{L})+\rm{median}(\mathcal{L})}{3}

(4)

where

denotes the length collection of responses whose predicted answers equal the most confident answer
\hat{a}

4.3
Main Results

The results averaged over five runs are shown in
Table

2
. The key findings from these results are following:

•

RL-enhanced models (e.g., the Qwen-3 8B/32B, QwQ-32B and Phi-4-Reasoning) consistently show better capabilities than models in other categories. Models trained with reasoning data distillation (e.g., R1-Distill-Llama-3.1-8B and R1-Distill-Qwen1.5B) perform worse than RL-enhanced models but still outperform the SFT-only baseline (Llama-3.1-8B).

•

Direct CoT prompting (DP) baseline always ranks at the bottom due to the lack of test-time compute scaling. On the other hand, in several test cases, DP can produce the shortest reasoning path compared to other methods, which could be attributed to the impact of greedy decoding. By scaling test-time compute, best-of-N (BoN) selection and self-truncation (ST) both achieve significantly better results compared to DP.

•

While showing the highest accuracy in every test cases,
EDIT
shows superior reasoning efficiency—it manages to preserve accuracy to the maximum extent and cut off the reasoning path. In most cases,
EDIT
nearly achieves or surpass the best baseline with concise reasoning process. Specifically, the largest performance gap is below relatively 2% with 20% fewer tokens produced in the reasoning paths.

•

EDIT
appears to struggle for decision on non-reasoning models. The only outlier on length is on AIMO using Llama-3.1-8B, where
EDIT
generate 25% more tokens to pursue a significant accuracy boost. In contrast, on MATH and GSM8K, it works similar to other models that strive to reach the pareto optimality.

4.4
Efficiency Measure

In
Section

4.3
, evaluations on two major metrics were reported. However, these two metrics can conflict with each other, obscuring a clear comparison between methods. Admittedly, efficiency (reasoning path length) is important, but we must remain aware that it is sometimes acceptable for the model to sacrifice a degree of efficiency in exchange for improved accuracy. Hence, in evaluating efficiency, we should also quantify how much cost the model has spent on generating incorrect answers. For this purpose, we provide two evaluation methods as follows.

4.4.1
Token-wise Efficiency

Accuracy per Effective Length (AEU)

Previous work
(
ma2025cot
)
adopts Accuracy per Computation Unit (ACU) as the efficiency indicator, which takes into account both the number of trainable parameters and the number of tokens.
Since there are no trainable parameters in our experimental settings,
we slightly modify this idea by removing the item of parameters and recalibrating the token counting methods, which can better reflect the integrated computational cost.
Formally, we define
\mathrm{AEU}=\frac{ACC}{\bar{l}}
.

Length Recalibration

Recall the batched inference process in vLLM
kwon2023efficient
, where the time complexity is determined by the longest generated sequence.
Hence, we formulate the effective length
\bar{l}_{pen}
as

4.4.2
Budget Constrained Accuracy (BCA)

BCA aims to answer the following question: given a fixed token budget
B
, how does the accuracy shift accordingly?
Intuitively, a smaller budget forces many correct but lengthy responses to terminate prematurely, thereby hurting the accuracy.
Therefore, the BCA curve provides a clear depiction of reasoning efficiency.

To obtain the BCA curve, we collect many budget–accuracy datapoints from different methods.
As shown in
Figure

3
, the accuracy generally improves as more generation budget is provided.
Notably, on all datasets,
EDIT
leads by a large margin over the other two baselines.
This trend highlights the strong capability of
EDIT
to produce correct answers under limited computational budgets, and probably offers an explanation about why
EDIT
usually has the lowest calibrated length as in
Table

3
.

4.5
Ablative Studies

We conduct a series of experiments on ablative settings to verify the necessity of the proposed design components.
In particular, we focus on three aspects: the choice of characteristic statistic, the maximum number of iterations, and the initial patience.
Experiments are carried out on two models (R1-distilled-Qwen-1.5B and Llama-3.1-8B) under the corresponding ablative settings using the MATH dataset. The results are shown in
Table

4
.

There are some common patterns in the results of the two models. First, changing the length indicator causes performance degradation, which demonstrates the validity of employing a comprehensive metric that jointly considers multiple statistics.

Next, regarding patience, a smaller patience value makes the search process overly cautious, preventing LRMs from producing concise solutions. In contrast, a larger patience value helps resist sampling noise and enables the search to continue toward the optimal solution.
Regarding the maximum number of iterations, the fixed sampling quota implies that increasing iterations reduces the sample size per iteration, resulting in answers with higher uncertainty. This effect is reflected in the observed drop in accuracy when the iteration count is set to 8.
Conversely, reducing the iteration upper bound has a similar effect to increasing patience: although more candidate reasoning paths are explored within each iteration, the search process may terminate prematurely at a shallow level, thereby failing to produce canonical answers.

Conclusion

In this paper, we propose
EDIT
(
E
fficient
D
ynamic
I
nference
T
rimming), a test-time scaling method aimed at generating concise and correct reasoning paths.

EDIT
leverages distributional shifts under different constraints
discovered in our preliminary experiments, and designs a dual-goal search algorithm to reach the optimal tradeoff between simplicity and correctness. Experimental results across a broad range of models and datasets demonstrate the advantage and robustness compared to other strong baselines.
To mitigate the deficiency of using separate metrics to assess model overall performance, we further propose two alternative evaluation strategies for efficient reasoning, which can better calibrate the tradeoff between accuracy and length.
We hope this work could provide new insights and inspiration for the LRM research community.

Limitations

In this paper, we investigate the approach to search the shortest and correct solution on different reasoning tasks.
We mainly focus on the statistical metrics on the same set of system configurations.
In fact, there are many possible directions from system’s perspective that can further optimize the influence efficiency, such as parallel programming, better memory I/O, etc.
We believe future work can explore the intersection of these two areas to maximize the efficiency of large reasoning models.

Broader Impacts

Besides what we mentioned in
Limitations
, we believe our work can inspire more researchers to explore more efficient reasoning models. While LRMs represent a promising future trend, they currently often suffer from low efficiency. There is an urgent need to reduce their potentially high computational cost.

A.2
Model Specifications

There are seven targeted models being tested. All targeted models can be divided into three categories:

•

Instruction tuned model (Llama series): This category is the most typical instruction-following models. They only experience pretraining and supervised fine-tuning stage.
Additional red-teaming and human-preference alignment may be further applied to make its output fulfill the HHH (Helpful, Honest, and Harmless) principle.
Although demonstrating powerful general task solving capability, they show poor performance when encountering complex reasoning problems.

•

Reinforcement Learning (RL) enhanced reasoning model (QwQ-32B, Qwen3 series): These models are endowed with better reasoning capabilities through extra RL stage on large-scale reasoning data before traditional SFT and RL-based value-alignment stages.

•

Distilled model (R1-distill-llama3-8B, R1-distill-Qwen-1.5B, Phi-4-reasoning-14B): These models are released along with DeepSeek-R1
