Exercise modality and subsequent musculoskeletal medical expenditures among middle-aged and older adults: A population-based longitudinal study
Authors: Eunjeong Choi, Seoyeong Choi, Suk-Yong Jang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/objectiveMusculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) contribute substantially to pain, disability, and health care spending in aging populations. Although physical activity has been associated with lower health care costs, less is known about whether medical expenditures differ by exercise modality. This study examined whether musculoskeletal medical expenditures differed between non-exercisers, unsupervised exercisers, and supervised exercisers among middle-aged and older adults.MethodsUsing Korea Health Panel data from 2019 to 2023, we examined the association between exercise modality assessed in a given year and musculoskeletal medical expenditures measured in the following year among adults aged ≥40 years. Exercise modality was classified as non-exercise, unsupervised exercise, or supervised exercise with paid professional instruction. Medical expenditures were modeled using generalized estimating equations with a gamma distribution and log link. Adjusted expenditure ratios (aERs) were estimated using pairwise comparisons, adjusting for sociodemographic characteristics, chronic disease status, and previous-year medical expenditures. Analyses were stratified by age group, with additional sex-stratified analyses conducted within age groups.ResultsAmong adults aged 40-59 years, musculoskeletal medical expenditures did not clearly differ by exercise modality. Among adults aged ≥60 years, non-exercisers had higher musculoskeletal medical expenditures than unsupervised exercisers (aER 1.17, 95% CI 1.06-1.30; P = .003). However, differences involving supervised exercise were not clearly observed, including supervised versus unsupervised exercise (aER 0.92, 95% CI 0.62-1.36; P = .67) and supervised exercise versus non-exercise (aER 0.78, 95% CI 0.53-1.16; P = .23). In additional sex-stratified analyses, the difference between non-exercisers and unsupervised exercisers appeared more evident among men aged ≥60 years.ConclusionAmong adults aged ≥60 years, non-exercisers had higher subsequent musculoskeletal medical expenditures than unsupervised exercisers, whereas supervised exercise was not clearly associated with additional expenditure differences beyond unsupervised exercise. These findings suggest that regular exercise participation may be relevant to musculoskeletal health care spending in older adults, while the incremental economic value of professional supervision requires further investigation.

Introduction

Globally, musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) are a major cause of pain-related reductions in quality of life and a key driver of increased health care utilization and costs.1 MSDs are a leading contributor to global disability and are increasing with population aging. Middle-aged and older adults, in particular, have a high prevalence of MSDs, leading to concentrated health care utilization due to chronic pain and functional decline.2 Because health care utilization for musculoskeletal conditions is closely related to pain severity and physical dysfunction, medical expenditure may serve as an objective indicator of disease burden and clinical needs, beyond being a financial metric alone. Consequently, preventive strategies targeting musculoskeletal health in this population are important for reducing health care burden and extending healthy life expectancy.

Regular physical activity is widely recognized as an important nonpharmacological strategy for preventing and managing MSDs, as it may reduce pain risk and preserve physical function.3,4 Beyond musculoskeletal health, physical activity has broader health benefits, and prior research has emphasized the relevance of activity volume and intensity for major health outcomes and longevity 5, 6, 7. A recent systematic review suggested that physical inactivity was generally associated with higher health care costs.8 Consistent with this evidence, recent population-based studies have linked higher levels of physical activity to lower health care expenditures, particularly among older adults.9,10 However, previous studies have primarily focused on the amount, intensity, or domain of physical activity, with limited attention to whether exercise is performed with or without professional supervision. Consequently, it remains unclear whether supervised exercise is associated with lower medical expenditures beyond those associated with exercise participation itself.

Behavioral economics provides a useful framework for understanding how exercise modalities may influence sustained participation. Behavioral compliance may improve when structural supports, commitment mechanisms, or immediate rewards are linked to behaviors with delayed benefits.11,12 Supervised exercise settings may provide additional external structure through scheduled sessions and instructor interaction, whereas unsupervised exercise depends more heavily on individual self-regulation. At the same time, supervised exercise may not always provide additional benefits over unsupervised or home-based exercise, and it may involve greater costs or heterogeneous exercise types. Therefore, whether supervision translates into lower medical expenditures remains an empirical question.

Physical activity promotion policies have emphasized improving access and supportive environments, including the expansion of facilities and equipment.13,14 South Korea provides a relevant context for examining these issues. While operating a single-payer National Health Insurance system, Korea has relatively high out-of-pocket payments, making medical expenditure a direct burden on household economies.15 Korea is also experiencing rapid population aging, with a projected increase in the burden of MSDs.2,16 In addition, the expansion of sports and fitness participation provides an opportunity to distinguish between unsupervised and supervised exercise groups.17 This study examined the association between exercise modality—classified as non-exercise, unsupervised exercise, and supervised exercise—and musculoskeletal medical expenditures among Korean middle-aged and older adults. Specifically, we aimed to distinguish whether medical expenditures differed between exercise participation and nonparticipation and whether supervised exercise was associated with additional differences in expenditures compared with unsupervised exercise.

Methods

Data source and study population

This study utilized data from the Korea Health Panel (KHP) covering the period from 2019 to 2023. The KHP is a nationally representative longitudinal survey jointly conducted by the Korea Institute for Health and Social Affairs (KIHASA) and the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS). Since 2008, the survey has been administered annually to track healthcare utilization, medical expenditures, health behaviors, and health insurance coverage among South Korean households. Trained interviewers visit the selected households to collect data on medical use and health-related information through face-to-face interviews.

The study population consisted of adults aged 40 years and older at the time of the survey. We restricted the sample to individuals aged ≥40 years to focus on an age range in which musculoskeletal-related medical expenditures are more commonly observed. We included participants with complete data on exercise participation (modality), musculoskeletal medical expenditures, and relevant covariates during the study period. Age was considered a key effect modifier in the relationship between exercise and medical costs. To reflect life-cycle differences in health behavior and medical needs, the population was stratified into two independent groups: middle-aged adults (40–59 years) and older adults (60 years and older).

Sample selection and study design

From the initial 64,554 person-year observations in the raw data from 2019 to 2023, restricting the sample to adults aged 40 years and older resulted in 45,195 observations. To establish temporal ordering between exercise modality and subsequent medical expenditures, we used a 1-year lagged design in which exercise modality in year t was linked to medical expenditures in year t+1. Accordingly, observations from 2023 were not used for exposure assessment because medical expenditure data for 2024 were unavailable. The final analytical sample included 34,025 person-year observations after excluding observations with missing values for exercise modality, medical expenditures, or covariates. Because individuals could contribute multiple observations across survey years, annual records were linked using unique personal identifiers to construct a longitudinal panel dataset. Repeated observations within individuals were accounted for in the statistical analysis. This design allowed us to examine two related questions: first, whether exercise participation was associated with subsequent medical expenditures compared with non-exercise; and second, whether supervised exercise was associated with additional differences in subsequent medical expenditures compared with unsupervised exercise.

Study variables

Exposure variable

The primary independent variable was exercise modality. Participants were categorized into three groups based on their reported exercise participation, exercise setting, and use of paid or professional instruction: (1) non-exercisers, defined as those who did not engage in regular exercise; (2) unsupervised exercisers, defined as those who engaged in regular exercise without professional instruction; and (3) supervised exercisers, defined as those who engaged in exercise under professional instruction with paid tuition, such as Pilates, yoga, swimming lessons, or personal training.

Outcome variables

The dependent variables were total medical expenditure and musculoskeletal medical expenditure. Medical expenditure data in the KHP were collected through computer-assisted personal interviews (CAPI) on health care utilization and payment amounts, supplemented by participants’ health diaries, medical receipts, and National Health Insurance utilization records when available. Total medical expenditure included all direct medical costs, including inpatient, outpatient, and pharmacy expenses, and covered both insured and noncovered services, including out-of-pocket expenses. Musculoskeletal medical expenditure was defined as medical costs incurred for the treatment of musculoskeletal disorders. All expenditures were calculated in South Korean Won (KRW). To establish temporal ordering between exercise modality and subsequent medical expenditure, exercise modality measured in year t was linked to medical expenditure in year t+1. Accordingly, exercise behavior preceded the expenditure outcome in each person-year observation.

Covariates

To control for potential confounders associated with both exercise participation and medical expenditure, the following covariates were included based on clinical relevance and prior literature: sex, household income, marital status, education level, chronic disease status, and residential region. Sex was categorized as male or female. Household income, used as a proxy for socioeconomic status, was divided into quartiles based on the distribution of the overall sample. Marital status was dichotomized as having a spouse, including married or cohabitating, and not having a spouse, including single, divorced, or widowed. Education level was categorized according to the highest level of education attained. Chronic disease status was defined as a binary variable indicating the presence of at least one major chronic condition at the time of the survey and was included to adjust for overall comorbidity burden. Previous-year medical expenditure was additionally adjusted for because prior health care use may influence subsequent medical expenditures and may reflect baseline differences in health care needs. Residential region was classified as metropolitan, including Seoul, Gyeonggi-do, and the six metropolitan cities, or non-metropolitan, including small-to medium-sized cities and rural areas. These variables were included as key sociodemographic and health-related determinants of healthcare utilization and expenditures.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics of the study participants were summarized using descriptive statistics according to exercise modality and age group. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. To evaluate the longitudinal association between exercise modality and subsequent medical expenditures, generalized estimating equations (GEE) with robust sandwich standard errors were used to account for the correlation of repeated observations within individuals in the panel data.18 GEE models were specified with a gamma distribution and log-link function because medical expenditure data are typically right-skewed among observations with positive expenditures. The exponentiated regression coefficients from these models were interpreted as adjusted expenditure ratios (aERs), representing the ratio of expected medical expenditures between comparison groups. Exercise modality was treated as a categorical variable rather than an ordinal variable. Accordingly, pairwise comparisons were conducted among the three exercise modality groups: non-exercise, unsupervised exercise, and supervised exercise. The main pairwise comparisons included non-exercise versus unsupervised exercise, supervised exercise versus unsupervised exercise, and supervised exercise versus non-exercise. In each comparison, the aER represents the expected expenditure for the first group relative to the second group.

Given the potential effect modification by age and life-course differences in health behaviors and healthcare needs, all primary analyses were stratified by age group: 40–59 years and 60 years or older. The same GEE model specification was applied within each age stratum. Multivariable models were adjusted for sex, household income, marital status, education level, chronic disease status, residential region, and previous-year medical expenditure. To account for pre-existing differences in healthcare use, previous-year medical expenditure was included as a time-varying covariate. Specifically, when exercise modality in year t was linked to medical expenditure in year t+1, medical expenditure in year t was additionally adjusted for. Previous-year total medical expenditure was adjusted for in models of total medical expenditure, and previous-year musculoskeletal medical expenditure was adjusted for in models of musculoskeletal medical expenditure. Additional analyses stratified by both age group and sex were performed to examine whether the associations differed across age-sex strata. These analyses used the same GEE specification and covariates, except for the stratification variables. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS Enterprise Guide version 7.1 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of person-year observations in the analytic sample according to exercise modality, stratified by age group. In both age groups, supervised exercisers had a higher proportion of women and tended to have higher socioeconomic status, as reflected by higher household income and educational attainment. Table 2 presents pairwise comparisons of total and musculoskeletal medical expenditures according to exercise modality, stratified by age group. The models were adjusted for previous-year medical expenditures and other covariates. For total medical expenditures, no statistically significant differences were observed between exercise modality groups in either age group. Among adults aged 40–59 years, exercise modality was not significantly associated with musculoskeletal medical expenditures in any pairwise comparison. Among adults aged 60 years and older, non-exercisers had significantly higher musculoskeletal medical expenditures than unsupervised exercisers (adjusted expenditure ratio [aER], 1.17; 95% CI, 1.06–1.30; P = .003). Supervised exercisers did not differ significantly from unsupervised exercisers (aER, 0.92; 95% CI, 0.62–1.36; P = .669) or non-exercisers (aER, 0.78; 95% CI, 0.53–1.16; P = .225). Although some point estimates for supervised exercise were in the direction of lower musculoskeletal expenditures among older adults, the confidence intervals were wide and included the null value. In analyses stratified by both age group and sex, the overall findings were generally consistent with the main analysis (Table 3). Among men aged 60 years and older, non-exercisers had significantly higher musculoskeletal medical expenditures than unsupervised exercisers (aER, 1.31; 95% CI, 1.10–1.55; P = .002). However, supervised exercise was not significantly associated with lower musculoskeletal medical expenditures compared with unsupervised exercise in any age-sex stratum. An isolated difference in total medical expenditure was observed among men aged 40–59 years for supervised exercise versus unsupervised exercise; however, the corresponding difference in musculoskeletal medical expenditure was not observed. Discussion

This study used data from the Korea Health Panel to analyze differences in musculoskeletal medical expenditures among middle-aged and older adults according to exercise modality. Among adults aged 60 years and older, musculoskeletal medical expenditures differed by exercise modality, with non-exercisers showing higher expenditures than unsupervised exercisers. However, evidence for additional differences in expenditures between supervised and unsupervised exercise was limited. These findings suggest that exercise participation among older adults may be relevant to musculoskeletal health care spending, whereas the incremental economic benefit of professional supervision requires further investigation. These findings may inform policies aimed at improving access to regular exercise opportunities for older adults, while highlighting the need for further evidence on the potential added value of instructor-led exercise programs.

Although direct comparisons are challenging because few studies have examined supervised exercise as a distinct exposure in relation to medical expenditures, our findings are consistent with observational evidence on regular or habitual physical activity and health care spending. A systematic review found that physical inactivity was generally associated with higher short-term health care costs.8 Population-based and population-level studies involving middle-aged and older adults have similarly reported associations between higher levels, prevalence, or frequency of physical activity and lower health care expenditures or selected expenditure components.9,10,19,20 Although these studies assessed the level, prevalence, or frequency of physical activity rather than professional supervision, they provide relevant context for our finding that non-exercisers had higher musculoskeletal medical expenditures than unsupervised exercisers among adults aged 60 years and older. In contrast, previous economic evaluations of structured exercise-based interventions have primarily examined the cost-effectiveness of specific programs for older adults or individuals with musculoskeletal conditions.21,22 Such studies do not isolate whether professional supervision itself provides additional economic benefits beyond exercise participation. In the present study, differences involving supervised exercise were not clearly observed. This pattern suggests that regular exercise participation itself may be more directly related to musculoskeletal health care spending than professional supervision alone. Regular exercise may help maintain physical function and muscle health in older adults, which could contribute to lower downstream musculoskeletal health care spending.23,24 Therefore, this study contributes to the literature by distinguishing exercise according to supervision status and showing that the incremental economic benefit of professional supervision requires further evaluation.

In additional sex-stratified analyses within age groups, the association between exercise modality and musculoskeletal medical expenditures appeared more evident among men aged 60 years and older, particularly for non-exercisers compared with unsupervised exercisers. This finding should be interpreted cautiously because the analysis was exploratory and the pattern was not consistently observed among women or middle-aged adults. Although prior evidence suggests that exercise-related changes in muscle mass, strength, and physical performance may differ by sex among older adults, whether such differences translate into health care expenditure differences remains unclear.25

Physical activity promotion policies have often included environmental approaches, such as expanding facilities and providing equipment. As reflected in the WHO's Global Action Plan on Physical Activity 2018–2030, creating “active environments” and “active societies” represents an important component of physical activity policy.13 Similarly, policies in South Korea, such as the 5th National Health Plan (Health Plan 2030), have largely focused on establishing physical infrastructure, including expanding walking paths, public sports centers, and open-access exercise equipment.14 Evidence reviews suggest that physical activity promotion requires a broad policy mix that includes setting- and target-group-specific strategies in addition to environmental approaches.26 In this context, our findings suggest that exercise participation among older adults may be relevant to musculoskeletal health care spending, whereas the additional economic benefit of supervised exercise was not clearly observed. Therefore, policy efforts should prioritize accessible and sustainable exercise opportunities for older adults while further evaluating whether instructor-led programs provide added value beyond exercise participation itself.

Despite its strengths, this study has several limitations. First, because this was an observational study, causal relationships between exercise modality and medical expenditures cannot be established. Although we adjusted for measured sociodemographic characteristics and prior medical expenditures, selection bias and residual confounding may remain because factors such as baseline physical function, health consciousness, social support, psychological well-being, adiposity, and MSD severity were not fully captured. Waist circumference was not available in the KHP data used for this analysis, and BMI had substantial missing data; therefore, adiposity measures were not included in the final models. Second, exercise participation and modality were based on self-reported questionnaires, which may be subject to recall bias and misclassification. In particular, the supervised exercise category was heterogeneous and may have included diverse activities, such as Pilates, yoga, swimming lessons, personal training, or other professionally instructed exercise. The KHP did not provide detailed information on exercise dose, intensity, duration, continuity, or whether participants started and discontinued supervised programs; therefore, the findings should not be interpreted as reflecting the effect of a specific supervised exercise intervention. Third, the sample sizes for certain subgroups, particularly supervised exercisers and sex-stratified groups, were relatively small. This resulted in wider confidence intervals and limited our ability to fully evaluate heterogeneity by sex and age. Therefore, subgroup and sex-stratified findings should be interpreted as exploratory. Fourth, some supervised exercise may have occurred as part of a medical care pathway, such as rehabilitation or prescribed therapeutic exercise. If such services were captured within musculoskeletal medical expenditures, this could complicate interpretation of the association between supervised exercise and subsequent expenditures.

Conclusion

Among adults aged 60 years and older, non-exercisers had higher musculoskeletal medical expenditures than unsupervised exercisers, whereas additional differences associated with supervised exercise were not clearly observed. These findings suggest that regular exercise participation may be relevant to musculoskeletal health care spending in older adults. Public health policies should support accessible and sustainable exercise opportunities for older adults, while further evidence is needed to determine whether instructor-led programs provide additional economic benefit beyond exercise participation itself.
