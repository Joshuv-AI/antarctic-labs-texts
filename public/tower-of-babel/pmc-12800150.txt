Use of gilsonite in the presence of salt for the control of formation and stability of aphrons
Authors: Fatemeh Ghazi Ardakani, Yousef Kazemzadeh, Soroush Ahmadi, Azizollah Khormali
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Colloidal Gas Aphrons (CGAs) are a next-generation intelligent fluid system in petroleum engineering with certain merits, such as improved interfacial tension (IFT) lowering, improved sweep efficiency, and increased stability, particularly under high-salinity conditions. However, their performance with mineral salts and solid additives is yet to be well established. In this work, we investigate in detail the synergistic effect of three cationic salts KCl, NaCl, and MgCl₂ at two salinity concentrations (5000 and 50000 ppm), constant concentration of cationic surfactant CTAB (300 ppm) and xanthan polymer (6.86 g/L) on the stability and microstructure of CGAs. We demonstrate that 5000 ppm KCl provided the most stable system with microbubbles in the size range predominantly larger than 50–100 μm. For enhancement of system robustness, two solid-phase additives were incorporated: natural gilsonite at 100 and 500 ppm, and Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles at 500 ppm (which is equivalent to 0.05 wt%). Combination of 100 ppm gilsonite and Fe₃O₄ exhibited optimum improvement in CGA stability. Physicochemical properties of the optimized systems in contact with heavy crude oil were examined in the final phase. Among all the tested salts, MgCl₂ exhibited lowest IFT (39.02 dynes/cm) and contact angle (20.58°), and shows enhanced oil-wettability alteration. This in-depth study not only provides new insights into salinity, nanoparticle, and natural additive synergistic impacts on CGA behavior but also paves the way for the development of specially formulated CGA formulations for the use in enhanced oil recovery from high-salinity reservoirs.

Introduction

Foam is a biphasic fluid composed of gas dispersed in a continuous liquid phase, exhibiting properties of both phases. Although its density is close to that of gas, its viscosity is significantly higher. In the context of enhanced oil recovery (EOR), foam has gained considerable attention due to its ability to improve sweep efficiency and control gas mobility, particularly in gas, CO₂, and steam flooding processes. By increasing apparent viscosity and reducing gas relative permeability, foam can mitigate viscous fingering and gravitational override effects in porous media1–3. The first demonstration of the use of foam in EOR was achieved by Boud and Holbrook, who showed that cyclic injection of surfactant solution and gas could be used to increase sweep efficiency in both immiscible and miscible situations4. Subsequently, Li et al. conducted a study to understand the mechanisms of foam mobility control in Enhanced Oil Recovery (EOR) processes, analyzing various factors such as foam stability, surface tension, the effects of pressure and temperature, and the use of chemical additives, which influence foam stability and movement in oil reservoirs5. Jia et al. found that due to the lower viscosity and density of gas compared to oil and water, the colloidal dispersion of gas foam in the liquid helps control gas mobility. This is achieved by increasing the apparent viscosity of the displacing fluid and reducing the relative permeability of the gas phase6.Thermodynamic instability, the need for continuous production, stability and diffusion in porous media highlight the need for the use of surface-active materials such as surfactants. Surfactants can improve the efficiency of oil recovery in enhanced oil recovery (EOR) processes by reducing interfacial tension and adsorption at the crude oil-water interface7. Field studies show that the application of this method, in addition to improving sweeping efficiency, reduces the impact of environmental damage of the conventional chemical flooding methods, making it a promising method for EOR8. The effectiveness of surfactant-stabilized foams may be compromised over time by reservoir conditions such as high salinity and temperature9. The foam-based systems for full-field enhanced oil recovery have also been validated through pilot-scale and laboratory studies. Recent research has gone to great lengths to show the outstanding performance of hybrid foam systems in low-permeability and fractured reservoirs, where conventional waterflooding cannot achieve a high sweep efficiency. For instance, this novel foam system consisting of sodium dodecylbenzene sulfonate (SDBS), nano-SiO₂, and water-soluble thixotropic polymer (WTP) can enhance oil recovery above 25% in fractured cores via blocking cracks and altering wettability, as revealed by NMR imaging and in Ordos Basin field tests10. A specially structured microbubble system with bubble diameters of 10 to 100 micrometers, which is prepared by stirring an aqueous solution of xanthan gum surfactant driven by an electric motor at high speed (above 4000 rpm) through a rotating disk. It was first known as colloidal gas aphron (CGA) in 197111. Aphron has a spherical core, which can be gaseous or liquid, and a multi-layer protective film that plays a key role in maintaining stability12. Aphron fluid differs from foam used in drilling fluids and the petroleum industry by having smaller bubbles with greater strength and stability due to a multi-layer coating of surfactant and polymer. Aphrons are formed by an outer shell of film with three layers of a much stronger surfactant, as opposed to a regular air bubble which is stabilized by a single layer of surfactant13. The hydrophobic and non-polar end of the surfactants is oriented towards the core, and the hydrophilic end faces the second phase. The second phase, containing mainly water, has at its outer boundary the aphron stabilizer, a thickener and a carrier of a surfactant. The hydrophilic end of surfactants spreads in the fluid and prevents adjacent aphrons from merging. CGAs can exhibit liquid properties very similar to those of pure water and can be easily separated from the bulk liquid phase. The specific gravity of CGA is approximately 0.35 and the air content is up to 65% by volume. Another advantage is that CGA maintains compression resistance up to a pressure of at least 27.3 MPa, which is 10 times the maximum pressure that conventional bubbles can withstand14,15. CGAs are composed of three main parts: water, polymer and surfactant. A number of unique features of CGAs, including large surface area due to their small size, have led to their wide application in various industries, especially in the petroleum industry. Their use in drilling mud improvement, enhanced oil recovery, cementing and well completion makes them popular and is seen as a solution to the problems in this industry. Ramirez et al. (2002) evaluated the use of aphron as a suitable fluid for addition to drilling fluid in drained formations16. Similar results were observed in microbubble-assisted CO₂ flooding with 10–50 μm bubbles, showing 26.38% incremental recovery compared to conventional CO₂ flooding with enhanced flow redirection in heterogeneous sandpacks due to increased gas-blocking capacity17. Their integration into enhanced oil recovery programs increases productionrate, demonstrating their superiority over traditional polymer injection methods. The ability to block highly permeable microbubble flow paths promises to revolutionize enhanced oil recovery techniques. The concentrations of polymer and surfactant are factors that directly affect the stability of aphron. The concentration of surfactants increases the ingress of air into the fluid system during mixing18. Experiments conducted in 2015 by Ghazanfari et al. on the stability of aphron showed that anionic and cationic surfactants play an important role in the formation of aphron fluids. They proposed using appropriate concentrations of xanthan gum and CMC11,19. However, high temperatures can cause surfactants and polymers to degrade and adsorb when in contact with oil, forming a gas-liquid layer20. Nanoparticles (NPs), due to their resistance under high temperature, pressure, and salinity conditions, can serve as a promising solution to enhance the stability of foam/aphron21. Owing to their small size and high surface reactivity, nanoparticles can effectively adhere to the foam structure, reinforcing its mechanical properties. The use of magnetic nanoparticles, with their ability to respond to magnetic fields, can be recycled for repeated use, helping to reduce enhanced oil recovery costs and improve the quality of the produced oil. The use of Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles, due to their ability to be attracted by a magnetic field, can be effective in enhancing the stability of foam/aphron22. Previous studies23,24have examined the role of gilsonite in the stability of emulsion systems in petroleum processes. Gilsonite is a natural form of asphaltene that has the ability to dissolve in aromatic substances. It can also be used as a new stabilizing agent to enhance foam/aphron stability. Increasing the asphaltene content leads to improved stability in emulsion systems by creating a spatial barrier and increasing the hardness of the surface layers. In general, gilsonite can act as a surfactant to enhance the stability of water/oil emulsions. This stability can change depending on the concentration of gilsonite25,26.

Recent studies have established that bubble diameter and surfactant–polymer interactions play a decisive role in stabilizing aphrons in the long term. Riazi et al. (2025) have shown that bubble diameter increase leads to reduced stability due to bubble coalescence, which verifies the direct proportionality of aphron stability with bubble size27. Furthermore, Pourbehzad et al. (2022) confirmed that surfactant-like natural additives can enhance the stability of semi-aphron fluids by slowing down liquid drainage by as much as 70%28.

However, no previous work has been conducted to investigate the synergistic effects of both Gilsonite nanoparticles and salts on CGA system properties.

The results of this study indicate that the mixture of salts with nanoparticles and Gilsonite has an outstanding effect on CGA stability. Additionally, the consideration of high salinity levels in the experimental design gives a high degree of applicability in real reservoir conditions, and the results are therefore highly generalizable in the design of efficient injection fluid formulations in reservoirs with high salinity. Besides, the detailed study of the effects of various concentrations of salt on interfacial tension (IFT) and contact angle (CA) has enabled the more accurate understanding of interfacial behavior, thereby contributing significantly to the optimum selection of formulation constituents.

This work is dedicated specifically to the influence of cationic salts in the presence of CTAB surfactant and, for the first time, introduces new additives such as Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles and Gilsonite as effective enhancers of CGA performance and stability.

Materials and methods

Materials

In this experiment, a polymer solution with a concentration of 6.86 g/l (in 100 ml of distilled water) was used as the viscous phase. The cationic surfactant type CTAB was selected at three concentration levels29. The studies of Pasdar et al. were used to select the polymer and surfactant concentrations13. To study the effect of salting, testing was carried out by adding three types of salt NaCl, KCl and MgCl2 to the solution at two concentrations: 5000 and 50,000 ppm1.

After that, the design of the surfactant concentration was done in such a way that it is possible to investigate the properties of CGA-based liquids during the change in the critical micelle concentration. Thus, for CTAB in the presence of KCl salt, three concentrations were selected: 150 ppm (below the CMC concentration), 300 ppm (CMC concentration) and 600 ppm (above the CMC concentration). Then, the effect of gilsonite was investigated by adding it at two concentration levels of 100 ppm and 500 ppm in two steps, and then adding Fe3O4 nanoparticles at a concentration of 500 ppm into the enhanced solution to investigate the effect of nanoparticles30. The oil used in this experiment is taken from one of the Iranian oil fields and has an API of 21.49° and a relatively high asphaltene content of about 16% by weight (Fig. 1).

Experimental procedure

For preparation of base fluid, the same concentration polymer was mixed with 100 mL deionized water and homogenized for 5 min at 4000 rpm to create a clear solution and be completely homogeneous. Cationic surfactant CTAB was incorporated in concentration 300 ppm, its critical micelle concentration (CMC), and blended for 5 min at the same settings.

In the second step, for the testing of salt composition and ionic system strength in a systematic way, three selected inorganic salts (NaCl), (KCl), and (MgCl₂) were added to the system separately in two concentrations (5000 ppm and 50,000 ppm).

For additional studies on the role of concentration by surfactant, CTAB was also investigated at 150 ppm and 600 ppm concentrations in other samples under otherwise similar conditions to develop some insight on concentration-dependent behavior of surfactant electrolyte systems.

Following the selection of the most effective formula, a 300 ppm CTAB and 5000 ppm KCl reference matrix was used. Then Gilsonite was added at concentrations of 100 and 500 ppm, followed by a test for the effect under normal mixing conditions (5 min at 4000 rpm). The role of magnetic Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles with mass fraction 500 ppm was then added, and their effect on aphron properties was also investigated.

At the final stage of study, crude oil at two constant concentrations was blended with NaCl and MgCl₂ containing brine systems to produce oil-based aphrons. Characterization of emulsion formed for interfacial properties was obtained by the measurement of contact angle (CA) and interfacial tension (IFT), giving qualitative characterization of oil–brine–surfactant interactions in a spectrum of ionic environments (Table 1). The polymer concentration and mixing time were constant for all tests, equal to 6860 ppm and 20 min, respectively. The test analysis

In order to determine the interaction between the aqueous and oil phases, the experiments were conducted at laboratory ambient conditions on a DSA100 Krüss instrument (Germany). The apparatus was capable of measuring interfacial tension (IFT) and surface wetting through contact angle analysis.

To calculate interfacial tension, the pendant drop method was employed. Throughout the process, a specific volume of crude oil was loaded into a microsyringe connected to a programmable syringe pump, which discharged the oil gradually into a glass container holding the aqueous medium. As the oil came out through the tip of the needle, necessarily a droplet would be formed and held suspended in the ambient water-based solution. High-resolution images of the droplet were obtained in real time by a high-resolution camera that contained an integrated illumination system (Fig. 2).

Results and discussion

The concentration effect

The effect of adding salts to polymer solutions is very complex due to the different types of intermolecular interactions between ion and water, ion and polymer, and polymer and water. Structure-forming salts, composed of small ions, cause changes in the structure of the aqueous phase through strong interactions with the dipoles of water. These interactions lead to an increase in the network energy of water, which in turn alters the physical and chemical properties of water in the presence of these salts31. A change in the distribution balance of neutral organic solutions towards non-aqueous phases is one of the effects of adding salt to a polymer solution. This effect is known as salt-out-effect. KCl (Fig. 3), NaCl (Fig. 4) and MgCl2 (Fig. 5) salts were added to the base solution at concentrations of 5000 and 50,000 ppm to investigate the effect of salt concentration. Figures 6, 7 and 8 show the dependence of diameter of the particles on salt concentrations. From the figures it can be seen that the average bubble diameter was small when low salt concentration was used. At concentrations above the micelle concentration, surfactant molecules begin to accumulate and cause cluster formation. CMC is really important because of its influence on various physicochemical properties of the solution. CMC is affected by salinity, so lower concentrations may improve solution properties32. As shown in Fig. 6, in a solution with a concentration of 5000 ppm, the aphron stability increases with the addition of salt. This phenomenon is due to the creation of enough crystals at the interface of the bubbles and the distances between them. But in higher concentrations it gives adverse results. Increasing the concentration of electrolytes enhances the penetration and interaction properties of surfactant molecules in the solution33.These changes lead to an improved performance of these molecules in reducing surface tension and enhancing surface behavior. Figure 7 shows that using high salt concentrations above the CMC at a concentration of 50,000 ppm results in increased bubble growth rate, decreased stability, and increased bubble diameter. It is known that the amount of accumulated particles and the formation of stable CGA is controlled by the concentration of salt34. It can be seen that at a concentration of 5000 ppm, the aphron formation ability of all three electrolytes is high. Increasing the concentration of cationic ions Na+, K+ and Mg2+ in the system causes them to react with the halide in the CTAB surfactant, resulting in an increase in the number of larger monomers and a weakening of the adsorption dynamics. As a result, aphron generation decreases as more salt is added35–37.

Effect of salt type

Among the efforts to interpret the complex behavior of cationic surfactants and salts such as CTAB, differential response towards foam stabilization by monovalent (Na⁺, K⁺) and divalent (Mg²⁺) ions was investigated. DLVO theory predicts the divalent ions to lower the electrical double layer to a considerable extent, reducing electrostatic repulsion but enhancing van der Waals attraction. This can accelerate film thinning or induce flocculation. But foam stability like in CTAB systems indicates DLVO interactions are insufficient to stabilize the processes. Surfactant assembly of structure at the air–water surface makes the surface elastic and generates Marangoni stresses as a restoring force when there is surface perturbation. Dynamic resistance formed is a hindrance to destabilization by multivalent ions like Mg²⁺. Thus, quantification of an interaction between surfactant molecule structure and ionic strength in accounting for foam behavior is justified with focus on the contribution of non-DLVO forces such as hydrophobic interaction and interfacial rheology38,39. The application of electrolytic salts in the presence of surfactants with high concentrations (more than CMC), in addition to reducing the amount of CMC and increasing stability, can reduce the environmental harm caused by the use of surfactants. The influence of various salts on the Aphron generation of aqueous solutions of CTAB and the stability of their aphrons was studied. The use of electrolytes NaCl, KCl and MgCl2 showed that salts in concentrations up to 5000 ppm may well play a stabilizing role for aphron stability and be a factor that has a significant impact on aphron formation. Salts with various capacities can have different effects on the stability of a surfactant solution. Salt capacity directly affects surfactant adsorption due to their different effect on electrostatic charge screening. Figure 6 depicts that the use of KCl has the strongest effect compared to NaCl and MgCl2 in creating a solution with more aphron and forming bubbles with greater stability. The formation of bubbles with greater stability and cohesion in the solution containing KCl compared to the other two salts indicates that the K+ cation, which has a larger ionic radius, can create electrostatic protection against repulsion between the air-water interface (reducing the electrostatic potential). The increase in stability in the presence of salt is due mainly to a decrease in surface diffusion caused by the accumulation of surfactant molecule tails as a result of their ability to reduce the repulsion between the positively charged head groups of the molecules40. The addition of KCl to the polymer solution had a more effective effect on reducing the interfacial tension and electrostatic forces. One of the factors that influences the stability of bubbles is their ability to reduce merging with each other. Bubble merging occurs when the liquid layer of each bubble discharges and the gas it contains flows into the next bubble37,41. After this, the average diameter of the bubbles increases and their stability decreases. As presented in Fig. 3, KCl increases stability by reducing the rate of coalescence as a result of reduced fluid flow around the bubble40. Figure 8 in the case of using the Mg2+ cation, due to the counteraction caused by the divalent nature of this cation, a greater separation between the positive and negative centers occurred. on, a greater separation of the positive and negative centers occurred. The attractive force between the divalent cation and the two surfactant molecules results in a close packing of the molecules at the air-water interface. In addition, the Mg2+ cation caused a decrease in foam/aphron volume and much lower stability due to a greater decrease in IFT compared to monovalent cations41. As it can be seen in Fig. 9, In general, a stable solution with a small average diameter is created in the presence of cations in the order K+>Na+>Mg2+.

Surfactant effect

Surfactants are among the most critical injection water additives after dissolved ions. The amphiphilic molecules containing hydrophilic as well as hydrophobic portions play an active role in the oil–water interface, significantly reducing interfacial tension. Classified into non-ionic, anionic, cationic, and amphoteric surfactants based on their ionic head group portions, surfactants find widespread application in the process of enhanced oil recovery (EOR). In addition to lowering interfacial tension, they change the wettability of rock reservoirs and support emulsion and foam stabilization. Surfactants are applied either separately or in combination with polymers and alkalis, both natural and synthetic42. Surfactants are mainly used to enhance the stability of microbubbles. Therefore, their formation and stabilization are largely influenced by the surfactants used. The adsorption mechanism of cationic surfactant CTAB with different salts (NaCl, KCl, and MgCl₂) is controlled by electrostatic attraction between the charge porous medium surface and the CTAB cationic head group. The divalent cations such as Mg²⁺ enhance the adsorption of the surfactant considerably owing to compression of the electrical double layer and reduced electrostatic repulsion. For comparison, monovalent salts NaCl and KCl have relatively lower adsorption. The findings are in line with literature and highlight the importance of salt composition, ionic strength, and valency on the adsorption properties of the surfactant. The adsorption isotherm follows a Langmuir model, and the type of salt and concentration will need to be optimized so that losses of surfactants will be minimized and the efficiency of chemical enhanced oil recovery processes will be maximized43. As mentioned in the previous sections, the critical micelle concentration (CMC) is one of the most important parameters in surfactant systems. At this concentration, the formation and aggregation of micelles by surfactants begins. The properties of surfactants differ significantly at concentrations above and below the CMC. Maximum adsorption usually occurs at CMC, so CMC is of particular importance. Figure 10 shows the effect of surfactants at concentrations above and below the CMC. It can be seen that at concentrations above the CMC, the bubbles move more towards multiple ones. The decrease in the number of smaller bubbles at this concentration, along with the increase in the average bubble diameter, may be due to the release of gas from the small bubbles by mass diffusion into the larger bubbles41. As depicted in Fig. 11 at high surfactant concentrations, the elasticity of the bubble film decreases, which leads to their collapse and transformation into bubbles with a larger average diameter. Overall, it can be concluded from the figure that the need for CMC is the main requirement for bubble development. At this concentration, the stability and size of the bubbles are significantly improved compared to the concentration below CMC. Meanwhile, increasing the concentration above the CMC does not have a significant effect on the bubble structure (Fig. 11).

Effect of gilsonite/nanoparticle

In recent years, the possibility of adding nanoparticles simultaneously with the surfactant has been considered in order to improve foam stability. Nanoparticles increase foam stability through the mechanism of adsorption and accumulation at the gas-liquid interface. The reason for the increase in this stability when using nanoparticles is the increase in the particle detachment energy and the maximum capillary pressure for merging bubble foam44. Nanoparticles are also capable of reducing direct contact between liquids, resulting in a decrease in the rate of gas diffusion and bubble bursting. For the same reason, film drainage is also reduced. Their smallness makes it possible for them to clog the pores of their throat when passing through a porous medium. One of the characteristics of nanoparticles is their very high stability in harsh reservoir conditions such as high pressure and temperature, high salinity and the presence of oil due to their solid nature. Nanoparticles, when combined with various chemical groups, can improve the wettability of solutions and the aqueous stability. They also enhance the ability of CO2 to dissolve and adhere to the gas-liquid interface. A distinctive feature of nanoparticles from other traditional foaming agents is their extraordinary adsorption at the interface. The separation energy of nanoparticles is very high due to (lamellar), which leads to the fact that their adsorption at the interface can be considered irreversible. While conventional foaming agents are easily adsorption and desorbed from the gas-liquid surface of foam bubbles. This absorption depends on the concentration of nanoparticles and their accumulation at the liquid-gas interface. The decrease in capillary pressure, which occurs due to the decrease in interfacial tension in the presence of nanoparticles, leads to a decrease in the pressure difference between bubbles located near each other, and for this reason gas diffusion decreases. The stability of the nanoparticle arrangement is controlled by their resistance to exit from the interface. The nanoparticle network can prevent film thinning and gas evolution by creating thick and strong lamellar. This phenomenon occurs after increasing the viscosity of the surfactant solution and preventing a decrease in gas emission32. In this section, hydrophilic gilsonite at two concentrations and Fe3O4 were used to investigate the effect of nanoparticles on aphron-containing solutions (Figs. 12 and 13). As presented in Fig. 14, at lower gilsonite concentrations, the particles were more stable and smaller in size. Also, Fig. 14 shows that the average particle diameter was smaller when lower concentrations of gilsonite were used. Proper concentration of nanoparticles and achieving optimal concentration of nanoparticles can significantly improve aphron stability. However, increasing the concentration by crossing critical values ​​caused a decrease in stability and an increase in the average particle diameter (Fig. 14). At the second step, Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles were introduced to ascertain the impacts of different nanoparticles on bubble structure. Since the average bubble diameter, in the case of mostly Fe₃O₄ in a range of 100–200 micrometers, is somewhat bigger compared to systems without nanoparticles, such growth can be attributed to augmented structural strength and resistance against bubble coalescence (Fig. 13and Fig. 15). These characteristics suggest that Fe₃O₄ plays a critical role to maintain long-term stability and robustness of the structure of the aphrons. It should be emphasized, however, that a thorough evaluation of long-term stability and dynamic behavior was not the objective of the current investigation and should be investigated further45,46.

IFT changes

These two main techniques for mobilizing residual oil include driving force enhancement (pressure drop) or reduction of interfacial tension (IFT). Injection of a surfactant is mentioned as an effective technique in traditional CEOR processes due to its high capability to reduce IFT. Depending on the crude oil polarity (according to resin and asphaltene content), the interaction between the surfactant and the crude oil can result in the development of natural soaps, which also contribute to the decrease in IFT. Injected surfactants can work more effectively and synergistically when there are such compounds present. Overall, effective contact angle and IFT reduction—guided by soap production and crude oil polarity—are primary factors in wettability alteration at high temperatures. Interdependent effects enhance oil recovery performance in chemical EOR processes. It is clear from the results that LSW–AOS (1000 ppm)–NaOH (100 ppm) solution injection can increase oil recovery by 42.24% and 71.67% for low- and high-polarity crude oil samples, respectively47–49. The indigenous IFT between crude oil and deionized water, without the introduction of any additives (surfactants or salt), was established at 120.5 dynes/cm. This was established as a reference for determination of the effect of various additives on IFT in subsequent experiments. When only the base fluid blended with polymer (Xanthan Gum) and surfactant (CTAB) was used, the IFT went down to 90 dynes/cm, reflecting the initial effectiveness of these components in reducing interfacial tension. From Fig. 16 IFT diagram in the presence of different salts with varying concentrations, it can be observed that adding salts to the solution significantly reduces the IFT (Interfacial Tension). Salts can alter the distribution of natural surfactants (including asphaltenes and resins) at the interface due to electrostatic forces, and as a result, they can lead to changes in IFT13. The greatest reduction in IFT occurred when a lower concentration of the divalent salt MgCl2 was used (39.02 dynes/cm). In fact, the polar organic components of natural surfactants can react with divalent ions from MgCl2 to form complex ions, which can easily dissolve in the aqueous phase and reduce IFT. The reduction in IFT was less when NaCl salt was used. This reduction could be due to the relatively weak synergistic effect of the monovalent NaCl and natural surfactant in crude oil16. Compared to MgCl2, NaCl has a lesser impact on resins and asphaltenes, which is why it results in a smaller reduction in IFT. Divalent ions are more efficient than monovalent ions in directing surfactant molecules to the water-oil interface17. Additionally, at lower concentrations, both salts showed better effects in reducing IFT. A concentration of 5000 ppm was considered the optimal concentration for reducing IFT. On the other hand, at a concentration of 50,000 ppm, due to the increased salinity, there were no significant changes in reducing IFT.

CA changes

Oil, with a significant impact on bubble stability, plays a key role in foam performance, which can create stability through the mechanism of dispersion and penetration at the air-water interface. Using oils that contain compounds with a relatively lighter phase results in a reduction of foam stability. A series of foam flow tests showed that foams generated in the presence of heavy oils in porous environments are more effective and have higher stability11,12. The selection of heavy oil as a stabilizing agent for creating stable foam was carried out in this part of the study13. Along with adding oil to polymer solutions, changes in interfacial tension and contact angle in the presence of different salts were considered. One of the factors affecting the stability of foams with particles is the wetting of particles against the air/fluid surfaces, which is controlled by the contact angle (θ). The contact angle is a determining factor in the amount of energy absorbed by the particles. Adding salts to the solution containing oil was done with the aim of improving bubble stabilization and increasing the accuracy of contact angle measurements. As with the solutions under study, aphron stability in the presence of oil and salt increased with the increase in salt concentration. It was observed from Fig. 17 that the contact angle changed with the addition of different salts to the oil-containing solution. This change is due to the differences in the ionic capacities of the salts. The maximum contact angle (77.06°) was observed at a concentration of 50,000 ppm of MgCl2 salt. The relatively high contact angle in the presence of oil can reduce aphron concentration and result in fewer bubbles. In contrast to the significant changes seen with MgCl2 at two concentration levels, adding NaCl at a higher concentration did not cause a significant increase in the contact angle compared to the lower concentration. An increase in the contact angle can also lead to an increase in particle flocculation in the solution. Oil-based aphrons with lower flocculation degrees have a better and more stable structure14,15. The lowest contact angle (20.58°) was observed in the solution containing MgCl2 salt at a concentration of 5000 ppm. Overall, the effect of salt in the presence of oil at concentrations below the critical micelle concentration can be considered as a stabilizing agent that enhances stability and reduces the surface tension of aphron in the presence of oil.

The findings of this study are valuable guidelines for aphron formulation design to be applied in improved oil recovery operations, e.g., selection and amount of salts to obtain best stability and surface characteristics. The demonstrated benefit of the utilization of potassium chloride (KCl) in aphron stabilization along with the synergetic behavior of nanoparticles and gilsonite reveals promising routes to field-scale applications where bubble size control and stability directly affect the efficiency of oil displacement. Moreover, the strong influence of magnesium chloride (MgCl₂) on wettability alteration and interfacial tension reduction also suggests that it can be used in designing targeted chemical injection programs for reservoirs of unusual rock-fluid interactions.

Future work

While this study was conducted under controlled laboratory conditions at ambient pressure and temperature, placing the findings within the context of actual reservoir conditions is relevant to evaluating the applicability of the findings for generalization. Elevated temperatures in oil reservoirs will affect interfacial behavior, colloidal structure stability, and dynamic multiphase system behavior. Besides, reservoir pressure, particularly at and near the minimum miscibility pressure (MMP), is very important in controlling mass transfer mechanisms, changes in wettability, and phase behavior. According to the literature, high pressure and temperature can reduce interfacial tension, raise the stability of multiphase systems, and improve the efficacy of chemical injection procedures43,50. Therefore, while the current data were obtained under ambient conditions, the nature of the mechanisms being examined suggests that one should be able to see similar trends under actual reservoir conditions, although the magnitude and intensity of such effects may be a function of thermodynamic parameters. This calls for additional studies under HPHT conditions to confirm the current findings.

In subsequent research, we plan to study surfactant solution interfacial tension (IFT) reaction as a function of added potassium chloride (KCl) at a more refined level to further clarify the effect of salinity on surfactant performance under reservoir-relevant conditions. The aim of the research is to customize surfactant formulations that are prepared for application in enhanced oil recovery (EOR) processes in saline reservoirs in an effort to maximize overall process efficiency.

In addition, intensive comparative investigation on anionic surfactants and biosurfactants will be conducted to contrast their wettability alteration, aphron formation, and incremental oil recovery efficiencies. Environmentally friendly biosurfactants with unique molecular structures hold a lot of promise for being a next-generation agent that can provide sustainable and effective solutions against conventional chemical surfactants.

In addition, systematic stability tests of aphron will be carried out to evaluate its temporal stability and performance at dynamic flow conditions. These will include extensive drainage tests to quantify the rates of aphron collapse and lifetime, of prime significance in scaling up laboratory results to field-scale applications.

Collectively, these research fields of the future will enhance our mechanistic understanding and practical know-how in utilizing surfactant-stimulated EOR techniques to ultimately achieve more efficient and sustainable hydrocarbon production.
