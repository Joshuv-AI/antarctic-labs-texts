Bifidobacterium enhances the antitumor efficacy of carboplatin in glioblastoma cells: targeting apoptotic and cell cycle regulatory pathways via Caspase, AKT/PTEN, and P53/P21 signaling
Authors: Abbas Asoudeh-Fard, Moein Yeylagh-Beygi, Mohadeseh Asoudeh-Fard, Mohammad Abbasi, Mohammad Bagher Nazari, Ahmad Gholami, Asghar Parsaei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlioblastoma multiforme (GBM) is an aggressive brain tumor with poor response to chemotherapy such as Carboplatin, mainly due to drug resistance and systemic toxicity. Recent studies suggest that probiotics like Bifidobacterium reuteri may have anti-tumor properties and enhance the efficacy of chemotherapy. This study evaluates the potential of B. reuteri Ab.338 SH (GenBank: PV961363), isolated from traditional yogurt, in potentiating Carboplatin’s effects on U-87 MG glioblastoma cells.MethodsB. reuteri Ab.338 SH was isolated and molecularly characterized. U-87 MG cells were treated with Carboplatin, B. reuteri, and their combination. Cell viability was evaluated by MTT assay, apoptosis by Annexin V-FITC/PI staining and flow cytometry, and gene expression of apoptosis-related markers by qRT-PCR. Selectivity was confirmed using HUVEC cells as a normal control.ResultsT treatment markedly reduced the effective dose of Carboplatin and enhanced apoptosis compared with either treatment alone. Flow cytometry showed increased late apoptotic cells with co-treatment. qRT-PCR analysis revealed upregulation of pro-apoptotic genes (he combinationBAX, Caspase-3/8/9, Fas, PTEN, IκB, P53, P21) and downregulation of AKT, mTOR, and Bcl-2. B. reuteri alone showed no cytotoxicity toward normal endothelial cells.ConclusionB. reuteri Ab.338 SH enhances Carboplatin efficacy in GBM cells while lowering its required dose and potential toxicity. This probiotic may serve as a promising adjuvant in GBM therapy, warranting further in vivo validation.Graphical abstract

Introduction

Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) is recognized as the most aggressive and lethal primary brain tumor in adults, characterized by rapid proliferation, diffuse infiltration, and resistance to conventional therapies [1]. Despite advances in surgical resection, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy, the prognosis for patients with GBM remains dismal, with median survival not exceeding 15 months [2]. One of the main challenges in GBM management is the tumor’s intrinsic and acquired resistance to chemotherapeutic agents, which is often mediated through evasion of apoptosis, dysregulated cell cycle checkpoints, and activation of pro-survival signaling pathways [3]. Carboplatin, a second-generation platinum-based chemotherapeutic agent, exerts its cytotoxic effects primarily through the formation of DNA cross-links, leading to cell cycle arrest and apoptosis [4, 5]. Although carboplatin is less nephrotoxic than cisplatin and is widely used in the treatment of various solid tumors, including gliomas, its effectiveness in GBM is limited due to suboptimal tumor penetration and cellular resistance mechanisms [6, 7]. Consequently, there is an urgent need to identify adjuvant strategies capable of enhancing its antitumor efficacy while minimizing toxicity to normal tissues. In recent years, growing attention has been directed toward the use of probiotic bacteria as complementary agents in cancer therapy [8, 9]. Among these, species belonging to the genus Bifidobacterium, a group of anaerobic, Gram-positive bacteria naturally residing in the human gut and commonly found in fermented dairy products, have shown promising anticancer potential [10]. These microorganisms are known to modulate the host immune system, reduce systemic inflammation, and interfere with cancer cell signaling [11]. Furthermore, certain strains of Bifidobacterium have demonstrated the capacity to sensitize tumor cells to chemotherapeutic agents through the induction of apoptosis and suppression of survival pathways [12]. In the present study, we isolated Bifidobacterium strains from 60 samples of traditional yogurt collected from local producers in Fars Province, Iran, with the aim of evaluating their synergistic potential with carboplatin in the treatment of glioblastoma. To elucidate the underlying mechanisms, we focused on key molecular regulators of apoptosis and cell cycle progression. Specifically, we analyzed the expression of CASP3, CASP8, and CASP9 (caspase-3, −8, and − 9), which orchestrate the execution of apoptosis via both intrinsic (mitochondrial) and extrinsic (death receptor) pathways; AKT and PTEN, which serve as antagonistic modulators of the PI3K/AKT pathway that governs cell survival; and TP53 (P53) and CDKN1A (P21), central players in the DNA damage response and cell cycle arrest [13–15]. In addition, we investigated the expression of BAX and BCL-2, key pro- and anti-apoptotic members of the Bcl-2 family whose relative balance dictates mitochondrial membrane permeabilization and apoptotic commitment; mTOR (mechanistic target of rapamycin), a central regulator of cell growth and proliferation frequently hyperactivated in GBM; FAS (CD95), a cell surface receptor initiating extrinsic apoptosis; and IκB (inhibitor of NF-κB), which modulates inflammatory and survival responses through the NF-κB signaling cascade. By evaluating these molecular markers, our study aims to determine whether Bifidobacterium can enhance carboplatin-mediated cytotoxicity in glioblastoma cells, thereby offering a novel combinatorial strategy to overcome treatment resistance and improve therapeutic outcomes in GBM. Specifically, we investigated the anti-cancer potential of Bifidobacterium reuteri Ab.338 SH (PV961363), a strain isolated from traditional yogurt samples in Fars Province, Iran, in combination with carboplatin on glioblastoma cell lines. Through MTT assays, flow cytometry, and quantitative real-time PCR analysis of key genes involved in apoptosis (CASP3, CASP8, CASP9, BAX, BCL2, FAS), survival (AKT, PTEN, mTOR), and cell cycle regulation (P53, P21, IκB), we aimed to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the synergistic interaction between B. reuteri and carboplatin.

In this study, we aimed to investigate the potential anti-cancer effects of Bifidobacterium reuteri Ab.338 SH (PV961363), a strain isolated from traditional yogurt samples in Fars Province, in combination with the chemotherapeutic agent carboplatin on glioblastoma cell lines. Given the emerging evidence supporting the role of probiotics in modulating apoptosis and enhancing chemotherapy sensitivity, we hypothesized that this strain may potentiate carboplatin-induced cytotoxicity by influencing key molecular pathways related to cell death and survival. Using MTT assays to assess cell viability and flow cytometry for apoptosis and cell cycle analysis, we examined the combinatorial effects of B. reuteri and carboplatin on glioblastoma cell proliferation. In addition, quantitative real-time PCR was employed to evaluate the expression of crucial genes involved in apoptotic signaling, cell cycle regulation, and inflammatory responses, including PTEN/AKT, BAX/Bcl-2, Caspase-3, −8, and − 9, Fas, mTOR, P53, P21, and IκB. Our findings aim to provide mechanistic insights into how B. reuteri can enhance the chemotherapeutic efficacy of carboplatin and potentially serve as an adjuvant strategy to improve treatment outcomes in glioblastoma.

Materials & methods

Materials

The Glioblastoma (GBM) cell line U-87 MG (ATCC® HTB-14™) and normal endothelial cells derived from human umbilical vein HUVEC (ATCC® CRL-1730™) were procured from the Pasteur Institute of Iran. All cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Gibco, UK; Cat. No. 31600-083), enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, UK; Cat. No. 10270106) and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin solution (Gibco, UK; Cat. No. 15140122). Cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ incubator. Cells were passaged upon reaching ~ 80% confluency using 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA (Gibco, UK; Cat. No. 25200056). For viability assessment, the MTT reagent (Sigma-Aldrich, UK; Cat. No. M5655) was used. Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol® reagent (Invitrogen, USA; Cat. No. 15596026), and cDNA synthesis was performed utilizing the iScript™ cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad, USA; Cat. No. 1708891). Taq DNA Polymerase Master Mix RED amplicon (Ampliqon, Denmark; Cat. A180301) Quantitative PCR reactions were carried out with SYBR® Green-based master mix (Ampliqon, Denmark; Cat. No. A322701). To evaluate apoptosis, the Annexin V-FITC/PI detection kit (eBioscience, Austria; Cat. No. 88–8005-74) was employed, and samples were analyzed using a FACSVerse™ flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA).

Isolation and molecular characterization of bifidobacterium spp

To isolate Bifidobacterium spp., a total of 60 traditional yogurt samples were aseptically collected from various rural and urban areas of Fars Province, Iran. Samples were transported to the laboratory under refrigerated conditions and processed within 4 h. Each sample (10 g) was homogenized in 90 mL of sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.2) and subjected to serial 10-fold dilutions. Aliquots from appropriate dilutions were plated onto modified de Man, Rogosa and Sharpe (MRS) agar supplemented with 0.05% (w/v) L-cysteine hydrochloride to promote anaerobic growth of Bifidobacterium spp. Plates were incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 48–72 h using an anaerobic jar system equipped with gas-generating sachets (AnaeroGen™, Oxoid, UK). Colonies exhibiting typical Bifidobacterium-like morphology (creamy-white, convex, and smooth) were subcultured onto fresh MRS-cysteine agar to ensure purity. Isolates were then grown in MRS-cysteine broth and subjected to preliminary phenotypic characterization, including Gram staining, catalase and oxidase tests, and motility assessment. Gram-positive, non-motile, catalase- and oxidase-negative bacilli were presumptively identified as Bifidobacterium spp. Moreover, the isolate designated as B. reuteri Ab.338 SH exhibited typical probiotic-associated characteristics, consistent with previous reports on B. reuteri species, including tolerance to acidic and bile conditions, and bile salt hydrolase activity as described in related studies [16]. Although only preliminary biochemical characterization was conducted in the current work, comprehensive probiotic profiling encompassing acid and bile tolerance, hydrophobicity, antioxidant, and β-galactosidase activities is underway and will be presented in a forthcoming study. Two morphologically distinct isolates were randomly selected for further molecular identification and functional assays [17].

Molecular identification of the isolates

Genomic DNA was extracted from the isolated Bifidobacterium strains using the phenol-chloroform extraction method. The quality and concentration of the extracted DNA were evaluated by measuring the A260/A280 absorbance ratio using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Genus-specific PCR amplification targeting the 16 S rRNA gene was performed using Bifidobacterium-specific primers (primer sequences listed in Table 1). PCR reactions were carried out in an Eppendorf Mastercycler Gradient thermal cycler (Eppendorf AG, Hamburg, Germany) under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min; 30 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 75 s, annealing at 54 °C for 60 s, and extension at 72 °C for 60 s; followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. The amplified products were purified and subjected to Sanger sequencing. The obtained sequences were analyzed using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) against the NCBI GenBank nucleotide database for species-level confirmation. The verified sequences were deposited in the NCBI GenBank database. The novel strain was registered as Bifidobacterium reuteri Ab.338 SH (NCBI accession number PV961363), ensuring robust molecular characterization and precise taxonomic identification. Handling of this strain was performed under Biosafety Level 1 (BSL-1) conditions, following institutional and national biosafety guidelines for non-pathogenic probiotic bacteria, with all procedures involving live cultures conducted in a certified biosafety cabinet. Cell culture

The human glioblastoma cell line U-87 MG and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were obtained from the Pasteur Institute of Iran (National Cell Bank). Both cell lines were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂ and subcultured upon reaching ~ 80% confluency using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA. Prior to experimentation, both U-87 MG and HUVEC cell lines were routinely screened for Mycoplasma contamination using a PCR-based MycoAlert™ detection kit (Lonza, USA), and only mycoplasma-free cultures were utilized for subsequent assays to ensure data reliability. Prior to treatments, cells were seeded at optimized densities and allowed to adhere and reach the desired confluency. U-87 MG cells were exposed to B. reuteri and carboplatin to evaluate synergistic cytotoxic effects, whereas HUVECs were used solely to assess the safety profile of B. reuteri by evaluating the absence of cytotoxicity on normal endothelial cells.

Cell viability assay

To evaluate cytotoxicity, the MTT assay was employed to assess the individual and combined effects of Bifidobacterium reuteri Ab.338 SH (PV961363) and Carboplatin on U-87 MG glioblastoma cells. In this assay, live B. reuteri cultures were introduced into a co-culture system using Transwell® inserts (pore size 0.4 μm), enabling metabolite exchange without direct bacterial-cell contact. These inserts were positioned in 96-well plates seeded with U-87 MG cells to facilitate indirect interactions mediated by bacterial secretions. Cells were initially plated at optimal densities and incubated overnight to ensure adherence prior to treatment. Experimental groups included exposure to increasing concentrations of Carboplatin (16.5, 18.5, 20.5, and 22.5 µg/mL) (Actoverco, Iran), as well as live B. reuteri at various optical densities (OD₆₀₀ = 0.8, 1, 1.25, and 1.5), each applied at a final volume of 10 µL/mL. Additionally, to assess potential synergistic effects, combination treatments were prepared using sub-cytotoxic concentrations of both agents based on previously determined IC₅₀ values, specifically (128 ng/mL + 1.0 OD, 138 ng/mL + 1.0 OD, 148 ng/mL + 1.0 OD, and 158 ng/mL + 1.0 OD) for U-87 MG cells. All treatment conditions were applied for a duration of 24 h. Following incubation, 20 µL of MTT reagent (5 mg/mL in PBS) was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 4 h to allow for formazan crystal formation by metabolically active cells. Crystals were then solubilized using 200 µL of Isopropanol, and absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a BioTek ELx808 microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, USA). Cell viability was expressed relative to untreated control wells. To evaluate cytocompatibility and rule out off-target toxicity, a parallel experiment was performed using human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs). In this assay, HUVECs were cultured under identical conditions and exposed solely to B. reuteri at the same OD₆₀₀ values (0.8, 1, 1.25, and 1.5; 10 µL/mL) using the same Transwell® co-culture configuration. Neither carboplatin nor combination treatments were administered to HUVECs. This design allowed for specific assessment of B. reuteri-induced effects on normal cells, confirming its selective cytotoxicity toward tumor cells without notable detrimental impact on healthy endothelial cell viability [19].

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis

After determining the IC₅₀ values for U-87 MG cells, the cells were seeded into 6-well plates and treated for 24 h under one of the following conditions: (1) Carboplatin alone (20.5 µg/mL), (2) Bifidobacterium reuteri Ab.338 SH (OD₆₀₀ = 0.1), or (3) a combination of carboplatin and B. reuteri (148 ng/mL + 1.0 OD). To enable interaction via bacterial metabolites while preventing direct contact and overgrowth, treatments were applied using Transwell® inserts with 0.4 μm pore size membranes, separating bacterial suspensions from the glioblastoma cells. Following treatment, total RNA was extracted from each group using TRIzol® reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, including chloroform phase separation. The concentration and purity of RNA samples were assessed using a NanoDrop™ 1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), ensuring A260/A280 ratios between 1.8 and 2.0. Complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis was performed from 1 µg of total RNA using the iScript™ cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad, USA). Quantitative real-time PCR was carried out on a Bio-Rad iQ5 real-time PCR detection system (Bio-Rad, USA) using SYBR® Green Master Mix (Ampliqon, Denmark). Gene-specific primers targeting key apoptotic and survival-related genes including PTEN, AKT, BAX, BCL-2, CASP3, CASP8, CASP9, P53, P21, FAS, mTOR, and IκB were used, with GAPDH serving as the endogenous housekeeping control (see Table 1 for primer sequences). Thermal cycling conditions were optimized per gene, and melting curve analyses were performed to confirm amplification specificity. Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method, and statistical comparisons among treatment groups were conducted to elucidate differential gene expression patterns associated with apoptotic induction and survival pathway modulation following treatments [20].

Flow cytometry analysis

For flow cytometric assessment of apoptosis and cell cycle distribution, U-87 MG cells were treated for 24 h with a combination of Carboplatin and Bifidobacterium reuteri Ab.338 SH at previously optimized concentrations. Following treatment, cells were harvested by trypsinization, washed twice with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and resuspended in 100 µL of 1X binding buffer. Apoptotic staining was performed using the Annexin V-FITC/Propidium Iodide (PI) Apoptosis Detection Kit (eBioscience, Austria) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, cells were incubated with Annexin V-FITC and PI for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Stained cells were immediately analyzed using a BD FACSVerse™ flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA), and data acquisition was performed using FlowJo software (Tree Star Inc., Ashland, OR, USA). The proportions of viable (Annexin V⁻/PI⁻), early apoptotic (Annexin V⁺/PI⁻), late apoptotic (Annexin V⁺/PI⁺), and necrotic (Annexin V⁻/PI⁺) cells were quantified. Comparative analysis was conducted to evaluate the synergistic apoptotic effects of the combined treatment relative to untreated controls and single-agent treatments [21].

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate and data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, confirming Gaussian distribution for all datasets (p > 0.05). Differences among experimental groups were evaluated by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons where appropriate. For comparisons between two groups, unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was applied. Each experimental condition included three independent biological replicates performed on separate days using different cell passages, with each replicate measured in triplicate. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Levels of significance were reported as: p < 0.05, p < 0.01, and p < 0.001. Statistical analyses and graphical data visualization were performed using SPSS Statistics version 24 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Cell proliferation

All experiments were performed in three independent biological replicates, and results are expressed as mean ± SD. The cytotoxic potential of Bifidobacterium reuteri Ab.338 SH was evaluated in U-87 MG glioblastoma and HUVEC endothelial cells using MTT-based viability assays. Cells were exposed to escalating concentrations of the bacterial suspension (OD₆₀₀ = 0.8, 1.0, 1.25, and 1.5; 10 µL/mL). Quantitative analysis demonstrated a dose-dependent reduction in U-87 MG cell viability, declining from 57.45 ± 1.36% at OD₆₀₀ = 0.8 to 38.44 ± 1.25% at OD₆₀₀ = 1.5. Notably, the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) for B. reuteri in U-87 MG cells was approximately OD₆₀₀ = 1.0, corresponding to 51.05 ± 2.33% viability (Figure.1). In contrast, HUVEC cells exhibited minimal sensitivity, maintaining viability levels above 98% at all doses tested (from 99.70 ± 1.21% at OD₆₀₀ = 0.8 to 98.98 ± 1.05% at OD₆₀₀ = 1.5), confirming the selective cytotoxic effect of the probiotic strain toward glioblastoma cells (Figure.2). To assess baseline sensitivity to chemotherapy, U-87 MG cells were treated with Carboplatin across a concentration range of 16.5–22.5 µg/mL. A progressive decline in viability was observed, with values decreasing from 70.83 ± 1.64% at 16.5 µg/mL to 46.84 ± 2.13% at 22.5 µg/mL. The IC₅₀ for Carboplatin in U-87 MG cells was determined to be approximately 20.5 µg/mL, where cell viability dropped to nearly 50% (50.39 ± 1.39%) (Figure. 3). To explore potential synergistic effects, a co-treatment strategy was applied using a fixed concentration of B. reuteri (OD₆₀₀ = 1.0; 10 µL/mL) along with subtherapeutic doses of Carboplatin (128, 138, 148, and 158 ng/mL). Co-treatment led to an enhanced cytotoxic effect, with U-87 MG viability reduced from 70.04 ± 1.14% at 128 ng/mL to 48.18 ± 1.53% at 158 ng/mL in the presence of the probiotic. The effective IC₅₀ under combined conditions was reached at 148 ng/mL of Carboplatin + OD₆₀₀ = 1.0 of B. reuteri, where cell viability was reduced to 50.76 ± 1.23% (Figure. 4). IC₅₀ values for both carboplatin and B. reuteri Ab.338 SH were calculated using nonlinear regression analysis of dose–response curves derived from triplicate MTT assays. Statistical analyses and curve fitting were performed using SPSS Statistics version 24 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Gene expression profiling by quantitative real-time PCR

All experiments were performed in three independent biological replicates, and results are expressed as mean ± SD. To uncover the molecular mechanisms underlying the cytotoxic and pro-apoptotic effects of Bifidobacterium reuteri Ab.338 SH, carboplatin, and their combination in glioblastoma, a comprehensive gene expression profiling study was conducted using quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR). U-87 MG cells were exposed for 24 h to each treatment at their respective IC₅₀ concentrations: carboplatin (20.5 µg/mL), B. reuteri (OD₆₀₀ = 0.1), and the combination of carboplatin (148 ng/mL) with B. reuteri (OD₆₀₀ = 1.0). Total RNA was extracted, and the expression levels of critical genes involved in intrinsic (mitochondrial), extrinsic (death receptor-mediated), and PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathways were quantified. Monotherapy with B. reuteri alone induced substantial upregulation of pro-apoptotic genes, including Caspase-3 (3.43-fold), Caspase-8 (3.42-fold), and Caspase-9 (3.67-fold), reflecting activation of both extrinsic and intrinsic apoptotic pathways. Expression of Bax (3.26-fold), PTEN (3.13-fold), P53 (2.14-fold), and P21 (1.95-fold) was also increased, indicating mitochondrial pathway activation and cell cycle arrest. Simultaneously, a downregulation of survival-associated genes was observed, including AKT (0.62-fold), mTOR (0.72-fold), IκB (0.64-fold), and Bcl-2 (0.53-fold). A modest increase in Fas (1.28-fold) suggested partial engagement of the death receptor signaling route. Carboplatin monotherapy similarly upregulated apoptotic mediators. The expression of Caspase-3 (3.21-fold), Caspase-8 (4.17-fold), and Caspase-9 (2.86-fold) confirmed dual-pathway activation, while Bax (3.17-fold), PTEN (2.26-fold), P53 (2.39-fold), and P21 (3.42-fold) indicated activation of the mitochondrial apoptotic cascade and checkpoint regulation. Downregulation of pro-survival and anti-apoptotic genes included AKT (0.61-fold), mTOR (0.54-fold), IκB (0.65-fold), and Bcl-2 (0.42-fold), while Fas expression increased to 1.81-fold, suggesting a more prominent extrinsic apoptotic response compared to probiotic treatment alone. Strikingly, the combination of B. reuteri and low-dose carboplatin induced the most robust transcriptional modulation among all groups. Apoptotic gene expression was markedly enhanced, with Caspase-3 (5.83-fold), Caspase-8 (4.97-fold), and Caspase-9 (4.89-fold) showing the highest induction. Mitochondrial and tumor suppressor genes such as Bax (4.59-fold), PTEN (6.45-fold), P53 (3.17-fold), and P21 (4.67-fold) were also significantly upregulated, indicating synergistic enhancement of mitochondrial apoptotic signaling and cell cycle inhibition. Discussion

The present study provides compelling evidence supporting the synergistic antitumor efficacy of Bifidobacterium reuteri Ab.338 SH, a probiotic strain isolated from traditional yogurt in Fars Province, in combination with Carboplatin against human glioblastoma U-87 MG cells. Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) remains one of the most aggressive and treatment-refractory malignancies, necessitating novel combinatorial strategies to overcome therapeutic resistance and reduce chemotherapeutic toxicity [22, 23]. Our findings highlight the potential of probiotic-assisted chemotherapy as a promising approach to address these challenges. It is important to note that B. reuteri Ab.338 SH shares several hallmark probiotic features with previously characterized B. reuteri strains, including acid and bile tolerance, bile salt hydrolase activity, antioxidant potential, and strong surface hydrophobicity, which facilitate gastrointestinal survival and epithelial adhesion [13, 16]. Although comprehensive probiotic assays were beyond the scope of the current anticancer-focused investigation, these well-documented properties support the probiotic classification of the strain used in this study and justify its selection for combination therapy. Through a series of in vitro assays, we demonstrated that B. reuteri Ab.338 SH significantly enhances the cytotoxic effects of carboplatin in glioblastoma cells. Notably, while monotherapy with carboplatin required a concentration of 20.5 µg/mL to achieve 50% growth inhibition (IC₅₀), the addition of B. reuteri enabled a comparable cytotoxic effect at a markedly reduced carboplatin dose of 148 ng/mL representing an approximate 99.3% reduction in the required drug dosage (Fig. 4). This substantial decrease not only preserves therapeutic efficacy but also holds critical clinical implications by potentially minimizing systemic toxicity and adverse side effects commonly associated with platinum-based chemotherapeutics. The MTT-based viability assays revealed a dose-dependent cytotoxic effect of B. reuteri against U-87 MG cells, with minimal impact on normal HUVEC endothelial cells. Even at the highest bacterial density tested (OD₆₀₀ = 1.5), HUVEC viability remained above 98%, underscoring the selective cytotoxicity of the probiotic strain toward malignant cells while sparing healthy tissue (Figs. 1 and 2). This safety profile is of paramount importance in the context of GBM therapy, where protecting normal brain vasculature and minimizing systemic toxicity are key considerations.

Mechanistically, the combinatorial treatment resulted in robust activation of apoptotic signaling pathways, as confirmed by quantitative real-time PCR and flow cytometric analyses. Gene expression profiling showed marked upregulation of intrinsic and extrinsic apoptotic markers including Caspase-3, −8, −9, Bax, PTEN, P53, and P21 following the combination treatment, indicating enhanced induction of programmed cell death (Fig. 5). Conversely, key pro-survival genes such as AKT, mTOR, and Bcl-2 were significantly downregulated, suggesting suppression of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis, a pathway commonly hyperactivated in GBM and associated with treatment resistance. Interestingly, Fas expression reached its highest level in the combination group, implicating an intensified engagement of the death receptor-mediated extrinsic apoptotic pathway. The pronounced increase in PTEN (6.45-fold) further supports disruption of oncogenic AKT signaling and reinforces the role of tumor suppressors in mediating the synergistic response. Flow cytometric analysis corroborated these molecular findings, revealing a significant increase in late apoptotic cell populations (Annexin V⁺/PI⁺) in glioblastoma cells treated with the combination of carboplatin and B. reuteri (Fig. 7 and quantitative summary in Fig. 8). The lack of increased necrotic cell populations further indicates that apoptosis, not necrosis, is the predominant mode of cell death induced by this treatment, a favorable outcome that may reduce pro-inflammatory responses and damage to surrounding tissues in vivo. While our proposed mechanism (Fig. 6) is supported by mRNA expression data, we acknowledge that protein-level confirmation (e.g., Western blot or ELISA) was not performed in this study. Therefore, the mechanistic claims remain preliminary, and future studies are warranted to validate these molecular changes at the protein and functional levels. Our results align with recent studies demonstrating probiotic strains’ ability to modulate tumor microenvironment and sensitize cancer cells to chemotherapy. For instance, recent work by Zhang et al. highlighted the role of Lactobacillus species in enhancing temozolomide efficacy against glioma through apoptosis induction and oxidative stress modulation [24]. Similarly, Wang et al. reported that Bifidobacterium metabolites can downregulate PI3K/AKT signaling in glioma models, corroborating our observations of pathway inhibition [25, 26]. These emerging findings collectively underscore the translational potential of probiotics as adjuncts in GBM therapy [27]. Collectively, these results suggest that B. reuteri Ab.338 SH acts not merely as a passive adjunct but as a biologically active modulator of tumor cell fate. In the present study, B. reuteri Ab.338 SH was applied in a non-contact co-culture system using transwell inserts, ensuring that only diffusible bacterial metabolites and soluble cell-associated molecules could interact with glioblastoma cells. This experimental design minimizes direct bacterial–cell contact and indicates that the observed enhancement of carboplatin efficacy is predominantly mediated by secreted bioactive compounds rather than whole bacterial cells. In the present study, while we did not directly quantify specific metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), exopolysaccharides, or bioactive peptides, previous research indicates that Bifidobacterium strains produce these compounds, which are capable of modulating apoptosis, oxidative stress, and signaling pathways in cancer cells. Therefore, it is plausible that these secreted bacterial factors contribute to the observed enhancement of carboplatin cytotoxicity. We acknowledge that future studies including direct metabolite profiling will be essential to firmly establish the mechanistic link between bacterial metabolites and the potentiation of chemotherapy effects. Such extracellular factors, including short-chain fatty acids, exopolysaccharides, and small peptides, have been shown in prior studies to exert cytotoxic and immunomodulatory effects on cancer cells while maintaining host cell safety [28, 29]. Future work will focus on isolating and characterizing these active metabolites to elucidate their precise molecular mechanisms in modulating chemotherapy response. While the observed transcriptional modulations clearly indicate activation of apoptotic and tumor-suppressive pathways, the interpretation of fold-change values was performed with caution. Only genes exhibiting ≥ 2-fold changes with consistent reproducibility across triplicate experiments were considered biologically significant. Moderate alterations (1.5–2.0 fold) were interpreted in the context of their role within interconnected pathways rather than as independent indicators of regulation. It should also be noted that gene expression data represent transcriptional trends and may not fully reflect protein-level activation. While the Transwell co-culture approach effectively restricts physical interaction and allows only diffusible bacterial products to influence glioblastoma cells, it remains to be determined whether the observed effects stem primarily from live bacteria or their secreted metabolites. Future studies incorporating heat-killed B. reuteri and cell-free conditioned medium controls will be critical to delineate these mechanisms and confirm whether the enhancement of carboplatin efficacy depends on bacterial viability or on soluble bioactive factors.

Through secretion of bioactive metabolites and immunomodulatory molecules delivered via a non-contact co-culture system the probiotic appears to sensitize glioblastoma cells to low-dose carboplatin, facilitating apoptosis through both mitochondrial and receptor-mediated mechanisms [30, 31]. This dual-pathway engagement enhances therapeutic depth and may limit the likelihood of resistance development.

It is important to note that the carboplatin concentrations used in this study (20.5 µg/mL for monotherapy and 148 ng/mL in combination with B. reuteri) were derived from in vitro IC₅₀ determinations and therefore may not directly reflect clinically achievable plasma levels in GBM patients. Nonetheless, the pronounced reduction in the effective carboplatin dose observed under co-treatment conditions highlights a strong sensitization effect mediated by the probiotic. This synergistic interaction suggests that probiotic-assisted chemotherapy could potentially allow for lower systemic drug exposure while maintaining comparable therapeutic efficacy, a hypothesis that should be further evaluated in in vivo pharmacokinetic and preclinical efficacy studies.

From a translational perspective, the tenfold reduction in effective carboplatin dosage observed in the combination therapy could significantly lower the risk of nephrotoxicity, myelosuppression, and other systemic complications commonly seen in GBM patients undergoing platinum-based chemotherapy [32, 33]. Furthermore, the excellent safety profile of B. reuteri in HUVEC cells reinforces its potential for clinical application, especially in adjuvant or maintenance settings where long-term administration is envisioned.

In a physiological context, B. reuteri and its metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids, exopolysaccharides, and bioactive peptides are known to act systemically after intestinal absorption. These compounds may indirectly influence the central nervous system by modulating systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and cytokine balance, which are closely linked to blood–brain barrier (BBB) integrity. Although the current study was conducted in vitro, previous reports have demonstrated that certain Bifidobacterium-derived metabolites can cross or regulate the BBB through gut–brain axis signaling. Thus, it is plausible that the observed sensitization effects on glioblastoma cells may also be relevant in vivo, mediated via circulating bacterial metabolites that affect brain microenvironment and endothelial function.

In conclusion, our study highlights the therapeutic potential of integrating probiotic-based interventions with conventional chemotherapy in GBM treatment. The observed synergistic effects, coupled with selective cytotoxicity and minimal impact on healthy cells, provide a strong rationale for further preclinical and clinical evaluation of Bifidobacterium reuteri Ab.338 SH as an adjuvant strategy to potentiate carboplatin efficacy. This novel approach may ultimately contribute to the development of safer, more effective, and personalized treatments for patients suffering from this devastating malignancy.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates, for the first time, the potent synergistic anticancer activity of Bifidobacterium reuteri Ab.338 SH in combination with Carboplatin against human glioblastoma U-87 MG cells. Through a multifaceted in vitro approach incorporating cell viability assays, gene expression profiling, and apoptosis analysis, we show that the probiotic strain significantly enhances the cytotoxic effects of Carboplatin while exerting minimal toxicity toward normal endothelial cells. Remarkably, co-treatment achieved comparable therapeutic efficacy at a drastically reduced Carboplatin concentration approximately 148 ng/mL versus 20.5 µg/mL in monotherapy translating to a ~ 99.3% reduction in the required chemotherapeutic dosage. Mechanistically, the combinatory regimen strongly activated both intrinsic and extrinsic apoptotic pathways, as evidenced by increased expression of Caspase-3, Caspase-8, Caspase-9, Bax, P53, PTEN, and P21, and concurrent downregulation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling cascade and anti-apoptotic Bcl-2. These molecular changes were mirrored by a substantial shift toward late apoptotic cell populations in flow cytometric analyses. Notably, the absence of necrotic death underscores apoptosis as the dominant mode of cytotoxicity, potentially minimizing inflammatory responses. The selective cytotoxicity of B. reuteri Ab.338 SH toward malignant glioblastoma cells, coupled with its ability to sensitize tumors to low-dose chemotherapy, positions this strain as a promising adjuvant candidate in GBM treatment. These findings not only advance our understanding of microbe-drug synergy in cancer therapy but also provide a foundation for future preclinical and clinical investigations into probiotic-assisted chemotherapy. Further in vivo validation and mechanistic exploration are warranted to fully elucidate the therapeutic potential of this approach and to translate these insights into clinically viable strategies for the management of glioblastoma.

Limitations and future

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, although Bifidobacterium reuteri Ab.338 SH was identified genomically (GenBank accession PV961363), phenotypic and functional characterization including metabolite profiling, SCFA production, or exopolysaccharide analysis was not performed. Future studies should aim to characterize these bacterial metabolites to better link the observed synergistic effects with specific bioactive compounds. Second, our mechanistic conclusions are based solely on mRNA expression data at a single 24-hour time point. While transcriptional analyses revealed consistent modulation of apoptosis-related and tumor-suppressive pathways (Caspase cascade, AKT/PTEN, and P53/P21), gene-level changes may not directly correspond to protein-level activation. Therefore, future work should include kinetic analyses across multiple time points and protein-level validation using Western blot or ELISA to confirm pathway activation. Third, while HUVEC cells were used to assess general cytotoxicity and confirm the safety of B. reuteri toward normal human endothelial cells, the absence of a normal glial or brain-derived control line is a limitation. Incorporating primary astrocytes or other normal glial models in future studies will help determine whether the observed selectivity is specific to malignant glioblastoma cells rather than a general cellular response. Addressing these limitations in subsequent studies will provide a more comprehensive mechanistic understanding of how B. reuteri potentiates carboplatin-induced apoptosis in glioblastoma cells and strengthen the translational relevance of these findings.
