Propagation of chaos for the Landau equation with very soft and Coulomb potentials
Côme Tabary

Abstract
We consider a drift-diffusion process of N N stochastic particles and show that its empirical measure converges, as N → ∞ N\rightarrow\infty , to the solution of the Landau equation. We work in the regime of very soft and Coulomb potentials using a tightness/uniqueness method. To claim uniqueness, we need high integrability estimates that we obtain by crucially exploiting the dissipation of the Fisher information at the level of the particle system. To be able to exploit these estimates as N → ∞ N\rightarrow\infty , we prove the affinity in infinite dimension of the entropy production and Fisher information dissipation (and general higher-order versions of the Fisher information through an abstract theorem), results which were up to now only known for the entropy and the Fisher information.



1.  Introduction

1.1.  Background

The homogeneous Landau equation in three space dimensions writes

\partial_{t}f_{t}(v)=\nabla_{v}\cdot\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\alpha(|v-w|)a(v-w)(\nabla_{v}-\nabla_{w})f_{t}(v)f_{t}(w)dw\mathchar 59\relax

(1)

where the unknown
f_{t}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
is the time-dependent distribution of velocities in a plasma.
The matrix-valued function

a(z):=|z|^{2}\Id-z\otimes z

is the projection on
z^{\perp}
up to a
|z|^{2}
factor. The non-negative function
\alpha
is called the interaction potential, and depends on the nature of the interactions between the plasma particles. In this work we will consider power-law interaction potentials

\alpha(r)=r^{\gamma}

and will focus on so-called
very soft potentials

\gamma\in[-3\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}-2]
. The case
\gamma=-3
corresponds to the most physically relevant Coulomb interactions.

The Landau equation has been widely
used in plasma physics as it is derived from the Boltzmann equation in the limit of
grazing collisions
(that is to say, when very few momentum is exchanged during a single interaction between two particles). Its statistical nature makes it a description of the plasma at a
mesoscopic scale
, bridging the microscopic scale of individual particles and the macroscopic scale of fluid dynamics.
For an in-depth review of these kinetic models, we refer to
[
29
]
for a physicist’s approach and to
[
42
]
for a mathematician’s.

The rigorous derivation of time-irreversible kinetic equations from a microscopic reversible Newtonian particle system is a deep question initiated by Boltzmann himself in his 1872 work
[
2
]
. Showing that, as the number of particles becomes infinite, they can be statistically described by the solution to a kinetic equation is called
propagation of chaos
. This name refers to the fact that particles are necessarily correlated in the finite system because they collide with each other, but in the limit they become independent, or
chaotic
. Indeed, the Boltzmann and Landau equations intrinsically postulate that before any collision, two particles have "forgotten" their past and are uncorrelated: this is the hypothesis of "molecular chaos". A testimony of this is the tensor product
f_{t}(v)f_{t}(w)
in (
1
), which is the distribution of velocities of two
independent
particles. For a long time Lanford’s result
[
26
]
was the best but still partial answer to this complex question, at least until the recent breakthrough
[
12
]
.

In 1956, Kac
[
25
]
proposed the simpler goal of obtaining the space-homogeneous Boltzmann equation from a Markov
N
-particle jump process by performing a mean field limit, rather than starting from Newtonian dynamics. He studied a one-dimensional toy model, for which he proved the first result of propagation of chaos, towards the associated toy equivalent of the Boltzmann equation. These results were later extended to higher dimensions and more physical cases, we refer in particular to
[
31
]
and the references therein.

The same goal can be set for the space-homogeneous Landau equation (
1
): can the Landau equation be obtained as a mean-field limit from a suitable stochastic particle system?
The answer has been proven positive for a variety of interaction potentials
\alpha
(and slightly different particle systems to start with), that we will review in more detail below. To summarize, propagation of chaos was shown for the case of moderately soft to hard potentials
\alpha(r)=r^{\gamma}\mathchar 59\relax\gamma\in(-2\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}1)

[
15
,
4
,
17
,
33
,
6
]
and partial results of convergence towards the BBGKY-Landau hierarchy were proven in the Coulomb case
[
30
,
7
]
. Very soft and Coulomb potentials
\gamma\in[-3\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}-2]
were, to our knowledge, never covered until the very recent result of Feng and Wang
[
14
]
. The present work was pursued in parallel to
[
14
]
, with a very different approach, differences that we discuss in more detail below.

1.2.  Main result

In this paper, we show propagation of chaos for the Landau equation with very soft (or Coulomb) potentials
\gamma\in[-3\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}-2]
. We will start from a regularized version of the particle system considered in
[
30
,
4
]
(and also studied in
[
33
]
), to which we refer for details. Consider a
N
-particle jump process where each pair
(i\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}j)
of particles with velocities
V^{N}_{i}
and
V^{N}_{j}
overcomes elastic collisions with a rate
|V^{N}_{i}-V^{N}_{j}|^{\gamma}
: this system correponds to Kac’s original proposition to approximate the Boltzmann equation. One can pass this system to the grazing collision limit and (formally) obtain a diffusion-drift
N
-particle system approaching the Landau equation.

This resulting system is not clearly well-posed for
\gamma<0
because of the singularity at the origin of the interaction potential
\alpha
. As done in
[
17
]
for moderately soft potentials, we regularize
\alpha
for each number
N
of particles and let the regularization vanish as
N\rightarrow+\infty
. We emphasize that since the regularization can be taken arbitrarily close to the original
\alpha
and can disappear arbitrarily fast, this does not affect the physical meaning of the system.

We consider a pointwise non-decreasing sequence of positive, smooth and bounded functions
(\alpha^{N})_{N\geq 2}
, such that
\alpha^{N}\nearrow\alpha
, and such that
\alpha=\alpha^{N}
outside the ball
B(0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\eta_{N})
for some
\eta_{N}\xrightarrow[N\rightarrow\infty]{}0
. We also suppose that for each
N\geq 2
, for any
r>0
,

\frac{r|(\alpha^{N})^{\prime}(r)|}{\alpha^{N}(r)}\leq\frac{-\gamma}{0.99}.

(2)

Note that this condition is automatically satisfied for
r>\eta_{N}
because the left-hand side equals
-\gamma
for
\alpha^{N}(r)=r^{\gamma}
. We will give an example of such a regularization in Section
2
. (We fixed the value
0.99
for nicer explicit constants in estimates but it can be lowered to anything strictly greater than
(-\gamma)/\sqrt{22}
, see Remark
3.6
.)

Using these potentials we can present the particle system we will start from. We define the drift and the diffusion

\displaystyle b^{N}:=\nabla\cdot(\alpha^{N}a)=\sum_{k=1}^{3}\partial_{k}(\alpha^{N}a_{kl})=-2\alpha^{N}v\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}

\displaystyle\sigma^{N}:=\left(\alpha^{N}a\right)^{1/2}=\sqrt{\alpha^{N}}|v|^{-1}a.

We consider a family of independent Brownian motions indexed by the ordered pairs of particles,
(B^{ij})_{1\leq i<j\leq N}
, and let
B^{ji}=-B^{ij}
. The regularized particle system is the following system of stochastic differential equations for the
\mathbb{R}^{3}
-valued velocities
(V^{N}_{i})_{1\leq i\leq N}
:

\displaystyle V^{N}_{i}(t)=V^{N}_{i\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}0}

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{N-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}b^{N}(V^{N}_{i}(s)-V^{N}_{j}(s))ds

\displaystyle+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{N-1}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{N}(V^{N}_{i}(s)-V^{N}_{j}(s))dB^{N}_{ij}(s).

(3)

Here, the initial data
(V^{N}_{i\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}0})_{1\leq i\leq N}
is a random variable independent of the Brownian motions. We suppose that initial chaos holds,
i.e.
that the law of
(V^{N}_{i\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}0})_{1\leq i\leq N}
is given by the tensor product
g_{0}^{\otimes N}
for some initial distribution
g_{0}
.

Remark 1.1
.

The original particle system derived in
[
30
,
4
]
is exactly (
1.2
) but defined with
\alpha
instead of
\alpha^{N}
in the drift and diffusion. Its coefficients are hence singular at the origin, so its well-posedness is a potentially difficult issue that is not the topic of this work.

To state our result, we define the random empirical measure
(\mu^{N}_{t})_{t\in[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]}\in C([0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
, the space of probability-valued continuous functions on
[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]
, by letting

\mu^{N}_{t}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{V^{N}_{i}(t)}.

The space
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
of probabilities over
\mathbb{R}^{3}
will always be endowed with the (metrizable) topology of weak convergence (against bounded continuous functions) and
C([0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
is endowed with the uniform topology.
Our main result is the following (see below for the classical definition of Fisher information and entropy):

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\gamma\in[-3\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}-2]
. Let
g_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
have unit mass, finite energy
E_{0}
, finite entropy
H_{0}
, finite Fisher information
I_{0}
, and finite moment of order
m

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|v|^{m}g_{0}dv<+\infty

for some
m>3(2-\gamma)
; and let
(g_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the classical solution to the Landau equation (
1
) with initial condition
g_{0}
.
Then, for any
T>0
, the random empirical measure converges in probability in
C([0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
to the deterministic
(g_{t})_{t\in[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]}
:

(\mu^{N}_{t})_{t\in[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]}\xrightarrow[N\rightarrow\infty]{\mathbb{P}}(g_{t})_{t\in[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]}.

Remark 1.3
.

In view of the topology we endowed
C([0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
with, the convergence in probability in the above theorem means that, for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\in[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]}d\left(\mu^{N}_{t}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}g_{t}\right)>\varepsilon\right)\xrightarrow[N\rightarrow\infty]{}0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}

where
d
is any metric for the weak topology on
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
(for instance, a Wasserstein metric associated to a bounded distance on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, see
[
44
, Theorem 6.9, Corollary 6.13]
).

Remark 1.4
.

The hypotheses on
g_{0}
are stronger than those of
[
24
, Theorem 1.5]
, that guarantees the existence of a classical solution to the Landau equation with constant energy and non-increasing entropy (and non-increasing Fisher information, but we will not use it directly). It will likewise be shown in Proposition
2.2
that (
1.2
) admits a pathwise unique solution defined for all positive times, so every object in the above theorem makes sense.

This formulation, which is natural in the setting of our proof, might not be the most common way to state propagation of chaos. It is indeed usually expressed in terms of the law of the particle system and its marginals. Let
F^{N}_{t}
be the law of
(V^{N}_{i}(t))_{1\leq i\leq N}
, and for any
1\leq j\leq N
, let
F^{N:j}_{t}
be its
j
-th marginal,
i.e.

F^{N:j}_{t}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3(N-j)}}F^{N}_{t}dv_{j+1}...dv_{N}.

It actually does not matter over which variables we integrate, because of the exchangeability of particles. Then Theorem
1.2
entails:

Corollary 1.5
.

Under the same hypothesis on
g_{0}
as in Theorem
1.2
, for any
t\geq 0
, and any
j\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, the weak convergence

F^{N:j}_{t}\xrightharpoonup[N\rightarrow\infty]{}g_{t}^{\otimes j}

holds in
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3j})
.

In this form the term
propagation of chaos
can be better understood: note that our hypothesis on
(V^{N}_{i\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}0})_{1\leq i\leq N}
is exactly
F^{N}_{0}=g_{0}^{\otimes N}
, so that we suppose molecular chaos (independence of the particles) at initial time, and we show that this chaos is
propagated
to later times in the large
N
limit. For more details on the different statements of chaoticity and quantitative equivalences between them, we refer to
[
20
]
.

1.3.  Literature review and discussion

Propagation of chaos is an expansive topic, reaching beyond kinetic theory. For a general review, we refer to
[
9
,
10
,
20
,
39
]
. Below we will focus on the case of the Landau equation which already enjoys a wide literature.

On particle systems.

As stated above, the particle system we consider in this work was derived by Miot, Pulvirenti, Saffirio, and Carrapatoso
[
30
,
4
]
, as a grazing collision limit of a jump process approximating the Boltzmann equation. (Actually, in both works the grazing collision limit is performed on the Kolmogorov equation for the
law
of the process, and in
[
4
]
proposes an actual stochastic system such that its law satisfies the limit equation.) An important consequence is that, as does the jump process, the drift diffusion system almost surely conserves momentum and energy:
\sum_{i}V^{N}_{i}
and
\sum_{i}(V^{N}_{i})^{2}
are constant in time. This means that the process is confined on the corresponding Boltzmann sphere (the sphere of dimension
3(N-1)-1
formed by the points of
\mathbb{R}^{3N}
of constant given momentum and energy), so there is no diffusion in the orthogonal directions. These constraints also mean that approximate independence of the particles is harder to achieve. Propagation of chaos for this conservative system in the case of Maxwellian molecules
\gamma=0
was shown in
[
4
]
adapting the abstract framework developed in
[
31
]
, and Fournier and Guillin covered the case of hard potentials
\gamma\in[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}1)
in
[
33
]
with a probabilistic coupling method. These methods both provide (polynomial in
N
) rates of convergence.

There exists a slightly easier to study but slightly less natural particle system, that does not conserve the energy. It is obtained by considering (
1.2
) with
N^{2}
independent Brownian motions rather than setting
B^{ij}=-B^{ji}
. Propagation of chaos towards the Landau equation was shown by Fontbona, Guerin, Méléard
[
15
]
for Maxwell molecules. This system is also considered by Fournier and Hauray
[
17
]
in the only complete result of propagation of chaos with soft potentials we are aware of: they cover moderately soft potentials
\gamma\in(-2\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}0)
.
They also regularize the particle system (by convolution) to make it well-posed. We believe the difference in regularization has little influence on the system since both are arbitrarily small (in fact, their regularization could probably fit in our method but some proofs and inequalities would be less straightforward). The case
\gamma\in(-1\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}0)
is treated by a fine probabilistic coupling (providing convergence with a polynomial rate in
N
), and the case
\gamma\in(-2\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}-1)
is by a tightness-uniqueness argument. We also mention a variation of this non-conservative particle system, obtained with
N
independent Brownian instead of
N^{2}
, considered in
[
6
,
8
]
.

These non-conservative systems still preserve the expected energy
\mathbb{E}(\sum_{i}(V^{N}_{i})^{2})
and, at first glance, their difference with (
1.2
) seems light. However we point out that their laws obey a different Kolmogorov equation, for which it is not clear that the Fisher information is non-increasing, as it will be the case with the Kolmogorov equation of (
1.2
). We believe this makes the conservative system (
1.2
) even more preferable to the non-conservative ones, as the Landau equation is now known to dissipate the Fisher information
[
19
]
.

On the Kolmogorov/Master equation.

Although we did not need it to state Theorem
1.2
, the law
F^{N}_{t}
of the random velocities
(V^{N}_{i}(t))_{1\leq i\leq N}
plays a central role in both parts of the tightness/uniqueness method we use. First, showing tightness of the empirical measures
\mu^{N}
requires estimates on their laws and hence on
F^{N}_{t}
. Second, any regularity of the cluster points of
(\mu^{N})_{N\geq 2}
is obtained by passing to the limit in the regularity of
F^{N}_{t}
(once expressed in adequate quantities that behave well as the number
N
of particles, and hence the dimension, goes to infinity: this is discussed below).

The law
F^{N}_{t}=F^{N}_{t}(v_{1}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}...v_{N})
obeys the following Kolmogorov (also known as
Master
) equation (in a weak form) (see
[
4
,
7
]
, and Appendix
A
for a derivation):

\partial_{t}F^{N}_{t}=\frac{1}{(N-1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\mathchar 59\relax j=1\\
i<j\end{subarray}}^{N}Q^{N}_{ij}(F^{N}_{t})\mathchar 59\relax

(4)

with

Q^{N}_{ij}(F^{N}_{t})=(\nabla_{i}-\nabla_{j})\cdot\left[\alpha^{N}a(v_{i}-v_{j})(\nabla_{i}-\nabla_{j})F^{N}_{t}\right]\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}

\nabla_{i}
denoting the nabla with respect to
v_{i}
. It is possible to forget the particle system itself and only work on its law to show propagation of chaos: it amounts to showing Corollary
1.5
directly, without Theorem
1.2
. This is the approach of
[
4
,
6
,
7
,
30
]
.

The operator
Q^{N}_{ij}
is the same that appears in the landmark proof of the monotonicity of the Fisher information by Guillen and Silvestre
[
19
]
(up to
\alpha^{N}
being replaced by
\alpha
). Therein, the question for the nonlinear Landau equation (
1
) is reduced to the monotonicity for the "lifted" linear equation
\partial_{t}F(v_{1}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}v_{2})=Q_{12}(F)(v_{1}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}v_{2})
on
\mathbb{R}^{6}
, which is nothing but (
4
) for
N=2
. From there it is easy to see that the Fisher information does not increase along the flow of the general
N
-particle Kolmogorov equation because it decreases along each
Q^{N}_{ij}
. This was first observed by Carrillo and Guo in
[
7
]
in the Coulomb case
\gamma=-3
, where the well-posedness of (
4
) without the regularization of
\alpha
is studied. The authors use the (uniform in
N
) control provided by the Fisher information to show tightness of the sequences of
j
-marginals
(F^{N:j}_{t})_{N}
for any
j
. Suitable subsequences in
N
converge to some limit
G^{j}_{t}
which satisfies a BBGKY-type infinite hierarchy of equations:
G^{j}_{t}
obeys an equation depending on
G^{j+1}_{t}
. Because the tensorization
(g_{t}^{\otimes j})_{j}
of any solution of the Landau equation is a solution to the hierarchy, uniqueness of solutions to the hierarchy would imply the propagation of chaos
F_{t}^{N:j}\rightharpoonup g_{t}^{\otimes j}
. Unfortunately, it is not reached in
[
7
]
. We also mention the earlier work
[
30
]
, also in the Coulomb case, where (
4
) is further regularized with a Laplacian (vanishing as
N\rightarrow+\infty
), and a similar convergence to a hierarchy is shown (in a weaker sense).

Remark 1.6
.

Since the divergence with respect to
v_{2}
vanishes in the integral, the Landau equation (
1
) can be rewritten

\partial_{t}f_{t}(v_{1})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}Q_{12}(f(v_{1})f(v_{2}))dv_{2}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}

which highlights the formal proximity between the particle system and the Landau equation. If we suppose that
F^{N}_{t}
is tensorized at all times,
F^{N}_{t}=f_{t}^{\otimes N}
, then integrating (
4
) over
(v_{2}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}...\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}v_{N})
and using symmetry on the remaining terms we see that
f_{t}
must solve the Landau equation.

Remark 1.7
.

The equation (
4
) is a degenerate parabolic equation. As the particle system is confined to Boltzmann spheres of constant impulsion and energy, the diffusion in (
4
) only occurs in the directions tangent to these spheres. When restrained to the unit Boltzmann sphere
\{(v_{1}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}...v_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{3N}|\sum_{i}v_{i}=0\mathchar 59\relax\sum_{i}|v_{i}|^{2}=1\}
, it reduces for
N=2
to the heat equation with the Laplace Beltrami operator on the sphere and a diffusion coefficient proportional to the interaction potential
\alpha^{N}
. In contrast, in higher dimension it still degenerates at certain points where the velocities are aligned. The structure of the equation (
4
) might not be completely understood.

Hierarchy versus probabilistic point of view.

We believe uniqueness to the BBGKY-type Landau hierarchy mentioned in the above paragraph, which has yet to be proven, is strictly stronger than the uniqueness part of our tightness/uniqueness method. Working at the level of the Landau hierarchy as in
[
7
,
30
]
amounts to showing tightness of
(F^{N:j}_{t})_{N}
for each time
t
and extract a limit
G^{j}_{t}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3j})
to build a hierarchy
(G^{j}_{t})_{j}
, solution to the infinite BBGKY-Landau system. To such a hierarchy can be associated a unique element
G_{t}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
by the de Finetti-Hewitt-Savage theorem, such that

G^{j}_{t}=\int\rho^{\otimes j}G_{t}(d\rho)
. The limit object is thus an element
(G_{t})_{t\in[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]}\in C\left([0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))\right)
, and the uniqueness part of the tightness/uniqueness method in the setting of the BBGKY-Landau hierarchy would amount to show that
G_{t}=\delta_{g_{t}}
(where
g_{t}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
is the solution to the Landau equation at time
t
).

In the present work, however, we show tightness at the level of the particle system, more precisely at the level of the law of empirical measures
(\mu^{N}_{t})_{t\in[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]}
. Our limit object
\pi
hence lives in the space
\mathcal{P}\left(C([0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))\right)
, which can be continuously mapped to
C\left([0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))\right)
by considering the time marginals
(\pi_{t})_{t\in[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]}
(
\pi_{t}
is the law of
f_{t}
if
f=(f_{t})_{t\in[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]}
has law
\pi
). The converse cannot be done, since a probability is in general not characterized by its marginals. This means
\pi
contains more information than
(G_{t})_{t\in[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]}
, so the uniqueness of
\pi
we show in this work, that is to say
\pi=\delta_{(g_{t})_{t}}
, is less general than the uniqueness in the hierarchy approach. In some sense, we trade a stronger tightness against an easier uniqueness.

It is highly probable that the hierarchy
(\pi^{j}_{t})_{j}
solves the same BBGKY-Landau equations satisfied by the
(G^{j}_{t})_{j}
. If so, uniqueness for the hierarchy would entail
\pi_{t}=\delta_{g_{t}}
. In this deterministic case knowledge of the marginals is sufficient to claim
\pi=\delta_{(g_{t})_{t}}
, so uniqueness for the hierarchy would indeed be stronger. We did not investigate this point further.

Uniqueness in the Landau equation.
In the tightness/uniqueness method, once tightness is proven, we suppose that (up to a subsequence)
(\mu^{N}_{t})_{t}\rightharpoonup(f_{t})_{t}
for some
C([0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
-valued random variable
f=(f_{t})_{t}
and want to conclude that
(f_{t})_{t}=(g_{t})_{t}
, the classical solution to the Landau equation. To do so, we first show that
f
solves the Landau equation in some suitable weak sense, and then claim uniqueness of such weak solutions. Uniqueness does not hold without some regularity on
f
: we rely on Guérin and Fournier’s results
[
18
,
16
]
which applies to weak solutions in
L^{1}([0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
for
p>3/(3+\gamma)
and
L^{1}([0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
for
\gamma=-3
. We will show that for any value of
\gamma
,
f\in L^{1}([0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
almost surely.

Other results exist in the literature such as the Prodi-Serrin-type criteria
[
1
,
11
]
. A uniqueness property for H-solutions (in the sense of Villani
[
43
]
) without any integrability assumptions was proven by the author in
[
40
]
. However all these results rely on the
H-theorem
:
f
is supposed to be a weak solution (or H-solution) with the additional assumption that the formal a priori estimate on the entropy given by the Landau equation (
1
) holds. We stress that such a H-theorem is not automatic for weak solutions, so checking that
f
lies in the appropriate functional space would not be enough.

Behavior of functionals in the infinite dimension limit.
We detail what we consider both one of the most crucial and most original parts of this work: the extension of typical properties of the entropy and Fisher information to more general and higher-order functionals. As described in the previous paragraph, to claim uniqueness we need to recover some regularity or integrability of the limit
f_{t}
of the empirical measures
\mu^{N}_{t}
. Because empirical measures enjoy virtually no regularity, we have little hope of getting good estimates directly from this limit. What is common in similar proofs of propagation of chaos
[
17
,
37
,
38
]
is to recover estimates on the expectation
\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{F}(f_{t}))
for some norm or regularity-quantifying functional
\mathcal{F}
, by using uniform-in-
N
estimates on
\mathcal{F}(F^{N}_{t})
.

Because the dimension increases as
N\rightarrow\infty
, not every functional can be passed to the limit (think of the
L^{p}
norm of a tensor product
f^{\otimes N}
which scales as
\|f^{\otimes N}\|_{L^{p}}=\|f\|_{L^{p}}^{N}
). Two better-suited candidates are the entropy
H
and the Fisher information
I
of a probability measure
F^{N}
on
\mathbb{R}^{3N}
:

\displaystyle H(F^{N}):=\frac{1}{N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}F^{N}\log F^{N}dV\mathchar 59\relax

\displaystyle I(F^{N}):=\frac{1}{N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}\frac{|\nabla F^{N}|^{2}}{F^{N}}dV\mathchar 59\relax

(5)

taking the value
+\infty
if
F^{N}
has no density (or its density has no gradient in the case of
I
). Both
H
and
F
are convex and lower-semicontinuous for the weak convergence of probabilities, which is very convenient in our setting. Their nice behavior comes from the super-additivity property: for any symmetric (definition recalled in the next section) probability
F^{N}
on
\mathbb{R}^{3N}
, and any
1\leq j\leq N
, if
F^{N:j}
is the
j
-th marginal of
F^{N}
,

\displaystyle H(F^{N:j})\leq H(F^{N})\mathchar 59\relax

\displaystyle I(F^{N:j})\leq I(F^{N})

(6)

and
H(f^{\otimes N})=H(f)
,
I(f^{\otimes N})=I(f)
. This means, given a
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
or (equivalently, by the de Finetti-Hewitt-Savage theorem) the sequence
(\nu^{j})_{j}
obtained by taking the marginals
\nu^{j}=\int\rho^{\otimes j}d\nu
, superadditivity entails that
H(\nu^{j})
and
I(\nu^{j})
are increasing in
j
, because
\nu^{j:k}=\nu^{k}
for any
k\leq j
, so one can consider the so-called
level 3
, or
mean
quantities

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(\nu):=\sup_{j}H(\nu^{j})=\lim_{j}H(\nu^{j})\mathchar 59\relax

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\nu)=\sup_{j}I(\nu^{j})=\lim_{j}I(\nu^{j}).

As a supremum of convex functions, these quantities are convex in
\nu
. The remarkable property is their
affinity
: they are known to satisfy

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(\nu)=\int_{\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}H(\rho)\nu(d\rho)\mathchar 59\relax

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\nu)=\int_{\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}I(\rho)\nu(d\rho).

(7)

If
\nu=\pi_{t}
is the law of the random variable
f_{t}
, the right-hand side is
\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{F}(f_{t}))
for either
\mathcal{F}=H
or
\mathcal{F}=I
. This expectation is controlled by the
(\mathcal{F}(\pi_{t}^{j}))_{j}
, which we can actually bound by
\mathcal{F}(F_{t}^{N:j})
using lower semi-continuity, and by
\mathcal{F}(F_{t}^{N})
by super-additivity. Hence super-additivity (
6
) and affinity in infinite-dimension (
7
) are the two key properties to pass to the limit
N\rightarrow+\infty
.

For our proof, we need higher integrability than what the Fisher information provides by Sobolev embeddings. In particular, we want to be able to pass to
N\rightarrow+\infty
limit (a part of) the dissipation of the Fisher information, which is a second-order version of the Fisher information, of the form

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}\frac{|\partial\partial F|^{2}}{F}dV

(8)

with
\partial=\tilde{b}\cdot\nabla
a derivation operator along some vector field
\tilde{b}
. We will also need to pass to the limit in the entropy production, which is a first-order generalized Fisher information
\frac{1}{N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}|\partial F|^{2}/FdV
(for a similar derivation operator
\partial
). Their super-additivity is proven along the same lines as for the Fisher information. To show infinite-dimensional affinity, we expand in Appendix
C
on the framework developed by Rougerie
[
36
]
for the Fisher information and its fractional versions, to provide an abstract theorem (Theorem
C.6
) that we apply to the Fisher information and the entropy production. Note that we are unable to prove the exact affinity (
7
) for the Fisher dissipation (
8
) but only up to explicit constants (which suffices for our needs).

Although not detailed in this work, we claim that the abstract theorem allows us to show infinite-dimension affinity of (
8
) for fairly general
b
, including the straightforward case of usual partial derivatives
\partial=\partial_{i}
with little to no adaptation of the proof for the Fisher dissipation. We believe the nice behavior in infinite dimension of those different Fisher-type functionals is a novelty of this work and could prove useful beyond the study of the Landau equation.

Comparison with Feng and Wang
[
14
]
.
The day before the submission of the first version of this work on arXiv, X. Feng and Z. Wang submitted the remarkable preprint
[
14
]
, in which they show propagation of chaos for the Landau equation with Coulomb interactions for the first time in the literature, and their method is also able to treat very soft potentials with little to no modification. The author pledges that he did not know of the work
[
14
]
prior to its submission, and that the present work was pursued in parallel to theirs.

The method in
[
14
]
has very little in common with the current paper and provides another point of view on propagation of chaos. The authors of
[
14
]
work solely at the level of the law of the particle system (
4
), without regularizing the potential. Their starting point is the recent duality method introduced by Bresch, Duerinckx and Jabin in
[
3
]
, which they elegantly tailor to the Landau setting. To handle the singularity of the system, they expand on the method by combining it with key estimates on the Landau equation. This clever use of the duality approach ultimately reduces the problem to the question of uniqueness for a certain dual hierarchy of equations, which seems easier to handle than the Landau-BBGKY hierarchy described earlier. The main similarity with the present paper is that both proofs crucially rely on some second-order version of the Fisher information, indicating that the high regularity it provides is certainly essential to handle very singular potentials.

1.4.  Notations and layout of the proof

Definitions and notations.

The space of (Borel) probability measures on a complete separable metric space
\mathcal{X}
is denoted by
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
. Endowed with the topology of weak convergence against continuous bounded functions, it is itself a complete separable metric space. A probability measure

is said to be

if, for any permutation
\sigma
of
\{1\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}...\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}n\}

F^{n}(dv_{1}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}..\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}dv_{n})=F^{n}(dv_{\sigma(1)}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}...\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}dv_{\sigma(n)}).

For any such symmetric probability and
1\leq j\leq n
we can consider the marginal
F^{n:j}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3j})
obtained by

F^{n:j}(dv_{1}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}...dv_{j})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3(n-j)}}F^{n}dv_{j+1}...dv_{n}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}

or by integrating over any other set of
n-j
distinct variables.

By

of a probability
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
we mean the second moment
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|v|^{2}\mu(dv)
, and by momentum the vector-valued first moment
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}v\mu(dv)

The
\nabla
operator applied to any function is the gradient in all variables, unless an index is specified, such as
\nabla_{i}
w.r.t.
v_{i}
,
\nabla_{ij}
w.r.t.
(v_{i}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}v_{j})
, and so on.

\displaystyle b_{k}(v_{1}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}v_{2}):=e_{k}\times(v_{1}-v_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\mathchar 59\relax

Strategy and Layout.

In Section

) for the velocities
(V^{N}_{1}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}...\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}V^{N}_{N})

First, in Section

Second, in Section

In the last Section

Remark 2.1
.

An example of a sequence

can be built as follows:
let
\chi:\mathbb{R_{+}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R^{*}_{+}}
be a smooth increasing function such that

for

We now fix, for any number of particles

which is non-increasing in

and goes to zero at infinity. We can take

This short section is dedicated to the well-posedness of the particle system:

For any

) admits a pathwise unique solution

3.  Entropy and Fisher information in the Kolmogorov equation

In this section we study the law of the particle system. We gather estimates on the energy, entropy and Fisher information. In particular, the remarkable monotonicity of the Fisher information, which was proven in
[
19
]
for
N=2
to obtain monotonicity for the non-linear Landau equation, and extended to general

in
[
7
]
, will enable us to show tightness of the particle system in the next section. The other estimates will turn useful later on when studying the cluster points of the particle system.

For any
t\geq 0
, any
N\geq 2
, we let
F^{N}_{t}
be the law of
(V^{N}_{i}(t))_{1\leq i\leq N}
, so that

is a deterministic probability measure on

Using the Itô Formula to compute the derivative
\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi(V^{N}_{1}(t)\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}...\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}V^{N}_{N}(t))\right]

is a distribution solution to

\partial_{t}F^{N}_{t}=\mathcal{Q}^{N}(F^{N}_{t}):=\frac{1}{(N-1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\mathchar 59\relax j=1\\
i<j\end{subarray}}^{N}Q^{N}_{ij}(F^{N}_{t})

(9)

where the operator

Q_{ij}^{N}(F):=(\nabla_{i}-\nabla_{j})\cdot\left[\alpha^{N}a(v_{i}-v_{j})(\nabla_{i}-\nabla_{j})F\right].

(10)

Note that the only dependence on

is through the regularization of the interaction potential. Two integration by parts show that

is formally self-adjoint. The full derivation of (
9

.

Remark 3.1
.

The theory of degenerate parabolic equations ensures uniqueness of weak solutions to (

The following quantities are constant for all times

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}F^{N}_{t}dV

\displaystyle=1\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}v_{i}F^{N}_{t}dV

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}vg_{0}dv\mathchar 59\relax

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}|V|^{2}F^{N}_{t}dV

Since

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}\varphi F^{N}_{t}dV=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}\varphi F^{N}_{0}dV+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}\mathcal{Q}^{N}(\varphi)F^{N}_{s}dV\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}

We compute

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}H(F^{N}_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}\mathcal{Q}^{N}(F^{N}_{t})\log F^{N}_{t}dV

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N(N-1)}\sum_{i<j}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}Q^{N}_{ij}(F^{N}_{t})\log F^{N}_{t}dV

Proposition 3.5

For any

I(F^{N}_{t})+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{k=1}^{3}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}\frac{\alpha^{N}(v_{1}-v_{2})}{|v_{1}-v_{2}|^{2}}\left((\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla_{12})(\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla_{12})\log F^{N}_{s}\right)^{2}F^{N}_{s}dVds\leq I_{0}.

Proof.

The monotonicity of the Fisher information for the Kolmogorov equation (with
\alpha

Step 1.
We write, using symmetry:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}I(F^{N}_{t})

\displaystyle=\langle I^{\prime}(F^{N}_{t})\mathchar 59\relax\mathcal{Q}^{N}(F^{N}_{t})\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i<j}\langle I^{\prime}(F^{N}_{t})\mathchar 59\relax Q^{N}_{ij}(F^{N}_{t})\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{2}\langle I^{\prime}(F^{N}_{t})\mathchar 59\relax Q^{N}_{12}(F^{N}_{t})\rangle

We cut the Fisher information in two

Step 2.
We claim that
\langle I^{\prime}_{other}(F^{N}_{t})\mathchar 59\relax Q^{N}_{12}(F^{N}_{t})\rangle\leq 0
. Indeed, because of (

\sqrt{\alpha^{N}}\tilde{b}_{k}
are divergence free.
Since, for

\nabla_{j}

\mathcal{L}_{k}:=\sqrt{\alpha^{N}}\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla_{12}

\displaystyle\langle I_{other}^{\prime}(F)\mathchar 59\relax\mathcal{L}_{k}F\rangle

Remark 3.7
.

In all rigor, a regularization argument is necessary to perform the computations above on weak solutions. We can solve
\partial_{t}F^{N\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\varepsilon}_{t}=\mathcal{Q}^{N}(F^{N\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\varepsilon}_{t})+\varepsilon\Delta F^{N\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\varepsilon}_{t}

as

which is easily seen to be a weak solution to (

. We can easily pass the estimates on the entropy and the Fisher information to the limit because all the terms are lower semi-continuous for the weak convergence of probability measures (it is well known for

for the production/dissipation terms). The lower semi-continuity makes it so we only obtain an inequality rather than an equality in the estimates.
We kept a formal approach to lighten the presentation. For a more detailed study of (

) without any regularization on
\alpha
, see
[
7
]
.

4.  Tightness of the particle system

We are now ready to show that the sequence of empirical measures of the particle system is tight.

We begin with a key estimate on the proximity of particles, that will be used to control the singularities of the system. It relies strongly on the control of the Fisher information.

Lemma 4.1
.

For any
N\geq 2
:

\sup_{t\geq 0}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|V^{N}_{1}(t)-V^{N}_{2}(t)\right|^{-2}\right]\leq I_{0}.

Proof.

We write the expectation using the law
F_{t}^{N:2}
of
(V^{N}_{1}(t)\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}V^{N}_{2}(t))
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|V^{N}_{i}(t)-V^{N}_{j}(t)\right|^{-2}\right]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\left|v_{1}-v_{2}\right|^{-2}F_{t\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}2}^{N}(v_{1}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}v_{2})dv_{1}dv_{2}

(16)

where
F_{t}^{N:2}
is given by the
2
-marginal

F_{t}^{N:2}(v_{1}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}v_{2})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3(N-2)}}F^{N}_{t}(v_{1}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}v_{2}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}v_{3}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\ldots\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}v_{N})dv_{3}\ldots dv_{N}.

Then we make use of the unitary change of variables:

\phi:(v_{1}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}v_{2})\mapsto\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(v_{1}-v_{2}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}v_{1}+v_{2})\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}

which allows us to write

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\left|v_{1}-v_{2}\right|^{-2}F_{t}^{N:2}(v_{1}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}v_{2})dv_{1}dv_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\left|w_{1}\right|^{-2}F_{t}^{N:2}\circ\phi^{-1}(w_{1}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}w_{2})dw_{1}dw_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\left|w_{1}\right|^{-2}f(w_{1})dw_{1}\mathchar 59\relax

by letting
f(w_{1})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}F_{t}^{N:2}\circ\phi^{-1}(w_{1}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}w_{2})dw_{2}
.
The Hardy inequality applied to
\sqrt{f}
writes:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\left|w_{1}\right|^{-2}f(w_{1})dw_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\frac{\sqrt{f}}{|\cdot|}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{2}\leq 2\left\|\nabla\sqrt{f}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}I(f).

Using super-additivity of Fisher information,
I(f)
is less than two times (because of our normalization) the Fisher information of
F_{t}^{N:2}\circ\phi^{-1}
, since it is its
1
-marginal. The Fisher information being invariant under the unitary transform

, we finally obtain

\frac{1}{2}I(f)\leq I(F_{t}^{N:2}\circ\phi^{-1})=I(F_{t}^{N:2})\leq I(F^{N}_{t})\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}

where super-additivity was used again for the last inequality.
Using the monotonicity of the Fisher information (Proposition
3.5
) to bound this quantity by
I_{0}
concludes.
∎

This control is enough to show tightness of the sequence of empirical measures. In fact, one can check that the following proof extends to
\gamma>-4
with little to no adaptation.

We claim that there exists a sequence

But the estimate

\displaystyle\left(\nabla\varphi\left(v\right)-\nabla\varphi\left(w\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\min(2\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\|\nabla^{2}\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}|v-w|)

\displaystyle\leq C(\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\nabla^{2}\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}})|v-w|^{-\frac{\gamma+2}{2}}

holds for some absolute constant
C>0
, since
0\leq-\frac{\gamma+2}{2}\leq 1
, and we also have
\|\sigma^{N}(v-w)\|\lesssim|v-w|^{\frac{\gamma+2}{2}}.

Hence the integrand above is bounded and

\big[\mathcal{M}^{N}(\cdot)-\mathcal{M}^{N}(s)\big]_{t}\leq C(\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\nabla^{2}\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}})^{2}\frac{N(N-1)}{2}|t-s|.

We thus reach

\mathbb{E}|\mathcal{M}^{N}(t)-\mathcal{M}^{N}(s)|^{4}\leq C(\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\nabla^{2}\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}})^{4}\left(\frac{N(N-1)}{2}\right)^{2}|t-s|^{2}.

(23)

Notice that this also yields a control of
\mathcal{M}^{N}(t)
since
\mathcal{M}^{N}(0)=0
.
We now rely on the following Sobolev embedding
[
32
, Theorem 8.2]
: for any
f
,

\sup_{s\neq t\in[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]}\frac{|f(t)-f(s)|}{|t-s|^{\frac{1}{8}}}\leq C_{\mathrm{Sob}}\left(\int_{0}^{T}|f(t)|^{4}dt+\iint_{[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]^{2}}\frac{|f(t)-f(s)|^{4}}{|t-s|^{\frac{5}{2}}}dsdt\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}.

With
f=\mathcal{M}^{N}
, taking expectations and using
\mathbb{E}(|X|^{\frac{1}{4}})\leq(\mathbb{E}|X|)^{\frac{1}{4}}
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\left[\sup_{s\neq t\in[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]}\frac{|\mathcal{M}^{N}(t)-\mathcal{M}^{N}(s)|}{|t-s|^{\frac{1}{8}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C_{\mathrm{Sob}}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}(|\mathcal{M}^{N}(t)|^{4})dt+\iint_{[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]^{2}}\frac{\mathbb{E}(|\mathcal{M}^{N}(t)-\mathcal{M}^{N}(s)|^{4})}{|t-s|^{\frac{5}{2}}}dsdt\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\mathrm{Sob}}(\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\nabla^{2}\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}})(N(N-1)/2)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{0}^{T}t^{2}dt+\iint_{[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]^{2}}|t-s|^{-\frac{1}{2}}dsdt\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{T}(\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\nabla^{2}\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}})(N(N-1)/2)^{\frac{1}{2}}

thanks to (
23
). We finally obtain the following control of the third term (
20
):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sup_{s\neq t\in[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]}\frac{\sqrt{2}|t-s|^{-\frac{1}{8}}}{N\sqrt{N-1}}

\displaystyle\Bigg|\sum_{i\neq j}\int_{s}^{t}\nabla\varphi\left(V^{N}_{i}(u)\right)\cdot\sigma^{N}\left(V^{N}_{i}(u)-V^{N}_{j}(u)\right)dB^{N}_{ij}(u)\Bigg|\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{s\neq t\in[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]}\frac{\sqrt{2}}{N\sqrt{N-1}}\frac{|\mathcal{M}^{N}(t)-\mathcal{M}^{N}(s)|}{|t-s|^{\frac{1}{8}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{T}}{\sqrt{N}}(\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\nabla^{2}\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}).

(24)

Step 4.
Using that
(1+\frac{\gamma+2}{2})\geq\frac{1}{8}
, combining (
21
), (
4
) and (
24
), we get a control of the Hölder seminorm of exponent
\frac{1}{8}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{s\neq t\in[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]}|t-s|^{-\frac{1}{8}}

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\varphi(d\mu^{N}_{t}-d\mu^{N}_{s})\right|\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq C_{T\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}I_{0}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\gamma}(1+N^{-\frac{1}{2}})(\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\nabla^{2}\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}).

Taking
\varphi=\varphi_{n}
and summing, we get that for all
N\geq 2
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{s\neq t\in[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]}\frac{d(\mu^{N}_{s}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mu^{N}_{t})}{|\mu^{N}_{s}-\mu^{N}_{t}|^{\frac{1}{8}}}\right]\leq C_{T\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}I_{0}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\gamma}\mathchar 59\relax

because
\|\nabla\varphi_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\nabla^{2}\varphi_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq 1
by hypothesis. This is the desired result.
∎

We can now use this Lemma to show tightness of the empirical measures, that is to say prove Proposition
4.2
.

Proof of Proposition
4.2
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. We need to find a compact set
\mathcal{S}\subset C\left([0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\right)
such that for all
N\geq 2
,
\mathbb{P}(\mu^{N}\in\mathcal{S})\geq 1-\varepsilon.

We first find a candidate for
\mathcal{S}
. Recall that for the weak topology, for any
R>0
, the set

S_{R}=\left\{\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\Bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|v|^{2}\nu(dv)\leq R\right\}

is compact in
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
as a consequence of Markov’s inequality. For
R>0
, we will consider the set

\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{R}:=\left\{\nu=(\nu_{t})_{t}\in C\left([0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\right)\Bigg|(\forall t\in[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]\mathchar 59\relax\nu_{t}\in S_{R})\text{ and }\sup_{s\neq t\in[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]}\frac{d(\nu_{s}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\nu_{t})}{|\nu_{s}-\nu_{t}|^{\frac{1}{8}}}\leq R\right\}\mathchar 59\relax

(25)

which is compact by the Arzèla-Ascoli theorem for continuous functions with values in a general metric space. We treat the two conditions in the two steps below.

We show that there exists
R>0
such that
\forall t\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\ \mu^{N}_{t}\in S_{R}
, with high probability. Thanks to Proposition
2.2
, almost surely, for any
t\in[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|v|^{2}\mu^{N}_{t}(dv)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(V^{N}_{i}(t))^{2}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(V^{N}_{i\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}0})^{2}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}

so, since
\mathcal{L}(V^{N}_{i\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}0})=g_{0}
, taking the supremum over
[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]
and then the expectation, we get

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|v|^{2}\mu^{N}_{t}(dv)\right]=\int|v|^{2}g_{0}dv=E_{0}.

Hence, by Markov’s inequality,

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\in[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|v|^{2}\mu^{N}_{t}(dv)>\frac{2E_{0}}{\varepsilon}\right)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\mathchar 59\relax

so that for any
R\geq\varepsilon/(2E_{0})
,
\mathbb{P}(\forall t\mathchar 59\relax\mu^{N}_{t}\in S_{R})\geq 1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

We finally deal with the Hölder continuity. By Lemma
4.3
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{s\neq t\in[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]}\frac{d(\mu^{N}_{s}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mu^{N}_{t})}{|\mu^{N}_{s}-\mu^{N}_{t}|^{\frac{1}{8}}}\right]\leq C_{T\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}I_{0}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\gamma}.

Hence, by a similar application of Markov’s inequality,

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{s\neq t\in[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]}\frac{d(\mu^{N}_{s}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mu^{N}_{t})}{|\mu^{N}_{s}-\mu^{N}_{t}|^{\frac{1}{8}}}\leq\frac{2C_{T\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}I_{0}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\gamma}}{\varepsilon}\right)\geq 1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\mathchar 59\relax

so that, for
R\geq 2\max{(E_{0}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}C_{T\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}I_{0}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\gamma})}/\varepsilon
, it holds that

\mathbb{P}\left(\mu^{N}\in\mathcal{S}_{R}\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}

and the proof is concluded.
∎

Knowing the tightness of the random variables
(\mu^{N})_{N\geq 2}
, Prokhorov’s Theorem implies that this sequence admits convergent-in-law subsequences.
We now want to show in the next sections that any cluster point
\pi
of
(\mathcal{L}(\mu^{N}))_{N\geq 2}
charges only the classical solution
(g_{t})_{t}
to the Landau equation with initial data
g_{0}
,
i.e.

\pi=\delta_{(g_{t})_{t}}
.

5.  Infinite-dimension limit of the entropy and Fisher information estimates

We fix
\pi\in\mathcal{P}\left(C([0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))\right)
a cluster point of
(\mathcal{L}(\mu^{N}))_{N\geq 2}
for the rest of the proof. This means that up to a subsequence (that we omit to write)
\mathcal{L}(\mu^{N})
converges weakly to
\pi
. The goal is to show that
\pi=\delta_{(g_{t})_{t}}
, so that the whole sequence converges and propagation of chaos holds. Let
f=(f_{t})_{t}
be any
\pi
-distributed random variable. In this section we want to recover some properties of
f
using the estimates on the law
F^{N}
of the particle system. As announced in the introduction, to show that
f
enjoys almost surely some regularity, we will bound
\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{F}(f))
for some regularity-quantifying functional
\mathcal{F}
(typically, the Fisher information). To do so, we will work on the law
\pi
and will rely on the affine behavior of the entropy, Fisher information and dissipation functionals in infinite dimension and their super-additivity.

We begin by relating more directly the cluster point
\pi
and the law
(F^{N}_{t})_{t}
. We denote by
\pi_{t}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
the law of
f_{t}
,
i.e.
the pushforward of
\pi
by the evaluation map
f\mapsto f_{t}
. To any
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
, we recall that we can associate a hierarchy of symmetric marginals
\nu^{j}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3j})
by letting

\nu^{j}:=\int_{\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\rho^{\otimes j}\nu(d\rho).

This hierarchy is compatible, in the sense that the marginals
\nu^{j:k}=\nu^{k}
for any
1\leq k\leq j
. The Hewitt-Savage theorem
[
21
]
asserts that such a compatible hierarchy uniquely characterizes an element of
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
, but we will not need such a result. The limit
\pi_{t}
and the
F^{N}_{t}
are linked by the following:

Proposition 5.1
.

For any
j\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, for any
t\in[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]
,

F^{N:j}_{t}\xrightharpoonup[N\rightarrow\infty]{}\pi_{t}^{j}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}

in the sense of weak convergence of probability measures (along the common subsequence in
N
for which
\mathcal{L}(\mu^{N})\rightharpoonup\pi
, that we omit to write).

Proof.

Let
\hat{F}^{N}_{t}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
be the law of the random empirical measure
\mu^{N}_{t}
, or in other words, the pushforward of
F^{N}_{t}
by the empirical measure map

(v_{1}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}...\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}v_{n})\mapsto\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\delta_{v_{i}}
.
By
[
20
, Theorem 5.3, (1)]
, we only have to show that
\hat{F}^{N}_{t}\rightharpoonup\pi_{t}
. But since
\mathcal{L}(\mu^{N})\rightharpoonup\pi
by definition, we obtain the desired convergence by pushing forward
\mathcal{L}(\mu^{N})
and
\pi
by the evaluation map at
t
(which is continuous from
C([0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
to
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
).
∎

The first estimate we pass to the infinite-dimension limit is the energy conservation:

Proposition 5.2
.

For any
t\in[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]
,
\pi_{t}
has finite mean second moment, bounded by the initial energy:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|v|^{2}\pi^{1}_{t}(dv)\leq E_{0}.

Proof.

Using Proposition
3.2
and symmetry, for any
t

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|v|^{2}F^{N:1}_{t}(dv)=\frac{1}{N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|V|^{2}F^{N}_{t}(dV)\leq E_{0}.

The portemanteau theorem applied to the bounded below and lower semi-continuous function
v\mapsto|v|^{2}
yields the result since
F^{N:1}_{t}\rightharpoonup\pi_{t}^{1}
by Proposition
5.1
.
∎

Remark 5.3
.

We call the quantity in the Proposition above
mean second moment
because if
f_{t}
is a
\pi_{t}
-distributed random variable, then

\mathbb{E}\left[\int|v|^{2}f_{t}(dv)\right]=\int|v|^{2}\mathbb{E}[f_{t}](dv)=\int|v|^{2}\int_{\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\rho\pi_{t}(d\rho)dv=\int|v|^{2}\pi^{1}_{t}(dv).

More generally, we will refer to integrals against
\pi
as mean quantities. They are also sometimes called
level-3
quantities in the literature.

We now want to pass to the limit in the entropy, the Fisher information and their production/dissipation terms. We begin by rewriting the entropy dissipation in a more concise form and define an associated functional. For a symmetric non-negative function
F
on
\mathbb{R}^{3N}
(
N\geq 2
), we let:

D^{N}(F):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}\alpha^{N}a(v_{1}-v_{2}):\left[(\nabla_{1}-\nabla_{2})\log{F}\right]^{\otimes 2}\ FdV\mathchar 59\relax

(26)

so that Proposition
3.3
rewrites:

H(F^{N}_{t})+\int_{0}^{t}D^{N}(F^{N}_{s})ds\leq H_{0}.

(27)

We also define
D
as
D^{N}
but with
\alpha
instead of
\alpha^{N}
, that is, without cut-off. We will see below that these functionals are convex, lower semi-continuous, and super-additive. As these properties are shared with the entropy and Fisher information, that makes
D
a good candidate to behave nicely in infinite dimension, as do
H
and
I
.

We now turn to the dissipation of Fisher information. We define the derivation operator

\partial^{N}_{b_{k}}:=|v_{1}-v_{2}|^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\alpha^{N}(|v_{1}-v_{2}|))^{\frac{1}{4}}\tilde{b}_{k}(v_{1}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}v_{2})\cdot\nabla_{12}\mathchar 59\relax

(28)

in which the vector-field is still divergence-free (it is a direct consequence of (
11
)). We also define the operator
\partial_{b_{k}}
as
\partial^{N}_{b_{k}}
but with
\alpha
instead of
\alpha^{N}
. With this derivation operator we can rewrite the Fisher dissipation (
13
) in a more concise form as

I(F^{N}_{t})+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{k=1}^{3}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}\left(\partial^{N}_{b_{k}}\partial^{N}_{b_{k}}\log F^{N}_{s}\right)^{2}F^{N}_{s}dVds\leq I_{0}\mathchar 59\relax

(29)

(using (
11
) to freely move
|v_{1}-v_{2}|^{-2}\alpha^{N}(|v_{1}-v_{2}|)
in and out of the derivation).

Contrary to the entropy production, the Fisher dissipation is actually not a nice functional as it is. We rather define

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{N}(F):=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}\frac{\left(\partial^{N}_{b_{k}}\partial^{N}_{b_{k}}F\right)^{2}}{F}dV

(30)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}\frac{\alpha^{N}(v_{1}-v_{2})}{|v_{1}-v_{2}|^{2}}\frac{\left((\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla)(\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla)F\right)^{2}}{F}dV\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}

which is better-behaved: it is convex, lower semi-continuous and super-additive, by Proposition
5.4
below. We also define a version without cut-off,
K
, with
\alpha
instead of
\alpha^{N}
(
i.e.
with
\partial_{b_{k}}
instead of
\partial^{N}_{b_{k}}
). It is shown in Appendix that it is comparable to the formulation with a
\log
: by Proposition
B.1
with
\beta=0
and
\partial=\partial^{N}_{b_{k}}
, we have

\frac{1}{4}\mathcal{K}^{N}(F)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}\left(\partial^{N}_{b_{k}}\partial^{N}_{b_{k}}\log F\right)^{2}FdV\leq\mathcal{K}^{N}(F).

Using the left hand side, the Fisher dissipation (
13
) now writes:

I(F^{N}_{t})+\frac{1}{16}\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{K}^{N}(F^{N}_{s})ds\leq I_{0}.

(31)

As explained in introduction, to obtain something in the
N\rightarrow\infty
limit, we need several key properties of the functionals
\mathcal{K}^{N}
and
D^{N}
, which are already known to hold for
H
and
I
. In particular, they are
super-additive
:

Proposition 5.4
.

For any
N\geq 2
, the functionals
D^{N}
and
\mathcal{K}^{N}
can be defined for any symmetric probability measure
F\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})
, and are convex and lower semi-continuous for the weak convergence of probability measures. They are super-additive: for any
2\leq j\leq N
, if
F^{:j}
is the
j
-th marginal of a symmetric
F\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})
, then

\displaystyle D^{j}(F^{:j})

\displaystyle\leq D^{N}(F)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{j}(F^{:j})

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{K}^{N}(F).

Proof.

We follow the proof of
[
20
, Lemmas 3.5 and 3.7]
which is for the Fisher information.
We begin with
D^{N}
.
Using that
a=\sum_{k=1}^{3}b_{k}\otimes b_{k}
, the integrand rewrites:

\displaystyle\alpha^{N}a(v_{1}-v_{2}):\left[(\nabla_{1}-\nabla_{2})\log{F}\right]^{\otimes 2}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{N}(|v_{1}-v_{2}|)\sum_{k=1}^{3}\left|b_{k}\cdot(\nabla_{1}-\nabla_{2})\log{F}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{N}(|v_{1}-v_{2}|)\sum_{k=1}^{3}\left|\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla_{12}\log{F}\right|^{2}.

For any three smooth functions
\psi_{k}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3N}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbb{R})
, we have

\sum_{k=1}^{3}\left|\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla_{12}\log{F}\right|^{2}\geq\sum_{k=1}^{3}\left(\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla_{12}\log{F}\right)\psi_{k}-\frac{|\psi_{k}|^{2}}{4}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}

so that

\displaystyle D^{N}(F)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}\alpha^{N}(|v_{1}-v_{2}|)\left(\left(\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla_{12}\log{F}\right)\psi_{k}-\frac{|\psi_{k}|^{2}}{4}\right)F

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}\alpha^{N}(|v_{1}-v_{2}|)\left(\left(\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla_{12}F\right)\psi_{k}-\frac{|\psi_{k}|^{2}}{4}F\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}\alpha^{N}(|v_{1}-v_{2}|)\left(-\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla_{12}\psi_{k}-\frac{|\psi_{k}|^{2}}{4}\right)F

the last identity holding by integration by parts, and using that the gradient of
\alpha^{N}
is parallel to
v_{1}-v_{2}
so vanishes against
\tilde{b}_{k}
.
Using smooth
\psi_{k}
approximating
2\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla_{12}\log{F}
, we can saturate this inequality, so that

D^{N}(F)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{3}\sup_{\psi_{k}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3N}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbb{R})}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}\alpha^{N}(|v_{1}-v_{2}|)\left(-\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla_{12}\psi_{k}-\frac{|\psi_{k}|^{2}}{4}\right)F.

This formula makes sense in
[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}+\infty]
for any probability measure
F\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})
, since the integrals are against continuous bounded functions.

Since
D^{N}
is the pointwise supremum of linear continuous functionals, it is convex and lower semi-continuous. Restricting the supremum to
\psi_{k}
depending only on
v_{1}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}...\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}v_{j}
, we get

\displaystyle D^{N}(F)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{3}\sup_{\psi_{k}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3j}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbb{R}^{3})}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}\alpha^{N}(|v_{1}-v_{2}|)\left(-\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla_{12}\psi_{k}-\frac{|\psi_{k}|^{2}}{4}\right)F^{:j}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{3}\sup_{\psi_{k}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3j}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbb{R}^{3})}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3j}}\alpha^{j}(|v_{1}-v_{2}|)\left(-\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla_{12}\psi_{k}-\frac{|\psi_{k}|^{2}}{4}\right)F^{:j}

\displaystyle=D^{j}(F^{:j})\mathchar 59\relax

using that
\alpha^{N}\geq\alpha^{j}
for
j\leq N
(we defined the regularization precisely to satisfy this compatibility).

We now turn to
\mathcal{K}^{N}
. Similarly, for any three smooth functions
\psi_{k}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{N}(F)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{k=1}^{3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}\frac{\alpha^{N}(|v_{1}-v_{2}|)}{|v_{1}-v_{2}|^{2}}\left(\frac{\left((\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla_{12})(\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla_{12})F\right)\psi_{k}}{F}-\frac{|\psi_{k}|^{2}}{4}\right)F

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{k=1}^{3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}\frac{\alpha^{N}(|v_{1}-v_{2}|)}{|v_{1}-v_{2}|^{2}}\left((\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla_{12})(\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla_{12})\psi_{k}-\frac{|\psi_{k}|^{2}}{4}\right)F\mathchar 59\relax

so that taking the supremum

\mathcal{K}^{N}(F)=\sum_{k=1}^{3}\sup_{\psi_{k}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3N}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbb{R})}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}\frac{\alpha^{N}(|v_{1}-v_{2}|)}{|v_{1}-v_{2}|^{2}}\left((\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla_{12})(\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla_{12})\psi_{k}-\frac{|\psi_{k}|^{2}}{4}\right)F.

From there we proceed exactly as for
D^{N}
and the proof is concluded.
∎

Remark 5.5
.

From the proof, we see that the super-additivity and convexity hold for the functionals without cut-off,
\mathcal{K}
and
D
. For the lower semi-continuity, we used that
\alpha^{N}
was continuous and bounded which is not the case of
\alpha
. However, semi-continuity is obtained by remarking that
\mathcal{K}
and
D
are a supremum of l.s.c. functions, by letting the regularization vanish: the formula

D(F)=\sup_{j}\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}\alpha^{j}a(v_{1}-v_{2}):\left[(\nabla_{1}-\nabla_{2})\log{F}\right]^{\otimes 2}\ FdV\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}

holds by monotone convergence in
j
, and similarly for
\mathcal{K}
.

As discussed in the introduction, the second property that goes hand in hand with super-additivity is the affinity in infinite dimension. Indeed, taking the example of the entropy, what we easily control is
H(\pi^{j}_{t})
since
\pi^{j}_{t}
is the weak limit of
F^{{N:j}_{t}}
, the entropy of which we control by
H(F^{{N}_{t}})
by super-additivity. The next result shows that such a control at the level of
\pi^{j}_{t}
for all

is equivalent to a control of the expectation with respect to
\pi_{t}
:

Theorem 5.6
.

Let
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
have finite mean Fisher information, i.e. such that

\sup_{j\geq 1}I(\nu_{j})<+\infty\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}

and have finite mean second moment

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|v|^{2}\nu_{1}(dv)<+\infty.

Then it holds that

\int_{\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}H(\rho)\nu(d\rho)=\lim_{j\geq 1}H(\nu^{j})=\sup_{j\geq 1}H(\nu_{j})\mathchar 59\relax

\int_{\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}I(\rho)\nu(d\rho)=\lim_{j\geq 1}I(\nu^{j})=\sup_{j\geq 1}I(\nu_{j})\mathchar 59\relax

\int_{\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}D(\rho\otimes\rho)\nu(d\rho)=\lim_{j\geq 2}D^{j}(\nu^{j})=\sup_{j\geq 2}D^{j}(\nu_{j})\mathchar 59\relax

\lim_{j\geq 2}\mathcal{K}^{j}(\nu^{j})\leq\int_{\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\mathcal{K}(\rho\otimes\rho)\nu(d\rho)\leq 16\lim_{j\geq 2}\mathcal{K}^{j}(\nu^{j})\mathchar 59\relax

and the limit is also a supremum in this last formula.

Proof.

The theorem is already known to hold for the entropy and Fisher information
[
20
,
36
]
. The proof for
D
and
\mathcal{K}
can be found in Appendix
C
.
∎

Remark 5.7
.

This theorem can be interpreted as an affinity result: the integral expressions are obviously affine in
\nu
, meaning that the convex functionals
H\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}I\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}D\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathcal{K}
all become affine in infinite dimension, rather than just staying convex (as a supremum of convex functions).

Theorem
5.6
is the keystone to pass our estimates on the
N
-particle system to the infinite-dimension limit, to obtain
mean
(or
level-3
) estimates:

Proposition 5.8
.

The following two estimates hold for all
t\in[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]
:

\int_{\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}H(\rho)\pi_{t}(d\rho)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}D(\rho\otimes\rho)\pi_{s}(d\rho)ds\leq H_{0}\mathchar 59\relax

(32)

and

\int_{\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}I(\rho)\pi_{t}(d\rho)+\frac{1}{256}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\mathcal{K}(\rho\otimes\rho)\pi_{s}(d\rho)ds\leq I_{0}.

(33)

Proof.

The proof is identical for both estimates, starting respectively from (
27
) and (
31
). We prove the second one.
Let
j\leq N
, by super-additivity (see Proposition
5.4
) we have:

I(F^{N:j}_{t})+\frac{1}{16}\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{K}^{j}(F^{N:j}_{s})ds\leq I_{0}

We take the
\liminf
in
N
(along the common subsequence for which
F^{N:j}_{s}\rightharpoonup\pi^{j}_{s}
for all
s
), together with Fatou’s lemma and lower semi-continuity of
I
and
K^{j}
, to obtain:

I(\pi^{j}_{t})+\frac{1}{16}\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{K}^{j}(\pi^{j}_{s})ds\leq I_{0}.

We take the limit in
j
, by monotone convergence in the integral (since
(K^{j}(\pi^{j}_{s}))_{j}
is increasing by super-additivity), we get

\lim_{j}I(\pi^{j}_{t})+\frac{1}{16}\int_{0}^{t}\lim_{j}\mathcal{K}^{j}(\pi^{j}_{s})ds\leq I_{0}.

Using Theorem
5.6
, we obtain the result.
∎

We can formulate these estimates in terms of random variables rather than laws, leading to some almost sure regularity of
\pi
-distributed random variables:

Proposition 5.9
.

Recall that
\pi\in\mathcal{P}\left(C([0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))\right)
is a cluster point of
\left(\mathcal{L}\left(\mu^{N}\right)\right)_{N\geq 2}
. Let
f
be a
\pi
-distributed random variable. Then, almost surely, the following quantities are finite:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{cccc}\int_{0}^{T}H(f_{t})dt\mathchar 59\relax&\int_{0}^{T}I(f_{t})dt\mathchar 59\relax&\int_{0}^{T}D(f_{t}\otimes f_{t})dt\mathchar 59\relax&\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{K}(f_{t}\otimes f_{t})dt.\end{array}

Proof.

We can rewrite (
32
) as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[H(f_{t})+\int_{0}^{t}D(f_{s}\otimes f_{s})ds\right]\leq H_{0}.

(34)

It is classical
[
20
, Lemma 3.1]
that the energy
E(f)=\int|v|^{2}f(dv)
bounds the entropy from below: for some
C>0
,

H(f)\geq-C(1+E(f)).

Hence
\mathbb{E}[H(f_{T})]\geq-C(1+\mathbb{E}[E(f_{T})])\geq-C(1+E_{0})
by Proposition
5.2
and Remark
5.3
. Hence
\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}D(f_{s}\otimes f_{s})ds\right]
is finite, meaning that the quantity inside the expectation is almost surely finite.

Similarly

\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{K}(f_{t}\otimes f_{t})dt
is a.s. finite by using (
33
) and that
I(f_{T})\geq 0
. For
I
and
H
, we integrate over
[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]
the estimates (
32
) and (
33
), and drop the nonnegative dissipation terms.
∎

Remark 5.10
.

The equation (
34
) is exactly the H-theorem in expectancy:
H(f_{t})+\int_{0}^{t}D(f_{s}\otimes f_{s})ds\leq H_{0}
is the formal apriori estimate on the entropy that stems from the Landau equation (
1
).

Remark 5.11
.

Even if
\mathbb{E}(H(f_{t}))
(as well as
\mathbb{E}(I(f_{t}))
) are bounded in time, we have little hope to recover
(H(f_{t}))_{t}\in L^{\infty}([0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T])
because it would require bounding
\mathbb{E}(\sup_{[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]}H(f_{t}))
, with the supremum inside the expectation, a quantity that we cannot easily relate to the particle system. A lot of the difficulties we will encounter when showing that
(f_{t})_{t}
is the strong solution
(g_{t})_{t}
stem from this issue.

In the next section, we will use Proposition
5.9
for the Fisher information to show that
f
is almost surely a weak solution of the Landau equation. The last section will use the estimates on
D
and
\mathcal{K}
to (essentially) show that
f\in L^{1}([0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
, which will allow us to claim uniqueness.

6.  The cluster points only charge almost sure weak solutions

Recall that
\pi\in\mathcal{P}\left(C([0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))\right)
is a cluster point of
\left(\mathcal{L}\left(\mu^{N}\right)\right)_{N\geq 2}
. In this section we show that a
\pi
-distributed random variable is almost surely a weak solution of the Landau equation.

We make our notion of weak solution precise. This is particularly important as we will manipulate another formulation later on.

Definition 6.1
.

A
weak solution
to the Landau equation on
[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]
with initial data
g_{0}
is a function

f=(f_{t})_{t}\in L^{1}([0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]\mathchar 59\relax L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))\cap L^{\infty}([0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]\mathchar 59\relax L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))

for some
p>\frac{3}{\gamma+5}
such that, for any
\varphi\in C^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, for any
t\in[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]
:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\varphi(v)f_{t}(v)dv

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\varphi(v)g_{0}(v)dv

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}b(v-w)\cdot(\nabla\varphi(v)-\nabla\varphi(w))\ f_{s}(dv)f_{s}(dw)\ ds

(35)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\alpha a(v-w):\nabla^{2}\varphi(v)\ f_{s}(dv)f_{s}(dw)\ ds\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}

where we recall that
b(z)=\nabla\cdot(\alpha a(z))=-2\alpha(|z|)z
.

Remark 6.2
.

This definition is mainly taken from Guerin and Fournier
[
18
, Definition 1.2]
, because we want in the end to apply their uniqueness result
[
18
]
for
\gamma\in(-3\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}-2)
, and
[
16
]
for
\gamma=-3
. The only difference is that we symmetrized the

\nabla\varphi(v)
by
(\nabla\varphi(v)-\nabla\varphi(w))
. One can check that it allows one to make sense of every term in (

(it will be done in the proof of Lemma
6.10
below). Without symmetry, one needs
p>\frac{3}{\gamma+4}
to make sense of the
b

[
18
,
16
]
at the end of this work, we will have already shown that
L^{1}([0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]\mathchar 59\relax L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
, so that the unsymmetrized formulation is also properly defined.

In
[
18
, Definition 1.2]
, the authors also require the energy to remain bounded, which is in fact not needed for
\gamma\leq-2
. However, we will show later that the conservation of energy holds anyway (this is done in Proposition
7.14
).

The goal of this section is to prove the following:

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
f=(f_{t})_{t}
be a
\pi
-distributed random variable. Then, almost surely,

f\in L^{1}([0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]\mathchar 59\relax L^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))

and
f
is a weak solution of the Landau equation with initial data
g_{0}
, in the sense of Definition
6.1
.

We fix a
\pi
-distributed random variable
f
and cut the proof of Proposition
6.3
into several lemmas. We begin by showing the claimed regularity of
f
:

Lemma 6.4
.

Almost surely,

f\in L^{1}([0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]\mathchar 59\relax L^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))\cap L^{\infty}([0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]\mathchar 59\relax L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})).

Proof.

By Proposition
5.9
, almost surely, the Fisher information of
f
is integrable in time:
I(f_{t})\in L^{1}([0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T])
. This means that for almost all

,
f_{t}
has finite Fisher information. It hence admits a density (still denoted
f_{t}
). Since
f_{t}
is a probability measure for all
t
, this implies
f\in L^{\infty}([0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]\mathchar 59\relax L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
.

Since
I(f)=4\|\nabla\sqrt{f}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}
, by the Sobolev embedding
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\hookrightarrow L^{6}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
in three dimensions, we have

f\in L^{1}([0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]\mathchar 59\relax L^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}

as desired.
∎

We then deal with the initial time.

Lemma 6.5
.

Almost surely,
f_{0}=g_{0}
.

Proof.

Step 1.
We show that the random initial empirical measures

A priori, the event of probability

∎

\displaystyle(\mathbb{E}|\mathcal{B}|)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{B}^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{N^{2}(N-1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}j=1\\
i<j\end{subarray}}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\psi^{N}_{ij}(s)\cdot\sigma^{N}\left(V^{N}_{i}(s)-V^{N}_{j}(s)\right)dB^{N}_{ij}(s)\right]^{2}

Remark 6.12

At this point, if we knew a uniqueness theorem for the Landau equation in the class

]
require higher integrability in space, going all the way to

Even if we managed to show that

In the next final section, we will use the -yet unexploited- dissipation of the Fisher information to essentially show that

(g_{t})_{t}
. In other words,
\pi=\delta_{(g_{t})_{t}}
.

Remark 7.2
.

The strategy we will follow is by now classical in the study of the Landau equation: Desvillettes
[
13
]
showed that
D(f\otimes f)
, which is a partial Fisher information in the directions
(\tilde{b}_{k})_{k=1\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}2\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}3}
, controls a full (weighted) Fisher information of
f
. Ji
[
24
]
proved a second order version of this result, showing that a functional that is very similar to
\mathcal{K}(f\otimes f)
(but with second derivatives in more directions) controls a full (weighted) second order Fisher information
\int f\langle v\rangle^{\gamma-2}|\nabla^{2}\log f|^{2}
. We were not able to apply this strategy to
\mathcal{K}
itself, but there exists a first order term
J\leq\mathcal{K}
for which we can do so, and it is enough to conclude.

The estimates cited above hold under the assumption that
f
has finite entropy (and energy), with a constant exponential in
H
. Even if this is to be expected for any reasonable solution of the Landau equation, we do not have a uniform-in-time bound on the entropy of
f
: it is not known to be smooth enough for the H-theorem to hold. Using the strategy introduced in
[
17
]
, we will replace the entropy of
f
by a weaker quantity measuring that the mass of
f
is not too concentrated (i.e. not distributed along a single line). For this quantity uniform bounds are achievable for short times by continuity arguments.

7.1.  A conditional

We show that starting from (

We begin by isolating a first order term from

Lemma 7.3
.

Let

\mathcal{J}(f):=\sum_{k=1}^{3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}f(v)f(w)\frac{\alpha(v-w)}{|v-w|^{2}}\left((\tilde{b}_{k}(v-w)\cdot\nabla)\log(f(v)f(w))\right)^{4}dvdw.

\mathcal{K}=\sum_{k=1}^{3}K_{1}^{\partial_{b_{k}}}\geq\sum_{k=1}^{3}\frac{4}{9}J^{\partial_{b_{k}}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}

Lemma 7.4

The following inequality holds:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(f)

\displaystyle\geq\mathcal{J}_{1}(f)-0.99^{\gamma}\cdot 432I(f)\mathchar 59\relax

Remark 7.5
.

The cut-off potential is only introduced so that it is possible to bound the error term

. The same is done in
[

,
24
]
to prove similar estimates.

One would like to claim that
\mathcal{J}_{1}(f)
controls
\int f\langle v\rangle^{l}|\nabla_{v}\log f|^{4}dv
for some weight

. The classical way to show this would be to use the finite entropy of

to say that

cannot be too concentrated, so that the integral over

makes the vector
b_{k}(v-w)
span all directions. However, we cannot show better than
H(f_{t})\in L^{1}([0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T])
a.s., which is not enough. To circumvent the use of the entropy, we will apply the technique developed in
[

based on the notion of triplets of
\delta
-non-aligned points:

Definition 7.6
.

[

Let

and, if
p_{(v_{2}-v_{1})^{\perp}}

(v_{2}-v_{1})^{\perp}

|p_{(v_{2}-v_{1})^{\perp}}(v_{3}-v_{1})|\geq 24\delta+2\sqrt{\delta}|v_{3}-v_{1}|.

If a probability

\delta
-non-aligned points. If it has finite energy, we can also ensure that most of the mass of

as a suitable generalization of the entropy.
We fix a smooth bump function

such that

on

.

[

\delta

Lemma 7.9

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{3}\left(b_{k}(v-w)\cdot\xi\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{\bar{\alpha}(v-w)}{|v-w|^{2}}\left(b_{k}(v-w)\cdot\xi\right)^{4}\mu(dw)\geq

Lemma 7.11
.

Let

. There exists
c_{p}>0
such that, for any

\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\leq c_{p}\left[1+\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\langle v\rangle^{\gamma-2}f(v)\left|\nabla\log f(v)\right|^{4}dv\right)^{\frac{p}{4}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\langle v\rangle^{\frac{p(2-\gamma)}{4-p}}f(v)dv\right)^{1-\frac{p}{4}}\right]\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}

is in fact continuous on

Proof.

We assume the right-hand side finite, which implies
f
has a density.
We use Hölder inequality to get:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(v)

\displaystyle\left|\nabla\log f(v)\right|^{p}dv

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\langle v\rangle^{\gamma-2}f(v)\left|\nabla\log f(v)\right|^{4}dv\right)^{\frac{p}{4}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\langle v\rangle^{\frac{p(2-\gamma)}{4-p}}f(v)dv\right)^{\frac{4-p}{4}}.

We rewrite the left-hand-side as a proper Sobolev seminorm:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(v)\left|\nabla\log f(v)\right|^{p}dv=p^{p}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\left|\nabla f^{\frac{1}{p}}\right|^{p}dv=p^{p}\|\nabla f^{\frac{1}{p}}\|_{L^{p}}^{p}.

(of

) embeds into continuous bounded functions and

\|f^{\frac{1}{p}}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{p}\leq c_{p}\|f^{\frac{1}{p}}\|_{W^{1\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}p}}^{p}=c_{p}\left(\|f^{\frac{1}{p}}\|^{p}_{L^{p}}+\|\nabla f^{\frac{1}{p}}\|_{L^{p}}^{p}\right).

But
\|f^{\frac{1}{p}}\|^{p}_{L^{p}}=1

is a probability, so

\|f\|_{L^{\infty}}=\|f^{\frac{1}{p}}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{p}\leq c_{p}\left(1+\|\nabla f^{\frac{1}{p}}\|_{L^{p}}^{p}\right)

and chaining the inequalities above we get the result.

7.2.  Propagating moments

The propagation of moments for the Landau equation was shown in

relies on both the "usual" weak formulation of the Landau equation (the one we used up to now) and the H-formulation introduced by Villani

for H-solutions, and we have yet to show that it holds. For a test function

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\varphi(v)f_{t}(v)dv-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}

\displaystyle\varphi(v)f_{0}(v)dv

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}(\nabla_{v}

\displaystyle\varphi(v)-\nabla_{w}\varphi(w))

(52)

\displaystyle\cdot\alpha a(v-w)(\nabla_{v}-\nabla_{w})\log(f_{s}(v)f_{s}(w))f_{s}(dv)f_{s}(dw)ds.

The right-hand side of this formula only makes sense using the entropy production
[
43

by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}

\displaystyle(\nabla_{v}\varphi(v)-\nabla_{w}\varphi(w))\cdot\alpha a(v-w)(\nabla_{v}-\nabla_{w})\log(f_{s}(v)f_{s}(w))f_{s}(v)f_{s}(w)\ dvdwds\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}|\nabla_{v}\varphi(v)-\nabla_{w}\varphi(w)|^{2}|v-w|^{\gamma+2}f_{s}(v)f_{s}(w)\ dvdwds\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\cdot\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\alpha a(v-w):\left((\nabla_{v}-\nabla_{w})\log(f_{s}(v)f_{s}(w))\right)^{\otimes 2}f_{s}(v)f_{s}(w)\ dvdwds\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathchar 59\relax

but the very last term is exactly (two times) the entropy production

. The other term can be shown to be finite using the

regularity of
\varphi
, see
[
43
, Equation (42)]
for details. The equivalence between the H-formulation (

) and the usual weak formulation is simply an integration by parts (which require some care for rough functions, but that can be made rigorous using finiteness of the entropy dissipation). It leads to:

Lemma 7.12
.

Almost surely, the H-formulation (
7.2
) for the random variable
(f_{t})_{t}
holds for any test function
\varphi\in C^{2}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
.

Proof.

Almost surely, the weak formulation holds for
(f_{t})_{t}
.
Also, from Proposition
5.9
, the entropy production is a.s. in
L^{1}([0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T])
, that is to say

\int_{0}^{T}D(f_{t}\otimes f_{t})dt<+\infty.

Hence we can make sense of the right-hand side in (

), which we call below the (H-formulation with
\alpha
).

We cut
\alpha=\alpha_{1}^{\epsilon}+\alpha_{2}^{\epsilon}
into a small singular part
\alpha_{2}^{\epsilon}
with support in
B(0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\epsilon)
for some
\epsilon>0
, and a smooth
\alpha_{1}^{\epsilon}
. Since the formulations are linear with respect to
\alpha
, we can write

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}

\displaystyle\varphi(v)f_{t}(v)dv-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\varphi(v)f_{0}(v)dv

\displaystyle=(\text{weak formulation with }\alpha_{1}^{\varepsilon})+(\text{weak formulation with }\alpha_{2}^{\varepsilon})

and

\displaystyle(\text{H-formulation with }\alpha)=(\text{H-formulation with }\alpha_{1}^{\varepsilon})+(\text{H-formulation with }\alpha_{2}^{\varepsilon}).

By the arguments of
[
43
, Section 6]
(more precisely, the paragraph with equation (52) therein), the two formulations are equivalent far from the singularity (the idea is to approximate
f(v)f(w)
by smooth functions, for which the formal integration by parts is rigorous, and we can then pass to the limit
because
the singularity is avoided, see
[
43
]
for details). In other terms, for any
\epsilon>0
, the parts of each formulation with
\alpha_{1}^{\epsilon}
coincide. But the parts with
\alpha_{2}^{\epsilon}
vanish as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, by dominated convergence (dominating
\alpha_{2}^{\epsilon}
by
\alpha
), because the full formulations are known to be finite. We conclude that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\varphi(v)f_{t}(v)dv-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}

\displaystyle\varphi(v)f_{0}(v)dv=(\text{H-formulation with }\alpha)

as wanted.
∎

Remark 7.13
.

We emphasize that we have not proven that
(f_{t})_{t}
is a H-solution in the sense of
[
43
]
because it does not a priori satisfy the H-theorem

H(f_{t})+\int_{0}^{t}D(f_{s}\otimes f_{s})ds\leq H(f_{0})\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}

which is part of the definition of H-solutions. The definition also includes that the energy is conserved, which we do not know yet but will be able to show it below. If we knew that
(f_{t})_{t}
was a H-solution, we could apply the uniqueness result
[
40
]
and directly conclude that
(f_{t})_{t}=(g_{t})_{t}
. We only know the finiteness of
\int_{0}^{t}D(f_{s}\otimes f_{s})ds
(but with no quantitative bound), which is just enough for making sense of the formulation.

Also, without finiteness of
\int_{0}^{t}D(f_{s}\otimes f_{s})ds
, we cannot a priori use the H-formulation, and the dominated convergence in the proof above does not hold. In other terms, we did not show that any weak solution satisfies the H-formulation.

Knowing that we can rely on both formulations, we can prove the propagation of moments. We begin with the conservation of energy:

Lemma 7.14
.

Almost surely, for all times
t\in[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|v|^{2}f_{t}(v)dv=E_{0}.

Proof.

Step 1.
We begin by showing that the energy remains bounded by a Gronwall argument. Let

E(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|v|^{2}f_{t}(v)dv.

By Lemma
7.12
, almost surely,
f
satisfies the H-formulation.
Consider a smooth radially decreasing
\chi
, such that
0\leq\chi\leq 1
, and which equals
1
on
B(0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}1)
and vanishes outside
B(0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}2)
. Fix a radius
R>0
and consider the test function

\varphi_{R}(v):=\chi\left(\frac{v}{R}\right)|v|^{2}.

We can plug it in the H-formulation.
We compute

\nabla_{v}\varphi_{R}(v)=2v\chi\left(\frac{v}{R}\right)+\frac{1}{R}\nabla\chi\left(\frac{v}{R}\right)|v|^{2}.

For
(v\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}w)\in B(0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}R)^{2}
,

\nabla_{v}\varphi_{R}(v)-\nabla_{w}\varphi_{R}(w)=2(v-w)\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}

so since
a(v-w)(v-w)=0
, this disappears in the H-formulation (
7.2
) and we are only left with the integral over the complement
(B(0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}R)^{2})^{c}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\varphi_{R}(v)f_{t}(v)dv-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}

\displaystyle\varphi_{R}(v)f_{0}(v)dv

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\mathbf{1}_{(B(0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}R)^{2})^{c}}\left(\nabla_{v}\varphi_{R}(v)-\nabla_{w}\varphi_{R}(w)\right)

(53)

\displaystyle\cdot\alpha a(v-w)(\nabla_{v}-\nabla_{w})\log(f_{s}(v)f_{s}(w))f_{s}(dv)f_{s}(dw)ds.

We have for any
t\in[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\varphi_{R}(v)f_{t}(v)dv\xrightarrow[R\rightarrow\infty]{}E(t)

by monotone convergence, since
\chi\left(\frac{v}{R}\right)
is increasing with

.

We now want to control the right-hand side of (

). By Cauchy-Schwarz and using

we can bound it by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\mathbf{1}_{(B(0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}R)^{2})^{c}}|\nabla_{v}\varphi_{R}(v)-\nabla_{w}\varphi_{R}(w)|^{2}|v-w|^{\gamma+2}

\displaystyle f_{s}(v)f_{s}(w)\ dvdwds

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}D(f_{s}\otimes f_{s})ds.

(54)

As we already used in the previous lemma, the second integral is a.s. a finite constant (Proposition
5.9
).
We differentiate again

\nabla^{2}_{v}\varphi_{R}(v)=2\chi\left(\frac{v}{R}\right)\Id+\frac{2}{R}v\otimes\nabla\chi\left(\frac{v}{R}\right)+\frac{2}{R}\nabla\chi\left(\frac{v}{R}\right)\otimes v+\frac{1}{R^{2}}\nabla^{2}\chi\left(\frac{v}{R}\right)|v|^{2}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}

which is bounded uniformly in
v
by some
C_{\chi}

independent
of

, since
|v|\leq 2R
on the support of
\chi
. Hence

\left|\nabla_{v}\varphi_{R}(v)-\nabla_{w}\varphi_{R}(w)\right|\leq C_{\chi}|v-w|.

So

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\mathbf{1}_{(B(0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}R)^{2})^{c}}

\displaystyle|\nabla_{v}\varphi_{R}(v)-\nabla_{w}\varphi_{R}(w)|^{2}|v-w|^{\gamma+2}f_{s}(v)f_{s}(w)\ dvdwds

\displaystyle\leq C_{\chi}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}|v-w|^{\gamma+4}f_{s}(v)f_{s}(w)dvdwds

\displaystyle\leq C_{\chi\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\gamma}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}(1+|v|^{2}+|w|^{2})f_{s}(v)f_{s}(w)

\displaystyle\leq C_{\chi\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\gamma}\int_{0}^{t}(1+2E(s))ds.

(55)

since

.
Hence remembering that (

) bounds the right-hand side of (

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\varphi_{R}(v)f_{t}(v)dv

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\varphi_{R}(v)f_{0}(v)dv

\displaystyle+C_{\chi\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\gamma}\left[\int_{0}^{t}(1+2E(s))ds+\int_{0}^{T}D(f_{s}\otimes f_{s})ds\right]

which yields as

By Gronwall’s lemma

remains bounded on
[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]
by some constant depending on the (random but a.s. finite) quantity

.

Step 2.
Now that we know that

is bounded on

As the right hand side of (

7.3.  Propagating that

To obtain that

\delta
-non-aligned balls. We can show that for small

is regular over this short time. Its entropy can hence only decrease and we can repeat the argument starting from

We begin this program by proving the Hölder continuity claimed above. Recall that

(defined just before Lemma

(f_{t})_{t\in[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\tau]}\in L^{1}([0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\tau]\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
holds when we apply the theorem, the unsymmetrized version makes sense and is equal to the symmetrized version.

8.  Conclusion

Proof of Theorem
1.2
and Corollary
1.5
.

We summarize the proof of Theorem
1.2
and Corollary
1.5
: Recall that

\mu^{N}_{t}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{V^{N}_{i}(t)}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3})

is the empirical measure of the particle system (
1.2
).

By Proposition
4.2
, the family
\left(\mathcal{L}\left((\mu^{N}_{t})_{t\in[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]}\right)\right)_{N\geq 2}
is tight in
\mathcal{P}\left(C\left([0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\right)\right)
.
By Proposition
7.1
, the only cluster point of this sequence is
\delta_{(g_{t})_{t\in[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}T]}}

By Proposition

Derivation of the Kolmogorov equation

In this section we provide the derivation of the Kolmogorov equation (

) from the particle system (
1.2
). We hence worked with
N\geq 2
fixed.
We rewrite (
1.2
) in a more canonical form as

d\mathbf{V}(t)=\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{V}(t))dt+\mathbf{\Sigma}(\mathbf{V}(t))d\mathbf{B}(t).

(58)

Here
\mathbf{V}(t)=(V^{N}_{1}(t)\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}...\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}V^{N}_{N}(t))\in\mathbb{R}^{3N}
, and the drift
\mathbf{b}(V)\in\mathbb{R}^{3N}
is defined as

\left[\mathbf{b}(V)\right]_{i}=\frac{2}{N-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{N}b^{N}(v^{i}-v^{j})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}

recalling that
b^{N}=\nabla\cdot(\alpha^{N}a)
(the bracket
[\cdot]_{i}
designating the components in the
i

\mathbb{R}^{3}

\mathbb{R}^{3N}=\mathbb{R}^{3}\times...\times\mathbb{R}^{3}

We also let
\mathbf{B}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3N(N-1)/2}
be the column vector of the
B^{N}_{ij}(t)
in lexicographic order:

\mathbf{B}(t)=(B^{N}_{12}(t)\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}B^{N}_{13}(t)\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}...\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}B^{N}_{1N}(t)\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}B^{N}_{23}(t)\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}...\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}B^{N}_{(N-1)N}(t))^{T}

and the matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}(V)
with
3N

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}=\mathbf{\Sigma}(V)=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{N-1}}\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccccccc}\sigma^{12}&...&\sigma^{1N}&0&...&0&0&...&0\\

with shorthand notation

(recall that
\sigma=\sqrt{\alpha^{N}a}
and note that
\sigma^{ij}=\sigma^{ji}
).

Consider a test function
\varphi(V)
, the Itô formula yields:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}(\varphi(\mathbf{V}(t)))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla\varphi(\mathbf{V}(t))\cdot\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{V}(t))+\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{2}\varphi(\mathbf{V}(t)):\mathbf{\Sigma}(\mathbf{V}(t))\mathbf{\Sigma}^{T}(\mathbf{V}(t))\right]\mathchar 59\relax

which, rewritten in terms of the law

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}\varphi(V)F^{N}_{t}(dV)

\partial_{t}F^{N}_{t}=-\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{b}F^{N}_{t})+\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{2}:(\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{T}F^{N}_{t})

We need to compute the

\mathbf{\Gamma}:=\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{T}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Gamma}=\frac{2}{N-1}

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{c c c}\sum_{k\neq 1}(\alpha^{N}a)^{1j}&0&0\\
0&\sum_{k\neq 2}(\alpha^{N}a)^{2j}&0\\
&...&\\
0&0&\sum_{k\neq N}(\alpha^{N}a)^{Nj}\end{array}\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{2}{N-1}\ \left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}0&(\alpha^{N}a)^{12}&...&(\alpha^{N}a)^{1N}\\
(\alpha^{N}a)^{21}&0&(\alpha^{N}a)^{23}&(\alpha^{N}a)^{2N}\\
&...&\\
(\alpha^{N}a)^{N1}&...&(\alpha^{N}a)^{N(N-1)}&0\end{array}\right]

(with
(\alpha^{N}a)^{ij}=\alpha^{N}(|v_{i}-v_{j}|)a(v_{i}-v_{j})
) or in coordinates, for
i\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}j\in\{1\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}...\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}N\}

[\mathbf{\Gamma}]_{ij}=\frac{2}{N-1}\left[\delta_{i=j}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq i\end{subarray}}^{N}(\alpha^{N}a)^{ik}-\delta_{i\neq j}(\alpha^{N}a)^{ij}\right].

We deduce the following relation

\displaystyle[\nabla\cdot\mathbf{\Gamma}]_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{N-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\nabla_{j}\cdot\left[\delta_{i=j}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq i\end{subarray}}^{N}(\alpha^{N}a)^{ik}-\delta_{i\neq j}(\alpha^{N}a)^{ij}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{N-1}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq i\end{subarray}}^{N}b^{N}(v_{i}-v_{k})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{N}b^{N}(v_{i}-v_{j})\right)

\displaystyle=2[\mathbf{b}(V)]_{i}.

Expanding the derivatives in (
59

\displaystyle\partial_{t}F^{N}_{t}=

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{b}F^{N}_{t})+\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{2}:(\mathbf{\Gamma}F^{N}_{t})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{b})F^{N}_{t}-\mathbf{b}\cdot\nabla F^{N}_{t}+\frac{1}{2}(\nabla^{2}:\mathbf{\Gamma})F^{N}_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\Gamma}:\nabla^{2}F^{N}_{t}+(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{\Gamma})\cdot\nabla F_{t}^{N}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}(\nabla^{2}:\mathbf{\Gamma})F^{N}_{t}-\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{\Gamma})\cdot\nabla F^{N}_{t}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}(\nabla^{2}:\mathbf{\Gamma})F^{N}_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\Gamma}:\nabla^{2}F^{N}_{t}+(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{\Gamma})\cdot\nabla F_{t}^{N}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{\Gamma})\cdot\nabla F^{N}_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\Gamma}:\nabla^{2}F^{N}_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{\Gamma}\nabla F^{N}_{t})

(60)

where we have used the relations
2\mathbf{b}=\nabla\cdot\mathbf{\Gamma}
and
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{b}=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla^{2}:\mathbf{\Gamma})
to go from the second line to the third. Notice that the last line provides an explicit expression of the Kolmogorov equation in divergence form.

To obtain (
9

\mathbf{\Gamma}
, differentiating between diagonal and non diagonal terms:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}F^{N}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(N-1)}\sum_{i\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}j=1}^{N}\nabla_{i}\cdot(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{ij}\nabla_{j}F^{N}_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(N-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\nabla_{i}\cdot(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{ii}\nabla_{i}F^{N}_{t})+\frac{1}{(N-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j\neq i}\nabla_{i}\cdot(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{ij}\nabla_{j}F^{N}_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(N-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j\neq i}\nabla_{i}\cdot((\alpha^{N}a)^{ij}\nabla_{i}F^{N}_{t})-\frac{1}{(N-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j\neq i}\nabla_{i}\cdot((\alpha^{N}a)^{ij}\nabla_{j}F^{N}_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(N-1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\mathchar 59\relax j=1\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{N}\nabla_{i}\cdot((\alpha^{N}a)^{ij}(\nabla_{i}F^{N}_{t}-\nabla_{j}F^{N}_{t}))

Symmetrizing the sum, and then indexing on ordered pairs, we get:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}F^{N}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2(N-1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\mathchar 59\relax j=1\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{N}(\nabla_{i}-\nabla_{j})\cdot((\alpha^{N}a)^{ij}(\nabla_{i}F^{N}_{t}-\nabla_{j}F^{N}_{t}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(N-1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\mathchar 59\relax j=1\\
i<j\end{subarray}}^{N}(\nabla_{i}-\nabla_{j})\cdot((\alpha^{N}a)^{ij}(\nabla_{i}F^{N}_{t}-\nabla_{j}F^{N}_{t}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(N-1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\mathchar 59\relax j=1\\
i<j\end{subarray}}^{N}Q_{ij}^{N}(F^{N}_{t})\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}

which is (

Appendix B
On the Fisher dissipation and auxiliary functionals

In this section we study different second order functionals related to the dissipation of the Fisher information and show how they compare to one another.

We place ourselves in a quite general context: let
b=b(v_{1}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}v_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
be a divergence-free vector field, we define the derivation operator

\partial=b\cdot\nabla_{12}

which acts on functions on
\mathbb{R}^{3N}
for any
N\geq 2
. Those hypotheses apply to the derivation operators

\partial_{b_{k}}^{N}=|v_{1}-v_{2}|^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\alpha^{N}(v_{1}-v_{2}))^{\frac{1}{4}}\tilde{b}_{k}(v_{1}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}v_{2})\cdot\nabla_{12}

that appear in the Fisher dissipation, or their analogues
\partial_{b_{k}}
defined with
\alpha
instead of
\alpha^{N}
, but also to the usual Euclidean partial derivatives with respect to the components of
v_{1}
(or
v_{2}
).

For any symmetric function
F:\mathbb{R}^{3N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
, the first order Fisher information along
\partial
satisfies

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}\frac{(\partial F)^{2}}{F}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}F(\partial\log F)^{2}=4\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}(\partial\sqrt{F})^{2}.

But we will see that the analogous second order Fisher informations are all different. Let us define

\displaystyle K^{\partial}_{1}(F)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}\frac{(\partial^{2}F)^{2}}{F}

\displaystyle K^{\partial}_{0}(F)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}F(\partial^{2}\log F)^{2}

\displaystyle K^{\partial}_{1/2}(F)

\displaystyle:=4\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}(\partial^{2}\sqrt{F})^{2}.

More generally for
\beta\in(0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}1]
we can define:

\displaystyle K^{\partial}_{\beta}(F)

\displaystyle:=\beta^{-2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}F^{1-2\beta}(\partial^{2}F^{\beta})^{2}

These functionals are all comparable to one another (excepted
K^{\partial}_{1/3}
), and the difference can be expressed in terms of the first order functional

J^{\partial}(F):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}F(\partial\log F)^{4}.

(61)

Moreover, at the exception of
K^{\partial}_{1/3}
, they all control a multiple of
J^{\partial}
. Indeed, we have:

Proposition B.1
.

For any divergence-free vector field
b(v_{1}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}v_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
and
\partial=b\cdot\nabla_{12}
, and for any
\beta\in[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}1]
:

\displaystyle K^{\partial}_{\beta}

\displaystyle=K^{\partial}_{1}+(\beta-1)(\beta+\frac{1}{3})J^{\partial}

\displaystyle=K^{\partial}_{1/3}+(\beta-\frac{1}{3})^{2}J^{\partial}\mathchar 59\relax

so that

\frac{9}{4}(\beta-\frac{1}{3})^{2}K^{\partial}_{1}\leq K_{\beta}^{\partial}\leq K_{1}^{\partial}.

Proof.

We omit the superscript
\partial
. For
\beta>0
, we write

K_{\beta}(F)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}F\left(\frac{\partial^{2}F^{\beta}}{\beta F^{\beta}}\right)^{2}

and use the identity

\frac{\partial^{2}F^{\beta}}{\beta F^{\beta}}=\frac{\partial^{2}F}{F}+(\beta-1)\frac{(\partial F)^{2}}{F^{2}}.

For
\beta=0
, it also holds that

\partial^{2}\log F=\frac{\partial^{2}F}{F}+(\beta-1)\frac{(\partial F)^{2}}{F^{2}}.

So, in both cases,

\displaystyle K_{\beta}(F)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}F\left(\frac{\partial^{2}F}{F}+(\beta-1)\frac{(\partial F)^{2}}{F^{2}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}\frac{(\partial^{2}F)^{2}}{F}+2(\beta-1)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}\frac{\partial^{2}F(\partial F)^{2}}{F^{2}}+(\beta-1)^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}\frac{(\partial F)^{4}}{F^{3}}.

Notice that the first integral is
K_{1}(F)
and the last integral is
J(F)
. Since
b
is divergence-free, we can integrate by parts with
\partial
. We have, by integrating
\partial^{2}F
by parts, that the middle term writes

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}\frac{\partial^{2}F(\partial F)^{2}}{F^{2}}=-2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}\frac{\partial^{2}F(\partial F)^{2}}{F^{2}}+2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}\frac{(\partial F)^{4}}{F^{3}}\mathchar 59\relax

so that it equals
\frac{2}{3}J^{N}(F)
. Hence

\displaystyle K_{\beta}(F)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N}}\frac{(\partial^{2}F)^{2}}{F}+\left(\frac{4}{3}(\beta-1)+(\beta-1)^{2}\right)J(F)

\displaystyle=K_{1}(F)+(\beta-1)(\beta+\frac{1}{3})J(F).

In particular,

K_{1/3}=K_{1}-\frac{4}{9}J
, so that

K_{\beta}=K_{1/3}+\left(\frac{4}{9}+(\beta-1)(\beta+\frac{1}{3})\right)J=K_{1/3}+(\beta-\frac{1}{3})^{2}J.

Finally,

\displaystyle\frac{9}{4}(\beta-\frac{1}{3})^{2}K_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{9}{4}(\beta-\frac{1}{3})^{2}\left(K_{\beta}+(1-\beta)(\beta+\frac{1}{3})J\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{9}{4}(\beta-\frac{1}{3})^{2}K_{\beta}+\frac{9}{4}(1-\beta)(\beta+\frac{1}{3})K_{\beta}

\displaystyle=K_{\beta}\mathchar 59\relax

because
(\beta-\frac{1}{3})^{2}J\leq K_{\beta}
.
∎

The functional
K_{1}^{\partial}
is the most convenient because it is convex lower-semi-continuous (for the weak convergence of probabilities) and super-additive because it admits a dual representation. Similarly, a Legendre-Fenchel transform yields the convex, l.s.c, super-additive behavior of
J^{\partial}
(properties that we will not use).

Another important functional is
K^{N}_{1/2}
because it fits nicely in the Hilbert structure in which lies
\sqrt{F}
. It allows us to setup the framework for the proof of the (approximate) affinity in infinite dimension: for
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))

\lim_{j}K_{1}(\pi_{j})\leq\int_{\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}K_{1}(\rho\otimes\rho)\pi(d\rho)\leq 16\lim_{j}K_{1}(\pi_{j}).

The first inequality is a direct consequence of the convexity of
K_{1}
, and the second is by adapting the proof from
[
36
]
(as we did for the entropy dissipation).

Appendix C
Infinite-dimension affinity of

\mathcal{K}

C.1.  Preliminaries and an easy inequality

In this section we prove Theorem
5.6
. The case of the entropy is well known
[
20
]
, and the case of the Fisher information can be found in
[
20
,
36
]
. Although this theorem plays a key role in the propagation of chaos, its proof is quite orthogonal to the rest of this work. It also requires a new framework, explaining why we isolated it in this appendix.

Most of our proof uses parts of the work by Rougerie
[

as a black box, but we will summarize it for the reader’s convenience. Below we prove one easy half of the theorem. We then introduce the Hilbert framework which will end in us proving an abstract version of the other half in Theorem

We fix

satisfying the hypothesis of Theorem
5.6

Lemma C.1
.

It holds that

\sup_{j\geq 2}D^{j}(\nu^{j})=\lim_{j\geq 2}D^{j}(\nu^{j})\leq\int_{\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}D(\rho\otimes\rho)\nu(d\rho)\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}

The proof is the same for both functionals, we treat the case of
D
. By super-additivity (Proposition
5.4
),
(D^{j}(\nu^{j}))_{j}
is increasing in

so the limit and the supremum are equal.
By convexity of
D^{j}
(still Proposition
5.4
) and Jensen inequality,

D^{j}(\nu^{j})=D^{j}\left(\int_{\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\rho^{\otimes j}\nu(d\rho)\right)\leq\int_{\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}D^{j}(\rho^{\otimes j})\nu(d\rho).

But
D^{j}(\rho^{\otimes j})\leq D(\rho^{\otimes j})
since
\alpha^{j}\leq\alpha
, and
D(\rho^{\otimes j})=D(\rho\otimes\rho)
by a direct computation.
∎

C.2.  The Hilbertian framework and proof for an abstract operator

The reverse inequality will require the introduction of an Hilbertian framework, following
[
36
]
. Recall that
I(\nu^{j})<+\infty
by hypothesis so that
\nu^{j}
admits a density, which we still denote by
\nu^{j}
. We define

\psi^{j}=\sqrt{\nu^{j}}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3j})

which satisfies
\|\psi^{j}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3j})}=1
. The reason for introducing the square roots is that our functionals admit nice expressions in terms of it (for instance, recall that the Fisher information
I(\nu^{j})=j^{-1}4\int|\nabla\psi^{j}|^{2}
). We let

\Gamma^{j}
be the orthogonal projection on the span of
\psi_{j}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3j})
. It is of course a finite rank operator.

We recall that the trace of an operator
A
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3j})
is defined by, when the formula makes sense,

\Tr(A):=\sum_{k}\langle e_{k}\mathchar 59\relax Ae_{k}\rangle

(62)

where
(e_{k})_{k}
is any orthonormal basis of the separable Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3j})
. The trace norm is

\|A\|_{\Tr}=\Tr((A^{*}A)^{\frac{1}{2}})\in[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}+\infty].

It is always well-defined because
(A^{*}A)^{\frac{1}{2}}
is a non-negative operator so the series (
62
) features only non-negative terms. We say that
A
is trace-class if its trace norm is finite (its trace
\Tr(A)
is then defined). The trace-class endowed with the trace-norm forms a Banach space and the duality bracket

(A\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}B)\mapsto\Tr(AB)

identifies the trace-class as the dual of compact operators, and as the predual of bounded operators.

Since
\Gamma^{j}
is of finite rank, it is trace class, and its trace is

\Tr(\Gamma^{j})=\|\Gamma^{j}\|_{\Tr}=\|\psi^{j}\|_{L^{2}}=1.

For trace-class operators, instead of taking marginals, we can consider partial traces. For
1\leq k\leq j
, the partial trace
\Gamma^{j:k}
of
\Gamma^{j}
is the operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3k})
defined by

\Tr(A\Gamma^{j:k})=\Tr((A\otimes\text{Id}^{\otimes(j-k)})\Gamma^{j})

for any bounded operator
A
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3k})
.

We have set up the needed definitions. Following
[
36
]
, we first build a sequence of operators akin to the sequence of probability measures
(\nu^{j})_{j}
:

Lemma C.2
.

Up to a subsequence in
j
that we omit to write (and which is independent of
k
), the weak-star convergence

\Gamma^{j:k}\xrightharpoonup[j\rightarrow\infty]{*}\gamma^{k}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}

holds, where
\gamma_{k}
is a trace class operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3k})
.

Proof.

By the Banach-Alaoglu theorem, the unit ball of the trace-class on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3k})
, which is the dual of compact operators, is compact for the weak-
*
topology, so that from the sequence
(\Gamma^{j:k})_{j}
we can extract a converging subsequence. A diagonal argument allows us to make this sequence independent of
k
.
∎

One can upgrade this convergence and show that the operators
\gamma_{k}
are compatible with each other:

Lemma C.3
.

The convergence
\Gamma^{j:k}\xrightarrow[j\rightarrow\infty]{}\gamma^{k}
holds in trace norm.
Moreover, for all
k\geq 1
,
\Tr(\gamma^{k})=1
, and the sequence
(\gamma_{k})_{k}
is compatible in the sense that

\gamma^{k+1:k}=\gamma^{k}
.

Proof.

The proof is done in [Paragraph 3.3,
[
36
]
], we summarize it for the sake of self-containedness. For any
i
, we denote by
\Delta_{i}
the Laplacian in the
v_{i}
variable. Let any
j\geq k
, by symmetry of
\psi^{j}
,

\displaystyle I(\nu^{j})

\displaystyle=4\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3j}}|\nabla_{1}\psi^{j}|^{2}

\displaystyle=4\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3j}}(-\Delta_{1}\psi^{j})\psi^{j}

\displaystyle=4\langle-\Delta_{1}\psi^{j}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\psi^{j}\rangle

by integration by parts, denoting with
\langle\cdot\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\cdot\rangle
the
L^{2}
scalar product.
Then, using symmetry again

\displaystyle\langle-\Delta_{1}\psi^{j}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\psi^{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\langle\sum_{i=1}^{k}(-\Delta_{i})\psi^{j}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\psi^{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\Tr\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}(-\Delta_{i})\Gamma^{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\Tr\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}(-\Delta_{i})\Gamma^{j:k}\right)

by definition of the partial trace. The same manipulation with the energy
|\cdot|^{2}
instead of the Laplacian yields

\Tr\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left((-\Delta_{i})+|v_{i}|^{2}+1\right)\Gamma^{j:k}\right)=\frac{k}{4}I(\nu^{j})+k\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(|v|^{2}+1)\nu^{1}(dv).

By hypothesis the right hand side is bounded uniformly in
j
. Let
L_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left((-\Delta_{i})+|x_{i}|^{2}+1\right)
, it is well known that this operator has compact resolvent. By cyclicity of the trace,

\sup_{j}\Tr\left(L_{k}^{1/2}\Gamma^{j:k}L_{k}^{1/2}\right)<+\infty\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}

so that we can further extract a weak-
*
convergent subsequence. By identifying the limit against smooth finite rank operators,

L_{k}^{1/2}\Gamma^{j:k}L_{k}^{1/2}\xrightharpoonup[j\rightarrow\infty]{*}L_{k}^{1/2}\gamma^{k}L_{k}^{1/2}.

By compactness of
L_{k}^{-1}
, we can use this operator to test the weak-
*
convergence:

1=\Tr(\Gamma^{j:k})=\Tr\left(L_{k}^{-1}L_{k}^{1/2}\Gamma^{j:k}L_{k}^{1/2}\right)\xrightarrow[j\rightarrow\infty]{}\Tr\left(L_{k}^{-1}L_{k}^{1/2}\gamma^{k}L_{k}^{1/2}\right)=\Tr(\gamma^{k})=1\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}

where cyclicity of the trace has been used twice. Hence the trace norm converges, which implies strong convergence of
\Gamma^{j:k}\rightarrow\gamma^{k}
.

The compatibility is an easy consequence: for any bounded operator
A
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3k})
,

\Tr(A\gamma^{k+1:k})=\Tr((A\otimes\Id)\gamma^{k+1})=\lim_{j}\Tr((A\otimes\Id)\Gamma^{j:k+1})=\lim_{j}\Tr(A\Gamma^{j:k})=\Tr(A\gamma^{k})\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}

the limits being justified because the bounded operators are the dual of the trace-class. This means
\gamma^{k+1:k}=\gamma^{k}
.
∎

Remark C.4
.

In the above proof, we used the formula

I(\nu^{j})=4\Tr(-\Delta_{1}\Gamma^{j}).

Written in terms of
\Gamma^{j}
, the Fisher information becomes not only convex but linear. This linearity in
\Gamma^{j}
is the cornerstone of the proof in
[
36
]
of Theorem
5.6
for the Fisher information. Basically, linearity allows us to exchange the Fisher information and the integral in the expression (
63
) below, with an equality rather than the inequality we obtain when using Jensen’s. We will generalize this, replacing
-\Delta_{1}
by an abstract operator.

The same way the family of compatible marginals
(\nu^{j})_{j}
are related to a overarching probability
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
which makes it able to write
\nu^{j}
as a superposition of tensorized probabilites
\rho^{\otimes j}
, the quantum de Finetti-Hewitt-Savage Theorem provides a probability over the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
which allows us to decompose
\gamma^{k}
as tensorized states:

Lemma C.5
.

There exists
\xi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{S}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})))
such that, for all
k\geq 1
:

\gamma^{k}=\int_{\mathbb{S}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))}p(u^{\otimes k})\xi(du)

(63)

where
p(\phi)
is the orthogonal projection on the span of
\phi\in\mathbb{S}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3k}))
, i.e.
p(\phi)=\phi\langle\phi\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\cdot\rangle
.
Moreover,
\xi
and
\nu
are connected as follows: if
\Theta:\mathbb{S}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
is the continuous map
\phi\mapsto\phi^{2}
, then
the pushforward of
\xi
is
\nu
:
\Theta\#\xi=\nu
.

Proof.

The probability
\xi
is given by applying the quantum de Finetti-Hewitt-Savage theorem
[
22
]
, the hypotheses holding by Lemma
C.3
. The link between
\xi
and
\nu
is provided by
[
36
, Lemma 3.2]
after recalling that a Borel probability measure over a metric space is entirely determined by the integral of continuous bounded functions. We reproduce the proof here:
it suffices to check

\int_{\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\Phi(\rho)\nu(d\rho)=\int_{\mathbb{S}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))}\Phi(\Theta(u))\xi(du)

for
\Phi
of the form
\Phi(\rho)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3k}}\rho^{\otimes k}\varphi(v_{1}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}...\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}v_{k})
, because these functions generate a dense sub-algebra of the continuous bounded functions over
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
(Those ’monomials’ can be found for instance in
[
31
]
). We then have, for any
j\geq k
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\Phi(\rho)\nu(d\rho)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3k}}\int_{\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\rho^{\otimes k}\nu(d\rho)\varphi

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3k}}\nu^{k}\varphi

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3j}}\nu^{j}\varphi

\displaystyle=\Tr((\varphi\otimes\Id^{\otimes(j-k)})\Gamma^{j})

\displaystyle=\Tr(\varphi\Gamma^{j:k})

Letting
j\rightarrow\infty
, the multiplication by
\varphi
being a bounded operator:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\Phi(\rho)\nu(d\rho)

\displaystyle=\Tr(\varphi\gamma^{k})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))}\Tr(\varphi p(u^{\otimes k}))\xi(du).

The computation above in reverse yields

\Tr(\varphi p(u^{\otimes k}))=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3k}}\varphi(|u|^{2})^{\otimes k}=\Phi(|u|^{2})=\Phi(\Theta(u)).

The proof is concluded.
∎

Using this quantum hierarchy, we can prove an abstract version of the desired inequality from Theorem
5.6
:

Theorem C.6
.

Let
Q
be a (possibly unbounded) non-negative self-adjoint operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3k_{0}})
for some
k_{0}\geq 1
. Assume that for all
u\in\mathbb{S}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
in the support of
\xi
(given by Lemma
C.5
),

\langle Qu^{\otimes k_{0}}\mathchar 59\relax u^{\otimes k_{0}}\rangle\geq\langle Q|u|^{\otimes k_{0}}\mathchar 59\relax|u|^{\otimes k_{0}}\rangle.

Then

\limsup_{j}\langle Q\psi^{j}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\psi^{j}\rangle\geq\int_{\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))}\langle Q\sqrt{\rho}^{\otimes k_{0}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\sqrt{\rho}^{\otimes k_{0}}\rangle\nu(d\rho).

Remark C.7
.

Here and below,
\langle Q\psi\mathchar 59\relax\psi\rangle
should be understood as the quadratic form associated to
Q
, so that
\langle Q\psi\mathchar 59\relax\psi\rangle=\langle Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\psi\mathchar 59\relax Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\psi\rangle
if
\psi
is in the domain of
Q^{\frac{1}{2}}
and
+\infty
otherwise.

Proof.

We approximate pointwise from below the operator
Q
by bounded self-adjoint non-negative operators:
By the spectral theorem for self-adjoint operators, there exists an isometry
U
between
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3k_{0}})
and another separable Hilbert space
L^{2}(X\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}d\mu)
such that
UQU^{-1}
is the multiplication operator by some real function
q
. We have
q\geq 0

\mu-
a.e. by non-negativity of
Q
. We define approximations by cutting off
q
to be bounded: for a positive integer
m
,

\tilde{Q}_{m}:=U^{-1}\max(q\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}m)U
, which is a non-negative self-adjoint bounded operator (of norm less than
m
).
The monotone convergence theorem ensures that
\langle\tilde{Q}_{m}\psi\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\psi\rangle
is non-decreasing in
m
and

\langle\tilde{Q}_{m}\psi\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\psi\rangle\xrightarrow[m\rightarrow+\infty]{}\langle Q\psi\mathchar 59\relax\psi\rangle\in[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}+\infty].

By tensorization with the identity, we can make
\tilde{Q}_{m}
act on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3j})
for any
j\geq k_{0}
.

We have, for any
j
and any
m
,

\displaystyle\langle Q\psi^{j}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\psi^{j}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\langle\tilde{Q}_{m}\psi^{j}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\psi^{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=\Tr(\tilde{Q}_{m}\Gamma^{j})

\displaystyle=\Tr(\tilde{Q}_{m}\Gamma^{j:k_{0}})

Since the operator
\tilde{Q}_{m}
is bounded,

\Tr(\tilde{Q}_{m}\Gamma^{j:k_{0}})\xrightarrow[j\rightarrow\infty]{}\Tr(\tilde{Q}_{m}\gamma^{k_{0}})

because of the strong convergence from Lemma
C.3
. But

\Tr(\tilde{Q}_{m}\gamma^{k_{0}})=\int_{\mathbb{S}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))}\Tr(\tilde{Q}_{m}p(u^{\otimes k_{0}}))\xi(du)

by Lemma
C.5
. Finally,

\Tr(\tilde{Q}_{m}p(u^{\otimes k_{0}}))\xi(du)=\langle\tilde{Q}_{m}u^{\otimes k_{0}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}u^{\otimes k_{0}}\rangle

and monotone convergence in
m
in the inequality

\limsup_{j}\langle Q\psi^{j}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\psi^{j}\rangle\geq\int_{\mathbb{S}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))}\langle\tilde{Q}_{m}u^{\otimes k_{0}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}u^{\otimes k_{0}}\rangle\xi(du)

yields

\limsup_{j}\langle Q\psi^{j}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\psi^{j}\rangle\geq\int_{\mathbb{S}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))}\langle Qu^{\otimes k_{0}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}u^{\otimes k_{0}}\rangle\xi(du).

We then use the hypothesis to write

\langle Qu^{\otimes k_{0}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}u^{\otimes k_{0}}\rangle\geq\langle Q|u|^{\otimes k_{0}}\mathchar 59\relax|u|^{\otimes k_{0}}\rangle=\langle Q(\sqrt{|u|^{2}})^{\otimes k_{0}}\mathchar 59\relax(\sqrt{|u|^{2}})^{\otimes k_{0}}\rangle

(64)

so that by Lemma
C.5
,

\int_{\mathbb{S}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))}\langle Qu^{\otimes k_{0}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}u^{\otimes k_{0}}\rangle\xi(du)\geq\int_{\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))}\langle Q\sqrt{\rho}^{\otimes k_{0}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\sqrt{\rho}^{\otimes k_{0}}\rangle\nu(d\rho).

∎

C.3.  Proof for the entropy production

To conclude the proof of Theorem

C.6

in terms of

\displaystyle=2\sum_{k=1}^{3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3j}}\left(\sqrt{\alpha^{j}}\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla_{12}\psi^{j}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=-2\sum_{k=1}^{3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3j}}\left((\sqrt{\alpha^{j}}\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla_{12})(\sqrt{\alpha^{j}}\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla_{12})\psi^{j}\right)\psi^{j}

It remains to check the hypothesis of Theorem

For any
