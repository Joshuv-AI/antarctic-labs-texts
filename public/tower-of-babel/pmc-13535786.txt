Progress and Prospects in Otorhinolaryngology—Head and Neck Surgery in Ethiopia
Authors: Daniel G. Eyassu, Eliyas Asfaw, Christian Schaaff, Cody L. Messick, Assaye Nibret, Seid Temam, Nardos Getachew Bulfeta, Kassaw Ketemaw, Gebrewahd Alemu, Michael Kebede, Joshua P. Wiedermann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionThis paper aims to explore the current state of otorhinolaryngology—head and neck surgery (ORL‐HNS) training and practice in Ethiopia, identify major barriers to development, and also highlight the optimistic prospects for the future, with strategies to build a self‐sustaining ORL‐HNS specialty capable of meeting the growing needs of the Ethiopian population.MethodsThis study employed a mixed‐methods approach, combining quantitative data from a cross‐sectional survey, qualitative data from semi‐structured interviews with otolaryngologists practicing in Ethiopia, and quantitative data from a review of relevant literature and publicly available datasets.ResultsA total of 53 otolaryngologists practicing in Ethiopia participated in the survey. Head and neck surgery has progressed, with all major oncology surgeries now performed. Training opportunities for higher specialization are limited. Generally, there is a shortage of ORL‐HNS clinical and surgical care equipment, outdated equipment, and poor maintenance availability.DiscussionEven with remarkable workforce growth, training for audiologists, speech therapists, and fellowship‐trained otolaryngologists in the country is needed. Sustainable advancement and access to safe and affordable surgery will require strong collaborative efforts from multiple fronts, including improvements in the surgical supply chain, access to affordable surgical equipment, and institutional‐level horizontal funding.ConclusionDespite strides over the past two decades, significant deficits remain in Ethiopia's capacity to provide otolaryngologic care. Efforts aimed at bridging these disparities must focus not only on providing general and advanced training opportunities for local physicians but also on expanding access to basic resources and equipment needed for practice and making care more widely available outside of major population centers.

Introduction

The Lancet Commission on Global Surgery reported in 2015 that over five billion people worldwide lack safe access to surgical care [1]. Annually, basic, life‐saving surgical care could prevent at least 77.2 million disability‐adjusted life‐years (DALYs) [2]. In response, the commission has set an ambitious goal for all countries to perform at least 5000 surgical procedures per 100,000 people by 2030 [1].

Ethiopia, a low‐income country in the Horn of Africa, is home to over 120 million people [3] and has made significant strides in medical education and healthcare over the past few decades. The country's median age is about 19 years [4], and over 70% of the population is employed in agriculture [3]. Despite a gross domestic product per capita of just $1920 in 2024 [5], Ethiopia's commitment to healthcare is evident in the expansion of its medical schools, from 5 in 2003 to 23 by 2009, aimed at achieving universal healthcare coverage [6].

The first medical school, established in 1963 at Addis Ababa University, laid the foundation for medical education [7]. Post‐graduate training (residency) began in 1980 at Addis Ababa University, with three surgeons trained [8]. However, the expansion of medical education has been relatively recent, with the rapid growth of medical schools only taking place in the last two decades.

Otorhinolaryngology−head and neck surgery (ORL‐HNS) as a specialty in Ethiopia began in the 1980s at one of the country's oldest public hospitals, Menelik II Referral Hospital, primarily introduced by Russian expatriates [9]. The government partnered with Cuba to train the first two Ethiopian otolaryngologists, leaving general surgeons to provide emergent services [9]. Despite these initial efforts, the specialty stagnated until 2008, when the first ORL‐HNS residency program was established at Addis Ababa University with the support of global outreach [10]. This program marked a significant turning point, starting with just three candidates.

Despite these advancements, national efforts to strengthen surgical care, such as the development of the national Saving Lives Through Safe Surgery (SaLTS) plan [11], and participation in regional initiatives, capacity development of ORL‐HNS practice in Ethiopia remains reliant on international support. Short‐term surgical trips and long‐term collaborations have been instrumental in sustaining the specialty in the past [12, 13]. This paper aims to explore the current state of ORL‐HNS training and practice in Ethiopia and identify barriers to development within access to resources, training programs, and the workforce. We will also highlight the importance of international collaboration and strategies to build a self‐sustaining ORL‐HNS specialty capable of meeting the growing needs of the Ethiopian population.

Methods

Study Design

This study employed a mixed‐methods approach, combining quantitative data from a cross‐sectional survey, qualitative data from semi‐structured interviews with otolaryngologists practicing in Ethiopia, and quantitative data from a review of relevant literature and publicly available datasets.

Participants

An electronic survey was developed and distributed to actively practicing otolaryngologists in Ethiopia from July to October 2024 (Supporting Information S1: Figure 1). Subjects were identified through the ORL‐HNS Professionals Society of Ethiopia. The survey collected data on basic demographics, training history, and practice patterns across various regions of the country. Responses were anonymized, and descriptive statistics were used to analyze workforce characteristics, training background, geographic distribution, and practice settings of respondents.

Data Analysis

As part of the study design, two Ethiopian otolaryngologists, who are also co‐authors, participated in semi‐structured interviews conducted by other members of the research team. These interviews explored their perspectives on training programs, clinical practice conditions, global collaborations, challenges in the specialty, and development opportunities (Supporting Information S1: Figure 2). Narrative insights from the co‐author interviews were used to contextualize and supplement descriptive findings on surgical institutional capacity, training programs, and resource distribution. These insights were paraphrased and integrated into sections 3.0 and 4.0 to highlight trends and information not captured through the survey or literature review.

Data visualization and mapping were created using Python (Matplotlib and GeoPandas libraries).

Literature Review

We conducted targeted searches for peer‐reviewed literature in PubMed, Embase, and Scopus using the keywords “otolaryngology” (and other common variants) and “Ethiopia.” Titles and abstracts were screened for relevance to our study objective. We reviewed studies, reports, and reviews that discussed ORL‐HNS training, workforce, clinical practice, healthcare infrastructure, and the impact of international collaborations on the development of the specialty in Ethiopia. We extracted comparative data on otolaryngologist workforce availability in neighboring countries from relevant published literature to contextualize Ethiopia's standing within the region. To identify additional contextual information, we used targeted internet searches with terms related to Ethiopia, healthcare equipment, infrastructure, and capacity. We included publicly available documents from government and health organizations, such as reports from the Ethiopian Ministry of Health and the World Health Organization.

Results

ORL‐HNS Workforce

A total of 53 otolaryngologists practicing in Ethiopia participated in the survey. The majority (79.2%) were male and between the ages of 31 and 40 years. Residency training was primarily completed at Addis Ababa University (60.4%), followed by St. Paul Millennium Medical College (26.4%) and Mekelle University (9.4%). Two otolaryngologists completed their residency training outside of Ethiopia. Among respondents, the workforce grew from 1 in 2010 to 53 by 2023 (Figure 1); 80.0% (n = 39) completed their residency training in 2018 or later, and 7 otolaryngologists (13.2%) had fellowship training. Among these, five trained in otology/neurotology, one in pediatric otolaryngology, and one in Head and Neck Surgery. In terms of clinical practice, 79.2% of the otolaryngologists primarily worked in public institutions and 3.8% in both sectors (Table 1). Notably, 77.4% of the facilities where participants practiced were teaching institutions. Regional distribution of otolaryngologists shows the majority are concentrated in Addis Ababa (50.9%), with smaller proportions in Amhara (11.3%), Tigray (9.4%), Oromia (7.5%), and Sidama (7.5%) regions, among others.

Training Programs

There are currently four ORL‐HNS residency programs in Ethiopia: Addis Ababa University, St. Paul's Hospital Millennium Medical College (SPHMMC), Mekelle University, and Bahir Dar University (ST and AN; Table 3). Residents complete a general internship year and complete a national residency entrance exam before being eligible to apply. Each program admits an average of 3–4 residents per year and aims to graduate residents after a 4‐year program.

There are only two formal fellowship training programs in the country: a combined Pediatric Otolaryngology and Facial Plastics fellowship and an Advanced Head and Neck Surgery fellowship. These fellowships are both at Bahir Dar University and were established in October 2024 with the help of NGO collaboration: Children's Surgery International (CSI). Outside of these, it is incumbent upon residents to seek fellowship opportunities abroad, with financial constraints and lack of established pipelines being the main barriers.

ORL‐HNS Institutional Capacity

ORL‐HNS services in Ethiopia are predominantly available in Addis Ababa, including SPHMMC, Tikur Anbessa, Yekatit 12, and Menelik II referral hospitals (ST and AN). Outside Addis Ababa, Ayder Hospital in Mekelle serves as a major referral hospital for ORL‐HNS services. Tertiary hospitals with ORL‐HNS are also found in Adama, Bahir Dar, Jimma, and Hawassa, but often offer more limited ORL‐HNS surgical services.

Recent advances include the emergence of fellowship‐trained otolaryngologists and significant self‐driven training efforts by local surgeons. Otology has seen growth with mastoidectomy, ossicular chain reconstruction, and lateral skull base routinely performed over the last 5 years (AN). Head and neck surgery has also progressed, with all major oncology surgeries, including laryngectomy with reconstruction and salivary gland tumor surgeries, now performed. However, there remains a large gap in reconstructive head and neck surgery, especially those with free flaps. Rhinology has recently seen the introduction of minimally invasive endoscopic sinus and anterior skull base surgeries, though there are no fellowship‐trained rhinology and anterior skull base surgeons.

Other key components of comprehensive ORL‐HNS care remain limited. The audiology and speech‐language pathology service has only one formally trained audiologist and one speech therapist in the country. Additionally, there are no training programs for speech‐language pathology or audiology. Neonatal hearing screening is not routinely performed, and cochlear implantation is not available. Many deaf children are instead directed toward sign language‐based education, as hearing aids are difficult to access and only one private facility in the country offers hearing aid fitting services.

Resources and Equipment

Generally, there is a shortage of ORL‐HNS clinical and surgical care equipment, outdated equipment, and poor maintenance availability (AN and ST). The availability of equipment varies significantly between institutions. Yekatit 12, Ayder Hospital (teaching hospital of Mekelle University), and SPHMMC have better equipment availability than most hospitals due to receiving equipment through donations or investment from the government. However, in this environment, the shelf life of these instruments becomes limited, and recurrent donations are necessary to maintain capacity.

Ethiopia faces significant challenges in its healthcare system due to a critical shortage and inequitable distribution of medical devices. The country lacks a robust policy for medical devices, hindering effective regulation and management [15]. Key deficits include a limited number of essential equipment like MRI machines (18), CT scanners (48), and a complete absence of PET scanners [16]. Furthermore, a substantial portion of existing equipment in areas like ORL‐HNS is outdated, poorly maintained, and distributed unevenly.

Discussion

International Partnerships

Though ORL‐HNS as a specialty in Ethiopia has been available since the 1980s, it is still an emerging specialty with gaps in training, workforce growth and distribution, and surgical capacity. However, the field has been marked by much development over the past two decades. A key part of this growth has been partnerships with organizations in Europe and the United States such as Global ENT Outreach [17], ENT+ (ENTplus), the Ethio‐American Hearing Project, Children's Surgery International, Healing the Children [18, 19], and Partnerships for Global Surgery (PGS) [12, 13]. Many of these collaborative efforts started around the late 2000s and 2010s and have taken short‐term surgical trips to Ethiopia at least twice a year. During these trips, these visiting teams have not only provided direct patient care but also worked to expand ORL‐HNS education and service capacity through training senior surgeons, improving resident surgical education, and donating essential equipment and consumables [17, 20, 21, 22]. In addition to ORL‐HNS, these collaborations have supported audiology services and anesthesiology through multidisciplinary team efforts [19, 21, 23].

Despite these successes, the partnerships with these organizations have recently weakened. This has affected the surgical education of residents and capacity for various surgeries that rely on donated consumables such as stapedectomy. For instance, though there have been many collaborative efforts that have worked with Addis Ababa University, it currently only maintains contact with ENT+, Bahir Dar with CSI, and Mekelle University maintains its relationship with PGS, although efforts are underway to reestablish old collaborations. This is likely multifactorial to the COVID‐19 pandemic, recent Tigray war, ongoing war in the Amhara and Oromia regions, related imposed travel restrictions, and fractured communication. The recently re‐established ORL‐HNS Society of Ethiopia recognizes the importance of these global partners and has made it one of its primary objectives to strengthen these relationships. As the ORL‐HNS workforce continues to grow in Ethiopia, revitalizing and further strengthening these partnerships will be important for sustaining expansion in surgical volume and capacity.

ORL‐HNS Workforce

The otolaryngologist workforce in Ethiopia has seen growth since the opening of the first otolaryngology residency in 2008. In 2009, there was a reported total of 11 otolaryngologists in the country, and today the ORL‐HNS Society estimates 80–90 practicing otolaryngologists [24]. Despite this growth, the ratio of otolaryngologists to the population remains far below 1 per 100,000. According to a 2023 survey study, Ethiopia's otolaryngologist density is among the lowest in sub‐Saharan Africa, trailing behind most neighboring countries such as Kenya, Uganda, and Sudan [14]. This gap mirrors broader surgical workforce shortages across Ethiopia. A national cross‐sectional study found that overall surgical workforce density had improved from 0.35 to 5.19 per 100,000 over a 5‐year period, yet this still falls short of the Lancet Commission's target of 20 per 100,000 [25]. Our survey shows the majority of this workforce is in the early stages of their career. Additionally, according to our survey, over 50% of otolaryngologists are concentrated in Addis Ababa, where only approximately 3.7% of the population resides [26].

Ethiopia faces a critical shortage of audiology and speech‐therapy specialists and training programs. As is the case in many other low‐income countries, audiometry in Ethiopia is typically performed by nurses or otolaryngologists with some training in audiology [21]. Global surgery partnerships have previously attempted to expand these services through basic training [23]. There was a plan to start a formal audiology program at Mekelle University; however, the COVID‐19 pandemic halted its progress. Additionally, at Mekelle University, the first cohort of residents had limited audiology training through online courses. The scarcity of these services in the country restricts the capacity to provide comprehensive ORL‐HNS service, particularly outside of Addis Ababa, and is a critical area to improve.

Most otolaryngologists practice in government settings, but some also engage in private practice due to the low salaries in government hospitals. In a 2022 study at Ethiopia's largest public hospital, physicians, including two otolaryngologists, reported their salary was unacceptably low, even by the standards of other low‐income countries [27]. This poor compensation has also been recognized by the Ethiopian Ministry of Health in its Health Sector Transformation Plan, which attempted to develop incentive packages to motivate and retain physicians and other healthcare workers [28]. Low compensation, along with limited professional development opportunities, is a primary reason for migration of Ethiopian medical graduates to neighboring, developed countries. In 2010, a World Bank Report found 26.4% of Ethiopian‐trained physicians practiced abroad [29]. Secondary to the political climate and lack of institutional capacity in rural areas, many recent graduates have plans to take the US Medical Licensing Exams to find opportunities in the United States. Some, desperate to practice outside of Ethiopia, would willingly give up their pursuit of otolaryngology to do so. This brain drain poses a threat to the growth of the otolaryngologist workforce in a country with an already limited number of training programs.

The otolaryngology workforce in Ethiopia is largely composed of general otolaryngologists. There are currently 10 fellowship‐trained otolaryngologists in the country—two head and neck surgeons, six otologists, one neurotologist, and one pediatric otolaryngologist. Though the opportunity for fellowship training is extremely limited, many Ethiopian general otolaryngologists are very knowledgeable and driven to expand capacity for specialized surgeries in the country. As a result, over the past few years, specialized procedures, such as endoscopic anterior skull base surgery, stapedectomy, and ossicular chain reconstruction, have started to become more available in Addis Ababa, even though there are no fellowship‐trained rhinology surgeons and very few otology/neurotology surgeons in the country. However, these efforts are dependent on and limited by the infrastructure and resources available to surgeons. Visiting surgeons from global surgery partnerships have supported this training effort by providing surgical equipment in addition to surgical skills and knowledge transfer. A collaborative and well‐organized educational initiative from high‐resource institutions, focused on virtual and in‐person longitudinal transfer of surgical training, represents a promising pathway forward.

Training

ORL‐HNS residency in Ethiopia is relatively new, with the first program founded in 2008 at Addis Ababa University. Additionally, the three other residency programs in the country were all created in the past 10 years. In Ethiopia, to become an otolaryngologist, students complete 6 years of undergraduate and medical school training followed by a 1‐year internship as general practitioners. To match into the highly selective ORL‐HNS residency match, graduates must take an exam, which only recently became standardized across all residency programs. The few selected candidates then start a 4‐year training program, which includes rotations in anesthesiology, plastic surgery, oral and maxillofacial surgery, and oncology.

Despite this structured nationally standardized curriculum, operative training within ORL‐HNS residency programs is limited by availability of equipment, operating rooms, and time and surgical experience of senior otolaryngologists to teach residents. This has contributed to gaps in surgical skills among many of the domestically trained otolaryngologists [18, 21]. Visiting surgeons have improved surgical training among residents by providing training experience and equipment that are otherwise not available to residents [20]. Residents have especially benefited from improved otology training, as many of their efforts focused on otologic surgery knowledge and skill transfer. For instance, notable successes of the global collaborative efforts include establishing the first temporal bone lab [30] and course in the country and training several otolaryngologists in middle ear surgery [31], where only two otolaryngologists in the country were comfortable in performing middle ear surgery [22].

Formal fellowship training programs in Ethiopia are scarce, with only two available in the country. There was previously an otology fellowship [22], which graduated several otologists but has not continued due to lack of local institutional capacity after the regional civil war. Many otolaryngologists aspire to pursue fellowship training abroad, but this is challenging due to the need to entirely self‐fund and secure opportunities independently. Some otolaryngologists have managed to take self‐supported short observerships abroad to gain additional exposure and training. The American Academy of Otolaryngology‐Head and Neck Surgery has also provided several highly motivated Ethiopian otolaryngologists opportunities for observerships in the United States through its visiting scholars program [20]. Bidirectional education, observerships, and fellowships broaden Ethiopian surgeons’ clinical knowledge, expose them to diverse techniques, and enhance surgical care and leadership upon their return [32]. Grant programs should consider support for Ethiopian Otolaryngologists who desire these learning opportunities.

Institutional Capacity

Recent advances in institutional capacity have been driven by the introduction of fellowship‐trained otolaryngologists and rigorous self‐driven training efforts by otolaryngologists. A 2022 survey of otolaryngologists in Ethiopia and Kenya identified microvascular reconstruction, advanced otologic surgery, and functional endoscopic sinus surgery to be the three most desired skills surgeons wanted to acquire [13]. Otology was previously limited to tympanoplasty and simple procedures, but in the last 5 years, surgeries like mastoidectomy, ossicular chain reconstruction, and glomus tumor paraganglioma have become more available. Head and neck surgery has also seen considerable progress, with a wide range of procedures, including laryngectomy with reconstruction, salivary gland tumor surgery, and maxillary swing, now being performed. However, there remains a gap in the availability of free flap surgery. These advancements in head and neck surgery are largely due to the presence of a fellowship‐trained surgeon at Addis Ababa University. In rhinology and anterior skull base surgery, there has been a recent introduction of endoscopic sinus surgery and endoscopic tumor surgery for paranasal sinuses and the skull base. These advancements reflect the ongoing efforts to expand the range of services available and improve patient care. Sustainable advancement and access to safe and affordable surgery, comprising the full range of otolaryngology, will require strong collaborative efforts from multiple fronts, including but not limited to improvements in the surgical supply chain, access to affordable surgical equipment, and institutional‐level horizontal funding.

Resources and Equipment

Ethiopia's limited and unevenly distributed medical imaging and surgical equipment requires a multifaceted strategy focused on policy and resource allocation. Compared to other Sub‐Saharan African countries like South Africa and Kenya, Ethiopia faces major gaps in equitable healthcare access. Budget constraints in public hospitals hinder the acquisition of even basic surgical equipment, reflecting limited attention from policymakers toward ORL‐HNS services, partly due to low awareness and the specialty's relatively recent development. This has slowed infrastructure growth, service expansion, and postgraduate training.

Currently, the supply chain for otolaryngology surgical equipment is overly complicated, inconsistent, and expensive, rendering it unsustainable. Irregular access to both essential and advanced surgical tools creates a bottleneck in providing safe and high‐quality surgical care—one that cannot be addressed by training alone. Increasing awareness of ORL‐HNS among policymakers, alongside a dedicated national policy for medical devices, could lay the groundwork for sustainable procurement and distribution of essential equipment. Such a policy should be supported by investments in training medical engineers, strengthening maintenance practices, and targeting efforts to address infrastructure gaps. Additionally, strategic partnerships with international donors and collaborations with private entities may help Ethiopia overcome financial barriers and reduce disparities between urban centers and more remote regions. Ultimately, improving the availability and accessibility of medical devices will be vital for improving the overall quality of care and patient outcomes across Ethiopia.

Opportunities for Progress

International partnerships have played an important role in expanding Ethiopia's ORL‐HNS services by providing surgical education, donating equipment, and supporting more specialized training. Moving forward, Ethiopia's ORL‐HNS community, in collaboration with global partners, should form sustained partnerships that focus on capacity building, education, and institutional support. Additionally, there needs to be more decentralization of these efforts beyond Addis Ababa to other regions of the country, though there are understandably challenges with conflicts in certain regions. Initiatives like PGS and GEO offer example models of this.

To strengthen these efforts, international organizations are encouraged to invest in long‐term collaborations that emphasize skills transfer, development of subspecialty training programs, and infrastructure support. International collaboration can strengthen local research through grants to develop locally applicable, innovative, cost‐saving surgical interventions and provide technical support to standardize otolaryngology outcomes through electronic data platforms. Additionally, interorganizational communication, data sharing, and programmatic planning would strengthen capacity building and minimize duplicated efforts. There is also a need for increased recognition of ORL‐HNS as an essential component of surgical care among government policymakers to address workforce shortages, hospital budget constraints, and gaps in clinical and surgical equipment. Empowering Ethiopian teams to study their patient populations and outcomes can help drive policy creation. Local professional societies and academic institutions should continue advocating for the integration of ORL‐HNS into broader health strategies and push for improved access to training and resources.

Conclusion

There is a growing need for both basic and specialty otolaryngologic services in Ethiopia. Despite strides over the past two decades, significant deficits remain in Ethiopia's capacity to provide otolaryngologic care. Efforts aimed at bridging these disparities must focus not only on providing general and advanced training opportunities for local physicians but also on expanding access to basic resources and equipment needed for practice and making care more widely available outside of major population centers. Achieving these aims likely requires the revitalization and establishment of international partnerships capable of fostering sustainable capacity building and increasing recognition of ORL‐HNS as a critical component of surgical care among government policymakers.
