Underweight at age 20 and pre‐pregnancy and large‐for‐gestational‐age births in Japanese women with gestational diabetes mellitus: Hamamatsu GRACE Study 4
Authors: Shinya Furukawa, Ayumi Kanamoto, Yukino Toyama, Kei Takeshita, Takuya Hashimoto, Teruki Miyake, Daisuke Tsuriya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAims/IntroductionWhile maternal obesity is a well‐established risk factor for large‐for‐gestational‐age (LGA) infants, the impact of maternal underweight on LGA in women with gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) remains inconsistent. This issue is particularly relevant in Japan, given the high prevalence of underweight women. We investigated the association between maternal weight and body mass index (BMI) at age 20 and pre‐pregnancy and the risk of LGA in Japanese women with GDM.Materials and MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed 537 Japanese women with GDM. Participants were categorized by BMI (underweight <18.5 kg/m2, normal, and obesity ≥25.0 kg/m2) and by weight quartiles at age 20 and pre‐pregnancy. Multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to calculate adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between underweight at age 20 and pre‐pregnancy and LGA.ResultsThe prevalence of underweight at age 20, underweight pre‐pregnancy, and LGA was 30.4%, 13.4%, and 9.7%, respectively. After adjustment for confounders, underweight based on BMI at age 20 (adjusted OR 0.22, 95% CI: 0.06–0.75) and pre‐pregnancy (adjusted OR 0.17, 95% CI: 0.03–0.64) was significantly and inversely associated with LGA compared with obesity. Similarly, the lowest weight quartiles at both time points were independently associated with a low risk of LGA.ConclusionsMaternal underweight at age 20 and pre‐pregnancy was significantly associated with a lower risk of LGA in Japanese women with GDM. These findings suggest that weight status from early adulthood may play a crucial role in fetal growth outcomes.Keywords: Body mass index, Fetal outcomes, Gestational diabetes mellitus

INTRODUCTION

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) has emerged as a major global public health burden, reflecting changes in maternal risk profiles and diagnostic practices 1 , 2 , 3 . As the Hyperglycemia and Adverse Pregnancy Outcome (HAPO) study demonstrated, among women with GDM, maternal glucose levels show a continuous, graded association with the risk of adverse outcomes, particularly large‐for‐gestational‐age (LGA) infants, without an obvious threshold 4 . Generally, being underweight before pregnancy is considered a protective factor against LGA 5 , 6 , 7 . Similarly, in Japanese women, maternal underweight is significantly inversely associated with LGA 8 , 9 .

Among women with GDM, several Chinese studies have investigated the association between underweight and LGA. However, evidence regarding this association remains inconsistent: most studies found that underweight women with GDM still had a lower risk of LGA 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , whereas two Chinese studies found no such association 14 , 15 . Thus, it is not yet clear whether the “protective effect” of being underweight is maintained under the hyperglycemic conditions of GDM.

While BMI distributions vary between Japanese and Chinese women, a distinct difference lies in the prevalence of underweight, which is significantly higher in Japan 9 , 16 , 17 . In China, the prevalence of underweight among women of reproductive age is only 7.8% 16 . In contrast, approximately 20.7% of Japanese women in their twenties are underweight 17 , and the prevalence of underweight is similarly high at 18.2% among Japanese pregnant women 9 . Because of this substantial difference, findings from Chinese populations cannot be directly applied to the Japanese context.

To date, no study has investigated the association between maternal underweight and LGA among Japanese women with GDM. Thus, the aim of this study was to examine the association between maternal BMI and weight at age 20 and pre‐pregnancy and the risk of LGA in Japanese women with GDM.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study population

In this study, we enrolled 641 pregnant women who were suspected of having GDM at Hamamatsu University School of Medicine Hospital or affiliated local clinics, underwent a 75‐g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), and were diagnosed with GDM based on the International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Groups (IADPSG) criteria 18 , and were referred to the Department of Endocrinology and Metabolism at Hamamatsu University School of Medicine Hospital between 2013 and 2024. After excluding 104 cases with missing data, the final number of participants was 537. Informed consent was obtained using an opt‐out approach via a website, and all participants were given the opportunity to decline participation in the study. The study protocol was developed in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hamamatsu University School of Medicine (Approval No. 25–110, July 28, 2025) and Ehime University Hospital (No. 2025A050).

Measurements

Data on parity, family history of diabetes, and smoking were obtained through self‐reported questionnaires and medical records. Information on HbA1c, insulin use, multiple pregnancies, gestational age at delivery (weeks), and gestational weight gain was obtained from medical records. Smoking status was defined as positive if participants reported that they currently smoked or had ever smoked, even a small amount. A family history of diabetes was defined as having at least one first‐degree relative with diabetes.

Body mass index and definitions of underweight and obesity

Height was measured to the nearest millimeter using a height gauge, with participants standing upright. Data on weight at age 20 and pre‐pregnancy were obtained from self‐reported questionnaires and medical records. BMI at age 20 and pre‐pregnancy was calculated by dividing self‐reported weight at age 20 and pre‐pregnancy weight (kg) by measured height (m2). In accordance with the definitions of the Japan Society for the Study of Obesity, underweight and obesity were defined as BMI <18.5 kg/m2 and BMI ≥25.0 kg/m2, respectively 19 .

Weight at age 20 and at pre‐pregnancy

For a more detailed analysis of potential dose–response patterns, weight was categorized into quartiles and analyzed using the following four groups. Weight at age 20 was classified as: (1) lowest, <45.0 kg, (2) lower‐middle, 45.0–48.9 kg, (3) upper‐middle, 49.0–54.9 kg, and (4) highest, ≥55.0 kg. Pre‐pregnancy weight was classified as: (1) lowest, <48.0 kg, (2) lower‐middle, 48.0–52.9 kg, (3) upper‐middle, 53.0–62.9 kg, and (4) highest, ≥63.0 kg.

Definition of large‐for‐gestational‐age (LGA) and macrosomia

Based on data extracted from medical records from the Departments of Pediatrics and Obstetrics at Hamamatsu University Hospital, LGA was defined as birth weight above the 90th percentile for gestational age according to established population reference curves. 20 Macrosomia was defined as a birth weight ≥4,000 g, irrespective of gestational age 21 .

Statistical methods

BMI was classified into three categories: underweight: (BMI <18.5 kg/m2), normal (18.5–24.9 kg/m2), and obese (BMI ≥25.0 kg/m2, reference). Weight at age 20 and pre‐pregnancy was classified into four categories. Weight at age 20 was categorized as: (1) lowest, <45.0 kg, (2) lower‐middle, 45.0–48.9 kg, (3) upper‐middle, 49.0–54.9 kg, and (4) highest, ≥55.0 kg (reference). Pre‐pregnancy weight was categorized as: (1) lowest, <48.0 kg, (2) lower‐middle, 48.0–52.9 kg, (3) upper‐middle, 53.0–62.9 kg, and (4) highest, ≥63.0 kg (reference). The trend of the association was assessed using a logistic regression model, in which consecutive integers were assigned to the categories of BMI.

Crude odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the associations between maternal underweight at age 20 and pre‐pregnancy and LGA were estimated using logistic regression analysis. Covariates included in the multivariable models (maternal age, smoking status, HbA1c, insulin use, parity, gestational age at delivery, and gestational weight gain) were selected a priori based on their clinical relevance and evidence from previous studies. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc, Cary, NC, USA).

DISCUSSION

Among Japanese women with GDM, underweight (BMI <18.5 kg/m2) at age 20 and pre‐pregnancy was independently associated with a lower risk of LGA. Similarly, low body weight at age 20 and pre‐pregnancy was inversely associated with LGA. This is the first study to demonstrate this association among Japanese women with GDM. The inverse association between underweight at age 20 and LGA may also represent a novel finding and may not be limited to GDM pregnancies.

While maternal underweight is widely recognized as a risk factor for SGA, it is generally considered protective against LGA. Meta‐analyses by Liu et al. and Rahman et al. have shown a consistently lower risk of macrosomia in underweight women compared to those with normal weight 6 , 7 . In a meta‐analysis examining this association among Japanese women, maternal underweight was similarly shown to be inversely associated with LGA. 8

Similarly, a meta‐analysis involving Chinese women with GDM reported an inverse association between maternal underweight and LGA. 10 In Chinese studies of 832 women with GDM, 11 and 14,578 women with GDM, 13 maternal underweight was significantly inversely associated with LGA compared to the normal weight group. Among 622 Chinese women with GDM, unadjusted analyses indicated differences in the prevalence of macrosomia and LGA between obese and underweight subjects, 12 The findings of the present study are consistent with these previous Chinese studies. However, in a Chinese study of 1,049 women with GDM, maternal underweight was not associated with LGA (reference: normal BMI 18.5–22.9 kg/m2), 14 and in a cohort of 2,909 Chinese women diagnosed with GDM, maternal underweight was similarly not associated with LGA (reference: normal weight). 15

It is crucial to acknowledge that the prevalence of obesity and underweight, as well as their metabolic implications, varies substantially across racial and ethnic populations. 22 , 23 Notably, the prevalence of underweight among young women in Japan is uniquely high compared to other East Asian populations, representing a specific public health concern. 24 Furthermore, differences in perinatal management and healthcare systems between China and Japan may differentially influence fetal growth and pregnancy outcomes. Therefore, demonstrating the consistency of the inverse association between maternal underweight and LGA within a Japanese cohort is clinically significant.

Body weight at age 20 was based on self‐report, and BMI at age 20 was calculated using self‐reported weight. Thus, recall bias may have affected the reported values of maternal weight and BMI at age 20. An integrative review of the literature has shown that women tend to overestimate their height and underestimate their weight. Thus, self‐reported BMI at age 20 might be systematically biased and could underestimate the true BMI, potentially leading to misclassification. 25 In Japanese women who had attended health check‐ups, strong correlations were observed between measured and self‐reported values for height, weight, and BMI. 26 However, any non‐differential misclassification would likely bias the ORs toward the null. Despite these limitations, body weight and BMI at age 20 were still associated with the risk of LGA, suggesting that optimizing weight not only immediately before pregnancy but also in early adulthood may be important for LGA prevention.

Although maternal underweight may be protective against LGA, this potential benefit should be interpreted alongside its clinically important downside—namely, an increased risk of SGA and LBW. In a smaller study of 764 Chinese women with GDM, underweight was associated with SGA. 27 In another Chinese study of 832 women with GDM, underweight was not associated with SGA. 11 However, in a study of 14,578 Chinese women with GDM, underweight was positively associated with SGA. 13 Additionally, in a Japanese study of women with GDM, maternal underweight was significantly associated with SGA and LBW. 28 In Chinese studies of women with GDM, a meta‐analysis similarly indicated that underweight is associated with SGA. 10 Taken together, while underweight may protect against LGA, it is a known risk factor for SGA and LBW. 9

Several mechanisms may underlie the inverse association between maternal underweight and LGA in women with GDM, although they have not been fully established. Even in the presence of GDM, an underweight phenotype may limit fetal overgrowth through lower maternal body size and restricted substrate availability. First, lower BMI may be associated with smaller uterine volume and pelvic size, which could limit fetal growth potential and contribute to placental insufficiency. Second, lower BMI may reflect chronic undernutrition and adverse early‐life conditions and is often associated with lower pre‐pregnancy BMI and inadequate gestational weight gain, thereby increasing the risk of fetal undernutrition. 23 , 24 Third, maternal low BMI may genetically reduce fetal growth potential through shared height‐associated alleles. 29 Fourth, limited maternal adipose reserves may reduce the lipid substrates required for fetal fat accretion, acting as a rate‐limiting factor even under hyperglycemic conditions. 30 In addition, underweight women with GDM may be characterized by preserved insulin sensitivity and pancreatic beta‐cell dysfunction rather than marked insulin resistance. 31 This metabolic profile may partly attenuate the effect of maternal hyperglycemia on fetal growth. However, these findings should not be interpreted as support for underweight status. Further studies are warranted to clarify the mechanisms underlying this association.

This study has several limitations. First, data on energy intake and physical activity were not available in this cohort. Second, because therapeutic interventions are typically implemented after the diagnosis of GDM, post‐diagnosis interventions may have influenced the observed association between pre‐pregnancy BMI/weight and LGA. Third, the study focused exclusively on Japanese women, a population characterized by a relatively low obesity rate. While national registry data indicate that 18.2% of pregnant women in Japan are underweight, 9 the prevalence in our cohort was notably lower at 13.4%. This reduction is expected, given that higher BMI is a major risk factor for GDM, naturally limiting the proportion of underweight individuals in this group. Nevertheless, the mean pre‐pregnancy BMI in this study (22.78 kg/m2) aligns closely with previous reports on Japanese women with GDM. 32 Fourth, BMI at age 20, calculated using self‐reported weight, may be subject to recall bias. Finally, participants were referred to a university hospital for suspected GDM, which may have introduced selection bias. Due to the referral process and tertiary care setting, the study population may represent individuals with more severe or atypical clinical presentations.

In conclusion, underweight status at age 20 and before pregnancy was associated with a lower risk of LGA in Japanese women with GDM. However, underweight status may increase the risk of SGA and LBW and should not be interpreted as a desirable or recommended condition. Given the observational nature of this study, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Historical weight factors may provide additional information when assessing the risk of LGA in women with GDM.
