Hidden correlations in quantum jumps: A theory of individual trials
Hiroshi Ishikawa

Abstract
Quantum trajectory theories have not fully reconciled discrete quantum jumps with continuous unitary evolution. We address this challenge by developing a hidden variable formulation that reveals hidden correlations in individual trials. We represent jump sequences as logical propositions on energy eigenstate occupations, where logical variables describe their truth values and chain operators describe their probabilities. To circumvent established no-go theorems, we introduce a proposition selection rule that requires well-defined joint probabilities for a given density operator. This rule unifies compatibility criteria for quantum and hidden variable theories, eliminating incompatible propositions that lead to value assignment paradoxes. Unlike conventional criteria mandating operator commutativity, it permits propositions that become deterministic for specific initial conditions. This framework systematically captures deterministic relations in individual trials, including the exclusivity of state occupation implied by the observed jumps. This exclusivity, distinct from the Pauli exclusion principle, enables the formalization of a quantum version of Boolean logic as laws for quantum propositions. These assumptions circumvent Bell-type inequalities by prohibiting arithmetic operations between incompatible logical variables, while enabling classical descriptions within the complementarity. The resulting theory reconciles discrete jumps with continuous evolution, providing a direct description of individual trials while preserving standard probability predictions.

Introduction

Quantum jumps have been observed in diverse systems, including photon counters

This contradiction is particularly evident in photon counting experiments, where individual measurements yield discrete integers

Such integer functions are non-differentiable at jump points and thus cannot be derived from any standard differential equations.
This observation reveals an intrinsic duality of quantum theory: individual outcomes do not emerge as solutions to its equations of motion

Consequently, integer functions are found experimentally, even though they themselves cannot be derived from any differential laws.

When quantum jumps were first observed for single quantum systems, this fundamental conflict between experiments and theories prompted two distinct interpretations.
The early interpretation

In contrast, modern theoretical frameworks

These approaches, however, face inherent limitations in their definitions of quantum trajectories and do not resolve the coexistence of multiple physical mechanisms for the discontinuities.

Specifically, the open systems and quantum jump approaches

define quantum trajectories using quantum master equations, numerically demonstrating state collapse due to environmental decoherence

These approaches, however, fail to predict the collapse of coherent states and cannot describe the absorption of coherent light.
While discrete photodetector responses are conventionally attributed to photons, both the photon concept

The consistent histories approach

Yet, this approach also faces challenges in restoring classical responses of apparatus within unitary dynamics.

These limitations now warrant reconsideration of the early view: observed jumps may indicate true discontinuities, despite theoretical prohibitions.
Indeed, detector counts

This suggests that logical variables emerge in experimental contexts and may require theoretical analyses.
In particular, these logical variables should describe the photoelectric effect at each detection site—single-shot events that initiate counting sequences.
Current experimental techniques for manipulating single quantum systems should enable more quantitative verification of such logical variables.
Meaningful tests, however, require a theoretical framework that at least admits their existence.
We therefore reexamine the premises of major no-go theorems and develop a HV formulation that describes quantum jumps using logical variables.

II
Challenges

We must address three interrelated challenges simultaneously.
First, any self-consistent theory must circumvent established no-go theorems against HV theories.
These theorems concern: (i) value assignments to operators (Kochen-Specker
Kochen1967
, CHSH
CHSH
, GHZ
GHZ
); (ii) continuity of density operators (Gleason
Gleason1957
;
Peres
); and (iii) Bell’s model of local HVs
Bell
.
Value assignment constitutes the primary obstacle, while other paradoxes present unique challenges that require careful treatment.
Second, we must identify a proper classical limit to reconcile the discontinuity of logical variables with the continuity of wavefunctions.
While this issue arises in both classical motion
EPR
;
EPR-Bohr
and macroscopic two-level systems

Third, we must harmonize Boolean logic with quantum theory.
This challenge, partially addressed in the consistent histories approach, is connected to the first through incompatibility problems specific to the theories of state occupations.

III
Methods

We address these challenges through three interdependent modifications to the consistent histories approach: (A) restricting projectors to energy eigenstates, (B) revising compatibility criteria, and (C) establishing Boolean logic as laws for individual trials.
These modifications work together to provide a self-consistent framework.

III.1
Restricting projectors to energy eigenstates

The first modification restricts projectors to those specified by the spectral decomposition of a single observable.
This restriction eliminates the need to assign values to all commuting observables.
The same principle
Held2022
previously justified Bohm’s HV theory
Bohm1952
, which employs position operators to establish an alternative interpretation.
We instead use the energy operator to describe the observed jumps between energy eigenstates
QuantumJump2
;
QuantumJump1
.
Specifically, these states are defined as the orthonormal eigenstates of the non-interacting Hamiltonian in non-relativistic QED
Messiah
.
Our formulation thus concerns the occupation of Bohr’s stationary states, where these stationary states are now generalized to represent both atomic systems and radiation fields.

For non-degenerate systems with unique stationary states, assigning 1 to the occupied state and 0 to others yields stochastic logical variables that describe individual trials.
Quantum theory can calculate the occupation probability of any such state.
For degenerate systems, however, paradoxes persist due to the non-uniqueness of stationary states.
For example, a spin-1 system in zero magnetic field has three degenerate stationary states
|a;{\mathrm{x}}\rangle
, where the parameter
{\mathrm{x}}
represents a combination of three orthogonal measurement axes (“setting”), and
a\,{\in}\,\{(1,0,0),(0,1,0),(0,0,1)\}
denotes quantum numbers for the squared spin components along these axes (“outcome”).
The degeneracy permits arbitrary choice of setting
{\mathrm{x}}
and the corresponding projectors
|a;{\mathrm{x}}\rangle\langle a;{\mathrm{x}}|
.
The Kochen-Specker theorem
Kochen1967
shows that assigning values to all mathematically allowed projectors results in contradiction when the number of projectors exceeds 117.

III.2
Revising compatibility criteria

III.2.1
Quantum trajectories as logical propositions

The second modification revises compatibility criteria to reduce the number of projectors requiring value assignment.
To this end, we represent stationary state occupations as logical propositions, describing their probabilities and truth values using projectors and logical variables, respectively.
Here, projectors yield probabilities through the Born rule, while binary logical variables directly encode truth values.
As described above, this dual representation becomes paradoxical when binary values are assigned to projectors for incompatible settings.
We resolve this paradox by imposing a selection rule on propositions, which disallows incompatible settings.
Such a rule unifies the compatibility criteria for projectors and logical variables, thereby excluding settings that quantum theory never considers simultaneously.

To describe causal relationships between propositions, we represent time sequences of stationary state occupations as compound logical propositions.
While projectors describe state occupations at each time, chain operators describe whole sequences specified by compound propositions.
We show that this chain operator, specifically defined for energy eigenstates, generalizes the chain operator of a history in the consistent histories approach.
As a new compatibility criterion, we require these compound propositions to be constructed from single-time propositions that have well-defined joint probabilities for a given density operator.
To see why such a simple rule resolves the paradoxes, we must examine conventional compatibility criteria in HV and quantum theories.

III.2.2
Problems in conventional criteria

For HV theories, mathematical no-go theorems have employed minimal assumptions, typically including the operator commutativity and the sum and product rules
Kochen1967
.
These assumptions yield a theory in which the sum and product exist whenever observables have definite values, permitting unphysical observable combinations that ignore measurement contextuality
Peres
.
In Kochen-Specker’s proof for spin-1 systems, the 117+ projectors include incompatible settings never realized simultaneously in any single measurement.
For each setting, each measurement requires value assignment to only three commuting projectors.
Thus, the paradox arises from an incomplete commutativity requirement that does not exclude arbitrary parameter choices.

Quantum theory treats operator commutativity differently from HV theories.
The key requirement is that joint probabilities must be consistently defined for
all
density operators
Breuer
.
This criterion effectively requires operator commutativity without parameter arbitrariness, as typically seen in the von Neumann-Lüders theory
Lueders1951
and quantum information theory
Heinosaari2010
.
These criteria, however, are too restrictive by uniformly disallowing value assignment to non-commuting operators.
The complementarity principle
EPR-Bohr
, in contrast, dictates that quantum theory should admit classical descriptions when uncertainty is large.
Thus, conventional compatibility criteria are
too permissive
for HV theories yet
too restrictive
for quantum theory.

III.2.3
Advantage of the revised criterion

Our intermediate criterion requires well-defined joint probabilities for
a given
density operator.
This criterion admits two types of propositions: (I) those constructed from single-time propositions with commuting projectors, and (II) those that become deterministic for a specific density operator.
The commutativity in this context eliminates parameter arbitrariness.
For single two-level systems, this rule effectively reproduces the conventional criterion in quantum theory, since all deterministic propositions become trivial.
For the spin-1 systems, criterion (I) eliminates incompatible settings, while criterion (II) permits some trivially deterministic propositions.
Hence, our criterion resolves the Kochen-Specker paradox.
For many-level systems, criterion (II) allows propositions with non-commuting projectors and sufficiently large uncertainty—precisely the type of propositions needed to justify classical descriptions.

Still, to justify classical descriptions, we must bridge the gap between energy eigenstates and observable eigenstates.
We consider measurement apparatus for this purpose.
For example, practical particle detectors determine classical trajectories via localized energy eigenstates, as exemplified by cloud chambers that detect specific thermodynamic phases of probe molecules.
The observed trajectories then define empirical maps from energy eigenstates to position/momentum eigenstates.
Here, the former describes a realistic system including the apparatus, while the latter defines an idealized system excluding the apparatus.
If we assume the equivalence of probabilities between the two descriptions, the maps preserve our selection rule.
Then, position/momentum projectors must have well-defined joint probabilities for a given density operator.
This requirement is fulfilled only under criterion (II)—that is, when the projectors cover the entire wavefunction spread (
\Delta{q}\Delta{p}\,{\gg}\,\hbar
).
Consequently, our framework admits classical descriptions within the complementarity principle.
This discussion also shows that our logical variables can be both stochastic and deterministic, depending on uncertainty levels.

We now briefly discuss how this framework circumvents other no-go theorems.
First, our formulation avoids inconsistencies surrounding Gleason’s theorem by not applying the Born rule to individual trials.
We thus
define
logical variables as hidden variables inexpressible by density operators.
Second, our logical variables are distinct from Bell’s HVs since they do not satisfy Bell’s locality assumption.
In our framework, the locality implies the statistical independence of propositions—an impossible assumption even for non-interacting subsystems with local operators.
Third, when logical variables define instantaneous values of observables, the selection rule prohibits arithmetic operations between incompatible logical variables.
This eliminates contradictions raised by Bell-type inequalities (see Section
V
).
To define arithmetic operations between logical variables, however, we need to unravel hidden correlations in individual trials.

III.3
Incorporating Boolean logic

The third modification formalizes Boolean logic as fundamental laws for quantum propositions.
As discussed in Section
I
, logical variables are indifferentiable and do not follow the equations of motion.
Applying the Born rule to binary variables also leads to inconsistencies, as indicated by Gleason’s theorem.
Thus, our framework requires laws for individual trials beyond the equations of motion and the Born rule.
Although such laws remain unexplored due to the long-standing prohibition of HVs, our dual theory allows us to infer the laws for logical variables from the algebraic rules for chain operators.
Crucially, the consistent histories approach has established that chain operators follow Boolean logic
Griffiths2001
; however, its utility was limited by the rejection of HVs and the need for additional consistency conditions.

To establish Boolean logic in the framework of HV theories, we postulate the exclusivity of state occupations—a direct consequence of the observed quantum jumps.
This exclusivity, requiring that only one stationary state be occupied at each time, is indispensable to guarantee that the addition of logical variables again yields a logical variable.
Moreover, it differs from the Pauli exclusion principle, as the jumps are observed in both fermionic and bosonic systems
QuantumJump1
;
Haroche1
.
Given this exclusivity and the new proposition selection rule, Boolean logic (negation
\lnot
, conjunction
\wedge
, and disjunction
\vee
) holds strictly as transformation rules for chain operators and logical variables.
These rules have the same mathematical forms as in digital computing, with the sole exception that a conjunction for chain operators includes a time-ordering symbol
{\mathcal{T}}
.
This symbol is necessary to restore the standard formulas for quantum coherence functions
Glauber1963
.

Previously, the consistent histories approach introduced the consistency condition to guarantee the additivity of chain operators for mutually exclusive histories.
In our dual framework, however, this additivity follows naturally from Boolean logic and the exclusivity, together with the additivity of logical variables.
If we start with Boolean logic and the exclusivity, our proposition selection rule turns out to be a consistency requirement between Boolean logic and the Born rule.
In this way, HVs, exclusivity, Boolean logic, and our proposition selection rule form a tightly constrained theoretical framework, each supporting the others.
This self-consistent logical structure circumvents established no-go theorems, enabling a consistent HV formulation that captures deterministic relations in individual trials.

III.4
Scope and physical background

Based on the three modifications described above, we construct a theory of logical propositions that calculates probabilities using chain operators, while representing truth values using logical variables.
Since this theory appears more fundamental than initially anticipated, we explicitly define our scope.
This paper aims to provide a direct description of individual trials for quantum jumps observed in quantum optics experiments.
We formulate our theory within non-relativistic QED, particularly based on the traditional approaches, as presented in Mandel
Mandel
and Milonni

;
Milonni1993

We address optical coherence, entanglement, and wave-particle duality, with straightforward extensions to include relativistic effects in a fixed frame.
On the other hand, we do not consider covariant formulation, mass renormalization, and gauge ghosts.
These limitations imply that our theory remains phenomenological, awaiting investigations from more fundamental perspectives.

From a practical viewpoint, our analysis builds on the Heisenberg-picture description of macroscopic quantum optical channels.
In these systems, light interference and detection are more naturally described using field operators than density operators

In rigorous modeling of complete transmitter-receiver systems
Milonni2004

For two-level atoms, this approach successfully derives Einstein causality

Our HV formulation emerges when we extend this framework to encompass

Conventionally, the quantum trajectory methods have focused on quantum jumps in light emission processes.
In contrast, our theory directly describes light absorption by considering the occupation of atomic scattering states.

It should be noted that this theory depends on conventional frameworks in describing statistical mixtures.
When describing individual trials in a noisy environment, conditional probabilities must be conditioned on both inter-state transitions and external decoherence.
The former, representing the quantum jumps in our theory, describes jumps associated with unitary evolution by known Hamiltonians.

Although we do not provide such a unified theory in this paper, our formulation, explicitly modeling apparatus, should complement the general information theoretic approach

In this respect, we only object to the unrestricted prohibition of HVs, which stems from the unphysical premises in conventional HV theories.

IV
Formulation

Our theory consists of the following eight assumptions: (1) Born rule, (2) Unitarity, (3) Non-relativistic QED, (4) Duality, (5) Boolean logic, (6) Stationary states, (7) Proposition, and (8) Compatibility.
While conventional formulations use only (1)–(3) and prohibit (4), our formulation justifies (4) and (5) through constraints imposed by (6)–(8).
We present these assumptions in an order that most naturally introduces our basic concepts.

V.6
Light quantum hypothesis

VI
Conclusion

In this study, we have reexamined the fundamental question of whether quantum theory can describe integer functions.
This question, originally raised by Einstein in 1905, created the photon concept and subsequent controversies over quantum observations.
These controversies recently led to decoherence-based descriptions of quantum jumps.
As an alternative, we developed a theory of logical variables, which explicitly addresses unexplored correlations in individual trials.
Our analysis started by acknowledging the duality of observations: that binary logical variables exist experimentally, though differential laws permit no such solutions.
We then identified assumptions that reconcile Boolean logic with quantum theory, establishing Boolean logic as laws governing individual trials.
Specifically, we focused on the occupation of stationary states and optimized the compatibility criterion to enable a direct description of deterministic relations in individual trials.
We found that the exclusivity of state occupations correlates logical variables and, combined with the new compatibility condition, justifies both the hidden variable formulation and Boolean logic.
This correlation imposes nontrivial constraints on arithmetic operations between logical variables, suggesting a novel mechanism for measurement compatibility and contextuality.

More specifically, the fundamental building blocks of our theory are compound propositions that represent specific time sequences of state occupations.
Their probabilities are calculated using chain operators and density operators, while their truth values are represented by binary logical variables that specify the correlation scope through trial indices.
Our eightfold assumptions describe how to apply the Born rule and Boolean logic to these chain operators and logical variables.
We also presented three concrete examples on Bell’s theorems, the original EPR paradox, and the light quantum hypothesis.
We demonstrated that (i) our nonlocal logical variables avoid the classical CHSH bound by prohibiting incompatible arithmetic operations; (ii) mapping stationary state occupations to position/momentum eigenstate occupations provides proxy representations that permit classical descriptions; and (iii) the light quantum hypothesis is a state-specific relation rather than a general law for physical reality.
We also provided a simulation of the double-slit experiment to show the ability to simulate the entire quantum optical channel.
This example also illustrates how our theory describes quantum jumps in the absorption of coherent light—a phenomenon that conventional approaches have struggled to describe.

From a fundamental viewpoint, our approach is unique in postulating both hidden variables and stationary states.
This means that our framework integrates Einstein’s interpretation of quantum theory (allowing hidden variables) with Bohr’s model of physical reality (postulating stationary states).
In our framework, quantum jumps occur stochastically within the unitary evolution of the entire system including measurement apparatus.
As a result, our framework eliminates the need for external observers, describing quantum jumps and wave-particle duality based only on transitions between stationary states.
A remaining challenge is to develop a unified statistical description of inter-state transitions and external decoherence.
Ultimately, our hidden variable formulation raises a new question about what physical reality is.
Further discussion of the definition of stationary states is necessary to refine our phenomenological analysis.
We believe this framework, though currently limited, offers a unique perspective in exploring the fundamental nature of physical reality.

Appendix A
Definition of statistical concepts

This section provides a concise summary of key statistical concepts used throughout the paper.

Exclusivity:

Two propositions ‘
\mathrm{C}_{1},\,t_{1}
’ and ‘
\mathrm{C}_{2},t_{2}
’ are considered mutually exclusive when their joint probability equals zero:

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Pr}}(\mbox{`$\mathrm{C}_{1},\,t_{1}$'}\,{\wedge}\,\mbox{`$\mathrm{C}_{2},t_{2}$'})\equiv\big\langle{x}(\mbox{`$\mathrm{C}_{1},t_{1}$'$\wedge$`$\mathrm{C}_{2},t_{2}$'})\big\rangle=0.

(126)

This implies that their conjunction is always false:

\displaystyle{x^{(j)}}(\mbox{`$\mathrm{C}_{1},t_{1}$'$\wedge$`$\mathrm{C}_{2},t_{2}$'})=0,~~\forall j.

(127)

Consequently, the logical variables of mutually exclusive propositions are always additive.

\displaystyle{x^{(j)}}(\mbox{`$\mathrm{C}_{1},t_{1}$'$\vee$`$\mathrm{C}_{2},t_{2}$'})={x^{(j)}}(\mbox{`$\mathrm{C}_{1},t_{1}$'})+{x^{(j)}}(\mbox{`$\mathrm{C}_{2},t_{2}$'})

\displaystyle,~~\forall j.

(128)

Independence:

Two propositions ‘
\mathrm{C}_{1},t_{1}
’ and ‘
\mathrm{C}_{2},t_{2}
’ are independent if their conditional probabilities equal their marginal probabilities:

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Pr}}(\mbox{`$\mathrm{C}_{1},t_{1}$'}|\mbox{`$\mathrm{C}_{2},t_{2}$'})

\displaystyle={\mathrm{Pr}}(\mbox{`$\mathrm{C}_{1},t_{1}$'})

(129)

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Pr}}(\mbox{`$\mathrm{C}_{2},t_{2}$'}|\mbox{`$\mathrm{C}_{1},t_{1}$'})

\displaystyle={\mathrm{Pr}}(\mbox{`$\mathrm{C}_{2},t_{2}$'}),

(130)

where the conditional probabilities are defined in Eq.(
51
).
For independent propositions, the joint probability factorizes into the product of marginal probabilities as

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Pr}}(\mbox{`$\mathrm{C}_{1},t_{1}$'}\,{\wedge}\,\mbox{`$\mathrm{C}_{2},t_{2}$'})={\mathrm{Pr}}(\mbox{`$\mathrm{C}_{1},t_{1}$'})\,{\mathrm{Pr}}(\mbox{`$\mathrm{C}_{2},t_{2}$'}).

(131)

Deterministic propositions:

A proposition ‘
\mathrm{C}_{1},t_{1}

\displaystyle\langle{x}(\mbox{`$\mathrm{C}_{1},t_{1}$'})\rangle\in\{0,1\}.

A deterministic relation exists between propositions ‘
\mathrm{C}_{1},t_{1}

\displaystyle{x^{(j)}}(\mbox{`$\mathrm{C}_{1},t_{1}$'})={x^{(j)}}(\mbox{`$\mathrm{C}_{2},t_{2}$'}),\quad\forall{j}.

(133)

\displaystyle 1={\mathrm{Pr}}(\mbox{`$\mathrm{C}_{1},t_{1}$'}|\mbox{`$\mathrm{C}_{2},t_{2}$'})={\mathrm{Pr}}(\mbox{`$\mathrm{C}_{2},t_{2}$'}|\mbox{`$\mathrm{C}_{1},t_{1}$'}),

(135)

Note that deterministic relations do not necessarily require that the propositions involved be deterministic.

\displaystyle=\frac{1-p_{\mathrm{TF}}}{\left(p_{\mathrm{TT}}-p_{\mathrm{TF}}\right)}\left\langle{x}(\mbox{`$\mathrm{C}_{1},t_{1}$'})\right\rangle

(144)

\displaystyle=\left(1-p_{\mathrm{TF}}\right)\left\langle{x}(\mbox{`${\mathrm{C}}^{\prime}_{2},t_{2}$'})\right\rangle+\frac{\left(1-p_{\mathrm{TF}}\right)p_{\mathrm{TF}}}{\left(p_{\mathrm{TT}}-p_{\mathrm{TF}}\right)}.

(145)

When the error probabilities are sufficiently small such that
p_{\mathrm{TT}}\,{\cong}\,1
and
p_{\mathrm{TF}}\,{\cong}\,0
, Eq.(
145
) simplifies to

\displaystyle\langle{x}(\mbox{`$\mathrm{C}_{1},t_{1}$'$\wedge$`${\mathrm{C}}^{\prime}_{2},t_{2}$'})\rangle\cong\langle{x}(\mbox{`$\mathrm{C}_{1},t_{1}$'})\rangle\cong\langle{x}(\mbox{`${\mathrm{C}}^{\prime}_{2},t_{2}$'})\rangle.

(146)

Thus, approximate propositions have reasonable statistical properties if errors are independent of given propositions and error probabilities are sufficiently small.

Appendix C
Compatibility of deterministic propositions

This section presents an elementary derivation of the compatibility condition (Eq.(
37
)).
Consider a compound proposition ‘
{\bf C},{\bm{t}}
’ consisting of
N
propositions ‘
{\{{{\mathrm{C}}_{n}}\}},t_{n}
’ (
n\,{=}1,\ldots,N
) and suppose that these propositions are deterministic:

\displaystyle\left\langle{x}(\mbox{`${{\{{\mathrm{C}_{n}}\}}},t_{n}$'})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{{|{\phi_{n}}\rangle}}^{{\{{\mathrm{C}_{n}}\}}}{\langle{\phi_{n}}|}\hat{\rho}(t_{n}){|{\phi_{n}}\rangle}=1.

(147)

Our general assumptions then yield

\displaystyle\left\langle{x}(\mbox{$\lnot$`${{\{{\mathrm{C}_{n}}\}}},t_{n}$'})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{{|{\phi_{n}}\rangle}}^{\notin{\{{\mathrm{C}_{n}}\}}}{\langle{\phi_{n}}|}\hat{\rho}(t_{n}){|{\phi_{n}}\rangle}=0,

(148)

where each term in the sum is non-negative due to the positivity of density operators.
Thus, Eq.(
148
) implies

\displaystyle{\langle{\phi_{n}}|}\hat{\rho}(t_{n}){|{\phi_{n}}\rangle}=0,~~\forall{|{\phi_{n}}\rangle}\notin{\{{\mathrm{C}_{n}}\}}

(149)

Applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to
\hat{\rho}(t)^{1/2}|\phi_{n}\rangle
, we further obtain

\displaystyle|{\langle{\phi_{n}}|}\hat{\rho}(t_{n}){|{\phi_{m}}\rangle}|\leq\sqrt{{\langle{\phi_{n}}|}\hat{\rho}(t_{n}){|{\phi_{n}}\rangle}{\langle{\phi_{m}}|}\hat{\rho}(t_{n}){|{\phi_{m}}\rangle}},

(150)

which, on substituting Eq.(
149
), yields

\displaystyle 0={\langle{\phi_{n}}|}\hat{\rho}(t_{n}){|{\phi^{\prime}_{n}}\rangle}={\langle{\phi^{\prime}_{n}}|}\hat{\rho}(t_{n}){|{\phi_{n}}\rangle},

\displaystyle~~\forall{|{\phi_{n}}\rangle}\,{\notin}\,{\{{\mathrm{C}_{n}}\}},~~\forall{|{\phi^{\prime}_{n}}\rangle}\,{\notin}\,{\{{\mathrm{C}_{n}}\}}.

(151)

Now, denoting the minimum element of
\mathcal{C}
by
n_{1}\equiv\min{\mathcal{C}}
and the remaining elements of
{\mathcal{C}}\,{=}\,\{n_{1},{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}\}
by
{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}
, the left-hand side of the compatibility condition (Eq.(
37
)) can be rewritten as follows:

\displaystyle{\mathrm{tr}}\left[\,\hat{K}_{\mathcal{C}}\,\hat{\rho}(t)\,\hat{K}^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{C}}\,\right]

\displaystyle={\mathrm{tr}}\left[\,\hat{K}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}\,{\hat{z}}(\mbox{`${{\{{{\mathrm{C}}_{n_{1}}}\}}}$'})\,\hat{\rho}(t)\,{\hat{z}^{\dagger}}(\mbox{`${{\{{{\mathrm{C}}_{n_{1}}}\}}}$'})\,\hat{K}^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}\,\right],

(152)

\displaystyle=\sum_{|\phi\rangle}^{{\{{{\mathrm{C}}_{n_{1}}}\}}}\sum_{|\phi^{\prime}\rangle}^{{\{{{\mathrm{C}}_{n_{1}}}\}}}\langle\phi|\hat{K}^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}\hat{K}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}|\phi^{\prime}\rangle\langle\,\phi^{\prime}|\hat{\rho}(t)|\phi\rangle.

(153)

Extending the ranges of the sums using Eq.(
151
), we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathrm{tr}}\Big[\,\hat{K}_{\mathcal{C}}\,\hat{\rho}(t)\,\hat{K}^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{C}}\,\Big]

\displaystyle=\sum^{{\{{{\mathfrak{E}_{n_{1}}}}\}}}_{|\phi\rangle}\sum^{{\{{{\mathfrak{E}_{n_{1}}}}\}}}_{|\phi^{\prime}\rangle}\langle\phi|\hat{K}^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}\hat{K}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}|\phi^{\prime}\rangle\langle\phi^{\prime}|\hat{\rho}(t)|\phi\rangle

(154)

\displaystyle={\mathrm{tr}}\Big[\hat{K}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}\hat{\rho}(t)\hat{K}^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}\Big],

(155)

where
{\{{{\mathfrak{E}_{n_{1}}}}\}}
denotes the complete set of stationary states for time
t_{n_{1}}
.
By repeatedly using Eq.(
155
), we find that

\displaystyle{\mathrm{tr}}\Big[\hat{K}_{\mathcal{C}}\hat{\rho}(t)\hat{K}^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{C}}\Big]={\mathrm{tr}}[\hat{\rho}(t)]=1,

(156)

which shows the left-hand side of Eq.(
37
) is independent of operator ordering.
Similarly, we obtain
{\mathrm{tr}}[\hat{K}_{\mathcal{C}}\hat{\rho}(t)\hat{K}^{\dagger}_{\mathcal{C}}]\,{=}\,0
irrespective of operator ordering when one of the single-time propositions is invariably false for a given density operator.

Appendix D
Non-interacting subsystems

We define non-interacting subsystems as a system approximated by an effective Hamiltonian of the form:

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{0}=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\hat{H}^{(l)}_{0},

(157)

where
\hat{H}^{(l)}_{0}
(
l\,{=}\,1,\ldots,L
) denotes the non-interacting Hamiltonian of the
l
-th subsystem.
For each subsystem, stationary states are defined by the eigenvalue equation

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{0}^{(l)}{|{\phi_{l}}\rangle_{{l}}}={E}^{(l)}(\phi_{l})\,{|{\phi_{l}}\rangle_{{l}}},\quad{}_{{l}}{\langle{\phi_{l}}|{\phi^{\prime}_{l}}\rangle}_{{l}}=\delta_{\phi_{l},\phi^{\prime}_{l}},

(158)

where
{|{\phi_{l}}\rangle_{{l}}}
denotes the stationary state of the
l
-th subsystem characterized by quantum number
\phi_{l}
.
The stationary states of the entire system are tensor products,

\displaystyle|\phi\rangle={|{\phi_{1}}\rangle_{{1}}}{\otimes}\cdots{\otimes}{|{\phi_{L}}\rangle_{{L}}},\quad\phi\equiv(\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{L}),

(159)

and the corresponding projectors have the form:

\displaystyle{\hat{z}}(\mbox{$\mbox{`${{\{\!\!\{{\phi}\}\!\!\}}}$'}$})

\displaystyle={|{\phi}\rangle}{\langle{\phi}|}

(160)

\displaystyle{\hat{z}}(\mbox{$\mbox{`${{\{\!\!\{{\phi_{l}}\}\!\!\}}_{{l}}}$'}$})

\displaystyle={|{\phi_{l}}\rangle_{{l}}}{{}_{{l}}\langle{\phi_{l}}|}.

(161)

Here, all projectors
{\hat{z}}(\mbox{$\mbox{`${{\{\!\!\{{\phi_{l}}\}\!\!\}}_{{l}}}$'}$})
with different
\phi_{l}
and
l
commute with each other, and thus all subsystem propositions ‘
{{\{\!\!\{{\phi_{l}}\}\!\!\}}_{{l}}},t
’ satisfy the Compatibility assumption.

From these definitions, the following equalities hold for non-interacting subsystems:

\displaystyle{\hat{z}}(\mbox{`${{\{\!\!\{{\phi}\}\!\!\}}}$'})

\displaystyle=\prod^{L}_{l=1}{\hat{z}}(\mbox{$\mbox{`${{\{\!\!\{{\phi_{l}}\}\!\!\}}_{{l}}}$'}$})={\hat{z}}\left(\mbox{$\displaystyle\bigwedge^{L}_{l=1}\mbox{`${{\{\!\!\{{\phi_{l}}\}\!\!\}}_{{l}}}$'}$}\right)

(162)

\displaystyle{\hat{x}}(\mbox{`${{\{\!\!\{{\phi}\}\!\!\}}}$'})

\displaystyle=\prod^{L}_{l=1}{\hat{x}}(\mbox{$\mbox{`${{\{\!\!\{{\phi_{l}}\}\!\!\}}_{{l}}}$'}$})={\hat{x}}\left(\mbox{$\displaystyle\bigwedge^{L}_{l=1}\mbox{`${{\{\!\!\{{\phi_{l}}\}\!\!\}}_{{l}}}$'}$}\right),

(163)

which, by the Born rule and Boolean logic, lead to the equality for the expectation value:

\displaystyle\left\langle{x}(\mbox{`${{\{\!\!\{{\phi}\}\!\!\}}}$'})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle{x}\left(\mbox{$\displaystyle\bigwedge^{L}_{l=1}\mbox{`${{\{\!\!\{{\phi_{l}}\}\!\!\}}_{{l}}}$'}$}\right)\right\rangle=\left\langle\prod^{L}_{l=1}{x}(\mbox{$\mbox{`${{\{\!\!\{{\phi_{l}}\}\!\!\}}_{{l}}}$'}$})\right\rangle.

(164)

Based on the Duality assumption, we assume that the corresponding relations also hold for truth values:

\displaystyle{x^{(j)}}(\mbox{`${{\{\!\!\{{\phi}\}\!\!\}}}$'})

\displaystyle={x^{(j)}}\left(\mbox{$\displaystyle\bigwedge^{L}_{l=1}\mbox{`${{\{\!\!\{{\phi_{l}}\}\!\!\}}_{{l}}}$'}$}\right)=\prod^{L}_{l=1}{x^{(j)}}(\mbox{$\mbox{`${{\{\!\!\{{\phi_{l}}\}\!\!\}}_{{l}}}$'}$}).

(165)

From Eqs.(
162
)–(
163
) and Eq.(
165
), we obtain the formal identity between propositions:

\displaystyle\mbox{`${{\{\!\!\{{\phi}\}\!\!\}}}$'}{}\,{=}\,\bigwedge^{L}_{l=1}\mbox{`${{\{\!\!\{{\phi_{l}}\}\!\!\}}_{{l}}}$'}.

(166)

We can now see that projectors, observables, and logical variables factor into those in subsystems.
When the density operator is not separable, however, none of these relations require the factorizability of the expectation value:

\displaystyle\left\langle{x}(\mbox{`${{\{\!\!\{{\phi}\}\!\!\}}}$'})\right\rangle=\left\langle\prod^{L}_{l=1}{x}(\mbox{$\mbox{`${{\{\!\!\{{\phi_{l}}\}\!\!\}}_{{l}}}$'}$})\right\rangle\neq\prod^{L}_{l=1}\left\langle{x}(\mbox{$\mbox{`${{\{\!\!\{{\phi_{l}}\}\!\!\}}_{{l}}}$'}$})\right\rangle.

(167)

In other words, subsystem propositions ‘
{{\{\!\!\{{\phi_{l}}\}\!\!\}}_{{l}}}
’ may not be statistical independent even when operators are local and subsystems do not interact.
This shows that our logical variables are generally nonlocal in Bell’s sense.

EPR state vector

This section shows the explicit forms of the wavefunctions corresponding to Eqs.(

)–(
86
).
In the original EPR paper, they considered a two-particle wavefunction of the form:

\displaystyle\phi({\bm{r}}_{\mathrm{A}},{\bm{r}}_{\mathrm{B}})=\phi({\bm{r}},{\bm{R}})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{V}}\delta({\bm{r}}\,{-}\,{\bm{r}}_{0})

(168)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{V}}\iiint\delta({\bm{r}}_{\mathrm{A}}\,{-}\,{\bm{r}}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{B}}-{\bm{r}}_{0})\delta({\bm{r}}_{\mathrm{B}}\,{-}\,{\bm{r}}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{B}})d{\bm{r}}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{B}},

(169)

where
{\bm{r}}
and
{\bm{R}}
denote the relative position and the center-of-mass position, respectively.
The factor

stems from the center-of-mass wavefunction
\chi({\bm{R}})=e^{-{\mathrm{i}}{\bm{P}}\cdot{\bm{R}}/\hbar}/\sqrt{V}
for
{\bm{P}}\,{=}\,{\bm{p}}_{\mathrm{A}}\,{+}\,{\bm{p}}_{\mathrm{B}}\,{=}\,{\bf 0}
, where
{\bm{P}}
denotes the center-of-mass momentum.
In the momentum representation, this wavefunction becomes

\displaystyle\phi({\bm{p}}_{\mathrm{A}},{\bm{p}}_{\mathrm{B}})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{V}}e^{-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}{\bm{p}}_{\mathrm{A}}\cdot{\bm{r}}_{0}}\delta({\bm{p}}_{\mathrm{A}}+{\bm{p}}_{\mathrm{B}})

(170)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{V}}\iiint e^{-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}{\bm{p}}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{A}}\cdot{\bm{r}}_{0}}\delta({\bm{p}}_{\mathrm{A}}-{\bm{p}}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{A}})\delta({\bm{p}}_{\mathrm{B}}+{\bm{p}}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{A}})d{\bm{p}}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{A}}.

(171)

These equations directly yield Eqs.(
83
)–(
86
).

Appendix F
Photoionization rate

In this section, we present a formal derivation of the standard photoionization rate formula under approximations commonly used in quantum optics.

To this end, suppose that stationary states of the photodetector material can be classified into two subsets
{\{{E>0}\}_{{\mathrm{A}}}}
and
{\{{E<0}\}_{{\mathrm{A}}}}
such that the minimum eigenenergy of the former is larger than the maximum eigenenergy of the latter.
For every stationary state
{|{\phi_{+}}\rangle_{{\mathrm{A}}}}
in the upper manifold
{\{{E>0}\}_{{\mathrm{A}}}}
, let de-excitation operators be defined by

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}(\phi_{+})\equiv{|{\mathrm{ref}_{-}}\rangle_{{\mathrm{A}}}}{{}_{{\mathrm{A}}}\langle{\phi_{+}}|},

(172)

where
{|{\mathrm{ref}_{-}}\rangle_{{\mathrm{A}}}}
is a reference state taken from the lower manifold
{\{{E<0}\}_{{\mathrm{A}}}}
.
Excitation operators are Hermitian conjugates of the de-excitation operators.
By Eq.(
172
) and the definition of stationary states, all de-excitation and excitation operators commute except for the combination of
\hat{\pi}(\phi_{+})
and
\hat{\pi}^{\dagger}(\phi^{\prime}_{+})
with
\phi_{+}\,{=}\,\phi^{\prime}_{+}
.
We assume that all subsystem are initially in one of the lower states:

\displaystyle\hat{0}=\hat{\pi}(\phi_{+})\hat{\rho}=\hat{\rho}\hat{\pi}^{\dagger}(\phi_{+})

(173)

and that the photodetector material is only weakly excited at the time of interest:

\displaystyle{\mathrm{tr}}\left[\hat{\pi}^{\dagger}(\phi_{+})\hat{\pi}(\phi_{+})\hat{\rho}(t)\right]={{}_{{\mathrm{A}}}\langle{\phi_{+}}|}\hat{\rho}(t){|{\phi_{+}}\rangle_{{\mathrm{A}}}}\ll 1.

(174)

Using the definition of proposition ‘ionized,
t
’ (Eqs.(
47
)–(
48
)), we obtain the following expressions for the photoionization probability:

\displaystyle\langle{x}(\mbox{`ionized,$t$'})\rangle

\displaystyle={\rm{tr}}\left[{\hat{z}}(\mbox{`ionized,$t$'})\,\hat{\rho}\,{\hat{z}^{\dagger}}(\mbox{`ionized,$t$'})\right]

(175)

\displaystyle={\rm{tr}}\left[\,\left(\sum_{{|{{\phi_{+}}}\rangle_{{\mathrm{A}}}}}^{{\{{E>0}\}_{{\mathrm{A}}}}}{|{{\phi_{+}}}\rangle_{{\mathrm{A}}}}{{}_{{\mathrm{A}}}\langle{{\phi_{+}}}|}\right)\hat{\rho}\,\right]

(176)

\displaystyle={\rm{tr}}\left[\,\left(\sum_{{|{{\phi_{+}}}\rangle_{{\mathrm{A}}}}}^{{\{{E>0}\}_{{\mathrm{A}}}}}\hat{\pi}^{\dagger}(\phi_{+},t)\,\hat{\pi}(\phi_{+},t)\right)\hat{\rho}\,\right],

(177)

which is the starting point for the derivation of standard photoionization rate formula
[
39
]
.

To evaluate Eq.(
177
), we use the first-order perturbation approximation:

\displaystyle\hat{U}(t)

\displaystyle=\hat{U}_{0}(t)+\frac{1}{{\rm{i}}\hbar}\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\hat{U}_{0}(t-t^{\prime})\hat{H}_{\mathrm{I}}\hat{U}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}

(178)

\displaystyle\cong\hat{U}_{0}(t)+\hat{U}_{1}(t),

(179)

where the zero-th and first order contributions take the form:

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{0}(t)\equiv e^{-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}\hat{H}_{0}t},~~\hat{U}_{1}(t)\equiv\hat{U}_{0}(t)\,\frac{1}{{\rm{i}}\hbar}\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\tilde{H}_{\mathrm{I}}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}.

(180)

The Heisenberg-picture and interaction-picture operators are defined as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{O}(t)

\displaystyle\equiv\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t)\hat{O}\hat{U}(t)

(181)

\displaystyle\tilde{O}(t)

\displaystyle\equiv\hat{U}_{0}^{\dagger}(t)\hat{O}\hat{U}_{0}(t).

(182)

Applying the long wavelength approximation and neglecting the non-linear term with respect to
\tilde{\bm{A}}_{\mathrm{T}}
, the interaction Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}_{\mathrm{I}}(t)
in Eq.(
180
) takes the form:

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{\mathrm{I}}(t)\cong-\sum^{K}_{k}\frac{e_{k}}{m_{k}}{\tilde{\bm{p}}}_{k}(t)\cdot\tilde{\bm{A}}_{\mathrm{T}}({\bm{r}}_{0},t),

(183)

where
{\bm{r}}_{0}
represents the location of the photoionization site.

Eqs.(
179
)–(
183
) cast the Heisenberg-picture de-excitation operator into the form:

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}(\phi_{+},t)

\displaystyle\cong\tilde{\pi}(\phi_{+},t)+\tilde{\pi}(\phi_{+},t)\left(\frac{1}{{\rm{i}}\hbar}\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\tilde{H}_{\mathrm{I}}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~-\left(\frac{1}{{\rm{i}}\hbar}\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\tilde{H}_{\mathrm{I}}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}\right)\tilde{\pi}(\phi_{+},t)

(184)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\pi}(\phi_{+},t)+\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{\bm{h}}(\phi_{+},t,t^{\prime})\cdot{\tilde{\bm{A}}_{\mathrm{T}}}({\bm{r}}_{0},t^{\prime})dt^{\prime},

(185)

where the homogeneous term
\tilde{\pi}(\phi_{+},t)
is defined by

\displaystyle\tilde{\pi}(\phi_{+},t)\equiv\hat{\pi}(\phi_{+})e^{-{\rm{i}}\omega_{\phi_{+}}t},

(186)

with
\omega_{\phi_{+}}\,{\equiv}\,(E(\phi_{+})-E({\mathrm{ref}}_{-}))/{\hbar}
as the transition angular frequency.
The coefficient
\tilde{\bm{h}}(\phi_{+},t,t^{\prime})
has the form:

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{h}}(\phi_{+},t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\equiv\left[\frac{1}{{\rm{i}}\hbar}\sum^{K}_{k=1}\frac{e_{k}}{m_{k}}\tilde{\bm{p}}_{k}(t^{\prime}),\tilde{\pi}(\phi_{+},t)\right]_{-}

(187)

\displaystyle\cong-\sum_{{|{{\phi_{-}}}\rangle_{{\mathrm{A}}}}}^{{\{{E<0}\}_{{\mathrm{A}}}}}\left(E(\phi_{+})-E(\phi_{-})\right){{}_{{\mathrm{A}}}\langle{{\phi_{+}}}|}\hat{\bm{D}}{|{{\phi_{-}}}\rangle_{{\mathrm{A}}}}

\displaystyle~~~~e^{{\rm{i}}(\omega_{\phi_{+}}-\omega_{\phi_{-}})t^{\prime}}e^{-{\rm{i}}\omega_{\phi_{+}}t}\,{|{{\mathrm{ref}}_{-}}\rangle_{{\mathrm{A}}}}{{}_{{\mathrm{A}}}\langle{{\phi_{-}}}|},

(188)

where
\hat{\bm{D}}\,{\equiv}\,\sum^{K}_{k=1}e_{k}\hat{\bm{r}}_{k}
is the dipole moment operator.
To obtain Eq.(
188
), we used the well-known identity

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{\rm{i}}\hbar}\sum^{K}_{k=1}\frac{e_{k}}{m_{k}}\hat{\bm{p}}_{k}=\left[\hat{H}_{0},\hat{\bm{D}}\right]_{-}

(189)

and dropped diagonal and near-diagonal matrix elements of
\hat{\bm{D}}
, which are non-dominant.
We also neglected the population of the upper manifold against the lower manifold.
These approximations are not specific to our formulation, but approximations for Heisenberg operators (like Eq.(
185
)) are valid only when evaluating probabilities for a specific density operator.

Below, we summarize the standard derivation
[
39
]
[§14.2] to confirm that our assumptions do not conflict with the standard calculation procedure.
Substituting Eq.(
185
) into Eq.(
177
) and differentiating with respect to time, we find that the photoionization rate has the form:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\langle{x}(\mbox{`ionized',\,$t$})\rangle=\frac{d}{dt}{\rm{tr}}\Bigg[\Bigg(\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{2}\sum_{\xi}^{x,y,z}\sum_{\xi^{\prime}}^{x,y,z}

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~\hat{\zeta}_{\xi\xi^{\prime}}(t_{1}-t_{2}){\tilde{A}_{{\mathrm{T}}\xi}}({\bm{r}}_{0},t_{2}){\tilde{A}_{{\mathrm{T}}\xi^{\prime}}}({\bm{r}}_{0},t_{1})\Bigg)\hat{\rho}\,\Bigg],

(190)

where used Eq.(
173
) to drop the homogeneous term
\tilde{\pi}(\phi_{+},t)
.
We focus on a quasi-monochromatic light centered at an angular frequency
\omega_{\mathrm{o}}
and assume that an exposure time
t
is sufficiently longer than the optical period
2\pi/\omega_{\mathrm{o}}
.
To distinguish between terms containing annihilation and creation operators, we divide the field operator into its positive and negative oscillating parts:

\displaystyle{\tilde{A}}_{\mathrm{T}\xi}({\bm{r}}_{0},t)={\tilde{A}}_{\mathrm{T}\xi}^{(+)}({\bm{r}}_{0},t)+{\tilde{A}}_{\mathrm{T}\xi}^{(-)}({\bm{r}}_{0},t).

(191)

Substituting Eq.(
191
) into Eq.(
190
), the product
{\tilde{A}_{{\mathrm{T}}\xi}}({\bm{r}}_{0},t_{2}){\tilde{A}_{{\mathrm{T}}\xi^{\prime}}}({\bm{r}}_{0},t_{1})
yields the sum of a commutation relation and a normally-ordered product:

\displaystyle:{\tilde{A}_{{\mathrm{T}}\xi}}({\bm{r}}_{0},t_{2}){\tilde{A}_{{\mathrm{T}}\xi^{\prime}}}({\bm{r}}_{0},t_{1}):

(192)

\displaystyle\equiv{\tilde{A}^{(+)}_{{\mathrm{T}}\xi}}({\bm{r}}_{0},t_{2}){\tilde{A}^{(+)}_{{\mathrm{T}}\xi^{\prime}}}({\bm{r}}_{0},t_{1})+{\tilde{A}^{(-)}_{{\mathrm{T}}\xi}}({\bm{r}}_{0},t_{2}){\tilde{A}^{(-)}_{{\mathrm{T}}\xi^{\prime}}}({\bm{r}}_{0},t_{1})

\displaystyle+{\tilde{A}^{(-)}_{{\mathrm{T}}\xi}}({\bm{r}}_{0},t_{2}){\tilde{A}^{(+)}_{{\mathrm{T}}\xi^{\prime}}}({\bm{r}}_{0},t_{1})+{\tilde{A}^{(+)}_{{\mathrm{T}}\xi}}({\bm{r}}_{0},t_{2}){\tilde{A}^{(-)}_{{\mathrm{T}}\xi^{\prime}}}({\bm{r}}_{0},t_{1}).

Using the explicit form of
\hat{\zeta}_{\xi\xi^{\prime}}(t_{1}-t_{2})
, we find that the contribution of the commutation relation—so-called “vacuum contribution to the light detection probability”—vanishes for
t\,{\gg}\,2\pi/\omega_{\mathrm{o}}
.
Dropping the rapidly oscillating terms at double the optical frequency, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\langle{x}(\mbox{`ionized',\,$t$})\rangle

(193)

\displaystyle={\rm{tr}}\left[\left(\sum_{\xi}^{x,y,z}\sum_{\xi^{\prime}}^{x,y,z}\hat{\kappa}_{\xi\xi^{\prime}}(\omega_{\mathrm{o}}){\tilde{A}_{{\mathrm{T}}\xi}}^{(-)}({\bm{r}}_{0},t){\tilde{A}_{{\mathrm{T}}\xi^{\prime}}}^{(+)}({\bm{r}}_{0},t)\right)\hat{\rho}\,\right],

where we defined the operator coefficient
\hat{\kappa}_{\xi\xi^{\prime}}(\omega_{\mathrm{o}})
by

\displaystyle\hat{\kappa}_{\xi\xi^{\prime}}(\omega_{\mathrm{o}})

\displaystyle\cong\sum_{{|{\phi_{+}}\rangle_{{\mathrm{A}}}}}^{{\{{E>0}\}_{{\mathrm{A}}}}}\sum_{{|{\phi_{-}}\rangle_{{\mathrm{A}}}}}^{{\{{E<0}\}_{{\mathrm{A}}}}}2\pi\,\delta\left(\omega_{\phi_{+}}-\omega_{\phi_{-}}-\omega_{\mathrm{o}}\right)\left(E(\phi_{+})-E(\phi_{-})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle~~~~{{}_{{\mathrm{A}}}\langle{\phi_{-}}|}\hat{D}_{\xi}{|{\phi_{+}}\rangle_{{\mathrm{A}}}}{{}_{{\mathrm{A}}}\langle{\phi_{+}}|}\hat{D}_{\xi^{\prime}}{|{\phi_{-}}\rangle_{{\mathrm{A}}}}\,{|{\phi_{-}}\rangle_{{\mathrm{A}}}}{{}_{{\mathrm{A}}}\langle{\phi_{-}}|}.

(194)

The combination of Eqs.(
193
)–(
194
) agrees with Eq.(14.2-24) of Mandel&Wolf, except that the double sum is replaced by an energy integral weighted by the atomic density of states.

Appendix G
Photoelectric effect of hydrogen atoms

As a more concrete example, this section examines photoionization rate calculations for the photoelectric effect in hydrogen atoms
[
42
,
72
]
.
For hydrogen atoms, the non-interacting Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{0}
takes the form:

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{0}=\hat{H}_{{\bm{r}}\mathrm{S}}+\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}+\hat{H}_{\mathrm{F}},

(195)

where the Hamiltonians for relative motion (
{\bm{r}}
), Zeeman interaction (
\mathrm{S}
), center-of-mass motion (
\bm{R}
), and radiation field (
\mathrm{F}
) are respectively given by

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{{\bm{r}}\mathrm{S}}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{2m}\|{\hat{\bm{p}}}\|^{2}-\frac{e^{2}}{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}}\frac{1}{\left\|\hat{\bm{r}}\right\|}

\displaystyle~~~~+\frac{g_{\mathrm{p}}e}{2m_{\mathrm{p}}}{\hat{\bm{s}}}_{\mathrm{p}}\cdot\hat{\bm{B}}(\hat{\bm{r}}_{\mathrm{p}})-\frac{g_{\mathrm{e}}e}{2m_{\mathrm{e}}}{\hat{\bm{s}}}_{\mathrm{e}}\cdot\hat{\bm{B}}(\hat{\bm{r}}_{\mathrm{e}})

(196)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{2M}\|{\hat{\bm{P}}}\|^{2}

(197)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle\equiv\iiint\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{2}\left[\|\hat{\bm{E}}_{\mathrm{T}}({\bm{x}})\|^{2}+{c^{2}}\|\hat{\bm{B}}({\bm{x}})\|^{2}\right]d{\bm{x}}.

(198)

\hat{\bm{r}}_{k}
,
\hat{\bm{p}}_{k}
, and
{\hat{\bm{s}}}_{k}
(

) denote the position, momentum, and spin of the electron and proton, respectively.
The stationary states of the entire system have the form:

\displaystyle|\phi\rangle=|\phi\rangle^{\!{\bm{r}}\mathrm{S}}\otimes|\phi\rangle^{\!\bf R}\otimes\prod_{{\bm{k}}\lambda}|\phi\rangle^{\!{\bm{k}}\lambda},

(199)

where
|\phi\rangle^{\!{\bm{r}}\mathrm{S}}
,

, and

denote the orthonormalized eigenstates of
\hat{H}_{{\bm{r}}\mathrm{S}}
,
\hat{H}_{\bm{R}}
, and
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{F}}
, respectively.

{\bm{k}}\,{\in}\,{\mathbb{R}^{3}}
and
\lambda\,{\in}\,\{1,2\}
denote the wavenumber and polarization of each field mode.

We now find that Eqs.(
195
)–(
198
) define non-interacting subsystems.
Moreover, the eigenenergies of scattering states are almost independent of spin directions when external magnetic fields are not applied, although the Zeeman interaction in Eq.(

Hence, the atomic system proposition ‘
{{\{{E>0}\}_{{\mathrm{A}}}}}
’ describing the occupation of scattering states can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mbox{`${{\{{E>0}\}_{{\mathrm{A}}}}}$'}=\mbox{`${{\{{E>0}\}_{{{\bm{r}}}}}}$'},

(200)

where the scattering states for the relative motion are defined by

\displaystyle{\{{E>0}\}_{{{\bm{r}}}}}\equiv

\displaystyle\left\{\,{|{{\bm{p}}}\rangle_{{{\bm{r}}}}}\,\Big|\,\hat{H}_{\bm{r}}{|{{\bm{p}}}\rangle_{{{\bm{r}}}}}=E^{\bm{r}}({\bm{p}}){|{{\bm{p}}}\rangle_{{{\bm{r}}}}},\,{}_{{{\bm{r}}}}{\langle{{\bm{p}}}|{{\bm{p}}^{\prime}}\rangle}_{{{\bm{r}}}}=\delta_{{\bm{p}},{\bm{p}}^{\prime}},\right.

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~\left.E^{\bm{r}}({\bm{p}})\geq 0\phantom{\Big|}\right\}.

(201)

In this expression, each
{\bm{p}}
denotes a relative momentum of the incoming plane wave that generates outgoing partial waves (
\approx 1/\|{\bm{r}}\|

Substituting Eq.(

) into Eq.(
176

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\big\langle{x}(\mbox{`$\mathrm{ionized},t$'})\big\rangle=\frac{d}{dt}\iiint{{}_{{\bm{r}}}\langle{{\bm{p}}}|}\hat{\rho}_{\bm{r}}(t){|{{\bm{p}}}\rangle_{{\bm{r}}}}\,d{\bm{p}},

where the reduced density operator is defined by

(203)

with
{\mathrm{tr}}_{\overline{\bm{r}}}
denoting the partial trace over all degrees of freedom except for the relative motion.
When the initial state is separable and pure, the reduced density operator has a relatively simple form in the first-order perturbation approximation.
Consider the simplest case where the atom is initially in the 1s state
{|{\mbox{1s}}\rangle_{{{\bm{r}}}}}

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}={|{\mbox{1s}}\rangle_{{{\bm{r}}}}}{{}_{{{\bm{r}}}}\langle{\mbox{1s}}|}\otimes{|{\mbox{rad}}\rangle_{{{\bm{k}}\lambda}}}{{}_{{{\bm{k}}\lambda}}\langle{\mbox{rad}}|}\otimes\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{r.o.w.}},

(204)

where
{|{\mbox{rad}}\rangle_{{{\bm{k}}\lambda}}}
denotes the initial state of the
{\bm{k}}\lambda

\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{r.o.w.}}
represents the density operator of the rest of the world.
Substituting Eq.(
204
) and Eq.(
179
) into Eq.(
203

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{\bm{r}}(t)

\displaystyle\cong{|{\psi(t)}\rangle_{{\bm{r}}}}{{}_{{\bm{r}}}\langle{\psi(t)}|}

(205)

\displaystyle{|{\psi(t)}\rangle_{{\bm{r}}}}

\displaystyle\equiv\hat{U}_{\bm{r}}(t)|\mathrm{1s}\rangle,

(206)

where the reduced time evolution operator
\hat{U}_{\bm{r}}(t)
for the
n\,{=}\,1

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{\bm{r}}(t)={\mathrm{tr}}_{\overline{{\bm{r}}{\bm{k}}\lambda}}\left[\,{{}_{{{{\bm{k}}\lambda}}}\langle{1}|}\hat{U}_{0}(t){|{1}\rangle_{{{{\bm{k}}\lambda}}}}+{{}_{{{{\bm{k}}\lambda}}}\langle{0}|}\hat{U}_{1}(t){|{1}\rangle_{{{{\bm{k}}\lambda}}}}\,\,\right],

(207)

while for the coherent state

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{\bm{r}}(t)={\mathrm{tr}}_{\overline{{\bm{r}}{\bm{k}}\lambda}}\left[\,{{}_{{{{\bm{k}}\lambda}}}\langle{\tilde{\alpha}}|}\hat{U}_{0}(t)+\hat{U}_{1}(t){|{\tilde{\alpha}}\rangle_{{{{\bm{k}}\lambda}}}}\,\right].

(208)

To obtain these expressions, we employed similar approximations, as shown in Section

Notably, the time evolution is semiclassical, since all field operators are replaced by their matrix elements.

When the angular optical frequency
\omega_{\mathrm{o}}\,{\equiv}\,c\|{\bm{k}}\|

\omega_{\mathrm{o}}\,{>}\,|E({\mathrm{1s}})|{/}\hbar

\displaystyle{|{\psi(t)}\rangle_{{\bm{r}}}}=c_{\mathrm{1s}}(t){|{\mbox{1s}}\rangle_{{\bm{r}}}}+\iiint c_{\bm{p}}(t){|{{\bm{p}}}\rangle_{{\bm{r}}}}d{\bm{p}},

where the c-number coefficients are given by

\displaystyle c_{\mathrm{1s}}(t)

\displaystyle={{}_{{\bm{r}}}\langle{\mbox{1s}}|}\hat{U}_{\bm{r}}(t){|{\mbox{1s}}\rangle_{{\bm{r}}}}

(210)

\displaystyle c_{\bm{p}}(t)

\displaystyle={{}_{{\bm{r}}}\langle{{\bm{p}}}|}\hat{U}_{\bm{r}}(t){|{\mbox{1s}}\rangle_{{\bm{r}}}}.

(211)

Substituting Eq.(
205

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left\langle{x}(\mbox{`$\mathrm{ionized},t$'})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\lim_{\Delta{t}{\to}0}\iiint\frac{1}{\Delta{t}}\left(|c_{\bm{p}}(t+\Delta{t})|^{2}-|c_{\bm{p}}(t)|^{2}\right)\,d{\bm{p}}.

(212)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left\langle{x}(\mbox{`$\mathrm{ionized},t$'})\right\rangle

\displaystyle\approx\mbox{const.}\iiint\frac{\cos^{2}({\bm{e}}_{\lambda},{\bm{p}})}{\nu_{o}^{7/2}}\,\delta\left(\frac{\|{\bm{p}}\|^{2}}{2m}-E_{\mathrm{1s}}-h\nu_{o}\right)d{\bm{p}}

\displaystyle~~~~\times|c_{\mathrm{1s}}(t)|^{2}\times\mbox{(light power)},

(213)

where
{\bm{e}}_{\lambda}
is the unit vector indicating the linear polarization vector, and
({\bm{e}}_{\lambda},{\bm{p}})
denotes the angle between
{\bm{e}}_{\lambda}
and
{\bm{p}}

Note that Eq.(

\displaystyle h\nu_{o}+E_{\mathrm{1s}}=\frac{\|{\bm{p}}\|^{2}}{2m}.

(214)

A more specific derivation is found in Chap.5 of Loudon’s textbook
[
42
]
.

If we take advantages of

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left\langle{x}(\mbox{`$\mathrm{ionized},t$'})\right\rangle=-\frac{d}{dt}|c_{\mathrm{1s}}(t)|^{2}.

By comparing Eq.(

\displaystyle\big\langle{x}(\mbox{$\lnot$`$\mathrm{ionized},t$'})\big\rangle=e^{-\gamma{t}},

where the decay constant is given by

\displaystyle\gamma\approx

\displaystyle\mathrm{\,const.}\iiint\frac{\cos^{2}({\bm{p}},{\bm{e}}_{\lambda})}{\nu_{o}^{7/2}}\,\delta\left(\frac{\|{\bm{p}}\|^{2}}{2m}+\Big|E_{\mathrm{1s}}\Big|-h\nu_{o}\right)d{\bm{p}}

\displaystyle\times\mbox{(light power)}.

(217)

Appendix H
Derivation of a useful relation

In this section, we derive a useful relation for the expectation values of normally-ordered products of de-excitation and excitation operators.
This relation plays a key role in deriving joint detection probabilities for multiple photodetectors (Eq.(
115
)).

Statement:

Consider a system consisting of
I
non-interacting subsystems and classify each subsystem’ stationary states into two sets
{\{{E>0}\}_{{i}}}
and
{\{{E<0}\}_{{i}}}
(
i\,{=}\,1,\ldots,I
), according to the sign of eigenenergy
E
(see Section
F
).
For every upper state
{|{\phi}\rangle_{{i}}}
in
{\{{E>0}\}_{{i}}}
, let de-excitation operator
\hat{\pi}_{i}(\phi)
and excitation operator
\hat{\pi}^{\dagger}_{i}(\phi)
be defined by

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}_{i}(\phi)\equiv{|{\mathrm{ref}_{i}}\rangle_{{i}}}{{}_{{i}}\langle{\phi}|},~~\hat{\pi}^{\dagger}_{i}(\phi)\equiv{|{\phi}\rangle_{{i}}}{{}_{{i}}\langle{\mathrm{ref}_{i}}|},

(218)

where
{|{\mathrm{ref}_{i}}\rangle_{{i}}}
is a reference state taken from
{\{{E<0}\}_{{i}}}
.
Due to the definition of stationary states, these operators commute except for the combination of
\hat{\pi}_{i}(\phi)
and
\hat{\pi}^{\dagger}_{i^{\prime}}(\phi^{\prime})
for
i\,{=}\,i^{\prime}
and
\phi\,{=}\,\phi^{\prime}
.
We assume that all subsystem are initially in one of the lower states
{\{{E<0}\}_{{i}}}
, which requires

\displaystyle 0=\hat{\pi}_{i}(\phi)\hat{\rho}=\hat{\rho}\hat{\pi}_{i}^{\dagger}(\phi),~~\forall\phi\in{\{{E>0}\}_{{i}}}.

(219)

Now, consider evaluating a trace of the form:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Z}}_{N}

\displaystyle\equiv{\mathrm{tr}}\left[\left({\mathcal{T}}\prod_{n=1}^{N}{\hat{z}}\left(\mbox{`${{\mathrm{C}}_{n}},t_{n}$'}\right)\right)\hat{\rho}\left({\mathcal{T}}\prod_{n=1}^{N}{\hat{z}}\left(\mbox{`${{\mathrm{C}}_{n}},t_{n}$'}\right)\right)^{\dagger}\right],

(220)

where the projectors are given by

\displaystyle{\hat{z}}\left(\mbox{`${{\mathrm{C}}_{n}},t_{n}$'}\right)=\sum_{{|{\phi}\rangle_{{i_{n}}}}}^{{\{{E>0}\}_{{i_{n}}}}}\hat{\pi}_{i_{n}}^{\dagger}(\phi,t_{n})\,\hat{\pi}_{i_{n}}(\phi,t_{n}),

(221)

and
i_{n}
denotes the index of a subsystem described by ‘
{{\mathrm{C}}_{n}},t_{n}
’.
Under the first-order perturbation approximation, the de-excitation operators take the form:

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}_{i}(\phi,t)\cong\tilde{\pi}_{i}(\phi,t)+\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{\bm{h}}_{i}(\phi,t,t^{\prime})\cdot{\tilde{\bm{A}}_{\mathrm{T}}}({\bm{r}}_{i},t^{\prime})dt^{\prime},

(222)

where we neglected the non-linear interaction and used the long-wavelength approximation.
The homogeneous term and the operator coefficient are defined by

\displaystyle\tilde{\pi}_{i}(\phi,t)=\hat{\pi}_{i}(\phi)e^{-{\rm{i}}\frac{E_{i}(\phi)-E_{i}({\mathrm{ref}}_{-})}{\hbar}t}

(223)

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{h}}_{i}(\phi,t,t^{\prime})\equiv\left[(\hat{H}_{0,i}\hat{\bm{D}}_{i}-\hat{\bm{D}}_{i}\hat{H}_{0,i}),\tilde{\pi}_{i}(\phi,t)\right]_{-},

(224)

where
\hat{H}_{0,i}
and
\hat{\bm{D}}_{i}
are the non-interacting Hamiltonian and the dipole moment operator for the
i
-th subsystem, respectively (see Eq.(
189
)).

{\bm{r}}_{i}
represents the location of the
i
-th subsystem.

Under these conditions, Eq.(
222
) can be replaced by the following simpler expression when evaluating the trace in Eq.(
220
):

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}_{i}(\phi,t)=\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{\bm{h}}(\phi,t,t^{\prime})\cdot{\tilde{\bm{A}}^{(+)}_{\mathrm{T}}}({\bm{r}}_{i},t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}.

(225)

Note:

This expression is applicable to most practical photodetector analyses, but not necessarily for two-level atoms where the first-order perturbation approximation fails when evaluating the long-time behavior.
Below, we suppress the time-ordering symbol in Eq.(
220
) by assuming
t_{1}\,{\leq}\,t_{2}\,{\leq}\,{\cdots}\,{\leq}\,t_{N}
.

Proof:

To show that the homogeneous term
\tilde{\pi}_{i}(\phi,t)
in Eq.(
222
) does not contribute to
{\mathcal{Z}}_{N}
, consider an operator
\hat{\Theta}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\Theta}_{N}
defined by the asymptotic equation,

\displaystyle\hat{\Theta}_{n+1}={\hat{z}}(\mbox{`${{\{{\mathrm{C}_{n}}\}}},t_{n}$'})\,\hat{\Theta}_{n}\,{\hat{z}^{\dagger}}(\mbox{`${{\{{\mathrm{C}_{n}}\}}},t_{n}$'}),~~\hat{\Theta}_{1}\equiv\hat{\rho},

(226)

and let Eq.(
220
) be expressed as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Z}}_{N}={\mathrm{tr}}\left[\hat{\Theta}_{N}\right].

(227)

Substituting Eq.(
221
) into Eq.(
226
), we have

\displaystyle\hat{\Theta}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{|{\phi_{n}}\rangle_{{i_{n}}}}}^{{\{{E>0}\}_{{i_{n}}}}}\sum_{{|{\phi^{\prime}_{n}}\rangle_{{i_{n}}}}}^{{\{{E>0}\}_{{i_{n}}}}}\hat{\pi}_{i_{n}}^{\dagger}(\phi_{n},t_{n})\,\hat{\pi}_{i_{n}}(\phi_{n},t_{n})\,\hat{\Theta}_{n}\,

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\hat{\pi}_{i_{n}}^{\dagger}(\phi^{\prime}_{n},t_{n})\,\hat{\pi}_{i_{n}}(\phi^{\prime}_{n},t_{n}),

(228)

while Eq.(
218
) yields

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}_{i_{n}}(\phi^{\prime}_{n},t_{n})\hat{\pi}_{i_{n}}^{\dagger}(\phi_{n},t_{n})=\delta_{\phi^{\prime}_{n},\phi_{n}}{|{\mathrm{ref}_{i_{n}}}\rangle_{{i_{n}}}}{{}_{{i_{n}}}\langle{\mathrm{ref}_{i_{n}}}|}.

(229)

Substituting Eq.(
228
) and Eq.(
229
) into Eq.(
227
) and using the cyclic relation
{\mathrm{tr}}[\hat{A}\hat{B}\hat{C}]\,{=}\,{\mathrm{tr}}[\hat{C}\hat{A}\hat{B}]
, for
n=N-1,\ldots,1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\hat{\Theta}_{n+1}=\sum_{{|{\phi_{n}}\rangle_{{i_{n}}}}}^{{\{{E>0}\}_{{i_{n}}}}}\hat{\pi}_{i_{n}}(\phi_{n},t_{n})\,\hat{\Theta}_{n}\,\hat{\pi}_{i_{n}}^{\dagger}(\phi_{n},t_{n}),

(230)

which further leads to

\displaystyle\hat{\Theta}_{N}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{|{\phi}\rangle_{{i_{N}}}}}^{{\{{E>0}\}_{{i_{N}}}}}\cdots\sum_{{|{\phi}\rangle_{{i_{1}}}}}^{{\{{E>0}\}_{{i_{1}}}}}\hat{\pi}_{i_{N}}(\phi_{N},t_{N})\cdots\hat{\pi}_{i_{1}}(\phi_{1},t_{1})\,\hat{\rho}\,

\displaystyle~~~~~~\hat{\pi}_{i_{1}}^{\dagger}(\phi_{1},t_{1})\cdots\hat{\pi}_{i_{N}}^{\dagger}(\phi_{N},t_{N}).

(231)

Substituting Eq.(
222
) into this equation and using Eq.(
219
) and Eq.(
223
), we find that the first term of Eq.(
222
) does not contribute to
{\mathcal{Z}}_{N}
.

Comparing Eq.(
231
) with Eq.(
218
), we find that
\hat{\Theta}_{N}
will vanish if different propositions refer to the same subsystem (i.e., if
i_{n}\,{=}\,i_{n^{\prime}}
for
n\,{\neq}\,n^{\prime}
).
Otherwise,
{\mathcal{Z}}_{N}
takes the form:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Z}}_{N}

\displaystyle\cong{\mathrm{tr}}\Bigg[\,\sum_{\xi^{\prime}_{N}}^{\{x,y,z\}}\sum_{\xi^{\prime\prime}_{N}}^{\{x,y,z\}}\cdots\sum_{\xi^{\prime}_{1}}^{\{x,y,z\}}\sum_{\xi^{\prime\prime}_{1}}^{\{x,y,z\}}

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~\int_{0}^{t_{N}}dt^{\prime}_{N}\int_{0}^{t_{N}}dt^{\prime\prime}_{N}\cdots\int_{0}^{t_{1}}dt^{\prime}_{1}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}dt^{\prime\prime}_{1}

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~\hat{\zeta}_{\xi^{\prime}_{N}\xi^{\prime\prime}_{N}}(t^{\prime}_{N}-t^{\prime\prime}_{N})\cdots\hat{\zeta}_{\xi^{\prime}_{1}\xi^{\prime\prime}_{1}}(t^{\prime}_{1}-t^{\prime\prime}_{1})

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~\left({\tilde{A}}_{{\mathrm{T}}\xi^{\prime\prime}_{1}}({\bm{r}}_{1},t^{\prime\prime}_{1})\cdots{\tilde{A}}_{{\mathrm{T}}\xi^{\prime\prime}_{N}}({\bm{r}}_{N},t^{\prime\prime}_{N})\right)

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~\left({\tilde{A}}_{{\mathrm{T}}\xi^{\prime}_{N}}({\bm{r}}_{N},t^{\prime}_{N})\cdots{\tilde{A}}_{{\mathrm{T}}\xi^{\prime}_{1}}({\bm{r}}_{1},t^{\prime}_{1})\right)\hat{\rho}\Bigg].

(232)

where we defined the operator coefficient
\hat{\zeta}_{\xi^{\prime}\xi^{\prime\prime}}(t^{\prime}-t^{\prime\prime})
by

\displaystyle\hat{\zeta}_{\xi^{\prime}\xi^{\prime\prime}}(t^{\prime}-t^{\prime\prime})\equiv\tilde{h}_{\xi^{\prime}}^{\dagger}(\phi,t,t^{\prime\prime})\tilde{h}_{\xi^{\prime\prime}}(\phi,t,t^{\prime}).

(233)

Assuming that incident field is quasi-monochromatic and that
t_{1},\ldots,t_{N}
are sufficiently larger than the optical period
2\pi/\omega_{\mathrm{o}}
, each double time integral can be evaluated as in Section
F
.
Consequently, we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Z}}_{N}

\displaystyle=t_{1}\cdots t_{N}\,{\mathrm{tr}}\Bigg[\,\sum_{\xi^{\prime}_{N}}^{\{x,y,z\}}\sum_{\xi^{\prime\prime}_{N}}^{\{x,y,z\}}\cdots\sum_{\xi^{\prime}_{1}}^{\{x,y,z\}}\sum_{\xi^{\prime\prime}_{1}}^{\{x,y,z\}}

\displaystyle~~~~\hat{\kappa}_{\xi^{\prime}_{N}\xi^{\prime\prime}_{N}}\cdots\hat{\kappa}_{\xi^{\prime}_{1}\xi^{\prime\prime}_{1}}\left({\tilde{A}}^{(-)}_{{\mathrm{T}}\xi^{\prime\prime}_{1}}({\bm{r}}_{1},t_{1})\cdots{\tilde{A}}^{(-)}_{{\mathrm{T}}\xi^{\prime\prime}_{N}}({\bm{r}}_{N},t_{N})\right)

\displaystyle~~~~\left({\tilde{A}}^{(+)}_{{\mathrm{T}}\xi^{\prime}_{N}}({\bm{r}}_{N},t_{N})\cdots{\tilde{A}}^{(+)}_{{\mathrm{T}}\xi^{\prime}_{1}}({\bm{r}}_{1},t_{1})\right)\hat{\rho}\,\Bigg],

(234)

where the operator coefficient is defined by

\displaystyle\hat{\kappa}_{\xi^{\prime}_{n}\xi^{\prime\prime}_{n}}(\omega_{\mathrm{o}})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{\zeta}_{\xi^{\prime}_{n}\xi^{\prime\prime}_{n}}(\tau)\exp\left(-{\mathrm{i}}\omega_{\mathrm{o}}\tau\right)d\tau,

(235)

and the explicit form of
\hat{\kappa}_{\xi^{\prime}_{n}\xi^{\prime\prime}_{n}}(\omega_{\mathrm{o}})
is shown in Eq.(

).
Thus, Eq.(

) is equivalent to Eq.(

) when calculating the trace of Eqs.(

)–(

Appendix I
Counting statistics

In this section, we derive the standard formulas for the counting statistics
[
73
]
.
This calculation demonstrates how Boolean logic translates a complex calculation procedure into a compact compound proposition.
Specifically, we determine the form of the proposition ‘
{\mathcal{N}},t
’ that represents ‘
{\mathcal{N}}
output signals are observed during time
t
’, evaluating its probability using Boolean logic.
This calculation is cumbersome for two reasons: it goes beyond the original scope of the first-order perturbation approximation, and repetitive measurements require multiple photoionization sites.
Below, we focus on a single photodetector case for simplicity.

To begin with, the previous discussions through Section
F
to Section
H
suggests that the first-order perturbation approximation is justified when photoionization occur only once in a given time interval.
Thus, we divide the time interval
t
into
M
small subintervals,

\displaystyle s_{m}\equiv m\,\Delta{t},~~\Delta{t}\equiv\frac{t}{M},~~m=1,\ldots,M,

(236)

and reduces the problem of finding the expression of ‘
{\mathcal{N}},t
’ to the problem of selecting
{\mathcal{N}}
subintervals from
M
possible candidates.
This hypothesis is mathematically expressed as

\displaystyle\mbox{`${\mathcal{N}},t$'}=\bigvee_{(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{{\mathcal{N}}})}^{{m_{1}}<{m_{2}}<\cdots<{m_{\mathcal{N}}}}\mbox{`$(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{{\mathcal{N}}})$'},

(237)

where the proposition on the right-hand side represents ‘output signals are observed only in the
m_{1}
-th,
m_{2}
-th,
\ldots
, and
m_{{\mathcal{N}}}
-th subintervals’, and the tuple
(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{{\mathcal{N}}})
denotes any combination of subinterval indices satisfying
{m_{1}}\,{<}\,{m_{2}}\,{<}\,\cdots\,{<}\,{m_{\mathcal{N}}}
.

From Boolean logic and the physics suggested by traditional stationary state models, the proposition ‘
(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{{\mathcal{N}}})
’ should have the following form:

\displaystyle\mbox{`$(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{{\mathcal{N}}})$'}\equiv\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\bigwedge_{m}^{(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{{\mathcal{N}}})}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\mbox{`$\mathrm{output},[s_{m},s_{m+1})$'}

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~\wedge\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\bigwedge_{m}^{{\notin}\,(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{{\mathcal{N}}})}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\lnot\,\mbox{`$\mathrm{output},[s_{m},s_{m+1})$'},

(238)

where the proposition ‘
\mathrm{output},[s_{m},s_{m+1})
’ represents whether an output signal is observed during the
m
-th subinterval.
Since photodetectors must have multiple photoionization sites to produce multiple detection signals, the proposition ‘
\mathrm{output},[s_{m},s_{m+1})
’ must be a disjunction over all photoionization sites:

‘
\mathrm{output},[s_{m},s_{m+1})
’

\displaystyle\equiv\bigvee_{l=1}^{L}\mbox{`$\mathrm{output}_{l},[s_{m},s_{m+1})$'}.

(239)

‘
\mathrm{output}_{l},[s_{m},s_{m+1})
’

\displaystyle\equiv\mbox{`$\mathrm{output}_{l},s_{m+1}$'}\wedge\lnot\mbox{`$\mathrm{output}_{l},s_{m}$'}.

(240)

Here, the proposition ‘
\mathrm{output}_{l},t
’ in Eq.(
240
) is the basic proposition derived in Eq.(
110
).
Note that stationary state occupations are exclusive only within each photoionization site, while Eq.(
238
) implies the exclusivity of ‘
(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{{\mathcal{N}}})
’:

\displaystyle\mbox{`$(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{{\mathcal{N}}})$'$\wedge$`$(m^{\prime}_{1},m^{\prime}_{2},\ldots,m^{\prime}_{{\mathcal{N}}})$'}=\mathrm{False}

\displaystyle~~\mbox{unless $(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{{\mathcal{N}}})=(m^{\prime}_{1},m^{\prime}_{2},\ldots,m^{\prime}_{{\mathcal{N}}})$}.

(241)

Our general assumptions restore the standard formulas for the counting statistics from the heuristic expression in Eq.(
237
).
Using the Born rule, Boolean logic, the definition of ‘
{\mathcal{N}},t
’ (Eq.(
237
)), and the exclusivity of ‘
(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{{\mathcal{N}}})
’ (Eq.(
241
)), we obtain

\displaystyle\left\langle{x}(\mbox{`${\mathcal{N}},t$'})\right\rangle

\displaystyle={\mathrm{tr}}\left[\,{\hat{z}}(\mbox{`${\mathcal{N}},t$'})\,\hat{\rho}\,{\hat{z}^{\dagger}}(\mbox{`${\mathcal{N}},t$'})\,\right]

(242)

\displaystyle=\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{{\mathcal{N}}})}^{m_{1}<m_{2}<\cdots<m_{{\mathcal{N}}}}\!\!\!\!\!\!{\mathrm{tr}}\Big[\,{\hat{z}}(\mbox{`$(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{{\mathcal{N}}})$'})

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~\,\hat{\rho}\,{\hat{z}^{\dagger}}(\mbox{`$(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{{\mathcal{N}}})$'})\,\Big].

(243)

Using de Morgan’s laws and Eqs.(
238
)–(
239
), the operator
{\hat{z}}(\mbox{`$(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{{\mathcal{N}}})$'})
can be expressed as

\displaystyle{\hat{z}}\left(\mbox{`$(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{{\mathcal{N}}})$'}\right)

\displaystyle=\hat{z}\left(\bigwedge_{m}^{(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{{\mathcal{N}}})}\Bigg(\bigvee_{l=1}^{L}\mbox{`$\mathrm{output}_{l},[s_{m},s_{m+1})$'}\Bigg)\right.

\displaystyle~~~~\wedge\!\!\!\!\bigwedge_{m}^{{\notin}\,(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{{\mathcal{N}}})}\!\!\!\!\lnot\left(\bigvee_{l=1}^{L}\mbox{`$\mathrm{output}_{l},[s_{m},s_{m+1})$'}\right)

(244)

\displaystyle=\hat{z}\left(\bigwedge_{m}^{(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{{\mathcal{N}}})}\lnot\Bigg(\bigwedge_{l=1}^{L}\lnot\mbox{`$\mathrm{output}_{l},[s_{m},s_{m+1})$'}\Bigg)\right.

\displaystyle~~~~\wedge\!\!\!\!\!\!\bigwedge_{m}^{{\notin}\,(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{{\mathcal{N}}})}\left(\bigwedge_{l=1}^{L}\lnot\mbox{`$\mathrm{output}_{l},[s_{m},s_{m+1})$'}\right).

(245)

Then Boolean logic yields

\displaystyle{\hat{z}}(\mbox{`$(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{{\mathcal{N}}})$'})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\prod_{m}^{(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{{\mathcal{N}}})}\!\!\left(\hat{1}-\prod_{l=1}^{L}(\hat{1}-{\hat{z}}(\mbox{`$\mathrm{output}_{l},[s_{m},s_{m+1})$'}))\right)

\displaystyle\prod_{m}^{\notin(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{{\mathcal{N}}})}\!\!\left(\prod_{l=1}^{L}(\hat{1}-{\hat{z}}(\mbox{`$\mathrm{output}_{l},[s_{m},s_{m+1})$'}))\right).

(246)

If the number of subintervals
M
is infinitely large and the number of photoionization sites
L
is large but finite, the terms involving

\displaystyle\mbox{`$\mathrm{output}_{l},[s_{m},s_{m+1})$'}\wedge\mbox{`$\mathrm{output}_{l^{\prime}},[s_{m},s_{m+1})$'},~~l\neq l^{\prime}

(247)

are negligibly small.
We thus obtain

\displaystyle{\hat{z}}(\mbox{`$(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{{\mathcal{N}}})$'})

\displaystyle\cong\mathcal{T}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\prod_{m}^{(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{{\mathcal{N}}})}\!\!\left(\sum_{l=1}^{L}{\hat{z}}(\mbox{`$\mathrm{output}_{l},[s_{m},s_{m+1})$'})\right)

\displaystyle\prod_{m}^{\notin(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{{\mathcal{N}}})}\left(\hat{1}-\sum_{l=1}^{L}{\hat{z}}(\mbox{`$\mathrm{output}_{l},[s_{m},s_{m+1})$'})\right).

(248)

By virtue of the time ordering symbol, we also obtain

\displaystyle{\hat{x}}(\mbox{`$(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{{\mathcal{N}}})$'})

\displaystyle\equiv{\hat{z}^{\dagger}}(\mbox{`$(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{{\mathcal{N}}})$'}){\hat{z}}(\mbox{`$(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{{\mathcal{N}}})$'})

(249)

\displaystyle\cong\mathcal{T}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\prod_{m}^{(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{{\mathcal{N}}})}\!\!\left(\sum_{l=1}^{L}{\hat{x}}(\mbox{`$\mathrm{output}_{l},[s_{m},s_{m+1})$'})\right)

\displaystyle\prod_{m}^{\notin(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{{\mathcal{N}}})}\left(\hat{1}-\sum_{l=1}^{L}{\hat{x}}(\mbox{`$\mathrm{output}_{l},[s_{m},s_{m+1})$'})\right),

(250)

where the operator on the right-hand side has the following form:

\displaystyle{\hat{x}}(\mbox{`$\mathrm{output}_{l},[s_{m},s_{m+1})$'})

\displaystyle\equiv{\hat{z}^{\dagger}}(\mbox{`$\mathrm{output}_{l},[s_{m},s_{m+1})$'})\,{\hat{z}}(\mbox{`$\mathrm{output}_{l},[s_{m},s_{m+1})$'})

(251)

\displaystyle\cong\eta_{\mathrm{amp}}\,{\hat{z}^{\dagger}}(\mbox{`${\{{E<0}\}_{{l}}},s_{m}$'})\,{\hat{z}^{\dagger}}(\mbox{`${\{{E>0}\}_{{l}}},s_{m+1}$'})\,

\displaystyle~~~~{\hat{z}}(\mbox{`${\{{E>0}\}_{{l}}},s_{m+1}$'})\,{\hat{z}}(\mbox{`${\{{E<0}\}_{{l}}},s_{m}$'})

(252)

\displaystyle=\eta_{\mathrm{amp}}\sum_{{|{\phi}\rangle_{{l}}}}^{{\{{E>0}\}_{{l}}}}\hat{\pi}_{l}^{\dagger}(\phi,t)\hat{\pi}_{l}(\phi,t).

(253)

If we define the photoionization rate at time
s_{m}
by

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{R}}(s_{m})

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\hat{\mathcal{N}}(s_{m+1})-\hat{\mathcal{N}}(s_{m})}{\Delta{t}}

(254)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Delta{t}}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\hat{x}(\mbox{`$\mathrm{output}_{l},[s_{m},s_{m+1})$'})

(255)

and set
\hat{\mathcal{N}}(0)\,{=}\,\hat{0}
, the first factor in Eq.(
250
) casts into the form:

\displaystyle\prod_{m}^{(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{{\mathcal{N}}})}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\hat{\mathcal{R}}(s_{m})\Delta{t}=\frac{t^{\mathcal{N}}}{M^{\mathcal{N}}}\prod_{m}^{(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{{\mathcal{N}}})}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\hat{\mathcal{R}}(s_{m}).

(256)

Since the detector count
{\mathcal{N}}
is negligibly small compared to the number of subintervals
M
, the second factor in Eq.(
250
) can be approximated as

\displaystyle\prod_{m}^{\notin(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{{\mathcal{N}}})}\left(\hat{1}-\sum_{l=1}^{L}{\hat{x}}\left(\mbox{`$\mathrm{output}_{l},[s_{m},s_{m+1})$'}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\cong\prod_{m=0}^{M-1}\left(\hat{1}-\hat{\mathcal{R}}(s_{m})\Delta{t}\right)\cong e^{-\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\hat{\mathcal{R}}(s_{m})\Delta{t}}.

(257)

As a result, Eq.(
250
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle{\hat{x}}\left(\mbox{`$(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{{\mathcal{N}}})$'}\right)\cong\frac{t^{\mathcal{N}}}{M^{\mathcal{N}}}\,{\mathcal{T}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\prod_{m}^{(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{{\mathcal{N}}})}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\hat{\mathcal{R}}(s_{m})\,e^{-\hat{\mathcal{N}}(t)}.

(258)

Substituting Eq.(
249
) and Eq.(
258
) into Eq.(
244
) and summing over different combinations, we obtain

\displaystyle\langle{x}(\mbox{`${\mathcal{N}},t$'})\rangle

\displaystyle={\mathrm{tr}}\left[\,{\hat{x}}(\mbox{`${\mathcal{N}},t$'})\,\hat{\rho}\,\right]

(259)

\displaystyle{\hat{x}}(\mbox{`${\mathcal{N}},t$'})

\displaystyle\cong\frac{{}_{M}{\mathrm{C}}_{{\mathcal{N}}}}{M^{{\mathcal{N}}}}\,{\mathcal{T}}\left(\prod_{m=1}^{\mathcal{N}}\hat{\mathcal{R}}(s_{m})\,t\right)e^{-\hat{\mathcal{N}}(t)}

(260)

\displaystyle\cong\frac{1}{{\mathcal{N}}!}\,{\mathcal{T}}\left(\prod_{m=1}^{\mathcal{N}}\hat{\mathcal{R}}(s_{m})\,t\right)e^{-\hat{\mathcal{N}}(t)},

(261)

where we assume that the photoionization rate does not change appreciably during
t
.
We also used Stirling’s formula to approximate the binomial coefficient
{}_{M}{\mathrm{C}}_{{\mathcal{N}}}\,{\equiv}\,{M!}/((M\,{-}\,{\mathcal{N}})!{\mathcal{N}}!)
.
The operators
\hat{\mathcal{R}}(s_{m})
mutually commute due to Eq.(
240
) and Eq.(
255
).
Consequently, Eq.(
261
) can be further approximated as

\displaystyle{\hat{x}}(\mbox{`${\mathcal{N}},t$'})

\displaystyle\cong\frac{1}{{\mathcal{N}}!}\hat{\mathcal{N}}(t)^{\mathcal{N}}e^{-\hat{\mathcal{N}}(t)}.

(262)

To express the operator
\hat{\mathcal{N}}(t)
in terms of field operators, we first express it using de-excitation and excitation operators as

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{N}}(t)

\displaystyle\cong\eta_{\mathrm{amp}}\sum_{l=1}^{L}{\hat{z}}(\mbox{`${{\{{E>0}\}_{{l}}}},t$'})

(263)

\displaystyle=\eta_{\mathrm{amp}}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\sum_{{|{\phi}\rangle_{{l}}}}^{{\{{E>0}\}_{{l}}}}\hat{\pi}^{\dagger}_{l}(\phi,t)\hat{\pi}_{l}(\phi,t),

(264)

where the de-excitation and excitation operators mutually commute for
l\,{\neq}\,l^{\prime}
, but not for
l\,{=}\,l^{\prime}
.
Accordingly, Eq.(
262
) do not simply cast into the standard form in which
\hat{\pi}_{l}(\phi,t)
and
\hat{\pi}^{\dagger}_{l}(\phi,t)
are arranged in the normal order (see Section
H
).
However, if we expand Eq.(
262
) as

\displaystyle{\hat{x}}(\mbox{`${\mathcal{N}},t$'})

\displaystyle\cong\sum_{\mu=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{\mu}}{{\mathcal{N}}!\mu!}\hat{\mathcal{N}}(t)^{\mu+{\mathcal{N}}},

(265)

the operator on the right-hand side takes the form:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{N}}(t)^{\mu+{\mathcal{N}}}\cong\eta_{\mathrm{amp}}^{\mu}\sum_{l_{1}=1}^{L}\cdots\sum_{l_{\mu+{\mathcal{N}}}=1}^{L}

\displaystyle~~{\hat{z}}(\mbox{`${{\{{E>0}\}_{{l_{1}}}}},t$'})\cdots{\hat{z}}(\mbox{`${{\{{E>0}\}_{{l_{\mu+{\mathcal{N}}}}}}},t$'}).

(266)

Then the terms that have the same
l
only makes the contribution of
O(L^{1})
within the sum over
L^{2}
elements, which is negligible when
L
is sufficiently large.
Consequently, the operator
\hat{\mathcal{N}}(t)
can be expressed in the normal order of de-excitation and excitation operators.
Applying Eq.(
225
), we obtain

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{N}}(t)

\displaystyle=t~\sum_{l=1}^{L}\sum_{\xi}^{\{x,y,z\}}\sum_{\xi^{\prime}}^{\{x,y,z\}}\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{\xi\xi^{\prime},l}\,{\tilde{A}^{(-)}_{{\mathrm{T}}\xi}}({\bm{r}}_{l},t)\,{\tilde{A}^{(+)}_{{\mathrm{T}}\xi^{\prime}}}({\bm{r}}_{l},t),

(267)

where the operator coefficient is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{\xi\xi^{\prime},l}(\omega_{\mathrm{o}})=\eta_{\mathrm{amp}}\sum_{{|{\phi_{+}}\rangle_{{l}}}}^{{\{{E>0}\}_{{l}}}}\sum_{{|{\phi_{-}}\rangle_{{l}}}}^{{\{{E<0}\}_{{l}}}}2\pi\,\delta\left(\omega_{\mathrm{o}}-\frac{E(\phi_{+})-E(\phi_{-})}{\hbar}\right)

\displaystyle\left(E(\phi_{+})-E(\phi_{-})\right)^{2}{{}_{{l}}\langle{\phi_{-}}|}\hat{D}_{\xi}{|{\phi_{+}}\rangle_{{l}}}{{}_{{l}}\langle{\phi_{+}}|}\hat{D}_{\xi^{\prime}}{|{\phi_{-}}\rangle_{{l}}}\,{|{\phi_{-}}\rangle_{{l}}}{{}_{{l}}\langle{\phi_{-}}|}.\phantom{\Big|}

(268)

If we further assume that (i) the density operator is separable as
\hat{\rho}\,{=}\,\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{A}}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{F}}
, (ii) the field amplitude does not vary significantly over the detector dimension and the measurement time
t
, and (iii) the detector response is isotropic (
\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{\xi\xi^{\prime},l}\,{=}\,\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{l}
), Eq.(
267
) yields

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{N}}(t)\cong\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{det}}}{\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{o}}}\hat{I}({\bm{r}}_{0},t)\,t,

(269)

where
{\bm{r}}_{0}
is the detector location, and
\hat{I}({\bm{r}}_{0},t)
is the intensity operator defined by

\displaystyle\hat{I}({\bm{r}}_{0},t)\equiv S\,\varepsilon_{0}c\,{\tilde{\bm{E}}}^{(-)}_{{\mathrm{T}}}({\bm{r}}_{0},t)\cdot{\tilde{\bm{E}}}^{(+)}_{{\mathrm{T}}}({\bm{r}}_{0},t).

(270)

\displaystyle{\tilde{\bm{E}}}^{(\pm)}_{{\mathrm{T}}}({\bm{r}}_{0},t)={\pm}\,{\mathrm{i}}\omega_{\mathrm{o}}{\tilde{\bm{A}}}^{(\pm)}_{{\mathrm{T}}}({\bm{r}}_{0},t)

(271)

The quantum efficiency of the photodetector is given by

\displaystyle{\eta}_{\mathrm{det}}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\hbar}{\varepsilon_{0}c\,\omega_{\mathrm{o}}}\,\mathrm{tr}\left[\underline{\hat{\mathcal{K}}}\,\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{A}}\right],

(272)

where
\underline{\hat{\mathcal{K}}}\,{\equiv}\,(1/{S})\sum_{l=1}^{L}\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{l}
represents an average response function per unit area.
The attenuation of optical fields in photosensitive materials is beyond the scope of this approximation; dispersive effects require higher-order perturbation approximations.
