ESG Risk: Lessons Learned from Utility Theory
Sebastian Geissel, Christoph Knochenhauer

Abstract




Abstract

We propose a new class of monetary risk measures capable of assessing financial and ESG risk.
The construction of these risk measures is based on an extension of classical shortfall risk measures in which the loss function is replaced by a multi-attribute utility function.
We present an extensive theoretical analysis of these risk measures, showing in particular how properties of the utility function translate into properties of the associated risk measure.
We furthermore discuss how these multi-attribute risk measures can be used to compute minimum risk portfolios and show in a numerical study that accounting for ESG risk in optimal portfolio choice has a significant influence on the composition of portfolios.

ESG-Finance
has grown into a vibrant area of research in the academic world.
One important observation is that explicitly accounting for ESG reveals a more nuanced picture on financial decision making.
Following
Hartzmark and Sussman 2019
, there is strong empirical evidence that sustainability aspects have an effect on investment decisions.
However, there are opposing views on whether such changes in decision making lead to better performing portfolios.
While there is a stream of literature including
Hong and Kacperczyk 2009
,
Bolton and Kacperczyk 2021
,
Cornell 2021
, and
Hsu et al. 2023
arguing that ESG has a detrimental effect on financial portfolios, arguments can similarly be made that ESG has a neutral effect on performance (
Naffa and Fain 2022
,
Pástor et al. 2022
) or even leads to outperformance of non-ESG aware portfolios (
Kotsantonis et al. 2016
,
Lins et al. 2017
).
An explanation of these diverging findings is that, as
Pedersen et al. 2021
put it, “… ESG is not fully priced in the market”.
Similarly, a key implication of the findings of
Avramov et al. 2022
is that purely financial considerations do not fully describe the risk profile of an investment.

This discussion allows for but one conclusion: in the assessment of risk, it is necessary to explicitly account for ESG.
This is the central theme of the present paper.
We propose an approach to the measurement of combined financial and ESG risk, similar in spirit to the axiomatic perspective on risk measurement taken by the landmark paper of
Artzner et al. 1999
.
This is achieved by extending the classical framework of
Föllmer and Schied 2002
for financial risk measurement, where it is shown that a large class of convex monetary measures of risk take the form

\hat{\rho}[X]\coloneqq\inf\bigl\{m\in\mathbb{R}:\mathbb{E}\bigl[\ell(-X-m)\bigr]\leq x_{0}\bigr\},

where
X
is the net financial value of the position to be evaluated,
\ell:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
an increasing and convex loss function, and
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
a fixed reference level.
Our observation is that any such risk measure can equivalently be expressed as

\hat{\rho}[X]\coloneqq\inf\bigl\{m\in\mathbb{R}:\mathbb{E}\bigl[\hat{u}(X+m)\bigr]\geq 0\bigr\},

where
\hat{u}(x)\coloneqq-\ell(-x)+x_{0}
is a concave increasing utility function.
This allows us to interpret
\hat{\rho}[X]
as the minimum amount of cash
m\in\mathbb{R}
such that
X+m
has positive expected utility.
In light of this interpretation, we propose a generalization in which investors consider an additional non-financial source of risk
S
—accounting e.g. for ESG factors.
This is achieved by replacing
x\mapsto\hat{u}(x)
with a multi-attribute utility function
(x,s)\mapsto u(x,s)
, leading to what we refer to as an ESG risk measure of the form

\rho[X,S]\coloneqq\inf\bigl\{m\in\mathbb{R}:\mathbb{E}\bigl[u(X+m,S)\bigr]\geq 0\bigr\}.

The advantage of this definition is that we can adhere to the general axiomatic foundations of utility theory.
In doing so, we can translate basic assumptions on (the interplay of) financial and ESG risk to structural properties of
u
and, consequently, the risk measure
\rho[X,S]
.

This is in contrast to the related literature, where typically ad hoc definitions of ESG risk measures are adopted.

Capelli et al. 2021
use the dispersion of ESG scores to predict the volatility of financial assets and
Capelli et al. 2023
extend value at risk by including ESG information.

Torri et al. 2023
propose to apply classical coherent risk measures to convex combinations of the financial returns and ESG scores.

Escobar-Anel 2022
introduces a multiple risk aversions utility framework to attach different risk aversion levels to different financial assets and applies this model to ESG investments.
Moreover, we refer to
Gallucci et al. 2022
for a systematic review of the literature on the conceptualization of ESG risks in portfolio studies.
Finally, from a utility perspective, the works of
Bollen 2007
,
Jessen 2012
, and
Dorfleitner and Nguyen 2017
are closely related to our work in that they propose a multi-attribute utility function of the form
u(x,s)=u_{1}(x)+u_{2}(s)
applied in optimal investment problems.
In terms of developing a general framework for risk measurement, however, we point out that this additive form of the utility function implies that the joint distribution of
X
and
S
is ignored.
This means that, without loss of generality, the financial and ESG component of the position are assumed to be independent.
In our setting, we only impose the weaker assumption of mutual utility independence, which is equivalent to assuming
u(x,s)=u_{1}(x)+u_{2}(s)+ku_{1}(x)u_{2}(s)
for some
k\in\mathbb{R}
.
Crucially, the dependence structure of
X
and
S
is taken into account when measuring risk.

The main contributions of our paper can be summarized as follows:

1.

We develop a new paradigmatic approach embedded into a rigorous mathematical framework to assess risk based on multiple factors.
We apply this framework to construct a class of risk measures combining financial and ESG risk.

2.

Financial and ESG exposure are modeled as separate—possibly dependent—random variables, which are combined using a multi-attribute utility function.
This allows for a clear separation of concerns in that the risk-return profile and the investor’s risk preferences are modeled separately.

3.

We contribute to understanding ESG risk in international stock markets by analyzing ESG ratings and stock returns from all S&P 500 companies over an extended time period.
We find that our risk measures are capable of painting a fine-grained picture of the inherent risk profile of assets with similar financial performance but differing ESG exposures.
These findings are substantiated by empirical results showing that minimum risk portfolios using ESG risk measures lead to portfolios with a significantly better ESG score without sacrificing returns.

The rigorous construction of the utility-based risk measures is carried out in Section
2
.
This section also gives a detailed account of basic properties such as translation invariance, monotonicity, and convexity, and presents multiple examples of ESG risk measures.
We furthermore discuss ESG risk premia and characterize favorable and unfavorable ESG exposures.
In Section
3
we apply our results to a dataset comprising all S&P 500 companies to investigate how ESG risk measures perform in practice, both in terms of single-asset risk measurement as well as in the construction of minimum risk portfolios of a basket of assets.

2 Risk Measures for ESG Assets

We propose a class of risk measures capable of taking into account both
financial and ESG risk associated with an investment position. The financial
exposure of the position is identified with a random variable
X:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}

defined on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})
. As is common practice, we
interpret
X(\omega)
as the net value (in monetary units) of the position in
the scenario
\omega\in\Omega
. In the same spirit, the ESG exposure is measured in
terms of a random variable
S:\Omega\to\mathbb{S}
where
\mathbb{S}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
.
Here, we think of
S(\omega)
as the future ESG rating of the position in
the scenario
\omega\in\Omega
, so that
\mathbb{S}
corresponds to the set of
ratings.
Most commonly,
\mathbb{S}
will either be a finite set such as
\mathbb{S}=\{1,\dots,N\}
corresponding to different rating classes or a bounded interval of the form
[0,1]
or
[0,100]
if the ESG rating is score-based.

2.1
Utility-Based Shortfall Risk Measures

To motivate our definition of ESG risk measures, let us recall the notion of
shortfall risk measures introduced by
Föllmer and Schied 2002
. Given an
increasing and convex loss function
\ell:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and a fixed number

x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, the shortfall risk of a financial position
X
is defined as

\hat{\rho}[X]\coloneqq\inf\bigl\{m\in\mathbb{R}:\mathbb{E}\bigl[\ell(-X-m)\bigr]\leq x_{0}\bigr\}.

In this setting, the risk associated with a financial position
X
is considered
acceptable provided that
\mathbb{E}[\ell(-X)]\leq x_{0}
. If
X
is not acceptable, a
fixed amount
m\in\mathbb{R}
of cash can be added to the position such that
X+m
is
acceptable, that is,
\mathbb{E}[\ell(-X-m)]\leq x_{0}
. To wit, the shortfall risk measure
corresponds to the minimal amount of cash that needs to be added to

X
to make it acceptable.
Now, if we let
\hat{u}\coloneqq-\ell(-\,\cdot\,)+x_{0}
, the shortfall risk
measure can be written as

\hat{\rho}[X]\coloneqq\inf\bigl\{m\in\mathbb{R}:\mathbb{E}\bigl[\hat{u}(X+m)\bigr]\geq 0\bigr\}.

Observe that
\hat{u}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is concave and increasing, that is,

\hat{u}
is a utility function. We may thus interpret acceptable
positions as those having non-negative expected utility. In light of this, a
natural generalization of the classical shortfall risk measure
\hat{\rho}
capable
of accounting for multiple sources of risk is obtained by replacing the
single-attribute utility function
\hat{u}
by a multi-attribute utility

u
. This leads to the following definition of shortfall risk measures for
ESG assets.

2.1
.

For a measurable function
u:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
, we refer to
4
4

4

Here,
u_{+}\coloneqq\max\{u,0\}
denotes the positive part of

u
. The integrability assumption thus guarantees that
\mathbb{E}[u(X+m,S)]

exists, but may possibly be equal to
-\infty
.

\mathcal{Q}\coloneqq\Bigl\{(X,S):\Omega\to\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}\text{ measurable}\;\Big|\;\mathbb{E}\bigl[u_{+}(X+m,S)\bigr]<\infty\text{ for all }m\in\mathbb{R}\Bigr\}

as the associated set of
quantifiable positions
. With this, we define the

shortfall risk measure for ESG assets
or simply
ESG risk measure

as

\rho[X,S]\coloneqq\inf\bigl\{m\in\mathbb{R}:\mathbb{E}\bigl[u(X+m,S)\bigr]\geq 0\bigr\},\qquad(X,S)\in\mathcal{Q}.

The interpretation of
\rho[X,S]
is the same as in the single-attribute
case. Any position
(X,S)
with non-negative expected utility
\mathbb{E}[u(X,S)]\geq 0

is considered acceptable. Unacceptable positions can be made acceptable by
adding cash to the financial component
X
, and

\rho[X,S]
is the minimal cash amount required to make
(X,S)
acceptable.

Just as for classical financial shortfall risk measures, it is not difficult to see that
\rho[X,S]
is translation invariant in
X
, which allows us to interpret
X\mapsto\rho[X,S]
as a monetary measure of risk.
This statement is formalized in the following theorem.

Theorem 2.1

(Translation Invariance)
.

Any ESG risk measure
\rho
is translation invariant, that is

\rho[X+\eta,S]=\rho[X,S]-\eta\qquad\text{for all }\eta\in\mathbb{R}

for any position
(X,S)\in\mathcal{Q}
. In particular,
\rho
satisfies

\rho\bigl[X+\rho[X,S],S\bigr]=0

whenever
\rho[X,S]\in\mathbb{R}
.
\boldsymbol{\diamond}

Proof.

Translation invariance follows directly from

\displaystyle\rho[X+\eta,S]

\displaystyle=\inf\bigl\{m\in\mathbb{R}:\mathbb{E}\bigl[u(X+\eta+m,S)\bigr]\geq 0\bigr\}

\displaystyle=\inf\bigl\{\tilde{m}\in\mathbb{R}:\mathbb{E}\bigl[u(X+\tilde{m},S)\bigr]\geq 0\bigr\}-\eta=\rho[X,S]-\eta

for any
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
and
(X,S)\in\mathcal{Q}
. If
\rho[X,S]\in\mathbb{R}
, this implies

\rho\bigl[X+\rho[X,S],S\bigr]=\rho[X,S]-\rho[X,S]=0,

which concludes the proof.
∎

In light of Theorem
2.1
we can indeed interpret

\rho[X,S]
as the minimal amount of cash required to make
(X,S)

acceptable. In particular,
X\mapsto\rho[X,S]
is a monetary measure of
risk.

Observe that, in the definition of
\rho
, the utility function
u
can
take the value
-\infty
. This allows, for example, to consider risk measures
which render all positions
(X,S)
in which the ESG rating drops below a
threshold
\bar{s}\in\mathbb{S}
unacceptable. Indeed, this situation arises as
soon as
u
satisfies

u(x,s)=-\infty\qquad\text{for all }(x,s)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}\text{ with }s<\bar{s}.

Building on this observation leads to the following instructive example of an
ESG risk measure.

2.1
Penalty and Threshold ESG Risk Measures.

Suppose that
u_{1}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
is measurable and let

\hat{\rho}
be the associated classical financial shortfall risk measure given by

\hat{\rho}[X]\coloneqq\inf\bigl\{m\in\mathbb{R}:\mathbb{E}\bigl[u_{1}(X+m)\bigr]\geq 0\bigr\}.

Let moreover
\bar{s}\in\mathbb{S}
and
\eta\in[0,+\infty]
, and define

u_{2}:\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
by

u_{2}(s)\coloneqq\begin{cases}0&\text{if }s\geq\bar{s}\\
-\eta&\text{if }s<\bar{s}\end{cases}\qquad\text{for all }s\in\mathbb{S}.

Finally, suppose that the multi-attribute utility function
u
takes the
additive form

u:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\},\qquad(x,s)\mapsto u(x,s)\coloneqq u_{1}(x)+u_{2}(s).

If we denote by
\rho
the ESG risk measure constructed
from
u
, it follows that

\rho[X,S]=\hat{\rho}[X]+\eta\mathbb{P}[S<\bar{s}]\qquad\text{for all }(X,S)\in\mathcal{Q}.

In other words, the ESG risk measure
\rho
coincides with the classical
financial risk measure
\hat{\rho}
for all positions with an ESG exposure above
the threshold
\bar{s}
, but adds a penalty of
\eta\mathbb{P}[S<\bar{s}]
to all positions which have a chance of dropping
below
\bar{s}
.
Moreover, choosing
\eta=+\infty
renders these positions entirely unacceptable.
In light of this, we refer to
\rho
as a

penalty ESG risk measure
if
\eta<+\infty
and call
\rho
a
threshold ESG risk measure
if
\eta=+\infty
.
\boldsymbol{\diamond}

An advantage of the utility-based view on shortfall risk measures is that
we can adhere to the general axiomatic theory for multi-attribute utility
functions to translate assumptions on the interplay of financial and ESG risk
to structural properties of the utility function
u
. In particular, in
all that follows, we impose the following assumption.

Standing Assumption
.

The attributes “financial risk” and “ESG risk” are mutually utility
independent.
\boldsymbol{\diamond}

For the sake of clarity, let us recall the notion of utility independence.
Suppose that
X_{1},X_{2}
are two financial positions. Then the attribute
“financial risk” is said to be utility independent of the attribute “ESG
risk” if the preference for
X_{2}
over
X_{1}
does not depend on any fixed level
of ESG exposure, that is

\mathbb{E}\bigl[u(X_{1},\bar{s})\bigr]\leq\mathbb{E}\bigl[u(X_{2},\bar{s})\bigr]\qquad\text{for some $\bar{s}\in\mathbb{S}$ with $(X_{1},\bar{s}),(X_{2},\bar{s})\in\mathcal{Q}$}

if and only if

\mathbb{E}\bigl[u(X_{1},s)\bigr]\leq\mathbb{E}\bigl[u(X_{2},s)\bigr]\qquad\text{for all $s\in\mathbb{S}$ with $(X_{1},s),(X_{2},s)\in\mathcal{Q}$.}

Our standing assumption of mutual utility independence thus states that
financial risk is utility independent of ESG risk and vice versa. A classical
result in the theory of multi-attribute utility theory, see e.g.
Keeney and Raiffa 1993
, states that the utility function
u
is mutually
utility independent if and only if there exists a constant
k\in\mathbb{R}
and two
single-attribute utility functions

u_{1}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty,+\infty\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad u_{2}:\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty,+\infty\}

such that
5
5

5

Here, we impose the convention
\infty-\infty=-\infty
.

u(x,s)=u_{1}(x)+u_{2}(s)+ku_{1}(x)u_{2}(s),\qquad(x,s)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}.

In light of this, we subsequently always assume that
u
takes this form for
suitable
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
.

2.1
.

Note that for
k\neq 0
the representation of
u
is equivalent to

1+ku(x,s)=\bigl(1+ku_{1}(x)\bigr)\bigl(1+ku_{2}(s)\bigr),\qquad(x,s)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S},

which is to say that there exists a linear transformation
y\mapsto 1+ky

of
u
which is multiplicative in the linear transformations of
u_{1}

and
u_{2}
. If on the other hand
k=0
, the representation of
u
in
terms of
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
is additive, that is

u(x,s)=u_{1}(x)+u_{2}(s),\qquad(x,s)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}.

This is the class of multi-attribute utility functions that the related literature restricts to, see

Bollen 2007
,
Jessen 2012
,
Dorfleitner and Nguyen 2017
. However, following e.g.
Keeney and Raiffa 1993
, it is known
that this additive representation is equivalent to assuming that the attributes financial
risk and ESG risk are
additive independent
, which is to say that
preferences only depend on the marginal distributions of
X
and
S
,
but not on the joint distribution of
(X,S)
. In other words, as the joint
distribution is not taken into account, the risk associated with
(X,S)
is computed as if
X
and
S
are independent.
While it is arguably fruitful to allow for
additive utility functions, it is our opinion that restricting entirely to such utilities is too strong of an assumption to impose.
\boldsymbol{\diamond}

2.2
Monotonicity of ESG Risk Measures

One of the basic properties a reasonable risk measure should satisfy is monotonicity: increasing net values of the financial position or improving ESG ratings should decrease the overall risk.
Our utility-based point of view makes it straightforward to derive assumptions on
u
which guarantee such monotonicity properties.

Theorem 2.2

(Monotonicity)
.

Suppose that
u
is non-decreasing in both its arguments and assume that

(X,S),(X^{\prime},S^{\prime})\in\mathcal{Q}
are such that
X\leq X^{\prime}
and
S\leq S^{\prime}
. Then

\rho[X,S]\geq\rho[X^{\prime},S^{\prime}].

Proof.

Monotonicity of
u
implies

\mathbb{E}\bigl[u(X+m,S)\bigr]\geq\mathbb{E}\bigl[u(X^{\prime}+m,S^{\prime})\bigr]\qquad\text{for all }m\in\mathbb{R},

from which the conclusion follows.
∎

It is natural to construct the multi-attribute utility function
u
from
the single-attribute utilities
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
. Insisting that
u
be monotone hence imposes restrictions on
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
. In fact,
since
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
are utility functions in their own right and
model preferences towards financial and ESG exposures, respectively, it makes
sense to assume them to be monotone as well.
However, in general, monotonicity of the single-attribute utilities
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
does
imply monotonicity of the multi-attribute utility
u
and vice versa. The following result clarifies this relationship.

Proposition 2.1

(Monotonicity of
u
)
.

Suppose that
u_{1}
is non-decreasing.
Then, for any
s\in\mathbb{S}
,

x\mapsto u(x,s)\text{ is non-decreasing on $\mathbb{R}$}\qquad\text{if and only if}\qquad 1+ku_{2}(s)\in\{-\infty\}\cup[0,\infty].

Analogously, if
u_{2}
is non-decreasing and
x\in\mathbb{R}
is fixed, the mapping

s\mapsto u(x,s)\text{ is non-decreasing on $\mathbb{S}$}\qquad\text{if and only if}\qquad 1+ku_{1}(x)\in\{-\infty\}\cup[0,\infty].

Proof.

Observe that
u
can be expressed as

u(x,s)=\bigl(1+ku_{2}(s)\bigr)u_{1}(x)+u_{2}(s)\qquad\text{and}\qquad u(x,s)=\bigl(1+ku_{1}(x)\bigr)u_{2}(s)+u_{1}(x)

for all
(x,s)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}
. From this, we immediately obtain the
result.
∎

2.2
.

The previous proposition shows that monotonicity of the utility functions

u,u_{1}
, and
u_{2}
imposes restrictions on the range of the mappings

x\mapsto 1+ku_{1}(x)\qquad\text{and}\qquad s\mapsto 1+ku_{2}(s).

If
k>0
, these restrictions are equivalent to

u_{1}\text{ and }u_{2}\text{ take values in }\{-\infty\}\cup[-1/k,\infty).

Imposing a lower bound of
-1/k
on
u_{2}
is not problematic if the set
\mathbb{S}
of ESG ratings is bounded—which typically is the case.
As such, it is straightforward to construct ESG risk measures which are guaranteed to be monotone in the financial position.
Imposing a lower bound on
u_{1}
, on the other hand, is certainly a much stronger assumption to impose.
Such a cut-off has the undesirable effect that the risk associated with tail events in the financial position is capped.
In that case, it may hence be more appropriate to instead forfeit monotonicity of
s\mapsto u(x,s)
for small
x
.

Similar observations can be made if
k<0
. In particular, monotonicity of
u
implies that

u_{1}\text{ and }u_{2}\text{ take values in }(-\infty,-1/k]\cup\{+\infty\}.

Finally,
for
k=0
there are no restrictions on the ranges of
u_{1}
and

u_{2}
as
1+ku_{1}=1+ku_{2}=1
. Hence, in this case,
monotonicity of
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
implies monotonicity of
u
without
imposing any additional assumptions. This is in fact obvious as
k=0
just means

u(x,s)=u_{1}(x)+u_{2}(s),\qquad(x,s)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}.

In view of the discussion above, we now present a procedure to construct
monotone multi-attribute utility functions from monotone single-attribute
utility functions.

2.2
Canonical Construction.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{R}
and two non-decreasing single-attribute utility functions

\hat{u}_{1}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
and

\hat{u}_{2}:\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
. Define

\mathrm{dom}(u)\coloneqq\bigl\{(x,s)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}:1+k\hat{u}_{1}(x)\in[0,\infty)\text{ and }1+k\hat{u}_{2}(s)\in[0,\infty)\bigr\}

and let
u:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
be

u(x,s)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\hat{u}_{1}(x)+\hat{u}_{2}(s)+k\hat{u}_{1}(x)\hat{u}_{2}(s)&\text{if }(x,s)\in\mathrm{dom}(u)\\
-\infty&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

We subsequently refer to this construction of monotone multi-attribute utilities
as the
canonical construction
. The set
\mathrm{dom}(u)
is referred to
as the
effective domain
of
u
and is precisely the set on which

u
is finite.
\boldsymbol{\diamond}

2.3
Entropic and Capped Entropic ESG Risk Measures.

As an example of the canonical construction in action, let us consider a
generalization of the entropic risk measure. For this, let us assume that

\hat{u}_{1}
and
\hat{u}_{2}
are exponential utility functions with
parameters
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}>0
, that is

\hat{u}_{1}(x)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\gamma_{1}}\Bigl(1-e^{-\gamma_{1}x}\Bigr)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\hat{u}_{2}(s)\coloneqq c\frac{1}{\gamma_{2}}\Bigl(1-e^{-\gamma_{2}(s-s_{0}))}\Bigr),\qquad(x,s)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S},

where
c>0
is a scaling factor and
s_{0}\in\mathbb{S}
is referred to as the baseline.
Observe that
\hat{u}_{2}(s)\geq 0
if and only if
s\geq s_{0}
and
\hat{u}_{2}(s)\leq 0
if and only if
s\leq s_{0}
, so the role of the baseline
s_{0}
is to normalize the utility function
\hat{u}_{2}
.
The role of the scaling factor
c
is discussed in Example
2.4
below.

Following
Föllmer and Schied 2002
, the classical financial risk measure

\hat{\rho}
constructed from
\hat{u}_{1}
is the entropic risk measure

\hat{\rho}[X]=\frac{1}{\gamma_{1}}\log\Bigl(\mathbb{E}\bigl[e^{-\gamma_{1}X}\bigr]\Bigr).

(1)

At this point, we have two alternatives to construct the multi-attribute utility function
u
.
The first approach is to forfeit global monotonicity of
u
by setting

u(x,s)\coloneqq\hat{u}_{1}(x)+\hat{u}_{2}(s)+k\hat{u}_{1}(x)\hat{u}_{2}(s)\qquad\text{for all }(x,s)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}.

(2)

The risk measure associated with this choice of
u
is subsequently referred to as the
entropic ESG risk measure
.
A quick calculation shows that, for
k>0
,

1+k\hat{u}_{2}(s)\geq 0\qquad\text{if and only if}\qquad s\geq\underline{s}\coloneqq-\frac{1}{\gamma_{2}}\log\Bigl(1+\frac{\gamma_{2}}{ck}\Bigr)+s_{0},

so that
u(x,s)
is monotone in
x
whenever
s\geq\underline{s}
.
Similarly, we have

1+k\hat{u}_{1}(x)\geq 0\qquad\text{if and only if}\qquad x\geq\underline{x}\coloneqq-\frac{1}{\gamma_{1}}\log\Bigl(1+\frac{\gamma_{1}}{k}\Bigr),

so that
u(x,s)
is monotone in
s
whenever
x\geq\underline{x}
.

Alternatively, we can use the canonical construction to obtain a globally monotone utility function
u_{cap}
which is finite on the effective domain

\mathrm{dom}(u_{cap})=\Bigl([\underline{x},\infty)\times[\underline{s},\infty)\Bigr)\cap\bigl(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}\bigr)

and equal to
-\infty
outside this set. We refer to the risk measure corresponding to
u
as the
capped entropic ESG risk measure
.
It may appear at first sight that, with this, the canonical construction makes many positions
(X,S)
unacceptable as it may assign a risk of
+\infty
.
Let us highlight, however, that in the literature on risk measures it is common practice to assume financial positions
X
to be bounded.
In this situation, lower boundedness of
X
entails the existence of
m_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

m+X\geq\underline{x}\qquad\text{for all }m\geq m_{0}.

This implies that the only way the capped entropic ESG risk measure evaluates to
+\infty
is when the
event
\{S<-\underline{s}\}
has positive probability, i.e. if the ESG rating makes the
position unacceptable. This argument shows that the
conditions
u_{1},u_{2}\geq-1/k
are in fact not as restrictive as they might appear.
\boldsymbol{\diamond}

2.3
ESG Risk Premia and Indifference Positions

To get a better understanding of the effect of the ESG component
S
on the
evaluation of risk, we subsequently study the relationship between an ESG risk
measure
\rho
constructed from the multi-attribute utility function
u=u_{1}+u_{2}+ku_{1}u_{2}

and the corresponding purely financial shortfall risk measure
\hat{\rho}

constructed from
u_{1}
, that is

\hat{\rho}[X]\coloneqq\inf\bigl\{m\in\mathbb{R}:\mathbb{E}\bigl[u_{1}(X+m)\bigr]\geq 0\bigr\}.

We may then think of

\mathfrak{p}[X,S]\coloneqq\rho[X,S]-\hat{\rho}[X],\qquad(X,S)\in\mathcal{Q},

as the risk premium arising from the consideration of ESG criteria and, as such,
we refer to
\mathfrak{p}[X,S]
as the
ESG risk premium
. To develop a deeper understanding of the properties of

\mathfrak{p}[X,S]
, a crucial role is played by the set of ESG exposures

\mathcal{I}\coloneqq\Bigl\{S:\Omega\to\mathbb{S}\;\Big|\;\mathbb{E}[u_{2}(S)]\text{ exists in }\mathbb{R}\text{ and }\mathbb{E}[u(X,S)]=\mathbb{E}[u_{1}(X)]\\
\text{ for all }X:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}\text{ with }(X,S)\in\mathcal{Q}\Bigr\},

which we refer to as the
indifference ESG positions
. By definition, the
ESG exposures
S\in\mathcal{I}
are the positions for which

\rho[X,S]=\hat{\rho}[X],\qquad\text{meaning that}\qquad\mathfrak{p}[X,S]=0.

That is,
\mathcal{I}
consists of the ESG ratings for which the risk
measure does not distinguish between ESG risk and pure financial risk. The
condition that
\mathbb{E}[u_{2}(S)]
exists in
\mathbb{R}
is merely a technical assumption
to guarantee that
(x,S)\in\mathcal{Q}
for all constant
x\in\mathbb{R}
, meaning that
it is actually possible to quantify the ESG risk.

Proposition 2.2

(Characterization of Indifference ESG Positions)
.

Suppose there exists
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
1+ku_{1}(\bar{x})\in(0,\infty)
.
Then

\mathcal{I}=\bigl\{S:\Omega\to\mathbb{S}\;\big|\;S\text{ is measurable
and }\mathbb{E}[u_{2}(S)]=0\bigr\}.

Moreover, if
\mathbb{S}
is an interval and
u_{2}
is continuous and non-decreasing on
\{u_{2}\neq-\infty\}
, the set

\mathcal{I}
is non-empty if and only if there exists
s_{0}\in\mathbb{S}

satisfying
u_{2}(s_{0})=0
.
\boldsymbol{\diamond}

Proof.

Let
(X,S)\in\mathcal{Q}
such that
\mathbb{E}[u_{2}(S)]
exists and

\mathbb{E}[u_{1}(X)]=\mathbb{E}[u(X,S)]=\mathbb{E}[u_{1}(X)+u_{2}(S)+ku_{1}(X)u_{2}(S)].

Clearly, this identity is equivalent to

\mathbb{E}\bigl[u_{2}(S)\bigl(1+ku_{1}(X)\bigr)\bigr]=0.

Now this is only possible if
|1+ku_{1}(X)|=\infty
has zero
probability, hence
1+ku_{1}(X)\in[0,\infty)
.
For
S\in\mathcal{I}
, the above identity must in particular hold for
all constant
X=\bar{x}
with
1+ku_{1}(\bar{x})>0
, from which we conclude that

S\in\mathcal{I}\qquad\text{if and only if}\qquad\mathbb{E}[u_{2}(S)]=0.

Finally, if
\mathbb{S}
is an interval and
u_{2}
is continuous and non-decreasing, the condition
\mathbb{E}[u_{2}(S)]=0
is
satisfied if and only if there exists
s_{0}\in\mathbb{S}
with
u_{2}(s_{0})=0
.
∎

Recalling that
\rho
is non-increasing if
u
is non-decreasing, it follows that the set of
indifference ESG positions separates the positions with positive and negative
ESG risk premia. Indeed, let
(X,S),(X^{\prime},S^{\prime})\in\mathcal{Q}
such that

X\leq X^{\prime}
and
S\leq S^{\prime}
. Then
Theorem
2.2
shows that

\rho[X^{\prime},S^{\prime}]\leq\rho[X,S].

In particular, choosing
X=X^{\prime}
and
S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}
reveals

S\geq S^{\prime}\text{ for some }S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}\qquad\text{implies}\qquad\rho[X,S]\leq\rho[X,S^{\prime}]=\hat{\rho}[X].

In other words, the indifference ESG positions
S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}
constitute a
threshold. Favorable ESG exposures
S
satisfying
S\geq S^{\prime}
decrease the
risk, whereas unfavorable ones with
S\leq S^{\prime}
increase the risk. This is
particularly true if
S^{\prime}=s_{0}
with
s_{0}\in\mathbb{S}
such that
u_{2}(s_{0})=0
.
In that sense, the value
s_{0}
separates favorable from unfavorable ESG
exposures.

2.4
Calibration of ESG Risk Measures.

Indifference ESG positions play a crucial role in the calibration of ESG risk measures.
As an example, let us recall the (uncapped) entropic ESG risk measure discussed in Example
2.3
, where
u(x,s)=u_{1}(x)+u_{2}(s)+ku_{1}(x)u_{2}(s)
for
k\in\mathbb{R}
and

u_{1}(x)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\gamma_{1}}\Bigl(1-e^{-\gamma_{1}x}\Bigr)\qquad\text{and}\qquad u_{2}(s)\coloneqq c\frac{1}{\gamma_{2}}\Bigl(1-e^{-\gamma_{2}(s-s_{0}))}\Bigr),\qquad(x,s)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S},

for parameters
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},c>0
and
s_{0}\in\mathbb{S}
.
The starting point of the calibration is the observation that
u_{1}
determines the financial entropic risk measure

\hat{\rho}[X]=\frac{1}{\gamma_{1}}\log\Bigl(\mathbb{E}\bigl[e^{-\gamma_{1}X}\bigr]\Bigr).

As this is a classical risk measure, we assume that
\gamma_{1}
has already been chosen and only discuss a procedure to calibrate the parameters
\gamma_{2},c,s_{0}
determining
u_{2}
as well as the parameter
k\in\mathbb{R}
determining the interaction of
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
in
u
.
The baseline level
s_{0}
and the risk aversion parameter
\gamma_{2}
can be calibrated by using indifference positions.
Indeed,
s_{0}
is chosen as the unique deterministic ESG rating for which
u_{2}(s_{0})=0
, that is, it is the threshold separating favorable and unfavorable ESG positions.
Now pick another (random) indifference position
S\in\mathcal{I}
with two outcomes. Since
S
is an indifference position, we must necessarily have that both
S<s_{0}
and
S>s_{0}
have positive probability and

0=\mathbb{E}[u_{2}(S)]=\frac{c}{\gamma_{2}}\Bigl(1-\mathbb{E}\Bigl[e^{-\gamma_{2}(S-s_{0})}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{S<s_{0}\}}\Bigr]-\mathbb{E}\Bigl[e^{-\gamma_{2}(S-s_{0})}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{S>s_{0}\}}\Bigr]\Bigr).

Since both
S<s_{0}
and
S>s_{0}
have positive probability, this equation can be solved uniquely for the unknown risk aversion parameter
\gamma_{2}
.
Finally, the parameters
c>0
and
k\in\mathbb{R}
can be used to scale
u_{2}
to fit the magnitude of
u_{1}
.
To see this, we first express
u
as

u(x,s)=\bigl(1+ku_{2}(s)\bigr)u_{1}(x)+u_{2}(s),

(3)

from which we see that it is possible to interpret
u_{2}(s)
as a means to adjust a given financial utility
u_{1}(x)
to account for ESG preferences.
This adjustment occurs in two ways.
On the one hand, there is the additive term
u_{2}(s)
appearing at the very end of (
3
) which acts as an absolute adjustment, shifting the financial utility either downwards by at most
u_{2}(s_{\min})\leq u_{2}(s_{0})=0
or upwards by at most
u_{2}(s_{\max})\geq u_{2}(s_{0})=0
.
The scaling factor
c>0
can hence be used to fix the maximum downward and upward shift of the financial utility due to the presence of ESG risk.
On the other hand, there is the scaling factor
ku_{2}(s)
in front of
u_{1}(x)
which acts as another relative adjustment of the financial utility
u_{1}(x)
.
Depending on the sign of
k
, the minimum and maximum scaling is given by either
ku_{2}(s_{\min})
or
ku_{2}(s_{\max})
.
Thus, the last missing parameter
k
should be chosen in such a way to determine the magnitude of the relative scaling.

\boldsymbol{\diamond}

A similar argument as in the proof of Proposition
2.2

allows us to establish the following variation on the effect of ESG ratings on
risk assessment in case the financial position and the ESG rating are independent.

Proposition 2.3

(Favorable and Unfavorable ESG Exposures)
.

Let
(X,S)\in\mathcal{Q}
such that
X
and
S
are independent and assume that both
u_{2}
and
s\mapsto u(s,x)
are non-decreasing for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Then

\mathbb{E}[u_{2}(S)]\leq 0\qquad\text{implies}\qquad\rho[X,S]\geq\hat{\rho}[X],

whereas

\mathbb{E}[u_{2}(S)]\geq 0\qquad\text{implies}\qquad\rho[X,S]\leq\hat{\rho}[X].

Proof.

We only prove the inequality
\rho[X,S]\geq\hat{\rho}[X]
, as the second one
clearly follows by the same line of reasoning. By definition of
\rho
and

\hat{\rho}
, the result follows provided we can show that

\bigl\{m\in\mathbb{R}:\mathbb{E}\bigl[u(X+m,S)\bigr]\geq 0\bigr\}\subseteq\bigl\{m\in\mathbb{R}:\mathbb{E}\bigl[u_{1}(X+m)\bigr]\geq 0\bigr\}.

Let us therefore fix
m\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathbb{E}[u(X+m,S)]\geq 0
and proceed to
show that this implies
\mathbb{E}[u_{1}(X+m)]\geq 0
. For this, we compute

\displaystyle 0\leq\mathbb{E}[u(X+m,S)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl[u_{1}(X+m)\bigr]+\mathbb{E}\bigl[u_{2}(S)\bigl(1+ku_{1}(X+m)\bigr)\bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl[u_{1}(X+m)\bigr]+\mathbb{E}\bigl[u_{2}(S)\bigr]\mathbb{E}\bigl[1+ku_{1}(X+m)\bigr],

where the last identity follows from independence of
X
and
S
. Now

\mathbb{E}[u(X+m,S)]\geq 0
is only possible if
u(X+m,S)\neq-\infty
almost
surely, and hence
\mathbb{E}[1+ku_{1}(X+m)]\geq 0
by
Proposition
2.1
. But then
\mathbb{E}[u_{2}(S)]\leq 0
implies

0\leq\mathbb{E}\bigl[u_{1}(X+m)\bigr]+\mathbb{E}\bigl[u_{2}(S)\bigr]\mathbb{E}\bigl[1+ku_{1}(X+m)\bigr]\leq\mathbb{E}\bigl[u_{1}(X+m)\bigr],

which concludes the proof.
∎

A close inspection of the proof of Proposition
2.3

reveals that instead of assuming independence of
X
and
S
and

\mathbb{E}[u_{2}(S)]\leq 0
, it suffices to assume that

\mathbb{E}\bigl[u_{2}(S)\bigl(1+ku_{1}(X+m)\bigr)\bigr]\leq 0\qquad\text{for all }m\in\mathbb{R}\text{ with }\mathbb{E}\bigl[u(X+m,S)\bigr]\geq 0

(4)

to conclude that
\rho[X,S]\leq\hat{\rho}[X]
. However, this is certainly a
less convenient condition to check. In particular, even though one can assume

1+ku_{1}(X+m)\geq 0
almost surely, the sign of the first expectation in
(
4
) does in general depend on the particular choice
of
X
and
S
as well as their joint distribution.

2.4
Convex ESG Risk Measures

We conclude the theoretical discussion of ESG risk measures by introducing
conditions for convexity in
X
and
(X,S)
. Not surprisingly, convexity
properties of
\rho
are directly inhereted from concavity properties of

u
.

Theorem 2.3

(Convexity)
.

Suppose that
x\mapsto u(x,s)
is concave for all
s\in\mathbb{S}
and let

S:\Omega\to\mathbb{S}
. Then

X\mapsto\rho[X,S]\qquad\text{is convex on }\bigl\{X:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}\;\big|\;(X,S)\in\mathcal{Q}\bigr\}.

Similarly, if
u
is jointly concave, then

(X,S)\mapsto\rho[X,Y]\qquad\text{is convex on }\mathcal{Q}.

Proof.

Let
\lambda\in[0,1]
and
(X_{1},S_{1}),(X_{2},S_{2})\in\mathcal{Q}
, where we assume
in addition that
S_{1}=S_{2}
if we are in the case in which
u
is only
assumed to be concave in the variable
x
. In either case, we have to show that

\rho[\lambda X_{1}+(1-\lambda)X_{2},\lambda S_{1}+(1-\lambda)S_{2}]\leq\lambda\rho[X_{1},S_{1}]+(1-\lambda)\rho[X_{2},S_{2}].

If either
\rho[X_{1},S_{1}]=\infty
or
\rho[X_{2},S_{2}]=\infty
there is
nothing to show, so we may in fact assume these two quantities to be finite. But
then there exist
m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\mathbb{E}\bigl[u(X_{1}+m_{1},S_{1})\bigr]\geq 0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbb{E}\bigl[u(X_{2}+m_{2},S_{2})\bigr]\geq 0.

By concavity of
u
, we find that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\lambda\mathbb{E}\bigl[u(X_{1}+m_{1},S_{1})\bigr]+(1-\lambda)\mathbb{E}\bigl[u(X_{2}+m_{2},S_{2})\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\bigl[u\bigl(\lambda X_{1}+(1-\lambda)X_{2}+m_{\lambda},\lambda S_{1}+(1-\lambda)S_{2}\bigr)\bigr],

where
m_{\lambda}\coloneqq\lambda m_{1}+(1-\lambda)m_{2}
. Note that the argument is also correct if
u
is only concave in
x
since we assumed
S_{1}=S_{2}
in that case.
In any case, it follows that

\rho[\lambda X_{1}+(1-\lambda)X_{2},\lambda S_{1}+(1-\lambda)S_{2}]\leq m_{\lambda}=\lambda m_{1}+(1-\lambda)m_{2},

so that minimization over both
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
yields convexity of
\rho
.
∎

While convexity of a risk measure in the financial component
X
is a desirable property as it favors diversification, it is up for debate if a risk measure should also be convex in the ESG component.
In fact, the evaluation of ESG exposures is arguably less straightforward to quantify than the evaluation of financial exposures, and hence it is much more reasonable to also allow more general utility functions
u_{2}
which are not necessarily convex such as the S-shaped utility considered in the following example.

2.5
S-Shaped ESG Preferences.

A reasonable choice for a non-concave utility function to model ESG preferences is, for example, an S-shaped utility of the parametric form

u_{2}(s)=\begin{cases}(s-s_{0})^{\gamma}&\text{if }s\geq s_{0}\\
-\lambda(s-s_{0})^{\gamma}&\text{if }s\leq s_{0}\end{cases}\qquad s\in\mathbb{S}.

Here, the reference point
s_{0}\in\mathbb{S}
satisfies
u_{2}(s_{0})=0
, that is, it is the benchmark ESG level
separating favorable from unfavorable exposures in the spirit of Proposition
2.3
.
\boldsymbol{\diamond}

3 ESG Risk of S&P 500 Companies and Minimum Risk Portfolios

In this section, we discuss how ESG risk measures perform in practice by applying them to market data.
We focus on the uncapped entropic ESG risk measure introduced in Example
2.3
and base our calculations on a data set of stock prices and ESG ratings of the S&P 500 companies ranging from October 2021 to January 2025.
We compare the entropic ESG risk measure with its classical financial counterpart—the entropic risk measure defined in Equation (
1
)—and provide a detailed discussion of the effects of uncertainty in the ESG rating on the risk assessment.
We conclude our empirical study with a discussion of the effect of ESG risk on portfolio selection by computing and comparing minimum risk portfolios with and without ESG risk.

3.2
Forecasting of Stock Prices and ESG Ratings

In the following subsections, we compute risk measures associated with a position
(X,S^{\mathrm{norm}})
over an investment horizon of
\Delta\coloneqq 1/12
years, that is, one month.
To compute the risk measures, we need to impose assumptions on the distribution of
(X,S^{\mathrm{norm}})
.
Before doing so, we first introduce another transformation acting on the normalized ESG ratings
S^{\mathrm{norm}}
in order to achieve comparable codomains of stock prices and ESG ratings.
To be precise, we subsequently consider

S^{\mathrm{norm}}\mapsto S^{\mathrm{rescaled}}\coloneqq\tan\Bigl(\frac{\pi}{2}S^{\mathrm{norm}}\Bigr),

leading to rescaled ESG ratings
S^{\mathrm{rescaled}}
with values in the positive real line
\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
With this, we model the monthly
\log
-returns of the stock and monthly
\log
-changes of rescaled ESG ratings by the respective random variables

R_{X}\coloneqq\mu_{X}\Delta+\sqrt{\Delta}Z_{1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad R_{S}\coloneqq J_{S}\Bigl(\mu_{S}\Delta+\sqrt{\Delta}Z_{2}\Bigr).

Here,
\mu_{X},\mu_{S}\in\mathbb{R}
denote the annualized mean returns of the stock and the rescaled ESG rating, respectively.
The random variable
J_{S}
is assumed to be Bernoulli with values in
\{0,1\}
.
We interpret the value

p\coloneqq\mathbb{P}[\{J_{S}=1\}]\in[0,1]

as the probability of a change in the ESG rating occurring within one month.
As pointed out in the previous subsection, this is necessary since ESG ratings generally do not change on a monthly basis.
The random vector
Z=(Z_{1},Z_{2})^{\top}
is drawn from a two dimensional normal distribution with zero mean and covariance matrix

C\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{X}^{2}&\rho\sigma_{X}\sigma_{S}\\
\rho\sigma_{X}\sigma_{S}&\sigma_{S}^{2}\end{pmatrix}

with
\sigma_{X},\sigma_{S}>0
denoting the annualized standard deviation of the returns of the stock and the ESG rating, respectively.
Moreover,
\rho\in[-1,1]
denotes the correlation between the two returns conditional on
J_{S}=1
, that is, conditional on the ESG rating changing within a month.
Observe that while the random variables
J_{s}
and
Z
are assumed to be independent, the returns
R_{X}
and
R_{S}
are clearly correlated due to the dependence of
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
.

If we denote by
N\in\mathbb{R}
today’s dollar investment in the stock, the financial exposure one month into the future is given by

X\coloneqq N\bigl(e^{R_{X}}-1\bigr).

(5)

This definition implies that we assume
\log
-normal stock prices as, for example, in the Black–Scholes model.
Similarly, the rescaled ESG rating after one month is assumed to be given by

S^{\mathrm{rescaled}}\coloneqq S^{\mathrm{rescaled}}_{0}e^{R_{S}},

where
S^{\mathrm{rescaled}}_{0}
denotes today’s rescaled ESG rating.
Observe that this definition implies that
S^{\mathrm{rescaled}}
is
\log
-normal if we condition on a change in the ESG rating, that is, conditional on
J_{S}=1
.
Moreover, we can recover the normalized ESG rating one month into the future via

S^{\mathrm{norm}}\coloneqq\frac{2}{\pi}\arctan\bigl(S^{\mathrm{rescaled}}\bigr)=\frac{2}{\pi}\arctan\bigl(S^{\mathrm{rescaled}}_{0}e^{R_{S}}\bigr).

(6)

The parameters
\mu_{X},\mu_{S},\sigma_{X},\sigma_{S},\rho
and
p
determining the distribution of
(X,S^{\mathrm{norm}})
can be calibrated from the dataset discussed in the previous subsection.
The investment amount
N
appearing in Equation (
5
) is chosen to as
N\coloneqq\$1
for simplicity.
This makes it possible to sample from the distribution of
(X,S^{\mathrm{norm}})
, the financial and normalized ESG position one month into the future.

.

X_{i}

\log

R_{X}^{i}
of the stock and the monthly
\log
-changes of rescaled ESG ratings
R_{S}^{i}

R_{X}^{i}\coloneqq\mu_{X}^{i}\Delta+\sqrt{\Delta}Z_{1}^{i}\qquad\text{and}\qquad R_{S}^{i}\coloneqq J_{S}^{i}\Bigl(\mu_{S}^{i}\Delta+\sqrt{\Delta}Z_{2}^{i}\Bigr),\qquad i=1,\dots,11,

In the multi-stock setting considered here, we allow both the Bernoulli random variables

and the normal random variables

to exhibit dependence.

(Z_{1}^{1},Z_{2}^{1},\dots,Z_{1}^{11},Z_{2}^{11})^{\top}

This approach allows us to capture correlations between jump times in the ESG rating, jumps of the ESG ratings, and stock price returns also across different companies.

Short-selling is assumed to be prohibited, and the fraction of wealth per company is capped at 20% to incentivize diversification.

\Xi\coloneqq\Bigl\{w=(w_{1},\dots,w_{11})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{11}:\sum_{i=1}^{11}w_{i}=1\text{ and }0\leq w_{i}\leq 0.2\text{ for }i=1,\dots,11\Bigr\}.

\min_{w\in\Xi}\hat{\rho}[X^{w}]\qquad\text{and}\qquad\min_{w\in\Xi}\rho[X^{w},S^{w}],

.

The parameters determining the distribution of
(X_{i},S_{i}^{\mathrm{norm}})_{i=1,\dots,11}

The minimum risk portfolio is updated on a monthly bases, with the parameters always based on the previous 20 months of data.

4 Conclusion

This paper introduces a new class of risk measures for ESG assets capable of combining financial risk and risk arising from an asset’s ESG rating.
The construction is based on ideas from multi-attribute utility theory, allowing us to build upon axiomatic utility theory to develop ESG risk measures with desirable properties such as translation invariance, monotonicity, and convexity.
In an extensive empirical study, we apply ESG risk measures to an S&P 500 dataset.
Our results show that ESG risk measures are capable of differentiating assets with similar financial performance based on their ESG assessment.
Moreover, we show how ESG risk measures can be used to improve the ESG performance of an investment portfolio without sacrificing financial performance.
