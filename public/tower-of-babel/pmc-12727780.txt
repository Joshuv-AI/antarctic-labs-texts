Effect of combining targeted therapies or chemotherapy with stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) on the prognosis of patients with brain metastases from non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC)
Authors: Guy N. Ron, Ilan G. Ron, Halit Kantor, Eyal Fening, Shlomit Yust-Katz, Alexandra Amiel, Yosef Laviv, Benjamin W. Corn, Andrew A. Kanner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRecent studies suggest that stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) increases penetration of systemic drugs into the CNS, and induces immune response to tumor-specific antigens on the surface of tumor cells. Radiation treatment concomitant with immunotherapies and biological treatments for brain metastases (BM) can enhance the body’s immune system to fight the tumor.MethodsA 10-year retrospective cohort study of 61 patients, 18 years and older, with BM from primary non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) tumors was divided into those who received targeted therapy according to tumor cell mutations or immunoexpression, and those who received standard chemotherapy for metastatic NSCLC with BM. All patients received the treatment concomitant with or within 2 months following the SRS procedure. Brain progression-free survival (brain-PFS), overall survival (OS), and Karnofsky performance score (KPS) were compared between the two groups.Results Significant differences favoring targeted therapy were observed in brain-PFS (P = 0.001); Five-year OS was 40% vs. 19%, indicating a non-significant trend toward improved survival (P = 0.06). Within the first 6 months, 50% of the chemotherapy group experienced BM disease progression. In contrast, patients receiving targeted therapies showed brain-PFS below 50% over 12 months with a 5-year survival rate of 40%, compared to 19% in the chemotherapy group. After 12 months, 76% of the target group maintained localized brain tumor control (22/30) versus 29% in chemotherapy (9/31) (P = 0.012). Distal BM control was seen in 13% of the targeted therapy group (3/23), while none in chemotherapy exhibited response or control. No significant differences were found in systemic response or systemic PFS between the groups.Conclusion Targeted therapies together with SRS for BM in patients with primary NSCLC tumors significantly improved response and survival rates. Combining these two modalities produced greater response to treatment for both local and distal metastases, longer survival, and improved overall performance compared to the traditional radiation and chemotherapy.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Brain metastases (BM) constitute a major cause of morbidity and mortality in patients with metastatic cancer, with an incidence of 10–40% during the course of the disease [1]. The incidence of BM is highest for lung cancer (19.9%), followed by melanoma (6.9%), renal (6.5%), breast (5.1%), and colorectal (1.8%) cancers [2]. The mean overall survival (OS) of patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) BM is poor: 5–8 months with chemotherapy plus whole-brain radiotherapy (WBRT) and less than 3–6 months when left untreated [3].

For many years, it was believed that the central nervous system (CNS) is a sanctuary organ with minimal drug penetration. As a result, the management of BM focused on achieving local control and prolonged survival using a limited choice of therapeutic methods. Until recently, WBRT was considered the standard of care for multiple BM (despite its neurotoxic profile) [4]. In particularly dire circumstances, the therapeutic default was supportive care treatment alone and systemic chemotherapy was often discontinued. The development of BM was considered an oncological terminal event. The lack of a focal ablative non-invasive treatment set the stage for the development of stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) – a focused ablative radiation treatment delivered with sub-millimeter precision to the tumor using three-dimensions in 1–5 fractions. The typical SRS dose, which is 18–25 Gy, is delivered in a single treatment and is substantially higher than the biologically equivalent dose of a commonly prescribed WBRT dose of 30 Gy delivered in 10 fractions [5]. Advances in local therapies, particularly with SRS, have improved the control of BM and prolonged survival. However, none of these treatment methods showed an effect on the risk of developing new BM and/or extracranial disease, or on the OS rates [4, 6–8].

Given the limitations of localized therapies, systemic therapies may benefit BM while simultaneously treating extracranial disease. There is growing evidence of durable responses to immunological and biological therapies, including activated infiltration of T-lymphocytes in the areas of BM [9, 10], and effective penetration of the blood-brain barrier (BBB) by targeting mutation treatment [11]. As a result, there has been an increase in the use of genetic characterization of BM aimed at designing new targeted therapies [12]. These therapies have become the standard first-line treatment choice in patients with metastatic NSCLC with a known mutation or a high PD-L1 expression, either as a single agent or in combination with chemotherapy [13]. Most importantly, immune checkpoint inhibitors (CPIs) and tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) have documented activity in the CNS [10, 14, 15], and emerging data suggest that outcomes following SRS delivery may be improved in patients receiving targeted therapy [9, 10, 16–20].

Advances in molecular genetics have led to enhanced understanding of the characterization of BM lesions, and the development of new treatment modalities that include specific targeted therapies [12]. These new approaches hold promise of increasing survival while protecting the healthy brain tissue when delivered to patients with a specific mutation or immunoexpression [21–24]. In this study, we compared the efficacy of SRS with targeted therapy vs. SRS with chemotherapy in patients with NSCLC BM tumors over a 12 month period.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition and treatment

A retrospective cohort study was conducted on patients with primary NSCLC with BM treated at the Rabin Medical Center, Israel, between the years 2008 and 2018. A total of 282 cases were reviewed. Inclusion criteria required patients to have undergone SRS and received systemic treatment either concurrently or within two months following radiation. After applying exclusion criteria, such as primary brain cancers, BM not originating from NSCLC, prior whole-brain radiotherapy, systemic therapy outside the timeframe, patients without follow-up imaging, or age under 18 years, 61 cases were included.

Patients were divided into two groups:

Group A (targeted therapies)

31 patients treated with immunotherapy or biological therapy in conjunction with or within two months following SRS.

Group B (chemotherapies)

30 patients who received chemotherapy either concurrently with SRS or within two months following the procedure.

Patient characteristics and demographic data including age, sex, SRS dose, number of BM at the time of SRS, status of local and systemic disease, and extent of systemic disease, were provided by the electronic medical record and MRI\PETCT images (Table 1). Status of local and systemic disease was defined by RECIST criteria based on MRI and PETCT scans with the help of neuro-radiologists.

Treatment was planned with ARIA software (Varian, Palo Alto, CA) using high-resolution, thin-slice (1 mm), gadolinium-enhanced MRI scans obtained prior to SRS planning. Radiation doses were prescribed based on a modified Radiation Therapy Oncology Group (RTOG) sliding scale, adjusting for lesion volume. A median dose of 18 Gy (interquartile range [IQR], 16.5–20 Gy) was delivered to the 80% isodose line at the lesion margin, adhering to the guidelines published by Shaw et al. [25] to ensure precise tumor targeting while sparing healthy tissue.

CNS responses were evaluated at 2–3 months, 6 months, and 12 months post-treatment using imaging to assess local response at the radiated metastases and distal recurrence at metastases in non-radiated brain areas, following RECIST criteria.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Rabin Medical Center, affiliated to the Gray Faculty of Medicine, Tel Aviv University.

Patient follow-up and response assessment

Patients were monitored through regular clinical examinations and imaging studies. Contrast-enhanced MRI of the brain was performed within 8 weeks of SRS, every 3 months for the first 2 years, and less frequently thereafter to monitor treatment response and disease progression. Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) and Recursive Partitioning Analysis (RPA) scores were recorded to assess patient performance and survival probabilities.

Brain disease progression was classified into two categories: distant brain failure and local failure. Distant brain failure was defined as the appearance of new lesions in the brain outside the prior SRS target area, while local failure was determined by a 20% increase in enhancement on MRI axial slices and increased perfusion on perfusion-weighted imaging, according to RECIST 1.1 criteria. To differentiate true progression from pseudo-progression, neuroradiology and oncology reports were utilized following detailed MRI sequence examinations. Additionally, initial brain metastases velocity (iBMV) was calculated as the number of brain metastases present at initial SRS divided by the time (in years) from the initial cancer diagnosis to the first SRS. Neurologic death, as defined by Patchell et al. [26], occurred when patients died with progressive neurologic decline (regardless of extracranial disease status) or with severe neurologic dysfunction while succumbing to intercurrent disease.

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed with the SPSS version 27 for Windows (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Ill, USA), R version 3.5 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and SAS version 9.4 (SAS Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Data were summarized using count (frequency), mean, median (IQR) and standard deviation (SD). Patients were stratified by receipt of targeted therapy or chemotherapy. Categorical data were compared using the Fisher exact or chi-squared test as appropriate. Continuous data were compared using the T test, Mann-Whitney U Test, or Kruskal Wallis Test. All time-to-event analyses were performed from the time of SRS. Follow-up time was calculated using Kaplan-Meier estimate of potential follow-up. Kaplan-Meier curves, with the log-rank test, were used to compare survival according to the treatment. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was used to evaluate independent variables associated with survival. The multivariable Cox regression analysis included the clinical parameters that were significant on univariable analysis. Possible interactions were also assessed. A two-sided P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical and demographic data of the cohort [Table 1]

The two cohorts were similar in size: n = 31 and n = 30 for the chemotherapy and targeted therapy groups, respectively. There were no differences between the two groups with respect to mean age, number of BM, volume of BM and radiation values. Findings on three parameters that impact survival – RPA, iBMV and ECM (extra cranial metastases) – were in favor of the chemotherapy group, but the targeted therapy group had a significantly better outcome vis-a-vis survival. (P = 0.047, P = 0.012, P = 0.023, respectively).

The particular targeted therapy was selected according to the patient’s tumor mutations, or by PD-L1 expression. In some cases, patients exhibited multiple mutations or expressed PD-L1. PD-L1 was found in 50% of patients (n = 15), and EGFR and ALK mutations were present in 40% and 13.3%, respectively. Other treatable mutations were found in 16.7% of the 30 patients [Table 2].

Characteristics of the lesions

PFS and OS

Brain PFS

At the end of the 12-month follow-up, the targeted therapy group exhibited progression of the tumors in 43.3% of cases compared to 87.1% in the chemotherapy group. The median time of progression was not reached during the follow-up in the targeted group, and the average time to progression was 10 months. 50% of the chemotherapy group exhibited progression of the BM disease during the first 6 months of follow-up, and average time to progression was 7 months.

No brain disease progression occurred in 56% of the targeted therapy group compared to 12.9% of the chemotherapy group (P = 0.001) (Fig. 1).

Brain progression-free survival (PFS, in months from SRS treatment)

There was no statistical difference between two subgroups of the targeted therapy group: the immunotherapies (ICIs) and the tyrosine kinase inhibitors (EGFR-TKI, ALK-TKI) in terms of progression. At the end of the 12 months, 58.3% of the TKI subgroup and 55.6% of the ICIs subgroup showed no progression. Mean time for progression was similar as well: 11.2 months and 10.4 months, respectively (P = 0.75).

On univariate analysis, patients with a poor performance score on the KPS scale prior to the SRS treatment and patients with lower RPA classification (especially RPA 3) had worse brain-PFS outcomes in both the chemotherapy and targeted therapy groups (P = 0.006, HR 0.95 CI 0.93–0.98, P = 0.001, HR 0.12 CI 0.03–0.45, respectively).

There was no correlation between brain PFS and age, gender, ECM, number or volume of brain lesions, time of starting the systemic treatment (concurrent or within 2 months of the SRS), or iBMV in both groups.

A multivariate analysis was conducted to determine the impact of prognostic factors, namely RPA and KPS on brain-PFS. Cox regression analysis revealed that the RPA classification was a superior prognostic factor for assessing the progression of brain lesions (P = 0.001). Specifically, RPA-1 and RPA-2 were found to significantly influence the outcome, acting as protective factors for brain-PFS (P = 0.003, HR 0.13, 95% CI 0.033–0.510; P = 0.001, HR 0.081, 95% CI 0.019–0.337, respectively).

Systemic PFS

No statistically significant difference was found between the two groups in terms of ECM progression (P = 0.47).

Overall survival

At the end of the follow up period, 15 (25%) patients had died: 10 (32.2%) in the chemotherapy group and 5 (16.6%) in the targeted therapy group.

After 5 years, 12 (40%) patients in the targeted therapy group and 6 (19%) in the chemotherapy therapy group were alive. The median survival was 39 months in the targeted therapy group and 18 months in the chemotherapy group. Average time to death was 44 ± 6 months, and 19 ± 2.5 months in the targeted therapy group and the chemotherapy group, respectively (P = 0.06) (Fig. 2).

There was no significant difference in OS between the two targeted therapy subgroups – immunotherapy (ICIs) and biological treatment (TKIs) (P = 0.6): mean 35.9 months and 34.8 months, respectively, and median 39.5 months and 29 months, respectively.

Response to treatment

Response of local BM to therapy

The 6 months’ and 12 months’ local control rates (CR + PR + SD) were significantly better in the targeted therapy group compared to the chemotherapy group (83.3% vs. 51.6%, p < 0.001 and 76% vs. 29%, p = 0.012; respectively).

Response of distal BM to therapy

The 6 months’ and 12 months’ distal control rates (CR + PR + SD) were significantly better in the targeted therapy group compared to the chemotherapy group (19.2% vs. 0%, p < 0.001 and 13% vs. 0%, p = 0.27; respectively).

Systemic response to treatment

Repeated systemic scans (total body CT/PET-CT) showed no evidence of statistically significant differences between the two groups.

KPS over 12 months of treatments

Performance status was assessed by routine oncology check-ups following SRS. Performance was rated using KPS at 3, 6 and 12 months. At time of SRS initiation, the median KPS score was 90 in the chemotherapy group and 80 in the targeted therapy group.

Six months following radiation, the median KPS score was 80 in both groups, reflecting a decrease only in the chemotherapy group.

Twelve months following radiation, the median KPS score was 80 in the targeted therapy group and 70 in the chemotherapy group; there was no change in the median KPS of the targeted group, while the score in the chemotherapy group had decreased 20 points from the initial 90 to 70.

At the end of the follow up period, there was a clear downward shift and greater dispersion of KPS scores in the chemotherapy group, whereas the targeted therapy group maintain a narrower distribution and relatively stable performance status (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Following the proven ability of immunotherapy to improve PFS and OS in patients with metastatic cancer across several tumor types, the possibility of combining this modality with radiotherapy has become the focus of considerable research interest [27]. Our study demonstrates significant survival and prognostic differences between the two modalities in patients with NSCLC and BM: SRS combined with targeted therapies, and SRS combined with chemotherapies. The PFS in patients who developed BM and received targeted therapies exhibited less than 50% progression during the 12 months of follow up (p = 0.001), and a 5-year survival rate of 40% compared to 19% in the chemotherapy group (p = 0.06). The 6 m and 12 m local control rates were significantly higher in the targeted therapy group (P = 0.001 and P = 0.012, respectively). We also observed an impact at the distal BM sites 6 months after the SRS treatment (P < 0.001), and some advantage, albeit not statistically significant, after 12 months. Better performance, measured by the KPS, was maintained in the targeted therapy group compared to the chemotherapy group. Indeed, there was a significant correlation between brain PFS and KPS; outcome was worse when KPS was 70 or below (P = 0.0001) (Fig. 4). These results can be explained by the disruption of the (BBB) caused by the combined effect of radiotherapy (SRS) that enhanced vessel permeability at the tumor site, and the increased immune response due to the penetration of targeted therapies to the brain that potentially increased their effectiveness [28].

Until recently, patients with untreated BM were excluded from immunotherapy and biological clinical trials due to the brain’s status as an ‘immune privileged’ site.

However, evidence is accumulating suggesting that durable responses to immunological and biological therapies can be achieved [29]. The presence of lymph vessels and activated T cells can trigger immune responses and increase the permeability of the BBB [9, 30]. In addition, TKIs, being small molecular drugs, are able to cross the BBB, enabling achieving a therapeutic level of the drug in the brain [11, 31, 32].

However, several studies have demonstrated certain brain metastases that are resistant to immunological and biological therapies due to their gene heterogeneity compared with the primary tumors [33–35]. This may explain the lower intracranial efficacy of systemic therapy, especially immunotherapy, and the resistance acquired following treatment with TKI in brain metastasis of patients with NSCLC [22, 24].

There is mounting evidence of a synergistic effect between the targeted radiation therapy (SRS) to brain tumors and the brain’s immune response [29, 36]. Extensive tumor cell injury and death produced by the radiation induces a massive release of tumor-specific antigens, as well as pro-inflammatory and pro-oxidant cytokines. The antigens and cytokines create a cascade of positive immune responses that results in the disruption of antigen regulation processes or immune responses that activate specific T cells against the metastasis [37–40].

CNS radiation is thought to enhance the permeability of the BBB [41], thereby increasing the concentration of TKIs in the CSF and reducing the occurrence of the T790M mutation [42, 43].

Our study demonstrated that treatment with radiation in combination with targeted therapies prevented the deterioration of the brain disease, lengthened life expectancy, and enabled improved overall performance. These factors existed regardless of age, gender, number and volume of metastases, radiation dosage and timing of the treatments. In addition to the strong brain response to the combined treatments, there were also noticeable effects of the protocol on the distal disease, albeit of lesser significance.

We found better clinical outcomes with the combination of SRS and targeted therapies than with the traditional systemic chemotherapy protocols. At the end of 12 months the disease had progressed in less than 50% of patients receiving the targeted therapy combination compared with 87% of the patients in the chemotherapy group. The average time to progression was 10 months in the targeted therapy group vs. 7 months in the chemotherapy group (P = 0.001). The median 5-year survival time was 39 months in patients who received targeted therapy compared to 18 months in the chemotherapy group (P = 0.06). An analysis of immunotherapies and TKIs yielded no statistically significance difference between intracranial PFS and 5-year OS. A possible explanation for these results is the mechanism of the CNS stereotactic radiation transient disruption of the BBB that enabled more efficient delivery and therefore enhanced bioavailability of therapeutics agents.

Importantly, even though the chemotherapy group had lower numbers of ECM lesions, better RPA classification and lower iBMV score, all of which predict better survival, the targeted therapy group actually had superior survival rates with significant statistical impact.

Several studies have compared targeted therapies with conventional systemic treatments, [21, 22, 44, 45] and reported better survival outcomes in patients receiving targeted therapy alone. Still other studies showed that SRS alone can prolong survival and control disease regardless of the number of brain metastases [28, 46, 47]. Several recent studies investigating the combined effect of SRS and targeted therapy are obtaining even better survival results, similar to those we present here [46–49]. A retrospective study that analyzed the responses to SRS combined with ipilimumab, nivolumab or pembrolizumab in 260 patients with NSCLC, melanoma, or renal cell carcinoma with BM, found that concurrent SRS/ICI was associated with longer OS (24.7 months) than SRS alone (12.9 months) or SRS with nonconcurrent ICI (14.5 months) [49]. Johung et al. [50] reported improved outcomes (PFS 11.5 months and OS 49.5 months) following a combination of radiation therapy (SRS/WBRT) and TKIs in patients with ALK rearranged NSCLC, reaching the uppermost range of survival reported in Lung-molGPA [51], which has an estimated overall survival of 46.8 months, despite considerable heterogeneity across other prognostic factors. In another multicenter study, [52] the median OS for the combination EGFR-TKI + SRS, EGFR-TKI + WBRT, and EGFR-TKI alone was 46, 30, and 25 months, respectively.

Beyond the effect on survival, our study showed additional favorable results of the combination of targeted therapy and SRS on BM compared to the results of chemotherapy and SRS. The effect on the local disease persisted during the whole 12 months of the study. By the end of the 12 months, 3 of the 30 patients in the targeted treatment group demonstrated continued response, 20 showed an effect on control of the local disease, and 7 had died. Of the 31 patients in the chemotherapy group, 3 demonstrated continued response, 6 showed control effect, disease progressed in 4, and 18 had died (P = 0.012).

A similar effect was noted in the distal brain disease sites. At the beginning of the study, 23 patients in the target group exhibited no distal brain disease; at the end of the year 12 remained free, 3 had controlled disease, and 8 exhibited progressive disease. Twenty-five patients in the chemotherapy group were disease free outside the radiated field at the beginning of the study; and by the end only 6 remained without disease, 7 showed progression, and 18 had died.

A meta-analysis of the synergistic approach of radiation therapy and immunotherapy or TKIs reported similar results in both survival and local brain disease, with superior local and distant response and control rate, prolonged time to progression, and OS with minimal adverse events [36, 42]. The better effect of combined radiation and targeted therapy on local and distant control response in BM can be explained by the direct and peripheral effects the radiation exerts in the CNS within a specific window in time. Radiation therapy has the advantage of being very effective in killing cancer cells by depositing its energy at the tumor site and damaging its DNA. Ionizing radiation has the potential to not only alter tumor tissue, but also to damage the glial, neuronal, and vasculature compartments of the brain. While damage to normal brain tissue is often considered an adverse consequence of ionizing radiation, the targeted and controlled application of radiation to intentionally damage brain tissue may be key in the use of ionizing radiation to increase BBB permeability. SRS radiation has been shown to increase the penetration and cerebral concentration of TKIs, and facilitate increased release of proinflammatory signals and activated T-cells that augment the efficacy of immunotherapy.

On multivariate analysis, performance status according to KPS and RPA appeared to be a predictive factor of the success of the treatment. Throughout the study, patients in the targeted group were able to maintain a high KPS score, making them less symptomatic and conferring a better quality of life. In addition, correlations between progression of brain disease and KPS at all time points provided a marker of the point of deterioration of the brain disease. KPS data was recorded until PFS of BM occurred, and used to produce an ROC curve (Fig. 4) that can predict the KPS threshold point of deterioration and brain progression. According to our results, the threshold KPS score of 70 coincides with NSCLC BM progression (P = 0.0001).

Other studies demonstrated statistically significant correlations between number of BM, tumor volume and extra-cranial metastases that significantly affected OS/PFS [53, 54]. We attribute the absence of these correlations in our study to our strict inclusion criteria and the relatively small number of patients in the two groups. We predict that in larger studies, tumor metastasis volume, number of BM and ECM will show significant differences between the two groups. Also, while most recent studies identify systemic progression as the predominant cause of death in NSCLC patients with brain metastases [55, 56], our cohort showed a higher proportion of neurologic deaths. This may partly reflect the inclusion of eight patients (18.2% of all deaths) who died during the COVID-19 pandemic, when treatment delivery itself was maintained but adherence to follow-up and monitoring was disrupted. In addition, defining neurologic death is inherently complex and may vary across reports. Finally, our longer follow-up period compared with prospective trials such as FLAURA [31] likely contributed to the higher recorded incidence of neurologic deaths.

The results suggest that the combination of SRS and targeted therapies, which have shown significant efficacy in treating brain metastases from NSCLC tumors, may provide a model for expanding these treatments to brain metastases from other tumor types. This highlights the potential for broader application of combined SRS and targeted therapy approaches, supporting ongoing research and clinical trials aimed at optimizing these strategies across various cancers.

Limitations

Among the limitations of this study are its retrospective design and single-institution setting. Additionally, the relatively small sample size limits the scope of multivariate analysis. However, the significant results observed provide strong evidence and warrant validation in larger, multi-center studies. Another limitation is the large number of oncologists who contributed to the selection of patients and periodic evaluation of their tumors during follow-up. We sought to overcome this limitation by strict inclusion and exclusion criteria, which narrowed the number of patients but created homogeneity within the groups except for the ECM, RPA and iBMV subgroups, which favored the chemotherapy group. Notwithstanding this, the results clearly showed that the targeted therapy produced a better outcome. A further limitation is the inclusion of treatment modalities from several generations of TKIs with different BBB penetration rates. The LUX-lung [57] and FLAURA [31] studies, which were conducted on patients with NSCLC-BM using all three generations of TKIs, yielded median PFS of 8.2–15.2 months, and OS of 15.9–22.4 months. Better results were obtained in our study with the combination of SRS and targeted therapies.

Conclusion

The study demonstrates the superiority of combining the modalities of SRS-BM and systemic targeted therapy in the treatment of local brain metastases, in addition to highlighting the advantages of the combination to achieving better local and distal control of BM, longer survival rates, and better quality of life, superior to the common systemic modality of radiation and chemotherapy. The findings highlight the importance of conducting large-scale, prospective, randomized studies to confirm and expand upon these results.
