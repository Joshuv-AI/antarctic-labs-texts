CPSMI2025: A curated dataset of conventional Pap smear microscopy images for deep learning-based cervical cancer screening
Authors: José Ocampo-López-Escalera, Martha Rosete-Aguilar, Héctor Ochoa-Díaz-López, Xariss M Sánchez-Chino, César A Irecta-Nájera, F Guillermo Domínguez-Gómez, Saúl D Tobar-Alas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCervical cancer remains one of the most common and deathly cancers in low-resource regions, where conventional Pap smear screening - though affordable - faces diagnostic bottlenecks due to limited specialist availability. To support the development of automated screening tools, we present CPSMI2025: a curated dataset of 2169 high-resolution Pap smear microscopy images representing nine clinically relevant cytology categories, derived from >350 manually screened slides obtained from Hospital General de Zona No 2 (IMSS) and a private pathology practice in Tuxtla Gutiérrez, Chiapas, Mexico. All slides were pre-classified by both a pathologist and cytotechnologist. Images were captured using a replicable open-source low-cost microscopy platform.Keywords: Cervical cytology automation, Point-of-care diagnostics, AI in cervical screening, Papanicolaou test, Chiapas Mexico

Background

Cervical cancer is still one of the most common and deadly cancers worldwide, despite being highly preventable and highly curable when detected in early stages. In 2022, cervical cancer took the lives of nearly 350,000 women worldwide, with >90 % of deaths occurring in low- and middle-income countries [2]. One of the main reasons for this disparity is the lack of effective infrastructure for early detection in low-resource settings.

Unlike most cancers, cervical cancer has a well-established cause: a persistent infection with high-risk strains of the human papillomavirus (HPV). Without effective treatment, these infections could progress to cancer over time. This progression typically occurs over 10 to 20 years, during which precancerous cellular changes (dysplasia)—detectable under a conventional microscope—develop [3]. Regular screening helps to identify these abnormalities before they become cancerous and to treat them properly. In high-income countries, modern screening methods—such as the human papillomavirus (HPV) test—have been implemented, demonstrating high sensitivity and specificity. However, deploying these tests in low-resource settings is often unfeasible due to their high cost and the need for advanced laboratory equipment and trained personnel [4]. On the other hand, conventional Pap smear is far more affordable and requires minimal infrastructure, making it the primary screening method for cervical cancer in resource-limited regions. However, it still relies on specialists to manually analyze samples under a microscope, creating a diagnostic bottleneck.

Deep learning (DL) algorithms have emerged as a promising solution for automating slide analysis, reducing dependence on specialists [5]. Yet, training these models requires large, pre-classified datasets. The dataset presented in this paper complements existing public datasets while offering distinct advantages.

This dataset comprises 2169 Pap smear microscopy images organized into nine subclasses further grouped within four major classes (Fig. 1). While not all subclasses directly correspond to categories in The Bethesda System (TBS) [6]—the most widely adopted framework for reporting cytological findings—each can be meaningfully associated with a TBS class. These subclasses were defined based on morphological characteristics and image availability, ensuring they contain distinguishable features suitable for classification by artificial intelligence models.

All images share consistent technical specifications: 3 channel JPG format with dimensions of 2028 × 1520pixels.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

The image dataset was curated using >350 cytology slides, each from a distinct patient, previously classified by both a pathologist and a cytotechnologist. These slides were sourced from Hospital General de Zona No 2 of the Instituto Mexicano del Seguro Social (IMSS) and Dr. Tobar’s private practice, both located in Tuxtla Gutiérrez, Chiapas, México. Including two sources allows to achieve certain variation in staining techniques, which is a positive characteristic for generalization purposes.

Images were captured using a replicable, automated low-cost microscope (described in [1]), equipped with a Newport MVC-20X objective offering 20 × magnification, 0.4 numerical aperture, and 8.72 mm working distance. The microscope also integrates an HQ Raspberry Pi camera featuring a 12.3-megapixel Sony IMX477 sensor. To account for variable lighting conditions caused by differences in cell density across fields of view, images were captured at full sensor resolution with auto-exposure adjustment. Subsequently, they were downsampled to one-quarter of their original dimensions using Python's Pillow library, employing the LANCZOS filter for high-quality resampling.

During curation, slides were manually scanned to identify fields of view FoVs representative of the previously described nine categories. Each FoV could contain multiple cell types; abnormal images were classified based on the most advanced lesion present, while normal images were categorized by the most abundant type. A custom Python script facilitated image capture, saving each FoV to a subclass-specific folder based on keyboard input.

A post-curation quality check was performed to eliminate severely out-of-focus or duplicated images. It is important to highlight that slightly out-of-focus images were intentionally retained to simulate real-world conditions where perfect focus may not always be achievable by autofocusing algorithms, ensuring the dataset’s practical relevance.

Limitations

•The diversity of cellular and non-cellular elements in cervical cytology is vast. However, the current dataset does not encompass the full spectrum of images typically encountered during a full Pap smear slide review.

The diversity of cellular and non-cellular elements in cervical cytology is vast. However, the current dataset does not encompass the full spectrum of images typically encountered during a full Pap smear slide review.

•The nine subcategories encompassed in the repository are defined on the basis of cytologic characteristics as well as the final slide diagnosis. It is important to note that cytology is a complex discipline that, in practice, integrates information from multiple fields of view to reach a diagnosis. As a result, the classification of an individual FoV can be challenging and, at times, may appear somewhat arbitrary between particular classes. However, for this reason, the nine subcategories are consolidated into broader, more robust major classes.

The nine subcategories encompassed in the repository are defined on the basis of cytologic characteristics as well as the final slide diagnosis. It is important to note that cytology is a complex discipline that, in practice, integrates information from multiple fields of view to reach a diagnosis. As a result, the classification of an individual FoV can be challenging and, at times, may appear somewhat arbitrary between particular classes. However, for this reason, the nine subcategories are consolidated into broader, more robust major classes.

•Additionally, the dataset exhibits class imbalance across diagnostic subcategories. For instance, the metaplasia class is underrepresented (22 images) compared to the squamous class (658 images). Such imbalances should be accounted for during model training to avoid skewed performance.
