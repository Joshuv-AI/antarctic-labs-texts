Racial, sex, and geographic disparities in mortality involving stroke and chronic kidney disease: a CDC WONDER database analysis, 1999–2020
Authors: Abhishek Kumar Shah, Suhana Chaulagain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Chronic kidney disease (CKD) increases the risk of stroke, and disparities exist in related mortality. Few studies have examined the mortality patterns involving both conditions together. This study evaluates national trends and disparities in stroke- and CKD-related mortality in the United States from 1999 to 2020.Methods:We analyzed death certificate data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Wide-Ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research (CDC WONDER) database using ICD-10 codes I60–I69 for stroke and N18 for CKD. Age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs) per 100 000 population were calculated and stratified by sex, race/ethnicity, age group, census region, and urban–rural classification. Temporal trends were analyzed using Joinpoint regression to estimate annual percent changes with 95% confidence intervals.Results:Between 1999 and 2020, a total of 140 501 stroke- and CKD-related deaths were recorded. The overall AAMR declined from 3.42 in 1999 to 2.71 in 2020. Females had the most deaths, but males had slightly higher AAMRs. The Black population exhibited the highest AAMR. Mortality increased with age and was highest among those aged 85 years or older. Geographic disparities were evident, with the highest rates in the southern states (the Stroke Belt).Conclusion:Mortality from stroke and CKD has declined over two decades, but disparities still persist across race and ethnicity, geography, and sex. Targeted prevention and healthcare access are essential to reduce these disparities.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a strong risk factor for stroke, which remains a major cause of illness and death globally. Individuals on dialysis have a tenfold greater risk of stroke, with mortality rates as high as 90%[1]. In addition to increasing the incidence of stroke, CKD also worsens outcomes after stroke. This elevated risk is driven by both traditional cardiovascular risk factors, such as hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia, and CKD-specific nontraditional risk factors, including albuminuria, fluid overload, inflammation, and vascular calcification[2]. Substantial disparities exist in stroke and CKD outcomes across demographic groups. Higher incidence and mortality rates are observed in Black and Hispanic populations compared to White individuals[3]. Similarly, men often exhibit higher mortality from stroke, while women tend to have a higher prevalence of CKD[4,5]. The “Stroke Belt” states in the southeastern US have disproportionately high mortality rates, thus showing geographic differences[6]. These disparities suggest the influence of social determinants of health, unequal access to care, and variations in healthcare quality.

While numerous studies have examined racial and sex disparities in stroke or CKD when considered separately, few have explored mortality patterns involving both conditions simultaneously[7,8]. Moreover, most previous analyses of cardiovascular mortality among patients with CKD have been restricted to earlier years or to specific regions or age groups, thereby limiting the understanding of nationwide trends. Importantly, there are no studies that address stroke-specific outcomes in this population. With the availability of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Wide-Ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research (CDC WONDER) database, long-term, population-level trends in mortality can now be systematically assessed by demographic and geographic factors. This study aimed to evaluate racial, sex, and geographic disparities in mortality involving stroke and CKD in the United States from 1999 to 2020 using the CDC WONDER database.

Methods

Study setting and data source

A retrospective analysis of stroke- and CKD-related mortality was conducted using data obtained from the CDC WONDER database. The study period comprised data collected between 1999 and 2020. Mortality from 1999 to 2020 was examined using codes I60–I69 for stroke and N18 for CKD from the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Tenth Revision (ICD-10). Multiple cause-of-death public-use records were examined to identify deaths in which stroke or CKD was listed as either the underlying or a contributing cause of death, and these records have been used in prior studies[9]. Individuals with both conditions listed as either the primary cause of death or a contributing factor were included in our analysis. This study was exempt from local institutional review board approval because it used a publicly available, de-identified, government-issued dataset. The analysis and reporting followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) and TITAN guidelines[10,11].

Data extraction

The records were extracted from the database using ICD-10 codes I60–I69 for stroke and N18 for CKD and were stratified by year, population size, absolute number of deaths, gender, race/ethnicity, 10-year age groups, states, urban-rural status, census region, and place of death. The genders included were male and female, as recorded on the death certificates. Race/ethnicity was categorized as American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian or Pacific Islander, Hispanic or Latino, Black or African American, and White. The 10-year age groups were predefined as 35–44, 45–54, 55–64, 65–74, 75–84, and 85+ years.

This information depends on data reported on death certificates and has been used in prior analyses of the CDC-WONDER database[12].

Urban–rural classification followed the 2013 US Census and the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) definitions, categorized as large central metropolitan, large fringe metropolitan, medium metropolitan, small metropolitan, micropolitan, and noncore. These urbanization groups were consolidated into two urban–rural tiers: metropolitan (small metro, medium metro, large fringe metro, and large central metro) and nonmetropolitan (micropolitan and noncore). According to the US Census Bureau definitions, the census regions were defined as Northeast, South, West, and Midwest[13].

Statistical analysis

Nationwide trends in stroke- and CKD-related mortality were examined using crude mortality rates (CMRs) and age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs), calculated per 100 000 population from 1999 to 2020. Rates were stratified by year, gender, race/ethnicity, 10-year age groups, place of death, state, urban-rural status, and census regions, with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). CMRs were calculated by dividing the number of deaths attributed to stroke or CKD by the respective US population for that year. AAMRs were standardized to the year 2000 US standard population[14]. The Joinpoint Regression Program (Joinpoint version 5.4.0, National Cancer Institute; produced by the Statistical Research and Applications Branch, National Cancer Institute) was used to assess trends in AAMRs and CMRs[15]. This method identifies statistically significant changes in mortality trends over time by fitting log-linear regression models and detecting points at which trends shift in direction or magnitude. Annual percent changes (APCs) with 95% CIs were calculated for each time segment at joinpoints using the Monte Carlo permutation test. APC trends were classified as increasing or decreasing when the slope of the regression line was significantly different from zero, as determined by two-tailed t-tests. A P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

There were a total of 140 501 deaths due to stroke and CKD (Fig. 1 and Supplemental Digital Content Table S1, available at: https://links.lww.com/MS9/B380). Of the 140 501 deaths, data on place of death were available for 140 144 deaths. Of these, 47.51% of deaths occurred in a medical facility (inpatient), 25.83% occurred in nursing home/long-term care, 15.01% in the decedent’s home, 4.65% in a hospice facility, 3.23% in a medical facility (outpatient or emergency room), 3.14% in other locations, 0.21% in a medical facility (dead on arrival), 0.19% in an unknown location, and 0.12% in a medical facility (status unknown) (Supplemental Digital Content Table S2, available at: https://links.lww.com/MS9/B380).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to offer a comprehensive analysis of stroke- and CKD-related mortality stratified by demographic factors and regions across the United States from 1999 to 2020. Over this period, a total of 140 501 stroke- and CKD-related deaths were reported. There was an overall decrease in mortality rates from 1999 to 2020. This might be due to several factors, such as medical advancements, technological innovations, and national programs aimed at increasing awareness of cardiovascular risk factors. This decrease may also be due to the effective control of hypertension, which has lowered both stroke incidence and case-fatality rates. There have also been improvements in diabetes, lipid, and smoking-cessation interventions[16].

The majority of deaths occurred among females, yet they had a lower AAMR than males. This contrasts with a retrospective study conducted in the United States, where males had a higher number of deaths than females and also a higher AAMR[17]. The higher number of deaths in females might be because they make up a larger share of the elderly population, and mortality from CKD and stroke is higher in elderly women. A review stated that women exhibit a higher or comparable stroke incidence compared with men in younger age groups[18]. During middle age, stroke incidence becomes higher among men[18]. However, as women reach the postmenopausal period, this sex difference narrows, and by their 80s, the incidence in women approaches or even exceeds that of men[19]. The same review also stated that a lower age-adjusted incidence of stroke in women than in men has been consistently reported[18]. Nonetheless, there was an overall decrease in AAMRs in both males and females from 1999 to 2020.

Individuals with CKD face a significantly higher risk of both ischemic and hemorrhagic strokes. CKD increases stroke risk through multiple mechanisms, including vascular calcification, arterial stiffness, chronic inflammation, and impaired regulation of cerebral blood flow. In patients with advanced CKD or those on dialysis, these factors, along with uremia and anticoagulation-related bleeding risk, further elevate the likelihood of both ischemic and hemorrhagic strokes[20]. Effective control of blood pressure is crucial in reducing stroke risk among CKD patients[21,22].

Black or African American individuals had the highest AAMR but also showed the most significant decline, whereas White individuals had the lowest AAMR with minimal change. Structural racism, socioeconomic inequalities, and limited access to care and neighborhood resources contribute to higher burdens of social determinants of health in Black populations, which help explain their elevated mortality (AAMR) compared with White individuals [23]. The higher AAMR in Black individuals is likely due to the greater prevalence and impact of hypertension and diabetes, while their significant decline reflects improvements in the management of these risk factors[24]. The second-highest AAMR was in the Hispanic or Latino population. This poor health outcome may be due to limited access to health insurance and preventive care, employment in high-risk occupations, cultural barriers, racism, and communication barriers[23,25].

All four US census regions initially saw a decline in age-adjusted mortality, followed by a brief surge, then a subsequent decline, with recent years showing a sustained upward trend. While the timing was similar, the magnitude of changes varied slightly between regions. The Northeast has a lower AAMR than the other regions. This is consistent with a similar study[9]. States in the 90th percentile had approximately double the AAMR compared with those in the 10th percentile. The same study also reported similar results[9]. The Stroke Belt phenomenon seems to apply here too, as the states in those regions are in the top 90th percentile. The findings from Douthit et al (2015) highlight the importance of addressing healthcare access disparities to improve health outcomes[26]. The observed urban–rural differences may reflect variations in healthcare access, socioeconomic conditions, and population risk profiles. Urban areas likely experienced greater fluctuations due to rapid demographic and lifestyle changes, while rural regions showed steadier trends, possibly indicating slower improvements in prevention and healthcare delivery. The differences in AAMRs across race, ethnicity, and geography in our study show disparities among these groups. Another study also highlighted emerging disparities by race, ethnicity, and geography[27].

Mortality was lowest in younger adults and highest among those aged 85 years and above. The CMR was high in older adults, especially those aged 65 years and above. This suggests that older adults with CKD remain at a high and persistent risk of stroke mortality. The risk of stroke occurrence is higher in older adults with CKD, and this elevated susceptibility likely contributes to the higher stroke-related mortality observed in this population[2]. Age-related vascular changes and accumulated comorbidities further exacerbate both the incidence and severity of strokes in these patients[28].

Limitations

The analysis is predicated on death certificate data from the CDC WONDER database, which may be incomplete or misclassified in terms of cause of death. Accurate identification of stroke and CKD depends on certifying physicians correctly assigning ICD-10 codes (I60–I69 for stroke and N18 for CKD). This coding could change over time and between states. The patient’s socioeconomic status, healthcare access, treatment type, and method of diagnosis are not disclosed by CDC WONDER. This study, being retrospective and based on aggregate population-level data, cannot establish causal relationships or control for potential confounding factors. There is no information on patients’ comorbidities, risk factors, or laboratory results. Among 140 501 deaths, data on place of death were accessible for 140 144 deaths. The remaining data were suppressed, most likely for confidentiality reasons. This suppression limited our ability to evaluate differences by place of death. CDC WONDER reports only CMRs when data are stratified by age groups or place of death, and it does not provide AAMRs. Also, urban–rural and regional classifications were based on the 2013 NCHS and US Census definitions, which may not perfectly reflect population changes or urbanization patterns during the entire 1999–2020 period.

Conclusion

Stroke- and CKD-related mortality in the United States declined overall between 1999 and 2020. Despite this progress, disparities remain across sex, race, age, and geography. Targeted interventions are needed to reduce these persistent demographic and regional disparities.
