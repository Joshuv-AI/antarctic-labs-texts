Patterns of social determinants of health screening and health-related social needs among patients with newly diagnosed breast cancer in a safety-net system
Authors: Malcolm Su, Hannah Klatzkow, Ronald Bass, Samar Bhat, Teresita Oaks, Marieshia Person, Shifa Kanjwal, Samira Syed, Arthur S. Hong, Deborah G. Freeland, Navid Sadeghi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeVarious social determinants of health (SDOH) have been shown to negatively impact breast cancer outcomes. Though there are recommendations for comprehensive SDOH assessment, little is known about the optimal frequency of reassessments and change in health-related social needs (HRSN) over time for specific patient populations. We sought to characterize SDOH screening and HRSN patterns among patients with breast cancer in a safety-net system.MethodsA retrospective study was conducted to evaluate SDOH survey responses in the electronic medical records and screening frequency from patients diagnosed with breast cancer between January 2021 to January 2024 at Parkland Health.ResultsThere were 566 patients diagnosed with breast cancer with a mean age at diagnosis of 55.0 (standard deviation, SD: 11.4) years. There were 54 (9.5%) with Stage IV breast cancer. Patients were initially at risk for 1.36 (SD: 1.39) out of the 11 HRSN domains. Patients completed an average 17.3 (SD: 8.9) out of 28 questions on initial survey. The most common risk domains identified were Alcohol Use and Food Insecurity. No patients were screened for Interpersonal Violence. The median time from initial survey to first change in risk level for any domain was 8.7 months. Patients with Stage IV disease underwent fewer SDOH re-screenings compared to patients with Stage I, II, and III disease (p < 0.001).ConclusionWithin our system, greater attention should be placed towards fully assessing patients’ HRSN and screening for interpersonal violence. SDOH screening at 6-month intervals may help to capture changes in HSRNs and assess the impact of resource interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10549-026-08095-0.

Introduction

Social determinants of health (SDOH) are non-medical conditions that reflect an individual’s community and environment [1, 2]. Known as health-related social needs (HRSNs), these determinants affect health through unreliable transportation, housing instability, treatment unaffordability, difficult access to healthy foods, among others [3]. Unmet HRSNs have been shown to negatively impact the lives of patients with breast cancer and are associated with poor outcomes [4, 5]. Disparities in SDOH are associated with decreased patient engagement with age-appropriate screening prior to diagnosis and delayed access to cancer treatments [6]. As a result, it is crucial to identify SDOH and address HRSNs which may impact the care received by patients with breast cancer.

Within breast cancer care, socioeconomic status, neighborhood location, and racial identity have been associated with delayed presentation and more aggressive tumor biology [7, 8]. To address these disparities earlier, there have been efforts to encourage regular SDOH screening during outpatient oncology clinic visits [9]. By incorporating SDOH screening into the ambulatory setting, patients may have greater access to resources to address their needs [10, 11]. Recommendations from the American Psychosocial Oncology Society and Association of Oncology Social Work encourage reviewing SDOH and patient hardships as part of routine care when assessing distress management [12].

Many health systems have begun adopting these recommendations for comprehensive SDOH assessment and screening for patient HRSNs during office visits [13]. However, little is known about the optimal timing and frequency of SDOH reassessments and whether these result in improvement of HRSNs for specific patient populations. For instance, assessing whether patients can access the resources provided is crucial to ensuring their needs are effectively addressed [14, 15].

When considering the complexity of organizing breast cancer care, there may be time constraints and resource limitations towards regular SDOH screening [14]. Additionally, as many of the SDOH electronic medical record (EMR) based screening tools are newly developed, regular screening may not be fully integrated into routine clinic workflows [15]. While classic SDOH factors include domains such as housing instability and food insecurity, the Parkland EPIC screening tool also captures risk behaviors such as alcohol use. Although these domains are conceptually distinct, we refer to this collectively as SDOH screening, consistent with its designation within the EPIC EMR. In this report we sought to characterize SDOH screening and HRSN risk levels among patients with breast cancer in our safety-net hospital system. As there are no standardized SDOH screening guidelines within our institution, we hypothesized that fewer than half of patients would undergo rescreening.

Methods

Overview and setting

We reviewed retrospective EMR data for all female patients who were diagnosed with new invasive breast cancer between January 2021 and January 2024 at Parkland Health, an academically affiliated, but clinically distinct, county safety-net system for Dallas County. Parkland Health is the main provider of cancer treatment for uninsured patients in Dallas County, a county with one of the highest uninsured rates in the United States. Parkland Health Breast Oncology clinics began regularly SDOH screening via the Epic EMR system in January 2021. Sociodemographic information, biopsy results, age of diagnosis, and SDOH screening is collected into the EMR as a regular part of care.

SDOH Screening Tool

Parkland’s SDOH screening tool utilizes 28 questions along with the PHQ-9 survey and 3 question history survey regarding tobacco use and smoke exposure to calculate the 11 HRSN risk domains: Alcohol Use, Food Insecurity, Housing Instability, Physical Activity, Tobacco Use, Financial Resource Strain, Stress, Social Connections, Transportation, Depression, Interpersonal Violence (IPV). The EPIC SDOH tool includes a series of questionnaires integrated in the EMR to systematically document and display patient SDOH data [16]. These surveys are primarily conducted by medical assistants during initial patient encounters. Surveys were administered by medical assistants as part of standard patient intake procedures during patient visits. The screening questions were available in English only, as it was administered through the EPIC EMR platform. Supplemental Table 1 provides a list of questions used by the screening tool. These clinically validated SDOH assessments were created in response to the National Academy of Medicine guidelines for SDOH factors that should be documented in health records [17]. For each domain, HRSN risk levels were determined based on the grading of corresponding questionnaires, as described in EPIC’s Social Determinants of Health Setup guidebook [18]. We noted each patient’s score for each domain as well as the number of questions answered for each screening completed over time.

The screening questionnaires within the EMR included the Finnish Institute of Occupational Health (for Stress); Protocol for Responding to and Assessing Patients’ Assets, Risks, and Experiences (PRAPARE) (for Transportation); Social Connection and Isolation Panel (for Social Connections); Exercise Vital Sign Screening Tool (for Physical Activity); Housing Stability Vital Sign Screening Tool (for Housing Stability); Children’s HealthWatch Hunger Vital Signs (for Food Insecurity); Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (for Depression); the HARK Screening Tool (for IPV); Alcohol Use Disorders Identification (AUDIT-C) test (for Alcohol Use); and questions adapted from the Coronary Artery Risk Development in Young Adults (CARDIA) and Study of Women’s Health Across the Nation (SWAN) studies (for Financial Strain) [19–26]. Further information regarding the psychometric properties and validity for the questionnaires are shown in Supplemental Table 1. Risk for Tobacco Use is determined by the answers documented in the Epic History tab separately and assesses the patient’s exposure to tobacco use and smoking, smokeless tobacco use, and passive smoke exposure.

Statistical analyses

We used descriptive statistics to examine cohort characteristics, as well as survey completion and repeat completion rates. We used a linear mixed model to characterize the change in number of survey questions answered over time. We used a multivariable linear regression model to identify associations between patient sociodemographic and clinicopathologic characteristics and the total number of SDOH screenings. For responses, we used ANOVA to compare the mean number of screenings and questions answered by patients grouped by the stage of their cancer diagnosis. All statistical tests were considered significant at a two-sided p-value of less than 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.2) [27]. This research was approved by the local Institutional Review Board (STU-052017-085).

Results

Cohort

Shown in Table 1, our cohort included 566 patients diagnosed with newly invasive breast cancer with a mean age at diagnosis was 55.0 (SD: 11.4) years. There were 386 (68.2%) White, 154 (27.2%) Black, and 19 (3.4%) Asian, Native Hawaiian, or other Pacific Islander patients. There were 345 (61.0%) patients who identified as Hispanic. There were 265 (46.8%) English speaking and 280 (49.5%) Spanish speaking patients. The remaining 21 (3.7%) patients spoke other languages including French, Amharic, Malayalam, Arabic, and Tagalog. The primary payor at time of diagnosis included 47 (8.3%) privately insured, 63 (11.1%) Medicaid, 75 (13.3%) Medicare, and 351 (62.0%) uninsured patients. There were 392 (69.3%) patients presenting with Stage I, 72 (12.7%) with Stage II, 48 (8.5%) with Stage III, and 54 (9.5%) with Stage IV breast cancer at time of diagnosis. A total of 87 (15.5%) presented with Grade I, 257 (45.8%) with Grade II, and 217 (38.7%) with Grade III disease on biopsy.

HRSN at-risk domains

Complete reporting of domains at risk identified are in Table 2. The most common domains identified across all screenings were Alcohol Use and Food Insecurity: half of patients were at risk in these domains (314 or 55.5%; 280 or 49.5%, respectively). No patients were screened for IPV. Table 3 displays the time from initial HRSN screening to subsequent change in risk level for each risk domain. There were no changes in risk level over time for Alcohol Use and Social Connections. The median (IQR) time from initial survey to first change in risk level for any domain was 8.7 (3.5–18.0) months. Patients responded to a mean 17.3 (SD: 8.9) questions during the initial survey. The number of answered questions increased by an estimated (95% confidence interval) 8.04 (7.52, 8.57; p < 0.001) questions every year from initial survey.

Discussion

Our study characterized how most patients are reassessed about 1.5 years after their initial SDOH survey within our health system. This rate of reassessment is longer than the yearly screening goals followed by other large health systems [28]. As demonstrated by Table 3, changes in risk level for any domain from baseline survey responses were typically captured within the first few months from initial evaluation. As a result, our patient. population may benefit from more frequent HRSN re-screening to help identify all HRSN a patient may experience. By engaging in more comprehensive SDOH screening initially, patients may have more opportunities to complete more questions on the HRSN survey and reflect any changes to preexisting risk domains. The number of questions answered during SDOH screenings increased significantly over time, suggesting that patients may become more comfortable or more engaged with the screening process as patient rapport develops. Taken as a whole, our health system may benefit from surveying HRSN needs ideally every 6 months initially given the lower baseline completion rate and rate of change in HRSN risk levels. Prior studies have also suggested that social needs may remain stable across three to six month time frames, and screening for social risks may be appropriate at 6 month intervals especially if patients desire resources to improve their care [29].

We note that the most frequently identified risk domain in our patient cohort was Alcohol Use. However, there were no documented changes in Alcohol Use reflected for any patient in updated screenings. This may reflect prior findings that the rates of alcohol use re-screening are generally low, which hinders effective monitoring [30, 31]. This is particularly crucial to address, as risk behaviors such as increased alcohol consumption has been associated with breast cancer recurrence [32]. Additionally, there are barriers to effective HRSN assessments utilizing the EPIC screening tool that may be impacting our findings. For instance, changes in alcohol use may be documented outside the tool and hidden in locations such as social history tabs or written in clinical notes [33, 34]. Social connection risk levels similarly remained unchanged across all follow-up screenings, which may reflect either true stability in this domain or inadequate reassessment of social isolation over time. Our findings illustrate that greater attention should be paid in assessing common HRSNs within our patient population and capturing changes appropriately in a centralized location for future providers to also follow-up.

For our health system, improving the accuracy of alcohol use monitoring may require consolidating scattered documentation rather than relying solely on the structured screening tool. For instance, there are various tabs and fields that allow providers to document Alcohol Use patterns, but only certain fields are considered by the SDOH screening tool. In other studies, electronic health record (EMR) clinical decision support tools paired with a standardized rescreening workflow have been shown to increase screening completion rates in other settings [35]. Our institution is currently working to first centralize this documentation to a single location on the EMR to improve provider visibility and consistency of follow-up.

In this study, no patients were screened for IPV on initial and follow-up SDOH screening surveys. Prior studies have shown that IPV screening is low among patients due to various factors [36], ranging from provider discomfort with the issue or lack of knowledge. Some hospital systems may also lack organizational infrastructure and policies regarding IPV which may hinder screening behaviors [37]. To screen for IPV within our health system, the staff conducting the survey is prompted on the EMR interface with a question if they can screen the patient for abuse or neglect. This portion of the survey also reminds the staff to conduct this portion of the survey if the patient alone and in a condition appropriate to ask screening questions (found in Appendix 1). The staff is also given a section allowing documentation of any observed signs of abuse, neglect, or trafficking. However, patients may not feel comfortable disclosing such information on initial visits or with a staff member they are not familiar with, and may rather share information with a trusted provider [38].

The prevalence of IPV is higher in breast cancer patients compared to the general population [39, 40]. Particularly, treatment for breast cancer can be associated with increased emotional and physical strain which may increase vulnerability to IPV [41]. These negative impacts lower patient’s quality of life and persist into cancer survivorship [42]. As a result, it remains important for breast oncologists to screen for IPV after establishing rapport with patients, especially if no other provider or staff member has been able to do so with the patient. Taken together, patients should be screened at least once for IPV by clinic staff, ideally within the two years of entering our hospital system. Providers should review this portion of the survey to assess if IPV screening has been attempted at least once and consider screening if not.

Alternatively, approaches to interpersonal violence screening from obstetric care may offer a useful complement to rapport-based screening. ACOG recommends universal screening at every prenatal visit, conducted privately and with non-stigmatizing language [43]. The CUES framework (Confidentiality, Universal Education, Empowerment, Support), originally validated in family planning clinics [44], can be utilized to educate all patients about IPV resources rather than relying on a single trusted provider to elicit disclosure. A universal, protocol-driven approach integrated at routine intervals may better serve our patient population [45].

In our cohort, patients with advanced disease appear to undergo fewer SDOH screenings. Our findings also suggest that older age may negatively predict number of screenings, while other factors such as language, survival, and race did not. These findings may relate to competing demands within cancer care and limited time to address patient needs [46, 47]. For instance, patients with advanced cancer may undergo more intensive treatment and require concerted, interdisciplinary care from multiple specialists [48]. The SDOH screening tool also does not specific focus on geriatric needs, including evaluation functional status and caregiver needs, which may take additional time to assess. As a result, there may be greater time constraints during visits and fewer opportunities for these patients to participate in frequent SDOH screening. Implementing standardized re-screening at regular intervals, such as every 6 months, may allow clinic staff to independently initiate re-screening rather than relying on providers to identify when reassessment is due, potentially reducing the impact of time constraints on monitoring patient needs.

Patients with cancer have often reported unexpressed needs, particularly financial and psychological distress [48]. Patients may have the perception that there is not enough time to share these needs with provider and prioritize other discussion, such as cancer treatment and prognosis [49]. It becomes increasing important to efficiently identify and recognize any HRSN that may require intervention. Strategies such as delegating SDOH screening among various team members, such as oncology social workers and trained staff members, can help reduce the burden and time constraints faced by providers [12].

There are several limitations to our study. Considering our study is based on responses in the SDOH survey, there may be HRSN assessments that are conducted utilizing other tools that are not captured. Patients may express social needs that are promptly addressed by staff members and providers but not reflected in the survey tool. Further work is needed to investigate physician and patient attitudes towards HRSN screening, particularly surrounding IPV and other sensitive conversations. By analyzing how patient rapport is built within our Parkland.

clinics, our hospital system can better understand communication or social barriers that may limit effective SDOH screening.

In summary, our study is one of few to analyze patterns in SDOH assessments and changes in HRSN risk levels across patients with breast cancer in a large safety-net system. Within our hospital system, greater attention should be placed towards fully assessing patients’ HRSNs. Particularly, we found that patients diagnosed with advanced stage breast cancer may undergo fewer re-screenings. As a result, SDOH screening at 6-month intervals may help our breast oncology clinic better capture changes in HSRNs and assess the impact of resource interventions.
