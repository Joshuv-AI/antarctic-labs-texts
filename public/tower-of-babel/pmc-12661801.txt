Woody plant species diversity, vegetation structure, and regeneration status of Modi-Geyi Forest in Andracha District, Southwest Ethiopia
Authors: Ashebir Awoke, Muluye Asnakew, Gezehagn Chagito, Andualem Arimo, Zewdie Kassa, Moa Megersa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Modi-Geyi Forest, a moist Afromontane forest in southwestern Ethiopia, is an important biodiversity reservoir that provides critical ecological services and supports local livelihoods. However, increasing anthropogenic disturbances and land-use pressures threaten its woody species diversity, vegetation structure, and regeneration capacity. Assessing its ecological status is therefore essential for guiding conservation strategies.MethodsFifty 20 × 20 m plots (400 m² each) were systematically established along five transects, with 200 m spacing between plots and 500 m between transects. Two 5 × 5 m subplots were nested within each main plot to assess seedlings and saplings. All woody plants with DBH ≥ 2.5 cm were identified and measured. Background

Forests are among the most critical ecosystems, providing biodiversity conservation, carbon sequestration, water regulation, and livelihoods for millions of people worldwide [1, 2]. In tropical regions, and particularly in Africa, they serve as reservoirs of biological diversity and sustain local communities through essential ecosystem services. Woody plants trees, shrubs, and lianas form the structural backbone of forests, influencing ecological resilience, habitat complexity, and regeneration capacity [3, 4]. In Africa, forests cover about 675 million hectares (35% of the continent’s land area) [5].

Ethiopia is recognized as a biodiversity hotspot, harboring around 6,027 plant species, 12% of which are endemic [6]. Historically, forests covered nearly 40% of Ethiopia’s land area, but this has declined drastically to 2.4% due to agricultural expansion, settlement, and unsustainable harvesting [7–9]. Moist evergreen Afromontane forests, in particular, are under severe pressure despite their high biodiversity and ecosystem service values [10–14]. Key canopy species such as Aningeria adolfi-friederici (Engl.) Robyns & Gilbert, Schefflera abyssinica (Hochst. ex A.Rich.) Harms, and Syzygium guineense (Willd.) DC. contribute to forest structure, while understory shrubs like Brucea antidysenterica J.F.Mill. and Dracaena afromontana Mildbr. enhance habitat diversity [12, 14]. Yet, logging, firewood collection, grazing, and conversion to tea and coffee plantations continue to degrade these forests [10, 12, 13].

Previous studies in Ethiopia have documented woody species diversity and forest structure in both moist and dry Afromontane forests, as well as in small church forests. These studies report uneven regeneration patterns and dominance of a few species, reflecting both natural processes and human disturbance. However, information remains scarce for less-studied forests such as Modi-Geyi in Andracha District, which forms part of the moist Afromontane ecosystem. Systematic, site-specific studies are essential to strengthen baseline data for conservation planning at local and national scales.

The Modi-Geyi Forest is a representative moist Afromontane forest fragment in southwestern Ethiopia. Despite its ecological importance, comprehensive studies of its woody plant diversity, structure, and regeneration status are lacking. Research in other forests has reported variation in species richness and regeneration for example, 46 species in Kahitassa and 48 in Alka [5, 15] underscoring the need for localized ecological assessments.

The objectives of this study were to: (i) assess the woody species diversity and floristic composition of Modi-Geyi Forest; (ii) identify and classify plant community types based on species composition and abundance; (iii) analyze vegetation structure through DBH and height class distributions, species density, basal area, and Importance Value Index (IVI); and (iv) evaluate the regeneration status of woody species across communities.

Accordingly, this study was guided by the following hypotheses: (i) Modi-Geyi Forest supports moderate to high woody species richness and diversity, comparable to other moist Afromontane forests in Ethiopia; (ii) DBH and height distributions follow an inverted J-shaped pattern, indicating ongoing regeneration alongside selective disturbance; (iii) regeneration status varies among species, with canopy dominants regenerating more strongly than understory species; and (iv) a few ecologically significant species contribute disproportionately to forest structure and composition, reflected in high IVI scores. By addressing these hypotheses, this study fills knowledge gaps on the ecological status of Modi-Geyi Forest and provides evidence to inform sustainable management and biodiversity conservation in Ethiopia’s moist Afromontane ecosystems.

Materials and methods

Description of the study area

The study was conducted in the Modi-Geyi Forest, located in Andracha District of the Sheka Zone, southwestern Ethiopia. Geographically, the area lies between 7.39°–7.81° N and 35.24°–35.50° E (Fig. 1). The district spans an elevation range of 1,001–3,000 m above sea level and covers approximately 102,000 ha, divided into 17 administrative kebeles, the smallest local administrative units in Ethiopia. Gecha, the district’s only urban center, is situated about 714 km southwest of Addis Ababa. Andracha is bordered by Masha District to the north, the Gambela Region to the southwest, the Oromia Region to the northwest, Yeki District to the south, and the Kafa Zone to the east.

Locational map of the study area. (source: generated by ArcGIS 10.8)

The district has a total population of 36,495, comprising 18,318 men and 18,177 women [16]. Most residents depend on farming and small-scale trade for their livelihoods. The dominant ethnic group is the Sheka, and Shekinono is the primary local language.

Climate data from the Ethiopian National Meteorological Agency (2001–2021) indicate a mean annual temperature of 20.6 °C, with monthly minima and maxima of 12.6 °C and 31.2 °C, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S1). The area receives an average annual rainfall of 2,074 mm, following a unimodal pattern with precipitation distributed across much of the year. This abundant and consistent rainfall supports moist evergreen Afromontane vegetation, which harbors a rich diversity of endemic and rare species. Agro-ecologically, the Andracha District comprises 56% highland, 24% midland (Woyna Dega), and 20% lowland (wet Qolla) zones [17].

Reconnaissance survey and sampling design

A reconnaissance survey was conducted from 10 September to 15 October 2022 to gain an overview of the study area and guide sampling design. The survey involved visual assessment of vegetation patterns to identify ecologically homogeneous sub-sites within the Modi-Geyi Forest. Four distinct sub-sites: Chacha, Mahewuta, Shishachi, and Yobba were delineated based on altitude, slope aspect, and disturbance intensity. This stratification approach, commonly used in ecological studies, ensures representative sampling across habitat types and accounts for spatial heterogeneity within Afromontane forests [18, 19].

Sampling plots were allocated proportionally: Mahewuta and Shishachi each received 15 plots (30% of the total), Chacha 13 plots (26%), and Yobba 7 plots (14%) (Table 1). In total, 50 plots measuring 20 × 20 m (400 m²) were established to assess mature trees, following established guidelines [20, 21]. Within each main plot, two 5 × 5 m subplots were nested one at the center and one at the corners to evaluate saplings and seedlings.

Plot distribution among visually classified vegetation groups

Plots were systematically arranged along five transects laid across central forest areas, with 200 m spacing between plots and 500 m between transects, in line with standard ecological sampling protocols [20].

Vegetation data collection

Vegetation data were collected using a systematic sampling method following [22]. In each main plot, all woody plant species were recorded, along with their regeneration status. Seedlings were defined as individuals ≤ 50 cm in height and ≤ 2.5 cm in DBH, while saplings were >50 cm in height but still ≤ 2.5 cm in DBH, following [23]. Environmental variables, including altitude and geographic coordinates, were measured for each plot using a Garmin GPS-72.

Woody species were first recorded using vernacular names and assigned specimen codes. Species that could not be identified in the field were collected with reproductive parts, properly pressed and dried, and sent to the National Herbarium at Addis Ababa University for identification. Taxonomic classification was based on morphological traits using standard references, including the Flora of Ethiopia and Eritrea (vols. 1–8) [24, 25].

For species identified in the field, nomenclature followed the Flora of Ethiopia and Eritrea with verification using [24, 26] and Plants of the World Online (https://powo.science.kew.org/). Final identifications were confirmed by Dr. Zewdie Kassa (botany specialist), and voucher specimens were deposited in the Herbarium of Mizan-Tepi University.

Data analysis

Species diversity and similarity indices

Species diversity, richness, and evenness were assessed using various indices. In this study, the Shannon-Wiener diversity index was applied, following the methodology described by [21, 22].

The Shannon diversity index (H′) is defined as follows: H′ = -Σ (Pi * ln(Pi)), where Pi represents the proportion of the total community abundance attributed to the ith species, ln(Pi) denotes the natural logarithm of Pi, S indicates the total number of species identified, and Σ signifies the summation from species 1 to species S. Plant community classification

Cluster analysis was performed in R version 4.4.2 to classify the vegetation into community types [27]. The percentage cover abundance of each species was converted into the 1–9 Braun-Blanquet scale. Community types identified from the cluster analysis were refined using a synoptic table, where species occurrences were summarized as synoptic cover-abundance values. The resulting dendrogram was examined to determine the optimal number of clusters, which were defined as distinct plant community types. Dominant species within each community type were identified based on their synoptic values, and community types were named accordingly.

Vegetation structure

DBH classification

To analyze the population structure of woody species, diameter at breast height (DBH) was measured for all trees, lianas, and shrubs with DBH ≥ 2.5 cm using a diameter tape within each sampling plot. For multi-stemmed individuals, the DBH of the largest stem was recorded, following [5, 25]. DBH values were then grouped into five classes: Class 1 (2.5–10 cm), Class 2 (11–20 cm), Class 3 (20.1–30 cm), Class 4 (30.1–40 cm), and Class 5 (>41 cm).

Height classification

Tree height was primarily measured using a clinometer. Heights were calculated using the formula: h = tan A × 2d, where h is tree height, A is the angle to the treetop, and d is the distance from the tree. When the clinometer could not be used, a calibrated bamboo stick was employed for direct measurement, with visual estimation applied to portions extending beyond the stick. Based on the frequency distribution of measurements, trees and shrubs were grouped into five height classes: Class 1 (2.5–10 m), Class 2 (11–20 m), Class 3 (20.1–30 m), Class 4 (30.1–40 m), and Class 5 (> 40 m).

Species density

Species density (D) was defined as the number of individuals per unit area. Tree density was calculated by summing all individuals recorded across plots and converting this value to a per-hectare basis, following [28–30].

Frequency (F):

Frequency (F) indicates the probability of encountering a species within a given area, measured at the plot level. It is calculated as the proportion of plots in which the species occurs relative to the total number of plots surveyed. This metric reflects the distribution uniformity of the species across the study area and offers valuable insights into its habitat characteristics.

Basal area (BA):

Basal area represents the cross-sectional area of a plant measured at breast height and is expressed in square meters per hectare (m²/ha) [28, 29]. This metric is key to assessing species dominance within a given area (Kent, 2012) and is directly related to the diameter at breast height (DBH). Analysis of the regeneration status of Woody plant species

The regeneration status of woody species was evaluated by recording seedlings, saplings, and mature trees within each plot, following [31]. Mature trees were defined as individuals with DBH >2.5 cm and height >1.5 m [5, 11]. Saplings were classified as plants with DSH < 2.5 cm and a height of 50–150 cm, while seedlings were defined as plants with DSH < 2.5 cm and a height of 1–50 cm [32–34].

Based on the relative abundance of these groups, regeneration status was classified into four categories. Species were considered to have good regeneration when the number of seedlings exceeded saplings and saplings outnumbered mature trees. Fair regeneration was assigned when seedlings were equal to or more abundant than saplings, and saplings were equal to or fewer than mature trees. Poor regeneration referred to species represented only by saplings, while not regenerated described species present solely as mature individuals [9, 11].

Results and discussion

Woody plant species composition and plant community types

A total of 56 woody plant species, representing 51 genera and 30 families, were recorded in the Modi-Geyi Forest (Supplementary file Table S1). This richness is characteristic of moist Afromontane forests and indicates moderate to high diversity. The number of species exceeds that reported for several moderately disturbed or fragmented forests in Ethiopia, such as southwest Ethiopia (48 species) [12], Borecha (43 species) [13], and Gerba Dima (50 species) [14], but is lower than more species-rich sites such as Agama (72 species) [11], Wurg (76 species) [35], and others [36]. Such differences are likely driven by variation in forest size, elevation gradients, disturbance history, and protection status.

Among the recorded species, Aningeria adolfi-friederici was the most abundant (485 individuals, 9.7%), followed by Dracaena afromontana (330, 6.6%), Syzygium guineense (309, 6.2%), and Ilex mitis (307, 6.1%). In contrast, Brucea antidysenterica was represented by only two individuals, likely reflecting regeneration failure, ecological specialization, or overexploitation.

Life form analysis revealed a dominance of trees (38 species, 67.8%), with shrubs contributing 32.2% (18 species). This structural pattern suggests the presence of a relatively intact canopy, typical of mature Afromontane systems. The high abundance of dominant tree species may reflect broader ecological tolerance, effective dispersal mechanisms, or resilience to anthropogenic disturbance such as selective logging.

At the family level, Rubiaceae was the most species-rich (9 species, 16.07%), followed by Asteraceae and Euphorbiaceae (5 species each, 8.93%), and Fabaceae (4 species, 7.14%). These patterns are consistent with reports from other montane forests of Ethiopia [1, 11, 31], where these families often include ecologically significant or keystone taxa central to forest structure and dynamics.

The forest also harbors several endemic species, including Vepris dainellii, Erythrina brucei, Millettia ferruginea, and Solanecio gigas, underscoring its conservation importance. The presence of endemic taxa highlights the forest’s role as a biodiversity hotspot with irreplaceable ecological value.

In addition, the forest supports numerous honeybee forage species, including Dombeya torrida, Maesa lanceolata, Vernonia auriculifera, Schefflera abyssinica, Allophylus abyssinicus, Apodytes dimidiata, and Phoenix reclinata. These species provide critical ecosystem services by sustaining pollinators while also supporting local livelihoods through apiculture [37, 38]. Such ecological-economic synergies create opportunities for community-based conservation models that integrate biodiversity protection with income-generating activities, as successfully demonstrated in Sheka and Gera forests.

Despite overall moderate diversity, the extremely low abundance of species like Brucea antidysenterica highlights ecological vulnerability. Targeted conservation measures, including enrichment planting, habitat restoration, seed banking, and ex situ propagation, are needed to prevent local extinction and enhance regeneration potential.

Plant community types

Cluster analysis of 50 sample plots identified five distinct plant community types using agglomerative hierarchical clustering, visualized through a reordered dendrogram in R (Fig. 2). Each community was named after the two dominant or characteristic species with the highest synoptic values: Oncoba spinosa–Chionanthus mildbraedii (Community Type 1), Aningeria adolfi-friederici –Croton macrostachyus (Community Type 2), Aningeria adolfi-friederici –Dracaena afromontana (Community Type 3), Dracaena afromontana–Psydrax parviflora (Community Type 4) and Ilex mitis– Aningeria adolfi-friederici (Community Type 5).

Community Type 3: Aningeria adolfi-friederici –Dracaena afromontana

Located at 2,138–2,162 m a.s.l., this community spans 12 plots and contains 42 species. Dominant species include Ilex mitis, Ficus sur, and Galiniera saxifraga. Less frequent species include Justicia shimperiana and Cordial africana.

Community Type 4: Dracaena afromontana–Psydrax parviflora

Represented by four plots between 2,135 and 2,166 m a.s.l., this community includes 34 woody species. Abundant taxa include Ilex mitis, Aningeria adolfi-friederici, and Vepris dainellii, while rarer ones include Maesa gracilipes and Rothmannia urcelliformis.

Community Type 5: Ilex mitis– Aningeria adolfi-friederici

At 2,119–2,186 m a.s.l., this community includes 45 species across 15 plots. Dominant species are Ilex mitis, Syzygium guineense, and Croton macrostachyus. Less common species include Azadirachta indica, Oncoba spinosa, and Olea welwitschii.

The classification of these five community types illustrates the ecological diversity and structural variability of the Modi-Geyi Forest, shaped largely by elevation, microclimate, and human disturbance. Species distribution clearly follows altitudinal gradients, with each community occupying a distinct ecological niche.

The repeated dominance of Aningeria adolfi-friederici across three communities (Types 2, 3, and 5) confirms its role as a keystone canopy species in Afromontane forests supporting findings from Wurg, Sheka, and Belete [26, 39, 40]. Ilex mitis and Dracaena afromontana also appear across multiple communities, often associated with mature, undisturbed forest conditions [8]. Community Type 1 occurring at lower elevations, features species indicative of secondary or disturbed forests (Ricinus communis, Albizia gummifera), suggesting anthropogenic influence, possibly from nearby agriculture. Similar trends are observed in the Bale Mountains and Chilimo Forest, where disturbance has altered species composition [38, 41]. Community Type 2 the richest in species, likely represents a mid-elevation transition zone with favorable conditions and intact structure consistent with biodiversity peaks observed in mid-elevation forests of [42, 43].

Communities 3 to 5, located at higher elevations, are dominated by montane evergreens like Psydrax parviflora and Polyscias fulva, and feature rare species such as Justicia shimperiana and Maesa gracilipes. These indicate relatively stable ecosystems with high conservation value, echoing patterns in Ethiopia’s moist montane forests [15, 44].

The presence of non-native or disturbance-tolerant species (Ricinus communis, Azadirachta indica) in some communities signals ecological degradation, as also noted in forest fragments of northern Ethiopia and southwestern Uganda [9, 44, 45].

The identification of distinct plant community types in the Modi-Geyi Forest carries several important conservation and management implications. First, stratified conservation is essential. Community Types 2, 3, and 4, which are rich in species and host several rare taxa, should be prioritized for in situ conservation and biodiversity monitoring to safeguard their ecological integrity. Second, the consistent presence of Aningeria adolfi-friederici, Ilex mitis, and Dracaena afromontana across multiple communities suggests that these species can serve as ecological indicators. Their population trends can be used to monitor forest health and structural stability over time. Third, targeted restoration should be implemented in communities showing signs of degradation particularly Community Type 1, which contains species typically associated with disturbed or secondary growth forests. Restoration actions may include reforestation with native species, enrichment planting, and regulation of resource extraction. Fourth, the elevation-linked diversity patterns observed underscore the importance of climate adaptation strategies. Maintaining habitat connectivity across altitudinal gradients is crucial to facilitate species migration and ensure long-term resilience to climate change. Fifth, the classification supports participatory forest management. Spatially informed zoning can help align conservation goals with local livelihoods by identifying zones suitable for biodiversity conservation, sustainable resource use, or ecotourism. This approach has already proven effective in areas like Gedeo and Kakamega, where community-based forest programs have enhanced both ecological and socioeconomic outcomes [46, 47].

Woody species diversity and evenness across plant communities

A Shannon-Wiener index of 2.92 places the forest in the “medium diversity” category, as defined by [22], indicating moderate species richness with some dominance. The high average evenness value (0.93) suggests a balanced distribution of individuals among species, pointing to ecological stability and minimal species dominance (Table 2).

Comparative analysis shows that Modi-Geyi Forest exhibits higher diversity than Kurib Forest H’ = 1.86, E = 0.91 [3], possibly due to lower disturbance or more complex habitat structure. However, diversity levels are slightly lower than those reported in nearby forests by [48, 49], who documented H’ values of 3.07 and 3.5, respectively suggesting local variability in environmental conditions or disturbance regimes.

Alpha diversity, calculated using both Shannon and Simpson indices, varied across community types. Community Type 3 (Aningeria adolfi-friederici –Dracaena afromontana) recorded the highest diversity (H’ = 3.729, Simpson = 32.761) and strong evenness (Shannon E = 0.907, Simpson E = 0.537), reflecting a rich and stable plant community (Table 2). Similar patterns have been observed in mid-elevation moist forests of the Yayu Biosphere Reserve and Sheka Forest, where favorable microclimates and reduced anthropogenic pressures support high biodiversity [50, 51].

In contrast, Community Type 1 had the lowest diversity (H’ = 3.197, Simpson = 18.231) but maintained relatively high evenness (0.879), suggesting fewer species but a more equitable distribution. This pattern may result from environmental filtering or recent disturbances and mirrors findings from secondary forests in Kakamega (Kenya) and Budongo (Uganda) [52, 53].

Beta diversity, measured using the Sorensen index (0.46), indicates moderate species turnover across plots. This level of dissimilarity suggests that while there is overlap in species composition, habitat heterogeneity driven by microclimate, topography, and disturbance plays a key role in shaping community structure. Similar observations have been reported in the Bale Mountains and Harenna Forest [41, 54].

The combination of moderate-to-high diversity and high evenness has important ecological and conservation implications. The relative balance in species abundance suggests limited disturbance and strong ecological integrity, making the forest more resilient to environmental stressors such as climate change, invasive species, and diseases [55–57].

Community types with both high diversity and evenness especially Community Type 3 should be prioritized for conservation due to their role in sustaining ecosystem services like carbon storage, watershed protection, and habitat provision. Conversely, areas like Community Type 1, with lower diversity but stable structure, present opportunities for ecological restoration through enrichment planting and reduced anthropogenic impact.

The moderate beta diversity underscores the need to maintain landscape-scale heterogeneity and protect all identified community types. These findings support ecosystem-based forest management approaches that integrate both within-site (alpha) and between-site (beta) diversity, as advocated by [58].

Similarity between plant community types

Species composition similarity among the five plant community types in the Modi-Geyi Forest was assessed using Sorensen’s Coefficient Index (Table 3). The highest similarity (48%) was recorded between Community Type 3 (Aningeria adolfi-friederici –Dracaena afromontana) and Community Type 5 (Ilex mitis– Aningeria adolfi-friederici), likely due to their overlapping elevation ranges (2,100–2,200 m a.s.l.), similar microhabitat conditions, and shared ecological niches. Community Types 2 and 5 showed a comparable similarity level (47%), while several other pairs such as 1 and 3, 2 and 3, and 3 and 4 shared approximately 46% similarity, suggesting moderate overlap in species composition.

In contrast, the lowest similarity (42%) was observed between Community Types 2 (Aningeria adolfi-friederici –Croton macrostachyus) and 4 (Dracaena afromontana–Psydrax parviflora). This lower overlap may be attributed to differences in altitude, canopy structure, and levels of disturbance, contributing to higher species turnover and reflecting moderate beta diversity across the forest landscape. These findings are consistent with patterns documented in Ethiopia’s Harenna and Wof-Washa Forests, where spatial heterogeneity and environmental gradients significantly influence species distribution [54, 59, 60].

Notably, 26 of the 56 woody species identified were common across all five community types, indicating a core assemblage of widely distributed and ecologically adaptable species. This aligns with observations from other Afromontane forests such as Sheka and Yayu, where generalist species like Croton macrostachyus, Ficus sur, Syzygium guineense, and Aningeria adolfi-friederici dominate due to their broad ecological tolerance [13, 50, 61]. These species often serve foundational roles in forest structure and regeneration, enhancing ecosystem resilience.

The moderate-to-high similarity among most community pairs suggests a degree of floristic homogeneity within the Modi-Geyi Forest, while still supporting sufficient variation to justify distinct community classifications. This balance between shared and unique species points to functional redundancy, which can buffer ecosystem processes against environmental fluctuations and anthropogenic pressures [58].

From a conservation perspective, community types with lower similarity to others particularly Community Type 4 may harbor unique or localized species assemblages, making them priorities for targeted conservation. These areas could be more sensitive to disturbance and thus require site-specific management approaches. Meanwhile, the overlap in species composition across the forest supports a landscape-level conservation strategy, aimed at preserving representative samples of each community to maintain beta diversity and ecological function.

The identification of a common species pool also has practical applications for restoration and adaptive management. Dominant and widely distributed species should be prioritized in restoration projects, enrichment planting, and climate-resilience strategies due to their proven adaptability and ecological significance. Moreover, incorporating similarity indices into biodiversity monitoring and forest zoning can help delineate ecologically meaningful management units and inform conservation priorities.

These findings mirror broader Afromontane trends observed in regions such as Uganda’s Mabira and Budongo Forests and Kenya’s Kakamega Forest, where species overlap declines with increasing environmental variability and human disturbance [62, 63]. Thus, the floristic patterns in the Modi-Geyi Forest not only reflect local ecological processes but also align with regional dynamics in montane forest ecosystems.

Vegetation structure

Density of woody species

Tree density, a key indicator of forest structure and sustainability, was 2,507.5 individuals per hectare in the Modi-Geyi Forest. Trees with DBH < 10 cm accounted for 80.5% (2,017.5 individuals/ha), while those in the 10–20 cm and > 20 cm DBH classes represented 10% (252 individuals/ha) and 9.5% (238 individuals/ha), respectively (Supplementary file Table S2). This distribution indicates dominance by smaller-diameter trees, reflecting active regeneration or past disturbance.

The ratio of trees in the 10–20 cm DBH class to those >20 cm a key indicator of structural progression was 1.06. This relatively low value suggests limited recruitment into mid-sized classes, a pattern less pronounced in other Ethiopian moist Afromontane forests, where higher ratios have been reported in Masha-Andracha (2.4), Menagesha (2.33), and Gura Ferda (1.9) [36, 64–66] (Supplementary file Table S3).

The skew toward smaller diameter classes in Modi-Geyi likely reflects ongoing anthropogenic pressures, including fuelwood collection, timber harvesting, and traditional beekeeping observed during field surveys. Such activities preferentially remove larger trees, disrupting stratification and hindering natural succession. Similar patterns have been documented in heavily used tropical forests in Uganda [67], whereas forests under stronger protection, such as Ankasa in Ghana or Cross River in Nigeria, maintain more balanced DBH distributions [68, 69].

These findings have important conservation and management implications. While the abundance of saplings indicates potential for regeneration, the underrepresentation of mid-sized trees suggests a structural bottleneck that may limit canopy development, species stratification, and long-term carbon storage [70]. Targeted interventions, such as enrichment planting, assisted natural regeneration, and community-based protection, are recommended to enable more individuals to reach maturity. Restoration programs in Ethiopian forests, including Wof-Washa and Debre Libanos, have demonstrated that reduced disturbance can improve DBH distributions and functional diversity within relatively short timeframes [60, 71].

Frequency distribution of woody species

Frequency, defined as the number of plots in which a species appears, provides insight into its spatial distribution and the structural heterogeneity of a vegetation community [72]. In the Modi-Geyi Forest, the majority of woody species were concentrated in lower frequency classes, indicating high floristic heterogeneity (Fig. 3). Such a pattern reflects an uneven species distribution, often shaped by microclimatic variability, soil conditions, and varying levels of human disturbance.

Frequency of woody species distribution of Modi-Geyi Forest, southwest Ethiopia

This finding aligns with similar studies, such as in the Gesha-Saylem Forest [73], where low-frequency distributions were linked to complex topography and land-use pressures. While heterogeneity is ecologically valuable, it can also suggest patchy regeneration in forests experiencing disturbance from activities like logging, grazing, or agricultural expansion.

Among the species observed, Aningeria adolfi-friederici exhibited the highest frequency, likely due to its adaptability and effective seed dispersal mechanisms involving animals, birds, and livestock. Its dominance across multiple plots points to its ecological resilience and ability to thrive under varied conditions. This pattern is consistent with other moist Afromontane forests, including Bonga and Harenna, where Aningeria adolfi-friederici and similar species commonly dominate due to similar traits [54, 74, 75].

In contrast, species such as Phoenix reclinata and Brucea antidysenterica were among the least frequent, reflecting narrower ecological niches, slower regeneration, or higher sensitivity to disturbance. Similar trends were reported by [23] in the Saleda Yohans Church Forest, where high-frequency species like Juniperus procera contrasted sharply with the sparse distribution of rarer taxa.

The prevalence of low-frequency species raises concerns about their vulnerability to local extinction, especially in the absence of targeted conservation efforts. Unsustainable harvesting, habitat alteration, and livestock browsing can restrict regeneration and further reduce the presence of less competitive species [76, 77]. Loss of such species may also impact ecosystem functions like nutrient cycling, carbon sequestration, and pollination.

From a forest management perspective, these frequency patterns underscore the need for both protection and promotion of rare species through measures such as assisted regeneration, enrichment planting, and preservation of seed dispersal zones. At the same time, it is important to monitor dominant species like Aningeria adolfi-friederici to avoid ecological imbalance, where over-dominance might suppress diversity and limit evenness [78].

Hence, the frequency distribution in Modi-Geyi Forest reveals a structurally diverse but ecologically uneven vegetation pattern shaped by natural and anthropogenic influences. These insights highlight the importance of using frequency data to inform site-specific management strategies aimed at conserving biodiversity and enhancing ecosystem resilience.

Diameter at breast height (DBH) distribution of woody species

The DBH distribution of woody species in the Modi-Geyi Forest exhibits a typical inverted J-shaped curve, with 80.5% of individuals concentrated in the smallest size class (0–10 cm). The number of trees declines progressively across the larger DBH classes, with only 3.1% and 1.7% of individuals in the fourth (30.1–40 cm) and fifth (> 40 cm) classes, respectively (Fig. 4). This pattern is indicative of a forest undergoing regeneration but experiencing continuous selective disturbance particularly the removal of larger, mature trees.

DBH class distribution of woody plants per hectare in Modi-Geyi Forest

Such skewed distribution suggests active recruitment of younger individuals but limited survival or retention of older cohorts, likely due to anthropogenic pressures such as harvesting for construction, firewood, fencing, and traditional beekeeping. This trend is consistent with findings from other moist Afromontane forests in Ethiopia, including Wurg [26] and Kaffa [74], where similar resource dependencies have resulted in forests dominated by smaller-diameter trees.

Despite this trend, the presence of some very large individuals Aningeria adolfi-friederici reaching up to 248 cm in DBH indicates the persistence of old-growth remnants. These trees are ecologically significant as seed sources and as habitat for various forest organisms. However, their rarity underscores the need for targeted protection to maintain genetic diversity and ecological functionality. Similar concerns have been reported in Majang [79] and Gedo Forests [80], where unsustainable use has reduced the representation of mature trees.

The forest’s height distribution mirrors the DBH pattern, reinforcing the interpretation of a regenerating but structurally imbalanced forest. Both horizontal and vertical structures are affected by disturbance, limiting canopy stratification and reducing habitat complexity particularly for shade-tolerant and arboreal species. This simplification may compromise vital ecosystem functions such as microclimate regulation, nutrient cycling, and carbon storage.

From a management perspective, the DBH profile signals an urgent need for intervention. Protection of large-diameter trees must be prioritized to ensure long-term ecological balance and seed dispersal. At the same time, promoting the regeneration of mid- and late-successional species through enrichment planting, natural regeneration, and reduced harvesting pressure will help restore structural diversity. Community-based forest management, emphasizing sustainable use and conservation incentives, can provide a viable path forward.

Lessons from international experiences further support this approach. In India’s Western Ghats and Cameroon’s Afromontane forests, initiatives such as logging bans, native species reforestation, and community engagement have successfully restored DBH balance and improved forest resilience [28, 81, 82]. Similar strategies adapted to local socio-ecological contexts could benefit Ethiopian forests.

In conclusion, while the inverted J-shaped DBH distribution in Modi-Geyi Forest indicates a regenerating system, the scarcity of mature individuals reflects ongoing anthropogenic pressure. Ensuring sustainable forest structure will require a combination of protection, restoration, and community involvement, aimed at balancing ecological integrity with local livelihoods.

Height class distribution of woody species

The height class distribution of woody species in the Modi-Geyi Forest reflects a pattern typical of disturbed Afromontane ecosystems, where the majority of individuals are concentrated in the lower height classes. Specifically, 32.7% of trees fall within the 2.5–10 m range (Class 1), followed by a gradual decline in taller classes, with only 8.7% exceeding 41 m (Class 5) (Table 4). This pattern mirrors the forest’s DBH distribution and suggests a structure dominated by young or small-statured individuals, indicative of a regenerating forest under anthropogenic pressure.

In Afromontane rainforests, a well-developed canopy structure typically includes an upper canopy of trees reaching 10–30 m, composed of species such as Albizia gummifera, Croton macrostachyus, and Schefflera abyssinica, and a lower layer of smaller trees like Brucea antidysenterica and Dracaena afromontana [83]. The low presence of tall individuals likely results from selective logging and resource extraction, similar to patterns observed in Gerba Dima Forest [14].

The reduced representation of trees in the upper height classes poses serious ecological concerns. Large trees are critical for maintaining habitat complexity, serving as seed sources, and regulating forest microclimates. Their decline may impair key ecosystem services, diminish biodiversity, and alter successional dynamics. Comparable height class shifts linked to overexploitation have been reported in Kenya’s Kakamega Forest and Tanzania’s Eastern Arc Mountains [52, 84].

While the high proportion of trees in the lowest height class suggests ongoing regeneration, the challenge lies in ensuring that these young individuals can survive and grow into the mature canopy. Without protection and sustainable management, these trees may never reach ecological maturity, especially in moist Afromontane forests, which, despite their biodiversity and carbon storage capacity, are highly vulnerable to degradation. Similar concerns have been documented in the Majang Forest [79], and the Bale Mountains [41], where human pressures have skewed forest structures toward smaller individuals.

From a conservation standpoint, the height distribution underscores the need to protect existing tall trees and to promote structural diversity across vertical layers. Strengthening community-based forest management (CBFM) programs paired with environmental education and sustainable livelihood alternatives can reduce extraction pressure and foster local stewardship. Incorporating height structure monitoring into management plans will also support early detection of degradation and inform adaptive responses.

Thus, the height class structure in Modi-Geyi Forest highlights a regenerating but ecologically imbalanced forest, shaped by both natural recovery and ongoing human impact. Addressing these challenges will require integrated strategies combining conservation, restoration, and community engagement to ensure long-term ecological sustainability and biodiversity conservation in Ethiopia’s Afromontane forests.

Basal area (BA)

Basal area (BA) is a key structural parameter that reflects forest stand density and the ecological significance of woody species. Unlike stem counts, BA incorporates both tree size and abundance, offering a more accurate assessment of species dominance and forest composition [85, 86].

In the Modi-Geyi Forest, the total basal area of mature woody plants was recorded at 119.84 m² per hectare, indicating a structurally rich ecosystem. Among the species assessed, Schefflera abyssinica contributed the highest share (13.56%), followed by Aningeria adolfi-friederici (10.87%), Syzygium guineense (9.20%), Prunus africana (7.20%), and Ekebergia capensis (7.04%) (Table 5). These species are ecologically significant due to their roles in canopy structure, habitat provisioning, and interactions within the forest community. Notably, Prunus africana is an internationally valued medicinal plant, while Aningeria adolfi-friederici is important both ecologically and economically. The BA value in Modi-Geyi is higher than those reported for several other Afromontane forests in Ethiopia such as Kurib 105.7 m² ha⁻¹ [3], Gura Ferda (69.9 m² ha⁻¹ [66], and Majang (63.6 m² ha⁻¹ [79] but lower than that of Masha Forest (142.61 m² ha⁻¹ [87] (Table 6). This suggests a forest with substantial biomass and mature individuals, though still subject to anthropogenic pressures that may limit its full ecological potential.

The dominance of large trees has significant ecological implications. These individuals play key roles in carbon storage, biodiversity maintenance, microclimate regulation, and soil stabilization. However, ongoing human activities including selective logging and harvesting of medicinal and timber species pose risks to the long-term sustainability of these critical forest elements, as also observed in Gerba Dima Forest [14].

To preserve the forest’s structural integrity, conservation efforts should prioritize the protection and monitoring of high-BA species. Community-based forest management (CBFM), sustainable harvesting guidelines, and reforestation using ecologically dominant native species are essential strategies for maintaining forest resilience.

Therefore, the basal area data from Modi-Geyi Forest reflect a relatively intact Afromontane ecosystem with high structural and ecological value. Protecting dominant species and managing human pressures through integrated conservation and sustainable use practices is crucial for ensuring the forest’s long-term viability.

Important Value Index (IVI)

The Important Value Index (IVI) is a widely used metric in vegetation ecology that integrates relative frequency, relative density, and relative dominance to evaluate a species’ structural and ecological importance within a community [22]. By combining these parameters, IVI provides a comprehensive measure of a species’ role and influence in forest ecosystems, making it valuable for setting conservation and management priorities [3, 32, 34, 89–92].

In the Modi-Geyi Afromontane Rainforest, species such as Aningeria adolfi-friederici, Schefflera abyssinica, Syzygium guineense, Ilex mitis, and Dracaena afromontana recorded the highest IVI values, indicating their ecological dominance (Table 7). These species contribute significantly to forest structure, biodiversity maintenance, and key ecological functions like seed dispersal, carbon storage, and habitat formation. Aningeria adolfi-friederici showed the highest IVI (22.83), reflecting its widespread presence, high density, and dominance traits that underscore its role as a keystone species, consistent with findings from other moist Afromontane forests in Ethiopia [92].

Population structure of woody species in the forest

The population structure of woody species, assessed through diameter at breast height (DBH) class distribution, offers critical insights into regeneration status, recruitment dynamics, and the ecological integrity of forest ecosystems [20]. In the Modi-Geyi Forest, several distinct DBH distribution patterns were identified, reflecting varied regeneration trends and disturbance responses among species.

The most prevalent pattern was the inverted J-shape, characterized by a high number of individuals in the lower DBH classes and a gradual decline toward the larger classes. This structure, typical of healthy, regenerating forests, indicates continuous recruitment and a balanced age structure. Species such as Croton macrostachyus, Aningeria adolfi-friederici, and Syzygium guineense followed this pattern, reflecting their resilience and adaptive capacity (Fig. 5). Similar patterns have been documented in other Afromontane forests in Ethiopia, including Endeba-Zend [1], Menagesha Suba [93], Ruruki [3], Woynwuha [94], and Wurg forests [26], underscoring a consistent trend of natural regeneration across these ecosystems.

Population structure of the most dominant tree species of Modi-Geyi Forest, Southwest Ethiopia

In contrast, Schefflera abyssinica exhibited a bell-shaped distribution, with most individuals concentrated in the middle DBH classes and fewer in the younger and older categories. This suggests limited seedling recruitment and potential senescence of mature individuals, possibly due to interspecific competition, environmental stress, or reduced reproductive success.

An irregular DBH distribution was observed in species like Prunus africana and Ekebergia capensis, with no consistent trend across size classes. Such patterns often result from sporadic regeneration, anthropogenic disturbance, or selective exploitation. For example, Prunus africana, a highly sought-after medicinal plant, has experienced overharvesting, which likely contributes to its disrupted population structure [26].

Additionally, species such as Dracaena afromontana and Psydrax parviflora were confined to the smallest DBH classes, indicating that they rarely survive beyond early growth stages. This could be attributed to ecological specialization, shading, browsing, or human-induced pressures. These trends raise concerns about recruitment bottlenecks and long-term viability, as similarly reported in montane forests of Kenya and Uganda, including the Budongo Forest Reserve [42, 62].

Understanding these structural patterns is essential for informed forest management. The dominance of the inverted J-shape among key species suggests favorable conditions for natural regeneration, providing a strong basis for conservation and enrichment strategies. These species should be prioritized for restoration and integrated into reforestation programs to maintain ecosystem resilience.

Conversely, species with bell-shaped or irregular patterns require targeted conservation actions. These may include regulated harvesting, propagation in nurseries, habitat protection, and reduced grazing pressure. For vulnerable species limited to early growth stages, mixed-species plantations and community-based conservation efforts can enhance survival and regeneration.

Thus, the population structure analysis of the Modi-Geyi Forest reveals a mixed regeneration status some species demonstrate robust recovery, while others show signs of ecological stress. These findings highlight the need for adaptive, species-specific management strategies that balance conservation with sustainable use, aligned with Ethiopia’s Climate Resilient Green Economy (CRGE) strategy and broader biodiversity commitments.

Regeneration status of the forest

The composition and density of seedlings and saplings are key indicators of a forest’s regeneration potential and ecological sustainability. In the study forest, 56 woody species were recorded, with average densities of 971.5 seedlings/ha (38.74%), 823.5 saplings/ha (32.84%), and 712.5 mature individuals/ha (28.41%) (Fig. 6). The observed ratios 1.36 seedlings to mature individuals and 1.16 saplings to mature individuals suggest a healthy regeneration trend, with more young individuals than mature trees, indicating ongoing recruitment and regeneration processes.

The regeneration status of woody species in Modi-Geyi Forest, Southwest Ethiopia

This pattern reflects generally favorable conditions for germination and early growth, likely supported by species adaptability and local environmental factors. However, the transition from seedling to mature stages may be hindered by anthropogenic disturbances such as firewood collection, traditional beekeeping, fencing, selective logging, and livestock grazing. These pressures contribute to the reduced presence of individuals in larger diameter classes and may affect long-term forest structure.

At the species level, Aningeria adolfi-friederici, Dracaena afromontana, Ilex mitis, Croton macrostachyus, Syzygium guineense, Ficus sur, and Allophylus abyssinicus showed strong regeneration, evidenced by high densities of seedlings and saplings. These species appear well-adapted to local conditions and may possess traits such as high seed viability, effective dispersal mechanisms, or tolerance to disturbance making them critical to forest structure and suitable candidates for conservation and restoration programs.

In contrast, six species Cyathea manniana, Erythrina abyssinica, Maesa lanceolata, Phoenix reclinata, Rothmannia urcelliformis, and Schefflera abyssinica lacked seedlings and saplings entirely, indicating poor or failed regeneration. This deficit may result from overharvesting, limited reproductive output, low germination rates, or high seedling mortality due to browsing and trampling. Similar regeneration challenges have been reported in other Ethiopian forests, including Tara Gedam, Abbo Sacred Forest, and Saleda Yohannes and Zijje Maryam church forests [23, 35, 95].

These findings underscore the importance of species-specific assessments in understanding forest regeneration. While overall regeneration appears positive, the lack of recruitment in certain species signals potential local extinction risks, reduced biodiversity, and loss of ecosystem functions. These species should be prioritized in conservation actions such as seed collection, nursery propagation, enrichment planting, and protection from grazing.

Comparable studies in Kenya’s Kakamega and Mau Forests and Uganda’s Budongo Forest Reserve highlight similar regeneration disparities driven by ecological traits and human pressures [42, 67], emphasizing the need to address both ecological and socio-economic factors in forest management.

To safeguard regeneration, targeted strategies are essential. These include establishing exclosures, reducing grazing pressure, regulating resource extraction, and enhancing community awareness. Integrating native species with low regeneration into restoration programs will strengthen ecosystem resilience.

Hence, while the forest demonstrates overall regenerative capacity, sustaining this potential requires proactive management. Incorporating regeneration data into forest planning supports Ethiopia’s Climate Resilient Green Economy (CRGE) strategy and contributes to global efforts such as the UN Decade on Ecosystem Restoration (2021–2030).

Ecological context and conservation implications

The floristic composition and structural attributes of Modi-Geyi Forest reflect a complex and dynamic Afromontane ecosystem shaped by both environmental variability and anthropogenic pressures. While the forest supports substantial species richness and diverse life forms, its biodiversity is moderate compared to neighboring forests such as Gera, Belete-Gera, and Godere. This variation is influenced not only by general human disturbance but also by localized ecological factors, including elevation, soil conditions, microclimate, and historical land use, which strongly affect species composition and diversity [11, 89, 90, 92].

Structurally, the forest exhibits an inverted J-shaped distribution of diameter and height classes, typically interpreted as evidence of active regeneration, as observed in Menagesha Suba [93] and Wurg [26]. However, this pattern may mask the impacts of selective logging of large trees, which reduces vertical complexity and disrupts ecological processes such as seed dispersal and nutrient cycling [20]. The relatively moderate basal area (119.84 m²/ha) further indicates reduced above-ground biomass, with implications for carbon storage, habitat quality, and ecosystem productivity, consistent with findings from East African forests such as Mau (Kenya) and Usambara (Tanzania) [96, 97].

Regeneration varies among species. Dominants such as Aningeria adolfi-friederici, Syzygium guineense, and Dracaena afromontana show robust recruitment, while Phoenix reclinata, Schefflera abyssinica, and Cyathea manniana exhibit regeneration failure. Such disparities are consistent with observations in Tara Gedam and Saleda Yohannes Church Forests [35, 95], where recruitment is limited by ecological constraints and human activity.

Observed regeneration bottlenecks in Modi-Geyi are primarily linked to livestock grazing, seedling extraction, and proximity to human settlements, which increase edge pressure [23, 31]. Fire and invasive species, notably Lantana camara, further compromise native regeneration. Fires favor disturbance-tolerant species and alter successional trajectories, as seen in the Bale Mountains [41], while invasives suppress native seedlings and disrupt forest structure, as reported in Western Kenya and Northern Tanzania [42, 44].

These ecological pressures are closely linked to socio-economic factors, including population growth, land scarcity, poverty, and reliance on forest resources. Similar challenges in Sheka Forest illustrate how overlooking human dimensions can undermine conservation outcomes [61]. Targeted actions for poorly regenerating species such as enrichment planting, nursery propagation, and seed banking are recommended. Integrating local communities into management through alternative livelihoods, energy-efficient technologies, and environmental education can reduce resource dependence. Successful participatory management in Gera and Chilimo highlights the effectiveness of aligning ecological conservation with community well-being [98, 99].

Conclusion

Modi-Geyi Forest is a moderately diverse and structurally dynamic Afromontane ecosystem, dominated by species in smaller diameter and height classes, indicating ongoing regeneration. Key species, including Aningeria adolfi-friederici, Schefflera abyssinica, and Syzygium guineense, as reflected by high Importance Value Index (IVI) scores, play a central role in maintaining forest structure and ecological function. However, the underrepresentation of mature individuals and lack of regeneration in certain species, such as Cyathea manniana and Phoenix reclinata, raise emerging conservation concerns. These patterns are linked to human-induced pressures, including agricultural expansion, firewood collection, livestock grazing, and traditional beekeeping, which collectively undermine forest resilience and biodiversity.

To ensure the long-term sustainability of Modi-Geyi Forest, integrated conservation strategies are urgently needed. These should include targeted restoration of poorly regenerating species, enrichment planting, and ex situ conservation measures. Community-based forest management (CBFM) offers a promising approach to strengthen local engagement, reduce illegal activities, and promote sustainable resource use. Aligning ecological goals with community needs through participatory and adaptive management will be critical for preserving biodiversity, enhancing ecosystem services, and securing the forest’s role in regional conservation efforts.

Limitation of the study

This study did not include soil seed bank assessments or soil mineral analyses due to limited resources, insufficient funding, and the lack of a well-equipped laboratory.
