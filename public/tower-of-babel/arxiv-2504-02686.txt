Degrees and prime power order zeros of characters of symmetric and alternating groups Thanks: This is the accepted manuscript for an article published in the Bulletin of the London Mathematical Society, available online at https://doi.org/10.1112/blms.70160 .
Eugenio Giannelli, Stacey Law, Eoghan McDowell

Abstract
. We show that the p p -part of the degree of an irreducible character of a symmetric group is completely determined by the set of vanishing elements of p p -power order. As a corollary we deduce that the set of zeros of prime power order controls the degree of such a character. The same problem is analysed for alternating groups, where we show that when p = 2 p=2 this data can only be determined up to two possibilities. We prove analogous statements for the defect of the p p -block containing the character and for the p p -height of the character.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
20C15, 20C30

1.
Introduction

Zeros of characters of finite groups have long interested mathematicians; a detailed account of the subject can be found in
[
DPS18
]
.
A recent striking result due to Miller
[
Mil14
]
is that, given a random irreducible character and a random element of a symmetric group
S_{n}
, the probability that the character value equals zero is asymptotically equal to
1
.
This has generated significant interest in the subject, see for example
[
GLM22
,
Mor22
,
MS25
,
PS25
]
.

Our focus in this article will be to examine how the zeros of
prime power order
determine properties of irreducible characters for symmetric and alternating groups.
Here, a
zero
of a character
\xi
of a group
G
refers to an element
g\in G
such that
\xi(g)=0
.
We denote by
\operatorname{Van}(\xi)
the set of all zeros of
\xi
in
G
(the
vanishing set
of
\xi
).

The existence of a prime power order zero for every non-linear irreducible character of a finite group was established in
[
MNO00
]
, and some implications for the structure of Sylow subgroups were studied in
[
DPSS10
,
Mad22
]
.
Meanwhile, for any prime
p
and finite group
G
with Sylow
p
-subgroup
P
, we know that the vanishing set
\operatorname{Van}(\chi\big\downarrow_{P})
of an irreducible character
\chi
restricted to
P
is closely related to
\chi(1)_{p}
, the
p
-part of the degree of
\chi
(i.e. the largest power of
p
dividing
\chi(1)
).
The extreme cases are well understood: on the one hand, a theorem of Brauer asserts that
\chi(1)_{p}=|P|
if and only if
\operatorname{Van}(\chi\big\downarrow_{P})=P\setminus\{1\}
(see
[
Isa76
, Theorem 8.17]
);
on the other hand, it is well known that if
\chi(1)_{p}=1
then
\operatorname{Van}(\chi\big\downarrow_{P})=\varnothing
.
The converse to the last statement does not hold in general, but it was proved to be true in the case of symmetric and alternating groups
[
GLLV22
, Theorem 3.16]
.
Another characterisation of when
\chi(1)_{p}=1
in terms of zeros was given by Morotti
[
Mor15
]
.
Our aim in this paper is to bridge these results by identifying the precise power of
p
dividing the character degree for symmetric groups and (as much as possible) for alternating groups.

The full vanishing set of an irreducible character of the symmetric group was shown to determine the character up to multiplication by linear characters by Belonogov
[
Bel04
]
(that is,
\operatorname{Van}(\chi)
determines
\chi
up to multiplication by the sign character).
In particular, one has that the full vanishing set
\operatorname{Van}(\chi)
determines the degree
\chi(1)
.
G. Navarro conjectured that, for the symmetric group,
\chi(1)
should be fully controlled just by the set of prime power order zeros of
\chi
.
We confirm this statement in
Corollary

S3
below.

The vanishing behaviour for characters of the alternating group is known to be more complicated: classifying which irreducible characters of the alternating group have the same zeros is an open problem, with a conjecture due to C. Bessenrodt reported by Bowman
[
Bow22
]
.
Nevertheless, we are able to show that the degree is determined by the prime power order zeros up to two possibilities, providing examples to illustrate each possible behaviour. Furthermore, we demonstrate that this cannot be improved.

1.1.
Results for symmetric groups
S_{n}

Theorem S1
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
p
be a prime.
Let
P\in\operatorname{Syl}_{p}(S_{n})
and let
\chi\in\operatorname{Irr}(S_{n})
.
The following numbers can be explicitly determined from the vanishing set
\operatorname{Van}(\chi\big\downarrow_{P})
:

(1)

the
p
-part of the degree of
\chi
;

(2)

the defect of the
p
-block containing
\chi
;

(3)

the
p
-height of
\chi
in its
p
-block.

We prove
Theorem

S1
in
Section

3
by expressing the desired numbers in terms of the
prime power weights
of the labelling partition of the character, which in turn are explicitly determined from the vanishing set in
Theorem

3.2
.
Our explicit determination has the following consequence.

Theorem S2
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
p
be a prime.
Let
P\in\operatorname{Syl}_{p}(S_{n})
and let
\chi,\psi\in\operatorname{Irr}(S_{n})
.

\text{If}\ \operatorname{Van}(\chi\big\downarrow_{P})\subseteq\operatorname{Van}(\psi\big\downarrow_{P}),\ \text{then}\ \chi(1)_{p}\leq\psi(1)_{p}.

In particular, if
\operatorname{Van}(\chi\big\downarrow_{P})=\operatorname{Van}(\psi\big\downarrow_{P})
, then
\chi(1)_{p}=\psi(1)_{p}
.

We remark that the converse of
Theorem

S2
does not hold; counterexamples for every prime are provided in
Examples

5.2
and
5.3
.
Nor does
Theorem

S2
hold if the containments and inequalities are made strict; a counterexample for
p=2
is given by the irreducible characters of
S_{10}
labelled by the partitions
(6,4)
and
(6,2,1,1)
.

By considering
Theorem

S2
for all primes
p
, we deduce the following corollary, that the set of zeros of prime power order of an irreducible character
\chi
of
S_{n}
determines the degree of
\chi
, confirming Navarro’s prediction.
For a character
\chi
, let
\operatorname{Van}_{\mathrm{pow}}(\chi):=\{g\in\operatorname{Van}(\chi)\mid g\text{ has prime power order}\}
.

Corollary S3
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\chi,\psi\in\operatorname{Irr}(S_{n})
.

\text{If}\ \operatorname{Van}_{\mathrm{pow}}(\chi)\subseteq\operatorname{Van}_{\mathrm{pow}}(\psi),\ \text{then}\ \chi(1)\leq\psi(1).

In particular, if
\operatorname{Van}_{\mathrm{pow}}(\chi)=\operatorname{Van}_{\mathrm{pow}}(\psi)
, then
\chi(1)=\psi(1)
.

Theorem

S1
can be strengthened by replacing the Sylow subgroup
P
by any subgroup containing a defect group of the block in which
\chi
lies.
This is explained in
Section

1.3
.
As a consequence of the strengthened theorem, we deduce the following blockwise version of
Theorem

S2
.
Given a
p
-block
B
of
S_{n}
, we denote by
\mathrm{Irr}(B)
the set of irreducible characters lying in
B
, and by
h_{p}(\chi)
the
p
-height of an irreducible character
\chi
in its
p
-block
(see
Section

2.4
for definitions).

Corollary S4
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
p
be any prime. Let
B
be a
p
-block of
S_{n}
with defect group
D
and let
\chi,\psi\in\operatorname{Irr}(B)
.

\text{If}\ \operatorname{Van}(\chi\big\downarrow_{D})\subseteq\operatorname{Van}(\psi\big\downarrow_{D}),\ \text{then}\ h_{p}(\chi)\leq h_{p}(\psi).

In particular, if
\operatorname{Van}(\chi\big\downarrow_{D})=\operatorname{Van}(\psi\big\downarrow_{D})
, then
h_{p}(\chi)=h_{p}(\psi)
.

1.2.
Results for alternating groups
A_{n}

We consider the same question for the alternating group.
When the prime
p
is odd, the answer is essentially the same as, and follows very quickly from, that for the symmetric group.
However, when
p=2
, the data of the character can in general only be determined up to two possibilities, and the proof is significantly more technical.

Theorem A1
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
p
be a prime.
Let
Q\in\operatorname{Syl}_{p}({A_{n}})
and let
\chi\in\operatorname{Irr}(A_{n})
.
The following numbers can be explicitly determined from the vanishing set
\operatorname{Van}(\chi\big\downarrow_{Q})
, except when
p=2
when they can be determined up to two possibilities:

(1)

the
p
-part of the degree of
\chi
;

(2)

the defect of the
p
-block containing
\chi
;

(3)

the
p
-height of
\chi
in its
p
-block.

The explicit method of determination is detailed in
Corollary

4.9
.
We remark that, when
p=2
, there are many cases when the numbers above can in fact be uniquely determined (for example, the defect can be uniquely determined whenever
n\not\equiv 3\pmod{4}
).
Nevertheless, in general, the statement cannot be improved: there exists pairs of irreducible characters with equal vanishing sets on
Q
but with differing
2
-heights, defects and/or
2
-parts of degrees.
Several examples of this are presented in
Section

5
.

The explicit determination in
Corollary

4.9
has the following consequence.

Theorem A2
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
p
be a prime.
Let
Q\in\mathrm{Syl}_{p}(A_{n})
and let
\eta,\theta\in\operatorname{Irr}(A_{n})
.

\text{If}\ \operatorname{Van}(\eta\big\downarrow_{Q})\subseteq\operatorname{Van}(\theta\big\downarrow_{Q}),\ \text{then}\ \begin{cases}\eta(1)_{p}\leq\theta(1)_{p}&\text{ if $p$ is odd;}\\
\eta(1)_{2}\leq 2\cdot\theta(1)_{2}&\text{ if $p=2$.}\end{cases}

In particular,
suppose
\operatorname{Van}(\eta\big\downarrow_{Q})=\operatorname{Van}(\theta\big\downarrow_{Q})
; if
p
is odd, then
\eta(1)_{p}=\theta(1)_{p}
,
while if
p=2
and without loss of generality
\eta(1)_{2}\geq\theta(1)_{2}
, then
\eta(1)_{2}\in\{\,\theta(1)_{2},\,2\cdot\theta(1)_{2}\,\}
.

As with the symmetric group, we obtain corollaries by by considering all primes simultaneously and by weakening the hypothesis to only require knowledge of the vanishing on a defect group.

Corollary A3
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\eta,\theta\in\operatorname{Irr}(A_{n})
.

\text{If}\ \operatorname{Van}_{\mathrm{pow}}(\eta)\subseteq\operatorname{Van}_{\mathrm{pow}}(\theta),\ \text{then}\ \eta(1)\leq 2\cdot\theta(1).

Moreover, if
\operatorname{Van}_{\mathrm{pow}}(\eta)=\operatorname{Van}_{\mathrm{pow}}(\theta)
and without loss of generality
\eta(1)\geq\theta(1)
, then
\eta(1)\in\{\,\theta(1),\,2\cdot\theta(1)\,\}
.

Corollary A4
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
p
be a prime. Let
B
be a
p
-block of
A_{n}
, let
D
be a defect group of
B

and let
\eta,\theta\in\operatorname{Irr}(B)
.

\text{If}\ \operatorname{Van}(\eta\big\downarrow_{D})\subseteq\operatorname{Van}(\theta\big\downarrow_{D}),\ \text{then}\ \begin{cases}h_{p}(\eta)\leq h_{p}(\theta)&\text{ if $p$ is odd;}\\
h_{2}(\eta)\leq h_{2}(\theta)+1&\text{ if $p=2$.}\end{cases}

In particular,
suppose
\operatorname{Van}(\eta\big\downarrow_{D})=\operatorname{Van}(\theta\big\downarrow_{D})
; if
p
is odd, then
h_{p}(\eta)=h_{p}(\theta)
,
while if
p=2
and without loss of generality
h_{p}(\eta)\geq h_{p}(\theta)
, then
h_{2}(\eta)\in\{\,h_{2}(\theta),\,h_{2}(\theta)+1\,\}
.

1.3.
Reduction to defect groups

The following proposition tells us that the interesting vanishing behaviour of an irreducible character occurs on a defect group for its block.
(The proofs for
S_{n}
and
A_{n}
can be found easily using the description of their defect groups
in
Section

2.4
.)

Proposition 1.1

(
[
Nav98
, Corollary 5.9]
)
.

Let
G
be a finite group, let
B
be a block of
G
and let
\chi\in\operatorname{Irr}(B)
.
Let
g\in G
.
If the
p
-part of
g
does not lie in a defect group of
B
, then
\chi(g)=0
.

This justifies our claim that we can strengthen our main theorems to only requiring knowledge of the vanishing set on a subgroup containing the defect group, and hence deduce
S4
and
A4
.
We nevertheless state our other main results using the full Sylow subgroup to avoid assuming that we
a priori
know the blocks or their defect groups.

We can in fact weaken the hypotheses of
Corollary

S4
further to permit
\chi
and
\psi
to lie in different blocks (and similarly for
Corollary

A4
).
More precisely, suppose
\chi
lies in a block
B
of
S_{n}
with defect group
D
and suppose
\psi
lies in a block
B^{\prime}
of
S_{n}
with defect group
D^{\prime}
, and suppose that
|D|\geq|D^{\prime}|
, so that, in particular,
D^{\prime}
is
S_{n}
-conjugate to a subgroup of
D
(see
Section

2.4
).
If
\operatorname{Van}(\chi\big\downarrow_{D})\subseteq\operatorname{Van}(\psi\big\downarrow_{D})
then we can deduce
\chi(1)_{p}\leq\psi(1)_{p}
, while
if
\operatorname{Van}(\chi\big\downarrow_{D})=\operatorname{Van}(\psi\big\downarrow_{D})
then we can deduce that
|D|=|D^{\prime}|
,
\chi(1)_{p}=\psi(1)_{p}
and
h_{p}(\chi)=h_{p}(\psi)
.

1.4.
What the prime power order zeros do
not
know

At the beginning of the introduction, we noted that given
\chi,\psi\in\operatorname{Irr}(S_{n})
, Belonogov’s work
[
Bel04
]
demonstrates that if
\operatorname{Van}(\chi)=\operatorname{Van}(\psi)
, then necessarily
\chi=\psi\cdot\zeta
for some linear character
\zeta
of
S_{n}
.
In contrast,

Theorem

S2
establishes that if
\operatorname{Van}_{\mathrm{pow}}(\chi)=\operatorname{Van}_{\mathrm{pow}}(\psi)
, then
\chi(1)=\psi(1)
.
This raises the natural question of whether
\operatorname{Van}_{\mathrm{pow}}(\chi)
suffices to uniquely determine the irreducible character
\chi
, up to multiplication by a linear character. The answer to this question is negative.
As a counterexample, consider the irreducible characters
\chi^{\lambda},\chi^{\mu}\in\mathrm{Irr}(S_{14})
corresponding to the partitions
\lambda=(6,3,3,2)
and
\mu=(5,5,2,1,1)
. Direct computations reveal that
\operatorname{Van}_{\mathrm{pow}}(\chi^{\lambda})=\operatorname{Van}_{\mathrm{pow}}(\chi^{\mu})
, yet
\chi^{\lambda}
is not equal to
\chi^{\mu}
multiplied by any linear character of
S_{14}
.
This underscores the distinction between
Theorem

S2
and Belonogov’s result.

For the alternating group, we already noted that the vanishing set on a Sylow
2
-subgroup is insufficient to determine the
2
-part of the degree.
This ambiguity cannot be resolved even if we consider all prime power order elements.
Consider
\lambda=(10,4,3)
and
\mu=(7,2,2,2,2,2)
,
and let
\eta=\chi^{\lambda}\big\downarrow_{A_{17}}
and
\theta=\chi^{\mu}\big\downarrow_{A_{17}}
denote the corresponding irreducible characters of
A_{17}
.
Direct computations show that
\operatorname{Van}_{\mathrm{pow}}(\eta)=\operatorname{Van}_{\mathrm{pow}}(\theta)
and that
\theta(1)=2\cdot\eta(1)\neq\eta(1)
.
This demonstrates that, for alternating groups, the set of prime power order zeros of an irreducible character does not uniquely determine its degree.

2.
Background on combinatorics and characters

Throughout, we let
\mathbb{N}
denote the set of natural numbers and
\mathbb{N}_{0}
the non-negative integers.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
p
a prime, we denote by
n_{p}
the maximal power of
p
dividing
n
, and by
\nu_{p}(n)
the
p
-adic valuation of
n
(so that
p^{\nu_{p}(n)}=n_{p}
).

We recall some basic properties of the irreducible characters of
S_{n}
and
A_{n}
, and refer the reader to
[
JK81
]
and
[
Ols93
]
for more detailed discussions of the topic.

2.1.
Partitions and characters

A
partition
of
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
is a sequence of weakly decreasing positive integers whose sum is
n
.
When writing partitions, we frequently collect parts of equal size, letting
e^{w}
denote
w
parts of size
e
.
(It should always be clear from context, e.g. by specifying the size of the partition, whether
e^{w}
refers to a single part of size
e^{w}
or
w
parts of size
e
.)
For each
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we let
\mathcal{P}(n)
denote the set of partitions of
n
.
We write
|\lambda|=n
to mean
\lambda\in\mathcal{P}(n)
.
The
Young diagram

Y(\lambda)
of a partition
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{z})
is defined as

Y(\lambda)=\{(i,j)\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}\mid 1\leq i\leq z,\ 1\leq j\leq\lambda_{i}\}.

Each element
(i,j)\in Y(\lambda)
is called a
node
of
Y(\lambda)
.
We denote by
\lambda^{\prime}
the conjugate partition of
\lambda
which is the partition satisfying
Y(\lambda^{\prime})=\{(j,i)\mid(i,j)\in Y(\lambda)\}
.

The irreducible characters of
S_{n}
are naturally parametrised by the elements of
\mathcal{P}(n)
.
Given a partition
\lambda
of
n
we denote by
\chi^{\lambda}
its corresponding irreducible character.

The irreducible characters of
A_{n}
can be easily described in relation to those of
S_{n}
.
For any
\lambda\in\mathcal{P}(n)
such that
\lambda\neq\lambda^{\prime}
, we have that
\chi^{\lambda}\big\downarrow_{A_{n}}=\chi^{\lambda^{\prime}}\big\downarrow_{A_{n}}\in\operatorname{Irr}(A_{n})
. On the other hand, if
\lambda=\lambda^{\prime}
then
\chi^{\lambda}\big\downarrow_{A_{n}}=\varphi^{\lambda+}+\varphi^{\lambda-}
with
\varphi^{\lambda+}\neq\varphi^{\lambda-}\in\operatorname{Irr}(A_{n})
. All of the irreducible characters of
A_{n}
are of one of these two forms. In other words,

\operatorname{Irr}(A_{n})=\{\chi^{\lambda}\big\downarrow_{A_{n}}\mid\lambda\neq\lambda^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(n)\}\sqcup\{\varphi^{\lambda+},\varphi^{\lambda-}\mid\lambda=\lambda^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(n)\}.

Given
\varphi\in\operatorname{Irr}(A_{n})
and
\chi\in\operatorname{Irr}(S_{n})
, we say that
\chi

covers

\varphi
if
\varphi
is a constituent of
\chi\big\downarrow_{A_{n}}
.

2.2.
Hooks

The
hook
of
\lambda
associated to the node
(i,j)
is the set of nodes

H_{i,j}(\lambda)=\{\,(i,j)\,\}\sqcup\{\,(i,y)\mid j+1\leq y\leq\lambda_{i}\,\}\sqcup\{\,(x,j)\mid i+1\leq x\leq\lambda^{\prime}_{j}\,\},

and we let
h_{i,j}(\lambda)=|H_{i,j}(\lambda)|
be the
length
of the hook
H_{i,j}(\lambda)
.
Following standard notation, we use the symbol
\mathcal{H}(\lambda)
to denote the multiset of hook lengths of
\lambda
.
We remark that in this article, the term ‘
e
-hook’ will always mean a hook of length equal to
e
.

The hook length formula (stated below; see also
[
JK81
, Theorem 2.3.21]
) expresses the degree of an irreducible character in terms of hook lengths.

Theorem 2.1

(Hook length formula)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\lambda
be a partition of
n
.
Then

\chi^{\lambda}(1)=\frac{n!}{\prod_{h\in\mathcal{H}(\lambda)}h}.

2.3.
Cores, quotients and weights

A hook can be
removed
from a partition by deleting the nodes of the hook from the Young diagram and sliding the remaining nodes north-west so that they form a new Young diagram.
This is illustrated below with the removal of the
8
-hook
H_{2,3}(\lambda)
(shaded in the first diagram) from the partition
\lambda=(9,8,6,5,1)
to form the partition
\lambda\setminus H_{2,3}(\lambda)=(9,5,4,2,1)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
e\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
and let
\lambda\in\mathcal{P}(n)
.
The
e
-core
of a partition
\lambda
, which we denote by
C_{e}(\lambda)
, is the partition obtained by iteratively removing all
e
-hooks from
\lambda
.
(This turns out to be independent of the order in which
e
-hooks are removed; see
[
JK81
, Theorem 2.7.16]
, for instance.)
The
e
-weight of
\lambda
, which we denote by
\mathbf{w}_{e}(\lambda)
, is the number of hooks removed from
\lambda
to form
C_{e}(\lambda)
; equivalently,
\mathbf{w}_{e}(\lambda)
is the number of hooks in
\lambda
of length divisible by
e
.
Note
n=|C_{e}(\lambda)|+e\mathbf{w}_{e}(\lambda)
.

The
e
-quotient
of
\lambda
, which we denote by
Q_{e}(\lambda)=(\lambda^{(0)},\lambda^{(1)},\dotsc,\lambda^{(e-1)})
, is an
e
-tuple of partitions that encodes the structure of the
e
-hooks of
\lambda
: see
[
JK81
, Chapter 2]
or
[
Ols93
, Section 3]
for details on how to define the
e
-quotient via James’s abacus (we record that, as in
[
Ols93
, Section 3]
, when calculating
e
-quotients we adopt the convention of using abaci which have a multiple of
e
many beads).
For our purposes, it is sufficient to note the following key property.

Proposition 2.2

(
[
Ols93
, Theorem 3.3]
)
.

Let
e\in\mathbb{N}
and
\lambda
be a partition. There is a canonical bijection
f
from the multiset of hooks of
\lambda
of length divisible by
e
, to the multiset of hooks in
Q_{e}(\lambda)
(meaning the union of the multisets of hooks in
\lambda^{(0)},\dotsc,\lambda^{(e-1)}
). Moreover,
|H|=e\cdot|f(H)|
for each such hook
H
of
\lambda
, and
Q_{e}(\lambda\setminus H)=Q_{e}(\lambda)\setminus f(H)
.

In particular, the
e
-quotient has size
|Q_{e}(\lambda)|=|\lambda^{(0)}|+|\lambda^{(1)}|+\dotsc+|\lambda^{(e-1)}|
equal to the number of hooks of
\lambda
of length divisible by
e
; that is,
\mathbf{w}_{e}(\lambda)=|Q_{e}(\lambda)|
.

For taking cores and quotients iteratively, we use the following notation.
For
\underline{\smash{\lambda}}
an
e
-tuple of partitions, we write
C_{r}(\underline{\smash{\lambda}})
for the
e
-tuple obtained by replacing every partition with its
r
-core, and we write
Q_{r}(\underline{\smash{\lambda}})
for the
er
-tuple obtained by replacing every partition with its
r
-quotient and concatenating.
The following facts can be deduced from
Proposition

2.2
, or can be seen readily from their construction on James’s abacus (indeed, part (ii) can be strengthened to the observation that
Q_{r}(Q_{e}(\lambda))
is a permutation of
Q_{er}(\lambda)
).

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\lambda\in\mathcal{P}(n)
and
e,r\geq 2
.
Then

(1)

C_{r}(Q_{e}(\lambda))=Q_{e}(C_{er}(\lambda))
;

(2)

\lvert Q_{r}(Q_{e}(\lambda))\rvert=\lvert Q_{er}(\lambda)\rvert
.

2.4.
Blocks and defects

Let us now fix a prime number
p
and a finite group
G
. Let
B
be a
p
-block of
G
. The
defect
of
B
is the non-negative integer
d
such that
|D|=p^{d}
, where
D
is a defect group of
B
. If
B
has defect
d
and
\chi\in\operatorname{Irr}(B)
, then the
p
-height
of
\chi
is the non-negative integer
h_{p}(\chi)
such that

\nu_{p}(\chi(1))=a-d+h_{p}(\chi)

where
p^{a}
is the order of a Sylow
p
-subgroup of
G
.

It is well known that
\chi^{\lambda}
and
\chi^{\mu}
lie in the same
p
-block of
S_{n}
if and only if
C_{p}(\lambda)=C_{p}(\mu)
(see
[
JK81
, 6.1.21]
, for instance). In other words, the
p
-blocks of
S_{n}
are naturally parametrised by
p
-core partitions.
The defect groups of the
p
-block containing
\chi^{\lambda}
are the Sylow
p
-subgroups of
S_{p\mathbf{w}_{p}(\lambda)}
, viewed as subgroups of
S_{n}
in the natural way (by permuting
p\mathbf{w}_{p}(\lambda)
of the
n
points on which
S_{n}
acts) (see
[
JK81
, 6.2.45]
).
Thus the defect of the block containing
\chi^{\lambda}
is
\nu_{p}((p\mathbf{w}_{p}(\lambda))!)
.

The block decomposition for the alternating group is discussed in
[
Ols90
]
.
If
B
is a
p
-block of
S_{n}
of positive defect, then
B
covers a unique block
b
of
A_{n}
.
Moreover, the defect groups of
b
are all the
A_{n}
-conjugates of
D\cap A_{n}
, where
D
is a defect group of
B
.
In particular, the defect groups of
b
and
B
are isomorphic unless
p=2
and
B
has positive defect, in which case
|D:D\cap A_{n}|=2
.

2.5.
The Murnaghan–Nakayama rule

The
Murnaghan–Nakayama rule
(stated below; see also
[
JK81
, 2.4.7]
) describes how to recursively compute any character value in terms of hook lengths.
The
leg length

\ell\ell(H_{i,j}(\lambda))
of a hook
H_{i,j}(\lambda)
is equal to one less than the number of rows of
Y(\lambda)
it occupies; in other words,
\ell\ell(H_{i,j}(\lambda))=\lambda^{\prime}_{j}-i
.

Theorem 2.4

(Murnaghan–Nakayama rule)
.

Let
e,n\in\mathbb{N}
with
e<n
, and let
\lambda\in\mathcal{P}(n)
.
Let
\rho\in S_{n}
be an
e
-cycle and let
\pi\in S_{n}
be a permutation of the remaining
n-e
numbers. Then

\chi^{\lambda}(\pi\rho)=\sum_{\mu}(-1)^{\ell\ell(\lambda\setminus\mu)}\chi^{\mu}(\pi)

where the sum runs over all partitions
\mu
obtained from
\lambda
by removing an
e
-hook.
In particular, if
\lambda
has no
e
-hooks, then
\chi^{\lambda}(\pi\rho)=0
.

The following result from
[
Ols93
, Proposition 3.13]
is fundamental in understanding the parities of leg lengths in hook removal processes on partitions.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
e\in\mathbb{N}
. Suppose
\lambda
and
\mu
are two partitions such that
\mu
is obtained from
\lambda
by removing a sequence of
r
many
e
-hooks for some
r\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, having leg lengths
b_{1},b_{2},\dotsc,b_{r}
respectively. Let
b=\sum_{i}b_{i}
. Then the residue of
b
(mod 2) does not depend on the choice of
e
-hooks being removed in going from
\lambda
to
\mu
.

We introduce some notation to discuss different ways of removing successive hooks.

Notation 2.6
.

Let
e\in\mathbb{N}
and
\lambda
and
\mu
be two partitions such that
\mu
may be obtained from
\lambda
by successively removing some number of
e
-hooks.
We use the notation

P:\lambda\xrightarrow{e}\mu

to mean that
P
is a specific sequence of hook removals, which we call a
path
, starting at
\lambda
and arriving at
\mu
where each successive hook removed has length
e
.
Equivalently,
P
is a sequence of partitions
\big(\alpha(0),\alpha(1),\dotsc,\alpha(v)\big)
for some
v\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
satisfying the following:
\alpha(0)=\lambda
,
\alpha(v)=\mu
, and
\alpha(i+1)
is obtained by removing an
e
-hook
H_{i}
from
\alpha(i)
for each
i\in\{0,1,\dotsc,v-1\}
(that is,
\alpha(i+1)=\alpha(i)\setminus H_{i}
).

For such a path
P
, we define the
sign
of
P
, denoted
\operatorname{sgn}(P)
, to be

\operatorname{sgn}(P):=(-1)^{\sum_{i=0}^{v-1}\ell\ell(H_{i})}.

We observe by
Lemma

2.5
that the value of
\operatorname{sgn}(P)
in fact depends only on
\lambda
,
\mu
and
e
, and not on the path
P
itself.
We thus define
\operatorname{sgn}(\lambda\setminus\mu)
to be the sign of any path
P\colon\lambda\xrightarrow{e}\mu
(where the parameter
e
will be understood from context).
We can therefore reformulate the Murnaghan–Nakayama rule in terms of paths as follows.

Theorem 2.7

(Murnaghan–Nakayama rule reformulated)
.

Let
e,r,n\in\mathbb{N}
with
er<n
, and let
\lambda\in\mathcal{P}(n)
.
Let
\rho\in S_{n}
be a product of
r
-many disjoint
e
-cycles and let
\pi\in S_{n}
be a permutation of the remaining
n-er
numbers. Then

\chi^{\lambda}(\pi\rho)=\sum_{\mu}\operatorname{sgn}(\lambda\setminus\mu)\,|\{P\mid P\colon\lambda\xrightarrow{e}\mu\}|\,\chi^{\mu}(\pi)

where the sum runs over all partitions
\mu
obtained from
\lambda
by removing a sequence of
r
-many
e
-hooks.
In particular, if
\lambda
has no
e
-hooks, then
\chi^{\lambda}(\pi\rho)=0
.

3.
Main results for the symmetric group

The goal of this section is to prove
Theorem

S1
by showing that the vanishing set on a Sylow subgroup determines the prime power weights of the labelling partition.
We begin by showing how to use the Murnaghan–Nakayama rule to identify non-zero character values.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
e,r,n\in\mathbb{N}
with
er<n
, and let
\lambda\in\mathcal{P}(n)
.
Let
\rho\in S_{n}
be a product of
r
-many disjoint
e
-cycles and let
\pi\in S_{n}
be a permutation of the remaining
n-er
numbers.
Suppose there is a unique partition
\mu
that can be obtained from
\lambda
by removing
r
many
e
-hooks.
Then
\chi^{\lambda}(\pi\rho)
is a non-zero multiple of
\chi^{\mu}(\pi)
.
In particular,
\chi^{\lambda}(\rho)\neq 0
.

Proof.

This is clear from our reformulation of the Murnaghan–Nakayama rule (
Theorem

2.7
).
∎

This suffices to determine the prime power weights from the vanishing set.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\lambda\in\mathcal{P}(n)
and
e\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
. The
e
-weight
\mathbf{w}_{e}(\lambda)
of
\lambda
is the maximal
w\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that
\chi^{\lambda}
is non-zero on an element of cycle type
(e^{w},1^{n-ew})
.

Proof.

The
e
-core of
\lambda
is the unique partition that can be obtained from
\lambda
by removing
\mathbf{w}_{e}(\lambda)
many
e
-hooks, so
\chi^{\lambda}
is non-zero on elements of cycle type
(e^{\mathbf{w}_{e}(\lambda)},1^{n-e\mathbf{w}_{e}(\lambda)})
by
Lemma

3.1
.
Meanwhile, for any
r>\mathbf{w}_{e}(\lambda)
, there is no partition that can be obtained from
\lambda
by removing
r
many
e
-hooks, so
\chi^{\lambda}
vanishes on elements of cycle type
(e^{r},1^{n-er})
by
Theorem

2.4
.
∎

It remains now only to express our three desired pieces of information – the
p
-part of the degree; the defect of the block; and the height – in terms of the prime power weights.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
p
be a prime, and let
\lambda\in\mathcal{P}(n)
.
Suppose
n=\sum_{r\geq 0}b_{r}p^{r}
is the
p
-adic expansion of
n
(i.e. so that
b_{r}\in\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\}
for all
r\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
).
Then

\nu_{p}(\chi^{\lambda}(1))=\frac{n-\sum_{r\geq 0}b_{r}}{p-1}-\sum_{i\geq 1}\mathbf{w}_{p^{i}}(\lambda).

Proof.

By the hook length formula (
Theorem

2.1
) we have
\nu_{p}(\chi^{\lambda}(1))=\nu_{p}(n!)-\nu_{p}(\prod_{h\in\mathcal{H}(\lambda)}h)
.
We have
\nu_{p}(n!)=\frac{n-\sum_{r\geq 0}b_{r}}{p-1}
by Legendre’s formula.
Meanwhile, since
\mathbf{w}_{p^{i}}(\lambda)
is the number of hooks of length divisible by
p^{i}
, we have
\nu_{p}(\prod_{h\in\mathcal{H}(\lambda)}h)=\sum_{i\geq 1}\mathbf{w}_{p^{i}}(\lambda)
.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

S1
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathcal{P}(n)
be the partition labelling
\chi
.
We write
a=\nu_{p}(n!)=\frac{n-\sum_{r\geq 0}b_{r}}{p-1}
, where
n=\sum_{r\geq 0}b_{r}p^{r}
is the
p
-adic expansion of
n
, so that
|P|=p^{a}
.

Theorem

3.2
shows that the prime power weights
\mathbf{w}_{p^{i}}(\lambda)
can be explicitly determined from
\operatorname{Van}(\chi\big\downarrow_{P})
.
The desired numbers can then be expressed as follows:

(1)

the
p
-part of the degree is
\chi(1)_{p}=p^{a-\sum_{i\geq 1}\mathbf{w}_{p^{i}}(\lambda)}
by
Lemma

3.3
;

(2)

the defect of the block containing
\chi
is
d=\nu_{p}((p\mathbf{w}_{p}(\lambda))!)
, since the defect groups are Sylow
p
-subgroups of
S_{p\mathbf{w}_{p}(\lambda)}
;

(3)

the height of
\chi
is
h_{p}(\chi)=d-\sum_{i\geq 1}\mathbf{w}_{p^{i}}(\lambda)
, since it satisfies
\chi(1)_{p}=p^{a-d+h_{p}(\chi)}
. ∎

Proof of
Theorem

S2
.

Let
\lambda,\mu\in\mathcal{P}(n)
be the partitions labelling
\chi,\psi
respectively.
Suppose
\operatorname{Van}(\chi\big\downarrow_{P})\subseteq\operatorname{Van}(\psi\big\downarrow_{P})
.
Then
\mathbf{w}_{p^{i}}(\lambda)\geq\mathbf{w}_{p^{i}}(\mu)
for all
i\geq 1
by
Theorem

3.2
.
Then
\chi(1)_{p}\leq\psi(1)_{p}
by
Lemma

3.3
.
∎

4.
Main results for the alternating group

We now turn our attention to alternating groups.
The goal of this section is to prove
A1
and
A2
.
We quickly deal with the case of
p
odd, before detailing the explicit method of (almost) determining the
2
-height, defect and
2
-part of degree of an irreducible character from its vanishing set in
Theorems

4.7
and
4.9
.

We first observe that the vanishing set of an irreducible character of
A_{n}
is the same as the vanishing set of any one of its covering characters of
S_{n}
restricted to even permutations.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Suppose
\chi\in\operatorname{Irr}(S_{n})
and
\eta\in\operatorname{Irr}(A_{n})
satisfy
[\chi\big\downarrow_{A_{n}},\eta]\neq 0
.
Then for any
g\in A_{n}
, we have
\chi(g)=0
if and only if
\eta(g)=0
.
That is,
\operatorname{Van}(\eta)=\operatorname{Van}(\chi)\cap A_{n}
.

Proof.

The assertion is clear if
\chi\big\downarrow_{A_{n}}\in\operatorname{Irr}(A_{n})
. Otherwise,
\chi=\chi^{\lambda}
where
\lambda=\lambda^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(n)
with
n>1
, and
\eta\in\{\eta^{\lambda+},\eta^{\lambda-}\}
.
In this case, the statement follows immediately from the description of the character values of
\chi^{\lambda}
and of
\eta
given in
[
JK81
, Corollary 2.4.8 and Theorem 2.5.13]
.
∎

The cases of
p
odd in the statements of
Theorem

A1
and
Theorem

A2

then follow immediately from the corresponding statements for the symmetric group: for
p
odd, a Sylow
p
-subgroup of
A_{n}
is precisely a Sylow
p
-subgroup of
S_{n}
, and the
p
-part of degree, defect of a
p
-block and
p
-height are the same for
\eta\in\operatorname{Irr}(A_{n})
as for
\chi\in\operatorname{Irr}(S_{n})
covering
\eta
.

We now proceed with the case
p=2
.
As for the symmetric group, our strategy will be to determine the
2
-power weights – though here we will only be able to identify them up to two possibilities.

We would like to use
Theorem

3.2
, but the elements used in that theorem may not lie in
A_{n}
.
Nevertheless, in the following proposition we identify a different non-zero character value that will get us most of the way towards our goal.
We remark that, compared to
Theorem

3.2
, the proof has become considerably more difficult, despite the small change to the hypothesis and the restriction to the case
p=2
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
l,n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\lambda\in\mathcal{P}(n)
. Suppose
w=\mathbf{w}_{2^{l}}(\lambda)
is odd. Let
\pi\in S_{n}
be a product of
(w-1)
pairwise disjoint
2^{l}
-cycles. Then
\chi^{\lambda}(\pi)\neq 0
.

Remark 4.3
.

The assumption that
w
is odd is necessary here.
For example, let
n=4
,
l=1
and
\lambda=(2,2)
.
Then
w=\mathbf{w}_{2}(\lambda)=2
is even,
\pi
is a single transposition and
\chi^{\lambda}(\pi)=0
.
∎

Proof.

Let the
2^{l}
-quotient of
\lambda
be denoted by

Q_{2^{l}}(\lambda)=\big(\lambda^{(0)},\lambda^{(1)},\ldots,\lambda^{(2^{l}-1)}\big).

For
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,2^{l}-1\}
, let
\mu^{(i)}
be the partition with the same
2^{l}
-core as
\lambda
, but with
2^{l}
-quotient given by

Q_{2^{l}}(\mu^{(i)})=\big(\varnothing,\ldots,\varnothing,(1),\varnothing,\ldots,\varnothing\big)

where
(1)
appears in the
i
th position.
Observe that if we remove
(w-1)
-many
2^{l}
-hooks from
\lambda
, we necessarily arrive at
\mu^{(i)}
for some
i
.
Recalling the definition of paths from
Notation

2.6
, we set
\varepsilon_{i}=\operatorname{sgn}(\lambda\setminus\mu^{(i)})
and use the Murnaghan–Nakayama rule (
Theorem

2.7
) to find

(4.1)

\displaystyle\chi^{\lambda}(\pi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\{0,1,\dotsc,2^{l}-1\}}\varepsilon_{i}\cdot\underbrace{\#\{\text{paths }\lambda\xrightarrow{2^{l}}\mu^{(i)}\}}_{=:\mathsf{P}(i)}\cdot\chi^{\mu^{(i)}}(1).

Fix
i\in\{0,1,\dotsc,2^{l}-1\}
. We calculate
\mathsf{P}(i)
, the number of different paths
P:\lambda\xrightarrow{2^{l}}\mu^{(i)}
. Clearly
\mathsf{P}(i)=0
if
\lambda^{(i)}=\varnothing
.
Otherwise, observe that such a path
P
is equivalent by
Proposition

2.2
to a way of removing
(w-1)
-many boxes in total from the (Young diagrams of the) components of
Q_{2^{l}}(\lambda)
, one at a time leaving partitions at each step, until we arrive at
Q_{2^{l}}(\mu^{(i)})
. Letting
w_{j}:=|\lambda^{(j)}|
, we therefore have

(4.2)

\mathsf{P}(i)=\binom{w-1}{w_{0},\dotsc,w_{i-1},w_{i}-1,w_{i+1},\ldots,w_{2^{l}-1}}\cdot\prod_{j\in\{0,1,\ldots,2^{l}-1\}}\chi^{\lambda^{(j)}}(1),

interpreting this as zero if
w_{i}=0
.
This is because the first multinomial term describes the sequence of components where each successive box is removed from, whilst the second product term describes for each component
\lambda^{(j)}
the number of paths
\lambda^{(j)}\xrightarrow{1}\varnothing
(i.e. removing boxes one by one whilst leaving a partition at each step), which is precisely the number of standard Young tableaux of shape
\lambda^{(j)}
and is equal to
\chi^{\lambda^{(j)}}(1)
. Indeed, for each
j\neq i
we must remove
w_{j}
boxes altogether from the component
\lambda^{(j)}
, whilst from the component
\lambda^{(i)}
itself we only remove
w_{i}-1
boxes, but there is a natural bijection between paths
\lambda^{(i)}\xrightarrow{1}\varnothing
and paths
\lambda^{(i)}\xrightarrow{1}(1)
since
\lambda^{(i)}\neq\varnothing
.

Expanding the multinomial term in (
4.2
) and rearranging, observe that
\mathsf{P}(i)
equals
w_{i}
multiplied by a non-zero expression that does not depend on
i
, namely

\mathsf{P}(i)=w_{i}\cdot\frac{(w-1)!}{w_{0}!\cdots w_{i}!\cdots w_{2^{l}-1}!}\prod_{j\in\{0,1,\dotsc,2^{l}-1\}}\chi^{\lambda^{(j)}}(1).

We now turn our attention to the
\chi^{\mu^{(i)}}(1)
terms in the expression for
\chi^{\lambda}(\pi)
in (
4.1
).
We first claim that the
\mu^{(i)}
all have the same
2
-power weights.
Indeed, the
\mu^{(i)}
all have the same
2^{l}
-core (equal to the
2^{l}
-core of
\lambda
) and have
2^{l}
-quotients which differ only by permuting the components.
Then we can use
Proposition

2.3
to express the weights
\mathbf{w}_{2^{r}}(\mu^{(i)})=\lvert Q_{2^{r}}(\mu^{(i)})\rvert
in terms of the
2^{l}
-cores and -quotients:
for
r\geq l
, we have
\mathbf{w}_{2^{r}}(\mu^{(i)})=\lvert Q_{2^{r-l}}(Q_{2^{l}}(\mu^{(i)}))\rvert
,
while for
r<l
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{2^{r}}(\mu^{(i)})

\displaystyle=\lvert C_{2^{l-r}}(Q_{2^{r}}(\mu^{(i)}))\rvert+2^{l-r}\lvert Q_{2^{l-r}}(Q_{2^{r}}(\mu^{(i)}))\rvert

\displaystyle=\lvert Q_{2^{r}}(C_{2^{l}}(\mu^{(i)}))\rvert+2^{l-r}\lvert Q_{2^{l}}(\mu^{(i)})\rvert.

Hence
\mathbf{w}_{2^{r}}(\mu^{(i)})
is independent of
i
, for all
r
.
Thus, by
Lemma

3.3
, the
2
-parts of the degrees
\chi^{\mu^{(i)}}(1)
are all equal.
Let
c\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
be such that for all
i
we have
\chi^{\mu^{(i)}}(1)=2^{c}m_{i}
where
m_{i}
is odd.

Substituting into (
4.1
), we have

\displaystyle\chi^{\lambda}(\pi)

\displaystyle=2^{c}\frac{(w-1)!}{w_{0}!\cdots w_{i}!\cdots w_{2^{l}-1}!}\prod_{j\in\{0,1,\dotsc,2^{l}-1\}}\chi^{\lambda^{(j)}}(1)\sum_{i\in\{0,1,\dotsc,2^{l}-1\}}\varepsilon_{i}w_{i}m_{i}.

Therefore, in order to conclude that
\chi^{\lambda}(\pi)\neq 0
, it suffices to show that the integer
\sum_{i}\varepsilon_{i}w_{i}m_{i}
is non-zero.
We do this by showing that it is odd. Indeed, we have
\varepsilon_{i}\equiv m_{i}\equiv 1\pmod{2}
and
w\equiv 1\pmod{2}
by hypothesis, so that

\sum_{i}\varepsilon_{i}w_{i}m_{i}\equiv\sum_{i}w_{i}=w\equiv 1\pmod{2}.\qed

Equipped with
Proposition

4.2
, we are able to almost uniquely determine the
2
-power weights as follows.

Definition 4.4
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\eta\in\operatorname{Irr}(A_{n})
. Let
Q
be a Sylow
2
-subgroup of
A_{n}
. For each
i\geq 1
we define the non-negative numbers
\widehat{w}^{0}_{2^{i}}
and
\widehat{w}_{2^{i}}
from the vanishing set of
\eta
on
Q
as follows:

\displaystyle\widehat{w}^{0}_{2^{i}}

\displaystyle:=\max\left\{\sum_{j\geq i}2^{j-i}b_{j}\ \middle|\ \ \parbox{154.0pt}{$\eta(\sigma)\neq 0$ for some $\sigma\in Q$ of cycle type $\big(\dotsc,(2^{2})^{b_{2}},(2^{1})^{b_{1}},(2^{0})^{b_{0}}\big)$ }\ \right\}

and

\displaystyle\widehat{w}_{2^{i}}

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}\widehat{w}^{0}_{2^{i}}+1&\text{ if $i=1$ and $n-2\widehat{w}^{0}_{2}$ is not a triangular number},\\
\widehat{w}^{0}_{2^{i}}&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Remark 4.5
.

The number
\widehat{w}_{2^{i}}
is a natural way to estimate the weight
\mathbf{w}_{2^{i}}(\lambda)
of a partition
\lambda
labelling an irreducible character of
S_{n}
covering
\eta
,
because
\widehat{w}^{0}_{2^{i}}
counts the maximum number of
2^{i}
-hooks that are removed from
\lambda
when calculating
\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)
for some
\sigma\in Q
with the Murnaghan–Nakayama rule.
When
i=1
,
\widehat{w}_{2}
offers an improvement over
\widehat{w}^{0}_{2}
by considering the fact that the
2
-core of a partition is necessarily of size a triangular number.
Nevertheless, for simplicity, the reader may prefer to ignore this improvement, and instead use the definition of
\widehat{w}^{0}_{2^{i}}
as the definition of
\widehat{w}_{2^{i}}
.
The statements of
Lemmas

4.6
,
4.7
and
4.9
below still hold as written with either definition (and by the same proofs, with only one trivial change in the first); however, the conditions for the estimates to be known to be correct are met less frequently when the unimproved definition is used.
∎

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and let
\eta\in\operatorname{Irr}(A_{n})
. Let
\lambda\in\mathcal{P}(n)
be such that
\eta
is covered by
\chi^{\lambda}
.
For each
i\geq 1
, let
\widehat{w}_{2^{i}}
be defined as in
Definition

4.4
.
Then:

(1)

\widehat{w}_{2^{i}}\leq\mathbf{w}_{2^{i}}(\lambda)
for all
i\geq 1
;

(2)

if
i\geq 1
is such that
\mathbf{w}_{2^{i}}(\lambda)
is even, then
\widehat{w}_{2^{i}}=\mathbf{w}_{2^{i}}
;

(3)

if
i\geq 1
is such that
\mathbf{w}_{2^{i}}(\lambda)
is odd, then
\widehat{w}_{2^{i}}\geq\mathbf{w}_{2^{i}}-1
;

(4)

if distinct
i,j\geq 1
are such that
\mathbf{w}_{2^{i}}(\lambda),\mathbf{w}_{2^{j}}(\lambda)
are odd, then
\widehat{w}_{2^{i}}=\mathbf{w}_{2^{i}}(\lambda)
and
\widehat{w}_{2^{j}}=\mathbf{w}_{2^{j}}(\lambda)
.

Proof.

Since
\widehat{w}^{0}_{2^{i}}
counts the maximum number of
2^{i}
-hooks that are removed from
\lambda
when calculating
\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)
for some
\sigma\in Q
with the Murnaghan–Nakayama rule, part
Item

1
is clear for
i>1
.
For
i=1
, we know
n-2\mathbf{w}_{2}(\lambda)
is the size of the
2
-core of
\lambda
and hence necessarily a triangular number; thus if
n-2\widehat{w}^{0}_{2}
is not a triangular number then
\widehat{w}^{0}_{2}<\mathbf{w}_{2}(\lambda)
and hence
\widehat{w}_{2}\leq\mathbf{w}_{2}(\lambda)
, as required.

If
\mathbf{w}_{2^{i}}(\lambda)
is even, a product of
\mathbf{w}_{2^{i}}(\lambda)
disjoint
2^{i}
-cycles lies in
A_{n}
and
\eta
is nonzero on this product by
Theorem

3.2
, yielding part
Item

2
.
If
\mathbf{w}_{2^{i}}(\lambda)
is odd, a product of
(\mathbf{w}_{2^{i}}(\lambda)-1)
disjoint
2^{i}
-cycles lies in
A_{n}
and
\eta
is nonzero on this product by
Proposition

4.2
, yielding part
Item

3
.

For part
Item

4
, without loss of generality suppose
i>j
.
Let
\rho
be a product of
\mathbf{w}_{2^{i}}(\lambda)
many
2^{i}
-cycles and let
\pi
be a product of
(\mathbf{w}_{2^{j}}(\lambda)-2^{i-j}\mathbf{w}_{2^{i}}(\lambda))
many
2^{j}
-cycles, all disjoint.
(Indeed, we can choose
\pi
and
\rho
to be disjoint since the number of moved points of
\rho
is
n-|C_{2^{i}}(\lambda)|
, and of
\pi
is
|C_{2^{i}}(\lambda)|-|C_{2^{j}}(\lambda)|
.)
Observe that
\pi\rho\in A_{n}
, and that
\eta(\pi\rho)\neq 0
: we have that
\chi^{\lambda}(\pi\rho)
is a non-zero multiple of
\chi^{C_{2^{i}}(\lambda)}(\pi)
by
Lemma

3.1
, and
C_{2^{i}}(\lambda)
has
2^{j}
-weight
\mathbf{w}_{2^{j}}(C_{2^{i}}(\lambda))=\mathbf{w}_{2^{j}}(\lambda)-2^{i-j}\mathbf{w}_{2^{i}}(\lambda)
(as can be verified using
Proposition

2.3
Item

1
), so
\chi^{C_{2^{i}}(\lambda)}(\pi)\neq 0
by
Lemma

3.1
again.
Thus the maximum in
Definition

4.4
is attained by
\pi\rho
.
∎

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and let
\eta\in\operatorname{Irr}(A_{n})
. Let
\lambda\in\mathcal{P}(n)
be such that
\eta
is covered by
\chi^{\lambda}
.
For each
i\geq 1
, let
\widehat{w}_{2^{i}}
be defined as in
Definition

4.4
.
Then

\mathbf{w}_{2^{i}}(\lambda)\in\{\,\widehat{w}_{2^{i}},\,\widehat{w}_{2^{i}}+1\,\}.

Furthermore:

(1)

if
\widehat{w}_{2^{j}}
is odd for any
j\geq 1
, then
\mathbf{w}_{2^{i}}(\lambda)=\widehat{w}_{2^{i}}
for all
i\geq 1
;

(2)

\mathbf{w}_{2^{j}}(\lambda)=\widehat{w}_{2^{j}}+1
for at most one
j\geq 1
.

Proof.

The main statement follows immediately from the first three parts of
Lemma

4.6
.

For part
Item

1
,
suppose there exists
j
such that
\widehat{w}_{2^{j}}
is odd.
From
Lemma

4.6
Item

2
,
Item

3
, we deduce that
\mathbf{w}_{2^{j}}(\lambda)
must be odd and equal to
\widehat{w}_{2^{j}}
.
Then if
i\neq j
is such that
\mathbf{w}_{2^{i}}(\lambda)
is odd, by
Lemma

4.6
Item

4
we have that
\mathbf{w}_{2^{i}}(\lambda)=\widehat{w}_{2^{i}}
(while if
\mathbf{w}_{2^{i}}(\lambda)
is even then we have
\mathbf{w}_{2^{i}}(\lambda)=\widehat{w}_{2^{i}}
already by
Lemma

4.6
Item

2
).

Finally we show
Item

2
.
By part
Lemma

4.6
Item

2
, if the
2
-power weights
\mathbf{w}_{2^{i}}(\lambda)
are even for all
i\geq 1
, then
\mathbf{w}_{2^{i}}(\lambda)=\widehat{w}_{2^{i}}
for all
i\geq 1
.
Meanwhile if there are at least two odd
2
-power weights, then we have that
\mathbf{w}_{2^{j}}(\lambda)=\widehat{w}_{2^{j}}
for all
j\geq 1
with
\mathbf{w}_{2^{j}}(\lambda)
odd by
Lemma

4.6
Item

4
, and hence
\mathbf{w}_{2^{j}}(\lambda)=\widehat{w}_{2^{j}}
for all
j\geq 1
using
Lemma

4.6
Item

2
again.
The only remaining situation is having exactly one odd
2
-power weight, in which only for the
j
such that
\mathbf{w}_{2^{j}}(\lambda)
is odd is it possible to have
\mathbf{w}_{2^{j}}(\lambda)=\widehat{w}_{2^{j}}+1
.
∎

Definition 4.8
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\eta\in\operatorname{Irr}(A_{n})
.
Let
m
be the binary digit sum of
n
(that is,
m=\sum_{i\geq 0}b_{i}
where
n=\sum_{i\geq 0}b_{i}2^{i}
is the binary expansion of
n
).
We define the non-negative numbers
\widehat{\nu}
,
\widehat{\smash{d}\vphantom{1}}\vphantom{d}
and
\widehat{\smash{h}\vphantom{1}}\vphantom{h}
in terms of the numbers
\widehat{w}_{2^{i}}
as follows:

(1)

\widehat{\nu}=n-m-\sum_{i\geq 1}\widehat{w}_{2^{i}}
;

(2)

\widehat{\smash{d}\vphantom{1}}\vphantom{d}=\max\{\nu_{2}((2\widehat{w}_{2})!)-1,\,0\}
;

(3)

\widehat{\smash{h}\vphantom{1}}\vphantom{h}=\widehat{\nu}+\widehat{\smash{d}\vphantom{1}}\vphantom{d}-(n-m-1)
.

Corollary 4.9
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
n\geq 2
, and let
\eta\in\operatorname{Irr}(A_{n})
. Let
\lambda\in\mathcal{P}(n)
be such that
\eta
is covered by
\chi^{\lambda}
.
The
2
-part of the degree of
\eta
, the defect
d
of the
2
-block in which
\eta
lies, and the
2
-height of
\eta
can all be determined up to two possibilities as follows:

\displaystyle\eta(1)_{2}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}2^{\widehat{\nu}}&\text{ if $\widehat{w}_{2^{i}}=\mathbf{w}_{2^{i}}(\lambda)$ for all $i\geq 1$ and $\lambda$ is not self-conjugate,}\\
2^{\widehat{\nu}-1}&\text{ otherwise;}\end{cases}

\displaystyle d

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\widehat{\smash{d}\vphantom{1}}\vphantom{d}&\text{ if $\widehat{w}_{2}=\mathbf{w}_{2}(\lambda)$ or $\widehat{w}_{2}=0$,}\\
\widehat{\smash{d}\vphantom{1}}\vphantom{d}+1&\text{ otherwise;}\end{cases}

\displaystyle h_{2}(\eta)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\widehat{\smash{h}\vphantom{1}}\vphantom{h}&\text{ if $\widehat{w}_{2}>0$, $\widehat{w}_{2^{i}}=\mathbf{w}_{2^{i}}(\lambda)$ for all $i\geq 2$, and $\lambda$ is not self-conjugate,}\\
\widehat{\smash{h}\vphantom{1}}\vphantom{h}-1&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Remark 4.10
.

The conditions to distinguish the pairs of options in
Corollary

4.9

(that is, the
2
-power weights and the self-conjugacy of the labelling partition) cannot in general be checked using vanishing on even permutations of
2
-power order.

However, there are many situations when the vanishing set can tell us which option holds.
For example, if there exists
j\geq 1
such that
\widehat{w}_{2^{j}}
is odd, then
\lambda
cannot be self-conjugate, and so (if also
\widehat{w}_{2}>0
) the first option holds for each of the three statistics by
Theorem

4.7
Item

1
.
As another example, we claim that if
n-2\widehat{w}_{2}\neq 3
or if
n\not\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, then
\widehat{w}_{2}=\mathbf{w}_{2}(\lambda)
and hence
d=\widehat{\smash{d}\vphantom{1}}\vphantom{d}
.
Indeed,
\widehat{w}_{2}=\mathbf{w}_{2}(\lambda)-1
occurs only if
\mathbf{w}_{2}(\lambda)
is odd,
\widehat{w}^{0}_{2}=\widehat{w}_{2}
is even, and
n-2\widehat{w}_{2}
is a triangular number.
But then
n-2(\widehat{w}_{2}+1)
is also a triangular number (being the size of the
2
-core of
\lambda
), and
1
and
3
are the only pair of triangular numbers differing by
2
, so
n-2\widehat{w}_{2}=3
and hence
n\equiv 3\pmod{4}
.
∎

Proof of
Corollary

4.9
.

We have
\nu_{2}(\chi^{\lambda}(1))=n-m-\sum_{i\geq 1}\mathbf{w}_{2^{i}}(\lambda)
by
Lemma

3.3
.
Then by
Theorem

4.7
, we have
\nu_{2}(\chi^{\lambda}(1))\in\{\widehat{\nu}-1,\widehat{\nu}\}
with
\nu_{2}(\chi^{\lambda}(1))=\widehat{\nu}
if and only if
\widehat{w}_{2^{i}}=\mathbf{w}_{2^{i}}(\lambda)
for all
i\geq 1
.
If
\lambda
is self-conjugate, then
\eta(1)=\frac{1}{2}\chi^{\lambda}(1)
and necessarily
\mathbf{w}_{2^{i}}(\lambda)
is even for all
i\geq 1
(which implies
\widehat{w}_{2^{i}}=\mathbf{w}_{2^{i}}(\lambda)
for all
i\geq 1
by
Lemma

4.6
Item

2
), so
\nu_{2}(\eta(1))=\nu_{2}(\chi^{\lambda}(1))-1=\widehat{\nu}-1
.
If
\lambda
is not self-conjugate, then
\eta(1)=\chi^{\lambda}(1)
and the result for
\eta(1)_{2}
follows.

Next, recall the block of
A_{n}
containing
\eta
has defect
d=\max\{\nu_{2}((2\mathbf{w}_{2}(\lambda))!)-1,0\}
(see
Section

2.4
).
Thus if
\widehat{w}_{2}\in\{\mathbf{w}_{2}(\lambda),0\}
then
d=\widehat{\smash{d}\vphantom{1}}\vphantom{d}
as claimed.
Otherwise,
\mathbf{w}_{2}(\lambda)=\widehat{w}_{2}+1
,
which can happen only if
\mathbf{w}_{2}(\lambda)
is odd by
Lemma

4.6
Item

2
.
Hence

\displaystyle\nu_{2}((2\mathbf{w}_{2}(\lambda))!)

\displaystyle=\nu_{2}((2\widehat{w}_{2})!)+\nu_{2}(2\mathbf{w}_{2}(\lambda){-}1)+\nu_{2}(2\mathbf{w}_{2}(\lambda))

\displaystyle=\nu_{2}((2\widehat{w}_{2})!)+1

and so
d=\max\{\,\nu_{2}((2\mathbf{w}_{2}(\lambda))!)-1,0\,\}=\nu_{2}((2\widehat{w}_{2})!)
.
On the other hand,
\widehat{\smash{d}\vphantom{1}}\vphantom{d}=\nu_{2}((2\widehat{w}_{2})!)-1
for
\widehat{w}_{2}>0
.
Thus
d=\widehat{\smash{d}\vphantom{1}}\vphantom{d}+1
is as claimed.

Finally,
h_{2}(\eta)=\nu_{2}(\eta(1))+d-(n-m-1)
(see
Section

2.4
, and use Legendre’s formula to find the order of a Sylow
2
-subgroup of
A_{n}
),
so it suffices to compare the values of the previous two statistics under the claimed conditions.
If
\widehat{w}_{2}>0
,
\widehat{w}_{2^{i}}=\mathbf{w}_{2^{i}}(\lambda)
for all
i\geq 2
, and
\lambda
is not self-conjugate, then either:
\widehat{w}_{2}=\mathbf{w}_{2}(\lambda)
in which case
\nu_{2}(\eta(1))=\widehat{\nu}
and
d=\widehat{\smash{d}\vphantom{1}}\vphantom{d}
so
h_{2}(\eta)=\widehat{\smash{h}\vphantom{1}}\vphantom{h}
; or
\widehat{w}_{2}\neq\mathbf{w}_{2}(\lambda)
in which case
\nu_{2}(\eta(1))=\widehat{\nu}-1
and
d=\widehat{\smash{d}\vphantom{1}}\vphantom{d}+1
so again
h_{2}(\eta)=\widehat{\smash{h}\vphantom{1}}\vphantom{h}
.
Otherwise:

•

If
\widehat{w}_{2}=0
: either
\mathbf{w}_{2}(\lambda)=0
and so
\lambda
is self-conjugate, or
\mathbf{w}_{2}(\lambda)=1
and so
\widehat{w}_{2}\neq\mathbf{w}_{2}
; in either case,
\nu_{2}(\eta(1))=\widehat{\nu}-1
. Also,
d=\widehat{\smash{d}\vphantom{1}}\vphantom{d}
, and so
h_{2}(\eta)=\widehat{\smash{h}\vphantom{1}}\vphantom{h}-1
.

•

If there exists
i\geq 2
such that
\widehat{w}_{2^{i}}\neq\mathbf{w}_{2^{i}}(\lambda)
: then
\nu_{2}(\eta(1))=\widehat{\nu}-1
.
Also,
\widehat{w}_{2}=\mathbf{w}_{2}(\lambda)
by
Theorem

4.7
Item

2
, and so
d=\widehat{\smash{d}\vphantom{1}}\vphantom{d}
. Thus
h_{2}(\eta)=\widehat{\smash{h}\vphantom{1}}\vphantom{h}-1
.

•

If
\lambda
is self-conjugate: then
\nu_{2}(\eta(1))=\widehat{\nu}-1
. Also,
\mathbf{w}_{2}(\lambda)
is even and thus
\widehat{w}_{2}=\mathbf{w}_{2}(\lambda)
by
Lemma

4.6
Item

2
, and so
d=\widehat{\smash{d}\vphantom{1}}\vphantom{d}
. Thus
h_{2}(\eta)=\widehat{\smash{h}\vphantom{1}}\vphantom{h}-1
. ∎

Corollary

5.
Examples of vanishing behaviour

In this final section, we present a number of examples to show that our main theorems cannot be strengthened further in terms of characterising degrees using vanishing behaviour.

We will construct examples in terms of their

-core towers

\lambda

e
-core and placing its
e
-quotient in the next layer.

e
-ary tree by placing an edge between each

-core and the components of the

-quotient at each step.

Repeated applications of
[
Ols93
, Proposition 3.7]
show that every partition is uniquely determined by its
e
-core tower, and moreover, we have that
n=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}|T_{e}^{C}(\lambda)_{k}|e^{k}
.

In addition, the
e
-core tower
T_{e}^{C}(\lambda^{\prime})
satisfies
T_{e}^{C}(\lambda^{\prime})_{0}=(C_{e}(\lambda)^{\prime})
, and for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the layer
T_{e}^{C}(\lambda^{\prime})_{k}
is obtained from
T_{e}^{C}(\lambda)_{k}
by taking the conjugate of each partition and reversing the sequence of partitions. In particular, if
\lambda
is self-conjugate (meaning
\lambda=\lambda^{\prime}
) and
e
is even, then
|T_{e}^{C}(\lambda)_{k}|
must be even for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Knowing the
e
-core tower layer sizes is equivalent to knowing the
e
-power weights. Given
\lambda
in its
p
-core tower form, it is easy to calculate
\chi^{\lambda}(1)_{p}
, as shown below.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
n,k\in\mathbb{N}
and
e\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
. Let
\lambda\in\mathcal{P}(n)
. The size of the
k
th layer of the
e
-core tower of
\lambda
is given in terms of the
e
-power weights of
\lambda
by

\lvert T^{C}_{e}(\lambda)_{k}\rvert=\mathbf{w}_{e^{k}}(\lambda)-e\mathbf{w}_{e^{k+1}}(\lambda).

Let
p
be a prime and suppose
n=\sum_{r\geq 0}b_{r}p^{r}
is the
p
-adic expansion of
n
.
Then

\nu_{p}(\chi^{\lambda}(1))=\frac{\sum_{r\geq 0}|T^{C}_{p}(\lambda)_{r}|-\sum_{r\geq 0}b_{r}}{p-1}.

Proof.

Using the definition of the
k
th layer given above and
Proposition

2.3
, we have

\displaystyle\lvert T^{C}_{e}(\lambda)_{k}\rvert

\displaystyle=\lvert C_{e}(Q_{e^{k}}(\lambda))\rvert

\displaystyle=\lvert Q_{e^{k}}(\lambda)\rvert-e\lvert Q_{e}(Q_{e^{k}}(\lambda))\rvert

\displaystyle=\lvert Q_{e^{k}}(\lambda)\rvert-e\lvert Q_{e^{k+1}}(\lambda)\rvert

\displaystyle=\mathbf{w}_{e^{k}}(\lambda)-e\mathbf{w}_{e^{k+1}}(\lambda)).

Summing over all
k
we find
\sum_{k\geq 0}|T^{C}_{e}(\lambda)_{k}|=n-(e-1)\sum_{i\geq 1}\mathbf{w}_{e^{i}}(\lambda)
, and the expression for
\nu_{p}(\chi^{\lambda}(1))
follows from
Lemma

3.3
.
∎

5.1.
Converses of main theorems fail

We give two families of examples to show that the vanishing set
\operatorname{Van}(\chi^{\lambda}\big\downarrow_{P})
of an irreducible character
\chi^{\lambda}\in\operatorname{Irr}(S_{n})
is
not
determined by the
p

-power weights of the corresponding partition
\lambda

(Converse of
Theorem

S2

.

Let

, and let
\lambda=(p(p-1),p-1,1)

\mu=(p(p-1),p)

That is,
\lambda
and
\mu
are the partitions with
p

Hence

5.2.
Cannot fully determine statistics of characters of
A_{n}
when
p=2

We give several examples to demonstrate that we cannot uniquely determine the
2
-part of the degree, the defect of the
2
-block, or the
2
-height of an irreducible character of
A_{n}
from the vanishing set on a Sylow
2
-subgroup
Q
of
A_{n}
.
We present our examples in
Table

1
; in each case, an example is given as a pair of distinct, non-conjugate partitions
\lambda
and
\mu
which label irreducible characters of
A_{n}
with equal vanishing sets on
Q
, and which have the claimed
2
-parts of degrees, defects of
2
-blocks,
2
-heights, and self-conjugacy.
That they have the claimed data is clear from the
2
-core towers of the partitions and
Lemma

5.1
.
That they have the same vanishing sets is verified by direct computation.
In the cases of
5.5
,
5.6
,
5.7
and
5.9
, in fact the
2
-core can be replaced by any larger
2
-core to obtain infinitely many examples with the same properties; the equal vanishing sets for these families can be shown using (sometimes challenging) applications of the Murnaghan–Nakayama rule and the hook length formula.

The significance of these examples is as follows.
Recall that
Corollary

4.9
tells us that, amongst irreducible characters with equal vanishing sets in
Q
, the three pieces of data in consideration differ by at most
1
.
Our examples show that such a pair of characters may agree or disagree on any combination of these three numbers, subject to the relationship between
2
-part of degree, defect and height requiring that if any two agree or disagree then the third must agree.
In particular, given two such characters with equal vanishing on
Q
, additionally specifying one of the three statistics is insufficient to determine either of the other two (so, for example, two irreducible characters lying in the same block with the same vanishing set on
Q
may still have different
2
-part of degree and
2
-height, as in
5.8
and
5.9
).

Our examples furthermore show that, with one exception, each of the combinations of disagreement can occur whether or not one of the labelling partitions is self-conjugate.
Self-conjugacy of the labelling partition is equivalent to the irreducible character of
S_{n}
splitting into a sum of two irreducible characters upon restriction to
A_{n}
, and hence the
2
-part of the degree halving – and so is a potential cause for disagreements in our statistics, in addition to the uncertainty in the
2
-power weights described in
Theorem

4.7
.
Our examples demonstrate that both causes indeed manifest, and that, in fact, when they occur together they can cancel out (as in
Ex.

5.7
).
This also highlights that the vanishing set on
Q
is insufficient to detect self-conjugacy of the labelling partition (even if given all three of our statistics, as in
Ex.

5.7
).

Note that if we did know that
\eta\in\operatorname{Irr}(A_{n})
was labelled by a self-conjugate partition, then by
Corollary

4.9
the three statistics are uniquely determined from the vanishing set.
Thus, in particular, if two irreducible characters of
A_{n}
with equal vanishing on
Q
are both labelled by self-conjugate partitions, necessarily they agree on all three statistics.

The one exception to self-conjugacy being possible is the following.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
Q\in\operatorname{Syl}_{2}(A_{n})
. Let
\eta,\theta\in\operatorname{Irr}(A_{n})
with
\operatorname{Van}(\eta\big\downarrow_{Q})=\operatorname{Van}(\theta\big\downarrow_{Q})
.
Suppose
\eta(1)_{2}\neq\theta(1)_{2}
and that
\eta
,
\theta
lie in distinct blocks.
Then
\eta
and
\theta
are labelled by partitions which are not self-conjugate.

Proof.

Let
\lambda,\mu\in\mathcal{P}(n)
be such that
\chi^{\lambda}
covers
\eta
and
\chi^{\mu}
covers
\theta
.
We use the notation of
Section

4
, with
\widehat{w}_{2^{i}}
,
\widehat{\nu}
and
\widehat{\smash{d}\vphantom{1}}\vphantom{d}
constructed from the common vanishing set of
\eta
and
\theta
.

Suppose towards a contradiction that
\mu
is self-conjugate.
Then
\mathbf{w}_{2}(\mu)
is even so
\widehat{w}_{2}=\mathbf{w}_{2}(\mu)
by
Lemma

4.6
Item

2
.
Also, by
Corollary

4.9
, we have
\theta(1)_{2}=2^{\widehat{\nu}-1}
, and in order that
\eta(1)_{2}\neq\theta(1)_{2}
, we must have
\lambda
not self-conjugate and
\mathbf{w}_{2^{i}}(\lambda)=\widehat{w}_{2^{i}}
for all

.
But then, by

4.9
again, the defects of the blocks containing
\eta
and
\theta

, and so
\eta
and
\theta
lie in the same block (since there is a unique
2
-core of any given size), a contradiction.
∎
