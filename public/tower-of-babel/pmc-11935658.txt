What Makes an Effective Chief of Pediatric Cardiology: Insights From Chiefs of North American Pediatric Programs
Authors: Michael E Kim, Wayne Franklin, Anne M Dubin, Jeffrey J Kim, Shubhayan Sanatani, R Krishna Kumar, Shubhika Srivastava, Thomas R Kimball, Mark Lewin, Joseph Rossano, Colin J McMahon
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough the position of a pediatric cardiology chief is often seen as the apex of one's academic career, its roles, responsibilities, and qualifications are not well defined in the literature. This study was done to gain further insight of the role and attributes of a pediatric cardiology chief by surveying those who are currently serving the position across North American centers.Methods and ResultsThis was a mixed‐methods study with a survey given to North American pediatric cardiology chiefs at programs with fellowship training programs. The survey was a semistructured questionnaire that was independently reviewed by 3 cardiologists. Smaller private practice groups and nonacademic programs were excluded. Survey inventory included items rated on a 5‐point Likert scale, open‐ended prompts, and targeted questions. A total of 40 of 65 (62%) pediatric cardiology chiefs responded to the survey. Respondents identified key chief attributes included communication skills, honesty/transparency, and conflict‐resolution skills. Likert scale data demonstrated participants were satisfied with their position, although many reported growing concerns of increased demands from administration, faculty, and pressures of program performance in the current era. There is also a noted paucity of diversity among those serving in leadership positions within pediatric cardiology, which was acknowledged by survey respondents.ConclusionsWe gathered information directly from current North American pediatric cardiology chiefs examining the current era of its role. There are resounding themes on the emphasis for communication, honesty, conflict resolution, and mentorship. Future studies should examine faculty perceptions and a global perspective of the role.Keywords: chief, collaboration, congenital heart disease, leadership, pediatric cardiology programSubject Categories: Health Services, Ethics and Policy, Pediatrics

The appointment as chief of pediatric cardiology represents the pinnacle in the career of most pediatric cardiologists. This is not dissimilar from other medical subspecialties. 1 , 2 , 3 However, little is documented in the literature about what the role entails, which attributes are required, and who is qualified to lead. Chiefs set the tone within departments, create the culture, and have critical roles in mentoring and supporting faculty and trainees, all while providing oversight and excellence in the provision of clinical care, research, and teaching. 4 Over time, pediatric cardiology departments have grown significantly with evolving priorities and goals focusing on clinical practice expansion, revenue targets, achieving excellence in patient care delivery and outcomes, and managing the demands with the corporate suite on optimizing resource use and expense reduction. 5 , 6

The growth in pediatric cardiology centers has resulted in greater development of subspeciality groups. This type of expansion is complicated by the inevitable development of silo mentalities within those same groups. 4 The chief role must facilitate these relationships and build an effective team between all the stakeholders, including cardiology, cardiac surgery, anaesthesiology, intensive care, nursing, and allied health care. The health care model in the United States is unique as a private health care model, which contrasts with many public or mixed care models espoused in Canada, Europe, and beyond. 7 , 8

Given the multiple tasks demanded of the chief (conflict resolution, managing uncertainty, and chairing multidisciplinary meetings) 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 and the knowledge gap on this topic in the literature, this study sought to explore the demands of the job, which personal characteristics and attributes are vital to succeed, lessons learned during the chiefs' tenure, changes in the job over the years, increasing challenges in the job, and, most importantly, the legacy that chiefs wanted to leave behind after their time in charge. To gain greater insight into these somewhat nebulous areas, we went directly to the source: the voices of the chiefs themselves. We specifically examined these research questions in the North American setting, where the pediatric cardiology chief role is well established, formally recognized, and an integral part of the performance of the cardiology team within the heart center.

METHODS

Ethical approval was waived by the Ethics Department at Children's Health Ireland, Crumlin, Dublin, Ireland, as this was a survey‐based study.

This was a mixed‐methods study with grounded theory using both quantitative and qualitative assessment using a semistructured questionnaire, which was approved by 3 independent pediatric cardiologists. The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request. The survey was created in SurveyMonkey to aid ease of completion by the respondents. Invitations to participate in the survey were distributed by email to all pediatric cardiology chiefs at training centers identified in the Electronic Residency Application Service online system (Data S1). We excluded small private practice groups, which may not have the traditional chief structure, and we wanted to sample centers that participate in training fellows and early career faculty. Need for consent to perform this survey‐based study was waived by the Ethics Department at Children's Health Ireland. Consent was requested from all respondents to complete the survey and to facilitate the results of the study being disseminated in a peer‐reviewed pediatric cardiology journal.

On initial survey data review with the author group, a supplemental survey was designed and approved, which examined further questions on diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI) within the departments and obtaining additional demographic information. We prioritized including items on inclusion of gender reporting both from participants as well as reporting the gender composition of current faculty and subdivision leaders. There were also additional items querying the current DEI efforts from participants. Participant gender identification was self‐reported as a free‐text option within the survey.

Statistical Analysis

Demographics and descriptive statistics, including Likert scale responses from the survey, were summarized by medians with interquartile ranges and frequencies with percentages, as appropriate. Within the survey, participants ranked items that were then calculated by means with SDs. The open‐ended prompts were manually coded by 2 independent reviewers, which then were compiled into themes that emerged from the data.

Key Attributes of Effective Chiefs

One portion of the survey tasked respondents to rank a list of traits in order of importance (Table 3). In summary, there were clear categories of traits established on the basis of the frequency of responses: Category 1 can be summarized as “fundamental attributes of an effective chief,” including excellent communication skills, conflict‐resolution skills, honesty/transparency, and focus on faculty development and program building. The next level includes attributes that delve deeper into humility, equitable treatment of staff, management skills, self‐awareness, and changing program culture. Following that level included being a visionary, promoting psychological safety, and academic excellence. At the bottom of the ranking includes teaching ability, negotiation skills, and professional reputation (Figure 1).

Likert Scale Responses

The survey items rated on a 5‐point Likert scale can be summarized in Figures 2A and 2B. Respondents overall reported satisfaction of their role as cardiology chief, and only a minority reported the job becoming more difficult as time progresses forward. Several items tasked respondents with evaluating the current landscape of pediatric cardiology practice relative to the start of their tenure, of which a majority expressed that expectations have increased across the board from patients, faculty, and hospital systems. There is also commentary on the change of work ethic around the faculty and trainees that were observed; when prompted for clarification, several responses indicated a newer emphasis on “work‐life balance” and “wellness” for faculty and that duty hour changes and program expectations changed more than the actual quality of effort from trainees. Finally, there is a strong emphasis from respondents that felt programs focused too much on quantifying center performance and center rankings (ie, U.S. News and World Report).

Qualitative Prompts and Themes

We asked a series of open‐ended questions to ascertain what traits are most important in becoming a successful pediatric cardiology chief, what did they learn, what did they regret, and what is needed for the role itself. Three main themes emerged from the identified coded data, which include the following: (1) The chief role is highly complex, requiring a diverse set of skills and traits. (2) Essential attributes for a chief include honesty, communication, and a strong work ethic. (3) Leading as a “master servant” is crucial to the success of the patients and faculty.

The first theme illustrated that the chief position is multifaceted and requires adaptability and resilience to navigate multiple disciplines (ie, cardiovascular surgery, anesthesiology, cardiology, and critical care medicine), to direct financial and administrative decisions, and to develop faculty and staff within their own division. Several responses highlighted the learning curve of business and administrative skills; some suggested that formal training in these areas would also be of benefit. Faculty mentorship and development was frequently mentioned as another area that would benefit from formal training.

…particularly leading by example. I think the best chiefs demonstrate what is desired in terms of work ethic, engagement with clinical activities, and overall attitude.

…the chief needs to be an example of the work ethic they expect. They need to behave professionally, work in the trenches to demonstrate solidarity with faculty, and promote high ethical standards.

The second theme entailed that the chief must have several intrinsic traits to be successful. Several of these characteristics revolve around communication skills, including the ability to manage conflict resolution, motivation, negotiation, and listening. Other notable areas include resiliency, adaptability, confidence, and transparency. There is also some mention (but to a much lesser degree) of emphasis on clinical skills, academic productivity, and reputation among peers.

Communication skills, treating everyone else (no matter level/rank) with dignity and respect, embodying the desired culture, inspiring others to follow along.

…ability to be a player‐coach…folks realise you are not asking them to do something you wouldn't do. Effective delegation of responsibilities in a way that enables others to assume leadership roles…ability to not react but rather interact constructively…ability to have hard conversations in a timely fashion.

Finally, the third theme highlighted the concept of servant leadership, which entails a combination of personal traits and extrinsic features. This is highlighted by the repeated emphasis on humility, honesty, patience, and empathy. Interestingly, this also manifests in the roles of the chief including aspects of mentorship, faculty recruitment/promotion, team building, and developing a well‐functioning and communicative division.

Honesty, integrity, humble but confident. You must truly put the other person success and advancement before your own.

Be a servant leader. Keep your moral compass pointing north. Listen.

When specifically asked about methods in which to promote high quality research among faculty, the most common items include providing protected time, securing funding and resources, and development of a research infrastructure to support investigators.

DISCUSSION

We surveyed two‐thirds of the North American pediatric cardiology chiefs working at academic cardiac centers with fellowship training programs. To characterize the role of the chief position, respondents highlighted the complexity of the job in terms of the wide array of responsibilities and interpersonal skills required. Communication skills and integrity were most consistently reported as the most important aspects of the position both in terms of ranking traits (Table 3) and from qualitative prompts. It is also important to highlight that the respondents were also largely satisfied with their career choice and used the survey as a point of reflection.

The survey participants illustrated the nuanced pressures of the position through the increased pressures to lead and perform. Although specific causes are not objectively outlined, the expansion of cardiac surgery programs and increased volume of patient referrals and outcomes‐based metrics are certainly plausible reasons. The Likert scale items highlighted the trends of both programs and faculty/fellow changes over time, and although there was no overwhelming agreement in facing pressures from these factors, there certainly is a significant group of respondents who felt expectations from faculty, trainees, and administrators to be higher in the current era.

The pediatric cardiology chief role, as complex and intense as it continues to be, has several stipulations that are unclear when it comes to succession planning and term limits. Respondents on average have been in the position for 5 years (interquartile range, 2–10 years) and have overwhelmingly stressed the importance of term limits as well as thoughtful transitions of the role (Table 4). These types of ambiguities may also be obstacles in individuals who may be interested in the position given there are no clear policies or cultural practices in institutions at large for the duration and transition of the role. Although the overall satisfaction is high for respondents who are currently serving as chiefs, several commented as their position is ending that there is a sense of relief and anxiety because of the thoughts of handing off their work as well as the level of intensity and rigor that encompassed the role.

Our decision to expand the initial survey yielded additional insights into DEI practices throughout the various cardiac centers. Despite most departments having at least 30% female faculty, there is a much smaller representation at the leadership level within the subdivision and cardiology chief positions. These data support other findings relating to gender and race throughout the field, with women and minorities continuing to be underrepresented in areas of leadership. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 Although there is greater awareness of this issue and with ongoing efforts to promote underrepresented groups, more work needs to be done. This survey highlights the need for frequent review of how faculty promotion and leadership positions are allocated and should be evaluated frequently to ensure biases are being addressed. Specifically, it would be important for programs to evaluate their hiring and promotional practices to identify any bias that can be assessed by third‐party consultants and advocates for diversity, equity, and inclusion. Another important aspect should include improvements in the support for faculty mentorship and professional development to encourage and identify leadership candidates within the field. Another important factor would be the inclusion of benchmarks for DEI in ranking programs (ie, U.S. News and World Report), which would influence intentional transformation and supporting development of an infrastructure to support the same.

Finally, and perhaps the most interesting data from the survey include the intrinsic and extrinsic traits of a pediatric cardiology chief that were highlighted. From these responses, it is plausible to create an archetype of the “ideal” chief: a servant leader, empathetic, communicative, resilient, facile with interprofessional matters, adept in financial and administrative tasks, a strong mentor, and a visionary who can build a strong cardiac program (Figure 2). Although this list is not finite, it is sufficient to conclude that the role is a highly diverse and multifaceted position that requires an individual who not only must possess the skills but the passion to lead.

Limitations

There are several limitations to this survey‐based study. The questionnaire, although reviewed by 3 cardiologists, was not a validated instrument as we could not find such a validated tool for assessing chiefs of pediatric cardiology divisions. We surveyed the chiefs themselves as we believed they have a unique perspective on their own role; however, the voice of faculty may have been just as informative. Also, although we investigated potential disparities with regard to gender in leadership roles, we did not ask questions on other DEI domains, including race. We acknowledge this is important and would recommend future studies would expand on this topic. We hope to study their perceptions of the qualities required in an effective chief in the future. We focused on academic pediatric cardiology units around North America and did not survey small private practice cardiac units as we wanted to hear the experience from chiefs in centers committed to education and training of faculty and trainees. Although we attempted to contact every unit in North America, there may have been a small number we could not reach because of changes in chief or an interim chief being appointed. There is also a limitation to the generalizability of our survey data as we did not include private practice programs. In addition, a more robust future survey might include greater detail around the division chief skill set required to (1) ensure equity, diversity, and inclusion, (2) manage an efficient and effective business, (3) develop a high‐quality research program, and (4) ensure that the referring provider community is well served. Future studies can also examine the role of the pediatric cardiology chief position from the perspective of faculty and from chiefs globally.

Proposals

Pediatric cardiology divisions should direct their focus to the high‐frequency reported attributes, including excellent communication skills, honesty, strong work ethic, and humility. Programs should not be swayed by reputation and numbers of articles published or grants secured. These latter abilities were not mentioned as key attributes to being an effective successful chief.

Individuals who become chiefs should be considered regardless of race, gender, or age. However, there is a clear preponderance in North America of White male chiefs. Further insight is needed within the selection process of program leadership to address any bias or barriers in terms of desired attributes or leadership styles to promote increased diversity in these crucial roles.

CONCLUSIONS

Pediatric cardiology chiefs have a complex and demanding role requiring multiple skill sets. Communications skills, honesty, hard work, and humility remain the cornerstone of characteristics key to being an effective chief. Reputation and academic excellence may be less important as an effective chief and should not be the primary focus of search committees. Facilitating greater communication between chiefs themselves, potentially through training days, may foster a greater collaborative environment and help mitigate stresses associated with the position.
