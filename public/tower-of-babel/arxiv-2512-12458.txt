Breaking the Curse of Dimensionality: On the Stability of Modern Vector Retrieval
Vihan Lakshman, Blaise Munyampirwa, Julian Shun, Benjamin Coleman

Abstract
Modern vector databases enable efficient retrieval over high-dimensional neural embeddings, powering applications from web search to retrieval-augmented generation. However, classical theory predicts such tasks should suffer from the curse of dimensionality , where distances between points become nearly indistinguishable, thereby crippling efficient nearest-neighbor search. We revisit this paradox through the lens of stability , the property that small perturbations to a query do not radically alter its nearest neighbors. Building on foundational results, we extend stability theory to three key retrieval settings widely used in practice: (i) multi-vector search, where we prove that the popular Chamfer distance metric preserves single-vector stability, while average pooling aggregation may destroy it; (ii) filtered vector search, where we show that sufficiently large penalties for mismatched filters can induce stability even when the underlying search is unstable; and (iii) sparse vector search, where we formalize and prove novel sufficient stability conditions. Across synthetic and real datasets, our experimental results match our theoretical predictions, offering concrete guidance for model and system design to avoid the curse of dimensionality.



1
Introduction

The
curse of dimensionality
is a notorious barrier to algorithmic efficiency in computational geometry. Informally, the curse states that points in high-dimensional metric spaces tend to be arbitrarily close in distance. Consequently, this property makes it challenging for techniques such as near-neighbor search and near-neighbor classification to identify the closest points to a query without resorting to a full brute-force scan, which can be prohibitively slow in practice. Nevertheless, modern vector databases, powered by stunning advances in neural representation learning, have achieved remarkable adoption in spite of high vector dimensionalities, powering applications such as retrieval-augmented generation
(
Lewis et al. 2020
)
, web-search
(
Huang et al. 2020
)
, and product search
(
Nigam et al. 2019
)
.

The success of modern vector retrieval raises a question that we find surprisingly unexplored in the current research literature:
Given that vector databases efficiently search high-dimensional embedding spaces, what properties do these search algorithms and embeddings possess that make them amenable to sub-linear time search algorithms?
In addition to enhancing our theoretical understanding of the curse of dimensionality, successfully answering this question also has implications for machine learning model developers and vector database engineers. In particular, if we can identify necessary or sufficient conditions for embedding-based search to “break“ the curse of dimensionality, practitioners can potentially leverage these insights when working to optimize models and search algorithms by maintaining the essential invariants that account for the success of these representations in high dimensions.

In this paper, we study this question from a theoretical and experimental lens, using the formal notion of
stability
to characterize the conditions under which the curse of dimensionality is overcome. We focus on three vector retrieval settings widely used in practice:

1.

Multi-Vector Search
which involves performing near-neighbor search in the case where both the query and the database items consist of
sets
of vectors. This style of search has become extremely popular due to the recent success of “late interaction” models such as ColBERT
(
Khattab & Zaharia 2020
)
.

2.

Filtered Vector Search
which involves near neighbor search in the presence of discrete attribute filters. This setting has also become widely deployed in vector databases in practice
(
Gollapudi et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2023
)
due to the need to filter results based on discrete metadata.

3.

Sparse Vector Search
which is near neighbor search in the special case where the embedding vectors are extremely high-dimensional (such as in the tens or hundreds of thousands) but only a small number of components are non-zero. This search style has become popular due to the success of learned sparse representation models such as SPLADE
(
Formal et al. 2021a
;
Formal et al. 2021b
)
and semantic models in recommender systems
(
Rajput et al. 2023
;
Penha et al. 2025
)
.

Intuitively, stability captures the notion that a slight perturbation to a query should not dramatically change the list of near neighbors to that query. The landmark paper of
(
Beyer et al. 1999
)
and a subsequent follow-up work of
(
Durrant & Kabán 2009
)
together establish a necessary and sufficient condition for stability via a quantity called
relative variance
. However, to our knowledge, no work has studied extending these prior results to modern vector database settings, particularly in the three cases that we focus on in this paper. Thus, our work fills a gap in the literature in understanding why modern neural representations enable efficient sub-linear vector search in high dimensions. Our specific contributions are as follows:

•

We prove that multi-vector search with the commonly-used Chamfer distance is stable if the
induced
single vector search problem is
strongly stable
(along with some technical conditions on the composition of the vector sets). However, not all aggregations are stability preserving; we give a counter-example showing that stability does not hold in general for average pooling. This result provides a theoretical justification for the choice of the Chamfer distance used in the popular ColBERT family of models
(
Khattab & Zaharia 2020
;
Santhanam et al. 2022
;
Faysse et al.
)
.

•

We prove that filtered vector search in the
distance penalty
model is stable provided that the penalty function applied to points that do not satisfy the filter is sufficiently large. Interestingly, we show that, for a suitably large penalty, filtered search can be stable even if the underlying vector search problem (without filters) is unstable. We also discuss why this distance penalty model encompasses the majority of hybrid vector search algorithms in the literature.

•

In the sparse vector search setting, we present the first formal definition of a property called
concentration of importance
, which was intuitively described in a paper of
(
Bruch et al. 2024
)
, as well as the definition of a new property we call
overlap of importance
. We then prove that concentration of importance and overlap of importance (along with some minor technical conditions preventing non-degeneracy) together imply stability.

•

In addition to our theoretical results, we also empirically validate each of these theorems on a combination of synthetic and real embedding datasets.

Background

In this section, we will introduce the necessary mathematical preliminaries for our main results. We begin with a formal definition of nearest-neighbor search.

Definition 2.1

(Near-Neighbor Search Problem)
.

A
near-neighbor search problem
is a 3-tuple
(Q,D,\delta)
for a query set
Q
and database collection
D
where
q,d\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
for all
q\in Q
and
d\in D
. For each
q\in Q
, we use the distance metric
\delta
to compute

\argmin_{d\in D}\delta(q,d)

In the remainder of this paper, we will assume that all database elements
d\in D
are sampled from some distribution and all query elements
q\in Q
are sampled independently from some distribution. Under this probabilistic model, the distance
\delta(q,d)
for
q\sim Q
and
d\sim D
is a random variable. In a seminal paper,
Beyer et al. 1999
introduced this probabilistic model to provide a precise formalization of the curse of dimensionality in the context of near-neighbor search by defining the notion of instability.

Definition 2.2

(Instability
(
Beyer et al. 1999
)
)
.

A nearest neighbor problem
(Q,D,\delta)
is
unstable
if for all
\epsilon>0

\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathop{\Pr}\limits_{q\sim Q}\left[\max_{d\in D}\{\delta(q,d)\}\leq(1+\epsilon)\min_{d\in D}\{\delta(q,d)\}\right]=1

If this property does not hold, we say the problem is
stable
.

Remark 2.3
.

For notational convenience, we will not explicitly index the random variable
\delta(q,d)
by the dimensionality, but it is important to remember that
\delta(q,d)
is a function of the dimension
m
.

We can also equivalently characterize instability as the convergence of the ratio of the maximum instance to the minimum distance as we take the dimensionality to infinity.

Corollary 2.4

(
Beyer et al. 1999
)
.

For a nearest neighbor problem
(Q,D,\delta)
, let
q\sim Q
and define
\mathrm{DMAX}=\max_{d}\delta(q,d)
and
\mathrm{DMIN}=\min_{d}\delta(q,d)
. A nearest neighbor problem is unstable if and only if
\frac{\mathrm{DMAX}}{\mathrm{DMIN}}\to_{p}1
where the sequence is taken over the dimensionality.

In other words, a nearest neighbor problem is unstable if the distance between the closest and farthest points across all queries becomes nearly indistinguishable. We can also interpret stability as the property that small perturbations to a query do not dramatically alter its list of near neighbors. This is a mathematical formalization of the curse of dimensionality, which states that high-dimensional spaces lack the contrast needed for meaningful near-neighbor search. In this setting, sublinear approximate search algorithms fail, and a brute force linear scan becomes necessary.

Example 2.5

(Example of Stability)
.

One concrete example of a stable near-neighbor search problem, originally proposed by
(
Beyer et al. 1999
)
, arises from constructing query and document vectors such that each component in every vector is equal, and this value is iid sampled from some nontrivial distribution.

Example 2.6

(Example of Instability)
.

An example of an unstable search problem, also originally identified by
(
Beyer et al. 1999
)
, is one where the query and document distributions have finite moments and each component of all vectors is iid sampled from these respective distributions.

To prove that a near-neighbor problem is stable or unstable, we will also stand on the shoulders of two prior theorems from
(
Beyer et al. 1999
)
and
(
Durrant & Kabán 2009
)
, respectively.

Theorem 2.7

(
Beyer et al. 1999
)
.

If
\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{\mathrm{Var}\left[\delta(q,d)\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\delta(q,d)\right]^{2}}=0
, then the nearest neighbor problem is unstable.

Theorem 2.8

(
Durrant & Kabán 2009
)
.

For a sufficiently large database size
|D|
, if
\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{\mathrm{Var}\left[\delta(q,d)\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\delta(q,d)\right]^{2}}\neq 0
, then the nearest neighbor problem is stable.

Following the convention established in
(
Beyer et al. 1999
)
, we refer to the quantity
\frac{\mathrm{Var}\left[\delta(q,d)\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\delta(q,d)\right]^{2}}
as the
relative variance
or RelVar. In this paper, we will utilize both the convergence in probability characterization and the relative variance criterion to prove stability.

Remark 2.9
.

We can equivalently state the condition in Theorem
2.8
as
\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{\mathrm{Var}\left[\delta(q,d)\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\delta(q,d)\right]^{2}}>0
since the relative variance is non-negative by definition. We will use both characterizations interchangeably in this work.

3
Related Work

3.1
Conditions for Stability

The paper of
(
Beyer et al. 1999
)
was a foundational work that inspired significant follow-up research in understanding the conditions for stable vector search.
(
Aggarwal et al. 2001
)
study stability under common
\ell_{p}
norm distance metrics, where they find that
\ell_{1}
and fractional distance metrics are theoretically and practically favorable to
\ell_{2}
distances. Furthermore,
(
Houle et al. 2010
)
prove that the so-called
shared-neighbor
distance function, which measures the number of near-neighbors in common between two points, is a more stable measure than absolute distance since the latter loses discriminatory power in high dimensions. In a different vein,
(
Hui & Gao 2021
)
study stability in the context of sequential data, such as genomes and sensor readings, and show that, under uniformly distributed sequences, the RelVar under edit distance goes to zero in the limit. Moreover,
(
Pestov 2013
)
extends the results of
(
Beyer et al. 1999
)
from
k
-nearest neighbor
search
to
k
-nearest neighbor
classification
where they find that instability of vector search also implies barriers to the learnability of near-neighbor classification.

Our work is a direct follow-up to this rich prior literature in understanding the conditions for stable vector search. We focus on settings that have recently gained significant popularity amongst practitioners and provide an explanation for why these high-dimensional search settings succeed in spite of the curse of dimensionality.

3.2
Intrinsic Dimensionality

A closely related concept to stability is
intrinsic dimensionality
(ID) which measures the dimensionality of the subspace that a collection of vectors lie within. Intuitively, if a database of high-dimensional vectors lies within a much lower-dimensional manifold, then a near-neighbor search over these points is insulated from the curse of dimensionality effect. Thus, much like stability, ID provides a foundational theoretical tool for explaining why modern vector retrieval is capable of circumventing the curse of dimensionality. Consequently, ID has been well-studied with multiple proposed formal definitions. Perhaps the most influential definition of ID is that of
Karger & Ruhl 2002
via a geomeric property called the
expansion rate
. Another popular definition heavily utilized in near-neighbor search algorithm design is the notion of
doubling dimension
proposed by
Gupta et al. 2003
. We refer interested readers to the recent book of
Bruch 2024
for the precise definitions. More recently, several works have measured the intrinsic dimensionality of modern neural embeddings and have reported that the ID is often much less than the ambient dimensionality. For instance,
Razzhigaev et al. 2024
study the intrinsic dimensionality of embeddings from transformer models
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
and find that the ID typically lies between 20 and 60 over the course of pretraining. In addition,
Gong et al. 2019
compute the intrinsic dimensionality of popular image model embeddings, finding that the ID in practice typically ranges from 12 to 19, well below the ambient dimensions of 128 to 512. Moreover,
Ansuini et al. 2019
go beyond embeddings and measure the ID of convolutional neural network (CNN) layers where they find the ID is an order of magnitude smaller than the ambient dimension.

Given that neural models consistently exhibit low intrinsic dimensionality, a natural question to ask is whether we still need to study why modern vector databases succeed in spite of the curse of dimensionality. On the contrary, we argue that intrinsic dimensionality alone is insufficient to explain the phenomena we see in practice. First and foremost, as we empirically measure in Section
4
, a dimensionality as low as 32, which is within the empirical ID measurements of real embeddings, is still high enough for the curse of dimensionality to diminish the search quality of approximate near neighbor search algorithms. This observation motivates the need to study stability explicitly. Secondly, modern embeddings produced by large language models and used in practice continue to grow in dimension into the thousands and tens of thousands of components. It is not clear if the intrinsic dimensionality will remain low as these embedding models scale and yet we observe they remain amenable to sub-linear vector search techniques. Moreover, stability analysis provides
actionable
guidance that ID
alone cannot. For instance, our multi-vector stability theorem explains
why ColBERT’s choice of Chamfer distance succeeds where average pooling
might fail, a distinction invisible to ID measurements which only
characterize the embedding space geometry, not the aggregation
function. Similarly, our filtered search results suggest that
practitioners can induce stability through sufficiently large
penalties, even when the underlying embeddings are intrinsically
high-dimensional.

3.3
Multi-Vector Search

The first retrieval setting we consider in this work is multi-vector search, where each query and document may be represented as
sets
of vectors under a chosen set-similarity function. This style of vector retrieval first gained traction with the celebrated ColBERT paper
(
Khattab & Zaharia 2020
)
. Since the introduction of ColBERT, multiple works have studied how to accelerate of multi-vector search algorithms, including PLAID
(
Santhanam et al. 2022
)
, DESSERT
(
Engels et al. 2023
)
, and MUVERA
(
Jayaram et al. 2024
)
. In addition,
Gollapudi et al. 2025
demonstrate that multi-vector search under the commonly-used Chamfer distance
(
Barrow et al. 1977
)
can be integrated into the existing graph-based DiskANN index
(
Jayaram Subramanya et al. 2019
)
originally conceived for single-vector search. Furthermore,
Faysse et al.
extend the multi-vector modeling paradigm pioneered by ColBERT beyond text to multimodal vision-language retrieval. However, despite the tremendous research interest in multi-vector search, we note that no prior work has studied how the use of vector sets relates to the curse of dimensionality. We address this gap in the literature.

3.4
Filtered Similarity Search

With the advent of vector databases in retrieval applications, there has also been a surge of interest in augmenting traditional near-neighbor seach indexes with the capability of filtering points according to additional metadata. For instance, an e-commerce search service might want to return relevant near-neighbor results subject to additional attributes associated to each point such as “free shipping eligible.” Consequently, a number of works have proposed techniques for augmenting traditional vector search algorithms with filtering capabilities, such as NHQ
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
, Airship
(
Zhao et al. 2022
)
, Filtered DiskANN
(
Gollapudi et al. 2023
)
, Acorn
(
Patel et al. 2024
)
, CAPS
(
Gupta et al. 2023
)
, IVF
2

Landrum et al. 2025
, and approximate window search
(
Engels et al. 2024
)
.

A unifying thread present in many of these approaches is the introduction of a
penalty function
to augment the standard vector distance metric in the case where a given point does not match the query filter predicate. We adopt this penalty function model of filtered similarity search in our theoretical stability analysis. Ultimately, we find that suitably large penalty values provides a sufficient condition for vector search stability.

3.5
Sparse Embedding Search

We conclude this section by turning our attention to a third form of embedding-based retrieval that has recently gained significant interest, namely sparse vector search. A sparse vector is one which is typically extremely high-dimensional (at least tens of thousands of components) but with only a small number of non-zero entries. In fact, sparse vectors predate the recent advent of neural representation learning as traditional keyword retrieval via inverted indexes
(
Schütze et al. 2008
)
can also be viewed as sparse vector search. Recently, sparse vectors have seen a renewal of interest in the form of
learned sparse embeddings
as pioneered by the influential SPLADE
(
Formal et al. 2021b
;
Formal et al. 2021a
)
family of models that combine the generalization power of neural representations with the interpretability properties of sparse vectors. Introduced in 2024, the Seismic inverted index-based approach
(
Bruch et al. 2024
)
remains the current state-of-the-art for efficient and scalable sparse vector search. The key insight driving the design of Seismic is a novel observation that learned sparse embeddings, such as SPLADE, exhibit a property called concentration of importance, which states that the total
\ell_{1}
mass of the sparse vector is primarily determined by a small subset of the non-zero coordinates. While the Seismic authors leverage this new concentration of importance observation to design an efficient indexing scheme, they do not consider the question of why learned sparse embedding search succeeds in spite of the extremely high vector dimensionalities. We take up that question in this paper and, inspired by this prior work, formalize the notion of concentration of importance. Although it turns out to not be sufficient for sparse vector search stability, we show that, under the additional assumption of
overlap of importance
and some mild technical assumptions, we can prove stability.

5
Stability of Multi-vector Search

We now turn our attention to the stability of multi-vector search. We begin by introducing a formal mathematical model of multi-vector search including a new, analogous definition of stability in this setting. We then proceed to our main theorem of this section identifying a sufficient condition for such stability to exist. In summary, we make the following contributions in this section:

1.

We introduce formal definitions for multi-vector search and multi-vector stability.

2.

We generalize the theorems of
(
Beyer et al. 1999
)
and
(
Durrant & Kabán 2009
)
and prove that a multi-vector search instance is stable if and only if the relative variance over the associated set aggregation distance function is positive.

3.

We prove that multi-vector search with Chamfer distance is stable provided that the induced single-vector search problem (a near neighbor search instance over the singleton vectors) is stable. We further give a counterexample showing multi-vector search with average pooling may not be stable even if the induced single-vector search instance is stable. Finally, we also validate our results experimentally.

5.1
Theoretical Model

Definition 5.1

(Multi-Vector Search Problem)
.

A multi-vector search problem is specified by a 4-tuple
(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{D},\delta,\mathrm{Agg})
, where:

•

\mathcal{Q}
is a collection of
query sets
. Each query
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
is a finite set of vectors.

•

\mathcal{D}
is a database of
document sets
. Each document
D\in\mathcal{D}
is a finite set of vectors.

•

\delta:\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is a
primitive distance metric
between individual vectors.

•

\mathrm{Agg}
is a
set aggregation function
that computes the distance between a query set
Q
and a document set
D
, based on the primitive distances between their constituent vectors.

For a given query
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
, the goal is to find:

\arg\min_{D\in\mathcal{D}}\mathrm{Agg}(Q,D)

Below, we define two concrete aggregation functions that we consider in this work.

Definition 5.2
.

For two finite vector sets
A
and
B
where
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and a vector distance function
\delta
, the
Chamfer Distance

(
Barrow et al. 1977
)
is defined as

\mathrm{Chamfer}(A,B)=\sum_{a\in A}\min_{b\in B}\delta(a,b)

Definition 5.3
.

For two finite vector sets
A
and
B
where
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and a vector distance function
\delta
,
Average Pooling
aggregation is defined as

\mathrm{AvgPool}(A,B)=\frac{1}{|A|\cdot|B|}\sum_{a\in A}\sum_{b\in B}\delta(a,b)

Now, we will define a new notion of stability in the multi-vector search setting.

Definition 5.4

(Multi-Vector Search Stability)
.

A multi-vector search problem is unstable if for all
\epsilon>0

\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathop{\Pr}\limits_{Q\sim\mathcal{Q}}\left[\max_{D\in\mathcal{D}}\{\mathrm{Agg}(Q,D\}\leq(1+\epsilon)\min_{D\in\mathcal{D}}\{\mathrm{Agg}(Q,D)\}\right]=1

With this definition in hand, we can now state our first theorem of this section generalizing the results of
(
Beyer et al. 1999
)
and
(
Durrant & Kabán 2009
)
to the multi-vector setting.

Theorem 5.5
.

A multi-vector search instance is stable if and only if

\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{\mathrm{Var}\left[\mathrm{Agg}(Q,D)\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{Agg}(Q,D)\right]^{2}}\neq 0

Proof sketch.

This result follows directly from the original arguments of
Beyer et al. 1999
and
Durrant & Kabán 2009
. In particular, the original proofs only need to assume that the distance metric is a non-negative scalar random variable. Since, by definition, a valid set aggregation function also meets these criteria, the result is immediate. For completeness, we provide a full proof in Appendix
A
which is a synthesis of the proofs presented in the original papers of
Beyer et al. 1999
and
Durrant & Kabán 2009
.
∎

With this extension of stability to the multi-vector setting in hand, we will now define a procedure for constructing a single-vector search instance from a multi-vector problem.

Definition 5.6

(Induced Single-Vector Search Problem)
.

Given a multi-vector search instance
(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{D},\delta,\mathrm{Agg})
, we define the
induced
single-vector search problem
(Q^{\prime},D^{\prime},\delta)
as follows

•

Q^{\prime}=\bigcup_{Q_{i}\in\mathcal{Q}}Q_{i}
is the set of unique query vectors, across all multi-vector query sets.

•

D^{\prime}=\bigcup_{D_{i}\in\mathcal{D}}D_{i}
is the set of unique document components, across all documents.

•

The distance metric
\delta
is the primitive distance function of
\mathrm{Agg}
.

5.2
Theoretical Results

From the previous definitions, we can now establish conditions for the stability of multi-vector search under Chamfer distance via the stability of the induced single vector search instance. The key idea behind our argument is to leverage the structure of the Chamfer distance, which, in essence, solves multiple scaled-down near neighbor search problems.

Before stating our main theorem of this section, we will need to define one additional property we call
strong stability
.

Definition 5.7

(Strong Stability)
.

A near-neighbor search problem
(Q,D,\delta)
is
c
-strongly stable for some constant
c>1
if
\frac{\mathrm{DMAX}}{\mathrm{DMIN}}>c
almost surely.

Intuitively, strong stability asserts that a near neighbor problem is not only stable but also stable with a gap. We will need this stronger property when analyzing the stability of multi-vector search because, even if the induced single-vector search problem is stable, the organization of document vectors into sets might contract the maximum distance gap seen in the single vector search problem. Strong stability ensures that, as long as the vector sets obey a certain mild non-degeneracy condition, we retain stability even if the stability gap contracts.

We will now proceed to our main results on the stability of multi-vector search. We will begin by proving a lemma establishing stability in the case where the query sets are singletons (each containing only one vector) while the document sets may be larger in size. Then, we will use this result to prove stability in the general case of both query and document sets with multiple elements.

Lemma 5.8

(Chamfer Stability with Singleton Query Sets)
.

Let
(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{D},\delta,\mathrm{Chamfer})
be a multi-vector search problem where
|Q_{i}|=1
for each query set
Q_{i}\in\mathcal{Q}
. Let
(Q^{\prime},D^{\prime},\delta)
denote the corresponding induced single-vector instance .
Let
q\sim Q^{\prime}
and let
\{D_{1},\dots,D_{n}\}
be the document sets in
\mathcal{D}
. Assume the induced single-vector search problem is
c
-strongly stable.

If the
non-degeneracy condition

c\cdot\max_{k}\min_{d\in D_{k}}\delta(q,d)\;\geq\;\max_{k}\max_{d\in D_{k}}\delta(q,d)

holds, meaning that there exists at least one document set whose vectors are all sufficiently far from a given query vector, then the multi-vector search instance is stable.

Theorem 5.9

(Multi-Vector Stability with Chamfer Distance)
.

Let
(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{D},\delta,\mathrm{Chamfer})
be a multi-vector search problem where
|Q|=k
for some constant
k\geq 1
for all
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
. Let
A_{i}=\min_{d\in D}\delta(q_{i},d)
be the nearest-neighbor distance for an individual query vector
q_{i}\in Q
. The problem is stable if:

1.

The induced single-vector search instance is
c
-strongly stable.

2.

The document sets satisfy non-degeneracy as defined in Lemma
5.8
.

3.

\sum_{i<j}\mathrm{Cov}(A_{i},A_{j})\geq 0
.

To experimentally validate Theorem

6
Stability of Filtered Vector Search

We now move to the second major vector-based retrieval setting we analyze in this paper, namely filtered similarity search. As in the previous section, we will begin by defining the formal mathematical model for filtered vector search we consider and then introduce our main theorem. In summary, we make the following contributions in this section:

1.

We present a formal mathematical model of
penalty-based
filtered vector search, which captures several common practical instantiations of this problem.

2.

We prove that filtered near neighbor search in the penalty-based model is stable if the distance penalty function for a filter mismatch is set sufficiently high.

3.

We demonstrate, both theoretically and experimentally, that filtered search with sufficiently high penalties can be stable even if the underlying standard near neighbor search problem is
unstable
.

6.1
Theoretical Model

We begin with a formal definition of the filtered (or hybrid) near neighbor search problem which combines traditional vector similarity retrieval with attribute matching.

Definition 6.1

(Filtered Near-Neighbor Search)
.

A
filtered near-neighbor search problem
is a 6-tuple
(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{D},\delta,A_{Q},A_{D},f)
where:

•

\mathcal{Q}=\{(q_{1},A_{q_{1}}),(q_{2},A_{q_{2}}),\dots\}
is a set of query tuples where each
q_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and each
A_{q_{i}}\subseteq A
is a set of
query attributes
.

•

\mathcal{D}=\{(d_{1},A_{d_{1}}),(d_{2},A_{d_{2}}),\dots\}
is the set of database tuples where each
d_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and each
A_{d_{i}}\subseteq A_{D}
is a set of
document attributes
.

•

\delta:\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is a standard vector distance metric.

•

f\colon A_{Q}\times A_{D}\to\{0,1\}
is a
filter function
that outputs 1 if the query and document attributes match and 0 otherwise.

For a given query
(q,A_{q})
, we compute

\argmin_{(d,A_{d})\in\mathcal{D}}\delta(q,d)\quad\text{subject to }f(A_{q},A_{d})=1

Remark 6.2
.

This definition of filtered near neighbor search places no restriction on how the filter function
f
operates. For instance, if the query and document attributes are discrete, then a natural definition of
f
might be to output 1 if
A_{q}\subseteq A_{d}
and 0 otherwise. Furthermore, our definition extends to other filtered search settings. In the
approximate window search
problem,
(
Engels et al. 2024
)
, each document is associated with a numeric label, and each query aims to find the nearest documents within arbitrary label ranges. In this case, we can model
A_{q}\subset\mathbb{R}
as a continuous range of real numbers and
A_{d}\in\mathbb{R}
as a scalar value. The filter function
f
in this setting would output 1 if
A_{d}\in A_{q}
and 0 otherwise.

In this paper, we will focus our analysis on
penalty-based
filtered near-neighbor search algorithms that augment the standard geometric vector distance metric with an additive penalty for points that do not satisfy the query filter. These functions are also referred to as
fusion
distances in the literature
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
. We will define the
\emph{penalized}
distance function as
\delta^{\prime}(q,d)=\delta(q,d)+\alpha(1-f(A_{q},A_{d}))
so that the distance metric incurs an additive scalar penalty of
\alpha
if and only if
f(A_{q},A_{d})=0
meaning the query and document attributes do not match.

We note that this formal model of fused distance functions captures nearly all filtered vector search algorithms in the literature. Some approaches, such as NHQ
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
and CAPS
(
Gupta et al. 2023
)
set the penalty value via heuristics or parameter tuning. On the other hand, many approaches in the literature, such as Acorn
(
Patel et al. 2024
)
, Filtered DiskANN
(
Gollapudi et al. 2023
)
, and window search
Engels et al. 2024
never consider points that violate the query filter predicate throughout the entire search process. These algorithms can also be modeled by our framework by setting
\alpha
to be infinite. Thus, we emphasize that our formalization of filtered vector search closely aligns with nearly all algorithmic approaches in the literature.

6.2
Theoretical Results

We now present our main theorem of this section, which proves that we can achieve stability in the filtered search setting by choosing a sufficiently large distance penalty value. In this theoretical analysis, we assume that the attributes are drawn from some distribution such that the filter mismatch probability is at most some constant
p_{\max}
where
0<p_{\max}<1
.

Theorem 6.3

(Stability of Filtered Search)
.

Consider the filtered near neighbor search setting with penalized distance functions. Suppose that the filter mismatch probability for any query-document pair is bounded by
0<p_{\max}<1
for some constant
p_{\max}
. If the penalty value satisfies
\alpha>\frac{2\Delta}{1-p_{\max}}
where
\Delta=\max_{q,d}\delta(q,d)
, then the filtered search problem is stable.

We defer the full proof of this theorem to Appendix
B
.

Remark 6.4
.

Theorem
6.3
holds regardless of whether the underlying vector search problem (without constraints) is stable or unstable since our proof handles both cases directly. The latter case is particularly interesting as it implies that, under the assumptions of Theorem
6.3
, the addition of filters can transform an
unstable
vector search problem into a
stable
one.

Remark 6.5
.

This model of filtered vector search is quite general and can encompass an arbitrary number of conjunctions and disjunctions of constraints simply by defining the attribute sets
A_{q}
and
A_{d}
accordingly. We only require that the constant
p_{\max}
remains non-trivial.

6.3
Experiments

In this section, we empirically validate Theorem
6.3
on a family synthetic vector search problems with 10,000 documents and 100 queries and varying dimensionality. We unit normalize all vectors so that the constant
\Delta\geq 2
and set the parameter
p_{\max}
to be
0.5
which satisfies the conditions of the theorem. Thus, as predicted by our theory, a sufficient condition for stability based on these parameters is setting the penalty value
\alpha
to be greater than
8
. For this experiment, we start with a base vector search problem that is unstable and we consider three different choices for the penalty value: in the first case we take the penalty to be 8.1, namely slightly above the minimum threshold for stability; secondly, we set the penalty value to be smaller than the threshold suggested by Theorem
6.3
and have it decay with the dimensionality; finally, we consider the case of a penalty value of 0. As suggested by the proof of Theorem
6.3
, we want to engineer filter mismatches such that there is a negative correlation between the distance function and attribute matches. We simulate this phenomenon by setting filter matches and mismatches based on each document’s average distance to all queries; documents that are close to queries on average become filter mismatches while documents further away are matches.

As predicted by Theorem
6.3
, we see that the large penalty value above the theorem’s threshold achieves stability. Moreover, the small, decreasing penalty value causes the problem to not satisfy the conditions of the theorem demonstrating that there exist non-zero penalty values that result in instability. Furthermore, when we set the penalty value to 0, the problem reduces to standard near-neighbor search where we observe instability by construction. Thus, this experiment validates that with a sufficiently high penalty value as prescribed by Theorem
6.3
, an unstable near neighbor search problem becomes stable with the addition of filters.

Stability of Sparse Vector Search

We now turn our attention to the final vector search setting we study in this work: sparse embedding retrieval. We make the following contributions in this section:

1.

We introduce a mathematical model for sparse vector retrieval, including a novel definition of the
concentration of importance
property first informally described by
(
Bruch et al. 2024
)
.

2.

We prove that concentration of importance, coupled with a new property we call
overlap of importance
and an additional mild assumption, implies sparse vector search stability.

3.

We validate our theorem empirically, showing that
concentration of importance
and
overlap of importance
together provide stability, but one property without the other remains insufficient.

7.1
Theoretical Model

In our setup, a sparse vector
d\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is characterized by its support
S_{d}\coloneqq\{i\in[m]\mid d_{i}\neq 0\}
. While in many sparse retrieval settings
|S_{d}|\ll m
(often
|S_{d}|=O(\log m))
, for full generality we impose no growth rate on
|S_{d}|
and only rely on the concentration and overlap of importance to establish stability. In other words, we do not restrict ourselves to any particular definition of sparsity. Our main theorem is more general in nature and may apply to certain dense vector settings as well. Nevertheless, the primary application of this theorem remains in the sparse setting.

Definition 7.1

(Concentration of Importance (CoI))
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}
and let
d_{(1)}\geq d_{(2)}\geq\dots\geq d_{(m)}
be its coordinates sorted by value and denote by
||d||_{p}
its
\ell_{p}
norm. For a constant
\kappa\ll m
where
\kappa
independent of
m
(i.e., not growing with the dimension), define

C_{d}(\kappa)\coloneqq\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{\kappa}|d_{(i)}|^{p}}{||d||_{p}^{p}}\in[0,1]

Let
Q,D\subset\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}
. We say that the pair
(Q,D)
satisfies concentration of importance with parameters
(\kappa,\alpha,R,\rho)
if

(a)

C_{q}(\kappa)\geq\alpha
for all
q\in Q
.

(b)

\mathop{\Pr}_{d\sim D}\left[C_{d}(R\kappa)\geq\alpha\right]\geq\rho
.

(c)

R\kappa\ll m
, where
R\geq 1
is fixed and independent of
m
.

Remark 7.2
.

Restricting to nonnegative vectors entails no loss of generality.
For any
d\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, define
\phi(d)=(d^{+},d^{-})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{2m}
with

d_{i}^{+}=\max\{d_{i},0\}
and
d_{i}^{-}=\max\{-d_{i},0\}
. For
p\geq 1,
we note that
||\phi(d)||_{p}=||d||_{p}
and
2^{-(1-1/p)}||x-y||_{p}\leq||\phi(x)-\phi(y)||_{p}\leq 2^{1/p}||x-y||_{p}
. Thus,
\phi
is bi-Lipschitz. We provide a full proof of this claim in Lemma
C.1
. Our results hold for signed vectors after adjusting constants by these fixed factors. We state our results for non-negative vectors for notational simplicity.

Definition 7.3

(Overlap of Importance)
.

Fix
p\geq 1
and
0<\kappa\ll m
. For
d\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}
, let
T_{d}
be the indices of the
top-
\kappa
coordinates of
d
by magnitude. We say a
(Q,D,\delta)
sparse vector
search problem exhibits
overlap of importance
if there exist constants

\gamma>0
and
\pi\in(0,1)
such that, for
q\in Q
and
d\in D
with

\|q\|_{p}=\|d\|_{p}=1
,

\Pr\!\Bigg[\,\sum_{i\in T_{q}\cap T_{d}}\min\{\,q_{i}^{\,p},\,d_{i}^{\,p}\,\}\ \geq\ \gamma\,\Bigg]\ \geq\ \pi.

Intuitively, the overlap condition necessitates that with non-vanishing probability, the two vectors share a set of coordinates that cumulatively carry at least
\gamma
in both.

7.2
Theoretical Results

Given the sparse vector search model introduced in the previous section, we now state our main result via concentration and overlap of importance.

Theorem 7.4
.

Let
(Q,D,||\mathord{\cdot}||_{p})
be a near neighbor search problem where
Q,D\subset\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}
with
||q||_{p}=||d||_{p}=1
for all
q\in Q,d\in D
and
p\geq 1.
Assume that

(a)

(Q,D)
satisfy
\text{CoI}(\kappa_{q},\alpha,R,\rho)
as stated in Definition
7.1
.

(b)

(Q,D)
satisfy overlap of importance with parameters
(\gamma,\pi)
as stated in Definition
7.3
.

(c)

There exists a constant
\tau
such that
\Pr[i\in T_{x}]\leq\frac{\tau}{m}
for any
i\in[m]
and
x\in\{d,q\}
.

Under assumptions
(a)
–
(c)
above, define
X\coloneqq(2-2\gamma)^{1/p}
and
Y\coloneqq\frac{\rho 2^{1/p}}{1-\pi}\left(\alpha^{1/p}-(1-\alpha)^{1/p}\right)
and assume that
Y>X
. Then,

\liminf_{m\to\infty}\mathrm{RelVar}_{m}\geq\frac{\pi}{4}(Y-X)^{2}>0.

Hence, the sparse vector search problem is stable by the relative variance criterion.

The above theorem is, to our knowledge, the first to prove a sufficient condition for stability in the context of sparse vector search, which is a particularly interesting setting given the high dimensionalities of learned sparse embeddings, such as SPLADE, used in practice.

We first point out that we state the theorem using
\liminf\mathrm{RelVar}_{m}
instead of
\lim\mathrm{RelVar}_{m}
. This is convenient because bounding
\liminf
away from zero is sufficient to prove stability without needing to prove that the relative variance terms converge, which may or may not happen.

The theorem shows that sparse vector search is stable whenever
X=(2-2\gamma)^{1/p}
is less than
Y=\frac{\rho 2^{1/p}}{1-\pi}\left(\alpha^{1/p}-(1-\alpha)^{1/p}\right)
. These two constants represent different geometric configurations for the vectors
q
and
d
. The quantity
X
is an upper bound on the expected distance when
q
and
d
overlap substantially in their head coordinates. In this case, the vectors partially align and the distance shrinks. In contrast,
Y
represents the expected distance in the opposite configuration where
T_{q}
and
T_{d}
are disjoint. Here the vectors place their largest coordinates in opposite directions, and hence the distance grows.

Equality
X=Y
corresponds to a single algebraic relationship between parameters
\alpha,\gamma
and
\pi
where the two geometric configurations happen to produce the exact same distance. Geometrically, this means that placing mass on overlapping head coordinates reduces the distance by precisely the same amount that shifting the mass into disjoint coordinates would increase it. This cancellation requires the parameters
(\alpha,\gamma,\pi)
to satisfy a rigid relation and therefore occurs on a set of measure zero on the parameter space. Everywhere else for all practically relevant parameterizations, the two configurations remain geometrically distinct, which ensures that the distribution of the distances does not collapse in the limit, which we require for stability.

We empirically validate the assumptions of Theorem
7.4
on

Lassance et al. 2024
embeddings across several benchmark datasets. We verify concentration of importance, overlap of importance, and finally check the gap condition
Y>X
required by the theorem.

Concentration of importance.

We fix a query head size
\kappa
and examine document head sizes of the form
R\kappa
where
R
is a small integer at most 8. For each candidate
R,
we estimate
\rho=\Pr[C_{d}(R\kappa)\geq\alpha]
over a random sample of documents and select the smallest value such that
\rho
exceeds a target level, which we set to
0.9
. The resulting
(\kappa,R,\rho)
values are reported in Table

Overlap of importance.

For random query-document pairs we compute the overlap statistic

S=\sum_{i\in T_{q}\cap T_{d}}\min\{q_{i}^{p},d_{i}^{p}\}

Empirically we measure
\pi_{\max}=\Pr[S>0]
, the fraction of pairs with strictly positive overlap. We set
\gamma
to be the
(1-\pi_{\max})
empirical quantile of
S,
i.e., the smallest positive overlap observed. The resulting tail mass is
\hat{\pi}=\Pr[S\geq\gamma]
. This guarantees that
\gamma>0
while using the largest feasible overlap tail mass supported by the data.

Since the theorem requires

8
Conclusion

Modern vector search, driven by advances in neural representation learning, has achieved remarkable success in enabling information retrieval across a variety of modalities. However, the high dimensions of these embeddings suggest that vector retrieval over neural representations should suffer from the curse of dimensionality where the distances between all points tends to converge to a uniform value, thereby rendering approximate search algorithms as ineffective. This paper investigates why modern sublinear vector retrieval algorithms succeed in spite of the curse of dimensionality by building off foundational results on near-neighbor search
stability
. We introduce formal models for three widely popular variants of vector retrieval, namely multi-vector search, filtered vector search, and sparse vector search, and identify sufficient conditions for which near-neighbor search remains stable in these settings. Crucially, we note that the mathematical techniques we present in this work are general in nature and can very likely be applied to other vector search settings as well. We believe this may constitute an exciting direction for future work.

Appendix A
Stability of Multi-Vector Search

Theorem
5.5

A multi-vector search instance is stable if and only if

\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{\mathrm{Var}\left[\mathrm{Agg}(Q,X)\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{Agg}(Q,X)\right]^{2}}\neq 0

Proof.

This result follows directly from the prior proofs of
(
Beyer et al. 1999
)
and
(
Durrant & Kabán 2009
)
since the original proofs require only that the distance metric is non-negative. We provide a full, self-contained argument here for completeness.

As a matter of notation, let
X_{m}=\mathrm{Agg}(Q,D)
be the random variable for the aggregated distance in an
m
-dimensional space. Let its expected value be
\mathbb{E}[X_{m}]
and its variance be
\text{Var}[X_{m}]
. The database consists of
n
samples of this random variable,
\{X_{m,1},\dots,X_{m,n}\}
. Let
\mathrm{DMIN}_{m}=\min_{i}X_{m,i}
and
\mathrm{DMAX}_{m}=\max_{i}X_{m,i}
.

\implies
This direction follows the argument from
(
Beyer et al. 1999
)
. We will show that if
\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{\text{Var}[X_{m}]}{\mathbb{E}[X_{m}]^{2}}=0
, then the multi-vector search problem is unstable. Define a normalized random variable
Y_{m}=\frac{X_{m}}{\mathbb{E}[X_{m}]}
. Its expectation is
\mathbb{E}[Y_{m}]=1
and its variance is
\text{Var}[Y_{m}]=\frac{\text{Var}[X_{m}]}{\mathbb{E}[X_{m}]^{2}}
The condition is thus equivalent to
\lim_{m\to\infty}\text{Var}[Y_{m}]=0
. Since
\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[Y_{m}]=1
and
\lim_{m\to\infty}\text{Var}[Y_{m}]=0
, the random variable
Y_{m}
converges in probability to 1. This is denoted
Y_{m}\xrightarrow{p}1
. Let
\{Y_{m,1},\dots,Y_{m,n}\}
be the normalized distances for the
n
items in the database. Since
\min()
and
\max()
are continuous functions, Slutsky’s Theorem implies that
\min(Y_{m,i})\xrightarrow{p}1
and
\max(Y_{m,i})\xrightarrow{p}1
. The ratio
\frac{\mathrm{DMAX}_{m}}{\mathrm{DMIN}_{m}}=\frac{\max_{i}Y_{m,i}}{\min_{i}Y_{m,i}}
.Since both the numerator and denominator converge in probability to 1, their ratio also converges in probability to 1. By definition,
\frac{\mathrm{DMAX}_{m}}{\mathrm{DMIN}_{m}}\xrightarrow{p}1
means that for any
\epsilon>0
, we have
\lim_{m\to\infty}P\left(\left|\frac{\mathrm{DMAX}_{m}}{\mathrm{DMIN}_{m}}-1\right|\leq\epsilon\right)=1
. Since
\mathrm{DMAX}_{m}\geq\mathrm{DMIN}_{m}
, this is equivalent to
\lim_{m\to\infty}P(\mathrm{DMAX}_{m}\leq(1+\epsilon)\mathrm{DMIN}_{m})=1
. This is precisely the definition of an unstable search problem.

\impliedby
This converse result follows the same proof presented by
(
Durrant & Kabán 2009
)
. We proceed by proving the contrapositive: If the problem is unstable, then the relative variance must be zero. Assume the problem is unstable. This means for any
\epsilon>0
,
\lim_{m\to\infty}P(\mathrm{DMAX}_{m}\leq(1+\epsilon)\mathrm{DMIN}_{m})=1
. This directly implies that the ratio of distances converges in probability to 1:
\frac{\mathrm{DMAX}_{m}}{\mathrm{DMIN}_{m}}\xrightarrow{p}1
. For a sufficiently large database, we can assume that the theoretical expectation
\mathbb{E}[X_{m}]
lies between the sample minimum and maximum. This allows us to bound the normalized random variable
Y_{m}=\frac{X_{m}}{\mathbb{E}[X_{m}]}
for any sample as follows:

\frac{\mathrm{DMIN}_{m}}{\mathrm{DMAX}_{m}}\leq\frac{X_{m,i}}{\mathbb{E}[X_{m}]}\leq\frac{\mathrm{DMAX}_{m}}{\mathrm{DMIN}_{m}}

From the instability assumption, we know both the lower bound
(\frac{\mathrm{DMIN}_{m}}{\mathrm{DMAX}_{m}})
and the upper bound
(\frac{\mathrm{DMAX}_{m}}{\mathrm{DMIN}_{m}})
converge in probability to 1 By the Squeeze Theorem, the random variable
Y_{m}
must also converge in probability to 1. A random variable that converges in probability to a constant must have a variance that converges to zero. Therefore:

\lim_{m\to\infty}\text{Var}[Y_{m}]=\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{\text{Var}[X_{m}]}{\mathbb{E}[X_{m}]^{2}}=0

This completes the proof of the contrapositive.
∎

Lemma
5.8
[Chamfer Stability with Singleton Query Sets]
Let
(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{D},\delta,\mathrm{Chamfer})
be a multi-vector search problem where
|Q_{i}|=1
for each query set
Q_{i}\in\mathcal{Q}
. Let
(Q^{\prime},D^{\prime},\delta)
denote the corresponding induced single-vector instance .
Let
q\sim Q^{\prime}
and let
\{D_{1},\dots,D_{n}\}
be the document sets in
\mathcal{D}
.

Assume the induced single-vector search problem is
c
-strongly stable

If the
non-degeneracy condition

c\cdot\max_{k}\min_{d\in D_{k}}\delta(q,d)\;\geq\;\max_{k}\max_{d\in D_{k}}\delta(q,d)

holds, meaning that there exists at least one document set whose vectors are all sufficiently far from a given query vector, then the multi-vector search instance is stable.

Proof.

Let

\mathrm{DMIN}=\min_{d\in D^{\prime}}\delta(q,d),\quad\mathrm{DMAX}=\max_{d\in D^{\prime}}\delta(q,d).

Also define

\mathrm{DMIN}^{\prime}=\min_{k}\min_{d\in D_{k}}\delta(q,d)=\mathrm{DMIN},\quad\mathrm{DMAX}^{\prime}=\max_{k}\min_{d\in D_{k}}\delta(q,d).

We thus have

\frac{\mathrm{DMAX}^{\prime}}{\mathrm{DMIN}^{\prime}}=\frac{\max_{k}\min_{d\in D_{k}}\delta(q,d)}{\mathrm{DMIN}}\geq\frac{1}{c}\frac{\mathrm{DMAX}}{\mathrm{DMIN}}>1.

where the last inequality follows from strong stability. Thus, since
\frac{\mathrm{DMAX}^{\prime}}{\mathrm{DMIN}^{\prime}}>1
almost surely, it follows that
\frac{\mathrm{DMAX}^{\prime}}{\mathrm{DMIN}^{\prime}}\not\to_{p}1
, completing the proof.
∎

Lemma A.1

(Dan’s Favorite Inequality
(
Spielman 2018
)
)
.

Let
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
and
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}
be positive numbers. Then

\min_{i}\frac{a_{i}}{b_{i}}\leq\frac{\sum_{i}a_{i}}{\sum_{i}b_{i}}\leq\max_{i}\frac{a_{i}}{b_{i}}

Theorem
5.9

Let
(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{D},\delta,\mathrm{Chamfer})
be a multi-vector search problem where
|Q|=k
for some constant
k\geq 1
for all
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
. Let
A_{i}=\min_{d\in D}\delta(q_{i},d)
be the nearest-neighbor distance for an individual query vector
q_{i}\in Q
. The problem is stable if:

1.

The induced single-vector search instance is
c
-strongly stable

2.

The document sets satisfy non-degeneracy as defined in Lemma
5.8

3.

\sum_{i<j}\mathrm{Cov}(A_{i},A_{j})\geq 0

Proof.

We will show that

\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{\mathrm{Var}[\mathrm{Chamfer}(Q,D)]}{\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Chamfer}(Q,D)]^{2}}>0

which implies the desired result.

To begin, we observe that

\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{\mathrm{Var}[\mathrm{Chamfer}(Q,D)]}{\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Chamfer}(Q,D)]^{2}}=\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{\mathrm{Var}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}\Big)}{\Big(\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}\right]\Big)^{2}}=\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{\mathrm{Var}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}\Big)}{\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}[A_{i}]\Big)^{2}}

where the second equality follows from the linearity of expectation.

We note that we can lower bound the numerator of the above expression as follows:

\mathrm{Var}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}\Big)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathrm{Var}[A_{i}]+2\sum_{i<j}\mathrm{Cov}(A_{i},A_{j})\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathrm{Var}[A_{i}]

where the inequality follows from the third assumption in the theorem statement.

Furthermore, we can upper bound the term in the denominator via the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}[A_{i}]\Big)^{2}\leq k\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}[A_{i}]^{2}.

Putting these bounds together, we have

\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{\mathrm{Var}[\sum_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}]}{\big(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}[A_{i}]\big)^{2}}\geq\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathrm{Var}[A_{i}]}{k\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}[A_{i}]^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{k}\lim_{m\to\infty}\min_{i}\frac{\mathrm{Var}[A_{i}]}{\mathbb{E}[A_{i}]^{2}}>0

where the last two inequalities follow from applying Lemma
A.1
and Lemma
5.8
respectively. This completes the proof.

∎

Remark A.2
.

Stability follows from applying Dan’s Favorite Inequality (Lemma
A.1
) to reduce the general multi-vector search problem to multi-vector search over singleton query sets, namely the setting in Lemma
5.8
, at the cost of an
\frac{1}{k}
overhead factor. The
1/k
factor suggests that stability weakens as the query set size grows, which is also consistent with empirical observations.

Remark A.3
.

A natural question to ask based on the proof of Theorem
5.9
is where we utilize the fact that our aggregation function is the Chamfer distance. In fact, the properties of Chamfer distance are critical to the argument, but the application is subtle. In particular, we rely on the minimum operator of the Chamfer distance in Lemma
5.8
to connect the stability of the induced single vector search problem to the vector set setting. Since we apply Lemma
5.8
to prove Theorem
5.9
, our main theorem in turn also crucially relies on the Chamfer distance.

Appendix B
Stability of Filtered Vector Search

Theorem
6.3

Consider the filtered near neighbor search setting with penalized distance functions. Suppose that the filter mismatch probability for any query and document is bounded by
0<p_{\max}<1
for some constant
p_{\max}
. If the penalty value satisfies

\alpha>\frac{2\Delta}{1-p_{\max}}

where
\Delta=\max_{q\in Q,d\in D}\delta(q,d)
, then the filtered search problem is stable.

Proof.

As a shorthand, let
\delta=\delta(q,d)
, let the indicator variable
1_{c}=\mathbf{1}{\{f(A_{q},A_{d})=0\}}
denote a filter mismatch, let and
p_{c}=\mathbb{E}[1_{c}]
. We analyze the limiting behavior of the Relative Variance of
\delta^{\prime}
, denoted
\mathrm{RelVar}^{\prime}
.

The expression for
\mathrm{RelVar}^{\prime}
can be derived as follows. First, the variance and expectation of
\delta^{\prime}
are:

\mathbb{E}[\delta^{\prime}]=\mathbb{E}[\delta]+\alpha p_{c}

\mathrm{Var}[\delta^{\prime}]=\mathrm{Var}[\delta]+\alpha^{2}\mathrm{Var}[1_{c}]+2\alpha\mathrm{Cov}(\delta,1_{c})

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathrm{RelVar}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{Var}[\delta^{\prime}]}{\mathbb{E}[\delta^{\prime}]^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{Var}[\delta]+\alpha^{2}\mathrm{Var}[1_{c}]+2\alpha\mathrm{Cov}(\delta,1_{c})}{(\mathbb{E}[\delta]+\alpha p_{c})^{2}}

Dividing both the numerator and the denominator by
\mathbb{E}[\delta]^{2}
then gives us

\displaystyle\mathrm{RelVar}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{\mathrm{Var}[\delta]}{\mathbb{E}[\delta]^{2}}+\frac{\alpha^{2}p_{c}(1-p_{c})}{\mathbb{E}[\delta]^{2}}+2\alpha\frac{\mathrm{Cov}(\delta,1_{c})}{\mathbb{E}[\delta]^{2}}}{(1+\frac{\alpha p_{c}}{\mathbb{E}[\delta]})^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\frac{\alpha^{2}p_{c}(1-p_{c})}{\delta_{\max}^{2}}+2\alpha\frac{\mathrm{Cov}(\delta,1_{c})}{\Delta\mathbb{E}[\delta]}}{(1+\frac{\alpha p_{c}}{\mathbb{E}[\delta]})^{2}}

where we use the facts that
\frac{\mathrm{Var}[d]}{\mathbb{E}[\delta]^{2}}\geq 0
and
\Delta\geq\mathbb{E}[\delta]

We next observe that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{Cov}(\delta,1_{c})}{\mathbb{E}[\delta]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\delta 1_{c}]-\mathbb{E}[\delta]\mathbb{E}[1_{c}]}{\mathbb{E}[\delta]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\delta 1_{c}]}{\mathbb{E}[\delta]}-\mathbb{E}[1_{c}]

\displaystyle\geq 0-p_{c}

where the last inequality results from the fact that both
\delta\geq 0
and
1_{c}\geq 0
.

Applying this bound to our previous expression gives us

\displaystyle\mathrm{RelVar}^{\prime}\geq\frac{\frac{\alpha^{2}p_{c}(1-p_{c})}{\delta_{\max}^{2}}+2\alpha\frac{\mathrm{Cov}(d,1_{c})}{\Delta\mathbb{E}[d]}}{(1+\frac{\alpha p_{c}}{\mathbb{E}[\delta]})^{2}}\geq\frac{\frac{\alpha^{2}p_{c}(1-p_{c})}{\Delta^{2}}-\frac{2\alpha p_{c}}{\Delta}}{(1+\frac{\alpha p_{c}}{\mathbb{E}[\delta]})^{2}}

Our goal is to show that this final expression is strictly positive, which is true if the following inequality holds

\frac{\alpha^{2}p_{c}(1-p_{c})}{\Delta^{2}}>\frac{2\alpha p_{c}}{\Delta}

which simplifies to
\alpha>\frac{2\Delta}{1-p_{c}}\geq\frac{2\Delta}{1-p_{\max}}

Thus, we can conclude that, if
\alpha>\frac{2\Delta}{1-p_{\max}}
, then

\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathrm{RelVar}^{\prime}>0

which completes the proof.
∎
