Uric acid alleviates the inflammatory response in LPS-induced BV2 cells and MPTP-induced PD mice by resisting ferroptosis through the Nrf2 signalling pathway
Authors: Dongmei Zhou, Tian Xiong, Bing Yang, Fanjie Liu, Xianglai Mo, Ting Chen, Peipei Lu, Xinghuan Liang, Li Li, Yingfen Qin, Decheng Lu, Feng Huang, Xi Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and ferroptosis are implicated in Parkinson’ s disease (PD) pathogenesis. Epidemiological studies suggest that elevated uric acid (UA) levels may reduce PD risk, but the precise molecular mechanisms involved remain unclear. In this study, we investigated the effects of UA in lipopolysaccharide (LPS) or MPP+-stimulated BV2 microglia and MPTP-induced PD mouse models.MethodsBV2 cells are recognized as a standardized and reproducible neural inflammatory cell model for mechanism exploration. The anti-ferroptosis and anti-inflammatory effects of UA were assessed in LPS or MPP+ stimulated BV2 microglia and in an MPTP-induced PD mouse model. Western blot, qPCR, ELISA, and immunofluorescence were used to analyse the expression of inflammasome-related markers. ROS, MDA, GSH, and Fe²⁺ levels were measured using testing kits, while mitochondrial ultrastructure was evaluated through transmission electron microscopy. PD-related markers were assessed by ethology and immunohistochemistry. The Nrf2 inhibitor ML385 was employed to validate pathway specificity.ResultsWe found that UA treatment reduced the expression of proinflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β), ROS production, lipid peroxidation, and intracellular Fe²⁺ in BV2 microglia while increasing the antioxidant capacity and preserving the mitochondrial ultrastructure. In MPTP-treated mice, UA improved motor performance, preserved dopaminergic neuron density in the substantia nigra, and reduced neuroinflammation. UA activated Nrf2 signalling and upregulated GPX4 expression, which were attenuated by ML385, confirming the Nrf2 dependence of these effects.ConclusionUA alleviates ferroptosis and neuroinflammation in LPS or MPP+ stimulated BV2 microglia and MPTP-induced PD mouse models through the activation of the Nrf2 signalling pathway.

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is one of the most common neurodegenerative disorders, and its incidence continues to rise with the ageing global population, which is characterised by the progressive degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta and the accumulation of intracytoplasmic Lewy bodies, which are primarily composed of α-synuclein [1]. In addition to classic motor symptoms such as bradykinesia, rigidity, and resting tremor, patients often experience cognitive decline, mood disturbances, and autonomic dysfunction, which could reduce patients’ independence and place a greater healthcare burden [2]. Mounting evidence has indicated that neuroinflammation plays a pivotal role in the progression of PD, with abnormal activation of microglia contributing to sustained neuronal damage and dysfunction [3,4].

Microglia, the resident immune cells in the central nervous system, play crucial roles in maintaining brain homoeostasis and responding to injury or infection [5]. Under pathological conditions, they can polarise into the proinflammatory M1 phenotype or into the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype [6]. In PD, microglial activation is skewed towards the M1 phenotype, contributing to chronic neuroinflammation and dopaminergic neuron loss [7]. Regulating microglial polarisation is therefore considered a promising therapeutic strategy [8]. Oxidative stress plays a critical role in PD pathogenesis [9]. In this context, endogenous molecules with antioxidant and immunomodulatory properties are particularly relevant. Among them, uric acid (UA), a product of purine metabolism, has garnered increasing interest for its potential neuroprotective effects. This compound reduces oxidative injury via scavenging reactive oxygen species, preventing lipid peroxidation, and binding metal ions [10]. These actions help limit DNA damage and preserve neuron viability [11]. In cellular, animal, and clinical models, urate has been shown to reduce neuronal injury by inhibiting oxidative and inflammatory responses [12–14], which also suppresses proinflammatory cytokine production in activated microglia, further contributing to neuroprotection [15].

Recent research has highlighted the role of ferroptosis in PD, a newly identified form of regulated cell death, characterised by iron accumulation and lipid peroxidation [16]. In addition, accumulating evidence from numerous studies has established ferroptosis as a critical contributing factor in a range of neurodegenerative disorders, including Alzheimer’ s disease, Huntington’ s disease, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), and multiple sclerosis [17,18]. This process is closely associated with oxidative stress and impaired antioxidant defences, which are also hallmarks observed in PD brains. Excessive iron deposition, particularly in the substantia nigra, can alter microglial gene expression, promoting a proinflammatory phenotype and increasing the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [19–21]. In turn, activated microglia exacerbate neuronal iron imbalance through the modulation of iron transport proteins [22]. Notably, microglia are more sensitive to iron-induced oxidative stress than are neurons or astrocytes, rendering them particularly susceptible to ferroptosis [23]. These interactions may create a vicious cycle between ferroptosis and inflammation reinforcement, thereby driving PD progression. However, the extent of current knowledge on this issue remains limited.

Evidence increasingly links ferroptosis to inflammatory signalling in the central nervous system. Studies show that inhibiting ferroptosis, using agents like ferrostatin-1 and liproxstatin-1 or targeting the GPX4/system Xc⁻ pathway, reduces pro-inflammatory mediators and microglia-driven neuroinflammation [24–26]. This inhibition also lessens LPS-induced inflammation and lowers key pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 [27]. These findings indicate that ferroptosis may enhance microglial inflammatory signalling, contributing to neuroinflammation in PD. Therefore, targeting ferroptosis could protect neurons from oxidative damage and reduce neuroimmune activation. Given this mechanistic interplay, targeting ferroptosis may provide a strategy to reduce neuroinflammation and slow PD progression.

Nuclear factor erythroid 2- related factor 2 (Nrf2) is a key transcription factor that regulates antioxidant responses and plays a protective role against ferroptosis by upregulating genes such as glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) [28]. UA has been shown to activate the Nrf2 pathway and reduce oxidative stress in various models of neurodegeneration [29,30]. Nevertheless, the precise molecular mechanisms through which UA influences ferroptosis in PD remain largely unknown.

In this study, we investigated the neuroprotective effects of UA in experimental models of PD, with particular emphasis on ferroptosis and microglial polarisation. We employed both lipopolysaccharide (LPS) or MPP+ stimulated BV2 microglia and a 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine (MPTP)-induced mouse model of PD. Our assessments included measurements of ferroptosis-related markers, inflammatory cytokines, mitochondrial morphology, dopaminergic neuron integrity, and motor function. Here, we demonstrated that UA attenuated microglial activation, suppressed ferroptosis, and improved motor behaviours in PD models via activation of the Nrf2 signalling pathway.

Materials and methods

Reagents

Uric acid (UA, Cat. No. U2625-25G), lipopolysaccharide (LPS, Cat. No. L2880), ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1, Cat. No. SML0583) were sourced from Sigma-Aldrich (Darmstadt, Germany). Probenecid (PBN, Cat. No. HY-B0545), ML385 (Cat. No. HY-100523), MPTP (Cat. No. HY-15608) and MPP+ (Cat. # HY-W008719) were purchased from MedChemExpress (New Jersey, U.S.A.). All antibodies and assay kits are detailed below in the relevant sections.

Cell culture and treatments

BV2 cells (Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd., China, Cat. No. CL-0493) were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, ThermoFisher Scientific, Cat. No. 10099141 C) and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator and passaged at 80% confluence for experiments. The complete medium was prepared by mixing 5 mL FBS with 44.5 mL DMEM/F12 (1:9, v/v) and adding 0.5 mL penicillin–streptomycin (1:100, v/v). The cells were treated as follows according to the experimental purpose: (1) control; (2) BV2 cells treated with 0.25, 0.5, or 1 μg/ml LPS for 24 hours; (3) BV2 cells treated with Fer-1 (10 μM) and LPS (1 μg/ml) for 24 hours; (4) BV2 cells pretreated for 30 minutes with UA (200 µM), followed by LPS (1 µg/ml) for 24 hours; (5) BV2 cells pretreated with PBN (1 mM) and UA (200 µM) for 30 minutes, followed by LPS (1 µg/ml) for 24 hours; (6) BV2 cells pretreated with 5 μM ML385 (an inhibitor of Nrf2) for 2 hours, followed by UA for 30 minutes and then LPS for 24 hours. (7) BV2 cells were pretreated with ML385 (5 μM) for 2 hours, followed by pretreatment with UA (200 µM) for 30 minutes, and finally intervened with MPP+ (100 µM) for 24 hours.

Cell viability assay

BV2 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 8 × 10^³ cells/well and treated with various concentrations of LPS, UA and/or Fer-1 for 24 hours. Subsequently, 10 μL of Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Hycezmbio, China) solution was added to each well, and the plates were incubated in the dark for 1 hour. The absorbance (OD) at 450 nm was then measured via a microplate reader, and the cell viability was calculated (relative to that of the untreated controls).

NO, LDH, and enzyme-linked immunosorbent (ELISA) assay

BV2 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and exposed to drugs for 24 hours. The cell supernatants were collected to measure Nitric oxide (NO, Beyotime, China, Cat. No. S0021S), LDH (Nanjing Jian Cheng, China, Cat. No. A020-2-2), IL-1β (Shanghai Enzyme-linked, China, Cat. No. ml106733) and TNF-α (Shanghai Enzyme-linked, China, Cat. No. ml002095) levels. The absorbance (OD) was determined via a spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher Scientific, Finland).

Measurement of ROS, MDA, GSH, and Fe2+ levels

The cells were seeded in 60 mm3 dishes (1.0 × 10^6 cells/well) and treated with drugs for 24 hours to collect cells and supernatants. ROS (Beyotime, China, Cat. No. S0033S), malondialdehyde (MDA, Solarbio, China, Cat. No. BC0025), glutathione (GSH, Nanjing Jian Cheng, China, Cat. No. A006-2-1), and iron content (Fe²⁺, Solarbio, China, Cat. No. BC5415) levels were assessed using specific assay kits following the manufacturer's instructions.

Real-time quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted from BV2 cells (TRIzol, Takara Bio, Japan, Cat. No. 9109) and reverse-transcribed (HIScript III, Vazyme, China, Cat. No. R323-01). qPCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme, China, Cat. No. Q711-02). GAPDH serves as the internal control. The relative expression of target genes was obtained via the 2-ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences are provided in Table 1.

Western blot

The preparation of protein samples from mouse brain tissues and BV2 cells involved the use of RIPA lysis buffer (Solarbio, China, Cat. No. R0010) and PMSF (Solarbio, China, Cat. No. R0100). SDS-polyacrylamide gels were used to separate protein samples, which were then transferred to PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk for 2 hours before incubation with the indicated primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C on a shaker. The blocking buffer (5%, m/v) was prepared by dissolving 2 g nonfat milk powder in 40 mL of 1× TBST with thorough mixing. Primary antibodies, including anti-Gpx4 (1:1000, Abcam, UK, Cat. No. ab125066), anti-COX2 (1:1000, CST, U.S.A., Cat. No. 12282S), anti-Nrf2 (1:1000, Proteintech, China, Cat. No. 16396-1-AP), anti-TH (1:1000, BIOSS, China, Cat. No. bs-0016R-50), and anti-GAPDH (1:5000, Proteintech, China, Cat. No. 60004-1-Ig), were diluted in primary antibody diluent at the indicated ratios (v/v) before incubation. The membranes were then incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H+L) (Proteintech, China, Cat. No. SA00001-2), diluted 1:10000 (v/v) in secondary antibody diluent, for 2 hours. Finally, chemiluminescence detection was performed via the Ultrasensitive ECL Detection Kit (Beyotime, China, P0018S).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Cells were treated for 24 hours, collected by centrifugation, and fixed in TEM fixative (Servicebio, China, Cat. No. G1102) at 4 °C. Samples were embedded in agarose, post-fixed with osmium tetroxide, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and embedded in resin. Ultrathin sections (60–80 nm) were prepared, stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and examined using a transmission electron microscope (HT7800, Hitachi, Japan).

Animals and ethics statement

In this study, 25 male C57BL/6J mice (6 weeks old, 18–22 g) were procured from the Animal Research Centre of Guangxi Medical University. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Guangxi Medical University (2024-E0-50-01) and conformed to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the National Institutes of Health (NIH Publications No. 8023, revised 1978). All animals spent a week acclimating in an SPF facility, where conditions were controlled: temperature was set between 20 °C and 24 °C, humidity ranged from 50% to 70%, and there was a 12-hour cycle of light and darkness, with unrestricted access to food and water. Sample size was not determined by formal power calculation; five mice per group were selected based on prior studies and pilot data for exploratory evaluation of UA effects. Blinding was applied during group allocation, experimentation, outcome assessment, and data analysis to minimise bias [29,31–35]. Mice were randomly divided into five groups (n = 5, n refers to number of animals) using a random number table: (i) control; (ii) MPTP (30 mg/kg); (iii) MPTP (30 mg/kg) + UA (50 mg/kg); (iv) MPTP (30 mg/kg) + UA (100 mg/kg); (v) MPTP (30 mg/kg) + UA (200 mg/kg). Cage placement and sample processing order were randomised to minimise confounding. The modelling period lasted 11 days. During days 1–5, mice received saline (MPTP group) or UA at the indicated doses. From days 6–10, MPTP (30 mg/kg, i.p.) was administered daily, with UA given 2 hours prior to MPTP in the UA groups [36]. On day 11, mice received saline (MPTP group) or UA alone, followed by behavioural assessments [29]. Subsequently, all mice were anaesthetised using chloral hydrate and positioned supine on an anatomical tray. Euthanasia was performed via cervical dislocation, and brain tissues were harvested for further analysis. The success of the animal model construction was assessed through comprehensive behavioural testing and quantitative analysis of tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) expression. All animals fulfilled the prespecified inclusion criteria and were therefore included in the final analysis.

Based on initial assessments of PD-related indicators, inflammatory markers, and GPX4 expression, a UA dose of 200 mg/kg was selected for further study. To investigate the protective mechanisms of UA in vivo, 20 male C57BL/6J mice (6 weeks old, 18–22 g) were randomly assigned to four groups: (1) control, (2) MPTP (30 mg/kg), (3) MPTP (30 mg/kg) + UA (200 mg/kg), and (4) ML385 (30 mg/kg) + UA (200 mg/kg) + MPTP (30 mg/kg). ML385 was administered via intraperitoneal injection, followed by UA 2 hours later, and MPTP 2 hours after UA [37]. Other treatments followed established protocols. On the 11th day, after the administration of UA, all mice were euthanized, and their brain tissues were harvested for further experimental validation.

Pole test

Mouse locomotion was assessed via the pole test [38]. A pole (50 cm in height, 1.2 cm in diameter) covered with sisal cloth and topped with a wooden ball was used. During testing, each mouse was placed at the top of the pole. The time taken for the mouse to orient its head downwards and descend to the base was recorded. Three independent trials were performed for each mouse, with a minimum interval of 30 minutes between trials.

Hanging test

Motor coordination was assessed via the hanging test. Each mouse was suspended by its forepaws from a horizontal wire (5 mm in diameter). Performance was scored according to the method described in reference [39]. Each mouse underwent three trials, with a minimum rest interval of 30 minutes between trials.

Immunofluorescence

The cells were treated with drugs for 24 hours, fixed, permeabilized, and blocked. The cells were subsequently incubated overnight at 4 °C with a primary antibody against CD86 (diluted 1:200, v/v, in 5% BSA; SAB, U.S.A., Cat. No. 32223), followed by incubation with an Alexa Fluor® 488–conjugated secondary antibody (diluted 1:60, v/v, in 5% BSA; Abcam, UK, Cat. No. ab150077) for 2 hours at room temperature in the dark. Autofluorescence was quenched using an antifading mounting medium (Solarbio, China, Cat. No. S2100), and the nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Solarbio, China, Cat. No. C0065). Fluorescence images were captured via an inverted fluorescence microscope (Zeiss Axio Observer, Zeiss, Germany).

Brains were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 hours, dehydrated in graded ethanol, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4–5 μm. Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval. After blocking with 3% BSA (v/v) prepared from 5% BSA in PBS for 30 min, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-CD86 primary antibody (1:200, v/v; SAB, U.S.A., Cat. No. 32223), followed by incubation with Cy3-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:300, v/v; Servicebio, China, Cat. No. GB21303) for 1 hour at room temperature in the dark. All antibodies were diluted in 5% BSA. Autofluorescence was quenched, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Servicebio, China, Cat. No. G1012). Sections were mounted with antifade medium and imaged using a Zeiss Axio Observer fluorescence microscope. Images were analysed with ImageJ.

Immunohistochemistry

Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval in sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 20 min. Endogenous peroxidase was quenched with 3% H₂O₂ (v/v in PBS), followed by blocking with 3% BSA (v/v in PBS), obtained by dilution of 5% BSA. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against TH (1:1000, v/v; BIOSS, China, Cat. No. bs-0016R-50), Iba-1 (1:800, v/v; Servicebio, China, Cat. No. GB12105-100), and GPX4 (1:100, v/v; BIOSS, China, Cat. No. bsm-61552R). All antibodies were diluted in 5% BSA. After washing, sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody at room temperature, developed with DAB, and counterstained with hematoxylin. Sections were dehydrated, cleared, mounted, and imaged using a Zeiss Axio brightfield microscope.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics 26 and GraphPad Prism 9. Data are presented as mean ± SD for normally distributed variables and median (interquartile range) for non-normally distributed variables. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For comparisons between two groups, an unpaired Student’s t-test was used for normally distributed data, whereas the Mann–Whitney U test was used for non-normally distributed data. For multiple-group comparisons, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was applied to normally distributed data, whereas the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple-comparisons test was used for non-normally distributed data. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

LPS induces neuroinflammation and ferroptosis hallmarks in BV2 microglia

To evaluate whether LPS induced cytotoxic effects, we performed a CCK-8 assay to assess cell viability. The results showed that treatment with 1 μg/mL LPS induced a significant reduction in BV2 cell viability (Figure 1A). Consistent with previous reports [15], we found LPS treatment activated BV2 microglia, significantly increasing the mRNA levels of the proinflammatory mediators IL-1β, TNF-α, inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), and IL-6 (Figure 1B–E), as well as observed in nitric oxide (NO) levels (Figure 1F). To investigate whether LPS induces ferroptosis in BV2 microglia, we evaluated key ferroptosis events, including the accumulation of redox-active iron, lipid peroxidation, and the depletion of glutathione (GSH) and GPX4. Compared with control conditions, LPS treatment dose-dependently increased the concentration of intracellular Fe2+ and MDA levels in BV2 microglia (Figure 1G,H). Given that imbalanced free iron and Fenton reaction-derived ROS are critical drivers of ferroptosis, we evaluated total ROS levels. Fluorescence assays confirmed that LPS dose-dependently increased the level of intracellular ROS (Figure 1I,J). We also observed that LPS exposure significantly downgraded the content of GSH (Figure 1K). LPS treatment markedly downregulated both the mRNA and protein expression of GPX4, the key antioxidant enzyme responsible for degrading lipid peroxidation products (Figure 1L–N). These results demonstrated a significant association between LPS-induced neuroinflammation and ferroptosis activation in microglia.

Ferrostatin-1 attenuates LPS-induced neuroinflammation by inhibiting ferroptosis

We investigated the mechanistic interplay between ferroptosis and inflammation via the specific ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) [40]. CCK-8 assays confirmed that Fer-1, at concentrations up to 20 μM, caused no significant cytotoxicity in BV2 cells (Figure 2A). We selected 10 μM Fer-1 for subsequent experiments based on previous reports and our results [41,42]. Compared with LPS treatment, Fer-1 effectively suppressed LPS-induced ferroptosis, as evidenced by reduced Fe2+ and MDA levels, elevated GSH levels, and increased GPX4 mRNA expression (Figure 2B–E). The qPCR results revealed that Fer-1 significantly inhibited the LPS-induced increase in the mRNA levels of the proinflammatory cytokines IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6 in BV2 microglia (Figure 2F–H). This anti-inflammatory effect was further confirmed by the reduced levels of these cytokines in the culture supernatant (Figure 2I,J). These results demonstrate that Fer-1 ameliorates LPS-induced neuroinflammation in BV2 microglia, primarily through the inhibition of ferroptosis.

UA suppresses LPS-induced neuroinflammation and ferroptosis in BV2 microglia

To examine the neuroprotective effect of UA, we found 400 μM UA significantly reduced BV2 cell viability (Figure 3A). To identify a safe and effective concentration, we measured lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release. Pretreatment with 200 μM UA significantly reduced LDH release induced by LPS (Figure 3B). Based on these findings, 200 μM UA was selected for further experiments. Quantitative PCR analysis revealed that UA treatment significantly suppressed the expression of proinflammatory mediators induced by LPS, including iNOS, IL-1β, and TNF-α (Figure 3C–E). The ELISA results confirmed that increasing concentrations of UA led to reduced levels of IL-1β and TNF-α in the culture supernatant (Figure 3F,G). Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that UA treatment significantly inhibited the LPS-induced expression of CD86, a marker of the M1 microglial phenotype (Figure 3H,I). These findings suggested that UA promoted a shift away from proinflammatory M1 polarisation. Western blot analysis demonstrated that UA suppressed COX-2 expression triggered by LPS stimulation (Figure 3J,K). These findings indicated that UA reduces LPS-induced neuroinflammation in BV2 microglia by downregulating proinflammatory gene and protein expression, modulating microglial polarisation, and inhibiting COX-2 activity. We next examined the effect of UA on ferroptosis under LPS-induced inflammatory conditions. Compared with those in the model group, UA treatment lowered the Fe2+ and MDA levels, whereas it elevated the GSH levels (Figure 4A–C). UA also significantly decreased intracellular ROS levels (Figure 4D,E). As shown in Figure 4F,G (data presented as mean ± SD, n = 3 per group), LPS stimulation significantly reduced GPX4 protein expression to 0.5933 ± 0.02525 compared with the control group (1.000 ± 0.06375) (P < 0.001), indicating a pronounced inhibitory effect of LPS on GPX4 expression. Pre-treatment with UA (UA + LPS group) markedly alleviated this suppression, restoring the level to 0.8891 ± 0.05062, which approached the baseline level of the control group. In contrast, treatment with UA alone (UA group) resulted in a slight decrease to 0.9268 ± 0.1028, with no significant difference compared to the control group, suggesting that UA itself exerts minimal effect on GPX4 expression under basal conditions. Consistently, as shown in Figure 4H, the mRNA expression pattern of GPX4 exhibited a similar trend. Collectively, these results indicate that UA pretreatment effectively attenuates LPS-induced suppression of GPX4, suggesting a potential protective role against ferroptosis.

Transporter blockade disrupts the anti-inflammatory and antiferroptotic functions of UA

UA inhibited LPS-induced inflammation and ferroptosis. To clarify whether this inhibitory effect was directly mediated by UA, the specific UA transporter inhibitor probenecid (PBN) was used for validation. The application of PBN significantly reversed the UA-mediated suppression of inflammatory factor expression (Figure 5A,B). Mitochondrial morphological changes associated with ferroptosis were observed in the LPS-induced cell model. UA treatment effectively alleviated these pathological changes, whereas cotreatment with PBN aggravated mitochondrial damage, as indicated by the orange arrows in Figure 5C. PBN treatment counteracted the effects of UA on Fe2+ and MDA levels and significantly suppressed the UA-induced increases in GSH and the upregulation of GPX4 (Figure 5D–G). These findings suggest that a reduction in intracellular UA levels reactivated LPS-induced inflammation and ferroptosis, further confirming that the neuroprotective effect of UA is concentration-dependent.

UA exerts neuroprotective effects via the Nrf2 pathway activation in BV2 microglia

Recent studies show that Nrf2 could protect against ferroptosis by regulating GPX4. To verify the relationship between UA and Nrf2, we investigated this mechanism via the specific Nrf2 inhibitor ML385 in BV2 microglia. LPS treatment significantly reduced the mRNA level and protein expression of Nrf2, whereas UA effectively reversed this suppression induced by LPS. ML385 abolished UA-induced Nrf2 activation (Figure 6A–C), confirming that the effects of UA depend on Nrf2. Furthermore, ML385 upregulated the expression of inflammatory markers, including IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6, thereby counteracting the anti-inflammatory effects of UA (Figure 6D–F). Activation of the Nrf2 signalling pathway by UA significantly reduced intracellular iron accumulation (Figure 6G), inhibited lipid peroxidation, as indicated by the MDA levels (Figure 6H), increased GSH levels (Figure 6I), and upregulated mRNA levels and protein expression of GPX4 (Figure 6J–L). Cotreatment with ML385 attenuated the UA-mediated effects in BV2 cells. These findings demonstrate that UA protects against LPS-induced neuroinflammation by activating the Nrf2 signalling pathway, which orchestrates antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-ferroptosis responses.

UA suppresses MPP+-induced neuroinflammation and ferroptosis in BV2 microglia

To further substantiate the neuroprotective effects of UA at the cellular level, we exposed BV2 cells to MPP⁺ to induce inflammatory activation and subsequently assessed the impact of UA on ferroptosis-related markers and inflammatory responses. Initially, qPCR analysis revealed a significant upregulation of tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) expression following MPP⁺ treatment, whereas UA pretreatment markedly attenuated this expression, indicating the successful establishment of a PD-related cellular model (Figure 7A). Subsequently, we investigated the expression of inflammatory mediators, finding that UA treatment significantly suppressed the MPP⁺-induced expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α (Figure 7B–D). Furthermore, UA pretreatment significantly decreased the levels of Fe²⁺ and MDA in BV2 cells induced by MPP⁺, while concurrently enhancing GSH content (Figure 7E–G). Subsequent analyses demonstrated that UA treatment significantly influenced the expression of ferroptosis-related genes activated by MPP⁺. Quantitative PCR results indicated that UA reduced the mRNA expression levels of SLC7A11 and GPX4 induced by MPP⁺ (Figure 7H,I). Additionally, our results confirmed that MPP⁺ treatment markedly downregulated the expression of Nrf2 mRNA, whereas UA pretreatment partially reversed this effect (Figure 7J). The findings suggest that UA effectively mitigates the inflammatory response induced by MPP⁺ and modulates key indicators associated with ferroptosis in BV2 cells, aligning with our observations in the LPS model.

UA ameliorates motor dysfunction and dopaminergic neuron degeneration by inhibiting neuroinflammation and ferroptosis

Behavioural assessments and TH immunohistochemistry were performed to validate the successful establishment of the animal model. Mice treated with MPTP exhibited pronounced motor deficits, as evidenced by prolonged climbing latency and reduced suspension scores. Notably, UA pretreatment ameliorated motor dysfunction in a dose-dependent manner. Administration of UA at 200 mg/kg significantly shortened climbing latency and restored suspension performance toward normal levels compared with the MPTP group (Figure 8A,B). Immunohistochemical analysis further demonstrated that MPTP administration markedly reduced the density of TH⁺ neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNpc), a hallmark pathological feature of Parkinson’s disease. Treatment with UA (200 mg/kg) effectively attenuated this neuronal loss (Figure 8C,D), confirming the successful establishment of the MPTP-induced PD mouse model. Given the critical role of M1 microglial activation in PD progression, microglial markers were subsequently evaluated. UA treatment dose-dependently suppressed MPTP-induced upregulation of Iba-1 (Figure 8E,F) and reduced the fluorescence intensity of CD86⁺ microglia (Figure 8G,H). In addition, UA reversed the MPTP-induced downregulation of GPX4, a key regulator of lipid peroxidation (Figure 8I,J). Collectively, these findings indicate that UA exerts neuroprotective effects in the MPTP-induced Parkinson’s disease model, potentially through the dual inhibition of neuroinflammation and ferroptosis.

UA activates Nrf2 signalling to inhibit ferroptosis and neuroinflammation in PD mice

To elucidate how UA regulates ferroptosis via Nrf2, we employed the Nrf2 inhibitor ML385 in MPTP-treated mice. UA significantly increased TH protein and mRNA levels expression in substantia nigra tissue, which was partially blocked by ML385 cotreatment (Figure 9A–C). UA significantly inhibited the MPTP-induced increase in cortical Iba-1, IL-1β and IL-6 mRNA levels (Figure 9D–F), and the anti-inflammatory effect that was partially reversed by ML385. Additionally, MPTP markedly increased intracellular Fe2+ levels; this effect was reversed by UA pretreatment and blunted by ML385 (Figure 9G). UA reversed the MPTP-induced reduction in GPX4 expression at both the protein and mRNA levels, whereas ML385 attenuated this effect (Figure 9H–J). Similar changes were observed for Nrf2 (Figure 9K–M). These findings indicate that UA confers neuroprotection by activating Nrf2 signalling, thereby upregulating GPX4, inhibiting ferroptosis, and suppressing neuroinflammation. Discussion

PD is recognised as the second most common neurodegenerative disorder. Although currently available therapies provide symptomatic relief, they are largely ineffective at slowing disease progression. Accumulating evidence implicates a complex interplay of oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and ferroptosis in the pathogenesis of PD [9,21,43,44]. UA, the final metabolite of purine catabolism, has garnered increasing attention because of its potent antioxidant properties and ability to cross the blood-brain barrier [13,15,29,43,45]. Epidemiological observations have revealed an inverse association between serum UA levels and both the incidence and progression of PD, suggesting a potential neuroprotective mechanism [46–48]. Nevertheless, the molecular underpinnings of this association remain poorly understood. Our data indicate that UA can inhibit lipid peroxidation and limit ferroptosis-driven inflammatory signalling by enhancing the antioxidant defence mechanism, activating the Nrf2 signalling pathway, and restoring glutathione homoeostasis in both MPP+- or LPS-stimulated BV2 microglia and MPTP-induced PD model mice. Ultimately, this can reduce the activation of microglia and neuroinflammation.

Ferroptosis has recently been recognised as an important regulator of neuroinflammation, particularly in microglia. Our findings support a mechanistic framework linking ferroptosis to microglial neuroinflammation. In inflammatory models triggered by LPS or the Parkinsonian toxin MPP⁺, disruption of iron homoeostasis results in intracellular iron overload and excessive production of ROS, accompanied by decreased GPX4 expression and increased lipid peroxidation products, such as MDA [49–51]. Mechanistically, increased Fe²⁺ promotes Fenton reactions, initiating phospholipid peroxidation and generating lipid-derived reactive aldehydes, including MDA. These oxidative products not only indicate ferroptosis stress but also act as inflammatory mediators [52]. Meanwhile, ferroptosis damage triggers the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), including HMGB1 and mitochondrial DNA, which activate pattern recognition receptors such as Toll-like receptors and NOD-like receptors [53–55]. When these receptors recognise the DAMPs, they trigger the activation of inflammatory signalling pathways. In microglia, these signals converge on key inflammatory cascades, including NF-κB signalling, NLRP3 inflammasome activation, and the JAK2/STAT3 pathway, ultimately promoting the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 [56–58]. This process establishes a self-reinforcing cycle in which inflammation further exacerbates oxidative stress and iron homoeostasis imbalance, thereby aggravating ferroptosis injury.

Fer-1 mitigates inflammatory injury primarily by suppressing ferroptosis, thereby limiting both cell death-associated inflammatory amplification and the propagation of lipid peroxidation [59]. In our model, Fer-1 reduced Fe²⁺ accumulation and restored GSH and GPX4, confirming effective inhibition of the ferroptosis programme. By contrast, its effect on MDA was comparatively modest. This is not unexpected, as MDA represents a downstream, cumulative readout of lipid oxidation and may lag behind early ferroptosis blockade. Moreover, Fer-1 mainly acts by trapping lipid radicals and interrupting peroxidation chain propagation, rather than removing oxidised lipid products that have already formed [60,61]. The limited reduction in MDA may also reflect the fact that LPS-driven oxidative injury extends beyond ferroptosis, involving NADPH oxidase activation, mitochondrial ROS production, and inflammatory signalling cascades that continue to fuel lipid oxidation [62]. Interestingly, Fer-1 alone produced a slight, non-significant increase in TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 mRNA, suggesting that basal suppression of lipid peroxidation may transiently perturb redox homoeostasis and trigger compensatory cytokine transcription rather than bona fide inflammatory activation. Collectively, these data indicate that LPS-induced lipid oxidative damage arises from both ferroptosis-dependent and ferroptosis-independent mechanisms, which may explain the partial but incomplete protection conferred by Fer-1.

Previous studies have reported that UA inhibits the activation of caspase-1 and the maturation of IL-1β and IL-18 by interacting with the NLRP3 inflammasome [63]. UA protected dopaminergic neurons from death induced by 6-hydroxydopamine or 1-methyl-4-phenylpyridinium in a PD cell model [34,64]. Although the anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties of UA are well recognised [14], its potential involvement in regulating ferroptosis under inflammatory conditions remains insufficiently characterised. In the present study, we demonstrate that UA markedly suppresses LPS-induced inflammatory responses and ferroptosis-associated alterations, revealing a previously underappreciated mechanism by which UA modulates ferroptosis during neuroinflammation. Our findings revealed that UA effectively attenuated M1-type microglial polarisation and inhibited the expression of CD86, COX2 and Iba-1, as well as the release of proinflammatory mediators, including TNF-α, IL-6, and NO. Importantly, in the MPTP-induced PD mouse model, UA treatment improved motor deficits and preserved dopaminergic neurons in vivo, further supporting its neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory effects. Beyond the direct regulation of microglial activation, preserved neuronal integrity may also contribute to the normalisation of microglial states. In animal models, UA has been reported to exert neuroprotective effects on dopaminergic neurons under Parkinsonian conditions. Preservation of neuronal integrity may indirectly attenuate microglial activation by limiting the release of neuronal stress signals (DAMPs) while restoring neuron-derived inhibitory cues that constrain microglial activity, including the CX3CL1–CX3CR1 and CD200–CD200R signalling axes [35]. At the cellular level, UA markedly reduced the accumulation of intracellular free iron and lipid peroxidation products triggered by LPS while concurrently increasing GPX4 expression and restoring GSH levels. These findings suggest that UA mitigates ferroptosis by bolstering antioxidant defences, alleviating iron overload, and limiting oxidative damage. Additionally, we observed robust activation of the Nrf2 signalling pathway following UA treatment, indicating that Nrf2 may serve as a central upstream regulator that mediates the multifaceted protective effects of UA. Together, these observations suggest that UA may exert neuroprotective effects through a dual mechanism—directly limiting ferroptosis-associated oxidative stress while indirectly restoring neuron–microglia homoeostasis. This multitarget action underscores the therapeutic potential of UA in the treatment of neurodegenerative diseases.

PBN blocks transmembrane transport and intracellular accumulation of UA by inhibiting URAT1/GLUT [10,11,15,65]. This study confirms that the neuroprotective effect of UA is dependent on URAT1/GLUT9 transporter-mediated intracellular uptake. When the transport inhibitor PBN was used to block UA influx, its effect on inhibiting LPS-induced pro-inflammatory factors (TNF-α/IL-6) and ferroptosis markers (free iron, MDA was increased, and GPX4 was down-regulated) was significantly attenuated. Electron microscopy showed that PBN could counteract the improvement effect of UA on mitochondrial damage characteristic of ferroptosis (loss of crest and volume reduction). This discovery reveals the regulatory effect of the transport system on the biological activity of UA, suggesting that the therapeutic effect of UA on neurodegenerative diseases may be improved by enhancing the efficiency of UA uptake in brain cells. Previous studies may have underestimated the physiological significance of URAT1/GLUT9 because of the use of supraphysiological concentrations of UA.

Nrf2 is widely acknowledged as a master transcriptional regulator of oxidative stress and ferroptosis [66]. Through its interaction with antioxidant response elements, Nrf2 facilitates the transcription of critical anti-ferroptosis genes, including GPX4 and solute carrier family 7 member 11, thereby preserving glutathione metabolism and intracellular iron homoeostasis [67]. In the present study, we demonstrated that UA strongly inhibited LPS-induced neuroinflammation and ferroptosis, primarily through activation of the Nrf2 signalling pathway. These findings suggest that UA may exert multifaceted neuroprotective effects in the context of neuroinflammatory responses. Following UA treatment, the expression levels of Nrf2 and its downstream antioxidant enzyme GPX4 were significantly increased. Concurrently, intracellular glutathione concentrations were restored, and the accumulation of lipid peroxidation products, such as MDA, was attenuated. These observations underscore the pivotal role of Nrf2 in regulating redox balance and suppressing ferroptosis-related cell death. Consistent with previous reports, Nrf2 upregulation has been shown to confer neuroprotection in models of cerebral ischaemia-reperfusion injury [68–70]. Although the antioxidant properties of UA have been previously documented in models of neurodegenerative diseases [12,29,34] and cardiovascular injury [71], its modulatory effect on ferroptosis under neuroinflammatory conditions remains insufficiently characterised. Our data provide compelling evidence that Nrf2 acts as a central molecular mediator through which UA exerts both anti-inflammatory and anti-ferroptosis effects. Notably, pharmacological inhibition of Nrf2 with ML385 significantly abrogated the neuroprotective effects of UA, further reinforcing the essential role of Nrf2 in mediating these responses. In summary, this study highlights the critical involvement of Nrf2 activation in the neuroprotective effects of UA. Moreover, these findings reveal a previously underexplored mechanism by which UA modulates ferroptosis, offering mechanistic insights and identifying a promising molecular target for the therapeutic management of neuroinflammatory disorders.

Despite these encouraging results, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the present findings. First, the apparent inconsistency in the magnitude of MDA elevation across experiments warrants cautious interpretation. Although MDA is a commonly used marker of lipid peroxidation, it reflects a relatively downstream and cumulative oxidative outcome rather than the dynamic kinetics of ferroptosis membrane damage. Current recommendations in the ferroptosis field emphasise that ferroptosis cannot be defined by any single biomarker, and that lipid peroxidation-associated readouts may differ substantially across experimental systems, methodological conditions, and temporal windows. In this context, the smaller increase in MDA observed in Figure 4B likely reflects differences in the timing or extent of lipid peroxidation at sampling, rather than an absence of responsiveness to UA or Fer-1. Notably, the anti-ferroptosis effects of UA and Fer-1 were supported by multiple independent molecular and phenotypic endpoints, lending robustness to the conclusion that both agents confer protective effects.

In addition, several methodological constraints remain. The use of BV2 cells instead of primary microglia may limit physiological relevance, while the limited specificity of the Nrf2 inhibitor restricts the strength of mechanistic inference. Furthermore, the pharmacogenetic characterisation of UA sources remains incomplete, potentially affecting reproducibility and hindering future translational applications. Subsequent studies should incorporate Nrf2-deficient models together with multi-omics analyses to define more precisely how UA regulates ferroptosis and to establish a rigorous dose-effect relationship. Finally, because systemic UA administration may carry potential adverse effects such as gout, future therapeutic development should prioritise centrally targeted UA derivatives or advanced drug delivery strategies to improve both safety and efficacy.

In conclusion, our study demonstrated that UA attenuates ferroptosis-mediated neuroinflammation in PD models through activation of the Nrf2 signalling pathway. These findings elucidate the multifaceted mechanism by which UA exerts both anti-inflammatory and anti-ferroptosis effects, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic agent targeting multiple pathological processes in neuroinflammatory conditions.
