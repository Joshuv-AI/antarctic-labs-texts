MXene electrochemical phosphate sensors
Authors: Thiba Nagaraja, Anupma Thakur, Andrey Krayev, A. Edward Robinson, Aleksandra Vojvodic, Babak Anasori, Suprem R. Das
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Rapid and accurate detection of molecular species with a high degree of selectivity and sensitivity constitutes the ultimate goal of designing sensors for various applications, from studying nutrients in soil-water systems to assessing physiological conditions in human health. With recent progress, two-dimensional (2D) transition metal carbides/nitrides have shown great potential for applications such as energy storage and electromagnetic interference shielding. However, the fundamental electrochemical studies and subsequent applications of MXenes for molecular sensing are still in their infancy. Here, we use 2D sheets of titanium carbide (Ti3C2Tx) MXene for electrochemical detection of phosphate, a key molecule for sustainability of life on earth, and major contributor to environmental pollution. Ti3C2Tx MXene sensors were demonstrated to be highly selective towards phosphate with a sensing range from 1 µM to 350 µM and a limit of detection (LOD) of 1.31 µM. This work lays the foundation for molecular sensing using Ti3C2Tx MXene in complex environments.

Introduction

Electrochemical sensors are ubiquitous due to their ability to interact and measure the presence of desired species in molecular form in diverse fields, including environmental monitoring, health diagnostics, industrial processes, and many more1–3. The key information is derived from the fundamental interactions between the sensor surface and its immediate environment (typically, a fluid environment in close contact with the sensor). In addition to the interfacial phenomena such as diffusion, adsorption, charge transfer, and double-layer formation, several other factors are also crucial in evaluating the success of an electrochemical sensor device, particularly in complex real-world applications4,5. These include the degree of signal transduction (sensitivity), reliability under repeated cycling, impact of physical stimuli such as pH and temperature, and selectivity amidst other interfering species. Nanomaterials with unique 1D and 2D geometries, such as nanowires, nanotubes, and nanosheets with semiconducting and metallic-type electronic properties, have demonstrated transformative sensing characteristics in the past for electrochemical sensing addressing low sensitivity issues6. However, these materials are often functionalized with different recognition agents that can cause instabilities such as leaching and low-binding affinities7–9. Therefore, achieving an optimal balance between sensitivity, stability, and reliability, while ensuring selective detection of molecular species in the environment through improved functionalization strategies or with materials that require no functionalization, is the primary goal in developing state-of-the-art sensor materials and devices.

Phosphorus in the form of phosphates (PO43-, hereby referred to as P) is essential for all life forms on earth to carry out many biological processes such as synthesis of genetic material (DNA and RNA), formation of cell membrane (phospholipids), cellular energy transfer (ATP), activation of enzymes, maintenance of blood pH, and many more10. In the agricultural ecosystem, P is one of the key macronutrients for plant growth and reproduction. However, the available P for plants in the soil (inorganic orthophosphates) is often limited due to its tendency to bind with soil particles and metals, forming complexes that render it inaccessible to plants, leading farmers to resort to the application of P fertilizers to maximize the availability of P in soil for plant uptake. Adversely, the unregulated use of P as fertilizer disrupts soil microbial activity and poses risks to crop health, ultimately reducing crop yield11. Furthermore, excess P gets leached into nearby water bodies, causing eutrophication. Therefore, maintaining an optimal presence of P in the soil environment is necessary. This urges careful monitoring of P with the development of reliable sensor technologies. While many laboratory-based techniques exist for detecting P, developing an in-field electrochemical P sensor is preferred for its portability, affordability, rapid response time, and sensitivity.

Several mechanisms have been proposed in the past to develop P-targeted electrochemical sensors. A common approach involved immobilizing synthetic anion receptors with an integrated redox active element on a working electrode in voltammetry techniques to create an electrochemical signal for detection12. Some sensing of P ions has been conducted with solution phase anion receptors with redox elements as opposed to immobilizing the receptor on the electrode, as mentioned above13. On the other hand, potentiometric technique often involved the use of an ionophore in a membrane matrix or metal as ion selective electrode (ISE) for the selective detection of P ions14,15. In other cases, a modification of the classical (and Environmental Protection Agency (EPA)-approved), colorimetric molybdenum blue method was used to detect P. In the classical method the phosphomolybdenum complex (PMC) formed in the presence of P and molybdenum in an acidified medium (Eq. 1) is reduced to a mixed molybdenum oxidation state using ascorbic acid as a reducing agent and potassium antimony tartrate as a catalyst (Eq. 2). The reduced PMC appears blue; the intensity of the color is quantified using spectrometric technique and is directly proportional to P concentration16,17. However, in the modified method, such as in an electrochemical method, the PMC formed is directly reduced at the working electrode surface without an external reducing agent and a catalyst, leading to a quantifiable electrochemical signal with respect to P concentration18–20. Recently, atomically thin 2D nanomaterials, such as graphene, have been used as working electrodes for various electrochemical sensors, including graphene-based electrochemical P sensors21. However, the use of 2D materials for P sensing remains in its early stages, presenting opportunities to build on previous research and explore additional 2D materials for this purpose. MXene with a general formula of Mn+1XnTx where “M” denotes a transition metal atom (such as Ti, Sc, Mo, Zr, V, Cr); “X” either a carbon, nitrogen or both, and “Tx” surface termination groups such as -O, -F, -Cl, and/or –OH, with “n” an integer ranging from 1 to 3 exhibit atomically thin 2D characteristics with active in-plane sites that may be functionalized to detect various environmental species. MXenes are synthesized by selectively etching A layers (elements from groups 13 to 16 of the periodic table, such as Al, Si, Ga) from the parent MAX crystals (general formula of Mn+1AXn) using either a fluorine-based or non-fluorine-based etchants22,23. The ease of solution processibility and the metal like electrical conductivity have attracted attention, with applications spanning energy storage, electromagnetic interference shielding, electrochemical sensors, catalysis, drug delivery, and water purification24–26. In the electrochemical systems, Ti3C2Tx MXene is used in the cathodic potential window as it undergoes an irreversible electrochemical oxidation in the anodic potential window, with the oxidation potential being primarily influenced by the electrolyte and the pH of the electrochemical system27,28. Therefore, identifying the appropriate potential range in investigating Ti3C2Tx MXene’s promise as an electrochemical sensor for various analytes is a valuable area of study.

In this report, we leverage the metal-like electrical conductivity and surface properties of Ti3C2Tx MXene to discover electrode processes that elucidate the electrochemical redox activities of heteropolymetalate ions, specifically PMCs, in the modified molybdenum blue method. Consequently, we utilize the underlying mechanism to design the Ti3C2Tx MXene-based electrochemical P sensor. The designed P sensor demonstrated a limit of detection (LOD) of 1.31 µM and a linear sensing range of 1 µM to 350 µM while being very selective to P amongst other interfering ions.

Ti3C2Tx MXene in this study was synthesized via wet chemical acid-based etching of Ti3AlC2 MAX phase material, followed by delamination of the resultant multilayered Ti3C2Tx MXene, as described in the methods section. All the results consistently show the presence of single-to-few layer Ti3C2Tx MXene. Prior to electrochemical characterization of the Ti3C2Tx MXene electrode, the individual Ti3C2Tx flakes were characterized29. The electrodes were also tested for stability over 135 days. Ti3C2Tx MXene/GCE was used for DPV sensing of 100 µM P on day 0 and stored in ambient conditions for 5 days before the next measurement. A similar protocol was carried out for the 10th day measurement and 135th day measurement. The results are shown in Fig. 8d. Although there was no significant difference in the change in current between day 0 and 5 (0.26 µA, 7 % change), a decrease in the change in current was observed between day 0 and day 10 by 14 % (0.55 µA). This could be ascribed to the possible partial oxidation of Ti3C2Tx MXene working electrode in the air, leading to a decreased conductivity and hence decreased change in current. By day 135, the change in current between day 10 and day 135 was observed to be only 5 % (0.2 µA). The smaller difference in the change in the current could be due to near complete oxidation of the Ti3C2Tx MXene surface which is leading to a more stable current. These results indicate that Ti3C2Tx MXene, in its oxidized state, still shows its potential for P sensing. The results highlight the promising sensing capabilities of Ti3C2Tx MXene in its oxidized state, which can be pursued for future experimental work for sensing applications. Although Ti3C2Tx MXene oxidizes in air, few reports show their stability over time following a certain degree of oxidation and their potential for various applications, including in electrochemical applications41,42.

Ex-situ Raman spectro-electrochemical characterization

To enhance further mechanistic understanding of the P redox processes at the surface of Ti3C2Tx MXene, an ex-situ Raman spectro-electrochemical measurement was conducted. The outcome of the experiment was used in correlating the structural changes in Ti3C2Tx MXene to the underlying P redox process. We hypothesize that the formation of the α-isomer in the electrolyte, followed by its isomeric transition (α-to-β) near the Ti3C2Tx MXene sensor surface during electron transfer, induces strain in the MXene crystal lattice manifesting as either compressive or tensile stress. Thus, comparing the Raman spectra of MXene before and after electrochemical cycling in the phosphate containing electrolyte medium can provide further insight into the P mechanism proposed in this work. The unprocessed Raman spectra of pristine MXene, MXene after immersion in α-isomer PMC solution, and MXene after electrochemical cycling involving α-to-β isomeric transition at its surface are shown in Supplementary Fig. 6. The characteristic lattice vibrational modes corresponding to various atomic bonds within lattice carbon, surface terminal groups, and the skeletal regions are identified in the Ti3C2Tx MXene sample before and after electrochemical cycling43. The comparable intensities of the deconvoluted peaks in the lattice carbon spectral region, relative to those in the skeletal and surface group regions, indicate that our Ti3C2Tx MXene primarily consists of single to few layer flakes, corroborating the AFM analysis43. Upon introduction of the α-isomer and subsequent electrochemical α-to-β isomeric transition near the Ti3C2Tx MXene surface, a systematic increase in the Raman spectral intensity of the skeletal vibrational modes (within the 50–300 cm−1 spectral region) is observed relative to the lattice carbon region (560–800 cm−1). This could be due to the skeletal strain imparted to the Ti3C2Tx MXene lattice by the PMC α-isomer formation and structural transition of the isomer from α to β in the vicinity of the electrode.

By deconvoluting the three spectral regions (skeleton, surface group, lattice carbon), the source of the individual phonon peaks in the pristine Ti3C2Tx MXene were identified43. The peak shifts in each region for α-isomer and β-isomer related to Ti3C2Tx MXene is reported in Supplementary Table 4. Almost all the peaks demonstrated either compressive (Raman blue shift) or tensile (Raman red shift) stresses, indicating the role of electron transfer at the isomeric transition to the individual and/or collective Ti3C2Tx MXene crystal lattice. The preliminary results of this work will be further examined in future experiments and first-principle modeling for more discoveries.

Discussion

In summary, our study here presents the first-time demonstration of the intrinsic property of atomically thin Ti3C2Tx MXene for detecting phosphate ions via electrochemical sensing. The electrochemical response of Ti3C2Tx MXene was studied with a supporting electrolyte to determine the electrochemical window for sensing molecular phosphates. At the identified potential window, phosphate detection at the surface of Ti3C2Tx MXene was facilitated by PMC via an electrode-analyte charge transfer mechanism. The sensor retains 90% of its phosphate-sensing capacity after 135 days of storage, indicating reasonable stability under our testing conditions. Ti3C2Tx MXene phosphate sensors exhibited consistent and highly selective responses towards phosphate molecules among other potential interfering ions with sensing ranging from 1 µM to 350 µM with a detection limit of 1.31 µM. This work paves the way for Ti3C2Tx and other MXene materials for the exploration of phosphate molecular sensing in complex environments such as agricultural, ecological, and biomedical domains. As a future direction, exploring the phosphate sensing performance of Ti3C2Tx MXene with varying surface terminations, along with systematic experimental and theoretical studies on MXene oxidation and its influence on sensor performance, will be a valuable advancement in the field of MXene-based molecular sensing. Additionally, developing effective strategies to mitigate MXene oxidation and assessing their impact on sensing capabilities will further advance the current state of knowledge in MXene based sensors. In-situ spectro-electrochemical measurements will also offer fundamental scientific insights into the mechanisms of redox reactions occurring at the electrode-electrolyte interface.

Methods

Materials

All chemicals used were of analytical grade and were used as obtained from the suppliers without further purification. Potassium chloride (KCl), hexaammineruthenium (III) chloride (H18Cl3N6Ru), sulfuric acid (H2SO4), ammonium heptamolybdate tetrahydrate ((NH4)6Mo7O24.4H2O), potassium dihydrogen P (KH2PO4) and sodium nitrate (NaNO3) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Potassium sulfate (K2SO4), sodium carbonate anhydrous (Na2CO3) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. The materials and solvents used in the synthesis of Ti3C2Tx MXene are detailed in a previous publication22. All solutions were prepared using ultrapure water with a resistivity of 18.2 MΩ cm at 25 °C. Real sample test was performed using tap water obtained directly from the laboratory and environmental water sample collected from a nearby lake (Tuttle Creek Lake located in Manhattan, Kansas).

Ti3C2Tx synthesis

Ti3C2Tx MXene was synthesized from the optimized Ti3AlC2 MAX phase according to the protocol elaborated in the earlier publication22. In short, for the synthesis of Ti3C2Tx MXene, 1 g of optimized Ti3AlC2 MAX was first washed using 9 M hydrochloric acid (HCl) for 18 h to remove intermetallic impurities and the mixed with an etchant solution (6:3:1 mixture (by volume) of 12 M HCl, deionized water, and 50 wt % hydrofluoric acid (HF). The etching reaction was set up for 24 h at 35 °C with continuous stirring at 400 RPM. The etched multilayered Ti3C2Tx MXene was washed with deionized water via repeated centrifugation at 3234 RCF (4-5 cycles with ~200 mL of deionized water) until the supernatant reached pH ∼6. For delamination, the multilayered Ti3C2Tx MXene was added to lithium chloride solution, typically 50 mL/per gram of starting etched powder, and stirred at 400 RPM for 1 h at 65 °C under an inert environment. The mixture was then washed with deionized water via centrifugation at 3234 RCF for 5, 10, 15, and 20 min. Then, the final Ti3C2Tx MXene mixture was vortexed, followed by centrifugation at 2380 RCF for 30 min to ensure the Ti3C2Tx MXene solutions were single-to-few-layered flakes. The synthesized Ti3C2Tx MXene had a density (ρ), zeta potential (ξ), and bulk electrical conductivity of 4.21 g cm−3, −40 mV, and ~21000 S/cm, respectively. The freshly prepared Ti3C2Tx MXene was used for electrochemical studies.

Electrode modification

Initially, the bare GCE (BASi, MF-2012) was polished with alumina slurry and rinsed using distilled water, followed by methanol. It was then water-sonicated for 10 min. 10 mg/mL of Ti3C2Tx MXene stock solution was prepared and further diluted to 4 mg/mL. Five microliters of the 4 mg/mL Ti3C2Tx was then drop coated on the clean GCE and dried under an IR lamp for a few minutes before use in the electrochemical study. Before each experiment, the GCE was polished with an alumina-water slurry and rinsed with distilled water followed by a drop coating of Ti3C2Tx MXene as described earlier.

Characterization of Ti3C2Tx MXene

XRD patterns of the as-synthesized Ti3AlC2 MAX and Ti3C2Tx MXene were analyzed using a Bruker D8 X-ray diffractometer with a Cu Kα (λ = 1.5406 Å) emitter and a VANTEC 500 two-dimensional x-ray detector (XRD2). A corundum standard was used to ensure each detector was calibrated. The precursor samples were mounted on Kapton tapes and scanned from 5° to 80° with a step size of 5° and a dwell time of 30 s per step. The traditional XRD plots were obtained by merging and integrating the XRD2 data in DIFFRAC.SUITE EVA software. SEM was performed on a JEOL JSM-7800F at an acceleration voltage of 15 kV to study the flake size and surface morphology. The solution concentration was maintained at <0.1 mg/mL and loaded on an anodic disc followed by vacuum drying for 2 h. The samples were gold sputtered to reduce the charging. FEI Tecnai Osiris S/TEM was used to obtain low and high-resolution images of the Ti3C2Tx flakes using an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. AFM and TERS characterization were performed on a LabRAM-Nano system (HORIBA Scientific). Seven hundred and eighty-five nanometer excitation with the laser power on the sample of ~200 µW was used for collecting TERS spectra. Both excitation and collection were done through the side 100X, 0.7 NA objective (Mitutoyo), inclined at 25° to the sample plane. Access-SNC-Ag TERS probes (APPNano) were used for the TERS imaging in Spec-TopTM (HORIBA) mode. The ex-situ Raman spectro-electrochemical study was conducted using Renishaw Invia Raman equipped with an argon laser (λ0 = 532 nm, source power 20 mW). 100X objective lens (NA = 0.90) was used to focus the laser beam with a power of about 1 mW at the Ti3C2Tx MXene coated glassy carbon surface. The Raman spectrometer was operated in an extended mode with spectral window covering 50 to 800 cm−1.

Humic acid characterization in real samples

An organic matter (OM) absorbance fluorescence spectroscopy method was used to characterize the presence and possible interference of the humic acid component in the real sample; tap water, lake water, and agricultural runoff. Distilled water (DI) was used as a calibration standard. In short, a HORIBA Aqualog fluorimeter was used for the optical absorbance measurement, where excitation wavelengths of 240 nm–450 nm window was used with a 3 nm step size. Simultaneously, the fluorescence was measured for emission wavelengths of 300 nm–600 nm (instrument default increment of 3.28 nm was used for the emission signal to collect). The Rayleigh scattering was blocked by a scatter masking and other filter corrections were used inside the fluorimeter to the raw fluorescent data and the processed data was corrected and normalized. Dissolved organic matter (DOM) was calculated by using the specific ultraviolet absorbance at 254 nm (Abs254). The Fluorescence Index (FI) was calculated from the fluorescence data as a measure of DOM. The humification index (HI) was calculated to estimate the extent of humification. The humic-like contributions were obtained from the intensity peaks of Peaks A, M, and C44,45.

Ti3C2Tx MXene/GCE sample preparation for Ex-situ spectro-electrochemical study

Five microliters of 4 mg/mL Ti3C2Tx MXene solution was drop coated on a glassy carbon electrode and let to dry under an IR lamp for a few minutes. The Raman spectrum of the dried, bare Ti3C2Tx MXene was obtained. The same Ti3C2Tx MXene coated electrode was then immersed for 5 min in a solution containing 1 mM ((NH4)6Mo7O24 ˙ 4H2O), 0.1 M H2SO4, 0.1 M KCl and 100 µM of KH2PO4. After immersion, it was air-dried until no visible moisture remained. Raman measurement was performed on the dried electrode to analyze perturbance of the α-isomer in the Ti3C2Tx MXene crystal lattice structure. The same electrode was subsequently used for DPV in the same electrolyte solution as above, using a platinum counter electrode and Ag/AgCl reference electrode scanning from 0 V to 0.24 V. Following DPV, the electrode was removed, air-dried, and Raman spectroscopy measurement was performed again to observe any spectral change associated with the disturbance of β-isomer on Ti3C2Tx MXene crystal lattice.

Electrochemical measurements

All electrochemical measurements were performed at room temperature using an Interface 1010E potentiostat from Gamry Instruments along with Ag/AgCl (3 M NaCl) reference electrode, platinum wire counter electrode, and Ti3C2Tx MXene modified GCE working electrode (Ti3C2Tx MXene/GCE). The redox reactions at the Ti3C2Tx MXene/GCE working electrode were investigated by first performing the cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements in a 0.1 M KCl supporting electrolyte with a potential window between −1 V vs. Ag/AgCl and 1 V vs. Ag/AgCl. The presence of an oxidation peak within the specified potential window required setting the potential range for subsequent redox probe and P sensing measurements below the oxidation potential of Ti3C2Tx MXene. The redox probe sensing was carried out in 0.1 M KCl and 5 mM H18Cl3N6Ru from −0.3 V vs. Ag/AgCl to 0.03 V vs. Ag/AgCl. P sensing utilized a solution consisting of 1 mM ((NH4)6Mo7O24 ˙ 4H2O), 0.1 M H2SO4, 0.1 M KCl as the base solution, with KH2PO4 (the source of P) analyte concentrations ranging from 1 µM to 1000 µM. The CV corresponding to the P sensing measurements was taken between 0 V vs. Ag/AgCl and 0.25 V vs. Ag/AgCl voltage window. Each CV measurement employed a 2 mV step size over 3 cycles. The differential pulse voltammetry (DPV) technique was used primarily for electrochemical sensing of various P concentrations, interference studies, the real sample analysis, and the hysteresis assessment. The DPV-based P sensing utilized the same solution medium as the CV P sensing. Interference studies were conducted in the base solution with the addition of 100 µM each of NaNO3, K2SO4, and Na2CO3. The real samples were measured using DPV within the base solution, and 1 ml of real sample was added. The optimized experimental parameters for DPV were as follows: the potential window of 0 V vs. Ag/AgCl to 0.24 V vs. Ag/AgCl, step size of 2 mV, sample period of 2 s, pulse time of 0.1 s, and pulse size of 50 mV. All the electrochemical experiments were repeated n number of times (n > 3) to ensure reproducibility of the results.
