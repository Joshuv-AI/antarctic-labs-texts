Quality‐driven reformulation of Plantago ovata enriched non‐fermented beef salami
Authors: Tulay Elal Mus, Tulay Ozcan, Gokce Keser, Tugce Necla Selvi, Figen Cetinkaya, Hakan Ustuner, Nursen Senturk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUND Plantago ovata (psyllium) is a hydrocolloid additive that is used widely in foods; however, meat applications of this bioactive ingredient are limited. This study evaluated the effects of 0% to 5% Plantago husk on non‐fermented beef salami during 60 days of storage. Proximate composition, physicochemical properties, texture profile, sensory characteristics, and multivariate analyses were used.RESULTSThe incorporation of Plantago husk significantly modified product composition and quality characteristics. Dietary fiber and ash content increased with higher inclusion levels of the husk, whereas fat content remained unchanged across all formulations. During storage, pH values fluctuated within a narrow range, whereas water activity remained relatively stable. Samples formulated with Plantago husk demonstrated improved technological properties, exhibiting enhanced emulsion stability and cooking yields at 30 days of storage. Color analysis revealed that L* and a* values changed during storage, whereas b* values were generally less affected. Texture profile analysis indicated that intermediate levels of husk addition resulted in the most pronounced increases in product hardness, gumminess, and chewiness. Sensory evaluation showed that all non‐fermented salami formulations were well accepted, with the intermediate inclusion levels yielding the most favorable sensory profiles.CONCLUSIONThe results indicated that the incorporation of Plantago husk into non‐fermented beef salami can contribute to product development by improving technological performance and increasing dietary fiber content. Overall, Plantago husk showed potential as a functional ingredient that supports acceptable product quality while providing fiber enrichment in meat formulations. © 2026 The Author(s). INTRODUCTION

Processed meats are meat products treated by techniques such as smoking, salting, and curing to improve product quality and storage stability. 1 Consumption of processed meat products is gradually increasing due to consumers' demand for taste, flavor, texture, nutrition, and ready‐to‐eat convenience. 2 There is also an increasing predisposition toward healthier eating habits. This prompts consumers to seek new healthy food options, especially products with beneficial nutritional and functional properties. 3

Natural plant‐derived ingredients are gaining attention in meat processing and are used to improve quality and create clean‐label formulations. Plantago ovata husk, with its high fiber content, is particularly suited for these applications. 4 The P. ovata seed husks are known as psyllium or isabgol, and contain a significant amount of soluble and insoluble fiber. 5

Psyllium is used in food for two purposes: as a dietary fiber enhancer and as a hydrocolloid additive. 6 It provides functional benefits when added to food formulations by increasing viscosity, water absorption, and solubility. The gel‐forming ability of psyllium husk makes it a useful texture‐improving agent in various foods. 7 In the meat matrix, psyllium enhances thickness, emulsion stability, fat and water retention, and the product's textural quality. 8 , 9 Myofibrillar protein emulsions exhibit improved emulsifying capacity and oxidative stability under low‐salt conditions with the incorporation of Plantago husk. 10 The shelf life of meat products can also be extended by adding psyllium and other dietary fibers. 11

In previous studies, many dietary fiber sources, including rice husk flour, 12 chickpea, 13 jack bean, 14 soy flour, 15 wheat bran, 16 and coconut flour, 17 were used in processed meat formulations. Unlike these fiber sources, Plantago (psyllium) is a Food and Drug Administration (FDA)‐approved dietary fiber with positive physiological effects on human health. 18 Due to this specification, Plantago is used as a functional ingredient in food formulations, and its application in the meat matrix may offer significant potential for enhancing both quality characteristics and functional value.

A recent study showed that consuming psyllium‐enriched hamburger meatballs decreased the postprandial rise of triglyceride, very‐low‐density lipoprotein level, and blood glucose level. 19 Existing research on the use of Plantago (psyllium) in processed meat formulations has predominantly focused on chicken sausage, 20 , 21 , 22 pork sausage, 23 , 24 pork salami, 25 pork meatballs, 26 beef hamburgers, 27 and goat meat patties. 28 However, limited information is available on specific studies investigating the application of Plantago fiber in processed beef products and its effects on product quality.

The psyllium–starch matrix provides a food‐grade, adaptable platform for stabilizing and delivering bioactive plant compounds. 29 Taking the above into account, a non‐fermented beef salami formulation was developed by replacing starch with Plantago husk fiber to enhance processed meat product quality and to deliver functional benefits for health‐conscious consumers. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the effect of the addition of Plantago husk fiber on the physicochemical properties, texture profile, sensory characteristics, and storage stability of the non‐fermented beef salami during a 60‐day refrigerated storage period.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

The beef meat, tallow, and lamb tail fat were procured from a local butcher in Bursa, Turkiye. The meat was transported under a cold chain to the Meat Products Unit of the Faculty of Veterinary Medicine at Bursa Uludag University for processing. Processing equipment utilized in the Meat Products Unit included an ice machine, meat grinder, bowl chopper, stuffing machine (Ari Makina, Istanbul, Turkiye), and smoking/scalding chamber (Hotec, Istanbul, Turkiye). The organic Plantago husk powder used as a fiber constituent in this study was obtained from Naturiga (Istanbul, Turkiye). It contained 77 g 100 g−1 dietary fiber, 4.3 g 100 g−1 carbohydrate, 4.3 g 100 g−1 protein, 2 g 100 g−1 fat, 0.3 g 100 g−1 sugar, and 0.06 g 100 g−1 salt. The potato starch, skim milk, ascorbic acid, nitrite curing salt (Kimbiotek, Istanbul, Turkiye), ginger, black pepper, sweet paprika, coriander (Knorr Spices, Istanbul, Turkiye), and granulated beetroot sugar (Balkupu, Aksaray, Turkiye) were used in the production of non‐fermented salami.

Preparation of salami with Plantago fiber incorporated

Six different non‐fermented salami formulations were produced in duplicate: a control and five Plantago husk‐incorporated treatments. The formulations consisted of 50% beef meat, 17% tallow, 3% lamb tail fat, 2.5% spices, 1% skim milk, 0.25% beetroot sugar, 20% ice, 1.25% nitrite curing salt, 5% potato starch or Plantago husk powder (PHP), and 500 mg kg−1 ascorbic acid. The control sample was prepared with 5% potato starch, whereas PHP was used at five different concentrations as a substitute for potato starch in the treatment groups. Plantago husk proportionally replaced potato starch within the total 5% binder content in the following treatment groups: PH1 (1% PHP + 4% starch), PH2 (2% PHP + 3% starch), PH3 (3% PHP + 2% starch), PH4 (4% PHP + 1% starch), and PH5 (5% PHP).

Beef meat, spices, skim milk, ascorbic acid, and nitrite curing salt were mixed in a bowl chopper for 3 min. Ice was then added at 20% (w/w), and mixing continued for an additional 2 min. Subsequently, the salami batter was portioned, and different levels of starch and Plantago husk were added and mixed for 3 min. The batter was filled, using a stuffing machine, into polyamide casings with a 51 mm diameter. The stuffed casings were tied and pre‐incubated at 55 ± 2 °C for 20 min, followed by smoking in a chamber at 65 ± 2 °C for 25 min. The salami formulations were then scalded to a core temperature of 71 ± 2 °C for 10 min and immediately cooled under cold water. Samples were vacuum packed in polyethylene bags, stored at 4 ± 1 °C for 2 months, and analyzed on days 1, 30, and 60.

Proximate composition

The proximate composition of the salami samples was determined on day 1 using Association of Official Analytical Chemists (AOAC) standard procedures for nutrient analysis. 30 Protein content was evaluated using the Kjeldahl method. Fat content was assessed using diethyl ether as an extraction solvent in a Soxhlet apparatus. Moisture content was determined through the gravimetric method, and ash content was measured by incinerating samples in an oven for 4 h at 550 °C. 30 Dietary fiber was analyzed using the enzymatic–gravimetric method. 31 Carbohydrate content was estimated by difference, subtracting the percentages of protein, lipid, fiber, ash, and moisture content from 100%.

pH and water activity

The pH of the samples was measured using a pH meter (Mettler Toledo, Columbus, OH, USA). 30 Water activity (aw) measurement was carried out using a water activity meter (Aqualab, Washington, DC, USA). Approximately 5 g of the salami sample was ground and placed in the device's cup at 25 ± 2 °C. The calibration of the device was performed according to the manufacturer's instructions. 32 The pH and aw values of each sample were determined on days 1, 30, and 60.

Color analyses

The instrumental color analysis of the salami samples was performed using a colorimeter (Konica Minolta CM508d, Osaka, Japan) on days 1, 30, and 60. Before color measurement, the salami formulations were equilibrated to room temperature for 30 min, and samples were cut to a height of 3 cm and a diameter of 2.5 cm to determine color properties. The color parameters L* (lightness), a* (redness/greenness), and b* (yellowness/blueness) were determined. Whiteness was calculated as described by Hunt et al. 33 Color was measured three times on the cut surface of each salami sample, and the average of the readings was recorded as the final value. Cooking yield and emulsion stability

Salami samples were placed in vacuum‐sealed, heat‐resistant polyethylene bags to prevent water intrusion and kept in an 80 °C water bath for 45 min to determine the cooking yield. Subsequently, the vacuum packages were removed from the water bath and cooled under running water. Cooking yield (%) was quantified as the ratio of the raw and cooked weights of the salami samples. 35 The emulsion stability of the samples was determined according to the method described by Jimenez‐Colmenero et al. 36 Approximately 50 g (exact weight recorded) of the raw emulsion of the salami was placed in a centrifuge tube and centrifuged (Runne Heidelberg, Heidelberg, Germany) for 5 min at 757 × g. Subsequently, the sample was heated in a water bath (N‐Biotek, Gyeonggi‐do, Korea) for 15 min at 40 °C and then for 20 min at 70 °C. The supernatant was removed, and the pellet was weighed. Emulsion stability values were calculated using the ratio of the pellet and raw sample weights. The cooking yield and emulsion stability of the salami samples were determined on days 1, 30, and 60 of storage.

Texture profile analysis

Instrumental texture profile analysis (TPA) was performed using a TA‐XT Plus instrument (Stable Micro System Ltd, Godalming, United Kingdom) at 22–24 °C with a cylindrical (P/35) probe on days 1, 30, and 60. The samples were compressed to 50% of their original height in two cycles at a speed of 1 mm s−1 to obtain a force–time curve. The data were then processed using Windows (Stable Micro System) texture expert software. Hardness (N), adhesiveness (N s), springiness, cohesiveness, gumminess, and chewiness (N) were determined. Measurements were performed in triplicate for each sample. 37

Hedonic consumer test

The study was reviewed and approved by the local ethical committee of Bursa Uludag University (approval number: 2024–01/1). Informed consent was obtained from each panelist prior to participating in the sensory evaluation. The acceptance test was conducted with 48 consumers. Participants were aged 18–60 and consisted of 25 females and 23 males. The selection criterion was that consumers had to habitually consume processed meat/functional products. Consumer evaluation was conducted in a sensory laboratory using panel cabinets. Samples were presented in a disposable container and labeled with a three‐digit code. They were rated on a 9‐point hedonic scale (1 – dislike extremely, 2 – dislike strongly, 3 – dislike, 4 – slightly dislike, 5 – neither like nor dislike, 6 – slightly like, 7 – like, 8 – like very much, and 9 – like extremely). The samples were evaluated in terms of color, appearance, structure and texture, odor, taste, saltiness, aroma, and general acceptability. 20 The sensory characteristics of the samples were evaluated at the beginning of storage.

Quantitative descriptive analysis

The sensory analyses were carried out with ethics committee approval (2024‐01/1). A panel of 12 trained individuals performed quantitative descriptive analysis (QDA) in a laboratory complying with ISO 8589:2007 regulatory standard requirements. The panel included academic staff, students, and civil servants. All participants were familiar with the sensory protocol and trained in sensory analysis. Samples were stored as slices and evaluated using a 0–15 scale ranging from ‘little’ to ‘very much’. 20

Data analysis

The experiment was conducted using a completely randomized design. Salami production was performed in two independent batches, and all analyses were carried out with at least three replicates for each treatment. The effects of sample type and storage time, as well as their interaction, were evaluated using analysis of variance (ANOVA). When significant differences were detected, means were compared using Fisher's multiple comparison test. Statistical significance was considered at P < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using MINITAB 17 statistical software (Minitab, Coventry, UK). Principal component analysis (PCA) and agglomerative hierarchical clustering (AHC) were performed using XLSTAT (Addinsoft, New York, NY, USA).

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Proximate composition

Proximate composition analyses were conducted at the initial stage of storage, and Table 1 presents the results. The standard for cooked, cured, and chopped meat in Codex Alimentarius is that the maximum fat content of sausage is listed at 35 g 100 g−1. 38 The fat content of the samples with Plantago incorporated varied between 13.71–14.02 g 100 g−1 compared with the control sample at 13.94 g 100 g−1. The protein content was significantly different in the control group (P < 0.05). The highest protein content was the control sample (16.64 g 100 g−1) and the lowest was PH3 (16.13 g 100 g−1). Adding psyllium mucilage to the beef hamburger formulation reduced the protein concentration. 27 The moisture content was 59.16 g 100 g−1 for the control and varied between 52.19 g 100 g−1 and 58.08 g 100 g−1 in the incorporated samples (P < 0.05).

The chemical composition results for the non‐fermented beef salami were consistent with those reported for rice‐husk‐fortified beef 12 and chickpea‐incorporated lamb sausages. 13 Higher protein, ash, and carbohydrate content, and lower fat content were reported in a study on lamb sausage fortified with jack bean flour. 14 The addition of Plantago husk significantly (P < 0.05) improved the crude fiber content of non‐fermented salami samples. Physico‐chemical properties

Table 2 reports the pH and aw values of the non‐fermented salami samples. A slight increase in pH values of salami formulated with Plantago husk was observed on day 1. The pH values of PH2, PH3, and PH4 were found to be significantly (P < 0.05) different from the control during storage. However, the pH values of PH1 and PH5 were not significantly (P > 0.05) different from the control in the first or second month of storage. A study on the application of black sorghum bran in beef sausage formulations showed pH changes similar to those observed in the salami samples produced with Plantago husk on the initial day and during storage. 39 The aw values were not significant (P > 0.05) among the samples or across storage time. Both pH and aw are of critical importance in controlling microorganisms in processed foods. 40 The incorporation of Plantago resulted in steady aw values and minor pH changes, which are unlikely to alter microbial profiles or chemical reactions.

Table 3 summarizes the effect of adding Plantago fiber on the cooking yield of beef salami. Cooking yield is a crucial production indicator when considering the conversion of raw materials into products, and it represents an important criterion for manufacturers. 41 The heating process positively affects meat tenderness, and cooking generates weight loss. 42 In the present study, significantly higher cooking yields than the control were observed in the PH3, PH4, and PH5 samples on day 1 (P < 0.05). At the end of storage, a decrease in cooking yield was determined in the control and Plantago‐containing samples (P < 0.05). Similarly, the addition of psyllium to a pork meatball formulation 26 and the chicken breast myofibrillar protein gel 43 improved the cooking loss characteristics. The use of a different plant fiber source, artichoke bract powder, in beef sausage formulation also enhanced the cooking yield. 44 The US Department of Agriculture revealed approximately cooking yield values of 80% for pork sausage and 77% for turkey sausage in its table of cooking yields for meat and poultry. 35 The results of the present study were due to the high water retention properties of Plantago fiber.

Emulsion stability is another essential criterion for salami production. As Table 3 reports, Plantago samples with husk incorporated presented higher emulsion stability than the control sample. Among all treatments, emulsion stability was lowest in the PH1 samples and highest in PH2 (P < 0.05). During storage, emulsion stability increased in control, PH1, and PH2 samples and decreased in PH3, PH4, and PH5 samples (P < 0.05). Conversely, replacing phosphates with psyllium resulted in a decrease in emulsion stability in frankfurters. 24 The use of rice flour in sausage formulation has also been shown to decrease emulsion stability. 45 The stability and texture of emulsified meat products depend on miscellaneous factors such as the type and amount of non‐fat meat, fats, water content, additives, and ingredients. 46

Color is one of the most important attributes influencing consumers' perception of food quality. When selecting products, consumers give particular attention to their appearance, especially color. Controlling the color of food products during processing is therefore crucial not only for aesthetics but also as an indicator of freshness, ripeness, or processing quality. 47

Table 4 reports the color properties of non‐fermented beef salami samples. The L* values of salamis enriched with Plantago husk ranged from 69.21–75.76 at the beginning of storage, with the lowest lightness observed in sample PH5. During storage, differences in L* values were observed among the samples. The lightness of the control, PH3, and PH5 samples increased by the end of storage, whereas it decreased in PH2. The reduction in lightness may be associated with the intrinsic color of Plantago, which may contribute darker color compounds to the product matrix and consequently modify the final product's visual appearance. The a* values of control, PH3, and PH4 decreased over the storage period, whereas no significant changes were observed in PH1, PH2, and PH5. No differences were detected in the b* values of the samples. On day 1 of storage, the lowest BI was observed in the control sample and the highest was recorded in PH5. On day 30 of storage, BI values did not differ significantly among the samples; however, on day 60, the highest BI was found in PH2.

Meat color variation arises from myoglobin autoxidation and pseudoperoxidase activity, as well as the binding of myoglobin with phenolic compounds from plants. 48 These biochemical reactions may account for color differences in non‐fermented salami samples. The present study's results are consistent with previous findings showing a decrease in color properties with the addition of plant fiber (mushroom powder) in sausage formulation. 49 The color attributes of processed meats are affected by ingredients. In particular, adding fiber sources to the formulations is an important factor contributing to changes in visual appearance. 22 , 50

Texture profile analysis

Figure 1 presents the textural properties of non‐fermented beef salami samples containing different levels of Plantago husk. At the beginning of storage, the hardness values of the samples were found to be similar whereas at the end of storage the highest value among the fiber‐containing samples was observed in PH3. At all storage periods, the hardness values of the fiber‐enriched samples were higher than those of the control (P < 0.05). Regarding adhesiveness, a decrease was observed in the PH3, PH4, and PH5 samples during storage (P < 0.05), whereas springiness was not affected by Plantago addition and showed no significant difference (P > 0.05). Cohesiveness increased in the PH3 and PH5 samples during storage, but gumminess showed an increase in the PH3, PH4, and PH5 samples. The chewiness property of the PH1 sample remained unchanged throughout storage. However, chewiness increased in samples with Plantago husk levels above 2%, indicating that the incorporation of higher Plantago husk content enhanced this property. These results demonstrate that the concentration of Plantago husk plays a critical role in shaping the textural quality of fiber‐enriched functional salami formulations.

Previous studies have shown that the textural response of meat products to dietary fiber depends strongly on the specific fiber source. In reduced‐fat lamb patties, carboxymethyl cellulose and inulin exerted a softening effect by decreasing hardness, springiness, cohesiveness, gumminess, and chewiness. 51 In contrast, oat fiber increased hardness, cohesiveness, and chewiness in low‐fat cooked Paio sausage, whereas inulin had no significant effect on hardness. 52 Similarly, pea fiber and pea starch fractions produced a firmer, more cohesive, and chewier structure in beef burgers, with firmness increasing as pea fiber content increased. 53 Likewise, in papain‐treated meat loaf, the incorporation of apple, oat, pea, and inulin fibers increased compression force and promoted a more rigid and cohesive structure, with oat fiber showing the strongest effect. 54 It has also been noted that the selection of appropriate dietary fibers and their concentrations, according to the product matrix, can affect the sensory and textural characteristics of meat products significantly. 20 , 54

Figure 2(a) presents the PCA results based on the textural properties of salami samples containing different levels of Plantago husk. CONCLUSIONS

Non‐fermented beef salami samples enriched with Plantago ovata husk fiber hold significant potential within the spectrum of functional foods. Considering both instrumental and sensory results, the incorporation of 3% to 4% Plantago husk powder provided the most balanced overall performance in non‐fermented beef salami. In particular, these formulations exhibited a favorable technological and textural profile without compromising consumer acceptability. Although sensory differences were not statistically significant, PH3 and PH4 numerically exhibited superior scores for several quality attributes, suggesting that intermediate Plantago husk levels may be more suitable than lower or higher incorporation levels for achieving an appropriate balance in product quality. These findings suggest that Plantago fiber can be considered not only as an innovative technological additive in beef salami and similar processed beef meat products but also as a sustainable contributor to the valorization of food industry by‐products, while supporting clean‐label product development strategies.

Further research is required to comprehensively evaluate this quality‐driven reformulation approach by investigating the interactions of P. ovata with different meat types, processing techniques, and storage conditions. In particular, the effects on pigment stability and oxidative color changes in processed meat products have not yet been fully elucidated. Comparative studies involving different dietary fiber sources and formulation strategies would enable the identification of the most effective reformulation approach for optimizing the technological and sensory properties of non‐fermented beef salami.
