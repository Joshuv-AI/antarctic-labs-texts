An Integrated Dataset of Clinical and Microbial Profiles for Fecal Microbiota Transplantation
Authors: Sen Yan, Le Wang, Yue Xu, Wanyong Zhu, Ning Li, Qiyi Chen, Long Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFunctional gastrointestinal disorders (FGIDs) are prevalent and burdensome, yet progress in microbiota-targeted therapies such as fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) has been hampered by the lack of large-scale, integrated datasets. Current studies are mostly limited in sample size and scope, constraining mechanistic insight and precision application. To address this gap, we established FMTdb, a curated dataset focused on FGID patients treated with FMT. This resource integrates demographic, clinical, and multi-omic microbiota data from 15 rigorously screened long-term donors, 370 FGID recipients, and 2,008 healthy community controls. Longitudinal metadata include donor follow-up and pre- and post-treatment observations of recipients across multiple time points. By providing a multi-layered dataset that connects microbial composition with host response, FMTdb offers a robust platform for biomarker discovery, mechanistic exploration, and the development of personalized microbiota-based interventions for FGIDs.

Background & Summary

Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) involves the transfer of processed fecal material from healthy donors into the gastrointestinal tract of recipients, with the goal of restoring microbial homeostasis and treating conditions associated with gut dysbiosis1. International guidelines currently recommend FMT as an effective therapy for recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection (rCDI)2. More recently, its application has expanded beyond inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)—including ulcerative colitis and Crohn’s disease—to a broader spectrum of gastrointestinal disorders3. Notably, FMT is increasingly employed in the management of functional gastrointestinal disorders (FGIDs), such as chronic constipation, functional diarrhea, and irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)4. However, current FMT studies are often limited by small sample sizes, inconsistent protocols, and lack of standardized donor–recipient metadata.

Despite its considerable therapeutic potential, the determinants of FMT efficacy are still under active investigation. Stricter donor screening criteria can markedly reduce donor qualification rates, while simultaneously improving the efficacy of FMT and decreasing the incidence of adverse events5. Given the variability of donor microbiota, precise donor–recipient matching is essential to achieve optimal therapeutic outcomes6. Indeed, recent clinical trials have demonstrated, in certain diseases, that higher microbial diversity in donor stool, as well as the presence of specific taxa such as Lachnospiraceae and Ruminococcaceae, have been reported to be associated with improved treatment efficacy7,8. The engraftment of donor microbiota and the clinical outcomes of FMT may be influenced by multiple factors, particularly on the recipient side, including genetic background, immune status, the composition and functionality of the recipient’s indigenous gut microbiota, lifestyle habits, clinical condition, and medication use8,9. Several studies have evaluated the similarity between donor and recipient microbiota before and after FMT by calculating the proportion of donor strains that successfully engraft in the recipient’s gut10. Some studies have reported that higher levels of donor bacterial engraftment are associated with better clinical responses to FMT11. However, the optimal strategy for donor–recipient matching—particularly in patients with FGIDs—remains to be determined12.

Addressing this question requires large-scale, high-quality datasets of FMT donors and recipients, yet such resources are currently scarce. Our data collection effort is specifically designed to bridge this critical gap. Our team has compiled a dataset encompassing 2,393 participants, including 2,008 healthy community controls (HC), 15 qualified FMT stool donors (Don), and 370 FMT recipients (Rec). For all participants, the dataset documents health statistics, lifestyle factors, clinical measurements, and microbiota analyses, thereby ensuring the consistent use of high-quality donor material (Fig. 1). This resource provides a foundation for precision donor selection, efficacy prediction, and mechanistic exploration of host–microbiota interactions.

Methods

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Shanghai Tenth People’s Hospital. The ethical approval numbers are SHSY-IEC-4.1/20–116/01 and SHSY-IEC-5.0/22K186/P01. All patients provided written informed consent to participate and agreed to the use of their data for research and publication. Permission to use patient samples was also obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Shanghai Tenth People’s Hospital. Relevant clinical trials are registered at ClinicalTrials.gov under identifiers NCT05703477, NCT04965584, NCT06020365, NCT05873348, and NCT05990972.

Donor screening, recipient enrollment and FMT procedure

The process for recruiting participants is as follows: first, we publish a donor recruitment announcement on social media platforms like our WeChat official account and website. This includes the purpose of stool donation, an introduction to FMT, details of compensation, selection criteria, and a registration link. We conduct an initial screening based on the information provided by volunteers. Dedicated personnel then contact those who pass this screening for a medical examination, which mainly includes routine blood, urine, and stool tests, infectious source testing, metagenomic analysis of stool, drug-resistant bacteria, and gene testing, as well as psychological evaluation and diagnosis. Only candidates with normal results in all these tests can become donors. Additionally, they must undergo regular follow-up tests to ensure continued eligibility for stool donation.

Donors underwent stringent screening protocols to ensure safety and efficacy. Criteria included no significant medical history, age between 18–30, and a BMI of 18.5–23.9 kg/m2. Following a series of questionnaires and physical examinations, only when it is confirmed that the volunteer has no psychological illness, no relevant medical or family history, no related pathogens in their blood or stool, and can guarantee the continuity and stability of stool donation, as well as being able to control their diet strictly according to the clinical needs of patients, can they become a qualified donor13,14. Also, a sample for sequencing is retained with each stool donation, and a sample must be cryopreserved for potential future tracing of clinical issues15. Through such stringent criteria, our center has enrolled 15 long-term and stable donors to provide a consistent supply of bacterial liquid for clinical patients.

Recipients included 370 individuals with various functional gastrointestinal disorders. Detailed clinical evaluations determined their eligibility. The FMT procedure followed established protocols, including pre-transplant preparations and microbial fluid transplants. The fecal suspension was thawed in a 37 °C water bath and was infused into the patient through a nasoenteric tube placed in advance, within 6 hours after thawing. Clinical indicators were monitored to assess treatment outcomes and identify adverse events16.

Sample collection

The sample collection process is divided into donor and recipient sections. The specific collection process is as follows:

Sample Collection from Donors: Donors collect their fresh stool in a specialized collection room using a collection container, minimizing air exposure time. The fresh stool is then taken to the operation room and prepared into a standard bacterial suspension for patient use with the following ratio: 100 g of stool is diluted to 300 mL with PBS, filtered through gauze to remove solid residues, mixed with sterile glycerol (final concentration 25%), and stored at −80 °C17.

Recipient Sample Collection: Patient stool and blood samples are collected before and after each treatment. For stool sample collection, patients deposit their fresh stool in a collection tube containing a preservation solution, which is a 1:1 mixture of saline and RNA preservation solution, and store it at −80 °C. Blood samples, including serum, plasma, and blood cells, are handled as follows: Serum Collection: Using a 5 mL BD coagulation-promoting blood collection tube, 3–4 mL of peripheral venous whole blood is collected. After resting at room temperature (20 °C–25 °C) for 30 minutes, it is centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 minutes. The supernatant is collected and stored at −80 °C; Plasma and Blood Cells Collection: Using EDTA (dipotassium ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid) anticoagulant tubes, 2 mL of peripheral venous whole blood is collected. After resting at room temperature (20 °C–25 °C) for 30 minutes, it is centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 minutes. The supernatant is plasma, and the lower layer contains blood cells, which are stored separately in freezing tubes at −80 °C.

Clinical data collection and analysis

The study meticulously gathered clinical data from both donors and recipients. For donors, this included demographic information, medical history, and results from physical exams and laboratory tests. For recipients, data encompassed demographics, pre-transplant evaluations, treatment outcomes, and post-transplant clinical indicators.

The recipients have been diagnosed with a variety of functional gastrointestinal disorders, including constipation, functional diarrhea, IBS, as well as more severe conditions like rCDI, IBD such as Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), radiation enteritis, and incomplete intestinal obstruction. Meanwhile, we showed FMT treatment efficacy for patients with various diseases and the efficacy of transplants from different donors. Both relief and cure were collectively defined as ‘response’. All clinical assessments for FMT efficacy were conducted by a team of experienced physicians. The response criteria were applied to a total of 370 individuals, and follow-up evaluations were carried out at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months post-treatment. These evaluations included clinical symptoms, laboratory data, and gut microbiota profiles. No third-party evaluations were involved in this study.

Data analysis and visualization

Microbial genomic DNA was extracted from fecal samples using the PowerMax Extraction Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA quantification was then carried out using a NanoDrop 2000c spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). To amplify the 16S rRNA V4 region, the universal primers 515 F (5′-GTGYCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3′) and 806 R (5′-GGACTACNVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) were used in a Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR)18,19. The PCR reactions were set up in a 50 µL system with an initial denaturation at 98 °C for 30 seconds, followed by 25 cycles of denaturation at 98 °C for 15 seconds, annealing at 58 °C for 15 seconds, and extension at 72 °C for 15 seconds, concluding with a final extension at 72 °C for 1 minute. The PCR products were purified using AMPure XP Beads (Beckman Coulter, Indianapolis, IN, USA), and the DNA concentration was measured using the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The fragment lengths of the PCR products were confirmed by gel electrophoresis. The prepared DNA libraries underwent sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq6000 platform (paired-end, 2 × 150 bp) after quality analysis was conducted at Shanghai Biotecan Pharmaceuticals Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China)20.

QIIME2 version 2023.2.021 was employed to carry out DADA222 processing of the raw sequencing data. Initially, data underwent quality filtering to remove adapter and barcode sequences. Sequences were then dereplicated, evaluated for sequence variants, merged, and subjected to chimera checking through a standard DADA2 procedure. The average number of reads sequenced in our collected data is 62,346, while the average number of reads in the community population is 13,510. Detailed sequencing depth information for individual samples is available on the database website download page under the file name “Detailed sequencing”. (https://gift2disease.net/FMTdb/). Sequences were subsequently assigned with the SILVA 138.1 dataset23. The resulting table and taxonomy artifacts were exported as a biom table and text file, respectively, for subsequent analyses following the addition of taxa data to the biom-formatted ASV table.

This publicly available dataset includes comprehensive metadata from 15 long-term stool donors and 370 FMT recipients, along with 2,008 healthy community controls recruited in parallel.

Donor recruitment and microbiota profiles

Our center adheres to a unified set of selection standards and a standardized donor screening protocol. Potential donors must first pass preliminary screening based on age, body mass index (BMI), and high-risk behaviors. Those who meet these criteria then undergo a comprehensive clinical evaluation to exclude individuals with microbiome-related diseases, transmissible infections, chronic conditions, or psychiatric disorders. To ensure only the highest-quality donors are selected, candidates are required to undergo extensive stool and blood testing, lifestyle assessments, and bi-monthly re-evaluations to continuously monitor health status (Fig. 2A). Such stringent selection and rescreening procedures greatly enhance the safety and efficacy of FMT.

Recipient characteristics and clinical outcomes

Among the 370 FMT recipients, the majority underwent treatment for functional gastrointestinal disorders (FGIDs), primarily chronic functional constipation, functional diarrhea, and irritable bowel syndrome (IBS). A smaller subset of recipients were diagnosed with other gastrointestinal diseases, such as inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), including Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis. Our dataset also documents the frequent presence of comorbidities and concurrent diagnoses, such as cases of FGIDs accompanied by extra-intestinal conditions like anxiety. The primary diagnostic distribution of FMT recipients is summarized in the dataset statistics (Fig. 2C). The detailed information is available on the database website download page under the file name “The primary diagnostic distribution of FMT recipients”.

After obtaining signed informed consent using a standardized FMT consent form, baseline (pre-FMT) stool samples were collected from recipients alongside corresponding clinical examinations. Post-FMT stool samples were collected and analyzed in the same manner. Recipients were followed up at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months after transplantation to evaluate clinical efficacy and monitor potential delayed outcomes. Figure 3A presents treatment outcomes across different patient groups, revealing variations in FMT efficacy among disease categories. Outcome labels collected during follow-up are included as part of the clinical metadata, enabling users to stratify samples by outcome categories for downstream analyses. Notably, gastrointestinal symptoms in FGID patients generally showed an improvement to some extent. These observations provide a resource for exploring FMT-related patterns across different clinical contexts. Complementing these findings, Fig. 3B compares treatment success rates across FMT material from different donors, visually illustrating donor-dependent variations in efficacy across multiple recipient groups.

Donor–recipient interactions and engraftment dynamics

Our data demonstrate that, even under strictly controlled conditions, FMT outcomes can vary substantially. For instance, two patients with the same diagnosis (e.g., IBS) who both received transplants from the same donor may nonetheless exhibit markedly different clinical responses. This underscores the need to jointly consider both donor- and recipient-related factors to fully understand the determinants of FMT success.

Figure 3C,D illustrate the longitudinal 16S rRNA sequencing profiles of two patients undergoing fecal microbiota transplantation (Rec278 and Rec350), alongside the microbial compositions of their respective donors. Prior to transplantation, both recipients exhibited markedly reduced microbial diversity, dominated by a limited set of taxa such as Enterobacterales and Oscillospirales. Following the introduction of donor microbiota, their gut microbial communities showed a progressive enrichment of donor-associated taxa, including Bacteroidales and Lachnospirales, indicating a shift toward the donor signature. Notably, the degree of “donorization” differed between the two patients: Rec278 displayed a pronounced and rapid convergence toward the donor microbial profile, whereas Rec350 demonstrated only a partial shift, retaining more features of the baseline community. The detailed information is available on the database website download page under the file name “The primary diagnostic distribution of FMT recipients”. These data illustrate patterns of donor-recipient microbial convergence and variability.

Technical Validation

All data collection and processing methods followed standardized protocols to ensure accuracy and consistency. Sequencing data were generated using validated high-throughput technologies, and bioinformatics pipelines were employed to analyze the data. Quality control measures were implemented to verify the integrity of the data.

Donor eligibility was determined through a multi-stage screening procedure, including clinical evaluation and laboratory testing to exclude infectious agents and multidrug-resistant organisms. Only donors meeting all predefined criteria were included.

Sample collection and processing were standardized. Stool samples were processed and frozen within 30 min of passage, and recipient samples were handled under controlled conditions with stabilization measures to preserve nucleic acid integrity.

DNA extraction and sequencing were performed using consistent protocols with standard quality controls. Negative controls were included during amplification. Sequencing generated high read counts per sample, with base quality scores exceeding Q30 at > 90% of positions.

Sequence data were processed using QIIME2 with DADA2, including quality filtering, chimera removal, and error correction. Rarefaction analysis indicated sufficient sequencing depth for most samples. Taxonomic profiles were consistent with expected gut microbiota composition.

Clinical data were validated through double-checking against source records and consistency checks across time points. Discrepancies were resolved prior to analysis, and routine audits ensured data completeness.

Detailed protocols, raw sequencing data, and de-identified clinical metadata are publicly available to support reproducibility.

Usage Notes

The dataset is designed to facilitate research on FMT and functional gastrointestinal disorders. Researchers can query the dataset for specific donor and recipient information, perform statistical analyses, and explore microbial composition data. The dataset promotes collaboration and data sharing within the scientific community, enabling advancements in FMT research and clinical practice.

Workflow and analytical functions of the FMTdb platform. The FMTdb web portal provides an integrated framework for exploring fecal microbiota transplantation data. (1) Search: users can query by disease, donor, recipient, or microbial taxa. (2) Browse: hierarchical navigation allows exploration of donor–recipient pairs, disease categories, and microbial features. (3) Download: users can obtain recipient sequencing data before and after FMT, laboratory test results, donor sequencing profiles, and clinical efficacy information. (4) Analytical tools: the platform supports donor timeline analysis, pre/post-FMT comparisons for recipients, correlation analysis linking clinical and microbial parameters with therapeutic outcomes, and differential analysis of gut microbiota between patients and healthy controls. Together, these functions enable efficient data retrieval, visualization, and hypothesis generation for FMT research and clinical translation.

The FMTdb integrates data from 2,008 healthy community participants recruited as part of a large-scale population-based study in China. All participants reported no history of disease and met strict health criteria (BMI < 24 kg/m², fasting blood glucose < 6.1 mmol/L, and no antibiotic use within one month prior to sampling). Extensive metadata were collected for each volunteer, including demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, and clinical measurements. Fecal microbiota composition was profiled using a standardized 16S rRNA gene sequencing protocol targeting the V4 region.

This large healthy cohort provides a robust baseline for gut microbiota composition and diversity, enabling direct comparison with FMT recipients. By contrasting sequencing data from FGID patients with these healthy controls, researchers can identify FGID-specific alterations in the gut microbiota and explore potential therapeutic targets. Furthermore, the inclusion of healthy population data is highly valuable for donor screening, as it defines the normal range of microbiome features in healthy individuals and supports the selection of optimal stool donors.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cohort is heterogeneous, with variability in underlying diseases, patient characteristics, and treatment contexts, which may introduce confounding and limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, the study is observational and lacks a controlled design, precluding causal inference and making the results susceptible to residual bias. Third, outcome definitions are not fully standardized across different disease settings, which may affect comparability and interpretation of treatment effects. Fourth, data availability is constrained by website access, download restrictions, and varying levels of data accessibility, potentially introducing selection bias and limiting reproducibility. Finally, the use of 16S rRNA sequencing imposes inherent taxonomic resolution limitations and is subject to batch effects, which may influence microbial profiling accuracy and downstream analyses.
