PSN Game: Game-theoretic Planning via a Player Selection Network
Tianyu Qiu, Eric Ouano, Fernando Palafox, Christian Ellis, David Fridovich-Keil

Abstract
While game-theoretic planning frameworks are effective at modeling multi-agent interactions, they require solving optimization problems with hundreds or thousands of variables, resulting in long computation times that limit their use in large-scale, real-time systems. To address this issue, we propose PSN Game —a novel game-theoretic planning framework that reduces runtime by learning a Player Selection Network (PSN). A PSN outputs a player selection mask that distinguishes influential players from less relevant ones, enabling the ego player to solve a smaller, masked game involving only selected players. By reducing the number of variables in the optimization problem, PSN directly lowers computation time. The PSN Game framework is more flexible than existing player selection methods as it i) relies solely on observations of players’ past trajectories, without requiring full state, control, or other game-specific information; and ii) requires no online parameter tuning. We train PSNs in an unsupervised manner using a differentiable dynamic game solver, with reference trajectories from full-player games guiding the learning. Experiments in both simulated scenarios and human trajectory datasets demonstrate that i) PSNs outperform baseline selection methods in trajectory smoothness and length, while maintaining comparable safety and achieving a 10 × 10\times speedup in runtime; and ii) PSNs generalize effectively to real-world scenarios without fine-tuning. By selecting only the most relevant players for decision-making, PSNs offer a general mechanism for reducing planning complexity that can be seamlessly integrated into existing multi-agent planning frameworks.



Keywords:
Multi-agent systems, Key player selection, Game-theoretic planning

1
Introduction

Game-theoretic planning frameworks are widely used in robotics to model multi-agent interactions in terms of Nash (or other) equilibrium strategies identified by optimization-based algorithms.
Most existing approaches, however, focus on static scenarios or involve only a small number of agents (e.g.,
\leq 5
), where computational efficiency is rarely a concern. In contrast, scenarios involving many dynamic agents demand frequent replanning, making computational efficiency a critical bottleneck.
In such settings, the number of optimization variables, typically proportional to the number of agents, can grow rapidly and lead to significant delays.
Prior work
[
1
,
2
]
has shown that even in simple linear-quadratic games, computation time scales cubically with the total number of state and control variables for all agents, rendering game-theoretic planners impractical for large-scale use.

Interestingly, similar limitations are observed in human driving. Prior research on drivers’ attention
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
reveals that in dense traffic, monitoring all surrounding vehicles simultaneously is hard for human drivers, leading to distraction, delayed reactions, and increased accident risk.
Although robots are not subject to distraction, they still face computational constraints that require longer planning times as the number of agents increases.
In contrast, human drivers instinctively prioritize nearby vehicles that pose an immediate threat, such as those cutting in, merging, or failing to yield.
This behavior suggests two key insights: i) it is infeasible to consider all agents at once; and ii) focusing on a strategically selected subset of agents is often sufficient for safe and effective driving.

For game-theoretic planning,
Chahine et al. 2023

proposed ranking-based approaches that prioritize agents based on simple heuristics, such as proximity to the ego player or their impact on cost.
However, these approaches face several limitations:
i) They often rely on access to control inputs or game-specific parameters, which are typically unavailable in practice;
ii) Using a fixed number of selected players requires manual tuning and may either exclude important agents or include irrelevant ones; and finally
iii) In receding horizon planning, frequently switching selected agents can cause inconsistency in planning behavior and degrade trajectory quality.

and updates its state using (

To address these limitations, we propose PSN Game—a novel game-theoretic planning framework that reduces computation time by learning a Player Selection Network (PSN).
Before each planning step, the ego agent observes all agents’ past trajectories and passes them through the PSN, which outputs a player selection mask identifying the most influential agents.
The ego agent then solves a masked game over the selected subset, reducing the number of optimization variables and accelerating planning (Fig.
1
).
Our contributions are threefold:

1.

We introduce an unsupervised Player Selection Network (PSN), trained with a differentiable dynamic game solver that enables backpropagation through the selection mask.

2.

We develop a receding horizon game-theoretic planning framework that uses the PSN to identify equilibrium strategies more efficiently by solving reduced-size games.

3.

We empirically validate PSN Game in multi-agent simulations and real-world human trajectory datasets, showing up to a
10\times
speedup in runtime compared to full-player game solvers, while maintaining comparable safety metrics.

Beyond computational acceleration, PSN Game offers two key advantages over prior selection methods:
i) Flexible data usage: at runtime, the PSN relies only on past trajectory data—either full-state (e.g., position and velocity) or partial-state (e.g., position only)—without requiring control inputs, cost functions, or other game-specific parameters.
It also eliminates the need for online parameter tuning.
ii) Improved trajectory performance: By leveraging temporal patterns in past trajectories, the PSN achieves more consistent player selection, resulting in smoother and shorter ego trajectories.
These features make PSN Game broadly applicable across diverse multi-agent planning scenarios.

2.2
Player Selection for Planning in Multi-agent Scenarios

Existing methods for selecting important players in multi-agent settings generally fall into two categories: threshold-based and ranking-based approaches.

Threshold-based methods
highlight agents that breach a predefined distance threshold around the ego agent.
This idea has been widely adopted in works such as
[
23
,
24
]
, where a safety margin is first defined to select key agents before applying prediction or planning frameworks.
However, these methods heavily rely on threshold tuning, making them hard to generalize across different scenarios.

Ranking-based methods
, by contrast, aim to select the top-
k
most influential agents according to certain heuristics, such as distance from the ego agent.
Rule-based planning methods
[
25
,
26
]
directly identify
k
nearest neighbors (kNNs) via queries to, e.g. k-d trees
[
27
]
, BD-trees
[
28
]
, or R-trees
[
29
]
.
In learning-based approaches
[
30
,
31
]
, pooling mechanisms are employed to fix the input size to a neural network trained to identify nearest neighbors.
In the context of game-theoretic planning,
Chahine et al. 2023
introduced ranking metrics based on, e.g., the sensitivity of the ego agent’s cost function to other agents’ state.

However, ranking-based methods present several challenges. Selecting a fixed number of agents can be too aggressive (omitting critical players) or too conservative (including irrelevant ones), often requiring environment-specific parameter tuning.
Furthermore, many methods assume access to agents’ controls or other game-specific information, which may not be available in practice.

In contrast, our proposed PSN Game addresses the limitations of both threshold-based and ranking-based approaches.
PSN Game is lightweight in information requirements, relying only on past position observations at runtime without needing access to agents’ velocity, control inputs, player cost functions, or other game-specific information.
In particular, although cost functions are used during training to generate trajectories, PSN Game does not require that information at test time, enabling flexible deployment across different environments with model mismatch.
Experiments demonstrate that it not only improves computational efficiency but also enhances ego-agent trajectory smoothness and length, while maintaining safety performance across a range of multi-agent navigation scenarios.

3.1
Preliminaries: Nash Games

A finite horizon, discrete-time, open-loop Nash game with
N
agents is characterized by their respective states
{x_{k}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}
, and control inputs
{u_{k}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}
,
{i\in\{1,\dots,N\}\equiv[N]}
, over time
k\in[T]
, where
T
is the planning horizon.
Each agent
i
’s state transition from time
k
to
k+1
is governed by the dynamics
{x_{k+1}^{i}=f^{i}(x_{k}^{i},u_{k}^{i})}
.
We denote agent
i
’s trajectory and control sequence compactly as
{\textbf{x}^{i}\coloneqq(x_{0:T}^{i})}
and
{\textbf{u}^{i}\coloneqq(u_{0:T-1}^{i})}
; and similarly, all agents’ states/control input at time
k
by
\textbf{x}_{k}
,
\textbf{u}_{k}
and their trajectory/control sequence over time by
x
,
u
, respectively.
The function
J^{i}(\textbf{x},\textbf{u};\textbf{w}^{i})\coloneqq\sum_{k=1}^{T}c_{k}^{i}(\textbf{x}_{k},\textbf{u}_{k};\textbf{w}^{i})
defines the
{i^{\text{th}}}
agent’s cumulative cost, and is parameterized by the weighting vector
\textbf{w}^{i}
.
Agents must satisfy private constraints
\textbf{P}\coloneqq\{\rho^{q}\}_{q=1}^{n_{\textbf{P}}}
encoded by functions
\rho^{q}(\textbf{x}^{i},\textbf{u}^{i})\geq 0
.
The game is thus fully characterized by all agents’ costs parameterized by weight
\textbf{w}\coloneqq(\textbf{w}^{1:N})
, initial states
\textbf{x}_{0}
, dynamics
\textbf{f}\coloneqq(f^{1:N})
and constraints, and is denoted as
{\Gamma(\textbf{w},\textbf{x}_{0},\textbf{f},\textbf{P})}
.
In this game, each agent aims to minimize its cost while adhering to dynamics and constraints, i.e., agent
i
solves the optimization problem minimizing the time-cumulative sum of private cost
c_{p,k}^{i}
and shared cost
c_{s,k}^{i}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{N}c_{k}^{ij}
, where
c_{k}^{ij}
is the pairwise shared cost:

\displaystyle\min_{\textbf{x}^{i},\textbf{u}^{i}}\qquad\underbrace{\sum_{k=1}^{T}[c_{p,k}^{i}(x_{k}^{i},u_{k}^{i};\textbf{w}^{i})+c_{s,k}^{i}(\textbf{x}_{k},\textbf{u}_{k};\textbf{w}^{i})]}_{J^{i}(\textbf{x},\textbf{u};\textbf{w}^{i})=\sum_{k=1}^{T}c_{k}^{i}(\textbf{x}_{k},\textbf{u}_{k};\textbf{w}^{i})}

(1a)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\qquad x_{k+1}^{i}=f^{i}(x_{k}^{i},u_{k}^{i}),\quad k\in[T],

(1b)

\displaystyle\hskip 31.2982pt\rho^{q}(\textbf{x}^{i},\textbf{u}^{i})\geq 0,\quad\rho^{q}\in\textbf{P},

(1c)

which is inherently coupled with each other agent’s (
j\neq i
) own problem.

Generalized Open-Loop Nash Equilibrium
: If inequalities

J^{i}(\textbf{x}^{*},\textbf{u}^{*};\textbf{w}^{i})\leq J^{i}(\textbf{x}^{i},\textbf{x}^{-i*},\textbf{u}^{i},\textbf{u}^{-i*};\textbf{w}^{i}),\quad i\in[N],

(2)

concerning trajectories and control inputs of agent
i
(
\textbf{x}^{i},\textbf{u}^{i}
) and other agents (
\textbf{x}^{-i},\textbf{u}^{-i}
),
are satisfied for all
\textbf{x}^{i},\textbf{u}^{i}
that remain feasible with respect to (
1b
) and (
1c
), then
\textbf{u}^{i*}
is called a generalized Open-Loop Nash equilibrium (GOLNE) strategy with
\textbf{x}^{i*}
being the corresponding GOLNE trajectory.
This inequality indicates that no agent can reduce their cost by unilaterally deviating from
{\textbf{u}^{i*}}
[
32
]
.

Differentiability of Nash game solvers.
A key property of solutions
(\textbf{x}^{*},\textbf{u}^{*})
to generalized Nash problems is that they are (directionally) differentiable with respect to problem parameters
w
, as discussed in
[
33
]
.
In our work, we employ the solver in
[
9
]
, which leverages implicit differentiation to efficiently compute those derivatives and thus enables backpropagation during training.

3.2
Masked Nash Game with Selected Players

We first define the concept of a
player selection mask
, which helps the ego agent to select the important agents during planning:

Definition 1

(Player Selection Mask)
.

Suppose that the
i^{\text{th}}
player is the ego agent.
Then, the player selection mask is denoted as
{M^{i}\coloneqq(m^{ij})\in\{0,1\}^{N-1}}
, where

m^{ij}\coloneqq\begin{cases}1,\quad&\text{Agent $j\in[N]$ is included in the game}\\
0,\quad&\text{Agent $j\in[N]$ is excluded from the game.}\end{cases}

(3)

Given the player selection mask
M^{i}
, we construct the
Masked Nash Game
as follows:

Definition 2

(Masked Nash Game)
.

Given an open-loop Nash game
{\Gamma(\textbf{w},\textbf{x}_{0},\textbf{f},\textbf{P})}
, a masked Nash game for agent
i
is denoted as
{\Gamma^{i}(\tilde{\textbf{w}},\tilde{\textbf{x}}_{0},\tilde{\textbf{f}},\tilde{\textbf{P}})}
,
where we define the set of unmasked agents
U^{i}\coloneqq\{j\neq i~|~m^{ij}=1\}
and let

\tilde{\textbf{w}}\coloneqq(\textbf{w}^{j})_{j\in U^{i}},\ \tilde{\textbf{x}}_{k}\coloneqq(x_{k}^{j})_{j\in U^{i}},\ \tilde{\textbf{f}}\coloneqq(f^{j})_{j\in U^{i}},\ \tilde{c}_{s,k}^{i}(\textbf{x}_{k},\textbf{u}_{k};\textbf{w}^{i})\coloneqq\sum_{j\in U^{i}}c_{k}^{ij}(\textbf{x}_{k},\textbf{u}_{k};\textbf{w}^{i}).

(4)

and the sets

\tilde{\textbf{P}}\coloneqq\left\{\rho^{q}~\Big|~\frac{\partial\rho^{q}}{\partial\textbf{x}^{j}}\neq\textbf{0}\text{ or }\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial\textbf{u}^{j}}\neq\textbf{0}~\forall j\in U^{i}\right\}

(5)

correspond to constraints which locally depend on the state and/or control input of unmasked agents.

The masked receding horizon game preserves only the specific parameters, states, and constraints relevant to those agents most relevant to agent
i
.
Agent
i
then solves the masked Nash game
{\Gamma^{i}(\tilde{\textbf{w}},\tilde{\textbf{x}}_{0},\tilde{\textbf{f}},\tilde{\textbf{P}};M^{i})}
, and employs its masked GOLNE strategy
{\tilde{u}^{i*}}
as its own plan.

3.3
Player Selection Network

Given this construction, we now turn to the challenge of identifying the set of key players which ego agent
i
should consider in its masked game model of the interaction in order to maintain safety and efficiency while minimizing the computational burden of solving the corresponding game to obtain its strategy at each time
k
.
We propose the Player Selection Network (PSN), which is a neural network whose task is to infer a mask
M^{i}
which balances performance with sparsity.
Based on the available information, we present two variants of the network: PSN-Full, whose input is all agents’ past states
\textbf{x}_{0:t}
, and PSN-Partial, whose input is only a partial observation of state, i.e.
\{h(\textbf{x}_{k})\}_{k=0}^{t}
for a known function
h
.
For example,
h(\textbf{x}_{k})
could return only the Cartesian position of all agents on the road, and not their velocities.
Both variants’ output is the inferred mask
M^{i}
.
Player
i
will then solve the masked game
{\Gamma^{i}(\tilde{\textbf{w}},\tilde{\textbf{x}}_{t},\tilde{\textbf{f}},\tilde{\textbf{P}};M^{i})}
to obtain his strategy
\hat{\textbf{u}}_{t:T}^{i}
.

Remark 1
.

In the ideal case, the mask
m^{ij}
should be binary as defined in (
3
) and there would be
2^{N-1}
possible masks
M^{i}
.
Although it is possible to train a neural network with categorical outputs via the Gumbel softmax reparameterization
[
34
]
, it becomes too expensive when the initial number of players
N
is large.
To overcome such limitations, as well as to enable backpropagation of the neural network, we set the output masks as a continuous value
m^{ij}\in[0,1]
, and loosen the constraints with a sufficiently small slack parameter
\varepsilon>0
, reinterpreting the constraints as

\rho^{q}(\textbf{x}^{i},\textbf{u}^{i})+\frac{\varepsilon}{m^{ij}}\geq 0,\forall\rho^{q}\in\tilde{\textbf{P}},

(6)

where
\varepsilon/m^{ij}\rightarrow\infty
as
m^{ij}\rightarrow 0
and
\varepsilon/m^{ij}\rightarrow 0
as
m^{ij}\rightarrow 1
.
This allows us to use a continuous
m^{ij}
to represent the filtering of constraints.
At runtime, a threshold
m_{\text{th}}
is introduced to convert the continuous output of PSN to binary masks (
1
if larger than
m_{\text{th}}
and
0
otherwise).

The loss function of the neural network is defined as
L=L_{\text{Binary}}+\sigma_{1}L_{\text{Mask}}+\sigma_{2}L_{\text{Sim}}

where

L_{\text{Binary}}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left|0.5-|0.5-m^{ij}|\right|,\qquad L_{\text{Mask}}=\frac{\|M^{i}\|_{1}}{N},\qquad L_{\text{Sim}}=\sum_{k=t}^{T}\|p_{k}^{i}-h(\hat{\textbf{x}}_{k}^{i})\|_{2}.

(7)

and
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
are the respective loss weights, and
p_{k}^{i}
for
k\in\{t,t+1,\dots,T\}
is the time-
k
observation associated with the trajectory of the ego agent
i
if it were to consider all other agents during planning.
We use
L_{\text{Binary}}
to encourage mask
m^{ij}
to converge to either
0
or
1
, and use
L_{\text{Mask}}
to encourage agent
i
to consider fewer other agents.
The similarity loss
L_{\text{Sim}}
implicitly preserves the trajectory and safety performance that arises when all agents are considered.

Advantages of Threshold-Based Methods over Ranking-Based Methods
: As a threshold-based method, PSN Game provides several advantages over ranking-based methods:

Adaptive selection:
Choosing an appropriate threshold (e.g.,
m_{\text{th}}=0.5
) is simpler and more robust across scenarios than fixing the number of selected players.

Variable player set:
Thresholding allows the number of selected players to adapt naturally to environmental density, unlike ranking-based methods that enforce a fixed number. Trajectory visualization in Fig.
3
illustrates that ranking-based methods may force the ego agent to select irrelevant players to meet a fixed quota, leading to extra computation without improving planning outcomes.

Better flexibility:
Threshold-based methods can easily convert to ranking-based selections if needed, but not vice versa due to metric variability across environments.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we propose PSN Game, a novel game-theoretic planning framework that leverages a Player Selection Network to address the escalating time complexity caused by large numbers of agents in multi-agent planning.
Empirical results on both simulated environments and real-world human trajectory datasets demonstrate that PSN Game significantly improves ego-agent trajectory performance while maintaining planning safety.
Moreover, PSN offers flexible data requirements and can be seamlessly integrated into existing multi-agent planning pipelines, enabling scalable, efficient, and safe planning across diverse scenarios.

6
Limitations

While PSN Games demonstrates a significant reduction in runtime and improvement in trajectory performance, we observe two main limitations for our framework: i) the model in (
1
) is
complete information
, i.e., it assumes that all agents know one another’s costs and constraints, which can be unrealistic in real world interactions;
ii) our framework only takes agents’ trajectories as input, while richer information (e.g. visual context, vehicle type, etc.) is ignored when inferring which are the key agents.
Future work should i) relax the complete information assumption and consider more complex equilibrium concepts which model the resulting interactions more precisely; and
ii) leverage vision-based and multimodal foundation models to condition the Player Selection Network on additional context information.

7.2
Game Structure

In this work, we use the following game setup for training and evaluation:
N
players are moving towards their goals while avoiding each other. At each time
k
, the
i^{\text{th}}
player’s state
x_{k}^{i}
encodes its position
{p_{k}^{i}=[p_{x,k}^{i},p_{y,k}^{i}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}
and velocity
{v_{k}^{i}=[v_{x,k}^{i},v_{y,k}^{i}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}
and evolves according to
double-integrator dynamics
, i.e.,

\displaystyle x_{k+1}^{i}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}p_{k+1}^{i}\\
v_{k+1}^{i}\\
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}I_{2}&I_{2}\Delta\\
\textbf{0}&I_{2}\end{bmatrix}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}p_{k}^{i}\\
v_{k}^{i}\\
\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{0}\\
I_{2}\end{bmatrix}\Delta\cdot a_{k}^{i}=\underbrace{Ax_{k}^{i}+B\Delta u_{k}^{i}}_{f^{i}(x_{k}^{i},u_{k}^{i})},\ k\in[T],\ i\in[N].

(8)

where the control input
{u_{k}^{i}=a_{k}^{i}=[a_{x,k}^{i},a_{y,k}^{i}]^{\top}}
denotes its acceleration and
\Delta
denotes the time interval between two adjacent steps. In addition, its position, velocity, and acceleration are restricted by the corresponding limit, which contributes to the private constraints:

\displaystyle p_{x,k}^{i}\in[p_{x,\textbf{min}},p_{x,\textbf{max}}],\ p_{y,k}^{i}\in[p_{y,\textbf{min}},p_{y,\textbf{max}}],k\in[T],

(9a)

\displaystyle v_{x,k}^{i}\in[v_{x,\textbf{min}},v_{x,\textbf{max}}],\ v_{y,k}^{i}\in[p_{y,\textbf{min}},v_{y,\textbf{max}}],k\in[T],

(9b)

\displaystyle a_{x,k}^{i}\in[a_{x,\textbf{min}},a_{x,\textbf{max}}],\ a_{y,k}^{i}\in[p_{y,\textbf{min}},a_{y,\textbf{max}}],k\in[T].

(9c)

Adhering to dynamics and constraints, its objective is to minimize the accumulative running cost
{c_{k}^{i}}
over time, where
{c_{k}^{i}}
is characterized by private costs
c_{p,k}^{i}
and shared costs
c_{s,k}^{i}
with non-negative weighting parameters
{\textbf{w}^{i}}
, i.e.:

\displaystyle c_{k}^{i}

\displaystyle=c_{p,k}^{i}+c_{s,k}^{i}

(10a)

\displaystyle c_{p,k}^{i}

\displaystyle=w_{1}^{i}\|p_{k}^{i}-p_{g}^{i}\|_{2}^{2}+w_{2}^{i}\|v_{k}^{i}\|_{2}^{2}+w_{3}^{i}\|u_{k}^{i}\|_{2}^{2}

(10b)

\displaystyle c_{s,k}^{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{N}c_{k}^{ij}=w_{4}^{i}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{N}\frac{1}{\|p_{k}^{i}-p_{k}^{j}\|_{2}^{2}}

(10c)

This form of objective guides the
i^{\text{th}}
player towards its destination
p_{g}^{i}
(
\|p_{k}^{i}-p_{g}^{i}\|_{2}^{2}
) with minimal velocity (
\|v_{k}^{i}\|_{2}^{2}
) and energy expenditure (
\|u_{k}^{i}\|_{2}^{2}
) while maintaining a safe distance from the other players (
1/\|p_{k}^{i}-p_{k}^{j}\|_{2}^{2}
).

7.4
Evaluation Metrics

We provide the mathematical formulation of evaluation metrics mentioned in Section
4.1
as follows, for the ego agent
i
:

\displaystyle\text{traj}_{S}\downarrow

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{k\geq 2}\left\|\frac{p_{k}^{i}-p_{k-1}^{i}}{\|p_{k}^{i}-p_{k-1}^{i}\|_{2}}-\frac{p_{k-1}^{i}-p_{k-2}^{i}}{\|p_{k-1}^{i}-p_{k-2}^{i}\|_{2}}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\text{(Trajectory Smoothness)}

\displaystyle\text{traj}_{L}\downarrow

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{t\geq 1}\|p_{k}^{i}-p_{k-1}^{i}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\text{(Trajectory Length)}

\displaystyle\text{Dist}_{m}\uparrow

\displaystyle\coloneqq\min_{k}\|p_{k}^{i}-p_{k}^{\neg i}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\text{(Minumum Mutual Distance)}

\displaystyle\text{Consistency}\uparrow

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{k}\left(1-\frac{\|M_{k}^{i}-M_{k-1}^{i}\|_{1}}{N-1}\right)

\displaystyle\text{(Player Selection Consistency)}

Lower
\text{traj}_{S}
indicates a gentler cumulative change in direction, thus it is more welcomed.

Lower
\text{traj}_{L}
indicates fewer detours for the ego agent to approach the goal.

Higher
\text{Dist}_{m}
indicates better safety performance.

Higher Consistency indicates fewer changes in player selection, which also promotes a smoother trajectory and shorter trajectory length.
