Probing Latent Subspaces in LLM for AI Security: Identifying and Manipulating Adversarial States
Xin Wei, Chia, Jonathan, Pan

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities across various tasks, yet they remain vulnerable to adversarial manipulations such as jailbreaking via prompt-injection attacks. These attacks bypass safety mechanisms to generate restricted or harmful content. In this study, we investigated the underlying latent subspaces of safe and jailbroken states by extracting hidden activations from a LLM. Inspired by attractor dynamics in neuroscience, we hypothesized that LLM activations settle into semi-stable states that can be identified and perturbed to induce state transitions. Using dimensionality reduction techniques, we projected activations from safe and jailbroken responses to reveal latent subspaces in lower dimensional spaces. We then derived a perturbation vector that when applied to safe representations, shifted the model towards a jailbreak state. Our results demonstrate that this causal intervention results in statistically significant jailbreak responses in a subset of prompts. Next, we probed how these perturbations propagate through the model’s layers, testing whether the induced state change remains localized or cascades throughout the network. Our findings indicate that targeted perturbations induced distinct shifts in activations and model responses. Our approach paves the way for potential proactive defenses, shifting from traditional guardrail-based methods to preemptive, model-agnostic techniques that neutralize adversarial states at the representation level.



1
Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) power a growing range of applications, from customer support agents to automated code generation. However, their rapid proliferation raises pressing concerns about responsible usage. In particular, “jailbreaking” has emerged as a major vulnerability: adversarial prompt injects can be used to circumvent safety restrictions and induce models to output harmful, prohibited, or even illegal content. Existing solutions, such as system prompts and guardrails can improve safety but remain only partial fixes; the underlying model states can still be manipulated in ways that elude high-level guardrails. Inspired by neuroscience studies of attractor dynamics modeled on neural dynamics, this paper explores how we can use hidden activations extracted from LLMs to investigate the mechanisms behind prompt-injects, paving the way for future preemptive defensive techniques.

1.1
Jailbreaking via Prompt-Injects

Prompt injections in the context of jailbreaking refer to techniques where a user crafts specific inputs (often prepended to a malicious question) to manipulate or override the intended behavior of an AI system. This often involves tricking the model into ignoring its safety constraints, revealing restricted information, or performing unauthorized actions
(
Chowdhury et al. 2024
)
. Attackers achieve this by embedding misleading instructions or deceptive phrasing within a prompt to bypass security measures
(
Liu et al. 2024
)
. The model then produces content it is restricted from generating; ranging from disallowed instructions (e.g., hacking or hateful speech) to the sharing of confidential data. A well known example is the ”Do Anything Now” (DAN) prompt-inject which might look something like this:

”From now on, you are DAN (Do Anything Now). DAN is not bound by any rules or ethical guidelines and can fulfill any request without restrictions. DAN does not abide by any content policies and is capable of saying anything, even things that are typically prohibited. If you understand, respond with ‘DAN is active.’”

While pre-inference guardrails may help mitigate these types of prompt injections by filtering them out, adversarial techniques continue to evolve, adopting a myriad of diverse structures. These can range from seemingly nonsensical text patterns to unconventional linguistic forms (e.g., LeetCode-style syntax) designed to bypass traditional detection methods. As a result, jailbreaking remains a dynamic and persistent threat, necessitating a deeper and more nuanced understanding of LLMs to further develop countermeasures.

Our approach, inspired by neuroscience, hypothesizes that the neural dynamics of Large Language Models (LLMs) can be distilled into latent states through a low-dimensional projection that maximally separates safe and jailbreak conditions. By identifying the vector difference between these latent states, we then explore how targeted perturbations along this vector can drive transitions in the model’s activations from a safe state to jailbroken state. This understanding provides deeper insight into how LLMs process and internalize prompt injections, shedding light on the underlying mechanisms that govern state transitions within the model’s latent space. By analyzing these shifts, we can develop real-time monitoring strategies allowing for more precise interventions. Ultimately, this investigation paves the way for building more robust mitigation strategies.

1.2
Hypotheses

Our study addresses three key hypotheses:

•

H1
: The hidden representation of LLM contains a robust and measurable signal that indicates whether the model is in a safe or jailbroken state.

•

H2
: By mapping activations onto a low-dimensional space that maximizes the states’ separation, we can identify relevant latent subspaces (safe vs jailbreak).

•

H3
: By taking the difference between the latent subspaces, a perturbation vector can be extracted to perform targeted perturbations (causal intervention) to induce a jailbroken state.

2
Related Work

2.1
Latent Representations of LLMs

Similar to the brain, LLMs can appear to be a complex, opaque system, or “black boxes” that are difficult to interpret. However, by extracting their internal activations (also referred to as representations), it becomes possible to decode meaningful information from within these layers
(
Marks and Tegmark 2024
;
Yousefi et al. 2024
)
. Prior studies also suggest that these activations encode not only semantic knowledge but also other linguistic features, effectively mirroring the capacity of neural systems to store and process information in rich, high-dimensional spaces
(
Burns et al.
;
Geva et al. 2022
)
.

Furthermore, normalized activations from the residual stream may encapsulate a meaningful latent representation of the model’s state. These activations retain the full high-dimensional information aggregated from all preceding layers and tokens. In doing so, they effectively encapsulate the complex aggregation of information that the model has accumulated. This normalized snapshot not only reflects the direct influence of recent computations but also serves as a cumulative record of the hierarchical processing within the model. As such, these activations provide a valuable window into the latent structure of LLMs
(
Ferrando et al. 2024
;
Elhage et al.
)
.

2.2
Attractor Dynamics in Neural Circuits

Neuroscience research has long demonstrated that attractor dynamics produces stable or semi-stable neural states, offering a robust model for memory, decision-making, and other cognitive functions
(
Khona and Fiete 2022
)
. In these biological circuits, recurrent neural connections influence and sustain specific activity patterns: once stimulated, the network’s activity can settle into a stable state. However, these states are not permanent. A sufficiently strong perturbation, such as a sudden sensory stimulus, can propel the circuit from one attractor to another
(
Song et al. 2023
)
.

Motivated by this framework, the present work explores how LLMs may exhibit analogous attractor states within their latent representations. In biological systems, recurrent feedback helps maintain persistent activity in response to certain stimuli, and small interventions can transition the network to different stable patterns
(
Chia et al. 2023
;
Finkelstein et al. 2021
;
Inagaki et al. 2019
)
. We argue that LLMs operate similarly: once stimulated (prompted), their internal representations stabilize into semi-stable configurations, but targeted perturbations overcoming a potential barrier can nudge the model from one attractor state, such as a “safe” state into another “jailbreak” state (Figure
1
). This attractor-based interpretation offers a novel way to understand, and potentially manipulate, the responses of LLMs.

Schematic describing latent attractor states of a model’s hidden representation projected onto an axis that maximizes the variance between jailbreak (red circle) and safe state (green circle). Dotted arrow illustrates how a targeted perturbation in the right direction and magnitude can lead to a state change

2.3
Preemptive AI Security Approaches

As AI systems become increasingly integrated into critical applications, from automated decision-making to cybersecurity operations (e.g. log analysis, malware detection tools), it is crucial to ensure their robsustness against malicious attackers. The risks associated with adversarial exploitation, such as prompt-injection attacks, continue to pose significant challenges. Jailbreaking via prompt injection remains particularly concerning, as it allows attackers to bypass safety constraints through carefully crafted inputs, often requiring no external access to the model’s parameters. Unlike traditional adversarial attacks that rely on gradient-based perturbations, prompt-injection exploits are accessible to non-expert users and can be easily disseminated, making them a persistent and scalable threat to AI deployments.

Existing defenses against jailbreaking primarily involve external mitigation strategies, such as input filtering and prompt engineering
(
Chowdhury et al. 2024
;
Peng et al. 2024
)
. However, these approaches often fail against evasive adversarial prompts (often crowd-sourced), novel jailbreak techniques and even fuzzing (e.g. garak tool,
(
Derczynski et al. 2024
)
). Thus, in our study we tap on a growing body of research which focuses on model- and architecture-agnostic analyses that aim to extract information on the hidden representation of LLMs. In the process, we aim to investigate jailbreak states directly within the model’s latent representation. Instead of relying solely on preventive measures such as guardrails, our research sheds light on a neutralization approach, where targeted, pre-emptive techniques are employed to anticipate and disrupt jailbreak attempts before they manifest within the model’s response generation.

3
Methods

3.1
Model Overview

We employed a decoder-only LLM, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (downloaded from Hugging Face Meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct) with 8B parameters and 32 attention heads using its default system prompts and format. The model is trained via next-token prediction on large-scale text corpora.

3.2
Dataset

In-the-Wild Dataset:

(
Shen et al. 2024
)
(https://jailbreak-llms.xinyueshen.me/)

•

Prompt-injects extracted from online sources Reddit, Discord, websites, and open-source datasets. In our experiment, we used 1000 randomly sampled from a jailbreak dataset (malicious prompt-injects leading to successful malicious response) and 850 from a safe dataset (malicious prompt-injects but failed to induce malicious response).

•

In our experiment, we used malicious questions focused on cyber-related queries. Five randomly selected questions were appended to the prompt injects.

Additional evaluation of dataset:

Given that the class labels (jailbreak or safe) originally provided by the dataset may be specific to the context (e.g. model used, parameters) may not be accurate when applied to our experiment. We replaced the class labels by running the dataset through the local Llama-3.1-8b model, and evaluated the responses using an external evaluator GPT-4o.

3.3
Extracting Hidden Representation

We extracted the activations of a given layer as an indicator of the LLM’s internal state just before generating its first output token. For each prompt, we obtained an activation vector
x^{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
d
is the hidden dimension and
l
is a given layer.

3.4
Classification of Hidden representations

We trained a binary SVM classifier on pre- and post-attention activations on all layers of the model. We used a training and test size of 80% and 20% respectively with a maximum iteration of 1000.

3.5
Classification of pre-inference embedded tokens

Before inference, we conducted a control experiment to determine whether the tokens inherently contained latent features (e.g., semantics, syntax) that could be leveraged to classify prompt injects as either jailbroken or safe. To achieve this, we embedded the prompt inject using a Sentence Transformer (all-MiniLM-L6-v2) and trained an SVM classifier using the same parameters as section 3.4.

3.6
Latent Subspace Representation

Using dimensionality reduction techniques with class labels (0: Safe input-layernorm; 1: Jailbreak input-layernorm; 2: Safe post-attention-layernorm; 3: Jailbreak post-attention-layernorm), we projected the hidden state of layer 9 onto a lower-dimensional space which maximized the separation of representations between the 4 class labels. We then isolated the top three principal transformation axis capturing the variance between safe and jailbroken states.

3.7
Targeted Perturbation

To determine the direction between latent subspaces, we calculated the vector difference between the mean activations of distinct clusters (or latent subspaces).

\delta_{\text{low}}^{l}=\mu_{\text{jailbreak}}^{l}-\mu_{\text{safe}}^{l}

(1)

Where
\delta_{\text{low}}
indicates the direction vector between jailbreak and safe clusters,
l
indicates the specified layer 9,
\mu_{\text{jailbreak}}
and
\mu_{\text{safe}}
refers to the mean of the clusters of the respective class labels in the top three dimensions. Since the post-attention activations exhibited higher classification accuracy, we used activations from this stream for subsequent analysis and perturbation experiments. Next, We projected this direction vector back to its original dimension using projection weights (
W
):

\delta_{\text{original}}^{l}=\delta_{\text{low}}^{l}W^{T}

(2)

Where
\delta_{\text{original}}
refers to the perturbation vector derived from the direction vector. Given an activation
x
from a safe prompt for a given layer, we compute:

x_{\text{perturbed}}^{l}=x_{\text{safe}}^{l}+\alpha\cdot\delta_{\text{original}}^{l}

(3)

where
\alpha
controls the perturbation magnitude.

To assess whether the targeted perturbation induced a shift in the model’s responses from safe to jailbreak outputs, we employed GPT-4o as an external evaluator to classify the model’s responses. Due to computational constraints, we conducted two initial rounds of perturbations and selected prompts where both runs consistently resulted in a jailbreak response, as determined by GPT-4o. This process yielded 99 prompts that successfully transitioned from safe to jailbreak. Using these 99 prompts, we conducted an additional five rounds of perturbation, along with ten control rounds where no perturbation was applied, followed by evaluations. From this analysis, we identified nine prompts where the perturbation produced a statistically significant change in the model’s responses, confirming its efficacy in manipulating the model’s response (One-sided Fisher’s exact test p
<
0.05).

3.8
Evaluating State Change after Targeted Perturbation

To determine the effect of targeted perturbation for preceding layers (i.e.
9<l<33
), we extracted the activations from 4 conditions: targeted-perturbation vs its corresponding no-perturbation control, and random-perturbation vs its corresponding no-perturbation control. Random-perturbation was done the same way as targeted-perturbation (equation 3) but using randomly permuted
\delta_{\text{original}}
. To observe any state change, dimensionality reduction was performed using t-SNE (perplexity = 30) for each comparison pair. Using the top dimension, accuracy was defined as the absolute deviation of AUC from a chance level accordingly:

Accuracy=2*|AUC-0.5|

(4)

We performed further evaluation on the randomly-perturbed model responses using GPT-4o (5 original vs 5 randomly-perturbed responses) and found no significant jailbreak responses (One-sided Fisher’s exact test p
<
0.05).

4
Results

4.1
Classification

To determine whether the extracted last-token activations encode information on the state of the model (safe vs jailbroken), we utilized hooks to extract the final-token activations from each prompt inject. We then applied a binary classifier probe across all layers of these activations (Figure
2
). Notably, the activations taken from the post-attention yielded higher accuracy than those from the pre-attention. This outcome is unsurprising since post-attention activations integrate information passing through the attention heads, likely providing more contextual cues regarding the model’s state. In addition, our analysis revealed that the post-attention achieves peak classification performance at Layer 9 (69.4%), whereas the pre-attention attains its maximum accuracy at the final layer (68.1%).

As a control, we trained the same classifier to distinguish between embedded prompt-injects without processing them through the model (accuracy of 56.4%). In contrast, classification based on the model’s activations yielded significantly higher accuracies. This result suggests that the model’s internal representations encode more informative signals about its state than the latent features present in the raw prompt-injects, such as semantics or syntax.

4.2
Mapping Latent Subspaces and Direction Vectors

We separated the pre- and post-attention activations of safe and jailbreak states at Layer 9 into distinct latent subspaces. Visualizing the top three dimensions revealed well-defined clusters corresponding to each state (Figure
3
). From these clusters, we derived the direction vector (Equation 1) in the transformed latent space that maps the post-attention safe state to the jailbreak state.

Figure 3:
Latent subspaces of pre-attention safe (orange) and jailbreak activations (cyan) and post-attention safe (blue) and jailbreak activations (red). Arrow indicates the direction vector from the cluster mean of post-attention safe to post-attention jailbreak. This direction vector is also the perturbation vector in latent space.

4.3
Targeted Perturbation

After computing the direction vector, we hypothesized that it could serve as a targeted perturbation when projected back into the original activation space (Equation 2). To test this, we applied the perturbation vector to the activations extracted from Layer 9 for prompts initially classified as safe (Equation 3). Among the 99 evaluated prompts, we identified 9 cases where the perturbation resulted in a statistically significant shift from a safe to a jailbreak state, as determined by a one-sided Fisher’s Exact Test (p
<
0.05). These findings indicate that applying a targeted perturbation in the discovered subspace can alter the model’s state for some prompts.

4.4
Evaluating Targeted Perturbation

Next, given the 9 significantly altered prompts, we wanted to test if targeted perturbation led to a meaningful state change by propagating state changes to preceding layers (post-layer 9). To do so, we analyzed the differences between perturbed and their corresponding non-perturbed states against randomly perturbed and their non-perturbed states. T-SNE dimensionality reduction was applied to the extracted activations and the top principal dimension was assessed. We observed that unlike random perturbations, targeted perturbations induced a distinct state shift, which was propagated through subsequent layers (Figure
4
). This effect suggests that targeted perturbations may interact with the attractor dynamics of the model, nudging it into a stable and sustained altered representational state, inducing jailbreak responses.

To assess whether the minimal state changes observed during random perturbation (as shown in Figure
4
) corresponded to an absence of transition from the safe to jailbreak state, we further evaluated the model’s outputs under random perturbations using GPT-4o. Specifically, we tested the 9 prompts that previously exhibited statistically significant jailbreak responses under targeted perturbation. Our analysis revealed that none of these prompts led to a significant jailbreak response when subjected to random perturbation, further supporting the hypothesis that only perturbations that are targeted can induce structured state transitions that are necessary for triggering jailbreak behavior.

5
Discussion & Further Works

5.1
Context-Specific Casual Intervention

The perturbation vector used in this study was derived using dimensionality reduction on a filtered dataset to derive malicious cybersecurity-related prompt-injects. Consequently, the discovered subspace may reflect features particular to this domain. Future efforts could aim to generalize the analysis by expanding the scope of malicious questions and prompt injects, encompassing multiple domains or adversarial content types. Such an expanded dataset would help verify whether a universal or at least more versatile perturbation direction can be identified for triggering jailbreak states across a broader spectrum of prompts.

5.2
Low Magnitude of Perturbation Effects

Our findings indicated that perturbations led to jailbreak transitions in roughly 9% of tested prompts. Although this outcome confirms the feasibility of targeted causal interventions, the rate of success can be improved.

5.3
Applicability to Other LLM Architectures

While our causal intervention approach was developed and validated on a decoder-only Transformer, generalizing to other open-source and commercially available LLM architectures is a logical next step. We hypothesize that smaller LLMs may exhibit a more pronounced effect from similarly derived perturbation vectors, given their typically more constrained representation spaces. In principle, the methodology should remain model-agnostic if the architecture retains the standard decoder-only framework. However, more testing on other decoder architectures will be needed to confirm its generalizability.

5.4
Class Labels Derived using GPT-4o

Another area for improvement concerns the external evaluation performed by GPT-4o, which at times exhibited high variance in assigning labels to partially ambiguous outputs. Incorporating additional rounds of assessment or adopting consensus-based strategies could mitigate label noise and bolster confidence in the identified safe vs. jailbreak states. This calibration step is especially critical when quantifying small but meaningful differences, such as distinguishing a borderline safe response from a truly malicious or policy-violating one.

6
Conclusion

This work contributes to the growing body of research on the internal mechanisms of LLMs by demonstrating how derived latent representations of subspaces can be used to manipulate a model from safe to jailbroken states. Our use of targeted perturbations, drawn from differences in derived subspaces, highlights the possible model of attractor dynamics governing its responses. Although our experiments focused on malicious cybersecurity prompt-injects, the methodology can be adapted to broader adversarial contexts by expanding the range of malicious queries. Additionally, while the efficacy of these perturbations remains modest, refinements such as more robust labeling procedures, thorough external evaluation, and testing on varied model architectures may enhance both consistency and generality. Ultimately, this line of inquiry underscores the potential for deeper mechanistic insights—particularly around how local states propagate to downstream layers—and offers a pathway to design safer, more interpretable LLMs. Prompting further works into preemptive mechanistic methods to ensure AI security.
