Discrete inflow and drainage dynamics of a thin film over a stalagmite of variable shape
Justine Parmentier 1, Vincent E. Terrapon 2 and Tristan Gilet 2

Abstract
Stalagmites in karstic caves preserve valuable palaeoclimate records through calcium-rich layered deposits, presenting curvature variations both across and within individual stalagmites. Stalagmites always remain covered by a thin water film fed by a discrete inflow of drops, which bring in new ions in solution for the stalagmites to grow. However, the gravity-induced drainage of this film and its response to the stalagmite underneath shape and the discrete drop inflow remain poorly characterised in existing growth models. To address these limitations, we develop a theoretical framework that captures the combined effects of shape curvature and discrete drop inflow on thin film drainage dynamics, starting from Reynolds lubrication theory expressed in curvilinear coordinates. From there, we show that the limiting cases of thickness-dominated and inclination-dominated drainage translate into distinct scaling laws for both the front propagation position and stationary film thickness. We further validate these results by numerically solving the governing equations. Finally, experimental measurements conducted in both cave and lab settings confirm the predicted stationary film thickness. Our findings provide insights into the influence of substrate shape and inflow dynamics on thin film drainage, with implications for stalagmite growth modelling and other gravity-driven surface flows.

Introduction

Palaeoclimatology refers to the study of past natural climate variations, as opposed to human-induced climate change (
e.g.
,
21
). A wide range of palaeoclimate proxies record past temperature and precipitation variations (
e.g.
,
5
), refining our understanding of long-term climate patterns and informing future predictions. Among them, stalagmites in karstic caves are particularly valuable, especially in regions lacking glacial ice cores or other continental proxies (
e.g.
,
44
). Their significance stems from (i) their global distribution (
e.g.
,
15
), (ii) their long-term preservation given the stability of environmental conditions (temperature, atmosphere composition) inside caves (
e.g.
,
8
) and (iii) the presence of a variety of palaeoclimate indicators, such as trace elements and isotopic ratios, that can be precisely dated (
e.g.
,
42
;
40
). Specifically, the laminae revealed in a stalagmite cross-sectional cut are correlated to the past upstream flows and soil coverage above the cave (
e.g.
,
45
;
36
). Hence, the shape of these laminae, and therefore of the stalagmite top surface, changes over time in response to variable conditions (
e.g.
,
12
;
2
;
28
). As a result, variations in stalagmite composition can be directly linked to external climate changes, such as shifts in precipitation or temperature, rather than local environmental noise.

Yet, the causal link between past flows and stalagmite shape variability remains poorly understood. Existing models describing stalagmite growth involve strong simplifying assumptions regarding the aerodynamics and hydrodynamics of drops impacting stalagmites in caves (
e.g.
,
12
;
30
). For instance, drops were assumed to always land at the apex of the stalagmite, thereby feeding the thin residual film covering it in one central point. However, it was recently shown that the falling drop interacts with its own wake. Hence, its impact position is sometimes scattered over several centimetres, which largely determines stalagmite width (
e.g.
,
31
). The concave shape exhibited by some stalagmites, called drip cups (
e.g.
,
6
), was also associated with drops splashing at impact. However, most drop impacts in caves lead to splashing and cannot, therefore, be related to a particular stalagmite shape (
e.g.
,
10
). Another assumption of existing stalagmite growth models is that the thin residual film lying on top of the stalagmite remains uniform in time and space. This overlooks the possible interplay between the intermittent inflow of discrete drops feeding the film and the effect of stalagmite shape on the film thickness. Hence, this assumption may not always be valid to describe stalagmite morphogenesis.

Due to the hydrophilicity of calcite, stalagmites are naturally covered by very thin films (
\sim$100\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$
) in comparison to their average width (between
5\text{\,}\mathrm{cm}
and
50\text{\,}\mathrm{cm}
) or the more or less constant radius of the falling drops (approximately
2.7\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}
, which is the capillary length of water in air,
e.g.
,
31
). Some exceptions include drip cups (
e.g.
,
6
), which can form pools of water up to
1\text{\,}\mathrm{cm}
deep due to their unique concave shapes. Thin film flows are ubiquitous in both natural and industrial settings. They can be categorised into (i) free surface thin film flows such as lava flows (
e.g.
,
11
;
17
) or free soap film drainage (
e.g.
,
3
), and (ii) flows between confined boundaries like synovial flows in joints (
e.g.
,
27
), Bretherton bubbles in narrow tubes (
e.g.
,
7
) or oil and gas transport in pipes (
e.g.
,
25
). We focus exclusively on free-surface thin film flows here, whose dynamics responds primarily to competing viscous and gravity effects. These flows can be induced by the spreading of a given liquid volume onto a substrate, or by a constant flow feeding it (
e.g.
,
20
;
37
). In both cases, the resulting thin film dynamics depends strongly on the boundary conditions considered, substrate geometry or other competing physical processes at play such as the deformability of the substrate (
e.g.
,
38
,
23
), its permeability which induces partial fluid loss (
e.g.
,
25
,
26
), or even inertial effects (
e.g.
,
29
).

In many configurations, self-similar solutions can be derived (
e.g.
,
20
;
16
;
22
), where the film thickness and its front position can both be expressed as power laws of time (
e.g.
,
26
). However, most of these models assume idealised or particularised geometries (flat or linear slopes) and continuous inflow conditions. In contrast, natural cave environments involve non-trivial substrate geometries and discrete drop impacts. Intermittent discrete inflows feeding the film are usually not taken into account in modelling thin film drainage (
e.g.
,
37
;
26
), although they could be of interest for geophysical or industrial chemical applications, such as trickle-bed reactors (
e.g.
,
19
). To our knowledge, there is no dedicated general model describing the film as a function of the shape of the substrate underneath, nor the effect of a discrete inflow on subsequent film drainage dynamics. This raises the following question: how does a thin film fed by discrete drops evolve over a curved stalagmite?

In this study, we thus model how the underneath stalagmite shape and the discrete drop inflow jointly affect the spatio-temporal response of the film to the competing gravity and viscosity. Using Reynolds lubrication theory expressed in a curvilinear framework, we derive in Sec.
2
the governing equations of gravity-driven drainage on an axisymmetric stalagmite of general shape. In Sec.
3
, we solve the equations numerically in idealised flat and parabolic geometries, which allows us to explore the transient and stationary dynamics of the film under discrete drop inflow. We then extend our theoretical analysis to limiting cases of flat and parabolic stalagmite profiles in Sec.
4
. We further compare these predictions to experimental film thickness measurements taken both in caves and in a controlled lab setting in Sec.
5
, using complementary experimental techniques. We conclude by discussing in Sec.
6
the interplay between inflow, geometry, and film thickness, and outline implications for natural cave formations. This work combines theoretical modelling, numerical resolution of the equations and experimental measurements that, altogether, highlight how the local and global film thickness dynamics may affect subsequent stalagmite growth.

2
Theoretical description

A stalagmite in a karstic cave grows upward by approximately
1\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}1000\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
over a year
(
e.g.
,
1
). It is formed by the progressive deposition of calcium carbonate brought by the successive water drops falling from the stalactite located directly above the corresponding stalagmite. In this study, we only focus on individual stalagmites associated with a single stalactite (see Fig.
1
(a)). Upon impact, each drop merges with the existing liquid layer, forming a thin film that spreads over the stalagmite surface. Due to the high humidity levels inside caves, this film persists and drains slowly under gravity, enabling mineral precipitation over time.

In this section, we describe the gravity-driven drainage of a thin liquid film lying on top of a stalagmite modelled as an axisymmetric body, a reasonable assumption for most stalagmites fed by a single drip (
e.g.
,
39
;
31
). As represented in Fig.
1
(b), the radial system of coordinates along a given polar angle,
(r,z)
, is aligned with the stalagmite axis of symmetry. The stalagmite top is either flat (horizontal) or curved, and may present macroscopic irregularities with a radius of curvature down to
1\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}
. By contrast, the film thickness lying on top of the stalagmite is of the order of
30\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}300\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
(
e.g.
,
32
), which is much smaller than the aforementioned minimum radius of curvature of the stalagmite surface. We thus consider a curvilinear coordinate system
\left(\xi,\zeta\right)
aligned with the local stalagmite geometry at each point. As shown in Fig.
1
(b),
\xi
, resp.
\zeta
, represents the direction parallel, resp. normal, to the stalagmite surface. The corresponding unit vectors
\textbf{e}_{\xi}
and
\textbf{e}_{\zeta}
are locally defined at each point of the surface, with
\textbf{e}_{\xi}
inclined by an angle
\varphi
with respect to the horizontal, counted as positive downward. The film thickness measured normally to the solid/liquid interface is
h
. We further approximate the stalagmite elevation profile,
\eta
, as either entirely flat or represented by a parabola oriented downward. These two shapes are commonly found among stalagmites (
e.g.
,
39
) and are defined by

\eta(r)=-\Psi r^{2}\,,\qquad 0\leq r\leq R\,,

(1)

where we denote
\Psi
the shape factor with units of inverse length, and where
R
is either the domain width or, in the degenerate case
\Psi=0
, the stalagmite top surface radius. We restrict our analysis to convex surfaces (
\Psi>0
), although the developed framework remains valid even for mildly concave surfaces (
\Psi\lesssim 0
).

We limit our study to an incompressible Newtonian fluid of density
\rho
and kinematic viscosity
\nu
. The ions in solution are assumed sufficiently diluted to neglect hydrodynamic interactions. Under the thin film approximation, velocity gradients within the film in the direction normal to the surface are much larger than those parallel to the surface. Additionally, inertial effects can be neglected if changes in velocity occur at a timescale
\gg h^{2}/\nu\sim${10}^{-2}\text{\,}\mathrm{s}$
, ensuring that the flow remains laminar (
i.e.
, the Reynolds number
\ll 1
). In this case, the flow is predominantly oriented along
\textbf{e}_{\xi}
, with a velocity
\mathbf{u}=u(\zeta)\,\textbf{e}_{\xi}
. The thin film approximation also implies that second-order derivatives parallel to the stalagmite surface become of negligible order. Therefore, under these approximations and in the absence of drop inflow, the Navier-Stokes momentum equation reduces to a balance between pressure gradients, and viscous and gravitational forces. We can thus adapt Reynolds lubrication theory (
34
) to quantify the drainage dynamics in our curvilinear coordinate system.

\sim\left(\partial_{\xi}h\right)\cos{\varphi}

To connect the local drainage dynamics with the global mass transport, the surface-parallel flux

e.g.
,
43
;
10

V_{\rm d}

e.g.
,
32

\textit{Q}_{\hskip 1.0pt\text{drip}}
, is given by

\textit{Q}_{\hskip 1.0pt\text{drip}}=\dfrac{V_{\rm d}}{t_{\rm{0}}}\,,

(6)

where
t_{\rm{0}}
is the dripping period between two successive drops, ranging from less than a second to several weeks in most caves (
e.g.
,
14
;
31
). The volume
V_{\rm d}
is approximately constant as it results from the balance between gravitational and surface tension forces when a drop hanging from the ceiling detaches (
e.g.
,
41
). Hence,
\textit{Q}_{\hskip 1.0pt\text{drip}}
varies solely in response to variable
t_{\rm{0}}
. We also consider that all the drops land at position
r=0
,
i.e.
, that there is no impact point position dispersal. Impact point dispersal can nevertheless be incorporated easily using a 2D description of the problem (
e.g.
,
33

A perfect balance between the drop inflow
\textit{Q}_{\hskip 1.0pt\text{drip}}
and drainage outflow
\textit{Q}_{\hskip 1.0pt\text{drain}}
ensures that the film remains at stationary state. However, because the drops enter the film one at a time, the stationary film thickness should oscillate between a maximum immediately after a new drop has been added to the film, and a minimum right before the next drop addition. In the following, we refer to this minimum film thickness as the stationary film thickness
h_{\text{s}}
, which defines the baseline envelope of the film oscillations.
Departures from the balance between
\textit{Q}_{\hskip 1.0pt\text{drip}}
and
\textit{Q}_{\hskip 1.0pt\text{drain}}
should result in transient evolution of the film before it reaches a new stationary state. If the stalagmite surface is initially dry, this transient state corresponds to the propagation of a liquid front on top of the stalagmite, solely induced by the inflow of drops. These phenomena illustrate the complex interplay between localised drop additions in the film and spatially distributed drainage on curved surfaces. In the following, we formalise how the non-linear structure of Eqs. (
4
) and (
5
) leads to various drainage regimes for either flat or parabolic stalagmites in response to variable inflow.

2.1
Characteristic scales and drainage timescale

The thin film approximation implies a clear decoupling between characteristic length scales across and along the liquid film (
e.g.
,
24
;
26
). Typical film thicknesses in caves are of the order of
100\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
while stalagmite radii usually reach a few centimetres. We therefore define two length scales to normalise variables and equations:
h_{\rm d}=$100\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$
for variations across the film, and
r_{\rm d}=$1\text{\,}\mathrm{cm}$
for variations along the film. These definitions match the typical height and radial extent of the puddle formed by a drop upon impact and correspond to a drop volume of the same order of magnitude (
V_{\rm d}=$1.6\text{\times}{10}^{-8}\text{\,}{\mathrm{m}}^{3}$
for a paraboloid of radius
r_{\rm d}
and height
h_{\rm d}
) as in lab experiments (
V_{\rm d}^{\rm lab}=$5.1\text{\times}{10}^{-8}\text{\,}{\mathrm{m}}^{3}$
) and caves (
V_{\rm d}^{\rm cave}\simeq$8.2\text{\times}{10}^{-8}\text{\,}{\mathrm{m}}^{3}$
).

If
t_{\mathrm{d}}
and
q_{\rm d}
designate the corresponding characteristic drainage timescale and surface-parallel flux scale, respectively, the variables appearing in Eqs. (
1
), (
4
) and (
5
) now write as

\displaystyle r=r^{\prime}r_{\rm d}\,\text{,}\;\;R=R^{\prime}r_{\rm d}\,\text{,}\;\;\xi=\xi^{\prime}r_{\rm d}\,\text{,}\;\;t=t^{\prime}t_{\mathrm{d}}\,\text{,}\;\;h=h^{\prime}h_{\rm d}\,\text{,}\;\;\eta=\eta^{\prime}h_{\rm d}\,\text{,}\;\;\Psi=\Psi^{\prime}\dfrac{h_{\rm d}}{r_{\rm d}^{2}}\,\text{,}\;\;q=q^{\prime}q_{\rm d}\,\text{,}

where the primes refer to the nondimensional variables. The prime notation will be systematically used in scaling analyses (see Sec.
4
), but will be dropped for the numerical resolution, where all variables remain nondimensional by construction.
The nondimensionalisation of the shape factor
\Psi
preserves the structure of Eq. (
1

The drainage timescale is thus defined as

t_{\mathrm{d}}=\dfrac{\nu r_{\rm d}^{2}}{gh_{\rm d}^{3}}\,,

(8)

which is about
10\text{\,}\mathrm{s}
for the chosen values of
r_{\rm d}
and
h_{\rm d}
, taking into account that
g\simeq$10\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-2}$
and that, for water,
\nu\simeq${10}^{-6}\text{\,}{\mathrm{m}}^{2}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}$

(9)

,

during one drainage timescale

.

In other words, adding drops with a dripping period equivalent to the drainage timescale can serve as an onset to efficiently compare high inflows such that

. However, high inflows such that

in dimensional units could, in practice, lead to the emergence of chaotic dripping or transition to jetting (

2.3
Discrete drop inflow

An inflow of successive drop impacts is added over the stalagmite to create the liquid film and maintain it at stationary state. To include this discrete inflow of drops, Eq. (
5
) is reformulated by including punctual source terms as follows:

\partial_{t}h+\dfrac{1}{r}\hskip 0.5pt\partial_{\xi}\left(qr\right)=\sum_{k}{\mathcal{H}_{\rm d}\hskip 0.25pt(\xi\hskip 1.0pt)\,\delta\hskip-2.5pt\left(t-kt_{\rm{0}}\right)}\,\text{,}\;k\in\mathbb{N}\,,

(12)

where
\delta
is the Dirac distribution and
\mathcal{H}_{\rm d}(\xi)
is the drop-shaped film thickness increment. To approach the early-stage shape of a drop crushing at impact (
e.g.
,
35
), we model each drop addition in the film using a parabolic profile spread over a radius
r=1
and height
h=1
.
The corresponding dimensional drop volume,
\pi r_{\rm d}^{2}h_{\rm d}/2
, sets the characteristic scale for the nondimensionalisation. The nondimensional drop volume is thus equal to 1 in the simulations, and the drop inflow is solely set by the dripping period
t_{\rm{0}}
. The exact added drop shape has limited effect on subsequent film thickness variations. This approach does not capture the exact dynamics of drop impact since inertial and capillary effects were not taken into account in Eqs. (
4
) and (
5
). However, the relevant timescales remain consistent with experimental measurements as drops can be viewed as instantaneously added to the film (a drop impact in a thin film typically lasts for
\lesssim$200\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}$
as shown in,
e.g.
,
13
;
32
).

Numerical results

Using the numerical resolution described in the former section, we illustrate how the interplay between discrete drop inflow and geometry-constrained drainage outflow modulates the stationary state of the film. We first describe the general phenomenology, then explore the response of the film stationary state to

and
\Psi
(or

All quantities in this section are nondimensional and written without primes. Legends and captions clarify the use of dimensional variables where needed, for consistency with the rest of the paper. Finally,
unless stated otherwise and when available, numerical fit exponents were chosen to match theoretical predictions which will be developed later in Sec.
4
. Slight deviations in their values should not significantly affect their interpretation.

3.2
Stationary regime

The stationary state of the film provides a physically meaningful and reproducible indicator of the system. We now explore in further details its response to variable inflow and stalagmite geometry, first through numerical measurements of the film thickness. Experimental measurements will be compared in Sec.

3.3
Transient front propagation

Heretofore, we have primarily examined the stationary behaviour of the film in response to a discrete inflow. We focus in this section on the early transient dynamics of the film propagation over the stalagmite, from when the first drop is added. The film advances over the stalagmite surface by covering an increasing area over time. The position of the propagating film tip, denoted by
\ell_{\text{f}}

We consider two asymptotic limits of the discrete drop inflow: (i)

Numerically, while imposing a constant volume simply corresponds to adding one drop on the stalagmite top surface, inner boundary condition should be modified for the continuous inflow. This jet inflow is considered laminar and is approximated by using a non-zero Dirichlet condition on the flux
q
at the centreline using
\textit{Q}_{\hskip 1.0pt\text{drip}}
. The exact derivation of this condition is reviewed in App.
B

The corresponding analytical limits of the regimes described in this section are discussed in Sec.
4
, where we come back to the dimensional form of the drainage equations.

4
Scaling analysis

In this section, we perform a theoretical analysis of the dimensional form of the drainage equations, corresponding to Eqs. (
4
) and (
5
). First, a time-independent solution to the film thickness is sought, for both flat and parabolic stalagmites. An analysis of time-dependent perturbations of the stationary film thickness profile is then made. Finally, an unsteady solution is developed for the parabolic case in the vicinity of the stalagmite centreline. The scaling laws obtained throughout this analysis are compared to the numerical results of Sec.
3
. All dimensional variables will be written without primes, while the nondimensional variables will be written using the prime notation introduced in Sec.
2.1
.

4.1
Stationary film thickness

In the former section, Figs.
2
and
4
revealed that, sufficiently far from the centreline (
i.e.
, for
r\gg 1
or
\xi\gg 1
), the film thickness becomes time-independent, namely, it is no longer influenced by the discreteness of the drop inflow. The film thickness thus corresponds to the stationary film thickness
h_{\text{s}}
introduced in Sec.
3
. Additionally,
\partial_{t}h_{\text{s}}=0
and the drainage equation becomes

q=\frac{gh_{\text{s}}^{3}}{3\nu}\left(\sin\varphi-\left(\partial_{\xi}h_{\text{s}}\right)\cos\varphi\right)=\frac{V_{\rm d}}{2\pi rt_{\rm{0}}}\,.

(14)

While there is no closed-form solution to this differential equation, asymptotic regimes may be obtained when either term in the brackets dominates the other.

4.1.1
Flat stalagmite

For flat stalagmites,
\Psi=0
and
\varphi=0

\forall\xi
. The derivatives
\partial_{\xi}\cdot
thus reduce to
\partial_{r}\cdot
and Eq. (
14
) becomes

-\dfrac{g}{3\nu}h_{\text{s}}^{3}\left(\partial_{r}h_{\text{s}}\right)=\dfrac{V_{\rm d}}{2\pi rt_{\rm{0}}}\,.

(15)

This translates in nondimensional form to

-r^{\prime}h^{\prime 3}_{\rm s}\left(\partial_{r^{\prime}}h_{\text{s}}^{\prime}\right)=\frac{3V_{\rm d}^{\prime}}{4t_{\rm{0}}^{\prime}}\,,

(16)

where we used the characteristic scales introduced in Sec.
2.1
, and included the same nondimensional drop volume as in the numerical resolution,
i.e.
,
V_{\rm d}=V_{\rm d}^{\prime}(\pi r_{\rm d}^{2}h_{\rm d}/2)
(see Sec.
2.3
).
Integrating the above equation yields

h_{\text{s}}^{\prime}=\left[\frac{3V_{\rm d}^{\prime}}{t_{\rm{0}}^{\prime}}\ln\left(\frac{R^{\prime}}{r^{\prime}}\right)+h^{\prime 4}_{\rm s}(R^{\prime})\right]^{1/4},

(17)

where
h_{\text{s}}^{\prime}(R^{\prime})
is the value of the film thickness at the stalagmite edge, in
r^{\prime}=R^{\prime}

r^{\prime}>R^{\prime}

r^{\prime}=R^{\prime}

r^{\prime}>R^{\prime}
by a cone of constant opening angle
\varphi_{\raisebox{-1.65pt}{\hskip-1.65pt\scriptsize{$\star$}}}

given by substituting

and
\varphi=\varphi_{\raisebox{-1.65pt}{\hskip-1.65pt\scriptsize{$\star$}}}
in Eq. (
25

h_{\text{s}}^{\prime}=\left[\frac{3V_{\rm d}^{\prime}}{t_{\rm{0}}^{\prime}}\ln\left(\frac{R^{\prime}}{r^{\prime}}\right)+\left(\frac{3h_{d}}{4r_{d}}\frac{V^{\prime}_{d}}{t_{\rm{0}}^{\prime}R^{\prime}\sin\varphi_{\raisebox{-1.65pt}{\hskip-1.65pt\scriptsize{$\star$}}}}\right)^{4/3}\right]^{1/4}.

(18)

The profile

\varphi_{\raisebox{-1.65pt}{\hskip-1.65pt\scriptsize{$\star$}}}

The opening angle of the cone at
r^{\prime}>R^{\prime}
has a negligible influence on
h_{\text{s}}^{\prime}
at
r^{\prime}<R^{\prime}
as long as
\varphi_{\raisebox{-1.65pt}{\hskip-1.65pt\scriptsize{$\star$}}}\geq 10^{\circ}
. This verifies that the value
\varphi_{\raisebox{-1.65pt}{\hskip-1.65pt\scriptsize{$\star$}}}=45^{\circ}

18

4.1.2
Parabolic stalagmite

For sufficiently curved stalagmites with
\Psi>0
, there exist radial positions for which
\tan\varphi\gg|\partial_{\xi}h_{\text{s}}|
. In such conditions, Eq. (
14
) reduces to

h^{3}_{\rm s}=\frac{3\nu V_{\rm d}}{2\pi gt_{\rm{0}}r\sin\varphi}.

(20)

The condition
\tan\varphi\gg|\partial_{\xi}h_{\text{s}}|
can be made explicit by first differentiating this equation with respect to
\xi
:

3h_{\text{s}}^{2}\left(\partial_{\xi}h_{\text{s}}\right)=\frac{3\nu V_{\rm d}\cos{\varphi}}{2\pi gt_{\rm{0}}}\partial_{r}\left(\frac{1}{r\sin\varphi}\right).

(21)

For a parabolic stalagmite of elevation
\eta=-\Psi r^{2}
,
\tan\varphi=2\Psi r
and

\partial_{r}\left(\frac{1}{r\sin\varphi}\right)=-\frac{1+\cos^{2}\varphi}{r^{2}\sin\varphi}\,.

(22)

We thus obtain

\displaystyle\frac{|\partial_{\xi}h_{\text{s}}|}{\tan\varphi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{8\nu V_{\rm d}\Psi^{4}}{9\pi gt_{\rm{0}}}\right)^{1/3}\frac{\cos^{10/3}{\varphi}\left(1+\cos^{2}{\varphi}\right)}{\sin^{8/3}\varphi}

(23)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{4V_{\rm d}^{\prime}\Psi^{\prime 4}}{9t_{\rm{0}}^{\prime}}\right)^{1/3}\left(\frac{h_{\rm d}}{r_{\rm d}}\right)^{8/3}\frac{\cos^{10/3}{\varphi}\left(1+\cos^{2}{\varphi}\right)}{\sin^{8/3}{\varphi}}\,,

where all the terms were nondimensionalised.
For
V_{\rm d}^{\prime}=1
,
t_{\rm{0}}^{\prime}=1
and
\Psi^{\prime}=1
, the condition
\tan\varphi\gg|\partial_{\xi}h_{\text{s}}|
is valid for
\varphi\gg$0.011\text{\,}\mathrm{rad}$
(or
0.63\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}
). For similar parameter values,
\varphi\ll 1
,
\sin\varphi\simeq\varphi
and
\cos\varphi\simeq 1
, so the validity region of Eq. (
20
) is

\varphi\gg\left(\frac{32V_{\rm d}^{\prime}}{9t_{\rm{0}}^{\prime}}\right)^{1/8}\left(\frac{h_{\rm d}}{r_{\rm d}}\right)\left(\Psi^{\prime}\right)^{1/2}.

(24)

Moreover, Eq. (
20
) translates in nondimensional form as

h_{\text{s}}^{\prime}=\left(\frac{3h_{\rm d}}{4r_{\rm d}}\frac{V_{\rm d}^{\prime}}{t_{\rm{0}}^{\prime}r^{\prime}\sin\varphi}\right)^{1/3}\,.

(25)

This scaling law rationalises the proportionality between
h_{\text{s}}^{\prime}
and
(t_{\rm{0}}^{\prime})^{-1/3}
observed far from the stalagmite centreline in the simulations reported in Fig.
8
(b). With the further assumption that
\sin\varphi\simeq\tan\varphi
at small to moderate
\varphi
, we finally obtain

h_{\text{s}}^{\prime}\simeq\left(\frac{3V_{\rm d}^{\prime}}{8t_{\rm{0}}^{\prime}\Psi^{\prime}r^{\prime 2}}\right)^{1/3}\,,

(26)

The thickness-dominated and drainage-dominated mechanisms over flat and parabolic geometries, respectively, thus yield distinct dependences of the stationary film thickness on drop inflow. We no examine how this stationary film responds to the perturbations caused by the discrete drop inflow.

4.2
Time evolution of a film thickness perturbation

As observed in Figs.
4
,
5
and
6
from Sec.
3
, the perturbation of the film thickness caused by the addition of a drop is simultaneously damped, diffused and advected outward. This combination of phenomena can be approached by considering the evolution of a small perturbation
\varepsilon
of the time-independent film thickness,
i.e.
,

h(\xi,t)\simeq h_{\text{s}}(\xi)+\varepsilon(\xi,t)
, with
|\varepsilon|\ll h_{\text{s}}.

The drainage equation (
34
) is then linearised in
\varepsilon
, which leads to

\partial_{t}\varepsilon=\frac{1}{r}\partial_{\xi}\left[\frac{gh^{2}_{\rm s}r\cos{\varphi}}{\nu}\left(\partial_{\xi}h_{\text{s}}-\tan\varphi\right)\varepsilon+\frac{gh^{3}_{\rm s}r\cos\varphi}{3\nu}\partial_{\xi}\varepsilon\right]\,.

(27)

Assuming a localised perturbation for which
|r\partial_{\xi}\varepsilon|\gg\varepsilon
, the linearised equation becomes

\partial_{t}\varepsilon+U\partial_{\xi}\varepsilon=D\partial_{\xi\xi}\varepsilon\,,

(28)

where
U
and
D
are functions of
\xi
, given by

U=\frac{gh^{2}_{\rm s}\cos\varphi}{\nu}\left(\tan\varphi-2\,\partial_{\xi}h_{\text{s}}\right)\,,\qquad D=\frac{gh^{3}_{\rm s}\cos\varphi}{3\nu}\,.

(29)

The perturbation therefore obeys an advection-diffusion equation, with
U
and
D
being interpreted as the effective propagation velocity and diffusion coefficient. Close to the stalagmite centreline,
\varphi\ll 1
so we can approximate
\sin\varphi\simeq\tan\varphi=2\Psi r
. According to Eq. (
20
),
h_{\text{s}}
is proportional to
\Psi^{-1/3}
in this case. This makes
U
proportional to
\Psi^{1/3}
, and
D
proportional to
\Psi^{-1}
. The local Péclet number that can be defined from there,
\text{Pe}=UL/D
, compares advection to diffusion at a characteristic distance
L
. This Péclet number is then proportional to
\Psi^{4/3}
for a fixed
L
. At sufficiently large
\Psi
for which
\text{Pe}\gg 1
, the perturbation should mostly be advected rather than diffused. This can be witnessed in Figs.
4
and
5
, where perturbations are mostly diffused for
\Psi^{\prime}\lesssim 1
, while they are mostly advected in the strongly curved case of
\Psi^{\prime}=10
.

In the advection-dominated case,
\tan\varphi\gg|\partial_{\xi}h_{\text{s}}|
and
\cos\varphi\simeq 1
. Substituting the scaling law of
h_{\text{s}}
from Eq. (
20
) in the above expression of the perturbation velocity
U
gives

U=\left(\frac{9gV_{\rm d}^{2}\sin\varphi}{4\pi^{2}\nu t_{\rm{0}}^{2}r^{2}}\right)^{1/3}\,,

(30)

or, in nondimensional terms,

U^{\prime}=\left(\frac{t_{\rm d}}{r_{\rm d}}\right)U=\left(\frac{9\Psi^{\prime}V^{\prime 2}_{\rm d}}{8t^{\prime 2}_{0}r^{\prime}}\right)^{1/3}.

(31)

The perturbation at position
r^{\prime}_{\rm p}
will then travel according to the following differential equation:

\frac{{\rm d}r^{\prime}_{\rm p}}{{\rm d}t^{\prime}}=\left.\vphantom{\dfrac{a}{a}}U^{\prime}\right|_{r^{\prime}=r^{\prime}_{\rm p}}\,.

(32)

Integrating this with an initial condition
r^{\prime}_{\rm p}=1
in
t^{\prime}=0
, we find

r^{\prime}_{\rm p}=\left[\left(\frac{8\Psi^{\prime}V^{\prime 2}_{\rm d}}{3t^{\prime 2}_{0}}\right)^{1/3}t^{\prime}+1\right]^{3/4}.

(33)

This prediction is in good agreement with the observed propagation of drop-induced perturbations in Fig.
5
(d), for which we had numerically found that the front evolves as
(r^{\prime})^{4/3}
within the domain of the heat map. Having established the behaviour of a local perturbation, we now turn to the global unsteady drainage.

4.3
Unsteady drainage on a parabolic stalagmite in the absence of inflow

When the drip inflow is low (namely, in the limit of large dripping period), the film may significantly drain between successive impacts. As formalised above, the perturbations associated with the drop impacts in the film are primarily advected. To predict the film thickness evolution in this case, Eqs. (
4
) and (
5
) are combined into a single differential equation for
h(\xi,t)
:

\partial_{t}h=\frac{1}{r}\partial_{\xi}\left[\frac{grh^{3}}{3\nu}\left(\left(\partial_{\xi}h\right)\cos{\varphi}-\sin{\varphi}\right)\right].

(34)

Under the condition
\tan\varphi\gg|\partial_{\xi}h|
,
i.e.
, for curved profiles, the equation above becomes

\partial_{t}h=-\frac{1}{r}\partial_{\xi}\left(\frac{grh^{3}\sin\varphi}{3\nu}\right).

(35)

Close to the stalagmite centreline,
\varphi\ll 1
so we can approximate
\sin\varphi\simeq\tan\varphi=2\Psi r
, and
\partial_{\xi}\cdot\simeq\partial_{r}\cdot
. We approximate the time-dependent solution using the following Taylor expansion:
h=h_{\rm u}+\alpha r^{n}
, with
n>1
since
\partial_{r}h=0
in
r=0
by symmetry. The notation
h_{\rm u}
refers to the unsteady film thickness. Substituting this Ansatz in Eq. (
35
) and considering the limit where
r\rightarrow 0
yields

\partial_{t}h_{\rm u}\simeq-\frac{4}{3}\frac{gh_{\rm u}^{3}\Psi}{\nu}\,.

(36)

Integrating this relation over time and considering the film thickness to be
h_{{\rm u},0}
at
t=0
, we further find that

h^{-2}_{\rm u}\simeq\frac{8g\Psi}{3\nu}t+h^{-2}_{{\rm u},0}\,.

(37)

At times verifying

t=t_{\rm{0}}\gg\frac{3\nu}{8g\Psi h^{2}_{{\rm u},0}}\,,

(38)

the stationary film thickness
h_{\rm u}
in
r=0
is approximated by

h_{\rm u}\simeq\left(\frac{8g\Psi t_{\rm{0}}}{3\nu}\right)^{-1/2}\,,

(39)

or, in nondimensional form,

h^{\prime}_{\rm u}\simeq\left(\frac{8\Psi^{\prime}t_{\rm{0}}^{\prime}}{3}\right)^{-1/2}\,.

(40)

This scaling law is observed in the numerical simulations of Figs.
7
(b) and
8
(b). Assuming that the initial film thickness is larger than
h_{\rm d}
, the criterion (
38
) becomes, in nondimensional form,

t_{\rm{0}}^{\prime}\Psi^{\prime}\gg\frac{3}{8}\,,

(41)

which is equivalent to saying that
h^{\prime}\ll 1
. This criterion is also verified in Fig.
7
(b), where the regime in
h^{\prime}\sim(\Psi^{\prime})^{-1/2}
is only valid at sufficiently large curvatures
\Psi^{\prime}>1
.

The unsteady film thickness
h^{\prime}_{\rm u}
given by Eq. (
40
) becomes smaller than the steady film thickness
h_{\text{s}}^{\prime}
given by Eq. (
26
) for a parabolic case when

r^{\prime}<\ell_{\text{sr}}^{\prime}=\left(\frac{8\Psi^{\prime}t_{\rm{0}}^{\prime}V^{\prime 2}_{\rm d}}{3}\right)^{1/4}.

(42)

The above relation suggests that unsteady drainage is more efficient close to the centreline, where the steady drainage solution produced a divergent solution.
One may consider
\ell_{\text{sr}}^{\prime}
as the aforementioned limit position surrounding a central, unsteady region where the discreteness of drop impacts influences the drainage (see Sec.
3
). Beyond this region, the unsteadiness of the inflow is smoothed out to produce a time-independent thickness profile.
For instance, using the values
t_{\rm{0}}^{\prime}=1
,
V_{\rm d}^{\prime}=1
and
\Psi^{\prime}=0.5
of Fig.
2
(f), we obtain
\ell_{\text{sr}}^{\prime}\simeq 1.1
, which matches well the radial distance beyond which the time-variations of
h
between two successive impacts become negligible. Similarly, in the configurations of Fig.
5
(b-d) with
t_{\rm{0}}^{\prime}=1
,
V_{\rm d}^{\prime}=1
and
\Psi^{\prime}=0.1
(resp.
\Psi^{\prime}=1
,
\Psi^{\prime}=10
), Eq. (
42
) predicts
\ell_{\text{sr}}^{\prime}=0.7
(resp.
\ell_{\text{sr}}^{\prime}=1.3
,
\ell_{\text{sr}}^{\prime}=2.3
). The heat maps shown in Fig.
5
confirm that time variations are indeed mostly confined in
r^{\prime}\lesssim\ell_{\text{sr}}^{\prime}
.
We further infer from Eq. (
42
) that there exists a corresponding dripping period
t^{\prime}_{0,{\rm u/s}}
delimiting the unsteady/steady regions at fixed radial position,
e.g.
, for
r^{\prime}\simeq 1
. This yields

t^{\prime}_{0,{\rm u/s}}=\frac{3}{8\Psi^{\prime}V^{\prime 2}_{\rm d}}\,.

(43)

Hence, the film thickness will be in the steady regime (resp. unsteady regime) for dripping periods
t_{\rm{0}}^{\prime}\lesssim t^{\prime}_{0,{\rm u/s}}
(resp.
t_{\rm{0}}^{\prime}\gtrsim t^{\prime}_{0,{\rm u/s}}
).

4.4
Transient propagation front

We finally come back to the early transient dynamics of the film propagation over the stalagmite, from when the first drops are added. The system of Eqs. (
4
) and (
5
) is hyperbolic at the liquid front position even though it becomes parabolic within the film (see Sec.
2.2
). Hence, it admits a similarity solution within this film, valid until the position of the moving front
\ell_{\text{f}}
, as derived by
20
in radial coordinates. Two particular cases were considered and correspond to limiting behaviours in our system covered in Sec.
3
: (i) a known liquid volume spreading under the form of a puddle over an initially dried horizontal surface (
i.e.
, a single drop), and (ii) a known flow rate continuously feeding this puddle (
i.e.
, a continuous inflow, or jet). If the volume of the puddle is equal to,
e.g.
, the volume of a drop
V_{\rm d}
in the first case, and if the flow rate feeding the puddle is
\textit{Q}_{\hskip 1.0pt\text{drip}}
in the second one, the puddle advances over the surface by covering a disk of radius
\ell_{\text{f}}(t)
, given by

\left.\ell_{\text{f}}\vphantom{\dfrac{a}{a}}\right|_{\rm flat}\simeq\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}0.9\left(\dfrac{gV_{\rm d}^{3}}{3\nu}\right)^{1/8}t^{1/8}\,\text{,}&\quad\text{(constant volume)}\,,\\
0.7\left(\dfrac{g\textit{Q}_{\hskip 1.0pt\text{drip}}^{3}}{3\nu}\right)^{1/8}t^{1/2}\,\text{,}&\quad\text{(continuous inflow)}\,,\end{array}\right.\quad\Psi=0\,.

(44)

We thus retrieve the same scalings as obtained numerically for a flat case. Replacing the continuous inflow
\textit{Q}_{\hskip 1.0pt\text{drip}}
by its discrete equivalent, both equations actually yield the same scaling
\ell_{\text{f}}\sim t_{\rm{0}}^{1/4}
, reminiscent of the crossover between both asymptotic behaviours observed in Fig.
9
(a).
In a general shape framework, there is no known analytical solution to the unsteady case of Eqs. (
4
) and (
5
), although asymptotic solutions were formerly derived in this section. These latter could further be extended numerically for variables curvature
\Psi
in the constant volume and continuous inflow regimes, along with the case of intermediate discrete drop inflow in Fig.
9
.

The front position scalings obtained numerically differ from the scaling obtained for the extent of the unsteady region,
\ell_{\text{sr}}
, that we reviewed in the previous section. The unsteady region position is time-independent even though it depends on the geometry and drop inflow considered. The front position
\ell_{\text{f}}
, however, is time-dependent. It characterises the instantaneous extent of the film tip, whereas
\ell_{\text{sr}}
denotes the length separating the unsteady, drop-dominated region from the quasi-steady region where the film evolves as under a continuous inflow. Hence, when
\ell_{\text{f}}>\ell_{\text{sr}}
, the film behaves as if fed by a continuous inflow, and the corresponding scaling laws converge toward those obtained in the continuous limit.
This suggests that at the scale of the front near vanishing film thickness, the propagation dynamics becomes insensitive to the origin of the flow forcing,
i.e.
, to whether the drainage is thickness-dominated or inclination-dominated. This behaviour can be interpreted as a manifestation of the hyperbolic nature of the governing equations at the front, where advective transport dominates over local accumulation.
This might also explain how the discrete drop additions do not influence the asymptotic behaviour of the front at large timescales, in contrast with,
e.g.
, the stationary film thickness at the stalagmite centre.
The front thus propagates as if fed by a continuous inflow regardless of the discrete nature of the drops once
\ell_{\text{f}}>\ell_{\text{sr}}
, in line with the observations made on Fig.
9
.

5
Experimental stationary film thickness

We now complete our theoretical and numerical analysis with experimental measurements of the stationary film thickness. We first review briefly the methods used to obtain these measurements, then compare them to the scalings obtained. All the measured quantities are dimensional, but will be presented in the same nondimensional framework as developed in Sec.
2.1
for consistency with the numerical results.

5.1
Methods

We report in Sec.
5.2
hereafter experimental measurements of the stationary film thickness as a function of the drop dripping period
t_{\rm{0}}
for a total of 20 stalagmites, 12 of which were considered as having a parabolic profile from photographs, and the remaining ones presenting a completely flat cap. Details are provided in Tab.
2
from App.
A
, which sums up the dimensional measurements of the drop dripping period
t_{\rm{0}}
, the stationary film thickness
h_{\text{s}}
, the radius
R
or shape factor
\Psi
. Among these stalagmites, 19 were
in situ, and one was transported to a controlled lab setting, after it had been broken during construction work in Aven d’Orgnac (France). Its top is nevertheless undamaged.
The caves provided a great diversity of shapes and dripping periods. During the lab measurements, the drop inflow could be varied systematically for a fixed shape. The measurements come from various caves.
The range of radii for flat stalagmites comes from the scattering in the drop impact point position, which affects the stalagmite spatial extension and increases with the falling height of the drops. Their falling height varied on a large range (from
\lesssim$1\text{\,}\mathrm{m}$
to
\gtrsim$25\text{\,}\mathrm{m}$
). The estimated shape factor
\Psi
for most stalagmites remains quite small, between
\sim 0.05
and
\sim 0.2
,
i.e.
, most stalagmites do not present a steep parabolic profile. This does not stem from image truncation nor visual bias but rather from the common decoupling observed, at the stalagmite scale, of a body part of more or less constant width, surmounted by a cap exhibiting a very flat or parabolic-like profile.

Measurements of the film thickness on cave stalagmites were obtained by a differential weighing technique, using a small piece of paper towel of known density and surface area (a
\sim$2\text{\,}\mathrm{cm}$
-side square, close to the crushing drop expansion diameter formed at impact, see Sec.
2.1
) to collect water at the centre of the stalagmite. This leads to surface-averaged measurements, hence the size of the towel paper piece was carefully chosen as not too close to the stalagmite radius, but still large enough for collecting a sufficient weight of water and not induce large errors in the weighing measurements. Measurements were made after artificially stopping the drop inflow at several post-impact times. This allows to infer the initial film thickness of the curve obtained. Regarding the lab measurements, a Keyence confocal sensor CL-P030 was used for live recording of the film thickness as drops were successively added into the film. Drops were created using an appropriate piece of plastic tubing and a
2\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}
-radius Luer-Lock plastic adapter to recreate drops of radius close to caves (
\sim$2.3\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}$

6
Discussion

The drainage of a thin film over a stalagmite of general shape results from the balance between gravity and viscosity, modulated by the local surface inclination. Within the lubrication framework, we analysed the film response to the combined effects of shape variability and discrete drop inflow. A summary of the main variables used throughout the study is provided in Tab.
1
.

Once at stationary state, the film thickness over a fixed stalagmite shape is uniquely defined by the discrete drop inflow. For flat stalagmites, numerical and experimental results consistently follow the theoretical scaling
h_{\text{s}}\sim t_{\rm{0}}^{-1/4}
for the stationary film thickness. For parabolic geometries, a local theoretical drainage balance rather predicts that the unsteady film thickness evolves as
\sim t_{\rm{0}}^{-1/2}
at the centreline. Away from the centreline, the stationary film thickness should follow
h_{\text{s}}\sim t_{\rm{0}}^{-1/3}
because the local surface slope predominates, and the film evolves as if fed from a continuous inflow. The scalings from numerical simulations and experiments are consistent with the theoretical derivations.
In particular, over parabolic stalagmites, the different protocols of measurements correspond to various expressions of the drainage at play. Pointwise lab measurements away from the dripping point over an inclined slope display a stationary behaviour, while drop surface-averaged cave measurements for an interrupted inflow capture the unsteady evolution of the film thickness.

All these results underlie the coexistence of local and global effects, as well as steady/unsteady regions, which compare the effect of individually adding drops to the film to the unsteady drainage.
The differences observed across shapes stem from the physical mechanisms at play. Over flat geometries, the film is driven by liquid accumulation and spreading over the entire surface whereas for parabolic stalagmites, the more efficient gravity-induced drainage prevents local build-up.
The asymptotic dependence of the stationary film thickness on the stalagmite shape can also be theoretically derived. For flat cases, the stationary film thickness follows
h_{\text{s}}\sim R^{1/4}
at first order, whereas parabolic geometries unsurprisingly yield a stationary film thickness decreasing with increasingly curved profiles.
The propagation of the drop-induced perturbations in the film is primarily driven by the different shapes as well. For flat stalagmites, the perturbations are damped within the film according to a diffusive process. Conversely, on curved surfaces, perturbations are more rapidly advected in a wave-like motion.

Additionally, the front propagation consistently follows a power-law scaling in both flat and parabolic geometries, although the relative exponents differ. There is nevertheless a shared scaling law
\ell_{\text{f}}\sim t^{1/2}
found for the front propagation resulting from various combinations of stalagmite shape and inflow regime considered,
i.e.
, different driving mechanisms. Accordingly, once the time-dependent front position lies outside of the steady region, it behaves as in the continuous limit regardless of the discrete nature of the drops. This suggests that the front locally behaves as a self-similar propagating wave.

The present modelling relies on several simplifying assumptions. We focused solely on axisymmetric stalagmites with perfectly smooth surfaces, thereupon avoiding possible film thickness variations due to lateral, capillary or rugosity effects. Similarly, drops were assumed to impact the film at a central position without splash, a condition that we could recreate in a lab setting. Although limiting, the hypotheses regarding the absence of splash at impact and the non-scattered drop impact point position distribution remain representative of low-falling height impacts in caves. On average, drops falling from high cave ceilings still land around the centre of the stalagmite. The impact point dispersal can thus be viewed as an additive local noise broadening the stalagmite width and unsteady/steady region frontier at first order. The splash at impact lasting for a few milliseconds only induces a short-term dynamics that is not considered here. Additionally, the mixing between the drop and the film at the impact location should be considered when modelling the entire coupling of film drainage combined with ion precipitation to more accurately describe stalagmite growth. These simplifications clarified the fundamental drainage mechanisms at play, but there are likely secondary local effects not captured here.

Overall, despite the simple measurement techniques used in caves, there is a reasonable correspondence between the theoretical scalings, and the numerical and experimental results. Each of them sheds light on different aspects of how the surface inclination and drop inflow affect gravity-driven drainage.
Altogether, the stationary film thickness and unsteady drop propagation analysed in this study reveal the richness of a process as simple as the thin film drainage over stalagmites.

7
Conclusions

In this study, we have investigated the interplay between discrete drop inflow and substrate geometry on the drainage dynamics of thin films over stalagmites. Starting from Reynolds lubrication theory expressed in a curvilinear coordinate system to account for the variable stalagmite shapes, we derived temporal equations relating the local film thickness to the surface-parallel flux within the film. By completing these equations with the continuity and physically relevant boundary conditions, we could solve the non-linear problem obtained numerically. Combining a theoretical analysis and the numerical results, we identified the dominant drainage regimes governing the stationary film thickness evolution that we compared to experimental measurements taken in cave and in lab.

The novelty in our modelling resides in the explicit inclusion of the discrete drop inflow, rather than a time-averaged inflow. This approach revealed that distinct regimes of steady or unsteady inflow appear at different locations and depending on the ratio between dripping period and a characteristic timescale related to the drainage outflow. These regimes are important for real stalagmites as the dripping period in caves naturally spans several orders of magnitude. The model also quantifies how the stalagmite shape directly affects the stationary film thickness and the attenuation of the perturbation caused by the discrete drops in the film.
Ultimately, this framework offers a foundation for future predictions aiming at coupling flow and ion transport to mineral deposition for stalagmite growth modelling and palaeoclimate reconstruction.

B.2
Constant pointwise inflow

We now consider the case of a constant inflow for the front propagation calculations. This inflow can be viewed as either an infinitesimally thin jet of undefined height arriving at the centre of the stalagmite, or, equivalently, as a column jet of arbitrary width. By fixing the inflow
\textit{q}_{\hskip 1.0pt\text{in}}
and width
r_{0}
of the column of liquid, we can approximate the height
h_{0}
of the film at the radial position corresponding to the liquid column outer radius.
It is related to the corresponding continuous inflow through
\textit{q}_{\hskip 1.0pt\text{in}}=-r_{0}h_{0}/(4t_{\rm{0}})
. Numerically, this inflo becomes

\textit{q}_{\hskip 1.0pt\text{in}}=\dfrac{-1}{4\Delta t\hskip 0.75ptn_{0}}\,,

(49)

with
n_{0}
the number of added drops.
At the column outer radius, the stalagmite underneath surface may be considered as relatively flat, such that
\varphi\simeq 0
