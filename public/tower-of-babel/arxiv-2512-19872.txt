Abstract
. In this paper we study the spectrality of arc-length measures supported on the union of two line segments in the plane. We show that any such spectral measure must admit a line spectrum. Moreover, when the two segments are non-parallel, such spectral measure admits only line spectra. Thus, in this case every spectrum is one dimensional. In addition we show that this property fails for unions of three or more segments in the plane. We construct some arc-length spectral measures supported on the union of at least three line segments such that none of its spectra is contained in a line. Finally, we work in the general framework of arc-length measures supported on finite unions of curves in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} . We show that the size of any orthogonal set for such a measure inside a ball of radius R R grows at most linearly in R R . We also give an alternative proof of this bound, and in fact obtain a more general result of growth rate of orthogonal sets for Ahlfors–David regular measures in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} (not restricted to the one-dimensional setting).

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Line spectra for arc-length measures supported on two line segments

A finite Borel measure

\mu
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

is called a
spectral measure
if there exists a countable set
\Lambda\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

such that the exponential functions

E_{\Lambda}=\{e^{2\pi i<\lambda,x>}:\lambda\in\Lambda\}

form an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mu)
.
Such a set
\Lambda
is called a
spectrum
of
\mu
. In particular, if the Lebesgue measure restricted on the set
\Omega
is a spectral measure, then we say
\Omega
is a
spectral set
. The theory of spectral sets traces back to the work of Fuglede
[
7
]
who proposed

Fuglede Conjecture:

\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a spectral set if and only if it tiles
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by translations.

Both directions of the conjecture fail when
d\geq 3

[
20
,
11
,
12
,
5
,
6
]
, but the conjecture is still open in both directions when
d=1,2
. Moreover, the problem of deciding whether a given measure is spectral, and of describing the possible spectra, is closely related to the Fuglede conjecture and has been extensively studied in recent decades for Lebesgue measure, for self-similar measures, and for various classes of singular measures.

Spectra can be surprisingly rich. A particularly natural class of spectra to consider for measures in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
are line spectra, that is, spectra contained in a straight line. One may then ask to what extent the geometry of the support forces spectra to be one dimensional. In this paper we focus on the case where the measure is supported on the union of finitely many line segments in the plane and is equal to a constant multiple of arc-length on each of these segments. We are interested in whether the spectra of such arc-length spectral measures lie on straight lines.
In particular, when the arc-length measure is supported on the union of two line segments, all known results so far show that every spectrum of the associated spectral measure is contained in straight lines. For example, several recent works have investigated the spectrality of the measure

\mu=\frac{1}{2}L_{[t,t+1]}\times\delta_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{0}\times L_{[t,t+1]},

where
L_{[t,t+1]}
is the Lebesgue measure restricted on the interval
[t,t+1]
and
\delta_{0}
is the Dirac measure on the origin.
Combining these papers
[
9
,
16
,
15
,
1
]
with the paper
[
13
]
by the first and third author, one obtains necessary and sufficient conditions for the measure
\mu
to be a spectral measure and shows that it has only line spectra. See also the relevant works
[
9
,
2
]

This leads to the following natural question:

Question 1
.

Given any two line segments in the plane, when is the arc-length measure supported on their union a spectral measure, and, if it is spectral, must all its spectra be line spectra?

In this paper, we provide a complete answer to this question. To help the reader follow the discussion, we briefly summarize the known results in this area in Remark
1

Non-parallel line segments.

Theorem 1
.

Given are real numbers
a_{1}
,
a_{2}
and
h_{1}\neq h_{2}
. Let
T_{1},T_{2}>0
and
T_{1}+T_{2}=2
. Then the measure
\nu=\frac{1}{2}L_{[a_{1},a_{1}+T_{1}]}\times\delta_{h_{1}}+\frac{1}{2}L_{[a_{2},a_{2}+T_{2}]}\times\delta_{h_{2}}

For a case of non-parallel line segments, i.e. the measure
\rho
of the form (
1
) with

T_{1}=T_{2}
, Lu
[
17
]
proved that
\rho
is a spectral measure if and only if

or
t_{1}-t_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
. However, in the paper
[
17
]
, he does not identify the possible structures of the line spectra. In this paper, we establish the sufficient and necessary condition of spectrality for general non-parallel line segments and show the structures of the line spectra: a spectral measure of non-parallel line segments has precisely two possible types of line spectra. Even for the case where
T_{1}=T_{2}
, our proof includes a completely different method from Lu’s
[
17
]
. Now we state our second result.

Theorem 2
.

Let
t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
and
T_{1},T_{2}>0
with
T_{1}+T_{2}=2
. Consider the measure

\rho=\frac{1}{2}L_{[t_{1},t_{1}+T_{1}]}\times\delta_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{0}\times L_{[t_{2},t_{2}+T_{2}]}.

Then
\rho
is a spectral measure if and only if one of the following statements hold.

(I)

When
T_{1}=T_{2}
, we have
t_{1}+t_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{-1\}
or
t_{1}-t_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
.

(II)

When
T_{1}\neq T_{2}
, we have
t_{1}+t_{2}\in 2\mathbb{Z}
or
t_{1}-t_{2}-T_{2}\in 2\mathbb{Z}
(equivalently,
t_{2}-t_{1}-T_{1}\in 2\mathbb{Z}
).

Moreover, if
\rho
is a spectral measure, then every spectrum of
\rho
that contains the origin is contained in either the line
y=x
or the line
y=-x
. More precisely, if
0\in\Lambda
is a spectrum of
\rho
, then
\Lambda
has one of the following forms:

(I)

If
T_{1}=T_{2}
, then there exist
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in(-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}]
such that

(2)

\Lambda=\begin{cases}\{(n,-n),(n+\alpha_{1},-(n+\alpha_{1})):n\in\mathbb{Z}\},&\text{if }t_{1}+t_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{-1\},\\[6.0pt]
\{(n,n),(n+\alpha_{2},n+\alpha_{2}):n\in\mathbb{Z}\},&\text{if }t_{1}-t_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}.\end{cases}

(II)

If
T_{1}\neq T_{2}
, then

(3)

\Lambda=\begin{cases}\{(\tfrac{n}{2},-\tfrac{n}{2}):n\in\mathbb{Z}\},&\text{if }t_{1}+t_{2}\in 2\mathbb{Z},\\[6.0pt]
\{(\tfrac{n}{2},\tfrac{n}{2}):n\in\mathbb{Z}\},&\text{if }t_{1}-t_{2}-T_{2}\in 2\mathbb{Z}.\end{cases}

Remark 2
.

Here we note that
t_{1}-t_{2}-T_{2}\in 2\mathbb{Z}
is equivalent to
t_{2}-t_{1}-T_{1}\in 2\mathbb{Z}
, since
T_{1}+T_{2}=2
.

The observations in Remark
1
together with Theorems
1
and
2
yield the following result.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\mu
be the arc-length measure supported on the union of two line segments in the plane. Then
\mu
is a spectral measure if and only if it admits a line spectrum. Furthermore, in the non-parallel case, the measure admits only line spectra.

Remark 3
.

As explained in
[
13
, §4]
the line spectra of a collection of line segments equipped with the arc-length on them always arise as one-dimensional spectra of the projection of these segments onto a straight line. Thus, in the case of two non-parallel line segments on the straight lines
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
the possible spectra are on either one of the two angle bisectors of
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
and therefore it suffices to project the two segments on each of these two lines and check (a) if the projection is injective and (b) that the resulting union of two line segments on the line is spectral. This last condition is known to be equivalent to this projection tiling the line
[
14
]
.

1.2.
No line spectra for some arc-length measures supported on more than two line segments

Suppose
\mu
is a measure on the plane which is arc-length on a finite number of line segments. Such measures have recently been examined for their spectrality
[
15
,
13
,
9
]
. In all examples known so far any such spectral measure
\mu
always had a spectrum contained in a straight line (it could possibly have other spectra too without this property).

It is therefore natural to suspect that this might be the general case. Here we show that this is not so. There are finite collections of line segments in the plane such that their accompanying measure is spectral and each of their spectra is not contained in a straight line.

Notice that for such a measure to have a spectrum contained in a straight line
S
it is necessary that the orthogonal projection from the support of the measure to
S
is injective. The reason is that all complex exponentials
e^{2\pi\lambda\cdot x}
with
\lambda\in S
are constant along the direction
S^{\perp}
. If the support of the measure contains two points on the same translate of the line
S^{\perp}
then a function on our segments that takes different values on these two points cannot be represented by these exponentials. (See Propositon
4
).

Thus it is rather easy to prove that a collection of line segments does not have a line spectrum: it is enough that on any line
S
the orthogonal projection is not injective on our set. And if such a collection happens to project injectively onto the line
S
, then for it to have a spectrum contained in
S
it must also project to a spectral measure on
S

[
13
, Theorem 1.2]
.

For the existence of the spectrum we will need the following theorem. It describes a situation where the convolution of two spectral measures produces a spectral measure, something which, of course, does not happen in general.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose
\mu_{1}
is a measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
L\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{k}\times{\left\{{0}\right\}}^{d-k}
is a spectrum of
\mu_{1}
. Suppose also that
\nu_{1}
is a measure on
{\left\{{0}\right\}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d-k}
and
M\subseteq{\left\{{0}\right\}}^{k}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d-k}
is a spectrum of
\nu_{1}
.
Then
L+M
is a spectrum of
\mu_{1}*\nu_{1}
.

By using Theorem
4
and projection, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 5
.

There exists a spectral arc-length measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, supported on the union of at least three line segments, such that every spectrum
\Lambda
of
\mu
is not contained in a straight line.

1.3.
Size of orthogonal sets of exponentials

In the previous subsections we dealt with very concrete arc-length measures supported on finitely many line segments. For these measures, every spectrum has strong one dimensional features. For example, in the case of two segments every spectrum is contained in a straight line. In the more general case, with three or more segments, we still need good control on how fast an orthogonal set can grow. A key underlying ingredient behind these results is always the same: the support is essentially one dimensional, so an orthogonal set cannot be too dense. Informally, the size of any orthogonal set inside a ball of radius
R
grows at most linearly in
R
.

The purpose of the final part of the paper is to isolate this phenomenon and to state it in a clean, abstract form that applies not only to our concrete examples but to any finite collection of curves.

Theorem 6

(Any finite collection of curves)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a finite union of curves in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, each of which is rectifiable and has finite, nonzero length, and let
\rho
be the arc-length measure on
\Gamma
. If
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an orthogonal set for
\rho
,
then
\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\#(\Lambda\cap B_{R}(x))=O(R)
as
R\rightarrow\infty.

Theorem
6
also yields the following corollary
7
.

Corollary 7

(Any finite collection of line segments)
.

Let
d\geq 2
be an integer.
Let
L_{1},\dots,L_{m}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be
m
line segments, and let
\rho
be the arc-length measure on
\bigcup_{k=1}^{m}L_{k}
. If
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an orthogonal set for
\rho
, then
\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\#(\Lambda\cap B_{R}(x))=O(R)
as
R\rightarrow\infty.

2.
Spectrality of two line segments

2.1.
Parallel line segments

Define the measure

Remark 4
.

The previous proof of Theorem
1
gives an infinity of line spectra for a set of two parallel line segments not on the same line. This infinite collection of different spectra has the additional property that they can be arbitrarily sparse: if the line onto which we are projecting our two segments is almost perpendicular to the intervals themselves then the projection of the two segments has support whose measure tends to 0. Therefore the spectra of the projection, which are also spectra of the initial set of two segments, have linear density that can be arbitrarily small.

Remark 5
.

It is worth pointing out that if we only care to show the existence of some spectrum for two parallel line segments not on the same line then we can project them onto a straight line such the projection is injective (with the exception of the endpoints) and the support of the projection is a single interval, which always has a spectrum. To find this straight line suppose without loss of generality that our segments are parallel to the

2.2.
Non-parallel line segments

According to Remark
1
(III), when studying the spectrality of two non-parallel line segments we can always reduce to the same question for two line segments that are perpendicular to each other. So let
\rho=\frac{1}{2}L_{[t_{1},t_{1}+T_{1}]}\times\delta_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{0}\times L_{[t_{2},t_{2}+T_{2}]}
with
T_{1}+T_{2}=2
and

, and let
\mathcal{Z}\left(\hat{\rho}\right)
denote the zero set of the Fourier transform

. We know (see for instance
[

]

\left(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\right)\in\mathcal{Z}\left(\hat{\rho}\right)
if and only if

(4)

\displaystyle e^{\pi i\left(\lambda_{1}\left(2t_{1}+T_{1}\right)-\lambda_{2}\left(2t_{2}+T_{2}\right)\right)}\frac{\sin\pi T_{1}\lambda_{1}}{\pi\lambda_{1}}+\frac{\sin\pi T_{2}\lambda_{2}}{\pi\lambda_{2}}=0,

which implies that
\mathcal{Z}\left(\hat{\rho}\right)=Z_{1}\cup Z_{2}
, where

Z_{1}=\frac{\mathbb{Z}\backslash\{0\}}{T_{1}}\times\frac{\mathbb{Z}\backslash\{0\}}{T_{2}}

and, defining
T\left(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\right):=\lambda_{1}\left(2t_{1}+T_{1}\right)-\lambda_{2}\left(2t_{2}+T_{2}\right)
,

Z_{2}=\left\{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}):T\left(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\right)\in\mathbb{Z}\ \ \text{and}\ \ (-1)^{T\left(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\right)}\frac{\sin\pi T_{1}\lambda_{1}}{\pi\lambda_{1}}+\frac{\sin\pi T_{2}\lambda_{2}}{\pi\lambda_{2}}=0\right\}.

In what follows, we use a sequence of lemmas to show that the projections of the spectrum of
\rho
onto the

Lemma 1
.

Let
0\in\Lambda
be a spectrum for
L^{2}(\rho)
. Then the following two statements hold.

(I)

If
T_{1}\geq T_{2}
, then
\Lambda
has at most one point on each vertical line.

(II)

If
T_{1}\leq T_{2}
, then
\Lambda
has at most one point on each horizontal line.

Proof.

We first prove (I). Suppose that
\Lambda
does not have at most one point on each vertical line. Then, since the difference of these two points of
\Lambda
must be a zero of
\widehat{\rho}
, it follows from (
4

(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\in\Lambda
and
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}^{\prime})\in\Lambda
with
\lambda_{2}\neq\lambda_{2}^{\prime}
such that

e^{-\pi i(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{2}^{\prime})\left(2t_{2}+T_{2}\right)}T_{1}+\frac{\sin\pi T_{2}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{2}^{\prime})}{\pi(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{2}^{\prime})}=0,

which means
{\left|{\frac{\sin\pi T_{2}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{2}^{\prime})}{\pi T_{2}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{2}^{\prime})}}\right|}=\frac{T_{1}}{T_{2}}
. Note that
{\left|{\frac{\sin\pi T_{2}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{2}^{\prime})}{\pi T_{2}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{2}^{\prime})}}\right|}<1\leq\frac{T_{1}}{T_{2}}
, which is a contradiction.
For (II), we also arrive at this conclusion through a proof similar to (I).

∎

We now show that any spectrum of
L^{2}(\rho)
is contained in a family of equidistant parallel lines by the next two lemmas.

Lemma 2
.

Let
0\in\Lambda
be a spectrum for
L^{2}(\rho)
. Then
\Lambda\not\subseteq\frac{\mathbb{Z}}{T_{1}}\times\frac{\mathbb{Z}}{T_{2}}
and

\Lambda\subseteq\left\{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}):T(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})=\lambda_{1}\left(2t_{1}+T_{1}\right)-\lambda_{2}\left(2t_{2}+T_{2}\right)\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}.

Proof.

Let

H_{1}=\left\{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}):\lambda_{1}\left(2t_{1}+T_{1}\right)-\lambda_{2}\left(2t_{2}+T_{2}\right)\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}

and

H_{2}:=\frac{\mathbb{Z}}{T_{1}}\times\frac{\mathbb{Z}}{T_{2}}
.

H_{1}
and
H_{2}
are both subgroups of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.
It follows from Lemma 11.4 in
[
8
]
that
\Lambda\subseteq H_{1}
or
\Lambda\subseteq H_{2}
.
Suppose that
\Lambda\subseteq H_{2}
.

If
T_{1}\geq T_{2}
, then we
take the function
f\in L^{2}(\rho)
which is
1
on the horizontal segment and
0
on the vertical segment. Notice that it does not matter if the two segments of
\rho
intersect as they will intersect on at most one point which has
\rho
-measure 0. Then, for any
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\in\Lambda
, we have
\lambda_{1}\in\frac{\mathbb{Z}}{T_{1}}
and

\displaystyle\left\langle f,e_{\lambda}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\int f(x,y)e^{-2\pi i\left(\lambda_{1}x+\lambda_{2}y\right)}d\rho(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{1}+T_{1}}e^{-2\pi i\lambda_{1}x}dx

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{T_{1}}{2}&\text{ if }\lambda_{1}=0;\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Moreover, Lemma
1
(I) implies that there is at most one point of
\Lambda
with
\lambda_{1}=0
, so this point is the origin, which means
f=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\left\langle f,e_{\lambda}\right\rangle e_{\lambda}
is constant
\rho
-almost everywhere (the series has one term only), a contradiction. Thus
\Lambda\subset H_{1}
.

If
T_{1}\leq T_{2}
, then we
take the function
f\in L^{2}(\rho)
which is
1
on the vertical segment and
0
on the horizontal segment and we can obtain the conclusion by a proof similar to the case
T_{1}\geq T_{2}
.

∎

Lemma 3
.

Let
0\in\Lambda
be a spectrum for
L^{2}(\rho)
. Then
\Lambda
is distributed on equidistant parallel lines.

Proof.

It follows from Lemma
2
that we have

\Lambda\subseteq\left\{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}):T(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})=\lambda_{1}\left(2t_{1}+T_{1}\right)-\lambda_{2}\left(2t_{2}+T_{2}\right)\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}.

Moreover,

[
9
, Theorem 1]
shows
2t_{1}+T_{1}
and
2t_{2}+T_{2}
cannot both be equal to zero. Otherwise,
\rho
is not a spectral measure. So
\Lambda
is distributed on equidistant parallel lines. (Refer to Fig.
5
.)

We define the projections of
\Lambda
on the
x
-axis and
y
-axis as

\Lambda_{x}=\{\lambda_{1}:(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\in\Lambda\}\ \ \text{and}\ \ \Lambda_{y}=\{\lambda_{2}:(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\in\Lambda\},

respectively. These sets can be multi-sets.

Lemma 4
.

If
\Lambda
is a spectrum of
\rho
, then

(5)

\displaystyle\frac{2}{T_{1}}=\sum_{\lambda\in T_{1}\Lambda_{x}}\left|\hat{\mathbf{1}}_{\left[0,1\right]}\right|^{2}\left(s-\lambda\right)\ \ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \ \frac{2}{T_{2}}=\sum_{\lambda\in T_{2}\Lambda_{y}}\left|\hat{\mathbf{1}}_{\left[0,1\right]}\right|^{2}\left(s-\lambda\right)

for
s\in\mathbb{R}
.
Additionally,

(I)

If
\Lambda
has at most one point on each vertical line, then
\Lambda_{x}
is a periodic set.

(II)

If
\Lambda
has at most one point on each horizontal line, then
\Lambda_{y}
is a periodic set.

Proof.

Assume first that
\Lambda
has at most one point on each vertical line. Consider the function
f\in L^{2}(\rho)
which is
0
on the
y
-axis and equal to
g(x)=e^{2\pi ix\frac{s}{T_{1}}}
on
[t_{1},t_{1}+T_{1}]\times\{0\}
.
It follows from Parseval’s theorem and
f(x,y)\in L^{2}\left(\rho\right)
that

\displaystyle\frac{T_{1}}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{1}+T_{1}}|g(x)|^{2}dx

\displaystyle=\int|f(x,y)|^{2}d\rho

\displaystyle=\sum_{\left(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\right)\in\Lambda}\left|\frac{1}{2}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{1}+T_{1}}f(x,0)e^{-2\pi i\lambda_{1}x}dx\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\lambda_{1}\in\Lambda_{x}}\left|\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{1}+T_{1}}g(x)e^{-2\pi i\lambda_{1}x}dx\right|^{2}

which gives

2T_{1}=\sum_{\lambda_{1}\in\Lambda_{x}}\left|\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{1}+T_{1}}e^{2\pi ix\frac{s}{T_{1}}}e^{-2\pi i\lambda_{1}x}dx\right|^{2}=\sum_{\lambda_{1}\in\Lambda_{x}}\left|\int_{0}^{T_{1}}e^{2\pi ix\frac{s}{T_{1}}}e^{-2\pi i\lambda_{1}x}dx\right|^{2}=T_{1}^{2}\sum_{\lambda_{1}\in\Lambda_{x}}\left|\int_{0}^{1}e^{2\pi ixs}e^{-2\pi iT_{1}\lambda_{1}x}dx\right|^{2}.

Therefore,

\frac{2}{T_{1}}=\sum_{\lambda\in T_{1}\Lambda_{x}}\left|\hat{\mathbf{1}}_{\left[0,1\right]}\right|^{2}\left(s-\lambda\right).

By Lemma
2
and Lemma
3
, we know
\Lambda\not\subseteq\frac{\mathbb{Z}}{T_{1}}\times\frac{\mathbb{Z}}{T_{2}}
and
\Lambda
is distributed on equidistant parallel lines. Following the arguments in the proofs of Lemmas 5.1, 5.3 and 5.4 in the recent paper
[

]
of the first and third author of this paper, we obtain that there exists a constant

such that for all

we have

and

is a set of finite complexity, so
\Lambda_{x}
is a periodic set.

The proof for the case when

has at most one point on each horizontal line is essentially identical.

The tiling conditions (

The next lemma provides a convenient normal form for discrete periodic multi-sets.

Lemma 5
.

Let
T,w>0
. Suppose that
0\in\Lambda
is a discrete periodic multi-set and

2w=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\left|\hat{\mathbf{1}}_{\left[0,T\right]}\right|^{2}\left(s-\lambda\right)

for
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Then
\Lambda
can be represented as

\Lambda=\{\lambda^{1},\lambda^{2},\cdots,\lambda^{m}\}+\frac{mT}{2w}\mathbb{Z}

for some
0<m\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and
0=\lambda^{1}\leq\lambda^{2}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda^{m}<\frac{mT}{2w}
.

Proof.

Since
\Lambda
is a discrete periodic multiset there exists
A>0
such that
\Lambda=\Lambda+A
and the number of points of
\Lambda
in any interval of length
A
is finite. Hence, there exists a positive integer
m
such that
\{\lambda^{1},\lambda^{2},\cdots,\lambda^{m}\}\subset[0,A)
with
0=\lambda^{1}\leq\lambda^{2}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda^{m}<A
. So
\Lambda=\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}(\lambda^{i}+A\mathbb{Z})
and

\displaystyle 2w

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A}\int_{0}^{A}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\left|\hat{\mathbf{1}}_{\left[0,T\right]}\left(s-\lambda\right)\right|^{2}\,ds

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{0}^{A}\left|\hat{\mathbf{1}}_{\left[0,T\right]}\left(s-\lambda^{i}-Ak\right)\right|^{2}\,ds

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{-Ak}^{-A(k-1)}\left|\hat{\mathbf{1}}_{\left[0,T\right]}\left(s-\lambda^{i}\right)\right|^{2}\,ds

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\hat{\mathbf{1}}_{\left[0,T\right]}\left(s-\lambda^{i}\right)\right|^{2}\,ds

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbf{1}_{\left[0,T\right]}\left(s-\lambda^{i}\right)\right|^{2}ds\ \ \ (\text{Parseval})

\displaystyle=\frac{mT}{A}.

This means that
A=\frac{mT}{2w}
and
\Lambda=\{\lambda^{1},\lambda^{2},\cdots,\lambda^{m}\}+\frac{mT}{2w}\mathbb{Z}
.

∎

The next theorem is a simple but powerful rigidity result in one dimension.
It says that if a periodic set
\Lambda
satisfies the Parseval-type identity coming from the interval
[0,T]
, then
\Lambda
has essentially only one possible form: it must be the integer translates of two points, and when
T>1
, it must be the half-integer lattice.

Theorem 9
.

Let
T\geq 1
and
m
be a positive integer. If
\Lambda=\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{2m}\}+m\mathbb{Z}

with
0=\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}<\cdots\alpha_{2m}<m
and

\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\left|\hat{\mathbf{1}}_{\left[0,T\right]}\right|^{2}\left(s-\lambda\right)=2T

for
s\in\mathbb{R}
, then
\Lambda=\{0,\alpha\}+\mathbb{Z}
for some
0<\alpha<1
. Moreover, we have
\Lambda=\frac{\mathbb{Z}}{2}
when
T>1
.

Proof.

Applying the Fourier transform to both sides of the equation

\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\left|\hat{\mathbf{1}}_{\left[0,T\right]}\right|^{2}\left(s-\lambda\right)=2T

and the distributional Poisson summation formula, we get

({\mathbf{1}}_{\left[0,T\right]}*\widetilde{\mathbf{1}}_{\left[0,T\right]})(x)\cdot\sum_{j=1}e^{2\pi i\alpha_{j}x}\cdot\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\delta_{\frac{k}{m}}=2T\delta_{0}.

Since
\hat{\mathbf{1}}_{\left[0,T\right]}*\tilde{\mathbf{1}}_{\left[0,T\right]}>0
in
(-T,T)
and
T\geq 1
, we conclude that

\sum_{j=1}^{2m}e^{2\pi i\alpha_{j}\frac{l}{m}}=0\ \ \text{ for any }\ \ l\in\{\pm 1,\pm 2,\cdots,\pm(m-1)\}.

Let
u_{j}=e^{2\pi i\frac{\alpha_{j}}{m}}
and
denote by
p_{k}\left(u_{1},\ldots,u_{2m}\right)
the
k
-th power sum

p_{k}\left(u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{2m}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{2m}u_{i}^{k}=u_{1}^{k}+\cdots+u_{2m}^{k}\ \ \text{ for}\ \ k\geqslant 1.

Then we have

(7)

\begin{cases}p_{1}\left(u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{2m}\right)=0,\\[6.0pt]
p_{2}\left(u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{2m}\right)=0,\\[6.0pt]
\cdots\\
p_{m-1}\left(u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{2m}\right)=0.\end{cases}

The polynomial with roots
u_{j}
may be expanded as

P(x):=\prod_{j=1}^{2m}\left(x-u_{j}\right)=\sum_{j=0}^{2m}(-1)^{j}e_{j}x^{2m-j},

where (these are the so-called Newton identities)

\begin{cases}e_{0}=1,\\[6.0pt]
e_{1}=p_{1},\\[6.0pt]
e_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(e_{1}p_{1}-p_{2}\right),\\[6.0pt]
e_{3}=\frac{1}{3}\left(e_{2}p_{1}-e_{1}p_{2}+p_{3}\right),\\
\vdots\end{cases}

Here
e_{j}
is the
j
-th elementary symmetric function of the numbers
u_{1},\ldots,u_{2m}
.

By (
7
), we have
e_{1}=e_{2}=\cdots=e_{m-1}=0
. This means

\displaystyle P(x)

\displaystyle=x^{2m}+(-1)^{m}e_{m}x^{m}+(-1)^{m+1}e_{m+1}x^{m-1}

\displaystyle\hskip 30.00005pt+(-1)^{m+2}e_{m+2}x^{m-2}+\cdots+(-1)^{2m}e_{2m}

\displaystyle=x^{2m}+0\cdot x^{2m-1}+0\cdot x^{2m-2}+\cdots+0\cdot x^{m+1}+(-1)^{m}e_{m}x^{m}

\displaystyle\hskip 30.00005pt+(-1)^{m+1}e_{m+1}x^{m-1}+(-1)^{m+2}e_{m+2}x^{m-2}+\cdots+e_{2m}.

Moreover,
P\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{2m}\left(\frac{1}{x}-u_{i}\right)=\sum_{j=0}^{2m}(-1)^{j}e_{j}x^{j-2m}
and

\displaystyle x^{2m}P\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)

\displaystyle=1+(-1)^{m}e_{m}x^{m}+(-1)^{m+1}e_{m+1}x^{m+1}

\displaystyle\hskip 30.00005pt+(-1)^{m+2}e_{m+2}x^{m+2}+\cdots+(-1)^{2m}e_{2m}x^{2m}

\displaystyle=e_{2m}x^{2m}-e_{2m-1}x^{2m-2}+\cdots+(-1)^{m+1}e_{m+1}x^{m+1}

\displaystyle\hskip 30.00005pt+(-1)^{m}e_{m}x^{m}+0\cdot x^{m-1}+\cdots+0\cdot x+1.

Note that since the roots of
P(x)
are
u_{1},u_{2},\cdots u_{2m}
and the roots of
x^{2m}P(\frac{1}{x})
are
\overline{u_{1}},\overline{u_{2}},\cdots\overline{u_{2m}}
, it follows that

P(x)=Cx^{2m}\overline{P}(\frac{1}{x})

for
C=e_{2m}
and
u_{1},u_{2},\cdots u_{2m}
are the roots of

x^{2m}+(-1)^{m}e_{m}x^{m}+C=0.

Since
u_{1}=1
it follows that the numbers
u_{1},\ldots,u_{2m}
are precisely the numbers

\{e^{2\pi i\frac{j}{m}}:j\in\{0,1,\cdots,m-1\}\}\cup\{e^{2\pi i\frac{\alpha+j}{m}}:j\in\{0,1,\cdots,m-1\}\}

for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
. It follows that the numbers
a_{1},\ldots,a_{2m}
are exactly the numbers

0,\alpha,1,\alpha+1,\ldots,m-1,\alpha+m-1

and therefore

\Lambda=\{0,\alpha\}+\mathbb{Z}
.

Moreover, if
T>1
, then

\sum_{j=1}^{2m}e^{2\pi i\alpha_{j}\frac{l}{m}}=0\ \ \text{ for any }\ \ l\in\{\pm 1,\pm 2,\cdots,\pm m\}.

So

p_{1}\left(u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{2m}\right)=p_{2}\left(u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{2m}\right)=\cdots=p_{m}\left(u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{2m}\right)=0

and
e_{1}=e_{2}=\cdots=e_{m}=0
. From the proof above, we can obtain
u_{1},u_{2},\cdots u_{2m}
are the roots of
x^{2m}+C=0
. Since
u_{1}=1
, this means that
C=-1
and the numbers
u_{1},\ldots,u_{2m}
are precisely the numbers

\{e^{2\pi i\frac{j}{2m}}:j=0,1,\ldots,2m-1\}.

It follows that the numbers
a_{1},\ldots,a_{2m}
are the numbers

0,\frac{1}{2},1,\ldots,m-\frac{1}{2}
and therefore
\Lambda=\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{Z}}
.

∎

\Lambda-\Lambda

by the spectrum condition. These constraints will be used repeatedly in the sequel.

Proposition 2
.

Let
0\in\Lambda
be a spectrum for
L^{2}(\rho)
,
then the following three statements hold.

(I)

If
T_{1}=T_{2}
, then for any
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\in(\Lambda-\Lambda)\setminus\{0\}
with

|\lambda_{2}|=|\lambda_{1}|

(II)

If
T_{1}\geq T_{2}
, then for any
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\in(\Lambda-\Lambda)\setminus\{0\}
with
|\lambda_{1}|=\frac{1}{2}
, we have
|\lambda_{2}|=|\lambda_{1}|=\frac{1}{2}
.

(III)

If
T_{1}\leq T_{2}
, then for any
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\in(\Lambda-\Lambda)\setminus\{0\}
with
|\lambda_{2}|=\frac{1}{2}
, we have
|\lambda_{1}|=|\lambda_{2}|=\frac{1}{2}
.

Proof.

(I). By Lemma
2
, we have
T(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}
, which means
{\left|{\frac{\sin\pi T_{1}\lambda_{1}}{\pi T_{1}\lambda_{1}}}\right|}={\left|{\frac{\sin\pi T_{1}\lambda_{2}}{\pi T_{1}\lambda_{2}}}\right|}.
The function
f(x)=\frac{\sin\pi x}{\pi x}
shown in the following Figure
6
takes each value only twice when
{\left|{x}\right|}\leq 1/2
. It follows from

that
|\lambda_{2}|=|\lambda_{1}|
.

Figure 6.

f(x)=\frac{\sin\pi x}{\pi x}

(II). If
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\in(\Lambda-\Lambda)\setminus\{0\}
with
|\lambda_{1}|=\frac{1}{2}
, by Lemma
2
, we have
T(\frac{1}{2},\lambda_{2})
or

, and in any case we get
{\left|{\frac{\sin\frac{1}{2}\pi T_{1}}{\frac{1}{2}\pi}}\right|}={\left|{\frac{\sin\pi T_{2}\lambda_{2}}{\pi\lambda_{2}}}\right|}
.
Since

.

(III). Its proof is similar to (II), so we omit it here.

With these results in hand, it remains to verify that

has multiplicity one, i.e.,

Proposition 3
.

Let
0\in\Lambda
be a spectrum for
L^{2}(\rho)
. Then

(I)

\Lambda
has multiplicity one;

(II)

\Lambda_{x}
and
\Lambda_{y}
are periodic sets.

Proof.

(I). If
T_{1}=T_{2}
, we can get the conclusion from Lemma
1
. If
T_{1}>T_{2}
, then
\Lambda_{x}=\frac{\mathbb{Z}}{2}
and
\Lambda
has at most one point on each vertical line by Theorem
9
and Lemma
1
.
Hence, for any
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\in\Lambda
and
(\lambda_{1}^{\prime},\lambda_{2})\in\Lambda
, we have
\lambda_{1}^{\prime}-\lambda_{1}^{\prime}\in\frac{{\mathbb{Z}}}{2}
and

{\left|{\frac{\sin(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{1}^{\prime})\pi T_{2}}{(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{1}^{\prime})\pi}}\right|}={\left|{\frac{\sin(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{1}^{\prime})\pi T_{1}}{(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{1}^{\prime})\pi}}\right|}={\left|{\frac{\sin(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{2})\pi T_{2}}{(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{2})\pi}}\right|}=T_{2},

\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{1}^{\prime}
. So
\Lambda

\Lambda
has multiplicity one. If

\Lambda
has multiplicity one using similar proof methods.

(II). We can get (II) by (I) and Lemma

.

With multiplicity one and the periodicity of

\Lambda_{x}
and

\Lambda_{y}
in hand, we can now pin down the spectrum completely. The next theorem gives the explicit classification of all possible
\Lambda
.

Theorem 10
.

Let
0\in\Lambda
be a spectrum for
L^{2}(\rho)
,
then the following two statements hold.

(I)

If
T_{1}\neq T_{2}
, then
\Lambda=\{(\frac{n}{2},\frac{n}{2}):n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
or
\Lambda=\{(\frac{n}{2},-\frac{n}{2}):n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
.

(II)

If
T_{1}=T_{2}
, then

\Lambda=\{(n,n),(n+\alpha,n+\alpha):n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
or
\Lambda=\{(n,-n),(n+\alpha,-(n+\alpha)):n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
for some
\alpha\in(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]\setminus\{0\}
.

Proof.

(I). If
T_{1}>T_{2}
, according to Remark
6
and Theorem
9
, we have
\Lambda_{x}=\{0,\frac{1}{2}\}+\mathbb{Z}
. And by Proposition
2
, we have
(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})\in\Lambda
or
(\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2})\in\Lambda
.
Suppose that
(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})\in\Lambda
, then from Proposition
2
and Proposition
3
, we have
(-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}),(1,1)\ \text{and}\ (-1,-1)\in\Lambda
. By induction, assume
(\frac{m}{2},\frac{m}{2})\in\Lambda
for all
|m|\leq n
. If
|m|=n+1
, there exists
\gamma
such that
(\frac{m}{2},\gamma)\in\Lambda
. Since
(\frac{m-1}{2},\frac{m-1}{2})\in\Lambda
, from Proposition
3
, we have
\gamma=\frac{m}{2}
. Hence,
\Lambda=\{(\frac{n}{2},\frac{n}{2}):n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
. Similarly, for the case where
(\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2})\in\Lambda
, we can get
\Lambda=\{(\frac{n}{2},-\frac{n}{2}):n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
.

If
T_{1}<T_{2}
, we can obtain similarly that

\Lambda=\{(\frac{n}{2},\frac{n}{2}):n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
or
\Lambda=\{(\frac{n}{2},-\frac{n}{2}):n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
.

(II). According to Remark
6
and Theorem
9
,
\Lambda_{x}
and
\Lambda_{y}
can be represented as
\Lambda_{x}=\{0,\alpha_{1}\}+\mathbb{Z}
and
\Lambda_{y}=\{0,\alpha_{2}\}+\mathbb{Z}
for some
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]\setminus\{0\}
.

Suppose that
\alpha_{1}=\frac{1}{2}
or
\alpha_{2}=\frac{1}{2}
, then from (I), we have
\Lambda=\{(\frac{n}{2},\frac{n}{2}):n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
or
\Lambda=\{(\frac{n}{2},-\frac{n}{2}):n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
.

Suppose that
\alpha_{1}\neq\frac{1}{2}
and
\alpha_{2}\neq\frac{1}{2}
. From Proposition
2
, we have
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}
or
\alpha_{1}=-\alpha_{2}
, and for any
(n,\lambda_{2}),(n+\alpha_{1},\lambda_{2}^{\prime})\in\Lambda
with
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, we have
\lambda_{2}=m
for some
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and
|\lambda_{2}^{\prime}-\lambda_{2}|=\alpha_{1}
, which means
\lambda_{2}^{\prime}=m+\alpha_{1}
or
\lambda_{2}^{\prime}=m-\alpha_{1}
. Moreover,

{\left|{\frac{\sin\pi\alpha_{1}}{\pi(n+\alpha_{1})}}\right|}={\left|{\frac{\sin\pi(n+\alpha_{1})}{\pi(n+\alpha_{1})}}\right|}={\left|{\frac{\sin\pi\lambda_{2}^{\prime}}{\pi\lambda_{2}^{\prime}}}\right|}={\left|{\frac{\sin\pi\alpha_{1}}{\pi\lambda_{2}^{\prime}}}\right|},

so
\lambda_{2}^{\prime}=n+\alpha_{1}
or
\lambda_{2}^{\prime}=-n-\alpha_{1}
, which means
\lambda_{2}=n
or
\lambda_{2}=-n
. Hence,
|\lambda_{2}|=|n|
.

If
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}
, then for any
(n,\lambda_{2})\in\Lambda
, we have

(8)

\frac{\sin\pi(n-\alpha_{1})}{\pi(n-\alpha_{1})}=\frac{\sin\pi(\lambda_{2}-\alpha_{1})}{\pi(\lambda_{2}-\alpha_{1})}\ \ \text{or}\ \ \frac{\sin\pi(n-\alpha_{1})}{\pi(n-\alpha_{1})}=-\frac{\sin\pi(\lambda_{2}-\alpha_{1})}{\pi(\lambda_{2}-\alpha_{1})}.

Combining (
8
) with
|\lambda_{2}|=|n|
, we have
\lambda_{2}=n
.
These imply that
\{(n,n):n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}\cup\{(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{1})\}\subset\Lambda
. So for any
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, we have
(n,n),(n+\alpha_{1},\beta)\in\Lambda
for some
\beta\in{\mathbb{R}}
. From Proposition
2
, we have
\beta=n+\alpha_{1}
or
\beta=n-\alpha_{1}
. Suppose that
\beta=n-\alpha_{1}
, we have

{\left|{\frac{\sin n\pi}{n\pi}}\right|}={\left|{\frac{\sin(n-2\alpha_{1})\pi}{(n-2\alpha_{1})\pi}}\right|},

which contradicts
\alpha_{1}\notin\frac{{\mathbb{Z}}}{2}
. Therefore,
\Lambda=\{(n,n),(n+\alpha,n+\alpha):n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
, where
\alpha=\alpha_{1}
.

If
\alpha_{1}\neq\alpha_{2}
, similar to
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}
, we can obtain
\Lambda=\{(n,-n),(n+\alpha,-(n+\alpha)):n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
.

Combining the above, we have completed the proof.

(I). If

For the reader’s convenience, we first restate Theorem

here.

Theorem
4
.

Suppose
\mu_{1}
is a measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
L\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{k}\times{\left\{{0}\right\}}^{d-k}
is a spectrum of
\mu_{1}
. Suppose also that
\nu_{1}
is a measure on
{\left\{{0}\right\}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d-k}
and
M\subseteq{\left\{{0}\right\}}^{k}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d-k}
is a spectrum of
\nu_{1}
.
Then
L+M
is a spectrum of
\mu_{1}*\nu_{1}
.

Proof.

Notice that the sums in
L+M
are all distinct by our assumptions and that we may assume that
\mu_{1},\nu_{1}
are probability measures. We will verify that

\sum_{\ell\in L,m\in M}{\left|{\widehat{\mu_{1}*\nu_{1}}}\right|}^{2}(x-\ell-m)=1

for almost all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

We have, writing
x=(x_{1},x_{2})
with
x_{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k},x_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d-k}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\in L,m\in M}{\left|{\widehat{\mu_{1}*\nu_{1}}}\right|}^{2}(x-\ell-m)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\in L,m\in M}{\left|{\widehat{\mu_{1}}}\right|}^{2}(x-\ell-m){\left|{\widehat{\nu_{1}}}\right|}^{2}(x-\ell-m)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\in L,m\in M}{\left|{\widehat{\mu_{1}}}\right|}^{2}(x_{1}-\ell,x_{2}-m)\,{\left|{\widehat{\nu_{1}}}\right|}^{2}(x_{1}-\ell,x_{2}-m)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\in L,m\in M}{\left|{\widehat{\mu_{1}}}\right|}^{2}(x_{1}-\ell,x_{2}-m)\,{\left|{\widehat{\nu_{1}}}\right|}^{2}(0,x_{2}-m)

(since
\widehat{\nu_{1}}
does not depend on the first
k
coordinates)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in M}{\left|{\widehat{\nu_{1}}}\right|}^{2}(0,x_{2}-m)\sum_{\ell\in L}{\left|{\widehat{\mu_{1}}}\right|}^{2}(x_{1}-\ell,x_{2}-m)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in M}{\left|{\widehat{\nu_{1}}}\right|}^{2}(0,x_{2}-m)\cdot 1

\displaystyle=1.

∎

The following Proposition
4
can be found in the paper
[
13
, §4]
by the first and third author. We include it here only to record a clean formulation.

Proposition 4
.

Let
\mu
be the arc-length measure supported on the union of finitely many line segments

I_{1},\dots,I_{N}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Let
L
be a straight line through the origin and let
\pi_{L}
be the orthogonal projection onto
L
.
Assume that
\mu
is a spectral measure and that it has a spectrum
\Lambda\subset L
.
Then
\pi_{L}
is injective
\mu
-almost everywhere on
{\rm supp\,}\mu
.

Proof.

Assume that
\pi_{L}
is not injective
\mu
-almost everywhere on
{\rm supp\,}\mu
,
then there exist two disjoint measurable subsets

E,F\subset{\rm supp\,}\mu
such that

\pi_{L}(E)=\pi_{L}(F)=J
. We have

\pi_{L}\mu|_{E}=c_{j}\,H^{1}|_{J}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\pi_{L}\mu|_{F}=c_{k}\,H^{1}|_{J}

for some constants
c_{j},c_{k}>0
.
Define

f=c_{k}\,\mathbf{1}_{E}-c_{j}\,\mathbf{1}_{F}.

Then
f\neq 0
in
L^{2}(\mu)
.
For any
\lambda\in\Lambda\subset L
, the exponential

e_{\lambda}(x)=e^{2\pi i\langle\lambda,x\rangle}

depends only on
\pi_{L}x
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\langle f,e_{\lambda}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mu)}

\displaystyle=c_{k}\int_{E}e^{-2\pi i\langle\lambda,x\rangle}\,d\mu(x)-c_{j}\int_{F}e^{-2\pi i\langle\lambda,x\rangle}\,d\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{J}e^{-2\pi i\langle\lambda,t\rangle}\,d(c_{k}\,\pi_{L}\mu|_{E}-c_{j}\,\pi_{L}\mu|_{F})(t)

\displaystyle=0.

Thus
f
is orthogonal to
\{e_{\lambda}:\lambda\in\Lambda\}
, contradicting the completeness
of
\Lambda
in
L^{2}(\mu)
. Hence
\pi_{L}
must be injective
\mu
-almost everywhere on
{\rm supp\,}\mu
.

4.
Growth bound for orthogonal sets in balls

4.1.
Any finite collection of curves

We will use Lev’s a result
[
16
, Lemma 3.1.]
to treat the case of arc-length Lebesgue measures supported on curves.

Lev’s condition
[
16
, Lemma 3.1.]
: Let
\mu
be a positive, finite measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Given a real number
\alpha
with
0\leqslant\alpha\leqslant d
, Lev considered the following condition

(9)

\liminf_{R\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{R^{d-\alpha}}\int_{|t|<R}|\widehat{\mu}(t)|^{2}dt>0

and proved the following theorem (for Bessel systems, not just orthogonal sets).

Theorem 12
.

[
16
, Lemma 3.1.]

Suppose that
\Lambda
is an orthogonal set for
\mu
, where
\mu
is a measure satisfying (
9
) for some
\alpha
with
0\leqslant\alpha\leqslant d
. Then

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\#(\Lambda\cap B(x,R))\leqslant CR^{\alpha}\quad(R\geqslant 1)

for a certain constant
C
which does not depend on
R
.

Now, we can prove Theorem
6
by using above the result.

Proof of Theorem
6
.

According to Theorem
12
, our goal is to prove that there exists a constant
C>0
and
R_{0}\geq 1
such that for all
R\geq R_{0}\geq 1

\int_{|t|<R}|\widehat{\rho}(t)|^{2}dt\geq CR^{d-1}.

Let us write
B_{R}:=B_{R}(0)
, and define

\displaystyle\psi_{R}(t)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{|B_{R/2}|}\,\big(\mathbf{1}_{B_{R/2}}*\mathbf{1}_{B_{R/2}}\big)(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|B_{R/2}(0)|}\int_{R^{d}}\mathbf{1}_{B_{R/2}(0)}(t-s)\,\mathbf{1}_{B_{R/2}(0)}(s)\,ds

\displaystyle=\frac{|B_{R/2}\cap(t+B_{R/2})|}{|B_{R/2}|}.

For every
R>0
, we have the following three basic properties for
\psi_{R}
.

(1)

0\leq\psi_{R}\leq 1
and
{\rm supp\,}\psi_{R}\subset B_{R}
;

(2)

For all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we have

\hat{\psi}_{R}(x)=\frac{1}{|B_{R/2}|}\,\Big|\hat{\mathbf{1}}_{B_{R/2}}(x)\Big|^{2}=\frac{1}{|B_{R/2}|}\cdot\left(\frac{R}{2}\right)^{2d}\cdot\,\Big|\hat{\mathbf{1}}_{B_{1}}\left(\frac{R}{2}x\right)\Big|^{2}\geq 0;

(3)

Since
\hat{\mathbf{1}}_{B_{1}}
is continuous and
\hat{\mathbf{1}}_{B_{1}}(0)=|B_{1}|
, there exist constants
\varepsilon_{0}\in(0,1)
and
\omega_{d}>0
(depending only on
d
) such that
\hat{\psi}_{R}(x)\ \geq\omega_{d}R^{d}
for all
|x|\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{R}
.

Hence, we have

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\psi_{R}(t)\,|\hat{\rho}(t)|^{2}\,dt\;

\displaystyle=\iint\bigg(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\psi_{R}(t)\,e^{-2\pi i\,t\cdot(x-y)}\,dt\bigg)\,d\rho(x)\,d\rho(y)

\displaystyle=\iint\hat{\psi}_{R}(x-y)\,d\rho(x)\,d\rho(y)

\displaystyle\geq\;\iint_{|x-y|\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{R}}\hat{\psi}_{R}(x-y)\,d\rho(x)\,d\rho(y)

\displaystyle\geq\omega_{d}R^{d}\iint_{|x-y|\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{R}}d\rho(x)\,d\rho(y).

Moreover, since
{\rm supp\,}\psi_{R}\subset B_{R}
,

\int_{R^{d}}\psi_{R}(t)\,|\hat{\rho}(t)|^{2}\,dt=\;\int_{|t|<R}\psi_{R}(t)\,|\hat{\rho}(t)|^{2}\,dt\leq\;\int_{|t|<R}|\hat{\rho}(t)|^{2}\,dt.

For any curve
\Gamma_{0}
in
\Gamma
the restriction
\rho|_{\Gamma_{0}}
is the arc-length measure on
\Gamma_{0}
.
Write
\gamma_{0}:[0,L_{0}]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
for the arc-length parametrization of
\Gamma_{0}
with
0<L_{0}<\infty
.

We have

\displaystyle\int_{|t|<R}|\hat{\rho}(t)|^{2}\,dt

\displaystyle\geq\omega_{d}R^{d}\iint_{|x-y|\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{R}}d\rho(x)\,d\rho(y)

\displaystyle\geq\omega_{d}R^{d}\,\iint\mathbf{1}_{\{|x-y|\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{R}\}}\,\mathbf{1}_{\{x\in\gamma_{k_{0}},\,y\in\gamma_{k_{0}}\}}\,d\rho(x)\,d\rho(y)

\displaystyle=\omega_{d}R^{d}\,\int_{\gamma_{k_{0}}}\int_{\gamma_{k_{0}}}\mathbf{1}_{\{|x-y|\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{R}\}}\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle=\omega_{d}R^{d}\,\int_{0}^{L_{k_{0}}}\int_{0}^{L_{k_{0}}}\mathbf{1}_{\{|\gamma_{k_{0}}(s)-\gamma_{k_{0}}(t)|\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{R}\}}\,ds\,dt.

Since
\gamma_{k_{0}}
is arc-length parametrizations of curve segments,
we have
|\gamma^{\prime}_{k_{0}}(s)|\equiv 1
and
|\gamma_{k_{0}}(s)-\gamma_{k_{0}}(t)|\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{R}
when
|s-t|\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{R}
. By simple calculation, we can get

\int_{0}^{L_{k_{0}}}\int_{0}^{L_{k_{0}}}\mathbf{1}_{\{|\gamma_{k_{0}}(s)-\gamma_{k_{0}}(t)|\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{R}\}}\,ds\,dt\geq\;\iint_{\begin{subarray}{c}s,t\in[0,L_{k_{0}}]\\
|s-t|\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{R}\end{subarray}}ds\,dt=\begin{cases}L_{k_{0}}^{2},&\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{R}\geq L_{k_{0}},\\[6.0pt]
\frac{2\varepsilon_{0}L_{k_{0}}}{R}-(\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{R})^{2},&\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{R}<L_{k_{0}}.\end{cases}

When
R>\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{L_{k_{0}}}
, we can obtain

\int_{|t|<R}|\widehat{\rho}(t)|^{2}\,dt\geq\omega_{d}\varepsilon_{0}L_{k_{0}}R^{d-1}.

Taking
R_{0}=\max\{1,\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{L_{k_{0}}}\}
and
C=\omega_{d}\varepsilon_{0}L_{k_{0}}
, we have
\int_{|t|<R}|\widehat{\rho}(t)|^{2}dt\geq CR^{d-1}
for all
R\geq R_{0}
. This completes the proof.

∎

\mathbb{R}
by

\mathcal{D}_{n}^{(1)}:=\left\{\left[\frac{k}{2^{n}},\frac{k+1}{2^{n}}\right):k\in\mathbb{Z}\right\},\ \ n\in{\mathbb{Z}}.

The
n
-th dyadic partition of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is then defined by

\mathcal{D}_{n}^{(d)}:=\left\{I_{1}\times I_{2}\times\dots\times I_{d}:I_{j}\in\mathcal{D}_{n}^{(1)}\right\}.

If there is no confusion, we usually omit the superscript and write
\mathcal{D}_{n}
for the
n
-th dyadic partition of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, where
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
stands for the space of probability measures on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. For a Borel set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we denote by

\mu_{K}(\cdot):=\mu(\cdot\cap K),

the measure
\mu
restricted on
K
. Moreover, if
K
is a dyadic cube in
[0,1]^{d}
with
\mu(K)>0
, we denote by

\mu_{K}^{\Box}(\cdot):=\frac{1}{\mu(K)}(S_{K})_{*}\mu_{K}(\cdot),

where
S_{K}
is the affine bijective map from
K
to
[0,1]^{d}
and
(S_{K})_{*}\mu_{K}(\cdot)
is the pushforward of
\mu_{K}
, i.e. the measure
\mu_{K}(S_{K}^{-1}(\cdot))
. Obviously, we have
\mu_{K}^{\Box}\in\mathcal{P}([0,1]^{d})
.

Lemma 10

(
[
18
]
, Lemma 4.2)
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then for any
0<\epsilon<1
, there exists
\delta=\delta(\epsilon,\mu)>0
such that for any
|\xi|<\delta
and for any
D\in\mathcal{D}_{n}
with
\mu(D)>0
, we have

\left|\widehat{\mu_{D}^{\Box}}(\xi)\right|>\epsilon.

For
\mu\in\mathcal{P}([0,1]^{d})
and
t,\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we write the inner products

\left\langle t,\lambda\right\rangle_{\mu}=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}e^{2\pi i(t-\lambda)\cdot x}d\mu(x).

We will only use the upper (Bessel-type) estimate from
[
18
, Lemma 4.1]
. While
[
18
, Lemma 4.1]
is proved in the frame spectral setting and yields two-sided bounds, our proof only requires the upper estimate (the Bessel side). We therefore state the result below in the form needed here, assuming only that

\Lambda
is an orthogonal set for
\mu
.

Lemma 11

(
[
18
]
, Lemma 4.1)
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and
\Lambda\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be an orthogonal set for
\mu
, i.e.

(10)

\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\left|\left\langle t,\lambda\right\rangle_{\mu}\right|^{2}\leq\mu(D).

Let
n>0
. Then for any
D\in\mathcal{D}_{n}
with
\mu(D)>0
, and for any
t\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we have

\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\left|\left\langle\frac{1}{2^{n}}t,\frac{1}{2^{n}}\lambda\right\rangle_{\mu_{D}^{\Box}}\right|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\mu(D)}.

The following theorem follows the same proof as
[
18
, Theorem 1.2]
. It also works for frame spectral measures, but we state it here for any measures in
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Theorem 13
.

For any measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and any orthogonal set
\Lambda
for
\mu
, there exist constants
C
and
\varrho
such that
t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
h>0
, we have

(11)

\#(\Lambda\cap B(t,h))\leq C2^{\sum_{D\in\mathcal{D}_{n}}-\mu(D)\log\mu(D)},

where
2^{n-1+\varrho}<h\leq 2^{n+\varrho}
.

Proof.

Notice that

(12)

2^{\sum_{D\in\mathcal{D}_{n}}-\mu(D)\log\mu(D)}=\prod_{D\in\mathcal{D}_{n},\mu(D)>0}\mu(D)^{-\mu(D)}.

Let
h>0
. Then there exists a positive integer
n_{h}
such that
2^{n_{h}-1}<h\leq 2^{n_{h}}
. Let
\epsilon>0
and let
\delta=\delta(\epsilon,\mu)
, which is defined in Lemma
10
. Let
\varrho
be the minimal integer such that
2^{-\varrho}<\delta
. For any
t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and any
D\in\mathcal{D}_{n_{h}+\varrho}
, noticing that

\left|\frac{1}{2^{n_{h}+\varrho}}t-\frac{1}{2^{n_{h}+\varrho}}\lambda\right|<\delta,\text{ for any }\lambda\in\Lambda\cap B(t,h),

by Lemma
10
, we have

(13)

\begin{split}\epsilon^{2}\cdot\#(\Lambda\cap B(t,h))&\leq\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda\cap B(t,h)}\left|\left\langle\frac{1}{2^{n_{h}+\varrho}}t,\frac{1}{2^{n_{h}+\varrho}}\lambda\right\rangle_{\mu_{D}^{\Box}}\right|^{2}\\
&\leq\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\left|\left\langle\frac{1}{2^{n_{h}+\varrho}}t,\frac{1}{2^{n_{h}+\varrho}}\lambda\right\rangle_{\mu_{D}^{\Box}}\right|^{2}\\
&\leq\frac{1}{\mu({D})}.\end{split}

The last inequality is due to Lemma
11
. Since
\sum_{D\in\mathcal{D}_{n_{h}+\varrho}}\mu(D)=1
and (
13
) holds for all
D\in\mathcal{D}_{n_{h}+\varrho}
with
\mu(D)>0
, we have

(14)

\begin{split}\#(\Lambda\cap B(t,h))&=\prod_{D\in\mathcal{D}_{n_{h}+\varrho},\mu(D)>0}(\#(\Lambda\cap B(t,h)))^{\mu(D)}\\
&\leq\epsilon^{-2}\prod_{D\in\mathcal{D}_{n_{h}+\varrho},\mu(D)>0}\mu(D)^{-\mu(D)}\\
&=\epsilon^{-2}2^{\sum_{D\in\mathcal{D}_{n_{h}+\varrho}}-\mu(D)\log\mu(D)}.\end{split}

The proof is complete.

∎

The measure
\mu
is called
Ahlfors–David regular
(or simply
Ahlfors regular
)
of dimension
s\geq 0
if there exist constants
c,C>0
such that for every ball

B(x,r)
with center
x\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)
and radius

0<r<\operatorname{diam}(\operatorname{supp}(\mu))
, we have

c\,r^{s}\leq\mu\bigl(B(x,r)\bigr)\leq C\,r^{s}.

By applying Theorem
13
, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 14
.

For any orthogonal set
\Lambda
of Ahlfors–David regular measures of dimension
s
, we have the estimate

\#(\Lambda\cap B(t,h))=O(h^{s}),\text{ for }t\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\text{ and }h>0.

In particular, we have the following estimates

(1)

\#(\Lambda\cap B(t,h))=O(h)
for any orthogonal set
\Lambda
of any finite union of
C^{1}
curves (for example, line segments).

(2)

\#(\Lambda\cap B(t,h))=O(h^{n})
for any orthogonal set
\Lambda
of any finite union of
n
-dimensional polyhedrons.

Proof.

We prove it for an Ahlfors–David regular measure
\mu
of dimension
s\geq 0
. For any such measure there exist constants
c,C>0
such that for every
n
-th dyadic set

D\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with center in
{\rm supp\,}\mu
, we have

c\,2^{-ns}\leq\mu\bigl(D\bigr)\leq C\,2^{-ns}.

By
\sum_{D\in\mathcal{D}_{n}}\mu(D)=1
, we have

2^{\sum_{D\in\mathcal{D}_{n}}-\mu(D)\log\mu(D)}\leq 2^{\sum_{D\in\mathcal{D}_{n}}-\mu(D)\log c2^{-ns}}=c2^{ns}\leq\hat{C}h^{s},

for some constant
\hat{C}
. Then by Theorem
13
, we have

\#(\Lambda\cap B(t,h))=O(h^{s})
.

∎
