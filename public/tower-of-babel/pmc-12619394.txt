Identification of ferroptosis- and mitochondrial metabolism-related biomarkers and the potential molecular mechanisms of poor ovarian response
Authors: Yunying Cai, Na Lin, Yijie Yin, Mei Tian, Ze Wu, Heng Su
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFerroptosis and mitochondrial metabolism are closely associated with the pathological processes of various diseases. However, the role of ferroptosis-related genes (FRGs) and mitochondrial metabolism-related genes (MMRGs) in poor ovarian response (POR) remains unexplored.MethodsFirst, transcriptome sequencing was conducted on ovarian granulosa cell samples from POR patients and controls. Candidate genes were screened through differential expression analysis and consensus clustering analysis. The biomarkers were subsequently screened using machine learning algorithms and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. Nomogram construction and evaluation, enrichment analysis, drug prediction analysis, and molecular docking were subsequently carried out using the biomarkers. Finally, the expression of the biomarkers was authenticated using reverse transcription‒quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT‒qPCR).ResultsPLA2G4B and PRKCG were identified as biomarkers by screening. The nomogram demonstrated that these two biomarkers could effectively predict the occurrence of POR. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed that PLA2G4B and PRKCG were both associated with terpenoid backbone biosynthesis. A TF-miRNA-mRNA network was constructed using the biomarkers. For PLA2G4B, dirithromycin had the highest score among the targeted drugs, whereas for PRKCG, bryostatins and enzastaurin had the highest scores. Molecular docking results indicated that their binding energies were less than − 5 kcal/mol. RT‒qPCR revealed that PLA2G4B and PRKCG were significantly upregulated in POR samples, which was consistent with the high-throughput sequencing results.ConclusionPLA2G4B and PRKCG have been identified as potential mitochondrial- and ferroptosis-related biomarkers in POR, providing valuable insights for exploring the pathogenesis of POR. These findings may also aid in the development of new diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for POR.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13048-025-01855-4.

Introduction

Poor ovarian response (POR) is one of the most significant challenges in modern assisted reproductive technology; approximately 15–20% of women undergoing in vitro fertilization experience POR [1, 2]. This condition is characterized by an inadequate ovarian response to gonadotropin (GN) stimulation [3, 4]. The etiology of POR remains poorly understood. Given the similarities between POR and premature ovarian insufficiency (POI), POR may share a common pathogenesis with POI [5]. Currently, known causes of POI include genetic mutations, immune or metabolic disorders, iatrogenic interventions, and exposure to environmental toxins. However, approximately 90% of women diagnosed with POI are classified as having idiopathic POI, and the pathogenic relationship between POR and POI remains unclear. Few practical methods are available to prevent and treat POR [3, 4]. To address this issue, more in-depth research is urgently needed to explore the underlying mechanisms of POR and develop targeted preventive and treatment strategies.

Granulosa cells (GCs) closely surround oocytes and establish bidirectional interactions via paracrine signals and gap junctions [6]. These interactions are crucial for normal oocyte development. However, in the context of POR, it contributes to the formation of an unfavorable microenvironment for oocyte growth and maturation [7, 8]. Therefore, investigating the dysfunction of GCs and revealing the underlying mechanisms can be of great scientific and clinical importance for increasing oocyte quality in patients with POR.

Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent, lipid peroxidation-induced programmed type of cell death closely associated with the onset and progression of various diseases [9]. Recent studies have indicated that ferroptosis may be closely associated with the ovarian reserve, potentially affecting ovarian function through mechanisms such as oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation [10, 11]. For example, Chen et al. [12] established an in vitro cellular model using H2O2 and reported that H2O2 can induce oxidative stress damage and ferroptosis in human ovarian granulosa cells. Moreover, activating the Nrf2/HO-1 signaling pathway increased granulosa cell viability and reduced oxidative stress, effectively inhibiting ferroptosis in these cells. Cisplatin can promote ovarian granulosa cell ferroptosis in rats by regulating the expression of the ferroptosis-related molecules ACSl4, ALOX15, SLC7A11, and GPX4. This process leads to follicular development abnormalities and ovarian tissue fibrosis, ultimately resulting in ovarian dysfunction in rats [11]. However, the specific molecular mechanisms of ferroptosis in POI remain to be further investigated.

Mitochondria primarily generate ATP through oxidative phosphorylation and play crucial roles in calcium homeostasis and programmed cell death [13]. Previous studies have established a strong correlation between ovarian aging and mitochondrial dysfunction [14, 15]. Research indicates that elevated levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS), decreased oxidative phosphorylation activity, and ATP production in oocytes are associated with POI [16, 17]. Furthermore, inhibiting the excessive activation of mitophagy can help prevent mitochondrial damage, ultimately improving ovarian function [18]. Additionally, numerous POI-associated genes expressed in the mitochondria, such as MRPS22, POLG, TWNK, LARS2, HARS2, and AARS2, are involved in mitochondrial DNA replication, gene expression, and protein synthesis and degradation [13]. Therefore, properly functioning mitochondria prevent ovarian dysfunction and infertility [19].

Ferroptosis is closely linked to mitochondrial function, and studies have indicated that mitochondrial dysfunction and damage can promote oxidative stress, leading to the induction of ferroptosis [20]. Additionally, the regulation of ferroptosis and mitochondrial dynamics is synergistic; an imbalance can disrupt intracellular homeostasis [20, 21]. The role of ferroptosis with mitochondria within the ovaries remains unclear. However, existing in vitro studies have shown that iron overload and disrupted ROS levels are associated with the upregulation of HO-1 and can induce ferroptosis through mitochondrial dysfunction in CTX-induced GCs [22]. Additionally, inhibition of the ferroptosis regulator FSP1 results in elevated intracellular Fe2 + levels and mitochondrial dysfunction in oocytes [23].Therefore, research focusing on ferroptosis and mitochondrial metabolism biomarkers could provide new therapeutic targets for POR, potentially improving pregnancy outcomes for affected patients.

In this study, RNA sequencing data from POR patient granulosa cells is used to explore the expression of ferroptosis- and mitochondria-related genes in these cells. Our study provides novel insights into POR pathogenesis and treatment strategies.

Materials and methods

Sample preparation and data collection

A total of 20 ovarian granulosa cell samples (10 from patients with POR and 10 from healthy controls) were collected at the First People’s Hospital of Yunnan Province. The samples were collected from fertile women without polycystic ovary syndrome [PCOS] or ovulatory disorders and women who were diagnosed with POR according to the POSEIDON criteria, (women aged < 35 years with < 4 mature follicles [diameter < 15 mm] on the day of human chorionic gonadotropin [hCG] administration). The two populations were not different in terms of age, body mass index, or infertility type. The patients’ clinical information can be found in Supplementary Table 1. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First People’s Hospital of Yunnan Province (Approval No. KYLL2024-KY039; Clinical trial number: not applicable). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before sample collection. From the FerrDb database (http://www.zhounan.org/ferrdb/), 910 ferroptosis-related genes (FRGs) were retrieved (Supplementary Table 2), and 1,234 mitochondrial metabolism-related genes (MMRGs) were retrieved from the literature [24] (Supplementary Table 3).

RNA sequencing and data preprocessing

Total RNA was extracted from 20 samples with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, CA, USA). Next, the quantity and integrity of the total RNA were assessed utilizing a NanoDrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer (NanoDrop, Wilmington, DE, USA) and a Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent, CA, USA), respectively. Then, 1 µg of total RNA was processed to separate poly(A) RNA utilizing Dynabeads Oligo (dT)25–61005 (Thermo Fisher, CA, USA) via 2 rounds of purification. The poly(A) RNA was subsequently fragmented into small pieces with the Magnesium RNA Fragmentation Module (NEB, cat. e6150, USA) at 94 °C for 5–7 min. The cleaved RNA fragments were then reverse transcribed to generate cDNA using SuperScript™ II Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen, cat. 1896649, USA). Sequencing was executed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform utilizing the paired-end 150 bp (PE150) sequencing mode. After sequencing, low-quality reads were eliminated utilizing Fastp software. Afterward, all the clean reads were mapped to the reference genome (Homo sapiens, GRCh38) utilizing the HISAT2 software. Finally, the FPKM value of each gene was calculated via StringTie software.

Recognition of candidate genes

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified utilizing the DESeq2 (v 1.42.0) package [25] and named POR-DEGs. The screening criteria were |log2fold-change (FC)| > 1.5 and P < 0.05. Moreover, the ggplot2 (v 3.5.1) package [26] and the ComplexHeatmap (v 2.18.0) package [27] were employed to generate volcano plots and heatmaps of the DEGs, respectively, with only the top 10 up- and downregulated genes (ranked in descending order by the absolute value of log2FC) being displayed. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis, including biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF) analyses, and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis, was performed on the DEGs using the clusterProfiler package (v 4.10.1) [28] (p value < 0.05). The consistent clustering method was subsequently utilized to obtain DEGs related to ferroptosis and mitochondrial metabolism (F-MM-DEGs). First, the ggvenn (v 0.1.10) package [29] was utilized to compare the of FRGs and MMRGs. Then, relying on the expression data of POR samples, the ConsensusClusterPlus (v 1.66.0) package [30] was used to perform consistent clustering on POR samples and to calculate the optimal number of clusters K. Using consensus clustering, the sample subtypes most relevant to directional genes can be identified, and the differential genes between these subtypes can be calculated as directional genes. For the POR samples divided into different subtypes, the same threshold was used to screen F-MM-DEGs. Finally, the ggvenn (v 0.1.10) package was utilized to compare the POR-DEGs and F-MM-DEGs to identify candidate genes.

Functional enrichment analysis of candidate genes

GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were carried out on the candidate genes with the clusterProfiler (v 4.10.1) package [31] (P < 0.05). The top 10 enrichment results were visualized utilizing the enrichplot (v 1.22.0) package [32]. Moreover, a protein‒protein interaction (PPI) network (interaction score > 0.15) was assembled utilizing the STRING database (https://www.string-db.org) and visualized utilizing Cytoscape (v 3.9.1) software [33].

Screening for biomarkers

The “glmnet” package (v 4.1–8.1) [34] was first used to perform least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) on all the candidate genes. LASSO applies the L1 regularization penalty, shrinking some regression coefficients to zero, thereby automatically selecting important features. It is especially suitable for handling high-dimensional data and effectively addresses noise and redundant features [35]. The gene expression levels were fitted using the glmnet function, with the family set to a Gaussian distribution and the best lambda value automatically selected. Fivefold cross-validation was conducted using the cv.glmnet function to choose the optimal regularization parameter lambda, and performance curves for each lambda value were plotted during the cross-validation process. The cv.glmnet function automatically performs stratified random sampling, ensuring that the ratio of positive and negative samples in each fold is consistent with the original data to avoid excessive missing or overrepresented samples due to random grouping. Additionally, setting seed(5) ensured that the fold partitioning of cv. glmnet remained consistent during each run of the code, indicating that while the sampling process was random, it was controlled. The best lambda value was selected by observing the point where the curve reached its lowest value, resulting in the LASSO feature genes. Moreover, the e1071 (v 1.7–14) package [36] was used to perform support vector machine recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE) analysis on the candidate genes, establishing the SVM model and evaluating the model’s lowest error rate, which led to the identification of SVM-RFE feature genes. SVM-RFE allowed for the accurate implementation of variable selection and the interpretation of the direction and strength of associations between predictors and outcomes using the proposed methods [37]. The final feature genes were obtained through the intersection of the LASSO and SVM-RFE feature genes utilizing the ggvenn (v 1.7.3) package. Moreover, the expression of these feature genes was analyzed, and the ROC curve of each feature gene was plotted using the pROC (v 1.18.5) package [38] (area under the curve (AUC) > 0.7).

Establishment and evaluation of a nomogram

To further analyze the reliability of biomarkers in predicting POR, a nomogram was constructed using biomarkers along with clinical features such as age, follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) levels, and anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) levels through the rms (v 6.8-1.8.8) package [39]. The calibration curve was subsequently plotted using the regplot (v 1.1) package [40], the decision curve analysis (DCA) was plotted using the ggDCA (v 1.2) package [41], and the ROC curve (AUC > 0.7) was plotted using the pROC (v 1.18.5) package.

Enrichment analysis of biomarkers

To further clarify the biological functions and potential signaling pathways associated with the biomarkers, GSEA was conducted. As the background set, c2.cp.v2023.2.Hs.symbols.gmt was retrieved from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/). First, Spearman correlations coefficients were calculated among the biomarkers and other genes utilizing the psych (v 2.4.3) package [42]. GSEA for each biomarker was subsequently performed by the cluster profile (v 4.10.1) package, with significance judged by a |normalized enrichment score (NES)| > 1, a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.25, and P < 0.05. Moreover, the top 5 pathways ranked in descending order of P values were displayed utilizing the enrichplot (v 1.22.0) package. In addition, genes associated with biomarker functions and the functions they are involved in were predicted from the GeneMANIA database (http://genemania.org), and a gene‒gene interaction (GGI) network was built.

Construction of a regulatory network

Transcription factors (TFs) that regulate the biomarkers were predicted using the NetworkAnalyst (http://www.networkanalyst.ca) database. Moreover, microRNA (miRNA)-targeting biomarkers were predicted utilizing the multiMiR (v 1.24.0) package [43] on the basis of the DIANA-microT and miRDB databases. The overlapping prediction results were combined with the predicted TFs to construct the TF-miRNA-mRNA network. The network was visualized using Cytoscape software (v 3.9.1).

Drug prediction

To explore potential drugs that target biomarkers, potential drugs or molecular compounds that interact with biomarkers were predicted using the DSigDB database (https://dsigdb.tanlab.org/DSigDBv1.0/) and the DGIdb (https://dgidb.org/).

Molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations

To verify the drugs predicted via bioinformatics, molecular docking was performed between the predicted drugs/small molecules and the target proteins of the biomarkers, and the binding energies were calculated. The 3D structure files (PDB files) of the target proteins of the biomarkers were retrieved and downloaded from the PDB database (https://www.rcsb.org/) and the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/). The SDF files of the predicted drugs/small molecules were retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), ensuring that the names of the predicted drugs were consistent with those in PubChem. The biomarkers and drugs were sorted by interaction score from high to low, and the highest-scoring biomarker and drug were selected for molecular docking. The possible docking positions on the target protein were subsequently calculated using the CB-Dock2 online tool (https://cadd.labshare.cn/cb-dock2/php/index.php), and docking simulations and visualizations were subsequently performed. Drugs and proteins with binding energies lower than − 5 kcal/mol were considered to have stable binding and the potential for docking. Molecular dynamics simulations were performed on the drug with the lowest binding energy from the molecular docking using GROMACS 2024.4 software to gain a deeper understanding of the mechanism of action of the drug, the stability of the complex formed between the drug and the biomarker, and the binding dynamics of the drug. The entire process was carried out following the rules of the AMBER99SB-ILDN force field, and the TIP3P (TIP3-point) water model was used, with the box shape set to a cube. A distance of 1 nm was maintained between the protein and the edge of the box, and ions were added to maintain the electrical neutrality of the system. Energy minimization was performed using the steepest descent method. The system then underwent a thermal bath (NVT, involving the particle number, volume, and temperature) and a pressure bath (NPT, involving the particle number, pressure, and temperature). During the NVT and NPT processes, temperature coupling was performed using the V-rescale method, with the reference temperature set to 300K, a timestep of 2 femtoseconds, and the entire process lasting 100 picoseconds. The molecular dynamics simulation was set to run for 20 ns. The root mean square deviation (RMSD) of the protein‒drug complex, the root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) of the protein, total energy, and number of hydrogen bonds were calculated.

Reverse transcription‒quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT‒qPCR)

Granulosa cell samples were acquired from another ten POR patients and ten controls from the First People’s Hospital of Yunnan Province. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First People’s Hospital of Yunnan Province (Approval No. KYLL2024-KY039), and informed consent was acquired from all participants. The total RNA extraction and reverse transcription methods for these 20 samples were the same as those utilized in Step 2.2. Supplementary Table 4 contains the sequences of primers used for RT‒qPCR. The RT‒PCR assay was performed with a CFX Connect real-time quantitative fluorescence PCR instrument (Bio-Rad, USA). GAPDH was utilized as the internal reference gene. The RT‒qPCR results were calculated by the 2−ΔΔCT method in Excel and then imported into GraphPad Prism 5 for statistical analysis and visualization (P < 0.05).

Statistical analysis

Bioinformatic analysis was conducted in R (v 4.2.2). P < 0.05 was considered significant. For RT‒qPCR analysis, a t test was used for statistical comparisons.

Identification of candidate genes

Overall, 127 POR-DEGs were screened (Supplementary Tables 5 and 6). The numbers of up- and downregulated genes were 57 and 70, respectively. The top 10 up- and downregulated genes and their expression levels are displayed in the volcano plots and heatmaps, respectively (Figs. 1A, B). GO and KEGG analyses were performed on the 127 POR-DEGs. Among the 119 enriched GO-BP terms, keratinocyte differentiation, epidermal cell differentiation, and skin development were the top enriched BP terms in the POR-DEGs. Among the 28 enriched GO-MF terms, guanyl nucleotide binding, guanyl ribonucleotide binding, and structural constituent of cytoskeleton activity were enriched mainly in the POR-DEGs. The 14 enriched GO-CC terms included blood microparticle, intermediate filament, and intermediate filament cytoskeleton (Supplementary Fig. 1A). KEGG analysis revealed 16 significantly enriched pathways, including pathways related to African trypanosomiasis, viral protein interaction with cytokine and cytokine receptors, malaria, ovarian steroidogenesis, and steroid hormone biosynthesis (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Moreover, a total of 120 overlapping genes were obtained from the FRGs and MMRGs (Fig. 1C). POR samples were divided into 2 subtypes (Fig. 1D), and 154 F-MM-DEGs were obtained (Supplementary Table 7). Molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations were performed on PLA2G4B and PRKCG

Molecular docking revealed that the binding energies between PLA2G4B and dirithromycin, PRKCG and bryostatin, and PRKCG and enzastaurin were − 7.4 kcal/mol, −5.7 kcal/mol, and − 10.1 kcal/mol, respectively. (Figs. 8A-C). The RMSD changes for the PRKCG and bryostatin -related system indicated that at the beginning of the simulation (0 ns), the RMSD value was relatively low. Over time, the RMSD value gradually increased with significant fluctuations, suggesting that the system structure underwent substantial changes and that the degree of deviation from the initial structure continued to increase, with fluctuations stabilizing in the later stages. For the PRKCG- and enzastaurin-related systems, the RMSD values were also initially low. The RMSD values changed relatively smoothly in the early stage, with overall fluctuations being more moderate than the black curve, indicating that the system structure was relatively stable in the initial phase, with smaller fluctuations and gradual increases in the later stages. The RMSD changes for the PLA2G4B- and dirithromycin-related systems revealed that from the start to the end, the RMSD value remained at a low level with almost no noticeable increasing trend, suggesting that the system structure remained relatively stable throughout the simulation period, with minimal deviation from the initial structure (Fig. 8D). The RMSF curve revealed fluctuations in protein amino acid residues, with regions with smaller fluctuations typically being more rigid, whereas regions with larger fluctuations were more flexible, and more rigid regions were likely binding sites (Fig. 8E). Throughout the simulation period, the energy of all three systems remained relatively stable, with no continuous energy accumulation or depletion (Fig. 8F). The variation in the number of hydrogen bonds between the small molecule and the protein indicated that PRKCG_ENZASTAURIN exhibited more significant changes in the number of hydrogen bonds at certain time points (Fig. 8G). Overall, these results emphasized the potential roles of PLA2G4B and PRKCG in POR treatment.

Discussion

POR refers to an insufficient follicular response to ovarian stimulation during in vitro fertilization (IVF) under standard controlled conditions. It significantly reduces the success rates of IVF. The specific pathophysiology of POR remains poorly understood. Both ferroptosis and mitochondrial dysfunction significantly impact the ovarian reserve [10, 15]. In this study, we used integrated bioinformatics and experimental approaches to investigate the previously unrecognized roles of granulosa cell mitochondrial dysfunction and ferroptosis in the development of POR. We identified two novel biomarkers for diagnosing and treating POR.

Our research identified PLA2G4B and PRKCG as MMRGs and FRGS in POR GCs. PLA2G4B is a relatively underresearched member of the cytosolic phospholipase A2 protein family [44]. Previous studies have indicated its involvement in the release of arachidonic acid, ultimately leading to eicosanoid formation [45]. PLA2G4B is also essential for prostaglandin biosynthesis during parturition [46].

The PRKCG gene is located at 19q13.2-q13.4 and encodes protein kinase C gamma (PKCγ), which belongs to the serine/threonine-specific protein kinase family and is involved in various cellular processes and signaling pathways [47]. Previous studies have indicated a link between PRKCG and several neurological and oncological disorders, including osteosarcoma, colon cancer, glioma, breast cancer, ovarian cancer, and spinocerebellar ataxia type 14 [48, 49]. For example, the upregulation of PRKCG has been shown to promote cell proliferation and glycolytic metabolism in breast cancer cells [50]. Furthermore, in models of oxygen‒glucose deprivation, the expression of PKC-γ was associated with hypoxia-induced mitochondrial depolarization and increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels [51]. In our study, qRT‒PCR analyses and RNA sequencing revealed a trend toward increased expression of PRKCG and PLA2G4B compared with that in controls. The nomogram and ROC curve analysis revealed that these two biomarkers could predict the occurrence of POR. Overall, these findings highlight the potential of PRKCG and PLA2G4B as biomarkers for POR.

Additionally, we found that the MAPK pathway, T-cell receptor signaling pathway, and terpenoid backbone biosynthesis pathway were significantly enriched in PRKCG, whereas pathways related to allograft rejection, graft-versus-host disease, and terpenoid backbone biosynthesis exhibited notable enrichment in PLA2G4B. These findings suggest that PRKCG is primarily involved in cellular signaling, whereas PLA2G4B is associated mainly with immune rejection responses. GSEA indicated that PRKCG is predominantly associated with the MAPK signaling pathway. Recent studies have implicated the MAPK signaling pathway in ferroptosis and mitochondrial damage. Specifically, the p38 MAPK pathway regulates the activities of key mitochondrial proteins that play essential roles in mitochondrial biogenesis, oxidative phosphorylation, thermogenesis, and iron homeostasis [52]. Activation of the MAPK pathway triggers ferroptosis by promoting the degradation of FTH1 and the autophagic degradation of ferritin. Conversely, inhibition of the MAPK signaling pathway may downregulate SLC7A11 expression through the action of Est1, subsequently affecting the transport activity of system Xc − and thereby inducing ferroptosis. This mechanism may play a crucial role in the pathogenesis of POR [53, 54]. These findings suggest that PRKCG may play a critical role in the process of ferroptosis associated with ovarian function through the MAPK pathway. Further investigations into PRKCG and the MAPK pathway could aid in the discovery of small-molecule compounds as potential therapeutic agents for POR.

To explore the transcriptional regulatory roles of PRKCG and PLA2G4B, we employed bioinformatics tools to investigate the interactions between TFs, miRNAs, and these genes. Our results revealed a regulatory relationship between PRKCG and several TFs, including PRDM1, HINFP, JUN, FOS, and ARID3A, as well as miRNAs such as miR-324-5p, miR-320a-3p, and miR-320c. For PLA2G4B, we identified eight TFs that may be involved in its regulation, including PPARG, FOXC1, RELA, TP53, TEAD1, RUNX2, and TFAP2A. Ke et al. reported that PRDM1 plays a role in ovarian development because it is a crucial transcriptional regulator necessary for specifying and migrating primordial germ cells [55]. Additionally, other studies have indicated that PRDM1 can promote ferroptosis in thyroid cancer by regulating the deubiquitination of SELENBP1 via USP15 [56]. TP53 is recognized as one of the core regulators of ferroptosis, and its bidirectional control over this process occurs through both transcription-dependent and transcription-independent mechanisms, depending on the context [57]. Despite numerous previous studies, including ours, suggesting potential associations between these TFs, ovarian development, and ferroptosis, further experimental validation is needed to confirm their roles in the occurrence and progression of POR.

Through molecular docking studies, bryostatins have been identified as potential therapeutic compounds that bind to PRKCG. Bryostatins are a class of highly oxygenated macrolides containing eleven chiral centers. The biological activity of these proteins is associated primarily with the modulation of protein kinase C (PKC), which can activate or inhibit PKC and regulate the cell cycle. In addition to their significance as latency-reversing agents in HIV treatment, bryostatins demonstrate robust in vivo antitumor activity in lymphocytic leukemia and B-cell lymphoma, highlighting their immense potential as unconventional anticancer agents [58]. Furthermore, the molecular docking results indicate that the selective inhibitor of protein kinase C-β, enzastaurin, has a strong binding affinity for PRKCG. Previous studies have shown that enzastaurin has significant anticancer effects by inducing G2/M phase cell cycle arrest [59]. Enzastaurin may provide a new therapeutic approach for POR patients by inhibiting PRKCG and its associated signaling pathways, thereby suppressing excessive hyperproliferation or abnormal apoptosis of granulosa cells. However, this hypothesis requires further clinical and experimental validation. Dirithromycin, a member of the macrolide class of antibiotics, was screened as a potential therapeutic compound related to PLA2G4B. Recent studies suggest that macrolide antibiotics may have antiviral effects by influencing cytokine expression [60]. However, the potential therapeutic effects of macrolides for POR require further experimental validation.

Despite the significant findings of this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the relatively small sample size in this study may have led to certain statistical biases in the results, potentially affecting the accuracy of the model. Additionally, given the heterogeneity of POR, the generalizability of the findings may be limited. To overcome this, large-scale, multicenter, and prospective studies are essential in the future. Second, a significant shortcoming is the lack of protein-level validation for the DEGs. Since mRNA expression does not always correlate with protein levels, future research should incorporate western blotting or other proteomic techniques to validate the DEGs at the protein level. Furthermore, future efforts should focus on identifying suitable datasets to validate the expression differences of PLA2G4B and PRKCG in transcriptomic sequencing samples and to compare these findings with RT‒qPCR results to further assess the potential clinical value of PLA2G4B and PRKCG. Finally, the specific regulatory mechanisms of MMRGs and FRGs in POR development remain largely unknown. Integrating molecular, cellular, and animal experiments will be crucial for comprehensively understanding these mechanisms. By addressing these limitations, future research can further validate the roles of the identified key genes and potentially translate these findings into clinical applications.

Conclusions

This is the first comprehensive report to study the differential expression of MMRGs and FRGs in POR GCs. Two core genes with high diagnostic performance for POR were identified and validated. Our findings contribute to a better understanding of the potential role of mitochondria-associated ferroptosis in the pathogenesis of POR and provide a theoretical basis for the development of new pharmacological treatments. This study not only presents potential biomarkers for the early diagnosis of POR but also offers new directions for the clinical translation of related therapeutic strategies.
