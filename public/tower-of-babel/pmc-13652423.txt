Successful conservative management of high-grade renal injury in a polytrauma patient: a rare case report

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Trauma is one of the major causes of death across all age groups and is the leading cause of mortality in individuals younger than 45 years. The abdomen is the most frequently injured area in trauma cases. In blunt abdominal trauma, the most affected organs are the liver and spleen, while renal injuries occur less frequently and are more difficult to ascertain[1].

Renal trauma can cause harm to the renal vasculature or parenchyma, potentially resulting in bleeding or injury to the collecting system, which may lead to urine leakage. In accordance with the American Association for the Surgery of Trauma (AAST), renal injuries are classified into five grades, with Grades IV and V considered high-grade injuries. This classification helps guide both management strategies and prognosis. There is a strong consensus among trauma and urologic surgeons that hemodynamically stable patients with renal trauma generally have better outcomes when managed initially with a nonoperative approach. Such an approach helps preserve kidney function and lowers the risk of unnecessary nephrectomy[2].

We report a rare case of simultaneous Grade IV renal and Grade IV splenic injuries in a hemodynamically stable adolescent, with associated left posterior rib fractures following blunt abdominal trauma.

The coexistence of high-grade injuries to both the kidney and spleen is especially difficult to deal with due to the greater potential for delayed hemorrhage, urine extravasation, and possible hemodynamic instability. While the treatment approach without surgical intervention has become standard practice in isolated cases of injury to solid organs in hemodynamically stable patients, there is insufficient literature regarding conservative treatment for concomitant high-grade injuries of the kidney and spleen.

This work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria[3].

Case presentation

An 18-year-old male, previously healthy, with no significant past medical or surgical history, no regular medications, and no known drug allergies, who was a non-smoker and did not consume alcohol or recreational drugs, presented to the emergency department four hours after falling from a bicycle onto his left flank. Aside from left flank pain and gross hematuria, his review of systems was unremarkable; his initial examination showed a conscious, oriented patient with left-flank tenderness and no focal neurological deficits.

On admission, he was hemodynamically stable but reported significant left flank pain and exhibited gross hematuria. Notably, baseline laboratory investigations revealed a serum creatinine level of 0.8 mg/dL and a normal estimated glomerular filtration rate, indicating that global renal function was well-preserved despite the severe anatomical injury. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) revealed high-grade left renal trauma with collecting system involvement (Grade IV) as shown in Figure 1A and B), a simultaneous high-grade splenic laceration (Grade IV) as shown in Figure 1C, nondisplaced fractures of the left posterior 10th and 11th ribs. The patient was admitted to the ICU and initially managed conservatively with bed rest, multimodal analgesia, chest physiotherapy, and close monitoring of vital signs, urine output, and hemodynamic status. On admission, his hemoglobin was 11 g/dL, which dropped to 8 g/dL during the first 48 hours; consequently, he was managed with a transfusion of two units of packed red blood cells to stabilize his hematologic profile. Furthermore, to rule out occult pancreatic complications, serum amylase and lipase levels were monitored and remained within normal physiological limits. On hospital day four, diagnostic laparoscopy excluded ongoing intraperitoneal hemorrhage, then a left double-J ureteral stent was placed for urinary diversion, and an intraperitoneal drain was placed. Follow-up measures included stent repositioning for migration and temporary pleural pigtail catheter insertion for an associated effusion; both were removed without complication.

The patient showed steady clinical improvement with gradual mobilization and resumption of oral intake, and was discharged in stable condition. At outpatient follow-up, he remained afebrile and asymptomatic, with clear urine and unremarkable physical examination findings. Subsequent follow-up imaging at 7 weeks confirmed the resolution of the pancreatic finding with no delayed complications such as pseudocyst formation. Serial blood pressure measurements were consistently normal. Drain output progressively decreased until the drain was removed, and follow-up imaging demonstrated resolution of perinephric changes, an expected nonenhancing infarction of the lower renal pole, and interval improvement in splenic findings, as shown in Figure 2. Ultimately, laboratory tests at 10 weeks post-injury confirmed stable renal function with a creatinine level of 0.82 mg/dL at the time of elective DJ stent removal. Discussion

High-grade injuries to solid abdominal organs, particularly AAST Grade IV–V renal trauma and severe splenic injury, remain challenging to manage. Over recent years, there has been a decisive shift toward conservative, non-operative management (NOM) for high-grade renal trauma in hemodynamically stable patients, accompanied by lower nephrectomy rates and improved organ preservation compared with routine operative approaches[4]. AAST guidelines emphasize that physiological stability is the primary determinant for NOM, as anatomical injury grade alone does not always necessitate surgical intervention[5,6]. A similar evolution has occurred for splenic trauma, where NOM, often complemented by splenic artery embolization (SAE), is employed for selected Grade III–V injuries[7].

In our case, the patient sustained a direct left flank injury in a bicycle accident, resulting in a shattered left kidney with active bleeding, collecting-system involvement, and a concomitant Grade IV splenic laceration, together with nondisplaced fractures of the left posterior 10th and 11th ribs. While isolated high-grade injury to either organ is well described, the simultaneous occurrence of Grade IV renal and Grade IV splenic trauma raises specific concerns regarding occult hemorrhage, delayed decompensation, and thresholds for operative or endovascular intervention. International studies on solid-organ injuries suggest that simultaneous NOM of both the spleen and kidney is feasible and safe in specialized centers[8,9].

Upon arrival, the admission contrast-enhanced CT scan demonstrated a shattered lower pole with loss of parenchymal integrity, active contrast blush, delayed extravasation indicating collecting-system injury, a large perinephric hematoma, and patent main renal vessels, alongside a grade IV splenic laceration. The patient was managed conservatively. The spleen was managed non-operatively, avoiding splenic artery embolization in this combined-injury context. Because of persistent hematuria and documented collecting-system extravasation, definitive urinary diversion was pursued on hospital day 5.

The European Association of Urology (EAU) guidelines on urological trauma support this approach, stating that most urinary extravasations in Grade IV injuries can be managed minimally invasively with ureteral stenting or percutaneous drainage[10]. Furthermore, diagnostic laparoscopy excluded active intraperitoneal hemorrhage; a left double-J ureteric stent was placed cystoscopically, and an intraperitoneal drain was inserted. Stent repositioning was performed about 2 weeks after the initial intervention due to stent migration with persistent extravasation; thereafter, hematuria subsided and pain improved. Follow-up CT performed about 6 weeks later confirmed resolution of the perinephric hematoma and a non-enhancing infarcted lower pole with a patent left renal artery and no pseudoaneurysm or delayed calyceal injury, together with marked improvement in the splenic laceration. Follow-up ultrasound showed mild hydronephrosis without perinephric collection, with persistent lower-pole infarction.

After continued clinical improvement and preserved renal function, elective stent removal was performed approximately 3 months after the initial trauma, without complications. At his most recent follow-up, he was asymptomatic, with normal blood pressure, no hematuria, and preserved kidney function.

Although conservative management of isolated Grade IV renal trauma has become increasingly accepted in hemodynamically stable patients, our case presented additional challenges due to the coexistence of multiple severe injuries. These included a shattered kidney with collecting-system involvement and active contrast extravasation, in association with a concomitant Grade IV splenic laceration. Despite the complexity of the injuries, both nephrectomy and splenectomy were successfully avoided, and the patient was managed using a stepwise, minimally invasive approach.

This case implies that the grade of injury should not be the only factor guiding management decisions in high-grade abdominal trauma, particularly in hemodynamically stable patients.

In conclusion, combined Grade IV renal trauma with collecting-system injury and Grade IV splenic laceration can be successfully managed without nephrectomy, splenectomy, or SAE in carefully selected hemodynamically stable patients. Structured surveillance and minimally invasive intervention allowed successful management with organ preservation and favorable functional result.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths

This case of a hemodynamically stable adolescent with polytrauma demonstrates the feasibility of conservative management. Organ preservation was achieved through a multidisciplinary approach, minimally invasive interventions, and close monitoring, thereby avoiding splenectomy or nephrectomy.

Weaknesses and limitations

The findings of this report are limited to a single case, and the long-term outcomes remain uncertain. Although successful in this patient, conservative management may not be applicable to hemodynamically unstable patients or to those with more severe injuries.

Conclusion

In a hemodynamically stable adolescent with concurrent AAST Grade IV renal trauma (with collecting-system injury) and AAST Grade IV splenic injury, structured NOM comprising ICU surveillance, temporary urinary diversion with a double-J stent, selective diagnostic laparoscopy, and careful follow-up achieved full organ preservation without splenectomy, nephrectomy, or SAE.

This case reinforces stability-based selection over injury grade alone and demonstrates that stepwise, minimally invasive adjuncts can safely extend conservative strategies in combined high-grade solid-organ trauma.
