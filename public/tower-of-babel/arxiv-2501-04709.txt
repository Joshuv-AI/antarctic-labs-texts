Hyperrigidity III
Paweł Pietrzycki, Marcel Scherer and Jan Stochel

Abstract
. In this paper, we study hyperrigidity for C ∗ C^{*} -algebras. We will show that hyperrigidity can be expressed solely in terms of representations, without the need to involve general unital completely positive maps.

1.
Introduction

Denote by
\mathbb{N}
the set of all positive integers.
Henceforth,
\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})
stands for the
C^{*}
-algebra
of all bounded linear operators on a (complex) Hilbert
space
\mathcal{H}
and
I
denotes the identity operator on

\mathcal{H}
. We always assume that completely positive maps
are linear and representations of unital

C^{*}
-algebras preserve units and involutions.

Motivated both by the
fundamental role of the Choquet boundary in classical
approximation theory, and by the importance of
approximation in the contemporary theory of operator
algebras, Arveson introduced hyperrigidity as a form
of ’noncommutative’ approximation that captures many
important operator-algebraic phenomena.

We say that
G
is a set of generators of a unital

C^{*}
-algebra
\mathscr{A}
, or that
G
generates
\mathscr{A}
,
if the smallest unital
C^{*}
-subalgebra of
\mathscr{A}

containing
G
is equal to
\mathscr{A}
.

Definition 1.1
.

A finite or countably infinite set
G
of generators
of a unital
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathscr{A}
is said to be

hyperrigid
if for any faithful representation

\pi\colon\mathscr{A}\to\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})
on a separable Hilbert
space
\mathcal{H}
and for any sequence
\varPhi_{k}\colon\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})\to\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})
(
k\in\mathbb{N}
) of unital
completely positive (UCP) maps:

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\|\varPhi_{k}(\pi(g))-\pi(g)\|=0\;\forall g\in G\implies\lim_{k\to\infty}\|\varPhi_{k}(\pi(a))-\pi(a)\|=0\;\forall a\in\mathscr{A}.

Arveson gave a
characterization of hyperrigidity that replaces the
limit process by the so-called
unique extension
property
(see Theorem
1.2
(iii)). On the other hand, Kleski provided a characterization of hyperrigidity in which norm topology is replaced by weak operator topology (equivalently strong operator topology) (see Theorem
1.2
(iv)).

Theorem 1.2

(
[
1
, Theorem 2.1]
,

[
17
, Proposition 2.2]
&
[
19
, Theorem B.2]
)
.

Suppose that
G
is a nonempty subset of a unital

C^{*}
-algebra
\mathscr{A}
. Consider the following
conditions:

(i)

G
is hyperrigid,

(ii)

for every Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, every
representation
\pi\colon\mathscr{A}\to\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})
and
every sequence
\varPhi_{n}\colon\mathscr{A}\to\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})

(
n\in\mathbb{N}
)
of UCP maps,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\|\varPhi_{n}(g)-\pi(g)\|=0\;\;\forall g\in G\implies\lim_{n\to\infty}\|\varPhi_{n}(a)-\pi(a)\|=0\;\;\forall a\in\mathscr{A},

(iii)

for every Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and every
representation
\pi\colon\mathscr{A}\to\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})
,

\pi|_{G}
has the unique extension property,

(iv)

for every Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
,
every representation
\pi\colon\mathscr{A}\to\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})

and every sequence
\varPhi_{n}\colon\mathscr{A}\to\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})

(
n\in\mathbb{N}
)
of UCP maps,

\displaystyle\mathop{{\sf{w}\textrm{-}}lim}\displaylimits_{n\to\infty}\varPhi_{n}(g)=\pi(g)\;\forall g\in G\implies\mathop{{\sf{s}\textrm{-}}lim}\displaylimits_{n\to\infty}\varPhi_{n}(a)=\pi(a)\;\forall a\in\mathscr{A}.

Then conditions (i)-(iii) are equivalent.
Moreover, if
\mathscr{A}
is separable, then conditions
(i)-(iv) are equivalent, and what is more
they are still equivalent regardless of whether the
Hilbert spaces considered in either of them are
separable or not.

In accordance with Šaškin’s insightful
observation, Arveson
[
1
]
conjectured that
hyperrigidity is equivalent to the noncommutative
Choquet boundary of
G
being as large as possible,
in the sense that every irreducible representation of

C^{*}(G)
, the unital
C^{*}
-algebra generated by
G
,
should be a boundary representation for
G
. This is
now known as
Arveson’s hyperrigidity
conjecture
(see
[
1
, Conjecture 4.3]
). Some
positive solutions of Arveson’s hyperrigidity
conjecture were found for certain classes of

C^{*}
-algebras (see

[
1
,
17
,
7
,
21
,
14
,
24
,
19
,
23
]
). In full
generality, Arveson’s hyperrigidity conjecture turns
out to have a negative solution. Recently, a
counterexample has been found by B. Bilich and A.
Dor-On in
[
3
]
(see also
[
2
]
).
However, Arveson’s hyperrigidity conjecture is still
open for commutative C*-algebras (even the singly
generated case is not resolved).

In recent years, this issue has attracted considerable
interest in various parts of operator algebras and
operator theory

[
15
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
12
,
10
,
13
,
11
,
8
,
16
,
26
,
9
,
20
]
.
In particular, it is related to the Arveson-Douglas
essential normality conjecture involving quotient
modules of the Drury-Arveson space
[
15
, Theorem
4.12]
as well as is closely related to
the problem of characterizing the spectrality of semispectral measures by their
operator-moments (see
[
19
]
).

2.
Hyperrigidity via representations

In this section, we show that the property of hyperrigidity can be expressed solely in terms of representations, without the need to involve general UCP maps.

Theorem 2.1
.

Suppose that
G
is a nonempty subset of a unital separable

C^{*}
-algebra
\mathscr{A}
. Then the following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

G
is hyperrigid,

(ii)

for every Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
,
every representations
\pi\colon\mathscr{A}\to\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})

and
\pi_{n}\colon\mathscr{A}\to\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})

(
n\in\mathbb{N}
)
,

\displaystyle\mathop{{\sf{w}\textrm{-}}lim}\displaylimits_{n\to\infty}\pi_{n}(g)=\pi(g)\;\forall g\in G\implies\mathop{{\sf{s}\textrm{-}}lim}\displaylimits_{n\to\infty}\pi_{n}(a)=\pi(a)\;\forall a\in\mathscr{A}.

First proof of Theorem
2.1
.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii) It follows from Theorem
1.2
.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i)
First note that there exists a representation
\sigma\colon\mathscr{A}\to\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{L})
, where
\mathcal{L}
is an infinite-dimensional separable Hilbert space, i.e.
\dim\mathcal{L}=\aleph_{0}
. Indeed, let
\tilde{\sigma}\colon\mathscr{A}\to\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{M})
be a faithful representation of
\mathscr{A}
. Since,
\mathscr{A}
is separable, so is
\mathcal{M}
. If
\dim\mathcal{M}=\aleph_{0}
we are done. If not, then
\sigma:=\tilde{\sigma}\oplus\tilde{\sigma}\oplus\tilde{\sigma}\oplus...\colon\mathscr{A}\to\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{L})
is the required (faithfull) representation, where
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{M}\oplus\mathcal{M}\oplus\dots
.

Let
\pi\colon\mathscr{A}\to\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})
be a representation and

\varPhi\colon\mathscr{A}\to\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})
be a UCP
map such that

\displaystyle\pi(g)=\varPhi(g),\quad g\in G.

(2.1)

It follows from the Stinespring dilation theorem (see

[
25
, Theorem 1]
) that there exist a Hilbert
space
\mathcal{K}\supseteq\mathcal{H}
and a representation

\rho\colon\mathscr{A}\to\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{K})
such that

\displaystyle\varPhi(a)=P\rho(a)|_{\mathcal{H}},\quad a\in\mathscr{A},

(2.2)

where
P\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{K})
is the orthogonal
projection of
\mathcal{K}
onto
\mathcal{H}
.

Now we will show that without loss of generality we can assume that
\dim\mathcal{H}=\aleph_{0}
.
We always have that
\dim\mathcal{H}\leqslant\aleph_{0}
as a consequence of the separability of
\mathscr{A}
.
Suppose
\dim\mathcal{H}<\aleph_{0}
. Consider
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}=\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{L}
(recall
\dim\mathcal{L}=\aleph_{0}
),
\tilde{\pi}:=\pi\oplus\sigma\colon\mathscr{A}\to\boldsymbol{B}(\tilde{\mathcal{H}})
is a faithful representation and
\tilde{\varPsi}:=\varPsi\oplus\sigma
is a UCP map such that
\tilde{\pi}|_{G}=\tilde{\varPsi}|_{G}
. Since
\dim\tilde{H}=\aleph_{0}
, we infer from the
\aleph_{0}
-version of the proof that
\tilde{\pi}=\tilde{\varPsi}
on
\mathscr{A}
, i.e.,
\pi\oplus\sigma=\varPhi\oplus\sigma
, which implies that
\pi=\varPhi
, and we are done.

Our next goal is to show that, without loss of generality
\dim\mathcal{K}=\aleph_{0}
.
For this set
\mathcal{K}_{\text{min}}=\bigvee_{a\in\mathscr{A}}\rho(a)\mathcal{H}
. Then
\mathcal{K}_{\text{min}}
reduces
\rho
and
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{\text{min}}
, so
\varPhi=P_{m}\rho_{m}|_{\mathcal{H}}
, where
P_{m}\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{K}_{\text{min}})
is the orthogonal projection of
\mathcal{K}_{\text{min}}
onto
\mathcal{H}
, and
\rho_{m}=\rho|_{\mathcal{K}_{\text{min}}}
. However, if
\bar{\mathscr{A}_{0}}=\mathscr{A}
with
\mathrm{card}\mathscr{A}_{0}\leqslant\aleph_{0}
, and
\bar{\mathcal{H}_{0}}=\mathcal{H}
with
\mathrm{card}\mathcal{H}_{0}\leqslant\aleph_{0}
then

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\text{min}}=\bigvee_{a\in\mathscr{A}_{0}}\rho(a)\mathcal{H}=\bigvee_{a\in\mathscr{A}_{0}}\rho(a)\mathcal{H}_{0},

so
\dim\mathcal{K}_{\text{min}}\leqslant\aleph_{0}
. However,
\aleph_{0}=\dim\mathcal{H}\leqslant\dim\mathcal{K}_{\text{min}}\leqslant\aleph_{0}
, so
\dim\mathcal{K}_{\text{min}}=\aleph_{0}

Our next step is to show that, without loss of generality, we can assume that
\dim\mathcal{K}\ominus\mathcal{H}=\aleph_{0}
. Clearly
\dim\mathcal{K}\ominus\mathcal{H}\leqslant\dim\mathcal{K}=\aleph_{0}
. Suppose
\dim\mathcal{K}\ominus\mathcal{H}<\aleph_{0}
. Consider
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}=\mathcal{K}\oplus\mathcal{L}
, and
\tilde{\rho}=\rho\oplus\sigma\colon\mathscr{A}\to\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{K}\oplus\mathcal{L})
. Then
\tilde{\rho}
is a faithfull representation and
\dim\mathcal{K}\oplus\mathcal{L}=\aleph_{0}
and
\varPsi=P^{\mathcal{K}\oplus\mathcal{L}}_{\mathcal{H}}\tilde{\rho}|_{\mathcal{H}}
on
\mathscr{A}
.

(
P^{\mathcal{K}\oplus\mathcal{L}}_{\mathcal{H}}
is the orthogonal projection of
\mathcal{K}\oplus\mathcal{L}
onto
\mathcal{H}
)

Note that, without loos of generality we can assume that

\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{K}\oplus\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{L}\oplus\mathcal{L}\oplus\mathcal{L}\oplus\dots
, where as before
\dim\mathcal{L}=\aleph_{0}
.

Let
S\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{K}\ominus\mathcal{H})
be the isometric shift of multiplicity
\aleph_{0}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle S(h_{0}\oplus h_{1}\oplus\dots)=0\oplus h_{0}\oplus h_{1}\oplus\dots,\quad h_{j}\in\mathcal{L}.

Set
V=I_{\mathcal{H}}\oplus S\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{K})
. Then
V
is an isometry such that
\mathop{{\sf{s}\textrm{-}}lim}\displaylimits_{n\to\infty}V^{*n}=P
, where
P\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{K})
is the orthogonal projection onto
\mathcal{H}
.

Define the representation
\sigma_{\infty}\colon\mathscr{A}\to\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{K})
by

\sigma_{\infty}=\sigma\oplus\sigma\oplus\dots
, where

\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{H}\oplus(\mathcal{K}\ominus\mathcal{H})=\mathcal{L}\oplus(\mathcal{L}\oplus\mathcal{L}\oplus\dots)

Note that

\displaystyle(I_{\mathcal{K}}-V^{m}V^{*m})(f\oplus(h_{0}\oplus h_{1}\oplus\dots))=0\oplus(h_{0}\oplus h_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus h_{m-1}\oplus 0\oplus\dots),

so
I_{\mathcal{K}}-V^{m}V^{*m}
is diagonal with respect to the same orthogonal decomposition
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{L}\oplus(\mathcal{L}\oplus\mathcal{L}\oplus\dots)
.
Also
\sigma_{\infty}(a)
is diagonal with respect to the same orthogonal decomposition. Thus

\displaystyle(I_{\mathcal{K}}-V^{m}V^{*m})\sigma_{\infty}(a)=\sigma_{\infty}(a)(I_{\mathcal{K}}-V^{m}V^{*m})

for all
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and
a\in\mathscr{A}
.

This implies that
\mathscr{A}\ni a\to(I_{\mathcal{K}}-V^{m}V^{*m})\sigma_{\infty}(a)\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{K})
is
*-
multiplicative and linear. In fact, we have

\displaystyle(I_{\mathcal{K}}-V^{m}V^{*m})\sigma_{\infty}(a)=0\oplus\underbrace{\sigma(a)\oplus\dots\sigma(a)}_{m}\oplus 0\oplus\dots

Define
\pi_{m}\colon\mathscr{A}\to\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{K})

\displaystyle\pi_{m}(a)=V^{m}\rho(a)V^{*m}+(I_{\mathcal{K}}-V^{m}V^{*m})\sigma_{\infty}(a),\quad a\in\mathscr{A}.

Note that

\displaystyle\pi_{m}(a)=\psi_{m}(a)|_{\mathscr{R}(V^{m})}\oplus\underbrace{\sigma(a)\oplus\sigma(a)\oplus\dots\sigma(a)}_{m},

where

\displaystyle\psi_{m}(a)=V^{m}\rho(a)V^{*m},\quad a\in\mathscr{A}.

As we know,
\pi_{m}\colon\mathscr{A}\to\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{K})
is linear and
*-
multiplicative, so

\psi_{m}(a)|_{\mathscr{R}(V^{m})}
is a representation. This implies that each
\pi_{m}
is a representation. Since, we have
\mathop{{\sf{s}\textrm{-}}lim}\displaylimits_{n\to\infty}V^{*n}=P
, we get

\displaystyle\mathop{{\sf{w}\textrm{-}}lim}\displaylimits_{n\to\infty}\varPhi_{n}(a)

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\sf{w}\textrm{-}}lim}\displaylimits_{n\to\infty}V^{m}\rho(a)V^{*m}+\mathop{{\sf{w}\textrm{-}}lim}\displaylimits_{n\to\infty}(I_{\mathcal{K}}-V^{m}V^{*m})\sigma_{\infty}(a)

(2.3)

\displaystyle=P\rho(a)P+(I-P)\sigma_{\infty}(a)=P\rho(a)|_{\mathcal{H}}\oplus\sigma(a)\oplus\sigma(a)\oplus\dots,\quad a\in\mathscr{A}.

By (
2.3
), we get

\displaystyle\mathop{{\sf{w}\textrm{-}}lim}\displaylimits_{n\to\infty}\pi_{n}(g)=\pi(g)\oplus\sigma(g)\oplus\sigma(g)\oplus\dots,\quad g\in G.

It follows from Theorem
1.2
(v) that

\displaystyle\mathop{{\sf{w}\textrm{-}}lim}\displaylimits_{n\to\infty}\varPhi_{n}(a)=\pi(a)\oplus\sigma(a)\oplus\sigma(a)\oplus\dots,\quad a\in\mathscr{A}.

(2.4)

Comparing (
2.3
) with (
2.4
) we obtain

\displaystyle\pi(a)=P\rho(a)|_{\mathcal{H}},\quad a\in\mathscr{A}.

In view of beging of the proof, we conclude that
\pi(a)=\varPhi(a)
for all
a\in\mathscr{A}
. Applying Theorem
1.2
, we obtain that
G
is hyperrigid set.
∎

Second proof of Theorem
2.1
.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i)
Let
\pi\colon\mathscr{A}\to\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})
be a representation and

\varPhi\colon\mathscr{A}\to\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})
be a UCP
map such that

\displaystyle\pi(g)=\varPhi(g),\quad g\in G.

(2.5)

It follows from the Stinespring dilation theorem (see

[
25
, Theorem 1]
) that there exist a Hilbert
space
\mathcal{K}\supseteq\mathcal{H}
and a representation

\rho\colon\mathscr{A}\to\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{K})
such that

\displaystyle\varPhi(a)=P\rho(a)|_{\mathcal{H}},\quad a\in\mathscr{A},

(2.6)

where
P\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{K})
is the orthogonal
projection of
\mathcal{K}
onto
\mathcal{H}
.
In the same way as in the first proof Theorem
2.1
we can assume without loss off generality that

\displaystyle\dim\mathcal{H}=\dim\mathcal{K}=\aleph_{0}.

(2.7)

Let
(e_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}
and set

F_{n}=\operatorname{span}\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}.

Then

F_{1}\subseteq F_{2}\subseteq\cdots\qquad\text{and}\qquad\overline{\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}F_{n}}=\mathcal{H}.

By (
2.7
),
\dim(\mathcal{H}\ominus F_{n})=\dim(\mathcal{K}\ominus F_{n})

for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence there exists a unitary operator
U_{n}\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{K}

such that

U_{n}|_{F_{n}}=I_{F_{n}}.

We claim that
\lim_{n\to\infty}U_{n}f=f
, for
f\in\mathcal{H}
.
Indeed, if
P_{n}
denotes the orthogonal projection of
\mathcal{H}

onto
F_{n}
, then

\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{n}f=f
. Since
U_{n}P_{n}f=P_{n}f
, we have

\displaystyle\|U_{n}f-f\|

\displaystyle\leq\|U_{n}(f-P_{n}f)\|+\|P_{n}f-f\|

\displaystyle=2\|f-P_{n}f\|.

Therefore passing with
n
to infinity, we get
\lim_{n\to\infty}U_{n}f=f
, for
f\in\mathcal{H}
.

Define

\pi_{n}(a)=U_{n}^{*}\rho(a)U_{n},\qquad a\in\mathscr{A}.

Then each
\pi_{n}\colon\mathscr{A}\to\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})
is a representation.

For
a\in\mathscr{A}
and
f,f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\langle\pi_{n}(a)f,f^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\langle\rho(a)U_{n}f,U_{n}f^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\rho(a)f,f^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle P\rho(a)|_{\mathcal{H}}f,f^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\varPhi(a)f,f^{\prime}\rangle.

Therefore

\displaystyle\mathop{{\sf{w}\textrm{-}}lim}\displaylimits_{n\to\infty}\pi_{n}(a)=\varPhi(a),\qquad a\in\mathscr{A}.

(2.8)

In particular, by (
2.5
)

\mathop{{\sf{w}\textrm{-}}lim}\displaylimits_{n\to\infty}\pi_{n}(g)=\varPhi(g)=\pi(g),\qquad g\in G.

we obtain

\mathop{{\sf{w}\textrm{-}}lim}\displaylimits_{n\to\infty}\pi_{n}(g)=\pi(g),\qquad g\in G.

Hence, by assumption
(ii)
,

\mathop{{\sf{s}\textrm{-}}lim}\displaylimits_{n\to\infty}\pi_{n}(a)=\pi(a),\qquad a\in\mathscr{A}.

Consequently,

\mathop{{\sf{w}\textrm{-}}lim}\displaylimits_{n\to\infty}\pi_{n}(a)=\pi(a),\qquad a\in\mathscr{A}.

This combined with (
2.8
) gives
\varPhi=\pi
.

Therefore
\pi
has the unique extension property relative to
G
.
Since
\pi
was arbitrary,
G
is hyperrigid.

∎
