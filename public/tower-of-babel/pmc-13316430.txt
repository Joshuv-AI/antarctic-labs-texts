Multivalent interactions mediate SNAIL transcription factor stimulation of the nucleosome deacetylase activity of the CoREST complex
Authors: Eunju Nam, Manuel Osorio Valeriano, Zhipeng A Wang, Samuel D Whedon, Hanjie Jiang, Maggie Xinran Zhang, Sarah DuBois-Coyne, Ishraq A Haque, Jennifer Jiang, Jennifer Ferreira, Lucas Farnung, Kwangwoon Lee, Philip A Cole
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSNAIL is a transcription factor that plays a role in development and cancer. SNAIL contains an N-terminal SNAG domain that is a high-affinity ligand for the histone substrate binding lysine-specific demethylase 1 (LSD1). SNAIL also contains a C-terminal zinc finger domain that binds to DNA E-box sequences. SNAIL and related transcription factor family members are known to recruit LSD1-containing protein complexes to specific sites in chromatin to regulate gene expression. LSD1 can form a multiprotein complex with histone deacetylase 1 (HDAC1) and CoREST scaffolding protein (LHC). In this study, we use a purified system to analyze the role of SNAIL in modulating nucleosome deacetylation by the LHC complex. We find that SNAIL enhances nucleosome deacetylase activity of the LHC complex at multiple histone H3 sites through multivalent interactions. Enhanced nucleosome deacetylation is dependent on SNAIL’s SNAG and zinc finger domains. Unexpectedly, we find that SNAIL-stimulated nucleosome deacetylation by LHC also involves interactions of the nucleosome histone acidic patch, including histone H2A acidic residues. Modeling and mutagenesis experiments suggest that this acidic patch could engage a basic patch in the disordered segment of LSD1. Together, these findings reveal how a transcription factor can influence a cascade of molecular recognition events to regulate chromatin structure.

Introduction

The architecture and functions of chromatin are regulated by various histone post-translational modifications (PTMs), including lysine acetylation and methylation [1–4]. Among PTMs, Lys acetylation and deacetylation on histone H3 tails are critical in epigenetic control. Lys acetylation is catalyzed by “writer” enzymes, lysine acetyltransferases (KATs), and removed by “eraser” enzymes, histone deacetylases (HDACs), respectively [1–4]. Aberrant activity of HDACs has been associated with a range of human diseases, including cancer [5]. Several HDAC inhibitors are now FDA approved for a few hematological malignancies [6], but the full spectrum of their therapeutic applications may be hampered by our limited understanding of HDAC complexes and their biochemical mechanisms.

There are eleven classical HDACs, HDAC1–11, that behave as Zn (II)-dependent hydrolases. HDAC1 and its close paralog HDAC2 are typically found in multiprotein complexes that include particular scaffolding proteins (e.g. CoREST, NuRD, Sin3A, MiDAC, RERE, and MIER) [5, 7]. Among the multiprotein complexes, the CoREST complex has attracted special interest because of its roles in numerous gene regulation and disease processes. The CoREST complex contains HDAC1, lysine-specific demethylase 1 (LSD1), and the CoREST scaffolding protein (LSD1–HDAC1–CoREST; LHC). The LHC complex is associated with gene silencing through its dual enzymatic activities, histone lysine deacetylation and histone H3K4 demethylation. Increased LHC has been associated with tumor progression, and LHC inhibitors are under development to target cancer and other diseases [3, 6, 8]. Although the function and structure of the LHC complex have been intensively studied [7, 9–11], the precise molecular mechanisms associated with LHC’s enzymatic site specificity are incompletely understood [9, 10].

Various transcription factors have been reported to recruit LSD1 and HDACs to particular DNA sequences in chromatin [12–18]. Among these, the SNAIL transcription factor has been widely studied because of its association with LSD1. SNAIL is a key regulator of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and appears to contribute to cancer invasiveness by regulating E-cadherin, claudins, and other genes [12–14, 19, 20]. SNAIL belongs to a family of transcription factors that possess an N-terminal SNAG domain, and the SNAG domain can directly bind with high affinity to the histone substrate binding site of LSD1 [12, 13, 14, 19, 20]. In addition to its SNAG domain, SNAIL contains at its C-terminus four DNA-binding zinc finger domains (Fig. 1A). Structural studies of a SNAIL SNAG domain peptide in complex with LSD1 have revealed that SNAIL’s Pro1 is a critical component of LSD1 engagement and mimicry of H3 N-terminal tail interaction with the demethylase substrate binding site [13, 14, 19, 21], inhibiting H3K4 demethylation. Whether and how SNAIL modulates histone deacetylation, however, has not yet been characterized at the molecular level.

In this study, we investigate the interaction between SNAIL and the LHC complex and the function of SNAIL on the deacetylase activity of the purified LHC complex. We have developed an efficient method to produce purified SNAIL protein and employed an engineered sortase in protein semisynthesis to generate various acetylated nucleosomes as LHC substrates. Our enzymatic studies have revealed that SNAIL enhances the deacetylation activity of the LHC complex at multiple H3 positions. Unexpectedly, this enzymatic enhancement appears to involve an N-terminal basic cluster of Arg and Lys residues in LSD1 engaging the nucleosome acidic patch in an SNAIL-dependent manner. Cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) analyses of nucleosome-LHC complexes revealed a well-defined density corresponding to the nucleosome, along with an additional density adjacent to the nucleosome surface at super-helical location 2 (SHL2), near the N-terminal H4 tail. AlphaFold modeling suggests that this density could correspond to HDAC1. Interestingly, HDAC1 has been reported to bind to a similar position on the nucleosome within the Rpd3S complex [22–24]. These studies advance our understanding of the molecular mechanisms of LHC and SNAIL in the context of chromatin.

Materials and methods

Reagents

Chemicals were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA), Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA), Chem-Impex (Wood Dale, IL, USA), Research Products International (Mt Prospect, IL, USA), and Ambeed (Arlington Heights, IL, USA) and used without further purification. Fmoc-protected amino acids were obtained from Sigma–Aldrich, Chem-Impex, Ambeed, P3 Biosystems (Louisville, KY, USA), and ApexBio (Houston, TX, USA). Solvents were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich and Fisher Scientific. For deacetylation assays, anti-H3 (Abcam, Waltham, MA, USA), anti-H3K9ac (Abcam), anti-H3K14ac (EMD Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA), anti-H3K27ac (Cell Signaling, Danvers, MA, USA), and HRP-linked anti-rabbit antibody (Cell Signaling) were used. Distilled deionized water (ddH2O) used for all experiments was obtained from a Hydro Picotech 2 water purification system (Research Triangle Park, NC, USA). Reagents for cloning and mutagenesis were purchased from New England Biolabs (Ipswich, MA, USA). Size exclusion chromatography (SEC) was performed with a GE AKTA Purifier 10 FPLC System (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA). We used a Waters high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) system with a 1525 binary pump coupled to a 2489 UV detector (Waters, Milford, MA, USA) and a TSKgel DEAE-5PW column (Tosoh Bioscience, Tokyo, Japan) for nucleosome purification. Peptides were synthesized using the Prelude Peptide Synthesizer (Gyros Protein Technologies, Tucson, AZ, USA). The HEK293F cell line was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Cambridge, MA, USA) and shown to be negative for mycoplasma contamination.

Widom 601 DNA preparation and purification

DNAs were prepared based on previously published methods [9, 25–27]. The sequences of DNA used in this study are described in the Supplementary data. Two types of 185-bp Widom 601 DNA containing E-box sequence were synthesized by Genscript (Piscataway, NJ, USA). Plasmids containing multicopy DNA sequences (16 × 147 bp, 12 × 185 bp, and 4 × 185 bp with E-box sequences) were transformed into DH5α cells. A single colony was inoculated in CircleGrow media supplemented with antibiotics (147 bp: ampicillin, 100 mg/l; 185 bp: kanamycin, 50 mg/l; 185 bp with E-box: ampicillin, 100 mg/l) for 24 h at 37°C. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 10 min. Cell pellets were resuspended in the lysis buffer [10 mM Tris, pH 8, 10 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA); 5 ml/g wet cell pellet mass], followed by lysis with lysozyme powder (2.5 mg/1 ml lysozyme solution) dissolved in 10 mM Tris, pH 8, 10 mM EDTA (5 ml/g wet cell pellet mass). After incubation for 5 min, 0.2 M sodium hydroxide with 1% sodium dodecyl sulfate was added (7.5 ml/g wet cell pellet mass), vigorously shaken, and incubated for another 5 min. The mixture was neutralized by 1.5 M potassium acetate in 1.5 M acetic acid solution (6 ml/g wet cell pellet mass). After centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 10 min, the supernatant was filtered using an Econo column (Bio-Rad) to remove insoluble materials. The solution was combined with 0.6 eq of isopropanol, gently mixed, and incubated for 5 min. The samples were pelleted by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 10 min, and the pellets were resuspended in the lysis buffer (15 ml/g wet cell pellet mass), and the supernatants were discarded. The resuspended solution was mixed with one eq of 5 M LiCl and kept on ice for 5 min. Solutions were centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 15 min, and then the pellets were discarded. The supernatant was combined with 0.6 eq of isopropanol, mixed by inverting, and allowed to stand for 5 min. The samples were pelleted by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 10 min, and the pellets were resuspended in 10 mM Tris, pH 8, 1 mM EDTA, and heat-treated RNAse A (0.005 volumes of 10 mg/ml). After incubation for 15 min, 3 eq of isopropanol was added, mixed, and incubated for 5 min. DNA was pelleted by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 10 min. Pellets were dried and dissolved in 10 mM Tris, pH 8, 1 mM EDTA. 10× rCutSmart buffer (NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA) and EcoRV-HF (45 units/1 mg plasmid DNA, NEB) were added to the solution and incubated overnight at 37°C. Upon completion of the cleavage, solutions were 10-fold diluted with 10 mM Tris, pH 8, and 1 mM EDTA. The diluted solutions were incubated with Q-sepharose FF resin (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA), and the slurry was transferred to an Econo column. The resin was washed with 40 ml of 10 mM Tris, pH 8, 1 mM EDTA, 300 mM NaCl, and the DNA was eluted with 20 ml of 10 mM Tris, pH 8, 1 mM EDTA, and 800 mM NaCl. The eluent was concentrated up to 1 ml and purified with SEC with a Superdex 200 column (Cytiva).

Peptide synthesis

Modified histone H3 peptides (aa 1–34), LANA (aa 1–23), and SNAIL peptide (aa 1–9) were prepared by solid-phase peptide synthesis and purified by reversed-phase HPLC as previously reported [9, 25–27]. The sequences of the peptides made here are listed in the Supplementary Table S1. Peptides were synthesized using the Fmoc-based solid-phase strategy with Rink Amide AM resin (EMD Millipore) on a Prelude Peptide Synthesizer (Gyros Protein Technologies). The synthesis deprotection and coupling cycles were generally carried out on a 0.1 mmol scale: N-terminal Fmoc groups were deprotected with 5 ml of 20% piperidine in dimethylformamide (DMF) and mixed for 10 min in the reaction vessel. The vessel was drained, and a second identical deprotection followed, and five more washes followed, alternating solvent delivery from the top and bottom of the reaction vessel. For coupling, 0.4 mmol (4 eq) of Fmoc-amino acid in 2 ml DMF, 0.375 mmol (3.75 eq) of hexafluorophosphate azabenzotriazole tetramethyl uronium (HATU), and 0.8 mmol (8 eq) of N-methylmorpholine (NMM) in 4 ml DMF were sequentially added to the reaction vessel and mixed for 90 min, followed by one wash with 4 ml DMF (as before) and a second identical coupling. After the final amino acid coupling, peptides were N-terminally deprotected, and the resin was sequentially washed with DMF and dichloromethane (DCM) and dried under vacuum. Cleavage of the peptides from the resin and removal of the side chain protecting groups was achieved by the addition of Reagent B [5% water, 5% phenol, 2.5% triisopropyl silane (TIPS), 87.5% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA)] and agitation for 2 h. The resin was removed by filtration, and the crude peptides were precipitated by adding 10 eq of ice-cold diethyl ether. The precipitates were washed two times with ice-cold diethyl ether before drying under a stream of nitrogen. The resultant mixtures were purified by reverse-phase HPLC with a C18 semi-preparative column (Varian Dynamax Microsorb 100, 250 × 21.4 mm). H3 (aa 1–34) peptides were purified using the C18 column and a linear gradient from 7% acetonitrile/0.05% TFA to 40% acetonitrile/0.05% TFA over 30 min at a flow rate of 10 ml/min. LANA (aa 1–23) and SNAIL (aa 1–9) peptides were purified using the C18 column and a linear gradient from 0% acetonitrile/0.05% TFA to 65% acetonitrile/0.05% TFA over 40 min at a flow rate of 10 ml/min. Purified fractions were assessed by mass spectrometry using an ESI-MS (Q Exactive, Thermo Scientific), then combined, lyophilized, and stored at −80°C as dry powders or concentrated stock solutions.

Incorporation of depsipeptide Fmoc-Thr(OtBu)-glycolic acid (TOG) into H3 peptides

The synthesis of TOG was done as described before [9]. After the coupling of the first Gly residue, the second amino acid coupled in each H3 peptide was the depsipeptide TOG, which was hand-coupled overnight with 0.2 mmol (2 eq) TOG, 0.18 mmol (1.8 eq) HATU, and 0.4 mmol (4 eq) N, N-diisopropylethylamine (DIPEA). No capping step was used, and subsequent couplings were performed according to the general protocol.

Cloning

To purify and generate the human SNAIL protein starting from the Pro residue, a 6×His tag and ubiquitin sequence were inserted upstream of the SNAIL sequence in the pET 28a vector using Gibson Assembly. The sequence of GB1–Strep tag was inserted downstream of the sequence of 6×His–Ubiquitin–SNAIL using Gibson Assembly. Mutations in SNAIL, LSD11–170, and H2A proteins were conducted by QuikChange site-directed mutagenesis. Primers for the mutagenesis were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies (Coralville, IA, USA) and are listed in Supplementary Table S2. The PCR products were purified by PCR cleanup kit (Zymo, Irvine, CA, USA), treated with DpnI (New England Biolabs), and transformed into DH5a cells. Single colonies were picked and grown in 5 ml of Luria Broth (LB) media supplemented with Kanamycin (50 μg/ml) overnight at 37°C, and pelleted using the Thermo Sorvall Legend XTR refrigerated centrifuge (5 min, 4000 rpm, 4°C). Plasmids were extracted and purified by mini-prep kit (Zymo) and Sanger sequenced, or sequenced by nanopore. Sequences of the modified proteins are listed in the Supplementary data.

Protein expression and purification

SNAIL and SNAIL mutants

6×His–Ubiquitin–SNAIL, 6×His–Ubiquitin–SNAILR2A, and 6×His–Ubiquitin–SNAILK160E/K169E, 6×His–Ubiquitin–SNAIL–GB1-Strep tag were subcloned into pET28a vectors. The plasmids were transformed into LOBSTR Rosetta DE3 competent E. coli (Kerafast). A single colony was inoculated in a starter LB media containing 50 mg/l kanamycin and 35 mg/l chloramphenicol at 37°C. The starter culture was further inoculated into a larger culture (1–6 L) until A600 reached 0.4–0.8. 0.1 mM of isopropyl β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) was treated for induction at 12°C for 20 h in an incubator shaker. The cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4000 rpm. Harvested cells were resuspended in lysis buffer (20 mM Tris, pH 8, 300 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM TCEP), followed by cell lysis by passage through a French press. After centrifugation at 12 000 rpm for 30 min (SA-600, 20 853 × g), the supernatant was incubated with nickel-nitriloacetic acid (NiNTA) resin (MCLAB, San Francisco, CA, USA) for 1 h. The resin was washed with washing buffer (lysis buffer containing 20 mM imidazole), and the protein was eluted with elution buffer (lysis buffer containing 200 mM imidazole). USP15 enzyme (30 μM) was added to the eluates and incubated for 48 h at 25°C to cleave the site between the C-terminus of ubiquitin and SNAIL (Gly-Gly-Pro). The cleavage was confirmed by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) stained with Coomassie. The protein samples were dialyzed against a buffer containing 20 mM Tris, pH 8, 300 mM NaCl, and 0.5 mM TCEP overnight at 4°C. The cleaved SNAIL protein with GB1–Strep tag was further incubated with the Strep-Tactin™XT 4Flow™ High Capacity Resin (IBA Lifesciences, Göttingen, Germany) for 1 h and washed with the washing buffer (SNAIL lysis buffer containing 1 M NaCl) and eluted with the elution buffer (SNAIL lysis buffer containing 50 mM biotin). Samples were dialyzed using a 3.5 kDa MWCO spectra/Por standard RC tubing against a buffer containing 50 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, and 0.5 mM TCEP overnight at 4°C. The purity and concentration of the protein were analyzed by SDS–PAGE calibrated with BSA standards. The correct molecular weight of SNAIL was confirmed by intact MS. Fractions with 90% purity were pooled and supplemented with 5% glycerol and stored at −80°C or directly used to make the cryo-EM sample.

LSD1–HDAC1–CoREST (LHC) and LHC mutants

Production of LHC was conducted based on the previously reported method [9, 11, 27]. Full-length human LSD1, 3×FLAG-tagged CoREST1 (aa 86–485), and full-length human HDAC1 in pcDNA 3.1 expression vectors were co-transfected into HEK293F cells with polyethyleneimine (PEI; Millipore Sigma, cat# 408727). Cells were harvested via centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 10 min, followed by resuspension in the cell lysis buffer [50 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 100 mM KCl, 0.3% Triton X-100, 5% glycerol, and protease inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific)]. The solution was sonicated and centrifuged at 12 000 rpm (SA-600, 20 853 × g) for 30 min. The supernatant was further purified by FLAG M2 affinity resin (Millipore Sigma). The resin was washed with the SEC Buffer (50 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 50 mM KCl, 0.5 mM TCEP), and then the immobilized protein mixture was treated with TEV protease (0.8 mg/ml, 40 μl) to elute the LHC mixture by cleaving at the tag attached to the N-terminus of CoREST1. The LHC sample was further purified with SEC with a Superose 6 10/300 GL column (Cytiva). The sample was concentrated with a 10 kDa molecular weight cutoff filter (MWCO, Millipore) to achieve a concentration higher than 2 μM. The LHC complex showed 1:1:1 stoichiometry and appeared >90% pure by SDS–PAGE stained with Coomassie. The concentration of the complex was determined by SDS–PAGE with BSA standards. LHC protein solutions were supplemented with 5% glycerol and stored at −80°C.

Fluorescein-labeled LHC

The fluorescein labeling of the LHC complex was prepared as previously described [27, 28]. A Cys point mutation was introduced adjacent to the TEV protease cleavage site of N-terminally truncated CoREST for reaction with fluorescein ester. A 2 mM NHS-fluorescein was incubated with 500 mM mercapto-ethanesulfonate (MESNA) in the reaction buffer (100 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 50 mM KCl, 1 mM TCEP) for 4 h at room temperature in the dark for transesterification. The LHC complex purified by FLAG M2 affinity resin was washed with reaction buffer and incubated with TEV protease for 5 h at 4°C. The complex was then mixed with 500 μl of the fluorescein/MESNA solution to make a final concentration of 0.5 mM fluorescein and 125 mM MESNA. The mixture was incubated for 48 h at 4°C in the dark. The complex was desalted by a Zeba spin desalting column (7 kDa MWCO) and further purified by SEC using a Superose 6 10/300 column (Cytiva). Fluorescein-labeling efficiency was analyzed by SDS–PAGE and fluorescence gel imaging (Amersham Typhoon FLA 9500, Cytiva). The purity of the complex was verified by SDS–PAGE, and fractions with 90%–95% purity were pooled and supplemented with 5% glycerol and stored at −80°C.

Histone octamer

Xenopus laevis histones H2A or His–TEV cleavage site (TEVcs)–H2AD90A/E92A, H2B, H3, or tailless H3 (aa 35–135), and H4 were bacterially expressed and purified as previously described [26]. cW11 sortase

Engineered sortase cW11 was generated based on the previously reported method [26]. In brief, mutant sortase was derived from S. aureus sortase A (Srt A). The previously reported F40 sortase has altered sorting motif preference for APXTG. Constructs were transformed into LOBSTR Rosetta DE3 competent E. coli (Kerafast). Starter cultures were grown overnight, supplemented with 100 mg/l ampicillin. The starter culture was further inoculated into a larger culture (6 L) until A600 reached 0.4–0.8. 0.3 mM IPTG was treated for induction at 37°C for 3 h, then cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C. Cell pellets were resuspended in 5 pellet volumes of ice-cold lysis buffer (20 mM Tris, pH 7.5, 0.1% Triton X-100), made uniform by Dounce homogenizer, and lysed by three passages through a microfluidizer. Lysate supernatants were incubated with NiNTA resin for 1 h. The resin was washed with 10 column volumes of lysis buffer, then 20 volumes of wash buffer (20 mM Tris, pH 7.5, 500 mM NaCl), and then 10 volumes of lysis buffer supplemented with 5% elution buffer (20 mM Tris, pH 7.5, 400 mM imidazole). Protein was eluted in five to ten fractions of elution buffer, each of 2 column volumes. Elution fractions were checked by SDS–PAGE (15%) and pooled. Purified protein was dialyzed three times against 40 volumes of 50 mM Tris, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, and 5 mM CaCl2 at 4°C. Protein was then concentrated by spin concentrator to 1–3 mM by the absorbance at 280 nm.

Nucleosome reconstitution

Nucleosome reconstitution was performed as previously reported [26, 27]. In brief, the core histone octamers were mixed with Widom 601 DNA (147 bp, 185 bp, or 185 bp with E-box) at a molar ratio of 1:1.3 in the nucleosome high-salt buffer (10 mM Tris, pH 7.5, 2 M KCl, 1 mM EDTA, and 1 mM DTT). The mixture was dialyzed using a gradient to the nucleosome low-salt buffer (10 mM Tris 7.5, 0.25 M KCl, 1 mM EDTA, and 1 mM DTT) for 48 h. The reconstituted nucleosome was purified by an HPLC system (Waters) with the TSKgel DEAE-5PW (Tosoh Bioscience) column using a linear gradient between buffer A (10 mM Tris, pH 7.5, 150 mM KCl, 1 mM EDTA) and buffer B (10 mM Tris, pH 7.5, 600 mM KCl, 1 mM EDTA) at a flow rate of 1 ml/min. The fractions were collected and dialyzed into storage buffer (10 mM Tris, pH 7.5, 1 mM DTT) or freezing buffer (10 mM Tris, pH 7.5 at 25°C, 25 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 20% glycerol) for 3 h and concentrated to higher than 3 μM using a concentrator (Amicon 30-kDa MWCO, Millipore Sigma). The concentration and purity of nucleosomes were determined by measuring absorbance at A260 and analyzed through native gel electrophoresis, respectively.

Sortase-mediated transpeptidation

Transpeptidation with a cW11 engineered sortase was performed based on the previously reported method [26]. Reconstituted nucleosomes containing truncated H3 (2 μM) were incubated with 15 eq of modified H3 N-terminal peptide (aa 1–34; 30 μM) and 100 eq of cW11 sortase (200 μM) in the buffer containing 40 mM Tris, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM CaCl2, 1 mM DTT overnight at 37°C. After centrifugation at 14 000 rpm (21 100 × g) for 3 min, the supernatant was purified by an HPLC system (Waters) with the TSKgel DEAE-5PW (Tosoh Bioscience) column using a linear gradient between buffer A (10 mM Tris, pH 7.5, 150 mM KCl, 1 mM EDTA) and buffer B (10 mM Tris, pH 7.5, 600 mM KCl, 1 mM EDTA) at a flow rate of 1 ml/min. The fractions were collected and dialyzed into storage buffer (20 mM Tris, pH 7.5, 1 mM DTT) for 3 h and concentrated to higher than 3 μM using a concentrator (Amicon 30-kDa MWCO, Millipore Sigma). The concentration and purity of nucleosomes were determined by measuring absorbance at A260 and analyzed by native gel electrophoresis (ethidium bromide) and gel/western blot, respectively.

Microscale thermophoresis

Samples for the MST binding assays were prepared based on the previously reported methods [27, 29]. To obtain the binding affinity of SNAIL to the LHC complex, various concentrations of SNAIL were prepared using a two-fold serial dilution in the binding buffer (20 mM HEPES, 10 mM Tris, 1 mM TCEP, pH 7.5, 75 mM NaCl, 2 mg/ml BSA, 0.1% Tween 20, 10 μM ZnCl2), starting from the highest concentration of SNAIL (1 μM). Fluorescein-labeled LHC (400 nM) was mixed and incubated with different concentrations of SNAIL in a 1:1 volume ratio. To monitor the interactions of SNAIL with the LHC complex in the absence and presence of 185 bp nucleosomes, SNAIL (1 μM) and fluorescein-labeled LHC (400 nM) were incubated for 10 min, followed by incubation with different concentrations of nucleosomes in a 1:1 volume ratio, starting from the highest concentrations of nucleosomes (5.12 μM). The resultant samples were transferred to MST premium capillaries and the MST signal was monitored by Monolith NT.115 (NanoTemper) with Nano BLUE detector. The excitation power was set to 100% and the MST power to medium. The output was fitted into either (i) the quadratic equation to extrapolate the Kd values or (ii) the Hill equation to calculate the EC50 values using MO Affinity Analysis software (version 3.2) [29].

Deacetylation assays

Acetylated nucleosomes (147 bp H3K9ac, 185 bp H3K9ac, 185 bp H3K14ac, 185 bp H3K27ac, 185 bp E-box H3K9ac, 185 bp E-boxlinker H3K9ac, 185 bp E-box H3K14ac, 185 bp E-box H3K27ac, 185 bp E-box H2AD90A/E92A H3K9ac) were diluted in a reaction buffer [50 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 100 mM KCl, 100 μM inositol hexaphosphate (IP6), 0.2 mg/ml BSA] to make a final concentration of 100 nM. The LHC complex, SNAIL, and additional peptides or inhibitors were treated with the acetylated nucleosomes. The resultant samples were incubated at different time points at 37°C. At each time point, 6.5 μl aliquots were taken from each sample and quenched with an equivalent amount of a quenching buffer [20 mM EDTA, 2× SDS loading dye]. The samples were boiled at 95°C for 3 min and resolved on 4%–20% SDS–PAGE pre-cast Tris-glycine gels (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Proteins were then transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (Transfer Stack, Invitrogen, IB301031) using the iBlot (Invitrogen) transfer system with P3 (20 V) for 5 min, followed by blocking in 5% BSA in TBST buffer [20 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Tween, pH 7.5] for 1 h at room temperature. The membrane was incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies [anti-H3K9ac (1:2000, Abcam); anti-H3K14ac (1:2000, EMD Millipore); anti-H3K27ac (1:2000, Cell Signaling); and anti-H3 (1:2000, Abcam)]. After washing with TBST five times (2 min each), the horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:2000) in TBST was added to the membrane and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. The membrane was visualized by ECL substrate (Bio-Rad) and G:BOX mini gel imager (Syngene). The bands on the membrane were quantified using Image J software, and the intensities were normalized by the intensity at T = 0. Data analyses were conducted with GraphPad Prism 10, and the data were fit to a single-phase exponential decay curve (GraphPad Prism 10, with constraints Y0 = 1 and plateau = 0) [30].

AlphaFold prediction

Amino acid sequences of full-length N-Pro SNAIL (Uniprot ID: O95863), full-length LSD1 (UniProt ID: O60341), and full-length HDAC1 (UniProt ID: Q13547) N-terminally truncated RCOR1 (86–485 aa) (UniProt ID: Q9UKL0), Xenopus laevis H2A (UniProt ID: P06897), H2B (UniProt ID: P02281), H3 (UniProt ID: Q6P179), H4 (UniProt ID: P62799), two 185-bp Widom 601 E-box DNA with E-box sequence, six Zn (II) ions, one FAD cofactor, and 50 K+ with or without 50 Cl– were submitted to the AlphaFold3 [31]. Six independent AlphaFold3 runs were conducted. Three runs included both K+ and Cl– ions, whereas the other three contained only K+ ions. In total, thirty structural models were generated from six runs. In all models, the SNAG domain was positioned within the LSD1 catalytic site. Twenty-nine of thirty models showed interactions between nucleosomal acidic patch and the LSD1 N-terminal region. Nineteen of thirty models displayed interactions of SNAIL zinc finger domain with the E-box DNA sequences. Among these models, one model showed the position of HDAC well aligned with the putative cryo-EM density corresponding to HDAC1 (Seed: 437 751 359). This model was therefore selected as the representative AlphaFold3 model of the SNAIL–LHC–nucleosome complex and further refined by Rosetta. All predicted models were analyzed by PyMOL (v3.1.6.1, Schrödinger, LLC) [32] and UCSF ChimeraX (v1.11.1) [33].

Complex formation for cryo-EM

LHC–SNAIL-GB1–nucleosome complexes were formed by incubating 2.8 μM H3K9Hd nucleosome, 8.4 μM LHC complex [LSD1 (ΔPro77–Gly86)–HDAC1–CoREST], and 8.4 μM SNAIL-GB1 in buffer containing 50 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 85 mM NaCl, 100 μM IP6, and 1 mM TCEP on ice for 1 h. The mixture was then dialyzed against 50 mM low salt buffer HEPES pH 7.5, 50 mM NaCl, and 1 mM TCEP. The sample was centrifuged at 14 000 rpm (21 100 × g) and 4°C for 10 min to remove any aggregates. Reactions were applied to a 10%–30% sucrose gradient generated by a Gradient Master mixer (BioComp) containing 0.1% glutaraldehyde in the heavy solution [34]. Samples were centrifuged at 4°C at 32 000 rpm for 16 h using a SW60 rotor. Fractions containing a large portion of fully assembled nucleosome–LHC–SNAIL-GB1 complex were collected, dialyzed, and analyzed on a 3.5% acrylamide TBE native PAGE and mass photometry using a TwoMP mass photometer (Refeyn). Fractions containing nucleosome-bound complex were pooled and dialyzed at 4°C overnight in low salt buffer and concentrated to 700 nM.

Sample preparation for cryo-EM

LHC–SNAIL-GB1–nucleosome complexes were frozen on Quantifoil R2/1 on 200 mesh copper grids that were glow discharged for 30 s at 15 mA with 10 s hold time using a Pelco Easiglow plasma discharge system. Two microliters of sample were applied on each side of the grid, incubated for 8 s, blotted with Ted Pella standard Vitrobot filter paper for 6 s with blot force 8, and vitrified by plunging into liquid ethane using a Vitrobot Mark IV (FEI Company), operated at 4°C and 100% humidity.

Protocol for Rosetta-based modeling: structural modeling and Rosetta refinement of the SNAIL–LSD1–HDAC–CoREST–nucleosome complex

Thirty AlphaFold-generated complex models were manually evaluated against available EM density, published SNAIL zinc finger mutational analyses, reported LSD1 N-terminal interactions with the nucleosome acidic patch, and insertion of the SNAIL SNAG domain into the LSD1 catalytic pocket. One model, fold_2026_01_29_17_47_snail_lhc_ncp_model_0_candidate.cif, best satisfied these criteria and was selected for refinement. Inspection revealed that the SNAIL ZNF1 zinc ion was absent despite near-ideal tetrahedral coordination geometry formed by Cys155, Cys158, His171, and His175. The model also lacked hexakis inositol phosphate (IP6) and the H3K9 hydroxamate modification, while containing additional zinc and potassium ions introduced during AlphaFold complex assembly. To obtain a chemically consistent and mechanistically interpretable structure, we refined this AlphaFold model using Rosetta v3.12 [36] and incorporated the missing cofactors and ligands.

Using the PyMOL structure alignment application, the missing ZNF1 zinc ion was introduced using coordinates adopted from other AlphaFold models generated in parallel that displayed correct tetrahedral coordination. IP6 coordinates were taken from the HDAC1-MTA1 structure (PDB: 5ICN) [37]. Three noncanonical components were incorporated prior to Rosetta refinement: IP6, a hydroxamate-modified lysine at H3K9, and flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD). The protonation state of IP6 was selected to represent physiological pH. 31P NMR titration studies indicate that cation-free myo-inositol hexakisphosphate predominantly exists as the H4IP68−microspecies near physiological pH [38]. Accordingly, IP6 was modeled in the H4IP68−state (net charge −8).

To prepare the ligands in a Rosetta-friendly way, the extracted IP6 molecule was converted to MOL2 format using OpenBabel v3.1.1 [39], and an initial ionization pattern was assigned at pH 7.4 (obabel −p 7.4). A single protonation state was enforced by protonating four terminal phosphate O− atoms to produce H4IP68−. The molecule was embedded in three dimensions using RDKit (v 2025.03.6) and energy minimized with the UFF force field. The resulting minimized conformer was used to generate Rosetta ligand parameters using molfile_to_params.py (on /PATH-to-Rosetta/main/source/scripts/python/public/molfile_to_par ams.py.

The hydroxamate-modified lysine was constructed from the SMILES string N[C@@H] (CCCCCC (=O)NO)C (=O)O. A three-dimensional structure was generated using Open Babel with the flags - -gen3d - -minimize to obtain an energy-minimized geometry. Rosetta-compatible noncanonical amino acid (NCAA) definitions were generated using the BioChemical Library toolkit v4.3.1 [40]. NCAA generation employed -input_filenames hydroxamicLys_3d.sdf -output_prefix model_sdf/hydroxamicLys -generate_3D -chirality auto -generate_partial_charge_file -logger File. Rotamer sampling was performed using -ensemble_filenames model _sdf/hydroxamicLys_0.sdf -conformation_comparer SymmetryRMSD 0.25 -max_iterations 5000 -top_models 500 -cluster -skip_bond_angle_sampling -skip_rotamer_dihedral_sampling -conformers_single_file conformers_sdf/hydroxamicLys_conformers.sdf -logger File. Rosetta residue parameter files were generated using molfile_to_params_polymer. py - -clobber - -all-in-one-pdb - -name hly - -partial_charges model_sdf/hydroxamicLys_0.PartialCharges.txt -i conformers_sdf/hydroxamicLys_conformers.sdf.

To represent the catalytically relevant hydroxamate O− state while maintaining nitrogen protonation, the hydroxamate O–H proton was removed from both the params and rotamer files, and the oxygen partial charge was decreased by 1.0 to represent the anionic state. PROTON_CHI directives were removed after conversion to the O− state to avoid proton sampling conflicts during Rosetta refinement. The resulting residue corresponded to the anionic hydroxamate form, with a protonated nitrogen and a deprotonated terminal oxygen, consistent with the zinc binding state in HDAC active sites.

FAD coordinates were extracted from the AlphaFold model and chemically curated. Protonation states of the phosphate groups were adjusted to better reflect physiological ionization. The phosphoryl P = O double bonds were retained as modeled, while the phosphate hydroxyl groups (P–OH) were converted to their deprotonated P–O⁻ forms to represent the predominant anionic state at physiological pH. The tetrahedral phosphorus geometry and overall flavin-ribityl-adenosine scaffold were otherwise left unchanged. The structure was converted from PDB to MOL2 using Open Babel, and Rosetta params were generated using molfile_to_params.py with --clobber -n FAD -p rosetta_params/FAD. FAD was aligned onto the AlphaFold model prior to relaxation, with the expectation that local geometry would be optimized during refinement.

The composite model containing zinc, IP6, hydroxamate lysine, and FAD was first processed using Rosetta score_jd2 with the flags -s 20 260 214_complex_cleaned_link-mod_ IP6H4_renumbered.pdb -out:pdb -ignore_unrecognized_res -extra_res_fa FAD.params HLY. params IP6.params -renumber_pdb to generate a cleaned structure suitable for refinement. Constrained FastRelax was then performed with -s 20 260 214_complex_cleaned_link-mod.pdb -ignore_unrecognized_res -extra_res_fa FAD.params HLY.params IP6.params -score:weights ref2015 -constraints:cst_fa_file combined_zn.cst -constraints:cst_file combined_zn.cst -score:set_weights atom_pair_constraint 3.0 -score:set_weights angle_constraint 2.0 -relax:fast -ex1 -ex2 -use_input_sc -nstruct 1 -out:pdb -out:prefix relax_. Zinc coordination within SNAIL zinc fingers was maintained using explicit atom pair and angle constraints. Ten independent trajectories were generated, and the lowest total score model was selected.

The selected model was rescored using score_jd2 with -ignore_unrecognized_res -extra_res_fa FAD.params HLY.params IP6.params -in:auto_setup_metals -ex1 -ex2 -use_input_sc -nstruct 1 -out:pdb -out:prefix score_. The -in:auto_setup_metals flag correctly defined zinc coordination within SNAIL zinc fingers but also introduced artificial covalent linkages involving the HDAC catalytic zinc and nearby potassium ions. Because these metal ions are coordinated but not covalently bound under physiological conditions, the artificial covalent connections were manually removed to restore native noncovalent coordination geometry. The final structure represents a Rosetta refined model of the SNAIL–LSD1–HDAC-nucleosome complex initially derived from the AlphaFold structure with chemically consistent ligand states and physiologically appropriate metal coordination.

Results

Interactions of SNAIL with the LHC complex and nucleosomes

Deletion of Met1 from SNAIL has been suggested to be essential for the SNAIL–LSD1 interaction [13, 21], but experiments with untagged N-Pro full-length SNAIL (hereafter referred to as SNAIL) have been lacking. To generate N-Pro full-length SNAIL, we prepared the His-ubiquitin-SNAIL fusion protein (Fig. 1B). Ubiquitin-specific protease 15 (USP15), a C-terminal hydrolase that can cleave Gly-Gly/Pro site as designed for the ubiquitin-SNAIL fusion, was employed to detach the His-ubiquitin segment from natural SNAIL (Fig. 1B) [41–43]. Upon incubation of His-ubiquitin-SNAIL with USP15 for 40 h, we identified that the protein fusion was completely cleaved to produce SNAIL as desired (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Fig. S1). The LHC complex composed of LSD1, HDAC1, and flag-tagged CoREST was expressed and purified from HEK293F mammalian cells as previously described [9, 11]. Purification through size exclusion chromatography showed a stable association of the LHC complex, by showing a 1:1:1 stoichiometric ratio of LSD1, HDAC1, and CoREST (Supplementary Fig. S2).

To examine the interaction between SNAIL and the LHC complex, we employed a microscale thermophoresis (MST) binding assay, which enables the quantitative analysis of the interactions between fluorescently labeled molecules and their ligands [29, 44]. To generate fluorescein-labeled LHC, a Cys residue was inserted at the N-terminus of CoREST, and LHC was chemoselectively labeled with in situ prepared fluorescein-MESNA thioester [27, 28] (Fig. 1C). With MST, we determined the Kd value of SNAIL to the LHC complex and found that SNAIL can bind to LHC with nanomolar affinity (Kd = 48 ± 13 nM) (Fig. 1D).

Previous studies have found that the LHC complex exhibits a weak nucleosome binding affinity (Kd > 12 μM) [27]. Given that SNAIL can strongly interact with the LHC complex (Fig. 1D) and also bind to DNA in nucleosomes through its zinc-finger domain [19], we investigated the effect of SNAIL on the binding of the LHC complex to nucleosomes (Fig. 1E). The fluorescein-labeled LHC complex was incubated with or without SNAIL and mixed with a series of increasing concentrations of 185 bp nucleosomes. Due to the possibility of cooperative and multiple binding states between SNAIL, LHC, and nucleosomes, the Hill equation was applied to describe the apparent EC50, rather than a quadratic binding Kd model. In the absence of SNAIL, no apparent change in fluorescence was detected (EC50 = n.d.). Meanwhile, we identified that the LHC complex incubated with SNAIL showed an EC50 of 31 nM, suggesting the importance of SNAIL in the binding of the LHC complex to nucleosomes. Note that the fitted curves may underestimate the apparent binding affinities between proteins, as saturation of the MST signal was not fully reached at the highest concentrations tested. In summary, our results indicate that SNAIL can tightly bind to LHC and help tether the LHC complex to nucleosomes.

Impact of SNAIL on the deacetylase activity of the LHC complex

To investigate whether SNAIL affects the deacetylase activity of the LHC complex on nucleosome substrates, we prepared various acetylated nucleosomes using a streamlined assembly strategy reported recently (Fig. 2). Histone octamers with two copies of unmodified H2A, H2B, H4, and N-terminally truncated H3 (Δ1–34) were generated and assembled into nucleosomes with 147 bp Widom 601 sequence as well as 185 bp Widom 601 sequence plus linker DNA ± an E-box DNA sequence (5′-CACCTG-3′), which is an established SNAIL recognition motif [45, 46]. This E-box sequence was placed in one of two locations, the entry site or in the linker region of the 185 bp DNA (Fig. 2). The H3 tailless nucleosomes were incubated with acetylated H3 tail peptides (aa 1–34) (Fig. 2, Supplementary Fig. S3, and Supplementary Table S1) and engineered cW11 sortase previously described [26]. Fully assembled site-specifically modified nucleosomes were purified by anion exchange chromatography and confirmed to have structural integrity by native gel electrophoresis (Supplementary Fig. S4). The enzymatic activity of the LHC complex was monitored by the deacetylation assay in the absence and presence of SNAIL (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table S3), and the deacetylation rates were calculated as velocity/enzyme concentration (V/[E]) [9, 25, 27, 30]. We first investigated the LHC’s deacetylation activity with 147 bp H3K9ac nucleosomes with or without E-box at the nucleosome entry site (N1 and N2, respectively), as K9ac generally shows rapid deacetylation. The linearity of the western blot signal for H3K9ac over the relevant concentration range has been previously characterized [25]. During 2 h, noticeable deacetylation appeared with 30 nM LHC (V/[E] = 0.046 ± 0.0065 min−1 for N1; V/[E] = 0.027 ± 0.0015 min−1 for N2). Adding SNAIL to the enzymatic reaction mixture showed only minor enhancement (<2-fold) on the deacetylation activities of the LHC complex (V/[E] = 0.052 ± 0.0015 min−1 for N1; V/[E] = 0.040 ± 0.0049 min−1 for N2) (Figs 3B, Supplementary Fig. S5, and Supplementary Table S3).

Given that the length of DNA in nucleosomes can alter the recognition and catalytic activities of histone-modifying enzymes [47, 48], we assayed H3K9ac nucleosomes with linker DNA (185 bp) (N3) that is symmetrically extended from the 147 bp DNA and measured the deacetylase activity of the LHC complex with or without SNAIL (Fig. 3C, Supplementary Fig. S5, and Supplementary Table S3). Compared with N1 nucleosomes, N3 nucleosome substrate in the absence of SNAIL showed a ∼2-fold decreased deacetylation rate (V/[E] = 0.046 ± 0.0065 min−1 versus 0.018 ± 0.0033 min−1). In previous studies, it has been reported that LSD1 shows enhanced demethylase activity with linker DNA containing nucleosomes compared with 147 bp nucleosomes. Therefore, it is possible that this reduced deacetylase rate may involve crosstalk between the LSD1 and HDAC1 in the LHC complex [9, 10, 47, 49]. When SNAIL was added to the LHC/N3 nucleosome reaction, the H3K9ac deacetylation rate increased by ∼7-fold (V/[E] = 0.13 ± 0.051 min−1). With 185 bp E-box at the nucleosome entry site (N4) as substrate in the absence of SNAIL, the deacetylation rate was similar to the N3 nucleosomes (V/[E] = 0.021 ± 0.0027 min−1). However, the addition of SNAIL to this N4 nucleosome led to a ∼40-fold enhancement in LHC-mediated deacetylation (V/[E] = 0.81 ± 0.22 min−1) (Fig. 3C, Supplementary Fig. S5, and Supplementary Table S3). We also tested 185 bp nucleosome substrate with the E-box in the linker (N5), which consistently showed a noticeable enhancement in LHC-catalyzed deacetylase velocity compared to the N4 nucleosome substrate (Supplementary Fig. S6).

We next examined the effects of SNAIL on entry site E-box containing substrates with H3K14ac (N7) and H3K27ac (N9) modified nucleosomes (Fig. 3D and E, Supplementary Fig. S7, and Supplementary Table S3). As previously reported, in the absence of SNAIL, the LHC complex showed a relatively low deacetylation rate with N7 nucleosomes (V/[E] = 0.0080 ± 0.0028 min−1). With SNAIL, the LHC complex displayed a ∼20-fold increase in deacetylation rate (V/[E] = 0.15 ± 0.0040 min−1). A similar SNAIL stimulation was observed for LHC-catalyzed deacetylation of N9 nucleosomes (V/[E] = 0.017 ± 0.0040 min−1 versus 0.35 ± 0.035 min−1). In the absence of the entry site E-box sequence (N6, N8), SNAIL induced only a ∼2-fold increase in the V/[E] of the LHC complex. Taken together, these findings reveal that SNAIL can sharply enhance the deacetylation rate of E-box-containing nucleosomes across a range of H3 acetyl-Lys sites.

Roles of the SNAG and zinc finger domains of SNAIL in regulating nucleosome LHC deacetylation activity

To investigate the SNAG domain in SNAIL in promoting nucleosome deacetylation by the LHC complex, we prepared a SNAG peptide (SNAIL1–9) (Supplementary Fig. S8) to serve as a potential competitor with N4 nucleosomes. As a control, we showed that the addition of 4.5 μM SNAIL1–9 peptide did not significantly impact LHC-catalyzed deacetylation in the absence of SNAIL protein (V/[E] of ∼0.025 min−1 with or without SNAIL1–9 peptide). However, in the presence of SNAIL, SNAIL1–9 peptide caused a three-fold inhibition of LHC complex-catalyzed nucleosome deacetylation. (V/[E] of 0.51 ± 0.029 min−1 without SNAIL1–9 peptide versus V/[E] = 0.17 ± 0.060 min−1 with SNAIL1−9 peptide) (Fig. 4A and D, Supplementary Fig. S9, and Supplementary Table S3). We next tested the impact of LSD1 inhibitor, GSK2879552, which forms a covalent adduct with the flavin ring in the LSD1 and blocks H3 tail substrate binding [50]. In the presence of GSK2879552, SNAIL protein’s stimulation of LHC’s deacetylation activity was dramatically reduced (Fig. 4B and D, Supplementary Fig. S10, and Supplementary Table S3). It should be noted that the concentrations of SNAIL1–9 and GSK2879552 were selected to be in molar excess relative to their previously reported Ki values to LSD1 active site (0.14 μM for SNAIL and 1.7 μM for GSK287552), allowing for robust competitive inhibitory effects against SNAIL. Moreover, we prepared full-length R2A mutant SNAIL as Arg2 is known to be important for SNAG domain binding to LSD1 [21]. Consistent with expectations, R2A mutant SNAIL protein was only weakly able to stimulate LHC-catalyzed nucleosome deacetylation, resulting in only ∼2-fold increase in V/[E] (0.018 ± 0.0046 min−1 versus 0.047 ± 0.0091 min−1) (Fig. 4C and D, Supplementary Fig. S11, and Supplementary Table S3). Taken together, these results indicate that inhibition of the SNAIL–LSD1 interaction by mutation or peptide or small molecule antagonists impedes SNAIL’s ability to stimulate LHC’s deacetylase activity on nucleosomes.

We next investigated the role of SNAIL’s zinc finger domain on LHC-catalyzed nucleosome deacetylation. To this end, we generated a double mutant of SNAIL protein, SNAILK160E/K169E, based on a previous report that this mutant demonstrates diminished DNA-binding ability [51, 52]. Deacetylation assays with LHC complex in the presence of SNAILK160E/K169E revealed that the mutant SNAIL is unable to enhance the deacetylation of N4 nucleosome by the LHC complex (V/[E] = 0.018 ± 0.0046 min−1 for LHC; V/[E] = 0.026 ± 0.0064 min−1 for LHC with SNAILK160E/K169E) (Fig. 4C and D, Supplementary Fig. S11, and Supplementary Table S3). These observations establish that zinc finger–nucleosome interactions are critical for SNAIL to enhance nucleosome deacetylation by the LHC complex.

Role of the nucleosome acidic patch region in SNAIL-stimulated LHC complex deacetylation activity

The H2A-H2B acidic patch region in the nucleosome is a cluster of negatively charged residues (E56, E61, E64, D90, E91, E92 of H2A; E102, E110 of H2B) [53]. This acidic patch is a key binding site of multiple peptides and enzyme complexes (e.g. H4 tail, RPD3S, SIRT) and is involved in chromatin folding [22, 53–55]. We therefore explored if perturbation of the nucleosome acidic patch might somehow influence LHC-catalyzed deacetylation in a manner influenced by SNAIL. We reconstituted N4 nucleosomes by conducting two alanine mutations on H2A in the acidic patch (H2AD90A/E92A) and tested these nucleosomes (N10) with LHC plus or minus SNAIL (Fig. 5A and C, Supplementary Fig. S12, Supplementary Table S3). In the absence of SNAIL, the deacetylation rate of the LHC complex with acidic patch mutant nucleosomes was similar to that observed with wild-type nucleosomes (V/[E] = 0.016 ± 0.0092 min−1). In the presence of SNAIL, the deacetylation of N10 nucleosomes by the LHC complex was increased by only ∼3-fold (V/[E] = 0.049 ± 0.0074 min−1), far less than the ∼40-fold with the corresponding wild-type nucleosomes. These results suggest that the acidic patch is involved in interactions with the SNAIL–LHC complex. To further probe the interactions between the acidic patch and the SNAIL–LHC complex, we examined the Latency Associated Nuclear Antigen (LANA) synthetic peptide from Kaposi’s sarcoma-associated herpesvirus as a competitive binder (Fig. 5B and D, Supplementary Figs S13, S14, and Supplementary Table S3) [49, 51]. The concentration of the LANA peptide was chosen in molar excess based on the previously reported Kd values, and 1 μM of LANA peptide had a minimal impact on the deacetylation activity of N4 nucleosomes by the LHC complex in the absence of SNAIL. However, in the presence of SNAIL, the addition of LANA reduced the deacetylase activity by ∼4-fold (V/[E] of 0.94 ± 0.097 min−1 without LANA versus 0.23 ± 0.052 min−1 with LANA). These findings with LANA reinforce the mutagenesis findings that SNAIL stimulation of LHC complex-catalyzed nucleosome deacetylation involves the nucleosome acidic patch.

The N-terminal region of LSD1 in SNAIL-stimulated nucleosome deacetylation by the LHC complex

To elucidate how the nucleosome acidic patch region may facilitate the effects of SNAIL on deacetylation, we employed computational modeling with AlphaFold3 [31]. We generated models of the SNAIL–LHC complex bound with an N5 nucleosome. These models showed consistent interactions between a basic amino acid cluster of the N-terminal region of LSD1 and the nucleosome acidic patch with a moderate level of interface-predicted template modeling (ipTM) scores (∼0.5) (Supplementary Fig. S15). In these LSD1–nucleosome acidic patch models, the side chains of the LSD1 basic amino acid cluster, 108RRTSRRKR115, appear to undergo electrostatic interactions with acidic residues in histone H2A (i.e. E61, E64, D90, E92) (Supplementary Fig. S15). To experimentally test this model, we hypothesized that an LSD1 N-terminal protein segment might be able to serve as an antagonist of SNAIL-induced LHC complex deacetylation. We prepared the LSD1 N-terminal fragment (aa 1–170) that conserves the cluster of basic residues 108RRTSRRKR115 (Supplementary Fig. S16) and showed that 1 μM of this N-terminal fragment of LSD1 can inhibit SNAIL-mediated stimulation of the deacetylase activity of the LHC complex (V/[E] of 0.72 ± 0.17 min−1 without LSD11–170 versus 0.14 ± 0.0053 min−1 with LSD11–170) (Fig. 6, Supplementary Fig. S17, and Supplementary Table S3). While 1 μM of LSD11–170 did not show much effect on the V/[E] of LHC in the absence of SNAIL, with SNAIL present, there was about a five-fold reduced deacetylase rate of the LHC complex compared with the LSD11–170-absent condition. These results suggest that the basic cluster of LSD1 in the LHC complex could contribute to nucleosome acidic patch interactions. To further investigate this possibility, we generated two mutants of LSD11–170 (108AATSAAAA115, 108EETSEEEE115) that would disrupt the electrostatic interactions with the acidic patch region (Supplementary Fig. S16). The use of mutant LSD11–170 fragments induced no significant impact on the V/[E] of the LHC complex in the presence of SNAIL (Fig. 6B and C, Supplementary Fig. S17, and Supplementary Table S3), revealing that these mutations lose the ability of the LSD1 N-terminal region to bind the acidic patch. Based on these findings, we generated a mutant LHC complex in which the six basic residues of the LSD1 cluster were substituted with Ala (Supplementary Fig. S2). This mutant LHC complex was expressed and purified from HEK293F cells without incident, indicating that the LSD1 basic cluster was not critical for LHC complex stability. We then tested the basic cluster LHC mutant for nucleosome deacetylation activity in the absence and presence of SNAIL (Fig. 7, Supplementary Fig. S18, and Supplementary Table S3). Notably, the basic cluster LHC mutant was unstimulated by the addition of SNAIL. Collectively, our results suggest that the N-terminal basic cluster of LSD1 associates with the acidic patch in nucleosomes in a SNAIL-dependent fashion, which in turn contributes to the enhancement of the deacetylation activity of the LHC complex by SNAIL.

Structural investigation of the LHC–SNAIL–nucleosome

To gain further insight into how SNAIL, the LHC complex, and nucleosome interact with each other, we performed a structural investigation using cryo-EM. Isolating and analyzing the LHC–nucleosome complex has been inherently challenging, likely due to its flexibility and relatively modest basal affinity [10, 27]. To address this, we prepared semisynthetic nucleosomes with 185 bp entry site E-box DNA that are designed to trap the LHC complex with high affinity. Site-specifically modified nucleosomes incorporating analogs of histone PTM marks have been employed as an effective approach to investigate enzyme-nucleosome structures and analyze enzyme functions [9, 10, 25]. We designed and synthesized asymmetric nucleosomes containing one unmodified histone H3 tail and another H3 tail in which the K9 side chain was incorporated with a hydroxamic acid (Hd) moiety, capable of coordinating with Zn(II) ions in the HDAC1 catalytic center [56] with a high binding affinity (Kd < 10−7 M) (Fig. 2, Supplementary Figs S8A and S19A). In addition, we used the LHC complex with the LSD1 mutation (Δ77–86), which removes hydrophobic stretches in LSD1 and enhances LHC production from HEK cells and tightens interactions with transcription factors [15, 28]. Note that the LSD1 truncation (Δ77–86) does not affect the deacetylation activity of the LHC complex (Supplementary Fig. S20). We conducted nucleosome electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) to investigate the binding of the LHC complex and SNAIL to H3K9Hd nucleosomes (N11). H3K9Hd nucleosomes exhibited reduced band intensity and a weak band shift only in the presence of both the LHC complex and SNAIL, whereas neither the LHC complex nor SNAIL alone produced these effects (Supplementary Fig. S19B and C). Of note, H3K9ac nucleosomes lacked changes in band intensity and mobility shift under the same conditions, suggesting that the incorporation of the hydroxamic acid moiety into nucleosomes helps stabilize the protein complex (Fig. 8B).

Based on these findings, we prepared samples for cryo-EM using N11 nucleosomes, the LHC complex, and SNAIL. Due to the low yield of SNAIL, we incorporated the B1 domain of streptococcal protein G (GB1) into the C-terminus of SNAIL to increase its expression levels and solubility (SNAIL-GB1; Supplementary Fig. S1) [57]. EMSA with N11 nucleosome, SNAIL-GB1, and the LHC complex showed results consistent with those obtained with SNAIL (Fig. 8B and Supplementary Fig. S19D). Therefore, we used SNAIL-GB1 for cryo-EM studies and incubated the nucleosome with SNAIL-GB1 and the LHC complex at a 1:3 ratio, which was the maximum achievable due to the limited protein concentrations. The N11 nucleosome–SNAIL-GB1–LHC complex was prepared by the GraFix method [34] and analyzed by native PAGE gel and mass photometry (Supplementary Fig. S19E and F). Fractions containing a large portion of fully assembled nucleosome–LHC–SNAIL-GB1 complex were dialyzed and concentrated for single-particle cryo-EM analysis. We were able to reconstruct nucleosomes with an extra density at SHL2 from 11,931 particles, achieving a resolution of the overall 3D envelope of 4.2 Å (Supplementary Fig. S21 and Supplementary Table S4). Although this resolution is insufficient to build a detailed structural model, we observed partial nucleosome unwrapping and additional density (∼10.0 Å) that can accommodate the AlphaFold3-predicted structure of HDAC1 (Supplementary Fig. S22).

To improve the structural analysis, we prepared similar nucleosomes with the E-box sequence positioned in the linker region (N12) (Fig. 2, Supplementary Fig. S23, and Supplementary Table S5). Using this nucleosome, we collected a larger data set on a Titan Krios microscope. We reconstructed nucleosomes from 39 591 particles with an overall resolution of 3.3 Å (Fig. 8D). Our nucleosomes again contain an extra density at SHL2, which likely corresponds to HDAC1; however, the low resolution (∼7.0 Å) of the extra density impeded our efforts to obtain a detailed structural model.

Despite the limited resolution, the cryo-EM maps obtained from the two complexes displayed similar features, suggesting the validity of our structural investigation. Both maps revealed the extra densities at SHL2 and near the N-terminal H4 tail (Fig. 8C and D). The density of the H3 tail was not detected in our cryo-EM map, likely due to the limited resolution; however, we found that the position of extra density in our maps resembles that of HDAC in the Rpd3S complex on the nucleosome [22–24]. The Rpd3S complex has its active site positioned above the N-terminal H4 tail and in close proximity to the H3 tail, thereby facilitating both H3 and H4 deacetylation [22–24]. Thus, the position of HDAC1 in our model suggests that it can readily access the H3 tail and enable deacetylation.

To further investigate SNAIL–LHC–nucleosome interactions, we performed AlphaFold3 modeling of the SNAIL–LHC–H3K9ac nucleosome. Specifically, we performed six independent runs and obtained a total of 30 models. All models showed the position of SNAG domain in the LSD1 active site. Twenty-nine out of thirty models showed the interactions between nucleosomal acidic patch and LSD1 N-terminal regions. Nineteen out of thirty models displayed interactions of SNAIL zinc finger domain with the E-box DNA sequences. Among these models, one model showed the position of HDAC close to the putative cryo-EM density corresponding to HDAC1. The minor discrepancies between the position of HDAC1 and the cryo-EM density may arise from the conformational flexibility of the LHC complex [10] and the disordered C-terminal region of HDAC1, which is predicted with low confidence (pLDDT < 50). Therefore, we further refined this model using Rosetta for optimizing interfaces and conformational sampling (Fig. 8E and Supplementary Fig. S24). H3K9ac was replaced with H3K9Hd to match the substrate used in the experiments, and IP6, a cofactor of HDAC, was included in the model. The resulting model supports our biochemical results, indicating multivalent binding among SNAIL, nucleosome, and the LHC complex (Fig. 8E and Supplementary Fig. S24). In addition, the CoREST SANT2 domain contacts the phosphate backbone of nucleosomal DNA at approximately the SHL +2.5 position, but does not engage H4. Previous studies have shown that HDAC1 can interact with the CoREST SANT2 domain, which contains DNA-binding residues and has been proposed to associate with H4 [10, 47]. However, in our AlphaFold models, 20 out of 30 structures show that the SANT2 domain does not directly engage either DNA or H4. We attribute this to a more heterogeneous nucleosome-binding mode of LSD1, where the SNAG domain of SNAIL serves as the primary anchor instead of the H3 tail. The ∼100-residue region between the SNAG domain and ZNF1 likely provides additional flexibility for nucleosome engagement, together with contacts between the LSD1 N-terminal region and the nucleosomal acidic patch. This may contribute to the variability in binding, consistent with our EM density lacking well-resolved density corresponding to LSD1–CoREST. Across the AlphaFold models, LSD1–CoREST adopts heterogeneous conformations, while remaining anchored through SNAG–LSD1 and acidic patch–LSD1 interactions. This flexibility, together with these anchoring interactions, may allow HDAC1 to access acetylated histone H3, leading to enhanced deacetylation of H3, including H3K14ac, a mark that is typically poorly deacetylated by the CoREST complex [7, 30].

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate a powerful role of SNAIL in nucleosome deacetylation by the LHC complex (Fig. 9). As SNAIL’s occupancy of the LSD1 active site precludes its demethylase activity, it is logical that gene repression mediated by SNAIL is achieved through histone deacetylation of chromatin. Blocking SNAG–LSD1 interactions increases H3 acetylation in cells, implicating the role of SNAIL in the deacetylation by the LHC complex; however, the precise molecular mechanism has remained unexplored [16]. Our findings that there is broad tolerance in the positional effects of the SNAIL DNA binding sequence, the E-box, in the nucleosome regarding deacetylase stimulation, suggest that recruitment to chromatin has functional plasticity. Moreover, SNAIL’s similar deacetylase rate acceleration across three different histone H3 acetylation sites supports the enzymatic flexibility of SNAIL’s recruitment effects on the LHC complex. Our mechanistic studies on SNAIL’s allosteric role in enhancing the LHC complex’s deacetylation of nucleosomes confirmed key roles for SNAIL’s N-terminal SNAG domain and its C-terminal zinc fingers in LSD1 recruitment and DNA E-box binding, respectively. The AlphaFold prediction of the SNAIL–LHC–nucleosome complex also provides insights into these interactions (Supplementary Fig. S24), suggesting a role for SNAIL in recruiting the LHC complex to the nucleosome through SNAG binding to LSD1, as well as through interactions of its zinc finger regions with extranucleosomal DNA, particularly at the E-box region. Disruption of these interactions, either by competition with the SNAG peptide or by mutation of the zinc finger regions (SNAILK160E/K169E), may impair efficient recruitment of the LHC complex to the nucleosome, thereby providing a possible explanation for the reduced effect of SNAIL on LHC deacetylase activity observed under these conditions. Our use of mutant SNAILK160E/K169E at zinc finger 1 region was intended to perturb SNAIL’s zinc finger–mediated DNA engagement [51, 52], but we do not exclude the possibility that other zinc fingers also contribute to nucleosome engagement. The intermediate SNAIL-mediated activation of 185-bp DNA-containing nucleosomes that lack an E-box indicates that even non-specific DNA interactions, presumably the DNA linker region, can convey a modest level of LHC recruitment. It is also interesting that linker DNA appears to be key for SNAIL’s stimulation of nucleosome deacetylation, as 147 bp nucleosomes lacking linker DNA failed to show a strong effect, regardless of E-box content.

HDAC is closely located near H4 and induces rapid H3 deacetylation.

An unanticipated discovery from our mechanistic studies of SNAIL’s effects is unveiling a role for the nucleosome acidic patch in modulating LHC complex nucleosome deacetylation. Although a number of histone-modifying enzymes [58], such as Dot1L, Sirt6, Compass, SAGA, PRC1, and RNF168, engage the nucleosome acidic patch as part of their catalytic action, LSD1 and class I HDACs have not previously been shown to do so. Indeed, in the absence of SNAIL, the nucleosome acidic patch does not appear to be important in LHC complex-catalyzed deacetylation. To our knowledge, this is the first example of a conditional role for the nucleosome acidic patch in driving histone-modifying enzyme action that is dependent on a transcription factor. It appears that SNAIL recruitment drives an allosteric interaction between a basic amino acid cluster in the unstructured region of LSD1, 108RRTSRRKR115, and the nucleosome acidic patch. Our enzymatic data, mutational analysis, and AlphaFold3/Rosetta modeling support a structural mechanism in which SNAIL recruitment promotes engagement of a basic amino acid cluster within the intrinsically disordered region of LSD1 (108RRTSRRKR115) with the nucleosome acidic patch, although we cannot fully exclude alternative, indirect models of acidic patch involvement in the deacetylase reaction. It is notable that this LSD1 basic amino acid cluster includes a well-established phosphorylation site, Ser111. Ser111 phosphorylation has been observed in a number of phosphoproteomic studies. This PTM has been associated with changes in circadian rhythm, breast cancer EMT and metastasis, immune response modulation, and protection against oxidative stress in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [59–61]. We speculate that this Ser111 phosphorylation event in LSD1 can weaken the electrostatic interaction between the basic amino acid cluster and the nucleosome acidic patch. This could influence LHC catalysis to prevent unrestrained deacetylase activity or tighten site selectivity within chromatin. Future work will be needed to explore this.

Our cryo-EM analyses, while limited in resolution and restricted in protein coverage, did capture nucleosome–HDAC1 engagement. Using nucleosomes with hydroxamic acid (Hd) as a warhead of HDAC1, we observe the putative HDAC1 density near H4 and nucleosomal DNA. This interface is reminiscent of the Sin3A HDAC complex bound to nucleosomes. Further work will be needed to determine if histone H4 tail acetylation can influence LHC-catalyzed nucleosome deacetylation at histone H3. The absence of electron density corresponding to SNAIL, LSD1, and CoREST in our cryo-EM map suggests that there is considerable plasticity and dynamics in the overall assembly of the LHC–SNAIL–nucleosome macromolecular assembly. Our AlphaFold/Rosetta modeling suggested a candidate structural framework for LHC–SNAIL–nucleosome, visualizing interactions of SNAIL with LSD1 catalytic domain and DNA E-box, position of H3K9Hd to HDAC1, LSD1 basic patch–histone H2A/H2B acidic patch interaction, and close proximity of HDAC1 to the putative HDAC1 density in our cryo-EM maps. The positions of LSD1 and CoREST in the LHC complex differ from those observed in the previous structural model of the LSD1–CoREST (LC) complex, which utilized N-terminally truncated LSD1 [47], likely due to differences in sequence composition as well as the presence of SNAIL and HDAC1. Additional tools will likely be needed to visualize protein–protein interactions missing from our experimental structure.

Our ability to produce SNAIL protein containing an N-terminal Pro benefited from the use of USP15 to cleave the ubiquitin fusion protein. This might be a broadly applicable method to generate N-terminal Pro-containing proteins. The use of cW11 sortase facilitated the generation of symmetric and asymmetric nucleosomes that were instrumental to the enzymological analysis in the study. We propose that the application of this engineered sortase can be widely useful for the biochemical analysis of nucleosomes in which histone H3 is of special interest.
