Early detection of dead broilers in commercial farms using temporal persistence of stationary behavior
Authors: Eunji Park, Juwhan Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEarly detection of dead broilers is essential for maintaining productivity and biosecurity in commercial farms; however, dead broilers are difficult to distinguish from live broilers in deep resting states using single-frame images. This study aimed to develop and evaluate an early warning pipeline that detects dead broiler candidates by quantifying the temporal persistence of stationary behavior in broilers aged 1 to 10 days. Top-view CCTV videos were collected from a commercial farm housing approximately 30,000 broilers, and frames were sampled at a rate of 1 frame per minute. Broilers were detected using a YOLO-based object detector, and bounding boxes from the first frame were fixed to extract broiler-level image sequences. A ResNet-BiLSTM classifier categorized each 8-frame sequence as ‘stationary’ or ‘moving’, and consecutive predictions were accumulated to compute stationary duration for each broiler. To set an operational threshold, stationary duration distributions from 93,085 normal observations in the June dataset were analyzed to derive three criteria: the top 1%, top 0.1%, and day-of-age-specific maximum values, which were then evaluated on an independent July dataset. All criteria achieved a recall of 0.95, but precision differed markedly. The maximum stationary duration observed among normal broilers was 91 frames, approximately 91 min. Based on this empirical upper bound, a conservative fixed threshold of 92 frames yielded a precision of 0.7917 and an F1 score of 0.8636. A more conservative threshold of 113 frames increased precision to 0.9048 and the F1-score to 0.9268. When the time interval between exceeding the stationary duration threshold and manual removal was defined as the early detection interval, mean intervals were 479.8 min, approximately 8.0 h, at 92 frames and 458.8 min, approximately 7.6 h, at 113 frames. These results suggest that dead broiler detection can be approached not as a single-frame morphological discrimination problem, but as a temporal persistence problem that evaluates how long a stationary state is maintained. Furthermore, thresholds derived from the behavioral distribution of normal broilers may enable a practical early warning system for detecting dead broilers in commercial broiler farms.Keywords: Broiler farming, Dead broiler detection, Temporal persistence of stationary behavior, Behavior classification, Threshold selection

Introduction

Rapid and accurate detection of dead broilers is a critical component of maintaining productivity and biosecurity in broiler production systems. Delayed removal of dead broilers can increase secondary risks, including disease transmission and odor generation, thereby compromising farm productivity and biosecurity (Gržinić et al., 2023). In commercial broiler production, first-week mortality (FWM) is widely used as an independent management indicator and accounts for a substantial proportion of total flock mortality. An average FWM of 1.40% and mortality after the first week (MAFW) of 3.05% in Ross 308 broilers were reported, indicating that approximately 31% of total mortality occurred within the first seven days (Forseth et al., 2024). Similarly, analysis of 1,142 production cycles reported a median FWM of 0.95% and total mortality of 3.42%, suggesting that early mortality accounts for approximately 28% of overall losses (Slegers et al., 2025). These findings indicate that mortality during the first week typically represents approximately one-quarter to one-third of total flock mortality, emphasizing the importance of early detection during the initial growth phase. Immediately after placement, chicks undergo physiological adaptation to a new environment. Immature thermoregulation, limited immune competence, and environmental stress increase the risk of early mortality.

In practice, the standard method involves farm workers patrolling the poultry house to visually identify and remove dead broilers (Olejnik et al., 2022). However, in high-density rearing environments housing thousands to tens of thousands of broilers, the labor burden is significant, and there is a high probability that dead broilers may be missed or left unattended for extended periods. As time passes, unattended dead broilers may be deformed by the movement and contact of surrounding birds or obscured by litter, making visual identification increasingly difficult. Specifically, once a dead broiler is buried in the litter, farm workers are substantially more likely to miss it, which may compromise farm biosecurity by increasing the risk of disease transmission. Therefore, early detection within the critical window before burial is essential to facilitate worker operations.

Early studies on automated broiler monitoring established foundational approaches for detecting inactivity and mortality using invasive sensor-based methods and machine vision techniques (Okinda et al., 2019). Among these approaches, one representative method directly measured individual movement using accelerometers and detected anomalies by exploiting the characteristic flat signal patterns that appear when a bird ceases to move (Bao et al., 2021). While these studies demonstrated that mortality could be determined by quantifying the difference between activity and inactivity, attaching sensors to individual birds presents significant limitations for large-scale application in commercial farms.

Non-invasive image-based approaches have also been actively researched. Methods combining thermal and visible light cameras were proposed to detect dead broilers by extracting high temperature living birds from thermal images and removing them from visible light images to isolate dead broilers (Muvva et al., 2018). Subsequently, numerous studies have employed You Only Look Once (YOLO)-based object detection models for dead-broiler detection, including YOLOv3 (Hao et al., 2022), YOLOv5 (Bist et al., 2023), and YOLOv8, YOLOv9, YOLOv10, and YOLOv11 (Bumbálek et al., 2025). Although high performance with a mAP of over 98% has been reported, the challenge lies in the fact that broilers in a deep resting state and actual dead broilers are structurally similar in images (Liu et al., 2021). For instance, transformer-based dual-stream networks have been proposed to integrate detection and segmentation to improve dead-bird identification in farm environments, achieving a mean intersection over union (IoU) of 88% on test data (Ham et al., 2024). However, they remain primarily reliant on single-frame visual information and do not explicitly address the temporal persistence of immobility.

Accordingly, recent research has expanded to incorporate temporal information. A previous study proposed a method for screening dead-broiler candidates by calculating the Average Optical Flow Intensity (AOFI), followed by confirming mortality based on how frequently each individual was classified as candidate death across the video (Hao et al., 2025). Another study reported a two-stage approach that first detects stationary regions in CCTV footage and then utilizes a DeiT-Tiny-based classifier to determine if the region is an actual broiler. This study also used prolonged stationary state as a key indicator for mortality determination (Khanal et al., 2025). These studies are significant in that they move beyond simple object detection to utilize the persistence of inactivity along the time axis as a core metric.

However, most existing studies have relied on arbitrarily defined thresholds for long-term inactivity, without quantitatively establishing the temporal boundary between stationary behavior and mortality. In one study, chickens were experimentally inoculated with Salmonella and continuously monitored for two weeks using cameras; individuals showing no movement for 30 min or more were flagged as potential infection cases (Kim et al., 2023). While this research demonstrated that prolonged inactivity could serve as a practical early warning signal, the inactivity threshold was pre-defined rather than derived from the actual distribution of stationary behavior in healthy chickens. There is still a lack of research that derives thresholds from the distribution of stationary durations in healthy broilers and validates these thresholds within actual commercial farm environments. Therefore, detecting dead broilers requires an approach that goes beyond single-time-point classification and incorporates temporal persistence.

To address this need, we propose an integrated analysis pipeline that combines object detection with time-series behavior classification. First, broilers are detected using YOLO-based object detection, and bounding boxes obtained from the initial frame are fixed to subsequent frames to construct bird-level sequences. Subsequently, a ResNet-BiLSTM-based behavior classification model categorizes each sequence as 'stationary' or 'moving' and consecutive stationary predictions are accumulated to quantify the stationary duration for each individual broiler. Furthermore, we analyze the stationary duration distribution of normal broilers to derive a data-driven threshold for stationary duration, which is then used as a criterion for detecting dead broilers.

Materials and methods

Data collection

This study was conducted at a commercial broiler farm housing approximately 30,000 broilers. To collect video data, two IP cameras (IPC675LFW-AX4DUPKC-VG, Uniview) were installed at a height of 4.5 m above the floor inside the poultry house. The cameras were positioned to provide a top-down view, looking vertically at the interior of the house. All collected video footage was connected to a Network Video Recorder (NVR; model: UHD-IN504P, UNDPRO) for stable recording and storage.

Video data were collected during two production cycles. The first collection period ranged from June 11 to June 20, 2025, and the second from July 27 to August 5, 2025. Both periods captured the early growth phase from day 1 to day 10 of age. A total of four video datasets were obtained by filming two distinct zones within the house. The specific usage of each dataset is outlined in Table 1. The number of broilers detected within the field of view of each camera ranged from a minimum of 100 to a maximum of over 900. The original video resolution was 2,880 × 1,620 pixels. To improve analysis efficiency, the footage was cropped to 2,800 × 1,440 pixels to exclude the timestamp area. Additionally, frames were extracted at a rate of one frame per minute to reduce the data processing load. Only frames between 03:00 and 21:00, during which lighting conditions remained stable, were selected to construct datasets for statistical analysis, model training, and validation.

YOLO-based detection of broiler individuals

To estimate stationary persistence at the individual level, a YOLO-based object detection model was employed to detect broiler individuals and their locations. To construct the training dataset, broiler locations were manually labeled with bounding boxes in a total of 30 original images from the No. 1 dataset, selecting three images for each day of age. Given that a single image contains hundreds of individuals, which can degrade detection accuracy for small objects, and to compensate for the limited quantity of training data, a sliding window technique was applied. The original images were divided into small patches of 320×320 pixels, and a 20% overlap was applied between adjacent windows to prevent the loss of objects located at boundary regions. This approach enhanced detection performance for small objects while effectively expanding the scale of the dataset. The final constructed dataset consisted of 2,274 images, which were partitioned into 1,592 images for training, 342 for validation, and 340 for testing.

Upon comparing the YOLOv8 (Varghese and Sambath, 2024) and YOLOv11 models, YOLOv8l demonstrated the most stable performance, achieving a precision of 0.963, a recall of 0.94, and an mAP50-95 of 0.809, across different model sizes, both YOLOv8l and YOLOv11l achieved the highest precision of 0.963, as shown in Table 2. Minimizing false positive detections was crucial since the detected broiler regions were used as inputs for the subsequent stationary behavior classification stage. Because YOLOv8l showed a slightly higher recall than YOLOv11l, it was selected for the subsequent experiments.

•Stationary: As shown in Fig. 1, this label denotes a state in which the individual remains within the detected area or its original positional range throughout the sequence, without substantial displacement. In high-density rearing environments, this category may include minor tremors or posture changes caused by external factors, such as contact with surrounding individuals or trampling, provided that no substantial positional movement occurs.

Dead broiler detection pipeline based on temporal persistence of stationary behavior

The proposed pipeline for individual broiler analysis is presented in Fig. 3. A sliding window-based sequence construction method was employed to generate individual-level time-series sequences, from which stationary duration was quantified. Letting the chronologically ordered frames be t0,t1,⋯,tn, the analysis was performed repeatedly by setting the frame corresponding to the top of each hour (e.g., 03:00, 04:00, ..., 20:00) from 03:00 to 20:00 as a new starting point. Specifically, the frame at the top of each hour was defined as t0, and stationary duration was accumulated independently using subsequent continuous frames. At each start time t0, YOLO-based object detection was performed to detect broiler bounding boxes. The bounding box coordinates of each detected individual were fixed and applied identically to subsequent frames. Using the initial position as a reference, the same coordinate area was cropped from continuous frames to construct individual sequences. Age-specific threshold derivation using normal broiler stationary duration distributions

To establish the threshold, Dataset No. 2 (refer to Table 1) was used to analyze the distribution of Tstationary for normal broilers. Individuals confirmed as dead broilers were excluded from the statistical analysis, and the distribution was estimated based on 93,085 observations of normal broilers detected in the footage. This exclusion was necessary because including dead broilers would overestimate the duration of prolonged stationary states, potentially distorting the estimation of the upper bound of the stationary duration distribution for normal behavior. If a Tstationary value was calculated for the same individual across multiple time slots, only the Tstationary value from the initial detection was used as the representative value for the analysis. Therefore, by estimating the distribution of stationary duration solely for normal broilers, we aimed to define the range of physiologically possible resting behaviors.

Among all normal observations, 6,721 (approximately 7.2%) exhibited a continuous stationary state lasting at least 8 frames. This indicates that stationary behavior lasting at least 8 frames, approximately 8 minutes, was observed only in a limited subset of normal observations. Because observations without any stationary sequence have no measurable stationary duration beyond the initial classification, threshold derivation for dead broiler candidate detection was performed using only the subset of normal observations in which at least one stationary sequence was detected. The distribution of stationary duration in normal broilers allows for the estimation of the upper bound of physiologically normal resting behavior; stationary states that exceed this upper bound can be interpreted as anomalous behaviors outside the general physiological resting range. Since dead broilers typically maintain a stationary state in a similar posture at the same location for an extended period until removal by the farmer, continuous stationary states exceeding the upper bound of normal stationary duration provide a rational basis for classification as dead broiler candidates. As shown in Fig. 6, the distribution of initial 8-frame sequence classifications showed that most normal observations were classified as moving, whereas only a limited proportion exhibited an initial stationary sequence. The proportion of observations with an initial stationary sequence was relatively high during the early rearing period and gradually decreased with age. This trend may reflect the frequent occurrence of prolonged resting behavior during the early adaptation phase, followed by increased activity, feeding, and exploratory behavior as the broilers grew. As shown in Fig. 7, Fig. 8, three statistical criteria were derived for each day of age: the top 1%, the top 0.1%, and the maximum value. The analysis results indicated that the maximum number of stationary frames was relatively high during the early ages, particularly from days 1 to 5, with the highest value of 91 frames recorded on day 3. This can be attributed to the greater tendency of young chicks to remain inactive for extended periods during the early adaptation stage. The top 0.1% threshold during this period was also relatively high, ranging from 49.8 to 78.9 frames. Conversely, the frequency of prolonged stationary states decreased sharply as the broilers reached later days of age. The decrease in prolonged stationary states can be attributed to increased activity accompanying growth, a higher frequency of interactions among individuals, and the activation of feeding and movement behaviors.

To evaluate the dead broiler detection performance of the proposed system and the threshold-setting strategy, precision, recall, and F1-score were used as evaluation metrics. Individuals with a stationary duration Tstationary(i) greater than or equal to the threshold θ were predicted as dead broiler candidates, and a case in which the intersection over union (IoU) between the predicted bounding box and the ground truth (GT) bounding box was 0.3 or higher were defined as a true positive (TP). Cases in which the IoU was less than 0.3 or no corresponding GT existed were classified as false positives (FP). Additionally, GT dead broilers that were not matched with any predicted bounding box with an IoU of 0.3 or higher were defined as false negatives (FN). Results and discussion

Dead broiler detection performance based on age-specific statistical thresholds

The thresholds were evaluated by applying the three statistical criteria derived from the June data, namely the age-specific top 1% value, top 0.1% value, and maximum value, to Datasets 3 and 4 (refer to Table 1), which were not used for training. The evaluation results are shown in Table 5. All three criteria achieved a recall of 0.95. In terms of precision, however, substantial differences were observed depending on the threshold-setting method. The precision based on the top 1% criterion was very low at 0.1044, indicating that the top 1% threshold failed to sufficiently exclude the general resting behaviors of normal broilers. When the age-specific top 0.1% threshold was applied, precision improved to 0.3455, but cases of prolonged resting behavior in normal broilers were still included. Setting the threshold based on the age-specific maximum value resulted in a further improvement in precision to 0.4043; however, limitations still remained in completely distinguishing prolonged normal resting behavior from dead states. Because stationary duration can be influenced not only by age but also by farm-specific conditions, including the rearing environment and chick health and adaptation status, thresholds determined separately for each day of age may be biased when derived from a single rearing cycle. Therefore, rather than determining separate age-specific thresholds, we further evaluated dead broiler detection performance using population-level thresholds derived from the entire set of normal broilerobservations.

The results of analyzing the stationary duration distribution for detection cases classified as normal broiler observations in the June data are presented in Fig. 10. Based on the stationary duration distribution of the entire normal population, the top 1% value, top 0.1% value, and observed maximum value were derived as 39.00, 64.28, and 91.00 frames, respectively. Among these values, 91 frames represents the empirical maximum stationary duration observed in the large-scale normal population and can be interpreted as the empirical upper bound of long-term resting behavior that can occur in normal broilers. Accordingly, dead broiler detection performance was analyzed by incrementally increasing fixed thresholds within the range of 40-120 frames, including 40 frames, 65 frames, and 92 frames, which exceed the top 1%, top 0.1%, and maximum-value criteria, respectively. The results are shown in Fig. 11. As shown in Table 6, recall remained constant at 0.95 across all thresholds, whereas precision improved monotonically as the threshold increased. Specifically, precision increased from 0.1863 at 40 frames to 0.6786 at 65 frames and further to 0.7917 at 92 frames, with corresponding F1-scores of 0.3115, 0.7917, and 0.8636, respectively. When the threshold was increased to 113 frames, precision rose to 0.9048, and the F1-score reached its highest value at 0.9268. This indicates that false positives can be further reduced while maintaining a recall of 0.95.

Analysis of missed detections

All false negative cases, that is, missed detections, occurred because the individuals were not detected during the YOLO-based object detection stage due to occlusion; consequently, adjusting the threshold had no impact on these cases. Examples of such missed detections are presented in Fig. 12.

Analysis of early detection lead time before manual dead broiler removal

To quantify the operational validity of the proposed system, the time difference between the moment when the system identified a stationary state exceeding the threshold and the moment when the worker actually removed the dead broiler was defined as the early detection lead time. As shown in Fig. 13, when the 92-frame threshold was applied, the mean early detection lead time was 479.8 minutes (approximately 8.0 hours), ranging from 87 to 1,890 minutes. When the threshold was increased to 113 frames, the mean early detection lead time decreased slightly to 458.8 minutes (approximately 7.6 hours), ranging from 66 to 1,869 minutes. These results indicate that both thresholds provided an average lead time of more than 7 hours for intervention. Therefore, the threshold can be selected according to operational priorities, considering the trade-off between earlier detection and improved detection accuracy.

Conclusions

This study redefined the problem of dead broiler detection in commercial broiler farms from a single-frame-based stationary classification issue to a problem of analyzing temporal persistence along the time axis. In high-density rearing environments, dead broilers and normal broilers in a deep resting state appear structurally similar in single images; thus, it is difficult to accurately distinguish dead broilers from resting normal broilers using only simple object detection-based approaches. Therefore, this study proposed an integrated pipeline that quantifies stationary duration at the individual level by combining YOLO-based object detection with a ResNet-BiLSTM-based time-series behavior classification model. We analyzed the stationary duration distribution of normal individuals to derive an empirical upper bound and presented a threshold-setting strategy that defines continuous stationary states exceeding this bound as dead broiler candidates.

The proposed threshold (θ=92) achieved a recall of 0.95, precision of 0.7917, and an F1-score of 0.8636 on an independent dataset. When a more conservative threshold (θ=113) was applied, precision and F1-score improved to 0.9048 and 0.9268, respectively. Furthermore, the proposed system detected dead broiler candidates an average of 7-8 hours before the actual removal time, providing sufficient response time for farm workers to intervene in advance. The results suggest that early detection can be achieved while minimizing false positives by defining dead broiler candidates based on the statistical upper bound of normal behavior. In practical farm management, the proposed pipeline can provide early alerts together with the estimated locations of dead broiler candidates before routine removal by farm workers. Because detected candidates can be represented as bounding-box coordinates within the camera field of view, farm workers can more easily identify locations that require inspection and removal. This may also help workers recover dead broilers that could be missed by visual observation alone due to litter coverage or occlusion. In addition, the system could be extended to record the number and spatial distribution of detected dead broiler candidates over time, thereby supporting mortality monitoring and timely removal in commercial broiler farms.

Future research should focus on verifying the generalizability of the thresholds through long-term data accumulation, mitigating occlusion issues through multi-camera or tracking-based approaches, and expanding the system to 24-hour monitoring under nighttime conditions. These advancements are expected to contribute to the establishment of automated health monitoring systems in commercial broiler farms.
