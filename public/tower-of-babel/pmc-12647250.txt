Migrant Electoral Rights (MER) New Data from 165 Countries and Six Decades
Authors: Sebastian Umpierrez de Reguero, Klaudia Wegschaider, Rainer Bauböck
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSuffrage is a central element of democracy. Over time, electoral rights have increasingly become available to migrants—both as non-citizen residents (NCs) and as non-resident citizens (NRs). However, existing datasets either focus on only one of those categories, exclude candidacy rights, or have limited temporal and geographic scope. This article introduces the GLOBALCIT Dataset of Migrant Electoral Rights (MER), the most comprehensive dataset on migrant suffrage to date. MER covers voting and candidacy rights for both NCs and NRs. The coding differentiates between different types (legislative, executive, referendum) and levels (local, regional, national) of elections, as well as a range of eligibility, access, and modality conditions attached to these rights. Overall, the dataset contains up to 488 indicators across 165 countries from 1960 to 2020. By offering unparalleled data coverage and transparent documentation, MER facilitates the study of global trends in the political inclusion and exclusion of migrants, thus contributing to comparative political science, migration studies, and electoral policy analysis.Subject terms: Politics, Geography

Background & Summary

International migration transforms societies worldwide, reshaping demographics, economies, and sociopolitical landscapes1. As the number of international migrants increases, addressing their integration, representation, and political participation becomes ever more relevant.

Migrants’ electoral rights are increasingly recognized as essential for ensuring that democracy remains representative and inclusive in contemporary societies2,3. Whereas the extension of electoral rights to non-resident citizens (NRs) is often framed as a strategy for maintaining ties with diasporas and encouraging their transnational engagement4–7; expanding candidacy and/or voting rights to non-citizen residents (NCs) can be seen as a way to promote integration and democratic political socialization in the residence country8–10.

Despite growing academic and policy interest, research on migrants’ electoral rights has been hindered by the lack of comprehensive and standardized global data. Although the same individuals tend to be simultaneously NRs of one polity and NCs in another polity, studies of migrants’ electoral rights have mostly separated these two categories. We study NCs and NRs jointly. Most, but not all, of the former are immigrants, and the latter are emigrants. For ease of reading, we use the term “migrants” when referring to both categories. However, we recognize that NRs also include non-migrants who are granted external citizenship based on ethnicity, ancestry, reparations for historical injustice, or investment. In countries without jus soli citizenship, NCs also include descendants of immigrants born in the country. Accordingly, most datasets on this topic cover only one of the two categories (see Table 1). Since coding principles and procedures vary, a simple combination of existing datasets would not yield a consistent picture. Notable exceptions are the ELECLAW11 and Conditions of Electoral Rights (CER)12 datasets, which both include NCs as well as NRs. However, these two datasets are limited in their temporal and geographic coverage.

Overall, existing datasets have made pioneering contributions to the field. However, their scope is mostly restricted to specific regions, time periods, or dimensions of electoral participation. On the NR side, datasets such as IDEA’s Voting from Abroad13 or the Emigrant Policies Index14,15 have been valuable contributions but they either lack candidacy rights (the former) or broad geographic coverage (the latter). Even the thus-far most comprehensive data on NR suffrage, the Extraterritorial Voting Rights and Restrictions Dataset16, considers candidacy rights only in the context of special representation. On the NC side, datasets provide only limited coverage beyond developed countries17–20. This fragmented landscape constrains cross-national analyses of migrant electoral rights.

Another challenge with existing datasets on migrants’ electoral rights has been their mixed record on transparency or accessibility. EVRR16, the most comprehensive dataset on NR voting rights, impresses with a detailed and clear codebook. Yet, supporting documentation enabling users to trace the information underlying the coding is only available upon request. On IDEA’s website, visualizing data on Voting from Abroad13, users can find primary source hyperlinks for most but not all information. CER12 and ELECLAW11 build on a collection of national legislation and a set of country reports provided by the Global Citizenship Observatory (GLOBALCIT), but this dense background information is not accessibly condensed. This makes it challenging and cumbersome to evaluate whether discrepancies across datasets are due to differing coding rules or errors.

To address these shortcomings, we introduce the GLOBALCIT Dataset of Migrant Electoral Rights (MER). Covering electoral rights for both NCs and NRs across 165 countries from 1960 to 2020, this dataset is the most comprehensive one on this topic. MER codes up to 488 indicators, encompassing candidacy and voting rights across legislative and executive elections as well as referendums at national, regional, and local levels. Among datasets that cover both categories of migrants, MER provides unparalleled geographic and temporal coverage, enabling detailed longitudinal and cross-national analyses. Additionally, this dataset is accompanied by a roughly 400-page Factbook [https://cadmus.eui.eu/server/api/core/bitstreams/aa629006-6854-4f30-83e8-4e1db1aa241a/content], including a brief description and references for each country.

There are three main motivations for developing MER. First, the dataset provides a comprehensive and standardized resource for scholars and policymakers. This contribution is particularly valuable considering ongoing debates on citizenship and political inclusion around the globe. Second, the dataset offers a robust empirical foundation for future research on electoral rights, including the interplay with migration policies, democratic governance, integration, and transnational engagement. Third, the MER dataset—comprising both the quantitative data and the accompanying Factbook—establishes new transparency standards by providing the most systematized documentation compared to similar datasets.

In what follows, we describe the design, structure, coding rules, and technical details underpinning MER. We also highlight key trends in the evolution of electoral rights globally.

Methods

MER covers the electoral rights of NCs and NRs in 165 countries between 1960 and 2020. The dataset documents the state of electoral legislation. The unit of analysis is the country- year. In the first step, we determine whether electoral rights—encompassing both candidacy and voting rights—are granted for NCs and NRs. In the second step, we code the eligibility criteria, access conditions, and modalities associated with these rights. MER distinguishes between election type and election level. In this regard, this dataset takes inspiration from prior efforts—specifically CER12 and ELECLAW11—while also significantly extending the geographical scope, temporal coverage, and range of indicators.

The dataset captures the existence of electoral rights as prescribed by law. The information for each country-year reflects the state of legislation in effect on 1 January of that year.

For example, if a parliament passes a change to the electoral law in November 1997 that takes legal effect on 15 December 1997, then MER records the reform as of 1998. The coding does not consider whether specific legislation has been implemented. Users interested in implementation may wish to consult EVRR16. This dataset, however, does not cover non-citizen residents. MER codes laws on the books for two reasons: first, whether migrants are publicly recognized as members of the electorate is a matter of legal norms more than of their implementation; second, coding the implementation of laws across countries needs to rely strongly on expert judgments and is therefore necessarily less transparent than coding legal text. MER also does not code whether elections are free and fair. Datasets that cover such indicators, such as Varieties of Democracy (V-Dem)22, can easily be combined with MER. As explained below, MER does, however, exclude countries that V-Dem has consistently coded as closed autocracies without multi-party elections. In such a context, elections do not exist or are meaningless.

Sample

Our sample provides a global perspective on migrant electoral inclusion from 1960 to 2020. The sample selection is based on coding by V-Dem22. Specifically, we loaded the full V-Dem dataset and applied the exclusion criteria below. (see replication code: MER Sample.r).

This yielded a sample of 163 countries. In addition, we included the Bahamas and Belize (which are not coded in V-Dem). The two countries are scored as free and described as democracies by Freedom House. After applying all exclusion criteria, our sample comprises 165 countries, with mostly balanced coverage across the five continents. We applied the cut-off criteria uniformly by region. In some regions the percentages of countries not covered is somewhat higher, such as in North Africa and the Middle East because of higher levels of authoritarianism, and in the Caribbean and Oceania, due to the prevalence of small states. The dataset also excludes certain election types. We do not cover elections below the municipal level (e.g., district or parochial elections in larger cities). Executive elections are included only when citizens participate in direct elections; for instance, we exclude cases where heads of state or government are elected by parliaments. Additionally, popular elections to other public offices, such as judges or police officers, are not covered. Our focus is limited to general elections and excludes elections for special consultative bodies, such as elected Ausländerbeiräte for NCs in Germany or the Assemblée des Français de l’étranger for NRs in France.

Sources

The dataset’s information is compiled from multiple sources. Wherever possible, we rely on the relevant legal texts, such as constitutions and electoral laws. For earlier periods or where legal documents were unavailable online, we used additional sources, including electoral authorities, secondary literature, and input from country experts. To ensure transparency, we accompany our dataset with a Factbook of roughly 400 pages [https://cadmus.eui.eu/server/api/core/bitstreams/aa629006-6854-4f30-83e8-4e1db1aa241a/content]. This additional documentation covers all countries in our sample in alphabetical order. For each country, we first provide a general overview of the electoral structure, clarifying the relevant levels and types of election. We then specify for which years, if any, electoral rights were available for NCs and NRs. Finally, we specify the conditions attached to these rights.

Whenever possible, we direct readers to additional secondary sources for each country. This transparent documentation will not only allow for replications of our work but also serves as a valuable starting point for case studies and small-N comparisons.

Coding structure

The coding follows a hierarchical structure (see Fig. 1). At the highest level, the dataset differentiates between two categories of potential voters: NCs and NRs. Unlike IDEA13 or ELECLAW11, we do not measure out-of-country voting by resident citizens who are only temporarily absent. Although there might be cases where long-term NRs are still registered in the country of citizenship, we distinguish those who have their principal residence abroad from absentee resident citizens. The second level accounts for the national, regional, and local government tiers. In countries with more than one regional tier, only the tier with the greatest political authority is included. The third level in the hierarchy distinguishes between legislative elections, executive elections, and referendums. At the local level, elections for municipal assemblies are treated as legislative elections, even when municipalities are constitutionally limited to administrative roles. In defining the executive, we adopt a contextual approach, acknowledging that in some countries the local executive will be the mayor, while in others, the local executive comes in the form of an executive council or a combination of both. Where there is variation in whether a directly elected body is present at the local level, we code the rights for locations where such bodies exist. We code referendums in which the general electorate participates, independently of whether they are called by governments, parliaments or emerge from a popular initiative. We do not distinguish between referendums where the scope of the franchise depends on the matter to be decided. In some Swiss cantons, for example, NCs can vote in subnational referendums but not when these referendums address a constitutional matter. The next level in our hierarchical structure differentiates the right to vote from the right to run as a candidate. This distinction does not apply to referendums.

The final level of our coding structure captures the specific conditions of eligibility, access, and modality attached to voting or candidacy rights. Eligibility conditions define broad categories of potential voters and candidates, as explained below. Access conditions refer to the specific hurdles that eligible individuals must overcome to exercise their right to vote. Restrictions that also apply to resident citizens—such as those based on age, criminal record, or mental disability—are not considered. Modality conditions encompass provisions that do not impact the inclusiveness of electoral rights, such as mandatory voting (for both categories) and special representation or vote aggregation rules (for NRs only).

This hierarchy directly informs the labeling system for variables. Variable names are composed of characters that represent voter category, election level, election type, and electoral rights. The variable NRNREV, for example, denotes non-resident citizens [NR], the national level [N], referendums [RE], and voting rights [V]. Similarly, the variable NCLLECRES indicates non-citizen residents (NC), the local level (L), legislative elections (LE), candidacy rights (C), and the residence-based conditions (RES) attached to those candidacy rights. General coding rules

Our coding distinguishes between basic variables that indicate whether a specific electoral right exists and condition variables that capture under which conditions NRs or NCs can exercise this right. A positive value on the former variables indicates the presence of migrant electoral rights; a zero value for eligibility and access conditions means that the right is broadly available to NRs or NCs without restrictions. By contrast, modality conditions capture features of electoral rights that cannot be ranked in terms of inclusiveness.

We first determine whether a country’s laws make voting and candidacy rights available for NRs or NCs in an election at a specific level (national, regional or local) and of a specific type (legislative, executive or referendum). The most basic information about whether an electoral right exists is contained in the variables ending in V (for voting rights) and C (for candidacy rights). A code “0” for these basic variables refers to the absence of a right. A code of “1” or higher indicates that the right exists. Users should consult the detailed Codebook for the specific meaning of the variables of interest to them. For example, for national and regional legislative elections in bicameral systems, we additionally differentiate—for countries where both chambers are directly elected—whether rights are available for both chambers (“2”) or only one of them (“1”). If a particular electoral right exists, we also code the applicable eligibility, access, and modality conditions. Our coding scheme captures the most common eligibility conditions.

For NRs, these include restrictions based on prior residence, profession, or mode of citizenship (restrictions for dual citizens or for naturalized citizens). For NCs, we cover eligibility requirements referring to prior residence duration, permanent residency status, and specific nationalities of origin. There are rare cases of unusual eligibility conditions. For example, in Poland (1990–1998), NC voting rights at the local level were restricted to stateless persons. Such exceptional cases are not captured by our coding but are recorded in the accompanying Factbook. Where electoral rights are granted only to those meeting such exceptional eligibility conditions, we do not code these countries as positive cases. In other cases, there may be additional eligibility conditions that are rarely used and thus excluded in our coding scheme. For example, in Burkina Faso, NCs have local voting rights if they have at least 10 years of residence, a legally recognized profession, and no arrears with their income tax payments. In such a case, we code only the residence condition but not the additional ones. Eligibility restricted to specific professions is coded for NRs, where the condition occurs more frequently, but not for NCs, for whom this restriction is highly exceptional. If an electoral right does not exist, the condition variables attached to these rights are coded as not applicable (“99”). The code “0” indicates the presence of a right and absence of restrictive conditions.

In our dataset, the type of variable is specified by the respective label ending (_bin/_cat/_ord/_con). Most eligibility and access indicators are coded as ordinal variables, and most modality indicators as categorical variables (i.e., their codes are not ranked in terms of inclusiveness). In select cases, we also include continuous variables. Binary indicators (_bin) measure whether a specified right or condition is absent (coded as “0”) or present (“1”). Categorical indicators (_cat) additionally cover several types of conditions that cannot be ranked ordinally (“0” if absent, then “1”, “2”, “3”, and so forth). Ordinal indicators (_ord) rank conditions in an ascending order of restrictiveness with regard to the condition (“0” if the restrictive condition is absent, “1” for the most inclusive type of restriction, “2”, “3”, etc., up to the most restrictive condition). Continuous variables (_con) record numerical values of conditions (e.g., required years of residence). For categorical, ordinal and continuous variables, we also provide additional binary coding for users who just want to know whether a certain condition is absent or present.

The fine-grained coverage of our data allows users to adjust the indicators based on their needs. For example, our binary coding on the V and C variables aims to include as many positive observations of electoral rights for NCs and NRs as possible. In some cases, this means that country-years are coded as positive even if electoral rights exist in only a special territorial subunit. For users who find this approach overly inclusive, we suggest using the ordinal coding instead.

Eligibility conditions

Eligibility conditions delineate the broad categories of potential voters and candidates. That means that when a category of electors—NCs or NRs—has electoral rights, eligibility conditions further specify which subset of NCs or NRs is eligible. Common eligibility conditions are based on residence (for NCs and NRs), permanent residence status (for NCs), nationality (for NCs), profession (for NRs), or discrimination based on mode of citizenship (for NRs). Following this order, we briefly discuss each of the eligibility condition types below. In MER, eligibility restrictions are coded as binary, categorical, ordinal, and—where applicable—continuous variables.

Being eligible for an electoral right is frequently attached to a residence requirement (see Tables S4 and S5). This applies to both NCs and NRs, although the nature of the requirement differs. Often, these residence requirements specify a duration. For NCs, this eligibility requirement usually comes in the form of a minimum number of residence years in the country. In Malawi, for example, NCs can vote at the local and national levels after seven years of residence in the country. For NRs, the residence requirement is often in terms of a maximum number of years spent abroad. For example, the United Kingdom originally applied a five-year restriction to NR electoral rights when these were introduced.

In some cases, however, a minimum amount of time spent abroad is required. An example of this is Mozambique, which requires its NRs to reside at least one year outside the national territory before being able to register and vote from abroad. Elsewhere, the residence condition for NRs relates to their connection to the country of citizenship—in the form of prior residence or birth in the national territory. For example, being eligible to vote in Chilean elections from abroad depends on past residence at any point in time or birth in the territory.

In some countries, electoral rights for NCs are tied to permanent residency status. While the terminology for this status varies by country, we define permanent residency based on two key elements. First, it generally provides indefinite permission to remain in the country, although this may be subject to the condition of not staying outside the country for a prolonged period. Second, permanent residency is granted individually upon application. Even though the specific requirements for obtaining permanent residency vary significantly across countries, it is typically a selective status. Oftentimes, this is based on the length of time a person has lived in the country, making it an indirect residence requirement.

Some countries restrict NC electoral rights to specific nationalities. Our coding distinguishes between electoral rights granted unilaterally to specific other nationalities from those granted on the condition of reciprocity. Thus, we do not determine inclusiveness based on the number of countries whose nationals are enfranchised. While not coded in the dataset, this information is included in the accompanying Factbook. The most inclusive version grants rights to the nationals of specific other states independently of reciprocity—as in the case of the franchise for Commonwealth citizens. The Commonwealth franchise is provided by a small subset of Commonwealth member states, but this is not a formal requirement in the Commonwealth Charter, and these rights are also granted to Commonwealth citizens whose countries do not reciprocate.

In most cases with nationality-based restrictions, electoral rights are granted conditional on reciprocity to the citizens of states that grant similar rights to nationals of the respective country in their territory. Reciprocity comes in three different arrangements. In the first type, electoral rights reciprocity is based on bilateral agreements, such as that between Russia and Kyrgyzstan. In the second type, a state unilaterally grants voting rights to another state’s nationals, regardless of whether the other state has a nationality-based condition. An example is the local franchise in Portugal, which is granted to Norwegian nationals because Portuguese citizens can vote in local elections in Norway, whereas Norway grants local voting rights to all non-citizen long-term residents and not specifically to Portuguese citizens. The third type of reciprocity concerns electoral rights for the citizens of member states residing in another state of a union of independent states. In such cases, reciprocal rights are included in multilateral agreements, such as in the Nordic Passport Union, or in supranational law, as in the EU Treaties.

To allow users different ways of accounting for the union-based franchise—the third reciprocity type—we offer two parallel nationality indicators. The first indicator merges union-based reciprocity with the other two types; it merely distinguishes between unilateral and reciprocity-based extensions of the franchise to specific other nationalities. The second indicator has a separate code for countries that restrict the franchise to union citizens (meaning those with citizenship of another union member state) and those that additionally provide electoral rights to third-country nationals (meaning NCs who are not citizens of another union state). This makes it possible to capture different combinations between a union-based franchise and unilateral or reciprocity-based extensions to third-country nationals adopted by individual states. The ordinal ranking of the second indicator is contestable, so users may want to interpret this variable as a categorical one. The parallel nationality-indicators can be told apart based on the variable names—ending in “1” or “2”, respectively.

Returning to NRs, there are some countries that restrict voting rights to specific professions, often those who reside abroad because of a state mandate. For instance, Ireland and Israel only allow diplomatic and military personnel to vote outside the national territory.

There are other cases, such as Denmark, Fiji, and Ghana, which also include employees of public companies and/or employees of international organizations along with students abroad.

Similar to profession-based discrimination, NR eligibility can be based on factors related to the mode of citizenship. Specifically, we encountered two types of restrictions related to NR citizenship. First, in some cases, it mattered how the citizenship was originally obtained, differentiating between citizenship at birth or citizenship by naturalization. A well-known example that applies also to resident citizens are the offices of the president and vice-president of the United States of America, who must be “natural-born citizens” as specified in the constitution. Second, another citizenship-related restriction for NRs relates to dual citizenship. A few states explicitly exclude dual citizens from voting from abroad or from standing as candidates. For example, in Sudan and Venezuela, NRs may be required to present residence permits identifying them as foreign nationals in the country from where they want to vote.

Access conditions

Access conditions refer to the specific hurdles that eligible individuals must overcome to exercise their right to vote. They capture how easily the right to vote can be exercised. The dataset includes registration requirements (for both NC and NR) and voting methods (only for NR, since these are generally the same for all resident voters). Below, we discuss both of these access conditions. They represent additional enabling or constraining conditions for the exercise of voting rights by those who fulfill all eligibility conditions. For NRs, prior research has pointed to access conditions as one strategy for incumbents to contain or amplify the overseas turnout to their advantage23,24.

Registration is generally required for voting. Our coding captures the varying ease of registration (see Tables S11 and S12). Where registration is automatic, it does not represent a barrier to the exercise of the vote by those who are eligible. For NCs, for example, this would mean that they are automatically added to the electoral register by the relevant authorities once they fulfill eligibility requirements. For NRs, automatic registration can be managed by diplomatic offices overseas. By contrast, active registration requirements can represent steep hurdles. In some cases, active registration is required only once, and voters stay on the electoral register thereafter. In other cases, registration needs to be renewed at regular intervals or even before each election (coded as non-permanent active registration).

In the case of NCs, information on registration procedures in previous decades is often hard to find. Therefore, these indicators will, in many instances, be coded as missing for observations far in the past. For NRs, this problem does not appear to the same extent as their special position means that registration procedures are often explicitly included in the enfranchising legislation.

The available voting method significantly shapes the accessibility of NR voting rights. The most restrictive variant requires non-resident citizens to return to their country of citizenship to vote. For these so-called in-country voting cases, we distinguish between countries where the return travel is publicly subsidized versus privately financed. More inclusive voting methods range from voting at overseas polling stations to mail ballot voting and e-voting.

We code (combinations of) voting methods in an ordinally ranked indicator to capture the ease or cost of voting by NRs, with ‘multiple methods of voting from abroad including e-voting’ as the most flexible practice and ‘in-country OR cross-border voting only, travel not subsidized’ as the most restrictive practice. If a country combines methods for voting from abroad for some of their non-resident voters with in-country voting for other non-resident voters (e.g., Cyprus), we code the most accessible option.

Modality conditions

Modality conditions encompass a residual set of regulations of voting rights that do not impact the inclusiveness of electoral rights. As detailed below, MER covers mandatory voting (for both categories), special representation (for NRs only), and vote aggregation rules (for NRs only). Unlike eligibility and access conditions, modality conditions cannot be ordered in terms of restrictiveness but still shape the right to vote in meaningful ways.

Some countries make voting mandatory. Yet, even in countries where this provision applies to resident citizens, it does not always extend to NCs and NRs. Occasionally, these categories are either exempt from mandatory voting (e.g., Ecuador) or their vote is only mandatory after optional registration (e.g., Luxembourg). We code whether the law specifies legal sanctions for not voting but do not further differentiate whether these sanctions are enforced and how severe they are. This indicator is ranked in order of ascending strictness of the obligation to vote.

Special representation, meaning reserved seats in the legislature for NR representatives, is currently offered by sixteen countries25. The number of seats can be fixed by the electoral law or depend on the demographic size of extraterritorial constituencies23. Most countries with special representation of NRs create distinct extraterritorial constituencies differentiated by world region26. Colombia, Croatia, and the Cook Islands, before the latter abolished NRs voting rights in 2003, put together all external votes in one standalone electoral district.

Another distinct feature of the NR franchise is how votes are aggregated. The most common provision is that countries count the overseas votes in domestic electoral districts, merging them with the domestic votes of resident voters. This is called assimilated voting27.

There are several subtypes of this provision, depending on how external votes are allocated to domestic districts. One version is to count non-resident votes in a district to which the voter is linked through her previous own residence or residence of close family. For instance, if a non-resident Spanish citizen with previous residence in Galicia votes extraterritorially, her vote will be counted in Galicia and not in another Spanish region. Another option is to add non-resident votes to a single electoral district, most likely the capital district (e.g., Warsaw in Poland and Jakarta in Indonesia). The alternative to assimilated voting is to assign the external votes to special extraterritorial districts for NRs, as discussed above.

While vote aggregation rules may appear like a mere technicality, they can determine the potential impact of NRs’ votes on election results.

Special coding rules

Subnational variation

In electoral federations, subnational entities have at least some capacity to set electoral rights. As a result, the type and conditions of electoral rights at regional and local levels are not always consistent across a country’s territory. For local and regional indicators, we go beyond the binary code that simply records whether the right exists or not and capture the territorial coverage of the right across subunits. We differentiate between regular and special subunits. For the purposes of electoral rights indicators, special subunits are either those where elections are held that do not exist in the main national territory or those where there are no elections that are otherwise held in the main national territory. Regular subunits are those where elections of the same kind are held across all such units. We do not consider administrative or cultural territorial divisions of a main national territory where no elections are held at this level, such as the five Danish regions or the 25 Swedish provinces28. The case of special subunit-only elections can be illustrated by Finland, where the Åland Islands are the only subnational territory that holds elections to a provincial assembly29. The US exemplifies the reverse case since subnational elections are held in all 50 states, but not in Washington D.C.30. The United Kingdom might be seen as a case that could be classified either under the former or the latter category. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have their own assemblies/parliaments with devolved powers. While there are no regional elections in England, we consider Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland as special subunits in the United Kingdom and do not have to define England as either a special or a regular territory31. To clarify, this does not mean that special subunits also always have the right to independently determine the boundaries of the franchise. The franchise in regular subnational elections can be set by national/federal law, or the power to define electoral rights can be held by or devolved to subunits.

Unlike indicators that measure the conditions for exercising electoral rights, which are ranked by increasing restrictiveness, indicators for the existence of electoral rights are ranked by increasing inclusiveness, from “4” (maximum inclusiveness) to “0” (no inclusiveness) (see Table S2). Code “0” indicates no electoral rights (for NCs or NRs) in any subunit, “1” means the right exists in some special territories, “2” means the right exists in some but not all regular subunits, “3” means the right exists in all regular subunits but not in special territories, and “4” means the right exists in all subunits of the state.

While we account for subnational variation in the existence of candidacy and voting rights, we do not code the conditions for exercising these rights. We distinguish cases where the existence of a right varies across regular subunits (code “2”) from those where no information is available (code “88”). For example, in Switzerland, cantons decide whether to enfranchise NRs and NCs in cantonal and municipal elections. Since only some of the Swiss cantons have extended voting rights to NCs at the local level, the corresponding indicator, NCLLEV, is coded “2”. We do not code the conditions for exercising subnational electoral rights whenever the relevant legislation is set by a sub-unit; we use instead a special subnational variation code (“77”). This indicates that a provision may vary at the subnational level. To fully capture subnational variation regarding eligibility, access and modality conditions, a separate dataset would be required. Since our basic unit is country-years, we do not compare subnational units to each other but capture subnational variation as a feature of a national franchise regime.

Variation across legislative chambers

Many countries hold direct elections for representatives in two legislative chambers, particularly at the national level. To reflect differences in the franchise across two chambers, we use ordinal coding to capture the electoral rights of NCs and NRs for all directly elected legislative chambers—whether for a unicameral legislature, both chambers of a bicameral system, or only one chamber of a bicameral legislature (see Table S3). We consider the latter case as an important restriction of electoral rights. This distinction concerns mostly NRs. An example is Paraguay, which extends national legislative electoral rights to NRs only in Senate elections but not in those to the Chamber of Deputies. Systems where one of two chambers is not directly elected, such as the British House of Lords, are considered as if they were unicameral.

Where eligibility, access and modality conditions differ between the two chambers, we pragmatically choose the more powerful chamber to code these conditions. Although this strategy may not capture all the variations of electoral rights, coding electoral rights for two chambers as a separate set of variables would only reflect variation in cases of unequal provisions for NCs and NRs. To our knowledge, this variation occurs only in a small subset.

Furthermore, we do not separately code variation of conditions within unicameral systems, where legislative electoral rights for either NCs or NRs differ for nationwide and district-based seats, such as in Ecuador since 2007. In such cases, we code conditions for the larger number of seats in the legislature or for the nationwide constituency if numbers are equal.

Variation across legislative chambers is coded at national and regional levels. Although subnational bicameralism is rare, it has existed in several countries (e.g., Argentina, Australia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Germany, India, Russia and the United States) in the period covered, and some of these countries have allowed NCs and/or NRs to vote and/or run as candidates in subnational elections. Since the code for subnational variation overlaps with the code for variation across legislative chambers, we added a numeric modifier to differentiate the relevant indicators. This applies to variables ending in V and C at regional and local levels. Those indicators ending in “1” capture variation across chambers; those ending in “2” capture subnational variation (e.g., NCRLEV1_ord/bin; NCRLEV2_ord/bin).

Special Indicators for the local EU franchise

All member states of the EU provide some electoral rights for those who are citizens of other EU countries. Local electoral rights are available across all member states; some additionally provide electoral rights at the substate regional tier. Beyond the EU franchise, some countries have additionally extended electoral rights to so-called third-country nationals, meaning those NCs who do not hold EU citizenship.

Countries that provide both the EU franchise and a third-country franchise generally apply different conditions to them. In previous cross-country comparative research, scholars have sometimes chosen to code only the third-country franchise18.

To facilitate the separate study of the EU franchise and the third-country franchise, we code an additional set of variables that cover only the rights available to citizens of other member states. The labels of these variables, coded at the local and regional levels, end with the EU suffix. The EU variables mirror the general coding structure except that the nationality condition variable is omitted (since this franchise, by definition, applies to citizens of other EU member states). We code these indicators as of 1 January 1994 (the Maastricht Treaty entered into force on 1 November 1993). For countries that joined the EU after the first of January, we only code these variables from their first full year of membership. For example, for states that became members as part of the expansion on 1 May 2004, the EU indicators are coded from 2005 onward. In all other cases—prior to Maastricht, prior to joining, or states that are not part of the European Union—these indicators are coded as not applicable.

That said, in countries where NC voting rights exist only for non-national EU citizen residents, the general variables (i.e., those without the EU suffix) also reflect the state of their rights. This is depicted in Table 2. Excluding the special voting rights for non- national EU citizen residents from consideration would distort the comparison with non-EU countries where we also sometimes find voting rights for the nationals for specific other countries. However, users should consider that in the general variables, we code electoral rights in EU countries for the default category of ‘third-country nationals’ where these exist, while we code voting rights for EU citizens where there are no such rights for third-country nationals. This is especially important in cases where countries first introduce electoral rights for non-national EU citizen residents and subsequently also extend electoral rights to NCs that are not from other EU member states. To recognize this shift, users must pay attention to changes in the second nationality variable. This is important to stress as there would otherwise be the risk of misunderstanding such an extension of electoral rights as a restriction of eligibility conditions. This is because stricter eligibility rules usually apply for NCs from non-EU countries than to those from EU countries. For instance, the required residence duration in the general code might shift from 0 to 5 years. This, nonetheless, is not a tightening of rules as nothing changed for EU voters with the residence requirement remaining at 0 years; instead, third-country nationals were newly enfranchised with the eligibility condition of 5 years of prior residence.

Data Records

The GLOBALCIT Dataset of Migrant Electoral Rights (MER), hosted at the CADMUS repository of the European University Institute, is publicly accessible32. The dataset is provided in.CSV format. It is structured as a longitudinal country-year dataset, spanning 61 years (1960–2020) and includes data on electoral rights for NCs and NRs across 165 countries.

Dataset accessibility

MER is publicly accessible and open access, accompanied by a detailed Codebook as well as documentation in the form of a Factbook. Researchers are encouraged to use, critique, and build upon this resource.

Each observation in the main dataset is uniquely identified by country-year and includes binary, categorical, ordinal and continuous variables indicating the presence or absence of migrant voting and candidacy rights as well as additional variables capturing eligibility, access and modality conditions. The dataset follows FAIR principles, ensuring it is Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable, with a clear versioning system for updates. Technical Validation

We prepared the detailed Codebook and the general and special coding rules for migrant electoral rights with great care, drawing on prior contributions and existing datasets (i.e., ex-ante content validation). This section reports ex-post validation. Specifically, we first conducted technical checks of MER for data integrity, consistency, structural conformity, and encoding issues. We then performed convergent validation by comparing voting-rights indicators in MER with EVRR (for NRs) and ELECLAW (for NCs).

An R script for technical validation was used to ensure data integrity. Missing values were systematically treated as ‘88’ where appropriate, ensuring the dataset remained complete for core analyses. Only three variables measuring years of residence requirements for NRs (NRNLEVRES_con, NRNEXVRES_con, and NRNREVRES_con) were created solely for Australia, Canada, and the United States. Consequently, we moved them to the end of the dataset and refrained from recoding ‘NA’ values as ‘88’ for the other countries.

We also conducted a review for duplicate rows and columns, identifying and removing a small set of duplicates from the merging of country-specific coding sheets. After implementing minor adjustments, re-running the technical validation R script confirmed no inconsistencies across rows and columns.

Furthermore, we ensured unique column names throughout the dataset. To prevent duplication, numerical suffixes were added to interrelated indicators (e.g., NCRLECNAT1_bin and NCRLECNAT2_bin). Given that MER indicators are primarily binary, categorical, and ordinal, we did not conduct an outlier detection check.

Comparison between EVRR and MER

We chose to compare our dataset with EVRR, as that is the most extensive existing dataset on NRs voting rights. The comparison between EVRR and MER was successfully conducted, revealing over 90 percent alignment between the datasets. To maintain the accuracy of this comparison, dataset observations coded as “88” and “99” in MER or as missing values ‘NA’ in EVRR were excluded to avoid distortion. For better standardization, three MER variables—representing voting rights at the national legislative (NRNLEV bin), national executive (NRNEXV), and national referendums (NRNREV)—were combined into a standalone variable ‘NRNV’ comprising NR voting rights at the national level. This was then matched against ‘evrr_dejure,’ resulting in a high level of consistency between the two datasets and affirming the reliability of MER’s coding structure. This raw comparison yielded an alignment of more than 85 percent.

Discrepancies are partly due to differences in the type of NR suffrage coded. Specifically, MER and EVRR differ in that the latter does not code cases where only in-country voting is available and where voting is restricted to NRs holding specific professions. We therefore conduct a second comparison, drawing on our access and eligibility indicators, to ensure that the definition of positive cases matches between our datasets. When “NRNLEV bin,” “NRNEXV,” and “NRNREV” exclude NRs traveling home to vote or restrictive eligibility conditions limiting voting rights to specific groups (e.g., diplomats, military personnel, civil servants abroad), the alignment between EVRR and MER reaches more than 90 percent.

The remaining mismatches between EVRR and MER can, in part, be attributed to differences in the timing of when an observation is coded as positive. Specifically, EVRR codes the provision in the same year it is enacted and regulated (de jure enfranchisement), while MER follows a more legal approach, coding the provision as positive only starting from January 1 of the following year. For instance, in Albania (2020), EVRR records the adoption of a provision allowing NRs to vote in national legislative elections, while MER does not. If MER had coded 2021, this observation would have been recorded as ‘1’. Overall, this high degree of correspondence speaks to the quality of both datasets.

Comparison between ELECLAW and MER

On the side of NCs, we chose ELECLAW as the most meaningful point of comparison. ELECLAW is particularly suitable due to its fine-grained coding structure that also allows us to differentiate between rights for NCs overall and those specific to so-called third-country nationals (for EU countries). We performed the comparison for the year 2015, the year for which ELECLAW’s geographic coverage is greatest and includes 50 countries. While ELECLAW is limited to Europe, the Americas, and Oceania, these world regions include most positive cases of NCs voting rights.

Specifically, we compared electoral rights for local legislative elections. First, we compared NCs voting rights in general, including the franchise exclusively available to EU citizens. For this step, we used the indicator ‘VNC.LOLE’ from ELECLAW and ‘NCLLEV_bin’ from MER. The only mismatch between our two datasets is Australia, which continues to provide voting rights for those non-citizen residents who were on the electoral roll as British subjects before the cut-off date of 26 January 1984. In ELECLAW, this is coded as a positive case. In MER, we do not code cases where voting rights are granted solely based on a grandfather clause. Instead, our coding prioritizes the generally prevalent suffrage rules.

In a second step, we concentrated on EU member states and the franchise available to third-country nationals. Here, we used the indicator ‘VNCTCN.LOLE’ from ELECLAW and ‘NCLLEV_bin’ combined with ‘NCLLEVNAT2_ord’ from MER. We used the nationality-based eligibility indicator to include only those cases as positive where the NCs franchise is available beyond union citizens. This means that we adapted our coding to match what ELECLAW refers to as third-country national indicators. This second comparison yielded perfect alignment between the two datasets.
