Mucin armoured diethylaminoethyl-dextran cloaked rebamipide based oral nanoformulation for the therapy of inflammatory bowel disease
Authors: Chandrashekhar Jori, Anas Ahmad, Ajay Kumar, Nemat Ali, Young-Ok Son, Rehan Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOne of the major challenges in the treatment of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is the development of an oral drug delivery system that can provide extended GI residence time. In this work, a nanoformulation formed by Rebamipide (Re) encapsulated into mucin DEAE-dextran armored nanoparticles (M@D@Re-SCNPs) is introduced, which utilizes the core of caprylic acid (C)/6-O-stearoyl ascorbic acid (S). These M@D@Re-SCNPs were fabricated to provide both gastro-resistant and mucoadhesion properties of the nanoparticles. The M@D@Re-SCNPs showed pH-independent structural integrity in the gastric environment and proved to have a good mucosal binding capacity in the colon for long intestinal retention of up to 48 h. In terms of therapeutic efficacy, post-oral administration of M@D@Re-SCNPs strongly reduce the colon inflammatory status and the expression of inflammatory biomarkers like nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB), cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2), inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) when compared with free rebamipide. The trapped rebamipide inhibits the production of pro-inflammatory mediators and promotes the production of mucin 2 (Muc2) protein, which promotes colon mucosal regeneration and goblet cell viability. Based on the necessity of controlling drug release and regulation of drug action for complete IBD treatment, we believe that the M@D@Re-SCNPs are a promising drug delivery system for UC treatment.Keywords: Ulcerative colitis, Mucin-based nanoparticles, Rebamipide, Inflammation, DEAE-Dextran biomucoadhesive

Introduction

IBD (ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn's disease (CD)) is a chronic inflammatory condition of the gastrointestinal tract, which causes a failure of the epithelial barrier, immune system dysregulation and chronic inflammation of the mucosa [1,2]. IBD is a growing problem in the world, impacting more than 6.8 million people worldwide and resulting in a significant burden of disease for health care [3]. A complex network of genetic susceptibility, environment, oxidative stress, microbial dysbiosis, and intestinal mucosal homeostasis are all involved in the pathogenesis of IBD [4,5]. Continued intestinal inflammation can lead to very serious consequences such as fibrosis, colorectal cancer and poor health, making it essential to find better treatment strategies.

The current therapeutic approaches for IBD are primarily based on the use of corticosteroids, aminosalicylates, antibiotics, immunosuppressants and biologics [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. While these treatments can have a positive effect on the inflammation and induce a remission, their chronic efficacy is constrained by systemic side effects, the need for frequent dosing, lack of patient compliance and poor localization at the sites of inflammation in the intestine. The preferred route of administration is oral due to its convenience and non-invasiveness[[10], [11], [12], [13]]. Nevertheless, introduction of the drugs orally into the gastrointestinal tract is met with significant physiological hurdles, such as the presence of high levels of gastric acid, presence of enzymes, rapid gastrointestinal transit, mucus turnover and insufficient retention at inflamed sites in the colon[[14], [15], [16]]. Furthermore, diarrhoea-induced hypermotility also decreases the time spent in the gastrointestinal tract, resulting in suboptimal therapeutic responses [17,18]. For this reason, it is highly desirable to develop an oral drug delivery system with mucoadhesive and gastro-resistant characteristics that will allow for extended retention time in the intestine and provide effective treatment of IBD.

Rebamipide is among emerging therapeutic candidates which have garnered significant interest due to its anti-inflammatory, antioxidant and mucosal protective activity [19,20]. Rebamipide has been clinically approved as a gastric ulcer therapeutic agent and has been demonstrated to promote goblet cell proliferation and mucin secretion and to inhibit inflammatory signaling pathways. However, these therapeutic benefits have not yet been appreciated in the use of rebamipide in colonic inflammatory disorders because of its low oral bioavailability and inadequate retention in the lower colon. For this reason, to enhance the stability, retention and sustained release at inflamed intestinal sites, an optimal nanocarrier system must be developed for an effective delivery of rebamipide. In the treatment of IBD, nanotechnology delivery systems have been shown to be potentially beneficial for targeting and enhancing mucosal adhesion of drugs derived from oral formulations [21,22]. In particular, diethylaminoethyl-dextran (DEAE-dextran), a positively charged polysaccharide derivative, has been reported to have high electrostatic affinity with the negatively charged inflamed colonic mucosa with high content in sialic acid residues [21,22]. This can greatly improve the adhesion of nanoparticles and their residence time at inflammatory sites. But DEAE-dextran-coated systems may suffer from premature degradation under acidic gastric environment and may reduce their oral delivery efficiency. To overcome this limitation, a number of mucin based protective coatings have recently been attracted due to their good biocompatibility and gastro protection performance [23,24]. Gastric mucin can serve as a temporary barrier to prevent degradation of nanoparticles in the stomach, while allowing their release in the intestine where there is a different pH profile, thus exposing the mucoadhesive layer.

To develop an oral IBD therapy, we first designed a mucin (M)-armored DEAE-dextran (D) coated rebamipide (Re) loaded nanoparticles (M@D@Re-SCNPs). Caprylic acid (C) and 6-O-stearoyl ascorbic acid (S), the components of the nanoparticle (NP) core, are anti-inflammatory and antioxidant compounds, which are important in intestinal inflammation[[24], [25], [26], [27]]. The outer mucin layer was responsible for gastroprotection and the DEAE-dextran inner layer for the pH responsive mucoadhesion to inflamed colonic tissues. We hypothesized that this combinatorial approach would yield increased gastrointestinal stability, increase intestinal residence time, and increase the delivery of rebamipide to the gastrointestinal tract for effective treatment of colitis.

Materials and techniques used in experiments

Materials

Gastric mucin (porcine, Type III, HiMedia, CAS 84082-64-4) was used as a high molecular weight (∼2–4 MDa) glycoprotein with ∼80% carbohydrate content. It contains O-linked oligosaccharides rich in GalNAc, GlcNAc, galactose, fucose, and sialic acid, and exhibits mucoadhesive properties relevant for drug delivery. DEAE-dextran (Sigma-Aldrich, Merck, MW ∼40 kDa, CAS 9015-73-0) is a cationic derivative of dextran with ∼10–20% diethylaminoethyl substitution. It is water-soluble, positively charged at physiological pH, and commonly used to enhance drug complexation and cellular uptake. Rebamipide (Re, TCI; CAS 90098-04-7), 6-O-Stearoyl ascorbic acid (TCI; CAS 10605-09-1) is a lipophilic ester of ascorbic acid and stearic acid, used as an amphiphilic antioxidant [26]. Its lipid solubility enhances membrane interaction and formulation stability. Caprylic acid (TCI; CAS 124-07-2), also known as octanoic acid, is a medium-chain saturated fatty acid with amphiphilic properties [24]. It functions as penetration enhancer in lipid-based and drug delivery systems. Alcian blue, Toluidine blue, Griess Reagent and N-methoxysuccinyl-Ala-Ala-Pro-Val p-nitroanilide system were purchased from Alfa Aesar. Primary antibodies NF-kB p65, PARP-γ, MIP-1α, and MUC 2 were purchased from cloud clone. Potassium chloride (KCl), sodium chloride (NaCl), potassium dihydrogen phosphate (KH2PO4), disodium hydrogen phosphate (Na2HPO4), and paraformaldehyde etc. were purchased from Himedia Laboratories. All standard chemicals employed in this study were of high-quality research or analytical grade.

Methods

Preparation and synthesis of mucin/DEAE-dextran armored M@D@Re-SCNPs

To prepare rebamipide-loaded anionic nanoparticles (Re-SCNPs), rebamipide was added to a mixture of 6-stearoyl ascorbic acid and caprylic acid, which were dissolved in a 4:1 (w/w) ratio using a chloroform:methanol (2:1, v/v) solvent system. The solvent was then evaporated under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator to form a thin lipid film. The dried film was hydrated with PBS (pH 7.4) sonication (probe sonicator, 30% amplitude) to obtain a homogeneous nanoparticle suspension. Unencapsulated rebamipide was removed by centrifugation (15,000 rpm, 30 min, 4°C), and the resulting Re-SCNPs were characterized further. For DEAE-dextran (D) coating, the purified Re-SCNPs (1 mg/mL in PBS) were incubated with DEAE-dextran solution (2 mg/mL in PBS) at a 1:1 (w/w) ratio under gentle stirring (300 rpm, 1–2 h, RT). Unbound DEAE-dextran was removed by centrifugation (15,000 rpm, 30 min, 4°C), and the coated nanoparticles (D@Re-SCNPs) were resuspended in PBS for further characterization and stability studies. Successful coating was confirmed by zeta potential analysis and lyophilized. In order to armor the D@Re-SCNPs with mucin, 1% (w/v) mucin was slowly added into the 1:1 ratio of the D@Re-SCNPs blend and stirred slowly at 700 rpm for 120 min. Finally, mucin/DEAE-dextran armored rebamipide loaded nanoparticles (M@D@Re-SCNPs) collected by centrifugation and uncoated mucin, i.e. D@Re-SCNPs, were removed. Characterization methods

Techniques

The hydrodynamic diameter, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential of blank SCNPs and rebamipide-loaded M@D@Re-SCNPs (n = 3) were measured at 25°C using dynamic light scattering (DLS) and Phase Analysis Light Scattering (PALS) techniques with a Zetasizer Nano ZSP (Model ZEN5600; Malvern Instruments Ltd., UK). Each measurement was performed in triplicate. Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectra of Rebamipide, DEAE-dextran, Mucin, 6-stearoyl Ascorbic Acid, Caprylic Acid, and M@D@Re-SCNPs (5 mg each) were recorded using a Cary Agilent 660 spectrophotometer over 400–4000 cm−1 with 256 scans at 4 cm−1 resolution. For morphological assessment, Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FESEM) was conducted using a JEOL JSM-7600F microscope after drop-casting aqueous samples onto silicon wafers and drying overnight. Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) was performed using a JEOL JEM-2100 microscope operated at 120 kV; samples were prepared by drop-casting aqueous suspensions on carbon-coated copper grids and air-drying overnight. Detailed protocols of techniques and release kinetics protocols are provided in the Supporting information.

Cytocompatibility of M@D@Re-SCNPs

The cytocompatibility of M@D@B-SCNPs (rebamipide-free) and M@D@Re-SCNPs (with rebamipide) was evaluated using the MTT assay (3-[4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl]-2,5-diphenyl tetrazolium bromide) on immortalized normal human foreskin fibroblasts (hTERT-BJ) and Caco-2 cells, following previously established methods [28]. Briefly, cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well in a 96-well culture plate and exposed to varying concentrations of M@D@B-SCNPs and M@D@Re-SCNPs (with rebamipide) for 24 and 48 h. Cell viability was measured using the EZcount™ MTT Cell Assay Kit (Himedia) per the manufacturer's guidelines. After 3–4 h of incubation with MTT solution, 100 μL of solubilization buffer was used to dissolve the resultant formazan crystals. An ELISA plate analyzer was used to measure absorbance at 590 nm to determine the percentage of viable cells. Cell viability of untreated control cells was considered 100% for normalization.

Assessment of anti-inflammatory activity of M@D@Re-SCNPs

The anti-inflammatory potential of M@D@Re-SCNPs was investigated using a lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced inflammation model in vitro. To develop an inflammatory cellular model, RAW 264.7 cells were stimulated for 24 h with LPS (1 μg/mL); afterwards, anti-inflammatory therapeutic activity of the different nanoparticle formulations was evaluated. An inflammatory marker, nitrite formation, was quantified using the Griess reagent, with absorbance measured at 540 nm [26]. This assay enabled the evaluation of the M@D@Re-SCNPs effectiveness in reducing inflammation by determining their impact on nitrite levels in the LPS-induced inflammatory response.

Colitis model induction in mice

Swiss albino mice were used to develop ulcerative colitis by administering DSS (4% w/v) in unrestricted drinking water (ad.lib.) for 5 days. Five days after DSS therapy, stool consistency, physical activity, and rectal haemorrhage examination were used to measure colonic inflammation, confirming the establishment of colitis [31].

Assessment of physical parameters

An indicator of disease activity was created considering factors like body weight loss, blood in the stool via fecal occult blood test (FOBT), and stool consistency to evaluate the degree of inflammation in mice caused by DSS. One important factor that was assessed was colon shortening, which is a symptom of severe inflammation. Post-sacrifice assessment of colon length allowed for the evaluation of anti-inflammatory activity of Re and M@D@Re-SCNPs, facilitating quantification of inflammation severity and assessment of therapeutic interventions.

Histological, immunohistochemical, and biochemical assessments

To preserve cellular structure and morphology, the colons and vital organs, including the heart, kidneys, lungs, spleen, and liver, were promptly immersed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 24 h following euthanasia. Following fixation, the tissues were embedded in paraffin and sectioned into thin slices (5 μm thick) using a microtome. Histological evaluations of colonic tissues (5 μm thick) were conducted using Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining to assess epithelial damage, crypt abscesses, and inflammation, and Alcian Blue–Neutral Red (AB-NR) staining to visualize goblet cell depletion and mucosal integrity. High Iron Diamine–Alcian Blue (HID-AB) staining was performed to differentiate mucin types and investigate goblet cell dynamics under inflammatory conditions. Mast cells were identified using toluidine blue staining. Immunohistochemical analysis was conducted to detect the expression of NF-κB p65, PARP-γ, MIP-1α, and MUC2 in paraffin-embedded colonic sections using specific primary antibodies and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. Myeloperoxidase (MPO) activity, an index of neutrophil infiltration, was quantified in colon tissue homogenates and expressed as U/g protein. Nitrite levels, indicative of nitric oxide production, were determined using the Griess reagent with absorbance measured at 540 nm. Total protein concentration in tissue homogenates was measured using the Bradford assay with BSA as the standard. Detailed protocols for each procedure are provided in the Supporting Information.

Evaluation of adhesion properties of M@D@Re-SCNPs using DiR fluorescent dye

In vivo mucoadherence studies, the fluorescent dye DiIC18 (7) (1,1′-dioctadecyl-3,3,3′,3′-tetramethylindotricarbocyanine iodide, DiR) was used as a substitute for M@D@Re-SCNPs. The mice were separated up into two groups: a control group and a DSS-induced colitis group, where colitis was induced using DSS. DiR-loaded M@D@SCNPs was administered orally, and fluorescence in the colon was analyzed post-euthanasia. This experimental design facilitated both in vivo and ex vivo assessment of the mucoadhesion properties of M@D@Re-SCNPs, providing important information on how they interact with colonic mucosa in both healthy and colitis-related circumstances [32].

Single cell suspension of Gut Associated Lymphoid Tissue (GALT) (lamina propria and intraepithelial lymphocytes) from colons

In the study of gut immunity and its role of the M@D@Re-SCNPs in colitis mice, the experiment isolates and analyzes immune cells from gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT) in the colon, focusing on lamina propria (LP) and intraepithelial lymphocytes (IELs). Mechanical disruption and enzymatic digestion release cells, characterized by flow cytometry using specific surface markers. This enables immune cell subset identification, quantification, and functional profiling. Detailed protocol is provided in the Supporting information.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) was used to analyze the data. An unpaired Student's t-test was used for comparisons between two groups. For differences between two or more groups a One-way ANOVA was used with Bonferroni's post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Results and discussion

Preparation of mucin-coated DEAE-dex layered rebamipide-loaded nanoparticles M@D@Re-SCNPs

In this investigation, we developed mucoadhesive and gastroprotective nanoparticles (SCNPs) by thin film hydration and layer-by-layer coating (Fig. 1A). Using the thin film hydration process, the pillars of SCNPs were fabricated by utilizing antifungal, antibacterial, and anti-inflammatory caprylic acid (C) and antioxidant 6-stearoyl ascorbic acid (S). The purpose of this research was to create SCNPs that included the anti-ulcerative and anti-inflammatory drug rebamipide (Re-SCNPs) Fig. 1A. In order to improve mucoadhesion and gastroprotection, the Re-SCNPs were coated with cationic DEAE-dextran (D@Re-SCNPs) after interaction with anionic gastric mucin (M@D@Re-SCNPs), as shown in Fig. 1B. Fig. 1C shows the hypothetical representation of the therapeutic efficacy of the M@D@Re-SCNPs against the colitis model.

) A & B) Preparation of mucin-coated DEAE-dextran layered rebamipide-loaded nanoparticles (M@D@Re-SCNPs) and C) oral administration M@D@Re-SCNPs as a combined therapy against colitis through the restoration of intestinal barriers, reduced proinflammatory cytokine levels, and the upregulation of gut mucin homeostasis.

Characterization of nanoparticles (SCNPs, D@Re-SCNPs and M@D@Re-SCNPs)

A single factor experimental design was used to determine the optimum composition of the nanoparticles by changing the proportion of 6-O-stearoyl ascorbic acid and caprylic acid, while measuring the particle size, PDI, zeta potential and encapsulation efficiency of the nanoparticles. The formulation with 4:1 ratio showed the most optimum physicochemical properties as it had smaller particle size, narrow size distribution and high encapsulation efficiency and colloidal stability, which is most suitable for oral delivery applications (Table ST1-3). DLS was initially utilized to assess the size and polydispersity index (PDI) of blank SCNPs and rebamipide-loaded SCNPs (Re-SCNPs). DLS measurement analysis exhibits 160 ± 3.39 nm, 200 ± 8.66 nm, and 220 ± 15.73 nm of hydrodynamic diameter (particle size) for SCNPs (nanoparticles), D@SCNPs (DEAE-dextran coated nanoparticles), and M@D@SCNPs (mucin layered DEAE-dextran coated nanoparticles) respectively, due to layer-by-layer coating. Upon incorporating rebamipide (Re) into nanoparticles (Re-SCNPs) and subsequently coating them with DEAE-dex (D@Re-SCNPs), followed by mucin (M@D@Re-SCNPs), the mean hydrodynamic diameter exhibits increments to 200 ± 5.60 nm, 233 ± 3.27 nm, and 260 ± 1.92 nm, respectively (Fig. 2A and B, Figure S1). Moreover, Re-SCNPs, D@Re-SCNPs, and M@D@Re-SCNPs display monodispersity, as shown by polydispersity index (PDI) values of 0.314, 0.248, and 0.163 which suggest the lack of agglomeration formation. Zeta potential analysis showed a change from negative (Re-SCNPs −28.9 ± 0.51 mV) to positive (D@Re-SCNPs +38.43 ± 0.46 mV), which may be attributed to DEAE-dextran's positively charged amino groups. This indicates that DEAE-dextran has been coated on the particle surface (D@Re-SCNPs). The formation of M@D@Re-SCNPs by subsequent anionic coating with gastric mucin was confirmed by a zeta potential measurement of −20.1 ± 1.30 mV (Fig. 2A and B). FTIR spectroscopy was employed to verify the chemical structure, component integration, and successful surface modification of the M@D@Re-SCNPs. The FTIR spectrum of DEAE-dextran displayed characteristic absorption bands at approximately 3400 cm−1, attributed to O–H and N–H stretching vibrations, along with peaks at 2920 cm−1 (C–H stretching), 1650 cm−1 (N–H bending), and 1450 cm−1 (C–N stretching). Mucin exhibited distinct peaks near 3400 cm−1 (O–H/N–H), 1630 cm−1 (amide I), 1530 cm−1 (amide II), and 1050 cm−1, corresponding to the glycoprotein backbone's C–O–C stretching. The core lipids, 6-O-stearoyl ascorbic acid and caprylic acid, showed signature ester and fatty acid functional groups. Specifically, 6-O-stearoyl ascorbic acid presented strong absorption at 1735 cm−1 (C=O ester), 2920 cm−1 (CH2 stretching), 1450 cm−1 (C–H bending), and 1150 cm−1 (C–O stretching). Caprylic acid displayed absorption bands at 1700 cm−1 (C=O stretching), 2920/2850 cm−1 (alkyl chain stretching), and 930–950 cm−1 due to alkyl vibrations. For rebamipide, prominent FTIR features were observed at 1650 cm−1 for the amide C=O stretch, with broader bands near 3400 cm−1 related to NH/OH stretching, and additional signals at 1590 cm−1 and 1490 cm−1 indicating aromatic ring vibrations. In the composite M@D@Re-SCNPs spectrum, several characteristic peaks of individual components were retained, though shifts in peak positions and intensity changes were noted. The combined and altered spectral features confirm the successful encapsulation of rebamipide and the layered assembly of mucin and DEAE-dextran onto the nanoparticle surface (Fig. 2C). According to the UV-vis spectroscopy measurement, the drug loading was around 18 ± 0.01% and the encapsulation effectiveness was about 95 ± 0.07% which is impeccable for a rebamipide delivery (Fig. 2D). Given that the M@D@Re-SCNPs are intended for delivering medication regionally on the gastrointestinal membrane, the nanoparticles composition must be stable for sufficient time to gain access to the mucosa that is inflammatory and be disintegrated by the inflammatory cascades near the inflamed colon. The morphology of SCNPs and M@D@Re-SCNPs was evaluated using TEM and FESEM. Both analyses confirmed that the nanoparticles were nearly spherical, monodisperse, and free of agglomeration. SCNPs exhibited an average size of approximately 160 nm, while M@D@Re-SCNPs showed a size increase to around 260 nm, attributed to the sequential coating with DEAE-dextran, gastric mucin, and rebamipide loading. The TEM images further confirmed the successful layering of these materials on the nanoparticle surface (Fig. 2Ea & b).

Biocompatible, digestive enzyme-resistant, in vitro drug release kinetics, cytocompatibility and anti-inflammatory activity

We then gazed at the impact of the chemical environment on the gastrointestinal (GI) tract, which includes intestinal fluid and gastric acid, as well as the physiological procedure of peristalsis. To evaluate the pH-responsive disintegrating properties, we incubate the samples first in 1.2 pH simulated gastric fluid (Ga) and then in 6.8 pH PBS. We have used FESEM and TEM photomicrographs to assess the structural alterations. After SCNPs were incubated for 2 h at the pH 1.2 Ga, observable modifications in their structure and integrity were evident. Conversely, M@D@Re-SCNPs, post-coating, exhibited no discernible changes in morphology, maintaining a stable spherical shape and integrity at pH 1.2 (Fig. 3A). On the other hand, at the simulated intestinal fluid (In) pH 6.8, SCNPs began to lose their form after just 2 h, while M@D@Re-SCNPs started to swallow and disintegrate the mucin layer in 1 h. The DEAE-dextran coating layer swelled in an alkaline environment [33], increasing nanoparticle size. The M@D@Re-SCNPs slowly started to lose their shape and integrity after 4 h of incubation in simulated intestinal fluid, which would result in the cargo release (Fig. 3B). These findings indicated the gastroprotective efficacy of M@D@Re-SCNPs and cargo released in the specific inflamed colon vicinity, which is crucial for oral nanoformulation. Re-SCNPs and M@D@Re-SCNPs were utilized in vitro rebamipide release experiments under simulated gastric and gastrointestinal conditions. In Ga, Re-SCNPs demonstrated rapid rebamipide dissolution and rate of release within 2 h. In Re-SCNPs, the rebamipide was enclosed in a lipid-polymer matrix that may disintegrate quickly in an acidic environment. The release kinetics of free rebamipide were utilized for comparison with Re-SCNPs. The release kinetics indicated a slight delay in rebamipide release from Re-SCNPs compared to free rebamipide. This slower release observed in Re-SCNPs can be attributed to the encapsulation of rebamipide within the nanoparticle core, which is composed of 6-stearoyl ascorbic acid and caprylic acid. These core components create a barrier that moderates the diffusion of rebamipide; resulting in a slightly delayed release profile compared to free rebamipide i.e. Re-SCNPs was also unstable in the gastric environment. In M@D@Re-SCNPs, the release profiles demonstrated that in all samples, the DEAE-dextran and mucin coating thwarted drug release at the Ga (pH 1.2). It again confirms the gastroprotective behaviour of M@D@Re-SCNPs. There were notable variations in the rebamipide release behaviour at inflamed colonic pH 6.8 [34], with a slow release continuing for 4 h, as demonstrated in Fig. 3C. Consequently, the release of Re from M@D@Re-SCNPs was extended for up to 84 h, and about 80% of the Re was released from M@D@Re-SCNPs in 84 h (Fig. 3C). When treating IBD, a controlled release of drugs is preferred as it offers an advantage in reaching the appropriate concentration needed for targeted action and sustaining it for an extended amount of time within the intestine or colon. Further in vitro release measurements were conducted in the absence and presence of β-glucosidase enzyme in different gastrointestinal and colonic pH mediums to assess the pH-responsive and enzyme-triggered release profile of M@D@Re-SCNPs (Fig. S3). The nanoformulation showed very low release of rebamipide when pH was 5.0 and 5.5, while the release was gradually accelerated and sustained with the increase in pH. Interestingly, at the pH 6.8 (closely resembling the inflamed colonic environment) the maximum cumulative drug release was observed. In addition, the presence of β-glucosidase significantly promoted the release of rebamipide at all the investigated pH values, suggesting that the coated layers were degraded under the influence of the enzyme, resulting in the release of the cargo under the simulated colonic microflora conditions. The present results further confirm the gastrointestinal stability, colon-targeted release capacity and extended therapeutic potential of M@D@Re-SCNPs in oral therapy of IBD. M@D@B-SCNPs (without Re) and M@D@Re-SCNPs were screened for cytocompatibility against normal hTERT-BJ (human skin fibroblast BJ) cells and Caco-2 cells at 24- and 48-h intervals to make sure the healthy cells weren't being impacted by the biomaterial. Based on the non-significant change in cell viability, we noticed that both nanoparticles till 200 μg/mL dosage were not damaging, cytocompatible to hTERT-BJ and Caco-2 cells and had negligible effects (Fig. 3D and F). Alternatively, at an elevated concentration of 400 μg/mL at both time points, there was a marginal decline in cell viability observed, although this reduction was not statistically significant. Our results showed that a dosage of up to 200 μg/mL of nanoparticles could be considered safe for normal cells. M@D@Re-SCNPs prolonged in vivo gastrointestinal residence abilities

We anticipate that our mucoadhesive and spherical M@D@Re-SCNPs can adjust to the GI tract's fluid content, complex motility and sharp pH variation and form a long-lasting, large-area coating on the intestinal mucosa that ensures enough intestinal holding for prolonged drug release. Since inflamed colonic mucus contains negatively charged sialic acid substructures, DEAE-dextran's positively charged primary amines interact with them to provide targeted and facile colonic absorption. Due to microbial enzymatic degradation [35], the mucin layer of M@D@Re-SCNPs at the inflammatory site becomes disrupted, exposing the cationic DEAE-dextran surface, which adheres to the inflammatory colitis area by electrostatic interaction. This notion was further tested by assessing in vivo M@D@Re-SCNPs propensity to behave as biomucoadhesive coatings on the gastrointestinal mucosa. Control and DSS group mice were given a single oral gavage of M@D@DiR-SCNPs (modified with DiR fluorescent dye) to assess the fluorescence retention in the extracted GI tract. The mice were sacrificed after 0, 6, 24, and 48 h. The M@D@DiR-SCNPs in the DSS group stalled in the mice's GI tract for at least 48 h, as shown by the strong fluorescence signal consistent with the in-vitro biomucoadhesive characteristic. On the other hand, in the control group, after one day, the M@D@DiR-SCNPs showed minimal holding in the GI tract, as shown by the quantified fluorescence intensity (Fig. 5A and B). Regular stomach emptying occurs in 2 h, while colonic arrival usually occurs in 5 h in humans [36]. Thus, our M@D@DiR-SCNPs were shown to be retained in the gastrointestinal tract for a long time in the in vivo intestinal retention assays. One of the most essential aspects of oral nanomedicine for treating colitis is their ability to withstand the harsh conditions of the gastrointestinal tract and to remain stalled sufficiently long in the colon. To reach the colon, armored M@D@Re-SCNPs successfully resisted the gastric and intestinal environmental conditions. Additionally, the negatively charged surface of the Re-SCNPs and M@D@Re-SCNPs allows them to adhere to the positively charged proteins seen in an inflammatory colon like antimicrobial peptides and transferrin that employs covalent linkage, hydrogen bonding, cation-π interaction, and electrostatic interaction to interact with colitis inflamed colonic site [37] (Fig. 5C).

In the Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) stained colon sections, the DSS + M@D@B-SCNPs (without rebamipide) group demonstrated pronounced morphological damage compared to the other groups, particularly those treated with rebamipide-loaded lipid polymeric nanoparticles (M@D@Re-SCNPs), control, colitic mice, and free rebamipide groups. In the DSS + M@D@B-SCNPs group, there was significant epithelial disruption, crypt loss, and inflammatory cell infiltration, similar to the untreated colitic mice, indicating that the M@D@B-SCNPs lacked therapeutic effects. In contrast, the M@D@Re-SCNPs group showed marked histological improvements, including preserving crypt structure, reducing inflammatory cell infiltration, and restoring the epithelial lining. The differences observed under H&E staining highlighted the therapeutic role of rebamipide in mitigating colonic damage and inflammation, which was absent in the M@D@B-SCNPs group, reinforcing the importance of the rebamipide loaded nanoparticles, i.e. M@D@Re-SCNPs in protecting colon morphology (Fig. 7A). M@D@Re-SCNPs alleviate the signs associated with colitis by reducing innate immune reactions

The two general categories of macrophage activation are proinflammatory (M1) and anti-inflammatory (M2) polarisation [53] as well as macrophages, which express a wide range of chemokines and cytokines, are necessary to keep the mucosa in a healthy state. [42]. Many investigations emphasize the importance of the NF-κB pathway in suppressing the synthesis of cytokines in UC individuals. Furthermore, the NF-κB pathway is essential to immunological responses and gastrointestinal inflammation. Extensive colonic inflammation reactions are caused by an excess of cytokines (proinflammatory) and inadequate levels of anti-inflammatory agents. Interestingly, NF-κBp65 is elevated in a number of cellular levels in UC individuals, such as intestine mucosal epithelium, lamina propria monocytes, and crypt epithelial cells. In the nucleus, NF-κBp65 expression is noticeably more important than in the cytoplasm. In the pathophysiology of UC, interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), nuclear factor erythroid 2–related factor 2 (Nrf2), phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K), peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-gamma (PPAR-γ), inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), and cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2), human macrophage inflammatory protein-1 alpha (MIP-1α) play a pivotal role as inflammatory mediators and have been traced to the severity and advancement of the illness in UC. According to immunohistochemistry investigations, colitis scenarios had more significant factors of inflammation, including NF-κB, COX-2, IL-1β, Nrf2, PI3K, MIP-1α, and iNOS levels. Contrarily to free rebamipide and M@D@B-SCNPs, the M@D@Re-SCNPs therapy showed a marked reduction in the number of NF-κB, COX-2, IL-1β, Nrf2, PI3K, MIP-1α, and iNOS cells (Fig. 8A). Specific columnar epithelium cells called goblet cells play a key role in the release of mucin, which produces glycoproteins with high molecular weights that are necessary for the epithelium's defense towards inflammation, infections, and luminal stimuli [19]. The gut mucus main constituent, MUC2, acts as a barrier to protect the epithelium. Changes in goblet cell function, such as their absence or decrease, are a contributing factor to intestinal inflammation in IBD [54]. MUC2 is the primary intestinal mucin that stops preventing the migration of microbes from entering within the intestine. Intestinal epithelial cells stay in close contact with bacteria due to MUC2 deficiency, which triggers inflammation and the immune system [55]. Regarding the immune response and intestinal architecture, it has been shown that the MUC2 deletion in the mouse model faithfully mimics the intestinal inflammation associated with IBD [56]. In addition to mucin glycoproteins, goblet cells also release MUC5B along with MUC2. Immunohistochemical observation demonstrated that in the severe colitis group, MUC2 and MUC5B expression was either nonexistent or insignificant, yet M@D@Re-SCNPs therapy restored both MUC2 and MUC5B more than rebamipide treatment. By regulating the intestinal mucin's balance, this rebamipide reduces inflammation (Fig. 8A). This study provides strong evidence of the effective anti-inflammatory effects of employing M@D@Re-SCNPs nanotherapeutics in place of rebamipide (Fig. S8).

M@D@Re-SCNPs therapy reduces inflammatory cytokine levels

We evaluated the therapeutic efficiency of M@D@Re-SCNPs by assessing key anti-inflammatory markers, including myeloperoxidase (MPO), nitrite, TNF-α, and IL-1β. The results demonstrated a significant reduction in MPO activity, nitrite production, and levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, and IL-1β, in M@D@Re-SCNPs treated samples, highlighting their potential to mitigate inflammation and confirming their therapeutic efficacy. The granulates of neutrophils and monocytes contain the glycosylated enzyme myeloperoxidase (MPO). It acts as a indicators to determine an IBD [57]. Treatments using M@D@Re-SCNPs and free rebamipide significantly decreased MPO activity (Fig. 8B a), a neutrophil infiltration marker. Significantly elevated MPO level was seen in the tissue of colitis mice, indicating increased infiltration of neutrophils. The MPO levels in both the control and M@D@B-SCNPs groups were perpetually low. M@D@Re-SCNPs treatment significantly decreased the level of nitrite (Fig. 8B b), as well as the production of proinflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α (Fig. 8B c) and IL-1β (Fig. 8B d) in colitis mice exhibiting excellent anti-inflammatory capabilities. When compared to free rebamipide, M@D@Re-SCNPs showed a greater therapeutic effect on colitis.

The M@D@Re-SCNPs treated group shows a more pronounced suppression of inflammatory cytokine expression and a significant reduction in immune cell infiltration, reflecting enhanced anti-inflammatory effects. This improvement likely results from the sustained and targeted delivery of rebamipide by the nanoparticles, leading to better control of the inflammatory cascade. The superior performance of M@D@Re-SCNPs in reducing innate immune activation and cytokine levels compared to free rebamipide underscores the efficacy of nanocarrier systems i.e. lipid polymeric nanoparticles in enhancing therapeutic outcomes.

Safety evaluation of M@D@Re-SCNPs treatment

When developing new nanomedicine or new drug delivery systems, investigators are always quite worried about the possible toxicological consequences of nanomaterials during therapy [58]. The liver histological sections from the DSS + M@D@Re-SCNPs group and the Control + M@D@B-SCNPs group exhibited well preserved tissue architecture, indicating overall normal hepatic health. Hepatocytes in liver sections showed typical sizes and shapes without aberration, and after treatment, no infiltration of inflammatory cells was seen, nor were there any vascular abnormalities. Kidney sections H&E stained, tubules were intact with typical dimensions and shape. There were no signs in the glomerular tufts of inflammation or cellular infiltration, confirming the renal cortex general state of health. The histoarchitecture of spleen segments was entirely normal and healthy, with preserved lymphoid follicles and without inflammatory cell influx. Additionally, the histological analysis of the heart of mice in the Control (healthy) groups, DSS + M@D@Re-SCNPs (final treatment) and Control + M@D@B-SCNPs (without rebamipide) group revealed integrated and normal myofibrils without infiltration of cells or any signs of nuclear or cytoplasm disintegration. Owing to their vital blood supply, the lungs belong to the organs prone to the harmful effects of nanomedicine. Control groups, DSS + M@D@Re-SCNPs and Control + M@D@B-SCNPs treatment showed no evidence of inflammation or damage, comprehensive alveolar structures, comparatively intact bronchiolar epithelia, and preserved alveolar frameworks, all of which were indicative of normal lung conditions (Fig. 9). This elucidated that M@D@Re-SCNPs formulation is safe for treating IBD as it did not adversely affect the histoarchitecture of vital organs.

Conclusion

In this study, we successfully developed a multifunctional oral nanoparticle platform (M@D@Re-SCNPs) for the targeted treatment of ulcerative colitis. The formulation combines gastroprotective mucin coating with a mucoadhesive DEAE-dextran layer and encapsulated rebamipide within a 6-O-stearoyl ascorbic acid and caprylic acid-based core. This system demonstrated strong gastric resistance, targeted adhesion to inflamed colonic mucosa, and extended gastrointestinal retention. In vivo studies in a DSS-induced colitis mouse model revealed that M@D@Re-SCNPs significantly enhanced therapeutic outcomes compared to free rebamipide, including suppression of pro-inflammatory pathways (NF-κB, PI3K/AKT), upregulation of antioxidant signaling via PPAR-γ, and restoration of mucosal integrity through MUC2 expression. These findings highlight the potential of M@D@Re-SCNPs as a safe, effective, and targeted strategy for managing inflammatory bowel disease and potentially other gastrointestinal disorders.
