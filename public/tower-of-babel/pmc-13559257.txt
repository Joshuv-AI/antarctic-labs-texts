Nuances of perioperative lung isolation and airway management
Authors: Stuart T. M. Pasch, Harrison D. Pravder
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Effective lung isolation is fundamental to safe anatomical lung resection. This review examines practical techniques for achieving lung separation with double-lumen tubes (DLTs) and bronchial blockers (BBs), weighing the risks and benefits of each approach, and synthesizes the physiological principles and intraoperative considerations that govern one-lung ventilation (OLV). DLTs offer rapid and reliable lung separation, available suctioning and suction-guided lung collapse, and straightforward conversion between one- and two-lung ventilation, making them well suited to many adult thoracic procedures; however, their larger external diameter and stiffness increase the risk of trauma. BBs permit lung isolation through a single-lumen endotracheal tube, have a smaller airway footprint, facilitate pediatric lung isolation in smaller patients, and allow for postoperative ventilation without tube exchange, but they may provide slower or less complete lung collapse. Device choice should be individualized based on patient anatomy, surgical requirements, airway difficulty, and operator experience. Physiologically, OLV converts a portion of the pulmonary circulation into a shunt and can increase pulmonary vascular resistance; these effects make lung-protective ventilation strategies prudent with low tidal volumes, cautious positive end expiratory pressure (PEEP) to the dependent lung, and limited driving pressures. Intraoperative management prioritizes early confirmation of device position with bronchoscopy, proactive strategies for anticipated hypoxemia, close communication between surgical and anesthesia teams, and readiness to troubleshoot ventilation strategies. Meticulous technique, vigilant monitoring, and collaborative operative planning are essential to minimize complications and optimize both hemodynamic stability and surgical exposure.

Introduction

A cuffed endotracheal tube (ETT) was first reported by Guedel and Waters in 1928 (1). This ground-breaking invention was expanded upon in 1931 by Gale and Waters who described the use of a cuffed ETT to produce one lung ventilation (OLV) by advancing a pre-bent ETT into the mainstem bronchus and inflating the tube’s cuff (2). This facilitated ventilation of only the non-surgical lung (dependent lung), thereby allowing maximal exposure and greater independence of exposure between the lungs.

OLV is now routine in thoracic surgery, particularly with the expansion of minimally invasive surgical techniques. It is regularly performed with modern techniques. Despite routine use, OLV is associated with intraoperative hypoxemia and increased pulmonary complications, making airway strategy and intraoperative management relevant variables in ultimate surgical success (3,4).

Device selection, such as with an endobronchial double-lumen tube (DLT) or a bronchial blocker (BB), impacts malposition rates and desaturation incidence, with fiberoptic bronchoscopy (FOB) guidance and left-sided procedures conferring protective effects (5). As thoracic anesthesia evolves toward individualized perioperative lung-protective strategies, a comprehensive understanding of lung isolation techniques and their physiologic consequences remains essential to reducing the morbidity associated with anatomical lung resection.

The goal of this article is to comprehensively discuss thought processes surrounding the use of OLV, including preoperative assessment, equipment selection, methodological fine tuning, and use in unique populations.

Preoperative assessment and strategic planning

A majority of patients undergoing operations requiring lung isolation have a deficit in their cardiopulmonary system placing them at higher risk for general anesthesia. This risk can be mitigated with thorough preoperative assessment and planning. This section will focus on the planning for the placement of a DLT or BB.

Airway evaluation

Airway evaluation preoperatively can help one anticipate a difficult airway and ensure optimization of patient and structural factors prior to intubation. Predictors of difficult intubation with standard ETT also apply to the placement of DLT or BB. However, DLTs are larger in size, bulkier, and more rigid than conventional single lumen tubes and thus are typically harder to place even in airways with excellent glottic visualization. A prospective study by Palczynski et al. evaluated the ability of Mallampati score, thyromental distance, sternomental distance, and thyromental height to predict difficult placement of DLT (6). Decreased thyromental height was the only measure significantly associated with prediction of difficult placement of DLT; a cut off value of 50 mm was deemed optimal with 70% sensitivity and 70% specificity. For every 1 mm in thyromental height above 50 mm, the risk of difficult intubation decreased by 7%. Thyromental height is the height between the anterior border of the thyroid cartilage and the anterior border of the mentum; it is measured with a depth gauge, with the patient in supine position, head in neutral position, and with a closed mouth (Figure 1).

Additional valuable information is history of prior intubations, surgeries, and head and neck radiation. These can help delineate the risk of having edema or friable tissue, which can hinder DLT placement. However, no individual marker has been identified to predict a difficult airway with a level of sensitivity and specificity greater than 90%, so a thorough history and evaluation paired with intubator experience should supplement any preoperative assessment (7).

Pre-operative testing

All patients undergoing lung resection should have physiologic assessment including spirometry to measure forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) and diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO), allowing for calculation of anticipated postoperative lung function (8). If the predicted FEV1 and DLCO percentages are greater than 60%, the patient is considered low risk. If the values are less than 60%, the patient is considered greater than low risk and requires further testing, including an exercise test. Preoperative computed tomography (CT) imaging may be helpful to predict the proper DLT size and improve ease and success of DLT placement. Li et al. found in an randomized controlled trial (RCT) that CT-measured right main stem bronchus length and diameter (used to select tube size and anticipate depth) paired with bronchoscopy guidance compared to only FOB guidance significantly decreased DLT replacement rate (80% vs. 94%), shortened intubation time (75 vs. 101 sec), and reduced complications (2–13% vs. 15–35%) (9).

As mentioned above, some patient-specific factors (such as FEV1 and DLCO) can impact surgical outcomes. Older age does not independently impact surgical outcomes when properly selected and minimally invasive approaches are utilized; however, frailty and poor nutritional status are associated with adverse outcomes (10).

Lung isolation in the difficult airway patient

Anticipating difficult lung isolation or difficult intubation and need for advanced airway techniques can be life-saving and morbidity-limiting for patients undergoing thoracic surgery. Preparation for DLT intubation in patients with anticipated difficult airway is notably different than such circumstances involving single lumen tubes. Though awake fiberoptic placement of a DLT has been described, it is highly challenging to accomplish given the size of the tube and difficulty in blunting the laryngeal and carinal stimulation resulting during DLT placement (11,12). Some argue, and we agree, that placement of a single lumen tube using established difficult airway techniques in tandem with a BB is the safest means of accomplishing lung isolation in the difficult airway patient. If extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) may reasonably be needed, this should be prepared for preoperatively; some relevant situations may include certain types of surgeries, such as diaphragmatic hernia repair in the neonate, and unique patient populations, such as those deemed very high risk.

Monitoring

Standard monitoring should occur whenever a patient is under general anesthesia and a baseline set of vitals should be taken preoperatively. Standard intraoperative American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) monitoring includes monitoring oxygenation (standardly via pulse oximetry), ventilation (through end tidal carbon dioxide monitoring), circulation (blood pressure measured at least every 5 minutes and electrocardiogram monitoring), and temperature (13). Intraoperative monitoring may be augmented with placement of an arterial line; however, specific patient and case factors determine the necessity of such monitoring, and these decisions are best made in preoperative multidisciplinary conversations.

Devices and placement for lung isolation

The goal of all isolation devices is the same: the provision of good operating conditions with a deflated lung by sealing off select bronchi paired with sufficient temporary ventilation and oxygenation. Choosing which device to use for a particular scenario requires an understanding of the benefits and drawbacks of each device with some practical limitations, such as institutional availability. Many institutions will only carry one brand of DLT and one type of BB with some exceptions.

Double lumen endobronchial tubes

DLTs come in left and right variants (Figure 2) though the left sided tube is more common and easier to use given the longer left mainstem bronchus. DLTs contain separate tracheal and bronchial lumens with associated cuffs, allowing independent ventilation of each lung (Figure 3). Right sided tubes must contend with variable anatomy of the right upper lobe which can arise directly from the trachea (~2% of the time), so they have a distorted bronchial balloon (Figure 2) (16). DLTs come in similar lengths (~32 cm measured from the bronchial tip) despite varying sizes measured in the French (Fr) gauge system where 1 Fr =1/3 mm of the outer diameter (OD). Disadvantages of DLTs include sizing and potential ventilation difficulties. Given that these tubes represent two lumens within one, the lumen of each DLT is very small for the diameter of the device as a whole (Table 1). The inner diameter (ID) of even the largest DLT (41 Fr) is only 5.4 mm per lumen but this comes at the cost of a 13.7 mm external diameter which is 12.2% larger than a 9.0 mm single lumen ETT. While many providers would balk at the concept of placing a tube larger than 8.0 mm ID tube into a typical adult patient, the placement of a 35 Fr or greater DLT is considered routine. Increasing the size of a DLT does not increase the length but increases the individual lumen sizes which reduces peak airway pressures. Furthermore, a major limitation in DLT use is in cases requiring post-operative ventilation where a DLT should be replaced with a single lumen tube. This is discussed more in the “Postoperative airway management” section.

The decision of how to size a DLT, like a single lumen ETT, is largely driven by individual anesthesiologist experience rather than direct calculation based on known measurements. A study examining the relationship between clinical experience and size selection documented that more experienced providers tend to choose larger tubes than height-based equations suggest (17). However, a recent narrative review summarizes the expansive literature published on this topic, importantly noting that despite the availability of height- and gender- based formulas, mounting evidence underscores their correlation with real world airway anatomy (18). Image-based sizing decisions, based on X-ray and CT scan information, have been seen to be more accurate. The authors note that the most promising modality to DLT prediction lies with 3D reconstruction and artificial intelligence (AI) based modelling though these are in their infancy. Continued advancement in airway imaging and a greater appreciation for the complications associated with mismatched tube sizing will result in greater care being taken during size selection. At this time, it is likely beneficial to slightly downsize from the historic approach of “placing the largest possible tube” and instead choosing a tube one size smaller as a good compromise with one study which found similar rates of isolation failure and hypoxia when only using 35 Fr DLT (19).

While most cases can be performed with a left sided DLT there are some unique circumstances in which a right sided DLT may be desired: surgery involving the left main bronchus, proximal obstruction of the left main bronchus, and severe distortion of the left main bronchus. In all of these scenarios the bronchial lumen will be unable to accommodate intubation or may be in the field during resection. If a left sided DLT must be used in one of these situations, it is imperative to have clear communication between surgical and anesthesia teams, allowing for withdrawal of the tube prior to resection.

Prior to placement, it is helpful for the intubator to estimate the expected depth of insertion of the DLT. The classic formula by Brodsky et al. [1991] for expected depth of a left DLT describes a direct correlation between body height (BH) and required placement depth; the average depth of insertion for patients 170 cm tall (66.9 inches; 5 feet 6.9 inches) is 29 cm and for each 10-cm increase or decrease in height, average placement depth was increased or decreased by 1 cm (20). This methodology has been tested and slightly adapted since that time with a recent study comparing 5 different formulas, finding all formulas to be incredibly similar and likely sufficient (21). The most accurate formula found in this study in predicting correct insertion depth was a baseline insertion depth of 29.5 cm for a 170 cm patient, increased or decreased by 1 cm for every 5 cm increase or decrease in BH. Nonetheless, when available, the best way to determine insertion depth remains FOB intubation under direct visualization.

Double lumen tube placement

There are two phases of DLT intubation: (I) advancing the tube through the vocal cords and (II) achieving proper placement in the correct mainstem bronchus. For phase 1, video laryngoscopy is the best opportunity for first pass intubation success given the established advantages of hyperangulated blades on improving glottic visualization. Though, for an expert intubator, direct laryngoscopy is also an appropriate modality. It has been argued that DLTs should be placed with standard blade laryngoscopy (not hyperangulated blades) since the out of the box rigid hyperangulated stylet is not compatible with the DLT due to a length discrepancy, but the DLT stylet can be bent to match the shape of the hyperangulated blade with practice or placed without a hyperangulated stylet. FOB intubation is another option but users may struggle with providing enough torque over the bronchoscope given the stiffness of the tube. For DLTs, once the tracheal balloon is advanced past the vocal cords, the first phase of intubation is complete and only the tracheal cuff should be inflated.

FOB is classically used to confirm and guide final positioning of the DLT. The bronchoscope is first introduced through the tracheal lumen to identify the carina and ensure appropriate orientation. It is then advanced through the bronchial lumen into the intended mainstem bronchus, and the tube is advanced under direct visualization.

After positioning, the scope is withdrawn to the tracheal lumen to confirm that the bronchial cuff sits just distal to the carina. The bronchial cuff is then inflated under direct visualization to ensure an adequate seal while avoiding cuff herniation into the trachea or contralateral bronchus.

Right sided DLTs can be inserted in a similar manner to left-sided DLTs, but the endobronchial lumen is much shorter in right-sided DLTs to decrease the chance of obstructing the right upper lobe.

Despite a variety of BBs on the market, many of them have similar components and techniques. The use of any particular device is less important than familiarity with how to operate it. Many BBs are sold separately from a standard ETT (Arndt Endobronchial Blocker System, Cohen Flex-tip bronchial blocker, Uniblocker or Coopdech blocker) but some are packaged into a single delivery device (Univent tubes). The largest advantage of BBs is the ability to use them in patients with existing airways. A study by McLean et al. examined tube exchanges using airway exchange catheters and found the greatest risk of exchange failure occurred when removing a single lumen tube for a DLT (30). Thus the ability to avoid this maneuver where the airway may already be edematous from an existing tube is beneficial. Additionally, at the end of cases where the patient must remain intubated, the BB prevents the need for a tube exchange. Finally, BB allows for greater flexibility during lung isolation and potentially even lobar isolation instead of right vs. left lung. This greater flexibility comes at the cost of increased complexity where each size BB has a minimal recommended ETT ID and balloon inflation volume for endo intraluminal placement. Overall, many providers favor DLT because of the ease in size selection and quality of lung isolation. A meta-analysis by Palaczynski et al. found similar quality of lung isolation between DLT and BB but a malposition rate of 25.3% for DLT and 31.9% for BB [odds ratio (OR) =0.66; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.49 to 0.88; P=0.004] (31). Additionally, they found time to device placement in the DLT group was 2.5±2.1 min, compared to 3.1±2.1 min in the BB group [mean difference (MD) =−0.78; 95% CI: −1.35 to −0.21; P=0.007] and time for lung collapse in DLT and BB groups varied and amounted to 7.0±8.9 vs. 10.3±8.3 min, respectively (MD =−2.57; 95% CI: −3.73 to −1.41; P<0.001) (31). It is also important to note that the authors found the use of a DLT compared to a BB is associated with a higher risk of hypoxemia, hoarseness, sore throat and bronchus/carina injuries. Additionally, one study by Campos et al. found that anesthesiologists with limited thoracic experience (less than two lung isolation cases per month) failed to correctly position a BB style device in 37.8% of attempts (32). Despite these challenges, one study by Rispoli et al. designed a course to improve operator skill and comfort with EZ Blockers. 15 participants were enrolled and demonstrated significantly improved dexterity and confidence after just 6 attempts (33).

Physiologic principles of one-lung ventilation (OLV) and impact on ventilatory strategy

From the time of induction to the time of instituting OLV and the period beyond, the human body responds to atypical conditions. Certain factors influence how dramatic this response is, such as positioning, medication use, and ventilation strategy. This section reviews the relevant hemodynamic changes and correlates these manifestations clinically.

V/Q mismatch and shunt physiology

In normal positive pressure ventilation with a single lumen ETT placed above the carina, both lungs are ventilated (V) and both lungs receive a baseline level of pulmonary arterial blood flow (Q). When OLV ensues, this ratio of V to Q is altered. One lung (the dependent lung) receives all the V whereas Q remains roughly equal between the two lungs, a concept known as V/Q mismatch. This creates a right-to-left shunt where blood goes “through” the non-dependent lung without proper gas exchange, creating impaired oxygenation and resulting hypoxemia (defined as oxygen saturation less than 90%) in roughly 5–10% of patients (34). Over time, the body compensates with hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (HPV) where the vessels in the non-dependent lung constrict and thus limit Q in favor of the dependent lung, which decreases the shunt but does not eliminate it.

Some factors that determine the amount of V/Q mismatch are in-born biological factors, but others are based on ventilation strategy and changeable circumstances. For instance, Spadaro et al. compared levels of shunt in patients during OLV at different levels of PEEP for the dependent lung during OLV; the shunt fraction decreased as PEEP increased (35). The median decrease in shunt fraction was 5% at PEEP 5 cm·H2O and 11% at PEEP 10 cm·H2O (P<0.001) (35). One theoretical argument worries that increasing PEEP will over distend lung units in the dependent lung, shifting blood flow from the dependent to the non-dependent lung (thus increasing mismatch). Wittenstein et al. tested this hypothesis in pigs undergoing thoracotomy under OLV (36). They changed PEEP from 0 to 5 cm·H2O to 15 cm·H2O and titrated inspiratory pressures to the best respiratory system compliance (36). They found that as PEEP increased, intrapulmonary shunt actually decreased; shunt fraction at PEEP 15 cm·H2O was markedly decreased compared to the fraction at PEEP 0 cm H2O (39% vs. 48%, P<0.05) (36).

Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (HPV)

As discussed above, HPV is a preferential contraction of smooth muscle vasculature in the pulmonary circulation in lower-oxygenated areas (i.e., non-dependent lung) thus decreasing shunt fraction. HPV occurs in two distinct phases (37). Phase 1 begins within a few seconds of hypoxic conditions and is maximal in vasoconstriction intensity at 15 minutes (37). When moderate hypoxia remains for more than 30 to 60 minutes, phase 2 begins, with gradually increasing vasoconstriction up to at least 1 hour 45 minutes (38). After prolonged HPV, a return to normal oxygen levels does not lead to an immediate return to pulmonary vasculature tone; this process can take hours (37,38). Certain drugs (i.e. catecholamines and almitrine) augment HPV, whereas others (i.e. nitric oxide, phosphodiesterase inhibitors, steroids, and angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors) inhibit HPV (37). There is inconsistent data for physiologic factors affecting HPV, such as age, carbon dioxide levels and acidosis, and temperature (37).

Anesthetics can significantly influence degrees of HPV (Table 4). In general, inhaled halogenated anesthetics (sevoflurane, isoflurane, etc.) inhibit HPV to a higher degree than IV anesthetics (37). Halothane is a potent inhibitor of HPV with a minimum alveolar concentration (MAC) of 0.5 causing 50% inhibition of HPV in rats; isoflurane at 0.6 MAC leads to 50% inhibition of HPV (46). Sevoflurane seems to behave similarly to isoflurane but with less data available (41). Propofol does not significantly impact HPV though it may potentiate HPV (39,40). Ketamine seems to have minimal impact on HPV though limited data is available (40). Despite theoretical differences, clinically it is hard to delineate clinically-relevant changes in HPV between maintenance anesthetics.

HPV, hypoxic vasoconstriction; PVR, pulmonary vascular resistance.

Several investigations have compared the two most used modern-day maintenance anesthetics, sevoflurane and propofol. An earlier study in 2001 by Beck et al. randomly assigned patients to propofol or sevoflurane groups and assessed shunt fraction at three points: after 30 min of two-lung ventilation, after 30 min of OLV, and during OLV in the lateral position with chest open but prior to surgical manipulation; no differences in shunt fraction or hemodynamics were seen between groups at any of the time points (47). When titrated to a similar bispectral index (BIS) between 40 and 60, no difference in oxygenation is seen between patients maintained only on propofol versus only sevoflurane (48). Hahm et al. [2019] conducted an RCT with patients maintained on either (I) sevoflurane or (II) propofol undergoing OLV during thoracic surgery that found, when titrated to a BIS between 40 and 50, systemic oxygen delivery is the same between groups (49). In sum, all this work strongly suggests that any possible differences in HPV between sevoflurane and propofol maintenance anesthetics are clinically insignificant and thus the choice of maintenance anesthetic can be decided based on other factors.

Lung-protective ventilation

Just as lung protective ventilation has flourished in the treatment and prevention of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), lung protective OLV has proliferated in mainstream thought during thoracic surgery. The modern form of lung protective OLV includes low tidal volume, low driving pressure, short and mild recruitment maneuvers, and low-to-moderate PEEP (50).

The definition of “low” tidal volumes and “low-to-moderate PEEP” has conflicted between studies, but a 2019 expert consensus statement recommends initial settings of 6–8 mL/kg tidal volumes (ideal body weight) and PEEP of 5 cm·H2O (49). “Low” tidal volume generally refers to less than 8 mL/kg, and these volumes alone are associated with decreased post-operative respiratory complications (50,51). However, low tidal volume alone is not enough. A meta-analysis by Yang et al. found that although low tidal volume alone significantly decreases incidence of postoperative lung infection (OR 0.33; 95% CI: 0.16–0.68), no difference is found in the incidence of postoperative acute lung injury or atelectasis compared to higher tidal volume ventilation, but a complete lung protective ventilation strategy (low tidal volume, PEEP, and with recruitment maneuvers) showed statistically significant reduction in incidence of postoperative lung infection (OR 0.21; 95% CI: 0.09–0.5), acute lung injury (OR 0.15; 95% CI: 0.04–0.61), and atelectasis (OR 0.36; 95% CI: 0.20–0.64) (51).

High driving pressures have been described as considerable determinants of lung injury (52). Driving pressure is defined as the difference between plateau pressure and PEEP. A meta-analysis by Li et al. found that a driving pressure-oriented strategy of ventilation was associated with better perioperative oxygenation, fewer postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs), and improved compliance of the respiratory system (53). The exact method of driving pressure-oriented strategies differed in the seven studies included in this meta-analysis; generally, driving pressure strategy involved titrating PEEP to achieve the minimal driving pressure. The landmark RCT by Park et al. individualized PEEP for each patient by setting a tidal volume of 6 mL/kg (ideal body weight) and respiratory rate of 12 followed by titration of PEEP from 2 cm·H2O to 10 cm·H2O for 10 respiratory cycles; the PEEP with the lowest driving pressure was maintained for the entirety of the OLV period with constant tidal volume and respiratory rate titrated to end-tidal CO2 of 35–40 mmHg (54).

Oxygenation strategies

As seen above, current guidelines recommend the use of PEEP and recruitment maneuvers to improve oxygenation. Expert consensus recommends starting at a PEEP of 5 cm H2O (50). A recent meta-analysis by Gu et al. found that individualized PEEP titration by lung compliance was associated with reduced composite of PPCs (risk ratio 0.55, 95% CI: 0.38–0.78) (55). Further, a subgroup analysis suggests that PEEP titration by dynamic compliance rather than driving pressure was beneficial (55).

Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) deliverance to the non-dependent lung has also been suggested as a strategy to improve postoperative outcomes. One study by Kim et al. uptitrated CPAP by increments of 3 cm·H2O until the surgeon notified the anesthesia team of field obstruction (56). This study found improved oxygenation with 0% surgical field obstruction at a CPAP of 6 cm·H2O during OLV, while a CPAP of 9 cm·H2O improved oxygenation but resulted in field obstruction in 90% of patients (56).

Lateral decubitus positioning with the dependent lung inferiorly positioned allows for gravity-dependent improved blood flow to the dependent lung and thus decreased V/Q mismatch, as discussed in further detail above.

See the “Intraoperative management and troubleshooting” section for more insight on improving intraoperative oxygenation during OLV.

Permissive hypercapnia and acid-base tolerance

There is limited research assessing the impact of carbon dioxide on the pulmonary vasculature. In practice, hypercapnia is common during OLV, as relative minute ventilation is decreased and dead space is increased. Two recent RCTs evaluated the impact of permissive hypercapnia on oxygenation and pulmonary mechanics during OLV. Lee et al. performed a crossover RCT evaluating fifty adult patients at normocarbia [partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PaCO2): 38–42 mmHg] and hypercarbia (PaCO2: 45–50 mmHg); they found improvement of partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2) during hypercarbic conditions (79.7 vs. 66.5 mmHg, P<0.01) and improvement in oxygen saturation, arterial oxygen content, static compliance of the lung, and oxygen delivery (57). Joe et al. studied patients scheduled to undergo elective lung resection surgery utilizing OLV that were randomized to groups targeting PaCO2 of 40 mmHg, 50 mmHg, and 60 mmHg; the PaO2/FiO2 ratio was higher in the 50 mmHg and 60 mmHg group than the 40 mmHg group, but no difference was seen between the 50 mmHg and 60 mmHg groups (58). The incidence of PPCs was similar among all three groups.

While effective in patients with normal right ventricle and pulmonary vascular physiology, permissive hypercapnia must be approached with extreme caution in patients with underlying pulmonary hypertension or right sided heart failure. As carbon dioxide levels increase, pulmonary vasculature constricts in a linear relationship; thus causing a relative pulmonary hypertension to baseline conditions. In the healthy population, this can be well tolerated. In patients with elevated pulmonary arterial pressures at baseline or right heart failure, this increase can be detrimental, as there is insufficient body capability to compensate. A 2023 consensus statement from the American Heart Association recommends employing mitigation strategies in patients with underlying pulmonary hypertension during one lung ventilation to avoid hypercarbia, hypoxia, and increased airway pressures—all components that increase pulmonary arterial pressures (59). If permissive hypercapnia is necessary in this population, mitigation strategies including lower than usual airway pressures and higher oxygenation can be considered; though this may be impractical to sustain sufficient hemodynamics intraoperatively.

Intraoperative management and troubleshooting

Ventilation modes

The debate between pressure-controlled ventilation (PCV) and volume-controlled ventilation (VCV) during OLV continues to evolve. Meta-analyses demonstrate that PCV achieves lower peak inspiratory pressures compared to VCV, though oxygenation benefits remain modest, with improved PaO2/FiO2 ratios of approximately 11 mmHg (60). Guidelines from the Society for Translational Medicine give class IIa recommendations for PCV over VCV for patients undergoing lung resection (61). However, pressure-controlled ventilation-volume guaranteed (PCV-VG) modes may offer advantages by combining the benefits of both approaches, particularly in elderly patients where this mode reduces inflammatory markers and airway pressures (62).

Lung protective ventilation strategies recommend tidal volumes of 5–6 mL/kg predicted body weight during OLV as this does not worsen oxygenation while reducing postoperative complications (61,63,64). Recent evidence suggests that driving pressure-guided tidal volume titration may further optimize lung protection, with volumes as low as 4–5 mL/kg reducing inflammatory markers without compromising oxygenation (65). Smaller tidal volume ventilation resulting in therapeutic hypercapnia to a partial pressure of carbon dioxide at 50–70 mmHg was given a class IIa recommendation by the Society for Translational Medicine (61).

Recruitment strategies

Alveolar recruitment maneuvers combined with individualized PEEP titration represent an evolving approach to optimize lung mechanics during OLV. The iPROVE-OLV trial demonstrated that individualized open-lung approaches, when reducing driving pressure, significantly decrease PPCs (66). However, the recent PROTHOR trial found that fixed higher PEEP (10 cm·H2O) with routine recruitment maneuvers did not reduce postoperative pulmonary complications compared to lower PEEP (5 cm·H2O), while increasing intraoperative hemodynamic instability (63). This suggests that individualization based on respiratory mechanics may be more beneficial than standardized high-PEEP protocols.

Optimization of surgical conditions and coordination

Timing of OLV initiation significantly impacts surgical exposure and lung collapse quality. Recent evidence supports initiating OLV immediately after positioning the patient in the lateral decubitus position, which achieves faster satisfactory lung collapse compared to waiting until surgical incision (67). This preemptive approach allows adequate time to optimize ventilation parameters before surgical manipulation begins. Some practitioners employ disconnection techniques to accelerate lung collapse, though preemptive OLV appears superior for early lung deflation (67). Some surgeons will request suction be applied to the operative lung to improve collapse. We recommend initially allowing passive collapse of the isolated side and applying suction only if sub-optimal deflation is observed that impacts surgical exposure.

Lung re-expansion timing requires careful coordination between anesthesia and surgical teams. Standardized re-expansion of the non-ventilated lung should occur before resuming two-lung ventilation (63). Many surgeons perform re-expansion during chest tube placement or under water seal when removing final ports, allowing visual confirmation of complete lung expansion and identification of air leaks. This coordinated approach ensures adequate lung recruitment while maintaining surgical field visualization during critical closure phases.

Special scenarios

Hemoptysis, infected field, air leak

Lung isolation can serve a purpose other than surgical exposure, such as in massive hemoptysis, infected field, or large blowing air leak. In these instances, placement may be challenging due to poor visualization but lung isolation is protective of the contralateral healthy lung. Choice of isolation may depend on long term goals for lung isolation. Some of these problems may persist after the immediate operative period, so the durable isolation of DLTs may outweigh the hassle and unfamiliarity of using the DLT in the intensive care unit (ICU); possibly requiring one ventilator for each lung.

Redo thoracotomy

Redo thoracotomy offers a different challenge. Prior surgery results in adhesions and scarring that might attach the lung parenchyma to the chest wall or obstructions in the bronchial tree (68). Prior resections may distort the bronchial tree creating obstructions or angulations. Adhesions may result in incomplete lung collapse despite excellent isolation. This complicates dissection and increases the risk of lung injury during both chest entry and dissection. In such cases, providers should use a BB instead of a DLT, as it has the flexibility to isolate smaller individual lung segments.

Mature long-term tracheostomies have a well-established trach that can be safely cannulated with little risk of false passage. Temporary or new tracheostomies require a slightly different approach as the tracheal stoma is not well formed and an airway exchange would carry an unacceptably high risk of false passage (70). If intubation from above is possible, it can be considered though there may be difficulty fitting a second tube into the distal trachea. Instead, if the temporary tracheostomy is cuffed, then ventilation and BB insertion can happen directly through the tracheostomy.

Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) and robotic-assisted thoracic surgery (RATS)

Thoracoscopic approaches in thoracic surgery have greatly improved rates of PPCs, and these procedures are now the dominant approaches for lung resection. The ventilatory nuances of RATS compared to VATS include increased duration of OLV, carbon dioxide insufflation, and restrictive positioning once the robot is docked. Thus, patient selection must uniquely consider these factors and use RATS with caution specifically in populations with severe pulmonary hypertension and cardiac disease. However, limited large studies are available to make specific ventilatory recommendations for these procedures. Due to this relative lack of large-scale literature in this domain, we elaborate on current literature below, while saving a more comprehensive discussion for future works.

No large multicenter studies compare use of a DLT versus a BB in VATS/RATS. A 2025 meta-analysis by Wu et al., suggests that BB use in VATS is associated with lower risk of tracheal mucosal injury, hypoxemia, and pneumonia, while DLT shows marginal improvement in lung collapse quality (71). Notwithstanding, these differences based on available data appear to be marginal and selection between the two techniques should be guided by clinical experience and patient-specific characteristics.

Current thought recommends similar lung-protective measures in VATS/RATS to traditional approaches. However, some recent studies are challenging this. The PROTHOR trial, a large, multicenter, international RCT investigating higher PEEP (10 cm·H2O) versus lower PEEP (5 cm·H2O) during OLV for thoracic surgery found a benefit of high PEEP in patients undergoing thoracoscopic surgery that was not seen in non-thoracoscopic surgeries (63). An absolute risk reduction for PPC events of −6.99 was seen in thoracoscopic surgery compared to an increase of 2.11 in non-thoracoscopic approaches when comparing high PEEP versus low PEEP (P=0.02). This may be due to patient-specific characteristics guiding the selection of each procedure, but nonetheless warrants future investigation.

Pediatric lung isolation and airway management

Unique considerations

Pediatric lung isolation presents unique technical challenges driven by anatomic variability, small airway structures, and limited respiratory reserves compared to adults. The reduced caliber of pediatric airway structures limits device options, as not all devices are made in sizes small enough to satisfy pediatric dimensions. Even slight sizing errors can lead to an inability to achieve OLV. For instance, in infants less than 6 months old, the left mainstem bronchus has a median diameter of 3.9 mm, which is smaller than the diameter of standard cuffed and uncuffed 3.0 ETT (72). If one tries to mainstem a single lumen ETT into the left mainstem bronchus that is larger than the diameter of the bronchus, one risks structural injuries and inadequate seal (72). Additionally, decreased respiratory reserves in younger children limit time available to use advanced airway equipment to place and verify placement of the airway device.

Equipment limitations further complicate obtaining OLV in pediatric patients. Many centers that do not regularly perform surgeries requiring OLV in young children and infants do not stock flexible scopes that are able to transverse a 4.0 ETT or smaller. This makes difficult placement potentially even more challenging. Further, DLTs are sized down to 26 Fr size (approximately the same OD as a 6.5 ETT) and thus DLTs cannot be routinely used in pediatric patients younger than 8 years old.

Physiologic differences

Pediatric patients’ physiology is distinctly unique from that of the adult patient. In children, V/Q mismatch (and thus increased risk for hypoxemia during OLV) include (I) compression of the dependent lung due to less rigid cartilaginous rib cage and abdominal pressure transmitted through the diaphragm, reducing lung compliance and (II) a decreased hydrostatic gradient from the nondependent to the dependent lung, leading to less effective shunting of blood away from the nondependent and worsening V/Q mismatch during OLV (73). These factors in addition to a higher metabolic rate present in infants and young children can result in hypoxemia during OLV (73).

Techniques

The main techniques of achieving OLV in children are BBs, DLTs, and endobronchial intubation. BB use is more universal as these can typically be used in all pediatric age ranges, whereas DLT use is limited to use in children older than 8 years of age.

Endobronchial intubation is methodologically easier to perform. Right sided endobronchial intubation can typically be accomplished by simply advancing the ETT until left sided breath sounds disappear, while left sided endobronchial intubation can be accomplished with use of a FOB or by physical manipulation. A classic physical manipulation strategy involves turning the patient’s head to the right and rotating the bevel of the ETT 180 degrees (bevel facing right, murphy’s eye facing left) upon desired left mainstem insertion (74). However, endobronchial intubation is associated with a significantly higher rate of critical complications compared to use of BBs (75). This technique can be hindered by occlusion of the ETT by blood or airway secretions, leading to difficulties in ventilation and oxygenation (73). BBs are the most versatile tools in achieving OLV in the pediatric population. There are two primary strategies for placing BBs, intraluminal and extraluminal placement. Intraluminal is the classical adult approach whereas a BB is advanced through a preexisting ETT and extraluminal involves advancing the BB next to the ETT. Intraluminal BB placement is classically limited to patients 4 years and older due to the requirement of a 4.5 ETT to accommodate the size of the smallest BB. The technique is the same as that discussed in the adult population. Common extraluminal placement techniques include (I) placement of the BB with the ETT upon initial intubation and then advancing the BB; (II) fluoroscopic guidance to achieving proper placement; (III) initial advancement of a BB through an endobronchially placed ETT, followed by retraction of the ETT (only applied when the BB can fit in the internal diameter of the ETT) (73,74). There are multiple different types of pediatric BBs, which is outside the scope of this review.

DLT placement is discussed in the “Placement techniques and confirmation practices” section of this article and placement is like that in adults.

Postoperative airway management and outcomes

Extubation strategies

The main extubation strategies can be generalized to early and delayed extubation. “Early” refers to extubation in the operating room whereas “delayed” generally refers to extubation in the intensive care unit post-operatively. Delayed extubation leads to marked resource utilization and is associated with worse outcomes.

Cywinksky et al. sought to identify predictors of delayed extubation in patients after open thoracotomy for lung resection (76). The authors found that most predictors were difficult to modify characteristics (history of stroke, intraoperative red blood cell transfusion, lower preoperative FEV1, etc.) (76). The two characteristics that were strongly at the discretion of the medical team include (I) use of thoracic epidural analgesia and (II) use of lumbar epidural analgesia, suggesting that placement and use of an epidural may help facilitate early extubation (76).

Risk factors for reintubation

Patients who undergo thoracic surgery are at the highest risk for postoperative reintubation among all surgical disciplines (77). Burton et al. analyzed the records of more than 16,000 patients who underwent VATS and thoracotomy lung resections (78). They found that the statistically significant risk factors for the 3.5% of patients who underwent unplanned reintubation were age, ASA score ≥4, dyspnea with moderate exertion at rest, history of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, male sex, smoking use, functional dependence, preoperative regular steroid use, open thoracotomy, increased operation time, and low preoperative albumin and hematocrit (78).

Postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs)

PPCs can produce significant morbidity and mortality following thoracic surgery. Findings from the LAS VEGAS study, an international prospective observational study, found that PPCs after OLV may exceed 48% in high risk populations; however, this rate is notably higher than most other published figures in prospective cohort studies (79).

There are many possible complications of OLV; however, this is not the primary focus of this paper. Some common complications include atelectasis (see above for CPAP to non-ventilated lung as a strategy to mitigate this risk), aspiration, and ARDS. A large recent international RCT randomized patients undergoing lung surgery with one lung ventilation to (I) a high PEEP group (10 cm·H2O with periodic recruitment maneuvers) or (II) a low PEEP group (5 cm·H2O without routine recruitment maneuvers) (63). No difference was found in PPCs between the groups, suggesting that increasing PEEP alone with recruitment maneuvers may not improve postoperative pulmonary dynamics (63).

ICU considerations

Close postoperative monitoring is crucial in the post-operative surgical candidate who underwent OLV. If a DLT was employed, residual airway irritation due to the bulkiness of the tube is possible. Further, there is an increased risk of airway edema in thoracic surgery patients when compared with other types of surgery. Any signs of airway edema, such as inspiratory stridor, should prompt early escalation and evaluation.

An important caveat when delayed extubation is planned regards the type of ETT used in the intensive care unit where staff experience with DLTs is limited. DLTs are also bulky with larger ODs than standard ETTs and carry a higher risk of airway irritation and injury in long term use. Thus, the DLT should always be exchanged for a single lumen ETT prior to arriving in the intensive care unit except in very limited situations. Moreover, BBs may be preferable in these situations to avoid further airway manipulation with the exchange of ETTs.

Conclusions

One lung ventilation comes with a number of unique technical and physiological challenges. Understanding and appreciation of these factors allows for safe and more complete patient care. Similarly, while the selection of DLT or BB is often based on user preference, situations exist where one modality is superior over the other (two-sided surgery or patients with distorted anatomy), and in these cases, it is important for the providers to understand how to safely use both techniques.
