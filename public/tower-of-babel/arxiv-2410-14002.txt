A note on Bayesian R-squared for generalized additive mixed models
Abdollah Jalilian

Abstract
We present a novel Bayesian framework to decompose the posterior predictive variance in a fitted Generalized Additive Mixed Model (GAMM) into explained and unexplained components. This decomposition enables a rigorous definition of Bayesian R 2 R^{2} . We show that the new definition aligns with the intuitive Bayesian R 2 R^{2} proposed by Gelman, Goodrich, Gabry, and Vehtari (2019) [ The American Statistician , 73 (3), 307–309], but extends its applicability to a broader class of models. Furthermore, we introduce a partial Bayesian R 2 R^{2} to quantify the contribution of individual model terms to the explained variation in the posterior predictions. Keywords: Coefficient of determination; Fixed effects; Posterior predictive density; Random effects; Residuals; Sum of squares AMS classification: 62F15, 62J12, 62J20

Introduction

The coefficient of determination, commonly denoted as
R^{2}
, is a widely used metric in linear models to assess how well the model explains the variability in the data. Within the framework of frequentist statistical inference,
R^{2}
has been adapted to extend its applicability to generalized linear models (GLMs). Notable approaches include definitions based on likelihood ratio
(
Magee,, 1990
)
, Kullback-Leibler divergence
(
Cameron and Windmeijer,, 1997
)
, discrimination
(
Tjur,, 2009
)
, and variance function
(
Zhang,, 2017
)
. However, defining
R^{2}
for models with random effects, such as generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs), is not straightforward
(
Piepho,, 2019
)
. As discussed in
Nakagawa and Schielzeth, (2013)
,
Jaeger et al., (2017)
and
Rights and Sterba, (2019)
, there is no consensus in the statistical literature on the best definition of
R^{2}
for GLMMs and existing definitions face theoretical and practical challenges, such as incomplete partitioning of variance and inconsistent implementation across different software. Because of these issues, finding a universally accepted and comprehensive definition of
R^{2}
for the broader class of generalized additive models (GAMMs), which includes both GLMs and GLMMs as special cases, remains an unsolved problem.

Within the Bayesian statistical inference paradigm,
Gelman and Pardoe, (2006)
developed a Bayesian
R^{2}
measure by calculating the expected value of the residual sum of squares with respect to the posterior distribution of the vector of all unknowns in the model, including parameters and random effects. This approach aligns with the simple idea of using the posterior mean of the classical definition of
R^{2}
. However, as demonstrated by
Gelman et al., (2019)
using a toy example, this Bayesian version of
R^{2}
can produce values greater than one, leading to challenges in both application and interpretation. To resolve this issue,
Gelman et al., (2019)
proposed an alternative intuitive definition of Bayesian
R^{2}
, where sums of squares in the classical
R^{2}
are replaced by their corresponding expected values based on observations generated from the predictive posterior distribution. This new definition ensures that the Bayesian
R^{2}
remains within the interval
[0,1]
and offers a more interpretable measure of a model’s explanation power in the Bayesian framework.

The definition of the Bayesian
R^{2}
by
Gelman et al., (2019)
is intuitive and does not rely on the traditional approach of decomposing total variations and calculating the ratio of explained to total variations. In this paper, we introduce a decomposition of the expected variation in posterior predictions into explained and unexplained components. This decomposition allows us to naturally define the Bayesian
R^{2}
as the ratio of explained variations to total variations, resulting in a more consistent and interpretable definition for the Bayesian
R^{2}
. We show that this definition aligns with the one proposed by
Gelman et al., (2019)
.

Although the Bayesian
R^{2}
by
Gelman et al., (2019)
can be applied to any generalized linear model (GLM), it is currently only implemented for GLMs with Gaussian and binomial distributions in the
R

(
R Core Team,, 2024
)
package
rstantools

(
Gabry et al.,, 2024
)
. Our definition of Bayesian
R^{2}
extends to a broader class of GAMMs and is implemented for various commonly used distributions.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. First,
section 2
provides an overview of GAMMs, the classical
R^{2}
and a naive Bayesian version of
R^{2}
. Then,
section 3
presents the novel decomposition of posterior predictive variations and the new definition of the Bayesian
R^{2}
. In
section 4
, we introduce the concept of partial Bayesian
R^{2}
, which quantifies the contributions of different model terms to the posterior predictive variations. The application of the proposed
R^{2}
to a simulated dataset is demonstrated in
section 5
. Finally, in
section 6
offers some concluding remarks.

Background

Consider a GAMM for the observations,
(y_{i},\mathbf{x}_{i})
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, where
y_{i}
represents the response (dependent or outcome) variable taking values in the space
\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}
and

\mathbf{x}_{i}=\left(x_{i,1},\ldots,x_{i,m_{1}},z_{i,1},\ldots,z_{i,m_{2}},u_{i,1},\ldots,u_{m_{3}}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}

is the vector of
m=m_{1}+m_{2}+m_{3}
covariates (independent or explanatory variables). In this model, the conditional mean of the response variable is expressed as

\mu(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta},\mathbf{b},\boldsymbol{\gamma})=\mathbb{E}\left[y_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta},\mathbf{b},\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right]=g^{-1}\left(\eta(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta},\mathbf{b},\boldsymbol{\gamma})\right),

(1)

where
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a known link function, and

\eta(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta},\mathbf{b},\boldsymbol{\gamma})=\beta_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{m_{1}}\beta_{j}x_{i,j}+\sum_{j=1}^{m_{2}}b_{j}z_{i,j}+\sum_{j=1}^{m_{3}}\mathsf{f}_{j}(u_{i,j},\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{j})

is a linear combination of fixed effects
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{0},\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{m_{1}})
, random effects
\mathbf{b}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{m_{2}})
and smooth functions
\mathsf{f}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{f}_{m_{3}}
. Here, each smooth function
\mathsf{f}_{j}(u,\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{j})
is assumed to be given by

\mathsf{f}_{j}(u,\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{j})=\sum_{l=1}^{k_{j}}\gamma_{j,l}h_{j,l}(u),

where
h_{j,l}(u)
are known spline basis functions chosen to have convenient properties, and
\gamma_{j,l}
are unknown coefficients that need to be estimated. Often, the basis dimension
k_{j}
is fixed at a sufficiently large size and the smoothness of
\mathsf{f}_{j}
function is controlled by imposing a constraint on a
wiggliness
measure such as

\Omega_{j}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{j})=\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}S_{j}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{j},

(2)

where
S_{j}
is a semi-definite matrix of known constants
(
Wood,, 2017
)
.

Moreover, the conditional distribution of the random effects
\mathbf{b}
and the response vector
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})^{\mathsf{T}}
are given by

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbf{b}|\mathbf{X},\boldsymbol{\psi}\,\sim\,q(\mathbf{b}|\mathbf{X},\boldsymbol{\psi})\\
\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{X},\boldsymbol{\beta},\mathbf{b},\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\phi}\,\sim\,\prod_{i=1}^{n}f(y_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta},\mathbf{b},\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\phi})\end{array}\right.,

(3)

where
\mathbf{X}=[\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}^{\mathsf{T}}]^{\mathsf{T}}
is the matrix of covariates,
q(\mathbf{b}|\mathbf{X},\boldsymbol{\psi})
is the probability density function of random effects with parameter vector
\boldsymbol{\psi}
, and
f(y|\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\beta},\mathbf{b},\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\phi})
denote the probability density (mass) function of a distribution belonging to the exponential family of distributions which depends on the coefficients
\boldsymbol{\beta}
,
\mathbf{b}
and
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
and possibly nuisance parameters
\boldsymbol{\phi}
. The conditional variance

\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta},\mathbf{b},\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\phi})=\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}[y_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta},\mathbf{b},\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\phi}]=V\left(\mu(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta},\mathbf{b},\boldsymbol{\gamma}),\boldsymbol{\phi}\right)

(4)

depends upon the conditional mean (
1
) through the variance function
V(\,\cdot\,,\boldsymbol{\phi}):\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
as well as upon extra parameters
\boldsymbol{\phi}

(see
McCulloch et al.,, 2008
, Chapter 7)
.

2.1
Parameter estimation

Let
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{\phi})
denote the vector of all model parameters. The likelihood function for the response
\mathbf{y}
and covariates
\mathbf{X}
is given by

p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{X},\boldsymbol{\theta})=\int\prod_{i=1}^{n}f(y_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta},\mathbf{b},\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\phi})q(\mathbf{b}|\mathbf{X},\boldsymbol{\psi})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{b}.

In frequentist statistical inference, the maximum likelihood estimate of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is obtained by maximizing a penalised version of the above likelihood function with respect to
\boldsymbol{\theta}
; i.e.,

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\left(p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{X},\boldsymbol{\theta})+\sum_{j=1}^{m_{3}}\lambda_{j}\Omega_{j}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{j})\right),

where
\Omega_{j}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{j})
is a wiggliness measure as in (
2
) and
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m_{3}}
are smoothing parameters
(
Wood,, 2017
)
.

In Bayesian inference
(
Gelman et al.,, 2013
)
, model parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
are regarded as random variables and their uncertainty is quantified using a prior probability distribution with density function
\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\pi(\boldsymbol{\beta})\pi(\boldsymbol{\gamma})\pi(\boldsymbol{\psi})\pi(\boldsymbol{\phi})
. Random effects
\mathbf{b}
are also considered as unknown parameters of the model, with their prior distribution given by
q(\mathbf{b}|\mathbf{X},\boldsymbol{\psi})
. Moreover, improper priors such as

\pi(\boldsymbol{\gamma})\propto\exp\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{m_{3}}\lambda_{j}\Omega_{j}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{j})/\alpha_{j}\right)

can be defined to control the wiggliness of
\mathsf{f}_{j}
’s, where
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m_{3}}
are smoothing parameters and
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m_{3}}
are hyper parameters
(
Wood et al.,, 2013
)
. The posterior density

\pi(\mathbf{b},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})=\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{n}f(y_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta},\mathbf{b},\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\phi})q(\mathbf{b}|\mathbf{X},\boldsymbol{\psi})\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{X})}.

(5)

is used to make any inference about
\mathbf{b}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. For example, the posterior mean can be used to estimate
\mathbf{b}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}
.

Efficient computational implementations of the above-mentioned frequentist and Bayesian estimation approaches of the model parameters in
R
are available in packages such as
gamm4

(
Wood and Scheipl,, 2020
)
and
rstanarm

(
Goodrich et al.,, 2024
)
, respectively.

2.2
Classical coefficient of determination

In the case of a linear model (identity link function) with no random effects or smooth terms, and a conditional Gaussian distribution for the response variable, the conditional mean (
1
) and variance (
4
) are reduced to

\displaystyle\mu(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[y_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta}]=\eta(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta})=\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\beta,

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^{2})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}[y_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^{2}]\equiv\sigma^{2}.

In a frequentist framework, the response
y_{i}
can be expressed as

y_{i}=\overbrace{\mu(\mathbf{x}_{i},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})}^{\mathrm{model}}\,+\,\overbrace{e_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})}^{\mathrm{residual}},\quad i=1,\ldots,n,

where
e_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})=y_{i}-\mu(\mathbf{x}_{i},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=(\mathbf{X}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{y}
is the least squares and maximum likelihood estimate of
\boldsymbol{\beta}
. Moreover, the total sum of squares of deviations for the response vector
\mathbf{y}
,

\mathrm{TSS}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}-\bar{y})^{2}

is decomposed to

\mathrm{TSS}=\mathrm{RSS}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})+\mathrm{ESS}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}),

(6)

where

\mathrm{RSS}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y_{i}-\mu(\mathbf{x}_{i},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\right)^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(e_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}e_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{j},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\right)^{2}

(7)

is the residual sums of squares, and

\mathrm{ESS}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\mu(\mathbf{x}_{i},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})-\bar{y}\right)^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\mu(\mathbf{x}_{i},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu(\mathbf{x}_{j},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\right)^{2}

(8)

is the explained sum of squares. The last two equations in (
7
) and (
8
) hold because
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu(\mathbf{x}_{i},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})/n=\bar{y}
and hence
\sum_{j=1}^{n}e_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{j},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})=0
.

This decomposition breaks down the total variations in the observed response vector
\mathbf{y}
into two parts: the amount of variation explained by the covariates’ matrix
\mathbf{X}
through the specified model and the residual unexplained variation. Thus, the coefficient of determination

R_{\mathrm{classic}}^{2}=\frac{\mathrm{ESS}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})}{\mathrm{TSS}}=\frac{\mathrm{ESS}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})}{\mathrm{RSS}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})+\mathrm{ESS}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})}.

(9)

quantifies the proportion of variations in the response vector
\mathbf{y}
that is explained by the considered linear model.

In the case of a GAMM model defined by distribution structure (
3
) and conditional mean (
1
), unlike the parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, random effects
\mathbf{b}
are unobservable random quantities. As a result, the conditional mean
\mu(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta},\mathbf{b},\boldsymbol{\gamma})
and the residual

e_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta},\mathbf{b},\boldsymbol{\gamma})=y_{i}-\mu(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta},\mathbf{b},\boldsymbol{\gamma})

are not directly computable. This makes it challenging to define computable equivalents for the residual sum of squares (
7
) and the explained sum of squares (
8
)
(
Jaeger et al.,, 2017
;
Piepho,, 2019
)
.

2.3
Bayesian alternative

In the Bayesian approach, the random effects
\mathbf{b}
are treated as parameters, allowing the residual sum of squares (
7
) and the explained sum of squares (
8
) to be expressed in terms of the conditional mean
\mu(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta},\mathbf{b},\boldsymbol{\gamma})
and the residual
e_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta},\mathbf{b},\boldsymbol{\gamma})
instead of
\mu(\mathbf{x}_{i},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
and
e_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
. In this approach,
\mathrm{RSS}(\boldsymbol{\beta},\mathbf{b},\boldsymbol{\gamma})
and
\mathrm{ESS}(\boldsymbol{\beta},\mathbf{b},\boldsymbol{\gamma})
are considered random quantities, while the total sum of squares
\mathrm{TSS}
remains a constant. Within this framework, the coefficient of determination (
9
) can be replaced by the Bayesian version
(
Gelman and Pardoe,, 2006
)

R_{\mathrm{Bayes}}^{2}=\mathbb{E}\left[R_{\mathrm{classic}}^{2}\big|\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}\right]=\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{ESS}(\boldsymbol{\beta},\mathbf{b},\boldsymbol{\gamma})\big|\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}\right]}{\mathrm{TSS}},

where the numerator is the posterior mean of

\mathrm{ESS}(\boldsymbol{\beta},\mathbf{b},\boldsymbol{\gamma})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\mu(\mathbf{x}_{i},{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\mathbf{b},\boldsymbol{\gamma})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu(\mathbf{x}_{j},{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\mathbf{b},\boldsymbol{\gamma})\right)^{2};

i.e., expectation of
\mathrm{ESS}(\boldsymbol{\beta},\mathbf{b},\boldsymbol{\gamma})
with respect to the posterior density (
5
).

However, as demonstrated in a toy example by
Gelman et al., (2019)
, the posterior mean
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{ESS}(\boldsymbol{\beta},\mathbf{b},\boldsymbol{\gamma})\big|\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X}\right]
can be larger than the total sum of squares
\mathrm{TSS}
. This means that
R_{\mathrm{Bayes}}^{2}
can exceed one, making it impossible to interpret as the proportion of variations in the data explained by the model. In
section 3
we use the posterior predictive density to provide a similar decomposition of the total sum of squares and a definition of
R^{2}_{\mathrm{Bayes}}
without the above limitation.

3
Bayesian coefficient of determination

The posterior predictive density

\displaystyle p^{\mathrm{pred}}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}|\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})

\displaystyle=\int\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}f(\tilde{y}_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\phi},\tilde{\mathbf{b}})\right)\pi(\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})\mathrm{d}\tilde{\mathbf{b}}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}

(10)

represents the uncertainty about future observation
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}
for the response vector given the observed data
\mathbf{y}
and
\mathbf{X}
, after accounting for the uncertainty in random effects
\tilde{\mathbf{b}}
and model parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}

(see
Gelman et al.,, 2013
, Section 6.3)
. Note that the predictive posteior (
10
) assumes that given
\mathbf{X}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, the potential future response vector
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}
is conditionally independent of the observed response vector
\mathbf{y}
; i.e.,

p(\tilde{\mathbf{y}},\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{X},\boldsymbol{\theta})=p(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}|\mathbf{X},\boldsymbol{\theta})p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{X},\boldsymbol{\theta}).

3.1
Decomposition of posterior predictive variations

If
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}
is generated from the posterior predictive distribution
p^{\mathrm{pred}}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}|\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})
, it represents a potential new response vector with its associated random effects and parameter vector
(\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\sim\,\pi(\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})
. In other words,
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}
imitates what new data might look like based on the considered model and the observed data
\mathbf{y}
and
\mathbf{X}
. Thus, variation of
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}
reflects the plausible variability in future observations, stemming not only from the randomness in the observed data but also from the uncertainty in the model’s structure.

We consider the total sum of squares of posterior predictive deviations defined as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathrm{TSS}}(\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\tilde{y}_{i}-\bar{\tilde{y}}\right)^{2}\big|\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X},\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\theta}\right],

where
\bar{\tilde{y}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{y}_{i}/n
is the mean of the future response values. We define the posterior predictive residual

\tilde{e}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta},\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\gamma})=\tilde{y}_{i}-\mu(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta},\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\gamma})

as differences between future response value
\tilde{y}_{i}
and its corresponding conditional predictive mean
\mu(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta},\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\gamma})
. Similar to (
7
), we define the posterior predictive residual sum of squares as

\widetilde{\mathrm{RSS}}(\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\tilde{e}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta},\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\gamma})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\tilde{e}_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{j},\boldsymbol{\beta},\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\gamma})\right)^{2}\big|\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X},\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\theta}\right].

The quantity
\widetilde{\mathrm{TSS}}(\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})
captures the overall deviation of the future response values from their mean, providing insight into the variation in potential future responses based on the considered model and available data
\mathbf{y}
and
\mathbf{X}
. On the other hand,
\widetilde{\mathrm{RSS}}(\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})
measures the sum of squares of deviations for differences between future response values and their corresponding conditional means, representing the residual unexplained variations.

Analogous to the decomposition (
6
),
\widetilde{\mathrm{TSS}}(\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})
can be decomposed as (see
Appendix A
for details)

\widetilde{\mathrm{TSS}}(\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})=\widetilde{\mathrm{RSS}}(\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})+\widetilde{\mathrm{ESS}}(\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\theta}),

(11)

where, similar to (
8
), the explained sum of squares

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathrm{ESS}}(\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{X})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\mu(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta},\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\gamma})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu(\mathbf{x}_{j},\boldsymbol{\beta},\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\gamma})\right)^{2}

captures variations in the conditional mean (
1
). Unlike
\widetilde{\mathrm{TSS}}(\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})
and
\widetilde{\mathrm{RSS}}(\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})
,
\widetilde{\mathrm{TSS}}(\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{X})
does not depend on the observed response vector
\mathbf{y}
and only represents variations explained by the considered model over different rows of the covariate matrix
\mathbf{X}
. This decomposition means on average, the total variation in the potential future response vectors can be partitioned into two sources: variation due to the specified conditional mean (
1
) and residual unexplained variation.

3.2
New definition of Bayesian
R^{2}

Building on the same idea and structure as the classical
R^{2}
in (
9
), we define the Bayesian
R^{2}
for GAMMs as
the quantity

R^{2}_{\mathrm{Bayes}}(\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})=\frac{\widetilde{\mathrm{ESS}}(\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{X})}{\widetilde{\mathrm{TSS}}(\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})}.

This definition aligns with the Bayesian
R^{2}
proposed by
Gelman et al., (2019)
,

R^{2}_{\mathrm{Bayes}}=\frac{\mathrm{var}_{\mathrm{fit}}}{\mathrm{var}_{\mathrm{res}}+\mathrm{var}_{\mathrm{fit}}},

where
\mathrm{var}_{\mathrm{fit}}=\widetilde{\mathrm{ESS}}(\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{X})/(n-1)
,

\mathrm{var}_{\mathrm{res}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta},\mathbf{b},\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\phi}).

and
\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta},\mathbf{b},\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\phi})
is the conditional variance (
4
) (see
Appendix B
for details).

To estimate
R^{2}_{\mathrm{Bayes}}(\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})
, we can generate samples
(\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{1},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}),\ldots,(\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{L},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{L})\,\sim\,\pi(\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y})
from the posterior distribution (
5
) to obtain corresponding samples
R^{2}_{\mathrm{Bayes}}(\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{l},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{l}|\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})
,
l=1,\ldots,L
. These samples can then be used to approximate the distribution or specific properties (such as the mean) of
R^{2}_{\mathrm{Bayes}}(\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})
, which is a standard approach in Bayesian statistical inference.

4
Partial R-squared

As proposed by
Nakagawa and Schielzeth, (2013)
and
Jaeger et al., (2017)
, comparing a reduced model that includes either only fixed or only random effects with the full model that consists of both can serve as a basis for defining partial or marginal metrics of goodness of fitting.
However, the same idea can also be extended to compare any reduced model that is a simpler version of the full model by leaving out specific fixed effects, random effects or smooth terms. For example, by excluding the random effects and smoothing terms from the conditional mean (
1
), we obtain the reduced model

\mu_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta})=g^{-1}\left(\eta_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta})\right)

with
\eta_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta})=\mathbf{x}_{i,1}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{\beta}
.

Similar to the concept defined for linear models, we define the partial (also known as marginal or Type III) sum of squares for the terms in the full model that have been excluded in the reduced model.
This is defined as the conditional expected difference between the residual sum of squares of the full model and that of the reduced model; i.e., (see
Appendix C
)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathrm{ESS}}_{1}

\displaystyle(\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{X})=\widetilde{\mathrm{RSS}}_{0}(\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})-\widetilde{\mathrm{RSS}}(\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})

(12)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\left(\mu(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta},\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\gamma})-\mu_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta})\right)-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\mu(\mathbf{x}_{j},\boldsymbol{\beta},\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\gamma})-\mu_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{j},\boldsymbol{\beta})\right)\right)^{2}.

Here,
\widetilde{\mathrm{ESS}}_{1}(\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{X})
represents the variation attributable solely to the difference between the full and reduced models.
In other words,
\widetilde{\mathrm{ESS}}_{1}(\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{X})
can be interpreted as the sum of squares of deviation for including excluded terms (i.e., random effect) in the reduced model.

Equation (
12
) implies that the posterior predictive residual sum of squares for the reduced model,
\widetilde{\mathrm{RSS}}_{0}(\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})
, can be decomposed into two non-negative components:

•

one component,
\widetilde{\mathrm{RSS}}(\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})
, is the posterior predictive residual sum of squares for the full model, and

•

the other component,
\widetilde{\mathrm{ESS}}_{1}(\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{X})
, captures the sum of squared deviations caused by the inclusion of excluded terms in the reduced model.

Thus, the ratio

\frac{\widetilde{\mathrm{ESS}}_{1}(\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{X})}{\widetilde{\mathrm{RSS}}(\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})+\widetilde{\mathrm{ESS}}_{1}(\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{X})}

represents the amount of total posterior predictive variation not explained by the reduced model that can be attributed to the excluded terms from the full model. This ratio can be considered as a partial Bayesian
R^{2}
to quantify the contribution of the excluded terms.

Note that the above ratio is different than the ratio

\frac{\widetilde{\mathrm{ESS}}_{1}(\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{X})}{\widetilde{\mathrm{TSS}}(\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X})}

which measures the proportion of the total posterior predictive variation explained by the excluded terms in the full model.

The same approach can be repeated for any other term of the model by removing it from the conditional mean (
1
) to obtain the corresponding partial Bayesian
R^{2}
.

6
Conclusion

Despite its limitations and criticisms, such as being sensitive to sample size, not accounting for model overfitting, and potential for misleading interpretations,
R^{2}
remains a popular and widely used diagnostic tool in numerous applications due to its straightforward interpretation. The concept of
R^{2}
has evolved to include a broader range of statistical models beyond traditional linear models under the frequentist statistical framework. Notably,
Gelman et al., (2019)
proposed an intuitive definition for the Bayesian
R^{2}
based on the predictive posterior distribution.

In this work, we provided a clear decomposition of the total sum of squares of posterior predictive variations into explained and unexplained components, allowing us to naturally define Bayesian
R^{2}
as the ratio of explained variations to total variations. This approach provides a more consistent, applicable and interpretable measure of model fit for complex modelling
scenarios such as GAMMs. By integrating these advancements, the Bayesian
R^{2}

provides a promising method for accurately assessing goodness of fit across a wide range of statistical models.

For the reduced model, the posterior predictive residuals are given by

\tilde{e}_{0,i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta})=\tilde{y}_{i}-\mu_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta}),\quad i=1,\ldots,n,

and its conditional expectation under the full model is

\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{e}_{0,i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta})\big|\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X},\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\theta}\right]=\mu(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta},\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\gamma})-\mu_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta}).

They are related to the posterior predictive residuals of the full model with

\tilde{e}_{0,i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta})=\tilde{e}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta},\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\gamma})+\left(\mu(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta},\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\gamma})-\mu_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta})\right)

and hence

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}

\displaystyle\left(\tilde{e}_{0,i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\tilde{e}_{0,j}(\mathbf{x}_{j},\boldsymbol{\beta})\right)^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\tilde{e}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta},\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\gamma})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\tilde{e}_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{j},\boldsymbol{\beta},\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\gamma})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\left(\mu(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta},\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\gamma})-\mu_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta})\right)-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\mu(\mathbf{x}_{j},\boldsymbol{\beta},\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\gamma})-\mu_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{j},\boldsymbol{\beta})\right)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle+2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\tilde{e}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta},\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\gamma})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\tilde{e}_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{j},\boldsymbol{\beta},\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\gamma})\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\times\left(\left(\mu(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta},\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\gamma})-\mu_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\gamma})\right)-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\mu(\mathbf{x}_{j},\boldsymbol{\beta},\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\gamma})-\mu_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{j},\boldsymbol{\beta})\right)\right).

\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{y}_{i}\big|\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X},\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\theta}\right]=m(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta},\tilde{\mathbf{b}})
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\tilde{e}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\beta},\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\gamma})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\tilde{e}_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{j},\boldsymbol{\beta},\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\gamma})\right)\big|\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X},\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\boldsymbol{\theta}\right]=0

and hence the posterior predictive residual sum of squares for the reduced model is
