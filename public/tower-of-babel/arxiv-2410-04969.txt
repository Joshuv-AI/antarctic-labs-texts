Zariski pairs of conic-line arrangements with a unique conic.
Shinzo Bannai, Benoît Guerville-Ballé, Taketo Shirane

Abstract
. In this note, we present two pairs of conic-line arrangements admitting a unique conic and that form Zariski pairs, both of degree 9 9 . Their topologies are distinguished using the connected numbers.

1.
Introduction

A
Zariski pair
is a couple of two algebraic plane curves
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
–not necessarily irreducible– such that there exists a homeomorphism form
(\tub(\mathcal{C}_{1}),\mathcal{C}_{1})
to
(\tub(\mathcal{C}_{2}),\mathcal{C}_{2})
but no homeomorphism from
(\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{2},\mathcal{C}_{1})
to
(\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{2},\mathcal{C}_{2})
, where
\tub(\mathcal{C}_{i})
is a tubular neighborhood of
\mathcal{C}_{i}
. We often refer to these properties as, having the same
combinatorics
and having different
embedded topology
, respectively. As first noted in
[
3
]
, the first condition can be replaced by the equivalence of combinatorial data given by the local type of each singular point of
\mathcal{C}_{i}
and the correspondance between the irreducible components of
\mathcal{C}_{i}
and the local branches of each singular point. The first example of Zariski pair is, of course, due to Zariski
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
. It is formed by two irreducible sextics with six cusps, where in the first curve the cusps lie on a conic and not in the second one. The non-existence of a homeomorphism between their embedded topology is obtained by showing that the fundamental groups of their complements
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{i}
are non-isomorphic –one is Abelian and the other is not.

In
[
4
]
, Degtyarev proves that the rigid isopoty class of curves of degree at most 5 is determined by the combinatorial data. He also classifies in
[
5
]
the isotopy classes of the curves of degree
6
with simple singularities. In particular, his work implies that the minimal degree for there to exist a Zariski pair is
6
, which is realized by the seminal example of Zariski formed by irreducible curves. On the other end of the spectrum of algebraic plane curves, we find line arrangements whose irreducible components are all of degree
1
. Currently, the smallest known Zariski pairs of line arrangement have
11
lines
[
2
,
6
]
. By the work of Nazir, Yoshinaga and Fei
[
9
,
13
]
, we know that the smallest possible degree for a Zariski pair of line arrangement is
10
. The existence of such Zariski pair with
10
lines is still an open question. In between, one has reduced curves. In
[
1
]
, Artal gives the first example of a cubic-line Zariski pair formed by one smooth cubic and three inflexional tangents –so they are curves of degree
6
. In
[
12
]
, Tokunaga constructs two Zariski pairs of conic-lines arrangements of degree
7
. The first is formed by curves with
3
conics and
1
line, while the curves in the second one have
2
conics and
3
lines. Since these examples, several other conic-lines Zariski pairs have been discovered, but none of them admits a unique conic. The purpose of this note is to provide two examples of such conic-line Zariski pair with a unique conic. They are both of degree
9
with only nodes, ordinary triple points and tacnodes as singularities.

2.
Conic-line Zariski pairs with a unique conic

Before recalling the definition of the connected number and giving the two examples, we introduce some vocabulary to formulate more precisely the goal of this note.

Definition 2.1
.

A
(d_{1},\dots,d_{r})
-arrangement
is a reduced algebraic curve with
d_{i}
irreducible components of degree
i
, all of them being smooth.

For example, the Zariski pair given by Artal in
[
1
]
is formed by
(3,0,1)
-arrangements, while the two examples of Tokunaga in
[
12
]
are composed by
(1,3)
-arrangements and
(3,2)
-arrangements. With this notation a conic-line arrangement with a unique conic is a
(k,1)
-arrangement.

We could easily construct a Zariski pair of
(k,1)
-arrangements by taking a Zariski pair of line arrangements and adding a conic intersecting transversally the line arrangements. Nevertheless, in such a trivial example, the conic does not play a role in the difference of topologies. It appears natural to define a minimal Zariski pair as follow.

Definition 2.2
.

A Zariski pair
\mathcal{C}_{1}
,
\mathcal{C}_{2}
is
minimal
if no pair of proper sub-curves of
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
is a Zariski pair.

Using the vocabulary previously introduced, one can reformulate the goal of this paper as follow:

Construct a minimal Zariski pair of
(k,1)
-arrangements, for a small
k
.

2.1.
The connected number

The embedded topologies of the following examples are distinguished using the
connected number
introduced by the third author in
[
11
]
.
It is an invariant of the embedded topology of reducible algebraic plane curves in smooth varieties. In the following, for simplicity, we adapt the definition and main result of
[
11
]
to the particular case of algebraic plane curves in
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{2}
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
B
be an algebraic plane curve of
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{2}
and let
\Phi:X\rightarrow\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{2}
be a cyclic cover branched over
B
. Let
C
be an algebraic plane curve of
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{2}
such that
B
and
C
do not have common irreducible components, and
C\setminus B
is connected. The
connected number
of
C
for the cover
\Phi
is the number of connected components of
\Phi^{-1}(C\setminus B)
. It is denoted by
c_{\Phi}(C)
.

As explicitly described in the following theorem, the connected number is an invariant of the homeomorphism type of
(\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{2},B\cup C)
which respects an order on the irreducible components of the curve
B\cup C
.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
B_{1},B_{2}
be two curves of
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{2}
and let
m\geq 2
an integer. For each
i\in\{1,2\}
, let
\Phi_{i}:X_{i}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{2}
be a
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
-cover induced by a surjection
\theta_{i}:\pi_{1}(\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{2}\setminus B_{i})\twoheadrightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{m}
. Let
C_{1}
be as in Definition
2.3
, and assume that there exists a homeomorphism
h:\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{2}
and an automorphism
\sigma:\mathbb{Z}_{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{m}
such that
h(B_{1})=B_{2}
and
\sigma\circ\theta_{2}\circ h_{*}=\theta_{1}
. If we denote
C_{2}=h(C_{1})
then
c_{\Phi_{1}}(C_{1})=c_{\Phi_{2}}(C_{2})
.

To distinguish the two pairs of the present note, we only use surjection
\theta_{i}
on
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, so the automorphism
\sigma
can only be the identity. Also, the curves
C
will be nodal, so we can reformulate
[
11
, Corollary 2.6]
as follows.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
B
and
C
be two curves of
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{2}
such that
B
is of even degree,
C
is nodal with smooth irreducible components, and
B
and
C
intersect outside the nodes of
C
. If
\Phi_{B}
is the double cover branched at
B
then one has that
c_{\Phi_{B}}(C)=2
if and only if there exists a divisor
D
on
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{2}
with
2D_{\mid C}=B_{\mid C}
, and
c_{\Phi_{B}}(C)=1
otherwise.

Remark 2.6
.

If, for each
P\in B\cap C
, the local intersection multiplicity of
B
and
C
is
2
, then the existence of the divisor
D
corresponds to the existence of a curve
\mathcal{D}
of degree
\deg(B)/2
passing through all the points of
C\cap B
which do not contain irreducible components of
C
.

Theorem 2.7
.

There is no homeomorphism
h:\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{2}
such that
h(\mathcal{C}\cup\mathcal{B}^{1})=\mathcal{C}\cup\mathcal{B}^{2}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{C}
is the union of three lines in generic position, then it is a nodal curve. Then, the curves
B_{i}
are of even degree and all the intersection points between
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{B}_{i}
are outside this nodes. So one can apply Proposition
2.5
. Furthermore, since the intersection points between
B_{i}
and
C
are triple points and tacnodes, then the local intersection multiplicity is
2
. So, we are under the hypothesis of Remark
2.6
. Thus, we have to study the space of degree
\deg(\mathcal{B}^{i})/2=3
curves passing through the intersection points of
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{B}^{i}
, that is, through the
7
triple points and the
2
tacnodes listed above.

We denote them by
P_{1},\dots,P_{9}
, and by
[p_{i},q_{i},r_{i}]
their respective projective coordinates. The dimension of that space is equal to the corank minus
1
of the
9\times 10
matrix formed by the vectors
(p_{i}^{a}q_{i}^{b}r_{i}^{c})_{a+b+c=3}
, for
i\in\{1,\dots,9\}
.
1
1

1

The term
-1
is due to the fact that the equation of a curve is well-defined up to scalar.
A direct computation shows that this dimension is
1
for
\mathcal{C}\cup\mathcal{B}^{1}
and
0
for
\mathcal{C}\cup\mathcal{B}^{2}
. Since
P_{1},\dots,P_{9}
are on
\mathcal{C}
which is of degree
3

\mathcal{C}\cup\mathcal{B}^{2}
, there is no curve of degree
3
which pass through these
9

for

, while such a curve exist for

. By Remark
2.6

c_{\Phi_{\mathcal{B}^{1}}}(\mathcal{C})\neq c_{\Phi_{\mathcal{B}^{2}}}(\mathcal{C})

Let assume that there exists a homeomorphism

such that
h(\mathcal{C}\cup\mathcal{B}^{1})=\mathcal{C}\cup\mathcal{B}^{2}

are the only tangent lines of the unique conic

Theorem 2.9
.

There is no homeomorphism
h:\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{2}
such that
h(\mathcal{C}\cup\mathcal{B}^{1})=\mathcal{C}\cup\mathcal{B}^{2}
.

Proof.

We apply the same strategy as for Theorem
2.7
. The curve
\mathcal{C}
is formed by a smooth conic and a transversal line, so it is a nodal curve. As the intersection points with
\mathcal{B}^{i}
are outside the nodes and the local intersection multiplicities are
2

2.6

c_{\Phi_{\mathcal{B}^{1}}}(\mathcal{C})\neq c_{\Phi_{\mathcal{B}^{2}}}(\mathcal{C})

Assume that

h(\mathcal{C}\cup\mathcal{B}^{1})=\mathcal{C}\cup\mathcal{B}^{2}

2.4.
Minimality

Let us denote by
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{A})
the realization space of the combinatorics of
\mathcal{A}
. In other words,
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{A})
is the set of all algebraic curves combinatorially equivalent to
\mathcal{A}
. One can adapt
[
9
, Proposition 3.3]
to the case of
(k,1)
-arrangements as follow –the proof is straightforward and left to the reader.

Proposition 2.11
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a
(k,1)
-arrangement with lines labelled
L_{1},\dots,L_{k}
. We assume that there exists
t_{0}\in\{1,\dots,k-1\}
such that
L_{t}
intersects transversally the conic of
\mathcal{A}
, for all
t\geq t_{0}
. For
t\in\{t_{0},\dots,k\}
, we define
n_{t}
has the number of intersection points between
L_{t}
and
\mathcal{A}_{t}=\mathcal{A}\setminus\{L_{t},\dots,L_{k}\}
with a local intersection multipliticty greater or equal than
2
. If
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{A}_{t_{0}})
is irreducible and, for all
t\in\{t_{0},\dots,k\}
, we have
n_{t}\leq 2
, then
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{A})
is irreducible too and so it is connected.

Remark 2.12
.

If
\mathcal{A}
is the
(k,1)
-arrangement formed by a conic and one or two tangent lines, then the realization space
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{A})
is irreducible.

Using this proposition above, one can prove the following theorem.

Theorem 2.13
.

The two Zariski pairs described above are minimal.

Proof.

Let denote by
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}
one of the examples of Zariski pair above. The arrangements
\mathcal{A}_{1}\setminus C
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}\setminus C
are line arrangements with
7
lines. Due to the classification obtained by Nazir and Yoshinaga
[
9
]
, we know that there is no Zariski pair of
7
lines. By a case-by-case study, we have that, for all
i\in\{1,\dots,7\}
, the
(6,1)
-arrangements
\mathcal{A}_{1}\setminus L_{i}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}\setminus L_{i}
verify the condition of Proposition
2.11
–up to a relabelling of the lines. Using Remark
2.12
, we deduce that the realization space
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{A}_{1}\setminus L_{i})=\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{A}_{2}\setminus L_{i})
is connected. So,
\mathcal{A}_{1}\setminus L_{i}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}\setminus L_{i}
do not form a Zariski pair. By an iteration of these arguments, we obtain that no proper subarrangements of
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}
form a Zariski pair.
∎

3.
Discussion

3.1.
Difference between the two examples

The combinatorics of these pairs can be distinguish from each other as follow. In each one, the conic is unique so an equivalence in the combinatorics should preserve the number of singular points of each type on the conic. In the first example, the conic contains two triples points while it has four in the second one. Equivalently, one could compare the number of double points on the conic.

3.2.
Other methods of distinction

In the two examples presented, we distinguish the embedded topology using the connected number. It is an invariant of the embedded topology and so it does not provide any information one the complements and their fundemantal groups. Moreover, due to
[
11
, Remark 2.2 (iii)]
, we know that the connected number is not determined by the fundamental group of the complement.
