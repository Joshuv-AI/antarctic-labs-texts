Overcoming the streetlight effect: Qualitative examination of community experts’ perceptions of foundations of learning and development (FOLD) skills
Authors: Marilyn N. Ahun, Dana C. McCoy, Terri J. Sabol, Yuri Kim, Whitney Warren, Joshua Jeong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDevelopmental science has a long history of studying skills that children need to thrive. However, there has been a primary focus on academic skills, with little attention to the breadth and diversity of other skills that young children need to thrive. Furthermore, little is known about the extent to which community experts involved in early childhood care and education (ECCE) value different early developmental skills. This qualitative study examined how community experts (parents, preschool educators, clinicians, and ECCE leaders) defined and conceptualized six foundations of learning and development (FOLD) skills of child development (curiosity, creativity, critical thinking, self‐regulation and executive function, perspective taking, and internal representations of self). We conducted 60 virtual in‐depth interviews with a socioeconomically, racially, and geographically diverse sample of parents (n = 26), educators (n = 17), clinicians (n = 11), and ECCE leaders (n = 6) living in rural and urban areas across the United States. Participants universally acknowledged the importance of FOLD skills as central to development and well‐being in the early childhood period, while also recognizing their interrelatedness with one another. Most participants highlighted self‐regulation and executive function as a core skill. Findings affirm the value of these FOLD skills and highlight the importance of including them as target outcomes of ECCE interventions.

INTRODUCTION

Decades of research show that high‐quality early childhood care and education (ECCE) programs can positively impact children's learning outcomes, both in the short and the long terms. 1 , 2 Although this research has successfully galvanized billions of dollars in investments in public preschool programming across the United States, 3 it has also been criticized for benchmarking the success of ECCE programs against a somewhat narrow set of largely academic metrics. In doing so, researchers have raised concerns that this body of literature may be missing key insights regarding the ways in which ECCE is—or could be—promoting the broader set of diverse skills that children need to thrive in the 21st century. 4 , 5 A recent review explored ways to address this streetlight effect by shining light on the evidence‐based developmental skills that hold the most promise for supporting life‐long learning and development. 6 Drawing from more than 60 years’ worth of research in developmental psychology and related disciplines, the review highlights a set of foundations of learning and development (FOLD) skillsa that are aligned with the developmental imperatives of the early childhood period, and yet have largely been neglected as target outcomes of ECCE intervention evaluations. The six FOLD skills are: curiosity, creativity, critical thinking, self‐regulation and executive function, perspective taking, and internal representations of self (see Figure 1 for colloquial definitions of each skill). They are defined as being fundamental to and predictive of other key forms of learning, development, and knowledge acquisition; active and dynamic in nature, meaning that they are formed continuously through children's hands‐on engagement, reflection, construction, and interaction with the physical and social world; cross‐cutting across and not cleanly fitting into any one domain of development (e.g., cognitive, social, emotional, etc.); and developmentally salient in that the preschool ages (i.e., between 3 and 5 years) are a sensitive period for their formation. 6

Foundations of learning and development (FOLD) skills identified by McCoy and Sabol. 6

Importantly, there is a general consensus in the research literature on the definition of these skills (e.g., perspective taking is generally agreed to represent the ability to recognize and reason about the mental state of others), as well as evidence across developmental science suggesting that these skills are viable targets for ECCE interventions. 7 , 8 , 9 In particular, there is evidence that each of these FOLD skills is malleable, measurable, predictive of other key outcomes, and relevant across diverse contexts. Nevertheless, with the exception of self‐regulation, these skills are rarely evaluated as outcomes of ECCE interventions. 6 , 10 Indeed, findings from a recent systematic review of evaluations of interventions to support children during the preschool period show that these FOLD skills accounted for less than 13% of reported outcomes. 10

Although McCoy and Sabol shine a light on these often neglected, yet important FOLD skills, their review itself also suffers from the streetlight effect by focusing on the scientific literature and thus leaving out the voices of key community experts 2 invested in children's early education and development. 6 Understanding how community experts make meaning of FOLD skills and view their relevancy is critical for their uptake within the broader ECCE system, where various community experts play complementary and distinct roles in supporting child development. Parents have the most proximal view of their own children and the skills they value for their children. Educators often use measures as targets within their own classroom practices and communicate the results of performance on individual measures to families. Policymakers, system administrators, and other ECCE leaders also use the results of these measures to make data‐informed decisions about how to improve the quality of ECCE programs. Qualitative research is a useful methodology for addressing this important limitation and adopting a more user‐centered approach, as it can elucidate how various community experts perceive the relevance (or lack thereof) of the FOLD skills to the development of the child(ren) in their lives, as well as the meaningfulness they ascribe to these skills in the different settings in which they live and work. Given that different community experts interact with children in different contexts (e.g., school, home, and clinic) and under different circumstances (e.g., in groups versus one‐on‐one), understanding their unique perspectives on the FOLD skills will provide a more holistic understanding of whether and how to support these skills in ECCE settings.

Only a handful of prior studies have used qualitative approaches to examine community experts’ perspectives on the developmental skills of young children (between the ages of 2 and 6 years), with almost all of them focusing on their social and emotional behaviors and skills. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 These studies concluded that community experts placed a high value on social and emotional skills (e.g., conflict resolution and relationship‐building) in the preschool period, with some finding them more important than academic skills for children's development. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 However, these studies have drawn from relatively small samples that have focused on one group of community experts at a time—mostly comprising preschool educators and parents—thus precluding the comparison of different perspectives on children's developmental skills.

The present study draws on the lived experiences of contemporary parents/primary caregivers, educators, and other key community experts (e.g., clinicians and ECCE leaders) actively involved in the daily promotion of young children's learning and development to qualitatively explore their perceptions of these FOLD skills. Specifically, we qualitatively examined how nonacademic community experts perceive the six FOLD skills to further scientific understanding of how a large and diverse group of community experts involved in ECCE value FOLD skills for preschool children's development and success, with the eventual aim of informing the development and evaluation of ECCE interventions (Figure 1). METHODS

Study design and sites

We used a phenomenological qualitative design to examine community experts’ perspectives about the six FOLD skills identified in McCoy and Sabol's review and their relevance for preschool‐age children's development and well‐being. 6 , 15 We conducted individual in‐depth interviews (IDIs) with parents, preschool educators, clinicians, and ECCE leaders. The COREQ checklist guided the reporting of the methodology and study results 16 (see Supporting Information). IDIs were conducted virtually via Zoom meetings with individuals across the United States. Participants were based in rural and urban areas of 18 states (California, Connecticut, Delaware, Florida, Georgia, Illinois, Louisiana, Maryland, Massachusetts, Michigan, Nebraska, New Jersey, New York, North Carolina, Texas, Utah, Virginia, and West Virginia) and Washington, D.C.

Sampling

We used a stratified purposive sampling design to recruit a sample of racially, socioeconomically, and geographically diverse parents, preschool educators, clinicians, and ECCE leaders. First, we worked with a local research center that had connections with organizations across the country to obtain a list of organizations that focus on early childhood education, health, and development, and work with at least one of our community expert groups. We initially identified 10 organizations from this list, ensuring that there was diversity in the geographic location, type of community expert group the organizations worked with, and racial and socioeconomic compositions of targeted populations. Over time, to increase recruitment with educators and ECCE leaders, we used a snowball sampling technique by reaching out to colleagues to identify additional participants. Across all community expert groups, inclusion criteria were that they were an adult (18 years or older) who spoke English or Spanish fluently and were able to provide informed consent. Data collection

Data were collected using semi‐structured interview guides that were adapted for use in IDIs with each community expert group. Interview guides were written in English and translated into Spanish by a native Spanish‐speaking research assistant. The topic guide began with a set of open‐ended questions on community experts’ perspectives on what thriving looked like for 3‐ to 5‐year‐olds and the developmental skills children needed to thrive. Community experts’ responses to these initial, broad questions were analyzed separately and are reported in an upcoming manuscript. 17 After answering these questions, community experts were then shown Figure 1 and asked various questions including which FOLD skills they thought were most or least important, what they would change about the list of skills, and how they perceived these skills in relation to academic skills. These latter questions about the FOLD skills are the focus of the current paper.

Data were collected by a team of nine American and international Master's and PhD students (including Y.K. and W.W.) from Harvard University (T.H. Chan School of Public Health, Graduate School of Education, and Kennedy School), Northwestern University, and Stanford University who were supervised by a team of American and international researchers (M.N.A., D.C.M., T.S., and J.J.). All research assistants were female and either had prior experience working with children in ECCE services or conducting early child development research (or both). All research assistants speak English fluently and one, who is a native Spanish speaker, was responsible for conducting IDIs with Spanish‐speaking participants. The first author (M.N.A.) led a 5‐day training that involved 3 days of virtual instruction about qualitative research methods and effective interviewing skills and 2 days of virtual field‐based practice with nonstudy participants. Five pilot IDIs were conducted with parents, preschool educators, and ECCE leaders. Through piloting, the topic guides were iteratively modified to refine the wording of questions, add probes, and improve translations.

All interviews were conducted in a password‐protected secure Zoom meeting room that was audio‐recorded. Research assistants conducted these interviews from a private location. On average, the IDIs lasted 1 h. English audio files were uploaded to a paid online transcription service, and research assistants reviewed each file to verify the accuracy of the transcription. For Spanish audio files, an independent native Spanish‐speaking Master's student was hired to transcribe transcripts verbatim into English. Data were collected from April to August 2023.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using thematic content analysis. 18 First, an initial codebook was developed by M.N.A. based on the interview topic guides. Once the draft version was developed, five team members independently piloted the codebook using a set of 11 (three parents, two educators, and six clinicians) transcripts and iteratively refined it through a series of discussions in April–May 2023. The team of research assistants—after receiving 2 additional days of training on qualitative data coding and analysis—then independently conducted line‐by‐line coding of all transcripts from May to October 2023 using the Atlas.ti software. Weekly meetings were held in the first 2 months of coding to review memos, resolve any uncertainties, and make refinements to the codebook as needed. Finally, three analysts (M.N.A., Y.K., and W.W.) collaboratively synthesized the codes and had weekly meetings through August 2023 to discuss and consolidate themes and draft results. Discussions were held with all coauthors (D.C.M., T.S., and J.J.) to obtain their input in the write‐up and interpretation of findings. Results were triangulated across the perspectives of the different community expert groups (i.e., parents, preschool educators, clinicians, and ECCE leaders). Findings were shared with participants in a 1‐page lay summary and their feedback was solicited to validate our findings and interpretations. Only two participants shared their feedback, indicating that our summary resonated with their perspectives.

Ethics approvals

The protocol for this study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Boards of the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health (#IRB22‐1669), the Harvard Graduate School of Education (#IRB23‐0001), and Northwestern University (#STU00218772). Consent forms were e‐mailed to participants before each interview in their preferred language (English or Spanish). Research assistants were trained to answer any questions via email or at the beginning of the IDI. Informed verbal consent was obtained from all participants prior to each interview. Participants received a $30 VISA e‐gift card as compensation for their time after the IDI.

RESULTS

The descriptive characteristics of the study sample are summarized in Table 3 by the community expert group. On average, parents had two children, and the index child was 3.76 years old (SD = 0.9; 64% female). Sixty‐five percent of index children attended private preschools, 15% attended public or home‐based preschools, and the remaining 20% did not specify the preschool type. Educators primarily worked with children in the early childhood age range (0–6 years), whereas clinicians worked with children across a much wider age range (0–21 years). Of the 14 educators (out of 17) that specified the type of preschool they worked in, 36% were in private, 43% in public, and 21% in Head Start preschools. Most of them worked with a racially and ethnically diverse group of children (e.g., a mix of Latinx, Black, Southeast Asian, and White), with only three educators indicating that the majority of children they worked with were White. Educators had class sizes that ranged from 9 to 37 children per class (mean = 18) and the average years of teaching experience was 17.3 (SD = 11.5). Clinicians also mostly worked with racially and ethnically diverse groups of children. Of the 7 (out of 11) clinicians who specified their job, 43% were child development specialists and the remaining 57% consisted of a pediatrician, a speech pathologist, a social worker, and a psychologist. Almost all (83%) ECCE leaders held senior (i.e., CEO and director) positions at mostly nonprofit organizations serving young children and their families.

After being shown Figure 1, participants were asked to share their initial reactions. Overall reactions to the list of FOLD skills were positive (Table 4A). Many noted that they had already “named specifically and…alluded to” the FOLD skills when discussing the skills they thought were important for children's thriving (41‐year‐old female ECCE leader [Early Years Consultant]) (Table 4B and C). When asked if they agreed with the importance of the skills listed for preschool children's healthy development, all participants confirmed. One educator walked through how each skill related to her practice in the classroom. An ECCE leader shared that the definitions expanded her understanding of these terms. Two clinicians mentioned how helpful it was for them to have these skills defined. Parents felt that the skills are all interconnected and that many of them are things they hear and think about frequently. Despite this consensus on the importance of the FOLD skills, some participants—mostly parents—had issues with how these skills were defined. For example, a clinician and a parent believed that these skills were very high level (i.e., they may mean different things to different people), and one parent provided insight into the unfamiliarity of how some skills are termed (Table 4D). Overall, participants’ perceptions of the FOLD skills were divided between those who understood their definitions and were able to provide concrete examples of how the skills were exhibited by the children they work or interact with, and those who thought the definitions could be elaborated to be more comprehensive of other skills they thought were important for children's development. Curiosity

An ECCE leader (agency lead at an early childhood organization) described curiosity as “… a desire to learn or question.” Multiple parents, educators, and one clinician perceived curiosity as an innate skill for children, with the educator noting that children learned in stages and that this started with curiosity. As seen in these examples (Table 4E−G), the understanding that curiosity consists of asking questions and wanting to learn and that it is a skill that comes naturally to preschool children is a shared consensus among the different community expert groups. Nevertheless, community experts diverged in their perspectives on how curiosity should be promoted in children. While one educator commented that curiosity should not be taught, another educator and a clinician felt differently that it should be nurtured, fostered, and built on. Other parents mentioned that curiosity is a healthy sign in young children and something children will lean into naturally when given space. This perception of curiosity as being innate was also expressed by three clinicians who shared that children who had secure attachment with their parents were more likely to explore and that a lack of curiosity in a child was a cause for concern due to the perceived innateness of curiosity. In addition, some participants did not think the skill was generally valued, with one parent cautioning that she did not believe curiosity is valued equally across cultures.

Creativity

Participants’ reactions to creativity were similar to their reactions to curiosity. Specifically, although some parents shared that their children exhibited creativity frequently and naturally, two parents highlighted that they do not see this skill being encouraged and supported equally across cultures, and another mentioned that she found it hard to identify and quantify (Table 4H). In terms of definitions, participants across different community expert groups described creativity as children being imaginative or “inventive in their play” (31‐year‐old female educator [Head Start preschool]). One clinician, however, expressed her disagreement with the definition of creativity as a skill that requires novelty, giving an example of how a child's ability to create their own version of something they have experienced was still an example of creativity even if it did not involve a new idea.

Critical thinking

Across community expert groups, participants’ definitions of critical thinking were relatively similar, with most of them emphasizing that the key aspects of this skill were the ability to “interpret the information” (32‐year‐old female clinician [speech pathologist]), “break it into different parts” (52‐year‐old female CEO of nonprofit social services organization), and “take that information and put their own thoughts together from it” (30‐year‐old mother [4‐year‐old son]). However, there was some variation in their reactions to this skill. Some participants, mostly parents and clinicians, questioned whether this skill will develop or is important to develop during this age range. Another parent suggested that critical thinking is hard to evaluate at this age because a child might not have the vocabulary to articulate their internal thought process. However, one parent noted that critical thinking is important for relationships with others and for ensuring decisions are evaluated, instead of just blindly obeying others. One parent wished that her parents had emphasized critical thinking more in her early years and another valued teaching critical thinking, as she believed that many adults in the United States are currently lacking this skill. Finally, one clinician shared that while she believed that most 3‐year‐olds can engage in critical thinking, she thought that “making thoughtful conclusions” was a subjective component of the skill's definition.

Self‐regulation and executive function

Most participants paused to reflect on self‐regulation and executive function during their initial reactions. They generally perceived this skill as being linked to emotional regulation. For example, two parents described this skill as having “a lot of patience and understanding that like she has feelings and emotions and it's hard” (31‐year‐old mother [5‐year‐old daughter]) and another provided a concrete example of how it is the ability to not “throw tantrums and scream when she [her daughter] doesn't get her way” (42‐year‐old mother [5‐year‐old daughter]).

While almost all participants valued this skill, several participants questioned the wording of the definition and the universality of mastery for all children. Two parents noted that self‐regulation feels different than the other skills, as it must be taught very intentionally, whereas the other skills are more innate to young children. One parent said that they were unfamiliar with the term executive function, while another believed executive function is a skill to be worked on at school, while parents are capable of supporting the self‐regulation piece. Additionally, there was commentary around the use of the words “control emotions” in the definition of this skill (Table 4I and J). For example, one parent said that children cannot control their emotions if they cannot identify them. She believed self‐regulation is about navigating emotions, not just controlling them. An ECCE leader added that, while controlling your behavior is good and necessary, it is also acceptable to be mad sometimes. Additionally, a clinician highlighted the role of frustration tolerance in developing a reaction but did not like the word “control.”

Two parents mentioned that mastering self‐regulation and executive function is likely harder for children with disabilities or developmental delays than it is for neurotypical children and that supporting them in this work required more patience and understanding on the parent's part. Neither parent highlighted another FOLD skill as being harder for neurodivergent children to master. Some parents shared that self‐regulation and executive function should be a priority at this age as it will support children in achieving their goals and working with others. However, an ECCE leader expressed concern that not all children have access to learning about this skill in equitable ways. She believed that while upper‐middle‐class White children are taught using research‐based best practices, Black and Brown children, especially boys, get taught a version of self‐regulation that involves shutting everything down and controlling, instead of processing, their emotions.

Perspective taking

This skill was primarily described as relating to children's social‐emotional skills. Specifically, participants described it as a good skill for socializing because of the ability to understand, interact, and participate in the same activity together. Additionally, several parents, an educator, and a clinician listed empathy as a skill they valued for thriving and wondered if empathy would be included in perspective taking. Two parents also highlighted that they believe empathy and kindness should be highlighted more within this definition, while another said that the definition needed more information for them to understand it well.

There was a diversity of opinions on whether perspective taking was developmentally appropriate for preschool‐aged children. On one hand, an educator, a few parents, and some clinicians explained that although they thought the skill was important, they felt that it was not developmentally appropriate as it was too challenging for preschoolers to understand others’ points of view. Some clinicians suggested that it could only be developed toward the end of the 3‐ to 5‐year age range. On the other hand, other educators, parents, and clinicians asserted that all the FOLD skills can be taught in age‐appropriate ways. For example, perspective taking can be asking a child who does not want to share how they might feel if their friend chose not to share them.

Internal representations of self

While many participants highlighted how internal representations of self supports children's thriving, this skill also received the greatest number of questions. Participants expressed a lack of clarity about the meaning of this skill, perceived it as the most abstract of the FOLD skills, a new concept for most of them, or that it was not really an idea they thought about. An ECCE leader and a parent suggested that it is difficult to understand as the definition is overly academic and another parent asked for further clarification so that participants could understand better. Two mothers who took an attempt at defining the skill described it as instilling confidence and promoting a positive and “healthy” sense of self (Table 4K and L).

Participants who felt that they had an understanding and prior knowledge of this term expressed that internal representations of self helps you know where you are the best fit, allows you to recognize yourself and be well in your mind and body, and supports the development of self‐esteem and confidence. One parent valued internal representations of self because it encourages “privileged White families” to approach hard‐to‐talk‐about topics like race. ECCE leaders, including a CEO of a nonprofit social services organization, valued it because it enables children to advocate for themselves, and an assistant director at a nonprofit childcare center valued it as knowing different cultures. As with perspective taking, one educator also identified internal representations of self as a skill that develops later in life.

Changes to the list of FOLD skills

After sharing their initial reactions, participants were asked if they would like to remove or add anything to the list of FOLD skills. All but four participants said that they would not remove anything from the list. One parent said that she would remove internal representations of self because it is not an age‐appropriate skill, an educator and a clinician explained that they would remove curiosity because it was too vague and confusing a term, and one educator stated that he would replace perspective taking with communication because “communicating is a couple of steps before their perspective taking.” (37‐year‐old male educator [private play‐based preschool]).

In terms of adding skills, the most frequently cited suggestion, from eight participants (a mix of parents, clinicians, and ECCE leaders), was communication skills, which one parent defined as not just the ability to speak, but also to listen, absorb information, decide what you want to communicate, and to use body language. A number of participants also suggested the inclusion of the following skills: independence, self‐efficacy or self‐help, physicality and fine or gross motor skills, optimism, positivity, and expression of joy, play, or fun. Additionally, some participants felt that the list of skills was missing social‐emotional skills or emotional intelligence, consideration for different learning styles, attention to diversity, the importance of boundaries, and attention to how these skills can be used to benefit the community, not just the individual. Some participants questioned whether the skills they believed to be missing could fit under the definition of one of the existing FOLD skills, for example, including patience under self‐regulation, kindness and empathy under perspective taking, and sequential thinking and the ability to follow directions under critical thinking. Parents often noted that they wanted to include skills that they noticed their child struggling with. For example, one parent discussed how her 3‐year‐old needs to develop gross motor skills due to frequently running into things.

Perceptions of the relative importance of FOLD skills and their relatedness to one another

Across community expert groups, when participants were asked what the most important FOLD skill was, the two most common responses were self‐regulation and executive function and curiosity.

Those who named self‐regulation and executive function primarily focused on its importance for children's emotional well‐being and their ability to function well in any environment, with most participants specifically highlighting the importance of this skill for doing well in school and work environments (Table 4M and N). These participants also described self‐regulation as the basis of the other skills, with one participant sharing that, “if we can teach them [children] to do that [referring to self‐regulation and executive function], then we can move on to…[discusses other skills]” (54‐year‐old female clinician [child development specialist]). However, four participants—three parents and an educator—also identified self‐regulation and executive function as the least important of the FOLD skills. The parents felt that it was a “very adult” skill that was helpful for doing well in work and social environments, but that it was not necessary for children to be happy. One parent explained that although they thought the skill was important, they felt that it was “over fetishize[d]” in American culture in a way that was counterproductive (Table 4O).

Participants who identified curiosity as the most important skill justified it as being core to the other skills. One educator emphasized its importance by stating that “the only way you can grow is to continue to ask questions about things” (45‐year‐old female educator [public preschool]).

Overall, there were differences across community expert groups regarding the FOLD skill that participants thought was the most important. All expert groups identified self‐regulation and executive function as being among the most important, and all groups with the exception of clinicians also named curiosity as a very important skill. In addition, parents included perspective taking in their list of most important skills, whereas ECCE leaders added internal representations of self and creativity. Clinicians, for the most part, only named self‐regulation and executive functioning as the most important skill.

For some parents, perspective taking was seen as the most important because of the significant role of relationships over the life course and the importance of sharing for fostering those relationships. Other parents listed perspective taking along with internal representations of self as the most important skills because recognizing how you and others feel is necessary to build the other FOLD skills. The two ECCE leaders who named internal representations of self as the most important skill emphasized the importance of knowing one's self and being “grounded in yourself first and foremost” (34‐year‐old agency lead) as key initial steps children had to take before being able to develop the other skills, as some of them are more focused on external factors.

When asked to identify the least important skill, most participants said that they could not single any of the skills out because they were all equally important and interconnected; it was not possible to have any of the FOLD skills without any of the others (Table 4P). This perspective that all the skills were equally important was not shared by the four aforementioned participants who stated that they would remove some skills (i.e., internal representations of self, curiosity, and perspective taking) from the list. Although other participants did acknowledge the importance and interrelatedness of all the skills, a few of them ranked internal representations of self as the least important skill. Reasons for this included that it would develop as children get older, uncertainty about whether it is important to belong to a social group (as indicated in the definition of the skill), not clearly understanding the definition, and concerns that the term was overly academic.

Across subgroups, educators primarily identified perspective taking as the least important skill because they felt that children were still learning about themselves between the ages of 3 and 5, and it was, therefore, difficult for them to understand the perspective of others. They shared that this was a skill that was developed in older 5‐ and 6‐year‐olds, when the brain was better able to understand that other people have their own thoughts and feelings. The majority of parents and the majority of clinicians shared that they could not single out one skill as the least important. However, there were a lot of diverging opinions among parents, with several of them identifying internal representations of self, creativity, or critical thinking as the least important skill. There was no consensus among ECCE leaders about the least important foundational skill. For example, only one ECCE leader named internal representations of self as the least important, explaining that he did not understand the definition and why this skill was separated from perspective taking and self‐regulation and executive function.

All but four community experts agreed on and emphasized the interrelatedness of the FOLD skills to one another. However, there was less consensus regarding the specific nature of relationships among skills. As stated earlier, many participants identified self‐regulation and executive function as the basis of all the other skills. In some cases, participants named it as the sole core skill, but in others, self‐regulation and executive function was grouped with perspective taking and internal representations of self as the core skills needed to develop the remaining FOLD skills. These three skills were viewed as having a lot to do with social and emotional skills, whereas the others (i.e., curiosity, creativity, and critical thinking) were viewed as practical skills development; and participants felt that the former were an important prerequisite for the development of the latter skills.

The make‐up of these core or prerequisite skills differed across participants. Some parents and one ECCE leader consistently named curiosity and creativity—often described as “two sides of the same coin” (33‐year‐old mother [3‐year‐old daughter])—and a third skill as the building blocks of the remaining FOLD skills. Two participants (a parent and an ECCE leader) named internal representations of self as the third skill and explained that all three skills were more self‐focused, so it made sense for preschool children—who tend to be more ego‐centric—to develop these skills first before going on to develop the remaining FOLD skills, which are more externally focused. Another parent identified critical thinking and perspective taking as the core skills because all the other FOLD skills were built into them.

DISCUSSION

The primary goal of the present study was to explore community experts’ perceptions of the six FOLD skills that prior research has shown to be central to children's development in the early childhood period yet are absent from the literature on ECCE evaluation. To address this question, we conducted a series of IDIs with 60 socioeconomically, racially, and geographically diverse parents, educators, clinicians, and ECCE leaders who regularly interact with preschool‐aged children. Findings from these interviews suggest that, overall, these community experts both understand the importance of and value curiosity, creativity, critical thinking, self‐regulation and executive function, perspective taking, and internal representations of self as interrelated foundational competencies during the early childhood period. Despite this general consensus, participants differed in their definitions of these skills, opinions on their relative importance in relation to one another, and perceptions of the extent to which certain skills should be encouraged in preschool children.

Community experts acknowledged the importance of FOLD skills as central to development and well‐being in the early childhood period. Upon hearing about these skills, many participants highlighted their importance for children's thriving and shared specific ways that they have seen these skills manifest in the lives of the children in their care, suggesting that they view these skills as relevant to children's day‐to‐day well‐being. Participants were also generally hesitant to recommend any skills for removal from the list, suggesting that all skills were valued to some extent by the different community expert groups. Collectively, these findings suggest that the views and values of community experts are well‐aligned with those of academic experts who—from the days of Piaget and Vygotsky through modern times—have consistently positioned FOLD skills as central to children's healthy development. 19 , 20 , 21

When asked to define each of the FOLD skills listed in Figure 1, participants generally provided descriptions that were in line with how these constructs are defined in the academic literature. This alignment suggests that FOLD skills are generally well‐understood by individuals involved in the day‐to‐day care of children. This shared understanding is an important prerequisite for developing tools to measure how ECCE programs impact FOLD skills. 22 It can also facilitate communication among community experts who are invested in promoting children's development across different contexts. Despite this shared understanding of FOLD skills across community experts, there were some instances in which their definitions of FOLD skills were misaligned with definitions in academic literature. As noted above, two key characteristics of FOLD skills according to McCoy and Sabol are that these skills are both “fundamental” and “cross‐cutting.” 6 In other words, although FOLD skills are hypothesized to support children's development of domain‐specific cognitive, social, and emotional competencies, they themselves cannot be categorized neatly into a given developmental domain. Community experts, on the other hand, occasionally offered definitions of FOLD skills that were more aligned with a particular developmental domain.

In line with prior qualitative studies highlighting the high value that parents and educators place on social and emotional skills, 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 community experts in this study primarily discussed the FOLD skills from a social‐emotional perspective. For example, the general definition of perspective taking in the academic literature takes inspiration from Piaget's classic research on egocentrism to conceptualize this skill as a domain‐neutral capacity to take on the perspective of another person. 8 , 23 Community experts, however, largely perceived perspective taking as a social‐emotional skill that is directly related to children's empathy and kindness. Similarly, whereas definitions of self‐regulation and executive function in the academic literature typically emphasize children's regulation of thoughts, feelings, and behaviors, community experts occasionally honed in on this skill's relevance to emotion management specifically. 24 These inconsistencies have been previously acknowledged within the social‐emotional literature, which has long suffered from ambiguity in its definitions and frameworks. 25 These results also suggest that additional work is needed to examine similarities and differences between how various experts (academic and community) are defining and understanding FOLD skills. Such work can help to inform the selection of specific aspects of FOLD skills to prioritize in school‐based measurement and intervention efforts, as well as to generate common frameworks for discussing and supporting these skills. 6

During discussions about perceived importance, multiple community experts independently raised questions about the malleability and universality of particular FOLD skills. For example, across different groups of community experts, some participants described curiosity as an innate skill, whereas others viewed it as a competency that needed to be fostered and encouraged. These diverging perspectives mirror those in academic literature, which often positions curiosity as a “trait” that is stable within individuals versus a “state” that varies across time/context. 7 , 26 Similarly, some community experts, mostly parents and educators, questioned whether skills like self‐regulation and executive function or curiosity are valued and socialized in similar ways across cultural or racial and ethnic groups. The academic research community is also working to address these same questions. Although several recent studies have shown evidence that self‐regulation and curiosity are considered to be valued skills for young children from a variety of cultural backgrounds, these same studies have also raised questions about whether these constructs are truly universal with regard to their manifestation, relevance, and measurability. 27 , 28 , 29

Despite some individual differences, we found few group differences in community experts’ understanding and value of the FOLD skills, suggesting that parents, preschool educators, clinicians, and ECCE leaders are generally aligned in their perceptions of the relevance of the FOLD skills to the development of the preschool children they work and interact with. Consistent with a bioecological and transactional perspective, 30 , 31 similarities and differences across community expert groups likely stem both from the specific contexts and circumstances in which each group interacts with preschool children, as well as the goals that experts establish for children within those contexts. For example, self‐regulation and executive function can help young children complete specific tasks asked of them, maintain positive social interactions with peers, and actively engage in their communities as they grow older. 32 Such competencies are critical across a wide range of settings (e.g., in one‐on‐one interactions at home and group‐based interactions at school), which may explain the widespread agreement across expert groups about their importance.

On the other hand, and in line with an ecological framework, there were some differences that could be attributed to experts’ differences in experiences (e.g., professional versus personal). For example, parents named perspective taking as one of the most important FOLD skills, which may be tied to their day‐to‐day experiences with raising young children, whereas several educators considered it to be developmentally premature and, in turn, cited it as among the least important. In addition, educators’ preference for curiosity could reflect this skill's importance in motivating classroom engagement and learning, whereas ECCE leaders’ inclusion of more than two‐thirds of the FOLD skills as being “the most important” may reflect their broader vision of the breadth of skills children need to function well in society. ECCE leaders selected internal representations of self as one of the most important skills in the list, citing the specific relevance of this competency for supporting the well‐being of children from racially and ethnically minoritized backgrounds, which may be directly tied to their own professional experience of serving families from diverse backgrounds. Importantly, these conclusions are largely speculative. As such, future research is needed to unpack not just how various community experts perceive FOLD skills, but why they perceive them the way they do.

Finally, community experts commented on the interrelatedness of the listed FOLD skills. For example, several participants noted that each of these skills builds upon the others, and, for this reason, it would not be sound to remove any one skill from the list. Community experts across all groups were particularly drawn to self‐regulation and executive function as a core skill on which the other competencies were built. This perception mirrors a recent meta‐analysis showing robust quantitative associations between measures of self‐regulation in childhood and domain‐specific competencies across the life course. 33 Nevertheless, empirical evidence regarding the interrelatedness of FOLD skills themselves is lacking. As such, it is unclear the extent to which these skills represent fundamentally distinct versus overlapping competencies and the extent to which any of these skills may pre‐date others. Future research untangling these connections is needed.

Limitations and future directions

Despite its many strengths, this study has limitations that are worth noting. Specifically, while we did have relatively good representation across racial and socioeconomic subgroups in the United States, we did use a convenience sample in terms of selecting organizations for recruitment. This limits our external validity for community experts (especially parents) who may not have a connection to early childhood organizations (and even the organizations we did sample may be more resourced compared to other organizations given their links to authors’ ECCE networks). Moreover, our interviews were only conducted in English and Spanish and did not include families who are proficient in other languages, thus limiting the linguistic diversity in our sample. We did not have the sample size to conduct an in‐depth analysis within demographic subgroups or intersectionality across groups (race and income). Additional work should continue to explore more systematically how findings may differ across specific subpopulations in the United States (beyond our subgroup analysis by community expert type). Lastly, we only sampled within the United States. Future work should replicate this study in other parts of the world as well as continue to push on questions of the universality of foundational contexts across subpopulations around the globe.

CONCLUSION

Overall, findings from this study affirm the importance of FOLD skills as critical competencies for young children in the preschool period. Mirroring the academic literature on these skills, results from community expert interviews suggest that curiosity, creativity, critical thinking, self‐regulation and executive function, perspective taking, and internal representations of self are independently valued yet interrelated skills that are foundational to other forms of learning and development. Together, this evidence affirms the need for a more explicit focus on measuring and supporting the development of these skills in ECCE settings.
