Impact of different teaching modes on medical students’ performance under the scoring criteria for multiple-choice questions: A meta-analysis
Authors: Zhaoqi Zhang, Zhuo Zhang, Ye Yu, Mengqi Jiang, Lili Deng, Lianying Guo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:Comparing the impact of different educational modes on medical students’ performance under the scoring criteria for multiple-choice questions, would provide a basis for selecting more appropriate and effective teaching modes for talent development.Methods:We searched databases such as CNKI, Wanfang, PubMed, and Web of Science to collect publications on the comparison of medical students’ performance under the scoring criteria for multiple-choice questions and different teaching modes, with a focus on studies conducted between 2015 and 2024. Chinese search terms included multiple-choice questions, multiple-choice exam, medical students, flipped classroom, simulation teaching, team-based learning, and blended teaching. Equivalent English search terms were “multiple-choice questions,” “multiple-choice exam,” “medical student,” “flipped classroom,” “simulation teaching,” “team-based learning,” and “blended teaching.” Two researchers independently conducted publications screening, data extraction, and quality assessment, and performed meta-analysis using R 4.3.0 software.Results:This study included a total of 24 publications, among them 6 on flipped classroom teaching, 13 on simulation teaching, 2 on team-based learning, and 3 on blended teaching, covering 13 countries and involving 2086 participants in the experimental group and 2113 participants in the control group. Using scores from multiple-choice questions as the criterion, flipped classroom teaching and simulation teaching demonstrated significantly higher scores compared to traditional teaching, with standard mean differences (MDs) of 0.39 (95% CI: 0.14–0.65) and 0.33 (95% CI: 0.18–0.48), respectively. However, team-based learning and blended teaching showed no significant differences in scores compared to traditional teaching, with standard MDs of 0.48 (95% CI: −0.36 to 1.31) and −0.63 (95% CI: −1.85 to 0.59), respectively.Conclusion:Compared to traditional teaching, flipped classroom teaching and simulation teaching were more beneficial for students’ performance. It is recommended to continue implementing high-quality flipped classroom teaching and simulation teaching to address the challenges in medical education reform.

1. Introduction

Medical education plays a crucial role in the development of healthcare systems and the delivery of quality care. Many countries are currently facing significant challenges such as shortages of medical professionals and unequal distribution of medical resources.[1] Addressing these issues through targeted educational reforms is essential to ensure an adequate and well-distributed medical workforce. “Which teaching modality is best for knowledge retention and other teaching outcomes?” This is a dilemma that medical education professionals need to solve, and only research can provide the appropriate answer.

Contemporary medical education relies on written test scores to assess students’ current performance levels and knowledge gaps, in order to measure students’ progress or graduation.[2] multiple-choice exams, as a reliable and fair assessment method and a powerful evaluation tool, is often used in medical schools to test students’ recall and understanding of knowledge.[3,4] Well-constructed multiple-choice questions have been proven to be an effective and reliable measurement tool, not only to differentiate students’ performance, but also to assess medical knowledge reasoning skills and higher-order thinking.[5,6] Nowadays, in the field of medical education, various assessment methods such as computer adaptive testing, programmatic assessment, and progress testing are emerging, and the demand for high-quality multiple-choice questions is also continuously increasing.[7]

Different teaching modes have their own advantages, disadvantages, technical requirements, and applicable disciplines. For medical education, the mainstream teaching modes include flipped classroom teaching, simulation teaching, team-based learning, and blended teaching.

Flipped classroom originated in the United States. It reverses the traditional classroom teaching structure, transferring the decision-making power of learning from the teachers to the students, allowing students to autonomously choose learning approaches and independently study course-related content.[8] Multiple studies have shown that the flipped classroom teaching mode is superior to traditional teaching mode in improving learning outcomes and student satisfaction.[9,10] However, Morton research[11] presented the opposite situation, with students’ performance declining after the implementation of flipped classroom teaching.

Simulation teaching is a complex clinical process that allows students to acquire medical knowledge through virtual representation, immersive practice, and feedback, while avoiding potential risks of real medical situations. Compared to traditional face-to-face teaching, simulation teaching helps shorten the learning curve and enhances the confidence and competitiveness of medical students.[12] However, studies by Bork[13] and Tawfik[14] found that simulation teaching may lead to a decline in students’ performance.

Team-based learning is an effective organizational method based on the combination of theory and practice, standing out among many new teaching methods. It empowers students with greater autonomy and creativity, enabling them to better engage in the learning process, collaborate with others to solve problems, enhance students’ active participation consciousness, and improve their self-directed learning ability.[15,16] However, there are also studies indicating that in the implementation process of team-based learning, there are drawbacks such as unclear division of labor, students lacking a sense of responsibility, and excessive reliance on other group members, leading to the inability to smoothly carry out team-based learning, thereby affecting learning outcomes.[17]

Blended teaching combines online media, electronic devices, and traditional lecture-style classrooms. Compared to traditional teaching, it offers more flexible time and space arrangements, making it more suitable for modern education.[18,19] Previous meta-analytical results have shown that blended teaching is an effective teaching strategy with good developmental potential, capable of improving students’ learning outcomes and satisfaction.[20] However, studies by Konia[21] and Ambwani[22] found that blended teaching may lead to a decline in students’ performance.

There is still controversy over the impact of the above teaching modes on students’ performance, and further research is needed. We aim to conduct a meta-analysis to compare the effects of different teaching modes on students’ performance under the scoring criteria for multiple-choice exams, in order to provide a scientific basis for choosing a more suitable teaching mode and addressing the challenges of medical education reform.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Search strategy

Researchers searched the databases of CNKI, Wanfang, PubMed, and Web of Science, and the publications published from 2015 to 2024 were retrieved. The Chinese search terms used included multiple-choice questions, multiple-choice exam, medical student, flipped classroom, blended teaching, simulation teaching, and team-based learning; the English search terms used mainly included multiple-choice questions, multiple-choice exam, medical student, flipped classroom, simulation teaching, team-based learning, blended teaching. The specific search strategies are detailed in Supplementary File 1, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/O225.

The search strategy was jointly developed by researchers ZZ and GL, and executed across databases by ZZ. Researchers ZZ and JM independently screened all publications, with the specific steps as follows: firstly, using the software NoteExpress (Beijing Zhongwen Zhongke Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) to remove duplicate publications. Then, excluding irrelevant publications based on titles and abstracts. Finally, reading the full text of the remaining publications and reselecting relevant publications based on inclusion and exclusion criteria. If there were disagreements during the publication selection process, GL would read the full text of the disputed publications and rigorously rescreen them according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Published studies were chosen; hence, ethical approval was not required.

2.3. Data extraction

The finally included publications were reviewed again by researchers ZZ, GL, and JM, and the data extraction was carried out by researchers ZZ and JM. During the data extraction process, ZZ and JM independently completed the data extraction for all publications and cross-checked their results. If there were any discrepancies, researcher GL would reread the publications with discrepancies and re-extract the data from them. The data extraction included author, year of publication, teaching content, teaching mode, student characteristics, test population, and multiple-choice questions scores for different teaching modes. A summary table was filled out, and a database was established to organize the extracted data.

2.4. Quality evaluation

The finally included publications were evaluated for quality using the modified Jadad scale. This evaluation scale consists of 4 items, including generation of random sequence, allocation concealment, blinding, withdrawal and dropout. A total score of 0 to 3 indicates low quality, while 4 or above indicates high quality. The quality assessment was conducted by researchers JM and YY.

2.5. Statistical analysis

We used R 4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) to conduct a meta-analysis of multiple-choice questions scores among medical students’ performance under different teaching modes. The standardized mean difference (SMD) was used as the effect indicator, and 95% CI were provided for each effect indicator. If the heterogeneity test results show P > .1 and I2 < 50%, it can be considered that there is no heterogeneity or the heterogeneity is small among the studies, and a fixed-effect model should be used. Conversely, if the results show P ≤ .1 and I2≥50%, a random-effects model should be used. Meanwhile, sensitivity analysis and publication bias testing were conducted, and the data were reanalyzed using MD as the effect indicator to verify the stability of the results.

3.3. Meta-analysis of the effects of different teaching modes on medical students’ performance

3.3.1. A comparison of flipped classroom teaching and traditional teaching

Among the publications included in this study, for example, Morton study[11] classified the types of cognition assessed in multiple-choice questions into 3 categories: knowledge, application, and analysis, and calculated the scores for each category separately. Orsolya Solymos’ study[28] recorded the scores of students who took the test immediately after implementation and retook the test 2 weeks later under 2 different teaching modes. Anita Singhal[30] adopted the cross-control method in her study, and applied different teaching methods and other research methods successively to test subjects, so that there might be multiple groups of controls in the 1 included publication.

A total of 11 groups compared flipped classroom teaching with traditional teaching. Due to high heterogeneity among the studies (I2 = 87%, P < .01), a random-effects model was employed. The analysis results indicated that the multiple-choice test scores in flipped classroom teaching were higher than those in traditional teaching, with a SMD of 0.39, 95% CI (0.14, 0.65), as shown in Figure 2. Using MD as the effect indicator, the trend of the results is consistent (Fig. S1, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/O226), and leave-one-out sensitivity analysis demonstrates good result stability (Fig. S2, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/O226). Although the funnel plot results indicate some publication bias, after using the trim and fill method for correction, the combined effect still holds statistical significance (Figs. S3 and S4, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/O226).

Comparison of flipped classroom teaching and traditional teaching.

3.3.2. A comparison of simulation teaching and traditional teaching

A total of 27 groups compared simulation teaching with traditional teaching. Due to high heterogeneity among the studies (I2 = 70%, P < .01), a random-effects model was used. The analysis results indicated that the multiple-choice test scores in simulation teaching were higher than those in traditional teaching, with a SMD of 0.33, 95% CI (0.18, 0.48), as shown in Figure 3. Using MD as the effect indicator, the trend of the results is consistent (Fig. S5, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/O226), and leave-one-out sensitivity analysis demonstrates good result stability (Fig. S6, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/O226). Although the funnel plot results indicate some publication bias, after using the trim and fill method for correction, the combined effect still holds statistical significance (Figs. S7 and S8, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/O226).

Comparison of simulation-based teaching and traditional teaching.

3.3.3. A comparison of team-based learning and traditional teaching

A total of 2 groups compared team-based learning with traditional teaching. Due to high heterogeneity among the studies (I2 = 69%, P = .07), a random-effects model was employed. The analysis results indicate that there is no significant difference in the scores between the 2 groups, with a SMD of 0.48, 95% CI (–0.36, 1.31), as shown in Figure 4. Using MD as the effect indicator, the results show that the multiple-choice test scores in team-based learning were higher than those in traditional teaching and have statistical significance, with a MD of 0.91, 95% CI (0.17, 1.66) (Fig. S9, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/O226). leave-one-out sensitivity analysis demonstrates good result stability (Fig. S10, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/O226). The funnel plot results indicate good symmetry, suggesting no publication bias (Fig. S11, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/O226).

Comparison of team-based learning and traditional teaching.

3.3.4. A comparison of blended teaching and traditional teaching

A total of 4 groups compared blended teaching with traditional teaching. Due to high heterogeneity among the studies (I2 = 97%, P < .01), a random-effects model was used. The analysis results indicated that the multiple-choice test scores in blended teaching were lower than those in traditional teaching, but there was no significant difference, with a SMD of −0.63, 95% CI (–1.85, 0.59), as shown in Figure 5. Using MD as the effect indicator, the results show a consistency (Fig. S12, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/O226). 4. Discussion

To explore the teaching effects of different teaching modes, this study conducted a comprehensive analysis of 24 publications from 13 countries, comparing the multiple-choice questions scores of medical students under different teaching modalities with those under traditional teaching. The results showed that flipped classroom teaching and simulation teaching were significantly higher than traditional teaching, with SMDs of 0.39 (95% CI: 0.14–0.65) and 0.33 (95% CI: 0.18–0.48), respectively, indicating that these 2 teaching modes have a significant advantage in improving student performance. In contrast, team-based learning and blended teaching showed no significant differences in scores compared to traditional teaching, with SMDs of 0.48 (95% CI: −0.36, 1.31) and −0.63 (95% CI: −1.85, 0.59), respectively. These results suggest that different teaching modes have varying impacts on student performance, with flipped classroom and simulation teaching demonstrating more positive effects, while group learning and blended teaching did not outperform traditional teaching.

The flipped classroom originated in the United States and has gradually developed in the field of education. In traditional teaching classrooms, most of the time is spent on the lowest 2 levels of Bloom taxonomy: remembering and understanding. In the flipped classroom approach, more classroom time is devoted to the higher levels of Bloom taxonomy: application, analysis, and evaluation.[42] Students have a higher preference for the flipped classroom than the traditional classroom, which can be explained by the fact that the flipped classroom provides more classroom activities and independent learning space, with a high degree of flexibility and autonomy, enabling students to master and apply the knowledge learned. Teachers can also provide more feedback to students through the flipped classroom.[43] The results of our study also confirm that flipped classroom teaching can effectively improve students’ learning performance compared to traditional teaching. In the literature included in this study, the findings of Morton research[11] indicated that traditional teaching is superior to flipped classroom teaching; however, there was no significant difference in performance between the 2, which may be related to the testing conditions and the examination settings of the participants. One advantage of the flipped classroom is a deeper analysis and understanding of the content, whereas the testing setup used by Morton[11] was too simplistic, making it difficult to clearly differentiate the impact of the 2 teaching methods on student performance. Additionally, Yonemoto study[44] mentioned that test-takers’ confidence in their initial choices during the exam process and whether they ultimately make changes will affect their exam results, indicating that test-takers’ exam scores are associated with their state in the exam room.

Medical simulation is an important subfield of simulation technology in the medical domain, widely used for education, training, and various research purposes. Medical simulation education typically employs 3D printing technology or virtual reality to replicate real organs, tissues, or patients, creating realistic surgical or emergency scenarios to replace real-world situations.[45] According to a meta-analysis conducted by Cook,[46] technology-enhanced simulation training in health professions education is consistently associated with significant effects on knowledge, skills, and behaviors, and moderate effects on patient-related outcomes. Moreover, Lei meta-analysis[47] on the impact of high-fidelity simulation teaching on nursing students’ knowledge, professional skills, and clinical abilities showed that high-fidelity simulation effectively enhances nurses’ knowledge acquisition, which is consistent with our research findings. The possible reasons for the increased knowledge gain through simulation teaching might include the active learning strategies based on simulation technology,[48] as well as the recognition of knowledge gaps in specific simulation scenarios, which can lay a foundation for reinforcing the learning of new knowledge.[49] In this study, the opposite results of Bork study[13] may be due to the time constraints leading traditional teaching to be more inclined to rigidly impart knowledge directly, resulting in higher scores for students in written exams, but neglecting the potential for simulated teaching to provide students with the opportunity for independent exploration and understanding of the 3D aspects of anatomy. While simulation teaching compensates for some of the shortcomings of traditional teaching in the classroom, it also has some disadvantages in its implementation, such as the need for certain teaching infrastructure and materials. Students may also lack in-depth thinking about the application of theory, focusing more on completing practical tasks and being unable to express their views in a timely manner.[50]

Although flipped classrooms and simulation teaching showed significant effects in this study, it should be noted that the effect sizes (SMDs of 0.39 and 0.33, respectively) only belonged to the small to moderate level, which may indicate that these teaching models have limited improvement on student performance or may also suggest that certain factors can affect the teaching effectiveness of these models. Therefore, in the development of medical courses, the advantages and disadvantages of these teaching models should be fully explored to maximize strengths and minimize weaknesses, in order to improve teaching effectiveness. As Appleyard[51] pointed out, flipped classrooms rely on the self-motivation of student-driven autonomous learning, and therefore may be more suitable for older students. In flipped classrooms, students may express dissatisfaction due to the requirement to watch videos before class, perceiving this as a time burden, which in turn hinders their effective participation in the flipped courses.[52,53] Lo[54] believes that teachers who adopt flipped classrooms should first promote students’ understanding by explaining the rationale and potential benefits of this teaching method. Hew[42] believes that limiting the total duration of video clips in flipped classrooms to around 20 minutes is appropriate, and that using quizzes at the beginning of each class will make the flipped classroom more effective. In medical simulation education, Bland [55] emphasizes the critical importance of theoretical learning and psychological preparation, suggesting that by providing preparatory materials and scenario introductions, students’ theoretical foundation and psychological readiness can be enhanced, thereby stimulating their learning interest and improving instructional effectiveness. Issenberg[56] argues that adequate practice time and appropriate training frequency will help students better master skills and consolidate knowledge, recommending the provision of more training time and opportunities to ensure that students have ample time for actual operations and repeated practice. A meta-analysis by Lei[47] reveals that multiple studies indicate how reflection and discussion postsimulation allow students to better understand their performance in practice and identify areas for improvement, highlighting that timely and constructive feedback is a crucial means to enhance the effect of simulation education.

Team-based learning is a structured form of small-group learning that promotes active engagement and collaboration among students. It involves students working together in teams to solve problems, and apply their knowledge to real-world scenarios. Team-based learning typically includes individual preparation, readiness assurance testing, and team application activities, aiming to enhance both learning outcomes and teamwork skills. In this study, when using MD as the effect size indicator, student team-based learning outperformed traditional teaching; whereas when using SMD as the effect size indicator, although showing a trend favoring student team-based learning over traditional teaching, the results were not statistically significant. The reason may be due to the relatively small number of studies included, suggesting the need for further research to explore the impact of team-based learning on students. While this study cannot confirm whether team-based learning is superior to traditional teaching, team-based learning provides students with a platform for cooperative interaction with peers, which can more easily stimulate their autonomy and creativity, making it a commendable teaching approach. However, in the process of team-based learning, inevitable collaboration with others to solve problems may lead to unclear division of labor, some students lacking responsibility, excessive reliance on other team members, and avoidance of task execution.[17] At the same time, a considerable number of college students currently have problems such as improper learning attitudes, unclear learning goals, and lack of interest in learning,[57] all of which may lead to difficulties in the implementation of team-based learning. Therefore, before schools implement team-based learning, they should comprehensively consider multiple factors such as students’ learning attitudes, whether teaching tasks are suitable for group division of labor, and whether the group peer evaluation system is reasonable. Another obstacle of team-based learning is that it requires students to have a certain level of responsibility in self-directed learning. When students are accustomed to traditional teaching methods, they may resist team-based learning. Therefore, effective communication between educators and students, as well as innovative plans for implementing team-based learning classroom activities, are crucial.[58]

Blended learning is a modern educational approach that combines traditional face-to-face classroom instruction with online learning activities. By utilizing teaching platforms and technology, blended learning provides students with flexible learning methods and schedules. In this study, the multiple-choice test scores of blended teaching were slightly lower than traditional teaching, but the difference was not statistically significant. The higher heterogeneity of the results may be related to the smaller number of studies included. Blended teaching combines the advantages of online and offline learning to some extent, but it also inherits their disadvantages. This phenomenon has led to numerous and unclear influencing factors regarding this teaching model. Research by Liu [59] indicates that long-term implementation of blended teaching may lead to students overly relying on electronic devices, thereby affecting academic performance. Prolonged online learning may reduce students’ social interactions, resulting in a lack of sense of belonging. Simultaneously, the implementation of blended teaching may vary due to factors such as teachers’ capabilities, curriculum design, and technical support, all of which could influence the effectiveness of blended learning.[60] Although blended learning did not show an advantage in scoring, a meta-analysis conducted by Li[61] identified that the use of blended learning can enhance student satisfaction with the course through the analysis of 8 studies involving nursing students. Similarly, another meta-analysis involving 25 studies and 2706 participants showed that blended learning can improve the satisfaction of nursing students.[20] Therefore, through positive student guidance, enhanced instructional design, and improved teacher capabilities, blended learning could be an effective teaching strategy.

There are several limitations in this study that need to be explicitly pointed out. Firstly, this study only examined the effectiveness of teaching using scores on multiple-choice questions as the sole assessment criterion, which may overlook other important forms of assessment. Currently, various assessment methods are used in medical education, including clinical skills exams, structured interviews, and scenario-based problem-solving. While multiple-choice questions have certain advantages in assessing knowledge mastery, they are not well-suited to measuring students’ clinical decision-making abilities, practical skills, and problem-solving abilities. Therefore, the results of this study may not fully represent the students’ overall performance. Additionally, although using multiple-choice scores as an assessment indicator is highly objective, it does not account for students’ perceptions of the classroom. As mentioned above, blended learning does not show an advantage in scores but has high levels of student satisfaction with this teaching model. Therefore, the results of this study also do not fully reflect the impact of each teaching model on students. Secondly, there are some deficiencies in the literature included in this study: There are relatively a few publications comparisons between team-based learning, blended teaching, and traditional teaching included. Not all the included publications have statistically processed, organized, or analyzed the baseline data, which may lead to differences in the level of knowledge mastery between the experimental and control groups before the experiment. In the included publications, although the scoring criteria for multiple-choice questions is objective enough, the differences in the number and difficulty of multiple-choice questions may affect the assessment of students’ performance. Due to the ethical reason, blinding was difficult to implement in the publications included in this research, resulting in a scarcity of high-quality publication.

5. Conclusions

In conclusion, this study compared the performance of medical students in multiple-choice questions under different teaching modes through a meta-analysis. The evidence provided indicates that compared to traditional teaching, flipped classroom teaching and simulation teaching are more beneficial for students’ performance. In the future, high-quality flipped classroom teaching and simulation teaching should continue to be developed to address the challenges in medical education reform.
