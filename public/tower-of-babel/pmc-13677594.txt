Service User Experiences and Perceptions of Virtual Mental Health and Substance Use Care in Canada: A Survey of the Wellness Together Canada Portal
Authors: Hajin Lee, Ibukun-Oluwa Omolade Abejirinde, Quynh Pham, Mary Bartram, Ciara Cafazzo, Karen Young, Maggie Inrig
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the perceived user impacts of Wellness Together Canada (WTC), a nationwide, open-access digital portal that delivered free-to-user, 24/7 mental health and substance use health (MHSUH) resources grounded in the Stepped Care 2.0 model.Patients and MethodsA mixed-methods observational design was used. Registered users were invited to complete the final wave of the program evaluation survey between March 26, 2024 and April 2, 2024, just before portal closure. The cross-sectional self-report survey collected demographic information, user experiences, and perceived impacts. In total, 1366 responses were collected.ResultsAmong the 1366 respondents (mean age, 48.2 years; 970 [71.0%] women; 1117 [81.8%] White), 821 (60.1%) reported accessing the portal via the website, 255 (18.7%) via the mobile application, and 184 (13.5%) via both. Over the 12-month period from April 2023 to March 2024, 838 (61.3%) reported using resources at least monthly. The most used resources were self-assessments and mood tracking tools (1202; 88.0%), followed by self-guided resources (648; 47.4%), counseling (295; 21.6%), crisis services (144; 10.5%), and peer-led resources (134; 9.8%). Overall satisfaction was high (1065; 78.0%), predominantly because of resource helpfulness and accessibility; 204 (14.9%) were neutral, and 91 (6.7%) were dissatisfied. Without WTC, 385 (28.2%) indicated they would have taken no action, often due to financial constraints, limited availability of support, or long waitlists. Additionally, 707 (51.8%) expressed moderate or extreme concern about the portal’s closure, with about one-third of comments (351; 29.3%) indicating no plans to address their MHSUH needs afterward.ConclusionFindings highlight positive public reception of this nationwide digital MHSUH services. As the only publicly funded, 24/7 continuum-of-care option during the COVID-19 pandemic, WTC addressed both emerging and longstanding gaps in public insurance coverage for MHSUH services.

According to Statistics Canada (2022),1 the prevalence of mood and anxiety disorders among Canadians nearly doubled over the past decade compared with that in 2012, yet only half of those with mental health needs are accessing care. The COVID-19 pandemic and public health measures implemented in March 2020 contributed to increased prevalence of anxiety, worry and stress, substance use, low mood, and depression at a population level, globally.2, 3, 4 Although it is too early and complex to fully evaluate the long-term impacts of the pandemic on population mental health, symptoms of anxiety, depression, and posttraumatic stress disorder among Canadians increased drastically by 2021; perceived mental health worsened three-fold, and the rates of harm due to substance use have risen above prepandemic levels.5, 6, 7 These impacts have been most profound among marginalized groups, including women,8 Indigenous peoples,9 racial and ethnic minority populations,10 two-spirit, lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender/trans, queer/questioning, intersexual, asexual/aromantic/agender, and other sexual and gender-diverse (2SLGBTQIA+) communities,11 and others experiencing intersecting forms of social and structural vulnerability.

In response to restricted access to in-person services during the pandemic, Canadian mental health systems rapidly transitioned to digital (virtual or e-)mental health care resources using information and communication technologies, such as mobile and web-based applications.12,13 This national approach to virtual care led to broad uptake across Canada, coinciding with a sudden rise in mental health and substance use concerns.14 Evidence indicates that e-mental health resources can offer a scalable, cost-effective, and flexible approach to improving access to mental health supports, while enabling more person-centered care.15, 16, 17, 18

However, gaps in coordinated and comprehensive pan-Canadian virtual care remained substantial early in the pandemic.19 To address this need, the Canadian government funded Wellness Together Canada (WTC), the first national virtual mental health and substance use health (MHSUH) care portal, from April 2020 to April 2024.20 Notably, WTC represented a first-of-its-kind opportunity whereby all people living across Canada and Canadians abroad were offered low-barrier and open access to publicly funded, free-to-user MHSUH care, which had traditionally been excluded from public insurance coverage unless provided by physicians or in hospitals.21

Stepped Care 2.0 (SC2.0) builds on previous evidence-based, stepped care frameworks that aim to guide the design of effective health care systems for diverse populations22 and contexts.23,24 Its guiding principles include delivering an organized continuum of MHSUH services, empowering service users to make informed decisions about their care through information sharing and routine monitoring with access to their data, ensuring seamless system integration with continuing care and referral pathways through multilevel collaboration, tracking outcomes through validated tools, and adapting to community needs by leveraging local expertise.25,26

Grounded in SC2.0, WTC was designed as an open-access, low-barrier approach to service delivery that offered free-to-user, confidential, 24/7 online MHSUH resources to all visitors. The portal’s resources were organized across a continuum of stepped care, including self-guided resources, self-assessments, and mood tracking, peer support, one-to-one phone counseling, and crisis services via text messages. These resources could be accessed anonymously without registration through the WTC website, the WTC application (PocketWell), or toll-free phone call or text, in both English and French. Because anonymous access did not require users to provide information such as geographic location or citizenship status, it was not possible to confirm whether all users were located in Canada or were Canadians accessing the resources from abroad. Users who chose to register with the WTC portal by creating an account were asked to complete biweekly self-assessments for symptom tracking. Additionally, program navigators were available by phone to assist portal visitors in finding what they wanted and/or needed on WTC or to answer questions about the various service options, so that they could make informed decisions based on their specific needs and preferences (Figure 1).

Wellness Together Canada’s continuum of stepped care.

During its implementation period, WTC reached diverse populations across Canada and abroad, with over 4 million unique visitors to the portal, while routine evaluations were conducted to inform service quality improvement. This study aimed to evaluate service users’ perceived impacts of the program and identify considerations to inform future digital MHSUH portals, using a final mixed-methods survey immediately before WTC’s termination. The survey was developed with consideration of prior WTC user feedback surveys and aligned with the WTC evaluation framework, which included the domains of equity, awareness, access, satisfaction, well-being, and system integration (Figure 2).

Wellness Together Canada (WTC) evaluation framework. Equity—absence of unfair, avoidable, or remediable differences among groups of people, whether those groups are defined socially, economically, demographically, geographically, or by other dimensions of inequality. Awareness—knowledge among people across Canada and Canadians living abroad that WTC existed. Access—availability of relevant resources and supports when needed through inclusive mediums and standards. Satisfaction—overall evaluation of the WTC portal, the WTC application, and/or supports and resources. Well-being—overall wellness. System integration—impact of WTC on the broader mental health, substance use health, and health care systems in Canada.

Patients and Methods

Design and Participants

This cross-sectional survey examined service users’ experiences and perspectives on WTC. To recruit approximately 1000 registered users who were active during the 12-month period from April 2023 to March 2024, email invitations were sent in each user’s preferred language to all active users who had agreed to be subscribed to WTC promotional emails (N=44,710) (Supplemental Appendix 1, pp 1-2, available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/). For the purposes of this study, active users were defined as those who met at least 1 of the following criteria during the study period: (1) signed up for the WTC portal, (2) logged in at least once, or (3) submitted at least 1 self-assessment or mood check-in.

Recruitment occurred between March 26, 2024, and April 2, 2024. The final survey data set comprised deidentified data provided by Greenspace Health, a hosting and cloud computing service provider responsible for supporting data collection, storage, and transfer for this evaluation. Ethics approval was granted by the Research Ethics Boards at Women’s College Hospital (2023-0057-E) and Veritas (2024-3331-18556-2).

Procedures

An anonymous web-based survey was created using Typeform, with all data encrypted and stored on a secure cloud server. Participants were invited to complete a 5- to 7-minute survey including 38 items (23 close-ended and 15 open-ended items) across 3 sections: demographic information, overall service experiences, and thoughts and plans for supporting MHSUH needs after WTC’s closure (Supplemental Appendix 1, pp 2-11). The English survey was translated by a professional translator into French and validated by 2 native French-speaking members of the research team. After providing informed consent, respondents were able to proceed with the survey.

Statistical Analyses

Data were exported from the secure cloud storage into Excel (Microsoft Corporation) and then imported into R software (version 4.4.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing) for analysis.27 Quantitative data handling and analysis were conducted using the tibble (version 3.2.1) and dplyr (version 1.1.4) packages, and visualizations were produced using ggplot2 (version 3.5.1). We used descriptive analyses to summarize participants’ responses to close-ended questions on demographic characteristics, service experiences, and perspectives; χ2 tests were used to assess differences in the distribution of sociodemographic characteristics in relation to service experiences (Supplemental Appendix 4, pp 26-32, available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/). Two-sided P values of <.05 were considered statistically significant.

Participants’ qualitative responses to open-ended questions were analyzed using ATLAS.ti (version 24)28 and organized according to the WTC evaluation framework domains of equity, access, satisfaction, well-being, and system integration, excluding the awareness domain (focused on overall portal access). Three members of the research team (the first, fifth, and sixth authors) participated in the qualitative content analysis. The first and sixth authors have extensive experience in health services research using qualitative methods, while the fifth author was a research assistant who was new to qualitative research and received training and supervision from the first author throughout the analysis process. Two coders (either the first and fifth authors or the first and sixth authors) independently reviewed qualitative data, co-developed a coding framework, and conducted a content analysis.29,30 Qualitative findings, including themes, illustrative quotations, and their frequencies, are presented in Supplemental Appendix 3 (pp 14-25, available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/).

Results

Equity

Among the 1366 respondents, 1245 (91.1%) completed the survey in English and 121 (8.9%) in French; 970 (71.0%) self-identified as women and 1117 (81.8%) as White. The mean age of respondents was 48.2 years (SD, 14.8 years). Among the 1282 respondents who reported before-tax household income, 162 (12.6%) met the Canadian low-income cutoff (LICO) threshold,31 defined by household32 and community size. Well-being

Among the 16 health- and wellness-related challenges assessed, respondents reported an average of 6 co-occurring concerns (Table 2). The most frequently reported issues were anxiety or worry (1149; 84.1%), stress (1065; 78.0%), depression or low mood (1014; 74.2%), sadness (726; 53.1%), and isolation or loneliness (673; 49.3%). Overall, 1268 (92.8%) indicated experiencing anxiety, depression, or stress, with 823 (60.2%) experiencing all 3. Further, 852 (62.4%) reported having health conditions that lasted 6 months or longer, and among the 850 who answered the follow-up question, 527 (62.0%) reported that these conditions always or often limited their daily activities. As summarized in Supplemental Appendix 3 (pp 15-16), respondents further described these long-term conditions, which were classified into physical health and chronic conditions (532; 46.6%), mental health, addictions, and neurodiversity (509; 44.6%), trauma and abuse (87; 7.6%), and stress-related concerns (14; 1.2%).

Access

Many respondents accessed WTC resources via the website (821; 60.1%), followed by the mobile application (255; 18.7%) or both platforms (184; 13.5%); the majority accessed them in English (1231; 90.1%). In the previous year, 838 (61.3%) reported using resources at least monthly, and 280 (20.5%) accessed them weekly (Table 3). As summarized in Supplemental Appendix 3 (pp. 16-17), reasons for preferring the website (758; 76.7%) included ease of use (330; 33.4%), resource engagement (184; 18.6%), lack of awareness of the application (129; 13.1%), technology-access barriers (59; 6.0%), application fatigue (36; 3.6%), and privacy or confidentiality concerns (14; 1.4%). Similar reasons were cited among those preferring the mobile application (230; 23.3%) as follows: ease of use (156; 15.8%), resource engagement (52; 5.3%), lack of awareness of the website (11; 1.1%), technology-access barriers (8; 0.8%), and privacy or confidentiality concerns (3; 0.3%).

The most frequently used resources were self-assessments and mood tracking (1202; 88.0%), followed by self-guided resources (648; 47.4%), counseling (295; 21.6%), crisis services (144; 10.5%), and peer-led resources (134; 9.8%). Additional comments on the continuum of WTC resources are provided in Supplemental Appendix 3 (p 17). About half (555; 46.9%) described the positive impact of self-assessments and mood tracking, including increased mental health awareness and reduced feelings of loneliness. Respondents also highlighted the value of self-guided resources (150; 12.7%), 24/7 counseling (145; 12.3%), peer-led supports (45; 3.8%), and crisis resources (29; 2.5%).

Satisfaction

Most respondents (1065; 78.0%) reported being satisfied with the services they received, whereas 204 (14.9%) were neutral, and 91 (6.7%) were dissatisfied (Table 3). Among additional comments on satisfaction (Supplemental Appendix 3, pp 17-21), satisfied users highlighted the helpfulness of resources and ease of access, whereas neutral and dissatisfied users described unmet needs, limited engagement, and navigation challenges. As a result of accessing WTC, 1163 (85.1%) respondents reported one or more improvements in mental health literacy and/or feelings of connection, while 160 (11.7%) reported no changes. Reported impacts included better tracking of mental wellness (897; 65.7%), increased understanding of their mental state (807; 59.1%), greater knowledge and skills to manage MHSUH (547; 40.0%), improved insight into activities affecting MHSUH (448; 32.8%), and feeling less alone (362; 26.5%) (Table 3). Other impacts included improved physical health through portal videos, access to counseling when local services were unavailable, referring others to WTC, and enhanced connection through peer support (Supplemental Appendix 3, p 21). Satisfaction and perceived impact were significantly associated with frequency of use, with users accessing resources at least monthly reporting greater impact and higher satisfaction than less frequent users (Supplemental Appendix 4, p 32; Supplemental Figures 1 and 2, available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/).

System Integration

When asked about alternatives if WTC were unavailable, many respondents reported they would have used provincially funded services, including family doctors (489; 35.8%), community or provincial mental health programs (299; 21.9%), emergency rooms (144; 10.5%), or walk-in clinics (133; 9.7%). Others indicated they would have relied on peer support (398; 29.1%) or paid for private providers (395; 28.9%). Notably, 385 (28.2%) stated that they would not take any action to address their needs. Among these respondents, the most common reasons were financial constraints (197; 51.2%), lack of readily available help (178; 46.2%), long waitlists (136; 35.3%), lack of knowledge about how or where to get support (133; 34.5%), and lack of confidence in the health care system or social services (123; 31.9%) (Table 3).

Supporting MHSUH Beyond WTC

We assessed respondents’ level of concern regarding the termination of WTC services following the COVID funding cessation: 707 (51.8%) were extremely or moderately concerned, 472 (34.6%) were somewhat or slightly concerned, and 174 (12.7%) were not at all concerned (Table 3). No significant differences in level of concern were identified across demographic groups (Supplemental Appendix 4, p 31). Additional comments addressed plans for supporting MHSUH needs after WTC’s closure (Supplemental Appendix 3, pp 24-25). About one-third (351; 29.3%) reported having no plans to meet these needs. Other comments described a range of supports as follows: private providers (237; 19.8%); publicly insured health care providers (126; 10.5%); self-care practices (123; 10.3%); other virtual care options (121; 10.1%); peer support (86; 7.2%); employer-based plans (70; 5.8%); and public, provincial, or community programs (66; 5.5%).

Discussion

This study described the characteristics, service experiences, and perspectives of 1366 registered users of a nationwide online MHSUH portal during its final implementation phase. Users represented a cross-section of the general population and most frequently used self-assessment and mood tracking, followed by self-guided resources, counseling, crisis services, and peer-led supports. Overall satisfaction with the portal was high, particularly regarding the helpfulness and accessibility of its resources, while slightly over one-fifth were neutral or dissatisfied. Satisfaction was higher than in prior surveys (78% vs 55%-70%), and users with higher engagement reported greater satisfaction, which may reflect increased perceived value among more engaged users during the wind-down period.33,34 However, the minimum level of engagement needed for users to benefit from similar online MHSUH resources remains unclear.35 Future portals should explore the benefits experienced by low-engagement users who may not actively use resources but still value having them available if needed.

We found that, without WTC, approximately one-quarter of respondents would have done nothing to support their needs, primarily because of financial constraints, lack of readily available help, and long waitlists, aligned with prior surveys. These findings suggest that, during its implementation, a period spanning the most acute phase of the pandemic and the subsequent lifting of public health measures, WTC may have served as the only publicly funded, free-to-user, 24/7 accessible option, underscoring the critical gap it addressed in MHSUH care. Although a growing proportion of respondents reported that they would have used provincially funded services without WTC, more than half expressed concern about its closure, and one-third mentioned having no plans to address their needs afterward. Future work should examine whether users have reverted to doing nothing after WTC closure or whether provincial/territorial resources have been able to expand access to meet their needs36 and whether preferences for care modalities have shifted after the rapid adoption of digital services during the pandemic.37

More than half accessed the portal at least monthly, mostly via the website, followed by the mobile application or a combination of both. When describing their preferred mode of engagement, respondents provided reasons largely related to device preferences: website users highlighted the ease of use with a desktop, while application users addressed the convenience of accessing the application on a mobile phone. Privacy and confidentiality concerns were raised for both modes but for different reasons: website users perceived lower information-security or cybersecurity risks, whereas application users noted personal privacy when using a mobile phone. These findings underscore the need for further research to better understand how perceptions of confidentiality and privacy shape user preferences and engagement with web- and application-based services.38, 39, 40

Complementing in-person services, the virtual delivery of a fulsome continuum of MHSUH care creates new opportunities for practice and access, reaching individuals who might not otherwise seek help or have access to help. Respondents frequently emphasized the helpfulness of validated self-assessments for tracking mood,41 functioning,42 and well-being,43 with one noting, “having the data from the biweekly assessment [is] helpful for self-reflection and advocacy, and for identifying where I need support from my health care teams.” Our finding also show how peer-led resources can function seamlessly within a choice-driven stepped-care model. While not everyone’s first choice, peer-led supports were selected by a notable subset of participants who reported meaningful benefits, as reflected in a comment: “I’ve gotten more help from the peer support group than from the counseling I’ve had over the years.”

Social determinants of health play a critical role in shaping mental health disparities in Canada.44 We observed poorer mental health among younger respondents, as well as those identifying as Indigenous, sexual or gender minorities, and individuals with low income.45, 46, 47 Although these findings should be interpreted with caution given the small sample size, they align with broader Canadian data on mental health disparities during the pandemic.48 Digital literacy and access to devices and internet connectivity are also recognized as important social determinants of health.49 The digital divide between individuals with skills and resources to access technology (as in our sample) and those without may have further widened mental health disparities during the pandemic.50 Our analyses relied on deidentified data provided by a hosting and cloud computing service provider, including self-reported Indigenous identity collected through an anonymous online survey. Future research should engage equity-denied communities, including Indigenous peoples, throughout the research process, from survey development to data collection, analysis, and stewardship, adhering to equity-based data governance principles such as the First Nations principles of ownership, control, access and possession.51

This study has several limitations. First, this exploratory study was conducted as part of a program evaluation and relied on self-reported data from a web-based, cross-sectional survey. As such, it was not designed to establish causality, which would require a more rigorous experimental design including a control group. Second, our analyses focused on registered users, who represent only a small subset of approximately 4.3 million WTC visitors during its implementation. Additionally, some sociodemographic groups in our sample were underrepresented or overrepresented relative to the 2021 Canadian Census.52 For example, only 24% identified as men, compared with nearly half of Canadians, aligning with gender differences in help-seeking behaviors53, 54, 55, 56; 82% identified as White (vs 67% of Canadians), 16% identified as members of racial and ethnic minority groups (vs 24%),57 and more than 90% were anglophone (vs 76%).58 These representativeness issues may limit the generalizability of our findings. Nonetheless, this study provides a unique opportunity to capture user experiences with WTC immediately before service closure, offering real-time insights into the perceived value of a national, open-access digital MHSUH portal. Future evaluations would benefit from best practices in collecting and using demographic data from more diverse communities during registration,59 including linking user characteristics with website analytics and self-reported outcomes to better assess engagement with intended populations and equity impacts.

Conclusion

In summary, WTC showed that a national, easily accessible digital MHSUH service, built on SC2.0 principles, offers an effective and scalable solution for addressing existing service gaps and overcoming common access obstacles such as fee for service and long wait times, positioning it as an impactful and necessary component of a comprehensive mental health system.
