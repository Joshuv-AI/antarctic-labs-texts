Why and how is private equity involved in assisted living community development and operations?
Authors: Paula Carder, Sarah Dys, Jacklyn N Kohon, John R Bowblis, Yashaswini Singh, Sean Shenghsiu Huang, Jennifer N Bunker, Lindsey Smith, Momotazur Rahman, Kali S Thomas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAssisted living (AL) cares for close to 1 million older adults who need long-term services and supports in the United States.MethodsThis qualitative study examines private equity (PE) involvement in AL mergers, acquisitions, development, and operations based on interviews with 46 senior housing professionals (including AL owners, operators, and developers, real estate brokers, lenders, PE fund managers) and state agents (licensing and regulatory oversight staff and Long-term Care Ombudsman Program leaders).ResultsParticipants indicate that PE involvement in AL responds to strong demographic demand and attractive financial returns, need for private funding sources, perceived social benefits, and absence of federal AL requirements. PE is primarily involved in AL through property ownership, but to varying degrees may also be involved in facility operations, including selecting and supporting qualified operators, and facility management.ConclusionA nuanced understanding of PE's multidimensional involvement in AL—distinct from healthcare settings—and the centrality of private capital to support the growing demand for AL is needed to guide future policymaking.Keywords: assisted living, private equity, public policy, qualitative research

Introduction

Private equity (PE) investment in healthcare, real estate, and other sectors has increased significantly in recent years.1,2 Large markets, high demand for services, and potential short-term gains have attracted PE investors to the healthcare sector.3,4 PE can benefit the healthcare sector through access to capital, improved systems, business expertise, and technological investments.5,6 However, PE acquisitions of healthcare entities are associated with operational decisions aimed at maximizing profit margins, including reductions in staff or services, price increases, and adverse outcomes (eg, increased hospitalizations and mortality).7-9 Perceptions of PE in healthcare vary, especially as both improvements and deficiencies in care are possible.9,10 Available evidence suggests that the impact of PE involvement depends on the type of setting and investment strategy.9,11

Due to population aging, the expected increase in demand for long-term services and supports, and consumer preference for assisted living (AL) vs other residential long-term care options (ie, nursing homes), this setting has received PE attention.12 There are an estimated 988 000 AL residents in 32 200 AL communities, which provide personal, social, and healthcare services, including assistance with activities of daily living, end-of-life care, and dementia-specific support services.13,14

AL originated as a private pay option for older adults who needed assistance with daily activities, but did not need nursing home-level of care. Since first being developed in the 1980s, the acuity level of AL residents has increased, with some memory care units in ALs having staffing levels on par with nursing homes.15 Unlike nursing homes, which are regulated at the state and federal levels, states license and oversee AL, resulting in state-level variation regarding many policy provisions, such as admission and discharge criteria, staff training, and the scope of services permitted.16

The development of the AL sector was largely supported by private capital, unlike hospital and nursing home developers, who could receive government-financed construction loans in the mid-1990s.17,18 AL businesses have traditionally involved 2 entities: the AL property owner who owns the real estate and an operator who manages the daily operations of the AL, including the provision or procurement of services such as staffing, food service, and social activities.19 This hybrid housing-and-care model and separable real estate and operating platforms create distinctive investment opportunities for PE (eg, rate flexibility, asset appreciation, roll-up strategies), which have been the subject of federal investigations in other settings such as hospitals and nursing homes.20 Importantly, while the separation of real estate ownership and operational management is increasingly characteristic of emerging investor-owned models in hospitals and nursing homes, this structure has long been common in AL settings, including noninvestor-owned ALs.19,21

Since 2006, PE has acquired at least 912 AL facilities in 252 unique acquisitions across 47 states, accounting for a reported transaction value of $17.3 billion in inflation adjusted 2023 dollars.12 While PE investment in healthcare continues to grow, the motivations and strategies behind these investments in AL remain opaque, particularly to residents, families, and frontline staff directly affected by changes in ownership and management.

Given the appeal of ALs to PE firms, stakeholder insights are critical for understanding the broader implications of PE investments in the AL industry and for identifying opportunities to align profit motives with care quality. In this context, our qualitative study asks why and how PE invests in AL by interviewing diverse stakeholders, such as PE fund managers, senior housing professionals, as well as state licensing agents and consumer advocates, to learn how they describe the intent and role of PE investments in AL, including daily operations.

This study attempts to holistically understand both the benefits and potential downsides of PE involvement in the AL industry.

Data and methods

Sampling

We interviewed 3 participant groups: (1) senior housing industry professionals with PE transaction or ownership experience (eg, AL owners, operators and developers, senior housing real estate brokers and lenders, PE fund managers, and others involved in AL), (2) state employees responsible for licensing and oversight of AL, and (3) employees of states’ Long-term Care Ombudsman Programs who advocate for AL residents, families, and staff (#2 and #3 are collectively referred to here as “state agents”). We identified potential senior housing professional participants by reviewing lists of housing finance experts published on senior housing organization websites and in gray literature, through professional networks, and via snowball sampling. We contacted potential participants from 75 senior housing and financing organizations, 15 state agencies, and 14 State Long-Term Care Ombudsman Programs via email to invite them to participate in a 30-60-minute virtual interview (see detailed methods in Supplementary Appendix A1). Study procedures were approved by Portland State University IRB, Protocol #605.

Data collection and analysis

We conducted 46 semistructured interviews with a purposive sample of senior housing professionals (n = 27) and state agents (n = 19) between February 2024 and April 2025. In addition to participants in organizations with a national scope of practice, 14 individual states are represented. We employed a practical thematic analysis22 and drew on our experience with other thematic analysis approaches.23,24 Two members of our analytic team (Authors 2 and 3) reviewed interview transcripts to develop an initial code list that was refined with input from all coauthors. The analytic team then individually coded interview transcripts in ATLAS.ti Web (version 5.8.0). For transparency, the full codebook is provided in Supplementary Appendix A2; not all codes were applicable to this analysis. At weekly meetings, the analytic team discussed discrepancies, opportunities for code consolidation, and themes, which they shared with the full team during monthly meetings. The findings are organized by primary themes and subthemes, accompanied by brief concepts and participant quotes that exemplify each theme.

Limitations

While we conducted interviews until we no longer heard “new” information (ie, data saturation),24 recruiting PE professionals, especially those from large PE firms, proved challenging (Supplementary Appendix A1). Although PE executives with AL expertise may be underrepresented, we interviewed AL owners, operators, and developers who described their experiences with PE. Some participants were unwilling to share the names of other professionals or organizations’ with the research team, limiting the reach of the snowball sampling strategy. Additionally, participants had different definitions of PE and, in some cases, included examples not relevant to AL (eg, skilled nursing facilities) or conflated PE involvement with any for-profit or corporate engagement in AL. To mitigate this problem, we asked research participants to discuss their understanding of PE involvement in AL and included only quotes that specifically referenced this sector. Thus, we reflect on and discuss how participants perceive PE involvement; even if these perceptions are not specific to PE or may not accurately reflect PE involvement, these views represent participants’ realities and understanding. While we aim to understand PE involvement in AL, findings may not generalize to every PE firm, AL community, or individual consumer's experiences. Understanding varied perspectives—including possible inaccuracies—can inform future research and policymaking. This study did not include interviews with AL residents and families, whose firsthand experiences can contribute to future research about the meaning of quality operations, management and ownership in this residential long-term care setting.

Why PE firms invest in AL

All participant types explained that population aging creates a demand for AL. Senior housing professionals explained that PE-backed investors have compared favorable AL returns to other investment classes, such as multifamily rental housing and storage units. At the same time, questions of profit motivation and reasonable returns were raised. An AL owner/operator said, “What makes the worst investors are probably the people that somehow think that they’re going to get these outsized returns,” and a state agent described “places that charge ten and twelve thousand a month for assisted living care, which is more than many nursing homes.”

Senior housing professionals spoke of the social benefit of investing in this sector, using phrases such as “it's a good thing,” “part of why I’m in this industry,” and having “a sense of purpose and mission.” Speaking to the role of institutional investors (such as state pension funds), 1 participant explained that AL “fits within the investment parameters of what many of these pension funds would be interested in investing in.” Another reason for PE investment in AL, according to senior housing professionals and state agents, is the need for private capital sources to support mergers and acquisitions, new developments, and renovations to existing properties. One AL operator described PE as “a critical part of our jet fuel,” and another said that only PE could provide the “$80 million” needed for a new development. Several participant types recognized that AL is less regulated compared to NH, which can attract PE investors with limited healthcare experience (Table 1).

When asked whether investors consider states’ regulations before acquiring ALs, senior housing professionals described the role of states in vetting operators and ensuring regulatory compliance, rather than in regulations that discourage investment. A real estate attorney said, “I’ve not seen investors or even lenders, really, worry a whole lot about the state regulatory framework when it comes to licensing these buildings.” A PE fund manager suggested that the financial cost of regulatory compliance has “pushed out moms and pops,” creating an environment that encourages PE-backed AL with resources to meet consumer demand.

How PE invests in AL

Senior housing professionals described the importance of understanding the organizational structure of AL communities when discussing PE involvement (Table 2). A commonly described structure includes the real estate owner (sometimes referred to as a “PropCo”) and an operations firm (sometimes referred to as an “OpCo”). Most participants explained that PE firms do not directly operate AL, though a few described how PE can influence operations (Table 3 and Figure 1). State agents described PE investment in the property rather than operations; one said that “investment entities” are interested in “a real estate investment” rather than long-term care businesses. A different state agent said, “They [PE firms] own the real estate. And they front the money for it, and they have someone else appointed to run and manage the operations.” A brokerage attorney summarized the relationship like this:

“So the PropCo owns the dirt and the building. The operator is the licensed operator who is charged with caring for the residents. It typically employs all of the staff members at the building. And it enters into the residency agreements. It holds the license. It holds the contracts with therapy, landscaping, all the different vendors that come in to service a building, you know, that's at that OpCo level. And then oftentimes you have a third-party manager who is managing the building for a fee.”

Other participants similarly described different roles of operators and managers based on their personal perceptions and firsthand experiences in the states where they work. Some mentioned the relationship between an Opco that has day-to-day responsibility for operating the AL and a “manager” or “management company” that provides business services related to budgets, billing, staffing, resident care, and other facility operations, though the “manager” is generally not involved in day-to-day operations. We heard that it is “best practice” for the operator to be a separate legal entity that oversees daily operations, and that some states license this entity to operate AL. PE might invest in companies that provide AL operations or management services. Some participants described PE firms’ ownership of these companies through a limited liability corporation or another entity that enables oversight of operations. Regardless of PE involvement in real estate only, real estate and operations, or management, participants agreed on the importance of having a solid operator with a proven track record in providing AL services to older adults (see Table 3).

Examples of increasing private equity control over assisted living operations with supporting quotes.

Senior housing professionals described varying levels of PE involvement in day-to-day operations, including indirect and active (see Figure 1). Examples of PE involvement include quarterly walk-throughs of AL properties, reviewing and providing feedback on financial reports, negotiating parameters in financial covenants (eg, internal rate of return, time horizon, expected occupancy level), modifying service and supply vendor contracts, and putting the PE firm's own operations or management company in charge of day-to-day operations. Senior housing professionals explained that PE investors might require operators to have an ownership stake in the property, or “skin in the game,” that builds accountability for operators to perform well financially.

State agents were asked to describe both positive and negative changes in operations that occurred following PE acquisition of AL. Positive examples included recognition that PE-backed capital could improve building facilities, staffing, and service quality, while negative examples included reduced staffing levels and increased monthly rates charged to residents. Additionally, some senior housing professionals described PE investors attracted to senior housing for the “wrong reasons,” lacking an understanding of the healthcare component and operational needs (Table 3).

Discussion

Recent news articles have highlighted how the senior living industry, including AL, cannot keep up with demand as baby boomers age.25,26 This demand attracts various private investors, including PE. To our knowledge, our qualitative study is the first to ask senior housing professionals and state agents why and how PE invests in AL communities. Our interviews echo the popular press, which reports that PE investments in AL respond to population aging and the need for sufficient private funding to develop AL communities.27,28 Senior housing professionals highlighted that investing in AL communities can both yield attractive financial returns and advance social good by caring for older adults, a perspective that differs from popular press characterizations. Several professionals expressed, “if not PE, then who?” as PE fills real or perceived funding capital gaps in both the development and acquisition of AL communities.

Though participants were certain about why PE invests in AL communities, the “how” part of PE investment in ALs is complex and varied, in part because states take different approaches to defining AL, with some ALs trending toward hospitality and others toward healthcare settings.16 States’ AL licensing requirements can be based on levels of care provided, admission policies based on resident characteristics (eg, dementia diagnosis, ability to evacuate during an emergency), and building features (eg, multistory, fire safety features). The complexity of AL as a hybrid form of hospitality, housing, and health-related services distinguishes it from licensed healthcare entities. Whether and how PE firms choose to invest in AL that operate on a hospitality, healthcare, or other model, and the legal responsibility of the PE firm under each model, is unknown.

We learned from participants that PE funds have varying motivations (eg, social good and profit) and expertise in the AL sector. AL owners and operators and other senior housing professionals, noted that PE firms are most attracted to the real estate component of ALs, though PE firms may also invest in operations and management companies with proven records. This structure of AL communities being associated with 3 separate entities (eg, real estate, operator, and management company) was commonly described by senior housing professionals, yet PE involvement in these entities varied. For example, the real estate, operator, and management company associated with an AL company could be the same “owners,” (19) or each entity could be owned by different investment groups.

Potentially complicating the implications of our findings, the use of terms such as “operator” and “manager,” as well as “owner,” lacks uniformity in either states’ AL regulations or common usage. However, multiple participants highlighted how PE funds’ were interested in the real estate aspect of AL, with day-to-day operations contracted to an operating or management company, mirrors some PE firms’ approach to operating NHs through a separate legal entity.29 Because each PE transaction is unique, it is unclear how common it is for PE firms to be involved in daily operational decisions that can impact AL resident outcomes, such as staffing levels and care planning. Without more information, painting all PE firms with a broad brush might obscure the fact that each PE firm has unique objectives, investment strategies, and an appetite for day-to-day operational decisions in AL communities.

All study participants agreed on the importance of high-quality AL operators to PE investors, as operators are ultimately responsible for day-to-day operations. Some stakeholders have argued that PE-backed acquisitions force operators to focus on short-term profitability, potentially leading to cuts to services deemed unprofitable.30 State agent participants, whose regulatory responsibilities expose them primarily to compliance issues and complaints, naturally encountered and highlighted more negative examples of PE involvement. In contrast, some senior housing professionals point to market discipline, which acts as a check on the AL, making it hard to cut staffing, services, reduce quality, or increase prices. They suggested that poor-quality ALs risk state sanctions and the loss of paying customers and competent staff who can move to another community. This behavior is somewhat unique to AL, in that the majority of residents pay out-of-pocket for their care and may have a choice of settings, especially in metropolitan regions.31,32

The only way to develop better evidence on the impact of PE involvement in AL communities is through better data. Existing AL research has examined whether resident outcomes are associated with organizational characteristics such as profit-based and chain ownership status.13,33 Future studies that examine whether the AL community had PE ownership could provide helpful insight for policymakers. However, the complex organizational structures revealed in this study suggest that blanket policies targeting “PE ownership” without more granular data will miss relevant questions of financial incentives, operational control, and competency. One solution proposed by policymakers is to increase financial and ownership transparency.34 However, recent concerns raised about the accuracy of nursing home ownership data,35,36 and our findings about complex AL ownership structures, should temper efforts to simply apply the same approach to developing transparency tools. It is imperative that policymakers partner with senior housing professionals to clarify the meaning and type of AL ownership, operational and management structures before implementing transparency rules, including those specific to PE.37

Though increased PE activity in healthcare and NH settings has motivated a burgeoning development of policy interventions and improved transparency,7,37,38 PE involvement in AL is not yet understood by policymakers, researchers, or the general public.39,40 Further, PE is only one of several private sources of financing AL deals, including real estate investment trusts, bank loans, and investment from family and friends.41 Without sound evidence on the role and effects of PE investment in AL development, acquisitions, and operations, proposed policies may have unintended consequences.

While not a major focus, this study raises awareness of the lack of public sources for developing AL, including “middle market” housing, an important topic also described by researchers and housing professionals.42 The federal government (through the Hill-Burton Act) authorized construction loans for nursing homes between 1946 and 1979, reflecting the government's role in regulating the supply of long-term care providers.18 No such federal investment has been made regarding AL supply. For example, the low-income housing tax credit program has been used to support the development of few AL properties,43 and the HUD-backed AL conversion program that provides funds to convert a portion of Section 202 units to AL has rarely been used.44 The potential role of PE in developing affordable and middle-market AL is an area ripe for future research and understanding.

It is clear that various stakeholders involved in AL settings, whether through the real estate, operations, or management company, often have a strong interest in providing high quality care and services. However, it is also true that there are reports of AL care that fails to meet residents’ needs. State regulators can ensure that AL operators deliver care in ways that support residents’ health and wellbeing through regulatory oversight. Our findings point to clear opportunities for improving state regulatory oversight. The delineation of real estate owners, operators and management companies point to 3 types of entities not always clearly delineated by state regulations or the people charged with overseeing quality in ALs. Improving clarity around the roles and legal responsibilities of these entities through improved definitions and awareness of these structures is an opportunity for future research and development of effective regulations.

For example, the model PE firms use to invest in ALs can have implications for PE firms’ legal responsibilities and their accountability to AL residents.

While this is beyond the scope of this study, stakeholders suggested that, to varying degrees, AL investors appreciate the importance of quality operators and consumer satisfaction. Given the differential investment methods and incentives, future research needs to examine AL resident and community outcomes when PE firms become involved in AL communities using these various models.

Conclusion

AL is a well-established, non-institutional, long-term residential care option that thousands of older adults choose every year. Private investors have long assumed responsibility for developing and operating AL, primarily for private paying residents and their families.14 This study indicates that as population aging increases demand, private funding sources—including PE—will become increasingly important to expanding and sustaining the AL sector; therefore, how quality, access, and costs to consumers can be aligned with profit motives must be thoughtfully addressed.
