Is telerehabilitation inferior to face-to-face rehabilitation at improving adherence and pain in patients with chronic low back pain? Protocol for a non-inferiority randomized clinical trial with economic evaluation
Authors: Camila Nepomuceno Broisler, Bruno Tirotti Saragiotto, Gisela Cristiane Miyamoto, Giovanna Laura Neves Antônio Gaban, Viviane Ribeiro da Silva, José Luiz Dias Siqueira, Luiz Fernando Approbato Selistre
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExercise is a key component in reducing pain and disability in patients with chronic low back pain (CLBP). Barriers such as geographic distance, treatment costs, lack of support, and low self-efficacy can limit access to physiotherapy and compromise adherence. Synchronous telerehabilitation (ST) has the potential to overcome these barriers, support self-management, and improve adherence to exercise. However, its clinical effectiveness and cost-effectiveness compared with face-to-face rehabilitation remain unclear. This study aims to investigate whether ST is non-inferior to face-to-face rehabilitation in improving adherence and reducing pain and its cost-effectiveness in patients with CLBP.MethodsThis non-inferiority randomized controlled trial (RCT), parallel, two-arm with economic evaluation, will be conducted in a middle-income country (Brazil). A total of 160 individuals with CLBP, aged between 18 and 65 years, will be recruited. Participants will be randomly allocated into two groups: ST or face-to-face rehabilitation. Both groups will receive seven individual 60-min physiotherapy sessions over 12 weeks, including pain education, exercise therapy, and self-management strategies. Primary outcomes will be adherence and pain intensity. Secondary outcomes will include function, global perception of change, satisfaction, self-efficacy, therapeutic alliance, healthcare attendance and health-related quality of life. Outcomes assessments will be conducted at baseline, post-intervention (3 month), and at 6- and 9-month follow-up after allocation. The economic evaluation will be conducted from a societal perspective with a 9-month time horizon. Cost-effectiveness and cost-utility analyses will be conducted for pain intensity and quality-adjusted life years, respectively.DiscussionIf ST is non-inferior to face-to-face rehabilitation, it could serve as a clinically viable, cost-effective alternative for managing CLBP, expanding access to care while maintaining treatment quality. The findings may guide physiotherapists, healthcare systems, and policymakers in integrating telehealth strategies into routine rehabilitation practice.Trial registrationBrazilian Clinical Trials Registry ID: RBR-57pgmjr. Introduction

Background and rationale {9}

Low back pain (LBP) is the leading cause of disability globally [1]. The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study estimated that in 2020 more than half a billion people were affected [2]. Although most people recover from an acute episode, 20–30% of individuals experience recurrent episodes and may develop chronic pain [3]. Given its high prevalence and disability burden, LBP imposes substantial economic costs on healthcare and social support systems, including direct expenditures (medications, physiotherapy, hospitalizations) and indirect costs (lost productivity, reduced quality of life, and social security expenses) [4].

Clinical practice guidelines recommend a multimodal approach to chronic low back pain (CLBP) that includes exercise therapy, pain education, self-management, and psychological therapies [5, 6]. Exercise therapy is the core intervention, with robust evidence supporting its benefits for pain reduction and functional improvement [7, 8]. However, adherence to prescribed exercise programs is often suboptimal, with nonadherence rates approaching 70% [9–11]. Adherence is essential for achieving sustained benefits, yet it is influenced by several factors: lack of support, low self-efficacy, inadequate beliefs about exercise, lack of motivation, fear of movement, poor therapeutic alliance, geographic isolation, and transportation barriers [9, 12–14].

Telerehabilitation has emerged as a promising strategy to improve adherence and support self-management by overcoming access barriers [10, 15, 16]. This modality gained prominence during the COVID-19 pandemic, increasing access to care, reducing travel and costs, and enabling patients to take a more active role in managing their conditions [17, 18]. Encouraging self-management is essential in physiotherapy treatments, especially to maintain long-term effects of interventions such as exercise therapy [19]. Synchronous telerehabilitation (ST), delivered in real time via videoconferencing, allows direct interaction between patient and therapist, facilitating therapeutic alliance and potentially enhancing satisfaction compared with other remote modalities [20, 21].

ST has shown benefits in several conditions, including knee osteoarthritis, total hip/knee arthroplasty, chronic respiratory disease, and stroke [10, 22, 23]. Systematic reviews have suggested that ST can be as effective as face-to-face rehabilitation for improving adherence and pain in musculoskeletal disorders [10, 22, 23]. However, most evidence comes from small, heterogeneous trials of low methodological quality, often focused on perioperative populations in high-income countries [10, 22, 23]. One notable exception, the study by Hinman et al. [18] in individuals with knee osteoarthritis, found ST to be non-inferior to face-to-face care for pain and function and cost-effective in the Australian healthcare setting. Despite these promising findings, the effects of ST in CLBP remain unclear, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, where additional barriers, such as lower educational levels, limited income, and reduced technological literacy, may influence feasibility and outcomes [24–26]. To date, no high-quality trials have tested the non-inferiority of ST compared to face-to-face rehabilitation for CLBP or included economic evaluation in this population.

This trial aims to address these gaps by evaluating whether ST is non-inferior to face-to-face rehabilitation in improving adherence and reducing pain in individuals with CLBP, while also assessing its cost-effectiveness from a societal perspective in a middle-income country context.

Objectives {10}

The primary aim of this randomized controlled trial is to determine whether ST is non-inferior to face-to-face rehabilitation for adherence and pain intensity in individuals with CLBP at short- (3 months), medium- (6 months), and long-term (9 months). Our hypothesis is that ST will not be inferior to face-to-face rehabilitation for adherence and pain intensity in all time-points (short, medium, and long term). Secondary aims are to investigate the effectiveness of other clinical relevant outcomes (function, satisfaction, therapeutic alliance, attendance at care, global perception of change, health-related quality of life, and self-efficacy) and the cost-effectiveness of ST care compared to face-to-face rehabilitation. The hypothesis is that there will be no difference for any of the secondary outcomes between the treatment modalities and that ST will be cost-effective compared to face-to-face rehabilitation.

Trial design {12}

This study protocol describes the design of a non-inferiority randomized controlled trial, parallel, two-arm with an economic evaluation and a 1:1 allocation ratio. This protocol has been developed according to the CONSORT [27, 28], SPIRIT [29], and CHEERS 2022 [30] guidelines and recommendations for reporting non-inferiority trials. The interventions are reported according to the TIDieR [31] template for intervention description and replication. The study was prospectively registered in the Brazilian Clinical Trials Registry (RBR-57pgmjr). Participant enrollment started on 01 October 2025, and recruitment is expected to be completed by June 2027.

Methods: participants, interventions, and outcomes

Study setting {13}

This study will be conducted at the Musculoskeletal Research Center of the Department of Physiotherapy of the Federal University of São Carlos (UFSCar) in Brazil. Furthermore, participants allocated to the ST group will receive the intervention via the Google Meet videoconferencing platform.

Eligibility criteria {14a}

Participants will be eligible for the study if they meet all the following inclusion criteria: (1) adults of both sexes, aged 18 to 65 years old, with chronic non-specific low back pain (defined as pain or discomfort between the costal margin and the lower gluteal folds, with or without irradiation to the lower limbs, with no known pathoanatomical cause, which persists for at least 3 months) [32, 33]; (2) score ≥ 3 on the Numerical rating scale (NRS) [34]. Participants may report other pain complaints, as long as the back pain is their primary complaint; (3) score > 14% on the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) [35]; (4) be able to speak and understand Portuguese well to ensure understanding of the information about the tests and educational interventions, as well as answer the questionnaires independently; and (5) access to a smartphone, tablet, or computer with internet connectivity.

Exclusion criteria {14a}

Participants will not be included in this study if they present any of the following: (1) pregnancy; (2) tumors, infections, or metastases; (3) specific causes of CLBP, such as radiculopathy or lumbar stenosis or chronic degenerative disorders; (4) heart disease, neurological or rheumatic diseases, and other conditions that were not suitable for safe intense exercise; (5) history of fracture, recent orthopedic surgery (in the last 12 months), or who have surgery scheduled for the next 6 months, or any invasive procedure within the last 6 months; (6) medical contraindication to physical exercise, ascertained by self-report [33]; (7) cognitive impairment that might compromise understanding of the intervention provided (score ≤ 2 on the Mini-Cog) [36]; (8) being treated for chronic low back pain; or (9) uncorrected hearing and visual impairments or any health condition that prevents safe and adequate participation in the online exercise sessions. Eligibility criteria will be determined by self-report, while radiculopathy will be screened using the straight leg raise (SLR), Bragard test, and SLUMP test [37, 38].

Eligibility criteria for sites and therapists {14b}

Not applicable, as no specific eligibility criteria were defined. The intervention was delivered by the study authors, who are licensed physiotherapists, at available and routinely used research sites.

Who will take informed consent? {32a}

The study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee from the UFSCar (CAAE: 88,446,325.8.0000.5504). All eligible participants will receive oral and written information about the study procedures, the importance of attending all assessment time points, and the potential risks and benefits of participation. Written informed consent will be obtained for all the participants. The study will be conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant national guidelines.

Additional consent provisions for collection and use of participant data and biological specimens {32b}

Additional consent provisions for the collection and use of participant data and biological samples are not applicable, as this trial does not collect biological specimens.

Participant timeline {18}

Flow of participants

The first contact with potential participants will be made through an online screening form to verify initial eligibility criteria. Individuals who appear eligible will then be invited for an in-person visit, during which they will undergo anamnesis and baseline assessment. Following this assessment, participants who meet all eligibility criteria will be asked to provide written informed consent.

After consent, participants will be randomly allocated to either ST group or face-to-face rehabilitation group. Both groups will receive the same therapeutic intervention protocol, differing only in the delivery method.

Interventions {15a and 15c}

Both groups will receive an intervention based on the recommended clinical guidelines for CLBP, which includes pain education, exercise therapy, and self-management strategies [5, 6]. Figure 2 outlines the components of each physiotherapy session. The intervention consists of seven sessions lasting 60 min each, delivered over a 12-week period (approximately in weeks: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, and 11) [39] (Fig. 3). Session frequency will be gradually reduced over time to promote patient autonomy and strengthen self-management skills. With the exception of the first session, which will last approximately 80 min and will be dedicated to the introduction to the study, the initial assessment of health conditions, and selection of the exercise program (including the demonstration and execution of a single set of each exercise to ensure adequate understanding of the movements, but without fully implementing the exercise program), all subsequent sessions will be standardized. The main components of each session are described in detail in Supplementary Material 1. In summary, physiotherapists will provide pain education, encourage self-management strategies, and prescribe an individualized exercise program consisting of 4 to 5 exercises to be performed three times per week either unsupervised or with one supervised and two unsupervised sessions, depending on the week.Fig. 2Components of the interventionFig. 3Flowchart intervention

Components of the intervention

Flowchart intervention

To standardize and facilitate intervention delivery, the Vedius platform for exercise prescription (https://vedius.com.br) will be used. The exercise program includes strengthening of the trunk muscles, spinal mobility, and general strengthening [8, 40–42]. The exercises will be selected by the physiotherapist and participant from a pre-selected list available on Vedius website. Each exercise will be offered in three levels of difficulty to allow progression or regression according to individual needs. The exercise selection will take into account the participant’s valued activities, preferences, and physical fitness level. All participants will start at level one regardless of their strength or previous experience. Participants will be instructed to maintain the intensity of the strengthening exercises between 5 and 7 (“hard” to “very hard”) on the Adapted Rating of Perceived Exertion (BORG) scale [43, 44]. Progression will follow the principles of the American College of Sports Medicine through adjustments in repetitions, increased difficulty, and/or changes in the support surface [45].

Exercises will be performed both under supervision during sessions and independently at home. During the sessions, the physiotherapists will show videos demonstrating the exercises using multimedia materials on the computer screen and may provide exercises demonstration when needed. For home practice, participants will receive either a personalized Vedius link with asynchronous exercise videos or a PDF file with the selected exercises, including instructions and parameters.

The exercise program will be reviewed and adjusted during sessions. Participants will be encouraged to progress independently within established limits, but any modifications must be discussed with the physiotherapist at the next session. In addition, to ensure safety, participants will be instructed to select an appropriate environment for exercise, and recognize warning signs requiring activity cessation. The exercises, their respective progressions, and guidelines for cases of acute worsening of symptoms are detailed in Supplementary Material 1.

Pain education will be structured based on the e-pain model developed by Reis et al. [46]. The program will consist of 9 stages, distributed throughout the sessions. These stages include (1) acceptance, (2 and 3) pain education, (4) sleep hygiene, (5) recognition of stress and negative emotions, (6) positive self-coping in lifestyle, (7) exercises, (8) communication, and (9) prevention and recurrence. Educational topics will be delivered through multimedia material displayed on the computer screen, accompanied by a dialogue lecture. During the presentation of the content, participants will be encouraged to interact, expressing questions and/or sharing related experiences.

All sessions will include behavioral change strategies to promote adherence to the exercise program (supervised and independent) and encourage self-management of the condition. The self-management component of the intervention will be based on the self-determination theory [47, 48]. The intervention will include 11 behavior change techniques (BCTs) selected based on the Taxonomy of Behavior Change Techniques [49]. The techniques and delivery methods are detailed in Supplementary Material 1. Participants will receive an exercise and care diary, to record the implementation of the exercise program, as well as possible deviations and adverse events. The aim of this diary is to enable self-monitoring of progress and discussion with the physiotherapist at a subsequent appointment. After intervention completion, participants will be encouraged to continue with their exercise program.

Mode of physiotherapy delivery

Synchronous telerehabilitation

The synchronous telerehabilitation (ST) program will be delivered via the Google Meet platform accessed via desktop, laptop, smartphone, or tablet internet-connected. Physiotherapists will display exercise videos via screen sharing to guide the participant through the exercises. Real-time communication will allow the physiotherapist to view the participant as they perform the exercises, providing immediate feedback and modifications whenever necessary. During their first in-person visit, participants allocated to this group will participate in a familiarization session with the Google Meet platform. All instructions for accessing and using the video conferencing software will be provided. They will also have access to a manual with written and illustrated instructions to facilitate the use of the platform and a telephone contact for additional support.

Face-to-face rehabilitation

Participants allocated to the face-to-face rehabilitation group will receive treatment in person, under the direct supervision of a physiotherapist. Physiotherapists will use the same strategies described for the ST group, but they will be delivered in person.

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated interventions {15b}

Participants may withdraw from the study at any time and for any reason without penalty. However, they may also be discontinued from the study for safety reasons, such as medical advice, complications related to pre-existing illnesses, or worsening of a clinical condition that makes participation in the exercise program contraindicated.

Relevant concomitant care permitted or prohibited during the trial {15d}

Participants may use other healthcare services for the management of CLBP, including pharmacological treatments, as they deem necessary. However, they will be instructed to report concomitant interventions on the economic evaluation form.

Provisions for post‑trial care {34}

After completing the study, patients will receive a report of the study results. If participants still express a need for care for their chronic pain after the trial, they will be advised to contact their general practitioner.

Treatment fidelity {25a}

To monitor the treatment fidelity, we adopted the framework developed by Borrelli et al. (2011) in both groups. With the participant’s consent, some of the sessions will be recorded to monitor the fidelity of intervention delivery, the duration of therapist-patient interactions, and the consistency of intervention components. Recordings will be selected randomly, based on a pre-established randomization schedule accessible only to the coordinating researcher. An independent researcher of the trial (i.e., not involved in recruitment, intervention, or assessment) will review the recordings monthly to verify the standardization and compatibility of intervention delivery across therapists. In addition, regular monitoring meetings will be held with the research team to monitor participant dropout rates for each physiotherapist, discuss clinical cases when relevant, and provide structured feedback to ensure protocol adherence.

Identification, measurement, and valuation of resource used

Primary outcome

Pain is one of the main symptoms that leads individuals with CLBP to seek physiotherapy care [50]. However, more significant improvements in clinical outcomes in adults with CLBP are associated with higher levels of exercise adherence [14]. In addition, the World Health Organization (WHO) has established improving adherence to long-term treatments in individuals with chronic conditions as a research priority [51]. Therefore, the primary outcomes of this randomized controlled trial are pain intensity and exercise adherence. Change scores will be calculated at baseline and at each end-point, with the primary end-point at 3months and secondary end-points at 6 and 9 months, using the following tools.

Exercise adherence rating scale

Exercise adherence will be assessed using Section B of Exercise Adherence Rating Scale (EARS), a self-report measure with well-established validity, reliability, and responsiveness in patients with CLBP [52]. The scale consists of six items scored on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 0 “strongly agree” to 4 “strongly disagree” [53]. Items 1, 4, and 6 are scored inversely [53]. The final score is calculated by adding the items and ranges from 0 to 24, with higher scores indicating greater adherence [53]. The instrument will be applied at approximately four-week intervals throughout the intervention period, and will not be administered at baseline, since its objective is to assess adherence behavior, and the intervention must have already begun. The score obtained in the first assessment (2nd week) will be used as the baseline value for adjusting the statistical analyses. A minimally important change (MIC) of 5.5 in the total score was identified as a meaningful improvement in adherence behavior (AUC = 0.82) [52]. Therefore, a score of 17/24 following a home exercise prescription can be considered a cutoff point [52].

Pain intensity

Average pain intensity during the last 7 days will be assessed using the NRS [54]. This is an 11-point scale composed of integers from 0 to 10, in which 0 is classified as “no pain” and 10 as “worst pain imaginable” [54]. The scale has demonstrated high reliability in Brazilians with CLBP [55]. A MIC of 2 in the total score was identified as a meaningful improvement [55].

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes include function, global perception of change, self-efficacy, therapeutic alliance, satisfaction, health-related quality of life, attendance at care, and adverse events. Outcomes will be measured at time points as indicated in Table 1.

Function

Function will be assessed using the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), a valid and reliable instrument for the Brazilian population with CLBP, composed of 10 domains [56]. The domains are scored on a 6-point ordinal scale, ranging from 0 “no difficulty” to 5 “most severe disability” [56]. Higher scores indicate a more significant level of disability related to low back pain [56].

Global perception of change

The global perception of (i) change in pain and (ii) change in physical function will be assessed using the Global Perceived Effect (GPE) Scale [57]. The scale has excellent reliability for the Brazilian population with CLBP and consists of 11 points, distributed from − 5 (much worse) to + 5 (much better), with the central point anchored at 0, representing the absence of perceptible change (no change) [55]. Higher scores indicate better recovery from the condition [57].

Self-efficacy

Self-efficacy will be assessed using the Pain Self-Efficacy Questionnaire (PSEQ-10) [58]. This reliable instrument for the Brazilian population with CLBP consists of 10 items scored on an ordinal scale ranging from 0 (not confident) to 6 (completely confident) [58]. Higher scores reflect greater self-efficacy in dealing with pain [58].

Therapeutic alliance

The therapeutic alliance will be assessed using the Working Alliance Inventory–Patient (WAI-RS) [59]. This reliable instrument for the Brazilian population with CLBP consists of 12 items, subdivided into 3 factors. Items are scored on a 5-point Likert scale [59]. The score for each factor ranges from 5 to 20, with higher scores representing a stronger therapeutic alliance [59].

Satisfaction

Satisfaction will be assessed using the reliable and validated Instrument for Measuring Patient Satisfaction with Physical Therapy Care (MRPS) for the Brazilian population [60]. The instrument consists of 13 items, divided into three factors [60]. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale [60]. Higher scores represent greater satisfaction [60].

Health-related quality of life

Health-related quality of life will be assessed using the EQ-5D-3L questionnaire [61]. This instrument consists of a descriptive system with five health domains (mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression) [61]. The responses in each domain generate a 5-digit numeric code that represents 243 different health states. These health states will be converted into utility using the Brazilian value set, ranging from 33,333 (representing the worst health state) to 11,111 (representing the best health state) [61]. This instrument also includes a visual analogue scale (EQ-VAS) that measures self-perceived health on a scale from 0 “the worst imaginable health state” to 100 “the best imaginable health state” [61].

Attendance at sessions

Attendance at appointments will be recorded by physiotherapists in treatment notes and by participants through recording in the “Exercise and Appointment Log.” The number of appointments attended, cancelled/rescheduled with prior notice/justification, and no-shows without notice will be recorded throughout the intervention period.

Adverse events {17}

In this study, an adverse event will be considered as any problem experienced in the back or any other part of the body that the participant considers to be a result of the exercise and/or advice given by the physiotherapist [18, 62]. The occurrences will be analyzed by recording the number and type of events that occurred during the intervention period, as well as the percentage of participants who experienced them. Participants will be systematically asked about any adverse events, and will be advised to discuss it with their physiotherapist, who will provide appropriate advice and/or adjustments to the exercise program.

Economic outcomes

The economic evaluation will be conducted from a societal perspective with a 9-month time horizon. The cost-effectiveness analysis will be conducted using pain intensity at 9 months after randomization. The cost-utility analysis will be conducted using quality-adjusted life-years (QALY). The QALY will be calculated using linear interpolation between the utilities measured at baseline, 3, 6, and 9 months after randomization.

The societal costs will include intervention, healthcare, patients and family, and lost productivity costs [63]. The intervention costs will be valued using the micro-costing approach [63]. The interventions costs will include the physiotherapy costs (based on the total number of sessions performed by each participant during the intervention period), the costs associated with the development and use of support materials (i.e., pain education videos and the Vedius digital platform), and the costs of printing the registration diary. For the ST group, the intervention costs will also include the costs of printing the manual with instructions for using the Google Meet platform. The physiotherapy costs will be valued using the unit prices for physiotherapy sessions derived from the Brazilian physiotherapy council [64]. The costs associated with the development and use of support materials will be depreciated based on an estimated time of use (in years) and the estimated number of patients benefited per year. The Vedius platform costs will be valued using the prices published on the company’s official website [65]. The pain education costs will be valued using the prices of a previous study [66]. The costs of printing materials (i.e., registration diary, and the manual with instructions for using the Google Meet platform) will be valued using prices derived from print shops.

All other cost measures (i.e., healthcare, patient and family, and lost productivity costs) will be assessed using a cost questionnaire at baseline, 3, 6, and 9 months after randomization. At baseline, patients will receive a cost diary with the same information of the cost questionnaire to fill all resources used due to low back pain daily. The information of the cost diaries will be collected by a blinded evaluator at 3, 6, and 9 months after randomization by phone. The cost questionnaire/diary was developed by experts in low back pain treatment and economic evaluation. After an initial round of suggestions and corrections, the cost questionnaire/diary was piloted in 4 patients, allowing for adjustments to improve clarity and usability {11}. Baseline characteristics

Descriptive measures collected at baseline include information such as recruitment source, age, gender, height, weight, body mass index, education level, occupation, residential geographic location, comorbidities, duration of symptoms, and frequency of symptoms during the week. In addition to these measures, (1) physical activity level (using Baecke Physical Activity Questionnaire–BPAQ) [67]; (2) frequency of technology use, including the type of device used and the digital activities performed; and (3) confidence in using technology in everyday life will be classified using a 4-point Likert scale with options ranging from “not at all confident,” “somewhat confident,” “moderately confident,” to “extremely confident.” The responses will then be dichotomized into less confident (not at all and somewhat confident) and more confident (moderately and extremely confident).

Sample size calculations {19}

The sample size for this non-inferiority trial was calculated using a simulation-based approach with a mixed-effects model to evaluate two co-primary outcomes: pain intensity (NRS) and exercise adherence (EARS). Non-inferiority margins (NIM) of 10 points for NRS [68] and 5.5 points for EARS were specified [52]. The simulation was designed to ensure a type I error rate of 0.05, a power of 90%, and accounted for a 30% loss to follow-up. Simulations assumed an expected mean difference of 7 points for pain intensity (SD = 7.57) [69] and 5 points for EARS (SD = 5.45) [52], based on prior studies. Data were generated under normal distributions for ST and face-to-face rehabilitation groups, with equal allocation. Each simulation included fitting mixed-effects models for both outcomes, with the group as a fixed effect and subjects as random effects. The non-inferiority criterion was met when the mean difference for both outcomes was below the margin and the upper bound of the 95% confidence interval remained below 10 points (pain intensity) and 5.5 points (EARS). Simulations were run for sample sizes between 50 and 300 in increments of 10, with 10,000 iterations per sample size. The sample size simulation suggested that a minimum of 80 participants per group provided sufficient power (90%) while meeting the non-inferiority criteria for both pain intensity and exercise adherence. Thus, the study will enroll a total of 160 participants.

Recruitment {20}

Participants will be recruited through electronic media, the institution’s website, posters, local newspapers and magazines, as well as through oral communication in São Carlos city, Brazil.

Assignment of interventions

Randomization, blinding, and allocation concealment {21a–24c}

The allocation sequence will be generated electronically (https://www.randomizer.org/) by a researcher not involved in any stage of the study. The randomization schedule will be stored on a password-protected website Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) at UFSCar and maintained by a researcher not involved in either participant recruitment or administration of outcome measures. Group allocation is revealed to the patient by this same researcher after baseline assessment has been completed.

This study will involve two blinded outcome evaluators, who will remain unaware of participants’ group allocation, and two physiotherapists responsible for delivering the interventions, who will remain blinded to the assessment results. Both physiotherapists will deliver the intervention to participants in both groups, thereby minimizing potential influences related to therapist charisma, affinity, or therapeutic alliance. Finally, participants will be blinded to the group allocation through a process of restricted disclosure. Participants will only be informed about the various modalities of delivery of physiotherapy services currently available, such as face-to-face care, telephone consultations, videoconferencing, home visits, individually or even in groups. Therefore, they will not be informed about the hypotheses of the study or the model of care received by the comparator group until collections are completed. This approach aims to reduce selection bias, particularly the preferential enrollment of participants favoring telerehabilitation, which could compromise the validity of the non-inferiority design [18].

Physiotherapist training {25a}

The physiotherapists responsible for implementing the intervention will be properly trained by a professional with over 10 years of experience in telerehabilitation for low back pain and by a psychologist with over 10 years of experience in the behavioral approach. The training program will be planned, standardized, and rehearsed to ensure that all participants receive the intervention with a consistent standard of care. The training will include (1) clinical recommendations for the optimal use of telerehabilitation; (2) familiarization and training in the use of the Google Meet platform; (3) preparation for intervention delivery, emphasizing exercise therapy, educational topics, and self-management strategies; (4) simulated videoconference consultations with a researcher acting as a patient and providing immediate feedback on performance; and (5) videoconference and in-person appointments with ten pilot patients with low back pain (two intervention sessions for each patient) to practice intervention delivery skills. A manual of the intervention protocol and study procedures will be given to each physiotherapist, allowing for consultation whenever necessary.

Statistical methods

Statistical analysis plan {27a–27d}

A blinded data analyst will perform all analysis using the Statistical Package for Social Science, version 23.0 (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL, USA). Baseline characteristics of participants will be presented using descriptive statistics. The normality of the data will be verified by the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of the histograms, while the equality of variances between groups will be verified by the Levene test.

Analysis of treatment effects will follow the principles of intention-to-treat, including all randomized participants regardless of attendance [28]. In the event of missing data during the follow-up period, repeated measures linear mixed models will take these into account automatically, under the assumption that data are missing at random. These approaches will be conducted to help assess robustness of conclusions (sensitivity analysis).

The between-group differences over time in exercise adherence and pain intensity will be analyzed using a linear mixed-effects model with fixed effects for group, time, baseline value, and the group × time interaction, and a random intercept for participants to account for within-subject correlation. Adjusted mean differences at each follow-up will be derived from the model with 95% confidence intervals. Noninferiority will be demonstrated if the lower bound of the two-sided 95% CI for between-group difference (ST minus face-to-face rehabilitation) is above − 5,5 points for change in adherence and/or 10 points for change in pain intensity, at 3 months after randomization [52, 68]. Primary outcomes will be interpreted and reported independently. For example, if ST is found to be non-inferior for exercise adherence but not for pain intensity, we will conclude that ST is non-inferior to face-to-face rehabilitation for improving exercise adherence but is inferior for reducing pain intensity.

Secondary outcomes will be interpreted using the superiority framework since no NIMs have been defined for these outcomes. Between-group comparisons will be performed using different statistical models, depending on the nature of the data: linear mixed-effects will be used for continuous outcomes (i.e., for function, self-efficacy, therapeutic alliance, satisfaction, and health-related quality of life); proportional odds models for ordinal variables (i.e., global perception change); and negative binomial or Poisson regression models for count/independence outcomes (i.e., attendance and adverse events), as appropriate. The adequacy of models will be assessed using graphical inspection and standard diagnostic methods to check assumptions. Since these analyses will be exploratory, p values will not be adjusted for multiple comparisons [71].

To test the robustness of the results, sensitivity analyses will be performed, comparing the results obtained with different statistical approaches. These analyses will include a per-protocol analysis of the primary outcomes, considering only participants who attended > 4 appointments, and adjustments for possible imbalances in baseline characteristics. In addition, a pattern mixture approach [72] will be used to assess the potential impact of violating the requested missing data assumption in the primary analyses.

Economic evaluation

The economic evaluation will also be performed according to the intention-to-treat principle using STATA (v.14, StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA). Missing data will be handled using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE). The multiple imputation model will include sociodemographic and clinical variables at baseline that are different between groups and/or different between patients with and without missing data, and variables associated with clinical and economic outcomes. Ten complete datasets will be created considering loss of efficiency < 5%. Pooled estimates will be estimated according to Rubin’s rules [63].

The mean differences in costs between groups will be reported for societal costs and disaggregated costs. Mean differences in costs and effects between groups will be estimated using seemingly unrelated regression analyses. In these analyses, differences in costs and effects will be adjusted for their baseline values, if available, while accounting for the potential correlation between costs and effects [63]. The incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs) will be calculated dividing the mean difference between intervention costs by the mean difference between intervention effects. Bootstrapping techniques with 5000 replications will be performed to estimate uncertainty surrounding the cost difference and incremental cost-effectiveness ratios. The ICERs of the original data and the bootstrapped ICERs will be graphically presented in cost-effectiveness plans [63]. Cost-effectiveness acceptability curves will be estimated to indicate the probability of ST being cost-effective compared to face-to-face rehabilitation at different willingness to pay values [63].

Sensitivity analyses will be performed to investigate the robustness of the cost-effectiveness analysis. The first sensitivity analysis will be performed from a healthcare perspective. The second sensitivity analysis will be performed per protocol (in which only patients who attended > 4 appointments will be included). Finally, complete-case analyses will be performed excluding participants with missing data for costs and effects in order to investigate the influence of missing data and/or the imputations in the analyses.

Interim analyses {28b}

No interim analyses are planned for this study. In addition, we do not anticipate any issues that could be detrimental to participants, or which would necessitate an interruption of the trial.

Oversight and monitoring

Composition of the coordinating center and trial steering committee {3d}

The coordinating center is located at the Department of Physical Therapy at the Federal University of São Carlos and comprises the Trial Coordinator, the principal investigator, 3 physiotherapists, and the data analysts. There is no trial steering committee or public involvement group.

Composition of the data monitoring committee, its role, and reporting structure {28a}

The Data Monitoring Committee (DMC) will meet biweekly to review safety data, including adverse events reported by participants. In addition, the DMC will assess recruitment progress and data quality, such as adherence rates and the quality of ear-EEG recordings.

Frequency and plans for auditing trial conduct {29}

The researcher group meets biweekly to discuss the trial development and upcoming issues. Study documents will be constantly checked for an independent researcher not involved with the present study to guarantee the accuracy of data tabulation at all time points. If any records are missing, the research team will report it to the local ethics committee.

Plans for communicating important protocol amendments to relevant parties (e.g., trial participants, ethical committees) {31}

All amendments to the protocol will be communicated and approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee from the Federal University of São Carlos, São Paulo. All deviations from the protocol will be documented thoroughly using a breach report form or described in the future publication of the original article of the randomized controlled trial.

Dissemination plans {8}

The results of this randomized controlled trial will be disclosed completely in international peer-reviewed journals. Both positive and negative results will be reported. In addition, participants will receive a report with all the results of the study. Oral presentation and poster presentation about the study results will be held.

Discussion

Although exercise therapy is considered the primary intervention for CLBP, adherence remains a major challenge. Given the enormous impact and incidence of CLBP, strategies that influence exercise adherence, pain relief, and self-management of the condition are urgently needed. Recent studies have highlighted the beneficial effects of telerehabilitation on adherence and pain in individuals with musculoskeletal disorders. However, the specific role of synchronous telerehabilitation (ST) in CLBP remains unclear, mainly in middle-income countries. Exercise adherence is influenced by multiple logistical, psychological, and social barriers, and in this context, ST offers the potential to expand access to treatment for people living with chronic pain. However, for ST to be sustainable and scalable, it is essential to demonstrate cost-effectiveness. To date, no studies have rigorously examined its non-inferiority compared to face-to-face care for CLBP, nor its economic impact. Economic evaluation is critical to inform healthcare managers and policymakers, yet the scarcity of robust evidence on the effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of ST limits its implementation as a viable alternative for physiotherapy service delivery.

Thus, considering the high prevalence of CLBP, the challenges related to exercise adherence, and the gaps in the scientific literature on telerehabilitation, particularly in middle-income countries, it is essential to evaluate both the clinical and economic effects of ST. Robust and high-quality non-inferiority randomized controlled trials may provide evidence that ST is not inferior to face-to-face rehabilitation in improving exercise adherence and pain intensity. In this context, our study seeks to fill this gap by generating relevant information for clinical practice and the healthcare system, including evidence on the effectiveness and potential economic benefits of ST in individuals with CLBP, with a direct impact on patients, professionals, and healthcare service funders.
