An improved version of a spectral inequality by Payne
Paolo Acampora, Emanuele Cristoforoni, Carlo Nitsch, Cristina Trombetti

Abstract
A celebrated inequality by Payne relates the first eigenvalue of the Dirichlet Laplacian to the first eigenvalue of the buckling problem. Motivated by the goal of establishing a quantitative version of this inequality, we show that Payne’s original estimate—which is not sharp—can in fact be improved. Our result provides a refined spectral bound and opens the way to further investigations into quantitative enhancements of classical inequalities in spectral theory. Keywords: Dirichlet eigenvalue, Buckling eigenvalue, spectral inequalities, convex sets MSC 2020: 35P15, 55J35, 31B30, 35B40

Introduction

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded convex set and let
\lambda(\Omega)
and
\Lambda(\Omega)
be the principal eigenvalues of the Dirichlet eigenvalue problem

\begin{cases}-\Delta u=\lambda u&\text{in }\Omega,\\[5.0pt]
u=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

and of the Buckling eigenvalue problem

\begin{cases}-\Delta^{2}v=\Lambda\Delta v&\text{in }\Omega,\\[5.0pt]
v=\partial_{\nu}v=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

where
\partial_{\nu}v
denotes the normal derivative on the boundary of
\Omega
, respectively. The two eigenvalues can be variationally characterized as

\lambda(\Omega)=\min_{u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}\dfrac{\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla u\rvert^{2}\,dx}{\int_{\Omega}u^{2}\,dx}\quad\text{ and }\quad\Lambda(\Omega)=\min_{v\in H^{2}_{0}(\Omega)}\dfrac{\int_{\Omega}(\Delta v)^{2}\,dx}{\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla v\rvert^{2}\,dx}.

Payne in
[
9
]
(see also
[
8
]
) proved that

\Lambda(\Omega)\leq 4\lambda(\Omega),

(1.1)

moreover, it was thought that the infinite strip asymptotically achieved the equality sign, and thus, the factor
4
could not be improved (see for instance
[
9
]
or
[
2
, Remark 8.13 (iv)]
). However, in the present paper, we will prove the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
n\geq 2
. There exists
C_{n}<4
such that for every convex set
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}

\Lambda(\Omega)\leq C_{n}\lambda(\Omega).

In the case
n=1
, inequality (
1.1
) always holds with the equality sign. Indeed if
\Omega
is the interval
(0,L)
, as it it well known

u(x)=\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi}{L}x\right),\quad\text{and}\quad\lambda(\Omega)=\dfrac{\pi^{2}}{L^{2}},

while, by explicit computations, one can prove that

v(x)=\dfrac{1}{2}\left(1-\cos\left(2\dfrac{\pi}{L}x\right)\right),\quad\text{and}\quad\Lambda(\Omega)=4\dfrac{\pi^{2}}{L^{2}}=4\lambda(\Omega).

We notice that in this case

v(x)=\dfrac{1}{2}\left(1-\cos\left(2\dfrac{\pi}{L}x\right)\right)=\sin^{2}\left(\dfrac{\pi}{L}x\right)=u^{2}(x).

The previous relation between the eigenfunctions of the one-dimensional Dirichlet and the Buckling eigenvalue problems is the main idea in the proof of inequality (
1.1
).

Lastly, we point out that inequalities of the type (
1.1
) also hold true in the context of Riemannian manifolds under positivity assumptions of the Ricci tensor (see
[
7
,
6
]
).

2
Computations for an Infinite Strip

To have an idea of why the factor
4
in (
1.1
) was thought to be sharp and why it is not, let
n=2
and let
H=\mathbb{R}\times(0,\pi)
be an infinite strip with
\lambda(H)=1
, and consider the boundary value problem

\begin{cases}-\Delta^{2}w=\Lambda\Delta w&\text{in }H,\\[5.0pt]
w=\partial_{y}w=0&\text{if }y=0,\pi.\end{cases}

(2.1)

In analogy to what happens with the Laplacian, one may think that the smallest
\Lambda
for which a non-trivial solution exists is achieved among functions that are constant with respect to the variable
x
, hence reducing the boundary value problem to the ODE

\begin{cases}-w^{({\mathrm{iv}})}=\Lambda w^{\prime\prime}&\text{in }(0,\pi),\\[5.0pt]
w(0)=w(\pi)=w^{\prime}(0)=w^{\prime}(\pi)=0\end{cases}

(2.2)

which admits non-trivial solutions if and only if
\Lambda>0
is a solution to

2\left(\cos\left(\sqrt{\Lambda}\pi\right)-1\right)+\sqrt{\Lambda}\pi\sin\left(\sqrt{\Lambda}\pi\right)=0.

(2.3)

For such
\Lambda
, the solutions to the equation (
2.2
) are proportional to

w(y)=-\left(\sqrt{\Lambda}\pi-\sin\left(\sqrt{\Lambda}\pi\right)\right)\left(1-\cos\left(\sqrt{\Lambda}y\right)\right)+\left(1-\cos\left(\sqrt{\Lambda}\pi\right)\right)\left(\sqrt{\Lambda}y-\sin\left(\sqrt{\Lambda}y\right)\right).

One can verify that the smallest positive solution to (
2.3
) is
\Lambda=4
, hence, the smallest eigenvalue of the strip would indeed be
\Lambda(H)=4=4\lambda(H)
. However, if we do not impose
u
to be constant with respect to
x
we can find different solutions with possibly smaller eigenvalues. For example, for every
\mu\geq 0
, we can look for solutions of the type

w(x,y)=h(y)\cos(\sqrt{\mu}x)

which exist if and only if
h
is a solution to

\begin{cases}-(h^{\prime\prime}-\mu h)^{\prime\prime}=(\Lambda-\mu)(h^{\prime\prime}-\mu h)&\text{in }(0,\pi),\\[5.0pt]
h(0)=h(\pi)=h^{\prime}(0)=h^{\prime}(\pi)=0.\end{cases}

(2.4)

The previous equation admits non-trivial solutions if and only if
\Lambda\geq\mu
is a solution to

2\sqrt{\mu}\sqrt{\Lambda-\mu}\left(\cosh\left(\sqrt{\mu}\pi\right)\cos\left(\sqrt{\Lambda-\mu}\pi\right)-1\right)+(\Lambda-2\mu)\sinh\left(\sqrt{\mu}\pi\right)\sin\left(\sqrt{\Lambda-\mu}\pi\right)=0.

(2.5)

For such
\Lambda
, the solutions to equation (
2.4
) are proportional to

h(y)=-\frac{\displaystyle\cosh\left(\sqrt{\mu}y\right)-\cos\left(\sqrt{\Lambda-\mu}\,y\right)}{\displaystyle\cosh\left(\sqrt{\mu}\pi\right)-\cos\left(\sqrt{\Lambda-\mu}\,\pi\right)}+\frac{\displaystyle\sqrt{\Lambda-\mu}\sinh\left(\sqrt{\mu}y\right)-\sqrt{\mu}\sin\left(\sqrt{\Lambda-\mu}\,y\right)}{\displaystyle\sqrt{\Lambda-\mu}\sinh\left(\sqrt{\mu}\pi\right)-\sqrt{\mu}\sin\left(\sqrt{\Lambda-\mu}\,\pi\right)}.

The same ODE was also studied in
[
5
]
to better understand the Buckling eigenvalue of an annulus in the limit when the difference in radii goes to zero. For every
\mu\geq 0
, let
\Lambda_{\mu}
be the smallest solution to (
2.5
).
From a variational point of view, we have

\Lambda_{\mu}=\min_{h\in H^{2}_{0}(0,\pi)}\Set{\mu+\frac{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi}(h^{\prime\prime})^{2}\,dy+\mu\int_{0}^{\pi}(h^{\prime})^{2}\,dy}{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi}(h^{\prime})^{2}\,dy+\mu\int_{0}^{\pi}h^{2}\,dy}}.

(2.6)

Which can be estimated from above using
h(y)=\sin^{2}(y)
as a test function, obtaining

\Lambda_{\mu}\leq\mu+\dfrac{16+4\mu}{4+3\mu},\quad\text{and}\quad\min_{\mu\geq 0}\Lambda_{\mu}\leq\dfrac{8}{3}\sqrt{2}.

Numerically, the minimum with respect to
\mu
of
\Lambda_{\mu}
can be estimated to be

\min_{\mu\geq 0}\Lambda_{\mu}\approx 3.7570\dots

Hence, the couple
w(y)=\sin^{2}(y)
and
\Lambda=4
is a solution to the boundary value problem (
2.1
), but
4
is not the smallest value of
\Lambda
for which a non-trivial solution exists. Namely, if we define the buckling eigenvalue of an unbounded set as the infimum of the eigenvalues of its bounded open subsets, we can formalize the previous observations to have the following

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
L>0
, and let
H=\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times(0,L)
be an infinite strip in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then

\Lambda(H)\leq\sigma\lambda(H),

where

\sigma:=\min_{\mu\geq 0}\Lambda_{\mu}<4.

Proof.

Starting from the solutions to (
2.4
), we can construct appropriate test functions on a monotone sequence
\Omega_{k}
of parallelepipeds to show that for every
\mu>0

\lim_{k}\dfrac{\Lambda(\Omega_{k})}{\lambda(\Omega_{k})}\leq\Lambda_{\mu},

where
\Lambda_{\mu}
is defined in (
2.6
).
We will show the result for
n=2
, as it is analogous for
n\geq 3
. Since the ratio

\dfrac{\Lambda(\Omega)}{\lambda(\Omega)}

is scaling invariant, we can assume
L=\pi
so that
\lambda(H)=1
. For every
\mu>0
let
h
be a minimizer to (
2.6
) with

\int_{0}^{\pi}\left(h^{\prime}\right)^{2}\,dy+\mu\int_{0}^{\pi}h^{2}\,dy=\dfrac{2\pi}{\sqrt{\mu}}.

Let
L_{\mu}=\pi/\sqrt{\mu}
and
R_{1}=(0,L_{\mu})\times(0,\pi)
, and notice that the function
w_{1}(x,y)=h(y)\cos(\sqrt{\mu}x)
solves

\begin{cases}-\Delta^{2}w_{1}=\Lambda_{\mu}\Delta w_{1}&\text{in }R_{1},\\[5.0pt]
w_{1}=\partial_{y}w_{1}=0&\text{if }y=0,\pi,\\
\partial_{x}w_{1}=\partial_{x}\Delta w_{1}=0&\text{if }x=0,L_{\mu}.\end{cases}

In particular,

\int_{R_{1}}(\Delta w_{1})^{2}\,dx=\Lambda_{\mu}\int_{R_{1}}\lvert\nabla w_{1}\rvert^{2}\,dx

and, by construction,

\int_{R_{1}}\lvert\nabla w_{1}\rvert^{2}\,dx=1.

The idea is to extend
w_{1}
periodically on the infinite strip
H
. Let
k
be a natural number, and consider the sequence of rectangles

\Omega_{k}=(-kL_{\mu},kL_{\mu})\times(0,\pi).

Let
\varphi_{k}
be a sequence of smooth cut-off functions, equi-bounded in
C^{2}
, such that

\varphi_{k}(x)=1\quad\text{ when }\quad\lvert x\rvert\leq(k-1)L_{\mu},

and with
\supp\varphi\subset(-kL_{\mu},kL_{\mu})
. We define
w_{k}(x,y)=h(y)\cos(\sqrt{\mu}x)\varphi_{k}(x)
, so that
w_{k}\in H^{2}_{0}(\Omega_{k})
. We notice that, by periodicity, for every
j
between
-k+2
and
k-1
we have that on the rectangle
R_{j}=((j-1)L_{\mu},jL_{\mu})\times(0,\pi)
it holds

\int_{R_{j}}(\Delta w_{k})^{2}\,dx=\int_{R_{1}}(\Delta w_{1})^{2}\,dx=\Lambda_{\mu}\int_{R_{1}}\lvert\nabla w_{1}\rvert^{2}\,dx=\Lambda_{\mu}\int_{R_{j}}\lvert\nabla w_{k}\rvert^{2}\,dx,

and in particular,

\int_{\Omega_{k}}(\Delta w_{k})^{2}\,dx=2(k-1)\Lambda_{\mu}+Q_{1}

where
Q_{1}
is a remainder uniformly bounded with respect to
k
. Analogously

\int_{\Omega_{k}}\lvert\nabla w_{k}\rvert^{2}\,dx=2(k-1)+Q_{2}

where
Q_{2}
is uniformly bounded as well. Hence, using
w_{k}
as a test function for
\Lambda(\Omega_{k})
, we get

\Lambda(\Omega_{k})\leq\frac{\int_{\Omega_{k}}\left(\Delta w_{k}\right)^{2}\,dx\,dy}{\int_{\Omega_{k}}\lvert\nabla w_{k}\rvert^{2}\,dx\,dy}\leq\frac{2(k-1)\Lambda_{\mu}+Q_{1}}{2(k-1)+Q_{2}}.

Then, passing to the limit as
k
goes to infinity, and using the fact that
\Omega_{k}
is an increasing sequence converging to
H
, we have that

\lim_{k}\Lambda(\Omega_{k})\leq\Lambda_{\mu}.

Hence, recalling that
\sigma=\min_{\mu\geq 0}\Lambda_{\mu}
and that
\lambda(H)=1
, we have

\Lambda(H)\leq\sigma\lambda(H).

∎

Proof of the main result

In the following, we will use the improved
\log
-concavity estimate proved in
[
1
, Theorem 1.5]
for positive Dirichlet eigenfunctions

Theorem 3.1

(Improved
\log
-concavity)
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded convex open set, let
D(\Omega)
be its diameter, and let
u
be a positive first eigenfunction associated to
\lambda(\Omega)
. If
v=\log(u)
, then for every
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and for every
x\in\Omega
we have

-\langle D^{2}v(x)\eta,\eta\rangle\geq\frac{\pi^{2}}{D(\Omega)^{2}}\lvert\eta\rvert^{2}.

Proposition 3.2

(Improved Payne inequality)
.

Let
n\geq 2
and let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded convex set. Then

\Lambda(\Omega)\leq\left(4-2\mathcal{T}(\Omega)\right)\lambda(\Omega),

(3.1)

where

\mathcal{T}(\Omega)=\dfrac{(n-1)\pi^{2}}{D(\Omega)^{2}\lambda(\Omega)}.

Proof.

Let
u\geq 0
be a Dirichlet eigenfunction associated to
\lambda(\Omega)
. Following the Payne approach, we can use
u^{2}
as a test function in the variational definition of
\Lambda(\Omega)
.

\Lambda(\Omega)\leq\dfrac{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left(\Delta u^{2}\right)^{2}\,dx}{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\nabla u^{2}\right\rvert^{2}\,dx}=\dfrac{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left(\lambda(\Omega)^{2}u^{4}+\lvert\nabla u\rvert^{4}-2\lambda(\Omega)u^{2}\lvert\nabla u\rvert^{2}\right)\,dx}{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u^{2}\lvert\nabla u\rvert^{2}\,dx}.

(3.2)

Using the equation satisfied by
u
, we immediately have

\lambda(\Omega)\int_{\Omega}u^{4}\,dx=3\int_{\Omega}u^{2}\lvert\nabla u\rvert^{2}\,dx.

(3.3)

While, letting

H_{u}=-\divv\left(\dfrac{\nabla u}{\lvert\nabla u\rvert}\right)

the mean curvature of the level sets of the function
u
, we can write

\lvert\nabla u\rvert^{4}=\divv\left(u\lvert\nabla u\rvert^{2}\nabla u\right)+3\lambda(\Omega)u^{2}\lvert\nabla u\rvert^{2}-2u\lvert\nabla u\rvert^{3}H_{u}.

(3.4)

Hence, joining (
3.2
) with (
3.3
) and (
3.4
),

\Lambda(\Omega)\leq 4\lambda(\Omega)-2\dfrac{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u\lvert\nabla u\rvert^{3}H_{u}\,dx}{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u^{2}\lvert\nabla u\rvert^{2}\,dx}.

Finally, we claim that the improved log-concavity of
u
(
Theorem 3.1
) allows us to estimate

H_{u}\geq\dfrac{(n-1)\pi^{2}}{D(\Omega)^{2}}\dfrac{u}{\lvert\nabla u\rvert},

(3.5)

which ensures equation (
3.1
). Indeed, at every
x\in\Omega
with
\nabla u(x)\neq 0
, by definition of
H_{u}
, we can rewrite it as

H_{u}=-\dfrac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\langle D^{2}u(x)\tau_{i},\tau_{i}\rangle}{\lvert\nabla u(x)\rvert},

(3.6)

where
\set{\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\dots,\tau_{n-1},\nabla u(x)/\lvert\nabla u(x)\rvert}
is an orthonormal base of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Then, using the improved log-concavity of
u
, we have that for every
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

-\langle D^{2}(\log(u))\eta,\eta\rangle\geq\dfrac{\pi^{2}}{D(\Omega)^{2}}\lvert\eta\rvert^{2},

so that, if
\eta
is orthogonal to
\nabla u(x)
, we have,

-\langle D^{2}u(x)\eta,\eta\rangle\geq\dfrac{\pi^{2}}{D(\Omega)^{2}}u(x)\lvert\eta\rvert^{2},

which, together with (
3.6
) implies estimate (
3.5
).
∎

Definition 3.3
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a convex set. We define the
support function
of
\Omega
as follows: for every
\nu\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
,

h_{\Omega}(\nu):=\max_{x\in\Omega}(x\cdot\nu).

Then, we define the
minimal width
of
\Omega
as

w(\Omega)=\min_{\nu\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\left(h_{\Omega}(\nu)+h_{\Omega}(-\nu)\right).

Remark 3.4
.

The symmetric support function
h_{\Omega}(\nu)+h_{\Omega}(-\nu)
measures the distance between two supporting hyperplanes of
\Omega
and orthogonal to the directions
\nu
and
-\nu
. The minimal width is then, by definition, the minimum of such distances, while the diameter gives the maximum. Hence, the ratio of the minimal width and the diameter of a convex set is a commonly used measure to quantify the "thinness" of a convex set.

Remark 3.5
.

Let us notice that the correction term

\mathcal{T}(\Omega)=\dfrac{(n-1)\pi^{2}}{D(\Omega)^{2}\lambda(\Omega)},

in equation (
3.1
) is a measure of the thinness of the set
\Omega
. Indeed, let us recall that for convex sets
\Omega
there exists a dimensional constant
c=c_{n}
such that

\dfrac{1}{c}\leq w(\Omega)^{2}\lambda(\Omega)\leq c.

(3.7)

For the upper bound it is sufficient to consider an infinite strip
H
of width
w(\Omega)
containing
\Omega
and, by monotonicity,

\lambda(\Omega)\geq\lambda(H)=\dfrac{\pi^{2}}{w(\Omega)^{2}}.

For the upper bound, we consider
r_{\Omega}

the inradius of
\Omega
, and by
[
4
, §10.44 Formula (9)]
we know that there exists
c_{n}>0
such that,

w(\Omega)\leq c_{n}r_{\Omega}.

(3.8)

Then, if
B_{r_{\Omega}}
is a ball inscribed in
\Omega
, by monotonicity, we have that

\lambda(\Omega)\geq\lambda(B_{r_{\Omega}})=\dfrac{c_{n}}{r_{\Omega}^{2}},

which together with (
3.8
) proves the upper bound.

Using (
3.7
) we estimate
\mathcal{T}(\Omega)
as

\dfrac{1}{c}\left(\dfrac{w(\Omega)}{D(\Omega)}\right)^{2}\leq\mathcal{T}(\Omega)\leq c\left(\dfrac{w(\Omega)}{D(\Omega)}\right)^{2}.

Therefore, the factor in front of
\lambda(\Omega)
in (
3.1
) gets closer to
4
as the set
\Omega
becomes thinner, meaning that the inequality (
3.1
) is a “good” improvement of Payne’s inequality only for non-thin sets. In the following, we will prove a different inequality, which will be “good” for thin sets.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
n\geq 2
, let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded convex set, and let
u
be the first Dirichlet eigenfunction on
\Omega
. Then, for every
\nu\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and for every
\alpha>0
, we have

\Lambda(\Omega)\leq\left(\dfrac{16-8\mathcal{T}(\Omega)+8\alpha+3\alpha^{2}}{4+3\alpha}+\dfrac{48\alpha}{4+3\alpha}\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u^{2}(\nabla u\cdot\nu)^{2}\,dx}{\displaystyle\lambda(\Omega)\int_{\Omega}u^{4}\,dx}\right)\lambda(\Omega)

(3.9)

Proof.

Step 1: Estimating with oscillating test functions

We claim that for every
\mu>0
and for every
h\in H^{2}_{0}(\Omega)
we have

\Lambda(\Omega)\leq\mu+\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\Delta h)^{2}\,dx+\mu\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla h\rvert^{2}\,dx}{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla h\rvert^{2}\,dx+\mu\int_{\Omega}h^{2}\,dx}+4\mu\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\nabla h\cdot\nu)^{2}\,dx}{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla h\rvert^{2}\,dx+\mu\int_{\Omega}h^{2}\,dx}.

(3.10)

Let
h\in H^{2}_{0}(\Omega)
and for every positive
\mu
let

s_{\mu}(x)=\sin(\sqrt{\mu}\,x\cdot\nu),\qquad\qquad c_{\mu}(x)=\cos(\sqrt{\mu}\,x\cdot\nu).

We define
\varphi_{c}(x)=h(x)c_{\mu}(x)
and
\varphi_{s}(x)=h(x)s_{\mu}(x)
. By definition of
\Lambda
we have that

\Lambda(\Omega)\leq\min\left\{\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\Delta\varphi_{c})^{2}\,dx}{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla\varphi_{c}\rvert^{2}\,dx},\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\Delta\varphi_{s})^{2}\,dx}{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla\varphi_{s}\rvert^{2}\,dx}\right\}\leq\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left((\Delta\varphi_{c})^{2}+(\Delta\varphi_{s})^{2}\right)\,dx}{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left(\lvert\nabla\varphi_{c}\rvert^{2}+\lvert\nabla\varphi_{s}\rvert^{2}\right)\,dx}.

Straightforward computations give (
3.10
).

Step 2: Choosing
h
as the square of the first Dirichlet eigenfunction

Let
u
be the first Dirichlet Laplacian eigenfunction associated to
\lambda(\Omega)
. If we take
h(x)=u^{2}
then, following the computations in
section 3
we have the following two estimates:

\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\nabla u^{2}\right\rvert^{2}\,dx=\dfrac{4\lambda(\Omega)}{3}\int_{\Omega}u^{4}\,dx,

\int_{\Omega}\left(\Delta u^{2}\right)^{2}\,dx\leq\left(4-2\mathcal{T}(\Omega)\right)\lambda(\Omega)\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\nabla u^{2}\right\rvert^{2}\,dx.

In particular,

\Lambda(\Omega)\leq\mu+\frac{\displaystyle 8(2-\mathcal{T}(\Omega))\lambda(\Omega)^{2}+4\mu\lambda(\Omega)}{\displaystyle 4\lambda(\Omega)+3\mu}+\dfrac{48\mu}{4\lambda(\Omega)+3\mu}\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u^{2}(\nabla u\cdot\nu)^{2}\,dx}{\displaystyle\lambda(\Omega)\int_{\Omega}u^{4}\,dx}.

Finally, the assertion follows choosing
\mu=\alpha\lambda(\Omega)
.
∎

Proposition 3.7

(Improved payne inequality for thin sets)

Let

Proof of

.

Let

and let
\Omega_{k}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a maximizing sequence of convex sets for

\sup_{\Omega\text{ convex}}\dfrac{\Lambda(\Omega)}{\lambda(\Omega)}.

Then, either there exists
C>0
such that other
\mathcal{T}(\Omega_{k})\geq c
or

\lim_{k}\mathcal{T}(\Omega_{k})=0.

If
\mathcal{T}(\Omega_{k})\geq c
, using (

), we have

\sup_{\Omega\text{ convex}}\dfrac{\Lambda(\Omega)}{\lambda(\Omega)}=\lim_{k}\dfrac{\Lambda(\Omega_{k})}{\lambda(\Omega_{k})}\leq 4-2c<4.

If

\lim_{k}\mathcal{T}(\Omega_{k})=0,

Remark 3.8
.

For higher dimensions, we can use estimate (
3.9
) to obtain an explicit estimate of the constant
C_{n}
in
Theorem 1.1
. In particular, if
n\geq 5
then for every bounded convex set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have

\Lambda(\Omega)\leq\dfrac{8}{3}\left(\sqrt{2-\dfrac{4}{n}}+\dfrac{2}{n}\right)\lambda(\Omega).

(3.13)

Indeed, let
u
be a first Dirichlet eigenfunction on
\Omega
, then

\inf_{\nu\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\int_{\Omega}u^{2}(\nabla u\cdot\nu)^{2}\,dx\leq\dfrac{1}{n}\int_{\Omega}u^{2}\lvert\nabla u\rvert^{2}\,dx=\dfrac{\lambda(\Omega)}{3n}\int_{\Omega}u^{4}\,dx.

Therefore, using (
3.9
), we have that for every
\alpha>0

\Lambda(\Omega)\leq\left(\dfrac{16+8\left(1+\dfrac{2}{n}\right)\alpha+3\alpha^{2}}{4+3\alpha}-\dfrac{8\mathcal{T}(\Omega)}{4+3\alpha}\right)\lambda(\Omega).

Minimizing the function

\alpha\in[0,+\infty)\mapsto\dfrac{16+8\left(1+\dfrac{2}{n}\right)\alpha+3\alpha^{2}}{4+3\alpha},

for
n=2,3,4
we have once again the estimate (
3.1
), while for
n\geq 5
we have

\begin{split}\Lambda(\Omega)&\leq\left(\dfrac{8}{3}\left(\sqrt{2-\dfrac{4}{n}}+\dfrac{2}{n}\right)-\sqrt{\dfrac{2n}{n-2}}\mathcal{T}(\Omega)\right)\lambda(\Omega)\\[10.0pt]
&\leq\dfrac{8}{3}\left(\sqrt{2-\dfrac{4}{n}}+\dfrac{2}{n}\right)\lambda(\Omega)\end{split},

that is (
3.13
).
