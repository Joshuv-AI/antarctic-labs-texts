Integrating task-state electroencephalography and motor function for intelligent assessment of recovery status from prolonged disorders of consciousness
Authors: Longhao Zhang, Hongzhen Cui, Xiaoyue Zhu, Guilong Zhang, Yunfeng Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurately assessing recovery in patients with prolonged disorders of consciousness (PDoC) is essential for evaluating the therapeutic efficacy and optimizing clinical care. However, substantial individual variability and poorly understood recovery mechanisms render this task exceptionally challenging.MethodsWe collected task-state electroencephalography and clinical records from 78 PDoC patients to develop an intelligent assessment framework. Power spectral densities (PSDs) and coherence values (CVs) derived from event-related potentials (ERPs) were extracted and integrated with motor function scores to construct a multidimensional feature set. Linear support vector machine (SVM) and logistic regression (LR) models were built and rigorously evaluated via leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV). SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) was utilized for visual explanation.ResultsThe LOOCV indicated that both LR and SVM achieved optimal, similar performance in integrating alpha-band features with motor function (Macro-Average F1-score [Ma-F]: 76.79% versus 75.54%). The Receiver Operating Characteristic curves (with Area Under the Curve values of 77.57% for LR and 72.82% for SVM) and Precision-Recall curves (with Average Precision values of 68.87% for LR and 59.85% for SVM) suggested that the framework performs stably. The reliability curves indicated that both models demonstrate good calibration and are reliable for assessment.ConclusionsThe study provides preliminary evidence that alpha-band PSDs and CVs derived from ERPs potentially enhance the motor function assessment performance. This integrated paradigm provides a valuable indicator. Overall, our findings suggest that combining neuroscience with intelligent technology holds promise for improving personalized diagnosis and optimizing the PDoC management.Keywords: Event-related potentials, Motor function, Prolonged disorders of consciousness, Machine learning, SHAP analysis, Intelligent assessment

Introduction

Prolonged disorders of consciousness (PDoC) are pathological conditions characterized by a loss of consciousness lasting more than 28 days [1], resulting from brain injury caused by various etiologies. Subtypes include unresponsive wakefulness syndrome (UWS) and minimally conscious state (MCS) [2]. The latter can be subdivided into MCS- and MCS+ based on the degree of non-responsiveness [3]. Statistically, the global prevalence ranges from 0.5 to 5 cases per 100,000 population, with an annual incidence of 2.5 per 100,000 [4]. Long-term disabilities require costly life-sustaining care, which strains medical resources and increases the burden on society [5]. Stigmatization has caused severe consequences for patients and families [6, 7]. PDoC patients aren’t always unable to awaken, and early intervention may improve wakefulness. Accurate diagnosis of recovery is crucial for assessing intervention and guiding care [8, 9]. However, due to individual differences and the uncertainty of the recovery process, the assessment remains a clinical challenge [10, 11].

Behavioral assessment is commonly used to assess consciousness, including the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) and the Coma Recovery Scale-Revised (CRS-R). The GCS is a 15-point scale consisting of eye, verbal, and motor scores [12]. It provides a standardized basis for assessing acute brain injuries, such as traumatic brain injury (TBI) and stroke. The limitations include its failure with patients on artificial airways, information loss due to equal-weighted summation [13], and its inability to detect latent consciousness [14]. The CRS-R was developed for PDoC and is considered the gold standard [15]. Compared with the GCS, it is more complex and relies more heavily on experience [16, 17]. Furthermore, its performance remains limited, with a misdiagnosis rate of about 40% [18], and it still can’t detect latent consciousness [19]. Therefore, improving behavioral assessment to enhance the reliability of PDoC recovery diagnosis has become an urgent priority.

Bioelectricity, such as electroencephalography (EEG), offers solutions [3, 20]. EEG analysis includes event-related potential (ERP) approaches and methods that preserve the full time-series information without heavy averaging [21, 22]. Traditional ERP analysis relies on time-locked averaging across trials [23], implicitly assuming stationarity and that background activity is uncorrelated noise that cancels out. It yields robust estimates of phase-locked responses but discards trial-to-trial variability and non-phase-locked (induced) activity, which may reveal consciousness and plasticity in PDoC. In contrast, EEG is probabilistic, non-stationary, and random. Thus, techniques that reduce trial counts while preserving information, such as single-trial projections, spatial filtering, or optimization-based decomposition methods, have been proposed and shown promise in low-trial scenarios [24, 25]. They offer complementary perspectives: ERPs highlight deterministic stimulus-locked responses linked to sensory/cognitive processing, while non-averaging or induced-activity methods better capture dynamic network states and variability underlying consciousness recovery. Integrating power spectral densities (PSDs) and coherence values (CVs) on ERP epochs bridges these paradigms. Although it inherits averaging assumptions, the issue could be mitigated in future studies by introducing single-trial or adaptive methods.

EEG is useful for capturing latent consciousness. However, clinically, it is susceptible to non-neural artifacts, individual differences, and environmental factors [26], and unstable for isolated analysis. In addition, its interpretation involves higher professional barriers and is less widely adopted than behavioral assessment [27]. Combining EEG and behavioral assessment is more reliable. Bodien et al. [28] noted that EEG can detect latent brain responses in some patients who are unable to follow commands, suggesting that it enhances the behavioral assessment. Du et al. [10] developed a method to distinguish UWS, MCS, and locked-in syndrome using EEG during repeated imagined movements. They confirmed that task-related activity in the sensorimotor and parietal cortices can classify patients, and that EEG-based measures can support behavioral assessment. Pruvost-Robieux et al. [21] also noted that combining repeated clinical assessments, neuropathology, and neurophysiology improves prognostic assessment. These findings indicate that the combination holds great potential. However, the study is still in its early stages, and there are currently no standards for selecting and integrating behavioral assessment criteria and EEG features, nor has it been sufficiently validated.

Based on this, we have proposed an intelligent framework for assessing PDoC recovery status by integrating task-state EEG (tEEG) with behavioral assessment records. The main contributions of this study are as follows:

We integrated ERP features with motor function to construct intelligent models, enhancing the readability of tEEG and improving assessment quality.

Materials and methods

Data sources and study cohorts

We used the consciousness-detection EEG dataset [29] established by the Center for Brain-Computer Interfaces (BCI) and Information Processing at South China University of Technology. It contains 78 patient records from the General Hospital of the Southern Theater Command of the People’s Liberation Army and the Guangdong Provincial Work Injury Rehabilitation Hospital, covering the period from 2013 to 2019. The provider has performed de-identification, ethical review, and obtained written informed consent from the legal representatives, ensuring that the data may be made publicly available under the CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 license. We submitted an application and were granted.

Inclusion criteria include: (a) The diagnoses were UWS or MCS- before the experiment, and no detectable response to commands within one week of admission. (b) The brain injury occurred more than one month ago. (c) No history of visual or hearing impairment. (d) No sedatives were administered within 12 h before collection. (e) A visual startle response was present according to the CRS-R, or visual evoked potentials were normal when this response was not present. Exclusion criteria include: (a) Currently using neuromuscular blocking agents or sedatives. (b) Complete CRS-R assessments were conducted fewer than twice.

The acquisition system was NuAmps (Compumedics Neuroscan, Australia). The signals were referenced to the right mastoid and digitized at a sampling rate of 250 Hz. The electrode impedance remained below 5kΩ. The collection employed three hybrid BCI paradigms to improve adaptability for subjects. Each subject participated in either a photograph, a digital, or an audiovisual paradigm. The collection procedure is described by Pan et al. [30]. A single EEG recording (single block) consists of multiple trials. For multiple blocks, since subjects tend to tire easily and struggle to work continuously, the procedures were often scheduled on different days.

Task-state EEG preprocessing and feature engineering

Figure 1(a) illustrates the preprocessing and feature extraction workflow. We selected nine channels commonly used in ERP analysis [31, 32], including Pz, P3, P4, Cz, Fz, FCz, Oz, O1, and O2. Since power-line noise, eye-movement artifacts, and other factors can mask the weak raw signals, we have integrated multiple methods to mitigate interference. We selected a 50 Hz notch filter to eliminate power-line interference from the power supply to the equipment and circuits. Next, a bandpass filter with a frequency range of 0.5–80 Hz was used to extract the required frequency band [33]. Furthermore, by re-referencing to establish a common zero point for multichannel signal analysis, we eliminated the systematic noise and bias introduced by the original reference point. Finally, we used independent component analysis (ICA) to remove eye movement artifacts.

We performed feature engineering on the preprocessed tEEG. Current evidence suggests that EEG features across frequency bands can indicate recovery in PDoC [34]. We studied the features derived from ERP waves across various frequency bands. Taking into account the timing of waveforms such as the N100 and P300, we extracted the signal from each channel from 200ms before to 800ms after stimulus onset for each trial, thereby dividing the tEEG into multiple epochs. Given the sampling frequency, a single epoch consists of 250 data points. We calculated the average amplitude using data from the 200ms preceding each stimulus in each channel as a baseline, then subtracted it from each value within a single epoch.

We concatenated the signals from multiple epochs for each channel within the block and filtered various frequency bands. These bands include delta (0.5–4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–12 Hz), beta (12–30 Hz), and gamma (30–80 Hz). Inspired by Park et al. [35], we calculated the PSD for each band and the CV for the same band across different channels. PSD reflects the distribution of oscillatory energy across different bands, and its variations are associated with alertness levels and cognitive tasks [36]. CV denotes the degree of synchronization between oscillatory rhythms across brain regions, indicating the strength of functional connectivity among them and helping to elucidate the collaborative mechanisms of brain networks.

The PSD estimation of ERP time windows was performed using the multitaper method [37]. We took into account that global Fourier spectrum estimates, such as the periodogram, rely on the assumption of full-path stationarity of the signal. The assumption contradicts the inherently non-stationary nature of the EEG, and it results in significant spectral leakage. The performance of the AR model spectral estimation is highly dependent on the manually selected model order [38]. The multitaper applies multiple orthogonal window functions (the Slepian sequence) to the EEG, calculates the discrete Fourier transform and power spectrum separately for each window, and then averages the results, effectively reducing the risk of spectral leakage. Meanwhile, it avoids manual configuration of segmented window lengths and overlap ratios, and optimizes spectral estimation accuracy by jointly processing multiple sets of orthogonal windows. We calibrate the values using frequency points and the sampling frequency to obtain the standard PSD. The method for calculating the PSD is given in Eq. (1). We calculated the mean, minimum, maximum, standard deviation, variance, sum, and count across multiple blocks for each subject. By aggregating multiple records, we have minimized random variations in signal segments while preserving authentic, stable physiological features. After aggregation, the feature space comprises 1,575 dimensions (315 related to PSDs and 1,260 to CVs).

Since motor function is a core item in CRS-R, GCS, and the Full Outline of UnResponsiveness (FOUR), and is crucial for indicating recovery status, we included it. We assessed improvements in motor function using the CRS-R subscale. Specifically, if the motor score improved, it was marked 1; otherwise, it was marked 0. We integrated the binarized motor function with the tEEG features to construct the feature set. To eliminate unit effects, we standardized the set. Meanwhile, to reduce redundancy, we performed principal component analysis (PCA). Finally, the set was used to assess PDoC’s recovery status.

We employed a concurrent assessment paradigm within the same time window, aiming to reliably assess PDoC recovery by combining tEEG features with improvements in motor function. Unlike tasks involving the prediction of long-term outcomes, in this case, both the analytical data (tEEG, improvements in motor function) and the label (PDoC recovery status) fall within the same observation window. In particular, data on improvements in motor function and on assessment of PDoC recovery were collected simultaneously over the same time period. tEEG acquisition took place within the same time window. We didn’t include follow-up data collected after the cut-off date, thereby eliminating issues such as temporal misalignment and overlapping follow-up periods, which effectively prevented data leakage.

PDoC recovery criteria

Each subject underwent a CRS-R assessment and a diagnosis by an experienced physician, both before and after the experiment. Based on the diagnostic results, we classified subjects whose consciousness level improved (from UWS to MCS- or MCS+, or MCS- to MCS+) as the “recovered group”, while others (remaining in the UWS or MCS-) as the “non-recovered group”. We binarized the recovery status, assigning 1 to the recovered group and 0 to the other group.

For the SVM, we selected the linear kernel and enabled probability estimates by setting the probability to True to facilitate the calculation of evaluation metrics. The maximum number of iterations, max_iter, was left at its default. For the LR, the penalty item was L2, the solver was lbfgs, and the maximum number of iterations was set to the default value of 100. The random_state for both models was set to the standard value of 42.

Due to the limited samples, we employed leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) to assess performance comprehensively. We used each unique subject record as the test set and the remaining samples as the training set. The LOOCV was repeated 78 times, and the corresponding models were built sequentially. Throughout the training and testing process, records for the same subject were strictly limited to either the training or the test set, thereby avoiding data leakage.

We strictly limited the processing sequence for standardization and PCA to avoid potential data leakage issues that could lead to an overestimation of performance. We avoided performing standardization and PCA on the entire dataset. During the iterative process, we built a standardizer and a PCA model on the training set for each fold. Feature scaling and PCA for the test set are performed using the tools developed for the training set as described above. All preprocessing steps are strictly confined to each training fold.

To determine a reasonable number of principal components, we analyzed the cumulative variance explained, as shown in Fig. 2. The first 30 principal components explain 95.74% of the variance, capturing most of the information in the original features. The cumulative variance explained by the first 45 principal components reached 98.65%, indicating an extremely high level of information retention. It provides an important reference for determining the input dimension during model training.

Performance evaluation

We used various metrics from diagnostic tasks to evaluate the performance. Validity analysis includes seven metrics: Accuracy, Macro-Average Precision (Ma-P), Macro-Average Recall (Ma-R), Ma-F, Area Under Curve (AUC), Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), and Kappa. Explanations of evaluation metrics

Additionally, we report the confusion matrix, ROC curve, and Precision-Recall (PR) curve. The confusion matrix is a 2 × 2 table that shows the number of subjects who were correctly and incorrectly assessed. ROC and PR (where the area under the curve denotes Average Precision [AP]) evaluate the framework’s overall robustness and its ability to handle imbalanced data, respectively. The proportion of the recovered group determines the baseline value. The AP above this value is considered superior to the random level.

To evaluate the reliability of the framework, we used the Brier score and reliability diagram to obtain its calibration. The Brier score is the average of the mean-squared errors between observations and model outputs. It ranges from 0 to 1. The lower the score, the better the calibration. The reliability diagram illustrates the alignment between the recovery probabilities estimated by the framework and the observation. Their combination can help determine whether the assessments are reliable.

To enhance decision-making transparency, we report the parameters and the SHAP decision plot for the outperforming model. The parameters reveal the overall patterns of feature dependence, thereby aiding understanding of its decision-making mechanisms. It quantifies the extent to which each PC contributes to the assessment. The SHAP decision plot visually illustrates the framework’s decision-making process for evaluating a test subject. It demonstrates how the framework aggregates baseline scores to the final assessment score at the top, based on the contributions of different features.

Statistical analysis

For baseline characteristics, discrete variables were presented as counts and percentages, while continuous variables were reported as the mean ± standard deviation and analyzed using an independent-samples t-test. To analyze differences in assessment results between the models, we used McNemar’s test because the labels are binary. We calculated the 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) for the primary metrics of the two models by performing 1,000 bootstrap replicates. To compare the AUCs across frequency bands and models, we used DeLong’s tests. We used the Wilcoxon test for the statistical analysis across different PCA dimensions. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using Python 3.8.20. MNE-Python 1.6.1 [39] was used for tEEG preprocessing, ERP analysis, and feature extraction. Scikit-learn 1.3.2 was used for standardization, PCA, model building, and performance analysis. SHAP 0.44.1 was used for explainability analysis. Matplotlib 3.7.5 and seaborn 0.13.2 were used for visualization.

We included 78 subjects, see Table 2. The cohort’s average age was 38 ± 14 years, with 34.62% of subjects being female. There were three causes: 21.79% were anoxic brain injury (ABI), 52.56% were TBI, and 25.64% were cerebrovascular disease (CVD). All subjects had been symptomatic for more than one month, with the longest duration being 20 months. Regarding the BCI paradigms, 35 underwent the photograph paradigm, 13 the number paradigm, and the rest the audiovisual paradigm. During the follow-up period, 29 showed improvements in motor function, while others did not. 35 showed consciousness recovery.

Although the alpha band demonstrated the best performance across various metrics, the DeLong test (p-value > 0.05) did not reach statistical significance. The non-significant result is primarily attributable to insufficient statistical power due to the limited sample size, rather than to the invalidity of the alpha-band information. The current evidence is insufficient to support a statistically significant conclusion regarding the alpha band, but it shows the most promising leading trend. Its absolute superiority, however, requires further validation in a larger population.

According to McNemar’s test, there is no significant difference between the assessment results of the two models (p-value = 1.00), as confirmed by the confusion matrices. LR leads SVM by a narrow margin in correctly assessing one non-recovered subject. Overall, both models performed well and accurately assessed the majority of cases. Figure 3 shows the ROC and PR curves. The AUC values for both models exceed 72.00%, and the AP values exceed the proportion of the recovered group (44.87%), indicating that these two models outperform random guessing. The AP of LR is higher than that of SVM, suggesting that LR has an advantage in handling imbalanced data. Although the AUC for LR is higher, the DeLong test indicates that the difference is not statistically significant (p-value = 0.22), suggesting that their overall discriminatory performance is comparable. Additionally, the AP values for both models are lower than the AUC, suggesting that their performance in distinguishing the recovered group needs improvement.

To validate the advantage of our framework, we compared two models (LR and SVM) with several tree-family models commonly used in EEG research, as shown in Table 4. Analysis shows that the first two achieved the best performance on the current dataset. The reason is that, on the one hand, due to limited sample size, tree-based models are prone to overfitting and exhibit poor generalization, making it difficult to leverage their nonlinear advantages. On the other hand, PCA projects high-dimensional features into a low-dimensional space through linear transformations, with each principal component being a linear combination of the original features. It is consistent with the feature modeling characteristics of LR and linear SVM.

Figure 5 illustrates the impact of the principal component dimensions on performance. We report on Ma-F and MCC to comprehensively analyze the framework’s performance limits. As shown in the figure, SVM and LR achieve optimal performance across various dimensions. Specifically, SVM at 30 dimensions, while LR at 45. When fewer than 10 are present, the performance of both models declines significantly. It suggests that low dimensions provide insufficient information for assessment, making it difficult to reason with the models. However, when it exceeds 45, there is no improvement, and even a decline. It suggests that excessively high dimensionality leads to redundancy, which interferes with the assessment. Therefore, clinically, an appropriate threshold should be determined based on the feature distributions to maximize performance.

Explainability analysis

To understand the framework’s decision-making mechanism, we use LR as an example. Figures 7 and A1 (see Appendix A) present the correctly assessed subject (ID: P01) and the incorrect case (ID: P07). The left figure shows the LR coefficients (which can also be interpreted as feature importance). The figure on the right shows the decision path for assessing an individual subject, with the vertical axis representing the importance of local features. There is consistency between the two figures. For example, for PC23, the coefficient is negative, and the subject’s value is also negative. Multiplying them increases the assessment probability. Therefore, the marginal contribution is also positive. PC44 to PC31 causes rapid score accumulation, increasing the risk of misdiagnosis. PC45 to PC1 corrects the score, enabling the LR to produce the correct result. For the misdiagnosed subject P07, the LR can’t learn the optimal coefficient due to the limited number of samples. Several locally important features actually exacerbate the misdiagnosis.

Figure 10 shows the contribution of the CVs across channel pairs to the principal components. We report the top 5 brain functional connections by contribution within each PC. Among these, the CVs for Cz and FCz contribute the most to all principal components. It is because their electrode positions are most similar and both are located along the brain’s midline, resulting in high signal-space correlation and strong synchronization. Previous studies have shown that artifacts are less likely to occur in central brain regions than in peripheral regions, and that these regions exhibit higher test-retest reliability [42], which may lead them to dominate in PCA. The impact on the assessment is that higher-dimensional principal components will preserve more comprehensive information on brain functional connectivity, which may help indicate the recovery status. However, more complex patterns may also introduce redundancy.

Although the explainability analysis has enhanced the transparency of the framework’s assessment mechanism, there is still room for improvement. The SHAP analysis was performed on principal components rather than directly explainable physiological variables. Although it explains the framework’s internal operational logic and the contribution patterns of composite factors, it still falls short of analyzing the direct mechanisms underlying clinical physiological variables. Future research will build upon this foundation to eliminate barriers to clinical interpretation through modeling original physiological features and attribution analysis, thereby further enhancing clinical utility and medical reference value.

Discussion

In this study, we developed and validated an intelligent assessment framework of PDoC recovery status. We extracted tEEG features, including the PSDs and CVs derived from ERP waves. Ultimately, the features and motor function were fully integrated into the framework. We constructed two models: the linear SVM and the LR. The results show that LR (Ma-F: 76.79%, MCC: 53.67%) performs similarly to SVM (Ma-F: 75.54%, MCC: 51.31%). McNemar’s test indicated no significant difference in the assessment results (p-value = 1.00).

A review of the results shows that motor function is critical to the models’ performance. The effectiveness of motor function has been demonstrated in previous studies [43, 44]. We also confirmed that it is useful for assessing recovery status (both models achieving AUCs of 70.86% and MCCs of 42.60%). However, its performance fell short of expectations. We found that an increase in the CRS-R motor score is not linearly correlated with PDoC recovery. For example, P06 showed recovery, but the motor score did not improve. P11 and P12 didn’t recover, but their motor scores improved. Thus, motor function is difficult to capture latent consciousness and may interfere with decision-making. Therefore, it is necessary to explore ways to enhance the assessment of motor function.

Brain-computer interfaces offer a novel approach to detecting latent consciousness. Du et al. [10] confirmed the important role of the BCI paradigm in diagnosing PDoC and in enhancing behavioral assessment, offering insights into addressing the above issues. Meanwhile, the Chinese Society of Rehabilitation Medicine [20] also recommended using EEG as supplementary information to improve the performance of motor function assessment. However, current studies combining motor function and EEG to assess PDoC recovery status remain limited. Our study provides an analysis of this issue. The results confirmed that ERP features are beneficial in enhancing motor function assessment (Ma-F improved by 4.71% with SVM and by 5.93% with LR). Therefore, this integrated approach is expected to improve the quality of recovery status assessment.

We highlight the importance of the alpha band in ERPs. Dong et al. [45] and Zhou et al. [46] demonstrated that power and coherence in the alpha band are vital brain network features for assessing consciousness states and cognitive recovery, respectively. Our study further validates the importance of alpha-band frequency and spatial features in assessing the recovery status of PDoC. Earlier studies have shown that alpha waves dominate during rest, while they attenuate during thinking or calculation [47]. In PDoC patients, the depression of alpha-band power in UWS is much stronger than in MCS, and the power in MCS is significantly higher than in UWS [41]. Therefore, power in this band is beneficial for reflecting changes in consciousness.

In the alpha band, PSD yields a larger performance gain than CV (15.61% vs. 4.43% for Δ [SVM, Ma-F]). It suggests that BCI-induced PSD fluctuations hold promise as prognostic biomarkers, consistent with Ling et al. [48]. It offers advantages over functional connectivity in the brain. Nevertheless, we recommend the combination, as they provide more comprehensive information on neural regulation for assessment. In addition, based on electrode placement and changes in PSDs, we can also infer that the posterior cortex is involved in the formation or maintenance of consciousness.

Our study has significant clinical implications. First, we provide an intelligent framework for assessing the recovery status of patients with PDoC. The framework integrates signal processing, feature engineering, and machine learning to establish a correlation between tEEG and motor function and recovery status. On the one hand, tEEG preprocessing and feature engineering based on intelligent technologies help reduce noise interference and improve signal interpretability and analytical capabilities. Meanwhile, it provides preliminary validation for the cross-application of BCIs in PDoC. On the other hand, machine learning can automatically model the complex interactions between tEEG features and motor function, thereby improving information utilization and indicating its potential for clinical applications.

We have preliminarily demonstrated that combining ERPs’ PSDs and CVs with motor function constitutes an effective paradigm for assessing PDoC recovery status. Evoked potentials from audio-visual BCI paradigms can provide information on cortical activity, supplementing insights into underlying neural fluctuations that are difficult to capture or are easily overlooked through external behavioral observation [19]. Therefore, the multi-dimensional feature fusion achieves higher accuracy than relying solely on motor function. Finally, to address the black-box bottleneck in model inference [49], we conducted an explainability analysis that included feature importance and decision paths. Additionally, we report the contributions of features, channels, and functional connections across principal components to reduce PCA ambiguity. It effectively enhances the transparency of the reasoning mechanism and narrows the gap between the study and clinical practice.

Our framework can be directly integrated into the standard CRS-R workflow. In clinical practice, core data is collected at fixed follow-up time points. The status of motor function is recorded using standardized scales. The tEEG is collected during the matching period. The features acquired during the same time window are fed into the framework, enabling joint analysis of behavioral assessment and neurophysiological activity. The framework is not intended to replace the CRS-R assessment, but rather to use the result of quantitative analysis as objective evidence of trends in the consciousness recovery and the status of latent neurological function during the same period. It helps to calibrate for the subjective bias introduced by a single scale.

Based on data modeling results, LOOCV performance, and the rigor of clinical rehabilitation assessments, we established a symmetric high-confidence threshold scheme. In this study, the model was set to use a confidence level ≥ 0.80 (i.e., a probability of non-recovery ≤ 0.20) as the threshold for determining high clinical confidence. When the confidence level reaches the threshold mentioned above, the model’s conclusions provide strong corroboration of the CRS-R score. It can serve as a reliable basis for clinically assessing improvements in consciousness and adjusting rehabilitation intervention plans. If the results fall within the grey zone, they should be used only as an auxiliary reference for assessing the patient’s current rehabilitation status. They must be analyzed in conjunction with the overall disease course and clinical signs. If necessary, retesting and a repeat concurrent combined assessment may be conducted at a later date. The threshold was established to balance data stability and safety in clinical assessment, and is aligned with the practical needs of long-term follow-up and phased efficacy assessments.

This study still has limitations. Due to medical privacy policies, we used a single-center dataset and have not yet validated it with external data. Simultaneously, clinical ethics, family preferences, stringent data-collection requirements, and costs further limit the cohort size. Although we used the LOOCV to enhance reliability, a larger dataset would be preferable, as it would improve performance. It will be explored in a future study. Additionally, PCA complicates explainability, as it represents the combined contributions of different features rather than their original values. Therefore, it is necessary to explore new feature engineering techniques. Finally, as our study is a proof of concept, we were unable to conduct a comprehensive assessment of its impact on clinical practice at this time.

Conclusion

We propose a novel framework for assessing the recovery status in patients with prolonged disorders of consciousness based on task-state EEG and motor function. The results indicate that combining intelligent technology with event-related potential features and motor function is viable. The PSDs and CVs of the alpha band in ERP show promise in reflecting patients’ recovery status and have the potential to enhance the performance of motor function assessments. Explainable artificial intelligence enhances the transparency of reasoning mechanisms, thereby contributing to this study’s translational value.

In future research, we will first focus on the external validation of the framework (including establishing collaborations, unifying workflows, and evaluating cross-center generalizability), aiming to improve its accuracy and stability by integrating and expanding multi-center clinical data. Meanwhile, it will help assess translational potential in clinical applications. In addition, we will explore the effectiveness of other novel paradigms that integrate EEG features (such as different PSD variants and non-averaging methods) with behavioral assessment and develop better models that ensure accuracy while avoiding excessive ambiguity. Finally, we will conduct preliminary explorations of its clinical applications and refine it based on feedback from healthcare professionals.
