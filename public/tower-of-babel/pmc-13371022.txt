Effects of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi on the bacterial communities of three compartments in strawberry roots
Authors: Jing Xu, Yuxue Duan, Wenjing Wu, Fengwei Diao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundArbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi can regulate microbial communities in rhizosphere soil and promote plant growth. However, few studies have examined the regulated differences in microbial communities between new and old roots. A pot experiment was performed to study the effects of inoculation with Funneliformis mosseae (FM) and Rhizophagus intraradices (RI) on bacterial communities associated with new and old strawberry roots.ResultsCompared with the non-inoculation control (CK), both FM and RI treatments increased the fresh and dry weights of strawberry plants and decreased the soil available nutrition. Bacterial communities were analyzed in old root rhizosphere (ORR), new root rhizosphere (NRR), and new root endosphere (NRE). Among these, ORR had the highest number of bacterial genera. Both fungal inoculations reduced the richness of NRR bacterial communities, while RI treatment additionally reduced community evenness. Compared with CK, FM treatment significantly increased the relative abundance of Arthrobacter, whereas RI treatment significantly increased the relative abundance of Lysobacter in NRR communities, and of Allorhizobium-Neorhizobium-Pararhizobium-Rhizobium in NRE communities. Overall, AM affected bacterial recruitment and assembly rather than changing the original community. Network structure analysis showed that both AM fungi decreased network density and average degree, increased the positive correlation ratios among bacterial genera, and enhanced network modularity. The genus Ellin6067 served as a keystone in both FM and RI treatments. Correlation analysis further showed that this genus was significantly correlated with total plant dry weight.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that AM fungi tend to regulate the assembly of bacterial communities associated with new growth roots and enrich functional bacteria that may synergistically promote plant growth. Thus, this study provides additional evidence to the development direction for green and sustainable production in strawberry.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09051-2.

Introduction

Strawberry (Fragaria × ananassa) is widely known as the “Queen of Fruits”, because it contains various vitamins, amino acids, flavonoids, and minerals [1]. Additionally, the plant has a short production cycle and rapid fruit maturation. In recent years, the global cultivation area of strawberry has expanded continuously [1–2]. However, strawberry has a significant demand for fertilizers during the production process. In particular, facility cultivation with high input and output can lead to rapid and severe soil degradation [3]. Therefore, the development of environmentally friendly and efficient bio-fertilizers is urgently required to promote the healthy and sustainable growth of the strawberry industry.

Arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi can enhance plant nutrient uptake and improve fertilizer use efficiency [4]. That is one of the reasons that AM fungi regulate root-associated microbial communities and influence their assembly [5–6]. Diao et al. studied the effects of AM fungi on the assembly of the bacterial community in tomato roots, which reported that AM fungi enriched the Arthrobacter genus and increased positive correlations among bacteria [7]. Similarly, Li et al. have shown that AM fungi can enhance the complexity and stability of rhizosphere microbial network in maize under drought stress, thereby improving plant growth and production [8]. Thus, the ability of AM fungi to regulate and assemble root-associated microbial communities may be a key factor in the development of a green and sustainable strawberry industry.

Previous studies have demonstrated that AM fungi often enrich bacteria involved in nutrition metabolism (e.g., phosphorus metabolism) [9–10]. However, many of these conclusions were based on test plants cultured in aseptic substrates. For instance, Zhang et al. reported that AM fungi mediated rhizospheric and endophytic bacterial communities of Allium schoenoprasum, based on soil sterilized at 100 °C for 1 h per day over three consecutive days to eliminate indigenous microbiota [11]. Tang et al. used γ-irradiated dry soil to examine the changes in rhizosphere microbial community structure induced by Funneliformis mosseae [12]. Few studies which used an aseptic soil have additionally employed a part original substrate, carrying indigenous microbes, to evaluate the community assembly driven by AM fungi. These carried microbes may develop complex relationships with the inoculated AM fungi. In the study, after soil from a greenhouse that cultivated five times consecutive strawberry crops was autoclaved and sterilized, it was used as a test substrate. Strawberry seedlings, containing the nursery substrate which took along many microbes, were used as test plants. AM fungi F. mosseae and Rhizophagus intraradices were applied separately to assess their effects on rhizospheric and endophytic bacterial communities in strawberry roots. This study aims to provide scientific basis for developing AM fungus-based specialized microbial fertilizers for strawberry cultivation, thereby promoting the sustainable and healthy development of the strawberry industry.

Materials and methods

Experimental materials

Strawberry seedlings were provided by the Pomology Institute, Shanxi Agricultural University (Shanxi, China). Seedlings were propagated by layering, using sterilized peat as the substrate, with branches pressed into trays for rooting. The seedlings were 31 days old, and the cultivar was ‘Miaoxiang’.

The tested AM fungi were F. mosseae (NO. BGC HEB07B) and R. intraradices (NO. BGC BJ09), both provided by the Institute of Plant Nutrition and Resources, Beijing Academy of Agriculture and Forestry Sciences (Beijing, China). To produce inoculum, pot cultures were established for 3 months using maize (Zea mays) and white clover (Trifolium repens) as hosts, and a sand soil mixture (1:1, w/w) as the propagation substrate. The resulting mixture, containing fungal spores, mycelium, and root segments, served as the AM fungal inoculum [13]. Each 10 g of inoculum contained approximately 120 spores.

The test soil was collected from a greenhouse in Qinbi (Hongtong, Shanxi, China), where strawberry had been cultured consecutively five times. The soil properties were as follows: pH, 7.16; electrical conductivity (EC), 0.58 mS·cm− 1; organic matter content, 4.41%; available nitrogen (N), 4.35 mg·kg− 1; available phosphorus (P), 88.10 mg·kg− 1; and available potassium (K), 28.95 mg·kg− 1. The soil was air-dried naturally, passed through a 2-mm sieve, and then sterilized by autoclaving at 121 °C, 0.24 MPa for 90 min [13].

Conditions and process of pot culture

The pot experiment followed a randomized block design and was conducted from November 10, 2023, to February 5, 2024, in a glass greenhouse at Shanxi Agricultural University (Shanxi, China). The greenhouse temperature was maintained between 10 °C and 25 °C, under natural light conditions.

Three treatment groups were established: inoculation with F. mosseae (FM), inoculation with R. intraradices (RI), and a non-inoculation control (CK). Each treatment had four pot replicates, with one strawberry seedling per pot. Pots measured 14.0 cm bottom diameter, 17.0 cm top diameter, and 18.0 cm height.

Each pot was filled with 2.0 kg of sterilized, air-dried soil. The FM group received 100 g of F. mosseae inoculum, while the RI group received 100 g of R. intraradices inoculum; the CK group received 50 g each of sterilized F. mosseae inoculum and sterilized R. intraradices inoculum (autoclaved for 60 min to kill all viable microorganisms); the soil and inoculum were mixed evenly. Seedlings were selected by weight before transplanting to ensure equivalence. One seedling whose root adhered the peat from the propagation phase was transplanted into each pot, and 100 g of sterilized air-dried soil was added to fill the gaps. Subsequently, 20 mL of filtrate was added to each pot. The filtrate was prepared from an equal mixture of F. mosseae and R. intraradices inocula suspended in in water, stirred, settled, and filtered through a 0.25 μm filter membrane to remove AM fungi) [14–15]. Eight pots were initially established in each treatment. Soil moisture was maintained at 75% of water-holding capacity using a weighing method, with daily water supplementation. After 15 days, four uniform surviving plants were retained per treatment group. After 87 days of cultivation, plants were harvested via destructive sampling. Shoots and roots were separated at the stem base.

From each pot, 1 g of root sample was randomly collected and cut into segments. Of these, 45 root segments were randomly selected, decolorized, stained with 0.05% trypan blue, and examined. Arbuscules, hyphae, and other colonization structures were observed in all root segments from the FM and RI groups, whereas no colonization was detected in the CK group. Subsequent measurements were performed.

Sample collection

At harvest, strawberry roots could be clearly divided into old roots grown in the peat and new roots grown in the soil. Rhizosphere soil associated with both the old roots and new roots were collected separately for bacterial community analysis. Roots were first shaken to remove non-adhesive soil, then washed in sterile water to collect tightly adhesive soil. The resulting suspension was centrifuged at 4 °C, 8000 rpm for 10 min; the pellets were collected and stored at -80 °C [16–17]. Endophytic bacterial communities of the new roots were also analyzed. Approximately 1 g of new roots was randomly sampled, washed with distilled water, immersed into 10% sodium hypochlorite solution for 3 min, then rinsed repeatedly with phosphate-buffered saline and sterile water. The roots were placed in a cryogenic vial and frozen at -80 °C. The final sterile water that had been used to rinse the roots was used to inoculate culture medium to confirm sterility.

Additionally, soil obtained by shaking the new roots from each pot was used to determine available N, P, and K content. After cleaning out the peat and roots debris, the remaining soil was mixed and collected for the measurement of pH and EC.

Measurement of plant weights and soil properties

The harvested shoot and root samples were rinsed clean, and wiped dry. Their fresh weights were measured separately. Samples were then placed in an oven at 70 °C for 48 h, and dry weights were recorded.

All collected soil samples were air-dried and passed through a 2-mm sieve. For pH measurement, 10.0 g of soil was sub-sampled and suspensions in water at a soil-water ratio of 1:2.5, stirred, allowed to stand for 30 min, and measured using a pH meter (FE22-Standard, Mettler toledo, Swiss). For EC measurement, 5.0 g of sub-sampled soil was suspensions in water at a soil-water ratio of 1:5, stirred, allowed to stand for 30 min, and measured using an EC meter (FE32-Standard, Mettler toledo, Swiss).

Available N was determined from 2.00 g of sub-sampled soil using the alkaline hydrolysis diffusion method, based on the Sichuan Local Standard of China (DB51/T 1875–2014) [18]. Available P was determined from 2.50 g of sub-sampled soil using the sodium hydrogen carbonate solution-Mo-Sb anti spectrophotometric method, adhering with the National Environmental Protection Standard of China (HJ 704–2014) [18]. Available K was determined from 5.00 g of sub-sampled soil using neutral ammonium acetate extraction-flame photometry, according to the Forestry Industry Standard of China (LY/T 1234–2015) [19].

High-throughput sequencing

Bacterial communities in the old root rhizosphere (ORR), new root rhizosphere (NRR), and new root endosphere (NRE) were subjected to high-throughput sequencing. Four biological replicates per treatment were analyzed. Sequencing was performed by the Shanghai Majorbio Technology Company (Shanghai, China).

Briefly, total genomic DNA of the microbial community was extracted using the E.Z.N.A.® soil DNA Kit (Norcross, GA, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA quality and quantity was assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 UV-vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Wilmington, USA). Next, the V3-V4 hypervariable region of the bacterial 16 S rRNA gene was amplified using primers 338 F (5’-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3’) and 806R (5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’). Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) conditions were as follows: pre-denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, 27 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, extension at 72 °C for 30 s, and a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. The reaction system contained 4 µL of 5× Buffer, 2 µL of 2.5 mM dNTPs, 0.8 µL of each primer (5 µM; forward and reverse), 0.4 µL of DNA polymerase, 10 ng of template DNA, and ddH₂O to reach a 20 µL volume.

The amplification products were verified by agarose gel electrophoresis and recovered using the AxyPrep 96DNA Gel Extraction Kit (Axygen, USA). After quantification, sequencing was performed on the Illumina PE300 platform (Illumina, USA). The raw reads of all samples were uploaded as supplementary materials and furthermore deposited in the Sequence Read Archive database of the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) (Accession number: PRJNA1370698).

Data analysis and statistical analysis

Raw sequencing data were quality-filtered using Fastp 0.19.6 software and assembled using Flash 1.2.11 software. Low-quality sequence fragments were removed. Uparse 7.0.1090 software was used to cluster optimized sequences with similarity greater than 97% into operational taxonomic units (OTUs). Finally, RDP Classifier 2.11 software was used to compare the data against the Silva 138 database, with a confidence threshold set at 70%.

Sequences from the NRE bacterial communities annotated as chloroplasts or mitochondria were removed. The sequencing data for the ORR, NRR, and NRE bacterial communities were flattened to the minimum sample size within each dataset. Mothur 1.30.2 software was used to calculate α-diversity indices, including Sobs, Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson indices. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was used to analyze the bacterial communities of the different compartments and treatments at genus level, based on Bray-Curtis distances, using Qiime 1.9.1 software, and R 4.2.1 software was used in visualization.

Network structure analyses were performed using top the 200 genera in ORR and NRR bacterial communities, and the top 100 genera in NRE bacterial communities (per treatment). Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated using R 4.2.1 software, with p-values adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Correlations with |r| > 0.6 and adjusted p < 0.05 were selected [17, 20]. Gephi 0.9.2 software was used to visualize and calculate topological structure characteristics. Correlation analysis based on Spearman was performed using R 4.2.1 software.

Plant fresh weight, dry weight, pH, EC, available N, available P, and available K, were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance using SPSS 16.0 software, and multiple comparisons were performed using Duncan’s test (p < 0.05).

Results

Effects of AM fungi on plant fresh and dry weights in strawberries

Compared with CK, RI treatment significantly increased shoot fresh weight by 32.0% (Fig. 1A). Both FM and RI treatments significantly increased shoot dry weight by 31.8% and 38.6%, respectively (Fig. 1B). RI treatment significantly decreased root dry weight by 25.6%. overall, both FM and RI treatments increased the total fresh and dry weights of the strawberry plants.

Shared and unique taxa of bacterial communities among treatments

The Venn diagram, comparing the ORR, NRR, and NRE bacterial communities at the genus level, showed that a total of 312 genera were shared among all three compartments, and the ORR bacterial communities contained the highest number of bacterial genera (970), whereas the NRE communities contained the lowest (371) (Fig. 2A).

The Venn diagram of ORR bacterial communities across treatments showed that a total of 652 bacterial genera were shared among the CK, FM, and RI treatments, and the number of unique genera in CK, FM, and RI treatments were 69, 56, and 67, respectively (Fig. 2B).

The Venn diagram of NRR bacterial communities across treatments showed that the shared bacterial genera were 409, accounting for 55.27% of all detected genera, and the CK treatment contained more unique bacterial genera (135) than the FM and RI treatments (Fig. 2C).

The Venn diagram of NRE bacterial communities across treatments showed that the shared bacterial genera among the treatments numbered 110, accounting for 35.37% of the total detected genera. The unique bacterial genera for CK, FM, and RI numbered 44, 46, and 34, accounting for 14.15%, 14.79%, and 10.93%, respectively (Fig. 2D).

Compared with CK, the inoculation treatments did not significantly affect the Sobs and Chao1 indices in the ORR bacterial communities (Fig. 3A and B). Both FM and RI treatments significantly decreased the Chao1 index of the NRR bacterial communities (Fig. 3F). Neither treatment significantly affected the Sobs or Chao1 indices of the NRE bacterial communities (Figs. 3I and J).

Effects of AM fungi on the structural compositions of bacterial communities

For the ORR bacterial communities, the cumulative percentage of the top 10 phyla in each treatment was greater than 97.0% (Fig. 5A), and the cumulative percentage of the top 10 genera was greater than 31.8% (Fig. 5B). No significant differences in relative abundance of the top 10 phyla were observed among the treatment groups (Table S1). Similarly, the relative abundance of the top 10 genera showed no significant differences, except that RI treatment significantly increased the relative abundance of Rhodanobacter compared with CK (Table S2).

For the NRR bacterial communities, the cumulative percentages of the top 10 phyla for CK, FM, and RI treatments were 97.7%, 96.8%, and 98.1%, respectively (Fig. 5C). Compared with CK, both FM and RI treatments significantly decreased the relative abundance of Gemmatimonadota (Table S1). The cumulative percentages of the top 10 genera for CK, FM, and RI treatments were 52.2%, 53.4%, and 64.1%, respectively (Fig. 5D). FM treatment significantly increased the relative abundance of Arthrobacter, whereas RI treatment significantly increased that of Lysobacter compared with CK (Table S2).

For the NRE bacterial communities, the cumulative percentage of the top 10 phyla was greater than 99.7% in each treatment (Fig. 5E). Compared with CK, both FM and RI treatments significantly increased the relative abundance of Acidobacteriota (Table S1). The cumulative percentage of the top 10 genera was greater than 70.0% in each treatment (Fig. 5F). RI treatment significantly increased the relative abundance of Allorhizobium-Neorhizobium-Pararhizobium-Rhizobium compared with CK (Table S2).

Network structure analysis of bacterial genera

The top 200 genera from the ORR and NRR bacterial communities, and the top 100 genera from the NRE bacterial communities were selected for correlation network analysis (Fig. 6). Compared with CK, both FM and RI treatments decreased the total link number, density, and average degree (Table 2). In contrast, both inoculated treatments increased the proportion of positive correlations between bacteria and increased modularity.

Correlation analyses of selected genera from the NRR bacterial communities

Four genera, Lysobacter, Arthrobacter, Bauldia and Ellin6067, were selected for correlation analyses against total fresh weight (TFW), total dry weight (TDW), and soil physicochemical properties, including pH, EC, available N (AN), available K (AK), and available P (AP) (Fig. 7). Discussion

AM fungi can influence bacteria recruitment in the plant rhizosphere and the α-diversity of bacterial communities [21–23]. In the present study, three compartments of the strawberry root system were sampled, and the results showed that AM fungi regulated the NRR bacterial communities to a greater extent than they did the ORR and NRE communities. The α-diversity indices of the ORR and NRE bacterial communities were not significantly affected by the two fungi, but those of NRR bacterial communities were done. These findings suggest that AM fungi primarily affect bacterial recruitment and assembly rather than altering the original established communities. This interpretation is further supported by PCoA.

The top 10 phyla and genera with the highest relative abundance in the three compartments were also analyzed. AM fungi altered the structural composition of NRR and NRE communities more strongly than that of ORR communities, indicating a greater regulatory effect on new roots. Two genera that differed significantly between inoculated and control treatments in NRR bacterial communities were Lysobacter and Arthrobacter. Previous studies have showed that most Lysobacter species exhibit prominent antagonistic activity against pathogenic fungi, Gram-positive bacteria, and nematodes, and are often selected as biocontrol agents [24–26]. Therefore, enrichment of this genus is beneficial for enhancing the resistance of strawberry plants to pests and diseases.

Many members of Arthrobacter can drive nitrification and are classified as heterotrophic nitrobacteria [7, 27]. They are often described as plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR), including species such as Arthrobacter pascens, Arthrobacter nitroguajacolicus, Arthrobacter nicotinovorans, Arthrobacter humicola, Arthrobacter woluwensis and Arthrobacter protophormiae [14, 28]. In the present study, correlation analysis also showed that Arthrobacter abundance was significantly correlated with soil available N. It suggests that the high abundance in the genus can accelerate nitrogen cycling and promote nitrification in the strawberry rhizosphere, consistent with the observations made by Diao et al. [7]. Furthermore, R. intraradices significantly increased the relative abundance of the Allorhizobium-Neorhizobium-Pararhizobium-Rhizobium, a known nitrogen-fixing bacterial complex, in the NRE bacterial communities, which may improve the nitrogen-fixation capacity of strawberry roots [29].

Network structure analysis was conducted to explore the correlations among bacterial communities within the three compartments and to infer community stability [30]. Both AM fungi reduced the total link number, density, and average degree, while increasing the proportion of positive correlations within the communities. In networks, positive correlations indicate co-occurrence relationships, whereas negative correlations indicate co-exclusion relationships [30]. These patterns imply that AM fungi enhance interdependent or mutually beneficial interactions among bacteria, consistent with pervious reports [7, 22].

Network modularity reflects the niche differentiation within a community, with high values indicating strong adaptability to environmental changes or loss of restricted species [31]. In the present study, inoculation treatments increased network modularity, which suggests that AM fungi can promote the differentiation of the root bacterial community and enhance its ability to cope with environmental risks. Additionally, the genus with the highest connectivity is considered as a key genus in network structure analysis [9]. Ellin6067 emerged as the key genus in both inoculated treatment groups. It has been reported as a beneficial nitrifying bacterium that can increase soil N availability and influence plant growth and N status [32]. Correlation analysis further showed a significant correlation between Ellin6067 abundance and strawberry total dry weight, indicating a potential role in plant growth promotion.

Conclusion

Both F. mosseae and R. intraradices significantly promoted dry matter accumulation in strawberry shoots and consumed soil available nutrients. Comparative analysis of bacterial communities among the ORR, NRR, and NRE compartments showed that AM fungi affected the bacterial communities associated with new roots more strongly than those associated with old roots. In the NRR communities, both fungi decreased the richness of the bacterial communities, while RI treatment also reduced community evenness.

Structural composition analysis showed that FM treatment significantly increased the relative abundance of Arthrobacter in the NRR bacterial communities, indicating enhanced heterotrophic nitrification capacity, and RI treatment significantly increased the relative abundance of Lysobacter, suggesting improved resistance to pests and diseases. Moreover, RI treatment significantly increased the relative abundance of Allorhizobium-Neorhizobium-Pararhizobium-Rhizobium in the NRE bacterial communities, indicating enhanced nitrogen-fixation potential in strawberry roots.

Network structure analysis showed that AM fungi increased the proportion of positive correlations among bacterial genera, enhancing synergistic interactions. Furthermore, AM fungi increased network modularity, which may improve the ability of bacterial communities to withstand environmental changes. Future research should focus on the synergistic mechanisms between AM fungi and these enriched bacterial taxa in promoting strawberry growth.
