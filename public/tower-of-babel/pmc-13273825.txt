SCOPE and FRAME: Context-Specific Tools for Virtual Care Service Delivery in Rural and Remote Communities
Authors: Joseph P. Deason, Ahmad Rahman, Miranda Ong Smith, Veronica McKinney, Scott J. Adams, Ivar Mendez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo develop and pilot test 2 context-specific digital tools—Standardized Community Overview for Planning and Evaluation (SCOPE) and Facility Review for Assessing Medical Environments (FRAME)—designed to support virtual care service delivery and health care planning in rural and remote communities.Patients and MethodsThis study used a participatory case study design informed by user-centered development principles to design and pilot the SCOPE and FRAME tools. Development involved collaborative workshops with clinicians, health care administrators, community partners, and a software development team. Publicly available data sources were combined with community-verified information to populate community-specific profiles. The tools were piloted in 3 communities in Saskatchewan, Canada—Stanley Mission, La Loche, and Whitecap Dakota Nation—from October 1, 2024, to March 31, 2025. During pilot implementation sessions, participants interacted with the prototype and qualitative feedback regarding functionality, usability, and practical utility was collected.ResultsParticipants reported that the SCOPE and FRAME tools supported health care planning and service coordination by providing centralized access to community-specific demographic, infrastructure, and logistical information. Users indicated that dynamic site management features enabled real-time updates to community profiles, allowing the tools to reflect evolving health care service availability. Data visualization dashboards, including interactive graphs and maps, were reported to support interpretation of health care trends and identification of service gaps. Clinicians also noted that the tools provided useful contextual information to support onboarding of virtual care clinicians and improve understanding of local care pathways.ConclusionFindings from the pilot implementation suggest that context-specific digital tools such as SCOPE and FRAME may support improved coordination, planning, and contextual awareness in virtual care delivery for rural and remote communities.

Rural and remote communities face persistent barriers in accessing essential health care services due to geographic isolation, limited health care infrastructure, and workforce shortages.1, 2, 3 These systemic challenges contribute importantly to health disparities between rural and urban populations. Moreover, for Indigenous peoples in Canada, the impacts of colonization are fundamental determinants of health.4 In northern Saskatchewan, which is almost half of all of Saskatchewan’s geographic area, there are over 70 remote communities with ∼85% of residents identifying as Indigenous, including Cree, Dene, Métis, and Inuit peoples.5,6 Traditional health care models have consistently fallen short of meeting the distinct needs of rural and remote populations.1,7 Virtual health care solutions offer promise in addressing these gaps; however, barriers such as inadequate health care infrastructure, limited workforce capacities, resistance to change, and various factors unique to each community make implementation and care delivery complex and can further amplify inequitable access for remote populations.7, 8, 9

The absence of contextualized, community-specific data severely limits informed clinical and logistical decision-making, leading to inefficiencies and sub-optimal allocation of resources.8,10 Existing resources and data sources typically lack the necessary granularity, real-time responsiveness, and cultural sensitivity needed to effectively inform health care delivery in rural and remote contexts.7,11 Consequently, this information asymmetry yields gaps in our current systems and models, impacting the ability to effectively address the needs of Indigenous peoples and communities.12 For instance, navigating the northern Saskatchewan health care system is complicated by funding structures and travel requirements for Indigenous patients, producing complex referral patterns and community-specific locations for accessing medical imaging and laboratory services. These unique factors present challenges for health care providers coordinating their care and may be exacerbated for remote clinicians who are less aware of the local context.13 Moreover, for Indigenous peoples in Canada, the impacts of colonization are fundamental determinants of health. In addition, systemic racism and a lack of culturally safe care environments have been identified as important barriers affecting both Indigenous patients and health care providers within the Canadian health care system. These factors can contribute to mistrust in health care institutions, reduced care engagement, and increased strain on clinicians working in culturally complex and resource-limited environments.14,15

Rural and remote health care providers report challenges with professional isolation and siloed environments with respect to accessing and coordinating with other health care providers and specialists.13 In rural settings, clinical consultations and referrals between rural family physicians or nurses and urban specialists are a critical component of clinical management and comprehensive patient care.16 During these consultations, factors unique to remote clinicians’ practice environment are often poorly understood by their urban counterparts and hard to convey, especially in emergency situations.16 Improving urban physicians’ understanding of rural challenges such as resource variability and scarcity, transfer logistics and safety, and the social costs of medical travel is key to improving the quality and effectiveness of the consultations and, thus, patient safety and care.16 Broader system-wide dissemination of contextual rural information can considerably improve accessibility, coordination, and provision of patient care.

Partnering with Indigenous peoples, local providers, and knowledge users to develop tools for providing community and cultural context can support successful implementation of new services and solutions at the community-level.10 Indigenous communities must be understood within the specific context of their community’s culture, language, geography, politics and social conditions to understand the individual strengths and needs of their community.17 Broader literature emphasizing the importance of Indigenous community engagement and leadership in health system initiatives reports that meaningful collaboration with Indigenous communities is essential to ensure that health care interventions reflect local priorities, knowledge systems, and cultural contexts.18,19 Furthermore, information and communication technology is regarded as a main facilitator of knowledge sharing that can help health care providers, administrators, and health care organizations to provide the highest quality of patient care.20

In this article, we introduce Standardized Community Overview for Planning and Evaluation (SCOPE) and Facility Review for Assessing Medical Environments (FRAME)—interactive data-driven information and communication technology tools specifically designed to capture the unique characteristics and data of rural and remote communities. Through the integration of demographic, geographic, health care infrastructure, experiential knowledge, and culturally relevant data, these tools are a dynamic support to the implementation, management, and delivery of health care services in remote communities. SCOPE and FRAME were developed by the Virtual Health Hub (VHH), a provincially and federally funded program that focuses on improving access to health care for Indigenous, rural, and remote communities by leveraging technology and co-developing solutions through community partnerships.21 The VHH has over a decade of experience working with rural, remote, and Indigenous communities in Saskatchewan to implement virtual care solutions, including remote presence robotics for primary care and pediatric short-term care, telerobotic ultrasound imaging services, and other advanced virtual care modalities tailored to community-specific needs.22, 23, 24, 25, 26

SCOPE and FRAME are designed to work in sequence and synergy with the VHH’s battery of standardized implementation tools, including the Comprehensive Evaluation Tool (CET) to assess community capacity and readiness for virtual care implementation,27 and the Technology Selection Tool to inform appropriate virtual care technology deployments based on the CET’s conclusions.28 SCOPE and FRAME are designed to be applied during the onboarding of new communities as they undergo CET assessments that include a standardized clinical needs assessment, a technology readiness assessment, and a human resources and workflow readiness assessment.

This paper presents the development and pilot implementation of SCOPE and FRAME in 3 Saskatchewan locations. Specifically, we explore how these tools enhance the capacity of health care professionals and administrators to implement and deliver contextually responsive virtual care. We examine their impact on operational and clinical decision-making, discuss key lessons learned, and highlight the need for further work in understanding rural and remote communities in their specific contexts.

Patients And Methods

Study Design

This study employed a participatory case study design informed by user-centered design principles to guide the development and pilot implementation of the SCOPE and FRAME tools. The study incorporated elements of user-centered design and participatory research, allowing health care professionals, VHH team members, and community partners to contribute directly to the design and refinement of the tools. This approach was selected to ensure that community-specific experiential knowledge and operational realities were directly incorporated into the development of the tools. Engaging directly with community partners allowed the research team to capture locally grounded insights that would otherwise be unavailable.

A participatory project team recruitment approach was used, wherein VHH clinicians and administrators were invited to participate in a series of workshops. The team consisted of 5 physicians—3 primary care physicians, 1 vascular surgeon, and 1 pediatrician—alongside 2 health care administrators experienced in operationalizing virtual health care services in remote settings. Twelve workshop sessions, held from October 2024, to March 2025, supported the discovery and requirements-identification phase of tool development. Nine of the workshops involved members of the VHH’s clinician and administrator project team, and 3 involved engagements with 3 community partners—including a community health director, nursing manager, and local clinical staff from the communities of Stanley Mission and Pelican Narrows. These workshops included structured discussions and collaborative review of prototype tool components to identify key information needs and define the data elements required for SCOPE and FRAME.

Three communities—Stanley Mission, La Loche, and Whitecap Dakota Nation—were selected to pilot the tools and assess their functionality, usability, and contextual relevance. These communities represent diverse geographic, demographic, and health care service contexts within Saskatchewan. Each community has unique health care infrastructure, referral pathways, and logistical considerations that influence the delivery of care. The selected communities also represent locations where the VHH has established prior relationships through previous virtual care initiatives. Tool Development and User-Centered Design

SCOPE and FRAME were developed using a user-centered design approach that emphasized iterative refinement and inclusive partner engagement.29 User-centered design focuses on meeting the requirements, preferences, and feedback of various users. Actively engaging end users in the development process improves the usability of systems and ensures long-term viability.29 The 5 VHH clinicians, 2 health care administrators, and 3 community partners were consulted early in the design process to identify key information needs, including accessibility, cultural responsiveness, real-time data integration, and relevance to clinical and administrative workflows. Through this design and requirement gathering process, feedback regarding the structure, usability, and relevance of the SCOPE and FRAME profiles was captured directly during discussions and documented in real-time by the research team. This enabled rapid incorporation of user input into the development process, allowing the software development team to iteratively refine interface components, data fields, and profile structures in response to participant feedback. Qualitative feedback and insights were gathered during workshop sessions and communicated directly to the software development team and incorporated into successive prototype refinements. Multiple cycles of iterative prototype improvement occurred throughout the development process, leading to 6 major revisions and 15 minor revisions. Successive iterations of the prototype focused on improving usability, interface design, and the organization of profile data fields. Each of the 12 workshop sessions involving clinicians, administrators, and community partners informed refinements to navigation, data source requirements, labeling, and visual layout to enhance clarity and ensure the tool aligned with real-world health care coordination workflows.

The initial version of the SCOPE and FRAME tools consisted of a high-fidelity functional interactive prototype that allowed users to view and populate profile fields during consultation sessions. This prototype was shared with participants during workshop discussions and community consultations and served as a working interface that supported real-time discussion and iterative refinement of the tool design. Because the prototype was functional, community-specific information could be entered directly into the profiles during these sessions, allowing participants to see how their local context would be represented within the tool.

Data Collection and Verification

Data collection followed a structured process consisting of 3 stages: (1) identification and compilation of publicly available information sources, (2) organization of these data into preliminary SCOPE and FRAME profile fields, and (3) verification and contextual refinement through consultation with community health care professionals and administrators. Sources for the publicly available information included government databases such as Statistics Canada, health authority reports, First Nation and tribal council websites, Google Maps, and publicly available community Facebook groups. Key information was manually compiled and documented with corresponding sources. Once the collected data was organized into preliminary SCOPE and FRAME profile fields, it was then confirmed and supplemented through consultation with local community partners from each pilot community. In most cases, this verification occurred through a 60-minute real-time group video conference session involving 1 to 3 community partners, depending on participant availability. Common roles for these individuals within their community health systems were community health directors or nurse managers, who were particularly knowledgeable about local health care operations and service pathways. Screen sharing during these video sessions allowed the research team to review the SCOPE and FRAME profiles together with community partners, enabling real-time data entry, correction, and discussion (Figure 1). Through these interactions, participants reviewed and provided feedback on profile content, confirmed the accuracy of community-specific information, and provided additional contextual details regarding local health care services, referral pathways, transportation logistics, and other operational considerations.30 Rather than using a formal interview guide, the SCOPE and FRAME prototypes served as the structured format for discussion. Piloting SCOPE and FRAME

The tool was trialed in collaboration with local community partners, and their feedback emphasized the need for capturing operational knowledge, often held informally by staff, that is not found in existing documentation systems.31 Initial lists of services and contact details were compiled from online resources and then verified and expanded on through conversations with local health care providers. Responses highlighted resource constraints and the need for clinicians working in urban settings to understand local service limitations.32 FRAME complements the broader, community-level data of SCOPE and focuses on supporting continuity of care, interprofessional collaboration, and efficient care navigation.

The pilot evaluation used a formative usability assessment approach to examine how the SCOPE and FRAME tools functioned in practice. Evaluation focused on 3 domains: (1) tool functionality, defined as the ability of the platform to support the entry, modification, and retrieval of community-level data; (2) visualization and usability, including clarity of the interface, navigation, and interpretability of the data visualizations; and (3) practical utility for health care planning and coordination within rural and remote contexts.

During the pilot phase, one pilot implementation session with each of the 5 VHH clinicians were held. During these in-person sessions, participants interacted directly with the prototype while the research team observed how users navigated the system, accessed information, and interpreted dashboard outputs. Qualitative feedback from clinicians was collected during these sessions and documented by the research team. Observations and participant feedback were used to identify issues, confirm the relevance of the data fields, and assess whether the tools supported the intended operational and clinical decision-making tasks. Challenges related to usability, navigation, or completeness of information were documented by the research team’s software developer lead and research coordinator. These field notes were reviewed by the research team’s leads to identify key issues and guide subsequent prototype refinements and improvements. For example, key areas for refinement included simplifying navigation pathways, improving data filtering options, and allowing for greater customization of community-specific data fields. In response, the development team enhanced dashboard clarity, introduced flexible note-taking features, and improved the searchability of content within the tools.

Overview of Tools

SCOPE

Standardized Community Overview for Planning and Evaluation (SCOPE) provides a broad overview of each community, including demographics, geography, available health care services, transportation options, disease prevalence, and social infrastructure. The tool was designed as a dynamic tool, allowing for continuous updates and adaptation through community feedback to support clinical decision-making and enhance cultural competence (Figure 2).Figure 2User interface for entering data in Standardized Community Overview for Planning and Evaluation (SCOPE).

Facility Review for Assessing Medical Environments (FRAME) provides real-time clinical information, contextual to the facility that is present or nearby in the community, including details about accessing diagnostic services, referral pathways, staffing resources, a directory of community contacts, and pharmacy access (Figure 3). Evaluation of Tools

Evaluation of Tool Functionality

During pilot implementation sessions, clinicians reported that the SCOPE and FRAME tools supported health care planning and service coordination tasks within the participating communities. Clinicians noted that the dynamic site management features allowed users to add, edit, and update community-level data in real-time, enabling the profiles to remain current and responsive to changes in service availability.

Clinicians also highlighted the value of the platform’s modular design and integrated data feeds, which allowed users to track changes in service availability, infrastructure developments, and staffing patterns. These capabilities were considered particularly useful in rural and remote contexts where health care access may change due to seasonal accessibility challenges, workforce turnover, or shifting resource availability.

Evaluation of Data Visualization and Usability

During pilot implementation sessions, clinicians reported that the data visualization dashboards supported interpretation of complex health care and demographic information at both the community and provincial levels. Interactive visualizations, including graphs, maps, and tables, allowed users to quickly identify service gaps, population health trends, and logistical considerations relevant to care planning.

Clinicians also noted that the visual presentation of the data improved the efficiency of operational planning by reducing the effort required to interpret large volumes of information. Feedback gathered during the pilot sessions also informed iterative refinements to the user interface, including improvements to navigation pathways and dashboard layout to enhance clarity and ease of use.

Evaluation of Practical Utility for Health Care Planning

Observations from the pilot implementation sessions suggested that SCOPE and FRAME provided practical utility for health care planning and coordination within the participating communities. Clinicians reported using the tools to support clinical scheduling, improve communication among health care teams, and identify service and infrastructure gaps.

Participants also indicated that SCOPE supported onboarding and orientation for new virtual clinicians by providing contextual information about local demographics, health care infrastructure, cultural considerations, and logistical factors affecting care delivery. This contextual knowledge helped clinicians better understand the local health care environment and the operational realities of delivering care in remote communities.

FRAME was reported to support care coordination by providing centralized access to referral contacts, transportation considerations, and current service availability. Participants indicated that this information helped improve understanding of patient care pathways and logistical requirements for accessing services outside the community.

Across the pilot communities, a common theme that emerged during the development of the SCOPE and FRAME profiles was the importance of locally held operational knowledge. Community partners frequently highlighted that many aspects of health care delivery, such as referral pathways for diagnostic services or transportation logistics for patients requiring specialized care, were not consistently documented in publicly available sources. Instead, this information was often maintained informally by local health care staff who were closely familiar with community-specific processes. As a result, direct engagement with community representatives was essential to ensure that the profiles accurately reflected real-world care coordination pathways and operational realities.

Challenges and Solutions

Several challenges emerged during deployment. Data inconsistencies across communities, including outdated, varying, or incomplete publicly available information, required extensive manual verification and direct engagement with community leaders and health care providers.

Variation in digital literacy among users necessitated customized onboarding sessions and technical support for effective utilization of the tools. Additionally, users requested flexible data entry fields to accommodate unique community characteristics such as seasonal transportation access issues or important cultural elements.

These challenges were addressed through iterative technical updates and sustained engagement with end users. The pilot phase highlighted the importance of maintaining an open feedback loop with dedicated community engagement processes to keep data current, emphasizing modularity in design, and prioritizing cultural responsiveness to ensure the tools’ long-term utility and scalability.

Discussion

Findings from the implementation pilot suggest that SCOPE and FRAME may support enhanced health care planning and virtual service delivery in rural and remote communities. Traditional health care planning tools often rely on static, infrequently updated data sources that fail to capture the evolving realities of rural and remote communities. In rural and remote areas, certain services may be unavailable locally, and system changes are needed so urban providers can easily consider the lack of local services when making recommendations and determine how to support local care providers working in these areas.33 In Canada, physician groups have identified the need to develop specific resources and infrastructure within local and regional health authorities to improve access for rural communities as a specific action to help recruit and retain rural family physicians.34

The development of structured communication tools is a targeted way to support the consultation challenges specific to rural health care systems.17 Further, when providing virtual care, remote health care providers need access to a centralized, user-friendly knowledge base that outlines the health system resources available in the patient’s community.35 SCOPE and FRAME are aimed toward meeting these requirements as a solution that reduces challenges experienced by rural health care providers, enables more effective and contextually appropriate consultations, and overall, promotes patient-centered care for rural and remote communities.

Context-specific digital tools that are Indigenous-focused and rural-based are necessary to improve health care for rural, remote, and northern communities.30 The integration of demographic, geographic, health care infrastructure, and community data into a single, dynamic platform allows for a more nuanced understanding of community needs and can be utilized for all communities, including non-Indigenous. Future iterations can also include integrating evaluation tools related to the implementation and deployment of virtual care technologies in communities, which further utilizes SCOPE and FRAME as information repositories.27

Investment in these digital platforms is crucial as they help bridge health care accessibility gaps.36 However, the effectiveness of the tools is highly dependent on the availability, accuracy, and timeliness of data sources. Despite efforts at manual verification, inconsistencies in publicly available data remain a relevant challenge, requiring considerable resource investment to address. Ongoing system maintenance, user support, and data updates are required to sustain the tools’ effectiveness over time. Jurisdictions with limited technical infrastructure or administrative capacity may face challenges in scaling and maintaining such tools without dedicated resources and support.

Observations from the pilot implementation sessions and feedback from participating clinicians indicated that tools such as SCOPE and FRAME may support more agile and contextually informed health care planning by providing access to real-time updates and community-specific information. Participants reported that the centralized profiles helped improve understanding of local health care infrastructure, referral pathways, and logistical considerations, which in turn supported more coordinated care planning and service delivery. Clinicians also noted that the tools were useful for orienting virtual providers to community-specific contexts and operational realities. These observations suggest that platforms such as SCOPE and FRAME may contribute to improved continuity of care, more efficient referral coordination, and stronger operational planning when implementing services such as virtual care within existing health care systems.

Broader use of the tools could include integration with additional sectors such as education, transportation, and housing. There are important opportunities for digital tools to support holistic approaches to health and well-being and to improve health care accessibility for rural and remote communities. The adoption of context-specific digital tools like SCOPE and FRAME has the potential to become a foundational step in educating health care providers and decision-makers about the multifactorial and historical contexts affecting the health of the communities they serve.

The findings of this study also highlight the importance of relational and team-based approaches to virtual care delivery in rural and remote contexts. Previous literature has emphasized that effective telehealth services depend not only on technology but also on strong professional relationships, trust, and clear communication between remote clinicians and local care teams.37,38 In resource-limited settings, decision-making often relies on contextual knowledge held by local providers, making collaborative interactions between virtual consultants and community-based clinicians essential for safe and effective care delivery. These relational dynamics are particularly important when working with Indigenous communities, where culturally safe care requires an understanding of local context, community priorities, and historical health care inequities. Approaches that prioritize collaboration, respect, and contextual awareness align with principles of cultural safety in health care delivery.39 The SCOPE and FRAME tools aim to support these relational aspects of care by making community-specific information more visible to external clinicians and facilitating more informed and culturally responsive clinical decision-making.

Our experience suggests that community-informed, data-driven tools can offer improvements in health care for patients from rural and remote communities and can enhance the effectiveness of virtual health care delivery. However, the long-term impact of these tools on health care outcomes, access, and system efficiency has yet to be formally evaluated. Although the present pilot primarily focused on feasibility and early usability insights obtained through qualitative feedback from clinicians and community partners involved in the development process, future work will include more formal evaluation of the tools, including quantitative assessments of usability, adoption, and impact on clinical workflows and care coordination. Future research should incorporate outcome-based metrics, such as improvements in care access, patient satisfaction, continuity of care, and health equity measures. Inn addition, as these tools are implemented across communities, teams will need to ensure that implementation is built on respect, reciprocity, and mutual understanding, prioritizing the needs and vision of Indigenous communities.36

A notable strength of this study lies in the participatory approach used to develop the SCOPE and FRAME tools. Clinicians, administrators, and community partners were involved throughout the development process, allowing individuals with direct experience in rural and remote health care delivery to inform the design. This collaborative engagement helped ensure that the tools incorporated locally relevant information and reflected the practical realities of health care coordination within the participating communities. However, the study also has several limitations. The tools were piloted in a limited number of communities in Saskatchewan, which may constrain the generalizability of findings to other jurisdictions with different health care structures or community contexts. In addition, while qualitative insights from clinicians and community partners informed the development process and early assessment, formal quantitative evaluation of the tools’ impact on clinical workflows, care coordination, or patient outcomes was beyond the scope of this initial pilot. Future work will focus on expanding the implementation of the tools across a broader range of communities and conducting formal evaluations of their impact on health care planning, service delivery, and care coordination in rural and remote settings.

Conclusion

SCOPE and FRAME represent tools toward transforming how virtual care is designed, implemented, and sustained in rural and remote communities. The provision of structured, evidence-informed tools that center local context and operational practicality offer a scalable pathway to more equitable service delivery. Early implementation has reported their potential to streamline decision-making, strengthen care coordination, and ensure that virtual care deployments are responsive to real community needs. As health care systems continue to expand digital and remote service models, SCOPE and FRAME provide a foundation on which future innovations can be integrated, adapted, and evaluated. Their continued refinement and application hold promise for advancing high-quality, culturally grounded, and sustainable virtual care to patients regardless of their geographical location. It is an ethical imperative to harness the power of technology to improve health care access to the most underserved and marginalized populations.
