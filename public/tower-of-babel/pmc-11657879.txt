The frequency of serious bacterial infection in febrile infants less than 90 days infected with SARS-CoV-2 and other respiratory viral pathogens
Authors: Hande Yigit, Emel Ulusoy, Songul Tomar Guneysu, Ozlem Colak, Ayse Gultekingil, Oksan Derinoz Guleryuz, Durgul Yilmaz, Murat Duman, Ozlem Teksam
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe primary aim of this study was to compare the groups, SARS-CoV-2-positive and -negative patients, in terms of the frequency of SBI. The SARS-CoV-2-positive group was compared with the other-RVPs-positive group as a secondary evaluation in terms of serious bacterial infection (SBI).MethodsThis retrospective multicenter cohort study was conducted at four university hospitals from March 2020 to December 2021. Patients under 90 days old, with a measured fever of ≥ 38 °C, who were tested for SARS-CoV-2 and had blood and urine cultures taken, were included in the study. The patient groups who tested positive and negative for SARS-CoV-2 were compared regarding clinical characteristics, laboratory data, and the frequency of serious bacterial infections. As a secondary analysis, among patients tested for other respiratory viruses, the SARS-CoV-2-positive group and other respiratory virus-positive groups were compared in terms of the frequency of SBI.ResultsA total of 208 patients met the inclusion criteria. Two distinct comparisons were conducted among the patients included in the study: the first between the SARS-CoV-2-positive and SARS-CoV-2-negative patient groups, and the second between the SARS-CoV-2-positive and other RVPs-positive patient groups. In the first comparison, fifty-five patients were SARS-CoV-2-positive and 153 were SARS-CoV-2-negative. The rate of SBI in the SARS-CoV-2-positive patients was 16.4%, whereas that in the SARS-CoV-2-negative patients was 47.1%. The laboratory parameters for infection suspicion were significantly lower in the SARS-CoV-2-positive group than the SARS-CoV-2-negative group. In the second comparison, 90 patients who were tested for both SARS-CoV-2 and other RVPs were evaluated. The mean WBC, ANC and CRP levels were significantly lower in the SARS-CoV-2-positive group than the other RVPs-positive group, however, there was no significant difference in the mean ALC, NLR and PCT levels and the frequency of SBI between the SARS-CoV-2-positive and other RVPs-positive patient groups.ConclusionsThese results suggest that febrile infants younger than 90 days with SARS-CoV-2 have lower rates of SBI than patients without SARS-CoV-2. These data are consistent with previous studies describing lower risks of SBI in febrile infants with RVPs.Trial registrationNot applicable.Keywords: Fever, COVID-19, SARS-CoV-2, Infant, Serious bacterial infection, Respiratory viral pathogens

Background

Fever is a nonspecific complaint that is one of the most common causes of emergency presentations in young infants. Approximately 2% of full-term newborns seek hospital care for fever within the first 90 days of their lives [1, 2]. Although it is mostly caused by a benign viral disease, it can also be the initial presentation of a serious bacterial infection (SBI). Serious bacterial infections might be associated with high morbidity and mortality rates in this age group.

The prevalence of SBI in this age group varies from 6 to 13% according to different studies [3–6]. SBI can also accompany patients with viral infections [7, 8]. When studies on the frequency of SBI in infants with viral infection were evaluated, the frequency of SBI was between 3.7% and 13% in patients with a viral infection and between 12.3% and 24% in patients without a viral infection [7, 9, 10]. The rate of bacteremia and urinary tract infection (UTI) is significantly lower in febrile infants with respiratory viral infections [7, 8, 11, 12].

The SARS-CoV-2 agent became a potential cause of fever in infants presenting with fever during the COVID-19 pandemic. However, whether the risk of SBI is lower in infants with SARS-CoV-2 is still being investigated. In a recent study, the SBI rate in the SARS-CoV-2-positive group was lower than that in the SARS-CoV-2-negative group [13].

In the prepandemic period, infants with a proven viral infection are known to have a significantly lower risk of SBI. There are also some studies comparing viral infections in the prepandemic period and COVID-19 disease in the pandemic period in terms of SBI. In these studies, the proportion of concomitant SBIs among young infants with SARS-CoV-2 infection was similar to the proportion of infections caused by bacterial coinfection with other respiratory viruses before the pandemic [9, 14]. Several studies have shown that the rate of SBI is lower in SARS-CoV-2-positive patients than in SARS-CoV-2-negative patients, as observed in infants infected with other RVPs [9, 15]. The aim of this study is, firstly, to compare the group of SARS-CoV-2-positive infants with the group of SARS-CoV-2-negative infants, and secondly, to compare the group of SARS-CoV-2-positive infants with the group of infants positive for other RVPs in terms of SBI.

Methods

This was a retrospective multicenter cohort study which conducted at four tertiary university hospitals in Turkiye between 11 March 2020 and 31 December 2021.

The electronic medical records of infants younger than 90 days who presented with fever were evaluated. Infants with a documented temperature of 38 °C or higher (at home or in the pediatric emergency department) and underwent nasopharyngeal real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT–PCR) test for SARS-CoV-2 were reviewed for eligibility. The diagnosis for all patients was established by detecting SARS-CoV-2 RNA in nasopharyngeal swabs using a commercial RT-PCR test (The Bio-Speedy Direct RT-qPCR SARS-CoV-2 Nucleic Acid Detection Kit, Bioeksen, Turkey) performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Patients with comorbidities/chronic diseases (congenital heart disease, chronic lung disease, any condition that can cause infection, such as a tracheostomy, gastrostomy, or hydrocephalus with ventriculoperitoneal shunts, etc.) were excluded. Patients who were screened for sepsis along with urine culture and blood culture were included in the study. Since cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis was decided based on the patient’s clinical condition and laboratory results, not every patient had CSF sampling.

The data were obtained from electronic medical records, and medical charts were recorded in a database sent to each participating center. The participants from each center provided their center data, and the data were combined by a principal investigator (HY).

The patients’ age, sex, maximum temperature, appearance (classified as well-appearing or ill-appearing), whether chest radiography, urine culture, blood culture and CSF collection, laboratory results, hospital admission, length of hospital stay and parenteral antibiotic treatment were recorded. Ill-appearing status was assessed based on the patient’s appearance and was defined as the presence of at least one of the following findings: weak cry, poor suck, grunting, limpness, apnea, cyanosis, or unresponsiveness [16]. The results of the patients who were tested for other RVPs via RT–PCR were also recorded. Since RT-PCR tests for other RVPs were occasionally available in the participating centers, a limited number of patients could be tested.

Patients were classified according to their SARS-CoV-2 status (positive or negative). Also, patients who underwent RT-PCR tests for both SARS-CoV-2 and other RVPs were divided into two groups: SARS-CoV-2-positive and other RVPs-positive.

Serious bacterial infection was defined as the isolation of a pathogenic bacteria from urine, blood or cerebrospinal fluid. In febrile infants, UTI was initially considered if pyuria (≥ 5 WBCs/hpf) and/or positive leukocyte esterase or nitrites were detected in urine samples obtained via catheterization or suprapubic aspiration and the UTI treatment was started. Among these patients, a UTI diagnosis was confirmed by the growth of ≥ 50,000 CFU/mL of urine samples collected via catheterization or a single pathogen in urine samples obtained via suprapubic aspiration [17]. For this study, among the patients suspected of having a UTI, those with confirmed bacterial growth in urine culture were defined as having a UTI. Bacteremia and bacterial meningitis were considered as invasive bacterial infection (IBI) (2).

Study data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). p < 0.05 indicated statistical significance. Categorical variables are expressed as numbers (n) and percentages (%), and continuous variables are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation or median and interquartile range (IQR). The chi-square test was used to analyze categorical data. Independent variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test.

Results

During the study period, 435 infants younger than 90 days with no comorbidity or chronic disease, presented with fever. The nasopharyngeal RT–PCR test for SARS-CoV-2 was performed to 366 patients. Sixty-nine patients who did not have a nasopharyngeal RT–PCR test were excluded. Also, for sepsis screening, 158 patients who did not have blood or urine cultures taken were excluded. Among the remining 208 patients, nasopharyngeal RT–PCR tests for both SARS-CoV-2 and other RVPs were performed for 90 patients.

A total of 208 patients were included in the study; 123/208 (59.1%) were male, and the mean age was 42 ± 3 days. Fifty-five infants were SARS-CoV-2-positive, and 153 were SARS-CoV-2-negative. The total WBC, ANC, ALC, NLR, CRP, and PCT were significantly lower in the SARS-CoV-2-positive group than in the SARS-CoV-2-negative group (p < 0.001, p < 0.001, p = 0.012, p = 0.013, p < 0.001 and p = 0.009, respectively).

Chest radiographs were obtained less often in the SARS-CoV-2-positive patient group (p = 0.001). There was no difference between the two groups in the frequency of CSF sampling (p = 0.31). Thirty infants were ill-appearing, 4 were SARS-CoV-2-positive, 26 were SARS-CoV-2-negative. Among the ill-appearing infants, CSF sampling was performed on all 4 SARS-CoV-2-positive and 19 of 26 SARS-CoV-2-negative infants.

The rates of hospital admission and parenteral antibiotic treatment were significantly lower in the SARS-CoV-2-positive patient group (both p < 0.001). There was no significant difference in the length of hospital stay in both groups (p = 0.12).

Table 2 presents the SBI rates for both groups, overall and divided by age groups. The frequency of SBI in the SARS-CoV-2-positive group was 16.4% (9 patients), and in the SARS-CoV-2-negative group was 47.1% (72 patients) (p < 0.001). In the SARS-CoV-2-positive group there were 7 UTI caused by by Klebsiella pneumoniae (n = 5), Escherichia coli (n = 1) and Enterococcus faecalis (n = 1); one patient had Micrococcus luteus bacteremia, and one had meningitis caused by Escherichia coli. We believe that isolating Micrococcus luteus from blood culture might have been a contaminant, but this patient’s treatment was completed as bacteremia. The rates of UTI, bacteremia, SBI and IBI were significantly lower in the SARS-CoV-2-positive group (p = 0.002, p = 0.029, p < 0.001 and p = 0.046, respectively). Among the ill-appearing 30 infants, rates of SBI were 2/4 (50%) in the SARS-CoV-2-positive group (one UTI and one meningitis) and 16/26 (61.5%) in the SARS-CoV-2-negative group (6 UTI, 8 bacteremia, 3 meningitis).

Discussion

Our results showed that febrile infants younger than 90 days with SARS-CoV-2 have lower rates of SBI than SARS-CoV-2-negative controls. Studies evaluating febrile infants have shown that many young febrile infants present with viral or presumed viral infections, and similar to the findings of our study, the risk of SBI was lower in febrile infants with documented viral infections [7, 11, 12]. Similarly, the results of the studies evaluating the frequency of SBI in febrile infants infected with SARS-CoV-2 showed that SBI rates are lower in patients with SARS-CoV-2 [9, 18].

A multicenter prospective study evaluating the risk of SBI in 60-day-old and younger infants with and without documented RSV infections revealed UTI rates of 7.0% and 12.5%, bacteremia rates of 1.1% and 2.3% in RSV-positive and RSV-negative infants, respectively [11]. Payson et al. compared SARS-CoV-2-positive infants younger than 90 days with age- and sex-matched SARS-CoV-2-negative controls in terms of SBI. The rate of SBI in the SARS-CoV-2-positive infants was 8%, whereas it was 34% in the control group [9]. In a recent study by Hernandez-Bou et al., the rate of SBI was lower in the SARS-CoV-2-positive group than in the SARS-CoV-2-negative group (5.9% vs. 22.9%) [13]. In our study, similar to the findings of these studies, the SBI rate was 9.7% in the SARS-CoV-2-positive group and 34.3% in the SARS-CoV-2-negative group. Invasive bacterial infection was detected in 2 of 124 (1.6%) SARS-CoV-2-positive patients (1 with bacteremia caused by Micrococcus luteus and 1 with meningitis caused by E. coli). According to a very recently published systematic review and meta-analysis evaluating the IBI in SARS-CoV-2-positive infants younger than 90 days, the pooled prevalence of IBI was 0.14% (95% CI, 0.02–0.27%) [19].

In the present study, the WBC, ANC, ALS, NLR, CRP and PCT values were significantly lower in the SARS-CoV-2-positive group than in the SARS-CoV-2-negative group, similar to the findings in the literature. Previous studies have shown that inflammatory marker levels are lower in patients with viral infections than in patients without respiratory tract viral pathogens [8, 20]. In a study comparing febrile infants younger than 57 days during the COVID-19 pandemic period and pre-pandemic period, 20 infants in the SARS-CoV-2-positive group were found to have lower WBC, neutrophil, and lymphocyte counts than 81 SARS-CoV-2-negative infants [18]. Paret et al. also showed that WBC, ANC, and CRP values in the SARS-CoV-2-positive group were significantly lower than those in the SARS-CoV-2-negative group [15].

With the widespread use of rapid viral testing, it seems that some clinicians are less likely to perform comprehensive evaluations for SBI in infants with confirmed viral infections [7, 8, 11, 12, 21]. In the literature, there are a few studies comparing the frequency of laboratory tests performed for the evaluation of SBI in SARS-CoV-2-positive and SARS-CoV-2-negative infants [9, 15]. Payson et al. showed that chest X-rays were taken significantly more often in the SARS-CoV-2-positive group than in the SARS-CoV-2-negative group. On the other hand, there were no differences in the frequency of urine culture, blood culture or CSF sampling [9]. Similarly, Paret et al. reported no differences between the groups of SARS-CoV-2-positive and -negative patients in terms of the frequency of urine culture, blood culture, CSF sampling, or chest X-ray [15]. Contrary to these studies, in our results, chest radiography, blood culture, urine culture and CSF sampling were significantly lower in the SARS-CoV-2-positive patient group. In the pediatric emergency department, observing the course of a patient’s fever could be an approach, while evaluating for SBI, especially in well-appearing infants. In the evaluation of febrile infants, the role of viral testing for SARS-CoV-2 and other RVPs remains unclear. During the COVID-19 pandemic, RT-PCR tests for SARS-CoV-2 in our study were resulted during the observation period. Based on this, it could be considered that rapid viral RT-PCR tests may be added to febrile infant approach algorithms.

In the COVID-19 pandemic era, restriction measures including school closures, stay-at-home orders and decreases in interpersonal contact, contributed to a lower frequency of other RVPs than did SARS-CoV-2 [22, 23]. In the study of Leibowitz et al. comparing the rate of SBI in infants in the pandemic period (2020) and previous years (2018–2019), there were significantly fewer infants with a positive respiratory viral panel in 2020 (15%) than in 2018–2019 (53%) [20]. According to our results, during the pandemic, the frequencies of SARS-CoV-2-positive and other RVPs-positive patients were similar (25.7% and 25%, respectively), and there were no patients who were both SARS-CoV-2-positive and other RVPs-positive. According to the study of Yildiz et al., other viral pathogens in children were more common in the SARS-CoV-2-negative group than the SARS-CoV-2-positive group (13.2% & 4.7%) [24]. In the literature, there are many studies showing that the likelihood of SBI is lower in patients with a viral respiratory pathogen [8, 11, 25, 26]. In our study, a comparison of the SARS-CoV-2-positive, other RVPs-positive and both -negative groups revealed that the SBI rate was significantly greater in the both-negative group.

The strength of this study is that it was a multicenter study and therefore included a large number of patients.

Limitations

However, there are several limitations. This study is retrospective and thus limited by the diagnostic testing performed at the time the infant was evaluated. Although the general recommendation in clinical practice is to test all febrile infants via urinary tests, urine culture and blood culture, some febrile infants have not undergone these laboratory investigations. Therefore, infants with incomplete evaluation were not included in this study.

The number of patients tested for other RVPs was low, as testing for other RVPs was occasionally available in limited numbers at participating centers.

Another limitation is that the study population comprises patients admitted during the pandemic. The applicability of the results to the current period when the pandemic ends is limited.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study demonstrated that febrile infants younger than 90 days with SARS-CoV-2 have lower rates of SBI than SARS-CoV-2-negative controls. But there was no difference between SARS-CoV-2-positive and other RVPs-positive infants in terms of SBI. These data are consistent with previous studies describing lower risks of SBI in febrile infants with concomitant viral respiratory tract infections. Based on these results, while evaluating febrile infants younger than 90 days in the pediatric emergency department, the nasopharyngeal RT–PCR tests for SARS-CoV-2 or other RVPs may be added to febrile infant approach algorithms.

Therefore, the use of reliable point-of-care tests is important in pediatric emergency services. Although the role of viral diagnostic studies in the management of febrile infants is unclear, these results can be confirmed by larger prospective multicenter studies. Therefore, the need for further testing and hospitalization may be reduced in febrile infants with positive RVP, including those with SARS-CoV-2.
