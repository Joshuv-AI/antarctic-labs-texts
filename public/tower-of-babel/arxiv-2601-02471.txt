Gaussian time-translation covariant operations: structure, implementation, and thermodynamics
Xueyuan Hu, Lea Lautenbacher, Giovanni Spaventa, Martin B. Plenio, Nelly H.Y. Ng, Jeongrak Son

Abstract
Time-translation symmetry strongly constrains physical dynamics, yet systematic characterization for continuous-variable systems lags behind its discrete-variable counterpart. We close this gap by providing a rigorous classification of Gaussian quantum operations that are covariant under time translations, termed Gaussian covariant operations. We show that several key results known for discrete-variable covariant operations break down in the Gaussian optical setting: discrepancies arise in physical and thermodynamic implementation, in the extensivity of asymmetry, and in catalytic advantages. Our results provide comprehensive mathematical and operational toolkits for Gaussian covariant operations, including a peculiar pair of asymmetry measures that are completely non‑extensive. Our findings also reveal surprising consequences of the interplay among symmetry, Gaussianity, and thermodynamic constraints, suggesting that real-world scenarios with multiple constraints have a rich structure not accessible from examining individual constraints separately.



Introduction

Time flows whether we have a clock or not.
Without a clock, however, we cannot keep track of temporal information: how much time has passed since a state is prepared, when an operation is applied, etc.
Consider two scenarios in which an identical quantum channel is applied to a system.
In the first scenario, the channel is applied immediately after state preparation; in the second, the channel is applied after some time has passed.
To observers without a clock, these two scenarios must look identical.
This means we only have access to information that is invariant whether a time-translation is applied before or after the channel, i.e. information symmetric under time translations.
Mathematically, this corresponds to time-averaged versions of channels, which form the class of phase-covariant (or
\mathrm{U}(1)
-covariant) operations
Bartlett et al. 2007
.

To access the full information, timekeeping devices such as clocks are needed.
Then the operations before the time-averaging, which are not covariant in general, can be considered.
Nevertheless, when timekeeping devices that provide the required precision are treated as another quantum system, the operation on this extended system becomes covariant.
Hence, any physically realizable process can be embedded in a globally covariant operation.
Furthermore, timekeeping devices generally degrade with use unless additional resources are supplied
Erker et al. 2017
;
Woods et al. 2019
; thus, to study the cost of these devices, a good understanding of covariant operations is crucial.

Quantum resource theories
Gour 2025
are developed precisely for such careful accounting of resources.
The properties of covariant operations and how they utilize timekeeping devices are extensively studied in the context of the resource theory of asymmetry
Bartlett et al. 2007
;
Gour and Spekkens 2008
;
Mashhad 2012
.
This approach has proved useful for understanding quantum metrology
Streltsov et al. 2017
;
Marvian and Spekkens 2014
, quantum clocks and reference frames
Bartlett et al. 2007
, and the cost of realizing quantum measurements
Tajima et al. 2025
.
Most resource-theoretic studies to date have focused on finite-dimensional systems, whilst time-translation symmetry for continuous-variable systems, especially Gaussian optical ones, has been studied separately under the name phase-insensitive operators.
In particular, seminal results, such as studies on linear amplifiers
Haus and Mullen 1962
;
Caves 1982
;
Clerk et al. 2010
, the resolution of the minimum output entropy conjecture for Gaussian bosonic channels
Giovannetti et al. 2004a
;
Giovannetti and Holevo 2014
;
De Palma et al. 2017
;
De Palma et al. 2016
, extensive studies of the classical and quantum capacities of such channels
Holevo and Werner 2001
;
Giovannetti et al. 2004b
, and analyses of boson sampling
Aaronson and Arkhipov 2011
;
Hamilton et al. 2017
, have relied heavily on time-translation symmetry.
Yet, Gaussian operations that are time-translation symmetric—which we call Gaussian covariant operations in the rest of this letter—have rarely been studied in their full generality.

In this work, we unify and generalize existing studies on Gaussian phase-insensitive operations into a framework that encompasses input and output systems with arbitrary numbers of modes.
In particular, we establish both mathematical and physical characterizations of Gaussian covariant operations, namely i) a necessary and sufficient condition for them to be freely dilatable and ii) a monotone function that never increases after a Gaussian covariant operations with several peculiar properties.
Each of these results showcases surprising deviations from general time-translation symmetric operations and from Gaussian operations when both conditions are imposed.

Lastly, we apply our characterization results to thermodynamics, where time-translation symmetry plays a central role
Lostaglio et al. 2015a
;
Korzekwa et al. 2016
;
Smirne et al. 2016
;
Kwon et al. 2018
;
Shiraishi 2025
.
Since time-translation-covariant unitaries coincide with energy-preserving unitaries, demanding the first law (total energy preservation) amounts to requiring operations to be time-translation covariant unless free coherence is supplied via auxiliary systems.
Moreover, a version of the second law can be encoded as Gibbs‑state preservation (non-increasing non‑equilibrium free energies).
Enhanced thermal operations
Ćwikliński et al. 2015
are defined as the set of all operations satisfying these two conditions.
However, not all enhanced thermal operations are freely implementable: there exists a gap
Ding et al. 2021a
between them and the set of operations realizable with a Gibbs-state ancilla and an energy-preserving unitary, known as thermal operations
Janzing et al. 2000
.
Understanding the exact nature of this gap (beyond its origin in asymmetry manipulation) remains a long-standing open problem
Lostaglio et al. 2015b
;
Gour et al. 2018
;
Lie et al. 2025
; progress here will elucidate the genuinely quantum aspects of thermodynamic operations.
In Gaussian optics, time-translation-covariant unitaries correspond exactly to passive elements
Serafini 2017
;
Weedbrook et al. 2012
, and the Gaussian analogue of thermal operations has been introduced
Serafini et al. 2020
;
Narasimhachar et al. 2021
;
Yadin et al. 2022
.
We show that, in this setting, the gap closes: Gaussian thermal operations and Gaussian enhanced thermal operations coincide, resolving the question for a large and experimentally relevant class.

Notations

We consider continuous-variable systems that can be represented by
m
bosonic modes, whose Hamiltonian is written as
H=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\omega_{j}\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{j}
, where
\hat{a}_{j}
and
\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}
are the annihilation/creation operators and
\omega_{j}>0
is the
j
th mode frequency.
A Gaussian state of this system is fully characterized by its first and second moments defined as
r_{j}=r_{m+j}^{*}=\langle\hat{a}_{j}\rangle
for
j=1,\cdots,m
and
M_{j,k}=M_{k+m,j+m}=\frac{1}{2}\langle\{\hat{a}_{j},\hat{a}_{k}^{\dagger}\}\rangle-\langle\hat{a}_{j}\rangle\langle\hat{a}_{k}^{\dagger}\rangle
,
M_{j,k+m}=M_{j+m,k}^{*}=\frac{1}{2}\langle\{\hat{a}_{j},\hat{a}_{k}\}\rangle-\langle\hat{a}_{j}\rangle\langle\hat{a}_{k}\rangle
for
j,k=1,\cdots,m
, respectively.
Accordingly, the first moment vector
\vec{r}
and the second moment matrix
M
can be written as

\displaystyle\vec{r}=\begin{pmatrix}\vec{\alpha}^{*}\\
\vec{\alpha}\end{pmatrix},\ M=\begin{pmatrix}\mu^{*}&\chi\\
\chi^{*}&\mu\end{pmatrix},

(1)

where
\vec{\alpha}
is a
m
-dimensional vector and
\mu
and
\chi
are
m\times m
-dimensional matrices that are Hermitian and symmetric, respectively.
Hence, a Gaussian state is fully characterized by
(\vec{\alpha},\mu,\chi)
.
Moreover, the second moment must obey the uncertainty principle, which corresponds to
M+\mathbf{Z}\geq\mathbf{0}
with a
2m\times 2m
matrix defined as
\mathbf{Z}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{I}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&-\mathbb{I}\end{pmatrix}
, where
\mathbb{I}
is the identity matrix.
Note that we use the first and the second moments defined with
\hat{a}_{j}
,
\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}
operators instead of the more popular choice of
\hat{x}_{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}+\hat{a}_{j})
,
\hat{p}_{j}=\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}(\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}-\hat{a}_{j})
; these two representations are equivalent up to a simple transformation and we choose the former for notational simplicity in most of our proofs.

Similarly, Gaussian operations are specified by the tuple
(X,Y,\vec{d})
acting on the first and second moments as
\vec{r}\rightarrow X\vec{r}+\vec{d}
and
M\rightarrow XMX^{\dagger}+Y
, with the constraint
Y\geq X\mathbf{Z}X^{\dagger}-\mathbf{Z}
.
We also allow scenarios where the input and the output modes are different.
These operations emerge when e.g. input system
S_{\mathrm{in}}
interact with ancilla system
S_{\mathrm{out}}R
followed by the partial trace of
S_{\mathrm{in}}R
.
In such cases,
X
may not be square matrices, and the constraint becomes
Y\geq X\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{in}}X^{\dagger}-\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{out}}
.
For Gaussian unitary operations, the tuple simplifies to
(S,0,\vec{d})
, where
\vec{d}
describes the displacement operation, and
S
(satisfying
S\mathbf{Z}S^{\dagger}=\mathbf{Z}
) is the total effect from beam-splitters, phase-shifters and squeezing.

Mathematical Characterization of Gaussian Covariant Operations

In this work, we are mainly interested in operations that are covariant to time-translation under the free Hamiltonian.
A
Gaussian covariant operation (GCO)
can be formally defined as a Gaussian channel
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{GCO}}
that commutes with time-translation:

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{GCO}}\circ\mathcal{U}_{t}^{\mathrm{in}}=\mathcal{U}_{t}^{\mathrm{out}}\circ\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{GCO}}

(2)

for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
, where
\mathcal{U}_{t}^{\mathrm{in/out}}(\cdot)=e^{-iH_{\mathrm{in/out}}t}(\cdot)e^{iH_{\mathrm{in/out}}t}
, and
H_{\mathrm{in/out}}
are the free Hamiltonians of the input and output systems of
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{GCO}}
.

The commutation relation Eq. (
2
) greatly simplifies the structure of GCO.
Proposition
S1
in Appendix
I
demonstrates that input modes
j,k
of different frequencies
\omega_{j}\neq\omega_{k}
evolve independently under GCO.
It is therefore sufficient to study the case where all input and output modes have the same frequency
\omega
.
Hence, the time-translation operation
\mathcal{U}_{t}
is equivalent to an overall phase shift by
\theta=\omega t
, corresponding to a tuple
(R(\theta),0,0)
, where
R(\theta)=\cos(\theta)\mathbb{I}-i\sin(\theta)\cdot 2\mathbf{Z}
.

The mathematical characterization of GCOs is derived in the literature
Heinosaari et al. 2010
;
Holevo 2015
;
Frank and Lieb 2017
with the name Gaussian gauge-covariant operations.
In our notation, the characterization can be stated as follows:
Any
m_{\mathrm{in}}
-mode to
m_{\mathrm{out}}
-mode GCO is
fully characterized
by a pair
(A,B)
of
m_{\mathrm{out}}\times m_{\mathrm{in}}
matrix
A
and
m_{\mathrm{out}}\times m_{\mathrm{out}}
matrix
B
, transforming first and second moments as:

\displaystyle\vec{\alpha}

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle A\vec{\alpha},

(3)

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle A\mu A^{\dagger}+B,

(4)

\displaystyle\chi

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle A^{*}\chi A^{\dagger},

(5)

with the constraint

B\geq\pm\frac{1}{2}(\mathbb{I}-AA^{\dagger}).

(6)

For completeness, we provide a self-contained proof in Lemma
S.1
, Appendix
I
.
This characterization greatly simplifies the analysis of GCO by reducing the tuple
(X,Y,\vec{d})
to a much smaller one
(A,B)
and showing that second moments
\mu
and
\chi
evolve independently.
Furthermore, if a GCO
(A,B)
is unitary, corresponding to passive elements in optics or energy-preserving unitaries in thermodynamics, the reversibility of unitary operations demands
B=0
, and in turn,
AA^{\dagger}=\mathbb{I}
from Eq. (
6
).
Therefore, a unitary GCO is fully characterized by a single
m\times m
unitary matrix
A=V
, which acts on the first and second moments as
\vec{\alpha}\rightarrow V\vec{\alpha}
,
\mu\rightarrow V\mu V^{\dagger}
, and
\chi\rightarrow V^{*}\chi V^{\dagger}
.

Physical and operational characterization of Gaussian covariant operations

Beyond the mathematical characterization, we provide operational ones.
Typically, a desired quantum channel is implemented by its dilation, i.e. by preparing an auxiliary state and applying a unitary.
For this dilation to be free, the auxiliary and the unitary both need to be free.
We define a class of GCOs with such free dilations (also known as physically implementable operations in the literature
Gour 2025
).

Definition 1

(Freely dilatable GCO)
.

A GCO
\mathcal{E}
on system
S
has a
free dilation
, if it can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\cdot)=\mathrm{Tr}_{R}\left[\mathbf{U}^{\mathrm{PC}}(\cdot\otimes\rho_{R})\mathbf{U}^{\mathrm{PC}\dagger}\right],

(7)

where
\rho_{R}
is a Gaussian symmetric state satisfying
[\rho_{R},H_{R}]=0

for the Hamiltonian
H_{R}
, and
\mathbf{U}^{\mathrm{PC}}
is a Gaussian covariant unitary on
SR
, i.e.
[\mathbf{U}^{\mathrm{PC}},H_{S}+H_{R}]=0
.

Here symmetric states are defined as those invariant under time-translation, or equivalently, under phase-shifts.
In our
(\vec{\alpha},\mu,\chi)
notation, this corresponds to
\vec{\alpha}=0
and
\chi=0
.
In the following lemma (proved in Lemma
S.2
, Appendix
II
), we provide a complete characterization of the set of GCOs that admit a free dilation.

Lemma 1
.

A GCO
(A,B)
is freely dilatable if and only if the following two conditions are satisfied.

(F1)
\mathbb{I}-AA^{\dagger}\geq 0
;

(F2)
\mathrm{supp}(B)=\mathrm{supp}(\mathbb{I}-AA^{\dagger})
.

Lemma
1
reveals that additional constraints arise when demanding a free dilation.
This is not the case for general time-translation covariant operations, where any covariant map can be dilated into a symmetric auxiliary and a covariant unitary
Keyl and Werner 1999
including the infinite-dimensional ones
Mashhad 2012
.
Our next result shows that these additional constraints indeed deny free dilation of some GCOs.
This establishes a fundamental limitation:
Gaussian resources alone are insufficient to realize all GCOs, and non-Gaussianity acts as a key ingredient for physical implementation.

Theorem 1
.

If a GCO mapping a system to itself has no fixed-point that is a valid quantum state, then it is not freely dilatable.
However, the converse is not true.

See Theorem
in Appendix
II
for the proof. Lemma
1
and Theorem
1
are in sharp contrast with the recent results assigning a Gaussian covariant dilation to any GCO
Koukoulekidis and Marvian 2025
.
The main difference lies in the allowed auxiliary systems.
In Ref.
Koukoulekidis and Marvian 2025
, auxiliary systems can have negative frequencies, permitting the energy levels to be unbounded from below.
Ref.
Koukoulekidis and Marvian 2025
justifies this choice by arguing that there exists a rotating frame known as ‘modulation picture’ in which some bosonic modes behave as if they have negative frequencies.
If Definition
1
allows
\mathbf{U}^{\mathrm{PC}}
to be covariant in this modulation picture, then modes with (effective) frequencies
\omega
and
-\omega
can interact via, e.g. two-mode squeezing unitaries.
However, if we account for the strong pump beam needed for the modulation picture,
\mathbf{U}^{\mathrm{PC}}
is no longer energy-preserving.

Definition
1
indeed accounts for all physical modes needed for implementing a GCO.
The most salient examples displaying the difference between our work and Ref.
Koukoulekidis and Marvian 2025
are Gaussian amplifiers
Holevo 2015
, breaking the condition (F1) in Lemma
1
.
They continually increase the system’s energy, preventing the existence of a fixed-point.
Theorem
1
then places them outside the set of freely dilatable GCOs, whereas in Ref.
Koukoulekidis and Marvian 2025
they are regarded freely implementable via e.g. two-mode squeezing unitaries and negative frequency auxiliaries.
In particular, any Gaussian amplifier that are not unitary
(AA^{\dagger}\neq\mathbb{I})
, shares this feature, highlighting amplification as a process fundamentally difficult to realize using only free resources.
Its implementation must therefore incur a
cost
.

The cost of an operation can be measured as an amount of resource one needs to put in the auxiliary state to implement the desired operation; such a framework is standard in quantifying the thermodynamic
Bennett 1982
;
Faist et al. 2015
;
Faist and Renner 2018
and asymmetry cost
Tajima et al. 2020
;
Tajima et al. 2025
;
Tajima and Takagi 2025
.
However, in the case of GCO, any auxiliary state—non-Gaussian and/or asymmetric—cannot implement GCOs that are not freely dilatable using Gaussian covariant unitaries.
In other words, the cost of implementing such operations is infinite.

On the other hand,
Gaussian thermal operations (GTO)

Serafini et al. 2020
;
Narasimhachar et al. 2021
constitute an important class of freely dilatable GCOs.
A GTO is defined as a freely dilatable operation with the constraint that the ancilla
\rho_{R}
is a Gibbs state at ambient temperature
\beta^{-1}
, i.e.
\rho_{R}=e^{-\beta H_{R}}/\mathrm{Tr}[{e^{-\beta H_{R}}}]
.
It is the Gaussian analogue of thermal operations
Janzing et al. 2000
defined as channels implementable with an energy-preserving unitary and a Gibbs state ancilla.
Thermal operations exactly constitute the set of freely dilatable operations in thermodynamic theories, yet they are notoriously hard to characterize, as are the state transformations resulting from them.
This motivated the definition of a slightly larger set known as enhanced thermal operations
Ćwikliński et al. 2015
.
Enhanced thermal operations are defined axiomatically as the set of covariant operations that preserve the Gibbs state at the ambient temperature, and are therefore easier to characterize
Gour et al. 2018
.
Unfortunately, the set of enhanced thermal operations is strictly larger than that of thermal operations; this strict inclusion also holds for corresponding sets of state transformations
Ding et al. 2021a
.
In other words, some enhanced thermal operations are not freely dilatable thermodynamic operations.

The
Gaussian analogue of enhanced thermal operations (GEnTO)
can be defined similarly, as the subset of GCO that preserves a Gibbs state.
Surprisingly, we show that every GEnTO is freely dilatable using thermodynamically free and Gaussian resources.

Theorem 2
.

For any Gaussian enhanced thermal operation
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{GEnTO}}
on a system
S
, there exist an auxiliary Gibbs state
\gamma_{R}=\frac{e^{-\beta H_{R}}}{\mathrm{Tr}[e^{-\beta H_{R}}]}
and a unitary GCO
\mathbf{U}^{\mathrm{PC}}
, such that

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{GEnTO}}(\cdot)=\mathrm{Tr}_{R}\left[\mathbf{U}^{\mathrm{PC}}(\cdot\otimes\gamma_{R})\mathbf{U}^{\mathrm{PC}\dagger}\right].

(8)

This implies the sets GEnTO and GTO are equivalent.

See Theorem
S.2
, Appendix
II
for a proof.
This result contrasts with the finite-dimensional scenario where, as mentioned above, a gap exists between thermal operations and enhanced thermal operations even at the level of state transformations.
Theorem
2
indicates that not only does this gap vanish, but a stronger equivalence at the channel level is achieved once Gaussian-preserving constrains are imposed on both classes of operations.

Consider the simplest case of a single-mode GCO
(A,B)
.
If it has a physical fixed-point
(\vec{\alpha},\mu,\chi)
, it also fixes a Gaussian symmetric state
(0,\mu,0)
, which are Gibbs states at some effective temperatures for single-mode systems.
Thus Theorem
2
implies that any single-mode GCO with a fixed-point is a GTO, and consequently, is freely dilatable.

Unlike the single-mode scenario, the existence of a fixed-point is no longer sufficient to guarantee physical implementability for multi-mode systems, as shown in Theorem
1
.
Nevertheless, Theorem
2
guarantees that if a Gibbs state is one of the fixed-points, a GCO is freely dilatable.

Completely non-extensive monotones for Gaussian covariant operations

We have so far focused on the properties of GCO channels themselves; below we analyze second moments transformations
(\mu,\chi)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{GCO}}}{{\longrightarrow}}(\mu^{\prime},\chi^{\prime})
induced by these channels.
For this purpose, we identify a pair of monotones.

Definition 2
.

Let
(\mu,\chi)
be the second moment of a quantum state.

\mathrm{Sl}_{\pm}(\mu,\chi)
are defined as

\mathrm{Sl}_{\pm}(\mu,\chi)\coloneqq\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1}\left[\left(\mu^{*}\pm\frac{\mathbb{I}}{2}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\chi\ \left(\mu\pm\frac{\mathbb{I}}{2}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right],

(9)

where
(\cdot)^{-\frac{1}{2}}
denotes the pseudoinverse of
(\cdot)^{\frac{1}{2}}
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1}[\cdot]
denotes the largest singular value.

As an illustrative example, consider a single-mode system with the second moment
(\mu,\chi)
, in which case the monotones are given as
\mathrm{Sl}_{\pm}(\mu,\chi)=\frac{|\chi|}{\mu\pm\frac{1}{2}}
.
In Figure
1
, single-mode states are visualized in a two-dimensional plot where
\mathrm{Sl}_{-}
corresponds to the slope of the line connecting the single-mode vacuum and the state of interest, whereas
\mathrm{Sl}_{+}
corresponds to the other slope relevant for GCO state transformations, as formalized in Lemma
2
.

Intuitively,
\mathrm{Sl}_{\pm}(\mu,\chi)
quantify the asymmetry of a state as they increase when
\chi
becomes more prominent with
\mu
fixed and decrease when
\mu
grows with
\chi
fixed.
Yet, they do not capture the all aspects of asymmetry.
Most obviously, they are independent of the first moment of the state and thus fail to sense the asymmetry induced by displacements (known as type-1 asymmetry) while measuring the asymmetry in the second moment (type-2 asymmetry).
Type-1 asymmetric (but type-2 symmetric) states include displaced thermal states including coherent states, while type-2 asymmetric (but type-1 symmetric) states include squeezed thermal states.
It has been found that type-1 and type-2 asymmetry are not interconvertible via GCO
Koukoulekidis and Marvian 2025
.
Therefore, we focus solely on type-2 asymmetry in this letter.

We now prove several important properties of
\mathrm{Sl}_{\pm}
functions.

(P1)

Finiteness and faithfulness:
0\leq\mathrm{Sl}_{\pm}(\mu,\chi)<\infty
with
\mathrm{Sl}_{\pm}(\mu,\chi)=0
if and only if
\chi=0
.

(P2)

Monotonicity:
\mathrm{Sl}_{\pm}(\mu,\chi)\geq\mathrm{Sl}_{\pm}(\mu^{\prime},\chi^{\prime})
whenever
(\mu,\chi)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{GCO}}}{{\longrightarrow}}(\mu^{\prime},\chi^{\prime})
.

(P3)

Complete non-extensiveness:
\mathrm{Sl}_{\pm}(\mu_{1}\oplus\mu_{2},\chi_{1}\oplus\chi_{2})=\max\{\mathrm{Sl}_{\pm}(\mu_{1},\chi_{1}),\,\mathrm{Sl}_{\pm}(\mu_{2},\chi_{2})\}
.

(P1–3) are proved in Proposition
S2
, Lemmas
S.6
and
S.8
, respectively.
Note that
(\mu_{1}\oplus\mu_{2},\chi_{1}\oplus\chi_{2})
represents the second moment of a tensor product of two states each with the second moments
(\mu_{1},\chi_{1})
and
(\mu_{2},\chi_{2})
.

Among the properties, the complete non-extensiveness of (P3) is rare and entails surprising consequences for state transformations.
Importantly, it rules out any multi-copy or distillation processes achieving higher
\mathrm{Sl}_{\pm}
values.
To see this, suppose that
(\oplus_{i=1}^{N}\mu,\oplus_{i=1}^{N}\chi)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{GCO}}}{{\longrightarrow}}(\oplus_{i=1}^{M}\mu^{\prime},\oplus_{i=1}^{M}\chi^{\prime})
.
(P2) and (P3) imply that
\mathrm{Sl}_{\pm}(\mu,\chi)\geq\mathrm{Sl}_{\pm}(\mu^{\prime},\chi^{\prime})
, regardless of
N
and
M
.
Therefore, it is impossible to achieve even a single copy of a state with
(\mu^{\prime},\chi^{\prime})
using however many copies of a state with
(\mu,\chi)
if
\mathrm{Sl}_{\pm}(\mu^{\prime},\chi^{\prime})>\mathrm{Sl}_{\pm}(\mu,\chi)
.
Similar no-go theorems have been established for various tasks when imposing operations to be Gaussian, including distillation of entanglement
Eisert et al. 2002
, steering, and squeezing
Lami et al. 2018
, quantum error correction
Niset et al. 2009
, and quantum sensing
Wang and Zhou 2025
.
Yet, our result on asymmetry is especially extreme, as its finite-dimensional counterpart has the diverging marginal asymptotic distillation rate for almost all initial states
Shiraishi and Takagi 2024
.
Note that our monotones only forbid the distillation of type-2 asymmetry (concerning second moments) while type-1 asymmetry is known to be distillable via GCOs
Yadavalli and Marvian 2025
.

Finally, we study whether catalysts can relax state transformation conditions for GCOs.
Catalysts are auxiliary systems that participate in the process to activate previously prohibited transformations
Jonathan and Plenio 1999
;
Lipka-Bartosik et al. 2024
.
For instance, even if the direct transformation
\rho_{S}\to\rho^{\prime}_{S^{\prime}}
cannot be performed using a set of allowed operations, a transformation
\rho_{S}\otimes\tau_{C}\to\rho^{\prime}_{S^{\prime}}\otimes\tau_{C}
(strict catalysis) or
\to\varrho_{S^{\prime}C}
such that
\mathrm{Tr}_{C}[\varrho_{S^{\prime}C}]=\rho^{\prime}_{S}
and
\mathrm{Tr}_{S^{\prime}}[\varrho_{S^{\prime}C}]=\tau_{C}
(correlated catalysis) is often achievable with an allowed operation.
In both strict and correlated catalysis, the catalyst state
\tau_{C}
is (marginally) preserved and can be reused for an indefinite number of times.
Correlated catalysis, in particular, is found to relax all state transformation conditions except for a single monotone inequality in various resource theories, including the theory of athermality
Shiraishi and Sagawa 2021
;
Son and Ng 2024
;
Shiraishi 2025
;
Shiraishi and Takagi 2025
, nonuniformity
Wilming 2022
, entanglement (for pure states)
Kondra et al. 2021
, essentially overcoming all finite-size restrictions.
In the case of
\mathrm{U}(1)
-asymmetry, i.e. when covariant operations (not necessarily Gaussian) are allowed, correlated catalysis even enables arbitrary amplification of the asymmetry
Ding et al. 2021b
;
Shiraishi and Takagi 2024
;
Kondra et al. 2024
.
Remarkably, correlated catalysis loses its power when Gaussianity is imposed.

(P4)

\mathrm{Sl}_{\pm}(\mu,\chi)
are monotonic under (correlated) catalytic transformations.

A full technical statement and its proof is in Lemma

Theorem 3

Discussions

In this work, we studied the interplay between Gaussianity and time-translation covariance, two properties well-studied separately but rarely together.
Our main technical results are the characterization of Gaussian covariant operations.
We fully characterized the subset with a free dilation, the structure of operations with a fixed-point, and monotone functions that never increase after an operation.
We also provide a useful decomposition for operations with an asymmetric fixed-point as Lemma
in Section
II
.
These characterizations will be useful for future studies on Gaussian covariant operations, which encompass numerous important operations in optics.
In particular, we expect the new, completely non-extensive monotones to serve as simple and powerful tools for studying general state transformation problems.

The significance of our results is not limited to Gaussian covariant operations.
By contrasting general time-translation‑symmetric quantum operations and thermal processes with their Gaussian subset, we obtain three main contrasts arising from Gaussianity.
First, we identify a subset of Gaussian covariant operations that cannot be dilated into any free unitary, indicating a discrepancy between axiomatically defined and operationally defined sets.
This separation is reminiscent of similar ones that appear when additional restrictions such as Markovianity
Spaventa et al. 2022
;
Korzekwa and Lostaglio 2022
or locality
Marvian 2022
are imposed.
Second, we prove that asymptotic or catalytic advantages vanish for single-shot restrictions given by the monotones
\mathrm{Sl}_{\pm}
introduced here.
This mirrors the impossibility of Gaussian resource distillation in various setups
Eisert et al. 2002
;
Lami et al. 2018
.
Finally, we demonstrate that Gaussianity can remove the separation between axiomatically and operationally defined sets by proving that Gaussian thermal operations are identical to Gaussian enhanced thermal operations.
Such closure of the gap between thermal and enhanced thermal operations has not been observed at the channel level in other settings including those allowing catalytic channels
Son et al. 2024
.

What are the reasons behind these contrasts?
We outline a few possible explanations.
Given that Gaussian thermal operations are Markovian
Serafini et al. 2020
, one might ask whether Markovianity is the decisive factor.
Ref.
Haagerup and Musat 2011
gives a negative answer by investigating the Markovian versions of thermal operations and enhanced thermal operations and explicitly constructing a counterexample showing the gap between the two.
This is a clear sign that other aspects of Gaussianity are more crucial.
Another physical intuition is that Gaussian operations, generated by quadratic Hamiltonian, decompose into two-mode interactions, unlike general symmetric operations
Marvian 2022
.
This locality might underlie the absence of asymptotic/catalytic advantages and the absence of separation between GEnTO and GTO.
Further work on the complete characterization of asymptotic and catalytic transformations beyond our single‑mode and second‑moment results would help test this intuition.

We finally point out two promising avenues for future work.
First is the calculation of approximate implementation cost of GCOs.
We established that the exact implementation cost diverges for some GCOs including amplifiers, yet they are used in practice.
It means that with sufficiently large cost, GCOs without free dilation can be approximated well.
Determining the trade-off between approximation accuracy and implementation cost would impact theorists and practitioners alike.
Second is the better understanding of
\mathrm{Sl}_{\pm}
monotones.
Definition
2
suggests that a single mode, corresponding to the vector achieving the optimal ratio of
\chi
and
\mu
, determines the monotone values, but it is not clear what this mode is for general multi-mode systems.
We anticipate that better insight into this optimal mode should illuminate hidden structure in Gaussian states.
