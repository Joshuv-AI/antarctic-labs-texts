SynergyImage: image-based model for drug combinations synergy score prediction
Authors: Maryam Mehrabani, Amir Lakizadeh, Alireza Fotuhi Siahpirani, Ali Masoudi-Nejad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe potential of synergistic drug combinations in cancer research has been acknowledged for their ability to enhance treatment outcomes. Instead of depending on costly experimental methods, computational techniques have emerged to forecast such combinations. Recent advancements in computational methods have utilized vast datasets comprising chemical, genomic, and pharmacological information. These methods aim to predict drug interactions by analyzing complex biological data and identifying patterns that are indicative of synergistic effects.MethodsHere, SynergyImage employs an unsupervised pre-learning technique, ImageMol, to extract features from the chemical structure images of the drugs. Concurrently, gene expression data is transformed into image formats using the DeepInsight method, enabling the extraction of image-based features corresponding to the cancer cell lines through a convolutional neural network. Following a dimensionality reduction step, the framework utilizes a multi-layer perceptron network to predict the synergy scores for various drug combinations.ResultsExperimental results show that SynergyImage surpasses leading methods on the O'Neil benchmark datasets, highlighting its capability to predict synergistic drug combinations. In particular, SynergyImage recorded an MSE of 73.402 ± 1.185 and a PCC of 0.83 ± 0.003. These numerical results further highlight the superior performance of SynergyImage compared to other models.

Introduction

Compared with monotherapy, in most situations, combination therapy can gain better clinical therapeutic effects in cancer patients because it reduces drug toxicity and conquers drug resistance [1]. Many drug combinations synergistically work to enhance therapeutic selectivity and are currently receiving increased recognition [2]. The level of synergy between a combination of drugs is typically assessed using in vitro or in vivo studies, and the results are normally expressed through a calculated synergy score. This score helps researchers determine whether the drugs are more effective when used together than when used individually. Several models have been developed to quantify drug synergy, like the Loewe additivity model [3], the Bliss independence model [4], the Highest Single Agent (HSA) model [5], and the Zero Interaction Potency (ZIP) model [6]. Despite the rapid advancements in high-throughput screening (HTS) technologies, it remains impractical to experimentally assess every possible drug combination for its synergistic effects on different cell lines. This is due to the immense number of available drugs and the diversity of cancer cells [7]. Consequently, there is a need for innovative computational approaches to aid in the discovery of drug combinations. The rapid progress in computational modeling, especially with deep learning techniques, has greatly enhanced the predictive abilities of these models. These techniques can effectively utilize a wide range of features, such as chemical and structural properties of drugs and omics data from cell lines [8]. One of the pioneering computational models in this field is DeepSynergy. This model utilizes a feed-forward neural network architecture with two hidden layers. It combines chemical descriptors of drugs and genomic profiles of cell lines as input features to predict drug synergy outcomes [9]. This model was trained utilizing a leading pharmacogenomic screening dataset obtained from cancer cell lines, known as the O'Neil dataset [10], and outperformed several traditional ML algorithms for predicting novel drug combinations. To enhance predictive performance, Wang et al. introduced the PRODeepSyn model by incorporating auxiliary data and increasing the complexity of the DL structure [11]. In another attempt at pathway deconvolution of drug combinations, the TranSynergy approach, developed by Liu and his colleagues, aims to enhance the clarity of predictions regarding synergistic drug interactions. This model assesses the cellular impacts of drug actions by integrating cell line gene dependencies, gene–gene interactions, and a comprehensive examination of genome-wide drug-target interactions. Additionally, a novel technique called Shapley Additive Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (SA-GSEA) has been introduced to pinpoint the genes that contribute to the synergistic effects of drug combinations, thereby enhancing the model's interpretability [12]. Next, Lin and his colleagues developed an innovative method called GTextSyn to investigate the complex interactions between drugs and cell lines, to predict the synergistic effects of drug combinations. This approach utilizes a sentence classification model based on Natural Language Processing (NLP), where drugs and cell lines are assigned biological significance. The combinations of drug pairs with cell lines are interpreted as sentences that reveal important biochemical interactions. The creativity behind this method greatly enhances the accuracy of predicting synergy scores [13]. Another study conducted by Rafiei and colleagues, referred to as CFSSynergy, presented a combination of feature-based and similarity-based approaches to gain a more comprehensive understanding of drug features and cell line characteristics, thereby enhancing the accuracy of predicting effective drug combinations [14]. Recently, Zhao and their colleagues introduced a novel model called PermuteDDS, which stands for Permutable Feature Fusion Network for Drug-Drug Synergy prediction. This model is specifically designed to accommodate various representations of both drugs and cell lines. By utilizing a one-dimensional convolutional neural network (CNN), they effectively learned and extracted high-level features from these different representations [15]. Huangfu et al. introduced a machine learning framework called Similarity Network-based Synergy prediction (SNSynergy). This framework utilizes simple models to enhance the performance of predicting synergistic effects on novel cell lines and drug combinations. They developed a model based on the assumption that similar cell lines and drug combinations exhibit comparable synergistic effects [16].

Despite the notable success of deep learning-based methods, which have shown significant promise in predicting drug synergy, these approaches commonly utilize one-dimensional sequential notations, such as the Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System (SMILES) and the International Chemical Identifier (InChI), or two-dimensional (2D) graphical representations to depict drug attributes. Additionally, they use one-dimensional descriptors for cell lines, such as gene expression profiles or copy number variants (CNVs). This study introduces an innovative approach leveraging image-based representations for drug molecules and cell lines. We employ molecular images by utilizing a pre-trained ImageMol model [17] to represent drugs, achieving high accuracy and low computational cost. Additionally, we've used a DeepInsight method [18], to convert cancer cell transcriptome data into images, potentially revealing hidden mechanisms. We've designed a straightforward deep-learning model named SynergyImage to predict synergy score for novel cell lines and drug combinations. This model takes advantage of image-based representations for drugs and cell lines by applying CNN layers to capture the intricate patterns of features indicative of synergistic interactions.

The article is structured as follows: Sect. "Materials and methods" describes a detailed explanation of the proposed method. Section "Results" includes the evaluation results of this method. Finally, Sect. "Discussion and conclusion" discusses future work and offers conclusions.

Materials and methods

Overview of SynergyImage

The SynergyImage model consists of three steps for predicting drug synergy scores.i.Image-based features related to each drug are extracted using a pre-trained ImageMol model [17] (Fig. 1, Part A).ii.Each cancer cell line gene expression is converted to an image by using the DeepInsight approach [18], and then applied the ResNet18 [19], an encoder to extract a latent cell line`s features (Fig. 1, Part B).iii.The features of both drugs are encoded using shared fully connected layers, while the features of the cell lines are encoded using their own separate fully connected layers. After encoding, the drug and cell line features are combined by concatenating them. A multi-layer Perceptron (MLP) is then applied to the concatenated features to predict the cell line-specific drug combination synergy score (Fig. 1, Part C).

Drugs encoder by ImageMol

We extract drug features using ImageMol, an unsupervised deep learning framework that has been pre-trained on 10 million unlabeled bioactive molecules. The goal of the ImageMol framework is to develop pre-trained chemical representations from these unlabeled molecular images. It focuses on capturing both the local and global structural characteristics of molecules based on pixel-based data [17]. ImageMol showcases impressive effectiveness in evaluating molecular characteristics, including drug metabolism, brain permeability, and toxicity. It also clearly outlines molecular target profiles across a wide range of benchmark datasets. In this approach, a molecular encoder is created to extract latent features from approximately 10 million molecular images. To improve the molecular encoder's latent representation, five different pretraining strategies were employed, integrating chemical insights and structural information obtained from the molecular images of the drug. These strategies include:

(i) Multi-granularity chemical clusters classification: Initially, the MACCS key—a 166-bit sequence of 0 and 1 serving as a molecular fingerprint descriptor—was utilized to depict molecular fingerprints for clustering purposes. K-means clustering was applied to categorize the molecules into groups based on varying levels of specificity. Following that, a ResNet18 model was utilized to encode the molecular images. Additionally, a multitask structural classifier with three fully connected layers was employed to predict pseudo labels for the latent features. Cell lines encoder

In this part, we've used the CCLE gene expression profile, which overlaps with the LINCS project's landmark genes. The project carefully selected 978 genes that can predict the expression levels of about 81% of transcripts that haven't been quantified yet [21]. To consider hidden mechanisms, such as pathways, as well as make differences and similarities of cancer cell lines more straightforward to see, we used the DeepInsight approach to turn gene expressions into a structured and organized image format [18]. DeepInsight constructs an image by clustering similar features and separating dissimilar ones. Thus, converting gene expressions into images by grouping them as clusters is more meaningful than treating each one individually (which would ignore neighborhood information). This method converts the feature vector of gene expression values into a feature matrix using a specific transformation function. The features are placed in Cartesian coordinates based on their similarity. After establishing the positions of these features, a convex hull algorithm identifies the minimal bounding rectangle that encloses them. The orientation of this rectangle is then adjusted by rotating the image to achieve either a horizontal or vertical alignment, optimizing it for processing by a CNN. Subsequently, the Cartesian coordinates are converted to pixel values. This conversion process involves averaging certain features, particularly since there are limitations on the number of pixels in the image. Finally, gene expression profiles are transformed into images using the CNN layers of the ResNet18 architecture [19]. The convolution layers of ResNet18 help in detecting various features in the images. These layers utilize filters to capture spatial hierarchies between pixels by applying convolution operations, which help to understand the underlying patterns in the gene expression data.

After embedding the drug and cell features, we employed fully connected layers to encode both drug combinations. Similarly, cell line features are encoded by their own fully connected layers. We then concatenated the two drug features with cell line features and applied a multi-layer Perceptron (MLP) to predict the cell line-specific drug combination synergy score. A comprehensive manual grid search was performed to optimize critical hyperparameters, encompassing learning rates (9e−5, 9e−4, 9e−3), batch sizes (16, 32, 64), hidden layer dimensions (512, 256, 128), dropout rates (0.2, 0.3, 0.5), and L2 regularization coefficients (1e−5, 1e−4, 1e−3). The optimal hyperparameter configuration was determined to be a learning rate of 9e−4, a batch size of 32, an L2 regularization coefficient of 1e−4, a dropout rate of 0.2, and a maximum of 1600 training epochs, incorporating early stopping. The finalized neural network architecture comprised drug and cell line branches with sequential layers of 512 → 256 → 128, followed by combined layers of 256 → 128 → 32 → 1. To minimize any potential bias from the order of drug combinations, we utilized shared fully connected layers to encode the A-B combinations. The shared encoding layers enable the network to regard both orders with equal importance when predicting the drug combination, A-B.

Dataset

Drug synergy scores employed in this study were obtained from the DrugComb database [22], specifically version v1.5, accessible at https://drugcomb.org/download/. We obtained the O'Neil dataset from the DrugComb database, which consolidates information from 37 extensive combinatorial drug screening studies. The original dataset contained 92,208 entries, with each entry corresponding to a unique two-drug combination evaluated on an individual cancer cell line [10]. This dataset is quite diverse, including 39 distinct types of cancer cells originating from six different tissue sources, along with a collection of 38 drugs. Out of these drugs, 24 have been approved by the FDA, while the remaining 14 are still in the experimental stage. The dataset encompasses a total of 583 unique drug combinations. We began by examining the unprocessed data, which encompassed several replicated experiments. We then calculated the final synergy score for each unique combination of drugs and cell lines by averaging the scores from all the repeated experiments, and retained these averaged values without applying any normalization to maintain the original distribution and prevent bias from data transformation. We used the Loewe synergy scores using the most widely used Loewe model [3]. The analyzed dataset included 19,822 experiments, and the synergy scores varied widely, ranging from −106 to 35, covering a broad spectrum of values. Discussion and conclusion

SynergyImage is a novel strategy that leverages image-based representations of drug molecules and gene expression profiles of cell lines to predict the synergy scores of drug combinations in specific cell lines. SynergyImage used a pre-trained ImageMol model that extracted pharmacologically significant features of drugs from their molecular image representations, demonstrating improved accuracy in predicting compound-protein interactions [35] and showing improvement in the prediction of other molecular characteristics of drugs [17]. Furthermore, this study employed the DeepInsight method to transform gene expressions into an organized image format for CNN analysis, which has recently shown a notable enhancement in predicting disease-free survival outcomes for patients with clear cell renal cell carcinoma [36]. Then, SynergyImage utilized the convolution layers of ResNet18 to detect various features in the gene expression images. Subsequently, SynergyImage applied a multi-layer Perceptron (MLP) to predict cell line-specific drug combination synergy scores. The superior performance of SynergyImage compared to existing methods, along with its significantly improved drug synergy predictions, suggests that SynergyImage’s innovative approach to feature extraction and combination—utilizing pre-trained convolutional neural network (CNN) layers to extract features from the image representations of drugs and cell lines—can potentially capture complex patterns and unveil hidden mechanisms that are not easily discernible using traditional methods. However, most of the results in this study remain computational predictions. The top-ranked combinations in Table 6 represent the highest predicted Loewe scores; while existing studies support some, others are speculative and require experimental validation. Thus, Table 6 should be regarded as prioritized candidates for testing rather than confirmed therapies, with SynergyImage serving as a tool to prioritize drug pairs, narrow the search space for validation, and guide mechanistic studies in preclinical pipelines. SynergyImage offers a promising direction for future research, potentially providing a new paradigm in drug synergy prediction, drug toxicity prediction, and applications in personalized medicine through image-based data representations. Nevertheless, while our method exhibits superior performance compared to other methods, as demonstrated in Tables 2 and 3, it is constrained by some limitations, such as poor performance in terms of RMSE and PCC on a completely independent test set, as indicated in Table 5. This issue is similar to other studies, such as the work by Yejin Kim et al. on anticancer drug synergy prediction, which used independent test sets that shared very few cell lines or drugs with the training data and also reported poor performance on this external test set [37]. One possible reason is that SynergyImage was trained on a relatively small dataset, which includes only 34 cell lines and 38 drugs. Expanding the dataset to include more drugs and cell lines could improve the model's robustness and generalizability. Moreover, the model does not consider additional data regarding chemical–target interactions and target–target interactions, which can provide additional sources of information to improve the model predictions. In future research, we aim to incorporate cell morphological responses to chemical perturbations to enhance our understanding of cellular responses to drug treatments and facilitate the discovery of novel drug combinations with improved therapeutic effects. To summarize, our research suggests that the SynergyImage model holds significant promise in detecting synergies among anticancer drugs and offers valuable insights into the underlying mechanisms that facilitate drug synergy.
