Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes display prognostic signatures associated with chemotherapy response in TNBC patients
Authors: Shayantan Banerjee, Vijay K Tiwari, Karthik Raman, Mohammad Inayatullah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryTriple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is an aggressive subtype often resistant to neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC), and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) are important predictors of response. Using single-cell RNA sequencing datasets of pre-treated TNBC tumors, we identified an 80-gene TIL-specific signature that captures immune activation and stratifies tumors by TIL abundance. Pathway analysis showed enrichment of immune-regulatory programs, including allograft rejection, consistent with adaptive immune activity. Validation across single-cell and bulk datasets linked the signature to favorable response and relapse-free survival. A multi-stage feature selection pipeline refined the panel to 30 genes, achieving strong prediction of pathological complete response versus residual disease across eleven independent TNBC cohorts (n = 680; mean area under the ROC curve [AUROC] = 0.77). Network analysis identified consensus hub genes, including CD8A, LCK, and CTLA4, central to T cell signaling. Regulatory analysis revealed a conserved set of TIL-associated transcription factors, supporting an immune program with clinical utility in TNBC stratification and therapeutic targeting strategies.Subject areas: Microenvironment, Bioinformatics, Cancer

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is one of the most prevalent cancers, affecting millions worldwide.1 The molecular classification of BC subtypes is based on the expression of receptors such as estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2).2 Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), which accounts for approximately 15%–20% of BC cases, is particularly aggressive due to the absence of ER, PR, and HER2, high risk of recurrence, and poorer long-term survival.3 The inefficacy of endocrine and anti-HER2 therapies against TNBC often leaves chemotherapy and surgery as the primary treatment options.4 Neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) targets the tumor’s micro-metastatic components, thereby providing a breast-conserving alternative. However, only about half of patients achieve pathologic complete response (pCR) after NAC, and pCR is generally associated with improved recurrence-free survival (RFS). The remaining patients experience poor overall survival (OS) and a high risk of recurrence.5 Adopting a precision medicine approach, which includes understanding the TNBC transcriptome and identifying molecular markers, can significantly enhance patient stratification and clinical decision-making. Stratifying patients by predicted chemotherapy response based on their molecular profiles can improve treatment selection and lead to more individualized care.

Several studies have explored the transcriptional landscape of TNBC at both the bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and single-cell level.2,6,7,8 Many studies have revealed that TNBC tumors exhibit high heterogeneity due to cell differentiation, plasticity, cancer cell stemness, and the tumor microenvironment (TME).9,10 Notably, some single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) studies, such as the one by Kim et al.,11,12 performed transcriptional profiling of eight pre- and post-NAC-treated TNBC patients and identified two distinct response patterns: “clonal extinction,” characterized by the disappearance of tumor cells after NAC treatment, and “clonal persistence,” marked by the enrichment of tumor clones post-treatment, indicating a lack of response. While Kim et al. attributed these divergent clonal trajectories to intrinsic genomic and transcriptional adaptations, their observations also raised the possibility that tumor-extrinsic factors, such as immune infiltration, may modulate adaptive resistance. In this context, tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) have emerged as informative indicators of anti-tumor immune activity within the TME. Recently, multiple studies have established an association between TIL abundance and the progression of TNBC.13,14,15,16 Specifically, a high abundance of TILs before treatment is typically associated with a high incidence of pCR.17 Similarly, at least 60% of TILs in TNBC patients with residual disease (RD) after NAC treatment were associated with better outcomes.18 On the basis of these data, we hypothesized that gene markers extracted from the TIL subpopulation of cells, as determined by single-cell expression measurements, could serve as prognostic indicators for evaluating the NAC response in TNBC patients.

With the recent advancements in machine learning-assisted identification of gene biomarkers from publicly available expression datasets, their evaluation and use in patient stratification, therapy response prediction, and precision treatment design have increased.19,20,21,22,23 However, there is a strong unmet need to integrate scRNA-seq and bulk RNA-seq expression measurements to identify predictors of response from the TIL subpopulation of cells belonging to TNBC patients. To address this, we first leveraged pre-treatment scRNA-seq data from multiple TNBC cohorts to derive an 80-gene TIL signature that captures the transcriptional profile of immune-active lymphocytes within the TME. This signature was extensively validated across diverse single-cell and bulk expression datasets, confirming its enrichment in immune cell compartments and association with pathological complete response. We subsequently identified a refined 30-gene predictive subset related to chemotherapy response and distilled it into a 10-gene consensus hub gene module, using integrative network analysis. These hub genes, which were consistently associated with a conserved TIL-associated regulatory program central to adaptive immune signaling, were validated across multiple independent TNBC cohorts, where their expression was significantly elevated in patients achieving favorable clinical outcomes.

Results

A TIL subpopulation-specific gene signature derived from pre-treatment TNBC identifies immune-enriched tumors with superior response and survival

To understand the underlying signatures of chemosensitivity, we conducted an integrated analysis of scRNA-seq expression profiles from three publicly available scRNA-seq datasets curated by Pang et al.,24 namely Wu_2021 (GEO: GSE176078),25 Xu_2021 (GEO: GSE180286),26 and Bhupinder_2021 (GEO: GSE161529).27 Together, these datasets comprised 14 TNBC samples, each with approximately 36,000 cells (Figure 1). Each dataset had already undergone standardized preprocessing, batch correction, and expert-curated cell-type annotation, as described by Pang et al.24 (original manuscript sections: “scRNA-seq data processing,” “batch effect correction,” “cell clustering,” and “cell-type annotation”). In our study, we did not re-integrate these datasets at the cell level; instead, we restricted each dataset to pre-treatment TNBC cells, re-processed them using a standard Seurat v.4.4 workflow as described in STAR Methods (“single-cell data processing”), and identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between our data-driven TIL-high and TIL-low states within each dataset. Dimensionality reduction via uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) revealed diverse levels of heterogeneity across distinct cellular subpopulations (Figures 2A–2C, left).

We utilized CopyKAT to infer copy number variations and distinguish between tumor cells and stromal and immune components. This method effectively distinguishes between malignant (aneuploid) and non-malignant (diploid) cells in each dataset. While diploid cells mainly mapped to the immune and stromal clusters, aneuploid cells were more concentrated in tumor-associated regions of the UMAP plot (Figure S1). To confirm these findings, we generated pseudo-bulk expression profiles of diploid and aneuploid cells and then applied the ESTIMATE algorithm. Across all datasets, diploid clusters exhibited significantly elevated “StromalScore” (mean ± SD: 518.33 ± 357.26) and “ImmuneScore” (3,200.41 ± 164.21) compared with aneuploid clusters (Figure S2), confirming the strong presence of non-malignant cell signatures.

TIL module scoring reveals intratumoral heterogeneity in immune infiltration

To identify TILs within the diploid cluster of each single-cell dataset, we first defined a TIL marker gene set comprising canonical T and B cell markers (see STAR Methods). For each cell, we measured the expression of the markers to build individual composite TIL marker scores, as a more accurate strategy to detect TILs, and named it the “TIL-combined” score. Next, we performed Gaussian mixture modeling (GMM; G = 1–6) of the combined scores and identified dataset-specific optima by Bayesian information criterion (BIC) (G = 3, 4, and 5 for Xu_2021 [GEO: GSE180286], Wu_2021 [GEO: GSE176078], and Bhupinder_2021 [GEO: GSE161529], respectively) (Table S1). However, while comparing the cluster components against the original cell-type annotations (T cells, B cells, and plasmablasts), we observed that the two-component model (G = 2) showed the maximum agreement with the expected TIL versus non-TIL cluster assignments. Across all datasets, G = 2 demonstrated optimal performance with the highest silhouette widths (0.51–0.56), the strongest agreement with annotated cell types (adjusted Rand index [ARI] = 0.41–0.75; normalized mutual information [NMI] = 0.34–0.64), as well as the lowest mean posterior uncertainty (0.08–0.09). Additionally, G = 2 also exhibited the highest TIL coverage ranging from 0.89 to 0.97 with optimal purity-coverage scores (0.4–0.53). These findings indicate a reliable separation between TIL-rich and TIL-depleted subpopulations (Table S2). Conversely, the higher-order models (G = 3–5) led to optimal BIC scores but showed reduced TIL coverage and increased classification ambiguity, suggesting over-segmentation of the TIL-rich subpopulation. Moreover, the effect size for separation based on the TIL-combined score in the two-component model (Cohen’s d) ranged from 2.48 to 2.55 across all datasets, demonstrating significant separation of the subpopulations that were TIL-high from TIL-low. Additionally, bootstrapping stability analysis confirmed the stability of the G = 2 models. This is reflected in the mean ARI values exceeding 0.98 (SD < 0.014), while the overall and median consistency indices approximated 1.0, as shown in Table S3. Independent validation using the 60-gene TIL signature by Danaher et al.28 (Table S4) established that across all datasets, the TIL-high cells had markedly higher immune-infiltration scores than the TIL-low cells (Wilcoxon p < 0.05) establishing the biological validity of the subgroups derived from GMM.

These findings suggest that across several independent TNBC cohorts, the two-component model remains the most biologically coherent representation of TIL heterogeneity. Using this model, we classified the diploid cells into two groups (“TIL-high” and “TIL-low”) based on their posterior probabilities. Components were labeled based on their mean “TIL-combined” score, with the higher scoring component designated as “TIL-high.” On the basis of this separation, we found a robust overlap within GMM (G = 2)-classified TIL-high clusters with T and B lymphocytes (Figures 2A–2C, right). On the other hand, the TIL-low component was mainly localized in the stromal and malignant cell compartments. This analysis revealed distinct spatial separation of immune-infiltrated regions within the TME (Figures 2A–2C, right). We also found considerable variation in immune infiltration (TIL-high), confirmed by the cell counts and proportions across datasets: 10493 (59.8%) in Wu_2021, 822 (51.9%) in Xu_2021, and 8699 (54.4%) in Bhupinder_2021.

TIL-high cells exhibit distinct transcriptional programs and enriched immune pathways

In all three datasets, differential expression analysis between the TIL-high and TIL-low cells revealed distinct transcriptional patterns. With absolute log fold-change threshold of 1 and an adjusted p value cutoff of 0.05, we identified 138 upregulated genes in Wu_2021 (GEO: GSE176078), 70 in Xu_2021 (GEO: GSE180286), and 81 in Bhupinder_2021 (GEO: GSE161529). We then found 80 genes, listed in Table S5, that were consistently upregulated in TIL-high cells in a minimum of two TNBC scRNA-seq datasets (Figure 3A). We refer to these as “TIL signature genes” that potentially define lymphocyte subpopulations. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed strong associations with a host of immune-related biological processes such as “regulation of T cell activation,” “lymphocyte differentiation,” “leukocyte cell-cell adhesion,” and “T cell differentiation” (Figure 3B). These results support findings from previous studies by Savas et al.29 and Dushyanthen et al.30 that active lymphocyte differentiation and T cell activation processes within the TME are important markers of immunotherapy response and prognosis in TNBC. Moreover, it also confirms the findings of Mao et al.31 and Oshi et al.,32 which argue that active functional markers of lymphocytes, not simply counts of lymphocytes, represent a better measure of anti-tumor immunity. Thus, the signature genes appear to act as primary facilitators of a TME that is heavily populated by immune cells.

Subsequently, we employed pathway enrichment analysis utilizing the curated MSigDB hallmark collection of 50 established pathways. The significant immunological signatures pertaining to our TIL gene set, as shown in Figure 3C, ranked allograft rejection as the most highly enriched pathway (gene ratio: 0.45, FDR < 0.05). Other immune-related pathways, including IL2-STAT5 signaling, complement activation, TNF-α signaling via NF-κB, and inflammatory response, which further support the immunological potential of the TIL signature genes, were also significantly enriched. These results closely follow the observations of Oshi et al.33 who demonstrated that the allograft rejection signature is a strong surrogate for anti-cancer immune activity, which also provides a prognostic advantage specifically in TNBC patients, despite coexisting features of aggressive cancer biology. We also estimated the prognostic relevance of our TIL signature by using Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival analysis across independent TNBC cohorts comprising over 200 patients, using the KMPlotter tool. Patients with higher expression of the signature genes exhibited significantly improved 5-year RFS (log-rank p = 0.011; HR = 0.38; 95% CI: 0.17–0.82; Figure 3D). Similarly, in the TNBC METABRIC cohort (n = 276), high expressions of the TIL signature genes were associated with better RFS (log-rank p = 0.0017), and this result was still independently prognostic after controlling for age, tumor size, tumor grade, lymph node status, and chemotherapy (adjusted HR = 0.56; 95% CI: 0.37–0.83) (Figure S3A).

Validation of the TIL signature in large TNBC cohorts using bulk expression data

To assess the biological relevance and clinical utility of our 80-gene TIL signature, we aimed to validate the signature across multiple bulk RNA-seq datasets. This included comparisons with established immune classification frameworks, pathway-level immune correlation, and clinical response outcomes. Together, these analyses assess the robustness, specificity, and predictive value of the TIL signature genes in TNBC.

Complementary scoring strategies reveal robust and subtype-specific TIL activity in BC

We quantified TIL activity by using two complementary approaches: (1) gene set variation analysis (GSVA), a primary scale-invariant measurement using the TIL signature genes (hereafter referred to as the TIL-80 or TIL signature score), and (2) the mean expression of the same genes in each patient. GSVA computes sample-specific rank-based enrichment scores, using non-parametric techniques that quantify coordinated expression patterns within the gene signature. It is resilient to expression scale differences, batch effects, and outliers. By contrast, the mean expression provides a direct and interpretable measure of average TIL abundance, which is helpful for clinical translation and visualization. The strong correlation (Pearson’s r = 0.95) demonstrates the consistency between the two methodologies (Figure 4G; Figures S3B and S3C). However, their joint use mitigates any method-specific bias and the possibility that the observed immune activity is an artifact of any scoring algorithm. Substantial variation in signature scores was observed across BC subtypes, corroborating established differences in immune cell infiltration patterns.34 Across three independent datasets containing pre-treated bulk expression measurements from TCGA-BRCA,35 METABRIC,36 and GEO: GSE96058,37 TNBC demonstrated the highest immunogenic potential, exhibiting significantly elevated GSVA scores and mean expression levels compared with other molecular subtypes, as reported previously38 (Figures 4A and 4B).

The TIL signature is indicative of the amount of anti-cancer immune cell infiltration

Next, we investigated the relationship between the TIL-80 score and immune activity in the TME of BC. We found that the score was strongly correlated (Pearson’s r = 0.89–0.95, p < 0.001) with the cytolytic activity score (CYT) in all three cohorts (Figures 4C–4E), which reflects the immune system’s ability to destroy cancer cells, leading to better prognosis and response to immunotherapy.39

Next, we systematically characterized how the TIL-80 score captures immune cell infiltration across tumors by applying three complementary deconvolution frameworks, namely xCell, CIBERSORT, and ConsensusTME, across three independent bulk expression cohorts (TCGA, METABRIC, and GEO: GSE96058) (Tables S6 and S7). Both xCell and CIBERSORT displayed consistently strong positive correlations between the TIL-80 score and the infiltration of cytotoxic and effector lymphocyte populations, including CD8+ T cells, CD8+ central and effector memory T cells, activated/memory CD4+ T cells, T follicular helper cells, M1-polarized macrophages, and memory/plasma B-cell subsets (Spearman’s ρ: typically 0.6–0.9; p < 0.01 in most comparisons) (Figure 4F; Tables S8 and S9). Using an additional deconvolution framework, ConsensusTME, we observed similar results for the major immune compartments captured by that method, with strong positive correlations for CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, B cells, natural killer (NK) cells, and M1 macrophages across all three cohorts (Spearman’s ρ: approximately 0.83–0.98; Figure S3D). In contrast, endothelial cells showed only modest positive associations (ρ: approximately 0.32–0.39), whereas cancer-associated fibroblasts showed weak or inconsistent correlations (ρ: approximately 0.08–0.24), supporting that the TIL-80 signature primarily reflects immune-rich rather than stromal components. These findings were reproducible across all three datasets and confirmed independently by xCell, ConsensusTME, and CIBERSORT, illustrating the complex, dualistic nature of immune infiltration in BC and the presence of a mixed inflammatory environment characteristic of immunologically “hot” tumors.40,41

Notably, the deconvolution frameworks also showed that TIL-80 strongly correlates with global immune infiltration. The xCell “ImmuneScore” was highly correlated with TIL-80 across all cohorts (ρ ≈ 0.80–0.93; p < 0.001). Similarly, the CIBERSORT “absolute immune score,” which quantifies the total leukocyte content in a scale-independent manner, also exhibited strong correlations (TCGA: ρ = 0.81; METABRIC: ρ = 0.92; GEO: GSE96058: ρ = 0.87; all p < 0.001). Consistent with these findings, ConsensusTME also showed strong positive correlations between TIL-80 and both the ConsensusTME cytotoxicity score (TCGA: ρ = 0.88; METABRIC: ρ = 0.95; GEO: GSE96058: ρ = 0.94) and the ConsensusTME immune score (TCGA: ρ = 0.93; METABRIC: ρ = 0.97; GEO: GSE96058: ρ = 0.96; all p < 0.001), further supporting that the TIL-80 signature captures a highly immune-infiltrated and cytotoxic TME (Figure S3D).

We used different algorithms to check the reproducibility of immune cell subset behavior in different datasets. When examining the CIBERSORT results, positive correlations became evident with activated NK cells, whereas weak negative correlations could be observed with resting NK cells, suggesting a shift toward an activated NK phenotype in TIL-80-high tumors. For Th2-like signatures, moderate positive correlations were observed, while Th1-like signatures and neutrophils showed minimal or inconsistent associations. Systematic disagreement was observed between the two methods for dendritic cells (DCs), where xCell consistently detected strong positive correlations for both plasmacytoid and myeloid DCs. CIBERSORT, in contrast, estimated positive correlations only for resting DCs and weaker or negative correlations for activated DCs, consistent with the known differences in DC modeling between the two systems.42 Lastly, the overall immune infiltration, stromal content, and tumor purity were inferred using the ESTIMATE algorithm (Table S10). Across all three cohorts, the ESTIMATE ImmuneScore showed strong and significant correlations with the TIL-80 signature (TCGA: ρ = 0.91; METABRIC: ρ = 0.96; GEO: GSE96058: ρ = 0.93; all p < 0.001), entirely consistent with the xCell “ImmuneScore” and the CIBERSORT “absolute immune score.” The ESTIMATEScore, reflecting combined stromal and immune mixture, was also strongly correlated with TIL-80 in all datasets (ρ ≈ 0.76–0.91; p < 0.001), whereas tumor purity showed the expected inverse pattern (ρ ≈ −0.79 to −0.91; p < 0.001). In contrast, StromalScore demonstrated weaker yet marginally significant correlations across datasets (p value ≈ 0.03–0.09), indicating that high-TIL-80 score tumors exhibit both increased immune infiltration and modest stromal enrichment.

The TIL signature robustly captures tumor immunogenicity across diverse immune classification frameworks

We evaluated the immunological specificity of our gene signature by using established immune gene sets and log2-transformed RNA-seq data from pre-treated TNBC samples within the TCGA-BRCA cohort (Figure 5A).

We first utilized the pan-immune gene set enrichment scores curated by Thorsson et al.,43 which encompass several immune processes including cytotoxicity, macrophage polarization, cytokine signaling, and stromal activation. We calculated Spearman’s correlation between the TIL-80 score and 14 selected immune gene signatures (Figure 5B). The score showed strong positive correlations with leukocyte infiltration metrics (LIexpression_score), cytotoxic effector populations, Colony Stimulating Factor 1 (CSF1) response, regulatory T cells (Tregs), B cells, CD8+ T cells, DCs, M1 macrophages, general macrophage infiltration, and interleukin 13 response pathways, all consistent with its derivation from immunologically active tumors. Positive correlations were also observed with Treg and B cell signatures. This likely reflects their co-localization within organized immune compartments such as tertiary lymphoid structures commonly found in highly immune-infiltrated tumors.44,45

In contrast, only M2 macrophages showed a significant negative correlation with our signature, consistent with their immunosuppressive role in the TME. Several other immune markers, including NK cells, transforming growth factor β signaling (TGF-β_score_21050467), and the CHANG serum response signature, showed non-significant associations, indicating neutral relationships with these pathways. Together, these results confirm that the TIL-80 signature aligns with pro-inflammatory and cytotoxic immune features while being inversely associated with immunosuppressive and stromal programs, which support its utility as a robust indicator of tumor immune activation.

Then, we contrasted the TIL-80 score with a pre-existing model of TIL scores developed by Danaher et al.,28 which consists of 60 marker genes belonging to 14 immune cell types. We applied the same methodology as the initial study by correlating each cell type score with the pan-leukocyte marker, CD45 (PTPRC) transcript. We considered cell types where the Spearman’s correlation coefficient was greater than 0.7 for further analysis. The resulting “Danaher TIL score” was computed as the mean of the retained cell type scores, which quantified the extent of immune infiltration in each sample. We then created a scatterplot matrix to assess the agreement with the TIL-80 score. As shown in Figure 5C, the Danaher TIL score correlated strongly (Spearman’s ρ = 0.96; p < 0.001) with the TIL-80 score, supporting the use of our signature as a robust proxy for immune infiltration. The Danaher TIL score also closely tracked estimates of cytotoxic cell score (ρ = 0.88) and CD8+ T cell score (ρ = 0.64) in accordance with the signature being mostly active and in the enlarged effector immune cell compartments.

We implemented the strategy described in the section “scoring of immune-related gene sets” in “STAR Methods” and utilized the 17-gene immunity panel proposed by Yang et al.46 to classify TNBC tumors from the TCGA-BRCA cohort into “iStrong” and “iWeak” groups based on the median immunity score. We then measured the differences in the TIL-80 score for the “iWeak” and “iStrong” groups and, as shown in Figure 5D, found that the “iStrong” immune tumors had much higher TIL-80 scores (Wilcoxon p < 2.2 × 10-16), with differences in central tendency and shape of the distribution. These findings confirm that the TIL-80 signature strongly aligns with the established immune classification frameworks.

The TIL signature captures pre-existing immune infiltration and predicts chemotherapy responsiveness in TNBC

To determine whether immune infiltration activity, as measured with the TIL-80 signature, functions as a predictive indicator of the response to NAC in patients with TNBC, we examined its association with clinical outcomes in two independent bulk expression cohorts, GEO: GSE123845 and GEO: GSE106977. In GEO: GSE123845, TIL signature scores determined using mean log2 expression were significantly greater in pCR patients than in those with RD (Wilcoxon p = 0.0038, Figure 5F). This suggests a correlation between greater immune infiltration and more favorable treatment outcomes. In GEO: GSE106977, the violin plot (Figure 5E) also showed pCR patients as having a statistically significant increase in TIL scores (Wilcoxon p = 0.00063; Figure 5E), calculated as the mean log2 expression. Consistent with the mean expression-based analysis, the GSVA-derived TIL-80 scores also significantly distinguished pCR from RD tumors in both validation cohorts (GEO: GSE106977 and GEO: GSE123845; Figure S4). This suggests that the observed trends were not solely the result of a specific scoring method (mean expression vs. GSVA) and were, instead, the result of actual TIL activity in response to the 80-gene TIL signature. Hence, the TIL-80 signature demonstrated similar trends of immune activity and treatment response captured in both datasets, which reinforces its predictive potential in TNBC.

Validation of the TIL signature using single-cell expression data

The characteristics of our TIL signature such as cell type specificity and clinical relevance were further corroborated using multiple scRNA-seq datasets from primary TNBC tumors. These analyses focused on (1) determining the extent of immune signature enrichment within immune and non-immune cell compartments, (2) determining its predictive value for therapeutic response at baseline, and (3) analyzing signature score alteration patterns over time during treatment.

The TIL signature is enriched in pre-treatment immune cells from responders to immunochemotherapy

The pre-treated TNBC samples from patients enrolled in the NeoTRIPaPDL1 trial (GEO: GSE26691947), which evaluated the addition of an immune checkpoint blockade (ICB; atezolizumab, anti-PD-L1) to NAC, were analyzed to assess the activity of our 80-gene TIL signature at single-cell resolution. Scores were calculated as the average log-normalized expression of the signature genes per cell across four immune cell types (B cells, CD8+ T cells, myeloid cells, and NK cells). Violin plots displayed significant elevation in the TIL-80 scores in responders (R) in all compartments compared with non-responders (NR) (p < 0.001; Figure 6A). CD8+ T and B cells were the most enriched, confirming the activity of tertiary lymphoid structures and effector cytotoxic function. Overall, using single-cell data, we determined that our TIL signature effectively captures the immune activation state of pre-treated patients responding to ICB-based combination therapy.

Although bulk xCell analysis did not detect an association between NK cells and the TIL signature score, single-cell analysis revealed modest but significant NK cell enrichment in responders, underscoring the value of combining bulk and single-cell approaches (Figure 6A). This illustrates the disadvantages of bulk deconvolution in identifying cell-intrinsic signals. In contrast, single-cell transcriptional profiling captures cell-intrinsic transcriptional states that are masked by bulk deconvolution, illustrating the value of integrating the two approaches to identify immune signals more accurately.

The TIL signature distinguishes immune and epithelial compartments at single-cell resolution

We analyzed all 515 primary BC cells from the GEO: GSE7568848 dataset by quantifying the TIL signature score at single-cell resolution and additionally performed a targeted analysis on a subset of cells derived from TNBC tumors from the same dataset. Among the cell populations, the TIL gene signature was found to be expressed at significantly higher levels in immune cells than in the tumor and stromal cells (Kruskal-Wallis, p < 2.2 × 10−16; Figures 6B–6E; Figures S5A and S5B). T and B cells consistently exhibited high scores, followed by myeloid cells, whereas tumor cells showed markedly suppressed TIL activity, and thus validated the gene signature specificity. The results remained consistent when restricted to the TNBC cohort, comprising 153 single-cell observations. The immune-specific nature of our signature genes was further confirmed using an independent TNBC epithelial single-cell dataset, GEO: GSE13853649,50, which comprised 275 tumor epithelial cells classified into basal, luminal progenitor, and mature luminal subtypes. In all epithelial cell subtypes, we found uniformly low enrichment scores, with median values below zero and no statistically significant differences between the groups (pairwise Wilcoxon test; p > 0.6; Figure 6F).

Baseline immune activity inferred using our TIL signature predicts chemotherapy response

We analyzed single-cell profiles from TNBC tumors at three clinical timepoints: pre-treatment, mid-treatment, and post-treatment, during NAC, using the Kim et al. dataset. In pre-treatment cells, we observed a significantly higher TIL signature score (Wilcoxon p < 2.2 × 10−16) in the extinct group than in the persistent group, indicative of a strong baseline immune activation among responders to NAC (Figure 7A). Across the three treatment time points, we observed two distinct temporal patterns: extinct tumors showed high baseline TIL scores that declined over time, whereas persistent tumors remained uniformly low (Figure 7B). The same trend was observed when the analysis was restricted to CD45+ immune cells, indicating true immune resolution after clonal extinction (Figure 7C). Immune pathway activity predicted using GSVA followed the same pattern as TIL scores, where tumors undergoing clonal extinction (pCR) exhibited strong pre-treatment enrichment of interferon γ (IFN-γ) response, IFN-α response, allograft rejection, inflammatory response, and TNF-α signaling via NF-κB (Figure 7D). These signatures remained elevated at mid-treatment but declined post-treatment, showing immune contraction after tumor clearance.

On the other hand, chemoresistant tumors had very little pathway activation at all time points, showing the presence of an “immune-cold” microenvironment. A heatmap of GSVA scores and faceted line plots confirmed this immune trajectory (Figures 7D and 7E). These results align with those of the original study, which confirmed that post-treatment pCR tissues were dominated by normal stromal and immune cells, reflecting tumor clearance. However, while these findings align with prior observations, an important limitation must be acknowledged: Kim et al.’s longitudinal scRNA-seq dataset does not contain any peripheral blood measurements (e.g., absolute lymphocyte counts or CBC profiles); because systemic hematologic data are entirely absent from the published metadata, we cannot formally distinguish tumor-specific immune contraction from chemotherapy-induced lymphopenia. Our conclusions regarding the temporal decrease in TIL signature score, therefore, reflect only the intratumoral immune dynamics, and not systemic immune status.

Concordance between TIL-80 signature scores and immunogenomic TNBC subtypes

Next, we evaluated the discriminatory power of the 80-gene TIL signature in stratifying the molecular subtypes of TNBC. Given growing evidence51 that subtype-specific stratification leads to better therapeutic outcomes, we assessed how well TIL signature scores aligned with two widely adopted TNBC subtyping frameworks, using pre-treated TNBC samples from TCGA-BRCA and METABRIC datasets.

Using the TNBC type-6 classification from Lehmann et al.,52 we plotted the GSVA scores by using our 80-gene TIL signature across six molecular subtypes, namely “immunomodulatory” (IM), “mesenchymal stem-like” (MSL), “basal-like 1” (BL1), “basal-like 2” (BL2), “luminal androgen receptor” (LAR), and “mesenchymal” (M). For both datasets, we observed a significant elevation of our signature in the IM subtype (characterized by high immune cell infiltration leading to a better prognosis) as compared with the BL1 (characterized by high proliferation and DNA damage response), BL2 (characterized by growth factor signaling and myoepithelial markers), LAR (characterized by androgen receptor signaling and luminal gene expression), and M (characterized by epithelial-mesenchymal transition and motility pathways) subtypes52 (Figures 8A and 8C). The enrichment of the TIL-80 signature in the IM and MSL subtypes likely reflects the shared contribution of the TME to their transcriptomic profiles. Prior studies have shown that transcripts defining these subtypes originate from infiltrating lymphocytes (IM) and tumor-associated stromal cells (MSL), indicative of low tumor cellularity and strong microenvironmental influence.52,53

Next, we evaluated the TIL signature’s performance by using an alternative TNBC classification system. TNBC tumors were stratified into three immunity-based clusters (“immunity-low,” “immunity-medium,” and “immunity-high”) according to the immunogenic profiling framework of He et al.,54 utilizing 29 immune-related gene sets. Data obtained through both the TCGA-BRCA and METABRIC cohorts revealed significant enrichment of the TIL signature in the “immunity-high” cluster compared with both “immunity-medium” and “immunity-low” clusters (Figures 8B and 8D). TIL signature scores were the highest in the “immunity-high” cluster due to its characteristic immune cell infiltration and its correlation with good prognosis, thus reaffirming the gene signature’s predictive potential across the various TNBC classification schemes.

Robust feature selection and subsequent machine learning analysis identify a small set of TIL-based gene markers predictive of NAC response

In TNBC, the absence of residual tumor after NAC, referred to as pCR, is strongly associated with improved survival and offers a valuable early marker for assessing treatment benefit.55,56 On the basis of this, we hypothesized that our 80-gene TIL signature may contribute to chemosensitivity in TNBC patients treated with NAC. To test this, we developed predictive models to distinguish between patients achieving pCR and those with RD, using two publicly available bulk gene expression datasets for model training. Model performance was then evaluated on eleven independent external validation cohorts. Details of all derivation and validation datasets are provided in Table 1.

The model-building and evaluation framework comprised three components: (1) a multi-stage feature-selection pipeline to reduce the initial panel of 80 genes to a smaller subset of highly predictive features, hereafter referred to as Gselected, using the derivation datasets; (2) a model-building or internal-validation phase, in which the classification models were trained on the derivation data across varying feature-subset sizes derived from Gselected (i.e., the top 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, and 30 genes) to evaluate the impact of feature dimensionality on predictive performance; and (3) an external-validation phase, in which the trained models were applied to unseen independent test datasets to assess generalizability and evaluate the predictive value of the selected gene subsets across diverse TNBC cohorts.

The repeated cross-validation experiments, followed by consensus rankings (see STAR Methods: “feature selection and ranking pipeline), generated a set of 30 most stable and predictive features (Figure 9A). Upon evaluation of the derivation datasets, these features demonstrated strong predictive performance across multiple binary classifiers (Figure S6). Of all the classifiers that were examined, the ExtraTrees classifier had the best performance overall (see Tables S11 and S12). Specifically, the model yielded a mean sensitivity of 0.79 ± 0.085, specificity of 0.81 ± 0.074, area under the ROC curve (AUROC) of 0.87 ± 0.05, F1 score of 0.8 ± 0.06, Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) of 0.6 ± 0.12, and overall accuracy of 0.8 ± 0.06 across 50 repeated cross-validation folds (Figure 9B).

Additionally, the set of 30 features (Gselected) demonstrated consistent classification performance across the multiple independent validation datasets (n = 680) with different microarray technologies, clinical outcomes, and sample sizes. These datasets yielded an average AUROC of 0.77 ± 0.09, with a mean sensitivity of 0.73 ± 0.12, specificity of 0.8 ± 0.13, and F1 score of 0.74 ± 0.13, demonstrating a good balance of true-positive and false-positive predictions. The MCC, a robust metric ranging between −1 and 1 and accounting for class imbalance, averaged 0.55 ± 0.18, while the overall classification accuracy was 0.78 ± 0.10 (Table 2). Combined ROC curves across multiple test datasets are shown in Figure 9C. Permutation test p values and mean AUROC from Table 2 supported the statistical significance of these results, confirming that the observed model performances are unlikely to be due to random chance. Although several immune and proliferation-based signatures have been developed for TNBC, their generalizability across diverse patient cohorts and platforms remains an ongoing challenge.71,72,73,74 To address this critical unmet need, we show that our 30-gene marker, TIL-30, has comparable predictive power across four established gene sets (BA100, four-gene signature, TNBC-DX, and DetermaIO) predictive of pCR, using the same external validation analysis framework. Notably, across the 11 datasets, the 30-gene TIL signature achieved the highest mean MCC and F1 score among all evaluated gene sets in both upper-bound (MCC = 0.55 ± 0.19; F1 score = 0.74 ± 0.13) and fixed pipeline (MCC = 0.33 ± 0.20; F1 score = 0.60 ± 0.17) analyses (see STAR Methods: “comparison with other immune-related gene signatures”). In the fixed pipeline setting, TIL-30 yielded a sensitivity of 0.63 ± 0.13, specificity of 0.63 ± 0.15, accuracy of 0.62 ± 0.10, and AUC of 0.63 ± 0.10, while under the upper-bound configuration, it achieved a sensitivity of 0.73 ± 0.12, specificity of 0.80 ± 0.13, accuracy of 0.78 ± 0.10, and AUC of 0.77 ± 0.09. Other signatures showed comparable AUC, sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy, indicating that the superior MCC and F1 score of TIL-30 reflect more balanced and robust classification performance across heterogeneous patient populations, assay platforms, and cohort sizes (Tables S13 and S14).

External validation results using the top 30 highly discriminative features

For clinical outcome annotations, while some cohorts provided the pCR status, others lacked explicit pCR labels. In these cases, we leveraged alternative clinical endpoints, such as disease-free survival (DFS), event-free survival (EFS), and overall survival (OS) as surrogate markers of treatment response, enabling robust cross-cohort evaluation of model generalizability in TNBC. Briefly, the score was computed using the same set of genes, Gselected (mean Z score), and the samples were stratified into TIL-high vs. TIL-low by median split. Both Kaplan-Meier and Cox proportional hazards models were applied. In GEO: GSE21653, higher TIL-30 was strongly associated with better DFS (HR = 2.852; 95% CI: 1.486–5.476; p = 0.0016), while in GEO: GSE103091, lower TIL-30 was associated with worse OS (HR = 0.545; 95% CI: 0.309–0.963; p = 0.0367). The weak association for GEO: GSE103091 was expected given that OS is influenced by multiple post-treatment factors and is a weaker surrogate for immune infiltration. These results show that Gselected preserves biologically meaningful prognostic value, even in the absence of pCR labels (STAR Methods: “survival association analysis of Gselected in cohorts without pCR labels”). All external validation experiments were conducted using the fixed 30-gene feature set (Gselected) derived from the internal training data. Although different classifiers and sampling strategies yielded the highest performance across external datasets (Table S15), no model tuning was performed using external labels, ensuring a strictly independent assessment of generalizability. Further, among the external validation cohorts, several TNBC-restricted subsets had relatively small sample sizes (e.g., GEO: GSE50948, n = 16; GEO: GSE31519, n = 18; GEO: GSE21974, n = 32; GEO: GSE32646, n = 26; GEO: GSE140494, n = 22), while others had larger sample size (e.g., GEO: GSE20194, n = 71; GEO: GSE21653, n = 85; GEO: GSE103091, n = 107). For the smaller datasets, we explicitly report their sample size and confirm that their results were concordant with those from the larger cohorts, further supported by permutation-based significance analyses (Table 2).

To directly evaluate the impact of the platform- and preprocessing-level differences on model generalization, we next performed a systematic cross-dataset gene-expression distribution similarity analysis (STAR Methods: “cross-dataset distribution similarity analysis”). Using each dataset in its original published preprocessing form, the majority of cohorts demonstrated high gene-wise distributional overlap with the training set (median overlap, typically 0.78–1.00), with unstable estimates arising only in very small datasets (≤20 samples). Class-specific expression trends were also preserved, with direction consistency averaging 0.63 ± 0.08 across cohorts (Table S16; Figures S7–S12). Importantly, no batch correction was applied to maintain strict independence of external test sets. Together with the permutation-based robustness analysis (Table 2), these results provide strong evidence that the 30-gene signature generalizes reliably across heterogeneous platforms and clinically diverse validation cohorts.

Additional assessments of the 30-gene signature (Gselected) through enrichment analysis showed strong functional relationships with the immune system. GO “biological processes” showed “T cell activation,” “antigen receptor-mediated signaling,” and “negative regulation of viral processes” to be the most enriched (Figure 9D). Reactome pathway analysis highlighted important pathways such as the “immune system,” “adaptive immune system,” and “immunoregulatory interactions between lymphoid and non-lymphoid cells,” which confirmed that the gene set is highly enriched for immune activation mechanisms (Figure 9E). In order to evaluate the prognostic relevance of the 30-gene signature (Gselected), we investigated its association with RFS in two independent TNBC cohorts. In the METABRIC TNBC cohort (Figure 9F), patients were divided into high- and low-expression groups based on the median value as a cutoff. Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed that patients with high expression had significantly better RFS than those with low expression (log-rank p = 0.013; Figure 9F). The corresponding univariate Cox model captured this effect with HR = 0.63 (95% CI: 0.43–0.91; p = 0.0139). In the multivariable Cox model, with adjustments for age, tumor size, histological grade, lymph node status, and chemotherapy, the association was still significant, and the high TIL-expression sample group had lower recurrence (adjusted HR = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.45–0.91; p = 0.0126). This finding was independently validated using the KMPlotter tool on a separate TNBC cohort (Figure 9G), where a high expression of the signature was again associated with significantly improved survival (log-rank p = 0.0018; HR = 0.39, 95% CI: 0.21–0.72). These findings demonstrate the potential of Gselected as a prognostic biomarker for RFS in TNBC patients.

Both training and validation of Gselected made use of microarray datasets. Therefore, to determine the feasibility of the gene signature on data derived from RNA-seq platforms, we selected two distinct RNA-seq cohorts (GEO: GSE16388275 and GEO: GSE12384576). For both datasets, patients with pCR had a higher mean expression of the signature than patients with RD, as demonstrated by Wilcoxon rank-sum tests (p = 0.0038 and p = 0.0066) (Figures 9H and 9I). To further evaluate the signature’s compatibility with clinically deployable assays, we employed GEO: GSE185507,77 an 800-gene NanoString nCounter panel profiled from 115 Formalin-Fixed, Paraffin-Embedded (FFPE) TNBC biopsies obtained prior to NAC. Sixteen of the 30 signature genes were present on this platform, resulting in the construction of a reduced TIL-16 score (mean Z score across the 16 matched genes). Yet, TIL-16, despite the fewer genes, remained significantly correlated with treatment response measured using residual cancer burden (RCB). RCB-I patients had significantly higher TIL-16 scores than RCB- II/III patients (Wilcoxon p = 0.0030). Moreover, TIL-16 was confirmed as a statistically independent predictor of low residual burden, as each unit increase in TIL-16 raised the odds of achieving RCB-I by ∼19 times (OR = 18.9, p = 0.0199) through logistic regression. Although only 16 of the original 30 genes were measurable on the NanoString panel, the resulting TIL-16 score strongly correlated with histopathological TIL% (Spearman’s ρ = 0.74, p < 0.0001), and both metrics independently discriminated low versus high residual cancer burden (TIL-16: p = 0.0030; pathologist TIL%: p = 0.00068), further supporting the assay’s compatibility with clinically deployable platforms. These findings demonstrate that the signature maintains the predictive signal, even on a reduced NanoString-compatible subset, supporting its feasibility for FFPE-based clinical implementation.

Additional assessments to determine the biological consistency of Gselected in cohorts lacking pCR labels were carried out by examining its association with alternative clinical endpoints such as DFS and OS. As detailed in “survival analysis” in Figures S13 and S14 and Table S17, Gselected showed a strong and significant association with DFS in GEO: GSE21653 (continuous Cox: HR = 2.85, 95% CI = 1.49–5.48, p = 0.0016), indicating that higher immune activation predicts reduced recurrence risk. In GEO: GSE103091, where only OS was available, Gselected demonstrated a directionally consistent but weaker association (HR = 0.55, 95% CI = 0.31–0.96, p = 0.037), which is expected given the multi-factorial nature of OS. These results confirm that the immune-responsive biology captured by the signature extends beyond pCR and remains informative across multiple clinically relevant endpoints.

Construction of the PPI network, functional enrichment, and hub gene discovery from the marker signature

The protein-protein interaction (PPI) network for the 30-gene signature (Gselected) was imported into the STRING database. The MCODE algorithm identified one significant module of the PPI network, with an MCODE score of 11.39 (11 nodes and 148 edges) (Figure 9J). GO analysis showed that the 11 genes in the cluster were related to the antigen receptor-mediated signaling pathway, T cell activation, and B cell receptor signaling pathway (Figure S15). Reactome pathway analysis showed that the genes were primarily enriched in adaptive immune system and immunoregulatory interactions between lymphoid and non-lymphoid cells. The top 10 hub genes were identified using seven network centrality algorithms implemented in the CytoHubba plugin of Cytoscape. Following rank aggregation across all methods (description in STAR Methods), the resulting consensus hub genes were CD8A, CD27, KLRB1, CCL5, LCK, CTLA4, CD3G, LAG3, CD96, and CD7 (Figure 9K), representing the most functionally influential nodes within the 30-gene signature predictive network. GO analysis revealed that the genes were mainly involved in “T cell activation,” “T cell receptor signaling pathway,” “negative regulation of T cell differentiation,” “antigen receptor-mediated signaling pathway,” and “enzyme-linked receptor protein signaling pathway.” Further validation in three independent TNBC cohorts, namely GEO: GSE123845 (n = 93), GEO: GSE163882 (n = 90), and METABRIC (n = 115), revealed that the 10 consensus hub genes were significantly overexpressed in patients with favorable outcomes. Specifically, higher expression was observed in patients achieving pCR compared with those with RD in the GEO: GSE123845 and GEO: GSE163882 datasets, as well as in patients without recurrence compared with those with recurrence in the METABRIC cohort (Wilcoxon p < 0.05; Figures S16A–S16C).

Cross-dataset identification of core TIL-associated transcription factors

Using three independent TNBC single-cell datasets (GEO: GSE161529, GEO: GSE176078, and GEO: GSE180286), we reconstructed transcription factor (TF) regulon activity for the 10 hub genes derived from the Gselected and evaluated their behavior across datasets (Figure 10A). As illustrated in Figure 10B, ChEA3 integration of regulatory evidence from multiple sources resulted in a consistent set of high-confidence TFs whose target sets strongly overlapped with the hub gene module. From these, we restricted our analysis to the top 20 TFs ranked by ChEA3 mean rank and recurrence, highlighting a densely interconnected TF module (Figure 10C). To evaluate whether these TFs are functionally activated in TIL-high cells, we reconstructed genome-wide regulons for each TF from the ChEA3 primary library and quantified TF activity at single-cell resolution, using integrated regulon scoring (AddModuleScore from Seurat). All regulons showed significant activation differences (FDR < 0.05) between TIL-high and TIL-low cells across all datasets. Further, for each dataset, Cohen’s d values were binned into three effect-size categories (large: |d| ≥ 1; medium: 0.5 ≤ |d| < 1; small: |d| < 0.5). This yielded a compact summary table of regulon behavior across the three cohorts: Wu_2021 (GEO: GSE176078) contained 16 large, 2 medium, and 2 small effects; Bhupinder_2021 (GEO: GSE161529) contained 5 large, 7 medium, and 8 small effects; and Xu_2021 (GEO: GSE180286) showed no large effects, with 7 medium and 13 small effects. Notably, as stated earlier, the number and proportion of TIL-high cells varied across the datasets, indicating heterogeneity in the immune infiltration patterns across TNBC samples. Due to the stronger separation of TF activity and the larger TIL-high populations, we focused on Wu_2021 (GEO: GSE176078) and Bhupinder_2021 (GEO: GSE161529) for the main regulon activity visualization (see forest plots in Figures 10D and 10E).

(D and E) Forest-style plots of direction-corrected regulon effect sizes (Cohen’s d) for the top 20 TFs in (D) GEO: GSE176078 and (E) GEO: GSE161529. In GEO: GSE176078, a set of ten TFs—ZNF831, ZNF80, SP140L, IKZF3, SCML4, GFI1, AKNA, FOXP3, TCF7, and TBX21—exhibited large activation differences (effect size d > 1.5) between TIL-high and TIL-low cells, reflecting a highly activated immune transcriptional program. In contrast, GEO: GSE161529 retained a smaller subset of five TFs with large effects (ZNF80, ZNF831, SP140L, IKZF3, and SCML4) and seven TFs with medium effects, underscoring strong cross-dataset agreement on a core set of regulators despite quantitative differences in effect size. Pairwise comparison of the regulon effect sizes demonstrated strong concordance across the datasets, with particularly high agreement between GEO: GSE176078 and GEO: GSE161529 (Spearman’s ρ = 0.97; p < 0.05) and moderate-to-strong correlations with GEO: GSE180286 (Spearman’s ρ = 0.70–0.75; p < 0.05), highlighting the reproducibility of the TF activity. The complete TF-by-dataset effect size table, including category assignments (large/medium/small) for all 20 regulons, is provided in Table S18. Collectively, these results demonstrate the existence of a conserved, cross-dataset TF regulon structure that robustly defines the TIL-high phenotype in TNBC and validates the hub gene-derived regulons as stable markers of immune activation.

Discussion

TNBC is heterogeneous, and increasing evidence shows that an activated immune microenvironment influences chemotherapy response and improves outcomes under NAC.78 TILs are central to this process and are strong prognostic markers. Yet, most studies rely on bulk transcriptomic or cytolytic scores,2,32,33,79,80 which overlook the complexity of immune infiltration, lack resolution of cellular origins, and are rarely validated across single-cell and bulk datasets.

Using single-cell transcriptomic data, we computationally identified immune cell subpopulations and the 80-gene TIL signature within the diploid, microenvironment-enriched compartment of treatment-naive TNBC tumors. The signature reflects active immune programs and predicts chemotherapy response and RFS across independent datasets. Furthermore, differential expression analysis between the two groups led to the identification of an 80-gene marker list that was consistently upregulated across multiple TNBC single-cell datasets, in line with prior studies demonstrating that TNBC harbors the highest degree of immune cell infiltration and immunogenicity among all BC subtypes.32,81 TIL-high populations were enriched for immune-regulatory pathways such as allograft rejection, IL2-STAT5, and TNF-α-NF-κB.79,82 Enrichment of the allograft rejection pathway, consistent with prior reports,33 and higher expression of the signature were associated with improved survival.

Across bulk datasets, the TIL-80 signature correlated strongly with the cytolytic activity and the Danaher TIL score,28,83 captured effector and regulatory immune states, and was inversely associated with M2 macrophages.33 Elevated scores predicted pCR in two cohorts (p = 0.0038 and p = 0.00063).12,84,85 Temporal dynamics during NAC confirmed that responders had high baseline TIL activity that declined post-treatment, whereas resistant tumors remained immune-cold, supporting the role of pre-existing immunity in chemosensitivity.

Unlike prior signatures, often limited by small cohorts and platform bias,57,71,72,73,86,87,88,89,90 our multi-stage feature selection with cross-validation identified a stable 30-gene panel (Gselected). Validated in eleven external cohorts (n = 680), this represents one of the most comprehensive immune-based predictive frameworks in TNBC. Its robustness likely stems from its single-cell origin, capturing TIL-specific activity absent in bulk-derived signatures.

Network analysis of the 30-gene signature revealed a tightly connected immune module dominated by T cell receptor signaling. From this, ten hub genes—CD8A, CD27, KLRB1, CCL5, LCK, CTLA4, CD3G, LAG3, CD96, and CD7—emerged as key regulators.75,81,91 Several of these showed independent prognostic significance: low KLRB1 correlated with poor survival,92 while high LCK and CTLA4 predicted better outcomes and pCR.93,94,95 High LAG3 expression was also reported to be associated with favorable prognosis including pCR96 and better DFS97 in TNBC. Consistent with this, preclinical studies have shown that LAG3 frequently co-modulates T cell exhaustion pathways, alongside PD-1 and CTLA-4, with combinatorial blockade enhancing antitumor immunity.98,99 Therefore, these hub genes may serve both as biomarkers for stratification and as mechanistic indicators of immune response.

TF enrichment analysis established the regulatory relationships of the 10 hub genes, and the resulting regulon-scoring framework highlighted a systematic transcriptional program that underscores the TIL-high state in TNBC. Independent transcriptomic studies100,101 have shown that response to TIL-based therapies is linked to increased activity of exhaustion-associated and CD28-co-stimulated CD8+ T cell regulons, including RUNX3, EOMES, TBX21, NFAT5, IRF1, BATF, and members of the KLF and ETS families, together with increased activity of zinc-finger factors such as ZNF831 and HIVEP1. Complementing these findings, a separate reverse-engineering analysis100 of transcriptional control networks in BC stem-like cells identified a distinct immune-response regulon, driven by SCML4, ZNF831, SP140, and IKZF3, whose expression correlated with improved chemotherapy response and RFS. Notably, a substantial proportion of these response-associated regulators overlapped with the TFs we identified as strongly activated in TIL-high cells in our analysis: ZNF831, SP140L, IKZF3, TBX21, EOMES, FOXP3, AKNA, RUNX3, GFI1, and STAT4 all displayed medium-to-large regulon-activation effects in at least one of the single-cell datasets.

Importantly, high-effect TFs such as TBX21, EOMES, and RUNX3 reinforce cytotoxic CD8+ T cell identity, while IKZF1/IKZF3 stabilizes lymphoid differentiation. TCF7, an important regulator of memory and progenitor-exhausted CD8+ T cells, further underscores the lineage-specific regulatory activity supporting antitumor immunity. Regulators such as BATF and STAT4 contribute to the differentiation and cytokine-driven effector programs, whereas FOXP3 reflects the presence of immunosuppressive states within the TIL compartment. Strong activity of chromatin-immune modulators (SP140L, ZNF831, and SCML4) suggests an epigenetically maintained immune architecture consistent with prior observations in BC stem cells.100 Together, these regulon-defined programs align with the hub gene network and indicate that TIL-high tumors are driven by a coordinated, multi-factorial immune program that further explains their favorable therapeutic response.

Limitations of the study

Our study has several limitations. First, the retrospective nature of the cohorts and platform-specific variability across independent test sets may limit generalizability. Second, the identified TIL-high and TIL-low subpopulations were computationally derived from single-cell transcriptomic data and not validated by immunohistochemistry (IHC) or flow cytometry. Future work should, therefore, prioritize (1) analytical and clinical validation of the gene panel in prospective multi-center trials; (2) incorporation of spatial transcriptomics, multiplex immunofluorescence, and targeted perturbation assays to characterize the spatial organization of TIL-high versus TIL-low regions and define functional relationships among key immune-regulatory genes; (3) integration with routine pathology workflows; (4) functional studies to determine whether core signature genes modulate chemosensitivity, thereby advancing the signature toward real-world clinical utility; and (5) development and validation of the model using RNA-seq training cohorts or cross-platform harmonization approaches to improve generalizability.

Method details

Identification of tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte subpopulation using single-cell sequencing analysis

Single-cell data processing

To characterize a distinct subset of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, we analyzed three publicly available single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) BC datasets processed by Pang et al.,24 namely, Wu_2021 (GEO accession: GSE176078),25 Xu_2021 (GEO accession: GSE180286),26 and Bhupinder_2021 (GSE161529).27 We included only pre-treatment TNBC samples for our analysis, using the sample identifiers reported in the original publications.

To infer large-scale copy number variations from single-cell expression profiles, we employed the CopyKAT package (version 1.1.0),106 enabling us to differentiate between malignant (aneuploid) and non-malignant (diploid) cells, which was then carried forward for tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte subpopulation analysis. Furthermore, to validate that the “diploid” predictions by CopyKAT were enriched for non-malignant, microenvironmental (stromal and immune) signatures, we derived tumor purity scores using the ESTIMATE algorithm.103 Pseudo-bulk expression profiles were generated by calculating the mean expression of every gene across all CopyKAT-defined diploid and aneuploid cells. The resulting two-sample matrix—genes in rows, “Diploid” and “Aneuploid” in columns—was supplied to the ESTIMATE R package (v1.0.13). We utilized the estimateScore() with the default stromal and immune gene sets to derive StromalScore, ImmuneScore, and combined ESTIMATEScore for each profile. We then applied the clustering approach and processed the single-cell data exactly as Pang et al. described. The Seurat104 (version 4.4.0) package was used to process single-cell expression data. From each dataset, we removed cells with less than 200 detected genes or fewer than 500 total UMIs. We also removed cells with more than 20% mitochondrial reads to eliminate low-quality cells. We log-normalized the expression data after filtering out genes detected in fewer than 5 cells. We continued with downstream analyses in Seurat to normalize the data, select variable features, reduce dimensionality, and cluster the cells, visualizing the clusters in 2D (UMAP). We kept the cell type annotations for all three datasets the same as in the original manuscript.

Identification of tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte subpopulations and marker gene discovery

We combined canonical T cell markers (CD3D, CD3E, CD3G, CD4, CD8A, CD8B, GZMB, PRF1, PDCD1, TIGIT) and B-cell markers (MS4A1, CD19, CD79A, CD79B) into a single list, and extracted all cells expressing one or more of these genes from the CopyKAT-defined diploid subset. We then applied Seurat’s AddModuleScore() function using the combined marker list to compute a “TIL_Combined” module score for each cell, which was used to quantify and visualize the tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte population.

To partition CopyKAT-defined diploid cells based on this score, Gaussian mixture modeling (GMM) was performed using the mclust package107 (v6.1) in R. Mixture models with component numbers (G = 1–6) were fitted independently for each dataset. Model quality was evaluated using Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) values (Table S1). To assess biological coherence, we quantified the TIL purity and coverage of each GMM component using the original cell-type annotations (T cells, B cells, and plasmablasts labeled as “TILs”). For each model, we computed component-level TIL purity, TIL coverage (fraction of all TILs captured within TIL-dominant components), and a weighted score combining both metrics. Weighted purity × coverage scores, together with Adjusted Rand Index (ARI), Normalized Mutual Information, mean uncertainty, and silhouette width, were compared between the two-component model (G = 2) and the dataset-specific BIC-optimal model (G = 3–5). Detailed description of the individual metrics is available in the STAR Methods section titled “Quantitative assessment of GMM-derived TIL subpopulations.” This integrative analysis demonstrated that a two-component GMM led to the most biologically coherent segmentation. Consequently, the final G = 2 model was selected as the optimal configuration. Stability of the model was assessed using 100 bootstrap resamples, each refitting the GMM on a random 80% subset of cells (frac = 0.8) and comparing component assignments across runs via Adjusted Rand Index and consistency metrics. After stratification, the resulting binary labels (“TIL-high” and “TIL-low”) were added to the Seurat object’s metadata and projected onto a UMAP embedding using the DimPlot().

Quantitative assessment of GMM-derived TIL subpopulations

To evaluate the correspondence between Gaussian mixture model (GMM)-derived components and tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) annotations from the original studies, we computed quantitative metrics reflecting purity, coverage, entropy, and cluster quality.

Let N denote the total number of diploid cells analyzed, G the number of Gaussian components, and C = {1, … G} the component labels assigned by the GMM. For each component g∈C, let ng denote the number of cells in that component ng/N) = componentsize, and kg the number of cells labeled as TIL (T cell, B cell, or plasmablast) within that component.

Component-level purity

The TIL purity of the component g was defined as the fraction of TIL cells among all cells assigned to that component pg=kgng. A component was designated as TIL-dominant if pg≥τp and ng/N≥τs. τp = 0.75 (puritythreshold) τs = 0.03 (minimumsizethreshold).

Weighted purity

Overall purity of the TIL-dominant subset was expressed as the size-weighted mean purity across all retained components:

Pw=∑g∈C∗ngNpg,whereC∗={g|pg≥τp,ng/N≥τs} denotes the set of TIL-dominant components. This formulation ensures that large, high-purity clusters contribute proportionally more to the total.

TIL coverage The coverage of the TIL population by the retained components was defined as: Coverage=∑g∈C∗kgK where K=∑g∈Ckg is the total number of TIL-labeled cells in the dataset. This represents the fraction of all TIL cells captured by high-purity components.

Combined purity–coverage score: A composite index summarizing both purity and coverage: S=Pw × Coverage

Mean uncertainty or Posterior uncertainty for each cell was computed as 1-maxgpi,g, where pi,g denotes the posterior probability of cell i belonging to component g in the Gaussian mixture model. The mean uncertainty across all cells was used to assess the confidence of component assignments. Lower values indicate crisper boundaries between TIL-High and TIL-Low states, while higher values reflect overlapping or ambiguous memberships. Models with more than two components exhibited substantially higher mean uncertainty, consistent with over-segmentation of the same score distribution.

Other metrics: Silhouette width, mean posterior uncertainty, adjusted Rand index (ARI), and normalized mutual information (NMI) were computed by comparing GMM-assigned clusters with TIL (T and B cells) versus non-TIL cell annotations. Across all datasets, G = 2 yielded the highest silhouette width and ARI/NMI, as well as the lowest classification uncertainty, confirming that the two-component model provides the most stable and biologically coherent separation of immune versus non-immune populations. Higher-order models (G > 2) produced smaller or mixed clusters with declining stability metrics, reflecting over-segmentation of the same biological data. For each dataset, stability was evaluated using 100 bootstrap iterations, in which 80% of cells were randomly sampled without replacement, and the two-component GMM was refitted. The resulting posterior classifications were compared pairwise using the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI). The mean and standard deviation of ARI across iterations, along with global and median consistency measures, quantified the robustness of the clustering solution. The consistently high ARI (≥0.98) and perfect median consistency (1.0) confirmed that the G = 2 model is reproducible under resampling perturbations.

Bulk data acquisition and processing

Bulk gene expression and corresponding clinical data were acquired from four independent cohorts. The Molecular Taxonomy of Breast Cancer International Consortium (METABRIC) cohort36 comprising 1,903 patients and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)35 cohort of 1,069 BC patients were accessed as processed tabular data via the cBioPortalData package108,109 in R. Transcriptional profiles from 3,273 resected tumors in the Swedish Breast Cancer Analysis Network (SCAN-B) cohort37,38 were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (accession: GSE96058). Additionally, we incorporated a microarray dataset (accession: GSE10697768,69) containing expression profiles from 119 TNBC patients with documented pathological response rates (pCR) to chemotherapy to validate our TIL signature. For microarray datasets, probe-to-gene mapping was performed, and expression values from multiple probes corresponding to the same gene were averaged to generate consolidated gene-level expression data.

Additionally, to evaluate the association between our 80-gene tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) signature and chemotherapy response in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), we analyzed two pre-treatment bulk-expression cohorts, GEO: GSE12384576 and GEO: GSE106977.68,69 Samples were filtered to include only TNBC cases based on immunohistochemistry subtype annotations. For each cohort, we used the expression values reported in the original study and computed a TIL signature score for each sample as the mean log2 expression across the 80 curated TIL marker genes. Clinical response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy stratified patients into pathologic complete response (pCR) and residual disease (RD) groups.

Scoring of immune-related gene sets

TIL-activity was quantified across patients using Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA)119 as the primary, scale-invariant metric. In addition, the mean expression of all TIL-associated markers per patient was chosen as an intuitive and direct alternative for assessing overall marker abundance. Cytolytic activity (CYT) score83 was calculated using the geometric means of the genes GZMA and PRF1. To characterize the composition of the tumor microenvironment of the bulk samples, we applied the xCell algorithm,111 which infers the relative abundance of 64 immune and stromal cell populations within each tumor sample based on gene expression profiles. In parallel, we performed immune deconvolution using CIBERSORT110 with the LM22 leukocyte gene signature matrix, running 500 permutations in absolute mode to obtain robust estimates of immune cell fractions independent of sample-specific scaling effects. The use of both deconvolution techniques allowed us to assess the consistency of cell type characterization across signature-based enrichment (xCell) and support-vector–based mixture deconvolution (CIBERSORTx via the online tool https://cibersortx.stanford.edu/), thereby minimizing method-specific biases. We also applied ConsensusTME112 through the immunedeconv framework using the BRCA indication and the default ssGSEA-based setting to derive tumor microenvironment cell-state enrichment scores from the same bulk expression cohorts. For the Yang et al.46 gene set, we computed an immunity score for each TNBC sample in the TCGA-BRCA cohort by averaging the log2-normalized expression of 17 immune-infiltration-associated genes, and scaling the result as described in their publication (30 × [raw score +1.4]). Because nearly all TNBC tumors fall within the high-proliferation category defined in Yang et al., we stratified samples into immune strong (or “iStrong”) and immune weak (“iWeak”) groups based on a median split of this immunity score to capture intra-cohort immune variation.

Deriving TNBC subtypes using bulk expression measurements

We obtained Lehmann’s classification of TNBC subtypes using the TNBCtype tool.120 In addition, the classification strategy proposed by He et al.54 defines three immunogenomic subtypes: Immunity-High (H), Medium (M), and Low (L). We compiled the 29 immune-related gene sets described in the original study into a unified non-redundant gene list. Using a Gaussian kernel to compute single-sample enrichment scores, GSVA (Gene Set Variation Analysis) was performed on log-transformed expression data from untreated TCGA and METABRIC TNBC samples. The resulting GSVA scores were min–max normalized across all samples to generate a scaled immune activation score. We then performed hierarchical clustering on the scaled scores using Euclidean distance and Ward’s linkage. The dendrogram was cut at k = 3 to derive three immune subgroups. Subtypes were annotated as Immunity_High, Immunity_Medium, and Immunity_Low by ranking the clusters according to their median GSVA scores, from highest to lowest immune activity.

Differential gene expression and functional enrichment analysis

Differentially upregulated marker genes with an absolute logFC cutoff of 1 and an adjusted p-value of 0.05 were identified using the FindAllMarkers() in Seurat. The ClusterProfiler121 package in R was used to perform gene ontology (GO) analysis of the identified gene markers, and the corresponding Biological Process (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular function (MF) terms were reported. Fifty hallmark genes were downloaded from the Molecular Signature Database (MSigDB),122 and pathway scores were calculated for each cell using the AddModuleScore() in Seurat.

Identification of signature genes and construction of a prediction model based on chemotherapy response status

We analyzed bulk RNA sequencing data from patients with triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), each annotated with pathological response labels following neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC). The binary clinical response variable was encoded as pCR (pathological complete response) and RD (residual disease). Details of the microarray/RNA-seq datasets used in this study are outlined in Table 1. Raw expression files (CEL) were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO). The ReadAffy() from the “Affy” package113 was used to load the raw expression values in R, and the “gcrma” package114 was used to perform the robust multi-average (RMA) parameter method of normalization and background correction. For the microarray datasets, GEO: GSE25055, GSE25065, GSE20271, and GSE20194, the Affymetrix probes were matched to gene symbols using the Affymetrix Human Genome U133 Plus 2.0 (hgu133plus2.db). The average expression values of multiple probes that match the same gene were considered. The two derivation (GEO: GSE25055 and GSE25065) and validation datasets (GEO: GSE20271 and GSE20194) of the GPL96 platform were combined separately into large training/test sets. The resulting batch effect was removed using the ComBat() from the “sva” package123 in R. For the remaining validation datasets, probe annotations were matched to gene symbols using the corresponding platform-specific annotation packages based on the metadata information provided in GEO. Log2-transformed RMA-normalized expression values were used for all downstream analyses wherever available (Table 1). For specific validation datasets where explicit pCR labels were unavailable, we leveraged alternative clinical endpoints (such as event-free survival, disease-free event, and overall survival) as surrogate markers of treatment response, given that these datasets exclusively involved TNBC patients treated with chemotherapy. The normalized TPM count matrices of the RNA-seq datasets GEO: GSE163882 and GEO: GSE123845 were downloaded from the GEO database, and TNBC samples were selected based on the annotations provided by the authors.

Feature selection and ranking pipeline

To ensure robust identification of predictive features, we implemented a multi-stage feature selection framework embedded within repeated stratified cross-validation. Specifically, we used repeated stratified 5-fold cross-validation with 10 repetitions, yielding 50 independent train–test splits. All feature selection steps were performed strictly within each training fold to avoid information leakage. Because the training data were imbalanced, each split was first resampled using six complementary strategies (SMOTE, CCN, ENN, RENN, IHT, and RUS; Table S19). Feature selection was performed independently on each resampled dataset. Within each resampled training fold, three complementary selection methods were applied.

(1)LASSO logistic regression implemented using LogisticRegressionCV with an L1 penalty, the “saga” solver, 5-fold internal CV, max_iter = 10,000, ROC-AUC scoring, and adaptive search over inverse regularization strengths (C), effectively tuning the regularization parameter λ = 1/C. After fitting, features with an absolute coefficient magnitude |βj| > 1 × 10−5 in the final model were considered LASSO-selected. (2) Random forest feature importance, using a RandomForestClassifier with 100 estimators (n_estimators = 100, random state = 42) and default impurity-based importance scores, from which the top 30 features were retained and (3) Mutual information–based selection, using mutual_info_classif with default parameters, retaining the top 30 features with the highest relevance scores. Features identified by any of the three methods were pooled to form the candidate set for that fold. Within a given training fold, the candidate feature set was defined as the union of features selected by any of the three methods: feature_set = LASSOselected∪RFselected∪MIselected

LASSO logistic regression implemented using LogisticRegressionCV with an L1 penalty, the “saga” solver, 5-fold internal CV, max_iter = 10,000, ROC-AUC scoring, and adaptive search over inverse regularization strengths (C), effectively tuning the regularization parameter λ = 1/C. After fitting, features with an absolute coefficient magnitude |βj| > 1 × 10−5 in the final model were considered LASSO-selected. (2) Random forest feature importance, using a RandomForestClassifier with 100 estimators (n_estimators = 100, random state = 42) and default impurity-based importance scores, from which the top 30 features were retained and (3) Mutual information–based selection, using mutual_info_classif with default parameters, retaining the top 30 features with the highest relevance scores. Features identified by any of the three methods were pooled to form the candidate set for that fold. Within a given training fold, the candidate feature set was defined as the union of features selected by any of the three methods: feature_set = LASSOselected∪RFselected∪MIselected

In other words, the three methods are given equal status: there is no additional weighting among LASSO, random forest, and mutual information beyond their contribution to this union set. This candidate set was then refined using recursive feature elimination with cross-validation (RFECV) applied only to the training data of that fold, producing a final selected subset of predictive features for that iteration. Across the 50 cross-validation iterations and six resampling strategies, we recorded how often each feature was selected after RFECV. where countgm refers to the total number of times a feature was selected. We then defined a total consensus score for each gene as the sum of frequencies over all sampling methods: totalscoreg=∑mfreqgm

Finally, totalscoreg across the six independent resampling strategies yields a cumulative robustness score between 0 and 600, which was linearly scaled to the interval [0, 1]. This score constitutes the consensus ranking, where features with consistently high recurrence across diverse resampling distributions and selection criteria receive the highest robustness scores.

Model selection and tuning

Class imbalance poses a significant challenge to judge the efficacy of classification models. We applied multiple resampling strategies to balance the training dataset (Table S19) to address this. Hyperparameter optimization for eight binary classifiers was conducted using a 5-fold stratified grid search procedure, repeated 20 times. The AUROC scoring metric was used to assess model performance across the given range of grid parameters. The best_estimator_ argument in scikit-learn116 was used to extract the best-performing model for subsequent evaluations. Model performance was further evaluated using an additional set of top-ranked features of varying sizes (N = 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, and 30) to assess the impact of feature dimensionality on classification accuracy and stability. Our binary classifiers were evaluated using widely used metrics: sensitivity, specificity, Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), area under the ROC curve (AUROC), and F1 score.

To evaluate whether the immune gene signature was comparable across heterogeneous microarray platforms, we performed a cross-dataset distribution similarity analysis for each validation cohort. This included gene-wise assessments of distributional overlap and concordance in the direction of class-specific mean shifts relative to the training dataset. Full methodology, formulas, and cohort-level results are provided in the following section titled “Cross-Dataset Distribution Similarity Analysis”.

Dataset acquisition and preprocessing

Comparison with other immune-related gene signatures

To evaluate the performance of our 30-gene TIL signature, we benchmarked it against four independently published immune-related gene expression signatures selected for their established association with immune infiltration and therapeutic response in TNBC, and for the availability of well-defined gene lists in their manuscript. The BA100 transcriptomic classifier predicts chemotherapy response by integrating proliferation- and immune-related modules. The four-gene signature was developed using multivariable modeling of immune-associated transcripts linked to pathologic complete response after neoadjuvant therapy. The DetermaIO 27-gene panel represents a validated immune activation signature capturing interferon-γ signaling, antigen presentation, and cytotoxic effector pathways. The TNBC-DX 14-gene assay quantifies tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte activity and stratifies TNBC tumors across B-cell, T cell, and cytotoxic programs. For each gene signature, the exact gene list used in our re-analysis was extracted directly from the corresponding publication and harmonized to HGNC symbols. Genes absent from the GPL96-derived training dataset (GEO: GSE25055 and GEO: GSE25065) were removed prior to analysis. The complete, consolidated gene lists for each external signature used in our study are provided in Table S20.

To assess the predictive performance of each gene signature, we evaluated all combinations of sampling strategies and machine-learning classifiers using the GPL96-derived training cohort (GEO: GSE25055 and GEO: GSE25065). Model selection was performed exclusively on the training data using cross-validated performance to identify the optimal configuration for each signature. Two complementary evaluation strategies were implemented. First, an upper-bound performance estimate was obtained by applying, to each external dataset, the best-performing sampling-classifier pair selected for that dataset based on training-set cross-validation. Second, to enable an unbiased comparison across signatures, a single fixed pipeline (sampling-classifier) was selected using cross-validated performance of the 30-gene TIL signature alone using the training set and subsequently applied without modification to all signatures and all external TNBC datasets.

Permutation-based significance testing of classifier performance

To assess whether the observed classifier performance could be obtained by chance, we performed a permutation test on the final model derived using the top 30 gene signature. For a given training–test split, the model was first trained on the true labels using the full pipeline (class-imbalance correction, feature scaling, and model fitting) and evaluated on the held-out external cohort, yielding the observed test UCObs. We then generated a null distribution under the hypothesis of no association between the features and the labels by repeatedly permuting the training labels while keeping the feature matrices and test labels fixed. In each of B permutations (B = 500), the training labels were randomly shuffled, the model was refit using exactly the same settings (same 30-gene feature set, same resampling strategy, and StandardScaler normalization learned on the training data and applied to the test data), and the test AUCB was recomputed on the unshuffled test set. Network analysis and hub gene identification

The online STRING tool (version 12.0: https://string-db.org/) analyzed the intersecting gene markers obtained from the integrated analysis of a differentially expressed and manually curated list of genes. A combined score cutoff of at least 0.4 was selected as the interaction confidence, and the results were visualized using the Cytoscape software (v. 3.20.2). MCODE (v.2.0.3) was used to identify highly interconnected modules from the network (MCODE score ≥ 5). The top 10 hub genes were selected using seven centrality-based algorithms: MCC, Degree, DMNC, EPC, MNC, Betweenness, and Stress, implemented via the CytoHubba plugin in Cytoscape, and the consensus genes identified through rank aggregation were considered for further analysis.

Transcription Factor Discovery, Regulon Construction, and Cross-Dataset Activity Scoring

Identification of candidate transcription factors using ChEA3

To identify transcription factors (TFs) regulating the core immune activity in TNBC, we performed transcription factor enrichment using ChEA3117 (https://maayanlab.cloud/chea3/), which integrates regulatory evidence derived from multiple large-scale resources. The algorithm performs transcription factor enrichment analysis (TFEA) by quantifying the concordance between the input gene list and TF-specific target sets assembled from multiple sources such as ChIP-seq profiles from ENCODE, ReMap, and published datasets; co-expression networks inferred from GTEx and ARCHS4 RNA-seq matrices; co-occurrence patterns from thousands of gene lists curated in Enrichr, and perturbation-derived signatures measuring transcriptome changes following TF knockdown or overexpression. ChEA3 returns several ranking metrics; however, the mean-rank score has been shown by the authors to provide the best-performing prioritization of TFs across diverse biological contexts. We therefore used the mean-rank score to select the highest-confidence TF regulators for the 10 hub genes, which were themselves derived from the 30-gene TIL signature and represent the most topologically central immune markers in the TIL-high versus TIL-low differential expression network.

TF–target gene mapping and regulon construction

For each high-confidence TF identified by ChEA3, genome-wide TF regulons were obtained from the “Primary Library” section of ChEA3, which provides six curated gene-set collections in GMT format. These TF-specific target sets define the regulon for each transcription factor. Because our hub-gene set represents a high-confidence immune module, TF–target relationships were filtered to retain TFs that (i) ranked highly by ChEA3 mean rank and (ii) showed recurrence across multiple ChEA3 sub sources (ChIP-seq, co-expression, perturbation).

Quantifying regulon activity at single-cell resolution

To quantify TF activity across individual tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, we computed regulon activity scores using AddModuleScore (Seurat v4.4). For each TF, its regulon gene set was used as input, producing a cell × TF-regulon score matrix across all three scRNA-seq datasets (GEO: GSE161529, GEO: GSE176078, GEO: GSE180286). This matrix represents the per-cell transcriptional activity of each TF’s regulatory program allowing direct comparison of TF activity between TIL-high and TIL-low immune cells.

Differential regulon activity: Δ score and effect size

For each transcription factor, we quantified differences in regulon activity between TIL-high and TIL-low cells using two complementary metrics that capture both absolute and standardized shifts in activity. First, we computed the Δ(regulon score), defined as the difference between the median regulon activity in TIL-high versus TIL-low cells. Second, we measured Cohen’s d, a size normalized metric that measures the extent of separation of the two distributions (TIL-high vs. TIL-low). These separation measures, both Δ scores and Cohen’s d, were calculated for each dataset in isolation to prevent cross-dataset leakage, and to ensure that downstream integration and ranking represented true biological reproducibility, not mere technical noise. Cross-dataset TF ranking was based solely on the magnitude of the standardized effect size (mean Cohen’s d across datasets) (Figure 10A).

Survival analysis

We conducted survival analysis for the top gene predictors using two TNBC cohorts independently. In the first analysis, we used the online KMPlotter tool. In the “input multiple genes” field, we entered the gene symbols and selected “mean expression of genes” to generate a single curve for the 80-gene set. We selected ER, PR (IHC), and HER2 array status as negative and RFS as the clinical endpoint, resulting in a subset of 220 TNBC patients with survival information and expression values. The second analysis was conducted using the METABRIC TNBC cohort (N = 276), where patients were stratified into high and low expression groups based on the median expression of the selected top marker genes and adjusted for relevant clinical covariates available in METABRIC, including age at diagnosis, tumor size, histological grade, lymph-node status (positive vs. negative), and receipt of chemotherapy (yes vs. no). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis and univariate Cox proportional hazards modeling were used to assess the association between gene expression and RFS in both cohorts. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals were reported to assess the independent prognostic value of the 30-gene expression signature. Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log rank test. The survminer package124 was used to visualize survival curves with confidence intervals and risk tables.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.0.1 and Python version 3.14.0. R packages and software used for analysis included Seurat v4.4.0, CopyKAT v1.1.0, ESTIMATE v1.0.13, mclust v6.1, affy, gcrma, sva/ComBat, GSVA, xCell, CIBERSORT/CIBERSORT-ABS, ConsensusTME, survival, survminer, STRING v12.0, Cytoscape, MCODE, CytoHubba, ChEA3, and KMPlotter. Machine-learning analyses were performed in Python using scikit-learn v1.7.1, imbalanced-learn v0.14.1, NumPy v2.4.0, pandas v2.3.3, and related scientific-computing packages. Software versions and identifiers are listed in the key resources table.

All statistical details are provided in the figure legends, Results, main Tables 1 and 2, Tables S1–S20. Unless otherwise stated, tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For analyses involving multiple hypothesis testing, p values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery-rate method, unless otherwise specified. Exact p values are reported where available; p values below the numerical reporting limit of R are reported as p < 2.2 × 10−16. No formal prospective sample-size calculation was performed because this study used retrospective, publicly available transcriptomic cohorts.

For bulk transcriptomic, survival, and machine-learning analyses, n denotes the number of independent patients or tumor samples. For single-cell analyses, n denotes the number of cells unless explicitly stated otherwise; where relevant, the corresponding number of patients or tumor samples is also reported in the figure legends. Because cells from the same patient are not statistically independent biological replicates, single-cell p values are interpreted as cell-level enrichment statistics unless analyses were aggregated at the patient/sample level. For model evaluation, n denotes the number of patients/samples, while repeated cross-validation results are summarized across repeated train-test folds. For pathway-enrichment analyses, n denotes the number of genes or gene sets tested, as appropriate.

For data visualization, boxplots show the median as the center line, the interquartile range as the box, and whiskers extending to 1.5× the interquartile range unless otherwise stated. Violin plots show the kernel-density distribution of the plotted values, with embedded boxplots where shown. For performance metrics and model benchmarking, values are reported as mean ± SD across repeated cross-validation folds or across independent validation cohorts, as indicated. Heatmaps encode the stated quantitative metric, such as GSVA score, correlation coefficient, enrichment score, or regulon score; overlaid significance markers are defined in each corresponding legend.

Differential expression between TIL-high and TIL-low cells was performed using Seurat FindAllMarkers(). Differentially expressed genes were defined using an absolute log fold-change threshold of 1 and an adjusted p-value threshold of 0.05. Genes upregulated in TIL-high cells in at least two of the three TNBC single-cell datasets were retained as the 80-gene TIL signature. Gene Ontology, MSigDB Hallmark, and Reactome enrichment analyses were evaluated using FDR-adjusted p values, with FDR <0.05 considered significant.

TIL-80 signature activity was quantified using two complementary approaches: GSVA scores and mean expression of the TIL-80 genes. For comparisons between two independent groups, including pCR versus residual disease, responder versus non-responder, and iStrong versus iWeak tumors, Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were used. For comparisons across more than two groups, including molecular subtype, immune subgroup, or cell-type comparisons, Kruskal-Wallis tests were used, followed by pairwise Wilcoxon tests with Benjamini–Hochberg correction when post hoc pairwise comparisons were shown. Pearson correlation was used for comparisons between continuous signature scores where linear association was assessed, including GSVA versus mean-expression concordance and cytolytic activity correlations. Spearman correlation was used for rank-based associations, including immune-cell deconvolution scores, established immune signatures, and cross-dataset regulon-effect-size concordance.

For machine-learning analyses, n represents the number of independent TNBC patient or tumor samples. Internal model evaluation was performed in the derivation cohort of 170 patients/samples and summarized across repeated cross-validation folds, whereas external validation was performed across eleven independent TNBC cohorts comprising 680 patients/samples. Classifier performance was quantified using AUROC, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, F1 score, and Matthews correlation coefficient. Internal cross-validation performance is reported as mean ± SD across repeated cross-validation folds, and external validation performance is reported as cohort-level values and mean ± SD across validation cohorts. Cohort-specific performance statistics, including sensitivity, specificity, AUROC, MCC, F1 score, accuracy, permutation-test null AUROC, and permutation-test p value, are provided in Table 2.

For survival analyses, patients were stratified into high- and low-expression groups using the median expression or median signature score unless otherwise stated. Kaplan-Meier curves were estimated for relapse-free survival, disease-free survival, event-free survival, or overall survival, depending on cohort annotation, and survival distributions were compared using the log rank test. Cox proportional-hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios with 95% confidence intervals. Survival curves were visualized using survminer, with confidence intervals and risk tables where shown. For cohorts lacking explicit pCR labels, alternative clinical endpoints, including disease-free event, event-free survival, or overall survival, were used as surrogate response-related outcomes as described in Table 1 and the STAR Methods.

Permutation testing was used to assess whether observed classifier performance exceeded chance-level prediction. Observed AUROC values were compared against null AUROC distributions generated by label permutation, and permutation-test p values are reported in Table 2. No model tuning was performed using external validation labels; external cohorts were used only for independent performance assessment. Comparisons of signature scores between pCR and RD groups were performed using two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Exact sample sizes, endpoint definitions, statistical tests, p values, and effect estimates are provided in the corresponding figure legends and Table 2. For transcription-factor analysis, regulon activity was compared between TIL-high and TIL-low cells. Δ(regulon score) was defined as the difference between median regulon activity in TIL-high and TIL-low cells. Cohen’s d was used as the standardized effect size. Regulon effect sizes were computed independently in each dataset, and cross-dataset ranking was based on mean Cohen’s d across datasets. Spearman correlation was used to assess concordance of regulon effect sizes across datasets.
