Optimized lentivirus-derived virus-like particles for efficient delivery of Cas9-based genome editors
Authors: Boya Liu, Denise Klatt, Chad Harris, Meaghan McGuinness, Christian Brendel, David A Williams
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImplementation of therapeutic genome editing requires a potent, versatile, and transient delivery system to enable safe and effective in vivo applications. Here, we report on an optimized virus-like particle (VLP) platform for protein-based delivery of Cas9 ribonucleoproteins and Cas9-derived base editors and prime editors, termed LV-VLP-MA, that enables flexible editor deployment. By systematically engineering a panel of truncated Gag–Cas9 fusion variants, we identify a minimal MA-Cas9 configuration that maximizes editor packaging while effectively preserving efficient particle production and functional delivery. Systematic refinement of VLP production parameters enhances particle yield, supporting robust editing activity across diverse genomic targets. Importantly, systemic administration of LV-VLP-MA mediates efficient in vivo editing of the Pcsk9 locus with functional target suppression, establishing proof-of-concept for therapeutic application. Together, these results define a programmable, modular VLP-based platform that combines potency, flexibility, and transient delivery to expand the scope of in vivo genome engineering for therapeutic development.

Introduction

Programmable genome-editing technologies—including Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats-associated protein 9 (CRISPR–Cas9) nucleases, base editors (BEs), and prime editors (PEs)—have profoundly expanded the therapeutic possibilities for manipulating the human genome [1–8]. Their precision and versatility now allow correction of pathogenic variants, modulation of gene expression, and selective elimination of disease-associated cells across diverse preclinical models [9–19]. As these tools continue to advance in efficiency and specificity, the central barrier to clinical translation remains the development of delivery systems that can efficiently transport genome-editing ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes into primary human cells and target tissues safely, with high potency, and optimally in a transient fashion [20–22]. Integrating viral vectors such as lentiviral vectors offer robust delivery but depend on the transfer and genomic integration of nucleic acids encoding the editor, resulting in prolonged enzyme expression and potential off-target risks [23–27]. Adeno-associated virus-based vectors enable efficient delivery to non-dividing cells and are typically regarded as non-integrating; however, rare but detectable integration events have been reported, particularly in the context of genome editing [28–31]. In contrast, non-viral modalities such as electroporation, lipid nanoparticles, and polymeric carriers, which enable transient delivery, often suffer from significant cytotoxicity, poor performance in primary cells, or limited biodistribution in vivo [20, 32–35].

To address these limitations, engineered virus-like particles (eVLPs) and other enveloped protein-delivery systems have been developed to exploit the efficiency of viral assembly pathways without delivering genetic material. Early work demonstrated that retroviral structural proteins could be reprogrammed to package and deliver CRISPR–Cas9 RNPs into mammalian cells [36–41]. Building on this foundation, a wide array of platforms have been developed that incorporate optimized Gag–cargo linkers, protease-cleavable motifs, and capsid engineering to enhance protein loading and release [42–44]. Related platforms include eVLPs and enveloped delivery vehicles (EDVs), which similarly exploit viral assembly pathways to package and deliver genome-editing proteins but differ in structural origin, particle architecture, and cargo-loading strategies. Among these systems, lentivirus-derived VLP platforms have been developed by leveraging the HIV-1 Gag/Gag-Pol assembly pathway, in which genome-editing proteins are fused to the Gag polyprotein to enable incorporation of Cas9 RNPs during particle assembly [45]. Within lentivirus-derived VLP systems, Gag architecture has emerged as an important determinant of cargo incorporation and functional delivery. In this context, modifications such as truncation or domain-specific fusion strategies provide a framework for improving editor packaging efficiency. EDVs have further expanded this concept by incorporating cell-targeting ligands, achieving in vivo editing of human immune cells following systemic administration [46]. Lentivirus-derived nanoparticles have similarly demonstrated efficient protein loading and delivery of large genome editors, including BEs and PEs that can support transient genome modification both ex vivo and in vivo [47, 48]. Collectively, these advances establish protein-loaded VLPs as a rapidly maturing strategy for potent, precise, and integration-free genome editing.

Despite this progress, substantial challenges continue to limit the broad applicability of existing VLP systems. Efficient incorporation of large genome-editing enzymes remains highly sensitive to the VLP architecture, including the length of the Gag–editor fusion, with small design variations producing large differences in particle yield, protein loading, and functional potency [42, 44, 48]. Several of the most efficient constructs rely on evolved or specialized capsids generated through directed evolution, which can improve packaging efficiency and transduction but may restrict flexibility across editor classes or complicate scalable manufacturing [43]. From an application standpoint, robust editing in primary human cells, particularly hematopoietic stem progenitor cells (HSPCs), remains difficult to reliably achieve [36, 48–50]. Likewise, although in vivo genome editing has been demonstrated across multiple VLP frameworks, consistent tissue specificity, dose responsiveness, and functional protein-level outcomes remain areas requiring further optimization.

Here, we develop an optimized lentivirus-derived VLP system engineered to address these challenges. By anchoring genome editors to the Gag matrix (MA) domain and generating a shortened Gag–Cas9 fusion variant, we demonstrate substantially increased editor incorporation without compromising particle yield. This optimized architecture, termed LV-VLP-MA, supports efficient and dose-dependent delivery of Cas9 RNPs, adenine BEs, and PEs. The platform achieves robust editing across multiple genomic loci in human primary CD34⁺ HSPCs and maintains consistent performance across constructs of varying sizes and stoichiometries. Furthermore, as proof-of-principle systemic administration of LV-VLP-MA results in potent, liver-specific in vivo editing of Pcsk9, accompanied by marked reductions in serum Pcsk9 protein and no detectable histopathological toxicity.

Together, these results establish LV-VLP-MA as a lentivirus-derived VLP platform for efficient, transient delivery of genome-editing proteins. By optimizing Gag–Cas9 fusion architecture within the existing lentiviral assembly framework, rather than eliminating Gag-Pol dependency, this system provides a generalizable framework for protein-based genome editor delivery. The performance of LV-VLP-MA across multiple editor classes, primary human cells, and in vivo contexts highlights its potential as a broadly useful RNP delivery system for therapeutic genome engineering.

Materials and methods

Plasmid construction

The plasmid encoding Gag–Cas9 was ordered from Addgene (no. 171060), while the 3xNES-2x p53-NLS fragment was ordered from Twist Bioscience (San Francisco, CA). An 700-bp fragment consisting of 3xNES-2x p53-NLS was inserted into the GAG–Cas9 plasmid (no. 171060) digested with MfeI-HF and AgeI-HF using HiFi DNA Assembly (New England Biolabs). The resulting plasmid, named LV-gag-Cas9-NES-NLS, where “LV” refers to the lentiviral Gag origin of the construct, was digested with XhoI and SphI, and the fragment CA-P2-NES or CA-P2-mut-NES (Twist Bioscience) inserted to generate LV-gag-p2-Cas9-NES-NLS and LV-gag-p2mut-Cas9-NES-NLS. To generate the P2mut variant, amino acid substitutions were introduced at the HIV-1 protease cleavage site. Specifically, the original sequence “PATIM↓IQKG” was mutated to “SATIM↓MQRG”, where the arrow indicates the protease cleavage site [51]. LV-gag-Cas9-NES-NLS was digested with EcoRI, XhoI and SphI, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) fragments derived from the same plasmid amplified with primers (Forward1: 5′-GTCTCATCATTTTGGCAAAG-3′, Reverse1: 5′-AGCCGCCAGAGCTCATTGCTTCAGCCAAAA-3′, and Forward2: 5′-AGCAATGAGCTCTGGCGGCTCACTTCAACT-3′, Reverse2: 5′-ATCGGATAGTTCTGGCTAAC-3′) were inserted via HiFi DNA Assembly to generate LV-gag-CA-Cas9-NES-NLS. LV-gag-Cas9-NES-NLS was digested with EcoRI, XhoI and SphI, PCR fragments derived from the same plasmid amplified with primers (Forward1: 5′-GTCTCATCATTTTGGCAAAG-3′, Reverse1: 5′-AGCCGCCAGAGTTCTGCACTATAGGGTAAT-3′, and Forward2: 5′-AGTGCAGAACTCTGGCGGCTCACTTCAACT-3′, Reverse2: 5′-ATCGGATAGTTCTGGCTAAC-3′) were inserted via HiFi DNA Assembly to generate LV-gag-MA-Cas9-NES-NLS.

Cell culture

HEK293T cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA) supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum (Summerlin Scientific, Las Vegas, NV) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA). Jurkat and MEL cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium (Cytiva) supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum (Summerlin Scientific, Las Vegas, NV), 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA).

LV-VLP production

LV-VLPs were produced by seeding 3.5–4 million HEK293T cells into 10 cm tissue culture dishes and transfecting the cells the following day with 2 μg VSV-G glycoprotein (VSV-G), 6.7 μg Gag–Cas9, 3.3 μg Gag-pol (psPax2), and 10 μg U6-single guide RNA (sgRNA) using linear polyethylenimine (PEI) (Polysciences, Warrington, PA) at a 3:1 PEI : plasmid ratio (by weight in µg). Plasmids were mixed with 1 ml DMEM and PEI, incubated for 15–20 min at room temperature, and added dropwise to the culture dish. The medium was changed 14 h later and virus supernatants were collected 48h post-transfection, filtered through a 0.45-mm PES membrane (Millipore Sigma, Burlington, MA), and then concentrated by ultracentrifugation at 23 000 rpm for 2 h at 4°C in a Beckmann XL-90 centrifuge with SW-28 swinging buckets. Engineered delivery vehicles (EDVs, Addgene 201914) [37], ENVLPE⁺ particles (Addgene 232427, 232430, 232438) [42], and eVLPs (Addgene 181752) [44] were produced using the same method as described earlier, and the corresponding plasmids were obtained from Addgene (Watertown, MA).

LV-VLP quantification and characterization

LV-VLPs were quantified and characterized using a combination of biochemical, biophysical, and functional assays. Functional titers (TU/ml) were determined based on target gene knockout efficiency measured by flow cytometry in transduced Jurkat cells or by sequence-based quantification using Sanger sequencing, depending on the assay format. For flow cytometry-based functional titration, 200k Jurkat cells were transduced with 10, 2.5, or 0.625 µl of LV-VLP. Four days after transduction, cells were stained with a PE-conjugated anti-B2M antibody, and the reduction in B2M surface expression was quantified by flow cytometry to calculate the functional titer, which was derived from the fraction of B2M-disrupted cells normalized to the number of target cells and the volume of lentivirus-derived virus-like particles (LV-VLPs) added. The p24 antigen concentration was measured using a commercial p24 ELISA kit (XpressBio, Frederick, MD) according to the manufacturer’s instructions to estimate total viral particle yield, and the Cas9 protein concentration within LV-VLPs was quantified using a Cas9-specific ELISA kit (XpressBio). Particle size distribution and concentration were analyzed by nanoparticle tracking analysis (ZetaView, software version 8.05.16 SP3, Meerbusch, Germany).

Transduction of human CD34+ cells

Human CD34+ HSPCs from the mobilized peripheral blood of anonymized three independent healthy donors were obtained from the Fred Hutchinson Cancer Research Center, Seattle, Washington. CD34+ cells were pre-stimulated for 24 h in Stem Cell Growth Media (CellGenix, Portsmouth, NH, USA) supplemented with 100 ng/ml each of stem cell factor, FMS-like tyrosine kinase 3 ligand, and thrombopoietin, all from Peprotech (Rocky Hill, NJ, USA). 50k CD34+ cells were then enumerated and transduced with the LV-VLPs at the indicated MOI for 24 h, then the medium was replaced with fresh culture medium. Five days post-transduction, cells were collected for flow cytometry and genomic DNA extraction.

Flow cytometry assay

Cells were collected and washed with 1 ml phosphate buffered saline (PBS), then stained with B2M-PE (clone 2M2, BioLegend, San Diego, CA), CD45-APC (clone HI30, BioLegend), CD44-PE-Cy7(clone IM7, BioLegend), CD29-APC (clone MAR4, BD Biosciences, WUborn, MA), CD47-FITC (clone miap301, BioLegend), and viability dye (Fixable Viability Dye eFluor™ 780, eBioscience San Diego, California) antibodies at 4°C for 20 min at dilutions recommended by the manufacturer. Then cells were washed with 1 ml PBS and analyzed by flow cytometry (LSRFortessa, BD Biosciences).

Amplicon sequencing

Gene editing efficiencies on the genomic level were determined by quantifying the presence of insertions or deletions at the Cas9 cleavage site using Sanger sequencing and subsequent analysis with Synthego’s Inference of CRISPR Edits (ICE) program (https://ice.synthego.com). Cells were pelleted and genomic DNA was extracted using the QIAGEN DNeasy protocol. An amplicon containing the target sequence was amplified via PCR with Q5 polymerase (New England Biolabs) and the resulting sample was purified by QIAGEN QIAquick PCR Purification Kit. PCR amplicons were analyzed via Sanger sequencing (Quintara Biosciences, Cambridge, MA) and the resulting traces were deconvolved with Synthego’s ICE program. For selected samples, PCR amplicons were additionally analyzed by deep amplicon sequencing using the Premium PCR service provided by Plasmidsaurus (Louisville, KY). Raw sequencing reads were aligned to the reference sequence to quantify wild-type (WT), edited, and indel-containing alleles. For base editing, edited alleles were further classified to identify bystander edits within the editing window. For prime editing, sequencing data were used to quantify intended edits and indel frequencies. The primers used for amplification are as follows:

B2M: Forward: 5′-ACAGACAGCAAACTCACCCA-3′, Reverse: 5′-GAAGTCACGGAGCGAGAGAG-3′

CD29: Forward: 5′-ACACTTGGGTCAGTTCTGGG-3′, Reverse: 5′-AGCAGGATGAGGAGGAGAGG-3′

CD44: Forward: 5′-TCCTAGCCAGAAGTGGGTGT-3′, Reverse: 5′-AGGAGGGCTCTCCCCAAAAT-3′

CD45: Forward: 5′-AGTACATGCAAGTCCTGCAC-3′, Reverse: 5′-GTGGTCATCTTTCTTCCACA-3′

CD47: Forward: 5′-CCTTTCCAGGATCAGCTCAG-3′, Reverse: 5′-CTGGGACTTGTCATCCCTTG-3′

Pcsk9: Forward: 5′-CAGGCGTCCAGTACCCACAC-3′, Reverse: 5′-GGCTGCACTTAGAGACCACC-3′

Western

LV-VLPs were lysed in 4X Laemmli sample buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.0, 2% sodium dodecyl sulfate, 10%(v/v) glycerol, 2 mM dithiothreitol) by heating at 95°C for 15 min. Samples were run on NuPAGE 3%–8% Tris-Acetate gel (Thermo Fisher Scientific) prior to transfer onto a methanol-soaked polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF, Bio-Rad, Waltham, MA) membrane. The membrane was blocked for 1 h at room temperature with rocking in blocking buffer: 5% non-fat milk in Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween 20 (TBST) (150 mM NaCl, 0.5% Tween-20, and 50 mM Tris–HCl). After blocking, the membrane was incubated overnight at 4°C with rocking with mouse anti-Cas9 (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA; 14 697, 1:1000 dilution). The membrane was washed three times with 1× TBST for 5 min each time at room temperature, then incubated with goat anti-mouse antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, 91 196, 1:5000 dilution in blocking buffer) for 1 h at room temperature with rocking. The membrane was washed as before and imaged.

LV-VLPs-packaged sgRNA extraction and RT-qPCR

LV-VLPs were treated with DNase (Qiagen; 79254) followed by RNA extraction using the QIAmp Viral RNA Mini Kit (Qiagen; 52904) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Extracted RNA was reverse transcribed using SuperScriptTM IV First-Strand Synthesis SuperMix (Thermo Fisher Scientific; 18091050) and an sgRNA-specific DNA primer (see Supplementary Table S1) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. RT-qPCR analysis of the resulting complementary DNA was performed with SYBR green dye (Applied Biosystems, A25742) with primers. The relative LV-VLP-packaged sgRNA abundance was calculated as log2[fold change] (ΔCt).

In vivo experiments

All animal experiments were approved by the Boston Children’s Hospital Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. C57BL/6J mice were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME; stock 026 622). LV-VLPs were delivered to 4- to 6-week-old female C57/BL6 mice (n = 3-6) intravenously via retro-orbital injection. The injection volume was adjusted to 150 µl per mouse with PBS, and untreated mice receiving PBS served as controls. One week after treatment, all mice were euthanized, and peripheral blood, bone marrow, and organs (liver, spleen) were collected. Peripheral blood was centrifuged to obtain serum, which was stored at –80°C for subsequent PCSK9 analysis. Liver tissue was divided into two portions: one portion was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histological analysis, and the remaining liver tissue, together with other harvested organs, was used for genomic DNA extraction to assess genome editing efficiency.

Serum Pcsk9 measurements

To track serum levels of PCSK9, blood was collected using a submandibular bleed in a serum separator tube. Serum was separated by centrifugation at 2000 × g for 15 min and stored at −80°C. PCSK9 levels were determined by ELISA using the Mouse Proprotein Convertase 9/PCSK9 Quantikine ELISA Kit (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The serum samples were diluted with the Calibrator diluent at a ratio of 1:200 and then added to the microplate wells coated with capture antibody in advance and bound for 2 h onto microplate wells. The wells were washed three times with the washing buffer for 5 min each. The samples were then sequentially incubated with the PCSK9 conjugate followed by the PCSK9 substrate solution, with the washing process repeated three times between each step. Finally, the content of PCSK9 in serum was evaluated using a microplate reader by measuring absorbance at 450 nm, and concentrations were determined from the standard curve.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All of the data are reported as mean ± SD unless otherwise stated. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). The statistical significance between two averages was established using the unpaired t-test. When the statistical significance between three or more averages were evaluated, a one-way analysis of variance was applied, followed by multiple paired comparisons for normally distributed data (Tukey test). All of the statistical tests were two-tailed; statistical significance differences are indicated with asterisks (*P < .05, **P < .01, ***P < .005), and ns denotes P > .05.

Results

Optimization of Gag–Cas9 fusion for efficient LV-VLP packaging

Building on recent advances in eVLP systems designed for transient and non-integrative delivery of Cas9-based genome editors as RNPs, we sought to engineer an optimized LV-VLP platform to enable efficient Cas9 protein delivery. To this end, we constructed LV-VLPs by co-transfecting HEK293T cells with four plasmids encoding Gag–Cas9 fusion protein, Gag–Pol, and envelope plasmid (VSV-G), together with a B2M-targeting sgRNA (Fig. 1A). Cas9 was fused to the Gag polyprotein to facilitate its incorporation into budding particles through natural virion assembly pathways. To benchmark our LV-VLP design against the previously described eVLPs [44] and EDVs [37], which were generated according to the published constructs described in those studies, we first compared particle production and editing efficiencies. All LV-VLP, eVLP, and EDV production systems produced VLPs with comparable functional titers (107–108 TU/ml; Supplementary Fig. S1A), indicating that efficient particle assembly can be achieved across these distinct VLP architectures despite differences in scaffold design and cargo-loading strategy. We next transduced primary human CD34⁺ hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells with varying doses of eVLPs, EDVs, or LV-VLPs loaded with the same Cas9-sgRNA complex targeting B2M. Flow cytometry analysis revealed no significant difference in B2M knockout percentages between cells treated with eVLPs, EDVs, and LV-VLPs across a wide dose range (Supplementary Fig. S1B and C), with Supplementary Fig. S1C providing a fitted visualization of the overall dose–response trend across the three delivery systems, demonstrating that the LV-VLP platform achieves genome-editing efficiencies equivalent to those of the benchmark eVLP and EDV systems. To further address potential biases arising from functional titer-based MOI normalization, we also plotted editing efficiency as a function of LV-VLP preparation volume, which yielded consistent trends across all platforms (Supplementary Fig. S1D). Cell viability remained high (>95%) and was comparable across all conditions at the highest dose tested, indicating that differences in editing outcomes were not confounded by cytotoxicity (Supplementary Fig. S1E). Representative flow cytometry gating strategy is shown in Supplementary Fig. S2. These results establish a robust and scalable lentiviral framework for Cas9 RNP delivery that matches the efficiency of the established eVLP and EDVs systems, providing a flexible backbone for further structural optimization of Gag–Cas9 fusion designs. Notably, this LV-VLP framework shares the same underlying lentiviral Gag/Gag-Pol assembly architecture as previously reported EDV-based systems, and therefore should be considered an engineering optimization of Cas9 incorporation and processing.

We next focused on optimizing the Gag–Cas9 fusion configuration to maximize Cas9 incorporation and functional delivery. We generated a series of truncated Gag–Cas9 constructs of decreasing size, including variants with Cas9 fusions to the MA, capsid (CA), or spacer peptide 2 (P2) regions of Gag, as well as a P2-cleavage-site mutant (P2mut). The P2mut variant was designed based on reported HIV-1 protease cleavage-site mutations that differ from the P2 sequence in the Gag construct and are known to alter processing efficiency, in which the original sequence “PATIM↓IQKG” was mutated to “SATIM↓MQRG” [51], allowing us to assess the effect of modified P2–nucleocapsid (P2-NC) cleavage on Cas9 incorporation. These constructs included LV-gag-Cas9-NES-NLS (6060 bp), LV-gag-P2-Cas9-NES-NLS (5706 bp), LV-gag-P2mut-Cas9-NES-NLS (5706 bp), LV-gag-CA-Cas9-NES-NLS (5664 bp), and LV-gag-MA-Cas9-NES-NLS (4971 bp) (Fig. 1B), sequences are shown in Supplementary Table S2, these constructs were co-expressed with the standard gag/pol and envelope plasmids in HEK293T cells to produce LV-VLPs (LV-VLP, LV-VLP-P2, LV-VLP-P2mut, LV-VLP-CA, and LV-VLP-MA) for systematic comparison. Initial characterization showed that all variants produced comparable functional titers (Supplementary Fig. S3A) and particle concentrations (Supplementary Fig. S3B), with no significant difference in size distribution (Supplementary Fig. S3C). These findings indicate that truncation of the Gag–Cas9 fusion did not impair LV-VLP assembly or secretion.

We next evaluated the functional genome-editing activity of each construct on CD34+ cells. When applied to deliver B2M-targeting sgRNA, LV-VLP-MA achieved the highest B2M knockout efficiency, reaching up to ∼40% indel frequency on CD34+ cells, whereas other variants produced markedly lower editing levels (Fig. 1C and D). Importantly, there was a clear dose-dependent relationship, where increasing amounts of LV-VLPs resulted in progressively higher editing efficiency (Fig. 1C and D), with Fig. 1D providing a fitted visualization of the overall dose–response trend across these LV-VLPs,confirming that LV-VLP-mediated genome editing scales with input dose. Consistent with these observations, the specific activity expressed as editing units per 109 physical particles was highest for LV-VLP-MA (Supplementary Fig. S3D). Further analysis showed that LV-VLP-MA exhibited the highest mean Cas9 protein concentration among the tested LV-VLP variants (Fig. 1E), consistent with a trend toward improved packaging efficiency, although the difference did not reach statistical significance. To further assess RNP loading, we quantified sgRNA incorporation by RT-qPCR, which showed a similar trend of increased sgRNA abundance in LV-VLP-MA relative to other LV-VLP variants (Fig. 1F). In addition, comparison with a fusion-based eVLP system indicated that LV-VLP-MA achieved higher Cas9 protein levels and increased sgRNA incorporation (Supplementary Fig. S4), supporting enhanced cargo loading in the MA-based design. Moreover, editing efficiency generally tracked with Cas9 content, with the most apparent increase observed at the highest ratio (Fig. 1G). Western blot analysis using an anti-Cas9 antibody revealed both Gag–Cas9 fusion protein and released Cas9, with the MA-based construct showing the highest level of free Cas9 among the tested configurations (Supplementary Fig. S5), consistent with more efficient processing of LV-VLP-MA. Assessment of capsid protein p24 levels by ELISA showed increased p24 concentrations for the size reduced variants P2, P2mut, and CA, but a pronounced reduction in the MA-variant, where the capsid portion is deleted (Supplementary Fig. S3E). Although this reduced signal may also reflect altered Gag processing and therefore represent an underestimation of the p24 ELISA in the MA-based construct, p24 levels showed some association with editing efficiency (Supplementary Fig. S3F). These data confirm that the MA-based Gag–Cas9 design enables potent and tunable genome editing. Collectively, these results identify the MA–Gag–Cas9 fusion as the optimal configuration for efficient Cas9 packaging and delivery using the LV-VLP system.

To further optimize production parameters, we systematically examined the effect of the gag–pol to gag-Cas9 plasmid ratio on particle assembly, Cas9 packaging, and editing activity (Supplementary Fig. S6). Ratios ranging from 4:1 to 1:4 were tested in parallel transfections. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S6A and B, ratios of 1:2 and 1:3 produced the highest Cas9 incorporation and B2M knockout efficiencies, whereas lower or higher gag–pol expression markedly reduced performance. Quantitative analysis of Cas9 molecules per 109 particles showed that the 1:2 and 1:3 ratios provided a favorable balance between particle formation and Cas9 loading (Supplementary Fig. S6C and D). Collectively, these results indicate that fine-tuning the gag–pol to gag–Cas9 ratio can influence both yield and functional potency of LV-VLP-MA particles.

Efficient delivery of base and prime editors by LV-VLPs

To expand the LV-VLP platform beyond nuclease delivery, we incorporated the adenine BE ABE8e [44] into the MA-based Gag fusion [LV-gag-MA-ABE8e-NES-NLS (5562 bp); Fig. 2A]. The construct was designed to deliver Cas9–ABE8e RNP complexes guided by a B2M-targeting sgRNA (Fig. 2B). Production analysis showed that LV-VLP-MA/BE particles achieved high functional titers comparable to those of LV-VLP-MA/Cas9 (Fig. 2C), indicating that fusion of the larger BE enzyme did not compromise particle assembly or release of LV-VLPs. When applied to primary human CD34⁺ cells, LV-VLP-MA/BE induced efficient A-to-G conversions at the B2M locus in a clear dose-dependent manner, reaching over 30% base-editing efficiency at higher particle inputs (Fig. 2D). Adjusting the gag/pol to gag/BE expression ratio from 3:1 to 1:3 did not result in significant differences in either functional titer or editing efficiency (Fig. 2E), suggesting a broad tolerance of this system to different expression levels of gag/BE. A quantitative dose–response curve confirmed a proportional relationship between particle number and A-to-G editing (Fig. 2F), underscoring the robustness and tunability of the LV-VLP-MA/BE design for base-editor delivery. Amplicon deep sequencing further confirmed these editing outcomes and revealed multiple edited nucleotides, consistent with bystander editing within the base editing window (Supplementary Fig. S7). In addition, benchmarking against a fusion-based eVLP v4 system showed that LV-VLP-MA/BE achieved higher base-editing efficiency under comparable conditions (Supplementary Fig. S8), further supporting the enhanced delivery capability of the MA-based design.

We next adapted the MA-based LV-VLP architecture for delivery of the prime editor (PE) PE7 [52], which mediates template-guided sequence changes via a prime editing guide RNA (pegRNA) (Fig. 3A and B). Despite the substantially larger size of the PE7 complex, LV-VLP-MA/PE produced functional titers comparable to LV-VLP-MA/BE (Fig. 3C), confirming that PEs can also be efficiently packaged. When tested in primary CD34⁺ cells, LV-VLP-MA/PE induced precise T-to-G edits at the B2M locus in a dose-dependent fashion, with editing efficiencies of 30%–35% at higher doses (Fig. 3D). Similar to the BE system, varying the gag/pol to gag/PE ratio from 3:1 to 1:3 produced no significant difference in yield or editing activity (Fig. 3E). A quantitative dose–response curve further confirmed a proportional increase in editing efficiency with particle number (Fig. 3F), validating LV-VLP-MA/PE as an effective vehicle for PE delivery. Amplicon deep sequencing further confirmed these editing outcomes and showed low indel frequencies at the target locus (Supplementary Fig. S9). In addition, benchmarking against ENVLPE⁺, a system optimized for prime editing delivery, showed that LV-VLP-MA/PE achieved lower prime-editing efficiency under the tested conditions (Supplementary Fig. S10), indicating that further optimization may be required for PE delivery in the LV-VLP format.

Together, these results demonstrate that the LV-VLP-MA platform enables efficient, dose-dependent delivery of both base and PEs without the need for DNA integration or stable expression. Despite the larger sizes of ABE8e and PE7, both editors were efficiently incorporated into LV-VLPs and maintained strong editing activities in primary human cells, establishing a versatile and tunable system for the transient delivery of diverse genome-editing enzymes.

Comparative evaluation and target versatility of the LV-VLP-MA platform

To further assess the generality of our LV-VLP system, we compared its performance with an alternative LV-based VLP platform, engineered nucleocytosolic vehicles for loading of programmable editors [ENVLPE⁺(Cas9)] [42]. This system utilizes aptamers embedded into sgRNAs to tether RNPs into budding VLPs via interaction with PCP (PP7 coat protein) in the NC component of gag. We tested both systems side by side for particle production and genome-editing efficiency (Supplementary Fig. S11). The results showed that LV-VLP-MA and ENVLPE⁺(Cas9) produced comparable functional titers across a range of particle inputs (Supplementary Fig. S11A), indicating similar production efficiency. To further address potential biases associated with functional titer-based normalization, we also plotted editing efficiency as a function of VLP preparation volume, which revealed distinct dose–response relationships between the two platforms (Supplementary Fig. S11B). Cell viability remained high (>95%) and comparable across conditions at the highest dose tested, indicating that these differences were not attributable to cytotoxicity (Supplementary Fig. S11C).Together, these results indicate that the MA-based design provides a robust and effective lentiviral framework for Cas9 RNP delivery in primary CD34⁺ cells, with performance comparable to ENVLPE⁺(Cas9) under MOI-matched conditions.

We next examined whether LV-VLP-MA performance is consistent across multiple targets utilizing a variety of different genes. Using separate LV-VLP-MA particles, each carrying Cas9 RNPs with a single sgRNA targeting CD45, CD44, CD29, or CD47, we observed efficient gene disruption at all four loci (Supplementary Fig. S12A–C). The resulting indel frequencies and knockout percentages were consistent across targets, confirming that LV-VLP-MA enables reliable genome editing across diverse genomic contexts. Together, these results validate that LV-VLP-MA exhibits performance comparable to the ENVLPE⁺ system while maintaining broad target flexibility and robust editing efficiency, establishing it as a versatile and adaptable platform for genome-editing protein delivery.

In vivo genome editing by LV-VLPs

An important potential use of LV-VLPs is their application for in vivo genome editing. In proof-of-concept studies, we targeted the Pcsk9 gene a clinically relevant target regulating cholesterol metabolism in liver cells. We first tested three published sgRNAs (sg1–sg3) for Pcsk9 disruption in MEL cells using LV-VLP-MA particles (Supplementary Fig. S13). All three guides produced detectable editing, while sgRNA3 exhibited the highest indel frequency of 60%–70%; sgRNA3 was therefore selected for subsequent in vivo experiments (Fig. 4A). To evaluate the in vivo genome-editing potential of the LV-VLP-MA system, we performed retro-orbital injections in mice of LV-VLP-MA particles carrying Cas9 RNPs and a Pcsk9-targeting sgRNA, followed by tissue collection and analysis one week post-treatment.

A dose-escalation study further demonstrated a proportional increase in both indel frequency (Fig. 4B) and serum PCSK9 reduction (Fig. 4C) with higher LV-VLP-MA doses. Linear regression analyses confirmed strong correlations between the number of LV-VLPs administered and both editing efficiency and Pcsk9 suppression (R2 = 0.97 and 0.82, respectively; Supplementary Fig. S14). Based on this dose-dependent response, the highest dose was selected for detailed characterization. Targeted Sanger sequencing analyses revealed that 1.8e7 dose of LV-VLP-MA induced robust genome editing of up to 37% of alleles in the liver, whereas only minimal background editing was detected in control groups, including WT mice and animals receiving non-MA LV-VLPs (Fig. 4D). As shown in Fig. 4E, significant indel formation was restricted to the liver, with negligible editing observed in bone marrow and spleen, indicating relative tissue specificity and limited off-target delivery. Consistent with these molecular results, serum PCSK9 protein levels were markedly reduced in LV-VLP-MA–treated animals compared with controls (Fig. 4F), demonstrating functional suppression of Pcsk9 expression. Histopathological examination of liver tissue by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed normal hepatic architecture and no signs of inflammation or tissue damage (Fig. 4G), suggesting that highest dose of LV-VLP-MA delivery was well tolerated.

Together, these data show that systemic administration of LV-VLP-MA enables efficient, dose-dependent, and liver-specific genome editing in vivo, accompanied by functional target gene knockdown and good biocompatibility, underscoring its potential for therapeutic genome-editing applications.

Discussion

In this study, we developed an optimized LV-VLP platform that improves the packaging and delivery of CRISPR-based genome editors. By anchoring genome editors to the Gag MA domain and employing a shortened Gag–Cas9 fusion design, we increased editor incorporation while maintaining particle production within the established lentiviral assembly framework. These architectural changes led to consistent improvements across multiple quantitative measures, including Cas9 content per particle, editing units per packaged editor, and overall genome-editing efficiency. Importantly, the benefits of MA anchoring were observed across different editor classes, including Cas9 nucleases, adenine BEs, and PEs, indicating that this LV-VLP platform supports efficient delivery of large and structurally diverse proteins.

Relative to existing protein-delivery VLP systems, we were able to directly benchmark LV-VLP-MA against several representative platforms, including eVLP-based designs, EDVs, ENVLPE⁺ systems, and previously reported lentivirus-derived nanoparticle architectures [37, 42, 44, 46]. LV-VLP-MA consistently achieved genome-editing efficiencies comparable to or exceeding those of established platforms while maintaining similar functional titers, demonstrating that MA anchoring and Gag truncation do not compromise LV-VLP production. LV-VLP-MA exhibited improved editor incorporation and increased editing output on a per-particle basis, suggesting that the observed functional gains arise from enhanced cargo loading rather than increased particle numbers. An independent work on bioRxiv has also reported the use of Gag truncation to improve protein packaging efficiency in VLP-based systems [53, 54]. Consistent with our observations, that work also highlights the importance of Gag architecture in regulating cargo incorporation. These convergent findings suggest that optimization of Gag architecture may represent a generalizable design principle for enhancing cargo incorporation across VLP platforms. This improvement here in editor loading translated into robust and dose-dependent genome editing in human primary CD34⁺ hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells. Editing efficiencies scaled with LV-VLP input, and performance was maintained across different Gag/pol ratios and editor constructs, suggesting that LV-VLP-MA is not highly sensitive to expression stoichiometry. This robustness may be advantageous for adapting the platform to future genome editors with distinct sizes or domain compositions. These findings are consistent with recent reports demonstrating efficient genome editing in CD34⁺ hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells using lentivirus-derived nanoparticle and VLP-based delivery systems [49, 50].

The in vivo performance of LV-VLP-MA further supports the functional relevance of this design. Systemic administration resulted in efficient and liver-restricted editing of Pcsk9, accompanied by corresponding reductions in serum PCSK9 protein levels. These results are consistent with prior studies demonstrating in vivo PCSK9 editing using VLP-based delivery systems [44]. Editing outcomes exhibited clear dose dependence, and correlation analyses demonstrated a linear relationship between LV-VLP dose, indel frequency, and protein-level effects, indicating that improved editor delivery translated into measurable and biologically meaningful protein modulation in vivo. Importantly, although this analysis was limited in nature, no overt histopathological abnormalities were observed in examined tissues, consistent with the transient and non-integrating nature of protein-based LV-VLP delivery.

Despite these advances, several limitations remain. First, although MA anchoring enhanced editor incorporation, the precise molecular mechanisms governing editor loading and release are not fully understood and may vary among different editors. Second, while LV-VLP-MA supports delivery of large genome editors, the upper size or complexity limits of this platform have not been systematically defined. Third, in vivo delivery in this study was largely restricted to the liver, consistent with the use of VSV-G pseudotyping. Extending this platform to alternative envelopes or targeted ligands will be necessary to enable editing of additional tissues or cell types. Finally, while particle production and editing efficiency were robust at the experimental scale, further work will be required to assess scalability, manufacturing consistency, and long-term safety for translational applications, particularly given the continued reliance on Gag-Pol for particle assembly. In addition, off-target editing was not directly assessed in this study. However, the Pcsk9 sgRNA used here has been previously validated in VLP-based delivery systems with minimal off-target activity [44], and RNP-based delivery is generally associated with reduced off-target effects due to the transient presence of the editor. Future studies will be needed to systematically evaluate off-target activity across different dosing conditions.

In summary, LV-VLP-MA represents a modular lentivirus-derived VLP platform that enables efficient, transient delivery of diverse genome editors. By improving editor incorporation and functional delivery efficiency, this platform reduces the particle input required to achieve robust editing and protein-level effects in primary human cells and in vivo tissues. By optimizing Gag–Cas9 fusion architecture within the existing lentiviral assembly framework, rather than eliminating Gag-Pol dependency, this system provides a generalizable framework for protein-based genome editor delivery. Together, these results support the continued development of protein-loaded LV-VLPs as a generalizable delivery strategy for therapeutic genome editing.
