Use of clinical findings to distinguish between intestinal lymphoma or inflammatory bowel disease in horses
Authors: Jolie Demchur, Alicia E Long, Holly L Stewart, Darko Stefanovski, Daniela Luethy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIdentifying clinical findings that are distinct between inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and intestinal lymphoma could be helpful for providing preliminary guidance for clients regarding their horse’s prognosis while awaiting histopathologic results.Hypothesis/ObjectivesTo describe and compare the clinical findings of horses with a final histopathologic diagnosis of IBD vs intestinal lymphoma.AnimalsForty-six adult (≥1-year-old) horses with a final histopathologic diagnosis of IBD or intestinal lymphoma on endoscopic duodenal biopsy, surgical biopsy, or necropsy.MethodsRetrospective single-center case series via electronic medical record review of histopathology.ResultsEighteen horses had a final diagnosis of lymphoma (11 intestinal only, 7 multicentric including the intestinal tract) and 28 had IBD (22 lymphoplasmacytic IBD, 5 eosinophilic IBD, 1 mixed lymphoplasmacytic and eosinophilic IBD). Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a well-recognized disease complex in horses. There are multiple histologic categories of IBD, including granulomatous enteritis, lymphocytic-plasmacytic enterocolitis, or eosinophilic enterocolitis (which can be further subdivided into idiopathic focal eosinophilic enterocolitis, diffuse eosinophilic enterocolitis, and multisystemic eosinophilic epitheliotropic disease). 1–3 Clinical signs of IBD can include recurrent colic, weight loss, diarrhea, lethargy, or ventral edema.2,4–6 Intestinal lymphoma is also documented in horses, and frustratingly can present very similarly to IBD yet is more often associated with a poorer prognosis. Clinical signs of lymphoma in horses are variable and dependent on organ system(s) affected, but often include colic, weight loss, diarrhea, and fever.7 Therefore, based on previous reports, the two conditions often appear clinically indistinguishable, preventing the clinician from prioritizing a preliminary working diagnosis.

Identifying distinct clinical findings between the two diseases is important for providing guidance for clients regarding their horse’s prognosis and treatment decisions, particularly before receiving histopathologic results or in cases where advanced diagnostics such as biopsies might not be available. Although the early literature suggested a uniformly poor prognosis for IBD, outcomes vary based on the subtype, and a larger, more recent study found a 3-year survival of 75% in horses that responded to initial treatment.2,8 In contrast, the prognosis for intestinal lymphoma in horses is poor.9,10 While literature exists describing the clinical findings of IBD and intestinal lymphoma in horses, no study has directly compared the clinical findings of these two conditions within a single hospital population.2,4–7 Therefore, the objective of this study was to describe and compare the clinical findings of horses with a final histopathologic diagnosis of IBD or intestinal lymphoma within a single hospital population.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was a retrospective case series involving a medical record search at University of Pennsylvania’s New Bolton Center. A medical record search from 2017 to 2023 was conducted for horses with a diagnosis of “lymphoma” or “inflammatory bowel disease” in the diagnosis code. Records from all horses that underwent endoscopic duodenal biopsies, surgical biopsies, or necropsy were retrieved from the Pennsylvania Animal Diagnostic Laboratory System (PADLS) database and selected for a histopathologic diagnosis of intestinal lymphoma or inflammation. Finally, data collected from 2006 to 2015 and previously published was reviewed for horses that underwent endoscopic duodenal biopsies, surgical biopsies, or necropsy for a final diagnosis of IBD or lymphoma affecting the gastrointestinal (GI) system.11 Once cases were identified in both time frames, all cases were included in the same analysis.

Inclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were adult horses (≥1 year of age) who had endoscopic duodenal biopsies, surgical biopsies, necropsy, or a combination of these tests with a histopathologic diagnosis of lymphoma or intestinal inflammation. Rectal biopsy results were collected if available, but diagnosis based on rectal biopsy alone was not considered a sole diagnostic.11 Multicentric lymphoma cases were included if there was gastrointestinal involvement.

Additional details regarding inclusion and exclusion criteria are provided in the Appendix.

Data collection

Data were collected on signalment (age, breed, sex), physical exam findings, history and presenting complaint, clinicopathologic results (for blood collected on the day of admission), and diagnostic results. Horses were classified into 2 primary disease categories: (1) GI lymphoma (including multicentric, if GI involvement); (2) “IBD”; lymphoplasmacytic or eosinophilic.

In horses with a final diagnosis of lymphoma, lymphoma subtype was recorded if noted. Lymphoma subtyping was performed using a combination of histopathologic features and immunohistochemistry for B- and T-cell lymphocyte markers. In some cases, clonality testing (PARR, PCR for Antigen Receptor Rearrangement) was used to aid in determination of subtype, or to help differentiate between lymphoma and IBD. If the subtype was not reported, this was considered “lymphoma-not further subtyped.” In horses with a final diagnosis of IBD, it was noted if this was lymphoplasmacytic, eosinophilic, or mixed. There were no cases of granulomatous IBD in this case sample. Region of the intestine affected (small intestine vs large intestine vs both) was recorded.

Definitions and details of the statistical analyses are provided in the Appendix.

Results

Signalment and final diagnosis

A total of 46 horses met the final inclusion criteria and had sufficient clinical data available. Twenty-eight horses had a final diagnosis of IBD (22 lymphoplasmacytic IBD, 5 eosinophilic IBD, 1 mixed lymphoplasmacytic and eosinophilic IBD). Of the horses diagnosed with eosinophilic IBD, 4 were presumed diffuse, and 1 was consistent with idiopathic focal eosinophilic colitis (IFEC), based on review of descriptions of the cases in the medical records.13 Eighteen horses had a final diagnosis of lymphoma (11 intestinal only, 7 multicentric including the intestinal tract). Diagnostic results

Gastroscopic findings were recorded in 7 of 18 horses with lymphoma and 20 of 28 horses with IBD. In the 7 horses with lymphoma, gastroscopic findings included squamous gastric disease (n = 5; grade 1 to grade 4), abnormal duodenal mucosa (n = 2), glandular gastric disease (n = 1), or no significant findings (n = 1). In the 20 horses with IBD, gastroscopic findings included squamous gastric disease (n = 9; grade 1 to grade 4), abnormal duodenal mucosa (n = 1), glandular gastric disease (n = 2), or no relevant findings (n = 9).

Abdominal ultrasonographic findings were recorded in 15 of 18 horses with lymphoma and 25 of 28 horses with IBD. Intestinal wall thickening was noted on ultrasonography in 13/15 (87%) horses with lymphoma and 21/25 (84%) of horses with IBD. There was no difference detected in the proportion of horses with intestinal wall thickening on ultrasonography between horses with IBD and horses with lymphoma (P = .82). When intestinal wall thickening was noted, median maximal wall thickness was 6.5 mm (range, 4.3-28.6 mm) in IBD and 11.6 mm (range, 5-41.9 mm) in lymphoma (P = .07). Ultrasonographically-apparent abdominal mesenteric lymphadenopathy was more commonly identified in horses with lymphoma (9/15, 60%) than horses with IBD (0/25, 0%, P < .001).

Correlation between antemortem diagnosis and postmortem diagnosis

Ten horses had both antemortem biopsy and necropsy testing performed. A final diagnosis of lymphoma was reached in 6 of these 10 horses, and in 4 of these 6 horses, there was direct agreement between the histologic diagnosis on antemortem biopsy (either endoscopic duodenal, surgical, rectal, or a combination) and postmortem examination. In 2 of the 6 horses, antemortem endoscopic duodenal biopsy findings were suspicious for lymphoma, which was then confirmed on postmortem histopathology.

Of the 10 horses that had antemortem biopsy and postmortem testing performed, 4 horses had a final diagnosis of IBD. One of these horses had antemortem surgical biopsies and necropsy findings that correlated well with the final diagnosis of IBD on both samples. In contrast, 2 of the 4 horses did not have any lesions observed on endoscopic duodenal biopsies. Additional surgical biopsies detected lymphoplasmacytic infiltrates, which were also confirmed with postmortem histopathology in both horses. The last horse in this group had moderate correlation between antemortem endoscopic duodenal biopsies and necropsy findings, with more robust inflammation detected postmortem and in additional locations (small intestine, cecum, and large colon), confirming the antemortem diagnosis of IBD.

Discussion

Our study described and compared the clinical findings of horses with a final histopathologic diagnosis of IBD or intestinal lymphoma, with the aim to determine whether clinical findings can be utilized to help guide clinical diagnosis. Results of this study demonstrated that acute or recurrent colic were more common in horses with IBD and ultrasonographic evidence of mesenteric lymphadenopathy was more common in horses with lymphoma. While these findings are not pathognomonic for either disease category, they can provide more support for clinicians when discussing their clinical suspicions with clients. The remainder of clinical findings were not useful for differentiation between these two conditions.

In this cohort, acute or recurrent colic was a more common presenting complaint in horses with IBD than horses with lymphoma. The most consistent clinical signs reported in the literature associated with IBD in horses include chronic weight loss, reduced performance, recurrent colic, and diarrhea, while commonly reported clinical signs with intestinal neoplasia are similar and include weight loss, reduced appetite, colic, diarrhea, fever, and hypoproteinemia.2,4,7,14,15

In our study, ultrasonographic evidence of abdominal lymphadenopathy was more commonly identified in horses with intestinal lymphoma than horses with IBD. This observation is consistent with previous reports describing abdominal ultrasonography as a valuable adjunctive diagnostic tool for detecting intra-abdominal masses and enlarged mesenteric lymph nodes in horses with lymphoma.16 However, lymphadenopathy is not pathognomonic for lymphoma, as reactive hyperplasia can also occur in response to inflammation or infection. While ultrasonographic lymphadenopathy could increase clinical suspicion for lymphoma, it should be interpreted cautiously and in conjunction with nodal and intestinal histopathology to achieve a definitive diagnosis. In our study, there was no difference in the proportion of horses with ultrasonographic intestinal wall thickening or the maximal wall thickness between IBD and lymphoma, similar to previous studies.17

Clinicopathologic findings were largely indistinguishable between diseases in this study, highlighting the importance of histopathology for diagnosis. However, biopsy samples can be limited by site selection/access, sampling depth, or disease distribution. Endoscopic duodenal biopsies are restricted to the mucosa and submucosa and can miss disease affecting more aboral sections of the intestine. The assessment of endoscopic samples is further complicated by sampling artifacts, difficulty in achieving anatomic orientation for accurate evaluation of morphologic features, and the subjective interpretation of inflammatory cell infiltrates. The likelihood of achieving an antemortem diagnosis is improved with evaluation of full-thickness samples from multiple sites, performed during abdominal surgery where the entirety of the GI system can be evaluated. Even histopathologic evaluation of full-thickness surgical samples or postmortem samples can present a diagnostic challenge in some cases. In four horses not included in the final analysis, histopathology and immunohistochemistry for lymphocyte markers were unable to reach a definitive diagnosis of IBD or lymphoma. In three of these cases, PARR testing was also inconclusive. PARR was not yet available at the time of submission for one horse and was not performed retrospectively. Definitive diagnosis of lymphoma can remain difficult in horses that present early in their disease. It has been proposed that lymphocytic-plasmacytic IBD might be a precursor to intestinal lymphoma, further complicating the clinical picture and diagnostic interpretation.3,8,18 Additionally, the most common subtype of equine lymphoma can have histologic overlap with inflammation. These factors might necessitate the use of additional diagnostic tools (immunohistochemistry and clonality testing) in conjunction with routine histopathologic evaluation to facilitate differentiation between lymphocytic IBD and intestinal lymphoma, as has become a routine part of the diagnostic approach in other species.19

This study has several limitations inherent to its retrospective design. The retrospective design holds the potential for selection and information bias, as diagnostic workups were not standardized, and medical records lacked certain information at times. It is also possible that the relatively small sample size limited statistical power to detect differences between groups. A larger multicenter study could help to address this limitation in the future. Additionally, the horses included in this study were admitted to a tertiary referral hospital and could represent a population with more severe/advanced disease than what might be seen in the general population, and therefore the generalizability of these findings are limited. There was a bias for postmortem diagnosis more commonly reflecting a lymphoma diagnosis, while antemortem diagnosis more commonly reflected a IBD diagnosis. This is likely due to the overall poorer prognosis with lymphoma; some of the cases undergoing necropsy were horses in which treatment with corticosteroids was attempted, but ultimately the horse’s condition deteriorated. The previously-mentioned four lymphoplasmacytic enteropathy inconclusive cases not included in the final analysis represent this bias well, as potentially with further sampling or a postmortem examination, these cases would have been more clearly classified as either lymphoma or IBD. It is also possible that case selection was biased toward horses undergoing exploratory laparotomy due to acute or recurrent colic, and the inclusion of multicentric lymphoma cases might also have biased clinical findings. Despite these limitations, this study compares clinical findings of these two disease processes within a single hospital population.

In conclusion, while many of the clinical findings of IBD and intestinal lymphoma were indistinguishable in this study cohort, our study suggests that recurrent or acute colic might be more commonly seen in horses with IBD than those with lymphoma. Additionally, in this sample, mesenteric lymphadenopathy was more frequently detected in horses with lymphoma compared to IBD. These findings, although not definitive, could help clinicians guide diagnostic prioritization and client communication regarding their clinical suspicions. However, histopathology is essential for definitive diagnosis of these disease processes, and continued research into improved antemortem diagnostics, such as biomarkers, is warranted.

Appendix

Additional details regarding inclusion criteria

All pathology specimens were routinely evaluated for alterations in tissue architecture and cellular infiltrates by a board-certified veterinary pathologist at the time of original submission. As diagnoses were reported by multiple different pathologists and terminology varied, all reports were reviewed by a boarded anatomic pathologist (J.D.) for final inclusion and categorization (lymphoma or IBD and subtype of inflammation). Slides were evaluated when reports did not contain the data required or were non-confirmatory in their diagnosis.

Exclusion criteria

Horses were excluded if they were <1 year of age or if they did not have a final diagnosis of IBD or intestinal lymphoma. Excluded diagnoses included: anterior enteritis, infectious enteritis, or infectious colitis. Cases with incomplete medical records for review were excluded; minimum clinical data required included signalment, history and presenting complaint, and histopathologic diagnosis. If pathology reports were non-confirmatory in their diagnosis, or did not contain the data required, the original slides were reviewed by a boarded anatomic pathologist (J.D.). Cases were excluded if “minimal” inflammation was noted to be of uncertain significance by the issuing pathologist, tissues exhibited collection or processing artifacts interfering with evaluation, or if samples lacked critical components (eg, if only villus tips were included in a biopsy specimen). Horses diagnosed with lymphoma that was not documented to involve the gastrointestinal system, based on the attending clinician’s interpretation of the case, were also excluded.

Definitions

Related to historical and presenting complaint information, acute diarrhea was defined as new diarrhea within the last 48 hours, while chronic diarrhea was any diarrhea persisting or recurring for more than 48 hours. Weight loss and poor body condition score were considered present if they were recorded in the medical record by the attending clinician. If the horse was presented for emergency evaluation of colic signs, this was defined as “acute colic.” If the clinician noted a history of recurrent colic, this was defined as “recurrent colic,” although a set definition of number of colic episodes was not utilized. In our hospital, recurrent colic is defined as repeated colic episodes in between which the horse has a period of at least 5 days without colic signs, is eating normal ration, and not receiving any analgesic or sedative medication.

Information on gastroscopy findings was recorded as available. This included location of any gastric ulceration or mucosal abnormalities (squamous, glandular, or duodenal). If gastroscopy was recorded as normal or no significant findings, this was reported as “no significant findings.” Grade of squamous ulceration was recorded when available, based on the grade provided in the medical record.12

Abdominal ultrasound reports were reviewed when available. Ultrasonography was performed by a dedicated large animal ultrasound service at our institution. The standard procedure for comprehensive abdominal ultrasound is for the hair to be shortly clipped, followed by cleaning of the skin and the use of ultrasound gel and isopropyl alcohol to improve contact. Ultrasonographic evidence of intestinal wall thickening was defined as the ultrasonographer noting increased thickness of the intestinal wall. In our institution, wall thickening is defined as greater than 3 mm.20,21 If noted, the maximal wall thickness measurement was recorded. Ultrasonographic evidence of abdominal mesenteric lymphadenopathy was based on the ultrasound report noting this finding. Determination of increased intestinal wall thickness or lymphadenopathy was at the discretion of the ultrasonographer writing the report and may have been subjective, particularly for lymphadenopathy.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using standard statistical software (StataNow/BE 19.5, StataCorp, State College, TX) with two-sided tests of hypotheses and a P-value <.05 as the criterion for statistical significance. Continuous data was assessed for normality using Shapiro–Wilk test and found in majority of variables to be non-normally distributed data. Therefore, results are reported as median and range. Frequency counts and percentages were used for summarizing categorical variables. Continuous variables (age, clinicopathologic parameters) were compared between groups (IBD vs lymphoma) using a Wilcoxon rank sum test. The proportion of animals with categorical clinical findings was compared using a χ-squared test. Exploratory analysis using univariate and multivariate logistic regression failed to yield a final predictive model, and therefore these results are not reported.
