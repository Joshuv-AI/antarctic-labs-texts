Background

Severe native aortic regurgitation (AR) is traditionally managed surgically1; however, advanced age and comorbidity may render combined valve and ascending aortic repair prohibitive. Off-label transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) use has expanded in native AR,2 although the absence of annular calcification limits anchoring reliability.Take-Home Message•In carefully selected nonsurgical candidates with chronic localized Stanford type A dissection and severe native aortic regurgitation, transapical transcatheter aortic valve replacement may represent a mechanism-based rescue strategy by avoiding arch manipulation and enabling controlled deployment.

In chronic ascending aortic dissection, transfemoral TAVR introduces additional biomechanical risk. Arch traversal with stiff guidewires may increase intimal stress, false lumen cannulation, or dissection destabilization.3 These considerations support minimizing interaction with the dissected segment.

Case Presentation

An elderly frail patient presented with symptomatic severe native AR. Multimodality imaging confirmed severe native AR and localized chronic Stanford type A dissection.

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) confirmed a short ascending dissection segment above the annulus, without sinus or coronary involvement or imaging signs of instability.3 Annular sizing and 3D reconstruction confirmed suitability for transcatheter valve implantation and enabled planning of an apical-to-aortic trajectory.

How We Did It

Given the localized chronic dissection and high surgical risk, transapical access was selected.

Step 1. Access rationale

The objective was to minimize mechanical stress on the dissected ascending aorta. Transapical access allows direct left ventricle entry and straight-line advancement into the ascending aorta, avoiding arch traversal and reducing intimal stress compared with transfemoral delivery.4,5

Step 2. Imaging-guided planning

CT was used to confirm dissection stability, size the annulus, and optimize a coaxial trajectory. Chronic localized dissections without sinus involvement may remain hemodynamically stable under careful selection.3

Step 3. Guidewire strategy

A soft J-tipped guidewire was advanced from the left ventricle apex into the ascending aorta under fluoroscopic and transesophageal echocardiography guidance to confirm true lumen passage before exchange to a supportive platform. Rapid pacing stabilized valve deployment.

Step 4. Valve selection and deployment

Given the absence of calcification, a leaflet-based non–calcium-dependent fixation concept was selected.2 Deployment under fluoroscopy and transesophageal echocardiography ensured coaxial alignment and controlled implantation depth. The procedural details are illustrated in Video 1.

Step 5. Completion

After release, imaging confirmed stable prosthesis position and satisfactory hemodynamics. Apical closure was performed with pledgeted purse-string sutures, and the delivery system was withdrawn under careful hemostatic control.

Outcome

The procedure was completed without intraprocedural complications. Imaging demonstrated optimal valve position, preserved coronary flow, and no significant residual regurgitation. Postprocedural CT confirmed unchanged dissection morphology. On follow-up, the patient remained clinically stable with durable prosthetic valve function. Discussion

In extreme-risk aortic anatomy, procedural success depends as much on access-route biomechanics as on valve implantation. Although transcatheter therapy has expanded in native AR,2 chronic ascending dissection introduces risk related to arch manipulation and intimal shear stress.3

Transapical access represents a mechanism-based alternative by eliminating arch traversal and facilitating coaxial delivery. Contemporary reports support its feasibility in complex aortic disease and emphasize reduced aortic manipulation compared with transfemoral strategies.4,5

In noncalcified native AR, anchoring strategy is equally critical.2 Integration of pathology-driven access selection with non–calcium-dependent fixation enabled a coherent risk-mitigating procedural strategy.

Access selection in dissection anatomy should be individualized rather than defaulting to a routine transfemoral-first strategy.3, 4, 5

Conclusions

In selected nonsurgical candidates with severe native AR and localized chronic Stanford type A dissection, transapical TAVR may represent a rational mechanism-based rescue strategy that minimizes arch manipulation and facilitates controlled deployment.3, 4, 5
