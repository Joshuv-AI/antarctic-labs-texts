Bounded Rewriting Induction for LCSTRSs
Kasper Hagens ORCID:0009-0005-2382-0559, Cynthia Kop ORCID:0000-0002-6337-2544

Abstract
. Rewriting Induction (RI) is a method to prove inductive theorems, originating from equational reasoning. By using Logically Constrained Simply-typed Term Rewriting Systems (LCSTRSs) as an intermediate language, rewriting induction becomes a tool for program verification, with inductive theorems taking the role of equivalence predicates. Soundness of RI depends on well-founded induction, and one of the core obstacles for obtaining a practically useful proof system is to find suitable well-founded orderings automatically. Using naive approaches, all induction hypotheses must be oriented within the well-founded ordering, which leads to very strong termination requirements. This, in turn, severely limits the proof capacity of RI. Here, we introduce Bounded RI : an adaption of RI for LCSTRSs where such termination requirements are minimized. Traditionally, RI can be used not only to prove equivalence, but also to establish ground confluence. Moreover, for ground confluent TRSs, RI can be extended to a system for disproving inductive theorems. We will show that this is also possible with Bounded RI.



1.
Introduction

Rewriting Induction (RI) is a proof system for showing equations to be inductive theorems.
It was introduced by Reddy
[
22
]

as a method to validate inductive proof procedures based on Knuth-Bendix completion.
Classically, RI is used in equational reasoning to prove properties of inductively defined mathematical structures
like natural numbers or lists.
For example, one could use RI to
prove an equation

\mathsf{add}(x,y)\approx\mathsf{add}(y,x)
, expressing commutativity of addition on the natural numbers.
RI has been extended to constrained rewriting
[
12
,
13
,
21
]
, and recently to higher-order constrained rewriting
[
18
]
.
These formalisms closely relate to real
programming and therefore have a natural place in the larger toolbox for program verification.
Programs are represented by term rewriting systems, and equivalence of two functions within a program is modeled by an equation being an inductive theorem.

Why constrained rewriting?

Using RI for program equivalence somewhat differs from the standard setting in equational reasoning where, for example, the Peano axioms are used to prove statements about the natural numbers.
In our case, we are not so much interested in proving properties about numbers themselves, but about programs that operate on them.
Of course, we can define functions like
\mathsf{add}
,
\mathsf{mul}
and

\mathsf{greater}
, express the Peano axioms as rewrite rules and use this to
define programs on natural numbers.
However, doing so
studying program equivalence becomes a cumbersome experience that requires both reasoning about the arithmetic and the program definition itself.
Moreover, in practice, we typically want to reason about integers or even bit vectors rather than natural numbers, which requires correspondingly harder arithmetic reasoning.
Intermingling these two different kinds of reasoning makes it hard to scale analysis.
Ideally, we would want arithmetic to be given “for free”, as it is in real life programming.
With standard term rewriting this is not possible.

Constrained term rewriting provides a solution here, as it natively supports primitive data structures, such as integers, bit vectors and floating point numbers.
This makes it possible to distinguish between the program definition (represented by rewrite rules), and underlying data structures with their operators
(represented by distinguished terms with pre-determined semantical interpretations).
This allows us to shift some of the proof-burden from the rewriting side to the semantical side, where dedicated SMT solvers can be used.

In constrained rewriting, rewrite rules have a shape
s\to t\ [\varphi]
where the boolean constraint
\varphi
acts as a guard, in order to manage control flow over primitive data structures.
Here, we will consider Logically Constrained Simply-typed Term Rewriting Systems (LCSTRSs), which considers applicative higher-order rewriting (without
\lambda
abstractions) and first-order constraints
[
16
]
.
In particular, we will build on our earlier work
[
18
]
where we defined RI for LCSTRSs (but do not assume familiarity with this work or other definitions of RI).

Goals

Our goals are threefold:

\triangleright

Redefine RI for LCSTRSs in such a way that we minimize the termination requirements.

Our primary goal.
Induction proofs in RI require induction hypotheses to be oriented in a well-founded ordering.
This has the potential to give very strong termination requirements – which, as we will see,
is not necessary. By adapting the definition of RI, we can significantly reduce these termination
requirements and thereby make (automatic) proof search easier.

\triangleright

Using RI as a method for proving ground confluence.

For first-order unconstrained rewriting it has been shown that RI can be combined with critical pairs to obtain a method for proving ground confluence
[
2
]
.
Here, we extend this result to LCSTRSs.

\triangleright

Disproving equations.

For first-order, ground confluent LCTRSs, RI can also be used to prove that equations are not inductive theorems
[
13
]
,
but the higher-order proof system in
[
18
]
does not yet support this.
Here, we extend this result to LCSTRSs.

Termination requirements

The name
Rewriting Induction
refers to the principle that for a terminating rewrite system
\mathcal{R}
, the reduction relation
\to_{\mathcal{R}}^{+}
defines a well-founded order on the set of all terms, and therefore can be used for proofs by well-founded induction.
In many cases, however, we will need a well-founded order
\succ
which is strictly larger than
\to_{\mathcal{R}}^{+}
.

In particular, the role of induction hypotheses in RI is taken by equations, which,
when applied, must be oriented w.r.t.
\succ
.
That is, we
can only
use an induction hypothesis
s\approx t
if
s\succ t
or
t\succ s
holds.
Consequently, termination of
\mathcal{R}
itself is not enough, since equations are not usually orientable by
\to_{\mathcal{R}}^{+}
.
Instead, we for instance might let
\succ\ =\ \to^{+}_{\mathcal{R}\cup\{s\to t\}}
, or in the case of multiple induction hypotheses, orient them as rewrite rules,
collect them into a set
\mathcal{H}
, and use
\succ\ =\ \to^{+}_{\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{H}}
.
However, doing so leaves us with an
obligation to show termination of
\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{H}
.
Even if
\mathcal{R}
is known to be terminating, it may not be easy or even
possible to prove the same for
\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{H}

(think for instance of an induction hypothesis
\mathsf{add}(x,y)\approx\mathsf{add}(y,x)
, which is not orientable in either direction).
In such a situation a RI proof might get stuck.

As already observed by Reddy
[
22
]
, we do not necessarily need every induction hypothesis to be oriented, so long as we guarantee that an induction rule
s\to t
is only applied to terms
\succ
-smaller than
s
.
For this, it is not required to choose the well-founded ordering
\succ\ =\ \to^{+}_{\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{H}}
.
Reddy proposed to use modulo rewriting, to build a well-founded
\succ
which may not need to contain all induction rules.
This approach was investigated by Aoto, who introduced several extensions of RI for first-order unconstrained rewriting
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
.
Here, we will follow a strategy along the same idea: by redefining RI we construct a well-founded relation
\succ
during the RI process, aiming to keep it as small as possible.

Paper setup and contributions

In
section 2
we recap LCSTRSs, equations and inductive theorems. The following sections present our contributions:

\triangleright

In
section 3
we introduce Bounded RI for LCSTRSs.
This system builds on the one introduced in
[
18
]
– and is thus designed for
higher-order
,
constrained
systems – but provides a more semantic approach and strictly contains it.
Contributions include:

–

We introduce the notion of
bounding pair

(\succ,\succeq)
, providing the fundamental ingredient by which we express the ordering requirements for induction proofs.

–

We replace equations by
equation contexts
, containing the extra information of two
bounding terms
which define an upper bound for applying an induction hypothesis.
More specifically, the bounding terms provide us with a way to keep track of terms to be in used in the ordering requirements (along the equation we are proving) –
instead of orienting the induction hypothesis themselves – yielding milder termination requirements.

–

In contrast to
[
18
]
, we also include derivation rules to deduce non-equivalence (
subsection 3.3
) for ground confluent LCSTRSs. This is thus far only achieved for first-order definitions of RI
[
13
]
.

\triangleright

In
section 4
, we prove soundness and completeness of Bounded RI.
Apart from its necessity for the results of
section 3
to be meaningful, there are two additional contributions:

–

We show that Bounded RI can be used to prove that equations are
bounded ground convertible
, which is strictly stronger than the
property of ground convertibility that was used in
[
18
]
, and which explicitly relates to the bounding pair
(\succ,\succeq)
.

–

We structure our soundness proof in a very modular way, by focusing on two properties that all but one of our deduction
rules satisfy. This makes it possible to
easily add new derivation rules to the existing system without having to redo the full proof.

\triangleright

In
section 5
we show that Bounded RI can be used to prove ground confluence of an LCSTRS. This is a known result, but only for first-order systems without constraints
[
2
]
.

–

We recap the definition of first-order critical pairs for LCTRSs from
[
23
]
, extend it to LCSTRSs and prove the Critical Peak Lemma.
This is a first critical peak / pair definition for higher-order constrained systems.

–

We introduce the Ground Critical Peak Theorem, and show how this allows us to use RI for proving ground confluence.
This follows the idea of
[
2
]
, but makes important adaptations to work well with the new RI method.

\triangleright

In
section 6
we discuss two strategies to construct a suitable well-founded ordering.
In particular, we can define the ordering as a reduction relation
\to^{+}_{\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{Q}}
and briefly discuss
strategies to choose the set
\mathcal{Q}
. We also show that we can use a version of the dependency pair framework directly
to obtain more liberal requirements.

\triangleright

In
section 7
we illustrate how the two soundness properties –
Preserving Bounds
(
subsection 4.1
)
and
Base Soundness Property
(
subsection 4.3
) – can be exploited to obtain new deduction rules,
by extending Bounded RI with some practically useful deduction rules.

\triangleright

We have implemented all methods in this work in our tool
Cora
, to allow for human-guided equivalence proofs
with fully automatic termination and SMT implication checks.
In
section 8
we discuss our implementation.

We relate our contributions to existing work in

, and conclude in

.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Logically Constrained Simply Typed Rewriting Systems

We will recap LCSTRSs
[
16
]
,
a higher-order rewriting formalism with built-in support for data structures such as integers and booleans
(or in fact any theory like bitvectors, floating point numbers or integer arrays) as well as logical constraints to model control flow.
This considers
applicative
higher-order term rewriting (without
\lambda
) and
first-order
constraints.

Types

Assume given a set of sorts (base types)
\mathcal{S}
; the set
\mathcal{T}
of types is defined by the
grammar

\mathcal{T}::=\mathcal{S}\mid\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{T}
.
Here,
\to
is right-associative, so all types may be written as

\mathit{type}_{1}\to\dots\to\mathit{type}_{m}\to\mathit{sort}

with
m\geq 0
.

We also assume given a subset
\mathcal{S}_{theory}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
of
theory sorts
(e.g.,
\mathsf{int}
and

\mathsf{bool}
), and define the
theory types
by the grammar

\mathcal{T}_{theory}::=\mathcal{S}_{theory}\mid\mathcal{S}_{theory}\to\mathcal{T}_{theory}
.
Each theory sort
\iota\in\mathcal{S}_{theory}
is associated with a non-empty interpretation set
\mathcal{I}_{\iota}
(e.g.,

\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{int}}=\mathbb{Z}
, the set of all integers).
We define
\mathcal{I}_{\iota\to\sigma}
as the set of all total functions from
\mathcal{I}_{\iota}
to
\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}
.

Terms

We assume given a signature
\Sigma
of
function symbols
and a disjoint set
\mathcal{V}
of
variables, and a function
\mathit{typeof}
from
\Sigma\cup\mathcal{V}
to
\mathcal{T}
; we require that there
are infinitely many variables of all types.
The set of terms
T(\Sigma,\mathcal{V})
over
\Sigma
and
\mathcal{V}
are the expressions in

\mathbb{T}
– defined by the grammar

\mathbb{T}::=\Sigma\mid\mathcal{V}\mid\mathbb{T}\ \mathbb{T}
– that are
well-typed
:

a::\mathit{typeof}(a)
for
a\in\Sigma\cup\mathcal{V}
, and
if
s::\sigma\to\tau
and
t::\sigma
then
s\ t::\tau
.
Application is left-associative, which allows all terms to be written in a form

a\ t_{1}\cdots t_{n}
with
a\in\Sigma\cup\mathcal{V}
and
n\geq 0
.
Writing
t=a\ t_{1}\cdots t_{n}
, we define
\mathit{head}(t)=a
.
For a term
t
, let
Var(t)
be the set of variables occurring in
t
.
For multiple terms
t_{1},\dots,t_{n}
, let
Var(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})
denote

Var(t_{1})\cup\dots\cup Var(t_{n})
.
A term
t
is
ground
if
Var(t)=\emptyset
.
It is
linear
if no variable occurs more than once in
t
.

We assume that
\Sigma
is the disjoint union
\Sigma_{theory}\uplus\Sigma_{terms}
, where

\mathit{typeof}(\mathsf{f})\in\mathcal{T}_{theory}
for all
\mathsf{f}\in\Sigma_{theory}
.
We use infix notation for the binary symbols, or use
\mathsf{f}
for prefix or partially
applied notation (e.g.,
+\ x\ y
and
x+y
are the same).
Each
\mathsf{f}\in\Sigma_{theory}
has an interpretation
[\![\mathsf{f}]\!]\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathit{typeof}(\mathsf{f})}
.
For example, a theory symbol
*::\mathsf{int}\to\mathsf{int}\to\mathsf{int}
may be interpreted as
multiplication on
\mathbb{Z}
.
Symbols in
\Sigma_{terms}
do not have an interpretation.
Values are theory symbols of base type, i.e.

\mathcal{V}\textit{al}=\{v\in\Sigma_{theory}\mid\mathit{typeof}(v)\in\mathcal{S}_{theory}\}
.
We assume there is exactly one value for each element of
\mathcal{I}_{\iota}

(
\iota\in\mathcal{S}_{theory}
).
Elements of
T(\Sigma_{theory},\mathcal{V})
are called
theory terms
. For
ground
theory terms, we define

[\![s\ t]\!]=[\![s]\!]([\![t]\!])
, thus mapping each ground term of type

\sigma
to an element of
\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}
.
We fix a theory sort
\mathsf{bool}
with
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{bool}}=\{\top,\bot\}
.
A
constraint
is a theory term
s
of type
\mathsf{bool}
, such that
\mathit{typeof}(x)\in\mathcal{S}_{theory}

for all
x\in Var(s)
.

{exa}

Throughout this text we will always use
\mathcal{S}_{theory}=\{\mathsf{int},\mathsf{bool}\}
and

\Sigma_{theory}=\{+,-,*,<,\leq,>,\geq,=,\wedge,\vee,\neg,\mathtt{true},\mathtt{false}\}\cup\{\mathsf{n}\mid n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
, with
+,-,*::\mathsf{int}\to\mathsf{int}\to\mathsf{int}
,
<,\leq,>,\geq,=::\mathsf{int}\to\mathsf{int}\to\mathsf{bool}
,

\wedge,\vee::\mathsf{bool}\to\mathsf{bool}\to\mathsf{bool}
,

\neg::\mathsf{bool}\to\mathsf{bool}
,

\mathtt{true},\mathtt{false}::\mathsf{bool}

and
\mathsf{n}::\mathsf{int}
.
We let
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{int}}=\mathbb{Z}
,
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{bool}}=\{\top,\bot\}
and interpret all symbols as expected.
The values are
\mathtt{true},\mathtt{false}
and all
\mathsf{n}
. Theory terms are for instance

x+\mathsf{3}
,
\mathtt{true}
and
\mathsf{7}*\mathsf{0}
. The latter two are ground. We have

[\![\mathsf{7}*\mathsf{0}]\!]=0
.
The theory term
x>\mathsf{0}
is a constraint, but the theory term
(f\ x)>\mathsf{0}
with

f\in\mathcal{V}
of type
\mathsf{int}\to\mathsf{int}
is not (since
\mathit{typeof}(f)\notin\mathcal{S}_{theory}
),
nor is
>\ \mathsf{0}::\mathsf{int}\to\mathsf{bool}
(since constraints must have type
\mathsf{bool}
).

Remark 1
.

Most programming languages have pre-defined (non-recursive) data structures and operators, e.g. the integers with a multiplication operator
*
.
This makes it, for instance, possible to define the factorial function without first having to define multiplication.
The same is true for LCSTRSs:
\Sigma_{theory}
contains all pre-defined operators, including constants.

Substitutions

A substitution is a type-preserving mapping
\gamma:\mathcal{V}\to T(\Sigma,\mathcal{V})
.
The domain of a substitution is defined as
\mathit{dom}(\gamma)=\{x\in\mathcal{V}\mid\gamma(x)\neq x\}
,
and its image as
\mathit{im}(\gamma)=\{\gamma(x)\mid x\in\mathit{dom}(\gamma)\}
.
A substitution on finite domain
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
is often denoted
[x_{1}:=s_{1},\dots,x_{n}:=s_{n}]
.
A substitution
\gamma
is extended to a function
s\mapsto s\gamma
on terms by placewise
substituting variables in the term by their image:
(
i
)
t\gamma=t
if
t\in\Sigma
,
(
ii
)
t\gamma=\gamma(t)
if
t\in\mathcal{V}
, and
(
iii
)
(t_{0}\ t_{1})\gamma=(t_{0}\gamma)\ (t_{1}\gamma)
.
If
M\subseteq T(\Sigma,\mathcal{V})
then
\gamma(M)
denotes

\{t\gamma\mid t\in M\}
.

A
ground substitution
is a substitution
\gamma
such that for all
variables
x
in its domain,
\gamma(x)
is a ground term.
A
unifier
of terms
s,t
is a substitution
\gamma
such that
s\gamma=t\gamma
; a

most general unifier
or
mgu
is a unifier
\gamma
such that all other unifiers are
instances of
\gamma
. For unifiable terms, an mgu always exists, and is unique
modulo variable renaming.

We say that a substitution
\gamma

respects
a constraint
\varphi
if

\gamma(Var(\varphi))\subseteq\mathcal{V}\textit{al}
and
[\![\varphi\gamma]\!]=\top
.
We say that a constraint
\varphi
is
satisfiable
if there exists a substitution

\gamma
that respects
\varphi
.
It is
valid
if
[\![\varphi\gamma]\!]=\top
for all substitutions
\gamma
such that
\gamma(Var(\varphi))\subseteq\mathcal{V}\textit{al}
.

Contexts and subterms

Let
\square_{1},\dots,\square_{n}
be fresh, typed constants (
n\geq 1
).
A context
C[\square_{1},\dots,\square_{n}]
(or just:
C
) is a term in
T(\Sigma\cup\{\square_{1},\ldots,\square_{n}\},\mathcal{V})

in which each
\square_{i}
occurs exactly once.
(Note that
\square_{i}
may occur at the head of an application.)
The term obtained from
C
by replacing each
\square_{i}
by a term
t_{i}
of the same type is denoted by
C[t_{1},\dots,t_{n}]
.

We say that
t
is a
subterm
of
s
, notation
s\unrhd t
,
if there is a context
C[\square]
such that
s=C[t]
.
We say that
t
is a
strict
subterm of
s
, notation
s\rhd t
, if
s\unrhd t
and
s\neq t
.

Rewrite rules

A rule is an expression
\ell\to r\ [\varphi]
.
Here
\ell
and
r
are terms of the same type,
\ell
has a form
\mathsf{f}\ \ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{k}
with

\mathsf{f}\in\Sigma
and
k\geq 0
,
\varphi
is a constraint and

Var(r)\subseteq Var(\ell)\cup Var(\varphi)
.
If
\varphi=\mathtt{true}
, we may denote the rule as just
\ell\to r
.

Fixing a signature
\Sigma
, we define the set of
calculation rules
as:

\mathcal{R}_{calc}=\left\{\mathsf{f}\ x_{1}\cdots x_{m}\to y\ [y=\mathsf{f}\ x_{1}\cdots x_{m}]\ \middle|\begin{array}[]{l}\mathsf{f}\in\Sigma_{theory}\setminus\mathcal{V}\textit{al},\text{all}\ x_{i}\ \text{and}\ y\in\mathcal{V},\\
\mathit{typeof}(\mathsf{f})=\iota_{1}\to\dots\to\iota_{m}\to\kappa\end{array}\right\}

We furthermore assume given a set of rules
\mathcal{R}
that satisfies the following properties:

\triangleright

for all
\ell\to r\ [\varphi]\in\mathcal{R}
:
\ell
is not a theory term
(such rules are contained in
\mathcal{R}_{calc}
)

\triangleright

for all
\mathsf{f}\ \ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{k}\to r\ [\varphi],\ \mathsf{g}\ \ell_{1}^{\prime}\cdots\ell_{n}^{\prime}\to r^{\prime}\ [\psi]\in\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{R}_{calc}
:
if
\mathsf{f}=\mathsf{g}
then
k=n

The latter restriction blocks us for instance from having both a rule
\mathsf{append}\ \mathsf{nil}\to\mathsf{id}

and a rule
\mathsf{append}\ (\mathsf{cons}\ x\ y)\ z\to\mathsf{cons}\ x\ (\mathsf{append}\ y\ z)
. While such rules
would normally be allowed in higher-order rewriting, we need to impose this limitation for the notion of

quasi-reductivity
to make sense, as discussed in
[
18
]
.
This does not really limit expressivity, since we can
pad both sides
with variables, e.g.,
replacing the first rule above by
\mathsf{append}\ \mathsf{nil}\ x\to\mathsf{id}\ x
.

For a fixed signature and rules
\mathcal{R}
as above, we define three classes of function symbols:

\triangleright

elements of
\mathcal{D}=\{\mathsf{f}\in\Sigma\mid
there is a rule
\mathsf{f}\ \ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{k}\to r\ [\varphi]\in\mathcal{R}\}
are called
defined symbols
;

\triangleright

elements of
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{V}\textit{al}\cup(\Sigma_{terms}\setminus\mathcal{D})
are called
constructors
;

\triangleright

elements of
\Sigma_{calc}=\Sigma_{theory}\setminus\mathcal{V}\textit{al}
are called
calculation symbols
.

For every defined or calculation symbol

\mathsf{f}::\sigma_{1}\to\dots\to\sigma_{m}\to\iota
with
\iota\in\mathcal{S}
,
we let
\mathit{ar}(\mathsf{f})
be the unique number
0\leq k\leq m

such that for every rule of the form
\mathsf{f}\ \ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{k}\to r\ [\varphi]
in

\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{R}_{calc}
we have
\mathit{ar}(\mathsf{f})=k
.
(By the restrictions above, such
k
always exists.)
For all constructors
\mathsf{f}\in\mathcal{C}
, we define
\mathit{ar}(\mathsf{f})=\infty
.

We say that a substitution
\gamma
respects
\ell\to r\ [\varphi]
if it respects
\varphi
.

Remark 2
.

Note that it is theoretically possible for a rule to have a calculation symbol at the head of
its left-hand side, so a function symbol
can
be both a calculation symbol and a defined
symbol. However, this would rarely occur in practice – it is allowed because methods like
rewriting induction might create “rules” of unusual forms to be used in a termination proof, and
it does not harm our method or the difficulty of the proofs to admit such rules.

Reduction relation

For a fixed signature
\Sigma
and set of rules
\mathcal{R}
as discussed above, the reduction relation

\to_{\mathcal{R}}
is defined by:

C[l\gamma]\to_{\mathcal{R}}C[r\gamma]\text{ if }\ell\to r\ [\varphi]\in\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{R}_{calc}\text{ and }\gamma\text{ respects }\varphi

Note that by definition of context, reductions may occur at the head of an application.
For example, if
\mathsf{append}\ \mathsf{nil}\to\mathsf{id}\in\mathcal{R}
, then we could reduce

\mathsf{append}\ \mathsf{nil}\ s\ \to_{\mathcal{R}}\mathsf{id}\ s
.
We say that
s

has normal form

t
if
s\to_{\mathcal{R}}^{*}t
and
t
cannot be reduced.
If we want to emphasize that reduction
s\to_{\mathcal{R}}t
is performed with a rule in
\mathcal{R}_{calc}
, we write
s\to_{\mathcal{R}_{calc}}t
instead.

LCSTRS

A
Logically Constrained Simply-typed Term Rewriting System (LCSTRS)
is a pair

(T(\Sigma,\mathcal{V}),\to_{\mathcal{R}})
generated by
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S}_{theory},\Sigma_{terms},\Sigma_{theory},\mathcal{V},\mathit{typeof},\mathcal{I},[\![\cdot]\!],\mathcal{R})
.
We often refer to an LCSTRS by
\mathcal{L}=(\Sigma,\mathcal{R})
, or just
\mathcal{R}
, leaving the rest implicit.

{exa}

Let
\mathcal{R}
consist of the following rules

\begin{array}[]{lrcll}\textbf{(R1)}&\mathsf{recdown}\ f\ n\ i\ a&\to&a&[i<n]\\
\textbf{(R2)}&\mathsf{recdown}\ f\ n\ i\ a&\to&f\ i\ (\mathsf{recdown}\ f\ n\ (i-\mathsf{1})\ a)&[i\geq n]\\
\textbf{(R3)}&\mathsf{tailup}\ f\ i\ m\ a&\to&a&[i>m]\\
\textbf{(R4)}&\mathsf{tailup}\ f\ i\ m\ a&\to&\mathsf{tailup}\ f\ (i+\mathsf{1})\ m\ (f\ i\ a)&[i\leq m]\\
\end{array}

We have

\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{theory}=\{\mathsf{int},\mathsf{bool}\}
,

2.2.
Rewriting induction prerequisites

Finally, we will recap the notions of equations, inductive theorems, and the restrictions that an
LCSTRS must satisfy to be able to apply rewriting induction.
For this, we follow the definitions from
[
18
]
.

Equations and inductive theorems

An
equation
is a triple
s\approx t\ [\varphi]
with

\mathit{typeof}(s)=\mathit{typeof}(t)

and
\varphi
a constraint.,
If the constraint
\varphi
is
\mathtt{true}
, we will simply write the equation as
s\approx t
.
A substitution
\gamma
respects
s\approx t\ [\varphi]
if
\gamma
respects
\varphi

and
Var(s,t)\subseteq\mathit{dom}(\gamma)
.

An equation
s\approx t\ [\varphi]
is an
inductive theorem
(aka
ground convertible
)
if
{s\gamma}\mathrel{\leftrightarrow^{*}_{\mathcal{R}}}{t\gamma}
for every ground substitution
\gamma
that respects

s\approx t\ [\varphi]
.
Here
\leftrightarrow_{\mathcal{R}}\ =\ \rightarrow_{\mathcal{R}}\cup\leftarrow_{\mathcal{R}}
,
and
\leftrightarrow_{\mathcal{R}}^{*}
is its transitive, reflexive closure.

For a set of equations
\mathcal{E}
, we define
\leftrightarrow_{\mathcal{E}}
as follows:

C[s\gamma]\leftrightarrow_{\mathcal{E}}C[t\gamma]\text{ if }s\approx t\ [\varphi]\in\mathcal{E}\text{ or }t\approx s\ [\varphi]\in\mathcal{E}\text{ and }\gamma\text{ respects }\varphi

Note that
\mathcal{E}
consists of inductive theorems if and only if
\leftrightarrow_{\mathcal{E}}\ \subseteq\ \leftrightarrow^{*}_{\mathcal{R}}
holds on ground terms (i.e. when restricting to ground substitutions
\gamma
and ground contexts
C
).

Remark 3
.

In higher-order rewriting, there are multiple ways to define inductive theorems.
In particular one could choose to take into account extensionality
[

, which equates two functions if they are equal on all their arguments
(for example, in such a definition

would be an inductive theorem,
which is not the case in our definition).
Here, we will not further motivate our choice, as this is a separate topic that we have extensively discussed in
[
18
]
.
One may also consider

[
3
]
, which limits inductive theorems to those equations that are still ground convertible in “any reasonable extension” of
\mathcal{R}
.
We will briefly discuss this in

{exa}

The LCSTRS from
subsection 2.1
admits an equation
\mathsf{recdown}\ f\ n\ i\ a\linebreak\approx\mathsf{tailup}\ f\ n\ i\ a
.
Since it has constraint

, any substitution on domain

In

we will prove that this equation is an inductive theorem, meaning that
{(\mathsf{recdown}\ f\ n\ i\ a)\gamma}\mathrel{\leftrightarrow^{*}_{\mathcal{R}}}{(\mathsf{tailup}\ f\ n\ i\ a)\gamma}
for any ground substitution
\gamma
.

We will limit our interest to
quasi-reductive
LCSTRSs (defined below), which is needed to guarantee soundness of RI.
Intuitively, this property expresses that pattern matching on ground terms is exhaustive (i.e. there are no missing reduction cases).
For example, the rewrite system
\mathcal{R}=\{\textbf{(R1)},\textbf{(R2)}\}
is quasi-reductive because

and

together cover all ground instances of

. But if, for example, we replace

by

then it is not, as we are missing all ground reduction cases for

(for example
\mathsf{recdown}\ *\ \mathsf{0}\ \mathsf{0}\ \mathsf{0}
does not reduce anymore).

For first-order LCTRSs, quasi-reductivity is achieved by requiring that there are no other ground normal forms than the ground constructor terms
T(\mathcal{C},\emptyset)
.
For higher-order LCSTRSs, however, this definition does not work as we can have ground normal forms with partially applied defined symbols
(for example,
\mathsf{recdown}\ +
).
We therefore use the higher-order generalization of the notion of constructor terms that was introduced in
[
18
]
.

Semi-constructor terms

Let
\mathcal{L}=(\Sigma,\mathcal{R})
be some LCSTRS.
The semi-constructor terms over
\mathcal{L}
, notation

{enumerate*}
[(i). ]

\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{SCT}_{\mathcal{L}}

if

and

and

, then

Semi-constructor terms are always normal forms.
Furthermore, as
\mathit{ar}(\mathsf{f})=\infty
for every

are contained in

However, also terms with partial applications, such as

An LCSTRS

or

. Put differently, the only irreducible ground terms are semi-constructor terms.
Weak normalization and quasi-reductivity together ensure that every ground term reduces to a semi-constructor term.
Note that, if

are ground normal forms and

\mathsf{f}\ s_{1}\cdots s_{n}
is a ground normal form if and only if

.

We say that an LCSTRS has

if the only

).
Hence, it is not for instance allowed to have a constructor
\mathsf{error}::\mathsf{int}
. While we do not explicitly limit interest to LCSTRSs with inextensible
theory sorts, it is satisfied in all our examples, because in practice it is
very difficult to achieve quasi-reductivity without this property.

3.
Rewriting Induction

RI was introduced by Reddy
[
22
]
in the year 1990 as a deduction system for proving inductive theorems, using unconstrained first-order term rewriting systems.
Since then, many variations on the system have appeared (e.g.,
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
12
,
13
,
18
]
).
All are based on well-founded induction, using some well-founded relation
\succ
.
Depending on how
\succ
is being constructed these versions of RI can be categorized into two approaches

\triangleright

The first, being used in
[
22
,
12
,
13
,
18
]
,
employs a fixed strategy to construct a
terminating rewrite system
A\supseteq\mathcal{R}
and then chooses
\succ\ =\ \to_{A}^{+}
.

\triangleright

The second, used in
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
, employs a well-founded relation
\succ
that satisfies
certain requirements (like monotonicity and stability, but also ground totality), and constructs the proof
in a more targeted way.
This relation may either be fixed beforehand (e.g., the lexicographic path ordering), or constructed
during or after the proof, as the proof process essentially accumulates termination requirements.

In practice, the former approach leads to quite heavy termination requirements because it forces
A

to include all (oriented) induction hypotheses (represented by rewrite rules), while the latter is
designed to keep termination requirements as mild as possible; for example by
orienting some requirements
using a relation
\succeq
rather than

\succ
.
However, the latter approach imposes more bureaucracy, since derivation rules rely on several steps
being done at once – for example, by reasoning
modulo
the set of induction hypotheses.
This makes it quite hard to use especially when

\succ
is not fixed beforehand but
rather constructed on the fly.

In
subsection 3.1
, we will introduce Bounded RI, which aims to combine the best of both
worlds.
We reduce termination requirements by using a pair
(\succ,\succeq)
, which may either be fixed in advance, or
constructed as part of the proof process.
In particular, we do not orient induction hypotheses themselves: we only require that a particular instance of the induction hypothesis is allowed to be applied whenever it is strictly dominated by some efficiently chosen bounding term, being associated to the particular equation under consideration.

Importantly, we do not impose the ground totality requirement (which would be extremely restrictive
in higher-order rewriting!), and thus allow for
\succ
to for instance be a relation
(\to_{A}\cup\rhd)^{+}
, or built by a construction based on dependency pairs (see
section 6
).

First, we will define the properties that our pair
(\succ,\succeq)
should satisfy:

{defi}

[Ordering and Bounding Pair]
For a fixed set
\mathcal{A}
,
an
ordering pair
is a pair
(\succ,\succeq)
of a
well-founded partial
ordering

\succ
on
\mathcal{A}
(that is,
\succ
is a transitive, anti-symmetric,
irreflexive and well-founded relation) and a
quasi-order

\succeq
(that is,

\succeq
is a transitive and reflexive relation) such that
\succ\;\subseteq\;\succeq
, and
both
\succ\cdot\succeq\;\subseteq\;\succ
and
\succeq\cdot\succ\;\subseteq\;\succ

(that is, for
a,b,c\in\mathcal{A}
,
a\succ b\succeq c
and
a\succeq b\succ c

imply
a\succ c
).
A
bounding pair
for a fixed LCSTRS with rules
\mathcal{R}
is an ordering pair

(\succ,\succeq)

on
T(\Sigma,\emptyset)
such that
s\succeq t
whenever
s\to_{\mathcal{R}}t

or
s\rhd t
.

We extend an ordering pair
(\succ,\succeq)
to non-ground terms with a constraint as follows.
A substitution
\gamma
respects
s,t,\psi
if
Var(s,t)\subseteq\mathit{dom}(\gamma)

and
\gamma
respects
\psi
. Then:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}s\succ t\ [\psi]&\Longleftrightarrow&s\gamma\succ t\gamma\text{ for all ground substitutions }\gamma\text{ that respect }s,t,\psi\\
s\succeq t\ [\psi]&\Longleftrightarrow&s\gamma\succeq t\gamma\text{ for all ground substitutions }\gamma\text{ that respect }s,t,\psi\end{array}

3.1.
Bounded Rewriting Induction

Traditionally, RI is a deduction system on proof states, which are pairs

\left(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{H}\right)
, where (in the existing literature),

\mathcal{E}
is a set of equations, describing all proof goals, and
\mathcal{H}
is the set
of induction hypotheses that have been assumed. At the start
\mathcal{E}
consists of
all equations that we want to prove to be inductive theorems, and

\mathcal{H}=\emptyset
.
With a deduction rule we may transform a proof state
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{H})
into another
proof state
(\mathcal{E}^{\prime},\mathcal{H}^{\prime})
, denoted
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{H})\vdash(\mathcal{E}^{\prime},\mathcal{H}^{\prime})
.
We write
\vdash^{*}
for the reflexive-transitive closure of
\vdash
.
Soundness of RI is guaranteed by the following principle:
“If
(\mathcal{E},\emptyset)\vdash^{*}(\emptyset,\mathcal{H})
for some set
\mathcal{H}
, then every equation in
\mathcal{E}
is an inductive theorem”.
Thus, we aim to make
\mathcal{E}
empty.

In bounded RI, we deviate in one respect: instead of letting
\mathcal{E}
be a set of equations, we will
use a set of
equation contexts
. This new notion lets us avoid the bureaucracy of
combining steps by keeping track of two
bounding terms
, which together really dictate a bound: we are only allowed to apply induction hypotheses below this bound.

{defi}

[Equation context]
Let a bounding pair
(\succ,\succeq)
be given.
Let
\bullet
be a fresh symbol, and define
\bullet\succ s
and
\bullet\succeq s
for all

s\in T(\Sigma,\mathcal{V})
, and also
\bullet\succeq\bullet
.
An
equation context

(\varsigma\ ;\ s\approx t\ ;\ \uptau)\ [\psi]

is a tuple of two elements
\varsigma,\uptau\in T(\Sigma,\mathcal{V})\cup\{\bullet\}
, two terms
s,t
and a constraint
\psi
.
We write
(\varsigma\ ;\ s\simeq t\ ;\ \uptau)\ [\psi]
(so with
\simeq
instead of
\approx
) to
denote either an equation context
(\varsigma\ ;\ s\approx t\ ;\ \uptau)\ [\psi]
or an
equation context
(\uptau\ ;\ t\approx s\ ;\ \varsigma)\ [\psi]
.
A substitution
\gamma

respects
an equation context

(\varsigma\ ;\ s\approx t\ ;\ \uptau)\ [\psi]

\gamma
respects
\psi

An equation context couples an equation with a bound on the induction: we
implicitly use the induction hypothesis: “all ground instances of an equation in
\mathcal{H}

that are strictly smaller than the current instance of

Remark 5

To avoid confusion we will clarify what we mean by soundness.

The deduction rules for Bounded RI are provided in

Figure 1
, and will be explained in detail in
subsection 3.2
.
While they assume a fixed bounding pair as given, in practice we can leave this
pair undecided, use the proof system to collect requirements about it,
and then select a suitable bounding pair at the end.

First, we introduce a particular notation, used by some of the deduction rules in
Figure 1
.

{defi}

[
\models^{\delta}
]
Let
\delta
be a substitution and
\varphi
,
\psi
be constraints.
We write
\psi\models^{\delta}\varphi

if
\delta(Var(\varphi))\subseteq\mathcal{V}\textit{al}\cup Var(\psi)
, and

\psi\Longrightarrow\varphi\delta
is a valid constraint.

Hence, if
\psi\models^{\delta}\varphi
and
\gamma
is a substitution that respects

\psi
, then
\gamma
also respects
\varphi\delta
.
This is for example used in the deduction rule (Simplify) to ensure that every ground instance of an equation

C[\ell\delta]\approx t\ [\psi]
can be reduced with a rewrite rule

\ell\to r\ [\varphi]
.

{exa}

Consider the equation
\mathsf{recdown}\ f\ i^{\prime}\ i\ a\approx t\ [i^{\prime}=i+1]
and rewrite rule

\mathsf{recdown}\ f\ n\ i\ a\to a\ [i<n]
, and let
\delta=[n:=i^{\prime}]
.
We have
(i<n)\delta=i<i^{\prime}
and
(i^{\prime}=i+1)\models^{\delta}(i<n)
holds, because

\triangleright

\delta(Var(i<n))=\delta(\{i,n\})=\{i,i^{\prime}\}\subseteq Var(i^{\prime}=i+1)=\{i,i^{\prime}\}

\triangleright

(i^{\prime}=i+1)\Longrightarrow(i<n)\delta
is valid

As we will see in
subsection 4.1
, the proof system in
Figure 1
satisfies a property called
Preserving Bounds
, meaning that it only produces a specific kind of equation contexts:

{defi}

[Bounded equation context]
A
bounded equation context
is an equation context
(\varsigma\ ;\ s\approx t\ ;\ \uptau)\ [\psi]
such that both

\varsigma\succeq s\ [\psi]
and
\uptau\succeq t\ [\psi]
.

This property plays an important role in the soundness proof of Bounded RI,
since it allows us to avoid ordering requirements that would otherwise
need to be added to
Figure 1
.

In many practical cases, we will also consider an even stronger restriction:

{defi}
[Strongly bounded equation context]
An equation context
(\varsigma\ ;\ s\approx t\ ;\ \uptau)\ [\psi]
is
strongly
bounded
if either

\varsigma=s
or
\varsigma\succ s\ [\psi]
, and also either

\uptau=t
or
\uptau\succ t\ [\psi]
.

Clearly, all equation contexts at the start of a RI deduction are strongly
bounded, as all initial equation contexts have
\varsigma=\uptau=\bullet
.
At first it may not be clear why
strongly bounded
is a strictly stronger
property than
bounded
. To illustrate this, consider the following example
with a well-founded ordering
\succ
whose restriction to integers is defined as

n\succ m\Longleftrightarrow n>m\wedge n\geq\mathsf{0}

Then
n\succeq m\ [n\geq m\wedge n\geq\mathsf{0}]
holds, but
we have neither
n=m
nor
n\succ m\ [n\geq m\wedge n\geq\mathsf{0}]
.

In practice, we highly benefit from strong boundedness because many of
the required inequalities in
Figure 1
can be established by
a straightforward syntactic check: whenever
\varsigma\neq s
, we immediately
conclude
\varsigma\succ s\ [\psi]
. While strong boundedness is
not

necessarily preserved by the rules of
Figure 1
, we can
maintain it by a suitable choice of reduction pair and careful application of
the (Hypothesis), (Generalize), and (Alter) rules.

3.2.
Explanation by example

We will now elaborate on the rules of
Figure 1
, and
illustrate their use through examples. We will also introduce the definitions
of
cover set
and
generalizing
/
altering
, which are used in

Figure 1
.
To start, we will consider the LCSTRS from
subsection 2.1
applied on the
equation
\mathsf{recdown}\ f\ n\ i\ a\approx\mathsf{tailup}\ f\ n\ i\ a
.

Following
4
, we aim to show that there is a set
\mathcal{H}
such that

(\mathcal{E}_{1},\emptyset)\vdash^{*}(\emptyset,\mathcal{H})\ \text{ with }\mathcal{E}_{1}:=\{(\bullet\ ;\ \mathsf{recdown}\ f\ n\ i\ a\approx\mathsf{tailup}\ f\ n\ i\ a\ ;\ \bullet)\ [\mathtt{true}]\}

We do
not
fully choose the bounding pair
(\succ,\succeq)
in advance. Rather, we will
use the proof process to accumulate requirements on the well-founded ordering
\succ
to be
used.
However, we precommit to a bounding pair
where

\succeq
is the reflexive closure of
\succ
,
and

s\succ t
whenever
s\to_{\mathcal{R}}t
or
s\rhd t
.
With these assumptions, it is easy to maintain the property that all equation
contexts are
strongly bounded
. We will ensure this invariant throughout
our RI proof.

(Induct)

We introduce the following way to start an induction proof.

(\mathcal{E}\uplus\{(\varsigma\ ;\ s\approx t\ ;\ \uptau)\ [\psi]\},\mathcal{H})

(\mathcal{E}\cup\{(s\ ;\ s\approx t\ ;\ t)\ [\psi]\},\mathcal{H}\cup\{s\approx t\ [\psi]\})

Here, two things happen.
First,
the current equation is added to the set

\mathcal{H}
of induction hypotheses, making it available for later application of
(Hypothesis) or (
\mathcal{H}
-Delete).
Second, the bounding terms
\varsigma,\uptau
are replaced by
s,t
.
This ensures that, when an induction hypothesis is applied, it is only on equations that are
strictly smaller than
s\approx t\ [\psi]
.

In our running example, we use (Induct) to obtain
(\mathcal{E}_{1},\emptyset)\vdash(\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{H}_{2})
where

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathcal{E}_{2}&=&\{(\varsigma_{2}\ ;\ \mathsf{recdown}\ f\ n\ i\ a\approx\mathsf{tailup}\ f\ n\ i\ a\ ;\ \uptau_{2})\ [\mathtt{true}]\}\\
\mathcal{H}_{2}&=&\{\mathsf{recdown}\ f\ n\ i\ a\approx\mathsf{tailup}\ f\ n\ i\ a\}\\
\end{array}

We will retain
\varsigma_{2}=\mathsf{recdown}\ f\ n\ i\ a
and
\uptau_{2}=\mathsf{tailup}\ f\ n\ i\ a
, for later reference.

(Case)

Comparing the equation in
\mathcal{E}_{2}
to
\mathcal{R}
, which of the rules should we apply?
As we will see in (Simplify), this requires information about how the variables
i,n
in the equation are instantiated;
in this example the reduction behavior depends on which of the two cases,
i<n
or
i\geq n
, holds.
This is where (Case) can help us, splitting an equation into multiple cases.
Of course, we have to make sure that the cases together cover the original equation.

{defi}

[Cover set]
A cover set of an equation
s\approx t\ [\psi]
is a set
\mathcal{C}

of pairs
(\delta,\varphi)
, with
\delta
a substitution and
\varphi
a
constraint, such that for every gsc substitution
\gamma
respecting

s\approx t\ [\psi]
, there exists
(\delta,\varphi)\in\mathcal{C}

and a substitution
\sigma
such that

\gamma(x)=\delta(x)\sigma
for all
x\in\mathit{dom}(\gamma)
, and

\sigma
respects
\psi\delta\wedge\varphi
.
(Hence,
s\gamma\approx t\gamma\ [\psi\gamma]
is an instance of

s\delta\approx t\delta\ [\psi\delta\wedge\varphi]
.)

Now, the deduction rule (Case) reads as follows:

(\mathcal{E}\uplus\{(\varsigma\ ;\ s\approx t\ ;\ \uptau)\ [\psi]\},\mathcal{H})

\begin{aligned} &\mathcal{C}\text{ a cover set of }s\approx t\ [\psi]\\
&\text{(see \autoref{def:coverset})}\end{aligned}

(\mathcal{E}\cup\{(\varsigma\delta\ ;\ s\delta\approx t\delta\ ;\ \uptau\delta)\ [\psi\delta\wedge\varphi]\mid(\delta,\varphi)\in\mathcal{C}\},\mathcal{H})

Continuing our example, we observe the only gsc terms of type
\mathsf{int}

are values (since we have inextensible theory sorts). Hence,
\mathcal{C}=\{([],i<n),\ ([],i\geq n)\}

is a cover set of
\mathsf{recdown}\ f\ n\ i\ a\approx\mathsf{tailup}\ f\ n\ i\ a
.
Using (Case), we obtain
(\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{H}_{2})\vdash(\mathcal{E}_{3},\mathcal{H}_{2})
with

\mathcal{E}_{3}=\left\{\begin{aligned} (\varsigma_{2}\ ;\ \mathsf{recdown}\ f\ n\ i\ a\approx\mathsf{tailup}\ f\ n\ i\ a\ ;\ \uptau_{2})\ [i<n]\\
(\varsigma_{2}\ ;\ \mathsf{recdown}\ f\ n\ i\ a\approx\mathsf{tailup}\ f\ n\ i\ a\ ;\ \uptau_{2})\ [i\geq n]\\
\end{aligned}\right\}

Note that the bounding terms
\varsigma_{2},\uptau_{2}
are unchanged because the
substitutions in the cover set were both empty. This is, however, not true in general
(and we will see an alternative situation later in this section).
Strong boundedness is still satisfied, by
\varsigma=s
and
\uptau=t
.

(Simplify)

Next,
we can use a rule
\ell\to r\ [\varphi]\in\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{R}_{calc}
to rewrite an equation

.

(\mathcal{E}\uplus\{(\varsigma\ ;\ C[\ell\delta]\simeq t\ ;\ \uptau)\ [\psi]\},\mathcal{H})

\begin{aligned} &\ell\to r\ [\varphi]\in\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{R}_{calc}\text{ and }\psi\models^{\delta}\varphi\end{aligned}

(\mathcal{E}\cup\{(\varsigma\ ;\ C[r\delta]\approx t\ ;\ \uptau)\ [\psi]\},\mathcal{H})

The requirement
\psi\models^{\delta}\varphi
makes sure that the
\delta

is actually applicable.
The bounding terms are not affected by the reduction.

Continuing our example,
the first equation in
\mathcal{E}_{3}
has constraint

, so we apply (Simplify) on both sides, using
(R1)
and
(R3)
.
For the second equation, we also apply (Simplify) to both sides, using
(R2)
and

We obtain
(\mathcal{E}_{3},\mathcal{H}_{2})\vdash^{*}(\mathcal{E}_{4},\mathcal{H}_{2})

\mathcal{E}_{4}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\ \ \ (\varsigma_{2}\ ;\ a\approx a\ ;\ \uptau_{2})&[i<n]\\
(\varsigma_{2}\ ;\ f\ i\ (\mathsf{recdown}\ f\ n\ (i-\mathsf{1})\ a)\approx\mathsf{tailup}\ f\ (n+\mathsf{1})\ i\ (f\ n\ a)\ ;\ \uptau_{2})&[i\geq n]\\
\end{array}\right\}

Note that for both equations

\varsigma_{2}\succ s\ [\psi]
and
\uptau_{2}\succ t\ [\psi]
, since

\to_{\mathcal{R}}
is included in
\succ

(Delete)

The following deduction rule allows us to remove an equation that has an unsatisfiable constraint, or whose two sides are syntactically equal.

(\mathcal{E}\uplus\{(\varsigma\ ;\ s\approx t\ ;\ \uptau)\ [\psi]\},\mathcal{H})

\begin{aligned} &\psi\text{ unsatisfiable, or }s=t\end{aligned}

(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{H})

In our example, we use (Delete) we obtain

(\mathcal{E}_{4},\mathcal{H}_{2})\vdash(\mathcal{E}_{5},\mathcal{H}_{2})
with

\mathcal{E}_{5}=\left\{(\varsigma_{2}\ ;\ f\ i\ (\mathsf{recdown}\ f\ n\ (i-\mathsf{1})\ a)\approx\mathsf{tailup}\ f\ (n+\mathsf{1})\ i\ (f\ n\ a)\ ;\ \uptau_{2})\ [i\geq n]\right\}

(Alter)

It is often useful to rewrite an equation context to another that might be syntactically
different, but has the same ground semi-constructor instances.
Indeed, this may even be necessary, for instance to support the application of a
rewrite rule through (Simplify). This is supported by the deduction rule (Alter), which relies on
the following definition:

{defi}

We say that an equation context
(\varsigma^{\prime}\ ;\ s^{\prime}\approx t^{\prime}\ ;\ \uptau^{\prime})\ [\psi^{\prime}]

\triangleright

generalizes

(\varsigma\ ;\ s\approx t\ ;\ \uptau)\ [\psi]
if for every gsc substitution
\gamma
that respects

(\varsigma\ ;\ s\approx t\ ;\ \uptau)\ [\psi]
there is a substitution
\delta
that respects
(\varsigma^{\prime}\ ;\ s^{\prime}\approx t^{\prime}\ ;\ \uptau^{\prime})\ [\psi^{\prime}]

such that
s\gamma=s^{\prime}\delta
and
t\gamma=t^{\prime}\delta
, and
\varsigma\gamma\succeq\varsigma^{\prime}\delta
and
\uptau\gamma\succeq\uptau^{\prime}\delta
.

\triangleright

alters

(\varsigma\ ;\ s\approx t\ ;\ \uptau)\ [\psi]
if both

–

(\varsigma^{\prime}\ ;\ s^{\prime}\approx t^{\prime}\ ;\ \uptau^{\prime})\ [\psi^{\prime}]
generalizes
(\varsigma\ ;\ s\approx t\ ;\ \uptau)\ [\psi]
, and

–

(\varsigma\ ;\ s\approx t\ ;\ \uptau)\ [\psi]
generalizes
(\varsigma^{\prime}\ ;\ s^{\prime}\approx t^{\prime}\ ;\ \uptau^{\prime})\ [\psi^{\prime}]
.

Now, (Alter) is defined as follows

(\mathcal{E}\uplus\{(\varsigma\ ;\ s\approx t\ ;\ \uptau)\ [\psi]\},\mathcal{H})

\begin{aligned} &(\varsigma^{\prime}\ ;\ s^{\prime}\approx t^{\prime}\ ;\ \uptau^{\prime})\ [\psi^{\prime}]\text{ alters }(\varsigma\ ;\ s\approx t\ ;\ \uptau)\ [\psi]\\
&\text{and }\varsigma^{\prime}\succeq s^{\prime}\ [\psi]\text{ and }\uptau^{\prime}\succeq t^{\prime}\ [\psi]\end{aligned}

(\mathcal{E}\cup\{(\varsigma^{\prime}\ ;\ s^{\prime}\approx t^{\prime}\ ;\ \uptau^{\prime})\ [\psi^{\prime}]\},\mathcal{H})

There are many ways to use this deduction rule, but following the discussion in
[
18
]
, we
will particularly consider two ways:

(1)

Replacing a constraint by an equi-satisfiable one: that is, altering

(\varsigma\ ;\ s\approx t\ ;\ \uptau)\ [\psi]
into

(\varsigma\ ;\ s\approx t\ ;\ \uptau)\ [\psi^{\prime}]
if

(\exists\vec{x}.\psi)\Longleftrightarrow(\exists\vec{y}.\psi^{\prime})
is logically valid,
where
\{\vec{x}\}=Var(\psi)\setminus Var(\varsigma,s,t,\uptau)
and

\{\vec{y}\}=Var(\psi^{\prime})\setminus Var(\varsigma,s,t,\uptau)
.
(This assumes that the system has inextensible theory sorts; if not, we must
also require that
Var(\psi)\setminus\{\vec{x}\}=Var(\psi^{\prime})\setminus\{\vec{y}\}
.)

A particular example of this case is to replace

(\varsigma\ ;\ s\approx t\ ;\ \uptau)\ [\psi]
by

(\varsigma\ ;\ s\approx t\ ;\ \uptau)\ [\psi\wedge\linebreak x_{1}=u_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge x_{n}=u_{n}]
, where
all
x_{i}
are fresh variables, with
x_{i}\notin Var(u_{j})
for
j\leq i
.

(2)

Replacing variables by equivalent variables or values: that is, altering

(\varsigma\ ;\ s\approx t\ ;\ \uptau)\ [\psi]
into

(\varsigma\gamma\ ;\ s\gamma\approx t\gamma\ ;\ \uptau\gamma)\ [\psi]
if

\gamma=[x_{1}:=u_{1},\dots,x_{n}:=u_{n}]
and
\psi\Longrightarrow x_{1}=u_{1}\wedge\dots x_{n}=u_{n}
is valid, where
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
are variables and
u_{1},\dots,u_{n}
are each
variables or values.

Back to our example! By case
1
above, we can use (Alter) to obtain

(\mathcal{E}_{5},\mathcal{H}_{2})\vdash(\mathcal{E}_{6},\mathcal{H}_{2})
:

\mathcal{E}_{6}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}(\varsigma_{2}\ ;\ f\ i\ (\mathsf{recdown}\ f\ n\ (i-\mathsf{1})\ a)\approx\mathsf{tailup}\ f\ (n+\mathsf{1})\ i\ (f\ n\ a)\ ;\ \uptau_{2})\\
{}\hfill{}[i^{\prime}=i-\mathsf{1}\wedge n^{\prime}=n+\mathsf{1}\wedge i\geq n]\\
\end{array}\right\}

To allow this rule to be applied, we must have
\varsigma_{2}\succeq f\ i\ (\mathsf{recdown}\ f\ n\ (i-\mathsf{1})\ a)\ [\varphi]

and
\uptau_{2}\succeq\mathsf{tailup}\ f\ (n+\mathsf{1})\ i\ (f\ n\ a)\ [\varphi]
where
\varphi
is the constraint

i^{\prime}=i-\mathsf{1}\wedge n^{\prime}=n+\mathsf{1}\wedge i\geq n
. But this follows immediately from the fact that our
previous equation context was strongly bounded:
if
\varsigma\succ s\ [i\geq n]
then also
\varsigma\succ s\ [i^{\prime}=i-\mathsf{1}\wedge n^{\prime}=n+\mathsf{1}\wedge i\geq n]
, and similar for
\uptau\succ t\ [i\geq n]
. Moreover, strong boundedness is clearly still satisfied.

(Generalize)

Consider the latter equation context. We could try to continue with the rules as they are,

3.3.
Completeness

Thus far, we have used rewriting induction to prove inductive theorems.
However, we can also use it to derive that some equations are
not
inductive theorems.

{exa}

Consider

\Sigma_{terms}=\{\mathsf{G}::(\mathsf{int}\to\mathsf{int})\to\mathsf{int}\to\mathsf{int}\to\mathsf{int},\ \mathsf{H}::(\mathsf{int}\to\mathsf{int})\to\mathsf{int}\to\mathsf{int}\to\mathsf{int}\to\mathsf{int}\}

with rules

\begin{array}[]{rcll}\mathsf{G}\ f\ n\ x&\to&\mathsf{G}\ f\ (n-\mathsf{1})\ (f\ x)&[n>\mathsf{0}]\\
\mathsf{G}\ f\ n\ x&\to&x&[n\leq\mathsf{0}]\\
\mathsf{H}\ f\ n\ m\ x&\to&\mathsf{H}\ f\ (n-\mathsf{1})\ m\ (f\ x)&[n>\mathsf{0}]\\
\mathsf{H}\ f\ n\ m\ x&\to&\mathsf{H}\ f\ (m-\mathsf{1})\ n\ (f\ x)&[m>\mathsf{0}]\\
\mathsf{H}\ f\ n\ m\ x&\to&x&[n\leq\mathsf{0}\wedge m\leq\mathsf{0}]\\
\end{array}

Intuitively,
\mathsf{G}
computes the function
(f,n,x)\mapsto f^{n}(x)
for all
n\geq 0
, and
\mathsf{H}
computes the function
(f,n,m,x)\mapsto f^{(n+m)}(x)
for all
n,m\geq 0
.
In particular, we have an inductive theorem
\mathsf{G}\ f\ k\ x\approx\mathsf{H}\ f\ n\ m\ x\ [k=n+m\wedge n\geq\mathsf{0}\wedge m\geq\mathsf{0}]
.
The condition
n,m\geq 0
is really necessary:
the equation

\mathsf{G}\ f\ k\ x\approx\mathsf{H}\ f\ n\ m\ x\ [k=n+m]
is
not
an inductive theorem.
For example, there does not even exist a single ground substitution
\gamma=[x:=t]
such that
{(\mathsf{G}\ (+\ \mathsf{1})\ (-\mathsf{1})\ x)\gamma}\mathrel{\leftrightarrow^{*}_{\mathcal{R}}}{(\mathsf{H}\ (+\ \mathsf{1})\ \mathsf{1}\ (\mathsf{-2})\ x)\gamma}
.

To prove non-equivalences, we will extend RI with a new deduction rule (Disprove).
However, we must take some care: it is entirely possible, in the course of a RI proof, to end up
with unsound equations even if the original equations are all inductive theorems. For example,

\mathsf{recdown}\ +\ n\ \mathsf{0}\ a\approx\mathsf{recdown}\ +\ n\ (\mathsf{-1})\ a
is an
inductive theorem, but if we use (Generalize) in the RI proof to obtain an equation

\mathsf{recdown}\ +\ n\ i\ a\approx\mathsf{recdown}\ +\ n\ i^{\prime}\ a\ [i^{\prime}=i-\mathsf{1}]
, we
will encounter a contradiction. Hence, we must carefully consider the derivation path.

{defi}

[Completeness Property]
We say a deduction rule has the Completeness Property if, whenever

\mathcal{R}
is a weakly normalizing, quasi-reductive, ground confluent LCSTRS, if
we can deduce

(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{H})\vdash(\mathcal{E}^{\prime},\mathcal{H}^{\prime})
by this rule, then

\leftrightarrow_{\mathcal{E}}\cup\leftrightarrow_{\mathcal{H}}\ \subseteq\ \leftrightarrow^{*}_{\mathcal{R}}\text{ on ground terms}\quad\ \ \Longrightarrow\ \ \quad\leftrightarrow_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}\cup\leftrightarrow_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}}\ \subseteq\ \leftrightarrow^{*}_{\mathcal{R}}\text{ on ground terms}

Here,
\leftrightarrow_{\mathcal{E}}
is the relation
\leftrightarrow_{\mathcal{A}}
where

\mathcal{A}=\{s\approx t\ [\psi]\mid(\varsigma\ ;\ s\approx t\ ;\ \uptau)\ [\psi]\in\mathcal{E}\}
.

Lemma 8
.

The derivation rules (Simplify), (Case), (Delete), (Induct), (Hypothesis), (Alter) and (
\mathcal{H}
-Delete) all have the Completeness Property, as does the limitation of (Semi-constructor) to cases with

f\in\Sigma
.

Hence, all deduction rules of
Figure 1
other than
(Generalize), (Postulate) and the general case of (Semi-constructor), are complete.

Proof 3.1
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{R}
is weakly normalizing, quasi-reductive and ground confluent, and that

\leftrightarrow_{\mathcal{E}}\cup\leftrightarrow_{\mathcal{H}}\ \subseteq\ \leftrightarrow^{*}_{\mathcal{R}}

on ground terms.
Write
\mathcal{A}=\{s\approx t\ [\psi]\mid(\varsigma\ ;\ s\approx t\ ;\ \uptau)\ [\psi]\in\mathcal{E}\}
.

For any
s\approx t\ [\psi]\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
we have either
s\approx t\ [\psi]\in\mathcal{H}
or, if
(Induct) was used,
s\approx t\ [\psi]\in\mathcal{A}
. Either way, a step by this equation
can also be done by
\leftrightarrow_{\mathcal{H}}\cup\leftrightarrow_{\mathcal{E}}
, so indeed

\leftrightarrow_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}}\ \subseteq\ \leftrightarrow^{*}_{\mathcal{R}}
on ground terms.
To show that
\leftrightarrow_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}\;\subseteq\;\leftrightarrow^{*}_{\mathcal{R}}
on ground terms,
let
\gamma
be a ground substitution respecting some equation context in
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
.
Note that we only need to consider equation contexts in
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{E}
because for an
equation context
(\varsigma\ ;\ s\approx t\ ;\ \uptau)\ [\psi]\in\mathcal{E}
we can immediately conclude that

{s\gamma}\mathrel{\leftrightarrow^{*}_{\mathcal{R}}}{t\gamma}
by assumption.
We consider every deduction rule in the lemma.

(Simplify), (Hypothesis).

Then
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{E}=\{(\varsigma\ ;\ C[r\delta]\approx t\ ;\ \uptau)\ [\psi]\}
while

\mathcal{A}
contains
C[\ell\delta]\approx t\ [\psi]
for either some

\ell\to r\ [\varphi]\in\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{R}_{calc}
or some

\ell\simeq r\ [\varphi]\in\mathcal{H}
,
and
\delta
such that
\psi\models^{\delta}\varphi
.
Since
\gamma
respects
\psi
we have
{C[\ell\delta]\gamma}\mathrel{\leftrightarrow^{*}_{\mathcal{R}}}{t\gamma}
by
assumption on
\leftrightarrow_{\mathcal{A}}
.
Furthermore, in either case we have
{C[r\delta]\gamma}\mathrel{\leftrightarrow^{*}_{\mathcal{R}}}{C[\ell\delta]\gamma}
,
whether because
\leftarrow_{\mathcal{R}}
is included in
\leftrightarrow_{\mathcal{R}}^{*}
or by assumption on

\leftrightarrow_{\mathcal{H}}
.

(Case).

Then the only elements of
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{E}
have a form

(\varsigma\delta\ ;\ s\delta\approx t\delta\ ;\ \uptau\delta)\ [\psi\delta\wedge\varphi]
, where

s\approx t\ [\psi]\in\mathcal{A}
.
As
\gamma
respects
\psi\delta\wedge\varphi
, it respects
\psi\delta
.
Therefore,
\sigma=\delta\gamma
respects
\psi
, and is a ground substitution;

\mathit{dom}(\sigma)\supseteq Var(s,t)
.
Hence,
s\delta\gamma={s\sigma}\mathrel{\leftrightarrow^{*}_{\mathcal{R}}}{t\sigma}=t\delta\gamma
, as desired.

(Delete), (
\mathcal{H}
-Delete).

We have
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{E}=\emptyset
so there is nothing to prove.

(Induct).

For all
(\varsigma\ ;\ s\approx t\ ;\ \uptau)\ [\psi]\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
we have
s\approx t\ [\psi]\in\mathcal{A}
.

(Alter).

Then
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{E}=\{(\varsigma^{\prime}\ ;\ s^{\prime}\approx t^{\prime}\ ;\ \uptau^{\prime})\ [\psi^{\prime}]\}
and there is

4.2.
Multiset orderings

The proofs in the following subsections make substantial use of the multiset
extension of an ordering pair
(\succ,\succeq)
on
\mathcal{A}=T(\Sigma,\mathcal{V})
.
We particularly consider multisets containing exactly 2 elements, which allows
for a much simpler representation than the common definitions in the literature
(where multisets may have infinite size). Here, we present our version of the
multiset extension of an ordering pair, as follows:

For an ordering pair
(\succ,\succeq)
on
\mathcal{A}
, a
size-2 multiset
over
\mathcal{A}
is an unordered pair
\{\!\{a,b\}\!\}
with
a,b\in\mathcal{A}
(it is allows to have
a=b
). We say that:

•

\{\!\{a_{1},a_{2}\}\!\}=\{\!\{b_{1},b_{2}\}\!\}
if either
a_{1}=b_{1}
and
a_{2}=b_{2}
,
or
a_{1}=b_{2}
and
a_{2}=b_{1}

•

\{\!\{a_{1},a_{2}\}\!\}\succeq_{mul}\{\!\{b_{1},b_{2}\}\!\}
if one of the following holds:

–

there is some
i\in\{1,2\}
such that both
a_{i}\succ b_{1}
and
a_{i}\succ b_{2}

–

either
a_{1}\succeq b_{1}
and
a_{2}\succeq b_{2}
, or
a_{1}\succeq b_{2}
and

a_{2}\succeq b_{1}

•

\{\!\{a_{1},a_{2}\}\!\}\succ_{mul}\{\!\{b_{1},b_{2}\}\!\}
if one of the following holds:

–

there is some
i\in\{1,2\}
such that both
a_{i}\succ b_{1}
and
a_{i}\succ b_{2}

–

there exist
i,j\in\{1,2\}
such that
a_{i}\succ b_{j}
and
a_{3-i}\succeq b_{3-j}

Note that
\{\!\{a_{1},a_{2}\}\!\}\succ_{mul}\{\!\{b_{1},b_{2}\}\!\}
implies

\{\!\{a_{1},a_{2}\}\!\}\succeq_{mul}\{\!\{b_{1},b_{2}\}\!\}
, because
a_{i}\succ b_{j}
implies

a_{i}\succeq b_{j}
.
Hence, we easily see that the multiset extension
(\succ_{mul},\succeq_{mul})
is
itself an ordering pair on size-2 multisets over
\mathcal{A}
.

\mathcal{E}
be a set of equation contexts.
An equation context

To start with the proof, we observe that both relations

and
\mathrel{\xleftrightarrow[\mathcal{R};\mathcal{H};\mathcal{E}]{\{a,b\}\ *}}

We also immediately see that our relations are preserved under contexts:

Lemma 13
.

Let

{enumerate*}

If

.

, then
D[u]\mathrel{\xleftrightarrow[\mathcal{R};\mathcal{H};\mathcal{E}]{\{a,b\}\ *}}D[v]
.

Proof 4.4
.

Obvious by definition of the relation: in each place where we use a context

we can also
use the context

Similarly, the relations are preserved under an increase of the bounding terms

Lemma 14
.

Suppose

.

{enumerate*}

If

u\mathrel{\xleftrightarrow[\mathcal{R};\mathcal{H};\mathcal{E}]{\{c,d\}\ }}v

If
u\mathrel{\xleftrightarrow[\mathcal{R};\mathcal{H};\mathcal{E}]{\{a,b\}\ *}}v

u\mathrel{\xleftrightarrow[\mathcal{R};\mathcal{H};\mathcal{E}]{\{c,d\}\ *}}v

Proof 4.5

The second statement follows immediately from the first. For the first, recall that

Hence, both cases

by case

there exists some

.

In the proof of

The most difficult deduction rule in these proofs is (Induct). To present the

{defi}

[Base Soundness Property]

We say a deduction rule has the Base Soundness Property if, whenever we can deduce

(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{H})\vdash(\mathcal{E}^{\prime},\mathcal{H}^{\prime})
by this rule and all equation contexts in
\mathcal{E}
are
bounded, then
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}=\mathcal{H}
and for all
(\varsigma\ ;\ s\approx t\ ;\ \uptau)\ [\psi]\in\mathcal{E}\setminus\mathcal{E}^{\prime}

\gamma
that respects
\psi
, we
have:
s\gamma\mathrel{\xleftrightarrow[\mathcal{R};\mathcal{H};\mathcal{E}^{\prime}]{\{\varsigma\gamma,\uptau\gamma\}\ *}}t\gamma
.

Now we can see that all rules other than (Induct) indeed have this property.

4.4.
Soundness of Bounded Rewriting Induction

Now, instead of proving
4

Theorem 23

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a weakly normalizing, quasi-reductive LCSTRS;
let
(\succ,\succeq)
be a bounding pair, and
\mathcal{E}_{1}

Suppose

\mathcal{R}/\mathcal{H}_{i}/\mathcal{E}_{N}

bounded ground convertibility rather than merely ground convertibility. Moreover, it easily allows
us to extend the system of

properties that are often easy to prove

\mathrel{\xleftrightarrow[\mathcal{R};\mathcal{H};\mathcal{E}]{\{a,b\}\ *}}

A first-order unconstrained term rewriting system is ground confluent when all its

We can use rewriting induction as a method to prove bounded ground convertibility.
We will generalize this result to LCSTRSs with bounded rewriting induction.

6.
Finding a Bounding Pair

The bounding pair
(\succ,\succeq)
is an important component of bounded
rewriting induction. Yet, how to find it in practice? As we have seen, we do
not have to fix the pair in advance, but can use the process of bounded
rewriting induction to accumulate requirements.
Let us say the process delivers a set
\mathsf{REQS}
of requirements of the form

s\succ t\ [\varphi]
or
s\succeq t\ [\varphi]
with
s\neq t
(if

s=t
, then we already know
s\succeq t\ [\varphi]
is true and

s\succ t\ [\varphi]
is false if
\varphi
is satisfiable).
But having done so, we are still left with the difficulty of proving that a
bounding pair for those requirements exists. We discuss two different
approaches to define such a pair.

6.1.
Using a reduction ordering

A
reduction ordering
is a monotonic (i.e.
s\sqsupset t
implies
C[s]\sqsupset C[t]
for every context
C[\square]
) well-founded partial ordering
on the set of terms.
Monotonicity does not imply that
s\sqsupset t
whenever
s\rhd t

but we
can
use a reduction ordering that includes
\to_{\mathcal{R}}
to construct
a bounding pair, as follows:

\triangleright

define
a\succ_{1}b
if
a\sqsupset b
or
a\rhd b
;
define
a\succ b
if
a\succ_{1}^{+}b
, and
a\succeq b
if
a\succ_{1}^{*}b
;

\triangleright

require that
\ell\gamma\sqsupset r\gamma
for each
\ell\to r\ [\varphi]\in\mathcal{R}
and ground substitution
\gamma
that respects
\varphi

The monotonicity requirement ensures that well-foundedness of
\sqsupset
is
preserved in
\succ
: if
s\sqsupset t=C[t^{\prime}]
and
t^{\prime}\sqsupset u
, then
s\sqsupset C[t^{\prime}]\sqsupset C[u]
, so any infinite sequence of
\succ
steps can
be converted into an infinite sequence of
\sqsupset
steps.

There are various methods to define a reduction ordering in traditional term
rewriting, with recursive path orderings and polynomial interpretations being
among the most well-known. Unfortunately, there are not as many methods for
higher-order or constrained term rewriting, and even fewer for the combination
(although a variant of the recursive path ordering for LCSTRSs exists

[
16
]
).
That being said, while the method of Bounded RI is defined on LCSTRSs, it can
also be used on unconstrained first-order term rewriting systems, and there we
have many methods at our disposal.

Using a rewrite relation

However, it is worth noting that if
\mathcal{Q}
is a set of rules such that
\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{Q}
is a terminating rewrite system, then
\to_{\mathcal{Q}\cup\mathcal{R}}^{+}
is by
definition a reduction ordering. So, if every
s\succ t\ [\varphi]
or

s\succeq t\ [\varphi]
in
\mathsf{REQS}
gives a valid rewrite rule
s\to t\ [\varphi]
, we can simply let
\mathcal{Q}
be this set of rules, and use any method to
prove termination. Thus, we could in particular use the dependency pair
framework
[
8
,
14
]
, which allows reduction orderings to
be used in a more liberal way by requiring
weak
instead of full
monotonicity.

A downside of this approach is that it is possible to encounter requirements
that are not valid rules, and this is especially common in higher-order
rewriting since the left-hand side of a rule may not have a variable as the
head, and must have the same type as the right-hand side.
For example, suppose
\mathsf{REQS}\ni\mathsf{f}\ (F\ x)\ y\succ y-\mathsf{1}\ [x>y]
,
with
\mathsf{f}::\mathsf{int}\to\mathsf{int}\to\mathsf{list}
. We cannot include a rule

\mathsf{f}\ (F\ x)\ y\to y-\mathsf{1}\ [x>y]
in
\mathcal{Q}
, because the two sides
have a different type (
\mathsf{list}
versus
\mathsf{int}
). Even if they had the same
type, the only dependency pair framework for LCSTRSs that has thus far been
defined
[
15
]
does not support rules whose left-hand side
contains applied variables, so the subterm
F\ x
is problematic.

A solution to this issue is to not derive
\mathcal{Q}
from the set
\mathsf{REQS}
directly,
but merely to ensure that
s\gamma\ (\to_{\mathcal{Q}\cup\mathcal{R}}\cup\rhd)^{+}\ t\gamma
for all
s\succ t\ [\varphi]
or

s\succeq t\ [\varphi]
in
\mathsf{REQS}
and
\gamma
that respect

\varphi
. For example, if we introduce a new function symbol

\mathsf{inttolist}::\mathsf{int}\to\mathsf{list}
, then the above requirement is
handled by including a rule
\mathsf{f}\ z\ y\to\mathsf{inttolist}\ (y-\mathsf{1})\ [\mathtt{true}]
in
\mathcal{Q}
.
While it is not immediately obvious how to build such an abstraction in
general, at least it is easy to avoid the issue of different types through
the introduction of constructors like
\mathsf{inttolist}
.

An advantage of using a reduction relation is that it generates a bounding pair
that satisfies the premise of
11
. Hence,
by taking some care with the use of (Hypothesis), (Alter) and (Generalize), we
can ensure that equation contexts are always
strongly
bounded. This
yields substantially fewer ordering requirements – and thus an easier proof
search.

6.2.
Separating top and inner steps

The idea explored above – of deriving our bounding pair from a single
reordering – is quite powerful and allows us to reuse existing termination
methods. In particular the approach of letting
\sqsupset
be a terminating
relation
\to_{\mathcal{Q}\cup\mathcal{R}}
is reminiscent of traditional ways of
using RI (e.g.,
[
22
,
12
,
13
,
18
]
), where
the induction hypotheses in
\mathcal{H}
are oriented as rules, and we prove
termination of
\to_{\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{H}}
.

However, in these approaches we do not fully take advantage of the weaker requirements in the
present setting. In particular, the elements of
\mathsf{REQS}
do not need to be oriented with a

monotonic
relation. For example, if
\mathcal{R}=\{\mathsf{f}\ \mathsf{a}\to\mathsf{f}\ \mathsf{b}\}

and
\mathsf{REQS}=\{\mathsf{b}\succ\mathsf{a}\}
, there is no reduction ordering
\sqsupset

that orients
\mathsf{REQS}
, since then we would need both
\mathsf{f}\ \mathsf{a}\sqsupset\mathsf{f}\ \mathsf{b}
and
\mathsf{f}\ \mathsf{b}\sqsupset\mathsf{f}\ \mathsf{a}
(by monotonicity). Yet, there
are

bounding pairs for these requirements: e.g., the relation
(\to_{\{\mathsf{b}\to\mathsf{a}\ [\mathtt{true}]\},\mathtt{top}}\cup\to_{\mathcal{R}}\cup\rhd)^{+}
, whose subrelation
\to_{\mathcal{Q},\mathtt{top}}
is defined by:

{defi}

For a set
\mathcal{Q}
of triples

of two terms and a constraint, let

(\ell,r,\varphi)\in\mathcal{Q}
and a substitution
\gamma
that respects
\varphi
, such that

and

.

In terms of the (first-order) dependency pair framework, the difference can roughly be
summarised as follows: termination of
\to_{\mathcal{Q}\cup\mathcal{R}}
coincides with finitess of the DP
problem
(\mathtt{DP}(\mathcal{Q}\cup\mathcal{R}),\mathcal{Q}\cup\mathcal{R})
, whereas termination of
\to_{\mathcal{Q},\mathtt{top}}\cup\to_{\mathcal{R}}\cup\rhd
coincides with finiteness of

(\mathtt{DP}(\mathcal{Q}\cup\mathcal{R}),\mathcal{R})
.
In higher-order rewriting, there are multiple dependency pair frameworks, and their full introduction
requires some theoretical development that is beyond the scope of this paper. However, we will
show how the question whether a suitable bounding pair exists can be reduced to the existence of
something much like a dependendency pair chain.

Reduction triples

To directly investigate the presence of strong chains we can use an extension of
the notion of reduction ordering:

Corollary 36
.

A reduction triple is a triple
(>,\gtrsim,\geq)
of three relations such that:

•

>
is a well-founded partial ordering on the set of terms

•

\gtrsim
is a quasi-ordering on the set of terms, such that
s>t\gtrsim u
implies

s>u

•

\geq
is a monotonic quasi-ordering on the set of terms, such that
s\geq t
implies

s\gtrsim t

There exists no infinite strong chain if we can find a reduction triple

(>,\gtrsim,\geq)
such that:

•

for all
(\ell,p,\varphi)\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{strong}}
and all
\gamma
that respect
\varphi
:

\ell\gamma>p\gamma

•

for all
(\ell,p,\varphi)\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{weak}}
and all
\gamma
that respect
\varphi
:

\ell\gamma\gtrsim p\gamma

•

for all
\ell\to r\ [\varphi]\in\mathcal{R}
and all
\gamma
that respect
\varphi
:

\ell\gamma\geq r\gamma

The advantage of a reduction triple compared to a reduction ordering is that the
monotonicity requirement is replaced by
weak
monotonicity: only
\geq

needs to be monotonic. It also allows us to more easily take advantage of the
difference between
\succ
and
\succeq
requirements.

Reduction triples can be constructed using for instance argument filterings or
weakly monotonic algebras. While there has not yet been extensive research in
this direction for higher-order constrained rewriting, this is a natural
direction for future work.

Obtaining new deduction rules more easily

In
section 4
, we set out to prove soundness and completeness of
Bounded RI in a modular way: rather than merely proving that the system as given
in

4

Let us discuss two naturally arising situations for which we can exploit this approach to introduce a new deduction rule, and establish its soundness relatively easy.

Calculations

In the definition of Bounded RI, we follow the approach of
[
18
]
for
the definition of the (Simplify) (and (Hypothesis)) rules: these rules are much
more basic than the corresponding rule in the first-order RI definition for
LCTRSs
[
13
]
which uses a more sophisticated definition of
“constrained reduction”. Essentially, a slightly weaker version of the
(Alter) step is included in the (Simplify) rule of
[
13
]

The price for this simplicity, however, is that we often require extra steps,
especially when it comes to calculation rules. For example, in

subsection 3.2
we encountered the equation (for the sake of clarity we
omit the bounding terms of the equation context):

f\ i\ (\mathsf{recdown}\ f\ n\ (i-\mathsf{1})\ a)\approx\mathsf{tailup}\ f\ (n+\mathsf{1})\ i\ (f\ n\ a)\ [i\geq n]

Here, we first had to apply (Alter) to obtain the equation

f\ i\ (\mathsf{recdown}\ f\ n\ (i-\mathsf{1})\ a)\approx\mathsf{tailup}\ f\ (n+\mathsf{1})\ i\ (f\ n\ a)\ [i^{\prime}=i-\mathsf{1}\wedge n^{\prime}=n+\mathsf{1}\wedge i\geq n]

turning the equation into

f\ i\ (\mathsf{recdown}\ f\ n\ i^{\prime}\ a)\approx\mathsf{tailup}\ f\ n^{\prime}\ i\ (f\ n\ a)\ [i^{\prime}=i-\mathsf{1}\wedge n^{\prime}=n+\mathsf{1}\wedge i\geq n]

With constrained reductions, we could do the (Simplify) steps directly to obtain
the same result.
Fortunately, we can regain this facility by observing that the following deduction rule

(Calc)

(\mathcal{E}\uplus\{(\varsigma\ ;\ C[s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}]\approx t\ ;\ \uptau)\ [\psi]\},\mathcal{H})

&s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\text{ theory terms}\\
&x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\text{ distinct fresh}\\

Therefore, it immediately satisfies the two invariants.

We may use (Calc) to replace an equation

9.
Comparison to related work

As briefly mentioned in
section 3
, improvements on the termination requirements for the basic RI system
[
22
]
were already introduced in
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
.
These works also explore ways to have more flexibility in the construction of a well-founded ordering
\succ
, either fixed beforehand (e.g., the lexicographic path ordering), or constructed
during or after the proof.
Essentially, they already employed a bounding pair
(\succ,\succeq)
, using a second (milder) ordering
\succeq
, to allow for reduction steps
with an induction hypothesis to be oriented with
\succeq
rather than the default

\succ
.
However, there are differences compared to our approach using equation contexts and bounding terms.
In our case, we do not orient induction hypotheses themselves: we only require that a particular instance of the induction hypothesis is strictly dominated by the bounding terms of the equation context under consideration.
Another notable difference is that their approach imposes more bureaucracy, since derivation rules rely on several steps being done at once, by reasoning
modulo
a set of induction hypotheses.
This makes it quite hard to use especially when the relation
\succ
is not fixed beforehand but
constructed on the fly.
Finally, a difference compared to our work is that we do not impose a ground totality requirement, allowing us, for instance, to choose
\succ\ =(\to_{A}\cup\rhd)^{+}
, or to use a construction based on dependency pairs (see
section 6
).
This is particularly important in higher-order rewriting, where very few
ground-total orderings exist.

(Expand)

Many of the existing RI systems in the literature include a deduction rule called
(Expand), used to perform induction proofs.
At a first encounter, the (Expand) rule can be difficult to grasp, since it
combines many conceptual steps simultaneously — something that is also undesirable from the perspective of modularity.
This motivated us to decompose the rule, making the individual proof steps explicit, which in turn naturally led to the introduction of two new deduction
rules: (Induct) and (Case).
In the setting of Bounded RI, (Expand) can be viewed as the consecutive
execution of three deduction rules:

(1)

(Induct).
Given a proof state
\mathcal{P}_{0}=(\mathcal{E}\uplus\{(\varsigma\ ;\ s\approx t\ ;\ \uptau)\ [\psi]\},\mathcal{H})
we apply (Induct) to obtain

\mathcal{P}_{1}=(\mathcal{E}\cup\{(s\ ;\ s\approx t\ ;\ t)\ [\psi]\},\mathcal{H}\cup\{s\approx t\ [\psi]\})
.

However, in most of the literature, the set
\mathcal{H}
contains
oriented

equations, and
\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{H}
is required to be terminating. In our
system we can model this by choosing one side of the equation where we will
apply (Expand), and orienting the equation in that direction.
For example, if we choose the left-hand side
s
, then we require

s\succ t\ [\psi]
.
(In this setting, we let
\succeq
be the reflexive closure of

\succ
and impose
\to_{\mathcal{R}}\,\subseteq\,\succ
.)

(2)

(Case).
In the (Expand) rule, the user provides a position
p
in the chosen side of
the equation (in our example: in
s
), such that the subterm
s|_{p}
at this
position has a form
\mathsf{f}\ s_{1}\cdots s_{n}
with
\mathsf{f}\in\mathcal{D}
,
n\geq\mathit{ar}(\mathsf{f})
, and such that
s_{i}
is a semi-constructor term for all
1\leq i\leq\mathit{ar}(\mathsf{f})
. With these ingredients we define a cover set as follows:

\mathcal{C}_{\texttt{EXP}}=\left\{(\delta,\varphi\delta)\middle|\begin{array}[]{l}\ell\to r\ [\varphi]\in\mathcal{R},\ \delta=\text{mgu}(\mathsf{f}\ s_{1}\cdots s_{k},\ell),\\
\delta(Var(\varphi)\cup Var(\psi))\subseteq\mathcal{V}\textit{al}\cup\mathcal{V}\end{array}\right\}

Due to the assumption of quasi-reductivity, this really is a cover set.
We apply (Case) to
\mathcal{P}_{1}
, using
\mathcal{C}_{\texttt{EXP}}
to obtain
the following proof state

\mathcal{P}_{2}=(\mathcal{E}\cup\{(s\delta\ ;\ s\delta\approx t\delta\ ;\ t\delta)\ [\psi\delta\wedge\varphi]\mid(\delta,\varphi)\in\mathcal{C}_{\texttt{EXP}}\},\mathcal{H}\cup\{s\approx t\ [\psi]\})

(3)

(Simplify) on each of the equations obtained in (2), using the
corresponding rule
\ell\to r\ [\varphi]\in\mathcal{R}
. Due to the choice of
cover set, the conditions of (Simplify) are satisfied.
This yields
\mathcal{P}_{3}=(\mathcal{E}\cup\mathcal{E}^{\to_{\mathcal{R}}}_{\texttt{EXP}},\mathcal{H}\cup\{s\approx t\ [\psi]\})
with
\mathcal{E}^{\to_{\mathcal{R}}}_{\texttt{EXP}}=

\left\{(s\delta\ ;\ s[r\ s_{k+1}\cdots s_{n}]_{p}\delta\approx t\delta\ ;\ t\delta)\ [(\psi\delta)\wedge(\varphi\delta)]\ \middle|\begin{array}[]{l}\ell\to r\ [\varphi]\in\mathcal{R},\\
\delta=\text{mgu}(\mathsf{f}\ s_{1}\cdots s_{k},\ell),\\
\delta(Var(\varphi,\psi)\cup Var(\psi))\subseteq\mathcal{V}\textit{al}\cup\mathcal{V}\end{array}\right\}

Hence, we can still do the traditional (Expand) rule in our work by using
multiple steps, and improve on it by not requiring
s\succ t\ [\psi]
.
Instead, a typically weaker requirement is imposed when we apply the induction
hypothesis
s\approx t\ [\psi]
in a (Hypothesis) or (
\mathcal{H}
-Delete) step.

10.
Conclusion & Future Work

With the introduction of Bounded Rewriting Induction, we revised the existing
RI system for LCSTRSs, making fundamental changes with the aim of reducing termination requirements.
We replaced a well-founded order
\succ
or terminating relation

\to_{\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{H}}
with a
bounding pair

(\succ,\succeq)
allowing
for less strict ordering requirements with
\succeq
.
We replaced equations by
equation contexts
, which contain
bounding terms
, keeping track of induction bounds efficiently.
As a byproduct, we have obtained a more intuitive proof system with a higher degree of modularity,
which makes it easy to add new deduction rules to the system.

Easier induction proofs

In Bounded RI, we deviate from our earlier work by not requiring the induction hypotheses themselves
to be oriented. In particular, the (
\mathcal{H}
-Delete) rule is beneficial, as it often allows us to complete
a proof with minimal termination requirements (e.g., requirements that are immediately satisfied if

\to_{\mathcal{R}}
is included in
\succ
). This allows us to entirely avoid challenges we encountered
in our previous work. We provide two examples.

(1)

The paper
[
17
]
proves
\mathsf{sum2}\ x\approx\mathsf{sum3}\ x
in the LCSTRS

\displaystyle\mathsf{sum2}\ x

\displaystyle\to\mathsf{add}\ x\ (\mathsf{sum2}\ (x-\mathsf{1}))

\displaystyle[x>\mathsf{0}]

\displaystyle\mathsf{sum3}\ x

\displaystyle\to\mathsf{v}\ x\ \mathsf{0}

\displaystyle\mathsf{sum2}\ x

\displaystyle\to\mathsf{return}\ \mathsf{0}

\displaystyle[x\leq\mathsf{0}]

\displaystyle\mathsf{v}\ x\ a

\displaystyle\to\mathsf{v}\ (x-\mathsf{1})\ (a+x)

\displaystyle[x>\mathsf{0}]

\displaystyle\mathsf{add}\ x\ (\mathsf{return}\ y)

\displaystyle\to\mathsf{return}\ (x+y)

\displaystyle\mathsf{v}\ x\ a

\displaystyle\to\mathsf{return}\ a

\displaystyle[x\leq\mathsf{0}]

As explained in
[
17
]
, it is easy to find the lemma equation
\mathsf{add}\ x\ (\mathsf{v}\ y\ z)\approx\mathsf{v}\ y\ a\ [a=x+z]

that is needed for a rewriting induction proof to succeed.
In the RI system of
[
17
]
, however, this is not a suitable induction hypothesis, as
neither the rule
\mathsf{v}\ y\ a\to\mathsf{add}\ x\ (\mathsf{v}\ y\ z)\ [a=x+z]
nor

\mathsf{add}\ x\ (\mathsf{v}\ y\ z)\to\mathsf{v}\ y\ a\ [a=x+z]
is terminating.
Further effort needs to be spent to find invariants
x>0
and
z\geq 0
to obtain a terminating induction rule.
In Bounded RI this is not necessary: we can directly use the equation as induction hypothesis, since (
\mathcal{H}
-Delete) imposes lower requirements.

(2)

The paper
[
18
]
proves

\mathsf{sumfun}\ f\ n\approx\mathsf{fold}\ +\ \mathsf{0}\ (\mathsf{map}\ f\ (\mathsf{init}\ n))[n\geq\mathsf{0}]
in the LCSTRS

\displaystyle\mathsf{fold}\ g\ v\ \mathsf{nil}\to v

\displaystyle\mathsf{map}\ f\ \mathsf{nil}\to\mathsf{nil}

\displaystyle\mathsf{fold}\ g\ v\ (h:t)\to\mathsf{fold}\ g\ (g\ v\ h)\ t

\displaystyle\mathsf{map}\ f\ (h:t)\to(f\ h):\mathsf{map}\ f\ t

\displaystyle\mathsf{init}\ n\to\mathsf{nil}\quad[n<\mathsf{0}]

\displaystyle\mathsf{init}\ n\to n:\mathsf{init}\ (n-\mathsf{1})\quad[n\geq\mathsf{0}]

The proof requires the induction rule

x+(\mathsf{fold}\ +\ y\ l)\to\mathsf{fold}\ +\ z\ l\ [z=x+y]
which is non-standard, as it has a theory symbol
+
as root symbol on the left.
Termination
can
be proven, but requires a very advanced method that is not easy to
use automatically.
In practice, we would like to avoid such induction rules as much as possible.
Again, Bounded RI provides a solution, since the application of (
\mathcal{H}
-Delete) with induction hypotheses
x+(\mathsf{fold}\ +\ y\ l)\approx\mathsf{fold}\ +\ z\ l\ [z=x+y]
does not impose such a requirement.

Ground confluence

Since Bounded RI proves
bounded
ground convertibility, rather than merely ground convertibility as in, e.g.,
[
18
]
, we can use the system to prove ground confluence:
we defined critical pairs for LCSTRSs and showed that a terminating LCSTRS is ground confluent if all its critical peaks are bounded ground convertible.
Finally, as a natural application of this result, we showed that for ground confluent LCSTRSs, Bounded RI can be extended into a system for disproving inductive theorems.

We would like to conclude with some practically motivated directions for future research.

Global rewriting induction

A property not discussed in this work, but a major topic in
[
3
,
18
]
, is
extensibility
.
This means that if an equation is an inductive theorem in
\mathcal{L}
, it remains an inductive theorem in any “reasonable”
extension of
\mathcal{L}
(intuitively, a reasonable extension is one that represents a real-world program, in which
\mathcal{L}
is a separate module).
Such inductive theorems are called
global inductive theorems
.
In terms of functional programming, if two functions are equivalent this should not
change when these functions are used inside a larger program.
Extensibility is a way to express that local reasoning extends globally, at least for such reasonable LCSTRSs.
From a perspective of software verification this is a desirable property: to prove properties about a small part of a larger system, we only need to consider the rules that are directly related.

Global Rewriting Induction

[
18
]
is an extension of RI, designed to prove global inductive theorems.
An interesting question is of course whether we can apply a similar construction to Bounded RI.
The major challenge in this will be to guarantee that a bounding pair
(\succ,\succeq)
on
T(\Sigma,\mathcal{V})
can be extended to a bounding pair
(\succ^{\prime},\succeq^{\prime})
on
T(\Sigma^{\prime},\mathcal{V}^{\prime})
, for any reasonable extension
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}=(\mathcal{R}^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime})
of
\mathcal{L}=(\mathcal{R},\Sigma)
.
In particular, we will have to reconsider every deduction rule in
Figure 1
that has an ordering requirement, including (Hypothesis), (
\mathcal{H}
-Delete), (Generalize) and (Alter).

Ground convertibility modulo axioms

In practice, we encounter inductive theorems which are only provable once we are allowed to utilize our knowledge about the instantiation of certain variables.
For example, an equivalence
\mathsf{P}_{1}\ f\approx\mathsf{P}_{2}\ f
may hold only
under the assumption that
f::\mathsf{int}\to\mathsf{int}\to\mathsf{int}
is instantiated by a
commutative function.
So instead of considering convertibility under
all
ground substitutions, we restrict to those ground substitutions that instantiate
f
as a commutative function.

Designing a corresponding RI system is non-trivial, because this new kind of equivalence is no longer based on the standard notion of inductive theorem, but on a restricted form of ground convertibility.
This likely brings us into a setting similar to term rewriting modulo a set
\mathcal{A}
of axioms.
A natural first step is therefore to examine how the soundness proofs from
section 4
can be adapted to the setting of rewriting modulo
\mathcal{A}
.

Automating RI proofs

Currently, any proof by Bounded RI is (almost) completely human driven.
Ideally, we would like to incorporate some tactics in
Cora
for automatically proving inductive theorems with Bounded RI, building on what has been implemented in
Ctrl

[
13
]
for first-order LCTRSs.
As observed in
section 8
, a major obstacle in this is finding
lemmas automatically.
Here, existing generalization methods can be employed, but it remains a challenge to determine when and how to apply them effectively.
Of course, as program equivalence in general is undecidable, we cannot expect them to be exhaustive.

Unbounded RI

The definition of Bounded RI takes advantages of the restriction that all our
equation contexts are bounded (and remain so because our derivation rules have
the Preserving Bounds property). However, this restriction is not fundamental:
as shown by the proofs in
section 4
, what matters most is that the
right multiset ordering conditions are imposed. To obtain these conditions we
currently use the property that equation contexts are bounded, but we could
alternatively require it directly in the derivation rules. This would for
instance replace the (Hypothesis) rule by

(\mathcal{E}\uplus\{(\varsigma\ ;\ C[\ell\delta]\simeq t\ ;\ \uptau)\ [\psi]\},\mathcal{H})

\begin{aligned} &\ell\simeq r\ [\varphi]\in\mathcal{H}\text{ and }\psi\models^{\delta}\varphi\text{ and}\\
&\{\!\{\varsigma,\uptau\}\!\}\succ_{mul}\{\!\{\ell\delta,r\delta\}\!\}\ [\psi]\end{aligned}

(\mathcal{E}\cup\{(\varsigma\ ;\ C[r\delta]\approx t\ ;\ \uptau)\ [\psi]\},\mathcal{H})

And (Induct) would become:

(\mathcal{E}\uplus\{(\varsigma\ ;\ s\approx t\ ;\ \uptau)\ [\psi]\},\mathcal{H})

\begin{aligned} &\{\!\{\varsigma,\uptau\}\!\}\succeq_{mul}\{\!\{s,t\}\!\}\ [\psi]\end{aligned}

(\mathcal{E}\cup\{(s\ ;\ s\approx t\ ;\ t)\ [\psi]\},\mathcal{H}\cup\{s\approx t\ [\psi]\})

On the upside, we could then weaken the restrictions for (Alter)/(Generalize) (since it is no longer necessary to preserve bounds). We have checked all the
proofs for this altered system, and it is sufficient to derive ground
convertibility, though not
bounded
ground convertiblity.

The advantage of such a change is that we can further weaken the ordering
requirements: instead of requiring that
\to_{\mathcal{R}}
is included in
\succeq

it would suffice if
s\succeq s\downarrow_{\mathcal{R}}
for all ground terms

s
.
However, a downside is that it is not obviously usable for proving ground
confluence.

The most important complication is that six different combinations of
\succ

and
\succeq
are admissible when realizing a multiset ordering. This is not a
problem if the bounding pair
(\succ,\succeq)
is fixed in advance, but

is
challenging if the search for a bounding pair is done afterwards.
Naïvely exploring the entire search space – i.e. considering all options at
every deduction step that imposes requirements on the ordering – would lead to
exponential growth in the length of the deduction sequence.
From this perspective, Bounded RI can be seen as a heuristic that provides
guidance on which ordering requirements to impose.
In future work, it would be interesting to further explore Unbounded RI and
determine if there are strategies to take advantage of the greater generality,
and still find a bounding pair easily.
