Explainable machine learning predicts overall survival in female bladder cancer patients after radical cystectomy
Authors: Ming Yan Zhong, Xin Chang Zou, Pei Huang Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and purposeDespite the higher incidence of bladder cancer in males, females face a disproportionately worse prognosis with more advanced disease. Accordingly, accurate survival prediction is crucial. This study sought to develop multiple machine learning models for predicting postoperative overall survival (OS) in female bladder cancer patients who have undergone radical cystectomy (RC).Patients and methodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on female patients who underwent RC with postoperative pathological confirmation of bladder cancer in the SEER database from 2004 to 2022. These patients were randomly divided into a training set and an internal validation set at a 7:3 ratio. Additionally, 67 female bladder cancer patients from the Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University were included as an external validation set. LASSO-Cox regression and Cox regression were used to identify independent prognostic factors for bladder cancer. Based on these factors, prediction models were constructed using five machine learning algorithms: Support Vector Machine (SVM), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Gradient Boosting Decision Tree (GBDT), Random Forest (RF), and XGBoost. The predictive performance of the models was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), accuracy, precision, F1 score, concordance index (C-index), calibration curves, and decision curve analysis. The SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) method was employed to interpret the most impactful features of the best-performing model.ResultsThe study included a total of 4,603 patients. Lasso-Cox regression screening and Cox regression analysis revealed that T stage, N stage, age, tumor size, marital status, chemotherapy, and the number of examined lymph nodes (ELN) during surgery were significantly associated with the OS of female bladder cancer patients. Compared with other models, GBDT demonstrated superior discriminative ability in predicting 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year survival rates (1-year AUC(95%CI) = 0.771(0.742–0.801), 3-year AUC(95%CI) = 0.757(0.730–0.783), 5-year AUC(95%CI) = 0.745(0.721–0.770)), along with higher prediction accuracy, precision, C-index and F1 score. The calibration curve and decision curve analysis (DCA) confirmed the excellent predictive accuracy and clinical benefits of the GBDT model. These results were also validated in the external validation cohort. T stage, N stage, and chemotherapy were the most significant features of the optimal model, and SHAP analysis identified their important contributions within the model.ConclusionWe have developed interpretable machine learning models to predict the OS of female bladder cancer patients following radical surgery. This model is intended to assist in clinical prognosis evaluation and provide a reference for individualized treatment decision-making.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-04275-4.

Introduction

Bladder cancer ranks as the tenth most common malignant tumor globally, with both incidence and mortality rates remaining high, and the annual number of new cases exceeding 600,000 [1–3]. There is a significant gender disparity in bladder cancer incidence, with the rate markedly higher in males than in females. However, female patients are often diagnosed at a later stage and with a higher grade, resulting in poorer prognosis compared to males [4, 5]. Radical cystectomy is currently the standard treatment for muscle-invasive or high-risk non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer. Despite continuous advances in surgical techniques, considerable heterogeneity persists in postoperative survival outcomes among patients [6, 7]. Therefore, there is a pressing clinical need to develop more precise postoperative OS prediction tools for this specific female population to guide individualized postoperative management and adjuvant treatment decisions.

In recent years, machine learning has developed rapidly within artificial intelligence and has been widely applied across multiple healthcare domains, from medical imaging analysis to clinical prognosis prediction, often outperforming traditional statistical methods [8, 9]. In urological oncology, machine learning has successfully aided in the diagnosis and grading of bladder cancer, as well as the prediction of survival outcomes in advanced bladder cancer [10–12], laying a solid foundation for its application in bladder cancer [13]. However, research focusing specifically on predicting and modeling the long-term survival rates of female bladder cancer patients after radical surgery remains limited, with substantial potential for exploration and clinical impact.

This study systematically compares five machine learning algorithms, including SVM, KNN, GBDT, RF, and XGBoost, to achieve accurate, personalized prediction of OS in female bladder cancer patients following radical surgery.

Patients and methods

Patients

Figure 1 illustrates the workflow of our research design and analysis. The data analyzed in this study were derived from all female bladder cancer patients in the SEER database between 2004 and 2022, which encompasses multiple medical centers across the United States. The inclusion criterion is: all female bladder cancer patients. The exclusion criteria are as follows: (1) did not undergo radical cystectomy with pelvic lymph node dissection (n = 70696); (2) M1 or Mx staging (n = 380); (3) unclear clinicopathological records, including unknown T staging, N staging, and grading (n = 696); (4) unknown regional lymph node examination (n = 4); (5) unknown tumor size (n = 1260); (6) unknown marital status and race (n = 131); (7) histology unknown (n = 10). Ultimately, eligible female patients with bladder cancer (n = 4536) were included in this study, and all patients were randomly divided into a training set (n = 3175) and an internal test set (n = 1361) using the R language random seed method. OS was defined as the time from the date of radical cystectomy to the date of death from any cause. Patients still alive at the last follow-up were censored on that date. Sixty-seven female patients with bladder cancer who underwent radical cystectomy and pelvic lymph node dissection at the second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University from March 2005 to May 2020 were collected. The inclusion and exclusion criteria employed were entirely consistent with those of the SEER database. This study has been reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University, and informed consent was waived.

Selection of clinical and pathological characteristics

The clinical and pathological characteristics of the training set were initially screened using LASSO-Cox regression to identify features significantly associated with postoperative OS. This was achieved by introducing a penalty parameter (λ) to shrink the coefficients of the features toward zero, thereby enabling automatic feature selection. Subsequently, univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed to determine the independent factors influencing postoperative OS.

Development of machine learning models

As a binary classification model, the fundamental model of SVM is defined as a linear classifier that maximizes the margin in the feature space. By maximizing the margin, it can be transformed into the solution of a convex quadratic programming problem [14]. KNN is an instance-based learning algorithm that assigns the category of a sample by computing its distances to all samples in the training set and then voting among the K nearest neighbors [15]. GBDT is an ensemble learning method that constructs multiple decision trees in sequence, with each tree learning the residuals of the previous one, culminating in a strong classifier through a weighted sum [16]. RF is an ensemble method based on Bagging that enhances the model’s generalization ability and reduces the risk of overfitting by generating multiple decision trees and aggregating their votes [17]. XGBoost is an optimized gradient boosting framework that further improves performance and training efficiency by incorporating regularization terms, second-order derivative information, and parallel computing atop GBDT [18]. We developed the aforementioned machine learning models on the training set, evaluated their performance on the internal validation set, and conducted further validation on the external test set.

Model evaluation and validation

The predictive performance of the five models was evaluated and compared using accuracy, precision, F1 score, C-index, confusion matrix, and the AUC. Calibration curves and DCA were further employed to assess model calibration, stability, and potential clinical utility. Differences in AUC values between models were statistically compared using DeLong’s test. To enhance the interpretability of the optimal model, SHAP analysis was performed. The SHAP values were computed using a kernel-based approach on a randomly sampled subset (80%) of the test set, with 10,00 iterations to ensure robust estimation of feature contributions.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed and processed using SPSS 27.0, R software (version 4.3.1, http://www.r-project.org/), or Python software (https://www.python.org/downloads/release/python-3.8.0/). All categorical variables were expressed as numbers (%). LASSO-Cox regression was used to screen for significant predictors. The glmnet package in R was employed for LASSO-Cox regression, while the construction and evaluation of machine learning models were performed using Python. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed p-value < 0.05.

Results

Clinical and pathological characteristics of patients

The patients’ ages predominantly ranged from 60 to 80 years (65.78%), and 47.29% were married, with an overwhelmingly White racial composition (84.02%). Regarding tumor pathology, 92.06% had high-grade tumors and 86.99% had urothelial carcinoma. In clinical staging, 39.22% were at T3 and 73.92% were N0. The proportions receiving radiotherapy and chemotherapy were 3.84% and 45.55%, respectively, and 56.97% had tumors larger than 4 cm. The training and test sets remained well balanced across all baseline characteristics (all P > 0.05), supporting the reliability of subsequent model comparisons (Table 1). Detailed information on the external validation cohort is provided in the supplementary materials.

Model development and validation

The key features identified above were incorporated into the construction of five machine learning models: SVM, KNN, GBDT, RF, and XGBoost. To ensure model stability, ten-fold cross-validation was employed in the training set for iterative testing and tuning, with the aim of determining the optimal hyperparameters and generating the best-performing models. In the validation set, GBDT demonstrated superior discriminative ability for predicting 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year survival rates (1-year AUC (95%CI) = 0.771(0.742–0.801), C-index = 0.703, 3-year AUC (95%CI) = 0.757(0.730–0.783), C-index = 0.703, 5-year AUC (95%CI) = 0.745(0.721–0.770), C-index = 0.700), significantly outperforming SVM (1-year AUC (95%CI) = 0.683(0.645–0.718), C-index = 0.692, 3-year AUC (95%CI) = 0.742(0.718–0.767), C-index = 0.692, 5-year AUC (95%CI) = 0.743(0.716–0.768), C-index = 0.695), KNN (1-year AUC (95%CI) = 0.683(0.649–0.720), C-index = 0.512, 3-year AUC (95%CI) = 0.675(0.646–0.702), C-index = 0.550, 5-year AUC (95%CI) = 0.662(0.634–0.689), C-index = 0.546 ), RF (1-year AUC (95%CI) = 0.721(0.685–0.754), C-index = 0.658, 3-year AUC (95%CI) = 0.718(0.691–0.746), C-index = 0.669, 5-year AUC (95%CI) = 0.703(0.677–0.731), C-index = 0.667), and XGBoost (1-year AUC (95%CI) = 0.735(0.700-0.766), C-index = 0.672, 3-year AUC (95%CI) = 0.732(0.705–0.759), C-index = 0.680, 5-year AUC (95%CI) = 0.720(0.694–0.747), C-index = 0.678) (Fig. 3). Additionally, GBDT showed favorable performance in prediction accuracy, precision, and F1 score, though it did not surpass all other models across every metric (Table 3). The calibration curve and DCA indicated that the GBDT model possesses good predictive accuracy and clinical benefits (Fig. 4). The confusion matrix revealed the model’s excellent predictive specificity and sensitivity, as detailed in the supplementary materials. DeLong’s test shows that GBDT has significantly better predictive performance than the other models (p < 0.05), although the difference between GBDT and SVM in the fifth-year model is present but not statistically significant. To better determine the importance and impact of each independent risk factor, we performed SHAP analysis on the optimal GBDT model (Fig. 5). The novelty of our work lies not in discovering new prognostic factors, but in developing a high-performance, validated, and interpretable machine learning model that integrates established factors to provide individualized risk prediction. The results demonstrated that T stage, N stage, and chemotherapy were the most significant features, with SHAP analysis confirming their substantial contributions and elucidating the prediction pathways.

To further validate the generalization ability of the GBDT model, we collected 67 female bladder cancer patients from the Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University as an external validation cohort (Supplementary Material), which demonstrated that our GBDT model maintains robust performance in an external independent dataset (1-year AUC(95%CI) = 0.839(0.732–0.926), 3-year AUC(95%CI) = 0.801(0.689–0.904), 5-year AUC(95%CI) = 0.747(0.621–0.853)), significantly outperforming the other models (Fig. 3). The C-indices for predicting 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall survival were as follows: GBDT (0.724, 0.750, 0.716), SVM (0.718, 0.743, 0.716), KNN (0.496, 0.592, 0.550), RF (0.673, 0.712, 0.679), and XGBoost (0.692, 0.718, 0.695). Among all models, the GBDT model achieved the highest C-index across all time points, demonstrating its superior predictive performance. In the external validation cohort, the GBDT model also exhibited favorable prediction accuracy, precision, and F1 scores. DeLong’s test indicated that the GBDT model differed from the other models, with a statistically significant difference when compared to the KNN model, while no significant differences were observed with the remaining models, suggesting the need for further validation in the future. The calibration curve and DCA illustrated good predictive accuracy and clinical benefits (Fig. 4), and the confusion matrix confirmed the model’s excellent specificity and sensitivity, as shown in the supplementary materials.

Discussion

The global incidence of bladder cancer in women is gradually increasing [19]. The annual incidence in women is approximately 5.4 cases per 100,000 individuals, which is lower than the ~ 16.5 cases per 100,000 in men, yet women are often diagnosed at a later stage and with more aggressive disease, resulting in a relatively poorer prognosis [20, 21]. Radical cystectomy (RC), as the primary treatment for muscle-invasive bladder cancer, can significantly improve patient survival, but there is substantial individual variation in postoperative outcomes, with the overall five-year survival rate remaining around 50–60% [22–24]. Accurately predicting postoperative survival is crucial for formulating individualized adjuvant treatment plans, optimizing follow-up strategies, and improving prognosis. However, due to the complexity of tumor biology and the diversity of clinicopathological factors, precise prediction of survival outcomes remains a significant challenge in clinical practice [25, 26].

Previous studies on bladder cancer prognosis have predominantly relied on traditional statistical methods, particularly the Cox proportional hazards model [27, 28]. Nomograms based on these models were the mainstream predictive tools in the past. Sun et al. developed and validated a nomogram for predicting recurrence and progression of non–muscle-invasive bladder cancer [29], while Obayashi et al. effectively predicted prognosis for bladder cancer patients undergoing radical cystectomy using a combination of the lung immune prognostic index and lymph node metastasis [30]. These studies successfully identified key prognostic factors such as TNM stage, age, and lymph node metastasis, providing utility for risk assessment in clinical practice. Nonetheless, traditional models have notable limitations: they typically assume linear relationships between variables and risks, making it difficult to capture complex nonlinear interactions; they have limited capacity to handle large numbers of variables and high-dimensional data, often leading to overfitting or underutilization of information; and their predictive accuracy and generalizability often reach a bottleneck, constraining personalized predictions in the era of precision medicine [31, 32].

With the advancement of artificial intelligence, machine learning has shown substantial potential in medical applications, particularly in oncology prognosis prediction [33]. In bladder cancer research, Dai et al. achieved high-precision prediction of cancer-specific mortality in patients undergoing radical cystectomy by integrating multiple machine learning algorithms, with performance substantially surpassing traditional models [34]. A multicenter study by Fan et al. confirmed that prediction models based on machine learning can effectively predict urethral recurrence after radical cystectomy [35]. These successes demonstrate that machine learning can efficiently process high-dimensional, nonlinear data, automatically extract deep feature patterns, and play a crucial role in the diagnosis, staging, recurrence, and survival prediction of bladder cancer, offering a new paradigm for improving patient management [36].

This study focuses on the female bladder cancer population, who typically have poorer prognosis, and systematically compares, for the first time, the performance of five machine learning algorithms—SVM, KNN, GBDT, RF, and XGBoost—in predicting short-term and long-term survival after radical surgery. Based on the large-sample SEER database, we identified seven key prognostic factors—T stage, N stage, age, tumor size, marital status, chemotherapy, and ELN—through LASSO-Cox regression and Cox risk regression analysis, and constructed predictive models accordingly. The study found that among all models, the GBDT model performed the best, with internal validation AUCs for predicting 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year survival rates consistently above 0.74, demonstrating excellent and stable discriminative ability. Its calibration curve and DCA further confirmed that the model not only has high predictive accuracy but also yields significant net benefits for clinical decision-making. This advantage was reaffirmed in an external independent validation cohort from the Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University, highlighting the model’s excellent generalization capability. Through SHAP analysis, we clearly interpreted the decision-making basis of the GBDT model, confirming that T stage, N stage, and chemotherapy are the most important features influencing the predictions, greatly enhancing the model’s interpretability and clinical credibility. This study successfully demonstrates that a machine learning–based predictive model can efficiently and accurately assess the postoperative survival risk of female bladder cancer patients, providing a robust data-support tool for achieving individualized prognosis management. To facilitate clinical interpretation, our SHAP summary plot offers clinicians an intuitive visualization: features are vertically ranked by importance, with the most influential predictors at the top. Each point represents a patient’s feature value, colored from blue (low) to red (high). The horizontal position shows the feature’s impact on predicted risk, where points farther to the right increase risk prediction while those to the left decrease it. For example, red points (high values) for T stage predominantly appearing on the right visually confirm that advanced T stage correlates with elevated mortality risk. This visualization helps clinicians quickly identify key prognostic factors and understand individual risk predictions.

This study has several limitations inherent to its retrospective design based on the SEER database. Our analysis lacks several critical prognostic variables, such as surgical margin status and comorbidity scores, which may limit the model’s comprehensiveness. Furthermore, the exclusion of cases with missing data could lead to selection bias. Furthermore, the limited documentation of therapeutic specifics, including exact regimens, drug types, cycle numbers, and dosages for chemotherapy and radiotherapy, represents a significant source of potential confounding. The model’s generalizability may be limited by the small sample size of the external validation cohort. Moreover, it did not account for geographical variations in treatment protocols and healthcare systems. We explicitly acknowledge that these constraints could influence model performance and emphasize that future multicenter, international prospective studies are essential to validate and refine our model across diverse populations.

The novelty of our work lies in developing and rigorously validating an interpretable machine learning model, rather than in discovering new prognostic biomarkers. This model integrates established factors to provide individualized risk prediction. The model demonstrates clear potential for clinical application in guiding adjuvant therapy decisions, stratifying follow-up intensity, and selecting candidates for clinical trials. However, the use of variables such as marital status requires careful ethical consideration, as while they may serve as proxies for unmeasured socioeconomic factors, their inclusion introduces potential bias. Further validation is necessary to assess the broader applicability of the GBDT framework, including evaluations in male-only and mixed-sex cohorts, as well as across more diverse racial and ethnic populations. Methodologically, this study builds on recent advances in deep learning-based survival analysis for bladder and other cancers, offering a framework that balances methodological rigor with translational relevance. This work successfully integrates established prognostic factors into an effective risk assessment framework. By doing so, it paves the way for future integration of multi-omics data and advanced algorithms, ultimately advancing personalized diagnosis and treatment in bladder cancer.

Conclusion

We have developed several machine learning models to predict the survival rate of female bladder cancer patients after radical surgery. Among them, the GBDT model demonstrated the best predictive performance, with its discriminative ability, calibration, and clinical applicability all validated. This model provides an effective tool for individualized prognostic assessment and holds promising potential for clinical translation.
