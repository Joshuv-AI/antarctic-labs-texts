Computational design, synthesis and biological evaluation of novel pyridinone derivatives as NNRTIs
Authors: Niloofar Movahednia, Ali Ramazani, Afshin fassihi, Pouria Shirvani, Dominique Schols
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe emergence of resistance against first-generation non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NNRTIs) necessitates the development of novel scaffolds with improved antiviral efficacy. In this study, a series of pyridinone derivatives were designed and synthesized as potential NNRTIs. The antiviral evaluation against wild-type HIV-1 in MT-4 cells revealed that most of the compounds displayed limited or no activity, often accompanied by cytotoxic effects. However, two derivatives, 4e and 4i, demonstrated remarkable inhibition of HIV-1 replication, with 4i exhibiting low-nanomolar potency against HIV-1 (EC50 = 5.3 nM), having almost one half of the Rilpivirine activity. Molecular docking confirmed critical hydrogen bonding interactions with Lys103 and π–π stacking with Trp229 and Tyr181, while 300 ns molecular dynamics simulations verified the stability of the compound-enzyme complexes. Introduction

Human immunodeficiency virus type 1 (HIV-1) continues to represent a major global health concern, with nearly 39 million people currently infected worldwide and about 1.3 million new cases reported each year as of 20241. Since the introduction of combination antiretroviral therapy (cART) in the mid-1990s, which incorporates multiple agents targeting distinct steps of the viral replication cycle-including reverse transcriptase (RT) inhibitors-the rate of AIDS-related deaths has been significantly reduced. As a result, the life expectancy of HIV-positive individuals now approaches that of the general population2,3.

Non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors constitute an essential component of cART. These compounds interact with the non-nucleoside inhibitor binding pocket (NNIBP) of RT at an allosteric site, distinct from the catalytic active site, thereby disrupting reverse transcription without the need for metabolic activation4,5.

To date, six NNRTIs have been approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for clinical use: Nevirapine (NVP), Delavirdine (DLV), Efavirenz (EFV), Etravirine (TMC-125), Rilpivirine (TMC-278), and Doravirine (MK-1439)6,7. The chemical structures of these agents are presented in Fig. 1. Unfortunately, the effectiveness of the first generation of NNRTIs (NVP, DLV and EFV) has been seriously decreased by the rapid emergence of mutant HIV-1 strains8,9.

Structures of NNRTIs drugs approved by the US FDA.

The high mutation rate of HIV-1 facilitates the emergence of resistant variants. These variants often arise through single amino acid substitutions in the RT gene, such as K103N and Y181C, which markedly diminish NNRTI susceptibility and complicate therapeutic regimens. Current data suggest that 2–7% of newly diagnosed cases already harbor NNRTI-resistant strains10–15.To combat resistance, novel NNRTI scaffolds with better properties and enhanced antiviral activity are designed to explore diverse chemical cores and specific frameworks16,17. Innovative approaches like combinatorial chemistry and structure-based optimization are rapidly identifying potent derivatives, including indolylarylsulfones and pyrimidines active against resistant strains18,19.

Hydroxypyridinones (HPOs) comprise a versatile class of bidentate chelating molecules that display a broad spectrum of pharmacological activities, including antibacterial, antiviral, antitumor, and metal-chelating properties20. Their ability to coordinate biologically important metal ions, combined with their favorable physicochemical features and structural flexibility, has generated considerable interest in medicinal chemistry as potential scaffolds for targeting metalloproteins and pathogenic enzymes20. Recent progress in synthetic methodologies has facilitated the development of HPO-based NNRTIs with enhanced inhibitory potency and improved pharmacokinetic profiles21. An example of a hydroxypyridinone-containing NNRTI with notable activity is illustrated in Fig. 222.

Chemical structure of an NNRTI possessing hydroxypyridinone scaffold in the structure.

Previously we have reported 3-hydroxy-4-pyridinone derivatives as anti-HIV-1 agents exerting their action by inhibiting HIV-1 integrase through coordination of the metal ions located in the enzyme’s active site23,24. In light of our continued research on the anti-HIV agents, now we report the design, synthesis and anti-HIV evaluation of some novel hydroxypyridinones. As shown in Fig. 3, the parent reference compound was employed as the starting scaffold25. This scaffold has exhibited notable inhibitory activity against HIV-1 reverse transcriptase, with IC₅₀ and EC₅₀ values in low micromolar range, reflecting its strong binding affinity and effective antiviral potential. Molecular modeling studies have shown the uracil core involved in a crucial hydrogen bonding with Lys103, along with π–π stacking interactions with Tyr188 and Trp229 within the binding pocket. These interactions contribute to stabilizing the inhibitor within its binding site. In the designed compounds the pyrimidinedione dione ring is replaced by a pyridinone moiety. The aminoacetamide moiety substituted with a terminal aromatic ring was retained as a critical pharmacophore to preserve essential hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions with the key residues in the RT binding site to modulate overall binding affinity.

Experimental

Materials and methods

All chemicals, solvents, and reagents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich or Merck and used as received unless otherwise noted. Analytical-grade solvents (DMF, ethanol, and methanol) were distilled prior to use. Reactions were monitored by thin-layer chromatography (TLC) on silica gel 60 F254 plates (Merck) and visualized under UV light at 254 nm. FTIR spectra were recorded on a Shimadzu FTIR-8300 spectrophotometer using potassium bromide (KBr) as the diluent. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra were obtained on a Bruker Avance 400 MHz spectrometer using DMSO-d6 or CDCl3 as solvents. Chemical shifts were reported in ppm, and coupling constants (J) were given in hertz (Hz). High-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) was performed on an Agilent G7081B system.

3-(Benzyloxy)−2-methyl-4H-pyran-4-one (2)

Maltol 1 (25.22 g, 0.2 mol) was dissolved in methanol (250 mL) and sodium hydroxide (8.8 g, 0.22 mol) dissolved in water (50 mL) was added to it. This solution was heated to reflux and benzyl chloride (25.3 g, 0.22 mol) was then added dropwise over 15 min. The reaction mixture was refluxed overnight. The orange oil obtained after the evaporation of the solvent was mixed with 100 mL CH2Cl2 and washed with 5% aqueous sodium hydroxide (3 × 70 mL) followed by water (3 × 70 mL). The organic fraction was then dried over magnesium sulfate, filtered and concentrated. The residue was solidified by crushing with a glass rod in diethyl ether. The obtained precipitate was washed with diethyl ether to give 2 as a pure white solid. Yield: 78%; mp: 51.0–52.5 °C (lit. mp: 51.0–52.0 °C)26.

2-(3-Benzyloxy-2-methyl-4-oxopyridin-1(4H)-yl) acetic acid (3)

Glycine (15.7 g, 0.21 mol) was added to a stirred solution of benzylmaltol 2 (34.6 g, 0.16 mol) in ethanol (200 mL). Then, 10 M sodium hydroxide was added to the reaction mixture until the pH = 13 was obtained. After reflux for 18 h, the solvent was partially removed and the pH was adjusted to 4 by HCl 37%. After cooling in ice bath, the yellow precipitate was filtered, washed with ethanol and recrystallized in ethanol/water to provide 3 as a pale yellow solid. Yield: 83%; mp: 186.0–188.5 °C27.

General procedure for the synthesis of pyridinone derivatives 4a-k

Compound 3 (0.64 g, 1.34 mmol) was dissolved in DMF (25 ml). After complete dissolution, N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) (0.308 g, 2.68 mmol) and N, N’-dicyclohexylcarbodiimide (DCC) (0.552 g, 2.68 mmol) were added to the solution. The reaction mixture was stirred at ambient temperature for 16 h. The precipitated urea was separated to give compound 4 in solution28. Triathylamine (0.376 mL, 2.68 mmol, 2 equivalents) and aniline derivatives (2.68 mmol, 2 equivalents) dissolved in DMF (5 mL) were added to the solution of compound 4. The reaction mixture was stirred overnight at room temperature. By adding cold water, a precipitate was formed, and the precipitation process was complete by cooling the mixture for 10 min. The resulting precipitate was filtered, collected and dried in the vacuum oven to give products 4a-k28.

In vitro anti-HIV-1 assay

The inhibitory effects of antiviral drugs on HIV-induced cytopathic effects (CPE) in MT-4 cell cultures were evaluated using the MTT assay29. This assay is based on the reduction of the yellow-colored 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)−2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) by the mitochondrial dehydrogenases of metabolically active cells into a blue formazan product, which was measured spectrophotometrically. The 50% cell culture infective dose (CCID50) of the HIV strains was determined by titrating the virus stock on MT-4 cells. For drug susceptibility assays, MT-4 cells were infected with 100–300 CCID50 of the HIV strains in the presence of fivefold serial dilutions of the antiviral compounds. The concentration of each compound that conferred 50% protection against HIV-induced CPE, defined as the 50% effective concentration (EC50), was determined. Similarly, the concentration causing 50% cytotoxicity in MT-4 cells, defined as the 50% cytotoxic concentration (CC50), was also measured.

Computing system setup

The in-silico experiments and analyses were conducted using a dual-system setup with Linux Ubuntu 24.04.1 LTS and Windows 10. Molecular dynamics simulations were performed on the Linux platform using Desmond (Schrödinger, version 2021-2)30,31, while Maestro (Schrödinger, version 2024-2) was employed on Windows31. Both systems ran on an Asus K556U laptop equipped with an NVIDIA Tesla V100S GPU for dynamic simulations.

Molecular docking

The crystal structure of HIV-1 reverse transcriptase (RT) (PDB ID: 2YNG, 2.12 Å) was obtained from the Protein Data Bank and refined by correcting bond orders, formal charges, missing hydrogen atoms, and terminal groups. Water molecules beyond 5 Å from heteroatoms were removed, hydrogen bonds were assigned, and a restrained energy minimization was performed with the OPLS2005 force field (RMSD tolerance of 0.3 Å).

Ligand structures were created in ChemDraw Ultra 8.0, converted to mol2 format, and processed with the Schrödinger LigPrep module30. During preparation, stereochemistry, ionization, tautomers, and geometry were optimized, salts were removed, and chiral centers corrected. Final minimization was performed with the OPLS4 force field, and one low-energy conformation was generated for each ligand.

The grid boxes for all the docking experiments were generated with the Glide module with 40 × 40 × 40 Å dimensions. The grid boxes were centered at −8.0 ± 0.1, 66.3 ± 0.1, 16.9 ± 0.1 Å for x, y and z coordinates, respectively. Docking precision was validated by re-docking the co-crystallized ligand and the RMSD between the predicted and experimental conformations was determined 1.32 Å.

Glide docking of the designed molecules was then performed using the prepared receptor grid in Extra Precision (XP) mode with the OPLS4 force field31. Ligand poses were generated in flexible mode, filtered for spatial compatibility, and scored for hydrophobic, hydrogen-bonding, and metal-ligation interactions. Steric clashes were penalized, and minimized poses were re-scored with the GlideScore function.

Molecular dynamics simulation

To assess the stability and dynamic behavior of the top hybrid ligand-protein complexes, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were conducted as the final step32. The system was prepared using Schrödinger’s automated system (DESMOND) preparation tool, with solvation performed in an orthorhombic box containing a 10 Å buffer and 90° angles in all dimensions. The protein–ligand complex was solvated in a TIP3P water box, and eight chloride ions (Cl−) were added to neutralize the system. Additionally, a 0.15 mM NaCl solution was introduced to mimic physiological conditions. All force-field calculations were carried out using the OPLS4 force field.

Initial atomic velocities were randomly assigned at a low temperature, and Newton’s equations of motion were applied to simulate the system. The temperature was gradually raised to 300 K. Simulations were performed under NPT conditions (constant pressure and temperature), with the pressure maintained at 1.01325 bar using the Martyna–Tobias–Klein barostat and the temperature held at 300 K using the Nose–Hoover thermostat. These settings ensured system stability throughout the simulation. The MD simulations were run for 300 ns, with snapshots recorded every 10 ps to enable detailed monitoring of structural changes. Prior to the production run, the system underwent a relaxation phase to establish a stable initial conformation.

Ligand–receptor interactions were monitored throughout the simulations to confirm that the ligands remained stably bound within the active site. The results were evaluated by calculating the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD), root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF), and Ligand-Protein Contacts, providing valuable insights into the dynamic stability of the binding interactions.

Results and discussion

Chemistry

To synthesize pyridinones 4a–k (Scheme 1), maltol 1 was benzylated following a previously reported convenient procedure and then reacted with glycine under basic conditions to afford acid 333. A comparison of the FTIR spectra of compounds 2 and 3 revealed that compound 3 displayed a strong and broad band corresponding to the hydroxyl group of the carboxylic acid moiety, which was absent in the IR spectrum of compound 2. Subsequently, compound 3 was condensed with a series of substituted anilines in DMF in the presence of NHS and DCC, affording the corresponding products 4a–k.

The structures of the final products (4a–k) were confirmed by FTIR, ¹H-NMR, ¹³C-NMR, and mass spectrometry analyses. In the FTIR spectra, the carbonyl group of the heterocyclic ring displayed absorption bands in the range of 1624–1629 cm−¹, whereas the amide carbonyl group exhibited characteristic peaks in the 1685–1718 cm−¹ region. Furthermore, the NH stretching vibration appeared at 3264–3401 cm−¹. In the ¹H-NMR spectra, a singlet in the δ 4.77–5.10 ppm region was assigned to the N-CH₂ protons, while another singlet between δ 10.00–11.20 ppm was attributed to the NH protons. In the ¹³C-NMR spectra, downfield resonances in the δ 56.2–58.5 ppm range were assigned to the N-CH₂ carbon, and the carbonyl carbons were observed between δ 162.6–170.6 ppm.

In vitro anti-HIV-1 activity

The prepared compounds (4a-k) were evaluated for their antiviral activity against WT HIV-1 strain IIIB in human T-lymphocyte (MT-4) cells based on the MTT assay. The cytotoxicity of these compounds against T-lymphocyte (MT-4) cells was determined in parallel. The biological results, presented as CC50 (cytotoxicity) and EC50 (anti-HIV activity), are summarized in Table 1. The FDA approved drug, rilpivirine (RPV) was included for comparison purposes. As shown in Table 1, most of the studied compounds showed no activity against HIV-1 replication in cell culture. In fact, the majority of them were toxic to the host cell, thus their anti-HIV-1 activity could not be reliably determined. Among compounds, two derivatives strongly inhibited HIV-1 RT with EC50 values of 0.57 µM for 4e and 0.0053 µM (5.3 nM) for 4i. In particular, the 4i derivative demonstrated exceptional antiviral potency having almost one half of the Rilpivirine activity. At the anti-HIV-1 concentration, it was almost non-toxic to the host cell. These findings highlight the significant therapeutic potential of 4i and suggest that it deserves further investigation in future studies.

Since CC50 (host toxicity) values were almost all above 100 µM, thus, calculation of selectivity indices for the compounds was not possible. Even without SI values a clear evaluation of therapeutic potential is possible. In case of the two most potent compounds, 4e and 4i, the EC50 values (0.57 ± 0.019 and 0.0053 ± 0,0025 µM, respectively) imply that both compounds are quite potent anti-HIV-1 agents. Meanwhile CC50 values (both > 100 ± 0 µM) show that more than 5000 and 40,000 times of 4e and 4i anti-HIV-1 concentrations, respectively, are needed to kill the host cells.

As the main point in structure-activity relationships(SAR) analysis it can be seen that the structural differences between 4a and 4j were all in the type and position of the substitution located on the benzamide moiety. 4a with 4-OH substituent was toxic for the host cell, but not for the HIV-1 and HIV-2 viruses. The same was true for 4d which had a 3-Cl group in this position. 4-CH3 (4b), 3-OH (4f), 4-F (4 g), 3-CH3 (4 h), and 4-OCH3 (4k) substituents exerted no toxic effect on both the host and virus cells. Even 4c with no substituent on the benzamide moiety was non-toxic against all cells. 4e having 3,5-dichloro and 4i with 4-Cl both had low toxicity for the host cell, but were toxic in nano molar concentrations against HIV-1 and HIV-2 viruses. Considering the low potency of the 4d which had a 3-Cl group in the structure against the viruses it may be concluded that the introduction of the second chlorine in the structure (4e) increases the potency, maybe by increasing the compound lipophilicity which increases the target accessibility by facilitating the compound diffusion into the host then the viral cell. However, 4i which only possesses one chlorine substitution is almost one hundred times more potent than 4e with two chlorines. Thus, lipophilicity seems not to be a parameter determining the compound’s potency. Comparison among 4a, 4i and 4 g which all have an electronegative substitution on the benzamide reveals that this physicochemical parameter has no effect on the potency. 4a and 4 g had no anti-HIV activity while 4i was a really potent compound. The substituent position might be a determining parameter for the compound’s potency. 4a with a 4-OH substituent was more potent than 4f with –OH in the 3-position. In the same way, 4d (3-OH) is less potent than 4i (4-OH). However, 4b (4-CH3) and 4 h (3-CH3) were both evaluated as weak compounds. Methylation of the 4a hydroxyl group decreased the anti-HIV potency. One may assume that the hydrogen bond formation potential of the hydroxyl group would decrease upon this methylation. But, considering the molecular interactions of the two compounds as provided in Fig. 4, it is realized that no hydrogen bonding can be found in 4a/4j complexed with the reverse transcriptase (RT) target. However, 4a-RT binding is stronger than the 4j-RT binding.

In sum, it should be noticed that more compounds with substituents differing in physicochemical properties such as electronegativity and lipophilicity located in different positions of the main scaffold are needed to be able to come to a reliable SAR conclusion.

Molecular docking simulation analysis

To understand the binding mode of the prepared compounds, molecular modeling studies were performed. The compounds were individually docked into the non-nucleoside binding pocket (NNBP) of wild-type HIV-1 reverse transcriptase (PDB code: 2YNG) using Schrodinger Maestro version 9.3. Compounds 4a–k exhibited strong binding affinity for the receptor, indicating their potential as effective inhibitors (Table 2). The docking results were analyzed, and the binding modes within the NNBP are illustrated in Fig. 4. Among these, compounds 4b, 4f, and 4i achieved higher Glide scores compared with the other derivatives, owing to stronger lipophilic interactions and additional hydrogen bonding, which allowed them to fit more effectively into the active site. For compounds 4e and 4i, the pyridinone motif formed hydrogen bonds between the amide carbonyl group and Lys103 (H-bond length 2.77 Å), which are crucial for anti-HIV-1 activity. Furthermore, the phenyl moieties of 4e and 4i were oriented toward Tyr181 and Trp229 residues, thereby establishing π–π stacking interactions. These interactions are considered essential for the design and optimization of NNRTIs.

Molecular dynamics simulation analysis

The dynamic stability and structural variations of the 4e−2YNG complexes and 4i−2YNG complexes were assessed by tracking the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) and root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) during 300 ns of molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. These metrics provide insights into the overall stability of the protein-ligand structures and fluctuations during the simulation. In addition, the time-dependent interactions between the ligands and receptor residues, including hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic contacts, were analyzed.

To assess the conformational stability of the complexes, RMSD values were calculated and plotted (Fig. 5). The average RMSD of the compound 4i−2YNG complex (5.79 Å) was slightly higher than that of compound 4e−2YNG complex (5.20 Å), suggesting that the former underwent greater structural deviation from the initial conformation. 4i−2YNG complex reached equilibrium at approximately 110 ns and remained stable thereafter, with only minor fluctuations throughout the rest of the 300 ns trajectory. In contrast, 4e−2YNG complex required a longer equilibration time, stabilizing only after ~ 150 ns, but once equilibrium was reached, the fluctuations were similarly confined within a narrow range. The overall RMSD profiles therefore indicate that while both complexes ultimately maintained structural stability, 4e−2YNG complex exhibited a more gradual stabilization process, whereas 4i−2YNG complex equilibrated earlier but with slightly larger deviations from the starting structure. These observations suggest subtle differences in the dynamic behavior of the two complexes, which may reflect variations in their binding interactions and conformational flexibility.

(A) 4e−2YNG and (B) 4i −2YNG protein and ligand root mean square deviation (RMSD) obtained from the MD simulation trajectories.

The root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) was evaluated to examine the flexibility of individual amino acid residues throughout the MD simulations (Fig. 6). As expected, the highest fluctuations were observed at the terminal regions of the protein, which are typically more flexible. In contrast, residues located within the active site-such as Ile94, Pro95, Pro97, Leu100, Lys102, Lys103, Val108, Val179, Tyr181, Tyr183, Tyr188, Val189, Gly190, Pro225, Phe227, Trp229, Leu234, Pro236, and Tyr318-exhibited consistently low RMSF values (< 1.6 Å), indicative of their rigid behavior. The limited fluctuations of these key residues suggest that the binding pocket remained structurally stable during the simulations, thereby supporting the stable accommodation of the ligands within the active site of the protein.

The contact profiles of the 4e–2YNG and 4i–2YNG complexes were derived from the simulated trajectories, as illustrated in Figs. 7 and 8, respectively. Compound 4i maintained a strong hydrogen bond with LYS103 throughout the simulation, while an additional hydrogen bond was established with TYR318. Remarkably, LYS103 contributed to hydrogen bonding for over 99% of the total simulation time. Hydrophobic interactions were also detected with LEU100, VAL106, TRP229 and TYR318 residues, which collectively reinforced the stability of the protein–ligand complex.

In comparison, compound 4e formed a hydrogen bond with LYS103 for approximately 58% of the simulation time and engaged in hydrophobic contacts with VAL106, TYR181, TRP229, andTYR318. These molecular interactions supported complex stabilization, albeit less consistently than those observed for 4i.

In general, analysis of the MD simulations confirmed the hydrogen bond formation with Lys103 that had been predicted in the molecular docking studies. Furthermore, hydrophobic interaction analysis revealed that both inhibitors established hydrophobic contacts with Trp229 and Tyr188, which play a crucial role in the binding mode of these compounds.

Conclusion

In summary, a series of eleven pyridinone derivatives (4a-k) were synthesized as potential RT inhibitors, and their structural characterization was confirmed by FTIR, NMR, and mass spectrometry. Biological evaluation revealed that most compounds exhibited cytotoxicity against MT-4 cells and lacked antiviral activity; however, two derivatives (4e and 4i) demonstrated promising inhibitory effects against HIV. To further explore their binding behavior, molecular docking and 300 ns molecular dynamics simulations were performed. Docking revealed that hydrogen bonding with Lys103 and hydrophobic interactions with Trp229 and Tyr188 were critical for ligand recognition, which was further validated by MD simulations. Both compounds formed stable complexes with RT throughout the trajectory, supporting their potential as lead scaffolds for further optimization.
