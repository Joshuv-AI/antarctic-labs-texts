Assessing the environmental sustainability prospects of financial development, biocapacity, and renewable energy
Authors: Elma Satrovic, Ojonugwa Usman, Seyi Saint Akadiri, Magdalena Radulescu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
While previous studies have examined the impact of financial development on environmental sustainability, its interaction with ecological capacity remains underexplored. By integrating the modified STIRPAT framework with advanced panel quantile estimators, this study provides the first comprehensive assessment of the role of biocapacity in moderating the financial development-environmental sustainability nexus among selected countries with biocapacity reserves. Given that higher biocapacity typically indicates greater reserves of renewable natural resources (e.g., biomass, soil, water, forests), broader financial support in such countries may favour renewable energy projects over non-renewable ones, thereby mitigating environmental challenges. In this context, this study investigates the environmental implications of financial development, biocapacity, and renewable energy for ten biocapacity-rich countries from 1995 to 2020. Also, this study unveils the effectiveness of biocapacity in moderating the effect of financial development on environmental sustainability. The results indicate that biocapacity, economic complexity, and renewable energy enhance environmental sustainability, whereas financial development, economic growth, and population growth exert negative effects. Notably, the marginal effect of financial development on sustainability increases with biocapacity, suggesting that a joint expansion of both factors yields positive outcomes. These findings underscore the need for policies that protect ecosystems and regulate resource use to integrate biocapacity considerations into financial development strategies. Also, governments should align such measures with the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) to address environmental challenges and secure long-term environmental stability.

Introduction

As the human demand for resources increases due to economic growth, consumption, and population expansion, the need to achieve environmental sustainability becomes more pressing [48]. Ecological deterioration, ecosystem threats, and the acceleration of climate change are all results of the overuse of natural resources [17]. Policymakers are prompted by the loss of natural wealth to create plans that combine economic activity with environmental preservation, in line with the SDGs. In response, several countries have implemented sustainable transformation strategies to prevent resource overuse and strengthen ecosystems’ capacity to produce renewable resources and absorb waste [3].

While climate change is among most pressing global problems, the world average ecological footprints remain higher than the biocapacity. According to the World Population Review [55], the world average ecological footprint was about 2.6 global hectares per person, and the average biocapacity was 1.5 global hectares per person, leaving the world average deficit of 1.1 global hectares per person. This has posed growing global concerns about ecological degradation despite considerable natural resource endowments in some countries. Countries with biocapacity reserves are expected to maintain higher levels of environmental sustainability. However, the demand for rapid economic growth, urbanisation, and rising consumption patterns can put pressure on these ecological assets. Therefore, it is important to understand how biocapacity relates to environmental sustainability to determine whether these reserves support long-term ecological balance. As noted, sustainable resource use has come under scrutiny due to the ecological effects of accelerated economic growth and the global drive for financial inclusion [45]. Growing financial systems have raised questions about whether easier access to capital encourages environmentally harmful investments, especially in resource-intensive sectors [13]. The outcomes of research on the combined effects of biocapacity and financial development on the environment remain contentious, particularly in nations with abundant natural resources, where these resources are expected to act as a buffer. This necessitates examining the intricate interactions between biocapacity and financial systems and their effects on environmental sustainability, since environmental degradation remains a major problem worldwide amid the expansion of the financial industry and technological advancements.

This study is specifically designed to address the following research question: How does financial development affect environmental sustainability in countries with biocapacity reserves (BRC)? How does biocapacity influence the relationship between financial development and environmental sustainability in countries with BRC? It is worth noting that several factors affirm the choice of countries with BRC. First, biocapacity outshines the ecological footprint, implying that these countries are qualified to be net exporters of resources. Second, BRC countries can maintain their ecosystems’ capacity to generate natural resources and protect biodiversity. Third, both developed and developing countries have biocapacity reserves. This signals that some developed countries are still not consuming all the materials on earth that can be extracted annually. Fourth, although BRC countries can import materials from the earth and emit anthropogenic emissions, a biocapacity reserve enables these countries to supply resources faster than their populations grow. Fifth, BRC countries are experiencing low levels of economic complexity, particularly since the COVID-19 pandemic (see Fig. 1), with their index rankings continuing to decline [1]. This means that countries with biocapacity reserves may not perform well at exporting more numerous and complex products (i.e., economic complexity). Given this background, this study provides a novel idea that unveils the effects of biocapacity, financial development, economic complexity, renewable energy, and population on environmental sustainability for ten countries with biocapacity reserves within the Stochastic Impacts by Regression on Population, Affluence, and Technology (STIRPAT) model. In addition, we employ interactive models to determine how biocapacity moderates the effect of financial development on the environmental sustainability of countries with biocapacity reserves. It deviates from previous studies in how it measures environmental sustainability. In contrast to previous studies, carbon productivity proxies can provide a holistic economic assessment of environmental degradation [19, 44]. These are expected to capture environmental sustainability properly. We also employ two proxies for financial development: the financial development index and the financial institution index. These indices capture diverse aspects of financial development. This also makes our study unique and distinguishes it from previous related studies. Furthermore, our study focuses on countries with biocapacity reserves. This is quite different from previous studies, as these countries offer a unique setting to assess how the simultaneous increase in biocapacity and financial development affects environmental degradation. Existing literature has largely focused on ecological deficits, while countries with biocapacity surpluses remain underexplored. Examining this relationship would offer valuable insights into effectively managing and preserving natural ecological capacity to achieve the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goals.

In summary, this study makes several new contributions that differentiate it from the existing literature and make it more broadly relevant. To begin with, biocapacity is introduced as a moderating factor in the financial development-environmental sustainability nexus; ecological endowments are brought into consideration in a strand of literature that has largely ignored natural capital conditions. Second, rather than using conventional environmental indicators such as CO₂ emissions or ecological footprint, as previous studies have done, this study uses carbon productivity and carbon footprint productivity, which together represent both economic and environmental dimensions of sustainability. Third, this study uses dual proxies of financial development—financial development and financial institutions indices—for a more comprehensive evaluation of dynamics within the financial system. Fourth, by focusing on nations with biocapacity reserves, the analysis highlights a relatively underexplored group of economies in which an ecological surplus may shape sustainability outcomes differently. Finally, advanced panel quantile techniques have been applied here, enabling identification of heterogeneous effects across different levels of environmental sustainability. This offers deeper insights for both policy and academic discourse.

Literature review

The pursuit of environmental sustainability has attracted considerable scholarly attention, particularly considering the environmental problems associated with increasing industrialisation. The literature now in publication emphasises how several ecological and economic factors, such as biocapacity, economic complexity, financial development, and the use of renewable energy, affect sustainability. Research indicates that although economic expansion benefits human well-being, it often leads to greater resource exploitation and pollution, making it harder for ecosystems to maintain natural resource levels and absorb waste [39, 46]. According to scholars, regulations that incorporate sustainable practices are necessary because the depletion of these resources disrupts ecological equilibrium and accelerates climate change [57].

The ability of an ecosystem to replenish resources and reduce dangerous pollutants is known as biocapacity, and it is widely accepted as a crucial element of environmental resilience. According to earlier studies, biocapacity and sustainability are positively correlated, with higher biocapacity levels being associated with smaller ecological footprints and better biodiversity preservation [22, 23]. For example, research on countries involved in the Belt and Road Initiative shows that biocapacity can serve as a buffer against ecological degradation by reducing the negative externalities associated with resource extraction [43]. But in resource-rich yet economically insecure nations, where demand for resources often outpaces regenerative capacity, this ability is commonly underutilised or overstressed.

The growth of financial services and the availability of capital, or financial development, have conflicting effects on environmental sustainability. On the one hand, access to reasonably priced financing might help industries grow, potentially increasing resource consumption and emissions [12]. Particularly in developing nations with lax environmental restrictions, affordable loans may encourage carbon-intensive industrial operations, hence accelerating environmental degradation [30]. On the other hand, by encouraging green investments and the adoption of clean technologies, financial development can positively impact sustainability by increasing energy efficiency and reducing emissions [18]. The dual character of financial development suggests that the ecological context, including the availability of biocapacity, may influence its implications on sustainability.

Another important factor is economic complexity, which shows a country’s level of expertise and capacity to manufacture high-tech goods. Higher productivity and innovation in environmental technologies are characteristics of complex economies, which may reduce emissions intensity [2]. However, when manufacturing depends on non-renewable resources, greater economic complexity can also lead to higher energy demand and pollution. According to Khaliq & Mamkhezri [24], developed countries can reduce their environmental impact by adopting cleaner industrial techniques. In contrast, developing countries frequently struggle to implement eco-friendly practices, which can have adverse ecological effects. This relationship between complexity and sustainability emphasises how economic structures shape environmental outcomes, in low-regulation contexts, some research indicates that greater economic complexity can lead to lower environmental resilience [26, 28].

The adoption of renewable energy is also widely recognised as a foundational element of sustainable development. It has been demonstrated that switching from fossil fuels to renewable energy sources reduces carbon emissions and lessens environmental stress [16, 31]. Because they reduce reliance on scarce resources, renewables offer long-term economic benefits and generate very little waste [45]. Research highlights that green energy promotes biodiversity conservation and increases resource availability for future generations [30]. Moreover, Khan et al. [25] investigated how the deployment of technology and green energy influences environmental sustainability in G8 industrialised nations. Using the load capacity factor to measure environmental quality, the study found that technology improves the load capacity factor, thereby enhancing environmental quality, particularly in countries with middle- or high-load capacity factors.

Recently, several studies have used different methods to examine the role of artificial intelligence (AI)-powered renewable energy transitions in environmental sustainability. For instance, Zhang et al. [59] focused on how AI technological innovation affects carbon emissions efficiency, employing a machine-learning methodology based on meta-frontier analysis. To achieve this, they employed a panel of 75 countries. Their results suggested that AI-based technological innovation not only improves carbon emission efficiency but also narrows technological gaps. The relationship between AI and efficiency is also characterised by an inverted U-shape, with the greatest gain detected in the middle-tier technology groups. Wang et al. [52], meanwhile, examined the influence of AI on environmental sustainability and further tested what they called the "AI-EKC" in the context of SDGs 7 and 13 across 81 countries between 2000 and 2020. Based on the entropy-based TOPSIS composite AI index, their findings revealed evidence of an EKC pattern associated with AI. This means that, initially, the use of AI increases energy and resource demand, thereby intensifying environmental threats. However, as AI adoption continues to deepen, substantial gains in energy efficiency and stronger integration of renewable energy would occur.

Furthermore, given that AI reshapes the landscape of sustainable development, Zhang et al. [58] developed a new AI-based index using a projection-pursuit genetic algorithm to examine how AI affects environmental sustainability, accounting for the synergistic effect of human capital. Although the impact of AI on sustainability follows an inverted-U relationship, the use of renewable energy enhances AI’s ability to reduce carbon emissions. However, the environmental benefits of human capital and AI show diminishing returns.

Satrovic et al. [47] reveal that in the G7, economic complexity could increase ecological pressure until clean innovation and clean energy redirect it toward stronger load capacity. Xinyu et al. [49] also report for China that green technological innovation reduces the ecological footprint, the environmental effect of financial development is conditional rather than universally positive. Extending the sustainability debate, Wen and Yang [51] find that artificial intelligence improves carbon-emission efficiency through innovation and talent agglomeration, while Huang et al. [20] underscore structural transition and efficiency differences between high- and low-carbon industries as important drivers for low-carbon development.

Compared with these studies, our study makes a more precise contribution by zooming in on financial development, biocapacity, and renewable energy in biocapacity-reserve countries. It finds that environmental sustainability is enhanced by biocapacity, economic complexity, and renewable energy, while financial development is detrimental per se but beneficial when moderated by biocapacity. This differs from Satrovic et al. [47], where complexity is environmentally detrimental unless accompanied by clean innovation support, and hence complements Xinyu et al. [49] in the sense that the finance–environment nexus depends not only on policy conditions but also on ecological endowment.

By investigating the relationship between biocapacity and financial development in the context of environmental sustainability, this study builds on earlier studies. By examining the relationship between ecological capacity and financial sector growth across multiple nations, this study fills a knowledge gap in the scant literature on how biocapacity could mitigate the environmental effects of financial sector expansion. The work contributes to a more nuanced understanding of the relationship between the environment and the economy by employing the environment-economy nexus across various quantiles of environmental indicators to uniquely capture how selected factors influence sustainability outcomes across heterogeneous levels of carbon and carbon footprint productivity. The summary of prior studies on the financial development–environment nexus (mixed findings and gaps) is presented in the Appendix (Table A1).

Data and methodology

Data clarification

In this study, we use the annual longitudinal data for 10 BRC countries (Australia, Bolivia, Brazil, Canada, Finland, Madagascar, Mongolia, Mozambique, Paraguay, and Uruguay). We examine the determinants of environmental sustainability, such as biocapacity, economic growth, financial development, economic complexity, renewable energy, and population, from 1995 to 2020. In doing so, we consider the twin aspects of environmental sustainability, namely, the economic rate of return on carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions (carbon productivity) and the economic rate of return on the carbon component of the ecological footprint (carbon footprint productivity).

Given their natural resource endowments, these countries’ exports are characterised by low-complexity products such as minerals and a limited array of advanced and distinctive export categories. The selection of the time span is entirely based on data availability. The lack of data on the economic complexity index before 1995 led to selecting this year as the starting point. Due to the unavailability of renewable energy data for 2021, 2020 is chosen as the ending year. Theoretical and empirical model development

The literature to date has established that economic expansion may boost anthropogenic emissions and considerably impair environmental sustainability [15, 36, 39]. To identify the environmental impact of economic expansion in countries with biocapacity reserves, we developed an advanced IPAT model that treats Population (P), Affluence (A), and Technology (T) as vital determinants of environmental impact (I). This model, as advanced by Dietz & Rosa [10], is known as STIRPAT. Furthermore, the second refinement to the traditional STIRPAT approach includes the biocapacity-environmental sustainability interlock as an indicator of the ecosystem’s capacity to supply usable natural wealth and absorb harmful pollutants emitted by human activities. Biocapacity refers to the capacity of biologically productive areas to regenerate materials from the earth and to treat waste attributable to human activities [14]. Biocapacity expansion is associated with favourable impacts on environmental sustainability, as it improves the ecosystem’s capacity to assimilate waste and reduce anthropogenic emissions [22]. As a third refinement to the conventional STIRPAT model, this study includes financial development to assess its relationship with environmental sustainability. In this study, financial development refers to an increased supply of low-cost, easily accessible, sustainable loans. It can be regarded as the process of broadening the financial system by increasing its efficiency, depth, and access, as well as the availability of financial products [9]. Notably, the present study is based on two financial development proxies: the FBD-financial development index and the FBI-financial institutions index. The former captures the aggregated index depicting the development of both financial markets and institutions in terms of efficiency, depth, and access [21]. As such, FBD reflects the capability and expansion of the entire financial system. However, the FBI specifically emphasises the access, financial performance, and efficiency of financial institutions (banks, insurance companies, mutual funds, and pension funds). Subsequently, this index unveils whether financial institutions promote financial inclusion, i.e., access to financial services [21]. To offer a more thorough investigation of financial development, the present study relies on heterogeneous yet complementary indicators to examine whether environmental sustainability is uniquely shaped by the financial system’s capability (FBD) and the availability of financial services (FBI).

There are two opposing perspectives on the theoretical relationship between financial development and the environment. From one outlook, the positive nexus between environmental deterioration and financial development is underlined [11, 30, 60]. From another perspective, financial development may have a favourable impact on environmental sustainability, thereby driving the business sector towards green innovations and energy-efficient production facilities [18, 33]. Therefore, the financial development is hypothesised to have either a positive or a negative association with environmental degradation (i.e. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\beta}_{4}=\frac{\partial ECD|ECF}{\partial FBD|FBI}\;<\,0\,or\,>\,0$$\end{document}).

Another important determinant is economic complexity, which measures the knowledge a nation generates, as reflected in the commodities it manufactures. The persistent increase in the economic complexity index ranking underlines a transition towards a greater variety of sophisticated merchandise. This shift is not without environmental consequences, as it increases energy consumption and induces anthropogenic emissions [24, 26]. Herein, economic complexity leads to a pervasive decline in the environmental sustainability of developing countries, as they impose less stringent environmental laws and enable the production and export of harmful goods. Conversely, a positive environmental impact of economic complexity has been sustained recently [4, 5, 28, 50] as it reduces energy intensity. The positive economic complexity-environmental sustainability nexus is established because countries with greater know-how are expected to produce and export more sophisticated, more environmentally friendly goods, thereby fostering environmental resilience. Accordingly, economic complexity may either lead to favourable or destructive environmental outcomes (i.e. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\beta}_{5}=\frac{\partial ECD|ECF}{\partial ECX}\,>\,0\,or\,<\,0$$\end{document}).

Further refinement assesses the correlation between renewable energy and environmental sustainability. A shift away from non-renewables towards green energy has been affirmed as a yardstick for reducing carbon emissions [16, 31]. It is important to note that renewable energy is a clean source that effectively reduces environmental pressure (i.e. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\upbeta}_{6}=\frac{\partial ECD|ECF}{\partial RWS}>0$$\end{document}) and paves the way towards sustainable fulfilment of societal needs [30, 34]. Finally, population dynamics have had significant impacts on economic, social, cultural, educational, and other aspects of society, and are strongly correlated with the environment. Migration to urban areas in search of jobs places significant pressure on resources such as water, food, land, and energy, leading to severe environmental challenges. Population growth frequently exceeds the pace of infrastructure development and natural resource availability, resulting in heightened environmental pressures [8, 32, 53]. Herein, the population is hypothesised to hinder the environmental sustainability of the on-hand sample (i.e. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\upbeta}_{7}=\frac{\partial ECD|ECF}{\partial PP}<0$$\end{document}).

Most importantly, this study introduces an interaction term (Mod1) to assess the role of biocapacity in moderating the financial development-environmental sustainability nexus. Thereby, an examination of biocapacity can help environmental protection agencies better understand the critical role that natural resource availability plays in mitigating the adverse environmental impacts of economic expansion. Given the arguments adduced in this study, biocapacity can positively or negatively moderate the financial development-environmental sustainability nexus (i.e. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{\partial ECD|ECF}{\partial \left(CPB\times FBD|FBI\right)}\,>\,0\,\,\,\mathrm{or} \,\,\frac{\partial ECD|ECF}{\partial \left(CPB\times FBD|FBI\right)}\,<\,0$$\end{document}). Analytical framework

Before estimating our model, we check for cross-sectional dependence (CRC) among the variables. This is because the countries under examination are likely to experience CRC, given the globalisation process that amplifies persistent interdependence and erodes pervasive differences among them. The selection of the optimal analytical approach also depends on the heterogeneity of slope coefficients (HDH), as neglecting a high degree of HDH and CRC can lead to unreliable findings. Against this backdrop, Pesaran [40] is employed to explore the persistence of CRC formulated as Eq. 5:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$CRC=\sqrt{\frac{2T}{N(N-1)}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\sum_{j=i+1}^{N}{\widehat{\rho }}_{ij}\right).$$\end{document}

In Eq. 5, the on-hand countries are denoted by N while the period of analysis (T) spans from 1995 to 2020. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\widehat{\rho }}_{ij}$$\end{document} indicates the correlation between the two variables being inspected. Under H0, errors are independent across N, whereas under H1, the CRC issue persists.

Recalling the CRC issue, the present study estimates slope heterogeneity. This is because balanced panel data can be plagued by HDH, which can lead to biased findings if ignored. To resolve this issue, we use the Pesaran & Yamagata [42] HDH test, which examines the characteristics of slope coefficients. The issue is evaluated through Eq. 6 and Eq. 7 as follows:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\widetilde{\Delta }}_{HDH}={\left(N\right)}^{1/2}{\left(2k\right)}^{1/2}\left(\frac{1}{N}\widetilde{S}-k\right),$$\end{document}7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\widetilde{\Delta }}_{AHDH}={\left(N\right)}^{1/2}{\left(\frac{2k(T-k-1)}{T+1}\right)}^{-1/2}\left(\frac{1}{N}\widetilde{S}-2k\right).$$\end{document}

Based on Eqs. 6 and 7, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\widetilde{\Delta }}_{HDH}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\widetilde{\Delta }}_{HDH}$$\end{document} are the notations for the delta and delta-adjusted tests. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\widetilde{S}$$\end{document} outlines the t-statistics, with independent variables highlighted by k.

Given the economic, social, and political differences across countries with biocapacity reserves, HDH and CRC issues are likely to occur in panel data analysis. Subsequently, first-generation unit root tests may not yield plausible findings, calling for the use of second-generation unit root tests to examine the stationarity of the determinants of environmental sustainability. Against this backdrop, this study establishes the Cross-section Im, Pesaran, and Shin (STF) test by Pesaran [41] as shown in Eq. 8:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\widehat{STF}={N}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{CADF}_{i},$$\end{document}where the cross-sectional augmented Dickey-Fuller (CADF) is used to estimate the STF statistics under the H0 that the longitudinal data is non-stationary. At the same time, H1 suggests that the selected variable is stationary and does not exhibit a unit root.

The next strategy involves using the panel cointegration test (PCG), applicable to panel data samples attributable to HDH and CRC issues. In particular, the Westerlund [56] test is applied to assess the long-term association between various determinants of environmental sustainability. The rejection of H0 provides sufficient evidence to conclude that environmental sustainability has a long-run nexus with the selected independent variables. The selection of Westerlund [56] is justified because it addresses cross-sectional dependence [6]. Moreover, when individual slope coefficients are considered, Westerlund [56] is a suitable panel cointegration test for slope heterogeneity [29].

After confirming the cointegration relationship, we applied the Method of Moments Quantile Regression (QLMM) to investigate the impacts of the selected independent variables on environmental sustainability. This technique is used to assess the effects of independent variables on ECD and ECF across selected nations with low, middle, and high levels of ecological sustainability. According to Machado and Silva [35], mean-effect analytical frameworks are likely to yield inaccurate results when outliers are present. In addition, Koenker & Bassett [27] verified that regression coefficients based on mean-effects estimators may be over- or under-rated. To address these issues, our study employs the QLMM, which performs better in the presence of outliers. In addition, QLMM yields more reliable findings when there are variations across the low, medium, and high quantiles of environmental sustainability [45]. This analytical approach is also fruitful in mitigating HDH, CRC, and endogeneity issues and is well-suited to assessing nonlinear associations. Another advantage of the QLMM approach is that it conveys trustworthy findings in the case of asymmetric associations, underscoring the heterogeneities between scale and location effects. Given these attributes, QLMM is effective at estimating conditional quantiles and outlining critical policy consequences. Table 7 and Fig. 2 present the findings of the long-run relationship between ECD and the selected independent variables, estimated using the QLMM and OLS, with DK standard errors. The coefficients on economic growth are negatively associated with the economic rate of return on CO2 emissions and are statistically significant in BRC economies with middle- and upper-middle levels of ECD. Models 1 and 2 (without the interaction term) show a statistically significant positive coefficient for the quadratic term of economic growth, confirming the U-shaped relationship between LECS and environmental sustainability. The coefficients of LECS decrease from lower to higher quantiles of environmental sustainability, whereas the coefficients of LECS2 increase. Subsequently, economic growth curtails environmental sustainability in the early stages, with a more pronounced impact in selected countries with lower levels of ECD. The positive coefficient on LECS2 and the negative coefficient on LECS indicate that economic growth substantially inhibits environmental sustainability in the early stages of growth. In contrast, at higher stages of growth, environmental quality improves.

The specifications with the interaction term (Models 3 and 4) align with Models 1 and 2 and are consistent with Satrovic et al. [47], who found a harmful impact of economic activity on environmental sustainability in the initial waves of growth for G7 countries. This is because these countries benefit from ECS’s favourable environmental impact in later waves of growth. Similarly, Satrovic et al. [46] reported that the preliminary stage damaging effect of economic growth transforms into a desirable one in the final waves of growth. Likewise, Khaliq & Mamkhezri [24] found that economic progress undermines environmental sustainability in the early stages of growth.

In contrast, later stages are associated with environmental improvement, supporting the EKC hypothesis in South Asia. However, Jabeen et al. [22] reported findings that did not support an inverted U-shaped relationship between ECS and the environment. Notably, the EKC theory is not confirmed considering the Belt and Road countries. The non-confirmation of the EKC hypothesis is justifiable because many Belt and Road countries are still in the early stages of growth, with energy- and carbon-intensive economic activities, and are struggling to reach the turning point. Moreover, these countries are strongly dependent on fossil fuels. Subsequently, intensified economic activity is likely to fortify anthropogenic emissions, thereby obstructing the downward phase of EKC. In a sample of emerging countries, Fan et al. [12] validated the EKC hypothesis, showing that economic growth initially increases the ecological footprint. In contrast, later stages lead to environmental improvements.

The coefficients of LCPB indicate that the excessive dependence of the selected countries on their ecosystems’ capacity to generate natural resources and absorb waste mitigates environmental challenges. The coefficient of biocapacity is positive and statistically significant across selected countries with low, middle, and upper levels of environmental sustainability, displaying a decreasing trend. Herein, across all quantiles of environmental sustainability, biocapacity improves environmental sustainability with more substantial impacts in selected nations with lower levels of ECD.

It is pertinent to note that the positive coefficients of LCPB indicate that biocapacity serves as a prominent solution to environmental deterioration in the selected countries. The feedback from the specifications without a moderator indicates that the LCPB’s advantageous environmental impact is weakened at higher quantiles, suggesting that biocapacity strongly drives carbon productivity in the selected countries with lower environmental sustainability, and vice versa. Specifically, countries with higher carbon intensity (i.e., lower carbon productivity) tend to increase their biocapacity, as it is recognised as a key tool for alleviating environmental challenges toward a greener future. The economic logic underlying the beneficial association between biocapacity and environmental sustainability rests on the notion that biocapacity enables the atmosphere to absorb and filter more waste, thereby reducing levels of environmental pollutants. Numerous studies to date have mentioned the beneficial environmental impact of biocapacity [22, 23, 43, 57]. Accordingly, these studies reinforce evidence of beneficial ecological impacts in the ten BRC economies.

The environmental impacts of financial development (Models 1—4) are significant, undermining environmental sustainability and indicating that financial development impedes the achievement of BRC’s carbon mitigation targets. This impact is persistent across the odd and even quantiles, indicating that its extent decreases from lower to higher QA. Intuitively, an increase in financial development constrains BRC’s environmental resilience, resulting in a larger impact in nations with lower carbon productivity. These findings verify the robustness of our main findings in the light of an alternative proxy for financial development.

It is equally evident from QLMM’s empirical findings that BRC’s dependence on financial development exacerbates carbon intensity and environmental deterioration. The possible reasoning is that financial expansion facilitates access to cheap loans that sustain BRC’s industrialisation, which is accompanied not only by economic welfare but also by environmental challenges. Similarly, Fan et al. [12] found that the accumulation of funds fails to achieve the environmental sustainability objectives of emerging countries. It is justifiable because most BRCs are developing, and their governments are prioritising short-term targets over long-term sustainability gains. Likewise, Bu et al. [7] revealed the devastating environmental impact of financial development in emerging economies. Economic growth triggers an upswing in production and consumption, thereby intensifying pollutant emissions and degrading environmental quality.

Economic complexity positively influences environmental sustainability across all specifications and is statistically significant at the low, middle, and upper quantiles. The outcomes show an upward trend: economic complexity strengthens the environmental sustainability of selected countries, with a more pronounced effect in nations with higher levels of ECD. Models with the interaction term (Models 1 and 2) reveal that economic complexity significantly increases carbon productivity and curbs BRC’s carbon intensity, thereby playing a vital role in promoting environmental sustainability. The coefficient of LECX is statistically significant and positive across all QAs, with an increasing trend, indicating that improvements in economic efficiency will enhance the environmental resilience of BRC economies with higher levels of carbon productivity. Similarly, specifications with the interaction term (Models 3 and 4) yield an impact consistent with composition and technique effects.

A possible reason for a positive environmental impact of economic complexity is that countries advancing in economic complexity foster the implementation of green projects. These countries are exporting greener items that enable a shift towards net-zero emissions targets. Countries with complex economies are committed to capitalising on the frontiers of eco-friendly machinery by replacing traditional energy sources with new, energy-efficient, and carbon-productive equipment. In addition, more complex economies may address environmental issues by transferring technology and supporting its adoption in simpler economic structures. Our findings align with Bakhsh et al. [4], who found that economic complexity improves environmental quality in OECD countries by reinforcing energy efficiency and shifting away from non-renewable energy sources toward green energy sources.

Similarly, Bakhsh et al. [5] reported an advantageous environmental impact of economic complexity, in that higher economic complexity endows countries with the essential human and financial capital to effectively address the threats and seize the opportunities associated with green energy transformation. In contrast, Satrovic et al. [47] posited the negative environmental impact in the sample of G7 countries. This finding is reasonable, as sophisticated production often requires substantial energy and may involve intensive extraction of natural resources, thereby generating environmental pressures in leading industrialised countries. Moreover, G7 countries rely heavily on energy-intensive, cutting-edge technologies. If the energy used to support these technologies is generated from non-renewable sources, environmental challenges are likely to intensify. In addition, given their high incomes, citizens of G7 countries can afford energy-intensive, technologically sophisticated products (e.g., electronics), which further stimulate both demand- and supply-driven anthropogenic emissions.

Renewable energy supports the carbon productivity of BRC nations, portraying its vital importance in achieving the environmental sustainability objectives across all models in Table 7. There is a statistically significant decreasing trend across the low and upper quantiles of environmental sustainability. Herein, the selected nations with lower levels of ECD are more likely to capitalise on the environmental benefits of renewable energy. The environmental advantage of renewable energy is that energy generated from renewable sources is cleaner, emitting little to no pollutants. Consequently, it is a critical tool for tackling environmental deterioration and climate change, as adroitly explained by Hassan et al. [16]. Similarly, Ma et al. [34] indicated that LRWS plays a vital role in the transition towards energy- and carbon-efficient production and consumption, thereby establishing a low-carbon society. Similarly, Li et al. [30] postulated that renewable energy positively influences the ecological performance of emerging countries by reducing dependence on fossil fuels, stimulating the adoption of modern production equipment, and securing sustainable export revenue from sophisticated, diversified products.

Furthermore, population impedes improvements in carbon productivity, as PP and ECD are negatively associated across all model specifications and decline. This can be attributed to the fact that population growth intensifies urbanisation, which is associated with greater consumption of natural resources. To meet this rising demand, natural resource extraction increases, thereby triggering increased pollutant emissions and environmental deterioration. Despite the positive economic, social, and cultural effects of urbanisation, its negative environmental impacts should not be overlooked, as it fosters deforestation and biodiversity loss and intensifies ecological deterioration [32]. Likewise, Chen [8] argued that urbanisation causes environmental deterioration in emerging countries by leading to land degradation, increased waste, and increased pollution.

To comprehensively assess the impact of biocapacity on the financial development-environmental sustainability relationship, Models 3 and 4 introduce moderators to formalise specifications with the interaction terms. Based on the empirical results in Table 7 and Fig. 2, the coefficient for the combined influence of biocapacity and financial development on environmental sustainability is statistically significant and positive across all quantiles. Herein, biocapacity is confirmed as a channel through which financial development impacts environmental sustainability.

The coefficient of the financial development-biocapacity interaction term is positive and statistically significant across all ECD quantiles, indicating a decreasing trend. Thus, biocapacity reduces the adverse environmental impact of financial development, especially in the selected countries with low environmental sustainability. Despite the negative impact of financial development on the ECD, it is pertinent to note that it supports environmental sustainability when paired with biocapacity. In this light, biocapacity amplification enables nations to reap the benefits of natural capital exports by generating additional earnings from foreign markets, strengthening the exchange rate, combating inflation, and reducing interest rates. Consequently, firms, individuals, governments, and households will capitalise on affordable credits to encourage green projects and technological advancement.

The positive coefficient of the moderator is reasonable, as increased capacity of biologically productive areas paves the way for reducing adverse ecological effects associated with financial development. Specifically, FBD and FBI may spur manufacturing and investment in energy- and carbon-intensive economic activities, necessitating higher levels of biocapacity to absorb anthropogenic emissions and mitigate environmental pressure. Moreover, financial development may serve as a vital mechanism to support projects focused on naturally replenishing energy sources rather than non-renewable ones, thereby advancing environmental sustainability, especially in countries with abundant biocapacity reserves. In countries with sizable biocapacity reserves, financial development may ensure enough funds for green technologies, eco-friendly production facilities, and eco-friendly agriculture. In countries where the ecological footprint is below biocapacity (i.e., biocapacity reserve), financial development, particularly through green finance, can drive policies to conserve and replenish natural capital. Therefore, biocapacity mitigates the adverse environmental impacts of financial development and bolsters progress in environmental sustainability when LFBD and LFBI interact with LCPB.

Finally, this study conducts robustness checks using alternative dependent variable (the economic rate of return on the carbon component of the ecological footprint (LECF) – Models 5—8) and an alternative analytical framework (STER) to uncover the mean effects (Tables 7 and 8). It is equally pertinent to note that expanding biocapacity is paramount to enhancing BRC’s carbon footprint productivity by substantially reducing resource- and pollution-intensive production at its sites. The findings further revealed that economic complexity and renewable energy are effective in enforcing environmental sustainability targets in the inspected countries, whereas population size exacerbates environmental issues. As far as the mean effects are concerned, an incremental increase in economic growth leads to a 0.879%, 0.820%, 1.790%, and 1.788% decline in carbon productivity and carbon footprint productivity in models 1, 2, 5, and 6, respectively. The escalating impacts of biocapacity, economic complexity, and renewable energy on environmental sustainability are evident from the positive coefficients of LCPB, LECX, and LRWS, respectively, across all model specifications. However, financial development and population are yielding unfavourable environmental impacts, as evidenced by the negative coefficients for LFBD, LFBI, and LPP.

Estimating alternative model specifications yields findings that do not differ from our baseline in sign or significance, supporting their regularity from economic and econometric perspectives and thus providing a plausible basis for identifying policy implications.

Conclusion and policy implications

This study examined how the interaction between financial development and biocapacity influences environmental sustainability in 10 countries with biocapacity reserves. Our empirical findings showed that the interaction term between financial development and biocapacity can mitigate pollutant emissions and improve environmental sustainability in BRC economies. financial development is presented to strengthen environmental sustainability through the lens of biocapacity. Furthermore, our results suggested a U-shaped relationship between economic growth and environmental sustainability. While financial development and population growth impede environmental sustainability, biocapacity, economic complexity, and renewable energy promote environmental sustainability in countries with biocapacity reserves.

Based on these findings, the following policy frameworks can be implemented to sustain not only low-carbon emissions but also a net-zero path in these countries: First, policymakers need to design environmental frameworks that preserve biocapacity, ensuring ecosystems regenerate resources faster than consumption rates. To this extent, governments should design and implement stricter regulations to prevent the excessive exploitation of natural resources, deforestation, and land degradation. Additionally, programs should be designed by governments and policymakers to protect ecological zones and biodiversity conservation. Safeguarding biocapacity and enhancing its capacity to support sustainable economic activities are imperative for a sustainable environment.

Second, since the study shows that biocapacity moderates the environmental effect of financial development, governments should incorporate biocapacity indicators into financial sector policies to ensure that financial development does not exceed ecological limits. To this extent, governments and their managers could introduce green credit allocation frameworks to align financing projects with ecosystem regeneration. Additionally, financial institutions should conduct ecological risk assessments as a prerequisite for loans for large-scale resource projects. This will help the financial sector pursue an eco-friendly, sustainable investment environment.

Third, governments and policymakers should promote financing and investment in clean, renewable energy, as it supports environmental sustainability. In this case, governments should promote financial expansion for clean energy investments through instruments such as green bonds, renewable energy credit guarantees, and sustainability-linked loans. Governments should also encourage public–private partnerships for green and renewable energy projects in solar, wind, biomass, and hydro. Moreover, governments should offer incentives and subsidies for financial institutions that fund clean and renewable energy infrastructure. This will encourage financial institutions to channel their capital into renewable energy sources that support environmental sustainability.

Fourth, the finding that greater economic complexity improves environmental sustainability suggests that economies that produce higher-value-added goods tend to adopt cleaner technologies. Therefore, governments and policymakers should encourage technological innovation and green manufacturing by implementing appropriate industrial policies and providing financial support for low-carbon industries, green technology start-ups, and environmentally friendly production processes. Furthermore, investment in R&D in sustainable technologies should be prioritised. These strategies can all shift economic structures toward less resource-intensive production.

Fifth, as population growth has been found to worsen environmental sustainability, governments should implement policies to reduce environmental pressures associated with demographic growth. Such policies include promoting sustainable urban planning and smart cities, investing in energy-efficient housing and public transport, and encouraging resource-efficient consumption and waste management systems. These policy measures could dampen the environmental impact of population growth.

Finally, our study faces some limitations. First, since renewable energy and economic complexity improve environmental sustainability in this study, it would advance our knowledge if future research could capture the moderating effects of these variables on the relationship between financial development and environmental sustainability. Also, our findings are restricted to countries with biocapacity reserves, which may limit their generalizability. We suggest that future research consider countries with biocapacity deficits and verify whether these results hold. Furthermore, although the endogeneity issue could be addressed using Driscoll-Kraay standard errors, future studies could employ a causal approach. Finally, to make the analysis robust, future studies could employ dynamic panel models, disaggregated financial indicators, or sub-national biocapacity data.
