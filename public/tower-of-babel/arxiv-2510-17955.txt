Sub-Snowline Formation of Gas-Giant Planets in Binary Systems
Ilay Kamai, Hagai B. Perets, Jakob Stegmann, Evgeni Grishin

Abstract
Giant planets are thought to build their cores beyond the “snow line”, where water ice solids catalyze efficient planetary core growth ( Pollack et al. 1996 ; Armitage 2024 ) . In close binary star systems, however, the companion star’s gravity shrinks the region where orbits are long-term stable ( Holman and Wiegert 1999 ) , effectively excluding the zone where giants should form. Nevertheless, here we show that such systems exist and are not rare. Among 393 binary systems with measured orbits and circumstellar gas giants, we identify 19 for which the snowline region is unstable. The distribution of their metallicities and eccentricities is consistent with the background population, making a capture origin or enhanced solids abundance unlikely causes for their formation. Instead, we show that the sub-snowline formation paradox can be resolved by the tidal torque from the companion, which truncates the protoplanetary disk and creates a pressure bump and effective dust trap that facilitates the formation of gas giants. Using this model, we can successfully p redict the observed locations of gas giants from their host binary properties. Moreover, the main inconsistent outliers from these predictions are found to be evolved stellar systems (white dwarfs or red-giant stars), further supporting the model and pointing to these being second-generation planets. Our results have therefore key implications for understanding planet formation processes and provide observational support for sub-snowline gas-giant formation and the role of trap-dusts in their formation.

Planetary formation is thought to be a bottom-up process
Safronov 1972
, where dust particles accrete through aerodynamic and gravitational collisions
(
Dominik and Tielens 1997
;
Wada et al. 2009
)
. This accretion is constrained by the gaseous protoplanetary disk lifetime (approximately 3-10 Myr
(
Ercolano and Pascucci 2017
)
) and the solids abundances. For a gas giant to form, it is thought that a solid core exceeding a critical mass (around  5-10
M_{\oplus}
) should form within the lifetime of the gaseous disk, allowing a phase of runaway growth and accretion of its massive gaseous envelope
(
Greenberg et al. 1978
;
Pollack et al. 1996
;
Armitage 2024
;
Ikoma and Kobayashi 2025
)
. To rapidly reach the critical core mass, dust aggregation must be highly efficient. An enhanced efficiency can result from the sharp change in material properties of the disk around the snow line, the distance to the host star at which the temperature is low enough for ice to form. In fact, ice aggregation is approximately ten times more effective compared to typical materials found below the snow line
(
Blum and Wurm 2008
;
Gundlach and Blum 2015
)
, suggesting the snow line as a critical lower limit for gas giant formation. However, here we identify observational evidence for sub-snowline formation of gas giants in binaries and develop a physically motivated mechanism that links measured binary orbits to predicted planet radii. We further show that the same framework explains outliers when accounting for stellar evolution or higher multiplicity, and it yields testable, system-by-system predictions.

When a planet is part of a binary system rather than a single star, the gravitational tides raised by the stellar companion limit the region where stable orbits exist.
Holman and Wiegert 1999
investigated the stability of circumstellar orbiting planets in a binary system, and derived the following relationship for the critical semi-major axis of an inner (circumstellar) planet:

\frac{a_{c}}{a_{b}}=0.464-0.380\mu-0.631e_{b}+0.586\mu e_{b}+0.150e_{b}^{2}-0.198\mu e_{b}^{2}.

(1)

Here,
a_{c}
is the critical semi-major axis for stability,
\mu=m_{2}/(m_{1}+m_{2})
is the stellar mass ratio, with
m_{2}
being the companion mass, and
m_{1}
the primary mass,
e_{b}
is the binary eccentricity, and
a_{b}
is the binary semi-major axis. This expression implies that, for a given binary with specific values of
a_{b}
,
e_{b}
, and
\mu
, there is an upper bound for a circumstellar planet’s position,
a_{c}
, beyond which it becomes dynamically unstable. There are other, more sophisticated, variants for this stability criteria, like
Quarles et al. 2020
, for example. However, they require information about the mutual inclination, which usually not exists.
Combining these definitions, we see that a gas giant in a circumstellar system has two constraints on its formation: the snowline,
r_{\rm snow}
, which defines a minimal formation distance, and the critical semi-major axis,
a_{c}
, which defines a maximum formation distance. This leads to a straightforward conclusion: if
r_{\rm snow}>a_{c}
, we do not expect gas giant formation to be possible.

Surprisingly, we do identify such systems. Analyzing a catalog of
811
circumstellar systems
(
Thebault and Bonanni 2025
)
, among which
393

host gas giants, we found
19
gas giants where the snow line is bigger than
0.8a_{c}
(we conservatively choose a prefactor 0.8 given the uncertainties, and the effects of perturbations even somewhat below the snowline; see
Methods
for a discussion of this; the specific choice does not affect our overall results and conclusions). Figure
1
shows all the
19
systems together with their
a_{\rm c}
and
r_{\rm snow}
.

Finding such a sample of planets that does not follow the standard theory has great potential to test our understanding of planet formation processes, and in particular, the formation of gas giants, and potentially opens the door for new formation channels.

There might be several explanations for this apparent paradox. In-situ sub-snowline formation of gas-giants was suggested to be potentially possible through (i)
Pebble accretion at inner pressure traps
—at the dead-zone inner edge, silicate sublimation front, or magnetospheric cavity—can concentrate drifting solids and accelerate core growth; this pathway predicts giants clustered near trap radii at relatively small 0.1 AU up to 1 AU separations for solar-type stars and metal-enriched envelopes
(
Lambrechts and Johansen 2012
;
Lambrechts and Johansen 2014
;
Bitsch et al. 2015
;
Chatterjee and Tan 2014
)
. (ii)
Reduced runaway thresholds via envelope enrichment and low opacities
allow gas accretion at smaller core masses, favoring metal-rich hosts and atmospheres enriched in refractories relative to volatiles
(
Hori and Ikoma 2011
;
Venturini and Helled 2017
)
. (iii)
Local assembly by mergers of migrating super-Earth cores
can push a merged core over the runaway limit interior to the snow line, often implying nearby companion super-Earths/Neptunes, relatively modest eccentricities, and high core masses
(
Hansen and Murray 2013
;
Boley et al. 2016
)
.
However, most of our identified planets are at larger separations than these models suggest (see Figure
1
), and the systems’ metallicities are consistent with the background population of snowline stable populations (see Figure
7
in
Extended Data
).

Other possibilities exist. A straightforward one is that the snow line is a dynamical property that evolves with stellar luminosity, and at the onset of formation, it was actually located in a stable region. To test this, for each unstable giant, we examined the luminosity of a young single star with the same metallicity and mass, using Modules for Experiments in Stellar Astrophysics
(
Paxton et al. 2011
;
Jermyn et al. 2023
, MESA;)
. Out of
13
systems with measured metallicities and masses, only three had a stable snow line at formation time (around
2\,
Myr, see example in Figure
3
on
Extended Data
); while this may explain some of the systems, it does not provide a robust explanation, nor does it predict the specific locations of these planets (as we discuss below). Another possibility is that the planet formed before the binary system. This scenario is unlikely, as the time scale for binaries to form at wider separations and migrate inwards is usually much shorter than the disk lifetime
(
Generozov et al. 2025
, e.g.)
. A third possibility is that the planet was captured from a different system through exchange. In this case, we would expect to see a significant difference in the eccentricity (and inclinations, but these are usually not available) distribution of this population compared to the general population of gas giants in circumstellar systems. However, we do not see such a difference in the eccentricity distribution between our sample and the general gas giants population (see Figure
7
in
Extended Data
), making this less likely (though the statistics are small).

We can also consider capture in the same system.
Gong and Ji 2018
investigated the capture of a circumbinary planet into a close circumstellar orbit. However, the probability for such capture decreases dramatically for small binary separations, high eccentricity, or high mass ratios. Therefore, while this might be a viable option in extreme cases, it cannot explain the general sample.

Instead, we propose a novel channel for gas-giant planet formation in binaries, which not only can explain their sub-snowline formation, but can also explain and predict the specific system-by-system location of the gas giants. As the companion deposits angular momentum into the protoplanetary disk, the balance between tidal torque
T_{\rm tide}
and viscous torque
T_{\rm visc}
creates a truncation radius
r_{t}
for the disk
(
Artymowicz and Lubow 1994
;
Pichardo et al. 2005
;
Miranda and Lai 2015
)
. If the tidal torque at
r_{t}
is stronger than the viscous torque, we expect a pressure maximum to form (see
Methods
), which may create a trap for dust particles. The formation of a trap is because the drift velocity of dust particles is proportional to the pressure gradient
(
Whipple 1972
;
Weidenschilling 1977
)
, and a local pressure maximum would cause the particles to converge toward this point and remain there
(
Whipple 1972
;
Brauer et al. 2008
)
. Dust traps have indeed been suggested to catalyze planet formation in planetary systems around single stars (see above) and have even been observed by the Atacama Large Millimeter/sub-millimeter Array (ALMA)
(
van der Marel et al. 2013
;
Cazzoletti et al. 2018
;
Stadler et al. 2025
)
. Here, we show that dust traps not only can form through
binary
interaction, but we also provide observational support for their role in gas-giant formation.

Estimation of
r_{t}
is usually done by looking at the innermost Lindblad resonance, which satisfies a gap-opening criterion (
(
Artymowicz and Lubow 1994
)
). However, all our samples have much stronger tidal torque compared to the viscous torque at the innermost resonance with an analytic expression (see
Methods
). Therefore, we used the formulation of
Pichardo et al. 2005
, who studied the effect of periodic gravitational perturbations on test-particle orbits in binary star systems.
They introduced the concept of invariant loops- closed curves that repeat after each binary orbit and serve as the backbone for possible stable particle trajectories. By numerically integrating a large set of test-particle orbits and identifying the outermost non-intersecting invariant loops around each star, they were able to define the maximum extent of stable circumstellar discs. By fitting a power law in
e_{b}
, and
\mu
, they derived an expression for the truncation radius of circumstellar discs:

r_{t}=0.733\;a_{\rm bin}\mathcal{R}_{\rm RL}(q)(1-e_{b})^{1.20}\mu^{0.07},

(2)

where

\mathcal{R}_{\rm RL}(q)=\frac{0.49\,q^{2/3}}{0.6\,q^{2/3}+\ln(1+q^{1/3})}

(3)

is the Eggleton Roche-lobe factor
(
Eggleton 1983
)
and
q\!=\!m_{2}/m_{1}
.
The resulting pressure trap affects not only the formation of the planet but also its migration. For gas giants, the migration rate is given by the radial drift velocity of the gas due to viscous evolution
(
Nelson et al. 2000
)
. Since radial drift is negligible in the trap, we assume no significant migration for this formation channel, as long as the trap exists. We also assume that the disk has a small inclination, as this might affect the truncation radius (see e.g.
Miranda and Lai 2015
). These, albeit simplified assumptions, enable us to predict the position of a planet conditioned on the binary parameters. As we will see later, this can also be used in the opposite direction: if we have measurements of the planet’s position, we can constrain the binary parameters, assuming the planet was formed through this channel. Another possible use case is an estimation of future planets: Although planet formation has, in general, many unknown parameters, we can predict
r_{t}
for any close binary system and estimate the position of future possible gas giants.

Among the
19
systems in our sample, several unique cases warrant careful consideration.
HD 28192
and
HIP 86221
are part of a triple system
(
Feng et al. 2022
;
Paredes et al. 2021
)
, and the interaction of the third companion with the protoplanetary disk can significantly alter the dynamics.
Gamma Leonis
,
HD 59686
,
HIP 90988
,
HD 145934
, and
nu Octantis
are known to have evolved primary or companion
(
Han et al. 2010
;
Ortiz et al. 2016
;
Jones et al. 2021
;
Feng et al. 2015
;
Cheng et al. 2025
)
. Change in luminosity and mass transfer can have dramatic effects on the stability and formation channels of such systems. In particular, mass transfer can give rise to formation of a second generation protoplanetary disk possibly catalyzing formation of second generation planets and/or growth and migration of pre-existing planets
Perets 2010
;
Perets and Kenyon 2013
;
Stegmann et al. 2025
, and thereby allow gas-giant planet formation in cases where the snowline zone is unstable, as in the case of the evolved binary planet system
Gliese 86

Perets 2010
.
nu Octantis
, for example, is a close retrograde planet in a system consisting of a
K
type primary and a white dwarf secondary. The progenitor red giant lost
\sim 75\%
of its mass
(
Cheng et al. 2025
)
. Indeed,
Cheng et al. 2025
concluded that
nu Octantis
is probably a second-generation planet.
HD 59686
is also a system of
K
type primary and likely a white dwarf companion, with progenitor mass loss of
\sim 70\%
, and was also suggested to be a second-generation planet
(
Ortiz et al. 2016
)
. In total, there are seven systems for which we do not expect the suggested formation channel to hold.
There are two additional systems with no measured eccentricity -
HIP 107773
and
TOI 1736
. They are unstable even when assuming a circular binary orbit. As mentioned before, instead of guessing their eccentricity, we can use their observed location to predict the eccentricity, which could be tested in future surveys.

r_{\rm t}
against the observed semi-major axis (

The fact that we were able to identify and explain the outliers further supports our hypothesis and indicates that these special cases may have formed through different processes, for example, as second-generation planets in systems with an evolved companion. By examining other populations, such as giants with a stable snow line and terrestrial planets with an unstable snow line, we observe that they do not align with our predictions (see Figure

). This discrepancy is expected, as these populations likely follow different formation channels not represented in our sample. There is also a difference in the ratio of gas giants between general and unstable systems: while for the entire sample the ratio of gas giants to terrestrial planets is approximately

(

Those differences suggest that our model introduces a novel formation channel, specific to gas giants in binary systems, which becomes the most probable pathway when formation beyond the snow line is not possible because of the binary companion star. This formation channel might also explain extreme systems like
TOI 4633
, which was suggested to be in a retrograde orbit by
Stegmann et al. 2025

TOI 4633

The only unexplained outlier remaining in our sample is
\tau
Boötis

Justesen and Albrecht 2019

Using the same argument but inferring the binary orbit from the planet orbit, we can now use Eq. (
2
) to predict the eccentricity at formation, for binary systems with unknown eccentricity. For that, we rearrange Eq. (
2
) to get

e_{b}=1-\left(\frac{r_{t}}{0.733\;a_{\rm bin}\mathcal{R}_{\rm RL}(q)\;\mu^{0.07}}\right)^{\frac{1}{1.2}},

(4)

and plug the measured planet semi-major axis as
r_{t}
. Using this, and assuming (somewhat arbitrarily) a
5\%
uncertainty in the binary semi-major axis, we predict for HIP 107773 an eccentricity of
0.85\pm 0.01
, and for TOI 1736 an eccentricity of

. Those predictions can be verified in future observations, unless the binary eccentricity changes substantially due to environmental effects after planet formation, such as flybys from ambient stars or tides exerted by the host environment.

We conclude that the sample of gas giants with unstable snow lines must have been formed through a different formation process, and our explanation of dust trapping close to the truncation radius aligns very well with the observed positions. Moreover, this model enables, for the first time, as far as we know, the provision of system-by-system predictions of gas-giant planet positions. Alternatively, using the observed planet position enables the prediction of binary properties.
Planet formation theory and modeled processes are notoriously difficult to verify observationally, given the short timescales involved, and the challenges in probing the early stages of planet formation with high-resolution observations. The study of snowline-unstable gas-giant planetary systems in binary systems provides a unique probe into these processes, allowing for observationally testing the physical mechanisms and processes suggested in planet formation theory, and even serves as a quantitative system-by-system laboratory for planet formation predictions.

Methods

Sample selection

First, we estimate the snow line for each system in the catalog. For that, we assume an irradiated disk where the temperature profile can be expressed as

\displaystyle\frac{T(r)}{T_{\odot}(1\,\text{AU})}=\left(\frac{L}{L_{\odot}}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\left(\frac{r}{1\,\text{AU}}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}},

(5)

which, when plugging in the condensation temperature, gives the snow line distance as

\displaystyle r_{\rm snow}\approx 2.7\left(\frac{L}{L_{\odot}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\text{AU}.

(6)

This result is consistent with the positions of gas giants in our solar system. Next, we calculated the critical distance for stability,
a_{\rm c}
, using Eq. (
1
). Next, we define a gas giant as a planet with
M_{p}\geq 0.15\;M_{\rm Jup}
and an ’unstable gas giant’ as a gas giant with
r_{\rm snow}\geq 0.8\;a_{\rm c}
. Out of the
811
samples in the catalog,
393
are gas giants.
19
samples are unstable, according to the above-mentioned criterion, with
17
systems having measured eccentricity (
31
out of the total
393
giants). The factor of
0.8
is to take into account the fact that both
a_{c}
and
r_{\rm snow}
should have some width, but its value is somewhat arbitrary; using different values slightly changes the sample size, but not the overall results. For example, taking
0.7
or
0.9
results in
20
or
15
samples, respectively. Interestingly, the latter choice of
0.9
mostly excludes the outliers in the full sample, though, as discussed in the main text, their origins are likely different

We note that retrograde orbits are more stable than coplanar orbits with a typical factor of up to
2
(see
Perets 2026
and references therein). Although we assumed coplanar orbits, we added in Figure
1
a line at
2a_{\rm c}
, for completeness.

Pressure maximum

For a geometrically thin disk, and neglecting self-gravity, we can write a steady-state balance between tidal torque and viscous torque as
(
Lynden-Bell and Pringle 1974
;
Kanagawa et al. 2015
)

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}r}\!\left[\dot{M}\,\ell\;-\;2\pi r^{3}\nu\Sigma\frac{{\rm d}\Omega}{{\rm d}r}\right]\;+\;\frac{{\rm d}T_{\rm tid}}{{\rm d}r}\;=\;0

(7)

In steady-state, the mass flux around
r_{t}
is negligible (
\dot{M}\sim 0
). Using this simplified assumption, we can express the viscous torque in terms of a torque density. Thus, we have

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}r}\!\left[\;-\;2\pi r^{3}\nu\Sigma\frac{{\rm d}\Omega}{{\rm d}r}\right]\;=2\pi r\Sigma\Lambda(r)\;

(8)

Which for a Keplerian disk (
\Omega_{p}\propto r^{-\frac{3}{2}}
), reads

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}r}(3\pi\nu\Sigma r^{2}\Omega)\;=\;2\pi r\Sigma\Lambda(r).

(9)

Dividing by
3\pi\nu r^{2}\Omega
we get

\frac{d\ln(\nu\Sigma r^{2}\Omega_{K})}{d\ln r}\;=\;\frac{2}{3}\,\frac{\Lambda}{\nu\,\Omega_{K}},

(10)

And the density profile is

\frac{d\ln\Sigma}{d\ln r}\;=\;\frac{2}{3}\,\frac{\Lambda}{\nu\,\Omega_{K}}\;-\;\frac{d\ln\nu}{d\ln r}\;-\;\frac{1}{2}\;

(11)

The first term on the RHS expresses the balance between tidal and viscous torque densities. The second term is the viscosity profile. Using the
\alpha
prescription
(
Shakura and Sunyaev 1973
)
we have
\nu\propto c_{s}^{2}/\Omega_{K}\propto r^{3/2-\beta}
, where
\beta
is the radial temperature profile. Thus, we can write

\frac{d\ln\Sigma}{d\ln r}\;=\;\frac{2}{3}\,\frac{\Lambda}{\nu\,\Omega_{K}}\;+\;\beta-2

(12)

To obtainain a pressure maximum, we require
\frac{d\ln P}{d\ln r}=0
, and using
P\propto\Sigma c_{s}\Omega_{k}
we get

\frac{\Lambda}{\nu\,\Omega_{K}}\;=\;\frac{3}{2}\,\left(\frac{7}{2}-\frac{\beta}{2}\right)\;=\;\frac{21-3\beta}{4}

(13)

Since
\beta\leq 1
(e.g.
Chiang and Goldreich 1997
), the RHS is always positive, and we see that for pressure maxima, we must have the tidal torque being stronger than the viscous torque.

Estimating truncation radius

To estimate
r_{t}
, we initially used the gap-opening criteria as derived in
Artymowicz and Lubow 1994
. For a circumstellar disk, the location of the inner Lindblad resonance is given by

R^{m,l}_{\rm LR}=\left(\frac{m-1}{l}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}\mu{{}_{\star}}^{\frac{1}{3}}a,

(14)

where
\mu_{\star}=m_{1}/(m_{1}+m_{2})
, and
a
is the binary semi-major axis. Note that the first nontrivial line is with
m=2
. The truncation radius is defined as the innermost line that satisfies the gap-opening criteria (Eq.
16
in
Artymowicz and Lubow 1994
for the circumstellar case):

\alpha^{\frac{1}{2}}h(r)\leq\left(\frac{a\lvert\phi_{ml}\lvert}{GM}\right)\frac{(\pi m)^{\frac{1}{2}}(m-1)^{\frac{1}{6}}3m}{3\mu^{\frac{2}{3}}_{\star}l^{\frac{2}{3}}},

(15)

Where
\alpha
is the viscosity parameter,
M
is the total mass,
G
is the gravitational constant, and
h(r)
is the scale height which is given by:

h(r)\equiv\frac{H}{r}=\frac{c_{s}}{v_{\rm K}}=\left(\frac{k_{B}T}{\mu m_{p}}\right)^{1/2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{GM_{*}/r}}\simeq 0.033\left(\frac{L_{*}}{L_{\odot}}\right)^{1/8}\left(\frac{M_{*}}{M_{\odot}}\right)^{-1/2}r^{1/4}.

(16)

Another criterion is that the gap-opening time scale would be short relative to the disk lifetime. This is given by

\displaystyle t_{\rm open}\approx(\Delta r)^{2}/\nu,

(17)

where
\nu=\alpha c_{s}H
and
c_{s}
is the sound speed. Using
H=c_{s}/\Omega
, and assuming
\Omega=\frac{2\pi}{P}
is Keplerian, we can get

\displaystyle\frac{t_{\rm open}}{P}\approx\left(\frac{\Delta r}{r}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{2\pi\alpha}\left(\frac{r}{H}\right)^{2}\approx\frac{1}{2\pi\alpha}

(18)

For
\alpha
in the range
0.1-0.001
, we get
t_{\rm open}
is approximately
1P-100P
, which is short compared to the disk lifetime, so we assume this criterion is always met.

We checked the gap-opening criterion for the first two resonances (
m,l=2,3
and
m,l=2,4
), using equations
27,28
from
Artymowicz and Lubow 1994
. The results of our clean sample (after removing higher order systems, evolved systems, and systems with no measured eccentricity) can be seen in Fig.
4
in
Extended Data
. It can be seen that for all the systems,
T^{2,4}_{\rm tide}
is approximately an order of magnitude stronger than
T_{\rm visc}
, which means that the actual disk is smaller than the
(2,4)
line. Since there is no analytic expression for higher resonance orders, we assigned
r_{t}
according to Eq. (
2
). To derive Eq. (
2
),
Pichardo et al. 2005
used test particles and therefore implicitly assumed that viscosity is negligible. Although we cannot accurately calculate the tidal torque at
r_{t}
in Eq. (
2
), this assumption aligns with what we found on calculable resonances.
