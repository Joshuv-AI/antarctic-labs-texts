Assembly and comparative analysis of the complete mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes of the medicinal plant Vincetoxicum Pycnostelma
Authors: Jinlong Han, Beijing Tian, Chenggang Shan, Li Ren, Hongqing Xie, Yun Zhou, Shuang Wu, Fangzhou Zhao, Yanwei Zhu, Yujuan Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVincetoxicum pycnostelma is a plant with pharmaceutical importance in traditional Chinese medicine, because its roots and rhizomes contain abundant anti-tumor and anti-inflammatory active compounds. Although sequencing its organelle genomes is critical to understand the genetic basis of these medicinal traits and advance molecular breeding programs, the mitochondrial genome (mtDNA) of V. pycnostelma has not yet been reported.ResultsHere, we sequenced and assembled the organelle genomes of V. pycnostelma with PacBio and BGI sequencing technologies, and conducted a comprehensive comparative genomic analysis. The complete mtDNA (703,719 bp, GC content 43.98%) encoded 38 protein-coding genes (PCGs), 18 tRNAs, and 3 rRNAs, whereas the chloroplast genome (cpDNA; 159,426 bp, GC content 37.85%) contained 79 PCGs, 29 tRNAs, and 4 rRNAs. Codon usage analysis indicated that both the mtDNA and cpDNA exhibited conserved codon usage preferences. Additionally, both genomes contained abundant microsatellite, tandem, and dispersed repeat sequences, and cpDNA showed a more concentrated distribution of repeats. We identified 59 homologous fragments between the mtDNA and cpDNA of V. pycnostelma, including 16 complete PCGs and 5 complete tRNA genes. Phylogenetic analysis and collinearity assessments demonstrated that V. pycnostelma exhibited the closest genetic affinity to Asclepias syriaca. A comparative analysis of nucleotide diversity and boundary regions in Vincetoxicum chloroplast genomes revealed relatively conserved genomic features alongside highly variable regions.ConclusionsOur findings provide essential technical support for molecular breeding of V. pycnostelma and rapid identification of its medicinal materials, which may facilitate sustainable industrial development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12460-6.Keywords: Vincetoxicum pycnostelma, Mitochondrial genome, Chloroplast genome, Genome evolution, Phylogenetics

Background

Vincetoxicum pycnostelma Kitag. (syn. Vincetoxicum bungei, commonly known as Xuchangqing), a medicinal species of the Apocynaceae family, contains bioactive compounds in its roots and rhizomes, and has been widely used in traditional medicine to treat inflammatory and pain-related conditions [1]. Recent studies have isolated more than 150 bioactive compounds from Xuchangqing, including C21 steroids, volatile oils, and benzoindole alkaloids, which exhibit antitumor, antiviral, and neuroprotective properties [1–3]. Despite this pharmacological potential, genomic resources for V. pycnostelma, particularly its organelle and nuclear genomes, are severely lacking. The unresolved phylogenetic relationships within the Vincetoxicum genus and the absence of robust molecular markers for germplasm authentication have further impeded both medicinal exploitation and conservation efforts for this plant. Therefore, understanding the genetic basis of its bioactive compound production and species identity is crucial. Organellar genomes can provide a powerful starting point for such investigations.

Plant organellar genomes (chloroplasts and mitochondria) provide unique insights for the genetic improvement of industrial crops [4, 5]. The chloroplast genome (cpDNA), with its high copy number and transformation efficiency, is a critical platform for metabolic engineering, particularly for producing high-value metabolites and proteins [6]. The mitochondrial genome (mtDNA) regulates cytoplasmic male sterility (CMS), a key trait for hybrid crop breeding [7]. Additionally, codon usage bias, repetitive sequence distribution, and gene transfer patterns in organellar genomes are closely associated with plant ecological adaptation [8–10]. For example, mitochondrial plastid DNA transfers (MTPT) might coordinate energy metabolism through co-evolutionary mechanisms [8, 9]. Recent advances in third-generation sequencing technologies (e.g., PacBio HiFi and Oxford Nanopore) have enabled precise resolution of complex repeats and gene transfer events, thereby providing novel tools for crop genome design [11–13]. Collectively, the analysis and application of plant organellar genomics information can not only elucidate evolutionary history but also provide theoretical foundations and practical tools for genetic improvement and conservation.

Despite the rapid expansion of plant organelle genome research in recent years, because of the complexity of mitogenome assembly, comprehensive determination of mtDNAs for Gentianales and their phylogenetically proximate lineages has markedly lagged and constrained comparative genomic analyses. Currently, whereas more than 100 complete cpDNAs have been reported in the Apocynaceae family in Gentianales [14], mtDNAs have been published for only six species: Asclepias syriaca [15], Cynanchum wilfordii [16], Hancornia speciosa [17], Hoya lithophytica [18], Rhazya stricta [19], and Trachelospermum jasminoides [20]. For the Vincetoxicum genus, the current lack of mitochondrial genome data has limited studies on its evolutionary mechanisms and functional genomics. Although one cpDNA of V. pycnostelma is registered in GenBank (OK271107.1), the structural characteristics and sequence variation in its cpDNA have not been comprehensively analyzed. The absence of comprehensive genomic data has hindered research on population genetics, phylogenetic relationships, and species identification in V. pycnostelma.

Herein, we used PacBio RSII long-read and BGI sequencing technologies to assemble the mtDNA and cpDNA of V. pycnostelma. Subsequently, we extensively analyzed structural characteristics, sequence variations, and gene transfer events within these organellar genomes. Finally, we conducted phylogenetic analysis to determine the evolutionary placement of V. pycnostelma within the Apocynaceae family and to explore its phylogenetic relationships with closely related genera. Our findings enhance genomic understanding of V. pycnostelma and provide a theoretical foundation and methodological framework to support its molecular breeding, metabolic engineering, and industrial development.

Materials and methods

Sequencing and assembly of organelle genomes

Given the highly conserved nature of organellar genomes, we used a single-sample sequencing strategy. A cultivated individual V. pycnostelma plant (line XCQ-01, Fig. 1A) was grown and collected at the perennial medicinal herb germplasm resources base at Shandong Academy of Agricultural Sciences in Jinan, Shandong Province, China (36.98° N, 116.98° E). XCQ-01 was identified by the principal investigator Chenggang Shan (Shandong Academy of Agricultural Sciences, Jinan, China), and its seeds have been deposited in the Crop Germplasm Resources Bank of Shandong Province under accession number Z9U00100. Genomic DNA was extracted from fresh leaves of the V. pycnostelma plant with the CTAB method [21], and DNA quality and quantity were assessed with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer and agarose gel electrophoresis. A SMRT cell sequencing library was constructed with 15–20 kb fragments and sequenced on the PacBio RSII platform at Wuhan Benagen Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Paired-end sequencing (150 bp reads) was performed on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform (BGI, Shenzhen, China). By integrating long-read and short-read data, we assembled the mitochondrial genome with Flye (v2.8.3) [22] and visualized it with Bandage (v0.8.1) [23]. The cpDNA was assembled in ptGAUL software (v1.0.5) with default parameters [24].

Organelle genome annotation

Protein-coding genes (PCGs), tRNAs, and rRNAs in the mtDNA were annotated with a method previously described for Angelica biserrata [25]. The mtDNA annotation was performed in GeSeq software (v2.03) [26], with Arabidopsis thaliana (NC_037304) and Liriodendron tulipifera (NC_021152.1) serving as reference genomes. Splice sites and trans-splicing genes were annotated with the online tool IPMGA (http://www.1kmpg.cn/ipmga/). Transfer RNAs and ribosomal RNAs in the mitochondrial genome were annotated with tRNAscan-SE (v2.0.11) and BLASTN (v2.13.0), respectively [27, 28]. Annotation errors were manually corrected with Apollo software (v1.11.8) [29]. For cpDNA annotation, the CPGAVAS2 tool (v1.0) [30] was used, and the cpDNA map was visualized with OGDRAW (v1.3.1) [31]. The annotation of tRNAs and rRNAs, along with manual correction of annotation errors for the cpDNA, followed the mtDNA annotation pipeline.

Codon usage and repetitive sequence analysis

Protein-coding sequences were extracted from mtDNA and cpDNA with PhyloSuite (v1.1.16) [32]. Codon usage analysis was performed on these PCGs with MEGA (v7.0) [33], and relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) values were calculated. Microsatellites (SSRs) were identified with MISA (v2.1) with the following parameters: 10 for mononucleotides; 5 for dinucleotides; 4 for trinucleotides; and 3 for tetra-, penta-, and hexanucleotide repeats [34]. Tandem repeats were detected with TRF (v4.09) with parameters of 2 7 7 80 10 50 500 -m -f -d [35]. Dispersed repeats (forward, reverse, complementary, or palindromic) were identified with REPuter (parameters: -c -f -p -r -l 30 -h 3 -best 5000) [36].

Analysis of inter-organellar DNA transfer

Homologous fragments between cpDNA and mtDNA were analyzed with BLASTN (v2.13.0) [28], with a focus on sequences longer than 35 bp and those containing complete genes. The Circos package (version 0.69.9) was used to visualize the results related to the MTPT sequences [37].

Phylogenetic tree construction and collinearity analysis

Complete mtDNAs and cpDNAs of 30 angiosperm species (Table S1) were downloaded from the NCBI database. The species selection criteria were as follows: (1) all 11 Gentianales species with published mtDNAs to capture organelle genome structural diversity; (2) one or two representatives per genus from Gentianales’ sister orders Solanales and Lamiales, to balance taxonomic coverage; and (3) two Ericales species as outgroups, with their early divergence from Gentianales used for evolutionary rate calibration. With PhyloSuite (v1.1.16), we identified 26 shared mitochondrial PCGs (atp1, atp4, atp6, atp8, atp9, ccmB, ccmC, ccmFC, ccmFN, cob, cox2, cox3, matR, mttB, nad1, nad2, nad3, nad4, nad4L, nad5, nad6, rpl2, rps3, rps4, rps12, and rps13) between V. pycnostelma and the 30 species, as well as 26 shared chloroplast PCGs (atpA, atpB, atpE, atpF, atpH, clpP, matK, ndhB, rpl14, rpl16, rpl2, rpl20, rpl22, rpl36, rps11, rps14, rps15, rps18, rps19, rps2, rps3, rps4, rps7, rps8, ycf1, and ycf2) [32]. Multiple sequence alignment was performed with MAFFT (v7.505) [38], and maximum likelihood phylogenetic trees were reconstructed with IQ-TREE (v1.6.13) [39] with 1,000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates. The optimal models for mtDNA and cpDNA were GTR + F + I + R2 and GTR + F + I + G4, respectively. Phylogenetic trees were visualized with iTOL (v6) [40]. To further investigate the evolutionary position of V. pycnostelma within the Vincetoxicum genus, we constructed a separate phylogeny of 12 Vincetoxicum species on the basis of 26 shared chloroplast PCGs, with the same method used for the phylogenetic reconstruction of chloroplast genomes from the aforementioned 31 species.

Comparative genomic analysis

Homologous sequences (> 500 bp) between the mtDNA and cpDNA of six Apocynaceae species were identified with BLASTN (v2.13.0) [28]. Collinear regions were analyzed with MCscanX [41] and visualized with Mauve [42]. Nucleotide diversity (Pi) across cpDNAs of 12 Vincetoxicum species was calculated with DnaSP (v6.0) [43], with a sliding window approach (window length: 500 bp; step size: 120 bp). Sequence similarity and boundary regions of four Vincetoxicum cpDNAs were analyzed with mVISTA (Shuffle-LAGAN mode) [44] and CPJSdraw [45], respectively.

Results

Characteristics of the organelle genomes of V. pycnostelma

Through PacBio sequencing, a total of 5,132,274 high-quality reads were generated, covering 91.62 Gb in total, with an average length of 17,865 bp and an N50 length of 17,835 bp. BGI sequencing produced 323,261,044 clean reads, with an average Q30 value of 96.88% and a GC content of 38.99%. The preliminary assembly of the V. pycnostelma mtDNA, derived from long-read sequencing data, comprised 14 nodes (Fig. 1B). After removing redundant regions according to the PacBio HiFi data, we constructed the primary circular contig (ctg1-ctg10_copy-ctg3-ctg14-ctg6-ctg11-ctg12-ctg4-ctg11_copy-ctg12_copy-ctg7-ctg14_copy-ctg2-ctg13-ctg5-ctg11_copy_copy-ctg8-ctg10-ctg9-ctg13_copy), which spanned 703,719 bp and had 43.98% GC content (Fig. 1B). A comprehensive map of the V. pycnostelma mtDNA was generated through detailed assembly and annotation (Fig. 1C). The mtDNA was annotated to include 38 PCGs, comprising 24 mitochondrial core genes and 14 non-core genes, along with 18 tRNA genes (five with multiple copies) and three rRNA genes (Fig. 1C and Table S2). The PCGs covered 34,620 bp (4.92% of the mtDNA), whereas non-coding regions accounted for > 94% (Table S3). Introns were identified in 11 genes: ccmFC, cox1, cox2, rpl2, rps3, rps10, nad1, nad2, nad4, nad5, and nad7 (Table S4).

The assembled cpDNA of V. pycnostelma exhibited a quadripartite circular structure comprising 159,426 bp with a 37.85% GC content (Fig. 1D). The highest GC content was observed in the inverted repeat (IR) regions (90,486–115,390 bp and 134,522–159,426 bp) (43.30%), followed by the large single copy (LSC) region (1–90,485 bp, 36.11%), and the small single copy (SSC) region (115,391–134,521 bp, 31.94%). The cpDNA was annotated with 112 genes, including 79 PCGs (seven multi-copy), 29 tRNA genes (eight multi-copy), and four rRNA genes (all multi-copy) (Fig. 1D and Table S2). PCGs spanned 78,990 bp (49.55% of the cpDNA), whereas tRNA and rRNA genes accounted for 1.84% and 5.68%, respectively (Table S3). Twelve PCGs contained introns and exons, most of which contained one intron and two exons, whereas clpP and ycf3 contained two introns and three exons (Table S4).

Codon usage analysis of PCGs

Codon preference was analyzed for 38 mitochondrial and 79 chloroplast PCGs (Fig. 2 and Table S5). Codons with RSCU > 1 were preferred for amino acid encoding. Both organelles showed 30 codons with RSCU > 1, thus indicating conserved codon usage preferences. For example, the termination codon UAA and alanine codon GCU were preferred in mitochondrial PCGs (RSCU = 1.63; Fig. 2A). In chloroplast PCGs, leucine strongly favored UUA (RSCU = 1.91), whereas alanine preferred GCU (RSCU = 1.75; Fig. 2B). UAA was the dominant termination codon in both organelles. High-frequency codons (RSCU > 1) predominantly had A/U at the third position (96.7%), whereas most low-frequency codons (RSCU < 1) contained G/C at the third position (90.3%). This pronounced A/U-ending preference in synonymous codon usage probably represents a combined effect of mutational bias and natural selection for translational efficiency within the organellar genetic systems.

Analysis of codon usage preference in the mitochondrial (A) and chloroplast (B) genomes of V. pycnostelma

Repetitive sequences in organelle genomes

Repetitive sequences in the mtDNA and cpDNA of V. pycnostelma were extensively characterized (Fig. 3A). We identified 169 SSRs in mtDNA and 86 SSRs in cpDNA (Fig. 3B, Table S6). Tetrameric SSRs predominated in mtDNA (30.2%), whereas monomeric SSRs were most abundant in cpDNA (61.6%). Tandem repeats in mtDNA (29 repeats; 5–41 bp, > 76% similarity) and cpDNA (108 repeats; 7–144 bp, > 69% similarity) were cataloged (Fig. 3C, Table S7). Dispersed repeats included 456 pairs in mtDNA (203 palindromic, 251 forward, and 2 reverse) and 311 pairs in cpDNA (73 palindromic, 223 forward, and 15 reverse) (Fig. 3C, Table S8). The repeat distribution in cpDNA was concentrated: 113 pairs were identified between 130 and 140 kb, and 53 pairs were identified between 60 and 65 kb (Fig. 3A). No complementary repeats were detected in either genome. The abundance and distribution of these repetitive sequences, particularly the concentrations in specific cpDNA regions, highlighted their potential roles in promoting genomic rearrangements and contributing to the dynamic evolution of organelle genomes.

Homologous sequence analysis of organelle genomes

Fifty-nine homologous fragments (56,814 bp, 8.07% of mtDNA) were identified between mtDNA and cpDNA (Fig. 4, Table S9). These MTPTs ranged from 30 to 5,168 bp, and ten exceeded 2,000 bp. The fragments included 16 PCGs (atpB, atpE, petL, petN, psbE, psbF, psbH, psbN, psbT, rbcL, rpl2, rpl23, rpl36, rps11, rps14, and ycf4) and five tRNA genes (trnfM-CAU, trnH-GUG, trnM-CAU, trnN-GUU, and trnW-CCA), thereby indicating prevalent chloroplast-to-mitochondrion gene transfer. No mitochondrial-origin PCGs were found in cpDNA.

Phylogenetic analysis

Analysis of 40 mitochondrial and 80 chloroplast PCGs across V. pycnostelma and 30 species revealed conserved core genes (Tables S10, S11). V. pycnostelma mtDNA lacked rps2 and rps14, whereas cpDNA lacked ycf15. Phylogenetic trees were constructed with 26 shared mitochondrial PCGs and 26 shared chloroplast PCGs, respectively (Tables S10, S11; Fig. 5). Both trees aligned with Angiosperm Phylogeny Group classification, placing V. pycnostelma in a subclade with Apocynaceae species (Asclepias syriaca, Hoya lithophytica, Trachelospermum jasminoides, Rhazya stricta, and Hancornia speciosa). The genetic affinity was strongest with A. syriaca and weakest with H. speciosa. Mitochondrial phylogenetic analysis suggested that Gentiana straminea (Gentianaceae) was closely related to the Rubiaceae family; however, the chloroplast analysis supported its placement within the Apocynaceae family. This incongruence in phylogenetic signals between organellar genomes highlights distinct evolutionary trajectories, which might potentially be attributable to factors such as incomplete lineage sorting, differences in organellar evolutionary rates, or ancient hybridization/introgression events.

Phylogenetic relationships among Apocynaceae species. Panels (A) and (B) show phylogenetic trees based on genes shared by mitochondria and chloroplasts, respectively

Furthermore, we constructed a phylogenetic tree for 12 species within the Vincetoxicum family by using A. syriaca as the outgroup. Clustering analysis revealed that these species segregated primarily into three distinct clades (Fig. 6). Notably, V. pycnostelma, V. junzifengense, V. versicolor, and V. atratum exhibited a relatively close genetic relationship.

Collinearity analysis of organelle genomes in Apocynaceae

Mitochondrial collinearity analysis of six Apocynaceae species identified numerous short homologous blocks (Fig. 7A and Table S11). Collinearity analysis findings aligned with those from the phylogenetic analysis and demonstrated notably larger collinear segments in more closely related species. The collinearity proportions between each pair of mtDNAs are detailed in Supplementary Table S12. The collinearity between the mtDNAs of V. pycnostelma and H. lithophytica accounted for approximately 66% of the V. pycnostelma genome, whereas that between V. pycnostelma and A. syriaca covered 64% of the V. pycnostelma genome. Specifically, V. pycnostelma shared more than 20 collinear mitochondrial DNA blocks longer than 5,000 bp with A. syriaca and 21 such blocks with H. lithophytica (Table S11). Additionally, certain unique regions in the mitochondrial DNA of V. pycnostelma were found to have no genetic resemblance to other species (Fig. 7A). Substantial sequence rearrangement was indicated by the different configurations of collinear blocks in six Apocynaceae mtDNAs (Fig. 7A). In contrast, the collinearity analysis of cpDNAs revealed no clear gene rearrangements among the cpDNAs of these six species, thereby indicating that their cpDNAs are relatively conserved and exhibit high similarity (Fig. 7B).

Comparative analysis of nucleotide diversity and boundary regions in Vincetoxicum cpDNAs

Sliding window analysis of 12 Vincetoxicum cpDNAs identified two hypervariable regions (rbcL-accD, ycf1; Pi > 0.02), thus indicating genomic conservation (Fig. 8A). LSC and SSC regions showed higher Pi values than IR regions. Sequence similarity analysis of four species (V. pycnostelma, V. junzifengense, V. versicolor, and V. atratum) revealed high cpDNA conservation, with greater conservation of IR regions than LSC/SSC regions (Fig. 8B). Certain regions with high variability could be used to identify V. pycnostelma species, such as rbcL-accD, trnS-GCU-trnG-UCC, ndhC-trnM-CAU, ycf4-cemA, rpl16, and ycf1. The four species showed variations in the lengths of the LSC, SSC, and IR regions, such that V. pycnostelma exhibited a shorter SSC region than the other three species (Fig. 8C). Genes such as rps19, rpl2, ycf1, ndhF, trnN, and trnH were found near the IR and SC boundaries (Fig. 8C). An 842 bp segment of the ycf1 gene in all four species extended into the IRa region. Furthermore, in V. pycnostelma and V. versicolor, the ycf1 gene comprised a 46 bp segment located in the SSC region and an 842 bp segment situated in the IRb region (Fig. 8C). IR expansion/contraction was evident across species.

Discussion

The mtDNA and cpDNA of V. pycnostelma were found to exhibit distinct evolutionary trajectories and structural features reflecting both lineage-specific adaptations and conserved organellar genomic principles. Our comprehensive analysis not only clarified the genomic architecture of this medicinally important species but also provided critical insights into molecular breeding and the dynamic interplay between organellar evolution and phylogenetic relationships.

Genomic architecture and its implications in molecular breeding

V. pycnostelma mtDNA (703,719 bp) was found to comprise a remarkably high proportion of non-coding regions (94%) and 456 dispersed repeats (≥ 30 bp), with forward repeats dominating (55%, 251 pairs), in agreement with observations in other plant species such as Vigna reflexo-pilosa [46]. This structural plasticity exceeded that reported in most medicinal plants, such as Astragalus membranaceus [47], and was characterized by a high density of dispersed repeats, which are known drivers of mitochondrial genomic rearrangement. Frequent recombination events in plant mitochondrial genomes generate chimeric open reading frames associated with CMS. Mitochondrially targeted TALEN (mitoTALEN) editing technology has shown substantial potential for organellar genome editing, and has successfully been used to validate the function of CMS-associated open reading frames in mitochondrial genomes of male-sterile lines in Oryza sativa, Brassica rapa, and Solanum lycopersicum [48–50]. Notably, the nad1 and nad5 genes, which are critical for respiratory chain function, contain complex intron structures (Table S4) that might serve as recombination hotspots. These features suggest that V. pycnostelma mitochondria might potentially be engineered to develop CMS lines through targeted disruption of repeat-associated regions with the mitoTALEN system, thereby enabling hybrid breeding strategies to enhance biomass and bioactive compound yields. The V. pycnostelma cpDNA (159,426 bp) displayed a conserved quadripartite architecture but contained hypervariable regions (ycf1 and rbcL-accD, Pi > 0.02) that outperformed conventional barcodes in species discrimination (Fig. 8A). This finding aligns with recent advances in Panax ginseng authentication [51] and directly addresses the pressing need for reliable identification tools in the herbal market, in which adulteration with morphologically similar Vincetoxicum species remains widespread.

Interorganellar gene transfer and functional crosstalk

Interorganellar gene transfer has been crucial in shaping cellular function and complexity. Fifty-nine MTPT fragments (56,814 bp total) were identified in the V. pycnostelma mitochondrial genome, including 16 protein-coding genes (e.g., psbE, rbcL) and five tRNA genes (e.g., trnM-CAU). Their length distribution (30–5,168 bp) resembled that in Panax notoginseng but exhibited greater gene diversity [52]. This phenomenon, which is widespread in angiosperms, might reflect the selective retention of chloroplast-derived genes that enhance mitochondrial function or regulatory networks [5, 19, 53]. For example, rbcL (RuBisCO large subunit) transfer might potentially influence photorespiratory pathways, although experimental validation is required to confirm functional integration. The absence of mitochondrially derived genes in the chloroplast genome underscores the asymmetry of interorganellar DNA transfer, potentially because of stricter functional constraints in chloroplasts or differential DNA repair mechanisms. The prevalence of MTPTs prompts intriguing questions regarding organellar crosstalk, wherein transferred sequences might mediate redox signaling or metabolic coordination between mitochondria and chloroplasts [54]. For instance, chloroplast-derived psbE (a photosystem II component) in mitochondria could potentially modulate retrograde signaling under stress and consequently influence secondary metabolite biosynthesis. Such interactions warrant exploration in V. pycnostelma, given its rich repertoire of bioactive compounds.

Phylogenetic discordance and evolutionary insights

Phylogenetic reconstructions based on mtDNA and cpDNA yielded congruent placement of V. pycnostelma within the Apocynaceae clade but revealed conflicting topologies for outgroup taxa (e.g., Gentiana straminea). Mitochondrial trees positioned G. straminea closer to Rubiaceae, whereas chloroplast trees aligned it with Apocynaceae, thereby mirroring conflicts observed in Pinus phylogenies [55]. This discordance is likely to arise from differential evolutionary rates: mitochondrial genomes, with high recombination rates and lax selection on non-coding regions, tend to accumulate homoplastic mutations that obscure deep phylogenetic signals. In contrast, chloroplast genomes, constrained by conserved gene content and structure, provide more reliable markers for resolving higher-order relationships, in agreement with bamboo phylogenies [56].

To assess the effects of such evolutionary dynamics at the genus level, we further examined the phylogenetic relationships within Vincetoxicum. The chloroplast-based clustering of V. pycnostelma with V. junzifengense, V. versicolor, and V. atratum contrasted with findings from prior studies [57], possibly because of sequencing artifacts or incomplete lineage sorting. To further investigate this issue, we conducted a comparative sequence analysis, which revealed that the chloroplast genome of V. pycnostelma assembled in this study (PV747498) was 1,600 bp longer than the registered V. pycnostelma chloroplast genome (OK271107.1) in NCBI, with which it shared 98.81% overall sequence similarity. Notably, the 99.78% similarity between the registered sequence (OK271107.1) and V. mongolicum (ON854661.1) exceeded that observed between the registered sequence and our assembly. This discrepancy might suggest possible misidentification of the relevant sequences in NCBI or previously underrecognized cryptic speciation. Therefore, the use of multi-locus datasets for rigorous taxonomic validation is particularly important in phylogenetic studies.

Conclusions

Herein, we successfully assembled and annotated the complete mitochondrial (703,719 bp) and chloroplast (159,426 bp) genomes of V. pycnostelma by using PacBio and BGI sequencing technologies. The mtDNA demonstrated extensive dynamic evolution: non-coding regions comprised 94% of the genome and contained 456 dispersed repeat pairs, thus suggesting recombination-driven genetic diversity. In contrast, although the cpDNA was highly conserved, hypervariable regions (ycf1 and rbcL-accD) exhibited notable polymorphism and therefore might potentially serve as effective molecular markers for species identification. Furthermore, 59 MTPTs were identified in the mitochondrial genome, thus highlighting interorganellar gene transfer as a functional mechanism. This study addressed the genetic data gap in V. pycnostelma organelle genomes through comprehensive analysis and provides novel methods that may enable its industrial advancement through species identification, molecular breeding, and quality control.
