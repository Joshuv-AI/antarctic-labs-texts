The professional experiences of male nurses in a healthcare setting of East Africa: the effort to be seen by society and gain professional identity
Authors: Rahma Yusuf Haji Mohamud, Derya Suluhan, Ecem Koyun, Nur Adam Mohamed, Ahmed Isak Ali, Ifrah Adan Hilowle
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeMale nurses around the world face numerous challenges within healthcare settings. Therefore, this study aimed to explore the experiences of Somali male nurses in relation to professional identity, social acceptance, and working life.Patients and methodsA descriptive qualitative research was employed to explore the experiences of male nurses. The study included 15 male nurses working in Mogadishu, East Africa, with nursing experience ranging from one to ten years. Data were analyzed using thematic analysis method. The study was reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research.ResultsThe majority of the participants were ≤ 30 years of age (73.3%). The four main themes and thirteen sub-themes were identified. The four main themes were “professional identity”, “cultural norms”, “coping with challenges” and “increasing interest being a male nurse”.ConclusionThe findings suggest that the experiences of male nurses and societal perceptions of their roles exhibit notable similarities across different regions of the world. Nursing education administrators, healthcare policymakers, and hospital managers should implement targeted strategies to reduce and ultimately eliminate the barriers faced by male nurses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-025-04241-z.

Background

Societal perceptions of male nurses are complex and multifaceted. The fact that the nursing profession has been predominantly practiced by women since its early time may negatively affect society’s perspective [1, 2]. The perception of nursing as a female profession leads male nurses to face social prejudices and various disadvantages [2–4]. In the systematic review and meta-synthesis conducted by Wong et al., it was reported that nursing is perceived as a feminized profession, male nurses are exposed to discriminatory behaviors in the workplace, and male nurses are often stereotyped based on expectations of ‘physical strength.’ Despite these challenges, male nurses also reported having positive experiences [5]. In clinical and educational settings, male nurses are at risk of social exclusion and oppression, and may face discrimination [6].

The World Health Organization reports that nursing, the largest occupational group in the health sector, accounts for about 59% of health professions. According to this report, nursing still remains a gendered profession, with approximately 90% of the nursing workforce being made up of women [7]. The percentages of female/male nurse staff by WHO region are 76/24 in Africa, 87/13 in the Americas, 89/11 in the South East, 89/11 in Europe, 78/22 in the Eastern Mediterranean, and 95/5 in the Western Pacific.

Factors affecting male nurses’ motivation levels for the profession have been identified in the literature as both supportive and unsupportive. Supportive factors include career development opportunities and job satisfaction [8, 9], salary [9], perceiving the profession as a means of self-achievement [8, 9], organizational commitment [8], the desire to help people [10], social support, career opportunities [10, 11], having a relative working in the health field [4], a supportive team culture [4, 11–13], increasing numbers of male nurses [10], male mentors and role models [4], and emotional resilience [11]. These are identified as leading determinants of the intention to stay in the profession for male nurses. Conversely, cultural influences, gender-based stereotypes such as “perception of nursing as women’s work” [4], workload [4], being single [8], role conflict [8], working in a medium-sized city [8], the strange feeling of being in the minority in education [10], discriminatory behavior in the workplace [5], gender-based labeling, such as the expectation of “physical strength” and [4], low wages [4, 12], negatively affect male nurses’ intention to choose and stay in the profession. Most existing research on men in nursing was conducted in high-income countries, where cultural values, educational backgrounds, and societal stereotypes shape the professional experiences of male nurses [13]. This study serves as a pioneering contribution for Somalia in Africa, a low-resource setting. In the literature, there is no research on Somali male nurses’ perceptions of the profession, career motivations and experiences related to social roles. In Somalia, the nursing profession has historically been seen as a female-dominated field and the representation of male nurses in this field is quite limited. This study aimed to examine the experiences of Somali male nurses regarding professional identity, social acceptance and working life. Thus, the results of the study will provide important contributions to the development of health policies that can positively affect the intention of male nurses to stay in the profession in educational and health institutions in Somalia.

Methods

Study design

A descriptive qualitative research design was used in this study utilising a non-probability, convenience method of sampling. Qualitative research design allows researchers to engage closely with the data and to obtain direct descriptions of events or phenomena that are not yet well understood [14, 15]. The study was reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) [16].

Participants and recruitment

Participants were recruited between June and July 2025 using purposive sampling in a tertiary healthcare facility in Somalia, East Africa. Purposive sampling was chosen to ensure the inclusion of information-rich participants who met the predefined criteria, as recommended for qualitative study designs [17]. At the time of recruitment, the hospital employed 411 nurses (283 female, 68.9%, 128 male, 31.1%).

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) being a male nurse, (2) graduating from a nursing program, and (3) having experience in direct clinical care. Nurses who were on administrative leave (n = 1) or not involved in patient care during the study period (n = 3) were excluded. Data saturation was reached after the 15th interview, when no new information, codes, or themes emerged. Subsequent interviews confirmed the redundancy of the data [18].

A total of fifteen nurses met the eligibility criteria and provided informed consent to participate. The participants represented a range of clinical areas, levels of professional experience, and demographic backgrounds, thereby supporting the diversity expected in purposive sampling for qualitative research.

Data collection

Data were collected through face-to-face, in-depth individual interviews conducted by a trained researcher experienced in qualitative methods. This approach was selected to explore the participants’ perspectives and lived experiences within the Somali healthcare system [17]. A semi-structured interview guide, developed by the research team, was used during the interviews and is provided as Supplementary File 1. The first three interviews served as a pilot to evaluate the clarity and relevance of the questions. These interviews were retained in the final dataset because no substantial modifications were required.

Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. With participants’ permission, interviews were audio-recorded, and field notes were taken to document contextual information. Recordings were transcribed verbatim for analysis. Data saturation was reached after the 15th interview, when successive interviews yielded no new insights or emergent themes, indicating that additional data would not contribute meaningfully to the findings.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the Mogadishu Somali-Türkiye Recep Tayyip Erdoğan Training and Research Hospital (approval number: MSTH/22043, Date: 04/06/2025). All procedures adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants were informed verbally and in writing about the study objectives, confidentiality measures, and their right to withdraw at any stage without any adverse consequences. Written informed consent was obtained prior to data collection.

Data analysis

Data analysis was done using Braun and Clarke’s six-phase inductive thematic analysis approach: (1) familiarization with the data, (2) generating initial codes, (3) searching for themes, (4) reviewing themes, (5) defining and naming themes, and (6) producing the report [19]. During the first three phases, three researchers (RYHM, DS, and EK) independently examined the audio recordings and transcripts, recording notes in Microsoft Word™ and Excel™. In the fourth phase, the initially identified themes were jointly reviewed by the same researchers. Subsequently, all team members met to refine and reorganize the themes until consensus was achieved, after which final theme names were established. Participant quotations were numbered and linked to their corresponding data sources. The analysis was conducted manually using printed transcripts, without the aid of qualitative data analysis software.

Trustworthiness

To enhance the rigor and trustworthiness of the study, five qualitative criteria were applied: credibility, dependability, confirmability, authenticity, and transferability [20]. Credibility was supported through the use of purposive sampling to ensure the collection of relevant and contextually appropriate data. All co-authors were involved in the analysis process and conducted cross-checks to strengthen dependability and confirmability. Authenticity was enhanced by incorporating direct participant quotations to illustrate and substantiate key findings. To support transferability, detailed descriptions of the study context, participant characteristics, and the phenomenon of interest were provided.

Their professional experience ranged from 1 to 10 years, with the majority (n = 10) having worked in the field for 6–10 years. Detailed demographic characteristics are presented in Table 1. The analysis of participants’ perspectives on professional identity, societal acceptance, and work life revealed four main themes and thirteen sub-themes (see Fig. 1).

Theme 1: professional identity

The first theme reflects participants’ views that both professional roles and personal characteristics shape their engagement with the nursing profession. This theme comprises two sub-themes: (1) Professional roles, and (2) Overlapping personal characteristics with the profession.

Professional roles

In this sub-theme, participants characterized nursing as a multifaceted profession that requires the assumption of diverse roles. The most frequently emphasized roles among male nurses were helping and caregiving (n = 8), followed by advocacy (n = 5) and communication and collaboration (n = 4). Participants underscored their responsibility to safeguard patients’ rights and promote equity in healthcare delivery.

Being a nurse involves providing care, advocating for patients, and ensuring their recovery and safety (P1).I view myself as a caregiver, advocate, educator, and a bridge between the patient and the healthcare system.

Being a nurse involves providing care, advocating for patients, and ensuring their recovery and safety (P1).

I view myself as a caregiver, advocate, educator, and a bridge between the patient and the healthcare system.

Overlapping personal characteristics with profession

Participants noted that success and effectiveness in nursing are closely linked to the alignment between personal attributes and the fundamental values of the profession (n = 6). They emphasized that qualities such as compassion, empathy, integrity, and mercy are deeply embedded in their professional roles. The personal values motivated them to pursue a nursing career.

I am a kind and compassionate person. I have always felt a strong desire to support others during difficult times. The nursing profession has enabled me to transform this passion into a career and has given me the opportunity to make a meaningful difference in people’s lives (P8).

I am a kind and compassionate person. I have always felt a strong desire to support others during difficult times. The nursing profession has enabled me to transform this passion into a career and has given me the opportunity to make a meaningful difference in people’s lives (P8).

The close relationship between personal values and professional identity was highlighted by the partcipants.

I consider nursing to be part of my personal identity. I take genuine pride in helping those in need, and this profession has profoundly shaped my personality, attitude, and motivation (P4).

I consider nursing to be part of my personal identity. I take genuine pride in helping those in need, and this profession has profoundly shaped my personality, attitude, and motivation (P4).

Theme 2: cultural norms

The majority of participants reported that cultural norms strongly shaped their experiences in the nursing profession. They described encountering both cultural and structural barriers arising from the societal perception of nursing as a predominantly female occupation. These challenges included being viewed by the public as inappropriate or unsuitable for the profession, facing instances in which female patients rejected their care, and confronting widespread beliefs that nursing is inherently a woman’s role.

Gendered work

The participants were faced with the perception that nursing is traditionally considered a profession for women in Somali society.

In Somalia, nursing is traditionally viewed as a female profession. Therefore, some people may find it unusual or even inappropriate for men to work as nurses. (P10)

In Somalia, nursing is traditionally viewed as a female profession. Therefore, some people may find it unusual or even inappropriate for men to work as nurses. (P10)

The participants also described their experiences of being restricted in the care process due to female patients preferring to receive care only from female nurses, which created challenges in fulfilling their professional roles.

Some patients prefer to speak with a doctor rather than accept me as a nurse. Female patients often prefer female nurses. Some express discomfort because I am a man, and this sometimes makes it difficult for me to carry out my professional responsibilities (P14).

Some patients prefer to speak with a doctor rather than accept me as a nurse. Female patients often prefer female nurses. Some express discomfort because I am a man, and this sometimes makes it difficult for me to carry out my professional responsibilities (P14).

The participants disscused core nursing values including compassion, empathy, and emotional sensitivity that are commonly perceived by society as inherently feminine attributes.

Some people believe that male nurses cannot be as compassionate as female nurses, as if such qualities belong exclusively to women (P7).

Stereotyped

Some participants reported frequently encountering widespread stereotypes about the nursing profession in society, which contributed to prejudice regarding their professional identity. Several participants noted that men are more commonly associated with the role of a doctor rather than that of a nurse, following:

There is a perception in society that men become doctors and women become nurses. When we say we are male nurses, people are surprised and find it strange. (P7)In Somali culture, men are more often seen in the role of a doctor. When I tell people I’m a nurse, many are surprised as if I have chosen the wrong profession. (P3)

Changes to public perception of male nurses over time

Five participants reported that the gender-based barriers traditionally associated with nursing have gradually diminished over time. They noted that their family, friends, and colleagues had increasingly come to value their professional role.

I have observed a growing acceptance of male nurses in Somali society over time. (P5) At first, some people were surprised because nursing is usually seen as a female profession. But over time, my family, friends, and colleagues have respected and appreciated my role. (P10)

I have observed a growing acceptance of male nurses in Somali society over time. (P5)

At first, some people were surprised because nursing is usually seen as a female profession. But over time, my family, friends, and colleagues have respected and appreciated my role. (P10)

Stigmazed

Two participants stated that being a male nurse in Somalia is perceived negatively and, in some cases, considered a source of shame. They explained that, while female nurses are generally accepted and respected, men who enter the profession frequently encounter social exclusion and condescension.

One participant noted, “In our culture, it is often considered shameful for a man to be a nurse. While female nurses are respected, male nurses are looked down upon” (P2).

Another participant similarly stated, “Sometimes I feel disrespected because nursing is seen as a profession for women. When people find out I am a nurse, I often receive condescending looks” (P3).

Theme 3: coping with challenges

Participants described employing a range of coping strategies to manage the challenges they encountered in their professional lives. These strategies included drawing on supportive social environments, utilizing personal strengths, seeking mentorship, and job satisfaction.

Supportive social environment

Some participants stated that the support they received from their families and close social circles served as a key resource in helping them cope with difficulties. As one participant explained, “My family’s support was the greatest source of motivation for me to continue in this profession.” (P2).

Another participant similarly noted, “My family, friends, and colleagues respected and appreciated my profession. They always offered me encouraging words.” (P10).

Utilizing personal strengths

The experiences shared by the participants revealed that personal characteristics played a determining role in coping with the challenges they faced in their professional lives. Three prominent traits emerged: fulfilling professional responsibilities regardless of external judgments, accepting difficulties as they are, and actively asserting their professional identity.

Four participants reported that they continued to carry out their nursing duties with professionalism despite societal criticism and negative perceptions of the role. One participant described how he maintained his professional stance against societal perceptions as follows:

If I cared about what others think of what I do, I would have quit this job a long time ago. I carry out my nursing duties without being concerned about opinions of people. (P1)

If I cared about what others think of what I do, I would have quit this job a long time ago. I carry out my nursing duties without being concerned about opinions of people. (P1)

Seeking mentorship

Most participants emphasized the value of working with experienced colleagues for guidance and support when dealing with challenges. They stated that mentoring is not limited to simply transferring knowledge. Moreover, it also provides emotional support that builds self-confidence, supports professional development, and strengthens intentions to remain in the profession. One participant reported:

My colleagues not only shared knowledge but also guided me, encouraging both my professional and personal development. This support increased my self-confidence and significantly contributed to my self-improvement. (P6)

The sense of fulfillment derived from contributing to recovery of patients, receiving their gratitude, and receiving spiritually meaningful responses such as prayers was identified by five participants as a significant source of motivation. Theme 4: increasing interest in being a male nurse

The theme “Increasing interest in being a male nurse” addresses participants’ suggestions for enhancing male participation in the nursing profession within Somali culture. The analysis yielded three sub-themes: internal motivation, extrinsic motivation, and breaking gender stereotypes.

Internal motivation

Internal motivation refers to the personal drive that encourages individuals to pursue nursing for intrinsic rewards such as personal growth, professional satisfaction, and the desire to help others. Five participants emphasized that the increasing interest of men in nursing is primarily influenced by the meaning they attribute to the profession and their intrinsic desire to help others. One participant emphasized that being male does not pose a barrier to choosing nursing and highlighted the importance of the willingness to help others:

Being a man is not an obstacle to becoming a nurse. If you truly want to help people from the heart, you should choose this profession. Don’t listen to what others say—this is your profession. (P4)

Being a man is not an obstacle to becoming a nurse. If you truly want to help people from the heart, you should choose this profession. Don’t listen to what others say—this is your profession. (P4)

Similarly, another participant shared:

For me, this is not just a job. It is about helping others and making a difference in their lives. Being a man doesn’t change my intention. If you are compassionate, empathetic, and love to help others, nursing is your path. (P8)

For me, this is not just a job. It is about helping others and making a difference in their lives. Being a man doesn’t change my intention. If you are compassionate, empathetic, and love to help others, nursing is your path. (P8)

Extrinsic motivation

Extrinsic motivation refers to the influence of external factors that encourage individuals to pursue or continue a profession, such as financial incentives, career advancement opportunities, recognition, or educational support. Some participants emphasized the role of external factors in increasing male engagement in the nursing profession. Notably, improving salaries and enhancing educational support emerged as key elements that both motivate individuals and support their retention in the profession.

One participant highlighted the critical importance of financial incentives due to the burden of societal roles and the demands of the profession:

Here, men are seen as the primary breadwinners in the family. While carrying that responsibility, we also bear the heavy burden of the profession. To increase the number of male nurses in this field, salary improvements are essential. (P14)

Here, men are seen as the primary breadwinners in the family. While carrying that responsibility, we also bear the heavy burden of the profession. To increase the number of male nurses in this field, salary improvements are essential. (P14)

Another participant emphasized the importance of educational support among external factors, suggesting that guidance, encouragement, and the creation of supportive environments in schools and hospitals could enhance both participation and retention:

Guiding and encouraging young men, and creating supportive environments in educational institutions and hospitals, can help more men join the nursing profession and remain in it. (P8)

Guiding and encouraging young men, and creating supportive environments in educational institutions and hospitals, can help more men join the nursing profession and remain in it. (P8)

Breaking gender streotypes

The majority of participants emphasized that dismantling gender stereotypes is a key solution to increasing male interest in the nursing profession. They asserted that the perception of nursing as a profession exclusively for women must be challenged, and that male nurses should take on more visible and active roles to reshape this societal belief. Furthermore, participants suggested that the nursing profession should be promoted in a more inclusive and egalitarian manner both in the community and within healthcare institutions. They highlighted the influence of social and cultural norms on career choices and stressed the need to counter negative perceptions.

In our society, nursing is often seen as a woman’s job. For the profession to grow and for more men to choose nursing, raising societal awareness is essential. The public needs to see that both men and women can perform this job. (P1)Society must realize that men can perform the nursing role just as successfully and effectively as women. Without raising public awareness on this issue, many men may be afraid to pursue this profession. (P5)

Discussion

This study demonstrates the influence of cultural norms, societal expectations, and self-professional identity development on the professional experiences of male nurses in Somalia. The similarity of the findings in this study with the findings of studies conducted in China [9, 11, 21], Jordan [2], South Korea [4, 8, 13], Lithuania [10], United Arab Emirates and New Zealand [22] proves that gender-related role tensions and concerns about visibility in the profession are a universal situation for the nursing profession.

Despite encountering cultural stereotypes and limited societal acceptance, participants adopted adaptive strategies such as continuing education and peer support to manage these pressures. Furthermore, the increasing interest of men to enter the nursing profession indicates a shift in gender dynamics within the profession and highlights the need for gender-sensitive workforce planning in Somalia.

Professional identity

In this study, the adoption of both primary nursing roles such as caregiver, advocate, consultant, communicator, and collaborator as well as secondary nursing roles was found to influence the participants’ career choices. In the study conducted by Saleh et al., which aimed to explore the needs and challenges experienced by Jordanian male nurses, some participants emphasized the importance of communication and caring skills in supporting their interactions with patients [2]. Similarly, a study conducted in China that highlighted the caregiving role reported that male nurses perceived their caregiving skills to be comparable to those of their female colleagues, and that they received positive feedback from patients regarding their communication abilities and approaches to patient care [23]. In this study, the fact that participants did not confine themselves solely to the fundamental caregiving role but also embraced consultancy, advocacy, and communication roles reveals that they adopt a professional approach to the nursing profession.

Altruism, defined as the desire to help and care for the public, to do something useful in society, and to make a difference, is the most frequently cited reason for wanting to join a health profession [24]. However, male nurses could experience psychological conflict between their caring instinct and the traditional masculine traits [25]. Similar to Shudifat et al., participants chose nursing not only for recognition and appreciation but also to help and care for those in need, indicating that emotional labor is compatible with the personality traits of male nurses in this study [13].

Cultural norms

In various societies, gendered work, stereotyped assumptions, and stigmatizing attitudes towards nursing remain prevalent in many contexts [13, 26]. Literature indicates that male nurses frequently experience discrimination from patients, female colleagues, family members, and peers due to gender-based stereotypes [1, 3, 21]. Male nurses working in female-dominated professions often encounter various challenges, including social exclusion within the workplace, lack of support, and concerns regarding visibility [13, 27, 28]. One study reported that male nurses were occasionally marginalized by their female colleagues and faced discriminatory attitudes in professional settings [29]. Similarly, Chen’s study demonstrated that many male nurses and nursing students were subjected to ridicule and discriminatory behaviors [21]. Similarly, Meadus’s study revealed in Canada that male nursing students encounter gender prejudice across educational settings, clinical practice, and broader societal perceptions, with prevailing beliefs that nursing is not an appropriate career choice for men [30]. In China, perceptions of nursing among male nurses tend to be more negative, a situation influenced by recruitment practices, gender-related biases within educational environments, and broader societal understandings of the profession [31]. The findings highlight that societal expectations towards male nurses in Somalia are consistent with those reported in studies conducted in other countries. In addition, participants noted that men in the nursing profession feared being labeled as “unmanly” or being socially excluded. This is in line with existing evidence showing that individuals who enter non-traditional occupations are subject to social devaluation and ridicule due to gender-based assumptions [32–34]. Participants in this study also reported that some members of the Somali community regarded being a male nurse as shameful.

In line with evolving perceptions of the nursing profession, Payne [35] suggested a shift in public attitudes, increasingly recognizing nurses as well-educated, diligent, and autonomous professionals. According to a study by Chen et al., a male nurse noted that patients’ attitudes improved once they saw he could perform nursing duties as effectively as female nurses, or even better. They emphasized that promoting public awareness of the nursing profession is more important than receiving formal respect [23]. In this study, two participants stated that individuals in their social circles and the broader community are aware of the nursing shortage, and that attitudes towards male nurses have recently begun to shift in a more positive direction to help meet healthcare needs. However, since only a small number of participants expressed this view, it may be considered a limitation to the generalizability of this finding.

Coping with challenges

A supportive social environment may strengthen the professional motivation of male nurses. Family members and peers play an influential role in young people’s career choices [36, 37]. A systematic review highlighted that the perceptions, encouragement, and recommendations of relatives, friends, or peers who possess prior knowledge or experience related to the profession influence men’s decisions to pursue nursing [36–38]. Consistent with these findings, the present study demonstrates that positive feedback from family and friends enhanced participants’ self-confidence and strengthened their willingness to choose nursing as a career. Social support may facilitate men’s entry into a profession traditionally dominated by women.

Male nurses expressed that they fulfill their nursing duty without caring about the negativity coming from others and the difficulties that they faced in the profession. Similarly, in a study of Chou et al., a male nurse resolved to continue with his nursing education [36] in spite of feelings of discomfort, embarrassment, and social stigma when talking about the profession with others. In a study conducted by Rajacich, a male nurse described the fast-paced environment, exposure to life-and-death situations, and challenging conditions as stimulating factors that drew him to the nursing profession [39].

Harding reported that a common theme through most of the narratives of the men was that nursing provided fulfilment through greater work satisfaction in line with our study result [40]. Salary in nursing has a favorable outcome for the person [41]. In a study of Yang et al., a male nurse stated one day he noticed a statement in the newspaper which said, ‘Male nurses earn as much as NT$ 50,000 per month’, so he put ‘nursing department’ down on his form as his choice [42].

Providing male role models and supervisors during clinical practice experiences may help challenge and dismantle certain stereotypes associated with nursing [38].

Similar to our findings, male nursing students tended to feel isolated and excluded from an academic and clinical perspective [29]. A supportive environment can foster male nurses’ caregiving skills and boost their motivation, contributing to job satisfaction and retention [23, 38].

Increasing interest in becoming a male nurse

Salary was identified as one of the most common reasons for choosing a career in nursing, particularly among male nurses [43, 44]. Previous studies indicate that men tend to place greater emphasis on salary when considering a career in nursing [13, 45]. For example, Rajapaksa found that men were more likely than women to cite better pay as a reason for leaving the nursing profession [46], while da Silva discovered that male nurses often chose to leave hospital environments in pursuit of higher wages [12]. These findings are consistent those of the present study [23, 43, 45, 46], highlighting the significant role of financial considerations in shaping the participation of male nurses’ in the profession.

Male nurses utilize various strategies to improve their knowledge and skills to attain higher levels of satisfaction and enhanced opportunities for promotion such as choosing specialty areas [42]. Our study indicates that the suggestions by the participants for increasing interest in becoming a male nurse are to develop educational programs for resilience in nursing, establish social support systems such as mentors, counsellor nurses, support groups which is consistent with previous studies [4, 5, 11].

It is recommended that nursing educators foster gender equality in the learning environment, support male students in building professional identity by emphasizing their strengths, and promote peer support by student-led groups [36]. The nursing educators, especiale male nurses as a mentor, have a key role for male nursing students to stay in nursing [47]. In a study of Whittock, lack of career advice for young men in relation to the nursing profession were reported [37]. Thus, there is a need for more male nurse educators [47]. Increasing the number of postgraduate nursing programs in Somalia may be beneficial for expanding the number of male nurse educators and supporting their professional development. Nursing educators should encourage male students to value themselves and rethink their perceptions of the male nursing role [36].

The World Health Organization recommends that countries engage in deliberate planning for gender-sensitive nursing workforce policies [7]. The studies by Cude and Chou revealed that male nurses aimed to eliminate gender-based differences in the workplace and preferred to focus on professionalism in their practice [36, 48]. Breaking gender stereotypes, a striking finding of our study, confirms these results. Male nurses in this study suggested that the number of male nurses should be increased and that society should raise awareness about the roles, contributions, and professional capabilities of male nurses. Media communications play a role in improving the acceptance of male nurses by both patients and coworkers, as they help question and reshape traditional gender norms in the workplace [49].

Limitation

The small sample size and the single healthcare setting limit the generalizability of the findings, as the reported experiences may not fully represent the views and experiences of all male nurses in Somalia. Additionally, although purposive sampling is commonly used in qualitative research, it may introduce self-selection bias, as individuals who are more willing to share their experiences are more likely to participate. Considering the cultural sensitivity surrounding gender-related issues in Somalia, social desirability bias may also have influenced participants’ responses. Nevertheless, rigorous data collection and analysis procedures were followed to enhance the credibility of the findings. In this regard, future studies in Somalia that examine the perspectives and attitudes of the society and females towards male nurses may provide a more comprehensive understanding of this issue.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the perceptions and lived experiences of Somali male nurses reflect broader global patterns, indicating that gender related challenges in nursing transcend cultural boundaries. Addressing these challenges requires coordinated efforts from nursing education leaders and policymakers to advance gender inclusive approaches that improve public awareness, counter persistent stereotypes, and strengthen support for men entering and remaining in nursing. Strategies such as increasing the visibility of male nurses in media campaigns, incorporating gender-sensitive content into nursing curricula, and involving nursing students in school-based outreach may help encourage greater male participation. Additionally, fostering supportive work environments and expanding mentorship opportunities can further enhance retention. Collectively, these measures have the potential to improve gender diversity within the nursing workforce and contribute to a more resilient and sustainable healthcare system in Somalia.
