Quercetin’s regulation of glucose and lipid metabolism in gestational diabetes mellitus: role of the PCSK9/LDLR axis
Authors: Letong Hong, Shuting Xia, Niankun Chen, Zilian Wang, Zhuyu Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study explored the ability of quercetin to improve glucose and lipid metabolism disorders induced by a high-fat diet (HFD) in a mouse model of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) on the basis of the PCSK9/LDLR axis and its potential molecular mechanisms. For the animal experiments, pregnant C57BL/6J mice were randomly divided into three groups: the control group (CD group), which was fed a standard diet; the model group (HFD group), which was fed a HFD; and the quercetin (CAS: 117–39–5) intervention group (HQ group), which was fed a HFD and given 75 mg·kg− 1·day− 1 quercetin by gavage. Moreover, we created an in vitro GDM hepatocyte model in which BNL CL.2 cells were cultured in high glucose and treated with 16 µM quercetin. The results showed that quercetin significantly improved glucose tolerance in GDM mice, reduced serum total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) levels. It also alleviated hepatic steatosis and pancreatic islet cell hypertrophy. Additionally, it improved the abnormal weights of the placenta and fetus. In in vitro experiments, quercetin regulated the PCSK9/LDLR axis and activated the PI3K/AKT/GSK3β signaling pathway, increasing glucose uptake in liver cells. Molecular docking experiments confirmed that quercetin could directly occupy the PCSK9 catalytic pocket, with binding energies of -9.056 kcal mol− 1 for PCSK9 (human), -9. 193 kcal mol− 1 for PCSK9(mouse) and − 9.1 kcal mol− 1 for EGF-A interface of LDLR, which would sterically hinder LDLR interaction. This study revealed that quercetin improves glucose and lipid metabolism disorders in a GDM model by regulating the PCSK9/LDLR axis and the PI3K/AKT/GSK3β pathway. Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), defined as glucose intolerance first recognized during pregnancy, is the leading metabolic complication of gestation and imposes acute and long-term risks on mothers and fetuses [1]. By 2021, the global prevalence of GDM had reached 14% and is anticipated to rise steadily [2]. Its precise pathogenesis remains incompletely understood and is caused primarily by hormonal imbalances, persistent insulin resistance (IR), and β-cell dysfunction resulting from physiological alterations during pregnancy [3]. Lipid dysregulation precedes and amplifies this process, with most patients exhibiting lipid metabolism disorders prior to diagnosis [4, 5].

The low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR), a cell membrane receptor that binds LDL-C and lowers blood lipid levels, is an essential component of lipid metabolism [6]. PCSK9, a serum protease from hepatic cells, degrades LDLR [7]. As a key cholesterol metabolism regulator, PCSK9 is a target for treating hyperlipidemia [8, 9] and modulates β-cell secretion [10]. Given that GDM is closely linked to liver lipid metabolism [11, 12] and that circulating PCSK9 level is linked to the risk of GDM in Chinese people [13], we speculate that the balance between LDLR and PCSK9 might be tied to insulin resistance in GDM. Although monoclonal PCSK9 inhibitors (Alirocumab, Evolocumab) are effective for hyperlipidemia, they are costly and have side effects [14, 15], prompting the search for pregnancy-safe, natural alternatives. Quercetin is one of the most abundant dietary flavonoids, ubiquitously present in fruits (e.g., capers, grapes, berries), vegetables (e.g., onions, broccoli, kale), herbs (e.g., dill, fennel) and other common dietary sources [16]. The highest known natural source of quercetin is found in capers, which contain 1.6–960 mg/100 g FW [16]. Beyond the classical antioxidant and anti-inflammatory roles, quercetin and its derivatives can also modulates glucose and lipid homeostasis by enhancing insulin-stimulated glucose uptake, activating AMPK, targeting SREBP-1c and PPARα and inhibiting key enzymes of hepatic lipogenesis [17–19]. These pleiotropic actions have prompted investigations into quercetin as a low-cost, plant-derived candidate for metabolic diseases, including GDM.

Few studies have addressed quercetin’s effects on GDM. Animal experiments have shown that pre-pregnancy quercetin lowers maternal blood glucose in diabetic mice, improves embryonic morphology, and increases embryo number, possibly via IGF1 receptor regulation and Wnt-β-catenin activation [20]. Quercetin also improves blood glucose and plasma insulin levels in offspring of high-fat diet-induced overweight or hyperlipidemic pregnant mice [21, 22], likely by alleviating endoplasmic reticulum stress in the liver and adipose tissue. However, its regulation of the PCSK9/LDLR axis during pregnancy and its impact on GDM lipid–glucose metabolism remains unclear.

Therefore, this study examines whether pregnancy-long quercetin (75 mg kg⁻¹) ameliorates GDM-related dysglycemia and dyslipidemia by acting as a modulator of the PCSK9–LDLR axis, thereby offering preclinical evidence for a pregnancy-compatible, orally administered PCSK9-targeting strategy for GDM.

Materials and methods

Animals and diets

5- to 8-week-old C57BL/6J mice (female: male = 2:1) were obtained from the Experimental Animal Center of Sun Yat-sen University (SCXK (Yue) 2021–0029). They were housed under a 12 h light/dark cycle with free access to water and a standard chow diet (CD, 11.8% kcal from fat; D12450B, Beijing KaiGuo). The animal experiment was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Sun Yat-sen University (approval NO. 2023003264).

After one week of adaptive feeding, the mice were randomized into chow diet (CD) or high-fat diet (HFD) groups. The HFD group received a high-fat diet (45% kcal from fat; D12492, Beijing KaiGuo). Body weight was recorded weekly. After six weeks, the mice were mated (female: male = 2:1). Two days before mating, fasting blood glucose (FBG) was measured via a glucometer (Sinocare GA-3) and corresponding test strips via tail-vein blood sampling. After mating, the mice were housed individually, with the presence of a vaginal plug indicating GD0.5. During pregnancy, the CD group continued the standard chow diet. The HFD group was divided into two subgroups: the HFD group and the quercetin-treated HFD group (HQ). Quercetin (Sigma Q4951) was prepared as a 2 mg mL⁻¹ suspension in 0.9% NaCl by brief vortex and administered immediately to the HQ group at 75 mg kg⁻¹ day⁻¹ by gavage; an equivalent volume of quercetin-free saline was given as the vehicle control to both the CD and HFD groups, which was administered according to Srinivasan et al. [23].

Glucose and insulin tolerance tests

On gestational day (GD)15.5, for the glucose tolerance test (GTT), the mice were fasted for 12 h and then injected intraperitoneally with 2 g kg⁻¹ body weight of 20% (w/v) glucose in saline. For the insulin tolerance test (ITT) on GD18.5, the mice were fasted for 6 h and injected intraperitoneally with insulin (0.5 IU kg⁻¹). Blood glucose levels were measured from tail blood samples at 0, 15, 30, 60, 90 and 120 min postinjection.

Tissue and blood collection

On GD19.5, the mice were fasted for 6 h and anesthetized with 1% sodium pentobarbital (50 mg kg⁻¹, i.p.). Blood was collected via cardiac puncture under anesthesia. During cesarean section, fetuses, placentas, pancreata, bilateral gonadal white adipose tissue (gWAT), and livers were dissected and weighed. The samples were either snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C or fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining or immunohistochemistry (IHC). The fetal and placental weights of each litter were also recorded.

Measurement of serum lipids, insulin and PCSK9 levels

Blood samples of pregnant mice were obtained from terminal cardiac puncture under anesthesia (GD19.5, 6 h fasting), collected into 1.5 mL microtubes without anticoagulant and processed within 30 min. After clotting at room temperature for 30 min, blood samples were centrifuged at 3,000×g, 4 ℃, 20 min to obtain serum, aliquoted, stored at − 80 ℃ until analysis. Triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TC), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels were measured using commercial enzymatic kits (A110-1-1, A111-1-1 and A113-1-1; Nanjing Jiancheng, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Serum insulin and PCSK9 levels were determined with a mouse insulin ELISA kit (RX202485M, Ruixin Bio, China) and a mouse PCSK9 ELISA kit (EM30424S, Shanghai Weiao, China), respectively. Technicians performing biochemical assays were not blinded to group allocation; however, all samples were run in duplicate on the same 96-well plate to minimize inter-assay variability.

Histological and morphometric analyses

Mouse liver, pancreas, and adipose tissues were fixed in 4% PFA, dehydrated, cleared, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and rehydrated. For routine histology, the sections were stained with H&E. Tissue morphology was observed and recorded under a microscope. Islet area (µm²) and adipocyte cross-sectional area (µm²) were measured via ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, USA). Hepatic neutral lipids were visualized in 10-µm cryosections via Oil Red O (Beyotime C0157S) staining. Images were captured under blinded conditions (investigator conducting image acquisition and quantification was blinded to group allocation) using a light microscope at 400× magnification. Five random fields per liver section were analyzed per animal, and the percentage of Oil Red O-positive area was quantified using ImageJ with a standardized color threshold.

Immunohistochemistry for rabbit anti-INS(1:1000, Proteintech 15848-1-AP), rabbit anti-LDLR (1:500, Proteintech 10785-1-AP) or rabbit anti-PCSK9 (1:300, Proteintech 27882-1-AP) was performed on liver or pancreatic sections after antigen retrieval (citrate buffer, pH 6.0, 95 °C, 20 min). Bound antibodies were detected with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody and DAB substrate. Positive staining density was quantified by an investigator blinded to sample identity by using ImageJ with a standardized threshold.

Hepatic lipid biochemical assay

Exactly 100 mg of frozen liver from GD19.5 dams (n = 5 per group) was weighed, homogenised in 1 mL ice-cold absolute ethanol on an ice-water bath, centrifuged at 2,500 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant aliquoted. TG, TC and FFA were assayed simultaneously using commercial kits (A110-1-1, A111-1-1, A042-2-1; Jiancheng, Nanjing, China); values were normalised to 100 mg wet liver and expressed as nmol per 100 mg tissue.

Western blotting

Tissues were homogenized in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China) and incubated for 30 min on ice. Protein concentrations were measured using the BCA method (Thermo Scientific). Thirty micrograms of protein were separated on a 10% SDS‒PAGE gel (Shanghai Epizyme, China) and transferred to PVDF membranes (0.45 μm, Millipore IPVH00010). After blocking, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against total PI3K (T40115), phospho-PI3K (T40116), total Akt (T55561), phospho-Akt (PC0876), total GSK-3β (MT3006; Abmart, China), phospho-GSK-3β (67558-1-Ig), phospho-IRS1(85238-1-RR), total IRS1(17509-1-AP), LDLR (10785-1-AP), and PCSK9 (27882-1-AP; Proteintech, Wuhan, China). Proteins were visualized via ChemiDoc Touch Imaging System (Bio-Rad), and band intensities were quantified with ImageJ (NIH, USA) and normalized to those of β-actin (20536-1-AP, Proteintech).

Cell culture and treatment

BNL CL.2 cells (ATCC TIB-73; BCRC 60180) were used to simulate a GDM hepatocyte model. Cells were maintained in complete DMEM (DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin) at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator. Following the manufacturer’s instructions, we used the commercial HG/HF kit (KT001, Xi’an Kunchuang Technology Co., China) to prepare HGPA medium: glucose stock (1 mol/L) and palmitate-BSA stock (10 mmol/L) were added to complete DMEM to a final concentration of 45 mM glucose and 0.25 mM palmitate, which served as the in-vitro high-glucose/high-palmitate medium for establishing a GDM hepatocyte model, using the matched vehicle control of the kit for baseline comparisons. Quercetin was prepared as 100 mM stock in DMSO, stored at − 20 °C; 16 µM working solution was prepared in complete medium or HGPA medium (final DMSO ≤ 0.1%). MK-2206 (AmBeed, China) was prepared as a 10 mM stock in DMSO and stored at − 20 °C. For rescue experiments, cells were pre-treated with 1 µM MK-2206 (final DMSO ≤ 0.01%) for 30 min before quercetin addition and maintained throughout the 24 h incubation. Vehicle control received 0.1% DMSO. All the cells were cultured under identical conditions throughout the 24-h treatment period.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed via the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; GOONIE, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, BNL CL.2 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 10³ cells/well in 100 µL of complete medium. After 24 h of incubation (37 °C, 5% CO₂), cells were treated with serially diluted concentrations of quercetin (2 µM to 64 µM) for 24 h. Control wells received equal volumes of vehicle (1% DMSO). Following treatment, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, and plates were incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm via a microplate reader (Agilent BioTek 800TS, Agilent Technologies, USA). Wells containing medium + CCK-8 (no cells) served as blanks.

Cell viability was normalized to that of vehicle-treated controls (100% viability). Dose‒response curves were generated by plotting viability (%) against the compound concentration. The half-maximal effective concentration (EC50) was calculated via nonlinear regression analysis (three-parameter logistic model) in GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). Experiments were performed in triplicate and independently repeated at least three times.

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted via an RNA extraction kit (400–105, GOONIE, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and cDNA was subsequently prepared via a cDNA synthesis kit (P1561, TIANYA BIOTECH, China). We quantified gene expression using quantitative PCR (qPCR) with Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q712-02, Vazyme, China), cDNA and primers (Table 1) on an iQ5 PCR thermal cycler (Bio-Rad). The relative mRNA expression levels were analysed using the 2ΔΔCt method and normalized to the level of Actb.

DiI-LDL flow cytometry

To detect DiI-LDL internalization, after 24 h-interval of quercetin or vehicle control, the cells were washed and incubated with DiI‐LDL (Ex/Em = 554/571nm, YB-0011, Yiyuan Biotechnology, Guangzhou, China) at 10 µg/ml for 4-hour interval. The cells were then detached with trypsin, washed thoroughly, chilled on ice, and analyzed with a flow cytometer (CytoFLEX S, Beckman Coulter, USA). The flow-cytometry data were analysed using FlowJo™ v10.8 Software (BD Life Sciences).

2-NBDG uptake assay

To assess glucose uptake, we used 2-NBDG, a fluorescent glucose analog. After 24 h-interval of quercetin or vehicle control, BNL CL.2 cells were washed twice with PBS and starved in glucose-free DMEM for 1 h and then incubated with 2-NBDG (Ex/Em = 485/535nm, 186689-07−6, APExBIO) in glucose-free DMEM for 30 min. After incubation, cells were washed thoroughly with PBS. Leica DMI8 inverted fluorescence microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany) was used to observe 2-NBDG uptake after quercetin treatment.

For 2-NBDG uptake flow cytometry, cells were detached with trypsin, washed thoroughly and analyzed with a flow cytometer (CytoFLEX, Beckman Coulter, USA). The flow-cytometry data were measured by FlowJo™ v10.8 Software to quantify the effect of quercetin on hepatic glucose uptake.

Molecular docking

To predict the binding mode and affinity between quercetin and PCSK9or EGF-A interface of PCSK9-LDLR complex, the 3D structures of Human PCSK9 (PDB ID: 2P4E), mouse PCSK9 (AlphaFold: Q80W65) and LDLR EGF-A (PDB: 3BPS) were downloaded from the Protein Data Bank (http://www.rcsb.org) and processed using PyMOL (Schrödinger, LLC). to remove water and cofactors. The 3D structure of quercetin was obtained from the PubChem database and converted to PDB or PDBQT formats and energy-minimized using BIOVIA Discovery Studio or AutoDock. Molecular docking was performed with AutoDock Vina 1.2.0 using the AutoDock4.2 force field and Python binding.s. During docking, the protein’s active pocket was set as the center coordinate, and the search space covered the surrounding area. For each system, 20 independent docking runs were performed, and the best pose with the lowest binding energy was selected for analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD. Comparisons among ≥ 3 groups were performed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test; two-way repeated-measures ANOVA was used for GTT and ITT curves. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.3.0 (GraphPad Software, USA).

Results

Maternal quercetin supplementation restores gestational glucose homeostasis

To determine whether chronic quercetin administration mitigates gestational dysglycemia, female C57BL/6J were exposed to HFD for six weeks before conception and subsequently treated with quercetin (75 mg kg⁻¹ day⁻¹, gavage) throughout pregnancy. As anticipated, pre-gravid HFD exposure elevated maternal body weight without altering FBG (Fig. 1A, B) significantly. HFD-induced weight gain was more pronounced postconception, yet quercetin intervention during pregnancy partially attenuated this increase (Fig. 1C).

On GD15.5, GTT revealed pronounced glucose intolerance in the HFD group, as evidenced by elevated 30- and 60-min glucose excursions and an enlargement of the area under the curve (AUC) relative to those in the CD group (P < 0.001; Fig. 1D, E). Quercetin intervention significantly reduced both peak glucose levels and the AUC (P < 0.01 vs. HFD), indicating partial restoration of glucose tolerance. However, ITT on GD18.5 did not show a significant trend toward improved glucose clearance in the HQ group (Fig. 1F, G; P > 0.05).

Concomitantly, HFD increased fetal and placental weights—markers of macrosomia—whereas quercetin normalized these parameters at the litter level (Fig. 1H–J; n = 10 litters per group). These data demonstrated that maternal quercetin supplementation ameliorated HFD-induced gestational glucose intolerance and mitigated adverse fetal outcomes.

Quercetin treatment attenuates HFD-induced dyslipidemia and increases serum PCSK9

We assessed the effects of quercetin treatment on major lipid variables and blood PCSK9 levels in mice. Compared with the CD group, the HFD group exhibited an increase in serum TC (3.32 ± 1.18 vs. 1.69 ± 0.51 mmol L⁻¹, P = 0.0043), and LDL-C followed a similar upward trend, whereas TG levels remained unchanged (Fig. 2A–C). Quercetin treatment (75 mg kg⁻¹ day⁻¹) reversed these increases, restoring TC (1.74 ± 0.68 mmol L⁻¹, P = 0.054 vs. HFD) and LDL-C (0.40 ± 0.21 mmol L⁻¹, P < 0.001 vs. HFD) to CD levels without altering TG.

The absolute liver weight was greater in the HFD group (1484 ± 95.37 vs. 1268 ± 108.3 mg, P < 0.001), and this difference was reversed by quercetin (1343 ± 76.04 mg, P = 0.0303 vs. HFD) (Fig. 2D). The gonadal white adipose tissue (gWAT) mass tended to be lower in the quercetin group (315.7 ± 60.79 mg) than in the HFD group (372.9 ± 106.1 mg, P = 0.4471) but did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 2E).

Moreover, fasting blood glucose on GD19.5 was modestly elevated in the HFD group (P < 0.001) and normalized by quercetin (P = 0.01 vs. HFD) (Fig. 2F). Quercetin treatment improves tissue morphology of main metabolic organs

We used H&E staining to analyze the pathological morphology of major metabolic organs. In liver tissue, the HFD group showed numerous vacuolar lipid droplets and significant steatosis compared with the control group, along with a larger Oil Red O positive area. Quercetin intervention reduced the number of vacuolar lipid droplets, alleviated steatosis, and decreased the Oil Red O staining area (Fig. 3A, B, E), indicating that quercetin can improve HFD-induced hepatic lipid overload. In pancreatic tissue, the HFD group exhibited islet hypertrophy, characterized by enlarged islet cells, reduced nuclear staining density, and cytoplasmic vacuolization. Quercetin treatment improved islet cell morphology, alleviated hypertrophy, and significantly decreased islet size (Fig. 3A, C). In gWAT, the HFD group had enlarged adipocytes with an increased average cross-sectional area compared with the control group, and quercetin treatment resulted in smaller adipocytes (Fig. 3A, D). To biochemically corroborate the observed reduction in hepatic steatosis, we quantified triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TC) and free fatty acid (FFA) levels in GD19.5 livers. Consistent with Oil-Red-O morphometry, HFD respectively increased hepatic TG, TC and FFA to nearly 1.26-, 1.64- and 2.55-fold compared with CD (all P < 0.05). Quercetin intervention reversed the TC and FFA increases (P < 0.01 vs. HFD) without affecting TG (Fig. 3F–H). Thus, quercetin-mediated protection against hepatic lipid overload is confirmed both histologically and biochemically. And these data showed quercetin maintained the histoarchitecture of major metabolic organs under conditions of gestational high-fat diet.

Quercetin-mediated regulation of LDLR and PCSK9 exhibits tissue-specificity

To determine whether quercetin differentially regulates the PCSK9/LDLR axis in metabolically active tissues, we performed immunohistochemistry and immunoblotting on liver and pancreas samples obtained at GD19.5. In the liver, HFD feeding significantly elevated PCSK9 abundance while concomitantly reducing LDLR protein levels (Fig. 4A–C). Quercetin administration reversed these changes, restoring PCSK9 and LDLR expression toward the values observed in the CD group (P < 0.01 vs. HFD). In contrast, the pancreas displayed a compartment-selective regulatory pattern. Using consecutive sections, we performed single-color IHC for insulin (to demarcate islets), PCSK9 and LDLR (Fig. 4A). Morphometric analysis revealed that, under control conditions, acinar cells exhibited higher PCSK9 signal but lower LDLR staining than islet areas; no such gradient was detected in CD mice. Relative to CD, HFD reduced acinar PCSK9 without altering islet PCSK9. Quercetin (HQ) restored and even surpassed PCSK9 levels in both compartments versus HFD. Conversely, HFD raised LDLR expression in both acinar and islet regions; this elevation was completely reversed by quercetin. Western blotting of whole-pancreas lysates (not islet-enriched) corroborated the IHC findings: HFD did not change total PCSK9 but markedly increased LDLR (P < 0.001 vs. CD); quercetin further elevated PCSK9 while normalizing LDLR (P < 0.01 vs. HFD).

Quercetin restores hepatic AKT/GSK3β signaling and enhances glucose uptake in GDM

Although quercetin is known to activate PI3K/AKT signaling and the activation of PI3K/AKT enhances SREBP-mediated LDLR transcription [24–26], whether this mechanism operates in GDM hepatocytes remains undefined. We therefore examined hepatic Akt activity in gestational mice. Western blotting showed that compared with CD, the HFD group presented lower hepatic p-Akt/Akt ratio; quercetin intervention restored Akt phosphorylation (1.049 ± 0.168 vs. 0.7923 ± 0.0847, P = 0.0054, HQ vs. HFD) (Fig. 5A).

We further investigated quercetin’s regulation of IRS1/PI3K/AKT at the cellular level. BNL CL.2 mouse embryonic liver cells were treated with a series of quercetin concentrations. Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8) assay, and the half-maximal effective concentration (EC50) was determined to be 14.83 µM (Fig. 5B). Relative mRNA levels of Ldlr and Pcsk9 under different concentrations were quantified by qPCR, and significant differences were observed at 16 µM, which was used for subsequent experiments (Fig. 5C). Additionally, we determined the half-life of LDLR in BNL CL.2 cells using CHX (50 µg/mL). Western blot results (Supplementary Fig. 1) showed that the LDLR half-life was similar between the quercetin-treated (16 µM) and vehicle-control (≤ 0.1% DMSO) groups. To determine whether quercetin improves glucose metabolism at the cellular level, we assessed 2-NBDG uptake in BNL CL.2 hepatocytes by fluorescence microscopy and flow cytometry. After 24 h, quercetin (16 µM) significantly increased 2-NBDG uptake relative to the control group (P < 0.001), indicating enhanced glucose uptake by hepatocytes (Fig. 5G).

To corroborate the in vivo findings, we exposed BNL CL.2 cells to 25 mM higher D-glucose plus 0.25 mM palmitate (HGPA) as an in vitro surrogate of GDM-related hepatosteatosis. Quercetin (16µM) was applied for 24 h of culture as the pharmacological intervention. Given that quercetin restored hepatic Akt activation in HFD mice and increased glucose uptake in hepatocytes—a process normally dependent on Akt signaling—we added the selective Akt inhibitor MK-2206 (1 µM) to the medium as a rescue experiment to verify whether quercetin’s effects are Akt-dependent. Western blotting and qRT-PCR (Fig. 5D, E) showed that, at the mRNA level, Pcsk9 transcription was increased and Ldlr was decreased in the disease-model (HGPA) group versus control. Quercetin intervention reversed these changes, up-regulating LDLR and down-regulating PCSK9 expression in BNL CL.2 hepatocytes at both mRNA and protein levels (mRNA: Ldlr 1.16 vs. 0.72, Pcsk9 1.54 vs. 2.09; protein: LDLR 1.38 vs. 0.89, PCSK9 0.84 vs. 1.33; HGPA + QUE vs. HGPA, P < 0.001). And following Akt inhibitor MK-2206 treatment, quercetin-induced up-regulation of LDLR at the protein level was reversed (0.80 vs. 1.38, HGPA + QUE + MK-2206 vs. HGPA + QUE, P < 0.001). To verify LDLR expression and LDL-handling capacity at the cellular level, we performed flow-cytometric Dil-LDL uptake assays. Relative to untreated controls, the HGPA model markedly reduced Dil-LDL uptake; quercetin (16 µM) significantly restored this capacity, whereas co-incubation with the Akt inhibitor MK-2206 (1 µM) almost completely abolished the quercetin-mediated increase (Fig. 5F).

We next examined activation of the IRS1/PI3K/AKT/GSK3β axis. Western blotting revealed that, relative to HGPA, quercetin restored the ratios of p-PI3K/PI3K, (Ser473) p-Akt/Akt and (Ser9) p-GSK-3β/GSK-3β(P<0.05).Upon Akt inhibitor MK-2206 treatment, quercetin-induced Akt activation was blocked and the (Ser9) p-GSK-3β/GSK-3β ratio declined (Fig. 5H).

To elucidate a putative direct interaction, we performed in silico docking of quercetin (CAS 117–39–5) into the catalytic domains of human PCSK9 (PDB 2P4E), mouse PCSK9 (AlphaFold Q80W65) and EFG-A interface of PCSK9-LDLR complex (PDB 3BPS). The binding energy, RMSD and residues involved in hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic formation are presented in Fig. 5I; Table 4. Discussion

Our results demonstrated that quercetin can alleviate the HFD-induced gestational diabetes phenotypes, improving glucose tolerance, the histoarchitecture of major metabolic organs and macrosomia markers in HFD-induced GDM mice. Collectively, our data revealed a pregnancy-safe, orally administered flavonoid that simultaneously rectifies maternal dysglycemia and dyslipidemia by modulating hepatic PCSK9/LDLR and PI3K/AKT/GSK3β signaling. In addition to previous observations that quercetin improves embryonic outcome in diabetic pregnancy [20], we showed that a once-daily 75 mg kg⁻¹ dose throughout gestation restores glucose tolerance and lowers LDL-C without altering serum insulin, indicating an insulin-independent mechanism.

PCSK9 is predominantly secreted by liver [27]and drives lysosomal LDLR degradation through PCSK9-LDLR complex formation and subsequent endocytosis, thereby elevating plasma LDL-C levels and disrupting lipid homeostasis [28, 29]. Emerging evidence links circulating PCSK9 levels to GDM susceptibility. Although PCSK9 inhibitors (alirocumab, evolocumab) [14, 15] effectively reduce hypercholesterolemia, their high cost and adverse effects have prompted investigations into natural alkaloids, flavonoids and phenolics as alternative PCSK9-targeted therapies. Quercetin has been shown to suppress PCSK9 and upregulate ABCA1/ABCG1/LXR-α, attenuating atherosclerotic plaques in ApoE⁻/⁻ mice [30] and inhibiting macrophage foam-cell formation [31]. In our study, quercetin ameliorated HFD-induced dyslipidemia and hyper-PCSK9emia in pregnant mice without altering peripheral insulin levels (Figs. 1 and 2), suggesting that PCSK9 inhibition may contribute to this effect. The parallel decrease in LDL-C and PCSK9 implies that quercetin-induced suppression of PCSK9 may facilitate LDLR recycling and consequently enhance LDL-C clearance. Additionally, similar with previous researches [17, 32, 33], we found that quercetin exhibited tissue-selective PCSK9/LDLR regulation, with PCSK9 suppression in the liver being consistent with increased LDL-C clearance (Figs. 2 and 4). Quercetin may thus improve HFD-induced systemic metabolic disorders by enhancing hepatic lipid metabolism. However, whether the opposite regulatory effect of quercetin on pancreas maintains β-cell cholesterol homeostasis, protects islet cell function and increases insulin secretion, as proposed by Marku et al. [10], remains to be determined.

We further explored the pivotal role of the PI3K/AKT/GSK3β pathway in the ability of quercetin to improve glucose metabolism. In the livers of GDM mice and in hepatocyte models induced by high glucose and high palmitate, quercetin restored hepatic Akt-Ser473 phosphorylation and suppressed GSK3β activity (Fig. 5A, H). Previous studies showed that activation of Akt not only enhances glycogen synthesis by promoting the phosphorylation of GSK3β [34], but also improves the glucose uptake capacity of hepatocytes by upregulating the expression of glucose transporters [35], which is consistent with the increased 2-NBDG fluorescence intensity observed in the quercetin group in our present study (Fig. 5G). This finding also corroborates the strong-binding hypothesis between quercetin and Akt1 proposed by recent molecular-docking studies [36, 37], further supporting the notion that quercetin can ameliorate metabolic disorders via Akt signaling. Additionally, the selective Akt inhibitor MK-2206 reversed the quercetin-induced upregulation of LDLR (Fig. 5D, E, F), suggesting that quercetin may restore Akt (Ser473) phosphorylation, thereby enhancing SREBP-mediated LDLR transcription [24–26] and that PI3K/AKT pathway is central to the lipid-modulating effects of quercetin. Additionally, our results revealed another non-mutually exclusive mechanism appear to cooperate: quercetin could directly occupy the PCSK9 catalytic pocket, with binding energies of −9.056 kcal mol⁻¹ for PCSK (human) and − 9.1 kcal mol⁻¹ for EGF-A interface of LDLR, which would sterically hinder LDLR interaction.

Compared with the hepatocyte-centered mechanism described above, recent studies have explored quercetin’s effects in other metabolic cell types. Rendine et al. [38] demonstrated that in 3T3-L1 adipocytes, quercetin metabolites—quercetin-3-glucuronide and isorhamnetin—enhance lipid metabolism primarily through AMPK-mediated ACC phosphorylation. Additionally, a 2025 systematic review on polyphenol mechanisms [39] summarized that quercetin regulates adipocyte lipid and glucose metabolism by activating AKT and AMPK, promoting ACC phosphorylation, inhibiting lipogenic regulators such as SREBP-1c and their target genes, and enhancing fatty acid oxidation through upregulation of Ppara and Acsl1. In contrast, our GDM hepatocyte model highlights a distinct, Akt-centered LDLR recycling pathway. Together, these findings position hepatic PCSK9/LDLR regulation as a complementary, pregnancy-compatible target of quercetin that operates in parallel with the well-established AMPK/adipocyte axis.

Notably, the improvement in placental and fetal weights in GDM mice treated with quercetin (Fig. 1F, I, J) may be associated with its ability to restore maternal metabolic homeostasis. HFD-induced lipotoxicity can increase the risk of obesity and metabolic disorders in offspring, whereas quercetin appears to mitigate these effects on placental function by lowering the circulating TC, LDL-C and hepatic lipid accumulation (Figs. 2A-C and 3A). Importantly, the reduction in Oil-Red-O-positive area was paralleled by significant decreases of hepatic TC and FFA (Fig. 3G–H), validating that quercetin’s histological protection translates into measurable biochemical improvement.

Previous animal experiments have consistently reported that quercetin lowers blood glucose and lipids and ameliorates insulin resistance [40–43]. In our present study, once-daily oral quercetin (75 mg kg⁻¹) restored glucose tolerance and lowered LDL-C without altering circulating insulin in HFD-induced GDM mice, extending prior observations that 30 mg kg⁻¹ quercetin given before conception reduced fasting glucose in streptozotocin-diabetic mice [20]. In contrast, maternal quercetin (100 mg kg⁻¹) administered during mid-gestation failed to significantly improve glycaemia [44], underscoring the importance of dose and treatment window. In terms of human intervention studies, in a randomized clinical trial, quercetin 250 mg d ⁻¹ for 8 weeks improved the antioxidant status of type 2 diabetic patients without significantly affecting blood glucose [45]; but 400 mg, once for 30 min could reduce postprandial hyperglycemia in another RCT [46]. In previous reports, average dietary intake of quercetin in humans is 16–25 mg per day, whereas doses of 500–1,000 mg per day are recommended when the compound is used as a nutritional supplement [47], so extra supplementation beyond normal dietary intake maybe needed. To our knowledge, randomized clinical trials specifically targeting gestational diabetes mellitus have not yet been reported. Quercetin exhibits low oral bioavailability: limited absorption in the human gastrointestinal tract and rapid phase-II metabolism markedly reduce the amount reaching the systemic circulation, resulting in sub-therapeutic circulating levels [48–50]. This pharmacokinetic drawback has likely constrained both the design of clinical trials and the magnitude of therapeutic effects reported to date.

Despite some significant progress in this study, there are still several limitations. First, preclinical animal models allow controlled timing and standardized diets, but also have the limitation: rodent placental kinetics, pregnancy-induced metabolic shifts and especially the quercetin bioavailability differ markedly from the human situation [48–50]. Thus, without physiologically based pharmacokinetic modeling, direct mg kg⁻¹ extrapolation to the clinic remains unreliable. Another limitation is the regulatory mechanisms of the PCSK9/LDLR axis have not been thoroughly elucidated in pancreatic β cells; hence, cell-specific knockout models are needed to clarify their function. Additionally, the molecular docking results only suggest the potential for the quercetin-PCSK9 interaction, and validation by surface plasmon resonance (SPR) or isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) is required to confirm the binding affinity.

Conclusions

Our study revealed that quercetin can improve the disruption of glucose and lipid metabolism in mice with GDM induced by a high-fat diet. These findings support its use as a natural way to regulate PCSK9 and as a potential therapeutic strategy.
