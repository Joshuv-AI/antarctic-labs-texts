Methane Emissions Show Recent Decline but Strong Seasonality in Two US Northeastern Cities
Authors: Anna Karion, Subhomoy Ghosh, Israel Lopez-Coto, Kimberly Mueller, Sharon Gourdji, Joseph Pitt, James Whetstone
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Urban methane emissions estimated using atmospheric observations have been found to exceed estimates derived by using traditional inventory methods in several northeastern US cities. In this work, we leveraged a nearly five-year record of observations from a dense tower network coupled with a newly developed high-resolution emissions map to quantify methane emission rates in Washington, DC, and Baltimore, Maryland. Annual emissions averaged over 2018–2021 were 80.1 [95% CI: 61.2, 98.9] Gg in the Washington, DC urban area and 47.4 [95% CI: 35.9, 58.5] Gg in the Baltimore urban area, with a decreasing trend of approximately 4–5% per year in both cities. We also find wintertime emissions 44% higher than summertime emissions, correlating with natural gas consumption. We further attribute a large fraction of total methane emissions to the natural gas sector using a least-squares regression on our spatially resolved estimates, supporting previous findings that natural gas systems emit the plurality of methane in both cities. This study contributes to the relatively sparse existing knowledge base of urban methane emissions sources and variability, adding to our understanding of how these emissions change in time and providing evidence to support efforts to mitigate natural gas emissions.

Introduction

To mitigate the worst effects of climate change, national governments have launched efforts to reduce their emission of climate-warming greenhouse gases (GHG), including carbon dioxide (CO2) and methane (CH4).1,2 In addition to global and national efforts, state, local, and municipal governments have also pledged to ambitious GHG emissions reduction goals, often relying on inventory estimates of their GHG emissions to track progress.3 Emissions of CH4 may be less economically painful for some industries to mitigate than those of CO2, and CH4 emissions reductions may be able to provide some short-term benefits, reducing atmospheric warming in the near-term until more complete structural changes can be made to mitigate CO2.

However, recent studies have indicated that many sources of urban CH4 to the atmosphere are fugitive (i.e., unintentional) emissions from the natural gas and waste sectors and are underestimated by traditional accounting methods. For example, Washington, DC, and Boston, Massachusetts, were two of the first cities whose streets were sampled using a mobile platform to identify natural gas distribution pipeline leaks,4−6 calling attention to the prevalence of this source of CH4 emissions in cities. Additionally, cities across the US have been the focus of studies quantifying total CH4 emissions using observations from both airborne and stationary platforms,7−15 including several studies in Washington, DC, and Baltimore, Maryland.16−19 These studies relied on atmospheric observations of CH4, generally integrating the contributions of all emission sources together. As such, atmospheric methods are less precise in the spatial allocation of emissions sources than traditional accounting methods but are also less prone to bias and can provide the means for verification of more traditional methods.

A few studies have suggested that postmeter emissions from residential and commercial natural gas consumers may comprise a significant portion of US CH4 emissions.20−22 Several of these measured natural gas leaks and emissions from household appliances, including furnaces, water heaters, and stoves,22−24 or entire single-family homes.21 As a result, the US EPA has incorporated postmeter emissions for the first time in the most recent 2022 Greenhouse Gas Inventory.25 To our knowledge, three top-down studies to date support the hypothesis that postmeter emissions may play an outsized role in US cities by finding higher emissions in winter, when natural gas consumption for heating is greater due to colder weather.8,9,26 A recent study estimated that all natural gas emissions in Los Angeles were related to consumption with a very large wintertime increase, without attributing them to any specific part of the supply chain (e.g., pipelines or postmeter).27

In this study, we continue this line of atmospheric observation-based analyses to estimate CH4 emissions from Washington, DC, and Baltimore, Maryland, for a nearly five-year period from May 2017 through December 2021. Our approach uses a dense network of tower-based CH4 observations along with a custom emissions map within a Bayesian inversion framework to optimize emissions at a spatial resolution of 0.01 degrees. Our posterior results allow for a comparison of the two cities as well as an investigation of the seasonal variability and trends in emissions over these years.

Methods

Domain, Observations, and Background

Our study focuses on the Census-designated urban areas28 of Washington, DC, and Baltimore, MD, two adjacent large metropolitan areas in the US Northeast (Figure 1A). The Washington DC urban area (DC UA), with a population of approximately 4.6 million and a land area of 3423 km2, is significantly larger than the Baltimore urban area (Balt UA), at 2.2 million and 1857 km2 (per the 2010 Census). There are nine landfills in the Balt UA (6 of which are closed), including two large active landfills (Alpha Ridge and Quarantine Road). Within the DC UA, all four landfills are closed. In both UAs, natural gas is widely used for residential and commercial heating. Wetlands in the domain are generally found outside the two UAs in the southeast of the modeling domain in the Eastern Shore area, although there are freshwater reservoirs and rivers (Potomac, Anacostia) and the Baltimore Harbor within the UAs.

We use CH4 observations from the Northeast Corridor tower-based atmospheric concentration observing network from May 2017 to December 2021 in our analysis, with the number of urban stations varying between 6 and 11 through the time period as the network expanded.29,30 CH4 dry air mole fractions (presented here as nmol mol–1) are measured continuously by commercial cavity ring-down analyzers at approximately 0.4 Hz from two different heights on communications towers throughout the area. Here, we use CH4 observations from the tallest level, averaged hourly, as documented in detail by Karion et al.29 Our network was designed specifically to maximize coverage of the Baltimore and Washington, DC urban regions31 with observations from three additional sites near the edges of our modeling domain used to determine the background conditions32 (Figure 1 and Supporting Information (SI) Table S1). We use hourly averaged observations during local afternoon hours, defined as between 5 h after local sunrise and before sunset (SI Figures S1 and S2). Our analysis relies on CH4 enhancements caused by emissions within the modeling domain, around the DC and Balt UAs (Figure 1A). In order to isolate the urban enhancements, the background mole fraction of CH4 entering the domain must be removed from the total CH4 measured at the urban sites. Here, we use two different background determination methods that were found to be unbiased at monthly scales in a previous analysis,33 with details in SI Section S1.

Transport Modeling

We used six different transport model configurations in the analysis. All configurations use the Stochastic Time-Inverted Lagrangian Transport (STILT) model34 to simulate transport and dispersion, driven by meteorological fields from three models: North American Mesoscale (NAM), ECMWF Re-Analysis (ERA 5),35 and the Weather Research and Forecast (WRF) model36 (SI Table S2). STILT was run with and without a near-field correction for each meteorological model (SI Section S2). The footprints provide good coverage over the urban areas for each year (SI Figure S3), with similar coverage in winter (DJF) and summer (JJA) (SI Figure S4). Notably, winter footprints are stronger overall than those of summer, reflecting generally shallower mixing heights in winter.

Prior Emissions Map

As part of our modeling framework, we developed several versions of a custom high-resolution (0.01°, i.e., gridded at approximately 0.86 km by 1.11 km) emissions map based on methods developed by Pitt et al.37 to use as priors (Figure 1B). This emissions map, which is time-invariant, provides the Bayesian inversion system with the best chance of estimating emissions at high resolution. The map covers our geographical modeling domain, bounded by 38.4°N, 39.6°N, 77.8°W, and 76.2°W.

We included nine sectors in our prior emissions map. Broadly, these are natural gas (NG) distribution, NG transmission, landfills, wastewater, composting, mobile and stationary combustion, agriculture (manure management and enteric fermentation), and wetlands (including freshwater features). The NG distribution sector is the largest component of the prior, and itself has two components which are summed: an estimate of emissions using traditional EPA methodology38 with some emission factors also from Weller et al.39 and an estimate of additional loss on residential and commercial annual consumption (0.5–1.5%). Landfill emissions are the second largest sector, with emissions for most derived from the EPA’s greenhouse gas reporting program (GHGRP40). Additional detail on all sectors is provided in SI Section S3, SI Figures S5–S7, and SI Tables S3–S5. A comparison of our priors with the 2012 gridded EPA inventory41 indicates that our priors are higher than the EPA inventory in the urban areas, with generally higher NG emissions and lower landfill emissions (SI Figure S6).

We use five different versions of this prior for our set of inversions (SI Table S6), all based on the initial emissions map but altering the magnitudes of some sectors in each to better assess the impact of plausible choices made and uncertainties in the emission factors. We use three different options for a natural gas (NG) loss rate, as well as one prior with three times the emissions from waste and one with tripled wetlands emissions. Since the priors are constant in time, the seasonality and trend in our posterior are not driven by changes in prior emissions.

Details of the configurations (summarized in SI Table S7) and their impact are found in SI Section S6 and SI Figures S12–S14. Inversions run to test sensitivities to model-data mismatch or number of observations are not included in the final ensemble.

Sectoral Attribution of Emissions

To attribute our emissions totals to different sectors, we first combine the nine sectors from our prior into three groups: Thermogenic (Mobile and Stationary Combustion, NG Transmission, NG Distribution), Biogenic-Waste (Landfills, Wastewater, Compost), and Biogenic-Wetlands&Ag (Wetlands, Agriculture). These three groupings provide maps that are spatially uncorrelated, and we estimate the contribution of each group to our total mean posterior for each season using multiple linear regression to the prior maps of the groups, with no offset term. The regression is performed spatially, i.e., pixel-wise, with the total posterior map being regressed against the three explanatory variables, i.e., the three different maps (one for each grouping). We average posterior emissions for each inversion ensemble member for June, July, and August (JJA) of all years for summer, and for December, January, and February (DJF) for winter. As with the total posterior emissions, after estimating mean emissions from each group for each of the 60 inversions, we average them and use their spread to show confidence intervals with the results. We also calculate the thermogenic fraction as the fraction of total emissions attributable to the thermogenic group, for each of the 60 inversions (and for summer and winter seasons). The resulting estimated fractions are averaged with their distribution used to derive the 95% CI on the mean value. SI Section S8 shows additional details of the method, along with the dependence of the thermogenic fraction on the prior (SI Figure S16).

Analysis of Relationship to Natural Gas Consumption

To estimate natural gas (NG) use in the two UAs, we spatially downscale NG consumption data reported by the local distribution companies43 (LDCs, SI Figure S5) in the region using gridded residential and commercial CO2 emissions from Vulcan 3.044,45 assuming that gas use spatially scales with on-site combustion of natural gas and oil. We downscale temporally to monthly consumption using reported state-level monthly commercial and residential NG consumption for Maryland as a proxy.46 We perform an ordinary least-squares regression between monthly posterior emissions and estimated NG usage in each UA for each inversion ensemble member and report the mean slope and confidence intervals (SI Figure S13 shows the impact of inversion configuration on the slope).

Results and Discussion

Urban Area Totals and Spatial Distribution of Emissions

Aggregating the posterior emission rate of CH4 over the Baltimore urban area (Balt UA) and Washington, DC urban area (DC UA), we report mean emissions for each over the entire period (Figure 2; impact of model configuration in SI Figure S12). Mean posterior estimates are generally consistent with previous estimates for this region,16,17,47 and slightly lower than the City of Baltimore inventory (SI Section S7 and SI Figures S15 and S18). The spatial distribution of posterior emissions and the difference from the NG15 prior indicates an adjustment of emissions downward in the center of DC UA, but upward around downtown Baltimore (Figure 1C). Some areas to the northwest of both UAs in the farther suburbs are also adjusted downward.

A comparison of total emissions, averaged over the entire study period, between the two UAs suggests slightly higher posterior emissions in the Balt UA than in the DC UA per unit area and significantly higher emissions per capita (Figure 2; SI Section S7 for methods). Our priors also contain larger per capita emissions in the Balt UA, with the difference between the two areas dominated by landfill emissions; the Balt UA contains more landfills within its boundary than DC. This example illustrates that emissions comparisons, even after normalizing by area or population, sometimes come down to a specific domain definition. For example, the Brown Station Landfill in Prince George’s County, MD, a suburb of Washington, DC lies one grid cell outside our DC UA, although it serves the DC UA’s population. Thus, a more in-depth examination or interpretation of the drivers of differences in cities’ emissions should include Scope 2 and Scope 3 emissions (i.e., including emissions that occur outside the UA due to activity within the area). Bottom-up inventories conducted by localities and metropolitan areas, including those of Baltimore and Washington, DC, generally include Scope 2 and sometimes Scope 3 out-of-area emissions, and differences in Scope remain one challenge in robustly comparing bottom-up inventories with top-down analyses.48

Seasonality of Emissions

The mean emissions from our inversions indicate distinct seasonality in total CH4 emissions in both cities, with emissions 1.44 times higher in the winter than in the summer (Figure 1D). Spatial differences between the seasons suggest more wintertime emissions in both core downtown areas with the highest density of population, buildings, and natural gas usage. Higher wintertime emissions have been found in previous studies in Boston8 and Los Angeles,9 as well as in the Washington, DC and Baltimore region.18 In those studies, the higher winter emissions were attributed to NG leakage, possibly postmeter. Here, we also find a strong correlation in our monthly posterior emissions estimates with residential and commercial natural gas use in both UAs (R2 = 0.52 in the DC UA and R2 = 0.42 in the Balt UA) (Figure 3). We discuss this relationship further in Section 3.4.

Sectoral Distribution of Emissions

A multivariate linear regression analysis using our customized high-resolution priors separated by sector groupings (Thermogenic, Biogenic-Waste and Biogenic-Wetlands&Ag) provides some understanding of the sources of emissions in the two UAs in winter and summer.

Our results suggest that thermogenic emissions from fossil-fuel combustion and NG losses are higher in winter than in summer, supporting the hypothesis that overall emissions seasonality is driven by the NG sector. Interestingly, waste emissions are also higher in the winter than in the summer in both UAs. This result follows from the spatial allocation of the winter-summer difference (Figure 1D) along with the spatial allocation of NG distribution and landfill emissions in the priors. In this analysis, it is likely that the spatial overlap of large landfills within the same areas with large NG emissions does not allow for this methodology to adequately distinguish between these two sources and results in both NG emissions and landfill emissions being higher in winter. Possible seasonality in landfill emissions is discussed further in Section 3.4. Wetlands and agriculture are slightly higher in summer than in winter in DC, although not significantly so (Figure 4).

Estimated sector-grouped emissions for DC (A) and Balt (B) for the mean of the priors, the posterior mean for winter and summer. (C) Estimated thermogenic fraction of emissions for each UA. Legend in (B) applies to all three panels; error bars represent the 95% CI based on the ensemble of inversions.

We note that these results are necessarily dependent on the spatial allocation and relative magnitudes of these sector groups in the prior, both because of the general posterior dependence on the spatial map of the prior and because we are using linear regression to scale each group, which does not account for posterior changes in the spatial distribution of emissions within a group. Therefore, we emphasize that a high-quality emissions map is crucial for using this method to attribute emissions to sectors.

Given the estimates of the sectoral distribution of posterior emissions, we can determine the thermogenic fraction: the fraction of emissions from thermogenic sectors (NG distribution, NG transmission, mobile combustion, and stationary combustion; Figure 4C). In the Balt UA, the thermogenic fraction is 0.68 [95% CI: 0.59, 0.79] and 0.69 [95% CI: 0.59, 0.78] in summer and winter, respectively; in DC it is 0.73 [95% CI: 0.64, 0.81] and 0.82 [95% CI: 0.76, 0.88]. Baltimore’s fraction is lower than DC’s because there are more landfills within the Balt UA. When calculating this fraction for the entire model domain, the fractions fall to 0.50 [95% CI: 0.40, 0.62] in summer and 0.58 [95% CI: 0.48, 0.70] in winter, as would be expected since in our model most thermogenic emissions occur within the urban centers. We note that although our posterior sectoral distribution is closely correlated with the prior, the thermogenic fraction does change slightly from the prior value (Figures 4C and S16) and varies marginally by season, indicating that our observations are able to inform this analysis beyond our prior attribution.

We did not expressly include emissions from either sewer leaks or wood burning in our prior maps; both sectors are likely to be spatially allocated similarly to NG distribution or postmeter emissions and would thus not be able to be distinguished using our methods. Using literature-based estimates of annual emissions from these two sources (SI Sections S3.3 and S3.5), we calculate that if they were both attributed to biogenic rather than thermogenic sectors, the thermogenic fractions would decrease to 0.68/0.79 (summer/winter) in the DC UA and 0.64/0.66 in the Balt UA, values that are within our uncertainty estimates.

Our estimated thermogenic fractions are generally consistent with previous measurements for these UAs based on ethane/methane ratios, although direct comparisons are difficult given the spatial disparity between the different studies. This suggests that our analysis using a combination of a custom prior and atmospheric observations may inform sectoral attribution without using a cotracer, although not as definitively. Floerchinger et al.26 estimated fractions of 0.26–0.30 and 0.63–0.69 in summer and winter, respectively, over the DC/Balt domain. The winter values correspond well with ours, while their summer estimates are significantly lower than our values, even when we consider the entire domain. We suspect that their summer flight may have had significant river and wetland influence, and it could be that our tower network is not sensitive enough to fluxes outside urban areas, minimizing our estimate of those biogenic emissions (either agricultural or natural). Plant et al.17 also estimated the thermogenic fraction for the Balt UA (0.92) and DC UA (0.80) in spring 2018, values better aligned with our winter estimates for the UAs. In general, direct comparisons of the thermogenic fraction are subject to difficulties due to spatial misalignment. The balance of sources differs between the denser urban areas and the surrounding regions so that the mix of sources observed varies depending on the specific area sampled by an observation.

Relationship between Emissions and Natural Gas Use

Analyzing the monthly posterior emission rate and NG consumption in each UA, we estimate the slope of their relationship, i.e., the emission rate per unit of NG consumed (Figure 3B, D). The slope is slightly greater in the Balt UA (0.9 [95% CI: 0.6, 1.3] %) than it is in DC UA (0.6 [95% CI: 0.4, 0.9] %), indicating a slightly higher rate of emission in Balt than in DC per unit gas consumed, although the overlapping uncertainties on each indicate that this difference is not significant at the 95% CI. For comparison, Sargent et al.8 reported a relationship of 2.1% between NG emissions in Boston and NG use in the state of Massachusetts on a per unit area basis. This rate is not directly comparable to our finding because we have scaled NG use specifically for our UAs, and using state average NG use per unit area would yield a higher percentage. He et al.9 found a slope of 1.4 ± 0.1% total CH4 emissions per unit of residential and commercial NG use in the LA basin, while Zeng et al.27 recently reported 2.8 ± 0.18% using the same methods over a longer time frame. These values are significantly larger than those we find in the DC and Balt UAs. Further investigation of emissions in both cities would be required to determine the source of these large differences, along with an analysis of the differences in infrastructure, at both the LDC and building and appliance level.

We note that the emission rate of 0.6 and 0.9% of NG use does not represent the overall loss rate of NG in the UAs; it represents only the component of emissions that varies according to NG consumption. The intercepts in the relationship between emissions and NG use represent 78–79% of total emissions in each UA, and only 21–22% vary with NG consumption. Given the thermogenic fractions measured by other studies,17,26 additional NG losses that do not directly vary with NG use (e.g., system leaks that exist regardless of usage) also contribute to total emissions. Conversely, it is possible that some other emission source is also higher in winter than in summer and contributes to the seasonal variability, as discussed below.

The second largest contributor to urban emissions in our priors is the landfill sector, and therefore, it is the most likely (after NG distribution) to affect the seasonal variation in overall emissions at such a large scale. While landfill emissions can be seasonal, due to the dependence of CH4 production, diffusion rates, and oxidation rates on soil temperature and moisture,49,50 most inventory methods generally do not assign any seasonality to landfill emissions.41 Variability in landfill emissions to the atmosphere has been found to depend largely on specific practices and operations (such as type of cover material or changes in landfill infrastructure) in addition to local climate and weather, including barometric pressure.51,52 The various emissions drivers compete, and the overall dependence of net emissions on season depends on the specific location and practices at a given landfill. We would advocate for future work to focus on understanding waste sector emissions, but accurate emissions estimates will likely require specific information on the individual landfills in question in order to model emissions and their variability properly.

Other biogenic sectors, such as wastewater (including from sewers) and wetlands, are unlikely to drive higher wintertime emissions. Wastewater and wetland emissions are generally higher in summer due to greater methanogenic activity at warmer temperatures;53−55 any seasonality would show higher summer emissions, so we do not expect these sectors to be contributing to the higher wintertime emissions observed.

We conclude that the most likely source of the higher winter emissions in the DC and Balt UAs is NG-related leakage from the distribution network and related infrastructure and/or postmeter emissions (these could include both leakage within buildings and incomplete combustion). Distribution networks may emit more in winter if pipes are maintained at higher pressures in winter to meet demand, but such pressure data are not publicly available, and we do not have any other evidence of this. While Fischer et al.21 found significant postmeter leakage from quiescent sources (i.e., not directly related to usage), they suggest that transient emissions may also play a large role, as has been found in other studies, leading to higher losses with more frequent usage.22,24 Therefore, we cannot determine definitively to what extent either distribution network or postmeter losses cause the seasonal variability found here.

Interannual Variability and Trend of Emissions

An analysis of the annual average posterior emissions over time indicates a declining trend in both UAs from 2018 to 2021 (Figure 5). We exclude the partial 2017 year from this analysis as the average from May to December is not representative of an annual mean given the strong seasonality we have observed. We calculate the trend for each inversion configuration separately to understand the influence of the configuration on the result (SI Figure S14) and characterize the uncertainties using the spread across the inversion configurations. Our estimates of the decline in each UA are −3.6 [95% CI: −5.2, −1.8] Gg a–1 in DC and −2.8 [95% CI: −3.5, −2.2] Gg a–1 in Balt. These declines represent approximately 4.2 [95% CI: 6.1, 2.1] % and 5.4 [95% CI: 6.8, 4.3] % of 2018 average annual emissions per year in DC and Balt, respectively. In addition, we note here that contrary to emissions of CO2,56 CH4 emissions do not show any observable anomaly during 2020, when the COVID-19 pandemic-induced slowdown in economic activity occurred, although such a signal may have been obscured by other sources of variability.

Given our results correlating emissions to NG use, we also investigate the trend in NG consumption in our two UAs, to determine whether reductions in gas use could explain the decrease in emissions. Considering 2018–2021, residential and commercial NG consumption in the DC and Balt UAs declined by approximately 3 and 6% per year on average, respectively, according to data reported by local distribution companies.43 In Section 3.4, we determined that for every additional unit of CH4 consumed from residential and commercial NG use above the minimum value, total emissions increased by 0.006 and 0.009 (in DC and Baltimore, respectively). Therefore, we would expect that a decrease in NG use would result in a corresponding decline in emissions. Other losses from NG systems that are not seasonally varying (such as distribution pipeline leaks, for example) are not directly proportional to consumption rates so would not decline when consumption drops. Given the proportionality of emissions to NG use found in Section 3.4, the reductions in NG use would lead to CH4 emissions declines of 0.31–0.69 Gg a–1 in DC and 0.42–0.90 Gg a–1 in Baltimore (ranges consider the 95% CI on the relationship between NG use and emissions). Therefore, while reductions in NG use likely contributed to some of the observed decrease in emissions (6–38% in DC and 12–41% in Baltimore), other factors also played a large role. These findings are further supported by an analysis calculating separate trends from warmer months and cooler months (SI Section S9). This analysis indicates that while wintertime emissions showed larger relative declines than summer, emissions from both declined over time (SI Figure S17).

Discussion of Main Findings

In this study, we determine average CH4 emission rates of 80.1 [95% CI: 61.2, 98.9] Gg a–1 in the Washington, DC urban area and 47.4 [95% CI: 35.9, 58.5] Gg a–1 in the Baltimore urban area (mean of annual averages from 2018 to 2021). We also find emission rates that are 44% higher in winter and correlated with NG consumption, with emissions of 0.6 [95% CI: 0.4, 0.9] % and 0.9 [95% CI: 0.6, 1.3] % of NG use in DC and Baltimore, respectively, over a baseline seasonally invariant emission rate. Spatial patterns in our posterior estimates suggest that NG loss drives the seasonal variability, with wintertime emissions higher in the city centers.

Normalizing emissions by either area or population indicates a slightly higher emissions intensity in Balt relative to DC, consistent with the larger rate of emissions per unit of gas use. While our results point to a four-year declining emissions trend of approximately 4–5% per year in both cities, we are unable to attribute this entire trend to decreasing NG use. Future work will focus on extending the period of analysis to better examine both the relationship to gas use and the trends over time. We expect that additional years of analysis will allow for more robust trend detection by reducing the uncertainties associated with the observed trends. Additional measurements of other trace gas species that are emitted by NG leaks (e.g., ethane) and isotopes of CH4 are also needed to better disentangle the various urban sources of CH4 to the atmosphere.

Overall, our study contributes to the sparse existing knowledge base of CH4 emissions in cities, adding a valuable understanding of how these emissions change in time. Understanding the urban sources of CH4 may enable policymakers from local to national levels to better target mitigation efforts. Several municipalities have already passed legislation to limit new natural gas hookups, including Montgomery County, Maryland, which lies in our study area.57 Some LDCs, including Baltimore Gas and Electric, have invested in repairing and upgrading NG distribution infrastructure (https://marylandstride.com/) to improve reliability and avoid critical failures while also reducing the GHG footprint of NG use, and it is possible that these improvements contributed to the observed emissions declines. This study provides evidence to support mitigation efforts aimed at reducing the loss of NG in urban areas, but additional information that could more specifically point to the source of NG emissions and their variability is needed to further guide investments and regulation. Specifically, future studies should be designed to provide more granularity in emissions sectors to better inform inventory methods and track mitigation effort success.
