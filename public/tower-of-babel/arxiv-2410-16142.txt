Introduction

Let
D
be an integral domain and
K
its quotient field. The set denoted and defined by:

(1)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Int}(D):=\{f\in K[X];\;f(D)\subseteq D\},

is known to be a commutative
D
-algebra and is called
the ring of integer-valued polynomials over

D
. The study of these rings dates back to 1919, when Pólya
[
23
]
and Ostrowski
[
21
]
introduced and investigated
\mathrm{Int}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
, where
\mathcal{O}_{K}
denotes the ring of integers of a number field
K
. This investigation gave rise to an interesting subfield of Algebraic Number Theory called Pólya Theory, which remains a very active area of research. Later, in 1970, Cahen and Chabert generalized these rings, by considering an arbitrary integral domain
D
instead of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
(cf. (
1
)). It is noteworthy that the theory of these rings plays an important role in the Multiplicative Ideal Theory and has led to some very surprising results. Particularly, it is a very rich source of examples and counterexamples.

In 1991, Anderson
et al.

[
1
]
treated the ring of
D
-valued
B
-polynomials
, defined as follows:

\mathrm{Int}_{B}(D):=\{f\in B[X];\;f(D)\subseteq D\},

where
D\subseteq B
is an extension of integral domains, as a generalization of rings of integer-valued polynomials. Thereafter, in 1993, Cahen
[
4
]
considered another generalization of
\mathrm{Int}(D)
, namely, the ring of
integer-valued polynomials over a subset

E
of
K
, defined by:

\mathrm{Int}(E,D):=\{f\in K[X];\;f(E)\subseteq D\},

where
D
is an integral domain with quotient field
K
. A natural generalization of these rings is defined by:

\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D):=\{f\in K(X);\;f(E)\subseteq D\},

which is called
the ring of integer-valued rational functions on

E

over

D.
The ring
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
is investigated in
[
5
, Chapter X]
, and recently, in 2023, it was studied by B. Liu in his Ph.D. thesis
[
18
]
.

In 2020, the fourth named author introduced the concept of
D
-valued
B
-polynomials on
E
in
[
26
]
. This ring is formally defined as:

\mathrm{Int}_{B}(E,D):=\{f\in B[X];\;f(E)\subseteq D\},

where
D\subseteq B
represents an extension of integral domains, and
E
is a subset of the quotient field of
D
. These rings are considered to be a generalization of both
\mathrm{Int}_{B}(D)
and
\mathrm{Int}(E,D)
. A recent study investigating various properties of these rings is presented in
[
9
]
.

Let us now focus on giving a quick review of the studies established concerning the ring of integer-valued rational functions.
The study of these rings started in 1978 with P.-J. Cahen in his work
[
3
]
, where he introduced and studied them. Specifically, he showed that for any discrete valuation domain
V
with quotient field
K
and any subset
E
of
K
, the ring
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,V)
is a localization of
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(K,V)
and it is Prüfer. Additionally, he also studied its prime spectrum. Thereafter, in 1991, A. Prestel and C. Rippol
[
24
]
provided a remarkable result. Explicitly, they proved that: for a field
K
and a non-trivial valuation ring
V
of
K
with
\mathfrak{m}
as its maximal ideal, the equality
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(V)=\left(1+\mathfrak{m}\mathrm{Int}(V)\right)^{-1}\mathrm{Int}(V)
holds if and only if the completion of
(K,V)
is either locally compact or algebraically closed. In 1994, K.A. Loper
[
19
]
demonstrated that
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(D)
is a Prüfer domain for any monic Prüfer domain
D
. Three years after, Cahen and Chabert published the book
[
5
]
entitled
Integer-Valued Polynomials
, devoting its tenth chapter to the study of the ring
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
. Shortly after, in 1998, Cahen and Loper
[
6
]
were interested in the classification of integral domains
D
such that
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
is either Prüfer or Bézout, with special attention given to the case
D=V
is a valuation domain. In the same year, Gabelli and Tartarone in
[
11
]
investigated some properties of
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(D)
, including localization and the Bézout property. Recently, in 2022, M.H. Park
[
22
]
investigated the problem of when
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
is a globalized pseudo-valuation domain. Among other things, she showed that if

is a pseudo-valuation domain with associated valuation domain
V
and
E
is a precompact subset of the quotient field
K
, then
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
is a globalized pseudo-valuation domain with associated Prüfer domain
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,V)
, and moreover
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
is a localization of
\mathrm{Int}(E,D)
. Subsequently, in 2023, B. Liu in his Ph.D. thesis
[
18
]
investigated many questions concerning these rings, and more precisely whether
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(V)
is a Prüfer domain or not for valuation domains
V
. Despite the many mouth of studies concerning these rings, the community still in need to invest more and more efforts for studying and discovering the rings
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
. Surprisingly, till now, no investigation has been conducted on the module structure of
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
as either a
D
-module or a
D[X]
-module.

We note that this ring has been studied in the literature much less than the classical ring of integer-valued polynomials, due to several intrinsic difficulties related to it. Therefore, as a continuation of the previous investigations, we introduce and then study the ring of
D
-valued
B
-rational functions on

, which defined as follows:

\mathrm{Int}^{\mathrm{R}}_{B}(E,D):=\{f\in B(X);\;f(E)\subseteq D\},

where

is an extension of integral domains,

is a subset of the quotient field of

, and

denotes the set of all rational functions over

It is clear that
\mathrm{Int}_{B}(E,D)\subseteq\mathrm{Int}^{\mathrm{R}}_{B}(E,D)\subseteq B(X),
and that the ring
\mathrm{Int}^{\mathrm{R}}_{B}(E,D)

(resp.,
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(D)

\mathrm{Int}^{\mathrm{R}}_{B}(E,D)
(resp.,
\mathrm{Int}^{\mathrm{R}}_{B}(D,D)

We now give a brief description of the content of this paper. In Section

. Section

Throughout this paper,

1.
Preliminary definitions and properties

We start this section by recalling some definitions and notation. Let
D
be an integral domain with quotient field
K
. A subset
E
of
K
is said to be a
fractional subset
of
D
if there exists a nonzero element
d
of
D
such that
dE\subseteq D.
For a nonzero fractional ideal
I
of
D
, we let
I^{-1}:=\{x\in K;\;xI\subseteq D\}
. On
D
the
v
-
operation
is defined by
I_{v}:=(I^{-1})^{-1}
, the
t
-
operation
is defined by
I_{t}:=\bigcup J_{v}
, where
J
ranges over the set of all nonzero finitely generated ideals contained in
I
; and the
w
-
operation
is defined by
I_{w}:=\{x\in K;\;xJ\subseteq I
for some nonzero finitely generated ideal
J
of
D
with
J^{-1}=D\}
. A nonzero ideal
I
of
D
is
divisorial
(or
v
-
ideal
) (resp.,
t
-
ideal
,
w
-
ideal
) if
I_{v}=I
(resp.,
I_{t}=I
,
I_{w}=I
). In general, for each nonzero fractional ideal
I
of
D
, we have the inclusions
I\subseteq I_{w}\subseteq I_{t}\subseteq I_{v},
and then
v
-ideals are
t
-ideals and
t
-ideals themselves are
w
-ideals. If
\star
denotes either
t
or
w
, A
\star
-ideal that is also prime is called a
\star
-
prime ideal
, and a
\star
-
maximal ideal
is a maximal ideal among all
\star
-ideals of
D
and the set of all
\star
-maximal ideals of

is denoted by
\star
-
\mathrm{Max}(D)
. Moreover, we have
t
-
\mathrm{Max}(D)=w
-
\mathrm{Max}(D)
. Notice that each height-one prime is
t
-prime and when each
t
-prime ideal of

has height-one we say that

has

-
dimension one
which we denote by

-
\mathrm{dim}(D)=1
; in this case, we have

-
\mathrm{Max}(D)=X^{1}(D)
, where

is the set of all height-one prime ideals of

.

An integral domain
D
is said to be a
Prüfer
v
-multiplication domain
(for short, P
v
MD) if
D_{\mathfrak{m}}
is a valuation domain for each
t
-maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
of
D
. An integral domain

is said to be a

-

if

is a DVR (here by a DVR we mean a rank-one discrete valuation domain) for each

-maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
of

. Trivially, Krull domains and almost Dedekind domains are

-almost Dedekind domains and
t
-almost Dedekind domains are P

MDs. An integral domain

is a
strong Mori domain
(resp.,
Mori domain
) if it satisfies the ascending chain condition on
w
-ideals (resp.,
v
-ideals) of
D
. Clearly, Noetherian domains and Krull domains are strong Mori and strong Mori domains are Mori. An integral domain
D
is said to be
locally Mori
(resp.,

) if any localization of

at a maximal ideal is a Mori (resp., a Krull) domain. Obviously, almost Dedekind domains and Krull domains are almost Krull domains, and almost Krull domains themselves are locally Mori. Lastly, an integral domain

is said to be of

(resp.,
t
-
finite character
), if every nonzero element of
D
belongs to only finitely many maximal ideals (resp.,
t
-maximal ideals) of

. It is worth noting that Mori domains have

-finite character.

From the definition of the ring of
D
-valued
B
-rational functions on
E
as stated in the introduction, we deduce directly the following:

Proposition 1.1
.

Let
D_{1}\subseteq D_{2}
be two integral domains with the same quotient field
K
and
E\subseteq F
two nonempty subsets of
K
. Let
D_{i}\subseteq B_{i}
be two extensions of integral domains for
i\in\{1,2\}
. If
B_{1}\subseteq B_{2}
, then
\mathrm{Int}^{\mathrm{R}}_{B_{1}}\left(F,D_{1}\right)\subseteq\mathrm{Int}^{\mathrm{R}}_{B_{2}}\left(E,D_{2}\right)
.

Corollary 1.2
.

The following statements are equivalent:

(1)

E\subseteq D
,

(2)

\mathrm{Int}^{\mathrm{R}}_{B}(D)\subseteq\mathrm{Int}^{\mathrm{R}}_{B}(E,D)
,

(3)

D[X]\subseteq\mathrm{Int}^{\mathrm{R}}_{B}(E,D)
.

Proof.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(2) Just take
D_{1}=D_{2}=D
,
B_{1}=B_{2}=B
and
F=D
in the previous proposition.

(2)
\Rightarrow
(3) It follows from the trivial inclusion
D[X]\subseteq\mathrm{Int}^{\mathrm{R}}_{B}(D)
.

(3)
\Rightarrow
(1) Assume that
D[X]\subseteq\mathrm{Int}^{\mathrm{R}}_{B}(E,D)
. As the polynomial
\psi(X)=X
belongs to
D[X]
, we deduce that
\psi\in\mathrm{Int}^{\mathrm{R}}_{B}(E,D)
, and hence
\psi(e)\in D
for each

. Therefore,
E\subseteq D
.
∎

Notice that the previous corollary says that
\mathrm{Int}^{\mathrm{R}}_{B}(E,D)
is an overring of

if and only if
E
is contained in
D
.

We next rewrite
[
5
, Proposition X.1.4]
as follows:

Proposition 1.3
.

If
E
is infinite, then,
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)=\mathrm{Int}^{\mathrm{R}}_{L}(E,D)
, for any field extension
L
of
K
.

We can extend this proposition to any extension
B
of
D
. Unlike the well-known case of integer-valued polynomials, where
\mathrm{Int}_{B}(E,D)\neq\mathrm{Int}(E,D)
in general (for instance, see Example

), the following result shows that
\mathrm{Int}^{\mathrm{R}}_{B}(E,D)
and
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
coincide when

is infinite. To do this, we need the following lemma.

Lemma 1.4
.

If
L
denotes the quotient field of
B,
then
B(X)=L(X)
, and hence
\mathrm{Int}^{\mathrm{R}}_{B}(E,D)=\mathrm{Int}^{\mathrm{R}}_{L}(E,D).

Proof.

The inclusion
B(X)\subseteq L(X)
is clear. For the reverse inclusion, let
\varphi=\frac{f}{g}\in L(X)
with
f
and
g
are in
L[X]
. Then, there exist two nonzero elements
u
and
v
of
D
such that
uf\in B[X]
and
vg\in B[X],
and hence
\varphi=\frac{uvf}{uvg}\in B(X).
Thus,
L(X)\subseteq B(X)
, and this completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 1.5
.

If
E
is infinite, then
\mathrm{Int}^{\mathrm{R}}_{B}(E,D)=\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
. In particular,
\mathrm{Int}^{\mathrm{R}}_{B}(D)=\mathrm{Int^{R}}(D)
.

Proof.

Assume that
E
is infinite. If
L
denotes the quotient field of
B,
it follows from Lemma
1.4
that
\mathrm{Int}^{\mathrm{R}}_{B}(E,D)=\mathrm{Int}^{\mathrm{R}}_{L}(E,D),
and this last ring is simply
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
as asserted in Proposition
1.3
because
E
is infinite. For the particular case, we just take
E=D
in the proved equality.
∎

As previously stated in Proposition
1.5
, we have
\mathrm{Int}^{\mathrm{R}}_{B}(D)=\mathrm{Int^{R}}(D)
. However, the behavior of
\mathrm{Int}_{B}(D)
differs, as illustrated by the next example. To provide such an example, we recall that
D
is called a
d
-
ring
if
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(D)=\mathrm{Int}(D)
.

Example 1.6
.

Let us consider the ring
\mathbb{Z}[\frac{1}{3}]
as an overring of
\mathbb{Z}.
It is known that
\mathbb{Z}
is a
d
-ring by
[
5
, Example VII.2.2]
, so
\mathrm{Int}^{\mathrm{R}}_{\mathbb{Z}[\frac{1}{3}]}(\mathbb{Z})=\mathrm{Int^{R}}(\mathbb{Z})=\mathrm{Int}(\mathbb{Z})
and we also have
\mathrm{Int}(\mathbb{Z})\neq\mathrm{Int}_{\mathbb{Z}[\frac{1}{3}]}(\mathbb{Z})
because the polynomial
\psi(X)=\frac{X(X+1)}{2}
lies in
\mathrm{Int}(\mathbb{Z})
but not in
\mathrm{Int}_{\mathbb{Z}[\frac{1}{3}]}(\mathbb{Z})
.

The following result gives a necessary condition on
E
for
\mathrm{Int}_{B}(E,D)
(resp.,
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
) to contain a non-constant polynomial.

Proposition 1.7
.

If
D^{\prime}
denotes the integral closure of
D
and
B
is an overring of
D
, then each of the following statements implies the next:

(1)

\mathrm{Int}_{B}(E,D)
contains a non-constant polynomial;

(2)

\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
contains a non-constant polynomial;

(3)

E
is a fractional subset of
D^{\prime}
.

Proof.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(2) follows from the inclusion
\mathrm{Int}_{B}(E,D)\subseteq\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
.

(2)
\Rightarrow
(3) Let
f=\sum_{0\leq i\leq n}a_{i}X^{i}
be a polynomial of degree
n\geq 1
in
\mathrm{Int}^{\mathrm{R}}_{B}(E,D)
. By multiplying
f
by a common denominator of its coefficients, we can always suppose that
f\in D[X]
. For each
e\in E
, we have
a_{n}^{n-1}f(e)\in D
. From the equality

a_{n}^{n-1}f(e)=(a_{n}e)^{n}+a_{n-1}(a_{n}e)^{n-1}+\dots+a_{0}(a_{n})^{n-1},
we deduce that
(a_{n}e)^{n}+a_{n-1}(a_{n}e)^{n-1}+\dots+a_{0}(a_{n})^{n-1}-a_{n}^{n-1}f(e)=0,
which means that
a_{n}e\in D^{\prime}.
Hence,
a_{n}E\subseteq D^{\prime}
, and thus
E
is a fractional subset of
D^{\prime}
.
∎

Corollary 1.8
.

If
D
is integrally closed, then
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
contains a non-constant polynomial if and only if
E
is a fractional subset of
D
.

The next example illustrates that it is possible to have
\mathrm{Int}_{B}(E,D)=D
while
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)\neq D
for a non-fractional subset
E
of an integrally closed domain

.

Example 1.9
.

Consider the set
E=\{1,\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{4},\dots\}
. Since
\mathbb{Z}
is integrally closed and

is not a fractional subset of
\mathbb{Z},
it follows from Proposition

that
\mathrm{Int}_{B}(E,\mathbb{Z})=\mathbb{Z},
while
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,\mathbb{Z})\neq\mathbb{Z}
because
\varphi(X)=\frac{1}{X}
lies in
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,\mathbb{Z})
but not in
\mathbb{Z}
.

Now, we provide a necessary and sufficient condition on the subset
E
of
K
for
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
to contain a polynomial of degree one. Explicitly, we show that
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
contains a polynomial of degree one if and only if

is a fractional subset of

.

Proposition 1.10
.

For any overring
B
of

, the following assertions are equivalent:

(1)

\mathrm{Int}_{B}(E,D)
contains a polynomial of degree one;

(2)

\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
contains a polynomial of degree one;

(3)

E
is a fractional subset of
D
.

Proof.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(2) This follows directly from the inclusion
\mathrm{Int}_{B}(E,D)\subseteq\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
.

(2)
\Rightarrow
(3) Suppose
f(X)=aX+b
is a polynomial of degree one in
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
with coefficients
a
and
b
in
K
. Then there exists a nonzero element

in
D
such that
cf(X)=caX+cb\in D[X]
. For each

,
cf(e)\in D
implies

. Therefore,

is a fractional subset of
D
.

(3)
\Rightarrow
(1) follows from the existence of a nonzero element

such that
dE\subseteq D
. Consequently,

, and this completes the proof.
∎

2.
Localization and ideals of
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)

This section focuses on studying some (prime) ideals of
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
and investigating the behavior
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
under localization.

We start by stating the following useful lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

For any prime ideal
\mathfrak{P}
of
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
, we have

D_{\mathfrak{P}\cap D}=\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)_{\mathfrak{P}}\cap K.

Proof.

This is quite trivial.
∎

Definition 2.2
.

Using the above notations and assumptions, let
I
be an ideal of
D
and
a
an element of
E
. We define the set
\mathfrak{I}_{I,a}
as follows:

\mathfrak{I}_{I,a}:=\{\varphi\in\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D);\;\varphi(a)\in I\}.

(1)

\mathfrak{I}_{I,a}
has the structure of an ideal of
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
.

(2)

Ideals of
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
of this form are called pointed ideals.

(3)

If
I
is a prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}
(resp., maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
) of
D
, we use the notation
\mathfrak{P}_{\mathfrak{p},a}
(resp.,
\mathfrak{M}_{\mathfrak{m},a}
) instead of
\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{p},a}
(resp.,
\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{m},a}
) and refer to these as pointed prime ideals (resp., pointed maximal ideals).

Proposition 2.3
.

For any prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}
of
D
and for any element
a
of
E
, the following statements hold:

(1)

\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)/\mathfrak{P}_{\mathfrak{p},a}\simeq D/\mathfrak{p}.

(2)

\mathfrak{P}_{\mathfrak{p},a}
is a prime ideal of
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
above
\mathfrak{p}
.

(3)

\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)_{\mathfrak{P}_{\mathfrak{p},a}}\cap K=D_{\mathfrak{p}}
.

Proof.

(1) It suffices to consider the map

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\psi_{a}:&\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)&\rightarrow&D/\mathfrak{p}\\
&\varphi&\mapsto&\psi_{a}(\varphi)=\varphi(a)\mod(\mathfrak{p}).\end{array}

(2) This is a consequence of the previous statement.

(3) The desired equality follows from statement (2) and Lemma
2.1

∎

Remark 2.4
.

(1) We note that Proposition
2.3
justifies the notations
\mathfrak{P}_{\mathfrak{p},a}
and
\mathfrak{M}_{\mathfrak{m},a}
used in the last statement of Definition
2.2
.

(2) Notably, as mentioned in
[
5
, page 259]
, integer-valued rational functions do not behave well under localization, even in the Noetherian case. Recently, Liu, in
[
17
]
, points out that the inclusion
S^{-1}\mathrm{Int^{R}}(D)\subseteq\mathrm{Int^{R}}(S^{-1}D)
does not hold in general (see
[
17
, Example 1.22]
).

Proposition 2.5
.

If
D=\cap_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}}D_{\mathfrak{p}},
where
\mathcal{P}
is a nonempty subset of
\mathrm{Spec}(D)
, then
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)=\cap_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D_{\mathfrak{p}})=\cap_{a\in E}\cap_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)_{\mathfrak{B}_{\mathfrak{p},a}}.

Proof.

By Proposition
1.1
, we have
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)\subseteq\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D_{\mathfrak{p}})
, for each
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}
, and then
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)\subseteq\cap_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D_{\mathfrak{p}})
. For the other inclusion, we let
\varphi
be an element of
\cap_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D_{\mathfrak{p}})
. For each
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P},
we have
\varphi(E)\subseteq D_{\mathfrak{p}}
and
\varphi\in K(X)
. Hence,
\varphi(E)\subseteq\cap_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}}D_{\mathfrak{p}}=D
, and thus
\cap_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D_{\mathfrak{p}})\subseteq\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
.

To prove the second equality, we only need to check the reverse inclusion. So, let
\psi\in\cap_{a\in E}\cap_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)_{\mathfrak{P}_{\mathfrak{p},a}}
. Then, for each
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}
and each
a\in E
,
\psi\in\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)_{\mathfrak{P}_{\mathfrak{p},a}},
and hence there exists
\varphi\in\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)\backslash\mathfrak{P}_{\mathfrak{p},a}
such that
\varphi\psi\in\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D).
Thus,
\varphi(a)\notin\mathfrak{p}
and
\varphi(a)\psi(a)\in D
, and therefore
\psi(a)\in D_{\mathfrak{p}}
. Consequently, for each
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}
,
\psi(E)\subseteq D_{\mathfrak{p}}
, which implies that
\psi(E)\subseteq\cap_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}}D_{\mathfrak{p}}=D
, that is,
\psi\in\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
.
∎

Corollary 2.6
.

We always have the following:

(1)

\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)=\cap_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathrm{Spec}(D)}\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D_{\mathfrak{p}})=\cap_{a\in E}\cap_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathrm{Spec}(D)}\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)_{\mathfrak{P}_{\mathfrak{p},a}}.

(2)

\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)=\cap_{\mathfrak{m}\in\mathrm{Max}(D)}\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D_{\mathfrak{m}})=\cap_{a\in E}\cap_{\mathfrak{m}\in\mathrm{Max}(D)}\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)_{\mathfrak{M}_{\mathfrak{m},a}}.

(3)

If
(D,\mathfrak{m})
is a local domain, then
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)=\cap_{a\in E}\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)_{\mathfrak{M}_{\mathfrak{m},a}}.

Theorem 2.7
.

If

Lemma 2.8

Proof of Theorem

The direct inclusion is trivial. For the reverse inclusion, let

Lemma 2.14

For any nonzero finitely generated ideal

(J\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D))_{v}\cap D=J_{v}.

Proof.

Proposition 2.15

For any nonzero ideal

Let

3.
The transfer of some properties in
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)

In this section, we aim to investigate the transfer of some ring-theoretic properties to rings of integer-valued rational functions.

We start with a complete characterization of when
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
is integrally closed, which proposed in
[
5
]
as an exercise.

Proposition 3.1

(
[
5
, Exercise X.2]
)
.

The integral domain
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
is integrally closed if and only if so is
D
.

Proof.

Suppose that
D
is integrally closed and let
\psi\in K(X)
be integral over
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
. We have:

\psi^{n}+\varphi_{n-1}\psi^{n-1}+\dots+\varphi_{1}\psi+\varphi_{0}=0,

where
\varphi_{i}\in\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
for each
i
. Then, for each
e\in E
, we have :

\psi^{n}(e)+\varphi_{n-1}(e)\psi^{n-1}(e)+\dots+\varphi_{1}(e)\psi(e)+\varphi_{0}(e)=0,

and hence
\psi(e)
is integral over
D
. Therefore
\psi(e)\in D
, for each
e\in E
, that is,
\psi\in\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
. For the converse, if
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
is integrally closed then so is
D
because
D=\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)\cap K
(an intersection of two integrally closed domains).
∎

Remark 3.2
.

This last proposition implies that the integral closure of
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
is contained in
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D^{\prime}),
where
D^{\prime}
denotes the integral closure of
D
.

An integral domain
D
with quotient field
K
is said to be
seminormal
if, for each
\alpha\in K
, whenever
\alpha^{2},\alpha^{3}\in D
, then
\alpha\in D
. Example of seminormal domains are Prüfer domains. It is noteworthy that
\mathrm{Int}(D)
is a seminormal domain if and only if so is
D
(cf.
[
1
, Proposition 7.2(3)]
). We next provide an analogue of this result for
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
.

Proposition 3.3
.

The integral domain
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
is seminormal if and only if so is
D
.

Proof.

Assume that
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
is seminormal and let
\alpha\in K
such that
\alpha^{2},\alpha^{3}\in D
. Since
D\subseteq\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
, we have
\alpha^{2},\alpha^{3}\in\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
, and then by seminormality of
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
,
\alpha\in\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
. Hence,
\alpha\in D

is seminormal. Conversely, assume that

is seminormal and let

for all

∎

In the following, we will provide a necessary condition on

is either Krull, Mori, almost Krull, or locally Mori.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
(\mathcal{P})
denotes one of the following properties for domains: Krull, Mori, almost Krull or locally Mori. If
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
has the property

then

has the same property.

Proof.

Since
D=\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)\cap K
and the intersection of two Krull (resp., Mori) domains is a Krull (resp., Mori) domain, if
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
is Krull (resp., Mori) then

is also Krull (resp., Mori). Now, assume that
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
is an almost Krull domain, and let
\mathfrak{m}
be a maximal ideal of
D
and
a
an element of
E
. It follows from Proposition
2.3
that
\mathfrak{M}_{\mathfrak{m},a}
is a maximal ideal of
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
and then
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)_{\mathfrak{M}_{\mathfrak{m},a}}
is a Krull domain. Again, by Proposition

,
D_{\mathfrak{m}}=\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)_{\mathfrak{M}_{\mathfrak{m},a}}\cap K,
and so
D_{\mathfrak{m}}
is a Krull domain as an intersection of two Krull domains. Therefore,

is an almost Krull domain. Lastly, the case of locally Mori is similar to that of almost Krull.
∎

For strong Mori domains, we have the following:

Proposition 3.5
.

If
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
is a strong Mori domain then so is

.

Proof.

Assume that
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
is a strong Mori domain. By

, we need to show that every

-prime ideal of

is of

-finite type. So, let
\mathfrak{p}
be a

-prime ideal of

\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
is a strong Mori domain,

(\mathfrak{p}\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D))_{w}=(I\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D))_{w},
for some finitely generated subideal

of

. By Proposition
3.4
,

is a Mori domain because any strong Mori domain is Mori, and then, by Theorem

\mathfrak{p}_{w}=(\mathfrak{p}\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D))_{w}\cap D=(I\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D))_{w}\cap D=I_{w}

Remark 3.6
.

It is important to note that the converse of the previous two propositions does not hold in general. For instance, while the ring of integers

is locally Mori (as it is a PID),

We recall the following definition of locally finite intersection of integral domains. Let
\{D_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\Lambda}
be a family of integral domains having the same quotient field. The intersection
\cap_{\alpha\in\Lambda}D_{\alpha}=:D
is said to be

if every nonzero element of

is a unit in

for all but finitely many
\alpha\in\Lambda

is local with maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}
, the above intersection is locally finite if and only if each nonzero element of

belongs to only finitely many ideals
\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}
.

Proposition 3.9
.

If
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
is a locally finite intersection of a family of its localizations then so is
D
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)=\cap_{\mathfrak{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)_{\mathfrak{P}}
, where
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathrm{Spec}(\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D))
such that the intersection
\cap_{\mathfrak{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)_{\mathfrak{P}}
is locally finite. We set
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}:=\{\mathfrak{P}\cap D;\mathfrak{P}\in\mathcal{P}\}
. By Lemma
2.1
, we have
D_{\mathfrak{P}\cap D}=\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)_{\mathfrak{P}}\cap K
, and then
\cap_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}D_{\mathfrak{p}}=\cap_{\mathfrak{P}\in\mathcal{P}}(\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)_{\mathfrak{P}}\cap K)=\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)\cap K=D.

Thus,
D=\cap_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}D_{\mathfrak{p}}
and this intersection is locally finite since the intersection
\cap_{\mathfrak{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)_{\mathfrak{P}}
is locally finite.
∎

Remark 3.10
.

The converse of the previous proposition is not true in general. For instance,
\mathbb{Z}
has finite character but
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(\mathbb{Z})
does not have a locally finite intersection of a family of its localizations. Otherwise,

that

which is not the case.

4.
The module structure of
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
and
\mathrm{Int}_{B}(E,D)

In this section, we will investigate the properties of

as a module over

and also as an overring of

when

is a subset of
D
. We first start by stating the following remark:

Remark 4.1
.

We have the following :

(1)

The ring
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
also has the structure of a module over
D
.

(2)

The
D
-module
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
is torsion-free.

(3)

\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)\cap K=D.

Based on the last statement of the previous remark and by virtue of

, we derive the following:

Proposition 4.2
.

If
D
is a Prüfer domain, then
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
is faithfully flat as a

-module.

As an illustrative example, consider the following:

Example 4.3
.

The integral domain

, where

is an indeterminate over
\mathbb{Q}
, is known to be Prüfer. By Proposition
4.2
,
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
is a faithfully flat

-module for any subset

of
\mathbb{Q}(T)
.

We next provide the
w

Following
[
16
]
, a module

over

is said to be

if, for every short exact sequence

w
-flatness. A module

over

is said to be

-

if it is
w
-flat and
(M/\mathfrak{p}M)_{w}\neq 0
for all
\mathfrak{p}\in w

.

Proposition 4.4
.

For any P
v

, the
D
-module
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
is
w
-faithfully flat.

Proof.

Using
[
15
, Proposition 2.5]
), we only need to show that
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)_{\mathfrak{m}}
is faithfully flat

-module for all
w
-maximal ideals
\mathfrak{m}
of

. To do this, let
\mathfrak{m}
be a
w
-maximal ideal of
D
. As

is a P

MD,

is a valuation domain and then it follows from
[

, Remark 3.4]
that
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)_{\mathfrak{m}}
is a faithfully flat

-module because
D_{\mathfrak{m}}=(\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)\cap K)_{\mathfrak{m}}=\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)_{\mathfrak{m}}\cap K
. Thus,
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
is

-faithfully flat

-module, as desired.
∎

This last result and its proof represent a particular case of a result established in
[

]
. For the sake of completeness, we have included the previous proof.

Our next theorem shows that the concepts of flatness and faithful flatness coincide for the two
D
-modules
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
and

.

Theorem 4.5
.

The
D
-module
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)

(
resp.,
\mathrm{Int}_{B}(E,D))
is flat if and only if it is faithfully flat.

Proof.

Assume that
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
is flat. To prove that
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
is faithfully flat
D
-module, it suffices to check that, for all maximal ideals
\mathfrak{m}
of
D
, we have
\mathfrak{m}\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)\neq\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
(cf.
[
20
, Theorem 2, pages 25 and 26]
). By way of contradiction, suppose there exists a maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
of
D
such that
\mathfrak{m}\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)=\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
. This implies
1=m_{1}\varphi_{1}+\dots+m_{n}\varphi_{n}
for some
m_{1},\dots,m_{n}\in\mathfrak{m}
and
\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{n}\in\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
. So, necessarily at least one of
\varphi_{i},\dots,\varphi_{n}
must be nonzero (otherwise, we would have

, a contradiction). Then,

, for all

(because
\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{n}\in\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
). Hence,

, which is a contradiction. Consequently,
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
is faithfully flat

-module. The converse is trivial.

The proof for the case of
\mathrm{Int}_{B}(E,D)
is similar to that of
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)
.
∎

Proposition 4.6

For any nonempty subset

\mathrm{Int^{R}}(E,D)

Proof.

The implications (1)

(1) are straightforward. The implication (2)

since

∎

, Corollary II.1.6]
.

All established results regarding the flatness of
\mathrm{Int}(D)
as a
D[X]
-module assert that if the flatness of
\mathrm{Int}(D)
holds over
D[X],
then
\mathrm{Int}(D)
is necessarily trivial. However, the following proposition shows that it is possible to have
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(D)
flat over
D[X]
while
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(D)\neq D[X]
. Furthermore, it shows that
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(D)
can be faithfully flat over
D
without being faithfully flat over
D[X]
.

Proposition 4.9
.

For any valuation domain
V
with maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
, we have the following:

(1)

\mathrm{Int^{R}}(V)
is faithfully flat over
V
.

(2)

If
\mathfrak{m}
is principal with finite residue field, then
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(V)
is not faithfully flat over
V[X]
.

(3)

If the quotient field of
V
is algebraically closed, then
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(V)
is flat over
V[X]
but not faithfully flat over
V[X]
.

Proof.

(1) This follows from Proposition
4.2
.

(2) Assume that
\mathfrak{m}
is principal with finite residue field. Then, by
[
5
, Proposition I.3.16]
,
\mathrm{Int}(V)\neq V[X]
, and hence it follows from the previous corollary that
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(V)
is not faithfully flat over
V[X]
.

(3) Assume that the quotient field of
V
is algebraically closed. From
[
6
, Proposition 2.4]
, we have
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(V)=S^{-1}V[X]
, where
S=\{1+mX;\,m\in\mathfrak{m}\}
. Then,
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(V)
is flat over
V[X]
, and also it is not faithfully flat over
V[X]
since
S^{-1}V[X]
is not equal to
V[X]
.
∎

Corollary 4.10
.

Let
V
be a DVR with finite residue field. If the quotient field of
V
is algebraically closed, then the
V[X]
-module
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(V)
is flat but not faithfully flat.

Now, let us investigate the cyclicity of the two
D
-modules
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(D)
and
\mathrm{Int}_{B}(E,D)
.

Proposition 4.11
.

The
D
-module
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(D)
is never cyclic.

Proof.

By way of contradiction, suppose that
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(D)
is a cyclic
D
-module. Then there exists
\varphi\in\mathrm{Int^{R}}(D)
such that
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(D)=\varphi D
. Since
D\subseteq\mathrm{Int^{R}}(D)
, there exists
d\in D
such that
1=d\varphi
and then
\varphi
is an element of
K
. Thus,
\varphi
is an element of
D
and it is invertible in
D
because
\varphi\in\mathrm{Int^{R}}(D)
. Therefore,
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(D)=D
, which is a contradiction since
\psi(X)=X\in\mathrm{Int^{R}}(D)\backslash D
. Consequently, the
D
-module
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(D)
is never cyclic.
∎

Concerning the
D
-module
\mathrm{Int}_{B}(E,D)
, we have:

Proposition 4.12
.

For any overring
B
of
D
, the
D
-module
\mathrm{Int}_{B}(E,D)
is cyclic if and only if it is equal to
D
.

Proof.

We argue mimicking the proof of the above proposition to show the direct implication. The converse is trivial.
∎

It is clear that for any
d
-ring
D
, both
\mathrm{Int}(D)
and
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(D)
are simultaneously (locally) free as
D
-modules. Thus, we close our paper with the following open question.

Question 4.13
.

Is there any example of a non-
d
-ring
D
for which the
D
-module
\mathrm{Int^{R}}(D)
is (locally) free or not?
