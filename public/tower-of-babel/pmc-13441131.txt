Severity and Clinical Outcomes Among ICU Patients With Diabetic Ketoacidosis: An Analysis of the Japanese Intensive Care Patient Database
Authors: Reina Miyazaki, Hiromu Okano, Shunsaku Goto, Haruna Tanaka, Hiroshi Okamoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IMPORTANCE:Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) is an acute metabolic emergency characterized by hyperglycemia, ketosis, and acidosis due to insulin deficiency. While ICU admission is frequently considered for DKA, admission thresholds and resource utilization vary across countries and institutions. Current data linking admission severity to clinical outcomes among ICU-admitted patients with DKA remain limited.OBJECTIVES:To describe clinical outcomes and ICU length of stay (LOS) stratified by arterial pH recorded during ICU day 1 among ICU-admitted patients with DKA in Japan.DESIGN, SETTING, AND PARTICIPANTS:Retrospective, multicenter observational study of adult patients admitted with a diagnosis of DKA to 95 ICUs across Japan between fiscal years 2015 and 2022. Patients were stratified by arterial pH recorded during ICU day 1 into severe (pH < 7.00), moderate (7.00 ≤ pH < 7.25), and mild (pH ≥ 7.25) groups.MAIN OUTCOMES AND MEASURES:The primary outcome was the in-hospital mortality rate. The secondary outcomes were ICU mortality, hospital LOS, and ICU LOS.RESULTS:Among 866 eligible patients (mild, n = 449; moderate, n = 347; severe, n = 70), in-hospital mortality was low regardless of ICU day 1 pH severity (mild: 4.5%, moderate: 3.2%, and severe: 5.7%). ICU mortality was also low (mild: 1.6% [7/449], moderate: 1.4% [5/347], and severe: 2.9% [2/70]). Hospital LOS was similar across groups (mild: 17.0 d, interquartile range [IQR], 10.0–26.0 d; moderate: 15.0 d, IQR, 9.0–24.0 d; and severe: 15.0 d, IQR, 10.0–22.0 d), whereas ICU LOS tended to increase with severity (mild: 44.3 hr, IQR, 24.6–72.9 hr; moderate: 48.3 hr, IQR, 27.9–76.8 hr; and severe: 57.5 hr, IQR, 31.1–115.0 hr).CONCLUSIONS AND RELEVANCE:In this nationwide ICU registry, in-hospital mortality was low across arterial pH recorded during ICU day 1-defined severity strata, while ICU LOS tended to be longer in strata with more severe DKA. These findings are descriptive; clinically meaningful mortality differences cannot be excluded due to the small number of events, and further studies are needed to evaluate prognostic models and triage strategies.

KEY POINTS

Question: How do clinical outcomes and ICU length of stay (LOS) vary across arterial pH recorded during ICU day 1-defined severity strata among ICU-admitted adults with diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) in Japan?

Findings: In this nationwide cohort of 866 ICU-admitted adults with DKA, in-hospital mortality was low across ICU day 1 pH-defined severity strata, while ICU LOS tended to be longer in strata with more severe DKA.

Meaning: These descriptive data characterize ICU case-mix and resource use patterns among ICU-admitted patients with DKA; whether arterial pH recorded during ICU day 1 adds prognostic value beyond organ dysfunction markers requires further analytic studies with sufficient events and validated case definitions.

Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) is an acute metabolic emergency characterized by hyperglycemia, ketonemia, and metabolic acidosis, and severe presentations may require intensive care (1, 2). In high-income settings, contemporary reports suggest low in-hospital mortality, whereas hospitalization rates and ICU utilization vary widely across countries and institutions (3–8). Importantly, variation in ICU utilization has not consistently translated into differences in length of stay (LOS) or mortality, and there remains no consensus regarding which patients with DKA warrant ICU admission. Furthermore, ICU admission and ICU resource utilization are distinct concepts: admission to an ICU does not necessarily imply high resource consumption, and the intensity of care among admitted patients may vary substantially across institutions and patient case-mix.

Separate from the question of who should be admitted, the clinical course and resource use of patients already admitted to the ICU with DKA remain incompletely characterized. A previous Japanese multicenter study described outcomes across pH-based DKA severity categories, but that cohort included all admitted patients rather than focusing specifically on ICU-admitted patients (3, 9). Consequently, national-level data describing epidemiology, severity-stratified outcomes, and ICU LOS among ICU-admitted patients with DKA remain limited. The present study does not address ICU admission thresholds; rather, it focuses on describing clinical outcomes and ICU LOS—as a proxy for post-admission resource use—among patients already admitted to the ICU, stratified by DKA severity at ICU day 1.

To address this gap, we used the Japanese Intensive Care Patient Database (JIPAD), a nationwide ICU registry (10), to describe clinical outcomes and ICU LOS stratified by arterial pH recorded during ICU day 1 among ICU-admitted patients with DKA.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Design, Population, and Setting

This multicenter, retrospective, observational study analyzed data from the JIPAD, a nationwide ICU registry maintained by the Japanese Society of Intensive Care Medicine. As of June 2025, the database included 324,473 individuals from 95 facilities. The study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology statement (11).

Ethics approval was granted by St. Luke’s International University Research Ethics Committee for the study “Epidemiology and Description of Prognostic Factors of DKA in Japanese Intensive Care Units” (approval notification date: May 14, 2025; reference number: 25-R026). The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the responsible ethics committee and with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975.

Participants

Adult patients (≥ 18 yr old) admitted to participating ICUs with a diagnosis of DKA during the fiscal years 2015–2022 were included. Patients were excluded if their arterial blood gas pH or outcome data were missing. Patients were classified by arterial pH recorded during ICU day 1 as severe (pH < 7.00), moderate (7.00 ≤ pH < 7.25), and mild (pH ≥ 7.25) (2). Records with missing arterial pH recorded during ICU day 1 were excluded from the primary severity-stratified descriptions because severity classification required ICU day 1 pH; outcomes missing were excluded because they precluded outcome summarization.

Exposure and Outcomes

The principal exposure for comparative analyses was the DKA severity category (mild, moderate, or severe) on ICU day 1. The primary outcome was the in-hospital mortality rate. The secondary outcomes were ICU mortality, hospital LOS, and ICU LOS.

Variables

From the JIPAD, we extracted demographics (age and sex), comorbidities (e.g., congestive heart failure, respiratory failure, liver failure, cirrhosis, metastatic cancer, use of immunosuppressants, and dialysis), and clinical variables within the first 24 hours of ICU admission, including the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score, mean heart rate, mean blood pressure, respiratory rate, urine output, invasive mechanical ventilation, renal replacement therapy (intermittent and continuous), and catecholamine use (dobutamine, dopamine, noradrenaline, and adrenaline). Laboratory data included creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, glucose, and lactate levels. Severity of illness scores on ICU day 1 included the Acute Physiologic and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) II and III scores, the Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II), and the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score.

Statistical Analyses

Continuous variables are summarized as means with sds or medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Baseline characteristics were summarized descriptively by ICU day 1 pH-defined DKA severity group, with no formal between-group hypothesis testing and no p values reported for baseline variables. Clinical outcomes were likewise summarized using descriptive statistics; any numerical contrasts, when presented, were used only to describe observed patterns across severity strata and were not intended as confirmatory hypothesis testing in this descriptive observational study.

In-hospital mortality was summarized as counts and proportions. Hospital and ICU LOS were summarized as medians (IQRs). Outcomes were reported descriptively by ICU day 1 pH-defined DKA severity strata (mild, moderate, and severe), without formal hypothesis testing.

To further characterize the clinical profile of specific conditions, we performed descriptive subgroup summaries restricted to patients with euglycemic DKA (euDKA), defined as a maximum blood glucose level less than 250 mg/dL recorded on ICU day 1 (12). Outcomes were summarized descriptively across ICU day 1 pH-defined severity strata. We did not perform confirmatory hypothesis testing; any numerical contrasts were presented only to describe observed patterns.

To improve transparency regarding missing ICU day 1 pH, we additionally described patient characteristics and outcomes for records with missing vs. nonmissing ICU day 1 pH, to characterize potential differences related to missingness rather than to support confirmatory inference. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (Version 29.0; IBM SPSS, Chicago, IL) and R (Version 4.0.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

RESULTS

Participant Selection

Figure 1 summarizes the participant selection process. Between fiscal years 2015 and 2022, 935 adult patients were admitted to participating ICUs with a diagnosis of DKA. After excluding records with missing arterial pH recorded during ICU day 1 values (n = 69; characteristics of excluded patients are described in the Missing ICU Day 1 pH and Other Missing Data section), 866 patients met study criteria and comprised the analytic cohort. Patients were classified by ICU day 1 pH into mild (pH ≥ 7.25; n = 449), moderate (7.00 ≤ pH < 7.25; n = 347), and severe (pH < 7.00; n = 70) groups.

Missing ICU Day 1 pH and Other Missing Data

As noted in the Participant Selection section, 69 records (7.4%) were excluded due to missing ICU day 1 pH. Compared with patients with available ICU day 1 pH (n = 866), those with missing pH (n = 69) had a similar mean age (56.9 vs. 57.9 yr) and sex distribution (male, 58.3% vs. 56.5%). Markers of acute physiologic derangement tended to be less pronounced in the missing pH group, including lower illness-severity scores (APACHE II, 19.2 vs. 16.0; APACHE III, 74.6 vs. 63.7; and SAPS II, 39.7 vs. 34.0), and lower use of invasive mechanical ventilation within the first 24 hours (18.6% vs. 10.1%). Lactate levels were similar (3.0 vs. 2.8 mmol/L). Maximum blood glucose on ICU day 1 was lower in the missing pH group (546.8 vs. 447.0 mg/dL). These descriptive comparisons did not indicate marked systematic differences across the available characteristics (Supplemental Table 1, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B663). Regarding other variables, missingness was most frequently observed in catecholamine dose category variables (dobutamine/dopamine/noradrenaline/adrenaline), each with 105 missing records (11.2%). Missingness in consciousness (GCS ≤ 8) was rare (1/935, 0.1%), and missingness in other key variables used in the descriptive summaries was otherwise limited (Supplemental Table 2, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B663).

Participant Characteristics

Baseline characteristics stratified by survival status are shown in Table 1. Overall, the mean age was 56.9 ± 17.7 years, 58.3% were male, and 45.7% were 60 years old or older. Nonsurvivors (n = 35) were older (67.5 ± 18.3 vs. 56.5 ± 17.5 yr) and more frequently 60 years old or older (74.3% vs. 44.5%) compared with survivors. In the first 24 hours, invasive mechanical ventilation was more frequent among nonsurvivors (42.9% vs. 17.6%), and lactate levels were higher (5.9 ± 3.9 vs. 2.9 ± 2.6 mmol/L). Illness-severity scores on ICU day 1 were also higher among nonsurvivors (APACHE II 30.4 ± 9.6 vs. 18.7 ± 7.1; APACHE III 118.6 ± 34.0 vs. 72.7 ± 26.0; SAPS II 63.3 ± 18.7 vs. 38.7 ± 15.4; and SOFA 10.3 ± 4.6 vs. 4.5 ± 3.4). Catecholamine use within the first 24 hours of ICU admission was also more common among nonsurvivors (64.5% vs. 21.2%).

Clinical Outcomes

Clinical outcomes stratified by DKA severity are shown in Table 3. In-hospital mortality was low across ICU day 1 pH-defined severity strata (mild: 4.5% [20/449], moderate: 3.2% [11/347], and severe: 5.7% [4/70]). ICU mortality was also low (mild: 1.6% [7/449], moderate: 1.4% [5/347], and severe: 2.9% [2/70]). Hospital LOS was similar across groups (mild: 17.0 d [Q1–Q3, 10.0–26.0 d], moderate: 15.0 d [9.0–24.0 d], and severe: 15.0 d [10.0–22.0 d]). ICU LOS tended to be longer with more severe DKA (mild: 44.3 hr [Q1–Q3, 24.6–72.9 hr], moderate: 48.3 hr [27.9–76.8 hr], and severe: 57.5 hr [31.1–115.0 hr]), with distributions illustrated in Figures 2 and 3.

Subgroup Analysis

Among the 866 patients with DKA, 65 (7.5%) met the operational definition of euDKA and 800 (92.5%) were classified as non-euDKA; one patient had missing blood glucose data and was excluded. In-hospital mortality was low in both groups (euDKA: 6.1% [4/65] vs. non-euDKA: 3.8% [30/800]), as was ICU mortality (3.1% [2/65] vs. 1.3% [11/800]). Median hospital LOS was 13.0 days (Q1–Q3, 8.0–19.0 d) in euDKA and 16.0 days (10.0–25.0 d) in non-euDKA. Median ICU LOS was 38.7 hours (23.8–69.8 hr) in euDKA and 47.0 hours (27.0–79.0 hr) in non-euDKA (Supplemental Table 4, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B663).

Within the euDKA cohort (n = 65), outcomes were summarized descriptively by ICU day 1 pH-defined severity strata (mild: n = 40; moderate: n = 23; severe: n = 2; Supplemental Table 5, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B663). In-hospital mortality was 5.0% (2/40) in mild euDKA, 8.7% (2/23) in moderate euDKA, and 0% (0/2) in severe euDKA. ICU mortality was 2.6% (1/40), 4.3% (1/23), and 0% (0/2), respectively. Median hospital LOS was 11.5 days (Q1–Q3, 1.0–100.0 d) in mild euDKA, 15.0 days (2.0–63.0 d) in moderate euDKA, and 29.0 days (8.0–50.0 d) in severe euDKA. Median ICU LOS was 33.50 hours (12.0–168.7 hr), 44.25 hours (10.8–196.2 hr), and 77.55 hours (51.8–103.3 hr), respectively. The severe euDKA subgroup comprised only two patients; the reported IQRs for this stratum are therefore equivalent to the observed minimum and maximum values and are not interpretable as measures of distributional spread. All summaries for the severe euDKA stratum should be regarded as individual observations only and not as meaningful group-level estimates (see also Supplemental Table 5, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B663, footnote).

In the non-euDKA cohort (n = 800), in-hospital mortality was 4.2% (17/408) in mild, 2.8% (9/324) in moderate, and 5.9% (4/68) in severe non-euDKA; ICU mortality was 1.2% (5/408), 1.2% (4/324), and 3.0% (2/68), respectively (Supplemental Table 6, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B663). Median hospital LOS was 17.0 days (Q1–Q3, 11.0–28.0 d) in mild non-euDKA, 15.0 days (10.0–24.0 d) in moderate non-euDKA, and 15.0 days (10.0–22.0 d) in severe non-euDKA. Median ICU LOS was 45.0 hours (24.6–74.8 hr), 48.4 hours (28.8–75.7 hr), and 57.5 hours (31.0–115.4 hr), respectively.

Descriptive Analysis Excluding Long-Stay Outliers

As a descriptive check, we summarized outcomes after excluding long-stay outliers. The resulting analytic cohort included 796 patients: mild (n = 410), moderate (n = 322), and severe (n = 64; Supplemental Fig. 1, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B663). In-hospital mortality remained low across severity strata (mild: 4.4% [18/410], moderate: 2.8% [9/322], and severe: 4.7% [3/64]), as did ICU mortality (1.7% [7/410], 1.6% [5/322], and 3.1% [2/64], respectively). Median hospital LOS was 15.0 days (Q1–Q3, 10.0–23.0 d) in mild, 15.0 days (9.0–21.0 d) in moderate, and 14.0 days (9.0–20.0 d) in severe DKA. Median ICU LOS was 42.7 hours (23.9–68.2 hr), 47.6 hours (27.1–71.8 hr), and 55.7 hours (30.4–96.0 hr), respectively (Supplemental Table 7, Supplemental Figs. 2 and 3, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B663).

DISCUSSION

Summary of Findings

In this nationwide ICU registry cohort of adults admitted with a diagnosis of DKA, we summarized clinical outcomes and ICU LOS stratified by arterial pH recorded during ICU day 1-defined severity. Overall in-hospital mortality was low across severity strata, and we did not observe a clear monotonic gradient in mortality with increasing DKA severity. In contrast, ICU LOS tended to be longer in strata with more severe DKA; this association does not establish a causal mechanism, and the observed pattern may be confounded by unmeasured differences in illness severity or care processes. Because survivors and nonsurvivors differed in multiple demographic and illness-severity characteristics, and because mortality events were sparse—particularly in the severe stratum—these severity-stratified estimates should be interpreted as descriptive epidemiology rather than confirmatory evidence regarding prognostic “effects” of pH. In addition, our subgroup summaries for euDKA were limited by small numbers, especially in the severe euDKA category. Taken together, these findings primarily characterize the ICU case-mix, outcomes, and resource use patterns among ICU-admitted patients with DKA in Japan. Future studies with sufficient events and validated case definitions are needed to evaluate prognostic models and triage strategies.

Discussion of the Results in Relation to Previous Findings

Our finding of low mortality despite substantial physiologic heterogeneity aligns with contemporary reports from high-income settings (4–6), where protocolized DKA care yields favorable short-term outcomes. The absence of a clear mortality gradient across ICU day 1 pH-defined strata is also consistent with previous multicenter studies that did not restrict the analyses to ICU cohorts (3). Extending these insights to an ICU-admitted population using a standardized national registry, we observed that ICU LOS was longer in strata with more severe DKA, whereas in-hospital mortality was similarly low across severity groups. These associations suggest that DKA severity may be more strongly associated with longer ICU LOS than with in-hospital mortality, although this interpretation requires prospective validation.

Selection on ICU day 1 likely attenuates mortality gradients across pH strata; once triaged to the ICU, patients are enriched for physiologic instability, diluting pH-based differences while preserving gaps in resource needs. Contemporary ICU data support this finding; hyperlactatemia is independently associated with higher in-hospital death and greater use of inotropes and renal replacement therapy (adjusted odds ratio, 1.79; 95% CI, 1.12–2.84) (13), emphasizing organ dysfunction and treatment intensity over pH severity only. Notably, in an emergency department (ED) cohort, admission lactate levels showed excellent discrimination for mortality (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, 0.91; optimal cutoff > 3.5 mmol/L), whereas admission pH in that ED cohort did not differ between survivors and nonsurvivors, again pointing beyond pH to global physiologic stress (14). Accordingly, these descriptive findings suggest that a multidimensional approach incorporating organ dysfunction markers—such as lactate levels and the need for invasive mechanical ventilation—alongside ICU day 1 pH may be informative; prospective cohort studies are needed to evaluate this hypothesis.

In our cohort, euDKA accounted for 7.5% of ICU-admitted patients with DKA, which is comparable to previous reports (15–18). In-hospital and ICU mortality were numerically higher in the euDKA group, although the differences were numerically small and no formal hypothesis testing was performed, whereas ICU and hospital LOS tended to be shorter. These findings suggest that euDKA should not be regarded as a benign variant of DKA: despite a relatively rapid biochemical recovery once the precipitating factor is corrected, a subset of patients present with sufficient physiologic instability to warrant ICU admission and face a non-negligible risk of death (15, 18–22). Clinically, euDKA often arises in the context of sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitor use, prolonged fasting, pregnancy, chronic liver disease, heavy alcohol consumption, or recent insulin administration, and may lack marked hyperglycemia, which can delay diagnosis (12, 15, 19–22). Therefore, maintaining a high index of suspicion and promptly identifying underlying precipitants remain crucial. Given the modest sample size, the very small number of severe euDKA cases, and the post hoc nature of this analysis, our data should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating; larger, prospectively characterized cohorts are required to clarify the true prognostic profile of euDKA in the ICU setting (15–18, 22).

Implications for Clinical Practice and Future Research

These descriptive findings suggest two potential directions for clinical practice and research. First, future triage and risk-stratification efforts for ICU-admitted patients with DKA may benefit from incorporating more extensive markers of organ dysfunction, such as lactate levels and the need for invasive mechanical ventilation, as these indicators better reflect mortality risk and ICU resource demand, although prospective validation is needed before these markers are adopted for clinical decision-making. Second, the observed association between more severe DKA and longer ICU LOS—even in the absence of a clear mortality gradient—raises the hypothesis that care planning for DKA may need to account for prolonged resource use, which warrants investigation in prospective studies with adequate power.

Future research should: 1) develop and externally validate multivariate risk tools tailored to ICU-admitted patients with DKA using readily available physiologic and laboratory variables (with decision curves and calibration assessments); 2) quantify inter-institutional practice variability and protocol adherence, identifying modifiable drivers of ICU use; and 3) evaluate longer-term, patient-centered outcomes, including readmissions, glycemic control, and quality of life, to complement short-term metrics.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. First, this was an observational study and is therefore susceptible to residual confounding and selection bias, including intercenter variability in ICU admission criteria. Second, severity categorization relied on registry-recorded arterial blood pH during ICU day 1. Because the dataset does not reliably capture the exact sampling time within that window, assigning severity based on the lowest pH on ICU day 1 may not reflect the true admission-time status and could partially conflate initial severity with early physiologic evolution and treatment response. In addition, pH-based stratification may not capture other clinically relevant dimensions of hyperglycemic crises (e.g., effective osmolality, ketone burden, and precipitating causes). Third, DKA diagnoses were site-entered without centralized adjudication or uniform biochemical verification. Bicarbonate, anion gap, and ketone measurements are not captured in the current dataset; therefore, diagnostic misclassification cannot be excluded, including mixed DKA–hyperosmolar hyperglycemic syndrome phenotypes or nondiabetes-related ketoacidosis. This concern is particularly relevant for euDKA, where confirmation of ketonemia/ketonuria is essential. Fourth, treatment strategies and protocol adherence were not standardized across sites, which could have influenced outcomes and resource utilization. Fifth, lowest arterial pH recorded during ICU day 1 was missing in 7.4% of records; therefore, the primary severity-stratified summaries used a complete-case approach. To assess whether exclusion due to missing pH could introduce selection bias, we compared available characteristics between records with missing vs. nonmissing ICU day 1 pH (Supplemental Table 1, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B663). Across observed variables, the two groups were broadly similar in age and sex, while the missing pH gro

up tended to show lower severity scores and lower early use of invasive mechanical ventilation. These patterns are consistent with missingness that may be approximately random with respect to several observed variables, although the missingness mechanism cannot be confirmed. If missing pH was related to unmeasured factors or clinical workflows (i.e., not missing at random), the analytic cohort may over-represent more severe cases, potentially inflating estimates of overall severity and ICU resource use. In addition, catecholamine dose category variables had the highest missingness (~11.2%), whereas missingness in other key variables was limited (Supplemental Table 2, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B663).

Sixth, the severe DKA subgroup was relatively small (overall n = 70; within euDKA, severe n = 2), limiting precision for mortality estimates and reducing the ability to exclude clinically meaningful differences across severity strata. With only 35 in-hospital deaths overall and 4 deaths in the severe group, this study was substantially underpowered to detect clinically meaningful differences in mortality across severity strata. The probability of type II error is therefore high, and the absence of a clear mortality gradient across ICU day 1 pH-defined strata should not be interpreted as evidence of no clinically meaningful effect. Relatedly, the database did not systematically capture SGLT2 inhibitor exposure (medication history or timing), precluding assessment of whether individual euDKA cases were SGLT2 inhibitor-related and limiting etiologic interpretation of this subgroup.

Seventh, JIPAD does not capture detailed resource utilization metrics such as frequency of blood pressure or glucose measurements, number, route of administration, and titration of medications (including high-risk titratable drugs requiring more frequent monitoring), or frequency of nursing assessment and interventions; therefore, ICU LOS was used as the sole proxy for resource consumption in this study. Whether LOS adequately reflects care intensity in this patient population remains uncertain, and future studies using datasets with richer resource-use variables are needed to characterize post-admission resource consumption more fully.

Finally, outcomes were restricted to the index hospitalization; long-term sequelae and readmissions were not assessed. Despite these limitations, the use of an ICU-specific nationwide dataset and a consistent descriptive analytical approach strengthens the internal validity of these epidemiologic summaries.

CONCLUSIONS

In Japanese ICUs, in-hospital mortality from DKA was low and similar across ICU day 1 pH-defined severity strata, whereas ICU LOS was longer in patients with severe DKA. These descriptive findings suggest that organ dysfunction markers—such as lactate levels and need for invasive mechanical ventilation—may warrant consideration alongside ICU day 1 pH in future triage research, although prospective validation is needed.
