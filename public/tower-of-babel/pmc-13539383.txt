Dopant-controlled polysulfide anchoring on MoS2/graphene bilayers: insights from a complementary d-band–NMR descriptor framework
Authors: Thi Nhan Tran, Nguyen Vo Anh Duy, Trong Nhan Duong, Nguyen Truong Long, Viet Bac Thi Phung, Truc Anh Nguyen, Nguyen To Van, Nguyen Van Nghia, Yoshiyuki Kawazoe, Minh Triet Dang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rational design of sulfur hosts for room-temperature sodium–sulfur batteries requires simultaneous control over polysulfide anchoring and redox kinetics, yet a unified descriptor linking electronic structure to interfacial behavior remains elusive. Here, we establish a descriptor-level framework that integrates d-band theory with nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) signatures to elucidate dopant-controlled polysulfide interactions in MoS2/graphene bilayers. Using first-principles calculations, we show that heteroatom doping (B, P, and N) systematically modulates the Mo d-band center, governing the strength of Mo–S hybridization and interfacial charge transfer. These electronic perturbations are complemented by site-resolved NMR parameters, providing experimentally accessible fingerprints of local bonding environments and adsorption strength. We identify distinct dopant-dependent regimes: B doping promotes strong covalent hybridization and electronic activation, P doping achieves an optimal balance between adsorption and charge transport, and N doping induces pronounced charge localization and polarization. This unified descriptor framework consistently explains adsorption energetics, charge redistribution, and catalytic activity. Furthermore, dopant-induced electronic tuning lowers Na2S decomposition barriers, while selective dopants promote Na-ion diffusion, thereby accelerating redox kinetics. Our results demonstrate that the d-band center and NMR signatures act as complementary electronic descriptors that consistently explain polysulfide anchoring, charge redistribution, and catalytic activity, providing a generalizable strategy for designing electronically and chemically optimized sulfur hosts for advanced sodium–sulfur batteries.

Introduction

The rapid development of flexible electronics and renewable energy technologies has created a pressing demand for low-cost, high-energy-density, and fast-charging energy storage systems. Although lithium-ion batteries dominate the current market due to their high efficiency and long cycle life, concerns over the limited abundance and uneven distribution of lithium resources challenge their long-term sustainability. Consequently, alternative battery chemistries are being intensively explored. Among them, room-temperature sodium–sulfur (Na–S) batteries1–4 have emerged as promising candidates owing to their high theoretical capacity, low cost, natural abundance, and environmental friendliness. Notably, they can deliver theoretical energy densities up to 2600 Wh kg−1, far exceeding those of conventional Li-ion systems,5 enabled by multi-electron redox reactions of sulfur species.3,6–9 These advantages make Na–S batteries particularly attractive for large-scale, grid-level energy storage applications. However, their practical deployment is impeded by several intrinsic challenges. The most critical is the shuttle effect, arising from the dissolution and migration of high-order sodium polysulfides (Na2S4–Na2S8),8,10–12 which triggers parasitic reactions at the anode, leading to active material loss, low Coulombic efficiency, and rapid capacity decay.13–15 In addition, the extremely low electrical conductivity of sulfur (∼5 × 10−30 S cm−1) results in poor charge transport, high polarization, and severe performance degradation.16 To address these issues, conductive sulfur-host materials with strong affinity for sodium polysulfides (Na2Sx) have been widely employed. Effective hosts should provide binding interactions stronger than solvent–polysulfide interactions to suppress dissolution and mitigate the shuttle effect,11,17 while simultaneously ensuring high electrical conductivity for fast charge transfer. Therefore, the rational design of multifunctional hosts that integrate strong polysulfide confinement with efficient electron transport is crucial for achieving high-performance Na–S batteries.

Two-dimensional transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs) possess intrinsically polar surfaces that enable strong chemical interactions with lithium-sulfur8,18,19 and sodium–sulfur5,20,21 clusters, and promote their catalytic conversion, making them attractive sulfur hosts. However, their relatively low electrical conductivity and limited cycling stability restrict practical performance.22–24 In contrast, graphene offers exceptional conductivity and a large surface area but exhibits weak interactions with polysulfides due to its nonpolar nature.9,25 To overcome these limitations, TMD/graphene heterostructures have emerged as an effective design strategy that combines strong polysulfide binding with efficient electron transport. Such hybrid architectures synergistically combine strong polysulfide affinity with high electrical conductivity, enabling effective Na2Sx confinement and rapid electron transport, thereby suppressing the shuttle effect and enhancing electrochemical performance.26 Representative systems, including C3N/graphene27 and Nb3O8/graphene,28 demonstrate enhanced capacity and redox kinetics, highlighting the potential of TMD/graphene superlattices as advanced sulfur host materials.

MoS2/graphene heterostructures have been successfully synthesized using hydrothermal and in situ growth methods and exhibit excellent structural stability and electrochemical performance.29,30 As prototypical van der Waals hybrids, they integrate the complementary electronic properties of MoS2 and graphene while largely preserving the intrinsic characteristics of both components through weak interlayer coupling.31–34 Consequently, the intrinsic band structures of both graphene and MoS2 are largely preserved upon stacking, enabling the coexistence of graphene's high carrier mobility and the semiconducting character of MoS2. Moreover, these heterostructures also offer substantial structural tunability, as their electronic and catalytic properties can be engineered through variations in composition, surface functionalization, and layer thickness. Benefiting from high electrical conductivity, large specific surface area, and abundant active sites, these hybrids have been extensively explored as sulfur hosts. Compared with individual components, they exhibit enhanced polysulfide adsorption and improved electrochemical stability.35 Such structural tunability makes these heterostructures highly attractive for advanced battery applications, particularly in sulfur-based energy storage systems.

Despite these advantages, further enhancing the electrical conductivity of MoS2/graphene heterostructures and their affinity toward Na2Sx species remains important. Substitutional doping of surface S sites with B, N, and P provides an effective strategy for modulating the electronic structure of MoS2 and creating active sites for Na2Sx anchoring. Such doping has been experimentally demonstrated using remote N2 plasma treatment for N doping (up to ∼8.33% atomic concentration),36 CMOS-compatible plasma immersion ion implantation for P doping,37 and one-step hydrothermal method for B doping.38 The proposed B-, N-, and P-doped MoS2/graphene heterostructures are therefore experimentally feasible with existing fabrication techniques.

In this work, we systematically elucidate how heteroatom doping (B, P, and N) regulates polysulfide adsorption, interfacial electronic structure, and reaction kinetics in MoS2/graphene heterostructures using first-principles calculations. Heteroatom substitution induces pronounced electronic restructuring, including an upward shift of the Mo d-band center toward the Fermi level and the formation of dopant-induced electronic states, thereby strengthening interfacial hybridization and Na2Sx adsorption. The resulting adsorption behavior is governed by dopant-specific bonding, charge redistribution, and electronic polarization, which are further characterized by complementary NMR signatures of the local electronic environments. Distinct functional roles emerge for the three dopants: B promotes strong polysulfide anchoring and rapid Na-ion migration, P provides a favorable balance between adsorption, charge transfer, and Na2S decomposition, whereas N induces pronounced charge localization and interfacial polarization. Kinetic calculations further show that all three dopants lower the Na2S decomposition barrier, with P exhibiting the strongest catalytic enhancement, while B most effectively promotes Na-ion diffusion. These results establish heteroatom doping as an effective strategy for independently tuning polysulfide confinement, interfacial electronic coupling, and redox kinetics in MoS2/graphene sulfur hosts.

Computational details

We conduct density functional theory (DFT) calculations as implemented in the Vienna Ab initio simulation package (VASP),39,40 employing the projector-augmented wave (PAW) method41 within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) using the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) exchange-correlation functional.42 These calculations are conducted to investigate the adsorption behavior of Na2Sx clusters (x = 1–8) and S8 on N/B/P-doped MoS2/graphene heterostructures. To accurately describe interlayer interactions between graphene and doped MoS2 monolayers, van der Waals corrections are included.43 In addition, to better capture the localized electronic interactions associated with Mo d orbitals, DFT + U calculations with dispersion corrections are performed.

Based on previous reports indicating that a 4 : 5 MoS2/graphene bilayer exhibits minimal lattice mismatch compared to other configurations (e.g., 3 : 4 geometry34), we construct a heterostructure consisting of a 4 × 4 × 1 supercell of doped MoS2 stacked on a 5 × 5 × 1 graphene supercell. Brillouin zone sampling is carried out using Monkhorst–Pack k-point meshes of 4 × 4 × 1 for structural relaxation and 8 × 8 × 1 for electronic structure calculations. A plane-wave energy cutoff of 500 eV is employed throughout. Structural optimization was considered converged when the total energy difference between successive steps was below 10−5 eV, and the Hellmann–Feynman forces on each atom was less than 0.01 eV Å−1. A vacuum layer of 25 Å is introduced along the out-of-plane direction to eliminate spurious interactions between periodic images.

To determine the most stable adsorption configurations, multiple adsorption sites and rotational orientations of Na2Sx clusters are systematically sampled. The initial distance between the clusters and the substrate surface was set to 2.5 Å. More negative adsorption energies indicate stronger binding and higher stability. For each Na2Sx species, multiple configurations are evaluated, and only the most stable structures are considered for further analysis.

Finally, diffusion energy barriers for Na2S and Na ions on the heterostructures were evaluated using the climbing-image nudged elastic band (CI-NEB) method.47,48

A finite-difference k-space derivative step of 10−3 was employed to evaluate the magnetic susceptibility derivatives. The plane-wave cutoff energy was set to 500 eV, and the self-consistent field (SCF) convergence criterion was tightened to 10−6 eV to ensure accurate shielding tensors. Results and discussion

Adsorption of Na2Sx clusters on doped MoS2/graphene heterostructure

To investigate the adsorption of Na2Sx clusters on N-, B-, P-doped MoS2/graphene heterostructures, we first optimize the geometries of pristine graphene and MoS2 monolayers using the optB88-vdW functional.53 For the optimized 4 × 4 × 1 MoS2 supercell, the lattice parameters are a = b = 12.67 Å, α = β = 90°, δ = 120°, with a Mo–S bond length of 2.42 Å. For the 5 × 5 × 1 graphene supercell, the lattice constants are a = b = 9.83 Å, α = β = 90°, δ = 120°, with a C–C bond length of 1.42 Å. These values are in good agreement with previously reported theoretical results.27,34 Subsequently, a MoS2/graphene heterostructure is constructed by stacking the 4 × 4 × 1 MoS2 monolayer on a 5 × 5 × 1 graphene sheet, followed by full structure relaxation, as shown in Fig. S1(a). The optimized heterostructure exhibits in-plane lattice parameters of a = b = 12.46 Å, α = β = 90°, δ = 120°. The C–C bond increases slightly to 1.44 Å (∼1.4% expansion relative to pristine graphene), while the Mo–S bond length decreases marginally to 2.41 Å (∼0.4% contraction), indicating weak interlayer coupling. The interlayer spacing of the MoS2/graphene heterostructure is evaluated using four different vdW functionals (Table 1). The optB88-vdW functional yields an interlayer distance of 3.40 Å, in excellent agreement with experimental measurements54 and comparable to values reported for bulk graphite55 and related van der Waals heterostructures.32,56 Based on this agreement, the optB88-vdW was adopted for all subsequent calculations. At this level of theory, the optimized MoS2/graphene heterostructure exhibits a thickness of 6.59 Å and a volume of 3650.3 Å3. The calculated binding energy is −1.809 eV (−36.2 meV per atom), which is slightly higher than the value obtained using conventional GGA methods (∼27 meV per atom)57 and lower than that reported for WS2/graphene heterostructures (51.8 meV per atom).58 The negative binding energy confirms the exothermic nature of the interaction and demonstrates the structural stability of the MoS2/graphene heterostructure.

In the MoS2/graphene heterostructure, graphene serves as the underlying substrate and growth backbone for MoS2, (ref. 29) whereas the polar MoS2 surface provides active sites for Na2Sx adsorption. Accordingly, we considered substitutional doping of surface S sites with B, N, or P to directly modulate the adsorption properties of the exposed MoS2 surface. Substitution at C sites in graphene or S sites at the MoS2/graphene interface was not considered because these sites do not directly participate in surface Na2Sx adsorption. In our model, one of the 32 S atoms in the MoS2 supercell was replaced by a B, N, or P atom, corresponding to an atomic concentration (at%) of 3.125 at%. This value is lower than the experimentally demonstrated N concentration of up to ∼8.33 at%,36 further supporting the experimental feasibility of the proposed B-, N-, and P-doped MoS2/graphene heterostructures. Upon introducing B, P, or N dopants, the MoS2/graphene heterostructures exhibit negligible changes in the in-plane lattice constants (Table 2). The thickness h slightly decreases to 6.54–6.56 Å, accompanied by a minor reduction in the geometric volume (<3%). These results indicate that the overall structural framework of the MoS2/G heterostructure is well preserved upon doping. To elucidate the adsorption mechanism of Na2Sx clusters, we first determine their stable geometries via full structural optimization (Fig. S2). The Na–S bond length (dNa–S) increases from 2.41 Å in Na2S to 3.12 Å in Na2S8, whereas the S–S bond length (dS–S) remains nearly constant at ∼2.10 Å. These values are consistent with our previous study on Na2Sx clusters adsorbed on Tim+1CmO2 MXenes,5,47 confirming the reliability of the computational approach.

As a reference, we first examine Na2Sx adsorption on pristine MoS2/G. The optimized configurations are shown in Fig. 1, while those on B-, P-, and N-doped MoS2/G are presented in Fig. 2, S3, and S4, respectively. On pristine MoS2/G, Na2Sx clusters preferentially adopt configurations in which the Na atoms orient toward the surface, forming Na–S interactions with the substrate, whereas the sulfur atoms point away from the interface. In contrast, doping significantly modifies adsorption behavior. The Na2Sx clusters preferentially anchor at dopant sites, where an additional covalent bond forms between a sulfur atom in the cluster and the dopant atom. This interaction coexists with Na–S bonding between the cluster and the surface sulfur atoms of the heterostructure. Exceptions are observed for Na2S3 on P- and N-doped MoS2/G, and for Na2S3 and Na2S6 on N-doped MoS2/G, where alternative configurations are energetically favored. For pristine MoS2/G, the adsorption energies of Na2Sx clusters range from −1.655 to −0.221 eV, depending on sulfur content. The corresponding minimum interfacial distances vary from 1.97 to 2.80 Å. Upon doping, the adsorption strength is markedly enhanced (Table 3). For instance, the adsorption energy of Na2S on B-doped MoS2/G reaches −4.823 eV, representing an increase of approximately 3.0 times compared to the pristine system. This enhancement is accompanied by a pronounced reduction in the interfacial distance; the minimum separation decreases from 2.41 Å (pristine) to 1.67, 2.05, and 1.41 Å for B-, P-, and N-doped systems, respectively. These results demonstrate that dopant atoms act as highly active anchoring sites, significantly strengthening the interaction with Na2Sx species. Such enhanced binding is expected to effectively suppress the shuttle effect of sodium polysulfides during electrochemical cycling, thereby improving the stability and electrochemical performance of MoS2/G-based sodium–sulfur batteries.

The volumetric variation of electrode materials induced by Na2Sx intercalation plays a critical role in determining the long-term cycling stability of sodium–sulfur batteries. Previous studies have shown that reduced graphene oxide (rGO) frameworks provide high electrical conductivity and mechanical resilience, enabling effective accommodation of volume changes during electrochemical cycling while preserving structural integrity and continuous electron transport networks.59,60 To quantitatively assess this effect, we evaluated the relative volume change of pristine and doped MoS2/graphene (MoS2/G) heterostructures upon Na2Sx intercalation with respect to the corresponding unintercalated systems, defined as:3where Vinter and Vuninter denote the volumes of the heterostructure with and without adsorbed Na2Sx clusters, respectively. As summarized in Table 3, both pristine and doped MoS2/G heterostructures exhibit a slight volume contraction upon Na2Sx adsorption. For the pristine system, the volume reduction ranges from 2.6% to 7.1%, which is markedly smaller than the volumetric changes commonly reported for conventional electrode materials such as LiCoO2, (ref. 61) and layered NMC,62 where lattice expansion/contraction on the order of ∼10% can occur during repeated (de)intercalation. This limited structural deformation highlights the intrinsic mechanical robustness of the MoS2/G heterostructure and supports its suitability as a stable electrode material for sodium–sulfur batteries. Importantly, dopant incorporation further suppresses volume variation, reducing it to a narrow range of 0.1–4.4%. This diminished structural fluctuation indicates that dopant engineering enhances the geometric stability of the MoS2/G framework during repeated charge–discharge cycles, thereby contributing to improved structural integrity and extended operational lifetime. In addition to volumetric stability, the interfacial spacing of the MoS2/G heterostructure plays a key role in governing Na-ion insertion and diffusion kinetics. Our results show that Na2Sx adsorption induces negligible changes or a slight reduction in the interlayer distance for both pristine and doped systems. As listed in Table 3, the interfacial spacing remains within the range of 3.31–3.40 Å, which is favorable for Na-ion transport across the heterointerface. The combination of minimal volume variation and preserved interfacial spacing ensures structural stability during electrochemical cycling. Collectively, these results demonstrate that doped MoS2/G heterostructures possess the structural robustness and geometric characteristics required for high-performance sodium–sulfur battery electrodes.

Electronic properties of Na2Sx clusters adsorbed on doped MoS2/graphene heterostructures

Upon vertical stacking of a MoS2 nanosheet on a graphene layer to form the heterostructure, both components largely preserve their intrinsic electronic characteristics, as shown in Fig. S1. The MoS2 layer retains a band gap of 1.789 eV, while graphene exhibits its characteristic semi-metallic band structure with a negligible band gap of ∼3.4 meV, in good agreement with previous reports.33,34 As a result, the MoS2/graphene heterostructure exhibits an overall semi-metallic electronic character, as illustrated in Fig. 3a and S1, consistent with earlier studies.33,34 The Fermi level is located near the Dirac point of graphene and remains well separated from the valence and conduction bands of MoS2, in agreement with experimental observations from XPS measurements.54 The highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO), shown in Fig. 4a, is primarily composed of pz orbitals from the graphene layer, indicating that charge carriers are predominantly localized within graphene. This finding confirms that the high electronic conductivity of the MoS2/graphene heterostructure is mainly governed by the graphene component. Furthermore, only a negligible charge transfer (∼0.061e) from MoS2 to graphene is observed, consistent with experimental results.54

Introducing B, P, or N dopants significantly modifies the electronic structure of the MoS2/G heterostructure, as evidenced by the downward shift of the Fermi level from the Dirac point toward the valence band maximum of MoS2 (Fig. 3c, e, and g). Concurrently, the density of states (DOS) near the Fermi level increases for both MoS2 and graphene, indicating enhanced electronic states available for charge transport. This increase suggests improved carrier mobility and electrical conductivity, which is advantageous for electrode applications in sodium–sulfur batteries. Doping also introduces localized impurity states within the band gap of MoS2, effectively reducing the band gap to 1.463 eV (B doping), 1.687 eV (P doping), and 1.472 eV (N doping). These impurity states are located near the Fermi level and, in some cases, cross it, thereby contributing additional electronic states that facilitate charge transport. The presence of such states enhances electrical conductivity by enabling electronic transitions between valence and impurity levels.

The formation of these impurity states can be attributed to substitutional doping at sulfur sites. When an S atom is replaced by a B, P, or N atom, the dopant introduces acceptor-like behavior, attracting electron density from neighboring atoms due to its distinct electronegativity and valence configuration. This interaction induces localized electronic states near the Fermi level, consistent with the charge transfer observed from both MoS2 and graphene to the dopant atoms, as confirmed by Bader charge analysis (Table 4). Among the dopants, nitrogen exhibits the largest electron gain, reflecting its higher electronegativity relative to boron and phosphorus. Notably, the impurity bands intersect the Fermi level along the M-K direction, indicating that enhanced conductivity is primarily associated with these dopant-induced states. The highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) at the K-point (Fig. 4b–d) reveals delocalized charge distribution involving Mo d orbitals and dopant p orbitals, indicating strong hybridization. This orbital overlap facilitates efficient charge transport through the MoS2 layer, while contributions from graphene are comparatively less pronounced in these impurity states. Generally, dopant-induced electronic modulation enhances charge carrier density and transport pathways, thereby improving electron transfer kinetics. This effect is expected to accelerate electrochemical reaction rates and contribute to improved performance of MoS2/G-based electrodes during charge–discharge cycling.

To elucidate the influence of dopants on the Na2Sx intercalation mechanism in MoS2/G heterostructures, we systematically analyze the band structures, pDOS, and Bader charge distributions of Na2S-intercalated pristine and doped systems. For the pristine MoS2/G heterostructure with Na2S adsorption, the system retains a semi-metallic character with a negligible band gap of ∼3.4 meV, similar to that of the pristine MoS2/G. Upon Na2S intercalation, several weakly dispersive states emerge between the graphene Dirac cone and the valence band maximum of MoS2 (Fig. 3b). These states originate from hybridization between the electronic orbitals of the Na2S cluster and the MoS2 layer, indicating moderate interfacial interaction. Notably, the highest intercalation-induced state at the K-point lies near the Fermi level and acts as an acceptor-like state. As illustrated in Fig. 4e, this state involves contributions from S pz orbitals in the Na2S cluster and Mo d orbitals in the MoS2 layer, facilitating charge redistribution. The presence of such states promotes electronic transitions and enhances carrier transport by enabling charge excitation from the valence band, thereby contributing to improved conductivity upon intercalation. Bader charge analysis reveals a net charge transfer of 0.814e from the Na2S cluster to the MoS2/G heterostructure (Table 5), consistent with the charge redistribution observed in the charge density difference map (Fig. 5a). Despite this overall electron donation, significant charge accumulation is localized around the sulfur atom within the Na2S cluster, indicating a non-uniform charge distribution at the interface.

For doped MoS2/G heterostructures, the dopant-induced impurity states are significantly modified upon Na2S intercalation. These states exhibit strong contributions from both the intercalated Na2S cluster and the MoS2 layer, with pronounced pDOS near the Fermi level (Fig. 3e–h). This behavior indicates that the interaction between the cluster and the heterostructure is dominated by dopant-cluster coupling. Specifically, strong interactions between the dopant atom and the Na2S cluster lead to the formation of covalent bonds between the dopant and sulfur atoms in the cluster, consistent with optimized geometries. As a result, Na2S preferentially anchors at dopant sites, exhibiting significantly enhanced binding strength compared to the pristine system. The dopants act as electron-accepting centers, attracting charge from neighboring atoms and facilitating charge transfer from the Na2S cluster to the heterostructure. This process gives rise to distinct charge accumulation and depletion regions, as illustrated in Fig. 5. Such charge redistribution underpins the enhanced adsorption energies and strong anchoring of Na2S at dopant sites. Quantitatively, the charge transfer from Na2S to the heterostructure increases from 0.814e (pristine) to 1.109e for B doping and up to 2.139e for N doping (Table 5), indicating substantially stronger interfacial interactions. Notably, doping also promotes charge transfer to the graphene layer. For example, in the case of P doping, the charge transfer to graphene increases from 0.030e to 0.475e (∼15.8-fold enhancement), thereby improving the electrical conductivity of both the MoS2 and graphene components. This enhanced charge transfer facilitates faster carrier transport and is expected to accelerate electrochemical reaction kinetics in sodium–sulfur batteries.

To further elucidate the role of doping in polysulfide intercalation, we analyze the electronic structures of Na2Sx (x = 3, 5, 8) adsorbed on B-doped MoS2/G (Fig. S5), along with Bader charge analysis for Na2S6 on pristine and doped systems (Table 5). The results reveal pronounced orbital hybridization between the Na2Sx clusters and dopant atoms at impurity states near the Fermi level and the graphene Dirac point, consistent with the behavior observed for Na2S adsorption. This confirms that dopant-cluster interactions dominate the intercalation mechanism. Consequently, higher-order sodium polysulfides preferentially anchor at dopant sites, as shown in Fig. 3, S3, and S4, with significantly enhanced binding energies. This strong anchoring effectively suppresses the shuttle effect during charge–discharge cycling. Furthermore, Bader charge analysis indicates that doping promotes electron transfer to the graphene layer, contrasting with the pristine case, thereby enhancing graphene conductivity and further facilitating charge transport within the intercalated systems.

To elucidate Mo–polysulfide interactions from a catalytic perspective, we perform d-band center analysis for pristine and heteroatom-doped MoS2/G heterostructures, following the approach proposed in ref. 63. This analysis provides insight into how electronic structure modulation of the substrate governs Mo–S–adsorbate coupling and charge transfer. The d-band center serves as a key descriptor for Na2Sx adsorption on these bilayers. An optimally positioned d-band center enables sufficiently strong yet reversible chemisorption, thereby lowering reaction barriers and accelerating polysulfide conversion kinetics.63,64 In pristine MoS2/graphene, the deep d-band center (εd = −2.06 eV, Table 6) reflects a low density of Mo d-states near the Fermi level, indicative of weak adsorbate-surface hybridization. Heteroatom doping induces a pronounced upward shift of εd toward EF (−0.95 eV for B, −0.90 eV for P, and −0.99 eV for N), thereby increasing the availability of active d-states. Within the d-band/Newns-Anderson framework,63,64 this shift enhances orbital overlap with sulfur species and strengthens interfacial coupling. Concurrently, doping elevates the vacuum level and lowers EF, resulting in increased work functions (5.74–5.93 eV) relative to the pristine system (4.74 eV), consistent with enhanced surface dipoles and an electron-withdrawing character that promotes charge polarization at the interface.

Mechanistically, the doping-induced upshift of εd activates Mo sites by enhancing d–p orbital overlap. Among the dopants, P introduces the strongest electronic perturbation, with a large εd shift upon adsorption and a significant rise in EF, placing the system in a strong-coupling regime characterized by pronounced charge redistribution. In contrast, B doping yields a relatively higher post-adsorption εd (−1.20 eV) and moderate work function reduction, corresponding to an intermediate coupling regime that balances adsorption strength and reversibility. N doping, while inducing the deepest εd after adsorption (−2.20 eV), suggests stronger electronic stabilization of adsorbates, potentially at the expense of optimal conversion kinetics. In short, heteroatom doping provides an effective lever to modulate the Mo d-band position and surface electrostatics, thereby governing hybridization strength, charge transfer, and adsorption energetics of sulfur species. This electronic structure tuning generates undercoordinated, electronically activated Mo sites that optimize the balance between adsorption and conversion, underpinning the enhanced catalytic behavior of doped MoS2/graphene heterostructures.

In pristine MoS2/graphene, S atoms exhibit δiso ∼ −170 ppm with large anisotropy (δcsa ∼ 400–420 ppm; ηcsa ∼ −0.96), reflecting a highly anisotropic electronic environment governed by directional Mo–S bonding. Mo atoms show even larger anisotropy (δcsa ∼ 1017–1038 ppm; ηcsa ∼ 0.84–0.86), consistent with the intrinsic anisotropy of Mo d orbitals. The nearly identical NMR parameters across Mo sites indicate electronic homogeneity, in line with weak interlayer coupling and minimal charge transfer. Heteroatom doping induces distinct, dopant-dependent electronic modulation. In B-doped systems, Mo sites shift to more negative δiso (∼−539 to −641 ppm) with reduced asymmetry (ηcsa ∼ 0.40–0.44), indicating enhanced shielding and a more symmetric electronic environment. This reflects strong B–S covalent hybridization and charge delocalization, further supported by the large positive δiso of B (∼510 ppm). P doping instead yields moderate shielding (δiso ∼ −396 to −436 ppm) but increased anisotropy (δcsa ∼ 1130–1146 ppm; ηcsa ∼ 0.91–0.93), indicating pronounced electronic distortion driven by polarization rather than covalent bonding. The relatively small δcsa of P (∼286 ppm) suggests a locally symmetric dopant environment. In contrast, N doping produces the largest anisotropy (δcsa ∼ 1494–1503 ppm) with less negative δiso (∼−327 to −353 ppm), reflecting reduced shielding and strong charge polarization due to the high electronegativity of N. The N site itself remains relatively isotropic (δcsa ∼ 99 ppm), consistent with localized electron accumulation. These NMR results reveal three distinct electronic modulation mechanisms induced by dopants: (i) B doping promotes charge delocalization and symmetric electronic environments through strong covalent hybridization, (ii) P doping induces anisotropic distortion and polarization-driven electronic restructuring, and (iii) N doping generates strong charge localization and polarization effects due to its high electronegativity.

Upon Na2S adsorption, all systems exhibit pronounced changes in NMR parameters, reflecting strong adsorbate-substrate electronic coupling. These trends are fully consistent with the adsorption-induced downward shift of the d-band center and enhanced charge transfer discussed in Section 3.2. For pristine MoS2/G, Na2S adsorption leads to moderate redistribution of electronic density. The S atom in Na2S shows a dramatic upshift in δiso (∼−6 ppm) and reduced anisotropy (δcsa ∼ 240 ppm), indicating strong local charge accumulation and partial screening. Meanwhile, Mo sites exhibit reduced anisotropy (δcsa ∼ 890–907 ppm) compared to the pristine case, consistent with adsorbate-induced d-band broadening and partial occupation of antibonding states. This aligns with the modest upward shift of EF and relatively weak d-band perturbation.

In B-doped MoS2/G, the effect is significantly amplified. Mo sites display strongly negative δiso values (∼−517 to −636 ppm) and very large anisotropy (δcsa ∼ 1322–1336 ppm), indicating enhanced Mo–S orbital hybridization and strong electronic anisotropy. The B site exhibits near-zero δiso (∼−1 ppm) and low δcsa (∼90 ppm), suggesting substantial electronic redistribution and direct involvement in bonding. These features correlate with the relatively shallow post-adsorption d-band center (εd ∼ −1.20 eV), characteristic of intermediate coupling, where strong hybridization coexists with partial reversibility. For P-doped systems, Mo sites show even stronger δiso shifts (∼−685 to −864 ppm) with anisotropy spanning δcsa ∼ 745–1193 ppm, indicating substantial but spatially heterogeneous electronic restructuring. The P atom exhibits a large positive δiso (∼136 ppm) with moderate anisotropy, reflecting polarization-driven interaction. These results are consistent with the pronounced d-band downshift and Fermi-level upshift (EF ∼ 0.13 eV), placing the system in a strong-coupling regime characterized by significant charge transfer and electronic rehybridization.

In contrast, N-doped MoS2/G shows a distinct response dominated by polarization effects. Mo sites exhibit the largest anisotropy (δcsa ∼ 1457–1546 ppm) but less negative δiso (∼−160 to −333 ppm), indicating reduced shielding but extreme electronic anisotropy. The N site itself shows moderate δiso (∼91 ppm) with relatively high δcsa (∼379 ppm), reflecting localized electron accumulation. This behavior is consistent with the deep post-adsorption d-band center (εd ≈ −2.20 eV), indicative of strong electronic stabilization and localization, which enhances binding but may limit optimal catalytic turnover.

In short, Na2S adsorption induces pronounced, dopant-dependent NMR shifts that directly reflect adsorbate-substrate electronic coupling and track the evolution of the Mo d-band center. B- and P-doped systems exhibit large δiso shifts and enhanced anisotropy, consistent with strong hybridization and significant d-band modulation, whereas N doping yields highly anisotropic but more localized electronic environments associated with a deeper post-adsorption d-band. These results demonstrate that the d-band center and NMR parameters constitute complementary descriptors of the adsorption process. While the d-band center quantifies the intrinsic electronic activation of Mo sites governing adsorbate-substrate hybridization, the NMR response provides site-specific fingerprints of the resulting local electronic environment and charge redistribution. Together, these descriptors consistently explain the dopant-dependent polysulfide anchoring behavior in MoS2/graphene heterostructures.

Reaction pathways of Na2S clusters on MoS2/graphene heterostructures

The electrocatalytic performance of Na–S batteries is strongly influenced by Na2S decomposition and subsequent Na+ diffusion on the sulfur host. During charging, the final discharge product Na2S is oxidized into NaS and Na+ (Na2S → NaS + Na+ + e−), and the associated activation barrier can limit oxidation kinetics and cycling performance. To evaluate Na2S decomposition kinetics, we calculated the energy barriers along two pathways (A and B) corresponding to the a- and b-axis directions, respectively (Fig. 6a and b), with the corresponding energy profiles shown in Fig. 6c and d. Compared with isolated Na2S in vacuum (2.44–2.69 eV),10,22 MoS2/G substantially lowers the barriers to 1.26–1.36 eV. These values are also lower than those reported for graphene (1.94 eV), S-substituted V-, Fe-, and Ni-doped MoS2 (1.44–1.88 eV),66 Mo2TiC2T2 (1.59 eV),17 and SA@rg-C3N4 (1.44–1.56 eV),67 demonstrating the favorable catalytic activity of MoS2/G toward Na2S decomposition.

Conclusions

In summary, we demonstrate that substitutional doping of MoS2/graphene heterostructures with B, P, and N provides an effective strategy for tuning polysulfide interactions and redox chemistry at the atomic scale in sodium–sulfur batteries. First-principles calculations reveal that dopant incorporation markedly strengthens the interaction with Na2Sx clusters through the formation of directional dopant-sulfur bonds and enhanced interfacial charge redistribution, thereby effectively suppressing polysulfide shuttling. Distinct dopant-dependent electronic modulation mechanisms are identified. B doping promotes strong covalent hybridization and charge delocalization, leading to symmetric electronic environments and robust chemical anchoring. P doping introduces pronounced electronic anisotropy and polarization, enabling balanced interaction strength. In contrast, N doping induces strong charge localization and interfacial polarization due to its high electronegativity, resulting in highly anisotropic electronic environments. These electronic trends are consistently reflected by NMR signatures, demonstrating that d-band analysis and NMR spectroscopy provide complementary descriptors of dopant-induced electronic modulation. Concurrently, dopant incorporation introduces p-type characteristics and shifts electronic states toward the Fermi level, enhancing electrical conductivity and facilitating charge transfer between Na2Sx species and the host. This unique combination of strengthened adsorption and improved electronic transport accelerates reaction kinetics while maintaining structural integrity during cycling. Overall, this work establishes a complementary descriptor framework linking electronic structure, local bonding environments, and electrochemical function, demonstrating that dopant engineering of MoS2/G heterostructures enables simultaneous control over polysulfide anchoring, charge transfer, and electronic conductivity. These findings provide a general design principle for advanced sulfur hosts and highlight heterostructure doping as a viable pathway toward high-performance sodium–sulfur energy storage systems.
