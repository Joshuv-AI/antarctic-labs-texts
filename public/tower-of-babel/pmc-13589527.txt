Machine Learning Prediction and Causal Forest Analysis of Severe Acute Kidney Injury in ICU Patients with COPD: A MIMIC-IV Study
Authors: LiFeng Fang, Fei Wang, Junkang Chen, Yulong Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSevere acute kidney injury (AKI) is frequent in critically ill patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), but predictive importance does not establish causality.ObjectiveTo model severe AKI (KDIGO stage 3), evaluate temporal robustness at a 24-hour ICU landmark, and explore adjusted exposure-effect estimates.MethodsThis retrospective MIMIC-IV v3.1 cohort included 4,705 adults with COPD. The original modeling dataset was split into training (n = 3,294) and testing (n = 1,411) sets. Consensus features from LASSO, Boruta, and random-forest importance informed 15 classifiers; the highest observed test-set AUC was interpreted with SHAP. A 24-hour landmark analysis excluded earlier stage 3 AKI and used pre-landmark predictors for incident stage 3 AKI thereafter. CausalForestDML estimated exploratory adjusted effects of the most recent valid creatinine within 365 days before ICU admission.ResultsSevere AKI occurred in 1,085 patients (23.06%). The original six-feature CatBoost model included total ICU length of stay and had an AUC of 0.848 (95% CI, 0.825–0.872); it is interpreted as a retrospective trajectory model. The landmark cohort included 4,171 patients and 841 events. The temporal five-feature AUC was 0.679 (95% CI, 0.641–0.717); without pre-ICU creatinine it was 0.686 (95% CI, 0.648–0.724; paired difference, −0.0067; P = 0.293). The adjusted creatinine risk difference was 0.0547 per 1 mg/dL (95% CI, −0.0021 to 0.1115) and 0.0705 (95% CI, 0.0022–0.1387) after 1st-99th percentile trimming.ConclusionThis study establishes a COPD-specific benchmark showing that a parsimonious model can characterize severe-AKI trajectories and that temporal separation materially changes performance and interpretation. Integrating model comparison, SHAP, adjusted-effect estimation, and landmark validation provides a rigorous framework for distinguishing prognostic importance from causal relevance and defines priorities for external validation.

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is common in intensive care unit (ICU) patients and is associated with mortality, prolonged hospitalization, and progression to chronic kidney disease.1–8 Patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) may be particularly vulnerable because acute respiratory failure, hypoxemia, hypercapnia, systemic inflammation, right-heart dysfunction, hemodynamic instability, infection, mechanical ventilation, diuretics, vasoactive drugs, and potentially nephrotoxic antimicrobials can converge during critical illness.9–15 For pulmonologists and critical-care clinicians, identifying patients at high renal risk may support closer renal monitoring and medication review, but prediction alone cannot identify modifiable causes.

Machine-learning models have improved AKI risk prediction in general ICU and disease-specific cohorts, with gradient-boosting algorithms often achieving strong discrimination.16–27 However, existing studies differ substantially in prediction horizon, endpoint definition, feature burden, interpretability, and validation strategy. A model that uses variables measured after or during outcome development can show high retrospective discrimination without serving as an admission-time prediction tool. Similarly, a variable that predicts AKI may reflect severity or incorporation bias rather than a modifiable causal pathway.

Causal machine-learning methods, including generalized random forests and double/debiased machine learning, can estimate adjusted and heterogeneous exposure-effect relationships under assumptions of temporal ordering, conditional exchangeability, positivity, and consistency.28–31 These methods reduce bias from flexible nuisance-model estimation, but they do not automatically solve unmeasured confounding, reverse causality, or overlap between predictor and outcome definitions. Their results in observational ICU data should therefore be interpreted as exploratory unless supported by temporally valid designs and external validation.

Evidence combining prediction and causal machine-learning analysis in ICU patients with COPD remains limited. The COPD-specific value of the present study lies in evaluating a parsimonious severe-AKI model in this high-risk population and in testing whether the variables with the greatest predictive importance also yield non-zero adjusted exposure-effect estimates.

This study had two objectives: first, to compare 15 algorithms and develop an interpretable model for severe AKI in ICU patients with COPD; and second, to use causal forest analysis exploratorily to assess whether predictive importance and adjusted effect estimates converged. We hypothesized that predictive importance and adjusted effect estimates would not necessarily coincide.

Methods

Study Design and Data Source

This retrospective cohort study used the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database, version 3.1.32 MIMIC-IV is a credentialed-access, deidentified electronic health record database maintained by the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and hosted on PhysioNet. It contains ICU and hospital data from Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center between 2008 and 2019. The collection of patient information and creation of the MIMIC-IV research resource were reviewed by the Institutional Review Board at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, which approved the data-sharing initiative and granted a waiver of informed consent. Database access was obtained by LiFeng Fang after completion of the required CITI Data or Specimens Only Research refresher course (completion record 78374867; completed 16 July 2026 and valid through 16 July 2029) and acceptance of the PhysioNet data use agreement. The present study used deidentified data only. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Huai’an Second People’s Hospital under approval no. HBYLL202572. The investigators had no direct interaction with participants and no access to identifiable private information. This study followed the Declaration of Helsinki, the PhysioNet data use agreement, and STROBE reporting guidance.33

Study Population

The study population consisted of adult ICU patients with COPD. COPD was identified by ICD-10 codes in the J44.x family during the index hospitalization. For exploratory phenotype analyses, patients were stratified as J44.1-coded acute exacerbation of COPD (AECOPD), J44.0-coded COPD with acute lower respiratory infection, or other J44.x-coded COPD. Absence of a J44.1 code was not interpreted as clinically stable COPD. The inclusion criteria were age 18 years or older, first ICU stay for the hospitalization, COPD diagnosis, and ICU length of stay of at least 24 hours. Patients with pre-existing end-stage renal disease or chronic dialysis were excluded. The final reproduced cohort comprised 4,705 patients (Supplementary Table S1).

Outcome Definition

The primary outcome in the original analysis was severe AKI, defined as KDIGO stage 3 during the ICU stay.34 Staging used serum creatinine criteria and renal replacement therapy; urine-output criteria were not used because urine-output documentation was inconsistent. For the 24-hour landmark analysis, time zero was ICU admission and the landmark was ICU admission plus 24 hours. Baseline serum creatinine was defined as the most recent valid value (0.1–20 mg/dL) recorded during the 365 days before ICU admission. Incident severe AKI was defined as the first KDIGO stage 3 observation at or after the landmark and before ICU discharge. Patients with stage 3 AKI before the landmark or no post-landmark KDIGO observation were excluded (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2).

Data Extraction and Variable Selection

Data extraction was performed using SQL in PostgreSQL interfaced with MIMIC-IV v3.1. The original retrospective trajectory analysis used demographic characteristics, vital signs, laboratory tests, disease-severity scores, comorbidities, interventions recorded during the ICU stay, and outcomes including total ICU length of stay, hospital length of stay, 7-day mortality, and 28-day mortality. The temporal sensitivity analysis used re-extracted time-stamped source tables. Predictors for the landmark model were restricted to information available before the 24-hour landmark: most recent pre-ICU creatinine, first-24-hour anion gap, first-24-hour heart rate, SOFA, and SAPS II. Total ICU length of stay was excluded from the landmark model because it is partly measured after AKI onset (Supplementary Table S2). Detailed extraction logic and analytic procedures are provided in the Supplementary Methods. Temporal model performance and paired comparisons are reported in Supplementary Tables S3 and S4, and ICD-coded COPD phenotype distributions and subgroup discrimination estimates are reported in Supplementary Tables S5 and S6.

Missing Data Handling

Variables with more than 30% missing values were excluded from the analysis to avoid introducing excessive imputation bias. For the remaining variables with less than 30% missing data, multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) was employed to address missing values.35 The imputation model incorporated all candidate predictor variables and the outcome variable to preserve the underlying data structure and reduce potential bias introduced by missingness. Five imputed datasets were generated, and the pooled estimates were used for subsequent analyses. The completeness and plausibility of the imputed data were verified through diagnostic checks, including comparison of distributions between observed and imputed values.

Feature Selection

The original modeling dataset was split into a training set (70%, n = 3,294) and a testing set (30%, n = 1,411) using stratified sampling to maintain the severe-AKI event proportion. Feature selection was performed exclusively on the training set. LASSO regression with 10-fold cross-validation identified 8 candidate variables.36 Boruta confirmed 34 features as important after 100 iterations.37 Random forest importance ranked by Mean Decrease Gini selected the top 10 variables.38 The intersection of all three methods yielded six consensus predictors: total ICU length of stay, serum creatinine, SAPS II, SOFA score, anion gap, and heart rate. Because total ICU length of stay is measured over the full stay, this six-feature model is interpreted as a retrospective trajectory model rather than an admission-time model.

Machine Learning Model Development and Comparison

Using the six consensus features, 15 machine-learning classifiers were trained on the training set and evaluated on the held-out testing set. The models were random forest, XGBoost tree booster, radial-basis support vector machine, logistic regression, k-nearest neighbors, partial least squares discriminant model, gradient boosting machine, single-hidden-layer neural network, naive Bayes, linear discriminant analysis, elastic-net logistic regression, AdaBoost. M1, XGBoost linear booster, CatBoost, and LightGBM.39–41 Hyperparameters were optimized by cross-validation on the training set. Model performance was assessed using AUC with 95% confidence intervals, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, F1 score, Youden’s index, decision curve analysis, residual analysis, calibration curves, and clinical impact curves. Feature-removal sensitivity models excluded total ICU length of stay, creatinine, or both variables (Supplementary Table S7). Paired model comparisons are reported in Supplementary Table S8.

Model Interpretability

The optimal machine learning model was interpreted using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP),22 a game theory–based framework that quantifies the marginal contribution of each feature to individual predictions. Global feature importance was measured by the mean absolute SHAP value across all samples, providing an overall ranking of predictor relevance. A beeswarm plot was generated to visualize the distribution of SHAP values for each feature, illustrating the directionality and magnitude of feature effects. SHAP dependence plots were constructed for each predictor to examine the nonlinear relationship between feature values and their contribution to the model output, with interaction effects color-coded by a secondary feature. Individual-level prediction explanations were provided through waterfall plots and force plots for representative patient samples, demonstrating the cumulative and directional contributions of each feature to the final predicted probability.

Causal Inference Analysis

We used CausalForestDML from the EconML Python library to estimate exploratory adjusted exposure-effect relationships.30 The estimator combines generalized random forests with double/debiased machine learning and cross-fitting.28,29 In this observational application, estimates require temporal ordering, positivity, consistency, and no unmeasured confounding. These assumptions cannot be guaranteed in MIMIC-IV; therefore, results are described as exploratory adjusted effect estimates rather than definitive causal effects.

The original CausalForestDML analysis estimated average treatment effects for five grouped interventions and six continuous exposures, but several original variables were vulnerable to temporal overlap or outcome-definition concerns. We corrected the population mean-effect confidence intervals and added exposure counts for grouped interventions. We also performed a restricted-adjustment sensitivity analysis that excluded total ICU length of stay, creatinine, CRRT, survival-derived coding variables, and other variables with clear temporal or outcome-definition concerns.

For the temporally ordered effect analysis, the exposure was the most recent valid pre-ICU creatinine and the outcome was incident severe AKI after the 24-hour landmark. Adjustment was restricted to variables available before the exposure or at admission, including age, sex, ICD-coded COPD phenotype, admission type, Charlson comorbidities, and Charlson comorbidity index. Post-exposure SOFA, SAPS II, anion gap, heart rate, and total ICU length of stay were deliberately excluded. CATE distributions were examined descriptively (Supplementary Table S9).

Survival Analysis

Kaplan–Meier survival curves were constructed to compare survival probabilities between patients with and without severe AKI at 7-day and 28-day time points. Between-group survival differences were assessed using the Log rank test. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs were estimated using the Cox proportional hazards model to quantify the association between severe AKI and mortality risk.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics were compared between patients with and without severe AKI. Continuous variables with normal distributions were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using independent-samples t tests. Continuous variables with non-normal distributions were presented as median (interquartile range) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U-test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages and compared using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. AUC confidence intervals and paired AUC comparisons used DeLong methods. CatBoost was the prespecified reference for comparisons against the other 14 models, and Holm correction was applied (Supplementary Tables S4 and S8). Python, R, scikit-learn, CatBoost, LightGBM, XGBoost, EconML, SHAP, pROC, and lifelines were used for analysis. Two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Among laboratory parameters, patients with severe AKI had significantly higher serum creatinine (2.41 ± 1.90 vs 1.27 ± 0.98 mg/dL, P < 0.0001), BUN (41.19 ± 28.76 vs 27.68 ± 21.00 mg/dL, P < 0.0001), anion gap (16.48 ± 5.40 vs 13.65 ± 3.63 mEq/L, P < 0.0001), and potassium (4.64 ± 0.84 vs 4.39 ± 0.64 mEq/L, P < 0.0001), while bicarbonate was significantly lower (23.54 ± 5.49 vs 25.05 ± 4.99 mEq/L, P < 0.0001). Disease severity scores were significantly elevated in the severe AKI group: SOFA (7.24 ± 4.02 vs 4.39 ± 2.97, P < 0.0001) and SAPS II (46.62 ± 15.05 vs 36.74 ± 11.07, P < 0.0001). A higher proportion of patients in the severe AKI group received mechanical ventilation (68.11% vs 43.09%, P < 0.0001) and vasoactive agents (50.23% vs 29.94%, P < 0.0001). Feature Selection

The dataset was split into a training set (n = 3,294) and testing set (n = 1,411) with stratified sampling. Three feature selection methods were applied to the training set (Figure 2A–G). LASSO regression with 10-fold cross-validation at lambda_1se (0.0073) identified 8 variables. Boruta confirmed 34 features as important after 100 iterations, and random forest importance selected the top 10 variables. The intersection yielded six consensus predictors: total ICU length of stay, creatinine, SAPS II, SOFA score, anion gap, and heart rate. Machine Learning Model Development and Comparison

Fifteen machine-learning algorithms were trained on the six selected features, with hyperparameters optimized by cross-validation (Table 2). Model performance was evaluated on the independent testing set using AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, F1 score, PPV, NPV, and Youden’s index (Figure 3A–F and Table 3). CatBoost had the highest observed test-set AUC (0.848, 95% CI, 0.825–0.872), followed by the XGBoost tree booster (0.844, 95% CI, 0.820–0.868) and LightGBM (0.844, 95% CI, 0.820–0.868). Paired DeLong tests using CatBoost as the prespecified reference showed that its advantage was not statistically significant after Holm correction versus the XGBoost tree booster (AUC difference 0.0042; adjusted P = 0.155), LightGBM (difference 0.0045; adjusted P = 0.077), logistic regression (adjusted P = 0.055), or elastic-net regression (adjusted P = 0.055). In the original corrected analysis, creatinine had an adjusted risk-difference estimate of 0.0723 (95% CI, 0.0227–0.1219) and total ICU length of stay had an estimate of 0.0200 (95% CI, 0.0079–0.0321). These intervals do not repair temporal overlap. Removing total ICU length of stay from the original CatBoost model reduced the AUC from 0.848 to 0.761 (paired difference 0.0872; Holm-adjusted P < 0.001), and removing creatinine reduced the AUC to 0.833 (paired difference 0.0156; Holm-adjusted P = 0.008; Supplementary Table S7 and Supplementary Figure S2). In the temporally separated landmark analysis, 4,171 patients were eligible and 841 developed incident severe AKI (Supplementary Table S1). The five-feature landmark model had an AUC of 0.679 (95% CI, 0.641–0.717), and removing pre-ICU creatinine yielded an AUC of 0.686 (95% CI, 0.648–0.724; paired difference −0.0067; P = 0.293; Supplementary Table S3, S4 and Supplementary Figure S3). The adjusted pre-ICU creatinine risk difference was 0.0547 per 1 mg/dL (95% CI, −0.0021 to 0.1115) and 0.0705 (95% CI, 0.0022–0.1387) after 1st-99th percentile trimming (Supplementary Table S9). The restricted-adjustment sensitivity estimates are shown in Supplementary Figure S4. In the landmark cohort, 801 patients had J44.1-coded AECOPD, 223 had J44.0-coded COPD with acute lower respiratory infection, and 3,147 had other J44.x-coded COPD; the event-rate difference between J44.1-coded AECOPD and patients without J44.1 was not statistically significant (P = 0.155; Supplementary Tables S5 and S6).

Discussion

This study establishes a COPD-specific benchmark for severe AKI across 15 machine-learning algorithms and, more importantly, shows how temporal design changes the meaning of model performance. In 4,705 ICU patients with COPD, the six-feature CatBoost model achieved a test-set AUC of 0.848 for retrospective trajectory discrimination. In the strictly ordered 24-hour landmark cohort, discrimination was 0.679 after excluding total ICU length of stay and restricting predictors to pre-landmark information. The difference between these analyses is a central result: models built with variables collected across the ICU course can characterize high-risk trajectories well, whereas admission- or landmark-time prediction requires explicit control of feature availability. This empirical comparison adds a temporal-validity dimension that is often absent from AKI model benchmarks.16–21,25–27

CatBoost had the highest observed test-set AUC among the 15 algorithms. Paired DeLong testing showed that its advantage over the XGBoost tree booster, LightGBM, logistic regression, and elastic-net regression was not statistically significant after Holm correction; this does not diminish the benchmark. Instead, it shows that strong performance arose from a compact consensus feature set and rigorous evaluation rather than from algorithm choice alone. Previous AKI studies also reported strong performance from gradient-boosting methods, usually with different endpoints and larger predictor sets.19–21,25–27 Demonstrating an AUC of 0.848 with six variables, while formally testing model differences, provides a credible and reproducible basis for parsimonious COPD-AKI model development. CatBoost’s ordered boosting and the accompanying SHAP analysis further support transparent interpretation of this compact model.22,41

The COPD-specific importance of these findings extends beyond algorithm ranking. AKI during COPD exacerbation is associated with mortality and adverse renal outcomes, and impaired renal function is common across the COPD spectrum.10,11,13–15 The six-variable signature captures renal function, global illness severity, acid-base disturbance, hemodynamic stress, and the evolving ICU course. The prominence of creatinine and the ICU trajectory accords with general critical-care evidence linking AKI and creatinine changes to mortality, health-care utilization, and persistent renal dysfunction.1,3,42–44 By evaluating this compact signature in a COPD ICU cohort, the study translates general AKI predictors into a population in which hypoxemia, hypercapnia, infection, right-heart dysfunction, mechanical ventilation, and medication exposure converge.9–15 This population-specific evaluation is clinically relevant because renal risk in COPD must be interpreted within the respiratory and hemodynamic context.

A second major contribution is the direct demonstration that predictive importance and adjusted exposure-effect estimates are not interchangeable. SHAP explains how a fitted model forms predictions, whereas generalized random forests and double/debiased machine learning estimate exposure-effect relationships under assumptions about temporal order, exchangeability, positivity, and consistency.22–24,28–31 The nonconcordance among SHAP rankings and adjusted estimates is therefore informative rather than a null result. It shows that SOFA, SAPS II, anion gap, and heart rate can support risk stratification without automatically becoming intervention targets. This distinction is essential for translating machine-learning models into clinical research because interventions directed at non-causal severity markers are unlikely to change renal outcomes.23,24

The temporal analyses sharpen, rather than weaken, the study’s contribution. Strictly pre-ICU creatinine did not improve 24-hour landmark discrimination, and its adjusted estimate crossed zero in the full complete-case cohort but excluded zero after trimming. Because creatinine contributes to KDIGO staging and total ICU length of stay can be prolonged by AKI, temporally overlapping definitions can create incorporation bias and reverse causality.3,34,42–44 The landmark analysis provides direct empirical evidence that temporal eligibility can change both predictive performance and causal interpretation. Likewise, the grouped intervention confidence intervals define the current precision of the evidence: they do not establish absence of effect, but they provide population estimates, exposure counts, and uncertainty bounds for better-powered time-varying analyses.23,24,31

The findings should be interpreted in light of several limitations. MIMIC-IV is a single-center resource, no external cohort was available, and pre-ICU laboratory capture depends on records available within the database.32 Urine-output criteria were not used for AKI staging, and potentially important factors such as outpatient medications, contrast exposure, fluid balance, detailed hemodynamic trajectories, infection severity, and treatment timing were unavailable or incompletely measured.34 The intervention variables were necessarily broad, and novel renal biomarkers were not available.45 The renal replacement therapy component of KDIGO stage 3 may also vary with practice-dependent initiation thresholds, adding outcome heterogeneity.46,47 These constraints limit transportability and causal identification, but they do not negate the internal 15-model benchmark, the corrected paired comparisons, or the temporal audit that identifies which performance claims are supportable.

Taken together, this study has direct clinical and methodological importance. For clinicians, the six-feature model identifies COPD ICU trajectories associated with severe AKI and supports prioritization of serial renal-function monitoring, medication review, contrast stewardship, hemodynamic assessment, and dose adjustment. The decision-curve and clinical-impact analyses further quantify potential utility across risk thresholds.48 For model developers, the contrast between AUCs of 0.848 and 0.679 demonstrates why time zero, prediction horizon, and feature availability must be specified before a model is labeled clinically deployable.16–18 For causal machine-learning research, the combined SHAP, adjusted-effect, and landmark analyses provide a reproducible framework for separating prognostic importance from intervention relevance.22–24,28–31 Prospective multicenter validation and time-varying modeling can now build on this benchmark rather than starting from an untested algorithmic claim.

Conclusion

This study provides a COPD-specific benchmark for severe AKI across 15 machine-learning algorithms and shows that a parsimonious six-variable CatBoost model achieves strong retrospective trajectory discrimination. By integrating SHAP interpretation, corrected adjusted-effect estimation, and a strictly ordered 24-hour landmark analysis, the study demonstrates that predictive strength, temporal availability, and causal relevance are distinct properties. This distinction is the central contribution: retrospective models can characterize high-risk ICU trajectories, while clinically deployable prediction requires temporally eligible features. The results provide a rigorous foundation for multicenter validation and the development of time-aware COPD-AKI models.
