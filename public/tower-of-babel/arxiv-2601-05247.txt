Random Models and Guarded Logic This is the full version of a paper that appears in the Proceedings of STACS 2026.
Oskar Fiuk

Abstract
Building on ideas of Gurevich and Shelah for the Gödel Class, we present a new probabilistic proof of the finite model property for the Guarded Fragment of First-Order Logic. Our proof is conceptually simple and yields the optimal doubly-exponential upper bound on the size of minimal models. We precisely analyse the obtained bound, up to constant factors in the exponents, and construct sentences that enforce models of tightly matching size. The probabilistic approach adapts naturally to the Triguarded Fragment, an extension of the Guarded Fragment that also subsumes the Two-Variable Fragment. Finally, we derandomise the probabilistic proof by providing an explicit model construction which replaces randomness with deterministic hash functions.

Introduction

In this work we consider First-Order Logic (
FO
) without function symbols of positive arity.
The Guarded Fragment (
GF
) is a fragment of
FO
in which quantifiers are relativised by atomic formulas.
Syntactically, this requirement is realised by restricting quantification to the forms:

\forall{\bar{x}}\,\big(\gamma({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}})\rightarrow\psi({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}})\big)\quad\text{and}\quad\exists{\bar{x}}\,\big(\gamma({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}})\wedge\psi({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}})\big),

where
\gamma({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}})
is an atomic formula, called a
guard
, mentioning all variables in
{\bar{x}}
and
{\bar{y}}
.

For example, the following sentence, describing a professor–student scenario, is in
GF
:

\forall p,s\,\big(\mathsf{supervises}(p,s)\rightarrow\big(\neg\mathsf{graduate}(s)\wedge\exists t\,\big(\mathsf{prepares}(s,t)\wedge\mathsf{thesis}(t)\big)\big)\big),

(1)

where
\mathsf{supervises}(p,s)
and
\mathsf{prepares}(s,t)
serve as guards.
In contrast, the following sentence is not in
GF
, since the quantifier
\forall p,s
is not guarded by a single atomic formula:

\forall p,s\;\big(\big(\mathsf{professor}(p)\wedge\mathsf{student}(s)\big)\rightarrow\exists t\,\big(\mathsf{better\text{-}than\text{-}in}(p,s,t)\wedge\mathsf{topic}(t)\big)\big).

(2)

Andréka, van Benthem, and Németi
[
2
]
introduced the Guarded Fragment as a generalisation of modal logic, aiming to transfer its key properties into the richer framework of First-Order Logic.
They established the decidability of satisfiability, and Grädel
[
21
]
later proved the complexity to be
2
-
ExpTime
-complete; under bounded number of variables or bounded arity of relation symbols, the complexity drops to
ExpTime
-complete.

The decidability of
GF
is impressively robust: it is preserved under numerous extensions, including fixed points
[
19
]
, transitive or equivalence guards
[
27
,
26
]
, and (negated) conjunctive queries
[
5
]
.
Further decidable fragments have been obtained by relaxing the notion of a guard. They include the Loosely, Packed, and Clique-Guarded Fragments
[
32
,
28
,
20
]
, the Guarded Negation Fragment
[
6
]
, and the Triguarded Fragment
[
31
]
.

Motivations.

In addition to decidability, a central question in the study of logical fragments is the
finite model property
:
namely, whether every satisfiable sentence admits a finite model.
For the Guarded Fragment, the finite model property is known to hold, with a doubly exponential upper bound (in the length of the sentence) on the size of minimal models.

The first proof of the finite model property for
GF
was given by Grädel
[
21
]
, relying on a deep combinatorial theorem by Herwig
[
23
]
. While Grädel’s approach was elegant in its logical formulation—using Herwig’s result as a black box—the underlying construction is technically involved. Moreover, this proof yields only a triply-exponential upper bound on the size of minimal models, which is far from being optimal.
A significant improvement came from Bárány, Gottlob, and Otto
[
4
]
, who established an optimal doubly-exponential bound. Their approach involves analysing finite guarded bisimilar covers of hypergraphs and relational structures, substantially generalising Rosati’s finite chase
[
30
]
. In fact, their result extends beyond
GF
, covering the richer setting where (negations of) conjunctive queries are also allowed. A simplified version tailored specifically to
GF
appears in Pratt-Hartmann’s book
[
29
]
, though we believe that even this version remains challenging to follow.

As the existing proofs of the finite model property for
GF
are unexpectedly difficult, it is natural to ask:
Can we find simpler proofs of this fundamental result?

A perspective that connects abstract model theory with applied computer science arises in knowledge representation and reasoning, a subfield of artificial intelligence.
In this context, the Guarded Fragment can serve as a logical foundation for decidable reasoning frameworks, subsuming and extending the basic description logics (DLs) of the
\cal ALC
family (for an introduction to DLs, see, e.g.,
[
3
]
).
Here, objects from applications such as databases, knowledge bases, or computer programs are represented as logical structures, while formulas act as a declarative specification language describing their properties.
Algorithms for satisfiability then become reasoning engines: given a formula, they decide whether an object satisfying the imposed logical constraints exists.

Over the years, a variety of algorithms solving satisfiability for the Guarded Fragment have been developed, ranging from purely theoretical decision procedures (e.g.,
[
21
]
) to practically implementable methods based on resolution, saturation, or tableau (e.g.,
[
10
,
25
,
24
]
).
A key aspect is that many of these algorithms not only decide satisfiability but also produce a finite combinatorial object: a certificate of formula consistency.

Our interest lies in the subsequent step:
How to turn such certificates into explicit finite models?

Since smaller models are typically more useful—both for computational efficiency and for practical interpretability—the central challenge is to generate finite models as small as possible:
not only close to the theoretical doubly exponential bounds, typically considered up to polynomial slack in the second-level exponent, but actually sharpened to within constant factors and detailed structural parameters of sentences.

Main Contribution.

In this work, we give a new proof of the finite model property for
GF
,
yielding the optimal doubly exponential bound on the size of minimal models.
To the best of our knowledge, no earlier proof exhibits comparable simplicity and self-containment.

We employ a probabilistic approach inspired by Gurevich and Shelah’s proof of the finite model property for the Gödel Class
[
22
]
.
The central idea is the following: given a formula
\varphi
and a (possibly infinite) model
{\mathfrak{A}}
with
{\mathfrak{A}}\models\varphi
, we define a random procedure that generates a finite structure
{\mathfrak{B}}_{n}
with domain of size
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
We then prove that, once
n
exceeds a certain threshold depending only on
\varphi
, the probability that
{\mathfrak{B}}_{n}\models\varphi
becomes strictly positive.
Consequently, some finite
{\mathfrak{B}}_{n}
must be a model of
\varphi
, yielding the finite model property with an upper bound on the minimal model size that matches this threshold.

Applying this probabilistic method to the Guarded Fragment, we establish our main result:
every satisfiable
GF
-sentence
\varphi
has a finite model whose domain size is

2^{2^{\,{\mathcal{O}}(|\varphi|\cdot\log|\varphi|)}}.

In this work, we assume that
|\varphi|
measures the
uniform length
of
\varphi
:
formulas are viewed as words over an infinite alphabet consisting of parentheses, logical connectives, quantifiers, variables, relation symbols, and constants, with each symbol contributing
1
to the length.
1
1

1

The uniform length differs from the bit-length of formulas (finite alphabet), in which
n
distinct variables or symbols requires
\Theta(n\cdot\log n)
bits.

To witness the tightness of our upper bound, we construct a family of sentences
(\varphi_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
whose minimal models have domains of size at least

2^{2^{\,\Omega(|\varphi_{n}|\cdot\log|\varphi_{n}|)}}.

It is an improvement upon earlier approaches for enforcing large models in
GF
, as they only achieved domain sizes of

2^{2^{\,\Omega(\sqrt{|\varphi_{n}|})}}

(cf.
[
21
]
).
Consequently, our probabilistic model construction yields an essentially optimal upper bound, up to constant factors in the second-level exponent.

Theorem 1
.

There exist universal constants
0<C_{\mathrm{lb}}<C_{\mathrm{ub}}
such that the following holds.

1.

Every satisfiable
GF
-sentence
\varphi
has a finite model whose domain size is at most

2^{2^{\,C_{\mathrm{ub}}\cdot|\varphi|\cdot\log|\varphi|}}.

2.

For every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, there exists a satisfiable
GF
-sentence
\varphi_{n}

with
|\varphi_{n}|\geq n
such that any model of
\varphi_{n}
must have domain size at least

2^{2^{\,C_{\mathrm{lb}}\cdot|\varphi_{n}|\cdot\log|\varphi_{n}|}}.

The upper bound of Theorem
1
is clean and elegant, but it is stated solely in terms of the length of
\varphi
and an unspecified constant in the second-level exponent.
We complement this result by deriving a more precise bound on the size of minimal models.
For a concise formulation, we express it in terms of the number of
(atomic)
k
-types
—that is, maximal consistent configurations of literals over
k
variables (see Section
2
for a formal definition).

Given a sentence
\varphi
, its
induced signature

\sigma
is the set of all relation and constant symbols occurring in
\varphi
.
The
width
of
\sigma
, denoted
{\rm{wd}}(\sigma)
, is the maximum arity of any relation symbol in
\sigma
.
We define the
expanded normal-form signature

\sigma_{\mathrm{nf}}
from
\sigma
as follows:
for every subformula
\chi
of
\varphi
that begins with a maximal block of quantifiers and which is not a sentence (i.e.,
\chi
has free variables), we introduce a fresh relation symbol
R_{\chi}
whose arity equals the number of free variables of
\chi
(which is at most
{\rm{wd}}(\sigma)
, since
\chi
is required to be guarded).

Note that the construction of
\sigma_{\mathrm{nf}}
preserves width, i.e.,
{\rm{wd}}(\sigma_{\mathrm{nf}})={\rm{wd}}(\sigma)
,
does not introduce new constants, and keeps the overall size of
\sigma_{\mathrm{nf}}
linear in
|\varphi|
.

Theorem 2
.

There exist sequences
(C_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\varepsilon_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
with
C_{t}>0
,
\varepsilon_{t}\in(0,1/e)
, and
\varepsilon_{t}\to 0

such that the following holds for every
t\in\mathbb{N}
.

Let
\varphi
be any satisfiable
GF
-sentence, and let
\sigma_{\mathrm{nf}}
be its expanded normal-form signature.
Denote
k={\rm{wd}}(\sigma_{\mathrm{nf}})
.
If
k\geq t\geq 2
, then
\varphi
has a finite model whose domain size is at most

C_{t}\cdot\bigl|\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}^{\sigma_{\mathrm{nf}}}_{k}\bigr|^{\,1-1/e+\varepsilon_{t}},

where
\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}^{\sigma_{\mathrm{nf}}}_{k}
is the set of all
k
-types over
\sigma_{\mathrm{nf}}
.

The exponent in Theorem
2
converges to
1-1/e\approx 0.63
as
t\rightarrow\infty
.
Informally, Theorem
2
can thus be read as stating that every satisfiable
GF
-sentence admits a finite model of domain size proportional to

\bigl|\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}^{\sigma_{\mathrm{nf}}}_{k}\bigr|^{\,0.63},

provided that the width
k={\rm{wd}}(\sigma_{\mathrm{nf}})
is sufficiently large.

At first sight, working with the expanded normal-form signature rather than the induced one may seem unintuitive.
However, this choice is in fact a consequence of the semantics of guarded sentences:
a quantified subformula
\chi({\bar{y}})
with free variables
{\bar{y}}
naturally defines a relation in a structure
{\mathfrak{A}}
, namely

R^{{\mathfrak{A}}}_{\chi}=\{\,{\bar{a}}\mid{\mathfrak{A}}\models\chi({\bar{a}})\,\}.

To simplify the quantifier structure of a sentence, a standard technique is to transform it into a suitable
normal form
(see Section
3
for a definition).
The normal-form reduction makes such implicitly defined relations explicit by introducing corresponding relation symbols.
As a result, it distinguishes elements that are locally—i.e., at the level of atomic types—indistinguishable in the induced signature,
but differ in their satisfaction of quantified subformulas.

Example 3
.

To illustrate, consider the following (slightly abstract)
GF
-sentence:

\exists x\;\big(U(x)\wedge\exists y,z\;\big(P(x,y,z)\wedge\neg U(y)\big)\big)\;\wedge\;\exists u\;\big(U(u)\wedge\forall v,w\;\big(P(u,v,w)\rightarrow U(v)\big)\big).

This sentence is satisfied in a model
{\mathfrak{A}}
with domain
\{1,2,3\}
, where

U^{{\mathfrak{A}}}=\{1,3\}
and
P^{{\mathfrak{A}}}=\{(1,2,3)\}
.
The variables
x
,
y
,
z
are witnessed by the elements
1
,
2
,
3
; and the variable
u
by element
3
.
Notice, however, that in the induced signature
\sigma=\{P,\,U\}
, elements
1
and
3
are indistinguishable at the level of
1
-types:
both satisfy only the unary predicate
U
.

Let
\mu
and
\nu
denote the subformulas that begin with quantifiers
\exists y,z
and
\forall v,w
, respectively.
The expanded normal-form signature introduces two additional predicates
R_{\mu}
and
R_{\nu}
,
which record the different roles of
1
and
3
in the model:
R^{{\mathfrak{A}}}_{\mu}=\{1\}
and
R^{{\mathfrak{A}}}_{\nu}=\{2,3\}
.

Finally, note that the subformulas starting with
\exists x
or
\exists u
are subsentences;
and those with
\exists z
or
\forall w
do not begin with a maximal block of quantifiers.
Hence we do not introduce fresh relation symbols for them.
Thus the resulting signature is
\sigma_{\mathrm{nf}}=\{P,\,U,\,R_{\mu},\,R_{\nu}\}
.

Triguarded Fragment.

The Guarded Fragment (
GF
) and the Two-Variable Fragment (
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
) are among the most prominent fragments of First-Order Logic.
Both capture a wide range of modal and description logics and are decidable, yet they differ substantially and are incomparable in expressive power.
In particular,
GF
cannot express certain basic properties, expressible in
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
, such as

\forall s,d\;\big(\big(\mathsf{student}(s)\wedge\mathsf{dean}(d)\big)\rightarrow\mathsf{knows}(s,d)\big).

To unify these two perspectives, Rudolph and Šimkus
[
31
]
introduced the
Triguarded Fragment
(
TGF
), building on related ideas developed earlier in
[
25
,
8
]
.

The key idea of
TGF
is to relax the quantification restrictions of
GF
: formulas with at most two free variables may be quantified freely, while the guardedness requirement is retained for formulas with three or more free variables (hence the name “tri-guarded”).
This way,
TGF
subsumes both
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
and
GF
, while also capturing properties beyond their reach.
For instance, in formula (
2
), the quantifier
\forall p,s
is admissible in
TGF
because the quantified subformula has only two free variables,
p
and
s
, whereas the quantifier
\exists t
is required to be guarded, and indeed is by the atom
\mathsf{better\text{-}than\text{-}in}(p,s,t)
.
Hence (
2
) belongs to
TGF
.

An important distinction concerns the role of equality.
While both
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
and
GF
remain decidable in the presence of equality, the satisfiability problem for
TGF
with equality is undecidable.
The reason is that
TGF
is expressive enough to encode the Gödel Class (i.e., the prefix class
\forall\forall\exists^{*}
), which was shown to be undecidable in the presence of equality by Goldfarb
[
15
]
.
Excluding equality suffices to restore decidability of satisfiability, with complexity
2
-
ExpTime
without constants and
2
-
NExpTime
when constants are allowed
[
31
]
.

Returning to the finite model property,
Kieroński and Rudolph
[
26
]
proved that the equality-free fragment of
TGF
has the finite model property, with an optimal doubly exponential bound on minimal model size.
Their proof, however, is technically intricate: it uses the finite model property for
GF
as a black box and adds a further combinatorial construction that carefully glues several structures into a single model.

In contrast, our probabilistic approach to
GF
extends seamlessly to
TGF
, yielding a considerably simpler proof of the finite model property for this broader fragment. Moreover, it also achieves the optimal doubly-exponential upper bound on minimal model size.

Theorem 4
.

There exists a universal constant
C>0
such that the following holds.
Let
\varphi
be a satisfiable equality-free sentence of
TGF
.
Then
\varphi
has a finite model whose domain size is at most

2^{2^{\,C\cdot|\varphi|\cdot\log|\varphi|}}.

Derandomisation.

As mentioned earlier, the satisfiability problem for the Guarded Fragment is
2
-
ExpTime
-complete
[
21
]
.
Yet, although the probabilistic construction shows that every satisfiable sentence has a model of doubly exponential size, this alone does not imply that such a model can be
constructed
in time matching the decision complexity.
Indeed, the number of structures of doubly exponential size is triply exponential.

From a practical point of view, this gap is minor: our randomised construction succeeds with probability at least
1/2
while sampling structures over a domain of doubly exponential size.
Nevertheless, from a theoretical standpoint it leaves a conceptual separation between
reasoning
and
model building
: deterministic vs. randomised time complexity.

We close this gap by providing a constructive, fully deterministic version of Theorem
1
.
The central idea is to replace the random choices by carefully selected deterministic ones,
using families of hash functions that mimic the statistical properties of true randomness.
We then prove that these deterministic choices always produce a valid model.

In our formulation, we rely on the notion of a
witness of satisfiability
:
a finite combinatorial object—namely, a set of
k
-types satisfying specific closure and consistency properties—that certifies the satisfiability of a sentence.
Its definition is deferred to Section
3
;
for the present discussion it suffices to know that such a witness can be computed from a satisfiable sentence in
2
-
ExpTime

[
21
,
29
]
.
On this basis we obtain the following constructive guarantee:

Proposition 5
.

There exists a deterministic algorithm with the following property:
given a witness of satisfiability
{\mathcal{W}}
for a
GF
-sentence
\varphi
,
the algorithm constructs a structure
{\mathfrak{B}}
with domain of size
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
{\mathfrak{B}}\models\varphi
,
where the parameter
n
satisfies
n=2^{2^{\,{\mathcal{O}}(|\varphi|\cdot\log|\varphi|)}}
.
The algorithm runs in doubly exponential time in the length of
\varphi
.

Related Work.

Beyond Gurevich and Shelah’s approach for the Gödel class
[
22
]
,
the probabilistic method has been employed in several other proofs of the finite model property.

One of the earliest and most influential applications of probabilistic techniques in logic
is Fagin’s proof of the
0
–
1
law for First-Order Logic
[
11
]
.
A notable consequence of Fagin’s argument is that any finite subset of the theory of the Rado graph admits a finite model.

Goldfarb, Gurevich, and Shelah subsequently extended the approach of the latter two authors
to the so-called subminimal Gödel class with identity
[
14
]
.
Goldfarb later developed probabilistic proofs for additional decidable fragments,
namely a solvable Skolem class
[
17
]
and the Maslov class
[
16
]
.
For recent applications of the probabilistic method, see
[
13
,
12
]
.

Preliminaries and Notation

We denote the set of natural numbers including
0
by
{\mathbb{N}}
. For
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the notation
[k]
stands for the set
\{1,\dots,k\}
, with the convention that
[0]=\varnothing
. More generally, we use interval notation
[a,b]\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}
to denote the set
\{a,a+1,\dots,b\}
whenever
a\leq b
, and the empty set
\varnothing
whenever
a>b
.
For a set
S
, we denote by
2^{S}
the powerset of
S
, and by
\binom{S}{k}
the set of all
k
-subsets of
S
.
If
S\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}
, we also use
\binom{S}{k}
for the set
\{\langle a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\rangle\in S^{k}\mid a_{1}<\dots<a_{k}\}
.

First-Order Logic.

We assume general familiarity with First-Order Logic (
FO
).
The logical symbols are
=,\bot,\top,\vee,\wedge,\neg,\rightarrow,\leftrightarrow
, and the quantifiers
\forall,\exists
.
Formulas may also use non-logical symbols: relation symbols of arbitrary arity (from a countably infinite set), constant symbols (also from a countably infinite set), and variables (again countably many).
We do not allow function symbols.
The
length
of a formula
\varphi
, denoted
|\varphi|
, is defined as the total number of symbols it contains, where each occurrence of a symbol—be it a variable, relation symbol, or constant—contributes
1
.
We use
{\rm fv}(\varphi)
to denote the set of
free
variables of
\varphi
.

A
signature

\sigma
is a finite set of symbols, partitioned as
\sigma={\rm{Rels}}\uplus{\rm{Cons}}
, where
{\rm{Rels}}
is the set of relation symbols and
{\rm{Cons}}
is the set of constant symbols.
We require
{\rm{Rels}}\neq\varnothing
.
Every relation symbol
R\in{\rm{Rels}}
comes with associated arity, denoted
{\rm{ar}}(R)
.
We require
{\rm{ar}}(R)\geq 1
.
The
width
of
\sigma
, denoted
{\rm{wd}}(\sigma)
, is the maximum arity of any symbol in
{\rm{Rels}}
.
The
signature of a formula
is the finite set of relation and constant symbols that appear in the formula.

We use Fraktur letters such as
{\mathfrak{A}},{\mathfrak{B}},\dots
to denote structures, and the corresponding Roman letters
A,B,\dots
for their domains.
A
\sigma
-structure
{\mathfrak{A}}
is a structure that interprets the symbols in
\sigma
: a relation symbol
R
as a relation
R^{{\mathfrak{A}}}\subseteq A^{k}
with
k
denoting the arity of
R
; and a constant symbol
c
as an element
c^{{\mathfrak{A}}}\in A
.
If
B\subseteq A
, we write
{\mathfrak{A}}~\!\!\restriction\!\!~B
for the
restriction
of
{\mathfrak{A}}
to the subdomain
B
. Note that
B
must include all interpretations of constant symbols to remain a
\sigma
-structure.
The structure
{\mathfrak{A}}
is
empty
if it contains no facts, i.e.,
R^{{\mathfrak{A}}}=\varnothing
for all relation symbols
R
; however, we do not insist (and even forbid) that the domain is the empty set
\varnothing
.
The
size
of a structure is the cardinality of its domain.

With respect to satisfiability, equality between constants can be eliminated by a straightforward reduction:
if a formula has a model
{\mathfrak{A}}
satisfying
c^{{\mathfrak{A}}}_{1}=c^{{\mathfrak{A}}}_{2}
, then
c_{2}
can be replaced by
c_{1}
throughout the formula, without affecting satisfiability.
Thus we work under the
standard name assumption
requiring that constants are interpreted in structures by themselves.
Given a
\sigma
-structure
{\mathfrak{A}}
, we partition its domain
A
as
A_{0}\uplus{\rm{Cons}}{}
, where
A_{0}
and
{\rm{Cons}}
are disjoint sets of
unnamed
and, respectively,
named
elements.

Types.

Fix a signature
\sigma={\rm{Rels}}\uplus{\rm{Cons}}
.
For
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
\operatorname{Lit}_{k}(\sigma)
denote the set of all non-equality literals over relation and constant symbols from
\sigma
and variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}
.

For
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, a
k
-type
over
\sigma
is a maximal consistent subset of
\operatorname{Lit}_{k}(\sigma)
.
A
type
is simply a
k
-type for some
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
We write
\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}^{\sigma}_{k}
for the set of all
k
-types over
\sigma
, and
\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}^{\sigma}:=\bigcup_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}^{\sigma}_{k}
.

Claim 6
.

For every
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have

\big|\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}^{\sigma}_{k}\big|=\prod_{R\in{\rm{Rels}}}2^{(k+|{\rm{Cons}}|)^{{\rm{ar}}(R)}}\quad\text{and thus}\quad 2^{k^{{\rm{wd}}(\sigma)}}\leq\big|\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}^{\sigma}_{k}\big|\leq 2^{|{\rm{Rels}}|\cdot(k+|{\rm{Cons}}|)^{{\rm{wd}}(\sigma)}}.

We denote by
\tau_{\mathrm{all\text{-}neg}}
the type consisting exclusively of negative literals (the value of
k
will be clear from the context).

If
k\leq\ell
, we write
\tau_{2}\models\tau_{1}
to indicate that a
k
-type
\tau_{1}
is contained in an
\ell
-type
\tau_{2}
.

For
\tau\in\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}^{\sigma}_{k}
, we define:

\partial\tau:=\{\gamma\in\tau\mid{\rm fv}(\gamma)=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}\},

and
\operatorname{int}(\tau):=\tau\setminus\partial\tau
,
that is, the
boundary
and
interior
of
\tau
.
The set of all
boundary
k
-types
is
\partial\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}^{\sigma}_{k}:=\{\partial\tau\mid\tau\in\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}^{\sigma}_{k}\}
.

A
k
-type
\tau
is called
guarded
if either
k\leq 1
or
\tau
contains a positive literal
\gamma
with

{\rm fv}(\gamma)=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\},
i.e.,
\gamma\in\partial\tau
.
Note that necessarily
k\leq{\rm{wd}}(\sigma)
.

Given a
k
-tuple of distinct unnamed elements
\langle a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\rangle
of a
\sigma
-structure
{\mathfrak{A}}
, we write

{\rm tp}^{{{\mathfrak{A}}}}[{a_{1},\dots,a_{k}}]

for the unique
k
-type
realised
by
\langle a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\rangle
in
{\mathfrak{A}}
.
Formally,
{\rm tp}^{{{\mathfrak{A}}}}[{a_{1},\dots,a_{k}}]
is the set of literals
\gamma\in\operatorname{Lit}_{k}(\sigma)
such that
{\mathfrak{A}},f\models\gamma
, where
f
is the assignment that sends
x_{i}\mapsto a_{i}
for every
i\in[k]
.
The collection of all
k
-types realised in
{\mathfrak{A}}
is denoted

\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}^{{\mathfrak{A}}}_{k}:=\{\,{\rm tp}^{{{\mathfrak{A}}}}[{a_{1},\dots,a_{k}}]\mid a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\in A\setminus{\rm{Cons}}\text{ are pairwise distinct}\,\}.

It is convenient to view
k
-types themselves as
\sigma
-structures over the canonical domain
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}\cup{\rm{Cons}}
.
This allows us to use structure-like notation; for instance, if
\tau\in\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}^{\sigma}_{3}
, we may write

{\rm tp}^{{\tau}}[{x_{1},x_{3}}]

for the
2
-type induced by
\tau
on the variables
x_{1}
and
x_{3}
.

Remark 7
.

Our definition of
k
-types deviates slightly from the standard one in two aspects.
First, we only consider tuples of unnamed elements.
Second, we restrict attention to tuples of pairwise distinct elements.
This choice is convenient: to fully describe a structure over a domain
A_{0}\uplus{\rm{Cons}}
, it suffices to specify for each tuple of distinct elements of
A_{0}
the corresponding realised
k
-type, with each unordered tuple being considered precisely once.
Naturally, the
k
-types have to be assigned consistently with each other.
Since types include literals involving constants, all facts involving constants
are defined as well.

3
Technical Background on Guarded Fragment

In this section we formally introduce the syntax of the Guarded Fragment, together with the normal form and a satisfiability criterion.
The material presented here is mostly a direct adaptation of Grädel’s work
[
21
]
and is included primarily for the reader’s convenience.
In particular, no claims of novelty are made here.
For a comprehensive introduction to the Guarded Fragment, see also Chapter 4 in Pratt-Hartmann’s monograph
[
29
]
.

Syntax of GF.

We formalise the syntax of the Guarded Fragment in Definition
8
.

Definition 8
.

The
Guarded Fragment
(
GF
) is the set of formulas in First-Order Logic generated by the following rules:

{romanenumerate}

Every atomic formula belongs to
GF
.

GF
is closed under Boolean connectives.

Let
{\bar{x}}
be a tuple of variables, let
\psi
be a formula in
GF
, and let
\gamma
be an atomic formula.
If
{\rm fv}(\psi)\subseteq{\rm fv}(\gamma)
and
{\bar{x}}\subseteq{\rm fv}(\gamma)
,
then both
\exists{\bar{x}}\,(\gamma\wedge\psi)
and
\forall{\bar{x}}\,(\gamma\rightarrow\psi)
belong to
GF
.

The atom
\gamma
in rule (
8
) is called a
guard
.

Note that equality may serve as a guard: the formula
\forall x~(x=x\rightarrow\psi)
is in
GF
whenever
\psi\in\mbox{$\mbox{\rm GF}$}{}
and
{\rm fv}(\psi)\subseteq\{x\}
.
This allows for free quantification over individual elements.

Normal Form.

Let
\varphi
be a sentence in
GF
.
Then
\varphi
is in
normal form
if it is a conjunction of a finite number of guarded
existential
, guarded
universal
, and guarded
Skolem
sentences:

\bigwedge_{t}\exists{\bar{x}}\;\big(\alpha_{t}({\bar{x}})\wedge\psi_{t}({\bar{x}})\big)\;\wedge\;\bigwedge_{t}\forall{\bar{x}}\;\big(\alpha_{t}({\bar{x}})\rightarrow\psi_{t}({\bar{x}})\big)\;\wedge\;\bigwedge_{t}\forall{\bar{x}}\;\big(\alpha_{t}({\bar{x}})\rightarrow\exists{\bar{y}}\;\big(\beta_{t}({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}})\wedge\psi_{t}({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}})\big)\big),

where
{\bar{x}}
and
{\bar{y}}
are disjoint tuples of distinct variables,
\alpha_{t}({\bar{x}})
and
\beta_{t}({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}})
are guards, and
\psi_{t}
are quantifier-free formulas.
The notation
\psi({\bar{x}})
only highlights that the set of free variables of
\psi
is contained in the tuple
{\bar{x}}
.
However, it does not imply that all of them are actually used by
\psi
.
In particular, when
\psi
is an atom, the variables can occur in any order and with repetitions.
Also the actual sets of free variables of
\alpha_{t}
,
\beta_{t}
, and
\psi_{t}
can vary with
t
.
Nevertheless, we require that each conjunct is properly guarded.
For every relevant index
t
, the following must hold:
if
t
corresponds to an existential or universal conjunct, then

{\rm fv}(\psi_{t})\subseteq{\rm fv}(\alpha_{t})={\bar{x}}
;
if
t
corresponds to a Skolem conjunct, then

{\rm fv}(\alpha_{t})={\bar{x}}
and

{\rm fv}(\psi_{t})\cup{\bar{y}}\subseteq{\rm fv}(\beta_{t})\subseteq{\bar{x}}\cup{\bar{y}}
,
where in both cases the tuples
{\bar{x}}
and
{\bar{y}}
may depend on
t
.

Lemma
9
reduces the finite model property for
GF
to the normal-form case.
Moreover, any upper bound on minimal model size that depends only on the length and signature, and is proved for normal-form sentences, naturally carries over to arbitrary
GF
-sentences.

Lemma 9
.

Let
\varphi
be a
GF
-sentence over a signature
\sigma
.
Then there exists a normal-form
GF
-sentence
\varphi_{\mathrm{nf}}

over an expanded signature
\sigma_{\mathrm{nf}}\supseteq\sigma

such that the following conditions hold.

1.

The sentences
\varphi
and
\varphi_{\mathrm{nf}}
are equisatisfiable.

2.

If
{\mathfrak{B}}
is a
\sigma_{\mathrm{nf}}
-structure with
{\mathfrak{B}}\models\varphi_{\mathrm{nf}}
,
then the
\sigma
-reduct of
{\mathfrak{B}}
is a model of
\varphi
.

3.

|\varphi_{\mathrm{nf}}|\leq C\cdot|\varphi|
for some fixed constant
C>0
.

4.

The expanded signature
\sigma_{\mathrm{nf}}
is obtained from
\sigma
as follows:
for every subformula
\chi
of
\varphi
that begins with a maximal block of quantifiers
and is not a sentence (i.e., has free variables),
introduce a fresh relation symbol
R_{\chi}
whose arity equals the number of free variables of
\chi

(which is at most
{\rm{wd}}(\sigma)
, since
\chi
is required to be guarded).

Proof.

Let
\varphi
be a
GF
-sentence.
If
\varphi
is unsatisfiable, then the lemma holds trivially, e.g., take
\varphi_{\mathrm{nf}}:=\exists x\;(x\neq x)
.
Now, assume that
\varphi
has a (possibly infinite) model
{\mathfrak{A}}
.
We describe a method for rewriting
\varphi
into normal form.

W.l.o.g. we may assume that all quantifiers in
\varphi
are existential, as

\forall{\bar{x}}\;\big(\gamma({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}})\rightarrow\psi({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}})\big)
is equivalent to
\neg\exists{\bar{x}}\;\big(\gamma({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}})\wedge\neg\psi({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}})\big)
.

Consider a subformula
\chi({\bar{y}})
of
\varphi
in the form

\chi({\bar{y}})=\exists{\bar{x}}\;\big(\gamma({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}})\wedge\psi({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}})\big),

chosen to be as deep as possible (i.e., not containing further quantifiers inside
\psi
).
Here we consider only subformulas starting with a maximal block of consecutive quantifiers (consult Example
3
).

First, if
\chi({\bar{y}})
is a subsentence, i.e.,
{\bar{y}}=\varnothing
,
then replace the occurrence of
\chi
with
\top
if
{\mathfrak{A}}\models\chi
; and with
\bot
if
{\mathfrak{A}}\models\neg\chi
.
Then append to
\varphi
respectively either

\exists{\bar{x}}\;\big(\gamma({\bar{x}})\wedge\psi({\bar{x}})\big)\quad\text{or}\quad\forall{\bar{x}}\;\big(\gamma({\bar{x}})\rightarrow\neg\psi({\bar{x}})\big).

Otherwise, i.e., when
{\bar{y}}\neq\varnothing
,
introduce a fresh relation symbol
R_{\chi}
of arity
|{\bar{y}}|
.
Replace the occurrence of
\chi({\bar{y}})
in
\varphi
with
R_{\chi}({\bar{y}})
, and conjoin the following two sentences:

\forall{\bar{y}}\;\big(R_{\chi}({\bar{y}})\rightarrow\exists{\bar{x}}\;\big(\gamma({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}})\wedge\psi({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}})\big)\big)\quad\text{and}\quad\forall{\bar{x}},{\bar{y}}\;\big(\gamma({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}})\rightarrow\big(\psi({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}})\rightarrow R_{\chi}({\bar{y}})\big)\big).

The latter sentence is equivalent to
\forall{\bar{y}}\;\big(\exists{\bar{x}}\;\big(\gamma({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}})\wedge\psi({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}})\big)\rightarrow R_{\chi}({\bar{y}})\big)
.
Intuitively,
R_{\chi}
now acts as a placeholder for
\chi({\bar{y}})
, while the above sentences guarantee equivalence.

Repeating this process iteratively yields a sentence
\varphi_{\mathrm{nf}}
in normal form over an expanded signature
\sigma_{\mathrm{nf}}
. The required properties of
\varphi_{\mathrm{nf}}
are readily verified.
∎

Satisfiability Criterion.

We now formulate a sufficient criterion for the satisfiability of sentences in
GF
.
This allows us to modularise the argument and clearly separate the general properties of guarded logic from the new components of our proof.

Let
\sigma={\rm{Rels}}\uplus{\rm{Cons}}
be a signature.
A set of types
\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}\subseteq\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}^{\sigma}
is said to be
closed (under reductions)
if the following holds:
for every
\tau\in\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}
which is a
k
-type for some
k
, and for every
\ell\in[0,k]
and choice of distinct indices
i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell}\in[k]
, the type

{\rm tp}^{{\tau}}[{x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{\ell}}}]

also belongs to
\tau
.
Further, the set
\tau
is said to be
consistent
if it contains a unique
0
-type.

A natural candidate for a closed and consistent set of
k
-types is the collection of all
k
-types with
k\leq{\rm{wd}}(\sigma)
that are realised in some structure.

Definition 10
.

Let
\sigma={\rm{Rels}}\uplus{\rm{Cons}}
be a signature, and let
\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}=\bigcup_{k=0}^{{\rm{wd}}(\sigma)}\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}_{k}

be a family of sets of
k
-types over
\sigma
, where
\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}_{k}\subseteq\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}_{k}^{\sigma}
for each
k\in[0,{\rm{wd}}(\sigma)]
.
Let
{\mathfrak{A}}
be a
\sigma
-structure with domain
A
, and let
A_{k}=\{\langle a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\rangle\in(A\setminus{\rm{Cons}})^{k}\mid a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\;\text{are pairwise distinct}\}
.

{romanenumerate}

We say that
{\mathfrak{A}}
is
\tau
-guarded
if, for every
k\in[0,{\rm{wd}}(\sigma)]
and every
{\bar{a}}\in A_{k}
, whenever the
k
-type
{\rm tp}^{{{\mathfrak{A}}}}[{{\bar{a}}}]
is guarded, it holds that
{\rm tp}^{{{\mathfrak{A}}}}[{{\bar{a}}}]\in\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}_{k}
.

We say that
{\mathfrak{A}}
has the
\tau
-extension property
if, for every
k\in[0,{\rm{wd}}(\sigma){-}1]
and every pair
(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\in\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}_{k}\times\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}_{k+1}
such that
\tau_{2}\models\tau_{1}
, the following holds:
whenever
{\bar{a}}\in A_{k}
satisfies
{\rm tp}^{{{\mathfrak{A}}}}[{{\bar{a}}}]=\tau_{1}
, there exists an element
b\in A\setminus({\bar{a}}\cup{\rm{Cons}})
such that
{\rm tp}^{{{\mathfrak{A}}}}[{{\bar{a}},b}]=\tau_{2}
.

Lemma 11
.

Let
\varphi
be a normal-form
GF
-sentence over a signature
\sigma={\rm{Rels}}\uplus{\rm{Cons}}
.
Suppose
\varphi
is satisfiable.
Then there exists a closed and consistent family of sets of
k
-types
\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}^{*}=\bigcup_{k=0}^{{\rm{wd}}(\sigma)}\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}^{*}_{k}
, where
\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}^{*}_{k}\subseteq\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}^{\sigma}_{k}
for each
k\in[0,{\rm{wd}}(\sigma)]
, such that the following holds:
whenever a
\sigma
-structure
{\mathfrak{B}}
is
\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}^{*}
-guarded and satisfies the
\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}^{*}
-extension property, then
{\mathfrak{B}}\models\varphi
.

Proof.

Fix a model
{\mathfrak{A}}
of
\varphi
.
For each
k\in[0,{\rm{wd}}(\sigma)]
, let
\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}_{k}^{*}:=\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}^{{\mathfrak{A}}}_{k}
be the set of
k
-types realised in
{\mathfrak{A}}
.
It is straightforward to verify that
\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}^{*}\;:=\;\bigcup_{k=0}^{{\rm{wd}}(\sigma)}\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}_{k}^{*}
is closed and consistent.

Now suppose that
{\mathfrak{B}}
is a
\sigma
-structure that is
\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}^{*}
-guarded and satisfies the
\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}^{*}
-extension property.
We show that
{\mathfrak{B}}\models\varphi
.

Let
\forall{\bar{x}}\,\big(\alpha_{t}({\bar{x}})\rightarrow\exists{\bar{y}}\,\big(\beta_{t}({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}})\wedge\psi_{t}({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}})\big)\big)

be a Skolem conjunct of
\varphi
, and let
f\colon{\bar{x}}\to B
be an assignment such that
{\mathfrak{B}},f\models\alpha_{t}({\bar{x}})
.
Enumerate the elements of
f({\bar{x}})\setminus{\rm{Cons}}
as a tuple
{\bar{b}}=\langle b_{1},\dots,b_{k}\rangle
without repetitions.
Because the atom
\alpha_{t}
holds, the type
{\rm tp}^{{{\mathfrak{B}}}}[{{\bar{b}}}]
is guarded.
From the
\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}^{*}
-guardedness of
{\mathfrak{B}}
, there exists a tuple
{\bar{a}}=\langle a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\rangle
of pairwise distinct unnamed elements of
{\mathfrak{A}}
such that
{\rm tp}^{{{\mathfrak{A}}}}[{{\bar{a}}}]={\rm tp}^{{{\mathfrak{B}}}}[{{\bar{b}}}].

Define an assignment
g\colon{\bar{x}}\rightarrow A
by

g(x)\;=\;\begin{cases}a_{i}&\text{if }f(x)=b_{i},\\[5.69054pt]
f(x)&\text{if }f(x)\in{\rm{Cons}}.\end{cases}

Since
{\mathfrak{A}}\models\varphi
, and in particular
{\mathfrak{A}},g\models\alpha_{t}({\bar{x}})
, there exists an assignment

g^{\prime}\colon{\bar{x}}{\bar{y}}\rightarrow A
extending
g
such that
{\mathfrak{A}},g^{\prime}\models\beta_{t}({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}})\wedge\psi_{t}({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}}).

Let
g^{\prime}({\bar{y}})\setminus(g({\bar{x}})\cup{\rm{Cons}})=\{a_{k+1},\dots,a_{m}\}
be the unnamed elements introduced by
g^{\prime}
(without repetitions), and form the extended tuple
{\bar{a}}^{\prime}=\langle a_{1},\dots,a_{m}\rangle.

We next construct a tuple
{\bar{b}}^{\prime}=\langle b_{1},\dots,b_{m}\rangle
in
{\mathfrak{B}}
satisfying
{\rm tp}^{{{\mathfrak{B}}}}[{{\bar{b}}^{\prime}}]={\rm tp}^{{{\mathfrak{A}}}}[{{\bar{a}}^{\prime}}].

The first
k
coordinates are already fixed, namely
b_{i}
is as in
{\bar{b}}
for
i\leq k
.
For
i=k+1,\dots,m
, assume inductively that
b_{1},\dots,b_{i-1}
have been chosen.
Since
{\mathfrak{B}}
satisfies the
\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}^{*}
-extension property and

{\rm tp}^{{{\mathfrak{A}}}}[{a_{1},\dots,a_{i-1}}]={\rm tp}^{{{\mathfrak{B}}}}[{b_{1},\dots,b_{i-1}}],

there exists an element
b_{i}\in B\setminus{\rm{Cons}}
distinct from
b_{1},\dots,b_{i-1}
such that

{\rm tp}^{{{\mathfrak{B}}}}[{b_{1},\dots,b_{i}}]={\rm tp}^{{{\mathfrak{A}}}}[{a_{1},\dots,a_{i}}].

Iterating this construction yields the required tuple
{\bar{b}}^{\prime}
.

Finally, define an assignment
f^{\prime}\colon{\bar{x}}{\bar{y}}\rightarrow B
extending
f
by setting

f^{\prime}(y)\;=\;\begin{cases}b_{i}&\text{if }g^{\prime}(y)=a_{i},\\[5.69054pt]
g^{\prime}(y)&\text{if }g^{\prime}(y)\in{\rm{Cons}}.\end{cases}

By construction of
f^{\prime}
, we obtain
{\mathfrak{B}},f^{\prime}\models\beta_{t}({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}})\wedge\psi_{t}({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}}).

Similar arguments establish the satisfaction of existential and universal conjuncts of
\varphi
.
∎

Let
\varphi
be a
GF
-sentence in normal form.
If a family of sets of
k
-types
\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}^{*}=\bigcup_{k=0}^{{\rm{wd}}(\sigma)}\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}^{*}_{k}
satisfies the conditions of Lemma
11
, then we call it a
satisfiability witness
for
\varphi
.

W.l.o.g. we will always assume
\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}^{*}_{k}\neq\varnothing
for all
k\in[0,{\rm{wd}}(\sigma)]
.
By Claim
6
, we have that

\displaystyle|\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}^{*}|=\sum_{k=0}^{{\rm{wd}}(\sigma)}|\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}^{*}_{k}|\leq\sum_{k=0}^{{\rm{wd}}(\sigma)}2^{|{\rm{Rels}}|\cdot(k+|{\rm{Cons}}|)^{{\rm{wd}}(\sigma)}}=2^{2^{{\mathcal{O}}(|\varphi|\cdot\log|\varphi|)}},

(3)

provided that
\sigma={\rm{Rels}}\uplus{\rm{Cons}}
is the induced signature of
\varphi
.

Remarks on Computability.

The proofs of Lemmas
9
and
11
are inherently non-constructive, as their proofs work with an unspecified model of the sentence.
The same applies to the highlighted reduction to the standard name assumption (Section
2
).
We briefly outline computational aspects of these reductions; in particular, required by Proposition
5
.

Let
\varphi
be an arbitrary
GF
-sentence over a signature
\sigma={\rm{Rels}}\uplus{\rm{Cons}}
.
We first eliminate equalities between constants by considering all possible partitions
\mathcal{C}
of the set
{\rm{Cons}}
.
For each such partition we form a sentence
\varphi_{\mathcal{C}}
by identifying all constants within each block of
\mathcal{C}
and consistently replacing them by a fixed representative.

A similar idea allows us to make the normal-form reduction effective.
Rather than replacing subsentences by
\top
or
\bot
according to their truth value in some unknown model,
we systematically consider all Boolean assignments
\mathcal{P}
to the relevant subsentences of
\varphi
.
For each such assignment we obtain a normal-form sentence
\varphi_{\mathcal{C},\mathcal{P}}
from
\varphi_{\mathcal{C}}
in an expected way.

In this way we obtain an exponentially large family of normal-form
GF
-sentences
\varphi_{\mathcal{C},\mathcal{P}}
.
The original sentence
\varphi
is satisfiable iff at least one of these sentences is satisfiable under the standard name assumption.
Moreover, any model
{\mathfrak{A}}
of some
\varphi_{\mathcal{C},\mathcal{P}}
can be transformed into a model
{\mathfrak{A}}^{\prime}
of
\varphi
over the same domain by reinterpreting the constants in accordance with
\mathcal{C}
and then restricting to the original signature
\sigma
.

Finally, for each sentence
\varphi_{\mathcal{C},\mathcal{P}}
one can decide in
2
-
ExpTime
whether it is satisfiable under the standard name assumption and, if so, compute a corresponding satisfiability witness within the same complexity bound.
A particularly simple algorithm for this task is given in the book by Pratt-Hartmann
[
29
]
;
although the definitions there differ slightly from ours, adapting the algorithm to our setting is straightforward.

4
Probabilistic Model Constructions

In this section we establish Theorems
1
and
2
.
We begin with an informal overview of the probabilistic approach (Subsection
4.1
),
and then develop the technical details in two stages.
First, in Subsection
4.2
, we present the baseline method of
Independent Sampling
,
which already yields a doubly-exponential upper bound, sufficient for proving Theorem
1
.
Next, in Subsection
4.3
, we refine the construction via
Markovian Sampling
,
achieving a sharper bound that is necessary for proving Theorem
2
.

4.1
Informal Overview

To gain intuition,
let us start with Gurevich and Shelah’s probabilistic proof of the finite model property for the Gödel Class
[
22
]
.
Consider a sentence
\varphi
in the shape of
\forall x_{1},x_{2}\;\exists y\;\psi
, where
\psi
is quantifier-free and uses only predicates of arity
1
and
2
.
Assuming that
\varphi
has a model
{\mathfrak{A}}
, we generate a finite random structure
{\mathfrak{B}}
as follows.
First prepare a domain
B
of size
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and assign to its elements the
1
-types realised in
{\mathfrak{A}}
, so that each
1
-type is assigned roughly to the same number of elements.
Then we set the
2
-types: for each pair of distinct elements
b_{1},b_{2}
choose a
2
-type randomly from those
2
-types realised in
{\mathfrak{A}}
whose endpoints agree with the already defined
1
-types of
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
.
When
n
is large enough, the structure
{\mathfrak{B}}
becomes a finite model of
\varphi
.
To see this, consider any two elements
b_{1},b_{2}
of
{\mathfrak{B}}
. Let
\langle a_{1},a_{2}\rangle
be a pair in
{\mathfrak{A}}
realising the same
2
-type as
\langle b_{1},b_{2}\rangle
.
With probability at least
1-2^{-\delta n}
, for a certain fixed independent of
n
number
\delta>0
, some element
b_{3}
of
{\mathfrak{B}}
extends the
2
-type of
\langle b_{1},b_{2}\rangle
to a
3
-type in the same way as a correct witness
a_{3}
in
{\mathfrak{A}}
extends the
2
-type of
\langle a_{1},a_{2}\rangle
.
By the union bound over all such pairs
\langle b_{1},b_{2}\rangle
, we get
{\mathbb{P}}[{\mathfrak{B}}\not\models\varphi]\leq n^{2}\cdot 2^{-\delta n}\rightarrow 0
.

Imagine now a hypothetical generalisation of this method to sentences in the shape of
\varphi=\forall x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\;\exists y\;\psi
, where
\psi
uses symbols of arbitrary arity.
We first assign
1
-types, then randomly choose
2
-types, …and when trying to assign
3
-types, we get stuck.
Indeed, the independent random choices could generate a configuration of
2
-types on pairs
\langle b_{1},b_{2}\rangle
,
\langle b_{2},b_{3}\rangle
, and
\langle b_{1},b_{3}\rangle
which is not induced by any
3
-type realised in
{\mathfrak{A}}
.

Suppose now that
\varphi
is a guarded sentence in normal form.
If we decide not to put any facts speaking about
\{b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}\}
or any of its supersets, then a guarded sentence cannot notice that this unintended pattern has occured.
Therefore, the following strategy appears natural.
We first assign the
1
-types, then proceed by randomly choosing the

C>0
be as in Proposition
21
, and
let
\varepsilon_{t}\in(0,1/e)
be as in Lemma
26
.
Choose any
\varepsilon^{\prime}_{t}\in(\varepsilon_{t},1/e)
so that
\varepsilon^{\prime}_{t}-\varepsilon_{t}\leq 1/t
.
Then there exists a fixed constant
C_{t}>C
such that

C\cdot(\ln x)^{2}\leq C_{t}\cdot x^{\varepsilon_{t}^{\prime}-\varepsilon_{t}}
holds for all
x\geq 1
.
Consequently, for every signature
\sigma
with
{\rm{wd}}(\sigma)\geq t
, we have

C\cdot\frac{|\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}^{\sigma}_{{\rm{wd}}(\sigma)}|}{|\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}^{\sigma}_{{\rm{wd}}(\sigma)-1}|}\cdot\big(\ln|\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}^{\sigma}_{{\rm{wd}}(\sigma)}|\big)^{2}\leq C\cdot|\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}^{\sigma}_{{\rm{wd}}(\sigma)}|^{1-1/e+\varepsilon_{t}}\cdot\big(\ln|\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}^{\sigma}_{{\rm{wd}}(\sigma)}|\big)^{2}\leq C_{t}\cdot|\mbox{\large\boldmath$\tau$}^{\sigma}_{{\rm{wd}}(\sigma)}|^{1-1/e+\varepsilon^{\prime}_{t}}.

5
Tightness Analysis: Enforcing Large Models

Setting
{\rm{wd}}(\sigma)=\Theta(|\varphi|)
, Proposition
12
provides an upper bound of

2^{2^{{\mathcal{O}}(|\varphi|\cdot\log|\varphi|)}}

on the size of minimal finite models for sentences in
GF
.
In this section we show that this bound is essentially tight: we explicitly construct a family of
GF
-sentences whose minimal models match this doubly exponential upper bound, up to constant factors in the second-level exponent.
Hence we establish the lower bound of Theorem
1
:
for specific sentences
\varphi
, models of size
2^{2^{\Omega(|\varphi|\cdot\log|\varphi|)}}
are necessary.

Proposition 27
.

There exists a family of
GF
-sentences
(\varphi_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
such that
each
\varphi_{n}
contains neither constants nor equality,
is over a signature
\sigma_{n}
with
{\rm{wd}}(\sigma_{n})=n+4
,
satisfies
|\varphi_{n}|\leq C\cdot n
for some fixed constant
C>0
,
and is satisfiable, but only in domains of size at least
2^{n!}
.

The general strategy for enforcing large models is to encode within them a combinatorial object of the desired size.
A well-known technique for enforcing models of size
2^{2^{n}}
is to encode
2^{n}
-bit numbers from the range
[0,2^{2^{n}}-1]
.
However, let us highlight that the standard constructions appearing in the literature (e.g.,
[
21
,
29
]
) yield formulas of size
\Theta(n^{2})
.
Consequently, they enforce models of size only

2^{2^{\Omega(\sqrt{|\varphi|})}},

which is asymptotically much smaller than the upper bound
2^{2^{{\mathcal{O}}(|\varphi|\cdot\log|\varphi|)}}
from Proposition
12
; even when compared at the doubly logarithmic scale, since
\sqrt{n}/(n\cdot\log n)\rightarrow 0
.
That said, it should be noted that these lower bounds were developed primarily to establish complexity lower bounds, rather than to enforce large domain sizes.

The bottleneck arises from the way these encodings represent
2^{n}
-bit numbers:
fix two distinct elements
a,b
.
A pair
(a,b)
is used to encode a
2^{n}
-bit number
v
.
The
k
-th bit of
v
is set to
1
precisely when the atom
Bit(c_{1},\dots,c_{n})
holds,
where the tuple
\langle c_{1},\dots,c_{n}\rangle\in\{a,b\}^{n}
corresponds to the binary representation of
k
.
Otherwise the
k
-th bit of
v
is set to
0
.

Yet, specifying that some two sequences
\langle c_{1},\dots,c_{n}\rangle
and
\langle c_{1}^{\prime},\dots,c_{n}^{\prime}\rangle
correspond to respectively the
k
-th and
(k{+}1)
-st bits of
v
requires examining
n
different cases to account for potential carries, which forces formulas of size
\Omega(n^{2})
.

To reach the optimal lower bound of

2^{2^{\Omega(|\varphi|\cdot\log|\varphi|)}}

a different construction is required.
Our approach is to encode the powerset of the set of all permutations of
n
elements, which has cardinality

2^{n!}=2^{2^{\Theta(n\cdot\log n)}}.

We use a certain decomposition of permutations from
[
12
]
to achieve this with formulas of length
{\mathcal{O}}(n)
,
thereby ensuring the desired growth rate.

Proof of Proposition
27
.

Fix an integer
n\geq 3
. We shall construct a
GF
-sentence
\varphi_{n}
designed to encode the powerset of
S_{n}
(the set of permutations of
\{1,\dots,n\}
).
The signature of
\varphi_{n}
contains the following relation symbols:

\sigma_{n}\;=\;\{\,Wit,\,Mem,\,Adj,\,Gen,\,Inc,\,Dec,\,Succ,\,P_{1},\dots,P_{n},\,Q_{1},\dots,Q_{n-1}\,\},

(29)

where the arity of
Wit
and
Mem
is
n+1
; of
Adj
is
n+2
; of
Gen
,
Inc
, and
Dec
is
n+3
; of
Succ
is
n+4
; and the symbols
P_{i}
and
Q_{i}
are all unary.
We write
{\bar{x}}=\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\rangle
and
{\bar{y}}=\langle y_{1},y_{2}\rangle
for the short-hand tuples that occur repeatedly below. Let
\pi_{\mathrm{cyc}}
denote the cyclic permutation
1\mapsto 2\mapsto\cdots\mapsto n\mapsto 1
, and let
\pi_{\mathrm{swp}}
denote the transposition swapping
1
and
2
.

The construction relies on encoding subsets of the symmetric group
S_{n}
.
We begin with a fixed “ground set” of size
n
, represented by some
n
-tuple of elements, denoted
{\bar{a}}
.

For every subset
F\subseteq S_{n}
we postulate the existence of a
witness element

w_{F}
.
The witness
w_{F}
is linked uniformly to all permutations of the ground set: for each
\pi\in S_{n}
we require

Wit(\pi({\bar{a}}),w_{F})
.
The actual membership of a permutation
\pi
in
F
is then expressed via the predicate
Mem
:

Mem(\pi({\bar{a}}),w_{F})
if and only if
\pi\in F
.
This forces distinct subsets
F,F^{\prime}
to be represented by distinct witnesses
w_{F}\neq w_{F^{\prime}}
.
Thus, if we succeed in enforcing the existence of witnesses for all subsets of
S_{n}
, the model will contain at least
2^{n!}
distinct elements.

To navigate between different subsets, observe that one can transform any subset
F\subseteq S_{n}
into any other
F^{\prime}\subseteq S_{n}
by toggling permutations one by one.
We capture this stepwise modification using the relation
Adj
: the atom

Adj(\pi({\bar{a}}),w_{F},w_{F^{\prime}})

is intended to hold precisely when the symmetric difference of
F
and
F^{\prime}
is the singleton
\{\pi\}
.

To axiomatise the relation
Adj
, we employ a certain decomposition of permutations,
which will be implemented using the remaining auxiliary predicates.
Details of this decomposition and its encoding will be presented below.

We first postulate the existence of the ground set and an initial witness:

\exists{\bar{x}},y~\Big(Wit({\bar{x}},y)\wedge\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n}P_{i}(x_{i})\Big).

(30)

In what follows, we denote the tuple witnessing for
{\bar{x}}
by
{\bar{a}}=\langle a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\rangle
.
We require the components of
{\bar{a}}
to be pairwise distinct.
For this we axiomatise that
\neg P_{i}(x)\vee\neg P_{j}(x)
holds for distinct
i\neq j
.
However, the obvious sentence would be of size
\Theta(n^{2})
. To keep the formula of size linear in
n
,
we use the auxiliary predicates
Q_{1},\dots,Q_{n-1}
:

\bigwedge_{i=2}^{n}\forall x\;\big(P_{i}(x)\rightarrow Q_{i-1}(x)\big)\wedge\bigwedge_{i=2}^{n-1}\forall x\;\big(Q_{i}(x)\rightarrow Q_{i-1}(x)\big)\wedge\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n-1}\forall x\;\big(Q_{i}(x)\rightarrow\neg P_{i}(x)\big).

(31)

Next, we ensure that witnesses respect the intended invariant:
for every witness
y
and
every permutation
\pi\in S_{n}
we must have
Wit(\pi({\bar{a}}),y)
.
Since
S_{n}
is generated by
\pi_{\rm cyc}
and
\pi_{\rm swp}
, it suffices to enforce closure under these two transformations:

\forall{\bar{x}},y~\big(Wit({\bar{x}},y)\rightarrow\big(Wit(\pi_{\rm swp}({\bar{x}}),y)\wedge Wit(\pi_{\rm cyc}({\bar{x}}),y)\big)\big).

(32)

We now enforce that every set
F
can be extended to a set
F^{\prime}
differing precisely on a single permutation
\pi
.
Formally, we require that for each permutation tuple
\pi({\bar{a}})
and each witness
y_{1}
, there exists another witness
y_{2}
that differs exactly on the membership of
\pi
:

\forall{\bar{x}},y_{1}~\big(Wit({\bar{x}},y_{1})\rightarrow\exists y_{2}~\big(Adj({\bar{x}},y_{1},y_{2})\wedge Wit({\bar{x}},y_{2})\wedge\big(Mem({\bar{x}},y_{1})\oplus Mem({\bar{x}},y_{2})\big)\big)\big).

(33)

To axiomatise that the witnesses
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
agree on every other permutation
\pi^{\prime}\neq\pi
, i.e.,
Mem(\pi^{\prime}(a),y_{1})\leftrightarrow Mem(\pi^{\prime}(a),y_{2})
, we employ the following combinatorial lemma.

Lemma 28

(Lemma 7.2 in
[
12
]
)
.

Let
\pi,\pi^{\prime}\in S_{n}
be permutations.
Then
\pi^{\prime}\neq\pi
if and only if

\pi^{\prime}=\pi_{\rm cyc}^{-j}\circ\rho\circ\pi_{\rm cyc}^{k}\circ\pi

for some
0\leq j<k<n
and some
\rho\in S_{n}
fixing
n
(i.e.,
\rho(n)=n
).

Proof.

By the group action
x\mapsto x\circ\pi^{-1}
, assume that
\pi=\mbox{\large\boldmath$1$}
is the identity permutation.

First, suppose
\pi^{\prime}\neq\mbox{\large\boldmath$1$}
.
Necessarily, there exists
i\in[n]
such that
\pi^{\prime}(i)>i
.
We set
k:=n-i
and
j:=n-\pi^{\prime}(i)
, so that
0\leq j<k<n
.
Define
\rho:=\pi_{\rm cyc}^{j}\circ\pi^{\prime}\circ\pi_{\rm cyc}^{-k}
.
By definition, it holds that
\pi^{\prime}=\pi_{\rm cyc}^{-j}\circ\rho\circ\pi_{\rm cyc}^{k}
.
We verify that
\rho
fixes the position
n
:

\displaystyle\rho(n)=(\pi_{\rm cyc}^{j}\circ\pi^{\prime}\circ\pi_{\rm cyc}^{-k})(n)=(\pi_{\rm cyc}^{j}\circ\pi^{\prime}\circ\pi_{\rm cyc}^{i})(n)=(\pi_{\rm cyc}^{j}\circ\pi^{\prime})(i)=\pi_{\rm cyc}^{-\pi^{\prime}(i)}\big(\pi^{\prime}(i)\big)=n.

Now, we show that the conditions of the lemma holding for
\pi^{\prime}=\mbox{\large\boldmath$1$}
imply
\pi_{\rm cyc}^{-j}\circ\rho\circ\pi_{\rm cyc}^{k}\neq\mbox{\large\boldmath$1$}
.
Since
j,k\in[0,n{-}1]
and
\rho(n)=n
, we can have
\pi_{\rm cyc}^{j-k}=\rho
only for
j=k
.
However, the lemma requires
j<k
, and therefore
\pi_{\rm cyc}^{-j}\circ\rho\circ\pi_{\rm cyc}^{k}\neq\mbox{\large\boldmath$1$}
.
∎

We implement the decomposition of Lemma
28
using the auxiliary predicates

Gen
,
Inc
,
Dec
, and
Succ
.
Intuitively:

Inc
increases the counter along powers of
\pi_{\rm cyc}
;

Gen
introduces the action of
\rho
restricted to
[n{-}1]
, generated by
\pi_{\rm swp}
and
\pi^{\prime}_{\rm cyc}
:
1\mapsto 2\mapsto\cdots\mapsto n{-}1\mapsto 1
;

Dec
decreases the counter via powers of
\pi_{\rm cyc}^{-1}
;
and
Succ
provides the successor relation on the counter elements.
The counter itself is represented by the last argument, where an element marked by
P_{i}
with
1\leq i\leq n-1
indicates the difference
i:=k-j
(
j
,
k
as in Lemma
28
).
This ensures that we never reach
0
or
n
, thereby avoiding the trivial case
\pi^{\prime}=\pi
.

We axiomatise
Succ
as:

\displaystyle\forall{\bar{x}},{\bar{y}},z,z^{\prime}~\big(Succ({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}},z,z^{\prime})\rightarrow\bigvee_{i=1}^{n-2}\big(P_{i}(z)\wedge P_{i+1}(z^{\prime})\big)\big).

(34)

Next, we generate permutations of the form
\pi_{\rm cyc}^{k}\circ\pi
for
0<k<n
:

\displaystyle\forall{\bar{x}},{\bar{y}}~\big(Adj({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}})\rightarrow\exists z~\big(Inc(\pi_{\rm cyc}({\bar{x}}),{\bar{y}},z)\wedge P_{1}(z)\big)\big),

(35)

\displaystyle\forall{\bar{x}},{\bar{y}},z~\big(Inc({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}},z)\rightarrow\big(P_{n-1}(z)\vee\exists z^{\prime}~\big(Succ({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}},z,z^{\prime})\wedge Inc(\pi_{\rm cyc}({\bar{x}}),{\bar{y}},z^{\prime})\big)\big)\big).

(36)

Then, we generate permutations of the form
\rho\circ\pi_{\rm cyc}^{k}\circ\pi
, where
\rho
fixes
n
.
The subgroup
\{\rho\in S_{n}\mid\rho(n)=n\}
is induced by
\pi_{\rm swp}
and
\pi^{\prime}_{\rm cyc}
:
1\mapsto 2\mapsto\cdots\mapsto n{-}1\mapsto 1
.
We enforce:

\displaystyle\forall{\bar{x}},{\bar{y}},z~\big(Inc({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}},z)\rightarrow Gen({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}},z)\big),

(37)

\displaystyle\forall{\bar{x}},{\bar{y}},z~\big(Gen({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}},z)\rightarrow\big(Gen(\pi^{\prime}_{\rm cyc}({\bar{x}}),{\bar{y}},z)\wedge Gen(\pi_{\rm swp}({\bar{x}}),{\bar{y}},z)\big)\big).

(38)

Finally, we generate permutations of the form
\pi_{\rm cyc}^{-j}\circ\rho\circ\pi_{\rm cyc}^{k}\circ\pi
with
0\leq j<k<n
:

\displaystyle\forall{\bar{x}},{\bar{y}},z~\big(Gen({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}},z)\rightarrow Dec({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}},z)\big),

(39)

\displaystyle\forall{\bar{x}},{\bar{y}},z~\big(Dec({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}},z)\rightarrow\big(P_{1}(z)\vee\exists z^{\prime}~\big(Succ({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}},z^{\prime},z)\wedge Dec(\pi_{\rm cyc}^{-1}({\bar{x}}),{\bar{y}},z^{\prime})\big)\big)\big).

(40)

By Lemma
28
, every
\pi^{\prime}\neq\pi
admits a representation such that

Dec(\pi^{\prime}({\bar{a}}),y_{1},y_{2},z)
holds.
We then enforce membership agreement between
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
on all such permutations:

\displaystyle\forall{\bar{x}},y_{1},y_{2},z~\big(Dec({\bar{x}},y_{1},y_{2},z)\rightarrow\big(Mem({\bar{x}},y_{1})\leftrightarrow Mem({\bar{x}},y_{2})\big)\big).

(41)

We define
\varphi_{n}
to be the conjunction of the sentences (
30
)–(
41
).
Since each conjunct has length
{\mathcal{O}}(n)
, the resulting sentence
\varphi_{n}
satisfies

|\varphi_{n}|={\mathcal{O}}(n)
.
Moreover, the signature
\sigma_{n}
of
\varphi_{n}
has width
n+4
, as required.
Hence the syntactic assumptions of Proposition
27
are met.

In what follows, we prove Claims
29

and
30

that
\varphi_{n}
is satisfiable and that every model of
\varphi_{n}

has domain size at least

.
This completes the proof of Proposition

.

Claim 29

The sentence
\varphi_{n}
is satisfiable: there is a standard model

Proof.

Define the standard model
{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}
as follows. Take the ground tuple

Interpret

Claim 30
.

In every model
{\mathfrak{B}}\models\varphi_{n}

distinct elements.

.

{\bar{a}}

6
Extension to a Stronger Fragment: Triguarded Fragment

The
Triguarded Fragment
(
TGF
) extends the Guarded Fragment by admitting one additional rule in the syntax of
GF
(Definition
8
):

{romanenumerate}

Let
x,y
be variables and let
\psi
be a formula in
TGF
.
If
{\rm fv}(\psi)\subseteq\{x,y\}
, then both
\exists x\,\psi
and
\forall x\,\psi
belong to
TGF
.

Rule (
6
) relaxes the guarding requirement of
GF
in the case of formulas with at most two free variables.
Thus,
TGF
permits unguarded quantification over pairs of elements.
To compare, recall that
GF
allows for free quantification only over individual elements.

In this section we establish Theorem
4
, showing that the equality-free subfragment of
TGF
enjoys the finite model property with a doubly exponential upper bound on model size.

Proof of Theorem
4
.

To unify the syntax of
GF
and
TGF
, it is convenient (following
[
31
]
) to introduce an auxiliary fragment, denoted
GFU
.
This is simply
GF
over signatures extended by a
distinguished
binary symbol

Remarks on Equality.

We conclude this section by considering a semantic constraint that allows equality in Theorem
4
.
A similar observation has already appeared in Gurevich and Shelah’s work concerning the probabilistic proof for the Gödel class
[
22
]
.

The Triguarded Fragment with equality is undecidable
[
31
]
.
However, nowhere in our argument did we explicitly exclude the use of equality, except the final argument in Lemma
33
.
This observation allows us to recover decidability of
TGF
with equality under the following semantic constraint:
every
1
-type realised by an unnamed element in a model must have at least two distinct unnamed realisations.

Readers familiar with Grädel, Kolaitis, and Vardi’s proof of the finite model property for
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}

[
18
]
may recall a related notion, where elements realising unique
1
-types are called
kings
.
In our setting, a
king
is an unnamed element whose
1
-type is distinct from
1
-types of all other unnamed elements.
Naturally, under the above semantic constraint, kings are excluded.
This allows us to strengthen Theorem
4
, and as we discuss below, also Theorem
1
.

Corollary 34
.

Let
\varphi
be a
TGF
-sentence.
If
\varphi
has a model without kings, then it also has a finite model,
whose domain size satisfies the same bound as in Theorem
4
.

At first sight, banning kings may appear to be an artificial restriction.
However, we believe that it has natural applications.
For example, in the theory of directed graphs with a single binary predicate
E
, tournament graphs are modelled by

\forall x\,\neg E(x,x)
and
\forall x,y\,\big(x\neq y\rightarrow\big(E(x,y)\leftrightarrow\neg E(y,x)\big)\big)
.
This property of graphs is expressible neither in
GF
(with equality) nor in the equality-free
TGF
.
Naturally, non-singleton tournament graphs satisfy the restriction on kings: every vertex realises the same
1
-type
\{\neg E(x_{1},x_{1})\}
.

Moreover, the constraint of Corollary
34
is vacuous for
GF
(even with equality): take two disjoint copies of a model and glue them on constants. Standard arguments show that the resulting structure satisfies the same guarded sentences.
Consequently,
TGF
without kings strictly subsumes—at least from the perspective of satisfiability—not only the equality-free
TGF
but also
GF
(with equality).

Further applications come from description logics (DLs), which are often studied as fragments of
GF
via the so-called
standard translation
.
Corollary
34
does not limit the use of constants: atoms of the form
x=c
or
c=c^{\prime}
for constants
c
and
c^{\prime}
pose no problem.
In the standard translation, such atoms are often used to capture DLs with
nominals
.

Last but not least, constants can often be used to
simulate
kings.
By introducing a fresh constant for each king in a model, one can transform the model into a king-free one.
Such a transformation can even serve as a basis for a practical procedure semi-deciding the finite satisfiability problem for the full
TGF
—namely, a sentence of
TGF
is finitely satisfiable iff it has a king-free model over a signature expanded by
k
fresh constants for some
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
As Example
35
witnesses, this does not contradict the general undecidability of
TGF
.

Example 35
.

Consider the structure
{\mathfrak{N}}
over the natural numbers
{\mathbb{N}}
, interpreting a binary relation

Succ^{{\mathfrak{N}}}=\{\langle n,n+1\rangle\mid n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}

and a unary predicate

In
{\mathfrak{N}}
, the element
0

Now take any finite set

However,

Then the element

Thus, adding finitely many constants does not in general eliminate kings.

The duplication trick from Lemma

does not resolve the issue either:
the
TGF
-sentence
\forall x,y\;\big(\big(Zero(x)\wedge Zero(y)\big)\rightarrow x=y\big)
is no longer satisfied in the structure

.

For a formal proof that kings cannot be eliminated in general, see Goldfarb’s undecidability result for the Gödel class with equality
[

, which can be embedded into

8
Conclusions

In this work, we presented a new probabilistic proof of the finite model property for the Guarded Fragment.
Our methods yield tight bounds on the size of minimal models and extend naturally to the Triguarded Fragment.
To the best of our knowledge, no previous work on the Guarded Fragment has employed a similar probabilistic technique.

Several natural directions remain open.
A particularly appealing challenge is to extend the applicability of probabilistic methods to stronger fragments.
One candidate is the Clique Guarded Fragment (
CGF
), which is known to enjoy the finite model property
[
4
]
.
Here, however, a direct probabilistic approach seems out of reach.
For instance, in a graph-theoretic setting with a single relation
E
,

CGF
can express the absence of triangles via the sentence

\forall x,y,z\;\big(\big(E(x,y)\wedge E(y,z)\wedge E(z,x)\big)\rightarrow\bot\big).

Yet if edges were placed obliviously at random, a triangle would appear almost with certainty.
This suggests that some additional underlying structure is needed, and that future work may profit from combining probabilistic reasoning with classical model constructions.
