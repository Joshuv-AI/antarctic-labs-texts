A Silver-Zeolite Spray Powder for Pressure Ulcers and Minor Burns: Real-World Evidence from Primary Care
Authors: Lorel Mureșan, Dionisio Franco Barattini, Laura Simona Ianoși, Carlo Signorini, Rawia Harb
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPressure ulcers and minor burns are commonly managed in primary care. The tested device is an adsorbing spray powder combining ionic silver (2.5%) and micronized zeolite (97.5%), designed to provide exudate control and antimicrobial activity in a topical formulation.Materials and MethodsIn this multicenter, open-label, single-arm post-market clinical follow-up (PMCF) study, adult outpatients with chronic stage II–III pressure ulcers (NPUAP) unhealed after ≥3 weeks of standard care, or with superficial/partial-thickness burns (<10% body surface area) within 48 hours of injury, received the tested product for 4 weeks. Co-primary endpoints were 4-week changes in PUSH (pressure ulcers) and Vancouver Scar Scale (VSS; burns) scores; secondary endpoints included pain (100-mm Visual Analog Scale), patient satisfaction, Investigator Global Assessment of Performance, and safety.ResultsFifty-eight patients were enrolled and 56 analyzed per-protocol (32 burns, 24 pressure ulcers); two discontinued for reasons unrelated to safety. At Week 4, 96.4% (54/56) showed meaningful improvement and none worsened. Mean VSS decreased from 5.22±2.78 to 1.31±1.49 in burns (p<0.001; bias-corrected d_avg=1.72), and mean PUSH decreased from 11.67±2.41 to 3.29±1.60 in pressure ulcers (p<0.001; bias-corrected d_avg=4.01), with 95.8% of pressure-ulcer patients achieving a ≥5-point reduction. VAS pain decreased from Week 2 in both subgroups (p<0.001). At Week 4, the Investigator considered 68.8% of burns and 29.2% of pressure ulcers healed. Patient satisfaction was high (75.0% very satisfied, 25.0% satisfied); no adverse events, device effects or deficiencies were reported.ConclusionIn this single-arm, real-world study, the tested device showed a favorable performance and safety signal in mild-to-moderate pressure ulcers and minor burns; given the non-comparative design, these findings do not establish equivalence or superiority to other silver-based dressings. Ease of use may support outpatient self-managed care; adequately powered randomized trials versus alternative silver-based dressings are warranted to confirm these findings.Trial RegistrationClinicalTrials.gov, NCT05649332.

Introduction

Treating wounds represents a substantial part of the daily clinical practice of family physicians and general practitioners involved in primary care, particularly when caring with aging populations and in long-term care settings. Pressure ulcers and minor burn wounds1,2 are among the most frequently encountered lesions and require timely, effective management to promote healing and prevent complications. The first step in optimal wound management is a structured assessment of the lesion, which generally includes evaluating tissue viability, exudate, and signs of infection. The second step is cleansing and, when necessary, debridement. However, beyond these initial actions, the selection of an appropriate dressing is a critical and often challenging decision.3,4 In outpatient care, an ideal wound product should not only support physiological healing – by maintaining a moist environment, controlling exudate and limiting the microbial burden – but also meet the practical requirements of ease of use, tolerability, cost-effectiveness and suitability for patient or caregiver self-administration.

A wide range of wound dressings and topical agents is currently available. Commonly used options for pressure ulcers include hydrocolloids, foams, alginates and hydrogels, with antimicrobial agents such as silver or iodine added when infection is suspected. For minor burns, non-adherent dressings, hydrocolloids and topical antimicrobials are frequently used, alongside simple emollients for superficial lesions. Despite the wide availability of these options, current evidence does not clearly support the superiority of any specific dressing type over others in promoting wound healing because direct comparisons are very limited. For this reason, treatment selection remains largely guided by wound characteristics and by personal clinical judgment.5

The importance of a moist wound environment in supporting tissue repair is widely recognized, as is its role in facilitating cellular migration, angiogenesis, and autolytic debridement.6 Control of microbial burden is equally important, particularly in wounds at risk of infection or with critical colonization. Accordingly, silver-containing dressings are commonly used because of their broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity.7

Signorini Medicale S.r.l. (Albaredo d’Adige, Verona, Italy) has developed the tested device (Argogen®), a Class III medical device intended to combine effective wound management with the practical requirements of daily primary-care practice. This product is an adsorbing spray powder containing ionic silver (2.5%) incorporated into a micronized zeolite matrix (97.5%). Its dual mechanism of action is based on the complementary properties of its components: the porous structure of the zeolite enables effective adsorption and control of wound exudate, contributing to a clean local environment, while the silver ions exert broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity. Silver interferes with bacterial cellular functions and induces oxidative stress through the generation of reactive oxygen species.8,9 When incorporated into a zeolite framework, silver ions are released in a slow and sustained manner (first-order kinetics), potentially enhancing antimicrobial efficacy over time compared with formulations characterized by rapid silver release.10 Although the tested product is CE-marked and in widespread clinical use, prospective clinical evidence in pressure ulcers and minor burns remains limited. Based on these considerations, the present post-market clinical follow-up (PMCF) study was planned and performed to evaluate the performance and safety of the product in a real-world primary-care setting in patients with pressure ulcers or minor burns treated according to the Instructions for Use (IFU).

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This was a multicenter, open-label, non-randomized, non-comparative, single-arm, interventional PMCF study (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT05649332, date of registration December 14, 2022; protocol code OPSIG/0122/MD) evaluating the performance and safety of the tested spray powder over a 4-week treatment period. According to quasi-experimental design terminology, the present study follows a one-group pretest–posttest design, including patients with minor burn wounds or pressure ulcers.11 Due to its real-world setting and non-randomized nature, no strategies such as blocking or minimization were applied, and potential selection bias cannot be excluded. The study protocol has been deposited on Protocol.io and can be view at the following DOI: https://doi.org/10.17504/protocols.io.81wgbmdpnvpk/v2. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (64th WMA General Assembly, Fortaleza, 2013), ISO 14155:2020 and Regulation (EU) 2017/745. The protocol was approved on March 9, 2023 by the National Ethics Committees (Comisia Naţională de Bioetică a Medicamentului şi a Dispozitivelor Medicale, Romania) under the number 28DM. All participants provided written informed consent, and data were handled in compliance with the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR 2016/679). Specific written informed consent for digital photographs was obtained from every patient before enrollment; this consent authorized the use of photographs for wound assessment and documentation purposes within the clinical study. Patients were then offered a second, optional consent form seeking separate authorization to use anonymized images for publication in a scientific journal. Because only a small number of patients agreed to sign this second consent form, the resulting subset of anonymized images was not considered sufficiently representative of the overall study population; we therefore elected not to include patient photographs in the present manuscript.

The study is reported in accordance with the Transparent Reporting of Evaluations with Nonrandomized Designs (TREND) statement for non-randomized evaluations.12

Study Population

Adult outpatients were recruited at two primary care sites in Romania: S.C. Salvosan Ciobanca in Zalău and M.C. Medica in Craiova. All subjects who routinely visited these sites and had minor burn wounds or pressure ulcers were potentially eligible to participate in the study. The two patient subgroups were treated with the same device and analyzed separately.

Treatment

The tested medical device in spray powder formulation (2.5% ionic silver, 97.5% micronized zeolite; 10 g powder/125 mL or 4 g powder/50 mL aluminum aerosol can) was applied topically once or twice a day to the wound area at each dressing change throughout the 4-week study period, according to the manufacturer’s Instructions for Use. During routine outpatient visits, the investigators delivered the tested medical device to patients or caregivers. Each patient received a supply of the device for the entire four-week study. They also provided careful instructions on how to self-administer the treatment correctly at home and how to report missing doses in the patient diary. No advertising was performed during the study period to increase enrollment. On the contrary, compliance with scheduled appointments was reinforced with a telephone call to patients two days before each planned visit to the center.

Endpoints and Assessments

Study visits were scheduled at Day 0 (baseline), Week 1 (±1 day), Week 2 (±1 day) and Week 4 (±2 days; end-of-study visit). The investigators reported the protocol-requested data in a paper Case Report Forms. After the monitoring procedures were completed, the CRO entered these data into the study database.

The co-primary performance endpoints were the change from baseline to Week 4 in Pressure Ulcer Scale for Healing (PUSH) score, version 3.0 (based on wound length, width, exudate amount and tissue type) for pressure ulcers, and in VSS score (vascularity, height/thickness, pliability and pigmentation) for burn wounds, both assessed by the Investigator.

Secondary performance endpoints were: change in PUSH and Vancouver Scar Scale (VSS) scores at Week 2; percentage of patients considered healed by the Investigator; patient-reported pain on a 100-mm Visual Analog Scale (VAS); patient treatment satisfaction (4-point scale: 1 = very satisfied, 2 = satisfied, 3 = moderately satisfied, 4 = not satisfied) at Week 4; and Investigator Global Assessment of Performance (IGAP) on a 4-point scale (1 = very good, 2 = good, 3 = moderate, 4 = poor) based on photographic review at each visit. This evaluation was performed by a blinded investigator assessment of standardized photographs.

Safety was evaluated at each visit through monitoring of adverse events (AEs), serious adverse events (SAEs), adverse device effects (ADEs), serious adverse device effects (SADEs), anticipated serious adverse device effects (ASADEs), unanticipated serious adverse device effects (USADEs) and device deficiencies (DDs), and by the Investigator and Patient Global Assessments of Safety (IGAS, PGAS) at Week 4, both scored on a 4-point scale (1 = very good, 2 = good, 3 = moderate, 4 = poor). Adverse events, serious adverse events, adverse device effects and device deficiencies were defined in accordance with ISO 14155:2020 and Regulation (EU) 2017/745. At each scheduled visit, the investigator actively solicited adverse events through open, non-leading questioning of the patient and review of the patient’s diary, in addition to any events spontaneously reported by the patient between visits. Each event was recorded on a dedicated case report form page, including onset and resolution dates, severity, seriousness criteria, action taken, outcome, and the investigator’s assessment of causal relationship to the device. Statistical Analysis

In the absence of device-specific pilot data, sample-size calculation was based on a standardized effect size rather than an assumed mean and SD. A conservative, medium-to-large effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.6) was assumed, consistent with conventional benchmarks for effect-size magnitude.13 This assumption is conservative relative to prior evidence. In a randomized trial of comparable pressure-ulcer patients (grade III–IV) means and SDs of PUSH were reported at baseline and 8 weeks; the corresponding small-sample-corrected average-variance standardized mean difference (average-variance SMD;14) was 1.14 for the silver-based dressing arm and 1.25 for the standard-care cream arm,15 both markedly larger than the d = 0.6 assumed here. This assumption yielded a requirement of 31 evaluable patients per wound type (two-sided α = 0.05, corrected to α = 0.025 per co-primary endpoint; power 1−β = 0.80); allowing for an anticipated 10% drop-out, 34 patients per subgroup (68 in total) were planned for enrollment.

Achieved power was computed separately for each subgroup (burn and pressure-ulcer), both for the conservative planning assumption (d = 0.6) and for the effect size actually observed, and are reported in Table 2. Because the pressure-ulcer subgroup did not reach the planned sample size, the effect size actually observed exceeding 99.9% in both subgroups (bias-corrected d_avg=1.72 for VSS in burns; bias-corrected d_avg=4.01 for PUSH in pressure ulcers) should be interpreted together with its precision and the study’s noncomparative design. The large observed effect size does not eliminate the uncertainty related to the small sample size or establish comparative effectiveness.

Continuous variables were summarized as mean and standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range, as appropriate; categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Wilson method was used for all confidence intervals for proportions.

Within-subject changes from baseline in PUSH and VSS scores were analyzed with paired Student’s t-tests (or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests when normality assumptions were not met). Standardized mean differences were expressed as bias-corrected d_avg effect sizes. Repeated-measures analyses (ANOVA or mixed-effects models, as appropriate) were used for secondary longitudinal endpoints (eg, VAS). All analyses were performed on the per-protocol population using SAS version 9.2 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). A two-sided significance level of 0.05 was applied to all tests, except for the two co-primary endpoints (PUSH, VSS), for which a Bonferroni-corrected two-sided α = 0.025 was applied to account for multiplicity. Proportions of patients considered healed by the Investigator are reported with 95% confidence intervals calculated using the Wilson score method.

Results

Patient Disposition and Baseline Characteristics

The first patient was enrolled on April 21, 2023, and the last patient completed follow-up on December 6, 2023; database lock occurred on January 17, 2024. A total of 58 patients were enrolled (intention-to-treat population: 34 burns, 24 pressure ulcers). Two patients in the burn subgroup dropped out for reasons unrelated to safety. One patient was lost to follow-up, and the other patient had a major protocol deviation. Thus, the per-protocol population consisted of 56 patients (32 with burns and 24 with pressure ulcers). Discussion

This multicenter PMCF study evaluated the performance and safety of a spray powder medical device – an adsorbing silver-zeolite topical formulation – in the treatment of pressure ulcers and minor burn wounds in a real-world primary-care setting. Over a 4-week treatment period, a high proportion of patients showed improvement in the prespecified wound scores, with a favorable safety and tolerability profile. In particular, 96.4% of the overall population and 100% of patients with pressure ulcers showed wound improvement; mean VSS and PUSH scores decreased to a clinically and statistically relevant extent, with large-to-very-large effect sizes. These quantitative benefits were accompanied by marked pain reduction, high patient satisfaction and favorable Investigator global assessments, consistent with observations from previous experience with silver-based topical agents.16

These findings reflect what is typically observed in routine ambulatory practice. Family physicians and general practitioners are frequently involved in the management of uncomplicated wounds, including early-stage pressure ulcers and minor burns. In this setting, treatment decisions are guided not only by clinical effectiveness, but also by practical and personal considerations of the physician involved, such as ease of use, time efficiency, and the possibility of self-management by the patient or their caregiver. These elements should also be considered critical determinants of adherence and, ultimately, clinical outcomes. From a practical point of view, the tested product offers several attractive features: the spray-powder formulation allows rapid, painless application without specific training. In addition, a single can may be reused for multiple applications on the same patient. Finally, the dual mechanism of action addresses two core components of wound care – moisture balance and microbial control – within one product, potentially simplifying treatment regimens.17,18

No subjects were withdrawn because of tolerability or usability issues, and patient satisfaction was consistently high across both wound types.

We focused the study on mild-to-moderate lesions (stage II–III pressure ulcers and superficial/partial-thickness burns) as these represent the typical cases managed by general practitioners and family doctors. In contrast, more severe or complex wounds are usually referred to specialist centers. Therefore, the study population can be considered representative of a real-world primary-care scenario, and the observed healing rates are consistent with those expected in appropriately selected patients. The difference in healing rates between burns (68.8%) and pressure ulcers (29.2%) at Week 4 is expected given the acute versus chronic pathophysiology of the two lesion types and it is also in line with the expected clinical course of these kinds of lesions.

This study has several limitations that must be acknowledged. First, and most importantly, the open-label, single-arm, non-comparative design does not permit a randomized comparison with other wound-care products; consequently, the contribution of the tested device cannot be fully isolated from the natural course of wound healing or from concurrent standard-care co-interventions (wound cleansing, debridement, and, for pressure ulcers, offloading) that continued throughout the study. This pragmatic design reflects the primary regulatory objective of the present investigation — a PMCF study under Regulation (EU) 2017/745, intended to confirm performance and safety under routine conditions of use rather than to establish comparative superiority. For the pressure-ulcer subgroup, all patients had by inclusion criterion failed to heal after ≥3 weeks of standard care prior to enrollment, providing a degree of within-patient historical comparison that partially, though not fully, mitigates this limitation; this consideration does not apply to the acute burn subgroup. Dedicated randomized controlled trials comparing the tested device with alternative silver-based dressings or active interactive dressings are warranted to quantify any incremental therapeutic benefit and are proposed as the natural next step in the clinical development of this device.

Second, the pressure-ulcer subgroup (n = 24) did not reach the pre-specified target of 31 evaluable patients, reducing the a priori planned statistical power for this comparison. A post-hoc power analysis indicated that, at the effect size actually observed (bias-corrected d_avg = 4.01), achieved power exceeded 99.9%. Nevertheless, the large observed effect size does not eliminate the uncertainty related to the small sample size, which widens the confidence intervals around secondary, proportion-based estimates (eg, the Week-4 complete-healing rate). This further limits the generalizability of subgroup findings and the feasibility of formal subgroup analyses.

Third, the 4-week observation period, while appropriate for detecting an early performance signal in a pragmatic primary-care PMCF design, is short relative to the time typically required for complete closure of chronic stage II–III pressure ulcers, even under optimal standard care. This is the most likely explanation for the lower complete-healing rate observed in the pressure-ulcer subgroup (29.2%) compared with the acute burn subgroup (68.8%) at Week 4 and is the reason complete healing was pre-specified only as a secondary, descriptive endpoint for this subgroup, with the continuous PUSH score change as the co-primary measure of graded improvement. The sustained and progressive reduction in PUSH score already evident at Week 2 and continuing through Week 4 suggests a favorable healing trajectory that a longer follow-up period might have translated into a higher complete-healing rate; this cannot be confirmed by the present data, and dedicated studies with extended follow-up (eg, 8–12 weeks) are needed to characterize complete-healing outcomes in this population and to determine the optimal treatment duration in clinical practice.

In addition, the VSS was originally developed to assess burn scars, and its reliability, validity, and responsiveness have recognized limitations; these limitations should be considered when interpreting VSS changes during the early post-burn period.19

Finally, the inclusion of only mild-to-moderate lesions restricts the applicability of the results to more complex wound types. Nonetheless, the pragmatic, real-world design and the consistency of the improvement observed across multiple patient- and Investigator-reported measures support the external validity of the findings within the intended use scenario.

Conclusions

In this multicenter, open-label, single-arm, PMCF study, the tested device demonstrated favorable performance in the management of mild-to-moderate pressure ulcers and minor burns, with no adverse events, adverse device effects or device deficiencies observed in 56 patients (upper bound of the 95% confidence interval for the true event rate approximately 5–6%, rule-of-three approximation). The findings provide supportive real-world evidence of performance and tolerability of the silver-zeolite spray powder in the studied primary-care population. Given the single-arm, non-comparative design, these findings should be interpreted as a favorable performance and safety signal in real-world primary care rather than as evidence of equivalence or superiority to other silver-based dressings. The device’s ease of application and suitability for patient or caregiver self-management represent potentially useful practical characteristics for primary-care and outpatient settings,20 which may support treatment adherence; confirmation in adequately powered randomized controlled trials against alternative silver-based dressings, with broader patient populations and longer follow-up, is required before comparative or practice-recommendation claims can be made.
