Family relationships, perceived stigma, and social isolation among people living with HIV/AIDS: a cross-sectional study in China
Authors: Qi You, Liao Zhang, Haitao Huang, Kai Yang, Kunmei Cheng, Jirong Liu, Hong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSocial isolation is a critical determinant of health that disproportionately affects people living with HIV/AIDS (PLWHA). Although existing literature has identified associations of family relationships and perceived stigma with social isolation, the ways in which these factors are statistically related remain insufficiently explored. This study aimed to assess the level of social isolation among PLWHA in China and to examine whether perceived stigma is statistically associated with the relationship between family relationships and social isolation.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 294 PLWHA recruited through convenience sampling from Chengdu Public Health Clinical Center and the Xichang Center for Disease Control and Prevention between March 2023 and July 2024. Participants completed questionnaires on sociodemographic variables, disease-related information, General Alienation Scale, Berger HIV Stigma Scale, and Family Intimacy and Adaptability Scale. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, t-tests, ANOVA, Pearson correlation, and Bootstrap mediation analysis (PROCESS Model 4) in SPSS 27.0.ResultsA total of 294 PLWHA (78.9% male; mean age 42.31 years) were included in the analysis. Participants reported relatively high levels of social isolation (mean = 41.46 ± 7.25). Family relationships were negatively associated with perceived stigma (r = − 0.300, p < 0.01) and social isolation (r = − 0.623, p < 0.01), while perceived stigma was positively associated with social isolation (r = 0.478, p < 0.01). Mediation analysis indicated that perceived stigma was statistically associated with the relationship between family relationships and social isolation (indirect association = − 0.033, 95% CI [− 0.051, − 0.018]), accounting for 15.1% of the total association.ConclusionSocial isolation remains a prominent concern among PLWHA. Family relationships are associated with lower levels of social isolation, and this association is partially linked to perceived stigma. These findings highlight the potential importance of addressing both family support and perceived stigma in future research and intervention development. However, given the cross-sectional design, the findings should be interpreted as associational rather than causal.

Background

As of 2025, approximately 39.9 million people living with HIV/AIDS (PLWHA) globally, with this prevalence steadily increasing [1]. The advent of highly effective combination antiretroviral therapy (ART) has transformed HIV/AIDS into a manageable chronic condition by suppressing viral replication, restoring immune function, and reducing morbidity and mortality, thereby extending life expectancy. Nevertheless, significant psychological distress and compromised quality of life persist among this population. Individuals with HIV/AIDS face heightened vulnerability to internalized stigma and external discrimination, frequently resulting in social isolation, whether self-imposed or enforced. The World Health Organization [2] identifies social isolation as a key driver of health inequities, disproportionately affecting marginalized groups such as PLWHA, where stigma persists as structural and interpersonal barriers. Recent multi-country data indicate that fear of judgment leads individuals with HIV/AIDS to avoid social engagement, and highlight the association between social isolation and adverse health outcomes [3], underscoring the urgent need to address its underlying psychosocial mechanisms [4].

While the significance of family relationships is widely acknowledged, their specific role in mitigating or exacerbating social isolation within the context of perceived stigma remains underexplored. Current interventions predominantly target individual-level factors, overlooking the influence of family relationships and perceived stigma. This study aims to address this gap by investigating whether there is a statistically significant association between perceived stigma and family relationships and social isolation among PLWHA. The findings are intended to inform policies that concurrently strengthen family support systems and combat discriminatory practices.

Social isolation, as a complex, multifactorial phenomenon, arises from the interplay of biological, psychological, and sociocultural forces acting upon PLWHA. It manifests as objectively measurable social disconnection (e.g., reduced social network size, diminished frequency of social interactions, physical isolation) and subjective emotional detachment (e.g., feelings of loneliness, perceived isolation, and meaninglessness) [5, 6]. As a key psychosocial determinant of health, social isolation is particularly pronounced among PLWHA, primarily stemming from disease-related stigma. It is robustly associated with numerous severe adverse outcomes, including, but not limited to: HIV-associated lipodystrophy [7], increased all-cause mortality [8, 9], suboptimal HIV care outcomes [10], heightened inflammation and its sequelae [11], major depressive disorder [12], elevated risk of sudden death [13], increased hospitalization rates [8], and hypertension and other physical health impacts mediated via neurohormonal immune pathways [14]. Furthermore, disease-specific factors such as perceived stigma [15], restricted access to community resources (e.g., during pandemics [16]), , loss of social network members [17], and increased vulnerability [18]may further exacerbate the degree of social isolation among PLWHA, impeding their social participation.

Perceived stigma is considered an important psychosocial factor associated with social isolation among PLWHA. It refers to the subjective psychological state wherein PLWHA, following diagnosis, experience or anticipate negative evaluations from others due to their serostatus, subsequently leading to internalized negative emotions such as fear of disclosure, self-deprecation, self-denial, and shame [19]. Research indicates [20] that this perceived stigma significantly promotes social avoidance behaviors. PLWHA are disproportionately vulnerable to experiencing or anticipating perceived stigma due to their status. This perceived stigma directly constitutes a major barrier to establishing and maintaining positive social network relationships, thereby intensifying the risk of social isolation.

Family relationships serve as a core source of social support and play a vital role in mitigating social isolation for PLWHA. According to ecological systems theory [21], individual development is a process of continuous interaction with the environment. Strong family functioning has been demonstrated to significantly improve psychological adaptation for both patients and caregivers [22]. Among various forms of social support, family support is often regarded as one of the most critical factors in adapting to illness. The family functioning theoretical model emphasizes that the degree to which individuals feel understood and supported during recovery provides foundational security for social engagement. Given the unique characteristics of HIV/AIDS, such as perceived stigma and privacy concerns, families frequently emerge as the most important and reliable support system for PLWHA. Existing research confirms that positive family relationships effectively enhance social connectedness and alleviate feelings of isolation across various patient populations, including PLWHA [23]. When patients value family support and possess higher-quality family relationships, they are more likely to actively utilize this resource to cope with challenges [24]. Consequently, family relationships may directly predict lower levels of social isolation and may also exert an indirect effect on social isolation by reducing perceived stigma.

Although family relationships and perceived stigma have each been linked to social isolation, most existing studies have examined these factors separately. Limited research has explored how they may be jointly associated within an integrated framework. In particular, it remains unclear whether perceived stigma is statistically associated with the relationship between family relationships and social isolation. Therefore, this study aims to assess the level of social isolation among PLWHA in China and to examine the associations among family relationships, perceived stigma, and social isolation. An exploratory mediation model (see Fig. 1 hypothesized model) was used to investigate whether perceived stigma is statistically associated with the relationship between family relationships and social isolation. Grounded in micro-level theories of alienation etiology, this approach—integrating familial and social perspectives—enables healthcare institutions to design more precise, multidimensional intervention strategies, provide more effective support for PLWHA, foster inclusive environments, and ultimately reduce health inequities within this population.

Study

Aims

Aim to assess the level of social isolation among PLWHA in China and to examine the associations among family relationships, perceived stigma, and social isolation.

Design

We used a cross-sectional design for this study, which was conducted in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines.

Ethical considerations

This study strictly adhered to the ethical principles of biomedical research, including respect, beneficence, and justice. Prior to commencement, the study received approval from the Ethics Review Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University, with the ethical review number 2023 − 846.

Methods

Study setting and sampling

This study was conducted across Chengdu and Xichang from March 2023 to July 2024. Participants were recruited from the outpatient departments of the Chengdu Public Health Clinical Center and the Xichang Center for Disease Control and Prevention using convenience sampling. Potential participants were identified with the assistance of healthcare providers and trained research staff during routine clinical visits. Individuals who met the eligibility criteria were approached in person, informed about the purpose and procedures of the study, and invited to participate. Those who agreed provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. Recruitment was conducted consecutively during the study period to maximize sample inclusion. Sample size estimation was performed in PASS 20.0.6 software using the General Alienation Scale (GAS) total score as the primary outcome measure. Based on prior research reporting a mean GAS score of 11.07 (SD = 11.07) among PLWHA [25], parameters were set with α = 0.05, 95% confidence interval, and 1.5 permissible error. This yielded a minimum sample of 210. Taking into account the sampling error and possibility of invalid questionnaires, we distributed a total of 304 questionnaires. Invalid questionnaires were defined as those with incomplete responses, inconsistent answers, or patterns indicating random or non-serious participation. Finally, after excluding 10 invalid questionnaires, we analyzed 294 valid responses, resulting in a response rate of 96.7%.

Inclusion and/or exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: ① Individuals diagnosed with HIV infection; ②individuals who can read and communicate normally, and can understand the questionnaires; ③ aged ≥ 18 years; ④capable of signing an informed consent form and willing to cooperate with the survey Exclusion criteria: ①PLWHA with severe HIV/AIDS-related neurocognitive disorders or other conditions that prevent participation; ②individuals with a diagnosed mental illness.

Measures

Symptom assessment scale

This scale includes the 27 most common symptoms experienced by PLWHA. It uses a 4-point rating scale, with each symptom scored from 0 to 3, ranging from “no symptoms” to “severe.” The total score ranges from 0 to 81, with a higher score indicating greater distress caused by the relevant symptoms. The scale has a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.961.

Sociodemographic variables and disease-related information

Data were collected on gender, age, ethnicity, place of residence, religion, educational level, marital status, sexual orientation, employment status, forms of medical expenses payment, Per capita monthly household income, average monthly medical expenses, duration of diagnosis, route of infection, CD4 + T-cell count, viral load, comorbidities, disclosure of infection status, and symptom assessment scale scores.

General Alienation Scale (GAS)

The 15-item Chinese adaptation [26] of Jessor’s General Alienation Scale [27] was administered. This instrument comprises four dimensions: other-directed alienation (5 items), skepticism (4 items), self-alienation (3 items), and meaninglessness (3 items). Responses were recorded on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 4 = strongly agree), yielding total scores between 15 and 60, with higher scores indicating greater social isolation. Pretesting demonstrated good internal consistency (α = 0.877).

Family intimacy and adaptability scale

The Chinese version [28] of Olson’s FACES II (1982) was utilized. This 30-item instrument measures family cohesion (16 items) and adaptability (14 items) using a 5-point Likert scale (1–5). Higher composite scores indicate stronger family functioning. The scale showed good reliability in pretesting (α = 0.856) and has established retest reliability and validity in Chinese populations.

Berger HIV Stigma Scale (BHSS)

The 12-item short form [29] of Berger’s original scale [30] assessed four stigma dimensions: personalized stigma, disclosure concerns, negative self-image, and public attitudes (3 items each). Items were rated on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 4 = strongly agree), with total scores ranging 12 to 48 (higher scores = greater perceived stigma). Pretesting confirmed adequate internal consistency (α = 0.837).

Procedure

This study employed an investigator-developed questionnaire and was conducted from March 2023 to July 2024 in Chengdu and Xichang. Before data collection, all research personnel received standardized training to ensure a comprehensive understanding of the questionnaire. Pre-test results and participant feedback were analyzed to assess instrument reliability and estimate completion time. Data collection occurred in dedicated clinic rooms to minimize distractions. Following a detailed explanation of the study’s purpose and procedures, and upon obtaining informed consent, questionnaires were administered and collected immediately. Researchers verified questionnaire completeness in real-time, addressing participant queries concurrently. Each questionnaire underwent line-by-line review for missing data, omissions, or logical inconsistencies; identified issues were clarified or corrected with participants. Upon confirmation of accuracy and completeness, questionnaires were collected and assigned unique identifiers.

Statistical analysis

Data were independently entered into Epidata by two researchers and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 27.0. First, descriptive statistics such as frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation were used to describe the participants’ demographic characteristics, disease-related information, and scores for perceived stigma, family relationships, and social isolation. Second, the Harman single-factor test was used to assess common-method bias (CMB) [31]. Third, we examined the effects of general demographic and disease-related factors on social isolation among PLWHA. If the data met the assumptions of normal distribution and homogeneity of variance, we used an independent samples t-test or one-way ANOVA; otherwise, we used the Mann-Whitney U test or the Kruskal-Wallis test. Fourth, normality tests were conducted for all continuous variables. If perceived stigma, family relationships, and social isolation were normally distributed, Pearson’s correlation coefficient analysis was used to examine the relationships among these variables. Otherwise, Spearman’s correlation analysis was performed. Fifth, the mediating effect was tested using the SPSS PROCESS macro (Model 4) with bias-corrected bootstrapping (5000 resamples). A significant mediating effect was indicated if the 95% confidence interval (CI) excluded zero. Statistical significance for two-tailed tests was set at α = 0.05.

Results

Common method bias test

The data collected in this study were analyzed using Harman’s one-factor test. The results showed that 18 factors had eigenvalues greater than 1. Among these, the largest principal component explained 35.575% of the variance. Since this value is below the critical threshold of 40%, it can be concluded that there is no significant common method bias in this study.

Sociodemographic and disease-related characteristics associated with social isolation among PLWHA

A total of 294 PLWHA were included in the study. The majority were male (78.9%), with a mean age of 42.31 years. Most participants were of Han ethnicity (80.6%), lived in urban areas (74.8%), and had no religious beliefs (94.2%). Most had been diagnosed for more than two years (82.3%), with sexual contact as the primary route of transmission (32.0% same-sex and 44.2% opposite-sex). Additionally, 74.5% had disclosed their HIV status. Significant differences in social isolation were observed by gender (t = -2.169, P = 0.031), marital status (t = 2.646, P = 0.049), and sexual orientation (t = 2.696, P = 0.046), while no significant differences were found for other demographic or disease-related variables. Role of perceived stigma in the association between family relationships and social isolation

Bootstrapping methods were used to examine the statistical associations among family relationships, perceived stigma, and social isolation. Based on the results of correlation analysis, family relationships, perceived stigma, and social isolation were significantly associated with each other; therefore, perceived stigma was included in an exploratory mediation model to further examine its role in the association between family relationships and social isolation.

The mediation analysis was conducted using Model 4 of the PROCESS macro (version 4.1) in SPSS. As shown in Fig. 2; Table 4, family relationships were negatively associated with social isolation (B = -0.221, t = -12.128, p < 0.001), perceived stigma was positively associated with social isolation (B = 0.332, t = 6.353, p < 0.001), and family relationships were negatively associated with perceived stigma (B = -1.002, t = -5.234, p < 0.001).

Discussion

This study examined the associations among family relationships, perceived stigma, and social isolation among PLWHA, with a particular focus on the potential role of perceived stigma within this relationship. The results showed that family relationships were negatively associated with perceived stigma, while perceived stigma was positively associated with social isolation. The mediation analysis further suggested that perceived stigma was statistically associated with part of the relationship between family relationships and social isolation.

Findings indicated relatively high levels of social isolation among PLWHA, consistent with results reported by Gu et al. [25] (42.78 ± 11.07). These levels appeared higher than those reported in several comparison groups, including elderly patients with coronary heart disease (39.69 ± 11.20) [32], postoperative ovarian cancer patients (35.00 [31.00, 40.00]) [33], patients with Parkinson’s disease (40.50 ± 3.50) [34], patients with cerebral infarction (35.98 ± 3.89) [35]. This pattern suggests that PLWHA may experience comparatively greater social isolation than some other patient populations, potentially reflecting increased challenges in social adaptation and interpersonal functioning.

Sociodemographic factors associated with social isolation

Several sociodemographic characteristics were associated with social isolation among PLWHA. Individuals who were separated, divorced, or widowed reported higher levels of social isolation compared with those who were married or cohabiting, which may be associated with lower levels of perceived support [33, 36]. Female participants reported higher levels of social isolation than males, which may be associated with differences in social experiences and perceived stigma [37].

In addition, individuals with same-sex orientation reported lower levels of social isolation, which may be associated with their younger age and relatively more active social networks. These findings suggest that certain subgroups, including women and individuals who are divorced, separated, or widowed, may be at greater risk of experiencing social isolation and may warrant further attention in future research and intervention development.

Association between family relationships and social isolation

Within the context of China’s family-centered social structure, family relationships represent an important component of the social environment for PLWHA [38]. In this study, stronger family relationships were associated with lower levels of social isolation, highlighting the potential relevance of family functioning in relation to social connectedness.

This finding is consistent with prior studies indicating that family serves as a primary source of support for PLWHA and is closely associated with social well-being [39, 40]. One possible explanation is that supportive family environments may be associated with emotional security, acceptance, and practical assistance, which in turn may be linked to greater social engagement.

Family cohesion reflects emotional bonding among members, while adaptability represents the ability to respond to stress and changing circumstances [41]. Higher levels of cohesion and adaptability may therefore be associated with greater perceived support and lower levels of social detachment. Conversely, insufficient family support has been associated with poorer psychological outcomes and increased social withdrawal among PLWHA [42, 43].

Overall, these findings suggest that family relationships are closely associated with social isolation among PLWHA.

Role of perceived stigma in the association between family relationships and social isolation

This study further explored the potential role of perceived stigma in linking family relationships and social isolation among PLWHA. The findings suggest that perceived stigma was statistically associated with part of the relationship between family relationships and social isolation, indicating a pattern consistent with partial mediation. This result is broadly in line with previous research [44], which has reported that individuals raised in more supportive family environments tend to report lower levels of perceived stigma and lower levels of social isolation.

One possible explanation for this pattern is that family relationships may be associated with perceived stigma in relation to individuals’psychological experiences. Unsupportive family environments may be associated with more negative self-perceptions and increased sensitivity in social interactions. In contrast, individuals with stronger family cohesion and adaptability may receive greater emotional support, acceptance, and a sense of security within the family context. These factors may be associated with lower levels of internalized stigma, including reduced fear of disclosure, shame, and perceived negative evaluation [19].

In addition, perceived stigma has been consistently associated with higher levels of social isolation. Individuals reporting higher levels of perceived stigma may be more likely to limit social interaction and participation, potentially due to concerns about rejection or disclosure [45–48]. This pattern suggests that perceived stigma may represent an important psychosocial factor linking family relationships and social isolation.

Importantly, the findings indicate that family relationships were indirectly associated with social isolation through perceived stigma. This indirect association accounted for 15.1% of the total association, suggesting that additional factors may also be involved. Family relationships may also be directly associated with social isolation through factors such as social interaction opportunities, coping strategies, or social and psychological adaptability [49]. Furthermore, broader contextual factors, such as community support and healthcare accessibility, may also be associated with these relationships.

Overall, this study suggests that family relationships and perceived stigma are jointly associated with social isolation among PLWHA, with perceived stigma functioning as a statistical linking factor between them. However, given the cross-sectional design, these findings should be interpreted as associational rather than causal.

Implications for clinical practice

This study provides potential implications for addressing social isolation among PLWHA by highlighting the associations of family relationships and perceived stigma with social isolation. The findings suggest that these factors may be relevant considerations in the development of psychosocial interventions aimed at improving social connectedness.

From the perspective of PLWHA, individuals may benefit from seeking emotional support from family members and developing coping strategies to manage perceived stigma, such as enhancing self-awareness and communication skills. These approaches may be associated with improved social engagement and maintenance of social relationships.

Healthcare professionals may also play an important role by providing patient-centered care that takes into account the potential impact of perceived stigma and family relationships. Integrating psychosocial assessment into routine care, offering stigma-related counseling, and involving family members in care planning, where appropriate, may be relevant strategies to support patients’ social well-being.

At the community level, strengthening support systems through family-based interventions, stigma reduction initiatives, and community education programs may represent useful approaches for fostering a more supportive social environment. Efforts to improve public awareness and reduce perceived stigma may also be associated with greater social participation among PLWHA.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and the mediation results should be interpreted as reflecting statistical associations rather than causal pathways. Second, all data were self-reported, which may be subject to recall bias and social desirability bias, particularly given the sensitive nature of HIV-related stigma. Third, some potentially important variables, such as mental health status and external social support, were not included in the analysis, which may have resulted in residual confounding. In addition, the study did not distinguish between incident and prevalent cases, which may also have introduced confounding. Fourth, stigma was measured as a unidimensional construct focusing on perceived stigma, which may not fully capture its multidimensional nature. Finally, the use of convenience sampling may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations or settings.

Conclusions

Social isolation remains a prominent concern among PLWHA. Family relationships are associated with lower levels of social isolation, and this association is partially linked to perceived stigma. These findings highlight the potential importance of addressing both family support and perceived stigma in future research and intervention development. However, given the cross-sectional design, the findings should be interpreted as associational rather than causal.
