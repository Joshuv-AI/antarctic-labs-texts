Inferring tumor purity using multi-omics data based on a uniform machine learning framework MoTP
Authors: Qiqi Lu, Zhixian Liu, Xiaosheng Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractExisting algorithms for assessing tumor purity are limited to a single omics data, such as gene expression, somatic copy number variations, somatic mutations, and DNA methylation. Here we proposed the machine learning Multi-omics Tumor Purity prediction (MoTP) algorithm to estimate tumor purity based on multiple types of omics data. MoTP utilizes the Bayesian Regularized Neural Networks as the prediction algorithm, and Consensus Tumor Purity Estimates as labels. We trained MoTP using multi-omics data (mRNA, microRNA, long non-coding RNA, and DNA methylation) across 21 TCGA solid cancer types. By testing MoTP in TCGA validation sets, TCGA test sets, and eight datasets outside the TCGA cancer cohorts, we showed that although MoTP could achieve excellent performance in predicting tumor purity based on a single omics data type, the integration of multiple single omics data-based predictions can enhance the prediction performance. Moreover, we demonstrated the robustness of MoTP by testing it in datasets with Gaussian noise and feature missing. Benchmark analysis showed that MoTP outperformed most established tumor purity prediction algorithms, and that it required less running time and computational resource to fulfill the predictive task. Thus, MoTP would be an attractive option for computational tumor purity inference.Keywords: tumor purity prediction, machine learning, omics data, algorithm

Introduction

The presence of non-cancerous cells, such as immune cells and stromal cells, within tumor samples can significantly confound the analysis of tumor molecular data, thereby affecting the accuracy of diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic predictions in associated diseases [1–3]. Therefore, accurately estimating tumor purity, namely the proportion of cancerous cells within a tumor sample, has emerged as a fundamental step in cancer research and clinical oncology [4, 5]. Moreover, evaluating tumor purity will enable a clearer understanding of the tumor microenvironment and its implications for cancer progression and treatment responses [6–8].

Traditional methods for assessing tumor purity involve histopathological examination that is subjective and prone to inter-observer variability [4, 5, 9]. Moreover, these methods often require high-quality, fresh tissue samples and cannot provide real-time assessments [10]. In contrast, computational approaches leveraging multi-omics data, such as gene expression profiles [11–13], copy number variations [14–16], somatic mutations [14, 17, 18], and DNA methylation [9, 19, 20], may offer a non-invasive, scalable, and objective means to estimate tumor purity from readily available clinical samples [9, 21]. In general, these methods exploit the differential molecular signatures between cancerous and non-cancerous cells to infer the composition of tumor samples. However, few of the computational approaches have used machine learning methods to infer tumor purity, except CIBERSORTx [22], which infers tumor purity with a reference to single-cell transcriptome data. In addition, most established algorithms assess tumor purity based on a single omics data, except ISOpureR [13] which predicts tumor purity based on different species of RNA.

To fill the knowledge gap, here we proposed an algorithm, termed Multi-omics Tumor Purity prediction (MoTP), to infer tumor purity using multi-omics data based on a uniform machine learning framework. MoTP was designed to predict tumor purity based on four types of omics data, including mRNA expression, DNA methylation, miRNA expression, and lncRNA expression. MoTP utilizes the Bayesian Regularized Neural Networks (BRNN) as the prediction algorithm, and Consensus Tumor Purity Estimates (CPE) as labels [9], namely actual tumor purity. MoTP used the TCGA pan-cancer samples as the training set composed of 21 cancer types. Both Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), and R-squared (R [2]) between predicted and actual values were employed to evaluate the predictive performance. We have tested MoTP in TCGA cancer cohorts, a breast cancer single-cell dataset, and cancer cell line datasets. Moreover, we have compared MoTP with 11 established algorithms in tumor purity predictive performance.

Methods

Data acquisition

We obtained multi-omics and clinical data of TCGA pan-cancer from the University of California Santa Cruz Xena platform (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/). The multi-omics data included mRNA expression profiles, miRNA expression profiles, lncRNA expression profiles, and DNA methylation profiles (Methylation450K, beta value). The mRNA, miRNA, and lncRNA expression values were batch effects adjusted, RSEM or FPKM normalized, and then log2(norm_value+1) transformed. In addition, we downloaded TCGA tumor purity values evaluated by ABSOLUTE and immunohistochemistry (IHC) from a publication [23] and the cBioPortal database (https://www.cbioportal.org/). We also downloaded a single-cell breast cancer dataset GSE75688 [24] from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). In the single-cell dataset, we calculated the tumor purity of each sample as number of cancer cells divided by number of total cells involved in the sample from the cell annotation file. The samples with extremely low (< 0.1) and extremely high (> 0.98) tumor purity were flagged as missing data, as recommended by Ghoshdastider et al [25]. Additionally, we downloaded data of mRNA expression and DNA methylation profiles [26] in 815 cancer cell lines from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database (https://www.cancerrxgene.org/), as well as mRNA and miRNA expression profiles in 939 cell lines from the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) (https://depmap.org/portal/ccle/). Here we set the tumor purity of each cell line as 1. In addition, from GEO, we obtained a scRNA-Seq dataset for immune cells from health human tissues (GSE126030), a normal stromal single-cell dataset (GSE273279), and a scRNA-seq dataset for pan-cancer cell lines (GSE157220). Based on these scRNA-seq datasets, we generated five simulated datasets (SD1, SD2, SD3, SDD4, and SD5), consisting 10, 50, 100, 150, and 200 pseudo-bulk samples, respectively. The tumor purity of these pseudo-bulk samples ranged from 0.1 to 0.98. The gene expression values of each pseudo-bulk sample were the sum of the gene expression values of the 10 000 single cells comprising the pseudo-bulk. A summary of the datasets is shown in Supplementary Table S1.

Consensus tumor purity estimates

In training our models, we used the consensus tumor purity as ground truth. The consensus tumor purity was derived by averaging predictions from four distinct methods: ESTIMATE [11], ABSOLUTE [14], LUMP (Leukocyte Unmethylation for Purity) [9], and IHC [27], as detailed in the study by Dvir et al. [9]. ESTIMATE (mRNA-based tumor purity estimator) infers the extent of infiltration by stromal and immune cells within tumor samples through gene enrichment analysis of individual samples and examining gene expression profiles from immune and stromal cells [11]. It estimates tumor purity using an empirical cumulative distribution function. ABSOLUTE (DNA-based tumor purity estimator) measures somatic copy number variations and employs a maximum likelihood model to estimate tumor purity directly [14]. We downloaded the ABSOLUTE tumor purity values for TCGA samples from the NCI Genomic Data Commons Data Portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). LUMP (DNA methylation-based tumor purity estimator) assesses tumor purity by analyzing leukocyte demethylation levels. It calculates the degree of demethylation based on the average value of 44 non-methylated immune-specific CpG sites, thereby predicting tumor purity.

The IHC method uses image analysis of hematoxylin and eosin-stained slides to visually estimate tumor purity, which is defined as the percentage of tumor cells in slides. The integration of tumor purity inferred by diverse algorithms based on a wide array of genomic and histological features is expected to enhance the robustness and accuracy of the assessment.

Data preprocessing

Before the development and assessment of the models, any missing data values were imputed by substituting them with the mean value of the corresponding molecule’s expression or methylation levels in all samples.

Training and validation sets

In the mrnMoTP model development, we allocated 70% (5869 samples) in the TCGA mRNA dataset for training and the remaining 30% (2516 samples) as the validation set. For the other models’ development, as the sample size in the other omics data is smaller than that in the mRNA data, we took the samples involved in the mrnMoTP’s training set as their training sets and the samples involved in the mrnMoTP’s validation set as their validation sets. The sample size of training and validation sets for these models are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Feature selection and prediction algorithm

Using 10-fold cross-validation (CV) in the training set, we performed feature selection. In each loop of CV, we identified differentially expressed or methylated genes using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and identified 100 upregulated or 1000 hypermethylated genes and 100 downregulated or 1000 hypomethylated genes in tumor versus normal tissues with the smallest Wilcoxon rank-sum test P-values, respectively; then we employed LASSO regression to select the important genes from the 200 genes as the features in the loop of CV. The above feature selection method was applied to miRNA, lncRNA, and DNA methylation data. As for mRNA data, we used 258 genes as primary features, which included markers of malignant cells and non-malignant cells, as identified by comparing gene expression profiles between cancer and non-cancer cells [28]; then LASSO regression was used to select the important genes from the 258 genes in each loop of CV. Finally, we determined the genes as the features of our prediction model which were the important genes commonly selected among the ten loops of CV. The features are shown in Supplementary Table S2.

In the framework, the prediction algorithm we employ is the BRNN model, implemented with the caret R package [29]. In running the BRNN model, we adopt most of its default parameter values, including epochs = 1000, mu = 0.005, mu_dec = 0.1, mu_inc = 10, mu_max = 1 × 10 [10], and min_grad = 1 × 10−10. Here epochs, mu, mu_dec, mu_inc, mu_max, and min_grad indicate the maximum number of iterations to train, positive number that controls the behavior of the Gauss-Newton optimization, mu decrease ratio, mu increase ratio, maximum mu before training is stopped, and minimum gradient, respectively. In addition, we trained the parameter of hidden neuron numbers in BRNN through 10-fold CV which ranged from 1 to 10. Finally, we determined the number of neurons as 2, 5, 2, and 6 for BRNN in mrnMOTP, mirMOTP, lncMOTP, and metMOTP, respectively. The predicted tumor purity values outside the range of [0, 1] are rounded to the nearest boundary value.

Assessment of the model’s robustness

We adopted the approach of introducing Gaussian noise into the original data to simulate the random variations and measurement errors commonly encountered in experimental data. To do so, we first calculated the total number of elements within the test data matrix. Subsequently, we generated Gaussian noise with a standard deviation of 0.1, matching the quantity to the number of elements present in the test matrix. This step was accomplished using the rnorm R function. Finally, by adding the generated noise matrix to the original matrix, we produced a new matrix containing noise in which we tested the model’s performance. This methodology allows us to assess the model’s robustness.

Comparison of prediction performance among different algorithms

To show the advantage of our algorithm over other algorithms, we compared predictive performance (R [2], MAE, and RMSE), running time (using the R function ‘system.time’) and computational resource consumption (using the R function ‘gc’) between MoTP and 11 established algorithms. These algorithms included ESTIMATE [11], CIBERSORTx [22], Bayesprism [30], DeconRNASeq [31], DeMixT [32], EPIC [12], ISOpureR [13], LUMP [9], InfiniumPurify [20], Edec [33], and PAMES [34]. Among them, ESTIMATE, CIBERSORTx, Bayesprism, DeconRNASeq, DeMixT, and EPIC evaluates tumor purity based on mRNA expression data; LUMP, InfiniumPurify, Edec, and PAMES calculates tumor purity based on DNA methylation data.

Other algorithms were executed using R package, except CIBERSORTx being run via its web interface. All these tools were run with their recommended data inputs and default settings. The ISOpureR algorithm was designed to analyze mRNA, miRNA, and lncRNA expression matrices. However, due to its extensive computational demands and inability to utilize multithreading, we restricted this algorithm to only analyze the TCGA-BLCA and TCGA-CHOL samples. Detailed descriptions of these algorithms’ methodologies can be found in Supplementary Table S3.

Quantification of the enrichment levels of gene sets

We utilized the single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) algorithm [35] to calculate the enrichment scores of immune signatures and biological processes in tumors based on the expression profiles of their marker genes. The ssGSEA algorithm was run with the GSVA R package. The signatures genes are presented in Supplementary Table S4.

Evaluation of intratumor heterogeneity

We utilized the DEPTH2 [36] and DITHER [37] algorithms to measure intratumor heterogeneity (ITH). DEPTH2 scores ITH based on mRNA expression profiles, while DITHER quantifies ITH based on both somatic mutation and copy number alteration profiles in the tumor.

Results

Overview of MoTP

The MoTP model was designed to infer tumor purity based on four types of omics data: mRNA, miRNA, lncRNA, and DNA methylation profiles, for each of which MoTP has a sub-model, namely mrnMoTP, mirMoTP, lncMoTP, and metMoTP. We trained MoTP using the TCGA pan-cancer dataset with the CPE values as ground truth. The development of MoTP involved three key steps (Fig. 1). The first step was missing values imputing, where the TCGA mRNA training set contained ~ 0.007% missing values while the other datasets contained no missing values. The second step was feature selection and parameter optimization via 10-fold CV. Finally, the MoTP model was built using the BRNN algorithm and the selected features. For public use, we have developed an R package to implement the algorithm, which is available at GitHub (https://github.com/WangX-Lab/MoTP).

An overview of MoTP workflow in four omics datasets to predict tumor purity.

MoTP achieves high performance in predicting tumor purity in TCGA datasets

In the TCGA pan-cancer training set, MoTP’s predictions achieved RMSE of 0.066, 0.076, 0.077 and 0.063 based on mrnMoTP, mirMoTP, lncMoTP, and metMoTP, respectively. The corresponding MAE values were 0.051, 0.060, 0.060 and 0.049, and R2 values were 0.803, 0.736, 0.730, and 0.818 in the 10-fold CV (Fig. 2A). The RMSE, MAE, and R2 of the average predictions in the TCGA pan-cancer training set were 0.062, 0.048, and 0.828, respectively (Fig. 2A). In the TCGA pan-cancer validation set, the RMSE were 0.067, 0.080, 0.079 0.064, and 0.062, the MAE were 0.051, 0.062, 0.061, and 0.050, and R2 were 0.791, 0.700, 0.704, and 0.806 for mrnMoTP, mirMoTP, lncMoTP, and metMoTP, respectively (Fig. 2A). The RMSE, MAE, and R2 of the average predictions in the TCGA pan-cancer validation set were 0.062, 0.048, and 0.829, respectively (Fig. 2A). It indicates that integration of multi-omics data can achieve a higher accuracy in predicting tumor purity than using an individual data type. Besides, we examined the MoTP’s predictions in individual cancer types of the validation set. Notably, the RMSE of mrnMoTP- based predictions was less than 0.08 in all 21 individual cancer types, the MAE was less than 0.07 in 21 cancer types, and R2 exceeded 0.5 in 20 cancer types (Fig. 2B). The RMSE of metMoTP-based predictions was less than 0.09 in all 21 cancer types, the MAE was less than 0.07 in 21 cancer types, and R2 was greater than 0.5 in 19 cancer types (Fig. 2B). The RMSE of both mirMoTP- and lncMoTP-based predictions was less than 0.1 in all cancer types, the MAE was less than 0.08 in all cancer types, and R2 was greater than 0.5 in 13 and 14 cancer types, respectively (Fig. 2B). The RMSE and MAE of MoTP were less than 0.08 in all 21 cancer types, the corresponding R2 was greater than 0.5 in all cancer types (Fig. 2B). Overall, these results suggest that MoTP has high performance of tumor purity prediction in individual cancer types.

To evaluate the model’s robustness, we introduced Gaussian noise to the TCGA validation datasets, thereby creating a perturbed dataset. Notably, MoTP maintained excellent performance in the noise-enhanced datasets (Fig. 2C).

We further tested MoTP using the other TCGA pan-cancer samples as TCGA test set, which involved 10 cancer types (blood cancers were excluded). The RMSE of the MoTP’s predictions in this dataset was 0.086, 0.113, 0.102, and 0.122, MAE was 0.067, 0.086, 0.079 and 0.090, and R2 was 0.735, 0.533, 0.613 and 0.570 using mrnMoTP, mirMoTP, lncMoTP, and metMoTP model, respectively (Fig. 2D). The RMSE, MAE, and R2 of the average predictions were 0.079, 0.062, and 0.785, respectively (Fig. 2D). Again, it suggests that multi-omics data-based predictions are more accurate than single omics data-based predictions. In the ten individual cancer types, the RMSE of mrnMoTP-based predictions was less than 0.1 in nine individual cancer types, the MAE was less than 0.1 in all 10 individual cancer types, and R2 exceeded 0.565 in all 10 cancer types (Fig. 2D). The RMSE of metMoTP-based predictions was less than 0.1 in six cancer types, the MAE was less than 0.1 in eight cancer types, and R2 was greater than 0.6 in seven cancer types (Fig. 2D). The R2 of both mirMoTP- and lncMoTP-based predictions was greater than 0.5 in seven cancer types, the RMSE of mirMoTP- and lncMoTP-based predictions was less than 0.1 in five and eight cancer types, respectively. Additionally, the MAE of mirMoTP- and lncMoTP-based predictions was less than 0.1 in eight and nine cancer types, respectively (Fig. 2D). The RMSE and MAE of the average predictions by the four data types were less than 0.1 in nine and 10 cancer types, respectively. R2 was greater than 0.6 in all 10 cancer types.

We further evaluated the model’s robustness by introducing Gaussian noise in the TCGA test sets. In the test sets with Gaussian noise, MoTP demonstrated comparable performance (Fig. 2E). Considering that in practical datasets, some samples may not contain all features in our model, we evaluated the model’s performance by setting 10%, 20%, 30%, 40%, 50%, 60%, 70%, 80%, and 90% features missing in the test sets, respectively. We found that when more than 80% features were missing, the MoTP’s performance declined dramatically, whereas the performance remained stable when less than 80% features were missing (Fig. 2F). The results highlight the robustness and stability of our model.

Overall, these results demonstrate the high performance of MoTP in predicting tumor purity and that integration of multi-omics data can enhance the performance of single omics data-based predictions.

Testing MoTP using single-cell datasets and cancer cell line datasets

In a breast cancer single-cell dataset (GSE75688 [24]), which contained 14 cancer samples and 515 single cells, we determined each sample’s actual tumor purity as the proportion of its tumor cells to total cells. The samples with tumor purity >0.98 or < 0.1 were excluded as outliers. This dataset had matched bulk mRNA and lncRNA expression data available, for which we inferred tumor purity of each cancer sample by MoTP. The inferred tumor purity by mrnMoTP showed low RMSE and MAE (0.087 and 0.080, respectively), with an R2 of 0.880 (supplementary Fig. S1A). When inferred tumor purity by lncMoTP, the model could still achieve good performance, with RMSE of 0.148, MAE of 0.129, and R2 of 0.875 (supplementary Fig. S1A). We further evaluated the performance of MoTP using five simulated pseudo-bulk gene expression datasets. As the simulated datasets consisted of mRNA expression data, the values of tumor purity predicted by MoTP were equivalent to those by mrnMoTP. In the five simulated datasets, the RMSE values achieved were 0.083, 0.136, 0.144, 0.159, and 0.145, respectively; the MAE values were 0.049, 0.112, 0.118, 0.131, and 0.120, respectively; and the R2 values were of 0.939, 0.962, 0.963, 0.960, and 0.959, respectively (Fig. 3).

Tumor purity prediction performance of MoTP (mrnMoTP) in simulated datasets. R2 [2], RMSE, and MAE of the predictions by MoTP in five simulated pseudo-bulk datasets (SD1, SD2, SD3, SD4, and SD5).

Furthermore, we downloaded mRNA expression and DNA methylation data for 815 cancer cell lines from GDSC. We defined tumor purity of each cell line as 1. The mRNA-based predictions by mrnMoTP achieved an RMSE of 0.084, MAE of 0.046 and median tumor purity of 0.979. The RMSE and MAE of methylation-based predictions by metMoTP were 0.070 and 0.052, respectively. The median predicted tumor purity was 0.957. The RMSE and MAE of the average predictions by mrnMoTP and metMoTP were 0.050 and 0.039, respectively, and the median predicted tumor purity was 0.961 (supplementary Fig. S1B). Besides, we predicted tumor purity of cancer cell lines from CCLE. The mRNA-based predictions by mrnMoTP achieved an RMSE of 0.111, MAE of 0.068, and median predicted tumor purity of 0.947 (supplementary Fig. S1C). In addition, miRNA-based predictions by mirMoTP achieved an RMSE of 0.107, MAE of 0.076, and median predicted tumor purity of 0.937. The RMSE and MAE of the average predictions by mrnMoTP and mirMoTP were 0.092 and 0.064, respectively, and R2 was 0.952 (supplementary Fig. S1C).

Overall, the experiments in single-cell and cancer cell line datasets support the high tumor purity prediction performance of MoTP, and highlight that integration of multi-omics data can achieve a higher accuracy than using an individual data type.

MoTP has better predictive performance than most other algorithms

We compared the tumor purity estimation performance between MoTP and 11 established methods utilizing mRNA and methylation data in the TCGA validation cohort. Among the 11 methods, ESTIMATE, CIBERSORTx, BayesPrism, DeconRNASeq, DeMixT, EPIC, and ISOpureR inferred tumor purity based on mRNA expression. Notably, mrnMoTP predictions demonstrated significantly higher R2 along with lower RMSE and MAE values (supplementary Fig. S2A). Similarly, metMoTP predictions showed higher R2 and lower RMSE and MAE values than four methylation-based algorithms (InfiniumPurify, Edec, LUMP, and PAMES) (supplementary Fig. S2A). ISOpureR also can infer tumor purity using miRNA and lncRNA data. However, its performance was inferior to MoTP in transcriptome data in TCGA-BLCA tumor purity prediction (supplementary Fig. S2A). In the TCGA test set, we also compared MoTP with the other algorithms in tumor purity prediction performance. Again, MoTP demonstrated more excellent performance than the other algorithms in most cases. In predicting tumor purity for TCGA-CHOL using transcriptome data, ISOpureR’s performance was inferior to that of MoTP (Fig. 4A).

In addition, we compared the performance between MoTP and the other algorithms in eight datasets outside the TCGA cancer cohorts, including a breast cancer single-cell dataset (GSE75688), five simulated pseudo-bulk datasets, and two cancer cell line datasets (GDSC and CCLE). Because the count values of mRNA expression in these datasets are not available, we could not calculate tumor purity using BayesPrism and DeMixT. In the breast cancer single-cell dataset (GSE75688), five mRNA-based algorithms ESTIMATE, CIBERSORTx, EPIC, DeconRNASeq, and ISOpureR achieved RMSE of 0.272, 0.290, 0.222, 0.249, and 0.280, respectively, compared to 0.087 by mrnMoTP; the MAE of 0.215, 0.231, 0.169, 0.230, and 0.240, respectively, compared to 0.080 by mrnMoTP; the R2 by ESTIMATE, CIBERSORTx, EPIC, and DeconRNASeq was 0.462, 0.618, 0.264, 0.206, and 0.446, respectively, compared to 0.880 by mrnMoTP (Fig. 4C). Meanwhile, we used lncMoTP and ISOpureR to predict tumor purity based on lncRNA data in the dataset. Results showed that lncMoTP had higher accuracy than ISOpureR (R [2]: 0.927 versus 0.146; RMSE: 0.087 versus 0.418; MAE: 0.075 versus 0.318) (supplementary Fig. S2B). Notably, in the five simulated pseudo-bulk datasets, MoTP consistently outperformed the other algorithms in tumor purity evaluation, with the largest R2 and the least RMSE and MAE values (Fig. 4B).

Based on the GDSC cell line mRNA dataset, ESTIMATE, CIBERSORTx, EPIC, and DeconRNASeq achieved RMSEs of 0.048, 0.433, 0.254, and 0.641, respectively, compared to 0.084 by mrnMoTP; the MAE of the algorithms were 0.023, 0.249, 0.253, and 0.637, respectively, compared to 0.046 by mrnMoTP; the median tumor purity predicted by these algorithms was 0.995, 0.961, 0.751, and 0.359, respectively, compared to 0.979 by mrnMoTP (supplementary Fig. S2 C). Based on the GDSC DNA methylation dataset, four methylation-based algorithms LUMP, InfiniumPurify, PAMES, Edec achieved RMSEs of 0.135, 0.736, 0.798 and 0.717, respectively, versus 0.070 by metMoTP; their MAEs were 0.097,0.641,0.795 and 0.686, respectively, compared to 0.052 by mrnMoTP; the median tumor purity predicted by the four algorithms was 0.940, 0.118, 0.193, and 0.307, respectively, compared to 0.957 by metMoTP (Fig. 4D). In the CCLE mRNA dataset, ESTIMATE, CIBERSORTx, EPIC, and DeconRNASeq, had RMSEs of 0.048, 0.432, 0.253, and 0.651, respectively, compared to 0.111 by mrnMoTP; the MAE of the algorithms were 0.023, 0.249, 0.253, and 0.646, respectively, compared to 0.068 by mrnMoTP; the median tumor purity predicted by these algorithms was 0.996, 0.974,0.751, and 0.349, respectively, compared to 0.947 by mrnMoTP (supplementary Fig. S2C).

Taken together, these comparisons suggest higher tumor purity prediction performance of MoTP than the established algorithms

We also compared running time and computational resource consumption among these algorithms (Fig. 4C). Because our algorithm has a pre-trained model, it displays a significant advantage over other algorithms in running time. Moreover, MoTP required the least peak memory usage than other algorithms, excepted EPIC and DeconRNASeq in Ncells (Fig. 4C).

Associations of tumor purity inferred by MoTP with immune and stromal signatures and ITH.

We further analyzed the association between the predicted tumor purity and related biological signatures in TCGA test set (Fig. 5A). For a tumor bulk, lower tumor purity indicates higher immune and stromal components. As expected, in the TCGA test set, the tumor purity predicted by MoTP showed significant negative correlations with tumor immune infiltration levels and stromal signatures (Fig. 5B). Meanwhile, the tumors with higher tumor purity should display higher degree of ITH. Aligning with this, the tumor purity predicted by MoTP correlated positively with ITH scores by DITHER and DEPTH2 (Fig. 5C). Similar results were also observed in the TCGA validation set (Supplementary Fig. S3). These results support the reliability of the tumor purity inference by MoTP.

Discussion

Current algorithms for assessing tumor purity are limited to a single omics data, such as gene expression [11, 38], somatic copy number variations [14, 15], somatic mutations [14, 22], and DNA methylation [19, 20]. Although the ISOpureR [13] algorithm is also designed to predict tumor purity based on multiple types of RNA data, including mRNA, miRNA, and non-coding RNA, this algorithm is extremely time-consuming that limits its practical application. Here we have developed an algorithm (MoTP) for predicting tumor purity based on mRNA, miRNA, lncRNA, and DNA methylation data, respectively. Our analysis shows that MoTP has excellent performance in predicting tumor purity based on a single omics data type. Moreover, the integration of multiple single omics data-based predictions can enhance the accuracy of tumor purity predictions based on a single omics data. Indeed, our experiments showed that the multi-omics-based tumor purity prediction had higher accuracy than single omics-based predictions in most cases (Fig. 2, Fig. 4, Fig. S1, and Fig. S2). In a few cases, single omics-based predictions showed better performance than the multi-omics-based prediction. A potential explanation for this could be that the prediction performance by mrnMoTP, mirMoTP, lncMoTP, and/or metMoTP was much lower to compromising the performance of MoTP. Comparative analysis showed that MoTP has superior predictive performance than most established algorithms, and that MoTP requires less running time and computational resource to fulfill the predictive task. Thus, MoTP would be an attractive option for computational tumor purity inference.

In general, our algorithm is robust. First, because our algorithm can predict tumor purity based on any of the mRNA, miRNA, lncRNA, and DNA methylation data types, the absence of some data types will not affect our algorithm’s implementation. Of course, along with more data types available, our algorithm will be able to obtain more accurate predictions of tumor purity. Second, our algorithm can accurately predict tumor purity even when some features are missing, as evidenced by that MoTP can achieve high prediction performance even when 80% of features are missing. Lastly, our algorithm shows superior performance not only in data that are homogeneous with the training set, but also in the nonhomogeneous data, such as simulated pseudo-bulk data and cell line data.

In practice, because input datasets often contain a large number of missing values, the methods for imputing missing data have been proposed. Here we have adopted a commonly used imputing method, mean imputation. The main advantages of this method are easy implementation and maintaining the sample size. However, this method underestimates the variability of data. We tested our algorithm under the condition of varying proportions (ranging from 0% to 90%) of missing data in the TCGA pan-cancer test set. We observed that when the proportion of missing data is less than 30%, the MoTP’s performance declined slightly, whereas its accuracy dropped more than 10% compared to the original results when the proportion of missing data exceeds 30%. Thus, we recommend users use our model under the condition of the input data having <30% missing data.

This study has several limitations. First, MoTP relies on the markers of tumor and normal tissues or cells for prediction. Except the primary mRNA markers identified from single-cell pan-caner datasets, the markers for other data types were identified using the TCGA pan-cancer dataset. Therefore, although the predictive results are satisfactory in the TCGA datasets, it needs more datasets outside of TCGA to test the algorithm. Second, because MoTP is a supervised learning algorithm, its reliability heavily depends on the labels, namely actual tumor purity. Here we have used the CPE as labels, which are the average predictions from four types of data: mRNA (ESTIMATE [11]), DNA (ABSOLUTE [14]), methylation (LUMP [9]), and immunohistochemistry (IHC [27]). The CPE measure may not represent true tumor purity values, although there is no perfect method to estimate actual tumor purity.
