VCAM-1+ Mesenchymal Stem/Stromal Cells Reveal Preferable Efficacy Upon an Experimental Autoimmune Encephalomyelitis Mouse Model of Multiple Sclerosis Over the VCAM-1− Counterpart
Authors: Haixia Liu, Dongqing Cui, Shasha Huangfu, Xiaojun Wang, Xiao Yu, Hui Yang, Xiaolei Zheng, Yan Li, Jianzhong Bi, Leisheng Zhang, Ping Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Despite the considerable progress in mesenchymal stem/stromal cells (MSCs)-based novel intervention of multiple sclerosis (MS), yet the disease-modifying effect of VCAM-1− MSCs and novel VCAM-1+ counterpart is largely obscure. In this study, we took advantage of the EAE mouse model and VCAM-1+ human umbilical cord-derived MSCs (hUC-MSCs) for the evaluation of the therapeutic effect of systematic MSCs infusion. On the one hand, we compared the protective effect of VCAM-1− and VCAM-1+ hUC-MSCs against the clinical symptoms, demyelination, active glia cells and neuroinflammation in EAE mice by conducting multifaceted detections upon spinal cord and brain tissues. On the other hand, we conducted RNA-sequencing (RNA-SEQ) and multidimensional bioinformatics analyses for the evaluation of the transcriptomic features of spinal cord tissue in EAE mice after systematic hUC-MSCs infusion. Compared to those with VCAM-1− hUC-MSCs injection, VCAM-1+ mice showed further remission in clinical manifestations, and in particular, the inflammatory infiltration and active glial cells. Mice in all groups revealed conservations in overall gene expression profiling and somatic mutation spectrum. The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between EAE mice and those with hUC-MSCs infusion were mainly involved in neuroinflammation and inflammatory response. Our findings indicated the feasibility of VCAM-1+ hUC-MSCs for multiple sclerosis treatment, which would supply new references for the development of novel VCAM-1+ MSCs-based cytotherapy in future.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11064-024-04267-w.

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is an autoimmune inflammatory disease of the central nervous system (CNS) characterized by widespread demyelination and varying degrees of irreversible axonal injury and loss [1, 2]. According to the Atlas of MS database, approximately 2.5 million people are suffering from MS worldwide due to the mysterious pathogenesis and the deficiency of the current conventional treatment paradigm in clinical practice [1, 3]. State-of-the-art literatures have put forward the feasibility of the experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) model for mimicking the pathological features and demyelination of MS, together with evaluating the efficacy of therapeutic regimens for CNS-directed myelin-specific autoimmunity [4–6].

Mesenchymal stem/stromal cells (MSCs) are typically defined as heterogeneous multipotent stem cells with self-renewal and multi-lineage differentiation potential into diverse mesoderm-derived lineages [7, 8]. As the most promising modality of allogeneic cytotherapy, MSCs and the derivations of diverse origins have been employed in neurodegenerative diseases, such as Parkinson’s disease [9], Alzheimer’s disease [10] and Huntington’s disease [11]. As to EAE, Abramowski et al. reported the minimal effect of MSCs in ameliorating the pathological manifestations of CNS in mice, whereas Wang et al. described the favorable outcomes by systematic infusion of human embryonic stem cell-derived MSCs (hESC-MSCs) [12, 13].

Thus, there’s an urgent need in the development of novel cytotherapy for the administration of MS with the aid of the EAE models [14, 15]. However, the multiple inherent defects of gene-edited MSCs further hindered the potentially wide applications, including high cost, technological complexity and safety hazards. For the purpose, we and other investigators are devoted to identifying and preparing cell counterparts with specific properties. It has been reported that divergent signaling and pathways such as mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) and NF-κB are associated with VCAM-1 expression in its response to abundant stimuli [16, 17]. Also, VCAM-1 is known to be critical for T cell activation and leukocyte recruitment to the inflammation site [18]. Therefore, it plays a key role in evoking effective immune responses. Moreover, VCAM-1 is critical for MSCs-mediated immunosuppression [19]. Studle et al. and Du et al. demonstrated the functionally distinct NCAM1+ (CD56+) and VCAM-1+ (CD106+) MSC subsets with preferable efficacy upon cartilaginous disease and vascular disease, respectively [20]. Strikingly, we and the collaborators recently reported the high-efficient generation of VCAM-1+ MSCs for diverse disorders, including acute lung injury (ALI) [21], aplastic anaemia [22].

Therewith, we took advantage of our well-established VCAM-1+ hUC-MSCs for the evaluation of the efficacy upon EAE mice.

Materials and Methods

Cell Preparation

All hUC-MSCs we used meet ISCT criteria. The VCAM-1− hUC-MSCs were provided by Universal Biomedical Research Institute (Zibo, China). VCAM-1+ hUC-MSCs were purchased from Beijing H&B Biotech Co. Ltd (Beijing, China), which were prepared from VCAM-1− hUC-MSCs at passage 3–6 with precondition in MSC culture medium supplemented with the cytokine cocktails for 48 h, including 10 ng/ml recombinant human IL-4 (PeproTech), 10 ng/ml recombinant human IL-1β (PeproTech), and 20 ng/ml recombinant human IFN-γ (PeproTech). HUC-MSCs were maintained in MSC culture medium, including DMEM/F12 basal medium (Hyclone), 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco), 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco), 1% L-glutamine (Gibco), 2 ng/mL bFGF and 10 ng/mL EGF (PeproTech) (PeproTech). For cell passage, hUC-MSCs were incubated with 0.25% Trypsin/EDTA (Gibco, USA) and harvested by centrifugation at 300 × g, and seeded in DMEM basal medium (Gibco, USA), 1% penicillin and streptomycin (ThermoFisher, USA) and 10% FBS (Australia) at 37 °C, 5% CO2 as we detailed described [22].

Construction of EAE Model and hUC-MSCs Transplantation

For the induction of experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) model, female C57BL/6 J mice (aged 6–8 weeks) were purchased from HFKbio Co. Ltd (Beijing, China). Mice were housed under a 12 h light/dark artificial cycle in temperature- and humidity-controlled rooms for 2 weeks. Then, mice were randomly allocated to four groups (n = 7), including the negative control group (denoted as C), the EAE model group (denoted as E), EAE mice with VCAM-1− hUC-MSCs (denoted as U) or VCAM-1+ hUC-MSCs (denoted as V) infusion. For the induction of EAE model, mice were immunized with complete Freund adjuvant (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) containing 4 mg/ml Mycobacterium tuberculosis (strain H37Ra; Difco, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) and 300 mg MOG35-55 (day 0), and followed by intraperitoneal injection of 500 ng of pertussis toxin (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) as reported [23]. Non-injected C57BL/6 J mice were used as controls. 106 VCAM-1- hUC-MSCs or VCAM-1+ hUC-MSCs were injected intravenously via the tail vein on the day after the onset of illness.The weights and EAE scores of mice were evaluated daily from day 0 to day 28. Clinical scores of the EAE model were assessed based on KONO’s Score [24]. Ethical approval for all experimental operations was obtained from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of The Second Hospital of Shandong University (approval No. KYLL-2023LW099) according to the International Guiding Principles for Biomedical Research Involving Animals.

Quantitative Real-Time Polymerase Chain Reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNAs in spinal cord tissue and brain tissue was extracted using the TRIZol reagent (ThermoFisher, USA) as we recently described with several modifications [25]. Complementary DNAs (cDNAs) were synthesized using Evo M-MLV RT Kit (AG, Hunan, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. QRT-PCR was performed with qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) using QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA). The data were calculated by utilizing the 2−ΔΔCt method as we reported before [26]. β-Actin was served as internal reference. The list of the primers was available in Supplementary Information: Supplementary Table S1.

Western-Blotting Assay

Western-blotting assay was performed as we described before [25, 27]. In detail, total proteins were enriched using the RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) supplemented with a protease (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and phosphatase inhibitor (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) cocktail. Protein concentration was determined by the BCA kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China). After denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, the total protein was turned to PAGE gels (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and transferred to Immobilon-P polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, USA). Then, the membranes were blocked in buffer (5% nonfat dry milk, Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20) for 1 h at room temperature (RT), and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (MBP, β-actin) and the indicated secondary antibodies (zsbio, Beijing, China) for 1 h. Finally, the membranes were developed with the ECL kit (ThermoFisher, USA) under an automatic imager (Tanon 5200) and analyzed with the Image J software (NIH, USA). The list of the antibodies was available in Supplementary Information: Supplementary Table S2.

Pathological Histochemical Staining

Brain and spinal cord tissues were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Merck, Germany) for 48 h. Tissues were dehydrated in ethanol solution, followed by embedded with paraffin and sectioning. To evaluate the tissue structure and myelination, the sections were respectively stained with H&E (Solarbio, Beijing, China) and Luxol fast blue (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). As for Luxol fast blue, tissue sections were placed in preheated 0.1% LuxoL fast blue solution and stained at 65 °C for 4 h, then were immediately placed in differentiation solution. The expression of neural and inflammatory biomarkers was detected by immunohistochemical (IHC) staining (Zsbio, Beijing, China) with the indicated antibodies (Iba, Gfap, CD45, CD68, Olig2). The detailed information of the indicated antibodies was available in Supplementary Information: Supplementary Table S2.

Immunofluorescent Staining

For immunofluorescent (IF) staining, brain and spinal cord tissues were made into frozen sections. After Fixing with 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h, tissues were dehydrated in gradient sucrose solutions. Then, the sections were embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound (Sakura, Japan), and incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight and secondary antibodies (Abcam, USA) at RT for 2 h as well as DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 20 min. Finally, the sections were observed with the laser confocal microscopy (Zeiss LSM 800, Germany) and analyzed with the Image J software (NIH, USA). The detailed information of the indicated antibodies was available in Supplementary Information: Supplementary Table S2.

RNA-SEQ and Bioinformatics Analyses

Briefly, the total RNAs were harvested from spinal cord tissues of mice in the indicated groups (C, E, U, V) using the TRIZol reagent (ThermoFisher, USA) according to the manufacture’s instructions. Then, the RNA samples were sent to BGI (Shenzhen, China) for RNA-SEQ analysis. The multifaceted bioinformatics analyses were accomplished by utilizing the online database and platforms, including gene ontology biological process (GOBP), Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG), Venn Map, HeatMap, principal component analysis (PCA), volcano plot, and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) as we described before [28, 29]. The list of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was available in Supplementary Table S3.

Statistical Analysis

All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 Software (GraphPad Software Inc, USA) as we described before [25, 27]. In detail, unpaired parametric t test (two-tailed) was performed in data comparison of two groups. One-way ANOVA was used to assess multiple-group differences with multiple comparison tests. Differences between two or more experimental groups under multiple conditions or over time were analyzed using two-way ANOVA. Result was corrected for multiple comparisons using statistical hypothesis testing (Tukey–Kramer Test). All data were presented as Mean ± SEM. Only when P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

VCAM-1+ hUC-MSCs Showed Moderate Superiority Over the VCAM-1− Counterpart in Alleviating the Clinical Manifestations of EAE Mice

In recent years, we and other investigators have identified the preferable signatures of VCAM-1+ hUC-MSCs over the VCAM-1− counterpart, including pro-angiogenesis, the immunomodulatory and anti-fibrotic effects [22, 30]. In this study, we took advantage of the well-established EAE mouse model and conducted systemic infusion of the indicated VCAM-1+ and VCAM-1− hUC-MSCs to verify the efficacy upon multiple sclerosis. As shown in Fig. 1a, the body weight of EAE mice was gradually declined since day 10 compared to the C group, whereas it was efficiently rescued by hUC-MSCs treatment. In particular, VCAM-1+ hUC-MSCs rather than VCAM-1− counterpart revealed better reversal effect upon the decline of body weight in EAE mice. Instead, the continuous increase of mean clinical score in EAE mice was suppressed by both hUC-MSCs infusion, and ameliorative effect was more significant in the V group over the U group on the third week after immunization (Fig. 1b). Meanwhile, both the cumulative and maximal clinical scores in EAE mice showed further decrease after VCAM-1+ hUC-MSCs treatment compared to the U group (Fig. 1c–d). And the condition of demyelination was effectively relieved by both VCAM-1+ and VCAM-1− hUC-MSCs treatment (Fig. 2a). In consistence, the demyelination and the concomitant myelin loss in EAE mice were largely rescued by VCAM-1+ and VCAM-1− hUC-MSCs infusion according to IF staining of Mbp (Fig. 2b–c). Furthermore, with the aid of western blot analysis, we observed the significant rescue of the declined expression of Mbp in both spinal cord and brain tissues after hUC-MSCs treatment (Fig. 2d–f). What’s more, these results didn’t show differences between VCAM-1+ and VCAM-1− hUC-MSCs groups. Subsequently, we tried to verify the impact of both hUC-MSCs upon the expression pattern of proinflammatory factors in the central nervous system (CNS) of EAE mice. Both the spinal cord and brain tissue of EAE mice showed massive accumulation of diverse proinflammatory factors (Ifn-γ, Tnf-α, Il-6, and Il-1β), which could be effectively suppressed after hUC-MSCs injection. Notably, in spinal cord, Ifn-γ、Tnf-α and Il-1β were partially alleviated by VCAM-1− hUC-MSCs but completed rescued by VCAM-1+ hUC-MSCs (Fig. 4g). Meanwhile, with the aid of principal component analysis (PCA), we intuitively observed the variations in the affinity and gene expression among the indicated groups, which further confirmed the variations in the gene expression pattern (Fig. 5c). Subsequently, by conducting gene ontology biological process (GOBP) analysis, we found the specific enrichment of DEGs between the U and E groups in immunoregulation- and inflammatory response-associated bioprocesses, including distant hierarchical clustering relationship, innate and adaptive immune responses, and cellular response to proinflammatory factors (e.g., IFN-β, IFN-γ, IL-1β, and IFN-γ) (Fig. 5e). Collectively, these data indicated the conservations in the landscape of gene expression pattern and multidimensional variations in diverse bioprocesses between EAE mice and those after both hUC-MSCs treatment.

Discussion

According to previous data, MSCs therapy seemed to be an efficacious and safe therapeutic strategy in treating MS [32] Most of patients remained stable based on the EDSS score at least after MSCs treatment. In addition, as no major adverse events were identified. Recent studies are focused on development of new technology, evaluating its long-term effectiveness and optimisation of MSCs doses. Transplantation of IFN-γ-primed hUC-MSCs significantly reduced inflammation in EAE mice by via the Foxp3/ROR-gammat/STAT3 signaling pathway [33]. Lu et al. found that combined intravenous and intrathecal low-dose hUC-MSCs transplantation is safe and feasible in RRMS patients in the long term [34]. Another study proved that there was an advantage of administering two doses compared to one, which warrants more extensive studies on larger numbers of MS patients [35]. What’s more, it was reported that VCAM-1 is critical for MSCs-mediated immunosuppression [19]. For the purpose, in this study, we assessed the ameliorative effect of a single systemic infusion of VCAM-1− and VCAM-1+ hUC-MSCs both at the pathogenic and transcriptomic levels.

Firstly, we have shown that intravenously applied VCAM-1+ hUC-MSCs ameliorate clinical symptoms and induce recovery from EAE efficiently and quickly, especially at the peak of disease. Playing important roles in MS pathology, the interactions between glial, neural, and immune cells offer potential targets for therapeutic intervention [36]. Thus, to figure out the causes of different treatment effects, we detected the capacity for remyelination, inhibition of glia cells and inflammatory infiltration.

Compared to the VCAM-1− counterpart, VCAM-1+ hUC-MSCs showed moderate superiority in the remission of the activity of gliocyte and neuroinflammation in EAE mice. It is widely known that multiple sclerosis is a neuroinflammatory disease of CNS [37]. The pathogenesis of MS is described as autoreactive T cells activated in peripheral lymphoid organs migrating to the CNS where the inflammation occurs [38]. MSCs have multiple immunomodulatory properties via the direct contact with immune cells and their paracrine activity [39]. Previous studies showed MSCs inhibited EAE by suppressing Th1/Th17 activation [40], reducing infiltration of inflame cells [41] and inflammatory cytokine expression [42]. Vascular cellular adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1), also known as CD106, is a 90-kDa glycoprotein that mainly expresses in endothelial cells and MSCs [43]. Numerous literatures have indicated the involvement of VCAM-1 in adhesion and migration of leukocytes during inflammation, which is adequate to accelerate the response of MSCs to inflammatory factors [44, 45]. In recent years, Yang et al. and Wei et al. demonstrated the unique immunomodulatory properties of VCAM-1+ MSCs over the VCAM-1− counterpart upon vascular disease and aplastic anemia [22, 46]. Very recently, our group further illuminated the superiority in anti-fibrosis and cerebral vascular repair [21, 25]. In ALI mice, VCAM-1+ hUC-MSCs had preferable ameliorative effect upon the pathological appearance and proinflammatory cytokine secretion [21]. We found that VCAM-1+ hUC-MSCs exhibited improved efficacy against neurological defects in rats with cerebral infarction, accompanied by inhibition of the NLRP3-mediated inflammatory response [25]. Herein, we further dissected the preferable outcomes of EAE mice with a systemic injection of VCAM-1+ hUC-MSCs, including the significant reduction in the expression of inflammatory factors and in both spinal cord and brain tissues, which would benefit the further investigation of novel therapeutic MSC products for MS and the concomitant neuroinflammation.

Meanwhile, EAE mice with both hUC-MSCs transplantation revealed minimal differences in overall gene expression profiling and somatic variation but with diverse variations in inflammatory infiltration- and demyelination- associated gene sets when compared to the EAE mice. Besides, RNA-SEQ and bioinformatics analyses indicated that several signaling pathways participated in the MSCs’ treatment on EAE, such as NF-kappa B, IL-17, neurotrophin signaling pathway, which were consistent with previous studies [42, 47, 48]. According to the results of GSEA, we firstly found the differences of Wnt/β-catenin and PPAR pathway activation between U and E groups. Though the contributions of Wnt/β-catenin and PPAR pathway to MS pathogenesis have raised certain concerns [49–51], their association with MSCs treatment is still unclear, which deserves further study. Additionally, it’s great interesting to further verify the potential biofunction of the indicated signaling pathway between the V and U groups in future.

Besides, limited by small sample size and time, we mainly focused on the long-period effect of VCAM-1+ hUC-MSC administration, yet the detailed information during the treatment process still needs further illumination. In future, we will further continuously optimize the therapeutic regimen by increasing sample size and VCAM-1+ hUC-MSC doses. Overall, this study provides new references of VCAM-1+ hUC-MSCs as candidates for multiple sclerosis treatment, which may serve as a new strategy for the further development of novel MSC-based cytotherapy in future.
