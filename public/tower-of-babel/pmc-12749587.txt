Estimation of walking energy expenditure using a single sacrum-mounted IMU based on biomechanically-informed machine learning
Authors: Jinsung Jung, Hyerim Lim, Hyunho Jeong, Sameer Upadhye, Joo H. Kim, Sukyung Park
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Energy expenditure (EE) estimation during walking has significant applications in healthcare, sports science, and rehabilitation, but remains challenging to measure in real-world settings. Existing wearable approaches often require complex multi-sensor systems or extensive training datasets, limiting their practical implementation. We propose a biomechanically-informed machine learning approach to estimate EE during walking in healthy subjects, based on sagittal joint powers of the body segment, derived from a single sacrum-mounted inertial measurement unit (IMU). Segmental analysis confirmed that the stance-leg joint mechanical power exhibits the strong correlation with whole-body EE. Scaling relationships between efficiency-weighted segmental joint mechanical power and whole-body EE were first established by regression analysis. Segmental analysis revealed that sagittal-plane joint mechanical power of the body segment, particularly the stance leg strongly correlates with whole-body joint mechanical power (R > 0.9 across subjects and walking speeds). Leveraging this relationship and a lightweight artificial neural network that predicts segmental joint dynamics from an IMU data, the whole-body EE was estimated from the stance-leg sagittal power with efficiency coefficients and the regression-based scale factor. The approach was validated in 13 healthy adults walking at multiple speeds on a treadmill, with ground-truth EE measured via indirect calorimetry. The results demonstrated remarkable consistency both within individuals across speeds and across different subjects (coefficient of variation < 2%), suggesting a robust biomechanical linkage. Furthermore, joint dynamics of the stance leg were accurately estimated from single sacrum-mounted IMU data incorporating a single-leg stance partition and gait speed information. The resulting stance-leg power estimates enabled accurate EE estimation (RMSE 0.69 W/kg) across an independent cohort. This study demonstrates that sagittal-plane joint mechanical power of the body segment particularly the stance leg serves as a reliable biomechanical surrogate for whole-body EE during walking, which can be robustly inferred through the efficiency-weighting and regression-scaling. The proposed method offers a simple and practical solution for wearable EE monitoring, with potential applications in clinical rehabilitation, exercise prescription, and daily health tracking.

Introduction

Energy expenditure (EE) is a key physiological indicator widely used in various fields, including gait analysis, rehabilitation monitoring, exercise prescription, and control optimization of assistive devices1–4. In particular, accurate assessment of walking EE can provide valuable information for monitoring rehabilitation progress, evaluating the effectiveness of assistive devices, and estimating activity levels during daily living5–7. The gold-standard method for measuring EE is indirect calorimetry, which calculates metabolic energy consumption based on respiratory gas exchange8,9. However, the equipment and settings for the indirect calorimetry tests severely limit its applicability in outdoor and daily living environments, prompting a growing demand for more practical and convenient EE estimation methods10. Several predictive models6,11–17 have been developed to address the limitations of indirect calorimetry, but most of these require whole-body kinematic and kinetic data, including ground reaction forces (GRFs) and joint motions, as inputs. Collecting such data typically necessitates extensive laboratory-based sensing systems, such as force plates and motion capture systems, which are impractical for real-world or outdoor measurements. With the advancement of wearable sensor technologies, numerous efforts have been made to estimate the walking EE using wearable devices, particularly inertial measurement units (IMUs). These approaches primarily rely on machine learning models that predict EE from kinematic data obtained from limited body segments. For example, Beltrame et al.18 and Cook et al.19 used sacral accelerometers combined with electrocardiogram (ECG) sensors; Hedegaard et al.20 employed 17 IMUs with heart rate monitoring; Lopes et al.21 incorporated respirometry, heart rate, and electromyography (EMG) data; and Slade et al.22 utilized IMUs mounted on the thigh and shank. However, these approaches either require multiple sensors, which compromises convenience, or demand large amounts of labeled training data obtained through indirect calorimetry, making data collection costly and burdensome.

The relationship between mechanical work and EE has been explored for decades23–25. The whole-body joint mechanical power generated during walking is recognized as one of the primary determinants of EE11. Additionally, several studies on various types of human movement—including running and directional changes—have demonstrated that EE can be estimated through relatively simple linear relationships with mechanical power26,27. In this context, joint mechanical power represents the rate of mechanical work done by the joints, while EE refers to the physiological energy expended by the body. The latter is often estimated by converting mechanical power through experimentally derived efficiency weights. However, calculating whole-body joint power requires inverse dynamics analysis based on comprehensive kinematic data and GRFs, which in turn demands elaborate experimental setups involving motion capture systems, multiple reflective markers, and embedded force plates12. As a result, the EE estimation based on the whole-body joint power remains largely impractical outside of laboratory environments. To overcome these limitations, recent studies have explored methods for estimating joint dynamics using minimal sensor configurations28–31. Notably, Lim et al.32 proposed a method to accurately estimate the joint dynamics of the lower limb using a single sacrum-mounted IMU. This approach reconstructs the sagittal-plane joint dynamics of the lower limb based solely on the IMU signals and has demonstrated promising accuracy. If such single-IMU joint dynamics estimation methods can be extended to the EE estimation, it may become feasible to estimate EE without requiring direct measurements of the whole-body joint power.

In this study, we introduce a biomechanically-informed machine learning framework for the estimation of the whole-body EE during walking using a single IMU dataset. Different experiments are designed to collect data for distinct components in this study, including analysis, modeling, and machine-learning training and testing. The machine-learning method used to estimate joint dynamics from a single IMU is guided by the mechanical analysis of gait data. Biomechanical and energetic models enhance the accuracy and robustness of the machine-learning framework, as compared against an existing direct data-driven method. This approach offers a practical and interpretable solution for wearable EE monitoring with minimal sensor requirements.

Methods

The objective of this study was to develop a biomechanically-informed framework for estimating walking EE using a single sacrum-mounted IMU. Our approach aimed to identify which biomechanical variables fundamentally contribute to the whole-body EE during walking and leverage this understanding to construct a minimal yet effective estimation framework. Human walking emerges from whole-body coordination, rather than independent segmental motions. In the prior work by Lim et al.32, sacral kinematics — approximately representing the center of mass — have been shown to contain sufficient information to estimate lower-limb joint dynamics via machine learning. Building on these findings, we hypothesized that the sagittal-plane joint mechanical power of body segments may serve as a biomechanical proxy for whole-body joint power, and thus for EE estimation. This will allow whole-body EE to be inferred from sacral IMU data, based on its predictive joint dynamics.

The proposed framework consisted of two sequential stages. First, we experimentally examine the relationship between the segmental sagittal-plane joint mechanical power, the whole-body joint mechanical power, and EE across varying speeds and subjects. The segmental contribution analysis was conducted using comprehensive joint dynamics data to quantify how much sagittal-plane joint power of each body segment contributes to the whole-body joint power, thereby establishing the physiological relevance of focusing on segmental dynamics for reliable EE estimation during walking. Second, EE estimation modeling was performed by scaling the estimated joint dynamics of body segment using muscle efficiency-weighted factors that account for known differences in metabolic cost between positive and negative muscle work25,33. We demonstrate that once the joint power of the body segment is reliably estimated from a single sacrum-mounted IMU data using a lightweight neural network, EE can be accurately and robustly inferred via a regression-based scale factor.

Experimental data collection

Three distinct datasets (Fig. 1) were collected for complementary purposes in this study with experimental protocols approved by the Institutional Review Boards (NYU: 14-10136, KAIST: KH2023-091). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. All methods were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. First, segmental contribution analysis required comprehensive whole-body joint dynamics data to quantify how much each segment’s power contributes to whole-body joint power. For this purpose, a whole-body kinematic and kinetic dataset was utilized, in which joint dynamics were measured during overground walking at various speeds. Second, for developing and validating the single-IMU based EE estimation framework, a training dataset was collected under controlled treadmill conditions. This dataset included synchronized measurements of joint dynamics, sacrum-mounted IMU signals, and metabolic EE via indirect calorimetry. The training dataset enabled direct validation of whether the segmental sagittal power can accurately predict EE across different subjects and walking speeds under consistent experimental conditions. Using the training dataset, we also verified the accuracy of the estimated joint dynamics of body segment obtained from sacral IMU data. Third, to further evaluate the performance of the proposed method and to compare it with previously reported methods, an independent test dataset was collected. The experimental protocol for this test dataset was identical to that of the training dataset including sacrum-mounted IMU signals and metabolic EE, but included a completely independent cohort of participants. Whole-body kinematic and kinetic dataset: Segmental contribution analysis

Eight healthy adults with no history of lower limb musculoskeletal disorders were recruited (male: 3, female: 5; age: 34.8 ± 13.8 years; height: 172.6 ± 8.5 cm; mass: 68.0 ± 12.2 kg). Participants walked along a 30-meter walkway at five different speeds (70%, 85%, 100%, 115%, and 130% of self-selected speed) in randomized order, with five trials per speed condition. Whole-body kinematics were captured using a 12-camera motion analysis system (Motion Analysis, USA) with 38 reflective markers positioned on anatomical landmarks at 100 Hz. Ground reaction forces were measured using embedded force plates (Bertec and AMTI, USA) at 5000 Hz. Both kinematic and kinetic data were filtered using a zero-phase 4th-order Butterworth low-pass filter with a 6-Hz cutoff frequency12.

Training/test dataset: estimation of EE from a single IMU

Two independent subject groups for training and testing were recruited. Nine healthy adult males participated in the training group (age: 26.2 ± 3.3 years; height: 174.4 ± 5.9 cm; mass: 66.7 ± 9.6 kg) for developing the estimation model, performing leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) validation, and training during performance comparison. Thirteen healthy adult males participated in the test group (age: 27.7 ± 4.8 years; height: 172.1 ± 6.0 cm; mass: 71.9 ± 7.2 kg) exclusively for independent testing and were completely separate from the training group. All participants had no history of lower limb musculoskeletal disorders. Participants walked on an instrumented treadmill at five different speeds (1.0, 1.25, 1.5, 1.625, and 1.75 m/s) presented in a randomized order. Each trial lasted 6 minutes, and data from the final 3 minutes were used for analysis to ensure steady-state metabolic conditions. Three-minute rest periods were provided between trials to minimize fatigue. For the training group, lower-limb kinematics were collected using a 13-camera motion capture system (MX T-50, VICON, UK) with 24 reflective markers placed on the lower-limb segments at 200 Hz and ground reaction forces were measured using dual force plates embedded in the treadmill (FP6012, Bertec, USA) at 500 Hz. For both groups, a single sacrum-mounted IMU sensor (Opal, APDM, USA) recorded triaxial accelerations and angular velocities at 100 Hz. All motion capture, force plate, and IMU data were low-pass filtered using a zero-phase 4th-order Butterworth filter with a 6-Hz cutoff frequency. The EE was measured in both groups using indirect calorimetry (K5, Cosmed, Italy). It was calculated from respiratory gas exchange based on standard equations8, applying a fixed conversion constant that translates O2 consumption and CO2 production into EE uniformly across all trials. Net EE, defined as the increase in EE due to walking, was determined by subtracting the average resting EE (measured during quiet standing before exercise) from the average total EE during the final 3 minutes of each walking trial. In this study, this net EE was treated as the measured EE.

Analysis and modeling

The analysis and modeling consisted of two primary components: (1) Biomechanical and regression analyses for the weighted and scaled contribution of a segment’s joint power to the whole-body EE; and (2) Data-driven modeling for EE estimation using machine learning from the single IMU data.

Machine learning to estimate EE from single IMU data

The models from the biomechanical and regression analyses inform the machine learning approach for EE estimation using IMU data (Fig. 2). The IMU-based machine learning network for joint power estimation from a single sacrum-mounted IMU in this study extended the approach in a prior work32. The dataset used for estimation consisted of two-dimensional sagittal-plane acceleration signals obtained from a single sacrum-mounted IMU and the prescribed treadmill speed. While gait speed can also be estimated from IMU signals34,35, the use of prescribed speed in this study was intended to isolate the effect of biomechanical modeling from network complexity. The data were preprocessed by extracting time windows corresponding to the analysis interval of each segment (e.g., stance phase for the stance leg and swing phase for the swing leg) and interpolating them to a uniform length. Results and discussion

Segmental contributions to whole-body joint mechanical power

The whole-body 3D joint mechanical power during walking was predominantly generated by the lower limbs, particularly by the stance leg (Fig. 3A). The stance leg accounted for 84.6% of the positive whole-body joint power, while the swing leg and the upper body contributed 11.9% and 3.5%, respectively. For negative power, the stance leg contributed 76.2%, the swing leg 19.5%, and the upper body 4.3%. Here, the joint power is due to the net mechanical work done by each joint, which includes both active muscular work and passive contributions arising from elastic tendon recoil or damping in both the positive and negative domains. The dominant contribution of the stance leg reflects its primary role in weight-bearing and propulsion during walking37,38. Substantial muscular forces generated by the ankle, knee, and hip joints are transformed into joint torques, producing mechanical work and the resulting ground reaction forces39–42. In contrast, the swing leg plays a relatively passive role, primarily advancing the limb to prepare for the next step43,44, leading to a smaller contribution to total work45. The increased proportion of negative power by the swing leg (from 11.9% to 19.5%) reflects its role in controlling inertial movement and adjusting foot placement. This contrasts with the larger proportion of positive power generated by the stance leg. Analysis of the 3D components revealed that sagittal-plane power was dominant in both lower limbs. In the swing leg, sagittal-plane power accounted for 89.9% of positive and 92.1% of negative power, with frontal and transverse components remaining below 7% (Fig. 3B). This reflects the predominantly fore-aft motion of the swing leg. In the stance leg, sagittal-plane power accounted for 76.9% of positive and 75.8% of negative power, while frontal-plane power contributed 19.1% and 14.7%, respectively (Fig. 3C), highlighting its role in the mediolateral stability and balance control during weight transfer.46,47 From a methodological perspective, the finding that stance-leg joint power accounts for more than 84% of whole-body joint power implies that the stance leg could be a biomechanical surrogate for the whole-body power and EE. In addition, the relative contribution of each plane may show slight variation across walking speeds or subjects, which could be taken into account in the interpretation of these results.

Strong linear correlations between segmental sagittal power and whole-body joint mechanical power

Strong linear correlations were observed between the sagittal-plane joint power of each segment and the whole-body 3D joint mechanical power (Fig. 4, Table 1). For the positive power components, sagittal joint power of the stance leg, the swing leg, and the upper body showed correlation coefficients of R = 0.97, 0.92, and 0.772, respectively (Fig. 4A), all statistically significant. Similar trends were observed for negative power, with R = 0.93, 0.86, and 0.88, respectively (Fig. 4B). These strong correlations reflect the biomechanical roles of each segment. The stance leg, which accounts for approximately 84% of whole-body joint power, serves critical functions in weight-bearing and propulsion, resulting in its exceptionally strong correlation with whole-body joint power (R = 0.97). In contrast, the swing leg and the upper body primarily support balance control and assist with angular momentum generated by the stance leg48–50. Their movements are largely governed by pendular dynamics51–53, where energy cost scales with step frequency54, which itself is regulated by whole-body coordination patterns55,56. This likely explains their preserved linearity with whole-body joint power. When incorporating muscle efficiency by weighting positive and negative work components (efficiency-weighted sagittal power), strong linearity remained (Fig. 4C). The stance leg, the swing leg, and the upper body showed correlation coefficients of R = 0.98, 0.93, and 0.77, respectively. EE estimation using efficiency-weighted segmental sagittal power scaling

While the efficiency-weighted segmental sagittal power demonstrated strong linearity with EE, deriving absolute EE estimates comparable to measured EE requires an appropriate scale factor. In this study, the scale factors (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c_{seg}$$\end{document}) were derived for each segment to convert efficiency-weighted power into absolute EE estimates using linear regression. The resulting scaled values were defined as the estimated EE. The estimated EE showed excellent agreement with the EE measured via indirect calorimetry (Fig. 5). Across subjects, the stride leg (including both stance and swing phases of the same leg), the stance leg, and the swing leg achieved R = 0.91, 0.91, and 0.89, respectively, all statistically significant. The corresponding average scale factors were 1.40 (stride leg), 0.99 (stance leg), and 3.68 (swing leg), with very low coefficients of variation (1.43%, 1.50%, 1.92%, respectively), indicating minimal inter-subject variability (Table 3). As expected, the stride leg—which reflects combined stance and swing leg contributions—showed the highest overall consistency, followed closely by the stance leg. To contextualize our findings within established biomechanical frameworks, we compared the derived scale factors with the muscle work fraction concept described in prior studies27. Given the dimensionless net work cost of 4.83 calculated in this study and the observed ratios of segmental to whole-body joint power, our scale factors correspond to the muscle work fractions of approximately 0.60-0.65. These values align with established muscle work efficiency parameters in the biomechanics literature and are consistent with the “less elastic” muscle behavior expected during walking compared to running59–61, supporting the physiological validity of our approach. As noted in previous studies24,62, muscle work fraction ranges between 0 and 1 depending on task, muscle group, and loading conditions. The slightly higher muscle work fraction observed in the swing leg likely reflects its reduced involvement of elastic elements such as tendons compared to the stance leg, where tendinous structures play a more prominent role in energy storage and recoil. Inter-subject variability in regression slopes was also lowest for the stride and stance leg. The mean slopes (± standard deviation) for the stride leg, stance leg, and the swing leg were 0.87 ± 0.09, 0.87 ± 0.09, and 0.96 ± 0.26, respectively. Although the stride leg exhibited both the highest estimation accuracy and the lowest variability, the difference from those of the stance leg was negligible. The swing leg, on the other hand, exhibited slightly lower estimation accuracy and greater variability, as reflected by its higher coefficient of variation and slope dispersion. The comparison among different segments was intended to identify the segment that best represents whole-body EE during walking for practical estimation using IMU. Given that the stance leg captures the majority of mechanical power and offers simpler implementation with a single IMU, the stance leg was considered the most representative and robust segment for subsequent EE estimation. Therefore, the following framework development focused on the stance leg as the representative segment for IMU-based estimation. Estimation of stance-leg joint dynamics from single sacrum-mounted IMU

Using LOSO machine learning trained on the training group data, sagittal-plane joint dynamics for the ankle, knee, and hip of the stance leg were accurately estimated across the stance phase from the single IMU mounted at the sacrum (Fig. 6). Compared to the ground-truth motion capture data, the mean root mean squared errors (RMSEs) for joint angles were 4.23°, 4.90°, and 5.29°, respectively, and for joint torques were 0.16 Nm/kg for all three joints. The resulting estimated joint powers exhibited RMSEs of 0.53 W/kg (ankle), 0.34 W/kg (knee), and 0.25 W/kg (hip). When combined to compute the stance-leg sagittal joint power, the overall RMSE was 0.71 W/kg (Table 4). These estimation accuracies are comparable to the prior IMU-based joint dynamics estimation studies,29,30,63 supporting the validity of the proposed approach. The sacrum, being close to the body’s center of mass,64 effectively captures inertial characteristics of whole-body motion during walking,65 making it a suitable location for sensor placement in estimating lower-limb joint dynamics. While some deviations between the estimated and measured the joint dynamics were observed beyond 90% of the stance phase (Fig. 6), statistical comparison revealed no significant difference between RMSE values computed for the full stance phase and for the initial 0–90% stance phase. This indicates that late-phase discrepancies have limited impact on overall estimation accuracy. These terminal-phase errors may reflect abrupt biomechanical transitions occurring immediately before toe-off,38 where ground contact force rapidly decreases and joint coordination patterns shift, potentially producing dynamics that are difficult to fully capture using IMU signals alone. The sacrum-based single-IMU joint dynamics estimation method employed here achieved strong performance despite utilizing a simple single-layer ANN. Validation of the proposed EE estimation framework

The EE estimation framework based on the stance-leg sagittal joint power was validated using the independent test group of 13 subjects, applying the scale factors derived from the linear regression of the data from the initial training group of 9 subjects. Using only the single sacrum-mounted IMU, the proposed estimation method achieved a mean RMSE of 0.69 W/kg relative to the EE measured from indirect calorimetry (Fig. 7A). Segmental mechanical analysis revealed that the sagittal stance-leg joint power exhibited strong correlations (R > 0.9) with the whole-body joint power across subjects and walking speeds. For supplementary reference, a direct data-driven deep learning model36 without intermediate biomechanical features was additionally evaluated on the same test subjects. This model produced a higher mean RMSE of 0.95 W/kg (Fig. 7B, C). While this reference model demonstrates the feasibility of direct data-driven estimation from IMU signals, it exhibited larger estimation variability, particularly at higher EE values. Note that, while the direct data-driven approach estimates absolute EE values, the proposed method estimates net EE above resting levels, which corresponds to the measured EE in this study. Since resting EE varies substantially across individuals67, direct absolute estimation may be more sensitive to inter-individual variability, particularly in heterogeneous populations. Conversely, the approach based on the net EE may appear to perform more favorably under such conditions. Nevertheless, both approaches involve trade-offs depending on application needs: absolute EE estimation for comprehensive caloric assessment versus net EE estimation for robust activity-based monitoring. The achieved RMSE of 0.69 W/kg compares favorably to previously reported IMU-based EE estimation studies21,22,68–72, demonstrating that practical EE monitoring can be accomplished with a single IMU sensor. The ability to estimate joint dynamics from a single sacrum-mounted IMU highlights a promising path toward simplified, real-world wearable biomechanical monitoring systems without reliance on complex sensor arrays. Overall, the proposed method has significant potential for practical applications in individualized wearable monitoring of metabolic cost during rehabilitation, exercise prescription, and daily activity tracking without the need for complex multi-sensor systems1,73. The strong and consistent relationship between stance-leg sagittal power and measured EE supports the feasibility of real-time EE monitoring using only a single sacrum-mounted IMU. Quantifying segmental joint power contributions may also assist in evaluating compensatory mechanisms in patients with joint impairments74–76, enabling personalized therapeutic interventions or control of assistive devices.

Limitations and future work

Despite the promising results, several limitations remain. First, while consistent results were obtained within the tested cohort, broader generalizability across diverse populations remains to be established. Variations in body morphology, gait patterns, or musculoskeletal impairments may introduce inter-individual differences affecting estimation accuracy77. Incorporating individualized calibration could potentially improve performance in such populations but may also increase system complexity.

Second, the current study was conducted under controlled laboratory conditions with healthy adults walking at constant speeds on a level surface or treadmill. For broader real-world applicability, the model should be validated across a wider range of walking conditions, including variable speeds, inclines, stair ambulation, uneven terrains, and pathological gaits commonly observed in clinical populations78,79.

Third, constant efficiency coefficients were used respectively for positive and negative muscle work to account for their different metabolic costs in calculating the efficiency-weighted power. While this approach is widely accepted for estimating EE, particularly for cyclic tasks like gait27, its validity for general applicability across a wider range of conditions will need to be further explored. Muscle efficiency can vary with individual fitness, age, and sex80,81, potentially influencing weighting parameters. These are related to the dependency of the joint mechanical power and dissipated heat (and thus the efficiency) on the joint states and various other movement conditions11,82, which will be pursued in a future study. Nonetheless, in the absence of individualized physiology models, applying efficiency-weighted power with empirically derived scaling remains a reasonable and practical approach for periodic walking, supported by previous studies.25,27 Likewise, although the scale factors appeared stable across different speeds, their robustness across broader populations requires further validation. The scale factors may also vary with anthropometrics and gait patterns. Although low inter-subject variability was observed across 9 subjects and 5 walking speeds, the limited dataset may not fully capture variations related to body size, age, fitness level, surface conditions, or footwear. Furthermore, while treadmill walking provides controlled conditions, overground walking may introduce additional variability83,84.

Fourth, while the sacrum provides a biomechanically advantageous sensor location for capturing whole-body dynamics, the practicality of wearing a dedicated IMU at this site in daily life may be limited. Alternative sensor locations, such as the wrist or pocket where IMUs are already embedded in commercial devices like smartwatches and smartphones, may offer more user-friendly solutions85. However, these alternative placements provide less direct biomechanical information about lower-limb joint dynamics, necessitating more advanced algorithmic approaches to maintain estimation accuracy. Furthermore, practical deployment in real-world settings will require addressing challenges such as IMU placement variability, long-term sensor drift, signal noise during daily activities, and stability of system performance over prolonged use86,87.

Finally, although the current ANN architecture is computationally lightweight, further optimization is necessary to ensure efficient real-time implementation on wearable devices, which typically operate under strict constraints in processing power and battery capacity88,89.

Conclusion

The results in this study demonstrated that the sagittal-plane joint mechanical power of a body segment, particularly the stance leg, could be a valid biomechanical surrogate for whole-body joint power and EE during walking. With a reliably estimated stance-leg sagittal joint dynamics, EE could be accurately and robustly inferred via efficiency-weighted sagittal power and a regression-based scale factor. Our results further confirmed that such estimation is feasible with a single IMU mounted at the sacrum, providing practicality with accuracy and robustness. The integration of biomechanical models with machine learning offers an interpretable solution for wearable EE monitoring, with potential clinical and engineering applications, such as rehabilitation, exercise prescription, and health monitoring.
