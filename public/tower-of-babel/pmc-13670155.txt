Domesticated bacterial origin genes regulate homeostasis of the insect gut microbiome and empower adaptation
Authors: Zhenjia Zhang, Yubo Zhang, Kun Zhao, Guangyuan Cheng, Zhihao Lu, Dongju Wang, Yiqun Liu, Jianguo Wu, Liying Wang, Lisheng Zhang, Xinhai Ye, Li Yu, Wei Zhang, Yi Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSap‐sucking insects, represented by the globally distributed leafhoppers, have evolved a wide range of adaptive traits and play multiple ecological roles. However, their successful survival strategies in nature remain poorly understood. To explore the adaptive mechanisms of sap‐sucking insects, we established a research system using three representative leafhopper species from the same family and found that they have acquired multiple cholesterol‐dependent cytolysins (CDCs) derived from gram‐positive bacterial genes. Furthermore, we demonstrated that these CDCs have been domesticated and co‐evolved with leafhoppers, ultimately participating in various life activities of the host. Specifically, some CDCs exhibit high cytotoxicity, are specifically expressed in the egg serosa, and affect the feeding behaviors of leafhopper predators; others show non‐cytotoxic activity, are specifically expressed in the gut, and may regulate leafhopper growth and development by potentially maintaining the homeostasis of the leafhopper gut microbiome. This study uncovers leafhoppers as a paradigm for understanding how organisms can borrow microbial innovations to address ecological challenges and thrive as agricultural pests. It provides a comprehensive perspective to bridge the genotypes of acquired genes with the phenotypes of adaptive traits and demonstrates how homologous genes from donor organisms undergo functional differentiation in recipient hosts, enabling them to exploit new ecological niches. By decoding these mechanisms, we not only gain fundamental insights into evolutionary processes but also develop powerful strategies for sustainable pest management.Keywords: adaptation, cholesterol‐dependent cytolysin, functional domestication and diversification, gut microbiome, Leafhopper

INTRODUCTION

Sap‐sucking insects feed on the phloem sap of vascular plants with their needle‐like piercing mouthparts. Due to their unique foraging habits and life histories, they harbor complex ecological roles as prey or pollinators and as vectors of pests and plant pathogens, which are vital to ecosystems and require in‐depth understanding to facilitate biodiversity conservation and ecological balance maintenance [1, 2, 3, 4]. For example, the leafhoppers in Cicadellidae, representatives of a vast number of sap‐sucking insects, are widely distributed, with more than 20,000 described species, many of which are important agricultural pests and a primary group of confirmed insect vectors of plant pathogens [5, 6, 7, 8]. As a result, leafhoppers have become an ideal research system for investigating complex interactions among insects, symbionts, plants, and pathogens, yielding research methods and findings to benefit further exploration of their adaptive strategies [7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. As revealed in other insects, it is likely that the leafhoppers have developed specialized pathways to confer resistance to viral, bacterial, and fungal pathogens in response to numerous challenges from the environment, such as natural predators and pathogens [12, 13, 14, 15]. Nevertheless, little is known about how leafhoppers balance defense mechanisms and life history in such complex ecological roles.

Recilia dorsalis, Nephotettix cincticeps, and Nephotettix nigropictus are three agriculturally important leafhopper species in the family Cicadellidae. They are important pests of rice plants, are widely distributed in Asia, and are commonly found in rice agroecosystems. These three species are also vectors of diverse plant pathogens, including viruses and phytoplasmas, many of which are transmitted in a persistent and propagative manner [7, 8]. These pathogens primarily affect rice and other crops, causing recurrent disease outbreaks and significant agricultural losses. Therefore, understanding the evolutionary trajectories and adaptive mechanisms of these leafhopper species has important ecological implications and may provide insights into crop protection and food security.

Here, based on high‐quality genomes of these three leafhoppers, combined with bioinformatic, molecular, and biochemical information as well as cell biology and insect performance assays, we showed that the genomes of R. dorsalis, Ne. cincticeps, and Ne. nigropictus harbor multiple horizontally transferred genes encoding cholesterol‐dependent cytolysins (CDCs) of gram‐positive bacterial origin that lyse eukaryotic cells by forming pores in the cell membrane through the binding of cholesterol, the major target cell receptor [16, 17, 18, 19]. We further characterized these CDC genes in R. dorsalis (RdCDCs) and found that they have been domesticated and evolved with functional differentiation, ranging from strong to weak or even no cholesterol affinity and from high to weak or even no cytotoxicity to eukaryotic cells. A comprehensive study of RdCDC3, which has high affinity for cholesterol and high cytotoxicity, showed that it was expressed specifically in the egg serosa and affected the feeding behaviors of leafhopper predators, such as ladybugs. In contrast, RdCDC9, which has no cholesterol affinity or cytotoxicity, underwent positive selection and was expressed specifically in the gut and associated with the maintenance of gut microbiome homeostasis and leafhopper survival.

In summary, our results reveal a historical genome evolutionary event driving acquired adaptive traits in leafhoppers, which, overall, may deepen the understanding of environmental adaptations in the insect world and facilitate the development of novel strategies for the control of these widespread pests and the diseases they transmit. Moreover, leafhoppers and CDC genes may serve as a model system for future mechanistic studies of the domestication and evolution of genes in recipient insects.

RESULTS

Structural features and evolution of the leafhopper genome

To characterize genomic signatures related to adaptive traits in leafhoppers, we focused on three representative leafhopper species, R. dorsalis, Ne. cincticeps, and Ne. nigropictus, which are widely distributed and have adapted to various environmental conditions. These leafhoppers are particularly prevalent in Asia and the Pacific, where they are commonly found near rice crops (PlantwisePlus Knowledge Bank, CABI International) (Figure 1A). Chromosome‐level reference genomes were assembled by integrating PacBio long reads, Illumina paired‐end reads, and Hi‐C data (Figures 1A and S1; Table S1). Compared with the nearly identical genome sizes and high levels of synteny between the two Nephotettix species (788 Mb in Ne. cincticeps and 770 Mb in Ne. nigropictus) (Figure 1B), R. dorsalis exhibited a larger genome (1223 Mb), possibly due to the expansion of repetitive sequences, which constituted approximately 60% of the R. dorsalis genome (Table S2), and a few chromosomal fusions, fissions and translocations. In contrast, the sex chromosome was conserved between the two genera. Nephotettix chromosome 7 and its homologous sequence in R. dorsalis (chromosome 9) were identified as X chromosomes because their read coverage was half that of the other autosomes when Illumina reads from males were mapped to the assemblies (Figure 1B,C). Similarly, R. dorsalis has more species‐specific genes (3998) than Ne. cincticeps (474) and Ne. nigropictus (282), likely due to its larger genome and greater number of genes (Figure 1D and Table S2).

Using a species phylogeny and calibrated divergence times (approximately 174.72 Mya for Cicadellidae and 12.84 Mya for Nephotettix), we assessed the expansion and contraction of superfamilies in Hemiptera. Considering that these three species are widespread pests and can carry and transmit rice viruses and other pathogens [7, 8], we focused on superfamilies that underwent rapid evolution in the ancestors of the three species, including eight significantly expanded superfamilies and six significantly contracted superfamilies (Figure 1D,E). Among the expanded gene families, we found that the genomes of the three leafhopper species contained the thiol‐activated cytolysin superfamily (CDC, IPR036359), members of which encode a toxin produced by gram‐positive bacteria that has not been found in other insect genomes [20, 21, 22] (Figure 1F). Given that CDC genes have thus far been identified only in bacterial lineages, we hypothesized that leafhopper CDC genes may have been acquired from bacterial donors through horizontal gene transfer (HGT) and further tested this hypothesis using phylogenetic analysis.

HGT of CDC genes from bacteria to leafhoppers

Genome annotation revealed that all three species had multiple CDC genes. In R. dorsalis, three CDC genes (RdCDC6, RdCDC8, and RdCDC9) had introns in their coding DNA sequences (CDSs), whereas at least two CDC genes (RdCDC1 and RdCDC7) had introns in their 5'‐untranslated regions (5'‐UTRs). In contrast, all CDC genes in Nephotettix had only one exon in their CDSs, but transcriptome data showed that all of these genes had one intron in their UTRs (Figures 2A,B, S2, and S3). To confirm the existence of CDC genes in the genomes of these leafhoppers, we cloned all the CDC genes from genomic DNA and quantified their expression levels via polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and reverse transcription‐quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT‐qPCR) (Figure 2C). We also performed immunoprecipitation (IP) of the candidate proteins from the lysate of R. dorsalis using IgG and RdCDC9 antibodies. The IP products were separated via sodium dodecyl sulfate‐polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE) and analyzed by liquid chromatography‐tandem mass spectrometry (LC‐MS/MS), and the RdCDC9 fragments were identified using an anti‐RdCDC9 antibody but not IgG (Figure 2D).

The 16 CDC proteins ranged in length from 490 to 680 amino acids (aa), 13 of which were predicted to be secreted proteins because they had signal peptides at their N‐termini (Figure 2E). The C‐terminus was identified as the thiol‐activated cytolysin domain, which is found only in bacteria [21]. To determine the origin of the leafhopper CDC genes, we constructed a phylogenetic tree based on sequences from the related membrane attack complex/perforin (MACPF)/CDC superfamily [21, 22]. Consistent with previous results, proteins in the CDC family (Transporter Classification Database: TCDB ID 1.C.12), MACPF family (TCDB ID 1.C.39), and pleurotolysin B family (TCDB ID 1.C.97.1) clustered separately. Moreover, phylogenetic analysis revealed that leafhopper CDCs were nested within the clade of classical CDCs from gram‐positive bacteria, including Firmicutes and Actinobacteria (TCDB ID 1.C.12.1). In contrast, CDC homologs from the FCB group and Proteobacteria (TCDB ID 1.C.12.2 and TCDB ID 1.C.12.3) formed the basal lineages (Figures 2E and S4). The placement of leafhopper CDCs within a bacterial lineage indicates that these genes were derived from a bacterial ancestor. This nested phylogenetic pattern strongly supports a bacteria‐to‐insect HGT event.

Additionally, relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) values showed that the codon usage bias pattern of leafhopper CDCs significantly differed from that of bacterial CDCs (perfringolysin [PFO], listeriolysin [LLO], and streptolysin [SLO]) but was similar to that of other conserved single‐copy genes in the three leafhopper species (Figure 2F), indicating that the codon usage of the transferred genes has been domesticated and evolved to be consistent with that of the recipient genome.

To further investigate the evolutionary history of the insect CDCs, we first searched all available insect genomes in the NCBI RefSeq database for CDC homologs. Among the 322 insect species examined, CDC homologs were identified only in one additional leafhopper species, Macrosteles quadrilineatus (Table S3). Because CDC genes were also found in leafhopper species beyond the three species included in our initial analysis, we expanded our screening to all available genome resources from the family Cicadellidae to characterize the distribution of CDC genes within leafhoppers. Among the 29 leafhopper species analyzed, CDC homologs were detected in all 16 sampled species of the subfamily Deltocephalinae, whereas no homologs were identified in sampled species from other subfamilies, suggesting that the gene transfer event likely occurred in the common ancestor of Deltocephalinae (Figure S5A; Table S4). The copy number of CDCs varied between 3 and 14, and most gene copies were lineage‐specific, which indicates CDC genes are highly divergent and rapidly evolved after HGT (Figure S5B).

To further investigate the characteristics of these leafhopper CDCs, we examined the signatures of selection. Pairwise Ka/Ks ratios showed that some CDCs, especially RdCDC9, had higher nonsynonymous substitution rates, indicating that they may have undergone relaxed negative selection or positive selection (Figure S6A). We also evaluated selection pressure using the PAML branch‐site model and found that five leaf nodes in R. dorsalis (RdCDC4, RdCDC5, RdCDC6, RdCDC8, and RdCDC9) and internal nodes in Nephotettix showed signatures of positive selection (p < 0.05, Figure S6B), indicating the leafhopper CDCs may have undergone rapid adaptive evolution in the Nephotettix ancestor and R. dorsalis. Interestingly, the putatively selected site in RdCDC9 (C586, Bayes empirical Bayes analysis, p < 0.05) was a key amino acid in loop L2. L2 was one of four conserved motifs (loops L1 to L3 and the undecapeptide) responsible for cholesterol recognition and binding in bacterial CDCs [21]. Therefore, we checked the sequence alignments of the four motifs and found that the sequences were conserved in most leafhopper CDCs (Figure S7A), suggesting that these leafhopper CDC proteins have functions similar to those in bacteria. However, some gene copies have mutations in the conserved functional sites. More specifically, the cholesterol recognition motif (CRM, Thr‐Leu pair) in loop L1 was mutated in RdCDC7 and RdCDC9, and three proteins (RdCDC7, RdCDC9, and NcCDC4) had insertions or deletions in the undecapeptide region (Figure S7A). Additionally, RdCDC7, RdCDC8, and RdCDC9 lacked a signal peptide, and RdCDC9 had a potential transmembrane domain (Figure 2E). We also predicted these protein structures using AlphaFold 2 [23] and found that some RdCDCs, such as RdCDC6 and RdCDC9, exhibited obvious variations (Figure S7B). In summary, our results indicate that leafhopper CDCs have been domesticated and undergone sequence and functional differentiation after HGT from bacterial donors. Specifically, some CDCs, including NcCDC4, RdCDC7, and RdCDC9, may have been extensively domesticated in leafhoppers and may have partially or entirely lost their cholesterol‐binding and membrane‐anchoring ability and gained new functions. Conversely, other CDCs, such as RdCDC3, NcCDC1, and NnCDC3, did not exhibit signatures of relaxed negative selection or positive selection and may have maintained their original functions.

RdCDCs exhibit ex vivo cytotoxic differentiation

We next wondered whether the conserved CDCs retained functions similar to those of the bacterial CDCs. We observed that RdCDCs were dispersed throughout the genome. For instance, RdCDC3 and RdCDC9 are located on chromosome 9, which is the sex chromosome, whereas RdCDC6 is located on autosomal chromosome 8 (Figure 3A). We selected three representative CDCs, RdCDC3, RdCDC6, and RdCDC9, for functional research because they belong to three different CDC clusters that exhibit clear sequence and structural differentiation (Figures 3B, S7A, and S7B).

To investigate the physicochemical functions of these three RdCDCs, we initially transfected Spodoptera frugiperda 9 (Sf9) cells with recombinant baculoviruses [24]. Subsequently, we examined the expression and localization of these proteins using laser confocal microscopy. We observed that RdCDC3, RdCDC6, and RdCDC9 all formed granular aggregates in the plasma membrane (Figure S8A). Given that cholesterol is anchored within the cell membrane, we investigated the binding of RdCDCs to the cell membrane. We generated the recombinant proteins RdCDC3 ΔSP , RdCDC6 ΔSP , and RdCDC9 ΔTM tagged with glutathione S‐transferase (GST) at their N‐termini and cultured the proteins with Sf9 cells or vector cell monolayers (VCMs) from Ne. cincticeps. Through immunostaining with a GST antibody and subsequent fluorescence detection, we observed that GST‐RdCDC3 and GST‐RdCDC6 bound to the plasma membrane, whereas GST‐RdCDC9 did not (Figure 3C). Next, we used microscale thermophoresis (MST) experiments to measure the binding activity of the three RdCDCs to cholesterol, and the results revealed that RdCDC3‐GFP directly interacted with cholesterol in Sf9 cell lysates with an affinity (Kd) of 6.83 × 10−6 M. However, RdCDC6 exhibited weak binding activity, and RdCDC9 completely lost binding activity (Figures 3D and S8B).

To further evaluate the cytotoxicity of these RdCDCs, we first performed experiments in yeast cells. We cotransformed and expressed activation domain (AD)/binding domain (BD)‐RdCDC3, AD/BD‐RdCDC6, and AD/BD‐RdCDC9 in the AH109 strain. We found that, compared with control yeast, the yeast cotransformed with AD/BD‐RdCDC9 grew normally, whereas the growth of the yeast cotransformed with AD/BD‐RdCDC6 was strongly suppressed, and there was almost no growth of the yeast cotransformed with AD/BD‐RdCDC3; moreover, we quantified these observations by counting colonies (Figure 3E). To validate the cytotoxicity of these RdCDCs, we microinjected these recombinant RdCDC proteins into fourth‐instar larvae of Bombyx mori. Similarly, RdCDC3 significantly reduced the survival of B. mori in a dose‐dependent manner, whereas RdCDC6 and RdCDC9 displayed effects only at extremely high doses (Figure S8C). To confirm these findings, we conducted 3‐(4,5‐dimethylthiazol‐2‐yl)−2,5‐diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assays [20] to measure the viability of cells from various cell lines, including HeLa, Vero, HEK293T, and Sf9 cells. We cultured the cells in the presence of the recombinant fusion proteins GST‐RdCDC3, GST‐RdCDC6, and GST‐RdCDC9 purified from Escherichia coli for 24 h at the appropriate culture temperature. Surprisingly, only RdCDC3 exhibited potent toxicity to Vero, HeLa, and HEK293T cells, with doses leading to 50% cell death (IC50) of approximately 1–3 µg/mL; these proteins had relatively weak toxicity to Sf9 cells (Figures 3F,G, and S8D–S8G). Overall, RdCDC3 displayed a strong ability to bind cholesterol, caused a high mortality rate in B. mori, and was highly toxic to Vero, HeLa, and HEK293T cells. In contrast, RdCDC6 and RdCDC9 bound cholesterol weakly or not at all, were nontoxic to Vero and HeLa cells, and reduced the survival rate of B. mori at extremely high dosages.

To further investigate the role of cholesterol in RdCDC perforation, we established a giant unilamellar vesicle (GUV)‐based in vitro reconstitution system. The simplicity of in vitro‐synthesized GUVs allowed us to create cell‐mimicking vesicles with precisely controlled cholesterol concentrations while avoiding interference from other components in cells (Figure 3H).

We prepared rhodamine‐phosphatidylethanolamine (PE)‐labeled GUVs containing 0% to 20% cholesterol and used membrane‐impermeable fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)‐dextran as an indicator of GUV leakage. We found that the addition of RdCDC3 had distinct effects on GUVs with different cholesterol concentrations. Although GUVs without cholesterol showed no obvious dextran penetration or morphological changes after RdCDC3 treatment, GUVs containing 10% or 20% cholesterol showed immediate penetration of dextran. Moreover, with 10% cholesterol, gradual deformation was observed after penetration. With 20% cholesterol, simultaneous rupture was observed in 48% of GUVs. These results clearly revealed that cholesterol is essential for RdCDC3 perforation and that the kinetics are positively correlated with the cholesterol concentration on the target membrane. For the other two proteins, RdCDC6 showed a cholesterol‐dependent perforation effect with a much lower efficiency relative to that of RdCDC3, whereas RdCDC9 showed no significant perforation effect under any conditions (Figures 3I,J, S9A–S9F, and Videos S1–S2). To further compare perforation efficiency between bacterial CDCs and leafhopper CDCs, we selected pyolysin (PLO), which belongs to the bacterial CDCs and does not require thiol activation, similar to RdCDC3. Using the GUV‐based in vitro reconstitution system, we found that, at the same cholesterol concentration, the perforation effect of RdCDC3 was stronger and faster than that of PLO (Figure S9G, S9H, and Video S3), suggesting that RdCDC3 had evolved higher cytotoxicity than the original bacterial CDCs.

RdCDC3 is specifically expressed in the serosa of eggs and exhibits inhibitory effects on the feeding of natural leafhopper predators

As presented in the above section, our study provides strong evidence supporting the hypothesis that HGT events provided leafhopper species with CDCs exhibiting variable cytolytic activity, which prompted us to further decipher the ecological roles of these leafhopper CDCs. First, we examined the expression of these CDCs in leafhoppers of different sexes, and surprisingly, we found that RdCDC3 was predominantly expressed in females (Figure 4A). Further specific organ detection via reverse transcription‐polymerase chain reaction (RT‐PCR) and RT‐qPCR revealed that RdCDC3 mRNA was highly expressed in the ovaries and early‐stage eggs, whereas RdCDC6 and RdCDC9 were mainly expressed in the gut (Figure 4B,C). Interestingly, western blot analysis using an anti‐RdCDC3 antibody showed that the RdCDC3 protein was consistently expressed throughout the egg stage, with actin serving as an internal reference (Figure 4C). To further determine the localization of RdCDC3, we used western blotting, immunofluorescence, and immunogold labeling of ultrathin sections of eggs with an anti‐RdCDC3 antibody. The immunofluorescence and immunogold labeling results were obtained via laser confocal microscopy and transmission electron microscopy (TEM), respectively. To aid in understanding these results, we provided a picture of an egg, a schematic diagram of an egg, and an ultrathin section of the eggshell and serosa cuticle that illustrate the organization of the egg in Figure 4D. The immunofluorescence and western blot results showed that RdCDC3 was specifically expressed in the eggshell but not in the egg content (EC) (Figure 4E,F), and the immunogold labeling results further demonstrated that RdCDC3 was specifically expressed in the serosa (S) (Figure 4G).

The specific developmental stage and serosa expression of RdCDC3 suggest that expression of the toxin gene is under precise regulation, which, on one hand, avoids being toxic and harmful to the leafhopper itself. On the other hand, such regulation may be useful for defending the eggs and offspring, which are essential for maintaining leafhopper populations, against natural enemies. To verify this hypothesis, we evaluated the effect of RdCDC3 on Micraspis discolor (ladybugs), a natural predator that mainly feeds on leafhoppers in rice fields, using in vitro and in vivo experiments. We first examined whether the RdCDC3 protein affected the feeding preferences of the ladybugs. We mixed GST‐RdCDC proteins with the artificial food of M. discolor, which consisted of Ephestia kuehniella eggs and Brassica campestris pollen [25], with the GST protein serving as a control. We recorded the feeding time of M. discolor using a real‐time monitoring camera to evaluate feeding preference (Figure 4H, left). A linear mixed‐effects model (LMM) revealed a significant effect of food type on feeding time (F = 7.63, p = 0.008), whereas neither food position nor the interaction between food type and position had a significant effect (F = 0.01, p = 0.92; F = 0.22, p = 0.64, respectively). After accounting for the effect of position, feeding time was significantly shorter for food containing GST‐RdCDC3 than for the GST‐only control (Figure 4H, middle). In contrast, GST‐RdCDC9 showed a marginal effect on feeding time (F = 4.02, p = 0.05), with a trend toward increased feeding time relative to the GST‐only control (Figure 4H, right), suggesting that the predator exhibited a negative preference for the RdCDC3 protein. Therefore, we subsequently investigated whether the RdCDC3 protein would have a detrimental effect on the predator by microinjecting the GST‐RdCDC proteins into M. discolor. Both group and time significantly affected percent weight gain (LMM, group: F = 41.32, p < 0.001; time: F = 28.01, p < 0.001), whereas no significant group × time interaction was detected (F = 1.70, p = 0.15). Consistent with these overall effects, post hoc pairwise comparisons showed that M. discolor individuals injected with RdCDC3 exhibited significantly lower percent weight gain than those injected with RdCDC6 or RdCDC9 at all three time points (all adjusted p < 0.05) (Figure 4I, top). At 4 days post‐injection, almost all M. discolor individuals that had been injected with RdCDC3 had died, whereas the majority of those injected with RdCDC6 and RdCDC9 were still alive (Figure 4I, bottom), indicating that RdCDC3 was toxic to the ladybugs. To further confirm whether RdCDC3 proteins at physiological concentrations in leafhoppers had effects similar to those of purified proteins, we conducted a feeding trial in M. discolor by providing either pregnant female R. dorsalis or males that expressed or did not express RdCDC3, respectively (Figure 4J, left). The results showed that the percent weight gain of M. discolor preying on female leafhoppers was significantly lower than that of M. discolor preying on male leafhoppers (Figure 4J, right, p < 0.01, Student's t test), indicating that feeding on female leafhoppers was harmful to the growth of M. discolor and thus that female leafhoppers might not be preferred by predators under natural conditions. To rule out other possibilities resulting in differences between male and female leafhoppers, we next directly validated the protective function of RdCDC3 on eggs by inhibiting gene expression in vivo. We fed M. discolor with leafhopper eggs from the RdCDC3 knockdown individuals and control groups and recorded changes in weight over time. RT‐qPCR analysis confirmed efficient silencing of RdCDC3 in eggs from the knockdown group (Figure S10A and S10C). The results showed that group, time, and sex of M. discolor all significantly affected body weight (LMM, group: F = 11.56, p = 0.009; time: F = 20.16, p < 0.001; sex: F = 118.68, p < 0.001). A significant group × time interaction was also detected (F = 6.91, p < 0.001), indicating that the effect of group on body weight varied over time. Post hoc pairwise comparisons further showed that, during the later stages of the experiment (Days 7, 9, and 11), both male and female M. discolor feeding on eggs that lacked RdCDC3 had significantly higher body weights than their respective controls (all adjusted p < 0.05) (Figure 4K), supporting the conclusion that the presence of RdCDC3 effectively reduced the predation or utilization of leafhopper eggs by ladybugs. In summary, the above results suggest that the RdCDC3 protein is an important factor in making female leafhoppers and eggs relatively unpalatable and toxic to predators, demonstrating its crucial role in protecting leafhoppers from consumption.

RdCDC9 is specifically expressed in the gut of leafhoppers and maintains homeostasis of the gut microbiome

The above results demonstrated that RdCDC3 retained the characteristics of bacterial CDCs, such as protein structure and toxic function. However, RdCDC9 appears to have been domesticated and evolved more like a leafhopper gene, containing introns and losing cholesterol‐binding activity as well as cytotoxicity, suggesting that it may have evolved new functions. Therefore, we further explored the functions of RdCDC9 in leafhoppers. First, we examined the expression pattern of RdCDC9 and found that it was highly expressed in the gut (Figure 5A), which is a key organ in insect nutrient absorption, immune regulation, and microbial interactions. Thus, we evaluated whether RdCDC9 influenced the growth, development, or reproduction of leafhoppers. We silenced RdCDC9 by RNA interference (RNAi), and the expression of RdCDC9 was significantly suppressed (Figure S10B and S10D). Surprisingly, we found that the knockdown of RdCDC9 led to a significant reduction in weight gain (Student's t test, p < 0.001, Figure 5B). Survival was also significantly lower in the treatment group than in the control group (quasibinomial generalized linear model [GLM], F = 11.10, p = 0.03, Figure 5C). Additionally, treatment significantly affected offspring number (Poisson GLM, χ2 = 11.16, p < 0.001), with the mean number of offspring decreasing from 47.7 in the control group to 30.7 in the treatment group, corresponding to an approximately 35.7% reduction in offspring production (Figure 5D). Together, these results indicated that RdCDC9, despite its bacterial origin and lineage‐specific distribution, has acquired critical roles in multiple fitness‐related traits, including growth, survival, and reproduction.

Based on the gut‐specific expression patterns and important physiological functions of RdCDC9, we speculated that the silencing of RdCDC9 may disrupt the gut microbial community of leafhoppers and thus affect their nutrient metabolism and survival. Therefore, we compared the gut microbiomes of RdCDC9 knockdown (dsRdCDC9) and control (dsGFP) individuals using a quantitative metagenomic sequencing approach. After quality control, assembly, and redundancy removal, 1.15 × 106 contigs with a total length of 883 Mb were obtained, and 4.5 × 105 genes were annotated. On average, 44% of the sequences were assigned to bacteria (Figure S11A), whereas the relative abundances of all other microorganisms were less than 0.01%. Therefore, we focused on the gut bacterial community and detected 320 operational taxonomic units (OTUs), 262 of which were present in both dsGFP and dsRdCDC9 samples (Figure 5E). Additionally, these samples showed no significant differences in community richness and diversity (Figure S11B), such that the top six most abundant bacterial OTUs were identical and an unclassified Alphaproteobacteria dominated the microbial community at approximately 66% relative abundance (Figure 5F). Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) based on Bray–Curtis distance also showed that samples from the two groups partially overlapped (Figure S11C), and statistical testing via permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) indicated no significant differentiation between groups (F = 0.24, p = 0.70). These results suggested the core microbiome was relatively stable in RdCDC9 knockdown individuals. Nevertheless, we observed clear separation between the two groups by conducting supervised dimension reduction analysis, such as partial least‐squares discriminant analysis (PLS‐DA) (Figure 5G). Therefore, we wondered what bacteria drove the differences between groups and were associated with the silencing of RdCDC9. Student's t‐test showed that the absolute abundances of four OTUs were significantly increased in dsRdCDC9 samples (p < 0.05), including members of the genera Acinetobacter, Janthinobacterium, Sphingomonas, and Mitsuaria (Figure 5H). In contrast, the abundances of two endosymbiont bacteria of the genus Wolbachia were decreased when RdCDC9 was silenced (Figure 5H), which was also supported by linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) results (Figures 5I, S11D, and S11E). In addition to bacterial abundance profiling, we investigated the potential functions of the metagenome, with a particular focus on identifying factors that may be detrimental to leafhopper survival and reproduction. Thus, the functions of gene sets were annotated by searching the Virulence Factor Database (VFDB) (Figure S11F). Based on the abundance of genes related to virulence factors (Figure 5J), the samples were separated into two distinct clusters, which precisely corresponded to the RdCDC9 knockdown and control groups, respectively (Figures 5K and S11G), and the observed difference was significant (PERMANOVA test, F = 12, p < 0.05; analysis of similarities [ANOSIM] test, p < 0.05). Specifically, the gene abundances of four VFDB categories, such as the effector delivery system (VFC0086), immune modulation (VFC0258), adherence (VFC0001), and regulation (VFC0301), were significantly higher in RdCDC9 knockdown samples (Figure 5L, Wilcoxon test, p < 0.05). Among them, genes related to the effector delivery system had the highest abundance and mainly originated from the genera Pantoea, Acinetobacter, and Enterobacter (Figure 5M). Gene‐level analysis revealed six virulence factor genes with significantly increased abundance in dsRdCDC9 samples, which were functionally categorized into effector secretion (including the type VI secretion system proteins tssC and tssD, as well as the outer membrane protein ompA) and adhesion and motility of pathogenic bacteria (the Tap pili component tapT, elongation factor tufA, and flagella‐related gene cheY) (Figure S11H). These results indicated that silencing RdCDC9 altered the gut microbial community, leading to a reduction in beneficial endosymbiotic bacteria and the enrichment of potentially harmful bacteria carrying virulence factors. These microbial changes may partially contribute to the impaired growth and development observed in leafhoppers, suggesting that RdCDC9 could potentially influence host fitness through modulation of the associated microbial community.

To summarize, the results from this research established the new concept that the domestication and diversification of horizontally transferred genes of bacterial origin empower leafhopper adaptation through diverse functions, including helping females avoid predators and ensure reproduction and maintaining gut microbiome homeostasis in a favorable state (Figure 6).

DISCUSSION

In this research, we constructed three high‐quality genome assemblies representing two genera in the largest subfamily of Cicadellidae, Deltocephalinae, which will facilitate research on the phylogenetic relationships, chromosome evolution, and genomic basis of adaptive traits in leafhoppers [26]. In the genomes of the three leafhopper species, we identified multiple genes encoding CDCs, which are of gram‐positive bacterial origin (Figures 1F and 2E). These CDCs exhibit high sequence and structural variation, as well as functional variation. Understanding the functions of insect genes acquired via HGT, especially in non‐model organisms, is challenging. Recently, a few case studies have shown that multiple independent HGT events have occurred in Hemiptera, and HGT events have been found to play critical ecological roles in insects by detoxifying toxins in food, overcoming plant defenses, and providing resistance to their parasitoid wasps [12]. HGT can also contribute to male courtship in lepidopterans [27].

Here, we present a unique HGT event that allowed us to perform a systematic study to trace the evolutionary history and characterize the functional features of homologous transferred genes in the same recipient genome [28, 29]. The results demonstrated that the functional diversification and domestication of these horizontally transferred genes have enabled leafhoppers to adapt to and combat their natural enemies and balance their life history.

Among these CDCs, RdCDC3 exhibits typical bacterial CDC characteristics, such as strong cholesterol affinity and cytotoxicity, and the developmental stage and serosa expression specificity prevent it from damaging the leafhoppers themselves, instead making them unpalatable and toxic to the predator M. discolor. In contrast, RdCDC9 is domesticated and more like a leafhopper gene; it no longer has cholesterol‐binding capacity or cytotoxicity, but it is specifically expressed in the gut of the leafhopper. Our analyses suggest that RdCDC9 may contribute to maintaining gut microbial homeostasis and, consequently, influence leafhopper growth and development (Figure 6). We propose that RdCDC9‐mediated modulation of the gut microbiome represents a potential mechanism underlying these phenotypic effects. Nevertheless, the direct causal relationship between microbial alterations and host developmental outcomes remains to be established and requires further validation, for example, through experiments using gnotobiotic insect systems.

Additionally, RdCDC6 is apparently in a transient state between RdCDC3 and RdCDC9 in terms of sequence, structure, and function. Thus, we propose that these horizontally transferred CDCs were domesticated and coevolved in leafhoppers through natural selection during long‐term interactions with other organisms in the environment. Pressures from domestication and natural selection may result in sequence, structural, expression pattern, and functional diversification. Therefore, by performing an in‐depth functional characterization of these horizontally transferred CDCs, our study systematically revealed that these genes have experienced varying degrees of domestication in the recipient genome, undergone functional differentiation, and jointly benefited the recipient species in multiple ways.

This study presents a groundbreaking exploration of HGT as a key driver of adaptation in insects, using leafhoppers (Cicadellidae) as a model system. The findings reveal a sophisticated evolutionary strategy in which leafhoppers have harnessed bacterial genes to enhance survival in their ecological niches. In general, the findings from this study may lead to an in‐depth understanding of the adaptation of the insect world to the environment and the development of novel strategies for the control of sap‐sucking pests and the diseases they transmit through targeting CDC genes and microbiome engineering.

CONCLUSION

Leafhoppers acquired CDC genes from gram‐positive bacteria through HGT. These genes subsequently underwent functional diversification: conserved toxic CDCs are specifically expressed in the egg serosa and protect eggs against natural enemies, whereas nontoxic CDCs have evolved novel gut‐specific functions that likely contribute to microbiome homeostasis and promote leafhopper growth. Together, our findings demonstrate how the domestication and functional diversification of horizontally transferred genes can enhance host fitness through multiple mechanisms, highlighting HGT as an important source of evolutionary innovation in insects.

METHODS

Insect breeding

Leafhoppers were collected from Zhejiang Province, China, and maintained under experimental conditions for more than 10 generations. The insects were reared on rice seedlings (Zhonghua 11 rice variety) at 25 ± 1°C under a 14:10 h (light: dark) photoperiod with a relative humidity of 60%–70%.

Cell culture

Monkey (Vero) and human (HeLa and HEK293T) cell lines used for the MTT assay were cultured at 37°C with 20% (v/v) O2 and 5% (v/v) CO2. The cells were cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (Gibco, 11965092) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (Gemini, 900‐108), 2 mM l‐glutamine (Gibco, 25030081), 50 µM 2‐mercaptoethanol (Thermo Fisher, 60‐24‐2), and 1% (v/v) penicillin−streptomycin (Gibco, 15140163). Sf9 cells that were transfected to express GFP‐tagged CDCs were cultured in incubators at 25°C with SIM SF Expression Medium (Sino Biological, MSF1). Leafhopper VCM cells were cultured in growth media [30].

Genome assembly and quality assessment

PacBio long‐read and Illumina short‐read sequences were used for de novo assembly of the leafhopper genomes. For Ne. cincticeps and R. dorsalis, PacBio reads were assembled via the NextDenovo pipeline v2.3 [31], and the draft sequences were polished using NextPolish v1.2.4 [32] with the default parameters. To reduce assembly errors, PacBio reads were aligned to the assemblies using minimap2 v2.17 [33], and Polar Star (https://github.com/phasegenomics/polar_star) was applied to determine read depth. Then, outliers were found (100‐kb windows with a depth less than 0.75 times the average value or more than three times the average), and contigs were broken. For Ne. nigropictus, PacBio reads were first corrected with Canu v2.1 [34] and subsequently assembled using Wtdbg2 v2.5 [35], after which three rounds of polishing with Pilon v1.24 [36] were performed on the draft assembly via Illumina reads. Redundant contigs were removed using Purge Haplotigs v1.1.1 [37] based on the read coverage of the PacBio reads. The Hi‐C data were aligned to the draft assemblies with Juicer v1.6 [38], and the contigs were further anchored to superscaffolds using 3D‐DNA v180419 [39] based on the Hi‐C interaction results. Juicebox v1.11.8 (https://github.com/aidenlab/Juicebox) was used for visualizing the Hi‐C contact matrix and manually correcting misassembled chromosomes. To identify the sex chromosome, the Illumina reads were aligned to chromosome‐level assemblies using BWA‐MEM v0.7.17 [40], and the read coverage of 100‐kb windows was determined using SAMtools (v1.9) depth [41]. The completeness of the genome assemblies and annotations was evaluated against the Insecta_odb10 dataset, which included 1367 genes, using BUSCO v3.0.2 [42].

Repeat and gene annotation

To identify transposable elements (TEs) in the leafhopper genomes, a species‐specific TE library was constructed by combining the de novo library predicted by RepeatModeler2 v2.0.1 [43] and LTR_retriever v2.8 [44] with the Repbase v19.06 [45], REXdb v3.0 [46], and Dfam v3.2 [47] databases. TE sequences were identified and classified via a homology search against the library using RepeatMasker v4.10 (http://www.repeatmasker.org). Tandem repeats were annotated using Tandem Repeats Finder v4.09 [48] and the MIcroSAtellite identification tool v2.1 [49].

Ab initio prediction, homology searches, and transcript‐based assembly were integrated for protein‐coding gene prediction. Augustus v2.4 [50] and SNAP v2006‐07‐28 [51] were used for ab initio gene prediction. The homologous sequences of the Drosophila melanogaster (BDGP6, GCA_000001215.4) [52], Acyrthosiphon pisum (GCA_000142985.2) [53], Laodelphax striatella (GCA_003335185.2) [54], Nilaparvata lugens (NilLug1.0, GCF_000757685.1) [55], and Homalodisca vitripennis (v0.5.3 (i5k), GCA_000696855.2) (10.15482/USDA. ADC/1410182) gene models were searched using GeMoMa v1.7 [56]. Transcriptome data were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2 v2.0.4 [57], and genes were predicted using StringTie v1.2.3 [58], followed by GeneMarkS‐T v5.1 [59]. Trinity v2.11 [60] was used for the de novo assembly of transcripts, and genes were predicted by PASA v2.0.2 [61]. Final gene models were integrated using EVM v1.1.1 [62], and isoforms were updated by PASA. The functions and domains of protein‐coding genes were annotated based on sequence similarity. Gene Ontology (GO) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathways were annotated using eggNOG‐mapper v2 [63], and protein signatures were inferred by InterProScan v5.46 [64] against the InterPro database [65].

Comparative genome analysis

Synteny among the three leafhopper genomes was detected and visualized by MCScan (Python version) [66]. To identify orthologous genes between leafhoppers and other insects, the orthogroups among D. melanogaster, L. striatella, N. lugens, Oncopeltus fasciatus (OGSv1.2 (I5K), GCA_000696205.2) [67], H. vitripennis, and the three leafhopper species were assigned using OrthoFinder v2.5.4 [68]. The species phylogeny was inferred from the CDSs of all single‐copy orthologs (907 kb in length). The maximum likelihood tree was reconstructed using IQ‐TREE v2.4 [69], with the best‐fitting nucleotide substitution model GTR + F + I + G4, and 100 bootstrap replicates were used. The species divergence times were estimated using MCMCTree [70] with the following parameters: burn‐in 5000000, sample 5000000, Sampfreq. 30, and correlation 1 (calibration points). Gene family and superfamily evolution was inferred using CAFÉ v4.2.1 [71] based on the time‐calibrated species tree. The gene numbers of each superfamily in the SUPERFAMILY database [72] were summarized according to the InterProScan v5.46 [64] results for all eight species.

Evolution of key genes

Considering that the automatic annotation process may have led to errors, annotations of the focal gene families were manually checked according to the results of the homology search and transcriptome data alignments. For the homology method, the protein sequences of the CDC genes were aligned to the reference genome using GenBlastA v1.0.4 [73], and potential genes were predicted with GeneWise v2.4.1 [74]. To characterize the gene structure, SignalP v5.0 [75] and TMHMM v2.0 [76] were used to detect the signal peptide and transmembrane domain, respectively. Gene positions on the chromosomes were visualized using MG2C v2 [77].

To evaluate gene evolution rates, the CDSs of the homologs were aligned using PRANK v.170427 [78] with the translated alignment model, and the Ka/Ks ratios were calculated using KaKs Calculator v2.0 [79] with the LWL model. The branch‐site model in PAML v4.9 [70] was used to detect lineage‐specific positive selection signals, and the selected sites were inferred by Bayes empirical Bayes analysis.

Gene trees were constructed using RAxML v7.2.6 [80] with the amino acid substitution model PROTGAMMAJTT and 100 rapid bootstrap replicates based on the protein sequence alignments generated by MUSCLE v3.8.31 [81].

To determine phylogenetic relationships between the leafhopper CDCs and outgroup sequences, we used a hierarchical clustering method in accordance with previous studies, given that the dataset included prokaryote and eukaryote species and the sequences were highly divergent [21]. Specifically, protein sequences of the CDC family (Transporter Classification Database [TCDB] ID 1.C.12), the MACPF family (TCDB ID 1.C.39), and the pleurotolysin B family (TCDB ID 1.C.97.1) were downloaded from TCDB [82] (https://tcdb.org/). All available data for the CDC family and pleurotolysin B family were used, and only one sequence was selected to represent each cluster of the MACPF family. The sequences were pairwise aligned using BLASTP v2.2.26 [83], and for sequence pairs with an E‐value less than the cutoff (10), the distance between the two proteins was set to 100/bit. Otherwise, the distance was set to 10. The hclust function from the stats package in the program R was used for hierarchical cluster analysis based on the distance matrices, and the parameter “method” was set to average. The results were visualized with iTOL v6 [84].

To identify CDC homologs in other insect species, annotated protein datasets of insect genomes were downloaded from the NCBI RefSeq database (Table S3). Leafhopper CDC proteins were used as queries for BLASTP v2.2.26 [83] searches, and hits with an E‐value < 1e‐5 were considered potential CDC homologs. To trace the evolutionary history of CDC genes in Cicadellidae, all available Cicadellidae genome assemblies (as of August 2026) were downloaded from NCBI (Table S4). Due to the lack of genome annotation for some of these assemblies, we used BUSCO v3.0.2 [42] to predict the coding sequences of conserved insect genes (insecta_odb10 dataset) and used OrthoFinder [68] to obtain orthologous genes and construct the species tree. For the CDC gene family, as previously mentioned, GenBlastA v1.0.4 [73] and GeneWise v2.4.1 [74] were used for gene prediction.

RSCU was calculated using CodonW v1.4.4 [85] with default parameters and visualized using the pheatmap package.

Protein structure prediction

The structure of the full‐length CDC protein was predicted using AlphaFold2 [23]. The prediction with the highest confidence was aligned to the crystal structure of perfringolysin O (PDB ID: 1PFO) [18] and visualized using PyMOL (The PyMOL Molecular Graphics System, Version 2.0, Schrödinger, LLC).

Karyotyping assay

Testes were extracted from the abdomens of male adult leafhoppers, treated with hypotonic fluid (0.075 M KCl) for 15 min, and then fixed in Carnoy's fixative (ethanol: acetic acid = 3:1) for 2 h. Testicular follicles were separated on glass slides and stained in carbol fuchsin solution for 15 min. Then, the coverslips were pressed on. Images of cells in meiosis were captured under a microscope.

DNA extraction and PCR

DNA was extracted using an Animal Genomic DNA Quick Extraction Kit for PCR Analysis (Beyotime, D0065S). CDC genes were amplified using 2× Es Taq MasterMix (Dye) (CWBIO, CW0690S). The primers used for PCR are listed in Table S5.

RNA preparation and RT‐PCR and RT‐qPCR analyses

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen) and reverse‐transcribed with an M5 Super qPCR RT Kit containing gDNA remover (Mei5 Biotechnology, MF166). The cDNA obtained was used as the template for RT–PCR and qPCR. qPCR was performed using the CFX96 Real‐Time PCR Detection System (Bio‐Rad) with 2× Real‐time PCR Super Mix (SYBR Green, with anti‐Taq) (Mei5 Biotechnology, MF013). Relative transcript levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔC(t) method, with Profilin serving as the internal standard. The primers used for RT–qPCR are listed in Table S5.

Gene cloning and plasmid construction

RdCDC3 (Id09G003230), RdCDC6 (Id08G023250), and RdCDC9 (Id09G000860) sequences were used throughout this study. To clone the CDSs of RdCDC3, 6, and 9, total RNA was extracted from leafhoppers and used as a template for RT–PCR with the specific primers shown in Table S5. Full‐length RdCDC3, 6, and 9 open reading frames (ORFs) were cloned and inserted into pFastHTB‐GFP for baculovirus expression in Sf9 cells. For yeast transformation, the ORFs of RdCDC3, 6, and 9 were cloned and inserted into pGBK. For protein purification in E. coli, the ORFs of RdCDC3, 6, and 9 were cloned and inserted into pGEX‐KG to express the GST‐tagged proteins RdCDC3, 6, and 9, respectively. All constructs used in this research were sequenced from both ends.

Yeast activity assay

Yeast transformation and screening were performed according to the manufacturer's instructions (Clontech).

Western blotting

Total proteins were extracted from different leafhopper tissues in SDS buffer [10% β‐mercaptoethanol, 100 mM Tris (pH 6.8), 20% glycerol, 4% SDS, and 0.2% bromophenol blue], separated via SDS‐PAGE, and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. Protein accumulation was detected with the corresponding anti‐RdCDC3 (1:3000) polyclonal antibodies, and a goat anti‐rabbit IgG horseradish peroxidase conjugate (1:10,000) was used as a secondary antibody. Samples were subsequently incubated with Pierce ECL Plus chemiluminescent substrate before exposure.

Baculovirus expression of RdCDCs in Sf9 cells

Recombinant baculovirus shuttle vectors expressing RdCDC3‐GFP, RdCDC6‐GFP, and RdCDC9‐GFP were constructed and transformed into E. coli DH10Bac cells. Recombinant bacmid DNA with the expected constructs was transfected into Sf9 cells using Cellfectin Reagent (Invitrogen) [86]. P1 recombinant baculovirus stock was collected after 5 days (>106 pfu/mL). The virus was amplified by infecting Sf9 cells, after which the P2 recombinant baculovirus stock was collected (>107 pfu/mL). Finally, sufficient virus‐infected Sf9 cells were collected at 2 dpi to detect the target proteins by western blotting or immunofluorescence.

MST assay

To detect the ability of the RdCDCs to bind cholesterol, MST experiments were performed using a Monolith NT.115 (Blue/Red) instrument (NanoTemper Technologies GmbH) as previously described [87, 88]. Lysates of Sf9 cells expressing RdCDC3‐GFP, RdCDC6‐GFP, and RdCDC9‐GFP (above) were used as the sources of fluorescently labeled RdCDCs, whereas the concentration of cholesterol varied. After 15 min of incubation, the samples were loaded into MST standard glass capillaries. Measurements were performed at 22°C in MST buffer containing 10 mM Na‐phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), 1 mM MgCl2, 3 mM KCl, 150 mM NaCl, and 0.05% Tween‐20 to provide the optimal fluorescence intensity using a light‐emitting diode (LED) source with 40% LED power and 20% MST power on the MST machine. The Hill model was used to evaluate binding affinity, and each data point represents the mean ΔFnorm value from four independent experiments. Data were analyzed using NanoTemper analysis and MO.Affinity.

Protein purification

RdCDCs were recombinantly expressed with an N‐terminal GST tag in the pGEX‐4T vector in E. coli strain BL21 (DE3) after 18 h at 16°C. Bacterial lysates were applied to GSTrap columns (GE Healthcare) equilibrated with PB buffer (200 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris‐HCl, pH 7.5, and 10% [v/v] glycerol). The bound proteins were eluted using PB buffer containing 10 mM reduced glutathione (NEB).

Cell viability assay (MTT assay)

Cells were counted and plated in 96‐well microplates overnight at a concentration of 1 × 104/mL. Then, the cells were subjected to twofold serial dilutions of the RdCDCs in medium for 24 h at 37°C. Next, 100 µl of MTT diluted in serum‐free medium was added to each well, and the cells were incubated for 4 h at 37°C [20]. A total of 100 µL of solubilization solution (dimethyl sulfoxide) was added to each well, and the plate was incubated for an additional 10 min at room temperature, after which the absorbance of the generated formazan was measured at 493 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek). Cell viability curves were analyzed using Origin software (OriginPro 2020). The RdCDC concentration that resulted in 50% cell viability was defined as the IC50 value. Data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) from three independent biological replicates.

GUV preparation

To prepare GUVs containing different cholesterol concentrations, 0.05 mg of lipids were mixed at the desired ratios: 1,2‐dioleoyl‐sn‐glycero‐3‐phosphocholine (DOPC): 1,2‐dioleoyl‐sn‐glycero‐3‐phosphoethanolamine (DOPE) = 4:3, DOPC: DOPE: cholesterol = 4:3:1, or DOPC: DOPE: cholesterol = 4:3:2 for GUVs containing 0%, 10%, or 20% cholesterol, respectively. A total of 0.025% 18:1 Liss Rhod PE was added to fluorescently label the GUVs. GUVs were then prepared via the electroformation method using Vesicle Prep‐Pro (Nanion). Indium tin oxide (ITO)‐covered glass slides were gently cleaned with liquid soap, followed by three washes with distilled water and ethanol before use. A spacer (rubber ring) was attached to the ITO‐covered side of the slide to create a confined chamber. The lipid mixture was then deposited onto the confined glass surface and air‐dried to form a lipid film. The chamber was gently filled with 280 µL of 300 mM sorbitol, and electroformation was then carried out at 0.24 V and 9.9 Hz for 90 min at 37°C. The solution containing GUVs was collected and stored at 4°C before use.

GUV perforation assay

The GUV perforation assay was performed using a Nikon A1 HD25 confocal microscope. Images were acquired using a 60× objective at 1024 × 1024 pixels. GUVs were diluted with 10 μg/mL FITC‐dextran (10 kDa). Three droplets of diluted GUVs were separately deposited on a glass‐bottom confocal chamber (Cellvis) and allowed to settle for 0.5–1 h before imaging. A three‐phase time‐lapse imaging protocol was used to capture the dynamics of perforation. In the first phase, three images of GUVs before the addition of protein were acquired at 30‐s intervals. The second phase was a 60‐s pause without acquisition, during which RdCDCs or PLO were added to the GUV droplets. Three different conditions could be performed in a single trial. The third phase was a 15‐min time‐lapse video monitoring the leakage or deformation of GUVs after protein addition.

Data processing was performed using NIS‐Elements software. First, alignment was performed using NIS‐Elements to correct XY motions. Then, regions of interest (ROIs) were defined in areas corresponding to the lumen of individual GUVs. Three reference ROIs were defined in GUV‐free spaces. Finally, the fluorescence intensity of FITC‐dextran over time was measured for all ROIs, and the results were normalized using the values of the reference ROIs. The final results were plotted using GraphPad Prism.

Microinjection of dsRNA or RdCDC proteins into insects

Fragments were amplified by RT‐PCR, and the dsRNA specific to each leafhopper CDC and GFP was synthesized using a T7 RiboMAX Express RNAi Kit (Promega) following the manufacturer's instructions. Adult leafhoppers were injected with 1.5 µg/µL dsRNA via the thorax using a Nanoliter 2000 injector (World Precision Instruments). GST‐RdCDCs were purified using a previously described method. Adult M. discolor were injected with 1.5 µg of protein via the thorax using a Nanoliter 2000 injector (World Precision Instruments).

Cell and egg immunofluorescence

Leafhopper eggs were carefully punctured near the egg cap, and Sf9 and VCM cells were seeded onto cell culture dishes and incubated with the GST‐RdCDC3, GST‐RdCDC6, GST‐RdCDC9, and GST proteins. Then, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (w/v) at room temperature for 20 min or 4°C overnight. The cells or eggs were washed three times with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS), incubated for 10 min (30 min for eggs) after the addition of Triton X‐100 (final concentration, 0.5%), blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin diluted in PBS for 1 h, and incubated with primary antibodies against GST or RdCDC3 (1:100 dilution) for 1 h and fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies for 1 h. The slides were mounted using antifade mounting medium with 4',6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (DAPI; Meilunbio, MA0222‐1). Fluorescence images were captured with an LSM 710 confocal microscope. The images were analyzed using ImageJ.

Cryosection immunogold labeling

The eggs collected from the rice seedlings were fixed via high‐pressure freezing (HPF, Leica EM HPM100) with 5% sucrose as the cryoprotectant. Approximately 1–2 mL of acetone was added to an aluminum can, which was placed in liquid nitrogen until the acetone froze to form a solid. The frozen sample planchette was removed and placed on the aluminum with precooled tweezers. Freeze‐substitution was carried out as follows with 0.4% (w/v) uranyl acetate in acetone. First, the mixture was incubated at −85°C for 24 h, the temperature was slowly increased to −50°C over 5 h, and the mixture was rinsed in ethanol three times for an additional 2 h. Next, the temperature was slowly increased to −35°C and kept at this temperature for 48 h, after which the solution was changed to HM20 resin (Electron Microscopy Sciences, 14340) with gradually increasing concentrations (50%, 75% [v/v] in ethanol, 100%, 100%, 100%). Finally, the resin was polymerized at −35°C for 48 h. The sample was then cut into 80‐nm sections and placed on 100‐mesh nickel mesh. The sections were blocked with PBS containing 0.2% gelatin and 0.1% acetylated bovine serum albumin (BSA‐c) for 10 min. The primary antibody (diluted 100× in blocking solution) was added to each coverslip for 30 min of incubation at room temperature. Then, the samples were washed with PBS containing 0.1% BSA‐c for 2 min, and this process was repeated five times. Subsequently, sections were incubated with immunolabeled CMC‐Protein A‐gold (10 nm protein A‐gold; diluted 1:50) (Abcam) for 25 min and washed with PBS containing 0.1% BSA‐c 5 times for 2 min each. The sections were subsequently placed on copper wire mesh for observation using a JEOL JEM‐1400Flash (HC) transmission electron microscope (Japan).

Feeding test with a natural enemy

To investigate the potential effects of leafhopper CDC proteins on the feeding of leafhopper predators, we conducted a feeding behavior experiment using M. discolor ladybugs, which were starved for 2 h before the experiments. A total of 2 mg of E. kuehniella eggs and B. campestris pollen were mixed with 10 µg of GST or purified GST‐RdCDC3/GST‐RdCDC9 proteins. The two kinds of food were positioned on each side of a Petri dish, which was 5.5 cm in diameter. A ladybug was placed into the Petri dish and allowed to move freely for 4 h. A light box was used for illumination, and a Sony FDR‐AX60 video recorder (1920 pixels × 1080 pixels @ 25 fps) was used to capture videos. The videos were downsampled to 1 fps and analyzed using OpenCV modules in Python to trace the position of the ladybug in each frame and count the duration of contact between the ladybug and the food as the feeding time.
