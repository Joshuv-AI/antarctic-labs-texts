1.1
Our Results and Discussion

This work gives the first non-trivial lower bounds for query-based convexity testing. We prove three different lower bounds for three variants of the property testing model, which we now describe. As mentioned, the best known algorithm for convexity testing is the non-adaptive algorithm of
[
CFSS17
]
, which makes
2^{\tilde{O}(\sqrt{n})/\varepsilon^{2}}
non-adaptive queries (and makes two-sided error).

Our first result gives a polynomial lower bound for one-sided adaptive testers:

Theorem 1

(One-sided adaptive lower bound)
.

For some absolute constant
\varepsilon>0
, any one-sided (potentially adaptive)
\varepsilon
-tester for convexity over
N(0,I_{n})
must use
n^{\Omega(1)}
queries.

We also consider a challenging and well-studied extension of the standard testing model which is known as
tolerant testing

[
PRR06
]
.
Recall that an
(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2})
-tolerant tester for a class of functions is a testing algorithm which must accept with high probability if the input is
\varepsilon_{1}
-close to some function in the class and reject with high probability if the input is
\varepsilon_{2}
-far from every function in the class; thus the standard property testing model corresponds to
(0,\varepsilon)
-tolerant testing.

The sample-based algorithm for convexity testing that is given in
[
CFSS17
]
is based on agnostic learning results from
[
KOS08
]
. It follows easily from the analysis in
[
CFSS17
]
and results of
[
KOS08
]
that for any
0\leq\varepsilon_{1}<\varepsilon_{2}
with
\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon
, the
[
CFSS17
]
approach gives a
2^{\tilde{O}(\sqrt{n})/\varepsilon^{4}}
-query sample-based algorithm for
(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2})
-tolerant testing of convexity. As our final result, we give a mildly exponential lower bound on the query complexity of two-sided non-adaptive tolerant convexity testing:

Theorem 2

(Two-sided non-adaptive tolerant testing lower bound)
.

There exist absolute constants
0<\varepsilon_{1}<\varepsilon_{2}<0.5
such that any non-adaptive
(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2})
-tolerant tester for convexity over
N(0,I_{n})
(which may make two-sided errors) must use at least
2^{\Omega(n^{1/4})}
queries.

Returning to the standard testing model, our final result gives a polynomial lower bound for two-sided non-adaptive testers:

Theorem 3

(Two-sided non-adaptive lower bound)
.

For any constant
c>0
, there is a constant
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{c}>0
such that any non-adaptive
\varepsilon
-tester for convexity over
N(0,I_{n})
(which may make two-sided errors) must use at least
n^{1/4-c}
queries.

Since
q
-query non-adaptive lower bounds imply
(\log q)
-query adaptive lower bounds, Theorem
3
implies an
\Omega(\log n)
two-sided adaptive convexity testing lower bound. (This is in contrast to the
n^{\Omega(1)}
-query lower bound against one-sided adaptive testers given by Theorem
1
.)

Techniques

Our lower bounds rely on a wide range of techniques and constructions, and draw inspiration from prior work on
monotonicity testing
of Boolean functions
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}

[
CST14
,
BB16
,
CWX17
,
PRW22
,
CDL+24
]
.
6
6

6

Recall that a Boolean function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
is
monotone
if whenever
x,y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
satisfy
x_{i}\leq y_{i}
for
i\in[n]
, we have
f(x)\leq f(y)
.
Indeed, a conceptual contribution of our work is to highlight a (perhaps unexpected) connection between ideas in monotonicity testing and convexity testing.
Our work thus adds to and strengthens a recently emerging analogy between monotone Boolean functions and high-dimensional convex sets
[
DNS21
,
DNS22
,
DNS24
]
.
Establishing this connection requires a number of technical and conceptual innovations for each of our main results; we highlight some of the key ideas below.

1.2.1
The Nazarov Body

A central role in our lower bounds in Theorem
1
and Theorem
2
is played by the so-called “Nazarov body”
[
Naz03
,
KOS08
,
CFSS17
]
. This is a randomized construction of a convex set
\boldsymbol{B}
which is a slight variation of a construction originally due to Nazarov
[
Naz03
]
, which is essentially as follows: we choose
N\approx 2^{\sqrt{n}}
halfspaces
\boldsymbol{H}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{H}_{N}
in the space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where each halfspace
\boldsymbol{H}_{i}
is a
random halfspace
at a distance roughly
n^{1/4}
from the origin. In more detail, each halfspace is
\boldsymbol{H}_{i}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathbf{1}{\left\{\boldsymbol{g}^{i}\cdot x\geq r\right\}}
where
r\approx n^{3/4}
and
\boldsymbol{g}^{i}
is drawn from
N(0,I_{n})
. The convex set
\boldsymbol{B}
is obtained by taking the intersection of all
N
halfspaces with
\mathrm{Ball}(\sqrt{n})
, the origin-centered ball of radius
\sqrt{n}.
7
7

7

We remark that the original construction of
[
Naz03
]
differs from our construction in a number of ways: the distribution over random halfspaces is slightly different, and the body is not intersected with
\mathrm{Ball}(\sqrt{n}).
For technical reasons, our specific construction facilitates our lower bound arguments.

The exact parameters
r
and
N
are set carefully so that with high probability the “Gaussian volume” of
\boldsymbol{B}
, i.e.
\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})}[\boldsymbol{g}\in\boldsymbol{B}],
is a constant bounded away from
0
and
1
.

Note that for the Nazarov body
\boldsymbol{B}
and any point
x\in\mathrm{Ball}(\sqrt{n})\setminus\boldsymbol{B}
, there is a non-empty subset
J_{x}\subseteq[N]
such that
j\in J_{x}
iff
\boldsymbol{H}_{j}(x)=0
, i.e., the point
x

violates
the halfspace
\boldsymbol{H}_{j}
for all
j\in J_{x}
. Now, define a point
x\in\mathrm{Ball}(\sqrt{n})\setminus\boldsymbol{B}
to lie in the set
\boldsymbol{U}_{i}
if the set
J_{x}=\{i\}
, so
x\in\mathrm{Ball}(\sqrt{n})
lies in
\boldsymbol{U}_{i}
if
\boldsymbol{H}_{i}
is the unique halfspace violated by
x
.
The set
\boldsymbol{U}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\cup_{i\in[N]}\boldsymbol{U}_{i}
is thus the set of “uniquely violated points” in
\mathrm{Ball}(\sqrt{n}).

A crucial feature of the Nazarov construction is that the Gaussian volume of the set of points which are uniquely violated, i.e., Gaussian volume of the set
\boldsymbol{U}
, is “large” compared to the
Gaussian volume of the set
\mathrm{Ball}(\sqrt{n})\setminus\boldsymbol{B}

(see
Lemma

15
for the precise statement).

The original construction of Nazarov may be viewed as a Gaussian-space analogue of
Talagrand’s random CNF formula

[
Tal96
]
(see
[
DNS24
]
for a discussion of this connection). Talagrand’s random CNF has been very useful in lower bounds for monotonicity testing over the Boolean hypercube, as demonstrated by
[
BB16
,
CWX17
,
CDL+24
]
. We use our modified Nazarov body to obtain new lower bounds for convexity testing, as described below.

1.2.2
One-Sided Adaptive Lower Bound

Recall that a
one-sided
tester always outputs “accept” on convex sets and outputs “reject” on far-from-convex sets with probability at least
2/3
— this requirement implies that the tester rejects only if a certificate of non-convexity is found (i.e. a set of queries
x_{1},\dots,x_{t}
which lie in the body, and a query
y
in the convex hull of
x_{1},\dots,x_{t}
which is not in the body). In order to argue a
q
-query lower bound, it suffices to (1) design a distribution
{\cal D}_{\mathrm{no}}
over sets which are far-from-convex with high probability, and (2) argue that no
q
-query deterministic algorithm can find a certificate of non-convexity.

The key will be to “hide” the non-convexity within the uniquely violated sets of the Nazarov body. Consider working in
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
and first randomly draw an
n
-dimensional “control subspace”
\boldsymbol{C}
and the orthogonal
n
-dimensional “action subspace”
\boldsymbol{A}
; we embed the
n
-dimensional Nazarov body in the control subspace
\boldsymbol{C}
. A point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
lies in our (non-convex) body iff:

•

It has Euclidean norm at most
\sqrt{2n}
, and in addition,
x_{\boldsymbol{C}}
(the projection onto the control subspace) has norm at most
\sqrt{n}
; and

•

The point
x_{\boldsymbol{C}}
lies within an
n
-dimensional Nazarov body that we randomly sample within the control subspace
\boldsymbol{C}
;
or
, for every
j\in[N]
where
H_{j}(x_{\boldsymbol{C}})=0
, the projection
x_{\boldsymbol{A}}
on the action subspace lies outside of a “strip” of width
1
along a randomly sampled direction
\boldsymbol{v}^{j}
in the action subspace. (See
Section

4.1.2
).

Consider a line through a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
in direction
\boldsymbol{v}^{j}
, for
j\in[N]
such that
x_{\boldsymbol{C}}
lies in the uniquely violated region
\boldsymbol{U}_{j}
and
x_{\boldsymbol{A}}
lies inside the strip along
\boldsymbol{v}^{j}
(and therefore
outside
our body). Then, as the line proceeds out from
x
in directions
\boldsymbol{v}^{j}
and
-\boldsymbol{v}^{j}
, it remains in the uniquely violated region
\boldsymbol{U}_{j}
(since
\boldsymbol{v}_{j}
is orthogonal to
\boldsymbol{C}
) but exits the strip, thereby entering the body and exhibiting non-convexity. By design, the uniquely violated regions and the strips are large enough to constitute a constant fraction of the space, giving the desired distance to convexity (
Lemma

17
). Intuitively, detecting non-convexity is hard because the algorithm does not know
\boldsymbol{C}
, the halfspace
\boldsymbol{H}_{j}(\cdot)
, or the direction
\boldsymbol{v}^{j}
. In fact, we show that an algorithm which makes few queries cannot find, with probability at least
2/3
, two points
x,z
outside the same halfspace
\boldsymbol{H}_{j}(\cdot)
such that
x
lies inside and
z
outside the strip in direction
\boldsymbol{v}^{j}
.

Roughly speaking, the proof proceeds as follows. First, we show that, except with
o(1)
probability, any two queries
x,z
which are far (at distance at least
1000\sqrt{q}n^{1/4}
) cannot lie outside the same halfspace
\boldsymbol{H}_{j}(\cdot)
while having projections onto
\boldsymbol{C}
with norm at most
\sqrt{n}
(
Lemma

24
), and moreover it is extremely unlikely for a query to be falsified by more than
q
halfspaces (this follows from a calculation in
Lemma

11
). The argument is geometric in nature and is given in
Section

4.5
, and essentially argues that it is unlikely, since the algorithm does not know the control subspace
\boldsymbol{C}
or the vector defining the halfspace, that two far-away queries happen to uniquely falsify the same halfspace.

On the other hand, consider the halfspaces which are falsified by some query (and notice there are most
q^{2}
such halfspaces, since each query is falsified by at most
q
halfspaces). Since all such queries are within distance
1000\sqrt{q}n^{1/4}
of each other, the projection of any two such queries onto the direction defining the strip is a segment of length
O(\sqrt{q}/n^{1/4})
with high probability, and the precise location of this segment is uniform(-like, from Gaussian anti-concentration) (see
Section

4.5.1
). Therefore, the probability of any particular segment of length
O(\sqrt{q}/n^{1/4})
which goes from inside to outside the strip of width
1
is roughly
O(\sqrt{q}/n^{1/4})
. We take a union bound over the
q^{2}
possible halfspaces, each containing at most
q
queries which define segments which may “cross” the strip with probability
O(\sqrt{q}/n^{1/4})
, for a total probability of
O(q^{3.5}/n^{1/4})
. Since this must be at least
2/3
for the algorithm to succeed, this gives the
n^{\Omega(1)}
lower bound.

1.2.3
Two-Sided Non-Adaptive Tolerant Lower Bound

Continuing the analogy with monotonicity testing lower bounds, the proof of Theorem
2
is inspired by recent lower bounds on tolerant monotonicity testing, namely
[
PRW22
]
and the follow-up work of
[
CDL+24
]
. The basic idea of
[
PRW22
]

is to construct a family of functions by randomly partitioning the space of variables into
control variables
and
action variables
: if the control variables are not balanced, i.e. there are more 1s than 0s (or vice-versa), then the function is trivially set to
1
(resp. to
0
) both for
\boldsymbol{f}\sim{\cal D}_{\mathrm{yes}}
and for
\boldsymbol{f}\sim{\cal D}_{\mathrm{no}}
. If the control variables are balanced,
then, at a high level,

1.

for
\boldsymbol{f}\sim{\cal D}_{\mathrm{yes}}
the function on the action variables is close to monotone;

2.

for
\boldsymbol{f}\sim{\cal D}_{\mathrm{no}}
the function on the action variables is far from monotone.

Roughly speaking, the analysis in
[
PRW22
]
shows that unless the algorithm queries two points such that both these points (a) have the same setting of the control variables, and (b) the control variables are balanced, the algorithm cannot distinguish between
\boldsymbol{f}\sim{\cal D}_{\mathrm{yes}}
and
\boldsymbol{f}\sim{\cal D}_{\mathrm{no}}
. As the control and action variables are partitioned at random, it turns out that satisfying both (a) and (b) is not possible for a non-adaptive algorithm unless the algorithm makes
2^{\Omega(\sqrt{n})}
many queries. In particular,
[
PRW22
]
shows that distinguishing between functions which are
c_{1}/\sqrt{n}
-close to monotone versus
c_{2}/\sqrt{n}
-far from monotone (where
c_{2}>c_{1}>0
) cannot be done with
2^{o(\sqrt{n})}
queries.

The main modification in
[
CDL+24
]
vis-a-vis
[
PRW22
]
is the following: one can think of the balanced setting of the control variables in the construction described above as the “minimal satisfying assignments” of the Majority function. In
[
CDL+24
]
, the Majority function is replaced by Talagrand’s random monotone DNF
[
Tal96
]
, a well-studied function in Boolean function analysis and related areas
[
MO03
,
OW07
]
.
The specific properties of Talagrand’s monotone DNF allows
[
CDL+24
]
to obtain a
2^{n^{1/4}}
query lower bound for non-adaptive testers where the functions in
{\cal D}_{\mathrm{yes}}
are
c_{1}
-close to monotone and functions in
{\cal D}_{\mathrm{no}}
are
c_{2}
-far from monotone, where
c_{2}>c_{1}
are positive constants.

For Theorem
2
, the goal is to obtain lower bounds for tolerant convexity testing rather than monotonicity testing. Towards that goal, let us assume that the ambient space is
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. We choose a random
n
-dimensional subspace
\boldsymbol{C}
and think of it as the
control subspace
, and we view its one-dimensional orthogonal complement as the
action subspace

\boldsymbol{A}
(analogous to the notion of control and action variables in
[
PRW22
,
CDL+24
]
).
8
8

8

We remark that in the one-sided adaptive lower bound described above, it would not have been possible to use a one-dimensional action subspace because an adaptive algorithm would be able to detect that “global structure,” which is shared across all the
\boldsymbol{U}_{i}
’s; this is why the dimension of the action subspace
\boldsymbol{A}
was
n
in the earlier construction, and there was a different random “action direction”
\boldsymbol{v}^{j}
from
\boldsymbol{A}
for each
j\in[N]
in the earlier construction.
We embed the Nazarov body
\boldsymbol{B}
(described earlier) in the control subspace. We define the
{\cal D}_{\mathrm{yes}}
and
{\cal D}_{\mathrm{no}}
distributions in analogy with
[
CDL+24
]
, roughly as follows: for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
,

1.

If the projection
x_{C}
does not lie in the uniquely violated set of
\boldsymbol{B}
, then
\boldsymbol{f}(x)
is defined the same way for
\boldsymbol{f}\sim{\cal D}_{\mathrm{yes}}
and
\boldsymbol{f}\sim{\cal D}_{\mathrm{no}}
;

2.

If the projection
x_{C}
lies in the uniquely violated set, then
\boldsymbol{f}(x)
is set differently for
\boldsymbol{f}\sim{\cal D}_{\mathrm{yes}}
and
\boldsymbol{f}\sim{\cal D}_{\mathrm{no}}
(depending on the projection
x_{\boldsymbol{A}}
to the action subspace). In particular, for
\boldsymbol{f}\sim{\cal D}_{\mathrm{yes}}
,
\boldsymbol{f}
is defined in such a way that
\boldsymbol{f}^{-1}(1)
is close to a convex set, and for
\boldsymbol{f}\sim{\cal D}_{\mathrm{no}}
,
\boldsymbol{f}
is defined in such a way that
\boldsymbol{f}^{-1}(1)
is far from every convex set.
This crucially uses the fact that the Gaussian volume of
\boldsymbol{U}
is “large” compared to the
Gaussian volume of the set
\mathrm{Ball}(\sqrt{n})\setminus\boldsymbol{B}
, as mentioned in our earlier discussion of the Nazarov body.

At a high level, the indistinguishability argument showing that
q=2^{\Omega(n^{1/4})}
non-adaptive queries are required to distinguish
\boldsymbol{f}\sim{\cal D}_{\mathrm{yes}}
from
\boldsymbol{f}\sim{\cal D}_{\mathrm{no}}
is a case analysis based on the distance between any given pair of query vectors
x
and
y
(see
Lemma

33
and
Lemma

34
), combined with a union bound over all
{q\choose 2}
possible pairs of query vectors. Roughly speaking, if
\|x-y\|
is small, then the way that
\boldsymbol{f}
depends on the projection to the action subspace makes it very unlikely to reveal a difference between
\boldsymbol{f}\sim{\cal D}_{\mathrm{yes}}
and
\boldsymbol{f}\sim{\cal D}_{\mathrm{no}}
. On the other hand, if
\|x-y\|
is large, then it is very unlikely for
x
and
y
to lie in the same set
\boldsymbol{U}_{i}
, which must be the case for the pair
x,y
to reveal a difference between
\boldsymbol{f}\sim{\cal D}_{\mathrm{yes}}
and
\boldsymbol{f}\sim{\cal D}_{\mathrm{no}}
.
There are many technical issues and geometric arguments required to carry out this rough plan, but when all the dust settles the argument gives a
2^{\Omega(n^{1/4})}
lower bound for tolerant convexity testing.

1.2.4
Two-Sided Non-Adaptive Bound

Our approach to prove Theorem
3
is inspired by the lower bounds of
[
CDST15
]
on non-adaptive monotonicity testing.
As in most property testing lower bounds for non-adaptive algorithms, the high-level approach is to use Yao’s principle; we follow
[
CDST15
]
in that we use a suitable high-dimensional central limit theorem as the key technical ingredient for establishing indistinguishability between the yes- and no- distributions. In
[
CDST15
]
both the yes- and no- functions are linear threshold functions over
\{-1,+1\}^{n}
, but since any linear threshold function is trivially a convex set, the
[
CDST15
]
construction cannot be directly used to prove a convexity testing lower bound. Instead, in order to ensure that our no- functions are both indistinguishable from the yes- functions and are far from every convex set, we work with
degree-2 polynomial threshold functions (PTFs)
over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
rather than linear threshold functions over
\{-1,+1\}^{n}
. At a high level, degree-2 PTFs of the form
\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}x_{i}^{2}
where each
\lambda_{i}
is positive (note that any such PTF is a convex set) play the “yes-function” role that monotone LTFs play in the
[
CDST15
]
argument, and degree-2 PTFs of the form
\sum_{i}\lambda^{\prime}_{i}x_{i}^{2}
where a constant fraction of the
\lambda^{\prime}_{i}
’s are negative play the “no-function” role that far-from-monotone LTFs play in the
[
CDST15
]
argument. We show that having a constant fraction of the
\lambda^{\prime}_{i}
’s be negative is sufficient, in the context of our construction, to ensure that no-functions are far from convex, and we show that the multi-dimensional central limit theorem used in
[
CDST15
]
can be adapted to our context to establish indistinguishability and thereby prove the desired lower bound.

We recall the following standard tail bound on Gaussian random variables:

Proposition 5

(Theorem 1.2.6 of
[

[

Let

Then for all

\varphi(r)\left({\frac{1}{r}}-{\frac{1}{r^{3}}}\right)\leq 1-\Phi(r)\leq\varphi(r)\left({\frac{1}{r}}-{\frac{1}{r^{3}}}+{\frac{3}{r^{5}}}\right)

2.3
Property Testing and Tolerant Property Testing

Let
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{conv}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{conv}}(n)
denote the class of convex subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, i.e.

\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{conv}}=\big\{L\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}L~\text{is convex}\big\}.

Given a set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we define its
distance to convexity
as

\mathrm{dist}(K,\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{conv}})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\inf_{L\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{conv}}}\mathrm{Vol}(K\,\triangle\,L)

where
K\,\triangle\,L=(K\setminus L)\cup(L\setminus K)
denotes the symmetric difference of
K
and
L
. In particular, we will say that
K
is
\varepsilon
-close to (resp.
\varepsilon
-far from) a convex set if
\mathrm{dist}(K,\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{conv}})\leq\varepsilon
(resp.
\geq\varepsilon
).

Definition 7

(Property testers and tolerant property testers)
.

Let
\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}\in[0,0.5]
with
\varepsilon_{1}<\varepsilon_{2}
. An algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is an
\varepsilon
-tester
for convexity if, given black-box query access to an unknown set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, it has the following performance guarantee:

•

If
K
is convex, then
\mathcal{A}
outputs “accept” with probability at least
2/3
;

•

If
\mathrm{dist}(K,\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{conv}})\geq\varepsilon
, then
\mathcal{A}
outputs “reject” with probability at least
2/3
.

An algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is an
(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2})
-tolerant tester
(or simply an
(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2})
-tester
) for convexity if it has the following performance guarantee:

•

If
\mathrm{dist}(K,\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{conv}})\leq\varepsilon_{1}
, then
\mathcal{A}
outputs “accept” with probability at least
2/3
;

•

If
\mathrm{dist}(K,\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{conv}})\geq\varepsilon_{2}
, then
\mathcal{A}
outputs “reject” with probability at least
2/3
.

In particular, note that every
\varepsilon
-tester is a
(0,\varepsilon)
-tolerant tester.

Our query-complexity lower bounds for non-adaptive property testing algorithms are obtained via Yao’s minimax principle
[
Yao77
]
, which we recall below.
(We remind the reader that an algorithm for the problem of
(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2})
-tolerant testing is correct on an input function
f
provided that it outputs “yes” if
f
is
\varepsilon_{1}
-close to the property and outputs “no” if
f
is
\varepsilon_{2}
-far from the property; if the distance to the property is between
\varepsilon_{1}
and
\varepsilon_{2}
then the algorithm is correct regardless of what it outputs.)

Theorem 8

(Yao’s principle)
.

To prove an
\Omega(q)
-query lower bound on the worst-case query complexity of any non-adaptive randomized testing algorithm, it suffices to give a distribution
\mathcal{D}
on instances
such that for any
q
-query non-adaptive
deterministic
algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, we have

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{f}\sim\mathcal{D}}\big[\mathcal{A}\text{ is correct on }\boldsymbol{f}\big]\leq c.

where
0\leq c<1
is a universal constant.

(1)

2.

(3)

4
One-Sided Adaptive Lower Bound

For this section, it will be most convenient for us to work over
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
. Let us restate Theorem
1
in this setting:

Theorem 16

(One-sided adaptive lower bound, restated)
.

For some absolute constant
\varepsilon>0
, any one-sided
\varepsilon
-tester for convexity over
N(0,I_{2n})
(which may be adaptive) must use
n^{\Omega(1)}
queries.

At a high level, the proof of Theorem

works by (1) first defining a distribution
{\cal D}_{\mathrm{no}}
of “no-functions” (Boolean-valued functions over
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
, or equivalently, subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
), and showing that an
\Omega(1)
fraction of draws from
{\cal D}_{\mathrm{no}}
yield sets which are
\Omega(1)
-far from convex; and (2) then arguing that for a suitable absolute constant
c>0
, any
n^{c}
-query algorithm (even an adaptive one) has only an
o(1)
probability of querying a set of points whose labels are inconsistent with every convex set in
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
.
In the next subsection we describe the distribution
{\cal D}_{\mathrm{no}}
.

4.1
The distribution

of far-from-convex sets

4.1.1
Setup

We will see that every function

To describe how

The “control subspace,” the “action subspace,” and the Nazarov body.

For this choice of

We take

For a fixed setting of the control subspace

(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})_{j\in S_{2}\cup\cdots\cup S_{\ell^{\prime}}}
is
good
.
Fix any good outcome
(x_{j})_{j\in S_{2}\cup\cdots\cup S_{\ell^{\prime}}}
of the last
n-(p_{1}n+c^{\prime}_{1}\sqrt{n})
coordinates of
\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})
, and let
A^{\prime}\in[-A,A]
be the value such that the LHS of
Equation

51
is equal to
{\left(\sum_{i=2}^{\ell^{\prime}}d_{i}p_{i}n\right)}+A^{\prime}\sqrt{n}.

Recalling
Equation

47
, for this good outcome of the last
n-(p_{1}n+c^{\prime}_{1}\sqrt{n})
coordinates, the set (
49
) (viewed as an indicator function of coordinates
1,\dots,p_{1}n+c^{\prime}_{1}\sqrt{n}
) becomes

\mathbf{1}{\left[\sum_{j=1}^{p_{1}n+c^{\prime}_{1}\sqrt{n}}d_{1}x_{j}^{2}\leq p_{1}d_{1}n-A^{\prime}\sqrt{n}\right]}.

(52)

Recalling that
d_{1}<0
, this is equivalent to

\mathbf{1}{\left[\sum_{j=1}^{p_{1}n+c^{\prime}_{1}\sqrt{n}}x_{j}^{2}\geq p_{1}n-(A^{\prime}/d_{1})\sqrt{n}\right]}.

(53)

Let
q
denote the probability that (
53
) holds for independent standard Gaussians
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{p_{1}n+c^{\prime}_{1}\sqrt{n}}
; the Berry-Esseen theorem implies that
q
is a constant in
(0,1)
which is bounded away from both 0 and 1.
By the radial symmetry of the
N(0,1)^{p_{1}n+c^{\prime}_{1}\sqrt{n}}
distribution, it follows that the subset of
\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}n+c^{\prime}_{1}\sqrt{n}}
whose indicator function is given by
Equation

53
is
\Omega(1)
-far from convex, because it is
\Omega(1)
-far from convex on a “line by line” basis. In more detail, for each unit vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}n+c^{\prime}_{1}\sqrt{n}}
, the function (
53
) labels points on the corresponding line
\{tv\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}t\in\mathbb{R}\}
as follows:

(i)

if
|t|\geq\sqrt{p_{1}n-(A^{\prime}/d_{1})\sqrt{n}}
then (
53
) outputs 1 on
tv
;

(ii)

if
|t|>\sqrt{p_{1}n-(A^{\prime}/d_{1})\sqrt{n}}
then (
53
) outputs 0 on
tv
.

Since this labeling corresponds to the complement of an interval, and since both (i) and (ii) have constant probability as explained above, the distance to convexity is
\Omega(1)
, and the proof of
Lemma

45
is complete.
∎

6.3
Proof of Theorem
3

As is usual for a non-adaptive lower bound, we use Yao’s principle.
Let
\cal{X}
be a
q\times n
query matrix, so the
i
-th row
{\cal X}_{i\ast}=({\cal X}_{i1},\dots,{\cal X}_{in})
is a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
corresponding to the
i
-th query made by some deterministic algorithm.
We will argue that the behavior of such a deterministic algorithm will be almost the same on a target function
\boldsymbol{K}\sim{\cal D}_{\mathrm{yes}}
and on a target function
\boldsymbol{K}\sim{\cal D}_{\mathrm{no}}
.

First, since our analysis will only consider target functions drawn from
{\cal D}_{\mathrm{yes}}
and
{\cal D}_{\mathrm{no}}
, and any draw from either of these distributions always involves clipping (the
\mathrm{clip}_{C}
component of
Equations

44
and
45
), we may suppose without loss of generality that each query vector
{\cal X}_{i\ast}
has
\|{\cal X}_{i\ast}\|\leq\sqrt{n}+C
, i.e. it satisfies
\mathrm{clip}_{C}({\cal X}_{i\ast})=1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{R}_{\mathrm{yes}}
be the
\{0,1\}^{q}
-valued random variable obtained by drawing
\boldsymbol{K}\sim{\cal D}_{\mathrm{yes}}
(recall that this corresponds to drawing
\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{a}^{(1)},\dots,\boldsymbol{u}_{n},\boldsymbol{a}^{(n)}
) and setting the
t
-th coordinate of
\boldsymbol{R}_{\mathrm{yes}}
to be

\boldsymbol{K}({\cal X}_{t*})=\mathbf{1}{\left[\boldsymbol{u}_{1}(\boldsymbol{a}^{(1)}\cdot{\cal X}_{t*})^{2}+\cdots+\boldsymbol{u}_{n}(\boldsymbol{a}^{(n)}\cdot{\cal X}_{t*})^{2}\leq\mu\right]}.

Similarly, let
\boldsymbol{R}_{\mathrm{no}}
be the
\{0,1\}^{q}
-valued random variable obtained by drawing
\boldsymbol{K}\sim{\cal D}_{\mathrm{no}}
(recall that this corresponds to drawing
\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{a}^{(1)},\dots,\boldsymbol{v}_{n},\boldsymbol{a}^{(n)}
) and setting the
t
-th coordinate of
\boldsymbol{R}_{\mathrm{no}}
to be

\boldsymbol{K}({\cal X}_{t*})=\mathbf{1}{\left[\boldsymbol{v}_{1}(\boldsymbol{a}^{(1)}\cdot{\cal X}_{t*})^{2}+\cdots+\boldsymbol{v}_{n}(\boldsymbol{a}^{(n)}\cdot{\cal X}_{t*})^{2}\leq\mu\right]}.

To prove a two-sided non-adaptive lower bound of
q
queries, it suffices to show that for the
\boldsymbol{R}_{\mathrm{yes}},\boldsymbol{R}_{\mathrm{no}}
defined above, we have
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\boldsymbol{R}_{\mathrm{yes}},\boldsymbol{R}_{\mathrm{no}})=o(1).

Let us write
\overline{a}
to denote
\overline{a}=(a^{(1)},\dots,a^{(n)})
, and let us write
\boldsymbol{R}_{\mathrm{yes}}^{\overline{a}}
to denote the random variable
\boldsymbol{R}_{\mathrm{yes}}
conditioned on having the outcome of
\boldsymbol{a}^{(1)},\dots,\boldsymbol{a}^{(n)}
come out equal to
\overline{a}
, and similarly for
\boldsymbol{R}_{\mathrm{no}}^{\overline{a}}.

Using the coupling interpretation of total variation distance and the natural coupling between
{\cal D}_{\mathrm{yes}}
and
{\cal D}_{\mathrm{no}}
, we have that

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\boldsymbol{R}_{\mathrm{yes}},\boldsymbol{R}_{\mathrm{no}})\leq\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\overline{\boldsymbol{a}}\sim\text{Haar}}{\left[d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\boldsymbol{R}_{\mathrm{yes}}^{\overline{\boldsymbol{a}}},\boldsymbol{R}_{\mathrm{no}}^{\overline{\boldsymbol{a}}})\right]},

(54)

so it suffices to upper bound the RHS of
Equation

54
by
o(1)
.

Let us say that an outcome
\overline{a}=(a^{(1)},\dots,a^{(n)})\in(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{n}
is
bad
if
there is a pair
(t,j)\in[q]\times[n]
such that
(a^{(j)}\cdot{\cal X}_{t*})^{2}\geq{\frac{10\ln n}{n}}
.
Recalling that each query vector
{\cal X}_{t*}
has norm at most
\sqrt{n}+C
and that each
\boldsymbol{a}^{(j)}
is a Haar random unit vector scaled by
1/\sqrt{n}
, it is easy to show that bad outcomes of
\overline{\boldsymbol{a}}
have very low probability:

Lemma 46
.

\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}[\overline{\boldsymbol{a}}
is bad
]=o(1)
.

Proof.

Fix some pair
(t,j)\in[q]\times[n]
and let
r\leq\sqrt{n}+C
be the norm of the query vector
{\cal X}_{t\ast}
.
The distribution of
\boldsymbol{a}^{(j)}\cdot{\cal X}_{t*}
is precisely the distribution of the first coordinate of a Haar random point drawn from the
n
-dimensional sphere of radius
r/\sqrt{n}
. Hence, writing
\boldsymbol{u}\sim\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
to denote a Haar random point from the
n
-dimensional unit sphere, we have

\displaystyle\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}{\left[(\boldsymbol{a}^{(j)}\cdot{\cal X}_{t*})^{2}\geq{\frac{10\ln n}{n}}\right]}

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{u}\sim\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}{\left[{\frac{|\boldsymbol{u}_{1}|r}{\sqrt{n}}}\geq{\frac{\sqrt{10\ln n}}{\sqrt{n}}}\right]}

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{u}\sim\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}{\left[\boldsymbol{u}_{1}\geq\frac{\sqrt{10\ln n}}{r}\right]}

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{u}\sim\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}{\left[\boldsymbol{u}_{1}\geq{\frac{3\sqrt{\ln n}}{\sqrt{n}}}\right]}

(using
r\leq\sqrt{n}+C
)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-(9/2)\ln n}=1/n^{9/2},

using a standard bound on spherical caps (see
Lemma

4
).
Since there are only
qn<n^{5/4}
many pairs
(t,j)\in[q]\times[n]
, a union bound concludes the proof.
∎

Fix
\overline{a}=(a^{(1)},\dots,a^{(n)})
to be any non-bad outcome of
\overline{\boldsymbol{a}}
.
Recalling
Equation

54
, by
Lemma

46
it suffices to show that
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\boldsymbol{R}_{\mathrm{yes}}^{\overline{\boldsymbol{a}}},\boldsymbol{R}_{\mathrm{no}}^{\overline{\boldsymbol{a}}})\leq o(1)
; this is our goal in the rest of the proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
be the random column vector whose
t
-th entry is

\boldsymbol{u}_{1}(a^{(1)}\cdot{\cal X}_{t*})^{2}+\cdots+\boldsymbol{u}_{n}(a^{(n)}\cdot{\cal X}_{t*})^{2}-\mu,

and let
\boldsymbol{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
be the random column vector whose
t
-th entry is

\boldsymbol{v}_{1}(a^{(1)}\cdot{\cal X}_{t*})^{2}+\cdots+\boldsymbol{v}_{n}(a^{(n)}\cdot{\cal X}_{t*})^{2}-\mu.

The response vector
\boldsymbol{R}_{\mathrm{yes}}^{\overline{a}}
is determined by the orthant of
\mathbb{R}^{q}
in which
\boldsymbol{S}
lies and the response vector
\boldsymbol{R}_{\mathrm{no}}^{\overline{a}}
is determined by the orthant of
\mathbb{R}^{q}
in which
\boldsymbol{T}
lies.
So to prove a
q
-query monotonicity testing lower bound for non-adaptive algorithms,
it suffices to upper bound

d_{\mathrm{UO}}(\boldsymbol{S},\boldsymbol{T})\leq o(1),

(55)

where
d_{\mathrm{UO}}
is the “union-of-orthants” distance:

d_{\mathrm{UO}}(\boldsymbol{S},\boldsymbol{T})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\max\Big\{|\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}[\boldsymbol{S}\in\mathcal{O}]-\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}[\boldsymbol{T}\in\mathcal{O}]|\colon\text{$\mathcal{O}$ is a union of orthants in $\mathbb{R}^{q}$\Big\lx@text@rbrace}.

In what follows, we will show that

d_{\mathrm{UO}}(\boldsymbol{S},\boldsymbol{T})\leq o(1)
when
q=O(n^{1/4-c})
.
To this end, let
\mathcal{O}
denote a union of orthants such that

d_{\mathrm{UO}}(\boldsymbol{S},\boldsymbol{T})=\big|\hskip-0.85355pt\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}[\boldsymbol{S}\in\mathcal{O}]-\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}[\boldsymbol{T}\in\mathcal{O}]\hskip 0.28436pt\big|.

(56)

Following
[
Mos08
,
GOWZ10
,
CDST15
]
, we first use the Lindeberg replacement
method to bound

\big|\hskip-1.13791pt\operatorname{{\bf E}}[\Psi_{\mathcal{O}}(\boldsymbol{S})]-\operatorname{{\bf E}}[\Psi_{\mathcal{O}}(\boldsymbol{T})]\hskip 0.28436pt\big|,

(57)

and then apply
Proposition

39
to bound (
56
).

For all
i\in\{0,1\ldots,n\}
we introduce the
\mathbb{R}^{q}
-valued hybrid random variable
\boldsymbol{Q}^{(i)}
whose
t
-th coordinate is

(\boldsymbol{Q}^{(i)})_{t}=\sum_{j=1}^{i}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}(a^{(j)}\cdot{\cal X}_{t*})^{2}+\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}\boldsymbol{u}_{j}(a^{(j)}\cdot{\cal X}_{t*})^{2}.

Observe that
\boldsymbol{Q}^{(0)}=\boldsymbol{S}
and
\boldsymbol{Q}^{(n)}=\boldsymbol{T}
. Informally, we are considering a sequence of hybrid distributions between
\boldsymbol{S}
and
\boldsymbol{T}
obtained by swapping out each of the
\boldsymbol{u}
-summands for a corresponding
\boldsymbol{v}
-summand one by one. The main idea is to bound the difference in expectations

\big|\hskip-0.85355pt\operatorname{{\bf E}}[\Psi_{\mathcal{O}}(\boldsymbol{Q}^{(i-1)})]-\operatorname{{\bf E}}[\Psi_{\mathcal{O}}(\boldsymbol{Q}^{(i)})]\hskip 0.28436pt\big|\quad\text{for each $i$},

(58)

since summing (
58
) over all

gives an upper bound on

\big|\hskip-0.85355pt\operatorname{{\bf E}}[\Psi_{\mathcal{O}}(\boldsymbol{S})]-\operatorname{{\bf E}}[\Psi_{\mathcal{O}}(\boldsymbol{T})]\hskip 0.28436pt\big|=\big|\hskip-0.85355pt\operatorname{{\bf E}}[\Psi_{\mathcal{O}}(\boldsymbol{Q}^{(0)})]-\operatorname{{\bf E}}[\Psi_{\mathcal{O}}(\boldsymbol{Q}^{(n)})]\hskip 0.28436pt\big|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big|\hskip-0.85355pt\operatorname{{\bf E}}[\Psi_{\mathcal{O}}(\boldsymbol{Q}^{(i-1)})]-\operatorname{{\bf E}}[\Psi_{\mathcal{O}}(\boldsymbol{Q}^{(i)})]\hskip 0.28436pt\big|

using the triangle inequality.

To bound (
58
), we define the
\mathbb{R}^{q}
-valued random variable
\mathbf{R}_{-i}
whose
t
-th coordinate is

(\mathbf{R}_{-i})_{t}=\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}(a^{(j)}\cdot{\cal X}_{t*})^{2}+\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}\boldsymbol{u}_{j}(a^{(j)}\cdot{\cal X}_{t*})^{2}.

(59)

Writing

to denote the random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{q}

whose
t
-th coordinate is
\boldsymbol{v}_{i}(a^{(i)}\cdot{\cal X}_{t*})^{2}
and likewise for

, we have that

\big|\hskip-0.85355pt\operatorname{{\bf E}}[\Psi_{\mathcal{O}}(\boldsymbol{Q}^{(i-1)})]-\operatorname{{\bf E}}[\Psi_{\mathcal{O}}(\boldsymbol{Q}^{(i)})]\hskip 0.28436pt\big|=\big|\hskip-0.85355pt\operatorname{{\bf E}}[\Psi_{\mathcal{O}}(\mathbf{R}_{-i}+\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{v}_{i},a^{(i)}))]-\operatorname{{\bf E}}[\Psi_{\mathcal{O}}(\mathbf{R}_{-i}+\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{u}_{i},a^{(i)}))]\hskip 0.28436pt\big|.

Truncating the Taylor expansion of

at the

-th term
(Fact

), we get

\displaystyle\hskip-11.38092pt\operatorname{{\bf E}}\hskip-1.42271pt\big[\Psi_{\mathcal{O}}(\mathbf{R}_{-i}+\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{v}_{i},a^{(i)}))\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{|J|\leq\ell}\frac{1}{J!}\cdot\operatorname{{\bf E}}\left[\Psi_{\mathcal{O}}^{(J)}(\mathbf{R}_{-i})\cdot(\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{v}_{i},a^{(i)}))^{J}\right]

(60)

\displaystyle+\sum_{|J|=\ell+1}\frac{\ell+1}{J!}\cdot\operatorname{{\bf E}}\left[(1-\boldsymbol{\tau})^{\ell}\Psi_{\mathcal{O}}^{(J)}(\mathbf{R}_{-i}+\boldsymbol{\tau}\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{v}_{i},a^{(i)}))(\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{v}_{i},a^{(i)}))^{J}\right]

,
\boldsymbol{v}_{i}

and
\mathbf{R}_{-i}
).
Writing the analogous expression for

,
we observe that by

and
38

\sum_{|J|\leq\ell}\frac{1}{J!}\cdot\operatorname{{\bf E}}\left[\Psi_{\mathcal{O}}^{(J)}(\mathbf{R}_{-i})\cdot(\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{v}_{i},a^{(i)}))^{J}\right]=\sum_{|J|\leq\ell}\frac{1}{J!}\cdot\operatorname{{\bf E}}\left[\Psi_{\mathcal{O}}^{(J)}(\mathbf{R}_{-i})\cdot(\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{u}_{i},a^{(i)}))^{J}\right]

for each

Thus we may cancel

\big|\hskip-0.85355pt\operatorname{{\bf E}}[\Psi_{\mathcal{O}}(\boldsymbol{Q}^{(i-1)})]-\operatorname{{\bf E}}[\Psi_{\mathcal{O}}(\boldsymbol{Q}^{(i)})]\hskip 0.28436pt\big|\leq\sum_{|J|=\ell+1}\frac{\ell+1}{J!}\|\Psi_{\mathcal{O}}^{(J)}\|_{\infty}\left(\operatorname{{\bf E}}\big[|(\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{v}_{i},a^{(i)}))^{J}|\big]+\operatorname{{\bf E}}\big[|(\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{u}_{i},a^{(i)}))^{J}|\big]\right).

Observe that there are

many terms in this sum.
Recalling that each value of
(a^{(j)}\cdot\mathcal{X}_{t\ast})^{2}
is at most
{\frac{10\log n}{n}}
(because

that both
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{v}_{i}

37
and
38
),
and

,
we have that for any

\big|\hskip-0.85355pt\operatorname{{\bf E}}[\Psi_{\mathcal{O}}(\boldsymbol{Q}^{(i-1)})]-\operatorname{{\bf E}}[\Psi_{\mathcal{O}}(\boldsymbol{Q}^{(i)})]\hskip 0.28436pt\big|=O_{\ell}(1)\cdot\left(\frac{q}{\tau}\right)^{\ell+1}\cdot\left({\frac{10\log n}{n}}\right)^{(\ell+1)/2}.

(61)

Summing over all

\big|\hskip-0.85355pt\operatorname{{\bf E}}[\Psi_{\mathcal{O}}(\boldsymbol{S})]-\operatorname{{\bf E}}[\Psi_{\mathcal{O}}(\boldsymbol{T})]\hskip 0.28436pt\big|=O_{\ell}(1)\cdot\left(\frac{q}{\tau}\right)^{\ell+1}\cdot{\frac{(10\log n)^{(\ell+1)/2}}{n^{(\ell-1)/2}}}.

Since both
