Background

Acquired sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL) represents a growing global health burden with an increasing annual incidence; however, its underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood, and effective treatments are limited. Once considered an immune-privileged site because of the blood-labyrinth barrier (BLB), cochlea is now known to harbor diverse immune cell populations within its basilar membrane (BM), stria vascularis (SV), spiral ligament (SL), osseous spiral lamina (OSL), spiral ganglion neurons (SGNs), and spiral limbus (SLi), challenging this long-standing paradigm. Neuroinflammation, characterized by the pathological activation of cochlear resident macrophages and their dynamic crosstalk with the sensory epithelium, SGNs, and SV, has emerged as a central mechanism in SNHL pathophysiology. Orchestrated largely by macrophage subsets within distinct cochlear niches, this immune-mediated remodeling critically influences auditory function, positioning neuroinflammation as a pivotal interface between cochlear immunology and structural integrity [1–3].

Macrophages are the predominant cochlear immune cells [4]. Approximately 62% of these cells reside within neural compartments, underscoring the potential centrality of neuroinflammation in cochlear pathophysiology [5]. Macrophages in cochlea are categorized into tissue-resident and monocyte-derived infiltrating populations. Fate-mapping studies using Ccr2RFP/+ Cx3cr1GFP/+ dual-reporter mice to identify tissue-resident and infiltrated macrophages have shown that cochlear-resident macrophages (CoRMs) are pivotal mediators in response to acoustic trauma, ototoxic injury, and age-related degeneration [6–8]. Although they share a common developmental origin with central nervous system (CNS) microglia, CoRMs have evolved distinct phenotypic and functional attributes tailored to the unique microenvironment of the peripheral auditory system.

Recent advances in single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and spatial transcriptomics have revolutionized our understanding of CoRMs heterogeneity, revealing that CoRMs are not a homogeneous population but comprise specialized subsets with distinct spatial distributions and functions. CoRMs exhibit niche-specific phenotypic and transcriptional reprogramming dictated by spatially restricted microenvironments and ultimately acquire specific functions [9]. Such diversity underlies their context-dependent roles as sentinels that preserve cochlear integrity and facilitate repair or as saboteurs that propagate neuroinflammatory injury.

Despite these advances, the mechanisms governing the phenotypic fate of regionally distinct cochlear macrophage subsets remain unknown. This knowledge gap represents a major obstacle to the development of targeted and effective therapeutics for inflammation-induced SNHL. In this review, we synthesized the ontogeny, spatial distribution, and functional heterogeneity of CoRMs. We explored how specific subsets regulate tissue homeostasis, mediate damage response, and ultimately influence auditory function. Furthermore, we highlight innovative strategies aimed at modulating CoRMs activity to preserve hearing and suppress inflammation. By integrating insights from fundamental neuroimmunology and translational studies, we aimed to bridge the gap between current knowledge and therapeutic innovation. We conclude by proposing future directions involving spatial multi-omics, live imaging, and genetic models to dissect subset-specific functions and microenvironmental interactions, thereby advancing precision therapies for hearing loss.

Developmental ontogeny and maintenance of CoRMs

Cochlea contains heterogeneous populations of macrophages with distinct developmental origins and maintenance mechanisms that vary across the anatomical subregions. Research in murine models has established that the embryonic cochlea is populated by macrophages through two distinct ontogenetic pathways: Csf1r-dependent macrophages originating from the yolk sac (YS) and Csf1r-independent macrophages migrating from the fetal liver (FL) via systemic circulation [10].

The first wave originates from YS-derived progenitors as early as E7.5. This population is evidenced by Iba1+ macrophages residing in the periotic mesenchyme at E10.5. As development proceeds, Csf1 signaling facilitates the colonization of structures such as the SGNs and SL, a process largely complete by E17.5. In contrast, a second Csf1r-independent lineage arises from YS-derived erythromyeloid progenitors (EMPs) that migrate to the FL between E9.5 and E10.5. Within the FL, these EMPs differentiate into CD11b+ monocytic precursors, which subsequently enter the systemic circulation as fetal monocytes by E12.5. These cells ultimately migrate to peripheral tissues, including cochlea, where they establish residence by E14.5 [10–12] (Fig. 1).

During cochlear development, macrophage colonization follows a sequential and compartment-specific pattern. In the SGNs, YS–derived, Csf1r-dependent macrophages appear first at E10.5–E14.5 to establish a primitive macrophage pool. These are gradually joined by FL–derived, Csf1r-independent CD11b⁺ monocytes between E14.5 and E17.5, which mature into Iba1⁺ macrophages. Around birth (P0–P3), the SGNs macrophage density peaked, supported by in situ proliferation. With the onset of hearing at P14, bone marrow-derived Ms4a3⁺ monocytes infiltrate the SGNs and progressively replace the embryonic macrophages. By P60, Ms4a3⁺ cells constituted more than half of the resident population, indicating ongoing replenishment from circulating monocytes. A similar pattern occurs in the SL, where YS–derived macrophages initially seed the tissue, followed by the recruitment of FL–derived CD11b⁺ precursors that differentiate locally. After birth, SL macrophages display a strong proliferative capacity but are gradually replaced post-P14 by Ms4a3⁺ bone-marrow-derived cells, which dominate adulthood. In contrast, the SV exhibits a delayed and more static macrophage turnover, with embryonic stages showing few macrophages, which appear only after birth and are largely derived from YS and FL progenitors. Once the BLB is formed at P14, the infiltration of circulating Ms4a3⁺ monocytes becomes restricted, and embryonic macrophages persist as a long-lived, self-maintaining population through early adulthood. Collectively, these findings illustrate a developmental hand-off from embryonic (YS/FL) to bone marrow-derived macrophages in SGNs and SL, while the SV remains largely populated by stable embryonic macrophages with limited postnatal renewal [10, 13] (Fig. 2).

Phenotypic and molecular characterization of cochlea macrophages

Classical immunohistochemical and flow cytometric markers remain indispensable for deciphering the spatiotemporal dynamics of cochlear macrophages in health and disease. These markers can be broadly categorized into pan-macrophage identifiers, functional activation markers, recruitment-related markers, and signaling and interaction markers. This framework not only aids in distinguishing bone marrow-derived resident macrophages from peripherally recruited infiltrating populations, but also contextualizes their roles in cochlear homeostasis, injury responses, and pathogenesis of hearing loss.

Ionized calcium-binding adapter molecule 1 (Iba1) and F4/80 are widely used pan-macrophage markers. Iba1 is a Ca²⁺-binding cytosolic protein that associates with the actin cytoskeleton, drives membrane dynamics, and facilitates phagocytosis, with strong accumulation at podosomes during inflammatory activation [14–17]. F4/80 labels multiple mouse macrophage subsets (and some monocytes) and remains a standard identifier, although its mechanistic function has not been fully elucidated [18–21].

Functional markers capture the macrophage activation state. CD68 reflects lysosomal activity and phagocytosis, which are typically upregulated after SGNs or HCs injury [22]. CD163, which is associated with M2-like anti-inflammatory polarization, has been implicated in tissue repair and neuroprotection [23]. Galectin-3 (Lgals3) is increasingly recognized as a biomarker of inflammaging, and its upregulation correlates with age-related hearing loss (ARHL) and fibrotic remodeling [24].

Recruitment markers are critical tools for discriminating between infiltrating monocyte-derived macrophages. CD45 (high expression levels) [25, 26], integrin alpha M (CD11b) [27], and c-c chemokine receptor type 2 (CCR2) characterize peripherally recruited cells.

Signaling and interaction markers, such as the fractalkine receptor (CX3CR1), highlight the dialogue between macrophages and neurons. The CX3CL1-CX3CR1 axis is indispensable for synaptic maturation and repair; its deficiency exacerbates synaptic loss and delays recovery after noise trauma, underscoring the neuroprotective role of CoRMs [28–30] (Table 1).

Classical macrophage markers, functional mechanisms, and their relevance to hearing loss

Taken together, the systematic profiling of macrophage markers provides a powerful framework for decoding spatiotemporal dynamics of cochlear immune responses. These molecular signatures delineate the functional states across the surveillance–phagocytosis–repair continuum. Clinically, such profiles not only stratify pathological mechanisms, such as Lgals3-driven inflammaging in ARHL or CX3CR1 downregulation-associated synaptic vulnerability after acoustic trauma, but also inspire precision therapeutic strategies, ranging from targeted neutralization of proinflammatory mediators to gene-editing approaches for synaptic preservation in cochlea. However, reliance on a single marker can be misleading. Future approaches integrating multi-marker profiling, fate-mapping, and spatial transcriptomics will be essential to unravel the heterogeneity and functional plasticity of macrophage subsets in auditory disorders.

Highly heterogeneous spatiotemporal distribution, functional heterogeneity, and transcriptional characteristics of CoRMs across anatomical niches

Recent advancements in scRNA-seq have further enhanced our understanding of CoRMs, suggesting their remarkable heterogeneity across the anatomical regions of cochlea [31, 32]. This diversity is reflected in variations in cellular origin, spatial distribution, responses to stimuli, antigenic profiles, secretory molecules and functional roles [33]. CoRMs are involved in inflammation and more complex functions, such as orchestrating cochlear organogenesis, maintaining auditory homeostasis, and regulating processes such as synaptic refinement and neural metabolic support.

These findings have identified at least three CoRM subpopulations in cochlea, each occupying unique anatomical regions and exhibiting specialized transcriptional and functional profiles. The spatiotemporal diversity of CoRMs, shaped by ontogeny and microenvironmental factors, illustrates their multifaceted roles in cochlear physiology and pathology.

Identification and classification of CoRM subpopulations

Cochlea microglia-like macrophages (coMGLs)

Identified by markers such as Tmem119, P2ry12, and Hexb, resemble CNS microglia and are primarily localized to highly vascularized regions such as the SV and SGNs. These cells express transcription factors such as ​Sall1, Cebpa, and Irf8, which are core microglial identity genes, alongside ​Zfhx3 and Sox4, which are associated with angiogenesis, suggesting their roles in vascular regulation and synaptic pruning. CoMGLs adopt a ramified morphology, which is indicative of a surveillance state and is thought to play a key role in monitoring cochlear health and maintaining cochlear structural integrity.

Cochlear sciatic nerve-like macrophages (coMacs)

Were identified by the presence of CD206. They are mainly located in the BM and SLi, close to the synapses between HCs and SGNs, indicating a potential key role in synapse pruning. CoMacs can be classified into two major subsets: CD206⁺CD163⁻ and CD206⁺CD163⁺. The CD206+CD163− coMac subset is notable for its high expression of major histocompatibility complex class II (MHC II) molecules and antigen-presentation genes (e.g., Cd74), alongside key proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF and interleukin-6 (IL-6). This subset transcriptionally aligns with antigen-presenting macrophages, characterized by the high expression of CD74 and CD14 [34]. Another subset of coMacs, CD206+CD163+ coMacs, had a high concentration of genes involved in fluid shear stress and bone metabolism, ​with expressed markers including Cd38, Trf, and P2rx7. They also express Etv1 and Cepd, which are transcription factors associated with tissue repair and anti-inflammatory functions. In addition, they uniquely expressed genes involved in iron metabolism, such as Slc40a1, and metabolic regulation, such as Dock4. This M2-like phenotype suggests their involvement in inflammation resolution and tissue remodeling during the later stages of injury. Their role in iron and bone metabolism is consistent with the bony structural environment of cochlea. The abundance of this subset increases with age, reflecting its ongoing contribution to cochlear tissue maintenance and repair under both physiological and pathological conditions [33].

Spatial distribution and functional implications​

The functional diversity of CoRMs is shaped by their regional localization within cochlea, with each subset fulfilling distinct roles dictated by its anatomical niche (Fig. 3). Spatial transcriptomic technologies have revealed the extensive heterogeneity and highly organized spatial distribution of CoRMs, establishing a new framework for understanding them [31, 35]. Although multiple CoRM subpopulations coexist within the same anatomical region, each discrete microniche is defined by a functionally dominant macrophage subset that directs the local tissue homeostasis. This structured organization highlights the precision with which local microenvironments shape immune identity and functional specialization. CoRMs reside in the immediate proximity of sensory elements, enabling them to detect pathological changes and monitor immune activity. As cochlea functions as a mechano-electrical transduction organ, its normal physiology relies heavily on the maintenance of ionic balance and electrochemical gradients.

The sensory BM is predominantly populated by ​CD206+ macrophages (both CD163− and CD163+), which localize near the HC–neuron synapse and exhibit complement and synaptic pruning pathways, implying a role in synaptic integrity and sensory microenvironment surveillance. CoRMs in the BM are distributed in two distinct groups: Corti’s side and the tympanic side. Macrophages on the Corti’s side undergo developmental regression as the sensory epithelium matures, leading to their absence in the organ of Corti under steady-state conditions in mice [5, 36]. In contrast, studies on human temporal bones have identified morphologically distinct Iba1+ macrophages within the organ of Corti [37, 38]. Analyses using super-resolution structured illumination microscopy [39] and transmission electron microscopy have revealed cellular processes that penetrate the BM [40]. These macrophages, located on the perilymphatic side of the BM and in close proximity to sensory cells, are positioned within dual extracellular environments—contacting the BM/mesothelial cells on one side and the perilymph on the other—enabling them to monitor the immune status across the organ of Corti and the perilymphatic space. The observed discrepancies likely arise from differences in sample age, species, methodology, and technical approaches used (Fig. 3A). Within the SGNs, coMGLs constitute the dominant population; their microglial markers (Tmem119+, P2ry12+) and enrichment in neuro-supportive pathways indicate a primary role in preserving spiral ganglion neuron viability and synaptic connectivity (Fig. 3B).

SV houses the BLB, a specialized endothelial-glial interface that tightly regulates the transport of ions and fluids from the systemic circulation into the endolymph. This strict barrier function is fundamental for establishing the high potassium concentration gradient required to generate and maintain the endocochlear potential (EP), the driving force for hair cell mechanotransduction [41, 42]. The BLB comprises endothelial cells, pericytes, basement membrane, and perivascular macrophage-melanocytes (PVM/Ms), which envelop the capillaries to maintain their structural and functional integrity. PVM/Ms are crucial for preserving the EP, as their depletion in mice leads to a significant reduction in potential and subsequent hearing loss [41]. Recent transcriptomic studies have revealed that the SV is dominated by a subset defined as coMGLs, which express genes related to vascular homeostasis (F11r, Pdgfb). While classically termed PVM/Ms, these cells likely overlap significantly with the coMGL signature. They facilitate potassium (K+) recycling via Cx43-mediated endothelial coupling, thereby stabilizing the EP, contributing to K + recycling, acting as core regulators of cochlear fluid and ion balance [43] and supporting auditory mechanotransduction. Consequently, macrophage dysfunction disrupts BLB permeability, causing ionic imbalance and hearing loss, underscoring the vital role in maintaining the EP and auditory thresholds [44]. (Fig. 3C) The adjacent SL is dominated by CD206+CD163+ coMacs that exhibit a high expression of lymphatic vessel endothelial hyaluronan receptor 1 (LYVE1) and express genes related to bone metabolism, such as P2rx7, suggesting adaptation to mechanical stress and matrix remodeling (Fig. 3D).

Beyond homeostasis, CoRMs actively modulate the fidelity of auditory signal transduction through immune-neuro interface interactions. The intersection of immune features with core auditory processes is most evident at the ribbon synapses. Under physiological conditions, glutamate acts as the primary afferent neurotransmitter orchestrating communication between inner hair cells and SGN terminals [45] Consequently, AMPA receptors are abundantly expressed and are indispensable for mediating rapid synaptic transmission within cochlea [46].

However, under stress, activated macrophages release proinflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α, which disrupt the delicate glutamate cycle essential for sound encoding [47]. Specifically, TNF-α signaling appears to compromise postsynaptic integrity by promoting excitotoxicity. While direct intracochlear evidence is still evolving, parallels drawn from retinal models postulate that TNF-α drives the downregulation of the calcium-impermeable GluA2 subunit of AMPA receptors [48]. Since the regulated surface expression of these receptors is fundamental for limiting calcium influx and ensuring neuronal survival [49], such cytokine-mediated remodeling would plausibly render SGNs susceptible to calcium overload. This molecular shift creates a toxic environment that leads to the swelling of postsynaptic terminals and the uncoupling of ribbon synapses [47, 50]. Ultimately, these cellular deviations reduce the synchrony of neural firing, manifesting physiologically as a diminished amplitude of ABR Wave I or CAP [47].

CoRMs activity in cochlea and temporal coordination of cytokine signaling

Under injury conditions, such as noise exposure or ototoxicity, cochlear macrophages exhibit a complex functional duality, acting as a double-edged sword. They function as critical facilitators of repair, promoting synaptic recovery, supporting SGNs survival, and ensuring afferent signaling fidelity. Conversely, their activation drives inflammatory cascades that can exacerbate tissue damage. This dichotomy implies that macrophages undergo profound spatiotemporal reprogramming dictated by specific pathological contexts. To elucidate this complexity, we first categorized these responses based on specific etiologies, highlighting how distinct macrophage subsets and molecular pathways operate within their respective spatiotemporal niches (Fig. 4).

CoRMs in noise-induced hearing loss

In NIHL, the primary insult involves mechanical stress and excessive glutamate release at the ribbon synapses, leading to excitotoxicity and acute synaptopathy [28]. This is often followed by oxidative stress-mediated apoptosis of outer Hair Cells (OHCs), particularly in the basal turn. The immune response to this injury exhibits a distinct temporal duality. During the acute phase (0–24 h), noise-associated DAMPs trigger the rapid activation of resident macrophages (likely CD206+CD163− coMacs) in the lateral wall and SGNs. Early in the pathology, the upregulation of proinflammatory cytokines like TNF-α and IL-1β exacerbates synaptic swelling and excitotoxicity, acting as “saboteurs” [51, 52]. However, as the pathology progresses to the repair phase (3–7 days), a critical functional shift occurs. The CX3CL1-CX3CR1 signaling axis recruits coMGLs to the damaged synaptic poles. These cells then act as “sentinels” by performing synaptic pruning to remove damaged terminals, a process essential for spontaneous functional recovery [28, 53, 54]. Subsequently, during the resolution phase, a transition towards a reparative phenotype (likely CD206+CD163+ coMacs) is essential. CoRMs in drug-induced hearing loss

DIHL, particularly induced by cisplatin, is driven by the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and mitochondrial dysfunction, predominantly damaging the OHCs and SV. This pathology unfolds as a dynamic cascade characterized by distinct temporal phases of macrophage activation. In the Initiation Phase (0–24 h), the primary event is cisplatin accumulation. Sung et al. revealed that resident PVMs in the SV act as critical “gatekeepers” during this window, maintaining high BLB permeability to facilitate cisplatin influx [7]. The Amplification Phase (24–72 h) is marked by a “cytokine storm” in the organ of Corti. aligning spatially with the onset of OHC apoptosis and synaptic rupture [56]. In the Late Degeneration Phase (> 3 days), the sustained inflammatory output from activated macrophages contributes to secondary neurodegeneration [7]. Notably, depleting these macrophages significantly preserves SGNs density, confirming their role as drivers of secondary neuronal loss. Thus, the macrophage response in DIHL evolves from facilitating drug entry to driving acute inflammation, and finally to perpetuating chronic neurodegeneration (Fig. 5B).

CoRMs in age-related hearing loss

ARHL is characterized by “inflammaging”—a chronic, low-grade sterile inflammation. This process involves SV atrophy and fibrotic remodeling of the SL, driven by the accumulation of damage-associated molecular patterns (e.g., mtDNA) and sustained TLR9 signaling, which collectively impair EP maintenance and metabolic support for neurons [57]. In contrast to acute injuries, biological aging is characterized by “inflammaging”—a chronic, low-grade sterile inflammation driven by metabolic decline [58]. Anatomically, pathological changes are prominent in connective tissues such as the SL and SLi [57]. In these niches, there is a conspicuous expansion of CD206⁺CD163⁻ coMacs. These cells constitutively express high levels of MHC-II and proinflammatory mediators, such as TNF-α and IL-6, likely in response to TLR9 sensing of mitochondrial DNA from aging cells. Unlike the transient responses observed in noise trauma, this sustained activation contributes to fibrotic remodeling and gradual degeneration of cochlear structures [33].

CoRMs in infection-induced hearing loss (e.g., chronic suppurative otitis media)

Chronic Suppurative Otitis Media (CSOM) is a leading cause of permanent hearing loss, driven not by direct bacterial invasion but by a maladaptive host immune response. Pathogens in the middle ear trigger a cascading inflammatory response that permeates the inner ear. In this context, cochlear macrophages in the BM, SL and SV are the primary effectors. These resident macrophages sense bacterial products (e.g., peptidoglycans) and activate the NLRP3 inflammasome. This activation leads to the robust secretion of IL-1β, which acts as a central “saboteur.” Elevated IL-1β levels not only recruit further immune cells but also directly compromise OHC viability. Unlike in noise trauma, where macrophages may aid repair, in CSOM, sustained NLRP3-mediated inflammation is the primary driver of permanent SNHL. Therapeutic inhibition of NLRP3 (e.g., via MCC950) or IL-1β (via Anakinra) has been shown to effectively preserve OHCs, confirming the detrimental role of this macrophage-driven pathway [1].

Together, these findings indicate that the pathological activation of CoRMs is not uniform but follows etiology-specific patterns that determine which macrophage subsets are predominantly engaged, which activation trajectories (sentinel vs. saboteur) are favored, and which therapeutic strategies may be most effective in each condition. Although current studies rarely distinguish coMGLs and coMacs explicitly—largely because the nomenclature is new—integrating regional injury patterns with known macrophage phenotypes enables the construction of a mechanistic working model. Whether in noise trauma or ototoxicity, CoRMs exhibit marked temporal heterogeneity, progressing through distinct phases of recruitment, activation, cytokine release, and resolution. Understanding these time-dependent shifts is crucial because they reveal specific therapeutic windows in which interventions can be precisely timed to target pathogenic subsets while preserving reparative functions. To visualize this common temporal framework and guide time-specific treatments, we integrated the findings from key studies to construct a unified timeline focusing exclusively on the dynamic responses of cochlear resident macrophages (Fig. 5).

The functional repertoire of cochlear resident macrophages (CoRMs) is inherently heterogeneous, governed by temporal dynamics, spatial localization, and pathological context. Current evidence reveals a nuanced dichotomy: while global CoRM depletion via CSF1R inhibition does not alter baseline auditory thresholds [7], this finding masks their critical roles in specific niches, such as maintaining ionic homeostasis in the SV [40, 42]. This reliance on blunt “global depletion” paradigms precipitates contradictory findings. For instance, macrophages appear redundant during sterile developmental remodeling [63]yet are obligatory for synaptic repair in noise-induced hearing loss [53]. Similarly, in cisplatin ototoxicity, global depletion protects against hair cell loss [7], whereas specific ablation of strial macrophages compromises the BLB [42]. These paradoxes underscore major methodological limitations: current systemic knockout models lack spatiotemporal specificity, fail to target single macrophage subsets, and often conflate distinct hearing loss etiologies. Resolving this requires the development of highly specific inducible Cre-LoxP systems to dissect the roles of single macrophage subpopulations within defined time windows and specific injury models. Such precision is essential for advancing personalized medicine, enabling the design of therapies tailored to individual susceptibility and specific hearing loss types while minimizing off-target disruption.

Dual way signaling machinery: mechanisms and modulators

Although single-cell studies have mapped the heterogeneity of CoRMs, revealing subsets with dichotomous potential to either propagate injury or promote repair, a critical question remains: what signaling mechanisms dictate these divergent functional fates?​​

​The following sections delve into two pivotal signaling pathways in cochlear immunology: CX3CL1-CX3CR1 and TLR/NLRP3. We propose a unifying framework in which the functional specialization of distinct CoRM subsets is orchestrated through their differential engagement with these pathways. Specifically, subsets with neuroprotective properties, such as coMGLs, appear to rely heavily on homeostatic “dialogue” signals, such as CX3CL1-CX3CR1, to maintain their sentinel roles. In contrast, subsets predisposed to driving inflammation, such as certain CD206⁺CD163⁻ coMacs, are more readily activated by “danger” signals via the TLR/NLRP3 axis. Therefore, elucidating these pathways is not only key to understanding cochlear immune responses but is also central to deciphering the functional heterogeneity of resident macrophage subsets.​

Sentinels at the gate: CX3CL1-CX3CR1—a central regulator of macrophage recruitment and neuroprotection

CX3CL1, a chemokine encoded by the CX3CL1 gene, is highly expressed in neurons. In the mature cochlea, it is constitutively present in IHCs and SGNs, with increased levels after cochlear injury. In mice, the fractalkine receptor (CX3CR1) is found in different immune cells, such as monocytes and tissue macrophages, and is the only receptor for CX3CL1 [64]. The CX3CL1-CX3CR1 axis is central to macrophage–SGN interactions. CX3CL1 may enhance macrophage activity by interacting with CX3CR1 [65, 66].

Disruption of this signaling pathway, as observed in Cx3cr1 knockout models, significantly reduces macrophage recruitment to SGNs and exacerbates SGNs degeneration, underscoring its neuroprotective role [28, 66]. In NIHL, resident macrophages rapidly engage IHCs–SGNs synapses, and fractalkine signaling is required for effective post-injury synaptic maintenance and repair [53, 54, 67, 68]. In cisplatin-induced ototoxicity, disruption of fractalkine signaling exacerbates the loss of cochlear afferents and compromises HCs and SGNs survival [67, 69]. Fate-mapping studies have revealed that CX 3CR1⁺ macrophages display strikingly distinct spatial distributions and recruitment dynamics across the SGNs, OSL, and SL, with noise exposure eliciting region-dependent temporal responses [68]. However, this functional significance is not universal in cochlea. It does not significantly affect HC loss in chronic suppurative otitis media (CSOM) [70] In the vestibular utricle, genetic ablation of CX3CR1 does not impair macrophage entry into injured sensory epithelia, indicating that this axis is dispensable for macrophage recruitment in certain vestibular contexts [71]. Recent single-cell and functional studies have further extended this concept to the SV, where basal cells exhibit age-related overexpression of CX3CL1, which promotes CX3CR1⁺ macrophage recruitment and proinflammatory activation, positioning the SV as a key trigger zone for cochlear inflammaging [72]. Evidence from auditory physiology supports the functional consequences of macrophage modulation. Under baseline conditions, cochlear macrophage depletion does not significantly affect auditory thresholds, as shown by unaltered ABR and distortion product otoacoustic emissions (DPOAE) measurements in macrophage-ablated mice [7, 54]. However, upon acoustic challenge, macrophage loss markedly delays ABR recovery and reduces ribbon synapse repair [54], whereas excessive activation through the NLRP3–IL-1β axis exacerbates ABR threshold shifts [6, 73]. Pharmacological inhibition of TLR4 (e.g., TAK-242) restores synaptic integrity and auditory sensitivity [6], linking immune signaling directly to measurable auditory outcomes.

The saboteur vortex: Toll-Like Receptor 4 (TLR4) and Toll-Like Receptor 9 (TLR9) signaling pathways mechanisms of macrophage activation

Macrophages, as central components of innate immunity, sense both exogenous pathogens and endogenous danger signals through pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) [74]. Exogenous pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) include conserved microbial molecules such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS), viral nucleic acids, and structural proteins, whereas damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) are host-derived signals released from injured or necrotic cells, including mtDNA, adenosine triphosphate (ATP), ROS, and proinflammatory proteins [75, 76].Cellular receptors, such as Toll-like receptors (TLRs), NOD-like receptors (NLRs), and DNA sensors, act as key PRRs for both PAMPs and DAMPs, thereby orchestrating innate immune activation [77]. In cochlea, DAMPs generated by tissue injury are recognized by PRRs on resident macrophages, which in turn release proinflammatory cytokines and recruit additional immune cells [78]. Although essential for host defense, this response can exacerbate inflammation and contribute to cochlear pathology.

Among PRRs, TLR4 and TLR9 have been identified as pivotal regulators of the inflammatory signaling cascades that drive cochlear injury and progressive hearing loss.

TLR4: a canonical PRR in cochlear stress response

TLR4 is a well-characterized PRR that functions as the primary sensor of cochlear stress. It recognizes exogenous PAMPs, such as LPS from gram-negative bacteria, and endogenous DAMPs released from stressed or dying cells, such as the high-mobility group box 1. Upon activation, TLR4 initiates signaling through two downstream pathways: (1) myeloid differentiation primary response protein 88 (MyD88)-dependent pathway: TLR4 recruits the adaptor proteins MyD88, IRAK4, and TRAF6, leading to the activation of the IKK complex and nuclear translocation of nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB). This upregulates proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6) and chemokines (e.g., CCL2 and CX3CL1), thereby enhancing immune cell necroptosis. Necroptotic cells release DAMPs that are recognized by TLRs, further activating macrophages via the MyD88 pathway [79]. (2) TIR-domain-containing adapter-inducing interferon-β (TRIF)-dependent pathway: TLR4 also activates IRF3 through the adaptor protein TRIF, inducing the expression of type I interferons, such as interferon-beta (IFN-β), and chemokines, such as CXCL10, which enhance antiviral and chronic inflammatory responses.

TLR9: Linking sterile inflammation to inflammaging

Although traditionally associated with the recognition of unmethylated CpG motifs in bacterial and viral DNA, TLR9 has now been implicated in sterile inflammatory processes in cochlea. Although predominantly expressed in professional immune cells, such as B cells and dendritic cells, experiments have shown that a TLR9 agonist, CPG ODN, significantly enhances the infiltration of Iba-1+ macrophages into cochlea and stimulates their production of proinflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, suggesting that TLR9 may play an important role in the development of cochlea and that TLR9 may play a role in the activation of macrophages [80]. Further evidence from ARHL mouse models has revealed elevated TLR9 levels in cochlea, directly linking this receptor to age-associated inflammatory processes [81].

NLRP3 inflammasome: convergence point of cochlear inflammatory pathways

The NLRP3 inflammasome is a cytosolic multiprotein complex consisting of NLRP3, apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a CARD (ASC), and caspase-1. This complex converts upstream TLR activation into an amplified inflammatory cascade [82]. Its activation proceeds in two stages: priming via TLR4/NF-κB, which induces NLRP3 and pro-IL-1β expression [83, 84], and activation via DAMPs, such as extracellular ATP or ionic fluxes, leading to inflammasome assembly and caspase-1–mediated maturation of IL-1β and interleukin-18 (IL-18) [1, 85].

The involvement of the NLRP3 inflammasome in NIHL has been well documented. Acoustic trauma is a potent trigger of this pathway. Research on minipigs showed that acoustic injury elevated cochlear concentrations of NLRP3, IL-1β, IL-18, and active caspase-1 [86]. Similar results were observed in a mouse model of NIHL, in which NLRP3 activation was significantly increased in the noise-exposed cochlea. In this study, the IL-1 receptor antagonist anakinra and the NLRP3 inhibitor oridonin effectively attenuated NIHL [87]. Ototoxic drugs, such as aminoglycosides, can also cause hearing loss. In a mouse model of kanamycin- and furosemide-induced ototoxicity, activation of NLRP3 was observed in cochlea along with the loss of HCs, which was prevented by using the NLRP3 inhibitor Oridonin [88]. Cisplatin-induced ototoxicity also appears to depend on NLRP3 inflammasome activation [89]. In ARHL, the levels of DAMPs, such as ATP and ROS, increase in cochlea, potentially leading to sustained NLRP3 inflammasome activation and chronic inflammation. Transcriptomic analyses of the aged mouse cochlea have identified several differentially regulated genes, including inflammatory signaling molecules such as NF-κB, IL-1β, and caspase-1 [90].

TLR4, via MyD88–NF-κB signaling, provides a priming signal for inflammasome activation, whereas TLR9 promotes NLRP3 activity in aging models through NF-κB activation [91]. Thus, both TLR4 and TLR9 converge independently on NLRP3, creating redundant and robust proinflammatory pathways.

Although macrophages also possess reparative capacity mediated by the CX3CL1-CX3CR1 axis, the activation of TLR-NLRP3 appears to favor a destructive phenotype. A significant knowledge gap exists regarding the direct interaction between TLR signaling and the CX3CL1-CX3CR1 repair mechanism, which represents a crucial frontier for future therapeutic developments.

Compartmentalized signaling pathways underlie functional specialization of cochlear macrophage subsets and their therapeutic potential

The functional specialization of cochlear macrophage subsets is governed by a core set of evolutionarily conserved signaling pathways repurposed within unique anatomical niches, which can be inferred from their distinct transcriptional profiles and spatial distribution. This paradigm not only elucidates the roles of these molecules in homeostasis and pathology but also reveals promising therapeutic targets for hearing loss.

For the coMGL subset, high transcriptional homology with CNS microglia—marked by elevated expression of P2ry12, Tmem119, Sparc, and Sall1—strongly implicates the CX3CL1-CX3CR1 axis and transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) signaling as the central mechanisms for maintaining neuroprotection and synaptic integrity. Uniquely, coMGLs also exhibit enrichment in genes linked to platelet activation (Serpin2, F11r) and vascular homeostasis (Pdgfb), suggesting an extended role in monitoring vascular integrity and responding to microhemorrhages, which are likely shaped by cochlear microenvironment. Therapeutically, enhancing these pathways using recombinant CX3CL1 or TGF-β agonists could promote reparative functions in neural and vascular regions.

Conversely, the CD206+CD163+coMac subset, localized to areas such as the SL, displayed a transcriptional signature enriched for ​LYVE1/TIM4-mediated efferocytosis genes (e.g., Lyve1, Timd4, Folr2), aligning it with specialized resident macrophages in other organs that clear apoptotic debris non-inflammatorily. This implies a role in tissue remodeling and maintenance of homeostasis, particularly in response to mechanical stress. Targeting this pathway with IL-4/STAT6 agonists or TIM4 modulators may foster a reparative phenotype, mitigate fibrosis, and support structural recovery.

The CD206+CD163− coMac subset, often expanded in inflammatory niches such as the aged SLi, exhibits a robust immunostimulatory signature with high expression of proinflammatory cytokines (Tnf, Il6) and antigen-presentation genes (H2-Ab1, Cd74). This underscores the role of the TLR4-NLRP3 inflammasome axis and IFN-γ/STAT1 signaling as drivers of inflammatory responses and T-cell activation. Inhibition of these pathways via TLR4 antagonists (e.g., TAK-242), NLRP3 inhibitors (e.g., MCC950), or anti-IFN-γ therapies can suppress detrimental immune activation and preserve the auditory function.

This integrative framework synthesizes direct experimental evidence (e.g., genetic knockout models for CX3CR1) with an inferred biological rationale from transcriptomics to generate testable hypotheses for future therapeutic development. Although some associations (e.g., IL-4/STAT6 in CD206+CD163+ coMacs) were extrapolated from conserved gene modules and spatial correlations, they highlight the potential for subset-specific immunomodulation. Direct validation through niche-specific pathway manipulation (e.g., conditional knockouts or targeted drug delivery) is essential for translating these insights into precision therapies for hearing loss, ultimately bridging mechanistic understanding and clinical application (Fig. 6).

Targeted therapeutic strategies and clinical translation

The functional heterogeneity of cochlear macrophage subsets reveals a spectrum of potential therapeutic targets. However, successful translation requires not only spatial precision, which targets the right cell subset, but also temporal accuracy. As CoRMs transition from early proinflammatory states to late reparative phenotypes following injury, the therapeutic window becomes decisive. Based on the mechanistic framework summarized above, we categorized these approaches into signaling modulators, gene-based interventions, and biomaterial delivery systems. We critically evaluate these strategies, distinguishing between established preclinical effects and mechanisms inferred from transcriptional profiles, and propose a roadmap for precision medicine.

(a) signaling modulators

Enhancing neuroprotection by targeting coMGLs (late-phase repair)

The coMGL subset, which resides in the spiral ganglion and SV, is implicated as the primary sentinel for neuroprotection. In NIHL, particularly cochlear synaptopathy, the CX3CL1-CX3CR1 axis appears critical for the recruitment of coMGLs to damaged ribbon synapses during the post-acute repair phase. Therapeutically, transtympanic delivery of soluble fractalkine (sFKN) has been shown to recruit CX3CR1⁺ coMGLs, restoring ABR Wave-I amplitudes [53]. Similarly, TGF-β signaling is inferred to maintain coMGL homeostasis. In aminoglycoside-induced ototoxicity, amplification of this pathway protects spiral ganglion neurons (SGNs) from degeneration [93], suggesting that agonists targeting coMGL-specific pathways offer a precise avenue for neuroprotection, provided they are administered before irreversible neuronal loss occurs.

Suppressing inflammation by targeting CD206⁺CD163⁻ coMacs (early-phase intervention)

Conversely, the CD206⁺CD163⁻ coMac subset exhibited a transcriptional signature consistent with proinflammatory activation and is likely the primary driver of acute tissue damage in DIHL and CSOM. This subset shows robust expression of the TLR4/NLRP3 inflammasome machinery. Pharmacological inhibitors, such as MCC950 (NLRP3 inhibitor) or TAK-242 (TLR4 antagonist), are designed to block the “saboteur” function of macrophages. To be effective, these agents must be administered during the early inflammatory window to prevent the cytokine storm. Downstream blockade using IL-1 receptor antagonists (e.g., Anakinra) or TNF inhibitors (e.g., Etanercept) has also shown efficacy in preserving hair cells and SV integrity by neutralizing the cytotoxic mediators released by these macrophages [52, 94]. Furthermore, broad-spectrum agents, such as minocycline, may dampen the activation of this subset by inhibiting NF-κB signaling [95]. In ARHL, chronic inhibition of these pathways may mitigate low-grade “inflammaging” driven by this macrophage population.

Promoting Tissue Repair by Targeting CD206⁺CD163⁺ coMacs (Resolution Phase) The CD206⁺CD163⁺ coMac subset is spatially associated with the SL and is hypothesized to mediate tissue remodeling and fibrosis resolution based on its transcriptomic profile. Although direct functional validation is ongoing, the data suggest that IL-4/STAT6 signaling drives this reparative phenotype. Modulating this pathway could theoretically enhance the clearance of apoptotic debris and prevent fibrotic scarring during the resolution phase of NIHL or cochlear implantation trauma. Strategies targeting this subset aim to shift cochlear microenvironment from chronic inflammation to structural homeostasis.

(b) gene-based strategies

Gene-based approaches offer varying degrees of specificity but face genetic and temporal challenges. CX3CR1 gene modulation specifically targets the coMGL population to enhance neuron-macrophage crosstalk. This strategy is particularly vital for genetically susceptible populations; a recent study revealed that humans carrying the CX3CR1I249/M280 loss-of-function polymorphism exhibit impaired macrophage-mediated synaptic repair and exacerbated NIHL [96]. For these individuals, gene augmentation or editing to restore functional CX3CR1 signaling represents a precise, etiology-based therapy. In contrast, CSF1R inhibition (e.g., PLX5622) results in the global depletion of all resident macrophages (both coMGLs and coMacs). This creates a temporal paradox: while depletion effectively prevents the initial inflammatory surge in cisplatin-induced ototoxicity, it simultaneously removes neuroprotective coMGLs, potentially impairing long-term synaptic repair in NIHL [54]. This dichotomy highlights that while broad ablation may be effective in specific acute windows, preserving or restoring the “sentinel” function of coMGLs is essential for long-term structural homeostasis.

(c) biomaterials and delivery systems

Biomaterials provide spatiotemporal control over macrophage modulation to address the challenges of off-target effects and precise timing. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) and their derived extracellular vesicles (EVs) can modulate the local microenvironment, promoting the polarization of macrophages from an M1-like (inflammatory) state to an M2-like (reparative) state, thereby mitigating inflammation-driven hearing loss [97–101]. Additionally, surface modifications, such as zwitterionic coatings on cochlear implants, specifically target interface macrophages, reducing foreign body responses and fibrosis to preserve residual hearing [102]. The direct delivery of immunomodulatory proteins, such as PEDF, also holds promise for regulating perivascular macrophage activation and supporting homeostasis [103]. Applying macrophages-targeted strategies to hearing loss: opportunities and caveats

The functional similarities between CNS microglia and cochlear macrophages in maintaining tissue homeostasis and responding to injury or disease suggest that therapeutic strategies developed for microglia may also be applied to cochlear macrophages [106]. Despite promising preclinical results, several challenges remain in the clinical translation of these therapies. These include optimizing delivery methods (e.g., local versus systemic), ensuring the sustained release of therapeutic agents, and determining the optimal timing for intervention. Additionally, the specificity of therapies for cochlear macrophages, rather than other immune cells, is critical to avoid unwanted side effects. Future strategies combining multimodal therapies and personalized treatment regimens are likely to offer the best prospects for clinical success.

Summary and future perspectives

Over the past few decades, substantial progress has been made in defining cochlear macrophages, their residence, and their function. CoRMs comprise diverse resident and infiltrating populations, whose identities and developmental origins are only beginning to be elucidated. These cells exhibit striking regional heterogeneity across the BM, SV, SL, OSL, SGNs, and SLi, with each niche associated with distinct roles in maintaining homeostasis or mediating injury. Acting as both guardians and effectors of pathology, CoRMs exert their influence through microenvironmental cues, developmental imprinting, and dynamic responses to aging and ototoxic insults.

Recent single-cell and spatial transcriptomic studies have revealed multiple myeloid cell subsets and niche-dependent distributions [31, 33]. However, it remains unclear whether microenvironmental cues alone dictate macrophage specialization or whether ontogeny provides an additional long-lasting imprint [12, 107]. Evidence from other tissues suggests that fetal-derived macrophages, shaped by continuous signal integration within the niche, may not be fully replaced by short-lived monocyte-derived macrophages [12, 107, 108]. Thus, CoRM diversity is likely jointly governed by developmental origins and microenvironmental signals [109].

Critical gaps include reliance on static murine data, a lack of human temporal studies, and insufficient direct comparisons between models. Future research should employ longitudinal approaches (e.g., fate mapping and real-time imaging) to resolve subset-specific dynamics and guide targeted immunotherapies tailored to distinct etiologies and phases. This underscores the need for precision in understanding CoRM biology to advance the development of therapeutic strategies. To bridge the gap between preclinical findings and human physiology, future studies should prioritize complementary multi-scale approaches. First, advanced in vivo imaging techniques—such as two-photon microscopy, fluorescent microendoscopy, and near-infrared II (NIR-II) imaging—are essential for real-time visualization of macrophage dynamics. With further optimization, these modalities have the potential for noninvasive or intraoperative assessment in clinical settings. Second, post-mortem human cochlear analyses provide a critical avenue for validating macrophage phenotypes in situ. Combining multiplex immunofluorescence with structured illumination microscopy will allow for the high-resolution mapping of macrophage markers (e.g., IBA1 and CD163) and polarization states alongside neuronal stress indicators, thereby directly linking immune signatures to lifetime auditory histories. Finally, cross-species integration—leveraging non-human primate models and human auditory–immune correlation studies—is imperative to establish surrogate biomarkers, accelerating the translation of macrophage-targeted interventions from bench to bedside [110].

In conclusion, cochlear macrophages represent a double-edged sword in auditory pathology. A comprehensive framework that integrates ontogeny, niche signaling, and functional specialization is pivotal for identifying therapeutic targets. Precisely defining and characterizing these subsets and unraveling their dynamic responses to pathological stimuli, such as noise, aging, and ototoxic insults, are essential for harnessing their potential. Such knowledge will ultimately enable the development of interventions that selectively modulate specific CoRM populations to promote cochlear repair while mitigating the detrimental effects of neuroinflammation. By leveraging emerging technologies and cross-species approaches, it may be possible to selectively modulate pathogenic subsets while preserving protective functions, thereby opening new avenues for preserving auditory sensitivity and structural integrity [111]. Consequently, targeting these spatially and temporally dynamic macrophage subpopulations presents a promising therapeutic strategy for the precision treatment of hearing loss [24].
