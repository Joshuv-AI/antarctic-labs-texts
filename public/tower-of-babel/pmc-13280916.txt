Natural Biostimulants for Sustainable Agriculture: The Mechanisms and Functions of Humic Substances for Plant Growth Advancement
Authors: Zhanar E. Ibraeva, Faisal Kholiya, Ainur Kabdrakhmanova, Aida K. Gaipbergenova, Sana K. Kabdrakhmanova, Esbol Shaimardan, Veronika V. Davydova, Aida M. Maussumbayeva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Humic substances (HS) are important parts of soil organic matter and are very important for the health of the soil, the growth of plants, and the soil’s ability to withstand both biotic and abiotic pressures. HS are used a lot in sustainable farming because they have several different physicochemical qualities that make them useful for things such as soil conditioning, nitrogen management, and biostimulation. This review compiles existing information regarding the structure, origin, and methods of action of HS, focusing specifically on its impact on soil characteristics, nutrient availability, plant growth, and resilience to stress. Recent advancements in extraction, characterization, and application methodologies are examined, alongside novel notions that regard HS as supramolecular assemblies rather than distinct macromolecules. The paper talks about the main problems with HS heterogeneity, extraction artifacts, and standardization. It also points out the most important research areas that might help make HS safer and more effective in agriculture and related fields.

Introduction

The current environmental issues of climate change, soil deterioration, biodiversity reduction, and food security protection need innovative answers that stem from complete knowledge of basic natural processes. Soil degradation stands as a critical worldwide issue because scientists estimate that one-third of Earth’s land faces this problem, which results in annual fertile soil loss of 24 billion tons. The predicted 12% decrease in food production and substantial price rise in food products will occur during the following 20 years if this pattern continues. Soil restoration methods that deliver quick results at affordable costs need to be implemented as a matter of high priority. The stabilization of carbon through solid or dissolved forms by HS represents a promising method to enhance soil carbon sequestration, which blocks atmospheric carbon emissions. The Paris Climate Agreement created “4 per 1000” as a program that aims to boost soil carbon storage by 0.4% annually to fight climate change, improve food security, and resolve the CO2 crisis.

HS is now part of the resources that agronomists and ecologists use due to the enhanced soil structure, holds water effectively, promotes microbial activity, and makes nutrients readily accessible. HS in soils primarily arises from the prolonged biological and abiotic transformation (humification) of plant and microbial remains in the soil organic matter reservoir. The industrial production of humic substances depends on lignite and brown coal and leonardite as their primary sources. The multiple functional groups that include carboxyl, phenolic, and quinone moieties in HS create a complex structure, which enables them to bind various components, thus making them essential for soil systems and effective as biostimulants. Researchers in the present time study HS for their carbon sequestration properties while creating new methods to enhance soil fertility and resistance in contemporary agricultural systems. The combined use of conservation tillage with plant residue creates an effective system, which helps reduce soil erosion, which leads to better soil physical properties since they build up organic matter and create permanent soil aggregates. The enhancement of soil structure from this process facilitates the flourishing of microbial communities by establishing optimal environmental conditions that promote improved nutrient cycling through heightened microbial biomass and activity of essential microorganisms such as Acidobacteria and Actinobacteria. The application of corn straw as mulch material results in substantial soil organic carbon accumulation according to experimental data, which also shows that diverse microbial communities develop to enhance crop stress resistance. The application of these methods (i.e., crop rotation, mulching, intercropping, and use of conditioners such as humic products, biochar, and biocompost) together with agricultural waste biochar generates a combined positive outcome. Biochar demonstrates strong sorption properties, which enhance soil water management and create beneficial conditions for plant residue to impact soil operations. Soil organic matter management through agricultural practices including crop rotation, mulching, intercropping, and artificial soil conditioners like humic products, biochar, and biocompost helps sustain soil fertility while fighting climate change. Soil organic matter (SOM) develops through the natural process of humification, which takes many decades to decompose organic materials. , However, to address the decline in soil fertility, primarily due to continuous crop cultivation and intensive use of chemical fertilizers, artificial production of HS is necessary to maintain soil fertility.

As the largest component of soil organic matter, HS constitute 60–80% of the total SOM volume, accounting for 50–80% of dissolved organic matter in water, and 25% in groundwater. The substances enhance soil water retention properties while controlling its large aggregate structures, supporting carbon storage, and modifying microbial communities in the soil. Furthermore, natural biostimulants, including HS, accelerate plant growth, shorten development periods, increase yields, and activate processes such as phosphorus-containing and protein compound synthesis, respiration, cell reproduction, and biomass accumulation through the intensive consumption of environmental components. These biostimulants also positively affect animal condition by increasing weight gain and fertility and exert a significant effect on the reproduction and growth of protein yeasts. The combination of conventional and modern agricultural practices for boosting soil organic carbon storage enables both soil fertility enhancement and extended sustainability of agroecosystems under climate change and rising food requirements.

It is important to note that the way we think about HS has evolved since they initially emerged as unique, high-molecular-weight supramolecular associations. There is expanding evidence suggesting that humic compounds can be defined as groups of different kinds of small organic molecules that remain together through weak, noncovalent interactions such as hydrogen bonding, metal bridging, and hydrophobic forces. This perspective considers humic acids (HA) and fulvic acids (FA) as operationally defined fractions whose characteristics vary according to their extraction methods rather than legitimate polymers with stable molecular structures. The supramolecular point of view is better for this review since it shows HS in natural soil systems more precisely. Classical principles are still useful for both practical and comparative reasons.

The review aims to present existing knowledge about HS in sustainable agriculture through their structural and functional aspects and their practical applications for improving agroecosystem productivity, resilience, and other production systems. This review evaluates existing knowledge about HS mechanisms that affect soil health, plant development, and their role in plant resistance against environmental and biological threats. The research presents both traditional and modern viewpoints, which demonstrate that most HS data stems from alkali-extracted fractions, which might not accurately represent the original composition of soil organic matter. The review aims to organize existing information while establishing essential research paths that will enhance knowledge about HS in global ecosystems and their agricultural uses. The review serves to improve scientific knowledge while promoting joint research activities between soil science, agrochemistry, ecology, and biotechnology fields.

Chemical Nature and Properties of Humic Substances

It is well established that HS originate from the remains of living organisms, primarily of plant origin. The environment safeguards resistant organic matter components because their structural framework endures through both irregularities and mineral complexes according to previous report. The random stochastic processes that create HS produce disordered organic compounds, which appear as colored substances in soils and water bodies and coal-derived natural materials. These associating molecules compounds, ranging from 600 to 300,000 Da, consisting of various chemical components, including aromatic compounds such as benzene, quinoline, quinone, pyrrole, pyridine, and furan. The structure of HS contains four types of bridges including −O–, −NH–, −N=, −CH2–, and −CC– while its functional groups consist mainly of carboxyl, phenolic, amino, quinone and methoxyl groups.

Schreiner and Shorey conducted research in the early 20th century, which showed that HA contain resin acids and their esters as well as fatty acid glycerides (fats), agrosterol, phytosterol, paraffins, lignoceric, agroceric acids, and FA, which include crenic, apocrenic acids, dioxy-stearic acid, picolinic carboxylic acid, pentosans, xanthine, hypoxanthine, cytosine, histidine, and arginine. The composition of HA in one soil may differ significantly from that in another; likewise, the FA composition varies between soils. Organic compounds extracted from soil organic matter (SOM) with alkaline aqueous solutions can be divided into two groups: (1) compounds weakly degradable by microorganisms (resins, wax-like substances, partially fats, pentosans) and (2) degradation intermediates of proteins (amino acids, dioxy-stearic acid), nucleoproteins (cytosine, xanthine, hypoxanthine), alkaloids, and essential oils.

According to Trusov, some of the brown substances extracted from soils with alkaline solutions were not originally present in soils but formed due to the action of alkali on SOM, whereas others (e.g., tannins, chlorophylls, incrusting substances) turned brown. The late 20th and early 21st centuries brought forth new evidence, which supported this perspective. Oriez et al. The authors stated that HS develop through alkaline processing of nonwoody plant biomass materials including lignin, polysaccharides, and additional substance. The process produces black liquor through the combination of lignin and saccharide oligomers, which dissolve into the liquid aqueous phase to create a dark-colored colloidal solution. However, black liquor contains products not only of lignin degradation but also of its subsequent depolymerization. The use of mineral acid by Oriez et al. resulted in total lignin derivative precipitation. HS are subdivided into humic acids (HA), soluble in alkaline conditions but insoluble in acid; FA, which are soluble in both media; and humin, which is insoluble in both media. It should be noted that the separation of HS into HA and FA depends on their concentration. If the concentration of HS is below the critical micelle concentration (CMC), HA retains its aggregate stability in an aqueous solution at pH 1–2. Conversely, the higher the concentration of HS above the CMC, the more HA precipitates in a strongly acidic aqueous solution. HS should be considered as fractions defined operationally obtained in extraction procedures rather than as discrete and uniform supramolecular associations that exist in soils in an unchanged form. Hayes and Roulia (2025) demonstrated that alkaline extraction dissolved and accumulated organic compounds, which resulted in molecular accumulation by hydrogen interaction, hydrogen bonds, and cation interaction, causing heterogeneity. The mixture of black liquor contains various organic compounds with different molecular weights together with mineral-organic interactions and colloidal systems, which make it a complex and dynamic substance. The extraction conditions determine the HS structure because it is affected by pH, ionic strength, and concentration values. The separation of HA and FA in acidic environments depends on their chemical properties and their ability to form reversible aggregates, which depends on their concentration. The evaluation of biological activity in humic preparations needs to be performed with proper care. The effects of HS on plant growth and soil operations seem to result from the combined actions of their various chemical mechanisms instead of a single specific molecular arrangement. The system experiences physical transformations because of these elements, which affect nutrient distribution, membrane permeability, and antimicrobial behavior. The identification of operational and supramolecular characteristics in HS becomes essential to prevent incorrect structure–function relationships and to ensure laboratory results match natural soil processes. To be clear, black liquor, alkaline extracts, and HS are commonly discussed together but are different in terms of how they work and how they originate. Black liquor is a complex composition that is produced by the alkaline treatment of organic or carbonaceous materials. It is composed of a variety of dissolved organic and inorganic components. Alkaline extracts are the end products of extraction processes and might have both humic and nonhumic substances. The operational definition of HS includes three fractions that scientists obtain from extract processing: HA, FA and humin. The review investigates HS by recognizing their properties, which depend on extraction methods, while it maintains separate categories for these substances to avoid oversimplification.

The chemical structure of HS contains aromatic rings which connect to aliphatic chains that differ based on their source. The molecular weight of FA is lower than that of HA and humin, while its oxygen content is higher but its carbon and nitrogen content is lower. The solubility of HA is dependent on the pH of the medium. The deprotonation of HA takes place in alkaline conditions, which leads to the formation of hydrophilic anionic species including carboxylates and phenolates, that become soluble in solution. The HA precipitate develops in acidic conditions because of protonation and increasing hydrogen ion concentrations. The decrease in pH value reduces electrostatic repulsion between ionizable functional groups, which causes the humic macromolecular structure to adopt a more compact conformation. The structure contains hydrophobic elements which stay inside, while hydrophilic elements face the environment. The behavior of HA surfactants results in surface tension reduction because they create micelle-like structures through their surfactant properties. The molecules start by forming intramolecular aggregates, which then evolve into intermolecular aggregates that result in HA precipitation under acidic conditions. The distribution of functional groups in HA depends on three factors, which include the organic material origin, the degree of humification, and environmental conditions during their development. The chemical composition of HA depends on their origin from lignin-rich plant materials because they contain phenolic and aromatic compounds but their carboxyl and quinone content increases during advanced humification processes. The acidic properties of HA stem from its carboxylic acid and phenolic group content according to Kleber, M.; Lehmann, J. 2019. The carbon-to-nitrogen (C/N) ratio functions as a basic characteristic, which determines the properties of HA. Natural HA receives its nitrogen enrichment through microbial activity, decomposition processes and condensation reactions with amino compounds that occur in the soil. The organic molecules in HS take part in redox reactions and electron transfer processes which create complexes that bind with soil minerals and particles through electrostatic forces and hydrogen bonds. Due to this adhesive interaction, the components of soil develop stronger bonds that simultaneously enhance both soil structure stability and nutrient storage capacity. Also, the carboxyl and phenolic hydroxyl groups in HS enable them to regulate in acidity in soil. Research reveals that HS impact microbial respiration, soil nutrient availability, and metal binding properties, which results in better soil fertility and biogeochemical cycling. The key focus of research continues to utilize HS to improve agricultural yields and soil health because people require eco-friendly organic fertilizers that can replace NPK-based chemical fertilizers (NPK stands for nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium). HS are not a good source of primary nutrients like nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium for plants, but HS function as soil conditioners and plant growth supporters because they enhance nutrient accessibility while promoting root development and simultaneously improving both physical and chemical aspects of the soil. Agricultural soil constantly losing their organic matter while HS help to enhance the storage of organic carbon in soil. HS form soluble complexes with phosphorus and micronutrient cations because of their oxygen-containing functional groups which allows plants to obtain these nutrients regardless of soil pH. The nutrient-HS complexes stay in the soil solution to prevent leaching while they continue to be accessible to biological organisms.

The high molecular weight of HS along with its oxygen-containing functional groups enable it to form strong bonds with soil particles which results in improved soil aggregation and higher crop production. The research by Nardi et al. found that soil type and HS source determine how HS aggregates in the environment. Experiments confirming the penetration of HS components into plant roots were demonstrated using micro autoradiography. Although the detailed mechanisms of HS movement across the plant root surface (rhizoplane) are not yet sufficiently studied, they appear to depend on the molecular size distribution of HS components. The research by Kulikova et al. provides evidence that roots can transport substances to shoots through transpiration after penetration. (Kulikova et al., 2016). The study shows that plant membrane-associated proteins including high-affinity K+ transporter 1, phospholipase A2 and H+-ATPase experience regulation through HS.

Further research is needed to determine how HS components affect the activity of such enzymes; however, two probable mechanisms can be considered here. The presence of oxygen-based functional groups in HS causes pH changes in cellular environments, which leads to changes in how membrane proteins function. In particular, the alkalization of root cell membranes by the H+/NO3 symporter may be neutralized by the acidity of HS. he nonspecific adhesive properties of HS are expected to interact with functional proteins, which may be either activated or deactivated. The experimental results demonstrate that HS physically captures proteins through electrostatic bonds during laboratory testing.

Extraction and Processing Techniques for Humic Substances

Due to high ash, moisture, volatile matter content, and low calorific value, natural carbonaceous compounds are of no interest as a solid fuel; however, their high HS content makes them attractive as a source for obtaining these valuable materials. Technologies for extracting HA from natural carbonaceous materials include various types of extraction, including the use of physical intensification methods (based on hydromechanical, ultrasonic, electrodynamic treatment, and their combinations), oxidation, hydrothermal conversion, mechanochemical activation (Figure ). The extraction processes for the HA obtaining include acid-alkali extraction, acid precipitation, organic solvent extraction, and extraction using ion-exchange resins. Each of these methods combines both advantages and disadvantages. For example, acid extraction is simple and economically accessible, but leads to a high level of impurities in the final product and noticeable atmospheric emissions, making it environmentally unsafe for research and practical application. Notably, HA with higher molecular weight, reduced nitrogen, carbon content, increased oxygen, and hydrogen content are obtained by using acid extraction as a pretreatment before alkaline extraction. This is associated, according to the authors, with a reduction of the number of low-molecular-weight compounds during the acid pretreatment process. The extraction of HA from natural carbonaceous compounds is most commonly followed by leaching followed by acid precipitation. Depending on their solubility at different pH and concentrations, HS can be separated into fractions such as FA, brown HA, gray HA, and humin. FA dissolves at any environmental pH, while brown and gray HA become soluble at pH from 2 to 7, while humin remains insoluble at any pH. Chemical reagents used include sodium and potassium hydroxides, ammonia, soda, sodium fluoride, sodium acetate, sodium oxalate, sodium pyrophosphate, ammonium oxalate, as well as organic solvents: acetyl bromide, aqueous dioxane, furfural, and amines.

Methods for obtaining HS using chemical reagents have significant disadvantages, as the use of alkali negatively affects the structure of HS, reduces the biological and chemical activity, and destroys the natural structure of humic preparations. Furthermore, part of the HS is water-insoluble. The mechanochemical method of HA extraction is based on dispersing particles of humate-containing raw material with the simultaneous addition of chemical reagents. The mechanical process of breaking down raw materials produces bigger contact areas between phases while creating new pores, capillaries, and re-establishing contact points. The application of particular reagents leads to better production rates of the desired chemical products. The process of mechanochemical activation uses ball mills and mechanical activators to treat HA in the solid phase, which results in improved activity levels. This process creates paramagnetic centers in NCM, generates active oxygen and hydroxyl radicals on the particle surface by breaking various chemical bonds. The ball milling process transforms the catalytic transition state so that it can fully interact with the initial NCM. The advantage of the mechanochemical method lies in reducing the amount of chemical reagents and accelerating the process time. Mechanochemical activation of HA using nanocatalysts is an environmentally friendly alternative method that enhances HA activation, improving their solubility and effectiveness in agricultural crops. In these methods, instead of using chemical oxidizers, nanocatalysts are developed and prepared to increase the yield of the target product. The use of various nanocatalysts has been reported, such as 3D-MoS2–HN, TiO2–WO3, Fe3O4/LaNiO3, and α-MnO2/kaolinite. Typically, these catalysts are applied in low doses (<1%) in combination with alkali. Nitrogen-rich humic fertilizers can be obtained through mechanochemical activation based on nanocatalysts using extrusion. The method produces additional functional groups during activation, which results in better moisture retention, nitrogen absorption, and lower nitrogen release into water and soil. In addition to the usual alkaline extraction and acid precipitation, new research has shown new ways to isolate HS that do not change their structure or make artifacts during the extraction process. Hayes and Roulia (2025) describe extraction methods that use neutral-salt solutions, like sodium pyrophosphate at a pH near neutral, to dissolve HS by replacing polyvalent cation bridges instead of relying on strong alkaline hydrolysis. These methods help to partially recover humic and fulvic fractions while keeping the relationships between them similar to those in natural soil and water systems. ,

The review also discusses sequential fractionation methods that combine aqueous extraction with adsorption–desorption techniques that use polymeric resins, such as XAD-8 and XAD-4. These methods use resins to separate hydrophobic HA and FAs from more hydrophilic acidic parts of diluted and acidified extracts. This gives a more detailed operational classification of organic matter fractions. Resin-based methods are great for looking at how molecules are related, how polarities are spread out, and how HS interact with mineral phases.

Hayes and Roulia further describe alternative methods for isolating humin-type fractions, subsequent to the removal of soluble humic elements. These methods use urea-enhanced alkaline solutions and then acidified organic solvents to extract the humin-type fractions. All of these methods show that no extraction method is good enough to get native soil organic matter. However, using a combination of alkaline, neutral-salt, and resin-based methods can give us a better understanding of how HS are organized and how they act.

Among process intensification technologies, ultrasound has proven to be particularly effective in increasing the extraction efficiency of HS, especially the hydrophobic FA fraction. However, excessive duration and high intensity of ultrasound can negatively affect extraction efficiency. Ultrasound energy produces shock waves and microjets, which improve mixing, create cavitation effects, and generate highly reactive particles including hydroxyl radicals and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and ozone with strong oxidative properties. The ultrasonic process extracts HA at higher rates because potassium ions (K+) from KOH create additional active centers, which boost HA dissolution through bond disruption, better chemical diffusion, and mass transfer. The ultrasonic process results in improved HA extraction, because potassium ions (K+) from KOH create additional active sites that become saturated. Ultrasonic treatment allows HA dissociates by breaking up weak intermolecular forces such as hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonds that keep humic molecules stable in the solid phase. Cavitation and microturbulence also make it easier for solvents to get into and spread out, for mass to move between the solid particles and the liquid phase, resulting in making it easier for HS to release. The process of using ultrasound with oxidants enables the production of HAs and FAs from low-rank coals (LRCs) as shown in Figure B. The method produces higher HA yields while decreasing processing duration and the amount of oxidants needed. The process of OH• radical formation through ultrasound treatment results in LRC molecule breakdown, which creates new oxygen sites in HA structures and enhances functional groups. The method produces no direct CO2 emissions which makes it an environmentally friendly approach to convert LRCs into HS. The process of ultrasonic irradiation creates cavitation that results in effective particle size reduction and substantial extraction of water-soluble organic compounds into the liquid solution. The extracted HS show strong biological activity, which enables fast chemical reactions during hydrothermal synthesis. The ultrasonic extraction process takes place through ultrasonic devices that perform both dispersion and extraction operations for HS from humic-containing raw materials.

The production of HA shows promise through the implementation of electric and electromagnetic field technology. The anode of an electric field develops an oxidative environment which interacts with the substrate (Figure C). The substrate particles hit the electrode surface, which results in oxidation reactions that replace the oxygen evolution process at the anode. The oxidative environment breaks down the substrate structure through active chemical bond cleavage, which produces oxygen-containing functional groups that result in HA formation. The application of an electric field during extraction leads to benzene and condensed aromatic ring oxidation, which results in the formation of phenolic hydroxyl and quinone groups that enhance the oxygen-containing functional group content of HA. The process improves their ability to dissolve in alkaline solutions, which results in higher HA production. The high oxidative environment of electrochemical reactions enables the oxidation of bioresources containing low native HA content, such as compost and peat, which results in high HA production levels. In HA production methods utilizing electric fields, moderate oxidation is necessary to achieve an optimal yield. The process of electrochemical oxidation needs improvement for HA yield enhancement through controlled oxidation degree management. The production of HA through electric fields requires particular electrode materials together with exact voltage levels. Various anode materials (Cu, Ni, Pb, and Pt) have been used to generate HAs, oxygen (O2), carbon dioxide (CO2), and small amounts of carbon monoxide (CO). HA can be produced from various resources using electromagnetic field systems, such as vortex layer devices developed by GlobeCore (Figure D). The substrate goes through this process after being mixed with water at 75 to 95% moisture content and then exposed to powerful ferromagnetic and hydromechanical forces. The process generates particle collisions which produce particles smaller than 15 μm, and 80–90% of these particles fall within this size range. The process breaks down the substrate structure, which results in the release of HA and can be performed with or without the use of alkaline or oxidative agents. The combination of vortex layer technology with ferromagnetic elements in electromagnetic field devices leads to a 1.5-fold faster reaction rate and decreases both reagent usage and electricity needs by 20%. The mixture temperature stays between 60–90 °C throughout the processing period according to previous study. The extraction of HA and FA from peat, sapropel, compost, vermicompost, lignite and leonhardite using an electromagnetic field produces extraction yields of up to 19 and 30.8 g/L. Advantages of this method include the production of nanoscale particles that do not clog spray nozzles, low energy consumption (9.5 kWh) with a specific energy intensity of 1.9 kWh/m3, and compact equipment that can be integrated with other systems for fertilizer production. Reagent-free extraction methods yield a chemically pure and environmentally friendly product. HS are not considered as pure compounds according to traditional chemical standards. The researchers used different extraction and fractionation methods to achieve these operational fractions. Their “purity” depends on the lack of inorganic substances and the existence of merely low-molecular-weight impurities, instead of on an even distribution of their chemical structure. The laboratory uses three primary methods to evaluate sample quality, which include ash content measurement and elemental analysis and spectroscopic validation through FTIR and UV–Vis and NMR tests to verify efficient humic material extraction with minimal mineral and nonhumic substance contamination.

Molecular Mechanisms and Bioactivity of Humic Substances

Contemporary Concepts of the Molecular Mechanisms of Humic Substances at the Cellular and Organismal Level

The agricultural productivity of crops and soil fertility depends heavily on HS which forms a major part of soil organic matter. The distinct physical and chemical characteristics of HS enable them to control how plants interact with living organisms and nonliving components in their environment. Understanding the mechanisms of HS action at the molecular level as well as the specifics of their transport and distribution within plant tissues is critically important for developing effective rhizosphere management strategies and optimizing the use of HS in agriculture.

Contemporary understanding of the molecular mechanisms of HS action at the cellular and organismal levels is based on principles formulated by researchers as early as the late twentieth century. The alkaline extraction process of HS from soil organic matter results in the extraction of particular individual compounds as described by Orlov et al. Subsequent fractionation of HS likely involves concomitant reactions, such as the hydrolysis of associating molecules components to form low-molecular-weight compounds, as well as the incorporation of some low-molecular-weight degradation products into the structures of relatively associating molecules biopolymers. HS can influence plant physiology via complex transcriptional (gene-regulatory) networks. By regulation of the expression of numerous genes, most of which are involved in the cell cycle, meristem organization, and cytoskeleton dynamics, these compounds influence plant physiology and metabolism. The biostimulatory activity of HS is most pronounced in fractions with a relatively low molecular weight. , Moreover, relatively low-molecular-weight HS penetrates plant cells more readily than their high-molecular-weight analogues. The effectiveness of HS depends on their dosage level and their origin as well as molecular characteristics.

Some HS components, derived from partially degraded biological macromolecules, such as lignin, polysaccharides, and proteins, may occur as low-molecular-weight structural units that can be absorbed and assimilated by plants. Research studies have not completely explained how HS affects plant hormonal enhancement through macromorphological changes but scientists predict these effects will link to hormonal regulation through increased indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), ethylene (ET), nitric oxide (NO), and abscisic acid (ABA) levels in plant roots. Application of Humic Substances in Agriculture

The Role of Humic Substances in Improving Soil Structure and Aggregate Stability

The stability of agricultural soil aggregates depends on HS because these substances create a better soil structure. Organic compounds in the soil improve its properties through better water storage, enhanced microbial growth, and improved nutrient absorption. The addition of HS including HA enhances soil structure through enhanced water-resistant aggregate formation, improved water retention, and porosity but decreased bulk density and dispersion coefficients. The process results in better soil conditions and structural stability which benefits sandy soils with their naturally weak structure.

The agricultural sector and manufacturing industry use HA because their carboxyl and phenolic hydroxyl groups form stable organomineral complexes which enable soil to hold water, maintain cation retention and chelate essential micronutrients while preventing toxic metals from escaping the soil. The microorganisms improve soil fertility through their ability to create better soil structure and stability as well as porosity which enables water to move through the soil while preserving soil nutrients and binding micronutrients (Figure ). HA enhances soil structure through its ability to increase water retention and permeability, its resistance to salinization then its effects on cation-exchange capacity also nutrient absorption and soluble phosphorus levels. The sequestration of carbon occurs through HA because they boost photosynthesis levels and create conditions that support diverse microbial populations by enhancing cell membrane permeability and providing energy for microbial respiration also reducing nutrient competition as well as increasing specific soil microorganisms including Actinobacteria, Actinobacteria and Bacteroidetes. The physiological activity of HA varies depending on the composition and content of functional groups. HAs represent a continuum of organic matter transformation rather than discrete categories. The formation of HA occurs when plants decay in soil through microbial processes, which create compounds between 5,000 and 50,000 Da in weight and contain various carboxylic acid groups along their aliphatic chains. , The chemical makeup of HA, which forms from soil contains 45–50% carbon (C); 4–5% hydrogen (H); 1–2% nitrogen (N), and 35–45% oxygen (O) with C/N ratios ranging from 10 to 30 which indicates less humification. The molecular weight of HAs in leonardite exceeds 50,000 to 1,000,000 Da because this natural carbonaceous material undergoes extensive humification. , The presence of more than 50% condensed aromatic rings together with elevated carboxylic acid content in these materials results in better cation exchange capacity. The C/N ratios of Leonardite-derived HA range from 30 to 60 because the material has undergone extensive humification, which resulted in nitrogen depletion. Lignite-derived HAs have high molecular weights (50,000–500,000 Da) and aromatic content (57.5% C) but lower carboxylic acid content than leonardite. All HA types consist of carboxylic (−COOH) and phenolic (Ar–OH) functional groups which exist at different levels based on humification degree. , The two main functional groups in HA are phenolic (−OH) and carboxylic (−COOH) which serve as the primary components for soil enhancement and plant development promotion. , The molecular structure of HA contains functional groups which include phenolic hydroxyl, carboxylic and carbonyl groups that create bonds with clay minerals, metal ions and hydrated oxides, playing a key role in the retention and release of soil nutrients. HA creates bonds with clay minerals through van der Waals forces; hydrogen bonds; electrostatic attraction; and cation bridges which result in organomineral complexes that strengthen soil aggregate stability. Research has investigated how HA and alkali residue (AR) affect the structure of Northern Chinese saline-alkali soil aggregates by examining their impact on macroaggregates which are essential for soil health and stability. The results showed that HA addition caused MWD (mean weight diameter) and GMD (geometric mean diameter) values to increase first before they reached their highest point at 3% HA concentration and then began to decrease. The experimental data show that macroaggregate formation achieves its maximum point when HA reaches its highest concentration level. The fractal dimension (FD) followed an opposite pattern, which demonstrated that HA concentration creates complex effects on saline-alkali soil aggregate structure. The authors established that HA enhance soil structure and aggregate stability through their ability to create macroaggregates and their capacity to bind with soil colloids at higher frequencies, which stabilizes the soil colloidal system to create better aggregate structure in saline-alkali soil. The work presents the results of research on the biological reclamation of disturbed lands in the Far North. The authors developed a biogeochemical technology for reclaiming disturbed tundra soils using peat and potassium humate. Research data shows that potassium humate-based formulations create beneficial growth environments for young plants during their first developmental phase, which starts when seeds germinate and continues until they reach their first seedling stage. Research studies show that humate treatments lead to better seed germination results, and they create stronger seedlings that develop at a similar pace. The development process receives three main effects, which stem from better nutrient access, metabolic stimulation, and better water absorption during its initial growth period. The size of the response depends on types of crop species grows and how the application is done as well as which humate product is used but the results show potassium humate functions as an effective biostimulant for young plants. The chemical structure of HA contains amphiphilic, polymeric, and hydrophilic properties together with complexing, ion-exchange, redox and electron-transport functions. The properties of HA determine their biological activity, which enables them to maintain soil health; ecological balance in water and soil environments. The agricultural properties of these compounds result from their ability to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) through quinones and their deprotonation properties under neutral and alkaline conditions for phenols and carboxylic acids. The electron transfer mechanism in soil bioelectrochemical systems operates through phenolic and quinone functional groups in HA because these groups create active centers for electron donation and acceptance.

HA hydrophilic properties also its hydroxyl and carboxyl groups enable soil particle aggregation which results in better soil stability. The applied modification enhances soil water retention and, at the same time, improves soil permeability. The carboxyl and phenolic hydroxyl groups in HA form metal ion complexes that transform their functional properties into less toxic and less mobile forms which improve soil fertility through nutrient release. HA contains ion-exchange properties which enable its negatively charged surfaces to swap cations with other ions for improving soil fertility and maintaining nutrient availability. The acidic functional groups in HA (mainly carboxylic and phenolic groups) partially dissociate in soil solution to produce H+ ions and also create negatively charged sites (R–COO–, Ar–O–). The released H+ ions take part in cation exchange by removing multiple positively charged ions (Ca2+, Mg2+, and Fe3+) from clay and oxide surfaces. The deprotonated humic groups create cation bridges which allow them to link humic molecules to mineral particles through their ability to draw these cations which leads to the creation of HA–metal–clay complexes and organomineral aggregates. This structural change of HA in soil leads to better water retention, air circulation, improved infiltration and effective porosity which enables better root development and mineral nutrient availability for plants. Furthermore, the oxygen-containing functional groups of HA which include carboxyl, phenolic, carbonyl, and quinone structures serve as ligand and redox-active sites which enable microbial enzymes to bind to surfaces as well reveal metal–enzyme cofactors and perform electron transfer. The process speeds up organic matter breakdown while it enables soil microbial growth and shows the presence of available nutrients.

Plant Protection against Abiotic and Biotic Stresses

Plant stresses induced by elevated soil salinity, drought, temperature fluctuations, and toxic compounds are major causes of reduced agricultural crop yields. Plants respond to these abiotic stresses by partially activating the expression of stress-responsive genes, thereby enhancing their resilience. , HS can facilitate plant adaptation to abiotic stresses by stimulating various physiological responses, including the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which play a crucial role in signaling pathways that help plants cope with stress. When considering the overall plant stress response and the involvement of HS in these adaptations, the primary focus is on the maintenance of cellular homeostasis, specifically redox balance, under various abiotic stresses, of which a common feature is ROS accumulation. The research by Lamar shows that HS enhance plant development through their ability to act as redox buffers, which create favorable conditions for plant growth. The system acquires this ability because it contains two different chemical groups that imitate quinones in their oxidizing properties and phenolic and other oxygen-containing compounds in their reducing properties. The quinone and semiquinone structures function as pro-oxidant sites, which attract electrons, causing the development of temporary reactive oxygen species (ROS) and establishing redox signaling in plant roots. The phenolic hydroxyl groups serve as antioxidant sites which donate electrons to radicals and effectively neutralize them while preserving the redox balance throughout cells. Plants respond to HS through their antioxidant and pro-antioxidant properties which determine their effectiveness in establishing an optimal balance between these two characteristics. Pro-oxidant fragments may be involved in ROS generation, leading to apoplastic oxidative bursts. Conversely, antioxidant fragments (e.g., polyphenols) can mitigate the oxidative burst, in addition to activating the plant’s own antioxidant system to counteract excessive ROS production and restore redox homeostasis, thereby protecting membranes, proteins, and nucleic acids. This leads to stress tolerance or enhanced growth and productivity. Current knowledge regarding control mechanisms and strategies to counteract stress remains insufficiently explored. To prevent abiotic stresses, in addition to traditional strategies (optimizing plant growth conditions and providing water, nutrients, and plant growth regulators), biostimulants, including HS, are applied to regulate physiological processes in plants to enhance productivity. HS offer novel approaches for modifying plant physiological activity to improve growth and abiotic stress tolerance by modulating physiological and biochemical processes and activating numerous interconnected signaling pathways. Mitigating the impact of abiotic stresses on plant growth and development throughout ontogeny can significantly increase crop productivity.

HS promote plant development specifically in tomato plants while they control endophytic bacterial populations during osmotic stress conditions. The researchers applied barcoding analysis to determine bacterial DNA sequences from particular areas, which allowed them to identify root bacterial communities of tomatoes during hydroponic sterile system studies. The results showed that both HS and osmotic stress, as well as their combination, led to significant changes in the bacterial communities within tomato roots. Specifically, osmotic stress retarded plant growth and reduced the presence of certain bacteria (e.g., Bradyrhizobium sp.). The HS application under stressful conditions led to better plant development and created beneficial changes in bacterial microbial communities.

Research now focuses on HS as natural biostimulants which help plants defend themselves against biotic stresses that result from pathogens and pests and threaten global crop yields. The plant response to this treatment occurs through changes in biological, chemical, and molecular processes as well as through its impact on rhizosphere microbial populations. Plants that receive HA treatment begin their defensive compound production through the activation of phytoalexin, salicylic acid, and jasmonic acid synthesis genes. These compounds function as signaling molecules, which activate pathways that protect against fungal, bacterial, and viral infections. HS activate plant immune defenses against biotic stress through enzyme activation of peroxidased, β-1,3-glucanase, phenylalanine ammonia-lyase and through stress gene transcriptional control, which helps citrus plants fight diseases. The antimicrobial properties of HS enable them to directly inhibit pathogen multiplication. Research shows that HA stop phytopathogenic fungi including Fusarium and Phytophthora from growing through their ability to attach to fungal cell membranes, which causes membrane breakdown. The research shows that HS demonstrates its effectiveness as a method to combat soil-borne pathogens. HS can also enhance plant resistance to insect pests. FA treatment of plants results in higher production of secondary metabolites which include alkaloids and terpenoids that function as insect repellents and toxins. , The system enables gene expression control of volatile organic compound (VOC) synthesis genes, which attract pest natural enemies for better biological control. The rhizosphere functions as a critical interface between plants and microorganisms, which helps defend against biological plant threats. The environmental conditions created by HS allow Pseudomonas and Bacillus microorganisms to survive. The beneficial microbes produce antibiotics and other compounds that help fight against pathogenic microorganisms. The system of HS creates better conditions for plant-fungal symbiosis, which results in enhanced nutrient access and better stress tolerance. The successful application of HS for enhancing plant resistance to biotic stresses has been reported. The use of HA on tomatoes resulted in a 30% reduction of late blight infections, and farmers obtained a 20% increase in their tomato production. The research on wheat produced similar findings because FA treatment decreased rust disease occurrence while making plants more resistant to aphid attacks.

HS create beneficial effects on seed germination and plant stress resistance, but their useful effects are restricted to certain areas. Soil management requires HS as part of a complete strategy, which uses multiple approaches for effective results.

Textile Dye Removal from Wastewater

The environmental problem of textile wastewater dye pollution can be addressed using the dye adsorption abilities of HA and FA. The worldwide textile industry produces 10,000 tons of dyes per year, which makes textile effluent color contamination a major environmental concern. The adsorption of reactive dyes, including the Reactive Red, Acid Blue, and Direct Blue, onto HS occurs through multiple interactions, which include π–π stacking between the aromatic rings of the dyes as well as those of HS, electrostatic forces between dye ions and ionizable groups in HS, and hydrogen bonding with humic oxygen groups. ,, The batch study demonstrates that HA-modified adsorbents absorb 60–95% of different synthetic dyes at HA concentrations of 0.5–2.0 g/L. The Sips model suggests that HA and FA achieve their maximum adsorption capacity of 327–328 and 213–214 mg/g, respectively, at a temperature of 25 °C. , The performance evaluations, which bring together cost-effectiveness and biodegradability of HS compared to conventional activated carbon adsorbents, prove that HA-based materials can serve as a successful substitute for the treatment of point-source textile effluent and the removal of color from industrial processes. ,

Heavy Metal and Metalloid Immobilization in Contaminated Soils

The carboxylic acid (−COOH) and phenolic hydroxyl (Ar–OH) functional groups which exist in soil-derived and leonardite-derived HS produce prevailing organometal chelate complexes that bind to toxic metals including lead (Pb2+), cadmium (Cd2+), arsenic (As­(V/III)) and chromium (Cr­(VI/III)).The stable chelate complexes and surface complexes that HS form with toxic metals in contaminated soils decrease the amount of exchangeable toxic metals, which results in lower plant metal absorption and reduced human exposure to toxic metals through agricultural products. Research conducted in agricultural fields shows that adding HA amendments at 1–5% (w/w/w) to metal-contaminated soils decreases the amount of plant-accessible metals and metal accumulation in crops by 30–70% based on soil composition, metal type, and amendment dosage. , The redox-active components of HS, which include quinone and phenolic groups, enable electron transfer reactions that transform dangerous and mobile compounds into less toxic forms (Cr­(VI) → Cr­(III) and As­(V) → As­(III)). The combination of properties in HS-based amendments makes them essential for cleaning up contaminated agricultural areas and industrial waste sites, which have dangerous metal levels that threaten both food safety and environmental health.

Sorption of Organic Pollutants and Emerging Contaminants

The adsorption properties of HS enable them to effectively remove hydrophobic and persistent organic pollutants which include polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), pesticides (atrazine and glyphosate), pharmaceutical compounds and additional contaminants found in wastewater as well as contaminated groundwater systems. The sorption mechanisms consist of multiple processes which use hydrophobic contacts to attract nonpolar substances, electrostatic and hydrogen-bonding forces to attract polar substances thus allowing HSs to remove various pollutants with different polarities. For example, advanced studies employing thermally oxidized graphitic carbon nitride (TCN) modified with HS report HA adsorption capacities of up to 327–328 mg/g and FA capacities of 213–214 mg/g, with kinetic data demonstrating both pseudo-second-order chemisorption and intraparticle diffusion mechanisms; these materials show >90% removal efficiency for HS at low environmental concentrations (5–20 mg/L), simulating real surface water conditions. The combination of HS-amended biochar with engineered composite materials creates better sorption properties through the combination of biochar surface area and porosity with HS molecular properties, which results in practical wastewater treatment solutions for both concentrated and widespread water pollution.

Economic and Environmental Aspects of Humic Substance Application and Carbon Footprint Reduction

The use of HS in agriculture leads to better agroecosystem productivity and resistance, while creating economic advantages and environmental sustainability. The present worldwide conditions have made these elements more vital because of climate change, soil degradation, and the requirement for environmentally friendly agricultural practices. The agricultural application of HS creates economic value because it decreases costs for mineral fertilizers and pesticides while producing higher crop yields. Research shows that humic preparation treatment of soils and plants enables farmers to decrease mineral fertilizer usage by 20–30% while maintaining their production levels. The better plant nutrient availability and improved plant nutrient absorption rates led to this outcome. HS helps plants develop natural defense systems, which results in lower expenses for disease and pest management. The application of HA to tomatoes led to a 25% reduction in fungicide costs while farmers achieved a 15% increase in their tomato yields, confirming the economic benefit of using HS as an alternative to traditional agrochemicals. HS serve dual purposes in agriculture by promoting plant development and enhancing soil quality for sustainable farming practices while showing promise for solving major environmental problems that affect ecosystem recovery. The implementation of HS technology helps decrease the need for synthetic fertilizers, which produce nitrous oxide (N2O) emissions that contribute to greenhouse gas effects. The European Union implemented HS in organic farming, which resulted in a 25% decrease of N2O emissions and a 10–15% boost in crop production. HS stabilize organic carbon in the soil by slowing its mineralization and release as CO2, thereby contributing to the reduction of greenhouse gas emissions. Soil conservation stands as a critical practice because it protects the planet’s largest organic carbon storage, which exists in soil ecosystems. The study shows that Brazilian no-till farming systems, which use HS for treatment will increase their soil organic carbon content by 12% during five years thus storing 1.5 tons of CO2 per hectare annually.

The stable chelate complexes between HS and heavy metal cations, such as lead (Pb), cadmium (Cd), copper (Cu), zinc (Zn), nickel (Ni), and mercury (Hg), allow these compounds to effectively keep harmful metals from leaking into groundwater by keeping them in the soil. In general, the stability of these organometal complexes follows the Irving–Williams series (Cu > Pb > Zn > Cd). HA, on the other hand, have a strong affinity for copper and lead because they have several binding sites for carboxyl and phenolic hydroxyl groups. , The formation of these complexes decreases heavy metal availability for plant and microbial organisms which results in reduced toxic effects on plants and soil biogeochemical processes. The remediation of contaminated soils and the reduction of agrochemical environmental risks become possible through the use of HS as an effective tool. HS enhances the efficiency of water and nutrient use by plants, which reduces the demand for irrigation and fertilizer application. The implementation of these practices results in lower energy consumption for agrochemical production, distribution, and irrigation system management, which produces agricultural operations with lower carbon emissions. The method supports a low-carbon economy transition through its ability to decrease agricultural carbon emissions. Thus, the environmental problems of heavy metal soil contamination and soil fertility decline can be addressed through the use of HS as an effective solutions. These substances enable farmers to decrease environmental damage from human activities, while boosting their harvests and preserving sustainable farming systems for extended periods.

Limitations and Challenges in the Application of Humic Substances

Despite significant advances in the research and practical application of HS in agriculture, their widespread adoption faces a number of limitations and challenges. A complete solution must be developed to achieve safe and economically viable operation of HS systems. One of the most significant problems associated with the application of HS is their high variability, which depends on the source, extraction method, and processing, as well as climatic and soil conditions. The chemical structure of HS from peat, lignin, composts, and biowaste materials shows wide variations because of their different molecular weights, functional groups and biological properties. Research shows that HA obtained from peat possess different properties than those produced from lignin or compost materials, and in arid regions or on soils with high clay content, the effect of HS may be less pronounced. The standardization of humic preparations becomes challenging because of the different components that create variability and heterogeneity in these preparations. The diverse conditions make it challenging to predict the effectiveness of the humic preparations. Characterizing and classifying HS poses a distinct analytical challenge due to two interrelated factors: inherent source-based heterogeneity and variations in methodological sensitivity. To begin with, HS from various environmental sources has chemical compositions that are truly different. Elemental analyses of sedimentary HA from different sources show that the compositions can vary widely. For example, carbon can make up 8.26 to 15.27% of the acids, hydrogen can make up 1.30 to 2.89%, nitrogen can make up 0.65–1.21%, and oxygen can make up 12.62–25.41%. These differences are due to differences in the plant precursor material, soil conditions, and humification pathways. Second, different analytical methods used to characterize HS (UV–vis, FTIR, and 1H and 13C NMR) measure different structural parameters and give different results for the same HS samples. For more than 40 years, the International Humic Substances Society (IHSS) has kept standardized reference materials to make it easier to compare results. However, even IHSS standards show differences when different analytical methods are used, which shows that HS are not real polymers but rather are supramolecular assemblies. So, the problem with comparing HS data from different studies is that there is real compositional heterogeneity and that different analytical methods are complementary. The exact molecular processes that cause HS have not been fully explained by scientists. The complete understanding of their behavior with plant cell membranes and their effects on gene expression and signaling pathways also remains unclear. Scientists face an insurmountable challenge because they lack sufficient understanding of HS behavior, which prevents them from creating products with specific performance characteristics and developing proper HS application methods.

Scientists need more research to establish the environmental safety and long-term effectiveness of HS because current knowledge remains insufficient. Research shows HS enhances plant development, soil conditions, and plant resistance to stress, but scientists need additional research to determine its permanent impact on natural environments. The current research lacks sufficient information about HS effects on soil microbial populations, and nutrient processing systems, and prolonged organic carbon storage. HS application creates conditions that enable pathogenic microorganisms to multiply while damaging beneficial microbial populations which negatively affects soil health. Therefore, research needs to study HS effects on soil organic matter stability and greenhouse gas emissions at a deeper level. The environmental safety of HS needs continuous monitoring because it stands as a vital requirement. The combination of heavy metals and toxic organic compounds in industrial waste-derived humic preparations creates environmental and human health threats. Research extending over time needs to evaluate adverse effects, which include soil pollution accumulation and plant contamination under different soil environments and climate zones. The research should investigate new material sources, including agricultural waste and algae.

The implementation of HS-related technologies requires organizations to spend significant funds at the beginning of their deployment process. The implementation of HS technology for agricultural production requires substantial initial expenses to achieve its expected economic advantages. The production costs of humic preparations, along with standardization expenses, transportation fees, farmer training, and technology adoption expenses result in this situation. In developing countries with limited resources, this can pose a major barrier to the use of HS. The expenses for acquiring high-quality humic preparations from environmentally safe sources exceed the costs of conventional fertilizers. This makes them less accessible to smallholder farms, which constitute a substantial part of the agricultural sector in many regions of the world. The creation of budget-friendly production techniques for HS from agricultural waste and composts represents a vital milestone to decrease production expenses. The solution to this problem needs farmers and agronomists to grasp how HS benefits their operations through the correct application methods.

Conclusion

Soil degradation exists as a major problem, but human involvement does not always cause this issue. Natural ecosystem fluctuations occur across different environments, while soil recovery takes place automatically without human intervention. Soil fertility decline operates as a short environmental process because natural systems maintain their built-in environmental management systems. Furthermore, A thorough evaluation of soil degradation effects on worldwide temperature increase and food price escalation needs to consider various elements, which include economic frameworks, political institutions, and technological progress. The commercial value of HA remains high because it has multiple uses in different production industries. The natural process of HS formation takes a long time to produce substances with diverse structures that make their development challenging. The content of HA extractable from various natural carbonaceous materials is limited and dependent on their region of origin. The natural fluctuations in HA production make it impossible to meet the worldwide market requirements. The production of these materials depends on nonrenewable fossil raw materials. The rise in production has resulted in declining natural resource reserves and increased difficulties in locating premium raw materials. Researchers continue to face an important challenge in developing new methods to produce synthetic HA in high yields while achieving fast reaction times. The implementation of HS for soil remediation shows promise, but these methods do not always represent the best solution for all cases. Agronomic practices, such as crop rotation and organic farming methods, may prove to be more sustainable and less costly. It is important to consider alternative approaches to soil restoration that may be more effective in the long term.
