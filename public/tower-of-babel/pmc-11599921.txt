The expansion mechanism of expansive surrounding rock in Guiyang City, China
Authors: Chenggui Wu, Junying Rao, Xing Peng, Chaoying Chen, Yi Chen, Hongchao Cui
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Expansive surrounding rock, such as mudstone, poses significant risks to supporting structures like tunnel linings due to its tendency to expand. To explore the expansion mechanism of expansive surrounding rock and address underground engineering challenges associated with it, this study, based on the first phase of Guiyang Metro Line S1, samples expansive surrounding rock from the section and conducts basic physical tests, X-ray diffraction (XRD), and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analyses on the samples. The influence of water content (0%, 2.93%, 6.84%, and 8.20%) on the mechanical properties of mudstone, as well as its expansion characteristics under natural conditions, were examined. Results indicate that the microscopic surface of mudstone is rough, with cracks distributed around pores. These elements interconnect to form natural water channels, with clay minerals and non-clay minerals comprising 47.2% and 52.8% of the sample, respectively. Mudstone contains multiple pores that gradually develop as water content increases. Mudstone expansion significantly reduces its compressive strength. When lateral constraints are applied, mudstone exhibits significantly greater expansion compared to axial and radial changes. When mudstone encounters water, the expansion pressure fluctuates significantly. Upon water absorption following dehydration, cracks in the rock sample develop rapidly, accelerating collapse. These findings offer valuable insights for the construction and design of tunnels in expansive surrounding rock formations within karst areas.

Introduction

Due to its unique mineral composition, expansive surrounding rock contains numerous cracks, readily absorbs water, swells and softens, and easily disintegrates upon dehydration1,2. This leads to simultaneous expansion and extrusion within tunnels3, accompanied by a series of physical and chemical reactions4. Expansive mudstone occurs worldwide, including in locations such as the Lilla Tunnel in Spain5, the Chienberg Tunnel in Switzerland6, Baden-Wurttemberg in Germany7 and the Texas Tunnel in the United States8. These mudstones are prone to incidents like subsidence, landslides, longitudinal cracks, and collapses, posing significant risks to construction safety and progress9–12. Consequently, they are often referred to as “engineering cancer”. China has one of the most widespread distributions of expansive mudstone13–15, affecting over 20 provinces.

Constructing on expansive mudstone presents a significant challenge. Clay minerals in expansive mudstone are the primary factors influencing the mechanical strength of the rock mass. When water infiltrates the voids within the rock mass, the binding force between clay minerals weakens, further reducing the rock mass’s overall mechanical strength16. Higher clay mineral content in mudstone makes the rock mass prone to disintegration upon water absorption17. The mechanical properties of the rock mass are closely tied to moisture content, which affects pore and crack formation. Mudstone anisotropy increases as water content decreases18, and in a saturated state, the rock mass’s compressive and tensile strengths decrease significantly19. Unsaturated expansion tests reveal that as saturation increases, mudstone strength and stiffness decrease significantly, posing substantial risks to tunnel stability20. Additionally, the swelling properties of expansive mudstone are affected by wet-dry cycles. Throughout wet-dry and freeze-thaw cycles, expansion pressure and strain decrease as the cycle period extends. This phenomenon aids in predicting ultimate expansion stress and strain from seasonal changes; however, these cycles also lead to a gradual decline in the strength and stiffness of expansive mudstone21,22. Numerous cyclic load tests demonstrate that lime treatment effectively reduces clay mineral cohesion, thereby mitigating their expansive effects23.

The properties of expansive mudstone can be examined from multiple perspectives. Swelling can be considered a coupling process between humidity and mechanics, providing a theoretical basis for understanding tunnel uplift under high humidity conditions24, thus enabling exploration of the tunnel’s time-varying behavior. Studies on mechanical properties show that the cohesion of reshaped sandstone, tested in situ, has a quadratic relationship with external pressure25. Expansive force tests on reshaped mudstone reveal a clear correlation between swelling force, dry density, and initial water content under micro-deformation conditions26. In lateral unconfined expansive tests, the compressive strength of weakly swelling mudstone is influenced by saturation27. Additionally, hydraulic property studies using data fitting establish a relationship between the rock mass’s mechanical strength and water content, estimating its sensitivity to water changes. Based on swelling changes from water penetration in mudstone cracks, a decoupling analysis method for mudstone swelling is proposed28. A stress-seepage damage coupling model is constructed to analyze mudstone’s evolution, focusing on damage development and permeability changes29.

Differences in swelling potential of expansive mudstone vary across regions. Located in central Guizhou, the Guiyang area features karst landforms with complex underground hydrological conditions, creating a favorable environment for the development of expansive soils. However, few studies have examined the expansion characteristics of surrounding rocks under these geological conditions. Therefore, this study investigates the expansion mechanism of expansive mudstone along the geological section of Guiyang Metro Line S1. Currently, research on expansive mudstone mainly relies on numerical simulation methods, lacking empirical verification and experimental validation. Moreover, significant property differences exist between reshaped samples and original mudstone, complicating accurate reflection of the original mudstone’s behavior. Therefore, mechanical and indoor expansion characteristic tests on on-site rock samples are essential to reveal the mechanical properties and expansion mechanism of expansive surrounding rock in Guiyang, providing valuable references for underground engineering in karst areas.

Microstructural properties test

To explore the expansion characteristics of mudstone in Guiyang, it is essential to identify the composition and proportion of each component. This includes observing the surface characteristics of the rock’s microstructure, examining the arrangement of structural surfaces, and analyzing the distribution, shape, and size of pores and cracks.

Engineering background

The first phase of the Guiyang Metro S1 Line extends between Wangchengpo Station and Huanghe North Road Station. The starting and ending mileage of the tunnel in this section are YDK38 + 410.668 to YDK40 + 396.387. Preliminary geological exploration revealed rock mass expansion in the tunnel’s surrounding rock, as shown in Fig. 1. A total of 37 rock samples were collected for testing in the study section. Based on the cation exchange capacity (NH4+), montmorillonite content, and powder-free expansion rate of the field mudstone, it was classified as weakly and moderately expansive rock. Expansive surrounding rock constitutes 81.08% of the study section, with weakly expansive rock comprising 70.27%.

SEM test

Small rock samples with relatively flat upper and lower cross sections from various locations in the study area were selected for sampling. The samples were divided into four groups based on the presence of obvious joint structures: S1, S2, S3, and S4. S1 and S2 had no obvious joints, while S3 and S4 displayed distinct joint structures. The joint surfaces of S3 and S4 were designated as the experimental observation surfaces. To enhance imaging quality and ensure the performance of scanning electron microscopy (SEM) due to the poor electrical conductivity of mudstone, a conductive metal coating (gold) with an approximate thickness of 10 nm was uniformly applied to the sample surface. The coating process was performed using a HITACHI E-1010 ion sputter coater (Manufacturer: Hitachi, Japan). The coated samples were then mounted on an SEM specimen stage using conductive carbon tape to ensure good electrical contact and prevent sample movement during testing. Under high vacuum conditions, SEM analysis was conducted using a HITACHI S-3400 N scanning electron microscope (Manufacturer: Hitachi, Japan). The operating voltage was set to 15 kV, and the microscope was calibrated to ensure imaging accuracy. During the analysis, the samples were scanned at various magnifications ranging from 500× to 5000×, focusing on the microstructural characteristics of expansive mudstone, including crack morphology, particle arrangement, and surface texture. XRD test

X-rays, with their strong penetrating power, were used to determine the material composition and proportion of mudstone in the area. Expansive mudstone samples from different locations in the study area were labeled X1 and X2. The samples were placed in an oven at a temperature of 105 °C and dried for 24 h to completely remove moisture. The dried samples were then ground into fine powder capable of passing through a 200-mesh sieve to ensure sample uniformity and improve diffraction accuracy. The sieved powder was gently compacted into the sample holder, ensuring a flat surface flush with the edges of the holder. The prepared sample holder was mounted onto the XRD sample stage, ensuring proper alignment with the X-ray beam path of the diffractometer. XRD analysis was performed using a Rigaku Ultima IV X-ray diffractometer (Manufacturer: Rigaku Corporation, Japan), equipped with a Cu Kα1 radiation source (wavelength λ = 1.54059 Å). The measurement parameters were set as follows: scanning range of 5° to 90° (2θ), step size of 0.02°, and scan speed of 1°/min. These optimized parameters ensured precise detection of diffraction peaks, particularly in the low-angle region, which is critical for identifying clay minerals. The diffraction data were recorded and subsequently analyzed using Jade 6 software, enabling the identification of mineral phases and the calculation of the relative proportions of crystalline components. The XRD test procedure is illustrated in Fig. 4.

Indoor tests and test plans

Mechanical properties test

Investigate the effects of water content and confining pressure on the mechanical strength of rock samples.

The test was conducted using the RMT-301 Rock Mechanics Test System (Manufacturer: Wuhan Institute of Rock and Soil Mechanics, Chinese Academy of Sciences, China). The system has a maximum vertical load capacity of 1000kN and a maximum horizontal load capacity of 500kN, with interchangeable loading heads and supports to accommodate various mechanical testing requirements.

The rock samples were placed in an oven, with the temperature initially set to 65 °C and maintained for 12 h, followed by a gradual increase to 105 °C, where they were kept for at least 12 h until fully dried. During the drying process, the samples were weighed every 3 h to prepare test groups of rock samples with moisture content ranging between the dry and natural states. As expansive mudstone may disintegrate when directly immersed in water, a spray curing method was used to increase the moisture content of rock samples to levels higher than their natural moisture content. A spray bottle was used to evenly mist the surface of the samples from a distance of approximately 50 cm. The spray volume was controlled to form a uniform fine mist layer, after which the samples were left to stand for 15 min to allow the water to penetrate the sample interior. The samples were then placed in a sealed curing container with a damp sponge to maintain humidity. After 12 h, the samples were removed and weighed. This process was repeated as needed until the target moisture content was achieved.

According to the test objectives, four groups of rock samples with average moisture contents of 0%, 2.93%, 6.84%, and 8.20% were selected for uniaxial compressive strength (UCS) tests. The sample information is presented in Table 3, and all samples were cylindrical. The UCS test method was employed to evaluate the compressive strength of rock samples under different moisture conditions. The maximum vertical load applied during the test was 100kN, with displacement control at a loading rate of 0.002 mm/s.During the test, the rock samples were placed between the loading head and the support of the testing machine, ensuring stable installation. The test system was then initiated, and a constant loading rate was applied until the samples failed.

Additionally, triaxial compression tests were conducted on rock samples in their natural state under five different confining pressure levels: 0 MPa, 0.5 MPa, 1 MPa, 1.5 MPa, and 2 MPa. The sample information is presented in Table 4. The axial load was applied using a force-controlled loading method, with a confining pressure application rate of 0.05 MPa/s and an axial load application rate of 0.5 MPa/s. During the test, the rock samples were placed in the testing system, and the confining pressure was gradually increased to the target levels according to the predefined settings. Under the applied confining pressure, axial loading was performed until the rock samples failed. The testing process for the samples is illustrated in Fig. 5.

Free expansion characteristic test

Mudstone expands in both axial and radial directions when exposed to water in its natural state. Understanding the expansion characteristics of rock samples is essential for investigating their swelling mechanisms. The test was conducted using an SC-55 Rock Free Swelling Rate Tester (Manufacturer: Beijing Zhongke Luda Testing Instruments Co., Ltd., China). The main components of the apparatus include five dial gauges, metal permeable plates, water container, support frame, dial gauge holders, radial deformation transmission rods, a base, and metal plate, as shown in Fig. 6.

Fig. 6SC-55 rock free swelling rate tester.

SC-55 rock free swelling rate tester.

Natural-state rock samples were selected for the test, with detailed sample information provided in Table 5. Using a marker pen, right-angle symmetric centerlines were drawn on the upper and lower surfaces and around the sides of each sample to ensure alignment during installation. A metal permeable plate was placed at the center of the bottom of the water container, and the rock sample was positioned on top of it. The sample was adjusted according to the centerlines, and additional metal permeable plates and a metal plate were placed on top of the sample. Dial gauges were mounted on the dial gauge holders, ensuring that the four lateral dial gauges were coaxial with the radial deformation transmission rods. The top dial gauge was fixed on a support frame and positioned vertically to act on the cross-point of the metal plate. After installation, all dial gauges were zeroed. Before adding water, dial gauge readings were recorded every 10 min. When three consecutive readings showed no variation, the value was recorded as the initial reading. Water was then slowly added to the container until the water level submerged the upper permeable plate. During the first hour after adding water, dial gauge readings were recorded every 10 min. Starting from the second hour, the recording interval was adjusted to 1 h. When the deformation difference between three consecutive readings was less than 0.001 mm, the deformation was considered stable. The test duration was maintained at no less than 48 h, and the water level was kept constant throughout the test. The free expansion test procedure is illustrated in Fig. 7.

Lateral restraint test

The lateral swelling ratio reflects the deformation characteristics and stability of rock under lateral confinement. The test was conducted using an HYC-I Rock Lateral Confinement Swelling Tester (manufactured by Beijing Zhongke Luda Testing Instruments Co., Ltd., China). The main components of the apparatus include a water container, support frame, 5 kPa metal loading block, dial gauges, base, platform, metal ring, permeable plates, filter paper, and a timer, as shown in Fig. 8.

Fig. 8HYC-I rock lateral confining expansion tester.

HYC-I rock lateral confining expansion tester.

Standard rock samples in their natural state were selected for the test, with detailed sample information provided in Table 6. The samples were required to remain intact, free from cracks and visible defects. A thin layer of petroleum jelly was evenly applied to the inner wall of the metal ring to ensure proper sealing. The metal ring was placed on the base, followed by the sequential placement of a metal permeable plate and filter paper. The rock sample was then placed inside the metal ring, with additional filter paper and a metal permeable plate placed on top of the sample. The metal ring containing the rock sample and base was then placed in the water container. A 5 kPa metal loading block was placed on top of the rock sample to provide sustained pressure. A dial gauge was mounted vertically on top of the metal loading block and zeroed. Before adding water, dial gauge readings were recorded every 10 min. When three consecutive readings showed no variation, the value was recorded as the initial reading. Water was slowly added to the container until the water level submerged the upper permeable plate. During the first hour after water addition, dial gauge readings were recorded every 10 min. Starting from the second hour, readings were taken at hourly intervals. When the deformation difference between three consecutive readings was less than 0.001 mm, the swelling was considered stable.

The total test duration was no less than 48 h, and the water level was maintained constant throughout the test. The lateral constrained expansion test procedure is illustrated in Fig. 9.

Expansion pressure test

The expansion pressure reflects the evolutionary process within mudstone, with changes in internal pore pressure being the primary cause of swelling. Recording these characteristic changes is crucial for studying the swelling mechanism. The rock expansion pressure test was conducted using a YP-II Rock Swelling Pressure Tester (Manufacturer: Beijing Zhongke Luda Testing Instruments Co., Ltd., China). The main components of the instrument include a loading axis, support frame, movable beam, dial gauge, base, pressure gauge, casing, force sensor, and water container, as shown in Fig. 10.

Fig. 10YP-II Rock swelling pressure tester.

YP-II Rock swelling pressure tester.

Rock samples in their natural state were selected, with detailed sample information provided in Table 7. The inner wall of the casing was evenly coated with petroleum jelly and placed in the water container. Inside the casing, a metal permeable plate and filter paper were sequentially placed, followed by the rock sample. Another layer of filter paper and a metal permeable plate were then placed on top of the sample.

The pressure gauge and dial gauge were installed, and the casing was adjusted to align with the central axis of the loading axis. The loading axis was gradually adjusted to apply an initial pressure of 0.01 MPa to the rock sample. Dial gauge readings were recorded every 10 min. When three consecutive readings showed no variation, the value was recorded as the initial reading. Water was slowly added to the container until the upper permeable plate was fully submerged.

During the test, if the dial gauge showed a deformation greater than 0.001 mm, the loading axis was adjusted to maintain the rock sample’s height constant. After each adjustment, readings from the pressure gauge and dial gauge were recorded at 10-min intervals. When the difference between three consecutive dial gauge readings was less than 0.001 mm, the recording interval was adjusted to 1 h. If three consecutive readings at this interval still showed a difference less than 0.001 mm, the sample was considered to have reached a stable state. The entire test duration was no less than 48 h. The test procedure is illustrated in Fig. 11.

Water immersion test

In karst regions, climatic conditions significantly influence the swelling behavior of mudstone, with distinct differences in swelling under varying wet and dry conditions. To investigate the swelling mechanism of mudstone under dry and normal humidity conditions, a water immersion test was designed. Two groups of mudstone samples were selected.

Dry samples: Rock samples were dried to a constant weight in an oven at 105 °C to prepare dry mudstone samples. Normal humidity samples: Rock samples in their natural state.

Test results

SEM test result analysis

The micromorphological characteristics of mudstone without prominent joint structures are illustrated in Figs. 13 and 14. Under 500x magnification, the surface appears relatively rough, with particles of various sizes distributed throughout the sample. Pore diameters range from 10 to 50 μm, while crack lengths predominantly fall between 300 and 1200 μm. Particle contacts exhibit a range of interactions, including point-to-point, point-to-line, line-to-line, and surface-to-surface. These diverse contact types facilitate the formation of cracks and pores. At 5000x magnification, numerous clusters of clastic particles, along with blocky, curved, sheet-like structures stacked together, are visible, collectively forming the fundamental skeleton of the mudstone.

The micromorphological characteristics of mudstone with joint structures are depicted in Figs. 15 and 16. Within the sample’s microstructure, particles exhibit consistent contact modes: point-point, point-line, line-line, and surface-surface. Compared to S1 and S2, the ravines on the surfaces of S3 and S4 are deeper, with fewer clastic particles and voids but exhibiting more cracks with greater penetration. Crack widths range between 10 μm and 30 μm, with lengths spanning 700 μm to 1500 μm. The structural framework of the sample predominantly consists of stacked flat sheets and flat sheet frameworks.

The presence of joints in S3 and S4 results in larger structural cracks, potentially facilitating the accumulation of clay minerals in the mudstone, thereby promoting expansion. As a result, this can reduce the mudstone’s bearing capacity. In S1 and S2, numerous small-diameter clastic particle clusters are scattered throughout. This dense accumulation of granule clusters inhibits the development of cracks. Consequently, the adhesive minerals illite, kaolinite, and chlorite aggregate in the mudstone, and the presence of cracks and pores further enhances the expansibility of the mudstone.

XRD test result analysis

The XRD test diffraction pattern is shown in Fig. 17. Through XRD tests, the test results show that the mudstone in this area is mainly composed of non-clay minerals (hematite, calcite, quartz, dolomite, plagioclase, etc.) and clay minerals (montmorillonite, glauconite, kaolinite, dikaline Stone, illite, etc.), the proportion of each mineral content is shown in Table 9.

The average clay mineral content in the mudstone in this area is as high as 47.2%, predominantly consisting of illite (KAl2(Si3Al)O10(OH)2), kaolinite (Al2(Si2O5)(OH)4), and chlorite ((Mg, Fe, Al)6(Si, Al)4O10(OH)8), which account for 31.75%, 7.95%, and 7.50% respectively. The average non-clay mineral content is 50.8%, primarily comprising quartz (SiO2), calcite (CaCO3), plagioclase (Na(AlSi3O8), hematite (Fe2O3), and dolomite (CaMg(CO3)2). As detailed in Table 9, their average contents are 34.1%, 9.95%, 6.15%, 2.05%, and 0.55% respectively.

Illite, kaolinite, and chlorite all have a layered structure, resulting in relatively large interlayer spacing and weak interactions between the layers, primarily due to van der Waals forces. When water molecules enter the interlayer space, adsorption forces increase the spacing, resulting in mineral expansion. This expansion occurs due to interactions between water molecules and the illite surface. These interactions increase the distance between illite layers, thus increasing the overall volume.

Illite’s hydration capacity is between that of montmorillonite and kaolinite (or chlorite). However, the high illite content in the study area’s mudstone imparts significant expansive properties.

Analysis of water immersion test results

Unlike the previously mentioned expansion test, the dried state of mudstone in karst areas differs significantly from its natural state. The water immersion processes for the oven-dried and natural samples are shown in Figs. 31 and 32, respectively. No visible cracks were present in the oven-dried sample prior to water immersion; however, upon immersion, the cracks expanded rapidly. At 5 min, the left side of the sample collapsed; at 10 min, it had disintegrated into large pieces; by 120 min, the sample had collapsed further and scattered, with morphological changes stabilizing by 240 min. When observed from the side, the sample consisted of powder and 10 mm blocks, primarily small particles and fragments.

The morphological changes in natural mudstone differed significantly from those in dry mudstone. Microcracks appeared in the natural sample after approximately 20 min of water immersion. As the cracks slowly developed, they penetrated the rock sample over a period of approximately 60 min, forming new microcracks. By 360 min, the natural sample had broken into blocks of various sizes due to the cracks. Although the rock sample continued to absorb water afterward, it did not disintegrate further.

Comparing the morphological changes of the two, the dried sample began to disintegrate after 5 min. The disintegration speed was faster, the volume of the disintegrated rock blocks was smaller, and the degree of disintegration was more complete.

Discussion

The expansion of mudstone primarily depends on the degree of rock fissure development, clay mineral content, natural moisture content, intergranular cement, and cement type. Expansive mudstone exhibits a higher degree of consolidation compared to other clay-rich rocks. Natural moisture content significantly affects its strength and expansion characteristics.

In the previously mentioned expansion test, the rock sample’s expansion primarily occurred within the first hour. As water infiltrates the interior of the rock sample, it reacts with clay minerals, gradually slowing the expansion trend. After 5 h, the reaction is essentially complete, and expansion stabilizes. The water immersion test also demonstrated that as the rock sample loses water, the bonding ability between viscous mineral layers decreases, which enlarges internal cracks and increases the contact area between viscous minerals and water. If suddenly immersed in a large amount of water, the hydration reaction accelerates significantly, rapidly reducing the sample’s compressive resistance. Consequently, the dry sample exhibited a tendency to collapse after only 5 min of water exposure.

The clay minerals in the regional mudstone consist of montmorillonite (generally lower content), illite (higher content), kaolinite, and chlorite. Rock expansion is closely related to the enrichment levels of montmorillonite and illite. Compared to kaolinite and chlorite, the Na+ or Ca2+ ions in montmorillonite’s lattice structure can be replaced by water molecules, forming hydrated ions. As water molecules increase within the mineral, their interaction with the ions expands the lattice structure, increasing its volume. Illite differs slightly from montmorillonite; its lattice structure contains pores. When water molecules enter these pores and form hydrated ions, the lattice structure expands. Additionally, illite’s lattice structure is layered, allowing water molecules to move between the layers and further promote expansion. However, montmorillonite has a much greater expansion potential than illite.

Figure 33 illustrates the mechanism of shrinkage due to water loss and expansion due to water absorption in swelling mudstone. When mudstone encounters water, the water infiltrates the interior of the rock mass through existing cracks and contacts the clay minerals. The clay minerals absorb the water, causing an increase in volume and generating interaction forces between particles. If intergranular cementing is insufficient, the spacing between particles increases, allowing the clay minerals to fully absorb water. This results in the formation of a thicker water film, further enhancing mudstone expansion. Conversely, if the cementing between clay mineral particles is too strong, water absorption is limited, resulting in relatively minor expansion. When mudstone loses water, its particles shrink, increasing the rock’s susceptibility to cracking.

Fig. 33Expansion mechanism diagram of expansive claystone.

Expansion mechanism diagram of expansive claystone.

The test results indicate that the clay minerals in the expansile mudstone from the study section are primarily illite (31.75%), chlorite (7.95%), and kaolinite (7.50%). The development of internal cracks and pores in the rock provides the fundamental conditions for mudstone expansion. However, due to the absence of montmorillonite and the high consolidation of the rock mass, its expansion characteristics are not pronounced. When the mudstone is disturbed, secondary cracks develop further, and upon encountering water, its expansion characteristics are enhanced.

Conclusions

This paper focuses on the mudstone from the first phase of the Guiyang Metro S1 Line as the research subject. The rock samples underwent water content testing, SEM analysis, XRD analysis, mechanical strength testing, and expansion characteristics testing. The study emphasizes exploring the structure, expansion characteristics, and behavior of mudstone in its natural water-containing state. The main findings are as follows:
