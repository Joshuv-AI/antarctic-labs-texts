Effect of integrated medicines management on quality of discharge medication information—a secondary endpoint in a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Liv Mathiesen, Tram Bich Michelle Nguyen, Ingrid Dæhlen, Morten Mowé, Marianne Lea
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHigh-quality discharge information is important to promote patient safety when patients are transferred from hospital to primary care. Patients with multiple long-term conditions are especially vulnerable to insufficient transfer of medication information, as they use many medications and have complex interactions with the healthcare services. The aim of this study was to investigate the effect of integrated medicines management provided to hospitalized multimorbid patients on the quality of the discharge medication information.MethodsMultimorbid patients ≥18 years, using a minimum of four regular medications from a minimum of two therapeutic medication classes were recruited from the Internal Medicine ward, Oslo University Hospital, Norway, from August 2014 to March 2016 and randomly assigned, 1:1, to the intervention or control group. Intervention patients received integrated medicines management throughout the hospital stay. Control patients received standard care. This paper reports the results of a prespecified secondary endpoint analysis of the randomized controlled trial; the difference between the intervention and control group in the quality of discharge medication information.ResultsThe analysis population comprised 384 patients. Integrated medicines management improved the discharge summary quality score of the medication information from 5.8 ± 1.5 to 8.6 ± 2.6 [mean difference 2.7, 95% confidence interval (CI) 2.3–3.2, P < .001]. In total, 171 intervention patients (89%) received a patient discharge information letter, compared to 66 control patients (35%), P < .001. The quality score of the medication information in the patient discharge information letter was improved from 6.0 ± 1.8 in the control group to 10.0 ± 1.3 in the intervention group (mean difference 4.0, 95% CI 3.6–4.4, P < .001).ConclusionIntegrated medicines management delivered to multimorbid patients during a hospital stay improved the quality of the medication information in discharge summaries as well as patient discharge information letters and ensured that a discharge information letter in lay language was prepared for almost all patients who were involved in the management of their medications after discharge.Keywords: integrated medicines management, hospital discharge, discharge information, discharge summary, care transition, patient safety

Introduction

The discharge summary is the most important tool for conveying written information to the next level of care when patients are discharged from the hospital [1, 2]. Concerning medications, the discharge summary should contain complete information about all medications, including any changes and reasons for these [3, 4]. Discharge summaries are, however, often insufficient in content and form, among other things lacking information regarding both medications and diagnoses [5–7]. Insufficient transfer of medication information might cause medication errors [8]. These errors can result in clinically relevant negative patient outcomes such as adverse drug events (ADEs), early readmissions after discharge, and use of healthcare resources [5]. Poor communication and information transfer at hospital discharge are common, and transfer of care between healthcare levels is known to be a threat to patient safety [8].

Patients with multiple long-term conditions constitute a vulnerable group, as living with several conditions is associated with the use of a high number of medicines, a high risk of drug-related problems, more complex interactions with the healthcare services, failure of delivery and coordination of healthcare, and an increased risk of low-quality discharge information [9–12]. Managing patients with multiple long-term conditions is acknowledged as an important challenge to patient safety, and one of the greatest challenges to the healthcare system [10].

Integrated medicines management (IMM) was developed as a systematic approach to optimize and individualize medication therapy during a hospital stay [13]. It comprises three steps covering important stages of a patient’s hospital stay; medicines reconciliation at admission, medicines reviews during the stay, and medicines reconciliation and information at discharge [13]. Our research group has conducted a randomized controlled trial (RCT) of the effect of IMM to hospitalized patients with multiple long-term conditions, on readmissions and survival [14]. IMM had no statistically significant effect on the composite endpoint time to readmission or death, but a statistically significant effect on overall survival. The third step of the IMM intervention was medicines reconciliation and information at discharge. This included providing systematic, written information comprising a reconciled medication list with descriptions and justifications for all changes made during the hospital stay. The medication list was prepared in the digital medical record by a study pharmacist, and the attending physician was to implement the list into the discharge summary. In addition, a patient discharge information letter containing similar information as in the discharge summary, but in lay language, was prepared.

Previous studies on clinical pharmacists’ involvement in discharge management and care transitions, have shown improvement in the quality of medication information and reduced the frequency of medication discrepancies [15–19]. Only one of the studies involved the use of IMM [16]. To our knowledge on this research topic, no one has used the randomized, controlled design and no one studied the vulnerable group of patients with multiple long-term conditions.

The aim of the study reported in the current paper was to investigate the effect of IMM provided to hospitalized patients with multiple long-term conditions on the quality of the medication information at discharge. This was included as a predefined, process-related secondary endpoint in the study protocol of the RCT, published as a supplementary file to a previous publication [14].

Methods

The details of the design of this parallel-group RCT have been described in a previous publication [14]. Hospitalized patients were recruited from the internal medicine ward of Oslo University Hospital (Ullevaal), Norway in the period from August 2014 to March 2016. Inclusion criteria were: acute admission, age ≥18 years, and use of at least four regular medications from a minimum of two therapy classes (Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical at first level) at admission. As drug counts were considered a more reliable measure than disease counts at hospital admission, this was chosen as the preferred multimorbidity measure. Exclusion criteria were being terminally ill, isolated due to severe infections, unable to communicate in Norwegian or English (if no translator was available) or having been previously included in the study. All patients or next of kin gave written informed consent before inclusion.

Permuted block randomization was used to distribute patients to the intervention or the control group (1:1). A clinical pharmacist was added to the patient’s multidisciplinary treatment team in the intervention group. The clinical pharmacist performed medicines reconciliation and review at admission, repeated medicines reviews during the entire hospital stay and medicines reconciliation and tailored medicines information at discharge, according to IMM [14]. Six clinical pharmacists collected data and delivered the intervention. These pharmacists are throughout the rest of the paper referred to as the study pharmacists. All study pharmacists had a master’s degree in clinical pharmacy and training in IMM. All steps of the intervention were standardized using IMM procedures adapted to the Norwegian hospital setting.

The control group received standard care from other healthcare professionals at the hospital ward, but not IMM or any other service from clinical pharmacists. Importantly, this means that the ward staff did not have access to a medication list reconciled by a clinical pharmacist for these patients. It was not feasible to blind participants, study pharmacists, or ward physicians to group allocation, but the study was blinded in all steps possible to blind. An external center was responsible for generating the randomization procedure and providing this in closed envelopes, and the study pharmacists were blinded to block size. Furthermore, although ward staff knew which patients belonged to the intervention group, they were unable to distinguish between patients randomized to the control group and patients not included in the study.

Outcome

The quality of the medication information at discharge includes the quality of both the medicines list in the discharge summary and in the patient discharge information letter, as well as the proportion of patients who the patient discharge information letter was prepared for.

The main endpoint analyzed in this paper was the difference between the intervention and control group in the mean score of the discharge summary, using seven criteria of the National Patient Safety Program, as shown in Table 1 [20]. These seven criteria were similar to criteria covered in previous studies [6, 9, 15, 17, 21].

Both the discharge summary and the patient discharge information letter were digital and parts of the digital patient record. The discharge summary was the information to the next level of care. The patient discharge information letter was a version of the discharge summary adapted to lay language and should be printed and given to the patient and/or next of kin if they were expected to be involved in the handling of the patient’s medicines after discharge [14].

The maximum score of each criterion in the original National Patient Safety Program varies in terms of some criteria having a maximum score of two and some of three. We found that setting an equal maximum score for each criterion was more appropriate as it would not weight any criterion as more important—and is similar to previous publications [6, 17]. The maximum score for each criterion was set to two points, and each patient’s discharge summary and patient discharge information letter was evaluated. The maximum score was given when the requested information was present for all medications. When the requested information was present for some or none of the medications, one or zero points were given, respectively.

Study team and preparations

The discharge summaries and patient discharge information letters were scored according to the quality criteria by a master’s student in pharmacy (author T.N.) from October 2020 to February 2021. Before this, the master’s student and an experienced clinical pharmacist (author M.L.) piloted the use of the quality criteria on a selection of the included patients until agreement was reached on a detailed guide for how to use the quality criteria.

Sample size and statistical analysis

The sample size was calculated with respect to the primary endpoint of the study, time to readmission, or death [14]. To be included in the analysis for the endpoints reported in the current paper, patients needed to have received a discharge summary, which led to the exclusion of two control patients included in the main efficacy analysis of the study [14]. Mean scores were compared using Student’s t-test, proportions were compared using the chi-squared test, and P-values <.05 were considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed with the Statistical Program for Social Sciences (IBM SPSS) version 29.

We performed an inter-rater reliability analysis to investigate whether the scoring procedures reliably measured the quality of discharge summaries. To do this, an additional, and independent rater rescored 50, randomly selected discharge summaries. Based on these scores and those made by the original rater of the same 50 discharge summaries, we computed the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for the main endpoint [22–24].

When computing the value of ICC, we assumed that the main endpoint scores could be modeled as a two-way random effects model; hence, the coefficient for absolute agreement was computed rather than consistency among scorers. In the notation of Schrout and Fleiss, this corresponds to computing ICC(2,1) [22]. The unit of interest is the scores from a single person scoring the discharge summaries. The 95% confidence interval (CI) was calculated as described by Shrout and Fleiss using the R statistical software with the R-package irr [22, 25, 26].

Ethics

The study was approved by the Privacy Ombudsman at the Oslo University Hospital and the Regional Committee for Medical and Health Research Ethics (2014/704/REK Southeastern D). The study was registered in ClinicalTrials.gov, identifier: NCT02336113.

Results

The analysis population for the discharge summary analysis included 384 patients, 193 in the intervention group and 191 in the control group. The Consort flowchart is included in the previous publication for the primary analysis population comprising 386 patients, 193 in each treatment group [14].

The median age was 79 years (range 23–96) and 207 patients (54%) were women. A total of 252 patients (66%) were discharged to their own homes, and 197 patients (51%) were responsible for handling their medications after discharge. The median number of diseases was 7 (range 2–17) and the median number of regularly used medications was 8 (range 4–19). Baseline characteristics are presented in Table 2. There were no relevant differences between the groups at baseline.

Reliability

The ICC value was 0.789, which corresponds to good agreement [23]. The 95% CI was [0.527, 0.896]. All values in the CI are indicative of moderate to good agreement, which means that the scores used in the study are indeed reliable.

Discussion

Statement of principal findings

IMM delivered to patients with multiple long-term conditions during their hospital stay improved the quality of the medication information at discharge compared to standard care. In the discharge summary, this applied to both the main endpoint using seven criteria, where the scores increased from less than half of the maximum score to two-thirds, and for the additional endpoint using ten. Interestingly, however, the scores in the intervention group imply there is still room for improvement.

Strengths and limitations

The randomized controlled study design is a strength of our study and has not previously been used to study the effect of an intervention on the quality of the medication discharge information. However, due to the nature of the intervention, it was impossible to blind participants, ward physicians, or study pharmacists to group allocation. To limit bias, the study was blinded on all steps possible to blind [14]. Any spillover effect of the intervention to control patients would, in any case, reduce the effect estimate.

A further strength is the use of clinically relevant criteria for evaluation of the quality of the discharge information, which were in accordance with the international guidelines and previous literature [5, 7, 11, 12, 17]. Additionally, the current results showed good reliability. The scores in our control group were also comparable to observational studies on the quality of discharge summaries [7, 12], further validating our results.

The degree to which clinical pharmacy services are developed and integrated in standard care for patients varies between countries. According to a recent survey by the European Association of Hospital Pharmacists only about 40% responded that pharmacists were involved in medication reconciliation at hospital admission or transfer of medicines information when patients move between and within healthcare settings [27]. The results of our study are therefore considered applicable to current international practice.

An important limitation to the study is the fact that medicines discrepancies revealed during baseline assessments in control patients were only registered in the research database, and not discussed in the multidisciplinary treatment team [14]. Hence, the hospital ward staff did not have access to the study pharmacists’ reconciled medication list at hospital admission. This hindered an assessment of whether changes made to the patient’s treatment during the hospital stay were appropriately covered in the medication discharge summary. Thus, the primary endpoint reported in the current paper reflects the quality of the way the medication information is formatted, and not whether the treatment is optimal. The effect of IMM on the quality of the patients’ medication treatment, including the correctness of the medication list in the discharge summary, is however covered the main efficacy study [14].

Another limitation is that this was a single-center study, which might challenge the generalizability of the results. However, the limited exclusion criteria leading to the inclusion of a broad population and low drop-out rate both contribute favourably to external validity. The fact that the number of patients in the control group who received the patient discharge information letter not was registered due to the randomized design is a limitation. Therefore, we have presented the number of patients for whom the letter was prepared. In the intervention group, however, 151 patients received the letter, i.e. 88% of those for whom the letter was prepared [14].

Interpretation within the context of the wider literature

Very few previous studies have assessed the efficacy of similar interventions on the quality of the medication information at hospital discharge. However, a previous pre–post study in hip-fracture patients, using a similar assessment of the discharge summary as the primary endpoint, showed comparable efficacy to our study [17].

Most studies on the improvement of the quality of the medication discharge information have compared specific aspects of the information rather than used a summary score, showing an improvement on, e.g. the criteria ‘active ingredient indicated or medication changes being explained’ [15, 18]. The scores at almost every criterion in our study improved, except for the ‘active ingredient indicated or the sources for medication use at admission being described or the discharge summary and the hospital medication chart reconciled at the time for discharge’. For these three criteria, the explanation of no effect probably is that the National Patient Safety Program with the quality criteria for medication discharge information as one measure was introduced in 2011–12. This means that when we started inclusion to our RCT in 2014, the tool was still relatively new, and not well implemented. Naturally, many new demands at the same time resulted in a need to prioritize, even in the intervention patients. The criterion ‘discharge summary and the hospital medication chart reconciled at the time for discharge’ is fulfilled by only 20% of both intervention and controls, and this could be explained by the hectic situation at discharge for all health care personnel involved. Due to time pressure, the physicians’ main focus is to finish the discharge summary and it might be considered less important to update the hospital medicine chart accordingly [28].

Of note, the proportion of patients who had a patient discharge information letter prepared increased from one-third in the control group to nine out of ten patients in the intervention group. Although the effect of receiving patient information letters at discharge is argued as uncertain, it is seen as good practice [29, 30]. Patients who were discharged to their homes are likely to be responsible for managing their medications, although some might receive some assistance from either next of kin or home care nurses. In this subgroup of patients, who are likely to be the ones who would benefit most, nine out of ten in the intervention group received an information letter, compared to half of the control patients. In this regard, one should also note that not all patients in our study received a discharge conversation at the hospital [14]. This has also been reported previously, as well as patients not being sufficiently prepared for self-management after discharge [28, 31]. In the subgroup of patients discharged to a short-term stay at a nursing home, a patient discharge information letter was prepared for >80% of the intervention patients versus <10% of the controls. Short-term nursing home stays in Norway typically lasts from 2 to 6 weeks and are meant to prepare the patient to return to their own home. Since these patients are planned to be discharged to their home with full or partial responsibility for their medication administration after the short-term nursing home stay, it can be argued that the medicine discharge information letter might be an important contribution to patients maintaining a form for involvement and responsibility for their medicines during the care transitions, possibly resulting in increased self-management and patient safety when returning to their own home.

Concerning the clinical relevance of the results, previous studies have shown that receiving a medication management plan completed by a pharmacist reduces the risk of medication errors [32, 33]. Furthermore, low-quality or missing discharge summaries have been associated with a higher risk of rehospitalization [2, 34]. It would seem reasonable to suggest that the increased quality of the discharge medication information in the intervention group in our study could contribute to explaining the 34% increased overall survival [14].

Implications for policy, practice, and research

Healthcare resources are scarce and predicted as even more scarce in the future due to the aging and increasingly multimorbid population [10, 12]. Furthermore, aging and multimorbidity contribute to increased vulnerability in elderly patients, which further leads to a higher risk of adverse events [10]. Thus, incorrect or suboptimal medication treatment might have severe consequences. This underpins the importance of identifying healthcare interventions that efficiently increase the quality of medication information at hospital discharge and thus potentially improve patient safety and reduce negative patient outcomes and avoidable use of the healthcare system. Importantly, a significantly higher proportion of intervention patients had a medication reconciliation documented in the hospital record compared to the control group. This implies that the medication list is more likely to include the correct medications, although it remains to be specifically studied.

Conclusion

Integrated medicines management delivered to multimorbid patients during a hospital stay improved the quality of the medication information in discharge summaries as well as discharge information letters, and ensured that a discharge information letter in lay language was prepared for patients who were involved in the management of their medications after discharge.
