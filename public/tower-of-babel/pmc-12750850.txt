Effect of hyaluronic acid on the prevention of medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw in a bisphosphonate-treated rat model
Authors: Khalid Gachayev, Göksel Şimşek Kaya, Mahir Kaya, Öznur Özalp, Büşra Altunay, Havva Serap Toru
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMedication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw (MRONJ) remains a major clinical concern due to its complex pathogenesis and low treatment success rates. Hyaluronic acid (HA), a widely used biomaterial, has demonstrated regenerative and angiogenic potential.AimThis study investigated the effects of HA on the onset of MRONJ following tooth extraction in bisphosphonate (BP)-treated rats.MethodsTwenty-four adult male Wistar albino rats were randomly allocated to three groups: control (saline), medication control (zoledronic acid: ZA), and experimental (ZA + HA). Animals received ZA (0.06 mg/kg, intraperitoneally, weekly for 4 weeks), followed by extraction of the lower left first molar. In the ZA + HA group, HA gel was locally applied into the socket. Rats were euthanized 28 days postoperatively. Socket healing was evaluated using micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) parameters [bone volume fraction (BV/TV), bone surface density (BS/BV), trabecular number (Tb.N), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), trabecular separation (Tb.Sp)] and histomorphometric analysis of bone formation and osteocyte lacunae.ResultsMicro-CT analysis showed that rats in the ZA + HA and control groups exhibited significantly higher BV/TV, BS/BV, Tb.Th, and Tb.N and lower Tb.Sp compared with the ZA group (p < 0.05). Histomorphometric analysis confirmed reduced bone formation and a greater number of empty osteocyte lacunae in the ZA group, whereas the ZA + HA group demonstrated bone regeneration comparable to controls.ConclusionLocal application of HA may inhibit the onset of MRONJ and enhance post-extraction bone healing in BP-exposed bone.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07268-5.Keywords: MRONJ, Bone healing, Bisphosphonate, Hyaluronic acid

Background

Due to their anti-angiogenic properties and inhibitory effects on osteoclast activity, bisphosphonates (BPs) are widely used as anti-remodeling agents in the treatment of various bone diseases that lead to loss of bone mass, including osteoporosis and cancer-related bone metastases [1–3]. Their mechanism of action is based on their specific affinity for bone and minimal biotransformations. With their high affinity for hydroxyapatite [3, 4], these drugs play a role in diminishing bone turnover by suppressing both osteoclast and osteoblast function [2]. The reduction in remodeling capacity resulting from the action of BPs may also impair the bone repair process [1].

Previous human [5] and animal [1, 3, 6, 7] studies have reported that BP application may significantly impair the normal healing process in extraction sockets and could lead to the development of medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw (MRONJ), a complication with high morbidity characterized by progressive bone destruction in the maxillofacial region [6, 8]. In addition, tooth extraction has been identified as an etiological factor in 62% to 82% of patients diagnosed with MRONJ [9]. Various surgical protocols have been recommended for managing tooth extractions in patients at risk of MRONJ and minimizing the risk of complications. These include laser therapy [7], autologous platelet-rich plasma [10], piezosurgery or EDTA chelation [11], atraumatic extraction with orthodontic traction [12], and alveoloplasty with primary closure [13], all of which have been recommended for the management of tooth extractions in patients at risk of MRONJ to minimize complications. Given the increasing prevalence of patients requiring tooth extraction who are using BP medications and the detrimental impact of these agents on socket healing [14], it is imperative to assess the potential effects of the chosen surgical protocol on bone healing.

Hyaluronic acid (HA) is a naturally occurring, hydrophilic, non-immunogenic [15], anionic, linear, non-sulfated glycosaminoglycan made up of repeating units of D-glucuronic acid and N-acetyl-D-glucosamine linked by means of alternating β−1,3 and β−1,4 glycosidic bonds [16]. It possesses bacteriostatic, fungistatic, anti-inflammatory, antiedematous [17], and antithrombotic [18] properties. HA, involved in embryonic development, creates favorable conditions for tissue growth and regeneration [17]. It can bind to several key proteins in the healing cascade, including fibrin and collagen [19]. Exogenous hyaluronic acid increases intracellular hyaluronic acid levels and supports glycoprotein synthesis. In addition, it prevents the formation of immune complexes and polymorphonuclear cells, inhibits the migration of leukocytes and macrophages, and regulates fibroblast proliferation, thereby reducing the release of inflammatory mediators, matrix metalloproteinases, and inflammation-related cellular changes [20, 21].

HA plays a crucial role in bone repair by enhancing cell migration and adhesion, inducing the differentiation of mesenchymal cells into osteoblasts, and promoting their proliferation [22]. Moreover, it plays an indirect role in osteoclast adhesion to the bone surface and has the potential to enhance both angiogenesis and bone regeneration [21]. Despite the extensive research on HA as a potent biomaterial in tissue engineering [17], there is a lack of studies specifically investigating its impact on the healing of tooth extraction sockets in patients undergoing BP treatment. This study aimed to investigate the potential preventive effect of Hyaluronic Acid on the onset of MRONJ following tooth extraction in a rodent model.

Materials and methods

Animals

A total of twenty-four clinically healthy adult male Wistar albino rats, aged eight weeks and weighing 250 ± 25 g, were sourced from the Laboratory Animals Application and Research Center, Akdeniz University (Antalya, Turkey). The rats were housed in plastic cages with two animals per cage and were allowed to acclimate to the environment for one week before the study began. All cages were maintained in a humidity and temperature-controlled room (40–60% humidity and 22 ± 2 °C temperature) with a 12:12-hour light/dark cycle and a ventilation rate of 20 air changes per hour. All experiments were conducted between 9 a.m. and 7 p.m. to minimize diurnal variation. The rats had ad libitum access to standard pellet rat chow and filtered drinking water throughout the experiment. No experimental procedures were conducted within the animal housing facility to prevent any potential behavioral stress, immune responses, or metabolic changes. All procedures and reporting adhered to the guidelines outlined in the Animal Research: Reporting in Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines [23].

Experimental groups

Sample size calculation and random allocation

The sample size determination followed the resource equation approach [24] defined by the formula E = N – K = Kn – K = K (n – 1), where N is the total number of experimental units, n is the number of animals per group, K is the number of treatment groups, and E denotes the degrees of freedom for error. This can be rearranged as n = (E/K) + 1. Based on the commonly accepted recommendation that E should fall between 10 and 20 to balance statistical power and ethical animal use, an E value of 20 was selected. Given K = 3 groups, the resulting n was calculated as 7 animals per group. Allowing for a 10% attrition rate due to potential mortality, the final planned sample size was adjusted to 8 animal per group.

Stratified randomization was performed using Microsoft Excel, with the rats’ body weight as the reference. The information was inputted into a spreadsheet in ascending order of animal weight, after which the program generated random numbers for each rat (1 to 21), facilitating their random allocation into three groups. The lightest and heaviest animals in the study were homogeneously distributed into the following groups [25, 26].

Procedure

Drug administration

There was no consensus protocol for induction of MRONJ by bisphosphonates in animal models, as there are differences in drug metabolism between species. Therefore, the dose for this current study was adapted from Zandi et al. [26]. Animals in the ZA and ZA + HA groups received an intraperitoneal infusion of 0.06 mg/kg zoledronic acid (ZA; Zolenat® 4 mg/5 ml IV, Mustafa Nevzat İlaç Sanayi A.Ş, Istanbul, Türkiye) once a week for four weeks (a total of 5 BP injections) [26]. The dose of ZA (0.06 mg/kg, administered weekly for 4 weeks; cumulative 240 µg/kg/month) represents a supraphysiologic regimen compared to the clinical oncologic dose for rats (80 µg/kg/month) [27]. Animals in the control group received an equivalent volume of sterile saline solution (0.9% isotonic sodium chloride solution, Türk İlaç Serum Sanayi A.Ş., Ankara, Türkiye) administered at the same frequency and duration as the ZA injections.

Anesthesia protocol and tooth extraction

The left lower first molar was extracted four weeks after the start of ZA or saline administration. All surgical interventions were performed with a standard anesthesia protocol. Briefly, all rats received 20 mg/kg xylazine HCl (Alfazyne 2% ®, Ege-Vet, Turkey) as a pre-anesthetic agent by intraperitoneal injection and 90 mg/kg ketamine HCl (Ketalar®, Pfizer, Turkey) as a general anesthetic agent. Orotracheal intubation was performed, following which anesthesia was maintained during surgery by inhalation of an oxygen-isoflurane mixture (1–1.5.5 vol%). Before extraction, antisepsis of the buccal cavity was performed with 10% povidone-iodine. Articaine containing 1:200,000 epinephrine (Ultracain D-S Forte-Aventis, Istanbul, Türkiye) was injected into the tooth extraction area for local anesthesia and hemostasis. A gingival sulcus incision was then made around the left lower first molar using a #15 surgical blade. A mucoperiosteal flap was next raised with the assistance of a periosteal elevator, and the tooth extraction was performed using a sharpened dental explorer. Residual roots were removed from the extraction sockets using a medium straight headpiece and round fluted bur with a diameter of 1.0. Movable root fragments were removed with minimal manipulation using a sharp explorer, typically requiring only one or two attempts. Care was taken to avoid applying undue force within the extraction socket to prevent additional tissue damage. The bone ridge was then shaped and smoothed using surgical rongeurs. Each extraction socket was expanded (2 mm in diameter) using a rotary surgical bur with simultaneous saline irrigation to minimize thermal injury to the mandible bone [28, 29]. Mild subperiosteal debridement and advancement were performed on the buccal mucosa. In the ZA + HA group, HA gel was applied to the extraction socket, and wound margins were closed with 5.0 Vicryl sutures (Doğsan®, Türkiye) in all animals (Fig. 1A, B). No antibiotics were administered. Post-surgery, animals were provided with a soft diet for three days to minimize discomfort and pain during mastication. Throughout the experimental period, animals were monitored for pain, infection, and distress, and were observed daily for behavioral changes, feed and water intake, body mass fluctuations, and other clinical signs.

Euthanasia and sample collection

Four weeks after tooth extraction, rats from all experimental groups were euthanized to harvest the mandibles containing the extraction sockets. Rats were randomly selected for euthanasia using a computer-generated random number sequence (Microsoft Excel), ensuring an unbiased allocation of animals to euthanasia order. Euthanasia was performed under deep anesthesia using an overdose of sodium thiopental (200 mg/kg) (Pental®, IE Ulagay, Türkiye) until loss of all reflexes and rigor mortis was confirmed. The mandibles were carefully dissected, excess soft tissues were trimmed, and the specimens were placed in 10% buffered formaldehyde. Radiological and histomorphometric analyses were then conducted by investigators who were blinded to the study groups. Figure 2 shows the timeline of the experimental design.

Outcome measures

Radiological analysis (micro-CT)

The mandible samples were scanned using the Skyscan 1272 system (Bruker, Kontich, Belgium). The features of the device were an X-ray source; 40–100 kV, 10 W (< 5 μm spot size at 4 W), X-ray camera; 16 MP, and 4096 × 4096-pixel size at maximum magnification. The expression was < 0.35 μm for the 16 MP camera. The data from the scanned samples were transformed on NRecon v.1.6.3 software (Bruker-micro-CT, Belgium), while analysis was performed on CTAn v.1.12 software (Bruker-micro-CT, Belgium).

For the analysis, a cylindrical region of interest (ROI) was established in the extraction site of the first molars, measuring 3 mm in axis length and 1 mm in diameter, by segmenting the trabecular bone from the coronal to the apical thirds [30]. Morphological parameters of trabecular bone microarchitecture were assessed in accordance with the recommended guidelines [31]. Quantitative ROI analyses were conducted using CTAn (Bruker) software to evaluate the parameters of bone volume fraction (BV/TV; bone volume/tissue volume, %), bone surface density (BS/BV; bone surface/bone volume, mm2/mm3), and Tb.N (trabecular number, 1/mm), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th, mm), and trabecular separation (Tb.Sp, mm) measured on micro-CT.

Histomorphometric analysis

Hematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining was applied to histological sections obtained from the central region of the extraction socket, identified by the roots of the adjacent second molar as anatomical reference points. For each specimen, three consecutive sections representing the alveolar center were analyzed for histomorphometric evaluation. All sections were placed in 10% buffered formalin solution for at least 24 h, decalcified in 5% nitric acid solution, and subjected to closed system tissue processing procedures. The specimens were embedded in paraffin blocks, and 4-micron thick sections were cut, stained with hematoxylin and eosin, and were examined using a light microscope (Axioscope 2 Plus; Carl Zeiss Microscopy LLC, New York, NY) integrated with a high-resolution digital imaging system (AxioCam MRc; Carl Zeiss Microscopy GmbH, Göttingen, Germany). High-definition micrographs were acquired by a pathologist blinded to the experimental groups, ensuring unbiased documentation and analysis. Following manual delineation on the images of slices, images captured were transferred to the system and areas of new bone formation were measured [32]. The system consists of three distinct software modules (Angular SPA, NET Core REST API, and Python REST API) and an MSSQL relational database system. The Angular SPA web interface project is responsible for user interactions, the NET Core web service project for database operations, and the Python web service project for image processing procedures. The measurement results showed that the rats with a sample of ≥ 5 empty bone lacunae were determined to be the rat models of MRONJ in accordance with the method of Williams et al. [33]. All measurements were done three times to minimize measurement bias and the average calculated.

Statistical analysis

An a priori Shapiro-Wilk test was conducted to assess the normality of distribution; a non-parametric test being employed in the case of non-normal distribution. Variables that non-normal distribution was analyzed by the Kruskal–Wallis, followed by post-hoc group comparisons using the Bonferroni-adjusted Mann–Whitney U test. The significance level was set at 5%. All tests were performed with SPSS22.0 (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences, IBM, Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

The animals’ general health conditions remained constant during the study, and no losses occurred throughout the experimental period. The drug treatment and first molar extraction were both well tolerated. There was no significant difference determined in terms of mean body weights following the experimental period.

Radiological analysis

Micro-CT examination demonstrated that the extraction sockets in the ZA + HA and control groups exhibited higher radiodensity and preserved trabecular morphology compared with the ZA group (Fig. 3A-C). No significant difference in BV/TV was observed between ZA + HA and control groups (P ≥ 0.05). The quantitative analysis of bone parameters obtained from micro-CT revealed that, compared with rats receiving ZA alone, those treated with ZA + HA or saline exhibited significant increases in BV/TV, BS/BV, Tb.Th, and Tb.N values, while demonstrating a reduction in Tb.Sp (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4A-E). Furthermore, the ZA group exhibited reduced radiodensity and altered socket architecture on micro-CT, consistent with impaired bone healing.

Histomorphometric analysis

Histological analysis revealed new bone formation in all the groups (Fig. 5). Centrifugal growth from the bottom to the midpoint of the alveolar socket was observed in this newly formed bone.

Relatively less bone formation was determined in the alveolar floor and walls in the ZA group compared to the saline and ZA + HA groups (P < 0.05 for both) (Fig. 6A). These two groups exhibited similar histological characteristics. The histomorphometric results imply a compromised healing process in the extraction socket because of ZA exposure. In contrast, no statistically significant difference was determined between the ZA + HA and saline groups in terms of new bone formation (P ≥ 0.05) (Fig. 6A). Quantitative analysis revealed that the number of empty lacunae was markedly elevated in the ZA group compared to saline and ZA + HA groups (P < 0.001) (Fig. 6B). The elevated number of empty lacunae in the ZA group provides histological evidence of ZA-induced osteonecrosis.

Discussion

Delayed recovery following oral surgical procedures, such as tooth extraction, in patients undergoing bisphosphonate (BP) therapy is mainly attributed to inhibition of osteoclast activity, which impairs bone remodeling and healing in the jaw [34]. BPs further compromise bone repair by reducing vascularization and the release of angiogenic factors such as VEGF [6]. This disruption of vascularity and bone turnover may lead to persistent defective healing and even avascular necrosis of the alveolar bone [35]. In line with these mechanisms, our results also demonstrated impaired bone healing in ZA-treated animals. Both histomorphometric and radiological analyses confirmed that ZA reduced bone volume and trabecular parameters, consistent with previous reports in animals [4] and humans [5]. Therefore, promoting of bone remodeling processes constitutes a key strategy in addressing bone necrosis observed in MRONJ [36].

Considering these adverse effects of BPs, adjuvant biomaterials that can stimulate revascularization and bone regeneration may be of clinical value. Hyaluronic acid (HA) is one such candidate. While some studies, such as that of Galli et al. [37], found no effect of HA on tissue healing in the oral cavity, others reported beneficial outcomes. For instance, Jentsch et al. [38] demonstrated improved gingival healing in humans after HA gel application, and additional studies have shown that HA promotes soft tissue repair in oral surgery settings [36, 39]. Beyond soft tissue effects, HA has also been applied to bone defects either alone, with scaffolds, or as a delivery vehicle for growth factors, where it facilitated or augmented bone regeneration [19, 40].

Experimental studies further support the osteogenic role of HA. In a rat model, HA accelerated socket healing by upregulating osteogenic proteins such as BMP-2 and osteopontin during the early phase [21]. Sasaki and Watanabe [41] demonstrated that HA enhanced bone formation in femoral defects by stimulating mesenchymal cell differentiation. Our findings are consistent with these reports: animals in the ZA + HA group exhibited significantly higher BV/TV, BS/BV, Tb.Th, and Tb.N values and lower Tb.Sp than those in the ZA group, suggesting that HA can partially counteract the osteogenic deficits induced by bisphosphonate exposure.

HA may also exert indirect effects on bone repair through its pro-angiogenic activity. Raines et al. [42] showed that HA enhanced neovascularization after bone marrow ablation, and Kim et al. [43] reported accelerated wound healing and bone formation in infected sockets, attributing the effect partly to HA’s osteoinductive, bacteriostatic, and anti-inflammatory properties. Sarkarat et al. [44] similarly demonstrated enhanced bone healing in experimental MRONJ when HA was applied alone or with collagen sponge. Together with our results, these data suggest that HA exerts multifactorial effects—stimulating osteogenesis, modulating inflammation, and potentially supporting angiogenesis—that may explain the improved bone healing we observed in the ZA + HA group. However, we did not directly assess angiogenesis in this study, which remains a limitation. Future studies using immunohistochemistry (e.g., VEGF or CD31) are warranted to clarify whether enhanced vascularization contributes to the observed outcomes.

The rat remains a widely utilized model in biomedical research due to its accessibility, cost-effectiveness, and fewer ethical and logistical constraints, particularly in long-term studies [45]. Its skeletal system provides useful insights into bone dynamics and pharmacological effects on bone mass [46]. Regarding drug dosing, Hadad et al. [27] classified ZA regimens into physiological (≤ 200% of oncologic dose), supraphysiological (>200–1000%), and excessive supraphysiological (≥ 1000%). Physiological dosing typically induces MRONJ at rates of 10–40% but requires large animal numbers, whereas supraphysiological regimens raise incidence to 70–100%, allowing smaller cohorts but possibly involving different mechanisms. The dose used in our study (0.06 mg/kg weekly; cumulative 240 µg/kg/month) falls within the supraphysiologic range. While this limits direct extrapolation to clinical settings, it provides reliable induction of MRONJ with fewer animals, making it a reasonable and ethically justifiable choice.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, inherent physiological and bone turnover differences between rats and humans limit direct extrapolation of our findings to clinical populations. Second, the follow up interval before euthanasia was relatively short and may not encompass the full temporal course of socket healing and bone remodeling. Nevertheless, our findings provide encouraging preliminary evidence that locally applied HA may alleviate bisphosphonate-related impairment of alveolar bone healing and help prevent the onset of MRONJ.

Conclusion

This study provides promising preclinical evidence supporting the utility of HA as a local adjunctive agent for the prevention of MRONJ onset and the enhancement of post-extraction socket healing in compromised bone. Animals treated with HA exhibited improved bone regeneration, as demonstrated by increased bone volume, preservation of trabecular architecture, and a reduced number of empty osteocyte lacunae. These findings suggest that HA may mitigate the detrimental effects of zoledronic acid on bone remodeling by promoting osteogenic activity. Further long-term and comprehensive studies are warranted to validate these results and to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms of HA’s therapeutic effects in bisphosphonate-exposed bone tissue.
