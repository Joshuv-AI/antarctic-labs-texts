Ketamine for treatment resistant depression in individuals with eating disorders: a comparison study
Authors: Elizabeth Wassenaar, Dan V Blalock, Alan Duffy, Megan Riddle, Anne Marie O’Melia, Howard Weeks, Phillip S Mehler, Renee D Rienecke
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIndividuals with eating disorders (EDs) have an increased risk of treatment resistant depression (TRD) and treatment resistant bipolar disorder (TRBD) and a higher rate of suicidal ideation and death by suicide than individuals without EDs. Individuals with EDs are less responsive to conventional treatments for mood disorders than individuals without EDs. Ketamine has evidence for treatment of TRD and TRBD and for the treatment of acute suicidality. Ketamine has also been shown to be safe in individuals with EDs who are malnourished. This study is among the first to examine the efficacy of ketamine in TRD and TRBD in patients with co-occurring EDs at higher levels of care.MethodsParticipants were 85 adults admitted to an ED treatment facility who received subanesthetic intranasal generic ketamine compared to a sample of 85 adults matched for age, sex, gender, race, diagnosis, and level of care at admission admitted to the same facilities who did not receive ketamine. Participants completed the Eating Disorder Examination Questionnaire (EDE-Q), Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), suicidal ideation (PHQ-9 Item 9) and Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) at admission and discharge.ResultsPatients receiving ketamine and matched controls were not significantly different on age, sex, gender, race, diagnosis, or level of care at admission. There were no significant differences between groups on admission for all EDE-Q subscales, PHQ-9, suicidal ideation, or GAD. All patients, irrespective of use of ketamine, improved significantly from admission to discharge on all outcomes. There were no significant differences between groups for change in all EDE-Q subscales, change in PHQ-9, change in suicidal ideation, or change in GAD-7. Patients receiving ketamine had significantly longer lengths of stay than controls (b = 0.39, p = .01). The lack of significant differences was maintained irrespective of predicting raw change, controlling for admission scores, or additionally controlling for length of stay.ConclusionsIn a population of individuals seeking higher level of care for an ED and controls matched for age, gender, sex, diagnosis, level of care, and race, treatment with ketamine was associated with significant reductions in depression and ED severity, but not more so than individuals not treated with ketamine. Findings from the study suggest that ketamine may be a viable treatment option for individuals with EDs, however, further research is needed to determine ketamine’s efficacy in treating EDs with comorbid depression.Keywords: Ketamine, Eating disorder, Treatment resistant depression, Treatment resistant bipolar depression, Suicidality

Background

Treatment resistant depression (TRD) is a major public health problem despite decades of research into treatments for mood disorders [1]. Individuals with eating disorders (EDs) are at even higher risk of not responding to treatment for mood disorders than individuals without EDs and have an increased risk of death by suicide when compared to individuals with mental illness without EDs [2]. This is of much import given the very recent report of increasing suicidal thoughts in young adults, with ages often affect by EDs [3]. Individuals with EDs and mood disorders may have additional risk factors that decrease response or compliance to conventional treatments for mood disorders, including reduced response to medication while malnourished, altered medication absorption and kinetics, and factors that can lead to inadequate trials including lack of perceived efficacy or fear of side effects [4, 5].

Research has accumulated over the past two decades indicating that ketamine, a dissociative anesthetic and NMDA-antagonist that has been used for the induction and maintenance of anesthesia for the past 50 years, can have a robust and rapid antidepressant effect when given at subanesthetic doses in a variety of forms [6]. The antidepressant effect of a single dose of subanesthetic generic ketamine can be observed within 24 h and maintained for up to 7 days for a single dose, with effectiveness increasing with repeated dosing, for both TRD and treatment-resistant bipolar depression (TRBD) [7–10]. Importantly, ketamine has been shown to have a rapid impact on reducing suicidal ideation [11]. The use of subanesthetic generic ketamine off-label for psychiatric concerns is considered a fairly safe intervention when used as prescribed and with adequate medical oversight, including administering ketamine by a qualified health care provider in a supervised setting and careful medical monitoring for side effects including sedation, dissociation, anaphylaxis, breathing difficulties, and changes in vital signs [12–14]. There have been case series and case reports regarding the use of ketamine in patients with EDs and treatment of depressive symptoms and/or symptoms of EDs in a variety of treatment settings, but primarily in weight and nutritionally-restored individuals and in outpatient levels of care [15–25]. In a recent study, subanesthetic generic intranasal ketamine was found to be safe for patients with EDs at higher levels of care (HLOC) [26]. Individuals with more severe and refractory EDs, including those with severe malnutrition, ambivalence about treatment, and more psychiatric co-morbidities or safety concerns related to suicidality, can benefit from admission to HLOC to support their medical and psychiatric safety in the course of treatment for their ED [27, 28]. To date, there are no studies evaluating individuals with significant EDs requiring a HLOC for their ED who received subanesthetic ketamine for co-occurring TRD or TRBD [27, 28].

The primary aim of this study was to compare changes in depression symptoms at admission and discharge between patients receiving ketamine and those not receiving ketamine in a HLOC ED treatment facility. The secondary aim was to assess the change in suicidality, anxiety and ED symptom severity between these two cohorts. It was hypothesized that individuals receiving ketamine treatment would show greater improvement in depression, suicidality, anxiety and ED symptoms over the course of treatment compared to those who did not receive ketamine.

Methods

Participants and procedures

Participants in this study were a nonrandomized group of adults who were diagnosed with a Diagnostic and statistical manual of mental disorders- fifth edition (DSM-5) ED as determined by semi-structured interviews conducted by a licensed mental health clinician [29]. Participants were admitted to inpatient, residential, or partial hospitalization levels of care in a large United States-based ED treatment center from January 2021 to December 2024 in Bellevue, WA, Chicago, IL, Denver, CO, and Plano, TX. Participants provided informed consent for this study prior to completing measures. Demographic measures were collected at time of admission. For participants who received ketamine, additional informed consent was provided to receive the medication. To be eligible to receive ketamine, patients had to have a diagnosis of TRD or TRBD as assessed by a psychiatrist and were considered to have unsuccessfully responded to two or more antidepressants (for TRD), or mood stabilizers or antipsychotics (for TRBD). Individuals who met these criteria were eligible to receive ketamine during their admission. The decision to receive ketamine treatment included a variety of factors for each individual, including openness to a relatively new medication, an individual’s experience with previous psychopharmacology trials, and feasibility to fit a course of ketamine treatment into an individual’s treatment plan and goals. Unfortunately, we do not have qualitative or anecdotal reasons to explain why some patients chose ketamine and others did not. Each patient receiving ketamine completed a consultation with a physician, including a medical and psychiatric evaluation, and received ketamine in the facility with medical oversight All patients in this study were admitted to ED treatment programs that are funded by insurance, scholarship, or private funds. There was no additional treatment cost to patients for the ketamine administration, although in some locations the cost of the medication was assumed by the patient. The approximate cost of compounded generic ketamine is $100/1000 mg vial, which means each dose would be between $3.00 and $20.00 depending on how much ketamine the individual was prescribed.

An equal number of patients not receiving ketamine was included from a pool of greater than twenty-five hundred control patients from the same treatment center, using near exact matching on age, race, level of care, diagnosis, and admission score on the PHQ-9 within 1 point. Participants in both cohorts completed questionnaires at admission and discharge. This study was reviewed and received expedited approval by Salus Institutional Review Board.

Measures

Demographics

A self-report questionnaire was administered to participants on admission to care to assess sociodemographic characteristics (e.g., age, gender, race/ethnicity).

PHQ-9

The Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) was used to measure depression with nine symptoms scored on a scale from 0 to 3 [30]. Zero indicates “never” and 3 indicates “almost every day.” Scores of 5 to 9 are considered mild depression, 10–14 moderate depression, 15–19 moderately severe depression and 20–27 severe depression. Item 9 of the PHQ-9 was used to measure recent suicidal ideation [31]. This question asks how often in the last two weeks the individual has had, “Thoughts that you would be better off dead or of hurting yourself in some way?” The PHQ-9 has been shown to have good psychometric properties among patients with psychiatric disorders and has been used as a measure of treatment outcomes [32]. Internal consistency in the current sample was excellent at admission (α = 0.89) and discharge (α = 0.92).

Generalized anxiety disorder 7-item (GAD-7)

The GAD-7 is a 7 item self-report questionnaire that measures anxiety [33]. Each question is measured on a 0 to 3 scale, with 0 meaning the symptom is “never” experienced and 3 indicating that the symptom is experienced “nearly every day.” Total scores of 5 to 9 indicate mild anxiety, 10 to 14 moderate, and greater than 15 is indicative of severe anxiety. The GAD-7 has been previously shown to have good psychometric properties and is well validated [34]. Internal consistency in the current sample was excellent at admission (α = 0.87) and discharge (α = 0.83).

Eating disorder examination questionnaire (EDE-Q)

The EDE-Q is a well-established self-report questionnaire that assesses various aspects of eating disorder psychopathology over the last 28 days [35]. The EDE-Q has 28 questions across four subscales: eating concern, restraint, shape concern, and weight concern, scored on a scale from 0 to 6. Scores of 4 or greater are clinically significant [36]. Internal consistency in the current sample was excellent at admission (Eating Concerns α = 0.88, Restraint α = 0.77, Shape Concerns α = 0.85, Weight Concerns α = 0.80) and discharge (Eating Concerns α = 0.93, Restraint α = 0.82, Shape Concerns α = 0.84, Weight Concerns α = 0.79).

Analytic plan

Welch’s paired-samples t-tests were conducted to examine changes in outcomes from admission to discharge. Balance in patient characteristics among patients receiving ketamine and matched controls on matching variables was evaluated using standardized mean differences (SMDs), where values < 0.1 indicate no meaningful difference [37]. SMDs were calculated using the “tableone” package in R [38].

Comparisons between patients receiving ketamine and matched controls on categorical variables were conducted with chi-square test-of-independence. Comparisons between these groups on continuous variables were conducted using general linear models. Bivariate and multivariate general linear models were also used to examine differences on measures at admission, as well as raw change, residualized change (controlling for admission score), and residualized change controlling additionally for other variables that were significantly different between groups. Effect sizes for statistically significant effects are reported as a Cohen’s d with 95% Confidence Interval (95% CI). All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.3.3) [39].

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to compare outcomes between individuals with TRD or TRBD and EDs who received ketamine and those who did not receive ketamine while in HLOC ED treatment. It is the largest study to date examining treatment outcomes in patients with EDs treated with ketamine.

Individuals who received ketamine for the treatment of TRD or TRBD at HLOC showed significant improvements in scores of depressive symptoms, suicidal ideation, and ED subscale scores, but not more so than individuals who did not receive ketamine with matched scores on measures of depression. Individuals who received more doses of ketamine showed greater decreases in depressive symptoms, which is consistent with the response of the general population treated with ketamine [7]. Individuals who received ketamine for the treatment of TRD or TRBD had significantly longer lengths of stay in treatment (113 days vs. 92.9 days).

Interestingly, there were not notable end-point differences between the individuals who received ketamine and those who did not receive ketamine, and both groups showed significant improvement in symptoms of depression, suicidality, and ED symptoms from admission to discharge. All patients in this study received multidisciplinary team-based care in ED specific treatment settings at the inpatient, residential or partial hospitalization level of care. There are several possibilities for why the ketamine group did as well, but not better than the non-treatment group, including differences in the group that elected to receive ketamine that have not been well quantified and captured with information used to compare groups. Premature discharge from ED treatment may be a factor in poorer long-term outcomes and higher rate of re-hospitalization [40–42] and it is of note that in the current study, the group who received ketamine treatment had significantly longer lengths of stay; however it is not able to be interpreted for the impact of length of stay on outcome measures in this analysis. In this study, individuals who received ketamine may have been a population whose course of disease required a longer length of stay for remediation of symptoms, individuals who were more willing to seek out and receive treatment, individuals who otherwise might have had a foreshortened length of stay due to acute safety concerns who were able to stay in treatment due to reduced risk of suicidality, or for whom ketamine increased an overall willingness to remain in treatment. It is also possible that absent ketamine treatment this group would have done worse than the non-treatment group and only because of ketamine treatment were scores made equal. Future research is needed to investigate these questions more comprehensively.

There are few to no psychopharmacologic treatment options that directly address ED symptoms [43]. Although it was not the primary objective of this study to evaluate the response of ED symptoms to treatment with ketamine, it was possible to observe significant improvement in self-reported ED symptoms in both ketamine-treated and ketamine non-treated populations. It is not possible to determine whether the population treated with ketamine would have had significant treatment response without the use of ketamine. Future studies are needed to measure treatment outcomes with a prospective randomized control trial to better evaluate response to ketamine in this population.

The use of subanesthetic generic ketamine is not without risks, and the FDA has strongly advised against at-home off-label use of generic ketamine [13]. Patients should be carefully screened for contraindications, continuously monitored in a medical setting during treatment for side effects and carefully assessed for response to treatment [44, 45]. When used as prescribed and with appropriate medical oversight, the risk for abuse and/or diversion is thought to be limited but should be carefully evaluated [46].

This study has several limitations. This was an observational study, which limits our ability to interpret causality and increases the risk of confounding. It is difficult to say within this study design how the group who received ketamine would have responded to HLOC ED treatment without ketamine. The non-ketamine sample was matched on PHQ-9 scores in an attempt to control for the severity of depression at presentation to care but it is not certain that the non-ketamine sample had TRD or TRBD and there are a variety of reasons why the non-treatment group may not have received, which could have been due to personal choice, contraindication, or ineligibility. While many features of the non-treatment group were matched to the group that received ketamine, they may have differed in other ways not captured in the data, which may impact the interpretation of the results. Data were collected at admission and discharge, and there was no contemporaneous or post-discharge follow-up data. Thus, the impact of ketamine during the treatment course and long-term outcomes remain unknown. Due to sample size, we were unable to examine differences in outcomes between diagnostic groups. Treatment response was assessed by self-report measures, which may have some inherent limitations in individuals with EDs who may have alexithymia, which was not assessed in this analysis [47, 48]. Finally, we did not analyze information about the duration of illness or number of previous index HLOC admissions the two groups had which might impact outcomes.

The strengths of this study include the addition of new information to the limited literature on ketamine in individuals with EDs. This is the first study to evaluate the use of ketamine in an ED population for outcomes and treatment response at any level of care. In addition, the population evaluated in this study, individuals with EDs and TRD or TRBD at HLOC, are an under-researched group. Finally, outcomes were assessed with well-validated measures that are widely used in behavioral health and ED treatment.

There is much excitement and interest around the use of ketamine in EDs, however these findings underscore the complexity of evaluating pharmacologic interventions in ED populations, particularly in the absence of randomization. The lack of between-group differences does not negate the potential utility of ketamine but highlights the need for more rigorous trials. Future studies should stratify by TRD vs. TRBD, include follow-up assessments, and explore mechanisms by which ketamine may influence treatment engagement or symptom trajectories.[49]. A significant gap exists in the literature for individuals with EDs and randomized controlled trials for pharmacotherapy interventions. To our knowledge, there is only one randomized control trial for ketamine pharmacology in patients with EDs that will begin recruiting in the United Kingdom in 2025 [50]. Given the limited pharmacologic options available for EDs and the high burden of psychiatric comorbidity, further investigation into ketamine’s role in this context is warranted. While not specifically assessed, the current study suggests that intranasal ketamine treatment is acceptable to patients and feasible to study. A randomized controlled trial is necessary to determine its efficacy in reducing symptoms of EDs, depression, and suicidal ideation. In addition, future research should assess the efficacy of ketamine in individuals receiving lower levels of care, such as outpatient treatment. Future studies should explore not only clinical outcomes but also treatment history, engagement, safety, and long-term effects to better inform clinical decision-making.

Conclusion

Murray et al.’s seminal paper decrying the lack of recent substantial progress in the treatment in AN caused concern in this field [51]. Thus, ketamine is a potentially promising treatment option for individuals with EDs for several reasons: subanesthetic generic ketamine has limited side effects, especially related to weight changes, which may increase willingness to start medication for TRD or TRBD even for those with significant ongoing ED-related concerns about weight gain; subanesthetic generic ketamine is safe in a nutritionally compromised ED population; and subanesthetic generic ketamine for the treatment of TRD and TRBD is often more time-limited than conventional treatments for TRD and TRBD. In addition, subanesthetic ketamine offers a novel pharmacologic option in a group of individuals who may have a history of multiple failed medication trials and may be wary of continuing to try conventional options. In this comparison analysis of individuals at HLOC for their ED who did or did not receive subanesthetic ketamine, all participants had significant improvement in outcomes of depression, anxiety, suicidality, or eating disorder concerns. The lack of between-group differences highlights the need for prospective, randomized controlled trials to determine the efficacy, safety, and optimal use of ketamine in ED populations with comorbid mood disorders. In addition, because we were unable to determine which individuals in the comparison group have TRD, we are unable to make any conclusions about the specific impact of ketamine on depression among individuals with TRD; a randomized controlled trial comparing those with TRD receiving ketamine to those with TRD not receiving ketamine would be necessary to answer this question. The treatment of individuals with EDs who also experience TRD, TRBD, or suicidality presents a significant clinical challenge, and careful evaluation of emerging and novel treatment options will be an ongoing challenge to our field. In this analysis, ketamine for the treatment of TRD or TRBD in this population was not found to be superior but still may be a valid treatment option for an individual to consider. In a recent analysis of psychedelic-assisted treatment (PAT) trials, the issue of placebo response and true blinding was raised as an important confounding consideration in analysis of response to traditional antidepressants (tAD). PAT was not found to be superior to tAD for the treatment of major depression but was still found to have clinically meaningful improvements [52]. Similarly, in our study, ketamine was not associated with superior response to treatment as usual but still may have clinically meaningful impact on treatment outcomes. However, it is also possible that ketamine was simply not effective; future controlled studies will be needed to answer this question.

The ability to choose among non-inferior treatment options may provide much-needed flexibility and the ability to tailor one’s treatment plan is an important consideration. These data provide an initial indication that ketamine pharmacotherapy could be a viable consideration for specific individuals with EDs with TRD or TRBD.
