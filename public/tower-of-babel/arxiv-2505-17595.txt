NeUQI: Near-Optimal Uniform Quantization Parameter Initialization
Li Lin, Xinyu Hu, Xiaojun Wan

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) achieve impressive performance across domains but face significant challenges when deployed on consumer-grade GPUs or personal devices such as laptops, due to high memory consumption and inference costs. Post-training quantization (PTQ) of LLMs offers a promising solution that reduces their memory footprint and decoding latency. In practice, PTQ with uniform quantization representation is favored for its efficiency and ease of deployment since uniform quantization is widely supported by mainstream hardware and software libraries. Recent studies on ≥ 2 \geq 2 -bit uniform quantization have led to noticeable improvements in post-quantization model performance; however, they primarily focus on quantization methodologies, while the initialization of quantization parameters is underexplored and still relies on the suboptimal Min-Max strategies. In this work, we propose NeUQI , a method devoted to efficiently determining near-optimal initial parameters for uniform quantization. NeUQI is orthogonal to prior quantization methodologies and can seamlessly integrate with them. The experiments with the LLaMA and Qwen families on various tasks demonstrate that our NeUQI consistently outperforms existing methods. Furthermore, when combined with a lightweight distillation strategy, NeUQI can achieve superior performance to PV-tuning, a much more resource-intensive approach.

Introduction

In recent years, large language models (LLMs) like ChatGPT
[
1
]
have rapidly emerged, demonstrating strong capabilities across various tasks, including open-ended writing, knowledge-based question answering, and code generation. Given the high API costs of proprietary LLMs and the growing performance of open-source alternatives like LLaMA
[
28
,
10
]
and Qwen
[
32
]
families, there is a rising preference for deploying open-source LLMs locally. However, the deployment of large-scale models (e.g., LLaMA 3 70B) is often limited by compute resources and inference efficiency, especially on personal devices or consumer-grade GPUs like a single RTX 4090 with 24GB of memory. In this context, post-training quantization (PTQ)
[
15
]
, particularly using a uniform quantization scheme, offers a practical solution by converting model weights from
bfloat16
to low-bit-width integer formats such as
int4/3/2
, significantly reducing memory usage and inference latency.

Prior work on uniform PTQ mainly focuses on two directions. The first involves weight and activation processing to reduce quantization difficulty, such as smoothing via rotation transformations
[
5
,
29
,
3
]
, integrating rotations into model architectures
[
21
,
13
]
, and reducing weight magnitude
[
34
]
. The second emphasizes block-wise and global fine-tuning, including multi-block training
[
7
]
, rotation-based fine-tuning
[
21
,
13
]
, and direct optimization over quantized weights
[
22
]
.

Although these studies have made notable progress, they still adopt the conventional Min-Max initialization, which merely determines quantization parameters solely based on the extreme values in the weights while ignoring weight distribution and importance. As revealed in LeanQuant
[
35
]
, such initialization leads to a mismatch with the quantization objective in the commonly adopted GPTQ framework
[
8
]
.
Even under ideal conditions, specifically a uniform distribution, Min-Max remains suboptimal, as analyzed in Appendix
A
.
However, little research has focused on improving quantization initialization. Only recently has LeanQuant made a preliminary attempt by introducing a loss-aware strategy. But it still inherits a key limitation of Min-Max: restricting the zero-point to integers, which reduces the flexibility of uniform quantization and results in suboptimal initialization.

To address this gap, we propose NeUQI, a novel initialization method for uniform quantization, which removes the integer constraint on the zero-point, thus enabling a more flexible parameter space. We first apply a diagonal approximation to the quantization reconstruction objective and then decompose it into two tractable subproblems: (1) determining the optimal zero-point under a fixed scale and (2) finding the optimal scale given its corresponding zero-point. Both are efficiently solvable by exploiting the objective’s structure. More importantly, NeUQI is plug-and-play and orthogonal to quantization methods used in the calibration and fine-tuning stages. When combined with our NeUQI, the performance of quantized models significantly improves and even surpasses non-quantized models of comparable memory usage, as illustrated in Figure
1
. Furthermore, only applying a lightweight knowledge distillation, NeUQI can outperform PV-tuning
[
22
]
—a more sophisticated and resource-intensive fine-tuning method. It underscores the critical role of proper initialization: poor initial conditions are difficult to fix even with heavy fine-tuning, while a well-initialized model can achieve better performance with significantly less effort.

The main contributions of our work are summarized as follows:

1.

We revisit the conventionally adopted Min-Max quantization initialization and identify its fundamental limitation: the restriction of the zero-point to integers, which leads to reduced flexibility and suboptimal initialization.

2.

We propose NeUQI, a novel initialization method for uniform quantization that relaxes the zero-point to a continuous variable and exploits the structure of the optimization landscape. NeUQI can be directly combined with existing quantization methods to enhance their performance.

3.

Experiments with LLaMA and Qwen families of different sizes on various tasks demonstrate that NeUQI consistently outperforms existing methods. When further combined with a lightweight distillation strategy, NeUQI even surpasses relatively more resource-intensive PV-tuning.

Related Work

Uniform Quantization

In the 2-bit to 4-bit quantization regime, prior studies have explored many post-training quantization techniques to mitigate accuracy degradation. Early methods like AWQ
[
19
]
and SmoothQuant
[
31
]
applied mathematically equivalent transformations using diagonal matrices on activations and their inverses on weights, balancing quantization difficulty and laying the groundwork for later approaches.
Subsequent methods generalized the diagonal matrix to invertible forms for better smoothing, though activation-side transformations require online computation, leading to efficiency-focused designs. For example, QuIP
[
5
]
uses Kronecker products of two small rotations; DuQuant
[
18
]
adopts block-diagonal matrices with repeated rotation; QuIP#
[
29
]
and QuaRot
[
3
]
use Hadamard transforms; FrameQuant
[
2
]
applies a Fourier-based transformation variant. SpinQuant
[
21
]
and OSTQuant
[
13
]
introduce learnable rotations integrated into the model to avoid runtime overhead. Alternatively, MagR
[
34
]
optimizes weights while preserving model functionality to reduce quantization difficulty. In addition, there are fine-tuning-based approaches such as CBQ
[
7
]
, which performs block-wise tuning, and PV-tuning
[
22
]
, which jointly optimizes continuous and discrete parameters.

Quantization Parameter Initialization

Most uniform quantization methods rely on the conventional Min-Max initialization, which determines quantization parameters by the extreme weight values. Although Min-Max is straightforward and efficient, this heuristic neglects the impact of initialization on the final model performance. Some methods, such as MagR and OmniQuant
[
27
]
, introduce shrink-scale variants of Min-Max to suppress outliers and improve precision, but they remain simple extensions of the original method. LeanQuant
[
35
]
makes a significant step forward by proposing an optimization-based initialization with grid search. However, its optimization relies on enumerating different Min-Max value pairs, from which the scale and zero-point are computed based on the standard Min-Max formula. As a result, it inherits the core limitations of Min-Max, particularly the constraint of zero-points to integers. In contrast, our NeUQI is proposed to overcome the limitations of the Min-Max paradigm by removing the zero-point constraint and directly optimizing a well-founded initialization objective.

3
Background

Uniform Quantization

Model quantization is, in essence, the process of mapping floating-point parameters to a finite set of quantized values, allowing each parameter to be represented by a low-bit-width index, thereby reducing memory footprint and memory access latency. In the case of uniform quantization, the finite set
\mathcal{Q}
is defined by three parameters: the scale
s
, the zero-point
z
, and the bit-width
k
. Formally, this set is given by:

\mathcal{Q}=\left\{q_{i}=s\cdot(z+i)\,\middle|\,i\in\{0,1,\ldots,2^{k}-1\}\right\},

(1)

where
i
is the index of quantized values.

To reduce quantization error, each floating-point value is typically mapped to the nearest value in
\mathcal{Q}
. Given a floating-point input
x
, and assuming the parameters
s
,
z
, and
k
of
\mathcal{Q}
are known, the uniform quantization function
Q
is defined as:

Q_{s,z}(x)=s\cdot\left(\text{clamp}(\lfloor x/s-z\rceil,\,0,\,2^{k}-1)+z\right),

(2)

where
\lfloor\cdot\rceil
denotes rounding to the nearest integer, and
\text{clamp}(\cdot,a,b)=\text{max}(a,\text{min}(\cdot,b))
ensures the result lies within
[a,b]
; the subscript
k
is typically omitted from
Q
since the bit-width is usually known in advance.

Min-Max Initialization

Min-Max is a conventional initialization method that remains widely used in uniform quantization.
Min-Max determines the quantization parameters by mapping the minimum and maximum of the parameter to
s\cdot z
and
s\cdot(z+2^{k}-1)
, which correspond to the quantized values at indices
0
and
2^{k}-1
in the set
\mathcal{Q}
. Based on the above extremal-value mapping, the scale factor
s
and the zero point
z
are directly given by:

s=\frac{\text{max}(x)-\text{min}(x)}{2^{k}-1},\quad z=\left\lfloor\frac{\text{min}(x)}{s}\right\rceil.

(3)

Here,
z
is rounded to the nearest integer.
This is because, under the common assumptions that
\text{max}(x)\geq 0
,
\text{min}(x)\leq 0
, and
s>0
, we have
s\geq-\text{min}(x)/(2^{k}-1)
and
\text{min}(x)/s\leq 0
, which implies
\text{min}(x)/s\in[-(2^{k}-1),0]
.
Therefore, after rounding, it follows that
z\in[-(2^{k}-1),0]
, and thus
-z
lies within the range
[0,2^{k}-1]
, making it representable as a
k
-bit unsigned integer.

Method

In this section, we present
NeUQI
(
Ne

U
niform
Q
uantization
I
nitialization), a novel method designed to improve the parameter initialization of uniform quantization.
Unlike the conventional Min-Max initialization, which directly determines the scale and zero-point from the observed minimum and maximum values of the weights, NeUQI formulates the initialization as an optimization problem to find near-optimal quantization parameters.
We begin by revisiting the quantization loss formulation in Section
4.1
, which lays the foundation for our initialization strategy. Section
4.2
then introduces the NeUQI algorithm, detailing its formulation and practical implementation. Finally, in Section
4.3
, we introduce a lightweight distillation technique to demonstrate the efficiency of our method, which can further improve the performance of the quantized model with minimal overhead.

4.1
Quantization Loss

Following GPTQ
[
8
]
, the quantization loss function for a single linear layer is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(s,z)

\displaystyle=\|XW^{\top}-XQ_{s,z}(W)^{\top}\|_{F}^{2}

(4)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}((W-Q_{s,z}(W))H(W-Q_{s,z}(W))^{\top})

(5)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}(W_{i}-Q_{s,z}(W_{i}))H(W_{i}-Q_{s,z}(W_{i}))^{\top},

(6)

where
X\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times\mathrm{In}}
denotes the input activations,
W\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{Out}\times\mathrm{In}}
is the weight matrix of the linear layer, with
\mathrm{In}
and
\mathrm{Out}
representing the input and output dimensions respectively.
W_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times\mathrm{In}}
denotes the
i
-th row of
W
, and
H=X^{\top}X\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{In}\times\mathrm{In}}
is commonly referred to as the Hessian matrix.
This formulation naturally suggests adopting the row-wise quantization strategy, which aligns with common practices that treat one row as one or multiple groups for quantization.

4.2
NeUQI

Although the full Hessian is efficiently computable for a single linear layer, incorporating it into the optimization is nontrivial. To address this, we adopt a diagonal approximation, which is a standard technique in the machine learning literature
[
17
,
14
]
. This approximation removes cross-weight interactions and results in a structurally simpler loss function that, despite being globally coupled via shared quantization parameters, is significantly easier to optimize. Under this approximation, the loss function reduces to:

\mathcal{L}(s,z)=\sum_{i}H_{ii}(Q_{s,z}(w_{i})-w_{i})^{2}

(7)

To solve this problem efficiently, we decompose the optimization into two sequential steps. First, we fix the scale
s
and optimize the zero-point
z
, as discussed in Section
4.2.1
. Then, based on the solution of the first step, we further optimize the scale
s
, as described in Section
4.2.2
.

4.2.1
Optimization of Zero-Point

Following this decomposition, the loss function is further transformed into:

\mathcal{L}(z)=s^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{i}\left(\text{clamp}\left(\lfloor x_{i}-z\rceil,0,2^{k}-1\right)-(x_{i}-z)\right)^{2},

(8)

where
x_{i}=w_{i}/s
, and
h_{i}=H_{ii}
.

We first can observe that the single term
\text{clamp}(\lfloor x_{i}-z\rceil,0,2^{k}-1)
is a piecewise constant function of
z
. The transition points of this function occur at
z=x_{i}-(i+0.5)
,
i\in\{0,1,\cdots,2^{k}-2\}
, which divides the real line into
2^{k}
intervals.
Now consider the single-term quantization error:

(\mathrm{clamp}(\lfloor x_{i}-z\rceil,0,2^{k}-1)-(x_{i}-z))^{2}
,
which is a piecewise quadratic function of
z
, also divided into
2^{k}
intervals. Consequently, the total loss function (aggregated over
n
samples) consists of
O(n\cdot 2^{k})
intervals.
To find the minimum of the piecewise quadratic function, we first compute the local minimum in each interval and then select the smallest among them.
This idea reduces the problem to computing the quadratic, linear, and constant coefficients of the piecewise quadratic loss function in each interval. These coefficients can be efficiently calculated by sorting all transition points and accumulating the corresponding increments, a strategy we refer to as the increment-prefix-sum technique. Specifically, when
\text{clamp}(\lfloor x_{i}-z\rceil,0,2^{k}-1)
changes from
a
to
b
, the resulting increment in the loss function is
(b-(x_{i}-z))^{2}-(a-(x_{i}-z))^{2}
.
The detailed procedure is described in Algorithm
1
.

As previously discussed, the total number of transition points is
O(n\cdot 2^{k})
.
Since the coefficient computation involves sorting these points, the overall time complexity is
O(n\cdot 2^{k}\cdot\log(n\cdot 2^{k}))
, which increases overhead substantially as the bit-width
k
grows.
To alleviate this computational burden, we propose a two-stage efficient approximation strategy, which reduces the overall time complexity to
O(n\log n)
:

•

Rough Estimation
: At this stage, we approximate
x_{i}-z
within the low-loss interval
[-\frac{1}{2},2^{k}-\frac{1}{2}]
by its maximum value
(\frac{1}{2})^{2}
, while leaving the quantization loss unchanged outside this interval, resulting in a smoothed objective:

\text{max}\left(0.25,\left(\text{clamp}\left(\lfloor x_{i}-z\rceil,0,2^{k}-1\right)-(x_{i}-z)\right)^{2}\right),

Under this approximation, each sample reduces to exactly two transition points, located at
x_{i}-(2^{k}-0.5)
and
x_{i}+0.5
. Leveraging the same increment-prefix-sum technique, we can determine an approximate zero-point
z_{\mathrm{rough}}
in
O(n\log n)
time. The detailed procedure is described in Algorithm
2
.

•

Local Search
: The original, non-approximated loss function is then evaluated within a narrow interval
[z_{\mathrm{rough}}-1,z_{\mathrm{rough}}+1]
. As the number of transition points in this interval remains limited to at most two per sample, this local search step also maintains a time complexity of
O(n\log n)
. The detailed procedure is described in Algorithm
3
.

4.2.2
Optimization of Scale

After determining the optimal zero-point
z^{*}(s)
for a given scale
s
using the above algorithm, the loss function can be simplified into a univariate function that depends only on
s
. Although the loss for a portion of the rows in the weight matrix appears unimodal, many other rows exhibit non-unimodal behavior, as illustrated in Figure
2
. Specifically, as shown in the figure, the loss landscape for the 1st row has a single minimum, indicating unimodal behavior, whereas the 1203rd and 3709th rows each exhibit two local minima, clearly showing non-unimodal patterns.
As a result, it is challenging to apply continuous optimization techniques like gradient descent or even one-dimensional search methods like ternary search
1
1

1

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ternary_search
for selecting the scale
s
.

To address this issue, we perform a uniformly spaced grid search in the parameter space to ensure sufficient scale exploration. Specifically, the grid points for candidate scales are defined as follows:

\mathcal{G}_{T}=\left\{\frac{\text{max}(x)-\text{min}(x)}{2^{k}-1}\cdot\frac{i}{T}\,\middle|\,i\in\{1,2,\cdots,T-1,T\}\right\},

(9)

where
T
denotes the total number of grid points.
We evaluate the quantization error at each grid point and select the scale with the minimum error as the optimal solution.

To further enhance the search efficiency, we propose a coarse-to-fine grid search strategy, which consists of the following two phases:

•

Coarse Grid Search
: A preliminary search is conducted using a coarse grid
\mathcal{G}_{T_{c}}
, allowing rapid localization of a promising local region where the optimal solution may reside.

•

Fine Grid Search
: After obtaining the coarse search optimal solution
s_{c}
, we construct a local candidate set by selecting the
\lfloor T/(2T_{c})\rfloor
nearest neighbors on each side of
s_{c}
from a finer grid
\mathcal{G}_{T}
, along with
s_{c}
itself. This results in a total of
2\lfloor T/(2T_{c})\rfloor+1
scales, enabling more accurate estimation with relatively low overhead.

This strategy effectively reduces the original
T
expensive error evaluations—each involving the search for the minimum loss and its corresponding zero-point—to approximately
T_{c}+T/T_{c}
. In particular, when
T_{c}
is set approximately to
\sqrt{T}
, the total number of evaluations further decreases to around
2\sqrt{T}
. As a result, this approach significantly reduces computational overhead while still providing a good approximation to the optimal solution, achieving a favorable trade-off between quantization accuracy and search efficiency.

4.3
Lightweight Distillation

To better demonstrate NeUQI’s effectiveness, we incorporate additional lightweight distillation experiments for comparison. Specifically, to enhance the alignment between the behaviors of the quantized model and the original non-quantized model, we perform knowledge distillation on a small set of samples. During distillation, only the continuous parameters of the quantized model—specifically, the scales, zero-points, and the
RMSNorm
layers—are updated. The language model head and embedding layer, which remain in their original precision, are excluded from optimization to avoid distillation-induced bias and reduce memory overhead. The distillation loss is the mean squared error between the final-layer hidden states of the original and quantized models.

Conclusion

To the best of our knowledge, our proposed NeUQI is the first work to remove the zero-point integer constraint in parameter initialization for
\geq 2
-bit uniform quantization. It consistently outperforms existing methods and, under the comparable memory footprint, can even surpass non-quantized models from the same model family, improving the practicality of low-bit-width quantization.

Appendix A
Limitations of Min-Max Initialization

While practitioners widely adopt min-max initialization as a matter of convention, it is not optimal concerning quantization error—even under ideal conditions. To illustrate this, consider the following lemma:

Lemma 1
.

If weights are independently and uniformly drawn from an interval
[a,b]
, then the optimal parameters to for
k
-bit uniform quantization to minimize excepted quantization error
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{U}(a,b)}[(Q_{s,z}(x)-x)^{2}]
are given by

s=\frac{b-a}{2^{k}},\quad z=\frac{a}{s}+\frac{1}{2}.

(10)

The proof of Lemma
1
is in Appendix
B
. This result leads to a revised understanding of the low-error interval: it was previously defined as
[zs,(2^{k}-1+z)s]
, but is now more accurately characterized as
[(z-\frac{1}{2})s,(z+2^{k}-\frac{1}{2})s]
. Motivated by this observation, we propose an improved initialization strategy, termed
Min-Max+
, in which the quantization parameters are given by:

s=\frac{\text{max}(x)-\text{min}(x)}{2^{k}},\quad z=\left\lfloor\frac{\text{min}(x)}{s}+\frac{1}{2}\right\rceil.

(11)

Interestingly, Min-Max+ closely resembles the shrink-scale technique used in MagR, where a manually defined factor
\beta
is used to shrink the scale, and the scale is computed as
s=\beta\cdot(\text{max}(x)-\text{min}(x))/(2^{k}-1)
. In contrast, Min-Max+ implicitly sets
\beta=(2^{k}-1)/2^{k}
and simultaneously adjusts the zero-point to better align the low-error interval with the parameter range.

Proofs of Optimal Quantization Parameter and Lemma
1

We begin by relaxing the uniform quantization constraint that requires equal spacing between adjacent quantized values in the set
\mathcal{Q}
, allowing non-uniform spacing. However, the minimum error in the mean squared error objective is achieved when the spacing is uniform.

The expression for the expected quantization error takes the form

\mathcal{E}=\frac{1}{b-a}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\int_{b_{i}}^{b_{i+1}}(x-q_{i})^{2}\,dx,

(12)

where
N=2^{k}
,
a=b_{0}<\cdots<b_{N}=b
,
q_{i}
is the
i
-th quantized value and
[b_{i},b_{i+1})
assumed to be quantized to
q_{i}
. The probability density function is given by
f_{X}(x)=1/(b-a)
.

For fixed interval, minimizing

\int_{b_{i}}^{b_{i+1}}(x-q_{i})^{2}\,dx

(13)

requires solving

\frac{d}{dy}\int_{b_{i}}^{b_{i+1}}(x-q_{i})^{2}\,dx=\int_{b_{i}}^{b_{i+1}}-2(x-q_{i})\,dx=0,

(14)

which gives

q_{i}^{*}=\frac{1}{b_{i+1}-b_{i}}\int_{b_{i}}^{b_{i+1}}x\,dx=\frac{b_{i}+b_{i+1}}{2}.

(15)

The corresponding quantization error is

\int_{b_{i}}^{b_{i+1}}\left(x-\frac{b_{i}+b_{i+1}}{2}\right)^{2}dx=\frac{(b_{i+1}-b_{i})^{3}}{12}.

(16)

Denote
\Delta_{i}=b_{i+1}-b_{i}>0
with
\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\Delta_{i}=b-a
. Then

\mathcal{E}=\frac{1}{12(b-a)}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\Delta_{i}^{3}.

(17)

Since
g(\Delta)=\Delta^{3}
is strictly convex for
\Delta>0
, Jensen’s inequality implies
\sum\Delta_{i}^{3}
is minimized when all
\Delta_{i}
are equal, i.e.

\Delta_{i}=\frac{b-a}{N}=\frac{b-a}{2^{k}}.

(18)

Finally, the optimal uniform quantization parameters can be obtained by:

s=\Delta_{i}=\frac{b-a}{2^{k}},\quad z=\frac{q^{*}_{0}}{s}=\frac{b_{0}+b_{1}}{2s}=\frac{a+(a+s)}{2s}=\frac{a}{s}+\frac{1}{2}.

D.3.1
GPTQ Implementation

GPTQ builds upon the principle of Optimal Brain Surgeon (OBS)
[
12
]
, designed for pruning. OBS applies a Taylor expansion of the loss function around the original weight
w
. Under the assumption that the model has reached convergence, the first-order term vanishes as a result of the stationarity condition, and higher-order terms of order three and above are considered negligible. Therefore, the dominant contribution is given by:

\mathcal{L}(\hat{w})\approx\frac{1}{2}(\hat{w}-w)^{\top}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{L}}{\partial\hat{w}^{2}}(\hat{w}-w)+\mathcal{L}(w)

(20)

The quantization loss for a single-row in linear layer is the squared
\ell_{2}
-norm between the quantized output and the original output:

\mathcal{L}(\hat{w})=\frac{1}{2}\|X\hat{w}-Xw\|_{2}^{2},

(21)

where
\hat{w}
denotes the quantized weight vector and
w
represents the original weight vector. This formulation follows a standard least-squares loss structure. The Hessian matrix of this loss with respect to
\hat{w}
is computed as:

\nabla^{2}_{\hat{w}}\mathcal{L}(\hat{w})=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\hat{w}^{2}}\frac{1}{2}\|X\hat{w}-Xw\|_{2}^{2}=X^{\top}X.

(22)

Thus, the matrix
H
, representing the Hessian matrix, is given by:

H=\nabla^{2}_{\hat{w}}\mathcal{L}(\hat{w})=X^{\top}X.

(23)

To ensure that the Hessian matrix
H
is positive definite, it is common practice to add a small scalar
\lambda>0
to its diagonal entries. This modification is equivalent to augmenting the quantization loss with a regularization term
\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\hat{w}-w\|^{2}
, which penalizes large deviations between the quantized weights
\hat{w}
and the original weights
w
. In practice,
\lambda
is set to
0.01
times the mean of the diagonal entries of
H
, following GPTQ.

Given this formulation, we consider the problem of quantizing a single column vector
w
from the weight matrix. Specifically, let us focus on quantizing the element at a particular position
p
within the vector
w
. In other words,
w_{p}
denotes the scalar entry at the
p
-th position of
w
.
We define a compensation vector
\delta^{p}
, which compensates for the quantization-induced error in
w
resulting from quantizing
w_{p}
. In this formulation, the update at coordinate
p
reflects the quantization of
w_{p}
, while changes at other coordinates are to compensate for the loss caused by this quantization. The quantized vector is then given by
\hat{w}=w+\delta^{p}
, with the constraint that the
p
-th coordinate of
\hat{w}
matches the quantized value. Accordingly, we solve the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\text{min}_{\delta^{p}}

\displaystyle(\delta^{p})^{\top}H\delta^{p}

(24)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle e_{p}^{\top}\delta^{p}=\hat{w}_{p}-w_{p}

Using the method of Lagrange multipliers, we construct the Lagrangian:

\mathcal{L}(\delta^{p},\lambda)=(\delta^{p})^{\top}H\delta^{p}+\lambda\left(e_{p}^{\top}\delta^{p}-(\hat{w}_{p}-w_{p})\right)

(25)

Taking the gradient with respect to
\delta^{p}
and setting it to zero:

\nabla_{\delta^{p}}\mathcal{L}=2H\delta^{p}+\lambda e_{p}=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\delta^{p}=-\frac{\lambda}{2}H^{-1}e_{p}

(26)

Substituting back into the constraint:

e_{p}^{\top}\delta^{p}=-\frac{\lambda}{2}e_{p}^{\top}H^{-1}e_{p}=\hat{w}_{p}-w_{p}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\lambda=-\frac{2(\hat{w}_{p}-w_{p})}{(H^{-1})_{pp}}

(27)

Thus, we obtain:

\delta^{p}=\frac{(\hat{w}_{p}-w_{p})}{(H^{-1})_{pp}}H^{-1}e_{p},\quad\mathcal{L}(\delta^{p})=\frac{(\hat{w}_{p}-w_{p})^{2}}{(H^{-1})_{pp}}

(28)

Based on this result, we can adopt a greedy strategy: at each iteration, the position
p
that results in the minimal increase in quantization loss
\mathcal{L}(\delta^{p})
is selected, the corresponding update is applied. However, to better utilize GPU parallelism and improve computational efficiency, we follow a heuristic implemented in the GPTQ repository, specifically in the
gptq.py
script
7
7

7

https://github.com/IST-DASLab/gptq/blob/main/gptq.py
. In this approach, the indices are sorted and traversed in decreasing order of the Hessian diagonals
H_{pp}
.

Assuming we fix a quantization order
i=0,1,\ldots,n-1
, we define:

\delta^{i}_{i:}=\frac{(H_{i:,i:})^{-1}e_{i}}{((H_{i:,i:})^{-1})_{ii}},\quad\delta^{i}_{:i}=\textbf{0}

(29)

where,
\delta^{i}
denotes the update basis vector for position
i
, satisfying
(\delta^{i})_{i}=1
. The actual update applied to the weight vector is
(\hat{w}_{i}-w_{i})\delta^{i}
.

For any
j\geq i
, we have:

H_{j,:}\delta^{i}=e_{j}^{\top}H_{i:,i:}\left(\frac{(H_{i:,i:})^{-1}e_{i}}{((H_{i:,i:})^{-1})_{ii}}\right)=1_{i=j}\left(((H_{i:,i:})^{-1})_{ii}\right)^{-1}

(30)

Therefore, the update matrix satisfies:

H\Delta=U,

(31)

where
\Delta=[\delta^{0},\delta^{1},\ldots,\delta^{n-1}]
is a unit lower-triangular matrix, and the resulting matrix
U
is upper-triangular because for
j>i
,
U_{j,i}=0
.

This relation implies that
H
can be factorized as

H=U\Delta^{-1},

(32)

which corresponds to a UL decomposition of
H
, where
\Delta^{-1}
is unit lower-triangular. On the other hand, the Hessian matrix
H
is positive-definite and thus admits a unique LDL decomposition of the form

H=(L^{\top}D)L,

(33)

where
L
is also a unit lower-triangular matrix and
D
is diagonal matrix.

Since both (
32
) and (
33
) represent decompositions of the same matrix
H
with a unit lower-triangular factor on the right, and the unit lower-triangular factor in such factorizations is unique for positive-definite matrices, we conclude that
\Delta=L^{-1}
.

Finally, the algorithm is as follows:

Algorithm 4

GPTQ Quantization

1:

Hessian matrix
H
, original weight vector
w
, quantization operator
Q(\cdot)

2:

Compute LDL decomposition:
H=L^{\top}DL

3:

Compute inverse of unit lower-triangular matrix:
\Delta=L^{-1}

4:

Compute compensation matrix:
L\leftarrow\Delta-I

5:

Initialize quantized weight
\hat{w}
with zeros

6:

for
each index
i=0
to
n-1

do

7:

\hat{w}_{i}\leftarrow Q(w_{i})

8:

w\leftarrow w+L_{:,i}\cdot(\hat{w}_{i}-w_{i})

9:

end

for

10:

return

\hat{w}

D.3.2
LDLQ Implementation

LDLQ is a newly proposed method introduced in QuIP
[
5
]
, which has been empirically validated to be equivalent to GPTQ. It provides an alternative understanding of the GPTQ algorithm through a structurally distinct formulation. Specifically, when quantizing a single row of the weight matrix, LDLQ proceeds coordinate-wise in a fixed order: each coordinate is quantized one at a time, and the unquantized coordinates are adjusted to compensate for the quantization error. This process is captured through a strictly lower-triangular compensation matrix
L
, which defines the final quantized weights via

\hat{w}=Q(\tilde{w}),\quad\tilde{w}=w-L(\hat{w}-w)

(34)

The quantization-induced error is

\eta=\hat{w}-\tilde{w}=(I+L)(\hat{w}-w)

(35)

Solving for
\hat{w}-w
yields

\hat{w}-w=(I+L)^{-1}\eta

(36)

Substituting into the original quadratic loss function gives

\mathcal{L}(\hat{w})=\eta^{\top}(I+L)^{-\top}H(I+L)^{-1}\eta

(37)

Given the LDL decomposition
H=L^{\prime\top}DL^{\prime}
, LDLQ claims that the minimal loss is achieved when
I+L=L^{\prime}
, resulting in

\mathcal{L}(\hat{w})=\eta^{\top}D\eta

(38)

This diagonalization simplifies the loss into a weighted squared norm of the quantization error, enabling an efficient sequential update procedure as described below:

Algorithm 5

LDLQ Quantization

1:

Hessian matrix
H
, original weight vector
w
, quantization operator
Q(\cdot)

2:

Compute LDL decomposition:
H=L^{\prime\top}DL^{\prime}

3:

Initialize quantized weight vector
\hat{w}
with zeros

4:

Compute compensation matrix
L\leftarrow L^{\prime}-I

5:

for
each index
i=0
to
n-1

do

6:

\tilde{w}_{i}\leftarrow w_{i}-L_{i,:}\cdot(\hat{w_{i}}-w_{i})

7:

\hat{w}_{i}\leftarrow Q(\tilde{w}_{i})

8:

end

for

9:

return

\hat{w}

D.3.3
Equivalence between GPTQ and LDLQ

The following interpretation reinforces the equivalence between GPTQ and LDLQ. In particular, we can rewrite the GPTQ formulation as follows:

\hat{w}=Q(\tilde{w}),\quad\tilde{w}=w+(\Delta-I)(\hat{w}-\tilde{w})

(39)

We can derive a more explicit form of
\tilde{w}
by manipulating the second equation in (
39
) as follows:

\displaystyle\tilde{w}

\displaystyle=w+(\Delta-I)(\hat{w}-\tilde{w})

(40)

\displaystyle\Delta\tilde{w}

\displaystyle=w+(\Delta-I)\hat{w}

(41)

\displaystyle\tilde{w}

\displaystyle=\Delta^{-1}w+(I-\Delta^{-1})\hat{w}

(42)

\displaystyle\tilde{w}

\displaystyle=w-(\Delta^{-1}-I)(\hat{w}-w)

(43)

On the other hand, the LDLQ formulation is given by:

\tilde{w}=w-(L^{\prime}-I)(\hat{w}-w)

(44)

By comparing equations (
43
) and (
44
), we observe that the two formulations become equivalent if
\Delta^{-1}=L^{\prime}
. As established in the previous two sections, both
\Delta^{-1}
and
L^{\prime}
correspond to the
L
factor derived from the LDL decomposition. Therefore, LDLQ is mathematically equivalent to GPTQ.

a

D.5
Difference between LeanQuant and NeUQI with Integer Constraint

LeanQuant
{}_{\textit{aff}}

[
35
]
and NeUQI
{}_{\text{int}}
adopt a loss formulation of the form

\mathcal{L}(s,z)=\sum_{i}h_{i}(Q_{s,z}(w_{i})-w_{i})^{2},

(45)

but differ fundamentally in how the weight importance
h_{i}
is defined and how to find the optimal scale and zero-point.

In LeanQuant
{}_{\textit{aff}}
, the definition of
h_{i}
is inspired by the objective used in the first step of GPTQ, which selects the optimal quantization point by minimizing

\frac{(Q_{s,z}(w_{i})-w_{i})^{2}}{(H^{-1})_{ii}},

(46)

leading to
h_{i}=((H^{-1})_{ii})^{-1}
, and further generalized to
h_{i}=((H^{-1})_{ii})^{-p}
with a tunable strength parameter
p
. In contrast,
\text{NeUQI}_{\text{int}}
adopts a diagonal loss approximation by directly setting
h_{i}=H_{ii}
, avoiding the need to invert the Hessian matrix.

The methods also differ in parameter search strategies.
However, LeanQuant
{}_{\textit{aff}}
still operates within the Min-Max paradigm—its grid search essentially enumerates different min and max values, from which they derive the scale and zero-point using the standard Min-Max formula, with a time complexity of
\mathcal{O}(T^{2}n)
, where
T
(typically 2048) denotes the number of grid points and
n
the number of weights.
In contrast,
\text{NeUQI}_{\text{int}}
employs a grid search over candidate scale values, with the zero-point constrained to be a
k
-bit unsigned integer. A simple implementation of this scale-based search has a time complexity of
\mathcal{O}(Tn2^{k})
.
To further accelerate initialization, we identify a more efficient alternative by switching the search perspective from scale to zero-point. For a fixed zero-point, the objective remains a piecewise quadratic function of the scale, similar to the case discussed in Section
4.2
, which enables an optimized search using a similar method with total complexity reduced to
\mathcal{O}(2^{k}\cdot n2^{k}\log(n2^{k}))
, which is independent of
T
and scales better in practice.

D.6
PV-tuning

For training with uniform quantization, we set the learning rate of the quantization parameters to the optimal value determined in our lightweight distillation experiments. The learning rate for quantized weights is selected via grid search from the set
\{30\times,10\times,3\times\}
of the learning rate of the quantization parameters, consistent with the approach used in PV-tuning
[
22
]
. We also adopted PV-Tuning’s constraint that the L2-norm of each quantization update should not exceed 1% of the corresponding weight’s L2-norm. All other components remained consistent with our lightweight distillation framework.

Appendix E
Broader Impact

Large language models are inherently dual-use technologies, enabling beneficial applications and potential misuse. While our method does not enhance model capabilities, it facilitates broader deployment by reducing quantization-induced errors and improving consistency with the original model. This contributes to more reliable and trustworthy outputs, potentially lowering the risk of unintended behaviors such as hallucinations or misleading responses.

Appendix F
Limitations

Although we do not compare NeUQI with other quantization methods based on weight and activation transformations or fine-tuning, we believe our main experiments are sufficient to validate the effectiveness of our approach. These alternative methods typically require additional training or model modifications and adopt fundamentally different design principles. As such, they are orthogonal to our NeUQI and could be integrated with NeUQI to achieve further improvements, which we leave as an avenue for future work.
Our scheme supports arbitrary zero-points, which may not align with inference kernels such as ExLlamaV2
8
8

8

https://github.com/turboderp-org/exllamav2
. Nonetheless, NeUQI is fully compatible with flexible backends like FLUTE
[
11
]
, LUT-GEMM
[
24
]
, and BitBLAS
[
30
]
, which support mixed precision or lookup-based matrix multiplication.
