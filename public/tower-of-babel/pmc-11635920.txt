Azatriangulene‐Based Conductive C═C Linked Covalent Organic Frameworks with Near‐Infrared Emission
Authors: Ehsan Hamzehpoor, Pegah Ghamari, Yuze Tao, Muhammad Ghufran Rafique, Zhenzhe Zhang, Mahdi Salehi, Robin S. Stein, Jorge Ramos‐Sanchez, Arnaud W. Laramée, Gonzalo Cosa, Christian Pellerin, Ali Seifitokaldani, Rustam Z. Khaliullin, Dmytro F. Perepichka
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTwo near‐infrared (NIR) emissive π‐conjugated covalent organic frameworks (COFs) pTANG1 and pTANG2 are synthesized using Knoevenagel condensation of trioxaazatriangulenetricarbaldehyde (TATANG) with benzene‐ and biphenyldiacetonitriles, respectively. The morphology of the COFs is affected by the size of TATANG precursor crystals. Donor–acceptor interactions in these COFs result in small bandgaps (≈1.6 eV) and NIR emission (λmax = 789 nm for pTANG1). pTANG1 can absorb up to 9 molecules of water per unit cell, which is accompanied by a marked quenching of the NIR emission, suggesting applications as humidity sensors. p‐Doping with magic blue significantly increases the electrical conductivities of the COFs by up to 8 orders of magnitude, with the room temperature conductivity of pTANG1 reaching 0.65 S cm−1, the highest among reported C═C linked COFs. 1H NMR relaxometry, temperature‐dependent fluorescence spectroscopy, and DFT calculations reveal that the higher rigidity of the shorter phenylene linker is responsible for the more extended conjugation (red‐shifted emission, higher electrical conductivity) of pTANG1 compared to pTANG2.

Introduction

Covalent organic frameworks (COFs) are crystalline polymers in which the organic building blocks are linked via covalent bonds into 2D or 3D porous networks.[ 1 ] In 2D COFs, the planar aromatic building blocks can be predictably assembled into cofacial π‐stacking topology, facilitating the transport of exciton or charge carriers.[ 2 ] Fluorescent (and recently, phosphorescent)[ 3 ] COFs with emission in the visible range (400–650 nm) and photoluminescent quantum yields (PLQYs) as high as 81% (at λ max = 521 nm)[ 4 ] have been reported in the last decade[ 5 ] showcasing potential applications in white‐light emitting materials,[ 6 ] bioimaging/sensing,[ 3 , 7 ] and luminescent thermometry.[ 8 ] However, the design of luminescent COFs remains a significant challenge,[ 9 ] especially compared to the more developed applications of COFs in gas storage/separation or catalysis.[ 10 ]

Typical solid‐state quenching pathways, e.g. exciton trapping and bimolecular exciton annihilations such as singlet fission,[ 11 ] are further amplified in porous COFs due to their high surface area which introduces additional quenching mechanisms such as on surface defects and environmental traps (e.g., oxygen/water). The development of near‐infrared (NIR; >700 nm) emissive COFs is even more difficult because of the energy gap law, which predicts an exponential increase in the internal conversion with the decrease of the exciton energy.[ 12 ]

One common strategy to narrow the optical bandgap and achieve NIR emission in linear π‐conjugated polymers is introducing donor–acceptor (D–A) interactions.[ 13 ] In particular, various poly(arylene cyanovinylene)s have been employed since the 1990s to develop NIR polymer light‐emitting diodes.[ 14 ] Several D–A COFs have shown low bandgaps in the NIR region[ 9 , 15 ] (below 1.2 eV / >1000 nm),[ 16 ] but very few NIR emissive COFs have been reported to date and all of them are based on imine (Schiff base) links. (Table S1, Supporting Information).[ 17 ]

Trioxaazatriangulene (TANG), a stable closed‐shell heteroanalogue of the triangulene biradical (Clar's hydrocarbon),[ 18 ] was first synthesized in 2005 by Okada and coworkers.[ 19 ] As a highly electron‐rich (HOMO = −4.87 eV) and nearly planar[ 18 ] derivative of triphenylamine (a classical building block for hole‐transporting materials)[ 20 ] TANG has also attracted interest as a building block for π‐functional materials.[ 21 ] The trigonal symmetry of TANG makes it particularly suitable for creating 2D π‐conjugated lattices, with several theoretical and experimental studies unveiling promising electronic properties.[ 16 , 22 ] We have earlier reported the synthesis of TANG‐based 2D polymer monolayers through Ullmann coupling of tribromo‐TANG on Au(111)[ 22f ] and 2D COFs by Schiff base condensation of triamino‐TANG.[ 16 ] Recently, Dichtel and coworkers have synthesized another imine‐linked TANG‐benzodithiophene COF by transamination.[ 23 ]

Here, we report the synthesis of two NIR‐emissive (λmax > 750 nm) π‐conjugated COFs pTANG1 and pTANG2 using Knoevenagel condensation of TANG‐tricarbaldehyde (TATANG) with benzene‐ and biphenyldiacetonitrile, respectively. p‐Doping of these COFs leads to electrical conductivity of up to 0.65 S cm−1 at room temperature, the highest reported in C═C‐linked COFs (Table S4, Supporting Information).[ 2 , 24 ] We studied how the linker, temperature, and humidity affect the optoelectronic properties of these porous semiconducting materials.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Characterization of TATANG

The original[ 18 ] synthesis of TANG was based on a 6‐step transformation of the starting 2,6‐difluoronitrobenzene, with an overall yield of 19%. In 2020, we proposed a shorter synthetic methodology, using sequential Ullmann coupling of 1,6‐dimethoxyaniline, with a yield of up to ≈21% over four steps. However, the (still) low yield and synthetic reproducibility issues have limited the full exploitation of this building block in COFs. Herein we developed a new simplified synthetic route that affords TANG with a 40% yield in just three steps from commercially available precursors (Scheme 1 ). Bromination of thus prepared TANG results in the tribromo‐TANG (TBTANG) as a yellow solid, poorly soluble in common organic solvents at room temperature. Lithiation‐formylation of TBTANG could nevertheless be performed in a slurry in tetrahydrofuran. The resulting crude TATANG is only sparingly soluble in organic solvents and was first transformed into tris(ethylene acetal) for purification by column chromatography followed by deprotection in acidic conditions to yield pure TATANG as a light orange solid. The electron‐withdrawing aldehyde groups make TATANG more oxidatively stable than unsubstituted TANG (Figures S1–S2, Supporting Information), and significantly more so than triamino‐TANG used in the first TANG COF synthesis.[ 6 ]

Synthesis of TATANG, CNTANG, pTANG1, and pTANG2: i) Cu, CuI, K2CO3, 230 °C, 72 h; ii) BBr3 in CH2Cl2, −78 °C to room temperature, 12 h; iii) K2CO3, DMF, 160 °C, overnight; iv) PyH+Br3 −, EtOH/benzene, reflux, 3 h; v) n‐BuLi, THF, −78 °C, then DMF followed by acetal protection with (CH2OH)2, TsOH, toluene, reflux, 4 h and subsequent deprotection in HCl(conc.):acetone: H2O; vi–viii) corresponding arylacetonitrile derivative, KOH (4 m, aq.), dioxane, 90 °C, 18 h.

Synthesis and Characterization of pTANG1 and pTANG2

Cyanovinylene‐linked[ 2 , 25 ] COFs pTANG1 and pTANG2 were synthesized by base‐catalyzed (KOH) Knoevenagel condensation of TATANG with benzenediacetonitrile or biphenyldiacetonitrile, respectively (Scheme 1). The black precipitates formed were filtered, washed with different solvents (water, hot DMF, and acetone), and dried in vacuum to afford the desired COFs in a ≈85–88% yield. A model compound (CNTANG) was synthesized from TATANG and phenylacetonitrile under the same conditions.

The IR spectroscopy of the obtained COF powders and CNTANG shows the disappearance of the TATANG carbonyl stretch at 1685 cm−1 and the emergence of C≡N stretching at 2212 cm−1, indicative of the successful transformation of the aldehyde groups into cyanovinylene linkages (Figure 1a). Raman spectroscopy shows new C═C stretching at 1573 cm−1 (for pTANG1) and 1579 cm−1 (for pTANG2), further confirming the formation of vinylene linkages (Figure S4a, Supporting Information). The lower C═C frequency of pTANG1 compared to pTANG2 indicates a slightly lower bond order suggesting better resonance delocalization in the former. Cross‐polarization magic angle spinning (CP‐MAS) 13C NMR spectroscopy of the COFs shows the complete disappearance of the aldehyde signal at 192 ppm (Figure 1b). Powder X‐ray diffraction (PXRD, Figure 1d,e) and small angle X‐ray scattering (SAXS, Figure 1e; Figure S5, Supporting Information) measurements reveal the crystalline nature of both COFs, with characteristic diffraction peaks at 2θ = 3.35° (100), 5.7° (110), and 8.7° (210) for pTANG1 and 2.76° (100) and 4.75° (110) for pTANG2. Scherrer analysis of the (100) reflection width (FWHM = 0.8° and 0.9° for pTANG1 and pTANG2, respectively; inset of Figure 1d,e) suggests an average crystallite size of at least ≈10 nm for pTANG1 and ≈9.2 nm for pTANG2. The (001) reflection at 2θ ≈26° in both COFs corresponds to an interlayer stacking of ≈3.5 Å, and its broad appearance is an expected result of the turbostratic disorder in 2D COFs.[ 26 ] Full‐profile Pawley refinement (Figure 1d,e) confirmed that the PXRD patterns agree with the DFT‐calculated (HSE06) optimized unit cell of AA‐stacked COFs (a = b = 32.7 Å, c = 3.7 Å, α = β = 90°, γ = 120° for pTANG1 and a = b = 39.9 Å, c = 3. Å, α = β = 90°, γ = 120° for pTANG2; ≤0.1 Å difference between the DFT‐calculated and Pawley refined unit cells). The diffractograms agree best with the simulated XRD patterns of the eclipsed (AA) stacking arrangement, but we cannot exclude the relatively large (≤10 Å) interlayer slippage (Figure S6, Supporting Information).

Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) analysis of the N2 adsorption isotherm at 77 K shows an unexpectedly low surface area of 132 m2 g−1 for pTANG1 and 35 m2 g−1 for pTANG2 (Figure 1e; Figures S7 and S8, Supporting Information; theoretical value of 1290 and 1822 m2 g−1 for an AA‐stacked unit‐cell), possibly a result of the pore blockade due to the large interlayer slippage suggested above. Nevertheless, the average pore diameter of pTANG1 calculated from the N2 absorption isotherms is ≈23 Å (Figure 1c), in line with the simulated pore diameter of 24 Å (the shortest distance between the opposite CN groups). High‐resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR‐TEM) of COF particles (prepared by dispersion in N‐methylpyrrolidone) shows that the individual COF particles are made of small (≈1 µm) needle‐like crystals (Figure 1g; Figure S9, Supporting Information). HR‐TEM images further reveal a well‐ordered layered structure with an inter‐planar distance of ≈3.7 Å (Figure 1f; Figure S10, Supporting Information), with further evidence of crystalline order coming from the selected area electron diffraction (SAED, Figure 1h; Figure S11, Supporting Information). Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) shows high thermal stability of both COFs with no observable decomposition up to 400 °C (Figure S12, Supporting Information).

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) of as‐synthesized pTANG1 and pTANG2 depicts large rod‐like polycrystalline particles of ≈20–100 µm in length (Figure 2c,d; Figures S13–S15, Supporting Information). The resemblance of these particles to the crystals of the TATANG monomer (Figure 2) and the occasional observation of the hollow tube‐like morphology (Figure S15, Supporting Information) led us to hypothesize that the COF's growth is templated on the surface of the monomer crystals. We tested this hypothesis by comparing the morphology of the pTANG1 COF synthesized from large TATANG crystals (recrystallized from hot toluene)[ 27 ] and from TATANG powder (prepared by grinding the same TATANG crystals). The SEM images show that the size and morphology of the TATANG precursor are maintained in the resulting COF (Figure 2). Such dependence of the COF morphology on that of the monomer is rarely considered but is potentially interesting as a means to engineer novel COF‐based nano/microstructures.[ 28 ] Despite the different morphology of the two pTANG1 samples, their spectroscopic features are identical (Figure S4b, Supporting Information).

Spectroscopic Characterization

Both pTANG1 and pTANG2 are black solids with a broad absorption across the visible range, with absorption onset at 753 nm (1.65 eV) for pTANG1 and 718 nm (1.73 eV) for pTANG2 (Figure 3a; Figure S17, Supporting Information). The significant redshift of the absorption edges compared to the model compound CNTANG (613 nm, Figure 3a) is a result of the extended π‐conjugation in the COFs. Hybrid DFT calculations (HSE06) of free‐standing layers of COFs suggest a direct bandgap energy (E g) of 1.60 and 1.82 eV for pTANG1 and pTANG2, respectively, at the Γ point (Figure S18, Supporting Information) which are similar to the experimental optical gaps. Partial density of states (DOS) analysis shows that both HOMO and LUMO are delocalized, but as expected, the HOMO has the highest density on the TANG building block, whereas the LUMO has the highest density on the arylenedicyanovinylene linkers (Figure S18, Supporting Information). The conduction band of pTANG1 (−2.85 eV) is lower than that of pTANG2 (−2.52 eV), which is the prime reason for the lower E g of the former. This effect can be explained by the stronger electron‐withdrawing character of phenylenedicyanovinylene linker compared to biphenylenedicyanovinylene. The natural transition orbital (NTO) analysis for the first excited state (S1) of the CNTANG model compound confirms the intramolecular charge transfer nature of the transition (Figure S19, Supporting Information).

Photoexcitation of the COF powders results in a strong NIR emission: λmaxFl. = 789 nm, PLQY = 1.0 ± 0.2% for pTANG1; λmaxFl. = 730 nm, PLQY, 1.0 ± 0.2% for pTANG2 (Figure 3a). The red‐shifted emission of the pTANG1 is expected considering its lower bandgap as compared to the pTANG2. The average radiative lifetimes (obtained from the bi‐exponential fit) are 4.2 ns for pTANG1 and 2.4 ns for pTANG2 (Figure 3b; Table S2, Supporting Information), and are not affected by the presence of oxygen (Figure S20, Supporting Information) confirming the fluorescence nature of the emission. NIR fluorescence microscopy of the COF powders shows a homogeneous emission from all COF particles (Figure S21, Supporting Information).

We note that efficient luminescence in the visible range is a common property of many poly(phenylene vinylene) derivatives, and the vinylene‐linked conjugated COFs show some of the highest PLQY in this class of materials.[ 5a ] The large oscillator strength of the S1 state in such extended π‐conjugated systems,[ 29 ] as well as slow inter‐system crossing compared to many other conjugated polymers, are the main reasons for such behavior. However, realizing efficient NIR luminescence is inherently more challenging. In addition to lowering the bandgap (which we achieved through donor‐acceptor interactions), it also requires suppressing the vibrational relaxation, which is a dominant process for the low bandgap polymers. The previous study of the related cyanovinylene‐linked microporous polymer with non‐bridged triphenylamine, showed a significantly blueshifted emission (λmax = 609 nm)[ 30 ] compared to pTANG1, underscoring the importance of the electron‐rich and rigid TANG building block in the design of NIR emissive COFs. It is also possible that the above‐mentioned turbostratic disorder leads to the excitons localization within the 2D layers, minimizing their quenching on defects and self‐quenching.[ 11 , 31 ]

Upon cooling the COF powders to 77 K, the fluorescence emission intensity of pTANG1 slightly increases (by 50%) with almost no change in the lifetime (Figure 3b). In contrast, cooling pTANG2 to 77 K results in a >twofold increase of fluorescence intensity and ≈50% longer emission lifetime (Figure 3d), suggesting a more pronounced vibrational relaxation of excitons in pTANG2 as compared to pTANG1. We attribute this behavior difference to the higher flexibility of the biphenylenedicyanovinylene linker in pTANG2 compared to the phenylenedicyanovinylene linker in pTANG1. This flexibility also explains the more significant fluorescence blueshift upon cooling for pTANG2 (≈20 nm) compared to pTANG1 (≈4 nm), which is due to the freezing of the lowest energy twisted conformation (≈25° dihedral angle, Figure S24, Supporting Information) at 77 K.[ 32 ] A similar ≈25 nm blue shift of luminescence at 77 K was observed in the solution of CNTANG (Figure S3, Supporting Information), confirming the change of conformational population, rather than the suppressed exciton migration,[ 11c ] as its origin.

To quantify the rigidity of the COFs, we used variable temperature solid‐state 1H NMR relaxometry and studied the spin‐lattice relaxation times T 1 for both COFs in a 9.4 T (400 MHz) magnet.[ 33 ] T 1 reflects molecular motions in the MHz regime and a longer decay time indicates a reduced frequency of molecular vibrations compared to the Larmor frequency (minimum T 1 value).[ 29 , 34 ] Both COFs show a monoexponential decay of the integrated 1H NMR intensity at all studied temperatures (Figure S23, Supporting Information), suggestive of a uniform molecular environment (single crystalline phase) throughout the COF powder.[ 35 ] The monotonic decrease of T 1 as the temperature increases up to 100 °C indicates that the matrix in both COFs is rigid, with present motions slower than 400 MHz. Furthermore, the shorter relaxation times and their steeper temperature dependence in pTANG2 compared to pTANG1 (Figure S23c, Supporting Information) indicate more motion in pTANG2. DFT calculations show that the phenyl group rotation barrier in pTANG1 (7.0 kcal mol−1) is approximately twice as large as in pTANG2 (3.7 kcal mol−1, Figure S24, Supporting Information).

Effect of Humidity

When exposed to humid air, vacuum‐dried pTANG1 absorbs a noticeable amount of H2O. Solid‐state 1H NMR spectroscopy shows the presence of 9.2 H2O molecules per pore at ≈80% relative humidity (RH, Figure S25, Supporting Information). Furthermore, dynamic vapor sorption (DVS) analysis showed an S‐shape reversible adsorption/desorption water vapor isotherm (IUPAC Type V; Figure 4a) with an uptake of 9 H2O/unit cell at RH = 80%, in line with 1HNMR observation. While the water uptake (0.18 gwatergCOF−1) is lower compared to the benchmark COFs investigated for atmospheric water harvesting (0.3–0.7 gwatergCOF−1 at 100% RH), full recovery of adsorbed water is observed upon desorption, which is often an issue for more hydrophilic COFs.[ 36 ] The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) analysis of the DVS data reveals a surface area of 137 m2 g−1, which is consistent with the results of N2 isotherm at 77 K.

Inset: luminescence quenching (black) versus the DVS equilibration steps showing that the observed response over 20 min is in line with the water absorption kinetics of the COF.

Importantly, the adsorbed water significantly affects the luminescence of the COFs (Figure 4b,c; Figure S20, Supporting Information). Exposing an activated (vacuum‐dried at 120 °C, 10−3 Torr for 18 h) COF sample to high humidity (saturated vapor of H2O at room temperature, estimated partial vapor pressure of ≈24 Torr) quenches the NIR emission to nearly half the original intensity (Figure 4c). Compared to other imine‐linked COFs with moisture‐sensitive luminescence,[ 37 ] our COF can operate at the NIR range which is advantageous for distant sensing applications due to better penetration of NIR light.

The luminescence quenching/restoration is fully reversible over multiple cycles (Figure 3c). Luminescence lifetime analysis of the water‐adsorbed pTANG1 shows a decrease in the proportion of the fast decay component compared to dry COF, while the slow decay remains almost intact (Figure S26, Supporting Information). This behavior corroborates the presence of two different emissive sites: i) the shorter‐lived species that are exposed to guest molecules and ii) the longer‐lived species, presumably in the blocked pores, inaccessible to the quencher (water) molecules, which limits the overall quenching efficiency.

Electrical Characterization

The DFT calculated (HSE06) band structures of the COFs are characteristic of the hexagonal Kagome lattice and are qualitatively similar to the predicted band structure of other triangulate‐based 2D polymers.[ 38 ] The conduction band is flat (Figure S18, Supporting Information) resulting in an infinite effective electron mass (me ∗) for both COFs, and thus no electron transport is expected. However, the valence band shows a significant dispersion. The effective carrier mass for holes (mh ∗; calculated from the curvature of the valence band) is 0.29 m0 (pTANG1) and 0.46 m0 (pTANG2). These values are similar to previously reported TANG‐based hexagonal lattices, e.g. P2TANG (mh * = 0.31 m0;[ 22f ] mh* = 0.27 m0)[ 22d ] and TANG‐COF[ 16 ] (mh * = 0.28 m0). The lower effective carrier mass for pTANG1 compared to pTANG2 is in line with the previously mentioned better conjugation through a single phenylene bridge.

The direct current (DC) electrical conductivity (σ) of pTANG1 and pTANG2 compressed pellets was determined in the air using a two‐probe measurement. The pristine pTANG1 and pTANG2 pellets show a very low conductivity of 5.1 × 10−9 and 1.5 × 10−10 S cm−1, as no free charge carriers are expected for semiconductors with such band‐gap at room temperature. However, the conductivity can be markedly increased, by up to eight orders of magnitude, upon p‐doping with I2 vapor or a solution of magic blue (MB, [(4‐BrC6H4)3N]•+[SbCl6]‐)[ 23 , 24 ] (Figure 5a,b). The highest conductivity was observed for MB‐doped samples (σRT of 6.5 × 10−1 S cm−1 and 7.1 × 10−2 S cm−1 for pTANG1 and pTANG2, respectively). It is noteworthy that MB‐doped pTANG1 shows the highest conductivity among C═C‐linked COFs and is one of the highest reported to date for COFs (Table S4, Supporting Information).[ 24 ]

a) Current density–voltage curve (J–V) of pristine, I2‐doped, and MB‐doped pTANG1. b) Current density‐voltage curve (J–V) of pristine, I2‐doped and MB‐doped pTANG2. c) log(J)‐log(V) plot of the pristine and MB‐doped pTANG1 showing Ohmic region (<1 V) and trap‐limited/SCLC region (>1 V). d) Current density degradation under voltage stress (0.2 V) for constant voltage bias of I2‐doped and MB‐doped pTANG1. e) EPR signal of pristine, I2‐doped, and MB‐doped pTANG1 at room temperature in air. f) vis–NIR diffuse reflectance spectra of pristine versus I2‐doped and MB‐doped pTANG1 (solid line) and pTANG2 (dashed line) ground in KBr. Sample thickness for all conductivity measurements (in platforms a and b) was 0.6 mm, while the samples for SCLC measurements (c) were thicker >2 mm.

A log–log plot of the J–V curve for the pristine and MB‐doped pTANG1 pellets (Figure 5c) reveals linear ohmic behavior at low voltages (<1 V), from which bulk conductivities were derived using Ohm's law. At higher voltages, the J–V curve displays non‐linear behavior due to space‐charge limited current (SCLC) and trap‐limited charge transfer phenomena, commonly observed in organic semiconductors. While the linear current‐voltage dependence of MB‐doped COFs shows an Ohmic behavior, the iodine‐doped samples display a non‐linear behavior (Figure 5a,b). Furthermore, under a constant bias (0.2 V) the current significantly drops (≈50% over ≈100 s; Figure 5d) for the I2‐doped pellet suggesting a combination of electronic and ionic transport in this sample. The temperature‐dependent conductivity measurements of MB‐doped COFs (cooling from 300 to 77 K) reveal a thermally activated charge transport, typical of organic semiconductors, with a relatively small activation energy estimated at ≈0.1 eV (Figure S28, Supporting Information).[ 16 , 39 ]

Electron spin resonance (ESR) spectroscopy of the pristine pTANG1 reveals a very weak (<0.1% spin concentration) signal at a g‐factor of 2.007 (Figure 5e), possibly due to spontaneous doping in air. The spin concentration in pTANG1 increased after doping with I2 (≈23%) as well as MB (6%), with the g‐value remaining unchanged. Both I2‐ and MB‐doped samples exhibit a broad near‐infrared (NIR) absorption polaron feature, extending above 2500 nm (Figure 5f).[ 16 , 40 ]

Conclusion

We have developed a new 3‐step 40% yield synthesis of the popular heterotriangulene building block TANG. The new TANG‐tricarbaldehyde monomer was used in Knoevenagel polycondensation to synthesize C═C‐linked semiconducting 2D COFs. Scanning electron microscopy has provided insights into the COFs' macroscopic morphology, which appears to be influenced by the monomer's morphology, leading to the formation of COFs directly on the surface of the monomer (poorly soluble) crystals.

The presence of planar and highly electron‐rich TANG moiety and electron‐deficient cyanovinylene linkages (stronger in pTANG1 than pTANG2), resulted in low bandgap COFs with absorbance up to 789 nm. In their pristine (neutral) state, these COFs are electrically insulating (σRT ≈10−9 S cm−1) but doping with magic blue increases their electrical conductivity of up to 0.65 S cm−1, the highest value reported for C═C‐linked COFs to date. Neutral COFs display strong NIR (700–1000 nm) photoluminescence with a solid‐state quantum yield of 1%. The luminescence is significantly influenced by internal lattice vibrations and environmental factors, notably quenching by water molecules.
