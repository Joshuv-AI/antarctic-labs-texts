Aerobic exercise modulates macrophage polarization and promotes cardiac recovery after myocardial infarction in rats
Authors: Mingyan Zhu, Bing Zhou, Jianye Peng, Kong Chen, Yu Wang, Hao Zeng, Xia Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the protective effects of aerobic exercise (AE) on cardiac function and myocardial injury in rats with myocardial infarction (MI), as well as its impact on macrophage polarization.MethodsA rat model of chronic post-infarction remodeling was established by ligation of the left anterior descending coronary artery, while the Sham group underwent threading without ligation. After a 4-week recovery period, rats in the exercise group underwent an 8-week aerobic training program, with all assessments performed at approximately 12 weeks after MI induction. The maximal aerobic velocity test was performed to assess changes in exercise endurance. To evaluate cardiac architecture, inflammatory infiltrates, and the extent of fibrosis, we performed standard histological staining with hematoxylin-eosin and Masson’s trichrome. Triphenyltetrazolium chloride staining was applied to distinguish infarcted and viable myocardial regions. Commercial assay kits were used to detect myocardial injury markers, inflammatory cytokines, and oxidative stress indicators in myocardial tissue or serum. Western blotting and immunofluorescence staining were performed to assess macrophage polarization.ResultsAE markedly improved cardiac function in MI rats. It also attenuated MI size and fibrosis. Serum levels of cardiac damage-associated biomarkers (cTnI, CK-MB, and LDH) were significantly decreased following AE, suggesting attenuation of ongoing myocardial injury and adverse remodeling in the chronic post-infarction setting, as were pro-inflammatory cytokines and oxidative stress indicators. Anti-inflammatory and antioxidant capacity were also enhanced by AE. M1 macrophage markers were reduced, along with upregulation of M2 macrophage markers in the myocardial tissue after AE.ConclusionModerate-intensity AE alleviates myocardial injury, inflammation, and oxidative stress, and promotes macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype in rats with MI. These findings may provide evidence for exercise-based rehabilitation and immune-targeted therapeutic strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13019-026-04603-x.

Introduction

Myocardial infarction (MI) remains one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide. Although contemporary pharmacological and catheter-based treatments have markedly improved clinical outcomes, post-infarction cardiac remodeling and heart failure (HF) continue to pose major clinical challenges [1]. The pathological process following MI is complex, involving not only irreversible cardiomyocyte death due to ischemia and hypoxia but also a robust inflammatory response that contributes to secondary myocardial injury and adverse ventricular remodeling [2]. Accumulating evidence suggests that immune cell infiltration, particularly macrophage activation and polarization, plays a central role in orchestrating the inflammatory and reparative phases after MI [3]. Macrophages exhibit remarkable plasticity and can polarize into distinct functional phenotypes in response to microenvironmental stimuli. M1 macrophages, the classically activated subset, secrete key pro-inflammatory mediators, most notably tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-1β (IL-1β), thereby exacerbating myocardial inflammation and injury [4]. In contrast, the alternatively activated M2 phenotype secretes anti-inflammatory mediators such as interleukin-10 (IL-10) and promotes tissue repair, fibrosis resolution, and angiogenesis [5]. An imbalance favoring M1 polarization has been associated with persistent inflammation and maladaptive cardiac remodeling, whereas enhancing M2 polarization may facilitate myocardial healing and functional recovery. Thus, modulating macrophage polarization represents a promising therapeutic strategy for improving outcomes following MI [6].

Aerobic exercise (AE) is an effective non-pharmacological intervention for cardiovascular protection [7]. Regular AE also exerts systemic anti-inflammatory and antioxidative effects [8]. Recent studies indicate that AE can influence immune cell behavior, including macrophage phenotype switching, in various pathological contexts such as atherosclerosis, adipose tissue inflammation, and metabolic disorders [9]. However, the regulatory effects of AE on macrophage polarization specifically in the setting of MI remain incompletely understood. Previous investigations have focused primarily on the acute inflammatory phase following MI, leaving a significant knowledge gap regarding whether and how exercise modulates macrophage phenotype during the chronic post-infarction remodeling phase. Moreover, although the cardioprotective benefits of exercise are well recognized, the specific contribution of exercise-induced immunomodulation, particularly macrophage polarization, to cardiac recovery after MI has not been directly examined in a controlled animal model of chronic post-infarction remodeling.

It is worth noting that although the classical inflammatory response following MI is characterized by an early wave of macrophage infiltration and a transition from M1-like to M2-like phenotypes within the first days to weeks after infarction, macrophages persist in the myocardium during the chronic post-infarction phase and continue to participate in ventricular remodeling, fibrosis, and HF progression [10]. These chronic-phase macrophages exhibit substantial heterogeneity and contribute to ongoing inflammatory regulation, extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, and adverse cardiac remodeling [4, 11]. Despite this growing recognition of the role of macrophages in chronic post-infarction remodeling, no study to date has systematically investigated whether AE can modulate macrophage polarization during this chronic phase to promote cardiac recovery. In particular, the potential of exercise to shift the M1/M2 balance toward a reparative phenotype in the chronically remodeling heart has not been explored.

To address these knowledge gaps, the present study was designed with the following novel contributions: (1) it is the first to evaluate the effects of AE on macrophage polarization specifically during the chronic post-infarction stage (~ 12 weeks after MI), rather than the acute inflammatory phase; (2) it comprehensively assesses the impact of exercise on cardiac function, histopathological injury, inflammation, oxidative stress, and macrophage phenotype in a single integrated experimental framework; and (3) it provides direct experimental evidence linking exercise-induced macrophage polarization to improved cardiac remodeling and functional recovery. These novel aspects distinguish our study from previous reports that have largely focused on the acute phase or examined exercise effects on cardiac function without exploring the underlying immunomodulatory mechanisms.

Based on these considerations, we hypothesized that AE could improve cardiac function and mitigate myocardial injury in MI rats by modulating macrophage polarization, shifting the balance from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype toward the reparative M2 phenotype during the chronic post-infarction stage. Accordingly, the primary objectives of this study were: (1) to evaluate the protective effects of an 8-week moderate-intensity AE program on cardiac function, histopathological damage, inflammation, and oxidative stress in a rat model of chronic post-infarction remodeling; and (2) to determine whether these beneficial effects are associated with modulation of macrophage polarization from the M1 toward the M2 phenotype. By providing experimental evidence linking exercise-induced macrophage polarization with myocardial recovery in the chronic phase, our findings may offer new insights into exercise-based rehabilitation strategies and identify novel immunoregulatory targets for post-MI management.

Materials and methods

Animals

Thirty specific pathogen-free male Sprague-Dawley rats (8 weeks old, 200–220 g) were obtained from the Model Organisms Center, Shanghai, China (license SCXK [Hu] 2024-0007). After arrival, the animals were housed for one week under a 12-h light/dark cycle at 22 ± 1 °C and 55 ± 3% relative humidity, with ad libitum access to standard chow and water. All protocols were reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Hunan Evidence-based Biotechnology Co., Ltd and carried out in strict accordance with its guidelines (Ethics Number: XZ20258576).

Establishment of the MI rat model

The 30 rats were randomly assigned to two cohorts: a Sham group (n = 10) and an MI group (n = 20). The MI model was established by ligation of the left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery following the protocol described by Zhong et al. [12]. The sample size was determined based on previous studies using the same LAD coronary artery ligation model and AE intervention [13, 14]. In brief, rats were fasted for 8–12 h before surgery. After weighing, anesthesia was induced with 5% isoflurane in a sealed induction chamber until spontaneous movement ceased and respiration became steady. Rats were fixed in the supine position, and their hair on the neck and chest was shaved, followed by standard sterilization. A midline cervical incision was made to expose the trachea, and a catheter with a needle was inserted to establish an artificial airway, connected to a small animal ventilator. Electrocardiogram (ECG) monitoring was initiated. The skin was incised (4–5 cm) along the left midclavicular line, and the third to fourth intercostal space was opened to expose the heart. The LAD coronary artery was ligated 1–2 mm below the left atrial appendage using a 5 − 0 polypropylene suture. Successful MI induction was confirmed by the appearance of pale myocardium distal to the ligation site. In the Sham group, rats underwent the same surgical procedure without ligation. After air evacuation, the thoracic wall was closed in layers using 2 − 0 polypropylene sutures. Postoperatively, 200,000 U of penicillin was administered intramuscularly to prevent infection, and rats were kept warm and monitored.

Exercise protocol

A total of 30 rats were initially included (Sham group, n = 10; MI group, n = 20). During and after the surgical procedure, 4 rats died due to MI or perioperative complications, resulting in an overall mortality rate of 20%, which is consistent with previously reported outcomes for this model. No animals were excluded based on experimental results, and all surviving animals that met the inclusion criteria were randomly allocated to subsequent experimental groups. The 16 surviving MI rats were allowed to recover for 4 weeks and then randomly assigned to either the MI (n = 8) or the MI + AE group (n = 8). The MI + AE group underwent an 8-week aerobic treadmill training program. Before the experiment began, the animals underwent three days of familiarization on the treadmill, running for 15 min each day at 5 m/min on a flat (0°) surface. This adaptation procedure was identical for both Sham and MI animals to minimize baseline differences in locomotor acclimation. After adaptation, the maximal aerobic velocity (MAV) was determined: rats underwent a 15-min warm-up (5 m/min) followed by a 3-min rest. The incremental load test started at 7 m/min, with speed increased by 3 m/min every 3 min until exhaustion. Exhaustion was defined as the inability to maintain treadmill pace, lagging in the rear one-third of the lane more than six times, and failure to respond to sound-light-electric stimulation. The final completed speed was recorded as the MAV. All exercise intensities were normalized according to each animal’s MAV to ensure comparable relative training loads between groups.

The MI + AE group performed moderate-intensity treadmill running based on individual MAV values as follows: 50% MAV (13 m/min) for the first 2 weeks, then 60% MAV (15 m/min) for the following 6 weeks. Training duration started at 30 min on day 1 and was increased by 10 min every third day until a plateau of 60 min per session was reached; training then continued at this level five days per week for eight consecutive weeks. For Sham rats, treadmill procedures and acclimation were identical to those of MI rats, and the same MAV-based framework was used to define exercise conditions, ensuring consistency in relative exercise intensity across groups. All surviving rats completed the 8-week intervention protocol, and no additional mortality occurred during the training period.

Echocardiography

Twenty-four hours after the 8-week AE intervention, cardiac function was evaluated using echocardiography. Anesthesia was induced and maintained with 2% isoflurane delivered by inhalation. Rats were placed on a thermostatically controlled platform. Electrodes were attached to the limbs for ECG synchronization, and body temperature, respiration, and heart rate were continuously monitored. Cardiac geometry and function were assessed with a Vevo 2100 high-resolution ultrasound platform (VisualSonics, Toronto, Canada) fitted with an MS-250 transducer (16-MHz). Left ventricular end-diastolic and end-systolic diameters (LVEDd and LVESd) were recorded in B-mode, and ejection fraction (LVEF) was computed using the standard Teichholz formula [12].

For each rat, three consecutive stable cardiac cycles were recorded and averaged. Two experienced operators blinded to group assignment independently performed measurements, and the mean values were used for analysis.

In vivo hemodynamic monitoring

After the AE intervention, hemodynamic measurements were performed to assess cardiac function. Following anesthesia, rats were fixed on a thermostatically controlled surgical table. A midline cervical incision was made to expose the right common carotid artery, into which a saline-heparin prefilled catheter was carefully inserted and advanced into the left ventricle. The catheter was connected to a pressure transducer coupled with a multichannel physiological recording system (PowerLab, ADInstruments, Sydney, Australia) for real-time data acquisition. We monitored left ventricular systolic pressure (LVSP), end-diastolic pressure (LVEDP), and the peak rates of pressure development (+ dP/dt_max) and relaxation (− dP/dt_max). After data collection, rats were euthanized, and cardiac tissues were harvested for further analyses.

Histopathological staining

Myocardial infarcted tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, dehydrated, cleared in xylene, and embedded. Five-micrometer paraffin sections were processed with hematoxylin-eosin (HE) and Masson’s trichrome kits (Solarbio Life Sciences, Beijing), coverslipped with neutral balsam, and viewed under a light microscope. Representative images were captured.

Triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) staining

Hearts were excised and sliced transversely from apex to base along the atrioventricular groove into ~ 1 mm sections. Slices were immersed in 2% TTC at 37 °C for 15 min in darkness and then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. Viable myocardium appeared red, whereas infarcted regions remained white. Infarct area was measured with Image-Pro Plus (Media Cybernetics, Rockville, MD).

Biochemical assays

Myocardial reactive oxygen species (ROS) content was evaluated using the dihydroethidium fluorescence probe kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Briefly, frozen heart sections were stained according to the manufacturer’s protocol and observed under a fluorescence microscope.

Blood was collected from the orbital venous plexus, centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 10 min, and serum was stored at -80 °C. Myocardial tissue homogenates were centrifuged at 4 °C to collect the supernatant. Plasma lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB) were quantified with colorimetric kits from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute. Myocardial superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity and malondialdehyde (MDA) content were assayed using Beyotime Biotechnology reagents, following the suppliers’ protocols.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Serum levels of cardiac troponin I (cTnI), IL-1β, IL-10, and TNF-α were measured using ELISA kits (Lianke Bio, Hangzhou, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, standards and samples were added to antibody-coated 96-well plates and incubated, followed by washing, enzyme conjugate, and substrate reactions. Absorbance at 450 nm was recorded, and sample concentrations were derived from the corresponding standard curve.

Western blotting

Myocardial tissues were lysed in radio-immunoprecipitation assay lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors (Beyotime Biotechnology) on ice for 30 min. The lysates were cleared by centrifugation (12,000 × g, 15 min, 4 °C), and protein content was quantified with a bicinchoninic acid assay kit (Beyotime). Thirty-microgram aliquots were resolved on sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis gels, electro-transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (MilliporeSigma), blocked for 1 h in 5% non-fat milk at room temperature, and probed overnight at 4 °C with the indicated primary antibodies against inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), cluster of differentiation 86 (CD86), arginase-1 (Arg-1), cluster of differentiation 206 (CD206), TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-10, and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH; internal control). Following three Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 washes, the blots were exposed to horseradish peroxidase-linked secondary antibodies (1:5,000) for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoreactive bands were developed with Beyotime enhanced chemiluminescence reagents and quantified by densitometry in Image-Pro Plus, and relative protein expression was normalized to GAPDH.

Immunofluorescence staining

Myocardial tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, dehydrated through an ascending ethanol series, paraffin-embedded, and cut into 5-µm sections. After xylene deparaffinization and graded ethanol rehydration to phosphate-buffered saline, antigen retrieval was performed, followed by 0.3% Triton X-100 permeabilization (10 min) and 30-min blocking in 10% normal goat serum. Slides were then incubated overnight at 4°C with the indicated primary antibodies: F4/80 (macrophage marker, 1:200), CD86 (M1 marker, 1:200), or CD206 (M2 marker, 1:200). The following day, sections were incubated for 1 h at room temperature in the dark with Alexa Fluor 488- or 594-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500), then counterstained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole. Fluorescence images were acquired on a Nikon microscope, capturing five random fields per section. Fluorescence intensity was quantified in Image-Pro Plus to assess macrophage polarization.

Statistical analysis

Values are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical comparisons were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (San Diego, CA, USA). Comparisons between two groups were performed using an unpaired Student’s t-test. Differences among three or more groups were evaluated by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test for post hoc comparisons. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

AE improves cardiac function in MI rats

To evaluate the effects of AE on cardiac function after MI, a rat model was established by LAD ligation followed by 8 weeks of aerobic training. The MAV of rats in the Sham and MI groups is shown in Fig. 1A. The MAV of the MI group was 10 m/min lower than that of the Sham group (p < 0.01), indicating reduced exercise endurance after MI. Gross examination revealed that MI rats exhibited a pale anterior left ventricular wall and enlarged chambers, whereas these abnormalities were markedly improved after AE (Fig. 1B). Echocardiographic and hemodynamic analyses showed that MI rats had significantly increased LVEDd, LVESd, and LVEDP, and decreased LVEF, LVSP, +dP/dt_max, and − dP/dt_max (p < 0.01), suggesting impaired left ventricular diastolic function. Notably, AE markedly reduced LVEDd, LVESd, and LVEDP, while improving LVEF, LVSP, +dP/dt_max, and − dP/dt_max compared with sedentary MI rats (Fig. 1C–I, p < 0.05). These findings indicate that AE effectively improves left ventricular function and confers cardioprotection in MI.

AE alleviates histopathological injury and myocardial damage in MI rats

HE and Masson’s trichrome staining revealed that myocardial fibers in the Sham group were intact and well-organized, without inflammatory infiltration. In contrast, the MI group showed disrupted and disordered fibers, marked inflammatory infiltration, and fibrosis, indicating severe structural damage. AE markedly ameliorated these pathological changes, as shown by more organized fiber alignment and reduced fibrosis (Fig. 2A). To further evaluate the extent of cardiac damage and remodeling in the chronic post-infarction stage, serum LDH, CK-MB, and cTnI levels were measured. Compared with the Sham group, all three biomarkers were significantly elevated in MI rats at 12 weeks after MI induction (p < 0.01), indicating persistent myocardial injury and adverse cardiac remodeling in the chronic phase. AE significantly decreased LDH, CK-MB, and cTnI levels (p < 0.01), suggesting that exercise training attenuates ongoing myocardial damage and ameliorates post-infarction remodeling (Fig. 2B–D). Consistently, TTC staining showed that the MI area in MI rats after AE decreased by approximately 46% (Fig. 2E, p < 0.01).

AE suppresses inflammation and oxidative stress in MI rats

As shown in Figs. 3A–C, TNF-α and IL-1β levels were significantly increased in the MI group, whereas IL-10 was markedly decreased (p < 0.01), indicating a pro-inflammatory state. AE significantly downregulated TNF-α and IL-1β and upregulated IL-10 (p < 0.01), suggesting modulation of the inflammatory microenvironment. Regarding oxidative stress (Figs. 3D–G), the MI group exhibited elevated myocardial ROS and serum MDA levels and decreased SOD activity (p < 0.01), indicating oxidative injury. AE significantly reduced ROS and MDA levels while increasing SOD activity (p < 0.01). Collectively, these data indicate that regular AE lessens cardiac damage in post-MI rats by curbing inflammatory responses and oxidative stress.

AE promotes M2 macrophage polarization in MI rats

To investigate whether AE modulates macrophage polarization during the chronic post-infarction stage, we examined M1 and M2 macrophage markers in myocardial tissues. Notably, the macrophage populations assessed in this study represent remodeling-associated cardiac macrophages that persist in the chronic phase after MI, rather than transient inflammatory macrophages involved in the acute infarction response. This distinction is supported by accumulating evidence demonstrating persistent macrophage accumulation in the post-MI heart during the chronic phase [15]. Western blotting results showed that iNOS and CD86 were upregulated, while Arg-1 and CD206 were markedly downregulated in the MI group (p < 0.01), indicating a predominance of pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages. AE significantly decreased iNOS and CD86 expression while increasing Arg-1 and CD206 levels (Fig. 4A, p < 0.05), suggesting promotion of M2 polarization. Consistently, dual immunofluorescence staining revealed that F4/80⁺CD86⁺ cells were reduced, whereas F4/80⁺CD206⁺ cells were increased by AE (Fig. 4B, p < 0.01). Collectively, these results suggest that AE regulates macrophage polarization by suppressing the M1 phenotype and enhancing the M2 phenotype, which may underlie its protective effects on myocardial inflammation and tissue repair after MI.

Discussion

MI is a life-threatening cardiovascular event that triggers extensive myocardial necrosis and initiates a cascade of pathological remodeling processes [16]. Although reperfusion therapies have improved survival rates, post-infarction complications such as ventricular dilation, fibrosis, and HF remain significant clinical challenges [17]. Understanding the mechanisms that mitigate these secondary injuries is therefore critical for developing effective interventions to enhance myocardial recovery and preserve cardiac function. In the present study, we demonstrated that moderate-intensity AE significantly improved cardiac function, reduced myocardial injury, and alleviated inflammation and oxidative stress in rats following MI. Furthermore, AE promoted macrophage polarization from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype toward the reparative M2 phenotype, suggesting a potential immunomodulatory mechanism underlying its cardioprotective effects.

It is important to clarify the temporal context of the present study. All animals were evaluated approximately 12 weeks after MI induction (4 weeks of recovery followed by 8 weeks of exercise training), representing a chronic post-infarction remodeling stage rather than the acute phase of MI. Accordingly, the serum biomarkers cTnI, CK-MB, and LDH measured in this study were interpreted as indicators of persistent myocardial injury, ongoing adverse remodeling, and chronic cardiac dysfunction, consistent with their reported elevation in chronic HF models [18, 19]. Although these markers are classically associated with acute myocardial necrosis, accumulating evidence indicates that they can remain elevated in the chronic phase of post-infarction HF, reflecting continuous cardiomyocyte injury and progressive ventricular remodeling [20, 21].

First, our findings indicate that AE effectively improves cardiac function and attenuates structural damage in MI rats. The observed reductions in LVEDd, LVESd, and LVEDP, coupled with increases in LVEF, LVSP, +dP/dt_max, and -dP/dt_max after 8 weeks of exercise, reflect improved left ventricular systolic and diastolic function. These results are consistent with previous reports showing that structured aerobic training enhances cardiac contractility and reduces ventricular dilation following myocardial injury [22]. The improved gross morphology of the left ventricle, with reduced pallor and chamber enlargement, further corroborates the beneficial effects of exercise on myocardial remodeling [23]. Mechanistically, the improvement in cardiac function may be multifactorial. Potential contributors include reduced myocardial fibrosis, enhanced calcium handling in surviving cardiomyocytes, improved endothelial function, and favorable changes in ventricular geometry [24]. Importantly, our observation that exercise reduced infarct size by approximately 46% at 12 weeks post-MI suggests that the benefits of exercise extend beyond the acute salvage period and may involve long-term modulation of the ECM and reduction of ongoing myocyte loss. Similarly, a study [25] reported a reduction in infarct size with a similar exercise protocol but assessed at an earlier time point (8 weeks post-MI). Furthermore, the restoration of myocardial fiber integrity and reduced fibrosis observed in our exercised MI rats, as demonstrated by HE and Masson’s trichrome staining, are consistent with findings from reports that exercise training attenuates interstitial fibrosis in post-infarction HF [26, 27]. Collectively, these results suggest that exercise mitigates structural myocardial damage and preserves tissue integrity post-MI, with effects that are sustained into the chronic phase.

Beyond these structural and functional improvements, the protective effects of AE appear closely linked to modulation of inflammation and oxidative stress [8]. In the present study, MI induced a pronounced pro-inflammatory state, as evidenced by elevated TNF-α and IL-1β and decreased IL-10 levels [28], along with enhanced oxidative stress indicated by increased ROS and MDA levels and reduced SOD activity. These findings are in agreement with clinical observations in post-MI patients, where elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines are associated with adverse remodeling and poor prognosis [29]. AE significantly reversed these alterations, demonstrating potent anti-inflammatory and antioxidative properties. Notably, the reduction in inflammatory cytokines observed in our exercised group is comparable to that reported in a study of exercise interventions in post-MI rats [30], supporting the robustness of the exercise effect. The concurrent reduction in oxidative stress markers and enhancement of antioxidant defenses suggest a dual mechanism by which exercise creates a myocardial microenvironment conducive to tissue repair and functional recovery. This is consistent with the emerging concept of exercise-induced hormesis, whereby moderate-intensity exercise stimulates endogenous antioxidant and anti-inflammatory pathways [31]. By suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokines, enhancing anti-inflammatory mediators, and improving antioxidant defenses, AE likely promotes a shift from a maladaptive inflammatory milieu toward a reparative environment in the chronically remodeling heart.

Importantly, our study provides evidence that AE promotes M2 macrophage polarization in the infarcted myocardium during the chronic post-infarction phase. In the chronically remodeling heart, we observed a predominance of M1-like macrophages, as indicated by elevated iNOS and CD86 expression and reduced Arg-1 and CD206 levels [32, 33], suggesting an ongoing pro-inflammatory bias in the untreated post-MI heart. AE significantly shifted this balance, decreasing M1 markers and increasing M2 markers at both protein and cellular levels. These findings are consistent with recent studies demonstrating that exercise promotes an M2-like phenotype in various tissues, including adipose tissue [34] and atherosclerotic plaques [35]. However, our study extends these observations by demonstrating for the first time that this immunomodulatory effect occurs specifically in the context of chronic post-infarction remodeling, a stage in which macrophage persistence and plasticity have not been previously linked to exercise-induced cardioprotection. Mechanistically, the shift toward M2 polarization may involve exercise-induced activation of the signal transducer and activator of transcription 6 (STAT6) and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARγ) pathways, which are known to promote alternative macrophage activation [36]. Additionally, exercise-induced reduction in tissue hypoxia and oxidative stress may further create a microenvironment that favors M2 polarization [37]. The observed increase in reparative M2 macrophages and concomitant decrease in pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages suggests that AE facilitates resolution of chronic inflammation and supports tissue remodeling after MI. Notably, the M2 polarization observed in our study is comparable to that achieved with pharmacological interventions such as PPARγ agonists in post-MI models [38], suggesting that exercise may offer a non-pharmacological alternative with comparable immunomodulatory efficacy. This immunomodulatory effect likely underlies, at least in part, the functional and structural benefits observed in exercised rats. Furthermore, the sustained M2 polarization at 12 weeks post-MI in the exercised group indicates that exercise training may have lasting effects on the immune microenvironment, which could be clinically relevant for long-term post-MI management.

Having established that AE promotes a shift toward M2 polarization in the chronically remodeling heart, we next consider the biological basis for the persistent macrophage population observed at 12 weeks after MI. Although the classical paradigm describes an early wave of macrophage infiltration followed by a transition from M1-like to M2-like phenotypes within the first days to weeks after infarction, recent evidence has substantially revised this view. Studies have demonstrated that macrophages continue to accumulate in the chronically failing heart after MI through both local proliferation and sustained monocyte recruitment from the circulation [39]. Furthermore, macrophages in the chronic phase exhibit remarkable phenotypic heterogeneity beyond the classical M1/M2 dichotomy and actively participate in ongoing inflammatory regulation, ECM turnover, fibrosis progression, and ventricular remodeling [4, 10, 11]. Importantly, macrophage polarization, spanning both pro-inflammatory and reparative phenotypes, has been reported during the cardiac repair and remodeling phase after MI and remains closely associated with chronic inflammation, fibrosis, and HF progression [15, 40]. Therefore, the macrophage populations assessed in our study are more appropriately interpreted as remodeling-associated cardiac macrophages that persist during the chronic post-infarction stage, rather than transient inflammatory macrophages involved in the acute response to MI. The observed M1/M2 phenotypic differences reflect the ongoing balance between inflammatory and reparative macrophage activities in the chronically remodeled myocardium, and AE appears to shift this balance toward the reparative M2 phenotype, thereby contributing to improved cardiac remodeling and function.

The findings of this study have potential clinical and translational implications, particularly in the context of cardiothoracic surgery and postoperative cardiac rehabilitation. Patients undergoing coronary artery bypass grafting or other cardiothoracic interventions for ischemic heart disease often exhibit a heightened inflammatory state, which may predispose them to adverse postoperative outcomes, including perioperative myocardial injury, delayed recovery, and progression of HF [41]. The demonstration that moderate-intensity AE can modulate macrophage polarization toward a reparative M2 phenotype and reduce systemic inflammation suggests that structured exercise programs, when appropriately timed, could serve as a valuable adjunctive therapy to enhance perioperative myocardial protection and improve surgical outcomes. Specifically, our findings support the rationale for incorporating exercise training into prehabilitation (preoperative exercise conditioning) and postoperative cardiac rehabilitation protocols for patients undergoing cardiothoracic procedures. Preoperative exercise training has been shown to reduce postoperative complications and length of hospital stay in cardiac surgery patients [42], and our results provide a mechanistic basis for these clinical observations by implicating macrophage polarization and inflammation resolution as potential mediators. Furthermore, for patients with chronic post-infarction HF who are not candidates for surgical revascularization, exercise-based immunomodulation may offer a low-cost, low-risk strategy to mitigate ongoing myocardial damage and slow disease progression. While direct extrapolation from rodent models to clinical practice requires caution, our findings highlight the need for translational studies evaluating whether exercise-induced macrophage polarization contributes to improved outcomes in cardiothoracic surgery patients. Future clinical trials should consider incorporating immune biomarkers, including macrophage phenotype markers, as exploratory endpoints to validate the translational relevance of our preclinical observations.

Several limitations of the present work merit explicit mention. First, the experimental design employed three groups (Sham, MI, and MI + AE) rather than a full 2 × 2 factorial design that would include a Sham + exercise group. The absence of a Sham + exercise group limits our ability to distinguish between the general physiological effects of exercise and those specifically attributable to the post-MI condition. Future studies incorporating a Sham + exercise group are warranted to further delineate the disease-specific mechanisms underlying the cardioprotective effects of AE. Second, although the rat LAD ligation model mimics human MI pathophysiology, species differences and differences in exercise protocols, fitness levels, and comorbidities may limit clinical translation. Thus, our results should be viewed as hypothesis-generating, and well-designed clinical trials are warranted to validate the translational potential of exercise-induced macrophage modulation. Third, while we demonstrated that AE promotes macrophage polarization, the underlying molecular mechanisms, such as the involvement of specific signaling pathways, e.g., PPARγ, STAT6, or nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 [43], were not investigated and warrant further exploration. Fourth, this study focused on a single exercise intensity and duration; however, the optimal training parameters for maximal cardioprotection after MI remain uncertain. Future research should systematically compare different exercise intensities, durations, and timing of initiation to determine the most effective and safe rehabilitation protocol. Fifth, the present study included only male rats and a single age group; whether the observed effects are reproducible in female animals or in different age cohorts remains to be determined. Additionally, long-term follow-up after exercise cessation was not performed, and we did not longitudinally track macrophage dynamics from the acute to the chronic phase. Future studies employing time-series analyses would be valuable to delineate the temporal trajectory of macrophage polarization and to determine the precise time window during which AE exerts its immunomodulatory effects. Sixth, although cTnI, CK-MB, and LDH are classical biomarkers of acute myocardial injury, their measurement at a chronic post-infarction time point (~ 12 weeks) limits their interpretation as indicators of acute necrosis. Although we interpreted their persistent elevation as reflecting ongoing myocardial damage and adverse remodeling in the chronic phase, consistent with previous reports in chronic HF models [44], we acknowledge that their biological significance at this late time point differs from that in the acute setting. Future studies incorporating more chronic-phase-specific biomarkers, such as N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide or fibrosis markers, would provide a more comprehensive assessment of post-infarction remodeling. Seventh, this study is subject to survivor bias inherent in MI models. Only animals surviving the perioperative period were randomized, potentially representing a more resilient subset. The 20% mortality rate is consistent with previous reports [45], and deaths occurred within 48 h post-surgery due to arrhythmias or acute HF. This selection bias and the relatively small sample size (n = 8 per group) may limit generalizability and statistical power. Future studies with larger cohorts and formal power calculations would strengthen the conclusions.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that a structured moderate-intensity treadmill AE program initiated during the recovery phase of MI and continued for 8 weeks effectively improves cardiac systolic function and attenuates post-infarction histopathological damage in a rat model of chronic post-infarction remodeling. AE protects the heart largely by curbing inflammation, boosting antioxidant defenses, and nudging macrophages away from the pro-inflammatory M1 state toward the repair-oriented M2 state. These data offer pre-clinical evidence that structured exercise could serve as a valuable adjunctive therapy during post-infarct recovery and set the stage for deeper exploration of its immune-modulating and cardioprotective pathways.
