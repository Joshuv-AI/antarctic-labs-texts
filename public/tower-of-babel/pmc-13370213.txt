Reproductive phasiRNAs: At the core of small RNA-mediated male fertility in monocots
Authors: Muhammad Waseem
Published: 2026

Main text

Plant small RNAs (sRNAs; typically 21–24 nt long) form a multilayered regulatory network that safeguards male fertility in monocots by coordinating developmental programs, transposon control, and environmental responses in anthers. In grasses such as maize and rice, a distinct class of reproductive-phased secondary small interfering RNAs (phasiRNAs) accumulates specifically in anthers, where they resemble animal PIWI-interacting RNAs (piRNAs) in their high abundance, germline enrichment, and stage-specific accumulation (Liu et al., 2020; Tamotsu et al., 2023). These reproductive phasiRNAs are produced from PHAS loci transcribed by RNA polymerase II and processed through a conserved pathway in which 22-nt microRNA (miRNA) triggers, mainly miR2118 and miR2275, cleave PHAS precursors, followed by RNA-DEPENDENT RNA POLYMERASE 6-dependent double-stranded RNA synthesis and DICER-LIKE 4/5 (DCL4/DCL5) cleavage (Tamotsu et al., 2023). The resulting 21- and 24-nt phasiRNAs are sorted into ARGONAUTE (AGO) proteins according to size and 5′ nucleotide identity, generating diverse effector complexes with distinct spatial and temporal activities in the anther (Figure 1). Comparative studies suggest that PHAS loci have expanded and diversified across grass lineages, consistent with adaptive roles in fertility regulation (Gil and Meyers, 2026). Yet, despite the sheer number of PHAS loci, only a subset of reproductive phasiRNAs and AGO modules has been functionally linked to specific targets and fertility phenotypes, leaving much of this regulatory layer mechanistically opaque. Overall, reproductive phasiRNAs represent only one component of the broader sRNA landscape governing male fertility in monocots, alongside canonical miRNAs, heterochromatic siRNAs, and potentially other atypical sRNA classes.

Biogenesis and functional roles of small RNAs in plant reproductive development and male fertility.

(A) Schematic representation of microRNA (miRNA) biogenesis pathways and their regulatory roles in anther and pollen development. In the miRNA biogenesis pathway, miRNA genes are transcribed by RNA polymerase II to produce primary miRNAs (pri-miRNAs), which are processed into precursor miRNAs (pre-miRNAs) and subsequently into mature miRNA duplexes by DICER-LIKE 1 (DCL1) with assistance from HYL1 and SE. The mature miRNA is incorporated into ARGONAUTE (AGO) complexes to mediate post-transcriptional gene silencing through mRNA cleavage or translational repression.

(B) Phased small interfering RNA (phasiRNA) biogenesis pathway. PhasiRNA biogenesis is initiated by cleavage of target RNAs, including mRNAs and long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs), defined as PHAS transcripts or loci, typically, but not exclusively, by 22-nt miRNAs. After cleavage, the 5′ fragment of the target RNA is rapidly degraded by a 3′→5′ exonucleolytic complex, followed by RNA-DEPENDENT RNA POLYMERASE 6 (RDR6)-mediated double-stranded RNA synthesis from the 3′ cleaved fragment. The resulting double-stranded RNAs are processed by DCL proteins to generate phasiRNAs.

(C) Functional roles of miRNAs: Specific miRNAs, including miR156, miR528, miR408, and miR159, regulate transcription factors and metabolic pathways associated with pollen development, tapetum function, starch metabolism, and fertility.

(D) PhasiRNA biogenesis mediated by distinct AGO1-associated miRNA-triggered pathways. In the upper pathway, AGO1–miR2118 directs cleavage of PHAS transcripts, followed by DCL4 processing to produce 21-nt phasiRNAs. In the lower pathway, AGO1–miR2275-mediated cleavage initiates DCL5-dependent processing, resulting in the generation of 24-nt phasiRNAs.

(E) Reproductive phasiRNAs, classified by size class and AGO association, including AGO5, AGO18, AGO6, and AGO9, play distinct roles in meiosis, tapetal development, stress responses, and genome stability. Disruption of these pathways leads to abnormal anther development and male sterility. Overall, this figure highlights the coordinated action of miRNAs and phasiRNAs in controlling gene expression networks essential for reproductive development and fertility in plants.

Conserved temporal “waves” of reproductive phasiRNAs

Grasses produce reproductive phasiRNAs in two conserved temporal “waves”, although lineage-specific innovations have expanded this framework. In rice and maize, premeiotic 21-nt phasiRNAs are triggered by miR2118-cleaved long noncoding RNA-like PHAS precursors and accumulate in archesporial cells and somatic layers soon after germline cell specification, whereas meiotic 24-nt phasiRNAs are generated later from miR2275-triggered transcripts and peak during meiosis (Gil and Meyers, 2026). Cytological and cellular localization studies show that these classes accumulate in archesporial cells, the tapetum, and meiocytes with sharp developmental transitions, suggesting roles in buffering germ cell specification, meiosis, and early pollen maturation (Jouni et al., 2025). In wheat and barley, an additional premeiotic 24-nt reproductive phasiRNA class has been described, with distinct trigger requirements and probable AGO partners, expanding the original canonical two-wave model. Recently, studies in the maize genus further demonstrated that premeiotic 24-nt phasiRNAs are widespread, arise through pathways that are partly independent of miRNA-guided cleavage, and are likely associated with AGO effectors distinct from those used by meiotic 24-nt phasiRNAs (Zhan et al., 2024; Gil and Meyers, 2026). Together, this evidence supports a conserved reproductive framework comprising premeiotic 21-nt and meiotic 24-nt waves, together with an additional 24-nt lineage that provides both robustness and evolvability in monocot reproductive programs.

Genetic evidence linking phasiRNA pathways to male fertility

Genetic analyses in rice, maize, and wheat show that perturbations of reproductive phasiRNA biogenesis or AGO loading frequently cause male sterility, revealing essential and sometimes environment-sensitive roles in monocot fertility. In rice, loss of the AGO5c family member MEIOSIS ARRESTED AT LEPTOTENE1, which binds 21-nt phasiRNAs with a 5′ cytosine, disrupts pollen mother cell progression and causes complete male sterility. Under low-temperature conditions, OsAGO1d supports the trafficking of miR2118/miR2275 and associated phasiRNAs, thereby maintaining phasiRNA production and male fertility (Jouni et al., 2025). In maize, DCL5 is required for the accumulation of meiotic 24-nt phasiRNAs, tapetal differentiation, and fertility at yield-optimal growth temperatures; dcl5 mutants exhibit temperature-sensitive male sterility. Analogously, DCL5 mutations in durum and bread wheat abolish both premeiotic and meiotic 24-nt phasiRNAs and induce male sterility with minimal vegetative defects (Gil and Meyers, 2026). Together, these studies identify reproductive phasiRNA pathways as critical regulators of male fertility and attractive targets for genic male-sterile systems in hybrid seed production.

Specialized AGO modules and broader sRNA pathways

Specialized AGO proteins add further regulatory specificity to phasiRNA pathways. In rice, AGO18 promotes male gametophyte development and antiviral defense, and its loss alters reproductive phasiRNA and heterochromatic small interfering RNA (hc-siRNA) populations, suggesting a role in balancing genome defense with fertility. A distinct 5′-A 21-nt phasiRNA, acting in an OsAGO2-dependent manner to cleave OsARF7, modulates auxin-responsive fertility pathways, demonstrating that reproductive phasiRNAs can channel hormone signaling through non-canonical AGO partners (Shao et al., 2026). Multi-omics analyses have shown that AGO1d-dependent 21-nt phasiRNAs target fertility- and cold-tolerance-associated genes, including EDT1, TDR, OsPCF5, and OsTCP21, thereby linking phasiRNA activity to temperature-sensitive male sterility (Komiya et al., 2014). Comparative studies in wheat and barley suggest that 21-nt phasiRNA–AGO5 modules are particularly critical for premeiotic germ cell specification, whereas 24-nt phasiRNA–DCL5–AGO18/AGO6/AGO9 modules stabilize meiotic progression and pollen maturation, with lineage-specific differences in their relative contributions (Figure 1) (Gil and Meyers, 2026).

Beyond phasiRNAs, additional sRNA pathways also contribute to male fertility in monocots. Canonical 24-nt siRNAs produced by RNA polymerase IV and DCL3 drive RNA-directed DNA methylation in the tapetum and companion cells, silencing transposons and shaping chromatin in germ cells. Mobile siRNAs and miRNAs move between the tapetum and meiocytes to influence tapetal programmed cell death, nutrient allocation, and stress responses essential for pollen viability. Comparative profiling shows that reproductive phasiRNAs are largely grass-lineage-specific, whereas miRNA- and hc-siRNA-mediated pathways support conserved themes of genome defense and cell-to-cell communication in anthers. Representative examples from monocots underscore that male fertility regulation is not limited to phasiRNA pathways. For instance, the miR159–GAMYB module in rice (Millar et al., 2019), the miR159a–DUO1 module in citrus (Xu et al., 2024), and the miR159a–MYB33 module in melon (Figure 1) (Zhang et al., 2026) regulate anther and pollen development. Likewise, the rice miR528–OsUCL23 and miR408–OsUCL8 modules regulate pollen development, pollen intine formation, and pollen tube growth (Figure 1) (Zhang et al., 2018, 2020). Collectively, these studies support the view that fertility is controlled by a layered sRNA system rather than by reproductive phasiRNAs alone.

Together, these cases broaden the conceptual framework of male fertility regulation in monocots, integrating reproductive phasiRNAs with broadly expressed miRNAs and siRNAs that intersect with development, redox balance, hormone signaling, chromatin regulation, and stress adaptation. Reproductive phasiRNAs are thus best viewed as a grass-specific elaboration built on ancient miRNA/siRNA scaffolds, analogous to the diversification of animal piRNAs in fine-tuning spermatogenesis.

Open questions and future research directions

Decoding targets and mechanisms across canonical and specialized sRNA pathways

Despite recent advances, many aspects of sRNA-mediated fertility control in monocots remain unresolved. Only a small fraction of reproductive PHAS loci have experimentally validated targets. Integrating degradome sequencing, argonaute-crosslinking and immunoprecipitation, and single-cell RNA/sRNA profiling across grass lineages should help distinguish conserved regulatory circuits from lineage-specific innovations and clarify whether phasiRNAs act through mRNA slicing, chromatin regulation, or both.

Non-canonical effector modules and atypical sRNAs

Atypical effector modules, such as OsAGO2- and OsAGO1d-associated phasiRNAs and long noncoding RNA-derived reproductive sRNAs, should be functionally tested in additional grasses. Likewise, atypical sRNA species, including tRNA-derived sRNAs, may contribute to translational control, stress adaptation, or chromatin-associated regulation during reproduction and therefore warrant explicit consideration.

Moving beyond the “reproductive-enriched-only” view

An important conceptual question is whether fertility-relevant sRNAs must be strictly enriched in reproductive tissues. Broadly expressed regulators can exert decisive effects on male fertility at critical developmental windows through developmental, hormonal, metabolic, chromatin-related, or stress-response pathways. Defining how these broader sRNA pathways intersect with anther-specific reproductive programs will be essential for developing a predictive framework of monocot fertility control.

Integrating stress, metabolism, and fertility through sRNA networks

The interface among reproductive phasiRNAs, canonical miRNAs, and other reproductive siRNAs that regulate tapetal programmed cell death, carbohydrate supply, and hormone balance remains poorly defined, particularly under heat, cold, and drought stress, which can trigger tapetal defects and male sterility. Future work should reconstruct integrated regulatory networks that connect reproductive development with environmental resilience.

Engineering sRNA circuits for climate-smart hybrid breeding

Translating mechanistic insights into breeding applications requires flexible tools, such as temperature- or photoperiod-sensitive phasiRNA modules, DCL5-based conditional male-sterile alleles, and synthetic PHAS loci that can be deployed across diverse genetic backgrounds without compromising yield or stress tolerance. Comparative functional studies across monocots will be crucial for harnessing sRNA-guided regulation of male fertility as a scalable, climate-smart strategy for hybrid seed production.
