Contraction of Rényi Divergences for Discrete Channels: Properties and Applications
Adrien Vandenbroucque, Amedeo Roberto Esposito, Michael Gastpar

Abstract
This work explores properties of Strong Data-Processing constants for Rényi Divergences. Parallels are made with the well-studied φ \varphi -Divergences, and it is shown that the order α \alpha of Rényi Divergences dictates whether certain properties of the contraction of φ \varphi -Divergences are mirrored or not. In particular, we demonstrate that when α > 1 \alpha>1 , the contraction properties can deviate quite strikingly from those of φ \varphi -Divergences. We also uncover specific characteristics of contraction for the ∞ \infty -Rényi Divergence and relate it to ε \varepsilon -Local Differential Privacy . The results are then applied to bound the speed of convergence of Markov chains, where we argue that the contraction of Rényi Divergences offers a new perspective on the contraction of L α L^{\alpha} -norms commonly studied in the literature.



I
Introduction

The data-processing inequality (DPI) is a fundamental result in information theory, formalising the fact that post-processing cannot increase “information”. In the last decade, efforts have been made to better understand a tightening of DPI known as the Strong Data-Processing Inequality (SDPI), whose purpose is to quantify the information contraction guaranteed by the DPI
[
26
, Chapter 33]
. A growing body of work addresses both the computation of SDPI constants, their properties, and the inequalities governing their relationships. Part of the attractiveness comes from the applicability of such inequalities; they find practical use in information-theoretic settings such as local differential privacy
[
3
,
2
]
, information percolation, broadcasting on trees, and information reconstruction
[
24
,
25
,
17
]
, but also in the study of Markov processes or concentration inequalities, and their recent applications in machine learning
[
11
,
19
,
5
]
.

SDPIs for
\varphi
-Divergences have been thoroughly studied, and it is now established that a variety of contraction properties are common to all of them
[
27
]
. In this work, we restrict our attention to the contraction of Rényi Divergences of order
\alpha\geq 0
, a setting for which results are currently sparse
[
18
,
1
,
16
]
. We show that depending on the order of the Rényi Divergences, certain contraction properties characteristic of
\varphi
-Divergences
may or may not be recovered. Differences can manifest in drastic ways; for instance, channels may be viewed as contractive under
\varphi
-Divergences
but non-contractive under Rényi Divergences. After briefly connecting the contraction of the
\infty
-Rényi Divergence to Local Differential Privacy (LDP), we apply Rényi’s SDPIs to bound how fast Markov chains converge to their stationary distribution. In that context, a fresh perspective on bounds provided by Rényi Divergences is highlighted, namely that they can be seen as non-linear contractions of
L^{\alpha}
-norms.

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows.
Section

II
presents the necessary background, including notation and definitions of divergences and SDPIs. We then proceed by establishing various bounds and identities pertaining to SDPIs for Rényi Divergences in
Section

III
. Finally, a connection to
\varepsilon
-LDP is made in
Section

, followed by applying our result in the study of convergence of Markov chains.

II
Background and Definitions

In the following, we consider spaces with finite alphabets. For such a space
\mathsf{X}
, it is assumed that a
\sigma
-algebra
\Sigma_{\mathsf{X}}
is associated to it, rendering it a measure space. Since the considered spaces are finite, we can assume
\Sigma_{\mathsf{X}}
to be the power set of
\mathsf{X}
. Indeed, in case it is not, some symbols in
\mathsf{X}
can be merged to yield a new measure space with this property. We denote by
\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{X})
the set of probability measures on
\mathsf{X}
and by
\mathcal{F}(\mathsf{X})
the set of all real-valued functions on
\mathsf{X}
. The expectation of a function
f\in\mathcal{F}(\mathsf{X})
w.r.t. a probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{X})
is
{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[f\right]\triangleq\sum_{x\in\mathsf{X}}f(x)\mu(x)}
and its
L^{\alpha}
-norm is
{\|f\|_{L^{\alpha}(\mu)}\triangleq\big(\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[|f|^{\alpha}\right]\big)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}
for
\alpha\geq 1
. Given two probability measures
\nu,\mu
, we write
\nu\ll\mu
to denote that
\nu
is absolutely continuous w.r.t.
\mu
, in which case there exists a Radon-Nikodym derivative
\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\mu}
. The natural logarithm function is denoted by
\log
. The Binary Symmetric Channel with crossover probability
\varepsilon
is written as
\mathrm{BSC}(\varepsilon)
.

II-A

Markov Kernels

Hereafter, a channel will be viewed as a Markov kernel, or stochastic transformation.

1

A Markov kernel
K:\mathsf{Y}\times\mathsf{X}\to[0,1]
specifies transition probabilities from elements of a set
\mathsf{X}
to elements of a set
\mathsf{Y}
, and in particular

1.

\forall x\in\mathsf{X}
, the mapping
y\mapsto K(y|x)
is a probability distribution,

2.

\forall y\in\mathsf{Y}
, the mapping
x\mapsto K(y|x)
is a
\Sigma_{\mathsf{X}}
-measurable real-valued function.

A Markov kernel acts on probability distributions
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{X})
as

\mu K(y)=\sum_{x\in\mathsf{X}}K(y|x)\mu(x),\quad\text{for }y\in\mathsf{Y},

so that
\mu K\in\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{Y})
.
The set of all such kernels is denoted by
\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{Y}|\mathsf{X})
.

II-B

Divergences and (Strong) Data-Processing Inequalities

Let us define two families of divergences which will be central to this work, starting with Rényi Divergences
[
30
]
.

2

For two probability measures
\nu,\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{X})
such that
\nu\ll\mu
, the Rényi divergence of order
\alpha\geq 0
from
\nu
to
\mu
is defined as

D_{\alpha}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\mu}\right)^{\alpha}\right],

where the orders
0,1
and
\infty
are defined by continuous extensions.

The KL Divergence, denoted as
D_{\rm KL}(\nu\|\mu)
, corresponds to the limit
\alpha\to 1
.

The second family of divergences we consider are the
\varphi
-Divergences
[
7
]
.

3

Let
\varphi:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
be a convex function with
\varphi(1)=0
. For two probability measures
\nu,\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{X})
such that
\nu\ll\mu
, the
\varphi
-Divergence from
\nu
to
\mu
is defined as

D_{\varphi}(\nu\|\mu)=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\varphi\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\mu}\right)\right].

A variety of well-known divergences belong to the family of
\varphi
-Divergences, notably:

•

The KL Divergence, for which
\varphi(t)=t\log t
,

•

The Total Variation Distance, denoted as
\|\nu-\mu\|_{\rm TV}
, for which
\varphi(t)=|t-1|
,

•

The Hellinger Divergence of order
\alpha\geq 0
, denoted as
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}(\nu\|\mu)
, for which
\varphi(t)=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}(t^{\alpha}-1)
. The case
\alpha=2
corresponds to the
\chi^{2}
-Divergence, denoted as
\chi^{2}(\nu\|\mu)
.

All statistical distances defined above satisfy the Data-Processing Inequality (DPI), which for a divergence
D(\cdot\|\cdot)
and a Markov kernel
K
reads

D(\nu K\|\mu K)\leq D(\nu\|\mu)

for all
\nu,\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{X})
with
\nu\ll\mu
. Typically, the inequality is strict and one can give a more quantitative statement. For a fixed pair
(\mu,K)
, the DPI can be tightened by considering the associated distribution-dependent SDPI constant

\eta_{D}(\mu,K)=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{X}):\\
0<D(\nu\|\mu)<\infty\end{subarray}}\frac{D(\nu K\|\mu K)}{D(\nu\|\mu)},

(1)

yielding the stronger inequality
{D(\nu K\|\mu K)\leq\eta_{D}(\mu,K)D(\nu\|\mu)}
for all
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{X})
. One can similarly define a quantity which only depends on
K
, leading to the distribution-independent SDPI constant

\eta_{D}(K)=\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{X})}\eta_{D}(\mu,K)=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu,\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{X})\\
0<D(\nu\|\mu)<\infty\end{subarray}}\frac{D(\nu K\|\mu K)}{D(\nu\|\mu)}.

(2)

In the rest of the article, we use the following notation to refer to SDPI constants under various common divergences:
\eta_{\rm TV}
for Total Variation Distance,
\eta_{\chi^{2}}
for
\chi^{2}
-Divergence,
\eta_{\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}}
for Hellinger Divergence of order
\alpha
and
\eta_{\alpha}
for Rényi Divergence of order
\alpha
.

III-A

Bounds on the Rényi-SDPI Constants

In the study of contraction coefficients for
\varphi
-Divergences, certain bounds appear quite extensively in the literature, such as the lower bounds
\eta_{\varphi}(\mu,K)\geq\eta_{\chi^{2}}(\mu,K)
and
\eta_{\varphi}(K)\geq\eta_{\chi^{2}}(K)
, which hold for all thrice differentiable
\varphi
with
\varphi^{\prime\prime}(1)>0

[
27
, Theorem III.3]
. While the same results have been shown to also hold when considering the contraction of Rényi Divergence
[
18
]
, it is not known whether better lower bounds exist. Regarding upper bounds, the classical result
\eta_{\varphi}(K)\leq\eta_{\rm TV}(K)

[
27
, Theorem III.1]
, which holds for all
\varphi
-Divergence, is known not to extend to the Rényi Divergence
[
13
, Example 1]
. As we will see, certain contraction properties of
\varphi
-Divergences are mirrored whenever
\alpha\in[0,1]
, whereas the case
\alpha>1
exhibits distinct behaviours.

Our first step is to refine known bounds on the Rényi-SDPI constant by relating it to the SDPI constant associated with Hellinger Divergences. This connection is made possible through the use of
Lemma

1
(see
Section

-D
), a general result to compare the SDPI constants of divergences which are in one-to-one relationship, alongside the identity
{D_{\alpha}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\big(1+(\alpha-1)\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}(\nu\|\mu)\big)}
. The following result will be crucial in subsequent sections for establishing similarities and differences between the contraction of Rényi Divergences and
\varphi
-Divergences.

1

For any pair
(\mu,K)\in\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{X})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{Y}|\mathsf{X})
:

1.

If
\alpha>1
, then

\eta_{\alpha}(\mu,K)\geq\eta_{\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}}(\mu,K)\quad\text{and}\quad\eta_{\alpha}(K)\geq\eta_{\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}}(K)

2.

If
\alpha\in[0,1)
, then

\eta_{\alpha}(\mu,K)\leq\eta_{\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}}(\mu,K)\quad\text{and}\quad\eta_{\alpha}(K)\leq\eta_{\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}}(K)

1

Both the Rényi and Hellinger Divergence correspond to the KL Divergence when
\alpha\to 1
, so that their SDPI constants become equal in the limit.

While Part a) of
Proposition

1
specifies new lower bounds on the Rényi-SDPI constant, a natural question is whether they improve over those involving
\eta_{\chi^{2}}
. Such an improvement can be expected since in general
\eta_{\chi^{2}}(\mu,K)\leq\eta_{\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}}(\mu,K)
, and it can be observed in

Our next result establishes when the Dobrushin coefficient
\eta_{\rm TV}(K)

\eta_{\alpha}(K)

\alpha

2

Consider

1.

If
\alpha\in[0,1]

2.

If
\alpha>1

K\in\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{Y}|\mathsf{X})

Next, we identify the conditions under which Rényi-SDPI constants fail to guarantee a non-trivial contraction. For
\varphi
-Divergence, the condition
\eta_{\varphi}(K)=1
is known to be equivalent to the existence of orthogonal rows in
K
(when seen as a stochastic matrix)

\eta_{\alpha}(K)=1

[
16

2

Consider a Markov kernel

1.

For

{\mathrm{supp}\big(K(\cdot|x)\big)\cap\mathrm{supp}\big(K(\cdot|x^{\prime})\big)=\emptyset}

2.

For

{x,x^{\prime}\in\mathsf{X}}

{\mathrm{supp}\big(K(\cdot|x)\big)\neq\mathrm{supp}\big(K(\cdot|x^{\prime})\big)}
.

III-C

Contraction Properties for
\alpha\to\infty

The previous section demonstrates that there is a distinction depending on the range of
\alpha
; for
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the SDPI constant is akin to that of
\varphi
-Divergences, whereas for
\alpha>1
there are discrepancies. In this section, we unravel some of the properties of the SDPI constants in the latter case, specifically for the order
\alpha\to\infty
.

An extension of
[
18
, Theorem 4]
—which states that the distribution achieving the supremum in the definition of
\eta_{2}(\mu,K)
has support strictly smaller than the one of
\mu
—can be obtained for
\alpha\to\infty
(see
Section

-G
). In reality, the following result shows that the achieving distribution
\nu
is not merely of smaller support, but is precisely the restriction of
\mu
to a subset
A\subsetneq\mathsf{X}
.

4

Consider any pair
(\mu,K)\in\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{X})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{Y}|\mathsf{X})
. Then

\eta_{\infty}(\mu,K)=\sup_{A\subsetneq\mathsf{X}}\frac{D_{\infty}\left(\mu_{|A}K\|\mu K\right)}{D_{\infty}\left(\mu_{|A}\|\mu\right)},

where
\mu_{|A}(x)\triangleq\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{A}(x)\mu(x)}{\mu(A)}
denotes the restriction of
\mu
to the event
A
.

Even with the simpler expression put forth in
Theorem

4
, the computation of
\eta_{\infty}(\mu,K)
can still be prohibitive. This can be addressed by bounding this quantity with the SDPI constant of a different divergence that is more amenable to analytical evaluation. The following result establishes bounds on
\eta_{\infty}(\mu,K)
via the contraction of Total Variation Distance.

3

Consider a pair
(\mu,K)\in\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{X})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{Y}|\mathsf{X})
. Then we have

\eta_{\infty}(\mu,K)\leq\frac{\eta_{\rm TV}(\mu,K)}{\min_{y\in\mathrm{supp}(\mu K)}\mu K(y)}.

(3)

Moreover, if
\mu
has full-support,

\eta_{\infty}(\mu,K)\geq\eta_{\rm TV}(\mu,K)\cdot\min_{x\in\mathsf{X}}\mu(x).

(4)

The presence of minima in
Proposition

3
is reminiscent of certain Pinsker-type inequalities involving Rényi Divergence
[
29
]
. This is, however, not a coincidence: such inequalities form an effective way to compare the contraction of different
\varphi
-Divergences
[
21
,
14
,
16
]
.

IV
Connections and Applications

IV-A

Relation to
\varepsilon
-Local Differential Privacy

In this section, we show how the concept of
\varepsilon
-Local Differential Privacy (LDP) is equivalent to the contraction of Rényi Divergence of order
\infty
. A similar connection has been drawn between
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-LDP and the contraction of the Hockey-Stick Divergence
[
2
]
. Let us recall that a privacy mechanism is a (random) mapping from elements of a set
\mathsf{X}
to elements of a set
\mathsf{Y}
, which is usually represented by some Markov kernel
K\in\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{Y}|\mathsf{X})
. In the following, we shall only focus on
\varepsilon
-LDP, described below.

4

Let
\varepsilon\geq 0
. A privacy mechanism
K\in\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{Y}|\mathsf{X})
is
\varepsilon
-LDP if

\sup_{x,x^{\prime}\in\mathsf{X}}\sup_{A\subseteq\mathsf{X}}\frac{K(A|x^{\prime})}{K(A|x)}\leq e^{\varepsilon}.

The relationship with the SDPI constant of
\infty
-Rényi Divergence can then be formulated via
Theorem

5
.

2

A privacy mechanism
K\in\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{Y}|\mathsf{X})
is
\varepsilon
-LDP if and only if
\eta_{\infty}(K)\leq 1-e^{-\varepsilon}
.

Note that
Corollary

1
also allows us to view ultra-mixing as being equivalent to
\varepsilon
-LDP, a link that was previously observed in
[
3
, Table 1]
.

IV-B

Mixing of Markov Chains

SDPIs provide a powerful framework to analyse the convergence of Markov chains, a topic of great practical importance notably in the context of Markov chain Monte-Carlo algorithms
[
20
,
22
,
15
]
. Specifically, a Markov kernel
K\in\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{X}|\mathsf{X})
induces a Markov chain, and one can study the
n
-step evolution of any distribution
\nu
by looking at
\nu K^{n}
. When a Markov chain is irreducible and aperiodic, it is well-known that it admits a unique stationary distribution
\pi
(a distribution such that
\pi=\pi K
), and
\nu K^{n}
converges to
\pi
for any
\nu
as a function of
n
.

Traditionally, convergence is quantified in terms of
L^{\alpha}
-norms
for
\alpha\geq 1
via bounds
[
28
]
such as

\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu K^{n}}{\mathrm{d}\pi}-1\right\|_{L^{\alpha}(\pi)}\leq\gamma_{\alpha}^{n}\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\pi}-1\right\|_{L^{\alpha}(\pi)}\text{ for }\gamma_{\alpha}\in[0,1],

(5)

indicating that
\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu K^{n}}{\mathrm{d}\pi}-1\right\|_{L^{\alpha}(\pi)}
converges to 0 at geometric rate given by
\gamma_{\alpha}
. In contrast, using the Rényi-SDPI gives
{D_{\alpha}\left(\nu K^{n}\|\pi\right)\leq\eta_{\alpha}(\pi,K)^{n}D_{\alpha}\left(\nu\|\pi\right)}
, which can be equivalently formulated as follows.

4

Consider a Markov kernel
K\in\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{X}|\mathsf{X})
whose induced Markov chain is aperiodic and irreducible, with stationary distribution
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{X})
. Then for any
\alpha>1
and
n\geq 1
, one has

\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu K^{n}}{\mathrm{d}\pi}\right\|_{L^{\alpha}(\pi)}\leq\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\pi}\right\|_{L^{\alpha}(\pi)}^{\eta_{\alpha}(\pi,K)^{n}}

(6)

for all
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{X})
.

This type of contraction has already appeared in the literature, and is referred to as “non-linear”
[
24
,
9
,
17
]
. In this form, it still quantifies the convergence of
\nu K^{n}
to

Some Facts about Markov Kernels

This section collects some basic facts about Markov Kernels that are used in the proofs.

While
Section

II-A
introduces Markov kernels as acting on probability measures, one can also view them as acting on functions
f\in\mathcal{F}(\mathsf{Y})
as

Kf(x)=\sum_{y\in\mathsf{Y}}f(y)K(y|x),\quad\text{for }x\in\mathsf{X}.

For any pair
(\mu,K)\in\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{X})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{Y}|\mathsf{X})
, there exists a unique (dual) kernel
K^{\star}_{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{X}|\mathsf{Y})
such that

\left\langle Kf,g\right\rangle_{\mu}=\left\langle f,K^{\star}_{\mu}g\right\rangle_{\mu K},\quad\forall f\in\mathcal{F}(\mathsf{Y}),g\in\mathcal{F}(\mathsf{X})

where we have used the inner product notation
{\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{\mu}\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[f\cdot g\right]=\sum_{x\in\mathsf{X}}f(x)g(x)\mu(x)}
. In discrete settings like ours, the transition probabilities of this dual kernel can be explicitly computed as

K^{\star}_{\mu}(x|y)=\frac{\mu(x)K(y|x)}{\mu K(y)}.

We also note that for a pair
(\mu,K)
and any other probability measure
\nu\ll\mu
, we have

K^{\star}_{\mu}f=\frac{\mathrm{d}(\nu K)}{\mathrm{d}(\mu K)}\quad\mu K-\text{a.e.},

(9)

where
f=\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\mu}

[
12
, Lemma 1]
.

Relating SDPI Constants of Divergences in One-to-One Correspondence

1

Consider any divergence
{D(\cdot\|\cdot):\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{X})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{X})\to\mathbb{R}_{+}}
that satisfies the DPI. Let
{g:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}}
be a function such that
g(0)=0
, and let
D^{(g)}(\nu\|\mu)=g\big(D(\nu\|\mu)\big)
. Then, for any admissible pair
(\mu,K)
:

1.

If
g
is convex, then

\eta_{D^{(g)}}(\mu,K)\leq\eta_{D}(\mu,K)\quad\text{and}\quad\eta_{D^{(g)}}(K)\leq\eta_{D}(K),

2.

If
g
is concave, then

\eta_{D^{(g)}}(\mu,K)\geq\eta_{D}(\mu,K)\quad\text{and}\quad\eta_{D^{(g)}}(K)\geq\eta_{D}(K).

Proof:

Let us consider the case where
g
is convex first. Since
g
is non-negative and
g(0)=0
,
g
must be non-decreasing so that
D^{(g)}
also satisfies the DPI. Moreover in this case, the function
x\mapsto\frac{g(x)}{x}
is non-decreasing for
x>0
. Indeed, by convexity of
g
and the fact that
g(0)=0
, we have
g\big(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)\cdot 0\big)\leq\lambda g(x)
for
\lambda\in[0,1]
. Letting
y=\lambda x\in[0,x]
, the inequality reads
\frac{g(y)}{y}\leq\frac{g(x)}{x}
, establishing non-decreasibility. Choosing
y=D(\nu K\|\mu K)\leq D(\nu\|\mu)=x
in the last inequality and re-arranging gives

\frac{D^{(g)}(\nu K\|\mu K)}{D^{(g)}(\nu\|\mu)}\leq\frac{D(\nu K\|\mu K)}{D(\nu\|\mu)}.

Taking supremum over
\nu
on each side gives
\eta_{D^{(g)}}(\mu,K)\leq\eta_{D}(\mu,K)
and further taking supremum over
\mu
gives the distribution-independent result. The same argument applies when
g
is concave, but with reversed inequalities.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

1

We closely follow the proof in
[
23
]
, where the case
\alpha\to 1
was proven, and adapt it where necessary.
First, we consider
\alpha>1
. For two distributions
\nu,\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{X})
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
, define

M_{\lambda}(\nu,\mu)\triangleq H_{\alpha}(\nu K\|\mu K)-\left[H_{\alpha}(\nu\|\mu)\right]^{\lambda}

where
H_{\alpha}(\nu\|\mu)\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\mu}\right)^{\alpha}\right]
. This quantity is relevant because for any
\lambda\in(0,1)
such that
\eta_{\alpha}(K)\geq\lambda
, this equivalently means there must exist a pair
(\nu,\mu)
with
M_{\lambda}(\nu,\mu)\geq 0
, and hence

\eta_{\alpha}(K)=\sup\left\{\lambda\in(0,1):\sup_{\nu,\mu}M_{\lambda}(\nu,\mu)\geq 0\right\}.

We will now show that the inner supremum
\sup_{\nu,\mu}M_{\lambda}(\nu,\mu)
can be restricted to distributions supported on at most two points.

Fix any
(\nu,\mu)
and let
r(x)=\frac{\nu(x)}{\mu(x)}
. We are searching for a distribution
\hat{\mu}
that can potentially increase the value of
M_{\lambda}(\nu,\mu)
. Once
\hat{\mu}
is fixed, we associate to it the distribution
\hat{\nu}
through
\hat{\nu}(x)=r(x)\hat{\mu}(x)
. Since both
\hat{\nu},\hat{\mu}
must be valid probability distributions, we must ensure
\sum_{x}\hat{\mu}(x)=1
and
\sum_{x}\hat{\nu}(x)=\sum_{x}r(x)\hat{\mu}(x)=1
. This defines the space of potential
\hat{\mu}
to consider as

\mathcal{S}\triangleq\bigg\{\hat{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}\big(\mathrm{supp}(\mu)\big):\sum_{x\in\mathsf{X}}\frac{\nu(x)}{\mu(x)}\cdot\hat{\mu}(x)=1\bigg\}.

With this choice, notice how since
\mu\in\mathcal{S}
, we have that
\max_{\hat{\mu}\in\mathcal{S}}M_{\lambda}(\hat{\nu},\hat{\mu})=\max_{\hat{\mu}\in\mathcal{S}}M_{\lambda}\left(\frac{\nu}{\mu}\hat{\mu},\hat{\mu}\right)\geq g(\mu)=M_{\lambda}(\nu,\mu)
.

Next, let
g:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
be defined as
g(\hat{\mu})=M_{\lambda}\left(\frac{\nu}{\mu}\hat{\mu},\hat{\mu}\right)=H_{\alpha}\left((\frac{\nu}{\mu}\hat{\mu})K\|\hat{\mu}K\right)-\left[H_{\alpha}\left(\frac{\nu}{\mu}\hat{\mu}\|\hat{\mu}\right)\right]^{\lambda}
, which is the function we now want to maximise now that we have established it is sufficient to optimise over
\mathcal{S}
. The function
g
is convex because

•

\hat{\mu}\mapsto H_{\alpha}\left((\frac{\nu}{\mu}\hat{\mu})K\|\hat{\mu}K\right)
is convex by joint convexity of
(\nu,\mu)\mapsto H_{\alpha}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)
and since
\hat{\mu}\mapsto\big((\frac{\nu}{\mu}\hat{\mu})K,\hat{\mu}K\big)
is linear,

•

\hat{\mu}\mapsto\left[H_{\alpha}\left(\frac{\nu}{\mu}\hat{\mu}\|\hat{\mu}\right)\right]^{\lambda}=\left(\sum_{x}\hat{\mu}(x)\left(\frac{\nu(x)}{\mu(x)}\right)^{\alpha}\right)^{\lambda}

is concave since it is the composition of a linear function with the concave map
x\mapsto x^{\lambda}
.

It therefore follows that
\max_{\hat{\mu}\in\mathcal{S}}g(\hat{\mu})
is obtained at an extreme point of
\mathcal{S}
. Since
\mathcal{S}
is the intersection of the simplex with a hyperplane, its extreme points are supported on at most two atoms.

For
\alpha\to\infty
, the approach is similar, except we directly use

\eta_{\infty}(K)=\sup\big\{\lambda\in(0,1):\eta_{\infty}(K)\geq\lambda\big\}=\sup\left\{\lambda\in(0,1):\sup_{\nu,\mu}\frac{D_{\infty}(\nu K\|\mu K)}{D_{\infty}(\nu\|\mu)}\geq\lambda\right\}

and thus define
\widetilde{M}_{\lambda}(\nu,\mu)=\frac{D_{\infty}(\nu K\|\mu K)}{D_{\infty}(\nu\|\mu)}
. We use the same idea as before and define
\mathcal{S}
similarly. The function to be maximised is now
\tilde{g}:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
defined as
\tilde{g}(\hat{\mu})=\widetilde{M}_{\lambda}\left(\frac{\nu}{\mu}\hat{\mu},\hat{\mu}\right)=\frac{D_{\infty}((\frac{\nu}{\mu}\hat{\mu})K\|\hat{\mu}K)}{D_{\infty}(\frac{\nu}{\mu}\hat{\mu}\|\hat{\mu})}
. It is quasiconvex because

•

\hat{\mu}\mapsto D_{\infty}(\frac{\nu}{\mu}\hat{\mu}\|\hat{\mu})
is constant since
D_{\infty}(\frac{\nu}{\mu}\hat{\mu}\|\hat{\mu})=\log\max_{x\in\mathsf{X}}\frac{\nu(x)}{\mu(x)}
.

•

\hat{\mu}\mapsto D_{\infty}((\frac{\nu}{\mu}\hat{\mu})K\|\hat{\mu}K)=\log\sup_{y\in\mathsf{Y}}\frac{\sum_{x\in\mathsf{X}}r(x)\hat{\mu}(x)K(y|x)}{\sum_{x\in\mathsf{X}}\hat{\mu}(x)K(y|x)}
is quasiconvex. Indeed, for any fixed
y
, the inner ratio is quasiconvex since it is a linear-fractional function in
\hat{\mu}

[
4
, Example 3.32]
. Moreover the supremum of quasiconvex function is quasiconvex, and further composing with the logarithm retains quasiconvexity since it is a monotonically increasing function.

The rest of the proof follows since a quasiconvex function is maximised at an extreme point of the space.

Proof of
Proposition

We first prove Part a). Note that the cases
\alpha\to 0
or
\alpha\to 1
fall back to the already-known result
\eta_{\rm KL}(K)\leq\eta_{\rm TV}(K)
. For
\alpha\in(0,1)

\eta_{\alpha}(K)\leq\eta_{\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}}(K)
and since the Hellinger Divergence is in the family of
\varphi

\eta_{\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}}(K)\leq\eta_{\rm TV}(K)
.

Part b) is proven by considering
\mathsf{X}=\{0,1\}
and the Z-channel
K=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
1-\lambda&\lambda\end{bmatrix}
with
\lambda\in(0,1)
, for which
\eta_{\rm TV}(K)=\lambda
. Considering the input and output Rényi Divergences between the Dirac mass
\delta_{1}
and
\mu_{\varepsilon}\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}1-\varepsilon&\varepsilon\end{bmatrix}
, we find their ratio to be

\displaystyle\frac{D_{\alpha}\left(\delta_{1}K\|\mu_{\varepsilon}K\right)}{D_{\alpha}\left(\delta_{1}\|\mu_{\varepsilon}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\left((1-\lambda)^{\alpha}(1-\lambda\varepsilon)^{1-\alpha}+\lambda^{\alpha}(\lambda\varepsilon)^{1-\alpha}\right)}{\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\left(\lambda+(1-\lambda)^{\alpha}(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}-\lambda)^{1-\alpha}\right)}{\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}.

Letting
u(\varepsilon)=\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
and
g(x)=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\left(\lambda+(1-\lambda)^{\alpha}(e^{x}-\lambda)^{1-\alpha}\right)
, the ratio can be expressed as
1-\frac{g\big(u(\varepsilon)\big)}{u(\varepsilon)}
, and we find that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left(1-\frac{g\big(u(\varepsilon)\big)}{u(\varepsilon)}\right)=1-\lim_{u\to\infty}\frac{\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\left(\lambda+\frac{(1-\lambda)^{\alpha}}{(e^{u}-\lambda)^{\alpha-1}}\right)}{u}=1.

But by definition of the Rényi-SDPI constant, one has

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{D_{\alpha}\left(\nu K\|\mu_{\varepsilon}K\right)}{D_{\alpha}\left(\nu\|\mu_{\varepsilon}\right)}\leq\eta_{\alpha}(K)\leq 1,

so that
\eta_{\alpha}(K)=1
and in particular
\eta_{\alpha}(K)>\eta_{\rm TV}(K)
.

Since this is a limiting argument, one can wonder whether there in fact exists a pair
\nu,\mu
such that
\frac{D_{\alpha}\left(\nu K\|\mu K\right)}{D_{\alpha}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)}>\eta_{\rm TV}(K)
. We give a positive answer by showing that in our previous example the function
\varepsilon\mapsto 1-\frac{g(u(\varepsilon))}{u(\varepsilon)}
is decreasing, so that there always exists an
\varepsilon>0
small enough such that
\frac{D_{\alpha}\left(\delta_{1}K\|\mu_{\varepsilon}K\right)}{D_{\alpha}\left(\delta_{1}\|\mu_{\varepsilon}\right)}>\eta_{\rm TV}(K)
. Notice that

g^{\prime}(x)=\frac{e^{x}(1-\lambda)^{\alpha}}{\lambda(e^{x}-\lambda)^{\alpha}+(1-\lambda)^{\alpha}(e^{x}-\lambda)}=\frac{(1-\lambda)^{\alpha}(1-\lambda e^{-x})^{-1}}{\lambda(e^{x}-\lambda)^{\alpha-1}+(1-\lambda)^{\alpha}},

and since the numerator is decreasing in
x
and the denominator is increasing in
x
, it follows that
g^{\prime}(x)
is decreasing, or in other words
g
is strictly concave. By the same argument as in
Section

-D
, the strict concavity of
g
together with the fact that
g(0)=0
implies that
u\mapsto\frac{g(u)}{u}
is decreasing. As a consequence, the map
\varepsilon\mapsto 1-\frac{g(u(\varepsilon))}{u(\varepsilon)}
is decreasing.

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\mathsf{Y}}\left(K^{\star}_{\mu}\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\mu}(y)\right)^{\alpha}\mu K(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\mathsf{Y}}\left(\sum_{x\in\mathsf{X}}K^{\star}_{\mu}(x|y)\frac{\nu(x)}{\mu(x)}\right)^{\alpha}\mu K(y)

\displaystyle<\sum_{y\in\mathsf{Y}}\sum_{x\in\mathsf{X}}K^{\star}_{\mu}(x|y)\left(\frac{\nu(x)}{\mu(x)}\right)^{\alpha}\mu K(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathsf{X}}\left(\frac{\nu(x)}{\mu(x)}\right)^{\alpha}\sum_{y\in\mathsf{Y}}K^{\star}_{\mu}(x|y)\mu K(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathsf{X}}\left(\frac{\nu(x)}{\mu(x)}\right)^{\alpha}\sum_{y\in\mathsf{Y}}K(y|x)\mu(x)

\displaystyle=e^{(\alpha-1)D_{\alpha}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)},

where the strict inequality follows from Jensen’s inequality and the fact that
\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\mu}
is not constant. Combining the three cases (
D_{\alpha}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)\to 0,\infty
and
0<D_{\alpha}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)<\infty
), we conclude
\eta_{\alpha}(K)<1
.

Proof of
Theorem

4

Let us rewrite the optimisation problem equivalently as

\eta_{\infty}(\mu,K)=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{X})\\
0<D_{\infty}(\nu\|\mu)<\infty\end{subarray}}\frac{D_{\infty}(\nu K\|\mu K)}{D_{\infty}(\nu\|\mu)}=\sup_{t>0}\frac{\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{t}}D_{\infty}(\nu K\|\mu K)}{t},

where
\mathcal{P}_{t}=\{\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{X}):D_{\infty}(\nu\|\mu)\leq t\}
. Focusing on the inner supremisation, we can write

\displaystyle\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{t}}D_{\infty}(\nu K\|\mu K)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{t}}\log\max_{y\in\mathrm{supp}(\mu K)}\frac{\nu K(y)}{\mu K(y)}

\displaystyle=\max_{y\in\mathrm{supp}(\mu K)}\log\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{t}}\frac{\nu K(y)}{\mu K(y)}

\displaystyle=\max_{y\in\mathrm{supp}(\mu K)}\log\frac{\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{t}}\sum_{x\in\mathsf{X}}\nu(x)K(y|x)}{\mu K(y)},

where the swapping of the supremum with the maximum is allowed since the spaces have a finite number of elements and the logarithm is an increasing function. For any fixed
y
, notice how the inner supremisation can be seen as the following Fractional Knapsack Problem:

Maximise

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{x\in\mathsf{X}}\nu(x)K(y|x)

Subject to

\displaystyle\quad\nu(x)\leq e^{t}\mu(x)\quad\forall x\in\mathsf{X},

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{x\in\mathsf{X}}\nu(x)=1.

Writing
n=|\mathsf{X}|
, solving such a problem can be done in a greedy manner:

•

Index the elements of
\mathsf{X}
as
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
so that
K(y|x_{1})\geq K(y|x_{2})\geq\dots\geq K(y|x_{n})
.

•

To maximise the linear objective, the optimal
\nu
should put as much mass as possible for each index according to the established order while ensuring
\nu(x_{i})\leq e^{t}\mu(x_{i})
, and at the same time make sure that the constraint
\sum_{x\in\mathsf{X}}\nu(x)=1
is respected. In other words, denoting by
k
the largest index such that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}e^{t}\mu(x_{i})\leq 1
, the optimal solution
\nu^{\star}
is such that
\nu^{\star}(x_{i})=e^{t}\mu(x_{i})
for
i=1,\dots,k
and
\nu^{\star}(x_{k+1})=1-\sum_{i=1}^{k}e^{t}\mu(x_{i})
and
\nu^{\star}(x_{j})=0
for
j\geq k+1
.

For arbitrary fixed
y
and
t
, the argument above reveals that
\nu^{\star}
is in fact close to being a conditional distribution of
\mu
, which happens when the “remainder” term
\nu^{\star}(x_{k+1})
is either 0 or equal to
e^{t}\mu(x_{k+1})
. Indeed, using the notation
\mathsf{X}_{k}\triangleq\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}
, if
\nu^{\star}(x_{k})=0
then we can write
\nu^{\star}(x)=\frac{\mu(x)\mathbbm{1}_{\mathsf{X}_{k}}(x)}{\mu(\mathsf{X}_{k})}
, while if
\nu^{\star}(x_{k+1})=e^{t}\mu(x_{k})
we can write
\nu^{\star}(x)=\frac{\mu(x)\mathbbm{1}_{\mathsf{X}_{k+1}}(x)}{\mu(\mathsf{X}_{k+1})}
, which are both conditional distributions w.r.t.
\mu
. In the remainder of the proof, we show that one of those two cases is bound to happen.

A solution where the “remainder” term
\nu^{\star}(x_{k+1})
is at index
k+1
, must be such that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}e^{t}\mu(x_{i})\leq 1
and
1-\sum_{i=1}^{k}e^{t}\mu(x_{i})=\nu^{\star}(x_{k+1})\leq e^{t}\mu(x_{k+1})
, from which one deduces
t\in\left[\log\frac{1}{\mu(\mathsf{X}_{k+1})},\log\frac{1}{\mu(\mathsf{X}_{k})}\right]
. Note that
\nu^{\star}
is a conditional distribution w.r.t.
\mu
when
t
is at one of the boundaries. Moreover when the solution has the remainder term at index
k+1
, the function being maximised takes the value

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathsf{X}}\nu^{\star}(x)K(y|x)

\displaystyle=e^{t}\sum_{x\in\mathsf{X}_{k}}\mu(x)K(y|x)+\left(1-e^{t}\sum_{x\in\mathsf{X}_{k}}\mu(x)\right)K(y|x_{k+1})

\displaystyle=e^{t}\underbrace{\left(\sum_{x\in\mathsf{X}_{k}}\mu(x)\big(K(y|x)-K(y|x_{k+1})\big)\right)}_{\triangleq a_{y}\geq 0}+\underbrace{K(y|x_{k+1})}_{\triangleq b_{y}\geq 0},

and consequently the overall problem takes the form

\sup_{t\in\left[\log\frac{1}{\mu(\mathsf{X}_{k+1})},\log\frac{1}{\mu(\mathsf{X}_{k})}\right]}\frac{\max_{y\in\mathrm{supp}(\mu K)}\log(a_{y}e^{t}+b_{y})}{t}=\max_{y\in\mathrm{supp}(\mu K)}\sup_{t\in\left[\log\frac{1}{\mu(\mathsf{X}_{k+1})},\log\frac{1}{\mu(\mathsf{X}_{k})}\right]}\frac{\log(a_{y}e^{t}+b_{y})}{t}.

For
a_{y},b_{y}\geq 0
, the function
t\mapsto\log(a_{y}e^{t}+b_{y})
is convex for
t>0
and therefore
t\mapsto\frac{\log(a_{y}e^{t}+b_{y})}{t}
is quasiconvex
[
4
, Example 3.38]
. Hence, its maximum over any interval must occur at an endpoint, and it follows that
\nu^{\star}
must be a conditional distribution w.r.t.
\mu
. Lastly, since restricting
\mu
to
A=\mathsf{X}
gives back
\mu_{|A}=\mu
, it should not be considered in the optimisation defining
\eta_{\infty}(\mu,K)
. Consequently, we obtain the final expression

\eta_{\infty}(\mu,K)=\sup_{A\subsetneq X}\frac{D_{\infty}\left(\mu_{|A}K\|\mu K\right)}{D_{\infty}\left(\mu_{|A}\|\mu\right)}.

\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\mu}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mu)}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\in\Sigma_{\mathsf{X}}:\\

{\|\nu-\mu\|_{\rm TV}=\sup_{A\in\Sigma_{\mathsf{X}}:\mu(A)>0}\{\nu(A)-\mu(A)\}}
and so in this case

\displaystyle D_{\infty}(\nu\|\mu)

\log(x)\leq x-1

By

, it suffices to optimise over pairs of the form

\displaystyle\frac{D_{\infty}(\delta_{x}K\|\mu_{\varepsilon}K)}{D_{\infty}(\delta_{x}\|\mu_{\varepsilon})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\log\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}K(\cdot|x)}{\mathrm{d}\mu_{\varepsilon}K}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mu_{\varepsilon}K)}}{\log\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\log\max_{y\in\mathrm{supp}(\mu_{\varepsilon}K)}\frac{K(y|x)}{(1-\varepsilon)K(y|x)+\varepsilon K(y|x^{\prime})}}{\log\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\log\max_{y\in\mathrm{supp}(K(\cdot|x))}\frac{K(y|x)}{(1-\varepsilon)K(y|x)+\varepsilon K(y|x^{\prime})}}{\log\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\log\left(1-\varepsilon\left(1-\min_{y\in\mathrm{supp}(K(\cdot|x))}\frac{K(y|x^{\prime})}{K(y|x)}\right)\right)}{\log(1-\varepsilon)}.

Now that the ratio has been determined, it remains to find its maximum value over
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. To do so, we show that the map
\varepsilon\mapsto\frac{\log(1-c\varepsilon)}{\log(1-\varepsilon)}

. Writing

, the map

. The derivative

is concave, and since
