Identification and experimental validation of autophagy-related genes in focal segmental glomerulosclerosis by integrating bioinformatics and machine learning
Authors: Tianwen Yao, Qingliang Wang, Shisheng Han, Meng Jia, Yanqiu Xu, Zheling Su, Yi Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFocal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) is a common chronic glomerular disease characterized by podocyte injury. The aim of the present study was to investigate autophagy-related characteristics in FSGS. GSE200828, GSE99340, GSE47183, GSE108109, and GSE104948 were used as training sets, and GSE129973 was used as the validation set. 222 autophagy-related genes (ARGs) were incorporated. Autophagy-related differentially expressed genes (ARDEGs) were obtained and then analyzed using violin plots, PPI network analysis, functional enrichment analysis, gene set enrichment analysis, immune cell infiltration and cMAP. Two machine learning methods were applied to select candidate ARGs. Nomogram and receiving operating curve were conducted to assess diagnostic value and screen out core genes. An in vivo rat model of FSGS was established to verify expression of core genes via WB and qRT-PCR. The results indicated that nine ARDEGs were identified. PPI network contained nine nodes and 30 edges, and TP53 had the highest degree value. ARDEGs were significantly enriched in physiological processes and pathways related to autophagy and immunity. Patients with FSGS had higher levels of resting natural killer cells, monocytes and activated dendritic cells, and lower levels of plasma cells, follicular helper T cells, resting dendritic cells and resting mast cells. Through cMAP analysis, 10 small molecule compounds were identified which might work as potential therapeutic drugs in FSGS. Next, three candidate ARGs were obtained which were further evaluated by nomogram and diagnostic value. Among them, TP53 and RELA had high diagnostic values. In vivo, TP53 and RELA were at higher levels in FSGS than in the control group. In conclusion, TP53 and RELA are promising autophagy-related diagnostic and therapeutic markers in FSGS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-28556-8.Keywords: Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, Autophagy, Bioinformatics, Machine learning, BiomarkersSubject terms: Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, Chronic kidney disease, Glomerular diseases

Introduction

Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) is a common chronic glomerular disease, which is characterized by podocyte injury1,2. It is also considered the most prevalent acquired kidney disease leading to end-stage renal disease, which can occur in a variety of populations3,4. Despite the increasing number of studies on FSGS, the disease pathogenesis remains unknown5,6. More importantly, as a frequent cause of nephrotic syndrome, FSGS accounts for 40% of cases in adults and 20% of cases in children, respectively7. Although FSGS requires positive immunosuppressive therapy8, patients with FSGS often show resistance to those drugs. Therefore, patients with FSGS have a higher risk of progression to dialysis compared with non-FSGS or other glomerular diseases9. Besides, as a silent killer, symptoms rarely occur at early-stage FSGS. Patients often avoid seeking aggressive treatments for FSGS until severe manifestations occur. As it is a complex and difficult disease to cure, FSGS is considered a disease with poor prognosis10. It is therefore imperative to study further the pathogenesis and treatment of FSGS.

Autophagy is a complex and protective cellular process11. As an important means of cell degradation, autophagy is essential in maintaining kidney metabolic homeostasis12,13. An increasing number of studies suggest that autophagy can prevent kidney from oxidative stress during acute ischemic kidney injury, chronic kidney disease (CKD) and aging14–16. However, uncontrolled autophagy can lead to podocyte injury and accelerate the progression of FSGS, indicating that targeting autophagy is a potential approach in treating FSGS17. Autophagy is closely related to FSGS. It has been demonstrated that interleukin (IL)−15, a classical activator of signal transducer and activator of transcription 5 (STAT5) in immune cells, can increase STAT5 phosphorylation in human podocytes, and its administration alleviates FSGS by maintaining autophagy in podocytes18. Research has shown that sirtuins, a class of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide-dependent deacetylases, are promising therapeutic targets for FSGS by enhancing autophagy19. It has been shown that methyltransferase-like protein 14 (METTL14) is increased in renal biopsy samples from patients with FSGS. However, METTL14 knockout in podocytes improves glomerular function and alleviates podocyte injury, characterized by activation of autophagy and inhibition of apoptosis and inflammation20. The aforementioned studies reveal that autophagy participates in the progression and prognosis of FSGS.

Studies have shown that FSGS is an immune-mediated podocytopathy21,22. Autophagy participates in the progress of managing the immune microenvironment of kidneys, and it is regulated by a wide range of immune cells23. However, there is still a lack of strong evidence for the association between autophagy and FSGS prognosis. The development of comprehensive bioinformatics analysis provides more opportunities to identify potential diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers in diverse diseases24. In recent years, machine learning algorithms can also be applied to study potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets for various diseases25. Therefore, a systematic analysis of kidney immunity by integrating bioinformatics and machine learning can provide a whole new understanding of autophagy-related characteristics of FSGS.

In the current study, autophagy-related differentially expressed genes (ARDEGs) were obtained using training sets first. Then, violin plots, protein-protein interaction (PPI) network, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), immune cell infiltration and connectivity map (cMAP) analyses were carried out. Next, machine learning was applied to acquire candidate autophagy-related genes (ARGs) in deep. Subsequently, nomogram and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) were conducted to screen out core genes using the validation set. Finally, in vivo experiments were carried out. A rat model of FSGS was established to verify the expression of core ARGs. The present study firstly try to combine bioinformatics analysis, machine learning, and experimental validation to obtain autophagy-related core genes in FSGS. Therefore, the present study can contribute to the understanding of the connection between autophagy and FSGS, and also could help identify autophagy-related core diagnostic targets and potential small molecule compounds for patients with FSGS in the future (Fig. 1).

Materials and methods

Inclusion and exclusion criteria of datasets

The inclusion criteria of datasets were as follows: (1) Primary FSGS-related transcriptomics datasets in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database; (2) The organism was set as homo sapiens; (3) Glomerular or tubulointerstitial transcriptomes were from kidney biopsies of adult patients; (4) Healthy living donors or unaffected portion of tumor nephrectomies were included in the datasets. The exclusion criteria of datasets were as follows: (1) Transcriptomics datasets were form secondary FSGS or other kidney diseases in GEO database; (2) Kidney biopsies were not conducted in the datasets. (3) Datasets with no healthy living donors or unaffected portion of tumor nephrectomies.

Data collection and processing

A total of six datasets, GSE200828, GSE99340, GSE47183, GSE108109, GSE104948 and GSE129973, were downloaded from GEO database. The GSE200828 dataset contains 31 patients with primary FSGS and 6 controls, the GSE99340 dataset contains 21 patients with primary FSGS and 11 controls, the GSE47183 dataset contains 10 patients with primary FSGS and 3 controls, the GSE108109 dataset contains 30 patients with primary FSGS and 6 controls, the GSE104948 dataset contains 10 patients with primary FSGS and 18 controls, and the GSE129973 dataset contains 20 patients with primary FSGS and 20 controls. In the present study, the GSE200828, GSE99340, GSE47183, GSE108109, and GSE104948 datasets were merged as the training set, and the GSE129973 dataset was used as the validation set. Detailed information of those datasets was shown in Table 1.

Determination of ARDEGs in FSGS

A total of 222 ARGs were obtained from the Human Autophagy Database website (http://www.autophagy.lu/index.html). The ‘limma’ R package was applied to obtain differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the FSGS and control groups in the GSE200828, GSE99340, GSE47183, GSE108109, and GSE104948 datasets using |log FC| >0.5 and the adjusted P < 0.01 as criteria. The ‘Venn diagram’ R package was used to confirm ARDEGs.

Differential expression of ARDEGs

Differences in the expression of ARDEGs between the FSGS and control groups were represented via violin plots. P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistically significant differences.

PPI network and functional enrichment analyses

The STRING database (https://string-db.org/) was used to construct a PPI network among ARDEGs. Functional enrichment analysis included Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses26–28. The analyses were carried out using the ‘clusterProfiler’ R package for ARDEGs and visualized using the Sangerbox platform (http://www.sangerbox.com). Finally, biological processes (BPs), cellular components (CCs) and molecular functions (MFs) were acquired, as well as important signaling pathways.

GSEA analysis

Respective functions of each ARDEG were revealed using GSEA. According to median values of the gene expression levels, samples were distinguished into two groups, the low and the high expression groups. The ‘clusterProfiler’ R package was used to evaluate the distribution of ARDEG and compare their different contributions to FSGS. The ‘c2.cp.v7.5.1.symbols.gmt’ sets were downloaded from the MSigDB database (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/).

Immune infiltration analysis

The CIBERSORT algorithm was used to determine the different proportion of immune cells in tissues. The Barplot was used to visualize the specific proportion of each type of immune cells. The Vioplot was used to visualize the proportions of diverse types of immune cells between the FSGS and control groups. The correlation of 22 immune cells was carried out using the ‘corrplot’ R package.

cMAP analysis

cMAP (https://clue.io) was used to study connections between diseases, genes and potential small molecules. In the present study, the upregulated genes from the ARDEGs were inputted into the cMAP database to acquire potential small molecule compounds for treating FSGS. Finally, the top 10 compounds with the most negative correlation were confirmed.

Machine learning

Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) and random forest (RF) were used to screen ARGs in FSGS. The ‘glmnet’ R package was used to conduct the LASSO logistic regression, and the ‘randomForest’ R package was used to perform RF analysis in the current study. In the RF analysis, the minimum required importance score of genes was set as 1.0. The two intersecting genes were regarded as candidate ARGs for FSGS.

Nomogram construction and evaluation of ROC

The ‘rms’ R package and the validation dataset GSE129973 were used to construct a nomogram to visualize the impact of each candidate ARG on FSGS risk. The area under the curve (AUC) and the confidence interval (CI) were subsequently established to evaluate the value of each candidate ARG regarding FSGS diagnosis. If AUC was > 0.80, it was considered to show a strong diagnostic value. When AUC was ~ 1.0, it had the higher diagnostic value.

FSGS animal model establishment

A total of 20, 5–6-week-old healthy male Wistar rats weighing 160 ± 20 g were provided by Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd (licence no. SCXK 2017-0005). The animal experiments of the present study were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Yueyang Hospital of Integrated Traditional Chinese and Western Medicine, Shanghai, China (approval no. YYLAC-2023-218-3). This study was performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. All methods were reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines. The animal experiments were carried in the Experimental Animal Centre of Yueyang Hospital of Integrated Traditional Chinese and Western Medicine. All rats were housed in a specific-pathogen-free grade animal observation room, which was maintained at a temperature of 21–25 °C and a relative humidity of 60%, with a 12/12 h light/dark cycle. The rats were provided with a standard autoclaved commercial diet and filtered water. Those rats were randomly divided into two groups (the control group and the FSGS group; 10 rats per group). After adaptive feeding for 1 week, the rats in the FSGS group were administered 20 mg/kg adriamycin (ADR) by tail vein injection to induce FSGS29,30, and rats in the control group were administered 20 mg/kg saline by tail vein injection. All rats were observed for 21 days.

Animal euthanasia

Firstly, all rats were anesthetized using an intraperitoneal injection of 1% pentobarbital sodium (40 mg/kg). Then, the rats were euthanized by cervical dislocation, and animal death was confirmed by testing for loss of pain response. Next, kidneys were immediately harvested after euthanasia.

Histological analysis of kidney tissues using light and transmission electron microscopy

Kidney tissues were fixed in 10% neutral formalin phosphate buffer in each group, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 4-µm-thick sections. Those sections were dehydrated using anhydrous alcohol and stained with hematoxylin-eosin (HE). Then, small pieces (1-mm-thick cubes) of kidneys were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M sodium cacodylatebuffer (pH 7.4), postfixed with 2% (wt/vol) osmium tetroxide for examinations. Ultrathin sections were stained with uranyl acetate and examined under an transmission electron microscope.

Western blotting

Protein extraction from kidney tissues was conducted using RIPA lysis buffer. Protein concentration was detected using a BCA Protein Assay Kit. Next, proteins were separated through 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes by electroblotting at 200 mA for 60 min. The membranes were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin for 2 h at room temperature before being incubated overnight at 4˚C with primary antibodies (1:1,000) against TP53 (cat. no. 60283-2-Ig; Wuhan Sanying Biotechnology Co., LTD.) and RELA (cat. no. AF5006; Affinity Biosciences‌‌). After washing the membranes three times, the HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:20,000; cat. nos. SD0038 and SD0039; Shanghai Siding Biotechnology Co., LTD.) were incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Finally, the membranes were washed again, and proteins were detected through an ECL reagent (cat. no. SD0032; Shanghai Siding Biotechnology Co., LTD.). Protein bands were captured and analyzed using a Tanon 5200 (Tanon Science and Technology Co., Ltd.). Image analysis was performed using Image-Pro Plus software (version 6.0; Media Cybernetics, Inc.). The housekeeping protein β-actin (cat. no. SD0034; Shanghai Siding Biotechnology Co., LTD.) was used as a loading control.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

According to the manufacturer’s instructions, the mRNA expression of TP53 and RELA was measured by reverse transcription of the RNA using PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa, RR047A), and TB Green™ Premix Ex Taq™ (TaKaRa, RR420A). The TP53 primers were as follows: sense, GTTCGTGTTTGTGCCTGTCC; antisense, TCCGGGCAATGCTCTTCTTT. The RELA primers were as follows: sense, CCCAGCCCTATGCCTTTTCA; antisense, ACAGGTGCAGACAGGGATTG.

Statistical analysis

An independent Student’s t-test was used to compare normally distributed continuous variables between the two groups. The Wilcoxon rank sum test was used to analyze the non-normally distributed variables. P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistically significant differences.

Results

Identification of ARDEGs between the FSGS and control groups

A total of 102 patients with FSGS and 44 healthy controls were included. GSE200828, GSE99340, GSE47183, GSE108109, and GSE104948 were normalized (Fig. 2A). Principal component analysis (PCA) displayed the differential distribution between the FSGS and control groups in the GSE200828, GSE99340, GSE47183, GSE108109, and GSE104948 datasets (Fig. 2B). A total of 729 DEGs were identified using the GSE200828, GSE99340, GSE47183, GSE108109, and GSE104948 datasets, of which 536 genes were upregulated and 193 genes were downregulated (Fig. 2C). The expression of DEGs in the FSGS and control groups was shown as a heatmap (Fig. 2D). Finally, a total of 9 ARDEGs were obtained by taking the intersection of 729 DEGs and 222 ARGs (Fig. 2E); these were TP53, CAPN1, RELA, BAK1, BAX, FOS, CX3CL, DAPK2 and TNFSF10.

MF analysis showed that identical protein binding, protein heterodimerization activity and protein dimerization activity were the most significant items (Fig. 4E).

Immune infiltration analysis

The percentage of 22 immune cells was displayed as a barplot (Fig. 6A). Vioplot showed that FSGS had a higher number of resting natural killer (NK) cells, monocytes and activated dendritic cells. Meanwhile, a lower number of plasma cells, follicular helper T cells, resting dendritic cells and resting mast cells were observed in FSGS (Fig. 6B). Correlation analysis of the 22 immune cell types revealed that M0 macrophages were positively associated with resting mast cells (r = 0.51) and naive B cells were positively correlated with activated dendritic cells (r = 0.45). CD8+ T cells were negatively related to CD4+ activated memory T cells (r=−0.52; Fig. 6C). Varying degrees of immune cells were differentially infiltrated in FSGS, and they were anticipated to work as potential regulation points in treating FSGS.

Histopathological changes in a rat model of FSGS

As shown in Fig. 10A, there was no significant histopathologic abnormalities in the glomerular structures in the control group using light microscopy. However, the rats in the FSGS group exhibited pathological lesions characterized by abundant protein exudation in renal tubular lumens, and some segmental sclerosis with synechia to the Bowman’s capsule. The transmission electron microscopy showed more clear morphological ultrastructure changes of podocytes (Fig. 10B). Normal podocyte architecture could be observed in the control group. By contrast, massive effacement and extensive fusion of podocyte foot processes could be observed in the FSGS group.

Discussion

FSGS is one of the most frequent glomerulopathies in the world, considered a significant public health issue worldwide31. Through GEO datasets, nine ARDEGs were obtained which were closely related to the progress of autophagy and immunity in FSGS. Subsequently, a comprehensive bioinformatics strategy was applied to investigate features of ARDEGs, including violin plots, PPI network, KEGG, GO, GSEA and immune cell infiltration analyses. Besides, cMAP was conducted and 10 small molecule compounds were obtained as potential therapeutic drugs in the future. Then, LASSO and RF were further used to identify three candidate ARGs, TP53, RELA and TNFSF10. Moreover, the diagnostic specificity and sensitivity of TP53, RELA and TNFSF10 was assessed using a validation set. TP53 and RELA both had a high diagnostic value for FSGS, and they were regarded as core ARGs. Finally, a rat model of FSGS was constructed to verify the expression of core ARGs. Therefore, the present study revealed an autophagy-related mechanism in FSGS, and provided potential targets and small molecule compounds for diagnosing and treating FSGS.

Actually, FSGS is a frequent form of glomerulonephritis that may be caused by a soluble permeability factor and regulated by the immune system. Recently, it has been demonstrated that immune cells and inflammatory responses appear in stages of FSGS and play a crucial role in the progression of FSGS32,33. Although most FSGS cases are being treated with different immunosuppressant therapies, immune dysregulation in FSGS is not clearly understood. Therefore, various inflammatory factors and cellular infiltration have gradually become the focus of researchers. Not only NK and T cells, but also monocytes and B cells participate in the progression of damage and repair in FSGS34. It has been reported that activated CD8+ T cells lead to renal inflammation and podocyte injury by releasing microRNA (miR)−186-5p-enriched exosomes35. As one of the key cell type in the innate immune system, monocytes play a crucial role in FSGS by secreting soluble permeability factor calcium/calmodulin-dependent serine/threonine kinase to alter the glomerular filtration barrier36. In addition, M1 and M2 monocytes can be found in urine samples of patients with FSGS, which indicates that urine cell profiling can be used as a diagnostic and prognostic tool for FSGS37. Besides, it has been proved that monocytes play a marked role in promoting the expression of renal differentiation markers and organoid development by activating autophagy38. Activated dendritic cells are pivotal in regulating tolerogenic as well as immunogenic responses against microorganisms by directing the adaptive immune response. It has been demonstrated that the knockout of disabled homolog 2 in activated dendritic cells can inhibit autophagy and promote apoptotic cell death39. Based on our findings, patients with FSGS had a higher number of resting NK cells, monocytes and activated dendritic cells, which has similarities to previous studies40–42. Therefore, by gaining a deeper understanding of immune signaling mechanisms related to autophagy, the pathophysiology of FSGS can be better understood, thus providing more comprehensive and precise guidance for the search of effective therapeutic strategies.

TP53 has been extensively studied in the past two decades, especially in regulation of autophagy, apoptosis and adaptive immune responses43. An increasing number of studies support the involvement of TP53 in the progression of FSGS and podocyte injury, which is consistent with the present bioinformatic analysis and experimental validation, indicating that TP53 is upregulated in FSGS44,45. It has been reported that miR-770-5p was markedly upregulated in injured podocytes and could accelerate the development of podocyte apoptosis by targeting TP5346. As a factor exacerbating the progression of CKD, TP53 was activated in human injured podocytes to maintain the balance between proliferation, angiogenesis and cell cycle arrest by producing abundant cytokines in the urine47. It has been found that the positive expression range of TP53 in the kidney tissue of acute renal injury rats was large and the color was brown compared to the normal group, and the most of TP53 was distributed in podocytes48. In the kidney cortex of dehydrated camel, the expression of TP53 was increased significantly mainly in renal tubular epithelium and podocytes49. In summary, it is hypothesized that TP53 can represent a target for a new diagnose and treatment of FSGS.

The NF-κB P65 subunit RELA is a crucial component of the NF-κB family, which includes five related proteins50. These proteins from the NF-κB family are significant not only for inflammation and immune responses, but also for autophagy51. Activated RELA can drive the transcription of certain target genes by binding to a recognition site52. It has been proved that abnormal expression of RELA has an effect on the occurrence and progression of a number of fibrotic diseases, neuronal apoptosis, inflammatory disorders and cancers53–55. Due to its significant role in directing the immune response and autophagy-related pathways, RELA has attracted considerable interest, and has become a potential factor in the pathogenesis of various kidney diseases, including CKD, diabetic kidney disease and acute kidney injury56–58. Inhibiting the activation of RELA could effectively relieve proteinuria and prevent the progression of FSGS59. Further study demonstrates that RELA acts as an active player in podocyte disorders60. As one of the pyroptosis-related mediators in podocytes, RELA also increases the expression of target inflammasome-related genes, leading to pyroptosis priming. However, knockdown of RELA can offset the pyroptosis priming of podocytes61. It has been reported that RELA was overexpressed mainly in renal tubular epithelium both in the kidney tissue of CKD patients and unilateral ureteral obstruction (UUO) animal model, thus involving the progress of fibrosis and aging of kidney62. In a model of cadmium-induced chronic kidney injury, the localization of RELA also mainly expressed in renal tubular epithelium and contributed to pyroptosis63. Meanwhile, increased RELA expression was confirmed and located in renal tubular epithelium of the kidney tissues from lupus nephritis patients64. As one of the multiple NF-κB family members, RELA is overexpressed in FSGS. Thus, it is hypothesized that RELA represents a potential diagnostic and therapeutic target for FSGS.

Nowadays, an increasing number of researchers focus on identifying therapeutic small molecule compounds for various diseases and have achieved notable progress65. Compared with other methods, small molecule compounds have been widely used in treatment because of their strong penetrability, cost-effectiveness, high stability, good biocompatibility and convenient administration66. For example, since the outbreak of COVID-19 in 2019, the disease has become a global public health crisis, and few drugs have been applied to relieve infection of SARS-CoV-2. Recently, oridonin, a natural small molecule compound with strong antiviral activity, has been reported as an efficient antiviral agent against SARS-CoV-267. However, no research has reported small molecule compounds for FSGS by integrating bioinformatics. Thus, cMAP analysis was further conducted with help of upregulated genes from ARDEGs, and 10 compounds were acquired, phensuximide, benzanthrone, exemestane, SRT-1720, rilmenidine, XMD-885, XMD-1150, apicidin, dilazep and nitrendipine, as potential therapeutic drugs. The 10 compounds displayed the highest negative enrichment score, indicating they can inhibit abnormal gene expression of FSGS through various pathways, including activating SIRT and adrenergic receptor, blocking calcium channel, inhibiting aromatase and leucine rich repeat kinase. For example, as a first-line endocrine therapy for postmenopausal women, exemestane has been proved to promote cytoprotective autophagy and reduce inflammatory chemokine levels, thus blocking senescence induction68,69. Besides, it has been reported that the most frequent mutations in breast cancer patients include TP53, and exemestane can participate in the treatment of breast cancer via producing significant influence on TP5370. As the activator of sirtuin 1, SRT 1720 has been demonstrated to restore mitochondrial function and increase mitochondrial autophagy in renal endothelial cells. Besides, SRT 1720 has also been reported to alleviate podocyte injury through weakening glomerular endothelial cell-podocyte transmission71,72. As an imidazoline receptor agonist, rilmenidine has been proved to decrease malondialdehyde and increase diabetic rats’ total antioxidative capacity by modulating autophagic and antioxidant pathways73. As an antiplatelet drug, dilazep has been reported to reduce the number of urinary podocytes in diabetic patients with microalbuminuria, which may be useful for preventing podocyte injury in clinical74. Apicidin has been demonstrated to regulate distinct toxicological signatures and canonical signaling pathways in tumor cell lines. Among them, the TP53 signaling pathway is utilized by apicidin to effectively trigger cell death and apoptosis75. Therefore, it is anticipated that those small molecules may be potential drugs in treating FSGS by participating in autophagy-related processes and improving podocyte injury. However, further experimental studies are needed to prove drug effectiveness.

Conclusion

Through bioinformatics analysis, machine learning and experimental validation, two core ARGs, TP53 and RELA, were systematically obtained. The core genes screened in the present study are anticipated to be applied in diagnosing and treating FSGS. Additionally, 10 small molecule compounds were obtained as potential drugs in treating patients with FSGS via the cMAP database. In conclusion, the potential relationship between FSGS and autophagy was clarified, providing a new idea for clinical diagnosis and therapeutic drugs for future studies.

Limitations

Although new autophagy-related implications were suggested for future study of FSGS, the present study had several limitations. First, the number of samples used was still small despite the pooling of six FSGS datasets. Then, the six datasets (GSE200828, GSE99340, GSE47183, GSE108109, GSE104948, and GSE129973) were submitted from different countries by different organizations. For example, GSE200828 was conducted by University of Michigan in the United States of America, GSE129973 was conducted by Jinling Hospital affiliated to Nanjing University School of Medicine in China. So, dataset heterogeneity was difficult to avoid. In addition, although TP53 and RELA were mainly enriched in autophagy and immune-related pathways, this study lacks autophagy functional validation, and the interaction between those genes and autophagy remains unknown. Besides, the autophagy-related pathways found were not verified through in vitro experiments. Finally, machine learning requires a large number of high-quality data to train models. If the quality of data is not good enough, it may lead to the inaccuracy and instability. In the future, the focus of our subsequent studies will be to elucidate autophagy-related mechanisms of those genes and pathways on FSGS, including the localization of the key molecules in the rat kidneys. At the same time, we will pay more attention to verify the ten potential therapeutic agents through conducting in vivo and in vitro experiments to demonstrate their efficacy in treating FSGS.
