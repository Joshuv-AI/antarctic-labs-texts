Effects of an 8-week integrated neuromuscular training on lower limb functional asymmetry and balance in military personnel: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Yu Jiang, Panfeng Liu, Zhou Xiao, Jian Kang, Nanjun Chen, Zhikang Cheng, Xu Xiang, Ziao Li, Jiexing Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the effects of 8-week integrated neuromuscular training (INT) and traditional physical training on correcting lower limb functional asymmetry and improving balance ability in military personnel.MethodsTwenty military academy cadets were randomly assigned to the INT group (n = 10) or the traditional physical training group (n = 10). The intervention lasted for 8 weeks, with 3 sessions per week, each lasting 70–90 min. Lower limb asymmetry index was calculated through single-leg vertical jump, forward jump, and lateral jump tests, and balance ability was assessed using the Y-balance test at three time points: before the intervention, after 4 weeks of intervention, and after 8 weeks of intervention.ResultsThere were no significant differences in baseline data between the two groups (p > 0.05). Repeated measures analysis of variance showed a significant interaction effect (p < 0.05). After 8 weeks of intervention, the vertical jump asymmetry index in the INT group significantly decreased from 6.47 ± 1.46 to 4.22 ± 1.93 (ANCOVA, P = 0.01), the forward jump asymmetry index decreased from 4.68 ± 2.12 to 1.67 ± 0.88 (Holm-corrected repeated-measures ANOVA interaction, P = 0.006), and the lateral jump asymmetry index decreased from 5.14 ± 2.20 to 2.01 ± 1.10 (Holm-corrected repeated-measures ANOVA interaction, P = 0.045), while there was no significant improvement in any of the asymmetry indices in the traditional physical training group (p > 0.05). In terms of balance ability, the composite score of the INT group increased from 96.91 ± 5.89 to 101.55 ± 5.55 (Holm-corrected repeated-measures ANOVA interaction, P < 0.01), which was significantly higher than that of the traditional physical training group (98.83 ± 3.85 after 8 weeks, ANCOVA, P = 0.02). No adverse events occurred in any of the tests.ConclusionEight weeks of integrated neuromuscular training can effectively improve lower limb functional asymmetry and significantly enhance balance ability in military personnel. This training program can be used as an evidence-based intervention for military physical training and is conducive to reducing the risk of training injuries caused by asymmetry.Trial registrationNCT06663371, Registration Date: 22/10/2024.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13102-026-01760-3.Keywords: Integrated Neuromuscular Training, Military Personnel, Lower Limb Asymmetry, Balance Ability

Background

Integrated neuromuscular training (INT), formalized in the late 1990s, has evolved into a systematically structured paradigm that gained rapid acceptance across elite sport and rehabilitation settings in the early twenty-first century [1–3]. By concurrently targeting strength, balance, velocity, agility, fatigue resistance, and plyometric capacity within functional movement contexts, INT exerts its effects through up-regulated sensorimotor integration. Afferent signals arising from visual, vestibular, and proprioceptive sources converge on central neural networks, recalibrating efferent drive to skeletal muscle. This cascade enhances proprioception, neuromuscular control, postural stability, athletic performance, and injury prophylaxis [4–5].

Lower-limb functional asymmetry—defined herein as an inter-limb disparity in strength, power, or neuromuscular control that manifests during dynamic tasks—is a common phenomenon frequently observed in athletic, recreationally active, and military populations [6–8]. Such asymmetries do not simply overload the contralateral limb; rather, they induce a compensatory redistribution of mechanical and neural load across the kinetic chain. This compensation may overburden specific passive (e.g., articular cartilage, ligamentous) and active (musculotendinous) structures, while the less capable limb may rely on altered motor recruitment patterns due to reduced force attenuation capacity. Critically, this attenuation is governed not solely by intrinsic muscle properties but by central sensorimotor integration, as even transient changes in neural drive can modify joint loading and performance [9]. Consequently, asymmetry compromises multiplanar dynamic stability, elevating the risk of acute and overuse injury. Similarly, postural instability—operationally defined as the inability to maintain the centre of mass within the base of support during static or transient conditions—predicts both acute and cumulative musculoskeletal trauma [10–12].

Training-related injuries—including lower-extremity stress fractures, patellofemoral pain syndrome, chronic exertional compartment syndrome, and acute ankle sprains—undermine troop readiness by precipitating non-battle attrition, disrupting duty cycles, and generating substantial financial and logistical costs [13–14]. INT’s potential to mitigate these burdens lies in its capacity to optimise neuromuscular coordination and movement patterns. Specifically, by up-regulating sensorimotor integration and reinforcing efficient motor strategies, INT may directly counteract the compensatory loading patterns and dynamic instability associated with lower-limb functional asymmetry, thereby aligning with the multidimensional physical demands of military service [15–16].

Nevertheless, while traditional military physical training programs effectively develop general endurance and strength, they frequently lack targeted components to systematically address underlying neuromuscular deficits such as functional asymmetry and postural instability. Consequently, the empirical validation of structured INT protocols within this specific occupational context remains sparse, and its direct influence on operational preparedness and injury mitigation is unresolved [17–20].

Accordingly, we hypothesised that, in a cohort of active-duty service members presenting with baseline measures of functional lower-limb asymmetry and suboptimal postural stability, a targeted 8-week INT programme would (i) attenuate the magnitude of inter-limb functional asymmetry and (ii) enhance objective measures of postural stability. By addressing these objectives, the present investigation seeks to provide an evidence-based foundation for incorporating INT into military conditioning paradigms as a means to reduce the incidence of training-related injuries and sustain long-term force capability.

Methods

Study design

This assessor-blinded, two-arm parallel trial was conducted in accordance with the CONSORT guidelines. Eligible participants were randomly allocated (1:1) to an experimental group receiving INT or to a control group receiving general physical conditioning. A computer-generated randomisation sequence ensured concealment until the point of assignment. Although participants were blinded to group allocation, the interventionists were necessarily aware of condition to deliver the prescribed protocols. All outcome assessors and data analysts remained blinded throughout the study to safeguard objectivity.

This trial was prospectively registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06663371) on 22 October 2024. Registration occurred before the commencement of recruitment, in accordance with ICMJE requirements for prospective registration. Primary endpoints—lower-limb functional asymmetry and balance performance—were evaluated at baseline, week 4, and week 8. A two-factor (group × time) repeated-measures ANOVA was used to examine temporal changes. The protocol, including all procedures and consent documents, was reviewed and approved by the institutional ethics committee.

Participants

Thirty-two cadets from a military university were initially recruited through voluntary enrollment, of whom twenty met the predetermined inclusion criteria and completed the study. Eligibility requirements comprised: (1) age 18–22 years; (2) successful completion of the Functional Movement Screen (FMS) according to the established cut-off score (≥ 14 out of 21); (3) absence of documented psychiatric or psychological disorders; and (4) willingness to attend all scheduled training and testing sessions without unauthorized withdrawal. Exclusion criteria were: (1) history of major sports-related injury within the preceding 24 months (fractures, ligament ruptures, muscle or tendon tears, recurrent dislocations); (2) presence of cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, or visceral disorders; (3) failure to pass the FMS assessment (i.e., score < 14); and (4) concurrent musculoskeletal complaints or medical conditions that could compromise safe participation. Participants refrained from strenuous physical activity for 24 h preceding data collection and maintained habitual dietary and sleep patterns. Written informed consent was obtained from all volunteers after full disclosure of study objectives and procedures.

Functional movement screen (FMS)

The FMS is a functional assessment tool developed by Gray Cook and colleagues to evaluate an individual‘s fundamental movement quality. It consists of seven movement patterns: Deep Squat, Hurdle Step, Inline Lunge, Shoulder Mobility, Active Straight Leg Raise, Trunk Stability Push-up, and Rotary Stability. Each movement is scored on a 0–3 scale (0 = pain, 1 = inability to perform the pattern, 2 = compensation, 3 = correct performance), with a maximum total score of 21. The primary purpose of the FMS is to identify limitations in mobility and stability and to reveal compensatory movement patterns that may increase injury risk [21]. In this study, a total FMS score of ≥ 14 was used as the inclusion cut-off, consistent with previous literature indicating that scores below 14 are associated with a higher risk of sports-related injuries. Among the 20 participants who completed the study, the mean FMS score was 15.4 ± 1.2 (range: 14–18).

Sample size calculation and statistical power

We performed an a priori power analysis using G*Power 3.1.9.7 [22] to determine the required sample size for detecting a significant within-between interaction effect in our study design. Given the 2-group × 3-time-point structure (pre-intervention, 4 weeks post-intervention, and 8 weeks post-intervention), we employed a repeated-measures ANOVA F-test for within-between interaction. The analysis parameters were specified as follows: effect size f = 0.25 (medium effect, based on comparable clinical intervention studies); α error probability = 0.05; power (1-β) = 0.80; number of groups = 2; number of measurements = 3; correlation among repeated measures = 0.5; and nonsphericity correction ε = 1. This analysis indicated a required total sample size of 26 participants (13 per group).

To account for anticipated attrition, we applied a 20% dropout rate and initially recruited 32 participants. Following screening and enrollment, 12 participants were excluded: 6 failed the functional movement screen, 3 did not meet inclusion criteria, and 3 withdrew voluntarily. The remaining 20 participants were randomly assigned to two groups (n = 10 per group). Notably, the final sample size (N = 20) fell slightly below the a priori target of 26. To address this limitation, we conducted a sensitivity power analysis, which confirmed that our achieved sample size of 20 provides 80% power to detect a medium-to-large effect size (f = 0.28) at α = 0.05—an effect magnitude consistent with our intervention’s expected outcomes.

Experimental testing

To safeguard internal validity and enable adaptive monitoring of lower limb functional asymmetry and balance in military personnel, three longitudinal assessments were scheduled at baseline (week 0), mid-point (week 4), and post-intervention (week 8). An 8-week duration was chosen because it is sufficient to capture both early neural adaptations (weeks 1–4) and subsequent morphological and coordinative changes (weeks 4–8) that underpin improvements in asymmetry and balance, while remaining feasible within a military academic calendar. The week-4 assessment serves three critical purposes: (1) sequential testing of time-dependent changes in asymmetry and balance, enabling dynamic adjustment of training stimulus and enhancing participant motivation; (2) interim audits to detect and correct protocol deviations early, thereby increasing analytical efficiency and reducing injury risk; and (3) trajectory evaluation at a key transition point to provide prospective evidence that INT is driving hypothesised reductions in asymmetry and gains in balance, strengthening causal inference. Finally, stage-specific data from week 4 inform iterative refinements to the subsequent 4-week training block, ensuring scientific rigour throughout the trial.

Both experimental and control arms underwent pre- (week 0), mid- (week 4), and post-intervention (week 8) evaluations to quantify the effects of the 8-week INT regimen on lower limb functional asymmetry and balance. The experimental group completed the INT protocol three times per week (70–90 min per session), whereas the control group followed the standard physical-training curriculum with identical frequency and duration (3 sessions·week⁻¹, 70–90 min, 24 sessions in total), thereby equating total training volume between conditions. This study was conducted at a military university.

Testing indicators

Lower limb functional asymmetry test

Single-Leg Vertical Jump Test: An Eliga portable three-dimensional force plate (Portable Force Plate T136, ELIGA Sports Technology, Ningbo, China) was used for measurement. The primary measured parameter is the jump height (cm) during the single-leg vertical takeoff, reflecting lower limb explosive power; a higher score indicates better performance. Before testing, participants completed 10–15 min of warm-up activities, including familiarization exercises, jogging, and dynamic stretching. During the test, participants stood on one leg with the other leg flexed at the side of the body, maintaining an upright posture. Upon seeing the tester’s arm-swing signal, participants naturally squatted and jumped vertically upward with maximum effort and speed, then landed naturally in the same position. Tests were conducted on both the left and right sides, with three attempts per side. The highest score was recorded for analysis.

Single-Leg Lateral Jump and Forward Jump Tests: The primary measured parameters are the horizontal distance between the toes of both feet for the lateral jump (cm) and the distance from the takeoff line to the heel of the supporting leg for the forward jump (cm), reflecting unilateral lower limb explosive power and horizontal force production capacity; a higher score indicates better performance. Before testing, participants completed 10–15 min of warm-up activities. During the test, participants stood on one leg with the other leg flexed at the side of the body, maintaining an upright posture. Upon seeing the tester’s arm-swing signal, participants jumped as far as possible to the contralateral side (for the lateral jump) or forward (for the forward jump). A valid attempt was defined as landing on the opposite foot and maintaining balance for at least 3 s. Tests were conducted on both the left and right sides, with three attempts per side. The highest score was recorded for analysis.

Symmetry Index: The lower limb functional asymmetry index was calculated using the absolute asymmetry index formula proposed by Maines et al. [23]: ASI = (|L-R|)/((L + R)/2) × 100%. This index focuses solely on the magnitude of the difference between the two sides, without distinguishing between the dominant and non-dominant sides. A smaller ASI indicates better functional symmetry of the lower limbs, while a larger ASI indicates poorer symmetry.

Balance ability test

The Y-balance test (YBT) was used for assessment, following the methods described by Xu Fei et al. and Yang Wei et al. [24–25]. The primary measured parameters are the maximal reach distances (cm) in the anterior (A), posteromedial (PM), and posterolateral (PL) directions, as well as the composite score; a higher composite score indicates better dynamic balance ability. During the test, participants stood barefoot on one leg on the YBT device with their hands on their hips, using the toes of the non-supporting leg to slowly push the test plates as far as possible. The test order was first with the right leg as the supporting leg, then with the left leg as the supporting leg. Each direction was tested three times, and the maximal distance was used for analysis. During the test, participants were required to keep their supporting foot stationary and their hands on their anterior superior iliac spines. The composite score was calculated using the following formula: Composite Score = (A + PM + PL) / (3 × Lower Limb Length) × 100%. Lower limb length was measured during the familiarization phase, with participants lying supine, and the distance from the anterior superior iliac spine to the medial malleolus was recorded. The right lower limb length was uniformly used for calculation.

Training intervention protocol

The INT protocol in this study [26–33] primarily includes modules for resistance training, plyometric training, balance and coordination training, core stability training, and speed and agility training. The training intervention focused on the quality and accuracy of movement execution, with detailed records maintained for each exercise regarding repetitions, sets, and duration. Participants were instructed to stop an exercise if they were unable to perform it correctly due to fatigue or other reasons during the session. The training intervention lasted for a total of 8 weeks (3 sessions per week), conducted on Monday, Wednesday, and Friday afternoons. Each INT session lasted 70–90 min, including a structured 15-minute warm-up and a 15-minute cool-down period. On Monday, the INT training intervention comprised core stability training and plyometric training; on Wednesday, it included speed and agility training along with resistance training; on Friday, it involved speed and agility training, balance and coordination training, and plyometric exercises. The training for each module followed a circuit training pattern, with each module consisting of 6–8 exercise movements. For example, on Monday, the training intervention plan was to complete one primary module (e.g., plyometric exercises) before moving to another module (e.g., core stability training). After completing all sets of a specific exercise (e.g., box jumps), participants would proceed to the next exercise (e.g., consecutive depth jumps). (Table 1) details the specific INT training modules, exercise forms, content, load, and number of sets. The INT protocol maintained relatively fixed loads and volumes throughout the 8-week intervention. Importantly, the INT program incorporated exercises that directly mirror the primary outcome measures (e.g., single-leg vertical jumps, lateral/forward jumps, and balance tasks on unstable surfaces). This design ensures that the training specificity aligns with the assessment tasks, allowing any observed improvements to be attributed to the integrated neuromuscular training rather than to task familiarity.

INT program

The training regimen is adapted from relevant previous studies [26–33]

In this study, traditional physical training refers to a training method focused on enhancing individual fitness components. It typically incorporates resistance training, aerobic exercise, as well as basic speed, agility, and coordination drills. Unlike INT training, traditional training often employs a segmented approach, targeting specific fitness components such as speed, strength, endurance, and agility in isolation, with less emphasis on integrated neuromuscular development. For instance, exercises like sprints, squats, and long-distance running in traditional training are often designed with singular objectives, lacking the comprehensive training of core stability, balance, coordination, and power. Each traditional training session lasted approximately 70–90 min (including a 15-minute warm-up and a 15-minute cool-down). Specific program details are provided in (Table 2). The reason traditional training lacks the exercises listed in the INT protocol is that traditional training follows a component-separated philosophy (i.e., developing speed, strength, endurance, and agility independently), whereas INT emphasizes integrated neuromuscular adaptations, including core stability, dynamic balance, plyometric power, and multi-planar coordination. Consequently, exercises such as single-leg balance pad stands, BOSU ball squats, box jumps, and explosive medicine ball throws are inherently absent from the traditional curriculum.

Arrangement of traditional physical fitness training content

Attendance was recorded for every training session. In the INT group, the mean attendance rate was 92.5%, ranging from 85% to 100%, corresponding to an average of 22.2 out of 24 sessions completed. In the control group, the mean attendance rate was 90.8%, ranging from 82% to 100%, corresponding to 21.8 out of 24 sessions. No participant dropped out after randomisation. Exercise compliance, defined as performing the prescribed number of sets and repetitions with correct technique, was also monitored by the interventionists. The INT group achieved a compliance rate of 89%, indicating that in 11% of the training sessions participants reduced load or repetitions due to fatigue. The control group achieved a compliance rate of 94%, with minor deviations mainly observed in running drills. These data indicate acceptable adherence and compliance in both groups.

As INT training places greater emphasis on the development of balance and coordination, core stability, motor control, and agility, the intervention protocol in this study, while considering the balanced development of participants physical fitness, focused more on their balance ability, core stability, coordination, and agility. Rest intervals between sets were arranged to be relatively sufficient to ensure adequate recovery for the participants.

Primary outcome measures

This was an assessor-blinded randomized controlled trial. Due to the nature of the exercise intervention, participant blinding was not feasible; however, outcome assessors were blinded to group allocation to minimize detection bias. Participants were randomly assigned to either the experimental (INT training) or control group, with assessments conducted at three time points: baseline (pre-intervention), week 4, and week 8. Lower limb asymmetry indices and balance ability test scores were measured at each assessment point.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS (Version 27.0). All quantitative data were normally or approximately normally distributed. Baseline demographic data were compared using independent samples t-tests, while repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed for measurements across the three time points. If the assumption of sphericity was violated according to Mauchly’s test, the results were adjusted using the Greenhouse-Geisser correction in the univariate ANOVA. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The statistical methods used in this study were reviewed by a statistical expert.

Comparison of single-leg vertical jump test results among groups

Repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) revealed a significant main effect of group on left-leg vertical jump performance (F = 6.08, P = 0.02), a significant main effect of time (F = 102.33, P < 0.001), and a significant group × time interaction (F = 11.58, uncorrected P < 0.001, Holm-corrected P = 0.003).

To decompose this interaction, simple effects analysis indicated that the between-group effect for left-leg jump height was not statistically significant at baseline (F = 1.641, P = 0.217), but became significant at week 4 (F = 6.885, P = 0.017) and more pronounced at week 8 post-intervention (F = 9.950, P = 0.005). Simple effects of time demonstrated significant improvements under both INT training (F = 66.642, P < 0.001, partial η² = 0.887) and traditional physical training (F = 16.887, P < 0.001, partial η² = 0.665). Post hoc comparisons confirmed that both groups exhibited monotonic increases in left-leg jump height across pre-, mid-, and post-intervention assessments (all P < 0.05). The between-group mean difference at week 8 was 3.3 cm (95% CI [1.2, 5.4]).

For right-leg vertical jump performance, repeated-measures ANOVA showed no statistically significant main effect of group (F = 2.45, P = 0.14), but a significant main effect of time (F = 171.61, P < 0.01); the group × time interaction was not statistically significant after Holm correction (P = 0.09).

Analysis of the vertical jump asymmetry index via repeated-measures ANOVA revealed no statistically significant main effect of group (F = 0.16, P = 0.70) or time (F = 2.89, P = 0.07), but a marginally significant group × time interaction (F = 3.83, uncorrected P = 0.03, Holm-corrected P = 0.06).

Given baseline differences in the vertical jump asymmetry index between groups (experimental group: 6.47 ± 1.46 vs. control group: 4.69 ± 3.86), an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was conducted with baseline values as a covariate. Results showed a significant group effect at week 8 (F = 8.42, P = 0.01), with the experimental group demonstrating a significantly lower adjusted mean (4.15 ± 0.61) compared to the control group (5.08 ± 0.61); the adjusted mean difference was − 0.93 (95% CI [− 1.56, − 0.30]).

Simple effects decomposition revealed a significant simple effect of time under INT training (F = 6.050, P = 0.01), whereas no statistically significant effect was observed under traditional physical training (F = 0.23, P = 0.797). Post hoc pairwise comparisons showed that under the INT condition, significant reductions occurred from week 4 to week 8 (mean difference = − 2.31, 95% CI [− 3.52, − 1.10]) and from pre-intervention to post-intervention (mean difference = − 2.25, 95% CI [− 3.28, − 1.22]). Conversely, no significant temporal differences in the asymmetry index were observed under traditional training at any time point (P > 0.05). Detailed results are presented in (Table 4).

Comparison of single-leg forward jump test results between groups

Repeated-measures ANOVA for left-leg forward jump performance showed no statistically significant main effect of group (F = 1.75, P = 0.20), a significant main effect of time (F = 188.59, P < 0.001), and a significant group × time interaction (F = 15.80, uncorrected P < 0.001, Holm-corrected P = 0.001). Post hoc comparisons confirmed that both the INT and traditional groups exhibited significant increases in left-leg forward jump distance across pre-, mid-, and post-intervention assessments (all P < 0.05). The between-group mean difference at week 8 was 5.8 cm (95% CI [2.1, 9.5]).

For right-leg forward jump performance, repeated-measures ANOVA revealed no statistically significant main effect of group (F = 1.69, P = 0.21), a significant main effect of time (F = 414.03, P < 0.001), and a significant group × time interaction (F = 23.73, uncorrected P < 0.001, Holm-corrected P < 0.001).

Analysis of the forward jump asymmetry index via repeated-measures ANOVA showed no statistically significant main effect of group (F = 2.46, P = 0.13), but a significant main effect of time (F = 15.21, P < 0.001) and a significant group × time interaction (F = 10.53, uncorrected P = 0.002, Holm-corrected P = 0.006).

ANCOVA with baseline values as a covariate revealed a significant group effect at week 8 (F = 11.24, P = 0.003), with the experimental group showing a significantly lower adjusted mean (1.82 ± 0.28) compared to the control group (4.15 ± 0.28); the adjusted mean difference was − 2.33 (95% CI [− 3.52, − 1.14]). Post hoc comparisons indicated that the INT group demonstrated sequential reductions in the asymmetry index across pre-, mid-, and post-intervention: pre-intervention versus week 4, mean difference = − 1.89 (95% CI [− 2.98, − 0.80]); week 4 versus week 8, mean difference = − 1.12 (95% CI [− 1.86, − 0.38]); pre-intervention versus week 8, mean difference = − 3.01 (95% CI [− 4.25, − 1.77]). No significant temporal differences were observed in the traditional group (P > 0.05). Detailed results are presented in (Table 5).

Repeated-measures ANOVA for left-leg lateral jump performance revealed no statistically significant main effect of group (F = 1.66, P = 0.21), a significant main effect of time (F = 252.66, P < 0.001), and a significant group × time interaction (F = 18.74, uncorrected P < 0.001, Holm-corrected P < 0.001). Post hoc comparisons confirmed that both groups exhibited significant increases in left-leg lateral jump distance across pre-, mid-, and post-intervention assessments (all P < 0.05). The between-group mean difference at week 8 was 5.5 cm (95% CI [1.8, 9.2]).

For right-leg lateral jump performance, repeated-measures ANOVA showed no statistically significant main effect of group (F = 0.58, P = 0.46), a significant main effect of time (F = 100.20, P < 0.001), and a marginally significant group × time interaction (F = 5.07, uncorrected P = 0.03, Holm-corrected P = 0.045).

Analysis of the lateral jump asymmetry index via repeated-measures ANOVA revealed no statistically significant main effect of group (F = 2.26, P = 0.15), but a significant main effect of time (F = 25.95, P < 0.001) and a marginally significant group × time interaction (F = 4.71, uncorrected P = 0.03, Holm-corrected P = 0.045).

No significant temporal differences were observed in the traditional group (P > 0.05). Detailed results are presented in (Table 6).

Adverse events

No adverse events, such as muscle or ligament strains or joint injuries, occurred during either the training or testing periods.

Discussion

Within the military physical training paradigm, lower limb functional asymmetry — defined herein as an inter-limb disparity in strength, power, or neuromuscular control that manifests during dynamic tasks — and balance control capacity are recognized as relevant biomechanical variables influencing both mission execution efficiency and the risk of sports-related injuries. Functional asymmetry can not only trigger compensatory load distribution abnormalities along the kinetic chain but also exhibits a significant positive correlation with the incidence of lower limb injuries. This association is particularly pronounced during typical military movement patterns such as loaded marching, tactical direction changes, and jump landings [3, 34]. The central finding of the present study indicates that, compared to the traditional physical training model, INT demonstrates a significant interventional advantage in correcting lower limb functional asymmetry and optimizing balance control. More importantly, this advantage remained consistently different at both the 4-week and 8-week training time points, suggesting that the neural adaptation mechanisms induced by INT possess continuous and cumulative characteristics, rather than representing transient acute effects.

Specifically, the improvement in asymmetry can be analyzed in depth from three dimensions: training surface stability, training dose threshold, and the directional influence of exercise selection preference on asymmetry correction. Using this framework as a guide, we compared our results with those of previous studies.

First, regarding training surface stability, Sannicandro et al. [35] reported that after 16 sessions of unstable surface training, each lasting 30 min, over 8 weeks in Italian U15 male soccer players, the asymmetry score in the single-leg hop test dropped from 18.92% to 4.50%, with a very large effect size. Our study observed a similar directional improvement in military cadets, though the magnitude of change was smaller. This difference may be explained by population characteristics, i.e., the difference between adolescent athletes and military cadets, as well as differences in baseline asymmetry levels. Importantly, the enforced symmetry constraint induced by unstable surfaces, where continued reliance on the dominant limb leads to immediate instability, appears to be a shared mechanism across both studies, and our INT protocol also incorporated unstable surface exercises, such as single-leg balance pad stands and BOSU ball squats.

Second, concerning training dose threshold, Lei et al. [10] studied Chinese deaf table tennis athletes and found that after an 8-week INT intervention of 4 sessions per week, each lasting 60 min, the single-leg vertical jump power asymmetry index decreased by 5.81 and the lateral jump asymmetry index decreased by 5.25. Our study used a lower dose of 3 sessions per week, each lasting 70 to 90 min, yet still achieved significant asymmetry reduction, suggesting that even a moderate dose of INT is effective in a military population. In contrast, Roso-Moliner et al. [36] reported that a lower-dose INT protocol of 24 min per session, 3 times per week, in female soccer players improved sprint and change-of-direction performance but produced only a minimal reduction in lower limb asymmetry, with no significant between-group differences. This comparison supports the existence of a dose-response threshold: our protocol of 70 to 90 min per session likely exceeded the effective threshold, whereas the shorter duration used by Roso-Moliner et al. did not.

Third, regarding exercise selection preference, Mainer-Pardos et al. [37] found that in adolescent male tennis players, bilateral vertical and horizontal jumps improved significantly, but unilateral jump and change-of-direction asymmetry did not. The authors attributed this to a training design dominated by bilateral movements. In our INT protocol, we deliberately incorporated a substantial proportion of unilateral exercises, such as single-leg vertical jumps, single-leg lateral jumps, and single-leg balance pad stands. This difference in exercise selection likely explains why we observed significant improvement in asymmetry while they did not. Therefore, unilateral-unstable compound exercises appear to be essential for breaking the long-term preferential recruitment pattern of the dominant limb.

Regarding balance ability, our results are consistent with previous studies [19, 27, 38]. Xiong et al. [27] demonstrated in Chinese elite female table tennis players that an 8-week INT intervention of 4 sessions per week, each lasting 30 min, significantly increased Y-Balance Test reach distances in the posteromedial and posterolateral directions, with increases of 9.74% and 8.83% for the right leg and 7.14% and 6.18% for the left leg respectively, whereas the traditional physical training group showed no significant improvement. Our study observed a similar pattern. Benis et al. [38] reported that integrating neuromuscular training into warm-ups, twice a week for 8 weeks, led to improvements of 3.5% to 5.5% in posteromedial and posterolateral reach distances and 5.4% to 5.8% in composite scores for elite female basketball players. Panagoulis et al. [19] further confirmed in highly trained young male soccer players that an INT protocol performed twice weekly for 8 weeks produced significant, large-effect-size improvements in balance ability. The consistency across these studies, despite differences in populations, training frequencies, and durations, supports INT as a robust intervention for enhancing dynamic balance.

Looking ahead, certain limitations of this study warrant careful consideration. First, the sample size was limited and drawn from a single military unit. Participants were conventional combat soldiers, not including special forces or personnel from specialized environments like high-altitude or cold regions, which may constrain the generalizability and external validity of the findings. Second, the training intervention was conducted in a standardized setting, failing to fully simulate the cognitive load, extreme fatigue, and complex terrain interference of real combat scenarios, resulting in limited ecological validity. Finally, the 8-week intervention period is relatively short, and the persistence of training effects and long-term adaptations remain unclear.

Nonetheless, the demonstrated efficacy of INT in effectively reducing lower limb asymmetry and enhancing balance capacity in soldiers provides important empirical evidence for military physical training. Future research should employ larger sample sizes and multi-center randomized controlled designs, incorporating diverse military populations. It is also recommended to develop “battlefield-realistic” training modules that integrate stressors such as sleep deprivation, tactical decision-making fatigue, and load carriage, and to conduct longitudinal follow-ups to assess the sustained effects and actual task transfer rates of the training. This will systematically optimize the application paradigm of integrated neuromuscular training in the military domain.

Conclusion

An 8-week INT intervention effectively improved lower limb functional asymmetry, defined herein as an inter-limb disparity in strength, power, or neuromuscular control during dynamic tasks, and enhanced balance capacity in a military population. For future military training practice, INT can be adopted as a key interventional strategy to enhance lower limb functional symmetry and balance capacity, thereby potentially reducing the risk of training-related injuries associated with functional asymmetry in service members. Building upon the specific occupational characteristics and task demands of military personnel, particular emphasis should be placed on strengthening bilateral symmetry and achieving balanced development to mitigate the injury risk linked to asymmetry.
