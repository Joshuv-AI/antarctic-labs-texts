Prognostic Value of Composite Biomarkers Integrating Insulin Resistance and Coronary Inflammation for Major Adverse Cardiovascular Events in Post-PCI Patients with Coronary Artery Disease Across Glycemic Status: A Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Zixiang Ye, Chenyue Liu, Enmin Xie, Hao-Yu Wang, Chenxi Song, Rui Zhang, Kefei Dou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe study aimed to assess the prognostic utility of composite Insulin resistance (IR)- various inflammation biomarkers for predicting poor outcomes in coronary artery disease (CAD) patients stratified by glycemic status.MethodsThe prospective cohort included 1947 CAD patients undergoing PCI with pre-procedural coronary computed tomography angiography. Novel composite biomarkers integrating insulin resistance and inflammation (TyG-hsCRP, CTI, and TyG-fat attenuation index [FAI]) were constructed and calculated. Patients were categorized by glycemic status (normoglycemia, prediabetes, diabetes). Three-year follow-up tracked MACE (all-cause death, non-fatal myocardial infarction, unplanned revascularization). Analyses involved Cox regression, Kaplan-Meier curves, restricted cubic splines, receiver operating characteristic curves, and subgroup analyses.ResultsMACE occurred in 171 patients (8.8%). After multivariable adjustment, high versus low biomarker levels were associated with increased MACE risk: hazard ratio (HR) 1.23 (95% CI, 1.03–1.74; P=0.025) for TyG-hsCRP, 1.39 (95% CI, 1.04–2.04; P=0.032) for CTI, and 2.32 (95% CI, 1.67–3.25; P<0.001) for TyG-FAI. TyG-FAI demonstrated superior discrimination (AUC 0.641) compared with TyG-hsCRP (0.594), CTI (0.604). Joint stratification revealed the highest risk in patients with high TyG-FAI and diabetes (HR, 3.21 [95% CI, 1.65–6.30]; P=0.001 versus low TyG-FAI and normoglycemia). Similar gradients were observed for high CTI-diabetes (HR, 1.88 [95% CI, 1.13–3.08]; P=0.014) and high TyG-hsCRP-diabetes (HR, 1.88 [95% CI, 1.13–3.08]; P=0.014). Associations were consistent across subgroups (P for interaction >0.05).ConclusionComposite biomarkers integrating IR and inflammation provide modest incremental value in predicting MACE in CAD patients. The predictive utility of these biomarkers is modulated by underlying glycemic status, highlighting the role of local coronary inflammation and supporting their potential integration into risk stratification.

Introduction

Coronary artery disease (CAD) remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide,1 with major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) representing a critical endpoint for assessing patient prognosis following interventions such as percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). Insulin resistance (IR), a key component of metabolic dysfunction, is closely linked to atherosclerotic progression and adverse outcomes in CAD patients. The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index serves as a reliable surrogate marker for IR and has demonstrated prognostic value in cardiovascular disease.2

Inflammation plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of CAD, contributing to plaque instability and vascular remodelling.3 Systemic inflammation, often quantified by markers such as high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP) and C-reactive protein (CRP), reflects broader metabolic and inflammatory states that may influence cardiovascular risk.4–6 In contrast, local inflammation within the perivascular adipose tissue surrounding coronary arteries can be assessed non-invasively using the fat attenuation index (FAI) derived from coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA),7,8 which captures adipose tissue remodeling and inflammatory signaling specific to the vascular microenvironment.9,10

Although the TyG index is associated with CAD prognosis, its predictive utility may be enhanced by integration with inflammatory biomarkers.11,12 Although several previous studies have combined the TyG index with systemic inflammation markers (TyG-hsCRP, C-reactive protein–triglyceride glucose index [CTI]),6,13,14 the potential additive value of integrating insulin resistance with local coronary inflammation remains largely unexplored. Insulin resistance promotes local inflammation through oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and perivascular adipose tissue dysregulation, thereby directly linking insulin resistance to local coronary inflammation, which in turn drives plaque instability and vascular remodelling, ultimately contributing to adverse cardiovascular events. Furthermore, the comparative prognostic value of TyG adjusted by local inflammation versus TyG combined with systemic inflammation remains underexplored, particularly when stratified by glycemic status.

This study aimed to evaluate and compare the predictive ability of IR adjusted by local coronary inflammation marker and IR combined with systemic inflammation indices (TyG-hsCRP or CTI) for prognostic value for MACE in CAD patients undergoing PCI, stratified by glycemic status (normoglycemia, prediabetes, and diabetes), using a prospective cohort design. We also compared these biomarkers’ performance and their added value for risk stratification and evaluate their integrated stratification value alongside glycemic status.

Method

Study Design and Participants

This prospective cohort study enrolled consecutive CAD patients who underwent PCI with pre-procedural CCTA at a single tertiary center. Inclusion criteria required documented CAD confirmed by coronary angiography, PCI performance, and available baseline CCTA. Exclusion criteria included prior coronary artery bypass grafting, incomplete baseline laboratory data (hsCRP, CRP, glucose, triglycerides). A total of 1947 patients were included after applying these criteria (Figure 1). All participants provided written informed consent, and the study protocol was approved by the institutional review board, adhering to the Declaration of Helsinki. Data Collection and Measurements

Demographic and clinical data, including age, sex, BMI, comorbidities (hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, family history of CAD, smoking), and LVEF, were collected from electronic medical records. Laboratory parameters, such as HbA1c, total cholesterol, triglycerides, LDL-C, HDL-C, glucose, hsCRP, and CRP, were measured using standardized assays on fasting venous blood samples obtained pre-PCI.

CCTA examinations were conducted in strict compliance with the Society of Cardiovascular Computed Tomography (SCCT) guidelines with standard protocols and iterative reconstruction. FAI was automatically measured, quantified for the left anterior descending, left circumflex, and right coronary arteries (RCA), with values averaged across segment.7 The FAI of the RCA was used as the analysis indicator for local coronary inflammation as the strong predictive value in poor prognosis.8

Follow-up was conducted via clinic visits, telephone interviews, or medical record reviews. The primary outcome was MACE, defined as a composite of all-cause death, non-fatal myocardial infarction, and unplanned repeat revascularization.

Definitions of Indices

The TyG index was calculated as ln[TG (mg/dL) × glucose (mg/dL)/2].

CTI was derived as 0.412 × ln(CRP [mg/L]) + ln[TG (mg/dL) × glucose (mg/dL)/2].

TyG-hsCRP was computed as Ln[TG (mg/dL) × FPG (mg/dL)/2] + hsCRP (mg/L).

TyG-FAI was defined as Ln[TG (mg/dL) × FPG (mg/dL)/2] + FAI-RCA.

Median cutoffs were used for stratification: TyG ≤8.88 vs >8.88; CTI ≤9.31 vs >9.31; TyG-hsCRP ≤10.4 vs >10.4; TyG-FAI ≤-66.16 vs >-66.16.

Definition of Glycemic Status

Glycemic status was defined according to World Health Organization/International Expert Committee (WHO/IEC) criteria. Prediabetes was defined as an HbA1c level of 6.0–6.5% or an FPG level of 6.1–7.0 mmol/L.15 Diabetes was defined based on a self-reported physician’s diagnosis, current use of antidiabetic medication, or newly detected hyperglycemia (defined as FPG ≥7.0 mmol/L or HbA1c ≥6.5%). Individuals with glycemic levels below the threshold for prediabetes were classified as having normoglycemia.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean (SD) or median (IQR) and compared using t-tests, ANOVA, Mann–Whitney U, or Kruskal–Wallis tests. Categorical variables were presented as n (%) and analyzed with chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests.

Survival analyses employed Kaplan-Meier curves with Log rank tests to compare MACE-free survival across index strata and combined groups. Missing data were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE), incorporating all covariates from the final multivariable model. Cox proportional hazards models estimated HRs with 95% CIs for MACE associations, using unadjusted and adjusted models. Results are presented for both median-based categorical and continuous variable analyses. Variables for inclusion in the multivariable Cox regression were selected following assessment of multicollinearity using the variance inflation factor (VIF), ensuring no substantial collinearity. The proportional hazards assumption was verified through examination of Schoenfeld residuals, with no significant violations detected. Adjustments included gender, age, BMI, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, history of CAD, smoking, heart rate, systolic blood pressure, LVEF, severe coronary calcification, and medication use. Dose-response relationships were assessed via Restricted cubic spline (RCS). Subgroup analyses stratified by age (≥65 vs <65 years), sex (male vs female), BMI (≥30 vs <30), hypertension (yes vs no), and smoking (yes vs no) tested for interactions using likelihood ratio tests.

Analyses were performed with R (version 4.2.1) and STATA (version 17.0), with two-sided p<0.05 considered significant.

Associations of Biomarkers with MACE Across Glycemic Status

The prognostic value of the biomarkers for poor prognosis varied across glycemic statuses (Table 3). In normoglycemia and prediabetes, adjusted associations were significant for TyG-FAI (HR 1.06, 95% CI 1.03–1.10, P=0.001 and HR 1.10, 95% CI 1.04–1.16, P=0.001). Discussion

Our prospective cohort study involving 1947 patients with CAD who underwent PCI brings to light a key finding: integrating IR with local coronary inflammation via FAI (TyG-FAI) and systemic inflammation (TyG-hsCRP and CTI) offers strong prognostic insights into MACE. Notably, an elevated TyG-FAI emerged as an independent predictor, linked to a more than twofold increase in event risk after multivariable adjustment, and it demonstrated the best discrimination with an AUC of 0.641. Stratified analyses revealed amplified risks in diabetes subgroups, where joint effects with dysglycemia conferred the highest MACE hazards. Risk stratification integrating the composite biomarkers with glycemic status successfully identified patient subgroups at the highest MACE risk. Subgroup consistency across age, sex, BMI, hypertension, and smoking strata underscored its robustness.

The association between insulin resistance and systemic inflammation has long been recognized in cardiovascular pathology, with metabolic disturbances linked to a pro-inflammatory state that promotes atherosclerosis. The TyG index, as a practical surrogate for insulin resistance, shows enhanced predictive value when combined with inflammatory markers such as hsCRP and CRP, reflecting their interplay.16,17 For example, in cohorts of patients with ACS following intervention, higher TyG levels have been shown to predict adverse events, but this association strengthens in the context of modest hsCRP elevations below 2 mg/L, hinting at how inflammation might amplify TyG’s relevance.18 Broader population studies, such as the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study, further illustrate this: individuals with concurrently high TyG and hsCRP face about a 30% greater risk of cardiovascular disease, with evidence of bidirectional mediation where each factor exacerbates the other.19 Abnormal glucose metabolism and inflammation may worsen prognosis in CAD patients, partly via subclinical myocardial injury indicated by elevated troponin T and NT-proBNP levels.20 Glycemic status may also influence vascular adaptation and clinical outcomes, as a recent study linked higher admission blood glucose to prolonged hospital stay in acute heart failure.21 Turning to composite metrics, CTI incorporating CRP has been evaluated in settings like geriatric populations, where elevated levels correlate with 18% to 42% higher odds of cardiovascular morbidity or death, underscoring the compounded burden of metabolic and inflammatory stressors.22–25

Yet, it’s the localized inflammation around coronary arteries that may hold the key to more precise risk assessment, as captured by FAI on CCTA, which reflects PVAT changes driven by nearby vascular insults. This measure stands out because it pinpoints inflammation right at the site of plaque formation.8 However, FAI measurements are subject to technical variability due to differences in CT scanners, imaging protocols, and the current lack of standardized reference values and cut-off thresholds. Insulin resistance likely intensifies this local process; in conditions like obesity, it prompts adipose tissue to release pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6, disrupting the protective adipokine profile and creating a microenvironment conducive to atherogenesis.26 Animal models and human tissue studies suggest that insulin-resistant PVAT loses its vasoprotective role, shifting toward a phenotype that promotes endothelial dysfunction and plaque progression through altered signaling pathways.27,28

In our analysis, we specifically employed FAI measurements from the RCA to derive TyG-FAI, guided by prior evidence indicating that RCA-FAI is particularly prognostic for adverse outcomes in CAD patients, owing to its lower anatomical variability and heightened sensitivity to systemic inflammatory processes that correlate with global cardiovascular risk. Studies have consistently shown that elevated RCA-FAI independently predicts MACE, including cardiac mortality, potentially reflecting RCA’s dominance in right-dominant coronary circulations and its association with extensive ischemia.8,29–31

Mechanistically, the heightened risk of poor outcomes from this insulin resistance-inflammation nexus can be traced to several intertwined processes: impaired endothelial function, lipid imbalances, and chronic oxidative stress. Insulin resistance hampers key pathways like IRS-1/PI3K, reducing nitric oxide availability and favoring vasoconstriction, while inflammation—through agents like TNF-α—further blocks insulin signaling, establishing a feedback loop.26,32 In the arterial wall, this leads to lipid accumulation, macrophage infiltration, and eventual plaque rupture; metabolites such as diacylglycerols and ceramides build up, which in turn perpetuates vascular damage.33,34

The composite indices were constructed using a simple additive approach for clinical simplicity and practicality, as both components were independently associated with adverse outcomes and no validated weighting scheme currently exists. Our findings highlight that combining insulin resistance (TyG) with local inflammation (FAI) correlates more strongly with MACE than with systemic markers, suggesting that TyG-FAI captures a more direct pathway in CAD progression. This implies that localized inflammatory signals, being closer to the atherosclerotic core, might better reflect the metabolic vulnerabilities driving events in these patients.35 From a clinical standpoint, TyG-FAI could serve as a valuable tool for refining risk models, perhaps identifying candidates for intensified therapies—such as advanced anti-inflammatory agents or metabolic modulators—beyond standard care. Incorporating it into routine imaging protocols might also aid in tailoring secondary prevention, potentially lowering residual risk in post-PCI settings by highlighting subclinical hotspots for intervention.

Additional considerations include comparisons with prior literature; for instance, while TyG-hsCRP has proven useful in broader community cohorts for forecasting cardiovascular incidents, in our focused group of advanced CAD patients, the addition of FAI appears to provide that extra layer of precision, echoing observations from trials like CRISP-CT where perivascular inflammation independently reclassifies risk.8,36,37 This local emphasis might stem from PVAT’s paracrine effects, directly influencing endothelial cells in ways that systemic markers overlook.38 However, established clinical risk models, such as the GRACE and SYNTAX scores, do not incorporate inflammation-related factors or insulin resistance indices. Therefore, the potential incremental prognostic value of integrating these novel composite biomarkers into conventional risk models warrants further investigation in future studies.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, it was conducted at a single center, which may limit the generalizability. Second, the requirement for both PCI and pre-procedural CCTA may have introduced selection bias, potentially excluding a broader CAD population. Third, although we adjusted for multiple confounders, residual confounding cannot be entirely ruled out due to the observational nature of the study. Fourth, the composite biomarkers lack external validation, and their clinical utility requires confirmation in independent cohorts. Finally, the use of a composite MACE endpoint combines endpoints with heterogeneous pathophysiological mechanisms, which may dilute the biological specificity of the observed associations. Future multicenter prospective studies with external validation, longitudinal biomarker measurements, and more comprehensive adjustment are warranted to confirm and extend our findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the composite biomarkers integrating insulin resistance and local or systemic inflammation, particularly TyG-FAI, provide modest incremental prognostic value for MACE in CAD patients. The predictive utility of these biomarkers varies across different glycemic statuses, highlighting the central role of local coronary inflammation in metabolic-driven atherosclerosis. These findings suggest that integrating these biomarkers with glycemic status may improve risk stratification and optimize secondary prevention strategies in CAD patients.
