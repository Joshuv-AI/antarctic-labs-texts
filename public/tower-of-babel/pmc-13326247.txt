Understanding compassion fatigue among child and adolescent psychotherapists: insights from a thematic analysis
Authors: Alireza Sabetifara, Zahra Alsadat Haji Seyed Taghiya Taghavi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCompassion fatigue is a critical professional challenge for child and adolescent psychotherapists. It stems from prolonged exposure to clients’ trauma and emotional suffering, resulting in the depletion of emotional and empathic resources.MethodsThis qualitative study adopted a phenomenological approach and used thematic analysis to examine data collected through semi-structured interviews.ResultsParticipants described compassion fatigue as a gradual erosion of empathy and vitality that extends beyond emotional exhaustion to impair their ethical and relational capacities.ConclusionThe findings underscore the necessity of systemic and culturally sensitive supports, such as clinical supervision, balanced workloads, and organizational awareness, to protect therapists’ emotional well-being and maintain compassionate engagement in psychotherapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-04718-9.

Introduction

In recent decades, the increasing emotional complexity and psychological difficulties among children and adolescents have markedly heightened the demand for specialized psychotherapeutic services. Child and adolescent psychotherapists are continuously exposed to their clients’ suffering, traumas, and internal conflicts. This ongoing exposure, combined with professional and organizational pressures, places them at risk of developing compassion fatigue [1–3].

In the study of compassion fatigue, a critical and indispensable point is the existence of a theoretical ambiguity within the diverse yet overlapping definitions prevalent in the research literature. Specifically, professional confusion persists regarding the precise application and distinction of four key terms: burnout, secondary traumatization, vicarious traumatization, and compassion fatigue. These concepts are frequently employed interchangeably within both professional and self-help literature as well as in clinical discussions [4].

This introduction begins with a comprehensive review of studies concerning compassion fatigue, which reveals that these closely related terms, all generally referring to different dimensions of the phenomenon, are frequently grouped together without clearly defined distinctions. Subsequently, we will examine the terminology employed in Figley’s original framework and more recent studies to clarify the specific terminology adopted for the present research.

Compassion fatigue occurs when therapists, like other helping professionals, face repeated exposure to clients’ distress and an inability to relieve their suffering, potentially leading to secondary traumatic stress [5]. Ironically, the most effective therapists are most vulnerable to this phenomenon. Those who have enormous capacity for feeling and expressing empathy tend to be more at risk of compassion stress [6].

Research has shown that moderate to high levels of secondary traumatic stress and burnout are prevalent among mental health professionals, including child therapists. Working with traumatized children can be particularly emotionally demanding, increasing the risk of burnout and compassion fatigue [7]. This phenomenon is associated with physical symptoms of stress, anxiety, sleep disturbances, intrusive thoughts or avoidance behaviors, heightened risk of substance use and internalizing disorders, reduced work engagement, and lower job satisfaction [8, 9]. For instance, a recent study found that 75% of psychiatrists working in Irish child and adolescent mental health services reported moderate to high levels of burnout, and 70% expressed a desire to change their profession [10]. Similarly, in a study among Greek mental health professionals, 11.8% reported high emotional exhaustion, 19.9% depersonalization, and 35.9% reduced personal accomplishment [11].

Furthermore, personal experiences such as bereavement, trauma history, physical fatigue, and individual vulnerabilities can play a crucial role in either exacerbating or mitigating compassion fatigue and compassion satisfaction [12]. Overall, the growing prevalence of burnout, compassion fatigue, and declining compassion satisfaction among healthcare professionals underscores the urgent need for effective preventive and supportive interventions [13].

Figley [1, 6] and Figley and Roop [14] employ the terms secondary traumatic stress, secondary stress, compassion stress, and residual compassion stress as synonyms. When these forms of stress become acute or chronic, they develop into secondary traumatic stress disorder, a term that is also used interchangeably with compassion fatigue (see Fig. 1).

The classification of compassion fatigue into subtypes, as seen in recent self-assessment tools, appears to have reduced ambiguity and provided greater clarity in describing and explaining this phenomenon. In one such classification, Halamová et al. [8] divide compassion fatigue into two subscales: secondary traumatic stress and burnout. Compassion fatigue of the secondary traumatic stress type involves elements specifically related to trauma and traumatic symptoms. Examples include a therapist’s first-hand experience of childhood trauma leading to vulnerability toward similar traumatic events, experiencing dreams with clients’ traumatic content, or facing intrusive thoughts while working with certain clients. Furthermore, the burnout type of compassion fatigue encompasses issues related to professional and occupational challenges, such as dissatisfaction with the professional helping role, an inability to achieve professional life goals, and a sense of weakness or exhaustion as a caregiver [8, 15, 16].

The present study adopts Figley’s definitions as the conceptual framework for examining these concepts, as he pioneered the introduction of compassion fatigue into psychological literature and catalyzed further academic discourse and elaboration on the subject.

Compassion fatigue, a relatively recent concept in clinical psychology, was first introduced by Joinson [17] and later elaborated by Figley [6]. This phenomenon encompasses the cumulative stress, strain, and emotional burden inherent in providing care for individuals facing medical or psychological challenges [18]. Within therapeutic contexts, it emerges as a state of emotional exhaustion triggered by sustained exposure to client suffering, often presenting through a constellation of symptoms including emotional numbness, diminished empathy, pervasive fatigue, and a decline in professional efficacy [19–21]. This concept represents a complex and abstract construct encompassing the emotional and behavioral consequences of witnessing another’s traumatic experience, a condition frequently characterized as the “cost of caring” in helping professions [22]. It is also recognized as a key theoretical framework for understanding stress responses at work among therapists and appears in their experiences as a significant psychological burden [23]. This condition can impair professional functioning, threaten therapists’ mental health, and even contribute to premature career withdrawal [14, 24, 25]. In the field of child and adolescent psychotherapy, compassion fatigue may be particularly intensified due to the complexity of family systems, exposure to children’s traumatic experiences of abuse and neglect, parental conflicts, and environmental stressors [26–28].

Despite the global significance of this phenomenon, existing research has predominantly focused on Western cultural contexts [29, 30], with limited attention to cross-cultural variations in the manifestation and management of compassion fatigue. Regional studies have further indicated that organizational and social factors play a crucial role in shaping the experience of compassion fatigue [31, 32]. This research gap underscores the need for cross-cultural exploration to enhance understanding of the individual, occupational, and contextual determinants of compassion fatigue in non-Western societies and to inform international policymaking in professional mental health [33, 34].

By extending Figley’s model [35], this research offers culturally grounded insights that facilitate international comparisons and directly inform the development of professional training, clinical supervision, and mental health policy. Given the impact of global crises such as COVID-19, which underscored the inherent vulnerabilities of practitioners [36], these findings offer timely implications for sustaining well-being and preventing emotional exhaustion within the global psychotherapy community.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a phenomenological approach to qualitative inquiry. Six procedural steps were undertaken to conduct the phenomenological research [37] (see Fig. 2).

Fig. 2Procedures for conducting phenomenological research

Procedures for conducting phenomenological research

The present inquiry aims to capture the essence of the lived experiences of child and adolescent psychologists. Therefore, in Step 1, it is determined that a phenomenological research design is the most appropriate approach for this study. In Step 2, the phenomenon of compassion fatigue was selected based on the van Manen’s premise that doing phenomenology involves becoming infected with a certain pathos that creates an openness to the world and a wondering attentiveness, which ultimately serves as the trigger for phenomenological inquiry [38]. The psychological phenomenological approach [39] was adopted for Step 3 because it offers systematic procedures for data analysis, ensuring a rigorous and structured movement through the research process [37]. Step 4 is dedicated to data collection, involving the development of a semi-structured interview guide (see Supplementary Appendix 1 for the interview guide). The questions were designed to address the structural conditions of the compassion fatigue experience, reflecting the specific contexts, situations, and backgrounds through which individuals encounter the phenomenon [39]. Given that this study was conducted in a non-Western context, and aligned with the fundamental goal of phenomenological research to reach a universal essence, Step 5 prioritized description over interpretation of the participants’ lived experiences. To generate themes, thematic analysis was employed [40]; furthermore, to demonstrate the interculturality of the extracted themes, a comparative mapping was performed against Figley’s process model of compassion fatigue (see phases 7 and 8 in Fig. 3). As noted in Step 4, the semi-structured interview questions were designed to reflect the contextual conditions of the participants’ understanding of the phenomenon. Consequently, in Step 6, while providing a textural description of their immediate experiences, we are essentially presenting a synthesis of structural and textural descriptions to approach the overall essence of the phenomenon.

The present research was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Islamic Azad University, Medical Sciences Branch (IR.IAU.TMU.R EC.1404.064).

Participants

Participants in this study were psychotherapists practicing in the field of child and adolescent mental health in Iran. Inclusion criteria were: a minimum of three years of professional experience in child and adolescent psychotherapy; active employment in mental health centers or psychology clinics in Iran; and no self-reported diagnosis of psychological disorders. Purposeful sampling with maximum variation (heterogeneity) [41] documents a diverse range of individuals who differ in specific characteristics but share the common experience under study [37]. In the present research, the criteria creating this variation among participants included gender, age, level of higher education, therapeutic approach, and years of clinical experience in psychotherapy. These criteria were selected as they were identified as the most significant factors likely to diversify the experience and description of the compassion fatigue phenomenon. To enhance diversity and engage psychotherapists who are less likely to participate in research, several recruitment strategies were used: (1) an invitation appended to an online survey regarding experiences of compassion fatigue, disseminated through professional social media accounts and psychology associations; (2) in-person invitations by the researcher to psychotherapists attending child psychotherapy workshops in the major cities of Tehran and Mashhad; and (3) posting announcements in professional online psychology groups.

No prior relationship existed between the researchers and the participants before the commencement of the study.

Data collection

Data were collected through semi-structured qualitative interviews, the guide for which is provided in Supplementary Appendix 1, conducted between October 2024 and August 2025. The interview topics covered psychotherapists’ lived experiences of compassion fatigue in their work with children and adolescents, the contributing factors to its development, and its impact on their professional functioning and personal lives.

At the outset of the study, participants were asked for permission to schedule interviews at a time and location of their choice (in person or by phone). Due to the research sensitivity of the topic, all 35 interviews were conducted in person and lasted between 45 and 90 min (M = 64 min). Informed consent for participation was obtained in writing before the start of each interview. Participants were reminded that they could withdraw from the study at any point without penalty.

As a token of appreciation, each participant received a gift card valued at 500,000 Tomans. All interviews were conducted in Persian. Only the researcher and the participant were present during the interviews. Interviews were audio-recorded and professionally transcribed, and each participant was assigned an anonymous identifier with the prefix T (for therapist).

Data collection continued until data saturation was achieved, a point reached when participants’ responses became repetitive and no new codes or themes emerged [40].

Data analysis

Following Step 5 of the systematic phenomenological research procedures, an inductive thematic analysis was conducted, as it provided an effective approach for identifying meaningful patterns and themes within the in-depth interview data.

MAXQDA software was utilized to ensure a rigorous thematic analysis and to prevent any significant data from being overlooked. In this process, the recorded interviews with all participants were first transcribed verbatim by the primary researcher. The transcriptions, serving as the units of analysis, were subsequently reviewed and cross-checked against the audio files by the research assistant to ensure accuracy and completeness prior to being imported into the software. Subsequently, through repeated reading of the transcripts, key statements containing meaningful information related to the experience of compassion fatigue were identified. These statements reflected aspects such as the stressful nature of therapeutic work, exposure to intense emotions, lingering effects of therapy sessions (e.g., “Working with children and their families feels like standing beside a raging river,” or “Hearing certain stories feels like carrying a weight on my shoulders.”).

Using the organizational features of MAXQDA, datasets were categorized into hierarchical layers (see Fig. 3). Moreover, process of analysis was not linear, Instead, it is more recursive process, where you move back and forth as needed, throughout the phases.

Significant statements, specific codes, sub-themes, and main themes

Figley’s process model of compassion fatigue

By synthesizing a specific set of variables, Figley [1] introduced a causal model that has become widely cited for predicting compassion fatigue. To grasp the conceptual depth of Figley’s model and to align it with the themes extracted from the present inquiry, this model was expanded into three interconnected forms (see Figs. 6 and 7, and Fig. 8), which are described in the Findings section.

In his model (see Fig. 4), exposure to suffering represents the direct, face-to-face engagement with the suffering client. empathic concern (or simply concern) represents the motivation to respond to the suffering individual. empathic ability is the therapist’s aptitude for perceiving the pain of others. Without this empathic ability, there would be no empathic response, and subsequently, no compassion stress; ultimately, compassion fatigue would not occur. Empathic response refers to the therapist’s effort to understand the client empathically by placing oneself within the client’s perspective and offering an empathic response aimed at alleviating the client’s distress. When such an empathic response is balanced (not excessive) and is accompanied by two coping strategies, namely a sense of satisfaction and detachment (see Fig. 6), it may prevent the progression of compassion stress and can even lead to the experience of compassion satisfaction [8]. A sense of satisfaction refers to the therapist’s level of fulfillment derived from their efforts to help the client, while detachment denotes the psychotherapist’s capacity to consciously separate from the client’s suffering and to avoid becoming immersed in the turbulent flow of the client’s emotions and thoughts. However, even in the presence of a balanced empathic response, if the therapist is unable to achieve conscious detachment from the client’s suffering, corresponding to the concept of attachment, or experiences dissatisfaction, for example as a result of perceived failure in alleviating the client’s distress, the risk of progression of compassion stress and the emergence of compassion fatigue remains significant (see Fig. 7).

Furthermore, when empathic response exceeds its optimal balance due to excess in any of the components of empathic ability, empathic concern, or exposure to suffering, compassion stress may still develop, even if the therapist attempts detachment or experiences a sense of satisfaction from helping clients. Over time, this process can subsequently lead to compassion fatigue (see Fig. 8). Residual compassion stress refers to the accumulation of negative emotional energy resulting from empathic responses to clients’ suffering. When compassion stress persists on its own, or when it co-occurs with factors such as prolonged exposure to suffering, traumatic memories, and other life demands, the risk of the development of compassion fatigue becomes substantial (see Figs. 7 and 8).

Findings

During the initial coding phase, a total of 72 codes were assigned to the salient and meaningful statements identified within the interview transcripts. Throughout the subsequent analytical stages, these codes were categorized into ten overarching themes. In the final phase, to achieve interculturality in understanding the findings and to demonstrate theoretical cohesion between the extracted data and existing conceptual frameworks, these themes were mapped onto the factors influencing Figley’s model of compassion fatigue (see Fig. 5). In the ensuing conceptual mapping, these themes were integrated into our proposed graphical model, which extends Figley’s original framework through a more streamlined conceptual organization (see Figs. 6 and 7, and Fig. 8). This recasting of Figley’s model was undertaken to incorporate his subsequent theoretical refinements and to resolve certain ambiguities inherent in his initial condensed graphical representation. By utilizing strategic color-coding and enhanced visuals, we aimed to provide a more lucid understanding of Figley’s factors and their relationship to the themes identified in the present inquiry. Notably, in Figs. 6 and 7, and 8, numerical labels are assigned exclusively to the causal factors driving compassion fatigue in therapists; meanwhile, the elements highlighted in blue represent potential contributors to this process, should they deviate from a state of balance. The color green denotes the therapist’s coping actions, which are performed consciously to either forestall the onset of compassion fatigue or mitigate the progression of its development. The color blue identifies the baseline or normative state of a factor that remains beneficial or even essential to the therapist during the clinical encounter. Nevertheless, should these elements converge with specific risk factors indicated in yellow, they may forfeit their constructive influence and instead exacerbate the developmental trajectory of compassion fatigue. The color yellow denotes risk factors that signal a warning for the onset of compassion fatigue, typically arising when a particular element reaches an excessive level or deviates from its normative range. This representation highlights the active participation of such factors in the development of the fatigue phenomenon. Should a risk factor become exceptionally extreme or intense, its impact may become so pervasive that it overrides the presence of normal baseline factors or even the mitigating effects of green coping actions, thereby inevitably leading to the manifestation of compassion fatigue. The color red signifies the actual occurrence of compassion fatigue and is utilized within these figures solely to denote the final manifestation of this phenomenon.

Exposure to suffering

Two main themes were identified in relation to exposure to suffering (see Fig. 8): ‘Repeated exposure to heavy emotional content’, and ‘Emotional consequences of work experience’.

Repeated exposure to heavy emotional content

Psychotherapists described the psychological pressure of continuous engagement with children’s and adolescents’ traumatic narratives: “The day starts with a client who has just learned about their parents’ divorce… and by noon I’m seeing a teenager who no longer dares to speak in class” (T10). Working with severe anxiety, depression, or trauma was particularly challenging: “Especially when you work with adolescents struggling with deep and complex emotional issues” (T12). Continuous exposure often led to emotional exhaustion: “This feeling especially shows up after hours of being immersed in clients’ challenging stories” (T14).

Three quotations indicate that repeated, daily exposure to clients’ suffering, particularly the suffering of children and adolescents who are more vulnerable and elicit empathic engagement more rapidly and intensely, can cause the item of exposure to suffering to move out of its balanced state. When exposure to suffering exceeds its optimal balance, shifting from blue as a normal factor in Figs. 6 and 7 to yellow as a risk factor in Fig. 8, the empathic response likewise moves out of balance, changing from blue in Figs. 6 and 7 to yellow in Fig. 8. Under these conditions, even in the presence of the psychotherapist’s efforts toward detachment or a sense of satisfaction derived from helping clients, compassion stress may develop and, as this process continues, may ultimately lead to compassion fatigue.

Emotional consequences of work experience

One significant emotional consequence of working with trauma and intense emotional experiences is a reduced capacity for personal interactions, particularly with family: “After a long and demanding day at work, when you get home, it feels as if you cannot listen to your family’s problems… my mind is entirely occupied with the faces and stories I encountered today” (T07). Some therapists also reported the persistence of images and sounds related to clients’ narratives: “Sometimes a child’s crying or their painful story lingers in my mind for hours, as if I cannot get rid of it” (T09).

Clients’ gazes or questions were also reported as sources of increased psychological burden: “When a teenager asks you a question with that look full of despair, you cannot forget it even after the session” (T02). Reduced social interaction after work was also noted as a common consequence: “After a day at work, all I want is to go somewhere alone and not talk to anyone” (T13).

Four quotations reflecting this theme indicate that exposure itself to the suffering and trauma of young clients, for example their tearful, hopeless, and helpless expressions, can reduce emotional capacity. When the therapist lacks conscious awareness of their own condition and fails to create distance both objectively, such as by reducing the number of therapy sessions, and subjectively, through psychological distancing, this may result in an imbalanced empathic response and increase the risk of developing compassion fatigue (see Fig. 8).

Concern

One main theme was identified in relation to concern (see Fig. 8): ‘Pressure stemming from professional responsibility’.

Pressure stemming from professional responsibility

Psychotherapists reported that a deep sense of responsibility toward their clients, particularly children and adolescents, often contributed to additional psychological strain: “Every session feels like carrying a heavy burden, because you know this child relies on you to feel better” (T03). Concern for clients’ future was also identified as a source of psychological pressure: “At night, I think about what will happen to this child later and whether I really managed to help them” (T11).

Some therapists also described feelings of futility in their efforts, especially when clients’ progress was slow or dependent on factors beyond their control: “Sometimes you feel that no matter how hard you try, you’re not getting anywhere, because the children’s family problems are so complex” (T08).

The quotations illustrate that when a psychologist lacks self-awareness regarding the fact that they are not responsible for the client’s suffering and does not recognize where their professional responsibilities end and the client’s responsibilities begin, they may experience empathic concern in an excessive manner. This excess can lead to an imbalanced empathic response (see Fig. 8). Sense of dissatisfaction arising from perceived failure in helping the client (see Factor 6 in Fig. 7) are also closely associated with excessive empathic concern and can further contribute to the development of compassion stress.

Empathic ability

Two main themes were identified in relation to empathic ability (see Fig. 8): ‘The constant demand for full presence and engagement’, and ‘Emotional transmission and reflection from clients’.

The constant demand for full presence and engagement

Excessive emotional expenditure, particularly in creating a safe space for clients, was described as one of the main challenges of psychotherapists’ work: “To make the child feel safe, you have to give them your full attention, and that takes a lot of energy” (T16). The need for high concentration during sessions also imposed considerable pressure: “You can’t let your attention wander for even a moment, because every word or movement of the child can carry meaning” (T20).

Many therapists reported suppressing their own emotional reactions during sessions to maintain the therapeutic environment: “When a child is talking about a very painful experience, you can’t cry, you have to control yourself and just listen” (T18). This suppression, especially in sessions requiring empathy with multiple clients (e.g., the child and their parents), demanded even greater emotional energy.

If empathic ability is conceptualized as the client’s source of emotional nourishment that enables the therapist’s effective and healing presence within the therapy session, the quotations indicate that its excessive consumption, aimed at providing a high level of attention and full presence during sessions, as well as the occasional overregulation of personal emotions and behaviors due to the sensitive nature of working with children and adolescents, can disrupt the balance of the empathic response (see Fig. 8).

Emotional transmission and reflection from clients

Many therapists described experiencing their clients’ emotions reflected within themselves, so that clients’ feelings were mirrored in their minds and bodies. This process was especially intense when working with traumatized children who expressed strong or nonverbal emotions: “A child can fill the entire session with heavy emotions through their gaze or behavior, and you cannot escape it” (T32). The transfer of emotional burden from clients to therapists also created considerable psychological strain: “Sometimes I feel like I take a client’s anxiety or anger home with me” (T30).

From another perspective on this source of emotional nourishment, namely empathic ability, possessing a high level of empathic capacity, which is often an inherent disposition of the therapist, may lead to the transfer of negative emotional load from the client to the therapist. This transfer can, in turn, disrupt the balance of the empathic response (see Fig. 8).

Empathic response

One main theme was identified in relation to empathic response (see Fig. 8): ‘Emotional and mental exhaustion’.

Emotional and mental exhaustion

Emotional involvement, particularly in challenging sessions, often led to gradual and imperceptible burnout: “Every day, a part of your energy is depleted due to accompanying these pains and sufferings” (T31). Many therapists reported that this strain drained their emotional and cognitive resources: “After several intense sessions, you feel like there is nothing left of yourself” (T33).

Additionally, the high caseload was identified as a factor contributing to occupational burnout: “With this number of clients, even if you wanted to, you cannot find time for yourself to restore your mental health” (T12).

The high caseload referenced in the quotations may result from the imbalance of Factors 1, 2, and 3 in Figs. 6 and 7, and Fig. 8, whereby these factors shift from their balanced state, represented in blue in Figs. 6 and 7, to a risk state, represented in yellow in Fig. 8. This imbalance can lead to an imbalanced empathic response (Factor 4 in Fig. 8). In addition, the depletion of energy and even the experience of burnout described in the quotations may stem from an empathic response that has moved beyond its optimal balance.

Attachment (or positive form of detachment in the Figley’s model)

One main theme was identified in relation to attachment (see Fig. 7): ‘Work encroachment on personal life’.

Work encroachment on personal life

Duties at work and emotional involvement with clients’ distress often extended into therapists’ personal lives: “Obtaining a simple report from a school sometimes requires several phone calls, emails, and in-person follow-ups. These tasks usually happen during the hours that are supposed to be my rest time” (T05). Such responsibilities outside therapy sessions disrupted the work–life balance: “Even when I’m at home, my mind is still in the sessions because I have to coordinate with parents or the school” (T04).

Mental preoccupation after sessions was also reported as a common challenge: “After a heavy session, you can’t just press an off button and stop thinking about the client” (T22).

The quotations indicate that if a therapist is unsuccessful in achieving conscious detachment from clients’ suffering and, more broadly, from their professional identity, they may still move toward the risk factor of compassion stress, even when their empathic response remains balanced (the blue Factor 4 in Fig. 7). If this trajectory is accompanied by sense of dissatisfaction with oneself and one’s professional and personal functioning (Factor 6 in Fig. 7), the pace of movement toward this high-risk outcome is substantially accelerated.

Residual compassion stress

One main theme was identified in relation to residual compassion stress (see Figs. 7 and 8): ‘Physical strains and slow progress’.

Physical strains and slow progress

Working with children and adolescents, particularly in long-term cases, was often accompanied by slow therapeutic progress, which imposed considerable psychological and physical strain on therapists: “Some cases have been prolonged, and changes occur very slowly” (T04). In another example: “When you work with a child for months and still see no significant progress, it feels like you’re stuck in a swamp” (T09).

Fatigue resulting from prolonged client silence was also reported as a common challenge: “Some children remain silent for hours and you just have to wait, making this waiting truly exhausting” (T23).

Through the gradual and negative progression of the preceding risk factors, namely the first six factors in Fig. 7 and the first four factors in Fig. 8, compassion stress or residual compassion stress emerges in the therapist. The quotations associated with this theme may represent any of the earlier risk factors, for example feelings of failure and dissatisfaction, which clearly correspond to Factor 6 in Fig. 7. Notably, based on the quotations, a distinctive feature of this factor, residual compassion stress, is the experience of a psychological state characterized by defeat and hopelessness on the part of the psychotherapist.

Prolonged exposure to suffering

One main theme was identified in relation to prolonged exposure to suffering (see Figs. 7 and 8): ‘Work pressure and session density’.

Work pressure and session density

Therapists reported that intensive schedules and back-to-back sessions imposed considerable emotional and cognitive strain, often resulting in energy depletion over the course of the day: “By midday, you feel like you have nothing left to give to your clients” (T02).

Continuous workload and the relentless nature of the work were also identified as sources of occupational burnout: “Some days, it feels like you’re stuck in an endless marathon with no breaks” (T32).

Given the conceptual proximity between prolonged exposure to suffering and attachment, it might be argued that these two factors could have been integrated under a broader, more inclusive factor. However, in line with Figley’s judgment and the treatment of this factor as independent within Figley’s model, quotations related to heavy workload and densely scheduled therapy sessions were categorized under this factor. In combination with compassion stress, this factor may contribute to the development of compassion fatigue.

Other life demands

One main theme was identified in relation to other life demands (see Figs. 7 and 8): ‘Personal, familial, and economic strains’.

Personal, familial, and economic strains

Therapists frequently described how personal responsibilities and life pressures outside their professional roles compounded their sense of fatigue and emotional depletion. Balancing therapeutic duties with financial concerns, family obligations, or the demands of daily life often intensified their overall stress. For instance, some therapists noted that financial instability or the need to maintain multiple jobs limited their capacity for rest and self-care: “When I finish my sessions, I still have to think about how to cover my expenses this month” (T15). Others highlighted pressures within the family such as caregiving responsibilities or limited time with their children: “Sometimes I feel guilty because after listening to other children all day, I have no energy left for my own” (T09). These intersecting personal and economic challenges not only reduced their emotional resilience but also blurred the boundary between personal life and professional demands, reinforcing the cumulative nature of compassion fatigue.

A lack of organizational support further exacerbated these pressures: “When the clinic management does not provide adequate scheduling or a suitable work environment, it feels like you have to carry the entire burden alone” (T34).

What emerges from the quotations extracted from the interviews and the descriptions associated with this theme indicates its pervasive role in influencing other risk factors that lead to compassion fatigue. Undoubtedly, no psychologist can claim that personal commitments, financial obligations, and issues in personal life have no impact on professional identity. Accordingly, when this factor exceeds the level of a natural life demand and becomes a risk factor, it appears to function more as a catalyst. When combined with any of the risk factors presented in Figs. 7 and 8, it may accelerate the process leading to compassion fatigue.

Discussion

Comparison of the present findings with previous studies indicates that many of the themes identified in the experiences of child and adolescent therapists align with those reported in the existing literature on compassion fatigue. However, certain specific dimensions emerged in this study that have received less attention in prior research and can be attributed to local characteristics, the unique professional context of child and adolescent psychotherapy, and the sociocultural environment of the country.

From an alignment perspective, the theme of repeated exposure to heavy emotional content closely corresponds with Figley’s findings [1, 6]. In his model of compassion fatigue, Figley emphasizes that continual exposure to patients’ traumatic narratives is a primary driver of compassion stress and emotional exhaustion. Thompson, Amatea [42], in their study “Personal and contextual predictors of mental health counselors’ compassion fatigue”, highlight the volume, nature, and intensity of client problems as workplace and organizational factors, which directly relate to codes reflecting persistent engagement with children’s and adolescents’ heavy emotional narratives, exposure to clients’ distressing stories, and emotionally charged details. Similarly, Ghasemi, Abbasi Sooreshjani [43] note that intense emotional exposure can undermine empathy capacity and increase the risk of compassion fatigue. The present study corroborates and extends these relationships within the context of child and adolescent psychotherapy.

Regarding the themes of emotional consequences of work experience and work encroachment on personal life, the findings of this study align with previous research such as Robins, Meltzer [27] and Keidel [21]. These studies have demonstrated that compassion fatigue manifests not only as reduced energy and diminished empathy in the workplace but also infiltrates personal life boundaries, leading to disruptions in family and social relationships. The present data similarly reveal this pattern among child and adolescent therapists, indicating that distinguishing between “personal life” and “professional life” is often difficult, if not impossible, for this group.

The themes of the constant demand for full presence, engagement, and emotional transmission and reflection from clients is consistent with studies such as Huggard [20] and Sabo [28]. These studies also emphasize that the intensity of emotional engagement with clients and difficulty in maintaining emotional boundaries are key factors in the process of emotional exhaustion. However, the present research indicates that this experience takes on distinct dimensions in the context of child and adolescent therapy, as therapists must engage not only with the child but also with parents and the family system, thereby amplifying the emotional demands.

Regarding the themes of pressure stemming from professional responsibility and emotional and mental exhaustion, the findings of the present study align with Lynch and Lobo [44], who focused on the stress of accountability, as well as with Maslach and Leiter [45] and more recent studies such as Stevens and Stevens and Al-Abbadey [29], which identify occupational burnout and compassion fatigue as consequences of a heavy sense of responsibility toward clients. However, a key distinction in the present research is that the sense of responsibility among child and adolescent therapists extends not only to the improvement of clients’ conditions but, in many cases, also to the future of the family and parenting relationships, imposing a uniquely intense psychological burden.

The themes related to physical strain, session density, and organizational pressures are also consistent with findings from studies such as Wu and Lu [46], McHolm [47] and Asgharipour, Nouri Siahdasht [32]. These studies have shown that high workloads, lack of organizational support, and unfavorable working conditions significantly increase the risk of compassion fatigue and occupational burnout. Similarly, Yılkan [48] specifically highlights aspects of environmental and organizational pressures, which align with the codes in the present study. The authors note pressures arising from high client or stakeholder expectations, directly corresponding to the codes related to constraints imposed by family expectations and the high demands of clients/families. In the present research, participants’ emphasis on session density, limited rest opportunities, and inadequate organizational support mechanisms further corroborates this alignment.

Despite these consistencies, some meaningful differences were observed. First, previous studies primarily focused on nurses, physicians, or therapists who work with adults, whereas the findings of the present study indicate that child and adolescent therapists face more complex layers of emotional stress. This complexity arises because, in addition to working with children, they are inevitably engaged with parents, family conflicts, and the broader social context surrounding the child. Second, cultural factors have been less emphasized in most prior research, whereas participants in this study repeatedly highlighted the influence of social and cultural expectations, including the expectation for therapists to assume a “savior” role, an element that may exacerbate compassion fatigue within the Iranian context.

Overall, the comparison of the present study’s findings with the existing literature on compassion fatigue and stress experienced by therapists indicates that, while many established components of compassion fatigue are confirmed, this study also illuminates specific dimensions that have been explored less frequently. Notably, these include the amplified pressures resulting from working simultaneously with children and their families, the profound intrusion of work into therapists’ personal lives, and the influence of cultural expectations on the intensity of compassion fatigue experiences.

Strengths, limitations and suggestions for future research

Strengths

This study provides a rare, in-depth exploration of compassion fatigue among child and adolescent psychotherapists. Its qualitative design and use of thematic analysis enabled a deep understanding of therapists’ lived experiences, capturing the nuanced emotional and relational dynamics that contribute to compassion fatigue. By situating these experiences within personal, occupational, and organizational contexts, the study offers insights to inform multi-level interventions that support therapists’ resilience and sustain compassionate care.

Limitations

While the phenomenological inquiry and thematic analysis employed in this study facilitate a profound exploration of the lived experiences of psychotherapists, they inherently result in a relatively constrained sample size, which may constrain the extent to which findings can be applied to broader populations. In addition, thematic analysis, although effective in uncovering nuanced meanings, is not entirely immune to researcher interpretation. Efforts were made to enhance credibility through data triangulation, participant feedback, and supervisory consultation; however, the complete elimination of interpretive bias is not feasible.

Suggestions for future research

While this study offered valuable insights into compassion fatigue among child and adolescent psychotherapists, several directions for future research are proposed:

Methodological diversity

Future studies should employ mixed-methods designs that integrate the depth of qualitative inquiry with the generalizability of quantitative approaches. Intervention-based research, such as mindfulness, resilience training, and peer-support programs, can further evaluate strategies to mitigate compassion fatigue and strengthen therapists’ well-being.

Expanding the study population

Research involving diverse professionals, including adult psychotherapists, psychiatrists, social workers, and psychiatric nurses, would enable cross-contextual comparisons. Cross-cultural investigations could also clarify how cultural and social factors influence the development and expression of compassion fatigue.

Longitudinal perspectives

Longitudinal studies are needed to trace the evolution of compassion fatigue across different career stages and to examine recovery processes following emotional exhaustion. Such research would provide a deeper understanding of resilience and long-term professional sustainability among therapists.

Conclusions

Compassion fatigue among child and adolescent psychotherapists emerges as a multidimensional and systemic phenomenon shaped by the interplay of personal vulnerability, occupational strain, and sociocultural expectations. It is manifested not merely as emotional exhaustion but as a gradual erosion of empathy, blurred boundaries between professional and personal life, and a deep sense of inner depletion. The findings indicate that compassion fatigue is embedded within the relational and cultural fabric of psychotherapeutic work rather than being a purely individual reaction to clients’ suffering. Addressing this complex challenge requires a shift from individual-based coping strategies toward systemic and organizational reforms that prioritize therapists’ well-being. Institutionalized clinical supervision, balanced workloads, and culturally sensitive support structures are essential to preserving emotional integrity and ensuring sustainable therapeutic engagement. Ultimately, sustaining empathy, the cornerstone of effective psychotherapy, depends on protecting its very source: the therapist’s own inner well-being, which forms the foundation of truly compassionate and ethical care.
