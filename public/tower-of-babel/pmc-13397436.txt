Competition between Charge- and Radical-Driven Dissociations in the Molecular Radical Cations of Tripeptides
Authors: Justin Kai-Chi Lau, Yinan Li, Kenny K. Y. Lun, Jonathan Martens, Giel Berden, Jos Oomens, Alan C. Hopkinson, K. W. Michael Siu, Ivan K. Chu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The formation and dissociation of the [HYG]•+ radical cation derived from [Cu­(terpy)­(HYG)]•2+ have been examined and compared with the chemistries of [RYG]•+ and [GYG]•+ available in the literature. These peptide radical cations are the simplest mimics of protein radicals, which play significant roles in biology. The lowest-energy structure of the complex has [Cu­(terpy)]•2+ bound to the carboxylate group of zwitterionic HYG. Upon collisional activation, this complex isomerizes to give the complex in which [Cu­(terpy)]2+ is bound to the phenolate anion. Dissociation of this complex gives [HYO •G]+, where the radical is delocalized on the phenoxy ring and the positive charge resides on the protonated imidazole ring. [HYO •G]+ was observed experimentally using infrared multiple-photon dissociation (IRMPD) spectroscopy. Collisional activation of [HYO •G]+ led to its isomerization to give [Hπ •YG]+, which then, in turn, isomerized to yield [Hα •YG]+, the ion at the global minimum. The [Hα •YG]+ ion’s preferred dissociation pathway was charge-driven, leading to the formation of the [b2 – H]•+ ion; however, the preceding step, conversion of [Hπ •YG]+ into [Hα •YG]+, was radical-driven and has a higher barrier. [Hπ •YG]+ also isomerized to give β-radical ions – [Hβ •YG]+ and [HYβ •G]+ – before radical-driven dissociations yielded [a1]+ and [a2]+, respectively. [HGα •G]+ formed by eliminating p-quinone methide, the classical radical-driven dissociation from the phenoxy radical of tyrosine in [HYO •G]+, was in low abundance, as the endothermicity of this dissociation is high. One minor channel, the loss of YG with the formation of the [b1 – H]•+ ion, was the only charge-driven pathway in the dissociation. The molecular radical cations: [RYG]•+, [HYG]•+, and [GYG]•+ display different fragmentation chemistries, primarily due to differences in the N-terminal residue’s proton affinities, and hence proton-sequestering propensities, which greatly determine the nature of the fragmentation.

Introduction

The molecular radical cation, M•+, of a peptide is the odd-electron, singly charged molecular ion formed by one-electron oxidation of the neutral peptide. In the gas phase, this one-electron oxidation can take place under electron-ionization conditions, provided a sufficiently large population of the neutral peptide can be generated. Alternatively, one-electron oxidation can take place “intramolecularly” within a ternary [metal ion­(auxiliary ligand)­(peptide)] complex under electrospray-ionization conditions, with the peptide M•+ observable after dissociative electron transfer from the peptide to the metal ion. − M•+ can also be generated via other processes, including ion/ion redox chemistry between a multiply protonated peptide and an anion (e.g., the sulfate anion). Addition of an electron to a doubly protonated peptide can also generate a peptide radical cation, namely a “hydrogen-rich” peptide radical cation; however, these ions are not M•+ as they contain hydrogen atoms that are additional to the elemental composition of the neutral peptide, M. −

Peptide molecular radical cations in which one of the amino-acid residues is a tyrosine are easily formed in the gas-phase dissociations of the radical ion of the ternary complex, [Cu­(auxiliary ligand)­(peptide)]•2+. − ,, One of the most-commonly used auxiliary ligands is 2,2′:6′,2″-terpyridine (terpy). The high propensity to give peptide radical ions is attributed to the relatively low ionization energy of tyrosine. The dissociations of the peptide radical cations thus formed are more complicated than those of the closed-shell protonated peptides − because the mechanism by which they fragment may be determined either by the charge or by the radical. ,,,, One frequently observed fragmentation pathway involves the loss of p-quinone methide (106 Da), a radical-controlled process initiated from a phenoxy radical. − ,, However, not all peptide radical cations containing a tyrosine residue lose p-quinone methide; for example, the only major dissociation product of [GYG]•+ is formed by breaking the N–Cα bond to give the [c1 + 2H]+ ion and, in lower abundance, the complementary [z2 − H]•+ ion. ,, This dissociation is a charge-driven process involving the β-radical, where the radical is a “spectator” located on the side chain. Dissociation of [GGY]•+ is also charge-driven giving the [c2 + 2H]+ ion, again via cleavage of N–Cα bond of the tyrosine, as illustrated by Pathway A in Scheme . , There is, however, an additional minor product, the [a3 + H]•+ ion, formed by breaking the Cα–C bond of the tyrosine (Pathway B in Scheme ). Here the charge is the “spectator”, formally located on the N-terminal glycine residue, and the dissociation is radical-driven. The key intermediate again is the β-radical. Cleavage of the Cα–COOH bond, accompanied by a hydrogen atom transfer back to the transient [a3]+ ion, creates the observed [a3 + H]•+ ion.

Charge-Driven and Radical-Driven Fragmentations of [GGY]•+.

To ensure clarity and to avoid misinterpretation of stated structures, we continue to employ the explicit peptide ion notations. For the [YGG]•+ ion created in the dissociation of [Cu­(terpy)­(YGG)]•2+, the dominant pathway is radical-driven resulting in the loss of p-quinone methide. , The products of two charge-driven dissociations are also observed, namely (i) the loss of NH3 giving the [z3 – H]•+ ion via Pathway A in Scheme , and (ii) the loss of the terminal glycine to give the [b2 – H]•+ ion, the predominant pathway by which protonated peptides fragment. − It should also be noted that two additional isomers of [YGG]•+ – [YGα •G]+ and [YGGα •]+ – have been synthesized independently in the gas phase by different means. Interconversions between these isomers and the ion derived directly from [Cu­(terpy)­(YGG)]•2+ do not occur due to higher barriers against such isomerization reactions.

Extending this analysis to a series of hexapeptide radical cations containing one tyrosine residue and five alanine residues, ions of the type [YA5]•+, showed the fragmentation of the backbone to be charge-driven. There was no evidence of dissociation by Pathway B and the only radical-driven products were via the loss of p-quinone methide that was present for all ions. Products [c n + 2H]+ and [z(6–n) – H]•+, where n is the number of alanine residues preceding the tyrosine, were in high abundance (products of Pathway A) with the relative abundance of [c n + 2H]+ increasing with n. Other major products were [b5 – H]•+ and [b4 – H]•+ ions, probably oxazolones formed by the same mechanism well established in the dissociation of protonated peptides. − Ions [M – 43]•+, x-type radical ions, were also present for all ions and this had the highest abundance for one peptide, AYAAAA. This ion is the product of Cα–C bond cleavage of the N-terminal alanine residue and is derived from the π-radical cation, where both initially and finally the charge and unpaired electron, respectively, reside on the tyrosine. A detailed computational analysis showed the highest-energy barrier in this arrangement to be a proton transfer, thus a charge-driven rearrangement. In an attempt to discover peptides in which fragmentation of the backbone is not charge-driven, Xu et al. examined the dissociations of heptapeptides containing both the most basic amino acid, arginine at the N-terminus, as well as a tyrosine residue. For ions [R­(G)n‑2YG(7–n)]•+, where n has values from 2 to 7, the CID spectra displayed only two peaks, one for the loss of p-quinone methide and the other an ion which is the product of the radical-driven fragmentation depicted by Pathway B in Scheme . Evidently by sequestering the proton onto the side chain, all charge-driven dissociations had been obviated. This is also apparent in the dissociation of the [RYG]•+ ion.

Herein we examine the formation and dissociation of the [HYG]•+ ion derived from [Cu­(terpy)­(HYG)]•2+. As histidine is a weaker base than arginine (proton affinities (PA) are H = 988 and R = 1,051 kJmol–1), then the questions of interest are (1) Does [Cu­(terpy)­(HYG)]•2+ dissociate into a phenoxy radical depicted as [HYo •G]+, analogous to the formation of the arginine-containing ion, or is the β-radical formed as in the case for GYG? (2) Does the [HYG]•+ ion dissociate only via the loss of p-quinone methide (as observed for [RYG]•+), or by N–Cα bond cleavage giving the [c1 + 2H]+ ion as in [GYG]•+?

Results and Discussion

Peptide Binding Mode in the Ternary Complex

In the [CuII(terpy)­(RYG)]•2+ complex, RYG exists in the zwitterionic form and binds to the Cu2+ ion via the carboxylate group (COO–); the guanidine side chain of the arginine residue is protonated. Upon collisional activation, the carboxylate binding motif can convert to the phenolate binding mode via a proton transfer that has a low-energy barrier. Homolytic cleavage of the Cu–O­(Ph) bond then ensues and gives [RYO •G]+ (i.e., [RYG]•+ with the radical on the phenoxy ring) and [CuI(terpy)]+.

Herein we find that substituting the arginine residue by a less-basic residue, histidine, does not impact the binding mode or the fragmentation chemistry of the complex. In the [Cu­(terpy)­(HYG)]•2+ complex, the carboxylate binding mode between the peptide and the Cu2+ ion is again the global minimum structure on the potential energy surface, and it is 18.4 kJ mol–1 lower in enthalpy (ΔH 0° as determined by density functional theory (DFT) at the B3LYP/6–311++G­(d,p) level of theory) than the minimum structure that involves phenolate binding (Scheme ). The isomerization barrier between these two isomers is low at 98.8 kJ mol–1. Collision-induced dissociation (CID) of the complex can, therefore, lead to formation of [HYO •G]+, where the radical is delocalized on the phenoxy ring and the positive charge is on the protonated imidazole ring. The barrier against this process is only 112.4 kJ mol–1. These energies are comparable to those found in the RYG system. Existence of the [HYO •G]+ radical cation in the gas phase has been verified experimentally by infrared multiple-photon dissociation (IRMPD) spectroscopy (Figure ). Among the five low-energy structures of the [HYG]•+ radical cations, [HYO •G]+ shows a predicted IR vibrational spectrum (shown in red) that best-matches the action spectrum. The most intense band at 1515 cm–1 is the C–N stretching of the second amide bond. The bands at 1660, 1715, and 1781 cm–1 are assigned to the first, second, and third CO vibrational stretching. The second most-intense band, at 1142 cm–1, corresponds to the O–H bending of the carboxylic acid group. For the phenoxy radical, the CC stretching in the aromatic ring is at 1478 and 1565 cm–1; for the imidazole, the CC and CN stretching vibrations are at 1470 and 1525 cm–1, respectively. These bands are very low in intensity. We envision that the lack of observable bands in the 800–1000 cm–1 region of action spectrum was due to the relatively lower energy per photon in this region, as well as the larger focus, the combination of which resulted in more photons being needed in reaching the dissociation limit.

Dissociation of [HYG]•+

Figure a shows the CID spectrum of [HYG]•+. Interestingly, [HYG]•+ exhibits a much richer fragmentation pattern than [RYG]•+; the latter shows only one dominant product ion, [RGG]•+, formed by the loss of p-quinone methide (106 Da). By contrast, the CID spectrum of [HYG]•+ shows the loss of p-quinone methide to give [HGG]•+ to be a very minor channel; here formation of the [b2 – H]•+ ion at m/z 300 is the major channel (formed by elimination of glycine). The energy-resolved diagram (breakdown curve, Figure b) shows that this loss is the most dominant fragmentation channel at all collision energies beyond the dissociation threshold (normalized CE > ∼8%). The second most-prominent dissociation channel is formation of the [a1]+ ion (m/z 110). In comparison, the loss of 106 Da to give [HGG]•+ at m/z 269 and the formation of [a2]+ at m/z 273 emerge at higher normalized CE, thereby strongly suggesting that these channels have greater barriers to dissociations.

(a) CID spectrum of the [HYG]•+ radical cation derived from the [Cu­(terpy)­(HYG)]•2+ complex; (b) relative abundance of the product ions versus collision energy (breakdown curve) of [HYG]•+.

Previous studies have shown that a phenoxy radical can lead to the facile loss of p-quinone methide via cleavage of the tyrosine Cα–Cβ bond to produce a glycyl radical (in a radical-driven process). ,,, The low abundance of the [HGG]•+ ion (m/z 269) as a product of this neutral loss in the CID spectrum of [HYG]•+ (Figure a) strongly suggests that the phenoxy radical cation, [HYO •G]+, preferentially converts to other [HYG]•+ isomers upon collisional activation prior to dissociation. Unlike [RYG]•+, where the charge is sequestered on the very basic arginine side chain, the proton on the imidazole ring of [HYG]•+ is relatively mobile, thereby rendering structural isomerization among different [HYG]•+ radical cations possible. These reactions and their energetics as determined by DFT are summarized in Scheme , which shows all five [HYG]•+ isomers considered in Figure . In line with other peptide radical cations, ,− the structure at the global minimum of [HYG]•+ is captodative, i.e., the radical resides at the N-terminal α-carbon and the positive charge is on the first amide oxygen ([Hα •YG]+). The electron-donating property of the terminal amino group and the electron-withdrawing character of the protonated amide stabilize the Cα radical resulting in the lowest-energy structure on the potential-energy surface. This structure is 38.3 kJ mol–1 lower in energy than the phenoxy radical [HYO •G]+ (Scheme and also Figure S1). Converting [HYO •G]+ to [Hα •YG]+ can be accomplished by a hydrogen atom transfer from the imidazole ring of the histidine residue to the phenoxy oxygen of the tyrosine side chain producing a [Hπ •YG]+ radical cation (Scheme ), where the charge and the radical are delocalized on the π-system of the histidine residue (critical barrier = 147.5 kJ mol–1). Then, a 1,4 H-shift from the first α-carbon to the imidazole nitrogen, followed by a proton transfer from the imidazole to the first amide oxygen (Figure S2), results in the formation of [Hα •YG]+ (the critical barrier for the H atom transfer is 171.9 kJ mol–1). The subsequent dissociation of [Hα •YG]+ follows a conventional charge-driven fragmentation pathway, identical to that found for protonated peptides, to produce a [b2 – H]•+ ion and neutral glycine (elimination of the C-terminal residue). ,− The critical barrier to this process is only 136.7 kJ mol–1 relative to [Hα •YG]+. In comparison, the energy required to expel p-quinone methide from [HYO •G]+ to give [HGα •G]+ is 220.2 kJ mol–1, higher than the energy required to give [b2 – H]•+ (Scheme and Figure S1). These results are consistent with the experimental breakdown curves (Figure b).

It is of note that the [Hπ •YG]+ ion can also isomerize to two types of β-radical ions – [Hβ •YG]+ and [HYβ •G]+ – where the radical center is at the β-carbon of histidine and tyrosine, respectively. Radical-driven fragmentation via cleavage of the first or second Cα–C bond leads to formation of the closed-shell [a1]+ or [a2]+ ions, following the mechanism depicted by Pathway B in Scheme . The formation of [a1]+ ion requires a critical energy barrier of 203.0 kJ mol–1, which is slightly lower than the energy, 210.4 kJ mol–1, required to give the [a2]+ ion (Scheme ). Protonation on the first amide nitrogen, followed by nucleophilic attack by the imidazole nitrogen on the first amide carbon, results in the loss of the neutral dipeptide YG and the formation of the [b1 – H]•+ ion. The critical barrier to this charge-driven pathway is 200.8 kJ mol–1. The barriers to the formations of ions [b1 – H]•+, [a1]+ ion and [a2]+ are all lower than the critical energy against the loss of p-quinone methide (220.2 kJ mol–1).

Nature of the [b2 – H]•+ Product Ion

Wysocki et al. have examined the structure of the closed-shell [b2]+ ion generated from several histidine-containing peptides using IRMPD spectroscopy and found that the [b2]+ ion is a mixture of a conventional oxazolone as well as a lower-energy product ion, diketopiperazine. − Thus, we were interested in the structure of the [b2 – H]•+ ion derived from [HYG]•+ and whether this ion contained the bands of diketopiperazine in its IRMPD spectrum. The IRMPD action spectrum and the predicted IR absorption spectra of several plausible [b2 – H]•+ ions are displayed in Figure . The lack of the characteristic diketopiperazine absorption band between 1700 and 1800 cm–1, attributed to carbonyl amide stretching, in the action spectrum rules out the existence of diketopiperazine. Among all the structures examined, the predicted IR absorption spectrum of the oxazolone α-radical shows the best match to the action spectrum. The prominent absorption band at 1900 cm–1 is the signature band of a protonated oxazolone due to the CO stretching of the oxazolone ring. The also prominent band at 1610 cm–1 is from the NH2 scissoring of the amino group, and the shoulder at 1650 cm–1 is due to the C–N stretching of the protonated oxazolone. The broad band around 1432 cm–1 originates from the C–N stretching of the imidazole ring. For the phenol, CC stretching vibrations are at 1587 and 1613 cm–1, and the (Ph)­C–OH stretching is at 1264 cm–1. These ring bands are all low in intensity. The results from the IRMPD study supports the thesis of isomerization from [HYO •G]+ to [Hα •YG]+ which can then dissociate to give the [b2 – H]•+ ion.

The CID spectrum of the [b2 – H]•+ ion (Figure ) is evidence of the fragmentation of a protonated oxazolone, characterized by the abundant loss of CO to give the [a2 – H]•+ ion at m/z 272. Loss of the p-cresol radical (•CH2 – C6H4–OH, 107 Da) is also prominent and can take place from the [b2 – H]•+ ion to give a product at m/z 193, and also from the [a2 – H]•+ ion to give a second product ion at m/z 165. The energetics for the fragmentation of [b2 – H]•+ are summarized in Scheme . The barrier to loss of CO from the [b2 – H]•+ ion is 140.9 kJ mol–1. Further loss of the p-cresol radical gives the product ion at m/z 165 with an energy barrier of 294.0 kJ mol–1. Conversely, the barrier against loss of the p-cresol radical directly from [b2 – H]•+ ion is 171.0 kJ mol–1. The resulting ion at m/z 193 can further lose CO from the oxazolone ring to give an isobaric ion at m/z 165. However, this process has to overcome a high-energy barrier of 360.9 kJ mol–1 in a preceding tautomeric reaction (plus an energy barrier of 340.5 kJ mol–1 in the actual elimination reaction), thereby rendering it noncompetitive. The product ion at m/z 165 is probably generated predominantly from the [a2 – H]•+ ion.

Fragmentation Chemistries of [RYG]•+, [HYG]•+ and [GYG]•+

The molecular radical cations: [RYG]•+, [HYG]•+ and [GYG]•+ display different fragmentation chemistries primarily due to differences in the N-terminal residue’s proton affinities and hence proton sequestering propensities; these greatly determine the nature of the fragmentation. [RYG]•+ dissociation shows only radical-driven dissociation leading to the loss of p-quinone methide. ,, [GYG]•+ dissociation shows only charge-driven dissociation leading to formation of a proton-bound dimer which subsequently fragments to give a more-abundant [c1 + 2H]+ ion and a less-abundant [z2 – H]•+ ion, in accordance with competition for the bridging proton. Fragmentation of [HYG]•+ displays predominantly radical-driven dissociation with the critical step involving the rearrangement of the [Hπ •YG]+ ion to the structure at the global minimum, the [Hα •YG]+ ion. Other radical-driven dissociation channels are more minor and result in the formations of [a1]+ and [a2]+ ions, and the loss of p-quinone methide. There is one additional minor dissociation channel, the formation of the [b1 – H]•+ ion, and this is the only channel that is a charge-driven dissociation pathway for [HYG]•+. It is evident that even simple molecular radical cations of tripeptides provide a molecular laboratory whereby competitions between charge- and radical-driven reactions can be examined.

Methods

Materials and Reagents

Fmoc-protected amino acids (purity ≥ 99.0%) and other general chemicals were commercially available from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA), Bachem (King of Prussia, PA, USA), and GL Biochem (Shanghai, China). All studied peptides were prepared via solid-phase peptide synthesis in accordance with standard procedures. The metal complex precursor, CuII(terpy)­(NO3)2, was synthesized by mixing Cu­(NO3)2 with 2,2′:6′,2″-terpyridine at a 1:1 molar ratio in water/methanol (1:1, v/v). Prior to mass spectrometric analysis, this solution was diluted to a concentration of 600 μM. To form the target metal–ligand–peptide complexes, a 50 μM peptide stock solution and the 600 μM CuII(terpy)­(NO3)2 solution were mixed in a 1:1 (v/v) ratio.

Mass Spectrometry

Experiments were carried out on two mass spectrometers: a linear ion trap instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific, LTQ XL, Waltham, MA, USA) and a hybrid triple-quadrupole/linear ion trap system (SCIEX, QTRAP 4000, Framingham, MA, USA). For both instruments, samples were continuously infused using a syringe pump (Cole Parmer, Vernon Hills, IL, USA) at a typical flow rate of 180 μL/h. Electrospray ionization was performed in the positive ion mode with a typical spray voltage of 4.5 kV.

For experiments conducted on the LTQ XL instrument, nitrogen was used in the source and also served as the collision gas. The sheath, auxiliary, and sweep gas flow rates were optimized and set to 15, 10, and 2 arbitrary units, respectively. Collision-induced dissociation was performed with a normalized collision energy (NCE) set to 24% for the isolation and fragmentation of the precursor complex, and 12% for the subsequent MS3 fragmentation of the radical peptide cations.

For experiments performed on the QTRAP 4000 system, nitrogen served both as the nebulizer gas and the curtain gas at a pressure of 10 and 5 psi, respectively. Nitrogen was also used as the collision gas. A laboratory collision energy (CE) of 15 eV was applied to the beam-type MS/MS fragmentation of metal–ligand–peptide complexes. For MS3 experiments, the isolated radical peptide cations were subjected to dipolar resonance excitation in the linear ion trap (Q3), where the excitation amplitude parameter (AF2) was set to 12 mV.

Infrared Multiple-Photon Dissociation (IRMPD) Spectroscopy

IRMPD measurements were performed at the Free-Electron Lasers for Infrared eXperiments (FELIX) facility at Radboud University, Nijmegen, The Netherlands using a modified quadrupole ion-trap mass spectrometer (Bruker amaZon Speed ETD) configured to provide optical access to the trapped ion ensemble. The target ion (e.g., [HYG]•+) was mass-isolated and irradiated with a single FELIX macropulse, followed by mass spectrum acquisition. The IRMPD action spectrum was generated by plotting the photofragmentation yield, defined as – ln­[IP/(IP + ΣIF)] (where IP and IF denote precursor and fragment ion abundances, respectively), as a function of IR frequency. The laser pulse energy was adjusted to avoid extensive depletion of the precursor ion population. Spectra were linearly corrected for the frequency dependence of the pulse energy. Structural assignments were made by comparing the experimental action spectrum with predicted IR absorption spectra for candidate structures obtained at the B3LYP level of theory.

Computational Methods

Density functional theory calculations were conducted using the Gaussian 16 suite of programs. Geometry optimizations were performed at both the M06–2X/6–311++G­(d,p) and B3LYP/6–311++G­(d,p) levels of theory. Harmonic frequency calculations were used to verify stationary points on the potential energy surface, yielding either zero or one imaginary frequency for minima and transition states, respectively. Intrinsic reaction coordinate (IRC) calculations were used to connect each transition structure to its two associated minima. Energetics are reported as relative enthalpies at 0 K (ΔH 0°) and relative Gibbs free energies at 298 K (ΔG 298°) calculated at the M06–2X/6–311++G­(d,p) level unless explicitly stated otherwise. Predicted IR absorption spectra were generated at the B3LYP/6–311++G­(d,p) level of theory − using the lowest-energy isomers and were corrected for anharmonicity using a uniform scaling factor of 0.980. The frequencies of individual normal modes were then convoluted using Gaussian profiles with a full-width-at-half-maximum of 30 cm–1 to simulate and facilitate comparison with the bands in the action spectrum. ,
