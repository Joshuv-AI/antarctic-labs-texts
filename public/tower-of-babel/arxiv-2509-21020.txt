Abstract
Electric-vehicle (EV) battery disassembly requires precise multi-robot coordination, short and reliable motions, and robust collision safety in cluttered, dynamic scenes. We propose a four-layer task-and-motion planning (TAMP) framework that couples symbolic task planning and cost- and accessibility-aware allocation with a TP-GMM-guided motion planner learned from demonstrations. Stereo vision with YOLOv8 provides real-time component localization, while OctoMap-based 3D mapping and FCL(Flexible Collision Library) checks in MoveIt unify predictive digital-twin collision checking with reactive, vision-based avoidance. Validated on two UR10e robots across cable, busbar, service plug, and three leaf-cell removals, the approach yields substantially more compact and safer motions than a default RRTConnect baseline under identical perception and task assignments: average end-effector path length drops by − 63.3 % -63.3\% and makespan by − 8.1 % -8.1\% ; per-arm swept volumes shrink (R1: 0.583 → 0.139 ​ m 3 0.583\rightarrow 0.139\,\mathrm{m}^{3} ; R2: 0.696 → 0.252 ​ m 3 0.696\rightarrow 0.252\,\mathrm{m}^{3} ), and mutual overlap decreases by 47 % 47\% ( 0.064 → 0.034 ​ m 3 0.064\rightarrow 0.034\,\mathrm{m}^{3} ). These results highlight improved autonomy, precision, and safety for multi-robot EV battery disassembly in unstructured, dynamic environments.

This work introduces a modular TAMP framework that integrates a perception-driven task scheduler with a GMM-informed RRT motion planner. The scheduler handles object sorting, task reasoning, and dual-arm task allocation, while the motion planner exploits demonstrations for guided sampling and enables responsive replanning for collision avoidance and dynamic adaptation. Due to its modular design, the framework is scalable to different robot setups and adaptable to varying execution costs and optimisation criteria.

II
Related Work

Task-and-motion planning (TAMP) is central to multi–robot operation in shared workspaces: it must choose feasible action sequences, allocate limited resources, and coordinate agents while guaranteeing geometric and kinematic safety. The integration of symbolic task planning with continuous motion planning is widely recognised as a key enabler for autonomous execution in complex environments through logic–based representations and discrete decision mechanisms.

III
System Configuration

We implement a ROS-based multi-robot stack for safe, coordinated operation in shared human–robot workspaces. At its core, a MoveIt-based control node orchestrates motion planning, manipulation, trajectory execution, and collision avoidance. The
Planning Scene
aggregates robot kinematics, real-time sensor input, and environment representations into a single, continuously updated state used by the planner (OMPL back-end) and the collision checker (FCL). Fig.
1
outlines the architecture and data flow among perception, planning, and execution.

MoveIt and the planning problem

Planning and replanning are posed as a constrained optimisation over robot configurations
\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

\min_{\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{C}}\;f(\mathbf{q})\quad\text{s.t.}\quad d(\mathbf{q},t)\;\geq\;d_{\min},

where
f(\mathbf{q})
encodes path/time/energy costs, and
d(\mathbf{q},t)
is the
signed distance margin
to the nearest obstacle at time
t
(positive: safe, zero: contact, negative: penetration). Safety is enforced
along the trajectory
, i.e.,
d(\mathbf{q}(s),t)\geq d_{\min}
for all path parameters
s\in[0,1]
.

Environment collision

An OctoMap-based 3D occupancy model provides geometry for environment queries. Let
d_{\mathrm{env}}(\mathbf{q},t)
be the minimum signed distance from any robot link to mapped obstacles. The constraint

d_{\mathrm{env}}(\mathbf{q},t)\;\geq\;d_{\min}

ensures sufficient separation; whenever violated, the system triggers replanning or imposes local motion constraints to restore feasibility.

Self-collision

Self-collision is monitored via FCL using a precomputed collision matrix and continuous distance checks between link pairs. With
d_{\mathrm{self}}(\mathbf{q})
the minimum inter-link signed distance,

d_{\mathrm{self}}(\mathbf{q})\;\geq\;d_{\min}

guarantees collision-free internal configurations during execution.

Planning Scene (constraint aggregation)

The Planning Scene maintains both constraints concurrently; equivalently,

d_{\mathrm{total}}(\mathbf{q},t)\;=\;\min\{\,d_{\mathrm{env}}(\mathbf{q},t),\;d_{\mathrm{self}}(\mathbf{q})\,\}\;\geq\;d_{\min}.

This aggregation prevents false safety due to additive cancellation and supports consistent, real-time updates as perception refreshes the map and as robots move.

Hardware interface

Finally, time-parameterised trajectories are streamed through the hardware interface to the robot controllers, closing the loop between planning and physical actuation. Execution feedback (state, forces, timing) is returned to the planner to enable reactive constraint handling and fast replanning.

Robot Controller

The robot controller executes time-parameterised joint trajectories received via the hardware interface and streams per-joint setpoints to the robot drives. Depending on the vendor driver, the controller operates in position, velocity, or effort mode with model-based feedforward (e.g., gravity/friction compensation) and feedback (PID) to ensure accurate tracking. It performs high-rate interpolation and enforces safety limits (position/velocity/acceleration/torque), applies saturation and anti-windup, blends successive segments, and triggers a controlled stop on constraint violations or faults (e.g., E-stop, limit switches). Joint states and tracking errors are fed back to the planning stack, enabling fast replanning when deviations or collisions are predicted. This closes the loop between planning and physical actuation for synchronised dual-robot execution.

IV
Task-and-motion planning (TAMP)

This section presents our task-and-motion planning (TAMP) framework for autonomous multi-robot disassembly of EV battery packs. Implemented in ROS/MoveIt, the system comprises (i) a logic-based task planner that schedules symbolic actions under temporal, spatial, and resource constraints and (ii) a learning-guided motion planner that produces adaptive, collision-free trajectories satisfying geometric and kinematic feasibility. Real-time perception, online replanning, and low-level control enable robust, coordinated, and safe behaviour in dynamic, unstructured environments.

Fig. 2:
Overview of the proposed Task and Motion Planning (TAMP) framework in dual-arm setups. The framework integrates multi-level vision-driven object detection, task sorting, and scheduling under logical and physical constraints. The pipeline incorporates a learning-informed motion planning with collision avoidance, enabling multi-robot collaboration for complex tasks in dynamic, cluttered environments.

IV-A
1
Perception and Task Representation

The perception pipeline begins with a
task representation

[
30

to detect and segment battery-pack components at the part level. Following the methodology in
[
31

A dataset of 226 real images of NISSAN e-NV200 battery components was collected, covering four object classes: cable, busbar, leaf cell, and service plug. Of these, 204 images were allocated for training and 22 for validation. Manual labelling and dataset augmentation expanded the dataset to 542 images (474 training, 45 validation, and 23 test). Model training was performed using YOLOv8 on Google Colab (Ubuntu 20.04, Intel Core i7-13700H CPU, NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3050 GPU). Input images were resized to
640\times 640
pixels, balancing accuracy and computational efficiency. Training was run for 50 epochs and completed in approximately 0.317 hours. The final trained model was exported and integrated into the perception stage.
The segmentation masks are fused with depth information to recover each component’s 3D centroid and, when required, its 6-DoF pose. These estimates are then provided to the grasping and motion planning modules for execution. After 6-DoF pose acquisition, the information is passed to the
logical-task
stage, where objects are sorted into a predetermined disassembly sequence
[
32
]
. This ensures that task reasoning and robot/tool selection are performed in the correct order, enabling successful disassembly.

IV-B
1
LfD-Guided Motion Planning

As demonstrated in
[
33
]
, integrating Learning-from-Demonstration (LfD) techniques with sampling-based motion planning algorithms leads to highly efficient motion planners. Building on this approach, we extend the framework from a single-robot setup to a dual-arm system. The motion planning pipeline begins with the collection of demonstrations. Following the approach in
[
34
]
, demonstrations were recorded for a set of disassembly tasks, extended in this work to a dual-robot setup. In these sessions, one robot executed random motions within the shared workspace, while the other was teleoperated by a human expert. The demonstrations encode trajectories from start to goal poses while avoiding collisions with the other robot. These data capture expert approaches for safe and efficient manipulation in multi-robot environments and are subsequently used to train a probabilistic model that generalises the underlying motion patterns of both robots.

TP-GMM Learning and Reproduction

Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs) are employed to capture the variability of demonstrated trajectories in Cartesian space, representing motion data as a weighted sum of
K
Gaussian components. Model parameters are estimated using the Expectation-Maximisation (EM) algorithm, enabling probabilistic generalisation of expert demonstrations. However, a standard GMM is restricted to a fixed reference frame, which limits its adaptability to changing task conditions.
Task-Parameterised GMMs (TP-GMMs) overcome this limitation by encoding demonstrations with respect to multiple task-dependent frames. Following the approach in
[
33
]
, these frames are denoted as the start pose
\hat{f}^{(s)}
, goal pose
\hat{f}^{(g)}
, and obstacle poses
\hat{f}^{(o)}
, corresponding to
\mathbf{p}_{k}
and
\mathbf{x}_{ik}
(Equation
5
), all expressed relative to the robot’s
R_{j}
base frame.
Since this work extends
[
33
]
to a dual-robot setup, for each defined task (e.g., pick or place) two TP-GMMs are learned, one per robot. Now each task demonstration is expressed in the corresponding frames, yielding three GMMs that are subsequently combined. During reproduction, new task parameters are provided, and the model adapts its Gaussian components as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\hat{\xi}}^{(s)}_{t,k}

\displaystyle=\hat{A}^{(s)}_{t}\,\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(s)}_{k}+\hat{b}^{(s)}_{t},\quad\boldsymbol{\hat{\Sigma}}^{(s)}_{t,k}=\hat{A}^{(s)}_{t}\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(s)}_{k}\,(\hat{A}^{(s)}_{t})^{T}

(7)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\hat{\xi}}^{(g)}_{t,k}

\displaystyle=\hat{A}^{(g)}_{t}\,\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(g)}_{k}+\hat{b}^{(g)}_{t},\quad\boldsymbol{\hat{\Sigma}}^{(g)}_{t,k}=\hat{A}^{(g)}_{t}\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(g)}_{k}\,(\hat{A}^{(g)}_{t})^{T}

\displaystyle=\hat{A}^{(o)}_{t}\,\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(o)}_{k}+\hat{b}^{(o)}_{t},\quad\boldsymbol{\hat{\Sigma}}^{(o)}_{t,k}=\hat{A}^{(o)}_{t}\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(o)}_{k}\,(\hat{A}^{(o)}_{t})^{T}

IV-B
2
Active Collision Avoidance

In addition to path optimisation through LfD, the second critical component of our motion planner is
collision perception and avoidance
, which ensures safe execution of trajectories in a shared dual-arm workspace. We investigate two UR10e manipulators operating in proximity, where inter-robot collisions and interactions with dynamic obstacles are key challenges. To address this, the proposed Active Collision Avoidance module combines real-time perception with avoidance strategies, structured into two complementary parts:
Perception
and
Avoidance
.

Collision Perception

Collision detection is handled through the Flexible Collision Library (FCL) within a MoveIt Planning Scene. All collision objects, including the two robots (from their URDFs) and the environment, are monitored in real time.

Robot-to-Robot Collisions:

MoveIt’s Allowed Collision Matrix (ACM) is used to encode feasible body interactions. Since dual-robot systems require bidirectional awareness, we employ a digital twin strategy: each URDF embeds a replica of the other robot (Fig.
3
), enabling independent but coordinated collision checking. The ACM is further refined by excluding irrelevant body pairs to reduce computational load.

Fig. 3:
Integration of real and virtual (imaginary) robots in a shared simulation, where each URDF embeds the counterpart’s model to enable inter-robot collision checking through FCL.

Environment Collisions:

An Intel RealSense depth camera provides dynamic 3D workspace perception via MoveIt’s Occupancy Map Updater, which converts depth data into a voxel-based OctoMap with 0.03 m resolution. This OctoMap is broadcast to both robots’ planning scenes, allowing each to reason about environmental constraints in real time. Due to the computational demands of maintaining two planning scenes, the maximum achievable update rate is
\sim
10 Hz.

A safety threshold
\varepsilon=0.15
m is defined to detect near-collisions, with violations triggering reactive adjustments to increase separation. This threshold is operator-tunable, balancing clearance and efficiency. Limiting collision checks to distances
\leq\varepsilon
reduces planning complexity. When a proximity violation is detected, FCL returns 3D contact points between objects, which are visualised in RViz (
visualization_msgs/MarkerArray
) as shown in Fig.

Collision Avoidance

Collision avoidance is implemented through a dedicated
avoid_collision()
routine, enabling both robots to adapt dynamically to near-collisions in real time. The approach employs a velocity vector steering strategy in
SO(3)
, where the robot’s tool velocity vector
\vec{\mathbf{v}}
is reoriented when a collision vector
\vec{\mathbf{c}}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\vec{c_{n}}
(aggregated from detected near-collisions) indicates a potential obstacle (Fig.
4
). The steering axis is computed as
\vec{\mathbf{k}}=\vec{\mathbf{c}}\times\vec{\mathbf{v}}
, and Rodrigues’ rotation formula is applied to yield a rotated velocity vector
\vec{\mathbf{v}}_{\text{rot}}
.

\vec{\mathbf{v}}_{rot}=\vec{\mathbf{v}}+sin(\phi)\cdot(\vec{k}\times\vec{\mathbf{v}})+(1-cos(\phi))\cdot\vec{k}\times(\vec{k}\times\vec{\mathbf{v}})

(8)

A steering angle
\phi
regulates the correction magnitude. Rather than fixing
\phi
, we define it to be relative to the angle
\theta
between
\vec{\mathbf{c}}
and
\vec{\mathbf{v}}
:

\theta=\cos^{-1}\left(\frac{\vec{\mathbf{c}}\cdot\vec{\mathbf{v}}}{\|\vec{\mathbf{c}}\|\|\vec{\mathbf{v}}\|}\right),\quad\phi=\frac{\pi}{2}-\theta,

(9)

so that imminent collisions (small
\theta
) yield larger steering corrections, while tangential encounters (large
\theta
) produce minimal deviations.

\varepsilon
.

This adaptive behaviour avoids unnecessary trajectory deviations, helping to maintain path optimality while ensuring safety through timely avoidance manoeuvres. Algorithm
2
presents the overall Active Collision Avoidance component in the motion planner, with
avoid_collision
routine being invoked when
near-collision
state is flagged to interrupt the robot controller’s execution of the current planned trajectory. The routine keeps in operation until the
near-collision
state is changed. Each robot now requests a
replan
(Algorithm
1
) a collision-free trajectory to the first given target pose from the current pose/configuration. The Active Collision Avoidance algorithm continues to run iteratively until convergence to the target pose is achieved.

IV-C

Framework Overview (Dual-Arm Setup)

Fig.
2
illustrates the proposed Task and Motion Planning (TAMP) framework implemented on a dual-arm robotic setup, asynchronously controlling two robots to complete a full disassembly task. The framework integrates high-level task reasoning with low-level motion execution, ensuring both long-horizon task feasibility and safe, coordinated dual-arm manipulation. The pipeline proceeds sequentially as follows:

High-level layer Task Planner

The framework begins with perception-driven task reasoning. Two eye-in-hand cameras mounted on the robots acquire RGB-D images of the shared workspace. Each camera detects relevant objects, yielding their names and six-dimensional poses, which are passed into the task planner.

Low-level layer Task Planner

The task planner then determines a valid disassembly order, respecting predefined precedence constraints (e.g., certain components must be removed before others). Once the next step is identified, the appropriate tool is selected for execution. Subsequently, the planner assigns tasks to each robot by generating
sorted action lists
relative to their kinematic reachability and workspace constraints. These lists are updated iteratively as the disassembly progresses and the environment changes.

High-level Layer Motion Planner

The high-level task allocations are translated into motion planning goals for each robot. Each robot is assigned a target pose consistent with its designated sub-task. Prior to trajectory generation,
motion constraints
such as tool orientation, approach direction, or force/torque limits are imposed to ensure task feasibility.
Using a GMM-Informed RRT planner, motion plans are generated and executed concurrently by both robots. Throughout execution, the system monitors trajectory feasibility. If a constraint violation or collision risk is detected, execution is interrupted, and a
replanning
cycle is triggered for the affected robot(s).

Low-level Layer Motion Planner

The low-level layer ensures safe motion execution through continuous collision monitoring and active avoidance mechanisms. Using the Flexible Collision Library (FCL) within MoveIt, the system performs online collision checking against both (i) the environment (represented as an OctoMap) and (ii) the other robot (modelled from its URDF). A distance threshold
\varepsilon=0.15
m is imposed to detect near-collisions.
When a near-collision is detected, the
avoid_collision()
routine is invoked (Equation
8
-
9
). Once the near-collision is resolved, a
replan
request is triggered to compute a new collision-free trajectory from the robot’s current configuration to its target pose.

Therefore, the complete TAMP pipeline operates as a hierarchical loop:

1.

Object detection from cameras
\rightarrow
Task planner computes disassembly order.

2.

Task allocation
\rightarrow
Motion planner generates task-constrained trajectories.

3.

Collision monitoring
\rightarrow
If no collision, continue execution; if collision, invoke active avoidance and replan.

4.

Repeat until convergence to all disassembly goals is achieved.

Comparison Metrics

Each case study was evaluated under both planners, Default and TP-GMM, using

and

(Case Study II) and

Path length is computed as the cumulative Euclidean distance of end-effector positions along the trajectory between the specified start and end. Path duration denotes the total motion time (planning + execution). Trajectories and cumulative-distance plots are reported for qualitative inspection.

In Case Study II, we additionally evaluate the
manipulability index
, a configuration-dependent measure of dexterity. It quantifies the arm’s ability to generate end-effector motion across directions, enabling comparison of motion planners and highlighting proximity to singular configurations, particularly informative in cluttered or dynamic scenes. We compute the index following Yoshikawa’s formulation
[
37
]
. In Case Study II, manipulability is evaluated using Yoshikawa’s index, denoted
w

[
37
]
. Joint and end-effector velocities satisfy
\dot{x}=J(q)\dot{q}
where
\dot{x}

J(q)

\dot{q}
denote the end-effector velocity, the Jacobian at configuration
q

JJ^{T}
and report the condition number

w_{\lambda}=\frac{\lambda_{\max}}{\lambda_{\min}},

(10)

with larger values indicating proximity to singular configurations, thus loss of dexterity.

In Case Study III, we evaluate the

to quantify the manipulator’s spatial footprint, the 3D region traversed by the links and end-effector over the trajectory. This metric is pertinent in multi-robot or human–robot settings, where lower swept volume implies reduced collision risk and better workspace utilisation. We compute it via a voxel-occupancy model: the workspace is discretised into cubes of edge
h=1/r
(resolution
r

V=N_{\text{occ}}\,h^{3},

(11)

with
N_{\text{occ}}
the number of voxels occupied during motion. This provides a consistent basis for comparing planners in terms of spatial efficiency.

VII
Conclusions

This paper presented a comprehensive Task-and-Motion Planning (TAMP) framework for multi-robot EV battery disassembly. The proposed four-layer architecture integrates high-level perception, low-level task sequencing with collision-aware motion planning, enabling safe, adaptive, and efficient collaboration in dynamic and cluttered environments.
At the task level, stereo vision with YOLOv8 and depth sensing detects, segments, and localises components so that logical constraints (e.g., disassembly order) can be enforced. A hierarchical task scheduler then allocates tasks across robots while considering tool compatibility and execution cost. While distance-based cost was used in this study, the framework is general: the cost function can be redefined to optimise alternative criteria such as manipulability, collision risk minimisation, or energy efficiency.
For motion planning, demonstrations are processed into Task-Parametrised Gaussian Mixture Models (TP-GMM) that guide feasible, expert-like trajectories. Online 3D mapping via OctoMap and collision checking with FCL support active perception and continuous avoidance, while an adaptive velocity steering mechanism further ensures safety, and responsiveness to dynamic changes.

We validated the framework on a dual UR10e setup across three case studies: robot–robot collision avoidance, dynamic obstacle interaction, and EV battery disassembly scenario. In the end-to-end disassembly scenario, the TP-GMM–guided planner produced more compact motions and a shorter overall execution: the average end-effector path length decreased from
48.8\,\mathrm{m}
to
17.9\,\mathrm{m}
(
-63.3\%
), and the average makespan improved from
467.9\,\mathrm{s}
to
429.8\,\mathrm{s}
(
-8.1\%
) across 5 experimental trials. In dynamic environments, the GMM-Informed planner maintained higher trajectory consistency and robustness under varying obstacle conditions. In
Case Study III
(full disassembly), swept-volume analysis indicated substantially smaller per-arm volumes (
R1
:
0.583\rightarrow 0.139\,\mathrm{m}^{3}
,
R2
:
0.696\rightarrow 0.252\,\mathrm{m}^{3}
) and reduced overlap (
0.064\rightarrow 0.034\,\mathrm{m}^{3}
), suggesting safer workspace sharing.

Beyond the specific EV disassembly use case, the modular design of this TAMP framework makes it readily adaptable to larger numbers of robots and different sequential task domains. Provided that the vision pipeline is retrained on application-relevant data and the TP-GMM models are exposed to suitable demonstrations, the system can generalise to diverse industrial or service robotics tasks.
Thus, this work contributes a scalable and adaptable TAMP framework that integrates perception, task scheduling, and learning-informed motion planning. The results highlight its potential as a general solution for coordinated multi-robot manipulation in unstructured and dynamically changing environments.
