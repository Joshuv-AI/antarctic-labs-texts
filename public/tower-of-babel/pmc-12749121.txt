A lignin-enrichment approach for processing natural wood into highly NIR-selective optical filters
Authors: Shixu Yu, Yucheng Hu, Guohua Miao, Yimin Xie, Sheng Chen, Tingting You, Feng Xu, Qinghua Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTransparent wood engineering enables the transformation of natural wood into various optical materials. However, typical top-down strategies rely on lignin removal, which significantly reduces the near-infrared (NIR) selectivity of the resulting materials, making it challenging to develop wood-based NIR optical filters. Herein, we present a lignin-enrichment approach to fabricate highly NIR-selective optical filters directly from natural wood. By enriching the lignin content from 19.81% to 35.19%, we amplify its dual functionality in ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) light absorption and binding ability, increasing the NIR selectivity of wood after densification. The resulting wooden filters completely block light below 600 nm while achieving an NIR transmittance exceeding 80%, enabling them to harness 77.90% of solar NIR for phototherapy. This lignin-enrichment approach not only establishes a transformative strategy for designing NIR optical filters but also promotes the efficient utilization of natural wood and solar NIR energy.Subject terms: Composites, Optical materials, Biopolymers

Introduction

Since the dawn of human civilization, our planet has served as both a cradle and a source of invaluable resources, ranging from petroleum, rare earth elements, and precious metals to renewable energy sources such as solar, wind, and wave energy, all of which have fueled rapid societal development. Although modern society has advanced to unprecedented heights over recent decades with the aid of these resources and advanced technologies, the relentless exploitation of non-renewable resources has triggered serious environmental concerns1–3. This has compelled us to seek more sustainable pathways, particularly those involving renewable resources4. Wood, as one of the most prominent lignocellulosic materials derived from forestry practices, is widely recognized for being renewable, sustainable, cost-effective, and abundant5–7. Its unique hierarchical structure, combined with the abundance of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin, provides a robust foundation for creating multifunctional wood-based optical materials8–11. Among various optical materials, NIR optical filters are crucial for selectively separating NIR from the broad optical spectrum. Owing to their excellent NIR selectivity, these filters have been extensively used in optical encryption, privacy protection, NIR night vision, and medicolegal identification12–16. Achieving such NIR-selective functionalities in wood-based optical materials requires the ability to effectively block ultraviolet-visible (UV–Vis) light while allowing NIR light to pass through. However, traditional top-down approaches struggle to impart this unique optical selectivity to wood, as they often remove lignin to achieve high visible transparency for light-management purposes. Although the resulting wood-based optical materials have shown promise in optical applications such as energy-efficient windows17,18, illumination19,20, and light management layers for solar cells21, they remain inadequate for NIR-selectivity-dependent uses.

Lignin, owing to its abundance, multifunctionality, and wide-ranging applications, plays a vital role as a natural aromatic resource, making its valorization essential for sustainable biorefineries22. Incorporating lignin into composite material systems enables the transfer of its intrinsic properties (e.g., hydrophobicity, antibacterial activity, and UV resistance) to substrates, thereby broadening the application potential of both lignin itself and lignin-containing composites23. More importantly, lignin contains methoxylated phenylpropanoid subunits, giving rise to conjugated structures and chromophores24,25, which can be leveraged to prepare optical filters with UV–Vis shielding and NIR transparency15,16. Building on this understanding of lignin’s intrinsic optical properties, an intriguing concept termed the relignification approach26,27, which involves backfilling lignin into delignified wood, shows promise in altering the optical selectivity of wood-based optical materials28. Nevertheless, current relignification methods still encounter significant challenges in simultaneously achieving both a long blocking wavelength and high NIR transmittance, i.e., NIR selectivity. These approaches belong to cell wall engineering, which represents a promising top-down strategy to alter the physical and chemical properties of natural wood in situ through facile processing steps4. With regard to the remaining challenges in NIR selectivity, most conventional cell wall engineering methods involving delignification or lignin-modification prove counterproductive when designing desirable NIR wooden filters. In contrast, maintaining a high lignin content extends the blocking range, while increasing compactness and smoothing the surface are crucial for maximizing NIR transmittance16. Therefore, a method for enriching lignin content in wood-based substrates, followed by high densification and surface smoothing, is a prerequisite. Notably, the hierarchically oriented channels in wood hinder the efficient infiltration of lignin, while the naturally rigid cell walls resist compression. These constraints necessitate that the method not only integrates additional lignin but also softens the wood cell walls, enabling it to achieve a densified structure with high lignin content. Treating wood with pulping liquors can simultaneously soften the cell walls and generate a lignin-containing pulping liquor that permeates the wooden matrix. The lignin in the absorbed liquor can then be precipitated, thereby anchoring it within the substrates. This principle was exemplified by Nonaka et al.29, who demonstrated in their previous study that acidification could induce lignin precipitation from model kraft pulping liquor absorbed in wood pulp, resulting in a lignin-enriched pulp composite. Therefore, the concept of treating wood with pulping liquors followed by lignin precipitation holds great promise for in situ anchoring of lignin into cell wall-softened wooden substrates, producing compressible wooden substrates with retained lignin. However, this strategy is challenging to achieve the lignin content required for highly NIR-selective optical materials, as the pulping liquors dilute the overall lignin concentration in the treatment system. Moreover, further increasing lignin content may require additional impregnation with high-concentration lignin solutions prior to lignin precipitation, adding tedious processing steps and extending the treatment time.

To address these challenges, we propose a lignin-enrichment approach based on lignin-enriched infiltration and densification (Fig. 1a). In contrast to the widely accepted view of transparent wood fabrication, this approach enables the fabrication of highly NIR-selective wooden filters without lignin removal. In this process, lignin is directly infiltrated into wooden substrates using a boiling lignin/acetic acid solution, which not only efficiently softens the cell walls but also allows the lignin solution to penetrate deeply into wooden substrates. Through a subsequent solvent/antisolvent self-assembly driven by its amphiphilic nature, lignin is retained and embedded within the wood30,31. This approach increases the overall lignin content beyond that of native wood (from ~20% to ~35%), thereby extending the blocking wavelength of this special wood. The hot-pressing step further densifies the structure and enhances NIR transmittance by compressing cellulose microfibrils closer together, with lignin acting as a robust binder (Fig. 1a). This integrated two-step process not only infuses but also stabilizes a greater amount of lignin in wood substrates. Therefore, the resulting wooden filters (Fig. 1b) exhibit exceptional optical selectivity, transmitting NIR light while effectively blocking unwanted wavelengths (Fig. 1c). With these properties, the wooden filters represent a promising platform for harnessing solar NIR for potential use in natural NIR-assisted phototherapy (Fig. 1d). Beyond introducing a paradigm-shifting concept in top-down wood engineering for NIR optical materials, our work markedly advances the value-added utilization of lignin compared with traditional lignin removal approaches. It further highlights the broader prospects of leveraging renewable, wood-sourced feedstocks and solar energy to achieve both sustainability and human well-being.

Results

Construction of wooden filters and structural evolution

The proposed lignin-enrichment approach for processing poor NIR-selective natural wood into high NIR-selective wooden filters primarily involved two time-efficient steps: lignin-enriched infiltration and densification (Fig. 2a, b). Typically, a wood slice, with dimensions of 100 × 100 × 0.75 mm3, was first treated with a boiling aqueous acetic acid solution for just 5 min (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). This pretreatment was to soften cell walls and deconstruct cell corner regions, resulting in a cellular but open structure (Supplementary Fig. 3). This opened structure allowed for more efficient lignin-enriched infiltration, which took only 25 min for the infiltration of lignin into the wooden substrate in a lignin/acetic acid solution. To further embed lignin into the wooden substrate, the wood filled with lignin solution was placed in deionized water to remove acetic acid, promoting lignin self-assembly. The abundant hydroxyl groups in cellulose chains facilitated strong hydrogen bonding between the regenerated lignin and the cellulose microfibrils32. Consequently, lignin particles were deposited on the surface of the wood’s channels, giving the lignin-enriched wood a brown appearance (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5). In this state, the wood could be easily hand-shredded both along and perpendicular to the fibers (Supplementary Fig. 6), attributed to the acetic acid-induced hydrolysis that softened the cell walls, which also served as a criterion for evaluating the lignin-enriched infiltration process.

Densification was achieved through two steps. The wood was squeezed and preliminarily dried at 90 °C to discharge most of the water and compress the cavities within the lignin-enriched wood, resulting in a densified arrangement of cellulose microfibrils. This arrangement facilitated the formation of abundant hydrogen bonds between cellulose molecules from different microfibrils (Fig. 1a, bottom panel)33. However, the relatively low temperature (90 °C) led to lignin aggregation, which induced defects within the wood substrates16, making the resulting lignin-wood unable to achieve high NIR selectivity. To address this issue, a hot-pressing procedure was used to further fill the defects. The synergistic effect of pressure and temperature enabled lignin to tightly and uniformly wrap cellulose microfibrils and integrate them with lignin34, resulting in a significant increase in the densification of wood (Fig. 2b). As observed, natural wood exhibits a hierarchically oriented structure with a typical cell wall structure and rough surface (Fig. 2c–f and Supplementary Fig. 7), while the wooden filter demonstrates a densified structure with a smooth surface (Fig. 2g–j).

More importantly, the lignin-enrichment approach modified the chemical composition of the wood, particularly its lignin content (Fig. 2k and Supplementary Fig. 8). After pretreatment, the lignin content decreased from 19.81% (natural wood) to 13.75% (pretreated wood) (Fig. 2k and Supplementary Note 1), primarily due to the release of lignin from deconstructed cell corner regions (Supplementary Fig. 3). Subsequently, the lignin content significantly increased to 35.19% in the wooden filter (Fig. 2k), highlighting the effectiveness of lignin enrichment. In addition, the lignin-enrichment approach prevented the destruction of the cellulose crystalline structure (Fig. 2l) and maintained the anisotropic structure of natural wood (Fig. 2m, n and Supplementary Fig. 9).

Chemical evolution and molecular interactions in wooden filters

In addition to morphology, lignin-enrichment approach also altered chemical structures of the wood, which showed significant influences on the molecular interactions of wooden filters. According to Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy of lignin and deacetylated lignin (obtained through the methods in Supplementary Note 2), the lignin used in lignin-enriched infiltration was partially acetylated due to the obvious peak at 1730 cm–1 (Supplementary Fig. 10)32,35. These acetyl groups contributed to rearrangement and high densification of lignin molecules during hot-pressing process16, thus promoting the densification of wooden filters. During hot-pressing process, the chemical compositions of wooden filters were not significantly altered, while the chemical structures of lignin was heavily changed because of high pressure and temperature. To further investigate these changes, hot-pressed lignin was prepared according to the hot-pressing conditions for preparing wooden filters (Supplementary Note 3). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was conducted to analyze the changes in surface elements of lignin before and after hot-pressing (Supplementary Fig. 11). The intensities of C1 (C–C or C=C) and C2 (C–O) were significantly increased and decreased, respectively. This suggested hot-process likely broke ether bonds and induced carbon-carbon condensation of lignin, a process of lignin self-bonding36,37. In addition, lignin self-bonding was also suggested by two-dimensional heteronuclear single quantum coherence (HSQC) nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) recorded in the aliphatic region (δC/δH 50–100/2.5–5.5) and aromatic region (δC/δH 100–130/5.5–8.5) (Fig. 3a–d). After hot-pressing, the signal in aliphatic region declined due to the cleavage of side chain structures (Fig. 3a), and the signals of H in aromatic region at the positions of S2,6, G2, G5, and G6 also decreased, suggesting the involvement of aromatic rings in lignin self-bonding (Fig. 3b, d)36. Furthermore, 31P NMR was used to characterize the hydroxyl group contents of lignin, hot-pressed lignin, and deacetylated lignin (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 12). Lignin exhibited a lower hydroxyl group content compared to deacetylated lignin, which suggested the partial acetylation of lignin used in the lignin-enrichment approach, consistent with the results of FTIR spectroscopy. Equally important, hot-pressing process improved both aliphatic and phenolic hydroxyl group contents from 0.50 to 0.63 mmol g−1 and 1.73 to 2.37 mmol g−1, respectively, which is likely due to aryl ether cleavage. The induced increase in the contents of hydroxyl groups may improve molecular interactions between lignin and cellulose as well as lignin and itself through hydrogen bonding interactions26,32,34.

The structural transformations consequently altered the molecular weight distribution of lignin, as revealed by gel permeation chromatography (GPC) analysis (Supplementary Fig. 13 and Supplementary Note 4). Hot-pressing increased the number-average (Mn) from 2513 to 2713 Da and, more prominently, the mass-average (Mw) molecular weight from 4893 to 6968 Da, accompanied by a notable rise in the polydispersity index (PDI) from 1.95 to 2.57. These increases in molecular weight and PDI suggested that partial lignin fragments participated in self-bonding during the hot-pressing process36. Moreover, it is noteworthy that the lignin/acetic acid solution was regularly replaced to maintain constant concentration and volume during lignin-enriched infiltration. Therefore, a large fraction of lignin was recovered, with a yield of ~91.20% (Supplementary Fig. 14). Compositional analysis showed that the recycled lignin retained high purity (96.39% ± 0.62%) compared with raw lignin (98.84% ± 0.25%) (Supplementary Table 1). In addition, although HSQC spectra confirmed no significant changes in the chemical structure of recycled lignin compared with raw lignin (Fig. 3a, b and Supplementary Fig. 15), increases in hydroxyl group content (Supplementary Fig. 16) and molecular weight (Supplementary Fig. 17) were observed. These results suggested that lignin-enriched infiltration contributed to lignin self-bonding; however, its contribution was much lower than that of hot-pressing. To better understand the mechanism of lignin self-bonding induced by hot-pressing, electron spin resonance (ESR), a fundamental technology for the study of the structure and dynamics of molecular systems, was used to detect and identify free radical species in lignin during hot-pressing38. Therefore, ESR was carried out at 180 °C (Supplementary Fig. 18). The results exhibited a signal centered at g = 2.0034, suggesting typical phenoxy radicals37,38. Additionally, the intensity increased with the extended testing time, probably due to the persistent production of radicals enabled by high temperature. The formed radicals could induce a series of resonance structures, which could result in lignin self-bonding or newly-emerged phenolic hydroxyl groups39, in accordance with the speculation of 2D HSQC, 31P NMR, and GPC tests. Of note, the lignin self-bonding has the potential to make infiltrated lignin crosslinked with the native lignin in wood during hot-pressing36. As a result, both lignin self-bonding and increased hydroxyl groups promote the tight integration between lignin and cellulose, thus significantly increasing the compactness and stability of the obtained wooden filters (Fig. 3f).

High NIR selectivity of wooden filters

Lignin absorbs UV–Vis light, and natural wood’s porous structure causes light scattering, weakening the optical transmittance of natural wood (Fig. 4a, top panel)21,40. To cater to high transparency of wood-based optical materials, traditional methods paid attention to discoloration (lignin modification or removal) and densification (infiltration or compression)3,41–43, while suffering from poor NIR selectivity. In contrast to the widely accepted view, we proposed a unique lignin-enrichment approach for further increasing lignin content of target wooden filters, resulting in a densified lignin-bound structure that reduced light scattering and enhanced NIR transmittance (Fig. 4a, bottom panel). In this approach, a top-down principle could preserve the oriented structure of wood, which provided the resulting wooden filters with optical anisotropy (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 19). Crucially, the densified lignin-bound structure made the wooden filter transparent under NIR light (940 nm) but opaque under visible light, highlighting a high NIR selectivity that was significantly different from that of natural wood, pretreated wood, acetic acid-treated wood (AA-wood), and lignin-wood (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 20). To further investigate these optical changes, a UV–Vis–NIR spectrophotometer was used to measure the transmittance of natural wood, pretreated wood, AA-wood, lignin-wood, and the wooden filter from 200 to 1400 nm (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 21). Acetic acid pretreatment reduced the lignin content and increased wood compressibility, raising NIR transmittance at 1400 nm from 29.52% (natural wood) to 40.58% (pretreated wood) (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 21a). However, the relatively low lignin content of pretreated wood (13.75%, Fig. 2k) weakened its UV–Vis light blocking ability, resulting in poor NIR selectivity. Treating wood solely with the acetic acid solution further increased NIR transmittance but simultaneously reduced UV–Vis shielding (Supplementary Fig. 21b), deviating from the design requirements for NIR-selective wooden filters. These results underscore that the selective light absorption of lignin (Supplementary Fig. 22) is essential for imparting NIR selectivity. Lignin-enriched infiltration incorporated large amounts of lignin into the wood, enabling strong blocking of UV–Vis light (Fig. 4d). Nevertheless, lignin-wood became opaque in the NIR, with only 13.31% transmittance at 1400 nm (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 20c). Remarkably, densification at high temperature significantly improved the NIR transmittance of the wooden filter to 82.53% (over sixfold that of lignin-wood), while also exhibiting a long blocking wavelength of 600 nm, far exceeding that of natural wood (340 nm), pretreated wood (316 nm), and AA-wood (244 nm) (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 21). Therefore, in the lignin-enrichment approach, both lignin-enriched infiltration and densification are the key steps to achieving high NIR selectivity, enabling the wooden filter to block most visible light while transmitting most NIR light.

The optical properties of wooden filters can be tailored by adjusting processing temperature, lignin concentration, and thickness. An elevated temperature resulted in slight but important changes in optical properties of wooden filters. The increased temperature from 120 to 150 °C increased NIR transmittance from 79.79% to 84.46% but reduced the blocking wavelength from 575 to 550 nm, while further elevated temperature (180 °C) slightly decreased NIR transmittance to 82.53% but extended the blocking wavelength to 600 nm (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Fig. 23). These results may suggest that the decreased blocking wavelength at 150 °C is attributed to the increase in overall transmittance and that 180 °C contributes to a more extended blocking wavelength with a slight compromise in NIR transmittance. In addition, varying the concentrations of lignin/acetic acid solutions in lignin-enriched infiltration can also manipulate the NIR selectivity of the resulting wooden filters (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig. 24). As the concentrations increased from 1 to 3 wt% and then to 5 wt%, the blocking wavelengths were extended from 440 to 518 nm and then to 600 nm without compromising NIR transmittance at 1400 nm (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig. 24). Therefore, a high lignin/acetic acid concentration used in lignin-enriched infiltration is more favorable for the NIR selectivity of the resultant wooden filters. However, it needs to be clear that further increasing lignin/acetic acid concentration is not ideal, as it was difficult to further extend the blocking wavelength of the wooden filter and tended to reduce its NIR transmittance (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig. 24). Moreover, the thickness of the wooden filters is also a crucial parameter. Considering that an increase in the thickness of natural wood impedes the efficiency of lignin-enriched infiltration, wet lignin-wood was folded to prepare double-layer wooden filters (Supplementary Fig. 25). As a result, the wooden filters with double layers showed right-shifting spectra with an increased blocking wavelength but a compromising NIR transmittance due to the increased thickness (Fig. 4g). Due to high NIR selectivity, wooden filters consequently demonstrated unique optical characteristics compared to natural wood, pretreated wood, AA-wood, and lignin-wood (Fig. 4h). The optical scalability of wooden filters, enabled by varying lignin concentrations and the resultant thickness, also suggests significant potential for these wooden filters to cater to diverse optical demands. Equally important, the lignin-enrichment approach demonstrated excellent universality, enabling the processing of basswood (a hardwood with a higher density than balsa wood), larch wood (softwood), bamboo (a gramineous plant), and filter paper into NIR filters (Supplementary Figs. 26 and 27 and Supplementary Note 5). However, achieving such high NIR selectivity remains challenging for traditional transparent wood, chemically modified transparent wood, or lignin-infiltrated wood (Fig. 4i and Supplementary Table 2), demonstrating the uniqueness of both our lignin-enrichment approach and the optical properties of the prepared NIR filters.

Comprehensive properties of wooden filters

Achieving sufficient mechanical strength is crucial for the practical applications of wooden filters in addition to their high NIR selectivity. The lignin-enrichment approach efficiently improved the mechanical properties of wooden filters, accompanied by aligned wood cellulose microfibrils that resulted in anisotropic mechanical properties (Fig. 5a, b). Natural wood exhibited poor tensile strength of ~4.70 MPa along the longitudinal direction (L direction) (Fig. 5a). Although the subsequent drying process (hot-pressing under 90 °C) made lignin-wood more densified compared to natural wood, the lignin inside the wood lacked sufficient adhesion, thus minimally increasing the tensile strength to 7.68 MPa (Fig. 5a). With an elevated temperature of 180 °C, hot-pressing not only integrated lignin and cellulose more firmly but also promoted the self-bonding of lignin34, significantly enhancing the tensile strength of the wooden filter to 171.55 MPa, a 36.50-fold increase compared to natural wood (Fig. 5a). Consistent with what is widely known, natural wood, lignin-wood, and wooden filters demonstrated relatively low strength in the transverse direction (T direction). However, compared with natural wood, the collapse of cell walls and densification also improved the tensile strength of lignin-wood and wooden filters along the T-direction44. As a result, lignin-wood and the wooden filter showed 7.56-fold and 15.73-fold increases in tensile strength from 0.41 MPa (natural wood) to 3.10 MPa and 7.27 MPa, respectively (Fig. 5b).

In addition, lignin is more thermostable than cellulose due to its rich aromatic groups and C–C bonds, which can enhance the thermal stability of lignin-wood and wooden filters compared to natural wood45,46. Natural wood exhibited the highest thermal degradation rate and the lowest residual carbon ratio of 12.67% (Fig. 5c). By contrast, the additional incorporation of lignin alleviated the thermal degradation of lignin-wood and wooden filters, while wooden filters exhibited better thermal stability due to their slower degradation rate and higher residual carbon ratio (30.31% vs. 21.80%) (Fig. 5c). This is probably due to the formation of more stable chemical bonds between lignin molecules and the more densified structure of wooden filters. In addition to thermal stability, lignin is also considered a natural hydrophobic binder in plants47. However, natural wood is highly hydrophilic due to its porous structure and abundant exposed hydroxyl groups48, showcasing dramatic water absorption of over 900% (Fig. 5d, e). Lignin-wood showed a decreased water absorption of about 145% due to its enriched lignin content and relatively densified structure (Fig. 5d, e). In contrast to natural wood and lignin-wood, high-temperature hot-pressing significantly reduced the water absorption of wooden filters to 19% (Fig. 5d, e), which was attributed to highly densified structure and lignin self-bonding, demonstrating the excellent water stability of the resulting wooden filters. This superior water stability was also demonstrated by water contact angle (WCA) measurement (Fig. 5f). Water drops on the surface of natural wood were rapidly absorbed due to its high hydrophilicity, resulting in a rapid decline in WCA from 62.8° ± 0.4° to 0° within 5 s. Lignin-wood showed an enhanced WCA stability, attributed to its relatively densified structure and deposited lignin, while failing to prevent lignin-wood from water permeation within 180 s. Of note, wooden filters showed excellent WCA stability with a slight decrease of 14.12% from 67.3 ± 0.7° to 57.8 ± 1.3° within 180 s. The ability of wooden filters to prevent water permeation can also be used to resist other liquids such as milk, juice, and coffee (Fig. 5g). These results suggest that the lignin-enrichment approach can not only improve the mechanical properties of the resulting wooden filters but also enhance their stabilities.

Given the unique NIR selectivity and superior comprehensive properties, we summarized a series of wood-based optical materials regarding their optical selectivity, mechanical strength, water stability, processing time, and sustainability (Supplementary Table 3). Among various comparable properties, optical selectivity is the most important evaluation indicator for the wooden filters. Therefore, the wooden filter, a representative polymer infiltration-free wood20, and a representative polymer (lignin)-infiltrated wood28 were further compared in terms of the five parameters (Fig. 5h). As a consequence, the lignin-enrichment approach is capable of effectively fabricating desirable wooden filters with high NIR selectivity in a convenient manner. Most traditional strategies for creating transparent wood not only involve complex processes such as partial or complete delignification, TEMPO oxidation, polymer infiltration (including resins or lignin), solidification, and densification, but also fail to address high NIR selectivity. In addition, the lignin-enrichment approach provides excellent NIR selectivity, desirable water stability, and reliable mechanical strength for wooden filters without the use of petroleum-based resins, suggesting greater sustainability compared to those incorporating environmentally unfriendly components. Therefore, our proposed strategy is a unique and promising candidate for preparing high-performance NIR optical filters based on natural wood.

Applications of wooden filters

Given the high NIR selectivity of wooden filters, we demonstrated their potential applications in NIR optics. Consistent with previously reported NIR optical filters12–16, blocking UV–Vis light while transmitting NIR enables wooden filters to be used in optical encryption (Supplementary Figs. 28–30). More remarkably, we noted that the wooden filters enabled the separation of NIR from solar irradiation. NIR light possesses uniquely favorable characteristics, including low phototoxicity, minimal invasiveness, and deep-tissue penetration49–52. Its benefits extend to potential phototherapeutic applications (e.g., photobiomodulation) through enhanced mitochondrial functions and accelerated wound healing53,54. Given these advantages, various NIR-assisted phototherapy devices have been developed, such as the device mainly consisting of a halogen lamp, a heating-blocking filter, and an NIR filter (Supplementary Fig. 31). Based on this principle, wooden filters can be theoretically used to harness solar NIR for applications in NIR phototherapy. Therefore, we recorded the NIR intensities from natural sunlight as well as the intensities through natural wood, the wooden filter, and a commercial NIR filter (polyethylene terephthalate, PET, 50 μm) between 8:00 and 18:00 (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Figs. 32 and 33a). The results suggested that natural wood blocked a large proportion of NIR irradiation, only retaining the highest intensity of 129 W m−2 (28.22% of sunlight), while the wooden filters maintained relatively high NIR intensities with a maximum of 356 W m−2 (77.90% of sunlight). Of note, commercial PET NIR filter showed a higher NIR intensity of 419 W m−2 (91.68% of sunlight), while leaking much more visible light and even UV light compared to the wooden filter (Supplementary Fig. 34). Additionally, the NIR intensities could be controlled using a focusing lens, which significantly increased the maximal intensity of solar NIR from ~500 to ~5800 W m−2, whereas the intensity retained at ~4200 W m−2 with wooden filters (Fig. 6b). Notably, focusing lens with larger multiples can further increase the NIR intensity, ensuring various application scenarios.

To further investigate the feasibility of wooden filters in harnessing solar NIR for phototherapy, a xenon lamp was used to provide stable and continuous illumination, combined with a focusing lens to enhance the simulated sunlight with an NIR intensity of ~6000 W m−2 (Fig. 6c). Under this intensity of NIR irradiation, natural wood, the wooden filter, and the PET NIR filter featured excellent stability without obvious changes within 60 min (Fig. 6d and Supplementary Fig. 33b). Importantly, we found that wooden filters were able to block heat from the irradiation, outperforming commercial PET NIR filters, as recorded by an infrared thermal camera (Fig. 6e, f and Supplementary Movie 1). The results exhibited that focused irradiation quickly elevated the skin temperature, reaching a peak of 44.3 °C within 180 s, significantly exceeding the human comfort threshold. By contrast, both the wooden filter and PET NIR filter were able to reduce the increase in skin temperature. The PET NIR filter reduced the maximal skin temperature to 39.5 °C, while wooden filter maintained slight temperature fluctuations with the maximal skin temperature of 37.0 °C. Therefore, wooden filters not only possess widely controllable NIR intensity but also show a better heat blocking ability to avoid heavy tissue heat effect, demonstrating the feasibility and safety of the applications in various NIR-assisted phototherapy scenarios. Notably, natural solar irradiation is a precious and renewable resource in the energy field. According to the solar energy resources bulletin reported by CMA Wind and Solar Energy Centre55, solar irradiation is rich in China, particularly in the highlands, which also demonstrates a national average annual horizontal level total irradiation of 1496.1 kWh m−2 in 2023 (Fig. 6g). Therefore, a promising concept was proposed to process wooden filters into windows on rooftops (Supplementary Fig. 35). Compared with glass and PET NIR filter, the wooden filter was able to provides purer NIR light into the room (Fig. 6h and Supplementary Fig. 36), which may provide a natural NIR-assisted phototherapy environment driven by solar irradiation.

Discussion

Challenges in optical selectivity and facile fabrication have long persisted in traditional wood-based optical materials. In this work, we propose a lignin-enrichment approach for the rapid fabrication of highly NIR-selective wooden filters. In contrast to widely used methods for preparing transparent wood, this approach directly infiltrates lignin into wooden substrates without tedious modifications and significantly increases lignin content. Subsequent hot-pressing renders the resulting wooden filters significantly densified and stable due to structural evolution and molecular interactions, primarily including rearrangement of cellulose microfibrils and lignin molecules, self-bonding of lignin, and generation of hydrogen bonding. With the synergetic effects of enriched lignin content and highly densified structure, wooden filters are able to shield most UV–Vis light with a blocking wavelength of 600 nm but allow over 80% of NIR light to pass through, enabling to harness ~78% of NIR from solar irradiation. In addition, the lignin-enrichment approach can improve mechanical strength, thermal stability, and water-proof ability of the wooden filters, making them competent for practical applications. Given their unique optical selectivity and desirable comprehensive properties, wooden filters demonstrate the potential for the applications in optical encryption technologies and particularly in harnessing solar NIR for potential phototherapy applications. In the future, the combination of NIR harnessing and photothermal nanomaterials may further boost the development of NIR biological phototherapy technologies. Our proposed lignin-enrichment approach can not only provide a facile method for fabricating highly NIR-selective wooden filters, but also boost the development of other advanced wood-sourced materials for applications in optics and create a promising way to utilize NIR from solar irradiation.

Methods

Materials and chemicals

Balsa wood (Ochroma lagopus Sw., 94.43 ± 9.10 kg m–3), basswood (Tilia tuan Szyszyl., 426.43 ± 25.84 kg m–3), larch wood (Larix gmelinii (Rupr.) Kuzen., 452.19 ± 11.57 kg m–3), and bamboo (Phyllostachys edulis (Carrière) J. Houzeau, 560.08 ± 8.05 kg m–3) were purchased from Alibaba Co., Ltd (China). Lignin feedstock was the acetic acid lignin (with a purity of ~98.84%, Supplementary Table 1) derived from hardwood and provided by Yinnovator Biotech Co., Ltd (Guangzhou, China). Glacial acetic acid, sulfuric acid, acetic anhydride, pyridine, sodium hydroxide, hydrochloric acid, chromatography-grade tetrahydrofuran, and dimethyl sulfoxide-d6 were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). Chloroform-d, 2-chloro-4,4,5,5-tetramethyl-1,3,2-dioxaphospholane (TMDP), cyclohexanol, and chromium acetylacetonate were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Shanghai, China).

Preparation of wooden filters

The wooden filter was obtained by a lignin-enrichment approach involving lignin-enriched infiltration and densification. In lignin-enriched infiltration, balsa wood with a size of 100 × 100 × 0.75 mm3 (L × W × H) was pretreated with 1 L of boiling 90 vol% aqueous acetic acid solution containing 0.5 mL H2SO4 for 5 min to allow the solution to permeate the wood. Then, the wood was treated using 1 L of boiling 5 wt% lignin/acetic acid solution (90 vol%) containing 0.5 mL H2SO4 for 25 min. The resulting wood filled with lignin solution was subsequently placed in deionized water to remove chemicals to promote lignin self-assembly, obtaining lignin-enriched wood. In the densification process, the lignin-enriched wood was first dried at 90 °C and 5 MPa for 1 h to obtain the lignin-wood. Finally, lignin-wood was hot-pressed at 180 °C and 15 MPa for 1 h to obtain the wooden filter. Wooden filters with different lignin loading contents were produced under the same conditions, except by using the lignin/acetic acid solutions with concentrations of 1, 3, 5, and 7 wt%. Wooden filters with a two-layers thickness were obtained by hot-pressing the wet folded lignin-wood at 90 °C and 5 MPa for drying, followed by hot-pressing at 180 °C and 15 MPa for 1 h. Universality of the lignin-enrichment approach was demonstrated using basswood (hardwood), larch wood (softwood), bamboo (gramineous plant), and filter paper under the same processing conditions as wooden filters (see details in Supplementary Note 5).

Characterizations

Physical and chemical characteristics: The surface and cross-profile morphologies were observed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (SU8010, HITACHI, Ltd, Tokyo, Japan) (5 kV) and confocal Raman spectroscopy (XploRA™ PLUS, Horiba, Ltd, Japan). Two-dimensional small-angle X-ray scattering (2D SAXS) was conducted on the 1W2A station at BSRF (wavelength of 1.54 Å) equipped with a Pilatus 1MF detector. The sample-to-detector distance is 1819 mm and the exposure time is 5 s. Surface element analysis was conducted using an X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (XPS) (PHI5000 Versaprobe III XPS, Physical Electronics, Inc., USA). Electron spin resonance (ESR) was conducted on a paramagnetic resonance spectrometer (EMXplus-6/1, Bruker, Germany). The radicals produced by lignin were collected at 180 °C for 0 min and 5 min. Functional groups were determined by FTIR (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., USA) in the range of 650−4000 cm−1. Molecular structures of lignin were determined by 2D-HSQC NMR and 31P NMR on a 400 MHz spectrometer (Bruker AVIII, Germany). For HSQC spectral analysis, lignin, recycled lignin, and hot-pressed lignin were dissolved in 0.5 mL of deuterated dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO-d6). For 31P NMR spectral analysis, lignin, recycled lignin, hot-pressed lignin, and deacetylated lignin were phosphorylated by TMDP before testing. Cyclohexanol was used as an internal standard. Molecular weights of lignin, recycled lignin, and hot-pressed lignin were determined by gel permeation chromatography (GPC) (Shimadzu LC-20AD, Japan). Lignin samples were acetylated and then dissolved in tetrahydrofuran at a concentration of 5 mg mL–1. Polystyrene was used as the standard. X-ray diffraction (XRD) (Empyrean, PANalytical B.V., Almelo, Netherlands) with a Cu Kα monochromatic radiation source was used to characterize crystallization conditions with the 2θ angle ranging from 5° to 40° and a scanning speed of 5° min−1. The contents of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin were measured according to the NREL (National Renewable Energy Laboratory, USA) method56.

Properties: Transmittance of natural wood, pretreated wood, acetic acid-treated wood (AA-wood), lignin-wood, wooden filters, NIR filters in the universal experiment, and polyethylene terephthalate (PET) film from 200 to 1400 nm was recorded using a UV–Vis–NIR spectrophotometer (SolidSpec-3700, SHIMADZU Co., Ltd, Japan). Transmittance of lignin from 250 to 1350 nm was recorded after dissolving it in a 90 vol% aqueous acetic acid solution at concentrations of 1, 3, and 5 wt%. The tensile properties of natural wood, lignin-wood, and wooden filters (5 × 50 mm2, length × width), were measured using an electromechanical universal testing machine (E43.104, MTS Systems Co., Ltd, China) equipped with a 250 N load cell and a constant speed of 5 mm min−1. Water absorption was measured by the weights before and after immersing natural wood, lignin-wood, and wooden filters in deionized water for 3 days. Water contact angle (WCA) was measured using a contact angle measuring system (JC2000D1, Zhongchen Digital Technic Apparatus Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China) over a duration of 180 s at room temperature. Tensile, water absorption, and WCA tests were conducted three times. Thermal stability was measured using a thermogravimetric analyzer (TGA) (SDT Q600, Thermal Advantage Inc., USA) from 100 to 800 °C with a heating rate of 20 °C min−1 and a nitrogen atmosphere.

Applications: Photographs under NIR light were recorded using an NIR monitor with a 940 nm light source. The power densities of NIR light energy of sunlight before and after passing through natural wood, wooden filters, and PET were recorded between 8:00 and 18:00 at Beijing Forestry University, Beijing, China, on September 12, 2024. Natural sunlight was strengthened by a focusing lens to strengthen its irradiation density to verify the feasibility of controlling NIR densities under solar irradiation (13:00, Beijing Forestry University, Beijing, China, on September 14, 2024). A natural NIR-assisted therapy device was constructed according to a commercial NIR light device consisting of a light source, a heat blocking filter, an NIR filter, and a probe lens. A Xenon lamp (CEL-HXUV300, China Education Au-light Co., Ltd, China) was used as the simulated sunlight source, followed by a focusing lens that was applied to strengthen the light. Then, a wooden filter was used to block harmful ultraviolet light and most heat as well as to transport available NIR light. The stability of the NIR-assisted therapy device was tested through 1 h of continuous operation. The temperature of the irradiated site was recorded using an infrared thermal camera (325Pro, FOTRIC Inc., USA). All irradiation densities were recorded using an infrared power meter (LS122, Shenzhen Linshang Technology Co., Ltd, China) with a responsive wavelength from 1000 to 1700 nm. The radiation intensity data for the provinces in 2023 were gathered from China Wind and Solar Energy Resources Bulletin (2023) reported by CMA Wind and Solar Energy Centre.
