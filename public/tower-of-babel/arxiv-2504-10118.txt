Stochastic Multigrid Minimization for Ptychographic Phase Retrieval
Borong Zhang, Qin Li, Zichao Wendy Di

Abstract
We propose a novel stochastic multigrid minimization method for ptychographic phase retrieval. In our formulation, the challenging nonconvex and ill-posed inverse problem is recast as the iterative minimization of a quadratic surrogate model that majorizes the original objective function. Our general framework encompasses the Ptychographic Iterative Engine (PIE) family of algorithms. By efficiently solving the surrogate problem using a multigrid method, our approach delivers significant improvements in both convergence speed and reconstruction quality compared with conventional PIE techniques.

Introduction

Ptychography is a state-of-the-art coherent diffractive imaging technique that has attracted significant attention for its ability to reconstruct high-resolution, complex-valued images from a series of diffraction patterns. The technique finds applications in materials science
[

,
2

Since only the intensity of the diffracted waves is measured—and phase information is lost—the reconstruction task becomes a
phase retrieval problem
, requiring the recovery of both the amplitude and the phase of the object.

Mathematically, ptychographic phase retrieval is formulated as an inverse problem that is inherently nonconvex and ill-posed. To address these challenges, numerous algorithms have been proposed, including the well-known Ptychographical Iterative Engine (PIE) family
[
9

10

11

, among others. In this work, we present a unifying framework that subsumes the entire PIE algorithm family and studies its convergence behavior, yielding practical guidance for devising more efficient solution strategies.

To further accelerate convergence, one promising strategy is to exploit multigrid methods. These methods, which have a long history in the numerical solution of partial differential equations, exploit the inherent multi-scale structure of the underlying problem by solving it on a hierarchy of discretizations
[
12
]
. Coarse-grid approximations capture the global structure of the solution, while fine-grid corrections resolve detailed features.

In summary, we propose an efficient stochastic multigrid minimization strategy for ptychographic phase retrieval that leverages a surrogate model to reformulate the inverse problem as a tractable stochastic optimization task, thereby significantly accelerating convergence. 2.1
2D ptychographic phase retrieval

In 2D ptychographic phase retrieval, the goal is to reconstruct the object of interest
\bm{z}^{\ast}\in\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}}
(in vectorized form) from its observed diffraction patterns. The data
\bm{d}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m^{2}}

\bm{d}_{k}=|\mathcal{F}(\bm{Q}\odot P_{k}\bm{z}^{\ast})|^{2}+\epsilon_{k},\quad k=1,\ldots,N\,,

where
N

k

\bm{Q}\in\mathbb{C}^{m^{2}}

\mathcal{F}\in\mathbb{C}^{m^{2}\times m^{2}}
denotes the two-dimensional discrete Fourier transform operator,
\epsilon_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m^{2}}
corresponds to the noise associated with the
k
-th measurement,
\odot
denotes the Hadamard (element-wise) product, and
|\cdot|^{2}
represents the element-wise intensity. The diagonal elements of
P_{k}
are
1
for the columns corresponding to the illuminated pixels in the
k
-th scanning region. We also refer to
\bm{Q}\odot P_{k}\bm{z}^{\ast}
as the
k
-th exit wave. The overlap ratio refers to the fraction of the probe area that overlaps between adjacent scan positions. For example, a 50% overlap ratio means that the probe is shifted by half of its size in each direction when moving between neighboring positions. Higher overlap ratios increase data redundancy and generally improve the stability and convergence of reconstruction algorithms, at the cost of increased measurement acquisition

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{z}}\Phi(\bm{z})

\displaystyle\bm{u}\left(\bm{z}_{k}^{+}-\bm{z}_{k}^{(j)}\right)

\displaystyle=\overline{\bm{Q}}\left(\mathcal{R}_{k}\left(\bm{z}_{k}^{(j)}\right)-\bm{Q}\bm{z}_{k}^{+}\right)\,,

2.2
Multilevel optimization algorithm (
MG/OPT
)

Ptychographic phase retrieval contains an intrinsic resolution hierarchy, making it an ideal candidate for multigrid acceleration.
MG/OPT
is a multigrid solver for optimization. Given an objective function
f_{h}
defined on a fine grid, our goal is to solve
\min_{\bm{z}}f_{h}(\bm{z})
.
MG/OPT
accelerates this process by, when appropriate, lifting the problem to a coarser level where a simpler optimization problem can be solved more efficiently. By communicating information between fine and coarse grids and offloading expensive fine-grid computations onto the cheaper coarse level, the method speeds up overall convergence. It has already demonstrated faster convergence for the ptychographic phase-retrieval problem
[
20
]
. Here, we present the two-level

•

.

•

: Denotes the restriction operator (binning) that downsamples variables from the fine grid to the coarse grid via average pooling. Specifically,

0&0&1&1&0&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&0&1&1&0&\cdots&0\\
&&&&\ddots&&&&\ddots&&&\ddots\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&0&1&1&0&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&0&1&1\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{H}^{2}\times n_{h}^{2}}\,.

(5)

3
Surrogate Minimization

In this section, we introduce a surrogate objective for Eqn. (
1
). This surrogate not only majorizes Eqn. (
1
) (see Section
3.1
for a precise definition of majorization) and iteratively recovers its optimal solution, but is also sufficiently general to encompass the entire PIE family of solvers. An equivalent formulation, presented in
[
23
]
, has demonstrated superior performance on the general phase-retrieval problem.

Given
\bm{z}^{(j)}
as the solution at the
j
-th iteration of an optimizer (e.g.,
rPIE
), we define its corresponding quadratic surrogate model as:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Phi}\left(\bm{z};\bm{z}^{(j)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left\|\bm{Q}P_{k}\bm{z}-\mathcal{R}_{k}\left(P_{k}\bm{z}^{(j)}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}\,,

(7)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\widetilde{\Phi}_{k}\left(\bm{z}_{k};\bm{z}_{k}^{(j)}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left\|\bm{Q}\bm{z}_{k}-\mathcal{R}_{k}\left(\bm{z}_{k}^{(j)}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}\,.

where
\mathcal{R}_{k}
is defined in Definition
2.1
. We note that this term is ill-defined if
\theta\left(\left[\mathcal{F}\left(\bm{Q}P_{k}\bm{z}^{(j)}\right)\right]_{r}\right)=0
at some index
r
, so that we complement the original definition with the following specification,

\left[\mathcal{R}_{k}\left(P_{k}\bm{z}^{(j)}\right)\right]_{r}=\begin{cases}\left[\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\sqrt{\bm{d}_{k}}\right)\right]_{r}\,,&\text{if }j=0\,,\\
\left[\mathcal{R}_{k}\left(P_{k}\bm{z}^{(j-1)}\right)\right]_{r}\,,&\text{if }j\geq 1\,.\end{cases}

(8)

The
(j+1)
-th iteration solution is then a numerical solution to:

\bm{z}^{(j+1)}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{\bm{z}}\widetilde{\Phi}\left(\bm{z};\bm{z}^{(j)}\right)\,.

(9)

Moreover, observing that Eqn. (
7
) is expressed as a summation, it is natural to adopt a stochastic optimization strategy. Specifically, in each iteration, we shuffle the indices of the scanning regions
\{k\}_{k=1}^{N}
and update them sequentially:

\bm{z}_{k}^{(j)}\leftarrow\bm{z}_{k}^{+}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{\bm{z}_{k}}\widetilde{\Phi}_{k}\left(\bm{z}_{k};\bm{z}_{k}^{(j)}\right)\,.

(10)

One desirable feature of the surrogate model Eqn. (
7
) is that it is quadratic. Consequently, an explicit analytical solution is available. To avoid singularity caused by small or even near zero entries of
\bm{Q}
, we further relax
\widetilde{\Phi}_{k}
by adding a regularization term (similar to the PIE approach):

\bm{z}_{k}^{(j)}\leftarrow\bm{z}_{k}^{+}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{\bm{z}_{k}}\widetilde{\Phi}_{k}\left(\bm{z}_{k};\bm{z}_{k}^{(j)}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\bm{u}\cdot\left|\bm{z}_{k}-\bm{z}_{k}^{(j)}\right|^{2}\,.

(11)

Notice that the update formulation Eqn. (
11
) is equivalent to applying one step of the proximal point method
[
24
]
to Eqn. (
10
).

Proposition 3.1

(Majorization)
.

The quadratic surrogate
\widetilde{\Phi}\left(\bm{z};\bm{z}^{(j)}\right)
, as an approximation to the original objective function
\Phi(\bm{z})
in Eqn. (
1
), has the following properties:

•

\Phi(\bm{z})
and
\widetilde{\Phi}\left(\bm{z};\bm{z}^{(j)}\right)
agree at
\bm{z}^{(j)}
:

\Phi\left(\bm{z}^{(j)}\right)=\widetilde{\Phi}\left(\bm{z}^{(j)};\bm{z}^{(j)}\right)\,.

•

\Phi(\bm{z})
is dominated by
\widetilde{\Phi}\left(\bm{z};\bm{z}^{(j)}\right)
:

\Phi\left(\bm{z}\right)\leq\widetilde{\Phi}\left(\bm{z};\bm{z}^{(j)}\right)\quad\text{for all}\quad\bm{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}}\,.

(12)

•

The complex gradients of
\Phi(\bm{z})
and
\widetilde{\Phi}\left(\bm{z};\bm{z}^{(j)}\right)
agree at
\bm{z}^{(j)}
:

\nabla_{\bm{z}}\Phi\left(\bm{z}^{(j)}\right)=\nabla_{\bm{z}}\widetilde{\Phi}\left(\bm{z}^{(j)};\bm{z}^{(j)}\right)\,.

The proof for this proposition is included in Appendix
B.1
. The inequality (
12
) is the reason for the method to be termed majorization.

Furthermore, minimizing the surrogate (see Eqn. (
9
)) provides a descent direction for the original objective function, as implied in the following theorem:

Theorem 3.2

(Convergence and optimality)
.

Suppose that the quadratic surrogate
\widetilde{\Phi}\left(\bm{z};\bm{z}^{(j)}\right)
, given in Eqn. (
7
) has a global minimum
\bm{z}^{(j+1)}
. Then, for all
j=0,1,\dots
, we have

\Phi\left(\bm{z}^{(j+1)}\right)\leq\Phi\left(\bm{z}^{(j)}\right)\,.

Additionally,

\Phi\left(\bm{z}^{(j+1)}\right)=\Phi\left(\bm{z}^{(j)}\right)\iff\nabla_{\bm{z}}\Phi\left(\bm{z}^{(j+1)}\right)=0\,.

The proof for this theorem is included in Appendix
B.2
. We note that the specification made in Eqn. (
8
) is necessary in the proof for the optimality condition.

4
Stochastic Multigrid Surrogate Minimization

As demonstrated in
[
20
]
, the multigrid solver improves computational efficiency, while the surrogate majorization model provides favorable convergence properties. We now integrate these approaches into a single, tractable, and robust method, which we term the
Multilevel–Adaptive–Guided Ptychographic Iterative Engine
(
MAGPIE
). The name reflects its multigrid structure, adaptive construction of coarse-grid surrogates, and guided fine-grid reconstruction via coarse-grid search directions. We present the algorithm in Section
4.1
and defer discussion of its well-definedness, consistency, descent properties, and automatic regularization selection to Section
4.2
.

4.1
Multilevel–Adaptive–Guided Ptychographic Iterative Engine (
MAGPIE
)

In this section, we present the proposed algorithm in a two-level formulation, which can be readily extended to multiple levels.

For the
k
-th scanning region at the
j
-th iteration, we have the fine-grid surrogate model as

\min_{\bm{z}_{k}}\widetilde{\Phi}_{k}\left(\bm{z}_{k};\bm{z}_{k}^{(j)}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\bm{Q}\bm{z}_{k}-\mathcal{R}_{k}\left(\bm{z}_{k}^{(j)}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}\,,

(13)

and we define the associated coarse-grid surrogate model as:

\min_{\bm{z}_{H,k}}\widetilde{\Phi}_{H,k}\left(\bm{z}_{H,k};\bm{z}_{k}^{(j)}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\bm{Q}_{H}\bm{z}_{H,k}-\mathcal{R}_{H,k}\left(\bm{z}_{k}^{(j)}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}\,.

(14)

The coarse-grid model represents a weighted average of the fine-grid model, with precise definitions given below:

•

The restriction operator
\bm{I}_{h}^{H}
and the prolongation operator
\bm{I}_{H}^{h}
are defined in Eqn. (
5
) and Eqn. (
6

•

\bm{Q}_{H}=\bm{I}_{h}^{H}\bm{Q}\in\mathbb{C}^{(m/2)^{2}}
: downsampled probe by directly applying the restriction operator.

•

\bm{W}_{\mathcal{R}}\in\mathbb{C}^{m^{2}}

\mathcal{R}_{k}\left(\bm{z}_{k}^{(j)}\right)

•

\mathcal{R}_{H,k}\left(\bm{z}_{k}^{(j)}\right)=\bm{I}_{h}^{H}\left(\bm{W}_{\mathcal{R}}\odot\mathcal{R}_{k}\left(\bm{z}_{k}^{(j)}\right)\right)
: downsampled revised exit wave.

Both the fine- and coarse-grid subproblems are quadratic and, in principle, admit closed-form solutions. In practice, however, a direct solve is ill-posed: whenever entries of
\bm{Q}
or
\bm{Q}_{H}
vanish, the corresponding normal equations lose rank and the solution is no longer unique. As with the full surrogate problem in Eqn. (

\bm{u}
and coarse-grid regularization
\bm{u}_{H}

•

\bm{W}_{\bm{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m^{2}}_{\geq 0}
: constant weight vector for downsampling the current object estimate
\bm{z}_{k}^{(j)}
.

•

\bm{z}_{H,k}^{(j)}=\bm{I}_{h}^{H}\left(\bm{W}_{\bm{z}}\odot\bm{z}_{k}^{(j)}\right)
: downsampled current object estimate by weighted average pooling.

•

\bm{W}_{\bm{u}}^{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{(m/2)^{2}}_{\geq 0}
: constant weight vector for downsampling regularization term
\bm{u}
.

•

\bm{u}_{H}=\bm{W}_{\bm{u}}^{H}\odot\bm{I}_{h}^{H}\bm{u}_{h}
: the coarse-grid regularization term.

First, we derive the update for the coarse-grid model using the proximal point method in Eqn. (
14

\hat{\bm{z}}_{H,k}^{(j)}=\bm{z}_{H,k}^{(j)}+\frac{\overline{\bm{Q}_{H}}}{\bm{u}_{H}+|\bm{Q}_{H}|^{2}}\left(\mathcal{R}_{H,k}\left(\bm{z}_{k}^{(j)}\right)-\bm{Q}_{H}\bm{z}_{H,k}^{(j)}\right)\,,

Correspondingly, the fine-grid update drawn from the coarse level is:

\widetilde{\bm{z}}_{k}^{(j)}=\bm{z}_{k}^{(j)}+\bm{I}_{H}^{h}\underbrace{\left(\hat{\bm{z}}_{H,k}^{(j)}-\bm{z}_{H,k}^{(j)}\right)}_{\bm{e}^{(j)}_{H,k}}=\bm{z}_{k}^{(j)}+\bm{I}_{H}^{h}\bm{e}^{(j)}_{H,k}\,.

(15)

Then, we apply the proximal point method on Eqn. (
13
) at the fine level with a regularization as:

\bm{z}_{k}^{(j)}\leftarrow\bm{z}_{k}^{+}=\widetilde{\bm{z}}_{k}^{(j)}+\frac{\overline{\bm{Q}}}{\bm{u}+|\bm{Q}|^{2}}\left(\mathcal{R}_{k}\left(\bm{z}_{k}^{(j)}\right)-\bm{Q}\widetilde{\bm{z}}_{k}^{(j)}\right)\,.

For computational purposes, the weights (
\bm{W}_{\bm{z}}
,
\bm{W}_{\mathcal{R}}
, and
\bm{W}_{\bm{u}}

16

4.2
.

We term this multigrid solver for each surrogate model the

4.2
Converging properties of
MAGPIE
and weight selection

Different choices of the weight vectors lead to different performance of the algorithm. Now we specify our choices of the weight vectors below. These definitions of
\bm{W}_{\bm{z}}
,
\bm{W}_{\mathcal{R}}
, and
\bm{W}_{\bm{u}}^{H}
ensure the robust performance of
MAGPIE
.

\bm{W}_{\bm{z}}=\frac{|\bm{Q}|^{2}}{\bm{I}_{H}^{h}\bm{I}_{h}^{H}|\bm{Q}|^{2}}\,,\quad\bm{W}_{\mathcal{R}}=\frac{(\bm{I}_{H}^{h}\bm{Q}_{H})\odot\bm{W}_{\bm{z}}}{\bm{Q}}=\frac{(\bm{I}_{H}^{h}\bm{Q}_{H})\odot|\bm{Q}|^{2}}{\bm{Q}\odot\left(\bm{I}_{H}^{h}\bm{I}_{h}^{H}|\bm{Q}|^{2}\right)}\,,\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{W}_{\bm{u}}^{H}=\frac{|\bm{Q}_{H}|^{2}}{\bm{I}_{h}^{H}|\bm{Q}|^{2}}\,.

(16)

As will be shown more clearly in Propositions
4.1
,
4.2
, and
4.4
, and in Theorem
4.3
, these specific forms not only prevent singularities, maintain coarse-to-fine consistency, and preserve the descent property, but also ensure stability.

It is worth noting that the quantity
\bm{I}_{H}^{h}\bm{I}_{h}^{H}|\bm{Q}|^{2}
appears in both
\bm{W}_{\bm{z}}
and
\bm{W}_{\mathcal{R}}
. Its physical meaning is straightforward: in each
2\times 2
bin of
|\bm{Q}|^{2}
,
\bm{I}_{H}^{h}\bm{I}_{h}^{H}|\bm{Q}|^{2}
computes the average of the four entries and upsamples that average back to the original four positions, whereas
|\bm{Q}|^{2}
each entry’s original value. As a result,

\bm{z}_{H,k}^{(j)}=\bm{I}_{h}^{H}\left(\bm{W}_{\bm{z}}\bm{z}_{k}^{(j)}\right)

performs a bin-wise weighted average of
\bm{z}_{k}^{(j)}
, assigning greater weight to regions where
\bm{Q}
has higher magnitude (i.e., higher signal-to-noise ratio).

We first show that all weight vectors are element-wise bounded.

Proposition 4.1

(Well-definedness)
.

The element-wise magnitudes of the weight vectors defined in Eqn.(
16
) are bounded. In particular:

\left\|\bm{W}_{\bm{z}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq 4\,,\quad\left\|\bm{W}_{\mathcal{R}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq 4\,,\quad\text{and}\quad\left\|\bm{W}_{\bm{u}}^{H}\right\|_{\infty}\leq 1\,,

where each weight vector’s infinity norm equals the maximum magnitude among its entries.

See Appendix
E
for the proof.

We now show that the coarse-grid surrogate model is consistent with the fine-grid surrogate model.

Proposition 4.2

(Consistency)
.

Given the weight vectors defined in Eqn. (
16
), the coarse-level objective
\widetilde{\Phi}_{H,k}
(as defined in Eqn. (
14
)) is consistent with the fine-level objective
\widetilde{\Phi}_{k}
(as defined in Eqn. (
13
)) in the sense that, for all
\bm{z}_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{m^{2}}
, given
\bm{z}_{H,k}=\bm{I}_{h}^{H}\left(\bm{W}_{\bm{z}}\bm{z}_{k}\right)
, we have:

•

\widetilde{\Phi}_{H,k}\left(\bm{z}_{H,k};\bm{z}_{k}^{(j)}\right)\leq\frac{1}{4}\left\|\bm{W}_{\mathcal{R}}\right\|_{\infty}^{2}\widetilde{\Phi}_{k}\left(\bm{z}_{k};\bm{z}_{k}^{(j)}\right)
.

•

\left\|\nabla_{\bm{z}_{H,k}}\widetilde{\Phi}_{H,k}\left(\bm{z}_{H,k};\bm{z}_{k}^{(j)}\right)\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\|\bm{W}_{\bm{u}}^{H}\right\|_{\infty}\,\left\|\nabla_{\bm{z}_{k}}\widetilde{\Phi}_{k}\left(\bm{z}_{k};\bm{z}_{k}^{(j)}\right)\right\|_{2}
.

The proof for this proposition is included in Appendix
F
.

Furthermore, the parameters are chosen so that the coarse-grid solution of Eqn. (
14
), obtained via the proximal point method, provides a descent direction for the fine-level problem. This is formalized in the following theorem.

Theorem 4.3

(Descent property)
.

Given the weight vectors defined in Eqn. (
16
), a single proximal-point sweep on the coarse surrogate (
14
), initialized at
\bm{z}^{\prime}_{H,k}=\bm{I}_{h}^{H}(\bm{W}_{\bm{z}}\bm{z}^{\prime}_{k})
, provides a descent direction for the fine-grid surrogate (
13
) at
\bm{z}^{\prime}_{k}
. That is:

\nabla_{\bm{z}_{k}}\widetilde{\Phi}_{k}\left(\bm{z}_{k}^{\prime};\bm{z}_{k}^{(j)}\right)^{*}\bm{I}_{H}^{h}\left(\bm{e}_{H,k}\right)\leq 0\,,

where
\bm{I}_{H}^{h}\left(\bm{e}_{H,k}\right)=\bm{I}_{H}^{h}\left(\widehat{\bm{z}_{H,k}}-\bm{z}_{H,k}^{\prime}\right)
, and
\widehat{\bm{z}_{H,k}}
solves the proximal point on the coarse level:

\widehat{\bm{z}_{H,k}}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{\bm{z}_{H,k}}\widetilde{\Phi}_{H,k}\left(\bm{z}_{H,k};\bm{z}_{k}^{(j)}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\bm{u}_{H}\cdot\left|\bm{z}_{H,k}-\bm{z}_{H,k}^{\prime}\right|^{2}\,.

(17)

Moreover,

\displaystyle\bm{e}_{H,k}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\overline{\bm{Q}_{H}}}{\bm{u}_{H}+|\bm{Q}_{H}|^{2}}\left(\bm{Q}_{H}\bm{z}_{H,k}^{\prime}-\mathcal{R}_{H,k}\left(\bm{z}_{k}^{(j)}\right)\right)

(18)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\bm{u}_{H}+|\bm{Q}_{H}|^{2}}\frac{|\bm{Q}_{H}|^{2}}{\bm{I}_{h}^{H}|\bm{Q}|^{2}}\bm{I}_{h}^{H}\left(\nabla_{\bm{z}_{k}}\widetilde{\Phi}_{k}\left(\bm{z}_{k}^{\prime};\bm{z}_{k}^{(j)}\right)\right)\,.

The proof for this theorem is included in Appendix
G.1
.

Whenever a model is represented on both fine and coarse grids, parameters fixed on the fine grid must have consistent counterparts on the coarse grid. We show below that, with
\bm{W}_{\bm{z}},\bm{W}_{\mathcal{R}},\bm{W}_{\bm{u}}^{H}
designed as in (
16
), the regularization term can be transferred automatically between levels, requiring no manual tuning for the coarse-grid regularization term
\bm{u}_{H}
.

Proposition 4.4

(Automatic regularization selection)
.

Given a fine-grid regularization term
\bm{u}
and the weight vector
\bm{W}_{\bm{u}}^{H}
defined in Eqn. (
16
), by choosing

\bm{u}_{H}=\bm{W}_{\bm{u}}^{H}\bm{I}_{h}^{H}\bm{u}\,,

the induced coarse-grid regularization term
\bm{u}_{H}
ensures that, for each scanning region
k
, there exist mappings

\bm{e}_{k}:\mathbb{C}^{m^{2}}\to\mathbb{C}^{m^{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{e}_{H,k}:\mathbb{C}^{m^{2}}\to\mathbb{C}^{(m/2)^{2}}\,,

such that:

•

The mappings
\bm{e}_{k}(\bm{g})
and
\bm{e}_{H,k}(\bm{g})
yield the fine-grid update at
\bm{z}_{k}^{\prime}
and the coarse-grid update at
\bm{z}_{H,k}^{\prime}=\bm{I}_{h}^{H}(\bm{W}_{\bm{z}}\bm{z}_{k}^{\prime})
, respectively, for the fine-grid gradient
\bm{g}=\nabla_{\bm{z}_{k}}\widetilde{\Phi}_{k}(\bm{z}_{k}^{\prime};\,\bm{z}_{k}^{(j)})
.

•

For any coordinate index
r=1,\dots,(m/2)^{2}
, the coarse-grid update satisfies

\max_{\|\bm{g}_{1}\|_{\infty}\leq 1}\left|\bm{e}_{H,k}(\bm{g}_{1})\right|_{r}=\max_{\|\bm{g}_{2}\|_{\infty}\leq 1}\left|\bm{I}_{h}^{H}\,\bm{e}_{k}(\bm{g}_{2})\right|_{r}\,.

Thus, provided the fine-grid update is stable under a properly tuned regularization term, the maximum magnitude of the coarse-grid update is automatically controlled.

The proof for this proposition is included in Appendix
H
.

6
Conclusion

We have presented
MAGPIE
, a stochastic multigrid minimization framework for ptychographic phase retrieval that accelerates PIE-type updates via rigorously constructed coarse-grid surrogate models. The surrogate guarantees monotonic descent of the original nonconvex objective with sublinear convergence, embeds a multilevel hierarchy in which coarse-grid regularization terms are automatically selected by fine–coarse consistency, and retains the standard
rPIE
parameter set while significantly improving both convergence speed and reconstruction fidelity.

While we have employed a specific downsampling strategy, alternative approaches are possible. For example, one may define

\bm{Q}_{H}=\bm{M}_{h}^{H}(\bm{Q})\,,\quad\bm{z}_{H,k}=\bm{I}_{h}^{H}\bm{z}_{k}\,,\quad\text{and }\ \bm{W}_{\mathcal{R}}=\frac{\bm{I}_{H}^{h}\bm{Q}_{H}}{\bm{Q}}\,,

where
\bm{M}_{h}^{H}
selects the minimum-norm entry in each bin. In such variants, the properties established in Section
4.2
still hold, although their analysis becomes more challenging.

Looking ahead, we will extend the theoretical framework to blind ptychography and integrate a minibatch strategy for GPU-accelerated real-time feedback. Moreover, the multigrid-surrogate paradigm developed here is readily adaptable to other large-scale phase retrieval and coherent diffractive imaging applications.
