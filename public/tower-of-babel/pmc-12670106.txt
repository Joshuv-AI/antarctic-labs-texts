Compliance by code: The need for automated protocols in breath alcohol testing – case reports
Authors: Aaron Olson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe accuracy and reliability of forensic breath alcohol testing depends on adherence to established protocols for observation periods and operator responses to instrument status messages. This paper presents two case reports illustrating procedural errors following status messages produced by the DataMaster DMT breath alcohol analyzer. In both cases, operators failed to follow the required procedures after receiving messages indicating the presence of mouth alcohol or interfering substances. These cases demonstrate how operator discretion and failure to follow established protocols can result in unreliable or inadmissible results. The findings reinforce the need for automated software controls that eliminate operator discretion in breath alcohol testing procedures. Implementing automated protocols would enhance quality assurance, reduce legal challenges, and improve the scientific validity of breath alcohol testing in forensic contexts.Keywords: Breath alcohol, Automation, Mouth alcohol, Interfering substances, Operator error, Quality assurance

Introduction

Breath alcohol testing is a widely used tool in impaired driving cases. Its reliability depends on proper instrument calibration and maintenance, as well as adherence to testing protocols that prevent confounding factors such as mouth alcohol contamination and interfering substances [1,2].

Modern laboratories increasingly rely on automation to reduce human error, and ensure consistent application of analytical protocols [[3], [4], [5]]. Automation removes subjective decision-making from procedural steps, reduces the potential for contamination or protocol deviations, and provides documentation of compliance that supports legal admissibility.

Current challenges in breath alcohol testing automation

Despite well-defined procedures, breath alcohol testing remains vulnerable to operator error. When operators encounter error messages, they often proceed with another test instead of consulting the manual or restarting the required observation period.

In breath alcohol testing, a 15 or 20 min (depending on the jurisdiction) pretest observation period is critical to ensure the subject does not burp, regurgitate, vomit, or introduce any substance into the mouth [6]. Failure to observe this protocol can falsely elevate a result [7]. Evidential breath analyzers such as the DataMaster DMT attempt to detect mouth alcohol contamination through algorithmic safeguards. When contamination is detected, the operator should restart the observation period to allow any mouth alcohol to dissipate. However, this often does not occur in practice.

Similarly, when a DataMaster DMT produces an Interference message, this indicates that a compound other than ethyl alcohol is present on the subject's breath. The Minnesota DMT Operator Training Manual states:

“The breath sample submitted may contain a substance other than alcohol or a combination of alcohol and an interfering substance. Obtain a warrant and collect blood or urine for analysis [8].”

The DataMaster DMT's interference algorithm uses a cut-off threshold; compounds below that threshold may still contribute to elevated reported alcohol concentrations without triggering an interference flag [9]. Consequently, continuing to test the subject does not necessarily restore scientific integrity, even if the subsequent tests does not trigger an interference flag. The same interfering substance may be present, yet due to individual instrument characteristics, it may not trigger the cut-off threshold on a second test. Continuing to collect breath samples following an Interference message may produce falsely elevated results and violate approved procedures due to substances other than alcohol being present on the subject's breath [10].

Methods

The case reports included in this paper were selected from 2024 Minnesota impaired driving cases in which the author was retained as a forensic toxicology consultant to evaluate and interpret breath test results that were conducted on the DataMaster DMT (Intoximeters, Inc., St. Louis, MO, USA). Each case was chosen because it demonstrated significant procedural deviations following instrument status messages. The cases represent real-world examples of how operator discretion and protocol noncompliance can affect the validity of evidential breath alcohol testing.

All identifying information was removed, and only technical aspects relevant to the procedural errors and their forensic implications are described. Both cases were documented through official breath test records, instrument printouts, and observation notes reviewed as part of expert case evaluations.

Case reports

Case 1: interference status message

A subject received an Interference status message on the DataMaster DMT instrument during two consecutive breath tests. Instead of following the directive in the Minnesota DMT Operator Training Manual to obtain an alternative biological sample, the operator moved to another instrument and obtained a numerical alcohol result.

By failing to follow the training, the operator risked producing an artificially elevated result caused by a non-ethanol interfering compound. The absence of an interference flag does not confirm the absence of an interfering substance. Rather, it only indicates that interfering compounds fell below the flagging threshold.

Following the proper procedure by obtaining a blood or urine sample would have ensured a scientifically valid and legally defensible result. The decision to continue breath testing contradicted established training and potentially elevated the subject's final result.

Case 2: mouth alcohol contamination and observation period violation

In another case, a subject's breath testing sequence produced four Invalid status messages, accompanied by expirograms with a negative slope, consistent with mouth alcohol contamination as seen in Fig. 1.

During testing, the subject stated, “Sorry for burping,” confirming that contamination likely occurred. Despite this, the operator continued testing without restarting the observation period.

The DataMaster DMT operator stated, “I think the first one possibly could have been detected with mouth alcohol, so you might not even be that high.” This statement reflects a recognition that contamination may have occurred, yet the operator failed to perform a new observation period.

In addition, the subject was allowed to use the restroom unobserved, violating the continuous observation requirement. During an observation period, subjects must be carefully observed for at least 15 minutes prior to and during testing to ensure no burping, vomiting, regurgitation, or ingestion of any foreign substances occurs. If any of these events take place, the observation period must restart [6]. On the fifth and final test, a numerical result was obtained, but its validity was questionable due to the flawed observation period and repeated indications of mouth alcohol contamination.

Discussion

Legal precedent and protocol adherence

The two cases presented highlight two common failures in adherence to established procedures in breath alcohol testing. In both examples, the operators' actions deviated from their training and quality assurance practices in breath alcohol testing. These deviations compromised the test's validity and introduced doubt about the accuracy of reported alcohol concentrations.

The importance of proper procedure has been reinforced by recent judicial precedent. In Gackle v. North Dakota Department of Transportation (2025), the North Dakota Supreme Court ruled that failure to observe the full required observation period before retesting constituted a fundamental deviation from approved methods and rendered the results inadmissible [11].

It is important to recognize that observation period requirements differ by jurisdiction. Minnesota requires a minimum of 15 minutes, while North Dakota requires 20 minutes. These variations demonstrate the need for a standardized, automated system to consistently enforce observation and testing protocols, ensuring that all subjects are tested under uniform scientific conditions.

Automation as a corrective measure

Manual enforcement of procedural rules leaves too much room for human error. Automated systems can be programmed to ensure compliance by:

While automation may extend testing duration, the priority must always be the generation of accurate and scientifically valid results. Expedience should not take precedence over the integrity of the results.

Even with automation, operators must still remain responsible for oversight, subject observation, and documentation; automation simply standardizes procedural enforcement to prevent inadvertent or intentional deviations. If the subject is uncooperative or intentionally interfering with testing, the appropriate course of action is then to obtain an alternative biological sample rather than continue testing outside quality assurance protocols.

Automation should be implemented within a networked system so that an instrument generating an Interference or Invalid status message can communicate a temporary lockout for testing that individual across the network. This would prevent operators from bypassing the protocol by switching instruments.

Recommendations

These case reports highlight the potential for human error arising from the lack of automation in breath-testing protocols. While retraining and discipline are essential components of quality assurance, they are reactive and address errors only after they occur. Automation, by contrast, embeds procedural safeguards within the instrument, preventing errors from occurring in the first place and reducing the need for remedial training.

Focusing solely on correcting individual errors through retraining or discipline does not address the underlying problem. Automation can enforce observation periods and prevent retesting after “Invalid” or “Interference” messages. By embedding these safeguards into the software rather than relying on human judgment, errors can be prevented before they occur. While automation cannot replace the human operator, it should support them by embedding quality assurance steps within the instrument's software.

An automated systems-based approach would improve consistency and scientific reliability for breath alcohol tests. It would also strengthen confidence in the testing process by ensuring that every test is performed in accordance with established standards, regardless of who operates the instrument.

Conclusion

These cases demonstrate that even trained operators can deviate from established procedures, potentially compromising the scientific and legal integrity of breath alcohol results. Both cases show that manual systems relying on operator discretion are inherently vulnerable to human error.

Automation provides a practical solution by removing subjectivity from the process and ensuring compliance with established protocols. Automated systems would protect legal admissibility and enhance confidence in the results of breath alcohol tests.
