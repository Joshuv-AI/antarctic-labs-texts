“Investigating impact of Ni-Cd substitution on structural magnetic and optical properties of nanosize Al ferrites”
Authors: Shikhil S. Wanjari, Deoram V. Nandanwar, K.G. Rewatkar, Amit V. Gongal
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Ferrites are among the most extensively studied materials, specifically due to their amazing and diverse characteristics. Therefore, we synthesized NixCd1-xAlyFe2-yO4 (NCAF) ferrites using a microwave auto-combustion process, with x varying from 0.0 to 1.0 and y set at 0.1. We conducted a meticulous characterization of their structural, surface morphological, magnetic, molecular vibrational and optical properties. X-ray diffraction confirms the lattice parameter and a single-phase spinel structure. Rietveld refined XRD patterns identified the Fd-3m space group cubic spinel phase. HR-TEM examination shows pseudo-spherical particles with an average size of 40–45 nm. Surface topography exhibits tiny, spherical microstructures. The lognormal histogram showed an average particle size of 56.6 nm–124.8 nm, decreasing with nickel concentration. At a nickel concentration (x) of 0.6, saturation magnetization (Ms) increases and then decreases at 1.0. FTIR confirms no organic phase and spinel development. Absorption measurements determined the band gap energy, which Tauc plot revealed to be 2.5870–2.1657 eV. We used the Kubelka-Munk function to find the band gap energy in the UV–Vis diffuse reflectance spectra (DRS), which was between 2.0027 and 1.6817 eV.

Introduction

Ferrites are magnetic dielectric substances characterized by high resistance. They are employed in various applications, including inductors, memory cores, high-frequency transformers, and recording heads. Furthermore, their enhanced resistance and more affordable pricing have established them as superior magnetic materials compared to pure metals. Nickel ferrites that have aluminium added to them have higher electrical resistivity and lower eddy current losses, which means they can be used in microwave devices [1]. A significant rise in interest has emerged about the replacement of diamagnetic materials with mixed ferrites. Researchers have observed that the inclusion of non-magnetic ions in spinel ferrites alters their electrical and magnetic properties. Research has yielded significant insights into the exchange interaction, magnetization orientation, cation distribution, spin canting, and other pertinent factors [2].

Recently, many researchers began investigating magnetic nanoparticles composed of spinel ferrites because of their intriguing electrical and magnetic properties. Applications utilize spinel-type oxides, characterized by the general formula AB2O4. They serve as magnetic bulk cores, magnetic fluids, microwave absorbers, catalysts, and in medical diagnostics [[3], [4], [5]]. CdFe2O4 has a unique spinel structure, with Cd2+ ions occupying the tetrahedral (A) sites and Fe3+ cations occupying the octahedral sites. People regard polycrystalline ferrites as superior magnetic dielectric materials. The way they are made, the chemicals they contain, the sintering temperature and environment, and the type and distribution of cations between tetrahedral and octahedral sites are just some of the things that can change their physical properties [[6], [7], [8]].

Ni-Cd substituted Al ferrites (NixCd1-xAlyFe2-yO4) reveal significant properties that make them advantageous for a range of applications. The incorporation of Ni and Cd can significantly impact the magnetic characteristics, including saturation magnetization, coercivity, and magnetic anisotropy, rendering them suitable for various magnetic applications. Ferrites exhibit ferrimagnetic properties, facilitating distinctive interactions among various cations, which can result in enhanced performance for applications such as data storage and magnetic sensors. The capacity to establish stable ferrite phases across a spectrum of compositions ensures uniform performance in diverse applications. Their higher thermal stability renders them appropriate for applications involving high temperatures. The interplay of magnetic, electrical, and thermal properties facilitates applications across various domains, such as catalysis, energy storage, and microwave technology. Nickel and cadmium exhibit a notable abundance, which serves to decrease material expenses and improve sustainability in manufacturing processes. The capacity to precisely adjust the cation distribution within the lattice structure can result in significant changes in the material properties, facilitating the optimization for focused on applications [9]. Advancements in technology suggest that these materials may have significant potential for future applications in spintronics, sensors, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). The distinctive interplay of adjustable magnetic characteristics, coupled with their structural integrity and thermal toughness, positions Ni-Cd substituted Al ferrites as a fascinating topic for comprehensive investigation.

The substitution of Ni–Cd has been utilized in various practical applications, ranging from power quality enhancement and emergency reserves to telecommunications and portable services. The primary limitations of nickel-cadmium batteries include their comparatively raised initial investment and the challenges related to disposal management, caused by the hazardous nature of the heavy metals involved, namely nickel and cadmium. It is also said that other advances in the future may address the memory effect, sensitivity to overcharging, and slightly lower efficiency as additional limiting factors [10]. The implications of cadmium in Ni-Cd substituted Al ferrites carries significant implications that transcend mere technical characteristics, influencing environmental factors, regulatory frameworks, and public health issues. Although these ferrites exhibit beneficial performance attributes, the inherent risks demand thorough assessment and advancement in material design to guarantee safety and adherence to evolving standards. Advancing in this domain, prioritizing the development of cadmium-free alternatives is essential for enhancing the versatility of these ferrites and reducing their ecological footprint.

Nickel ferrite (NiFe2O4) has an inverted spinel structure. Substitution nickel with cadmium results in the creation of cadmium ferrite (CdFe2O4), which has a regular spinel structure. Furthermore, it has been determined that the magnetic saturation of nickel ferrite (Ni1-xCdxFe2O4) can be maximized through the progressive replacement of non-magnetic Cd2+ ions. We observe the peak saturation at x = 0.3, and any further increase in Cd2+ concentration leads to a decrease in magnetization [11]. Anjum et al. [12] Concentrated on the structural and magnetic properties of Cd-substituted Ni-Al spinel ferrites. The distribution of aluminum (Al) at sites A and B varies with changes in the value of x. The alterations have influenced the structural and magnetic properties of the ferrites. Batoo et al. [2] researched the electrical and dielectric characteristics of nanocrystalline Ni0.2Cd0.3Fe2.5-xAlxO4 ferrites, which exhibit a single-phase cubic spinel structure. The lattice parameter decreased with increasing Al content, as indicated by the results. Additionally, they noted that the electrical conductivity and grain size increased with rising temperature. Kamali et al. [13] used magnetic Compton scattering and first-principle simulations to look at how Al3+ was distributed in NiFe2-xAlxO4, which confirmed the values that had already been seen. The results reveal that the magnetic properties of nickel ferrite decrease with the replacement of the aluminum component. Thus, analyzing the effect of substituting a slight amount of the Al3+ cation on the magnetic properties of Ni0.7Cd0.3Fe2O4 is convincing given its enhanced magnetism compared to nickel ferrite [14].

Several approaches exist for material synthesis, including hydrothermal, solid-state, co-precipitation, sol-gel, and auto-combustion techniques. Microwaves are useful because they can quickly and evenly heat things, speed up reactions, increase chemical yields, shorten reaction times, and make pure, uniform products [15]. These properties make them useful for making different organic and inorganic compounds with different structures. Using the sol-gel auto-combustion method, Mande et al. [16] created nanocrystalline nickel ferrite nanoparticles that were mixed with zinc and aluminum. These particles have the formula Ni1-xZnxFe2-yAlyO4. Their research indicates a substantial decrease in synthesis duration, up to 60 times less than that mandated by conventional methods.

This research marks a notable progression in the comprehension of NixCd1-xAlyFe2-yO4 (with x varying from 0.0 to 1.0 and y held constant at 0.1) by probing a unique compositional domain that has not been thoroughly examined in earlier studies. Previous reports indicate that a limited number of authors have conducted research on vibrational spectroscopy, magnetic studies, SEM, and TEM analysis. No studies have been identified that involve UV analysis and Rietveld refinement concerning Wykoff's positions of cations in NiCdAl ferrites. Prior investigations indicate that the incorporation of particular atoms into the SF structures can facilitate the formation of nanoparticles exhibiting a range of microstructural and magnetic characteristics. The precise adjustment of cation distribution and the deliberate alteration of nickel and cadmium concentrations create opportunities for optimizing magnetic characteristics, which are essential for possible uses in sophisticated materials like ferrites for electronic applications, catalysis, magnetic devices, data storage solutions, sensors, and energy storage systems. This study investigates the effects of nickel substitution in the CdAl spinel ferrite system, utilizing the microwave-assisted Sol-Gel method. The focus is on how varying the nickel content (NixCd1-xAlyFe2-yO4, with x from 0.0 to 1.0 and y fixed at 0.1) influences the microstructural, morphological, magnetic, and optical properties of the spinel structure, while also considering the associated potential implications of nickel, cadmium, and aluminum.

Synthesis of MNPs

A microwave-assisted sol-gel auto-combustion method was adopted for the synthesis of a series of nickel-doped cadmium ferrites, NixCd1-xAl0.1Fe1.9O4. A lightning-fast, affordable and low-energy method for synthesizing the NPs. This method has multiple advantages, which are reduction in reaction time, highly effective consistency, control over aggregation and significantly more effective diffusion access. The synthesis approach involves the spontaneous combustion of redox mixtures, providing urea as a reducing reactant and metal nitrates as an oxidizing reactant. We adopted the concept of propellant chemistry to determine the initial composition of the metal nitrates and urea solution, taking into account the total oxidizing and reducing valences of the oxidizer and fuel. The precursors utilized were of high purity: nickel nitrate {Ni(NO3)2.6H2O}, cadmium nitrate {Cd(NO3)2.6H2O}, aluminium nitrate {Al(NO3)3.9H2O}, ferric nitrate {Fe(NO3)3.9H2O}, and urea. We prepared the solution by dissolving stoichiometric quantities of each precursor in 30 ml deionized water [1]. We carried out calculations using the oxidizing and reducing values of several elements to determine the molar ratios between metal nitrates and the reducing agent. The chemicals used were AR-grade, from Sigma-Aldrich.

In the next stage, the solution was heated progressively to 80 °C with constant stirring. On an optimum temperature of 80 °C, it continued to operate at this temperature beneath continual stirring while being heated. Three hours later, the solution changed from a liquid to a low-viscosity gel. The low-viscosity gel eventually transforms into the viscous gel and begins to bubble. We immediately heated the gel in a microwave until it turned into a limp charcoal, following a period of time during which it continued to react with the environment. We obtained the ad hoc, airy material and pulverized it in a pestle and mortar. The grinded powder underwent a 4hr thermal treatment at 800 °C before being reground into a fine powder using a pestle and mortar. The sintered material was ready for further analysis [15].

Sample characterization

The powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) patterns have been detected using a secondary monochromator-equipped Philips PW1800 diffractometer and Cu Kα radiation in Bragg−Brentano mode and θ−2θ geometry. The HTXRD patterns were determined using Cu Kα radiation and Bragg−Brentano mode and θ−2θ geometry on a PANalytical X'Pert Pro equipped with a high-temperature chamber (Brucker-D8 Advance). The Rietveld refinement was executed via the utilization of the Full-prof program method. The patterns were analyzed via a refined Thompson−Cox−Hastings pseudo-Voigt function peak shape function.

Analytical high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (Philips CM-200-A) working at 200 kV was utilized to perform TEM imaging. Applying a scanning electron microscope (JSM-6380A), the morphological analysis was carried out.

Evaluating the magnetic properties of the prepared nanoparticles, the Lakeshore 7400 series vibrating sample magnetometer (VSM) is used.

The infrared spectra of the particles were done by a Thermo Nicolet iS50 fitted with an ATR-SHIMADZU Fourier transformer infrared spectrophotometer, in the range of 375–4000 cm−1.

The particles' UV–Vis reflection spectra were obtained using an ILN-725 integrating sphere and a JASCO V-670 spectrophotometer in UV–Vis reflection mode. Meanwhile, the films' transmission spectra were determined using standard mode. The determination of the band gaps of the samples were conducted using the reflectance of the powder.

The crystallite size decreases due to defects in the crystalline structure, although it increases as stability and coalescence occur at higher doping levels. This complex interaction well calibrates the relation of doping concentration, lattice stability, and crystallite growth dynamics.

The substitution of different cations alters the lattice parameters through ionic size shifts, strains, and crystal structure distortions, leading to a significant shift in peak locations in the XRD patterns of Ni-Cd-substituted Al ferrites. The ionic radius generally decreases as positive charges increase and increases as negative charges increase. As reported earlier by Desai, Rucha et al. [40], the non-saturating behaviour observed in the curve indicates the presence of two components: (i) ferro/ferromagnetic and (ii) super-paramagnetic or paramagnetic. The anisotropy constant (K), squareness ratio (SQR = Mr/Ms), coercivity (Hc), and saturation magnetization (Ms) are among the magnetic parameters obtained from the hysteresis loop [41]. The increase in the value of Ms (up to x = 0.6) can be attributed to the cation distribution between octahedral (B) and tetrahedral (A) sites, as well as the crystallite size, as the substituent atom (Ni2+) increases. Under Neel's two-sub-lattice model, the magnetic moment orientation of the A-site is antiparallel, whereas the spins of the B-site are collinear. Consequently, the combined magnetization is denoted as M = MB-MA, where MA and MB represent the magnetic moments at the A- and B-sites, respectively. Ni2+ ions exhibit a preference for the B-site, while Cd2+ ions prefer the A-site. In contrast, Al3+ and Fe3+ ions are equally distributed in both the A and B-sites, and Ni2+ ions consistently opt for the octahedral B-site. In the present samples, the Fe3+ ions are substituted with Al3+ and Ni2+ ions. The Ms values of substituted compositions are observed to be greater than those of the progenitor composition. The observed phenomenon can be elucidated by considering the impact of cation distribution variations on the strength of magnetic interaction. To determine the overall magnetization, it is possible to recognize the subsequent cation distribution, [Cd1-x Al0.02 Fe0.18]A [Fe1.72 Al0.08 Nix]BO42-. Since the magnetic moment of Ni2+ is less than that of Fe3+, an increase in the concentration of Ni2+ results in a concomitant increase in magnetization at the B-site. Subsequently, the overall magnetization diminishes after increasing until x = 0.6. The confirmation of the occupation of the B-site by Ni2+ is supported by the low value of the calculated magnetic moment. The decrease in Ms after an increase at x = 0.6 may be attributed to the formation of multi domain crystals, although the specific mechanism remains unknown [43]. Furthermore, the band gap energies of the nanoparticles decreased as the average crystallite size decreased. This reduction in size resulted in a decrease in the confinement dimension, which in turn led to discrete energy levels in the materials. Additionally, the band gap broadened, which ultimately led to a decrease in the gap energy. Fig. 11(c) and (d) depict these findings, which we attribute to the smaller particle size of the transition metal. UV–visible diffused reflectance spectroscopy (DRS)

Using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer, we performed the analysis of UV–Visible diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (DRS) at room temperature on the NixCd1-xAl0.1Fe1.9O4 sample; where x values vary at 0.0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, and 1.0 across the wavelength range of 200–800 nm. Fig. 12(a) illustrates the reflectance curves (%) as a function of wavelength (λ). The UV–Vis DRS technique is preferred over UV–Vis absorption spectroscopy for the examination of the optical characteristics of powdered samples, due to its reduced sensitivity to scattering phenomena compared to absorption methods. We investigated the optical characteristics of the synthesized series of Ni-Cd nanoparticles (NPs) with varying Ni2+ concentrations using UV–Vis spectroscopy. We determined the bandgap (Eg) value by applying the Kubelka-Munk function F(R). The vertical axis of the Kubelka-Munk function corresponds to the absorption constant (α). The modified Tauc's relation consists of three variables, namely α, F(R), and R, which represent the absorption constant, the Kubelka-Munk function, and diffuse reflectance, respectively. Powder samples primarily employ the Kubelka-Munk function to convert diffused reflectance into an equivalent absorption coefficient. Therefore, we can express the modified Tauc's relation in this way:(20)F(R)hv=A(hv−Eg)n

The bandgap (Eg) is a range of energy within a material that lacks electron states. A constant, independent of energy, determines its value, while n determines the type of optical transition. For direct allowed transition, n = 1/2, whereas for indirect allowed transition, n = 2. We denote the plank constant as h [67]. Table 6 shows the determined bandgap (Eg) values for the Ni-Cd ferrite series, which are 2.0027 eV, 1.9476 eV, 1.8765 eV, 1.7437 eV, 1.7010 eV, and 1.6817 eV. Fig. 12(b) illustrates the plot of [F(R)hν]2 against hν. This report outlines our findings on the subject of diffuse reflectance measurements and presents a method for accurately determining the bandgap energy. Our research indicates that prior studies have underestimated this energy, and we propose a more precise approach for future investigations.

According to the quantum confinement theory, the size of a crystallite determines the bandgap of a semiconductor, and decreasing the crystallite size leads to an increase in the bandgap. However, when Ni doping occurs, the Burstein-Moss effect widens the bandgap by increasing the concentration of free carriers. Interestingly, our work shows the opposite behaviour. We observed a significant effect on the bandgap values upon substitution of Ni2+ ions in cadmium and Al3+ in Fe3+ ferrite nanoparticles. The bandgap (Eg) value of cadmium ferrite is 2.0027 eV, and it goes up as the Ni2+ concentration goes up, as shown in Fig. 12(c). Fig. 12(d) attributed the decrease in bandgap values to the reduction in gain size, a phenomenon known as the quantum confinement effect, as documented by numerous authors [68,69]. A lot of researchers have found that the sp-d spin-exchange interactions between the band electrons and the localized d electrons of transition metal ions [70,71], which take the place of the cation, tend to close the band gap in II-VI semiconductors doped with transition metals. In line with this, our synthesized samples exhibit a decrease in the band gap upon the addition of Ni to Cd. We attribute this to the s-d and p-d exchange interactions between the localized d electrons of Ni and the band electrons of Cd, which alter the energy band structure. This reduction of the band gap increases the absorption of solar energy in the visible spectrum, making it useful for applications in photocatalysis reactions, solar cell devices, and microwaves [72].

Conclusion

The microwave assisted sol-gel auto combustion approach synthesizes urea-fuelled NixCd1-xAl0.1Fe1.9O4 (NCAF) spinel nanoferrites. To examine microstructural and magnetic effects of calcination, these samples were calcined up to 800 °C. X-ray diffraction confirmed the single-phase, cubic spinel structure with Fd-3m space group. Substituting Ni2+ decreases lattice constant (a), X-ray density (dx), crystallite size (D), ionic radii, bond length, hopping length, and tetra/octa According to ImageJ, the HR-TEM investigation revealed pseudo-spherical morphologies with average particle sizes of 40–45 nm and interlayer spacing of 0.26 nm. Spherical, well-defined crystallite grains with huge open deficits and an average grain size of 124.8 to 56.6 nm appear in SEM micrographs and histograms. As nickel content increases, saturation magnetization rises to x = 0.6 and subsequently falls to x = 1.0. Lower cadmium ion concentrations decrease coercivity, possibly due to greater ionic radii. The produced samples showed multi-domain and pseudo single domain NPs with remanence ratios (R < 0.5) and (0.05 < 0.5). Ferrites' single-phase cubic structure was confirmed by FTIR absorption bands at 425.24–428.14 cm−1 and 534.81–543.66 cm−1, associated with tetrahedral and octahedral sites. The bandgap (Eg) from UV–Vis spectra's absorption peak from 463.41 to 498.68 nm decreased from 2.5870 to 2.1657 eV in Tauc plot. The Kubelka-Munk function F(R) bandgap (Eg) fell from 2.0027 to 1.6817 eV, as Ni2+ concentration increased. The structure and magnetic properties of Cd ferrites changed after Ni2+ substitution.
