Comparative Evaluation of Microleakage of Zinc Oxide Eugenol and Endoflas as Obturating Materials Using Different Root Canal Irrigants in Primary Teeth: An In Vitro Study
Authors: Yogesh J Kale, Sagar Misal, Mahesh Dadpe, Prasanna T Dahake, Shrikant B Kendre
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThe aim of this study was to evaluate and compare the apical microleakage of zinc oxide (ZOE) eugenol and Endoflas as obturating materials using different root canal irrigants in primary teeth.Materials and methodsA total of 80 extracted primary anterior teeth were divided randomly into four groups with different irrigants and obturating materials used. Group I [ZOE + sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl)], group II [ZOE + NaOCI + ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)], group III (Endoflas FS + NaOCI), group IV (Endoflas FS + NaOCI + EDTA). Cleaning and shaping of the root canals were done using specified irrigants, followed by obturation with ZOE eugenol and Endoflas as per the respective groups. Specimens were then immersed in 2% methylene blue dye solution and stored for 24 hours. The specimens were split longitudinally into two halves and observed under a stereomicroscope. Apical microleakage was measured from the apex to the most coronal extent of dye penetration.ResultsThe highest dye penetration was recorded for teeth filled with ZOE, group I (ZOE + NaOCl), with mean values of 1.924 ± 0.925. Endoflas group IV (Endoflas + NaOCI + EDTA) showed the lowest dye penetration with mean values of 0.901 ± 0.255. There was a statistically significant difference between the ZOE and Endoflas groups (p < 0.001). The result showed a statistically nonsignificant difference between the two Endoflas groups (p = 1.000). Minimum microleakage was found in group IV (Endoflas + NaOCI + EDTA).ConclusionEndoflas FS exhibits less apical microleakage than ZOE eugenol as an obturating material in primary teeth. Hence, Endoflas could be a potential alternative to ZOE eugenol with better sealing ability as an obturating material in primary teeth. Also, the addition of EDTA to irrigation protocol has shown a statistically nonsignificant improvement in microleakage reduction with both obturating materials.How to cite this articleKale YJ, Misal S, Dadpe M, et al. Introduction

A pulpectomy is still the preferred treatment for teeth with nonvital pulp or with irreversible pulpal damage in the primary dentition. The objective of this treatment is to maintain these affected teeth without any symptoms until their exfoliation during the transition from primary to permanent dentition. In pulpectomy, the pulpal tissue and debris are completely removed during biomechanical preparation, and throughout the instrumentation, irrigation is unavoidable. The use of irrigants enhances mechanical debridement by dissolving tissue, clearing debris, and disinfecting the root canal system. An ideal irrigant should have all the desired properties and not be harmful to the tissues. None of the currently available irrigating solutions can be considered ideal. The combination of irrigants, along with the correct sequence, leads to the successful outcome of the treatment.1

One of the major requirements for successful endodontic therapy is the three-dimensional sealing of the root canals.2 The properties of the obturating materials play an important role here. The obturating material must possess radiopacity, be nontoxic to peri radicular tissue and tooth germ, facilitate ease of insertion, exhibit nonshrinkage, and demonstrate disinfectant properties.3 Currently, no existing material fulfil the specified criteria.4 Traditionally, zinc oxide (ZOE) eugenol has been employed for obturating primary root canals, demonstrating a success rate ranging from 65 to 85%.5 Nonetheless, this material is known for its potential irritation to periapical tissues, slower resorption compared to roots, and the potential to induce necrosis in bone and cementum.6 In the recent past, Endoflas, which contains ZOE eugenol, iodoform, and calcium hydroxide, was introduced in South America.7

Even materials like ZOE eugenol and Endoflas show varying levels of microleakage as canal-filling materials. One of the reasons for microleakage is the smear layer. Effective irrigants may help remove the smear layer, thereby decreasing microleakage and improving the sealing ability of root canal-filling materials. Different irrigation protocols and regimens have been implemented over a period of time to remove the smear layer and create a structurally distinct dentinal surface, which is better suited for obturation.

A combination of sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) with ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) is more efficacious in removing the smear layer and preventing apical microleakage than using sodium hypochlorite alone. Studies have shown that 3% NaOCl is as efficient as 5% and more suitable for children, considering its harmful effects. Also, 17% EDTA solution used as an irrigant causes erosion of dentinal tubules and dentinal structures, especially in primary teeth with lower mineral content.8 To avoid this harmful effect of EDTA solution on dentinal tubules, a lower concentration of EDTA solution was suggested. Considering these factors, we have used a combination of 3% NaOCl and 10% EDTA to remove the smear layer and reduce the microleakage, thereby improving the sealing ability of the obturating materials and, effectively, the overall success of pulpectomy.

Hence, this study was carried out to evaluate and compare the microleakage of ZOE eugenol and Endoflas as primary root canal filling materials using different root canal irrigants.

Preparation of Specimens

The external surfaces of the samples were cleaned using curettes. Then, the specimens were disinfected using NaOCl and stored in deionized water until the beginning of the experiment.

Methodology

A total of 80 extracted primary anterior teeth were selected (Fig. 1). And further divided into four groups according to obturating materials and root canal irrigants used:

Around 80 primary anterior tooth

Group I: The ZOE eugenol was irrigated with normal saline + 3% NaOCl.

Group II: The ZOE eugenol irrigated with normal saline + 3% NaOCl + 10% EDTA.

Group III: Endoflas FS irrigated with normal saline + 3% NaOCl.

Group IV: Endoflas FS irrigated with normal saline + 3% NaOCl + 10% EDTA.

Group IV: Endoflas FS irrigated with normal saline + 3% NaOCl + 10% EDTA.

Technique

All the teeth from the four groups received standardized instrumentation procedures. The clinical crown was maintained to assist the hand holding each tooth. Access opening was done using high-speed round burs. Working length was measured from the intraoral periapical radiograph and kept 1 mm short of the apex. Biomechanical preparation of each specimen was done using K and H hand files up to the size 40–45.

Irrigation Protocols

After each instrument size, copious irrigation was performed as per different irrigation protocols in each group. For group I, all samples were irrigated with normal saline, followed by NaOCl as a final irrigant for 3 minutes.9 For group II, all the samples were irrigated copiously with normal saline, followed by NaOCl and the EDTA as a final irrigant for 3 minutes.9 For group III, the irrigation protocol was the same as group I, that is, normal saline followed by NaOCl, and for group IV, the same as group II, that is, normal saline followed by NaOCl, and EDTA as a final irrigant for 3 minutes.9

Obturation of the Samples

After irrigation, all the specimens were dried using paper points, and obturation of the canals was done according to the respective four groups and materials using a lentulo spirals paste carrier mounted on a slow-speed handpiece. Standardized obturation procedures for the individual materials were followed.10 Complete obturation of canal space in all the specimens till the working length was ensured, and a radiograph was taken for each tooth to ensure that obturating materials filled the apical third. After completion of obturation in each specimen, the access cavity was sealed with the help of Tempfil, and the specimens were then wrapped in wet gauze to maintain a humid environment and stored in individual coded glasses at 37°C in an incubator to ensure the setting of the obturation material.11

Root surfaces of all the specimens (groups I–IV) were painted with two coats of nail polish, except 1 mm around the apical foramen, and were then suspended in 2% methylene blue dye for 24 hours in individual glass vials.12 Later, the specimens were washed and air-dried before splitting the teeth for dye evaluation. Each tooth was split longitudinally in a labiolingual direction using a diamond disc and micromotor along the long axis of the tooth. The half showing more microleakage was considered for stereomicroscopic evaluation. The apical third part of each split specimen was then subjected to stereomicroscopic evaluation with a magnification of 10× (Fig. 2). Linear dye penetration from the apex, to the most coronal extent was measured in millimeters. Dye penetration for each specimen was evaluated by a single observer. The scores for each specimen were noted, and the data obtained was subjected to statistical analysis.

Discussion

As Schilder states, “the objective of root canal procedures should be the total three-dimensional filling of the root canal and all accessory canals.” A well-obturated root canal filling serves to inhibit the microleakage of periapical exudate into the canal, preventing reinfection and establishing a conducive biological environment for healing.13 The quest for an ideal apical seal has prompted the exploration of stable, nonirritating, obturating materials that can achieve a flawless seal at the apical foramen.

Even though the main principles used for endodontic therapy of permanent teeth apply to the primary teeth, certain criteria of primary root canal filling materials differ, which include the resorption of the obturating material at a similar rate as the physiological root resorption of the primary root. This contraindicates the use of solid, nonresorbable core materials to fill root canals in primary teeth.14 Another critical difference is the use of cement/sealer for root canal filling. In permanent teeth, the sealer is used to fill the gaps between the obturation material and the root canal walls. The pressure and condensation applied to the core material during obturation of the canals in permanent teeth result in better and closer adaptation of sealer and gutta-percha to the root canal walls. This condition does not exist in primary teeth, in which paste is used as a sole obturation material. Therefore, the sealing ability of the obturating material in primary teeth depends mainly on the material's ability to adhere to the root canal walls and the technique applied to introduce this material into the root canal.

Our study aimed to evaluate and compare the microleakage of ZOE eugenol and Endoflas as obturating materials in primary teeth using different irrigation regimes. In our study, extracted primary anterior teeth were used for minimal anatomic variations. No attempts were made to cut clinical crowns of the teeth to facilitate handling during root canal preparation and simulate the clinical situations. To reduce the variability, the random distribution of the teeth into experimental groups was performed, and the root canal preparations were completed as per the respective group protocols. The specimens were obturated as per the different groups and sectioned longitudinally with fine diamond disks followed by dye penetration measurement.

The results of the study were evaluated, and it was found that highly significant differences existed among all the groups. Group IV (Endoflas + NaOCI + EDTA) showed the least microleakage (0.901 ± 0.255), followed by group III (Endoflas + NaOCI) (0.908 ± 0.977) followed by group II (ZOE + NaOCI + EDTA) (1.823 ± 0.977) and group I (ZOE + NaOCl) (1.924 ± 0.925).

In intergroup comparison, group I (ZOE + NaOCl) showed higher microleakage compared to group II (ZOE + NaOCI + EDTA), but the difference was statistically not significant (p < 0.968). The change in irrigation protocol with the addition of 10% EDTA showed a limited effect on the reduction of microleakage. ZOE eugenol showed a higher tendency for microleakage, which might be because of the low flow of the ZOE eugenol, which results in poor adaptation of the material to the dentinal wall. This can be explained by the study conducted by Dultra et al.,15 in which ZOE eugenol showed higher microleakage than other materials. This finding was similar to the result of Alacam,16 which investigated the adaptation of ZOE after irrigating the canals of extracted primary canines with different irrigating solutions. They reported insufficient adaptation of the ZOE at different levels of the root canal, which was attributed to shrinkage of ZOE after setting.16

In intergroup comparison, group I (ZOE + NaOCl) showed higher microleakage compared to group III (Endoflas + NaOCl), which was statistically significant (p < 0.001). Though no study has been conducted on the comparison of ZOE eugenol and Endoflas microleakage evaluation in primary teeth, Bawazir and Salama11 found that ZOE demonstrated the highest apical leakage as compared to other materials used in the study. Ayer et al.18 conducted a study in which Endoflas FS showed lower apical microleakage than ZOE eugenol. The composition and the final consistency of the material seem to have a significant influence.19 One of the reasons for higher microleakage in ZOE eugenol might be the lower flow rate of the material compared to Endoflas.

In intergroup comparison, group I (ZOE + NaOCl) showed significantly higher microleakage compared to group IV (Endoflas + NaOCl + EDTA) (p < 0.001). The reason for the lower microleakage in group IV can be attributed to the fact that Endoflas, when used along with NaOCl and EDTA, shows a lower contact angle between the dentin surface and the material, which gives the better sealing ability to the material. This can be explained by the research conducted by Mulay et al.,20 which studied the wetting ability of various root canal obturating materials following the use of different irrigants. The reason for lower microleakage in group IV compared to group I might be because of better adaptation of Endoflas to the root dentin surfaces. In our study, group IV was treated with both EDTA and NaOCl, which may have created a close contact of Endoflas with the dentin surface due to better removal of the smear layer, resulting in its higher penetration into the dentinal tubules.

In intergroup comparison, group II (ZOE + NaOCl + EDTA) showed higher microleakage compared to group III (Endoflas + NaOCl), which was statistically significant (p < 0.001). The higher microleakage in group II might be because of the properties of the materials used in both groups; many studies have shown that the combined use of NaOCl and EDTA helps to remove the smear layer effectively. However, Ximenes et al.,9 in a study to evaluate the efficacy of a combination of NaOCl and EDTA as an irrigating solution, concluded that none of the combinations were capable of completely removing the smear layer produced inside the root canals of primary teeth. As there is less inorganic content and more collagen fibers in the primary teeth, there is less chelating effect of EDTA in the primary tooth. Thus, EDTA shows limited effect on the reduction of microleakage in primary dentin.

In intergroup comparison, group II (ZOE + NaOCl + EDTA) showed higher microleakage compared to group IV (Endoflas + NaOCl + EDTA), and the difference was statistically significant (p < 0.001). The probable reason behind this may be the flow, particle size, wetting ability, and penetration of the material into dentinal tubules. Endoflas has shown lower microleakage as compared to ZOE eugenol because there is less void formation in the Endoflas compared to ZOE eugenol, forming a tight apical seal, which restricted the penetration of tracing dye. Also, the consistency of Endoflas and mass formation after the mixture leads to the flow of the material into the dentinal tubule, which creates good adaptation with the dentinal wall. The hydrophilic property of Endoflas permits its use in humid canals. It adheres firmly to the surface of the root canals to provide a good seal.21 The use of 10% EDTA did not show a significant reduction in microleakage.

In intergroup comparison, group III (Endoflas + NaOCl) showed higher microleakage compared to group IV (Endoflas + NaOCl + EDTA), but the difference was not statistically significant (p = 1.000). Lower microleakage in group IV may be because of better smear layer removal due to the addition of EDTA in the irrigation protocol. Similar results were noted by Yuksel et al.,22 who conducted a study to evaluate the dentinal adaptation and apical microleakage values with different irrigation protocols in primary teeth. They concluded that NaOCl with EDTA may prove to be an effective irrigation protocol because of its ability to promote strong wall adaptation and effective sealing. It is suggested that achieving well-adapted and leakproof canal filling could enhance the overall success rate of root canal treatment in primary teeth.

Although care was taken to mimic the oral conditions, the continuous cleansing action of saliva and sudden temperature and pH changes in the oral cavity could not be replicated in the present study. Also, the quantity and quality of saliva, type of obturation technique, and irrigation protocol may influence the results, which need to be evaluated further.

Conclusion

Endoflas FS has exhibited less apical microleakage; it could be a potential alternative to ZOE eugenol with better sealing ability. Also, the effect of modification of irrigation protocol with the addition of EDTA needs to be explored with more studies, as it may improve the long-term clinical success of primary endodontic therapy.
