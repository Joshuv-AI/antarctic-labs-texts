Deep sequencing reveals underestimated burden of integrase inhibitor resistance mutations in people living with HIV: a global individual patient data meta-analysis
Authors: Defu Yuan, Fei Zhao, Yangyang Liu, Shanshan Liu, Hongxia Yan, Lifeng Liu, Tong Zhang, Bei Wang, Bin Su
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntegrase strand transfer inhibitors (INSTIs) have emerged as cornerstone agents in global antiretroviral therapy, though growing drug resistance presents clinical challenges. Conventional Sanger sequencing may underestimate the burden of low-frequency variants. This global meta-analysis systematically assesses the actual INSTI resistance burden among people living with HIV (PLWH) through deep sequencing technology. Our comprehensive search across Web of Science, PubMed, Cochrane Library, Embase, and Scopus (PROSPERO ID: CRD42023495535) identified studies utilizing deep sequencing for HIV integrase resistance detection. Through meta-analysis and individual participant data (IPD) analysis using HIVdb (v9.8), we differentiated pretreatment (PDR) and acquired (ADR) drug resistance, quantifying mutation rates, resistance rates, and missed detection across thresholds (above-threshold, high-frequency, low-frequency). The analysis incorporated 40 studies with 46 datapoints involving 10,778 patients. Treatment-naïve PLWH demonstrated above-threshold, high-frequency, and low-frequency mutation rates of 9.92%, 7.29%, and 5.88%, respectively, corresponding to resistance rates of 2.60%, 0.09%, and 3.90%. Treated patients showed elevated mutation rates of 19.02%, 16.64%, and 10.23%, with resistance rates reaching 16.10%, 8.67%, and 6.95%. IPD analysis of 394 mutation-positive individuals revealed 8.52% low-frequency resistance to dolutegravir and bictegravir in treatment-naïve populations, while treated patients exhibited significantly increased intermediate/high-level resistance to first-generation INSTIs, including raltegravir and elvitegravir. Conventional sequencing thresholds missed 3.61% pretreatment and 2.46% acquired drug resistance. These findings demonstrate that deep sequencing reveals substantially underestimated INSTI resistance burdens in PLWH. Although second-generation INSTIs maintain lower resistance rates, cross-resistance risks necessitate clinical vigilance. Optimized resistance surveillance strategies incorporating low-frequency mutation detection offer critical evidence for advancing global HIV control efforts.

Background

By the end of 2024, the global population of people living with HIV (PLWH) had reached 40.8 million, with 89% receiving antiretroviral therapy (ART). The widespread implementation of ART has transformed HIV infection from a fatal disease to a manageable chronic condition, significantly reducing mortality rates and transmission risks. However, more than 2 million PLWH still fail to achieve optimal therapeutic outcomes under current treatment regimens [1]. Uncontrolled viral replication not only increases mortality from AIDS-related illnesses and elevates HIV transmission risks but also substantially escalates disease burden, impairs quality of life, and undermines HIV prevention effectiveness, making AIDS control a critical global public health and social challenge [2].

The efficacy of ART is significantly constrained by drug resistance. World Health Organization (WHO) surveillance data reveal that pretreatment drug resistance (PDR) to efavirenz (EFV) and nevirapine (NVP) exceeds 10%, and PDR to nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NRTI) exceeds 5% among treatment-naïve PLWH [3]. This resistance profile becomes more pronounced in individuals experiencing virological failure (VF) during ART, with over 50% of VF cases developing resistance to first-line regimens containing EFV or NVP. Notably, resistance patterns demonstrate progressive intensification with prolonged treatment duration. Conventional Sanger sequencing, the gold standard for drug resistance testing, can only detect high-frequency mutations (>20% viral quasispecies prevalence) while failing to identify low-frequency resistant variants (<20%). Although the clinical impact of low-frequency mutations remains controversial [4], emerging evidence suggests that pretreatment low-frequency resistant quasispecies may negatively affect treatment response, particularly for low genetic barrier drugs compared to high genetic barrier regimens. Crucially, a dose–response relationship has been observed between non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NNRTI)-associated mutations and therapeutic outcomes, where clinical impact emerges only when the mutation frequency reaches a specific threshold, although this threshold remains undefined [5]. These findings underscore the need for deep sequencing technologies to provide a comprehensive assessment of resistance burden in clinical practice.

Integrase Strand Transfer Inhibitors (INSTIs) have emerged as core antiretroviral agents globally due to their advantages, including favourable safety and tolerability profiles, potent antiviral activity, and high genetic resistance barriers. The WHO 2018 guidelines recommended dolutegravir (DTG)-based regimens as the preferred first-line ART regimen for adults and children [6], and 116 countries had transitioned their preferred first-line ART regimens from NNRTI-based to DTG-based regimens by July 2023 [7]. However, emerging resistance to INSTIs has been observed with prolonged clinical use. Although subsequent studies reported either undetectable or low frequencies of integrase (IN) resistance mutations in PLWH [8], some researchers have noted an upward trend in INSTI resistance rates [9]. Our synthesis of 81 studies employing Sanger sequencing for INSTI resistance surveillance through 2023 demonstrated low PDRs for first – and second-generation INSTIs (0.41% and 0.04%, respectively). However, ADR increased to 7.60% and 4.93% for these agents [10], indicating that INSTI resistance warrants serious attention as the clinical utilization of INSTI expands. While deep sequencing enables the quantification of resistant variant proportions within viral quasispecies and provides enhanced information on resistance burden, global data remain limited regarding IN region resistance mutations detected by deep sequencing in PLWH, particularly given the relatively recent introduction of INSTIs compared to other antiretroviral classes.

To our knowledge, no systematic investigation has comprehensively characterized IN region resistance mutations across varying detection thresholds or elucidated INSTI resistance patterns through deep sequencing data analysis despite documented cross-resistance among INSTIs. Understanding the spectrum of INSTI resistance mutations at different thresholds and associated resistance patterns is crucial for optimizing treatment strategies and improving HIV management outcomes, particularly considering the impact of low-frequency variants on ART efficacy and the high PDR and ADR observed with other drug classes. This meta-analysis synthesizes deep sequencing data on IN mutation prevalence across detection thresholds in PLWH, employing individual patient data (IPD) meta-analysis to examine specific mutation profiles. Using the HIVdb algorithm for resistance interpretation, we analyze INSTI resistance patterns across thresholds and evaluate potential missed detection rates at conventional thresholds, thereby revealing previously underestimated INSTI resistance burdens. This study aims to provide critical evidence for optimizing resistance surveillance strategies and guiding clinical decision-making to support global HIV control objectives.

Methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

The databases Web of Science, PubMed, Cochrane Library, Embase, and Scopus served as the study's data sources (PROSPERO ID: CRD42023495535). Search terms included HIV, AIDS, Human Immunodeficiency Virus, Acquired Immune Deficiency Syndrome, INSTIs, INT, integrase inhibitors, Raltegravir, RAL, Elvitegravir, EVG, dolutegravir, DTG, bictegravir, BIC, cabotegravir, CAB, DR, drug resistance, drug-resistant, DRM, drug resistance mutations, drug resistance mutants, drug resistance variants. Before the formal search, all the search terms were also searched in Mesh Word List, and several commonly used expressions were added. An additional manual retrieval strategy was used to retrieve and supplement the related literature that was listed in the review. Without regard to article type, the retrieval time was from the earliest year provided by the database to 5 April 2025 (Full search strategies and results are contained in Table S1).

Inclusion criteria: (1) Studies on the drug resistance profiles of INSTIs in people living with HIV-1 published before 5 April 2025. (2) Studies published in English. (3) The study participants were aged 18 and above. (4) The sequencing method was a deep sequencing method. (5) The article reported the number of successfully amplified samples, cases with drug-resistant mutations (or mutation rate), or the patient's specific resistance mutation profiles. Exclusion criteria: (1) Case reports, conference abstracts, letters, and non-original studies (reviews, meta). (2) Studies with all participants co-infection with other diseases (TB, Hepatitis B, etc.). (3) Studies on specific HIV-1 subtypes, INSITs, or mutation sites. (4) Studies in which multiple sequencing assays have been used concurrently, but results obtained from each assay are indistinguishable. (5) Sequencing thresholds were unclear or only provided data above 20% but did not provide drug resistance mutation data below the 20% threshold. If different articles were published using the same data, only the article with the most detailed data description was selected for inclusion.

Literature screening, data extraction, and quality assessment

Data were extracted independently by two authors. Any disagreements will be settled through discussion until an agreement is reached or a third author is consulted. The following information will be extracted: the first author, the year of publication, the title, the name of the publication, the DOI number, the sample source, the study population, the collection sample, the sequencing platform, the sequencing threshold, the number of HIV/AIDS patients, successful amplification cases, the number of drug resistance or drug resistance mutations under different detection threshold, etc. If one study included ART-naïve and ART-experienced subjects and accurate data were available, they were extracted as multiple data points. The STROME-ID Statement and the features of the included studies were considered when we modified the literature quality assessment items following the AHRQ's requirements for evaluating cross-sectional studies, and 11 assessment items were eventually determined (Appendix 1, p 5).

Data analysis

Software R (version 4.4.2) was used for statistical analysis. The Metadata package evaluated the combined effect values of drug resistance rate and mutation rate among PLWH. The heterogeneity test employed the Q test and I2 test. The combined effect value was determined using the random-effects model when P ≤ 0.10 or I2 ≥ 25%; otherwise, the fixed-effects model was used. Subgroup and Meta-regression analysis were also conducted. Egger's test was employed to investigate publication bias; it was assumed there was no substantial publication bias if the P value was greater than 0.05. Sensitivity analysis were also conducted by eliminating studies one by one to evaluate the stability of the combined effect values.

Given that different studies have different algorithms for determining drug resistance degree and that even if the same algorithm is used, there are differences due to different versions of the algorithms, this study focused on extracting the specific variants profiles of each patient and using the latest version of the Stanford algorithm (HIVdb, version 9.8) to obtain the resistance degree to INSTIs, in this study, drug resistance refers to the low or higher level of resistance to at least one INSTI caused by variants.

Newly diagnosed and ART-naïve patients are included in the category of pre-treatment patients unless otherwise noted. In this study, pre-treatment drug resistance mutations (PDRM) refers to the prevalence of variants among pre-treatment patients; pre-treatment drug resistance (PDR) refers to the prevalence of drug resistance caused by variants among pre-treatment patients; acquired drug resistance mutations (ADRM) refers to the prevalence of variants among ART-experienced patients; and acquired drug resistance (ADR) refers to the prevalence of drug resistance caused by variants among ART-experienced patients. This article is written according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement (Appendix 1, 39–44).

For studies that provided complete IN region mutation profiles derived from deep sequencing in PLWH, this study calculated the missed detection rate of mutations by comparing variant identification between conventional high-frequency thresholds and deep sequencing thresholds. Additionally, the missed detection rate of drug resistance was quantified using the HIVdb 9.8 to interpret resistance levels conferred by mutations detected under different thresholds. Resistance underestimation was further stratified by INSTI generation.

Results

Literature screening process

Figure 1 illustrates the workflow for literature selection. A total of 5,155 references were initially retrieved from five databases. After removing 1,528 duplicate records, the titles and abstracts of the remaining 3,627 articles underwent preliminary screening, leading to the exclusion of 3,002 studies deemed irrelevant to the research topic. Full-text retrieval was performed for the remaining 625 articles. Following a full-text review, 523 studies were excluded due to unspecified sequencing methodologies or non-deep sequencing approaches. The remaining 102 articles underwent rigorous full-text evaluation, resulting in the exclusion of 66 studies that did not meet the inclusion criteria. Ultimately, 36 studies were included for analysis. During the screening process, 17 additional articles cited by other authors as potentially relevant were identified. After a full-text assessment, 13 studies were excluded, and four were incorporated, resulting in a final inclusion of 40 studies [11–50]. Cumulative meta-analysis

The cumulative meta-analysis, based on the publication dates of included studies (Appendix Figures. S1-S12), revealed divergent patterns between treatment-naïve and treated populations. In treatment-naïve patients, the above-threshold subgroup showed a decrease in PDRMs alongside an increase in PDR, while the high-frequency subgroup exhibited concurrent declines in both PDRMs and PDR, and the low-frequency subgroup displayed significant elevations in both PDRMs and PDR. In contrast, ART-experienced individuals showed consistent downward trends for both ADRMs and ADR across all three threshold-defined subgroups.

IPD meta-analysis of INSTI resistance patterns using deep sequencing data

Baseline characteristics of 394 PLWH with IN mutations

Our analysis incorporated 394 PLWH with IN region mutations from 37 studies (Appendix 1. Table S5), with 305 treatment-naïve and 89 ART-experienced individuals from at least 19 countries and regions (United States, United Kingdom, Australia, Uganda, Turkey, China, Switzerland, Spain, South Africa, Panama, France, Ethiopia, Cameroon, Togo, Jordan, Lesotho, Nigeria, Europe, and South America). Sequencing data were predominantly obtained through Illumina platforms (303/394, 76.90%).

Resistance profiles among 394 PLWH with IN mutations

Figure 6 displays INSTI resistance patterns across different mutation frequency thresholds among 394 PLWH with IN mutations. Among 305 treatment-naïve PLWH, drug resistance (low-level or above) to any INSTI was detected in 40.33% (123/305) above-threshold, 3.41% (6/176) high-frequency, and 85.51% (118/138) low-frequency cases. Eighty-nine ART-experienced PLWH showed resistance rates of 76.40% (68/89), 69.09% (38/55), and 78.85% (41/52) in respective threshold groups. Except for low-frequency thresholds where treatment-naïve individuals exhibited higher RAL, first-generation INSTIs, and any INSTI PDRs compared to ART-experienced PLWH, all other threshold groups demonstrated higher post-treatment resistance. At the same threshold, regardless of treatment status, first-generation INSTIs (RAL and EVG) consistently showed the highest resistance rates, followed by second-generation INSTI CAB, while DTG and BIC maintained the lowest resistance rates. Notably, pre-treatment resistance to DTG/BIC remained undetected (0%) at high-frequency thresholds but reached 8.52% in above-threshold groups.

The analysis of resistance profiles across all three detection threshold groups revealed that the proportions of moderate and high-level resistance to four INSTIs (RAL, CAB, DTG, BIC), including high-level resistance to EVG, consistently exceeded pretreatment levels among ART-experienced individuals, except moderate-level PDR to EVG, which demonstrated a higher proportion. Across all threshold groups, PLWH resistant to first-generation INSTIs (RAL and EVG) consistently exhibited the highest proportions, and those resistant to CAB showed the highest proportions among second-generation INSTIs. Conversely, the proportions of PLWH resistant to BIC and DTG remained consistently lowest.

Notably, in the high-frequency threshold group, all pretreatment-resistant PLWH maintained sensitivity to BIC and DTG, with only 33% demonstrating low-level resistance to CAB. However, in both low-frequency and above-threshold groups, over 65% of pretreatment-resistant PLWH exhibited CAB resistance, with approximately 20% reaching moderate or higher resistance levels, while more than 20% developed low-level or greater resistance to BIC and DTG.

Missed detection profiles

The missed detection analysis incorporated 20 studies that provided complete mutation profiles obtained through deep sequencing for 1498 PLWH. Successful sequencing was achieved in 1,241 specimens, with 288 cases demonstrating mutations in the IN region. The detailed mutation characteristics and corresponding resistance profiles under different detection thresholds are provided in Appendix 2.

For treatment-naïve PLWH (10 studies), we calculated the missed detection rates of IN mutations and INSTI resistance. Meta-analysis revealed a pooled missed detection rate of 3.61% (95% CI: 1.40%–5.82%) for PDRM and 3.11% (95% CI: 1.11%–5.11%) for PDR (Figure S13-A). The missed detection rates for first-generation and second-generation INSTIs were 3.11% (95% CI: 1.11%–5.11%) and 2.07% (95% CI: 0.55%–3.59%), respectively.

Among treatment-experienced PLWH (10 studies), the pooled missed detection rates were 2.46% (95% CI: 0.14%–4.77%) for ADRM and 2.29% (95% CI: 0.03%–4.55%) for ADR (Figure S13-B). The corresponding rates for first-generation and second-generation INSTIs were 2.29% (95% CI: 0.03%–4.55%) and 1.47% (95% CI: 0.00%–3.12%), respectively.

Publication bias and sensitivity analysis

Egger’s test indicated no significant publication bias except for ADRM, ADR in the above-threshold group, and PDR in the high-frequency group (funnel plots available in Appendix Figures S14-S16). Sensitivity analysis demonstrated stable pooled effect sizes with minimal changes upon sequential removal of individual studies (Appendix Figures S17-S28).

Discussion

This study presents the first comprehensive integration of deep sequencing data to characterize HIV integrase inhibitor resistance dynamics across detection thresholds. By establishing a multidimensional framework that distinguishes mutation frequencies and resistance levels, we offer new insights into the evolving landscape of INSTI resistance. Our findings demonstrate the significant influence of detection thresholds on resistance surveillance, revealing substantial pretreatment resistance burdens even for high-barrier INSTIs and showing that conventional sequencing may underestimate resistance prevalence by 1.47% to 3.61%. The identification of distinct resistance patterns between first- and second-generation INSTIs across thresholds, particularly the maintained susceptibility to DTG and BIC in treatment-naïve populations despite emerging low-frequency mutations, establishes a methodological foundation for precision resistance monitoring. These threshold-dependent profiles have immediate implications for optimizing INSTI-based regimens and underscore the need to consider standardized deep sequencing protocols in resistance surveillance programs to capture the full spectrum of resistance.

Studies have indicated that with the widespread adoption of INSTIs, the prevalence of INSTI resistance among treatment-naïve individuals has remained relatively stable [51]. Our findings reveal that the low-frequency PDRMs and PDR has shown a modest upward trend. This phenomenon may be attributed to the predominance of naturally occurring polymorphic sites in PLWH during the early stages of INSTI implementation, which had a minimal impact on drug susceptibility; however, as INSTI usage has expanded over time, drug-selective pressure has facilitated the emergence and transmission of resistance mutations which capable of stable transmission within populations and significantly reduce INSTI sensitivity, consequently contribute to the gradual increase in low-frequency PDR among treatment-naïve patients. Additionally, the extensive use of INSTIs in pre- and post-exposure prophylaxis may partially account for this upward trend [52]. Notably, our study observed a decline in ADRMs and ADR across three thresholds compared to earlier periods. This reduction likely reflects the progressive replacement of first-generation INSTIs with second-generation agents, coupled with improvements in patient education, medication adherence, stable drug supply, and expanded access to post-treatment drug resistance testing. Importantly, while at least 116 countries have adopted DTG as the backbone of first-line ART regimens [7], some nations still reserve INSTIs for second-line therapy. ART-experienced patients often present with comorbidities, suboptimal adherence, and multidrug resistance [53]. Inappropriate selection of a second-line regimen may lead to the development of cumulative resistance mutations during viral rebound and subsequent resuppression, ultimately fostering resistance to second-line ARVs. Therefore, despite the overall favourable trends in INSTI resistance control under current strategies, the development and implementation of more timely and standardized resistance monitoring protocols for next-generation ARVs, including INSTIs, remain critical to ensuring long-term ART efficacy.

This study demonstrated that both traditional and IPD meta-analyses revealed consistently higher ADR across all three detection thresholds for various drug classes compared to PDR, with a pattern consistent with previous studies [10], except for the low-frequency threshold group in the IPD meta-analysis, which showed higher PDRs to RAL, first-generation INSTIs, and at least one INSTI. The introduction of RAL in 2007 in the United States marked the advent of the INSTI era in ART. However, its frequent dosing requirements often compromised treatment adherence and facilitated the development of resistance [54]. While EVG, approved in 2012, demonstrated superior efficacy and simplified dosing compared to RAL, both ARVs share limitations, including low genetic barriers to resistance and cross-resistance patterns [55]. Furthermore, the extended clinical utilization of RAL and EVG compared to second-generation INSTIs has allowed for prolonged selection pressure, potentially facilitating resistance accumulation and transmission among PLWH [54]. The WHO recommended the adoption of DTG, which maintains high efficacy against HIV-1 strains resistant to RAL and EVG, as the backbone of first-line regimens replacing NNRTIs in 2018 [6]. Our study revealed that even among PLWH harbouring low-frequency INSTI resistance mutations, DTG exhibited the lowest resistance rate among the five INSTIs evaluated, confirming its durable effectiveness. Notably, the recently approved long-acting agent CAB, administered via intramuscular injection with an extended half-life to enhance adherence, has shown superior efficacy in PrEP compared to traditional regimens. However, emerging evidence indicates breakthrough infections and subsequent INSTI resistance development, even in adherent CAB users [56]. Our data further demonstrate higher low-frequency resistance rates to CAB than other second-generation INSTIs, with CAB-resistant mutations frequently conferring cross-resistance to first-generation INSTIs. These findings suggest potential limitations in future INSTI options for some patients, warranting careful consideration when implementing CAB-based PrEP in high-risk populations. Although low-frequency resistance mutations may not necessarily translate to clinical treatment failure, our results indicate that over half of PLWH with IN region low-frequency resistance mutations harbour variants that confer resistance to all five existing INSTIs. This observation highlights the need for ongoing surveillance of INSTI resistance patterns. Despite current data showing substantially lower low-frequency resistance rates for INSTIs compared to other ARVs [57], the expanding global implementation of INSTI-based regimens necessitates a comprehensive evaluation of INSTI resistance mutations, particularly through the integration of drug resistance burden assessments, to fully understand their clinical implications.

This study further reveals significant missed detection of INSTI resistance when using conventional high-frequency testing thresholds. Previous studies reported a 0.04% prevalence of PDR for second-generation INSTIs using Sanger sequencing [10], whereas our findings demonstrate substantially higher missed detection rates of 3.11% for PDRMs and 2.07% for PDR. This discrepancy primarily stems from the characteristics of viral quasispecies and the technical limitations of conventional sequencing methods. HIV exists as quasispecies populations in vivo [58], with Sanger sequencing only detecting dominant viral variants exceeding 20% frequency. However, emerging evidence from deep sequencing suggests that low-frequency variants (<20%) may possess equivalent biological significance [5]. Particularly under widespread INSTI usage, these low-frequency resistant variants could rapidly evolve into dominant strains through drug selection pressure. A prospective longitudinal monitoring study of ART-experienced individuals observed a gradual expansion of initially low-frequency INSTI-associated resistance mutations to dominant levels during follow-up [22], indicating that minor resistant variants may constitute a potential “resistance reservoir.” Our data corroborate this phenomenon, showing that over 50% of treatment-naïve patients with IN region mutations exhibited low-frequency PDR to second-generation INSTIs, which is significantly higher than the 3.41% high-frequency PDR rate, revealing the potential future burden of drug resistance for second-generation INSTIs. Notably, the relatively lower missed detection rates observed for BIC and DTG may be attributed to their higher genetic barriers; however, this should not diminish concerns about detection limitations because our study identified low-level or higher resistance to these drugs in 20% of treatment-naïve patients with IN mutations at low-frequency thresholds. The underestimated resistance burden could impact ART efficacy through dual mechanisms: pre-existing low-frequency mutations may predispose to treatment failure, while undetected resistance in failing regimens could lead to inappropriate regimen selection and subsequent accumulation of resistance mutations. Furthermore, undetected resistance carriers may serve as potential sources for the transmission of resistant HIV strains, thereby undermining the “treatment as prevention” strategy. However, the current technical complexity and cost of deep sequencing restrict its widespread implementation in resource-limited settings, necessitating cost–benefit balanced solutions for missed detection challenges. Future research should establish standardized protocols for detecting low-frequency mutations and conduct prospective cohort studies to clarify the clinical correlations of resistance mutation frequencies, particularly in determining critical “resistance quasispecies thresholds” for key mutation sites, thereby informing frequency-based therapeutic decision-making.

Interpretation of this meta-analysis must account for differences in sample types. Most included studies used plasma HIV RNA, which directly reflects the drug resistance profile of the actively replicating viral population. In contrast, the clinical significance of findings from the three studies [15,16,38] based on proviral DNA should be approached with caution [59]. The proviral DNA reservoir primarily comprises defective viral genomes, and the drug resistance mutations identified may not be replication-competent. Notably, among these three DNA-based studies, only one study [16] excluded APOBEC-mediated hypermutated sequences, while the others did not specify such processing. This omission may result in overestimation of functional drug resistance levels [60]. Conversely, DNA testing may underestimate the true prevalence of drug resistance due to lower sensitivity and reproducibility, and represents only a subset of the viral reservoir [59]. Consequently, directly pooling drug resistance prevalence from DNA and RNA studies presents inherent limitations. Future research should develop standardized procedures for DNA testing, including the identification of defective genomes, and correlate these results with clinical outcomes to clarify the value of DNA testing in guiding clinical decision-making.

Prior to initiating this study, we conducted a comprehensive review of existing INSTIs-related deep sequencing studies and found that due to the relatively low detection rates of low-frequency DRMs, most studies provided detailed mutation profiles of individuals in the texts or supplementary materials, while a minority translated genotypic resistance results using interpretation algorithms. Notably, significant discrepancies were observed in algorithm selection across different studies, and even when studies employing identical interpretation systems, version differences in these algorithms could lead to variations in reported resistance profiles. Considering such limitations, this study extracted detailed mutation information from individuals exhibiting low-frequency DRMs and uniformly applied the latest Stanford HIVDB algorithm (version 9.8) for resistance level determination instead of directly utilizing pre-interpreted resistance data from original studies to minimize the confounding effects of algorithm heterogeneity (both inter-algorithm discrepancies and intra-algorithm version differences) on pooled resistance rate estimates. This study also has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, substantial heterogeneity was observed among the included studies, but such challenges are also encountered in conventional Sanger sequencing-based research [61,62]. Second, while HIV subtype-specific resistance patterns have been documented in the literature, our analysis was constrained by data availability, with the extracted information pertaining solely to low-frequency DRM profiles rather than raw sequence data, precluding subtype characterization of minority viral quasispecies and comparative analysis of subtype-dependent mutation patterns. Third, platform-dependent variability in DRM detection was noted across studies. Although subgroup analysis revealed discernible platform-related variations in the low-frequency group, there were no significant differences in above-threshold group, high-frequency group, and the PDRMs in low-frequency group between the Illumina and other sequencing platforms (Appendix 1. Table S6). In addition, the wide range of sequencing thresholds used across studies prevented meaningful threshold-based subgroup analysis. This variability may underestimate the detection and reported prevalence of low-frequency variants; nevertheless, our study still reveals a non-negligible burden of low-frequency drug resistance, underscoring the need for future studies with standardized thresholds and platforms to better understand the true burden of low-frequency resistance. Fourth, this study included only a limited number of studies that used proviral DNA to assess drug resistance, which may have affected the pooled results. However, some studies accounted for APOBEC-mediated hypermutation, thereby limiting its influence in the present analysis. Future research should incorporate more studies to evaluate the impact of monitoring different sample types on drug resistance outcomes.

Conclusion

Through the first global individual patient data meta-analysis of deep sequencing for INSTIs resistance mutations, this study revealed that both the mutation rate and resistance rate of INSTIs above deep sequencing thresholds substantially exceed those previously reported by conventional sequencing thresholds, demonstrating that Sanger sequencing significantly underestimates the burden of INSTI resistance. Notably, second-generation INSTIs exhibited low high-frequency PDR but considerable low-frequency PDR among PLWH harbouring INSTI resistance mutations, confirming that while second-generation INSTIs possess higher genetic barriers, prolonged therapeutic pressure still leads to the accumulation of resistance mutations, highlights the need for vigilance regarding the clinical impact of low-frequency resistant mutations. Individual patient data analysis further revealed that conventional high-frequency thresholds missed 2.07% of PDR and 1.47% of ADR for second-generation INSTIs, which will potentially compromise the efficacy of ART regimens containing these agents, hence emphasizing the necessity of deep sequencing for precise resistance profiling. These findings provide critical evidence for optimizing HIV resistance surveillance strategies, guiding clinical drug selection, and formulating global resistance containment policies. While deep sequencing has uncovered a greater burden of low-frequency drug resistance, the impact of integrase low-frequency resistance on the clinical efficacy of ART remains unclear. Future research should further investigate the clinical implications of HIV low-frequency resistance mutations and identify the resistance quasispecies thresholds, thereby supporting the integration of deep sequencing into resistance detection systems and addressing increasingly complex resistance challenges.
