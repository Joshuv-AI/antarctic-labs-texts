Correlated insulating states in slow Dirac fermions on a honeycomb moiré superlattice
Authors: Dongyang Yang, Jing Liang, Haodong Hu, Nitin Kaushal, Chih-En Hsu, Kenji Watanabe, Takashi Taniguchi, Jerry I Dadap, Zhenglu Li, Marcel Franz, Ziliang Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStrong Coulomb repulsion is predicted to open a many-body charge gap at graphene’s Dirac point, transforming the semimetal into a Mott insulator. However, this correlated insulating phase remains elusive in pristine graphene, where the large Fermi velocity dominates interactions. To overcome this limitation, we realize a honeycomb moiré superlattice in twisted MoSe2 homobilayers, where a graphene-like band structure forms with a Fermi velocity reduced by nearly two orders of magnitude. These slow moiré bands, folded from the valence band maximum at the Γ valley with negligible spin-orbit coupling, simulate massless Dirac fermions in the strongly correlated regime with full SU(2) symmetry. By correlating Rydberg-exciton sensing with moiré exciton-polarons, we detect a Mott gap at the Dirac point persisting up to 110 K. We further identify correlated states at ν = − 1 with weak ferromagnetic coupling, and at fractional fillings. Our results highlight the potential for exploring quantum many-body phenomena in twisted two-dimensional materials.Subject terms: Two-dimensional materials; Surfaces, interfaces and thin films; Magneto-optics; Electronic properties and materials

Introduction

The physics of massless Dirac fermions has been extensively studied in monolayer graphene, whose two-dimensional honeycomb lattice hosts low-energy excitations with linear energy dispersion at the corners of the Brillouin zone (±K valleys)1,2. Near the Dirac point where the conduction and valence bands meet, the electron’s pseudospin (sublattice index) is collinear with its momentum, giving rise to opposite chiralities in the two valleys3. Theoretically, if the Coulomb interaction between Dirac electrons is sufficiently large, the material is predicted to transform from a semimetal to a Mott insulator through a Gross-Neveu transition4–6. In the Mott insulating phase, an energy gap opens at the Dirac point, and the chiral symmetry becomes broken7–9, giving rise to a range of exotic phenomena, including quantum spin liquids10, topological superconductivity11, and non-Fermi-liquid behavior12. However, such a correlated insulating state remains unobserved in pristine graphene since the Coulomb interaction between electrons is too weak compared to their hopping energy, or, in other words, the Fermi velocity is too large13. A prominent strategy to enhance correlations is to reduce the Fermi velocity, as demonstrated in twisted bilayer graphene, where the interference of two Dirac cones leads to significant flattening of the bands14,15.

Transition metal dichalcogenide (TMD) homo- and heterostructures have emerged as a powerful platform for exploring exotic physics with non-trivial topology and strong correlations16–20. Among these, the twisted MoSe2 homobilayer (t-MoSe2) is an ideal system for studying slow Dirac fermion physics for two reasons21,22. First, the moiré potential minima arising from the interlayer coupling are distributed symmetrically between the MX and XM sites (MX denotes the stacking where the Mo atom is on top of the Se atom and vice versa), forming the same honeycomb lattice as graphene (Fig. 1a, b). Second, the highest filled moiré bands are folded from the valence band maximum (VBM) in the Γ valley of the monolayer Brillouin zone, where all states are doubly spin-degenerate following Kramers’ theorem (Fig. 1c)23. As a result, a twisted MoSe2 homobilayer shares qualitatively the same single-particle effective Hamiltonian near the Dirac point as monolayer graphene, except that the Fermi velocity becomes over thirty times slower. Unlike in folded K-valley bands, the wavefunction in the moiré bands folded from the Γ-valley VBM has substantial interlayer overlap, leading to a strong layer-hybridized character24–26.

In this work, we utilize the honeycomb moiré superlattice in the twisted MoSe2 homobilayer to experimentally simulate slow Dirac fermions in the strong correlation regime. Since the hopping energy (t) between MX and XM sites increases more rapidly than the on-site Coulomb repulsion (U0) with twist angle, the strong correlation phenomena (U0 > t) must be explored in the small-twist-angle limit. Nevertheless, if the twist angle becomes too small, significant atomic relaxation can occur, reducing both the moiré potential and the on-site Coulomb energy27,28. We focus on t-MoSe2 homobilayers with twist angles between 2∘ and 4∘, where the moiré superlattice constant, aM, is comparable to the AB-BA domain wall width, ensuring that the lattice reconstruction is sufficiently weak to preserve the Coulomb repulsion strength. Using the Bistritzer-MacDonald continuum model, we calculate the single-particle band structure of an ideal 3.6∘ t-MoSe2 bilayer (Fig. 1d), where the Dirac point appears at the filling of two holes per moiré unit cell (ν = − 2). The top two valence bands (shown in red) are sufficiently isolated from the other bands so that we can map them onto a single-orbital Hubbard model with a t of about 5 meV and U0 of 100–200 meV21. In this large U0/t limit, correlated states with novel magnetic orders, single- and double-stripe phases, as well as unconventional superconductivity have been theoretically predicted10,29,30. Tuning the U0/t ratio in such artificial graphene-like systems opens new opportunities to revisit some interesting quantum electrodynamic phenomena on a different energy scale, such as the Klein tunneling effect3. The correlated states are optically probed through the 2s exciton in a nearby sensor layer, as well as the moiré exciton-polarons in t-MoSe2.

Results

Probing insulating states in t-MoSe2 homobilayers

Building on the optical studies of TMD heterostructures31, we employ the Rydberg-exciton sensing technique to investigate correlated states in a t-MoSe2 homobilayer with a target twist angle of 3.6∘, fabricated using the dry transfer method. In addition to fabricating the device in a dual-gate geometry that can independently control doping and electric field (E), we placed a WS2 monolayer 1 nm (the thickness of a trilayer hBN) from the t-MoSe2 as an exciton sensor (Fig. 1e and Fig. S1). Since the valence band of WS2 is much lower than that of MoSe2, holes are primarily doped into the MoSe2 layer, allowing precise tuning of the filling factor (ν) in the moiré superlattice. If the t-MoSe2 enters an insulating/incompressible state, the screening of the WS2 2s exciton is reduced. (In contrast to the 1s state, the 2s exciton has a Bohr radius of about 5 nm and is therefore highly sensitive to the dielectric environment32.) By monitoring the peak energy and oscillator strength of the WS2 2s exciton, we can optically probe the electronic compressibility of the t-MoSe2 homobilayer.

Figure 1 f shows the first energy derivative of the reflection contrast spectrum, d(ΔR/R)/dℏω, of the device under the intrinsic condition. The measurement is performed at 7 K to reduce nonlinear gating effects. The two absorption peaks at approximately 1.610 and 1.812 eV correspond to the A and B exciton resonances of the moiré MoSe2 bilayer. The strongest feature, centered at 2.072 eV, arises from the 1s exciton in the WS2 sensor layer, while the 2s exciton peak appears at 2.192 eV. The narrow linewidths of the WS2 excitons confirm that the energy and charge transfer between the sensor and sample layers are effectively suppressed by the hBN spacer.

Since our MoSe2 flake is chemically n-doped, the Fermi level of the system can be tuned through the intrinsic band gap by applying an outward electric field symmetrically between the top and bottom gates. The carrier doping density within the sample is controlled by an equivalent gate voltage Vg, defined as Vg=Vtg+(Cb/Ct)Vbg2 (Methods). Throughout this tuning, we continuously monitor the 2s exciton in the sensor layer with its doping dependence shown in Fig. 2b. At Vg ≈ − 1 V, we observe an abrupt 18-meV blueshift of the 2s exciton resonance and a large enhancement in its oscillator strength. (We define the oscillator strength as the peak-to-peak amplitude of the resonance, as shown in Fig. S2) According to the exciton screening picture, this enhancement indicates that the sample layer has reached the charge-neutral condition (with an initial doping level of ~1011 cm−2). Further increasing the gate voltage moves the Fermi level into the valence band, whose maximum resides in the Γ valley as shown below.

In the low-doping region near ν = 0, we observe a peak splitting in the 2s exciton resonance, which we attribute to the formation of interlayer attractive and repulsive Fermi polarons (IAP and IRP) arising from the Coulomb interaction between excitons in the WS2 layer and free carriers in the MoSe2 layer33,34. As the carrier density increases, the IRP gradually transfers its oscillator strength to the IAP (Supplementary Information S6 and Fig. S16). Interestingly, within the gate voltage range −4 < Vg < − 1 V, we observe a series of blueshifts in the IAP peak, accompanied by an enhancement of its oscillator strength. These behaviors result from reduced screening together with a renormalized quasi-particle band gap, indicating the emergence of insulating states at these doping levels. In contrast, the 1s exciton remains largely unaffected due to its smaller radius (Fig. S16).

We attribute the two distinct blueshifts observed at Vg = − 2.36 V and Vg = − 3.48 V to the two integer fillings (ν = − 1 and ν = − 2) for two reasons. First, the magnitudes of these blueshifts are notably larger than those at other fillings. Second, only at these two fillings do the blueshifts persist at elevated temperatures, as we will show in the next section. Since correlated insulating states at integer fillings are expected to exhibit larger charge gaps31,32, we associate them with the fillings of one and two holes per moiré cell, respectively. From the voltage separation between these two features, we extract the moiré cell density, nM ≈ 4.6 × 1012 cm−2, which corresponds to a twist angle of 3.8∘ (or 5.0 nm moiré period), consistent with the target angle of 3.6∘. These two insulating states at integer fillings were also observed at lower doping densities in another device D2, with a smaller twist angle of 2.9∘ (Fig. S6). The insulating states observed at ν = − 1 and − 2 clearly have a correlated nature, since in the single-particle picture, they correspond to a quarter- or half-filled honeycomb lattice with the Fermi level lying above or at the Dirac point, respectively21,29. The charge gap opens only when a particular filling is reached. According to our unrestricted Hartree-Fock calculations projected onto the two topmost moiré bands (Supplementary Information S1), the ν = − 1 state is a sublattice-polarized electronic crystal driven by the nearest-neighbor Coulomb repulsion (U1) while the ν = − 2 state is a Mott insulator arising from the on-site Coulomb repulsion (U0). We will provide more experimental evidence regarding the nature of these states later.

Next, we plot the oscillator strength R2s as a function of ν to reveal additional insulating states (Fig. 2b). The filling numbers are assigned by assuming a linear dependence between the gate voltage and doping density, based on the voltage spacing between two integer fillings. In the low-doping density regime, the combined oscillator strength of the IRP and IAP branches is denoted as R2s. The peaks in R2s correspond to charge-incompressible states. When additional holes are introduced into these charge-ordered states, the system undergoes a quantum melting process, during which the charge gap diminishes35. As a result, the mobile charges screen the 2s exciton and reduce its oscillator strength. Such a screening effect continues as the system transits into the next insulating state. The oscillatory behavior in the 2s oscillator strength therefore originates from the system transitioning between electronic crystal and fluid states as the doping increases. For 0 > ν > − 1, a series of insulating states appears at commensurate fractional fillings, including ν=−12,−23,and−56. These incompressible states are largely insensitive to the external electric field (Fig. 2c) because the wavefunction at Γ-valley VBM is strongly layer hybridized, with an expected coupling strength on the order of hundreds meV. However, some of these states (ν≤−12 and ν = − 2) and the phase boundary (ν = 0) exhibit dispersion with the electric field, which we attribute to the nonlinear gating effect associated with finite contact and gate resistances.

At low doping (ν≤−14), the dispersion with a negative slope is known to arise from the high contact resistance between the t-MoSe2 and the electrode36. This nonlinear gating effect may cause uncertainty in the filling assignment at low doping levels. As the doping density in the sample increases −32<ν<−12, the nonlinear gating effect is reduced, and the dispersive feature is eliminated at ν = − 1. Although the contact resistance is further reduced as the doping density increases to 1013 cm−2, the dispersion reappears near ν = − 2, which we attribute to the reduced tunneling resistance of the gate dielectrics when they are operated close to the breakdown voltage (A circuit model is discussed in Supplementary Information S4). The dispersion becomes much larger if the contact gate voltage is turned off (Fig. S11a), consistent with the assigned origin of these dispersions. In the meantime, the Γ valley character of doped holes is also supported by the minimal electric-field dependence of R2s, which varies by less than 10% for ν = − 1 and −23 at the strongest field limit (Fig. 2d). The reduction in R2s at ν = − 2 around E = − 50 mV/nm is due to the doped holes starting to leak into the sensor layer (Fig. 2c). Finally, both our Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations and experimental observation of the indirect exciton photoluminescence (Fig. S11b) confirm that the VBM in the Γ valley is about 200 meV higher than that in the K valley in the extended Brillouin zone of our sample.

Melting of the insulating states

As the temperature increases, the enhancement of the 2s exciton resonance at both integer and fractional fillings gradually disappears (Fig. 3a), indicating the melting of these insulating phases. Since the 2s exciton linewidth also broadens at elevated temperatures, we quantify the melting behavior by plotting the 2s exciton spectral-weight contrast, defined as the difference in the integrated spectral area around the 2s exciton between the insulating and conductive states (Fig. S13). The melting temperature, Tm, defined as the temperature at which the 2s spectral weight contrast decreases to 10% of its maximum value31, is ~ 65 K for the ν = − 2 state and ranges from 13 to 25 K for the fractional fillings (Fig. 3b, 3c). (Additional temperature-dependent data are provided in Fig. S3.) Unlike ν = − 2, the spectral weight contrast for ν = − 1 does not decrease monotonically with temperature; it first increases upon heating, reaches a maximum at T* (~15 K), and then decreases, vanishing at about 65 K. This non-monotonic dependence behavior was reproduced in another device (D3, twist angle 4.0∘, Fig. S14), resembling the Pomeranchuk effect reported in other TMD moiré systems37. The Tm at two integer fillings in devices D1 to D3 is summarized in Fig. 3d. Interestingly, as the twist angle is reduced from 3.8∘ to 2.9∘, the melting temperature of the ν = − 1 state shows little variation, while that of the ν = − 2 state nearly doubles.

The thermal melting of the correlated insulating states is known to be governed by two primary mechanisms38. First, when the thermal energy becomes comparable to the size of the charge gap (Δ), the insulating state gradually transitions into a conductive state. Second, phonons can displace electrons from their equilibrium positions at elevated temperatures. When the deviation exceeds a critical fraction of the interparticle distance (Lindemann criterion), the electronic crystal melts in a way similar to an ionic crystal39. Due to the interplay between these two mechanisms, as well as the potential loss of exciton sensitivity at high temperatures, the Tm measured from 2s exciton sensing provides a lower bound for Δ. The relatively low melting temperature of the insulating states at fractional fillings therefore suggests a smaller Δ, consistent with the long-range nature of the Coulomb repulsion in the generalized Wigner crystals. We do not observe any Umklapp excitation resonance states near the 1s resonance of t-MoSe2, likely due to their relatively high doping density (~1012 cm−2)40. On the other hand, the similar Tm values among the three devices at ν = − 1 suggest that the melting process at this filling is more likely dependent on the local moiré potential rather than the moiré period38.

Results and Discussion

Excitons coupling to correlated electronic states

Unlike the fractional-filling and ν = − 1 states, the charge distribution in the ν = − 2 state is the result of energy competition between the on-site Coulomb repulsion U0 and nearest-neighbor Coulomb repulsion U141. Depending on the ratio of U0 to U1, the ν = − 2 state can be either a Mott insulator where each moiré site (MX/XM) is filled with one doped hole, or a charge density wave (CDW) state where two holes occupy the same moiré site, leaving the other site empty (Fig. S4a, S4b). In the Mott phase, Coulomb interactions between the holes doped at the two sublattices break the chiral symmetry in the Dirac Hamiltonian and generate a many-body charge gap at the Dirac point. On the other hand, the charge gap can also be generated by breaking the AB-sublattice symmetry, like in the CDW phase, where two holes occupy the same sublattice, leaving the other empty. Experimentally, the two cases can be distinguished by analyzing the coupling between the excitons and correlated holes in t-MoSe2.

The doping-dependent reflection-contrast spectra near the 1s exciton ( ~ 1.610 eV) in devices D1 (left panel) and D2 (right panel) are presented in Fig. 4a. Upon hole doping, the oscillator strength of the charge-neutral exciton gradually decreases, while two new features emerge on the low-energy side, which we label as X1+ and X2+. In Device D1 (θ = 3. 8∘), X1+ has a smaller binding energy ( ~ 17 meV) and reaches the maximum amplitude at Vtg ~ 2.67 V, while X2+ has a larger binding energy ( ~ 30 meV) and maximizes at a higher doping density (Vg ~ 3.50 V), as shown in Fig. 4b. Interestingly, the doping levels at which X1+ and X2+ peak are well matched with the conditions ν = − 1 and ν = − 2, respectively. Although device D2 (with a twist angle ~2. 9∘) has a different set of doping densities for integer fillings, the same correlation is observed (right panel of Fig. 4b). The absorption of X2+ in D2 is slightly enhanced at ν = − 1, which we attribute to the reduction in screening; its amplitude is much smaller than at ν = − 2.

In the literature, the X1+ and X2+ peaks are assigned as a normal trion plus a charge-transfer trion in a moiré superlattice42. Nevertheless, such a real-space picture cannot fully explain the doping dependence of the peaks, especially regarding that of X2+. In this section, we propose an alternative picture, in which the two features arise from the hybridization between excitons and the charge excitations above the Mott or charge-transfer gap. (Fig. 4b)

In a doped monolayer MoSe2, excitons couple with a Fermi sea to form attractive and repulsive exciton-Fermi polarons. In correlated insulating states, there is no well-defined Fermi sea, but a similar polaron can be formed by dressing excitons with charge excitations associated with inter-site hopping (Fig. 4c). Our DFT calculation confirms that the Wannier orbitals of excitons are located at MX and XM sites (Supplementary Information S5). In the low-doping regime near ν = − 1, the dominant charge excitation involves nearest-neighbor hopping across the charge-transfer (CT) gap between MX and XM sites, as indicated by the green arrow in Fig. 4c. The ensemble-averaged amplitude of this first-order hopping process is maximized at ν = − 1, when every MX or XM site hosts one hole; adding more holes suppresses the amplitude via Pauli blocking, which matches the observed doping dependence.

A second class of excitations involves hopping across the Mott gap to create a holon-doublon pair. In this case, a doped hole must hop twice to form a doubly occupied state at an MX (or XM) site (yellow arrow in Fig. 4c), a second-order process that is small but finite at low doping–consistent with the weak oscillator strength of X2+ at ν = − 1. The holon-doublon creation probability increases toward ν = − 2, at which the doped holes only need to hop once between the nearest MX and XM sites, and subsequently decreases when some sites become doubly occupied, consistent with the maximum oscillator strength of X2+ at half-filling. The absorption peak of the attractive polaron X2+ corresponds to the simultaneous creation of an exciton and charge excitations from the lower Hubbard band to the upper Hubbard band. The holon-doublon excitations arise from exciton dressing induced by the strong exciton-charge interaction. The resonance energy of X2+ becomes lower than that of X1+, indicating that the binding energy difference between the two attractive branches exceeds their gap energy difference.

Based on the doping dependence, we tentatively interpret X1+ as an exciton dressed by a polarization associated with the charge excitation across the charge-transfer (CT) gap, whereas X2+ arises from excitons coupled to the charge excitation across the Mott gap. The existence of X1+ and X2+ also indicates that the ground state of ν = − 2 is a Mott insulator, where each sublattice is filled with one hole, rather than a CDW phase. On the other hand, exciton-polaron (or trion) excitation in a strongly correlated Fermi sea is a complicated phenomenon and more research is required to reveal its microscopic origin.

Given the Mott nature of the insulating state at ν = − 2, we can estimate the lower bound of the on-site Coulomb repulsion energy U0 based on its melting temperature Tm. According to numerical results in the literature43, U0 ≈ 4.75kBTm, which corresponds to about 25 meV in device D1. On the other hand, the nearest-neighbor hopping integral t is estimated to be about 5 meV in D1, following the MacDonald-Bistritzer model21. The resulting U0/t ratio is therefore 5, which is larger than the critical value of 4 for the semimetal-insulator transition in a honeycomb lattice44. A similar calculation shows that device D2 has a lower bound for U0/t of 17, which is also consistent with the correlated insulating phase we observed. In addition, comparing the retrieved U0 with our theoretical model, we obtain an effective dielectric constant ϵ ≈ 27 − 30. The Hartree-Fock calculations under such a condition agree with the observation that Tm of ν = − 2 decreases with increasing twist angle, indicating that the semimetal-insulator phase transition is expected to occur at a larger twist angle or under stronger screening conditions (Fig. S4).

Spin-spin coupling at integer fillings

Finally, we use the moiré exciton-polarons in t-MoSe2 to explore the magnetic properties of correlated insulating states at integer fillings. Figure 5a shows the magnetic circular dichroism (MCD) of D1 at different photon energies under an out-of-plane magnetic field at both ν = − 1 and −2. At ν = − 1, X2+ exhibits an MCD signal that is proportional to the magnetic field, while X1+ shows a negligible MCD signal. A peak near 1.620 eV shows a similar MCD signal with an opposite sign. In contrast, no appreciable MCD signal is observed at ν = − 2 even under the largest magnetic field applied (8 T).

We attribute the MCD signal in X2+ to the exchange interaction between the spin-polarized holes doped in the Γ valley and excitons in the ±K valleys. Due to exchange interactions, a Γ-valley hole preferentially couples to the exciton comprised of a hole with an opposite spin, leading to more exciton-polaron formation in a particular K valley. The high-energy feature is attributed to the repulsive polaron, which has been reported to exhibit the opposite optical selection rule compared to the attractive branch45. No significant absorption difference between σ+ and σ− is observed in X1+.

If we extend this picture to the ν = − 2 state, the absence of an MCD signal suggests a vanishing magnetic susceptibility, indicating that the insulating state may be an antiferromagnetic correlated state with a characteristic temperature well above the measurement temperature. Alternatively, theoretical investigations have proposed a gapped spin liquid (a short-range resonating valence bond state) in a half-filled honeycomb Hubbard model near the U0/t condition for device D110. Within this framework, our negligible MCD signal indicates that the spin gap is larger than the Zeeman or thermal energy. Experimentally, we cannot distinguish between these scenarios or other possibilities within the limited temperature range in our experiments. Because of the small exchange energy (~meV) in moiré exciton-polarons, the MCD signal no longer reflects the spin polarization of doped carriers at temperatures higher than 7 K, as indicated by the deviation from the Curie-Weiss (CW) law in Fig. 5c.

We further examine the spin-spin coupling among doped holes at ν = − 1 by measuring the magnetic field dependence of the MCD signal at various temperatures (Fig. 5b). At base temperature (T = 1.6 K), MCD increases linearly with the magnetic field until it becomes saturated at ∣B∣ = 3.8 ± 0.2 T (the MCD signal is integrated over a 5-meV window around 1.620 eV). At higher temperatures, the linear slope decreases, and the saturation field increases, a trend previously observed in TMD heterobilayers16,31. The magnetic susceptibility is extracted from the slope near the zero field (χzz=limB→0∂M∂B), which follows a linear CW law with a small positive CW temperature ΘCW = + 0.20( ± 0.20) K (Fig. 5c). The small yet finite CW temperature is confirmed by measurements at another point (Fig. S5, ΘCW = + 1.10(±0.40)). The magnetic field dependence of MCD becomes indistinguishable above 7 K, likely because of the limited exchange effect and spin sensitivity of moiré exciton-polarons.

The small positive ΘCW suggests a weak ferromagnetic interaction between the local moments at ν = −1, indicating a direct-exchange interaction larger than the antiferromagnetic superexchange interaction, which is consistent with our theoretical calculations (Supplementary Information S3). By including perturbations up to the fourth order in a Heisenberg model (Fig. S5), we theoretically find that the ferromagnetic spin-spin coupling J varies with the screening strength but remains negative in our range of interest (see the inset of Fig. 5c). These results highlight the importance of nonlocal Coulomb interactions in determining the magnetic ground states of correlated insulators on a honeycomb lattice46.

In conclusion, we have observed a range of correlated insulating states at integer and fractional fillings in twisted MoSe2 homobilayers arising from the Γ-valley moiré flat bands. Rather than forming a semimetal, the half-filled honeycomb lattice in our system is a Mott insulator. The quarter-filled state forms a correlated insulator with weak ferromagnetic coupling and a melting mechanism distinct from that of the Mott state. The strong correlation effect persists up to 110 K and is enabled by the substantially reduced Fermi velocity in our system. The moiré excitons coupled with different charge excitations and 2s exciton sensing techniques establish a powerful optical approach to probe the electronic compressibility, charge distribution, and spin-spin coupling among carriers doped in the Γ valley. The absence of a magnetic response at half filling points to an unconventional magnetic ground state, making its definitive identification a crucial step in understanding strongly correlated fermions on a honeycomb moiré superlattice.

Methods

Sample fabrication

The twisted MoSe2 moiré device is fabricated using the standard dry transfer method under ambient conditions47. Thin-layer hBN flakes, few-layer graphene, monolayer MoSe2, and monolayer WS2 are exfoliated on a 285 nm SiO2/Si substrate. The flake thicknesses are confirmed by atomic force microscopy (AFM) and optical contrast. A 1030 nm ultrafast laser is used to cut the few-layer graphite into specific shapes for gate electrodes. A thin film of polycarbonate, placed on polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), is used as a handle to pick up layers in the following sequence: top hBN, top graphite, top gate hBN, graphene contact, monolayer WS2 sensor, ~1 nm hBN spacer, twisted MoSe2 bilayer, bottom gate hBN, bottom graphite, and contact gate graphite. The monolayer MoSe2 is cut into two pieces by an ultrafast laser at 750 nm. Half of the MoSe2 monolayer is picked up first, and the other half is rotated by a target angle and then picked up at 60–100 ∘C. The pick-up temperature for other flakes is 100 ∘C. Finally, the device is released on a Si/SiO2 substrate with pre-patterned gold electrodes to form contact at 180 ∘C. The polycarbonate residue is removed in chloroform for 30 minutes and rinsed in IPA for 5 minutes.

Optical measurements

All optical measurements, including reflection-contrast spectroscopy and magnetic circular dichroism (MCD), are performed in a closed-cycle cryostat (Attodry-2100, base temperature 1.6 K) equipped with a superconducting magnet. A spatially filtered broadband tungsten halogen lamp is focused onto the device with a spot size of a few micrometers via a low-temperature objective lens (NA = 0.65). The intensity was kept below 10 nW/μm2 to prevent sample heating. The reflected light is spectrally resolved using a spectrometer (Princeton Instruments) with a thermal-electric-cooled CCD camera. To obtain the reflection contrast (ΔR/R) spectrum, we take a reference spectrum at the position outside the sample area. To extract the oscillator strength of the 2s exciton, we subtract a slowly varying background due to multilayer reflections, using third-order polynomial fitting in the photon energy range displayed in Fig. S2. Then, we use the peak-to-peak amplitude to represent the oscillator strength.

For MCD measurements, we used a combination of a linear polarizer and an achromatic quarter-wave plate to generate left-handed (σ+) and right-handed (σ−) circularly polarized light. The MCD spectrum is defined as the difference between the left- and right-handed reflected light intensity: MCD=(Rσ+−Rσ−)/(Rσ++Rσ−). The reflectance spectrum for σ+ and σ− light is acquired by using the reflected light spectrum of the sample under heavily hole-doped conditions as a reference. We integrate the MCD signal over a spectral window of 5 meV near the repulsive polaron (RP) resonance. Magnetic susceptibility is extracted by linear fitting of the MCD response at low magnetic fields (∣B∣≤1.2 T). No significant spin depolarization is observed48.

Twist-angle calibration and electrostatic model

We use the gate voltage of two integer fillings (ν = − 1 and ν = − 2) as benchmarks to calibrate the twist angle θ. The moiré density nM is determined by the voltage differences between the top and bottom gates (ΔVtg and ΔVbg) for the two integer fillings:

The relative permittivities are ϵhBN ≈ 3 and ϵWS2≈7.5 for hBN and WS2 sensor layer. The thicknesses of the top and bottom hBN layers are dt ≈ 6.6 nm and db ≈ 9.5 nm, respectively. (confirmed by AFM in contact mode). The spacer hBN thickness ds is 1 nm and the sensor thickness d0 ≈ 0.65 nm. For the position P1 examined in the main text, the moiré density of nM is 4.6 × 1012 cm−2, corresponding to a twist angle of θ=180πa32nM≈3.8∘, where a ≈ 0.33 nm is the lattice constant of the MoSe2 monolayer. The electric field (E) within the MoSe2 bilayer is E=12(Vtgdt+d0+ds−Vbgdb). To maintain a zero electric field condition, we set the ratio Vbg/Vtg = 1.15 (Fig. 2), as shown in Fig. 2.

DFT calculations

A Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculation of the t-MoSe2 homobilayer is performed to confirm the spatial distribution of the two moiré exciton-polarons observed (X1+ and X2+). The simulated moiré superlattice is constructed using two monolayers of MoSe2 with a twist angle of 3.89∘ and a lattice constant of 0.33 nm in the primitive cell. Structure relaxation is carried out using force-field calculations implemented in the LAMMPS package. Specifically, the Stillinger-Weber force field is used to describe the intralayer interactions, while the Kolmogorov-Crepsi force field accounts for the interlayer coupling. Electronic structure calculations based on the density functional theory of twisted MoSe2 (t-MoSe2) are performed using the SIESTA package with the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof functional. The wavefunctions are expanded using a double-zeta plus polarization basis.
