Fractional modeling of hepatitis B virus transmission via heterosexual and homosexual contacts and its disability burden
Authors: Kamel Guedri, Rahat Zarin, Basim M Makhdoum, Hatoon A Niyazi, Bandar M Fadhl, Olumuyiwa James Peter
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe hepatitis B virus (HBV) is a major public health problem worldwide. Sexual intercourse, heterosexual and homosexual, is an important factor in HBV transmission in adults. This study develops a fractional-order mathematical model to describe the spread of HBV, using fractional calculus with a Mittag–Leffler kernel to account for memory effects and hereditary properties of biological systems. The adult population is divided into the groups of susceptible, infected, carrier, and recovered, on the basis of sex and sexual habits. The model incorporates HBV-related disability using gender-specific probabilities that capture long-term functional impairments among carriers. We derive the basic reproduction number (R0) and analyze the disease-free equilibrium (E0). Theoretical results include conditions ensuring existence and uniqueness of solutions and the Hyers–Ulam stability of the system. Sensitivity analysis of R0 identifies infection rates among same-sex partners, contact frequency, and immunity levels as major factors influencing HBV dynamics. Numerical simulations based on the generalized Mittag–Leffler kernel illustrate the model’s behavior over time. The findings suggest that strengthening immunization programs, reducing the number of carriers, and promoting safe sexual practices can effectively control HBV transmission. The study highlights the value of fractional-order models for capturing complex disease processes and evaluating long-term health outcomes within affected populations.Keywords: Memory effects, Hepatitis b virus, Disability burden, Sensitivity analysis, Nonlinear systems, Numerical simulation.

Introduction

Hepatitis B is a major world health problem caused by the hepatitis B virus (HBV), which primarily targets the liver. The disease has a wide variety of clinical pictures ranging from acute self-limiting disease to chronic complications of the liver, such as cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma. Transmission of HBV occurs by contact with infected blood or by semen or other body fluids. High-risk behavior, e.g. multiple sexual partners and sexual intercourse in the absence of protection, particularly with those chronically infected with HBV, plays an important role in spreading HBV. HBV is extremely contagious as this virus is 50 to 100 times more infectious than HIV, and is much more stable environmentally than hepatitis C virus (see [1, 2]. Approximately 2 billion people worldwide have been infected by HBV with an annual mortality rate of almost 887,000 attributable to HBV complications in 2015 [3]. Despite advances made in examination, an effective cure for chronic HBV disease remains elusive. Consequently, vaccination with the hepatitis B vaccine forms the basis of all preventive measures and affording invaluable protection against HBV. Despite the development of anti-viral drugs capable of suppressing viral replication and reducing the inflammatory reaction in the liver, a definitive cure for chronic HBV remains elusive. Flow treatment approaches, such as nucleoside analogues and interferon therapy, aim to treat the infection and prevent possible disease development but these treatments are not curative. This reflects the prominence of counteractive measures as a viable anti-HBV strategy. Vaccination by means of hepatitis B immumization is a major cornerstone in the prevention of HBV. The vaccination application have been proved highly efficient in preventing HBV infection and its complications. General public vaccination programs incorporating hepatitis B antibodies have given rise to a conspicuous decrease in the prevalence of HBV, particularly in areas where the vaccination has been widely administered. This is especially the case in countries where the coverage with vaccination against HBV has been high. Here the incidence of HBV-related diseases, for instance, has been often greatly reduced in incidence, including childhood HCC [3].

Hepatitis B ranks in the main three irresistible illnesses with about 300,000 deaths associated to HBV-related ailment appendages for every year [4]. Data from the national hepatitis seroepidemiological survey in China (1992) suggested that there were about 120 million HBsAg carriers [5]. Further, hepatitis B is amongst the chief determinant of disease mortality in the country [6]. To reduce this public health burden, the China-GAVI Project (CGP) started vaccinating infants with hepatitis B vaccine in less developed areas such as western provinces and poor counties of central China in 2002. An estimated 3.8 million chronic HBV infections and 680,000 deaths were averted during the CGP (2003–2009) [7]. Retroactive routine vaccination of those under 15 years was then recommended in 2009. Although HBV infection has been substantially curtailed in children, the prevalence of this virus among adults is still very high. In those aged 20 years and older, the reported number of HBV case-patients increased by 22%, from 740,000 in 2004 to 903,000 in 2014 [8, 9].

The risk of chronic HBV carrier status is strongly influenced by the age at which infection occurs, with distinct transmission pathways in adults and children. Sexual transmission represents a significant route of HBV spread among adults. Heterosexual transmission has historically been identified as the primary mode of HBV dissemination in this demographic [10, 11]. Mathematical models have extensively explored heterosexual HBV transmission dynamics [9]. However, homosexual contact accounts for an estimated 10% of HBV cases, with male-to-male sexual transmission being particularly efficient [12]. The World Health Organization (WHO) indicates that MSM should undergo HBV testing as part of routine care because these individuals are at an increased risk of HEVs. Studies from the US and Europe as well as additional studies included from China has started to describe of transmission of HBV among MSM, but such studies are needed for the characterization in SW-China [13]. For example, the overall HBV prevalence among MSM was 7.7% in Kunming, a city located at southwestern China [14]. Women who have sex with women (WSW) There has been increasing awareness over the past decade of health issues and risks for WSW. HBV is more prevalent in males than females, but such evidence indicates that HBV transmission between women may be present. Risk factors among WSW include sexual relationships with men, multiple bisexual partners, and participation in contract marriages (unions between gay men and lesbian women) [15–17]. A sociological study in China revealed that 60–80% of WSW engaged in oral-genital contact, which presents a potential route for HBV transmission [17].

There is an increasing body of the medical literatures suggesting that chronic HBV-infection is an important cause for disability at population level. This effect is often measured using metrics such as YLDs and DALYs. The Global Burden of Disease Study 2019 estimated there were 316 million people who were chronically infected with HBV, resulting in around 555,000 deaths in this year alone; however, mortality data are not equivalent to the disease burden; chronic consequences including cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) will also lead to life-long morbidity and reduced quality of life [18, 19]. The DALY metric includes premature death and years spent living with functional limitation, thus reflecting the non-fatal burden of HBV infection including viral hepatitis sequelae in terms of chronic hepatic disorders, fatigue (loss of energy) and neurocognitive impairment [20]. In 2019, roughly 34.5% of age-standardized HBV DALYs were associated with modifiable risk factors including alcohol consumption, tobacco use, and high body mass index (BMI), demonstrating the influence of lifestyle behaviors on HBV-related disability [21]. This emphasizes the importance of preventive strategies in mitigating long-term disability among carriers. The global distribution of HBV-related disability is highly uneven; low- and middle-income regions experience greater DALY rates due to limited access to vaccination, testing, and treatment [18, 22]. Country-specific analyses have quantified these burdens more precisely, for instance, studies from Brazil and Iran report measurable YLD contributions from HBV-associated cirrhosis. In Brazil, chronic HBV and its sequelae attributed to approximately 57380DALYs (5.5 YLDs per 100,000) in 2008 of which the majority were due to premature mortality and a significant proportion arose from chronic disability [23]. Additionally, in 2013 about 1.1% of HBV-related DALYs could be attributed directly to injection drug use and shows how risk behaviors enhance disability in marginalized groups [24]. Together, these results confirm that HBV causes not only death but also long-term disability at the population level. From a modeling standpoint, YLD and DALY parameters translate carrier prevalence into estimated disability burden and facilitate needs-based planning for health and disability interventions.

Numerous mathematical models have been formulated to investigate the transmission dynamics and control of HBV, representing substantial global research efforts [25–29]. For example, the influence of control measures and age structure on HBV spread in New Zealand was examined in [30], while the effectiveness of vaccination as a control strategy was analyzed in [31]. The role of immunization in reducing HBV transmission was further explored in [32], and a multi-group modeling framework was presented in [33]. Additionally, cost-effectiveness analyses of HBV interventions were conducted in [34], and the critical role of vaccination in disease control was mathematically assessed in [35]. Other papers included diffusion effects and time delays to further characterize persistence of infection and control strategies [36]. Other research has considered alternative aspects of HBV transmission and control [37], the impact of migration on disease dynamics [38] and different multi-intervention strategies [39]. These classical models, often with integer-order derivatives, led to a better understanding of vaccination dynamics, vertical transmission as well as disease dynamics within age or behavioral categories. But integer-order systems are not suitable for modeling memory and hereditary properties contained in biological process. To address this limitation, recent models based on fractional-order derivatives have been proposed to more accurately reflect HBV dynamics, especially for sexual transmission and regional heterogeneity. Together, these studies provide valuable insights for designing region-specific and behavior-informed public health interventions.

Fractional calculus (FC) has emerged as a powerful tool in applied mathematics for addressing complex real-world problems. Fractional-order (FO) models are particularly advantageous over integer-order models due to their ability to capture memory effects and hereditary properties inherent in various systems [40–42]. Unlike integer-order derivatives, which are limited to describing instantaneous rates of change, fractional derivatives provide a more comprehensive analysis of the dynamics between two points in real-world phenomena. To overcome the constraints of classical local differentiation, several non-local FO differentiation approaches have been proposed in the literature. The Caputo [43], Caputo-Fabrizio [44, 45] and ADAtangana–Baleanu (AB) [45] fractional derivatives among them have received wide interests from scientists. The Atangana–Baleanu derivative in particular is well known for featuring a Mittag-Leffler (ML) memory kernel, as well as superior crossover representation of mean-square displacement, where it seems to be designed perfectly for a multitude of applications. Differentiation operators have applied been in a variety of engineering and physics systems. There applications have been successfully extended to biological and chemical models such as that of HIV/AIDS, HPV, covid-19, toxoplasmosis, tuberculosis, enzyme kinetics as well as pandemics responses [46, 47]. The flexibility and robustness of fractional-order models further enhance their suitability for capturing complex dynamics across diverse scientific domains [48, 49]. Using the AB fractional operator, researchers examined an HBV model that included a hospitalized class in [50] and an HBV model with treatment strategies in [51]. The Caputo derivative was utilized to study an HBV model incorporating hospitalization in [52]. Additionally, an FO HBV model that included the dynamics of antibody immune response was presented in [53]. Atangana and Baleanu recently made a critical commitment to fractional calculus by presenting operators for fractional integrals and derivatives in view of the Mittag-Leffler function [54]. The Mittag-Leffler capability has been demonstrated to be more successful than the traditional power function in describing problems related to natural phenomena.

A key novelty of this study lies in the integration of HBV-induced disability into a fractional-order transmission model that accounts for both heterosexual and homosexual dynamics. This work introduces gender-specific disability probabilities and onset rates directly into the existing structure. This allows the model to reflect long-term functional impairments without increasing structural complexity. Moreover, by employing the Atangana-Baleanu-Caputo fractional derivative, the model captures memory and hereditary effects more effectively than classical integer-order models. Together, these innovations offer a more biologically and clinically realistic representation of HBV transmission, shedding light on the hidden burden of disability and guiding more targeted public health interventions. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Sect. 2, we introduce the necessary preliminaries on fractional calculus operators. Section 3 presents the classical and extended HBV transmission models, incorporating heterosexual and homosexual transmission dynamics along with the disability burden. In Sect. 4, we derive the basic reproduction number R0 and analyze the disease-free equilibrium. Section 5 is dedicated to the existence and uniqueness analysis using fixed-point theory. In Sect. 6, we establish Hyers-Ulam stability of the model. Section 7 includes sensitivity analysis to determine the influence of key parameters on disease dynamics. Section 8 presents numerical simulations and discussion of the model’s behavior under various parameter settings. Finally, conclusions and public health implications are discussed in the closing section.

Local sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis is an important tool for identifying parameters that most strongly influence disease transmission. In epidemiological modeling, it helps determine how small perturbations in model parameters affect key outcomes such as the basic reproduction number (R0). Here, we apply the local (forward) sensitivity approach, which measures the normalized rate of change of R0 with respect to each parameter.

Global sensitivity analysis (LHS–PRCC)

In addition to the local (forward) sensitivity analysis, a global sensitivity analysis (GSA)was performed in order to consider joint and nonlinear effects among various parameters on R0. The Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS) technique was applied to perform a global analysis in combination with Partial Rank Correlation Coefficients (PRCC), a popular method in epidemiological modeling for assessing the importance of uncertain parameters on the model predictions. This method is effective for searching the high-dimensional parameter space and depicts nonlinear and interaction effects.

A set of 1000 parameters were created, where each parameter was fluctuated around 20% of its baseline value in order to sufficiently span the parameter space. Pairwise rank correlations between each input parameters and the resultant R0 values were subsequently computed. The larger is the absolute value of these PRCCs, the more a parameter influences R0, and its sign allows to tell if that parameter increases (for positive values) or decreases (−ve values) the reproduction number. The global analysis identified six parameters as being most influential: the vaccination rate (γ3), male-to-male transmission probability (βmm), female-to-female transmission probability (βff), male disability onset rate (θm), female disability onset rate (θf), and disability onset speed (δ). Among these, γ3 showed a strong negative correlation (), confirming its significant suppressive effect on HBV transmission. Conversely, βmm (PRCC = 0.957) and βff (PRCC = 0.838) exhibited strong positive correlations, highlighting their dominant contribution to increasing R0. The disability-related parameters θm, θf, and δ demonstrated moderate influence, reflecting the role of disease progression and disability in shaping transmission potential.

Figures 4, 5, and 6 summarize the results of the global analysis. The PRCC bar graph is presented in Fig. 4, whereby colored bars represent the direction and magnitude of parameter influence. In Fig. 5 the most influential six parameters in the model give histograms showing the uniformity of sampling, which has been established within their ranges. In Fig. 6 scatter plots of R0 against each of the key parameters are shown, which helps to illustrate the positive and negative relationship trends which were calculated using the PRCC values. Overall, the results of the global sensitivity analysis substantiate findings obtained from the local sensitivity analysis. The parameters γ3, βmm, and βff remain the most important drivers of the HBV transmission dynamics. Inclusion of disability parameters (, and δ) in the modeled biological reality adds further biological validity and emphasizes how disability changes subtly the effective infectious period. In summary, these results give a much more comprehensive understanding of the response of the model to parameter variation and reveals the important role played by vaccination and behavioral interventions in reducing R0.

The male susceptible class exhibits the same patterns, showing slower depletion under lower fractional orders and quicker transitions under higher orders. The male infective class shows corresponding delayed peaks under lower values of Ψ1, indicating prolonged infective states due to strong memory effects. The male carrier class shows close conformity with the female carrier trends, here strong memory effects (lower Ψ1) sustains the carrier class, and weak memory (higher Ψ1) causes a decline in the period of the carrier state and marked it easier to attain recovery. These simulations printout the importance of fractional order as a significant parameter which effects memory effects and system dynamics. Lesser orders of fractionalize represents systems which possess strong memory and in which past performances strongly influence performance, and consequently in which transitions between these compartments is slower than in high fractional order systems. The higher fractional orders approximate the classical systems more closely, in which the dynamics are more localized, and so transition is effected more readily. The employment of this fractional system does imply an ability to more correctly represent the complexities of the epidemic behaviours, and provides a good theoretical model for the understanding and control of disease dynamics under varying memory conditions. The recovered population of the model depends heavily on Ψ1, with lower fractional orders slowing recovery and higher orders accelerating it, resulting in quicker transitions to the recovered state.

Conclusion

This study develops a fractional-order mathematical model to describe the heterosexual and homosexual transmission dynamics of HBV, extending existing frameworks to include HBV-induced disability. By applying fractional calculus with the Mittag-Leffler kernel, the model captures the memory and hereditary characteristics inherent in biological systems. Analytical results concerning the existence, uniqueness, and Hyers–Ulam stability of the model confirm its theoretical soundness. A numerical method based on Newton polynomial was employed to simulate the dynamics of the system over time with a sufficient degree of precision. One of the main contributions of this study is the introduction of the disability parameters , and δ, which describe the incidence and manner of chronic HBV carrier long-term functional disability, while preserving the original compartmental structure. This contributes to greater biological realism and more thorough evaluation of the overall burden of disease. Sensitivity analysis of the basic reproduction number (R0) shows that the major driving forces in the spread of infection are the rates of recruitment (), transmission probabilities (βmm , βff ), and the contact rates (). Parameters which appear to lower R0 significantly are the vaccination rate (γ3), the disease progression rate (), and the disability related characteristics, especially the parameters θf and δ. The parameter θm has a slight positive effect, probably because of behavioral differences in male carriers before disability onset. Graphical results bring out some of the interrelationships in the parameters, and point to the need for integrated approaches to intervention. The inclusion of the disability dynamics also indicates an additional load of epidemiological disease and emphasizes the fact that functional health results are necessary for a complete understanding of HBV transmission.
