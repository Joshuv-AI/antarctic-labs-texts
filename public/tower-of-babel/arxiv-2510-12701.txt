Two-Sided Free Boundary Problems Arising From Branching-Selection Particle Systems
Jacob Mercer

Abstract
We introduce and analyse a two-sided free boundary problem arising from a generalization of the well-known N N -particle branching Brownian motion ( N N -BBM) model, called the ( N , p ) (N,p) -BBM, where selection can occur on both the leftmost and rightmost particles according to a parameter p ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) p\in(0,1) . The main result here establishes that, as N → ∞ N\to\infty , the empirical distribution of the ( N , p ) (N,p) -BBM converges to a deterministic hydrodynamic limit described by a free boundary problem on a finite interval with two moving boundaries, and explicit Neumann and Dirichlet boundary conditions parametrized by p p . We further prove that the asymptotic velocity v N , p v_{N,p} of the ( N , p ) (N,p) -BBM converges, as N → ∞ N\to\infty , to v p v_{p} , the unique travelling wave speed of the limiting free boundary problem. These results generalize previous one-sided models and connect to broader classes of free boundary problems found in evolutionary dynamics and flame propagation.

Survival-of-the-fittest is the mechanism by which, in an environment with limited resources, fitter members of a population out-compete less fit individuals. Over time, this ‘selective-pressure’ drives species to evolve towards higher and higher fitness. A number of questions naturally arise about this selective mechanism. At what rate does fitness increase in a population due to survival-of-the-fittest? How does this increase in fitness depend on the population size? What is the variance or distribution of fitness levels in a population?

Physicists Brunet and Derrida pioneered the study of so called ‘branching-selection particle systems’ or ‘Brunet-Derrida systems’ to understand these questions about survival-of-the-fittest in a mathematical framework (
[
3
]
,
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
). These branching-selection systems are characterised by having a fixed population size,
N
, and a selection mechanism in order to enforce this fixed population size when a birth occurs.

A well-studied branching-selection system is called the
N
-BBM. In the
N
-BBM, we have
N
particles on the real line which move about as independent standard Brownian motions. Each particle, at rate
1
, splits into
2
particles at the same location (the binary branching step) and simultaneously, we delete the particle with the leftmost position (the selection step).

Much is known about the
N
-BBM system. Firstly, we have a ‘hydrodynamic limit’ result (
[
9
]
); as
N\to\infty
, the empirical density of particles in the
N
-BBM system with initial distribution
\rho
(in the sense that each particle independently has initial distribution
\rho
) converges to a deterministic limit. Moreover, if
\rho
is sufficiently nice, this limit is the solution of the free boundary problem:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}u_{t}=\frac{1}{2}u_{xx}+u&x\in(L_{t},\infty),t>0,\\
u(x,t)=0&x\notin(L_{t},\infty),t>0,\\
\int_{L_{t}}^{\infty}u(y,t)dy=1&t>0,\\
u\text{ continuous},\\
u(x,0)=\rho(x).\\
\end{cases}

(1)

So the
\frac{1}{2}u_{xx}
term describes the diffusion of the Brownian motions, the
+u
term describes the fact that each particle is branching at unit rate, and the
\int_{L_{t}}^{\infty}u(y,t)dy=1
occurs due to the fact that the population size is constant (thus the empirical density truly remains a probability density for all time). The free boundary
L_{t}
represents the location of the leftmost particle, left of which we trivially have no mass. Note that because this is a free boundary problem, a solution is not just a function
u
, but a pair
(u,L)
satisfying (
1
).

We also know what the asymptotic speed of the particle system is; that is, the asymptotic speed at which fitness increases in the population due to the selection mechanism. If
X_{i}^{N}(t)
denotes the position of the
i
th
leftmost particle of the
N
-BBM at time
t
, then it is known (see Appendix to
[
19
]
) that

\lim_{t\to\infty}X_{i}^{N}(t)/t=v_{N}=\sqrt{2}-\frac{\pi^{2}}{\sqrt{2}\log^{2}N}+o((\log N)^{-2})

for any
i=1,2,\ldots,N
. Now the FBP (
1
) has infinitely many travelling wave solutions, one for each speed
c\in[\sqrt{2},\infty)
. Therefore we can observe that as
N\to\infty
, the
N
-BBM ‘selects’ the minimal travelling wave speed, since
v_{N}\to\sqrt{2}
. This is called the
weak selection principle
.

In this paper we will study a generalisation of the
N
-BBM which we call the
(N,p)
-BBM. In the
(N,p)
-BBM, we again have
N
particles moving about as independent standard Brownian motions on the real line. However in the
(N,p)
-BBM, when a branching event occurs, we may delete either the leftmost or rightmost particle. We kill the leftmost with probability
p
and otherwise we kill the rightmost. So the
(N,p)
-BBM is a generalisation, with
p\in\{0,1\}
corresponding to
N
-BBM and its reflection.

The motivation for studying this system is that, where the hydrodynamic limit of the
N
-BBM is a one-sided free boundary problem, the hydrodynamic limit of the
(N,p)
-BBM (for
p\in(0,1)
) is a two-sided free boundary problem. Two-sided free boundary problems appear in a number of different contexts. Most notably, two-sided free boundary problems have been considered to study predator-prey evolution (
[
21
]
, Section 5 of
[
11
]
) or to study flame propagation (
[
7
]
,
[
12
]
).

In particular, the main result of this paper is to prove that the hydrodynamic limit of the
(N,p)
-BBM particle system with initial distribution
\rho
, under suitable conditions, is the solution to the free boundary problem

\displaystyle\begin{cases}u_{t}=\frac{1}{2}u_{xx}+u&x\in(L_{t},R_{t}),t>0,\\
u(x,t)=0&x\notin(L_{t},R_{t}),t>0,\\
u_{x}(L_{t},t)=2p,u_{x}(R_{t},t)=2(p-1)&t>0,\\
u\text{ continuous},\\
u(x,0)=\rho(x).\end{cases}

(2)

Furthermore, the parallel for the weak selection principle of the
N
-BBM is to prove that the asymptotic speed of the
(N,p)
-BBM system converges to the unique travelling wave speed of the above free boundary problem.

Statement of results

Consider the following free boundary problem (

-BBM as an

Theorem 1
.

Consider the
(N,p)
-BBM process
(X^{N,p}(t))_{t\geq 0}

Moreover, when the solution of the FBP (

It may seem restrictive to impose that the solution to the free boundary problem has Lipschitz continuous boundaries
L_{t},R_{t}
. We may naturally ask: for which initial conditions should this FBP have locally Lipschitz continuous boundaries? We actually expect the Laplacian to have a strong smoothing effect on the free boundaries, so much so that we expect

and

to be smooth away from

is required for regularity of the free boundaries at

Conjecture 2
.

Let
u_{0}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be an initial condition to the FBP (
2
). Then the solution of (
2
) exists and the boundaries
L,R:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
are
L,R\in C^{\infty}((0,\infty))
. If further
u_{0}\in C^{2+\alpha}((L_{0},R_{0}))
for some
\alpha>0
with
u_{0}^{\prime}(L_{0})=2p
,
u_{0}^{\prime}(R_{0})=2(p-1)
and
u_{0}(L_{0})=u_{0}(R_{0})=0
, then the boundaries
L,R\in C^{1+\alpha/2}([0,\infty))
.

This conjecture is supported by the following two facts. A very similar family of FBPs is well studied in the field of flame propagation. The papers
[
7
]
,
[
12
]
, for example, consider a free boundary problem:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}u_{t}=(a(u_{x}))_{x}&x\in(L_{t},R_{t}),t>0,\\
u_{x}(L_{t},t)=\tan\alpha_{1},u_{x}(R_{t},t)=\tan\alpha_{2}&t>0,\\
u(L_{t},t)=-L_{t}\tan\beta_{1},u(R_{t},t)=R_{t}\tan\beta_{2}&t>0,\\
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),\end{cases}

(3)

with conditions on the function
a
and constants
\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}
. Choosing
a
as the identity,
\beta_{1}=\beta_{2}=0
, and
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
such that
\tan\alpha_{1}=2p
and
\tan\alpha_{2}=2(p-1)
, we recover our FBP with the omission of the ‘
+u
’ term. The authors of
[
7
]
prove that for
C^{2+\alpha}(L_{0},R_{0})
initial condition, the free boundaries are
C^{1+\alpha/2}([0,\infty))
(and hence locally Lipschitz continuous).

In
[
8
]
, the authors prove a similar result for the one-sided inverse first passage time problem with survival distribution
q
, which is connected to a similar free boundary problem which has a single free boundary. The inverse first passage time problem asks: given a function
q
, if we have a Brownian motion
B
and a hitting time
\tau:=\inf\{t\geq 0:B_{t}<L_{t}\}
, then can we find a function
L:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathbb{P}(\tau>t)=q(t)
? In
[
8
]
, it is proven that if the initial distribution of the Brownian motion
u_{0}\in C^{2\alpha}([0,\delta])
for some non-integer
\alpha\geq 1/2
and satisfies some compatability conditions with

L:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
, with
L\in C^{\alpha}([0,\infty))

These two results agree in the regularity of the boundary induced by the regularity of the initial condition, and hence we conjecture that the same holds for the free boundaries in our free boundary problem (
2
).

Next we show that this free boundary problem (
2
) has a unique travelling wave solution with explicit speed.

Proposition 3
.

For
p\in(0,1)
, the free boundary problem (
2
) has a unique (up to translation) travelling wave solution

(u(x,t),L_{t},R_{t})=(w(x-ct),ct,R_{0}+ct)=\left(\frac{2p}{\sqrt{2-c^{2}}}e^{-c(x-ct)}\sin((x-ct)\sqrt{2-c^2}),ct,\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{2-c^{2}}}+ct\right),

with unique wave speed
c=\sqrt{\frac{2\log^{2}\left(p/(1-p)\right)}{\log^{2}\left(p/(1-p)\right)+\pi^{2}}}
when
p\geq 1/2
and
c=-\sqrt{\frac{2\log^{2}\left(p/(1-p)\right)}{\log^{2}\left(p/(1-p)\right)+\pi^{2}}}
when
p\leq 1/2

Proof.

Fix
p
and suppose that
(w(x-ct),ct,R_{0}+ct)
is a travelling wave solution with speed
c
, with
w(x)\geq 0\iff x\in(0,R_{0})
. Plug
u(x,t)=w(x-ct)
into
u_{t}=\frac{1}{2}u_{xx}+u
to get
\frac{1}{2}w^{\prime\prime}+cw^{\prime}+w=0
. This is a second order linear ODE which has general solution
w(x)=Ae^{-(c-\sqrt{c^{2}-2})x}+Be^{-(c+\sqrt{c^{2}-2})x}
, where
A,B\in\mathbb{C}
.

Now let us apply the boundary conditions to
w
. The fact that
u
is continuous gives that
w(0)=w(R_{0})=0
, and
w(0)=0
implies that
A=-B
. Suppose for contradiction that there is a solution with wave speed
c>\sqrt{2}
. So
w(R_{0})=0
implies that
Ae^{-(c-\sqrt{c^{2}-2})R_{0}}-Ae^{-(c+\sqrt{c^{2}-2})R_{0}}=0
which means that we must have
A=0
or
R_{0}\in\{0,\infty\}
. Now trivially, we cannot have
A=0
or
R_{0}=0
, and
R_{0}=\infty
leads to the contradiction
2(p-1)=w^{\prime}(R_{0})=w^{\prime}(\infty)=0
. Therefore, we must have
c\leq\sqrt{2}
(when the exponents become complex). So using
e^{ix}=\cos(x)+i\sin(x)
gives that the solution must be of the form
w(x)=\tilde{A}e^{-cx}\sin(x\sqrt{2-c^2})
.

Then
w(R_{0})=0
and
w\geq 0
gives that
R_{0}=\pi/\sqrt{2-c^{2}}
. It remains to apply the conditions
w^{\prime}(0)=2p
and
w^{\prime}(R_{0})=2(p-1)
to find
\tilde{A}
and
c
.
w^{\prime}(0)=2p
gives that
\tilde{A}=2p/\sqrt{2-c^{2}}
and
w^{\prime}(R_{0})=2(p-1)
gives that
2(p-1)=-2p\exp(-c\pi/\sqrt{2-c^2})
, which we solve to get that
c=\pm\sqrt{\frac{2\log^{2}\left(p/(1-p)\right)}{\log^{2}\left(p/(1-p)\right)+\pi^{2}}}
, as required.
∎

Note that in the special cases
p=0
or
p=1
, the
(N,p)
-BBM is either an

-BBM system (which has asymptotic velocity converging to

-BBM (which has asymptotic velocity

This result also agrees with another heuristic argument using results about branching Brownian motion in a compact interval with absorption at the boundaries (see
[
13

for a study of this system in criticality). Consider branching Brownian motion with drift

(0,K)
in which a particle is killed when it exits
(0,K)

K=\pi/\sqrt{2-\mu^{2}}
(see for example Proposition 1 of
[
13
]
). At this critical width, the expected population size is roughly constant. Therefore we seek to choose
\mu
appropriately so that the system behaves roughly like an
(N,p)
-BBM. At this critical width, the density of particles is approximately

f(x):=Ze^{-\mu x}\sin(\frac{\pi x}{K})=Ze^{-\mu x}\sin(\sqrt{2-\mu^2}x),

for large times (see for example Lemma 5 of
[
2

), where

Now in order to see

, so that the system behaves roughly like
(N,p)
-BBM,

\frac{p}{p-1}=\frac{f^{\prime}(0)}{f^{\prime}(K)}=\frac{Z\sqrt{2-\mu^{2}}}{-Z\exp\left(-\frac{\pi\mu}{\sqrt{2-\mu^{2}}}\right)\sqrt{2-\mu^{2}}}\implies\mu=\pm\sqrt{\frac{2\log^{2}(p/(1-p))}{\log^{2}(p/(1-p))+\pi^{2}}}.

Therefore we prove that the asymptotic speed of the

-BBM does in fact exist and, as

Let

-BBM process with initial condition

\lim_{t\to\infty}X_{1}^{N,p}(t)/t=\lim_{t\to\infty}X_{N}^{N,p}(t)/t=v_{N},

\lim_{N\to\infty}v_{N}=c

Although it is not proven here, we expect that

3
Some properties of the
(N,p)
-BBM

To prove certain properties of the
(N,p)
-BBM it will be helpful to give a specific construction in terms of Brownian motions, a Poisson process, and uniform and Bernoulli random variables. From this construction it will be easy to prove subsequent properties. In what follows, we will write the initial condition of the
(N,p)
-BBM as a vector
\nu=(\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, describing the positions of the
N
particles on
\mathbb{R}
. However we will often think of this instead as a probability distribution which is the normalised sum of atoms
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\nu_{i}}
.

Therefore let
B=((B^{j}(t))_{t\geq 0})_{1\leq j\leq N}
be a family of
N
i.i.d. Brownian motions and
Q=(Q(t))_{t\geq 0}
a Poisson process of rate
N
. Let
I=(I_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be an i.i.d. sequence of random variables which are uniform on
\{1,\ldots,N\}
and
P=(P_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
an i.i.d. sequence of Bernoulli random variables each with parameter
p
. Then for any initial configuration
\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
we can write the
(N,p)
-BBM
(X^{N,p}(t))_{t\geq 0}
as a function

(X^{N,p}(t))_{t\geq 0}=\Theta^{N}\left(\Xi(\nu,B,Q,I,P,t)_{t\geq 0}\right),

where
\Theta^{N}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the function which orders components of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
-valued vectors.

This is constructed as follows. Firstly, we define
\Xi(\nu,B,Q,I,P,0)=\nu
. Now define
\tau_{0}=0
, and let
\tau_{1}<\tau_{2}<\cdots
be the discontinuities of the Poisson process
Q
, which will be the successive branching times of the process. Suppose for induction that
\Xi
is defined up to the time
\tau_{i}
. Thence we will define
\Xi
for
t\in(\tau_{i},\tau_{i+1}]
. Let
\Xi_{j}(\nu,B,Q,I,P,\tau_{i})
denote the
j
th
smallest particle of
\Xi(\nu,B,Q,I,P,\tau_{i})
, breaking ties arbitrarily. Then for
t\in[\tau_{i},\tau_{i+1})
, drive the particle at this location by the Brownian motion
(B^{j}(t))_{t\in[\tau_{i},\tau_{i+1})}
. So the particles of
\Xi(\nu,B,Q,I,P,t)
are at locations
\{\Xi_{j}(\nu,B,Q,I,P,\tau_{i})+B^{j}(t)-B^{j}(\tau_{i}):j=1,2,\ldots,N\}
. Now we define the operator
k:\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\{1,\ldots,N\}\times\{0,1\}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
by:

k(v,i,q)=\begin{cases}(v_{2},v_{3},\ldots,v_{i-1},v_{i},v_{i},v_{i+1},\ldots,v_{N})&\text{ if }q=1,\\
(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{i-1},v_{i},v_{i},v_{i+1},\ldots,v_{N-1})&\text{ if }q=0.\end{cases}

Then note that if
i
is a uniform random variable and
q
is a
Ber(p)
random variable, then this operator describes the branching/selection mechanism in the
(N,p)
-BBM process; a uniformly chosen particle branches into two and either the leftmost or rightmost particle is simultaneously killed (depending on the Bernoulli random variable). Thus finally, having defined the function
\Xi
up to the time
\tau_{i+1}-
, we define

\Xi(\nu,B,Q,I,P,\tau_{i+1})=k\left(\Theta^{N}(\Xi(\nu,B,Q,I,P,\tau_{i+1}-),I_{i+1},P_{i+1}\right).

Continuing inductively, we can define
\Xi(\nu,B,Q,I,P,t)
and thus
X^{N,p}(t)=\Theta^{N}(\Xi(\nu,B,Q,I,P,t))
for all
t
. From this description of the particle system the monotonicity property will clearly follow.

Before proving monotonicity, let us first define what we mean by
order
for an
N
particle system:

Definition 6
.

Let
A=\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{M}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
and
B=\{B_{1},\ldots,B_{N}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. We say that
A\preccurlyeq B
if and only if for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
:

|\{i:A_{i}\geq x\}|\leq|\{j:B_{j}\geq x\}|,

where
|S|
represents the cardinality of set
S
.

From this definition, it naturally follows that if
A=\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{N}\}
and
B=\{B_{1},\ldots,B_{N}\}
, then
A\preccurlyeq B\iff-B\preccurlyeq-A
, where
-A=\{-A_{1},\ldots,-A_{N}\}
.

Proposition 7
.

(Monotonicity) The
(N,p)
-BBM is monotonic in the sense that if
(X^{N,p}(t))_{t\geq 0}
and
(\tilde{X}^{N,p}(t))_{t\geq 0}
are both
(N,p)
-BBM processes with
X^{N,p}(0)=\nu\preccurlyeq\tilde{\nu}=\tilde{X}^{N,p}(0)
, then there exists a coupling of the processes such that
X^{N,p}(t)\preccurlyeq\tilde{X}^{N,p}(t)
for all
t\geq 0
almost surely.

Proof.

Define
(X^{N,p}(t))_{t\geq 0}=(\Theta^{N}(\Xi(\nu,B,Q,I,P,t)))_{t\geq 0}
and
(\tilde{X}^{N,p}(t))_{t\geq 0}=(\Theta^{N}(\Xi(\tilde{\nu},B,Q,I,P,t)))_{t\geq 0}
. Now we are given that
\nu\preccurlyeq\tilde{\nu}
, so
\Xi(\nu,B,Q,I,P,\tau_{0})\preccurlyeq\Xi(\tilde{\nu},B,Q,I,P,\tau_{0})
, and suppose for induction that

\Xi(\nu,B,Q,I,P,\tau_{i})\preccurlyeq\Xi(\tilde{\nu},B,Q,I,P,\tau_{i}).

This implies that
\Xi_{j}(\nu,B,Q,I,P,\tau_{i})\leq\Xi_{j}(\tilde{\nu},B,Q,I,P,\tau_{i})
for
j=1,2,\ldots,N
and therefore as the particles of
X^{N,p}
and
\tilde{X}^{N,p}
at these locations respectively are both driven by the same Brownian motion
B^{j}
, thus it is immediate that
\Xi_{j}(\nu,B,Q,I,P,t)\leq\Xi_{j}(\tilde{\nu},B,Q,I,P,t)
for
j=1,2,\ldots,N
and
t<\tau_{i+1}
. Then the result follows from the simple observation that when
v\preccurlyeq\tilde{v}
are ordered vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, then
k(v,i,q)\preccurlyeq k(\tilde{v},i,q)
for any
i=1,2,\ldots,N
and
q\in\{0,1\}
, as this implies that
\Xi(\nu,B,Q,I,P,\tau_{i+1})\preccurlyeq\Xi(\tilde{\nu},B,Q,I,P,\tau_{i+1})
and therefore by induction this ordering remains true for all
t\geq 0
.
∎

Next we prove that any time discretisation of the
X^{N,p}
, as viewed from its leftmost particle, is positive Harris recurrent. First we recall the definition of positive Harris recurrence.

Definition 8
.

(Definition 2.2 of
[
1
]
)
A discrete time stochastic process
(X_{n})_{n\geq 0}
on a measurable space
(S,\mathfrak{S})
is called
Harris recurrent
if there exists an element
A\in\mathfrak{S}
and a probability measure
\phi
on
A
such that
\mathbb{P}(X_{n}\in A\text{ for some }n\geq 1|X_{0}=x)=1
for all
x\in S
, and
\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\in E|X_{0}=x)\geq\lambda\phi(E)
for all
x\in A
and
E\subseteq A
. Further, it is called
positive Harris recurrent
if

\sup_{x\in A}\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{A}]<\infty,

where
\tau_{A}:=\inf\{n\geq 1:X_{n}\in A\}
is the hitting time of the set
A
.

Now let
X^{N,p}
be an
(N,p)
-BBM process, and let
X_{1}^{N,p}(t)
be the location of the leftmost particle of
X^{N,p}
at time
t
. Then we define the process
(\bar{X}^{N,p}(t))_{t\geq 0}:=(X^{N,p}(t)-X^{N,p}_{1}(t)\underline{1})_{t\geq 0}
to be the process
X^{N,p}
as viewed from its leftmost particle.

Proposition 9
.

Fix
\delta>0
. Then the stochastic process
(\bar{X}^{N,p}(n\delta))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is positive Harris recurrent, and consequently has a unique stationary distribution to which it converges.

Proof.

Whilst
X^{N,p}
is an
\mathbb{R}^{N}
valued process, note that
\bar{X}^{N,p}
is a
\{0\}\times[0,\infty)^{N-1}
valued process. Let
A=[0,1]^{N}
. We will first show that the random process
\bar{X}^{N,p}
returns to
A
in finite expected time.

Let us consider our construction

(X^{N,p}(t))_{t\geq 0}=\Theta^{N}\left(\left(\Xi\left(\nu,B,Q,I,P,t\right)\right)\right)_{t\geq 0}

of
X^{N,p}
as a function of random variables. Now consider the event
G_{n}=E_{n}\cap F_{n}
where we define the events

\displaystyle E_{n}:

\displaystyle=\{Q(n\delta)-Q((n-1)\delta)=N-1\}\cap\{I_{i}=1\text{ and }P_{i}=0\text{ for }Q((n-1)\delta)<i\leq Q(n\delta)\}

\displaystyle F_{n}:

\displaystyle=\left\{\sup_{(n-1)\delta\leq s<t\leq n\delta}|B^{j}(s)-B^{j}(t)|\leq 1/4N\text{ for }j=1,2,\ldots N\right\}

So
E_{n}
is the event that in the interval
[(n-1)\delta,n\delta]
, the leftmost particle of
X^{N,p}(t)
branches
N-1
times and at each of these branching events the rightmost particle is killed, and
F_{n}
is the event that the biggest displacement difference between two points of a Brownian motion
B^{j}
in the time interval
[(n-1)\delta,n\delta]
is at most
1/2N
.

Now we note that by our construction, under the event
E_{n}
, every particle trajectory of a particle of
X^{N,p}
is made up of at most
N
segments of Brownian paths
(B^{j}(u))_{s\leq u\leq t}
, since in-between discontinuity times of
Q
, a particle is driven by a single Brownian motion
B^{j}
, and under the event
E_{n}
there are
N-1
discontinuities of
Q
in
[(n-1)\delta,n\delta]
. Therefore the event
G_{n}
ensures that the distance between
X^{N,p}_{i}(n\delta)
and
X_{1}^{N,p}(n\delta)
is at most
1/4N\times N\times 2=1/2
. Thus on the event
G_{n}
, all particles in
X^{N,p}(n\delta)
are within distance
1
of each other, and therefore
\bar{X}^{N,p}(n\delta)\in A
. Therefore as the event
G_{n}
is independent of the configuration of particles at time
(n-1)\delta
, thus
\mathbb{P}(G_{n})=q_{\text{hit}}>0
is the same non-zero constant for each
n
. Thus the hitting time of
A
,
\tau_{A}:=\inf_{t}\{X^{N,p}(t)\in A\}
, from any configuration is bounded above by a geometric random variable with parameter
q_{\text{hit}}
, and hence
\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{A}]\leq q_{\text{hit}}^{-1}<\infty
for all
x\in\mathcal{S}
. Thus to prove that
(X(n\delta))_{n\geq 0}
is positive Harris recurrent, it just remains to prove that there exists a constant
\lambda
and a probability measure
\phi
such that
\mathbb{P}_{x}(\bar{X}^{N,p}(\delta)\in S)\geq\lambda\phi(S)
for any
x=(0,x_{2},x_{3},\ldots,x_{N})\in A
and
S=\{0\}\times S_{2}\times\cdots\times S_{N}\subseteq A
.

We will prove this inequality by taking
\phi
to be the Lebesgue measure on the cube
\{0\}\times[0,1]^{N-1}
, so that, for example,
Leb(S)=\prod_{i=2}^{N}\int_{S_{i}}dy_{i}
. Now consider the event
E:=\{Q(\delta)=0,B^{1}(\delta)-B^{1}(0)\in[-1,0]\}
that during
[0,\delta]
no particle branches and that the leftmost particle moves at most
1
unit to the left. This event occurs with a non-zero probability, say
q_{E}
, independent of the initial condition
x
. Thus we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{x}(\bar{X}^{N,p}(\delta)\in S)

\displaystyle\geq q_{E}\mathbb{P}_{x}(\bar{X}^{N,p}(\delta)\in S|E)

\displaystyle=q_{E}\mathbb{P}_{x}(X^{N,p}_{i}(\delta)-X^{N,p}_{1}(\delta)\in S_{i}\text{ for }i=2,3,\ldots,N|E)

\displaystyle\geq q_{E}\inf_{x_{1}\in[-1,0]}\mathbb{P}_{x}(X^{N,p}_{i}(\delta)-x_{1}\in S_{i}\text{ for }i=2,3,\ldots,N|E)

\displaystyle\geq q_{E}\inf_{x_{1}\in[-1,0]}\prod_{i=2}^{N}\int_{S_{i}}(2\pi\delta)^{-1/2}e^{-\frac{(y_{i}-x_{i}+x_{1})^{2}}{2\delta}}dy_{i}

\displaystyle\geq q_{E}\inf_{x_{1}\in[-1,0]}\prod_{i=2}^{N}\int_{S_{i}}(2\pi\delta)^{-1/2}e^{-2/\delta}dy_{i}

(4)

\displaystyle=q_{E}(2\pi\delta)^{-N/2}e^{-2N/\delta}\inf_{x_{1}\in[-1,0]}Leb(S)=q_{E}(2\pi\delta)^{-N/2}e^{-2N/\delta}Leb(S),

where the inequality (
4
) follows from the fact that
x_{1}\in[-1,0]
,
x_{i}\in[0,1]
, and
y_{i}\in S_{i}\subseteq[0,1]
, so
(y_{i}-x_{i}+x_{1})^{2}\leq 4
. Therefore we have shown that the exists a constant
\lambda
and measure
\phi
such that
\mathbb{P}_{x}(X^{N,p}(\delta)\in S)\geq\lambda\phi(S)
for any
x\in A
and
S\subseteq A
. Thus
(\bar{X}^{N,p}(n\delta))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is positive Harris recurrent. Therefore by Theorems 4.1 and 6.1 of
[
1
]
,
(\bar{X}^{N,p}(n\delta))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
has a unique invariant distribution to which it converges. ∎

Next we will use Kingman’s subadditive ergodic theorem to prove that the
(N,p)
-BBM has an asymptotic velocity. Let us first recall the following definitions. The first is from Kingman’s survey of subadditive processes
[
16
]
:

Definition 10
.

A family
\mathcal{Y}=(Y(s,t):s,t\in[0,\infty),s<t)
is called a continuous-parameter subadditive process if it satisfies the following properties:

(A1)

Y(s,u)\leq Y(s,t)+Y(t,u)
for all
s<t<u
.

(A2)

For all
T\in[0,\infty)
, the distribution of
Y(s+T,t+T)
is the same as the distribution of
Y(s,t)
.

(A3)

For all
t\in[0,\infty)
, the expectation
\mathbb{E}[Y(0,t)]
exists and satisfies
\mathbb{E}[Y(0,t)]\geq-\gamma_{0}t
for some
\gamma_{0}>-\infty
.

The second comes from the textbook of Todorovic
[
20
]
, Definition 1.7.1:

Definition 11
.

Let
T\subset\mathbb{R}
be an interval. A stochastic process
\{\xi(t):t\in T\}
is separable if there exists a countable dense subset
D\subset T
and null set
\Lambda\subset\Omega
such that for any open interval
I
and closed set
C

\{\omega:\xi(t,\omega)\in C,t\in I\cap D\}\setminus\{\omega:\xi(t,\omega)\in C,t\in I\}\subset\Lambda.

Theorem 12

(Kingman, Theorem 4, Section 1.4 of
[
16
]
)
.

If
\mathcal{Y}
is a continuous-parameter subadditive process then the limit
\gamma:=\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[Y(0,t)]/t
exists. If, in addition,
Y
is cadlag (and hence separable) and
\mathbb{E}[\sup_{s<t,s,t\in I}|Y(s,t)|]<\infty
for any bounded interval
I\subseteq[0,\infty)
, then
\lim_{t\to\infty}Y(0,t)/t
exists almost surely and in
L^{1}
and equals
\gamma
.

Proposition 13
.

For any initial distribution
\nu
with bounded initial configuration, the limits
\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{N,p}(t)]/t
and
\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[X_{N}^{N,p}(t)]/t
exist and are equal, and the limits
\lim_{t\to\infty}X_{1}^{N,p}(t)/t
and
\lim_{t\to\infty}X_{N}^{N,p}(t)/t
exist and are equal almost surely and in
L^{1}
.

Proof.

In order to prove that the limit
X_{N}^{N,p}(t)/t
exists, we will construct a continuous parameter subadditive process
\mathcal{Y}
such that
Y(0,t)=X^{N,p}_{N}(t)
. Initially, we will suppose that the initial condition is a constant vector
\nu(a):=(a,a,\ldots,a)
, a condition which we will relax later. Then for any
t\geq s\geq 0
let us define:

Z(s,t):=\Xi\left(\nu,((B^{j}(s+u))_{u\geq 0})_{1\leq j\leq N},(Q(s+u))_{u\geq 0},(I_{i})_{i>Q(s)},(P_{i})_{i>Q(s)},t-s\right).

This defines a stochastic flow of the
(N,p)
-BBM process. Simply, this is the
(N,p)
-BBM constructed so that for
s<t<u
, there is a natural coupling between the processes
(Z(s,w))_{w\geq u}
and
(Z(t,w))_{w\geq u}
. Now let
Z_{N}(s,t)
be the location of the rightmost particle of
Z(s,t)
, and consider
Y(s,t):=Z_{N}(s,t)-Z_{N}(s,s)
. Therefore by definition, both
Y(s,t)
and
Y(s+T,t+T)
describe the position of the rightmost particle of an
(N,p)
-BBM started from initial distribution
(0,0,\ldots,0)
at
t-s
units of time ago, and therefore have the same law. Thus
\mathcal{Y}
satisfies property
(A2)
.

Secondly, property
(A3)
is easy to prove. Clearly the
(N,p)
-BBM can be embedded inside a branching Brownian motion (BBM) started from
N
particles each with distribution
\nu
. Now if
(\{X_{u}(t),u\in\mathcal{N}(t)\})_{t\geq 0}
is a branching Brownian motion with particles at locations
X_{u}(t)
and particle set
\mathcal{N}(t)
at time
t
started from a single particle at the origin, then it is well known (see
[
17
]
for example) that
D(t):=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{N}(t)}\exp(\sqrt{2}X_u(t) - 2t)
is a martingale (called the derivative martingale). So then by Jensen’s inequality

\exp(\sqrt{2} \B{E}[\max_u X_u(t)] - 2t)\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(\sqrt{2}\max_u X_u(t) - 2t)\right]\leq\mathbb{E}[D(t)]=\mathbb{E}[D(0)]=1,

and therefore
\mathbb{E}[\max_{u}X_{u}(t)]\leq\sqrt{2}t
and by symmetry
\mathbb{E}[\min_{u}X_{u}(t)]\geq-\sqrt{2}t
. Therefore we can lower bound the expected position of the leftmost particle of a BBM started from
N
particles at the origin by
-N\sqrt{2}t
, therefore proving the property (
A3
).

To prove the sub-additivity property (
A1
), we note that trivially
Z(s,t)\preccurlyeq\nu(Z_{N}(s,t))
(recalling that
\nu(a)=(a,\ldots,a)
). Then by the definition of
Z
, we know that
(Z(s,t+v))_{v\geq 0}
and
(Z(t,t+v)-Z(t,t)+\nu(Z_{N}(s,t)))_{v\geq 0}
are two coupled
(N,p)
-BBM processes, which have initial configurations
Z(s,t)
and
\nu(Z_{N}(s,t))
respectively. Therefore by monotonicity (Proposition
7
), for any
v\geq 0
,
Z(s,t+v)\preccurlyeq Z(t,t+v)-Z(t,t)+\nu(Z_{N}(s,t))
. Hence for
v=u-t

Y(s,u)=Z_{N}(s,u)-Z_{N}(s,s)\leq Z_{N}(t,u)-Z_{N}(t,t)+Z_{N}(s,t)-Z_{N}(s,s)=Y(t,u)+Y(s,t),

thus proving the sub-additivity property
(A1)
. Therefore
\mathcal{Y}=(Y(s,t):s,t\in[0,\infty),s<t)
is a continuous parameter subadditive process.

Finally in order to apply Theorem
12
, it just remains to show that for any bounded interval
I\subseteq[0,\infty)
we have
E[\sup_{s<t,s,t\in I}|Y(s,t)|]<\infty
. So fix
I=[a,b]\subseteq[0,\infty)
. Again, recall that the
(N,p)
-BBM can be considered as being embedded into
N
independent BBMs, each started from a single particle at the origin, and hence
\sup_{s<t,s,t,\in I}|Y(s,t)|
is bounded by the maximum up to time
b-a
of
N
independent BBMs, all started from
0
(since
Y(s,s)=(0,\ldots,0)
when
\nu=(a,\ldots,a)
). Thus by the many-to-one lemma (see for example Lemma 2.1 in
[
15
]
), we certainly have that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{s<t,s,t\in I}|Y(s,t)|\right]\leq N\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{u\in\mathcal{N}(b-a)}\sup_{0\leq t\leq b-a}|X_{u}(t)|\right]=Ne^{b-a}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq b-a}|B(t)|\right]<\infty,

(5)

where
(B(t))_{t\geq 0}
is a standard Brownian motion and
\{X_{u}(t):u\in\mathcal{N}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
is a branching Brownian motion started with a single particle at the origin.

Therefore we can apply Theorem
12
to the process
\mathcal{Y}
to conclude that
\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[Y(0,t)]/t
exists, and
\lim_{t\to\infty}Y(0,t)/t
exists and is equal to
\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[Y(0,t)]/t
almost surely and in
L^{1}
. Therefore we have that for an
(N,p)
-BBM process
(X^{N,p}(t))_{t\geq 0}
with initial condition
\nu(a)=(a,\ldots,a)
, the limit
\gamma^{N,p}_{N}:=\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[X_{N}^{N,p}(t)]/t
exists and the limit
\lim_{t\to\infty}X_{N}^{N,p}(t)/t
exists and is equal to
\gamma_{N}^{N,p}
almost surely and in
L^{1}
. Then to generalise the result to any bounded initial condition
\tilde{\nu}
, say with each element of
\nu
bounded between
m
and
M
, we can couple the process
X^{N,p}(t)
with initial condition
\tilde{\nu}
by monotonicty so that

(\Xi(\nu(m),B,Q,I,P,t))_{t\geq 0}\preccurlyeq(\Xi(\tilde{\nu},B,Q,I,P,t))_{t\geq 0}\preccurlyeq(\Xi(\nu(M),B,Q,I,P,t))_{t\geq 0},

thus proving that the asymptotic velocity of
X^{N,p}_{N}(t)
exists for any initial condition with bounded support.

By symmetry, we can similarly apply Theorem
12
to the continuous-parameter subadditive process
\tilde{\mathcal{Y}}=(-Z_{1}(s,t)+Z_{1}(s,s):s,t\in[0,\infty),s<t)
, and therefore
\gamma_{1}^{N,p}:=\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{N,p}(t)]/t
exists and
\lim_{t\to\infty}X_{1}^{N,p}(t)/t
exists and equals
\gamma_{1}^{N,p}
almost surely and in
L^{1}
.

It just remains to show that
\gamma_{1}^{N,p}=\gamma_{N}^{N,p}
. Recall the event
G_{n}
that we defined in the proof of Proposition
9
, and define the random times defined by
\tau_{0}=0
and subsequently
\tau_{i+1}:=\inf\{n>\tau_{i}:n\in\mathbb{N},\;G_{n}\text{ occurs}\}
. Recall that, at the stopping times
\tau_{i}
, we have
|X_{N}^{N,p}(\tau_{i})-X_{1}^{N,p}(\tau_{i})|\leq 1
. Note that as
Q
has independent increments, and
I
and
P
are i.i.d families of random variables, thus
\tau_{i+1}-\tau_{i}
are i.i.d and have finite mean.

Therefore since
\tau_{i}\to\infty
as
i\to\infty
, it follows that

0=\lim_{i\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[X_{N}^{N,p}(\tau_{i})-X_{1}^{N,p}(\tau_{i})]/\tau_{i}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[X_{N}^{N,p}(t)-X_{1}^{N,p}(t)]/t=\gamma_{N}^{N,p}-\gamma_{1}^{N,p},

therefore
\gamma_{N}^{N,p}=\gamma_{1}^{N,p}
as required.
∎

4
Coupling particle systems with two-sided selection

The aim of this section is to construct upper and lower bounding particle systems which can be coupled to
X^{N,p}
. In particular, for any
\delta>0
, we want to construct particle systems
X^{N,p,\delta,+}
and
X^{N,p,\delta,-}
such that we have the order
X^{N,p,\delta,-}(k\delta)\preccurlyeq X^{N,p}(k\delta)\preccurlyeq X^{N,p,\delta,+}(k\delta)
at all discrete times
k\delta
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover, we will construct the upper and lower bounding systems such that
X^{N,p,\delta,\pm}
behave as branching Brownian motions for all intervals
(k\delta,k\delta+\delta)
.

Our couplings build on the ideas used in
[
9
]
, which constructs couplings for the
N
-BBM by discretising time to
\delta\mathbb{N}
and performing selection only at the discrete times. To do this, we will firstly construct the
(N,p)
-BBM as a process embedded in a branching Brownian motion (BBM) which we describe by colouring.

Construction of
X
:

Consider a branching Brownian motion with deletion,
\mathcal{B}^{N,\nu}=(\mathcal{B}^{N,\nu}(t))_{t\geq 0}=\{B_{u}(t):u\in\mathcal{N}^{N,\nu}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
, starting from
N
particles, whose initial locations are given by a an
N
-dimensional vector
\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. We define
X=X^{N,p}
as a function
\Gamma
of the BBM
\mathcal{B}^{N,\nu}
and a sequence of i.i.d.
\text{Bernoulli}(p)
random variables,
P=(P_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
. We initially set
X(0)=\Gamma(\mathcal{B}^{N,\nu},P,0)=\Theta^{N}(\nu)
, where
\Theta^{N}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the function which puts the
N
elements of a vector into increasing order.

The particles of the BBM will be coloured either blue or yellow, and initially we set the colour of all particles to yellow. Each time a particle branches, it gives birth to two particles of the same colour. Additionally, at the
i
th
branching event of a yellow particle, if
P_{i}=1
, we simultaneously recolour the leftmost yellow particle blue, and if
P_{i}=0
, we simultaneously delete the rightmost yellow particle. Thus in the coloured process
\mathcal{B}^{N,\nu}
there are at all times exactly
N
yellow particles. For
t>0
, we define
X(t)=\Gamma(\mathcal{B}^{N,\nu},P,t)
to be the vector of the locations of the
N
yellow particles of
\mathcal{B}^{N,\nu}(t)
in increasing order.

Now we will consider coupling
\mathcal{B}^{N,\nu}
to a branching Brownian motion starting from a different initial condition and with fewer than
N
particles. So let
\tilde{\nu}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
be such that
\nu\preccurlyeq\tilde{\nu}
. Then let
\mathcal{B}^{m,\tilde{\nu}}=(\mathcal{B}^{m,\tilde{\nu}}(t))_{t\geq 0}
be a branching Brownian motion starting with
m\leq N
particles, at the locations of the leftmost
m
particles of
\tilde{\nu}
. Again, we initially colour the
m
particles of
\mathcal{B}^{m,\tilde{\nu}}
yellow, although at later times we will also colour some particles of
\mathcal{B}^{m,\tilde{\nu}}
blue. Now for a coloured BBM
\mathcal{B}
, let us define
LY(\mathcal{B}(t),k)
(resp.
RY(\mathcal{B}(t),k)
) to be the vector of locations of the
k
leftmost (resp. rightmost) yellow particles of
\mathcal{B}(t)
. Therefore
\nu\preccurlyeq\tilde{\nu}
implies that we have initially
LY(\mathcal{B}^{N,\nu}(0),m)\preccurlyeq RY(\mathcal{B}^{m,\tilde{\nu}}(0),m)
.

We will couple
\mathcal{B}^{N,\nu}
to
\mathcal{B}^{m,\tilde{\nu}}
so that
LY(\mathcal{B}^{N,\nu}(t),k)\preccurlyeq RY(\mathcal{B}^{m,\tilde{\nu}}(t),k)
for all
t\geq 0
, for a suitable choice of
k
. Our coupling is constructed as follows. Define

\displaystyle LY(\mathcal{B}^{N,\nu}(\tau_{i+1}),y^{m}(\tau_{i+1}))

5
Convergence of the coupled upper and lower bounds

The reason that we defined the upper and lower bounds,
X^{\pm}

in terms of the Gaussian diffusion operator

G_{t}f(x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}(2\pi t)^{-1/2}e^{-(x-y)^{2}/2t}f(y)dy,

and the cut operators

C_{k}^{L}f(x):=f(x)\mathbbm{1}_{\{\int_{x}^{\infty}f(y)dy<k\}}\text{ and }C_{k}^{R}f(x):=f(x)\mathbbm{1}_{\{\int_{-\infty}^{x}f(y)dy<k\}}.

So
C_{k}^{L}
is the operator which cuts mass on the left and leaves a total mass
k
, and
C_{k}^{R}
is the operator which cuts mass on the left and leaves a total mass
k

\displaystyle\sup_{a\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\int_{a}^{\infty}\pi^{N,\delta,\pm}_{\rho,k\delta}(dr)-\int_{a}^{\infty}S_{k\delta}^{\delta,\pm}\rho(r)dr\right|>BN^{-\beta}\right)\leq B^{-4}Ce^{4k\delta}k^{5}e^{4\delta}N^{4\beta-2}

7
Probabilistic representation of solutions of free boundary problems

The aim of this section is to elucidate the connection between the solutions of free boundary problems (FBPs) and killed Brownian motions. We begin by remarking that, given
L:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
, the solution of the PDE

\displaystyle\begin{cases}u_{t}=\frac{1}{2}u_{xx}+u&x\in(L_{t},\infty),t>0,\\
u(t,x)=0&x\notin(L_{t},\infty),t>0,\\
u\text{ continuous},\\
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x).\end{cases}

(27)

satisfies the representation

\int_{x}^{\infty}u(y,t)dy=e^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}u_{0}(y)\mathbb{P}_{y}(B_{t}\geq x,\tau^{L}>t)dy

(28)

where
B
is a Brownian motion and
\tau^{L}
is the stopping time
\tau^{L}:=\inf\{s\geq 0:B_{s}\leq L_{s}\}
(see for example (1.5),
[
9
]
). This follows immediately from the Feynman-Kac representation theorem (see Theorem 3.47 of
[
18
]
, for example). Writing
g(t)=\int_{L_{t}}^{\infty}u(y,t)dy
we can easily calculate
g^{\prime}(t)=1-\frac{1}{2}u_{x}(L_{t},t)
, therefore as
g(t)\equiv 1
, the integral boundary condition
\int_{L_{t}}^{\infty}u(y,t)dy=1
can be rewritten as a Neumann boundary condition
u_{x}(L_{t},t)=2
. In terms of the Feynman-Kac representation, this boundary condition translates (by taking
x\to-\infty
in (
28
)) to
\int_{\mathbb{R}}u_{0}(y)\mathbb{P}_{y}(\tau^{L}>t)dy=e^{-t}
, which is to say that the hitting time of
L
by
B
, given initial distribution
u_{0}
, is exponentially distributed.

We now want to extend such a representation to our two-sided FBP (
2
) which has Dirichlet and Neumann conditions at the free boundaries. Recall the FBP (
2
) is the problem:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}u_{t}=\frac{1}{2}u_{xx}+u&x\in(L_{t},R_{t}),t>0,\\
u(t,x)=0&x\notin(L_{t},R_{t}),t>0,\\
u_{x}(L_{t},t)=2p,u_{x}(R_{t},t)=2(p-1)&t>0,\\
u\text{ continuous},\\
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x).\end{cases}

Therefore we state the following theorem:

Theorem 26
.

Let
(u,L,R)
be a solution to the FBP (
2
) such that
L
and
R
are boundaries which are locally Lipschitz continuous on
(0,\infty)
. Let
B
be a standard Brownian motion with initial distribution
u_{0}
, and
\tau^{LR}
be the stopping time
\tau^{LR}:=\inf\{s\geq 0:B_{s}\notin(L_{s},R_{s})\}
. Then we have the probabilistic representation:

\int_{x}^{\infty}u(y,t)dy=e^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}u_{0}(y)\mathbb{P}_{y}(B_{t}\geq x,\tau^{LR}>t)dy\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R},t\geq 0

and boundary conditions:

\displaystyle e^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}u_{0}(y)\mathbb{P}_{y}(\tau^{LR}<t,B_{\tau^{LR}}=L_{\tau^{LR}})dy=p(e^{t}-1)

\displaystyle\forall t\geq 0,

\displaystyle e^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}u_{0}(y)\mathbb{P}_{y}(\tau^{LR}<t,B_{\tau^{LR}}=R_{\tau^{LR}})dy=(1-p)(e^{t}-1)

\displaystyle\forall t\geq 0.

In order to prove this theorem, we will first prove the following proposition, after which Theorem
26
will easily follow.

Proposition 27
.

Let
s\mapsto L_{s}
and
s\mapsto R_{s}
be barriers which are locally Lipschitz continuous at
0
and which do not touch. Further, let
f(x)
be a continuous probability density with support
(L_{0},R_{0})
such that
f(L_{0})=f(R_{0})=0
. Furthermore, suppose that
f
has a right derivative at
L_{0}
and a left derivative at
R_{0}
, with
\lim_{x\downarrow L_{0}}f^{\prime}(x)=2p
and
\lim_{x\uparrow R_{0}}f^{\prime}(x)=2(p-1)
for
p\in(0,1)
. For Brownian motion
B
, let
\tau^{LR}:=\inf\{s\geq 0:B_{s}\notin(L_{s},R_{s})\}
be the first exit time of
(L_{s},R_{s})
by
B_{s}
. Then:

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{L_{0}}^{R_{0}}f(y)\mathbb{P}_{y}(\tau^{LR}<\delta,B_{\tau^{LR}}=L_{\tau^{LR}})dy=p

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{L_{0}}^{R_{0}}f(y)\mathbb{P}_{y}(\tau^{LR}<\delta,B_{\tau^{LR}}=R_{\tau^{LR}})dy=1-p

Proof.

We begin by noting that as
L,R
are Lipschitz continuous at
0
, there exists
C>0
such that for
\delta
sufficiently small,
|L_{\delta}-L_{0}|<C\delta
and
|R_{\delta}-R_{0}|<C\delta
, then we have:

\mathbb{P}_{y}(\tau^{LR}<\delta,B_{\tau^{LR}}=L_{\tau^{LR}})\leq\mathbb{P}_{y}\left(\min_{0\leq s\leq\delta}B_{s}<L_{0}+C\delta\right),

and:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{y}(\tau^{LR}<\delta,B_{\tau^{LR}}=L_{\tau^{LR}})

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}_{y}\left(\max_{0\leq s\leq\delta}B_{s}-R_{s}<0,\min_{0\leq s\leq\delta}B_{s}-L_{s}<0\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}_{y}\left(\min_{0\leq s\leq\delta}B_{s}<L_{0}-C\delta\right)-\mathbb{P}_{y}\left(\max_{0\leq s\leq\delta}B_{s}>R_{0}-C\delta\right),

therefore putting these bounds together and using the reflection principle, we have:

\mathbb{P}_{y}(\tau^{LR}<\delta,B_{\tau^{LR}}=L_{\tau^{LR}})\in\left[\max\left\{0,\erfc\left(\frac{y-L_{0}+C\delta}{\sqrt{2\delta}}\right)-\erfc\left(\frac{R_{0}-C\delta-y}{\sqrt{2\delta}}\right)\right\},\erfc\left(\frac{y-L_{0}-C\delta}{\sqrt{2\delta}}\right)\right],

where
\erfc(z)
is the complementary error function defined by
\erfc(z)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{z}^{\infty}e^{-s^{2}}ds
. Let us consider the integral
\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{L_{0}}^{R_{0}}f(y)\erfc\left(\frac{y-L_{0}\pm C\delta}{\sqrt{2\delta}}\right)dy
first. We may split up the integral as:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{L_{0}}^{R_{0}}f(y)

\displaystyle\erfc\left(\frac{y-L_{0}\pm C\delta}{\sqrt{2\delta}}\right)dy

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{L_{0}}^{L_{0}+W(\delta)}f(y)\erfc\left(\frac{y-L_{0}\pm C\delta}{\sqrt{2\delta}}\right)dy+\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{L_{0}+W(\delta)}^{R_{0}}f(y)\erfc\left(\frac{y-L_{0}\pm C\delta}{\sqrt{2\delta}}\right)dy,

(29)

where
W(\delta)
is some function which we are yet to determine. Consider the first term of (
29
). Changing variables so that
z=(2\delta)^{-1/2}(y-L_{0}\pm C\delta)
, and Taylor expanding
f
about
L_{0}
, we have:

\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{L_{0}}^{L_{0}+W(\delta)}f(y)\erfc\left(\frac{y-L_{0}\pm C\delta}{\sqrt{2\delta}}\right)dy=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\delta}}\int_{\pm\frac{C\delta}{\sqrt{2\delta}}}^{\frac{W(\delta)\pm C\delta}{\sqrt{2\delta}}}\left(\mp 2pC\delta+2p\sqrt{2\delta}z+O((C\delta+\sqrt{2\delta}z)^{2})\right)\erfc(z)dz.

Then as
\int_{0}^{\infty}\erfc(x)dx<\infty
, and
\int_{0}^{\infty}x\erfc(x)dx=1/4
, then certainly if
W(\delta)/\sqrt{2\delta}\to\infty
, we have:

\sqrt{\frac{2}{\delta}}\int_{\pm\frac{C\delta}{\sqrt{2\delta}}}^{\frac{W(\delta)\pm C\delta}{\sqrt{2\delta}}}(\mp 2pC\delta+2p\sqrt{2\delta}x)\erfc(x)dx\to p.

Now we deal with the
O((C\delta+\sqrt{2\delta}z)^{2})\erfc(z)
term of the integral. Now for
z\in\left(\pm C\delta/\sqrt{2\delta},(W(\delta)\pm C\delta)/\sqrt{2\delta}\right)
,
z=O\left(\max\{\delta^{1/2},W(\delta)/\delta^{1/2}\right\})
, therefore
\delta z=O(\max\{\delta^{3/2},\delta^{1/2}W(\delta)\})
and
\delta^{1/2}z^{2}=O(\max\{\delta^{3/2},W(\delta)^{2}/\delta^{1/2}\})
. Therefore if
W(\delta)^{2}/\sqrt{2\delta}\to 0
, then:

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{2}{\delta}}\int_{\pm\frac{C\delta}{\sqrt{2\delta}}}^{\frac{W(\delta)\pm C\delta}{\sqrt{2\delta}}}

\displaystyle O((C\delta+\sqrt{2\delta}z)^{2})\erfc(x)dx=\delta^{-1/2}O\left(\max\{\delta^{2},\delta^{3/2}z,\delta z^{2}\}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O\left(\max\{\delta^{3/2},\delta z,\delta^{1/2}z^{2}\}\right)=O\left(\max\{\delta^{3/2},\delta^{1/2}W(\delta),W(\delta)^{2}/\delta^{1/2}\}\right)\to 0.

Therefore choosing
W(\delta)=\delta^{3/8}
and putting the two limits above together, we get that

\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{L_{0}}^{L_{0}+W(\delta)}f(y)\erfc\left(\frac{y-L_{0}\pm C\delta}{\sqrt{2\delta}}\right)dy\to p,

Now consider the second term of (
29
). Once again chainging variables, we can upper bound the integral for sufficiently small
\delta
, by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{L_{0}+W(\delta)}^{R_{0}}f(y)

\displaystyle\erfc\Big(\frac{y-L_{0}\pm C\delta}{\sqrt{2\delta}}\Big)dy\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}\|f\|_{\infty}}{\sqrt{\delta}}\int_{\frac{\delta^{-1/8}\pm C\delta^{1/2}}{2^{1/2}}}^{\infty}\erfc(u)du

\displaystyle=\frac{2\sqrt{2}\|f\|_{\infty}}{\sqrt{\pi\delta}}\int_{\frac{\delta^{-1/8}\pm C\delta^{1/2}}{2^{1/2}}}^{\infty}\int_{u}^{\infty}e^{-t^{2}}dt\,du\leq\frac{2\sqrt{2}\|f\|_{\infty}}{\sqrt{\pi\delta}}\int_{\frac{\delta^{-1/8}\pm C\delta^{1/2}}{2^{1/2}}}^{\infty}\int_{u}^{\infty}te^{-t^{2}}dt\,du

\displaystyle=\frac{2\sqrt{2}\|f\|_{\infty}}{\sqrt{\pi\delta}}\int_{\frac{\delta^{-1/8}\pm C\delta^{1/2}}{2^{1/2}}}^{\infty}e^{-u^{2}}du\leq\frac{2\sqrt{2}\|f\|_{\infty}}{\sqrt{\pi\delta}}\int_{\frac{\delta^{-1/8}\pm C\delta^{1/2}}{2^{1/2}}}^{\infty}ue^{-u^{2}}du

\displaystyle=O(\delta^{-1/2}e^{-\delta^{-1/4}/2})\xrightarrow[\delta\to 0]{}0,

so putting together both terms of (
29
), we have that
\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{L_{0}}^{R_{0}}f(y)\erfc\left(\frac{y-L_{0}\pm C\delta}{\sqrt{2\delta}}\right)dy\xrightarrow[\delta\to 0]{}p
. We can similarly split up the integral:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{L_{0}}^{R_{0}}f(y)

\displaystyle\erfc\left(\frac{R_{0}-C\delta-y}{\sqrt{2\delta}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{L_{0}}^{R_{0}-W(\delta)}f(y)\erfc\left(\frac{R_{0}-C\delta-y}{\sqrt{2\delta}}\right)dy+\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{R_{0}-W(\delta)}^{R_{0}}f(y)\erfc\left(\frac{R_{0}-C\delta-y}{\sqrt{2\delta}}\right)dy,

where the first term converges to
0
as
\delta\to 0
and the second term converges to
1-p
. But notice that when
y\geq(R_{0}-L_{0})/2-C\delta
, then
\erfc\left(\frac{y-L_{0}+C\delta}{\sqrt{2\delta}}\right)<\erfc\left(\frac{R_{0}-C\delta-y}{\sqrt{2\delta}}\right)
, therefore the lower bound for
\mathbb{P}_{y}(\tau^{LR}<\delta,B_{\tau^{LR}}=L_{\tau^{LR}})
is
0
. Therefore if
\delta
is chosen sufficiently small so that
W(\delta)=\delta^{3/8}<(R_{0}-L_{0})/2-C\delta
, then certainly:

\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{L_{0}}^{R_{0}}f(y)\max\left\{0,\erfc\left(\frac{y-L_{0}+C\delta}{\sqrt{2\delta}}\right)-\erfc\left(\frac{R_{0}-C\delta-y}{\sqrt{2\delta}}\right)\right\}dy\to p,

and thus by sandwiching the result holds.
∎

Proof.

(of Theorem
26
)

By the Feynman-Kac representation theorem (see Theorem 3.47 of
[
18
]
, for example) we have that

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{\infty}u(y,t)dy=e^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}u_{0}(y)\mathbb{P}_{y}(B_{t}\geq a,\tau^{LR}>t)dy,

(30)

for any
a\in\mathbb{R}
. Now we want to show that this representation and Proposition
27
yields the boundary conditions in the statement of Theorem
26
. Conditioning on the value of
B_{t}
and using the Markov property, we can write:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}u_{0}(y)\mathbb{P}_{y}(\tau^{LR}\in[t,t+\delta),

\displaystyle B_{\tau^{LR}}=L_{\tau^{LR}})dy

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}u_{0}(y)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}_{y}(\tau^{LR}\in[t,t+\delta),B_{\tau^{LR}}=L_{\tau^{LR}}|B_{t}=z)\tilde{p}_{t}(y,z)dzdy

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}u_{0}(y)\mathbb{P}_{z}(\tau^{LR}<\delta,B_{\tau^{LR}}=L_{\tau^{LR}})\tilde{p}_{t}(y,z)dzdy,

where
\tilde{p}_{t}(y,z)
is the density function of
\{B_{t}=z,\tau^{LR}>t\}
given
B_{0}=y
; that is
\tilde{p}_{t}(y,z)=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\mathbb{P}_{y}(B_{t}\leq x,\tau^{LR}>t)|_{x=z}
. By taking the partial derivative of (
30
) with respect to
x
, we can calculate that
u(z,t)=e^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}u_{0}(y)\tilde{p}_{t}(y,z)dy
, therefore interchanging the order of intergrals and applying Proposition
27
, we have that

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{\mathbb{R}}u_{0}(y)\mathbb{P}_{y}(\tau^{LR}\in[t,t+\delta),

\displaystyle B_{\tau^{LR}}=L_{\tau^{LR}})dy=\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}_{z}(\tau^{LR}<\delta,B_{\tau^{LR}}=L_{\tau^{LR}})\int_{\mathbb{R}}u_{0}(y)\tilde{p}_{t}(y,z)dydz

\displaystyle=\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-t}u(z,t)\mathbb{P}_{z}(\tau^{LR}<\delta,B_{\tau^{LR}}=L_{\tau^{LR}})dz=\lim_{z\downarrow L_{t}}u_{z}(z,t)e^{-t}=pe^{-t}.

Then note that we can identify the lefthand side of the above equation as the partial derivative with respect to time of
\int_{\mathbb{R}}u_{0}(y)\mathbb{P}_{y}(\tau^{LR}<t,B_{\tau^{LR}}=L_{\tau^{LR}})dy
, therefore defining

f(t):=e^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}u_{0}(y)\mathbb{P}_{y}(\tau^{LR}<t,B_{\tau^{LR}}=L_{\tau^{LR}})dy,

it follows that
f^{\prime}(t)=f(t)+p
and so
f(t)=p(e^{t}-1)
, as required. An identical argument holds for the other boundary condition. ∎

8
A Comparison Theorem for the Free Boundary Problem

The aim of this section is to prove that for any
t=k\delta>0
, the solution
u(x,k\delta)
to the free boundary problem (
2
) with initial condition
\rho
is sandwiched in between the functions
S_{\delta}^{k\delta,-}\rho(x)
and
S_{k\delta}^{\delta,-}\rho(x)
in the sense that

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{\infty}S_{t}^{t/n,-}\rho(x)dx\leq\int_{a}^{\infty}u(x,t)dx\leq\int_{a}^{\infty}S_{t}^{t/n,+}\rho(x)dx.

(31)

We first prove this for the case that
k=1
and for the lower bound. That is, we prove that, for any fixed
\delta>0
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{\infty}C^{R}_{1}e^{\delta}G_{\delta}C^{L}_{1-p(1-e^{-\delta})}\rho(dr)\leq\int_{a}^{\infty}u(x,\delta)dx.

(32)

We can then inductively reapply the proposition to show that for any
k
, then inequality holds for
\int_{a}^{\infty}S_{k\delta}^{\delta,-}\rho(dr)\leq\int_{a}^{\infty}u(dr,t)
. By symmetry, the upper bound similarly holds, so that inequality (
31
) holds for any
t>0
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proposition 28
.

Let
u(x,t)
be the solution to the free boundary problem (
2
) with initial condition
\rho(x)
such that the boundaries
L,R
are locally Lipschitz continuous on
(0,\infty)
. Let
v(x,t)
be the solution to the PDE which satisfies
v_{t}=\frac{1}{2}v_{xx}+v
and has initial condition
\rho_{0}(x):=C^{L}_{1-p(1-e^{-\delta})}\rho(x)
. Then for any
r
:

1\wedge\int_{-\infty}^{r}v(x,\delta)dx\geq\int_{-\infty}^{r}u(x,\delta)dx

(33)

We will prove the above Proposition in a similar fashion to the Proof of 10.3.13 in
[
6
]
, by constructing 2 simpler particle systems, finding their hydrodynamic limits, and constructing a coupling between the two systems.

Proof.

We begin by noting that by Theorem
26
, the inequality (
33
) is equivalent to the inequality:

1\wedge e^{\delta}\int\rho_{0}(x)\mathbb{P}_{x}(B_{\delta}\leq r)dx\geq e^{\delta}\int\rho(x)\mathbb{P}_{x}(B_{\delta}\leq r,\tau^{LR}>\delta)dx

(34)

where
\tau^{LR}:=\inf\{s>0:B_{s}\notin(L_{s},R_{s})\}
and
L_{s},R_{s}
are locally Lipschitz continuous curves such that for all
t\in[0,\delta]

e^{t}\int\rho(x)\mathbb{P}_{x}(\tau^{LR}<t,B_{\tau^{LR}}=L_{\tau^{LR}})dx=p(e^{t}-1).

(35)

e^{t}\int\rho(x)\mathbb{P}_{x}(\tau^{LR}<t,B_{\tau^{LR}}=R_{\tau^{LR}})dx=(1-p)(e^{t}-1).

(36)

Since
e^{\delta}\int\rho(x)\mathbb{P}_{x}(\tau^{LR}>\delta)dx=1
, it suffices to show:

e^{\delta}\int\rho_{0}(x)\mathbb{P}_{x}(B_{\delta}\leq r)dx\geq e^{\delta}\int\rho(x)\mathbb{P}_{x}(B_{\delta}\leq r,\tau^{LR}>\delta)dx

So fix
n
and let
M:=\lceil p(e^{\delta}-1)n\rceil
and
N:=\lfloor(1-p)(e^{\delta}-1)n\rfloor
. Let
B^{(1)}_{i},i=M+1,...,M+N+n
be
N+n
standard Brownian motions, and independently for each
i
distribute
B^{(1)}_{i}(0)
according to the probability measure
\rho_{0}/\int\rho_{0}
. Let
B^{(2)}_{i},i=1,...,M+N+n
be
M+N+n
standard Brownian motions, which are removed when they hit
L_{s}
or
R_{s}
. Couple the particles so that
B^{(1)}_{i}(0)=B^{(2)}_{i}(0)
for
i=M+1,...,M+N+n
and distribute the particles
B^{(2)}_{i}(0),i=1,...,M
according to the measure
(\rho-\rho_{0})/\int(\rho-\rho_{0})
. Thus as
n\to\infty
, it is simple to check that the empirical distribution of
B^{(2)}(0)
converges to
\rho
.

Now we will couple the particles over the time interval
[0,\delta]
as follows. We will call the particles
B^{(1)}_{i}
the
(1)
-particles, and
B^{(2)}_{i}
the
(2)
-particles. Recall that
B^{(1)}_{i}(0)=B^{(2)}_{i}(0)
for
i=M+1,\ldots,M+N+n
, and we will call
\{B^{(1)}_{i}(0),B^{(2)}_{i}(0)\}
a ‘married couple’. Whilst 2 particles are ‘married’, they will be driven by the same Brownian motion.
The remaining uncoupled particles will be called ‘singletons’. We will call a married couple
\{B^{(1)}_{k}(t),B^{(2)}_{\ell}(t)\}
‘good’ if
B^{(1)}_{k}(t)\leq B^{(2)}_{\ell}(t)
and ‘bad’ otherwise.

So suppose that at time
\tau
the particle
B^{(2)}_{i}
exits
(L_{\tau},R_{\tau})
and is removed from the system. Then we describe how the particles are recoupled.

1.

If
B^{(2)}_{i}(\tau-)
is a singleton particle, then when
B^{(2)}_{i}(\tau)
is removed, we do not change the coupling. The numbers of good and bad couples are unchanged.

2.

If
B^{(2)}_{i}(\tau-)
is part of a married couple, say
\{B^{(1)}_{k}(\tau-),B^{(2)}_{i}(\tau-)\}
is a married couple:

(a)

If there are still
\geq N+n

(2)
-particles remaining at time
\tau
:

i.

If
B^{(2)}_{i}(\tau)
is removed because it hits
L_{\tau}
, then take any remaining singleton
(2)
-particle, say
B_{j}^{(2)}(\tau)
, and couple it to
B^{(1)}_{k}(\tau)
to form a married couple
\{B^{(1)}_{k}(\tau),B^{(2)}_{j}(\tau)\}
. Since
B_{i}^{(2)}(\tau-)<B_{j}^{(2)}(\tau-)
, we have either replaced a good couple by a good couple, or a bad couple with a good or bad couple. Therefore the number of bad couples has not increased.

ii.

If
B^{(2)}_{i}(\tau)
is removed because it hits
R_{\tau}
, then take any remaining singleton
(2)
-particle, say
B_{j}^{(2)}(\tau)
, and couple it to
B^{(1)}_{k}(\tau)
to form a married couple
\{B^{(1)}_{k}(\tau),B^{(2)}_{j}(\tau)\}
. Therefore the number of bad couples may increase by at most 1.

Thus in either case, whilst there are
\geq N+n

(2)
-particles remaining, all
(1)
-particles are married.

(b)

If there are
<N+n

(2)
-particles remaining

i.

If
B^{(2)}_{i}(\tau)
is removed because it hits
L_{\tau}
:

•

If
B^{(2)}_{i}(\tau)<B^{(1)}_{k}(\tau)
(ie.
B_{i}^{(2)}(\tau-)
was in a bad couple), then leave
B^{(1)}_{k}
as a singleton, thus decreasing the number of bad couples by 1.

•

If
B^{(1)}_{k}(\tau)\leq B^{(2)}_{i}(\tau)=L_{\tau}
, then consider any
(2)
-particle in a bad couple, and recouple it to
B^{(1)}_{k}
. Since all
(2)
-particles are in
(L_{\tau},R_{\tau})
at time
\tau
, this must form a good couple, and therefore we have decreased the number of bad couples by 1.

In either case, the number of bad couples decreases by 1 (unless there are no bad couples). If there are no bad couples, then no recoupling happens at the time
\tau
.

ii.

Suppose
B^{(2)}_{i}
is removed because it hits
R_{s}
. Then when
B^{(2)}_{i}
is removed, do not recouple. The number of married couples decreases by 1; the removed couple may be good or bad.

Now define:

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}(r)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=M+1}^{M+N+n}\mathbbm{1}_{\{B_{i}^{(1)}(\delta)\leq r\}}

\displaystyle\beta_{n}(r)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=1}^{M+N+n}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\text{$B^{(2)}_{i}(\delta)$ has not been removed}\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{B_{i}^{(2)}(\delta)\leq r\}}

Then by the law of large numbers

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{N+n}\sum_{i=M+1}^{M+N+n}\mathbbm{1}_{\{B_{i}^{(1)}(\delta)\leq r\}}\right]\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\rho_{0}(x)}{\int\rho_{0}}\mathbb{P}_{x}(B_{\delta}\leq r)dx.

Therefore since
(N+n)/n\to 1+(1-p)(e^{\delta}-1)=p+e^{\delta}-pe^{\delta}=e^{\delta}\int\rho_{0}
as
n\to\infty
, we conclude that

\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{n}(r)]\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}e^{\delta}\int\rho_{0}(x)\mathbb{P}_{x}(B_{\delta}\leq r)dx.

Again, by the strong law of large numbers, since
(M+N+n)/n\to e^{\delta}
as
n\to\infty
, thus

\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}[\beta_{n}(r)]\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}e^{\delta}\int\rho(x)\mathbb{P}_{x}(B_{\delta}\leq r,\tau^{LR}>\delta)dx

Therefore to prove the claim it suffices to show that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{n}(r)-\beta_{n}(r)]\geq 0
almost surely for all
r
. Let
A
be the number of
(2)
-particles at time
\delta
. Then by (
35
),(
36
)
\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}[A]=\frac{M+N+n}{n}(1-(1-e^{-\delta}))\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}1
.

Notice that whilst there are
\geq N+n

(2)
-particles alive, the number of married couples is
N+n
, and the number of bad couples is at most the number of particles which hit
R_{s}
, which by (
36
) has expectation
(1-p)M
. Then when there are fewer than
N+n

(2)
-particles alive, we remove a bad couple when a
(2)
-particle hits
L_{s}
(if any bad couples remain). By (
35
), the expected number of times this happens is
\mathbb{E}[p(N+n-A)]
. Let
\mathfrak{B}
be the number of bad couples at time
\delta
. Therefore the expected number of bad couples,
\mathbb{E}\mathfrak{B}
is at most
((1-p)M-p(N+n-A))
. Therefore since as
(1-p)M/n
and
pN/n
both converge to
p(1-p)(e^{\delta}-1)
as
n\to\infty
, thus
\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{B}]\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0
.

Now note that for each good couple
\{B_{k}^{(1)}(\delta),B_{\ell}^{(2)}(\delta)\}
, we have
\mathbbm{1}_{\{B_{k}^{(1)}(\delta)\leq r\}}-\mathbbm{1}_{\{B_{l}^{(2)}(\delta)\leq r\}}\geq 0
, and for every bad couple,
\mathbbm{1}_{\{B_{k}^{(1)}(\delta)\leq r\}}-\mathbbm{1}_{\{B_{\ell}^{(2)}(\delta)\leq r\}}\geq-1
.
Therefore

\alpha_{n}(r)-\beta_{n}(r)\geq\sum_{\text{married couples}}\left(\mathbbm{1}_{\{B_{k}^{(1)}(\delta)\leq r\}}-\mathbbm{1}_{\{B_{l}^{(2)}(\delta)\leq r\}}\right)-\frac{1}{n}\#\{\text{singleton $(2)$-particles}\}.

Let
S_{2}(n)
be the number of singleton
(2)
-particles at time
\delta
. Now by (
35
) and (
36
), each
(2)
-particle is, independently, alive at time
\delta
with probability
e^{-\delta}
. Therefore by the central limit theorem,
\frac{S_{2}(n)-n}{\sqrt{ne^{-\delta}(1-e^{-\delta})}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{d}Z
, where
Z
is a standard Normal random variable. Therefore

\mathbb{E}[S_{2}(n)]\leq M\mathbb{P}(S_{2}(n)>N+n)=M\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{S_{2}(n)-n}{\sqrt{ne^{-\delta}(1-e^{-\delta})}}>N\right)\approx p(e^{\delta}-1)n\mathbb{P}(Z>c\sqrt{n})\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0,

and so as

\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{n}(r)-\beta_{n}(r)]\geq-\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{B}]-\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}[S_{2}(n)]\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0
,
and therefore as

e^{\delta}\int\rho_{0}(x)\mathbb{P}_{x}(B_{\delta}\leq r)dx\geq e^{\delta}\int\rho(x)\mathbb{P}_{x}(B_{\delta}\leq r,\tau^{LR}>\delta)dx,

as required.
∎

Now we can observe that if
\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(x)dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(x)dx=1
, then
\int_{a}^{\infty}g(x)dx\leq\int_{a}^{\infty}h(x)dx
is equivalent to

. Also
\int_{-\infty}^{a}C^{R}_{1}g(x)dx=1\wedge\int_{-\infty}^{a}g(x)dx
for any

. Therefore Proposition
28
implies (

). Thus by induction and symmetry, the ordering (

) holds. We are now finally ready to prove the hydrodynamic limit result of Theorem
1
.

Proof.

(Of Theorem
1
)
Note that by the union bound we have that for any

, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\Big|

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{\infty}\pi^{N}_{\rho,t}(dr)-\int_{a}^{\infty}u(dr,t)\Big|>BN^{-\beta}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Bigg(\int_{a}^{\infty}\pi^{N}_{\rho,t}(dr)-\int_{a}^{\infty}u(r,t)dr>BN^{-\beta}\Bigg)+\mathbb{P}\Bigg(\int_{a}^{\infty}\pi^{N}_{\rho,t}(dr)-\int_{a}^{\infty}u(r,t)dr<-BN^{-\beta}\Bigg).

Then since

\displaystyle+\mathbbm{1}_{\{2e^{t}(e^{t/n}-1)>BN^{-\beta}/2\}},

where for the first term second inequality we have used the bound from Proposition

9
Convergence of the Leftmost and Rightmost Particles

As well as proving the convergence of the empirical density of particles to the solution of the free boundary problem, we also want to prove convergence of the leftmost and rightmost particles to the free boundaries
L_{t}
and
R_{t}
respectively, almost surely and in expectation. This is stated in Theorem
5
and this result will be helpful to prove the convergence of the asymptotic speed in the next section.

In proving these results it will become clear why we sought to prove a probabilistic bound like
1
as we did.

Proposition 29
.

Let
\rho
be an initial distribution such that the solution
(u(x,t),L_{t},R_{t})_{t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R}}
to the free boundary problem (
2
) exists and has locally Lipschitz continuous boundaries. Let
(X^{N}(t))_{t\geq 0}
be the
(N,p)
-BBM with initial condition
\rho
in the sense that the particle locations at time
0
are i.i.d. with distribution
\rho
. Then for fixed
t
,
X_{1}^{N}(t)\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{a.s.}L_{t}
and
X_{N}^{N}(t)\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{a.s.}R_{t}
.

Proof.

To prove this theorem we will use the first Borel-Cantelli lemma. So fix
\epsilon>0
and let
K
be the local Lipschitz constant of
L
for a small interval around
t
. Then define the event
F_{t}^{N}:=\{X_{1}^{N}(t)<L_{t}-2\epsilon\}
. We want to prove that
\sum_{N\geq 1}\mathbb{P}(F_{t}^{N})<\infty
. We will do this by considering the following events such that, when they occur simultaneously (which happens with high probability), we have
X_{1}^{N}(t)\geq L_{t}-2\epsilon
.

Firstly, we want to have
\pi^{N}_{\rho,t-N^{-1/40}}((-\infty,L_{t-N^{-1/40}}))<N^{-1/10}
, which is to say that there are at most
N^{9/10}
particles left of
L_{t-N^{-1/40}}
at time
t-N^{-1/40}
. Call this event
E_{t}^{N,1}
. Choosing
\beta=1/10
in Theorem
1
shows that there exists a constant
C
such that
\mathbb{P}(E_{t}^{N,1})\geq 1-CN^{-11/10}
for
N
sufficiently large.

Let us call the particles which are left of
L_{t-N^{-1/40}}
at time
t-N^{-1/40}
red particles, and say that in a branching event, a red particle branches into two red particles. Next we want that over the time interval
[t-N^{-1/40},t]
, the red particles (of which there are initially at most
N^{9/10}
) have at most
N^{19/20}
total descendants by time
t
. Call this event
E_{t}^{N,2}
. Note that the red particles branch at rate
1
, and we may delete the leftmost particle at branching events, therefore the number of descendants of a single particle in this system is bounded above by a Yule process. Thus the number of descendants of a particle after
N^{-1/40}
time is bounded above by a
\text{Geom}(e^{-N^{-1/40}})
random variable, therefore the probability of
E_{t}^{N,2}
is bounded below by:

\mathbb{P}(E_{t}^{N,2})\geq\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N^{9/10}}G_{i}\leq N^{19/20}\right),

where
G_{i}
are i.i.d.
\text{Geom}\big(e^{-N^{-1/40}}\big)
random variables. Thus by Chebyshev’s inequality, this happens with probability at least
1-\text{Var}\big(\sum_{i=1}^{N^{9/10}}G_{i}\big)/\big(N^{19/20}-\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_{i=1}^{N^{9/10}}G_{i}\big]\big)^{2}\sim 1-N^{-41/40}
, therefore for some constant
c_{2}
, we have
\mathbb{P}(E_{t}^{N,2})\geq 1-c_{2}N^{-41/40}
for
N
sufficiently large.

Let us call the remaining particles green, and say that at each branching event, a green particle branches into two green particles. So next we want that, over the time interval
[t-N^{-1/40},t]
, the green particles have at least
N^{19/20}
branching events in which the leftmost particle is deleted. Call this event
E_{t}^{N,3}
. Each green particle branches at unit rate, and at each branching event, the leftmost particle (red or green) is deleted with probability
p
. Note that, given the event
E_{t}^{N,2}
, there are, for all times in
[t-N^{-1/40},t]
, at least
N-N^{19/20}
green particles alive (since there are at most
N^{19/20}
red particles). Therefore we can bound the probability of
E_{t}^{N,3}
given
E_{t}^{N,2}
below by:

\mathbb{P}(E_{t}^{N,3}|E_{t}^{N,2})\geq\mathbb{P}(H\geq N^{19/20})
,
where
H
is a
\text{Pois}(pN^{-1/40}(N-N^{19/20}))
random variable. Then by the Chernoff bound
\mathbb{P}(\text{Pois}(\lambda)\leq a)\leq e^{a-\lambda}\lambda^{a}a^{-a}=\exp(-\lambda+a(1+\ln(\lambda/a)))
, we get that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(E_{t}^{N,3}|E_{t}^{N,2})

\displaystyle\geq 1-\exp\left(-pN^{39/40}+pN^{37/40}+N^{19/20}\left(1+\ln(pN^{1/40}-pN^{-1/40})\right)\right)

\displaystyle\sim 1-\exp(-pN^{39/40}),

therefore for some constant
c_{3}
we have

\mathbb{P}(E_{t}^{N,2}\cap E_{t}^{N,3})=\mathbb{P}(E_{t}^{N,2})\mathbb{P}(E_{t}^{N,3}|E_{t}^{N,2})>1-c_{2}N^{-41/40}-c_{3}\exp(-pN^{39/40}).

Finally, consider the Brownian motions driving the
N
particles. Consider that the
(N,p)
-BBM process can be embedded inside a branching Brownian motion with
N
particles at time
t-N^{-1/40}
. Say the
i
th
BBM has particles
\mathcal{B}_{u}
for
u\in\mathcal{N}^{i}(t)
at time
t
. We want that the maximum displacement of these Brownian motions over the time interval
[t-N^{-1/40},t]
to be at most
\epsilon/3
; that is,

\max_{i=1}^{N}\max_{u\in\mathcal{N}^{i}(t)}\sup_{s\in[t-N^{-1/40},t]}|B_{u}(s)-B_{u}(t-N^{-1/40})|\leq\epsilon/3.

Call this event
E_{t}^{N,4}
, and note that we have by the union bound, the many-to-one lemma, and the reflection principle

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(E_{t}^{N,4})

\displaystyle\geq 1-N\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{s\in[t-N^{-1/40},t]}|B_{u}(s)-B_{u}(t-N^{-1/40})|>\epsilon/3\text{ for some }u\in\mathcal{N}^{i}(t)\right)

\displaystyle\geq 1-Ne^{N^{-1/40}}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{s\in[t-N^{-1/40},t]}|B_{u}(s)-B_{u}(t-N^{-1/40})|>\epsilon/3\right)

\displaystyle=1-4Ne^{N^{-1/40}}\mathbb{P}(B(N^{-1/40})>\epsilon/3)

\displaystyle=1-12Ne^{N^{-1/40}}\exp(-\epsilon^2 N^{1/40}/18)/(\epsilon N^{1/80}\sqrt{2\pi}),

where
B
is a standard Brownian motion.

We will now show that on the event
E_{t}^{N,1}\cap E_{t}^{N,2}\cap E_{t}^{N,3}\cap E_{t}^{N,4}
, all particles are to the right of
L_{t-N^{-1/40}}-\epsilon
at time
t
. On the events
E^{N,1}_{t}
and
E^{N,2}_{t}
, there are at most
N^{19/20}
red particles at time
t
. On
E^{N,4}_{t}
, all green particles never go to the left of
L_{t-N^{-1/40}}-\epsilon/3
during the time interval
[t-N^{-1/40},t]
. Under
E^{N,3}_{t}
, during the time interval
[t-N^{-1/40},t]
, there are at least
N^{19/20}
events in which a green particle branches and the leftmost particle is simultaneously deleted. Suppose that at time
t
there is at least one red particle remaining which has not been deleted due to one of the
N^{19/20}
branching events of a green particle in which the leftmost particle is deleted. This means it must not have been the leftmost particle at any of these branching times. Since green particles never go to the left of
t-N^{-1/40}-\epsilon/3
during
[t-N^{-1/40},t]
, this red particle must have been, at some point in
[t-N^{-1/40},t]
, to the right of
L_{t-N^{-1/40}}-\epsilon/3
. Therefore, due to the event
E^{N,4}_{t}
, this red particle must always be right of
L_{t-N^{-1/40}}-\epsilon
during
[t-N^{-1/40},t]
. Therefore by time
t
, any red particle which has not been deleted is to the right of
L_{t-N^{-1/40}}-\epsilon
and all green particles are to the right of
L_{t-N^{-1/40}}-\epsilon/3
. Therefore we can conclude that at time
t
, all particles are to the right of
L_{t-N^{-1/40}}-\epsilon
.

Then as
L
is by assumption Lipschitz continuous in a small interval around
t
, with constant
K
, thus
|L_{t-N^{-1/40}}-L_{t}|<KN^{-1/40}
. For
N
sufficiently large,
KN^{-1/40}<\epsilon
, so then
X_{1}^{N}(t)>L_{t}-2\epsilon
for
N
sufficiently large. Therefore by the union bound and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{1}^{N}(t)<L_{t}

\displaystyle-\epsilon-KN^{-1/40})=\mathbb{P}(F_{t}^{N})\leq 1-\mathbb{P}(E_{t}^{N,1}\cap E_{t}^{N,2}\cap E_{t}^{N,3}\cap E_{t}^{N,4})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}((E_{t}^{N,1})^{c}\cup(E_{t}^{N,2}\cap E_{t}^{N,3})^{c}\cup(E_{t}^{N,4})^{c})

\displaystyle\leq c_{1}N^{-11/10}+c_{2}N^{-41/40}+c_{3}\exp\left(-pN^{39/40}\right)+c_{4}N^{79/80}\exp\left(-\epsilon^{2}N^{1/40}/18\right)

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{c}N^{-41/40}

for some constant
\tilde{c}
. Therefore
\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(F_{t}^{N})<\infty
, and therefore by the first Borel-Cantelli lemma,
X_{1}^{N}(t)<L_{t}-2\epsilon
occurs only finitely often with probability
1
.

Next we will prove that
X_{1}^{N}(t)>L_{t}+\epsilon
finitely often with probability
1
. Since
u
is continuous and positive on
(L_{t},R_{t})
, therefore
\ell:=\int_{L_{t}+\epsilon}^{\infty}u(y,t)dy<1
, and so applying Theorem
1
with
B=1-\ell
and
\beta=0
, for some constant
C
and for
N
sufficiently large:

\mathbb{P}(X_{1}^{N}(t)>L_{t}+\epsilon)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\int_{L_{t}+\epsilon}^{\infty}\pi^{N}_{t}\rho(y)dy-\int_{L_{t}+\epsilon}^{\infty}u(y,t)dy\right|<1-\ell\right)\leq C(1-\ell)^{-9}N^{-2},

therefore since
\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}C(1-\ell)^{-9}N^{-2}<\infty
, thus by the Borel-Cantelli lemma, the event
\{X_{1}^{N}(t)>L_{t}+\epsilon\}
occurs only finitely often with probability
1
.

Combining the upper and lower bounds, we have that
\{|X_{1}^{N}(t)-L_{t}|>2\epsilon\}
occurs finitely often with probability
1
. Therefore since
\epsilon
was arbitrary, we have that
X_{1}^{N}(t)\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{a.s.}L_{t}
. By symmetry the result holds for
X_{N}^{N}(t)\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{a.s.}R_{t}
.
∎

Proposition 30
.

Let
\rho
be an initial distribution such that the solution to the free boundary problem (
2
) exists and has locally Lipschitz continuous boundaries. Then for fixed
t
,
\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{N}(t)]\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}L_{t}
and
\mathbb{E}[X_{N}^{N}(t)]\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}R_{t}
.

Proof.

For ease let
Z=Z^{N}_{t}:=|X_{1}^{N}(t)-L_{t}|
, and fix some
t\in(0,\infty)
and
\delta>0
. We can write:

\mathbb{E}[Z]=\mathbb{E}\Big[Z\big(\mathbbm{1}_{\{Z\leq\delta\}}+\mathbbm{1}_{\{\delta<Z\leq(\log N)^{2}\}}+\mathbbm{1}_{\{(\log N)^{2}<Z\}}\big)\Big]\leq\delta+(\log N)^{2}\mathbb{P}(Z>\delta)+\mathbb{E}[Z\mathbbm{1}_{\{Z>(\log N)^{2}\}}].

(38)

First let us recall that from the proof of Proposition
29
that, for fixed
\epsilon>0
and fixed

, we have that for

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\{X_{1}^{N}(t)<L_{t}-\delta\}\cup\{X_{1}^{N}(t)>L_{t}+\delta/2\})\leq\tilde{c}N^{-41/40}

therefore if we choose
\epsilon=\delta/2
and
N
such that
KN^{-1/40}<\epsilon
, then it follows that
(\log N)^{2}\mathbb{P}(Z>\delta)\to 0
as
N\to\infty
.

To show that
\mathbb{E}[Z\mathbbm{1}_{\{Z>(\log N)^{2}\}}]\xrightarrow{N\to\infty}0
, note that the
(N,p)
-BBM with initial distribution
\rho
is embedded inside a branching Brownian motion (BBM) started from
N
particles each with distribution
\rho
. Let the
i
th
particle of the BBM at time

have, at time
t
, offspring
u\in\mathcal{N}_{i}(t)
, with locations
B_{u}^{i}(t)
for
u\in\mathcal{N}_{i}(t)
. Therefore

Z\leq\max_{i=1}^{N}\max_{u\in\mathcal{N}_{i}(t)}|B_{u}^{i}(t)|+|L_{t}|.

Then we observe that

\displaystyle Z\mathbbm{1}_{\{Z>(\log N)^{2}\}}\leq\max_{i=1}^{N}\max_{i\in\mathcal{N}_{i}(t)}(|B_{u}^{i}(t)|+|L_{t}|)\mathbbm{1}_{\big\{\max_{i=1}^{N}\max_{i\in\mathcal{N}_{i}(t)}|B_{u}^{i}(t)|>(\log N)^{2}-|L_{t}|\big\}}

so that combining (

\mathbb{E}[Z\mathbbm{1}_{\{Z>(\log N)^{2}\}}]\leq Ne^{t}\mathbb{E}[(|B(t)|+L_{t})\mathbbm{1}_{\{|B(t)|>(\log N)^{2}-L_{t}\}}],

as

∎

10
Convergence of Speeds

In this section, we will prove Theorem

-BBM process converges to the travelling wave speed

.

To prove this result, we will use the fact that the

-BBM is monotonic, and, as viewed from its leftmost (resp. rightmost) particle is positive Harris recurrent (Propositions
7
and
9
), the fact that the FBP (

(Proposition

-BBM converge in expectation to the left and right free boundaries of the free boundary problem (Proposition

). First we prove a technical lemgnma.

Lemma 31
.

Let
\bar{\pi}^{N}

\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{N,p}(t)|X^{N,p}(0)\sim\bar{\pi}^{N}]=v_{N}t

v_{N}:=\lim_{t\to\infty}X_{1}^{N,p}(t)/t

Fix
T\geq 0
and consider the
(N,p)
-BBM with initial distribution

. Now consider the random variable
X^{N,p}_{1}(t)-X^{N,p}_{1}(0)
and say it has distribution
\xi^{N,p}

(X^{N,p}(t))_{t\geq 0}
starts from the stationary distribution
\bar{\pi}^{N}

X^{N,p}_{1}(it)-X^{N,p}_{1}(it-t)

[
14
]
, for example) to show that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{N,p}(t)|X^{N,p}(0)\sim\bar{\pi}^{N}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{N,p}(t)-X_{1}^{N,p}(0)|X^{N,p}(0)\sim\bar{\pi}^{N}]

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(X_{1}^{N,p}(it+t)-X_{1}^{N,p}(it))=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}X_{1}^{N,p}(nt)=v_{N}t

∎

Proof.

(Of Theorem
4
)
Consider the travelling wave solution of the free boundary problem (
2

3

\hat{u}(x):=\frac{2p}{\sqrt{2-c_{p}^{2}}}e^{-c_{p}x}\sin\left(x\sqrt{2-c_{p}^{2}}\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\{-R_{0}+c_{p}t\leq x\leq c_{p}t\}}

for

and
c_{p}=-\sqrt{\frac{2\log^{2}(p/(1-p))}{\log^{2}(p/(1-p))+\pi^{2}}}
for

, and

.
Then by monotonicity (Proposition

), we have that
\mathbb{E}_{\hat{u}}[X_{1}^{N,p}(t)]\leq\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{N,p}(t)|X^{N,p}(0)\sim\bar{\pi}^{N}]
for any

and

. Notice that the expectation
\mathbb{E}_{\hat{u}}[X_{1}^{N,p}(t)]
is the expectation conditional on each particle having i.i.d. initial distribution drawn from the probability distribution
\hat{u}
on
\mathbb{R}
, whereas
\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{N,p}(t)|X^{N,p}(0)\sim\bar{\pi}^{N}]
is the expectation conditional on the initial configuration being drawn from the probability distribution
\bar{\pi}^{N}
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

Since the travelling wave solution has linear and therefore Lipschitz continuous boundaries, therefore we can apply Proposition
30
to
\mathbb{E}_{\hat{u}}[X_{1}^{N,p}(t)]
. Therefore
\mathbb{E}_{\hat{u}}[X_{1}^{N,p}(t)]\to\bar{L}_{t}
as

, where
\bar{L}_{t}=c_{p}t-R_{0}
is the position of the left free boundary in the FBP (
2
) with initial condition
\hat{u}
, as given by Proposition

. So fix
\epsilon>0
and let

. Moreover, let

be sufficiently large so that

. So then

Similarly, we can consider the stationary distribution of
X^{N,p}
as viewed from its rightmost particle which we will call
\hat{\pi}^{N}
, and the travelling wave solution of (
2
