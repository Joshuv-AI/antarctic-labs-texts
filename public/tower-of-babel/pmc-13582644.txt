Global Variation in Fusion Assessment Practices Following Anterior Cervical Discectomy and Fusion
Authors: Elyette Lugo, Krystell E Ceballos-Alvarez, Sydney Thai, K Daniel Riew, Sang Hun Lee, S Tim Yoon, Christopher Martin, Samuel Cho, Amit Jain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy DesignCross-sectional survey.ObjectivesTo characterize global variation in imaging modalities, fusion criteria, and decision-making practices for assessing fusion following anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF).MethodsA modified 30-item survey using Delphi-informed methodology was distributed to global cervical spine experts selected by research impact. Response rate was 40%. Items addressed surgeon demographics, preferred fixation and interbody techniques, bracing protocols, imaging strategies, and perceived strength of evidence for fusion assessment criteria. Responses were analyzed descriptively.ResultsSubstantial variation was observed in imaging preferences and fusion criteria. Most surgeons preferred plate fixation for both 1–2 level ACDF (mean 71%) and 3–4 level ACDF (mean 92%). Metallic cages were the most frequently used interbody devices, with mean preferences of 40% for 1–2 levels and 42% for 3–4 levels. Fusion status and patient satisfaction were the primary determinants of follow-up duration. Flexion–extension radiographs and CT were the most frequently used modalities. The strongest endorsed criteria for fusion were <1 mm interspinous motion, <5° angular motion, and graft bridging bone ≥2 mm on CT, though up to 20% of surgeons still rated these measures as moderate or poor evidence. While 77.8% endorsed the need for standardized criteria, only half were aware of existing guidelines.ConclusionsAlthough commonly used measures such as <1mm interspinous motion, <5° angular motion, and CT-based extra-graft bridging bone are supported by prior studies, the absence of standardized diagnostic criteria limits confidence in their validity and highlights the urgent need for a universally accepted, evidence-based standard for fusion after ACDF.Keywords: cervical, discectomy, ACDF, pseudarthrosis

Introduction

Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) is among the most frequently performed spinal procedures worldwide, commonly indicated for cervical radiculopathy, myelopathy, and degenerative disc disease. 1 Early clinical improvement is often attributed to effective neural decompression and alignment, while long-term success depends on achieving solid bony fusion. 2 However, complications such as pseudarthrosis and reoperation remain a concern. 3 Reliable confirmation of fusion is therefore essential for patient care, research comparability, and surgical decision-making.

Despite its importance, no universal standard exists for assessing fusion after ACDF.4-6 In fact, recent FDA IDE trials have demonstrated significant variability in the radiographic criteria used to define fusion. 7 The literature reveals marked variability regarding both the preferred imaging modalities used (most often dynamic flexion-extension radiographs or computed tomography (CT)) and the thresholds applied to define fusion.8-11 In a systematic review of 39 studies, Lin et al documented interspinous motion (ISM) thresholds ranging from no motion to <3 mm and angular motion cutoffs from 1° to 5°. 12 Similarly, definitions of extra-graft bridging bone (ExGBB) on CT remain inconsistent. Song et al demonstrated that an ISM cutoff of approximately 0.9 mm yielded optimal diagnostic accuracy. 13 While Riew et al identified ExGBB on CT as the most reliable single radiographic criterion, it is not universally adopted. 14 Such inconsistencies hinder cross-study comparisons, complicate clinical decision-making, and limit confidence in reported outcomes. 15

The absence of standardized fusion assessment criteria also raises important concerns regarding the interpretation of prior ACDF outcome studies. Many investigators evaluating interbody materials, graft choices, or fixation techniques rely on heterogeneous or inconsistently validated definitions of fusion, making meaningful comparisons difficult and compromising overall results.4,10 As a result, studies reporting no differences between implants or techniques may be due to a limitation in fusion assessment rather than true clinical equivalence, which in turn can discourage the adoption and approval of new technologies.

The criteria chosen in this survey were based on prior evidence-based investigations. A systematic review by Rhee et al demonstrated moderate evidence of <1mm ISM being more accurate than Cobb angle method for assessing anterior cervical fusion. 10 Subsequent studies have shown that angular motion measurement has a higher inter-rater reliability that does not require image calibration when compared to linear measurements; however, its diagnostic accuracy is limited when used alone. 16 Recent work suggests that an angular motion threshold of <5° may improve sensitivity compared to the traditional 4-degree threshold cutoff, which has been associated with missed pseudarthrosis. 17 CT-based assessment of ExGBB has been repeatedly validated as the most reliable single radiographic criterion when compared to surgical exploration, demonstrating high sensitivity and specificity.14,18 Furthermore, ExGBB is more reliable and valid than inter-graft bridging bone and is associated with increased motion segment stability and decreased trabecular bone fracture risk.19,20

Since fusion assessment remains a source of uncertainty, this study aimed to characterize global practice patterns among spine experts, focusing on (1) imaging modalities and timing, (2) radiographic and CT-based criteria deemed evidentiary for fusion, and (3) surgeon confidence in these methods. By identifying areas of convergence and divergence, this work seeks to inform future consensus-building and the development of standardized criteria for fusion assessment following ACDF.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

We conducted a cross-sectional, web-based survey of global spine experts to evaluate global practices and preferences in assessing fusion after anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF). Surgeons were deemed as a domain “expert” based on meeting any of the following criteria: 50-top ranked cervical spine researchers on Web of Science based on citations in cervical spine research, past presidents of Cervical Spine Research Society, or Cervical Spine Research Society Asia-Pacific, or being a member of the AO Spine Knowledge Forum steering committee. The final survey was distributed via email to 111 surgeons across the five global regions (Asia Pacific, Europe and Africa, Latin America, Middle East, and North America) between January 1 and February 1, 2025. Three reminder emails were sent during this period. Participation was voluntary, and all participants provided digital informed consent agreeing to the use of their responses for research purposes. Because the study collected no patient data, formal institutional review board approval was not required.

Survey Details

The questionnaire contained 30 items covering surgeon demographics, surgical practice, and methods of fusion assessment. Demographic questions included geographic region, specialty, years in practice, practice setting, and annual ACDF case volume. Surgical practice questions addressed fixation and interbody techniques. Fusion assessment questions covered imaging modalities (plain radiographs, flexion–extension radiographs, CT, and MRI), timing of evaluation (3, 6, and 12 months, or later), and specific radiographic criteria (<1 mm interspinous motion [ISM], bridging bone, absence of radiolucent lines, and structured scoring systems such as the Bridwell classification). Respondents were also asked about the role of clinical indicators in determining fusion and their confidence in each assessment method. Confidence was rated using a 4-point Likert scale with the following anchors: Strong Evidence, Moderate to Strong Evidence, Moderate to Poor Evidence, and Poor Evidence. Both multiple-choice and free-text responses were collected. The complete questionnaire and corresponding responses are available from the authors upon reasonable request.

Statistical Analysis

Survey responses were summarized using descriptive statistics. Categorical variables were reported as absolute (n) and relative (%) frequencies with denominators shown for each item. Statistical analysis of data was performed using the Chi-square test. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

Results

Participant Characteristics

Of 111 invited surgeons, 44 responded (40% response rate). Respondents were majority from North America (26/43, 60.5%), followed by Europe and Southern Africa (8/43, 18.6%) and Asia Pacific (6/43, 14.0%). Majority were orthopedic spine surgeons (23/44) or neurosurgeons (18/44). Over half of participants (24/44, 55%) had more than 10 years in practice. For 1–2 level ACDF, 15/44 (34.1%) performed 0–25 cases annually, while for 3–4 level ACDF, 33/44 (75%) reported the same volume. A left-sided surgical approach was most common (24/44, 54.5%), followed by right-sided (15/44, 34.1%) or patient-specific variation (5/44, 11.4%) (Table 1).

Fixation Method and Interbody Technique Preferences

For 1–2 level ACDF, plate fixation was most commonly preferred (71% ± 37%), followed by zero-profile devices (19% ± 31%), and cages without screw fixation (10% ± 26%). For 3–4 level ACDF, plate fixation was used in nearly all cases (92% ± 17%), with minimal use of zero-profile (7% ± 17%) and no-screw cages (1% ± 4%).

Metallic cages were the most common interbody device (mean 40% ± 44%), followed by allograft (27% ± 42%), PEEK cages (16% ± 31%), hybrid PEEK+metal (10% ± 26%), and iliac crest autograft (5% ± 16%). Surgeons reported that fixation choice was guided by patient age, bone quality, comorbidities, and pathology (instability, deformity, prior surgery), as well as anatomical considerations such as level of surgery and dysphagia risk.

Plate fixation was valued for its stability and fusion support, whereas zero-profile devices were often reserved for adjacent segment disease or patients at high risk of dysphagia. Interbody selection was influenced by bone quality, alignment goals, and implant availability. PEEK and titanium cages were commonly favored for their structural reliability and radiographic compatibility, with titanium often chosen in younger patients for its strength and osseointegration. Iliac crest autograft and allograft were selectively used in high-risk or infectious cases to enhance fusion potential. Cost and implant availability were also frequently cited as determining factors. Preferred fixation methods and interbody techniques are summarized in Table 2.

Surgeon Practices for Postoperative Monitoring and Bracing in ACDF

Many surgeons (18/44, 40.9%) discontinued routine follow-up at 1 year once fusion was confirmed, while 11/44 (25.0%) stopped at 6 months and 10/44 (22.7%) at 2 years (Figure 1).

For 1–2 level cervical fusions, bracing practices varied. Rigid cervical collars were used full-time by 6/42 (14.3%), full-time with exceptions (e.g., eating or sleeping) by 10/42 (23.8%) and rarely or never by 19/42 (45.2%). Soft collars (e.g., foam collars) were used full-time by 6/38 (15.8%), for comfort only by 5/38 (13.2%), and rarely or never by 19/38 (50.0%). For 3–4 level ACDF, rigid collars were more frequently prescribed, with 10/30 (33.3%) recommending full-time use and 11/30 (35.7%) recommending full-time with exceptions (Figure 2).

When asked what determined discharge from follow-up, most respondents cited confirmed fusion (32/40, 80%), followed by patient satisfaction (29/45, 64.4%), time after the index operation (24/45, 53.3%), and patient-reported outcomes (15/45, 33.3%). A minority (7/45, 15.6%) selected other factors.

Assessing Fusion

Most respondents (35/45, 78%) indicated that standardized radiographic criteria for fusion are very important. Awareness of existing definitions was evenly divided: 22/44 (50%) reported familiarity with established criteria, citing Bridwell, ISM thresholds, and FDA IDE protocols, while 22/44 (50%) reported no awareness (Table 3).

Across time points, static radiographs (lateral ± AP) were the most consistently used modality: 26/38 (68%) at 3 months, 30/38 (79%) at 6 months, and 29/38 (76%) at 1 year. Use of dynamic flexion–extension radiographs increased over time, from 7/37 (19%) at 6 weeks to 32/39 (82%) at 6 months and 32/37 (87%) at 1 year. Computed tomography (CT) was infrequently obtained in the early postoperative period but was used by 26/40 (65%) of respondents at 1 year. Advanced imaging was rarely reported; MRI was ordered by 10/15 (67%) of surgeons who used additional imaging at 1 year, while SPECT/CT was limited to a small subset (33% at 1–2 years) (Figure 3).

Discussion

The findings of this global survey demonstrate the wide variation in how fusion is assessed after ACDF. Large differences in imaging use, radiographic thresholds, and follow-up duration highlight the absence of a global consensus. Despite the widespread use of ACDF and its established clinical benefits, no standardized definition of fusion currently exists, complicating both clinical decision-making and limiting comparability across studies, while reflecting marked heterogeneity in radiographic fusion criteria. 7

It is important to acknowledge that radiographic pseudarthrosis does not uniformly correlate with poor clinical outcomes. Lambrechts et al demonstrated that among patients undergoing ACDF, 36% had radiographic pseudarthrosis on dynamic radiographs, yet only 4.9% required revision surgery. 21 Notably, patients with pseudarthrosis not requiring revision had worse Neck Disability Index (NDI), VAS neck, and VAS arm scores at 3 months, but these differences resolved by 1 year, indicating that clinical equivalence emerges over time rather than being present immediately. 21 Similarly, Crawford et al found that in the control ACDF arm of an FDA IDE trial, 44 of 345 patients (13%) had radiographic nonunion at 24 months with comparable patient-reported outcomes, although the nonunion group had significantly higher rates of secondary surgery at the index level over 84 months of follow-up (21% vs. 7%, p = 0.009). 22 Importantly, this discordance is not permanent. Lee et al reported that pseudarthrosis persisting at 2 years behaves differently, with only approximately 33% eventually achieving fusion and the nonunion group demonstrating significantly worse arm pain and NDI at 5-year follow-up. 23 Taken together, these findings suggest that clinical presentation should be considered alongside radiographic fusion status, and that radiographic pseudarthrosis alone, in the absence of symptoms, may not warrant intervention. However, symptom-radiograph discordance at 1 year does not exclude late deterioration, and the benefits of solid fusion may emerge only on longer follow-up. Because the magnitude of motion across the construct does not appear to predict patient-reported outcomes, it remains unclear which patients will become symptomatic from a nonunion, underscoring the need to interpret radiographic findings in the context of patients’ clinical course and to identify those at risk for late deterioration, who may benefit from closer surveillance or intervention. 24

With this clinical context in mind, surgeons in this survey most often favored CT-based criteria and dynamic radiographs with strict thresholds (<1 mm ISM and <5° angular motion) as the strongest indicators of fusion.13,17,19,25 The top-rated criterion was ExGBB ≥2 mm with remodeling on CT (69%), followed by intraoperative confirmation with mechanical perturbation (67%) and <1 mm ISM with or without angular motion (47%). However, even these criteria received dissenting ratings: 6% considered ExGBB unreliable, 15% rated intraoperative confirmation as moderate-to-poor or poor, and 20% expressed similar concerns about ISM with angular motion. These findings illustrate the variability in perceived reliability of even the most stringent criteria, reinforcing the need for standardized guidelines. This is consistent with the findings of Martin et al, which suggest that even among experienced spine surgeons, there was only modest agreement on fusion status using flexion-extension radiographs, showing the limitations of dynamic imaging when used in isolation.16,26 Importantly, these findings characterize how experts assess fusion and should not be interpreted as an endorsement of routine advanced imaging in all patients. Rather, the value of confirming fusion status lies primarily in standardizing outcome research and in risk stratifying patients.

Preferences for fixation and interbody devices differed by the number of levels fused. For 3–4 level ACDF, surgeons more often chose plate fixation (92%) and used rigid collars (69% full-time or with exceptions). This likely reflects concern for pseudarthrosis in longer constructs. These findings are consistent with the literature, which consistently shows higher nonunion rates in multilevel ACDF compared to single-level procedures.27,28 However, the choice of fusion constructs and use of autografts does not appear to influence fusion or overall complication rates following 1 or 2-level ACDF. 29 Lee et al reported that patients who had 2–3 level ACDF had significantly higher pseudarthrosis rates than those with single-level ACDF, with odds ratios of 1.844 and 3.147. 30 Other studies, including Lin et al and Wewel et al, have also shown that the risk of nonunion increases with each additional level fused.12,27

The timing and duration of follow-up also varied. Most surgeons stopped follow-up at 1 year once fusion was confirmed, but more than half extended it to 2 years or longer. This likely reflects concern for delayed pseudarthrosis, especially in patients with poor bone quality or smoking history. Lee et al found that pseudarthrosis was detected in 32.6% of patients, mainly after 1 year, with persistent nonunion in some patients at 2 years, particularly in multilevel cases. 30 These findings align with current literature from de Vries et al, who reported pooled fusion rates of 88.1% at 1 year and 91.8% at 2 years following ACDF, providing a useful benchmark for expected radiographic fusion over time. 31

Most surgeons in this study (77.8%) stressed the importance of standardized radiographic criteria, but only half were aware of existing definitions. This shows both the need for consensus and better awareness and adoption of validated criteria. 4 As summarized in Table 3, commonly cited studies and reference frameworks have utilized a wide range of radiographic and clinical definitions to assess fusion following ACDF, which vary widely in imaging modality, motion thresholds, and degree of validation. The results here are consistent with the North American Spine Society recommendations, but the low awareness rate indicates that more work is needed to disseminate and implement these guidelines globally. Without a unified standard, reported fusion rates and treatment outcomes will continue to vary widely.

In the context of our survey findings, the lack of standardization in fusion assessment has important implications for how prior ACDF outcome studies have been interpreted. The variability in fusion definition observed among surveyed surgeons and summarized in Table 3 suggests that many of these studies rely on heterogeneous or inconsistent criteria.4,7,10 As a result, null or no-difference findings reported in studies evaluating implants or techniques may be due to a limitation in fusion assessment rather than true equivalence. This uncertainty can limit confidence in the clinical value of new technologies and discourage their future implementation. Therefore, standardization could improve the interpretability, consistency, and clinical relevance of future studies. Our study represents a step towards establishing validated, standardized criteria for fusion assessment.

Our results suggest a discrepancy between evidence-based criteria and real-world clinical practice. Although most surgeons reported using criteria supported by stronger diagnostic evidence, such as <1mm interspinous motion and CT-based ExGBB, a substantial portion relied on less well-validated thresholds. This mismatch further emphasizes the need for standardized, evidence-based fusion criteria to improve consistency and clinical decision-making.

While these findings provide valuable insight into global variation, several limitations remain. The response rate was modest (40%) even among a highly selective group, thus limiting the generalizability of the results to the broader spine community. Further, some regions were underrepresented.

Conclusion

The present study further demonstrates that assessment of fusion after ACDF remains highly variable. Although certain criteria, such as <1 mm ISM, <5° angular motion, and CT-based ExGBB, are supported by prior studies, their validity remains incompletely established in the absence of a standard diagnostic criterion. The ongoing use of multiple unstandardized principles poses a risk of inconsistent fusion reporting and underscores the need for a standardized, evidence-based approach to advance patient care.
