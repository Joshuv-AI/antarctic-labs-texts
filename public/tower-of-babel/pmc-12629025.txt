First robot-assisted radical prostatectomy using Hugo™ robotic-assisted surgery system in a post-kidney transplant patient: a case report
Authors: Miruzato Fukuda, Tatsuya Ihara, Manabu Kamiyama, Katsuyuki Miki, Takayoshi Yokoyama, Yuki Nakamura, Yasuo Ishii, Shinji Uragami
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRobot-assisted radical prostatectomy in kidney transplant recipients presents unique technical challenges owing to anatomical alterations. However, Hugo™ robotic-assisted surgery system, with its independently mounted robotic arms, may offer advantages in such complex cases. Herein, we report the first case of robot-assisted radical prostatectomy using a Hugo robotic-assisted surgery system in a post-kidney transplant patient.Case presentationA 55-year-old Japanese male with a history of ABO-incompatible living donor kidney transplantation was diagnosed with high-risk prostate cancer (cT2bN0M0, Gleason score 4 + 4 = 8) at 1-year post-transplant. Robot-assisted radical prostatectomy was performed using a Hugo robotic-assisted surgery system. Despite encountering dense adhesions that led to inadvertent cystotomy during prevesical dissection, the procedure was completed within 336 minutes and with blood loss of 50 mL. Postoperative course was uneventful, with stable renal function, and pathological examination revealed a pT2bN0M0 adenocarcinoma with negative surgical margins.ConclusionThis case demonstrates the technical feasibility of Hugo robotic-assisted surgery system for radical prostatectomy in kidney transplant recipients. This offers practical insights into overcoming anatomical challenges post-transplantation, and may inform broader applications of complex pelvic procedures in this population.Keywords: Robot-assisted surgery, Hugo robotic-assisted surgery system, Radical prostatectomy, Kidney transplantation, Immunosuppression, Post-transplant complications

Background

Robot-assisted surgery has attracted significant interest because of its precise and minimally invasive nature. Hugo™ robotic-assisted surgery (RAS) system was introduced by Medtronic in 2021 (Medtronic Ltd, Fridley, MN), and it represents a technological advancement featuring independently mounted robotic arms and an open console with three-dimensional high-definition visualization. This system enables surgeons to tailor port placement according to individual anatomy and procedural needs. Recent studies have demonstrated its safety and efficacy, reporting outcomes comparable to the established da Vinci system [1, 2].

Patients who underwent living donor kidney transplantation are at an increased risk of malignancies due to immunosuppressive therapy, with non-Hodgkin lymphoma, non-melanoma skin cancers, and renal cell carcinoma being the most prevalent, and prostate cancer representing a significant concern [3]. These patients present unique surgical challenges, including anatomical alterations from previous transplant surgery, potential interference from the transplanted kidney, and tissue fragility due to immunosuppression [4]. Although several case reports have documented successful radical prostatectomy using robotic assistance in post-kidney transplant patients [4–6], there have been no reports using Hugo RAS system.

Herein, we present the world’s first case of robot-assisted radical prostatectomy (RARP) using Hugo RAS in a post-living donor kidney transplant patient, demonstrating the feasibility and potential benefits of this novel approach.

Case presentation

A 55-year-old Japanese male patient with end-stage renal disease secondary to diabetic nephropathy initially underwent peritoneal dialysis at 51 years of age and subsequently required additional hemodialysis sessions. At the age of 54 years, he successfully received an ABO-incompatible living donor kidney transplant from his spouse, enabling discontinuation of dialysis therapy. Following transplantation, the patient maintained stable graft function with an immunosuppressive regimen comprising tacrolimus (6 mg/day, target trough level of 4–6 ng/mL), mycophenolate mofetil (1500 mg/day), and prednisolone (5 mg/day).

The patient’s prostate-specific antigen (PSA) levels showed a concerning pattern: 7.88 ng/mL pre-transplant, with no radiological evidence of malignancy at that time. Following transplantation, the PSA levels were 6.44 ng/mL at 6 months post-transplant, increasing to 9.77 ng/mL at 1 year. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a 10 mm lesion in the left peripheral zone with restricted diffusion and decreased T2-weighted signal intensity, consistent with suspected prostate cancer. Pelvic lymphadenopathy was not observed, and no abnormalities were noted in the bladder wall or seminal vesicles. Subsequent transrectal prostate biopsy confirmed acinar adenocarcinoma (5/10 positive cores, Gleason score of 4 + 4 = 8, negative digital rectal examination, and prostate volume of 15 g). Systematic staging, including radionuclide bone scan (extent of disease score 0) and computed tomography, demonstrated no evidence of lymph node or distant metastases, thereby establishing a diagnosis of high-risk prostate cancer (cT2bN0M0).

Preoperative evaluation demonstrated stable renal function with serum creatinine level of 1.51 mg/dL and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of 39.1 mL/min/1.73 m2. On the basis of institutional experience, RARP using Hugo RAS system was planned. Given the surgical complexity in post-kidney transplant patients, comprehensive preoperative planning was undertaken, incorporating findings from published literature on post-kidney transplant RARP using the da Vinci system [7, 8] and performing multiple simulations to optimize the surgical approach (Fig. 1a and b).

During initial dissection of the Retzius space, extensive adhesions were observed. Dense adhesions were observed between the bladder apex and anterior bladder wall to the abdominal wall and the right lateral bladder wall to the pelvic wall. These adhesions resulted in superior displacement of the bladder toward the transplanted kidney. During dissection, an inadvertent cystotomy occurred near the bladder dome due to indistinct anatomical layers (Fig. 4a, b, and c).

After bladder injury repair, dissection was initiated from the left side of the Retzius space (Fig. 5a). The procedure then progressed systematically to the right side, achieving complete exposure of the anterior prostatic surface and pubic bone (Fig. 5b). A deep Douglas pouch was observed during the dissection, providing clear visualization of the posterior aspect of the prostate. Seminal vesicle dissection was performed, followed by lateral pedicle division, dorsal venous complex (DVC) control, and urethral transection. During vesicourethral anastomosis (Fig. 6a), the DVC was ligated and sutured to the puboprostatic ligaments to elevate the urethral stump and provide better alignment for the anastomosis. The Rocco sutures were subsequently placed to stabilize the urethral stump and reduce tension. However, extensive adhesions significantly limited bladder mobilization, resulting in tearing of the Denonvilliers’ fascia during approximation. Despite these challenges, a watertight anastomosis was ultimately achieved (Fig. 6b).

The procedure was completed in 336 minutes (console time, 235 minutes) with an estimated blood loss of 50 mL, successfully preserving the transplanted kidney and ureter. Urethral catheter was removed on postoperative day 4 without complications. The patient achieved immediate urinary continence and was pad-free at the time of discharge. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 6 with stable renal parameters, including a serum creatinine level of 1.2 mg/dL and an eGFR of 50.3 mL/min/1.73 m2. The final pathological examination revealed a pT2N0M0 adenocarcinoma with negative surgical margins (Gleason Score, 3 + 4 = 7). At 1 month of follow-up, the PSA level decreased to undetectable levels (< 0.01 ng/mL), and the patient maintained complete urinary continence following catheter removal while continuing his preoperative immunosuppression regimen.

Discussion

Prostate cancer is common among renal transplant recipients (RTRs), with an incidence rate of 0.72–3.1%. Long-term immunosuppressive therapy in these patients may lead to more aggressive tumor behavior and worse outcomes than those in the general population [9, 10].

Radical prostatectomy in RTRs presents with unique anatomical challenges. Dense adhesions in the prevesical space due to prior transplantation complicate surgical access. The transplanted kidney and previous ureteroneocystostomy further restrict entry into the Retzius space, making identification and preservation of the transplanted ureter technically demanding. These anatomical changes also impair vesicourethral anastomosis owing to reduced bladder mobility and limit the scope of pelvic lymph node dissection on the transplant side. Consequently, the retroperitoneal approach is often technically challenging, making the transperitoneal approach, which provides a wider operative field and improved visualization, generally preferable in this patient population [11, 12].

The anatomical differences in our post-kidney transplant patient were striking compared with the normal surgical anatomy (Fig. 4a). Extensive adhesions and right-sided bladder displacement significantly distorted the typical anatomical relationships, whereas the left side was relatively preserved. This anatomical asymmetry necessitated a modification of our surgical strategy. Dense adhesions obscured critical landmarks during the initial adhesiolysis, resulting in inadvertent bladder injury (Fig. 4b and c). To prevent such complications, we suggest using retrograde bladder filling for improved anatomical delineation, and when feasible, preoperative ureteral stent placement as a reference. By initiating dissection from the preserved anatomical planes on the left side and systematically progressing to the altered right side, effective prostate exposure was achieved despite severe adhesions (Fig. 5a and b). Additionally, a notably deep rectovesical pouch, likely related to prior peritoneal dialysis, provided enhanced visualization of the posterior prostate, facilitating seminal vesicle dissection. However, vesicourethral anastomosis after radical prostatectomy presents significant technical challenges, particularly in cases involving severe adhesions. Standard techniques, such as DVC fixation and Rocco’s suture, are intended to enhance urethral stump positioning and reduce anastomotic tension; however, their effectiveness may be limited under severe adhesion conditions. In our case, extensive adhesions significantly restricted bladder mobilization, resulting in increased tissue tension and inadvertent tearing of the Denonvilliers’ fascia. Although a watertight anastomosis was ultimately accomplished, this heightened tension could predispose the patient to postoperative stricture formation, highlighting the necessity for meticulous surgical techniques and vigilant postoperative monitoring. Despite these intraoperative challenges, the patient demonstrated excellent urinary continence immediately after catheter removal. One possible explanation is that the extensive perivesical adhesions may have provided increased anatomical support to the bladder neck and urethral anastomosis, thereby contributing to improved postoperative continence outcomes.

Although these anatomical challenges are inherent to post-transplant RARP, conventional robotic surgical systems with suspended robotic arms, such as the da Vinci Xi (Intuitive Surgical, Sunnyvale, CA, USA), present additional technical constraints. Specifically, the fixed four-arm design limits instrument maneuverability and increases collision risk in confined surgical fields [11]. Standard port placements frequently require modifications to prevent graft injury and optimize visualization [13]. Limited bladder mobility and extensive adhesions further complicate deep pelvic dissection, often necessitating additional assistant ports for effective exposure and vesicourethral anastomosis [14, 15]. These challenges underscore the critical importance of individualized preoperative planning and intraoperative flexibility in achieving optimal outcomes in RTRs.

In contrast, the independently mounted robotic arms of Hugo RAS system offer key technical advantages. Its modular design enables optimized port placement and flexible arm positioning, thereby improving surgical access and instrument maneuverability while reducing intraoperative arm collisions [1, 2]. This adaptability is particularly beneficial in patients with altered pelvic anatomy due to adhesions or prior surgery, where restricted exposure complicates dissection and reconstruction [1].

In our case, Hugo RAS system enabled dynamic port positioning to accommodate the transplanted kidney (Fig. 1a and b). To reduce graft contact risk, we repositioned the reserve arm to the patient’s left side and shifted the right-hand port caudally compared with standard RARP. Consequently, the entire port configuration was moved caudally. We initially inserted the camera through a slightly caudal camera port and determined optimal positions for remaining trocars on the basis of intraoperative visualization. This caudal adjustment created steeper robotic arm orientation, potentially increasing the range of motion during deep pelvic dissection and inter-arm interference risk. However, Hugo system’s flexible tilt adjustment effectively minimized these risks while maintaining adequate inter-port distances, despite the modified setup. This configuration optimized arm trajectories and instrument placement without compromising graft safety. Despite the limited pelvic space and dense adhesions, we encountered no difficulties in instrument maneuverability during adhesiolysis or vesicourethral anastomosis. Furthermore, no external arm collisions were observed, and the procedure was completed using the planned four robotic arms and a single assistant port, without the need for additional trocars, clearly demonstrating the practical advantages of Hugo system in anatomically challenging situations. Given these benefits, Hugo system holds promises for other pelvic surgeries in RTRs. Its flexibility could be particularly advantageous in posttransplant colorectal or gynecological cancers, where graft position and adhesions limit surgical access. This adaptability may help expand the minimally invasive options for this challenging and growing patient population.

The complication profile of RARP using Hugo system includes common events such as bleeding, bladder injury, urinary tract infection, urinary incontinence, and erectile dysfunction. Current data show no significant differences in intraoperative or postoperative complication rates compared with the da Vinci system, indicating a comparable safety profile [1, 2]. However, the long-term oncologic and functional outcomes of Hugo system remain insufficiently studied, highlighting the need for further data. In our case, no procedure-related complications or adverse events were observed during the 1-year postoperative follow-up period, although continued monitoring remains important.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates the technical feasibility and potential advantages of Hugo RAS system for RARP in RTRs. The independently mounted arms and modular design of Hugo system facilitated optimal port and arm positioning, significantly enhancing surgical maneuverability and safety, despite anatomical complexities related to the transplanted kidney and extensive adhesions. These findings suggest that Hugo system could offer valuable benefits in other complex pelvic surgeries for transplant patients, potentially expanding minimally invasive surgical options in this challenging patient population.
