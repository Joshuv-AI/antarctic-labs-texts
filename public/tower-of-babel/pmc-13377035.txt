Organic-inorganic hybrid scintillating glass for high-energy particle detection
Authors: Hongwei Li, Shichao Lv, Jianrong Qiu, Shifeng Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Glass scintillators are promising for large-scale radiation detection but traditionally suffer from low light yield and slow response. Here, we present highly transparent, homogeneous, and size-customizable organic-inorganic hybrid scintillating glasses, combining an inorganic glass host with organic components. The resulting hybrid glasses exhibit excellent radiation response, including rapid luminescence decay and enhanced scintillating efficiency. Notably, they achieve superior α-particle detection performance. In addition, their low density enables selective α-particle detection in mixed radiation fields and minimizes γ-ray interference, outperforming conventional glass scintillators. Furthermore, the glasses can be doped with elements such as lithium, boron, and gadolinium, offering efficient thermal neutron detection. These findings highlight the potential of organic-inorganic hybrid glasses as advanced scintillating materials for high-energy radiation field detection, with enhanced functionality for applications in nuclear medicine, oil exploration, and nuclear safety.

Introduction

High-energy particles play a very important role in modern scientific research. They include widely used particles such as α-particles, neutrons, and β-particles1–3. In nature, high-energy particles are mainly produced through radioactive decay, nuclear reactions, or particle accelerators4,5. These high-energy particles pose potential hazards to living organisms due to their ability to cause direct or indirect ionization6–9. These features also bring new scientific research and technological applications. For example, high-energy particles have also been extensively utilized for targeting specific cancers in radioactive therapy10–15. Meanwhile, high-energy particle imaging systems have also been applied in the fields of medical imaging and non-destructive evaluation of cultural relics16–19. Additionally, high-energy particles have been employed as characteristic markers for monitoring radioactive materials in the environment or exploring nuclear and atomic structures20,21. However, in practical applications, the presence of γ-ray interference is often inevitable22,23. Consequently, eliminating γ-ray interference during the detection of specific high-energy particles is essential for the safety and reliability of civilian and military nuclear facilities and equipment, encompassing radioactive waste management, nuclear emergency response, decommissioning of nuclear facilities, nuclear explosion monitoring, and nuclear safety8,24–26.

The rapid development of high-energy particle-related applications has given birth to many high-energy particle detection technologies. Up to present, various high-energy particle detectors such as gas detectors (e.g., ionization chambers and Geiger-Müller counters), semiconductor detectors (e.g., gold-silicon face-barrier and PIPS detectors) and scintillating detectors (e.g., CsI(Tl) and ZnS(Ag)) have been developed27–32. Among them, scintillating high-energy particle detectors, which involve the conversion of scintillating light signals from high-energy excitation into electrical signals via photomultiplier tubes (PMT) or silicon photomultipliers (SiPM), have attracted increasing attention in recent years33,34. They are featured by unique advantages including fast response, good sensitivity, high-light yield and better energy resolution, thus potentially overcoming most of the limitations of the other detector candidates. However, only a small fraction of scintillators are capable of accurately detecting high-energy particles, while they have generally been baffled by the low detection specificity and easy interference from γ-rays, which have become matters of substantial misdiscrimination concern in the mixed radiation field scene.

In this study, we propose and demonstrate highly transparent, customizable large-sized organic-inorganic hybrid scintillating glasses for high-energy particle detection with high specificity. The material exhibits highly uniform structural characteristics, which is achieved by rationally anchoring fluorescent molecules within the homogeneous amorphous structure, thereby forming a hybrid system. In addition, it features a low density. In terms of luminescent properties, the material exhibits high quantum efficiency and rapid decay. Regarding radiation detection capability, it demonstrates efficient and fast response to α-particles, and also possesses strong anti-interference capability against γ-rays. The prototype detector device is designed and built, achieving practical application in α-particle monitoring. It demonstrates the capability for selective detection of specific high-energy radiation particles. Furthermore, the hybrid system possesses highly designable characteristics, allowing for the incorporation of other isotopes (such as ⁶Li, ¹⁰B, and ¹⁵⁷Gd) into the hybrid matrix, as well as customization of the detector’s shape and size. The findings not only offer a new organic-inorganic hybrid scintillating glass system, but also point to promising applications for high-energy particle detection.

Results and Discussion

α-particles are the most representative high-energy particles in practical applications and scintillators play a crucial role in their identification. According to the high-energy particle-matter interaction theory, the excitation of α-particles on the scintillator material leads to the scintillation process, as illustrated schematically in Fig. 1a. When high-energy α-particles enter a scintillator, they interact strongly with the nuclei and electrons through Coulomb interactions, leading to energy deposition35. The α-particles’ positive charge (+2e) generates an electric field that causes ionization and excitation in the material. Ionization creates free electrons and positive ions, exciting atoms or molecules to higher-energy states. These processes generate optical signals as the energy is transferred to luminescent centers, which emit photons in the ultraviolet or visible spectrum36. Thus, the material with high α-particle blocking and transformation capability is the essential prerequisite. However, it is necessary to note that the α-particles are always accompanied by γ-rays. The latter can also effectively excite the scintillator and bring strong background noise, thus seriously degrading detection specificity. In principle, the candidates with excellent α-particle detection performance should possess the following critical characteristics: high-light yield to enhance sensitivity, rapid decay process for signal resolution, excellent radiation resistance for stability, low material density and low effective atomic number (Zeff) for minimizing γ-ray interference, as well as reasonable cost for practical applications. We compared the relevant performance parameters of various representative scintillators reported to date, as summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Based on these performance comparisons, we evaluated the relative strengths and weaknesses of each type of scintillator and summarized in the performance radar maps (Fig. 1b). Inorganic crystal scintillators like NaI and ZnS(Ag) are featured by the high-light yield but are limited by high density and slow decay dynamics37. Additionally, ZnS(Ag) tends to be opaque, which hinders the selective detection of α-particles. Glass scintillators, while easier to fabricate in large sizes with good transparency, have relatively low light yields38. Organic scintillators exhibit ultra-fast decay processes and higher transparency, but face challenges in radiation resistance39. The above comparison hints at the innovative materials that combine the advantages of both organic and inorganic materials might be the design direction. Keeping above ideas in mind, we are focusing our attention on exploring the organic-inorganic hybrid scintillating glass. For this purpose, we employed the sol-gel method, based on the acid-catalyzed hydrolysis and condensation of alkoxysilanes, because it provides an effective route toward low-density glass materials and circumvents the challenges associated with the differing melting points of organic and inorganic precursors. The synthesis route is summarized in Fig. 2a, and the full experimental details are given in the Experimental Section. The process involves the preparation of clear solution A, which contains organic luminescent substance dissolved in Tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS), and solution B, which contains ethanol, N,N-Dimethylformamide (DMF), and acid catalyst. Notably, we dissolved the organic luminescent molecules (e.g., anthracene (An), phenanthrene (Ph), 9,10-Diphenylanthracene (DPHA), 1,4-Bis(5-phenyl-2-oxazolyl)benzene (POPOP) and 2,5-Diphenyloxazole (PPO) in TEOS for obtaining homogenous solutions. They are highly transparent and exhibit uniform luminescence under 365 nm excitation (Supplementary Fig. 1). They were then thoroughly mixed to form a gel. Subsequently, the mixed solution was slowly dried under acidic conditions and in the presence of a drying control chemical additive (DCCA), ultimately yielding a monolithic organic-inorganic hybrid glass. The structure of this organic-inorganic hybrid glass is schematically presented in the inset of Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 2. In contrast to the conventional glass fabricated by melting-quenching method, the organic-inorganic hybrid glass is featured by co-embedded with organic luminescent molecules and inorganic silica network. Refractive index measurements indicate that the prepared organic-inorganic hybrid glass exhibits a refractive index of approximately 1.42 (Supplementary Fig. 3), which is close to, but slightly higher than, that of the blank sample without organic constituents (1.40). In addition, various other inorganic species such as Li, B, and Gd can also be easily incorporated by adding the corresponding precursors into solution B before mixing, as specified in Supplementary Table 2.Fig. 2Design and fabrication of the organic-inorganic hybrid glass samples.a Schematic of the fabrication process. b Photographs of various types of samples under natural light and 365 nm.

The strategy is characterized by high generality. We tested it by using various combinations of organic substances and inorganic matrices to synthesize a wide range of organic-inorganic hybrid material category, including pure silica-based (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Fig. 4), borosilicate-based (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Fig. 5), lithium silicate-based (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Fig. 6), gadolinium silicate-based (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Fig. 7), and lithium borosilicate-based (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Fig. 8) along with hybrid system containing other organic luminescent materials such as perylene, pyrene, fluorene, and naphthalene (Supplementary Fig. 9). All of the samples were prepared in a cylindrical form with a diameter of 2 cm and a height of approximately 1 cm (Fig. 2b), maintaining a large size and exhibiting high transparency without the precipitation of organic crystals. In addition, they can be easily shaped into various distinct forms, such as thin discs (Supplementary Fig. 10), films and fibers. Meanwhile, larger organic-inorganic hybrid glass samples can be fabricated by increasing the precursor volume and appropriately adjusting the container geometry and dimensions. For example, organic-inorganic hybrid glass samples in square shape with dimensions of 4 × 4 cm (Supplementary Fig. 11) were successfully prepared. Furthermore, they can keep the high activity of the precursor of organic luminescent molecules. These organic-inorganic hybrid glasses show intense luminescence under excitation with 365 nm, similar to that of the saturated TEOS solution (Supplementary Fig. 1).

The microstructures of the organic-inorganic hybrid glass samples were investigated by the multi-scale structural characterization techniques. Figure 3a presents a macroscopic photograph of the organic-inorganic hybrid glass sample under natural light, which clearly demonstrates the excellent transparency of the glass, as well as the absence of bubbles and visible particles. This indicates the homogeneity at the centimeter scale. Next, we performed optical microscopy on both the surfaces and freshly fractured cross-sections of the samples. The results show that both the surfaces (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 12) and cross-sections (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 13) of samples are uniform in millimeter-scale, and no obvious phase separation, particle aggregation, bubbles, or distinct layers can be observed. This confirms the homogeneity of the organic-inorganic hybrid glass at the millimeter scale. To further exclude possible differences between the surface and the bulk at higher resolution, we performed scanning electron microscopy (SEM) on representative sample surfaces and freshly fractured cross-sections. The SEM images for both the surfaces (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 14) and cross-sections (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 15) reveal consistent uniformity at the micron scale, with no evidence of phase separation, aggregation, bubbles, or stratification. Energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) mapping shows a homogeneous distribution of key elements (Supplementary Fig. 16). The combined SEM and EDS mapping results clearly demonstrate micron-scale uniformity of the organic-inorganic hybrid glass samples. Subsequently, we employed transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and selected area electron diffraction to evaluate the samples at the nanoscale. TEM imaging reveals no evidence of nanocrystal formation, phase separation, or interfacial inhomogeneity. The corresponding electron diffraction patterns verify the amorphous nature of the material, with no indication of local crystalline regions (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Fig. 17). Figure 3g and Supplementary Fig. 18 show the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns. Notably, in comparison with the used organic crystal precursors (Supplementary Fig. 19), all of the samples exhibit broad diffraction patterns in XRD, indicating their amorphous nature. This is likely attributed to the low polarity of the overall system during the synthesis, which allows the organic components to remain dissolved in the liquid phase and exist in an amorphous state within the glass matrix. Figure 3h and Supplementary Fig. 20 present the two-dimensional small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) patterns, which provide statistical evidence for the uniformity within the samples and the absence of dispersed phases or clusters. Figure 3i and Supplementary Fig. 22 present the Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra of the samples. Several characteristic peaks can be identified: the peak at around 1700 cm−1 corresponds to residual DMF in the system40; the peaks between 1400–1500 cm−1 are attributed to C=C bond vibrations in the organic aromatic rings; the peaks at around 600–950 cm−1 are associated with in-plane bending vibrations of the C–H bonds of the aromatic rings41,42; the band at around 1100 cm−1 observed at all samples is from Si–O–Si unit43; the weak features near 1200 cm−1 correspond to the characteristic peak of B–O44. These characteristic bands are well consistent with the fingerprint peaks of the organic luminescent precursor (Supplementary Fig. 23). Additionally, Raman analysis display characteristic vibrations of the C=C bond in the aromatic rings between 1400–1600 cm−1, the characteristic stretching peak of the C–H bond around 700–1250 cm−1, the C–C bond in the aromatic rings between 400–800 cm−1 range and Si–O–Si stretching at 420 and 800 cm−1 (Fig. 3j, Supplementary Fig. 24)45,46. The characteristic peaks of all organic components correspond to the organic compounds that were doped into the material (Supplementary Fig. 25). ICP analysis reveals the presence of B (1 wt%) with An-borosilicate glass, Li (3.5 wt%) with Ph lithium silicate glass, and Gd (17 wt%) elements with PP-gadolinium silicate glass. Above results collaboratively verify the successful incorporation of luminescent organic molecule and inorganic species in the hybrid system.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was used to determine the proportion of organic and inorganic components in the hybrid materials. All samples were maintained at 150 °C in the sample chamber for 1 hour and then allowed to naturally cool to room temperature before testing. The test results are shown in Fig. 3k and Supplementary Fig. 26. All samples exhibit weight loss before 200 °C, stabilizing around 500 °C. The weight loss before 500 °C can be attributed to the decomposition of organic matter, as shown in the weight loss statistics graph in Supplementary Fig. 27. According to TGA, the proportion of residual water, solvents and organic matter can be estimated to range from 15% to 35%. The differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) results indicate that the incorporation of organic components leads to a slight decrease in the glass transition temperature (Tg) of the organic-inorganic hybrid glass. Meanwhile, no transition from amorphous to crystalline phase was observed below 1300 °C (Supplementary Fig. 28). Primarily due to the unique open structure, the organic-inorganic hybrid system is featured by their rather low density. As shown in Fig. 3l, the densities of all systems are approximately 1.1 g/cm³, and even with the addition of the heavier Gd element, the density only increases to around 1.3 g/cm³.

The luminescent properties of the organic-inorganic hybrid glass samples were studied. The photoluminescence spectra of glass and the organic crystal precursor under excitation with 365 nm are shown in Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 30. They present similar luminescent properties: The samples containing anthracene display a broad peak at 410 nm and three narrow peaks at 380 nm, 400 nm, and 420 nm, consistent with the luminescence of anthracene crystals (Supplementary Fig. 31a); The samples containing phenanthrene exhibit a broad peak at 410 nm and three narrow peaks at 380, 405 and 430 nm, aligning with the luminescence of phenanthrene crystals (Supplementary Fig. 31b); The samples containing DP and PP exhibit a broad peak at 420 and 425 nm, showing a ~ 30 nm blue shift compared to their mixed crystal precursors. This shift occurs because, in the mixed crystals, DPHA and POPOP dominate the emission through tight interactions and energy transfer with PPO. The secondary peak at 380 nm results from PPO’s spontaneous emission (Supplementary Fig. 31c to 31 h). The fluorescence decay processes under 365 nm excitation are shown in Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 32. All organic-inorganic hybrid glass samples display short decay dynamics, similar to those found in the utilized organic crystal scintillators (Supplementary Fig. 33). The decay lifetime of sample An-lithium silicate glass, Ph-lithium silicate glass, DP-lithium silicate glass and PP-lithium silicate glass were estimated to be 5, 6, 7 and 2 ns, respectively (Fig. 4e). Such fast decay lifetimes allow the emission signals to be effectively distinguished from the instrument response function (IRF) (Supplementary Fig. 34). Notably, the decay process is one order of magnitude faster compared with the mainstream commercial GS20 glass (less than 62.09 ns) (Supplementary Fig. 35), indicating the great potential for scintillating application. The quantum efficiency was measured and summarized in Fig. 4f. The hybrid glasses containing phenanthrene exhibit the lowest quantum efficiency, around 15%, followed by the hybrid glasses containing anthracene at about 20%. This is slightly lower than that of GS20 glass (51%). Notably, the hybrid glasses containing DP and PP demonstrated high quantum efficiency, reaching approximately 70% and 80%, respectively. This is because the molecular structures of DPHA and POPOP include phenyl and oxazole groups, which enhance π-electron conjugation and energy transfer efficiency, typically resulting in higher quantum efficiency compared to anthracene and phenanthrene47,48.

The high transparency, short fluorescence decay lifetime, and high quantum efficiency of the organic-inorganic hybrid glass materials provide a unique opportunity for the efficient detection of high-energy particles, particularly α-particles. When an α-particle enters the hybrid material containing organic components, its high kinetic energy and positive charge interact with the atoms and molecules in the material, causing ionization and excitation. In the fabricated hybrid materials, the inorganic matrix serves as a physical support and transport medium, and the embedded organic components are responsible for luminescence. Specifically, the kinetic α-particle excites the organic molecules, causing them to transition from the ground state (S0) to the excited state (S1) or higher-energy levels. The secondary electrons generated from these primary events propagate through the material, inducing further ionization and excitation, leading to the cascade effect. Subsequently, the organic molecules in the excited state S1 return to the ground state S0 through radiative transitions, emitting photons typically in the ultraviolet or visible range, as illustrated in Fig. 5a49. We built a radiation detection system for characterize the α-particles detection performance, as illustrated in Fig. 5b. The system consists of several key components: detection material, photomultiplier tube (PMT), high-voltage power supply, multichannel analyzer, and computer. When ionizing radiation interacts with the detection material, it deposits energy and emits low-energy photons. These low-energy photon signals are subsequently detected and amplified by the PMT. Under excitation with 241Am α-particle source, the organic-inorganic hybrid glass scintillator exhibits bright blue emission, as the pictures shown in inset of Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 36. Figure 5c shows the pulse energy spectra of the typical lithium silicate-based glass system under α-particle excitation. The distinct bands can be observed with the channel number centered at 1671 for anthracene-lithium silicate glass, 1980 for phenanthrene-lithium silicate glass, 2190 for DP-lithium silicate glass, and 1780 for PP-lithium silicate glass. The distribution of the channel numbers is summarized in Fig. 5d. Compared to the widely used commercial inorganic glass scintillator GS20, which has a channel number of 815 under the same test conditions, all materials show larger channel numbers, indicating higher light yields than GS20 glass. The light yield of the organic-inorganic hybrid glass samples under α-particle excitation was estimated based on the reported value for GS20 glass (6600 photons/5.5 MeV)50. The calculated light yields are summarized in Supplementary Table 3, with values of approximately 13,500 photons/5.5 MeV for An-lithium silicate, 16,000 photons/5.5 MeV for Ph-lithium silicate, 23,300 photons/5.5 MeV for DP-lithium silicate, and 19,800 photons/5.5 MeV for PP-lithium silicate, respectively. The results demonstrate that the synthesized organic-inorganic hybrid glass samples possess a better α-particle detection performance with the largest light yield exceeding twice that of commercial GS20 glass. We performed pulse height spectroscopy at several different positions on the large-sized glass samples using α-particle excitation. The results are shown in Supplementary Fig. 37. The results show that the pulse height spectra are highly consistent across all of the tested locations, confirming that even large-scale organic-inorganic hybrid glass samples maintain good uniformity under high-energy radiation. We evaluated the α-particle radiation stability of the organic-inorganic hybrid glass, as shown in Supplementary Figs. 38 and 39. After 72 hours of continuous irradiation with a 241Am radioactive source, the pulse height spectrum exhibits no significant change compared to the initial state. The results demonstrate that the organic-inorganic hybrid glass exhibits excellent stability under these irradiation conditions. Supplementary Fig. 40 shows the pulse height spectra of the organic-inorganic hybrid glass under α-particle irradiation at different temperatures. The results indicate that the organic-inorganic hybrid glass exhibits relatively stable α-particle detection capability at temperatures below 130 °C. By accumulating and averaging 64 valid signals, the pulse signal spectra of the hybrid glass under α-particle excitation were obtained, as shown in Fig. 5e. The decay curve of the pulse signal can be fitted with a single exponential function (Supplementary Fig. 41), and the average decay time of the pulse signal is 18.87 ns for An-lithium silicate glass, 23.58 ns for Pn-lithium silicate glass, 12.45 ns for DP-lithium silicate glass and 10.51 ns for PP-lithium silicate, respectively. Such fast decay lifetimes enable clear discrimination from the IRF (Supplementary Fig. 42). The short decay lifetime is favorable for obtaining a high counting rate, which is particularly advantageous in low-throughput experimental settings. This special feature gifts the detection system with time-resolution function. To study it, the detection system was employed to monitor the α-particle from an active 241Am radiation source. As shown in Fig. 5f, a series of individual pulse signals can be clearly observed within 400 μs, demonstrating the great potential for monitoring the dynamic radiative process.

To further elucidate the radiation response characteristics of the organic-inorganic hybrid glass, particularly in comparison with conventional scintillators such as GS20 under various types of radiation, we systematically investigated its performance under γ-ray and X-ray excitation. For X-ray, X-ray-induced emission spectra were collected from organic-inorganic hybrid glass samples with a thickness of approximately 1 mm. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 43, the organic-inorganic hybrid glass exhibits weak but still distinguishable characteristic emission peaks under X-ray excitation, consistent with those observed in the photoluminescence spectra. By contrast, under identical conditions, the emission peak intensity of GS20 glass is several orders of magnitude higher, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 44. In addition, the corresponding signal-to-noise ratio of organic-inorganic hybrid glass is smaller than that of GS20 sample (Supplementary Fig. 45). These results demonstrate that the scintillation efficiency of the organic-inorganic hybrid glass is much lower than that of GS20. We further measured the scintillation response of the organic-inorganic hybrid glass samples under γ-ray excitation. Under irradiation with a 137Cs γ-ray source, no distinct luminescence of the organic-inorganic hybrid glass samples could be observed (Supplementary Fig. 46). We measured the pulse height spectra of the organic-inorganic hybrid glass samples under γ-ray excitation, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 47 and Supplementary Fig. 48. The pulse height spectrum of the organic-inorganic hybrid glass under excitation with a 137Cs source is almost indistinguishable from the background noise, further demonstrating that the organic-inorganic hybrid glass is insensitive to γ-rays. This can be attributed to the high-energy and strong penetration of γ-rays51. This is supposed to benefit from the low-density feature of the organic-inorganic hybrid glass samples (Supplementary Fig. 49). In summary, the organic-inorganic hybrid glass exhibits good detection capability for α-particles, but negligible response to higher-energy γ-rays. This unique selectivity suggests that, in practical nuclear-related scenarios where γ-rays and α-particles often coexist, the organic-inorganic hybrid glass holds promise for effective and selective α-particle identification.

Importantly, the organic-inorganic hybrid glass materials not only exhibit excellent capability for efficient α-particle detection but also possess a lower density than inorganic glass, enabling effective detection of particles in the mixed radiation field environments. For example, in the practical nuclear-related scenarios, γ-rays and α-particles often coexist. The low-density feature of the material can selectively identify α-particles while being relatively insensitive to γ-rays, thus providing a unique opportunity for their discrimination. To demonstrate it, we constructed a radiation detection system, which is shown in Fig. 6a. A mixed radiation field was generated by simultaneously irradiating the sample with multiple radioactive sources (¹³⁷Cs as the γ-ray source and ²⁴¹Am as the α-particle source). The resulting pulse height spectra, acquired using a PMT, were then analyzed to evaluate the material’s selective response to different types of radiation. Firstly, the radiation response properties of commercial GS20 sample under the mixed radiation fields were studied. As shown in Fig. 6b, two distinct bands centered at channels 815 and 137 can be observed. By analyzing the pulse height spectra of GS20 glass obtained under α-particle and γ-ray excitation, respectively, it can be further confirmed that the characteristic band observed in the mixed radiation field arises from the simultaneous responses to both α-particle and γ-ray excitation (Supplementary Fig. 50). The results indicate that GS20 sample does not have the ability for selective alpha particle recognition. We further studied the performance of the hybrid sample. The mixed-field pulse energy spectrum of an organic-inorganic hybrid material is displayed in Fig. 6c, with phenanthrene-lithium silicate glass used as an example. An intense characteristic band can be observed, with the channel number centered at 2010 for phenanthrene-lithium silicate glass in a mixed radiation field environment. By analyzing the pulse height spectra of the organic-inorganic hybrid glass obtained under α-particle and γ-ray excitation respectively, it can be further confirmed that the signal detected under mixed field conditions originated from the α-particle excitation (Supplementary Fig. 51). More encouragingly, the hybrid materials, which allow the incorporation of various neutron capturing elements such as Li, B, and Gd, provide new opportunities for thermal and fast neutron detection. As a validation experiment for practical applications, we conducted thermal neutron tests based on the Time-of-Flight (TOF) method. The TOF method is an analytical technique for determining neutron velocity and energy distribution by measuring the flight time, as illustrated in Fig. 6d52,53. High-energy protons were accelerated to bombard a solid tungsten target, producing pulsed neutron beams with various energies54. The energy range of pulsed neutron beams is 10−8 ~ 102 MeV. These neutrons travel along a path with the length of 11 m. Because their flight velocity is different, by recording the arrival time of neutrons, the energy information of neutrons can be calculated by using the de Broglie wavelength formula. The wavelength of characteristic neutron selected by monochromator is 1.6 Å, 2.8 Å, and 4.7 Å. According to the de Broglie wavelength formula, the recorded arrival time of these neutron are 5.7, 10.3 and 17.2 ms, as shown in Fig. 6e and Supplementary Fig. 52, well consistent with the theoretical predictions. These results demonstrate that the organic-inorganic hybrid glass materials can effectively detect thermal neutrons. In addition, the hydrogen atoms in organic-inorganic hybrid glass can also gift it with fast neutron detection function. As illustrated in Fig. 6f, when fast neutrons are incident on scintillators, the fast neutrons transfer their energy to the hydrogen atoms via elastic scattering and generate high-energy recoil protons. These recoil protons can excite luminescent center and produce visible photons55. As a proof-of-concept, the organic-inorganic hybrid glass was employed for monitoring the fast neutrons in 252Cf radioactive source, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 53 and Supplementary Fig. 54. The obtained pulse height spectrum of fast neutrons is shown in Fig. 6g. The band centered at channel 27 originates from the fast neutrons with an energy range of 0.5 ~ 6 MeV. The above results indicate that this organic-inorganic hybrid glass can achieve neutron detection with a wide energy range, such as thermal neutron and fast neutron.

In this work, we have proposed a series of transparent organic-inorganic hybrid scintillating glass with good optical quality, high transparency, and excellent scintillating performance. They are featured by high luminescence brightness, fast decay, and high quantum efficiency. Encouragingly, these materials exhibit significant potential for radiation detection of high-energy particles and neutrons. For high-energy particle detection. Their scintillation yield is over twice that of GS20 glass, and the decay time can be as short as 2–7 ns. The low-density feature can also provide a high signal-to-noise ratio in a mixed radiation field, with the signals of α-particle be easily separated from γ background by using adjusting the threshold. For neutron detection, the dopants of Li, B, and Gd elements in organic-inorganic hybrid glass can provide thermal neutron function, and the presence of hydrogen atoms of organic parts can also gift fast neutron detection ability. The progress has demonstrated that the organic-inorganic hybrid glass shows great promise for various practical applications such as environmental radiation monitoring, nuclear reactor monitoring, and security inspection systems.

Methods

Chemicals

Tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS, GC, 99%), anthracene (An, GC, 99%), phenanthrene (Ph, 97%), 2,5-diphenyloxazole (PPO, 99%), 9,10-diphenylanthracene (DPHA, 98%), 1,4-bis(5-phenyl-2-oxazolyl) benzene (POPOP, 98%), trimethyl borate (TMB, 99.99%), lithium nitrate (LiNO3, 99.99%), gadolinium nitrate hexahydrate (Gd(NO3)2·6H2O, 99.99%), ethanol (EtOH, GC, 99.9%), N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, GC, 99.9%) were purchased from Aladdin Reagent Company. Hydrochloric acid (HCl, 37%) were purchased from Aladdin Reagent Company. All chemicals were used without any further purification. Distilled water (approximately 17 M Ω cm−1) from a Milli-Q water system was used throughout the experiment.

Synthesis

All types of organic-inorganic hybrid glass samples were synthesized via a sol-gel method. Briefly, firstly, TEOS was used as the solvent, with organic scintillators (An, Ph, PPO, DPHA, POPOP) serving as the solutes. At room temperature, an excess of the organic scintillators was added to TEOS, and the mixture was sonicated for 10 minutes to ensure thorough mixing, resulting in a mixed TEOS solution. This solution was then left to stand overnight, and the supernatant was collected to obtain a saturated organic (An, Ph, PPO, DPHA, POPOP)-TEOS solution, which was used as the silicon source. Subsequently, 4 ml of EtOH and 4 ml of DMF were added to a glass vial containing a total of 4.17 g of the saturated organic-TEOS solution, thoroughly mixed to serve as the precursor solution. Next, 2 ml of 0.2 M dilute HCl solution was slowly added to the precursor solution. The mixture was stirred overnight to form a sol. Finally, the sol was placed in an oven set at 50 °C and slowly dried over a period of 7 to 21 days to obtain the final organic-inorganic hybrid glass. The saturated organic-TEOS solution in the precursor mixture could be composed of a single organic-TEOS saturated solution or a combination of multiple organic-TEOS saturated solutions in any proportion. For the preparation of organic-inorganic hybrid glass containing B, Li, and Gd elements, 1.04 g of TMB, 0.69 g of LiNO3, and 4.51 g of Gd(NO3)2·6H2O were added as sources of B, Li, and Gd, to the precursor solution, respectively.

Material characterization

The microscopy images were captured by a microscope (Digital Sight 10, Nikon, Japan). The sample images were captured by a digital camera (700D, Canon, Japan). Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis (2θ = 5° to 90°) was collected with a PANalytical (Almelo, Netherlands) diffractometer with the tube voltage and current of 40 kV and 40 mA. Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra were recorded on Perkin-Elmer Spectrum GX (PerkinElmer, Inc., USA) spectrophotometer with a spectral resolution of 1 cm−1 in the wavenumber region of 500–4000 cm−1. The Raman spectra of the samples in the range of 200–1800 cm−1 were acquired using an inVia laser confocal Raman spectrometer (Renishaw, U.K.). The excitation source was a 785 nm laser, and the spectral resolution was set to 1 cm−1. The thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) characterizations were conducted using a TGA2 (METTLER, Switzerland) instrument under an Ar atmosphere. The samples were placed in a platinum crucible situated on a sample holder of the TGA at room temperature. The samples were first heated at a rate of 10 °C min−1 to 150 °C, held at this temperature for 60 min, and then naturally cooled to room temperature. Subsequently, they were heated again at a rate of 10 °C min−1 to the target temperature. The differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) characterizations were conducted using a STA449C (NETZSCH, Germany) instrument under an Ar atmosphere. The microstructure of the sample was studied using the JEM-2100F high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM, JEOL, Japan), operated at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. The microstructures of the samples were examined using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, ZEISS EVO, Germany) equipped with an energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometer (EDS, Oxford AZtecEDS, U.K.). The small-angle X-ray scattering measurements were performed using a Xeuss 2.0 SAXS (SAXS, Xenocs, France) instrument. Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) measurements were performed using an ICAP-7400 spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). The transmittance and absorption spectra of the samples were recorded using a Lambda 950 spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer, USA) in the ultraviolet (UV)-visible spectral range from 250 to 800 nm. The photoluminescence spectra and decay curve of the samples were recorded by a spectrofluorometer (FLS920, Edinburgh Instrument Ltd., U.K.) equipped with a xenon arc lamp as excitation source. The fluorescence quantum yield was measured using a C9920 PLQY measurement system (PLQY, Hamamatsu, Japan) equipped with an integrating sphere (φ = 150 mm). The emission spectra of the sample under X-ray excitation were measured by using an X-ray tube (Mini-X-OEM, AMPTEK, USA) as the X-ray source and a fiber optic spectrometer (Maya 2000 Pro, Ocean Optics, USA) as the detector. The α-particle detector mainly includes a photomultiplier tube (H1949, Hamamatsu, Japan) with a sample heating device, a multi-channel analyzer (TOFBOX-4, TOFTEK, China), and a high-voltage power supply. The α-particle pulse height spectrum of the sample was measured with 241Am as the α source, and the high voltage was set to 1700 V. The pulse height spectra at different temperatures were obtained by adjusting the temperature of the heating device. The γ-ray pulse height spectrum of the sample was measured with 137Cs or 60Co as the γ source. The neutron detection performance of the sample was tested on the CSNS. The fast neutron pulse height spectrum of the sample was measured with 252Cf as the fast neutron source.
