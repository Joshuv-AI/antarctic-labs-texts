Tubuloside B ameliorates blood-brain barrier disruption and cognitive deficits after cerebral ischemia by inhibiting TRIC protein ubiquitination and degradation
Authors: Mingli He, Han Zhang, Ru Liu, Chunyang Zhang, Juan Li, Luming Li, Liang Li, Hao Yang, Qiu Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and purposeVascular cognitive impairment (VCI) is strongly linked to blood-brain barrier (BBB) dysfunction. This study investigated whether Tubuloside B (Tub-B), a primary bioactive compound of Cistanche tubulosa, ameliorates cognitive deficits by protecting BBB integrity through inhibition of tricellulin (TRIC) ubiquitination and degradation.MethodsThe main plasma metabolites of total glycosides of Cistanche (TCGs) were identified by UPLC-QQQ-MS. A bilateral common carotid artery occlusion (BCCAO) rat model was used to induce chronic cerebral hypoperfusion (CCH). Cognitive function was assessed using the Morris water maze, and BBB permeability was evaluated by Evans blue extravasation. The concentrations of inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), IL-10, and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), in the rat hippocampal tissues were quantified using ELISA kits. TRIC expression and ubiquitination were analyzed by Western blot, co-immunoprecipitation, and immunohistochemistry. An in vitro oxygen-glucose deprivation (OGD) model in brain microvascular endothelial cells was used to validate the effects on barrier function.ResultsTub-B was identified as the major circulating metabolite, accounting for 52.3% ± 2.1% of plasma components. In CCH rats, Tub-B (20 mg/kg) significantly improved spatial memory, increasing platform crossings (3.00 ± 0.58 vs. CCH, P < 0.05) and reducing Evans blue leakage (0.038 ± 0.007 µg/g tissue vs. 0.15 ± 0.02 µg/g in CCH, P < 0.001). Furthermore, Tub-B treatment dose-dependently suppressed pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β) and concomitantly enhanced anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-10, TGF-β) in the hippocampus. It also attenuated the downregulation of TRIC and suppressed its ubiquitination. In OGD-injured endothelial cells, Tub-B enhanced barrier integrity and inhibited TRIC ubiquitination.ConclusionTub-B attenuates CCH-induced BBB disruption and cognitive impairment by inhibiting TRIC ubiquitination and degradation, likely via interference with VEGF signaling. These results highlight its potential as a therapeutic agent for VCI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-025-03671-5.

Introduction

Vascular cognitive impairment (VCI) represents the second most common cause of cognitive dysfunction after Alzheimer’s disease, posing a growing clinical and societal challenge amid aging populations worldwide [1–5]. Its pathogenesis involves multifactorial mechanisms, including chronic cerebral hypoperfusion (CCH), neuroinflammation, neuronal damage, and blood-brain barrier (BBB) disruption [6–11]. In particular, BBB dysfunction is increasingly recognized as a pivotal early event triggering neuroinflammatory cascades and cognitive decline [7–9, 11].

The BBB is primarily composed of brain microvascular endothelial cells interconnected via tight junctions (TJs), which include proteins such as claudins, occludin, and tricellulin (TRIC) [12–16]. TRIC is specifically localized at tricellular contacts and is critical for sealing paracellular pathways against macromolecule leakage, thus maintaining CNS homeostasis [17–19]. Under CCH conditions, TJ integrity is compromised, resulting in increased BBB permeability, infiltration of inflammatory mediators, and subsequent neuronal injury [20–23]. Although ubiquitin-mediated degradation has been implicated in the regulation of TJ proteins such as occludin [24–29], the post-translational modulation of TRIC—particularly via ubiquitination—under ischemic conditions remains poorly understood [30, 31].

Cistanche tubulosa and its extract, total glycosides of Cistanche (TCGs), have demonstrated neuroprotective and cognitive-enhancing properties [32–34]. However, the specific active compounds responsible for these benefits and their mechanisms of action, especially concerning BBB protection, are not fully elucidated. Tubuloside B (Tub-B) is a key constituent of TCGs, yet its potential role in modulating TRIC stability and BBB function has not been investigated.

In this study, we combined metabolic profiling, network pharmacology, and experimental validation to examine whether Tub-B attenuates CCH-induced BBB disruption and cognitive impairment by inhibiting ubiquitin-dependent degradation of TRIC. Using both in vivo and in vitro models of cerebral hypoperfusion, we demonstrate that Tub-B stabilizes TRIC expression, reduces ubiquitination, preserves BBB integrity, and improves cognitive performance. Our findings reveal a novel mechanism by which Tub-B targets the VEGF–ubiquitin–TRIC axis, offering a promising therapeutic strategy for VCI.

Methods

Experimental animals and grouping

Specific pathogen-free male Sprague-Dawley rats (8–10 weeks, 250–300 g; Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd.) were housed under standard conditions. All experimental protocols were approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Xuzhou Medical University (XYDW-2023-001). Rats were randomly divided into four groups (n = 10/group) using a computer-generated randomization sequence: Sham (sham surgery + saline), CCH (BCCAO + saline), CCH + Tub-B-Low (BCCAO + 5 mg/kg Tub-B), and CCH + Tub-B-High (BCCAO + 20 mg/kg Tub-B). The doses of Tub-B (5 mg/kg and 20 mg/kg) were selected based on prior pharmacokinetic studies and preliminary dose-response experiments showing significant neuroprotective effects without toxicity. Tub-B and TCGs (purity > 98% and > 50%, respectively; Xi’an Leiberei Biotechnology) were administered by gavage starting 24 h after surgery for 8 weeks.

Plasma metabolite profiling

Plasma samples were collected from C57BL/6 mice (n = 6) orally administered TCGs and analyzed using UPLC-QQQ-MS (Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column; 2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm) with gradient elution (0.1% formic acid in water and acetonitrile). Detection was performed in negative ion ESI mode. Dexamethasone was used as an internal standard.

Cell culture and drug treatment

PC12 cells were maintained in DMEM with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Aβ₂₅–₃₅ oligomers were prepared by incubation at 37 °C for 7 days. Cells were pretreated with Tub-B (1, 5, 10 µM) for 2 h before Aβ₂₅–₃₅ (20 µM, 24 h) exposure. Cell viability was measured using CCK-8 assay.

Network pharmacology

Potential targets of Tub-B were predicted using SwissTargetPrediction and PharmMapper. Disease-related targets for “vascular cognitive impairment” and “blood-brain barrier dysfunction” were retrieved from GeneCards and OMIM. Protein-protein interaction networks were constructed with STRING and visualized in Cytoscape. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed using DAVID.

BCCAO model and drug administration

The CCH model was induced via permanent bilateral common carotid artery occlusion under isoflurane anesthesia. Sham-operated rats underwent identical procedures without ligation. Drug treatments began 24 h post-surgery and continued for 8 weeks.

Morris water maze

Spatial learning and memory were assessed over 5 training days with a hidden platform. A probe test was conducted on day 6. Escape latency, path length, and time in the target quadrant were recorded. Behavioral tests and data analysis were performed by investigators blinded to the group assignments.

Blood-brain barrier permeability

BBB integrity was evaluated using Evans blue (EB; 2%, 4 mL/kg) extravasation. EB was quantified fluorometrically (620/680 nm) in formamide-extracted brain tissues.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

Following the completion of the Morris water maze test, the rats were deeply anesthetized by an intraperitoneal injection of 2% sodium pentobarbital. The brains were then rapidly removed after decapitation. Bilateral hippocampal tissues were dissected immediately on an ice-cold plate. The tissues were weighed and homogenized in a predetermined volume of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) using an ice-water bath. The homogenates were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatants were collected for subsequent analysis. The levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-10, and TGF-β in the hippocampal supernatants were determined by following the specific protocol provided with each ELISA kit.

Western blot and co-immunoprecipitation

Brain tissues or cells were lysed in RIPA buffer. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes, and probed with antibodies against TRIC (1:1000), ubiquitin (1:1000), ZO-1 (1:1000), claudin-5 (1:1000), occludin (1:1000), and β-actin (1:5000). For Co-IP, lysates were immunoprecipitated with anti-TRIC antibody, followed by ubiquitin immunoblotting.

Immunohistochemistry

Brain Sect. (4 μm) were deparaffinized, subjected to antigen retrieval, and incubated with anti-TRIC antibody (1:200). HRP-conjugated secondary antibody and DAB were used for detection. Images were acquired with a light microscope.

In vitro BBB model and OGD

Primary rat brain microvascular endothelial cells (rBMECs) were cultured, and barrier integrity was monitored by transendothelial electrical resistance (TEER). For OGD, cells were placed in glucose-free DMEM in a hypoxic chamber (1% O₂, 5% CO₂, 94% N₂) for 4 h, then reoxygenated with or without Tub-B (10 µM) for 24 h. Permeability was assessed using FITC-dextran (4 kDa).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD from at least three independent experiments (biological replicates, n = 3). Analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0. One-way or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s or Bonferroni’s post hoc test was used. Statistical significance is indicated as follows: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ns (non-significant).

Results

Identification of Tub-B as the major circulating metabolite and its neuroprotective effects

UPLC-QQQ-MS analysis of plasma from mice administered with total glycosides of Cistanche (TCGs) revealed Tubuloside B (Tub-B) as the predominant bioactive metabolite, accounting for 52.3% ± 2.1% of the total relative peak area (Supplementary Table S1). In an Aβ₂₅–₃₅-induced PC12 cell injury model, Tub-B significantly increased cell viability in a dose-dependent manner, with the most potent effect observed at 10 µM (82.5% ± 2.8%, P < 0.001; Fig. 1). Furthermore, pre-treatment with Tub-B (50 µM) markedly reduced Aβ-induced apoptosis and caspase-3/7 activation (Figs. 2 and 3).

Network pharmacology implicates VEGF and ubiquitination pathways

Network pharmacology analysis identified five overlapping targets between Tub-B and chronic cerebral hypoperfusion (CCH), including KDR (VEGFR2) and MARVELD2 (TRIC) (Supplementary Table S2). Protein-protein interaction and KEGG pathway analyses highlighted significant enrichment in VEGF signaling, tight junction, and ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis pathways (Fig. 4A–E), suggesting that Tub-B may exert its effects through regulation of endothelial barrier integrity and protein stability.

Tub-B improves cognitive function and BBB integrity in CCH rats

In the Morris water maze test, CCH rats exhibited significantly prolonged escape latency and reduced time in the target quadrant compared to sham controls (P < 0.05; Fig. 5). Treatment with high-dose Tub-B (20 mg/kg) significantly improved spatial memory, increasing platform crossings and target quadrant occupancy (P < 0.05). Evans blue extravasation assays confirmed that Tub-B dose-dependently reduced BBB leakage, with the high-dose group showing near-normal permeability (0.038 ± 0.007 µg/g vs. CCH: 0.15 ± 0.02 µg/g, P < 0.001; Fig. 6).

Tub-B inhibits the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines in the hippocampal tissue of CCH rats

ELISA showed a significant elevation of TNF-α and IL-1β levels in the CCH group compared to the Sham group (all ***P < 0.001). Treatment with Tub-B particularly at the high dose, markedly suppressed this increase in pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α: *P < 0.05 for CCH + Tub-B-Low vs. CCH, ***P < 0.001 for CCH + Tub-B-High vs. CCH; IL-1β: *P < 0.05 for CCH + Tub-B-Low vs. CCH, ***P < 0.001 for CCH + Tub-B-High vs. CCH). Conversely, Tub-B administration enhanced the production of the anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-10 and TGF-β (***P < 0.001 for CCH + Tub-B-High vs. CCH). Furthermore, the levels of IL-10 and TGF-β in the CCH + Tub-B-High group were significantly higher than those in the CCH + Tub-B-Low group (all *P < 0.05). These results collectively demonstrate that Tub-B effectively attenuates neuroinflammation in the hippocampus following CCH ( Fig. 7).

Tub-B preserves TRIC expression and suppresses its ubiquitination

Immunofluorescence and Western blot analyses revealed that TRIC expression was significantly downregulated in the cortex of CCH rats and in OGD-treated rBMECs (P < 0.05; Figs. 8 and 9A). Tub-B treatment effectively restored TRIC protein levels. Co-immunoprecipitation demonstrated increased TRIC ubiquitination under both CCH and OGD conditions in rBMECs, which was markedly inhibited by Tub-B administration (Fig. 9B). The effect of Tub-B was comparable to that of the proteasome inhibitor MG-132, confirming its role in suppressing ubiquitin-proteasomal degradation of TRIC.

Tub-B enhances barrier function in an in vitro OGD model

In primary rat brain microvascular endothelial cells (rBMECs) subjected to OGD, Tub-B (10 µM) significantly increased transendothelial electrical resistance (TEER) and reduced FITC-dextran permeability, indicating improved barrier integrity. Consistent with in vivo findings, Tub-B treatment also attenuated OGD-induced TRIC ubiquitination.

Discussion

This study provides compelling evidence that Tubuloside B (Tub-B), the primary bioactive metabolite of total glycosides of Cistanche, confers significant protection against chronic cerebral hypoperfusion (CCH)-induced cognitive impairment by preserving blood-brain barrier (BBB) integrity through a novel mechanism involving inhibition of tricellulin (TRIC) ubiquitination and degradation.

Our findings establish that Tub-B is not only the major circulating compound after oral administration of TCGs but also a potent neuroprotective agent. It significantly attenuated Aβ₂₅–₃₅-induced cytotoxicity and apoptosis in PC12 cells, confirming its direct protective effects on neuronal cells. More importantly, through an integrated approach combining network pharmacology and experimental validation, we identified that Tub-B targets key pathways implicated in BBB regulation—specifically VEGF signaling, tight junction assembly, and ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis. Subsequent in vivo experiments demonstrated that Tub-B treatment markedly improved spatial memory and reduced BBB leakage in CCH rats. These functional improvements were mechanistically linked to the preservation of TRIC expression and inhibition of its ubiquitination, as well as a rebalancing of the inflammatory response through the suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and induction of anti-inflammatory factors.

The most significant mechanistic insight from this study is the demonstration that Tub-B inhibits ubiquitin-dependent degradation of TRIC, a critical protein localized at tricellular tight junctions that plays a vital role in sealing the paracellular pathway against macromolecule leakage [17–19]. Both in vivo and in vitro models consistently showed that CCH/OGD conditions promote TRIC ubiquitination and degradation, leading to compromised barrier integrity. Tub-B effectively reversed these changes, restoring TRIC protein levels and reducing its ubiquitination to an extent comparable to the proteasome inhibitor MG-132. These effects translated to functional improvements: Tub-B reduced Evans blue extravasation, enhanced transendothelial electrical resistance, and ultimately improved cognitive performance in CCH rats.

Our data further elucidate the upstream signaling events: under CCH/OGD conditions, VEGF/VEGFR2 activation triggers both PI3K/AKT and MAPK/ERK pathways, resulting in phosphorylation of ZO-1/ZO-2. This phosphorylation disrupts their crucial scaffolding function, impairing association with transmembrane proteins including TRIC. Concurrently, the E3 ubiquitin ligase TICH mediates K48-linked polyubiquitination of TRIC, targeting it for proteasomal degradation. Tub-B competes with VEGF for binding to VEGFR2, thereby inhibiting its activation and subsequent phosphorylation of ZO-1/ZO-2. This action helps stabilize the interaction between ZO proteins and transmembrane junctional proteins. Furthermore, Tub-B specifically blocks TICH-mediated TRIC ubiquitination and degradation. By these mechanisms, Tub-B effectively reduces BBB permeability and protects the neurovascular unit (see Fig S1).

The amelioration of neuroinflammation by Tub-B is likely secondary to BBB stabilization [10, 11]. By preserving TRIC at tricellular junctions, Tub-B limits the paracellular leakage of pro-inflammatory mediators and plasma components into the brain parenchyma, thereby attenuating the activation of innate immune responses in the CNS. This mechanism aligns with growing evidence that BBB breakdown is a critical initiator of neuroinflammation and cognitive decline in vascular cognitive impairment [6–9, 11].

While our study demonstrates the promising efficacy of Tub-B in preserving BBB integrity by inhibiting TRIC ubiquitination, several translational challenges remain to be addressed in future studies. First, the oral bioavailability of Tub-B requires thorough investigation, which may involve developing sophisticated formulations or prodrug strategies to enhance its gastrointestinal absorption and systemic exposure. Second, although our data confirm its pharmacological action in the brain, the precise extent of its BBB penetration needs to be quantitatively determined through dedicated pharmacokinetic studies. Finally, comprehensive investigational new drug (IND)-enabling studies, including long-term toxicology and safety assessments in advanced animal models, are essential prerequisites before its clinical potential for treating chronic cerebral conditions like vascular dementia can be realized.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, while network pharmacology and functional assays suggest that Tub-B interferes with VEGFR2 signaling, direct binding evidence (e.g., SPR, molecular docking, or cellular thermal shift assays) is still needed. Second, although our findings support a role for Tub-B in inhibiting TRIC ubiquitination, the use of TRIC or VEGFR2 knockdown or overexpression models would provide more definitive mechanistic evidence. Finally, the specific E3 ligase responsible for TRIC ubiquitination remains to be fully validated. These aspects will be the focus of future studies.

In conclusion, our results demonstrate that Tub-B ameliorates CCH-induced cognitive impairment by inhibiting VEGFR2 activation and TICH-mediated ubiquitination of TRIC, thereby preserving BBB integrity. This work not only provides insights into the regulation of tricellular tight junctions under ischemic conditions, but also highlights the therapeutic potential of targeting ubiquitination pathways for the treatment of VCI and other BBB-related neurological disorders. These findings position Tub-B as a promising therapeutic candidate and identify TRIC ubiquitination as a novel target for intervention in cerebrovascular diseases.
