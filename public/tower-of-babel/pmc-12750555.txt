Cerebrospinal fluid NPTX2 and [18F]FDG PET track serotonergic vulnerability to neurodegeneration in prodromal Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Federico Massa, Beatrice Orso, Francesca De Cesari, Virginia Pelagotti, Sara Garbarino, Stefano Raffa, Pietro Mattioli, Lucia Argenti, Lorenzo Lombardo, Mattia Losa, Giulia Bozzo, Wendy Kreshpa, Giulia Tomassini, Alessia Panza, Alessio Cirone, Luigi Lorenzini, Federica Bozzano, Davide Visigalli, Andrea Brugnolo, Nicola Girtler, Michele Piana, Dario Arnaldi, Silvia Morbelli, Gianmario Sambuceti, Antonio Uccelli, Matteo Pardini

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn Alzheimer’s disease (AD) the progressive accumulation of misfolded proteins, notably amyloid-β and hyperphosphorylated tau, initiates a cascade of metabolic and synaptic dysfunction, and ultimately neurodegeneration. These early molecular alterations interact with diffuse neurotrasmitter projection systems, contributing to cognitive and behavioural manifestations.MethodsThis study assessed baseline [18F]FDG-PET metabolism and its relationship with neurotransmitter receptor/transporter maps and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) markers of AD neuropathology (Aβ42/Aβ40, pTau181, t-Tau), neuroaxonal (NFL), and synaptic damage (NPTX2, neurogranin) in 49 Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI)-AD patients. Relative hypometabolism was derived by comparing [¹⁸F]FDG-PET scans of patients and 40 healthy controls. Spatial correlation coefficients between hypometabolism maps and normative cortical maps of neurotransmitter receptor/transporter density were computed and included in linear models with CSF biomarkers, adjusting for demographic and cognitive variables.ResultsPatients exhibited significant hypometabolism in bilateral temporo-parietal regions. Negative correlations emerged between hypometabolism and receptor distributions for 5-HT1A (r = − 0.36, p < 0.001), D1 (r = − 0.15, p = 0.03), and mGluR5 (r = − 0.14, p = 0.02), which indicates that reduced glucose uptake in MCI-AD was mostly found in areas with normally high receptor densities. Noteworthy, CSF NPTX2 levels correlated with the co-localization of hypometabolism and 5-HT1A receptor distribution (β = 0.003, p = 0.038) with no significant associations for other CSF markers.ConclusionsAccording to our findings, correlations between reduced glucose metabolism and specific neurotransmitter receptor densities suggest early involvement and vulnerability of these systems in MCI-AD. Moreover, the association between CSF NPTX2 and 5-HT1A receptor vulnerability indicates that synaptic dysfunction in AD may parallel serotonergic involvement, and supports CSF NPTX2 as a potential biomarker for disease staging.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13195-025-01893-6.Keywords: Cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers, [18F]FDG PET, Synaptic dysfunction, Mild cognitive impairment (MCI), Alzheimer's disease, Neuronal pentraxin-2

Background

Mild cognitive impairment due to Alzheimer’s disease (MCI-AD) refers to a specific condition in which cognitive deficits are attributed to the underlying AD neuropathology (ADNP) [1]. This prodromal phase serves as an intermediate stage between the cognitive changes of normal aging and the more pronounced impairments characteristic of dementia, offering a critical window for uncovering subtle neurobiological changes and biomarkers that can aid in patient stratification and inform targeted treatment strategies [2].

Among the early biological alterations in AD, dysfunction of neurotransmitter systems—including serotonin, dopamine, glutamate, acetylcholine, and noradrenaline—has gained increasing recognition as a relevant yet underexplored pathophysiological component. Their diffuse and integrative nature suggests they may significantly modulate both cognitive trajectories and neuropsychiatric symptoms, thus contributing to the clinical heterogeneity of prodromal AD [3–6].

Despite their relevance, neurochemical pathways remain difficult to assess in vivo. Researchers are integrating distinct imaging modalities with neurotransmitter system mapping, leveraging innovative methodologies such as the JuSpace toolbox [7]. This integrated approach enables a nuanced analysis of the spatial relationships between imaging abnormalities (whether morphological or molecular) and neurotransmitter cortical maps and has been employed in various neurocognitive degenerative conditions [8–10]. Notably, when applied to [18F]FDG PET, this methodology has demonstrated specific spatial associations between neurotransmitter maps and brain hypometabolism in patients with Dementia with Lewy bodies [8, 10]. These findings highlight the potential of [18F]FDG PET not only as a general marker of synaptic and neuronal dysfunction with established diagnostic and prognostic value [11, 12] but also as a tool to map the selective vulnerability of neurotransmitter systems to neurodegeneration.

Detecting early metabolic alterations in regions innervated by neurotransmitter circuits may offer functional insights into the breakdown of these pathways and their contribution to symptoms and AD progression. However, imaging alone may not adequately reflect the underlying biological complexity. A more comprehensive understanding may benefit from multimodal approaches that integrate imaging with fluid biomarkers to uncover pathological processes interacting with neurotransmitter dysfunction that may drive clinical variability and cognitive deterioration.

At the same time, there is growing recognition that the neuropathological heterogeneity of AD is not fully explained by core biomarkers of amyloidosis and tauopathy alone. Rather, it requires for biomarkers capturing additional facets and pathophysiological processes that co-occur within the AD cascade, even if not specific to the disease [1, 13].

Synaptic dysfunction and loss, for instance, is a pivotal process that shows a strong association with cognitive decline. It can now be measured using fluid biomarkers that reflect alterations in synaptic structure and function, offering a window into neuronal circuit integrity and compensatory failure in early disease stages [14–17]. Despite identifying several CSF synaptic proteins, evidence substantiating their role in AD pathophysiology remains incomplete [18, 19]. Among these proteins, neurogranin (Ng) has been pinpointed as indicative of post-synaptic integrity, correlating with cognitive decline and metabolic changes in AD [20–22]. Recent discoveries emphasize the significance of CSF Neuronal Pentraxin 2 (NPTX2) as a promising indicator for synaptic alterations, correlating with clinical progression and brain metabolism metrics in regions regulating cognitive function [23, 24]. In the broader landscape of neurodegeneration, neurofilament light chain (NFL) represents a key marker of axonal damage, complementing synaptic biomarkers by reflecting a distinct dimension of pathological progression. NFL has been demonstrated as the most reliable marker of axonal damage across a wide range of inflammatory and degenerative CNS conditions [1, 25]. NFL levels increase in AD, already at the MCI stage, and correlate with cognitive impairment, amyloid burden, cortical atrophy and transition time from MCI to dementia [22, 26, 27].

Given these premises, a multimodal investigation that integrates CSF biomarkers reflecting ADNP, synaptic dysfunction, and axonal damage with molecular imaging and neurotransmitter system mapping appears particularly promising in this transitional phase of cognitive decline. However, how these complementary modalities relate to one another—particularly in terms of the spatial correspondence between metabolic dysfunction, neurotransmitter system distribution, and fluid biomarker levels—has yet to be systematically investigated in AD.

This study, therefore specifically aims to examine how levels of CSF biomarkers related to ADNP (i.e., amyloid-beta and tau proteins), synaptopathy (i.e., neurogranin and NPTX2), and neuroaxonal damage (NFL) correlate with the spatial convergence between metabolic alterations observed on [18F]FDG PET and neurotransmitter system maps in prodromal AD.

We hypothesize that specific CSF biomarkers may reflect selective vulnerability in cortical regions densely innervated by diffuse neurotransmitter projections. Disruption of these systems could signal circuit-specific progressive neurodegenerative changes, providing novel insights into AD mechanisms at a prodromal stage.

Methods

Patients and controls

This retrospective study included 49 individuals (30 women), with a mean age of 75.2 years (range 50.3–85.1) and a mean formal educational attainment of 10.3 years (range 5–23). All patients had a diagnosis of typical, high-likelihood mild cognitive impairment due to Alzheimer’s disease (MCI-AD), based on the NIA-AA criteria [2], and were identified from the MCI-AD database of the IRCCS Policlinico San Martino hospital (Genoa, Italy), covering the period from May 2017 to April 2021. To be eligible, participants were required to have undergone both cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis for standard AD biomarkers (amyloid-β42/β40 ratio), phosphorylated tau protein at threonine 181, pTau181), and neuronal damage (total tau protein, t-Tau), to confirm an AD-compatible AT(N) profile [28], and [18F]FDG PET imaging, with the two procedures completed within a two-month interval. Additionally, a clinical follow-up of at least two years was considered essential for inclusion to secure diagnostic confirmation and minimize baseline misclassification. Moreover, other neurological and psychiatric disorders were excluded and subjects with changes in psychoactive or nootropic drugs in the six months preceding the acquisition of the PET scan were not considered eligible.

During the initial assessment, participants underwent comprehensive medical, neurological, and neuropsychological test evaluations, resulting in a diagnosis of amnestic MCI [29] based on objective impairment on delayed recall (≥ 1.5 SD below normative) and preserved daily functioning with Clinical Dementia Rating, CDR, of 0.5). To evaluate behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD), the Neuropsychiatric Inventory (NPI) and the Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS) were also administered. Blood tests and brain MRI scans were conducted to exclude non-degenerative causes of cognitive impairment as recommended [30].

We included a group of healthy controls (HC) consisting of 40 volunteers who underwent [18F]FDG-PET in previous research. These volunteers, comprising 20 females, had an average age of 75.6 years (range 54.9–85.1) and 10.7 years of education (range 5–17). Their cognitive status was assessed through clinical history and examination, ensuring an MMSE score above 27 and a CDR 0. Additionally, their [18F]FDG-PET scans were confirmed to be normal through independent visual assessment by two expert nuclear medicine physicians.

CSF biomarkers

CSF collection and initial processing followed standardized procedures outlined in the Supplementary methods. We utilized the Lumipulse G600 II® fully automated chemiluminescent enzyme immunoassay system (Fujirebio Europe, Gent, Belgium) to measure CSF AD standard biomarkers (Aβ42/Aβ40, pTau181, and t-Tau) in CSF samples from MCI-AD patients. The assay cartridge datasheet cut-offs were validated for application in our center: Aβ42/40 ratio 0.069, p-Tau181 56.5 pg/mL, t-Tau 404 pg/mL [22]. In the same samples, we quantified synaptic proteins, namely (i) NPTX2 using a commercially available ELISA assay (INNOTEST, Fujirebio Europe, Gent, Belgium), and (ii) neurogranin (Ng, truncated p75) using commercially available ELISA kits (Euroimmun, Lubeck, Germany). Neurofilament light chain (NFL) levels were assessed using the Simple Plex Ella automated microfluidic platform (ProteinSimple).

Concentrations for all these proteins were measured in pg/mL. The average coefficient of variation (CV) for intra- and inter-test reproducibility was below 10% and 15%, respectively, for all assays.

[18 F]FDG PET imaging data Preparation

[18F]FDG PET scans were acquired in accordance with EANM guidelines, following standardized fasting and resting protocols. Information on psychotropic medications potentially affecting cerebral glucose metabolism was collected for each participant [31]. Images were reconstructed with standard clinical methods and preprocessed using SPM12, including normalization to an MNI template and 10 mm Gaussian smoothing. Two experienced readers independently reviewed image quality and preprocessing accuracy. The methodology for acquisition and image pre-processing is detailed in prior research [22] and Supplementary methods.

Statistical analysis

Spatial correlation between brain metabolism and neurotransmitter maps

As a first step, we conducted standard voxel-wise analyses in SPM12 to compare. [18F]FDG PET scans of HC and MCI-AD patients. At the voxel level, a significance threshold of p < 0.001 (uncorrected) was applied. At the cluster level, statistical significance was assessed using a family-wise error (FWE) correction at p < 0.05, with a minimum cluster extent threshold of 100 voxels. This analysis identified areas of relative hypometabolism in patients, using HC as the reference group.

To evaluate whether these observed spatial metabolic patterns are connected to the cortical distribution of receptors or transporters of distinct diffuse neurotransmitter systems, we preliminarily extracted regional neurotransmitter distributions using the JuSpace toolbox (version 1.3, available at https://github.com/juryxy/JuSpace). Briefly, the normative neurotransmitter maps included serotonergic, dopaminergic, GABAergic, opioid, noradrenergic, cholinergic, and glutamatergic systems, derived from independent healthy volunteer populations included in the toolbox [7]. In this study, we focused on density maps of the GABA-A receptor, the metabotropic glutamate receptor type (mGluR5), the dopamine D1 and D2 receptors and the dopamine transporter (DAT), the 5-hydroxytryptamine 1a (5HT1a), 5HT1B, 5HT2a, and 5HT4 receptors and the serotonin transporter (SERT), the noradrenaline transporter (NAT), and the vesicular acetylcholine transporter (VAChT), to assess GABAergic, glutamatergic, serotonin, dopamine and cholinergic systems, in concordance with previous research in neurodegenerative dementias [9].

To study the possible relationship between [18F]FDG PET results and the aforementioned maps, we utilized the JuSpace toolbox with default settings. We calculated the Spearman correlation coefficients between voxel-wise [18F]FDG-PET data from both patients and HC and the neurotransmitter distribution maps included in the toolbox (option 5). These correlation coefficients were then transformed into z-scores, standardizing the values and enabling a more robust spatial covariance analysis between brain hypometabolism and neurotransmitter maps [7]. To determine whether the observed distribution of Fisher’s z-transformed individual correlation coefficients across patients deviated from a null distribution, exact permutation-based p-values were computed as implemented in JuSpace. This involved 10,000 permutations, where group labels were randomly assigned using orthogonal permutations. All analyses were corrected for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate (FDR) across the number of neurotransmitter maps tested. A negative correlation (resulting in a negative Fisher’s z-score) indicates that the decline in metabolism in MCI-AD patients, relatively to HC, is most pronounced in regions with normally high availability of the studied neurotransmitter receptor or transporter (as derived from independent healthy populations). This points to increased disease involvement in these cortical regions, potentially implicating diffuse neurotransmitter systems.

Correlations with CSF protein levels

We assessed the relationship between neurotransmitter outputs, specifically the Fisher’s z-transformed correlation coefficients derived from hypometabolism-neurotransmitter maps (obtained using the JuSpace toolbox), and CSF protein levels. The Fisher’s z-transformed outputs reflect the degree of spatial overlap between hypometabolism patterns and neurotransmitter systems, allowing for the evaluation of how these relationships are related with CSF protein levels (Aβ42/Aβ40, pTau181, t-Tau, NPTX2, Ng, and NFL). All variables were standardized to a uniform [0,1] range prior to analysis. Following normalization, we constructed a set of linear regression models in which the Fisher’s z-transformed correlation coefficients of hypometabolism-neurotransmitter maps served as the response variables, while CSF protein levels were included as predictors. Age, sex, education, and MMSE scores were added to the models as confounding variables to control for their potential effects. All these models were tested in the complete set of all MCI-AD subjects. Noteworthy, only negative Fisher’s z-transformed outputs were tested in the model, indicating regions where the relative reduction in metabolism among MCI-AD patients was most pronounced in areas normally characterized by high availability of the investigated neurotransmitter receptor or transporter.

In a post-hoc analysis, significant outputs from the models were correlated with neuropsychiatric scale scores (total NPI and GDS), both of which were log₁₀(x + 1) transformed to account for non-normal distribution and the considerable number of patients with a score of zero, which could otherwise introduce a floor effect and distort linear correlation analyses.

All statistical analysis was performed using R (version 4.1.1; http://www.r-project.org/), with p < 0.05 significance threshold.

In voxel-wise comparisons to HC, relative hypometabolism was observed in MCI-AD patients covering extensively bilateral temporo-parietal regions, as expected (Fig. 1).

The two-dimensional representation (axial, frontal, and sagittal cuts) illustrates areas of relative hypometabolism in MCI-AD patients compared to healthy controls (HC) in blue. These are overlaid on images depicting the distribution patterns of the 5HT1A receptor, measured using the radioligand [carbonyl-11 C]WAY-100,635 in a normative population [32], shown as a spectrum ranging from lower uptake in black to higher uptake in red. The spatial covariance analysis between [18F]FDG-PET hypometabolism data and neurotransmitter maps revealed that the pattern of relative hypometabolism in the AD group, compared to HC, was significantly associated with the spatial distribution of several neurotransmitter receptors and transporters. Specifically, we found a negative correlation, indicating that, in MCI-AD patients, glucose uptake was relatively reduced in regions with a high density of 5-HT1a (r = − 0.36, p < 0.001), D1 (r = − 0.15, p = 0.031), and mGluR5 (r = − 0.14, p = 0.020) receptors, compared to HC. Conversely, a positive correlation was observed in areas with a low density of 5-HT1b (r = 0.11, p = 0.006), GABA-A (r = 0.22, p < 0.001) receptors, and NAT (r = 0.10, p = 0.045), indicating a reduction in glucose uptake in patients compared to HC.

Correlations with CSF protein levels

We observed a significant correlation of CSF NPTX2 levels with the z-scores coefficients between regional relative hypometabolism and the maps of 5-HT1A receptors (β = 0.00027 ± 0.00013; p = 0.038). No other significant correlation was detected between CSF proteins and the Fisher’s z-transformed correlation coefficients of hypometabolism with respective neurotransmitter maps included in this analysis, as detailed in Supplementary Table 3s.

In the post hoc analysis, the correlation between NPI scores and CSF NPTX2 levels showed a trend toward a negative association (r = − 0.27, p = 0.065), without reaching statistical significance. No significant association was found between GDS-15 scores and CSF NPTX2 levels (p = 0.628). Additionally, correlations between either NPI or GDS-15 scores and the z-score coefficients of co-spatiality between 5HT1A distribution and relative hypometabolism maps yielded no significant results (p = 0.755).

Discussion

This study highlights the complex interplay between CSF biomarkers, brain metabolism, and neurotransmitter systems in individuals with prodromal AD. Our findings support the emerging paradigm of multimodal phenotyping, offering novel insights that may enable the development of combinatorial therapeutic strategies and guide patient stratification.

The spatial covariance analysis revealed intriguing patterns in the relationship between regional metabolic decline and neurotransmitter receptor distributions. Notably, significant negative correlations were identified between regional glucose uptake in the bilateral temporo-parietal regions— consistent with the characteristic [18F]FDG PET AD pattern [11, 12] — and cortical densities of 5-HT1A, D1, and mGluR5 receptors derived from normative maps [7]. This finding suggests greater disease involvement in these cortical regions during the early stages of AD, potentially reflecting the heightened vulnerability to the pathological changes of the serotonergic, dopaminergic, and glutamatergic systems, which are physiologically abundant in these areas. This pattern of selective vulnerability, identified through neurotransmitter distribution mapping, is consistent with Premi et al. [9] who observed significant correlations between gray matter volume (GMV) changes and the spatial distribution of serotonin, dopamine, and glutamatergic pathways. Notably, both studies demonstrate that specific neurotransmitter systems are particularly vulnerable to the progression of neurodegeneration across different conditions and methodological approaches.

5-HT1A receptors, primarily expressed in the hippocampus, temporal, and prefrontal cortex [32, 33], are critical for memory and learning, attention, spatial navigation, decision-making, emotional and behavioral regulation [34–39]. The 5-HT1A receptor declines in activity and density by approximately 10% per decade with aging [40]. This reduction is even more pronounced in patients with MCI and AD, where 5-HT1A receptor loss progresses with worsening cognitive impairment, particularly in the hippocampus and dorsal raphe nucleus [41, 42]. These brain region- and receptor-specific serotonergic alterations may contribute to lower cognitive reserve and accelerate cognitive and behavioral impairment in AD [34]. Similarly, dopaminergic dysfunction contributes to early cognitive and behavioral symptoms and disease progression in AD [43, 44]. D1 receptors, essential for learning and memory are highly vulnerable to early AD pathology and closely interact with cholinergic pathways, modulating acetylcholine release and thus influencing cognitive performance [44]. Beyond monoamines, glutamatergic signaling via the metabotropic glutamate receptor 5 (mGluR5) in AD pathophysiology is increasingly evident. mGluR5 is abundantly expressed in the hippocampus and modulates excitatory transmission and synaptic plasticity. Its dysregulation by amyloid-β oligomers leads to dendritic spine loss, altered NMDA signaling, and glial-mediated neuroinflammation, thereby contributing to the functional breakdown observed in vulnerable cortical regions and accelerate cognitive decline [45, 46]. This pattern of selective neurotransmitter vulnerability is consistent with Premi et al. [9] who observed significant correlations between gray matter volume (GMV) changes and the spatial distribution of serotonin, dopamine, and glutamatergic pathways. Notably, both studies demonstrate that specific neurotransmitter systems are particularly vulnerable to the progression of neurodegeneration across different conditions and methodological approaches.

In contrast, the significant positive spatial correlations with NAT, GABA-A, and 5-HT1B maps from our study indicate that relative hypometabolism in MCI-AD is more pronounced in regions less enriched in these targets in healthy individuals [33]. This spatial pattern may reflect a lower intrinsic vulnerability of these systems during prodromal AD. For instance, the noradrenaline transporter (NAT) expression is concentrated in areas that remain relatively spared from early metabolic decline, aligning with the notion that noradrenergic dysfunction emerges later in the disease [47, 48]. However, the locus coeruleus— the principal noradrenergic nucleus—is already affected in prodromal and even preclinical AD, with early tau accumulation and corresponding alterations on neuromelanin-sensitive MRI and tau PET [49], indicating that although cortical noradrenergic involvement may occur later, subcortical nuclei are engaged early. Similarly, the density of GABA-A receptors is lower in the temporo-parietal cortices—regions heavily affected by AD-related pathology—suggesting relative preservation in early stage [33]. Because GABAergic inhibition confers neuroprotection by constraining excitatory drive and stabilizing network activity [50, 51], the comparatively lower GABA-A​ receptor density in temporo-parietal association cortices—regions primarily affected by AD—may reduce inhibitory reserve and contribute to their early metabolic vulnerability, whereas primary sensory areas, which typically show higher GABA-A​ availability, are relatively preserved in initial stages [33]. Regarding the serotonergic system, while 5-HT1A receptors are highly expressed in the hippocampus and limbic regions, 5-HT1B receptors are particularly abundant in the frontal cortex and basal ganglia which are less affected in early AD [33, 52]. Their relative preservation in our MCI-AD sample aligns with prior reports showing that 5-HT1B loss is more pronounced in later stages, correlating with cognitive decline and behavioral symptoms [53]. Collectively, these findings indicate that the regional distribution of NAT, GABA-A, and 5-HT1B receptors may confer a degree of resilience to the early pathological processes of AD, potentially influencing the progression and clinical manifestations of the disease.

The specificity of these spatial associations emphasizes the distinct impact of AD-related processes on neurotransmitter systems and underscores the importance of spatially informed approaches when interpreting neurotransmitter involvement in disease progression.

Among the CSF biomarkers analyzed, only NPTX2 concentrations showed a significant positive correlation with the z-scores derived from the spatial covariance analysis. Noteworthy, in the correlation analysis with CSF biomarkers we focused solely on negative z-scores, as they indicate spatial co-localization between relative hypometabolism in MCI-AD patients and regions highly enriched in receptor/transporter, reflecting neuronal damage and disruption of neurotransmitter systems. NPTX2 is a pre-synaptic protein involved in excitatory synapse formation, plasticity, and homeostasis [23]. Given its function, altered CSF NPTX2 levels have been interpreted as a marker of synaptic dysfunction and loss, early pathological events in AD and associated with cognitive decline, metabolic impairment, and neurodegeneration [23, 24, 54–56]. In our previous study [24], we demonstrated that CSF NPTX2 levels correlated with glucose metabolism in the precuneus and varied according to the stage of MCI. Being higher in early than late MCI shortly approaching dementia, we proposed that CSF NPTX2 declines as AD progresses, supporting its potential as a biomarker for disease staging. Building on this, our current result indicates that decreasing CSF NPTX2 levels are associated with increasingly negative z-scores in the spatial covariance analysis, which captures the relationship between relative cortical hypometabolism and the normative distribution of 5-HT1A receptors. In contrast, CSF biomarkers reflecting amyloidosis, tauopathy, and neuroaxonal damage did not show a similar spatial correlation pattern. These findings support the hypothesis that reduced NPTX2 may reflect progressive dysfunction within serotonergic-enriched cortical circuits, thereby positioning it as a uniquely sensitive marker.

In the context of AD, the contribution of the serotonergic system has been extensively studied, with decreased receptor availability linked to impairments in mood regulation, cognitive function, and psychiatric manifestations [6, 34]. Synaptic degeneration may further exacerbate disruptions in 5-HT signaling, characterized by decreased receptor availability and dysfunction in serotonergic transmission [6]. Moreover, a significant association between NPTX2 and the serotoninergic system has been unraveled using different approaches. In animal models, for example, NPTX2 has been associated with modulation of anxiety levels [57] and with treatment with antidepressants, including serotoninergic agents [58].

Monoaminergic neurotransmitters are increasingly recognized as modulators of glial immunity and thus potential contributors to neuroimmune homeostasis in AD. DA is an endogenous regulator of inflammasome activation through DRD1-dependent pathways in glia to inhibit inflammatory signaling and reduces neuroinflammation in vivo [59]. Noradrenaline from the locus coeruleus mitigates microglial pro-inflammatory gene expression while enhancing microglial migration and Aβ phagocytosis [60]. In parallel, serotonin receptors on microglia—notably 5-HT2B and 5-HT7 in the hippocampus—shape surveillance, motility, phagocytosis, and cytokine output, underscoring the capacity of monoamines to control microglial responses, contributing to neuroimmune homeostasis [61]. Beyond microglia, monoaminergic–glial crosstalk involves the entire neuroinflammatory unit. Astrocytic activation is a central component and is quantifiable through glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), a reliable fluid marker of neuroinflammation in different neurological conditions [62]. Recent research showed that plasma GFAP correlates with tau-PET signal—predominantly in lateral temporal regions—predicts faster cognitive decline, and partially mediates the associations between amyloid and tau pathology and between tau and subsequent decline [63]. Incorporating plasma GFAP alongside our current biomarker panel would strengthen the mechanistic link between early monoaminergic dysfunction, synaptic alteration, and astroglial reactivity, thereby enabling testing of hypothesis-driven, region-specific associations between GFAP levels and neurotransmitter receptor topography. These receptor–glial couplings may precede—and contribute to—early synaptic and metabolic shifts and will be the focus of near-term analyses in an expanded MCI-AD cohort.

Validation in an independent dataset is paramount particularly regarding the correlation between CSF NPTX2 levels and cognitive-behavioral measures. In the post-hoc analysis, the correlation between NPI scores and CSF NPTX2 levels only revealed a trend toward a negative association. This lack of significance may be due to the relatively small sample size, which limits the statistical power and highlights the need for larger cohorts to evaluate the potential clinical relevance of this finding. Furthermore, no significant association was found between total NPI scores and the 5HT1A receptor vulnerability to neurodegeneration. Notably, the NPI is not specific to serotonergic dysfunction, as it captures a broad range of neuropsychiatric symptoms mediated by different neurochemical pathways. This likely contributes to the absence of a clear association between NPI scores and a single serotonergic marker, such as 5HT1A. These findings underscore the complex neurobiological underpinnings of BPSD in AD, as well as the need for more targeted clinical scales capable of selectively capturing dysfunctions related to specific neurotransmitter systems.

Finally, caution is needed when interpreting the spatial covariance results, which rely on normative neurotransmitter maps from various sources [7]. While our results suggest potential links between metabolic changes in regions with higher neurotransmitter distribution in healthy controls, they do not establish a causal relationship between hypometabolism and neurotransmitter alterations.

Anxiety–depressive symptoms, irrespective of their etiological relationship to AD, are associated with alterations in neurotransmitter signaling [64]. As discussed above, these alterations can modulate glial responses and neuronal excitability, thereby affecting regional metabolism and potentially confounding neurotransmitter-based interpretations. Accordingly, our findings should be read with this caveat, as symptom-related transmitter shifts may partially overlap with AD-related mechanisms. Moreover, in our cohort, most patients were already receiving psychotropic drugs from various classes, prominently serotoninergic agents to manage anxiety-depressive symptoms, at the time of the initial evaluation. This data aligns with existing literature indicating that neuropsychiatric symptoms may represent an early presentation modality of AD, requiring therapeutic interventions [1, 65, 66]. These observations further underscore the need to investigate the pathophysiological role of the diffuse neurotransmitter systems, which are modulated by these therapies. It should be noted that our analysis was not corrected for ongoing treatments. This methodological decision is justified by the reliance on neurotransmitter maps obtained from normative cohorts [7]. The potential impact of benzodiazepines on brain glucose metabolism was deemed negligible as only a minority of patients were taking this class of drugs (n = 5, 10.2%). In any case, all the treatments were stable for at least six months, minimizing the risk of transient, confounding effects.

Conclusions

By integrating [18F]FDG PET, neurotransmitter receptor maps, and CSF markers, we gained insight into how synaptic loss and neurotransmitter imbalances intersect with glucose metabolism deficits in MCI-AD. The observed correlation between CSF NPTX2 levels and 5-HT1A receptor density in hypometabolic regions highlights its potential as a biomarker of synaptic dysfunction and serotonin-related circuit vulnerability. These findings emphasize the importance of investigating the early pathophysiological processes of AD, beyond the canonical neuropathological hallmarks. Future studies should aim to disentangle the mechanisms driving the correlations from our study and assess how these patterns evolve throughout the disease trajectory. Incorporating diverse fluid biomarkers and advanced imaging techniques will help clarify AD pathology, with longitudinal studies in larger patient cohorts being crucial for validating findings and tracking disease progression. This broader perspective may open new avenues for both diagnostic refinement and therapeutic innovation.
