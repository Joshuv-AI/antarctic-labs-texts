Seasonal dynamics, resting behaviour and insecticide susceptibility of Anopheles mosquitoes across two differentially malaria-endemic regions in Ethiopia
Authors: Delelegn Woyessa, Delenasaw Yewhalaw
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Information on bionomics of Anopheles species composition, seasonal dynamics and insecticide susceptibility status is important to understand malaria transmission in any particular eco-epidemiological setting and for the design of effective vector control strategies. Here, adult mosquitoes were collected using CDC light traps (CDC LTs) and human landing catches (HLC) for 17 months between June 2018 and September 2020 from Lare District of Ethiopia. Larvae and pupae of anopheline mosquitoes were collected from breeding sites of Lare District and Jimma and reared to adulthood. Bioassays were conducted on non-blood-fed, 3–5-day-old emerged adult female mosquitoes using WHO insecticide-impregnated papers. Plasmodium spp. infection in Anopheles pharoensis and An. coustani were identified using TaqMan qPCR. Of 11,048 Anopheles mosquitoes collected, An. coustani was the most common (46.20%, n = 5103), followed by An. pharoensis (27.89%, n = 3082), An. gambiae (s.l.) (16.04%, n = 1772) and An. funestus (9.88%, n = 1091). Most of the mosquitoes (73.17%, n = 8084) were collected using CDC LTs and 26.83% (n = 2964) were captured using HLC. The mean density of An. gambiae, An. pharoensis and An. coustani was generally higher in June-November as compared to other months during the study period. However, the mean density of An. funestus was relatively higher through November-January following the peak malaria period in Lare. Anopheles pharoensis, An. gambiae (s.l.) and An. coustani complex were susceptible to pirimiphos-methyl, propoxur and bendiocarb. However, An. pharoensis and An. gambiae (s.l.) were resistant to DDT, deltamethrin, permethrin and malathion, whilst members of the An. coustani complex were resistant to DDT and deltamethrin. No Plasmodium spp. infection was detected from samples of Anopheles coustani and An. pharoensis. There was temporal variation in mean density of Anopheles mosquitoes and higher mean mosquito density was recorded during June-November (except for An. funestus). The higher outdoor mean mosquito density coupled with multiple insecticide resistance of malaria vector populations and occurrence of An. funestus in the dry season call for regular monitoring and intervention considering the behavioural dynamics of Anopheles species across seasons.

Introduction

Malaria is one of the most serious protozoan vector-borne diseases of humans and is largely intertwined with poverty (Bigoga et al., 2012). The scale-up and deployment of insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) and indoor residual spraying (IRS) interventions in sub-Saharan Africa have contributed to significant reductions in malaria transmission and mortality between 2000 and 2015 (Bhatt et al., 2015). However, the malaria burden is still high in the World Health Organization (WHO) African region, where 95% of the 247 million cases in the world and approximately 96% of the 619,000 deaths occurred in 2021 (WHO, 2022). This could be partly due to the threat posed by insecticide resistant malaria vectors (Ranson and Lissenden, 2016), climatic variables (Mazarire et al., 2024), diversity in species and behaviour of vectors that make disease transmission complex, and also difficulty in exact case prediction, necessitating regular vector surveillance and intervention to curb the disease burden.

In Ethiopia, malaria remains to be one of the major public health and socioeconomic problems despite its dramatic reduction in the last two decades (FMoH, 2020). The disease is highly seasonal with varying intensities of transmission due to altitudinal and climatic variation. Approximately 75% of the total area of Ethiopia is malarious (Girum et al., 2019; Adugna et al., 2020) and over 60% of the country’s population is estimated to be at risk of the disease (Ayele et al., 2012; FMoH, 2018).

Vector control is one of the pillar strategies implemented in Ethiopia to reduce the malaria burden using insecticide-treated bed nets (ITNs) and indoor residual spraying (IRS) as the two main malaria intervention tools (Taffese et al., 2018). However, the effectiveness of these interventions varies due to increased resistance of vectors to public health insecticides (Yewhalaw et al., 2010; Abate and Hadis, 2011; Yewhalaw et al., 2012; Fettene et al., 2013; Massebo et al., 2013; Alemayehu et al., 2017; Chanyalew et al., 2022), the vector species and their behavioural responses to the intervention tools (Bass et al., 2008). Dependence on a limited number of public-health insecticides due to pyrethroids being the only available insecticide so far for treating bed nets, and the availability of only four classes of insecticides for IRS are additional challenges that jeopardize disease control (Hemingway et al., 2013).

The behavioural plasticity of vector mosquitoes following the application of long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs) and IRS (Reddy et al., 2011; Kitau et al., 2012; Gatton et al., 2013) as well as residual transmission that is acquired mainly via outdoor or early biting (Gimnig et al., 2013) could threaten the anticipated disease control and elimination programme in Ethiopia. Studies reveal that a shift towards outdoor biting and resting vectors could result from interventions that target indoor feeding and resting vectors. For instance, behavioural shift in An. arabiencis and An. funestus following mass LLINs distribution was reported in Tanzania (Kreppel et al., 2020). A study in Ethiopia also indicated that certain malaria vector species have already undergone behavioural modifications, potentially in response to the scale-up of indoor-based malaria vector control tool (Gari and Lindtjørn, 2018). Hence, understanding resting and biting behaviour of Anopheles mosquitoes across seasons in a defined area would help to highlight the importance of mosquito behavioural adaptations to vector control interventions and subsequently design tools that can prevent outdoor as well as indoor malaria transmission.

Moreover, environmental factors are also important variables that play a fundamental role in determining mosquito populations’ survival, reproduction, growth, abundance, dispersal, distribution, and disease transmission (Lindsay et al., 1998). Seasonal dynamics of Anopheles mosquito populations are significantly influenced by a range of climatic factors including rainfall, temperature, wind and humidity (Munhenga et al., 2014; Abiodun et al., 2016; Asgarian et al., 2021; Mazarire et al., 2024). Rainfall and temperature primarily influence mosquito abundance and distribution, controlling their population in time and space (Afrane et al., 2012; Mazarire et al., 2024). Rainfall creates essential breeding habitats, directly impacting mosquito abundance and seasonality. For instance, from a study conducted in Kenya, Walker et al. (2013) reported that Anopheles gambiae populations peaked during rainy seasons, with certain lag between increased rainfall and increased mosquito abundance. In addition to rainfall, temperature is also a critical determinant of mosquito population dynamics as it governs mosquito development and biting rates and mosquitoes rely on moisture and warmth in the atmosphere (Mazarire et al., 2024).

Over 47 Anopheles mosquito species have been documented in Ethiopia (Irish et al., 2020). Of these, An. arabiensis is the main malaria vector, while An. pharoensis, An. funestus and An. nili play a role as secondary vectors (FMoH, 2018). Recently, an invasive malaria vector, An. stephensi, has also been detected in eastern, northeastern, central, northwestern and southwestern Ethiopia (Carter et al., 2018; Balkew et al., 2020; FMoH unpublished data) and is expanding its distribution to other parts of the country. Although information on insecticide susceptibility, infection rates and other bionomic features of An. gambiae (s.l.), from different parts of the country is available (Yewhalaw et al., 2010; Abate and Hadis, 2011; Yewhalaw et al., 2012; Fettene et al., 2013; Massebo et al., 2013; Alemayehu et al., 2017; Chanyalew et al., 2022) and most intervention efforts have primarily targeted this primary vector, malaria continues to be one of the serious diseases in Ethiopia. Secondary malaria vectors are considered to play minor roles in malaria transmission (Afrane et al., 2016). However, their potential in vectorial capacity and competence vary according to locality and season (Bhatt et al., 2015). For instance, recent unpublished data from circumsporozoite enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (CSP-ELISA) and an artificial infection experiment using a membrane-feeding assay showed that Ethiopian populations of An. coustani are susceptible to both Plasmodium falciparum and P. vivax (Tsegaye et al., 2022). However, there is a paucity of information on the bionomics, seasonal abundance, insecticide susceptibility status and vectorial capacity of secondary and suspected vectors in Ethiopia. Although Ethiopia aims to achieve nationwide malaria elimination by 2030 (FMoH, 2017), the complex and dynamic behavior of both principal and secondary malaria vectors, insecticide resistance and emergence of invasive vectors coupled with other anthropogenic factors may pose challenges to achieve the elimination goal by the country. Accordingly, information on vector composition, seasonal abundance, biting and resting behaviour, infection rates and susceptibility to insecticides is important for planning and selecting vector control interventions at a local level (Bhatt et al., 2015).

Gambella is one of the malarious areas of Ethiopia with high malarial endemicity (Chanyalew et al., 2022) and the Lare district of the region shares a border with South Sudan with high social cohesion between border communities. Such considerable international cross-border mobility may also contribute to the region having the highest prevalence of malaria in Ethiopia (Warkaw et al., 2022; Haileselassie et al., 2023). There is also significant seasonal variation in case numbers with the highest numbers reported to occur from July through September of each year partly caused by rainfall, temperature and humidity that result in increased malaria cases from June-October (Haileselassie et al., 2023). As changes in climatic factors such as temperature, rainfall and relative humidity may influence ecological relationships that are important to malaria vectors and subsequent disease transmission, investigation of vector bionomics, distribution, behaviour and insecticide susceptibility status at locality level can help optimize vector control strategies.

Therefore, this study aims to determine the species composition, resting and biting behavior, abundance and seasonal dynamics of Anopheles mosquitoes in Lare District. Moreover, the study aims to investigate insecticide susceptibility status of Anopheles mosquitoes in Jimma Zone and Lare District in southwest Ethiopia.

Materials and methods

Study locations

The study was conducted in the Jimma Zone of the Oromia Regional State and the Lare District of the Gambella Regional State in southwestern Ethiopia. Jimma has an agro-ecological setting of highland (15%), midland (67%) and lowland (18%). Jimma Zone receives high rainfall, ranging from 1200 to 2800 mm per annum. The mean annual temperature ranges between approximately 14 °C and 24 °C. The Gilgel Gibe Hydroelectric Dam I is located 260 km southwest of Addis Ababa and is also located in the Jimma Zone of Oromia Regional State. Jimma town, the capital of the zone, is located 354 km southwest of Addis Ababa, Oromia Regional State. Jimma lies to the west of the Great Rift Valley (7°40′N, 36°50′E) at an altitude between 1300 m and 2100 m and is characterized by a warm and humid climate. The Gambella Regional State is one of the 12 Regional States of Ethiopia. Lare District is situated in Nuer Zone, within the Gambella region of southwestern Ethiopia. It shares a border with Anuak Zone to the south and southeast. The Baro and Jikawo rivers separate Lare from Jikawo District to the east and northeast. South Sudan borders Lare District to the north and west. Lare is located at 8°33′N and 33°95′E with an elevation ranging from 410 to 430 m. The district is typically characterized by hot and humid weather conditions.

Since Lare District is an area with high malaria transmission, it was chosen for the study of both bionomics and insecticide bioassay while the Jimma Zone study sites were used only for sampling larvae and pupae for insecticide susceptibility bioassays (Fig. 1). Both study sites are known to be malaria-endemic, and Lare is a high malaria transmission area (Haileselassie et al., 2023), while the Jimma sites are relatively low to moderate malaria transmission areas (Jemal and Ketema, 2019).Fig. 1Map of the study areas in Lare and Jimma, southwest Ethiopia. Mosquito sampling

The survey was carried out for a total of 17 months between June 2018 and September 2020 in Lare District. Adult female Anopheles mosquito samples were collected using CDC light traps (BioQuip Model 2836BQ, USA) and human landing catches (HLC).

CDC light trap collection

Mosquito samples were collected from eight randomly selected houses from Lare. Each of the selected houses was visited for two consecutive nights every survey month for a total of eight different months (16 trap-nights/month) during the survey. The CDC light traps were suspended approximately 1.5 m above the floor. Traps were switched on at 18:00 h and switched off at 6:00 h. Traps were collected from each house every morning at 6:00 h and mosquitoes were killed using cotton wool treated with chloroform.

Human landing catches (HLC)

Mosquito collection was also conducted using human landing catches (HLC) from eight other randomly selected houses from Lare for nine months between November 2018 and October 2020. The HLC was performed both indoors and outdoors for two consecutive nights per house during each surveyed month. There were two teams of eight trained collectors who consented to the HLC operation. Four collectors performed the collection indoors, and four outdoors. HLC was performed between 18:00 h and 6:00 h with the period of collection divided into two 6-h segments. During these segments, two of the four trained volunteers collected indoors for 6 h and two collected outdoors. The other two collectors were allowed to sleep for 6 h. The team members conducted the collection every night and hence, HLC was operated for two nights per house every month of the study period for 32 person-nights (16 outdoor and 16 indoor) each month, making 288 person-nights during the nine months of the study period. The collectors put on long-sleeved shirts during the collections to prevent mosquitoes from landing and biting on their arms (Jemal and Ketema, 2019). The collectors were seated on stools with their legs exposed from foot to knee to capture mosquitoes using a flashlight and mouth aspirator as soon as they landed on exposed legs but before they commenced blood-feeding (Service, 1977; WHO, 2013). The collectors were given chemoprophylaxis (Mefloquine) as per the National Malaria Diagnosis and Treatment Guidelines (FMoH, 2012).

Sampling larvae and pupae

Potential breeding sites were systematically searched for the presence of mosquito larvae by going around and inspecting marshes, temporary pools (puddles), farming field ditches, sewerage, drains, gullies, rock pools, hoof-prints and stagnant water pools. Positive habitats were sampled with a 350 ml capacity mosquito scoop. Dipping was done using standard mosquito dipper and a 3-ml pipette following the World Health Organization guidelines and standard operating procedures for entomological surveillance (WHO, 2013). Sampling was conducted in the morning around 9:00–11:00 h and afternoon around 13:00–15:00 h during the wet season of the study period from Bacho Booree and Asendabo in Jimma and Lare for insecticide bioassays. Jimma was included for larvae and pupae sampling as sufficient numbers of larvae could not be obtained from Lare for the planned tests. Larvae were kept in a room at constant temperature (24.5 °C) and relative humidity (RH) (61% ± 5%) at Jimma and 28 ± 1 °C and 72% ± 5% RH at Lare, and fed powdered brewer’s yeast. Pupae were sorted and transferred to beakers and then placed inside a cage to emerge to adults in the respective study sites.

Mosquito identification

All the collected adult Anopheles mosquitoes using the CDC light traps and HLC were sorted by sex and morphologically identified to species using identification keys (Gillies and Coetzee, 1987). The mosquitoes were then counted and individually stored over silica gel in 1.5-ml Eppendorf tubes, labeled and transported to the Tropical and Infectious Diseases Research Center (TIDRC) Laboratory, Jimma University, and stored until further processing.

Insecticide bioassay

Non-blood-fed adult female mosquitoes (3–5 day-old) emerged from larvae and pupae were morphologically identified to species (Gillies and Coetzee, 1987) and exposed to insecticide-impregnated papers with discriminating concentrations of DDT (4%), deltamethrin (0.05%), malathion (5%), permethrin (0.75%), pirimiphos-methyl (0.25%), propoxur (0.1%), and bendiocarb (0.1%), following the WHO insecticide susceptibility test procedure (WHO, 2016). Insecticide-impregnated papers were obtained from the WHO Collaboration Centre, Vector Control Research Unit, School of Biological Sciences, Penang, Malaysia. Batches of 25 mosquitoes in four replicates were exposed to insecticide-impregnated papers for 1 h in WHO test tubes for all bioassays (WHO, 2016). Controls in two replicates, each with an equal number (n = 25) of mosquitoes, were exposed to untreated papers in parallel. After the exposure period, mosquitoes were transferred into holding tubes and soaked in 10% sucrose solution on cotton pads. Mortality was recorded 24 h post-exposure.

Detection of Plasmodium spp. infection

The heads and thoraces of 266 mosquitoes (144 An. coustani and 122 An. pharoensis) were dissected and analyzed with the TaqMan qPCR assay developed by Bass et al. (2008) to detect Plasmodium falciparum, P. vivax, P. ovale and P. malariae as per the protocol and primers. As studies of parasite infection rates were previously conducted on samples of An. funestus (Woyessa et al., 2023) and An. gambiae (s.l.) (Haileselassie et al., 2023) from a similar study site, this study focused only on samples of An. coustani and An. pharoensis for Plasmodium spp. detection.

Data analysis

Mosquito abundance and species of Anopheles mosquitoes from different collection methods and sites were expressed with descriptive statistics using percentages. The monthly average density of Anopheles mosquitoes using CDC light traps and HLC were expressed as the total number of female Anopheles mosquitoes collected per month divided by overall total monthly catches for each sampling method during the specific collection month. Mean Anopheles mosquito density (D) was calculated as D = (No. of females of each mosquito species/No. of houses)/No. of nights. Data cleaning and log (x+1)-transformations were performed to normalize the distribution of Anopheles mosquito count data prior to analysis. Differences in the mean density of Anopheles mosquitoes between outdoor and indoor catches using HLC were compared using a t-test. Mean monthly density differences among mosquito species were evaluated by analysis of variance (ANOVA). Correlation analysis was used to highlight the association between the overall seasonal Anopheles mosquito density and rainfall and temperature using one-way ANOVA. Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS statistics for Windows v.20.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA). In all tests, values were considered significant at P < 0.05.

Insecticide bioassay

Larvae and pupae emerged to adults belonged to three Anopheles species: An. gambiae (s.l.), An. pharoensis and An. coustani. The larvae and pupae of Anopheles gambiae (s.l.) and An. pharoensis were obtained from mosquito breeding sites in both Lare and Jimma while the larvae and pupae of An. coustani were recorded only from those collected in Jimma (Bacho Booree and Asendabo sites). None of the larvae and pupae that emerged was An. funestus. Hence, three species of Anopheles mosquitoes were tested against multiple WHO insecticides, and all were found to be susceptible to baseline level concentrations of two classes of three insecticides (Fig. 4). Accordingly, An. pharoensis, An. gambiae (s.l.) and An. coustani complex were susceptible to organophosphate (primiphos-methyl) and carbamate (propoxur and bendiocarb) as per the WHO-specified thresholds for resistance determination (Bass et al., 2008). However, An. pharoensis and An. gambiae (s.l.) mosquitoes were phenotypically resistant to three classes of insecticides (DDT, deltamethrin and permethrin and malathion) at the baseline level whilst An. coustani was phenotypically resistant to DDT and deltamethrin.Fig. 4Mortality of Anopheles mosquito species from Lare and Jimma sites, Ethiopia, in insecticide susceptibility bioassays. Discussion

The total number of Anopheles mosquitoes captured during the present 17-month survey was 11,048; these were identified to four spieces. Anopheles coustani was the most abundant and more than 73% of this species were collected using CDC light traps with the majority from outdoors. Similar to the present study, An. coustani was reported as the dominant Anopheles mosquito species from Itang, another district of Gambella region (Chanyalew et al., 2022) and the majority of those were sampled with CDC light traps and rested outdoors. However, in contrast to the present study, only three species of Anopheles (An. gambiae (s.l.), An. pharoensis, and An. coustani) were reported and An. funestus was not recorded from Itang possibly due to the fact that species composition of anophelines varies according to local eco-epidemiological setting. In another study conducted in Lare, Taye et al. (2017) reported four Anopheles species, An. gambiae (s.l.), An. pharoensis, An. nili and An. coustani, with An. gambiae (s.l.) being dominant, while An. funestus was not recorded in the study. The discrepancies in the different studies could be due to spatio-temporal variation in mosquito density, species composition and other factors such as resting and/or feeding behaviour.

The general increase in mean density of three Anopheles mosquito species namely, An. gambiae (s.l.), An. pharoensis and An. coustani, during June-November with variable degrees approximately coincides with the peak malaria season (September-December) in Ethiopia. This could be because these anophelines were favored by meteorological variables, such as rainfall, temperature and humidity that could impact the bionomics of malaria vectors which provided more breeding sites during June-November. However, we observed the appearance and subsequent increase in mean density of An. funestus through November-January when the population of the other three Anopheles species declined. This may possibly enable An. funestus to be responsible for residual malaria transmission in Lare as the application of IRS is usually conducted during earlier months mainly targeting the main malaria vectors in Ethiopia. The appearance of An. funestus late in the dry season agrees with previous report by Ferguson and Govella (2024) who reported that An. funestus may be the best adapted to dry conditions due to its longer persistence throughout the dry season. The seasonal variations in mosquito density observed in the present study indicate that changes in rainfall, humidity, temperature and other environmental factors could likely favour some vector species over others. Therefore, the National Malaria Control Programme (NMCP) needs to take into consideration the seasonal dynamics of both the primary and secondary malaria vectors at locality levels of malaria-prone areas to achieve the envisaged pre-elimination goal.

Although there were cases in which mosquito sampling was not conducted simultaneously in the same months regularly using CDC light traps and HLC during the 17 months of study, the number of anopheline mosquitoes collected using CDC light traps was about 2.73 times greater than those collected using HLC. The fact that An. pharoenis was most abundant among the mosquito species captured using HLC could be an indication that this vector plays a role in malaria transmission in the area. However, there was no Plasmodium spp. detected in this species, possibly because of small sub-samples used in the present study. HLC is the standard and reliable method to study vector bionomics, as this technique is focused on host-seeking mosquitoes, which may represent the most relevant proportion of the mosquito population for disease transmission (Okumu et al., 2008; Sriwichai et al., 2015) and hence enables the assessment of human exposure to Anopheles spp. biting (Namango et al., 2022). A higher proportion of Anopheles mosquitoes were collected outdoors compared to indoors using HLC in the present study. A study in 2020 on the ownership and use of long-lasting insecticidal nets in Ethiopia confirmed that the Gambella Regional State had the highest (87.9%) net utilization in the country (Nigatu et al., 2020). This could be attributed to the higher number of malaria vectors collected outdoors in the Lare District of the region. However, the present study did not confirm whether such local vector populations show such feeding and resting behaviour or whether it is in response to high coverage and prolonged utilization of IRS and ITNs in the area. Other studies (Russell et al., 2011; Kiuru et al., 2018; Zemene et al., 2021) supported replacement of predominant indoor transmission by greatly lowered residual transmission, while a greater proportion of residual transmission occurred outdoors as a result of LLINs and IRS that target endophilic, endophagic and anthropophilic vectors. The higher outdoor biting and/or resting behavior of the vectors in the study area could pose a challenge to the anticipated malaria elimination goal, as the use of ITNs and IRS are still the major vector control strategies currently used in the study areas and in Ethiopia in general. Hence, this calls for developing and implementing new or alternative innovative vector control tools that target outdoor feeding and resting malaria vectors.

We were not able to detect Plasmodium spp. using TaqMan qPCR from samples of An. coustani and An. pharoensis, possibly due to the smaller sample size used. However, the fact that none of the 266 (144 An. coustani and 122 An. pharoensis) samples were positive for infective stage parasite does not conclusively confirm the absence of parasite as the oocyst stage was not investigated in the present study, which may later develop to sporozoites from oocyst-infected mosquitoes. From previous studies, it was also observed that only as few as 1% of mosquitoes may be infected with infective stage parasites (sporozoite), indicating that very large mosquito samples should be collected and tested to accurately quantify mosquito infection rates (Maia et al., 2019). Therefore, locality and time-specific estimates of mosquito infection rates considering a sizable proportion of the vector population need to be regularly assessed to better understand the infection status and effective control of the disease.

In the present study, three species of Anopheles mosquitoes, An. gambiae (s.l.), An. pharoensis, and An. coustani, showed resistance and/or possible resistance to melathion, deltamethrin, permethrin and DDT, and all of them were susceptible to bendiocarb, pirimiphos-methyl and propoxur. Such resistance might be due to selection pressure as a result of increased ITN coverage (Kiuru et al., 2018). Another study from Tanzania has also confirmed susceptibility of An. arabiensis to carbamates (bendiocarb) and organophosphate (pirimiphos-methyl) but resistance to pyrethroids (permethrin and deltamethrin) and organochlorides (DDT) (Gimnig et al., 2013). Although not supported via molecular analysis, emergence of pyrethroid (deltamethrin) resistant An. coustani highlights that further surveillance is needed to understand its exact role in disease transmission and subsequent design of appropriate vector control programme (Bashar and Tuno, 2014). The development of insecticide resistance by malaria vectors and vector behavioural changes coupled with expansion of invasive vectors such as An. stephensi may challenge a national malaria elimination roadmap of the country which set the goal of eliminating malaria by 2030 (FMoH, 2017).

Although the correlation was non-significant, our study consistently confirmed that there was an overall increase in Anopheles mosquito abundance (except for the abundance of An. funestus) during rainy seasons in the Lare sites. Generally, raised mean monthly temperature was also obvious during the rainy seasons. The weak positive non-significant association between mosquito density and rainfall could obviously be due to the biology of lag time in addition to the destruction of mosquito larval habitats by floods and water currents created by heavy rainfall which reduced adult mosquito abundance during heavy rains. Weak non-significant positive associations between mosquito abundance and some climatic factors, including humidity (Bashar and Tuno, 2014), rainfall, and temperature (Syahrani et al., 2022) were also reported in other studies.

To the best of our knowledge, this is a relatively long-term mosquito entomological dataset from Lare site on bionomics and seasonal dynamics of Anopheles mosquitoes. The fact that we were unable to measure meteorological variables and depend on rainfall and temperature data from weather stations could be a limitation as direct association between mosquito seasonal dynamics and those variables may not necessarily reflect the actual situation during sampling. The other major limitation of this study is the inability to conduct the survey on a regular basis because of some logistic and other reasons such as heavy-flooding problems during some months in Lare. Although phenotypic insecticide resistance was observed in three Anopheles species, the resistance was not assessed via molecular methods to show the mechanistic basis of resistance. Insecticide resistance mechanisms and infection rates of An. arabiensis, a member species of An. gambiae (s.l.) were better investigated in different parts of Ethiopia (Yewhalaw et al., 2010; Abate and Hadis, 2011; Yewhalaw et al., 2012; Fettene et al., 2013; Massebo et al., 2013; Alemayehu et al., 2017; Chanyalew et al., 2022). There is also limited information on insecticide resistance mechanisms in An. pharoensis (Balkew et al., 2006) and An. funestus in the country (Woyessa et al., 2023). However, to the best of our knowledge, there is paucity of information on insecticide susceptibility levels of An. coustani in Ethiopia possibly due to low infection rates or because no infections were reported. Although no infections were detected in An. pharoensis and An. coustani in the present study, possibly because of small sample sizes, this does not rule out the possibility of infection via these vectors. For instance, recent unpublished data from a circumsporozoite enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (CSP-ELISA) and an artificial infection experiment using a membrane-feeding assay showed that Ethiopian populations of An. coustani are susceptible to both P. falciparum and P. vivax (Tsegaye et al., 2022).

Conclusions

A total of 11,048 anopheline mosquitoes belonging to four species namely, An. gambiae (s.l.), An. pharoensis, An. funestus, and An. coustani, were captured during the 17 months of survey from Lare. Temporal variation in the mean density of Anopheles mosquito species was observed during the survey. Anopheles gambiae (s.l.) appeared in relatively large numbers during June-November and An. phareonsis showed an approximately similar trend. Anopheles coustani also appeared in large numbers during the rainy season at the beginning of June before a relatively sustained stable population starting from August to the peak malaria season (September-November) in Ethiopia. On the other hand, An. funestus appeared in relatively large numbers through November-January when the population of the other three Anopheles species declined. This may indicate that this vector has a role in residual malaria transmission. A substantial number of An. pharoensis collected by HLC, indicates that this mosquito species may play role in malaria transmission although there was no Plasmodium spp. detection via TaqMan qPCR in the present study due to small mosquito sub-samples used. The majority of the Anophles mosquitoes showed exophagic behavior and resistance to multiple insecticides, which may jeopardize the commonly used IRS and ITN vector control strategies, necessitating regular monitoring of primary, secondary and suspected malaria vectors and the development of innovative vector control tools. Generally, there was an overall increase in the number of Anopheles specimens caught during the rainy season, compared to the dry season, with the most significant increase being a few weeks following rainfall. Additional information on mosquito fauna and abundance in relation to meteorological factors in a particular locality is essential for the development of efficient vector control interventions.
