Excess 40Ar retention in metamorphosed amphibole complicates 40Ar/39Ar geochronological interpretation of diabase
Authors: Wan-Feng Zhang, Jun-Jie Wang, Jian-Lin Chen, De-Wen Zheng, Lin Ma, Ming Xiao, Yu-Lian Zhang, Ying-De Jiang, Yi-Gang Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe 40Ar/39Ar dating method holds particular significance for determining the age of basic rocks. However, the presence of extraneous argon (40ArE) in some samples generates complex 40Ar/39Ar age spectra, compromising the technique’s utility in modern geochronology. This investigation focuses on deciphering the occurrence of 40ArE within a diabase and proposes strategies to mitigate its influence. Our results reveal distinct age patterns: separated feldspar yields a well-defined plateau age of 48.73 ± 0.07 (0.12) [2.06] Ma (2σ, MSWD = 0.86, P = 62%), while whole-rock analyses produce saddle-shaped age spectra. Through integration of EPMA major element data, K/Ca ratios from 40Ar/39Ar results, and TIMA-derived mineral proportions, we identify metamorphosed amphibole as the primary host of 40ArE in this study. Thus, separated components containing metamorphosed amphiboles should be excluded as suitable targets for 40Ar/39Ar geochronology. These findings provide critical guidance for sample selection and interpretation in basic rocks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-26406-1.Subject terms: Geochemistry, Geology

Introduction

The 40Ar/39Ar dating technique, a variation of the conventional K-Ar method, operates on the natural decay of 40K to 40Ar1. In principle, any rock or mineral containing the potassium element can be used for the 40Ar/39Ar dating2. The radiogenic argon measured in a sample accumulates from in situ 40K decay following the crystallization or recrystallization of the rock. Consequently, the fundamental assumption of 40Ar/39Ar dating requires samples to be initially devoid of radiogenic argon1. This assumption is violated by the presence of parentless extraneous argon (40ArE), yielding geologically implausible and anomalously old ages. This parentless extraneous argon component critically compromises the reliability of both K-Ar and 40Ar/39Ar geochronology. 40ArE can occur in some typical mineral phases, such as non-potassium phases (e.g., olivine1), low-potassium minerals (e.g., amphibole3), and even high-potassium minerals (e.g., biotite4). Although the 40Ar/39Ar dating method is particularly significant for determining the age of ultrabasic to basic rocks5, these challenges (including interpretive complexities in heterogeneous rock samples, ambiguous age spectra, and protracted experimental durations) collectively contribute to its declining utilization in contemporary geochronological studies. A prominent example of this phenomenon is observed in the kinematic framework of the Gangdese magmatic belt in southern Tibet. The Gangdese continental arc basaltic underplates and cumulates exhibit elevated K2O contents (mostly > 0.5 wt%), consistent with global continental arcs analogues6. Theoretically, such samples represent suitable candidates for 40Ar/39Ar dating. However, the formation time of the magmatic rocks in this belt is mainly established by U-Pb geochronology6–9, in contrast to 40Ar/39Ar geochronology. This phenomenon likely arises not only from the extended analytical duration of 40Ar/39Ar dating but also from whole-rock samples that probably yield ages inconsistent with established geological constraints. Therefore, characterizing extraneous argon components in whole-rock samples represents a critical advancement for enhancing the chronometric accuracy of 40Ar/39Ar dating in basic rock studies.

Although whole rock samples may contain 40ArE, 40Ar/39Ar dating remains an indispensable geochronological tool for basic rocks when minerals contributing 40ArEcan be effectively identified and excluded from analysis. In addition, U-Pb dating results from zircon are also controversial. U-Pb zircon ages from basic rocks commonly exhibit broad dispersion in basic rocks e.g10–12., and are generally not considered reliable for constraining crystallization ages13,14. On the other hand, the presence of zircon in basic rocks is controversial, as zircon is considered unlikely to crystallize from SiO2-undersaturated melts13,14. Zircons in basic rocks can originate from several sources13: (1) crystallization from primordial magma; (2) captured from surrounding rock during emplacement; (3) formation during late-stage fluid or metamorphic processes; and (4) incorporation of small vein material. Consequently, the origin of zircons in basic rocks is a complex and contentious issue, raising questions about whether the U-Pb dating of the selected zircons can accurately reflect the age of rock formation. Thus, results derived from a single dating system (such as U-Pb or 40Ar/39Ar dating method) may carry potential biases, necessitating cross-verification between different methodologies.

In this study, we employed the 40Ar/39Ar dating method on diabase from the Gyangze area of Tibet to investigate the formation age, which was previously determined by zircon U-Pb dating15. Although whole-rock sample yields a complex age spectrum due to 40ArE, feldspar consistently produces well-defined plateaus, demonstrating its robustness and establishing it as an ideal material for 40Ar/39Ar geochronology. This study also seeks to conduct comparisons between different geochronological systems. Our findings validate the selected zircon U-Pb results in conjunction with geological evidence, highlighting the utility and reliability of 40Ar/39Ar dating for these rock types.

Sample and analytical method

The Himalaya belt is the most active collisional orogen in the world. According to existing studies, including previous researches of the co-author of this study, the Himalaya represents the northern edge of the Indian craton, which is the result of the Indio-Eurasian continental collision around 60 ± 5 Ma16,17. Two critical episodes of Cenozoic metamorphism and magmatism have been identified from the Himalaya: (1) 48 - 35 Ma Barrovian-type prograde metamorphism with Eocene mafic and Na-rich adakitic melts18,19, and (2) late Oligocene - early Miocene retrograde metamorphism associated with leucogranite formation20,21. The late Oligocene - early Miocene retrograde metamorphism is mainly distributed around the South Tibetan Detachment System. The Miocene crustal anatexis accompanied by ultrahigh-temperature (900-970°C) metamorphism indicates a hotter crustal thermal state than the Eocene crustal anataxis events with moderate-temperature (∼600-750°C) metamorphism20,22. The Gyangze diabase outcrop (Fig. 1a) in this study are located in the Tethyan Himalaya where has not been disturbed by subsequent magmatic activities. Zircon grains from the Gyantse diabase sample JZ18-2-1 were dated using SIMS U-Pb to an age of 48.6 ± 0.5 Ma15. The diabase sample in this study was collected from the same outcrop as sample JZ18-2-1 (89°34’5.259’’, 28°57’25.316’’, Fig. 1a and b).

(a) Field photograph and (b) hand specimen image of the sample. (c) Illustration of mineral identification and mapping utilizing TIMA. (d) Photograph of the separated grains under reflected light. (e) Example of a backscattered electron (BSE) image of the separated grain.

A TESCAN Integrated Mineral Analyzer (TIMA) was employed to automatically identify and quantify minerals, revealing primary minerals assemblages of albite, amphibole, pyroxene, epidote, mica, and other minor minerals (Fig. 1c). The sample was crushed to 180–250 μm, with non-magnetic components selected for further preparation. White to transparent grains were hand-picked under a binocular microscope (Fig. 1d). Backscattered electron (BSE) images (Fig. 1e) and major element compositions were analyzed using a Cameca SXFive FE electron probe microanalyzer (EPMA) at the State Key Laboratory of Deep Earth Processes and Resources, Guangzhou Institute of Geochemistry, Chinese Academy of Sciences, following the analytical procedures outlined by He et al.23.

Whole rock and feldspar samples were selected for 40Ar/39Ar dating analysis. Grains underwent further processing, including sieving and cleaning via acid leaching with 1 N HNO3, followed by rinsing with ultraclean water and ethanol for 10 min in an ultrasonic bath and dried in an oven (50 ℃). The samples were irradiated in the High Flux Engineering Test Reactor for 14.5 h, using the FCs-EK to monitor the J value. Both samples and standards were analyzed using an Argus VI mass spectrometer. The sample was subjected to stepwise heating by continuously firing a 50 W custom-made CO2 laser (IR, 10.6 μm) for 100 s. The released gases were initially cleaned using a crytrap (−70 °C) to absorb the moisture, followed by further purification with two custom-made Zr/Al GETTERs at 400 °C and room temperature. During the stepwise heating process, blanks and air standards were analyzed every four and eight sample steps, respectively. The decay constants and age of FCs-EK (28.294 ± 0.036 Ma) used were those recommended by Renne et al.24. The mass discrimination factor was calculated according to the atmospheric argon ratio of 298.56 ± 0.3125. The total decay constant for 40K of (5.531 ± 0.013) × 10− 10 a− 1 24. The interference correction values of the reactor are (36Ar/37Ar)Ca = (2.424 ± 0.039) × 10− 4, (39Ar/37Ar)Ca = (7.015 ± 0.067) × 10− 4, and (40Ar/39Ar)K = (9.23 ± 0.73) × 10− 3. The 40Ar/39Ar age of the sample was calculated using the ArArCALC 2.52 software developed by Koppers26.

Results

Age

Uncertainties for the calculated weighted mean ages are reported at the 95% confidence level in the ± X (Y) [Z] format, where X represents the analytical uncertainty, Y encompasses the combined analytical and tracer (U-Pb) or J value uncertainty, and Z includes the uncertainties of analytical, J value, and 238U or 40K decay constant.

Feldspar

The age spectra of the feldspar 40Ar/39Ar dating results are shown in Fig. 2, with detailed analytical results provided in the Supplemental Material Table S1. Eighteen consecutive steps were analyzed, yielding a narrow 40Ar/39Ar age range from 48.53 to 49.05 Ma and defining plateau age of 48.73 ± 0.07(0.12)[2.06] Ma (MSWD = 0.86, P = 62%, Fig. 2a). This plateau age aligns with an inverse isochron age of 48.63 ± 0.30 (0.32) [2.07] Ma (MSWD = 0.90, P = 57%). The K/Ca ratio ranges from 1.055 to 0.309, with an average value of 0.467 ± 0.080 for the steps included in the age plateau calculations (Fig. 2a).

Feldspar laser stepwise heating 40Ar/39Ar dating (a) age spectrum and (b) inverse isochrones result.

The stepwise heating experiment produced a well-defined plateau, indicating homogeneous 40Ar* and potassium within the feldspar grains at the multi-grain level. The initial 40Ar/36Ar ratio of the sample from inverse isochron is 303.9 ± 14.2 (Fig. 2b), which is statistically indistinguishable from the atmospheric value ((40Ar/36Ar)air = 298.56 ± 0.3125), suggesting no detectable excess argon. Thus, the plateau age is preferred, representing the formation age of this diabase.

Whole rock

Stepwise heating experiments on the whole rock sample yield a complex age spectrum (Fig. 3; detailed analytical results are available in the Supplemental Material Table S1). Twenty-nine steps were analyzed, yielding a saddle-shaped age spectrum, which likely indicates some excess 40Ar within the whole rock. The first four steps yield relatively old apparent ages (from ~ 103 to ~ 53 Ma). Step 5 to 18 yield a narrow 40Ar/39Ar age ranges from 50.06 ± 0.68 to 51.51 ± 0.67 Ma, defining plateau ages of 50.87 ± 0.20 (0.22) [2.15] Ma (MSWD = 1.11, P = 35%, Fig. 3a). The K/Ca ratio ranges from 0.797 to 0.227, with an average value of 0.344 ± 0.059 for the steps included in the age plateau calculations (Fig. 3a). The initial 40Ar/36Ar ratio and inverse isochron age from inverse isochron (steps 5–18) is 296.3 ± 9.6 (Fig. 3a) and 51.05 ± 0.82(0.82)[2.30] Ma, respectively. Schaen et al.27 recommend that a plateau consist of at least five or more consecutive steps comprising more than 50% of the39Ar released. Although these steps account for 46.98% of the39Ar released, slightly below the recommended threshold, we still consider this a plateau age.

Whole rock laser stepwise heating 40Ar/39Ar dating (a) age spectrum and (b) inverse isochrones result.

The final 11 steps (steps 19–29) exhibit gradually increasing apparent ages from 52.10 to 55.39 Ma, with a gradual decrease in the K/Ca ratio (ranging from 0.092 to 0.175). The pseudo-plateau age is 53.36 ± 0.61 Ma (Fig. 3a). As the data cluster near the radiogenic x-axis, the linear isochron regression produced an initial 40Ar/36Ar ratio with relatively large uncertainty (232.2 ± 133.9, Fig. 3b).

Discussion

Scanning electron microscopy analysis shows that the separated feldspar grains are not pure albite (Fig. 1e). EPMA results reveal these grains mainly consist of albite, epidote, and muscovite (Fig. 1e). The epidote exhibits worm-like intersections (Fig. 1e). Its Pistacite ratio (Ps%, Fe/(Fe + Al) × 100) ranges from 27.8 to 37.5% with an average value of 31.77 ± 3.07 (1sd). The TiO2 content in epidote is below 0.2%. These values align with magmatic epidote composition (Ps%: 19–33%; TiO2 content: typically < 0.2 wt%28). Both the compositional characteristics and textural features of the epidote in our studied sample confirm its magmatic origin, indicating crystallization during magma emplacement. Consequently, this epidote can be used to trace the time of sample formation. The 40Ar/39Ar dating of these feldspar grains yields a well-defined plateau age of 48.73 ± 0.07 (0.12) [2.06] Ma (2σ, MSWD = 0.86, P = 62%, Fig. 2a), which is consistent with the zircon U-Pb age (48.6 ± 0.5 Ma, 1σ) within analytical uncertainty15.

In contrast, the whole rock sample exhibits a complex 40Ar/39Ar age spectrum (Fig. 3), displaying a significant discrepancy compared to both feldspar 40Ar/39Ar age (48.73 ± 2.06 Ma, Fig. 2) and the U-Pb age (48.6 ± 0.05 Ma8). The spectrum is characterized by high apparent ages (103.24 ± 8.77 Ma) in the first analytical step, decreases to a minimum apparent age (50.06 ± 0.68 Ma), then rises to elevated apparent ages (55.39 ± 1.04 Ma) in the final gas release step (Supplemental Material Table S1). This pattern forms a distinct saddle-shaped (Fig. 3a), widely interpreted as evidence of excess 40Ar contamination1,3,29,30. The minimum age in the saddle-shaped may approach, but does not reach, the true crystallization age of the mineral. As observed in previous studies1,3,31,32, such saddle-shaped results typically fail to yield geologically meaningful ages. Therefore, the successful application of the 40Ar/39Ar dating method to basic rock requires precise characterization of excess argon occurrence.

The whole rock contains a more complex mineral assemblage (Fig. 1c), including albite, epidote, muscovite, amphibole, clinopyroxene, and chlorite. The separated feldspar contains albite, epidote, and muscovite (Fig. 1e). Its flat plateau age (Fig. 2a) significantly differs from the saddle-shaped age spectrum (Fig. 3a). This rules out these minerals (albite, epidote, and muscovite) as sources of excess argon. Instead, chlorite, clinopyroxene, and amphibole can host excess argon, which can affect the 40Ar/39Ar dating results.

Altered minerals (i.e., chlorite) exhibit extremely fine grain sizes (Fig. 1c), rendering them susceptible to 39ArK loss via nuclear recoil during sample irradiation. This process leads to anomalously older apparent ages. Additionally, chlorite degasses at relatively low temperature stages33. Therefore, the extremely old ages in the initial four steps of the whole rock sample (Fig. 3a) result from chlorite degassing. When the laser power reaches 0.7 W (~ 810 ℃), chlorite is completely decomposed33. Consequently, chlorite can be ruled out as a source of influence on high temperature stages.

Clinopyroxene, a significant whole-rock component, has low potassium content (below 0.01 wt% %) and K/Ca ratio (0.001) (Table 1). Although clinopyroxene constitutes 14.73% of the whole rock, its low potassium content (< 0.01%) relative to albite is insufficient to account for over 50% of the39Ar release (Fig. 3a). Additionally, EPMA data show that the clinopyroxene contains 20.42 ± 1.00% CaO. The K/Ca ratio of albite ranges from 0.14 to 0.91, averaging 0.36, consistent with the K/Ca ratio of the separated feldspar grains and partial steps (steps 5–18) of whole rock. The K/Ca ratio declines with increasing temperature through in steps 19–29, reaching a value of ~ 0.1 in the high-temperature stage (Fig. 3a), significantly greater than the ratio for clinopyroxene (~ 0.0004, Table 1). In addition, TIMA analysis results confirm that all pyroxenes present in the studied whole rock sample are clinopyroxene (Fig. 1c). Ware and Jourdan34 found that clinopyroxene in whole rock analyses mainly degasses during high temperature heating steps. Thus, if excess argon were hosted in clinopyroxene, it would mainly affect high-temperature degassing stages. However, the whole-rock age spectrum (Fig. 3a) indicates that excess argon contamination affects age determinations across low to high temperature degassing steps. Therefore, the possibility of excess argon in clinopyroxene can be excluded.

Amphibole, comprising 15.39 vol% of the whole rock, has potassium content levels similar to albite (Table 1). Therefore, throughout the entire gas release process, it has a certain contribution to the 40Ar/39Ar stepwise heating results. The K/Ca ratio of the amphibole (~ 0.04, Table 1) aligns with the high temperature stages ratio (< 0.1, Fig. 3a), indicating amphibole degassing as the likely cause of the anomalously old whole rock 40Ar/39Ar age.

A relatively consistent age stage in the saddle-shaped age spectrum results from mixing degassing of feldspar and excess argon bearing in other minerals. If the excess argon is primarily hosted in amphibole, the gas released during whole rock stepwise heating would represent a proportional mixture of degassing contributions from both feldspar and amphibole. Therefore, feldspar and amphibole can be treated as distinct end-members. The separated feldspar grains provide an age of 48.73 ± 0.07 Ma (Fig. 2a), and assuming the last stage result (55.39 ± 1.04 Ma, Fig. 3a) represents the age of amphibole. The linear mixing model with 27.8–38.1% amphibole yields an age of 50.87 Ma. The main minerals containing39Ar in the whole rock samples are: albite, mica, and amphibole. The proportion of amphibole among these39Ar-bearing minerals is 29.2%, consistent with the two end-member linear mixing model. This agreement further confirms that amphibole contains excess argon, which consequently affects the whole rock results. Previous studies have found that metamorphic amphibole is prone to excess 40Ar contamination, producing complex release spectra and geologically inexplicable ages3,35–37. The amphibole in this study exhibits a distinctive poikilitic texture from a petrographic perspective (Fig. 1c), indicating potential interaction with fluids. Consequently, eliminating metamorphic amphibole during sample preparation would mitigate the issue of excess argon.

Application

(1) The systematic discrepancy between 40Ar/39Ar dating and U-Pb dating methods can significantly impact method selection by the application team, particularly when results need to be compared with previous research. The EarthTime workshops have addressed this analytical challenge through comprehensive studies of the FCs standard38, establishing rigorous protocols for inter-laboratory calibration of both 40Ar/39Ar and U-Pb methods. While the recommended FCs age varies across different laboratories, it can be recalibrated to a standardized result for comparison. For sample 23XZCD01, the plateau age calculated using the FCs age and decay constant from Renne et al.24 is 48.73 ± 0.07 (0.12) [2.06] Ma. Using the 40K decay constant (λ = (5.463 ± 0.214) × 10− 10 A− 1) and the FCs age (28.201 ± 0.046 Ma) from Kuiper et al.38, we obtain a recalculated plateau age of 48.57 ± 0.07 (0.12) [2.06] Ma for sample 23XZCD01. When recalculated using the FCs age (28.172 ± 0.028 Ma) from Rivera et al.39, the sample yields a plateau age of 48.52 ± 0.07 (0.12) [2.06] Ma. These results fall within the analytical uncertainty of the zircon U-Pb dating15, indicating negligible deviation between methods. Thus, the issue of systematic inconsistencies between 40Ar/39Ar and U-Pb dating methods can be effectively mitigated in method selection.

(2) This sample was collected, irradiated, and analyzed in July 2023, January 2024, and July 2024, respectively. The six-month period from irradiation to analysis aligns with the timeframe for typical SIMS U-Pb dating. Advances in the 40Ar/39Ar dating method enable high-precision results using micro-samples, such as the 60 mg sample used in this study. This approach effectively reduces external interference associated with processing larger volumes. The absence of any artificial screening ensures that the results are not biased by subjective interpretation. The 40Ar/39Ar dating method is particularly significant for determining the age of basic rocks5, which warrants renewed attention.

(3) Our results demonstrate that metamorphic products may contain excess argon, compromising 40Ar/39Ar age accuracy. However, when completely removed during sample pretreatment, the 40Ar/39Ar age result is accurate and reliably represents the formation age of basic rock. We therefore recommend the complete removal of these metamorphic products for basic rocks, enabling the 40Ar/39Ar method to provide reliable formation ages. Due to the potentially fine particle size of metamorphic products, we recommend crushing samples to 150–180 μm to facilitate separation.

(4) Although zircons are theoretically unexpected in basic rocks, local enrichment within a magma chamber can facilitate their formation. Furthermore previously reported zircon U-Pb ages15 are consistent with the ⁴⁰Ar/³⁹Ar dating results of feldspar from this study, further demonstrating that zircon crystallization can occur during the evolution of basic rock. These findings validate the reliability of zircon U-Pb ages when interpretations are constrained by geological evidence. Consequently, the 40Ar/39Ar and zircon U-Pb methods emerge as superior choices for dating basic rocks.
