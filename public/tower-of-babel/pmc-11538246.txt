Pulmonary diffusing capacity among individuals recovering from mild to moderate COVID-19: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Dana Yelin, Nassem Ghantous, Muhammad Awwad, Vered Daitch, Talya Kalfon, Michal Mor, Shira Buchrits, Yair Shafir, Irit Shapira-Lichter, Leonard Leibovici, Dafna Yahav, Ili Margalit, Dorit Shitenberg
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Impaired pulmonary diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO) following COVID-19 has been consistently reported among individuals recovering from severe-critical infection. However, most long COVID cases follow non-severe COVID-19. We assessed DLCO among individuals with long COVID recovering from mild to moderate acute illness. A cross-sectional study of adults with long COVID, assessed at a COVID recovery clinic > 3 months following the onset of acute infection, during 2020–2021. Participants subjectively ranked their dyspnea severity based on its impact on their daily living and underwent comprehensive pulmonary function testing (PFT). Clinical correlates for impaired DLCO (defined as < 80%) were assessed using multivariable logistic regression models. A total of 458 individuals, their mean age 45 (SD 16) and 246 (54%) of whom are women, were evaluated at an average of ~ 4 months following acute COVID-19. The most frequent PFT impairment was reduced DLCO, identified among 67 (17%) of the cohort. Clinical correlates of impaired DLCO included women (odds ration [OR] 3.64, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.78–7.45, p < 0.001), cigarette smoking (OR 2.25, 95% CI 1.14–4.43, p = 0.019), and moderate-severe dyspnea (OR 2.77, 95% CI 1.39–5.50, p = 0.004). BMI inversely correlated with DLCO (OR 0.90, 95% CI 0.85–0.96 per 1 unit, p = 0.002). Impaired DLCO was not uncommon among individuals recovering from mild to moderate COVID-19. Women are at a greater risk, and subjective dyspnea correlated with impaired DLCO. Clinicians can rely on self-reported significant dyspnea to guide further assessment.

Introduction

Dyspnea is the second most common long COVID symptom, reported to affect 18–34% of recoverees1. Usually, patient assessment relies on subjective report of typical symptoms, while decreased diffusing capacity of carbon monoxide is among the few objective findings2. Early during the pandemic, long-term impaired diffusing capacity was associated with moderate to severe acute Coronavirus disease-2019 (COVID-19)3–5. Since COVID severity correlates with viral involvement of the lower respiratory tract (i.e., viral pneumonia), alveolar damage and/or endothelial dysfunction was hypothesized as the likely pathomechanism6. Yet, long COVID dyspnea is prevalent also among individuals recovering from mild infection7.

Since most COVID-19 infections are mild, long COVID dyspnea may impact the lives of a large population and burden healthcare systems and providers.

However, the population of individuals with long COVID dyspnea recovering from mild acute infection is underrepresented in most studies assessing pulmonary function of COVID-19 recoverees.

The appropriate medical assessment of individuals reporting long COVID symptoms has yet to be elucidated. Pulmonary function testing (PFT) is frequently performed in these patients; nonetheless its clinical impact has not been defined. Ascertaining the potential association between long COVID dyspnea and impaired diffusing capacity may imply on the appropriate rehabilitative interventions. Additionally, as PFT is time and resource consuming, it is necessary to ascertain the patients that may benefit from its completion.

In this study we performed pulmonary function testing on individuals recovering from mild to moderate COVID-19, who attended a COVID recovery clinic.

Methods

Study design and participants

A cross-sectional study was conducted at Rabin Medical Center, Beilinson hospital on a cohort of COVID-19 recoverees attending the COVID recovery clinic, between May 2020 and July 2021 (representing the pandemic up to the SARS-CoV-2 Delta variant era). We included consecutive adults (≥ 18 years), who visited the clinic following a mild-moderate PCR-confirmed COVID-19, according to the WHO severity criteria8. The clinic’s staff included a multidisciplinary team of an internal medicine/infectious diseases physician, a pulmonologist, and a social worker. During their visit, patients underwent complete PFT, regardless of their symptoms (See ‘Procedures’ below). Patients presented to the clinic less than 3 months following COVID-19 diagnosis, those with severe acute illness and those who did not complete PFT were excluded.

Procedures and definitions

The acute COVID-19 phase was defined as the time between first positive PCR test and acute symptoms resolution. Data regarding this phase were collected retrospectively from patients’ charts. Additional data included demographics; anthropometric measurements (height, weight, BMI), baseline comorbidities (including smoking status); clinical characteristics of acute COVID-19 (disease severity, laboratory measures, and chest imaging). During the visit, patients were assessed by a multidisciplinary team for long COVID symptoms (respiratory and others) according to a pre-planned questionnaire. If present, symptoms were graded subjectively according to their impact on daily activities, as follows: low (1); moderate (2); and severe (3).

Patients referred to the clinic with dyspnea were requested to complete a chest radiogram prior to their arrival at the clinic. Due to ethical considerations, chest CT was not part of the study protocol.

PFT were conducted according to American Thoracic Society guidelines9 and included spirometry, plethysmography, and diffusing capacity (ZAN 300 nSpire, Germany). PFT measurements were expressed as percentage of predicted normal values according to gender, age, and height. Pulmonary diffusing capacity (DLCO) was calculated according to the European Community of Coal and Steel (ECCS) equation. Abnormal diffusion was defined as DLCO < 80% of predicted value9.

Statistical analysis

Categorical and continuous variables were compared using the Chi-square (or Fisher’s exact test) and Man Whitney U test, respectively. Univariate analyses were performed to compare baseline characteristics according to DLCO (normal vs. impaired). Risk factors for impaired DLCO were assessed using a multivariable logistic regression model. Variables were introduced to the model based on univariate analysis and on clinical judgement. Candidate variables were tested for co-linearity and were assessed in separate models whenever Spearman’s correlation coefficient was > 0.6. The final model was selected based on the best fit according to Aikaike Information Constant.

For all analyses, p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using the IBM SPSS version 28 (Armonk, New York, USA).

Ethical aspects

The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee at Rabin Medical Center. All patients signed informed consent forms and all methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Results

During the study period, 458 individuals recovering from non-severe COVID-19 were evaluated for long COVID at an average interval of 144 (SD 53) days from acute illness. Their mean age was 45 (SD 16) years and 246 (54%) were women. Sixty-four (14%) individuals declined or were unable to complete PFT and were excluded. The remaining 394 individuals were included in the analysis. Of these, 67 (17%) had impaired DLCO. Ongoing respiratory symptoms were more prevalent among individuals with impaired DLCO compared to their counterparts with normal diffusing capacity (39% vs. 19%, p = 0.006). (Fig. 1)

Discussion

In this report of 458 individuals with long COVID recovering from mild to moderate illness, the proportion of impaired DLCO was 17%. Correlates of diminished diffusing capacity included women, smoking, and report of dyspnea, while BMI was inversely associated.

Previous studies evaluating PFT among COVID-19 recoverees have assessed mostly patients recovering from severe to critical COVID-19, requiring hospitalization10–13. Patients recovering from asymptomatic to moderate disease were not systematically assessed for PFT, because parenchymal damage was discounted in patients whose acute illness did not significantly impact the lower respiratory tract. Here we focused on this thus far neglected population, and unexpectedly found a non-negligible proportion of DLCO impairment among these patients. This finding suggests that even in patients presenting with mild acute illness, there is sub-clinical pulmonary involvement that may cause lasting parenchymal damage. The pathophysiology of this observation is yet uncertain, though several hypotheses were suggested, such as chronic viral persistence14, endothelial dysfunction and microthrombi15, and direct alveolar injury6.

Other risk factors identified in our study were gender and smoking status. The association between women and decreased DLCO following acute COVID-19 has been repeatedly reported16,17. It was hypothesized that inter-gender differences in long COVID may be explained by hormonal effects on the regulation of inflammatory consequences of COVID-1918. However, thus far there is no convincing evidence for this extensively reported finding. The association between cigarette smoking and diffusion impairment is in line with prior evidence implying that long term parenchymal insult consequences in a diminished diffusion capacity19.

The inverse association of BMI and DLCO is in line with previous findings, unrelated to COVID-1920. Within the normal or slightly elevated BMI range, DLCO is usually not affected by obesity. However, DLCO may be increased in individuals with extreme morbid obesity21. A non-negligible proportion of the study population had obesity. Accordingly, it is possible that the alleged protective role of BMI is merely related to a higher baseline DLCO among these individuals.

Our study is somewhat limited by its cross-sectional design. Consequently, we cannot attest to the natural history of the observed DLCO impairment. The lack of baseline pulmonary function measurements hinders our ability to infer causality between COVID-19 and the observed diminished pulmonary diffusing capacity. However, this limitation was mitigated by using validated prediction equations, adjusted for gender, height, and age.

Additionally, considering that this was a single-center study and that individuals were referred based on personal volition, the external validity might not be optimal.

In the absence of validated assessment tools for long COVID dyspnea, classifying its severity is challenging. While most studies have been using a binary (yes/no) classification, we used a Likert scale of three severity degrees, according to the impact on daily living. This tool has not been validated. Yet, the several dyspnea questionnaires that have been validated for specific illnesses (COPD, asthma, fibrosis) throughout the years have also been using subjective ranking.

Patients reporting shortness of breath may be referring to fatigue or effort intolerance, diluting the realistic proportion of individuals with actual dyspnea. To overcome this, we classified only those whose breathlessness had a moderate-severe impact on daily living as having dyspnea. Accordingly, we suggest this classification rule for clinical decision making with respect to PFT screening for individuals with long COVID.

In conclusion, impaired DLCO is not uncommon among long COVID patients recovering from mild acute infection. Although vital signs and physical examination are usually unremarkable, self-report of dyspnea significantly correlates with decreased DLCO.

These findings imply that clinicians may rely on respiratory symptoms reported subjectively by their patients as a sufficient decision-making tool when considering the need for pulmonary function testing among individuals with long COVID following mild disease.
