Survey of equity, diversity, and inclusion in Canadian anesthesiology residency programs
Authors: Yasmin Valji, Aly Valji, Darren Lam
Published: 2025

Background

Embracing diversity is an important issue in current society. Over the last decade, movements across various sectors in society have brought increasing attention to equity, diversity, and inclusion (EDI) related concerns. Research in the business workplace has demonstrated that having a diverse workforce enhances creativity, innovation and productivity [1]. Additionally, leveraging diversity in the workplace can lead to positive organizational change and can enhance both individual and organizational performance [2]. The benefits of EDI have also been demonstrated in the healthcare field by leading to a higher quality of patient care, reducing health disparities, and enhancing team performance [3].

Women and individuals from minoritized groups continue to face underrepresentation in the medical workforce, and this becomes more apparent when looking at academic medicine and leadership roles [4]. Much research has been conducted in surgical subspecialities in Canada, which has demonstrated a disproportionately low representation of women and minoritized groups at both the resident and attending physician level [5–8]. Within anesthesiology, underrepresentation of women and minoritized groups has also been demonstrated in attending physicians and trainees in the United States [9, 10]. However, to our knowledge, no such data has been collected in Canada. To address this, increased diversity is needed within residency training programs to to better address the widening healthcare inequities affecting marginalized populations [11].

Recent research by Lopez et al. [12] demonstrated that the majority of Canadian anesthesiology department leaders felt EDI was important, however most Canadian hospital departments of anesthesiology do not have an EDI committee, and less than one-third of departments provide financial compensation for EDI initiatives. Currently, there are no studies describing either the demographics of Canadian anesthesiology residents or EDI initiatives within their training programs. This study aims to describe these to guide future EDI initiatives.

Methods

To gather data from anesthesiology residents across Canada, we designed a brief anonymous online survey. Ethics approval was obtained from the University of Alberta Research Ethics Board in August 2023. The survey was distributed to program coordinators at the seventeen Canadian anesthesiology residency programs for subsequent dissemination to their resident physicians. Only anesthesiology resident physicians actively enrolled in the post-graduate training at the time of survey dissemination were eligible. Of note, Canadian anesthesiology post-graduate training is five years in duration. An explanation of the purpose, risks, benefits, confidentiality, and anonymity of the study was provided to participants. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to participation. The survey was administered via the online platform Qualtrics and consisted of 17 optional, multiple-choice questions. The “prevent multiple submissions” feature of Qualtrics was employed to limit respondents to a single submission. Question topics were constructed broadly to address four main categories. Firstly, data related to post-graduate training year and residency program were collected. Next, self-identification questions related to ethnicity, gender identity, and sexual identity of current trainees were posed. Additionally, trainees were asked about their perceptions of diversity within their residency cohorts, of staff at their institutions, and broadly within anesthesiology as a speciality. Finally, knowledge of existing initiatives in residency programs to foster EDI was gathered. We define EDI initiatives as any strategies, actions, programming, or training, designed to foster diversity, equity and inclusion in the training program, focusing on gender, religious, ethnic, sexual, age, and disability diversity. Descriptive analyses of the resulting data were subsequently performed using Microsoft Excel®, to investigate trends and patterns. To be included in our analysis, a minimum of one answered question was required. To help prevent non-response error, multiple reminders were sent out, with clear rationale of the survey, its objectives, and its anonymous design. The Consensus Based Checklist for Reporting of Survey Studies (CROSS) was followed for reporting results.

Results

In total, 127 responses from 15 of 17 Canadian anesthesiology residency programs were received. Four responses did not meet inclusion criteria, leaving 123 responses for analysis. Responses were obtained from trainees across all five years of residency. Although training is five years in duration, some responses were classified as “other” for residents who have been enrolled for more than five years due to various other reasons. Descriptive statistics were generated from the response data. Of the respondents, 49% identified their gender as male, 48% as female, 2% preferred not to say, and 1% as non-binary or transgender (Fig. 2). Respondents self-identified their sexual identity as heterosexual (84%), bisexual (8%), lesbian or gay (7%), other (1%), and 1% preferred not to say (Fig. 3). Regarding ethnicity, 67% of respondents identified as White or European, 13% as East Asian, 8% as South Asian, 6% as Arab, 4% as Black, 1% as Southeast Asian, 1% as Indigenous, 0% as West Asian or Hispanic, and 2% as other (Fig. 4). Most respondents felt that their programs contained trainees of diverse background (81%) with 15% reporting they felt their programs did not contain trainees of diverse backgrounds, and 4% were not sure. Similarly, 73% of respondents felt staff anesthesiologists at their programs were of diverse backgrounds, 17% did not, and 10% were not sure.

Lastly, questions that focused on awareness of existing EDI initiatives revealed 52% of respondents reported no awareness of existing initiatives in their programs focusing on EDI. 22% of respondents selected they were “not sure” about any existing initiatives at their institution. The remaining 26% reported awareness of existing EDI initiatives at their residency institutions. Additionally, 43% of residents reported receiving no training in anti-racism, sexual harassment, gender inclusivity, unconscious bias, or microaggressions, as shown in Fig. 5.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is the first to describe the demographic data of Canadian anesthesiology residency trainees and the EDI initiatives found within their training programs. Therefore, this study aims to fill a critical gap in national data and support ongoing discussions on the vital importance of representation and institutional inclusivity. All demographic data obtained in the study was self-identified by respondents, which we feel, especially for sexual identify, was integral to reducing bias.

The overall gender representation seen in our data closely mirrors that of the 2021 Canadian census demographic data [13]. It is important to note that although male and female gender representation appears to be equal at the trainee level, the landscape vastly differs at the attending physician level. As per the Canadian Medical Association 2019 report, 33% of Canadian anesthesiologists identified as female and 67% identified as male [14]. Of note, the unequal representation at the attending level may be attributed to factors such as attrition, which can stem from many causes including familial obligations, burnout, and lack of professional fulfillment [15]. From our results, it appears that at this point in time anesthesiology as a speciality is attracting representative proportions of all genders. Furthermore, as of the early 2020 s, graduating medical school classes in Canada were 54% female [15]. Perhaps this increased number of female medical trainees compared to male, is now translating to gender parity at the resident level. Although equal representation at the trainee level is a step in the right direction, it is important to continue to track this data to see how it translates over time at the attending physician level for anesthesiologists in Canada.

In terms of ethnicity data, comparing our survey results to the 2021 Canadian census showed a relatively equal representation of White or European anesthesiology residency trainees when compared to the general population (67% vs. 70%) [13]. Our results also showed that proportions of Black, Arab, East Asian, and South Asian anesthesiology trainees were similar or more compared to the 2021 census data; however, Indigenous, Hispanic, Southeast Asian, and West Asian individuals were underrepresented [13]. Unfortunately, there is no publicly available data that characterises the ethnic demographics of Canadian attending anesthesiologists, and thus we are unable to compare the demographic data of trainees to the attending population. To our knowledge, the demographic features of anesthesiology residents or attendings are not tracked publicly by the residency accreditation body or the board certification body in Canada. In contrast, this information is tracked in other countries, including the United States, where it is publicly available. We recommend that a potential solution for understanding the state of EDI in Canadian anesthesiology residency programs as well was the anesthesiology attendings would be for governing bodies to track and publicise this information.

To characterize LGBTQ identification data, we compared our results to the Canadian 2018 Survey of Safety in Public and Private Spaces (SSPPS), where 4% of the Canadian population identified as lesbian, gay, bisexual or any other sexual identity other than heterosexual [16]. In comparison, our survey response revealed a total of 15% of respondents identifying as lesbian, gay or bisexual, making this minoritized group well represented in Canadian anesthesiology residency programs. However, it is important to note that the LGBTQ population in Canada is skewed towards younger populations. According to the SSPPS, 28.7% of the LGBTQ population in Canada is between the ages of 25–34, while 16.5% of the non-LGBTQ Canadian population is between these ages [16]. Assuming the majority of resident physicians are in this age range, our result of 15% is likely closer to the actual demographics of the resident physician age group.

Looking forward, most anesthesiology residents felt EDI is important in residency training, however, the majority (52%) were not aware of any existing initiatives within their program. As such, an important area of future growth includes further encouraging EDI initiatives to ensure that healthcare trainees reflect the diverse communities they serve. Additionally, seeing that 43% of trainees reported not receiving training in anti-racism, sexual harassment, gender inclusivity, unconscious bias, or microaggressions is quite concerning. Microaggressions and implicit bias have been shown to be harmful both to healthcare providers and patients. Repeatedly experiencing bias and microaggressions has been shown to lead to psychological distress and ultimately impair work performance for healthcare providers [17]. For patients, it can lead to the wrongful dismissal of symptoms and inferior care [17]. Of note, the national curriculum for Canadian anesthesiology residents, which is published by the accreditation body, The Royal College of Physicians and Surgeons of Canada, does outline the necessity of EDI training in residency. The most recent curriculum document, revised in 2022, states that part of the anesthesiology curriculum includes teaching residents to recognize and respect gender, religious, cultural, ethnic, sexual, age, and disability (mental and physical) diversity [18]. As such, implementing formal training on anti-racism, sexual harassment, gender inclusivity, unconscious bias, and microaggressions, is an important next step for anesthesiology residency programs that have not already done so.

A limitation of our study includes a low survey response rate of approximately 20%. This assumes the total number of anesthesiology residents in Canada is 600 based on our own conservative estimates, with no public data available for this. To obtain this estimate, we contacted all 17 Canadian anesthesiology programs inquiring about their enrollment in the 2023–2024 academic year. Unfortunately, while we were able to obtain exact numbers for almost all programs, there was a small remaining amount we were unable to reach and therefore estimated their numbers based on the publicly available Canadian Resident Matching Service (CaRMS) data [19]. This data therefore does not account for residents transferring in or out of programs.

Furthermore, our study focused on the demographic characteristics and perceptions of EDI among current anesthesiology trainees. We are unable to comment on sexual identity and ethnicity comparisons with attending anesthesiologists in Canada, as this information is not available. Additionally, the concepts of gender, sexual identity, and ethnic identity, are not truly binary or discrete concepts, but are presented this way for simplicity. Furthermore, we chose to analyze the results of our survey as representative of the landscape across the country as a whole, rather than by individual programs. Given our response rate of 123, we did not have sufficient data from each program to analyze each program individually for anonymity. We do recognize that the results can be biased by having more responses from certain programs, and thus recognise it as a limitation of our study.

Pertaining to the survey question that asked about a resident’s knowledge of existing EDI initiatives, it is important to note that this relies on a resident’s subjective knowledge. This may lead to underreporting, as EDI initiatives may exist unofficially, the respondent may be early in training and unaware, or the respondent may not have been involved with any of these programs. Thus, we cannot speculate how many programs have or do not have existing EDI initiatives based on this data. However, we argue that subjective awareness of EDI initiatives is important for residents, in order to create a culture of EDI transparency. Lastly, we recognize that training on various EDI topics such as anti-racism, gender inclusivity and microaggressions, may be delivered by programs at discrete time periods in residency. Thus, our results may be impacted by where the individual answering the question is at in their training, which we acknowledge as a limitation. However, we argue that these topics are extremely important, and thus training and teaching should be delivered to all residents, regardless of training year. Ultimately, we hope that programs can use our results as a prompt to conduct further introspective analysis of their own demographics, resident perceptions of diversity, and current EDI initiatives. This can in turn be used to guide future initiatives at the program level.

Conclusions

To our knowledge, this study is the first to describe the demographic data of Canadian anesthesiology residency trainees and the EDI initiatives found within their training programs. Our data revealed relative gender parity for male and female trainees across the country. Compared to Canadian demographic data, there is approximately a four times higher representation of LGBTQ individuals in Canadian anesthesiology training programs, however, given that the LGBTQ population is Canada is heavily skewed to younger ages, we suspect that this result is likely more equal than it seems. While the majority of ethnicities are adequately represented in Canadian anesthesiology training programs, when compared to the national population, Hispanic, Indigenous, Southeast Asian, and West Asian ethnicities remain underrepresented. Most residents believe EDI is important, however, the majority were unaware of any initiatives within their programs. Areas of future growth include better defining which EDI initiatives exist within training programs, studying how EDI programs are implemented in training programs, and examining perceived barriers to both having and implementing said initiatives. Through doing so, anesthesiology residency programs across Canada can best ensure their trainees reflect the diverse communities they serve and are educated to care for minoritized groups.
