α-pinene and β-sitosterol co-loaded alginate/gelatin-functionalized niosomes for enhanced antibacterial and anti-biofilm activity against methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus and carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae
Authors: Hoda Ghoraba, Fatemeh Ashrafi, Rezvan Mousavi Nadushan, Hosein Abbaspour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe rise of antibiotic resistance in infectious disease, particularly from carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) and methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), poses a significant global health challenge. This study aimed to fabricate and evaluate a novel biocompatible hydrogel scaffold (Nio-PIN/STL@SC) incorporating niosomes loaded with α-pinene (PIN) and β-sitosterol (STL) to enhance antibacterial and anti-biofilm activities against MRSA and CRKP isolates.MethodsNiosomes containing PIN and STL (Nio-PIN/STL) were prepared using the thin-layer hydration method and then incorporated into an alginate/gelatin hydrogel scaffold via a straightforward crosslinking reaction, forming Nio-PIN/STL@SC. The physicochemical properties of the scaffolds were characterized using DLS, SEM, TEM, and FTIR. In vitro assays assessed drug release, swelling, degradation, stability, cytotoxicity, antibacterial efficacy (MIC, disk diffusion, time-kill assay), anti-biofilm activity (CV assay, MBEC), and the expression of biofilm-related genes.ResultsThe optimized Nio-PIN/STL@SC exhibited an average particle size of 263.5 ± 5.2 nm, Polydispersity Index (PDI) of 0.231 ± 0.011, and entrapment efficiencies (EE%) of 78.2 ± 1.18% (PIN) and 79.7 ± 1.35% (STL). The scaffold demonstrated a sustained biphasic release profile, with only 40.85% of PIN and 43.25% of STL released over 72 h at pH 7.4. Nio-PIN/STL@SC showed markedly enhanced antibacterial activity, with MIC values of 0.97–1.95 µg/mL against MRSA and 0.97–3.9 µg/mL against CRKP, representing at least a 16-fold reduction compared to free drugs. MBEC values revealed a significant reduction in biofilm biomass, decreasing viable MRSA and CRKP cells to approximately 2.0 and 3.5 log10 CFU/mL, respectively. qRT-PCR analysis demonstrated significant downregulation of icaA and icaD in MRSA and mrkA, mrkD, and fimA in CRKP (P < 0.001), alongside upregulation of the biofilm repressor gene icaR.ConclusionsThe developed Nio-PIN/STL@SC hydrogel scaffold demonstrates a promising drug delivery system for the synergistic enhancement of antibacterial and anti-biofilm activities against multidrug-resistant pathogens like MRSA and CRKP with reduced cytotoxic effects, suggesting its potential for treating bacterial infections. Further in vivo studies are warranted to validate these findings.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

The increasing prevalence of antimicrobial resistance poses a serious challenge to the effective management of infectious diseases worldwide. Among resistant pathogens, carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) and methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) are of particular concern due to their high morbidity, mortality, and limited therapeutic options [1–4]. CRKP infections, especially in hospital settings, are associated with mortality rates ranging from 30% to 70%, largely due to delayed or ineffective antimicrobial therapy [5]. Similarly, MRSA has evolved multiple resistance and immune evasion mechanisms, enabling it to cause persistent and invasive infections [6].

The production of biofilm helps explain the continuation of infections as strains with increased virulence flourish within these protective mechanisms [7]. Attachment, creation and maturation, and dissemination are among the many phases of biofilm development. S. aureus, biofilm formation is largely mediated by the production of polysaccharide intercellular adhesin (PIA), regulated by the ica operon [8, 9]. In K. pneumoniae, adhesion and biofilm development are primarily associated with fimbrial structures such as type 1 and type 3 fimbriae encoded by fimA, mrkA, and mrkD genes [10, 11].

Hydrogels have emerged as promising platforms for antimicrobial delivery due to their three-dimensional structure, high water content, and tissue-like properties [12, 13]. Among natural polymers, alginate (ALG) and gelatin (GEL) are widely used because of their biocompatibility, biodegradability, and ability to form stable cross-linked networks [14, 15]. ALG-based hydrogels have been extensively applied in wound management and drug delivery, while GEL provides additional functional groups that enhance mechanical strength and biological performance [16–19]. Consequently, this study involved the preparation of hydrogels exhibiting antibacterial properties through the use of GEL, the complexation of ALG, and the incorporation of niosomes loaded with phytochemicals.

Nanotechnology’s use in medicine, known as nanomedicine, presents various promising opportunities in healthcare [20, 21]. Niosomes are vesicles composed mostly of nonionic surfactants and cholesterol, serving as excipients that may enhance medication absorption [22]. Recent improvements in niosome-based drug delivery systems have shown encouraging outcomes regarding enhanced therapeutic effectiveness, less toxicity, and improved targeted delivery [23]. Niosomes are seen as an alternative to liposomal phospholipids [24]. Niosomes may be categorized into lipid-based and polymer-based carriers according to their primary component [25]. Numerous publications have documented using niosomes to address various conditions, including cancer and bacterial infections [26, 27].

β-Sitosterol (STL) is a biological micronutrient present in the cells and membranes of all oil-producing plants [28]. It is available in preparative quantities as mixes with other phytosterols, mainly stigmasterol and campesterol [29]. STL has shown safety, non-toxicity, and efficacy as a dietary supplement, exhibiting significant potential health advantages across several uses, including antimicrobial properties [30]. Previous experimental experiments have shown that STL has antibacterial action against many bacterial species [31]. Luhata et al. demonstrated that STL had antibacterial action against S. aureus isolates [30]. Pinenes and bicyclic terpenes are present in the essential oils of several plants [32]. Pinene, a monoterpene molecule, has two active isomers, one being PIN, characterized by the chemical formula C10H16. PIN is found in several essential oils, and numerous studies have examined its antibacterial properties and potential to influence antimicrobial resistance [33]. Griffiths et al. documented the inhibitory impact of PIN on the proliferation of several microorganisms, namely Nocardia sp., Pseudomonas sp., and P. fluorescens [34].

Despite extensive investigations on phytochemical-based antimicrobials, niosomal drug delivery systems, and hydrogel scaffolds as separate strategies, no previous study has integrated these three approaches into a single, multifunctional platform specifically designed to target biofilm-associated multidrug-resistant pathogens. To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first report of an alginate/gelatin hydrogel scaffold embedding niosomes co-loaded with PIN and STL, engineered to achieve simultaneous enhancement of antibacterial potency, biofilm eradication, and genetic suppression of biofilm-associated virulence factors in both MRSA and CRKP clinical isolates. In contrast to prior works that primarily focus on planktonic antibacterial activity or phenomenological biofilm inhibition, the present study provides a mechanistically informed, multi-scale evaluation combining sustained-release behavior, quantitative MIC and MBEC analyses, time-dependent bactericidal kinetics, and direct modulation of key biofilm regulatory genes (icaA, icaD, icaR in MRSA; mrkA, mrkD, fimA in CRKP). This integrative design establishes Nio-PIN/STL@SC not merely as a carrier system, but as an active antibiofilm therapeutic platform, offering a distinct conceptual and functional advancement over existing phytochemical-, niosome-, or hydrogel-based antimicrobial systems.

Materials and methods

Materials

Alginate (ALG) and gelatin (GEL) were purchased from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Cholesterol (CAS No. 57-88-5), Span 60 (sorbitan monostearate, CAS No. 1338-41-6), and Tween 60 (polyoxyethylene sorbitan monostearate, CAS No. 9005-67-8) were obtained from Capot Chemical Co., Ltd. (China) and Henan Daken Chemical Co., Ltd. (China).

α-Pinene (PIN) and β-sitosterol (STL) were supplied by Merck (Germany). Chloroform and methanol (analytical grade) were purchased from Merck (Germany). Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4), calcium chloride (CaCl₂), sodium acetate, and crystal violet were obtained from Merck (Germany).

Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) and DMEM/F12 culture media, fetal bovine serum (FBS), and trypsin-EDTA were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (USA). Mueller–Hinton broth and agar were obtained from Merck (Germany).

MTT reagent was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). RNA extraction kits (RNX-Plus) were obtained from SinaGene (Iran), and cDNA synthesis kits were purchased from Yekta Tajhiz Azma (Iran). PCR reagents and primers were supplied by CinnaGene (Iran). All aqueous solutions were prepared using ultrapure deionized water.

Preparation of Nio-PIN/STL

Niosomes co-loaded with PIN and STL were prepared using the thin-film hydration method as previously described [35] with minor modifications. Briefly, cholesterol (300 µmol), Span 60, and Tween 60 (50:50 molar ratio) were dissolved in a methanol/chloroform mixture (2:1, v/v). Due to their hydrophobic nature, PIN and STL (10 mg/mL, 1:1 ratio) were co-dissolved with the lipid components in the organic phase prior to solvent evaporation.

The solvent was evaporated using a rotary evaporator (Heidolph, Germany) at 60 °C and 120 rpm for 1 h to form a thin lipid film. The dried film was hydrated with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) at 60 °C under gentle agitation (120 rpm) for 1 h to obtain multilamellar niosomes. The dispersion was subsequently probe-sonicated (SONOPULS ultrasonic homogenizer, 25% amplitude, 200 W) in an ice bath for 5 min to reduce particle size and obtain a homogeneous nanosuspension. The prepared Nio-PIN/STL suspension was stored at 4 °C until further use.

Preparation of Nio-PIN/STL@SC

Fabrication of Nio-PIN/STL-loaded alginate/gelatin scaffolds

To fabricate the three-dimensional scaffold, gelatin (0.06 g) was dissolved in 7 mL DMEM at 40 °C. Sodium alginate was then added to the gelatin solution and mixed for 1 h to obtain a homogeneous polymer blend. Subsequently, 3 mL of the previously prepared Nio-PIN/STL nanosuspension was incorporated into the polymer mixture to achieve a final polymer concentration of 6% (w/v), forming a bio-ink.

The bio-ink was centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 1 min to remove entrapped air bubbles and loaded into a 2 mL syringe equipped with a 21-gauge nozzle (0.51 mm inner diameter). Scaffolds (7 mm × 7 mm × 0.15 mm) were fabricated using a 3D-printing system at ambient temperature (25 °C), with a strand spacing of 1.3 mm.

The printed scaffolds were incubated at 4 °C for 1 h to stabilize the structure, followed by ionic crosslinking in 300 mM CaCl₂ solution for 15 min. Excess calcium ions were removed using DMEM/F12 medium. The printed constructs represented solid three-dimensional delivery platforms embedding the niosomal system.

Characterization of Nio-PIN/STL@SC

The mean size, PDI, and zeta potential of Nio-PIN/STL@SC were determined using dynamic light scattering (DLS) and ZetaPlas palladium electrodes (Brookhaven Instruments Corp., USA). The PDI is a statistic for evaluating sample variability depending on size. Polydispersity may arise from particle size variations or accumulation or aggregation during extraction or analysis. The PDI was assessed using DLS from Brookhaven Instruments Corp., USA. A micrograph of nanoparticles was obtained using a transmission electron microscope (TEM) (Philips, Leo 906E, Germany, operating at 80 kV) to examine the morphology of Nio-PIN/STL@SC. A droplet of Nio-PIN/STL@SC was deposited onto a copper grid for TEM analysis. Subsequently, the samples were rinsed with distilled water and dyed using 2% uranyl acetate. Imaging of the samples was conducted at 80 kV. The dimensional and morphological analysis of Nio-PIN/STL@SC was performed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM) model MIRA3 (TESCAN, Czech Republic). For SEM, Scaffolds were diluted with deionized water at a ratio of 1:100, and a drop was deposited on a silicon wafer, which was then dried for 24 h at an appropriate temperature. Subsequently, a 100 Å gold layer was deposited for 3 min under argon at a pressure of 0.2 atm and analyzed using field emission SEM. The Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) test was used to identify the functional groups of molecular compounds and, therefore, infer the potential structure of the substances. The FTIR spectra of Nio-PIN/STL@SC were analyzed on KBr discs using a PerkinElmer FTIR spectrophotometer (Spectrum Two, USA). FTIR analysis was performed within a scanning range of 4000 to 400 cm− 1 at a constant resolution of 4 cm− 1 at ambient temperature. A solid sample was analyzed using the KBr technique, and the transmission percentage was documented.

Evaluation of the entrapment efficiency

The entrapment efficiency (EE%) was determined by separating free (unencapsulated) drugs from niosomal formulations via high-speed centrifugation (utilizing a Wit Gamb laboratory apparatus, Korea) at 13,000 rpm for 90 min. The supernatant containing free PIN/STL was collected and quantified spectrophotometrically. The medications persisted in the supernatant, whereas the retained drug was in the sediment. In the subsequent stage, to determine the proportion of PIN and STL entrapment in Nio@SC, the collected silt was dissolved in 5 ml of isopropyl alcohol to isolate the niosome and release the entrapped medication. Subsequently, 2 ml of the resultant solution was diluted to a total volume of 25 ml with sterile distilled water, and the absorbance, along with that of the unbound drug in the supernatant, was measured using an ultraviolet-visible spectrophotometer (204 and 622 nm for PIN and STL, respectively) (Lambda 25, Perkin Elmer, USA). One ml of the sample was used to ascertain PIN and STL concentrations and was substituted with an equivalent volume of phosphate buffer. Stability of Nio-PIN/STL@SC

One method of checking scaffold stability is monitoring specific characteristics over a certain period. Briefly, 1 ml of a solution of Nio-PIN/STL@SC was examined for size, PDI, EE%, and Zeta-Potential criteria for two months at temperatures of 4 and 25 °C.

Bacterial isolation and antibiotic susceptibility pattern

Four hundred clinical samples were obtained from clinical specimens and cultured on blood agar. Identification was confirmed using biochemical tests and 16 S rRNA sequencing. Antibiotic susceptibility testing was performed using the disk diffusion method according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines. Briefly, each bacterial isolate was cultured overnight in Mueller–Hinton broth and adjusted to a turbidity equivalent to 0.5 McFarland standard (approximately 1.5 × 108 CFU/mL) to ensure uniform inoculum density across all tests. The sensitivity of K. pneumoniae to imipenem, meropenem, and ertapenem discs and S. aureus isolates to vancomycin, cefoxitin, ciprofloxacin, gentamicin, cotrimoxazole, ampicillin, amoxicillin, penicillin, azithromycin, and tetracycline were assessed in Muller Hinton agar media. Using a sterile swab, the standardized bacterial suspension was uniformly spread over the entire surface of each agar plate to form a bacterial lawn. Sterile paper disks (6 mm diameter) impregnated with test samples were then placed onto the inoculated agar surface [38]. It should be mentioned that the standard strains of K. pneumoniae BAA-1706 and S. aureus ATCC 43,300 were used as the positive control, and cefoxitin antibiotic discs were employed to detect MRSA and imipenem-resistant, meropenem, and ertapenem discs to detect CRKP. The bacterial genomic DNA was extracted per the instructions of the Cinna Gene company’s extraction kit to identify the genes under study in bacterial isolates. The purity of the collected DNA was confirmed using a spectrophotometer set to 260 nm. The biofilm decoding genes mrkA, mrkD, and fimA (in CRKP) and icaA, icaD, and icaR (in MRSA) were identified in isolates using the PCR.

Antibacterial activity of Nio-PIN/STL@SC

Determination of minimum inhibitory concentration

The antibacterial efficacy of PIN, STL, PIN/STL, and Nio-PIN/STL@SC formulations against MRSA and CRKP pathogens was assessed using broth microdilution [23]. Following the culturing of isolates in Müller-Hinton broth for 24 h, 5 × 108 CFU/mL (50 µl) of bacteria, 100 µl of various compounds (at concentrations of 0.97, 1.95, 3.9, 7.81, 15.625, 31.25, 62.5, 125, and 250 µg/mL), and 95 µl of pure Hinton broth were added to each well of a 96-well plate and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. The negative control well (containing pure culture medium) and the positive control well (containing culture media plus standard strains) were ascertained, and the test was duplicated three times.

Disk diffusion assay

The susceptibility of MRSA and CRKP isolates to PIN, STL, PIN/STL, and Nio-PIN/STL@SC was evaluated using the Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion method as previously described [23], with minor modifications. Briefly, bacterial suspensions were prepared in sterile saline and adjusted to 0.5 McFarland standard (approximately 1.5 × 10⁸ CFU/mL). The standardized inoculum was uniformly swabbed onto Mueller–Hinton agar plates.

Sterile blank disks (Bio-Rad, USA) were impregnated with the MIC concentration of each formulation and allowed to dry under sterile conditions. The disks were then placed on the surface of the inoculated agar plates at appropriate distances. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. After incubation, the diameters of the growth inhibition zones were measured in millimeters.

Time-kill assay

The antibacterial efficacy of PIN, STL, PIN/STL, and Nio-PIN/STL@SC against MRSA and CRKP pathogens was assessed over 48 h using the time-kill assay [23]. Time-kill kinetics were evaluated by incubating standardized bacterial suspensions (≈ 5 × 108 CFU/mL) with sub-MIC concentrations (½ MIC) of the test formulations. Samples were incubated at 37 °C, and bacterial growth was monitored by measuring optical density at 600 nm at predetermined time points (8, 16, 24, 32, 40, and 48 h). Untreated bacterial suspensions served as control at hours.

Anti-biofilm activity

The anti-biofilm effectiveness of PIN, STL, PIN/STL, and Nio-PIN/STL@SC against MRSA and CRKP biofilms was evaluated using a microtiter plate crystal violet (CV) assay [39]. Initially, 180 µl of Mueller Hinton broth (MHB) culture medium and 20 µl of pathogenic bacteria (0.5 McFarland standard (~ 1.5 × 108 CFU/mL) were added to each well, achieving an OD of 600 at 0.6. The mixture was incubated for 48 h at 37 °C and 120 rpm to promote biofilm formation. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h without agitation (static conditions) to allow initial attachment and biofilm development on the well surfaces. Subsequently, 100 µl of solutions were added at the ½ MIC concentration and unaltered MHB medium (the negative control). The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, the liquid phase containing the suspended particles was removed, and the wells were washed with 300 µl of PBS to remove unattached cells. The plates were subsequently air-dried. The biofilms were immobilized with 175 µl of a 2% sodium acetate solution and then treated with 175 µl of a 0.1% CV solution for 30 min without light. The wells were then washed with PBS to remove any excess color. Subsequently, 200 µl of ethanol was added to the wells, and their absorbance was assessed at a wavelength of 570 nm.

The minimum biofilm eradication concentration (MBEC) evaluates the efficacy of tested substances in dismantling established biofilms. The evaluated strains were allowed to form biofilms, as shown in the biofilm formation evaluation. To specifically assess the ability of formulations to disrupt pre-formed biofilms, biofilms were first allowed to develop under static conditions for 24 h. After formation, planktonic cells were removed by washing with PBS, and fresh medium containing the test treatment was added to the wells. Subsequently, the wells were washed with sterile distilled water, and 200 µL of each tested concentration of PIN, STL, PIN/STL, and Nio-PIN/STL@SC were added. The plates were incubated for 24 h under aerobic conditions at 37 °C. The OD of the biofilms was subsequently assessed at a wavelength of 570 nm. The negative control included uninfected Tryptic Soy broth, whereas the positive control consisted of a basic bacterial culture. The MBEC is the concentration at which the average biofilm OD equals or less than the negative control’s. The tests were performed in duplicate [39, 40].

Analysis of biofilm-related gene expression

The expression of the biofilm genes icaA, icaD, and icaR (in MRSA) and mrkA, mrkD, and fimA (in CRKP) was examined through qRT–PCR using the specific primers indicated in Table 1. Total RNA was extracted from treated and untreated bacterial cells using RNX-Plus reagent. cDNA synthesis was performed using a commercial kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Biofilm gene expression to the 16 S rRNA gene (as the reference gene) was examined using a Corbett 5 Plex HRM real-time PCR device (Corbett, Australia). The final reaction volume was 15 µl, containing 1 µl of cDNA, 1 µl of the forward primer and 1 µl of reverse primer, 8 µl of master mix and 4 µl of deionized water (Merck, Germany), and the temperature cycle included initial denaturation for five minutes at 95 °C followed by 40 cycles of 20 s at 95 °C, 40 s at 58 °C, and 40 s at 72 °C. The final stage was selected to draw melting curves at 53–95 °C. Quantitative real-time PCR was conducted using gene-specific primers, and relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method with 16 S rRNA as the reference gene.

Cytotoxicity study

The cytotoxicity test was conducted on HFF normal cells using the MTT technique, in accordance with standard ISO 10993-5. To assess cytotoxicity, a L929 confluence flask was positioned under a hood, and the cells were grown in total sterile DMEM with high glucose culture media. The cell surface was rinsed with PBS, and then trypsin was used to detach the cells from the fibroblastic morphology and render them spherical. Subsequently, DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS (fetal bovine serum) was added to the cells to inactivate trypsin, after which the cells were transferred to a Falcon tube and centrifuged at 1200 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was removed, DMEM with 10% FBS was added, and cell counts were conducted on a Neobar slide. Each well contained a 24-cell plate with 5 × 104 cells. Sterile samples of PIN, STL, PIN/STL, and Nio-PIN/STL@SC were positioned in the middle of each plate under aseptic circumstances, followed by the addition of cell suspension. The plates were then incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 24 h. In accordance with standard ISO10993-5, after a 24-hour period, the media was discarded, the cells were rinsed with PBS, and an adequate volume of MTT (1 mg/ml) was added to the cell layer. The culture plate was incubated for 3 to 5 h, after which the cells were rinsed with PBS to eliminate unreacted MTT. The interaction of Formosan solvent with isopropanol and the OD560 nm of each cell were assessed. Cell viability was determined from the absorption ratio of each sample relative to the control sample (polystyrene container). Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis of this study was conducted utilizing SPSS version 20 and GraphPad Prism V.8 software. The results were evaluated through one-way analysis of variance (One-way ANOVA), and the differential expression of target genes between control and treated samples was determined using Tukey’s HSD post-hoc test. The results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), with a P value of less than 0.05 deemed significant.

Results

Scaffold characterization

The niosomal formulations were produced using the thin-film hydration technique. The synthesized Nio-PIN/STL@SC was evaluated by assessing the drug EE%, particle size, zeta potential, and PDI using DLS. The validation of PIN and STL occurred at 204 and 622 nm, demonstrating linear regression with correlation coefficients of 0.9967 and 0.9997, respectively. Table 2; Fig. 1A provide the DLS findings for characterizing these samples. Formulation number 3 was identified as the optimal formulation. As shown in Table 2; Fig. 1A, the average dimensions, PDI, EE% for PIN and STL, and zeta potential of Nio-PIN/STL@SC (F3) were 263.5 ± 5.2 nm, 0.231 ± 0.011, 78.2 ± 1.18%, 79.7 ± 1.35%, and 20.2 ± 2.11, respectively. The average diameters and PDI of the blank-F3 formulation were 203.6 ± 4.1 nm and 0.187 ± 0.015, respectively.

The morphology of Nio-PIN/STL@SC was examined using SEM and TEM, as seen in Fig. 1B-C. The SEM of scaffolds exhibited a well-defined, very porous structure. The resultant hydrogel has a highly porous architecture characterized by linked pores. The TEM findings indicated that the produced niosomes inserted in the GT-AL polymeric composite (Nio-PIN/STL@SC) are spherical and vary in size from 60 to 80 nm. The particle sizes are much smaller than the hydrodynamic diameters obtained using DLS, which is attributable to the drying of materials during the TEM sample preparation process.

Figure 1D presents the FT-IR spectra for each component and synthesized product, including Nio, PIN, STL, Nio-PIN/STL, and Nio-PIN/STL@SC. The FTIR data reveal peaks associated with the functional groups in the niosome compounds, including the 1110 cm− 1 peak corresponding to the stretching C–O alcohol bond and the 1736 cm− 1 peak linked to the stretching C = O alcohol bond in the structures of cholesterol, Tween 60, and Span 60. The spectra of pure STL exhibited peaks at 2869 and 1053 cm− 1, confirming the existence of C-OH of secondary alcohol and CH2 in the STL structure. The peaks identified for pure PIN at 3051 cm− 1 and 1396 cm− 1 correspond to olefinic C-H stretching and C-O stretching, respectively. The observed bands at 3032 cm− 1, 1310 cm− 1, and 1032 cm− 1 may have facilitated the integration of PIN and STL into the niosome architecture. In Nio-PIN/STL@SC, the transformation of niosome into an ALG-GEL scaffold revealed the band corresponding to the stretching olefinic C-H bond of Nio-PIN at 2920 cm− 1, therefore confirming the incorporation of Nio-PIN/STL into the scaffold structure.

This work sought to assess the release patterns of PIN, STL, and Nio-PIN/STL@SC at a physiological pH of 7.4 (Fig. 2A and B). Figure 2A and B illustrate the release of PIN and STL from the fabricated scaffolds, indicating a biphasic release characteristic. The medication release rate is a critical parameter in drug delivery systems that need further exploration. Buffer incubation was used to assess drug release from scaffolds, with formulations tested by observing absorbance changes in the removed samples. A biphasic drug release was seen in loaded formulations, characterized by an initial fast release followed by a plateau or a more gradual release phase. After 8 h, 85.65% of the free PIN and 87% of the free STL were released (pH = 7.4), whereas a significantly regulated release of PIN (40.85%) and STL (43.25%) from scaffolds was seen over 72 h.

Bacterial identification and antibiotic resistance pattern

Out of 400 clinically specimens, 53 (13.25%) were identified as S. aureus and 89 (22.25%) as K. pneumoniae. The quantity of antibiotic-resistant S. aureus and K. pneumoniae isolates as determined by the antibiogram test. Forty-six (86.7%) of S. aureus exhibited resistance to Cefoxitin, representing the most prevalent resistance reported. Vancomycin susceptibility was observed in 49 (92.5%), indicating that the majority of the isolates are responsive to the vancomycin antibiotic. Of the 89 K. pneumoniae isolates, 23 (25.84%) exhibited carbapenem resistance, demonstrating concurrent resistance to the medicines imipenem, meropenem, and ertapenem, and were classified as the strains under examination. Of the 46 cefoxitin-resistant S. aureus isolates, 14 exhibited substantial biofilm formation and had the icaA, icaD, and icaR genes, as shown by PCR testing. Of the 23 K. pneumoniae isolates resistant to carbapenems, 18 isolates (78%) possess the mrkA gene, 17 isolates (73.9%) possess the mrkD gene, and 14 isolates (60%) possess the fimA gene. Of the isolates, 12 (52.1%) possessing all genes were chosen for further investigation, as confirmed by PCR testing.

Anti-biofilm activity of PIN, STL, PIN/STL and Nio-PIN/STL@SC

Our study evaluated the inhibitory efficacy of Nio-PIN/STL@SC against MRSA and CRKP biofilms, comparing it with free PIN, free STL, and PIN/STL (Figs. 7 and 8). Discussion

The use of plants in combating ailments and disorders is deeply entrenched in human history. Researchers are exploring plant extracts as medicinal alternatives due to several studies highlighting the antibacterial efficacy of their crude extracts, which may serve as superior replacements for conventional antibiotics. Recent publications suggest that medicinal plants with anti-MRSA properties may be viable options for treating MRSA and CRKP infections [43–46]. Nonetheless, their limited availability and suboptimal pharmacokinetic features is a primary obstacle in using phytochemicals as antibacterial agents. Consequently, drug delivery methods, including nanomaterials, may address these constraints [47]. This work investigated Nio-PIN/STL@SC, a hydrogel-based Niosomal formulation, to diminish the pathogenicity of MRSA and CRKP while enhancing antibacterial efficacy against S. aureus and K. pneumoniae.

The fabricated scaffolds exhibit nanoscale particle size with uniform distribution, as shown by the PDI results. The PDI was determined to be below 0.7 for the majority of formulations. The PDI is below 0.7, indicating a more restricted size distribution, in contrast to particles with a broad distribution prone to aggregation [48]. Cholesterol is an essential element in the production of sound. It enhances vesicle stability and is crucial for bilayer packing [49]. Elevating the surfactant-to-cholesterol ratio to 2:1 decreased the average particle diameter. This result corroborated the prior evidence, suggesting that a reduction in cholesterol led to a decrease in vesicle size [50]. This may be viewed as elevated cholesterol levels disturbing the niosomal membrane. Due to its amphiphilic nature, cholesterol intercalates inside the bilayer structure of niosomes by positioning its polar head towards the aqueous surface and aligning its aliphatic tail parallel to the hydrocarbon chains, forming enormous vesicles [51]. Aldawsari et al. found that a 2:1 surfactant-to-cholesterol ratio correlates with reduced size, decreased PDI, and increased EE% [52]. Consequently, the F3 formulation demonstrated superior size, PDI, EE%, and Zeta potential compared to the other formulations. ALG and GEL possess a negative charge; however, due to the substantial positive charge of niosomes, the Nio-PIN/STL@SC formulation exhibits a high positive Zeta potential, indicating excellent formulation stability [41, 53]. Furthermore, Span 60 (Surfactant) has an appropriate HLB value, enhancing its hydrophobic properties to encapsulate hydrophobic medicines inside its bilayer effectively. The SEM investigation reveals that most particles are diminutive and spherical, accompanied by a limited quantity of larger particles. Furthermore, the analysis of TEM images indicates that the shape of the shell is internal [54]. The discrepancy in size measurements between SEM and DLS techniques may be attributed to the drying process of SEM images [55]. The FTIR data further validate the co-loaded niosomal composition inside the scaffolds.

Incorporating PIN/STL into Nio@SC (Nio-PIN/STL@SC) has been shown to provide prolonged rather than explosive release of the PIN or STL. The controlled drug release profile of this investigation demonstrates a biphasic trend. The results indicate that niosomal formulations facilitated prolonged drug release. During the first phase, there was a fast drug release. This phase was likely associated with medications situated on the outer surface of the niosomes, and their release required little diffusion. A slow release of the medication was seen in the subsequent phase. This phase was likely associated with medicines confined inside the core or the bilayer of the niosomal vesicles [56]. Nonetheless, a reduced release rate with the inclusion of ALG and GEL has been previously documented [57, 58].

Furthermore, beta-hydroxyl groups from STL would have contributed to the augmentation of polar components at the surface, resulting in increased hydrophilicity [59]. This increased hydrophilicity improved swelling behavior and may augment water absorption [60]. Moreover, the presence of a tiny quantity of niosome, characterized by a bilayer lipid structure, and its interaction with GEL/ALG may have significantly contributed to lowering the hydrolytic degradation rate in Nio-PIN/STL@SC [61]. The analysis of the mechanical properties of the scaffolds indicated that incorporating the drug into the niosome enhances the modulus, strain at failure, and compressive strength, attributable to the formation of the shell-core structure and the liquid drug contained within, which can be ascribed to the drug’s softening effect on the polymer structure [62]. Furthermore, the stability of Nio-PIN/STL@SC was superior at 4 °C compared to 25 °C. At both temperatures, the size and PDI of the niosomal formulations increased with time, with a lesser increase seen at 4 °C compared to 25 °C, possibly because of the reduced mobility of niosomes at lower temperatures [63]. Conversely, ALG and GELs have significant thermal stability owing to their unique polysaccharides, which minimize drug leakage [64, 65].

The secondary objective of this work was to investigate the antibacterial and anti-biofilm properties of Nio-PIN/STL@SC, as well as the modulation of gene expression associated with biofilm formation prevention or reduction. The Nio-PIN/STL@SC MIC was at least 32.2-fold lower than the PIN/STL combination in MRSA and CRKP isolates, demonstrating the superior efficacy of Nio-PIN/STL@SC relative to free medicines and combination formulations. The Time-Kill assay and disc diffusion experiments corroborated these findings. This study examined the antibacterial efficacy of PIN and STL by incorporating these compounds into Nio@SC. In combination with treatment, natural materials provide their alleged therapeutic benefits. Conversely, using nanoparticles as therapeutic agents for oral administration might mitigate certain limitations and provide advantages over targeted chemotherapy, including less toxicity and promoting antibiotic resistance [66]. Research indicates that PIN exerts its antibacterial effects by compromising the cellular integrity of microbes [67]. STL may also merge into bacterial cell membranes, modifying their permeability and compromising integrity. This disturbance may result in cell lysis and subsequent mortality [68]. Silva et al. investigated the antibacterial efficacy of α- and β-pinene enantiomers against C. albicans, Cryptococcus neoformans, Rhizopus oryzae, and MRSA. The scientists discovered that the enantiomers α- and β-pinene had MIC values between 117 and 6250 µg/mL [32]. A further investigation showed that STL had significant bacteriostatic efficacy against S. aureus with a MIC of 7.81 µg/mL and K. pneumoniae with a MIC of 31.25 µg/mL [69]. The limited solubility of phytochemicals such as STL and PIN presents obstacles to their use as potent antibacterial agents, particularly in developing therapies targeting antibiotic-resistant bacteria [68]. Nonetheless, combination treatment aids in surmounting some related obstacles. In this context, PIN and STL were administered together to assess their antibacterial efficacy. The findings indicated that the co-delivery of PIN/STL exhibited antimicrobial efficacy against MRSA and CRKP isolates at reduced doses relative to their separate administration, aligning with other studies [70, 71].

The enhanced antibacterial properties of the synergistic form may result from improved medication penetration into the bacterial cell membrane [72, 73]. A multitude of research has investigated the antibacterial efficacy of niosomes containing phytochemicals. Research indicates that niosomes infused with phytochemicals may successfully suppress the proliferation of bacteria such as S. aureus, Escherichia coli, K. pneumoniae, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa [74–76]. This action may result from the potential fusion of the niosome with the bacterial cell membrane, facilitating the diffusion of curcumin into the bacterial cell, enabling targeted drug release, and augmenting antibacterial efficacy [76]. Hydrogels are secure nanocarriers suitable for extended use [77]. Curcumin-encapsulated hybrid hydrogels were developed and evaluated for their antibacterial efficacy against E. coli and S. aureus. The findings demonstrated enhanced antibacterial efficacy with regulated release and elevated loading efficiency [78]. In the work of Gezgin et al., a 3D thermo-sensitive hydrogel was infused with propolis and gentamycin to assess their combined impact. The fractional inhibitory concentration indices demonstrated significant synergy and partial synergy, both outside and inside 3D thermo-sensitive hydrogel, at concentrations ranging from 0.38 to 0.56 µg/mL [79]. The findings of Varlık et al. indicated that the antibacterial efficacy of the formulated gelatin cryogels infused with tea tree oil (Melaleuca alternifolia) against E. coli ranged from 21% to 50%, and against S. aureus ranged from 28% to 72% [80]. Scaffolds impregnated with solid lipid nanoparticle gels containing Zataria multiflora essential oil exhibited enhanced antibacterial efficacy against all standard and clinical strains of S. aureus and P. aeruginosa throughout incubation periods compared to the essential oil alone [81]. The core-shell polyvinyl alcohol/gelatin fibrous scaffold infused with Thymus diagenesis essential oil and Glycyrrhiza glabra L. extract exhibited significant antibacterial efficacy against both gram-positive S. aureus and gram-negative K. pneumoniae, surpassing the effectiveness of the free extract and their combinations [82].

On the other hand, the CV and MBEC assays demonstrated that Nio-PIN/STL@SC exhibited the maximum biofilm development and production relative to free medicines and PIN/STL. This behavior may be associated with the in vitro release outcomes, whereby the proportion of PIN/STL released over time was decreased for the scaffolds [83]. Niosomes have garnered interest for their potential in addressing biofilms produced by Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. Niosomes may encapsulate and distribute antibiotics more efficiently than free medicines, enhancing local concentrations at the infection site. The architecture of niosomes may improve the infiltration of therapeutic drugs through the biofilm matrix, facilitating more efficient bactericidal action. Niosomes may impair biofilm structure, increasing bacterial susceptibility to drugs [76]. CV test, MBEC, and minimum biofilm inhibitory concentration analyses in several studies showed that niosomes encapsulating antibacterial medicines exhibit more pronounced antimicrobial and anti-biofilm activities than free pharmaceuticals [35, 84, 85]. The work by Farias et al. indicated that morin included in the sodium alginate and gellan gum films has significant promise for regulating monospecies biofilms [83]. A study showed that the anti-biofilm efficacy of gelatin hydrogels infused with lactoferrin-functionalized bio-nanosilver was inferior to that of the nanoparticles alone against S. aureus and P. aeruginosa isolates, contradicting our findings; this discrepancy may be attributable to the characteristics of the synthesized silver nanoparticles [86]. Conversely, l-proline enriched alginate dialdehyde-gelatin hydrogel thin films showed significant anti-biofilm efficacy against Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria compared to free l-proline [87].

Recent research indicates that phytochemicals may substantially influence bacterial biofilm development. This research investigated the effects of Nio-PIN/STL@SC sub-MIC doses on the expression of icaA, icaD, and icaR genes in MRSA, as well as mrkA, mrkD, and fimA genes in CRKP isolates, demonstrating significant changes in gene expression relative to free medicines and the PIN/STL formulation. The observed synergistic effect between the niosomal nanocarrier and α‑pinene/β‑sitosterol can be explained by the enhanced intracellular delivery and sustained release of the phytochemicals, which led to a pronounced suppression of key biofilm‑associated genes (icaA, icaD in MRSA and mrkA, mrkD, fimA in CRKP). Downregulating the specified genes may impede transcription by directly affecting the interaction between Nio-PIN/STL@SC and transcription factors, leading to decreased expression of these genes. Fathi et al. demonstrated a more pronounced down-regulation of icaA and icaD gene expression in S. aureus isolates treated with Ciprofloxacin-encapsulated niosomes compared to those treated with Ciprofloxacin alone [22]. Bazargan et al. demonstrated that Niosome-loaded tetracycline-ampicillin reduced the expression of the icaA, mrkA, and ndvB genes in isolates of S. aureus, K. pneumoniae, and P. aeruginosa, respectively [23]. Larijanian et al. demonstrated that zingerone eradicated one- and three-day-old MRSA biofilms at a concentration of 1000 µg/ml, while zingerone-loaded niosomes eliminated biofilms aged 1, 3, and 5 days at concentrations of 250 µg/ml, 250 µg/ml, and 500 µg/ml, respectively [88]. Furthermore, Real-time PCR analysis from our prior work showed that Gingerol/vancomycin-loaded niosomes markedly decreased the expression of the fimH, blaKPC, mrkD, and Ompk36 genes in CRKP isolates [27]. In Taghiloo et al. study, chitosan/alginate nanoparticles containing Echinacea angustifolia extract with a size of approximately 335 nm were prepared, which showed stronger antibacterial and antibiofilm activity than the free extract and reduced the MIC by 32-fold. This nanoformulation was introduced as a stable and effective system to combat multidrug-resistant S. aureus with a significant reduction in the expression of biofilm genes (icaA, icaC, icaD) and acceptable cytotoxicity [89]. In our previous work, ceftizoxime (CEF) and curcumin (CUR) were successfully co‑loaded into niosomal nanoparticles and subsequently incorporated into a 3D‑printed gelatin–alginate scaffold, generating the Nio‑CUR/CEF@SC platform. This system displayed high drug encapsulation efficiencies for both CUR (78%) and CEF (80%), with well‑distributed nanoscale particle sizes ranging from 208 to 308 nm and a controlled, sustained release profile extending up to 72 h. The Nio‑CUR/CEF@SC formulation markedly enhanced antibacterial performance against MRSA and CRKP, lowering MIC values to 0.25–1 µg/mL—representing more than a 64‑fold improvement compared with the free drugs. In addition, it produced substantial inhibition zones (up to 31.5 mm) and demonstrated pronounced bactericidal and antibiofilm activity, achieving reductions exceeding 2 log₁₀ CFU/mL. Molecular analyses further revealed significant suppression of key virulence and resistance genes in MRSA (hla, hlb, pvl) and CRKP (blaTEM, blaCTXM, blaOXA‑48) [90]. Conversely, several investigations indicated that the encapsulation of pharmaceuticals inside alginate or gelatin-based scaffolds might modify the expression of virulence genes, which contradicts our findings [64, 91, 92].

The current research assessed the toxicity of several Nio-PIN/STL@SC formulations on HFF cells using the standard MTT assay, revealing a greater cell viability rate in groups treated with Nio-PIN/STL@SC compared to those treated with free PIN, free STL, or a combination of antibacterial agents. Nonetheless, elevated concentrations of PIN/STL induce considerable cytotoxicity in HFF cells, indicating that encapsulating antibacterial drugs inside Nio@SC is an optimal approach to mitigate adverse pharmacological effects in topical delivery. Increased concentrations of Nio-PIN/STL@SC may be cytotoxic. Consequently, reduced Nio-PIN/STL @ SC concentrations is advantageous for cytotoxicity investigations. Our data indicate that, whereas PIN and STL have cytotoxic effects on cancer cells, they seem to have no impact on normal cells and are non-toxic to them [93, 94]. The interactions in alginate–gelatin hydrogels demonstrate superior cytocompatibility regarding adhesion, proliferation, metabolic activity, and morphology of proliferating fibroblasts [95]. Furthermore, evaluating the vitality of MCF-10 A cells, which are healthy cells treated with paclitaxel-loaded niosomes functionalized by cross-linked gelatin/alginate composite, demonstrated reduced cytotoxicity compared to free paclitaxel [62].

Conclusion

In conclusion, Nio@SC encapsulation significantly enhanced the pharmaceutical performance and antibacterial efficacy of free PIN, free STL, and their combination against clinical MRSA and CRKP isolates, while maintaining low cytotoxicity. The results of real-time PCR, CV, and MBEC assays demonstrated that Nio@SC notably improved the anti-biofilm activity of these agents. However, this study was limited to in vitro conditions, and further in vivo validation, mechanistic investigations, and formulation optimization are required to support clinical translation. Overall, the Nio-PIN/STL@SC system represents a promising strategy for the treatment of biofilm-associated multidrug-resistant infections.
