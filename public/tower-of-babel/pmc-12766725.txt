Probing Hidden Vortices and Geometrical Effect via Surface-Projected Quasiparticle States
Authors: Ruijun Xi, Pei-Yao Liu, Dang Liu, Sha-Sha Xue, Qiao-Yan Yu, Xingsen Chen, Ji-Ce Sun, Yi Zhang, Xian Du, Xuhui Ning, Tingwen Miao, Pengyu Hu, Hao Yang, Dandan Guan, Xiaoxue Liu, Liang Liu, Yaoyi Li, Shiyong Wang, Canhua Liu, Zhu-An Xu, Yi Zhou, Hao Zheng, Jinfeng Jia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Vortices host quasiparticle excitations, such as Caroli–de Gennes–Matricon or Majorana states. Understanding how these states are modulated by various geometrical structures is essential for both fundamental superconductivity research and quantum device design. However, this relationship remains challenging to elucidate. Here, using a scanning tunneling microscope equipped with a dilution refrigerator, we clearly resolve quasiparticle states from lateral vortices buried beneath the surface of 2H-NbSe2 under in-plane magnetic fields. Our combined spectroscopic visualization and model calculation uncover a depth-dependent coupling behavior between vortices and the superconductor surface. Shallow vortices (depth of ∼1.5 coherence lengths) exhibit anomalous split quasiparticle states accompanied by a pseudogap, while deeper vortices (>4 coherence lengths) restore the conventional Caroli–de Gennes–Matricon characteristics. Our findings establish a rescaling law of vortex-bound states under a geometrical effect and identify a critical length scale relevant for superconducting devices aimed to utilize intrinsic vortex quasiparticles.

When a magnetic field is applied to type II superconductors, vortices, i.e., quantized whirlpools of circulating current, arise. , Vortices are not only topological defects but also serve as fundamental probes of superconducting properties. Within each vortex core, Cooper pairs break, giving rise to discrete quasiparticle-bound states known as Caroli–de Gennes–Matricon (CdGM) states. These states provide critical insights into the superconducting gap, pairing symmetry, and coherence length. Their study has greatly advanced our understanding of vortex dynamics and energy dissipation in high-magnetic-field applications. − Moreover, in chiral p-wave superconductors, vortices can host Majorana zero modes, which are topologically protected states with potential for fault-tolerant quantum computing. ,

Scanning tunneling microscopy (STM) has revolutionized the study of superconductors by directly probing their order parameter’s structure, , quasiparticle interference, , and topological phases. , When an out-of-plane magnetic field is applied (Figure a), vortices enter perpendicular to the surface, enabling the STM tip to directly access the vortex core and map the spatial distribution of quasiparticle wave functions, including CdGM states or Majorana zero modes. − However, an in-plane magnetic field presents a significant challenge. It induces vortices that lie parallel to the surface (Figure b), making their cores inaccessible to the STM tip. As a result, it remains an open question of whether the quantum states associated with these hidden vortices extend sufficiently to the surface to be detectable, thereby limiting our ability to study them.

The interplay between vortices and material defects crucially governs the superconductor behavior and device performance. These defects span multiple dimensionalities, including point-like [zero-dimensional (0D)], linear [one-dimensional (1D)], and planar [two-dimensional (2D)] geometries. − For instance, point defects can alter CdGM energy levels, potentially masking signatures of exotic states like Majorana zero modes. More crucially, planar defects, such as crystal surfaces, heterostructure interfaces, and device sidewalls, can induce geometric distortions in vortex distributions. − However, the impact of these geometric structures on vortex-bound quasiparticle states has remained theoretical due to the lack of direct experimental observations. ,

Here, we overcome this limitation by directly observing vortex-bound quasiparticle states on the surface of the type II superconductor 2H-NbSe2 (hereafter referred to as NbSe2), which originate from subsurface vortices induced by a 100 mT in-plane magnetic field. We discern spatial and energy patterns that result from the geometric coupling between the surface and the hidden vortices. By integrating our experimental observations with anisotropic Ginzburg–Landau (GL) and Bogoliubov–de Gennes (BdG) modeling, we quantify how this coupling modifies quasiparticle state energies. These findings elucidate the fundamental interactions between vortices and defects, thereby paving the way for the design of advanced vortex-based quantum devices.

NbSe2 is a s-wave superconductor with transition temperature T c = 7.2 K, in-plane coherence length ξ ab = 7.4 nm, and out-of-plane coherence length ξ c = 2.3 nm. We performed STM measurements on cleaved surfaces of high-quality NbSe2 in a dilution refrigerator at ∼20 mK (Figure S1) and find superconducting gap Δ = 1.29 meV by model fitting (Figure S2). We then applied an in-plane magnetic field of 100 mT parallel to the ab plane (Figure a). This field strength exceeds the lower critical field Hc1 (∼20 mT) but is below the upper critical field Hc2 (∼10 T), ensuring vortex formation without destroying superconductivity. The in-plane configuration buries vortices laterally beneath the surface, with their axes parallel to the ab plane, as opposed to the perpendicular piercing in the out-of-plane fields.

High-resolution differential conductance (dI/dV) spectroscopy revealed spectral features indicative of vortex-bound states extending to the surface. At zero bias, we observed elongated, stripe-like regions of enhanced local density of states (LDOS), spaced 100–200 nm apart, consistent with the expected Abrikosov lattice periodicity for this field (∼155 nm) , and prior reports. , These stripes, absent in zero-field measurements, align parallel to the applied field, confirming their vortex origin. Crucially, the line-cut dI/dV(y,V) diagram along these stripes exhibited in-gap peaks symmetric around zero bias, an indication of vortex-bound quasiparticle states (Figure c) [for comparison, when a 100 mT out-of-plane magnetic field is applied, CdGM states are directly detected, as shown in Figures S3 and S4; the energy level spacings of these CdGM states in NbSe2 are on the order of 10 μeV, , resulting in a spatial distribution that forms an “X” shape (Figure S4)]. Our result marks the first direct STM detection of quasiparticle states from buried vortices, demonstrating that their wave functions extend evanescently to the surface despite the inaccessible core.

Systematic mapping of the hidden vortex quasiparticle states revealed two distinct types of spatial and energy evolution, i.e., “split” and “non-split” (Figures and ). These patterns persist over distances exceeding the in-plane coherence length of ξ ab = 7.4 nm.

We first examine the split type, where the zero-bias stripe-like feature evolves with increasing energy. As the energy increases, this stripe gradually splits into two parts, which move apart from each other (Figure b). To examine the subgap quasiparticle excitations, we conducted a spatially resolved dI/dV(y,V) diagram perpendicular to the stripe, as depicted in Figure c. Benefiting from our ultralow-temperature (∼20 mK) high-resolution spectroscopy, we clearly reveal the emergence of quasiparticle states within the superconducting gap, spatially bound to the stripe region. The overall spatial and energy distribution of these quasiparticle states resemble a parabola superimposed on an inverted parabola, akin to an “avoided crossing” in the band structure of an ideal insulator. Single dI/dV spectra at various positions across the lateral vortex (Figure d) and zoomed-in spectra (Figure e) further illustrate this feature. Although the spectral weights of these quasiparticle states are weak compared to the intensity of the coherent peaks in NbSe2, our high-quality spectra still show that the quasiparticle states gradually shift to higher energies, accompanied by a decrease in intensity, as the distance from the vortex center increases. Furthermore, by comparing these results to a zero-field dI/dV(y,V) diagram taken at the same location (Figure S5), we observed blue shifts in the coherent peak energies, attributable to the supercurrent-induced Doppler shift on the NbSe2 surface.

In contrast, we identified a second type of quasiparticle state, the “non-split” type (Figure ). Here, energy-dependent dI/dV maps (Figure b) reveal a stripe structure that splits only at high energies, similar to the vortex in Figure . However, the spatial and energy distributions of the quasiparticle-bound states differ markedly. As evident from the dI/dV data in Figure c to e, the quasiparticle states exhibit an “X”-shaped configuration, resembling a gapless Dirac cone.

To elucidate the quasiparticle-bound states observed in hidden vortices, we employed numerical solutions to the anisotropic GL and BdG equations, capturing the geometrical effects of the superconductor surface on quasiparticle states. While NbSe2 is a multi-band superconductor, we mainly consider the Nb-derived band (Δ = 1.29 meV and ξ ab = 7.4 nm) in our simulations, since other bands contribute only minor broadening and do not alter the observed crossover scale.

Surface–vortex coupling modifies quasiparticle spectra significantly different from conventional CdGM states. For deep vortices (d/ξ c ≫ 4.0), energy spectra resemble CdGM states in isolated vortices, with CdGM levels ECdGM(α,ν)=νsin⁡αE0(ν=±12,±32,±52,...) , where α is defined by k F cos α = k x and E 0 ∼ Δ2/E F. However, as d decreases (d/ξ c < 4.0), the energy minimum shifts from k x = 0, breaking the ladder structure and altering quasiparticle-bound states (Figure b). At shallow depths (d/ξ c < 1.5), low-energy states shift upward, creating a pseudo-gap shown in Figure c. Spatial variance σ z of quasiparticle wave functions (see the Supporting Information) has a maximum value of ∼1.8ξ c , allowing surface detection only when d/ξ c < ∼3.6.

Simulated LDOS at the surface (z = 0) revealed depth-dependent features matching experiments, as illustrated in Figure d–i. For d = 1.0ξ c , LDOS shows an avoided-crossing shape with pseudo-gap E PG ∼ 0.3Δ, corresponding to the “split” type. As d increases, pseudo-gap E PG decreases, vanishing at 1.6 < d/ξ c < 2.0 to form an X-shaped “non-split” pattern, which reopens at larger d.

Comparing our simulations with the STM data (Figure ), we achieved excellent agreement. For a vortex depth of d = 1.49ξ c , both experimental and theoretical LDOS exhibit avoided-crossing shapes with a peak separation (at y = 0) of 2E PG ∼ 0.35Δ (Figure a to c). At a depth of d = 1.6ξ c , a single zero-energy peak emerges, faithfully reproducing the “non-split” type (Figure d to f). This confirms that geometric surface–vortex coupling drives the observed spectral behaviors.

Our model highlights a critical length scale (∼4ξ c ) for designing superconducting vortex devices, which emerges from the combined constraints (see the Materials and Methods) and from consistent trends across multiple vortices. Within our anisotropic GL + BdG framework, this scale marks the transition between surface-coupled vortex states and bulk-like CdGM behavior and is insensitive to moderate changes in model parameters or to the small fitting uncertainty in d. This robustness suggests that 4ξ c is a fundamental geometrical crossover length for vortex-bound quasiparticles in NbSe2 and potentially in other layered type II superconductors.

This critical depth has direct implications for the devices. In superconductor films with thickness d ≲ 4ξ c , any lateral vortex line is necessarily within this range of at least one surface or interface; therefore, its core states are unavoidably modified by geometric confinement. Thus, pseudo-gapped, “split”-type spectra should be expected in ultrathin superconductor devices, and surface-induced renormalization of vortex-core states must be considered when designing superconducting quantum devices based on hybrid structures. Our work identifies a practical design guideline.

We also considered alternative origins of the two spectral types. Local disorder can perturb CdGM levels, but we do not observe a correlation between visible defects in topography and the occurrence of “split” vortices, and clean regions host both types. Multi-band tunneling in NbSe2 can modify spectral weights but does not naturally generate the depth-dependent closing and reopening of the pseudo-gap reproduced by our BdG simulations. The charge density wave pattern and lattice periodicity remain uniform in areas containing both types, arguing against strain-driven effects. These observations, together with the quantitative agreement between experiment and depth-tuned simulations, support surface–vortex coupling, and vortex depth is the dominant control parameter.

In summary, we report the STM detection of subsurface vortex-bound quasiparticle states in 2H-NbSe2, uncovering “split” and “non-split” spectral features driven by geometric surface–vortex coupling. In combination with anisotropic Ginzburg–Landau and Bogoliubov–de Gennes modeling, our results solve a long-standing issue in superconductor physics. This work not only advances the fundamental understanding but also inspires strategies for engineering vortex-based devices, paving the way for innovations in quantum technologies and high-field applications.

Materials and Methods

Sample Preparation

Single-crystal 2H-NbSe2 was grown using the chemical vapor transport method. High-purity Nb powder (99.99%) and Se grain (99.99%) were mixed in a 1:2 molar ratio and sealed in an evacuated quartz tube with iodine as the transport agent. The tube was heated in a two-zone furnace: high-temperature zone at 1125 K for 825 min and low-temperature zone at 1095 K for 795 min. It was then maintained at these temperatures for 15 000 min before cooling to room temperature. Samples were cleaved in situ under ultrahigh vacuum (UHV) at room temperature to expose clean surfaces.

Scanning Tunneling Microscopy and Spectroscopy

The experiments were performed using an UHV dilution-refrigerator STM system equipped with a vector magnet capable of fields up to (B x , B y , and B z ) = (2, 2, and 9 T). All data were acquired at ∼20 mK with a base pressure of <2 × 10–10 mbar. Cleaved 2H-NbSe2 samples were immediately transferred to the STM head. Electrochemically etched tungsten tips were used after the in situ silver coating. Spectroscopy measurements employed standard lock-in techniques with a modulation voltage V mod at 901 Hz.

BdG Modeling

We solve the BdG equations on a 201 × 201 lattice in the y–z plane for d ≤ 6ξ c and a 601 × 601 lattice for larger d values, using open boundary conditions in both y and z directions. The lattice spacing is chosen such that one grid step corresponds to a = ξ ab /15, ensuring adequate resolution of the superconducting coherence length.

In our simulations, vortex core depth d is treated as a physical parameter representing the vertical distance between the sample surface and the vortex axis. While d is not directly measurable by STM, its range is constrained by (i) the Abrikosov lattice geometry under the applied in-plane field, which limits the possible trajectory of vortex lines before they intersect the surface and (ii) the distinct LDOS patterns (“split” versus “non-split”) that emerge only within narrow depth windows in the BdG simulations. Fitting the simulated spectra to representative experimental vortices yields d = 1.49ξ c for the split type and d = 1.6ξ c for the non-split type, with an estimated uncertainty of ±0.2ξ c . This uncertainty slightly broadens but does not remove the crossover that we identify at d ≈ 4ξ c . The latter scale is determined not from a single fit but from a systematic sweep over d, in which CdGM-like bound-state ladders are recovered for d/ξ c ≫ 4, while surface-induced spectral distortions persist for d/ξ c < 4. The universal nature of this crossover is robust to variations in the BdG parameters within the experimental bounds, shifting by less than 0.3ξ c in all tested cases.
