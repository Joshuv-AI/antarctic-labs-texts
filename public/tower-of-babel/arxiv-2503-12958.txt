FedSDP: Explainable Differential Privacy in Federated Learning via Shapley Values
Yunbo Li, Jiaping Gui, Yue Wu

Abstract
Federated learning (FL) enables participants to store data locally while collaborating in training, yet it remains vulnerable to privacy attacks, such as data reconstruction. Existing differential privacy (DP) technologies inject noise dynamically into the training process to mitigate the impact of excessive noise. However, this dynamic scheduling is often grounded in factors indirectly related to privacy, making it difficult to clearly explain the intricate relationship between dynamic noise adjustments and privacy requirements. To address this issue, we propose FedSDP, a novel and explainable DP-based privacy protection mechanism that guides noise injection based on privacy contribution. Specifically, FedSDP leverages Shapley values to assess the contribution of private attributes to local model training and dynamically adjusts the amount of noise injected accordingly. By providing theoretical insights into the injection of varying scales of noise into local training, FedSDP enhances interpretability. Extensive experiments demonstrate that FedSDP can achieve a superior balance between privacy preservation and model performance, surpassing state-of-the-art (SOTA) solutions.

Introduction

To mitigate the risk of privacy attacks, researchers have proposed various defense methods, which can be broadly categorized into homomorphic encryption (HE)

and differential privacy (DP)
[

,

,

,

obfuscate model parameters through noise injection, rendering transmitted data indistinguishable for privacy protection. Existing solutions determine the noise injection amount based on factors indirectly related to privacy, such as model weight changes

,

20

23

Therefore, a privacy-centric defense mechanism that guides noise injection based on the contribution of privacy to model training is desired. Fortunately, related work by
[
24
]
has demonstrated that we can determine the importance of a desirable attribute on model predictions. By leveraging this technique, we have devised a novel metric that quantifies the contribution of private attributes for a particular prediction during model training. As illustrated in Figure

Based on the above intriguing phenomenon, we introduce FedSDP, a novel differential privacy mechanism that injects noise based on the importance of private attributes. It consists of two modules:
Privacy Estimation (PE)
and
Noise Injection (NI)
. PE calculates the marginal benefit of each client’s private and non-private attributes in the current training round, while NI adjusts noise injection amounts dynamically based on private attribute contributions to local models. To accomplish this, we leverage Shapley values
[
24
,
25
]
to assess private information contribution to the training process, which is a unique advantage over existing defense strategies
[
19
,
20
]
on explaining model behavior under constraints
[
24
]
. The PE module computes a privacy contribution ratio, which serves as input to the NI module. NI dynamically adjusts the noise injection amount accordingly and ensures precise noise injection for private information protection, achieving an optimal balance between privacy and model performance. FedSDP’s quantitative and highly interpretable theoretical indicator empowers each participant to protect the local model with greater clarity and confidence. Experimental results demonstrate FedSDP’s superiority over current methods across various tasks, datasets, and models while also exhibiting robust protection against private data reconstruction attacks
[
2
]
.

Contributions.
(1) We introduce FedSDP, a novel DP-based algorithm that safeguards the privacy of FL from the perspective of privacy contribution. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first approach that seamlessly integrates dynamic noise injection with attribute explainability. (2) We provide a theoretical analysis of the convergence of FedSDP, demonstrating that its upper bound under constrained conditions exhibits an exponential decay. (3) Our experimental results show that FedSDP outperforms SOTA defenses, achieving better model performance while injecting less noise. Furthermore, FedSDP demonstrates robustness against real-world private data reconstruction attacks, highlighting its superior protection capabilities and intuitive privacy guarantees.

II
Background and Problem Formulation

In this section, we first present the background information of FL, followed by the threat model applied to our approach.

II-A

Federated Learning

In an FL scenario, there exist a set of clients
\mathcal{C}=\{\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2},...,\mathcal{C}_{N}\}
and an aggregation server
S
. For a client
\mathcal{C}_{i}
, we follow the literature
[
26
]
and assume that it has a comprehensive dataset
\mathcal{D}_{i}
which can be divided into a general attribute dataset
\mathcal{D}^{u}_{i}
and a private attribute dataset
\mathcal{D}^{p}_{i}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{D}_{i}=\mathcal{D}_{i}^{p}\bigcup\mathcal{D}_{i}^{u}
. Furthermore, this client employs a neural network with a parameter
w_{i,e}^{t}
and an optimizer
\mathcal{G}
to train its local dataset, where
e
and
t
represent the number of local and global training epochs, respectively.
Upon completing
E
local training epochs, each active client uploads
w_{i}^{t}\triangleq w_{i,E}^{t}
to the server. The server then aggregates the collected data
[
1
]
once sufficient data (e.g., from
K
clients) is gathered, resulting in a new global model
w_{g}^{t}
, which is subsequently disseminated to the participants.
Consequently, we have
w_{i,0}^{t+1}=w_{g}^{t}
.

II-B

Threat Model

We assume the server is honest and benign, faithfully executing aggregation tasks and disseminating global models without any malicious intent. On the client side, we posit the presence of Honest-But-Curious (HBC) participants who seek to extract private information from other participants during FL. For other non-malicious clients, the DP protection scheme
is employed to safeguard their models from HBC participants.

Definition 1
(
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP)

Given a privacy budget
\epsilon
and a failure probability
\delta
, we say that a randomized algorithm
\mathcal{M}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{M})
is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP if, for any two adjacent datasets
\mathcal{D},\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
and for all
\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{M})
, we have

Pr[\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D})\in\mathcal{Y}]\leq e^{\epsilon}Pr[\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{Y}]+\delta,

(1)

where two datasets are said to be adjacent if they only differ by one data sample.

III
Methodology

In this section, we introduce the proposed FedSDP algorithm and theoretically analyze its convergence properties.

III-A

System Overview

Figure
2
) shows a schematic overview of FedSDP. Below, we explain the PE and NI modules of FedSDP in more detail.

Schematic overview of FedSDP.

The PE module (marked as ➀ and ➁ in Figure
2
) aims to determine privacy contribution. However, quantifying private information’s impact on the model during local training rounds is challenging.
Previous research has suggested using model weight norms to reflect embedded information
[
16
,
18
,
27
,
28
,
29
,
30
]
, but these approaches are flawed since weights contain both private and non-private information. Larger norms do not necessarily imply higher proportions of private information.
Other researchers have attempted to evaluate mutual information between private and general information from an information-theoretic perspective
[
31
,
26
]
. Due to the substantial computational overhead, a feasible approach in practice involves estimating the mutual information
[
31
]
, which diminishes model performance. An alternative strategy is training a dedicated neural network to compute mutual information
[
26
]
, but this approach yields solutions with limited interpretability.

To address this issue, we apply Shapley values, inspired by the prior work
[
24
]
, due to its optimal unique solution
[
24
]
for explaining the contribution of data attributes to model training under three constraints: 1) local accuracy, 2) missingness, and 3) consistency. We define the details of our solution below.

Definition 2
(Shapley value)

Given all features
\mathcal{F}
and all subsets of
\mathcal{F}
as
\mathcal{F}_{s}
, where
\mathcal{F}_{s}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
, a model
f^{\prime}
is trained with the presence of a feature
r
, while another model
f
is trained with the feature withheld. Then, the Shapley value of feature
r
is computed as a weighted average of all possible differences:

\psi_{r}=\sum_{\mathcal{F}_{s}\subseteq\mathcal{F}\backslash\{r\}}\frac{|\mathcal{F}_{s}|!(|\mathcal{F}|-|\mathcal{F}_{s}|-1)!}{|\mathcal{F}|!}[f^{\prime}(x^{\prime})-f(x)],

(2)

where
x
and
x^{\prime}
represent the values of the input features in the set
\mathcal{F}_{s}
and
\mathcal{F}_{s}\bigcup\{r\}
, respectively.

It can be observed that Shapley values effectively elucidate the contribution of a specific attribute during model training. In FedSDP, they accurately explain the contribution of privacy attributes in local training and thus help all participants make more informed decisions for dynamic noise injection to achieve an optimal privacy-utility trade-off.

During training, each client in FedSDP trains separate global models using its private dataset
\mathcal{D}_{i}^{p}
and general dataset
\mathcal{D}_{i}^{u}
, yielding two trained models:
w^{p}_{i}
(private) and
w^{u}_{i}
(general). We consider
f,f^{\prime}
in Equation
2
as the validation functions for the trained models in FedSDP, with
\mathcal{F}
encompassing general attributes
u
(i.e., those embedded in
\mathcal{D}_{i}^{u}
) and private attributes
p
(i.e., those embedded in
\mathcal{D}_{i}^{p}
).
The client then validates these models on its local validation set, obtaining contributions for private attributes
c^{p}_{i}\triangleq f_{\mathcal{D}_{i}^{p}}(\mathcal{D}_{i}^{p})
and general attributes
c^{u}_{i}\triangleq f_{\mathcal{D}_{i}^{u}}(\mathcal{D}_{i}^{u})
. The client also trains its local model using the entire local training dataset
\mathcal{D}
, obtaining a fully trained local model
w_{i}
and overall contribution
c_{i}\triangleq f_{\mathcal{D}_{i}}(\mathcal{D}_{i})
for this round.

Therefore,
we can calculate the Shapley values
\gamma^{p}_{i}
for private attributes using Equation
3
, which reflects the marginal contribution of private attributes to the model during one training epoch. A larger
\gamma^{p}_{i}
indicates that the private data has contributed more information to the model during the current round of training, reflecting its impact on the model.

\begin{split}\gamma^{p}_{i}&=\sum_{\mathcal{F}_{s}\subseteq\mathcal{F}\backslash\{p\}}\frac{|\mathcal{F}_{s}|!(|\mathcal{F}|-|\mathcal{F}_{s}|-1)!}{|\mathcal{F}|!}[f_{\mathcal{D}_{i}}(\mathcal{D}_{i}^{p})-f_{\mathcal{D}_{i}^{p}}(\mathcal{D}_{i}^{p})]\\
&=\frac{1}{2}(c_{i}-c_{i}^{u}+c_{i}^{p})\end{split}

(3)

We obtain the Shapley values
\gamma_{i}^{u}
for general attributes in the same manner as
\gamma^{p}_{i}
. Then, we derive the contribution ratio of private attributes as follows:

R_{i}^{p}=\frac{\gamma^{p}_{i}}{\gamma^{u}_{i}+\gamma^{p}_{i}}.

(4)

Noise Injection (NI).

The NI module (➂ in Figure
2
) determines the amount of noise injection based on private quantities obtained from the PE module. We calculate the privacy tolerance
T^{p}_{i}
of client
\mathcal{C}_{i}
using Equation
5
, where
\delta
represents the failure probability and
\alpha_{i}>0
is a hyper-parameter adjusting the evaluation criterion based on clients’ standard private contributions.
A higher value of
T^{p}_{i}
indicates that the client requires more privacy protection, necessitating increased noise injection.

T^{p}_{i}=R_{i}^{p}-\alpha_{i}\ln\delta

(5)

We select Gaussian mechanisms with a clipping bound
C

[
22
]
on weights to evaluate the sensitivity as
\Delta f_{i}=\frac{2C}{|\mathcal{D}_{i}|}
for the federated problem. Based on this setting, following each local training round, participants inject noise
n
, which follows a distribution
n\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{i}^{2})
, where
\sigma_{i}
satisfies:

\sigma_{i}=\frac{2C\sqrt{\max(T^{p}_{i},\beta_{i})}}{\epsilon|\mathcal{D}_{i}|}

(6)

In our design, the noise variance is positively correlated with the contribution
R_{i}^{p}
of private data in each training round, indicating that participants will inject more noise when their private data has a greater impact on the training process. We also employ a lower bound
\beta_{i}>0
to constrain the minimum amount of noise injection, ensuring a baseline level of privacy protection. Participants can set the hyper-parameter
\beta_{i}
based on their acceptable threshold for noise injection.

Theorem 1
(The privacy guarantee of FedSDP)

Given the privacy budget
\epsilon
, the failure probability
\delta
, and the clip bound of weight
C
, by leveraging Equation
5

and Equation
6
, FedSDP will provide an
(\epsilon,\frac{\delta\alpha_{i}}{2})
-DP guarantee. Specially, if
\alpha_{i}=2
, FedSDP offers a standard
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP guarantee. For clients with higher privacy tolerance (e.g.,
\alpha_{i}>\frac{2}{\delta}
), FedSDP provides an
(\epsilon,e^{\beta_{i}}\delta)
-DP guarantee as the basic protection.

III-B

Convergence Analysis

We make the following assumptions about the federated learning task:

(1)

F(x)
is
L
-smooth.
F(x)
satisfies:
F(x)\leq F(y)+(x-y)^{T}\nabla F(y)+\frac{L}{2}||y-x||^{2}
for
\forall x,y
with
L>0
.

(2)

F(x)
is
\rho
-weakly convex. Let
G(x)=F(x)+\frac{\rho}{2}||x||^{2}
with
\rho\geq 0
, then
G(x)
is convex. If
\rho\neq 0
,
F(x)
is not convex.

(3)

The expected squared norm of local stochastic gradients is uniformly bounded, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[||\nabla F_{i}(w_{i})||^{2}]\leq G_{c}^{2}
.

(4)

The expected squared norm of local noise is uniformly bounded, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[||n_{i}||^{2}]\leq N_{c}^{2}
.

Theorem 2
(The convergence of FedSDP)

Assuming the above assumptions are met, given the activation rate
\phi=\frac{K}{N}
and the maximum local training epoch
E
, if the learning rate
\eta
fulfills the condition
\eta<\frac{1}{4L}
, we derive the following convergence results for the noised global model
\tilde{w}_{g}^{t}
in FedSDP:

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[F(\tilde{w}_{g}^{t})]-F^{*}&\leq\frac{L\mathcal{U}^{t}}{2}||w_{g}^{0}-w^{*}||^{2}+(1-\phi)\mathcal{V}\\
&\quad+\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}(1-\mathcal{U})\mathcal{W}+\sum_{l=1}^{K}lLN_{c}^{2},\end{split}

(7)

where
F^{*}
represents the global optima,
\mathcal{U}=(1-\eta\rho)
,
\mathcal{V}=2L\eta^{2}E^{2}G_{c}^{2}
, and
\mathcal{W}=(6LG_{c}^{2}+8(E-1)^{2}G_{c}^{2})
.

Theorem
2
shows that the theoretical convergence upper bound diminishes as the global round
t
increases, given that
0<\mathcal{U}<1
. The activation rate
\phi
and noise scale
\sum_{l=1}^{K}lLN_{c}^{2}
collectively influence this theoretical upper bound.
An increase in
\phi
implies an increase in
K
, potentially leading to a substantial rise in the noise scale, accumulating from
1
to
K
in the global model. This underscores the significant impact of noise on overall performance.

V
Summary

In this paper, we proposed FedSDP, a novel and explainable DP-based FL mechanism for privacy protection. In FedSDP, each client evaluates the contribution of its own private attribute based on Shapley values and dynamically adjusts the amount of noise during each training round, thereby offering enhanced interpretability. Experimental results show that FedSDP outperforms SOTA solutions, achieving a superior trade-off between privacy and utility.
