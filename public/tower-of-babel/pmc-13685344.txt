GlycA-gene interactions in cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome: evidence from genetic association
Authors: Mingyang Yuan, Suwen Cao, Xing Wan, Yuting Yang, Siyu Fan, Jiang Xie, Han Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveCardiovascular-Kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome is a concept introduced by the American Heart Association, describing systemic diseases resulting from the pathophysiological interaction among obesity, stroke, type 2 diabetes, chronic kidney disease, and cardiovascular disease. Glycoprotein acetyls (GlycA) is a novel biomarker for systemic inflammation and cardiovascular risk. This study aims to uncover the shared genetic architecture between GlycA and CKM, identify common risk loci and key tissues, and clarify the mechanisms linking these conditions.MethodsUsing summary data from large-scale GWAS studies, we evaluated both global and local genetic correlations between GlycA and various components of CKM. A cross-trait pleiotropic analysis was conducted to identify shared pleiotropic loci and related genes. FUSION was used to find susceptibility genes linked to GlycA and CKM. A comprehensive set of functional annotations and tissue-specific analyses was performed to uncover potential connections between these traits. Furthermore, a multi-trait colocalization method was utilized to explore the underlying mechanisms of the association between GlycA and CKM.ResultsOur study revealed a close genetic correlation between GlycA and CKM. We identified 260 potential SNPs that were corrected using the Bonferroni method, of which 30 passed the colocalization test (PP.H4 > 0.70). Notably, locus 9q34.2 appeared in both GlycA-T2D and GlycA-HF trait pairs. By integrating post-GWAS methods, we identified 53 potentially drug targets (CMIP, DNAJC5G, FCGRT, etc.). FUSION analysis identified 23 shared susceptibility genes for GlycA and CKM (ZPR1, IL6R, PABPC4, etc.). Analysis of tissue enrichment at the SNP level revealed pleiotropic mechanisms associated with whole blood and liver. Gene-level analysis highlighted important roles of metabolic signaling pathways. Furthermore, multi-trait colocalization analysis suggested that rs1260326 and rs2925979 may play significant roles in the mechanistic link between GlycA and CKM.ConclusionOur study confirmed a significant genetic association and shared genetic structure between GlycA and CKM, revealing potential mechanisms that may be involved.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13098-026-02252-7.

Introduction

The incidence of cardiovascular, renal, and metabolic diseases has significantly increased, closely correlating with the aging population, lifestyle changes, and dietary habits. Metabolic diseases, including type 2 diabetes (T2D) and metabolic syndrome (Mets), along with cardiovascular diseases and chronic kidney disease (CKD), have emerged as the primary health threats to humanity [1]. Recent research has unveiled a comprehensive network of interactions among various systemic diseases. The American Heart Association has recently introduced the concept of cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome (CKM), highlighting the correlations among obesity, T2D, CKD, cardiovascular disease, heart failure (HF), and stroke [2]. The core features included the interaction of various pathological processes, such as an enhanced systemic inflammatory response, elevated levels of oxidative stress, reduced insulin sensitivity, and abnormal endothelial function, which collectively promoted the malignant development of multiple organ injury [3].

The nuclear magnetic resonance spectral signals of glycoprotein acetyls (GlycA) primarily originate from the mobile N-acetyl methyl protons present in the glycan moieties of acute phase proteins, such as α1-acid glycoprotein, haptoglobin, and α1-antitrypsin [4]. As a composite biomarker, GlycA may reflect the systemic acute phase response more effectively than individual glycoprotein components. Numerous studies have investigated the correlation between GlycA and other inflammatory molecules, such as high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP), revealing that GlycA is the most promising serum biomarker for systemic inflammation. Previous studies have confirmed that inflammatory processes associated with adipose tissue may contribute to elevated circulating levels of glycosylated acute-phase proteins [5]. And prior studies have suggested that GlycA may be a reliable biomarker of cardiometabolic risk [6]. In addition, GlycA was found to predict the development of T2DM in a PREVEND study [7].

Despite some advancements in CKM research, there remains a pressing need for new biomarkers to identify high-risk individuals and guide precision treatment. Investigating the association between GlycA and CKM can enhance our ability to identify and stratify individuals at risk for cardiovascular and metabolic diseases, and it may also serve as a prognostic indicator for the onset of chronic inflammatory diseases, such as CKD, in addition to predicting mortality risk. Nonetheless, the specific mechanisms by which GlycA functions in CKM and its relationships with various components are yet to be elucidated, necessitating further in-depth research to uncover its potential mechanisms of action and clinical significance. Current studies are predominantly observational, resulting in significant gaps in our understanding of the complex mechanisms and potential genetic connections among these traits. To date, no studies have comprehensively detailed their shared genetic mechanisms. This highlights the necessity to identify shared genetic structures and risk loci between GlycA and CKM components, as well as the imperative to develop drug targets of druggable genes.

In this study, we primarily employed the newly developed genome-wide association study (GWAS) to investigate the genetic association between GlycA and CKM components. We introduce high-definition likelihood (HDL) based on GWAS data, along with local analysis of the (co) variant association (LAVA) method [8, 9]. These tools were employed to ascertain both global and local genetic correlations among multiple traits. Cross-trait analysis using GWAS data can accurately identify shared loci. Additionally, FUSION methods were applied to identify susceptibility genes associated with GlycA and CKM. A comprehensive series of functional annotations and tissue-specific analyses were performed to elucidate potential associations between these traits. Moreover, multi-trait colocalization approaches were implemented to investigate the underlying mechanisms linking GlycA and CKM. Methods

GWAS summary data source

The summary statistics from GWAS studies for CKM were obtained from large-scale GWAS or GWAS meta-analyses, and all these data were publicly accessible. Specifically, the included conditions were coronary heart disease [10], stroke [11], T2D [12], HF [13], and CKD [14]. Furthermore, we chose the most recent research findings regarding obesity from FinnGen. FinnGen functions as an extensive biomedical database that includes 500,000 biological samples from Finland, integrating whole-genome sequencing information with electronic health records to investigate how genetic variation influences disease mechanisms [15]. Summary GWAS statistics related to GlycA were sourced from the UK Biobank (UKB). This study encompassed the summary GWAS data for GlycA derived from all individuals of European ancestry published by UKB in 2020, which included a total of 115,078 participants of European heritage [16]. UKB, a significant biomedical resource, signifies a prospective cohort study. Information was gathered from 500,000 volunteers in the UK, aged between 40 and 69, encompassing a wide range of biomedical data. The comprehensive details of these datasets are presented in Additional file 2: Table S1.

To guarantee the precision and dependability of all GWAS data, this research emphasized enhancements in methodology designed to mitigate potential disruptions caused by rare variants. In particular, by implementing stringent criteria for minor allele frequency (MAF) filtering, it was required that MAF surpassed the threshold of 0.01, thereby effectively minimizing the influence of rare variants on the outcomes of the analysis. This selection approach shifted the research emphasis primarily toward common variants, which not only considerably boosted the effectiveness of statistical assessments but also significantly lowered the likelihood of false positive findings. The utilization of this technique established a more trustworthy framework for GWAS research and contributed to the enhancement of the validity of subsequent association analysis outcomes.

Genetic correlation

To investigate the genetic relationship between GlycA and various components of CKM, this research utilized linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC) for its analysis [17]. The LD score was developed using common SNP data derived from a European population sample (1KG EUR) within the framework of the 1000 Genomes Project during the calculations. In order to address attenuation bias, we applied a standard error correction while evaluating possible overlaps between traits through the intercept [18]. In order to validate the outcomes obtained from LDSC, we implemented HDL. In contrast to LDSC, this method utilized GWAS summary data more effectively and decreased the variance in genetic correlation estimation by approximately 60%, thereby enhancing the accuracy of the analysis [8].

Moreover, we evaluated the local genetic correlations involving GlycA and six components of CKM utilizing LAVA. This framework, LAVA, provided a robust approach for the systematic examination of 2495 independent LD blocks. The significance was assessed utilizing a threshold of 0.05 for the FDR-corrected p-value. This approach successfully reduced the biases caused by confounding elements, including assortative mating across traits, and precisely identified pleiotropic genetic loci present in the genome [9].

Assessment across gene, tissue, organ, and pathway dimensions

In this research, we employed stratified LDSC (S-LDSC) to thoroughly assess the heritable enrichment of GlycA and CKM across various tissues and cell types [19]. By evaluating the P-values associated with the Z-statistic and the regression coefficients, we successfully quantified the distribution characteristics of heritability for these components. To enhance our understanding of the shared mechanisms among loci, we associated neighboring genes with the leading SNP, offering significant insights into the regulatory networks governing these loci.

In addition, to clarify the shared mechanisms involved in identifying loci, we mapped adjacent genes based on the leading SNP within each locus and conducted a comprehensive multi-marker effect analysis of GWAS data by employing the multi-marker analysis of genome annotation (MAGMA) to assess the biological functions of these pleiotropic loci. Specifically, by effectively integrating LD into the markers, we uncovered pleiotropic genes and identified notable multi-marker effects [20]. To guarantee the statistical robustness of our results, we implemented Bonferroni correction to adjust the gene set, thereby reducing the likelihood of false positive outcomes [21]. This approach not only uncovered a deeper understanding of the heritability enrichment of GlycA and CKM components but also offered novel perspectives on the biological mechanisms underlying pleiotropic loci. Additionally, the integrity of the research conclusions was upheld through rigorous multiple test corrections.

Cross-trait analysis

A novel pleiotropy analysis under the null hypothesis (PLACO) method was employed to systematically uncover genetic associations between GlycA and CKM components at the SNP level. This statistical approach is specifically tailored to detect pleiotropy, facilitating the identification of genetic variants that are shared across different phenotypes. We focus on SNPs that attain genome-wide significance thresholds (P < 5 × 10− 8), which are characterized as pleiotropic variants, indicating that these SNPs have strong genetic correlations with multiple traits. Additionally, to validate the biological significance of these pleiotropic SNPs, we applied the FUMA tool to map them to specific genomic regions, which may help elucidate the potential functions of these SNPs [22]. Lastly, we conducted a Bayesian colocalization analysis to uncover risk loci that are common to these traits [23].

Summary-data-based Mendelian randomization (SMR)

The SMR integrates aggregated data from GWAS with eQTL information, centering around the concept of identifying the gene expression levels linked to complex traits influenced by pleiotropy [24]. The dependent heterogeneity instrument (HEIDI) further investigated whether this correlation may arise from colocalization, meaning whether the impact of SNPs on gene expression and complex traits originates from the same causal variation [25]. HEIDI serves as a crucial foundation for comprehensively understanding the genetic mechanisms behind complex traits. Through the SMR method, the regulatory mechanisms connecting genetic variation to the phenotype associations of GlycA and CKM component-related genes can be elucidated. These results not only enhance our comprehension of the genetic underpinnings of complex traits but also offer significant insights for future target discovery and drug development.

Multi-trait colocalization analysis

In this investigation, the method known as hypothesis prioritization for multi-trait colocalization (HyPrColoc) was employed to perform an extensive analysis of multi-trait colocalization. Our primary focus was on prioritizing hypotheses to pinpoint co-localized loci and identify potential causal variants [26]. HyPrColoc functions as a robust Bayesian split clustering algorithm that effectively ranks and groups colocalized traits by utilizing the prior probabilities associated with each phenotype. Throughout the analysis, we categorized the pertinent traits into clusters and computed posterior probabilities (PP) for the co-localized loci in each category. A locus was deemed to exhibit significant colocalization when its posterior probability exceeded 0.6 [27].

Transcriptome-wide association studies (TWAS)

TWAS employs a comprehensive approach that combines gene expression data with GWAS data to enhance the identification of genes related to disease susceptibility [28]. During the specific implementation phase, we incorporated FUSION, an automated analytical tool capable of efficiently handling the relationship analysis between gene expression and phenotypic data. This tool also integrates summary data from GWAS, allowing for a thorough multi-omics analysis that offers vital insights for functional validation and drug target identification [29]. This integrated analysis method overcomes the limitations of GWAS research and provides strong support for an in-depth understanding of the molecular mechanisms of diseases and the development of novel therapeutic strategies.

Results

Genetic correlation

We evaluated the genetic correlation between GlycA and different components of CKM. The data presented in the table demonstrated a strong alignment between the findings from LDSC and HDL (Additional file 1: Fig. S1; Additional file 2: Table S2). Specifically, we discovered six traits that exhibited a genetic correlation with GlycA through LDSC: HF, CHD, stroke, T2D, obesity, and CKD, all showing P values below 0.05. Furthermore, using HDL, we observed that GlycA also displayed significant global genetic correlations with these six traits, confirming a positive correlation across all of them. It was important that the genetic correlations maintained their significance even after the application of FDR and Bonferroni corrections.

LAVA was employed to investigate the local genetic correlations between GlycA and different components of CKM. Our analysis revealed notable genetic correlations between GlycA and CKM across 257 regions. Particularly noteworthy was the discovery of a strong genetic correlation between GlycA and CKD/stroke spanning chromosomes 1 through 22. Furthermore, a significant genetic correlation between GlycA and HF was observed, with the exception of chromosome 5. LAVA further confirmed the results of LDSC and HDL (Additional file 1: Fig. S2 and Fig. S3; Additional file 2: Table S3).

Assessment across SNP, gene, and pathway

Our study explored whether the heritability attributed to SNPs for each component of CKM and GlycA was enhanced in particular cells and tissues by utilizing the S-LDSC method. This was evaluated through the regression coefficient Z-score and the related P-value. The analysis focused on specific tissues and revealed that the SNP loci associated with GlycA were predominantly concentrated in the liver and whole blood. Interestingly, CKD and T2D also exhibited significant enrichment in the liver (P_CKD = 0.047; P_T2D = 0.044) (Fig. 2A; Additional file 2: Table S4).

The MAGMA gene enrichment analysis conducted using the FUMA tool revealed that the specific genes examined are integral components of crucial biological pathways, potentially influencing regulatory mechanisms associated with metabolic signal transduction. Notably, this had encompassed pathways related to lipid metabolism, cell proliferation and differentiation, as well as the TGF-βsignaling cascade. In total, this analysis identified a total of 15 significantly enriched genes (p < 0.05), with Bonferroni-corrected p < 0.05 for 8 gene-sets (Fig. 2B; Additional file 2: Table S5). Moreover, a more detailed tissue-specific analysis had indicated that these genes had primarily been enriched in 12 essential tissues or organs, including the liver, tibial artery, aorta, cervix, endocervix, and uterus, among others (Fig. 2C; Additional file 2: Table S6). Additionally, the gene sets associated with the GlycA-T2D/HF/stroke trait pair had exhibited significant enrichment specifically in the liver.

Identification of risk loci

By employing the PLACO approach, we uncovered 260 potential pleiotropic loci associated with GlycA across six different diseases. Following this, we utilized FUMA to identify 99 pleiotropic risk loci that were common (Fig. 3; Additional file 1: Fig. S4 and Fig. S5; Additional file 2: Table S7). Bayesian colocalization analysis revealed that 30 of these loci achieved PP.H4 values greater than 0.70. Furthermore, we discovered that at 13 specific loci, the PP.H4 values exceeded 0.96, including loci such as rs9295128, rs6455698, and rs4646272, among others. Importantly, the locus rs2068888 was identified as being common to both GlycA and stroke/CHD. We found that GlycA had 4 potential loci with HF (rs9295128, rs9411378, rs10846742 and rs11858836) with PP.H4 values exceeding 0.78 and delineated a regional map of each colocalized locus for this trait pair (Figs. 4 and 5). For further information, refer to the Additional file 1: Fig. S6 – S9, which includes the colocalization locus region maps for the other trait pairs. In addition to this, we found that genomic regions 4p16. 3, 8p21. 3, 16q22. 2 and 19q13. 33 were shared in GlycA and stroke/T2D/HF trait pairs. GlycA-T2D and GlycA-HF are all located on 9q34. 2, and its adjacent genes were GBGT1, SURF6, MED22 (Additional file 2: Table S8).

Drug targets of druggable genes

To filter and prioritize potential drug targets within complex genetic signals, we established a rigorous multi-step analytical framework. Initially, we identified candidate susceptibility genes by integrating the preliminary findings from polygenic analysis PLACO and FUMA. To clarify the functional relevance of these genes and enhance the robustness of our results, we employed summary data-based Mendelian Randomization (SMR) analysis, and cross-validated the expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) mappings from the eQTLGen and GTEx v8 (whole blood) databases, ultimately identifying reliable potential drug targets. Based on the aforementioned integrative strategy, we identified 53 genes that exhibited significant associations with a range of traits (Table 1 and Additional file 1: Fig. S10). To systematically evaluate and quantify the reliability of these targets, we categorized their priority into four levels (Level 1 to Level 4) based on the frequency with which these 53 genes were identified through various analytical methods. Genes that received the highest level of support from all methodologies were classified as the highest priority Level 1, specifically including DOCK7, FCGRT, HSPA4, and RCN3, which represent the most promising core drug targets

Multi‑trait colocalization analysis

We conducted a multi-trait colocalization analysis utilizing HyPrColoc to explore potential mechanisms that connect GlycA and CKM. The results of our analysis uncovered five pleiotropic loci: rs1260326, rs2925979, rs112875651, rs1332328, and rs3843467 (Additional file 2: Table S9). Each of these loci demonstrated a posterior probability exceeding 0.6, indicating a significant likelihood of their involvement in the traits under investigation. Altogether, these findings strongly suggest that GlycA may influence the development of T2D and CHD through various metabolic regulatory pathways.

TWAS

We evaluated the connection between gene expression predicted by genetic factors and disease risk through FUSION, an analysis that enhanced the understanding of common risk loci for seven diseases that GWAS had not identified. FUSION revealed 137 notable susceptibility genes (Additional file 1: Fig. S11-S12; Additional file 2: Table S10). In particular, we identified 52 susceptibility genes related to GlycA, which were illustrated in a Venn diagram (Fig. 6). In these 137 associations, EIF3C, PSRC1, and RCC1L exhibit cross-phenotype overlap. EIF3C is the only gene that demonstrates significant pleiotropy in both GlycA and obesity. In addition, we used the results of the local genetic correlation analysis method LAVA as input data for FUSION. Specifically, we extracted the significant regions identified by LAVA, we found 23 overlapping susceptibility genes, such as ZPR1, ATG4C, and EIF3C, among others (Additional file 2: Table S11). By integrating these findings with prior GWAS results, we discovered that GlycA shared IL6R with HF, CHD, and obesity. Furthermore, GlycA had shared PABPC4 with obesity, T2D, and CKD. Lastly, we presented the association comparison plots for LDSC, LAVA, and TWAS (Additional file 1: Fig. S13).

Discussion

In this research, comprehensive genetic approaches were employed to identify the genetic correlation and pleiotropic loci linking GlycA with CKM components, while also providing insights into the underlying mechanisms of this. Initially, we identified genetic correlation between GlycA and CKM components utilizing the LDSC approach, with findings that closely aligned with those derived from the HDL method. LAVA demonstrated local genetic correlations. Through the application of the PLACO, we discovered 260 potential pleiotropic loci connected to GlycA across six diseases, with Bayesian colocalization analysis revealing that 30 of these loci exhibited PP.H4 values exceeding 0.70. Additionally, it was found that rs2068888 is common to both GlycA and stroke/CHD. Moreover, the genomic regions 4p16.3, 8p21.3, 16q22.2, and 19q13.33 were identified as shared among GlycA and stroke/T2D/HF trait pairs. The S-LDSC enrichment analysis revealed that these SNPs were predominantly enriched in liver and whole blood. The MAGMA gene enrichment analysis indicated that these specific genes are integral components of crucial biological pathways, potentially influencing lipid metabolism, cell proliferation, and differentiation, as well as the TGF-β signaling pathway. By integrating FUMA PLACO and SMR analyses, we identified 53 potential drug targets. Furthermore, we evaluated the relationship between genetically predicted gene expression and disease risk using FUSION, identifying 137 significant susceptibility genes. This analysis not only supplemented the shared susceptibility genes of seven diseases that were not identified by GWAS but also further validated previously identified genes, such as IL6R and PABPC4.

Genetic correlation analysis showed that there was significant genetic overlap between GlycA and HF, CHD, CKD, obesity, stroke, and T2D; all six trait pairs were corrected by FDR and Bonferroni. GlycA serves as a biomarker for systemic inflammation, and elevated levels of GlycA during both acute and chronic inflammation can quantify the concentration and glycosylation status of various acute-phase proteins. Previous studies have established a correlation between GlycA and atherosclerosis, as well as cardiovascular disease [30]. Epidemiological evidence has preliminarily established a link between GlycA and T2D, with researchers measuring GlycA levels in 4,524 patients over an average follow-up period of 7.3 years. After adjusting for relevant covariates, GlycA is associated with T2D events, positioning it as a potential novel biomarker for predicting the progression of T2D [7]. Furthermore, studies indicated that GlycA was correlated with an elevated risk of various types of HF, particularly heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) [31]. Although there are few studies on GlycA levels and stroke, our genetic correlation analysis addresses this gap. We identified not only significant global genetic associations between GlycA and CKM components but also significant local genetic associations between GlycA and CKM in 257 regions. Notably, we observed a significant genetic correlation between GlycA and CKD/stroke across chromosomes 1 to 22. Furthermore, a significant genetic correlation exists between GlycA and HF, except on chromosome 5.

We employed the PLACO method to identify a series of genetic risk loci that may simultaneously influence these traits. Several loci were identified in multiple phenotypic pairs, including rs2068888, rs6717858, rs3936511, and rs9295128. Prior studies have demonstrated that some of these loci play critical roles in GlycA, stroke, T2D, and HF. For instance, it has been established that rs2068888 (localization gene: CYP26A1) is associated with blood lipids. Our results further suggest that CYP26A1 is implicated in the relationship between GlycA and stroke [32]. Additionally, rs6717858 (localization gene: COBLL1) has been linked to insulin resistance, with the polymorphism rs7607980 in the COBLL1 resulting in the substitution of cytosine with thymine. This polymorphism has been documented in several studies involving patients with T2D and Mets [33]. Previous GWAS studies have indicated that rs3936511 is associated with Mets, while our findings revealed a PP.H4 of 0.98 for the GlycA-T2D trait pair [34]. Furthermore, rs9295128 (localization gene: LPA) indicated that the level of circulating lipoprotein (a) was predominantly genetically determined by various genetic variations within the LPA gene. Lipoprotein (a) has been identified as a distinct genetic risk factor and a possible pathogenic contributor to conditions such as myocardial infarction, stroke, peripheral artery disease, and calcific aortic stenosis [35]. These findings underscored the involvement of these genes in the association between GlycA and CKM. We identified 11 genetic regions significantly associated with GlycA and CKM; for instance, GlycA and T2D/HF are both associated with the 9q34.2 locus. Furthermore, we mapped the adjacent genes GBGT1, SURF6, and MED22. The protein encoded by the GBGT1 gene is a glycosyltransferase, primarily responsible for transferring N-acetylgalactosamine onto globotriaosylceramide, thereby forming new sugar chain structures essential for cell recognition and signaling on the cell surface [36]. There is a limited amount of existing research on SURF6 and MED22. It is known that SURF6 is involved in the processing of ribosomal RNA, the assembly of ribosomal subunits, and other processes, and has important significance for cell proliferation and protein synthesis, which may affect the growth, differentiation, and repair of cardiomyocytes [37]. MED22 primarily regulates the transcriptional processes of genes. Our findings enhance the understanding of the potential functions of these genes about GlycA and CKM, from a genetic perspective.

Through the S-LDSC, we identified significant gene enrichment in whole blood and liver, indicating that regulatory elements in these tissues play crucial roles in phenotypic expression. The enrichment of SNPs in whole blood and liver underscores the pivotal function of GlycA in T2D and CKD. GlycA-related SNPs in blood and liver can influence spleen, adipose tissue, pancreas, and vascular functions through the circulatory system [38]. Furthermore, MAGMA performed gene-level association analysis, the results further reflected the mechanism shared between GlycA and CKM, particularly in lipid metabolism, cell proliferation, and differentiation, and the TGF-β signaling pathway. The worsening inflammation, as indicated by elevated GlycA levels, is associated with higher HDL and reduced cholesterol efflux. Impaired cholesterol efflux may elucidate some associations between GlycA and cardiovascular risk events [38]. Additionally, further tissue-specific analyses revealed that these genomes are predominantly enriched in 12 key tissues or organs, including the liver, tibial artery, aorta, cervix, endocervix, and uterus, suggesting that key genes in these tissues may influence phenotypic expression through the encoding of proteins or regulatory pathways.

We integrated PLACO, FUMA, MAGMA, eQTL, and SMR analyses to identify 53 potential drug target genes. Specifically, genes related to GlycA and T2D (such as APOA4, CHST4, FCGRT, and CMIP etc.), as well as genes related to GlycA and stroke (CMIP, DOCK7, FLT3LG, etc.), GlycA and HF (FAF1, RPL13A, TRPS1, etc.). Most of these genes have been previously identified in GWAS studies and are associated with liposomal and metabolic signatures (e.g., DOCK7, APOA4); some are also related to immunity (e.g., CMIP, FLT3LG). GWAS studies indicate that DOCK7 is closely associated with metabolic diseases, blood lipids, and liposome metabolism, and it has been identified as a risk gene for GlycA [16]. ApoA4 levels showed a positive correlation with both blood urea nitrogen and creatinine, whereas a negative correlation was observed with serum protein and albumin. This suggests that serum Con A-bound ApoA4 could act as a circulating biomarker for forecasting the advancement of kidney dysfunction in individuals suffering from diabetic nephropathy [39]. Gene polymorphisms of the C-Maf inducible protein (CMIP) have been reported to be associated with T2D [40]. Furthermore, CMIP, as a novel adaptor protein, facilitates the initial phenotype of T cells by selectively targeting the expression of transgenes. This represents a new mechanism for the negative regulation of T cells, which may serve as a therapeutic target [41]. Additionally, Fms-related receptor tyrosine kinase 3 ligand (FLT3LG) can directly influence CD8 T cells, promoting their proliferation and activation. This finding offers a new perspective for drug research and development based on immune mechanisms [42].

We identified five pleiotropic SNPs (rs1260326, rs2925979, rs112875651, rs1332328, and rs3843467) using HyPrColoc. A notable example is the common non-synonymous mutation in the glucokinase regulatory factor (GCKR) gene, specifically rs1260326, which has been extensively documented to exert pleiotropic effects on cardiometabolic and hematological parameters [43]. Interestingly, the rs2925979 (mapping gene: CMIP) shows an inverse association with T2D and obesity-related phenotypes, indicating that CMIP may have distinct pleiotropic effects on female T2D and obesity-related traits [40]. These findings suggest that GlycA may influence T2D and CHD through metabolic regulatory pathways.

We identified 23 shared susceptibility genes between GlycA and CKM using FUSION. By integrating the FUSION results with those from previous GWAS, we discovered that GlycA shares the risk gene IL6R with HF, CHD, and obesity. Previous studies suggest that targeting IL-6 signaling through IL-6 or IL-6R blockade may confer potential cardiovascular benefits, positioning IL-6R as a prominent target for clinical intervention [44]. Furthermore, we identified that GlycA also shares the risk gene PABPC4 with T2D, obesity, and CKD, among others. Knockdown experiments have demonstrated that poly (A)-binding protein 4 (PABPC4) plays a role in regulating adipocyte differentiation and energy metabolism [45]. This finding further reinforces the notion that lipid metabolism is a key mechanism linking GlycA and CKM.

When interpreting the results of this study, several limitations should be taken into account. Since the data primarily originates from individuals of European ancestry, the generalizability of the identified genetic targets in other ethnic groups remains to be further validated. From a methodological perspective, although this study strictly adhered to Bayesian colocalization analysis and the HEIDI test to distinguish shared causal variants from linkage disequilibrium signals, such summary data-based analytical frameworks still cannot completely rule out the possibility of residual horizontal pleiotropy. In addition, the stepwise TWAS (FUSION) strategy employed in this study has a specific analytical background. While the global screening successfully established EIF3C as a core anchor connecting GlycA and obesity among 137 susceptibility genes, the subsequent identification of 23 overlapping susceptibility genes (such as ZPR1, ATG4C, and EIF3C) is conditionally reliant on the significant local regions pre-screened by LAVA analysis. Although this targeted strategy effectively highlights regional co-localization signals, it is inherently exploratory and may be sensitive to local linkage disequilibrium structures. Since the findings of this study are fundamentally based on computation, translating these high-confidence candidate genes into clinical relevance still requires explicit functional validation. In the future, it is necessary to utilize in vitro or in vivo models (such as CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing and single-cell sequencing technology) to further elucidate the precise molecular pathways through which these genetic targets mediate the interaction between GlycA and CKM components.

Conclusion

This study enhances our understanding of the role of GlycA in CKM. Our findings reveal significant global and local genetic correlations, as well as shared genetic architecture between GlycA and the components of CKM. The prominent role of genetic factors was highlighted by identifying common genetic regions, such as 9q34.2. Additionally, we identified 53 potentially drug targets of druggable genes. Further analysis of genetic correlations and multiple colocalization, including rs1260326 and rs2925979, underscores the potential metabolic common mechanisms that underlie the interconnection between these factors.
