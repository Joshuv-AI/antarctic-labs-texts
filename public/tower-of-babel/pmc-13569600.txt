Association of Cachexia-Related Status With Treatment Outcomes in Advanced Gastric Cancer Receiving First-Line Therapy With or Without Nivolumab
Authors: Hironori Fujii, Daichi Watanabe, Yunami Yamada, Itaru Yasufuku, Wakana Chikaishi, Akitaka Makiyama, Atsuko Hayakawa, Mika Kitahora, Atsuko Tomida, Hirotoshi Iihara, Ryo Kobayashi, Nobuhisa Matsuhashi, Akio Suzuki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe clinical significance of longitudinal cachexia-related status during first-line therapy for advanced gastric cancer remains unclear in the era of nivolumab-containing chemotherapy. We evaluated its association with outcomes according to nivolumab use.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 121 patients with HER2-negative unresectable advanced or recurrent gastric cancer who received first-line oxaliplatin- plus fluoropyrimidine-based chemotherapy between 2017 and 2024. Modified Glasgow Prognostic Score (mGPS) 2 status and modified European Palliative Care Research Collaborative (EPCRC)-defined cachexia status were assessed as time-dependent covariates. Progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) were analyzed using time-dependent Cox proportional hazards models.ResultsThe cohort included 69 patients in the non-nivolumab group and 52 in the nivolumab-containing group. Median age was 71 years, 76 patients (63%) were male, and baseline mGPS 2 was present in 37 patients (31%). The cumulative proportion of patients who met the mGPS 2 criterion by 6 months was 55% in the non-nivolumab group and 57% in the nivolumab-containing group. Time-dependent mGPS 2 status was not significantly associated with PFS (hazard ratio [HR], 1.49; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.69–3.20; P = 0.30), but was associated with shorter OS (HR, 2.17; 95% CI, 1.15–4.10; P = 0.017). No statistically significant interaction between time-dependent mGPS 2 status and nivolumab use was observed for PFS or OS. Time-dependent modified EPCRC-defined cachexia status was not significantly associated with PFS or OS.ConclusionsTime-dependent mGPS 2 status during first-line therapy was associated with shorter OS, but not PFS.Keywords: Gastric cancer, Cachexia, Modified glasgow prognostic score, Nivolumab, Prognosis

Introduction

Advanced gastric cancer remains a disease with a poor prognosis, and optimization of first-line systemic therapy is essential to improve survival outcomes. In recent years, combination chemotherapy with nivolumab has become one of the standard first-line treatment options for patients with advanced gastric cancer and gastroesophageal junction adenocarcinoma. However, substantial interpatient variability in treatment benefit has been observed [1, 2], highlighting the need to identify clinical factors associated with therapeutic outcomes.

According to the international consensus proposed by the European Palliative Care Research Collaborative (EPCRC), cancer cachexia is a multifactorial syndrome characterized by an ongoing loss of skeletal muscle mass (with or without loss of fat mass) that cannot be fully reversed by conventional nutritional support and leads to progressive functional impairment [3]. The prevalence of cachexia has been reported to be approximately 50% in patients with gastrointestinal cancers [4] and to exceed 70% in those with advanced gastric cancer [5], indicating that it is highly prevalent in this population. Moreover, cancer cachexia is not only associated with poor prognosis but also with an increased incidence of chemotherapy-related adverse events [6, 7], making it a critical clinical issue in the management of advanced gastric cancer.

The modified Glasgow Prognostic Score (mGPS), which is based on C-reactive protein and serum albumin levels, is a well-established marker reflecting systemic inflammation and nutritional status, and has been shown to have prognostic value in patients with gastric cancer and other gastrointestinal malignancies [8, 9]. A post hoc analysis of the phase III EXPAND trial demonstrated that baseline mGPS was a strong independent predictor of overall survival (OS) in patients with advanced gastric and gastroesophageal junction cancers [10], whereas muscle-related parameters reflecting sarcopenia did not show a direct causal association with survival. These findings suggest that markers reflecting systemic inflammation and metabolic derangements may be important not only for disease characterization but also for treatment stratification.

Previous studies have suggested that pretreatment cachexia and impaired inflammatory or nutritional status are associated with poor clinical outcomes in cancer patients, including those receiving immune checkpoint inhibitors [11, 12]. However, most of these studies have focused on baseline conditions, and the clinical significance of longitudinal cachexia-related status during first-line treatment remains unclear. In particular, although cachexia-related inflammation and metabolic alterations may influence antitumor immunity, their impact on nivolumab-containing regimens has not been fully elucidated.

Therefore, we conducted this retrospective cohort study to evaluate the association between longitudinal cachexia-related status and survival outcomes during first-line therapy for advanced gastric cancer in the era of nivolumab-containing chemotherapy. Using time-dependent Cox models, we evaluated whether mGPS 2 status and modified EPCRC-defined cachexia status were associated with survival outcomes, with particular focus on potential differences according to nivolumab use.

Patients and Methods

Study Design and Patients

This was a single-center retrospective cohort study conducted at Gifu University Hospital. We reviewed consecutive patients with HER2-negative unresectable advanced or recurrent gastric cancer who received first-line oxaliplatin plus fluoropyrimidine-based chemotherapy between January 2017 and December 2024. Patients who started treatment before the approval of nivolumab for first-line gastric cancer were assigned to the non-nivolumab group, whereas those who started treatment after approval and received nivolumab in combination with chemotherapy were assigned to the nivolumab-containing group. Because nivolumab became available only after regulatory approval, treatment group assignment was largely determined by calendar period rather than randomization or physician preference alone. Patients were excluded if they lacked the clinical or laboratory data required for the time-dependent analyses.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Gifu University Graduate School of Medicine (approval number 2024 − 314) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments.

Data Collection

Clinical data were collected retrospectively from electronic medical records. Baseline variables included age, sex, body weight, body mass index, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, disease status at baseline (unresectable advanced or recurrent disease), and chemotherapy regimen. Body weight and laboratory data, including serum C-reactive protein and albumin levels, were collected before treatment initiation and before each treatment cycle during first-line therapy.

Assessment of Cachexia-Related Status

Cachexia-related status was assessed using two measures: mGPS 2 and modified EPCRC-defined cachexia. mGPS was classified as 0, 1, or 2 according to serum C-reactive protein and albumin levels, as previously described [8]. Briefly, mGPS was defined as 0 when C-reactive protein was ≤ 1.0 mg/dL, 1 when C-reactive protein was > 1.0 mg/dL and albumin was ≥ 3.5 g/dL, and 2 when C-reactive protein was > 1.0 mg/dL and albumin was < 3.5 g/dL. mGPS 2 was considered to represent an inflammation- and nutrition-related status.

Modified EPCRC-defined cachexia was defined as weight loss > 5%, or weight loss > 2% in patients with a body mass index < 20 kg/m². The original EPCRC definition also includes weight loss > 2% in the presence of sarcopenia; however, standardized radiological assessment of skeletal muscle mass was not routinely available at all relevant time points in this retrospective cohort. Therefore, the sarcopenia component was not applied, and to distinguish this operational definition from the original EPCRC definition, it is referred to as “modified EPCRC-defined cachexia” throughout the manuscript. For the baseline assessment of modified EPCRC-defined cachexia, body weight at the patient’s first visit to our hospital was used as the reference body weight for calculating weight loss.

Both mGPS 2 status and modified EPCRC-defined cachexia status were treated as time-dependent covariates. Patients who met the relevant criterion at baseline were classified as exposed from treatment initiation. Patients who did not meet the criterion at baseline were classified as unexposed until the first assessment at which the criterion was met and as exposed thereafter. Once classified as exposed, patients remained in the exposed category thereafter, even if the criterion was no longer met at subsequent assessments. Thus, at each event time, the hazard was compared between patients who had and had not met the relevant criterion by that time. Body weight and laboratory data were updated only during first-line treatment. After discontinuation of first-line treatment, the most recently determined exposure status was retained for the OS analysis until death or censoring.

Outcomes

The primary endpoint was PFS. Secondary endpoints were OS and the cumulative proportion of patients meeting the cachexia-related criteria during treatment.

PFS was defined as the time from initiation of first-line treatment to disease progression or death from any cause, whichever occurred first. OS was defined as the time from initiation of first-line treatment to death from any cause. Patients without an event were censored at the date of last follow-up.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized descriptively according to treatment group. The cumulative proportion of patients meeting each cachexia-related criterion was estimated using the cumulative incidence function separately in the non-nivolumab and nivolumab-containing groups, with patients meeting the criterion at baseline classified as having met it from treatment initiation. Death before fulfillment of the relevant criterion was treated as a competing event, whereas patients who discontinued first-line treatment or were lost to follow-up before meeting the criterion were censored at the date of their last evaluable assessment. The corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated at 6 and 12 months after treatment initiation. To evaluate the association between time-dependent cachexia-related status and survival outcomes while minimizing immortal time bias, time-dependent Cox proportional hazards models were used. mGPS 2 status and modified EPCRC-defined cachexia status were analyzed separately as time-dependent covariates, and an interaction term between each time-dependent status and treatment group was included in each model to assess whether the prognostic impact differed according to nivolumab use. Multivariable models were adjusted for age, sex, and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), which were selected on the basis of clinical relevance and the limited sample size to reduce the risk of overfitting. For visual presentation, Simon–Makuch plots according to time-dependent mGPS 2 and modified EPCRC-defined cachexia were generated separately in each treatment group, and hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs were estimated using Cox proportional hazards models within each group. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R version 4.2.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Patient Characteristics

A total of 121 patients with HER2-negative unresectable advanced or recurrent gastric cancer were included in the study. Of these, 69 patients were assigned to the non-nivolumab group and 52 to the nivolumab-containing group. Median age was 68 years (interquartile range [IQR], 62–77) in the non-nivolumab group and 73 years (IQR, 62–78) in the nivolumab-containing group. Male patients accounted for 61% and 65% of each group, respectively. Baseline body weight, body mass index, albumin, and C-reactive protein were generally comparable between the groups. Most patients had good performance status, with ECOG performance status 0–1 in approximately 94% of the non-nivolumab group and 93% of the nivolumab-containing group. At baseline, mGPS 2 was present in 31% of patients in both groups (Table 1).

Cumulative Proportion of Patients Meeting Cachexia-Related Criteria

The cumulative proportions of patients meeting the cachexia-related criteria were similar between the two treatment groups. The proportion of patients who had met the mGPS 2 criterion by 6 months was 55% (95% CI, 40%–68%) in the non-nivolumab group and 57% (95% CI, 41%–70%) in the nivolumab-containing group. At 12 months, the corresponding proportions were 55% (95% CI, 40%–68%) and 61% (95% CI, 43%–75%), respectively. The proportion of patients who had met the modified EPCRC-defined cachexia criterion by 6 months was 57% (95% CI, 37%–72%) in the non-nivolumab group and 62% (95% CI, 44%–76%) in the nivolumab-containing group. At 12 months, the corresponding proportions were 77% (95% CI, 37%–93%) and 80% (95% CI, 57%–92%), respectively (Table 2).

Association Between Time-Dependent MGPS 2 Status and Survival Outcomes

In the multivariable time-dependent Cox proportional hazards model, mGPS 2 status was not significantly associated with PFS (HR, 1.49; 95% CI, 0.69–3.20; P = 0.30), whereas it was associated with shorter OS (HR, 2.17; 95% CI, 1.15–4.10; P = 0.017) (Table 3A). No statistically significant interaction was observed between time-dependent mGPS 2 status and nivolumab use for either PFS (interaction HR, 1.81; 95% CI, 0.69–4.76; P = 0.20) or OS (interaction HR, 1.95; 95% CI, 0.74–5.15; P = 0.20). Group-specific estimates are presented descriptively in Fig. 1A–D; however, the interaction tests were not statistically significant.

Association Between Modified EPCRC-Defined Cachexia Status and Survival Outcomes

In the multivariable time-dependent Cox proportional hazards model, modified EPCRC-defined cachexia status was not significantly associated with either PFS (HR, 1.15; 95% CI, 0.57–2.30; P = 0.70) or OS (HR, 1.36; 95% CI, 0.77–2.41; P = 0.30). Likewise, the interaction analyses showed no evidence that the prognostic impact differed according to nivolumab use for PFS (interaction HR, 1.13; 95% CI, 0.43–2.97; P = 0.80) or OS (interaction HR, 0.95; 95% CI, 0.37–2.40; P > 0.90) (Table 3B). Group-specific estimates are presented descriptively in Fig. 1E–H, and no statistically significant interaction with nivolumab use was observed.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study of patients with HER2-negative unresectable advanced or recurrent gastric cancer receiving first-line oxaliplatin- plus fluoropyrimidine-based chemotherapy, time-dependent mGPS 2 status was associated with shorter OS but not PFS. In contrast, time-dependent modified EPCRC-defined cachexia status was not significantly associated with either PFS or OS. No statistically significant interaction with nivolumab use was observed for either cachexia-related measure. Because OS was a secondary endpoint and no significant association was observed for the primary endpoint of PFS, the observed association between time-dependent mGPS 2 status and OS should be considered exploratory.

Both mGPS and cachexia have been reported as poor prognostic factors in advanced gastric cancer. A key strength of the present study is that both modified EPCRC-defined cachexia and mGPS were analyzed as time-dependent variables, allowing their changes during treatment to be captured. Previous studies have largely relied on baseline evaluations before treatment initiation; however, the development of cachexia during treatment has also been associated with poor prognosis. Among patients with unresectable advanced gastric cancer, Namikawa et al. reported that cachexia was associated with an increased risk of mortality, and patients with cachexia at 6 months after initiation of systemic chemotherapy had a significantly shorter median OS (13.7 months) compared with those without cachexia (21.6 months) [7]. Furthermore, a meta-analysis in patients with gastric cancer demonstrated that weight loss was associated with shorter OS (HR, 1.48; 95% CI, 1.32–1.66; I² = 71.0%) and PFS (HR, 1.59; 95% CI, 1.17–2.16; I² = 52.0%) [13]. Likewise, mGPS has been identified as an independent prognostic factor in several studies, including a subanalysis of the phase III EXPAND trial [14–16]. Our findings are broadly consistent with these reports and extend them by evaluating mGPS 2 and modified EPCRC-defined cachexia longitudinally rather than solely at baseline.

mGPS, which is based on C-reactive protein and albumin levels, reflects systemic inflammation and nutritional status [17]. Systemic inflammation and malnutrition have been proposed as factors associated with impaired antitumor immunity and poorer outcomes during immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy [18]. In addition, growth differentiation factor-15 (GDF-15), a stress-induced cytokine, has been implicated in both cachexia and immunosuppressive tumor–immune interactions [19], and recent studies have suggested that GDF-15 neutralization may enhance responses to anti–PD-1 and anti–PD-L1 therapies in solid tumors [20, 21]. However, the present study did not assess immune-related biomarkers, GDF-15, body composition, or tumor microenvironmental features. Therefore, these mechanisms should be regarded as biological hypotheses rather than direct explanations for the observed associations.

No statistically significant interaction was observed between time-dependent mGPS 2 status and nivolumab use for either PFS or OS. Although group-specific estimates are presented descriptively, the wide confidence intervals and nonsignificant interaction tests preclude conclusions regarding differences according to nivolumab use.

In contrast, one possible explanation for the lack of association observed for modified EPCRC-defined cachexia is that assessments based on weight loss and body mass index may not adequately capture the biological changes relevant to prognosis. While the EPCRC definition has prognostic value, it does not directly reflect systemic inflammation or immune function. Moreover, as this definition was established primarily in Western populations, it may underestimate catabolic states in Asian populations with different body composition and metabolic characteristics when relying solely on weight loss. In this context, new cachexia criteria tailored to Asian populations have recently been developed [22]. In addition, in patients with advanced gastric cancer, body weight may fluctuate because of ascites or edema, underscoring the inherent limitations of weight-based assessments [23].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center retrospective observational study with a relatively small sample size, particularly in the nivolumab-containing subgroup. Therefore, statistical power may have been insufficient to detect interaction effects, and the generalizability of the findings is limited. Baseline mGPS 2 and mGPS 2 first identified during treatment were not analyzed separately because both were incorporated into a single time-dependent mGPS 2 variable. Given the limited sample size and number of events, separate analyses were considered likely to yield unstable estimates. Therefore, this study could not determine whether baseline or newly occurring mGPS 2 had a stronger prognostic association. Second, because nivolumab became available only after its approval for first-line treatment of gastric cancer, treatment allocation was largely determined by calendar time. Although this design may have partially mitigated confounding by indication, temporal changes in clinical practice, supportive care, patient selection, or subsequent treatment may have influenced the observed associations. Third, the modified EPCRC definition did not include skeletal muscle mass assessment because imaging-based evaluation was not routinely available. This may have underestimated cachexia prevalence and attenuated the association between modified EPCRC-defined cachexia and treatment outcomes. Fourth, other clinically relevant prognostic factors, including tumor burden, disease distribution, PD-L1 expression, ECOG performance status, and subsequent treatment, could not be fully accounted for. Finally, changes in cachexia-related status and mGPS may reflect tumor progression, and mechanistic interpretations linking mGPS, cachexia-related inflammation, and immunotherapy response remain speculative.

In conclusion, time-dependent mGPS 2 status was associated with shorter OS, but not PFS, in patients with HER2-negative unresectable advanced or recurrent gastric cancer receiving first-line oxaliplatin- plus fluoropyrimidine-based chemotherapy. Modified EPCRC-defined cachexia status was not significantly associated with either outcome, and no statistically significant interaction with nivolumab use was observed. Further multicenter prospective studies are warranted to clarify the prognostic relevance of longitudinal mGPS assessment.
