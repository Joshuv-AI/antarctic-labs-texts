A high-temperature limit penalizing high-frequency quantum fluctuations
Graeme Pleasance, Erik Aurell, Francesco Petruccione

Abstract
We revisit the Caldeira-Leggett model of quantum Brownian motion with Ohmic spectral density, and derive an additional contribution to the decoherence kernel in a new high-temperature limit at arbitrarily large cut-off frequency. This contribution reveals a novel mechanism for the classicalization of high-frequency quantum fluctuations. We further demonstrate that it leads to a Markovian master equation that is in guaranteed Lindblad form. Our approach considers in detail the behavior of the decoherence kernel at both the initial and final times of the process on the time scale of the bath memory.



Introduction.—
The time evolution of a quantum system interacting with an Ohmic bath of harmonic oscillators is one of the most widely studied models of open quantum systems theory
Breuer and Petruccione 2002
. It is applicable in numerous physical situations where the damping becomes friction-like (frequency independent) in the semiclassical limit
Weiss 2011
, such as for photon and phonon baths
Wilner et al. 2015
, as well as superconducting qubits coupled to resistive elements in quantum circuits
Leppäkangas et al. 2018
. Caldeira and Leggett famously used this model to investigate the classical limit of quantum Brownian motion
Caldeira and Leggett 1983a
and macroscopic tunnelling
Caldeira and Leggett 1981
;
Caldeira and Leggett 1983b
. It has also played a central role in later investigations by Leggett and collaborators of the spin-boson problem
Leggett et al. 1987
, as well as in the study of decoherence
Unruh and Zurek 1989
;
Zurek et al. 1993
;
Paz et al. 1993
. More recently, it has been used to study gravitational noise effects in the arm lengths of the LIGO interferometer arising from interactions with a quantized bath of gravitons
Parikh et al. 2020
.

Under a path integral treatment
Kleinert 2009
, the influence of the bath splits into dissipation, where the memory is limited by the spectral cut-off
\Omega
of the interactions, and decoherence, which is also limited by the inverse thermal time
k_{B}T/\hbar
. For phenomenological models and interactions with phonon baths,
\Omega
is a real physical parameter while for interactions with photons it is a regularizer that can eventually be taken to infinity in renormalization. In either case, as pertaining to fluctuations of the quantum system interacting with the bath on time scales longer than
\Omega^{-1}
, dissipation is memoryless. In the Caldeira-Leggett (CL) master equation
Caldeira and Leggett 1983a
this dissipation accounts for a renormalization of the system Hamiltonian and two Lindblad terms, both of which are formed of ordered pairs of position and momentum operators
(\hat{X},\hat{P})
and
(\hat{P},\hat{X})
.

Decoherence is different, in that it has memory. Provided that the thermal time

\hbar/k_{B}T
is much longer than
\Omega^{-1}
, the cut-off
\Omega
plays a negligible role, and can be directly taken to infinity.
As described in the pioneering paper by Feynman and Vernon in 1963
Feynman and Vernon 1963
, decoherence is indistinguishable from averaging over a classical Gaussian field
\xi
with the same force-force correlations as the memory kernel of the bath. The combined effects of dissipation and decoherence as outlined above are the ingredients of the stochastic Liouville simulations method of Stockburger and Mak
Stockburger and Mak 1999
. The decoherence memory kernel
k_{r}
is therefore of great importance both in fundamental understanding of the influence of harmonic baths, and in practical simulation tools applied to quantum technologies.

In the standard high-temperature limit
Caldeira and Leggett 1983a
, the kernel
k_{r}
has no memory and produces Lindblad terms derived from ordered pairings of the position operator
\hat{X}
with itself, i.e.
(\hat{X},\hat{X})
.
The classical random force
\xi
is then delta-correlated in time. In this Letter, we show that if correctly carried out, there is an additional
(\hat{P},\hat{P})
term in the master equation that serves to both (i) damp high-frequency quantum fluctuations, and (ii) maintain the positivity of the reduced system density operator for parameters consistent with the Born-Markov approximations, similar to what has been identified in previous works
Breuer and Petruccione 2002
;
Diósi 1993
;
Diósi 1993
;
Gao 1997
;
Vacchini 2000
;
Petruccione and Vacchini 2005
. However, our derivation considers a new high-temperature limit which applies to arbitrarily large cut-off
\Omega
at fixed
T
, and in fact leads to a master equation that is completely independent of
\Omega
. Our result is therefore valid over a broader temperature range than for analogous master equations derived in
Breuer and Petruccione 2002
;
Diósi 1993
;
Diósi 1993
, and potentially offers new insights into the study of quantum Brownian motion at medium to low temperatures in relation to the bath cut-off.

Feynman-Vernon theory.—
We focus on the CL model
Caldeira and Leggett 1983a
describing a quantum particle of mass
M
, momentum
\hat{P}
, and position
\hat{X}
, moving in a potential
V(\hat{X})
. The particle interacts with a thermal bath of harmonic oscillators at (inverse) temperature
T
(
\beta=1/k_{B}T
). As such, the total Hamiltonian reads

\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{S}+\hat{H}_{B}+\hat{H}_{I}+\hat{H}_{C},

(1)

where
\hat{H}_{S}=\hat{P}^{2}/2M+V(\hat{X})
and
\hat{H}_{B}=\sum_{k}\big(\hat{p}_{k}^{2}/2m_{k}+\frac{1}{2}m_{k}\omega^{2}_{k}\hat{x}_{k}^{2}\big)
are the system and bath Hamiltonians, respectively. The interaction part is taken to have a conventional bilinear form
\hat{H}_{I}=-\hat{X}\otimes\sum_{k}c_{k}\hat{x}_{k}
, with
c_{k}
denoting the system-bath coupling strength. We assume the interaction to only be switched on after some initial time
t_{i}
, before which the particle and bath are uncorrelated. The final part represents an additional counter term
\hat{H}_{C}=\hat{X}^{2}\sum_{k}(c^{2}_{k}/2m_{k}\omega^{2}_{k})
that ensures the translational invariance of the Hamiltonian (
1
). Assuming also a continuous distribution of bath modes, each sum may be replaced by an integral
\sum_{k}\rightarrow\int d\omega\,J(\omega)
, where
J(\omega)=\frac{\pi}{2}\sum_{k}(c^{2}_{k}/m_{k}\omega_{k})\delta(\omega-\omega_{k})
defines the bath spectral density
Breuer and Petruccione 2002
.

After integrating out the bath degrees of freedom, the time evolution of the reduced system density operator
\langle X_{f}|\hat{\rho}_{S}(t)|X^{\prime}_{f}\rangle
in the coordinate representation is captured by the propagator
\mathcal{J}(t_{f},t_{i})
connecting the initial
X_{i}
and final
X_{f}
positions of the particle at times
t_{i}
and
t_{f}
. The propagator is expressed as a double path integral over the forward path
X
from
X_{i}
to
X_{f}
and backward path
X^{\prime}
from
X^{\prime}_{i}
to
X^{\prime}_{f}

\mathcal{J}(t_{f},t_{i})=\int\mathcal{D}X\mathcal{D}X^{\prime}e^{(i/\hbar)(S_{S}[X]-S_{S}[X^{\prime}])}\mathcal{F}[X,X^{\prime}].

(2)

Here,
S_{S}[X]=\int^{t_{f}}_{t_{i}}dt\,[\frac{1}{2}M\dot{X}^{2}-V(X)]
is the free particle action along the path
X(t)
(i.e. the action in the absence of coupling to the bath), and
\mathcal{F}[X,X^{\prime}]
is the Feynman-Vernon (FV) influence functional accounting for effects of quantum decoherence and dissipation from the bath. Since the bath obeys Gaussian statistics, the influence functional can be written in the exponential form
\mathcal{F}[X,X^{\prime}]=e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}S_{i}[X,X^{\prime}]-\frac{1}{\hbar}S_{r}[X,X^{\prime}]}
. The quantities
S_{r}[X,X^{\prime}]
and
S_{i}[X,X^{\prime}]
, which couple forward and backward paths, define the real (decoherence) and imaginary (dissipation) parts of the FV action. In terms of the quasiclassical path
\bar{X}(t)=X(t)+X^{\prime}(t)
and fluctuation path
\Delta X(t)=X(t)-X^{\prime}(t)

Weiss 2011
, they are written as

\displaystyle S_{r}[\Delta X]

\displaystyle=\int^{t_{f}}_{t_{i}}dt\int^{t}_{t_{i}}ds\,k_{r}(t-s)\Delta X(t)\Delta X(s),

(3)

\displaystyle S_{i}[\bar{X},\Delta X]

\displaystyle=\int^{t_{f}}_{t_{i}}dt\int^{t}_{t_{i}}ds\,k_{i}(t-s)\bar{X}(t)\Delta X(s)

\displaystyle\qquad+\mu\int^{t_{f}}_{t_{i}}dt\,\Delta X(t)\bar{X}(t),

(4)

with
\mu=\sum_{k}c^{2}_{k}/(2m_{k}\omega^{2}_{k})
the bath reorganization energy (
\mu\sim\Omega
). The kernels
k_{r}
and
k_{i}
denote the real and imaginary parts of the bath correlation function
k(\tau)=\sum_{k}(c^{2}_{k}/2m_{k}\omega_{k})[\coth(\hbar\omega_{k}/2k_{B}T)\cos{(\omega_{k}\tau)}-i\sin{(\omega_{k}\tau)}]
. The real part—the FV kernel
Feynman and Vernon 1963
—governs the decay of quantum fluctuations along the off-diagonal paths
\Delta X(t)
. In the FV influence functional, it plays the role of a force-force correlation function for a Gaussian stochastic force with memory time
\tau_{B}
(see Eq. (
A high-temperature limit penalizing high-frequency quantum fluctuations
) below). For a bath with Ohmic spectral density
J(\omega)=2\eta\omega\theta(\Omega-\omega)
, where
\theta(x)
denotes the Heaviside-step function, the FV kernel reads

k_{r}(\tau)=\frac{2\eta}{\pi}\int^{\Omega}_{0}d\omega\,\omega\coth{\left(\frac{\hbar\omega}{2k_{B}T}\right)}\cos{\omega\tau}.

(5)

It is well known that the white-noise limit of

is realized at high-temperature

. In particular, let
\tau_{B}=\hbar/k_{B}T
. Taking
\Omega\tau_{B}\ll 1
(
\tau_{B}\rightarrow 0
), one can then approximate

to obtain the singular kernel
k^{(0)}_{r}(\tau)=(4\eta/\tau_{B})\delta(\tau)

\Omega^{-1}

Feynman-Vernon kernel and decoherence action.—
We proceed to now derive a new high-temperature limit for which the FV kernel provides a nonsingular contribution to the decoherence action. As we will show, this leads to an additional diffusion-type term in the CL master equation that serves to damp high-frequency quantum fluctuations. Our approach throughout this Letter is to treat the FV kernel as a tempered distribution,
k_{r}\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})

1

1

Here we denote by
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
the space of tempered distributions over
\mathbb{R}
—the continuous dual of the space of Schwartz functions
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
.
, where the corresponding test functions belong to
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
and are

assumed to exhibit negligible variation on time scales shorter than

(6)

From this expression it is clear that

exhibits two regimes of behavior:

(i)

Vacuum regime
\Omega^{-1}\ll\tau\ll\tau_{B}
: using the asymptotic expansion

k_{r}(\tau)\approx-\frac{2\eta}{\pi}\frac{1}{\tau^{2}},

k^{\rm vac}_{r}(\tau)=\frac{2\eta}{\pi}\left[-\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}+\Omega^{2}O\left(\frac{1}{(\Omega\tau)^{3}}\right)\right].

(8)

(ii)

\tau\gg\tau_{B}\gg\Omega^{-1}
: using that
{\rm csch}^{2}x\sim e^{-2x}

k_{r}(\tau)\approx-\frac{2\eta}{\pi}\frac{1}{\tau^{2}_{B}}e^{-2|\tau|/\tau_{B}}.

(9)

These relations indicate the kernel (
5

With the goal of evaluating the decoherence functional (
3

Conclusions.—
In this Letter, we have derived a new high-temperature limit of the CL model describing a quantum particle interacting with an Ohmic bath of harmonic oscillators. The reduced system dynamics in this limit has shown to be captured by a Markovian master equation in Lindblad form, in contrast to the CL master equation used in the conventional white-noise treatment of quantum Brownian motion
Caldeira and Leggett 1983a
, among other similar works
Diósi 1993
;
Diósi 1993
;
Gao 1997
;
Vacchini 2000
;
Petruccione and Vacchini 2005
. Since our result also applies to baths with an arbitrarily large cutoff
\Omega
, it is likely to be applicable outside the temperature regime stipulated in Refs.
Breuer and Petruccione 2002
;
Caldeira and Leggett 1983a
;
Diósi 1993
(c.f. Table
1
), provided that the temperature is still large enough for the Born-Markov approximation
\hbar\gamma\ll k_{B}T
to be satisfied. A more formal comparison between these master equations could be explored by benchmarking their predictions against the exact Hu-Paz-Zhang master equation
Hu et al. 1992
. Furthermore, our result may be systematically extended to lower temperature regimes by including higher order terms within the expansion of the fluctuation paths (
13
), which is a task left to future work.

Appendix I
I. Derivation of the Feynman-Vernon kernel

In this section we outline the derivation of the FV kernel given in Eq. (
6
) of the main text. Our approach is based partly on the derivation presented in Ref.
Breuer and Petruccione 2002
to evaluate the decoherence functional for a relativistic particle interacting with an Ohmic bath—see Eq. (12.172) therein—which for the sake of completeness, is reproduced here in full.

We start by defining the function

F_{\Lambda}(s)=\int^{\Lambda}_{0}dx\,x\coth\frac{x}{2}\cos{sx},

(23)

which is related to the FV kernel according to (
\tau_{B}=\hbar\beta
)

\displaystyle k_{r}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\eta}{\pi}\Big(\frac{1}{\hbar\beta}\Big)^{2}\int^{\Omega\hbar\beta}_{0}dx\,x\coth\frac{x}{2}\cos\frac{x\tau}{\hbar\beta}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\eta}{\pi}\frac{1}{\tau_{B}^{2}}F_{\Omega\tau_{B}}(\tau/\tau_{B}).

(24)

We further introduce the first and second primitives of Eq. (
23
),

\displaystyle G_{\Lambda}(s)

\displaystyle=\int^{\Lambda}_{0}dx\,\coth\frac{x}{2}\sin sx,

(25)

\displaystyle H_{\Lambda}(s)

\displaystyle=\int^{\Lambda}_{0}dx\,\coth\frac{x}{2}\left(\frac{1-\cos sx}{x}\right),

(26)

with

\frac{d^{2}}{ds^{2}}H_{\Lambda}(s)=\frac{d}{ds}G_{\Lambda}(s)=F_{\Lambda}(s),

(27)

and divide
H_{\Lambda}(s)
into a vacuum and thermal part
H^{\rm vac}_{\Lambda}(s)
and
H^{\rm th}_{\Lambda}(s)

Breuer and Petruccione 2002
:

\displaystyle H_{\Lambda}(s)

\displaystyle:=H^{\rm vac}_{\Lambda}(s)+H^{\rm th}_{\Lambda}(s)

\displaystyle=\int^{\Lambda}_{0}dx\frac{1-\cos sx}{x}+\int^{\Lambda}_{0}dx\left(\coth\frac{x}{2}-1\right)\frac{1-\cos sx}{x}.

(28)

Now, for the vacuum contribution it follows that

\displaystyle H^{\rm vac}_{\Lambda}(s)

\displaystyle=\int^{\Lambda s}_{0}dx\frac{1-\cos x}{x}

\displaystyle={\rm ln}\,g\Lambda s+\int^{\infty}_{\Lambda s}dx\frac{\cos x}{x}={\rm ln}\,g\Lambda s+O\left(\frac{1}{\Lambda s}\right),

(29)

where the second equality has been obtained through the identity
\int^{\infty}_{\Lambda s}dx\frac{\cos x}{x}=-\ln g\Lambda s+\int^{\Lambda s}_{0}dx\frac{1-\cos x}{x}
, and
\ln g\approx 0.577
is the Euler-Mascheroni constant. Upon differentiating Eq. (
I
), we also obtain the vacuum terms

\displaystyle G^{\rm vac}_{\Lambda}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s}+\Lambda\,O\left(\frac{1}{(\Lambda s)^{2}}\right)

(30)

\displaystyle F^{\rm vac}_{\Lambda}(s)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{s^{2}}+\Lambda^{2}\,O\left(\frac{1}{(\Lambda s)^{3}}\right).

(31)

For the thermal component of
G_{\Lambda}(s)
,

G^{\rm th}_{\Lambda}(s)=\int^{\Lambda}_{0}dx\left(\coth\frac{x}{2}-1\right)\sin sx,

(32)

we may extend the upper limit of the integration to infinity, since
(\coth\frac{x}{2}-1)
falls off exponentially for
x>1
. It therefore follows from the identity

\coth\frac{x}{2}-1=2\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}e^{-(n+1)x}

that

\displaystyle G^{\rm th}_{\Lambda}(s)

\displaystyle\approx 2\,{\rm Im}\int^{\infty}_{0}dx\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}e^{isx-(n+1)x}

\displaystyle=\pi\coth\pi s-\frac{1}{s},

(33)

where the second equality is obtained from the Matsubara decomposition

\coth\frac{x}{2}=2\left(\frac{1}{x}+2\sum^{\infty}_{n=1}\frac{x}{x^{2}+(2\pi n)^{2}}\right).

Next we may combine
G^{\rm vac}_{\Lambda}(s)
with Eq. (
I
), yielding

G_{\Lambda}(s)=\pi\coth\pi s+\Lambda O\left(\frac{1}{(\Lambda s)^{2}}\right).

(34)

The thermal part of
F_{\Lambda}(s)
may also be obtained by means of differentiating Eq. (
34
),

F^{\rm th}_{\Lambda}(s)=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{\sinh^{2}\pi s}+\frac{1}{s^{2}},

(35)

so that by combining both vacuum and thermal parts of
F_{\Lambda}(s)
, we get

F_{\Lambda}(s)=-\pi^{2}{\rm csch}^{2}\pi s+\Lambda^{2}O\left(\frac{1}{(\Lambda s)^{3}}\right).

(36)

Returning to Eq. (
I
), the FV kernel is then given as

k_{r}(\tau)=\frac{2\eta}{\pi}\left[-\frac{\pi^{2}}{\tau_{B}^{2}}{\rm csch}^{2}(\pi\tau/\tau_{B})+\Omega^{2}\,O\left(\frac{1}{(\Omega\tau)^{3}}\right)\right].

(37)

Appendix II
II. Evaluation of the decoherence functional

In this section we derive the explicit form of the decoherence functional given in Eq. (
A high-temperature limit penalizing high-frequency quantum fluctuations
) of the main text which excludes the end term contributions. Our starting point is to introduce the functional
T_{F}:\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,

T_{F}[x_{\bar{t}}]=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}ds\,F_{\Lambda}(s)x_{\bar{t}}(s),

(38)

which acts on the space of test functions
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
that includes the set of fluctuation paths
x_{\bar{t}}(s)=\Delta X(\bar{t}+\tau_{B}s/2)\Delta X(\bar{t}-\tau_{B}s/2)
. As stated in the main text, these paths are assumed to also exhibit negligible variation on time scales shorter than
\Omega^{-1}
. We proceed to partition the integral (
38
) into three disjoint subintervals set by the parameter
q
(see Fig.
2
),

T_{F}[x]=\int^{q}_{-q}ds\,F_{\Lambda}(s)x(s)+\int^{\infty}_{q}ds\,F_{\Lambda}(s)x(s)+\int^{-q}_{-\infty}ds\,F_{\Lambda}(s)x(s),

(39)

where the subscript on
x_{\bar{t}}
has been dropped for ease of notation (the
\bar{t}
-dependence of
x_{\bar{t}}
will be implicit hereon unless stated). By expanding
x(s)=x(0)+O(s)
, the first integral may be written as

\displaystyle\int^{q}_{-q}ds\,F_{\Lambda}(s)x(s)

\displaystyle=\int^{\Lambda}_{0}dy\,y\coth\frac{y}{2}\bigg(\int^{q}_{-q}ds\,x(s)\cos{sy}\bigg)

\displaystyle=x(0)\int^{\Lambda}_{0}dy\,y\coth\frac{y}{2}\bigg(\int^{q}_{-q}ds\,\cos{sy}\bigg)+...

\displaystyle=2G_{\Lambda}(q)x(0)+...\,.

(40)

Now assuming
\Lambda q\gg 1
, we can use the leading term of Eqs. (
34
) and (
II
) to infer that the distribution
F_{\Lambda}(s)
behaves as a Dirac delta on the interval
[-q,q]
for sufficiently small
q
:

x\mapsto\int^{q}_{-q}ds\,F_{\Lambda}(s)x(s)=2\pi\coth{(\pi q)}x(0),\qquad q\gg\Lambda^{-1}.

As such, we may also use Eq. (
36
) to write

T_{F}[x]=2\pi\int^{q}_{-q}ds\,x(s)\delta(s)\coth{(\pi q)}-\pi^{2}\bigg[\int^{\infty}_{q}ds\,x(s){\rm csch}^{2}{(\pi s)}+\int^{-q}_{-\infty}ds\,x(s){\rm csch}^{2}{(\pi s)}\bigg],

(41)

where integrating by parts terms in the square brackets yields

\displaystyle T_{F}[x]

\displaystyle=2\pi\coth{(\pi q)}\int^{q}_{-q}ds\,x(s)[\delta(s)-\delta(s+q)-\delta(s-q)]

\displaystyle\qquad-\pi\int^{\infty}_{q}ds\,\coth{(\pi s)}[x^{\prime}(s)-x^{\prime}(-s)].

(42)

Applying integration by parts twice again to the second line of the above then leads to

T_{F}[x]=2\pi\,\coth{(\pi q)}[x(0)-x(q)]-2\pi\int^{\infty}_{q}ds\,\coth{(\pi s)}\,x^{\prime}(s),

(43)

having used that
x(s)
is an even function. To evaluate the remaining integral the derivative
x^{\prime}(s)
can be written as

x^{\prime}(s)=\frac{d}{ds}[x(s)-x(0)].

(44)

This form of
x^{\prime}(s)
is needed else we end up with a diverging term in the result (since
\coth{(\pi s)}
is not integrable at the origin). Accordingly, by integrating the second term of Eq. (
43
) by parts, we get

T_{F}[x]=2\pi\bigg(x(0)-\pi\int^{\infty}_{q}ds\,{\rm csch}^{2}{(\pi s)}[x(s)-x(0)]\bigg).

(45)

Now taking the limit
q\rightarrow 0
, which is justified by simultaneously taking
\Lambda\rightarrow\infty
(
\Omega\rightarrow\infty
) to ensure
\Lambda q\gg 1
, (this can be controlled by e.g. fixing
q\sim\Lambda^{-\alpha}
,
0<\alpha<1
), one obtains

\lim_{\Lambda\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{q\rightarrow 0}T_{F}[x_{\bar{t}}]=2\pi\bigg(x_{\bar{t}}(0)-\pi\int^{\infty}_{0}ds\,{\rm csch}^{2}(\pi s)[x_{\bar{t}}(s)-x_{\bar{t}}(0)]\bigg),

(46)

having reintroduced the
\bar{t}
subscript on
x
. It should be noted that the integral converges in this limit since the derivative (
44
) vanishes at the origin. Finally using

S_{r}[x]=\frac{\eta}{\pi}\frac{1}{\tau_{B}}\int^{t_{f}}_{t_{i}}d\bar{t}\,T_{F}[x_{\bar{t}}]

(47)

and inserting (
46
) yields Eq. (
A high-temperature limit penalizing high-frequency quantum fluctuations
) of the main text.
