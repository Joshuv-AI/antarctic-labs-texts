Differential responses of Citrus species to Huanglongbing infection for physiological, biochemical, nutritional, bio-stress enzymes, and molecular markers
Authors: Heiplanmi Rymbai, Amrita Banerjee, Veerendra Kumar Verma, Pankaj Baiswar, Joiedevivreson Mawlein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCitrus greening disease, also known as Huanglongbing (HLB), caused by the bacterium Candidatus Liberibacter asiaticus (CLas), has a detrimental effect on plants and can be a factor in citrus decline, a major threat worldwide to the citrus industry. The reactions of different Citrus species to post-HLB infection are still enigmatic. Therefore, nine prominent Citrus species (Citrus reticulata, C. sinensis, C. limonia, C. karna, C. trifoliata, C. jambhiri, C. volkameriana, C. maxima, and C. latipes) were studied in the field experiment to understand their physiological, biochemical, nutritional, and enzymatic responses to HLB infection. Based on the morphological appearance of the plants, the incidence of CLas was confirmed using gene-based DNA markers OI1/OI2c (1160 bp) and A2/J5 (703 bp). The result showed that HLB incidence ranged from 0 to 100% across different Citrus species (PCR-based). Interestingly, C. latipes showed no typical symptoms and tested negative by PCR. Contrastingly, the incidence in other species was 91.7% in C. maxima, 80.0% in C. trifoliata, and 100% in the remaining cCitrus species. The severity of the symptoms ranged from 61.08 ± 7.5% (C. sinensis) to 0.69 ± 0.2% (C. latipes). In the infected species, C. trifoliata and C. maxima recorded the least reduction in chlorophyll (Chl), net photosynthetic rate (Pn), stomatal conductance (gs), nutrients, and enzyme activities. Comparative analysis revealed that the HLB-infected species exhibited lower Chl, Pn, gs, nutrient levels, and antioxidant enzyme activities. In contrast, potassium, protein, stress biomarkers (proline, H2O2, MDA), and starch content were higher in the HLB-infected plants. Therefore, C. latipes and C. trifoliata are immune to HLB and can be utilised in breeding and as rootstocks for commercial citrus cultivars.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47911-x.Keywords: Citrus species, Rootstocks, Citrus greening disease, Huanglongbing, Biochemical, Stress markersSubject terms: Microbiology, Molecular biology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Citrus is a notable commercial fruit crop worldwide and belongs to the Rutaceae family. The fruits of citrus are globally recognized for their health-stimulating bioactive compounds, including nutritional, medicinal, and cosmetic benefits. Over 140 nations cultivate citrus fruits, occupying 16.3 million hectares and producing 190.3 million tonnes1. It is expected that the citrus fruit market would generate a revenue of US$134.55 billion in 20232. Lately, there has been a rising global threat from citrus greening disease (CGD), presently known as huanglongbing (HLB). It is a Chinese term meaning “yellow dragon disease,” due to the typical features of infected plants: trees develop yellowing that usually begins from a single branch or segment before they die. The most prevalent symptoms of HLB are blotchy mottles and yellow shoots. The causal pathogen was a phloem-limited, fastidious α-proteobacterium, intracellular bacterium (Candidatus Liberibacter species), which is transmitted by the Diaphorina citri and Trioza erytreae citrus psyllids3. The pathogenic bacteria responsible for citrus HLB disease have now been identified as three species of Candidatus Liberibacter. Candidatus Liberibacter africanus is predominantly found in Africa and is highly sensitive to elevated temperatures. Candidatus Liberibacter americanus is reported only in Brazil. The most prevalent species is Candidatus Liberibacter asiaticus (CLas), which occurs in tropical and subtropical areas of Asia, Brazil, and North America4. The rapid spread of HLB to various citrus production zones worldwide has raised serious concerns about the sustainability of the citriculture industry. HLB has now spread to over 40 countries, including key citrus producer countries such as China, Brazil, the USA, and India. HLB has become one of the most damaging diseases affecting citrus worldwide. Consequently, the multibillion-dollar citriculture sector is projected to experience a significant financial loss5. This disease exhibits a typical symptom characterised by prominent yellowing, blotchy mottling, scattered green islands, and small, upright, thickened infected leaves. Symptoms are reactions to adverse effects on the activities of the phloem, metabolism, hormones, and secondary metabolite biosynthesis in CLas-infected plants6,7. Additionally, several pathways related to the transportation of source-to-sink, the process of photosynthesis, metabolism of sucrose and starch, and the synthesis and signalling of hormones are all altered in the post-infection of HLB8. Furthermore, citrus greening disease is very difficult to identify based on visual appearance due to its symptoms related to Zn deficiency.

HLB infection was initially reported in India in the 1960s9 and recognised as a major factor contributing to the decline of citrus in the country10. In north-east India, the disease has been well known since 1975. Initially, attempts were made to detect HLB disease using chromatographic techniques, and it was confirmed that the disease was present in most species and varieties . However, it was not considered a major contributing factor to citrus decline in these areas, as its incidence was negligible in Khasi mandarin11. In India, the emphasis on HLB disease increased after 2000, following the standardisation of rapid detection techniques using PCR12. This rapid detection technique confirmed the presence of the HLB pathogen in Assam, Meghalaya, Sikkim, Tripura, the Darjeeling hills of West Bengal, and Maharashtra13. HLB infection was also detected in C. macroptera (Satkara) from Tripura14.

The eastern Himalayas of India occupy a unique position in the “Citrus Growing Belt” of the world, due to their rich genetic resources of citrus, including both wild and cultivated varieties. As a result, the region is recognised as part of the Indo-Myanmar biodiversity hotspots of the world. It is also known as the centre of origin for several cCitrus species and the habitat of many endangered cCitrus species15. Reports indicate that more than 23 species, one subspecies, and 68 varieties of citrus are found in this region16. Presently, it is known that all commercial citrus cultivars and species are susceptible to HLB across all regions and stages17. Information on physiological, biochemical, and enzymatic processes that vary among different cCitrus species during the post-HLB infection period is very limited. Given the extensive genetic diversity, especially among wild citrus with potential utilisation, the most commonly used cCitrus species were selected for study to assess their responses to HLB infection. The present study aims to identify genetic resources that demonstrate resistance to HLB based on biochemical, nutrient, enzymatic, and physiological changes in cCitrus species. This comprehension will facilitate the elucidation of their response to HLB infection and will contribute to the development of effective management strategies.

Results

Identification of HLB-affected citrus

Results showed differential symptoms of HLB infection among different species, as determined by visual appearance screening. The varied symptoms observed were yellowing of veins, chlorosis of interveinal tissues, mottling and sectorial chlorosis, corkiness of mid-veins or under-rib, and Zn-deficiency-like symptoms (Fig. 1a). During the survey, the incidence of citrus psyllid (both nymph and adult) was also observed (Fig. 1b). The samples were collected, photographed, and documented as indicated in Fig. 1. In such symptomatic trees, the incidence of Candidatus Liberibacter asiaticus was confirmed by the presence of specific amplicons of 1160 bp (OI1/OI2c) and 703 bp (A2/J5) in the tested samples (Fig. 1c; Supplementary Fig. 1). Out of 92 screened samples, 83 plants showed HLB-specific positive amplification, indicating about 85.7% overall incidence of HLB in the surveyed orchards. However, species-wise, the disease incidence varied from 0 to 100% (Table 1).

The molecular detection confirmed the incidence of HLB in all tested samples of C. jambhiri (n = 15), C. sinensis (n = 10), C. karna (n = 9), C. limonia (n = 10), C. volkameriana (n = 10), and C. reticulata (n = 10), resulting in a 100% disease incidence. While C. maxima (n = 12) and C. trifoliata (n = 10) showed HLB incidence of 91.7% and 80.0%, respectively. Interestingly, none of the tested plants of C. latipes (n = 6) exhibited typical symptoms or positive amplification during the PCR assay against HLB (Fig. 1c; Table 1). Hence, all the plants appeared free of HLB infection, and thus C. latipes was excluded from further assays. For physiological analysis, plants showing typical symptoms and HLB-positive amplicons during PCR assays were considered as infected samples. On the other hand, PCR-negative plants, preferably those with no visual symptoms, were considered healthy. In the case of C. jambhiri, C. sinensis, C. karna, C. limonia, C. volkameriana, and C. reticulata, where all the tested plants showed a positive reaction for HLB, healthy plants were collected from farmers’ fields following visual inspection and PCR tests as described earlier. Two years after the experiment, a 17-year-old orchard infected with HLB is depicted in Fig. 2, indicating that the majority of the plants had been infected and removed, with only Khasi Papeda, Trifoliate orange, and Pummelo surviving under field conditions.

A brief description of tolerant/resistant species: the trifoliate orange (Citrus trifoliata, 2n = 2x = 18) is a species closely related to Citrus and belongs to the Rutaceae family. Trifoliate orange and Citrus species are sexually compatible; however, they exhibit numerous differences from Citrus in various traits. It is a deciduous shrub that can grow up to 2 m in height. The stem is terete, with conspicuous, stout spines up to 5 cm long. Shoots have large thorns. The rachis is flat and winged. It bears trifoliate leaves, each consisting of three leaflets measuring 2–5 cm in length and 1–3 cm in width. The leaf margins are crenate, and there are 3–5 pairs of secondary nerves. The leaves also contain oil glands. The flowers are simple and occur in the axils. Fruits are bitter and inedible, containing many seeds. C. latipes is a wild, non-edible citrus mainly found in the Khasi and Jaintia Hills of Meghalaya, as well as in some parts of northeastern India. The trees reach heights of up to 10 m and are characterised by dense foliage and winged petioles. Fruits are medium-sized (weight, 165–220 g) and contain few seeds (7.3 per fruit). This species is endangered and faces a high risk of extinction.

Severity of symptoms

The present results showed significant variation in symptom severity among the species (Fig. 3). The severity of the symptoms was the highest in C. sinensis (61.08 ± 7.5%), followed by C. jambhiri (51.33 ± 4.9%). The lowest symptom severity was observed in C. latipes (0.69 ± 0.2%), followed by C. trifoliata (5.78 ± 1.4%) and C. maxima (11.3 ± 2.6%).

Physiological parameters

Results indicated significant differences in the physiological activities among the healthy and HLB-infected cCitrus species (Table 2). In healthy plants, the highest chlorophyll a content was observed in C. latipes (1.68 ± 0.04 mg/g FW), followed by C. trifoliata (1.65 ± 0.05 mg/g FW). Similarly, C. trifoliata recorded the highest chlorophyll b (0.80 ± 0.04 mg/g FW) and total chlorophyll (2.47 ± 0.05 mg/g FW), which was followed by C. latipes and C. maxima. Furthermore, the net photosynthetic rate among the healthy plants was highest in C. maxima (11.13 ± 0.22 µmol/m²/s), followed by C. latipes (9.83 ± 0.14 µmol/m²/s) and C. reticulata. While stomatal conductance was noted as the maximum in C. latipes (0.16 ± 0.013 mol/m²/s), which was followed by C. limonia and C. maxima.

In the HLB-infected plants, a notable decline in physiological activities was observed in cCitrus species. C. trifoliata recorded the highest Chlorophyll-a (0.72 ± 0.04 mg/g FW), followed by C. maxima (0.62 ± 0.02 mg/g FW). Chlorophyll b also showed the highest value in C. trifoliata (0.34 ± 0.03 mg/g FW), followed by C. maxima and C. jambhiri. The maximum total chlorophyll in the HLB plants was found in C. trifoliata (1.01 ± 0.05 mg/g FW), followed by C. maxima. The net photosynthetic rate was the highest in C. maxima (6.53 ± 0.24 µmol/m²/s), followed by C. trifoliata (5.48 ± 0.45 µmol/m²/s) and C. reticulata. Similarly, C. trifoliata had the highest stomatal conductance (0.08 ± 0.007 mol/m²/s).

Nutritional analysis

Significant differences in nutrient content were observed among the cCitrus species, both in healthy and HLB-infected plants (Table 3). Among the healthy species, C. trifoliata recorded the highest N content (2.71 ± 0.02%), followed by C. latipes (2.65 ± 0.04%). The highest P content was found in C. latipes (0.14 ± 0.005%) and C. maxima (0.14 ± 0.001%); K content in C. latipes (2.17 ± 0.08%); Ca in C. limonia (2.33 ± 0.06%), followed by C. reticulata; Mg in C. latipes (0.49 ± 0.03%) and C. trifoliata, and Zn in C. latipes (21.24 ± 1.17%), C. jambhiri, and C. maxima. In the HLB-infected plants, the highest N content was found in C. trifoliata (2.43 ± 0.05%) and C. maxima (2.26 ± 0.04%). Similarly, C. trifoliata recorded the maximum P content (0.11 ± 0.02%), Mg (0.35 ± 0.014%), and Zn (15.86 ± 0.9 mg/kg). While K and Ca were found to be the highest in C. jambhiri (2.21 ± 0.08%) and C. trifoliata (1.78 ± 0.04%), respectively.

Enzyme assays

Significant differences in protein content and enzyme activities were observed among the cCitrus species (Table 4). In healthy plants, the highest protein content was found in C. reticulata (7.94 ± 0.1 mg/g FW), PAL in C. trifoliata (21.19 ± 1.1 U/mg protein), SOD in C. trifoliata (2.74 ± 0.12 U/mg protein), and POD in C. sinensis (5.17 ± 0.07 U/mg protein). Meanwhile, the maximum CAT (18.78 ± 0.54 U/mg protein) and GPx content (31.49 ± 2.03 U/mg protein) were recorded in C. latipes and C. maxima, respectively. Among the HLB-infected plants, C. reticulata had the highest protein content (9.46 ± 0.13 U/mg protein). PAL activity was highest in C. trifoliata (19.1 ± 0.5 U/mg protein). SOD (2.29 ± 0.32 U/mg protein) and CAT (14.59 ± 0.9 U/mg protein) were highest in C. karna, while POD was in C. sinensis (3.99 ± 0.08 U/mg protein), and GPx was in C. maxima (29.42 ± 1.13 U/mg protein).

Stress biomarkers and starch content

Significant differences in stress biomarkers and starch content were observed among the cCitrus species of healthy and HLB-infected plants (Table 5). In healthy plants, the highest proline content was found in C. latipes (0.83 ± 0.01 mg/g FW), H2O2 in C. jambhiri (6.62 ± 0.07 µM/g FW), MDA in C. volkameriana (469.25 ± 10.98 nM/mg protein), and starch content in C. sinensis (18.23 ± 1.3 mg/g FW). In the HLB-infected plants, the highest proline content was obtained in C. jambhiri (0.91 ± 0.06 mg/g FW), H2O2 in C. jambhiri (7.24 ± 0.48 µM/g FW), MDA in C. volkameriana (530.25 ± 20.1 nM/mg protein), and starch content in C. sinensis (43.88 ± 2.8 mg/g FW).

Discussion

The visual appearances observed in the surveyed orchard are described in the canopy, as well as the roots and fruits. The expression of symptoms in the canopy may be described in three progressive stages: (i) As symptoms progress, the infected leaves initially exhibit yellowing at the edges near the base and along the veins (yellowing of veins). Subsequently, this yellowing rapidly disseminates across the entire leaf, leading to the formation of yellow shoots (chlorosis of interveinal tissues). (ii) During the process of leaf colour transformation, infected leaves begin to turn yellow from the main vein and gradually exhibit mottled yellow symptoms. (iii) During the period of leaf maturity, leaves exhibit a lack of lustre, and the symptoms are similar to those observed in zinc (Zn) and manganese (Mn) deficiencies. Within the root system, deterioration of fibrous roots precedes the manifestation of foliar symptoms in infected trees, and the entire root system decays before symptoms are visible on branches, as previously reported by Johnson et al.18. Fruits on infected trees are diminutive and misshapen, characterised by inferior quality and premature ripening. Additionally, these fruits tend to fall prematurely, possess aborted seeds, and exhibit uneven peel colouration. The most vivid descriptions of fruits infected with HLB are the ‘Red-nosed fruit’ and ‘greening fruit’, and the deteriorated fruit quality.

The visual appearance of the present study showed that the severity of the symptoms was the highest in C. sinensis, followed by C. jambhiri. The lowest severity of symptoms was noted in C. latipes, followed by C. trifoliata and C. maxima. The current analysis found that even PCR-negative species, which show no amplicon, display symptoms as demonstrated in C. latipes. This rating may not represent the pathogens; however, it could be due to nutritional deficiencies. It was reported that vein corking, also known as citrus vein phloem degeneration disease, is known to be associated with other pathogens19. Therefore, it may not be considered the sole reliable parameter for the identification of the disease.

The present study, using molecular techniques with primer pair OI1/OI2c & A2/J5, confirmed a very high incidence (> 50%) of Candidatus Liberibacter asiaticus across all tested species except C. latipes, which showed 0.0% incidence. The majority of the species in our study, namely C. jambhiri, C. sinensis, C. karna, C. limonia, and C. volkameriana, exhibited a 100% incidence. Earlier workers also reported HLB infection in nearly every cultivar of citrus. The highest susceptibility to HLB infection was observed in cCitrus species closely related, such as mandarin, sweet orange, and tangelo. Conversely, the least susceptibility was identified in pummelo, trifoliate orange, and lime20. Species such as C. indica Tan and C. macroptera Montr. were reported to be symptom-free species despite high inoculum pressure21, whereas C. limetta exhibited no symptoms following laboratory inoculation22. Furthermore, C. paradisi was regarded as the most suitable host for the vector associated with HLB, followed by C. aurantium and C. jambhiri during the evaluation of various cCitrus species against the vector for HLB23. According to a survey based on symptomatology, there was a 50–80% incidence of fastidious bacteria in affected Khasi Mandarin (C. reticulata) plants in the Khasi Hills24. Therefore, the healthy citrus plants free of HLB were selected based on PCR results and symptom appearance25. Furthermore, the visual evaluation of symptoms in the present study corresponds with the PCR-based pathogen identification of the trees, which was in agreement with the earlier report by Pustika et al.19.

The severity of the symptoms was highest in C. sinensis and C. jambhiri, while the lowest was observed in C. latipes, C. trifoliata, and C. maxima. The current analysis found that even PCR-negative species, which show no amplicon, display symptoms as demonstrated in C. latipes. This rating may not represent the pathogens; however, it could be due to the result of nutritional deficiencies, such as zinc. The low titer value and sometimes poor quality of DNA extraction may also be ambiguous26,27. Furthermore, the visual evaluation of symptoms in the present study corresponds with the PCR-based pathogen identification of the trees.

Chlorophyll, photosynthetic rate, and stomatal conductance are important indicators of a healthy plant. The present investigation revealed that the HLB-infected plants had a 64.06% reduction in total chlorophyll content, photosynthetic rate (by 51.78%), and stomatal conductance (by 59.31%) as compared to healthy plants. Among the infected plants, the least change in Chl-t (56.52%) was observed in C. volkameriana, while C. trifoliata had the least reduction in Pn (30.82%) and gs (43.51%). Variation in physiological activities among species may be due to genetic differences. A degradation of chlorophyll in the HLB-infected plants might be due to the greater accumulation of starches in the leaves, as indicated by about 105.28% increase in starch of HLB-infected plants over healthy plants in our study. Earlier studies have confirmed greater starch deposits in the leaves of CLas-infected plants28–30. Our study also demonstrated that starch accumulation showed a significant negative correlation with chlorophyll (a, b, and total) in both the non-infected and HLB-infected plants, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 2.

The accumulation of starch might be related to the blockage of phloem transport mediated by CLas (Candidatus Liberibacter asiaticus) infections rather than to direct phloem damage, thereby significantly altering phloem function31,32. Therefore, the pathways that transport materials from source to sink (such as signal transduction and carbohydrate metabolism) have been severely disrupted, which is reflected in carbohydrate metabolism dysfunction as the primary symptom33,34. These have resulted in the aberrant build-up of starch and, finally, the rupture of thylakoid membranes and the disintegration of grana in citrus plants29,35. Consequently, this leads to the breakdown of the chloroplast thylakoid machinery32,36,37 and damages photosynthetic membranes, resulting in a decrease in chlorophyll concentration28. This may be the reason for typical chlorotic patterns in leaves of HLB-infected plants. Additionally, elevated quantities of sucrose or glucose can suppress genes associated with photosynthesis by downregulating these genes, leading to the inhibition of chlorophyll synthesis38,39. It was also reported that a CLas infection probably triggers enzymes that break down chlorophyll40, leading to the extensive degradation of chlorophyll and apparent chlorosis41.

Nutrient-disease interactions in plant systems are widely recognised; therefore, disease symptoms often correspond to the altered nutritional status of plants42. Our study revealed that HLB-infected plants exhibited a significant alteration in their nutritional status. The highest reduction for nitrogen (21.19%) was noted in C. jambhiri, phosphorus in C. karna (45.74%), potassium in C. trifoliata (2.66%), calcium in C. limonia (37.48%), magnesium in C. volkameriana (53.37%), and zinc in C. sinensis (42.99%). The least reduction in all nutrients was observed in C. trifoliata, C. maxima, and C. karna. Our results clearly showed that C. trifoliata and C. maxima exhibited the least changes in nutritional status among the infected plants compared to healthy plants. Variation in nutritional responses among species due to HLB infection can be attributed to the unique genetic constitution of each species. The poor nutritional content in HLB-infected plants may be due to interference with the vascular system during the development of HLB disease, which has caused the vascular systems to dysfunction and blockage29,43, which triggers localised or systemic nutritional imbalances that can be either excess or deficit. Root loss or restrictions in HLB-infected plants are very prominent, causing drought stress and altering mineral status in plant tissues44. This process was further corroborated by a negative correlation between starch and nutrient content (N, r = -0.600**; P, r = -0.701**; Ca, r = -0.818*; Mg, r = -0.372; Zn, r = -0.752**) in the HLB-infected plants (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Conversely, there was an increase in overall K content by 2.34% among the HLB-infected species as compared to healthy plants. The increase in K content among infected plants was higher in C. limonia by 13.78% compared with healthy plants. This indicated that the accumulation of potassium was higher in the leaf tissue of HLB-infected plants than in healthy plants, except for C. trifoliata. The increased K storage in leaf tissue of HLB-infected plants was most likely due to electrolyte leakage and disruptions in phloem K transport. The CLas pathogen-mediated alteration of membrane permeability represents a virulent mechanism, inducing K buildup in the host to sustain rising starch production and provide a steady source of carbon and other nutrients to this phloem-limited bacterium. On the other hand, these nutrients are not available to the host cells42. Potassium malate is known to augment the activity of starch anabolism linked to protein, which regulates the virulent mechanism of CLas45. The activation of starch synthase depends on K; as a result, an increase in K level in the HLB-infected plants could trigger starch synthase45. The present studies among the HLB-infected plants, starch exhibited a significant positive association with K (r = 0.462**), as indicated by a higher K concentration (2.34%) compared to healthy plants.

Stress factors are known to cause substantial oxidative damage in plants by elevating reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels. These situations may culminate in uncontrolled oxidative reactions that destroy vital cellular elements and inevitably contribute to cellular death. The defences of plants against the damaging effects of ROS involve synthesising enzymatic and non-enzymatic substances that neutralise ROS, thereby protecting cells from oxidative damage46. A significant reduction in antioxidant enzyme activities was recorded in the infected species compared to healthy plants. The present study showed a drastic decline in defence enzyme activities of HLB-infected species, with the highest decrease recorded for PAL in C. reticulata (15.12%), SOD in C. volkameriana (38.4%), CAT in C. limonia (54.60%), POD in C. karna (40.56%), and GPx in C. jambhiri (9.59%). While the minimum reduction in these enzymes was found in C. trifoliata and C. maxima.

In contrast, the protein content was found to increase in the HLB-infected plants, with the largest increase was observed in C. sinensis (30.23%). Similarly, significant changes in stress biomarkers and starch content were noted between healthy and HLB-infected plants. The largest increases for proline content (91.08%), MDA content in C. maxima (21.31%), and H2O2 content (74.13%) were observed in C. sinensis, and starch content (140.70%) in C. sinensis. The present study found that HLB-infected plants exhibited a declining level of antioxidant enzymes, indicating that HLB infection can impair the antioxidant activity in the host. Hussain et al.47 have also found that HLB-infected plants dramatically reduce the levels of defence enzymes, including CAT and POD. HLB disease is an immune-related condition that devastates the antioxidant defence system and genes related to ROS. The immune responses in citrus plants are thereby significantly altered by CLas infection48. The regulations, i.e., antioxidant enzymes (downregulation) and transmembrane-localised NADPH oxidases (upregulation), are further exacerbated by the consistent production of H2O2 triggered by CLas infection, which contributes to the oxidative stress response in citrus plants48. Therefore, HLB infection is expected to alter the redox balance in cCitrus species, resulting in oxidative damage.

Various biotic and abiotic stresses in plants can disrupt the electron transport chain, leading to the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which are strong oxidizing agents that can damage plant cells49. Biotic and abiotic stresses have been reported to increase MDA and H2O2 levels in plants50,51. MDA is considered the final product of membrane lipid peroxidation and a general indicator of membrane lipid peroxidation damage52. In our study, a significant increase in H2O2 (34.27%), MDA (11.73%), and proline (36.72%) content was observed in the HLB-infected plant compared to the healthy plant. Our findings are consistent with those of Iqbal et al.53 and Papry et al.54, who reported that proline content increases in plants under stress conditions such as disease infection. Proline production plays a crucial role in normalizing stress conditions, helps protect the plants from oxidative damage, and maintains osmotic balance. Proline also protects proteins from denaturation under adverse conditions55. Moreover, a significant positive correlation was noted between starch and compounds such as Proline (r = 0.559**), MDA (r = 517**), and H2O2 (r = 712**), which are produced under stress conditions (HLB infected plants) as indicated in Supplementary Fig. 2. The non-functional phloem under stress conditions leads to a significant accumulation of carbohydrates in leaves, which affects the activity of antioxidant enzymes in plants56.

Conclusion

The cCitrus species tested exhibited typical symptoms of HLB, with positive amplification results through the PCR assay for HLB. The incidence of HLB among the species ranged from 0% to 100%. C. maxima and C. trifoliata have a higher degree of HLB incidence. Notably, Khasi Papeda (Citrus latipes) was found to be immune to HLB, exhibiting no symptoms and testing negative for HLB based on DNA markers. The species (C. latipes) tested negative by PCR; however, it displayed symptoms that may not represent the pathogens and could be due to nutritional deficiencies. The visual evaluation of symptoms in the present study corresponds with the PCR-based pathogen identification of the trees. The HLB-infected plants exhibited lower physiological activities, including chlorophyll content, net photosynthetic rate, and stomatal conductance. Similarly, leaf nutrient content, including N, P, K, Ca, Mg, and Zn, was lower in HLB-infected plants than in healthy plants; however, K was slightly higher in infected plants. The reduction in antioxidant and defence enzymes, such as PAL, SOD, CAT, POD, and GPx, was significant in HLB-infected plants. Contrarily, a surge in the production of proline, H2O2, MDA, and starch was noted in HLB-infected plants as compared to healthy plants. The decline in biochemical, nutritional, and antioxidant enzyme levels in HLB-infected plants suggests that HLB infection can impair the antioxidant activity of the host plants. HLB incidence reduces the levels of physiological, nutritional, antioxidant, and defence enzymes in cCitrus species. C. latipes, C. trifoliata, and C. maxima had the least responses to HLB infection in terms of changes in physiological, nutritional, and enzyme activities. Khasi Papeda (C. latipes) and C. trifoliata may be considered immune/tolerant/resistant to HLB infection, and such species may be further utilised in gene mining and crop improvement in fighting against the menace of HLB in the citrus industry.

Materials and methods

Experimental location

The experiment was conducted at the Horticulture Experimental Farm, ICAR Research Complex for NEH Region, Umiam, Meghalaya, during 2019-20. The location coordinates are 25°41’24.6” North (latitude) and 91°55’15.4” East (longitude), with an elevation of 1006 m above mean sea level. The weather variables at the experimental site are depicted in Fig. 4. The total annual rainfall during this period was 2684 mm, of which more than 90% was received during May to October. The mean minimum and maximum temperatures range from 6.2 °C in January to 28.5 °C in August. Relative humidity ranges between 47.0% (minimum) during winter and 89.0% (maximum) during the rainy season. The soils of the hilly region primarily developed from acidic parent materials of shale and sandstone origin57. The soils of the experimental location were taxonomically classified as umbrisol. The soil characteristics of the selected orchard are bulk density (1.51 ± 0.03 mg m⁻³), pH (water extract, 4.76 ± 0.13), soil organic carbon (1.92 ± 0.05 g 100⁻¹), available N (246.6 ± 5.2 mg kg⁻¹), available P (4.17 ± 0.6 mg kg⁻¹), and available K (218.514 ± 0.3.6 mg kg⁻¹).

Experimental plants

A total of 92 trees from nine cCitrus species, viz., Khasi Papeda [C. latipes (Swingle) Tanaka, number (n) = 6], Flying Dragon (C. trifoliata L., n = 10), Rough Lemon (C. jambhiri L., n = 15), Volkamer (C. volkameriana Ten. & Pasq., n = 10), Karna khatta (C. karna Raf., n = 9), Rangpur Lime (C. limonia Osbeck, n = 10), Pummelo [C. maxima (Burm.) Merr., n = 12], Khasi mandarin (C. reticulata Blanco, n = 10), and Sweet Orange [C. sinensis (L.) Osbeck, n = 10], were used in the experiment. The citrus orchard was maintained in the Horticulture Farm, ICAR Research Complex for NEH Region, Meghalaya, under the recommended organic package of practices58,59. The planting distances for C. maxima and C. latipes were maintained at 7 × 7 m, while the other seven species were planted at 5 × 5 m spacing. Subsequently, visual symptoms of HLB appeared from 2015 onwards and gradually spread across the entire orchard due to the prevalence of the psyllid vector (Diaphorina citri). The observed psyllid populations were sufficient for conducting the experiment under naturally disease-spread conditions, as described by Hall et al.60 and Westbrook et al.61. The age of the plants used in the experiment ranged from 15 to 16 years old. Khasi Mandarin and Sweet Orange were grafted on Rough Lemon rootstock, while all other species were of seedling origin. Based on the morphological appearance and visual symptoms of the plants, DNA markers were used with CLas-specific primers (Table 6). A negative PCR test result indicated that the plants were healthy. The nutritional, physiological, biochemical, and enzymatic responses were studied in both healthy and HLB-infected species.

Severity of symptoms

The severity of HLB symptoms was assessed using 12 branches per tree. The tree canopy was partitioned into four quadrants: north, west, south, and east. Four branches were randomly selected from each quadrant of a tree, both at the top (left and right) and base (left and right), and assessed for symptoms. Symptoms, including corking of the vein, interveinal chlorosis, drying of leaves, and dieback of shoots, were assessed in the branches. The severity of symptoms for each tree was assessed following McKinney’s62 criteria.

Molecular detection of HLB

The incidence of HLB was confirmed through polymerase chain reaction (PCR) based detection. Symptom appearance was documented in the germplasm of 9 species comprising 92 plants; leaf samples were collected from each plant for analysis. Midribs and petioles (100 mg) were used for the extraction of total genomic DNA using the DNeasy Plant Mini Kit of Qiagen, Germany, as per the instructions of the manufacturer. Two sets of HLB-specific primers were used (Table 6). Primer pair OI1/OI2c was used to amplify the 1160 bp region of 16 S rDNA of Candidatus Liberibacter asiaticus, while primer pair A2/J5 was used to confirm the incidence of CLas by amplifying the 703 bp region of rplA/J63. The PCR was executed in a 20 µL reaction mixture containing 1 µL DNA (50 ng), forward primers (0.2 µM), reverse primers (0.2 µM), and 2X PCR master mix (10 µL) (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The PCR reaction was carried out in a master cycler (Applied Biosystem) with following reaction cycle: 3 min of initial denaturation (94 °C) followed by 35 cycles of denaturation for 1 min (94 °C), respective annealing temperature for 1 min, extension for 1 min (72 °C) and a final extension step for 7 min (72 °C). The DNA extracted from an HLB-positive plant (already confirmed through PCR) was used as a positive control, and the DNA extracted from a disease-free healthy citrus plant (maintained at controlled conditions) was used as a negative control. The amplified results were resolved in an agarose gel (1%) stained with ethidium bromide.

Physiological analysis

The amount of chlorophyll was estimated as per the established method64. One gram of fresh leaves from each species was collected, cut into smaller fragments, and pulverised thoroughly using a pestle and mortar. Then, 80% acetone (20 mL) and MgCO3 (0.5 g) were added, and the mixture was further grounded. The resultant mixture was incubated for 3 h (at 4 °C) and subsequently subjected to centrifugation at 2600 rpm (5 min). Transfer the supernatant to a volumetric flask (100 mL), then add 80% acetone to adjust the volume to 100 mL. This solution was used to determine chlorophyll content. The absorbance of the sample was recorded at wavelengths of 480, 645, and 663 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-1780), with the blank being 80% acetone. Photosynthetic rate (Pn) and stomatal conductance (gs) were determined using a Portable photosynthesis system (LCpro+, ADC BioScientific Limited, UK). A minimum of three measurements was taken between 9:30 and 11:30 a.m., as described by Shapiro et al.65 and Pons and Welschen66. Measurements of Pn and gs were obtained under ambient light and CO2 levels from fully mature leaves of three morphologically identical trees of each species.

Nutrition analysis

A representative sample of leaves was collected at a stage of the development of trees when the level of nutrition in the leaf tissues was assumed to be stable, i.e., during the spring cycle, when the leaves were 5–7 months old. The leaf samples were collected from the 2nd − 4th leaf positions of non-fruiting terminals between August and October. Healthy leaves (50 in number) were harvested at 1.5–1.8 m height above the soil surface from all four main axis directions (east and west; north and south) of 10 healthy trees57. The leaves were thoroughly washed to eliminate any surface contaminants, followed by oven-drying and powdering in a Wiley grinding machine (Lab Wiley MillGrinder) to achieve a homogeneous sample. This was subsequently digested in a 2:5:1 tri-acid mixture of HClO4, HNO3, and H2SO467. The tri-acid extracts were used to determine total phosphorus (P) as per the vanadomolybdate method68, total potassium (K) using flame photometry67, micronutrients (Zn) using an atomic absorption spectrophotometer69, and calcium (Ca) and magnesium (Mg) using the versenate (EDTA) method70. Total nitrogen (N) was estimated using the Microkjeldahl method71.

Biochemical and enzyme analysis

The freeze-dried leaf sample (1 g) was thoroughly macerated with 10 mL of a cold enzyme extraction solution containing 0.2% polyvinylpolypyrrolidone (PVP), 0.1 mM EDTA, and 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.8). The extracts were then centrifuged at 20,000 g at 2–5 °C for 10 min and subsequently stored on ice72. Various antioxidant enzyme assays were subsequently estimated on the supernatant.

Protein content was determined with bovine serum albumin (BSA) serving as the standard73. The activity of CAT (Catalase) was assessed following the procedure described by Aebi74. CAT activity was measured at room temperature, with absorbance at 240 nm indicating H2O2 breakdown recorded using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-1780). The reaction mixture consisted of 0.88 mL (50 mM sodium phosphate buffer, pH 7.0), 0.1 mL (100 mM H2O2), and 20 µL of extract in a total volume of 1.0 mL. CAT activity was determined using the molar extinction coefficient (43.6 cm− 1). One unit of CAT activity is specified as the nmoles of H2O2 utilised/min/mg of protein.

Peroxidase (POD) activity was measured spectrophotometrically using pyrogallol as the substrate to monitor oxidation to purpurogallin. The reaction mixture consisted of 2.40 mL of 100 mM phosphate buffer at pH 6.0, 300 µL of a 5% (w/v) pyrogallol solution, 200 µL of 0.5% (w/w) hydrogen peroxide, and 100 µL of enzyme extract. The change in absorbance was monitored at 420 nm over 0–90 s. During the measurement, a control reaction mixture was prepared that was devoid of any enzymes. The activity of POD was calculated from the slope of the initial linear part of the curve plotting absorbance against time. One unit of pyrogallol activity is defined as the enzymatic conversion of 1.0 mg of purpurogallin from pyrogallol within 20 s at pH 6.0 and 20°C75.

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was measured following the established protocol76, with slight modifications as described by Chatterjee (1998). The reaction mixture comprised 2.8 mL of buffer (50 mM Tris buffer, pH 8.6, 10 mM EDTA), 0.1 mL of pyrogallol (20 mM), and 0.1 mL of extract, with a final volume of 3 mL. Absorbance was measured every 30 s at 420 nm over a period of 180 s. One unit of SOD is defined as the amount of enzyme needed to inhibit 50% of the pyrogallol oxidation to pyrogallol-ortho-quinone per minute.

The activity of phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) was estimated with slight modifications to the procedure described by Song and Wang77. A 200 µL sample aliquot was incubated with 2 mL (0.01 M borate buffer at pH 8.7) and 1 mL (0.02 M L-phenylalanine) at 30 °C for 60 min. The reaction was halted by the addition of 1 mL of 6 M HCl, followed by centrifugation for 10 min at 12,000 × g to pellet the denatured protein. Before and after incubation, absorbance was measured at 290 nm, and the specific activity was calculated as units per milligram of protein, expressed as µmol of cinnamic acid.

Glutathione peroxidase activity (GPx) was measured using the technique described by Hafemann et al.78, with minor modifications. The reaction mixture contained 5 mM GSH (0.1 mL), 1.2 mM H2O2 (0.1 mL), 25 mM NaN3 (0.1 mL), and pH 7 phosphate buffer (1 M), with a total volume of 2.5 mL, incubated at 37 °C for 6 min. About 2 mL of 1.65% metaphosphoric acid was added to halt the reaction, and the mixture was centrifuged for 10 min at 3000 rpm. After agitating 2 mL of the supernatant with 2 mL of 0.4 M disodium hydrogen phosphate and 1 mL of 1 mM DTNB, the mixture was incubated for 10 min at 37 °C, and the absorbance was measured at 412 nm. One unit of enzyme activity was defined as the reduction in log GSH of 0.001/min, after subtracting the reduction caused by the non-enzymatic reaction in log GSH per minute.

Measurement of H2O2 and lipid peroxidation was conducted on the same extract. A 0.3 g sample of frozen, finely ground leaf powder (from the second vegetative flush) was homogenised in 0.1% (w/v) trichloroacetic acid and 4% (w/v) PVP, then centrifuged at 5,590 × g for 15 min at 4°C79. For H2O2 content, the supernatant was mixed with pH 7.0 potassium phosphate buffer (100 mM) and potassium iodide (1 M) in a 1:1:4 ratio. The mixture reaction was incubated under dark conditions at 4 °C for 1 h, followed by 20 min at 25 °C. The absorbance of the samples was estimated at absorbance (390 nm) and calculated according to a standard curve with known concentrations of H2O2.

Lipid peroxidation was quantified by detecting malondialdehyde (MDA), in accordance with the methodology outlined by Senthilkumar et al.80. To the supernatant sample, a 1 mL aliquot of a solution comprising 20% (w/v) trichloroacetic acid and 0.5% (w/v) thiobarbituric acid was added. The samples were then incubated at 95 °C for 30 min, and then rapidly cooled to 4 °C to arrest the reaction. The samples were recentrifuged at 12,100 × g for 5 min, and the supernatant was subsequently measured at wavelengths of 532 and 600 nm. The absorbance of the TBA-reactive substances formed was measured at 532 nm. The measurements were adjusted for unspecified turbidity by subtracting the absorbance at 600 nm. Utilising an extinction coefficient of 1.53 mM− 1 cm− 1, the quantity of MDA was determined and expressed as nanomoles per milligram of protein.

Proline content was determined as described by Idowu et al.81. A 500 mg fresh leaf sample was ground in 3% aqueous sulfosalicylic acid (10 mL). The samples were then left for 3 h to complete the extraction, after which they were centrifuged at 10,000 g for 10 min. 1 mL of supernatant was mixed with glacial acetic acid (2 mL) and acidic ninhydrin (2 mL). The samples were subjected to boiling in a water bath maintained at 100 °C for 60 min. Subsequently, the reaction was discontinued by transferring the samples to an ice bath. Toluene (4 mL) was added to the sample and mixed thoroughly. The toluene containing the chromophore was then aspirated from the aqueous phase after the mixture was kept in the dark for 30 min. The absorbance was measured at 520 nm using toluene as a blank, and the concentration was expressed as µg/g FW.

The analysis of starch content was estimated according to Brányiková et al.82. One gram of leaf samples was homogenised and extracted three times with 80% ethanol (4 mL) at 68 °C for 15 min. To ensure complete hydrolysis of starch, add 3.3 mL of 30% perchloric acid to the sediment, then agitate at 25 °C for 15 min, and subsequently centrifuge. This process was performed three times. The extracts were then combined and adjusted to a final volume of 10 mL. Subsequently, 0.5 mL of the extract was cooled to 0 °C, and 2.5 mL of anthrone solution (containing 2 g of anthrone in 1 L of 72% (v/v) H2SO4) was added and stirred. The mixture was maintained in a water bath at 100 °C for 8 min. Subsequently, the solution was cooled to 20 °C, and the absorbance was measured at 625 nm. Calibration was conducted simultaneously using glucose as the standard reference.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis of the data was carried out using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and mean comparisons were performed using the Tukey test in IBM SPSS (version 26.0). The relationships among various parameters of healthy and HLB-infected plants were examined using correlation analysis in IBM SPSS (version 26.0).
