Enantiosensitive molecular compass
Philip Caesar M. Flores, Stefanos Carlström, Serguei Patchkovskii, Misha Ivanov, Vladimiro Mujica, Andres F. Ordonez, Olga Smirnova

Abstract
Chirality describes the asymmetry between an object and its mirror image and underlies diverse functionalities across molecular, mesoscopic and bulk matter. A particularly intriguing example is chirality-induced spin selectivity (CISS), where chiral structures generate enantio-sensitive spin polarization. Despite extensive research, its microscopic origin and unexpectedly large magnitude remain unresolved. Here, we isolate the intrinsic coupling between chirality and spin by considering spin-resolved photoionization of randomly oriented chiral molecules under isotropic illumination. We show that electric-dipole photoionization in the presence of spin–orbit coupling generates intrinsic correlations between molecular orientation and photoelectron spin that survive complete isotropic averaging. We identify these spin–orientation correlations as the microscopic origin of CISS in photoionization and reveal their complementary manifestation: selecting the photoelectron spin orients the residual molecular ensemble, realizing spin–orientation locking, whereas selecting molecular orientation produces CISS. Both effects are governed by the same correlation strength, set by the magnitude of a molecular-frame photoionization Bloch vector that defines an enantio-sensitive molecular compass. An analogous compass emerges in photoexcitation. Our results establish spin–orientation correlations as a fundamental ingredient of chiral spin photodynamics and provide a microscopic framework for understanding and exploiting spin selectivity in chiral matter.

Introduction

Chiral molecular interactions are a remarkable example of a geometrically robust response active in living matter, maintained in complex and noisy environments and operating at ambient conditions
Evers et al. (2022)
;
Aiello et al. (2022)

Atzori and Sessoli (2019)

;

;

Beyond molecular systems, solid-state materials with chiral crystal lattices exhibit a related but more complex influence on spin: they stabilize noncollinear spin textures such as skyrmion crystals - topologically nontrivial spin configurations whose formation and control are governed by the chirality of the lattice
Muhlbauer et al. (2009)
;
Nagaosa and Tokura (2013)

Many CISS-related phenomena are induced by light, suggesting the possibility of ultrafast control over spin-sensitive chemical processes via photoexcitation or photoionization

;
Varillas et al. (2026)
. The interplay between spin and chirality naturally suggests spin-orbit coupling (SOC) as the underlying mechanism for spin selectivity. However, several experiments have shown that SOC alone cannot explain the observed effect

;
Varela et al. (2013)

;
Matityahu et al. (2016)
;
Varela et al. (2016)
;

, density functional theory

;
Díaz et al. (2018)
;
Zoöllner et al. (2020)
, scattering off magnetic impurities

, electron-phonon coupling

;

, non-adiabatic coupling

, and field-theoretical treatments

. Diverse challenges for interpretation emerge due to the presence of multiple factors that potentially affect this process in experimental measurements
thereby complicating its analysis

, e.g., leads or substrates for molecules, and impurities, defects, or spurious fields for solids.

Here, we examine the simplest yet ubiquitous example of spin-chirality coupling: spin-resolved photoionization of randomly oriented chiral molecules under isotropic illumination, thereby removing any orientational bias introduced by either the well-defined light polarization or initial molecular alignment.
Once ambiguities related to anisotropic targets or substrates, and complex detection schemes involving leads are removed, the fundamental origins of spin-chirality coupling associated with photodynamics emerge with a striking clarity in an approach which is equally applicable to photoionization and photoexcitation of randomly oriented molecules.

We find that molecular chirality induces an intrinsic correlation between molecular orientation and photoelectron spin that survives even in a fully isotropic setting. Remarkably, this correlation singles out a unique direction in the molecular frame along which spin and orientation are maximally correlated.
In phenomenological descriptions of CISS, coupling of the
electron spin to a molecular “chiral axis” has been invoked to describe
a preferred spin direction in the molecular frame

Naaman et al. (2020)
. Here, rather than assuming such an axis, we derive
it directly from the photoionization dynamics: a molecular-frame Bloch
pseudovector defines the direction of maximal
spin–orientation correlation, while its magnitude determines the
correlation strength. It therefore provides an intrinsic,
microscopically defined chiral molecular compass.
The correlation gives rise to two complementary effects: molecular orientation selects photoelectron spin, revealing CISS, while photoelectron spin selects molecular orientation, leading to the previously unreported phenomenon of enantio-sensitive spin–orientation locking.

The enantio-sensitive molecular compass operates in fields of arbitrary polarization, including fully isotropic polarization. We derive these results below in a fully general and compact approach, and perform
ab initio
calculations for a synthetic chiral system with extremely small ratio (
\simeq 0.01
) of spin-orbit interaction to the ionization potential. Remarkably, pronounced locking persists even for spin-unpolarized initial states, with the spin-conditioned mean molecular orientation reaching 0.22 corresponding to an approximate 66:34 head–tail imbalance. The mechanism bypasses the weak magnetic-field interaction, and solely relies on electric-dipole interactions between the light field and the molecule. In closed-shell molecules, photoionization directly links the spin of the cation (hole) to its orientation, establishing an initial spin–orientation correlation that can bias spin-selective phenomena in orientation-sensitive environments (e.g. interfaces or orientation-dependent molecular reactions). Although the hole spin subsequently evolves through spin-orbit and vibronic couplings, this evolution proceeds with an opposite phase in opposite enantiomers. Thus, enantio-sensitive spin-orientation locking triggers enantio-sensitive spin densities and currents in molecular cations. We show that correlations between photoelectron spin and molecular orientations underlying this phenomenon are enabled by chirality.

2.2
Spin-orientation locking in randomly oriented chiral molecules

We now show that one-photon ionization of randomly oriented enantiopure molecules can establish a correlation between molecular orientation and the detected photoelectron spin. To eliminate any preferred direction imposed by the light, we consider isotropic illumination, averaging over all directions of the linear polarization vector in the laboratory frame:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\vec{E}}^{L}_{p}=E_{\omega}^{L}\left(\sin\theta_{p}\cos\varphi_{p}\boldsymbol{\hat{x}}^{L}+\sin\theta_{p}\sin\varphi_{p}\boldsymbol{\hat{y}}^{L}+\cos\theta_{p}\boldsymbol{\hat{z}}^{L}\right),

(5)

where
(\theta_{p},\varphi_{p})
describe all possible polarization directions.

\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{M}

5.2

(6)

The synthetic chiral argon calculations employ
ab initio

The electronic structure is described at the two-component configuration-interaction-singles level using relativistic effective-core potentials derived from multiconfigurational Dirac–Fock calculations, while the continuum amplitudes are obtained using the multichannel infinite-time surface-flux method with Coulomb asymptotics. The approach has been benchmarked against fully relativistic calculations and experimental atomic energies and reproduces established spin-resolved photoionization phenomena
Carlström et al. (2025)
;
Carlström et al. (2022b)
;
Carlström et al. (2022a)

Thus, while synthetic chiral argon provides a deliberately minimal system, its electronic structure, spin–orbit coupling, and photoionization dynamics are treated quantitatively from first principles. Argon provides a particularly stringent test: its chirality is purely electronic, its atomic spin–orbit interaction is weak (1% of the ionization potential), and an atomic system excludes non-local spin–orbit interactions associated with neighboring atomic centers. We further average over all initial spin states, all molecular orientations, all degenerate states of the ion and all linear-polarization directions to realize isotropic illumination. Nevertheless, a pronounced spin–orientation correlation survives. Thus, while the system is deliberately minimal, the calculation itself is quantitative and
ab initio
; establishing analytically and numerically that the correlation survives this extensive averaging is one of the central results of this work.

3
Discussion

3.1
Spin–orientation correlations and the molecular compass

One of the central results of our microscopic calculation is the emergence
of correlations between molecular orientation and the detected
photoelectron spin in a completely isotropic setting. The microscopic origin of the spin–orientation correlation lies in the
photoionization amplitude itself. The electric-dipole interaction,

\boldsymbol{\vec{D}}^{M}_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M},\mu}\cdot\boldsymbol{\vec{E}}^{M}_{p}(\rho)
, makes the ionization probability sensitive to molecular orientation, while spin–orbit coupling renders the photoionization dipoles spin dependent. The spin-resolved
ionization rate defines two complementary conditioned
observables: (i) the mean spin direction for a selected molecular orientation:

\langle\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{L}\rangle_{\rho}=\dfrac{1}{N}\int d\Theta_{p}\int d\Theta_{s}^{L}\int d\Theta_{k}^{L}\,\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{L}W^{L}(\hat{\mathbf{k}}^{L},\hat{\mathbf{s}}^{L},\rho),

(14)

and, (ii) the mean molecular orientation for a selected spin projection along
\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{L}
:

\langle\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{L}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{L}}=\dfrac{1}{N}\int d\rho\int d\Theta_{p}\int d\Theta_{k}^{L}\,\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{L}W^{L}(\hat{\mathbf{k}}^{L},\hat{\mathbf{s}}^{L},\rho)

(15)

Equation (
14
) defines a fixed molecular orientation and averages the spin-detection axis
\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{L}
, with each axis weighted by the probability of detecting the positive spin projection along that direction; a non-zero first moment therefore directly signifies a net photoelectron spin polarization and determines its direction. Equation (
15
) defines a fixed spin-detection axis and the molecular orientation is averaged (see Methods
5.5
).
The two conditional averages are generally normalized by the corresponding
conditional yields and are typically not equal to each other. In the present isotropic setting, however, these
normalizations are common
N=S_{0}|E_{\omega}^{L}|^{2}/6
. Indeed, isotropic illumination makes the spin-integrated ionization rate
independent of the molecular orientation
\rho
, while the absence of net
spin polarization makes the orientation-integrated rate independent of the
selected spin-detection axis
\hat{\mathbf{s}}^{L}
. With the normalized
measures
\int d\rho=1
and
\int d\Theta_{s}^{L}=1
, both conditional
denominators are therefore equal to
N
.

The correlation between molecular orientation and photoelectron spin is therefore characterized by the corresponding second moment of the full joint distribution, i.e.,

C_{ij}^{L}(\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{M})\equiv\left\langle e_{i}^{L}s_{j}^{L}\right\rangle=\dfrac{1}{N}\int d\rho\int d\Theta_{p}\int d\Theta_{s}^{L}\int d\Theta_{k}^{L}\,e_{i}^{L}s_{j}^{L}W^{L}(\hat{\mathbf{k}}^{L},\hat{\mathbf{s}}^{L},\rho).

(16)

To expose the molecular-frame structure underlying the ensemble correlation

C_{ij}^{L}(\hat{\mathbf{e}}^{M})
, we introduce its orientation-resolved
counterpart,

C_{ij}^{L}(\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{M})\equiv\langle G_{ij}^{L}(\rho;\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{M})\rangle=\int d\rho\,G_{ij}^{L}(\rho;\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{M})

(17)

G_{ij}^{L}(\rho;\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{M})=\dfrac{1}{N}\int d\Theta_{p}\int d\Theta_{s}^{L}\int d\Theta_{k}^{L}\,e_{i}^{L}s_{j}^{L}W^{L}(\hat{\mathbf{k}}^{L},\hat{\mathbf{s}}^{L},\rho)=e_{i}^{L}\langle s_{j}^{L}\rangle_{\rho}

(18)

The orientation resolved correlation tensor
\mathbf{G}^{L}
is not introduced phenomenologically, but follows
directly as a mixed moment of the microscopic joint distribution. Since
\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{L}
is a unit vector, Eq. (
18
) immediately gives

\langle\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{L}\rangle_{\rho}=\left[\mathbf{G}^{L}\left(\rho;\hat{\mathbf{e}}^{M}\right)\right]^{T}\hat{\mathbf{e}}^{L}.

(19)

To see that the
\langle\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{L}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{L}}
also arises from the same correlation tensor
G_{ij}
, it is helpful to represent the joint distribution as:

W^{L}(\boldsymbol{\hat{k}}^{L},\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{L},\rho)=\frac{1}{2}\left(W_{0}^{L}+s_{j}^{L}W_{S,j}^{L}\right),

(20)

where the second term expresses spin-dependent part of the joint ionization rate in Eq. (
7
) in terms of the scalar product with:

\mathcal{W}_{S,j}^{L}=\dfrac{1}{S_{0}|E_{\omega}^{L}|^{2}}\int d\Theta_{p}\int d\Theta_{k}^{L}\,\text{Tr}\left[\sum_{I}\left(\boldsymbol{\vec{D}}_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M}}^{L}\cdot\boldsymbol{\vec{E}}_{p}^{L}\right)\boldsymbol{\hat{\sigma}}\left(\boldsymbol{\vec{D}}_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M}}^{L*}\cdot\boldsymbol{\vec{E}}_{p}^{L*}\right)\right]_{j}.

(21)

wherein the trace is performed in spin space and the index
j
denotes the
j^{th}-
component of the vector quantity
\text{Tr}(\dots)
. Using Eqs.(
7
),(
14
), (
18
), and (
20
), we obtain

\langle s_{i}^{L}\rangle_{\rho}=\dfrac{1}{3}\mathcal{W}_{i}^{L}(\rho)\quad,\quad G_{ij}^{L}(\rho;\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{M})=\dfrac{1}{3}e_{i}^{L}\mathcal{W}_{j}^{L}(\rho).

(22)

Finally, using Eq. (
22
) together with isotropy of the
spin-independent contribution and the absence of net spin polarization, we obtain the connection between the spin-conditioned orientational moment and the correlation tensor:

\langle e_{i}^{L}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{L}}=3\left(\int d\rho\,e_{i}^{L}\mathcal{W}_{j}^{L}(\rho)\right)s_{j}^{L}=3\langle G_{ij}^{L}(\rho;\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{M})\rangle\,s_{j}^{L}.

(23)

Having connected the correlation tensor to both conditioned moments we can use our earlier microscopic results (Eq. (
8
)) to precisely determine this tensor.
Indeed, the orientation-resolved correlation tensor has the exact microscopic form as that of Eq. (
18
), i.e.,

\mathbf{G}^{M}(\rho;\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{M})=\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{M}\otimes\langle\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{M}\rangle_{\rho},

(24)

which is an outer product of the molecular orientation
\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{M}
and the mean spin direction for a selected molecular orientation, and has rank one for a non-vanishing correlation. Since the molecular orientation
\hat{\mathbf{e}}
is a polar vector, whereas the spin-detection direction
\hat{\mathbf{s}}
is axial and
\mathbf{G}^{M}
is therefore a pseudotensor. Since its first direction is fixed by

\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{M}
, then its most general rank-one representation is

\mathbf{G}^{M}(\rho;\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{M})=g_{\parallel}\,\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{M}(\boldsymbol{\hat{b}}^{M})^{T},\qquad|\boldsymbol{\hat{b}}^{M}|=1,

(25)

where the spin-side direction unit polar vector
\boldsymbol{\hat{b}}^{M}
and pseudoscalar
g_{\parallel}
are initially
undetermined. The isotropic rotational average of a molecular-frame tensor obeys
\langle R_{\rho}\mathbf{G}^{M}R_{\rho}^{T}\rangle=\frac{1}{3}\text{Tr}(\boldsymbol{G}^{M}\mathbb{I})
, therefore,

\langle\mathbf{G}^{L}(\rho;\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{M})\rangle=\dfrac{g_{\parallel}}{3}\left(\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{M}\cdot\boldsymbol{\hat{b}}^{M}\right)\mathbb{I},

(26)

together with the microscopic calculation (Eq. (
8
)) and Eq.(
23
) gives the chain of equations that connects the microscopic result on the left with the result expressed via correlation tensor on the right:

\langle\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{L}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{L}}=\dfrac{\nu}{3}|\boldsymbol{\vec{S}}^{M}|\left(\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}_{\parallel}^{M}\cdot\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{M}\right)\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{L}=3\langle\mathbf{G}^{L}(\rho;\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{M})\rangle\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{L}=g_{\parallel}\left(\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{M}\cdot\boldsymbol{\hat{b}}^{M}\right)\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{L}.

(27)

Since left and right parts of these equations must be equal for any
\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{M}
,
we find that the microscopic result uniquely identifies the spin-side correlation
axis with the photoionization Bloch-vector axis,

\boldsymbol{\hat{b}}^{M}=\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}_{\parallel}^{M},\qquad g_{\parallel}=\frac{\nu}{3}|\boldsymbol{\vec{S}}^{M}|,

(28)

and hence

\mathbf{G}^{M}(\rho;\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{M})=g_{\parallel}\,\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{M}(\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}_{\parallel}^{M})^{T}.

(29)

The complementary orientation-conditioned response now follows directly
from Eq. (
24
),

\langle\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{M}\rangle_{\rho}=\left[\mathbf{G}^{M}(\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{M})\right]^{T}\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{M}=g_{\parallel}\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}_{\parallel}^{M}.

(30)

Thus, for a molecule of fixed orientation, the mean photoelectron spin is
locked to the molecular photoionization Bloch-vector axis. Its magnitude
is set by
|\boldsymbol{\vec{S}}^{M}|
, while its enantio-sensitive sign is carried
by the pseudoscalar
g_{\parallel}
.
For an orientation-conditioned measurement, exchange of enantiomers
reverses the molecular pseudoscalar,

g_{\parallel}^{(R)}=-g_{\parallel}^{(L)}
, and therefore reverses the
orientation-conditioned spin response for the same conditioned molecular
orientation,

\langle\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}\rangle_{\rho}^{(R)}=-\langle\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}\rangle_{\rho}^{(L)}.

(31)

Thus the pseudoscalar character of
g_{\parallel}
provides the
enantio-sensitive sign of the spin response, whereas

\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{M}\parallel\hat{\mathbf{S}}^{L}
specifies its direction.

4
Conclusions and Outlook

We established the two complementary manifestations of the same microscopic
spin–orientation correlation: (i) the orientation conditioned measurement of mean spin direction:

\langle\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{L}\rangle_{\rho}=g_{\parallel}\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}_{\parallel}^{L},

(32)

and, (ii) the spin conditioned measurement of mean molecular orientation:

\langle\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{L}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{L}}=g_{\parallel}\cos\theta\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{L}.

(33)

Selecting either the molecular orientation or photoelectron spin thereby exposes complementary first moments of the same joint distribution determined by the spin-dependent ionization rate. The direction of the photoionization Bloch vector defines the molecular axis along which this correlation is maximal, while its magnitude determines the strength of the corresponding molecular compass. Orientation-conditioned measurement corresponds to CISS, while spin-conditioned measurement corresponds to spin-orientation locking predicted in this work.

The absence of an additional angular factor in
Eq. (
32
) is important. The factor
\cos\theta=\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{M}\cdot\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}_{\parallel}^{M}
appearing in the spin-conditioned orientation
\langle\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{L}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{L}}^{\text{iso}}=g_{\parallel}\cos\theta\,\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{L}
quantifies how efficiently the chosen molecular vector
\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{M}
reads out the intrinsic compass direction
\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}_{\parallel}^{M}
. By contrast,

\langle\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{M}\rangle_{\rho}
is conditioned on the full
molecular orientation
\rho
and is therefore independent of which
molecular vector is used to represent that orientation. For example, if

\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{M}\perp\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}_{\parallel}^{M}
,

\langle\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{L}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{L}}=0,\qquad\langle\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{M}\rangle_{\rho}=g_{\parallel}\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}_{\parallel}^{M}\neq 0.

(34)

There is no contradiction: a molecule of known full orientation retains
its spin response along the intrinsic compass direction, whereas a
molecular vector perpendicular to this direction carries no net
spin-conditioned orientational signal after isotropic averaging.

Although the Bloch pseudovector can exist in achiral molecules, parity symmetry forbids any spin–orientation locking.
In an isotropically illuminated, randomly oriented ensemble such as, e.g., HCl, mirror reflection leaves both the molecular distribution and the light field unchanged.
The laboratory spin-detection axis
\hat{\mathbf{s}}^{L}
, being a pseudovector, also remains invariant under reflection.
If a correlation between spin and molecular orientation were present—for instance, H–Cl cations preferentially associated with spin-up electrons—the mirrored configuration would produce the opposite cation orientation (Cl–H) for the same spin direction.
Because the two mirror-related outcomes are equally probable, their contributions cancel in the ensemble average, and no net spin–orientation correlation can survive in achiral molecules.

Our analysis shows that quantum correlations between the photoelectron spin and the molecular skeleton orientation are unique to chiral molecules. Importantly, they survive averaging over random molecular orientations, with the coherence between the spin-up and spin-down final components of the photoelectron recorded in the Bloch pseudovector resulting from the reduced density matrix. These correlations underlie the main microscopic mechanism of chirality induced spin selectivity. Remarkably, we show that the strength of the spin-orientation correlations, the magnitude of the CISS effect in photoionization and the spin-orientation locking are all characterized by the direction and the magnitude of the Bloch vector. We identify the photoionization Bloch vector as a chiral molecular compass mediating spin-chirality coupling and offering significant control over the orientation of enantiomers in space. Substituting the photoionization dipoles by the photoexcitation dipoles, our results translate to photoexcitation and extend the concept of enantiosenstive molecular compass to the photoexcitation processes. Thus the chiral molecular compass also gives rise to enantio-sensitive spin dynamics in photoexcited oriented molecules. Our approach highlights that both the direction and magnitude of the chiral molecular compass depend sensitively on photon energy, leading to either strong or weak spin-orientation correlations and associated spin polarization or orientation phenomena within the same chiral medium. We expect that the enantio-sensitive molecular compass is also relevant for understanding electron spin polarization in scattering from gas-phase chiral molecules
Campbell and Farago (1987)
;
Mayer and Kessler (1995)
- the phenomenon that predated the formulation of the CISS effect.

5.1
Bloch vector associated with spin-resolved photoionization

Using perturbation theory, the full spinor electron wave-function at the end of the ionizing pulse is:

|\psi\rangle=|\psi_{o}\rangle+\sum_{I,\mu^{M}}\int\mathrm{d}\Theta_{k}^{M}\,c_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M},\mu^{M}}|I\Psi_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M},\mu^{M}}^{(-)}\rangle,

(35a)

c_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M},\mu^{M}}=i\left(\boldsymbol{\vec{D}}_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M},\mu^{M}}^{L}\cdot\boldsymbol{\vec{E}}^{L}\right),

(35b)

where,
|\Psi_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}},{\mu}}^{(-)}\rangle
are fully spin-orbit coupled continuum states, which are the two components of a spinor-valued scattering solution with opposite projections of spin (
{\mu}=\pm\frac{1}{2}
) onto the molecular z-axis

\boldsymbol{\hat{\zeta}}

subject to the orthogonality condition
\langle I\Psi_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}},\mu}^{(-)}|I^{\prime}\Psi_{I^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\vec{k^{\prime}}},\nu}^{(-)}\rangle=\delta_{I,I^{\prime}}\delta(\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}-\boldsymbol{\vec{k^{\prime}}})\delta_{\mu,\nu}
. The ion channel is denoted by
I
, while
\boldsymbol{\vec{D}}_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M},\mu^{M}}^{L}=\langle I\Psi_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M},\mu^{M}}^{(-)}|\boldsymbol{\vec{d}}^{L}|\psi_{0}\rangle

is the transition dipole matrix element, and
\boldsymbol{\vec{E}}^{L}
is the light field.

We now consider the full density operator describing the spin and spatial degrees of freedom for ionization into the final photoelectron momentum
k=\sqrt{2E}
and (degenerate) ion channels
I
(fixed by the energy conservation):

\displaystyle\hat{\varrho}^{M}=\sum_{I,I^{\prime}}\int d\Theta_{k}^{M}\int d\Theta_{k^{\prime}}^{M}\sum_{\mu^{M},\nu^{M}}c_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M},\mu^{M}}c_{I^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\vec{k^{\prime}}}^{M},\nu^{M}}^{*}|I\Psi_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k^{\prime}}}^{M},\mu^{M}}\rangle\langle\Psi_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M},\nu^{M}}I^{\prime}|.

(36)

To obtain the reduced spin-space density matrix, we perform a partial trace over the spatial continuum states and (degenerate) ionization channels:

\displaystyle{\varrho}_{\mu,\nu}^{M}=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}_{\text{(spatial+channels)}}(\hat{\varrho}^{M})=\sum_{I}\int d\Theta_{k}^{M}\langle I\Psi_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M},\mu^{M}}|\hat{\varrho}^{M}|I\Psi_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M},\nu^{M}}\rangle.

(37)

Using Eqs. (
36
) and (
37
), we obtain:

\displaystyle{\varrho}_{\mu,\nu}^{M}

\displaystyle=\sum_{I}\int d\Theta_{k}^{M}c_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M},\mu^{M}}c_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M},\nu^{M}}^{*},

(38)

which follows from the orthogonality condition.

A possible way of introducing the Bloch vector describing spin orientation of a two-level spin
1/2
system defined as
\boldsymbol{\vec{P}}\equiv\mathrm{Tr}(\varrho^{M}\boldsymbol{\hat{\sigma}}^{M})
is to use
\varrho^{M}
with the matrix elements given by Eq. (
38
). In this case, the Bloch vector would depend both on the properties of the laser field as well as the molecular geometry, which does not characterize an
intrinsic
enantio-sensitive spin orientation locking that solely relies on the molecular geometry.
We now introduce the photoionization Bloch vector
\boldsymbol{\vec{S}}^{M}
by averaging
\varrho_{\mu,\nu}^{M}
over random molecular orientations, i.e.,

\displaystyle\int d\rho\,\varrho_{\mu,\nu}^{M}=

\displaystyle\sum_{I}\int d\Theta_{k}^{M}\left[\int d\rho\left(\boldsymbol{\vec{D}}_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M},\mu^{M}}^{L}\cdot\boldsymbol{\vec{E}}^{L}\right)\left(\boldsymbol{\vec{D}}_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M},\nu^{M}}^{L*}\cdot\boldsymbol{\vec{E}}^{L*}\right)\right]=\dfrac{1}{3}|\boldsymbol{\vec{E}}^{L}|^{2}\tilde{\varrho}_{\mu,\nu}^{M}

(39a)

\tilde{\varrho}^{M}_{\mu,\nu}=\sum_{I}\int d\Theta_{k}^{M}\left(\boldsymbol{\vec{D}}_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M},\mu}^{M}\cdot\boldsymbol{\vec{D}}_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M},\nu}^{M*}\right).

(39b)

Normalizing
\tilde{\varrho}^{M}_{\mu,\nu}
in a standard way to its trace
\mathrm{Tr}[\tilde{\varrho}^{M}]=S_{0}
, where

S_{0}
is proportional to the total ionization rate:

\displaystyle S_{0}=\sum_{I}\left[\int d\Theta_{k}^{M}\left(\left|\boldsymbol{\vec{D}}_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M},\frac{1}{2}}^{M}\right|^{2}+\left|\boldsymbol{\vec{D}}_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M},-\frac{1}{2}}^{M}\right|^{2}\right)\right]

(40)

we can write in the following equivalent form:

\frac{\tilde{\varrho}^{M}}{\mathrm{Tr}[\tilde{\varrho}^{M}]}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\,\mathbb{I}+\boldsymbol{\vec{S}}^{M}\cdot\boldsymbol{\hat{\sigma}}^{M}\right).

(41)

Here,
\boldsymbol{\vec{S}}^{M}
is the photo-ionization Bloch pseudovector in real space

\boldsymbol{\vec{S}}^{M}=\frac{1}{S_{0}}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\tilde{\varrho}^{M}\boldsymbol{\hat{\sigma}}^{M}\right),

(42)

and is an intrinsic molecular property invariant under rotations of the spin quantization axis provided that these rotations are applied consistently to both the density matrix
\tilde{\varrho}^{M}
and the spin operator
\boldsymbol{\hat{\sigma}}^{M}
.

5.2
Quantifying spin-orientation locking

The spin-resolved ionization rate for a given orientation is obtained by projecting
the full wavefunction Eq. (
35
) onto the scattering and ionic states onto the spin detection axis
\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{L}

with energy
\mathcal{E}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle W^{L}(\boldsymbol{\hat{k}}^{L},\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{L},\rho)=\langle\psi|\mathsf{\hat{P}}_{\mathcal{E}}|\psi\rangle=\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{I,\mu,\nu}\left(\boldsymbol{\vec{D}}_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M},\mu}^{L*}\cdot\boldsymbol{\vec{E}}^{L*}\right)\left(\boldsymbol{\vec{D}}_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M},\nu}^{L}\cdot\boldsymbol{\vec{E}}^{L}\right)\left(\delta_{\mu,\nu}+\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{L}\cdot\boldsymbol{\hat{\sigma}}_{\nu,\mu}^{L}\right)

(43)

\mathsf{\hat{P}}_{\mathcal{E}}=\sum_{I,\mu,\nu}|I\Psi_{I,\vec{k}^{M},\mu}^{(-)}\rangle\left[\dfrac{\mathbb{I}+\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{L}\cdot\boldsymbol{\hat{\sigma}}^{L}}{2}\right]_{\mu,\nu}\langle I\Psi_{I,\vec{k}^{M},\nu}^{(-)}|,

(44)

where,

is the the momentum- and spin-resolved rate for a specific molecular orientation
\rho
,

\boldsymbol{\hat{\sigma}}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\vec{D}}_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M},\mu^{M}}^{L}=

\displaystyle\langle I\Psi_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M},\mu^{M}}^{(-)}|\boldsymbol{\vec{d}}^{L}|\psi_{o}\rangle=\langle I\Psi_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M},\mu^{M}}^{(-)}|R_{\rho}\boldsymbol{\vec{d}}^{M}|\psi_{o}\rangle=R_{\rho}\langle I\Psi_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M},\mu^{M}}^{(-)}|\boldsymbol{\vec{d}}^{M}|\psi_{o}\rangle=R_{\rho}\boldsymbol{\vec{D}}_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M},\mu^{M}}^{M}.

(45)

Meanwhile, by fixing the spin-detection axis in the lab frame, the spin projection operator
\hat{\mathsf{P}}_{\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}}=\left(\mathbb{I}+\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{L}\cdot\boldsymbol{\hat{\sigma}}^{L}\right)/2

\displaystyle W^{L}

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\hat{k}}^{L},\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{L},\rho)=\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{I,\mu,\nu}\left[\left(R_{\rho}\boldsymbol{\vec{D}}_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M},\mu}^{M*}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{\vec{E}}^{L*}\right]\left[\left(R_{\rho}\boldsymbol{\vec{D}}_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M},\nu}^{M}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{\vec{E}}^{L}\right]\left\{\delta_{\mu,\nu}+\bigg[\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{L}\cdot\left(R_{\rho}\boldsymbol{\hat{\sigma}}_{\nu,\mu}^{M}\right)\bigg]\right\}

(47)

The vectors that appear on the right-hand side of Eq. (
47

\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M}

5.3
Synthetic chiral matter

Quantification of spin-chirality coupling in photoionization requires a system where both electronic chirality and spin-orbit coupling are rigorously accounted. To this end, we construct chiral electronic densities in an Ar atom corresponding to excitation into a chiral superposition of excited states, resolved on the excited electron spin. The eigenstates and associated spin-resolved photoionization dipole matrix elements of Ar atom were calculated using an atomic configuration-interaction singles treatment
Carlström et al. (2022b)
;
Carlström et al. (2022a)
;
Carlström et al. (2025)
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\vec{D}}_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M},\mu^{M}}^{M}\equiv

\displaystyle\matrixelement*{I\Psi_{I,\fullvec{k},{\mu^M}}^{(-)}}{\fullvec{d}^M}{\psi_o}=\bra*{\psi_{I,\fullvec{k},{\mu^M}}^{(-)}}\langle\chi_{\mu^{M}}|[\hat{h}_{S},\theta(r-r_{s})]\sqrt{N}\matrixelement*{I}{(\hat{H}_0-\epsilon)^{-1}\fullvec{d}^M}{\psi_o},

(54)

In Eq. (
54
), the volume integral in the matrix element is replaced with a surface integral over a sphere of radius
r_{s}
where the exact
continuum state is matched to the scattering state in the
asymptotic region
Carlström et al. (2025)
and a time integral which has been explicitly evaluated. The resolvent
(\hat{H}_{0}-\epsilon)^{-1}
formally propagates the component of
\boldsymbol{\vec{d}}^{M}\ket{\psi_o}
with energy
\epsilon=E_{I}+k^{2}/2
to infinite time, where
E_{I}
is the energy of the ion state
I
, and the projection from the left by
\sqrt{N}\bra{I}
yields an energy-resolved Dyson orbital (
N
is the number of electrons). The commutator of the scattering Hamiltonian
\hat{h}_{S}
, i.e., the asymptotic Hamiltonian obeyed by the wave function and the scattering state in the region beyond the matching surface
r_{s}
and the Heaviside function
\theta(r-r_{s})
reduces the remaining one-electron volume integral to the surface integral
Carlström et al. (2025)
.

Chiral spin resolved electronic states in Ar are inspired by a similar spinless chiral superposition in the hydrogen atom
Ordonez and Smirnova (2019)
, and in the case of hydrogenic wave-functions could be represented as:

\displaystyle|\psi_{m,\mu}^{\pm}\rangle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|4p_{m},\mu\rangle\pm|4d_{m},\mu\rangle\right).

(55)

Aiming to create analogues of such states in a multielectron system such as the argon atom, we devised an optimization procedure that yields the best approximation to these states (see Appendix
6
for details).

5.4
Estimating the degree of orientation

To provide an intuitive interpretation of the calculated orientational
first moment, we estimate the fractions of molecules whose orientation
vector has a positive (“head”) or negative (“tail”) projection onto
the selected spin-detection axis. This estimate is obtained by adopting
the approach of Ref.
Ordonez et al. (2023)
to model the angular
distribution of oriented molecules.

We characterize the spin-conditioned orientation by

\mathcal{O}_{\hat{s}}\equiv\langle\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{L}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{L}}^{\mathrm{iso}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{L}.

(56)

Without loss of generality, we choose

\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{L}\parallel\boldsymbol{\hat{z}}^{L}
, such that

\mathcal{O}_{\hat{s}}=\langle\cos\theta\rangle
, where
\theta
is the
angle between the molecular orientation vector
\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{L}
and

\boldsymbol{\hat{z}}^{L}
. Following Ref.
Ordonez et al. (2023)
, we model the corresponding angular distribution using

\Psi(\theta,\varphi)=a_{0}Y_{0,0}(\theta,\varphi)+b_{0}Y_{1,0}(\theta,\varphi)+\frac{b_{-}}{\sqrt{2}}\left[Y_{1,-1}(\theta,\varphi)-Y_{1,1}(\theta,\varphi)\right]+i\frac{b_{+}}{\sqrt{2}}\left[Y_{1,-1}(\theta,\varphi)+Y_{1,1}(\theta,\varphi)\right],

(57)

with

|a_{0}|^{2}+|b_{0}|^{2}+|b_{-}|^{2}+|b_{+}|^{2}=1.

(58)

The fractions of molecules pointing into the positive and negative
hemispheres are then

\displaystyle N_{+\boldsymbol{\hat{z}}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\varphi\int_{0}^{\pi/2}d\theta\,\sin\theta\,|\Psi(\theta,\varphi)|^{2},

(59)

\displaystyle N_{-\boldsymbol{\hat{z}}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\varphi\int_{\pi/2}^{\pi}d\theta\,\sin\theta\,|\Psi(\theta,\varphi)|^{2}=1-N_{+\boldsymbol{\hat{z}}}.

(60)

Within this model, these populations are related to the orientational
first moment by

N_{+\boldsymbol{\hat{z}}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{3}{2}\mathcal{O}_{\hat{s}}\right),\qquad N_{-\boldsymbol{\hat{z}}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{3}{2}\mathcal{O}_{\hat{s}}\right).

(61)

Thus, for example,

\mathcal{O}_{\hat{s}}=0.19
(
0.22
) corresponds within this approximate
angular-distribution model to

N_{+\boldsymbol{\hat{z}}}\simeq 0.64
(
0.67
), with the remaining

N_{-\boldsymbol{\hat{z}}}\simeq 0.36
(
0.33
) pointing into the opposite
hemisphere. These head–tail populations provide an intuitive,
model-dependent interpretation of the calculated orientation; the
quantity
\mathcal{O}_{\hat{s}}
itself is the directly calculated measure
of spin–orientation locking.

5.5
Spin-orientation correlations

Both the orientation-conditioned measurement of mean spin direction
\langle\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{L}\rangle_{\rho}
, and spin-conditioned measurement of mean molecular orientation
\langle\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{L}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{L}}
can be expressed as complementary first moments of the same joint distribution, i.e.,

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{L}\rangle_{\rho}=\dfrac{1}{N}\int d\Theta_{p}\int d\Theta_{s}^{L}\int d\Theta_{k}^{L}\,\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{L}W^{L}(\hat{\mathbf{k}}^{L},\hat{\mathbf{s}}^{L},\rho)

(62)

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{L}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{L}}=\dfrac{1}{N}\int d\rho\int d\Theta_{p}\int d\Theta_{k}^{L}\,\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{L}W^{L}(\hat{\mathbf{k}}^{L},\hat{\mathbf{s}}^{L},\rho).

(63)

where,
N=S_{0}|E_{\omega}^{L}|^{2}/6
. We now define the orientation-resolved correlation tensor
G_{ij}^{L}(\rho;\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{M})
as follows

\displaystyle G_{ij}^{L}(\rho;\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{M})=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{N}\int d\Theta_{p}\int d\Theta_{s}^{L}\int d\Theta_{k}^{L}\,e_{i}^{L}s_{j}^{L}W^{L}(\hat{\mathbf{k}}^{L},\hat{\mathbf{s}}^{L},\rho)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e_{i}^{L}\left(\dfrac{1}{N}\int d\Theta_{p}\int d\Theta_{s}^{L}\int d\Theta_{k}^{L}\,s_{j}^{L}W^{L}(\hat{\mathbf{k}}^{L},\hat{\mathbf{s}}^{L},\rho)\right)=e_{i}^{L}\langle s_{j}^{L}\rangle_{\rho},

(64)

which immediately gives

\langle\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{L}\rangle_{\rho}=\left[\mathbf{G}^{L}\left(\rho;\hat{\mathbf{e}}^{M}\right)\right]^{T}\hat{\mathbf{e}}^{L}.

(65)

Thus, the orientation-conditioned mean spin direction
\langle\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{L}\rangle_{\rho}
arises from the correlation tensor.

In order to express the spin-conditioned mean molecular orientation
\langle\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{L}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{L}}
in terms of the correlation tensor
G_{ij}^{L}(\rho;\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{M})
, it will be convenient to evaluate
\langle s_{i}^{L}\rangle_{\rho}
as follows:

\displaystyle\langle s_{i}^{L}\rangle_{\rho}=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2N}\int d\Theta_{p}\int d\Theta_{k}^{L}\left[\sum_{I,\mu,\nu}\left(\boldsymbol{\vec{D}}_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M},\mu}^{L*}\cdot\boldsymbol{\vec{E}}_{p}^{L*}\right)\left(\boldsymbol{\vec{D}}_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M},\nu}^{L}\cdot\boldsymbol{\vec{E}}_{p}^{L}\right)\int d\Theta_{s}^{L}\,s_{i}^{L}\left(\delta_{\mu,\nu}+\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{L}\cdot\boldsymbol{\hat{\sigma}}_{\nu,\mu}^{L}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2N}\int d\Theta_{p}\int d\Theta_{k}^{L}\,\left[\sum_{I,\mu,\nu}\left(\boldsymbol{\vec{D}}_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M},\mu}^{L*}\cdot\boldsymbol{\vec{E}}_{p}^{L*}\right)\left(\boldsymbol{\vec{D}}_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M},\nu}^{L}\cdot\boldsymbol{\vec{E}}_{p}^{L}\right)\sigma_{(\nu,\mu),j}^{L}\right]\int d\Theta_{s}\,s_{i}^{L}s_{j}^{L}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{3}\mathcal{W}_{S,i}^{L}(\rho)

(66)

wherein,

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{S,i}^{L}(\rho)=\dfrac{1}{|E_{\omega}^{L}|^{2}S_{0}}\int d\Theta_{p}\int d\Theta_{k}^{L}\,\text{Tr}\left[\sum_{I}\left(\boldsymbol{\vec{D}}_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M}}^{L}\cdot\boldsymbol{\vec{E}}_{p}^{L}\right)\boldsymbol{\hat{\sigma}}\left(\boldsymbol{\vec{D}}_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M}}^{L*}\cdot\boldsymbol{\vec{E}}_{p}^{L*}\right)\right]_{i},

(67)

which is obtained from the identities
\int d\Theta_{s}\,s_{i}=0
and
\int d\Theta_{s}\,s_{i}s_{j}=\delta_{i,j}/3
. This then implies that the correlation tensor
G_{ij}^{L}(\rho;\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{M})
is equivalent to

\displaystyle G_{ij}^{L}(\rho;\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{M})=\dfrac{1}{3}e_{i}^{L}\mathcal{W}_{j}^{L}(\rho).

(68)

Explicit evaluation of
\langle\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{L}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{L}}
we then obtain

\displaystyle\langle e_{i}^{L}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{L}}=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2N}\int d\rho\int d\Theta_{p}\int d\Theta_{k}^{L}\,e_{i}^{L}\left[\sum_{I,\mu,\nu}\left(\boldsymbol{\vec{D}}_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M},\mu}^{L*}\cdot\boldsymbol{\vec{E}}_{p}^{L*}\right)\left(\boldsymbol{\vec{D}}_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M},\nu}^{L}\cdot\boldsymbol{\vec{E}}_{p}^{L}\right)\left(\delta_{\mu,\nu}+\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}^{L}\cdot\boldsymbol{\hat{\sigma}}_{\nu,\mu}^{L}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{12N}\left[\sum_{I,\mu,\nu}\delta_{\mu,\nu}e_{i}^{M}\int d\Theta_{k}^{M}\left(\boldsymbol{\vec{D}}_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M},\mu}^{M*}\times\boldsymbol{\vec{D}}_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M},\nu}^{M}\right)_{i}\right]\left[\int d\Theta_{p}(\boldsymbol{\vec{E}}_{p}^{L*}\times\boldsymbol{\vec{E}}_{p}^{L})\right]

\displaystyle+\left\{\dfrac{1}{2N}\int d\rho\,e_{i}^{L}\left[\int d\Theta_{p}\int d\Theta_{k}^{L}\sum_{I,\mu,\nu}\left(\boldsymbol{\vec{D}}_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M},\mu}^{L*}\cdot\boldsymbol{\vec{E}}_{p}^{L*}\right)\left(\boldsymbol{\vec{D}}_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M},\nu}^{L}\cdot\boldsymbol{\vec{E}}_{p}^{L}\right)\sigma_{(\nu,\mu),j}^{L}\right]\right\}s_{j}^{L}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\dfrac{3}{S_{0}|E_{\omega}^{L}|^{2}}\int d\rho\,e_{i}^{L}\left[\int d\Theta_{p}\int d\Theta_{k}^{L}\sum_{I,\mu,\nu}\left(\boldsymbol{\vec{D}}_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M},\mu}^{L*}\cdot\boldsymbol{\vec{E}}_{p}^{L*}\right)\left(\boldsymbol{\vec{D}}_{I,\boldsymbol{\vec{k}}^{M},\nu}^{L}\cdot\boldsymbol{\vec{E}}_{p}^{L}\right)\sigma_{(\nu,\mu),j}^{L}\right]\right\}s_{j}^{L}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 3\left(\int d\rho\,e_{i}^{L}\mathcal{W}_{S,j}^{L}(\rho)\right)s_{j}^{L}=3\left(\int d\rho\,G_{ij}^{L}(\rho;\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{M})\right)s_{j}^{L}=3\langle G_{ij}^{L}(\rho;\boldsymbol{\hat{e}}^{M})\rangle\,s_{j}^{L},

(69)

which also arises from the correlation tensor.
