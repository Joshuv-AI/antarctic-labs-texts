Genetic variants of the leptin–melanocortin pathway in a clinically selected Greek cohort with severe early-onset obesity and hyperphagia: implications for precision obesity medicine
Authors: Eirini Kostopoulou, Diamantina X Spilioti, Nikolaos D Pantzaris, Ioannis Kehagias, Dimitrios Kehagias, Georgios Markantes, Eleni Papachatzi, Neoklis Georgopoulos, Bessie E Spiliotis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRare genetic forms of obesity are characterized by severe, early-onset obesity and hyperphagia, often involving the leptin–melanocortin pathway. Although advances in genetic testing have improved the recognition of monogenic obesity, data from Southern European populations remain limited. We investigated the genetic landscape and associated clinical characteristics of Greek patients with severe early-onset obesity and hyperphagia.MethodsThis retrospective, single-center, observational study included 67 children and adults with severe obesity and hyperphagia, consecutively recruited at the University Hospital of Patras, Greece, between September 2019 and November 2020. Targeted next-generation sequencing, supplemented by Sanger sequencing, evaluated 101 genes associated with monogenic obesity. Variants were classified according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) criteria.ResultsAmong 67 participants, 37 (55.2%) carried obesity-associated variants, with 51 variant findings identified across 24 genes, predominantly involving PCSK1,ALMS1, and BBS20. Several variants occurred in more than one participant. Variant classification identified one benign, five likely benign, two variants of uncertain significance (VUS)-favoring benign, 37 VUS, one likely pathogenic, and five pathogenic variants. Nearly half of variant-positive participants (45.9%) carried variants in more than one obesity-associated gene, suggesting potentially oligogenic contributions.ConclusionGenetic variants affecting the leptin–melanocortin pathway were frequently identified in this cohort of patients, highlighting the marked genetic heterogeneity of rare obesity. From a clinical perspective, these findings support phenotype-driven genetic evaluation in patients with severe early-onset obesity and marked hyperphagia, particularly when syndromic, endocrine, developmental, or behavioral features are present. Genetic results may help establish an etiologic diagnosis, inform genetic counselling and family evaluation, and identify selected patients who may be candidates for mechanism-based therapies. Because most detected variants were VUS, results should be interpreted by clinicians with expertise in obesity and genetics and should not be used to guide treatment on the basis of a VUS alone.Keywords: Severe obesity, Monogenic obesity, Early-onset obesity, Hyperphagia, Leptin–melanocortin pathway, Genetic testing

Introduction

Obesity is a chronic, multifactorial disease resulting from complex interactions among genetic, environmental, behavioral, and socioeconomic factors [1]. Although most obesity is polygenic, genetic susceptibility contributes substantially to disease risk, accounting for an estimated 40–75% of interindividual variation in adiposity. In recent years, advances in genomic technologies have improved the recognition of rare genetic forms of obesity, enabling earlier diagnosis and the development of targeted therapeutic approaches [1,2].

Monogenic obesity represents a rare but clinically important subtype characterized by severe obesity beginning in early childhood, typically before 5 years of age, accompanied by marked hyperphagia and limited environmental contribution [3]. Most currently recognized forms result from pathogenic variants affecting the leptin–melanocortin pathway, a central regulator of appetite, satiety, and energy homeostasis [3,4]. Identification of these disorders has become increasingly relevant because several genetic defects are now potentially amenable to precision therapies.

In contrast, polygenic obesity develops through the cumulative effects of numerous common genetic variants interacting with an obesogenic environment [3,4]. Syndromic obesity represents another distinct category, in which obesity coexists with developmental abnormalities, dysmorphic features, intellectual disability, or multisystem involvement, as observed in disorders such as Prader–Willi and Bardet–Biedl syndromes (BBS) [3,4].

Despite increasing recognition of rare genetic obesity, data describing its genetic architecture in the Greek population remain scarce. Characterizing the spectrum of pathogenic and potentially pathogenic variants in patients with severe early-onset obesity may improve diagnostic accuracy, facilitate genetic counselling, and identify individuals who could benefit from emerging targeted therapies. In clinical practice, such information may also help clinicians recognize patients in whom persistent hyperphagia and severe early-onset obesity are not adequately explained by lifestyle factors alone, prompting appropriate referral for genetic assessment and multidisciplinary management.

We hypothesized that severe early-onset obesity accompanied by marked hyperphagia represents a genetically heterogeneous phenotype in which variants in multiple obesity-associated genes may contribute to disease susceptibility, with some individuals harboring variants in more than one gene.

Accordingly, the primary aim of this study was to characterize the spectrum and distribution of obesity-associated genetic variants identified in a clinically selected cohort of Greek children and adults with severe obesity and hyperphagia using a targeted 101-gene sequencing panel. Secondary exploratory aims were to describe the clinical characteristics of variant-positive individuals, assess the frequency of co-occurring variants in multiple obesity-associated genes, and characterize the clinical significance of identified variants according to ACMG criteria. Given the retrospective design, modest sample size, and genetic heterogeneity of the cohort, analyses of genotype–phenotype relationships were considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Subjects/methods

Study population

This was a retrospective observational study of a clinically selected cohort of children and adults with severe obesity and hyperphagia. Patients were consecutively recruited from the Division of Pediatric Endocrinology, the Division of Endocrinology, and the Division of Bariatric and Metabolic Surgery of the University General Hospital of Patras, a tertiary center providing multidisciplinary care for severe obesity. This study was conducted as part of the Rare Obesity Advanced Diagnosis™ (ROAD) genetic testing program, which aims to facilitate access to genetic testing for individuals with severe obesity and clinical features suggestive of rare genetic obesity. The study was designed to characterize the spectrum and distribution of obesity-associated genetic variants and was not designed as a case-control study. No normal-BMI control group was included.

Anthropometric measurements were obtained by a single physician. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2). Obesity in adults was defined as BMI ≥30 kg/m2. Although BMI ≥30 kg/m2 was used as the minimum adult obesity threshold during recruitment, all adult participants included in the analysis had BMI ≥35.6 kg/m2. In children and adolescents, obesity was defined as BMI ≥95th percentile for age and sex. Severe obesity in children and adolescents was defined as BMI >120% of the 95th percentile for age and sex (grade 1 severe obesity) or >140% of the 95th percentile (grade 2 severe obesity). Participants included in the present analysis were clinically selected on the basis of severe obesity and moderate-to-severe hyperphagia. Hyperphagia was assessed using the Dykens Hyperphagia Questionnaire [5].

Eligibility criteria included severe obesity (grade 1 or grade 2) and moderate-to-severe hyperphagia. Patients with obesity secondary to endocrine disorders (including Cushing syndrome and acquired hypothalamic dysfunction) or receiving medications associated with weight gain (including corticosteroids, antidepressants, or antipsychotics) were excluded.

The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the University of Patras School of Medicine {Institutional Review Board (IRB) No. 64904; approval date: 29 July 2019}. Written informed consent was obtained from all adult participants and from the parents or legal guardians of pediatric participants, with assent obtained from children whenever appropriate.

Genetic analysis

Peripheral blood samples were collected from all participants and analyzed at PPD Laboratories (Belgium). Genomic DNA was examined using a targeted panel of 101 genes associated with monogenic obesity (Fig. 1). Sequencing was performed using next-generation sequencing (NGS), with Sanger sequencing used to complement regions with insufficient NGS coverage. The analysis included all coding exons and approximately 10 base pairs of the flanking intronic regions.

Distribution of mutated genes among 37 participants.

Sequence variants were interpreted according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines and classified as pathogenic, likely pathogenic, variant of uncertain significance (VUS), likely benign, or benign [6] (Table 1).

Interpretation and clinical implications of genetic variant classifications according to the ACMG framework.

The ACMG/AMP framework provides a standardized approach for interpreting sequence variants using multiple lines of evidence, including population, computational, functional, segregation, and clinical data. The five standard classifications are benign, likely benign, variant of uncertain significance (VUS), likely pathogenic, and pathogenic. In clinical practice, pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants may contribute to establishing a molecular diagnosis when the gene, inheritance pattern, and clinical phenotype are concordant. In contrast, a VUS does not establish a molecular diagnosis and should not, by itself, be used to guide clinical management.

In the present study, the additional category “VUS favoring benign” reflects the classification terminology reported by the testing laboratory and should be interpreted as a laboratory-specific subdivision of uncertain significance rather than as a separate standard ACMG/AMP category. Because most variants identified in this cohort were VUS, careful clinical interpretation is essential, and genetic findings should be integrated with the patient's phenotype, family history, and other available evidence.

Clinical data collection

Clinical information was retrieved from medical records and included age at obesity onset, age at evaluation, severity of hyperphagia, obesity-related comorbidities, medication history, and family history of obesity.

Results

Study population

The study included 67 patients with severe obesity and hyperphagia, including 29 males (43.3%) and 18 children or adolescents (26.9%). The median age at evaluation was 24.4 years (range, 2.5–51.9 years). Among adults, the median BMI was 46.8 kg/m2 (range, 35.6–73.4 kg/m2), whereas among children and adolescents, the median BMI was 37.1 kg/m2 (range, 25.4–55.5 kg/m2). Because of the relatively small pediatric subgroup and the heterogeneity of the identified genetic variants, no formal statistical comparison between pediatric and adult participants was performed.

Genetic variants were identified in 37 participants (55.2%), with a total of 51 variants detected across 24 obesity-associated genes. The most frequently represented genes were PCSK1 (n = 6), ALMS1 (n = 6), and BBS20 (n = 5). Thirteen of the 37 variant-positive individuals (35.1%) were younger than 18 years. Distribution of genetic variants

Among the 51 identified variants, one was classified as benign, five as likely benign, two as VUS favoring benign, 37 as variants of uncertain significance (VUS), one as likely pathogenic, and five as pathogenic according to ACMG criteria (Table 2). The five distinct pathogenic variants were observed in six participants, as BBS10 p.C91W was identified in two participants.

All variants were identified in the heterozygous state except for one homozygous MC4R variant. Notably, 17 of the 37 variant-positive participants (45.9%) carried variants in more than one obesity-associated gene, suggesting a potential oligogenic contribution to disease susceptibility.

Gene-specific findings

Among the 37 participants with identified genetic variants, BBS-related genes were the most frequently represented, with variants detected in 19 individuals (51.3%). Within this group, BBS20, BBS14, and BBS10 were the most frequently represented genes. Nine participants carried variants in both BBS and non-BBS obesity-associated genes, indicating substantial genetic heterogeneity and frequent co-occurrence of variants across different obesity-related pathways.

The next most frequently represented genes were PCSK1 and ALMS1, with variants identified in six participants each (16.2%). Variants in PCSK1 were frequently accompanied by variants in other obesity-associated genes, whereas ALMS1 findings were also heterogeneous, with several participants carrying additional variants.

Variants in RAI1 (n = 4), AFF4 (n = 3), MC4R (n = 3), and SRC1 (n = 3) were less frequently observed. Variants in MC3R and KSR2 were identified in two participants each, while single heterozygous variants were detected in RAB23, HTR2C, CPE, LEP, SH2B1, POMC, and SIM1. One participant carried a homozygous MC4R variant. The complete list of identified variants and their molecular characteristics is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Regarding clinical significance, five distinct variants met ACMG criteria for pathogenicity: BBS10 p.C91W, BBS14 p.E97, BBS14 c.1824+1G > A, ALMS1 p.M3936fs, and MC4R p.A303T. These five distinct pathogenic variants were identified in six participants because BBS10 p.C91W occurred in two participants. One additional ALMS1 variant was classified as likely pathogenic. In contrast, 37 of the 51 identified variants (72.5%) were classified as variants of uncertain significance (VUS), representing the predominant ACMG category. Descriptive synthesis of the findings

Overall, the genetic findings demonstrated considerable heterogeneity. Of the 67 participants, 37 (55.2%) carried at least one obesity-associated variant. Among variant-positive participants, 17 (45.9%) carried variants in more than one obesity-associated gene. BBS-related genes were the most frequently represented, followed by PCSK1 and ALMS1. Pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants were identified in six participants, whereas 37 of 51 identified variants (72.5%) were classified as VUS.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive study evaluating leptin–melanocortin pathway variants in a well-characterized Greek cohort of children and adults with severe obesity and hyperphagia. More than half of the participants carried at least one variant in an obesity-associated gene, highlighting the substantial genetic heterogeneity of severe early-onset obesity. Although the majority of variants were classified as variants of uncertain significance (VUS), the high frequency of genetic findings in this clinically selected population reinforces the potential role of genetic testing as part of the diagnostic work-up of appropriately selected patients. Importantly, the clinical utility of testing should not be equated with the detection of a variant: only pathogenic or likely pathogenic findings, interpreted in the context of the phenotype and inheritance pattern, can establish a molecular diagnosis or directly alter management.

The overall diagnostic yield observed in our cohort is consistent with previous studies reporting that broad next-generation sequencing panels identify potentially relevant variants in approximately one-third to one-half of carefully selected patients with severe early-onset obesity [7]. Differences among studies probably reflect variation in patient selection criteria, panel composition, ethnicity, and variant interpretation.

Leptin-melanocortin pathway genes

The leptin–melanocortin pathway is the principal neuroendocrine regulator of appetite and energy homeostasis, and pathogenic variants affecting this pathway are recognized causes of rare genetic obesity [8]. While pathogenic variants in LEP, LEPR, POMC, PCSK1, and MC4R account for a relatively small proportion of severe obesity, broader sequencing panels increasingly identify variants in additional genes that may influence hypothalamic regulation of body weight either directly or indirectly [9,10]. In our cohort, variants were identified in 24 obesity-associated genes, illustrating the complex genetic architecture of severe obesity and supporting the concept that genetic susceptibility extends beyond the classical monogenic obesity genes.

Ciliopathy genes

Variants involving PCSK1, ALMS1, and BBS20 were the most frequently identified findings. In particular, variants in Bardet–Biedl syndrome (BBS) genes represented the largest proportion of genetic findings. Although BBS is an autosomal recessive ciliopathy requiring biallelic pathogenic variants for molecular diagnosis, increasing evidence suggests that heterozygous variants in ciliary genes may contribute to obesity susceptibility or modify disease severity in selected individuals [11,12]. Likewise, ALMS1, another ciliopathy-associated gene [[13], [14], [15]], was frequently represented in our cohort, further supporting the growing recognition of ciliary dysfunction as an important biological mechanism underlying severe obesity.

Other obesity associated genes

Among the additional obesity-associated genes identified in our cohort, RAI1, KSR2, RAB23, AFF4, SRC1, HTR2C, CPE, SH2B1, and SIM1 provide further evidence for the biological complexity of severe obesity beyond the classical leptin–melanocortin pathway. Although pathogenic variants in these genes are individually rare, they influence diverse molecular mechanisms involved in hypothalamic development, neuronal signaling, energy expenditure, adipocyte differentiation, endocrine regulation, and ciliary function.

Variants in RAI1 are responsible for Smith–Magenis syndrome, a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by obesity, hyperphagia, behavioral abnormalities, and dyslipidemia [16,17]. RAI1 regulates transcription of genes involved in neurodevelopment and appears to influence energy homeostasis through modulation of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) signaling [18]. Likewise, KSR2 plays an important role in cellular energy sensing through the Raf/MEK/ERK signaling cascade and has been associated with autosomal dominant early-onset obesity, hyperphagia, reduced energy expenditure, bradycardia, and severe insulin resistance [[19], [20], [21]].

RAB23, a negative regulator of Hedgehog signaling, is primarily associated with Carpenter syndrome, in which obesity may coexist with craniosynostosis, polysyndactyly, and congenital anomalies [22]. Although its direct contribution to obesity remains uncertain, interactions with primary cilia suggest biological overlap with Bardet–Biedl syndrome and other ciliopathies [23]. Similarly, AFF4 has recently emerged as a potential regulator of adipogenesis and adipocyte autophagy, supporting a possible role in white adipose tissue expansion and obesity susceptibility [[24], [25], [26]].

Several identified genes directly interact with hypothalamic appetite-regulating pathways. SRC1 functions as a transcriptional coactivator that enhances leptin-induced activation of POMC neurons through STAT3 signaling [27]. Pathogenic variants have been associated with severe childhood obesity, hyperphagia, metabolic complications, and endocrine abnormalities [[27], [28], [29]]. HTR2C, encoding the serotonin 2C receptor, is another important upstream regulator of the melanocortin pathway. Rare variants have been associated with severe early-onset obesity, hyperphagia, developmental delay, and behavioral disturbances, while clinical responses to MC4R agonists further support functional interaction between serotonergic and melanocortin signaling [30,31].

The CPE gene encodes carboxypeptidase E, a key enzyme involved in the maturation of multiple neuropeptides and peptide hormones, including proinsulin, proopiomelanocortin (POMC), and BDNF [32,33]. Deficiency of CPE disrupts neuroendocrine signaling and has been associated with obesity accompanied by endocrine dysfunction, glucose abnormalities, hypogonadism, and intellectual disability [[34], [35], [36]]. SH2B1 is an adaptor protein that modulates intracellular signaling downstream of leptin, insulin, growth hormone, and BDNF receptors [37,38]. Pathogenic variants consistently predispose to severe obesity with insulin resistance and metabolic dysfunction, emphasizing its central role in hypothalamic regulation of energy balance [39,40].

Finally, SIM1 encodes a transcription factor essential for normal hypothalamic and paraventricular nucleus development, where melanocortin signaling is integrated [41]. Haploinsufficiency of SIM1 results in severe hyperphagic obesity and contributes to Prader–Willi-like phenotypes, further highlighting the importance of hypothalamic developmental pathways in monogenic obesity [42].

Collectively, these findings illustrate that severe genetic obesity extends well beyond defects in the leptin–melanocortin pathway alone. Instead, multiple biological processes—including neuronal development, ciliary biology, intracellular signaling, neuroendocrine peptide processing, and adipocyte differentiation—appear to converge on common mechanisms regulating appetite, satiety, and energy homeostasis. The identification of variants in these genes reinforces the value of comprehensive multigene sequencing panels in carefully selected patients and supports the continued evolution of precision obesity medicine.

This study demonstrates that in a selected population, the ratio of patients carrying variants in genes involved in the leptin-melanocortin pathway is considerably high. The principal contribution of this study is not to establish when genetic testing should be considered in severe early-onset obesity, as these indications are already recognized. Rather, our study provides population-specific evidence regarding the genetic complexity and interpretative challenges encountered when broad genetic testing is applied to a clinically selected Greek cohort. The identification of variants across 24 obesity-associated genes, together with the frequent coexistence of variants in multiple genes, illustrates the considerable genetic heterogeneity underlying severe obesity. Equally importantly, the predominance of variants of uncertain significance highlights the limitations of interpreting broad genetic panels in routine clinical practice and reinforces the need for phenotype-guided interpretation, genetic counselling, and functional or segregation studies before assigning causality. In addition, current advances in scientific research favour the concept that polygenic and monogenic obesity is a continuing spectrum with overlapping genes and not two completely distinct entities [43]. Nonetheless, much remains unknown, hence the potential implication of the identified variants in the clinical phenotype of obesity needs to be further investigated and elucidated.

One of the most notable findings was that almost half of variant-positive participants carried variants in more than one obesity-associated gene. This observation is consistent with the emerging concept of oligogenic obesity, in which the combined effects of multiple rare variants contribute to disease expression rather than a single highly penetrant mutation [44]. Such interactions may partly explain the marked phenotypic variability observed among patients with apparently similar genetic findings and reinforce the view that obesity exists along a continuum between classical monogenic and common polygenic disease.

An additional important observation was the predominance of variants of uncertain significance, which accounted for more than 70% of all detected variants. This finding reflects one of the major challenges in clinical obesity genetics. Although high-throughput sequencing has substantially increased the identification of rare variants, establishing their pathogenicity remains difficult in the absence of segregation studies, functional assays, or large ancestry-specific reference datasets. Consequently, interpretation of genetic findings should always be integrated with the patient's clinical phenotype and family history, and VUS should not be considered diagnostic in isolation. From a clinical perspective, this high VUS rate means that genetic testing should be accompanied by appropriate pre-test and post-test counselling and, where available, expert variant interpretation and periodic re-evaluation of uncertain findings. It should also be noted though that significant disparities in access to recent advances in gene-sequencing technologies exist due to the financial burden, while limited genetic counselling capacity remains a challenge in many countries worldwide.

The inclusion of both pediatric and adult participants was intended to capture the genetic spectrum of severe obesity across the lifespan. However, the relatively small pediatric subgroup (18/67 participants) precluded robust age-stratified statistical analysis. Clinically, the implications of identifying a genetic etiology may differ by age: in children, a molecular diagnosis may facilitate early recognition of syndromic manifestations, developmental and endocrine surveillance, family counselling, and cascade testing, whereas in adults it may clarify the cause of long-standing severe obesity, inform family evaluation, and, for selected genetic disorders, support consideration of mechanism-based therapies. These age-specific implications should be interpreted cautiously and require confirmation in larger pediatric and adult cohorts.

Clinical implications for obesity practice

The present findings have several important clinical implications. Current international recommendations support genetic testing in individuals with severe early-onset obesity, marked hyperphagia, and additional clinical features suggestive of a genetic disorder [45]. Accordingly, clinicians should consider referral for genetic evaluation when obesity is severe and begins early in life, particularly when hyperphagia is prominent or when endocrine, developmental, behavioral, dysmorphic, ocular, renal, or other syndromic features and/or a strong family history are present [3,46]. In these patients, genetic testing can move the diagnostic approach beyond the assumption of common polygenic obesity and may provide an etiologic explanation for an otherwise difficult-to-manage phenotype [46,47]. A confirmed molecular diagnosis may facilitate genetic counselling, enable appropriate cascade or family testing, and support anticipatory assessment for syndrome-specific manifestations [3,46]. Moreover, establishing the underlying molecular defect is increasingly relevant to treatment selection because some disorders of the leptin–melanocortin pathway have mechanism-based therapeutic options, including melanocortin-4 receptor agonism for selected genetically defined conditions [48]. Thus, genetic information can potentially influence not only diagnosis but also treatment planning and referral to specialized obesity/genetics services. However, our results also demonstrate an important practical limitation: 37 of 51 detected variants (72.5%) were classified as VUS. Therefore, clinicians should not label a patient as having monogenic obesity or change therapy solely on the basis of a VUS. Instead, genetic results should be interpreted alongside the clinical phenotype, family history, inheritance pattern, and current ACMG criteria, with genetic counselling and periodic reinterpretation when appropriate [49]. These considerations support a phenotype-driven, multidisciplinary model in which genetic testing is targeted to patients with a sufficiently high pre-test probability rather than applied indiscriminately to all individuals with obesity.

The strengths of this study include the use of a comprehensive 101-gene sequencing panel, systematic ACMG variant classification, and detailed phenotypic characterization of a well-characterized, clinically selected Greek cohort recruited through specialized obesity clinics. These data provide one of the first descriptions of the genetic spectrum of severe obesity in the Greek population.

Limitation

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, this was a single-center, clinically selected cohort of patients with severe obesity and hyperphagia recruited from specialized obesity services. This referral-based selection may have enriched the cohort for individuals with genetic forms of obesity and therefore introduces potential selection bias. Consequently, the frequencies of specific genetic variants observed in this study should not be interpreted as estimates of their prevalence in the general Greek population or as evidence of population-specific enrichment relative to other populations.

Second, the sample size was relatively modest (n = 67), limiting the precision of the observed frequencies and the ability to draw reliable conclusions regarding individual genes or rare variants. The number of carriers for most genes was too small to permit robust genotype–phenotype comparisons or meaningful statistical comparisons between genetic subgroups. The study should therefore be considered primarily descriptive and hypothesis-generating. Although the present study was performed in a Greek cohort, the clinical phenotype prompting genetic evaluation—severe early-onset obesity accompanied by marked hyperphagia and, in some patients, additional syndromic or endocrine features—is not population-specific. Accordingly, the clinical principles emerging from this study, including phenotype-driven referral for genetic evaluation, careful ACMG-based interpretation of variants, genetic counselling, and avoidance of clinical decisions based solely on variants of uncertain significance, are potentially applicable across different healthcare settings.

In contrast, the specific frequencies and distribution of genetic variants observed in this cohort should not be extrapolated directly to other European or international populations. The study population was relatively small, single-center, and clinically selected, and genetic architecture may vary according to ancestry, population structure, and ascertainment. Our findings should therefore be regarded as population-specific and hypothesis-generating. Larger multicenter studies involving diverse European and international populations will be required to determine the reproducibility and broader generalizability of the observed genetic spectrum.

Third, functional validation and familial segregation analyses were not available for most variants, particularly those classified as variants of uncertain significance. Consequently, the presence of a variant cannot be assumed to establish causality, and the high proportion of VUS further limits the ability to define definitive molecular diagnoses.

Furthermore, complete nucleotide-level HGVS descriptions were not available for all variants in the original genetic laboratory dataset, precluding systematic cross-referencing of every identified variant with publicly available genomic and clinical variant databases. Future studies incorporating standardized nucleotide-level variant annotation and comprehensive database comparison will be important for further characterization of these findings.

Finally, the absence of an ancestry-matched control population and the single-center design preclude direct comparison of the observed genetic spectrum with that of other European or global populations. Larger, multicenter studies involving broader and more systematically recruited cohorts, appropriate control populations, functional validation, and longitudinal clinical follow-up will be required to determine the population frequency and clinical significance of the variants identified in this study.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates substantial genetic heterogeneity among Greek patients with severe obesity and hyperphagia, with more than half carrying variants in obesity-associated genes involved in or related to the leptin–melanocortin pathway. The frequent occurrence of multiple concurrent variants supports the emerging concept of oligogenic obesity and highlights the importance of comprehensive genetic evaluation in carefully selected patients. For clinical practice, the findings support a phenotype-driven approach to genetic testing in patients with severe early-onset obesity and marked hyperphagia, particularly when additional syndromic, endocrine, developmental, or behavioral features are present. A confirmed pathogenic or likely pathogenic result may improve etiologic diagnosis, guide genetic counselling and family evaluation, and identify selected patients for mechanism-based treatment or specialist referral. Conversely, the predominance of VUS emphasizes that genetic results must be interpreted cautiously and should not independently determine treatment. Larger multicenter studies incorporating functional validation and long-term clinical follow-up are needed to clarify the contribution of rare variants to disease pathogenesis and to advance precision obesity medicine.
