Effect of Yoga on Ventricular Premature Complex Burden and Quality of Life
Authors: Gautam Sharma, Arun EV, Aatish Rengan, Divya Singh, Udisha Singh, Mohd Sharique, Ajmal Ismail, Anu James Vibin, Man Singh Jat, Deepti Siddharthan, Nitish Naik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVentricular premature complexes (VPCs) are common cardiac arrhythmias with prognostic significance depending on clinical context and arrhythmia burden. Yoga modulates autonomic tone and may improve cardiac dysautonomia, including arrhythmias, while enhancing quality of life (QoL).ObjectivesThe objective of the study was to evaluate the efficacy of adjunctive yoga-based intervention on VPC burden, QoL, and left ventricular ejection fraction in patients with frequent VPCs.MethodsIn this single-center, prospective, randomized controlled trial, 134 patients with frequent VPCs (burden ≥5% on 24-hour Holter monitoring) were randomized (1:1) to a structured yoga intervention (n = 67) or standard care (n = 67) and followed for 24 weeks. The primary outcome was change in VPC burden. Secondary outcomes included QoL (36-Item Short Form Survey), left ventricular ejection fraction, major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events, psychological outcomes (Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scale-21 Items), and heart rate variability.ResultsThe baseline VPC burden was higher in the control group than in the yoga group (21.06 [10.48-34.67] vs 13.02 [8.87-21.60]). At 6 weeks, both groups demonstrated a reduction in VPC burden (control: 19.19 [7.50-30.70]; yoga: 11.62 [5.83-21.59]). A statistically significant between-group difference was observed at 12 weeks (P = 0.02). At 24 weeks, the yoga group showed significantly better 36-Item Short Form Survey scores across all domains and Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scale-21 Items scores.ConclusionsYoga as an adjunct therapeutic modality reduced VPC burden and improved QoL and psychological burden in patients with frequent VPCs. It may be useful to integrate a cost-effective, nonpharmacological intervention like yoga in the management of patients with frequent VPCs. (Effect of Yoga-based intervention on VPC burden, quality of life and LV functions in patients with Ventricular premature complexes [VPC]: A Randomized controlled Trial; CTRI/2021/06/034450).

Ventricular premature complexes (VPCs) are observed both in structurally normal and abnormal hearts in the form of solitary ectopic beats, couplets, or runs of nonsustained ventricular tachycardia.1 VPCs are seen in 1% of healthy individuals as detected by standard 12-lead electrocardiogram, and in 40% to 75% as detected by 24- to 48-hour ambulatory Holter recordings. Frequent VPCs, variably defined as >60/hour or 1/min or a burden of >20%, can also occur in apparently healthy subjects, with an estimated prevalence of 1% to 4% in the general population.2

Pre-existing cardiovascular disease increases the incidence and complexity of VPCs, and it could be up to 90% in populations with coronary artery disease (CAD) and dilated cardiomyopathy.3 VPCs are also associated with an increased risk of stroke, with the association being stronger for noncarotid embolic stroke than for thrombotic stroke, and the magnitude increased with increasing VPC burden.4 A meta-analysis analyzing the studies of frequent VPCs in the general population showed an associated increased risk for sudden cardiac death and total cardiac death.5

There are 3 mechanisms by which VPCs are generated, namely re-entry, enhanced automaticity, and triggered activity. The autonomic system has a substantial influence on VPC production through the latter 2 mechanisms as well as an intermediary role in re-entrant VPCs due to its effects on the electrical properties of myocytes.6 Given this autonomic influence on VPC generation, interventions targeting autonomic balance may have therapeutic potential.

Current therapeutic options for patients with symptomatic VPCs include antiarrhythmic drug (AAD) therapy and catheter ablation. Class IC AADs like flecainide, although highly effective at suppressing VPCs, are contraindicated in patients with pre-existing CAD due to their proarrhythmic effect.7 A reduction in VPCs and arrhythmic mortality has been seen with beta-blockers and amiodarone in patients with VPCs and a prior myocardial infarction (MI) or heart failure (HF).8,9 Yoga, a traditional Indian practice, based on the principles of mind-body medicine, has been shown to modulate the cardiac autonomic function using techniques of breathing practices (pranayama), deep relaxation, mindful stretching, and postures (asanas).10, 11, 12 Conditions such as atrial fibrillation and vasovagal syncope, both affected by an unbalanced autonomic tone, have been shown to improve following yoga-based intervention.13,14 Recent systematic reviews on yoga-based randomized controlled trials have reported clinical benefits in patients with cardiovascular disease and HF.15,16 Yoga has also been shown to reduce the symptoms of depression and anxiety, and improve the quality of life (QoL).17,18 Although yoga is known to positively influence autonomic function, its potential to reduce VPC burden in patients with frequent VPCs remains largely unexplored. We sought to assess the effect of a structured yoga-based intervention on the VPC burden, QoL, and left ventricular (LV) function in patients with frequent VPCs.

Methods

This was a single-center, open-label randomized controlled trial to evaluate the efficacy of yoga on clinical outcomes of patients with frequent VPCs (defined as VPC burden of 5% or more for the purpose of the study). Participant enrollment began in July 2021, and final follow-up was completed in March 2025. Eligible participants were randomized into the intervention or control group in a 1:1 ratio. The outcome assessors were blinded to the group allocation, and data analysis was performed by the trial statistician. Eligibility

The study included adults between 15 and 80 years of age with a VPC burden of ≥5% on 24-hour Holter monitoring, an LV ejection fraction (LVEF) ≥35%, and classified as NYHA functional class I or II, with structurally normal or abnormal hearts. Participants were required to be in sinus rhythm, free from active ischemia, on a stable medical regimen for at least 2 weeks before enrollment, and willing and able to practice yoga for 45 minutes at least 3 times per week. If there was a clinical suspicion of myocarditis or inflammatory cardiomyopathy, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging was performed. Individuals were excluded if they were indicated for an early automatic implantable cardioverter-defibrillator or cardiac resynchronization therapy, were pregnant, already practicing yoga, had undergone revascularization within the past 3 months, experienced MI or angina within the previous 8 weeks, lacked sinus rhythm, or were planned for ablation within a month.

Randomization and trial protocol

The study protocol received approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee (Ref. No.: IEC-1045/03.10.2020) and was registered with the Clinical Trials Registry–India (CTRI/2021/06/034450). Participants were recruited from the arrhythmia clinic of the Cardiothoracic Sciences Center at the All India Institute of Medical Sciences (AIIMS), New Delhi. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment. Patient randomization was conducted using a computer-generated random sequence, and allocation was concealed using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. The control group received standard care as per the discretion of the treating physician throughout the study period. Beta-blockers were commonly used, particularly in patients with a history of MI or congestive HF. AAD selection was guided by the presence or absence of structural heart disease, especially CAD. Class IC agents such as flecainide and propafenone were avoided in patients with CAD due to the risk of proarrhythmia and increased mortality. Amiodarone was considered in cases with significant VPC burden and a history of MI or HF. At baseline, participants randomized to the control group were neither practicing yoga nor were enrolled in any structured exercise program. The yoga intervention was delivered at the Center for Integrative Medicine and Research (CIMR), AIIMS, New Delhi. Participants randomized to the yoga group received training based on a structured yoga module specifically developed for this study by certified yoga physicians and therapists at CIMR and further validated by 5 national yoga experts.

The intervention included a sequence of preliminary stretching and loosening exercises, followed by breathing practices, isotonic yoga postures (asanas), pranayama (controlled breathing techniques), and dhyana (meditation) (outlined in Supplemental Table 2). All sessions were conducted under the supervision of certified institutional yoga therapists.

During the first 2 weeks, 6 supervised sessions, conducted in person at CIMR, AIIMS, were held for the participants. Thereafter, they were instructed to continue home-based practice for a minimum of 3 days per week. To support adherence, participants were provided with an illustrated booklet detailing the yoga module. They were encouraged to resolve any queries through phone calls, video communication, or in-person visits to CIMR.

Adherence in the yoga group was defined as participants who attended at least 4 of the 6 supervised sessions and performed at least 3 home-based sessions per week. Compliance with home-based yoga practice was monitored through twice-monthly telephone calls and a daily self-reported yoga practice log maintained by each participant. The overall adherence in the yoga group was 85%. Of the 67 participants randomized to the yoga group, 1 participant did not attend any supervised yoga session but attended home-based sessions. Thus, 66 participants attended at least 1 supervised session, of whom 60 attended at least 4 of the 6 supervised sessions. In addition, 65 participants reported performing home-based yoga practice at least 3 times per week. Overall, 57 of the 67 participants (85%) fulfilled both the predefined supervised attendance and home practice adherence criteria, whereas 10 participants (15%) did not meet the overall adherence criteria although most attended at least 1 supervised session. Of 134 randomized participants 12 were lost to follow-up resulting in overall attrition rate of 9%. Attrition was lower in the yoga group that is (4/67, 6.0%) than in the control group (8/67, 11.9%). All randomized participants (n = 134) were included in the intention-to-treat analysis and no separate response-based analysis was planned or performed. In addition to these regular phone calls, follow-up assessments were conducted at 6 weeks, 12 weeks, and 24 weeks. Outcomes

The primary outcome of the study was the reduction in VPC burden as assessed through 24-hour Holter monitoring (Schiller FD12+ Holter Machine). Secondary outcomes included improvement in QoL measured by the 36-Item Short Form Survey (SF-36) questionnaire, LVEF evaluated using Simpson’s method on echocardiography (Phillips Epiq 7C), assessment of emotional states—depression, anxiety, and stress—using the Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale-21 Items (DASS-21), major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events (MACCE) rates, and changes in heart rate variability (HRV) assessed via Holter monitoring.

Statistical analysis

For this pilot randomized trial, the sample size was calculated assuming a medium effect (Cohen’s d = 0.5) with α = 0.05, 80% power and a 1:1 allocation. The calculation yields 64 participants per group (N = 128). Allowing for a 5% attrition rate increases the required sample to 67 participants per group (N = 134).

An intention-to-treat analysis was performed to preserve randomization, including all randomized participants regardless of protocol adherence, and to provide an unbiased estimate of the intervention effect. The missing values were imputed using the baseline observation carried forward method. Normality of continuous outcomes was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test.

As the data were not normally distributed (P < 0.05), continuous variables are presented as median with IQR (Q1, Q3) and categorical variables as frequency (percentage).

The primary outcome variable, VPC burden was not normally distributed at baseline. To compare groups at follow-up (6 and 12 weeks) while adjusting for baseline differences in ventricular burden (VB), a rank-based analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed. In this approach, the outcome variable at each time point was transformed into ranks before analysis to minimize the impact of non-normality. The treatment group was included as the independent variable, and baseline VPC burden was included as a covariate.

The assumption of homogeneity of regression slopes was evaluated by testing the interaction between groups and baseline VB. As the interaction term was not statistically significant, the assumption was considered satisfied.

Adjusted between-group differences were assessed using estimated marginal means (EMMs) along with 95% CIs. Pair-wise comparisons between groups were derived from the EMMs, and corresponding P values were reported. Effect size was quantified using partial eta squared (η2p) obtained from the ANCOVA model.

As a sensitivity analysis, a linear mixed-effects model was performed to evaluate the robustness of the primary findings. The model included fixed effects for groups (control vs yoga), time (6 and 12 weeks), and group × time interaction, with participants included as a random intercept to account for within-subject correlation. Baseline VB was included as a covariate. EMMs with 95% CIs were reported, and between-group differences at each time point were derived from the model (Supplemental Tables 4 and 5).

For secondary outcomes, delta change scores were computed from baseline. Between-group differences were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-square or Fisher exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. The effect size was also calculated using the rank-biserial method.

A 2-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R-Studio (version 2024.12.1 + 563).

Outcomes and follow-up

VPC burden

At 6 weeks, VPC burden decreased in both groups (control: 19.19 [7.50, 30.70]; yoga: 11.62 [5.83, 21.59]). However, after adjusting for baseline values using rank ANCOVA, there was no statistically significant difference between groups (P = 0.588), with a negligible effect size (η2p = 0.002). At 12 weeks, a further reduction in VPC burden was observed in both groups, with a greater reduction in the yoga group (control: 16.89 [6.45, 30.69]; yoga: 6.55 [1.75, 15.93]). After adjustment for baseline, the difference between groups was statistically significant (P = 0.02) (Table 2). At 12 weeks, findings were consistent with the result obtained from the linear mixed model analysis, which was conducted as a sensitivity analysis (Supplemental Table 4). The EMMs of VPC burden were higher in the control group compared to the yoga group, with a mean difference of 11.45 (95% CI: 1.81-21.09), corresponding to a small effect size (η2p = 0.04) (Table 2).

The yoga group demonstrated a reduction in VPC burden compared to controls at both 6 and 12 weeks, reaching statistical significance at 12 weeks (P < 0.001), indicating a significant and sustained treatment effect of yoga in these patients group (Central Illustration). Most participants in both groups were on beta-blockers.

Secondary outcomes

Quality of life indicators (SF-36)

The yoga group showed significant improvements across multiple SF-36 domains, including Physical Functioning, Role Limitations due to Physical Health, Emotional Problems, Energy/Fatigue, Emotional Well-being, Social Functioning, Pain, General Health, and Health Change (P < 0.05, at 24 weeks) (Table 3).

LVEF

The median baseline LVEF was similar in both groups (54.4% in the control group vs 55.1% in the yoga group). Discussion

This randomized controlled trial evaluated the effect of yoga as an adjunct to standard care in patients with frequent VPCs. Patients randomized to the yoga group showed a significant reduction in the VPC burden at 12 weeks, along with significant improvements in QoL and psychological well-being. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first randomized trial to systematically evaluate yoga as an add-on therapy in this population.

Frequent VPCs are clinically important as they are linked to adverse cardiovascular outcomes and symptoms like palpitations, breathlessness, fatigue, and anxiety, substantially impairing QoL. Even asymptomatic frequent VPCs (>10%-20% burden) may impair LV function, cause VPC-induced cardiomyopathy, and increase sudden cardiac death and cardiovascular mortality risks.19 Current therapeutic options for patients with frequent VPCs include AADs, which are useful but often limited by side effects, contraindications, and incomplete efficacy response. Catheter ablation is generally considered for symptomatic, drug-refractory frequent VPCs, and suspected VPC-induced cardiomyopathy.7 Although effective in selected patients, it remains invasive, costly, and not universally accessible.20,21 Yoga, with its inherent effect on autonomic modulation, represents a simple, low-cost, and potentially scalable intervention, making it particularly attractive in resource-limited settings and for those whom long-term pharmacological therapy is undesirable.22,23

In our study, the patient populations in the 2 groups were similar at baseline with respect to age, sex, and underlying etiology; however, baseline VPC burden was lower in the yoga group. This difference in the baseline VPC burden was adjusted for using rank ANCOVA.

Both the yoga and control groups had reduced VPC burden at 6 weeks individually, but the between-group difference was not significant after adjustment. By 12 weeks, however, the yoga group demonstrated a significantly greater reduction compared with control group. This delayed yet progressive effect suggests that the benefits of yoga, including autonomic remodeling, may build gradually over time. As expected with behavioral interventions, the overall effect size was modest, but the widening treatment effect over time supports sustained benefits. Prior studies have suggested that relaxation-based practices may reduce ventricular ectopy, supporting the potential role of mind-body interventions; however, these studies were limited by small sample sizes and nonrandomized designs.24,25

Our study also demonstrated meaningful improvements in QoL, with benefits extending beyond arrhythmia reduction to overall well-being. This is clinically relevant because patients with frequent VPCs often experience persistent symptoms and arrhythmia-related anxiety that adversely affects physical, emotional, and social functioning. Improvements were observed across all SF-36 domains, including physical functioning, emotional well-being, energy levels, and social functioning, along with reductions in psychological burden as assessed by DASS-21. These findings are consistent with a prior study that evaluated yoga as an adjunct therapeutic modality in patients with paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.13 The American Heart Association Scientific Statements have also highlighted the potential role of meditation and related mind-body practices in cardiovascular risk reduction.26

With increasing recognition of arrhythmia-induced cardiomyopathy, reduction in arrhythmia burden has been shown to improve ventricular function, particularly in patients with baseline systolic dysfunction.27 In our study, however, the reduction in VPC burden was not accompanied by a significant change in LV function, likely reflecting the largely preserved baseline ventricular function in both groups and the modest arrhythmia burden studied. In addition, the 12-week duration may have been too short to reflect reverse remodeling. Similarly, HRV parameters did not differ significantly between groups, which may reflect the multifactorial nature of HRV responses, with similar observations reported in yoga-based cardiac rehabilitation trials post-MI.28 It is also plausible that factors such as baseline autonomic status and the use of beta-blockers may have attenuated the HRV differences. It may be the case that a longer follow-up is required for meaningful changes in autonomic indices alongside sustained reductions in VPC burden.

Autonomic dysregulation plays an important role in arrhythmogenesis. We postulate that the mechanisms underlying the VPC-lowering effects of yoga are multifactorial. Yoga’s emphasis on practices such as pranayama and mindfulness reduces sympathetic overactivity and enhances parasympathetic tone.29 Similarly, breath-based interventions, such as mindful slow breathing and bee-humming breathing practices, promote autonomic rebalancing.12 The positive effect of yoga in cardiac dysautonomia-associated disorders, such as vasovagal syncope and paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, has been demonstrated.14,30 In addition, physical postures incorporated in the structured yoga module contribute to improved cardiovascular fitness and lower the systemic inflammation.15,16,31 The yoga module was also devised to include relaxation techniques to address stress with the goal of mitigating the production of cortisol and catecholamines that contribute to arrhythmia initiation and perpetuation.32

The key strengths of this trial include the use of a specially designed structured yoga module validated by independent yoga experts. Along with the arrhythmia burden, the study also incorporated assessments of well-being and psychological health. The inclusion of multiple follow-up time points enabled us to study the temporality of yoga's effects, demonstrating both early and sustained benefits. Supervised sessions and adherence monitoring enhanced intervention fidelity and interpretability of the results.

Study Limitations

The modest sample size and single-center design may limit the generalizability of the findings. A longer follow-up would have been useful to demonstrate long-term effects. The absence of an active control group and blinding, as is frequent in behavioral trials weakens causal attribution, although outcome assessors were blinded. As most participants had idiopathic VPCs, applicability to structural heart disease or other VPC types remains uncertain.

Conclusions

Yoga-based adjunct intervention in patients with frequent VPCs significantly reduced VPC burden and improved psychological well-being and QoL. The additional cost-effectiveness and logistic favorability of yoga makes it an attractive add-on therapeutic option in these patients.PerspectivesCOMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: A structured VPC-directed yoga-based intervention significantly reduces VPC burden and improves QoL and psychological burden in patients with frequent VPCs.
