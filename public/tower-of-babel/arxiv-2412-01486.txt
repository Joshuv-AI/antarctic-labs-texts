Schauder estimates for germs by scaling
Jonas Sauer, Scott Andrew Smith

Abstract
. In this expository note, we show that the blow-up arguments of L. Simon adapt well to the corresponding Schauder theory of germs used in the study of singular SPDEs. We illustrate this through some representative examples. As in the classical PDE framework, the argument relies only on the scaling properties of the germ semi-norms and the Liouville principle for the operator.

Introduction

Recent work on pathwise approaches to singular parabolic SPDEs have two central analytic ingredients, known as ‘reconstruction’ and ‘integration’ in the terminology of rough paths, regularity structures, and paracontrolled calculus (
[
10

[

(\partial_{t}-\Delta)u=\sigma(u)\xi,

[

\sigma(u)\xi

\xi

(2)

(\partial_{t}-\Delta)u=-u^{3}+\xi,

the Da-Prato/Debussche

6

(\partial_{t}-\Delta)v=\xi

[
17

Writing down a single explicit equation for the remainder is often not possible or natural for many SPDEs of interest. The basic idea in the rough path/regularity structures approach to singular SPDEs is to instead consider a remainder depending on a base-point, so there is actually a ‘family of remainders’ each satisfying a slightly different equation. For example, freezing in the diffusion coefficient
\sigma(u)
near a space-time point

(\partial_{t}-\Delta)

One can then try to analyze the family of functions
(U_{z})_{z}

[

3

A typical goal is to show that despite
u
and
v

\alpha

U_{z}(w)
vanishes to order
\eta>\alpha

This is accomplished with the Schauder estimate for germs.

2.
An Illustration of the Method

In this section, we illustrate the main idea of how to use Simon’s
[
33
]
blow-up arguments for elliptic/parabolic regularity of germs. The basic setup is that we have an ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a subset
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The most relevant examples to keep in mind are when
D
is simply an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
or a lattice. We fix a grading of each of the

\mathfrak{s}=(\mathfrak{s}_{1},\cdots,\mathfrak{s}_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
, which determines a notion of degree: namely, for a multi-index
\gamma\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
we introduce

(3)

\displaystyle|\gamma|:=\mathfrak{s}\cdot\gamma=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathfrak{s}_{i}\gamma_{i}.

We are interested in the Schauder theory of an elliptic operator with constant (complex) coefficients of the form

(4)

\mathcal{L}=\sum_{|\gamma|=m}a_{\gamma}\partial^{\gamma},

with
m\in\mathbb{N}
. In the present context, ellipticity means that for every domain
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and distribution
u\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(D)
one has
u\in C^{\infty}(D)
if
\mathcal{L}u\in C^{\infty}(D)
, cf. Section 2 in
[
35
]
.
3

3

Some authors use the terminology hypoelliptic, but since we only consider scaling homogeneous operators with constant coefficients, we use the term elliptic to avoid confusion.
For most of the present section, we have in mind second order operators
\mathcal{L}
, the simplest examples being the Laplacian and the heat operator (which is of order
2
in an appropriate anisotropic framework), the treatment of general operators is postponed to Section

As alluded to in the introduction, for applications to singular SPDE one requires a Schauder theory not only for functions, but for germs, which we now define.

Definition 1

(Germs)
.

A germ
U
over a subset
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a family
(U_{x})_{x\in D}
of continuous functions
y\in D\mapsto U_{x}(y)\in\mathbb{C}

To measure germs, we use a generalization of the classical Hölder semi-norms. Recall that the scaling
\mathfrak{s}
determines a natural anisotropic distance
4

4

To lighten notation, in comparison to

Throughout the paper, given a base-point

the anisotropic ball with respect to

.

The basic goal of Schauder theory for germs is to estimate the following
G^{\eta}(D)

\|U\|_{G^{\eta}(D)}

The above norm appears already in a one-dimensional setting in the work of Gubinelli

Remark 1
.

In general, if

\mathbb{R}^{d}
, one may wish to include an additional weight in (

Typically, we refer to

Classical Schauder estimates, cf.
[
33
]

\mathcal{L}U
is understood to be a family of distributions
(\mathcal{L}U_{x})_{x\in D}
, that is
\mathcal{L}
is understood to be applied to the active variable with a

is a distribution-valued germ in the following sense.

Definition 2

(Distribution-Valued Germs)
.

A distribution-valued germ

over an open subset
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a family
(V_{x})_{x\in D}
where each
V_{x}\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(D)

We now introduce the
G^{\gamma}(D)
norm for
\gamma<0
. Let
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denote the subset of functions
\varphi

,

5

For simplicity, we don’t indicate the specific integer

Given a base-point

[V]_{G^{\gamma}(D)}:=\sup_{\varphi\in\mathcal{B}}\sup_{x\in D}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda>0\\

Note that the constraint

In the Schauder theory of germs, controlling (

, which we now introduce.
For an integer
k

\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denote the polynomials of anisotropic degree (according to (

k
. For
0<\alpha<\eta
, we define the

[U]_{G^{\eta,\alpha}(D)}

M>0
such that for all

P\in\mathcal{P}_{\lfloor\eta\rfloor}
such that for all

(8)

\big|(U_{x}-U_{y}-P)(z)\big|\leq M{d(y,z)^{\alpha}\big(d(x,y)+d(y,z)\big)^{\eta-\alpha}}.

Like (
6

[
11
]
in the one dimensional setting, where the polynomial there is of degree zero. The reader might be curious why the above semi-norm does not appear in classical Schauder theory: see Remark

We now comment on the scaling properties of the above semi-norms, as this is an important ingredient of the method of Simon
[
33
]
. To this end, we introduce some further notation: for
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
R>0
we define the rescaling/recentering map
S^{R}_{w}
by

\displaystyle y\mapsto S_{w}^{R}y:=w+R^{\mathfrak{s}}y,

where
R^{\mathfrak{s}}y:=(R^{\mathfrak{s}_{1}}y_{1},\ldots,R^{\mathfrak{s}_{d}}y_{d})
. This transformation has a natural action on germs. Indeed, given a germ
U
over
D
, we denote by
S^{R}_{w}U
the germ over
(S^{R}_{w})^{-1}D
defined by

(9)

\big(S^{R}_{w}U\big)_{x}:=U_{S^{R}_{w}x}\circ S^{R}_{w}.

Observe that in terms of the scaling transformations, we can also write
\varphi_{x}^{\lambda}=\lambda^{-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathfrak{s}_{i}}\varphi\circ(S_{x}^{\lambda})^{-1}
. Thus, for a distributional germ
V
over an open subset
D
, the change of variables formula suggests to define a distributional germ
S^{R}_{w}V
over
(S^{R}_{w})^{-1}D
via

\langle\big(S^{R}_{w}V\big)_{x},\varphi\rangle:=\langle V_{S^{R}_{w}x},\varphi^{R}_{w}\rangle.

The following lemma is then an elementary consequence of change of variables.

Lemma 1
.

Let
R>0
and
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For a subset
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
0<\alpha<\eta
the following identities hold for all germs
U
over
D

(10)

\|S^{R}_{w}U\|_{G^{\eta}((S^{R}_{w})^{-1}D)}=R^{\eta}\|U\|_{{G^{\eta}(D)}},\quad[S^{R}_{w}U]_{G^{\eta,\alpha}((S^{R}_{w})^{-1}D)}=R^{\eta}[U]_{{G^{\eta,\alpha}(D)}}.

Furthermore, if
D
is open and
\gamma<0
, then for all distributional germs
V
over
D
it holds

(11)

[S^{R}_{w}V]_{G^{\gamma}((S^{R}_{w})^{-1}D)}=R^{\gamma}[V]_{{G^{\gamma}(D)}}.

Proof.

For the first identity, observe that for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
it holds
d(S^{R}_{w}x,S^{R}_{w}y)=Rd(x,y)
, so the claim follows from observing that
S^{R}_{w}
is a bijection from
(S^{R}_{w})^{-1}D
to
D
. The second identity uses the previous observation, together with the bijection from
\mathcal{P}_{\lfloor\eta\rfloor}
to
\mathcal{P}_{\lfloor\eta\rfloor}
given by
P\mapsto P\circ(S_{w}^{R})^{-1}
.
For the third identity, we first note the following: for any
\lambda,\rho>0
and
x,z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
it holds

(12)

S_{x}^{\lambda}\circ S_{z}^{\rho}=S_{S_{x}^{\lambda}z}^{\rho\lambda},\qquad(S_{x}^{\lambda})^{-1}=S_{-(\lambda^{-1})^{\mathfrak{s}}x}^{\lambda^{-1}}.

Hence, we find that

(13)

\langle(S^{R}_{w}V)_{x},\varphi_{x}^{\lambda}\rangle=\langle V_{S^{R}_{w}x},(\varphi^{\lambda}_{x})^{R}_{w}\rangle=\langle V_{S^{R}_{w}x},\varphi^{R\lambda}_{S_{w}^{R}x}\rangle,

where we used (
12
) to obtain

(\varphi^{\lambda}_{x})^{R}_{w}=(\lambda R)^{-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathfrak{s}_{i}}\varphi\circ(S^{\lambda}_{x})^{-1}\circ(S^{R}_{w})^{-1}=(\lambda R)^{-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathfrak{s}_{i}}\varphi\circ(S^{R}_{w}\circ S^{\lambda}_{x})^{-1}=\varphi^{R\lambda}_{S_{w}^{R}x}.

The third identity now follows directly from (
13
) taking into account that
B_{\lambda}(x)\subseteq(S^{R}_{w})^{-1}D
if and only if
B_{R\lambda}(S_{w}^{R}x)\subseteq D
.
∎

For future reference, we note that (
11
) is particularly useful in the form

(14)

[\mathcal{L}S^{R}_{w}U]_{G^{\gamma}((S^{R}_{w})^{-1}D)}=R^{\gamma+m}[\mathcal{L}U]_{{G^{\gamma}(D)}},

which follows from noting that
\mathcal{L}S^{R}_{w}=R^{m}S^{R}_{w}\mathcal{L}
.

2.1.
From Germ Bounds to Hölder Bounds

In typical applications to singular SPDE, see for example
[
26
]
or
[
27
]
, the parameter
\alpha
in the
G^{\eta,\alpha}(D)
semi-norm is known in advance to be the expected optimal Hölder regularity of the function
U_{x}
for a fixed base-point
x
.
For instance, the germ based on freezing of coefficients of (
1
) mentioned in the introduction would have Hölder regularity to order
\alpha<\frac{1}{2}
for space-time white noise.
This however is generally not sufficient to apply the reconstruction theorem and obtain pathwise bounds on the (renormalized) non-linear term in (
1
).
The aim of Schauder theory for germs is to obtain a form of higher regularity
\eta>\alpha
, then retrieve traditional Hölder regularity as a corollary.
In fact, it turns out to be useful for the proof of the Schauder estimate to collect this corollary in advance: namely, for suitable subsets
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, if
\|U\|_{G^{\eta}(D)}+[U]_{G^{\eta,\alpha}(D)}<\infty
, then each
U_{x}
is (locally)
\alpha
-Hölder continuous.

Lemma 2
.

Let
0<\alpha<1<\eta<2
and assume
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a subset with the following property: for each
i\in[d]
with
\mathfrak{s}_{i}=1
, if
x,y\in D
then
y+d(x,y)\mathbf{e}_{i}\in D
.
Then for all germs
U
over
D
and all
R>0

(15)

\sup_{x\in D}[U_{x}]_{C^{\alpha}(D\cap B_{R}(x))}\lesssim\big(\|U\|_{G^{\eta}(D)}+[U]_{G^{\eta,\alpha}(D)}\big)R^{\eta-\alpha}.

Remark 2
.

The restriction
\eta\in(1,2)
is made for simplicity of exposition in this section, as this is the typical case of interest for applications to second order sub-critical SPDEs. It will be removed in Section
3
where we consider a more general class of operators.

Proof.

Let
U
be a germ with
\|U\|_{G^{\eta}(D)}+[U]_{G^{\eta,\alpha}(D)}<\infty
. Fix a base point
x\in D
, a radius
R>0
, and let
y,z\in D\cap B_{R}(x)
. We would like to estimate the increment
U_{x}(z)-U_{x}(y)
, and we first observe that since
[U]_{G^{\eta,\alpha}(D)}<\infty
and
\eta\in(1,2)
, there exists a polynomial
P_{x,y}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}
such that

(16)

\displaystyle\big|U_{x}(z)-U_{y}(z)-P_{x,y}(z)\big|

\displaystyle\leq 2[U]_{G^{\eta,\alpha}(D)}d(y,z)^{\alpha}\big(d(y,z)+d(x,y)\big)^{\eta-\alpha}

(17)

\displaystyle\lesssim[U]_{G^{\eta,\alpha}(D)}d(y,z)^{\alpha}R^{\eta-\alpha},

where we used the triangle inequality in the form
d(y,z)\leq 2R
in the second step. Since
\|U\|_{G^{\eta}(D)}<\infty
, we have
|U_{y}(z)|\leq\|U\|_{G^{\eta}(D)}d(y,z)^{\eta}\lesssim\|U\|_{G^{\eta}(D)}d(y,z)^{\alpha}R^{\eta-\alpha}
.
By the triangle inequality, (
17
) hence turns into

(18)

\big|U_{x}(z)-P_{x,y}(z)\big|\lesssim\big([U]_{G^{\eta,\alpha}(D)}+\|U\|_{G^{\eta}(D)}\big)d(y,z)^{\alpha}R^{\eta-\alpha}.

Note that
P_{x,y}(y)=U_{x}(y)
, so

(19)

P_{x,y}=U_{x}(y)+\sum_{i\in[d],\mathfrak{s}_{i}=1}\gamma^{i}_{x}(y)(\cdot-y)_{i}

for some
\gamma^{i}_{x}(y)\in\mathbb{R}
. We claim that for each
i\in[d]
with
\mathfrak{s}_{i}=1
,

(20)

\|\gamma^{i}\|_{G^{\eta-1}(D)}\lesssim\|U\|_{G^{\eta}(D)}+[U]_{G^{\eta,\alpha}(D)}.

Combining this with
|P_{x,y}(z)-U_{x}(y)|\leq\|\gamma^{i}\|_{G^{\eta-1}(D)}d(x,y)^{\eta-1}d(y,z)\leq\|\gamma^{i}\|_{G^{\eta-1}(D)}R^{\eta-\alpha}d(y,z)^{\alpha}
implies (
15
).

We now give the argument for (
20
) following Step 6 in Proposition 2 of
[
27
]
, which is modelled on the proof of Lemma 3.3 in
[
28
]
. Fix
i\in[d]
with
\mathfrak{s}_{i}=1
and consider any
x,y\in D
. By assumption, the point
w:=y+\mathbf{e}_{i}d(x,y)
belongs to
D
and from the definition (
5
), we see that
d(y,w)=d(x,y)
. From (
16
) (with
w
in place of
z
), (
19
) and the triangle inequality

\displaystyle|\gamma_{x}^{i}(y)|d(x,y)

\displaystyle\lesssim\big(\|U\|_{G^{\eta}(D)}+[U]_{G^{\eta,\alpha}(D)}\big)d(x,y)^{\eta}+\big|U_{x}(w)-U_{x}(y)-U_{y}(w)\big|

(21)

\displaystyle\lesssim(\|U\|_{G^{\eta}(D)}+[U]_{G^{\eta,\alpha}(D)}\big)d(x,y)^{\eta},

where we used the definition (
6
) and the triangle inequality in the last step, completing the argument for (
20
) and hence the proof of the Lemma.
∎

We now turn to a related property of the germ
\mathcal{L}U
, where we assume
\mathcal{L}
is given by (
4
). Namely, if
D
is an open subset and
\mathcal{L}U
has a finite

semi-norm for

and

has a finite

semi-norm, then for each base-point

, the function
\mathcal{L}U_{x}
is Hölder continuous of order

.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be given by (
4
) and
0<\alpha<\eta<m
and
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be open. Then for all germs
U
over
D
and
R>0

(22)

\sup_{x\in D}\|\mathcal{L}U_{x}\|_{C^{\alpha-m}(D\cap B_{R}(x))}\lesssim\big(\|\mathcal{L}U\|_{G^{\eta-m}(D)}+[U]_{G^{\eta,\alpha}(D)}\big)R^{\eta-\alpha}

Proof.

Let
x,y\in D
and
\lambda>0
satisfy
B_{\lambda}(y)\subseteq B_{R}(x)\cap D
. By the triangle inequality, for
\varphi\in\mathcal{B}
it holds

\displaystyle\big|\langle\mathcal{L}U_{x},\varphi_{y}^{\lambda}\rangle\big|

\displaystyle\leq\big|\langle\mathcal{L}U_{y},\varphi_{y}^{\lambda}\rangle\big|+\big|\langle U_{y}-U_{x},\mathcal{L}^{*}\varphi_{y}^{\lambda}\rangle\big|

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{L}U\|_{G^{\eta-m}(D)}\lambda^{\eta-m}+[U]_{G^{\eta,\alpha}(D)}\langle d(y,\cdot)^{\alpha}(d(x,y)+d(y,\cdot))^{\eta-\alpha},|\mathcal{L}^{*}\varphi^{\lambda}_{y}|\rangle

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\mathcal{L}U\|_{G^{\eta-m}(D)}\lambda^{\eta-m}+[U]_{G^{\eta,\alpha}(D)}\lambda^{\alpha-m}\big(d(x,y)^{\eta-\alpha}+\lambda^{\eta-\alpha}\big)

\displaystyle\lesssim\big(\|\mathcal{L}U\|_{G^{\eta-m}(D)}+[U]_{G^{\eta,\alpha}(D)}\big)\lambda^{\alpha-m}R^{\eta-\alpha},

where we used that
\mathcal{L}P=0
for any
P\in\mathcal{P}_{m-1}
together with the inequalities

and
\eta>\alpha
.

) and (

). We will prove the following Schauder estimate.

Theorem 1
.

Let
0<\alpha<1<\eta<2
. There exists a constant
C>0
such that for all germs
U
with
\|U\|_{G^{\eta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}<\infty
it holds

(23)

\|U\|_{G^{\eta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C\big([\Delta U]_{G^{\eta-2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+[U]_{G^{\eta,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\big).

The two main ingredients for Simon’s
[
33
]
method are
scaling
and
Liouville’s theorem
. The outline of the argument is as follows: since we can always multiply (
23
) by a constant, an equivalent statement is: there exists a
C>0
such for all germs with
\|U\|_{G^{\eta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=1
,

[\Delta U]_{G^{\eta-2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+[U]_{G^{\eta,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\geq C^{-1}.

Hence, if Theorem
1
is false, there exists a sequence of germs
U^{n}
with
\|U^{n}\|_{G^{\eta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=1
for which
[\Delta U^{n}]_{G^{\eta-2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+[U^{n}]_{G^{\eta,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
converges to zero.
The strategy is to use this information to construct a harmonic function which violates Liouville’s theorem.
To pass from germs to functions, we observe first that
\|U^{n}\|_{G^{\eta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=1
implies there is a base-point
x^{n}
where a ‘concentration’ occurs, namely there exists
y^{n}
for which
(R^{n})^{-\eta}|U_{x^{n}}^{n}(y^{n})|\geq\frac{1}{2}
, where
R^{n}:=d(x^{n},y^{n})
. We re-center and re-scale around
x^{n}
by setting

y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto u^{n}(y):=(R^{n})^{-\eta}U^{n}_{x^{n}}(x^{n}+R^{n}y).

The aim is to show that (modulo a subsequence),
u^{n}
converge locally uniformly to a harmonic function
u
with sub-quadratic growth, namely
|u(y)|\leq d(0,y)^{\eta}
. This will turn out to contradict Liouville’s theorem as in the classical work
[
33
]
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

If the statement is false, there exists a sequence of germs
U^{n}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\|U^{n}\|_{G^{\eta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=1
and
\|\Delta U^{n}\|_{G^{\eta-2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+[U^{n}]_{G^{\eta,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq\frac{1}{n}
. In particular, there exists
x^{n},y^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that for
R^{n}:=d(x^{n},y^{n})
, it holds

(24)

(R^{n})^{-\eta}|U_{x^{n}}^{n}(y^{n})|\geq\frac{1}{2}.

Recalling (
9
), consider the re-centered/re-scaled germs
(R^{n})^{-\eta}S^{R_{n}}_{x^{n}}U^{n}
and define a sequence of functions
u^{n}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
by choosing the base-point to be the origin.
That is, let
u^{n}:=(R^{n})^{-\eta}\big(S^{R_{n}}_{x^{n}}U^{n}\big)_{0}
. By scaling relations in Lemma
1
, combined with Lemmas
2
and
3
, and (
14
) we find for all
R>0

(25)

[u^{n}]_{C^{\alpha}(B_{R})}\lesssim R^{\eta-\alpha},\qquad\|\Delta u^{n}\|_{C^{\alpha-2}(B_{R})}\lesssim\frac{1}{n}R^{\eta},\qquad\sup_{y\neq 0}d(y,0)^{-\eta}|u^{n}(y)|\leq 1.

The Arzelà-Ascoli theorem provides compactness along a subsequence (not re-labelled), along with locally uniform convergence to a continuous limit
u:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
with sub-quadratic growth

(26)

\sup_{y\neq 0}d(0,y)^{-\eta}|u(y)|\leq 1.

Hence, by the second estimate in (
25
) it holds
\|\Delta u\|_{C^{\alpha-2}(B_{R})}=0
for all
R>0
, which implies
u
is harmonic.
Due to the upper bound (
26
) and
\eta\in(1,2)
, it follows from Liouville’s theorem that
u
is a polynomial of degree at most one, but the only polynomial satisfying (
26
) is identically zero. On the other hand, (
24
) implies
|u^{n}(z^{n})|\geq\frac{1}{2}
for
z^{n}:=(S^{R^{n}}_{x^{n}})^{-1}y^{n}
. Since
d(z^{n},0)=1
, the sequence
z^{n}
has a subsequential limit
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d(z,0)=1
, and due to the the local uniform convergence it holds
|u(z)|\geq\frac{1}{2}
, a contradiction.
∎

Remark 3
.

It is not too difficult to see that the above argument is robust enough to replace
\Delta
by any scaling homogeneous elliptic operator on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with constant coefficients and relax the restriction on
\eta
. Indeed, the only place which requires an additional argument is the bound (
15
) needed for compactness, which requires us to account for higher order polynomials in Lemma
2
. This is carried out in Section
3
below, see Lemma

.

2.3.
The Heat Operator on
(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}

In this section, we discuss how to adapt the ideas from Section
2.2
to analyze the initial value problem for the heat operator
\partial_{t}-\Delta
.
In line with Remark
3
, since this operator is elliptic, the corresponding full-space estimate (
23
) follows from an identical argument if we replace
\Delta
by
\partial_{t}-\Delta
, see Theorem
4
below.
Thus, in the present section our aim is to address the additional subtleties that arise from the presence of an initial time boundary. We use the ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
in this sub-section with the scaling
\mathfrak{s}=(2,1,\dots,1)
as well as the notation
x=(x_{0},x_{1})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
to distinguish time and space.

In comparison with the previous section, the Schauder estimate requires a control on the germ at the initial time
(0,x_{1})
. We denote the initial germ by
U\mid_{x_{0}=0}
, which is a germ on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
defined for a base-point
x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and active variable
y_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
by
U_{(0,x_{1})}(0,y_{1})
. The inclusion of a term monitoring the size of the initial behaviour mirrors the classical Schauder estimate for the heat equation

\displaystyle[u]_{C^{\alpha+2}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C\big([(\partial_{t}-\Delta)u]_{C^{\alpha}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}+[u(0,\cdot)]_{C^{\alpha+2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\big),

see e.g. Exercise 9.1.4 in
[
19
]
.
In the setting of germs, the estimate takes the following form.

Theorem 2
.

Let
0<\alpha<1<\eta<2
. There exists a universal constant
C
such that for all
T>0
and germs
U
over
[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\|U\|_{G^{\eta}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}<\infty
it holds

\displaystyle\|U\|_{G^{\eta}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C\big([(\partial_{t}-\Delta)U]_{G^{\eta-2}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}+[U]_{G^{\eta,\alpha}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\|U\mid_{x_{0}=0}\|_{G^{\eta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\big).

Proof.

We use
\mathcal{L}
to denote
\partial_{t}-\Delta
throughout the proof.
If the statement is false, there exists a sequence of times
T^{n}>0
and germs
U^{n}
over
[0,T^{n})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
[U^{n}]_{G^{\eta}((0,T^{n})\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}=1
while
[\mathcal{L}U^{n}]_{G^{\eta-2}((0,T^{n})\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\|U^{n}\|_{G^{\eta,\alpha}((0,T^{n})\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}+[U^{n}\mid_{x_{0}=0}]_{G^{\eta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq\frac{1}{n}
.
In particular, there exist
x^{n},y^{n}\in(0,T^{n})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that for
R^{n}:=d(x^{n},y^{n})
, it holds that
(R^{n})^{-\eta}|U_{x^{n}}^{n}(y^{n})|\geq\frac{1}{2}
.
We consider the re-centered/re-scaled germ
(R^{n})^{-\eta}S_{x^{n}}^{R^{n}}U^{n}
over

D^{n}:=\big(S_{x^{n}}^{R^{n}}\big)^{-1}\big((0,T^{n})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\big)=(R^{n})^{-2}(-x^{n}_{0},T^{n}-x^{n}_{0})\times\mathbb{R}^{d},

and define
u^{n}:D^{n}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
by
u^{n}:=(R^{n})^{-\eta}\big(S_{x^{n}}^{R^{n}}U^{n}\big)_{0}
. To obtain Hölder bounds, we rely again on Lemma
2
, noting in advance that
\mathfrak{s}_{i}=1
only for
i>1
, so the desired property of
D
is trivially satisfied in the spatial directions. Combining Lemmas
1
,
2
and
3
and keeping in mind (
14
), we find that for all
R>0

(27)

[u^{n}]_{C^{\alpha}(B_{R}\cap D^{n})}\lesssim R^{\eta-\alpha},\qquad\|\mathcal{L}u^{n}\|_{C^{\alpha-2}(B_{R}\cap D^{n})}\lesssim\frac{1}{n}R^{\eta},\qquad\sup_{y\in D^{n},y\neq 0}d(y,0)^{-\eta}|u^{n}(y)|\leq 1.

Furthermore, due to the inequality
[(R^{n})^{-\eta}S^{R^{n}}_{x^{n}}U^{n}\mid_{x_{0}=0}]_{G^{\eta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq\frac{1}{n}
, the third estimate can be improved along the initial time boundary
y_{0}=-(R^{n})^{-2}x^{n}_{0}
to

(28)

\sup_{y_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},y\neq 0}|y_{1}|_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{-\eta}|u^{n}(-(R^{n})^{-2}x^{n}_{0},y_{1})|\lesssim\frac{1}{n}.

Furthermore, for
z_{n}:=\big(S_{x^{n}}^{R^{n}}\big)^{-1}y^{n}

it holds
|u^{n}(z^{n})|\geq\frac{1}{2}
.

To obtain compactness, we can choose an extension
\hat{u}^{n}
of
u^{n}
to
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
while preserving the first uniform bound in (
27
), i.e.,
[\hat{u}^{n}]_{C^{\alpha}(B_{R})}\lesssim R^{\eta-\alpha}
.
In fact, by Theorem 2 of
[
23
]
, we may extend
u^{n}
for each
R>0
uniquely to
\overline{B_{R}\cap D^{n}}
such that
[u^{n}]_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{B_{R}\cap D^{n}})}\lesssim[u^{n}]_{C^{\alpha}(B_{R}\cap D^{n})}\lesssim R^{\eta-\alpha}
.
Since
R>0
is arbitrary, this yields a (unique) continuous continuation of
u^{n}
to
\overline{D_{n}}
.
Then
\hat{u}^{n}
can be defined as this continuation on
\overline{D_{n}}
and by extending the values on the boundary of the the time-space strip
\overline{D_{n}}
forwards and backwards in time, respectively.
By Arzelà-Ascoli’s Theorem, we find that
\hat{u}^{n}
converges (along a subsequence) locally uniformly to a continuous limit
\hat{u}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
.

We now argue that
\hat{u}
satisfies
\mathcal{L}\hat{u}=0
on a suitable domain
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
. To this end, first observe that from (
27
), for all
\varphi\in\mathcal{B}
and bounded sequences
(y^{n},\lambda^{n})\in D^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
B_{\lambda^{n}}(y^{n})\subseteq D^{n}
it holds

(29)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\langle\mathcal{L}u^{n},\varphi^{\lambda^{n}}_{y^{n}}\rangle=0.

This is an immediate consequence of taking
R
sufficiently large and using the definition of the
C^{\alpha-2}(B_{R}\cap D^{n})
semi-norm. Passing to a further subsequence if necessary, we may assume that
-(R^{n})^{-2}x^{n}_{0}
converges to
T_{1}\in[-\infty,0]
and
(R^{n})^{-2}(T^{n}-x^{n}_{0})
converges to
T_{2}\in[0,\infty]
. Note if
T_{1}>-\infty
, then by (
28
) it holds that
\hat{u}(T_{1},\cdot)=0
and hence if
T_{1}=T_{2}=0
, then
\hat{u}(0,\cdot)
is identically zero. However, in this case
z_{0}^{n}\to 0
so that
|\hat{u}(0,z_{1})|\geq\frac{1}{2}
for some
z_{1}\neq 0
, a contradiction.

Therefore, we may assume
T_{1}<T_{2}
and we define
D=(T_{1},T_{2})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
and claim that for all
\varphi\in\mathcal{B}
and
(y,\lambda)\in D\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
B_{\lambda}(y)\subseteq D
it holds

(30)

\langle\mathcal{L}\hat{u},\varphi^{\lambda}_{y}\rangle=0.

Indeed, this follows easily from (
29
) by choosing a sequence
(y^{n},\lambda^{n})\in D^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
converging to
(y,\lambda)
and satisfying
B_{\lambda^{n}}(y^{n})\subseteq D^{n}
. Using the compact support of
\varphi
, the local uniform convergence of
\hat{u}^{n}
(which agrees with
u^{n}
on the support of
\varphi^{\lambda^{n}}_{y^{n}}
) to
\hat{u}
, and the pointwise convergence of
\mathcal{L}^{*}\varphi^{\lambda^{n}}_{y^{n}}
to
\mathcal{L}^{*}\varphi^{\lambda}_{y}
, we find that sending
n\to\infty
to (
29
) yields (
30
). Using (
30
) to take
\lambda\to 0
as in the proof of Theorem
1
, we find that for all
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(D)
it holds
\langle\mathcal{L}\hat{u},\varphi\rangle=0
, and therefore by ellipticity of
\mathcal{L}
we find that
\hat{u}
is a smooth solution to
\mathcal{L}\hat{u}=0
in
D
.

We now contradict Liouville’s theorem.
On one hand, by construction (and possibly passing to a further subsequence), there exists
z\in\overline{D}
with
d(z,0)=1
such that
|\hat{u}(z)|\geq\frac{1}{2}
, and in particular
\hat{u}
cannot be identically zero in
D
(if
z\in\partial D
this would contradict continuity of
\hat{u}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
).
On the other hand, we have that
\hat{u}
is a classical solution to
\mathcal{L}\hat{u}=0
in
D
, which by the third point in (
27
) and the local uniform convergence satisfies the growth constraint
|\hat{u}(y)|\leq d(y,0)^{\eta}
for
\eta\in(1,2)
.
We claim that for
T_{1}<T_{2}
this violates Liouville’s theorem.
Indeed, if
T_{1}>-\infty
, then again by (
28
) it holds that
\hat{u}(T_{1},\cdot)=0
and therefore
\hat{u}(x_{0},\cdot)
is identically zero for all
x_{0}\in(T_{1},T_{2})
by standard uniqueness results for the heat equation for solutions with polynomial growth, see Theorem 7 in Chapter 2.3 of
[
9
]
.
If instead
T_{1}=-\infty
, then
\hat{u}
is an ancient solution to the heat equation, so Liouville’s theorem for ancient solutions (see
[
25
]
, or
[
20
, Theorem 1.2(b)]
for a more modern exposition) implies
\hat{u}
is a restriction of a polynomial to
D
, which gives a contradiction.
∎

2.4.
The Discrete Laplacian on
\epsilon\mathbb{Z}^{d}

In this section, we consider the necessary modifications of Simon’s method to analyze the discrete setting
\epsilon\mathbb{Z}^{d}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For a detailed study of discrete approximations in singular SPDE, we refer the reader to the works
[
16
]
,
[
7
]
, and
[
22
]
. We choose
\mathfrak{s}=(1,\cdots,1)
and take the principal operator to be the discrete Laplacian,
defined for
u:\epsilon\mathbb{Z}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
by

(31)

y\in\epsilon\mathbb{Z}^{d}\mapsto\Delta_{\epsilon}u(y):=\epsilon^{-2}\sum_{z\in\epsilon\mathbb{Z}^{d},d(y,z)=\epsilon}(u(z)-u(y)).

For
0<\alpha<\eta
, the
G^{\eta}(\epsilon\mathbb{Z}^{d})
and
G^{\eta,\alpha}(\epsilon\mathbb{Z}^{d})
semi-norms have already been defined in (
6
) and (
8
).
However, since
\epsilon\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is not an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have not yet defined the
G^{\gamma}(\epsilon\mathbb{Z}^{d})
semi-norm for
\gamma<0
.
We define this in analogy with the discrete negative Hölder norm used in
[
16
]
. Namely, we define the bilinear form
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\epsilon}
via

\langle f,g\rangle_{\epsilon}:=\epsilon^{d}\sum_{x\in\epsilon\mathbb{Z}^{d}}f(x)g(x),

and for a germ
V
over
\epsilon\mathbb{Z}^{d}
we set

(32)

[V]_{G^{\gamma}(\epsilon\mathbb{Z}^{d})}:=\sup_{\varphi\in\mathcal{B}}\sup_{x\in\epsilon\mathbb{Z}^{d},\lambda\geq\epsilon}\lambda^{-\gamma}\big|\langle V_{x},\varphi_{x}^{\lambda}\rangle_{\epsilon}\big|.

In comparison to the continuous setting, we require a further centering assumption on the germs. We say that a germ
U
on
\epsilon\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is
centered
(to order one) provided that
U_{x}(x)=0
and
D_{j}U_{x}(x)=0
for all
x\in\epsilon\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
j\in[d]
, where
D_{j}
is the forward difference operator on
\epsilon\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, i.e.
D_{j}U_{x}(x)=\epsilon^{-1}\big(U_{x}(x+\epsilon\mathbf{e}_{j})-U_{x}(x)\big)
.
6
6

6

In the continuum setting, the centering condition is automatically implied whenever the
G^{\eta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
norm is finite for
\eta\in(1,2)
, where
D_{j}
is replaced by the continuum directional derivative
\partial_{j}
.
The Schauder estimate for germs is now stated as follows.

Theorem 3
.

Let
0<\alpha<1<\eta<2
. There exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
\epsilon>0
and centered germs
U
on
\epsilon\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with
\|U\|_{G^{\eta}(\epsilon\mathbb{Z}^{d})}<\infty
it holds

(33)

\|U\|_{G^{\eta}(\epsilon\mathbb{Z}^{d})}\leq C\big([\Delta_{\epsilon}U]_{G^{\eta-2}(\epsilon\mathbb{Z}^{d})}+[U]_{G^{\eta,\alpha}(\epsilon\mathbb{Z}^{d})}\big).

As with the proof of Theorem
4
, the scaling of the above semi-norms plays an important role in establishing Theorem
3
and we need the following discrete analogue of (
14
), which can be proved based on a change of variables and the identity

(\Delta_{\epsilon R^{-1}}S_{w}^{R}U)_{x}=R^{2}(\Delta_{\epsilon}U)_{S^{R}_{w}x}\circ S^{R}_{w}.

Lemma 4
.

Let
R>0
,
w\in\epsilon\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, and
\gamma<0
. Then for all germs
U
over
\epsilon\mathbb{Z}^{d}

(34)

[\Delta_{\epsilon R^{-1}}S^{R}_{w}U]_{G^{\gamma}(\epsilon R^{-1}\mathbb{Z}^{d})}=R^{2+\gamma}[\Delta_{\epsilon}U]_{G^{\gamma}(\epsilon\mathbb{Z}^{d})}.

We now comment briefly on the proof of Theorem
3
. By Lemma
1
and Lemma
4
, we can reduce to the case
\epsilon=1
. The basic strategy is then the same as the proof of Theorem
1
. The difference now is that after zooming in near a point of concentration, we will have a sequence of functions on lattices of variable width. If the width stays bounded away from zero, we will extract (along a subsequence) a limiting discrete harmonic function and contradict the discrete Liouville theorem on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
On the other hand, if the lattice spacing goes to zero, we will use the extension operators from
[
22
]
to extract a limit and contradict Liouville’s theorem in the continuum, as in Theorem
1
.

Proof of Theorem
3
.

By re-scaling and using Lemma
4
we may assume
\epsilon=1
. Indeed, assume the estimate (
33
) is established for arbitrary centered germs on the unit lattice. Given a centered germ
U
over
\epsilon\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, applying (
33
) to the centered germ
S^{\epsilon}_{0}U

over
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
yields the desired bound in light of Lemmas
1
and
4
.

If the statement is false for
\epsilon=1
, there exists a sequence of germs
\tilde{U}^{n}
on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
x^{n},y^{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with
x^{n}\neq y^{n}
such that for
R^{n}:=d(x^{n},y^{n})
, it holds that

(35)

\displaystyle\|\tilde{U}^{n}\|_{G^{\eta}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})}=1,\qquad(R^{n})^{-\eta}|\tilde{U}_{x_{n}}(y_{n})|\geq\frac{1}{2},\qquad[\Delta_{1}\tilde{U}^{n}]_{G^{\eta-2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})}+[\tilde{U}^{n}]_{G^{\eta,\alpha}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})}\leq\frac{1}{n}.

We define
\epsilon^{n}:=(R^{n})^{-1}
and a new sequence of germs
U^{n}:=(R^{n})^{-\eta}S^{R^{n}}_{x^{n}}\tilde{U}
on
\epsilon^{n}\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
By Lemma
1
4
, the identity (
35
) now holds with
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
replaced by
\epsilon^{n}\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, that is

(36)

\displaystyle\|U^{n}\|_{G^{\eta}(\epsilon^{n}\mathbb{Z}^{d})}=1,\qquad|U^{n}_{0}(z^{n})|\geq\frac{1}{2},\qquad[\Delta_{\epsilon^{n}}U^{n}]_{G^{\eta-2}(\epsilon^{n}\mathbb{Z}^{d})}+[U^{n}]_{G^{\eta,\alpha}(\epsilon^{n}\mathbb{Z}^{d})}\leq\frac{1}{n},

where
z^{n}:=(R^{n})^{-1}(y^{n}-x^{n})
. We now define a sequence of functions on
\epsilon^{n}\mathbb{Z}^{d}
by
u^{n}:=U_{0}^{n}
. Note that by (
36
), for all
z\in\epsilon^{n}\mathbb{Z}^{d}
it holds

(37)

|u^{n}(z)|\leq d(0,z)^{\eta}.

To obtain a uniform Hölder bound, we apply Lemma
2
, noting that
\epsilon^{n}\mathbb{Z}^{d}
has the property that for
x,y\in\epsilon^{n}\mathbb{Z}^{d}
it holds
d(x,y)\in\epsilon^{n}\mathbb{Z}
, cf. (
5
) and hence
y+d(x,y)e_{i}\in\epsilon^{n}\mathbb{Z}^{d}
for all
i\in[d]
. Thus, we find that for all
R>0

(38)

[u^{n}]_{C^{\alpha}(\epsilon^{n}\mathbb{Z}^{d}\cap B_{R})}\lesssim R^{\eta-\alpha}.

Before extracting a suitable limit, we now argue that for
\varphi\in\mathcal{B}
,
y\in\epsilon^{n}\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
\lambda\geq\epsilon^{n}
,

(39)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\langle\Delta_{\epsilon^{n}}u^{n},\varphi^{\lambda}_{y}\rangle_{\epsilon^{n}}=0.

In fact, this is a straightforward adaptation of the corresponding argument in the proof of Lemma
3
, which we give for the convenience of the reader.

(40)

\langle\Delta_{\epsilon^{n}}u^{n},\varphi_{y}^{\lambda}\rangle_{\epsilon^{n}}=\langle\Delta_{\epsilon^{n}}U^{n}_{0},\varphi_{y}^{\lambda}\rangle_{\epsilon^{n}}=\langle\Delta_{\epsilon^{n}}U^{n}_{y},\varphi_{y}^{\lambda}\rangle_{\epsilon^{n}}+\langle\Delta_{\epsilon^{n}}(U^{n}_{0}-U^{n}_{y}),\varphi_{y}^{\lambda}\rangle_{\epsilon^{n}}.

By definition (
32
) together with (
36
), it holds
|\langle\Delta_{\epsilon^{n}}U^{n}_{y},\varphi_{y}^{\lambda}\rangle_{\epsilon^{n}}|\leq\frac{1}{n}\lambda^{\eta-2}\to 0
. For the second term,

\displaystyle\big|\langle\Delta_{\epsilon^{n}}(U^{n}_{0}-U^{n}_{y}),\varphi_{y}^{\lambda}\rangle_{\epsilon^{n}}\big|=

\displaystyle\bigg|(\epsilon^{n})^{d}\sum_{z\in\epsilon^{n}\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(U_{0}^{n}(z)-U_{0}^{n}(y)-U^{n}_{y}(z)+U_{y}(y))\Delta_{\epsilon_{n}}\varphi_{y}^{\lambda}(z)\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq[U^{n}]_{G^{\eta,\alpha}(\epsilon^{n}\mathbb{Z}^{d})}(\epsilon^{n})^{d}\sum_{z\in\epsilon^{n}\mathbb{Z}^{d}}d(z,y)^{\alpha}\big(d(0,y)+d(z,y)\big)^{\eta-\alpha}|\Delta_{\epsilon^{n}}\varphi_{y}^{\lambda}(z)|\to 0.

We now proceed to extract a limit and note that since
R^{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\epsilon^{n}
is a bounded sequence admitting a subsequential limit
\epsilon^{\infty}
(not relabelled). There are now two cases to consider:
\epsilon^{\infty}=0
and
\epsilon^{\infty}>0
.

We start with the case
\epsilon^{\infty}=0
and define an extension
\hat{u}^{n}
of

By (

converge locally uniformly (along a subsequence) to a continuous function

. We now argue that

is harmonic, for which (as in the proof of Theorem

) it suffices to show that for any

and

\langle\Delta u,\varphi_{y}^{\lambda}\rangle=0
. To this end, we take a sequence

converging to

and
\lambda^{n}\geq\epsilon^{n}
converging to
\lambda
, then by (

), using that
\hat{u}^{n}
agrees with

on
\epsilon^{n}\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\langle\Delta_{\epsilon^{n}}\hat{u}^{n},\varphi_{y^{n}}^{\lambda^{n}}\rangle_{\epsilon^{n}}=0.

However, by Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem, it holds that
\langle\Delta_{\epsilon^{n}}\hat{u}^{n},\varphi_{y^{n}}^{\lambda^{n}}\rangle_{\epsilon^{n}}
converges to
\langle\Delta u,\varphi_{y}^{\lambda}\rangle
, yielding that
\hat{u}
is harmonic (as in the proof of Theorem
1
). To obtain a contradiction, we only need to argue the growth bound: for all

it holds

|\hat{u}(y)|\leq d(0,y)^{\eta}.

Indeed, this follows from choosing

converging to

, then passing to the limit on both sides of (

to the continuous limit

. Hence,
\hat{u}
must be zero by Liouville’s theorem, but

We now turn to the case

\epsilon^{n}=\epsilon^{\infty}
for sufficiently large

due to

.
We start by extending to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
as above and extract a limit
\hat{u}
as before. In this case, we find that for all

and
\lambda\geq\epsilon^{\infty}
, it holds that
\langle\Delta_{\epsilon^{\infty}}\hat{u},\varphi^{\lambda}_{y}\rangle_{\epsilon^{\infty}}=0
. Since
\varphi\in\mathcal{B}
and
\mathfrak{s}=(1,\cdots,1)
, it is supported in the unit

ball on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with

, so setting

, we find that
\langle\Delta_{\epsilon^{\infty}}\hat{u},\varphi^{\lambda}_{y}\rangle_{\epsilon_{\infty}}=\Delta_{\epsilon^{\infty}}\hat{u}(y)\varphi^{\lambda}(0)=\Delta_{\epsilon^{\infty}}\hat{u}(y)(\epsilon^{\infty})^{-d}
, and hence the restriction of

to

is discretely harmonic. Moreover, it holds
|u(y)|\leq d(0,y)^{\eta}
and by the centering condition on the approximating germ,

vanishes on the vertices of the standard simplex in

, so by the discrete Liouville theorem on
\epsilon^{\infty}\mathbb{Z}^{d}
in Lemma

.
But
|u^{n}(z^{n})|\geq\frac{1}{2}
for all

fulfill

Passing to a subsequential limit

.
∎

3.1.

We now generalize Theorem

Let

As observed in

(ii)

Remark 4

To make a comparison with classical Schauder theory, let

3.2.
Locally Uniform Norms

In this section, we generalize Theorem

a bit further, considering again the elliptic problem in the whole space

The estimate is then shown to be uniform in the size of the neighborhood (and the convolution parameter), modulo an additional supremum norm of the germ which fades away as the size of the ball tends to infinity. {\displaystyle\sup_{\varphi\in\mathcal{B}}\sup_{\lambda<R}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lambda^{-\gamma}\big|\langle U_{x},\varphi_{x}^{\lambda}\rangle\big|}&\quad\text{if}\quad\gamma<0.\end{cases}
