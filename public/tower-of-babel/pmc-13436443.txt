Overexpression of Rubisco Activase Improves Photosynthesis and Plant Growth in Rice Under Fluctuating Light Under Future High CO2 Conditions
Authors: Wataru Yamori, Marika Inagaki, Hiroshi Fukayama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRubisco activase mediates the activation of Rubisco, the key photosynthetic CO2‐fixing enzyme, and plays a crucial role in regulating photosynthesis and plant growth. Rubisco activase restores catalytic competence to Rubisco active sites by promoting the release of inhibitors in an ATP‐dependent manner. The present study analyzed the role of Rubisco activase in the photosynthetic response to fluctuating light under future high CO2 conditions using transgenic rice plants that expressed different amounts of Rubisco activase. Under both low CO2 (200 μmol mol−1) and high CO2 (800 μmol mol−1) concentrations, overexpression of Rubisco activase resulted in a faster rate of Rubisco activation upon irradiation, leading to faster photosynthetic induction and greater photosynthetic response to long‐term fluctuating light conditions. In addition, the transgenic plants overexpressing Rubisco activase showed improved water use efficiency under long‐term fluctuating light conditions compared with wild‐type plants. Finally, under high CO2 conditions, the overexpression of Rubisco activase resulted in improvement in plant growth under fluctuating light conditions, whereas the plant growth under constant light was similar among genotypes. The results of this study showed that Rubisco activase plays a substantial role in the response to the natural dynamic light environment under future high CO2 concentrations.

Introduction

With the rapid growth of the world population, it is essential to increase crop yields to prevent mass starvation (FAO 2017; Qu et al. 2023). Numerous efforts to achieve high crop yields have been made using transgenic approaches and breeding techniques. Among these, improving the photosynthetic capacity of leaves is considered a promising strategy, since photosynthesis is a process that plays a critical role in plant growth and development, which directly translates into plant biomass and yield (Long et al. 2006; Parry et al. 2013). Previous studies with plants grown under high CO2 concentrations and with transgenic plants in which the characteristics of enzymes involved in CO2 fixation were altered have provided evidence that manipulation of the photosynthetic process has significant potential for increasing crop yield (Long et al. 2006; Parry et al. 2011). While considerable effort is now being placed into ways of improving the photosynthetic rate under steady‐state conditions, this overlooks the fact that the natural field environment where plants generally grow is almost never at steady‐state and is always fluctuating (Kromdijk et al. 2016; Papanatsiou et al. 2019; Tanigawa et al. 2024; Yoshiyama et al. 2024). Thus, mechanisms that accelerate photosynthetic responses to light fluctuations represent promising targets for improving crop productivity under field conditions, where plants frequently experience rapid changes in irradiance, particularly fluctuating light (Yamori 2016).

Field‐grown crops are often exposed to complex and fluctuating environments. In particular, the intensity of solar light on the leaves changes dynamically owing to intermittent cloud cover, movement of leaves in the wind, overshadowing canopy cover, and changing solar angles. The change in light intensity from low to high induces a gradual increase in the photosynthetic rate, a phenomenon known as “photosynthetic induction”. This slow response limits photosynthetic efficiency (Pearcy et al. 1994; Mott and Woodrow 2000; Deans et al. 2019). Simulation analyses have shown that the potential loss of daily carbon gain due to slow photosynthetic induction can exceed 20% in soybeans (Glycine max L.) (Tanaka et al. 2019) and wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) (Taylor and Long 2017) under simulated field light conditions. If photosynthetic induction could be accelerated, it has been considered that these losses could be reduced and intrinsic water use efficiency increased (Carmo‐Silva et al. 2015; Lawson and Blatt 2014; McAusland et al. 2016; Vialet‐Chabrand et al. 2017).

Proposed limiting factors for the photosynthetic rate during the photosynthetic induction phase include the following: (1) activation of electron transport; (2) activation of C3 cycle enzymes; and (3) responses of conductance to gas diffusion in leaves (Pearcy and Way 2012; Sakoda et al. 2021). In addition, photoprotection mechanisms induced by electron transport and thermal dissipation (non‐photochemical quenching, NPQ) have been suggested to contribute to the regulation of photosynthesis during the post‐illumination phase (e.g., Kromdijk et al. 2016; Yamori 2016; Yamori and Shikanai 2016). Recent work has shown that faster stomatal opening removes limitations of CO2 supply for photosynthesis under a CO2 concentration of 400 µmol mol−1, resulting in greater photosynthetic rate and plant growth under fluctuating light conditions in Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana; Papanatsiou et al. 2019; Shimadzu et al. 2019; Kimura et al. 2020; Sakoda et al. 2020; Katsuhama et al. 2025) and in rice (Oryza sativa L.; Yamori et al. 2020). Moreover, recent studies have also highlighted that, in addition to stomatal characteristics, Rubisco and its activation by Rubisco activase may represent important potential targets for crop improvement, especially with regard to photosynthesis under fluctuating light conditions (Yamori et al. 2012; Parry et al. 2013; Carmo‐Silva et al. 2015).

The activity of Rubisco activase is regulated by the redox status, the ADP/ATP ratio, and the Mg2+ concentration in chloroplasts (Robinson and Portis 1989; Zhang and Portis 1999; Hazra et al. 2015), thereby adjusting Rubisco activity to the prevailing irradiance level (e.g., during photosynthetic induction; Taylor and Long 2017). Under dynamic light conditions, transgenic tobacco with low contents of Rubisco activase shows a linear response between Rubisco activase content and the speed of photosynthetic induction during the transition from low‐ to high‐intensity light, indicating that Rubisco activase limits the photosynthetic induction (Hammond et al. 1998; Yamori and von Caemmerer 2009). In addition, overexpression of Rubisco activase has been shown to increase the speed of Rubisco activation, resulting in improvement of photosynthetic induction (Yamori et al. 2012). Furthermore, in Arabidopsis, Rubisco activase is present in two isoforms, of which the α‐isoform is regulated by the ATP/ADP ratio and redox state in the chloroplasts, and the β‐isoform is less sensitive to the ATP/ADP ratio and is not redox regulated (Zhang and Portis 1999; Zhang et al. 2002). In transgenic plants containing only the β isoform, photosynthetic induction upon irradiation was faster than in wild‐type plants, as Rubisco activase remains activated in low light (Carmo‐Silva and Salvucci 2013; Kaiser et al. 2016).

As mentioned above, the rate‐limiting factors of photosynthetic induction under the current atmospheric CO2 concentration (e.g., 400 µmol mol−1) have been gradually elucidated. However, there are few examples of studies on the photosynthetic response to fluctuating light in future high CO2 environments, although the photosynthetic response to high CO2 concentrations would be an important consideration given the current rapid rise in global atmospheric CO2 concentration (IPCC 2018). Increased CO2 concentration generally reduces stomatal conductance but stimulates the speed of photosynthetic induction, resulting in enhancement of photosynthesis and growth under fluctuating light conditions (Leakey et al. 2002). In previous work, where the CO2 concentration was controlled either during measurements (Chazdon and Pearcy 1986) or continuously during plant growth (Naumburg and Ellsworth 2000; Leakey et al. 2002; Tomimatsu and Tang 2012; Holišová et al. 2012), three out of the five studies found that high CO2 concentration led to faster photosynthetic induction (Chazdon and Pearcy 1986; Leakey et al. 2002; Tomimatsu and Tang 2012), one study found no differences in induction rates (Naumburg and Ellsworth 2000), while another study reported faster induction for one of two species under high CO2 concentration (Holišová et al. 2012). That is, the effects of high CO2 concentration on photosynthesis under fluctuating light have been examined in a number of studies across several species, although the reported effects on photosynthetic induction are not always consistent (Tomimatsu and Tang 2016; Kaiser et al. 2017). However, the regulatory mechanisms underlying photosynthetic induction under different CO₂ concentrations remain incompletely understood. According to the biochemical model of C3 photosynthesis (Farquhar et al. 1980), steady‐state photosynthesis is limited by ribulose‐1,5‐bisphosphate (RuBP) carboxylation or regeneration, depending on the CO2 concentration. However, how the regulatory mechanisms of photosynthetic induction differs depending on CO2 concentration (Mott and Woodrow 1993; Mott et al. 1997) remains largely unclear.

Here, using transgenic rice expressing increased levels of maize Rubisco activase and antisense plants expressing reduced levels of Rubisco activase, we analyzed photosynthetic induction, photosynthetic response to repeatedly fluctuating light, the time course of Rubisco activation under both low and high CO2 concentrations, and the effects of photosynthesis under fluctuating light conditions on plant growth under future high CO2 concentrations. Our objective was to investigate how Rubisco activase influenced photosynthetic reactions in rice under fluctuating light conditions under ambient CO2 and future high CO2 concentrations and to obtain new insight into strategies for improving photosynthesis in natural light environments under future high CO2 concentrations.

Materials and Methods

Plant Materials and Growth Conditions

Rice (cv Nipponbare) plants and the T3 progeny of several transformants expressing reduced amounts of Rubisco activase and those overexpressing maize Rubisco activase were used (Yamori et al. 2012). In the present study, the β form of maize Rubisco activase, which is not redox‐regulated, was expressed in rice plants (Zhang et al. 2002). Each seedling was planted in a 1.3‐L plastic pot containing Akadama soil with 1.0 g of a slow‐release fertiliser (Temairazu; Co‐Op Chemical Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) in an environmentally controlled growth chamber. For all plants, the chamber was operated with a day/night temperature of 25/22°C, a relative humidity of 65%, a PPFD (photosynthetic photon flux density) of 600 µmol photons m−2 s−1, a 12‐h photoperiod, and a CO2 concentration of 800 µmol mol−1. All plants were grown in the same chamber and periodically repositioned randomly. For the analysis of plant growth, plants grown for more than 40 days were transferred to either constant high‐intensity light or fluctuating low‐/high‐intensity light for another 40 days.

Gas Exchange, Chlorophyll Fluorescence, and P700 Measurements

The gas exchange rate, chlorophyll fluorescence, and the P700 redox state were measured simultaneously in the uppermost, fully expanded new leaves of 60‐ to 80‐day‐old plants with a portable gas exchange system (GFS‐3000; Walz, Effeltrich, Germany) and a pulse‐amplitude modulated fluorometer (Dual‐PAM‐100; Walz) as described previously (Qu et al. 2025). We analyzed all photosynthetic parameters at a cuvette temperature of 25°C and 70% RH. The leaves were first kept in darkness overnight and then treated with a saturating pulse to obtain maximum fluorescence and maximum oxidisable P700. The quantum yields of photosystem I (PSI) (Y(I)) and photosystem II (PSII) (Y(II)), NPQ, and the fraction of reduced PSII centres (1 − qL) were calculated as described previously (Yamori et al. 2011). The electron transport rate (ETR) was calculated as ETR I (or ETR II) = 0.5 × abs I × Y(I) (or Y(II)), where 0.5 is the fraction of absorbed light reaching PSI or PSII, and abs I is absorbed irradiance taken as 0.84 of incident irradiance.

The A–C i curve (CO2 assimilation rate (A) versus intercellular CO2 concentration (C i )) was analyzed at 1500 µmol m–2 s–1 irradiance with a portable gas‐exchange system (LI‐6400XT; Li‐COR, Lincoln, NE, USA). The steady‐state photosynthetic rate was measured at the ambient CO2 concentration of 800 µmol mol−1 and then at 600, 400, 250, 200, 150, 125, and 100 µmol mol−1. Then, the CO2 concentration was restored to 800 µmol mol−1 and increased to 1000 and 1200 µmol mol−1. The photosynthetic rate was recorded at 3 min after each change in CO2 concentration.

A–C i curves were fitted to the biochemical model of C3 photosynthesis (Farquhar et al. 1980), using Rubisco kinetic constants and temperature dependencies from tobacco (Bernacchi et al. 2001) to estimate the maximum rate of RuBP carboxylation V cmax (µmol m−2 s−1) and rate of electron transport J, also referred to as ETR (µmol m−2 s−1). In the main analyses, A– C i curves were fitted assuming infinite mesophyll conductance (gm), and therefore the reported V cmax and J values are based on intercellular CO2 concentration (C i). A under the RuBP‐carboxylation–limiting condition (A c) can be expressed as: (1) Ac=Vcmax(Ci−Γ*)Ci+KC(1+O/KO)−Rday, where K C (µmol mol−1) and K O (mmol mol−1) are the Michaelis constants for CO2 and O2, respectively, O (mmol mol−1) is intercellular O2 concentration, and Γ* (µmol mol−1) is the CO2 compensation point in the absence of daytime respiration, R day (µmol m−2 s−1).

A under the RuBP‐regeneration–limiting condition (A r) can be expressed as: (2) Ar=J(Ci−Γ*)4Ci+8Γ*−Rday.

In addition, we performed supplementary analyses incorporating mesophyll conductance (gm) using the A–C i curve‐fitting procedure described by Ethier and Livingstone (2004). In this approach, photosynthetic parameters are estimated with respect to chloroplastic CO2 concentration (C c ). For this analysis, gm was assumed to be constant among wild‐type plants, Rubisco activase overexpression lines, and antisense lines (8.2 µmol m−2 s−1 Pa−1 at 25°C; Scafaro et al. 2011; Qu et al. 2021).

For short‐term photosynthetic responses to fluctuating light, photosynthetic induction following a sudden increase in light intensity was analyzed using an LI‐6400XT gas‐exchange system combined with chlorophyll fluorescence measurements. Leaves were first acclimated to high light (1500 µmol photons m⁻² s⁻¹) for at least 30 min to reach steady‐state photosynthesis. Light intensity was then reduced to low light (100 µmol photons m⁻² s⁻¹) for 12 min, followed by re‐illumination with high light (1500 µmol photons m⁻² s⁻¹) to induce photosynthetic induction. Gas exchange and chlorophyll fluorescence parameters were recorded at 30‐s intervals. Photosynthetic rate, stomatal conductance, and electron transport rates through PSI and PSII were analyzed during the induction phase. Rubisco activation state was determined at 0, 3, 9, and 15 min after the increase in light intensity. For long‐term photosynthetic responses to fluctuating light, leaves were placed in a temperature‐controlled chamber under a CO2 concentration of 800 µmol mol–1 and a relative humidity of 70% in the Dual‐PAM‐100 and GFS‐3000 measuring systems. Photoinhibition was analyzed immediately after the measurements of photosynthesis under constant high light or fluctuating light. The leaves were exposed to constant high light (1500 µmol photons m–2 s–1) or fluctuating light for 5 h. In the fluctuating light treatment, high light (1500 µmol photons m–2 s–1) and low light (100 µmol photons m–2 s–1) were alternated every 10 min. During these measurements, photosynthetic parameters were recorded at 20 s intervals. The maximum level of the P700 signal (full oxidation of P700) in the dark and the maximum quantum yield of PSII (F v/F m) after being kept in the dark for 30 min were measured before and after the 5‐h light treatment (Yamori et al. 2016).

Net photosynthetic rates and stomatal conductance were also measured under natural fluctuating light conditions (Yamori et al. 2020). Light intensity varied naturally during the measurements, reflecting the dynamic light environment experienced in the field. Gas exchange parameters were recorded at 10‐s intervals using an LI‐6400XT system.

Determination of Rubisco and Rubisco Activase Contents

Leaf tissues from the same leaf used for gas‐exchange measurement were sampled immediately after gas exchange measurements, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C until analysis. The contents of Rubisco and Rubisco activase were determined by formamide extraction of SDS‐PAGE‐separated Coomassie Brilliant Blue R‐250–stained bands, as described by Yamori et al. (2012).

Determination of Rubisco Activation State

For analysis of Rubisco activation state during photosynthetic induction, rice leaves were illuminated at 1500 µmol photons m−2 s−1 for more than 30 min and were subsequently placed at an initial low level of 100 µmol photons m−2 s−1 for 12 min. The leaves were then re‐irradiated with high light (1500 µmol photons m−2 s−1), and leaf discs were sampled after 0, 3, 9, and 15 min of irradiation. The samples were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C. The Rubisco activity in the extract before and after activation with CO2 and MgCl2 at 25°C was measured spectroscopically by monitoring the oxidation of NADH at 340 nm. The activation state was calculated as the ratio of the Rubisco activity before and after activation (Yamori et al. 2006, 2012).

Plant Growth Analysis

Plants were grown in a chamber at a PPFD of 600 µmol m−2 s−1 with a 12‐h photoperiod in a CO2 concentration of 800 µmol mol−1 for 40 days. They were then transferred to either constant or fluctuating light. Under both conditions, plants were exposed to high light at 800 µmol photons m−2 s−1 for 9 h and low light at 100 µmol photons m−2 s−1 for 9 h a day. Under the constant light condition, the plants were exposed to low light for 4.5 h in the morning, and high light for 9 h in the middle of the day, and low light for 4.5 h in the evening. Under the fluctuating light conditions, high light for 10 min and low light for 10 min was alternated for 18 h a day. Above‐ground tissues of the plants were harvested at 80 days after sowing, before the heading stage, dried at 80°C for 72 h, and then weighed.

Statistical Analysis

The significance of variation in all traits among lines was evaluated by the Tukey–Kramer test in the SPSS statistical software package (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

Photosynthetic Components and CO2‐Dependent Response of Steady‐State Photosynthesis

Rubisco activase content differed among wild‐type plants, transgenic plants overexpressing maize Rubisco activase, and antisense Rubisco activase transgenic plants, whereas Rubisco content was similar among these lines (Figure 1A,B; Fig. S1), as has been reported (Yamori et al. 2012).

To examine the effects of Rubisco activase content on steady‐state photosynthesis, we measured the CO2‐dependent response curve of the photosynthetic rate and analyzed the limiting step for steady‐state photosynthetic rate (A) (Figure 1C). All lines showed similar dependence of A on the intercellular CO2 concentration (C i) (A–C i curve), and thus, V cmax and J were similar among wild‐type plants, the overexpression line, and the antisense line (wild‐type: V cmax = 144 ± 10, J = 185 ± 8; antisense: V cmax = 130 ± 12, J = 190 ± 9; overexpression: V cmax = 141 ± 8, J = 183 ± 10; values are means ± SE). This result is supported by the CO2‐dependent response of the chloroplast electron transport rates estimated from chlorophyll fluorescence (ETR II) (Figure 1D). These results indicate that the steady‐state photosynthetic capacities per unit leaf area in these lines were almost identical. Moreover, the limitation analysis showed that the photosynthetic rate under the growth CO2 concentration of 800 µmol mol−1 was limited by RuBP regeneration, whereas at CO₂ concentrations lower than 400 µmol mol⁻¹ it was limited by RuBP carboxylation (Figure 1C,D).

Non‐Steady‐State Photosynthesis after an Increase in Light Intensity

To investigate the role of Rubisco activase in the photosynthetic response to rapid changes in light intensity, changes in photosynthetic parameters were measured under a CO2 concentration of 200 µmol mol−1, where A 200 was limited by RuBP carboxylation and under a CO2 concentration of 800 µmol mol−1, where A 800 at the steady‐state was limited by RuBP regeneration (Figure 1). In leaves of rice plants that were first maintained for a period under high light of 1500 µmol photons m−2 s−1, all the steady‐state photosynthetic parameters of A, stomatal conductance (gs), the photosynthetic electron transport rate through PSII (ETR II), and the photosynthetic electron transport rate through PSI (ETR I) were similar among all genotypes, irrespective of the measurement CO2 concentration. In leaves of rice plants that had been kept under low light of 100 µmol photons m−2 s−1 for 12 min after high light irradiation were then subjected to high light of 1500 µmol photons m−2 s−1, all the photosynthetic parameters (A, ETR II, and ETR I) at 200 µmol mol−1 rapidly increased during the first few minutes, and then increased more slowly to a final steady‐state rate in wild‐type plants (Figure 2A,E,G). A and ETR II approached their steady state levels earlier in the overexpression line than in wild‐type plants, whereas in the antisense line, the speed was much slower than in the wild‐type plants. A and ETR II tended to reach steady‐state levels earlier in the overexpression line than in wild‐type plants. In contrast, the induction of A and ETR II was significantly slower in the antisense line than in both the wild‐type and overexpression lines. This difference was quantified using the induction speed parameter t50 (time required to reach 50% of the maximum value) (Figure 2I,O), as previously reported (Yamori et al. 2012). Although the difference in t50 between the two CO₂ concentrations was relatively small, this may partly reflect the relatively short low‐light period (12 min), which may not have allowed complete deactivation of Rubisco or full stomatal closure prior to the induction measurement. Previous studies have also shown that the effects of elevated CO₂ on photosynthetic induction can vary depending on the light history and environmental conditions (Kaiser et al. 2017). There were no significant differences in the response of ETR I to the rapid change in light intensity (Figure 2M). We also analyzed photosynthetic induction under a high CO2 concentration of 800 µmol mol−1 (Figure 2B,F,H). The speeds at which A and ETR II changed following an increase in light intensity under a CO2 concentration of 800 µmol mol−1 were also much faster in the overexpression line compared to wild‐type plants, whereas in the antisense line, the speed was slower than in wild‐type plants (Figure 2J,P), similar to the pattern observed at 200 µmol mol⁻¹ CO₂. There were no significant differences in the response of ETR I to the rapid change in light intensity (Figure 2N). To examine the effect of gs on photosynthetic induction, we analyzed changes in gs in response to increased irradiance (Figure 2C,D). The changes under both CO2 concentrations were similar in all lines, irrespective of CO2 concentration (Figure 2K,L), indicating that differences in photosynthetic induction could not be attributed to differences in gs.

Changes in Rubisco activation state following an increase in light intensity were also analyzed after exposure of a leaf to high light for various periods (Figure 2Q,R). Rice leaves were first illuminated for over 30 min with high light and were subsequently placed for 12 min at an initial low level of 100 µmol photons m−2 s−1. The leaves were then re‐irradiated with high light, and changes in Rubisco activation state following an increase in light intensity were analyzed. Before high‐light irradiation, Rubisco activation state in overexpressing plants tended to be greater than in the wild‐type plants under a CO2 concentration of 200 µmol mol−1 but not under a CO2 concentration of 800 µmol mol−1. Rubisco activation reached a steady‐state substantially faster in the overexpression line than in wild‐type plants, irrespective of CO2 concentration. After 15 min, Rubisco activation state in wild‐type and overexpression lines showed little further increase, whereas it continued to increase in the antisense line. Thus, both A and Rubisco activation state increased more rapidly in the overexpression line and more slowly in the antisense line than in wild‐type plants.

Photosynthetic Response to Long‐Term Fluctuating Light Conditions

In wild‐type plants, under constant high‐light conditions, all photosynthetic parameters (A, ETR I, ETR II, WUEi) were constant over a 5‐h period under two different CO2 concentrations (Figure 3). On the other hand, under fluctuating light conditions, A, ETR I, and ETR II during both low‐light and high‐light phases gradually decreased over the 5‐h period under the two different CO2 concentrations.

Next, we evaluated the impact of the overexpression and knockdown of Rubisco activase on photosynthesis under fluctuating light. Under constant high‐light conditions, all photosynthetic parameters (A, ETR I, ETR II, WUEi) were constant over the 5‐h period, and were virtually identical to those in wild‐type plants (Figure 3). On the other hand, in the antisense line under fluctuating light conditions, A, ETR I, and ETR II decreased in a stepwise manner at every transition to low light (Figure 3). As a result, photosynthetic capacity (i.e., A, ETR I, ETR II) progressively decreased in the antisense line under fluctuating light conditions as the redox state of the plastoquinone pool increased, because the photosynthetic electron transport system accumulated reducing power that could not be dissipated before the subsequent high‐light phases under both CO2 concentrations. In the overexpression line under fluctuating light conditions, A, ETR I, and ETR II were maintained at higher levels than in wild‐type plants even after 5 h of fluctuating light treatment.

Next, the cumulative photosynthetic rate (Sum A) and the averaged intrinsic water use efficiency (WUEi) for either constant high light or fluctuating light was analyzed under CO2 concentrations of 200 µmol mol−1 and 800 µmol mol−1 (Figure 4). Under constant high‐light conditions, Sum A and WUEi were similar among all genotypes, irrespective of CO2 concentration (Figure 4A,C,I,K). On the other hand, under fluctuating‐light conditions, both Sum A and WUEi were highest in the overexpression line. Under high CO2 concentration, the values were intermediate in wild‐type plants and lowest in the antisense line, whereas under low CO2 concentration the wild‐type and antisense lines showed similar values (Figure 4B,D,J,L). The gs under fluctuating light was not significantly affected in any of the lines (Figure 4E,F,G,H). These results suggest that the difference in photosynthetic response under fluctuating light was due to the physiological function of Rubisco activase. Also, we evaluated WUEi as A divided by gs. WUEi was higher in the overexpression line than in wild‐type plants, whereas it was lower in the antisense line than in wild‐type plants (Figure 4J,L). Thus, the overexpression line contributed to greater photosynthesis as well as WUEi under fluctuating light.

Extent of Photoinhibition and Rubisco Activation Under Fluctuating Light Conditions

We analyzed the effects of overexpression and antisense knockdown of Rubisco activase on photoinhibition before and after the light treatments (Figure 5). The maximum level of the P700 signal (P m, full oxidation of P700) in the dark and the maximum quantum yield of PSII (F v/F m) were measured before and after treatment with constant high light or fluctuating light for a 5‐h period under two different CO2 concentrations, in light treatments similar to those in Figure 3. Before treatment, P m and F v/F m were similar among all lines. In wild‐type plants, constant light significantly reduced F v/F m, whereas fluctuating light slightly reduced P m under both CO2 concentrations, indicating that PSII is susceptible to constant high light, whereas PSI is more easily influenced by fluctuating light than by constant high light (Figure 5), as previously reported (Sejima et al. 2014; Yamori 2016; Yamori et al. 2016). In the overexpression line and the antisense line, constant high light decreased F v/F m and P m to an extent similar to that of wild‐type plants under both CO2 concentrations. On the other hand, after the fluctuating light treatment, the P m value was maintained at the highest level in the overexpression line, at the lowest level in the antisense line, and at an intermediate level in wild‐type plants, irrespective of CO2 concentration.

Discussion

Under low CO2 concentrations where the photosynthetic rate is often limited by Rubisco (Figure 1), overexpression of Rubisco activase resulted in a faster rate of Rubisco activation upon irradiation, leading to faster photosynthetic induction (Figure 2) and greater photosynthetic performance under long‐term fluctuating light conditions (Figures 3, 4). This result is consistent with results of previous studies (Hammond et al. 1998; Yamori and von Caemmerer 2009; Yamori et al. 2012; Carmo‐Silva and Salvucci 2013). Similarly, under the high CO2 concentration expected in the future, where steady‐state photosynthesis is generally limited by RuBP regeneration (e.g., Calvin cycle enzymes or photosynthetic electron transport rate; Figure 1), overexpression of Rubisco activase resulted in improvement of photosynthetic rate (Figures 2, 3, 4, 7) and plant growth (Figure 8) under fluctuating light conditions. Moreover, the overexpression of Rubisco activase increased water use efficiency (WUEi) under long‐term fluctuating light conditions (Figures 4, 7). The rate of Rubisco activation during photosynthetic induction was faster in the overexpression line than in wild‐type plants under both low CO2 (200 μmol mol−1) and high CO2 (800 μmol mol−1) conditions. The results of the current study revealed that Rubisco activase plays an important role in the response to fluctuating light under both pre‐industrial (200 μmol mol⁻¹) and future elevated CO₂ concentrations (800 μmol mol⁻¹).

Rubisco Activase Limits Photosynthetic Induction Under Both Low and Future Elevated CO2 Concentration

The present study showed that photosynthetic induction under low CO₂ concentrations (200 µmol mol⁻¹) was accelerated by overexpression of Rubisco activase (Figure 2). This is consistent with several studies showing a significant role of Rubisco activase in photosynthetic induction under current CO2 concentration (e.g., ~ 400 µmol mol−1). Firstly, previous studies confirmed a close positive correlation between Rubisco activase content and the speed of photosynthetic induction under current CO2 concentration in Arabidopsis (Mott et al. 1997), tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum; Hammond et al. 1998; Carmo‐Silva and Salvucci 2013), and rice (Masumoto et al. 2012; Yamori et al. 2012). Secondly, a transgenic rice overexpressing the Rubisco activase β isoform from maize and a transgenic Arabidopsis expressing only the β isoform showed faster rates of photosynthetic induction under current CO2 concentration compared with that in wild‐type plants, because of rapid regulation by Rubisco activase upon irradiation and/or maintenance of a high Rubisco activation state at low light since the β isoform is insensitive to ATP/ADP and thus Rubisco activase remains activated in low light (Yamori et al. 2012; Carmo‐Silva and Salvucci 2013). These findings suggest that manipulation of Rubisco activase abundance and the balance of isoforms would be important for regulating dynamic changes in photosynthetic rates under both low and high CO2 concentration under fluctuating light conditions.

On the other hand, the effects of CO2 concentration on the dynamics of Rubisco activation via the regulation of Rubisco activase under fluctuating irradiance are less clear, although the response of photosynthetic induction to high CO2 concentration is an important consideration given the current rapid rise in global atmospheric CO2 concentration (IPCC 2018). Under high CO2 concentrations, regulation of C3 cycle enzymes likely limits photosynthesis to a greater degree than stomatal conductance (Sassenrath‐Cole and Pearcy 1992; Tomimatsu and Tang 2012; Graham et al. 2017), since CO2 supply rarely limits steady‐state photosynthesis under higher CO2 concentrations (Farquhar et al. 1980). The present study clearly shows that Rubisco activase also plays an important role in photosynthetic induction under high CO2 concentration (Figure 2). The rate of Rubisco activation following an increase in light intensity was slower in wild‐type plants than in Rubisco activase‐overexpressing plants, and was more rapid in wild‐type plants than in antisense plants (Figure 2). The Rubisco activation state at the onset of the high light treatment was slightly different among wild‐type plants, Rubisco activase‐overexpressing, and antisense plants under 200 µmol mol−1 CO2, but not under 800 µmol mol−1 CO2. Thus, we conclude that rapid regulation of the Rubisco activation state by Rubisco activase following an increase in light intensity contributed to improvement of photosynthetic induction under high CO2 concentration (Figure 2), leading to enhancement of photosynthesis (Figures 3, 4, 7) and plant growth (Figure 8) under repeatedly fluctuating light conditions. Our conclusion is supported by a previous model analysis of photosynthetic limitation during photosynthetic induction (Mott et al. 1997). According to the model analysis, photosynthetic induction is basically rate‐limited by either Rubisco activation or the speed of stomatal opening, and the Rubisco activation can be divided into carbamylation (which is a spontaneous reaction) or dissociation of sugar‐phosphate by Rubisco activase. However, recent studies have shown that other processes, such as RuBP regeneration and mesophyll conductance (gm), can also transiently limit photosynthetic induction under fluctuating light conditions (Sakoda et al. 2021; Sakoda et al. 2022). The model further shows that, as CO2 concentration increases, the rate of spontaneous carbamylation increases and the rate of the Rubisco activase reaction becomes a more important rate‐limiting factor. Taken together, Rubisco activase is a potential determinant of photosynthesis and plant growth under fluctuating light under future high CO2 conditions.

It should be noted that the acceleration of photosynthetic induction via increase of Rubisco activase resulted in greater water use efficiency under the repeated fluctuating light conditions (Figures 4, 7). Generally speaking, accelerating stomatal opening would cause a pronounced decrease in water use efficiency (Kimura et al. 2020), as the rate of increase in transpiration through the stomata is higher than the rate of increase in photosynthetic rate due to differences in water and CO2 concentration gradients between the atmosphere and the intracellular air spaces (Lawson and Blatt 2014). On the other hand, recent work has shown that improving stomatal closure kinetics and regulation in response to fluctuating light could substantially improve water use efficiency and plant growth (Papanatsiou et al. 2019; Kimura et al. 2020). The present study showed that overcoming photosynthetic limitations to improve photosynthetic efficiency by improvement of metabolism resulted in increases in water use efficiency (Figures 4, 7). Taken together, the present study indicates that Rubisco and its activation by Rubisco activase are important potential targets for crop improvement, especially with regard to photosynthesis and water use efficiency under fluctuating light conditions across a range of atmospheric CO₂ concentrations, including current and future elevated CO₂ concentrations.

Role of Rubisco Activase in Photosynthetic Regulation at High Temperatures and in Photosynthetic Induction

In the present study, we examined the role of Rubisco activase in photosynthetic regulation under fluctuating light conditions. Our results showed that overexpression of Rubisco activase enhanced the rate of Rubisco activation upon irradiation, leading to faster photosynthetic induction (Figure 2) and improved photosynthetic performance under long‐term fluctuating light conditions (Figures 3, 4, 7). These findings suggest that Rubisco activase plays an important role in regulating photosynthesis under dynamic light environments. In natural crop canopies, light fluctuations occur across a wide range of time scales, from minutes to very short sunflecks lasting only seconds (Durand and Robson 2023). Therefore, further studies are needed to determine how variation in Rubisco activase content influences photosynthetic responses under such complex and rapidly changing light environments.

Although high temperature was not directly examined in the present study, previous studies have shown that Rubisco activase is particularly sensitive to heat stress and that the thermal sensitivity of Rubisco activase can limit photosynthesis and plant growth under elevated temperatures (Kurek et al. 2007; Kumar et al. 2009; Qu et al. 2021, 2023). Improving the thermal stability of Rubisco activase has been shown to enhance photosynthetic performance and plant growth at high temperatures in several species, including Arabidopsis and rice (Wang et al. 2010; Scafaro et al. 2016, 2018; Wijewardene et al. 2020). Therefore, improving Rubisco activase function may contribute not only to faster photosynthetic induction under fluctuating light conditions, as observed in the present study, but also to improved photosynthetic performance under high‐temperature conditions expected under future climate scenarios.

Rubisco activase content can become a limiting factor for Rubisco activation under environmental stress conditions. It is possible that plants growing under fluctuating light and/or high temperature allocate more nitrogen to Rubisco activase in order to maintain efficient Rubisco activation. Rubisco activase is a relatively abundant protein, representing about 5% or more of total soluble leaf protein (He et al. 1997; Piques et al. 2009). In addition, recent studies have shown that a single amino acid substitution can substantially alter the thermal and regulatory properties of Rubisco activase (Degen et al. 2020), and that post‐translational modifications such as acetylation (Hartl et al. 2017) and phosphorylation (Kim et al. 2019) also regulate its activity. These findings suggest that improving the efficiency and stability of Rubisco activase could be an important strategy for enhancing photosynthesis under variable environmental conditions.

Photosynthesis in natural environments is strongly influenced by dynamic light fluctuations. When light intensity increases suddenly, photosynthetic induction occurs because the photosynthetic rate does not immediately reach its maximum but increases gradually over several minutes (Yamori et al. 2012). Although leaves in natural canopies are not always exposed to low light long enough for complete deactivation of photosynthesis, transient limitations in photosynthetic induction can still influence carbon gain under fluctuating light environments. The present study and previous studies demonstrate that improving Rubisco activation through Rubisco activase (Yamori et al. 2012; Carmo‐Silva et al. 2015) or accelerating stomatal opening (Papanatsiou et al. 2019; Kimura et al. 2020; Yamori et al. 2020; Katsuhama et al. 2025) can enhance photosynthesis and plant growth under fluctuating light conditions.

Conversely, when light intensity decreases from high to low levels, transient imbalances in photosynthetic processes can also occur. Under high light, excess absorbed energy is dissipated as heat through non‐photochemical quenching (NPQ). When light intensity suddenly decreases, the relaxation of NPQ takes several minutes, during which potential photosynthesis can be temporarily reduced. Recent work has shown that improving NPQ kinetics can accelerate recovery of photosynthesis after high‐to‐low light transitions and thereby enhance plant growth under natural fluctuating light conditions (Kromdijk et al. 2016). Taken together, improving Rubisco activation together with other regulatory processes such as NPQ relaxation may represent a promising strategy to enhance photosynthetic performance and plant productivity under future environmental conditions characterised by fluctuating irradiance and elevated CO₂ concentrations.
