A Formal Proof of Complexity Bounds on Diophantine Equations
Jonas Bayer, Marco David

Abstract
We present a universal construction of Diophantine equations with bounded complexity in Isabelle/HOL. This is a formalization of our own work in number theory [ 1 ] . Hilbert’s Tenth Problem was answered negatively by Yuri Matiyasevich, who showed that there is no general algorithm to decide whether an arbitrary Diophantine equation has a solution. However, the problem remains open when generalized to the field of rational numbers, or contrarily, when restricted to Diophantine equations with bounded complexity, characterized by the number of variables ν \nu and the degree δ \delta . If every Diophantine set can be represented within the bounds ( ν , δ ) (\nu,\delta) , we say that this pair is universal , and it follows that the corresponding class of equations is undecidable. In a separate mathematics article, we have determined the first non-trivial universal pair for the case of integer unknowns. In this paper, we contribute a formal verification of the main construction required to establish said universal pair. In doing so, we markedly extend the Isabelle AFP entry on multivariate polynomials [ 17 ] , formalize parts of a number theory textbook [ 15 ] , and develop classical theory on Diophantine equations [ 14 ] in Isabelle. Additionally, our work includes metaprogramming infrastructure designed to efficiently handle complex definitions of multivariate polynomials. Our mathematical draft has been formalized while the mathematical research was ongoing, and benefitted largely from the help of the theorem prover. We reflect how the close collaboration between mathematician and computer is an uncommon but promising modus operandi.

1
Introduction

1.1
Hilbert’s Tenth Problem

The tenth problem on David Hilbert’s 1900 list of twenty-three mathematical problems asks for an algorithm to decide the solvability of any arbitrary Diophantine equation
[
7
]
. Like many problems on his list, Hilbert’s Tenth Problem has provided inspiration for much mathematical research in the last century, and still does so until today. While the original problem was solved over fifty years ago
[
10
]
, this paper presents the formal verification of results related to a new quantitative theorem, stronger than the original solution.

A
Diophantine equation
is a multivariate polynomial equation over the integers. There are countably infinitely many such equations, and they form a very general language: many difficult problems across number theory and related fields can be equivalently expressed as a single Diophantine equation
[
13
]
. One often considers parametric equations of the type
P(a,z_{1},\ldots,z_{\nu})=0
where
a
is called the parameter and
z_{1},\ldots,z_{\nu}
are called unknowns. A
Diophantine set

A\subset{\mathbb{N}}
is then defined to be any set of parameters such that
a\in A
if and only if there are unknowns
z_{1},\ldots,z_{\nu}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
with
P(a,z_{1},\ldots,z_{\nu})=0
.

The Davis-Putnam-Robinson-Matiyasevich theorem (1970)
[
12
, Chapter 1]
states that every recursively enumerable set is Diophantine; this is a deep relation between number theory and computability theory. An enumeration argument shows that, conversely, every Diophantine set is recursively enumerable. The resulting equivalence implies that there are Diophantine sets which are not decidable, and hence proves that Hilbert’s Tenth Problem is undecidable.

1.2
Universal Diophantine Equations and Pairs

Because the set of all recursively enumerable (r.e.) sets is itself recursively enumerable, there must exist a corresponding Diophantine equation for this set, representing all r.e. sets, i.e. all Diophantine sets at once. An equation with this property is known as a
universal Diophantine equation
, and an explicit example can be found in an article by Jones
[
8
]
.

It is now natural to ask about the minimal complexity required for such universal equations. A popular measure of complexity is the number of unknowns
\nu
required and the total degree
\delta
of the polynomial. If all Diophantine sets can be represented by a polynomial in at most
\nu
unknowns and in degree at most
\delta
, we call
(\nu,\delta)
a
universal pair
. There is a trade-off between the two measures: one may reduce the degree by introducing auxiliary unknowns, and vice versa eliminate unknowns at the cost of increasing the degree.

There is a subtle but interesting distinction between allowing the unknowns
z_{1},\ldots,z_{\nu}
of
P
to take values in the natural numbers, including zero, versus in the integers. Universal pairs can be defined for both cases, and we shall write
(\nu,\delta)_{\mathbb{N}}
and
(\nu,\delta)_{\mathbb{Z}}
to make the distinction explicit. Previous work has primarily focused on universal pairs over the natural numbers.
In this case, it was Matiyasevich
[
11
]
who first announced the smallest currently known number of unknowns
\nu=9
. Jones
[
8
]
later published a complete proof that
(9,1.64\cdot 10^{45})_{\mathbb{N}}
is universal, together with the lowest currently known degree
\delta=4
. More precisely, the pair
(58,4)_{\mathbb{N}}
is universal, too.

Our work contributes the first derivation of a non-trivial universal pair over the integers. Note that any universal pair over
{\mathbb{N}}
induces a pair over
{\mathbb{Z}}
. Every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
can be written as
x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}+w^{2}
for integers
x,y,z,w\in{\mathbb{Z}}
using Lagrange’s four square theorem. Thus, a Diophantine equation with unknowns in
{\mathbb{N}}
can be transformed into an equation with unknowns in
{\mathbb{Z}}
by introducing additional unknowns. However, this conversion gives bounds that are far from optimal.

Using the four-squares substitution for
\nu=9
over the natural numbers would thus require 36 integer unknowns. A more efficient substitution is possible using the more sophisticated three squares theorem (cf.
\cref
sec:three-squares); this would yield universality for 27 integer unknowns.

1.3
First Nontrivial Bounds over
{\mathbb{Z}}

In this article, we formalize the main result leading to the following improvement to only eleven unknowns:

Theorem 1
.

Let
(\nu,\delta)_{\mathbb{N}}
be universal. Then

\big(11,\eta(\nu,\delta)\big)_{\mathbb{Z}}

is universal where
\eta(\nu,\delta)=226\,176\;\delta\,(2\delta+1)^{\nu+2}+96\;\delta\,(2\delta+1)^{\nu+1}+226\,176\;\delta+14\,656
.

In particular,

(11,1.16258\cdot 10^{63})_{\mathbb{Z}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad(11,9.19593\cdot 10^{53})_{\mathbb{Z}}

are universal over
{\mathbb{Z}}
.

The technique that is used to reduce the number of unknowns to eleven was developed by Sun
[
19
]
. However, that work does not consider the degree of the constructed polynomials.

Observe that, in principle, the second pair stated above is better than the first. We state both because the second pair depends on Jones’ universal pair
(32,12)_{\mathbb{N}}
of which there is no published proof in the literature. The first pair depends instead on
(58,4)_{\mathbb{N}}
.

\cref

thm:11-universal-pair implies the following stronger response to Hilbert’s Tenth Problem.

Corollary 2
.

Consider the class of Diophantine equations with at most 11 unknowns and at most degree
1.63\cdot 10^{63}
. Hilbert’s Tenth Problem is unsolvable for this class: There is no algorithm that can determine for all equations in this class whether they have a solution or not.

Theorem
1
crucially rests on the following result. Its proof contains an explicit construction of the polynomial
\tilde{Q}
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
P\in{\mathbb{Z}}[a,z_{1},\ldots,z_{\nu}]
. Then there is an explicit polynomial
\tilde{Q}\in{\mathbb{Z}}[a,z_{1},\ldots,z_{11}]
such that, for all
a\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

\exists z_{1},\ldots,z_{\nu}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}.\;P(a,z_{1},\ldots,z_{\nu})=0\iff\exists\tilde{z}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{z}_{11}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{11}.\;\tilde{Q}(a,\tilde{z}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{z}_{11})=0.

This theorem has been formalized in Isabelle/HOL and is the focus of this paper. It appears, together with Theorem
1
and Corollary
2
, in our recent manuscript
[
1
]
which has been written in parallel with the development of its formal proof presented here.

2
Related Work

Hilbert’s Tenth Problem and related statements have been formalized in a variety of different systems. Note that there are many different but equivalent options to define recursively enumerable sets, either algebraically or more by using some Turing-complete model of computation. In the following, we summarize formalizations of results related to Hilbert’s Tenth Problem, highlighting the different choices authors have made.

In Lean, Carneiro
[
5
]
has formalized that exponentiation is Diophantine, which has historically been the last step to be completed in the proof of the DPRM theorem. In Coq, Larchey-Wendling and Forster
[
9
]
have formalized the complete DPRM theorem, elegantly using Conway’s FRACTRAN language as a Turing-complete model of computation. They moreover formalize a reduction of their library of unsolvable problems, including two variants of the Halting Problem, to Hilbert’s Tenth Problem. In Mizar, Pak
[
16
]
has formalized the DPRM theorem, defining recursively enumerable sets by Davis’ normal form. In Isabelle, Bayer et al.
[
3
]
have formalized the DPRM theorem using register machines as a model of computation.

Mathematically, we rely on techniques originally developed for working with Diophantine equations with natural number unknowns; these have been developed by Matiyasevich, Robinson
[
14
]
and Jones
[
8
]
. Over the integers, Sun
[
19
]

first announced many results. In particular, he proved that any Diophantine equation can be reduced to an equivalent one with only eleven integer unknowns.

Unfortunately, the published literature often lacked sufficient detail to formalize, and sometimes even to understand, the existing proofs. As such, our detailed mathematical manuscript now reproduces many of the results and proofs cited above. The improved clarity in exposition is a notable product of this formalization.

3.1
Multivariate Polynomials in Isabelle

Throughout our work, we rely on the formalization of executable multivariate polynomials by Sternagel et al.
[
17
]

\displaystyle\alpha\;\texttt{mpoly}:=({\mathbb{N}}\Rightarrow_{0}{\mathbb{N}})\Rightarrow_{0}\alpha

for any type

\alpha

\alpha
to each monomial. Of course, this type inherits much algebraic structure from the underlying coefficient type: if
\alpha
is a ring or an integral domain, then so is
\alpha

mpoly

A key first addition to the existing library is an explicit expansion, for arbitrary

P(z_{0},\ldots,z_{\nu})=\sum_{i_{0},\ldots,i_{\nu}\,\in\,{\mathbb{N}}}c_{i_{0},\ldots,i_{\nu}}\prod_{s=0}^{\nu}z_{s}^{i_{s}}.

(2)

This expression is a polynomial as long as only finitely many coefficients

are non-zero. Below, we abbreviate the indices with a single multi-index

With
\delta:=\deg P
and the constraint

, we can reformulate the same expression as a finite sum, which is much better behaved for formal proofs in Isabelle.

P(z_{0},\ldots,z_{\nu})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\nu+1}\\
\|\mathbf{i}\|\leq\delta\end{subarray}}c_{i_{0},\ldots,i_{\nu}}\prod_{s=0}^{\nu}z_{s}^{i_{s}},

(3)

Custom Metaprogramming Infrastructure

Recall that
\cref
thm:11-unknowns asserts that any Diophantine equation can be expressed as a polynomial
\tilde{Q}
with only eleven integer unknowns. Consequently, the natural choice for the type of
\tilde{Q}
in Isabelle is
int mpoly
. Since
\tilde{Q}
is constructed by substituting various previously defined polynomials into each other, one might expect that these intermediate polynomials also have the type
int mpoly
.

However, this approach presents notable difficulties when formalizing the
\cref

a,f,g
, one must work with
Var 0
,
Var 1
,
Var 2
and frequently refer to the insertion map explicitly. More critically, substituting polynomials into one another requires care to avoid collisions of variable indices. These challenges cannot be fully mitigated through new notation alone.

To address these issues, this project developed a custom metaprogramming framework that effectively bridges Isabelle terms and the type
int mpoly
. This allows our formalization to benefit from the best of both worlds, significantly simplifying our proofs.

Using this framework, a polynomial
P
is initially formalized as a standard Isabelle term of type
\lstinline
P :: int =¿ int =¿ … =¿ int. The command

then generates a new term

of type
int mpoly
and automatically proves a lemma
P_correct
. This lemma guarantees that evaluating
P_poly
yields the same result as evaluating the original function
P
. The extraction succeeds if
P
is a valid polynomial: constructed from integer constants and variables, addition, multiplication, and any polynomial term
C
for which a corresponding correctness lemma
C_correct
exists.

3.2
The Three Squares Theorem

The three squares theorem states that for every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
there exist integers
x,y,z\in{\mathbb{Z}}
such that
n=x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}+z
. It is a consequence of the classical number theory developed in Nathanson’s textbook
[
15
, Lemma 1.9]
. Danilkin and Chevalier formalized the three squares theorem as well as its number theory prerequisites in Isabelle
[
6
]
. Using this result will constitute the last step in our proof. It serves to ultimately replace the single remaining non-negative integer
n\geq 0
in Lemma
5
below by three true integer unknowns.

3.3
Relation Combining

The idea of relation-combining is the following: imagine we want to check the two conditions
x>0,y\neq 0
with Diophantine polynomials in integer variables. A naive method would use the three-squares formula for each condition, i.e. the conditions are true if

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2}+c+1

\displaystyle y^{2}

\displaystyle=d^{2}+e^{2}+f^{2}+f+1

for some
a,b,c,d,e,f\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

However, there is a more efficient method because the two conditions together are equivalent to the single condition

xy^{2}=a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2}+c+1\;,

using only
a,b,c\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, cutting the number of integer variables required in half.

This technique has been mastered by Matiyasevich and Robinson with the following result. It combines a divisibility condition, an inequality, and checking whether integers are perfect squares into the existence of only one (!) non-negative integer.

Lemma 5

(Matiyasevich-Robinson
[

]

For every

with the property that for all integers

such that

, the following are equivalent:

1.

and

are squares,

\exists n\geq 0:\,M_{q}(A_{1},\dots,A_{q},S,T,R,n)=0

Specifically,

\begin{split}&M_{q}(A_{1},\dots,A_{q},S,T,R,n)\\
&=\prod_{\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{q}\in\{\pm 1\}^{q}}\left(S^{2}n+T^{2}-S^{2}(2R-1)\left(T^{2}+X_{q}^{q}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}\varepsilon_{j}\sqrt{A_{j}}X_{q}^{j-1}\right)\right)\\
\end{split}

The short-hand notation in equation (

Structure of the Proof

\cref

thm:11-universal-pair is based on three technical results that capture the core ideas behind universal Diophantine constructions: Coding, Lucas Sequences, and relation combining techniques. The dependencies between these results are illustrated in
\cref

The central challenge in obtaining a universal pair is reducing a general Diophantine equation — potentially involving many unknowns — to an equivalent equation with a fixed number of unknowns. This reduction is carried out in the aforementioned steps. In the process, the original equation is first transformed into intermediate expressions, which are then shown to be equivalent to a fixed Diophantine polynomial.

Crucially, all operations appearing in the intermediate expressions must be Diophantine, that is, expressible using polynomials. Many fundamental mathematical relations admit such representations
[
12
]
, including divisibility, congruence modulo an integer, and inequalities. Relations involving exponentiation, binomial coefficients and factorials also admit Diophantine representations. However, when deriving universal pairs, it is not enough to establish mere existence; the representation must also be efficient, meaning that it should involve a minimal number of unknowns and remain of low degree.

Coding

Coding is the key idea that translates a given Diophantine polynomial
P\in{\mathbb{Z}}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{\nu}]
with a large number of unknowns into a Diophantine relation of bounded complexity, i.e. only requiring a fixed number of unknowns.

Definition 6

(Code)
.

Fix a base
\mathscr{B}=2^{k}
for some integer
k>1
. Let

be given and
z_{i}<\mathscr{B}

with respect to a tuple

If the

are the given unknowns of the polynomial

consolidates them in a single large number. It is possible to define a number

such that the product

in a specific location of its base
\mathscr{B}
expansion.
1
1

1

More precisely, such

One can therefore translate the statement

[

To formalize codes and work with natural numbers in base

we make use of the AFP entry on digit expansions

The Coding Theorem introduces an exponential relation — namely that the base

Definition 8

Eleven Unknowns in
{\mathbb{Z}}

In the previous sections, we have detailed multiple translation theorems.
\Cref

Conclusion

We have formalized a universal Diophantine construction that implies a stronger version of Hilbert’s Tenth Problem. This construction leads to the novel Theorem
1
and, moreover, it has been developed largely in parallel to its Isabelle formalization. In the course of the project, we also built a substantial library of advanced facts about multivariate polynomials and formalized results from a textbook on number theory. The total development consists of 20,000 lines of code.

Experiences from an In-situ Formalization

The formalization was executed by a student workgroup at a time when only a very early draft of the underlying mathematics was available. Hence we say that this draft manuscript was formalized
in-situ
, in its natural environment—a mathematics department—, and before a version of publication quality had been prepared.

Interacting with a proof assistant significantly shaped the development of the mathematical text: we refined arguments, removed or weakened redundant assumptions, and corrected various minor bugs. For example, we had overlooked a dependency on a lemma in the literature
[
18
, Lemma 10]
which only became apparent in the formalization process. We found that for certain technical parts of the proof, Isabelle functioned as a true proof
assistant
: supporting us authors by keeping track of assumptions and bounds so that we could focus on the more conceptual elements of the proof.

Outlook

Future research could extend this work by formalizing universal pairs over
{\mathbb{N}}
. We believe that the infrastructure we have built in this project would provide a strong starting point for such work, eliminating the need to formalize further prerequisites.

Since this project bridges the frontier between mathematical research and formalization in a proof assistant, further progress will naturally also involve new mathematical discoveries. Hilbert’s Tenth Problem has been proven to be solvable for
\delta\leq 2
or alternatively
\nu=1

[
8
]
, but remains completely open for
\delta=3
and largely open for the whole range
2\leq\nu\leq 10
.
