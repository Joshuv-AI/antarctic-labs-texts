Cadmium-driven specific endophytic bacteria and their role in rice grains yield and cadmium accumulation
Authors: Yibin Lai, Weijun Gong, Xiong Zeng, Salma Saeed Khan, James F White, Dezhi Yang, Rongbo Li, Haiyan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsThe endophytic communities of plants are significantly influenced by heavy metal stress. Conversely, endophytes contribute to host plants’ heavy metal tolerance and accumulation. This study evaluates the impact of cadmium (Cd) on rice, and investigates the role of endophytes in Cd accumulation in rice.MethodsThe endophytic bacterial community of rice under Cd stress was investigated using both culture-dependent and culture-independent methods. In addition, the roles of endophytic bacteria in rice yield and Cd accumulation in rice grains were examined.ResultsThe composition of rice endophytic bacterial communities was shaped by both tissue type and soil Cd concentration. Elevated Cd concentration reduced the endophyte alpha diversity, altered the dominant genera, and promoted a less complex co-occurrence network. Particularly, specific bacterial endophytes (e.g., Methylocystis, Bradyrhizobium and Brevundimonas) were significantly correlated with grain Cd concentration, suggesting their potential role in regulating Cd accumulation in rice grain. A core microbiota, including Bradyrhizobium, Brevundimonas, Acinetobacter, Agrobacterium, etc. consistently presented across all Cd levels, suggesting their essential, stress-independent functions. Some of them exhibited multiple plant growth-promoting traits and Cd tolerance. Pot experiments showed the strain GCRD9 (Agrobacterium sp.) could promote rice growth and increase thousand-grain weight, while reducing Cd accumulation in grains by 55.6%.ConclusionsThese findings provide new insights into the soil-crop-endophyte interactions and highlight the potential of specific bacterial strains for developing endophyte-assisted crop strategies to ensure safe crop production under heavy metal stress.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12866-026-05440-7.Keywords: Cadmium contamination, Core microbiota, Endophytic bacteria, Food security, Rice grains cadmium concentration

Introduction

The increased human activities, such as mining, smelting and excessive application of fertilizers, have resulted in widespread contamination with heavy metals (HMs) in farmland, exacerbating public safety and health problems [1, 2]. Given its high toxicity and mobility, cadmium (Cd) can inhibit plant development and accumulate in plant and eventually threaten human health by the food chain [3, 4].

Previous studies have demonstrated that endophytes confer adaptive benefits to host plants under different stress conditions, including HM, heat, salinity and others [5–7]. Under HM stress, endophytic bacteria could improve host plants’ growth and reduce their HM accumulation by multiple mechanisms, such as enhancing minerals solubilization, regulating phytohormone synthesis and HM-responsive gene expression in plants, improved antioxidant enzyme activity in plants, etc [8, 9]. For example, Ran et al. [10] reported that endophytic copper (Cu)-resistant Bacillus sp. D2 from hyperaccumulator Commelina communis was able to produce 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic acid (ACC) deaminase and indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), and alleviate Cu toxicity and inhibit its upward transport through regulating multiple responses in rice, including antioxidant responses, phytohormone levels, and the expression of genes involved in Cu transporters and metallothionein. Similarly, Li et al. [11] found that inoculation of endophytic bacteria enhanced Cu/Cd tolerance and accumulation in castor (Ricinus communis L.) by improving the rhizosphere microenvironment, increasing soil Cu/Cd bioavailability, and regulating the synthesis of metabolites in castor root.

Rice is a staple food for nearly half of the world’s population. However, Cd is almost ubiquitously present in rice-growing regions of the world, and rice tends to accumulate Cd more than other grain crops [12–14]. Mu et al. [15] investigated the Cd concentration of 113 pairs of soils and rice from China, and found that the Cd concentration of 33.6% and 7.96% of soils and polished rice exceeded the national maximum allowable Cd concentration (soil: 0.3 mg/kg (pH ≤ 5.5), 0.4 mg/kg (5.5 < pH ≤ 6.5), 0.6 mg/kg (6.5 < pH ≤ 7.5), 0.8 mg/kg (pH > 7.5); polished rice: 0.2 mg/kg). Due to the variable soil properties, environmental conditions and others, the efficacy of traditional mitigation strategies (water management, foliar barrier agents, soil amendments, etc.) is often limited [13, 16, 17]. Therefore, it’s important to develop more effective and sustainable strategies for safe rice production in Cd-contaminated soils. Using endophytes to reduce Cd uptake in rice grains is a green, safe and efficient method. However, there are few studies revealing how Cd impacts rice endophytic bacteria, and the role of the latter in yield and grain Cd concentration.

This study specifically proposed the following hypotheses: rice endophytic bacterial communities were driven by soil Cd concentration, and there were some specific endophytic bacteria which can regulate rice grains Cd accumulation, and they can be explored in rice safety production. Therefore, in the present study, the endophytic bacterial communities of rice in lower (LC), moderate (MC) and higher (HC) Cd-contaminated sites were investigated through culturable and unculturable methods. Moreover, the Cd tolerance and plant growth-promoting (PGP) traits of culturable endophytic bacteria were assessed, and their role in growth, productivity and Cd accumulation of rice under Cd stress was explored.

Materials and methods

The soils and rice sampling and their Cd concentration

In rice tillering, filling and maturity stages (July to October 2023), samples were collected from private paddy fields with the permission of the field owners, respectively. Site LC was located in Zhehai Town, Huize County, Yunnan Province (103°34′39″ E, 26°30′50″ N), site MC was located in Shuikoushan Town, Changning County, Hunan Province (112°37′24″ E, 26°33′29″ N), and site HC was located in Wanshan Town, Wanshan District, Guizhou Province (109°11′49″ E, 27°30′49″ N). The sampling and treatment of rhizospheric soils and rice samples, as well as the basic physicochemical properties in soils have been described in our previous study [18]. All three sites were close to mining and smelting areas with a long history, and soils in sites LC, MC and HC contained 2.00 mg/kg, 2.93 mg/kg and 3.29 mg/kg total Cd, respectively. In addition, Cd concentration in site MC grains (seeds) exceeded the maximum allowable concentration (MAC) in rice grains of China (GB 2762 − 2022), and significantly higher than that of sites LC and HC [18].

DNA extraction, 16 S rRNA sequencing and analysis of rice endophytic bacteria

The total DNA from surface-sterilized rice tissue samples extracted and quantified according to our previous study [18]. The V5-V6 hypervariable region of bacterial endophytes16S rRNA genes were amplified with the primer pair 799 F/1107R [19]. The PCR mixture and reaction conditions, and the amplicon library preparation were conducted as the descriptions of Radouane et al. [20]. The libraries were subsequently sequenced on the Illumina Nextseq 2000 platform by Shanghai Majorbio Bio-pharm Technology Company (Shanghai, China).

The raw reads were merged, filtered and dereplicated by FLASH (v1.2.11), Vsearch (v2.3.4) and DADA2, respectively [21]. Based on 97% similarity, the effective sequences were clustered to operational taxonomic units (OTUs) using the UPARSE (v11). The feature abundance was normalized using relative abundance of each sample by SILVA (release 138) classifier. According to the correlation coefficient (> 0.6) and p value (< 0.05) in Spearman’s correlation analysis, the co-occurrence networks in rice were reconstructed using the fruchterman-reingold algorithm in Gephi (v0.10.1) at the genus level [22]. Subsequently, within-module connectivity (Zi) and among-module connectivity (Pi) were used to identify keystone genera within the co-occurrence networks of rice endophytic bacteria. As Zhang et al. [23] description, node topologies were organized into module hubs, network hubs, connectors, and peripherals, while the first three were considered as key taxa.

Isolation of culturable endophytic bacteria

To isolate endophytic bacteria, 2 g different surface-sterilized rice tissues (roots, stems, leaves and grains) from different growth stages collected in July to October 2023 were placed in a mortar, thoroughly ground with silica, respectively. Then, 18 mL sterile water was added into the mortar to prepare the plant stock solution, and the plant stock solution was diluted in a gradient to 10− 3. The bacterial endophytes in rice tissues were isolated in Luria-Bertani (LB) plates according to the method of Dejene et al. [24]. According to the conventional method, plates of the most suitable concentration (30–300 single bacterial colonies) were selected, and the colonies were picked and purified. Then, the isolated endophytic bacteria were identified using a combination of morphological and molecular techniques as the descriptions of Wu et al. [21] and Gong et al. [25].

Assessment of the characteristics of culturable endophytic bacteria

Totally, 38 strains were selected for Cd tolerance and PGP trait tests. Tolerance to Cd was estimated visually after incubation of bacterial strains for 3 d at 37 °C on LB plates, supplemented with 0 (control), and 25 mg/L Cd2+ (CdCl2·2.5H2O) respectively [26]. The IAA producing ability was assessed using Salkowski’s reagent [27]. Phosphate (P) solubilization ability was determined by inoculating strains into National Botanical Research Institute’s Phosphate (NBRIP) broth. After culture at 180 rpm for 6 d at 37 °C, the solubilized P in the culture supernatant was estimated using the Mo-blue method [21]. Siderophore production was detected using Chrome Azurol-S (CAS) reagent, and percent siderophore units (SU) were calculated by the formula as the descriptions of Jain et al. [28]. The SU < 10% was the negative strain of siderophore production. Triplicate was made for each test.

The effect of specific endophytic bacteria on rice grain yield and Cd accumulation

To validate the efficacy of specific endophytic bacteria on rice grain yield and Cd accumulation, the soils were collected from farmland in Jiangwei Village, Chenggong District, Kunming City, Yunnan Province, China. The 0, and 5 mg/kg Cd2+ (CdCl2·2.5 H2O) were added in soils, and thorough mixed [29, 30]. Subsequently, 15 kg of soil was transferred to each pot (high 37 cm/diameter 34.5 cm), and then stabilized at room temperature for two weeks.

After primary screening, the isolates Bacillus cereus HE2 (NCBI GenBank: PX986644) and Agrobacterium sp. GCRD9 (NCBI GenBank: PX986643) (Addition file 1, Fig. S1), which exhibited Cd tolerance and multiple PGP traits, were conducted to pot experiments in 2025. They were incubated in LB broth at 180 rpm at 37 °C for 24 h. Then, the bacterial cells were collected through centrifugation and resuspended in sterile water to a certain density (OD₆₀₀ approx. 1.2).

Rice seeds (O. sativa L. cv. Kunjing 6) were surface sterilized according to the method described in our previous study [18]. After sterilization, the seeds were soaked for 48 h either in the endophytic bacterial suspension or in sterile water (control) before being sown onto petri dishes containing moistened sterile filter paper for germination. After 10 days, they were transplanted into Cd-free soil for seedling. Healthy rice seedlings with two true leaves were transplanted into pots containing stabilized soils. Each treatment was performed in triplicate. Plants were cultured under controlled conditions (day/night: 28 °C/22°C and 12/12 h), and irrigated weekly with a 1/2-strength Hoagland’s nutrient solution and re-inoculated monthly with the respective endophytic bacteria strains and sterile water. To harvest, and the plants were carefully removed from the pots and rinsed thoroughly with tap water. Various growth parameters and agronomic indexes were measured, including shoot height, root length, grain yield per plant, thousand-grain weight, grain number per panicle, and effective tiller number [31]. Rhizosphere soils and plant tissues were air-dried. Subsequently, the dry weight of roots and shoots, the Cd content in roots and grains were determined [32], and the bioconcentration factor (BCF) of roots and grains was calculated using the formula as the descriptions of Luo et al. [33].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was carried out using R software (v4.4.3). One-way ANOVA (analysis of variance) with Duncan test was used to assess variations in growth parameters, agronomic indexes and Cd content of different treated rice. Kruskal-Wallis (KW) sum-rank test was applied to evaluate the difference in alpha diversity indexes of endophytic bacteria from rice at different growth stages from different Cd-contaminated sites. The bacterial alpha diversity indexes were presented as quartiles, while other data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Results

The endophytic bacteria community of rice (culture-independent approach)

Diversity of the rice endophytic bacterial community

After quality filters, 2,417,705, 2,388,055 and 2,321,799 valid bacterial reads were retained across 90 rice tissue samples, which were clustered in 9076 OTUs and identified to 42 phyla and 917 genera.

The variations of bacterial alpha diversity indexes across soil Cd concentrations and rice tissue types were separately compared at each growth stage to assess the influences of these factors on rice endophytic bacteria diversity. The results illustrated that tissue type was the primary factor affecting the alpha diversity of rice endophytic bacteria (Fig. 1). Generally, compared with roots, the species diversity (Shannon index) and richness (Chao1 index) of endophytic bacteria in stems and leaves significantly decreased (p < 0.05). Meanwhile, the alpha diversity of rice endophytic bacteria was also influenced by the soil Cd concentration. For example, at tillering stage, higher Cd concentration in soil significantly decreased the species diversity and richness of endophytic bacteria in rice stems (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1).

Alpha diversity indexes of rice endophytic bacteria at different growth stages from different sites. a, b showed Shannon and Chao1 indices of endophytic bacteria, respectively. LC, MC and HC represent lower, moderate and higher cadmium-contaminated sites, respectively. Different letters indicate significant variation between groups by Kruskal-Wallis sum-rank test (p < 0.05; Dunn’s post-hoc test)

To further discover the effect of soil Cd and rice tissue type in microbial community structure, principal co-ordinates analysis (PCoA) and permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) were conducted using Bray-Curtis distance metrics. The results revealed that compared with tillering stage, the microbial community structure in different samples were clearly distinguished in filling (r = 0.80613, p = 0.001) and maturity (r = 0.62009, p = 0.001) stages, and the primary axis mainly distinguished samples by rice tissue type (Addition file 1, Fig. S2). The PERMANOVA further illustrated that the rice tissue type was a dominant factor (r2 = 0.1295, p = 0.0013) shaping rice endophytic bacterial structure (Addition file 2, Table S1).

The composition of rice endophytic bacteria community

To further investigate the effect of soil Cd concentration in bacterial community composition, the taxonomic composition at the genus level were analyzed. The results showed that at tillering stage, the effect of soil Cd concentration on bacterial composition of rice roots was greater than that of stems and leaves (Addition file 1, Fig. S3a). At tillering stage, unclassified bacteria displayed the highest relative abundance in roots, accounting for 43.3%, 32.4% and 35.8% in sites LC, MC and HC, respectively. This was followed by the bacteria of Sulfurospirillum, Cellulomonas, Clostridium and Acidovorax in site LC, while Priestia, Bradyrhizobium, Clostridium and Acinetobacter in site MC, as well as Ciceribacter, Acidovorax, Paraburkholderia and Citrobacter in site HC. Generally, at tillering stage, the top 5 bacteria in stems and leaves were same, including Acinetobacter, unclassified bacteria, Perlucidibaca, Massilia and Bradyrhizobium (Addition file 1, Fig. S3a).

At filling stage, the dominant endophytic bacteria (relative abundance ≥ 5%) changed with the soil Cd concentration, and the number of dominant bacterial genera in roots was less than that in stems and leaves (Addition file 1, Fig. S3b). In roots, unclassified bacteria were the most dominant bacteria in 3 sites, followed by Clostridium in site LC, as well as Clostridium and Bradyrhizobium in site MC. Moreover, in stems, the endophytic bacteria in site LC were dominated by unclassified bacteria, Pantoea, Lactococcus, Rhodanobacter and Novosphingobium, while unclassified bacteria, Methylobacterium and Sphingomonas were the dominant bacteria in site MC, and the dominant bacteria in site HC were Acinetobacter, unclassified bacteria, Novosphingobium, Methylobacterium, Perlucidibaca and Sphingomonas. In addition, unclassified bacteria, Acinetobacter and Perlucidibaca were the dominant bacteria in leaves from sites LC and HC, while Xanthomonas and Methylobacterium were the other dominant genera in sites LC and HC, respectively. However, in leaves from site MC, the dominant bacteria included unclassified bacteria, Bacillus and Agrobacterium (Addition file 1, Fig. S3b).

Similarly, at maturity stage, the soil Cd concentration changed the dominant endophytic bacteria in rice (Fig. 2). In roots, unclassified bacteria were the most dominant bacteria in 3 sites, while Clostridium, Herbaspirillum and Ciceribacter were the dominant genera in sites LC, MC and HC, respectively. In stems, unclassified bacteria, Rickettsia, Herbaspirillum and Methylobacterium were the dominant bacteria in site LC, while bacteria in site MC were dominated by unclassified bacteria, Brucella and Acinetobacter, and the dominant bacteria in site LC were Acidovorax, unclassified bacteria and Methylobacterium. Moreover, in rice leaves, the dominant bacteria in site LC included Acinetobacter, Perlucidibaca, unclassified bacteria and Bradyrhizobium, while Acinetobacter, unclassified bacteria and Perlucidibaca were the dominant bacteria in site MC, and the bacteria in site HC were dominated by Pantoea, Herbaspirillum, unclassified bacteria, Agrobacterium and Acinetobacter. Furthermore, in rice seeds, unclassified bacteria, Acinetobacter and Perlucidibaca dominated in site LC, and the dominant bacteria in site MC included Pantoea, unclassified bacteria, Rhodanobacter and Acinetobacter, while Acinetobacter, unclassified bacteria, Perlucidibaca and Pantoea were the dominant bacteria in site HC (Fig. 2).

The effect of soil Cd concentration on rice endophytic bacterial composition at maturity. LC, MC and HC indicate lower, moderate and higher cadmium-contaminated sites, respectively

The correlations between dominant bacteria and environmental factors

Given soil Cd concentrations induced changes in dominant endophytic bacteria of rice, their potential role in rice Cd tolerance and grain Cd concentration was explored by Spearman’s correlation analysis between the dominant endophytic bacteria (top 20 genera) and environmental factors (Fig. 3). In roots, Rhizobium, Tolumonas, Actinomadura and Micromonospora were significantly correlated with the soil total Cd concentration, while the available Cd concentration in soils was significantly correlated with Priestia, Methylocystis, Methylosinus, Streptomyces, Tolumonas and Actinomadura. In addition, the total Cd concentration in rice grains was positively correlated with Paraburkholderia and Citrobacter, while being negatively associated with Methylocystis, Methylosinus and Actinomadura (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3a).

Spearman’s correlation between environmental factors and the top 20 bacterial genera of rice roots (a), stems (b), leaves (c) and seeds (d). AP: available phosphorus; AK: available potassium; OM: organic matter; HN: hydrolysable nitrogen; TCd-S: total cadmium concentration in soils; ACd-S: available cadmium concentration in soils; TCd-G: total cadmium concentration in rice grains. (*: p < 0.05, **: p < 0.01, ***: p < 0.001)

In rice stems, the total Cd concentration in soils was negatively associated with Rhodanobacter, and the soil-available Cd concentration was positively correlated with Citrobacter and Bradyrhizobium (p < 0.05), while the top 20 bacterial genera were not significantly correlated with grain Cd concentration (p > 0.05) (Fig. 3b). Similarly, some dominant endophytic bacteria in leaves, such as Naasia, Sphingobacterium, Acinetobacter, Rhizobium, etc. were significantly correlated with soil total Cd concentration (p < 0.05), while the top 20 bacteria were not significantly correlated with the soil-available and grain Cd concentration (p > 0.05) (Fig. 3c). Furthermore, seed endophytic Arcobacter was negatively correlated with the soil total Cd concentration, while seed endophytic Pantoea, Mycobacteroides, Naasia and Perlucidibaca were significantly associated with the available Cd concentration in soils (p < 0.05). And the grain Cd concentration was negatively associated with seed endophytic Bradyrhizobium and Brevundimonas (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3d).

Bacterial co-occurrence networks and their keystone genera

To find out the interactions among rice endophytic bacteria, the microbial co-occurrence networks of rice exposed to varying Cd concentrations were constructed. Higher Cd concentration in soils notably altered the topological structure of bacterial networks. The complexity of bacterial networks decreased with the soil total Cd concentration increasing, as shown by the decline of average degree and the number of edges (Fig. 4a-c). In addition, the networks exhibited good modularity, and modularity coefficients were above 0.5. The increase of the soil total Cd concentration enhanced the modularity of bacterial networks, and modularity coefficients were 0.507, 0.521 and 0.614 in sites LC, MC and HC, respectively (Fig. 4a-c). Additionally, regardless of the soil total Cd concentration, the taxa within the bacterial community mainly displayed synergistic relationships (positive correlation > 90%).

Co-occurrence network (a-c) and Zi-Pi analysis (d-f) of endophytic bacterial communities of rice from different sites. LC, MC and HC represent lower, moderate and higher cadmium-contaminated sites, respectively. In the network, node colors represent different modules (modularity classes) identified by the Louvain algorithm

Subsequently, keystone genera within endophytic bacterial co-occurrence networks were identified using Zi-Pi analysis. The results indicated that no network hubs were detected and most taxa were categorized as peripherals, which suggested that most genera possessed finite connections and were mostly linked within their own modules (Fig. 4d-f). Notably, Azorhizobium and Methylobacterium were classified as connectors in site LC, and Naasia was classified as the connector in site MC, which connect different modules in rice microbial co-occurrence network (Fig. 4d, e). In addition, Microbacterium was highly connected node within module and classified as the module hub in site MC (Fig. 4e).

The core endophytic bacterial microbiota

To identify the unique and shared rice core endophytic bacteria, a Venn diagram analysis of bacterial OTUs was performed. Generally, the increase of soil Cd concentration led to a decline of unique endophytic bacteria OTUs in rice roots, stems, leaves and seeds (Fig. 5a). Despite the presence of many unique OTUs in rice different tissue, 44 OTUs consistently presented in all groups, which were defined as the core endophytic microbiota of rice under Cd stress (Fig. 5a). The core taxa included Bradyrhizobium, Sphingomonas, Methylobacterium, Acinetobacter, Agrobacterium, Bacillus, Citrobacter, etc. (Fig. 5b). Notably, some core taxa were dominant genera and significantly correlated with Cd concentrations in soils and grains, such as Bradyrhizobium, Brevundimonas, Citrobacter, Pantoea, etc., suggesting their potential important role in regulating Cd accumulation in rice grains.

The diversity of rice endophytic bacteria discovered by the culture-dependent method

There were only 1387 endophytic bacteria which belonged to 23 genera isolated from rice tissues at different stages from 3 sites. It was found that the soil Cd concentration changed the composition of culturable endophytic bacteria of rice. At tillering stage, Bacillus was the most dominant taxon in rice roots in sites LC and SC, while in site MC, the most dominant bacterium in roots was Priestia. And in sites LC, MC and SC, the most dominant bacterium in stems was different, which was Brevundimonas, Bacillus and Microbacterium, respectively. In leaves, Microbacterium and Bacillus were the most dominant taxon in sites LC and SC, respectively. While in site MC, Microbacterium and Pantoea accounted for the same and the largest proportion in rice leaves at tillering stage (Addition file 1, Fig. S4a).

At filling stage, Bacillus, Rossellomorea and Acinetobacter were the most dominant bacterium in rice roots in sites LC, MC and SC, respectively. In addition, Sphingobacterium, Serratia and Aeromonas were the most dominant bacterium in rice stems in sites LC, MC and SC, respectively. And in leaves, the bacteria in sites LC and HC were most dominated by Microbacterium, while in site MC was most dominated by Serratia (Addition file 1, Fig. S4b).

At maturity stage, Bacillus displayed the highest relative abundance in roots in sites LC, MC and SC, while the other dominant bacteria in 3 sites were different. In rice stems, Bacillus, Microbacterium and Pantoea were the most abundant in site LC, and Microbacterium was the most dominant bacterium in site MC, while the majority in site SC were Microbacterium and Acinetobacter. Moreover, in leaves, endophytic bacteria in site LC were dominated by Pantoea and Microbacterium, and the bacteria in site MC were dominated by Microbacterium and Aeromonas, while the bacteria in site SC were dominated by Aeromonas and Bacillus. Interestingly, the number of endophytic bacterial genera in seeds was less than that of roots, stems and leaves, and 6 genera were detected. Pantoea, Sphingomonas and Sphingobacterium were the most dominant bacterium in rice seeds in sites LC, MC and SC, respectively (Fig. 6).

Composition of culturable endophytic bacteria at the genus level of different rice tissues at maturity stage. LC, MC and HC represent lower, moderate and higher cadmium-contaminated sites, respectively

Moreover, in the same tissue, the composition of culturable and unculturable endophytic bacteria was different, especially the dominant taxa. For example, in roots at maturity stage, Bacillus was the most dominant culturable bacterium in all 3 sites, while it was rarely found in unculturable endophytic bacteria (Figs. 2 and 6).

PGP traits and Cd tolerance of culturable endophytic bacteria

Based on composition of culturable endophytic bacteria, 38 strains belonging to 10 genera were selected for functional characterization (Fig. 7). The results illustrated that the ration of Cd tolerance strains in site HC were highest (50.0%), followed by site LC (40.0%). Considering the role of PGP traits in enhancing stress tolerance in plants and changing their HM accumulation, 3 key PGP traits were further quantified. In total, 34 (89.5%) strains were capable of producing IAA, with Agrobacterium sp. GCRD9 producing the highest concentration (58.10 mg/L), followed by Rhizobium sp. GCRC14 (57.95 mg/L) and Microbacterium sp. HCLD6 (55.32 mg/L). The siderophore production test indicated that only 3 strains could produce siderophores, with strains Microbacterium sp. HCRC3 (74.26% SU), Bacillus sp. GBRA29 (18.62% SU), and Priestia sp. HBRA17 (16.67% SU). Finally, 29 (76.3%) strains exhibited P-solubilizing ability, with Bacillus sp. HCRD9 (79.91 mg/L), Microbacterium sp. HCSA4 (78.14 mg/L), and Sphingomonas sp. GCZ5 (74.96 mg/L) displaying the highest capacities of P solubilization (Fig. 7). Given its strong PGP traits and Cd tolerance, the strain Agrobacterium sp. GCRD9 was considered as the candidate for further studies about safe rice production in Cd-contaminated soil. And Cd-tolerant strain Bacillus cereus HE2 with less PGP capability was considered as control.

The effect of endophytes on rice growth, productivity and Cd accumulation

To test whether endophytic bacteria with PGP traits and Cd tolerance affect rice growth, productivity and Cd accumulation, pot experiments were performed. The results illustrated under Cd stress, the inoculation of endophytic Agrobacterium sp. GCRD9 and B. cereus HE2 improved rice growth, especially the root lengths were significantly improved (p < 0.05) (Fig. 8a, b). Although the grain yield per plant of bacteria-inoculated rice was not significantly improved when compared with uninoculated rice (Fig. 8c). However, the thousand-grain weight was increased by bacterial inoculation. In contrast, the grain numbers per panicle and effective tiller number in rice under Cd stress were not changed by bacterial inoculation (Fig. 8d-f). Similarly, the inoculation of GCRD9 and HE2 didn’t change Cd accumulation in rice roots. Notably, inoculation of GCRD9 significantly inhibited grains Cd accumulation, and the Cd concentration and BCF in grains were reduced by 55.6% and 53.7%, respectively (Fig. 8g, h).

Discussion

Cadmium (Cd) contamination in paddy soils has become an increasing concern due to its adverse effect on rice yield and quality [34, 35]. It has been found that endophytic bacteria can enhance host plants HM tolerance and regulate their HM accumulation by promoting nutrient acquisition, enhancing antioxidant system, coordinating HM-responsive gene expression and others [22, 36]. Understanding rice endophytic bacterial communities that respond to Cd stress, and their roles in improving rice yield and reducing Cd accumulation, will potentially improve rice production. The present study found that the increase of total Cd concentration in soil generally decreased the alpha diversity of rice endophytic bacteria, especially the species diversity (Shannon index) and richness (Chao1 index) of endophytic bacteria in stems at tillering stage (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1). This finding suggested that HM contamination reshaped endophyte communities by imposing selective pressures, thereby reducing bacteria and forming a smaller pool of species that could tolerate and thrive under Cd stress [21, 37]. Spearman’s correlation analysis showed that some dominant endophytic bacteria were significantly correlated with soil Cd concentrations, available nutrients and pH, indicating that multiple soil physicochemical properties affected rice endophytic bacterial communities. In addition, the rice tissue type was the primary factor affecting the alpha diversity of rice endophytic bacterial communities; the diversity and richness of endophytic bacteria in stems and leaves generally decreased compared with roots (Fig. 1). The beta diversity results further revealed that the rice tissue was one of the major factors (r2 = 0.1295, p = 0.0013) shaping rice endophytic bacterial structure (Addition file 2, Table S1). Previous studies confirmed that tissue type is one of the dominant factors determining the endophyte community [38, 39]. Similarly, Cárdenas-Hernández et al. [40] found that the diversity of the endophytic microbial community in Rhizophora mangle roots was higher than that of leaves. The higher diversity of endophytes in roots maybe attributed not only to the fact that roots are direct contact with the soil with abundant and active microbes, but also to the higher exchange of metabolism between host plant and endophytes in roots [41, 42].

The soil total Cd concentration significantly changed the dominant endophytic bacteria in rice. Culture-independent results showed that some endophytic bacteria were dominant in rice at sites LC, MC and HC, but several genera were only dominant in rice at one site, such as Priestia in rice roots in site MC at tillering stage, and Xanthomonas in leaves in site LC at filling stage (Fig. 2 and Addition file 1, Fig. S3). Similarly, the most dominant genera of culturable endophytic bacteria in rice at 3 sites were generally different (Fig. 6 and Addition file 1, Fig. S4). These results are consistent with previous findings that HM contamination levels in soils had a significant influence on the dominant taxa of endophytes, suggesting that the selective recruitment of rice endophytic bacteria may play an essential role in rice Cd stress and grain Cd uptake [21, 43]. The correlation analysis of dominant endophytic bacteria with environmental factors supported this: some bacteria such as Methylocystis, Methylosinus, Bradyrhizobium and Brevundimonas were significantly correlated with Cd concentrations in rice grain (Fig. 3). These bacteria may possess Cd resistance and aid plants in Cd resistance. These microbes for example, could strongly resist and bind HMs (Cu, Pb, Cd, mercury (Hg) and others) by their multiple metal resistance genes and secreting methanobactins, thereby being used to alleviate metal toxicity in plants [44–46]. In addition, Faheem et al. [47] reported that the epipelon biofilm with multiple methanotrophs (Methylobacter, Methylosinus, Methylocystis, etc.) was able to efficiently remove Cd, lead (Pb) and chromium (Cr) in aqueous ecosystems with high methane. Methanotrophs may be important to remediate Cd-contaminated paddy and reduce Cd concentration in rice grains, considering that rice cultivation is one of the major anthropogenic methane sources in the world [48, 49].

Co-occurrence network analysis was conducted to explore the interaction among rice endophytic bacteria and their ecological roles in rice under Cd stress. The results revealed that with the increase of soil total Cd concentration, the complexity of endophytic bacterial network in rice decreased but its modularity increased (Fig. 4a-c). The observations align with findings from Yao et al. [50], who reported that the complexity of the endophytic bacterial network in Robinia pseudoacacia was decreased with the rise in soil HM concentration, while the bacterial network in higher HM-contaminated site showed the greatest modularity. These results suggested that higher Cd concentrations in soils may restrain interactions among endophytic bacteria, but lead to a variety of survival strategies and functional modules [22, 51]. Keystone taxa in bacterial networks were further assessed by Zi-Pi analysis, while 5 connectors and 1 module hub were detected (Fig. 4d-f). These genera might play important roles in the cohesion and inter-module interactions of endophytic bacterial network [52, 53].

Although endophytic communities change in response to environmental fluctuations, core microbiome populations tend to consistently present in plants, regardless of the impact of external factors [23, 54, 55]. In this study, 44 core OTUs were identified in rice at different growth stages from three sites (Fig. 5). Notably, several of these taxa, including Bradyrhizobium, Methylobacterium, Brevundimonas, Bacillus and Acinetobacter have also been found as core microbes in other plants in HM-contaminated soils [21, 56]. Particularly, some core taxa belonged to dominant genera and significantly correlated with soil and grain Cd concentrations, such as Bradyrhizobium, Brevundimonas, Citrobacter, etc. (Figures 3 and 5). These results suggested that these core bacterial taxa not only persist across different plant tissues but also have the potential to regulate HM tolerance and accumulation in host plants, further supporting the idea of their potential role in rice Cd tolerance and grain Cd accumulation [21, 57].

The difference of endophytic bacterial community was found between culture-dependent and culture-independent study. Totally, 917 genera were detected by culture-independent method but only 23 bacterial genera were isolated by culture-dependent method. Furthermore, the dominant endophytic bacteria in same tissue between two studies generally differ. For example, in roots at maturity stage, Bacillus was the most dominant culturable bacterium in all 3 sites, while it was rarely found in unculturable endophytic bacteria (Figs. 2 and 6). These findings can be explained by the fact that most of endophytes are recalcitrant or unculturable strains, which could not be separated and studied by the culturable method [58, 59]. It suggested that to understand the endophytic community comprehensively, combination of culture-dependent and culture-independent methods is necessary.

To further explore the potential role of rice endophytic bacteria in Cd tolerance and grain Cd accumulation, PGP traits and Cd tolerance of culturable endophytic bacteria and their role in rice under Cd stress were investigated. The results showed that 36.8% (14) investigated bacteria strains could tolerate 25 mg/L Cd2+, and most of the investigated bacteria strains possessed the ability of IAA production and P solubilization, but only 3 strains were able to produce siderophores (Fig. 7). Cd tolerance of endophytic bacteria was the basis for their survival and function under a Cd stress environment, while above 3 PGP traits in endophytic bacteria not only promoted the host plants’ growth but also regulated their HM accumulation through various ways [60–63]. For example, Cui et al. [64] found that IAA reduced the Cd phytotoxicity and its accumulation in Poa pratensis by increasing the amount of vacuole-compartmentalized and organelle-bound Cd in root cells, regulating the expression of genes associated with Cd transporters, phosphatases and others, as well as increasing the content of P- and oxalate-bound Cd. Further analysis revealed that Cd-tolerant Agrobacterium sp. GCRD9 exhibited strongest IAA production ability (58.10 mg/L) in all investigated bacteria strains and possessed high P solubilization ability (62.48 mg/L) (Fig. 7), suggesting that GCRD9 might play an important role in regulating Cd uptake in rice grains.

Pot experiments showed that inoculation of GCRD9 significantly reducing Cd concentration and BCF in rice grains. Under Cd stress, GCRD9 didn’t significantly improved the grain yield per plant, but significantly increased thousand-grain weight of rice, which is one of the major agronomic indexes determining rice yield (Fig. 8). The results indicated that GCRD9 was a potential target in safe rice production. Similarly, An et al. [65] found that soil Agrobacterium sp. T3F4 with a high ability to produce IAA and solubilize inorganic P, decreased As/Cd concentrations in brown rice and increased grain yield by reducing As/Cd bioavailability, stimulating iron (Fe) plaque formation, alleviating rice oxidative stress and enhancing soil enzyme activities. One limitation of this study is that pot experiments were conducted using the non-contaminated paddy field soils and then spiked with 0 and 5 mg/kg Cd2+ based on previous studies [29, 30], rather than the original contaminated paddy soils. Although this design minimized interference from varying soil physicochemical properties and allowed a focused assessment of the GCRD9 Cd-mitigation potential under controlled conditions, it does not fully represent the complex field environment. The effectiveness of GCRD9 under realistic conditions will be tested by field experiments in future research.

Conclusion

Tissue type contributed to rice endophytic bacterial community structure; soil Cd concentration also exerted a significant influence on it. Notably, some specific bacteria showed significant correlation with grain Cd concentration, such as Methylocystis spp., Bradyrhizobium spp. and Brevundimonas spp. The co-occurrence network analysis revealed that Cd stress simplified community interactions but increased modularity, suggesting a strategy of functional compartmentalization, while keystone taxa underpin the stability of the bacterial network. The core microbiome pointed to a conserved, potentially beneficial microbial backbone resilient to Cd contamination. And some of them exhibited multiple PGP traits and Cd tolerance. Importantly, pot experiments showed that the Agrobacterium sp. GCRD9 significantly increased roots length and thousand-grain weight of rice under Cd stress, while significantly reducing Cd concentration and BCF in rice grains, suggesting a potential application in regulating Cd accumulation in rice grains. These findings provide insights into the critical roles of endophytic bacteria in rice adaptation to Cd stress and highlight its potential to enhance crop production in metal-contaminated farmlands.
