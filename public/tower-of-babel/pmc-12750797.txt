Cost-effectiveness of pay-for-performance incentives for topical fluoride application among US children: a decision-analytic modelling study
Authors: Sung Eun Choi, Daniel Nolte, Ankur Pandya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePay-for-performance (P4P) programmes are increasingly implemented in healthcare to improve quality of care, but their application in dentistry remains limited. Evidence-based approaches are needed to guide incentive design in value-based dental care, particularly for preventive services, such as topical fluoride application in children. We sought to assess the potential cost-effectiveness of P4P incentives for increasing topical fluoride application among children and to illustrate how simulation modelling can identify conditions under which subgroup-specific incentive levels may be optimal.Design, setting and participantsWe developed and validated a decision-analytic microsimulation model using nationally representative data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES 2011–2016) to simulate a cohort of 100 000 US children aged 0–19 years over a 10-year period starting in 2024. The model incorporated heterogeneity in demographic and clinical characteristics to estimate changes in dental caries, quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) and healthcare costs under hypothetical P4P programmes that increased topical fluoride coverage by 2.5% to 50% relative to the baseline rate of 24.5%, with incentive amounts ranging from 2.5% to 50% of provider salary. Sensitivity analyses assessed robustness to variation in key parameters.Main outcomesCumulative incidence of dental caries, QALYs, total healthcare costs and incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs).ResultsAcross a broad range of incentive–coverage combinations, P4P incentives were generally cost-effective. For example, a 10% salary-based incentive linked to a 5% relative coverage increase reduced 186.3 cases of tooth decay and yielded 33.8 QALY gains per 10 000 children, resulting in an ICER of $8501 per QALY gained. The intervention was estimated to be cost-saving at coverage increases ≥27.5%. Subgroup analysis indicated larger absolute benefits among racial/ethnic minority children.ConclusionP4P incentives to increase topical fluoride application in children could be cost-effective, and potentially cost-saving, under certain conditions. Cost-effectiveness modelling can help define incentive–coverage combinations that are likely to promote both efficiency and equity. Empirical studies are needed to validate provider responsiveness and establish achievable benchmarks for programme design.

Introduction

Pay-for-performance (P4P) in healthcare is a payment model that attaches financial incentives or disincentives to provider performance.1 It is part of the national strategy to transition healthcare to value-based care. While it still uses the fee-for-service system, it encourages providers toward value-based care, determined based on the health benefits relative to their associated costs, by aligning reimbursement with evidence-based performance metrics.2 P4P models have been widely implemented and have been linked to improvements in preventive care, chronic disease management and outcomes in acute conditions.3 4 Dentistry, however, has lagged behind in adopting such reforms, despite oral health being a critical component of overall health and a driver of significant health expenditures.5

A few community-based initiatives and pilot studies by payers and dental practices have tested value-based dental care models, including P4P programmes that reward providers for meeting predefined targets for preventive services, such as fluoride application and routine evaluation.58 These early initiatives highlighted both opportunities and challenges. While preventive services, such as topical fluoride, are relatively low cost but highly effective, setting incentive levels has often relied on ad hoc judgement rather than a systematic approach, requiring programme revisions and uncertainty about long-term sustainability.6

Cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA) provides a rigorous framework for designing P4P programmes by quantifying the health gains relative to the incremental costs of incentive payments and increased service provision.9 10 Such an approach can identify incentive-coverage combinations that yield the greatest value and determine scenarios where higher incentives may be justified. For example, higher incentive payments may be justified for underserved populations to address disparities in care. This framework can extend beyond dentistry, offering tools for other disciplines of primary care and prevention where evidence-based but underutilised services could be scaled through incentive reform.

The goal of this study was to use a decision-analytic microsimulation model to estimate how hypothetical increases in topical fluoride provision relative to the current coverage rate would affect children’s oral health outcomes, quality-adjusted life years (QALYs), and healthcare costs under a range of P4P incentive levels. We sought to demonstrate how the CEA framework can inform the design of population-specific P4P programmes in dentistry while also illustrating its broader implications for value-based payment reform in preventive care.

Methods

We developed and validated a stochastic microsimulation model to estimate the impact of quality improvement, defined as increased provision of topical fluoride, on the incidence of tooth decay, QALYs and healthcare costs under various P4P incentive levels, relative to the status quo. The model incorporated heterogeneity in individual characteristics using a decision analytic framework (details in online supplemental eMethods).

Data sources

Table 1 summarises key model parameters and data sources with details in online supplemental eTable 1. Baseline demographic characteristics, dental utilisation, access to water fluoridation and oral health examination data were obtained from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES, 2011–2016, <20 years old, n=8484), which is a unique data source in that it is the only US national survey that contains clinical oral health examination data, providing a more objective assessment of dental disease than self-reported outcomes, which are often confounded by access to care.11 Survey sample weights were used to correct for differential sampling and non-response in the NHANES.12 Baseline population characteristics are provided in online supplemental eTables 2-3. Additional model input parameters, including effectiveness of fluoride application on reducing the risk of tooth decay, were obtained from peer-reviewed literature.13

Simulation model

We simulated a nationally representative sample of 100 000 Americans aged 0–19 years starting in 2024. The model projected cumulative incidence of dental caries, QALYs and healthcare costs, resulting from increases in the rate of fluoride application at given P4P incentive levels accounting for differences in demographic composition, socioeconomic status, disease risks and access to water fluoridation. The model was simulated over a 10-year period to be consistent with policy planning horizons and to minimise longitudinal uncertainty in our estimates. The synthetic population was stratified by key characteristics: age (0–5, 6–12, 13–19 years), sex, race/ethnicity (NHANES categories of Hispanic (Mexican American and other Hispanic), non-Hispanic (NH) Black, NH White and other race), income (low (≤130% federal poverty level (FPL)), middle (130%–300% FPL), and high (>300% FPL)), insurance (private, public and uninsured) and access to community water fluoridation (online supplemental eTable 2).

Dental caries was defined as having any signs of decayed, filled on the crown or enamel surface of a tooth, or missing teeth due to caries with the missing component excluded for primary teeth.14 Binary indicators for caries incidence were assigned to each simulated individual and summed to calculate the total number of decayed teeth for the simulated individuals as a function of age, race, insurance and access to water fluoridation (online supplemental eMethods). To ensure internal validity, we calibrated the model against dental caries prevalence from NHANES by age groups and race (online supplemental eFigure 1). Dental utilisation and incidence rates were updated annually in the simulation model to reflect age and secular trends. Children with untreated caries were modelled to face risks of progression to abscess and tooth loss.1517

Simulated interventions

We evaluated hypothetical P4P policies that increased topical fluoride application rates above the status quo, where only 24.5% of children receive such treatment.1820 Using our microsimulation model, we conducted a two-way sensitivity analysis varying (1) Topical fluoride coverage improvements (2.5% to 50% relative increases above the 24.5% baseline and (2) Financial incentive levels (2.5% to 50% of a typical dental practitioner’s wage for meeting quality targets). We modelled incentive payments as ‘target-based bonuses’, lump-sum payments equivalent to a fixed percentage of a provider’s annual salary, contingent on achieving a relative increase in fluoride application coverage among their patient panel. This approach approximates real-world incentive structures where providers are rewarded for meeting predefined quality improvement thresholds, rather than per-application bonuses. We estimated changes in total incidence of tooth decay, QALYs, cost and incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) accrued over a 10-year period when these two parameters were varied simultaneously compared with the status quo. Interventions were considered cost-effective if the ICER was below $50 000 per QALY gained.

Empirical evidence on the magnitude of fluoride coverage increases in response to financial incentives is limited. However, a prior implementation study demonstrated a P4P programme offering financial bonuses (10% of wage) for meeting quality targets.6 Based on this and expert judgement, we modelled plausible relative increases in fluoride coverage ranging from 2.5% to 50% above the status quo rate of 24.5%, resulting in absolute coverage levels between 25.1% to 37%. We conducted two-way sensitivity analyses varying both coverage and incentive amount to reflect a range of P4P programme designs. These scenarios were intended to inform policy design under varying assumptions of programme effectiveness and provider responsiveness.

Costs and utilities

Costs and QALY estimates were integrated over the simulated period for all simulated individuals from a healthcare perspective. Cost components included: topical fluoride application, treatment of caries, abscess and tooth loss, and provider incentive payments. Dental treatment costs were obtained from the American Dental Association and a prior CEA (table 1 and online supplemental eTable 1).2123 Incentive costs were calculated based on a typical dentist’s wage data and average annual patient volume from the Bureau of Labor Statistics and 2022 estimates by the ADA (online supplemental eTable 1).24 25 Provider wages were based on data from the Bureau of Labour Statistics.25 Disutility weights of disease states (dental caries, tooth abscess and tooth loss) used to calculate QALYs were based on large-scale survey data and prior cost-effectiveness analyses.2628 Disutility weights were applied at the individual level. A caries-related disutility was assigned when a child experienced one or more active carious lesions during a model cycle, rather than being applied per affected tooth, to avoid assuming linear aggregation of disutility across multiple affected teeth. All costs were expressed in 2024 US Dollars using the Consumer Price Index,29 Personal healthcare Dental Service and Personal Consumption Expenditure,30 and costs and QALYs were discounted at a 3% annually.

Sensitivity and uncertainty analyses

We performed a probabilistic sensitivity analysis by sampling from the probability distributions of all input parameters. The parameter ranges and distributions used in our sensitivity analyses are summarised in table 1. To capture uncertainty in our estimates, we generated 100 representative populations of 100 000 individuals each. This number of iterations was selected because it produced stable results (mean ICER differed by ≤1.1% when comparing 100 vs 200 iterations with no change in preferred strategy). For each population, we repeatedly Monte Carlo sampled from the probability distributions of all input parameters. Outcome estimates were summarised with 95% uncertainty intervals computed in accordance with reporting guidelines.31 32 A cost-effectiveness acceptability curve was generated to illustrate the probability that a hypothetical scenario would be considered cost-effective across a range of willingness-to-pay thresholds.

We also conducted one-way sensitivity analyses on key model parameters, such as effectiveness of fluoride, risk of tooth abscess and loss, treatment costs and disutility weights by varying individual parameters to their extreme plausible values (table 1). We evaluated how variations in model parameters are estimated to affect the outcomes assuming financial incentives provided at 10% and 20% of one’s wage along with a 20% increase in treatment coverage (ie, increasing coverage from 24.5% to 29.4%). These ‘base case’ scenarios were informed by a previous P4P pilot study.6 The Supplemental Appendix details all input data, equations and complete technical details. All analyses were performed in 2025 using R (V.4.3.1, The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna). This study was reported in accordance with the CHEERS guidelines. This study was determined to be not human subjects research by the Harvard Medical School Institutional Review Board (ID: IRB24-1658). The analysis relied exclusively on de-identified publicly available data, and no additional ethics approval or informed consent was required.

Patient and public involvement

Our study was informed by publicly available data. We did not involve patients or the public in the design or conduct of this research. There are currently no plans to disseminate this research to specific patient populations.

Results

Model validation

Following calibration of the incidence of dental caries as a function of age, race, insurance and water fluoridation status, the simulation model reproduced expected rates of dental caries across population subgroups. Under the status quo, our model estimated an overall dental caries prevalence of 59.6% (95% UI 59.5% to 59.7%), with age-specific rates of 17.1% (95% UI 16.9% to 17.3%) among 0–5 year-olds, 51.7% (95% UI 51.5% to 51.9%) among 6–12 year-olds, 58.9% (95% UI 58.7% to 59.0%) among 13–19 year-olds and 80.2% (95% UI 80.0% to 80.3%) among 20–29 year-olds (online supplemental eFigure 1).

Two-way sensitivity analysis results

Our model demonstrated that incentivising incremental increases in topical fluoride provision would generally be cost-effective across a wide range of plausible P4P incentive levels and treatment coverage increases (figure 1). For example, consider a 10% salary-based incentive that would result in a 5% relative increase in fluoride coverage (ie, from 24.5% to 25.7%). In our simulation model, this scenario was estimated to reduce the cumulative incidence of dental caries by 186.3 (95% UI 47.2 to 322.7) cases per 10 000 and yield 33.8 (95% UI 5.9 to 59.6) QALYs per 10 000. This quality improvement would cost approximately $0.29 million (95% UI 0.17 to 0.41) per 10 000 and yield an ICER of $8501 per QALY gained (95% UI 7984 to 9062) (table 2). At the same 10% incentive level, increases in fluoride coverage by at least 27.5% (ie, from 24.5% to ≥31.2%) would be cost-saving (figure 1).

Sensitivity and subgroup analysis results

None of the sensitivity analyses substantially changed our fundamental findings. In the one-way sensitivity analysis, model outcomes were most sensitive to four parameters (in decreasing order of sensitivity): effectiveness of topical fluoride on risk of dental caries, dentist salary, cost of fluoride application and cost of treating dental caries (figure 2 and online supplemental eTable 5). Varying the remaining parameters across their plausible ranges had minimal effect on cost-effectiveness estimates. Under the base-case scenario (assuming a 20% and a 20% increase in fluoride coverage), variation in the effectiveness of fluoride produced ICERs ranging from $1463 (95% UI 1412 to 1516) to $7889 (95% UI 7702 to 8102) per QALY gained. Similarly, varying the dentist salary across its range yielded ICERs from $636 (at the low salary value) to $6440 (at the high salary value) per QALY gained (figure 2 and online supplemental eTable 5).

In probabilistic sensitivity analysis, a wide range of P4P strategies was preferred to the status quo at willingness-to-pay (WTP) thresholds well below $50 000/QALY (online supplemental eFigure 2). Cost-effectiveness acceptability curves indicated that a 20% salary incentive linked to a 20% increase in fluoride coverage was more cost-effective in 100% of simulation runs at a WTP threshold of $50 000/QALY, and became the preferred strategy (in 54% of simulation runs) at a WTP of $3100/QALY. At a WTP of $2500/QALY gained, however, this strategy was expected to be preferred in only 15% of simulations. When the same incentive was tied to a 10% coverage increase, the strategy became preferred at WTP greater than $7800/QALY.

The benefits of quality improvements were greatest among racial minorities, specifically Hispanic and NH Black individuals, due to their higher baseline risk of tooth decay. For example, under the strategy offering a 20% salary incentive to increase fluoride coverage by 20%, the prevalence of dental caries would decrease by 0.95 percentage points (95% UI 0.88 to 1.02) among Hispanic individuals, compared with a reduction of 0.78 percentage points (95% UI 0.66 to 0.90) among NH White individuals. Similarly, this strategy would generate 153.5 (95% UI 148.0 to 158.9) incremental QALYs at a cost of $0.39 million (95% UI 0.36 to 0.42) per 10 000 Hispanic individuals, while it would yield only 125.2 (95% UI 118.3 to 132.1) incremental QALYs at $0.51 million (95% UI 0.48 to 0.54) per 10 000 NH White individuals, indicating lower incremental benefits in this population (figure 3). When incorporating these subgroup-specific differences, we found that at a 27.5% increase in fluoride coverage (where the programme becomes cost-saving for the general population with a 10% salary incentive), it would remain cost-saving for the Hispanic population even if the incentive increased to 12.5% of salary. In contrast, among NH White individuals, the programme would only remain cost-saving if the incentive level stayed at or below 7.5% of salary (figure 1 and online supplemental eFigure 3).

Discussion

This exploratory study used a microsimulation model to evaluate the potential value of P4P incentives for improving preventive dental care use and to illustrate its broader implication in the designing of such programmes in the absence of robust empirical data. We found that modest increases in topical fluoride application coverage, tied to relatively small financial incentives (eg, 10%–20% of provider salary), could reduce the incidence of dental caries, generate meaningful gains in QALYs, and represent cost-effective or even cost-saving strategies under some conditions.

Our findings indicated that P4P incentives for fluoride application could be cost-effective under certain conditions. Although the lack of empirical evidence on provider responsiveness could limit definitive policy implications,6 33 our study illustrates how CEA could serve as a decision-support tool to guide value-based payment models by quantifying incentive-performance trade-offs, particularly when evidence is lacking. In addition, the financial incentive levels required to be cost-effective in our analysis were relatively modest, suggesting that such programmes could be both affordable and scalable within the current dental delivery care model. However, it is important to note that barriers to broader implementation of P4P programmes exist, such as lack of standardised quality metrics in dentistry, limited infrastructure for performance tracking and reporting, and concerns among providers about administrative burden,83436 consistent with challenges faced in adopting value-based payment across healthcare. It would be important to address these challenges to ensure the feasibility and sustainability of P4P programmes in real-world settings.

Our simulation modelling demonstrates a framework to identify evidence-based optimal thresholds for both quality targets and financial incentive levels and highlights the fact that greater value can be realised among high-risk subgroups of population where the marginal health and economic returns from quality improvement are higher. These findings suggest that P4P programmes may be most efficient and equitable when financial incentive levels are determined to maximise value based on patient profiles, rather than applying uniform incentive levels across all providers or patients. Implementing tailored incentive levels based on population risk profiles may not only represent a more efficient use of resources in value-based payment models but also promote equity, emphasising the need for payment reform to account for social risk factors.

Concerns regarding fluoride toxicity are sometimes raised in public discourse; however, extensive evidence indicates that professionally applied topical fluoride, when delivered according to clinical guidelines, poses minimal risk of adverse effects or toxicity in children.37 38 The interventions evaluated in this study reflect standard preventive practices and do not involve fluoride exposures exceeding established safety thresholds.

Limitations

Our study has limitations inherent to decision-analytic modelling. First, we aimed to illustrate how decision-analytic modelling can support policy making when empirical evidence is lacking. By simulating a range of plausible scenarios, our findings are intended to guide future research and decision-making rather than to support immediate policy implementation. Second, the estimated effectiveness of fluoride application was derived from published literature, and although sensitivity analyses showed consistent results, the effectiveness may vary by care settings.39 Third, our model assumed that incentive payments would directly lead to increased fluoride coverage; however, in practice, the relationship between incentive levels and provider behaviour may be more complex and influenced by contextual factors such as administrative burden, provider capacity and patient preferences. Future empirical studies are needed to estimate the true elasticity of provider response to financial incentives. Additionally, we conducted our CEA from a healthcare perspective rather than a societal perspective and focused solely on the costs and benefits of interventions within the healthcare system itself. Because we did not model the costs and benefits to society of indirect outcomes, such as missed work and school, which were found to be substantial,47 our study may provide conservative estimates. Next, data from NHANES, which are subject to the limitations of survey studies, including recall biases, acceptability biases and underreporting, were used that may lead to underestimation of dental care use. However, because our model estimates the impact of intervention on a relative scale to the baseline, this bias would not change the fundamental findings of our study. Lastly, although uncertainty analyses were performed by sampling from distributions around the input parameter data sources, all possible uncertainties in a simulation model cannot be captured, hence our results should be interpreted in light of the assumptions embedded in our analytic framework.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrated that P4P incentives aimed at increasing preventive care among children, such as topical fluoride application, may be cost-effective and potentially cost-saving strategies under certain conditions. Tailoring incentive levels based on patient risk profiles may improve both efficiency and equity. Our study illustrated how CEA can guide value-based payment models for preventive services beyond dentistry when evidence is lacking; however, empirical evidence would be essential to establish achievable benchmarks and validate model-based findings before wider implementation.
