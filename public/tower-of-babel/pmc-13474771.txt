Terbium-doped 3D-printed carbonate hydroxyapatite scaffold enhances bone regeneration

Introduction

Oral and maxillofacial bone defects resulting from tumors, trauma, congenital abnormalities, and inflammatory diseases present significant clinical challenges, severely compromising masticatory, swallowing, and speech functions [1]. While autologous bone grafts remain the gold standard for reconstruction, their use is limited by donor site morbidity, insufficient supply, and poor adaptability. Allogeneic alternatives are similarly restricted by high costs, risks of immune rejection, and potential disease transmission. Bone tissue engineering (BTE), which employs a combination of seed cells, growth factors, and bioactive scaffolds, offers a promising strategy for regenerating critical-sized bone defects [2–4].

Sufficient bone volume is essential for successful dental implant rehabilitation [5]. However, post-extraction alveolar resorption frequently results in inadequate bone quality and volume, elevating the risk of implant failure. An ideal bone scaffold should exhibit customized biodegradation kinetics, osteoconductivity, and mechanical properties suitable for the dynamic oral environment. Although hydroxyapatite (HA) is a natural inorganic component of bone and has been widely investigated for BTE, conventional synthetic HA is stoichiometric and structurally divergent from biological apatite. Native bone mineral is poorly crystalline, contains carbonate substitutions (3–8 wt%), and incorporates various ionic replacements—features not present in synthetic HA. Consequently, pure HA scaffolds are often brittle, degrade slowly, and demonstrate limited interfacial bioactivity, greatly constraining their clinical utility [6–9].

Biological apatite mainly consists of carbonate-substituted HA (type-B CHA), in which carbonate ions replace phosphate groups, resulting in decreased crystallinity, increased solubility, and stronger collagen binding. These structural properties support bone remodeling and improve fracture resistance [10–12]. Furthermore, the strategic incorporation of osteogenic ions can enhance the biological performance of HA scaffolds [13]. Rare earth elements (REEs), whose ionic radii are similar to Ca²⁺, can be doped into the HA crystal lattice. At low concentrations, specific REEs such as Tb³⁺ have been shown to modify crystal morphology, improve corrosion resistance, and stimulate osteogenic differentiation through activation of calcium-sensitive signaling pathways [14–19]. Preliminary studies indicate that Tb³⁺ doping can significantly enhance pre-osteoblast proliferation, with Tb-doped materials showing up to a 10%-20% increase in cell viability compared to undoped controls, and markedly elevate alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity—a key early osteogenic marker—by approximately 30% after 7–14 days of culture.

To address the need for patient-specific bone regeneration in complex maxillofacial defects, this study developed a series of biomimetic HA-based powders via controlled carbonate substitution and Tb³⁺ doping. Scaffolds were produced using 3D printing and systematically assessed. Our key findings demonstrate that the 3D-printed Tb-CHA scaffold synergistically combines the advantages of structural precision and bioactive ion delivery. In vitro, Tb-CHA significantly outperformed pure HA and CHA, enhancing MC3T3-E1 cell proliferation by 6%-30% on day 5. We demonstrate that the synergistic effect of carbonate substitution and Tb³⁺ doping not only enhances new bone formation but also attenuates inflammatory response, providing a versatile platform for designing personalized scaffolds aimed at orofacial bone regeneration.

Materials and methods

The selection of scaffold materials is critical in bone tissue engineering. In this study, CHA was used as the primary material since its superior crystallinity and structural porosity are conducive to bone tissue reconstruction. Additionally, the porous structure of CHA facilitates binding with collagen and other proteins, reducing material brittleness and enhancing its plasticity. This study successfully developed a series of CHA biomimetic bone powder materials with varying concentrations of carbonate and rare earth ions by controlling the substitution of carbonate ions and doping with rare earth elements.

Materials

Preparation of rare earth element-doped hydroxyapatite and carbonated hydroxyapatite nano bone powder

K2HPO4(CAS: 7758−11−4), Ca(NO3)2(CAS: 13477−34−4), and RE(NO3)3 (CAS: 10361−80−5) (rare earth element nitrate, such as Tb(NO3)3) were dissolved in high-purity deionized water to prepare a 0.4-mol/L solution. 12.6 mL of KH2PO4 solution was transferred into a beaker and stirred with a magnetic stirrer. Then, 21.0 mL of Ca(NO3)2 solution was added and mixed evenly. Subsequently, a certain amount of RE(NO3)3 solution was added, followed by different amounts of K2CO3. Finally, 4.5 g of solid KOH was added to the beaker. After a white flocculent mixed precipitate was formed, it was transferred to a reaction kettle, reacted at 200 °C for 24 h, and dried overnight at 80 °C for subsequent testing and use [20].

Preparation and characterization of 3D-printed Tb-CHA scaffolds

The powders of various experimental materials were sieved through a 300-mesh screen. First, 1 g of hydroxypropyl chitosan was taken, and 2 mL of acetic acid and 7 mL of deionized water were added to dissolve to prepare a binder. The powders were mixed with the binder in a mass ratio of 1:1 and stirred thoroughly. Simplify 3D 4.0.1 was used to prepare the 3D scaffold model. After printing, it was dried at room temperature overnight and sintered at 600 °C for 5 h. After synthesis, the surface morphology of each group of scaffolds was observed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and the elemental composition of the sintered scaffold surface was analyzed using an X-ray diffraction spectrometer [20].

Methods

Biocompatibility evaluation of Tb-CHA scaffolds

① SEM Analysis of Cell-Scaffold Adhesion: Circular scaffolds measuring 6 mm in diameter were sterilized, hydrated, and positioned in a 96-well plate. MC3T3-E1 cells were inoculated onto the scaffolds at a density of 5 × 10³ cells per well. Following a 24-hour incubation period, the cell–scaffold constructs were harvested, rinsed with PBS, and subjected to fixation in 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C overnight. After subsequent PBS washes, the samples were dehydrated using a graded ethanol series (30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, and two changes of 100%), followed by vacuum drying at 37 °C overnight. Finally, the specimens were sputter-coated with gold and imaged via scanning electron microscopy to examine cell adhesion.

② Cell Proliferation Assessed by CCK-8 Assay: Sterilized and pre-wetted scaffolds were placed in a 96-well plate, immersed in culture medium, and equilibrated overnight at 37 °C. The medium was then aspirated, and the scaffolds were gently dried using sterile absorbent paper before a brief 1-hour incubation at 37 °C. MC3T3-E1 cells were seeded at 5 × 10³ cells per well, and the medium was refreshed every two days. On days 1, 3, and 5, cell proliferation was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay. At each time point, scaffolds were transferred to a new 96-well plate, rinsed with PBS, and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C in medium containing 10% CCK-8 reagent. After gentle pipetting to homogenize the solution, the scaffolds were removed, and the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

③ Live/dead staining for cell viability: Sterilized and hydrated scaffolds were placed in a 48-well plate, and MC3T3-E1 cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 10⁴ cells per well. The medium was replaced every other day. On day 7, cell viability was assessed using a live/dead double-staining kit. The 10× assay buffer was thawed and diluted to 1× with deionized water. Calcein-AM (2 mM) and propidium iodide (PI, 1.5 mM) solutions were thawed at room temperature for 20 min. Then, 5 µL of calcein-AM and 15 µL of PI were mixed into 5 mL of 1× assay buffer to prepare the staining solution (final concentrations: 2 µM calcein-AM and 4.5 µM PI). After removing the culture medium, the cell–scaffold constructs were washed twice with 1× assay buffer, incubated with 200 µL of staining solution for 15 min at 37 °C in the dark, and visualized under a laser scanning confocal microscope.

Osteogenic effects of Tb-CHA scaffolds

① ALP staining: The disinfected and pre-wetted scaffolds were placed in a 96-well plate; MC3T3-E1 cells were seeded onto the scaffolds in the 96-well plate at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well. The culture medium was changed to normal the next day; after 48 h, it was changed to cell culture medium containing osteogenic induction components (50 µg/mL ascorbic acid, 5 mM β-glycerophosphate), and the medium was changed every 2‒3 days thereafter. On the 7th and 14th days after changing to the osteogenic induction medium, the BCIP/NBT alkaline phosphatase coloring kit was used to detect the expression of ALP in each group. Staining was performed at room temperature in the dark for 2 h; the staining working solution was removed, followed by washing with distilled water twice to terminate the coloring reaction. Observations were made and photographs were taken under an inverted phase contrast microscope.

② Alizarin red staining: MC3T3-E1 cells were seeded at a density of 1.5 × 105 cells/well in a 6-well plate. The culture medium was changed to normal the next day; when the cells grew to 70‒80% confluence, the normal control group and each experimental group were changed to cell culture medium containing osteogenic induction components (50 µg/mL ascorbic acid, 5 mM β-glycerophosphate). The medium was changed every 2‒3 days thereafter. On the 21st day, after changing to an osteogenic induction medium, Alizarin Red staining was performed.

③ Western blot analysis of the effects of various scaffold materials on cell osteogenic differentiation: MC3T3-E1 cells were seeded at a density of 1.5 × 105 cells/well in a 6-well plate. The culture medium was changed to normal the next day. When the cells grew to 70 − 80% confluence, the normal control group and each experimental group (DMEM-H group, HA group, Tb-HA group, CHA group, Tb-CHA group) were changed to cell culture medium containing osteogenic induction components (50 µg/mL ascorbic acid, 5 mM β-glycerophosphate). The medium was changed every 2 − 3 days thereafter. After 7 days, Western blot was used to detect the expression of Type I collagen and Runx2 proteins.

Establishment of a rat calvarial defect model

Fifty male SD rats aged 8 weeks and weighing 250–300 g were selected and randomly divided into the HA, Tb-HA, Tb-CHA, CHA, and blank groups (n = 10). The rats were anesthetized before surgery, the hair on the skull area was shaved, and the skin was prepared. A 1.5-cm-long incision was made on the midline of the rat’s skull, the skin and subcutaneous tissue were bluntly separated to the sides, the periosteum was stripped, and an electric circular saw system was used for grinding. A physiological saline solution was sprayed throughout the operation for cooling. The full thickness of the skull was penetrated, and an 8-mm-diameter circular calvarial defect was prepared. The prepared scaffolds were implanted at the calvaria.

Result

Characterization of 3D-printed Tb-CHA scaffolds

Cylindrical scaffolds were fabricated via 3D printing to match bone defect geometry. After sintering at 600 °C, all scaffolds retained an interconnected, porous 3D network (Fig. 1A, B). Surface morphology, however, varied significantly between groups (Fig. 1C). Scaffolds of HA, Eu-HA, and Eu-CHA exhibited relatively smooth surfaces, while those of Tb-HA, CHA, and Tb-CHA displayed markedly rougher topography with a higher density of surface pores, which is beneficial for cell attachment and nutrient exchange. CHA scaffolds showed cubic crystals, whereas Tb-CHA scaffolds consisted of larger granular clusters.

3D Scaffold design, morphology, and elemental composition. (A) 3D scaffold model designed using Solidworks 2013 and Simplify 3D 4.0.1 software and digital photograph of the 3D printed scaffold. (B) SEM of the scaffold at low magnification. (C) SEM images of the scaffold after calcination. All scale bars represent 10 μm. (D) EDS mapping results of representative scaffolds: (a) CHA, (b) Eu-CHA, (c) Tb-CHA

EDS mapping (Fig. 1D) revealed distinct surface compositions. For CHA scaffolds, square crystalline regions rich in C and O but lacking P were identified as CaCO₃ phases, while Ca was uniformly distributed. In contrast, Eu-CHA scaffolds showed a homogeneous distribution of all elements (C, O, P, Ca, Eu) without distinct CaCO₃ phases. Tb-CHA scaffolds featured large (up to 10 μm) petal-like CaCO₃ structures, with Tb uniformly distributed throughout, confirming homogeneous doping. The Tb-CHA scaffolds successfully met the microstructural criteria for bone regeneration, possessing a 3D, open, and interconnected porous network.

Osteogenesis of 3D-printed Tb-CHA scaffolds

Cell adhesion, proliferation, and growth on scaffolds

Cell proliferation was assessed using the CCK-8 assay, with the results shown in Fig. 2(C). On day 1, the HA group showed the lowest optical density (OD) value. In contrast, the Tb-CHA group exhibited the highest OD value, surpassing even the Eu-HA and Eu-CHA groups, whose OD values were lower than their corresponding terbium-doped counterparts. On day 3, the Eu-HA group had the lowest OD value, while the Tb-CHA group had the highest, significantly outperforming the other groups. On day 5, the OD values of the Eu-HA and Eu-CHA groups were significantly lower than those of the CHA and Tb-CHA groups. The incorporation of terbium rare earth elements significantly promoted the proliferation of MC3T3-E1 cells, while the incorporation of europium rare earth elements partially inhibited cell proliferation. The pronounced pro-proliferative effect of Tb-CHA compared to Tb-HA can be attributed to the faster degradation rate of Tb-CHA, leading to increased release of terbium ions within a shorter timeframe, thereby promoting MC3T3-E1 cell proliferation.

(Scale bars: 10 um) (B) Viability of MC3T3-E1 cells seeding on the scaffolds after 7 days detected by Calcein-AM/PI staining. As shown in Fig. 2(A), cells adhered to all materials, and distinct morphologies were observed: a polygonal morphology on HA scaffolds, elongated spindle shapes on Eu-HA, CHA, and Eu-CHA scaffolds, and fully spread cells on Tb-HA and Tb-CHA scaffolds. These cell morphologies closely corresponded to the unique microstructural features of the scaffolds. Cell growth on rough surfaces with grooves and edges is markedly different from that on smooth surfaces, as the former facilitates pseudopodia attachment and enhances the interaction between cell layers and matrix materials. Furthermore, cells tended to orient and migrate along the protrusions on the Tb-CHA scaffold surface.

Cell growth activity in the various scaffold groups was further investigated using the Calcein-AM/PI dual staining reagent to assess the distribution of live and dead cells on different scaffold materials (Fig. 2B). On day 7, the Tb-HA, CHA, and Tb-CHA groups exhibited abundant live cells (green) with dense growth; in contrast, the Eu-HA and Eu-CHA groups showed fewer live cells, significantly fewer than the other four groups. The HA, Tb-HA, CHA, and Tb-CHA groups displayed scattered dead cells (red), with the HA group having the highest count. Smooth surfaces were less conducive to cell adhesion, as observed in the Eu-HA and Eu-CHA groups. Additionally, europium doping reduced cell proliferation, leading to a further decrease in cell number, a finding corroborated by the CCK-8 assay results. Therefore, the Tb-CHA scaffold is the most suitable candidate for cell regeneration.

Effect of Tb-CHA scaffold extracts on cell proliferation, morphology, and growth

The effect of scaffold extracts on cell proliferation was further evaluated using the CCK-8 assay. On day 1, the control group with DMEM-H medium (DMEM-H) showed the lowest OD value; in contrast, the Eu-HA group had the highest, with a significant difference (P < 0.05) between them (Fig. 3B). There was no significant difference in OD values among the Eu-HA, Tb-HA, CHA, Eu-CHA, and Tb-CHA groups. On day 3, the HA group showed the lowest OD value, while the Tb-CHA, CHA, and Eu-HA groups exhibited significantly higher OD values compared to the HA group (P < 0.01). By day 5, the OD values of the DMEM-H and Tb-CHA groups were the highest, significantly surpassing the other groups. The Tb-CHA group demonstrated the most significant effect in promoting cell proliferation, indicating excellent biocompatibility.

Cell morphology was assessed under a fluorescence microscope after 48 h of co-culture with various scaffold material extracts. Figure 3(A) shows that the cell morphology transitioned from elongated spindle to polygonal shape, with no significant differences among the groups. Actin, a component of the eukaryotic cell cytoskeleton, plays a pivotal role in regulating cell morphology, adhesion, migration, and cytokinesis. In vitro-cultured osteoblasts typically exhibit star-shaped or polygonal forms with several protrusions of varying lengths along their edges. Mechanical factors (such as compressive stress, tensile stress, fluid shear stress, etc.) can alter osteoblast morphology, thereby affecting their functions.

The Calcein-AM/PI dual staining reagent was used to assess the effect of various scaffold extract solutions on the growth of MC3T3-E1 cells. After changing the extraction medium and culturing for 3 days, Calcein-AM and PI were used to stain live and dead cells, respectively. Figure 3(C-D) shows that on day 3, the Eu-HA group exhibited a higher number of dead cells (red), while other groups had fewer and relatively dispersed dead cells. By day 7, no significant increase in dead cell count was observed in the other groups. On day 3, live cells (green) were relatively dispersed in all groups; by day 7, significant cell proliferation was observed in all groups, with cells growing tightly connected in sheets. Live cells are responsible for various physiological functions, and the addition of europium led to more cell death, indicating poorer cell biocompatibility compared to other groups. Rare earth elements exhibit a “low-promotion, high-inhibition” effect on cells, with 1% europium leading to more cell death, possibly due to the relatively high concentration.

ALP staining and quantitative analysis of Tb-CHA scaffold

Figure 4(A) presents the Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP) activity staining results after directly seeding MC3T3-E1 cells onto the scaffolds and inducing osteogenesis for 14 days. The results indicate scattered blue staining in all groups, with fewer blue-stained areas in the Eu-HA and Eu-CHA groups compared to other groups. The HA, Tb-HA, CHA, and Tb-CHA groups showed no significant differences in the area of blue staining. The relatively shallow staining areas and lower staining intensity compared to powder material extracts may be attributed to the relatively small number of cells adhering to the scaffolds.

ALP, an early marker of osteoblast differentiation, regulates osteoblast proliferation, migration, and differentiation. ALP activity is one of the standards for evaluating the osteogenic differentiation of MC3T3-E1 cells. Figure 4(A) shows the ALP staining results after 7 days of co-culture with various scaffold extract solutions. According to the results, by day 7, the Eu-HA and Eu-CHA groups exhibited less blue-stained ALP compared to other groups; in contrast, the HA, Tb-HA, CHA, and Tb-CHA groups showed no significant differences in ALP staining results. By day 14, the HA, Tb-HA, CHA, and Tb-CHA groups exhibited larger areas of deep blue staining, while the staining areas in the Eu-HA and Eu-CHA groups were reduced and displayed lighter colors compared to other groups. ALP activity is a good indicator of osteoblast differentiation and functional status, with higher activity indicating greater differentiation of pre-osteoblasts into mature osteoblasts that promote bone matrix mineralization.

Further quantitative analysis using an ALP assay kit showed the highest ALP activity in the Tb-CHA group on day 7, which was significantly higher than that in the Eu-HA, Tb-HA, CHA, and Eu-CHA groups. The Tb-HA group also showed higher ALP activity than the Eu-HA and Eu-CHA groups. By day 14, ALP activity increased in all groups compared to day 7, with the HA, Tb-HA, and Tb-CHA groups showing the most significant increases, and their ALP activity was higher than that of other groups. The ALP quantitative results were consistent with the ALP staining results, indicating that terbium enhanced the ALP activity of MC3T3-E1 cells, promoting their osteogenic differentiation and bone matrix formation.

Mineralization effect of 3D-printed Tb-CHA scaffolds

MC3T3-E1 cells were co-cultured with various scaffold extract solutions containing osteogenic induction components for 21 days, followed by Alizarin Red staining. Figure 4(B) shows large areas of red mineralized nodules in the Tb-CHA group, followed by the Tb-HA group. The DMEM-H, HA, and Eu-CHA groups showed approximately the same staining areas of mineralized nodules, but all were less than those in the Tb-CHA and Tb-HA groups. The Eu-HA and Eu-CHA groups showed the least staining areas of mineralized nodules, with only scattered small amounts of red staining. Alizarin Red is an anionic dye that chelates with calcium ions to form a deep red compound. Mineralized nodules are an important marker of the terminal stage of osteogenic differentiation and are a critical criterion for evaluating a material’s ability to promote osteogenic differentiation. These findings indicate that Tb-CHA significantly promoted the formation of extracellular calcium nodules by MC3T3-E1 cells in vitro.

Detection of osteogenic-specific protein expression on 3D-printed Tb-CHA scaffolds

Due to the lack of significant promotion of cell proliferation and osteogenic differentiation by europium doping in the above experimental results, the DMEM-H, HA, Tb-HA, CHA, and Tb-CHA groups were selected to evaluate their effects on the expression of osteogenic-specific proteins in MC3T3-E1 cells. As shown in Fig. 4(D), the Runx2 and type I collagen bands in the Tb-HA and Tb-CHA groups were significantly darker than those in the other groups, indicating that terbium facilitated the expression of Runx2 protein and type I collagen in MC3T3-E1 cells. Runx2, also known as cbfα-1, is a key transcription factor for osteoblast differentiation. It can bind to osteoblast-specific cis-acting elements to promote the transcription and translation of osteocalcin, osteopontin, bone sialoprotein, and type I collagen. Type I collagen is the main organic component in mature bone matrix and is primarily produced by osteoblasts. It contains the GDG sequence, which can bind to cell surface integrins and, in the presence of other proteins, participate in the mineralization process with calcium ions, making it an important indicator of osteogenesis. Therefore, terbium facilitated osteogenic differentiation and bone matrix formation in MC3T3-E1 cells.

In vivo osteogenesis promotion of 3D-printed Tb-CHA scaffolds

Dynamic micro-CT observation of 3D-printed Tb-CHA scaffold repair of skull defect models

Two, four, and six weeks after implantation, all the SD rat groups were euthanized using high-dose anesthesia, and dynamic Micro-CT scans were performed on skull specimens. The images were reconstructed in three dimensions using the Micro-CT software (Fig. 5A). From the radiographic perspective, 4 and 6 weeks after surgery, the Tb-CHA group exhibited significantly more new bone formation and volume around the bone defects compared to the HA group, with the blank control group showing only a small amount of new bone formation at the edges of the defect area. The defect area in the HA group decreased, with new bone growing inward around the periphery of the gap, suggesting that the Tb-CHA scaffold material exerted a superior reparative effect and promoted new bone formation.

HE staining detection of 3D printed Tb-CHA scaffold repair of skull defect model

HE tissue section staining was applied to observe the effects of scaffold implantation on bone tissue after implantation (Fig. 6A). No significant degradation of experimental materials was observed in the Tb-CHA scaffold group four weeks after surgery. Extracellular matrix formation was evident, with weak acidophilia of the extracellular matrix distributed around the scaffold and within the scaffold pores occupied by osteoblasts. Moreover, a network of intersecting fibrous connective tissues was observed within and around the scaffold. The Tb-CHA scaffold group exhibited more new bone tissue compared to the HA control group, in which a few inflammatory cells were still present at the defect site, and osteoblasts were observed around the material. However, the formation of a weak acidophilic extracellular matrix was not significant.

Six weeks after surgery, the interface between the Tb-CHA scaffold and the new tissue was significantly blurred, and extracellular matrix formation was evident within the scaffold, with noticeable mineralization of the extracellular matrix into the bone matrix. The newly formed mineralized tissue exhibited strong acidophilia, and its range was larger than that at 4 weeks (Fig. 6B). In the HA control group, no significant formation of new bone tissue was observed at the rat skull defect site, with only a large amount of fibrous tissue at the defect site. The increased density of osteoblast-like cells in the Tb-CHA group was more pronounced, and the degradation of the scaffold material was more evident in the peripheral and middle portions.

At the 8-week interval (Fig. 6C), degradation occurred in all implanted materials and within the scaffold materials, with most of the degraded areas replaced by newly formed bone. In the blank control group, no new bone formation was observed, except for the presence of new bone tissue at the bone ends, and the extension of new bone at the bone ends was greater than that at 4 weeks. The number of new bone formation areas in the HA group was relatively small, with the lowest degradation rate and almost no soft tissue or bone ingrowth within the material. Except for the elongated new bone tissue observed at the bottom of the HA material parallel to it, inflammatory soft tissue was still visible in other parts. In the Tb-HA group, noticeable phagocytic-like degradation lines were visible at the material’s edge, with new bone tissue surrounding the material on both sides, which was significantly wider compared to the 6-week interval, with less inflammatory tissue. Macroscopic observations revealed the highest degradation rate in the CHA group, with some parts completely surrounded by newly formed tissue, leaving no trace of the material and no inflammatory tissue around it, but less new bone tissue where the material was not completely degraded. In the Tb-CHA group, most of the material had degraded internally and was separated by newly formed soft and bone tissues, fully enveloped within them. Under low magnification, significant regression was observed at the interface between the implant material ends and the rat’s original bone ends, and the regression path was replaced by newly formed bone, with a significantly higher amount of new bone ingrowth compared to the other groups, indicating that the Tb-CHA group could induce more new bone formation.

Special staining and promotion of vascular regeneration in 3D-printed Tb-CHA scaffold repair of skull defect model

Masson’s trichrome staining, also known as Masson staining, distinguishes between muscle fibers and collagen fibers, with muscle fibers generally appearing red and collagen fibers appearing green or blue. Type I collagen fibers are important components of bone matrix and serve as templates for calcium salt deposition and mineralization. At the 6-week observation time point, large areas of red structures were observed in the HA group (Fig. 6D); a few light blue fibrous structures were interspersed in the gaps of the CHA group material, and some red muscle fiber bundles were still visible. The Tb-HA group showed a significant increase in blue collagen fiber content, with fewer red muscle fibers around the wrapped material. The Tb-CHA group exhibited the largest blue-stained area. These findings support the assertion that the Tb-CHA scaffold experimental group could better promote bone mineralization.

Vascular regeneration was also observed during the histological staining experiment of the implanted scaffolds in the rat body (Fig. 6D). At the 8-week interval, degradation began inside all scaffold materials, with the CHA group exhibiting significantly faster degradation than the other experimental groups. At 6 weeks, the edge of the CHA group material had significantly receded toward the scaffold center, and the regressing path was filled with newly formed connective tissue, with significantly more new blood vessels than in the other groups. At the 8-week interval, most of the implanted material in the CHA group had degraded and was separated by newly formed soft tissue, with less new bone tissue than in the other experimental groups. According to microscopic observations, sections around the wrapped material were filled with new blood vessels, with a significantly higher number than in the other groups, indicating more new blood vessel formation in the CHA group.

Discussion

The development of advanced bone graft materials that combine enhanced biological functionality with structural adaptability represents a critical direction in bone tissue engineering [21–23]. In this study, we designed a novel biomaterial through the incorporation of Tb³⁺ into CHA, leveraging the favorable properties of CHA—including high crystallinity, porosity, protein affinity, tunable mechanical properties, and similarity to native bone mineral. We hypothesized that Tb³⁺ doping would further augment the osteogenic performance and biocompatibility of CHA. Our comprehensive in vitro and in vivo results strongly support this hypothesis, underscoring the potential of Tb-CHA as a highly promising material for bone regeneration.

Systematic in vitro evaluation using six scaffold variants—HA, Eu-HA, Tb-HA, CHA, Eu-CHA, and Tb-CHA—revealed that Tb-CHA significantly promoted the adhesion, proliferation, and osteogenic differentiation of MC3T3-E1 pre-osteoblasts. Key markers such as ALP)activity, mineralization capacity, and the expression of osteogenesis-related proteins consistently indicated a pronounced pro-osteogenic effect of Tb-CHA. Notably, europium doping negatively impacted cellular proliferation, adhesion, and differentiation, highlighting the element-specific bioactivity of rare-earth dopants and underscoring the unique benefit of terbium in hydroxyapatite-based scaffolds.

These findings were further corroborated in a rat calvarial defect model. Micro-computed tomography and histomorphometric analyses—via H&E and Masson’s trichrome staining—demonstrated markedly enhanced new bone formation and mineralization in defects implanted with Tb-CHA scaffolds, outperforming both pure HA and CHA groups. Carbonate substitution was observed to facilitate vascular ingrowth, while Tb³⁺ doping significantly boosted osteogenic capacity and attenuated the local inflammatory response [17]. The synergistic effect between carbonate incorporation and Tb³⁺ doping appears to substantially enhance the bioactivity and bone regenerative efficacy of the scaffold, aligning with and extending our in vitro observations.

Despite these promising outcomes, several questions remain open. The precise molecular mechanisms through which Tb³⁺ modulates osteogenic signaling pathways have yet to be fully elucidated. Future work should focus on deciphering these mechanisms, optimizing the carbonate substitution ratio for specific clinical scenarios, and evaluating the long-term safety, biodegradation profile, and immunogenicity of Tb-CHA scaffolds.

In conclusion, this study establishes Tb-CHA as a composite material that successfully integrates favorable physicochemical properties with enhanced biological functionality, providing a robust and scalable platform for bone tissue engineering. Consistent positive outcomes across cellular and animal models confirm our initial hypothesis and lay a solid groundwork for future translational studies aimed at personalized bone repair strategies.

Conclusion

Investigations into the morphology, proliferation, and osteogenic differentiation of MC3T3-E1 cells, systematic evaluations of osteogenic performance using ALP staining and quantification, Alizarin Red staining, and the expression of osteogenic-related proteins showed that Tb-CHA has excellent osteogenic differentiation capabilities. Differences in the osteogenic performance of hydroxyapatite scaffolds with carbonate substitution and Tb doping have been revealed at the animal level. Toxicological studies on tissues such as the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys have confirmed that Tb doping has no toxic side effects in SD rats. Both carbonate substitution and Tb doping enhanced the osteogenic effect of hydroxyapatite scaffolds, with Tb-doped carbonate-substituted hydroxyapatite achieving the best osteogenic performance. In summary, this scaffold may hold great potential for significantly improving performance in artificial bone repair scaffolds for practical clinical applications.
