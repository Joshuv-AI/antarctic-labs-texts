A Retrospective Analysis Examining the Impact of Educational Attainment and Social Determinants of Health on Rifampicin-Resistant Tuberculosis Treatment Outcomes
Authors: Titilade Kehinde Ayandeyi Teibo, Rubeshan Perumal, Lateef Babatunde Amusa, Letitia Shunmugam, Nonkululeko Mzinyane, Darshan Chetty, Resha Boodhram, Radhamoney Narasimmulu, Takondwa Kachoka, Claudia Shozi, Max R O’Donnell, Allison Wolf, Sunitha Chotoo, Nalini Singh, Ricardo Alexandre Arcêncio, Nesri Padayatchi, Kogieleum Naidoo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRifampicin-resistant tuberculosis (RR-TB) remains a major public health challenge in TB-endemic regions. While social determinants of health affect infectious disease outcomes, their impact on RR-TB remains poorly understood. This study evaluated the relationship between educational attainment, social disadvantage, and RR-TB outcomes.MethodWe conducted a retrospective cohort study using data from 4 Centre for the AIDS Programme of Research in South Africa RR-TB clinical trials. Social vulnerability was measured using an exploratory Social Disadvantage Score (SDS) that incorporated education, employment, and incarceration. We used a latent class analysis (LCA) to identify classes with shared social vulnerability indicators. Associations with treatment outcomes were assessed using Cox regression models, with the exploratory SDS, its component indicators, and derived latent classes as predictors.ResultsEducation was not independently associated with unfavorable treatment outcomes (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] = 1.39, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.96–1.99, P = .077), whereas a history of incarceration doubled the hazard (aHR = 2.05, 95% CI: 1.33–3.16, P < .001). Restricting subsequent models to the subcohort with collected employment data, unemployment increased the hazard of unfavorable outcomes (aHR = 1.84, 95% CI: 1.20–2.82, P = .005). Using the same subcohort, increasing cumulative social disadvantage showed a strong dose–response relationship, with a 5-fold higher hazard for 3 disadvantages (aHR = 5.85, 95% CI: 2.73–12.56, P < .001). Latent class analysis revealed that Class 2 (moderate socioeconomic advantage) had a significantly higher hazard of unfavorable outcomes (aHR = 1.82, 95% CI: 1.19–2.79, P = .006).ConclusionsCumulative social risk score was associated with unfavorable RR-TB outcomes, supporting further evaluation of targeted support for disadvantaged populations.

Tuberculosis (TB) is a preventable and curable airborne disease, yet it remains the leading cause of death from a single infectious agent [1]. In 2023, the World Health Organization estimated that there were 10.8 million new TB cases and 1.25 million TB deaths globally, with rifampicin-resistant tuberculosis (RR-TB) affecting approximately 400 000 patients and diagnosed in approximately 3.3% of new notified TB cases and 17% of previously treated cases [2]. In 2022, Africa accounted for 24% of all TB cases notified worldwide, with South Africa accounting for approximately 2.5% of global TB and 3.3% of RR-TB cases [3]. Moreover, an estimated 70% of multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB) cases in South Africa are coinfected with Human Immunodeficiency Virus-1 (HIV), complicating treatment as antiretroviral therapy (ART) and antituberculosis treatment (ATT) can lead to drug–drug interactions, which complicate treatment regimens and increase the risk of adverse events [4]. Antiretroviral therapy and ATT regimens also have overlapping toxicity profiles, and their concomitant use may increase the risk of adverse events, such as hepatotoxicity and QT prolongation. The combined treatment of these 2 overlapping diseases, HIV and RR-TB, also increases the pill burden to patients. Furthermore, the initiation of ART can trigger immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome, which may worsen HIV progression and increase resistance, thus increasing mortality.

Rifampicin-resistant tuberculosis presents a significant challenge to TB control, as its diagnosis and treatment are significantly more expensive compared to drug-susceptible TB (DS-TB), placing a substantial financial burden on healthcare systems and society, especially in disease-endemic settings [5, 6]. Although efficient diagnostics and effective treatment options for RR-TB have been implemented to scale in settings, such as South Africa, achievement of optimal patient and programmatic outcomes remains elusive, largely due to social and economic determinants that heighten vulnerability to infection and poor outcomes [7, 8]. Furthermore, RR-TB was reported to have a lower treatment success rate (71%) compared to DS-TB (88%) [9]. Published literature has highlighted the role of poverty-related social vulnerabilities, such as unemployment, food insecurity, poor housing conditions, and limited education, in significantly hindering timely diagnosis, engagement in care, treatment uptake, and adherence [10, 11]. Other socioeconomic factors that influence RR-TB treatment outcomes include education level, income, and housing [12–14]. These factors directly impact the patient's ability to access and engage with health services, seek a timely diagnosis, adhere to treatment regimens, and follow-up with necessary care.

Latent class analysis (LCA) is a statistical method that categorizes individuals into distinct, nonoverlapping subgroups or “classes” based on shared characteristics, helping to uncover heterogeneity within populations, particularly relevant for analyzing complex health outcomes [15]. Latent class analysis identifies unnoticed (latent) subgroups within a population based on patterns in observed data and can distinguish distinct patient profiles based on combinations of social class indicators, educational attainment, and clinical characteristics [16]. Latent class analysis has facilitated the identification of vulnerable patient subgroups within diverse socioeconomic contexts and has been successfully applied in other RR-TB studies. It was used to identify classes that significantly predicted sputum smear conversion and treatment success, outperforming traditional phenotypic drug susceptibility testing [17]. Latent class analysis has also been used to identify adherence trajectories, challenging conventional adherence metrics [18]. Applying LCA could help identify high-risk socioeconomic subgroups facing heightened barriers to care. Identifying these distinct profiles may enable the development of more targeted interventions, with different treatment support strategies tailored to specific patient profiles, ultimately improving treatment adherence, clinical outcomes, and overall public health impact [19, 20].

The role of individual socioeconomic determinants of health in RR-TB outcomes is well documented in South Africa, where poverty, unemployment, food insecurity and malnutrition, and inadequate healthcare access significantly influence treatment success [21]. However, the evidence regarding the cumulative and independent impact of these determinants on RR-TB treatment outcomes is less consistent and more limited [11]. While the effect of novel biomedical approaches on RR-TB outcomes has been extensively researched in settings, such as South Africa [22, 23], there remains a gap in understanding the complex interplay of the socioeconomic factors influencing RR-TB treatment outcomes. This retrospective cohort analysis assessed the relationship between educational attainment and social determinants of health with RR-TB treatment outcomes. The findings will provide insights to guide targeted interventions, address social inequities, and improve treatment success rates, ultimately contributing to more equitable TB care and control.

METHODS

Study Design and Study Population

We performed a retrospective cohort analysis of people with newly diagnosed RR-TB who had previously enrolled in clinical trials (PROX, PRAXIS, InDEX, and TRiAD) conducted between 2009 and 2024 at the Centre for the AIDS Programme of Research in South Africa (CAPRISA) Springfield clinical research site. All these studies were prospective cohort trials involving adults aged 18 years and older with RR-TB and/or HIV coinfection. The PROX study was a prospective observational cohort study conducted to evaluate treatment response patterns in people with RR-TB and HIV coinfection, with a focus on improving understanding of disease progression and treatment monitoring within high HIV/TB burden settings. The PRAXIS studies were prospective interventional studies involving adults with RR-TB, MDR-TB, and extensively drug-resistant tuberculosis (XDR-TB), investigating novel approaches for the diagnosis and management of drug-resistant TB. The InDEX study was a randomized controlled trial evaluating individualized treatment strategies for people with MDR- and XDR-TB to assess treatment response, resistance patterns, and biomarkers associated with disease severity and outcomes. The TRiAD study was a phase IV observational study evaluating the implementation and effectiveness of the Xpert MTB/XDR assay to support rapid diagnosis and initiation of individualized all-oral RR-TB treatment regimens. The 4 contributing studies, including study design, eligibility criteria, and study procedures, have been described elsewhere [24–27]. All cohorts represented relatively unselected RR-TB populations from the same setting over the last 15 years. Participants enrolled in all the trials were managed within the national TB program at public health facilities [28–30].

Study Procedures and Data Collection

Data on sociodemographic and clinical characteristics, including age, gender, incarceration history, illicit drug use, housing status, employment, educational level, microbiological TB findings, chest x-rays, HIV status, and viral load suppression, were extracted from clinical files and study databases and then consolidated for analysis. Data were censored at RR-TB treatment outcome or at 24 months of follow-up. Clinical and laboratory monitoring of study participants is published elsewhere [24–27]. Drug-resistant tuberculosis definitions and outcomes were based on prevailing guidelines [28–30]. Participants with incomplete or missing data were excluded from the study to ensure the reliability and integrity of the dataset. Additionally, participants were excluded if they had no known RR-TB treatment outcomes. Educational attainment was described using the South African Department of Education definitions, whereby primary school education was defined by having completed grade 7 in a South African school, secondary education meant having a national senior certificate, National Qualifications Framework (NQF) level 4 or equivalent, and Tertiary education meant qualifying with NQF level 5 or above from an accredited higher education institution that is recognized by the South African Qualifications Authority.

Statistical Analysis

The demographic and clinical characteristics of participants were compared across educational attainment categories. Numerical or continuous variables were reported as median (interquartile range, Q1–Q3) and analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables were reported as n (%) and compared using Pearson's χ² test.

Because social risks often operate together rather than in isolation, an exploratory Social Disadvantage Score (SDS) was used to assess cumulative social risk. Social Disadvantage Score is a composite index based on 3 indicators: unemployment, history of incarceration, and education below secondary level. By assigning a score of 1 to each of the 3 indicators, the exploratory SDS was constructed as an unweighted sum, yielding a score from 0 to 3 (higher scores indicating greater vulnerability). We used equal weights to improve transparency and avoid subjective weighting in the absence of strong evidence to justify differential contributions of the component indicators, consistent with prior cumulative social risk approaches that assign a single point to each risk factor [31–35]. Kaplan–Meier curves were stratified by relevant risk factors for unfavorable treatment outcomes, and between-strata tests were assessed using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate the association between social characteristics and treatment outcomes, with estimated hazard ratios (HRs) adjusted for clinically and empirically relevant baseline variables. We assessed the proportional hazards assumption using Schoenfeld residuals and found no violations. The composite unfavorable outcome was defined as a binary indicator comprising death, treatment failure, or loss to follow-up. Patients achieving treatment success (cure or completion) were censored on the date of the outcome. Those remaining in care without an event by the study's conclusion were administratively censored at 24 months.

Potential covariates included demographic factors (age, gender, education, housing, employment), behavioral history (smoking, illicit drug use, alcohol, incarceration), and clinical markers (HIV/ART status, CD4 counts, viral load). We developed Cox proportional hazards models: an unadjusted model for exploratory purposes and a primary model that adjusted for age, gender, and HIV/ART status, plus any covariates identified as empirically relevant (P < .10) in the crude analysis. A separate Cox proportional hazards model was used to assess the association between SDS and time to unfavorable treatment outcomes, adjusting for age, sex, and HIV/ART status. Notably, CD4 counts and viral load were excluded from multivariable adjustments due to multicollinearity with HIV/ART status, which was confirmed via variance inflation factor diagnostics. To address potential confounding from heterogeneity across trials and calendar periods, our Cox models were stratified by trial cohort and adjusted for enrollment calendar time. This directly accounted for differences in eligibility, follow-up intensity, and selection effects across cohorts. Calendar time was modeled as a continuous linear variable; testing for nonlinear effects using quadratic terms and restricted cubic splines showed no significant improvement in model fit (P > .05).

Some variables with a high proportion of missingness, which include viral load, CD4 count, substance use, and employment status, were structurally missing, either due to (1) study-specific design limitations: variable not collected for a particular study (substance abuse for TRiAD and employment status for InDEX), or (2) selective data collection: data collection restricted to HIV-positive patients (viral load and CD4 count). These variables were consequently excluded in the confounder-adjusted models to preserve the integrity and robustness of the analysis. We excluded the InDEX study data from the exploratory SDSs because employment status, a key component indicator, was not collected. Given the low (<10%) proportion of random missingness in key variables and the systemic mechanisms underlying missingness in other variables, we used a complete-case analysis to address missing data in this study.

Latent class analysis was performed using social vulnerability indicators to identify subgroups characterized by shared social, structural, economic, and behavioral traits, including primary or no schooling, previously incarcerated, informal or no housing, smoker, unemployed, and alcohol drinker. To refine this set, we applied empirical criteria: Variables with limited variability were excluded because they lacked the capacity to discriminate between latent classes. Models with 1–5 classes were tested. The final model was selected by balancing statistical fit (using the Bayesian information criterion [BIC] and entropy), clinical interpretability, and class size adequacy. Participants were assigned to the class with the highest membership probability. We applied a pragmatic requirement that each class contain at least 10% of the total population, ensuring sufficient statistical power for downstream analyses and preventing overstratification. Missing data in class indicators were handled using complete-case analysis, except for employment status and substance abuse, for which “not collected” served as the missingness indicator and was treated as a separate category. To ensure identification of the global maximum and avoid local maxima, we used 100 random starts. The models were run until full convergence was achieved. The LCA class membership was further introduced as a predictor variable in a Cox regression to examine the association between class memberships with unfavorable treatment outcomes, adjusting for age, gender, and HIV status.

Latent class analysis models were fitted using the poLCA package (v1.6.0.1; Linzer & Lewis, 2011). Statistical significance was defined as a P value of <.05, and all analyses were performed in R (version 4.5.0; R Core Team 2025).

Ethical Considerations

The University of KwaZulu-Natal Biomedical Research Ethics Committee (BREC) provided ethical review and approval for all clinical trials used in this analysis (PROX, BF005/09; PRAXIS, BE242/16 and BFC613/17; InDEX, BFC584/16; and TRiAD, BREC/00002654/2021). Only clinical trials were registered with Clinicaltrials.gov (PRAXIS, NCT04032730; InDEX, NCT03237182; and TRiAD, NCT05175794).

RESULTS

Participant files of 939 RR-TB patients were reviewed; 6 were excluded due to pending treatment outcome status at 24 months, 34 were excluded for missing treatment outcome data, and 2 participants were excluded for missing educational attainment information. A total of 897 participants were included in this analysis (Figure 1), with 83.1% (745/897) having attained at least secondary school education. Among participants included in the analysis, 50% (448/897) were male, 25% (185/897) were employed, and 12% (109/897) had a history of incarceration. The proportion of participants living with HIV was 85% (765/897), with 88% (669/765) on ART, and 50% (233/466) virally suppressed. Highly educated individuals were more likely to be female (P < .001), younger (P < .001), and employed (P = .004) and had a lower prevalence of incarceration (P < .001) (Table 1).

Kaplan–Meier estimates of the probability of a favorable treatment outcome over time differed significantly by educational attainment (log-rank P = .007). Participants with a secondary school qualification or higher had a probability of 91.2%, 80.5%, and 70.8% of favorable outcomes at days 200, 400, and 600, respectively. As shown in Table 3, the hazard of unfavorable treatment outcomes declined by 9% for each additional treatment year (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] = 0.91, 95% CI: 0.88–0.94, P < .001). Educational attainment was not independently associated with unfavorable outcomes after adjustment (primary or no schooling: aHR = 1.39, 95% CI: 0.96–1.99, P = .077). A history of incarceration remained a significant predictor, with individuals having more than twice the hazard of unfavorable outcomes compared with those without a history of incarceration (aHR = 2.05, 95% CI: 1.33–3.16, P = .001). Among people who are HIV positive, those not receiving antiretroviral therapy had approximately twice the hazard of unfavorable outcomes compared with those receiving ART (aHR = 2.03, 95% CI: 1.11–3.69, P = .021).

For models restricted to the subcohort with collected employment data, increasing social disadvantage demonstrated a strong dose–response relationship with unfavorable outcomes, with the hazard increasing progressively from 1 disadvantage (aHR = 1.63, 95% CI: 1.03–2.58, P = .039) to 3 disadvantages (aHR = 5.85, 95% CI: 2.73–12.56, P < .001), compared with participants reporting no disadvantages. Unemployment was associated with an 84% higher hazard of unfavorable outcomes (aHR = 1.84, 95% CI: 1.20–2.82, P = .005). To ensure model stability, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by running the study-stratified Cox model for all covariates, excluding the InDEX cohort entirely. The HRs for the remaining social characteristics remained robust and stable, proving that the exclusion of this cohort did not skew our primary conclusions (Supplementary Table 1).

Model fit statistics for latent class models with 1–5 classes are presented in Table 4. Latent class analysis identified a 3-class model as the best-fitting solution, based on model fit indices, including the lowest BIC, an entropy value of 0.72, and substantive interpretability. Class 1, comprising 33.4% of the cohort, can be labeled as socioeconomically advantaged but high clinical and behavioral risk. It was characterized by a predominance of females (68.7%), slightly more than half being aged ≤ 35 years (51.0%), and all of them having attained at least a matric certificate. This group also exhibited high levels of HIV positivity (95.3%) and very high alcohol use (97.0%), alongside moderate levels of smoking (57.0%) and illicit drug use (18.2%). Notably, no individuals in this class reported any history of incarceration. Employment was relatively more common in this class (29.0%) compared with the other classes (Table 5).

Class 2, characterized by moderate socioeconomic advantage and low behavioral risk profile, accounted for 48.4% of the population. Specifically, it had the lowest prevalence of risk behaviors across all groups, including smoking (17.9%), alcohol use (1.1%), and illicit drug use (2.7%). Further, educational attainment was moderately high (80.0% having at least a matric education), and 85.1% had HIV. A small proportion reported a history of incarceration (1.4%), and informal housing was more common in this group (17.2%) than in Class 1.

Class 3 (18.2% of the population) can be labeled as low-resource (education/employment) and high behavioral risk. Predominantly male (87.2%) and aged over 35 (63.4%), this group had the lowest educational attainment (40.9% reported only primary education or no schooling). They exhibited the highest prevalence of adverse social and behavioral indicators, including a history of incarceration (62.8%), alcohol use (100%), smoking prevalence (93.3%), and illicit drug use (34.7%). HIV prevalence was lowest in this class relative to the other groups (67.7%). Additionally, 19.4% were employed, and 12.8% lived in informal housing.

Kaplan–Meier survival curves for favorable treatment outcomes, stratified by latent class membership (Figure 4), showed a significant difference in the probabilities of favorable outcomes over time (log-rank P = .009). A Cox proportional hazards model (Table 6) demonstrated that latent class membership was independently associated with the RR-TB treatment outcomes. Class 2 experienced a significantly higher hazard of unfavorable outcomes in both the unadjusted (HR = 1.72; 95% CI: 1.13–2.61; P = .011) and adjusted analyses (aHR = 1.75; 95% CI: 1.15–2.68; P = .009), indicating a consistently elevated hazard relative to Class 1, whereas Class 3 did not differ significantly from Class 1 in either the crude or adjusted analyses.
