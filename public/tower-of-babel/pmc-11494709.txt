Development and validation of a chronic kidney disease progression model using patient-level simulations
Authors: Mafalda Ramos, Laetitia Gerlier, Anastasia Uster, Louise Muttram, Dominik Steubl, Andrew H Frankel, Mark Lamotte
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic disease progression models are available for several highly prevalent conditions. For chronic kidney disease (CKD), the scope of existing progression models is limited to the risk of kidney failure and major cardiovascular (CV) events. The aim of this project was to develop a comprehensive CKD progression model (CKD-PM) that simulates the risk of CKD progression and a broad range of complications in patients with CKD. A series of literature reviews informed the selection of risk factors and identified existing risk equations/algorithms for kidney replacement therapy (KRT), CV events, other CKD-related complications, and mortality. Risk equations and transition probabilities were primarily sourced from publications produced by large US and international CKD registries. A patient-level, state-transition model was developed with health states defined by the Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes categories. Model validation was performed by comparing predicted outcomes with observed outcomes in the source cohorts used in model development (internal validation) and other cohorts (external validation). The CKD-PM demonstrated satisfactory modeling properties. Accurate prediction of all-cause and CV mortality was achieved without calibration, while prediction of CV events through CKD-specific equations required implementation of a calibration factor to balance time-dependent versus baseline risk. Predicted annual changes in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and urine albumin-creatinine ratio were acceptable in comparison to external values. A flexible eGFR threshold for KRT equations enabled accurate prediction of these events. This CKD-PM demonstrated reliable modeling properties. Both internal and external validation revealed robust outcomes.Keywords: Chronic kidney disease, disease progression model, microsimulation, validation

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a progressive disease of diverse etiologies characterized by gradual loss of kidney function, high morbidity burden, and risk of progression to kidney failure (previously referred to as end-stage kidney disease [ESKD]), necessitating kidney replacement therapy (KRT) in the form of dialysis or kidney transplantation [1]. Known risk factors for CKD onset and progression include advanced age, sex, inherited disorders, and a history of cardiovascular disease (CVD), hypertension, heart failure, diabetes, obesity, dyslipidemia, and smoking [2]. Markers of abnormal kidney function (decreased glomerular filtration rate) and kidney damage (albuminuria) are commonly used for CKD diagnosis and monitoring of progression over time [2]. To add to the complexity of the disease, comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes, heart failure, and CVD are significantly more likely to occur or worsen in the presence of CKD [3–5]. Increasing morbidity burden and heightened mortality risk are observed as CKD progresses to more advanced stages [6].

Disease progression models have been developed for several chronic conditions such as diabetes [7–11], metabolic syndrome [12], and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [13], as well as CKD [14–19]. Disease progression models use mathematical functions to describe quantitatively the time course of a specific condition, synthesizing data from multiple sources, including the results of risk prediction studies [20]. Longitudinal changes in biomarkers and risk factors are usually incorporated to predict the risk of complications over time [21]. A recent systematic review of existing CKD models that predict disease progression based on albuminuria (urine albumin-creatinine ratio [uACR]) or estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) decline concluded that the various modeling approaches failed to take sufficient account of patient heterogeneity and the need for extensive, high-quality data inputs [18]. Published progression models for CKD have predominantly focused on CV events and kidney failure outcomes [17,18], omitting to take into consideration other common CKD complications such as bone and mineral disorders [22] and anemia [23], and have provided limited representation of overall morbidity burden. The aim of the present study was to develop a comprehensive CKD progression model (CKD-PM), using CKD-specific algorithms to simulate CKD progression and provide reliable predictions for a broad range of CKD-related complications. A disease progression model for CKD may serve as a valuable tool for health technology assessments, healthcare resource management, and clinical decision making, and help inform the design of future clinical trials in CKD.

Methods

Patient-level microsimulation model

CKD-PM design, assumptions, and inputs were informed by findings from targeted literature searches. Key words and combinations of key words with appropriate Boolean operators were used to identify publications that provided information on the evolution of CKD risk factors over time, the risk of CKD-related complications and comorbidities, and the relationship between CKD-related complications and CKD progression. In addition, the websites of major CKD research groups identified in the literature search were screened for relevant information [24,25]. A total of 1062 publication titles were identified, from which 270 full-length articles and conference posters were selected after abstract and text review for data extraction. The key articles that informed the development of the CKD-PM are listed in Table S1. Information about the evolution of risk factors, as well as data and algorithms to predict the risk of complications and other outcomes, were sourced primarily from publications of the Chronic Kidney Disease Prognosis Consortium (CKD-PC) and the Chronic Renal Insufficiency Cohort (CRIC) research groups. The publications used to derive the risk of CKD-related complications over time are listed in Table S2.

A Markov state-transition microsimulation (patient-level) model, consisting of a discrete set of mutually exclusive health states, was constructed to characterize CKD progression over time. Often, this modeling approach involves the application of a transition matrix, which defines the probability of moving between each health state within a defined time period (cycle) [26]. For the purposes of CKD progression modeling, health state transitions were determined from predictions of eGFR and uACR values, using the Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) categories matrix [2]. The microsimulation approach allows the flexibility to study heterogeneous patient populations and to model a large number of complications, and is appropriate in settings such as CKD where the transition between health states depends on the patient’s history, baseline characteristics, occurrence of previous events, and evolution of risk factors over time.

The CKD-PM comprised 18 health states (and a ‘dead’ state), which were defined in terms of KDIGO CKD categories based on discrete eGFR and uACR thresholds [2]. According to the KDIGO classification, eGFR (ml/min/1.73 m2) is distributed into 6 categories: >90 (G1), >60 to ≤90 (G2), >45 to ≤60 (G3a), >30 to ≤45 (G3b), 15 to ≤30 (G4), and ≤15 (G5); and uACR (mg/g) is distributed into 3 categories: ≤30 (A1), >30 to ≤300 (A2), and >300 (A3). Change in eGFR and uACR in each cycle was programmed using published longitudinal data from CKD disease registries. A total of 10 submodules reflecting CKD complications and comorbidities, comprising death, hospitalization, ESKD (defined in our model as reaching G5 stage and/or initiating KRT), diabetes, hypertension, infection, CVD, bone and mineral disorders, and ‘other CKD complications’, were incorporated into the model structure. Modeling of risk factors

Both time-dependent and time-independent risk factors were entered as baseline characteristics into the CKD-PM (Table 1). Time-dependent risk factors comprised age, eGFR, uACR, body mass index (BMI), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein-cholesterol (HDL-cholesterol), systolic blood pressure (SBP), and comorbidities (diabetes, CVD, hypertension, heart failure, metabolic acidosis, schizophrenia/bipolar disorder, polycystic ovary syndrome, and learning disability). Time-independent risk factors were sex, race, current smoking status, family history of diabetes, history of comorbidities not covered as model events but as baseline characteristics, and background medication (antihypertensives, statins, atypical antipsychotics, and corticosteroids). Risk was converted to an event rate before applying hazard ratios, and then converted back to a probability using an established formula [27]. Seeding of random numbers generation was used to ensure that a given scenario yielded the same numerical results.

eGFR and uACR evolution

CKD progression was described in terms of the behavior of eGFR and uACR. Mean annual change in eGFR was estimated as a function of KDIGO category and diabetes status, using CRIC study outputs (Table 2), or as random changes in the modeled cohort based on data from the CKD-PC registry [28]. The variation of uACR over time was defined as random fold changes observed in the CKD-PC cohorts [29]. For the current cycle, eGFR and uACR values were updated by applying changes to the previous cycle values—additive in the case of eGFR, and multiplicative in the case of uACR. After initiation of renal dialysis, eGFR and uACR values were assumed to be constant. Following successful kidney transplantation, patients were assumed to move to the G3a-A1 health state, from where they could transition across eGFR- and uACR-defined KDIGO categories.

Risk of diabetes

The diabetes status of individuals with normal glucose tolerance (NGT) and prediabetes was re-assessed at each cycle in accordance with the updated QDiabetes-2018 prediction algorithm, which estimates the 10-year risk of developing diabetes as a function of age, sex, BMI, CVD history, current smoking status, family history of diabetes, and HbA1c [30]. For both the NGT and prediabetes subgroups, the evolution of HbA1c was modeled according to the Framingham Offspring Study and the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 2001–2004 [31]. The prevalence of controlled/uncontrolled hypertension at a given eGFR level was sourced from the NephroTest cohort study [32]. Progression of other time-dependent factors was modeled according to the Framingham Heart Study risk engines for total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein-cholesterol (HDL-C), and SBP [33]. For patients with diabetes, predictions of BMI and HbA1c evolution were based on the United Kingdom Prospective Diabetes Study (UKPDS) 90 [34]. For individuals without diabetes, BMI was assumed to progress following a constant increase in body weight of 0.296 kg/year [35] until the age of 66 years, after which a gradual decline in body weight occurs [36]. For those with NGT and prediabetes, HbA1c progression was programmed using data derived from the Framingham Offspring Study and the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 2001–2004 [31].

Risk of mortality

Mortality was modeled either as all-cause mortality or as its separate components of CV mortality, kidney-failure-related mortality (i.e., death due to any cause [including CV] after initiation of KRT), and death due to all other causes.

Risk of CV mortality over time was predicted using the Matsushita 2019 ‘CKD-Patch’ engine for low-risk countries [37], which takes into account the evolution of eGFR and uACR, added to the original SCORE risk engine [38]. The SCORE equation is a CV prediction model derived from 4 US community-based cohort studies that are included in the CKD-PC registry (ARIC, MESA, NHANES, and NHANES III) [38]. Life tables for general mortality by age and sex, and death counts by underlying cause were sourced from the UK Office for National Statistics (UK ONS) [39]. Kidney failure-related mortality data were derived from the UK Renal Registry (UKRR) report for 2020 [40]. To determine the risk of death due to all other causes, general mortality rates were adjusted to exclude deaths due to CV and kidney disease. The alternative approach used general mortality by age and sex, adjusting the risk based on eGFR and uACR classes as reported by Matsushita et al. [41].

Risk of CV events

The risk of CV outcomes in the model was estimated using the ‘CKD Patch’ engines for atherosclerotic CVD (ASCVD; fatal and nonfatal myocardial infarction and stroke) derived from CKD-PC registries. This is a CKD-enhanced version of the Pooled Cohort Equation (PCE) risk engines [38,42], and includes eGFR and uACR as predictors in addition to the PCE predictors of age, sex, race, TC, HDL-cholesterol, SBP, current smoking status, diabetes, and antihypertensive medication use (Table S3). ASCVD risk was adjusted to remove fatal events as these were already considered in the mortality submodule. The predicted nonfatal ASCVD risk was extrapolated to include transient ischemic attack and unstable angina as well, as they were not included in the original ASCVD definition [43]; the extrapolation was based on the risk distribution of events observed in the Framingham population [44,45]. Recurrent myocardial infarction and stroke events were predicted using the Framingham recurrent event equation [44,46]. Risk of hospitalization for heart failure was modeled using 5-year predictions stratified by KDIGO class and diabetes based on CRIC data [47]. Peripheral arterial disease risk was derived by multiplying local peripheral arterial disease event rates in the general population by the corresponding hazard ratios to obtain the differential risk according to KDIGO category of CKD [48,49].

Risk of other complications

The risk of other CKD complications, stratified by eGFR level, was determined from large, international studies. Incidence rates per patient-year were used to predict first and recurrent acute kidney injury (AKI) [50,51], the occurrence of infections [52] and bone fractures (including hip fractures) [22], as well as the risk of renal cell carcinoma and urothelial cancer in the nonfunctioning kidney [53]. Prevalence-based estimates were used to predict the risk of development of CKD-related bone and mineral disorders (hypocalcemia [54], hyperphosphatemia [55], and secondary hyperparathyroidism), anemia [23,55], metabolic acidosis, hyperkalemia, and hyperuricemia/gout [56].

Risk of ESKD

ESKD was defined as reaching the G5 stage and/or initiating KRT. For patients reaching G5 stage (eGFR <15 ml/min/1.73 m2), treatment options were either to initiate KRT with hemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis or, for patients <80 years of age, to undertake kidney transplantation or to follow a conservative approach. To determine whether patients would undergo KRT, a 6-variable risk equation for KRT [57], including age, sex, diabetes, hypertension, eGFR, and uACR as independent predictors, was used for base-case settings in each cycle. The following KRT-specific complications were modeled: peritonitis during peritoneal dialysis [58]; bloodstream infection [59] or arteriovenous access thrombosis during hemodialysis [60]; and kidney transplant rejection. Patient transitions between the 3 types of KRT were informed by the UKRR report for 2020 [40]. It was assumed that eGFR remained constant during dialysis and that eGFR and uACR values returned to levels corresponding to G3aA1 (eGFR 45–60 ml/min/1.73 m2; uACR 0-30 mg/g) following kidney transplantation [61,62]

Model outputs

Outputs of the CKD-PM included: time (years) in each health state; remaining life expectancy; the proportion of patients alive at the end of the modeling horizon; distribution of causes of death; incidences of CV events, AKI, bone, and mineral disorders, cancer, hospitalization, and infection, and prevalences of hypertension, diabetes, gout, anemia, and metabolic acidosis. Additionally, charts of the change of mean eGFR and uACR were displayed. For the ESKD submodule, model outputs included the incidence of switch in treatment modality (hemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, or kidney transplant) and complications of kidney rejection, arteriovenous access thrombosis, peritonitis, bloodstream infections, or death.

Model Simulations

Three levels of sampling were used in the model to (1) generate a virtual population of individuals with CKD by randomly assigning initial sets of demographic characteristics, baseline comorbidities, risk factors, and background medication; (2) generate random uACR changes in each cycle (or eGFR changes when this option was selected); and (3) check whether or not the event occurred, based on its eGFR- and/or uACR-determined probability, in that cycle. Uncertainty around assumptions and data was assessed differently, depending on the data available for each parameter: risk of events from patient registries published in peer-reviewed journals were replicated using relevant distributions and defined in terms of the variation around the mean [26]. Distributions were truncated to clinically plausible ranges. In the absence of standard errors of mean values in the published source data, a variation of ±10% was used to generate distribution parameters. Variation in the behavior of risk factors was defined differently for eGFR and uACR. In contrast to most other CKD models where a fixed mean eGFR change is assumed for an average CKD patient, in the current study the mean eGFR change was determined according to the KDIGO health state. For uACR, no such KDIGO class-specific changes were identified in the literature; instead, the variability in uACR change was modeled via a log-normal distribution based on CKD-PC registry data. Corresponding CKD-PC registry data for eGFR were used as an alternative source to model the annual eGFR slope via a normal distribution, simulating both improvement and worsening of eGFR, as well as slower and faster rates of decline.

Simulations were performed using a model cycle of 1 year, and in accordance with average life expectancy the maximum time horizon of the model was 80 years. The model was programmed in Microsoft Excel 365, with limited use of Visual Basic Analysis.

Model validation

Internal validation of the CKD-PM was performed by comparing outcomes observed in source studies with model-predicted values obtained utilizing patients’ profiles and modeling horizons in line with their respective follow-up times (Figure 2). Studies not used in model development were utilized for external validation. Model concordance was evaluated by plotting predicted values (y-axis) against observed values (x-axis) and fitting an ordinary least squares linear regression line (LRL), with an intercept of zero, to the data. The slope was used to categorize the quality of the prediction: LRL slopes of <1 and >1 were suggestive of model under-prediction and over-prediction, respectively. Deviation <25% from an LRL slope of 1 was considered mild, 25%−50% deviation was considered moderate, and >50% was considered severe [63,64]. Model validation focused on the endpoints of all-cause mortality, CV mortality, ASCVD, and ESKD/KRT, while face validity was checked for the other model endpoints (Figure 2). Base-case settings for model validation were: truncation of baseline age at 94 years, baseline eGFR and uACR values sampled from their mean ± standard deviation in the tested cohorts, no capping of age and uACR in risk equations for all-cause mortality, CV mortality, and ASCVD, and an eGFR threshold of 30 ml/min/1.73 m2 for predicted risk of KRT.

Results

Model predictions

All-cause mortality

Model-predicted life expectancy stratified by eGFR class was consistent with external data for patients of comparable age [65]. Life expectancy varied as expected when running life-long analyses in 4 cohorts of patients defined by eGFR and uACR levels (G2, 20 years; G3a, 15 years; G3b, 11 years; and G4–5, 9 years with the base-case model option) [47]. These values were in line with previously reported life-years stratified by age and eGFR category [65]. Predictions closest to the external benchmark data were achieved when using the eGFR change per KDIGO class and diabetes status [47], combined with uACR fold-change from a log-normal distribution [29]. This model setting was selected as the base-case setting for the remaining analyses unless otherwise specified. The predicted incidence of all-cause mortality (a composite of CV, renal, and other death) was compared to the observed incidence in 13 CKD-PC cohorts (ARIC, AusDiab, CHS, COBRA, Framingham, Gubbio, HUNT, MESA, NHANES III, PREVEND, Rancho Bernardo, REGARDS, and ULSAM), described in Matsushita 2010 [41], after aligning baseline characteristics and time horizons. Use of baseline eGFR and uACR values generated by sampling from normal and log-normal distributions, respectively, resulted in mild under-prediction of all-cause mortality (LRL slope = 0.94). In contrast, mild over-prediction of all-cause mortality (LRL slope = 1.14) was noted when baseline eGFR/uACR was set to correspond to the mid-point in the range of eGFR/uACR values used to define the KDIGO CKD categories (Figure 3). (For example, the mid-point eGFR value for the G2 category [range 60–90 ml/min/1.73 m2] would be 75 ml/min/1.73 m2.) However, within a given cohort the mean eGFR and uACR values of patients in each KDIGO CKD category do not necessarily coincide with the standard KDIGO mid-point, and this discrepancy may affect the accuracy of eGFR and uACR predictions over time. To illustrate this point, all-cause mortality was more severely over-predicted in the Framingham, Gubbio, Rancho Bernardo, and ULSAM cohorts when using eGFR and uACR mid-points. These cohorts had 10-year horizons, and thus the impact of estimating baseline eGFR and uACR values from standard mid-points rather than from actual means was more apparent. For subsequent validation steps, baseline values sampled from actual mean (SD) eGFR and uACR were preferred over ‘distribution-based’ values.

After the population of the CKD-PM with baseline data from 4 CRIC cohorts stratified by baseline eGFR [47], predicted 5-year all-cause mortality rates in the G1–2 (4%) and G3a (8%) health states were within the observed 95% confidence intervals (CI) of corresponding CRIC participants. In the G3b health state, the predicted 5-year all-cause mortality rate (21%) was above the observed upper 95% CI for CRIC participants in the A2 category (10%–17%), but within the observed range for those in the A3 category (16%–23%) (Table 4). The predicted 5-year all-cause mortality rate (48%) in the G4–5 health state was 2-fold higher than that observed in corresponding CRIC participants (A1 24%, A2 21%, A3 21%), and outside the observed 95% CIs (Table 4). A discrepancy between predicted and observed rates for the ‘death’ outcome would be expected, since research cohorts typically provide conservative estimates of mortality [47]. The predicted values are likely more representative of an equivalent CKD population encountered in real-world practice.

CV mortality

The predicted cumulative incidence of CV mortality obtained with the ‘CKD-Patch’ methodology was compared to the observed incidence in 14 cohorts used for derivation of the ‘CKD-Patch’ CV mortality equations [43]. The internal validity of CV mortality prediction was fair, with a trend for moderate under-prediction (LRL slope = 0.58) (Figure 4A). Possible reasons for this under-prediction include uncertainty regarding assigned causes of death in registries; and the use of random numbers to assign cause of death in the model (at each cycle, occurrence of renal death [i.e., death occurring after KRT] is checked first, then nonspecific death and lastly CV death; this process may yield fewer cases of CV death than expected.) The model’s tendency to under-predict CV mortality was confirmed in the external validation (LRL slope = 0.37, which is considered to be severe under-prediction) (Figure 4B). This finding was mainly due to inclusion of the ZODIAC cohort, which had a particularly low baseline uACR value (2 mg/g). Exclusion of this data-point from the regression analysis yielded an LRL slope of 0.69, which is indicative of moderate under-prediction.

Atherosclerotic CVD

The predicted cumulative incidence of ASCVD obtained using ‘CKD-Patch’ methodology was compared with the observed incidence in 14 cohorts included in the derivation of the ‘CKD-Patch’ equations. The internal validity of ASCVD prediction was good, with a trend for mild over-prediction (LRL slope = 1.12 after calibration to baseline risk) (Figure 5A). Before calibration, over-prediction was severe (LRL slope = 1.68). Possible reasons for this bias include variation across cohorts in the definition of ASCVD (stroke +/– transient ischemic attack, myocardial infarction +/– unstable angina, inclusion of fatal and/or nonfatal events), and the effect of time-dependent risk (ASCVD risk is re-estimated at each cycle, and most risk factors [age, eGFR, uACR, incident T2D] worsen over time). The impact of time-dependent risk was addressed by using a weighted average of the current updated risk (10%) and baseline risk (90%). This weighting ratio was determined to achieve minor over-prediction while still allowing retention of time-dependent risk. A tendency for the model to over-predict ASCVD risk was also noted on external validation, with very severe over-prediction in the absence of calibration (LRL slope = 2.1) and severe over-prediction (LRL slope = 1.81) after calibration to baseline risk (Figure 5B).

On population of the CKD-PM with baseline data from 4 CRIC cohorts stratified by baseline eGFR [47], predicted 5-year heart failure rates in the G3a (9%) and G3b (14%) health states were within the observed 95% CIs of corresponding CRIC participants. In the G1 health state, the predicted incidence (5%) was outside the observed 95% CI for CRIC participants in the A1 category (1%–3%) but within the observed range for those in the A2 category (3%–13%). In the G4–5 health state, the modeled incidence of heart failure (13%) mirrored the observed incidence among CRIC participants in the A2 category but was below the lower 95% CI of those in the A3 category (16%–25%) (Table 4). This trend for mild under-prediction might be driven by the lower heart failure rate applied to patients without T2D in G4–5 (9%) compared with G3b (13%). An assumed improvement in health state from G5 to G3a A1 following successful kidney transplantation may also account for the lower-than-anticipated 5-year heart failure rate among patients in the G4–5 health state at baseline.

Predicted cumulative 5-year rates of CVD (nonfatal and fatal stroke, myocardial infarction, and heart failure) in the G1–2 (7%), G3a (12%), and G4–5 (31%) health states were within the observed 95% CIs of corresponding CRIC participants. In the G3b health state, the predicted rate (27%) was marginally outside the observed 95% CI for CRIC participants in the A2 class (18%–26%), but within the observed range reported in the A3 class (24%–32%) (Table 4). These findings indicate that the model has good accuracy in predicting CVD, and that the applied calibration measure is effective in moderating the influence of time-dependent risk factors.

ESKD and KRT

External validation of the CKD-PM against 13 North American cohorts reported by Tangri et al. [57] indicated slight under-prediction of the rate of KRT per 1000 patient-years (LRL slope = 0.84), which was estimated as the cumulative incidence over the given time horizon (variable from cohort to cohort), divided by the number of life-years, and multiplied by 1000 (Figure 6A). Separate external validation conducted using baseline data from 15 cohorts located outside North America [57] likewise indicated moderate under-prediction of KRT rate (LRL slope = 0.65) (Figure 6B). Both validations were based on the assumption of an eGFR threshold of 30 ml/min/1.73 m2 for risk of KRT initiation.

Discussion

The current modeling project was initiated to address the need for a comprehensive CKD-PM capable of quantifying the burden of disease caused by CKD progression, and the complications and comorbidities associated with CKD. After reviewing previously published modeling research projects in CKD, a progression model was developed utilizing well-established patient-level simulation methods. The model allows the prediction of CKD progression across a broad range of patient risk profiles, reflecting the heterogeneity of the CKD population. In previous models, CKD was described and modeled as a homogenous cohort even though the risk of kidney failure varies considerably among patients with CKD, and is impacted by various risk factors, such as CKD etiology, sex, comorbidities, anemia, diabetes, hypertension, and proteinuria. Such disease heterogeneity presents challenges in the accurate modeling of CKD progression, and the identified models scarcely consider these factors [59]. The current model has the benefit of including multiple risk factors (e.g., eGFR, uACR, HbA1c, blood pressure, lipids, and BMI) to predict the risk of CKD progression, and also incorporates multiple submodules of CKD-related complications.

A CKD model using a population-based microsimulation approach has recently been developed as part of the Inside CKD program to project the prevalence and burden of CKD at the national level [19]. By factoring in country-specific data, the Inside CKD model is designed to assess the impact of different intervention strategies on disease progression and costs to local healthcare systems. As in the case of the CKD-PM, the Inside CKD model uses data from the published literature and national statistics to determine the probability of individual transitions between health states, while 5 modules are incorporated to allow data input on demographics, CKD status (CKD prevalence, eGFR, and albuminuria status), disease burden (ESKD and KRT prevalence, CKD-associated comorbidities and complications), health economics (CKD- and KRT-associated costs), and interventions.

Potential model outputs include the annual prevalence of CKD, CKD-related comorbidities, and KRT, annual and cumulative incidences of CV complications (heart failure, myocardial infarction, and stroke), costs, and health-related quality of life. In comparison to the CKD-PM, populations simulated with the Inside CKD model are assigned only a limited number of baseline characteristics (age, sex, BMI, eGFR, and albuminuria status), the microsimulations are limited to a 6-year time horizon, and the model outputs are less detailed.

Strengths of the CKD-PM include its broad range of inputs and outputs, its patient-level simulation approach, allowing a virtual population of hypothetical individuals to be followed over time and to experience first or recurrent events across different health states, and its incorporation of dynamic, modifiable risk factors rather than the static risk factors employed in more traditional Markov models where memory is limited to the previous state. For example, age is a risk factor for the development of diabetes, and the eGFR annual slope in turn depends on the patient’s glycemic status (patients experience a faster eGFR decline after having developed diabetes). Notably, the risk prediction studies incorporated in the CKD-PM captured the complex relationship between the parameters by studying interactions between CKD risk factors and/or non-linear associations with the predicted events. This contributed to the robust modeling properties revealed in internal and external validation testing. Internal validation of key model endpoints was satisfactory, according to pre-defined accuracy criteria. All-cause and CV mortality predictions (using the Matsushita 2020 model) [43] did not require calibration. ASCVD was accurately predicted after calibration to baseline risk. ESKD was accurately predicted when defined as the initiation of KRT.

Our study has a number of limitations that may have affected the structure and predictions of the progression model. Our structural approach was to represent CKD as 18 mutually exclusive KDIGO health states, plus additional states during KRT and death. All of these are dependent on the evolution of risk factors over time. Thus, changes in eGFR or uACR progression will lead to improvement or worsening of CKD severity, and this is quantified by which KDIGO class the patient is in. Nevertheless, numerically, it is possible for a patient to experience sudden eGFR and/or uACR worsening without a change in health state in those cases where the initial value is close to the upper threshold of eGFR category; while in case the values are close to the other extreme, a slight change in eGFR or uACR may lead to a change in health state. The limitations of eGFR-driven clinical endpoints [66] along with inherited modeling assumptions may simplify the true underlying mechanisms impacting disease progression. Nevertheless, the outcomes of the model are based on the average of all patients, thus opposing effects are likely balanced.

CKD risk factors and CKD complications represent a complex health problem spanning a range of therapeutic areas. Conducting a fully systematic literature review of the data generated on CKD was not feasible, given the large volume of research that has been published in recent years. Thus, a pragmatic approach was followed, involving a literature review targeting publications arising from major CKD cohort studies. Bringing together the evidence from several research groups added further complexity to the exercise and raised concerns about prediction validity, which were investigated by comparison with original or other study predictions. The model represents a composite of several published risk equations from different sources, and hence consistency across data inputs may not always be optimal. We acknowledge that at the individual patient level the predicted progression of eGFR and uACR may differ from that measured in real life. Nevertheless, the progression of eGFR and uACR has been measured in large cohorts, resulting in average values reflective of reality. Addressing how time-dependent risk factors are related to risk prediction is a highly complex endeavor, especially in the framework of a patient-simulation tool [67,68]. In generating baseline patient characteristics, it was not feasible to account for possible correlation between certain parameters (e.g., age was sampled independently from eGFR), and this may have affected the face validity of specific outcomes (e.g., age at initiation of KRT). The inclusion of patient outliers (e.g., patients with >1 kidney transplants) potentially distorts model outputs; however, the model in this regard reflects real-world practice. To simulate a plausible sample of patients, it was therefore important to source the mean baseline characteristics from a single study, where possible.

Along with the inclusion of patients with high-risk profiles, our microsimulation framework permitted the occurrence of several events per patient, even within the same cycle, including death. In patients with a high risk of developing ESKD, the risk of CVD and heart failure as well as mortality will also be high over a 5-year period: as shown in Table 4, the cumulative incidence can exceed 100%. However, as this is a reflection of what is happening in real life, no adjustments were made to the individual or cumulative risk.

Also, our model was developed as a treatment-agnostic tool, not linked to any specific country, and validated against Western-based studies. Most risk equations (diabetes, ASCVD, KRT) and clinical data applied in the model were derived from international registries covering several regions, with some exceptions that describe local management. Options in the model and risk factors used in the engine already account for ethnicity and region. Further investigations would be needed to confirm the validity of predictions for other regions (e.g., Africa, Asia) and, if necessary, adjustments could be considered. However, since the focus of the model is on the clinical background of the disease, the influence of geographic factors is expected to be minor. It should also be noted that social determinants of health, as well as access to individually optimized standard of care, are not part of the risk equations. If future research were to include these parameters, they could also be considered.

Adding treatment arms with specific treatment effects is possible in the CKD-PM. It should be emphasized that any impact on the risk of death, or CV or renal events (transitions to and from each health state and rate of events) is fully mediated via the treatment effect on eGFR, uACR, and, in the case of the modeled CV endpoints, also via changes in SBP, as well as glycemia level (via changes in HbA1c or diabetes status). This is achieved through the use of disease-specific, rather than treatment-specific, risk equations. Finally, microsimulation models are characterized by numerical and computational complexity, which limits the running of extensive scenarios with large sample sizes using current software; however, convergence tests were performed and indicated that outcomes achieve stability with 400 simulated patients (well short of the 1000 simulated patients allowed for in the CKD-PM).

In conclusion, the CKD-PM described here extends the scope of existing models by incorporating a broader range of demographic and clinical characteristics, including risk factors, comorbidities, and complications that reflect the complexity of CKD, and thereby allows detailed prediction of disease outcomes. The validation procedures indicate that our microsimulation is a reliable lifetime predictor of CKD progression and associated complications and that it has the potential to guide patient-centered improvements in clinical and economic outcomes in an era of emerging targeted therapies.
