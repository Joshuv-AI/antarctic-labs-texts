Differentiation between patent foramen ovale and pulmonary arteriovenous malformations via simultaneous contrast transthoracic echocardiography and transcranial doppler
Authors: Jingjin Wang, Zhenhui Zhu, Hong Meng, Changming Xiong, Linyuan Wan, Hongxia Qi, Jiande Wang, Qianqian Liu, Yanhua Wang, Jing Dong, Shiguo Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The objective is to differentiate between patent foramen ovale (PFO) and pulmonary arteriovenous malformations (PAVM) using simultaneous contrast transthoracic echocardiography (cTTE) and contrast transcranial cerebral Doppler (cTCD). A total of 193 patients suspected of having a right-to-left shunt were classified into four groups based on the reference standard, including PFO (n = 102), PAVM (n = 18), negative subjects (n = 53), and indeterminate group (n = 20). Simultaneous cTTE and cTCD were performed in all patients. The comparison between PFO and PAVM was as follows: (1) The cut-off for cardiac cycles was set at ≤ 3 for PFO and ≥ 4 for PAVM. (2) Regarding the quantity of left atrial (LA) bubbles at rest, less than 30 bubbles were observed in 76.4% of patients with PFO, while more than 30 bubbles were found in 94.4% of patients with PAVM. (3) The cut-off of incremental bubbles during modified Valsalva were set at ≥ 10 bubbles for PFO and ≤ 1 bubble for PAVM. (4) The percentages of observed bubbles crossing either interatrial septum or pulmonary vein were 11.8% and 72.2% in the PFO and PAVM groups, respectively. In PFO group, the simultaneous use of cTTE and cTCD demonstrated a high area under the curve (AUC) of 0.973 using incremental bubbles. The successful application of cTCD-guide Valsalva maneuver resulted in a higher quantity of LA bubbles observed in cTTE findings. Simultaneous use of cTTE and cTCD enables differential diagnosis between PFO and PAVM. Moreover, the use of cTCD-guide Valsalva maneuver is recommended for PFO diagnosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32229-x.

Introduction

The right-to-left shunt encompasses both intracardiac shunt and intrapulmonary shunt in relation to patent foramen ovale (PFO) and pulmonary arteriovenous malformations (PAVM). Previous studies have reported an incidence of PFO in 20 to 30% of individuals1,2, which can lead to paradoxical embolism, cryptogenic stroke, migraine, hypoxemia, etc. PAVM is characterized by an abnormal communication between the pulmonary artery and pulmonary vein (PV) resulting in hypoxemia, hemoptysis, paradoxical embolism, etc3. Initial screening often confuses PFO and PAVM due to their overlapping clinical symptoms and testing methods.

The current primary screening methods include contrast transthoracic echocardiography (cTTE) and contrast transcranial cerebral Doppler (cTCD). cTCD offered the advantage of a high positive rate of diagnosis and quantification of the right-to-left shunt in middle cerebral artery flow but lacked confirmation regarding intrapulmonary or intracardiac shunts. Also, the performance of a precise Valsalva maneuver is essential for the accurate diagnosis of PFO, TCD can also effectively monitor the impact of Valsalva maneuver4. On the other hand, cTTE was considered for evaluating intracardiac shunts or intrapulmonary shunting based on contrast agent time and bubbles crossing interatrial septum (IAS) or PV, although image quality may be limited during Valsalva maneuver.

In this study, both cTCD and cTTE were used simultaneously for assessing right-to-left shunt with the goal of increasing diagnostic accuracy and differentiating between intracardiac and intrapulmonary shunts.

Methods

Study design

The study was retrospectively conducted as a diagnostic research test for PFO and PAVM, utilizing simultaneous cTCD and cTTE. The ethics approval were obtained by the Ethics Committee of Fuwai Hospital, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. All methods were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant guidelines.

Participants

This study included consecutive 200 patients with suspected right-to-left shunt between 2022 and 2024 at Fuwai hospital in Beijing. Inclusion criteria consisted of: (1) symptom or syndrome with cryptogenic stroke, migraine (with aura or cerebrovascular disease), hypoxemia, or short of breath. (2) the minimum age was seven years old (the Valsalva maneuver can be effectively executed by individuals who are at least seven years of age). Exclusion criteria consisted of: (1) unrepaired atrial septal defect or ventricular septal defect diagnosed with transthoracic echocardiography (TTE); (2) poor acoustic windows; (3) unsatisfactory Valsalva maneuver; (4) pregnancy.

cTTE and cTCD simultaneously

The patient was positioned in the left lateral decubitus position, and two probes (S5-1 for cTTE, Philips, Best, The Netherlands, and Multi-DopX4 TCD detector for cTCD, DWL, Sipplingen, Germany) were placed in the apical four-chamber view and right middle cerebral artery, respectively. Following contrast agent injection, both cTTE and cTCD examinations were conducted concurrently. Each patient underwent this procedure three times, once at rest and twice while performing the modified Valsalva maneuver.

Contrast agent and modified Valsalva maneuver

A saline solution of nine milliliters mixed with 1 ml of air was rapidly injected as a bolus contrast agent via a left cubital vein. A modified Valsalva maneuver was then performed by having the patient blow into a syringe connected to a sphygmomanometer until reaching a pressure of 40mmHg, which was held for 15s (Supplementary Fig. S1).

The four phases of the Valsalva maneuver with respect to the right middle cerebral artery (RMCA) (Fig. 1). Simultaneous cTCD measurements showed that mean blood velocity in RMCA decreased by 35% in phase II, indicating successful performance of the Valsalva maneuver4. Unsuccessful performance of the Valsalva maneuver was defined as a mean decrease in blood velocity in the right middle cerebral artery of less than 35% during phase II.

The values of cTTE and cTCD

The following parameters were recorded using cTTE: (1) the cardiac cycle (defined as the period from the opacification of right heart until the appearance of the first bubble in the left atrium (LA), during which cardiac cycle is measured, (2) quantity of bubbles present in LA during rest or modified Valsalva maneuver conditions (the maximum count of bubbles in the LA within a specific frame of a apical four-chamber view, regardless of timing in the cardiac cycle), and (3) appearance of bubbles crossing IAS or PV (the appearance of bubbles crossing the IAS in the presence of PFO, or being observed in the PV with PAVM, was defined as bubbles crossing IAS or PV). (4) Incremental bubbles in LA referred to the difference between bubble quantities (maximum number of bubbles in any frame) after Valsalva maneuver compared to those at rest.

Using cTCD, we obtained information on quantity of bubbles at rest within 25 s after contrast agent injection (the total count of bubbles within a 25-second interval), bubble time from injection to appearance of first bubbles in right middle cerebral artery, quantity of bubbles during Valsalva maneuver (the total count of bubbles within a 25-second interval), and incremental bubbles during Valsalva maneuver. The bubbles were defined as a short-duration, high-intensity signal that was parallel to the blood flow. The large number of bubbles observed as the rain curtain sign was quantified as 33 bubbles (an increase of 10% compared to the maximum bubble count).

Reference standard

All patients underwent transesophageal echocardiography or contrast transesophageal echocardiography using an Epiq 7c ultrasound system (Philips, Best, The Netherlands) equipped with an X8-2t sector. PFO diagnosis was confirmed by transesophageal echocardiography or contrast transesophageal echocardiography showing dynamic shunt or bubble passage across IAS at rest or during Valsalva maneuver. Additionally, patients suspected of having PAVM underwent contrast-enhanced computed tomography scans (n = 74), while advanced digital subtraction angiography was performed in nine cases. PAVM diagnosis involved identifying abnormal communication between the pulmonary artery and PV on computed tomography scans or digital subtraction angiography.

Statistical analysis

The data were presented as mean with standard deviation, or median (interquartile range), or number of patients (percentages), as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test. One-way analysis of variance was employed to compare variables among PFO, PAVM, and control groups. Non-parametric methods for independent samples were utilized when studying populations with non-normal distributions. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated for independent variables to determine areas under the curve (AUC) and cut-off values related to PFO and PAVM. DeLong’s test was applied to demonstrate significant differences in AUCs among three models involving cTTE alone, cTCD alone, and cTTE and cTCD simultaneously. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software, Version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and Medcalc software (MedCalc Software Ltd., Ostend, Belgium). A significance level of P < 0.05 was considered.

Prior to initiating the study, we conducted a sample size calculation using the diagnostic test (ROC) analysis in PASS software (Version 15.0.5). Based on data from previous study5, the AUC for cTTE in diagnosing PFO was reported as 0.82. We hypothesized that the AUC would increase to 0.94. Accordingly, we set the significance level α = 0.05, power = 0.80, alternative hypothesis AUC1 (H1) = 0.94, and applied a two-sided test. The calculated minimum required sample size was 96 (N+ = 48, N− = 48). Our final cohort included 102 patients in the PFO group and 53 individuals in the control group, exceeding the estimated sample size.

On the other hand, the AUC for cTCD in diagnosing PAVM was not previously reported. Under the null hypothesis, we assumed AUC0 (H0) = 0.50, and hypothesized that the AUC would improve to 0.85 under the alternative hypothesis (H1). Accordingly, we set the significance level at α = 0.05, statistical power at 0.80, and applied a two-sided test. The calculated minimum required sample size was 18 (N + = 9, N− = 9). Our final cohort included 18 patients in the PAVM group and 53 individuals in the control group, which met the required sample size.

Results

Participants

Based on transesophageal echocardiography and computed tomography examination results, all patients (n = 193) were categorized into four groups: (1) PFO group (n = 102), (2) PAVM group (n = 18), (3) Negative group without PFO or PAVM (n = 53), and (4) Indeterminate group (n = 20). Group Indeterminate included subjects with right-to-left shunts that could not be diagnosed by transesophageal echocardiography and computed tomography due to technical limitations or unavailability of computed tomography imaging. A total of 173 subjects were ultimately included for analysis after excluding those in the indeterminate group .

Furthermore, Fifty-one subjects from the PFO group, who underwent transcatheter closure of PFO, had right heart catheterization, while nine individuals with PAVMs had digital subtraction angiography and right heart catheterization performed.

The clinical characteristics of 173 patients are presented in Table 1 with respect to PFO, PAVM, and Control groups. Migraine was observed in the majority (48.0%) of patients in the PFO group, while hypoxemia was more prevalent (44.4%) among patients in the PAVM group. Previous cardiac or pulmonary procedures were documented for six subjects with PAVMs (three prior closures of PFOs and three prior closures of multiple PAVMs). Among the six subjects with PFOs, previous cardiac or pulmonary procedures included three prior atrial septal defect closures, one prior ventricular septal defect closure, one prior patent ductus arteriosus closure, and one prior closure of a PAVM. The partial pressure of oxygen levels did not differ significantly among the three groups (P = 0.285).

cTTE results

The intergroup comparison are presented in Table 2. During the resting state of the examination, patients in the PAVM group exhibited a higher quantity of bubbles in the LA, with over 30 bubbles observed at rest in most patients (94.4%) within this group. In contrast, the majority of patients in the PFO group had less than 30 bubbles at rest (76.4%). Additionally, only two individuals in the control group had less than 30 bubbles at rest (3.8%). Among those with more than 30 bubbles at rest, right atrial pressure measured during catheterization was significantly higher in the PFO group compared to the PAVM group (8.5 ± 5.0 mmHg versus 4.3 ± 2.3 mmHg, P = 0.012). A significant difference was observed between the groups in terms of the number of cardiac cycles. Specifically, patients with PAVM exhibited longer cardiac cycles (more than four cardiac cycles, 83.3% of patients). In contrast, less than three cardiac cycles showed bubble appearance in the PFO group, accounting for 67.2% of patients. Furthermore, in the PFO group, there was no significant difference observed in the quantity of LA bubbles with Valsalva maneuver between individuals with migraine and those with stroke or transient ischemic attack (median 30 versus. 25, P = 0.165) (Fig. 3).

cTCD results

During the resting state of the examination, the quantity of bubbles in the right middle cerebral artery was higher in the PAVM group, consistent with that observed in cTTE. In the PAVM group, a majority of patients (88.9%) exhibited a large quantity of bubbles exceeding 25 at rest, whereas most patients in the PFO group (86.3%) had less than 25 bubbles at rest. Furthermore, the bubble time was significantly longer in the PAVM group compared to the PFO group (P = 0.046). While modified Valsalva maneuver increased the quantity of bubbles in right middle cerebral artery for patients with PFO, no change was observed in the PAVM group (P < 0.001).

cTCD-guide -Valsalva maneuvers and cTTE results in PFO group

Among the 102 patients in the PFO group, nine patients with a resting Quantity of LA bubbles > 100 did not perform Valsalva maneuver, while 93 patients underwent Valsalva maneuver. Additionally, Fifty-four patients (58.1%) were unable to successfully execute Valsalva maneuver under TCD guidance (with RMCA’s mean velocity reduction < 35% in phase II). Subsequently, these patients underwent TCD-guided Valsalva again and achieved successful execution. A total of 39 One-off successful TCD-guided Valsalva exercises were recorded (41.9%). We compared the RMCA’s mean velocity during Valsalva maneuvers between the groups that achieved successful and unsuccessful executions and also assessed cTTE results among these two groups (Supplementary Table S1). The application of cTCD in guiding the execution of Valsalva maneuvers was demonstrated in Fig. 4, showcasing the diverse outcomes resulting from distinct variations in these maneuvers.

PFO and PAVM diagnosis

ROC curves were utilized to determine the AUC and optimal cut-offs for PFO or PAVM with cTTE (Table 3). The presence of a large quantity of bubbles at rest indicated the likelihood of PAVM, with a cut-off value ≥ 21 bubbles. Additionally, analysis revealed that the cardiac cycle had moderate discriminatory power, with a cut-off value ≥ 4 cycles for detecting PAVMs (P = 0.038). In contrast, for the detection of PFOs, a cut-off value ≤ 3 cycles (P = 0.028). Furthermore, Valsalva maneuver induced an increase in bubble count specifically within the PFO group, resulting in AUC value of 0.94 for incremental bubbles, respectively (P < 0.001). However, in the PAVM group, the incremental bubbles with modified Valsalva maneuver demonstrated an AUC of 0.65, suggesting ≤ 1 bubble cut-off value.

Discussion

This study represents a systematic investigation that quantitatively examines both cTTE and cTCD simultaneously in relation to right-to-left shunting associated with PFO or PAVM. Our findings demonstrate that PFO is characterized by early cardiac cycles, a small quantity of LA bubbles at rest, significant increase in bubble count during modified Valsalva maneuver, infrequent observation of bubbles crossing IAS; whereas PAVM is associated with late cardiac cycles, a large quantity of LA bubbles at rest, no incremental increase in bubble count during modified Valsalva maneuver, and high incidence of observed bubbles crossing PV. Importantly, our results confirm the superiority of simultaneous cTTE and cTCD over cTTE alone for diagnosing PFO; furthermore, cTCD can also effectively monitor and guide the impact of Valsalva maneuver. cTTE is recommended for diagnosing PAVM due to its significant diagnostic value.

During initial screening, differentiation between PFO and PAVM was often challenging due to overlapping clinical manifestations and shared diagnostic approaches. Previous study6 suggested that patients suspected to have a PFO should also be evaluated for clinical features of PAVM.

Pioneering research conducted by Gazzaniga et al. 7 demonstrated that cTTE is a highly sensitive method for detecting PAVM, while Yang et al. 8 showed that combining cTCD and cTTE methods can enhance the specificity of PFO diagnosis. In terms of cTTE, cardiac cycles had been considered as a distinguishing factor between PFO and PAVM. However, our study revealed an overlap in timing within these cardiac cycles. Interestingly, 32.9% of patients with PFO exhibited more than 4 cardiac cycles, whereas 16.7% of those with PAVM had less than 3 cycles. Freeman et al. 9, in their investigation, found that relying solely on the number of cardiac cycles was insufficient for differentiating intracardiac and extracardiac shunts. Notably, late cardiac cycle patterns observed in individuals with PFO indicated intermittent opening at rest; thus, using cardiac cycle analysis alone yielded modest to moderate diagnostic accuracy for distinguishing between PFO and PAVM.

On the other hand, the quantity of LA bubbles at rest was considered a reliable parameter for distinguishing between PFO and PAVM. Due to its small and intermittent shunting, the quantity of bubbles associated with PFO was lower than that observed in cases of PAVM during rest. In PAVM cases, bubble quantity was categorized into grades 1 (1–29 bubbles), 2 (30–100 bubbles), and 3 (> 100 bubbles)10. However, bubble quantity for subjects with PFO was evaluated as grades 1 (1–9 bubbles), 2 (10–30 bubbles), and 3 (> 30 bubbles)11. Using cTTE, degrees of right-to-left shunting were identified in both PAVM and PFO cases. While computed tomography served as the standard method for assessing PAVM size, cTTE shunt grade correlated with lesion burden on CT scans. Notably, grade 1 shunts of cTTE were not indicative of treatable presence of PAVM12.

The modified Valsalva maneuver was employed to achieve and maintain a pressure of 40mmHg for a duration of 15 s in our study. Intrathoracic pressure of 40 mmHg induces a decrease in venous return and cardiac output. Concurrently, during continuous cTCD monitoring, the mean blood velocity in the right middle cerebral artery exhibited a significant decrease by 35%, indicating successful execution of the Valsalva maneuver4. The Valsalva maneuver consisted of four distinct phases; when intrathoracic pressure normalizes and venous return occurs (phase III), right atrial pressure surpasses left atrial pressure13. The determination of whether the Modified Valsalva maneuver meets the standard is contingent upon the grade of cTTE results. Therefore, establishing criteria for judging the success of Valsalva maneuver is a key objective. In our investigation, we employed a reduction in mean blood velocity greater than 35% in RMCA as an indicator to assess the efficacy of Valsalva maneuver. Notably, participants who achieved a velocity reduction greater than 35% exhibited higher cTTE shunt grades compared to those with reductions below this threshold.

Indeed, cTCD was the most sensitive method for detecting right-to-left shunt due to its non-invasive nature and simplicity, making it highly recommended in clinical practice. In cardiac department, TEE combined with saline contrast imaging not only enabled the detection of right-to-left shunting but also allowed for comprehensive assessment of the anatomical features of the PFO, including its size and the presence of associated atrial septal aneurysm. These parameters are clinically relevant to the decision-making process for PFO closure. Therefore, TEE was widely regarded as the reference standard and was recommended accordingly in current cardiac expert consensus11. On the other hand, as previous studies reported14, PFO detection was more sensitive when agitated saline was injected via the great saphenous vein of the lower extremity compared to the upper extremity. In our study, all contrast injections were administered through the left cubital vein, with patients positioned in the left lateral decubitus position. This approach facilitated a more convenient procedure and reduced the risk of complications such as superficial thrombosis, vasovagal syncope, and ecchymosis.

In our investigation, incremental bubbles observed with modified Valsalva demonstrated optimal diagnostic value for PFO’s AUC. An increase of more than ten bubbles was most strongly associated with the presence of PFO, whereas PAVM showed no significant increment in bubble formation. Notably, PAVM displayed consistent shunt degrees both at rest and during Valsalva maneuvers.

Our study provides novel findings indicating that PAVM bubbles are frequently observed through the PV; whereas bubble passage through PFO is rarely observed. We hypothesize that shunting in PAVM is typically larger in magnitude compared to that in PFO, and that while PAVM shunting is continuous, PFO-shunting tends to be intermittent.

Several other research groups have investigated the combined use of cTTE and cTCD for diagnosing PFO. Katsanos et al. 5 reported that TCD demonstrated a higher overall diagnostic yield compared to TTE in patients with cryptogenic cerebral ischemia. Our findings confirm that simultaneous utilization of cTTE and cTCD is superior to cTTE alone for diagnosing PFO. By using simultaneous cTTE and cTCD, the two methods complement each other. cTTE provides direct anatomical and hemodynamic visualization, enabling the identification of the source of right-to-left shunting in the PFO and ruling out other intracardiac shunts. Meanwhile, cTCD is a highly sensitive quantitative tool for right-to-left shunting, capable of precisely monitoring the bubble signals in the middle cerebral artery. As our research results show, if the Valsalva maneuver using TCD-guide is employed, it can meet the requirements for reducing blood flow, thereby allowing more bubbles to be produced in cTTE after the Valsalva maneuver. The combination of cTTE and cTCD can mutually verify each other to ensure the accuracy of the diagnosis. Secondly, when the image quality of cTTE is poor (such as in patients with poor acoustic windows) or the results are ambiguous, cTCD can serve as a decisive auxiliary tool. Conversely, if cTCD is positive but it is suspected to be PAVM rather than PFO, cTTE can quickly help locate the source of the shunt.

It is also debatable whether cTCD is useful in the detection of PAVM. Kijima et al. 15 reported that TCD demonstrates sensitivity in identifying PAVM among patients with hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia. Consistent with our findings, the quantity of bubbles observed during rest using cTCD or cTTE proved to be diagnostically valuable for PAVM; Moreover, although the bubble time measured by cTCD showed no significance, both the cardiac cycle and bubbles crossing PV detected by cTTE were statistically significant.

Study limitation

Our study, limited by a cross-sectional design, although demonstrating simultaneously cTTE and cTCD to differentiate between PFO and PAVM, would not serve as a substitute for the reference standard. The patient selection criteria included migraine. Although these patients also experienced aura and had concomitant cerebrovascular diseases, current large-scale randomized controlled trials have not demonstrated the efficacy of PFO closure for migraine management. This discrepancy may have introduced a potential bias in patient selection. One limitation of our study is the use of TEE as the reference technique for the diagnosis of PFO. Although TEE is widely regarded as the reference technique in clinical practice, studies comparing it with reference standards such as autopsy, cardiac surgery, or catheterization had demonstrated a sensitivity of 89% and a specificity of 91% for PFO detection16, indicating potential diagnostic discrepancies. Also, the exclusion of twenty cases with indeterminate group from the study had limited the sample size of our research. Nonetheless, the operator-dependency and reliance on sonographers with expertise impose limitations on both cTTE and cTCD. The observation of bubbles crossing the PFO or those originating from PV pathways was insufficient to confirm without a certain level of diagnostic expertise, which limited the clinical applicability of this parameter. With the rapid advancements in artificial intelligence technologies, automated and accurate analysis of cTTE and cTCD could become feasible. Conversely, Rubin et al. 17 discovered that utilizing robot-assisted cTCD may enable operators without prior cTCD training to successfully conduct examinations.

Conclusion

The right-to-left shunt comprises of PFO and PAVM, which often pose diagnostic challenges due to their overlapping clinical symptoms and testing methodologies. Simultaneous use of cTTE and cTCD enables differential diagnosis between PFO and PAVM. Also, the use of cTCD-guide Valsalva maneuver is recommended for PFO diagnosis.
