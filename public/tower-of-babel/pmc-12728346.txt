Endovascular Management of Complex Aortoiliac Occusive Disease with a Bifurcated Endograft: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Vasiliki Manaki, Argirios Giannopoulos, Nikolaos Lamprou, Ioannis Patsarikas, Ioannis Kontes, Vasileios Rafailidis, Kiriakos Ktenidis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSevere aortoiliac occlusive disease (AIOD), particularly TransAtlantic Inter-Society Consensus (TASC) II C/D lesions, presents a therapeutic challenge and has traditionally been managed with aortobifemoral bypass, which offers durable patency but carries substantial perioperative morbidity and mortality. Endovascular approaches, including bifurcated endografts, have emerged as less invasive alternatives, demonstrating favorable patency outcomes, technical advantages, and reduced hospital stays. We present the case of a 49-year-old male with complex TASC II D lesions who was successfully treated with a bifurcated endograft. The procedure achieved complete revascularization without perioperative complications, and one-year follow-up confirmed durable patency. This case highlights the feasibility of bifurcated endografts in selected high-risk patients with advanced AIOD. Although encouraging, their application in non-aneurysmal disease remains off-label, underscoring the need for long-term and comparative studies to validate outcomes and guide patient selection.Keywords: Aortoiliac occlusive disease, Endovascular procedures, Stent graft, Bifurcated endograft, Revascularization

INTRODUCTION

Peripheral arterial disease (PAD) affects more than 200 million individuals worldwide and is increasingly recognized as a major public health concern, particularly in aging populations and among patients with multiple cardiovascular risk factors [1,2]. A substantial proportion of PAD involves the aortoiliac segment, which has traditionally been managed with open surgical repair, such as bypass or endarterectomy [1,3,4]. While open surgery provides durable long-term patency approaching 90%, it carries perioperative mortality of approximately 3% and complication rates nearing 20% [3,5,6]. These limitations, combined with advances in imaging modalities and endovascular device technology, have driven a progressive shift from open surgery toward minimally invasive endovascular approaches, especially in patients with significant comorbidities [3,5].

According to the TransAtlantic Inter-Society Consensus (TASC) II guidelines, endovascular repair is recommended for TASC A and B lesions and for selected high-risk patients with TASC C lesions [7]. More recent European Society of Cardiology (ESC) and European Society for Vascular Surgery (ESVS) guidelines suggest endovascular treatment for short, isolated aortoiliac occlusions (<5 cm in length), hybrid procedures for iliofemoral involvement, and covered endovascular reconstruction of the aortic bifurcation (CERAB) when aortic involvement is present [1,8]. Kissing stents, historically considered the the standard endovascular technique for aortoiliac occlusive disease (AIOD), have demonstrated suboptimal long-term patency [6]. Consequently, covered stents and CERAB techniques have gained prominence for complex TASC II C and D lesions, although their three-year primary patency rates remain limited, not exceeding 85% in a recent meta-analysis [1,3,9]. Emerging evidence suggests that bifurcated endografts may provide superior durability, with a recent report demonstrating nearly 90% primary patency at three years [10].

Herein, we present the case of a 49-year-old male patient with chronic limb-threatening ischemia and TASC II D aortoiliac lesions, including high-grade infrarenal aortic stenosis and occlusion of the right common iliac artery (CIA) and external iliac artery (EIA). Although traditionally reserved for aneurysmal disease, the use of a bifurcated endograft in AIOD highlights the expanding role and adaptability of endovascular techniques in the management of complex non-aneurysmal vascular pathology. This study was approved by the Scientific Committee of “AHEPA” University General Hospital (No. 40381). Informed consent was obtained from the patient.

CASE

A 49-year-old male presented to the emergency department with rest pain in the right lower limb, which had worsened over the past month, along with a two-year history of intermittent claudication. On clinical examination, a monophasic Doppler signal was detected only over the right dorsalis pedis artery, and the ankle-brachial index (ABI) of the right limb was 0.3. The patient reported no sensory or motor deficits.

His medical history included hypertension, dyslipidemia, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and coronary artery disease (CAD), all of which were managed conservatively. His daily medications included aspirin (100 mg) and amlodipine (5 mg). There was no history of previous surgery or known allergies. He reported a 60-pack-year smoking history and social alcohol consumption.

Urgent computed tomography angiography (CTA) of the abdomen and lower extermities revealed occlusion of the right CIA with distal recanalization of the EIA via collateral vessels. The right internal iliac artery (IIA) was occluded, and approximately 60% stenosis was present in the right common femoral artery (CFA). In addition, high-grade stenosis of the infrarenal aorta with an unstable plaque and thrombus was identified (Fig. 1).

Preoperative computed tomography angiography demonstrated stenosis of the infrarenal aorta and occlusion of the right common and external iliac arteries.

The patient was transferred to the operating room the following day. Given his relatively young age and multiple significant comorbidities, the surgical team faced a complex decision regarding the optimal revascularization strategy. While open repair could provide durable results, the presence of COPD and CAD favored an endovascular approach.

Bare-metal or covered kissing stents were excluded due to thrombus burden and involvement of the infrarenal aorta. The CERAB technique was also not selected because it requires multiple stents in the infrarenal aorta and carries a higher likelihood of radial mismatch than bifurcated endografts, potentially increasing the risk of thrombosis. In the present case, a bifurcated endograft was selected instead of CERAB, primarily because of the patient’s young age and the superior patency rates reported with this technique. Femoro-femoral bypass was reserved as a last resort. A trimodular low-profile bifurcated aortic endograft (Minos endograft, Endovastec) was chosen.

Both CFAs were surgically exposed. Through the left femoral access, a 7-Fr, 45-cm sheath was advanced over a 0.035-inch hydrophilic wire into the suprarenal aorta, and angiography was performed. Thrombectomy using a Fogarty catheter was performed, and the thrombus was extracted from the right side. A subsequent angiogram demonstrated residual high-grade stenosis. The main body of the endograft (24×100 mm) was deployed from the left side, with the covered part positioned below the renal arteries (Fig. 2). Attempts to cannulate the right CIA and contralateral gate using the right femoral approach were initially unsuccessful. Subsequently, the left brachial artery was surgically exposed and accessed using a 7-Fr, 70-cm sheath, along with a 0.035-inch hydrophilic wire and a vertebral angiographic catheter. The contralateral limb (right, 10×80 mm) was then deployed, followed by deployment of the ipsilateral limb (left, 10×100 mm) above the origin of the left IIA.

Intraoperative angiographic images demonstrated the initial angiography (left), residual stenosis within the endograft (center), and correction with balloon-expandable stents deployed inside the endograft (right).

On the right side, two additional stents were deployed to extend the iliac limb: a balloon-expandable covered stent (Advanta V12TM, Atrium Medical; 8×59 mm) and a self-expanding stent (ProtégéTM, Medtronic; 8×60 mm) in the remaining CIA and EIA above the inguinal ligament. Subsequent angiography revealed residual stenosis in the proximal iliac limb segments immediately below the flow divider. Balloon-expandable stents (ExpressTM, Boston Scientific; 10×57 mm) were then deployed bilaterally in a kissing configuration within the endograft (Fig. 2). Final angiography confirmed excellent flow throughout the reconstructed segments. A right CFA endarterectomy was performed at the end of procedure, followed by patch angioplasty using a bovine pericardial patch.

Postoperatively, the patient was monitored in the intensive care unit (ICU) for 24 hours and then transferred to the vascular surgery ward. A superficial wound infection developed in the right groin and was managed conservatively with intravenous antibiotics. He was discharged on the 6th postoperative day with palpable dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial pulses and complete resolution of rest pain. The postoperative ABIs were 0.8 on the left and 0.85 on the right. At one-year follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic with sustained distal pulses, adherence to a weekly walking program, and successful smoking cessation. No complications were observed on one-year follow-up CTA (Fig. 3).

(A) Three-dimensional reconstruction image of computed tomography angiography (CTA) at the one-year follow-up demonstrated a well-patent bifurcated endograft. (B) Coronal image of CTA showed fully expanded both limbs without compression.

DISCUSSION

The management of severe AIOD, particularly TASC II D lesions involving the infrarenal aorta, has traditionally relied on open surgical procedures such as aortobifemoral bypass (ABF), which has long been regarded as the gold standard for revascularization [1,7]. This approach provides durable long-term patency and has demonstrated favorable clinical outcomes [5,6,11]. However, ABF is a highly invasive procedure associated with significant perioperative morbidity and mortality, particularly in patients with multiple comorbidities [1,6,11]. The present case of a 49-year-old male with severe AIOD highlights the ongoing clinical challenge of balancing procedural invasiveness against the need for durable revascularization and reflects the increasing preference for less invasive endovascular alternatives.

Endovascular treatment with bifurcated endografts represents a significant advancement in the management of complex AIOD. Evidence from the literature, including studies on devices such as the AFX (Endologix), Excluder (W. L. Gore & Associates), and Powerlink (Endologix) endografts, has consistently demonstrated high technical success rates, often approaching 100%, in restoring arterial patency [10,12-16]. Certain devices, such as the AFX, preserve the native aortic bifurcation and allow the possibility of future contralateral access if required [10]. Moreover, endovascular approaches are associated with significantly shorter ICU and overall hospital stays than open repair [1,6,11]. This reduced invasiveness renders bifurcated endografts particularly suitable for high-risk patients or those considered poor surgical candidates due to severe comorbidities.

When directly compared with ABF, the available evidence underscores the trade-off between procedural invasiveness and durability. Although ABF has historically provided superior long-term primary and secondary patency, particularly for extensive TASC II C and D lesions [5,6,11], this durability is offset by increased perioperative morbidity and higher 30-day mortality compared to endovascular approaches [6]. A recent meta-analysis reported a threefold increase in 30-day mortality after open surgery compared with standard endovascular treatment and CERAB [6]. Major complication rates were also frequent after open repair (15%) than after endovascular treatment (9%-10%) [6]. These findings, together with the clinical characteristics of our patient, further support the appeal of endovascular intervention in appropriately selected cases.

Bifurcated endografts may also be compared with alternative endovascular techniques such as kissing stents and CERAB [1]. Traditional bare-metal kissing stents have been widely used; however, multiple systematic reviews and contemporary guidelines now support the use of covered stents due to their superior patency and lower reintervention rates in complex TASC II C/D lesions [1,9,17,18]. Bare-metal stents leave the underlying atherosclerotic plaque directly exposed to circulating inflammatory cells, perpetuating the biologic processes associated with neointimal hyperplasia [19]. Furthermore, the inherent geometric configuration of kissing stents produces a radial mismatch between the stent and the native aortic lumen, which may promote disturbed flow patterns and predispose to thrombosis [3,10,19,20].

By contrast, CERAB, a covered stent-based approach, has demonstrated encouraging primary and secondary patency outcomes [3,6]. This technique not only provides mechanical protection against rupture and distal embolization, but also minimizes radial mismatch and provides favorable flow dynamics compared with kissing stents [19,20]. Nonetheless, a recent meta-analysis reported that CERAB primary patency remained below 84% at 36 months [3]. In comparison, pooled estimates from studies using bifurcated endografts reported primary patency rates of 93.93%, 91.46%, and 90.25% at 12, 24, and 36 months, respectively [10].

Despite the advantages of bifurcated endografts, several limitations should be acknowledged. Periprocedural complications, including groin wound morbidities (e.g., hematoma and infection), arterial dissection, rupture, and thromboembolic events, have been reported [10]. Although technical success rates are generally high, secondary interventions may be required during follow-up because of disease progression or graft-related complications [10]. One important limitation is the relatively large delivery systems required, which may be difficult to advance across severe stenoses; thus, lower-profile endografts are often preferred [10]. Moreover, cannulation of the contralateral limb may also be challenging in patients with a narrow aortic lumen. Therefore, many published studies have employed AFX endografts, which offer a precannulated contralateral limb. Another potential drawback is the coverage of existing collateral vessels, which may result in severe complications such as spinal cord or bowel ischemia, further augmenting morbidity and mortality. Importantly, the use of bifurcated endografts for non-aneurysmal diseases remains off-label, as these devices are approved primarily for the treatment of abdominal aortic aneurysms. This underscores the importance for a thorough preprocedural discussion with patients regarding the risks, benefits, and regulatory considerations, as was undertaken in this case. Finally, bifurcated endografts are more expensive than other therapeutic options, raising concerns about their cost-effectiveness, an additional factor that requires careful consideration.

In summary, endovascular reconstruction using bifurcated endografts represents a feasible and effective alternative to open repair for severe AIOD, particularly in high-risk patients. It offers shorter hospital stays and favorable short- to mid-term patency outcomes. The present case, involving a 49-year-old male with extensive TASC II D lesions successfully treated using a bifurcated endograft, adds to the growing body of evidence supporting this strategy. However, definitive conclusions regarding long-term efficacy and safety are limited. High-quality randomized controlled trials and long-term comparative studies are warranted to refine patient selection and optimize procedural standardization.
