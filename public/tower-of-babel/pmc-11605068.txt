ABAQUS-based research on the parameters of highway subgrade vibratory compaction and vibration wave propagation laws
Authors: Changwei Yang, Xianqing Xu, Mao Yue, Jia Luo, Ke Su, Hongsheng Ma, Ziqiang Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Vibratory rollers are generally used in the process of highway subgrade compaction. In the paper, the vibratory roller—subgrade finite element model was established to simulate the field construction by using ABAQUS. We used Hilbert–Huang Transform to analyze the compaction in the field test from the time–frequency domain. By changing the parameters of the vibratory roller and the filler, the comprehensive influence of the parameters such as the elastic modulus of the filler, rolling speed, excitation force, vibration frequency and thickness of the filler on the compaction quality of the subgrade was investigated. We studied the propagation pattern of vibration waves in three-dimensional space. The study shows that the signals of different frequency bands in the Hilbert spectrum represent the compaction degrees of fillers in different zones. The peak acceleration generally decreases with the increase of horizontal and vertical distance, but there is an increase at the boundary of the vibration field. There is an optimal combination of the excitation force, vibration frequency and thickness of the filler. The vibration waves propagate in the form of an ellipsoid in three dimensions, and the amplitude decreases with distance.

Introduction

Highway engineering has become an important medium for connecting various regions, and it provides a large amount of support in terms of human and material resources for the economic development of various countries and regions. The filler of most highway subgrades consists of gravels of different gradations, which are compacted during construction using different methods to improve their engineering properties1–5. So far, the total mileage of highway construction in China has reached 5.35 million kilometers, and there are still many highway projects under construction, which puts forward high requirements and great challenges to the construction process and construction quality of highway subgrade. vibratory compaction is a commonly used method in highway subgrade construction, which can not only effectively increase the denseness of the filler, but also ensure the construction progress and quality of the project, and more than 80% of the road compaction in the world adopts the vibratory compaction method6,7. Compared with traffic loads, subgrade is subject to the coupled influence of more diverse factors during construction, such as the excitation force of the roller, vibration frequency and vibration amplitude, etc., and the resulting dynamic response is more complex8–11.

Although the problem of subgrade compaction quality is widely emphasized by the engineering industry, it is difficult to accurately quantitatively describe the influence parameters in the compaction process due to the many factors mentioned above, and the research on the theory of subgrade compaction is constantly being updated and improved12–15. Simplification of complex dynamic models helps researchers to work on the main aspects of the problem, Steenbergen et al.16 used classical model of a beam on elastic half-space to simplify the pavement as a beam or slab on a half-space multilayer system, and described the dynamic response of the soil body under overhead loading using three traditional methods. Dong et al.17 used Galilean and Fourier transforms in the Cartesian coordinate system to simplify the soil to a multilayer system and the tire pressure to a moving homogeneous load, and derived the elastic and viscoelastic analytical solutions of the system. Based on the above and other theoretical models18–20, researchers conducted a series of indoor tests and field tests for further quantitative studies21–23. Wersäll et al.24 carried out a full-scale test to investigate the effect of different vibration frequencies of the roller on the compaction of crushed stone, and finally determined that the optimum compaction frequency was 18 Hz, and found that the topsoil layer was susceptible to loosening under high-frequency working conditions. Jia et al.25 conducted a field test to investigate the effects of changes in vibration frequency and material stiffness on compaction performance, and the study showed that the initial density of the filler was positively correlated with its stiffness, and the initial density was related to the vibration frequency. Xie et al.26 discussed the effects of different compaction conditions (including roller parameters and filler thickness) on compaction quality using indoor vibratory compaction tests, and the results showed that over-compaction rather negatively affected the overall quality. Yao et al.27 investigated the propagation law of vertical vibration waves along the forward direction of vibratory rollers in a site through a field test, and revealed the exponential relationship between the amplitude of the fundamental wave and the amplitude of the higher-order wave. Wu et al.28 conducted a large-scale field test at the construction site to analyze the propagation and evolution laws of vibration waves in soil-aggregate mixed filler, and determined the optimal vibration frequency and proposed the optimal filler thickness for compaction.

Field tests and indoor tests restore the real conditions during field construction to a large extent, but the drawbacks of traditional experiments also coexist. For example, the vibration parameters of the roller are often taken empirically, and due to the loss of mechanical power and other losses, the error generated in the compaction process is large29; and due to the limitations of sensors and space, the quality of compaction can only be described by the parameters of some specific time nodes or the results at the end of the test, instead of dynamically presenting the state of compaction. Due to the anisotropy and inhomogeneity of soils, various types of dynamic parameters cannot be accurately described during compaction tests, and field tests of granular materials are always expensive and time-consuming, so numerical simulation methods have been adopted by many scholars instead of field tests in most cases30–34. Peng et al.35 established a discrete element model of irregular rock and soil particles, coupled the subgrade and roller models to study the effect of frequency and amplitude of vibration on compaction quality. Fang et al.36 used ABAQUS to establish a vibratory compaction model, and the results showed that the dynamic response at different stages varied greatly and could be roughly categorized into five modes; with the increase of the modulus of the subgrade, the decoupling nonlinearities were gradually enhanced. Dan et al.37 investigated the dynamic response of mixed fillers at the particle scale based on the SmartRock sensor technology, and the results showed that the acceleration frequency reflected the energy attenuation of the vibration wave during vibratory compaction, and the vibratory acceleration attenuation was normally distributed with the distance from the SmartRock. Wu et al.38 used a two-dimensional discrete element method to research the compaction mechanism of gravel by rollers, and the research showed that there existed an optimal rolling speed and an optimal frequency, and the number of times of rolling and power consumption should be additionally considered when selecting the above optimal conditions.

The research on vibration compaction parameters and vibration wave propagation law of subgrade is getting more and more perfect with the progress of research methods, but most of the current research is focused on the influence of single parameter, and the comprehensive influence of elastic modulus, rolling speed, excitation force, vibration frequency and other parameters are not yet sufficient; and in the research of vibration wave propagation law, most of the researches are limited to the vertical direction along the filler, and there are less comprehensive researches on the propagation law of the vibration wave along the three-dimensional direction. According to the above, this paper uses ABAQUS software to establish the vibratory roller—subgrade finite element model, compare the field test data with the numerical simulation results, and apply the Hilbert–Huang transform to the field test data to discuss the compaction of the subgrade filler from the time–frequency domain. By inputting different working conditions, the influence of parameters such as elastic modulus of the filler, rolling speed, excitation force, vibration frequency and thickness of the filler on the compaction quality of the subgrade is explored, and the propagation law of vibration wave in the horizontal, vertical and three-dimensional directions is summarized.

Setting of working conditions and constitutive model

The interactions between the vibratory drum and the filler include normal and tangential interactions. The normal interaction is the pressure between the vibratory drum and the filler, including the self-weight of the vibratory drum and the excitation force of the eccentric block. The tangential interaction is the driving force, which is manifested by the friction between the drum and the filler. In the field construction, there are two conditions namely strong vibration and weak vibration. The vibration frequency of the strong vibration condition is 27 Hz, the excitation force is 416 kN, the nominal amplitude is 2.05 mm. The vibration frequency of the weak vibration condition is 31 Hz, the excitation force is 275 kN, the nominal amplitude is 1.03 mm. The centrifugal force generated by the rotation of the eccentric block inside the vibratory drum is the source of the excitation force. Hilbert–Huang transform

The Hilbert–Huang transform (HHT) is widely used in the analysis of vibration signals because of its superior time–frequency resolution and broadband signal processing capability. The results obtained by this method can clearly display the instantaneous amplitude corresponding to the individual frequency and time when conducting the field test. The HHT was proposed by Huang et al. This method is as follows: the signal is first subjected to Empirical Mode Decomposition (EMD) and transformed into Intrinsic Mode Functions (IMF). At this point, the time-domain signal is converted into continuously varying linear steady-state signal. Then each IMF is Hilbert transformed to obtain the instantaneous amplitude of the vibration signals at a certain moment and frequency43.

There are amplitudes with certain magnitude at 30 Hz, 300–350 Hz and 150–200 Hz frequency band during the compaction. With the increase of compaction times, the signal in the 150–200 Hz frequency band is obviously strengthened, while the amplitudes at 30 Hz and the 300–350 Hz band increase slightly. The amplitudes of the other frequency bands become smaller gradually. The signals attenuate mostly as getting close to 400 Hz. A sustained and uniform signal appears in the 200–300 Hz band besides the three main frequency bands mentioned above during the first compaction (Fig. 10a). And intermittent signals with small amplitudes also appear at approximately 100 Hz. Signals become stronger and more concentrated with increasing frequency in compaction. The signal in 200–300 Hz band become intermittent and the amplitudes become larger during the second compaction (Fig. 10b). Signals in the three main frequency bands become concentrated and the amplitudes increase to varying degrees. Amplitudes in the 150–200 Hz band increase the fastest while those in the other frequency bands appear to be weakening. During the third compaction (Fig. 10c), the amplitudes of each frequency band are not much different from those in the second time. But the signals are more concentrated and the amplitudes in the higher frequency band (300–350 Hz) attenuate.

The signal at 30 Hz is generated by the vibratory roller itself when it is working. This signal is present throughout the compaction process. The compaction degrees at different depths are different. The filler at the bottom gradually reaches the densest state after successive compaction. The signal in 200–300 Hz band indicates the state of compaction of the filler at the bottom and it becomes concentrated and strong with compaction times. The vibration waves inside the filler experience many times of reflection and superposition, which makes the vibration signals of the surface filler change, resulting in a certain intensity of “noise” interference in the spectrum. The interference gradually diminishes after the filler essentially reaches a dense state. The signal in the 300–350 Hz band indicates this situation, with concentrated and disorganized signals in this band at the beginning of compaction and a gradual attenuation as the times of compaction increase. Under the action of the excitation force, the surface the filler quickly reaches a dense state which does not change greatly during the compaction. The signal in the 150–200 Hz band reflects the compaction state of this part. The signal exists in the whole process of compaction and there is a certain extent of strengthening.

We conducted a field test at the same time and analyzed the compaction of the subgrade from the time–frequency domain using the Hilbert–Huang Transform. We compared the conditions and results of the field test and the numerical simulation. We investigated the effects of the modulus of elasticity of the filler, rolling speed, excitation force, vibration frequency, and thickness of the filler on the compaction quality of the subgrade.
