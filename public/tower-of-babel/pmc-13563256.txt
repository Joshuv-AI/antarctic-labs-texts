Commentary on: Genetic predictors of GLP1 receptor agonist weight loss and side effects (Nature volume 653, pages 770–775 (2026))
Authors: Gotaro Toda, Toshimasa Yamauchi
Published: 2026

In this article Su and coworkers 1 provide valuable insights utilizing the 23andMe database to search for factors that influence efficacy and side effects of glucagon‐like peptide (GLP)‐1 receptor agonist class of drugs (outlined in Figure 1). 23andMe is a pioneer in the direct to consumer (DTC) genetics industry founded in 2006, eventually acquiring genotyping and self‐reported health related data from well over 10 million subscribing customers who sent their samples for analysis, ultimately aiming to deliver individualized health solutions. The company which was reorganized as a nonprofit organization the 23andMe research institute in 2025 has continued its efforts to put forward research on the genetic basis of disease, utilizing its extensive database of research participants. The present study on factors influencing the effects and side effects of GLP‐1 receptor agonists, and gastric inhibitory polypeptide (GIP)/GLP‐1 receptor agonists was initiated by 23andMe in 2024 side to side with a telemedicine operation providing GLP‐1 receptor agonists. The size of the 23andMe dataset allows it to analyze not only the polymorphisms associated with a specific trait, as in a genome wide association study (GWAS) analysis, but also the phenotypes associated with a polymorphism in a given gene, for example a known target of an existing drug, termed phenome‐wide association.

Outline of research design.

GLP‐1 and GIP peptides are secreted from the small intestine after intake of food, with functions that range from suppression of appetite to accelerated secretion of insulin through their receptors. The agonists of their receptors have become central in the treatment of people with type 2 diabetes, as well as people that are overweight, with cardiac and renal benefits shown in clinical trials 2 .

The conclusions presented by the authors are important in that the authors use this vast resource to identify genetic and non‐genetic factors that collectively impact the effectiveness and side effects of GLP‐1 (Ozempic, Wegovy, compounded semaglutide) or GLP‐1/GIP (Mounjaro, Zepbound, compounded tirzepatide), which will for simplicity be indicated GLP‐1 below unless otherwise stated. The authors surveyed participants about GLP‐1 usage and were able to acquire over 27,885 respondents who had used GLP‐1. The majority of the participants were female (82.4%) and of European ancestry (78.3%), with a median age of 52. Before initiation of treatment, the median body mass index (BMI) of the participants was 35.1 kg/m2, and after a median treatment time of 8.3 months, they lost a median of 11.7% relative to their pretreatment weight. The authors assessed the role of non‐genetic factors in GLP‐1 efficacy in a linear model using age, sex, pretreatment BMI, drug type, drug dose, and treatment duration as the predictor variables. BMI loss was most dependent on sex, drug type, treatment duration and dose, in line with previous reports. Type 2 diabetes was also predictive of increased reduction in BMI as well as European ancestry. GLP‐1 was less effective in participants of Latino and African American ancestry. Efficacy of GLP‐1 showed decreases with age, with 10 years of age corresponding to 0.5% less decrease in BMI.

The authors conducted GWAS of percentage BMI loss phenotype and identified rs10305420 as the index single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP). This polymorphism changes the seventh amino acid in the signaling polypeptide of the GLP‐1 receptor from proline (C variant) to leucine (T variant), with a frequency of 40% in European ancestry groups, followed by Middle Eastern (38%), Admixed American (28%), South Asian (20%), East Asian (16%), and least common in African (7%) ancestry groups among the groups in the study. The authors observed a 0.641% BMI loss per effect allele (T), with no dominance effect. Though type 2 diabetes was detected to predict increased BMI loss, model testing for robustness of rs10305420 association showed the effect of the polymorphism was stable regardless of type 2 diabetes status. Along with the observation that rs10305420 showed the maximum posterior probability to be the causal variant among the top candidate variants, and that it was the only variant in the coding region, the authors conclude it is likely to be the causal polymorphism. The effect of rs10305420 reached significance in European populations but did not reach significance, although with a consistent trend in other populations. Other cohorts of various sample sizes and expected power have shown mixed results regarding the effect of this polymorphism, with consistent directionality in the All of Us cohort (n = 4,855), but not significant in UK Biobank though the number of people using GLP‐1 were not many (a maximum of 108 liraglutide users), leading to low power in this context.

The authors did further GWAS on vomiting and nausea, and reported rs11760106 as the index SNP for vomiting and rs9357296 as the index SNP for nausea. Both of these polymorphisms were in linkage disequilibrium with coding variants associated with BMI loss with GLP‐1 usage, such as rs10305420. In colocalization analysis which tests whether two traits share common variants, both BMI loss and vomiting, and BMI loss and nausea shared coding variants, leading the authors to conclude these are in fact the same signals. They translate this conclusion, describing these symptoms to be associated with BMI loss. As Tirzepatide is a dual GLP‐1/GIP receptor agonist, the authors performed GWAS in the population using Tirzepatide. The authors conclude a missense variant rs1800437 in linkage disequilibrium with the index polymorphism in the GIPR locus for vomiting where the G allele is protective and the C allele is the causal polymorphism. The effect of rs1800437 on vomiting was not observed in semaglutide users, although the difference between the frequency of tirzepatide users carrying the risk allele that experienced vomiting and semaglutide users that experienced vomiting was not significant.

In public data, the effector T allele for BMI loss in rs10305420 has been reported to be associated with reduction in type 2 diabetes related traits and slightly increased risk of smoking initiation. In phenome‐wide analysis in the 23andMe database, assessing the traits associated with rs10305420, consistent findings were reduced risk of type 2 diabetes in the T allele, and increased risk of having ever been a smoker. There was also no association with BMI, leading the authors to conclude the polymorphism has a stronger effect in usage of GLP‐1.

In a combined model of genetic, as well as non‐genetic variables on BMI loss, good performance was achieved with most of the variance explained by nongenetic factors such as type 2 diabetes, metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), and hypertension contributing to less BMI loss. Using data from electronic health record (EHR) not used in model construction, the authors tested whether the BMI loss model predicted weight loss over 6 months of GLP‐1 use, confirming that the top 25% predicted to lose BMI the most indeed showed a stronger BMI reduction.

In silico analysis of GLP‐1 receptor secondary structure suggested the change to hydrophobic leucine because of the rs10305420 polymorphism may have an impact on the function and dynamics of the receptor through a change of structure. The authors discuss previous reports on the effect of the polymorphism on GLP‐1 effect, which interestingly have different directionality from their conclusions. These studies had lower power and different cohort backgrounds, possibly accounting for these different conclusions. The authors mention large longitudinal datasets as a possible next step to develop ways to utilize genetics in decision‐making.

The present report utilizing the 23andMe database, a mammoth set of genetics data in conjunction with self‐reported information including health and medication status, shows genetic and nongenetic factors that can be modeled to predict BMI loss and side effects as a result of GLP‐1 usage. Among reported effects of the index SNP rs10305420 for BMI loss in this study includes a cohort of GLP‐1 users in Slovenia (n = 70) that did not show a significant association of on body weight loss 3 . In contrast to this study, a report on an overweight Chinese cohort with type 2 diabetes showed a reduced effect on weight loss and HbA1c by GLP‐1 with the T variant of rs10305420 4 as the authors discuss, perhaps reflecting strong dependence on ancestral background as reported by the authors. A study using human GLP‐1 receptor expressing Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells has shown receptor mediated cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) increase is blunted in cells expressing receptors with the T variant of rs10305420 5 , though the mechanistic link between polymorphism and the reported changes in medication efficacy is yet to be precisely determined. Given the impact of non‐genetic factors in the prediction model in this study, and different directionality in different backgrounds also discussed by the authors, the context in which genetic variation affects cellular/physiological function would seem to be of importance.
