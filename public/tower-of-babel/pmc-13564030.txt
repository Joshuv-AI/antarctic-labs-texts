Disambiguation of Product Ion Assignments, Including Internal Fragment Ions from Top-Down Mass Spectrometry, Using Ion Mobility-Aligned Fragmentation (MAF)
Authors: Novera Alam, Brad J. Williams, Michelle Yu, Paul Kowalski, Jane-Marie Kowalski, Jeffrey N. Agar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Advances in mass spectrometry instrumentation resulted in richer MSn spectra and additional ambiguity in peak assignments. Internal fragment ions (IFs) are often prevalent during top-down mass spectrometry (TDMS) and are particularly difficult to assignto the extent many practitioners currently forgo their assignment. Accounting for IFs during high-yield dissociation can increase the depth of sequence coverage by an order of magnitude but also results in a geometric increase in the size of the database and ambiguous peak assignments. The ambiguity in TDMS fragment ion assignments comes from the potential for one experimental peak to be assigned to two or more theoretical moleculesmost often in the form of isomeric internal fragment ions from distinct sequence regions. The disambiguation of such peaks remains a major challenge in TDMS and is the focus of this work. Ion mobility (IM) mass spectrometry can often separate isomers, including peptides. Starting from a TDMS spectrum containing both unambiguously and ambiguously assigned peaks, we address the hypothesis that ion mobility, or ion mobility followed by fragmentation, can help disambiguate fragment ion assignments by resolving isomeric ambiguity in the mobility domain. We demonstrate that mobility-aligned fragmentation (MAF) can disambiguate TDMS and peptide MS/MS peak assignments of isomeric terminal and internal ions. Consistent with previous studies, we also demonstrate that a single isomeric species (e.g., an individual b-, a-, or internal ion) can be comprised of multiple conformational isomers with different collisional cross-section (CCS) values.

Introduction

TDMS Is Characterized by More Complex Product Ion Spectra

In conditions where terminal ions predominate, e.g., low dissociation yield or resonant excitation, the theoretical database scales linearly with peptide length. In conditions where sequential product ion fragmentation occurs, e.g., high fragmentation yield, internal fragment ions are produced and the number of possible fragment ions begins to scale quadratically with polypeptide length. This creates considerably more ambiguity in fragment ion assignments from top-down mass spectrometry (TDMS) compared to middle-down MS and in middle-down compared to bottom-up MS. The phenomenon of successive fragmentation arises broadly across fragmentation methods and is not limited to in-source collisional dissociation (ISD). Consistent with this observation, internal fragment ions have been reported from multiple fragmentation methods, including CID, HCD, ETD, and UVPD. At higher collisional energy, the prevalence of ambiguous fragment ions is even more pronounced. Previous studies examining a range of collisional energies (CEs) in in-source collisional dissociation (ISD) showed a progression of the fragmentation mechanism. At lower CE, cleavages occur predominantly at proline residues, followed by isoleucine/valine residues, and at higher CE, at aspartic/glutamic acid residues. These observations suggest that, as energy increases in ISD and CAD conditions, additional pathways become available to promote successive fragmentation. Similarly, improvements in fragmentation yield have occurred in electron, photon, and collisional dissociation. These improvements also increase the probability that successive cleavage, and therefore internal fragments, will be represented in nonresonant TDMS spectra.

Peak assignment (i.e., the correlation of a peak to a molecule) ambiguity, the focus of this work, can be taken into account by the scoring functions of peptide or proteoform spectral matching (PSMs and PrSMs). As a result, the correlation of a spectrum to a gene product (i.e., peptide or protein ID) tolerates considerable peak assignment ambiguity. The detailed structural characterization of polypeptides, however, cannot tolerate peak assignment ambiguity. The concerted effects of instrument improvements, larger polypeptide analytes, and intolerance to ambiguous assignment result in internal fragment assignment being one of the most vexing problems in TDMS data processing. We previously described three types of ambiguity to consider when assigning internal ions. The first involves assignments that can become unambiguous with sufficient “mass accuracy.” The second and third types involve isomers with “arrangement ambiguity,” including the subset with “frameshift ambiguity”. Such ions have identical mass-to-charge (m/z) ratios but can have different arrangements (e.g., primary sequences) and conformations. The formation of these isomers is complex, resulting from various fragmentation pathways and structural rearrangements, all leading to ions that are indistinguishable in the mass domain. Frameshift ambiguity is a special case with an amino acid flanking either the N- or C-terminus of a core sequence as in N AGAR or AGAR N , which we showed allows assignment of the core sequence. Since considerable ambiguity cannot be addressed by mass accuracy alone, additional separation strategies are required. Approaches such as MSn, ion mobility spectrometry (IMS), or their combinationas demonstrated in this studymay provide the orthogonal information needed to disambiguate fragment-ion assignments in large polypeptide spectra.

IM-MS Techniques for the Gas-Phase Separation of Isomers

Ion mobility-mass spectrometry (IM-MS) provides gas-phase separation of isomeric species, offering an orthogonal dimension of structural information beyond m/z. Advancements in ion mobility separation (IMS) platforms coupled with mass spectrometry (MS) permitted the separation of isomeric compounds such as nucleic acid, carbohydrates, − lipids, and peptides. For example, IMS platforms such as structures for lossless ion manipulation (SLIM) have separated isomeric synthetic peptide mixtures, including reverse peptide sequences, leucine/isoleucine modification, pentasaccharide isomers, and cis/trans lipid double bonds. Gaskell and colleagues used post-IM fragmentation [also referred to as mobility-aligned fragmentation (MAF)] to correlate the arrival time distribution (ATD) of mobilogram peaks with peptide sequence and conformer. In MAF, the typical workflow consists of in-source fragmentation, quadrupole isolation, IM separation, and subsequent fragmentation of each IM peak, with the resulting spectra collectively referred to as “MAF” spectra. They demonstrated the separation of synthetic isomeric peptides using IMS and by conducting post-IM fragmentation were able to elucidate their fragmentation pathways. Similarly, Smith and colleagues showed that isomeric synthetic peptide mixtures could be separated using IMS and identified by correlating experimental and theoretical collision cross-section (CCS) values. Collectively, these studies suggest that MAF could facilitate isomeric peak assignments.

To date, however, the IM separation and MAF-based sequencing of isomeric fragment ions derived from intact proteins have not been reported. This gap is significant because intact protein analysis produces dense TDMS spectra, where isomeric fragment ions are common. Current commercial high-resolution IM-MS instruments include drift tube ion mobility (DTIMS, Agilent 6560); − traveling wave ion mobility (TWIM, SYNAPT G2 Si-Waters); − cyclic TWIM (cIMS, Waters); , field asymmetric ion mobility (FAIMS, Thermo); ,, differential mobility spectrometry (DMS, Sciex); trapped ion mobility (TIMS, Bruker); , and structures for lossless ion manipulation (SLIM, MOBILion). A brief description of resolving power (arrival time/peak full width at half height) ranges of different IMS instruments is reported in Table S1. Like many of the high-resolution IMS instruments (e.g., SYNAPT, SLIM, Drift, TIMS), the cyclic IMS can separate synthetic isomeric peptide mixtures, as demonstrated by Shaw and colleagues. , Cyclic IMS offers tunable IMS resolving power and post-IM fragmentation and will be utilized here to separate and sequence ambiguous TDMS fragment ions formed in the source region (in-source dissociation). Accurate and sensitive assignment of fragment ions in TDMS, in particular the assignment of internal fragment ions, is currently an intractable problem. Isomeric ambiguity is the largest challenge because it cannot be addressed at any mass resolving power, i.e., it is immune to instrument improvements, and because it requires additional stages of separation or fragmentation. Here, we test the hypothesis that MAF can contribute to the assignment of ambiguous TDMS fragment ions produced in the source region (in-source dissociation).

Methods

Sample Preparation

Bovine ubiquitin (8.6 kDa) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). A stock solution at a 1.0 mg/mL concentration was prepared in 18.2 MΩ water (MilliPore). The stock solution was further diluted to a 1.0 μM concentration in a 50:50 water:acetonitrile (Fisher Optima) solution containing 0.1% formic acid (Fisher Optima) prior to direct infusion into the mass spectrometer.

Mass Spectrometry and IMS Analysis

The top-down MS experiments were performed using a SELECT SERIES Cyclic IMS mass spectrometer (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA). The instrument was calibrated using sodium formate ion clusters from 50 to 2000 m/z. The protein sample was directly infused at a flow rate of 10 μL/min via a standard electrospray source using a capillary voltage of 2.0 kV. Collisional activated dissociation energy ranging from 100 to 120 V was applied in the source (StepWave) to generate a pseudo-MS2 spectrum with a high abundance of terminal and internal ions. Abundant fragment ions such as b13-, b14-, y8-, y9-, y10-, y11-, y12-, b12-H2O-, and yb- (internal ion) ions were isolated. The quadrupole was centered on a single isotopologue peak using an isolation window of ∼0.6 m/z to minimize interference from other product ions, before each IMS experiment was performed.

To optimize mobility separation, different numbers of passes around the cyclic racetrack were attempted for ions that were to be subjected to MAF, with the objectives of maximizing separation while ensuring the entire conformational space of a given ion was represented. For example, y-ions, which had few conformers, could traverse the cyclic racetrack (0.98 m) 20 passes, while internal ions, which had more conformers, could only traverse 3–10 passes before conformers were excluded. Each MS2-IMS experiment was collected for a duration of 2 min, and the scans were summed for further analysis. Some fragment ions, e.g., those where faster ions could “lap” and therefore overlap with slower ions, were subjected to multiple levels of IMS (IMSn) through the utilization of IMS slicing. Following the isolation (IMSn) and ion mobility experiment, the precursors of interest were subjected to mobility-aligned fragmentation using collisional activated dissociation at the MS3 level in the post-IM transfer segmented quadrupole collision cell (XS-transfer device).

Data Analysis

The pseudo-MS2 spectra were deconvoluted using MaxEnt3 in MassLynx (v4.2, Waters Corporation). The deconvoluted mass list was searched for matches against a theoretical mass list of ubiquitin comprised of all b-ions, y-ions, and yb-ions using Protein Prospector. Putative assignment of an experimental mass to a theoretical mass (or set of theoretical masses) required no more than ±5 ppm deviation. After mass-based assignments and manual inspection, abundant and ambiguous terminal and internal ions were selected for further analysis. For each of these isolated ions, IM was performed and the full arrival time distribution (ATD) was determined to analyze the purity and mass­(es) of the precursor(s). The mobility peaks (arrival time distribution) at the pseudo-MS2 level and MS3 (i.e., MAF) level are slightly shifted by the collision voltage and therefore require alignment. The experimental gain in the MS2 and MS3 raw data was determined by applying automatic peak detection to the lock-mass (leucine-enkephalin) channel, and the raw data were subsequently corrected by adjusting the gain. The full ATDs at the MS2 level and the MS3 (MAF) level were first aligned. At the ATD-MS3 level, ∼0.25 ms window from each separated mobility peak were summed to obtain a single MAF spectrum. The resulting MAF spectrum was then deconvoluted using MaxEnt 3 (MassLynx) with parameters appropriate to the precursor mass and charge and the mass resolving power. For low S/N precursors, we observed slightly diminished mass accuracy for pseudo-MS2-IM-MS3 (MAF) compared to pseudo-MS2. To account for this and improve the accuracy of assignments, the following process was employed. First, the following fragment ions: b-, y-, a-, and yb-ions, as well as neutral loss of H2O and NH3 for each ion type, were assigned automatically using Protein Prospector with a ±20 ppm cutoff. The fragment ions were assigned with both automatic and manual inspection. The peak annotations were performed manually in all the figures. For each fragment ion that was assigned, the deviation between theoretical and experimental mass was calculated in ppm, and the spectra were recalibrated by subtracting the average ppm deviation within a spectrumeffectively a one-point calibration. This process had a marginal effect on abundant (e.g., y-ions), but improved the mass accuracy of lower abundant ions (e.g., b- and internal ions) by up to 3-fold. Next, the standard deviation of the mass accuracy for all assigned ions within the recalibrated spectrum was calculated. Only MAF peaks that were within ±2 standard deviations of this were considered to be assigned peaks. Throughout this study, MAF spectra were annotated such that all peaks greater than 5% of base peak intensity were assigned. In addition to the ion types that were automatically assigned above, manual assignment was necessary to meet the 5% abundance criteria above for the following: for two precursors (b12-H2O-ion at m/z 665.8826 and internal ion at m/z 545.3369) to account for the loss of acetyl groups from y-ions and for one precursor (m/z 550.0003, Supporting Information, Figure S3) to account for the ab-type ion. For b-ions, y-ions, and internal ions, the average mass deviation for MAF assignments throughout this study was ±7 ppm, ±5 ppm, and ±6 ppm, respectively. The 2D heatmaps for the MAF spectra were generated using Driftscope (v3.0, Waters Corporation).

Results and Discussion

A peak is “unambiguously assigned” in situations where the database contains all the ions that could be observed in an MSn spectrum (e.g., yb-, a-, b-, y-ion masses) and only one database entry exists within a given peak’s experimentally determined window of certainty (e.g., mass ± 3σ). The alternative to this situation, i.e., disambiguation, is the focus of this work. Specifically, we focus on accurately assigning one MS peak to a database window containing two (or more) molecules. One should not assume disambiguation is being achieved when peak assignments are made using PSM techniques. For example, in dense spectra like TDMS, ambiguous and unambiguous peak assignments can be convoluted by the algorithm into one PSM score. Or, as is often the case, the problem can be ignored at the user level by limiting peak assignment only to terminal fragment ions. We will address whether IM alone or IM followed by fragmentation can disambiguate/assign fragment ions.

If positional isomers can be resolved by IM, IM together with a theoretical CCS database may be able to yield accurate peak assignments, thereby obviating the need for MS/MS. However, many studies show that certain b- ,− and y-type fragment ions exist in multiple conformational isomers, which requires advanced MD/MM calculations to simulate and complicates spectra when two ions overlap. If this is commonplace, then it is likely that IM will need to be followed by MS/MS. We therefore begin by surveying the conformational space of unambiguously assigned b- and y-type ions. Next, we disambiguated peaks using ion mobility-aligned fragmentation (MAF).

The MAF workflow used throughout this study involves intact protein MS → in-source dissociation MS (pseudo-MS2) → peak assignment → mass selecting a (e.g., ambiguously assigned) terminal and/or internal fragment ion → performing IM experiments to separate positional and conformational isomers → performing MS/MS on all isomers → unambiguously assigning MS/MS spectra to sequence (s) (Figure ). We used ubiquitin to develop our experimental workflow because it is the simplest and most extensively characterized protein for benchmarking new TDMS approaches.

Schematic of the workflow for this study. (a) MS to determine proteoform mass; (b) in-source dissociation (pseudo-MS2) to determine fragment ion masses, unambiguously assign fragment ions by mass where possible, and identify the subset of ambiguous assignments (peaks matching multiple molecules/sequences). The following additional steps must be performed for ambiguous assignments: (c) quadrupole isolation, (d) IM, and (e) MAF (post-IM fragmentation) of individual mobility peaks and sequence assignment to disambiguate.

MAF Analysis of Ubiquitin

In-source dissociation (pseudo-MS2) of ubiquitin (Ub) generated ions that were putatively assigned as terminal (ISCAD 50 v, 51 terminal ions) and internal ions (280 internal ions) (Figure ). Most of the ambiguity involves internal ions because Ub can create only 150 terminal ions compared to 2701 internal ions. Certain ions were selected for further analysis. First, relatively intense, unambiguously assigned terminal ions were analyzed as controls. Second, ambiguous fragment ions were analyzed. Fragment ions from pseudo-MS2 were isolated in the quadrupole region and analyzed by cyclic IM-MS and IM-MS/MS. Post ion mobility fragmentation experiments were enabled by XS transfer optics, which is a gas-filled segmented quadrupole that performs collision-induced dissociation. When interference from ions other than the intended precursor was observed, precursors were further isolated using IMSn slicing. Separation times varied between 1 and 20 cycles and were tailored to maximize IM separation while still encompassing the entire conformational space of a given precursor. MAF spectra were assigned to a particular sequence (Figure ). For the purposes of this manuscript, “conformation” will be used synonymously with “a resolved IMS peak” and therefore loosely represents a local conformational minimum. Nonresonant CAD was used here, and therefore, the number of fragmentation events that led to a particular fragment ion (e.g., 1 event = primary fragment ion, 2 = secondary ion, and so on) is often uncoupled with (and greater than) the stage of MS. For example, all MAF spectra, which are nominally MS3, must contain tertiary fragments; did also contain quaternary fragments; and probably contain even higher order fragments.

y-Ions (y8–y12) Occupied a Single Dominant Conformation, and Upon Collisional Activation, Larger y-Ions Produce Relatively More Internal Ions (Figure )

The most intense fragment ions in pseudo-MS2 data of Ub could be unambiguously assigned, by mass alone, as y-ions. Among these, y8–y12 were particularly intense and were therefore selected for MAF analysis (Figure ). The ion mobility peaks for each of the ions did not separate after 20 passes, consistent with a dominant conformation for each y-ion. MAF analysis led to a single sequence assignment for each y-ion; importantly, the same sequence was unambiguously assigned using only mass. In addition to canonical mobile proton/charge-directed pathways generating b-/y-ion pairs, interesting noncanonical fragmentation was observed in the MAF spectrum of every y-ion. For example, the fragments of y-ions exhibited an increased neutral loss of ammonia from secondary y-ions. They also exhibited significantly increased a-ion intensity, including atypically large relative intensity compared to their corresponding b-ion intensity. Such loss of ammonia has been associated with the following mechanism: proton transfer and rearrangement to form a cyclic intermediate between the two N-terminal-most amino acids, which then rearranges to lose CO, which can either form an a-/y-ion combination (i.e., noncanonical “a-y” fragmentation pathway) or rearrange and lose NH3. As detailed in the legend, the ions labeled as a- or b-ions in Figure are indexed to their pseudo-MS2 precursor’s N-terminus, not to that of intact ubiquitin. This creates more intuitive annotations that are analogous to the typical MS2 analysis of a peptide. Note, however, these peaks (e.g., fragments of a y-ion labeled as b- or a-ions) are technically internal ions, such that the y12 product ion labeled as b2 in Figure is i65–66 when indexed with respect to the intact Ub. The high IMS resolving power, high quantity of MSn peak assignments, and consistency between precursor mass-based and MSn-based assignments in Figure indicated MAF techniques could be applied to the cases below to resolve ambiguity in precursor ion assignment.

Mobility-Resolved b-Ions Exhibit Multiple Conformations with Distinguishable Fragmentation Patterns

Unambiguous b-ions, as assigned by mass from the pseudo-MS2 spectrum, specifically, b13 and b14, were selected for IM-MS analysis (Figure ). Multiple mobility peaks were separated and even after only one pass showed at least two conformations for each b-ion. The 2D MAF heatmap showed the most intense fragment ions aligned with the mobility peaks, as expected, as well as similar MAF spectra for both mobility peaks. b13- and b14-ions showed a high abundance of precursors in mobility peak I compared to peak II, presumably due to the higher stability of the associated conformation. Based upon the work of Gaskell and colleagues indicating that cyclic peptide ions have smaller CCS values than their linear isomers and the work of Clemmer and colleagues indicating that cyclic peptides exhibit decreased fragmentation yield compared to linear peptides, we assigned mobility peak I as a cyclic conformation and peak II as the linear conformation. High-resolution IM at five passes was also performed (Figure S1) and revealed 2 or 3 additional conformations for each b-ion. Consistent with previous work, our MAF analysis indicates that b-ions are conformationally complex, which will complicate their disambiguation using techniques that correlate theoretical and experimental CCS values.

MAF-Based Disambiguation of Frameshift Ambiguous Internal Ions

An ambiguous ion at m/z 545.3369 from the pseudo-MS2 spectrum of ubiquitin could be assigned to either (or both) of the two frameshift ambiguous sequences (sequence 1 = IFVKTLTGK and sequence 2 = FVKTLTGKTI, Figure ). This precursor was isolated within the quadrupole, and single-pass IMS was performed. Two mobility peaks were observed, including one composed of an unintended, coisolating precursor at m/z 546.2552. To minimize interference from the m/z 546.2552 ion, an MSn slicing experiment was performed (Figure S2), and the intended precursor was then subjected to five IM passes. This resulted in the observation of five mobility peaks, with peaks II, III, IV, and V resulting from the intended m/z 545.3369 precursor. The relatively small peak I resulted from a coisolating precursor at m/z 544.9911 that could be unambiguously assigned as an internal a15-ion (FVKTLTGKTITLEVE-CO) based on mass alone (theoretical 1632.9571 Da vs experimental 1632.9577 Da, 0.4 ppm). MAF analysis was performed, and spectra were assigned for each mobility peak, confirming the internal a15 assignment for peak I. Notably, mobility peaks II, III, IV, and V showed the presence of both isomeric sequences (IFVKTLTGK and FVKTLTGKTI). The relative abundance of sequence 1 (based upon the summed abundance of unique peaks) is seven times higher than sequence 2 in mobility peak II, eight times higher in peaks III and V, and equal in peak IV, respectively. MAF analysis is therefore consistent with the presence of two distinct isomeric sequences, in different relative abundances (∼7-fold higher IFVKTLTGK compared to FVKTLTGKTI), sharing similar conformations, albeit with different relative occupancy. For example, FVKTLTGKTI has relatively higher occupancy in the conformation corresponding to mobility peak IV.

Identification of Ion Types That Are Not Accounted for in the Theoretical Database

The remaining analyses address the trade-off between limiting false-positive peak assignments and limiting unassigned peaks. The assignment of internal ions creates a larger theoretical database (18-fold larger for Ub). This is exacerbated by accounting for additional ion types, e.g., loss of water and ammonia. For this reason, only (b-, y-, and yb-) ion types were accounted for in the theoretical database in the initial assignment of the pseudo-MS2 spectrum. In the first example, a peak at m/z 550.0003 at S/N = 336 (Figure S3) was not assigned in the pseudo-MS2 analysis yet was observed throughout method development (e.g., modulating the in-source collision energy). The precursor could be assigned by mass to an a15-ion sequence (MQIFVKTLTGKTITL-CO, 0.7 ppm) or isomeric internal ions ENVKAKIQDKEGIPP and VKAKIQDKEGIPPDQ (47 ppm mass error). While two of these three could be distinguished in Fourier transform instruments, at this Q-ToF’s resolving power (∼63,459), these peaks are convoluted. Whereas the 550.0003 peak could not be disambiguated by mass alone, MAF analysis confirmed only the a15-ion sequence was responsible for both mobility peaks. Our results were also consistent with findings from previous studies, which showed the existence of multiple conformations for a-ions. ,

In a second example, an intense precursor ion (S/N= 748) at m/z 665.8845 was observed in the pseudo-MS2 experiment shown in Figure . By the criteria we followed for pseudo-MS2 assignment (assign by mass if within 5 ppm of theoretical b-, y-, and yb-ions), this would have been unambiguously assigned to an internal ion at m/z 665.9052 (QIFVKTLTGKTI, 5 ppm). However, following quadrupole isolation and a five-pass IM experiment, two precursor ions were observed. These precursors were within 5 ppm and were therefore convoluted in the pseudo-MS2 spectrum. The first precursor, at m/z 665.8826, was associated with three distinct mobility peaks (I, II, and III). The second precursor at m/z 665.9052 was associated with three different, low-abundance mobility peaks (IV, V, and VI). Each of the six mobility peaks was analyzed using MAF, and their spectra were assigned. Precursor mass- and MS/MS-based assignments for the more abundant precursor at m/z 665.8826 were consistent with a b12-H2O-ion (MQIFVKTLTGKT, 1 ppm) and for the m/z 665.9052 precursor were consistent with the internal ion assigned above (QIFVKTLTGKTI, 5 ppm). Extracted ATDs were generated for both the b12-H2O- and internal ion, which indicated that the b12-H2O-ion exhibited ∼11-times the signal intensity of the internal ion. These results, particularly the ability to separate a single pseudo-MS2 peak into multiple ion mobility peaks, showcase the separation capability of IM. These results also demonstrate that false-positive identifications can result from failure to expand the theoretical database to account for all experimentally observed ion types.

Increasing Mechanistic Insight into Fragmentation Using MAF

The goal of this study was to develop IM or MAP methods for disambiguation. A common theme was that the MAF MSn assignments provide a wealth of information on the fragmentation mechanism. A few highlights were given above, and for those interested in peptide fragmentation mechanisms, we’ve included in-depth analysis in Supplementary. MAF spectra have interesting qualitative differences that are consistent with previously described mechanisms involving b-ion cyclization and threonine-dependent loss of water and acetyl groups. For example, in the Figure right panel, MS3-extracted ATDs for precursor 1 illustrate the loss of one and two water molecules and one acetyl group from y12-, y10-, and y9-ions. Notably, the less compact conformers were associated with the loss of more water groups and an acetyl group during fragmentation. Previous studies showed that water and acetyl loss proceed through intermediate cyclization and iare promoted by having a threonine near the C-terminus. This, together with MAF analysis detailed in Figure S4a, and Supplementary Mastersheet_MS3_spectra, leads to the following interpretation of precursor 1’s fragmentation mechanism. All conformations lost their first water at T12, all conformations lost more of their second water from T9 than from T7, and IM peak III lost both water and acetyl at T12.

Conclusion

This study began by testing the ability of IMaloneto disambiguate fragment ions. Unfortunately, the consistent observation of multiple conformations for b-, a-, and yb-type ions makes this intractable for laboratories like ours. As others have shown and we show here, MSn analysis is complicated by the tendency of isomers with different sequences to rearrange in the gas phase and dissociate into many of the same secondary fragment ions. For laboratories capable of generating and comparing the “CCS fingerprints” of fragment ions, e.g., of using MD simulations at a high level of theory, it may be possible. MAF, on the other hand, was able to disambiguate the many examples shown herealbeit in a process that currently requires considerable manual interpretation. An alternative approach to CCS-enabled IMS could be incorporation of different fragmentation methods after mobility separation such as electron capture dissociation (ECD), which has been shown to reduce b-ion water loss artifacts including isobaric aspartate residue disambiguation, thereby reducing manual data interpretation complexities. Overall, this work is one in a series that will challenge the current logic used for “disambiguation” in the context of peak assignment, namely, that a peak represents a single precursor molecule and therefore should only be assigned to one sequence from the database. The ambiguity of fragment ions as a function of protein length is generalizable and more pronounced for larger proteins. The techniques addressed here should also be applicable to middle-down and bottom-up techniques because these exhibit relatively less ambiguity by virtue of generating relatively fewer internal ions.
