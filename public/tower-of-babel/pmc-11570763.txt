What Are the Research Highlights of Periprosthetic Joint Infections From the 100 Most Cited Studies?
Authors: Marcos R Gonzalez, Jose I Acosta, Joshua Davis, Felipe Larios, Adam S Olsen, Antonia F Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeriprosthetic joint infections (PJIs) are a serious complication after total joint arthroplasty. Our study aimed to analyze the characteristics and research highlights of the top 100 most cited studies on PJI from an orthopaedic standpoint. Moreover, we sought to assess whether there has been a change in the level of evidence (LOE) throughout time.MethodsWe conducted a search of the PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science databases to identify the top 100 studies primarily focusing on PJI. Study characteristics assessed included publication year, LOE, journal of publication, and type of study. Research highlights of the included studies were classified into 6 sections. Linear regression was employed to assess correlation between LOE and publication year.ResultsThe aggregated citation count of all studies was 19,558 and the median citation number was 129.5. While cohort studies were the most prevalent, articles focusing on analysis of costs associated with PJI care or PJI definition garnered the highest yearly citation counts. The bulk of included studies were concentrated in 3 orthopaedic journals and published in the 2010s. The majority of studies had a LOE II (30%) or III (34%). On regression analysis, LOE was not correlated with publication year (Pearson’s r = 0.013, P = .61).ConclusionsThe most cited PJI articles assessed the definition of PJI and PJI cost. Despite growing interest in PJI, the majority of studies had LOE II or III, highlighting the difficulty of conducting prospective randomized controlled trials in PJI patients.Level of evidenceIV.Keywords: Periprosthetic joint infection, Prosthetic joint infection, Total knee arthroplasty, Total hip arthroplasty

Introduction

Total hip arthroplasty (THA) and total knee arthroplasty (TKA) are elective surgical procedures with excellent long-term results [1]. Periprosthetic joint infections (PJIs), however, present a serious and challenging complication of both procedures. The annual incidence of PJI after primary TKA and THA is approximately 1 and 1%-2%, respectively [2,3]. As the number of joint arthroplasty procedures continues to rise, the annual hospital costs related to PJI in the hip and knee have been estimated to be $1.85 billion by 2030 [4].

In the last 2 decades, there have been significant advances in the diagnosis and management of PJI. Notably, several societies have developed guidelines to better standardize the diagnosis of PJI, including the 2011 Musculoskeletal Infection Society (MSIS) criteria [5], the 2018 International Consensus Meeting criteria [6], and the 2021 European Bone and Joint Infection Society (EBJIS) criteria [7]. With regard to management, increased focus on interdisciplinary management and selection of the optimal strategy based on microorganisms and antibiotic susceptibility have led to successful treatment rates above 90% for infected THA and TKA [[8], [9], [10], [11]]. Despite progress made, effectively managing this condition remains challenging, and microbiologic failure rates of up to 40% after one- or two-stage revision have been reported in the literature [[12], [13], [14], [15]].

Publications play an essential role in the dissemination of information, and highly cited articles often help guide and improve disciplinary development [16]. While the number of publications has increased substantially since the 2000s, a few landmark studies have been critical in establishing the research trends in the subject. Additionally, it still remains uncertain whether the rise in PJI publication volume correlates with an improvement in the level of evidence (LOE) of published articles. Therefore, our study sought to (1) analyze the characteristics of the 100 most cited publications about PJI from an orthopaedic standpoint, (2) provide an overview of their research highlights, and (3) assess whether there has been a change in the LOE throughout time.

Material and methods

Search strategy

A first search of the Science Citation Index Expanded database of the Web of Science (WOS) Core Collection data was conducted on June 15, 2023, using the following search terms: "prosthetic joint infection" OR "periprosthetic joint infection" OR "PJI." This was followed by a second, expanded search of the PubMed, Embase, and WOS libraries on October 24, 2023. No language restrictions were imposed on our search.

Article screening

Our search resulted in a total of 5887, 7919, and 3393 studies from the PubMed, Embase, and WOS libraries, respectively (Fig. 1). After removing duplicates, the remaining 9644 studies were ranked based on the citation number. We established a cutoff value of greater than 100 citations, which excluded 9478 studies from our pool of potentially eligible studies. The chosen cutoff was arbitrarily selected in order to obtain a number slightly above 100 results, our target number of included studies. Titles and abstracts of the remaining studies were then independently screened by 2 reviewers (F.L. and J.D.) to confirm that the primary focus of the study was on PJI. In cases of disagreement between reviewers, the senior author was consulted, and a final decision was made through consensus. We did not exclude any articles based on a study’s methodology (eg, original article and systematic review). However, articles were excluded for the following reasons: focus on PJI from a nonorthopaedic standpoint; PJI was not the primary study focus; the study focused on orthopaedic infections broadly; and the study focused solely on shoulder PJI. After reviewing the 166 results obtained, we ultimately included the top 100 articles in our study.

Flowchart of the identification of relevant articles.

Data extraction

Manuscripts of the 100 included articles were manually reviewed, and data were extracted into a standardized sheet. The following variables were extracted from each study: year and decade of publication, manuscript name, LOE, journal of publication, citation count, and type of study. LOE was evaluated following the guidelines established by the Journal of Bone and Joint Surgery [17]. The following study designs were established before the start of the study: (1) cohort study, (2) controlled trial, (3) review (narrative or systematic), (4) large database study, (5) definition of PJI study, (6) cost analysis study, and (7) other.

We defined the following areas to cover in the Research highlights section: (1) epidemiology and cost analysis; (2) definition and diagnosis criteria; (3) laboratory tests; (4) prevention and risk factors for infection and/or recurrence; (5) treatment; and (6) complications. All 100 manuscripts were independently reviewed by 2 authors (M.R.G. and F.L.) to classify the main topic of each study and retrieve the key findings.

Statistical analysis

The correlation between LOE and year of publication was assessed using linear regression. The correlation coefficient (Pearson’s r) and coefficient of determination (R-squared) were calculated. A P-value ≤.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using StataSE 14 (StataCorp, College Station, TX).

Results

Characteristics of included studies

All articles included in our study were written in English (Supplementary Table 1). The total citation count ranged from 83 to 1131. The aggregated citation count of all studies was 19,558 and the median number of citations was 129.5. Median yearly citation count was 16.

Articles included were published from 1995 to 2021. Most studies (82%) were published in the 2010s, followed by the 2000s (14%) and 2020s (3%). Only one study (1%) was published in the 1990s. The included articles were published in 12 journals. The largest share of included articles was published in Clinical Orthopaedics and Related Research (35%), followed by the Journal of Arthroplasty (23%) and the Journal of Bone and Joint Surgery (20%) (Table 1). The main study methodologies were retrospective cohorts (26%), large database studies (22%), and prospective cohorts (16%) (Table 2). Studies using large, national databases accounted for 22% of the included articles, and 15% were review articles (narrative or systematic). Studies establishing a definition or diagnosis criteria for PJI and those focusing on the cost of PJI had the highest median yearly citation (34 and 23, respectively) (Table 2).

Highlights

Epidemiology and cost analysis

The 2 most cited studies on PJI focused on its incidence and associated costs. With regard to incidence, Pulido et al. reviewed 9245 patients from an arthroplasty registry and reported a PJI incidence of 0.7% [18]. The authors also reported a PJI incidence of 1.1% and 0.3% after TKA and THA, respectively. Additional studies on PJI incidence have reported an incidence rate of late PJI of 0.07% per prosthesis-year for TKA and THA [19], and a 5-year cumulative incidence of 0.64% for THA [20]. Kamath et al. quantified the burden of revision total joint arthroplasty for PJI and found that PJI was the first and third cause of revision TKA and THA, respectively [21]. From a cost perspective, Kurtz et al. reported that the annual cost of infected revisions in the United States increased from $320 million in 2001 to $566 million in 2009 and was projected to exceed $1.62 billion by 2020 [22].

Definition and diagnosis criteria

With regard to diagnosis, Parvizi et al. demonstrated significant variability in sensitivity (54%-100%), specificity (39%-100%), and accuracy (53%-100%) using 6 different published sets of diagnostic criteria [23]. In this context, several diagnostic criteria developed by consensus emerged, including the 2018 International Consensus Meeting criteria [6], the 2011 MSIS criteria [5], and the 2021 EBJIS criteria [7]. While these criteria are applied worldwide to diagnose PJI, several modifications to them have been implemented, such as the addition of the leukocyte esterase test as a minor criterion to the 2011 MSIS criteria [24] and alpha-defensin to the 2021 EBJIS criteria [7]. Moreover, additional publications derived from consensus meetings have established research priorities on PJI diagnosis [25] and questioned whether these diagnosis criteria can be universally applied to both hip and knee PJI [26].

Laboratory tests

The utility of laboratory tests, including inflammatory blood markers, has been highlighted by Berbari et al [27]. In this study, the authors reported that the diagnostic accuracy for PJI was best for interleukin-6, followed by C-reactive protein, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, and white blood cell count. Finding biomarkers for PJI have been a considerable research interest, with a study by Deirmengian et al. finding 100% sensitivity and specificity for 5 biomarkers (human α-defensin 1-3, neutrophil elastase 2, bactericidal/permeability-increasing protein, neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin, and lactoferrin) [28]. Other studies have highlighted the accuracy of synovial fluid α-defensin, reporting a 100% sensitivity and specificity for the diagnosis of PJI [29]. Emphasis has also been placed on the leukocyte esterase strip test, underscoring its ease of use and good accuracy [[29], [30], [31], [32]]. A systematic review by Wyatt et al. reported a PJI pooled diagnostic sensitivity and specificity of leukocyte esterase of 81% and 97%, respectively [30].

Prevention and risk factors for infection and/or recurrence

With the increase in PJI cases, studies have also focused on identifying risk factors for infection and treatment failure. In an analysis of the Medicare population, Kurtz et al. identified higher Charlson comorbidity index, male sex, subsidized premium, and longer operative time as risk factors for PJI after TKA [3]. In addition, Maoz et al. reported that a body mass index ≥40 kg/m2, operative time >115 minutes, nonsame-day surgery, and revision surgery were independent risk factors for PJI [33]. Other studies have focused on PJI microbiology, reporting that PJI caused by methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus [34] or Gram-negative bacteria [35] is at higher risk of failure. Therefore, several studies have focused on PJI prevention strategies, highlighting the use of proper wound care, preoperative patient optimization, and modification of the operative room environment (e.g, laminar airflow) [36,37].

Treatment

Development and standardization of an effective surgical strategy for PJI have been key objectives in the field of arthroplasty. In a cohort of 202 infected THA treated with two-stage revision, Berend et al. reported an 83% infection control rate among reimplanted patients [38]. Mortazavi et al. reported that 28% of patients with infected THA treated with two-stage revision underwent reoperation due to reinfection after reimplantation [39]. Additional studies reported infection control rates above 80% for infected TKA and THA, thus setting the stage for two-stage revision to be considered a standard for treating PJI [40,41]. With regard to alternative strategies, Haddad et al. reported similar infection recurrence rates between single-stage (0%) and two-stage (7%) revision arthroplasty in chronic knee PJI [42]. Finally, limited success has been reported with the use of debridement, antibiotics, and implant retention (DAIR) [[43], [44], [45]]. Deirmengian et al. found that only 35% of patients treated with DAIR successfully retained their components at the latest follow-up [45]. Other studies with higher sample sizes reported PJI treatment success rates of 44% [44] and 66% [43] for DAIR.

Complications

Despite advances in PJI diagnosis and management, this condition still poses a significant risk of complications, including death. Zmistowski et al. reported that mortality after revision for PJI was 3.7% at 90 days, 10.6% at one year, and 25.9% at 5 years, which was similar to the rate of mortality of colorectal, cervical, and head and neck cancer [46]. In agreement with these values, Gundtoft et al. reported that 8% of patients who underwent revision for PJI died within the year [47]. In a more recent systematic review, Natsuhara et al. found that 5-year mortality after revision for hip PJI was 21.1% [48]. While these numbers showcase the severity of PJI, Kurtz et al. reported that the risk of mortality after PJI has been decreasing between 2005 and 2015, likely due to advances in the field [49].

Level of evidence and publication year

We identified six articles with level I evidence, 30 with level II evidence, 34 with level III evidence, 11 with level IV evidence, and 8 with level V evidence (Fig. 2a). Median total citation counts were 148 (interquartile range [IQR] 119-853) for level I studies, 193 (IQR 107-335) for level II studies, 114.5 (IQR 103-136) for level III studies, 138 (IQR 120-181) for level IV studies, and 141 (IQR 121.5-179.5) for level V studies. There was a trend toward higher median total citation counts in articles with level II evidence compared to other groups (P = .052) (Fig. 2b).

On our regression analysis, there was no correlation between LOE and year of publication (Pearson’s r = 0.013, P = .61) (Fig. 3). This analysis indicated that a greater time period was not accompanied by an increase in the LOE.

Discussion

PJI, a severe complication after total joint arthroplasty, has seen a significant increase in publications over the last 2 decades. While some studies have examined the characteristics of highly cited papers in the PJI literature using bibliometrics [16,50], they lack practical insights for orthopaedic surgeons and focus solely on citation metrics. In contrast, our study aimed to outline the key findings of the top 100 most cited studies and evaluate whether the rise in peer-reviewed publications on PJI corresponded to enhanced study quality. Our analysis revealed that the majority of the top 100 most cited studies were cohort studies published primarily in 3 orthopaedic journals. Additionally, the most common topics of PJI publications encompassed the epidemiology, costs, definition and diagnosis, laboratory tests, prevention and risk factors for infection or recurrence, treatment, and complications of PJI.

Most studies on the epidemiology and costs associated with PJI relied on large national databases [[20], [21], [22]]. While the use of large databases has been criticized due to their lack of granular data, our study showed that valuable insight on economic trends and epidemiologic metrics can be obtained. With regard to the definition and diagnosis of PJI, multiple sets of criteria by different societies were established [[5], [6], [7],23]; however, only one assessed the diagnostic performance of the newly established criteria in an external cohort of patients [6]. Laboratory testing mainly focused on inflammatory markers, with particular interest in synovial α-defensin and leukocyte esterase. Well-designed studies assessing the performance of these markers were widely cited due to their reported excellent diagnostic accuracy, accessibility, and ease of use [[28], [29], [30], [31]]. Interestingly, all evidence on current surgical treatment for PJI stemmed from retrospective or prospective studies with limited sample sizes [38,39,[42], [43], [44]]. Although two-stage exchange revision is often considered the gold standard in the treatment of PJI, none of the landmark studies have demonstrated superior treatment success rates compared to single-stage exchange arthroplasty. Despite the increase in PJI publication volume over time, we did not see a corresponding increase in LOE. This highlights the fact that, although the management of PJI has evolved, the evidence supporting these changes is mostly derived from LOE II or III studies. Since the incidence of PJI is low, conducting prospective PJI studies requires a large number of patients. While conducting randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in PJI remains challenging, a recent pragmatic, parallel group, open-label RCT from the UK demonstrated the feasibility of such a study [51]. In this study, the authors compared patient-reported outcomes after single-stage and two-stage revision for hip PJI and found no superiority in patient-reported outcome at 18 months in the single-stage group [51].

Our study had several limitations. First, we restricted our analysis to the top 100 most cited papers on PJI. This could introduce selection bias, potentially making our sample not representative of all PJI literature. Second, our analysis was limited to studies focusing on PJI from the orthopaedic standpoint. This strategy was decided prior to the beginning of the study to avoid having an overabundance of papers focusing on the microbiology or medical management of PJI, which are usually submitted to medical journals with different impact factors and may have higher citation counts. Third, our selection of articles relied solely on the total citation count. However, there are alternative metrics, such as the Research Interest score and Altmetric score, designed to gauge publication impact. We decided to base our analyses purely on citation count due to its long-standing track record, particularly within the orthopaedic literature.

Conclusions

We performed the first comprehensive analysis of the PJI literature from the orthopaedic perspective. Most studies included had LOE II or III. Furthermore, our study described the research highlights of the top 100 most cited papers and found that there was no correlation between publication time and LOE. This last finding highlights the challenges of performing high-quality RCT in PJI, as well as the reliance on LOE II or III studies to establish diagnosis and management guidelines for PJI.
