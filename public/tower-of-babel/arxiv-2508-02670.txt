atommovr: An open-source simulation framework for rearrangement in atomic arrays
Nikhil K. Harle, Bo-Yu Chen, Bob Bao, Hannes Bernien

Abstract
The task of atom rearrangement has emerged in the last decade as a fundamental building block for the development of neutral atom-based quantum processors. However, despite many recent efforts to develop algorithms with favorable asymptotic scaling, no time-optimal algorithm has been developed for any rearrangement task. Moreover, no open-source code exists to reproduce or benchmark existing algorithms, and to assist the development of new rearrangement protocols. To address this deficiency, we develop an open-source simulation framework for developing, comparing, and benchmarking algorithms under realistic and customizable noise models. Using this framework, we a) numerically extract lower bounds for the scaling of a time-optimal rearrangement algorithm and compare it to existing heuristic algorithms b) develop a naive dual-species algorithm able to prepare arbitrary targets with near-unity success rate. With this framework, we hope to develop a common tool for the community to study rearrangement, lower the barrier to entry for new experimental groups, and stimulate progress in developing algorithms which approach time-optimal scaling. Background

In recent years, the field of quantum computing has welcomed a new platform: quantum processors comprised of individual atoms trapped in optical tweezers
Lee et al. 2016
;
Barredo et al. 2016
;

;

Atomic quantum processors are versatile and flexible platforms whose utility ranges from exploring fundamental physics to implementing early paradigms of quantum computing

;

;
Zhang et al. 2025a

A key subprocess of neutral atom processors is the physical rearrangement of optical tweezers to move atoms into particular geometric configurations (Fig.

The flexibility of target geometry is a key advantage of atomic quantum processors over solid-state quantum processors, such as superconducting qubits.
This ability, coupled with the ability to rearrange atoms in the middle of an experimental cycle, opens many possibilities, such as realizing many-body observables in quantum simulation and shuttling atoms between zones in various error correction schemes

;

In addition, rearrangement can be leveraged to realize effective cooling by removing entropy

As atomic quantum processors continue to mature and grow in size, so too does the need for efficient and scalable rearrangement algorithms.
The probability of successfully generating a defect-free atom array decreases sharply with the number of atoms.
For example, suppose that the vacuum-limited lifetime of an atom is
t_{\text{v}}
.
The probability of the atom remaining in the tweezer after time
t
is
P_{\text{survival}}=\exp{(-t/t_{\text{v}})}
. Given
N
atoms, the probability of developing a defect increases exponentially with the number of atoms:
P_{\text{lost}}=1-P_{\text{survival}}^{N}=1-\exp{(-Nt/t_{\text{v}})}
.
Currently, typical vacuum-limited lifetimes are on the order of
10-100~\text{s}

Schymik et al. 2020
, although recent experiments with optimized vacuum design have yielded times in excess of
1000~\text{s}

Zhang et al. 2025b
;
Pichard et al. 2024
.
Under current vacuum conditions, the lifetime of a 1000-atom array is limited to only
7
–
70~\text{ms}
, corresponding to a defect-free probability of approximately 50%.
Moreover, the scaling is not favorable: a linear increase in vacuum-limited lifetime leads to a sublinear increase in the size of defect-free target configurations (Fig.
2
a).

Thus, large-scale quantum processors are especially vulnerable to atom loss, which motivates the study of time-optimal rearrangement algorithms.
As multiple groups
Norcia et al. 2024
;
Pause et al. 2024
;
Pichard et al. 2024
;
Manetsch et al. 2024
;
Gyger et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2025c
have now successfully trapped over 1000 atoms simultaneously, this topic is highly relevant for large-scale experiments.

Move mechanisms and standard definitions
.
Up to now, we have not described the mechanism by which atoms are moved in the rearrangement process, nor the setting in which rearrangement occurs. In this work, we restrict our focus to rearrangement on a square lattice (though extensions to Bravais lattices can be easily made) and operations
\epsilon
performed by two crossed acousto-optical deflectors (AODs). The crossed-AOD device transforms a single incident beam into a movable rectilinear grid of beams that can be displaced, expanded, and/or contracted.
This approach is extremely advantageous in that it allows for the simultaneous implementation of many single-atom displacements in a single ‘AOD move’. So that we can later use the number of moves as a proxy for the total rearrangement time, we define an ‘AOD move’ as a valid AOD operation which displaces one or more atoms by a single site in the horizontal and/or vertical directions.
While there exist alternative approaches allowing continuous parallel movements not confined to a rectilinear grid, such as holographic AODs or moving spatial light modulators (SLMs)
Kim et al. 2016b
;
Treptow et al. 2021
;
Knottnerus et al. 2025
;
Wei et al. 2025
(see the
Discussion
), these are not studied in this work.

Defining the time-optimal rearrangement problem
.
Consider a spatial configuration of
m\times n
atoms, denoted as
\mathbf{X}\in\{0,1\}^{m\times n}
, where
\mathbf{X}_{ij}=1~(0)
indicates the presence (absence) of an atom in the tweezer located at the
i
th row and the
j
th column.
The rearrangement problem we consider is the following: given an initial configuration
\mathbf{X}_{0}
and a final configuration
\mathbf{g}
, how many operations does it take to transform
\mathbf{X}_{0}\xrightarrow{}\mathbf{g}
, and how does this scale with the total number of target atoms
N=\sum_{ij}\mathbf{g}_{ij}
(Fig.
1
b)?
More formally, the time-optimal rearrangement algorithm aims to address the following two questions:

Let
\Omega
be a finite configuration space and
S\subseteq\{\epsilon:\Omega\to\Omega\}
a finite set of ‘AOD move’ operators (as defined in the previous section).
Given an initial atom array configuration
\mathbf{X_{0}}\in\Omega
and a target configuration
\mathbf{g}\in\Omega
, what is
k^{\star}\coloneqq\min\{k\in\mathbb{N}~|~\exists~\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{k}\in S~\text{ s.t. }~\epsilon_{k}\circ\cdots\circ\epsilon_{1}(\mathbf{X}_{0})=\mathbf{g}\}
?
How does one explicitly construct
a sequence

\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{k^{\star}}

achieving
\epsilon_{k^{\star}}\circ\cdots\circ\epsilon_{1}(\mathbf{X}_{0})=\mathbf{g}
?

Existing algorithms

.There exist many easily implementable and heuristic algorithms which demonstrate high success rates in experiment, such as the Hungarian algorithm, i.e. linear sum assignment problem (LSAP) solver,
and its variations
König 1916
;
Egerváry 1931
;
Kuhn 1955
;
Lee et al. 2017
;
Schymik et al. 2020
, Balance and Compact
Vala et al. 2005
, Tetris
Wang et al. 2023
, and other algorithms which utilize sequential row and column operations
Tian et al. 2023
;
Cimring et al. 2023
.
Of these, only the Hungarian algorithm is optimal with respect to
distance
, in that it minimizes the sum of the distances traveled by each individual atom; however, it suffers from poor scaling compared to other algorithms (Fig.
4
) due to the lack of move parallelization.

However, to the best of our knowledge, no algorithm has been proven to be
time
-optimal, which is of greater experimental utility.
While optimal transport algorithms may naively appear to be well-suited for such a task, the crossed-AOD mechanism makes direct application challenging.
Thus, the asymptotic scaling of time-optimal rearrangement algorithms in this setting, remains an open question.

II
Rearrangement simulation environment

We develop and demonstrate the use of a Python-based simulation framework,
atommovr
, with the following features:

1.

Benchmarking
.
atommovr
enables easy comparison of the performance of various algorithms relative to one another through automatically calculating relevant quantities such as rearrangement time, filling fraction, and success rate (Fig.
2
).

2.

Library of existing algorithms
.

atommovr
collects a variety of representative single-species and dual-species atom rearrangement algorithms, including Parallel Sorting
Ebadi et al. 2021
, Balance and Compact
Vala et al. 2005
;
Weiss et al. 2004
, the Hungarian
König 1916
;
Egerváry 1931
;
Kuhn 1955
;
Lee et al. 2017
;
Schymik et al. 2020
, and some home-made algorithms, ParHungarian (see Appendix
VI.1
for detailed description; for both single-species and dual-species) and InsideOut (see Appendix
VI.3
for detailed description; for dual-species rearrangement).
Source code of all algorithms is available on Github.

3.

Realistic experimental conditions
.
We simulate the process of stochastic loading, atom loss due to the vacuum-limited lifetime and tweezer failure, and move failures due to moving tweezers failing to pick up/attract atoms.
Furthermore, our simulations include a number of relevant experimental parameters, such as lattice spacing, transport speed, and loading rate (Fig.
1
a).
All of the features described above are easily user-programmable.

4.

Visualization
.

atommovr
can generate movies of the rearrangement process to facilitate insight into algorithmic performance.
These include visualizations for various error processes, such as atom loss due to tweezer failure or collisions with background molecules.

5.

Customizable experimental toolkit
.
We intentionally developed
atommovr
in such a way that it would be easily accessible and modifiable to serve various purposes, from finding a suitable algorithm for an experimental setup (Fig.
4
) to studying fundamental limitations.
We have written tutorial notebooks that demonstrate the intended usage and walk readers through the process of building their own algorithms.
In addition, the repository is modular and customizable to facilitate further feature development from the community.

To demonstrate the utility of
atommovr
under an experimental setting, we compare the performance of two commonly used heuristic algorithms in noisy settings: the single-tweezer Hungarian algorithm and the Balance and Compact algorithm, which rearranges atoms by sequential row and column operations (Fig.
3

We simultaneously sweep over a range of typical values for vacuum-limited lifetimes (
0.1
-
10

0.32\%
), and compare the success probability of preparing a
10\times 10
fully filled configuration in a
14\times 14

60\%

We find that the Hungarian is advantageous when tweezer error dominates, as it is distance-optimal and requires the least number of tweezer moves (despite being relatively slow), while Balance and Compact is advantageous when vacuum-limited loss dominates (Fig.

III
Benchmarks and time-optimal scaling

We make use of
atommovr
to numerically extract lower bounds for time-optimal rearrangement algorithms and compare the scaling behavior of algorithms recently proposed in the literature (Fig.
4
, Table
1
).
To extract lower bounds, we solve the
linear bottleneck assignment problem
(LBAP)
Panagiotas et al. 2023
, also known as the
maximum bottleneck matching problem
(MBMP)
Burkard and Derigs 1980
in the graph-theoretic literature, which closely resembles LSAP.
The key difference is that, instead of minimizing the
total
distance traveled by all atoms, the solution to the LBAP/MBMP minimizes the maximum distance traveled by any particular atom.
Assuming that the speed of a moving tweezer is fixed, a lower bound on the rearrangement time
t_{\text{LB}}
can be easily extracted by the following formula:

t_{\text{LB}}=\frac{s\times d_{\text{minmax}}}{\lvert v_{\text{tweezer}}\rvert},

(1)

where
s
is the spacing between adjacent sites,
d_{\text{minmax}}
is the solution to the LBAP/MBMP, and
|v_{\text{tweezer}}|
is the (constant) tweezer speed.
We use the protocol and repository developed in
Panagiotas et al. 2023
to solve the LBAP/MBMP, which we refer to as ‘
Z^{\star}
’.

Our benchmarking results are shown in Fig.
4
and we present extracted scaling coefficients in Table
1
.
Notably, for a 50% loading probability, the lower bound scales as just under
\sqrt{N}
, while one of the leading algorithms (Tetris
Wang et al. 2023
), scales as just over
N
(where
N
is the number of target sites in a fully filled square array).
Moreover, we develop a variation of the Hungarian algorithm with move parallelization (ParHungarian), and compare its scaling to our implementation of the naive Hungarian, which we expect to be roughly
\mathcal{O}(N\sqrt{N})
(as this entails moving
\mathcal{O}(N)
atoms sequentially, each with a maximum distance of
\sqrt{N}
).
We find that our extracted scaling agrees (within error bars) with that previously found in
Wang et al. 2023
, and moreover, we see that the naive addition of parallelization to the Hungarian algorithm clearly improves the scaling from
N^{1.47(2)}
to
N^{1.27(2)}
.

However, the task of dual-species rearrangement is fundamentally more complex than the single-species problem: whereas in single-species rearrangement the exchange of any two atoms does not change the state of the array nor the minimum number of moves to prepare some target configuration, in dual-species such an exchange may be nontrivial, and pairs of exchange operations do not necessarily commute.
While this observation may appear obvious, it follows that there exist detrimental configurations in which an atom is effectively ‘blocked’ by neighboring atoms of the opposite species (Fig.
5
a,b).
Navigating such obstacles without ejecting atoms can introduce large time overheads.

While some initial algorithms have been proposed and realized experimentally to mitigate such issues
Sheng et al. 2022
;
Tao et al. 2022
;
Nakamura et al. 2024
;
Wei et al. 2025
, to the best of our knowledge, no algorithm has demonstrated the ability to prepare an arbitrary target configuration from any random initial configuration (with sufficient atom numbers and in the limit of large system size, i.e. an arbitrarily large reservoir) without moving atoms in the space between rows or columns.
To address this deficiency, we develop and benchmark a simple heuristic algorithm, InsideOut, which is able to prepare arbitrary target configurations with a near-unity success rate.

Our InsideOut algorithm is designed entirely to circumvent such ‘blocked’ configurations, and operates on a simple, layer-by-layer principle: first, it prepares the middle
2\times 2
sites of the target configuration, pushing outward any misplaced atoms in the process.
Then, it prepares the next ‘layer’ (in this case, the loop of twelve sites enclosing the middle
2\times 2
), and iteratively repeats until the target configuration is realized.
If at any point an atom of one species is blocking the path of an atom of opposite species, we simply push it to the side or away from the target configuration in a ‘clearing’ move.

Such a layer-by-layer approach reduces the chance to create ‘blocked’ configurations and ensures that previous layers will not be disturbed by such ‘clearing’ moves, thus presenting the emergence of ‘blocked’ configurations.
See Appendix
VI.3
for a detailed algorithm description of InsideOut.

Benchmarking results.

To verify the efficacy of InsideOut, we check its scaling with increasing system size, and benchmark against a naive dual-species extension of the single-species ParHungarian algorithm (See Appendix
VI.2
).
We observe that InsideOut maintains a near-unity success rate with increasing system size, while the success rate of ParHungarian decreases to under
50\%
in preparing a 10x10 target array (Fig.
5
c).

The behavior of both algorithms varies significantly with the choice of target configuration.
For the low-entropy ‘separate zones’ configuration, the rearrangement time is much longer, and the optimal loading probability for ParHungarian is significantly higher than for the high-entropy ‘zebra stripes’ and ‘checkerboard’ configurations.

Moreover, the existence of blocked configurations also raises the question of what optimal experimental conditions are for dual-species.
In particular, an advantageous strategy in single-species rearrangement is to increase the stochastic loading probability through experimental techniques including
\lambda
-enhanced gray molasses and dark-state enhanced loading
Brown et al. 2019
;
Shaw et al. 2023
.
Whereas in single-species rearrangement higher loading probability reduces the number of empty target sites and subsequently the number of moves required to fill the remaining target sites, in dual-species the likelihood of developing blocked configurations also increases. These conflicting tendencies raise the question of whether there exists an optimal loading probability, and whether the scaling behavior is consistent across all dual-species algorithms or varies from algorithm to algorithm.
Subsequently, we extend our benchmarking to study how initial loading probability impacts the success rate and the duration of the rearrangement process (Fig.
5
d,e).

As expected, the success rate for InsideOut remains at unity across all loading probabilities, but the success rate of ParHungarian decays at high loading probabilities (Fig.
5
d).
Moreover, the rearrangement time of InsideOut decreases monotonically, while ParHungarian displays minima which vary significantly with the target configuration. While considering this observation, it is important to note that ParHungarian features extensive move parallelization, whereas InsideOut only parallelizes ’clearing’ moves, in which multiple obstacles in a layer are pushed away from the center.

We see that ParHungarian is generally faster than InsideOut for low loading probabilities, but the trend is interestingly reversed for high loading probabilities (starting around
80\%
) (Fig.
5
e). In this regime, the paths for ParHungarian become increasingly full of obstacles, resulting in more circuitous paths and an overall slowdown, whereas the parallelized ‘clearing’ moves of InsideOut remove such obstacles efficiently.

V
Discussion

In the past decade, neutral atom quantum processors have grown to thousands of atoms, which motivates the development of fast rearrangement algorithms.
While there has been much activity in the experimental community to develop and implement new rearrangement algorithms, there is a sizable gap between the scaling of current leading algorithms utilizing sequential row and column operations (
\approx\mathcal{O}(N)
) and a lower bound on scaling for time-optimal algorithms (
\approx\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{N}))
, where
N
is the total number of atoms in a fully-filled target configuration.
This raises the question of whether there exists an algorithm in the crossed-AOD framework that outperforms the sequential row-column heuristic and achieves sub-linear scaling.
Moreover, none of the algorithms presented in the literature have open-source code implementations, making it difficult to compare the performance of newly developed algorithms.
By developing
atommovr
, we hope to create a common platform for the community to discuss, compare existing algorithms and develop new algorithms with time-optimal scaling.

Finally, we wish to highlight alternate approaches to the crossed-AOD mechanism and areas for further study.

•

Moving tweezers with SLMs.

SLMs, while typically used to generate a grid of ‘static’ tweezers over which atoms are rearranged through AOD-generated ‘mobile’ tweezers, have been used to move atoms between sites and eliminate the need for a separate set of mobile tweezers
Kim et al. 2016b
;
Kim et al. 2019
;
Lin et al. 2024
;
Knottnerus et al. 2025
.
The key advantage of this method is that it avoids the constraint of generating moving tweezers on a rectilinear grid, and can execute arbitrary combinations of moves in parallel.
However, in practice, this method is hindered by response times (frame generation rate) on the order of 1 ms (1 kHz) intrinsically limited by physical properties of the SLM
Jullien, Aurélie 2020
, the computational cost of calculating the input parameters for the desired hologram at each frame (typically by solving the Gerchberg-Saxton algorithm) and the discretization of the moving process.

•

Holographic AODs
. Recent work
Treptow et al. 2021
has proposed using AODs to generate phase- and amplitude-controlled holograms, instead of SLMs. This approach avoids the discretization of the moving process and the slow frame update rate, as AODs adapt in real time to inputs. However, this method of generating holograms is less developed than SLMs, and the high computational cost of calculating suitable holograms persists.

•

Non-heuristic machine learning methods.
Recently, advances in machine learning have enabled widespread advances in certain computational tasks.
In particular, reinforcement learning has been famously used to build engines in the highly complex games of Go and chess that have defeated top human players
Silver et al. 2016
;
Silver et al. 2017
;
Silver et al. 2018
.
Considering that the underlying structure of Go is very similar to the rearrangement problem (any board position can be represented in the same manner as a dual-species atom array, with each board position having either a single black piece, a single white piece, or no piece), this could be a promising area for future study. In particular, an implementation of a single-player version of AlphaZero
Moerland 2022
to solve the rearrangement problem would be an exciting approach. In practice, a learning-driven algorithm could also be particularly useful for integration into experimental setups, as it could learn underlying noise models and adapt to circumvent experimental sources of error.

Detailed description of ParHungarian.

First, the algorithm checks if the number of atoms given by the initial random loading is sufficient to prepare the target pattern, i.e.
\sum_{ij}\mathbf{X}^{\text{init}}_{ij}\geq\sum_{ij}\mathbf{g}_{ij}
(to keep notations clean, here we use

where the rows are target sites to be filled, i.e. site

\mathbf{X}^{\text{init}}_{ij}=0
and
\mathbf{g}_{ij}=1

(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
contains an excess atom if
\mathbf{X}^{\text{init}}_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}=1
and
\mathbf{g}_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}=0
, and the component

is given by the Euclidean distance between the
k
th target site and the
l

Lee et al. 2017
.
By solving the linear sum assignment problem corresponding to the cost matrix
\mathbf{C}
, ParHungarian assigns each target vacancy to an excess atom, denoted as a mapping
h:a\to t
, where
a
and
t
represent the set of excess atoms and the set of target vacancies, respectively.
According to the mapping relationship, ParHungarian generates
N^{\prime}

\{a_{\nu},t_{\nu}\}_{\nu=1}^{N^{\prime}}
, where
N^{\prime}
is the size of set
a
.
Note that the number of excess atoms is always greater or equal to the number of target vacancies due to the initial sufficient atom check.

According to the assignments, ParHungarian finds the shortest direct path between each pair.
If no direct path exists between
a_{\nu}
and

due to the presence of obstacle atoms, the path is decomposed into a sequence of ‘domino’ subpaths.
In this scheme, the final obstacle is first relocated to the target position
t_{\nu}
, after which each preceding obstacle successively occupies the position vacated by the following obstacle, until
a_{\nu}
can be moved to the location of the first obstacle along the path.
By the above process, ParHungarian generates

\{p_{\mu}\}_{\mu=1}^{N}
, where each path

is the

Here, we define

.
Firstly, ParHungarian removes the 1st moves in every path and adds them to a new list
q_{1}:=\{m_{\mu 1}\}_{\mu=1}^{N}
.
Then, ParHungarian checks if there are two or more moves in

If such a case or cases are found, ParHungarian only keeps one move in this round of execution and puts the others back in their respective path lists.

Move parallelization subroutine.

Abstractly, given a sequence of individual atom moves
\mathcal{M}=\{m_{1},\ldots,m_{M}\}
and an atom array
\mathbf{X}
, we divide
\mathcal{M}
into a set of ‘AOD moves’
\{\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{M^{\prime}}\}
.
Such parallelization is done following a greedy approach: the individual moves in
\mathcal{M}
are iteratively added to a temporary parallel move queue
\tilde{\epsilon}
, and subsequently eliminated from
\mathcal{M}
.
After each step, the algorithm checks whether
\tilde{\epsilon}
is a valid AOD move, and halts if not, removing the most recently added element. Then, a new list is created and the cycle repeats until all the moves in
\mathcal{M}
have been sorted.

To test if the move segments
\{\tilde{m}_{i}\}
in
\tilde{\epsilon}
can be implemented in parallel, the algorithm has to check whether parallelization of these moves affects the final configuration of the array.
If not,
\tilde{\epsilon}
is a valid AOD move and its composite move segments can be parallelized.
To check whether this is the case, the composite AOD move
\tilde{\epsilon}
is applied to one copy of the atom array and another set – the AOD moves corresponding to executing each individual move segment sequentially (i.e.
\{\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{k}\}
, where
k
is defined as the number of move items in
\{\tilde{m}\}
) – is applied to a second copy.
More formally, we compare the matrices
\mathbf{X}^{\prime}_{\text{Par}}=\tilde{\epsilon}(\mathbf{X})
and
\mathbf{X}^{\prime}_{\text{No-Par}}=\epsilon_{k}\circ\cdots\circ\epsilon_{1}(\mathbf{X})
.
If
\mathbf{X}^{\prime}_{\text{Par}}=\mathbf{X}^{\prime}_{\text{No-Par}}
, the moves
\{\tilde{m}_{i}\}
can be parallelized.

Advantage and limitation of the parallelization strategy.

Since ParHungarian checks whether any arbitrary set of moves can be parallelized, it potentially offers a higher degree of parallelism than algorithms which only parallelize moves within the space of a single row or column.

However, ParHungarian does not offer the optimal parallelization strategy due to its greedy approach.
The brute-force solution takes cost of
\mathcal{O}(2^{M})
to compute, where
M
is the number of moves to be parallelized, since each move element can be parallelized and not parallelized.
To make the algorithm scalable, we adapt the greedy approach to handle the tradeoff of degree of parallelization and computational efficiency.
The computational complexity of our greedy parallelization strategy is
\mathcal{O}(M^{2})
.

VI.3
InsideOut Algorithm (dual-species)

To overcome the unique challenges of dual-species rearrangement (discussed in Sec.
IV
), we develop the InsideOut algorithm.
The cornerstone of InsideOut is its simple and uniform layer-by-layer approach, which effectively prevents the emergence of ‘blocked cases’ shown in Fig.
5
a,b.
In case blocked configurations still occur, InsideOut pushes the obstacle atoms away from the center in a ‘cleaning’ step.
Visualization of the algorithm is shown in Fig.
S3
.

Detailed description of InsideOut.

First, InsideOut checks if the number of atoms in both species is sufficient to prepare the target pattern, i.e.

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&\sum_{ij}\mathbf{M}_{ij1}\geq\sum_{ij}\mathbf{T}_{ij1}\\
&\sum_{ij}\mathbf{M}_{ij2}\geq\sum_{ij}\mathbf{T}_{ij2}.\end{cases}

(S.2)
