A friendly introduction to triangular transport
Maximilian Ramgraber m.ramgraber@tudelft.nl, Daniel Sharp dannys4@mit.edu, Mathieu Le Provost, Youssef Marzouk ymarz@mit.edu

Abstract
Decision making under uncertainty is a cross-cutting challenge in science and engineering. Most approaches to this challenge employ probabilistic representations of uncertainty. In complicated systems accessible only via data or black-box models, however, these representations are rarely known. We discuss how to characterize and manipulate such representations using triangular transport maps , which approximate any complex probability distribution as a transformation of a simple, well-understood distribution. The particular structure of triangular transport guarantees many desirable mathematical and computational properties that translate well into solving practical problems. Triangular maps are actively used for density estimation, (conditional) generative modelling, Bayesian inference, data assimilation, optimal experimental design, and related tasks. While there is ample literature on the development and theory of triangular transport methods, this manuscript provides a detailed introduction for scientists interested in employing measure transport without assuming a formal mathematical background. We build intuition for the key foundations of triangular transport, discuss many aspects of its practical implementation, and outline the frontiers of this field.

Motivation

Who is this tutorial for?

This manuscript is an accessible introduction to
triangular transport
, a powerful and versatile method for generative modelling and Bayesian inference. In particular, triangular transport underpins effective algorithms for data assimilation, solving inverse problems, and performing simulation-based inference, with applications across myriad scientific disciplines.

This tutorial targets researchers with an interest in applied statistical methods but without a formal background in mathematics. Consequently, we will focus more on intuition, general concepts, and implementation, referring the reader to other relevant articles for more formal exposition and theory.

How does triangular transport work?

Like other measure transport methods, triangular (measure) transport is a framework to transform one probability distribution into another. This operation is highly useful, as it allows us to characterize a complex
target distribution

{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
by transforming a simpler, known
reference distribution

{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
. Throughout this manuscript, we use
orange
to denote variables associated with the (problem-specific)
target
distribution and
green
to denote variables associated with the (user-defined)
reference
distribution, e.g., a standard Gaussian. The idea of coupling two distributions is used in a wide range of applications. In
generative modelling
, for example, we are usually interested in creating samples of a target distribution
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
, such as the distribution of
400\times 400
pixel images of cats. Measure transport methods approach this challenge by first learning a transport map

\mathbf{S}
that transforms
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
to
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
, and then use
\mathbf{S}
to convert reference samples
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{z}}\sim{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
(here:
400\times 400
pixel
images of Gaussian white noise
) into samples from the target distribution
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{S}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{z}})\sim{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
(here:
400\times 400
pixel
images of cats
).

Some of these methods—among them triangular transport—can also characterize
conditionals

{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\boldsymbol{a}}|{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0.5352,0.7227}\boldsymbol{b}^{*}})
of the
joint
target distribution
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\boldsymbol{a}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\boldsymbol{b}})
of two random variables
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\boldsymbol{a}}
and
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\boldsymbol{b}}
. Here

{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0.5352,0.7227}\boldsymbol{b}}^{*}

Conditioning operations often arise as stochastic generalizations of evaluating deterministic processes (see

What makes triangular transport special?

At the heart of triangular transport is their eponymous
triangular
structure.
1
1

1

A triangular map is a vector-valued function whose
k
-th output depends only on the first
k
input variables. The name derives from the fact that the Jacobian of such a map is lower triangular.

This structure sets them apart from other measure transport methods such as normalizing flows
(
Rezende and Mohamed, 2015
;
Kobyzev et al., 2020
, e.g., )
, which compose together many simpler but somewhat ad hoc transformations, often interleaved with permutations, and even from conditional normalizing flows
(
van den Oord et al., 2016
, e.g., )
, which parameterize normalizing flows in order to represent block triangular (rather than strictly triangular) maps.
Triangular structure—discussed in greater detail in subsequent sections—has many important practical properties:

•

Parsimony
: The parameterization of the map function is at the user’s discretion (see
Section

3.1
). This means we can adjust the map’s overall complexity, down to the complexity with which it resolves individual variables and variable dependencies. This allows us to implement nonlinear maps that are as complex as necessary, and yet as simple as possible (see
Section

4.2
).

•

Sparsity
: Triangular maps have a natural ability to exploit
conditional independence
. This improves their computational efficiency, which enables these maps to scale to high-dimensional settings. Further, such structure makes them highly robust to spurious correlations and smaller sample sizes (see
Section

2.3.3
).

•

Numerical convenience
: Constructing triangular maps boils down to parameterizing simple one-dimensional monotone functions, a task with a rich body of supporting literature. Because of this, these maps are easy to optimize and invert, which we investigate in detail.

•

Explainability
: Triangular maps have a clear correspondence between their constituent elements and the statistical features they represent (see
Section

2.3
). We can then readily describe different factorizations of the target distribution using the elements of a triangular map, with a particular focus on combinations of various conditional and marginals of the target.

Theory

2.1
Bayesian inference

To begin, let us briefly revisit some basic concepts of Bayesian inference which will serve to motivate the operations explored in the following sections. In short, Bayesian inference is based on
Bayes’ theorem
: given two random variables (RVs)
\mathbf{a}
,
\mathbf{b}
with joint probability density function (pdf)
p(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})
, we see:

p\left(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{b}^{*}\right)=\frac{p\left(\mathbf{a}\right)p\left(\mathbf{b}^{*}|\mathbf{a}\right)}{p\left(\mathbf{b}^{*}\right)},

(1)

Equation

1
subsumes three sequential operations
(
Gelman et al., 2013
, see
Figure

3
; e.g., )
:

Figure 3:
Schematic illustration of Bayes theorem, using a Beta mixture prior
p(a)
and an observation model
p(b|a)=\mathcal{N}\left(\mu=2a^{3}-a,\sigma=0.075\right)
. Left: We can create a joint distribution
p(a,b)
from a prior
p(a)
and the observation model
p(b|a)
. Right: conditioning this joint distribution on a specific value

retrieves a posterior
p(a|b^{*})
.

1.

First, the marginal prior
p\left(\mathbf{a}\right)
is combined with a conditional observation model (sometimes also called
likelihood model
)
p\left(\mathbf{b}|\mathbf{a}\right)
, yielding a joint probability distribution
p\left(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\right)=p\left(\mathbf{a}\right)p\left(\mathbf{b}|\mathbf{a}\right)
over all possible combinations of
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{b}
. This joint distribution describes how the variable of interest
\mathbf{a}
and the predicted observations
\mathbf{b}
relate to each other.

2.

Next, this joint distribution is conditioned on a specific observation
\mathbf{b}^{*}
. In practice, this means evaluating
p\left(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\right)
for all possible values
\mathbf{a}
while keeping
\mathbf{b}
fixed at the value of
\mathbf{b}^{*}
. This extracts a slice
p\left(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}^{*}\right)
of this joint distribution at
\mathbf{b}^{*}
along different values of
\mathbf{a}
.

3.

Since the probability densities along this slice do not generally integrate to
1
, this slice does not constitute a valid pdf. The final step thus normalizes the probability densities against the slice’s probability mass
p\left(\mathbf{b}^{*}\right)=\int p\left(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{b}^{*}\right)d\mathbf{t}
, yielding the posterior pdf
p\left(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{b}^{*}\right)
.

In summary, Bayes’ theorem first constructs a joint distribution
p\left(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\right)
from a prior
p(\mathbf{a})
and an observation model
p(\mathbf{b}|\mathbf{a})
, then conditions it on a specific observation value
\mathbf{b}^{*}
and re-normalizes. In consequence, one could reformulate
Equation

1
equivalently as:

p\left(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{b}^{*}\right)=\frac{p\left(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}^{*}\right)}{\int p\left(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{b}^{*}\right)d\mathbf{t}},

(2)

where
p\left(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}^{*}\right)
evaluates the joint pdf
p\left(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\right)
for any desired
\mathbf{a}
while keeping
\mathbf{b}
fixed at
\mathbf{b}^{*}
, and the denominator acts as a normalizing constant. This equation, or reformulation thereof, lie at the heart of all Bayesian inference algorithms. Unfortunately, it is generally intractable to evaluate the normalized posterior density
p\left(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{b}^{*}\right)
. In some cases, it is also impossible even to evaluate the joint density.
To overcome these challenges, different Bayesian inference methods use different strategies. As we shall see in the following,
triangular transport addresses this challenge by first approximating
the joint pdf
p(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})
using the concept of measure transport (
Section

2.2.1
). Crucially, the structure of this transformation then allows us to evaluate any of its conditionals
p\left(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{b}^{*}\right)
(
Section

2.3.2
).

The remainder of this tutorial drops this generic notation for two RVs
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{b}
jointly distributed as
p(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})
in favour of a single RV
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}}
distributed according to a distribution
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}

{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}}
as an augmented RV
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}}=[\mathbf{b},\mathbf{a}]

{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}})=p(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})

p(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})
; we focus primarily on methods that will also allow us to characterize the desired conditionals
p(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{b})
.

2.2
The change-of-variables formula

The key to understand transport methods is the

\mathbf{S}
“stretch” or “squeeze” the spaces they are applied to. Accounting for this spatial distortion ensures that the probability mass is preserved and thus the transformed density
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{z}})
remains a valid pdf.

The change-of-variables formula allows us to describe how a probability density
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
changes when subjected to a specific transformation
\mathbf{S}
. An example is provided in
Figure

4
, which shows how a non-Gaussian target pdf
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
can be related to a Gaussian reference pdf
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
through an invertible transformation; this invertibility is equivalent to monotonicity in the scalar case.

Given the ability to change variables in this way, one can estimate many quantities of interest. Consider an arbitrary function
\mathbf{Q}:\mathbb{R}^{K}\to\mathbb{R}^{M}
. Then, one can use
Monte Carlo
samples,
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Z}}^{i}}\sim{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
for
i=1,\ldots,N
, to estimate the average value of
\mathbf{Q}
, i.e.,

\int\mathbf{Q}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}})\,\mathrm{d}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}})=\int\mathbf{Q}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}})\,\mathrm{d}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{S}^{\sharp}\eta}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}})=\int\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{S}^{-1}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{z}}))\,\mathrm{d}{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{z}})\approx\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{S}^{-1}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Z}}^{i}})).

(5)

Applications of this estimation scheme include, for example, the mean (
\mathbf{Q}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}})={\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}}
), the sum of marginal second moments (
Q({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}})=\|{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}
), or even the entropy (
Q({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}})=\log{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}})=\log{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{S}^{\sharp}\eta}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}})
).

{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x}

{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}

. We can evaluate

.

2.2.1
Connection to transport methods

The change-of-variables formula has three knobs to tune: the original density
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
, the map
\mathbf{S}
, and its transformed output
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
. When considering the change-of-variables in elementary calculus courses,
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
and
\mathbf{S}
are often assumed known, and we seek the transformed density
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
. Transport methods choose a slightly different approach. We assume
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
and
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
to be known (at least partially), and instead seek a map
\mathbf{S}
which relates the two distributions to each other.

As discussed in
Section

1
, we generally do not have access to the target pdf
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
in closed form.
Rather, we might only have samples
6
6

6

Sample approximations are common if
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
is only known from a dataset
or if the prior can be sampled but not evaluated.

{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}\sim{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
, or we might only be able to evaluate
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
up to a constant of proportionality (i.e.,
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\tilde{\pi}}={\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}m}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
, where
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}m}>0
is an unknown constant).
7
7

7

Unnormalized densities
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\tilde{\pi}}
often arise in Bayesian statistics, when the prior and likelihood can be evaluated at least point-wise, but it is infeasible to quantify the model evidence (see
Equation

1
).

On the other hand, the reference
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
is chosen to be a simple, well-known distribution, often a standard Gaussian
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathcal{N}}\left(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{I}\right)
(
Figure

5
).
The map
\mathbf{S}
is identified by minimizing an objective function over a specified class of functions; see the discussion in
Section

3.2
. By finding this map, we learn how to construct the unknown target distribution
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
by transforming a well-defined reference distribution
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
.

{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}}

{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{z}}

.

Among its other uses, learning the map
\mathbf{S}
allows us to cheaply draw new samples from the target distribution
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
. This is achieved by first sampling the reference
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
, then applying the inverse map
\mathbf{S}^{-1}
to the resulting reference samples
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{z}}
. This is especially useful in applications where sampling the target conventionally would involve computationally expensive simulations of, e.g., partial differential equations (
emulation
), or systems in which the sample-generating process is not known exactly
(
Baptista et al., 2024c
,
generative modelling
: e.g.,)
.

2.3
Triangular maps and their uses

Many classes of functions are viable choices for the transport map
\mathbf{S}
. Outside of the constructions shown here, common examples of transport maps include normalizing flows
(
Papamakarios et al., 2021
)
, optimal transport maps
(
Peyré et al., 2019
)
, and the transformations induced by flow-based generative models
(
Albergo et al., 2025
;
Lipman et al., 2023
)
.
By contrast, we consider
triangular
transport maps. In addition to sampling the target
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
, triangular maps are unique in allowing us to sample
conditionals
of
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
, which makes them a flexible and versatile tool for Bayesian inference.
Triangular maps are structured as follows:

\mathbf{S}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}})=\begin{bmatrix}[l]S_{1}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}})\\
S_{2}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{2}})\\
S_{3}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{2}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{3}})\\
\vdots\\
S_{K}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}},\dots,{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{K}})\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}[l]{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{1}}\\
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{2}}\\
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{3}}\\
\vdots\\
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{K}}\end{bmatrix}={\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{z}},

(6)

where the full map
\mathbf{S}:\real{K}\to\real{K}
is comprised of map components
S_{k}:\real{k}\to\real{}
,
k=1,\ldots,K
, each of which depends only on the first
k
entries of the target RV
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}}=\left[{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}},\dots,{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{K}}\right]^{\top}
and we
enforce
that
\partial_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}}S_{k}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}},\ldots,{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}})>0
for any feasible choice of
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}},\ldots,{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}
. When all of
S_{1},\ldots,S_{K}
satisfy this condition, we call the map
\mathbf{S}
“monotone.” The eponymous
triangular
nature of
\mathbf{S}
refers to the fact that the map’s partial derivatives with regards to
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}}
are lower-triangular; that is to say, the Jacobian matrix
\nabla\mathbf{S}
has all zeros above its diagonal. This structure
has a number of highly desirable properties:

1.

For any pair of target and reference pdfs,
8
8

8

The existence of a transport map, triangular or not, is more delicate if we do not assume that both distributions have densities. For example, if
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
is a discrete distribution, such that
X\sim{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
takes on only a finite number of values, and
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
is a Gaussian distribution, there is no map achieving
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{S}_{\sharp}\pi}={\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}

(
Villani and others, 2009
)
. The failure of existence is due to the fact that a deterministic map cannot split mass.

{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
and
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
, there exists a unique function
\mathbf{S}
satisfying this structure (i.e., a monotone triangular map) that achieves
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{S}_{\sharp}\pi}={\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
. This map is known as the
Knothe–Rosenblatt rearrangement

(
Knothe, 1957
;
Rosenblatt, 1952
)
or KR map.

2.

This triangular structure allows us to evaluate the
determinant of the map’s Jacobian

\det\nabla\mathbf{S}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}})
efficiently as the product of its diagonal entries
(
Marzouk et al., 2017
)
, which proves highly useful for the map’s optimization (see
Section

3.2
), not to mention when performing the density estimation itself for the changed variables:

\det\nabla\mathbf{S}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}})=\prod_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\partial S_{k}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}},\ldots,{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}})}{\partial{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}}.

(7)

3.

Triangular maps are
easily invertible
. In particular, we pick a class of functions that are monotone in their last input
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}
(with no requirements on the other inputs
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}},\dots,{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k-1}}
), i.e.
\partial_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}}S_{k}>0
everywhere; this guarantees the monotonicity and thus eases our inversion computation. We will discuss ways to guarantee this property in
Section

3.1.1
.

4.

Perhaps most importantly, triangular maps naturally
factorize the target distribution
into a product of marginal conditional pdfs. We will investigate this property in greater detail in the following sections.

Why does a unique triangular map exist? In the scalar-valued case
K=1
, it is well known that the cumulative distribution function (cdf) of
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
, defined as
F_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x})=\int_{-\infty}^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x}}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t
, is the unique monotone increasing function that pushes forward
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
to the uniform distribution on the unit interval,
\mathcal{U}(0,1)

(
Devroye, 1986
)
.
Using this property, it is straightforward to couple two univariate distributions,
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
and
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
, by thinking of the uniform distribution as an intermediate “bridge” between the two: concretely, the map
S=F_{{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}}^{-1}\circ F_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}}
pushes forward
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
to
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
. Since
S
is the composition of monotone functions, it is also monotone. This construction establishes existence; to guarantee uniqueness, we can notice that replacing the intermediate uniform distribution with any other distribution does not change the final map
S
. In particular, if we write the intermediate distribution as
B_{\sharp}\,\mathcal{U}(0,1)
for some invertible
B
(which is possible for any absolutely continuous distribution), we can easily see that
S=F_{{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}}^{-1}\circ B^{-1}\circ B\circ F_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}}=F_{{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}}^{-1}\circ F_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}}
.

In the more general multivariate case, a similar principle is applied
recursively
. As we shall see in Section
2.3.2
, a triangular map corresponds to a factorization of a multivariate pdf
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
into a product of univariate
marginal conditional
pdfs, i.e.,
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}|{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1:k-1}})

for any integer
k\leq K
. The corresponding marginal conditional cdfs,
F_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}|{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1:k-1}})=\int_{-\infty}^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}(t|{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1:k-1}})\,\mathrm{d}t
, along with the corresponding marginal conditional cdfs of the reference distribution
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
, then uniquely yield the monotone component functions
S_{k}
of the full KR map
\mathbf{S}
. For more details on the construction of the KR map from marginal conditional distributions, we refer to, e.g.,
Wang and Marzouk (2022)
;
Santambrogio (2015)
;
Figalli and Glaudo (2021)
;
Zech and Marzouk (2022a)
.

2.3.1
Map inversion

In the forward map evaluation (
Equation

6
), each of the map’s component functions
S_{k}
can be evaluated independently, even in parallel, and then assembled into the full reference vector
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{z}}
. However, the same does not hold for the inverse map:

\mathbf{S}^{-1}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{z}})=\begin{bmatrix}[l]S_{1}^{-1}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{1}})\\
S_{2}^{-1}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{2}};{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}})\\
S_{3}^{-1}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{3}};{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{2}})\\
\hfill\vdots\hfill\\
S_{K}^{-1}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{K}};{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}},\dots,{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{K-1}})\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}[l]{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}}\\
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{2}}\\
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{3}}\\
\hfill\vdots\hfill\\
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{K}}\end{bmatrix}={\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}}.

(8)

Here, the inverse map’s component functions
S_{k}^{-1}
must be evaluated in sequence and cannot be evaluated independently. This process begins by inverting the first map component
S_{1}^{-1}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{1}})
, a trivial one-dimensional root-finding problem,
9
9

9

This root finding problem has a single unique solution within the domain of
S_{k}^{-1}
, since we require
S_{k}
be monotone in
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}
.

which yields
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}}
. This output
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}}
serves as auxiliary input for the second map component’s inversion
S_{2}^{-1}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{2}};{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}})
, yielding another one-dimensional root-finding problem, which provides
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{2}}
. All subsequent map component inversions are similar one-dimensional root-finding problems that likewise depend on the outcomes of previous inversions. This dependence of each inversion
S_{k}^{-1}
on each of
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}},\ldots,{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k-1}}
, the outcomes of previous inversions, effectively factorizes the target distribution as a product of marginal conditionals:

\pi\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}}\right)=\underbrace{\pi\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}}\right)}_{S_{1}^{-1}\left({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{1}}\right)}\underbrace{\pi\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{2}}|{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}}\right)}_{S_{2}^{-1}\left({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{2}};{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}}\right)}\underbrace{\pi\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{3}}|{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{2}}\right)}_{S_{3}^{-1}\left({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{3}};{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{2}}\right)}\dots\underbrace{\pi\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{K}}|{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}},\dots,{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{K-1}}\right)}_{S_{K}^{-1}\left({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{K}};{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}},\dots,{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{K-1}}\right)},

(9)

where each term corresponds to, and is in turn sampled by, one of the inverse map components indicated in the underbraces. We also note that the variable ordering defines the factorization, and through it, the map structure; we will explore the consequences of different orderings in more detail in
Section

2.3.3
. Due to the chain rule of probability, such a triangular factorization always exists. For a particular sample
i
, each row of
Equation

8
can be used to generate a sample
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathsf{X}_{k}^{i}}
from a particular marginal distribution conditioned on
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathsf{X}_{1}},\dots,{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathsf{X}_{k-1}}
. Given a reference sample
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Z}}^{i}}\sim{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
, we get:

{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathsf{X}_{k}^{i}}=S_{k}^{-1}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathsf{Z}_{k}^{i}};{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathsf{X}_{1}^{i}},\dots,{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathsf{X}_{k-1}^{i}})\sim{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}|{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}},\dots,{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k-1}}\right).

(10)

The direct correspondence between
S_{k}^{-1}\left({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{k}};{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{1:k-1}}\right)
and
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}|{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{1:k-1}}\right)
in Equation (
9
) only applies if the reference distribution
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
has no dependence among any of its marginals
(
Rosenblatt, 1952
;
Spantini et al., 2022
)
, i.e.,
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{z}})={\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta_{1}}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{1}}){\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta_{2}}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{2}})\cdots{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta_{K}}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{K}})
. A standard Gaussian reference fulfills this property.
An intuitive explanation is that this tensor-product reference distribution, because it introduces no dependence among the random variables
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{1},\ldots,z_{K}}
, introduces no extraneous information about dependencies in the target distribution.
The absence of dependence in the reference measure forces the map
\mathbf{S}
itself to learn
all
dependencies among the random target variables
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1},\ldots,x_{K}}
.
Conditions for this correspondence can also be deduced from the explicit construction of the KR map via cdfs in the general case where
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
is not a product measure; see, e.g.,
Wang and Marzouk (2022, Sec. 2)
.

2.3.2
Sampling conditionals

As it turns out, the factorization of the target distribution
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
in
Equations

9
and
10
also allows us to sample
conditionals
of
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
, including the Bayesian posterior
p(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{b})
(assuming
p:={\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
and
[\mathbf{b},\mathbf{a}]:=[{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{1:k}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{k+1:K}}]
). This can be achieved by manipulating the inversion process. First, observe that the factorization in
Equation

9
can be aggregated into two blocks:

\pi\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}}\right)=\underbrace{\pi\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{1:k}}\right)}_{\mathbf{S}_{1:k}^{-1}\left({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{z}_{1:k}}\right)}\underbrace{\pi\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{k+1:K}}|{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{1:k}}\right)}_{\;\;\mathbf{S}_{k+1:K}^{-1}\left({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{z}_{k+1:K}};\,{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{1:k}}\right)}\hskip-8.00003pt.

2.3.3
Conditional independence

A related, very useful property of triangular transport maps is that they naturally allow for the exploitation of
conditional independence

by construction.
10
10

10

By default, many statistical methods assume that all RVs directly influence each other.
Conditional independence
arises whenever two RVs
\boldsymbol{a}
and
\boldsymbol{c}
only affect each other indirectly via a third RV
\boldsymbol{b}
. In this case, we say that
\boldsymbol{a}
is conditionally independent of
\boldsymbol{c}
(and vice versa) given
\boldsymbol{b}
, represented symbolically as
\boldsymbol{a}\perp\!\!\!\perp\boldsymbol{c}\;|\;\boldsymbol{b}
.
As an example, consider
a
as the chance of rain,
b
as the chance of wet ground (which can, but does not have to be caused by rain), and
c
as the chance of slipping. As rain (
a
) only affects the chance of slipping (
c
) via wet ground (
b
), we have
a\perp\!\!\!\perp c\;|\;b
.

Recalling the map’s generic factorization of the conditionals of target
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
in
Equation

9
, we might ask ourselves what happens in systems in which we can exploit conditional independence. For example, if we have a target distribution
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{1:4}}\right)
and conditional independence properties
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{3}}\perp\!\!\!\perp{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}}|{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{2}}
and
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{4}}\perp\!\!\!\perp{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{2}}|{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{3}}
(corresponding to
Markov structure
), the map’s factorization could be reduced as follows:

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{1:4}}\right)

\displaystyle={\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}}\right){\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{2}}|{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}}\right){\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{3}}|\cancel{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{2}}\right){\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{4}}|\cancel{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}}},\cancel{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{2}}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{3}}\right)

(15)

\displaystyle={\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}}\right){\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{2}}|{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}}\right){\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{3}}|{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{2}}\right){\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{4}}|{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{3}}\right).

We refer to this reduction as
sparsification
. Triangular transport maps allows us to leverage these conditional independence properties by simply dropping the corresponding arguments from the map components
S_{k}
:

\mathbf{S}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{1:4}})=\begin{bmatrix}[l]S_{1}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}})\\
S_{2}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{2}})\\
S_{3}(\cancel{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{2}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{3}})\\
S_{4}(\cancel{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}}},\cancel{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{2}}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{3}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{4}})\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}[l]S_{1}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}})\\
S_{2}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{2}})\\
S_{3}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{2}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{3}})\\
S_{4}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{3}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{4}})\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}[l]{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{1}}\\
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{2}}\\
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{3}}\\
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{4}}\end{bmatrix}={\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{z}_{1:4}}.

(16)

Equivalently, its inverse map would be:

\mathbf{S}^{-1}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{z}_{1:4}})=\begin{bmatrix}[l]S_{1}^{-1}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{1}})\\
S_{2}^{-1}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{2}};{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}})\\
S_{3}^{-1}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{3}};\cancel{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{2}})\\
S_{4}^{-1}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{4}};\cancel{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}}},\cancel{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{2}}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{3}})\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}[l]S_{1}^{-1}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{1}})\\
S_{2}^{-1}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{2}};{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}})\\
S_{3}^{-1}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{3}};{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{2}})\\
S_{4}^{-1}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{4}};{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{3}})\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}[l]{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}}\\
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{2}}\\
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{3}}\\
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{4}}\end{bmatrix}={\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{1:4}}.

(17)

Making use of conditional independence properties in this way is useful for two reasons:

1.

Robustness
: Any conditional independence we can enforce by construction is statistical information the map does not have to pry from the samples or the model, improving the overall fidelity and robustness of the approximation to
\pi
in settings with finite ensemble size.

2.

Efficiency
: The removal of superfluous dependencies reduces the number of input arguments to many of the map components
S_{k}
, decreasing the evaluation and inversion complexity (e.g., see
Section

3.1.1
for evaluation complexity). This property is often called sparsification as it turns the Jacobian
\nabla\mathbf{S}
into a sparse matrix.

This second property is the key to applying transport methods in high-dimensional systems. As each map component function
S_{k}
generically depends on all previous arguments
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{1:k-1}}
, the computational demand explodes with the dimension of the target
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
when optimizing or evaluating the map. With sufficient conditional independence, however, sparse maps can overcome this dramatic increase in complexity. For instance, the Markov structure in
Equations

16
and
17
results in a sparse map
\mathbf{S}
with numerical complexity scaling linearly in the target dimension
K
.

A comprehensive account of the link between conditional independence and the sparsity of triangular maps is given in
Spantini et al. (2018)
, through two main lines of results. First, given a sparse undirected probabilistic graphical model that encodes Markov properties of the target distribution
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
, it is shown how to predict the sparsity pattern of the triangular map
\mathbf{S}
. This process relies on an ordered graph elimination algorithm, and can thus be performed before learning the map itself. But the resulting sparsity pattern depends on the chosen ordering of the random variables, which underscores the fact that triangular maps are intrinsically
anisotropic
objects: a good ordering is necessary in order to maximize sparsity. We comment further on methods for finding such orderings in
Section

5
. Second,
Spantini et al. (2018)
show that a property somehow dual to sparsity is
decomposability
: given the Markov structure of some distribution
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
on
\mathbb{R}^{K}
,
the inverse of the map
\mathbf{S}
defined by
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{S}_{\sharp}\pi}={\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
can be represented as
the composition of finitely many low-dimensional triangular transport maps, where low dimensionality here means that each component map is a function only of a small number of inputs. The exact structure of this decomposition follows from, again, an ordered decomposition of the original graph. These results allow very high-dimensional problems to be broken into many smaller and more manageable parts, given some conditional independence.

2.3.4
Block-triangular maps

Recalling the block structure in
Section

2.3.2
, we have so far assumed that the map component “blocks” in
Equation

12
are internally triangular; i.e.,
\mathbf{S}_{1:k}
has output
[S_{1}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}}),\ldots,S_{k}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{1:k}})]
and similar for
\mathbf{S}_{k+1:K}
. We may instead use
any
invertible functions
\mathbf{S}_{1:k}:\real{k}\to\real{k}
and
\mathbf{S}_{k+1:K}:\real{K}\to\real{K-k}
, for example certain neural networks
(
Baptista et al., 2024c
, e.g., )
. This still permits sampling conditionals and can be numerically advantageous in high-dimensional systems, but can compromise the ability to exploit conditional independence within the map blocks. In the following, we will assume that the transport map is
fully triangular
, even where we adopt the block-triangular structure for ease of notation.

3
Implementation

In the preceding section, we have discussed the theoretical foundations of triangular transport, but stopped short of defining the precise method to evaluate component
S_{k}
or the optimization problem which identifies the specific map
\mathbf{S}
from
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
to
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
. In this section, we will fill these gaps, and provide guidance on the practical implementation of triangular transport methods in code.

First, though, a slight interlude about the “work” a map performs to transform
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
and
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
into one another. If
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
is standard Gaussian and
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
is also standard Gaussian distribution, then the transport map
\mathbf{S}
is trivially the identity.
If
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
is instead a general Gaussian distribution, an affine
\mathbf{S}
will suffice to transform between
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
and
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
.
Once
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
becomes non-Gaussian, however, the transport map must be strictly nonlinear, as an affine
\mathbf{S}
cannot create non-Gaussian features from a Gaussian reference. The nonlinearity of a map is therefore related to how “different” our reference and target distributions are in various senses.
This intuitive idea underpins many practical considerations; for example, it is expedient to choose a reference distribution
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
that shares approximately the same tail behavior as the target pdf
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
, i.e., to ensure that the quotient
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}(\mathbf{x})/{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}(\mathbf{x})
is approximately constant far from the origin. This allows us to use map component functions
S_{k}
that are linear in their inputs outside a certain radius; learning a map that instead changes the tail behavior would require learning nonlinear behavior in the regime
\|\mathbf{x}\|\to\infty
, which is typically outside the support of the training data and thus intrinsically challenging.

3.1
Structuring the map components
S_{k}

A core component of any triangular transport map
\mathbf{S}

, we obtain a unique tuple
({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathsf{Z}_{1}},\dots,{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathsf{Z}_{k}})
. There are several strategies that can ensure each map component satisfies these monotonicity requirements. We will explore these strategies in the following.

Monotonicity through integration

A general, powerful, but also computationally demanding method to enforce monotonicity makes use of a
rectifier
and
integration

(
Baptista et al., 2023
, e.g., )
. In this formulation, our starting point is a smooth, differentiable, but nonmonotone function
S_{k}^{\text{non}}:\real{k}\to\mathbb{R}
. An example of such a nonmonotone function is:

S_{3}^{\text{non}}\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{2}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{3}}\right)=\underbrace{c_{0}+c_{1}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}}+c_{2}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{2}}+c_{3}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}x_{2}}+c_{4}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}}^{2}}_{\text{nonmonotone part }g\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{2}}\right)}+\underbrace{c_{5}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{3}}+c_{6}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{3}}^{2}+c_{7}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{2}}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{3}}}_{\text{pre-monotone part }\hat{g}\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{2}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{3}}\right)},

(18)

where the coefficients
\{c_{i}\}
parameterize
S_{k}^{\text{non}}
. In
Equation

18
,we have conceptually separated the terms of
S_{3}^{\text{non}}
into a nonmonotone part
g
, which does not depend on
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{3}}
, and a “pre-monotone” part
\hat{g}
, which does. In general, the function
\hat{g}
will not be monotone in
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{3}}
, and consequently neither will be
S_{3}^{\text{non}}
. We can make it monotone, however, by first applying a rectifier
r:\mathbb{R}\to\real{+}
to
\hat{g}
, then integrating the rectified output over
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}
:

\displaystyle S_{k}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}},\ldots,{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}})=\underbrace{g({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}},\ldots,{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k-1}})}_{\text{nonmonotone part}}+\underbrace{f({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}},\ldots,{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}})}_{\text{monotone part}},

(19)

\displaystyle\text{where }\underbrace{f({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}},\ldots,{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}})}_{\text{monotone function}}=\int_{0}^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}}\overbrace{r(\underbrace{\hat{g}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}},\ldots,{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k-1}},t)}_{\text{nonmonotone function}})}^{\text{positive function}}\,\mathrm{d}t.

The two steps combined in
Equation

19
are as follows:

1.

Rectification
: First,
\hat{g}
is sent through the rectifier
r
, a function which maps its output to strictly positive values. Examples of useful rectifiers include exponential functions, the softplus function (i.e.,
\log(1+\exp(x))
) or Exponential Linear Units
(
Clevert et al., 2016
)
.

2.

Integration
: Numerically or analytically integrating the output of the resulting strictly positive function over
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}
yields a function
f
monotone in
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}
, which turn also guarantees the monotonicity of
S_{k}
in
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}
.

This process is visualized in
Figure

8
, and has a number of important advantages. First, the use of an integrated rectifier places very few limitations on the structure of
S_{k}^{\text{non}}
. Furthermore, it also permits the introduction of
cross-terms
such as
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}
between the last argument
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}
and previous dimensions
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1:k-1}}
, which are required for many of the more complex mapping operations (see
Section

3.1.2
). The drawback of this approach is that practically evaluating
Equation

19
often demands one-dimensional numerical integration via quadrature, increasing the computational demand of the map’s evaluation, optimization, and inversion.

. (B) Applying a monotone rectifier, for example

An important practical consideration when using the integrated map formulation is to ensure that the pre-monotone term
\hat{g}
reverts to a
constant
in the tails of
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}
. This can be ensured by defining
\hat{g}
as a combination of a constant term and Hermite functions (see
Section

3.1.3
), which revert to zero in the tails. To understand why this is important, consider the process illustrated in
Figure

8
. Through rectification and integration, increasingly negative values of
\hat{g}
become flat stretches in
S_{k}
. If one or both tails of the monotone term
f
become near-flat, it is possible for the root finding during the map’s inversion (see
Section

2.3.1
) to fail for outlying values. If, however,
\hat{g}
instead reverts to a positive constant in the tails, the resulting integrated monotone term
f
will extrapolate linearly and thus generally remain more robust to outliers during the inversion. This is an artifact from the discussion above where, while
\pi
and
\eta
may “look” very different for the bulk of the pdf, we expect the tails of both of them to behave similarly
(
Baptista et al., 2023
)
.

Monotonicity through variable separation

A more computationally efficient way to ensure monotonicity is to formulate a map component function
S_{k}
which is both
separable in

{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}
and
linear in the coefficients
, though not generally linear in the inputs
\mathbf{x}
. An example for such a map component function is:

S_{3}\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{2}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{3}}\right)=\underbrace{c_{0}+c_{1}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}}+c_{2}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{2}}+c_{3}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}x_{2}}+c_{4}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}}^{2}}_{\text{nonmonotone part }g\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{2}}\right)}+\underbrace{c_{5}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{3}}+c_{6}\operatorname{erf}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{3}})}_{\text{monotone part }f\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{3}}\right)},

(20)

where
c_{i}
once more are the map component’s coefficients, which are optimized to find the map from
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
to
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
, and
c_{5},c_{6}>0
. The key aspects in
Equation

20
are a clear separation into a nonmonotone term
g
, which may depend on all arguments except the last
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{1:k-1}}
, and a monotone term
f
, which may
only
depend on the last argument
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}
. To ensure that
S_{k}
remains monotone in
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}
, all terms in
f
must likewise be monotone basis functions (e.g.,
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}
and
\operatorname{erf}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}})
).

The advantages of this separable formulation are two-fold: First,
Equation

20
has no need for numerical integration, which reduces computational demand substantially. Second, as we shall see in
Section

3.2
, this separable formulation allows for extremely efficient map optimization. The price for this efficiency is that variable separation does not allow for cross-terms between the last argument and previous arguments (e.g.,
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}
), which limits the map’s expressivity. We will explore the consequences of this limitation in the next section.

3.1.2
Parameterization

Closely related to the concern of monotonicity is how each map component
S_{k}
relates
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}
, its last argument, to
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}},\ldots,{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k-1}}
.
This decision leads to three different kinds of map parameterizations, which permit maps of different complexity. In ascending order of complexity, these parameterizations are:

Marginal maps

If both the nonmonotone part
g
and the monotone part
f
depend exclusively on input
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}
, such map component functions
S_{k}
result in a
marginal
(or
diagonal
) map. As the name implies, such maps only transform the marginals of
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}}
without capturing any dependencies between its dimensions. An example of a marginal map would be:

S_{3}\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{3}}\right)=\underbrace{c_{0}}_{\text{nonmonotone part }g\left(-\right)}+\underbrace{c_{1}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{3}}+c_{2}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{3}}^{3}}_{\text{monotone part }f\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{3}}\right)}.

(21)

Recalling the implications of removing dependencies on earlier arguments
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{1:k-1}}
from the map component functions (see
Section

2.3.3
), marginal maps are of no direct use for the conditioning operations in
Section

2.3.2
. This is because marginal maps implicitly assume all entries of
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}}
are marginally independent, which in turn implies that
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{k+1:K}}|{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{1:k}})={\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{k+1:K}})
, that is to say, nothing can be learned from
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{1:k}}
about
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{k+1:K}}
. As marginal Gaussianization schemes, however, they can find limited use in Gaussian anamorphosis
(
Schöniger et al., 2012
;
Zhou et al., 2011
)
or as preconditioning tools in certain graph detection methods
(
Liu et al., 2009
)
. Marginal maps are only included here for completeness’ sake, and we use
g(-)
to denote that the nonmonotone part is constant in
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}}
, connecting better to the more complex parameterizations.

Separable maps

Closely related to the monotonicity scheme of the same name in the previous section,

separable
maps separate the map component
S_{k}
into the sum of a function
g:\mathbb{R}^{k-1}\to\mathbb{R}
that takes in
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{1:k-1}}
and a univariate monotone function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
that just takes in
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}
:

S_{3}\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{2}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{3}}\right)=\underbrace{c_{0}+c_{1}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}}+c_{2}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}}^{2}+c_{3}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{2}}}_{\text{nonmonotone part }g\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{2}}\right)}+\underbrace{c_{4}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{3}}+c_{5}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{3}}^{3}}_{\text{monotone part }f\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{3}}\right)}.

(22)

Separable maps are one level of complexity above marginal maps. Such maps can consider nonlinear dependencies between the different dimensions, but do not permit cross-terms with the last argument
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}
, which in turn limits the complexity of the target distributions
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
they can recover.
11
11

11

Linearity in coefficients and the existence of cross terms are not mutually exclusive; we could choose a map with cross terms that is linear in its coefficients. One must be careful, however, to ensure monotonicity in the final argument
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}
of the map for
any
choice of the other inputs
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{1:k-1}}
.

This limitation is subtle, and will be discussed shortly. An important advantage of separable maps is that, when linear in the coefficients, their optimization can be partially solved in closed-form, which can improve computational efficiency substantially. More detail on this is provided in
Appendix

A
.

Cross-term maps

The most versatile map parameterization are
cross-term
maps, which permit the greatest expressiveness at the cost of increased computational demand. As mentioned previously, cross-terms are basis functions which depend both on the last argument
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}
and preceding arguments
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{1:k-1}}
. The presence of these terms requires that such maps must use integrated rectifiers (
Section

3.1.1
) to ensure monotone
S_{k}
:

S_{2}\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{2}}\right)=\underbrace{c_{0}+c_{1}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}}+c_{2}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}}^{2}}_{\text{nonmonotone part }g\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}}\right)}+\underbrace{\int_{0}^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{2}}}\exp\bigg(\overbrace{c_{3}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}}^{2}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}t}}^{\text{cross-term}}+c_{4}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}t}+\overbrace{c_{5}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}t}^{2}}^{\text{cross-term}}\bigg)d{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}t}}_{\text{monotone part }f\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{2}}\right)}.

(23)

The presence of cross-terms permits increased control over the local details in the transformations from
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
to
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
.
In principle, it is also possible to make
Equation

23
separable in
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}
by removing the dependencies of
f
on
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{1:k-1}}
, yielding a separable map with monotonicity through an integrated rectifier. Since the resulting
S_{k}
would not be linear in the coefficients
c
, however, we cannot make use of a more efficient optimization scheme (
Appendix

A
). In the following subsection, we will develop some intuition about the strengths and limitations of different map parameterizations.

•

Marginal

), however, the pullback approximation does not approximate the true target pdfs well. This effect can be subtle: in the corner-multimodal distribution, it is only noticeable through the emergence of a fourth phantom mode.

•

Separable
maps provide much better approximations to the target pdfs, but have some interesting caveats: while they successfully recover the wavy target distribution, they struggle with the distribution once it is rotated by
90
°, and they yield a slight—if less pronounced—fourth phantom mode for the corner-multimodal target. We will discuss the reason for this below.

•

Cross-term
maps prove most versatile, recovering all three target distributions well at the cost of a larger parameter space.

In practice, cross-terms provide greater control over local features. To understand why, let us take a closer look at a single map component inversion
S_{k}^{-1}
, which forms the basis of the map inversion (see
Section

2.3.1
) that ultimately yields the pushforward samples in
Figure

9
. Consider a generic map component function comprised of a nonmonotone part
g
and a monotone part
f
:

{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{k}}=S_{k}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{1:k-1}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}})=g({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{1:k-1}})+f({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{1:k-1}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}).

(24)

As discussed in
Section

2.3.1
, the inversion of
S_{k}
relies on one-dimensional root finding. Reformulating this expression yields the root finding objective for
S_{k}^{-1}
, conditioned on the outcomes
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{1:k-1}}
of previous map component inversions:

(25)

If we now apply the simplifications of the three map parameterizations above, we obtain three different objectives for the root finding problem:

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{k}}-g(-)

\displaystyle=f({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}})

\displaystyle\text{(marginal maps)}

(26)

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{k}}-g({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{1:k-1}})

\displaystyle=f({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}})

\displaystyle\text{(separable maps)}

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{k}}-g({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{1:k-1}})

\displaystyle=f({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{1:k-1}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}})

\displaystyle\text{(cross-term maps)}

{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{S}^{{\sharp}}\eta}
we have observed in

. Each term above takes on a different role during the conditional inversion: the reference samples
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{k}}
are an independent input, unaffected by the map parameterization choice, and define a
random initial offset
for the root finding. The nonmonotone term
g
acts as an additional
dynamic offset
for the inversion based on previous values, and the monotone term
f
defines the
shape of the inverse function
for the one-dimensional root finding over the unknown
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}
. From this perspective, the differences between the map parameterizations emerge from how each handles the dependence on previous values
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{1:k-1}}
:

1.

Marginal
maps (
Figure

10
, top row) permit only a constant nonmonotone term
g(-)
, which results in no dynamic offset. Their monotone term
f({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}})
likewise only depends on
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}
. In consequence, marginal maps extract the same
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}
from a specific
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{k}}
for any given value of
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{1:k-1}}
.

2.

Separable
maps (
Figure

10
, center row) also feature monotone term
f({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}})
that depends only on
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}
, which keeps the inverse function’s shape constant for all values of
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{1:k-1}}
. Their off-diagonal term
g({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{1:k-1}})
can induce varying offsets for different
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{1:k-1}}
, introducing a simple dependence on previous values.

3.

Cross-term
maps (
Figure

10
, bottom row) can vary both the dynamic offset (nonmonotone term
g({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{1:k-1}})
) and the inverse function’s shape (monotone term
f({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{1:k-1}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}})
) with different
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{1:k-1}}
, and thus provide the most expressive maps, but often at higher computational cost.

These insights reveal why separable maps succeeded to recover the wavy distribution, but failed to capture its features once it is rotated (
Figure

9
). For the original wavy distribution, its (vertical) conditionals at different horizontal positions are mostly of the same shape, just shifted vertically, which makes them a perfect fit for recovery via separable maps. Once the distribution is rotated, however, the true vertical conditionals become more challenging to recover. Note that if we were to change the order of the target vector to
[{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{2}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}}]
instead of
[{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{2}}]
, separable maps would succeed in recovering the rotated wavy distribution and instead fail for the standard one. This illustrates the subtle influence of input variable ordering on a map’s approximation ability.

{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1}}
, providing the greatest degree of flexibility.

3.1.3
Basis functions

A very important part of the parameterization of the map component function
S_{k}
is its construction: what basis functions should be used to represent
S_{k}
and how much nonlinearity should they permit? In a nutshell, simpler—perhaps separable—parameterizations are more computationally efficient, but can struggle to recover features of the target pdf
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
that do not resemble the reference
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
, as discussed at the beginning of this section. By contrast, more complex parameterizations add the flexibility required to capture “localized” features of
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
(e.g., skewness, multi-modality, …), but risk unfavourable bias-variance trade-offs.
While there are alternative choices
(
Lunde and Ramgraber, 2026
)
, we discuss two common basis function families that trade off different advantages:

Polynomial basis functions

In function approximation, polynomials are a canonical choice for forming an approximation; in particular, harkening to polynomial chaos expansion and other traditional stochastic function approximation literature
(
Le Maître and Knio, 2010
;
Ernst et al., 2012
)
, we can choose orthogonal polynomials for our approximation function class. Since this work focuses on unbounded
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}}
, we will consider the probabilists’ Hermite polynomial family
\{\mathrm{He}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
, which has the orthogonality property:

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{He}_{i}(x)\mathrm{He}_{j}(x)\;\;\underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\tau}}\exp(-x^{2}/2)}_{\text{Gaussian weight}}\;\;\mathrm{d}x=j!\delta_{ij}=\begin{cases}j!&j=i\\
0&j\neq i\end{cases},

(27)

where
\tau=6.283185\ldots
is twice Archimedes’ constant. Broadly,
Equation

27
shows what it means for polynomials of different orders (e.g., linear, quadratic, cubic, etc.) to be orthogonal under the Gaussian weight. Similar to orthogonal vectors in linear algebra, orthogonal polynomials (more generally, orthogonal functions) are often chosen as a basis for function approximation, as their elements contain no “overlapping information” with respect to a particular weight (which may or may not match
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
). As these polynomials are dependent on the pdf, a few other orthogonal polynomial families with their respective weights and support include Legendre polynomials (respecting a constant weight on
(-1,1)
) and Laguerre polynomials (respecting weight
e^{-x}
on
(0,\infty)
)
(
Szegő, 1939
)
.
These polynomial families may be suited for different problems depending on characteristics of
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
and
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}

(
Wang and Marzouk, 2022
;
Sharp et al., 2026
)
.

Generally, if a polynomial family
\{\psi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
is orthogonal with respect to a weight
\rho
(e.g., the probabilist Hermite polynomials take
\rho
as the Gaussian weight),
one can approximate a given well-behaved function
f(x)
with
\hat{f}(x)=c_{1}\psi_{1}(x)+c_{2}\psi_{2}(x)+\cdots+c_{P}\psi_{P}(x)
using a finite number
P
of these polynomials. Guarantees for this approximation are often given when measuring the error according to our weight
\rho
via
\int(f(x)-\hat{f}(x))^{2}\ \mathrm{d}\rho(x)

(
Ernst et al., 2012
)
. If we believe that
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
and
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
are very similar, choosing polynomials orthogonal with respect to our reference
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
(which we know the orthogonal polynomial family of) will also perform well when the input is distributed according to
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
(which we generally will not know the orthogonal polynomial family of). On the other hand, if
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
and
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
are not very similar, such guarantees are not particularly helpful; practitioners see this manifest as a need for more complex map parameterizations (see
Section

3.1.2
).

As they have unbounded support, Hermite polynomials exert global influence and often require high-order terms to capture fine distributional features. While versatile, the use of polynomial basis functions has a few pitfalls which demand caution:

1.

Importance of standardization
: In response to the above, we would like to make
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
resemble
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
more closely by pre-processing the data we have from
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
.
When working with a Gaussian reference
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
, it is thus generally advised to standardize
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}}
by trying to match the mean and variance of
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
. We do this by (i) subtracting the empirical mean
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\bar{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}}=1/N\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}^{i}
and (ii) scaling each marginal to unit variance by dividing it through the unbiased estimator of the empirical standard deviation
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\sigma_{k}}
where
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\sigma}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}k}}^{2}=(N-1)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathsf{X}}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}k}}^{i}-{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\bar{\mathsf{X}}_{k}})^{2}
before formulating and applying a transport map
12
12

12

Alternatively, one may standardize the samples by forming the empirical cdf
\tilde{F}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi_{k}}}
along the marginal for
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}
, then standardize the samples by composing
\tilde{F}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi_{k}}}
with the inverse standard Gaussian cdf
F_{{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta_{k}}}^{-1}
, that is to say,
F_{{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta_{k}}}^{-1}\circ\tilde{F}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi_{k}}}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}})
, ensuring each marginal of the standardized samples has a Gaussian distribution.

\mathbf{S}
. All subsequent map operations are then implemented in this standardized space. Finally, the map’s outputs are transformed back into target space by reversing the standardization. In effect, standardization wraps a separate linear transformation around the transport map, and thereby does not affect the validity of the operations inside this wrapper. In the subsequent sections, we will assume by default that the samples have been standardized. As pointed out in
Morrison et al. (2022)
, invertible diagonal transformations retain the conditional independence structure of the target distribution. In other words, there is no loss of information while working in the marginally rescaled space.

2.

Edge control
: A practical challenge of polynomials is their growth in the tails for higher-order terms. This often leads to volatility if the map is evaluated or inverted far from zero. To address this issue, one useful option is the use of
edge-controlled terms
such as
Hermite functions

\mathcal{H}_{j}
. This variant of basis functions are defined as probabilist’s Hermite polynomials
\mathrm{He}_{j}
of order
j
multiplied with a Gaussian weight, which reverts the polynomial’s output to zero far from zero:

\mathcal{H}_{j}\left(x\right)=\mathrm{He}_{j}\left(x\right)\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{4}\right).

(28)

A nice feature of this form is that
\mathcal{H}_{i}(x)\mathcal{H}_{j}(x)=\mathrm{He}_{i}(x)\mathrm{He}_{j}(x)\exp(-x^{2}/2)
, and so
\{\mathcal{H}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
will inherit the same error properties as
\{\mathrm{He}_{j}\}
when measuring error without any weight
\rho
, i.e.,
\int(f(x)-\hat{f}(x))^{2}\ \mathrm{d}x
.

Illustrations of the first few orders of Hermite functions are provided in
Figure

11
A. Note that expressing the map exclusively in terms of Hermite functions causes the map component
S_{k}
to revert to zero in the tails, which can be undesirable whenever extrapolation may be required. In practice,
limiting the Gaussian weight term to polynomials of order two or larger is a good compromise, thus we recommend retaining the linear terms without a weight.

Figure 11:
Two types of edge-controlled basis functions based on Hermite polynomials. (A) Hermite functions are multiplied with a Gaussian weight, causing them to approach zero in the tails. (B) Replacing the Gaussian weight with a scaled cubic spline leads to a different edge-controlled basis function. This enforces limited support, ensuring the function reverts to zero at a finite distance
r
.

A practical issue with Hermite functions can emerge for target distributions
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
which include sharp features. From
(
Szegő, 1939
)
, the largest maximizer
x^{*}_{j}
of
\mathrm{He}_{j}
grows at a rate of
x^{*}_{j}=\sqrt{j}+\mathcal{O}(j^{-1/6})
, which means that if
\mathcal{H}_{j}
has nontrivial values on
(-r,r)
, we will have that
\mathcal{H}_{4j}
has nontrivial values on
(-2r,2r)
; e.g., it is reasonable to estimate from
Figure

11
(A) that
r>4
for
j=2
and so
\mathcal{H}_{8}
has nontrivial values on
(-8,8)
. Due to this “global” nature of polynomials influencing the edges of the Hermite functions, maps for such distributions often involve high-order terms with high-magnitude coefficients of opposing signs partially cancelling each other. While this is often unproblematic within the support of the training samples, the resulting high-magnitude coefficients can extend the influence of some Hermite functions farther into the tails, resulting in undesirable tail effects. As an alternative, we might prefer to employ a weight term that reverts the weights to zero at a finite distance. For instance, using a cubic spline weight yields a different
edge-controlled
Hermite polynomial:

\mathcal{H}_{j}^{\text{EC}}\left(x\right)=\mathrm{He}_{j}\left(x\right)\left(2\min\left(1,|x|/r\right)^{3}-3\min\left(1,|x|/r\right)^{2}+1\right),

(29)

where
r
is a finite edge-control radius specified by the user. Examples of such basis functions are illustrated in
Figure

11
B. When using these functions in the integrated-rectified formulation, this will create
\mathbf{S}
that is linear outside of the hypercube
(-r,r)^{d}
.

Radial basis functions

A second useful class of basis functions is
radial basis functions
(RBFs). RBFs exert more local influence than combinations of polynomial basis functions, and generally require two additional parameters: a position parameter
\mu
and a scale (or bandwidth) parameter
\sigma
. To avoid the need to optimize these parameters along with the map’s coefficients
c_{i}
, one can estimate
\mu
and
\sigma
from the empirical quantiles along the marginals of the training samples
\{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}

(
Spantini et al., 2022
)
. For the positions
\mu_{i}
, they propose to place each RBF at the empirical quantiles
q_{i/(j+1)}\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}\right)
for
i=1,\dots,j
, where
j
is the total number of RBFs along the
k
-th marginal. Based on the resulting
\mu_{i}
, the corresponding scales
\sigma_{i}
are determined by averaging the distances to neighbouring RBFs. To simplify notation, it can be useful to define local coordinates for each RBF:

{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}^{\text{loc},i}}=\frac{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}-\mu_{i}}{\sqrt{\tau}\sigma_{i}},

(30)

where the
\tau
is again twice the Archimedes’ constant. Radial basis functions are particularly useful for multimodal distributions because they render the map selectively expressive wherever the target distribution’s samples are located. Where a superposition of many high-order polynomials may be required to recover isolated modes, often only a few RBFs may suffice. Besides conventional RBFs, three useful related basis functions are
left edge terms
(LET),
integrated RBFs
(iRBF), and
right edge terms
(RET):

\displaystyle\operatorname{RBF}\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}^{\text{loc},i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\tau}}\exp\left(-{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}^{\text{loc},i}}}^{2}\right),

(31)

\displaystyle\operatorname{iRBF}\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}^{\text{loc},i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\operatorname{erf}\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}^{\text{loc},i}}\right)\right),

\displaystyle\operatorname{LET}\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}^{\text{loc},i}};\sigma_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\sigma_{i}\sqrt{\tau}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}^{\text{loc},i}}\left(1-\operatorname{erf}\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}^{\text{loc},i}}\right)\right)-\frac{4\sigma_{i}}{\sqrt{\tau}}\exp\left(-{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}^{\text{loc},i}}}^{2}\right)\right),

\displaystyle\operatorname{RET}\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}^{\text{loc},i}};\sigma_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\sigma_{i}\sqrt{\tau}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}^{\text{loc},i}}\left(1+\operatorname{erf}\left({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}^{\text{loc},i}}\right)\right)+\frac{4\sigma_{i}}{\sqrt{\tau}}\exp\left(-{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}^{\text{loc},i}}}^{2}\right)\right).

Illustrations of these functions are provided in
Figure

12
. Note that iRBFs, LETs, and RETs are monotone functions, and thus an excellent choice for a
linear separable
map (see
Section

3.1.2
). Other sigmoid-like functions and monotone “edge terms” that revert to linear in the tails include logistic and softplus functions, which may be computationally faster.

3.2
Optimization

With the structure and parameterization of the map component functions
S_{k}
defined, we may now address the question of how to identify the specific map
\mathbf{S}
that relates the target distribution
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
to the reference distribution
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
. In general, we have two different ways to estimate the map, depending on the type of information available:
maps from samples
, and
maps from densities
. Both follow a very similar approach, but differ fundamentally in the direction in which they define the transport map:

Maps from samples

•

require target samples
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}}\sim{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}

•

require evaluations of the reference
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}

•

seek a map
\mathbf{S}
such that
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{S}_{\sharp}\pi}={\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}

Maps from densities

•

require reference samples
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{z}}\sim{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}

•

require evaluations of unnormalized target
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\tilde{\pi}}

•

seek a map
\mathbf{R}
such that
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{R}_{\sharp}\eta}={\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}

3.2.1
Maps from samples

In the preceding sections, we have often implicitly assumed a
maps from samples
scenario. In other words, the target pdf
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
is only known through samples
, i.e., we have a collection of samples
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}^{i}\sim{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
. In this case, we seek to minimize the “distance” between the target pdf
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
and the pullback pdf
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{S}^{\sharp}\eta}
. While several notions of distance between pdfs are possible, the Kullback–Leibler divergence is commonly used for its computational tractability. For two arbitrary pdfs
p,q
and a generic RV
\mathbf{a}
, we define their Kullback–Leibler divergence
D(p\|q)
as:

D(p\|q)=\mathbb{E}_{p}\left[\log\frac{p(\mathbf{a})}{q(\mathbf{a})}\right]=\int p(\mathbf{a})\log\frac{p(\mathbf{a})}{q(\mathbf{a})}d\mathbf{a}.

(32)

Thus, we can formulate our optimization problem as finding the map
\mathbf{S}_{\text{opt}}
that minimizes the difference, measured in terms of the Kullback–Leibler divergence, between the target
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
and its approximation
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{S}^{\sharp}\eta}\approx{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
:

\mathbf{S}_{\text{opt}}=\arg\min_{\mathbf{S}\in\mathcal{F}}D({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}\|{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{S}^{\sharp}\eta}),

(33)

where
\mathcal{F}
is some appropriate class of functions (see
Section

3.1
). This is often referred to as the minimization of the
forward
Kullback–Leibler divergence. We note that
D({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}\|{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{S}^{\sharp}\eta})
can be expanded as:

D({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}\|{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{S}^{\sharp}\eta})={\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}}\left[\log\frac{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}})}{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{S}^{\sharp}\eta}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}})}\right]={\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}}\left[\log{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}})\right]-{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}}\left[\log{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{S}^{\sharp}\eta}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}})\right],

(34)

where the first term
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}}\left[\log{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}})\right]
does not depend on the map
\mathbf{S}
. Thus, we can alternatively view
Equation

33
as an attempt to maximize the log-likelihood (or minimize the negative log-likelihood) of the map’s pullback density
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{S}^{\sharp}\eta}\approx{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
over the target density
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
:

\mathbf{S}_{\text{opt}}=\arg\min_{\mathbf{S}\in\mathcal{F}}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}}\left[-\log{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{S}^{\sharp}\eta}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}})\right].

(35)

Figure 13:
The optimization objective of
maps from samples
maximizes the log-likelihood of a set of fixed samples
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}}\sim{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
over the map’s pullback density
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{S}^{{\sharp}}\eta}\approx{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
. In practical terms, the optimization seeks maps
\mathbf{S}
which mold the reference pdf
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
to the target samples
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}}
.

An illustration of the optimization process is provided in
Figure

13
. Substituting in
Equation

4
for
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{S}^{\sharp}\eta}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}})
into the cost function
Equation

35
and expanding the logarithms yields:

{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}}\left[-\log{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{S}^{\sharp}\eta}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}})\right]={\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}}\left[-\log{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}(\mathbf{S}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}}))-\log|\det\mathbf{\nabla}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}}}\mathbf{S}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}})|\right].

(36)

Due to the lower triangular structure of
\mathbf{S}
, we know that
\mathbf{\nabla}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}}}\mathbf{S}
is a lower triangular matrix. As established in
Equation

7
, the determinant of a lower triangular matrix is given by the product of its diagonal entries:

\log\det\mathbf{\nabla}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}}}\mathbf{S}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}})=\log\left[\prod_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\partial S_{k}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}})}{\partial{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}}\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\log\frac{\partial S_{k}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}})}{\partial{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}}.

(37)

Mind that, due to the monotonicity of
S_{k}
in
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}
(see
Section

3.1.1
), the derivative
\partial_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}}S_{k}
will always be positive, and the logarithm in the right-hand side sum of
Equation

37
will be defined.
Plugging this identity into the cost function, we only now enforce that our reference
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
is multivariate Gaussian by substituting its pdf into the expectation:

{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}}\left[-\log{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{S}^{\sharp}\eta}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}})\right]={\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}}\left[-\log\frac{1}{\tau^{D/2}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}S_{k}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}})^{2}-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\log\frac{\partial S_{k}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}})}{\partial{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}}\right].

(38)

where
\tau
is again twice the Archimedes’ constant. Recognizing that the first term is constant with respect to
\mathbf{S}
, it can be discarded from the objective function. Using
N
samples from the target distribution
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}^{i}\sim{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
and merging the two sums over
K
, we obtain a Monte Carlo approximation of the loss function:

\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{S})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\frac{1}{2}S_{k}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}^{i})^{2}-\log\frac{\partial S_{k}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}^{i})}{\partial{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}}\right).

(39)

From this expression, we can recognize that the summands of the full objective function
\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{S})
are independent of each other. As a consequence, we define separate objective functions
\mathcal{J}_{k}(S_{k})
for each map component
S_{k}
:

\mathcal{J}_{k}(S_{k})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{1}{2}S_{k}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}^{i})^{2}-\log\frac{\partial S_{k}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}^{i})}{\partial{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}}\right).

(40)

Equation

40
may then be minimized with off-the-shelf software developed for other optimization and machine learning tasks. If the linear separable formulation in
Section

3.1.1
is chosen, it is only necessary to optimize the monotone part
f
of each map component
S_{k}
numerically, as the coefficients for the nonmonotone terms
g
can be derived in closed form. More detail on this is provided in
Appendix

A
. Note that since the objective functions in
Equation

40
are independent of each other, the map component functions
S_{k}
can be optimized in arbitrary order, even in parallel. Each objective function
\mathcal{J}_{k}
balances a first quadratic term that drives samples
S_{k}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}^{i})^{2}
to zero with a second term that acts as a log-barrier function to prevent null diagonal entries in the Jacobian of the transport map
(
Le Provost et al., 2021
)
.

3.2.2
Maps from densities

Alternatively, we choose a different optimization strategy in the case that we can evaluate the target pdf
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
, but only up to a constant of proportionality; i.e., we have access to evaluating density
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\tilde{\pi}}={\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}m}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
for some unknown
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}m}>0
. A key difference when learning a
map from density

is that we choose to define the triangular map in the opposite direction as before: where we previously considered a forward map
\mathbf{S}
that maps target samples
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}
to reference samples
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Z}}}
, we now define a forward map
\mathbf{R}
mapping reference samples
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Z}}}
to target samples
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}
. Correspondingly, the pushforward distribution
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{R}_{{\sharp}}\eta}
now approximates the target distribution
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
via the forward map
\mathbf{R}
, and the pullback distribution
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{R}^{{\sharp}}\pi}
now approximates the reference distribution
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
via the inverse map
\mathbf{R}^{-1}
. To find the optimal
\mathbf{R}_{\text{opt}}
, we seek to minimize the Kullback–Leibler divergence between the reference distribution
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
and the pullback distribution
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{R}^{\sharp}\pi}

(
Marzouk et al., 2017
)
:

\mathbf{R}_{\text{opt}}\in\arg\min_{\mathbf{R}\in\mathcal{F}}D({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}\|{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{R}^{\sharp}\pi}),

(41)

where
\mathcal{F}
is some appropriate class of functions (see
Section

3.1.3
) and
\in
is used to suggest multiple minimizers (see
Baptista et al. (2023)
for a discussion of why this could happen). This is often referred to as the minimization of the
reverse
Kullback–Leibler divergence. We visualize minimization of
Equation

41
in
Figure

14
. Similar to
Equation

34
in the maps from samples scenario, we expand
D({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}\|{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{R}^{\sharp}\pi})
as:

D({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}\|{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{R}^{\sharp}\pi})={\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbb{E}_{\eta}}\left[\log\frac{{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{z}})}{{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{R}^{\sharp}\pi}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{z}})}\right]={\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbb{E}_{\eta}}\left[\log{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{z}})\right]-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbb{E}_{\eta}}\left[\log{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{R}^{\sharp}\pi}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{z}})\right],

(42)

Figure 14:
The optimization objective of
maps from densities
maximizes the log-likelihood of a set of fixed samples
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Z}}}\sim{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
over the map’s pullback density
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{R}^{{\sharp}}\tilde{\pi}}\approx{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
. In practical terms, the optimization seeks maps
\mathbf{R}
which mold the unnormalized target pdf
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\tilde{\pi}}
to the reference samples
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Z}}}
.

where the first term
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbb{E}_{\eta}}\left[\log{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{z}})\right]
does not depend on the map
\mathbf{R}
. Thus,
Equation

41
can be viewed as an attempt to maximize the log-likelihood (or minimize the negative log-likelihood) of the map’s pullback density
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{R}^{\sharp}\pi}\approx{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
over the reference density
\eta
:

\mathbf{R}_{\text{opt}}\in\arg\min_{\mathbf{R}\in\mathcal{F}}{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbb{E}_{\eta}}\left[-\log{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{R}^{\sharp}\pi}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{z}})\right].

(43)

We may now substitute the pullback density from the change-of-variables formula (
Equation

4
):

{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}\left({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{z}}\right)\approx{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{R}^{{\sharp}}\pi}\left({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{z}}\right)={\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}\left(\mathbf{R}\left({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{z}}\right)\right)\left|\det\mathbf{\nabla}_{{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{z}}}\mathbf{R}\left({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{z}}\right)\right|.

(44)

Equivalent to
Equation

37
, we express the log determinant of the forward map
\mathbf{R}
as a product of its diagonal entries:

\log\det\mathbf{\nabla}_{{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{z}}}\mathbf{R}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{z}})=\log\left[\prod_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\partial R_{k}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{z}})}{\partial{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{k}}}\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\log\frac{\partial R_{k}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{z}})}{\partial{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{k}}}.

(45)

Substituting the two equations above into the cost function of
Equation

43
, we obtain:

{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbb{E}_{\eta}}\left[-\log{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{R}^{\sharp}\pi}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{z}})\right]=-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbb{E}_{\eta}}\left[\log{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}(\mathbf{R}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{z}}))\right]-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbb{E}_{\eta}}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{K}\log\frac{\partial R_{k}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{z}})}{\partial{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{k}}}\right].

(46)

Since we assume we only have access to an unnormalized target density
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\tilde{\pi}}
, we can expand the first term
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbb{E}_{\eta}}\left[\log{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}(\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{z}))\right]
using the identity
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}={\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\tilde{\pi}}/{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}m}
, where
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}m}
is the unknown normalization factor:

{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbb{E}_{\eta}}\left[-\log{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{R}^{\sharp}\pi}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{z}})\right]=-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbb{E}_{\eta}}\left[\log{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\tilde{\pi}}(\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{z}))\right]+{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbb{E}_{\eta}}\left[\log{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}m}\right]-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbb{E}_{\eta}}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{K}\log\frac{\partial R_{k}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{z}})}{\partial{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{k}}}\right].

(47)

Recognizing that the normalization factor
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}m}
does not depend on the map
\mathbf{R}
, we can discard it from the objective function:

\mathbf{R}_{\text{opt}}\in\arg\min_{\mathbf{R}\in\mathcal{F}}-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbb{E}_{\eta}}\left[\log{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\tilde{\pi}}(\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{z}))\right]-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbb{E}_{\eta}}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{K}\log\frac{\partial R_{k}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{z}})}{\partial{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{k}}}\right].

(48)

Using
N
samples from the reference distribution
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Z}}}^{i}\sim{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
, we obtain a Monte Carlo approximation of the loss function:

\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{R})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(-\log{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\tilde{\pi}}\left(\mathbf{R}\left({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Z}}}^{i}\right)\right)-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\log\frac{\partial R_{k}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Z}}}^{i})}{\partial{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}z_{k}}}\right).

(49)

This yields the objective function for optimizing maps from densities. Two comments are in order regarding this optimization problem. First, observe that opposed to maps from samples, we have to first generate an ensemble of
N
i.i.d. samples
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Z}}}^{i}\sim{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
.
Second, since we now find a map
\mathbf{R}
to pulls the reference back to the target, we still avoid evaluating the inverse map
\mathbf{R}^{-1}
when calculating the loss
\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{R})
for some candidate map
\mathbf{R}\in\mathcal{F}
.
Opposed to optimizing a map from samples (
Section

3.2.1
), however, this objective function cannot generally be subdivided into individual objectives for each constituent map component
R_{k}
. As such, all map components are usually found via a single optimization problem.

We note that there are other loss functions that we could consider beyond the Kullback–Leibler divergence, for instance the variance diagnostic
(
El Moselhy and Marzouk, 2012
;
Richter et al., 2020
)
.We do not consider these options here in greater detail, as the Kullback–Leibler objective is widely used and quite tractable. Moreover, in the maps-from-samples setting, a minimizer of the forward Kullback–Leibler divergence corresponds to a
maximum likelihood estimator
of the transport map within the chosen function class; this link is useful for both interpretation and theory
(
Wang and Marzouk, 2022
)
.

3.2.3
Regularization

When the number of samples
N
is small relative to either the target’s dimensionality
K
or the number of parameters for the map components, using more complex maps (i.e., increasing the number of the parameters) risks overfitting.
In turn, this might lead to numerical instabilities. To prevent these issues, we can introduce an
L^{1}
or
L^{2}
regularization penalty to the objective functions introduced in the preceding section.
In the maps from samples (
Section

3.2.1
) case, we take map component
S_{k}
for some fixed
k
, and let
c_{i}
parameterize this component via, e.g., polynomial coefficients. The regularized objective function would be:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{k}^{\text{L1}}(S_{k})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{1}{2}S_{k}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}^{i})^{2}-\log\frac{\partial S_{k}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}^{i})}{\partial{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{j}\lambda_{i}|c_{i}|

\displaystyle L^{1}\ \text{regularization},

(50)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{k}^{\text{L2}}(S_{k})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{1}{2}S_{k}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}^{i})^{2}-\log\frac{\partial S_{k}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}^{i})}{\partial{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{j}\lambda_{i}c_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle L^{2}\ \text{regularization}.

where we add a
\lambda
-weighted penalty term to the objective functions, which penalizes non-zero parameters
c_{i}
for each of the
i=1,\ldots,P_{k}
basis functions of map component
S_{k}
(see
Section

3.1.3
). This creates an optimum closer to where the parameters are zero, which can be interpreted as penalizing map complexity.
The regularization factors
\lambda_{i}
are user-specified hyperparameters that should be tuned to the problem at hand, ensuring that the penalization is large enough to have an effect and small enough to avoid excessive biasing. For maps from densities, the optimization objective follows an equivalent structure to
Equation

50
. Similar to other applications with regularization parameters, a bit of experimentation is required to find a suitable balance between regularization and bias.

In practice, the use of
L^{1}
regularization is a common strategy to discover sparsity in the triangular map, which we recall corresponds to conditional independence (see
Section

2.3.3
). In many cases, it is more efficient to impose sparsity and parsimony by construction. We will discuss practical strategies to learn a parsimonious degree of map complexity in
Section

4.2
.

4
Practical heuristics

With the theoretical foundations and implementation-related details established, we now discuss a few important features and tricks that help to further enhance the potential of triangular transport maps.

4.1
Composite maps for conditional sampling

Composite maps are used to improve conditional samples from imperfect maps.
For complex target distributions
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
, it is often infeasible to define and optimize a map of sufficient complexity to capture all features of
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
. In such cases, the map
\mathbf{S}
will fail to completely normalize the target
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
, making certain features persist in the pushforward
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{S}_{\sharp}\pi}
. At the same time, the pullback
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{S}^{\sharp}\eta}
may not be a good approximation of
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
.

Figure

15
illustrates this for two different levels of map complexity.

This imperfection has important consequences for Bayesian inference with triangular transport maps. Since the (conventional) map’s conditional inverse only samples a conditional of the pullback distribution
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{S}^{{\sharp}}\eta}
, not of the real target
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
, the quality of the resulting posterior samples will depend on the mismatch between
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{S}^{{\sharp}}\eta}
and
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
.
Yet, we are often given a sample
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}=({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}_{1:k}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}_{k+1:K}})
of a joint distribution
\pi({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{1:k}},{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{k+1:K}})
and would like to create a high-quality sample
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}_{k+1:K}^{*}}
conditioned on some fixed
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0.5352,0.7227}\mathbf{x}_{1:k}^{*}}
. To do this, we apply a
composite map
to each sample
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}}
, defined as:

{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}^{*}_{k+1:K}}=\mathbf{S}_{k+1:K}^{-1}(\cdot\ ;\,{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0.5352,0.7227}\mathbf{x}_{1:k}^{*}})\circ\mathbf{S}_{k+1:K}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{k+1:K}};\,{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{1:k}}).

(51)

Note that
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0.5352,0.7227}\mathbf{x}_{1:k}^{*}}
and
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{1:k}}
are two different objects: The
former
comes from the real world (i.e., it is a fixed value, independent from the random sample
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}}
). For instance,
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0.5352,0.7227}\mathbf{x}_{1:k}^{*}}
may be specific measurement values on which we want to condition
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
. By contrast, the
latter
are jointly sampled with
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}_{k+1:K}}
from
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
.
While this may initially seem complicated, the key idea of composite maps is as follows:

Figure 15:
Simple maps capture only simple features. (A) For a non-Gaussian target
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
, a simple linear map does not yield a Gaussian pushforward
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{S}_{\sharp}\pi}\neq{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
. (B) Likewise, its pullback
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{S}^{\sharp}\eta}\neq{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
does not provide a good approximation to the target. (C & D) More complex nonlinear maps yield better approximations. Color in (A) and (C) indicates the sample’s original cluster membership, whereas in (B) and (D) color indicates a sample position’s angle w.r.t.
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
’s mean.

1.

We have an approximate map
\mathbf{S}
. Its
forward evaluation
thus does not transform samples from the true target
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}}\sim{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
into samples from the reference
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{z}}\sim{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
, but instead yields samples from the pushforward
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\tilde{\mathbf{z}}}\sim{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{S}_{{\sharp}}\pi}\neq{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
. These pushforward samples
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\tilde{\mathbf{z}}}
preserve features of the target distribution
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
the map did not capture. For instance, a linear map
\mathbf{S}
can only shift, scale, and rotate the target distribution
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
, which is insufficient if
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
is non-Gaussian (
Figure

15
A).

2.

Likewise, an approximate
inverse map
does not transform samples from the reference
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{z}}\sim{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
into samples from the true target
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}}\sim{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
, instead yielding samples from the pullback
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\sim{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{S}^{{\sharp}}\eta}\neq{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
which lack any features of
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
that the map has not captured. For instance, the pullback
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{S}^{{\sharp}}\eta}
of a linear map
\mathbf{S}
only samples a Gaussian approximation of the target distribution (
Figure

15
B).

3.

Applying an invertible approximate forward map, directly followed by its inverse, cancels the map’s approximation error and always restores the original target samples. By construction, we know that the composition of
\mathbf{S}
with its inverse
\mathbf{S}^{-1}
maps a sample to itself, i.e., if
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\tilde{\mathbf{z}}}=\mathbf{S}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}})\sim{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{S}_{\sharp}\pi}
, then
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{S}^{-1}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\tilde{\mathbf{z}}})
.

4.

This restoration of unresolved features persists in part during the conditioning operation. Instead of reference samples
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{z}}\sim{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
(
Figure

16
B), we might thus use pushforward samples
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\tilde{\mathbf{z}}}\sim{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{S}_{{\sharp}}\pi}
to preserve some features of
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
. This generally reduces the map’s approximation error (
Figure

16
C); see Proposition 11 in
Baptista (2022)
.

Keep in mind that if we instead use a sufficiently complex, nonlinear map formulation (e.g.,

{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{z}}\sim{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
or pushforward samples
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\tilde{\mathbf{z}}}\sim{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{S}_{{\sharp}}\pi}
, as both distributions will be quite similar (
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\mathbf{S}_{{\sharp}}\pi}\approx{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.6328,0.0352}\eta}
). In the general case, however, we may not be certain about the quality of our map approximation without explicit validation. In consequence, we recommend the use of composite maps as a safer strategy for conditional sampling.

4.2
Map adaptation

Identifying a suitable degree of map complexity can be a challenging task. For one, conditional independence structures (see
Section

2.3.3
) are not always known a-priori. Similarly, finding a suitable degree of complexity for each map component to achieve an optimal bias-variance trade-off is not an easy task. To address these issues, we may draw on map adaptation algorithms. Examples of and pseudocode for such algorithms are provided by
Baptista et al. (2023)
;
Lunde and Ramgraber (2026)
.

In brief,
the method starts with a triangular map that is viable and particularly simple; for example, one may use the identity map
S_{k}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}})={\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{k}}
.
The algorithm then proposes a list of candidate basis functions for each
S_{k}
(
Figure

17
). These candidate terms either (i) extend the dependence of
S_{k}
to a previous state dimension
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}x_{1:k-1}}
, or (ii) incrementally increase the complexity of an existing dependence by adding higher degrees of nonlinearity.

Figure 17:
Adaptive transport maps gradually expand the complexity and expressiveness of the map components, adding terms corresponding to the largest absolute gradient of the objective function (
Equation

40
) among a set of candidate terms. This identifies the necessary degree of map complexity gradually. The lower-row grids illustrate the order and composition of the map components at each iteration. Each filled cell is an accepted candidate, with coordinates
(3,5)
in the
S_{2}
block corresponding to a term
\mathcal{H}_{3}(x_{1})\mathcal{H}_{5}(x_{2})
.

To determine which candidate basis functions are added to the map, the algorithm in
Baptista et al. (2023)
calculates the gradient of the optimization objective function (see
Section

3.2
) with respect to the candidates’ coefficients. It then adds the candidate corresponding to the steepest derivative. The idea behind this approach is that candidate terms with steep optimization objective gradients promise to improve the map’s approximation significantly, whereas those with flat gradients add little beyond complexity. The algorithm then proposes new candidates to the expanded map further and repeats the procedure until a user defined stopping criterion is met.

This process gradually expands the complexity and expressiveness of each map component. Since this process is iterative, this adaptation algorithm can be expensive if the number of candidate basis functions is large, as is the case for
S_{k}
with many variable dependencies, particularly if the map parameterization permits cross-terms (
Figure

17
). The use of maps without cross-terms can drastically reduce this computational demand,
as it would restrict the expansion and exploration of candidates to basis functions corresponding to the outer edges in
Figure

17
, making a two-dimensional result look like the letter “L.”

5
Summary & Outlook

5.1
State of the art

Measure transport methods comprise an active and quickly evolving field in statistics and machine learning. Within this broader field, triangular maps occupy an important niche: transparent and versatile, they provide a powerful set of tools for conditional sampling from limited information. This, in turn, makes them highly useful for Bayesian inference.

To summarize, triangular transport methods have a number of important
advantages
:

1.

Parsimony
: The ability to fine-tune the parameterization of a triangular map, coupled with the sparse variable dependence that a triangular map inherits from conditional independence, allows triangular transport to be adapted to the computational demands, data availability, and distributional complexity of a given problem—increasing efficiency and accuracy.

2.

Numerical convenience
: Triangular maps are simple to learn and invert, and many common parameterizations provide optimization guarantees for the problem of learning maps from samples (see, e.g.,
Appendix

A
and
Baptista et al. (2023)
).

3.

Transparency
: Triangular maps provide a close correspondence between the map component functions
S_{k}
and a specific factorization of the target distribution
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
. This link makes it easy to predict the impact of changes to the map on the resulting statistical model,
and often allows us to decompose an inference or sampling task into smaller problems that are themselves interpretable.

At the same time, triangular maps face some important challenges. Ensuring the scalability of triangular transport with the dimension of the target distribution demands exploiting conditional independence to produce a sparse map, which in turn requires an appropriate variable ordering.
We reiterate that, under mild assumptions, a triangular map exists between any target and reference
(
Santambrogio, 2015
, Section 2.3)
, which includes any permutation of our target random variables. In practice, however, these maps may vary in sparsity and other notions of complexity:
see
Section

2.3.3
and
Figure

9
, respectively, and note that the rotated “wavy” distribution in
Figure

9
actually corresponds to a different variable ordering. There are many approaches to finding orderings that maximize sparsity: for example, minimum degree algorithms
(
Amestoy et al., 1996
;
Cuthill and McKee, 1969
, e.g., )
from graph theory, or other algorithms inspired by sparse Cholesky factorizations
(
Baptista et al., 2024d
;
Schäfer et al., 2021
)
. Setting aside sparsity, however, the impact of variable ordering on the difficulty of
approximating
a given map component function
S_{k}
is generally more difficult to predict, as it depends on finer properties of the target distribution at hand.

5.2
Broader context within transport

There are many ways to parameterize a triangular transport map that are not discussed here—for example, performing a closed-form integration over a parameterization of the target pdf. This can be done using squared polynomials
(
Zanger et al., 2024
)
, parameterizing the square-root of the pdf
(
Dolgov et al., 2020
)
, or even a composition of polynomials parameterizing this square-root pdf
(
Cui and Dolgov, 2022
)
, often in the context of numerical tensor methods that permit efficient integration. Regarding the
approximation
of triangular transport maps, there are several works elucidating approximation rates by, e.g., polynomials or neural networks
(
Zech and Marzouk, 2022a
;
Zech and Marzouk, 2022b
;
Baptista et al., 2024b
;
Westermann and Zech, 2025
)
; other works analyse the
statistical
consistency and convergence of triangular maps learned from finite samples
(
Wang and Marzouk, 2022
;
Irons et al., 2022
)
.

More generally, triangular maps sit within the broader field of measure transport, which has seen remarkable computational advances in recent years. Many other approaches to constructing transport maps exchange triangular structure for different assumptions or desiderata.
For example,
optimal
transport methods seek a mapping between two distributions that is as “close” to the identity function as possible, where closeness is encoded by a particular integrated
transport cost
(for instance, the distance between the input and output of the map)
(
Peyré et al., 2019
)
. Many works seek to approximate optimal transport maps by searching over an appropriate function class—e.g., by parameterizing classes of convex functions (rather than our monotone triangular formulation) and writing the map as the gradient of such a function
(
Makkuva et al., 2020
;
Wang et al., 2025
)
. There are some connections between triangular maps and optimal transport maps. First, each component
S_{k}
of a monotone triangular map is optimal in its last (scalar) input
x_{k}
, conditioned on the first
k-1
inputs
x_{1:k-1}
.
Conditional
optimal transport maps
(
Tabak et al., 2021
;
Carlier et al., 2016
;
Baptista et al., 2024c
;
Pooladian et al., 2025
)
generalize this idea from strictly triangular to block triangular structure. As described in
Carlier et al. (2009)
, triangular maps also arise as the limit of optimal transport maps obtained with increasingly anisotropic quadratic transport cost.

Normalizing flows
(
Papamakarios et al., 2021
)
are another widely used class of transport methods, which typically do not seek to approximate some canonical (i.e., optimal or triangular) map, but rather use a composition of many simpler invertible transformations to construct a transport map, increasing the number of functions (layers) in the composition until the desired expressivity is reached. Autoregressive normalizing flows in fact use triangular maps as a building block, with parameterizations ranging
in complexity from relatively simple to the complex rectified formulations discussed in
Section

3.1.1

(
Wehenkel and Louppe, 2019
;
Jaini et al., 2019
)
. These flows interleave such triangular layers with permutations of the variables, which mitigates ordering issues described above at the cost of sacrificing the conditional independence and sparsity properties of triangular maps. Such constructions are partly inspired by earlier autoregressive models that directly parameterize marginal conditional densities
(
Bond-Taylor et al., 2021
)
. Finally, many contemporary flow-based generative models
(
Song et al., 2021
;
Albergo et al., 2025
;
Lipman et al., 2023
;
Liu et al., 2023
)
can be understood as dynamical or continuous-time representations of transport maps; here the transport is represented by the flow map of a system of ordinary differential equations (ODEs) or by the corresponding evolution of densities under a system of stochastic differential equations. Some models of this kind explicitly employ triangular structure
(
Heng et al., 2021
)
, but triangular maps have also proven useful in the analysis of more general neural ODE models
(
Marzouk et al., 2024
)
.

Within this broader landscape, what is the
role of triangular transport
? We believe that the greatest strength of triangular transport lies in its extraordinary capacity for
parsimony
: Triangular maps may not only naturally exploit conditional independence—a special property among transport-based samplers—but they allow us to fine-tune the level of nonlinearity with which we resolve statistical dependencies. In consequence, these methods allow us to tailor the maps precisely to the system’s demands by building some structure of the target distribution into the map itself. In the small sample size regime, we believe this property holds the key to challenging the
prevalence of linear methods, which are still the state of the art for practical settings from meteorological data assimilation to subsurface parameter inference.

5.3
Where to go from here

As a relatively new method, much of the potential of triangular maps has yet to be realized. This includes a number of promising research directions in applications, methodology, and theory.
To unlock the full potential of their parsimony, a key effort for the operationalization of triangular transport is the development of efficient
adaptation strategies
. One of the greatest challenges of the parameterizations discussed in this paper is their scaling with the dimension of the target distribution. One way to mitigate this “curse of dimensionality” is to only include terms one knows will help, which is the problem that the adaptive algorithms above attempt to tackle. As briefly discussed in
Section

4.2
, these algorithms automatically seek to identify parsimonious maps for general inference problems. Increasing the efficiency of these methods will ease their applicability to higher-dimensional systems. Promising research avenues include exploring connections to graph structure learning algorithms that
estimate conditional independence structure
(
Baptista et al., 2024d
;
Liaw et al., 2025
;
Drton and Maathuis, 2017
;
Zheng et al., 2020
)
or developing information criteria
(
Lunde, 2025
;
Konishi and Kitagawa, 1996
)
to help identify suitable levels of map complexity.

A closely-related subject is research into the properties, advantages, and drawbacks of different
parameterizations
of triangular transport. While we have explored many practical details in this tutorial, we have still only scratched the surface of this topic. A promising avenue is to focus on known computational bottlenecks of existing parameterizations.
As a simple example, it is known the
\mathrm{erf}
function (see
Equation

31
) has unstable evaluations near the extremes of its domain and is often computationally expensive; exploring more efficient alternatives is of cross-cutting utility. Similarly, some parameterization heuristics such as centering RBFs at marginal quantiles of the target distribution remain largely ad hoc, especially given strong dependencies in
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\pi}
; what can we do to improve these heuristics?
What kinds of parameterizations should be employed when the distributions at hand are heavy-tailed: do the “edge terms” of maps need to increase sub- or super-linearly (depending on the direction of the map), and does the use of composite maps relieve the need to model tails when performing conditional sampling?
More broadly, is there a practical role for
nonparametric
representations of transport maps
(
Pooladian and Niles-Weed, 2021
)
in high-dimensional problems? And whether in parametric or nonparametric settings, how can
regularization
or penalization schemes for learning maps from limited information be more rigorously designed and scaled?
Relatedly, it is common nowadays to have multi-fidelity data sources (e.g., models that can be resolved at different discretization levels); how can we efficiently learn maps via samples and model evaluations of differing fidelities?
How can we learn maps from partially observed samples or censored data (i.e., when some samples have missing entries)? Can triangular transport be used in this and other settings for data imputation?

Furthermore, there are many open questions at the intersection of triangular transport with numerical analysis, relevant to probabilistic modelling and uncertainty quantification. For example, there has been recent work marrying deterministic quadrature or quasi-Monte Carlo methods with transport
(
Cui et al., 2025a
;
Klebanov and Sullivan, 2026
;
Liu, 2026
)
, promising higher-order convergence rates for the approximation of expectations weighted by complex distributions. It is interesting to consider how triangular structure could be leveraged in this setting and to understand broader links to cubature
(
Cools, 1997
)
or other high-dimensional integration schemes.

Finally, as we have argued that sparsity provides a route to scalability, it is important to understand the impact of
approximate
sparsity in transport, and hence
approximate
conditional independence in continuous non-Gaussian distributions—e.g., the error incurred by discarding weak conditional dependencies. (See
Johnson and Willsky (2007)
;
Jog and Loh (2015)
for analyses in the Gaussian case.) This line of work has direct links to
localization
methods widely used in data assimilation, whose analysis is a topic of much current research
(
Al-Ghattas and Sanz-Alonso, 2024
;
Tong and Morzfeld, 2023
;
Gottwald and Reich, 2025
)
. Moreover, sparsity is only one form of low dimensionality. Other work seeks scalable inference and probabilistic modelling via explicit dimension reduction—for instance, by searching for low-dimensional subspaces that capture important interactions between parameters and data, or by identifying low-dimensional “updates” from prior to posterior in the Bayesian setting
(
Baptista et al., 2022
;
Zahm et al., 2022
;
Constantine, 2015
;
Fukumizu et al., 2007
;
Härdle and Stoker, 1989
;
Cui et al., 2014
)
. These notions correspond naturally to specific transport representations
(
Brennan et al., 2020
;
Cao et al., 2026
;
Cui et al., 2025b
;
Chen and Liu, 2025
)

and enable the solution of problems that would otherwise—without the exploitation of structure—be intractable.

As we conclude, we want to leave you, the reader, with some concrete numerical tools to explore your own applications of triangular transport—if you do not want to code your own. given as:

\displaystyle({{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{P}_{k}^{\text{non}}}}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{P}_{k}^{\text{non}}}+\lambda\mathbf{I}){\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\widehat{\mathbf{c}}_{k}^{\text{non}}}

\displaystyle=-{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{P}_{k}^{\text{non}}}}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{P}_{k}^{\text{mon}}}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\text{mon}}}

(57)

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\widehat{\mathbf{c}}_{k}^{\text{non}}}

\displaystyle=-\underbrace{({{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{P}_{k}^{\text{non}}}}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{P}_{k}^{\text{non}}}+\lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{P}_{k}^{\text{non}}}}^{\top}}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{M}_{k,\lambda}}}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{P}_{k}^{\text{mon}}}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\text{mon}}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}-{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{M}_{k,\lambda}}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{P}_{k}^{\text{mon}}}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\text{mon}}}.

Following best practices from numerical linear algebra, this inversion should not be computed explicitly; rather, one should use a numerical solver for the systems induced. Then, substituting Expression (
57
) into the optimization objective, we obtain a new objective for
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\text{mon}}}
(i.e., entirely independent from the nonmonotone coefficients),

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{k}^{\text{mon}}({\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\text{mon}}};\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|-{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{P}_{k}^{\text{non}}}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{M}_{k,\lambda}}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{P}_{k}^{\text{mon}}}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\text{mon}}}+{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{P}_{k}^{\text{mon}}}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\text{mon}}}\|^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{b}}^{i}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}k}}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\text{mon}}}+\frac{\lambda}{2}(\|{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\widehat{\mathbf{c}}_{k}^{\text{non}}}\|^{2}+\|{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\text{mon}}}\|^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|\underbrace{(\mathbf{I}-{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{P}_{k}^{\text{non}}}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{M}_{k,\lambda}}){\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{P}_{k}^{\text{mon}}}}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{A}_{k,\lambda}}}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\text{mon}}}\|^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{b}}^{i}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}k}}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\text{mon}}}+\frac{\lambda}{2}(\|\underbrace{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{M}_{k,\lambda}}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{P}_{k}^{\text{mon}}}}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{D}_{k,\lambda}}}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\text{mon}}}\|^{2}+\|{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\text{mon}}}\|^{2})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}\|{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{A}_{k,\lambda}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\text{mon}}}\|^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{b}}^{i}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}k}}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\text{mon}}}+\frac{\lambda}{2}(\|{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{D}_{k,\lambda}}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\text{mon}}}\|^{2}+\|{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\text{mon}}}\|^{2}),

(58)

where
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{A}_{k,\lambda}}
,
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{D}_{k,\lambda}}
and
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{b}}^{i}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}k}}
can be precomputed prior to the optimization routine via evaluation of the basis functions. Remarkably, this method translates the original loss function into a very simple constrained convex optimization problem:

\boxed{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\widehat{\mathbf{c}}_{k}^{\text{mon}}}=\argmin_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\text{mon}}}\geq\mathbf{0}}\frac{1}{2}\|{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{A}_{k,\lambda}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\text{mon}}}\|^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{b}}^{i}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}k}}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\text{mon}}}+\frac{\lambda}{2}(\|{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{D}_{k,\lambda}}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\text{mon}}}\|^{2}+\|{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\text{mon}}}\|^{2}),\quad{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\widehat{\mathbf{c}}_{k}^{\text{non}}}=-{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{M}_{k,\lambda}}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{P}_{k}^{\text{mon}}}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\widehat{\mathbf{c}}_{k}^{\text{mon}}}.}

(59)

Note that the (element-wise) constraint of
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\text{mon}}}\geq\mathbf{0}
is vital to maintain monotonicity; this can be enforced explicitly during optimization using particular optimization algorithms, e.g., L-BFGS-B (i.e. Low-memory BFGS with box constraints), implicitly by constructing an objective with a log-barrier term, or employing a convex transformation of the optimization objective (e.g. optimize over
\mathbf{p}_{k}^{\text{mon}}:=\log{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\text{mon}}}
). Since we often will have coefficients with zero values, the first methodology might be preferable (though no empirical results appear here). This optimization objective, notably, requires no evaluations of the map during optimization, that is to say, the optimization is as fast as the combination of the implementation of linear algebra algorithms called and the optimization routine used.

In the case where we have more parameters than samples, it is worth noting that using this formulation is remarkably sensitive to
\lambda
because
{{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{P}_{k}^{\text{non}}}}^{\top}{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{P}_{k}^{\text{non}}}
is no longer full rank. Thus the calculation of
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{M}_{k,\lambda}}
solves a system with possibly poor numerical properties (the industry of methods for
ill-conditioned
systems is dedicated to such problems). For vanishingly small
\lambda
, this corresponds to the problem of overfitting and the fact that we have infinite choices of
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\widehat{\mathbf{c}}_{k}^{\text{non}}}
for any given choice of
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3125,0}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\text{mon}}}
.
