Microalbuminuria in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Andrea Erlac, Jesus Diez-Manglano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and objectivesWe conducted this systematic review and meta-analysis to determine the prevalence of microalbuminuria in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and its association with COPD severity.MethodsWe conducted a literature search in PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and the Virtual Health Library from inception to December 31th, 2024. The search strategy was: (‘chronic obstructive pulmonary disease’ OR COPD OR ‘chronic airflow obstruction’ OR emphysema OR ‘chronic bronchitis’) AND (microalbuminuria OR albuminuria OR proteinuria OR ‘urinary albumin creatinine ratio’ OR ‘urinary albumin excretion’). We included studies recruiting patients diagnosed by spirometry and providing data of microalbuminuria prevalence. We excluded studies in patients with other respiratory diseases associated with COPD, and studies published in predatory journals. The pooled prevalence of microalbuminuria was calculated using the DerSimonian-Laird binary random effects method, and the association of microalbuminuria with COPD and its severity was assessed using the Mantel-Haenszel random effects method.ResultsTwenty-five articles were included. The prevalence of microalbuminuria in patients with COPD was 49.3% [95% confidence interval (CI) 34.3–64.4%], which was higher than in the control population, with an estimated odds ratio of 3.94 (95% CI 2.25–6.60). In patients with severe to very severe COPD, it was higher than in those with mild to moderate COPD, with an estimated odds ratio of 4.55 (95%IC 2.85–7.28). Patients with COPD and microalbuminuria had an estimated decrease in PaO2 of 9.98 mmHg (95% CI 13.94–6.02) and an increase in PaCO2 of 8.08 mmHg (95% CI: 4.87–11.29).Interpretations and conclusionsMicroalbuminuria is highly prevalent in patients with COPD, with a much higher frequency than in the general population. It is more common in patients with more severe airflow obstruction and is associated with hypoxia and hypercapnia.

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a chronic, progressive, and heterogeneous disease of the airways and alveoli, preventable and treatable, characterised by persistent airflow limitation and the appearance of respiratory symptoms. Additionally, it is accompanied by comorbidities that influence its course and prognosis.1 Among these, cardiovascular diseases are very common and constitute one of the main causes of death in people with COPD.2

Chronic kidney disease is an understudied comorbidity in patients with COPD, despite its high prevalence.3-5 Several factors commonly associated with COPD, such as smoking, hypoxia, and systemic inflammation, can affect glomerular function.6,7 Microalbuminuria is an early biomarker of renal damage, reflecting endothelial dysfunction at the glomerular level, and has been used as an early indicator of cardiovascular disease.8 Some studies have reported that urinary albumin/creatinine excretion is increased in patients with COPD, and that microalbuminuria is associated with worse health outcomes.9,10 However, research on microalbuminuria in COPD is limited, and most available studies are small.

Our hypothesis was that albuminuria is a marker present in patients with COPD, both in the stable phase and during exacerbations. This systematic review was conducted with the main aim of assessing the prevalence of microalbuminuria in patients with COPD and comparing it with controls without COPD. Secondary objectives were to compare the prevalence of microalbuminuria between patients with mild-moderate and severe-very severe COPD, as well as the association between microalbuminuria and mortality, readmission rates, hospitalisations, and exacerbations.

Methods

Study registration

The protocol for this systematic review was registered in PROSPERO under the number CRD42025630144.

Data sources and search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Scopus, Web of Science, and the Virtual Health Library from inception to December 31, 2024. The search strategy was: (‘chronic obstructive pulmonary disease’ OR COPD OR ‘chronic airflow obstruction’ OR emphysema OR ‘chronic bronchitis’) AND (microalbuminuria OR albuminuria OR proteinuria OR ‘urinary albumin creatinine ratio’ OR ‘urinary albumin excretion). Additional searches were performed in Google Scholar and ResearchGate, and a manual review of the bibliographic references of retrieved articles was also conducted.

Study selection

To be included, studies had to meet the following inclusion criteria: diagnosis of COPD by spirometry and provision of microalbuminuria prevalence data. Additionally, for comparison with the general population, studies had to include a control group of individuals without COPD.

Exclusion criteria were studies with COPD diagnosed without spirometry, studies in patients with other respiratory diseases associated with COPD, and studies published in predatory journals. Predatory journals were identified as those listed at https://www.predatoryjournals.org/ . No language restrictions were applied.

When two or more studies were based on the same population, during the same period, and provided overlapping results, the most recent or the one providing more data was selected for inclusion. After removing duplicates, the authors independently reviewed studies by screening titles and abstracts. Studies meeting the inclusion criteria or with abstracts lacking complete information were retrieved for full-text analysis. Any discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

Data extraction

From each selected study, the following information was extracted: first author, year of publication, country, study type, sample size, mean age of participants, sex, BMI, smoking status, COPD status, forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1), and outcomes.

The outcome considered was the prevalence of microalbuminuria. When the case or control group was divided into subgroups, the weighted mean and standard deviation for these combined subgroups were calculated.

Airflow obstruction was assessed using FEV1 and was considered mild if > 80% of the predicted value, moderate if 50–80%, severe if 30–50%, and very severe if < 30%, according to the GOLD initiative.1

Quality assessment

The risk of bias in the included studies was independently assessed using the quality assessment tool for observational cohort and cross-sectional studies (National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute, NIH, USA), available at https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health-topics/study-quality-assessment-tools . The tool includes 14 items on objectives, population, eligibility rate, sample size, exposure, outcomes, blinded assessors, follow up, and confounding variables. Both authors rated the studies as good, fair, or poor quality. All studies were included in the meta-analysis; however, a sensitivity analysis was performed, including only those of good quality.

Synthesis and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using OpenMeta[Analyst] (Brown University, USA) and Review Manager version 5.3 (Cochrane Collaboration, Baltimore, MD, USA). The weighted prevalence of microalbuminuria was calculated using the DerSimonian-Laird binary random effects method, and results are presented as a percentage and 95% confidence interval (95% CI). The association of microalbuminuria with COPD compared to the general population was calculated using the Mantel-Haenszel random effects method, and results are presented as odds ratios and 95% CI.

Heterogeneity was determined using the I2 statistic. A low level of heterogeneity was considered if I2 < 50% and P>0.10. A meta-regression analysis was performed, including mean age, percentage of males, percentage of smokers, and mean %FEV1 in each study as covariates.

Several sensitivity analyses were planned, excluding poor-quality studies, including only studies with a sample size greater than 100 participants, and including studies from grey literature and predatory journals. Potential publication bias was analysed with a funnel plot.

Prevalence of microalbuminuria in patients with COPD

A total of 25 studies, including 3,846 participants, provided data on the prevalence of microalbuminuria in patients with COPD. The pooled mean prevalence was 49.3% (95%CI 34.3–64.4%). Heterogeneity was high (I2 99.4%).

Meta-regression analysis showed that the percentage of males and the mean %FEV1 could explain the heterogeneity (Supplementary Table I), indicating that a higher percentage of males was associated with a higher prevalence of microalbuminuria, and a lower %FEV1 was also associated with a higher prevalence of microalbuminuria ( Figs. 2 and 3).

Microalbuminuria in patients with COPD and controls

Ten studies, with a total of 12,282 participants, compared the prevalence of microalbuminuria between COPD patients and control groups.9,10,25-27,29-33 COPD was associated with microalbuminuria, with an estimated odds ratio of 3.98 (95%CI 2.38–6.36), P<0.001 ( Fig. 4). Heterogeneity was high (I2 85%).

Microalbuminuria and severity of airflow obstruction

Twelve studies, including 2,005 participants, compared the prevalence of microalbuminuria between patients with severe-to-very severe COPD and those with mild-to-moderate COPD.9,13-15,18,19,22-24,26,32,33 More severe COPD was associated with a higher prevalence of microalbuminuria, with an estimated odds ratio of 4.55 (95% CI: 2.85–7.28), P< 0.001 ( Fig. 5). Heterogeneity was high (I2 59%).

Forest plot of prevalence of MAU in severity stages of COPD.

Microalbuminuria and acute exacerbation of COPD

Three studies with 288 patients compared the prevalence of microalbuminuria in patients with stable versus exacerbated COPD.23,24,30 Exacerbations of COPD were associated with a higher prevalence of microalbuminuria, with an estimated odds ratio of 7.40 (95% CI: 3.83–14.28), P< 0.001 (Supplementary Fig. 1). Heterogeneity was low (I2 3%).

Influence of microalbuminuria on symptoms, exercise capacity, exacerbations, hospitalisations, mortality, hypoxia, and hypercapnia

The relationship between microalbuminuria and symptoms or exercise capacity is controversial. Some studies found that COPD patients with microalbuminuria experience more dyspnea and cover less distance in the six-minute walk test, while others reported no differences.13,21,26

Six studies with 719 participants reported on PaO2, and another six with 939 participants on PaCO2 in COPD patients with and without microalbuminuria.13,15,17,19,21,26,28 COPD patients with microalbuminuria had an estimated decrease in PaO2 of -9.98 mmHg (95% CI -13.94, -6.02) and an increase in PaCO2 of 8.08 mmHg (95% CI 4.87–11.29).1

Three studies provided data on C-reactive protein, with two reporting higher levels in patients with microalbuminuria.17,21,28

In the study by Bartziokas with 320 patients experiencing acute exacerbation, those with microalbuminuria had longer hospital stays, more intubations, and higher mortality. During a one-year follow up, they had more exacerbations, more hospitalisations, and higher mortality.21 In the study by Romunstadt, COPD patients with microalbuminuria had higher mortality over the following 12 y.9

Discussion

Our meta-analysis reveals that approximately half of patients with COPD have microalbuminuria, with a prevalence four times higher than in the general population and occurring more frequently in more severe stages of airflow obstruction.

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis to study the occurrence of microalbuminuria in COPD patients. The NHLBI pooled cohort study, which presented data from over 10,000 patients with more than 5 y of follow up, observed that albuminuria was associated with impaired lung function and a higher incidence of COPD.34 In the second Nord Trøndelag Health Study in Norway, with 12 y of follow up, individuals with an obstructive respiratory pattern and microalbuminuria had higher mortality.9 All this justifies the study of microalbuminuria in people with COPD and its possible use as a cardiovascular risk marker, as suggested by Casanova and Celli.35

At present, the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying the development of microalbuminuria in patients with COPD remain incompletely elucidated. However, three principal mechanisms have been proposed. First, tobacco use reduces renal blood flow, glomerular filtration rate, and filtration fraction, thereby increasing renovascular resistance and promoting arteriolar thickening.36 These alterations may result in renal parenchymal hypoperfusion. Transient and recurrent ischemic episodes under such conditions could lead to glomerular injury, characterised by compensatory hypertrophy and hyperfiltration of the remaining glomeruli, ultimately culminating in increased urinary albumin excretion. In support of this hypothesis, prior studies have demonstrated a positive association between both cigarette smoking and smokeless tobacco use and the presence of microalbuminuria.37-39

Second, the hypoxic insult induced by tobacco smoke may disrupt ventilation-perfusion matching, resulting in systemic hypoxemia. This hypoxic state has been implicated in the upregulation of vascular endothelial growth factor, downregulation of endothelial nitric oxide synthase, and the release of various vasoactive mediators from endothelial cells.40,41 These processes collectively influence vascular tone and promote phenotypic changes in smooth muscle cells, leading to increased arterial stiffness. Several studies have reported an association between increased arterial stiffness and microalbuminuria in individuals with COPD.20,27,29 Likewise, the hypoxia-induced endothelial stress is thought to contribute to sustained systemic inflammation and oxidative damage, processes exacerbated by protease-antiprotease imbalance and reactive oxygen species generated in the COPD lung microenvironment. These local inflammatory processes can extend systemically, as evidenced by elevated circulating levels of inflammatory markers. Notably, C-reactive protein concentrations have been associated with microalbuminuria in both COPD and non-COPD populations.12,17,28,42 Chronic inflammation may impair endothelial integrity, increase microvascular permeability, and precipitate endothelial dysfunction, thereby facilitating the abnormal transglomerular passage of albumin.

Third, chronic intermittent hypoxia has been reported to induce renal dysfunction.43 Previous studies under high-altitude hypoxia conditions have demonstrated elevated glomerular permeability44 and, in patients with type 2 diabetes, intermittent nocturnal hypoxia has been identified as a risk factor for microalbuminuria.45 The long-term intermittent hypoxemia present in COPD could trigger increased sympathetic activity, altering capillary endothelial permeability and causing proteinuria during acute exacerbations.46 We observed greater hypoxemia and hypercapnia in COPD patients with microalbuminuria. Taken together, these findings support the observation that microalbuminuria is more prevalent among patients with more severe COPD and those experiencing acute exacerbations, as they are more frequently subjected to recurrent hypoxic episodes.

Microalbuminuria has been associated with increased mortality in individuals with diabetes and in the general population.47,48 However, its clinical implications in patients with COPD remain insufficiently defined, and existing studies are limited. Nonetheless, current evidence suggests a potential relationship between microalbuminuria and adverse outcomes, including a higher frequency of exacerbations, increased hospitalisation rates, and elevated mortality.9,21 To clarify the prognostic relevance of microalbuminuria in COPD and assess the influence of disease management on its occurrence, further prospective longitudinal studies with adequate sample sizes and extended follow up durations are warranted. Observational data indicate that several pharmacological agents known to reduce microalbuminuria —such as angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, angiotensin II receptor blockers, and sodium-glucose cotransporter type 2 inhibitors—may also confer cardiovascular benefits and reduce exacerbation frequency in patients with COPD.49,50

Findings from our meta-analysis suggest that microalbuminuria determination may serve as a simple, cost-effective, and non-invasive biomarker for identifying patients with COPD who exhibit systemic endothelial dysfunction and are at elevated risk for target organ damage and cardiovascular complications, even in the absence of established comorbid conditions. Based on this evidence, we propose the routine inclusion of microalbuminuria screening via urine analysis in the periodic assessment of patients with COPD, particularly those with severe airflow limitation or a history of frequent exacerbations.

One of the principal strengths of our study is the rigorous and comprehensive literature search strategy employed, encompassing six major scientific databases without language restrictions. This approach facilitated the inclusion of studies representing diverse geographic and demographic populations. Nonetheless, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, we observed considerable heterogeneity among the included studies with respect to geographic origin, sociodemographic characteristics, and the proportion of smokers and female participants. While this variability necessitates cautious interpretation, it simultaneously enhances the external validity of our findings. Second, some studies lacked information regarding COPD status or the number of active smokers at the time of enrolment, which precluded their inclusion in specific analyses. Third, the predominance of cross-sectional designs among the included studies limits causal inference, although the observed associations between microalbuminuria and COPD are nonetheless informative. Finally, the funnel plots suggest possible publication bias. It is probable that small studies with extreme prevalences and positive results could be overrepresented. Given the established advantages of larger study populations in terms of statistical power and result reliability, this constitutes an additional limitation.

In conclusion, microalbuminuria appears to be more prevalent in patients with COPD, with levels correlating positively with disease severity and hypoxemia. Microalbuminuria may represent a promising biomarker for the identification of individuals with severe COPD and an elevated risk of exacerbations, mortality, and systemic comorbidities. Further investigation is required to determine whether therapeutic interventions targeting microalbuminuria can positively influence disease progression and clinical outcomes in COPD.
