Explainable deep unsupervised learning for flood monitoring and anomaly detection using multivariate DWLR sensors
Authors: Saiprasad Potharaju, Swapnali N. Tambe, Ravi Kumar Tirandasu, Dudla Anil Kumar, B. Kiranmai, M. V. V. Prasad Kantipudi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Floods remain one of the most destructive natural hazards, causing extensive loss of life and infrastructure, largely due to delayed warnings generated by conventional threshold-based monitoring systems. Such systems are inherently reactive and fail to account for evolving hydrological behaviour driven by complex interactions among environmental variables. To address these limitations, this study proposes an unsupervised, explainable anomaly-detection framework for early flood warning using multivariate time-series data from Digital Water Level Recorder (DWLR) sensors. The proposed approach utilises sliding-window temporal modelling and an LSTM autoencoder to learn normal hydrological patterns from water-level, rainfall, temperature, pH, and dissolved-oxygen measurements, without requiring labelled events. Anomalies are identified through reconstruction error and statistically grounded thresholding, enabling proactive detection of abnormal system behaviour. To enhance transparency and trust, Integrated Gradients–based explainability is incorporated to quantify feature- and time-wise contributions to detected anomalies. Experimental results on real-world DWLR data demonstrate that the framework consistently identifies anomalous behaviour several weeks before critical water-level exceedance, providing meaningful early warning signals. Explainability analysis reveals that anomalies often originate from chemical and environmental factors, such as changes in pH and dissolved oxygen, before an observable water-level rise. The proposed framework offers a robust, interpretable, and data-driven solution for smart flood monitoring systems, supporting informed decision-making and improved disaster preparedness.

Introduction

Floods are among the most devastating hydro-meteorological events, resulting in widespread mortality, damage, and long-lasting socio-economic impacts. Traditional flood monitoring approaches often rely on fixed water-level thresholds for warning1. While these systems are easy to set up, they are primarily reactive, as flood warnings are triggered only when a threshold is exceeded. This reduces response and evacuation time, preparedness, and mitigation2. Static thresholds may also fail to adapt to changing hydrology due to the effects of climate variability, land-use changes, upstream developments, and local environmental factors.

Data modelling has become increasingly popular in hydrology to address these issues. In particular, deep learning, such as recurrent neural networks and long short-term memory (LSTM) networks have demonstrated the capacity to learn nonlinear temporal patterns in water discharge and water level time series3. These have been shown to perform better than other, more traditional methods, both statistical and physically-based, particularly in the short-term prediction and monitoring of floods. But most deep learning-based flood prediction systems still rely on supervised learning, which requires training data labelled as floods4. However, in real-world applications, these data are either partial, inconsistent in location, unavailable, or nonexistent, which has led to supervised flood prediction models being non-scalable and non-transferable.

The need for greater accuracy has driven the adoption of explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) in hydrology and machine learning. Black-box models used in critical applications, such as flood early warning systems, need to be transparent, trustworthy, and accountable. Recent research has provided explanations of deep learning predictions for floods using saliency maps, attention, and SHAP5. These attempts have shown the varying contributions of hydrological factors, such as rainfall and pre-event water levels, to the model output. However, most existing work has focused only on explainable unsupervised prediction, and the mechanisms underlying unsupervised prediction remain largely unexamined6.

Unsupervised anomaly detection provides a promising alternative because it learns normal hydrological behaviour and identifies deviations without requiring a labelled flood-risk proxy event. Autoencoder-based models are particularly useful for multivariate time-series anomaly detection, as they can reconstruct normal patterns and produce higher reconstruction errors for abnormal behaviour7. However, existing hydrological anomaly detection studies are often limited to univariate water-level monitoring or focus mainly on detection accuracy. They rarely explain which sensor variables and time steps contribute to an anomaly, reducing their usefulness for decision-makers in flood monitoring applications8.

The exploration of anomaly detection with autoencoders is a recent study relevant to sensor data and water infrastructure monitoring. Studies show that detecting reconstruction error is useful for explaining abnormal water-level behaviour in the absence of labelled events [9 ]. Many studies proposed approaches to estimate reconstruction error based on a single hydrological variable (i.e., water level). This singular focus on one variable makes the approaches less robust to complex interactions with the environment. Moreover, most studies do not explain the details of the approaches used, leaving resource managers without interpretative answers to the anomalies detected. This limits the approach to explaining the detected anomalies and assessing the critical risk, while the managers’ actions and decisions are based on the risk assessment.

Hydrological systems are complex, and the factors that influence water level changes can be attributed to combinations of meteorological, environmental, and biochemistry changes. Before the water level increases, other factors, e.g., temperature, rainfall, water pH, and dissolved oxygen, can significantly change and are also upstream, in the systems, or are the result of stagnation or human-related activity. Ignoring these factors will negatively affect detection and lead to the absence of early warning. Thus, future monitoring frameworks must be multidimensional, label-free, and able to explain the full potential of DWLR sensors while also providing a rationale for management experts.

In time series analysis, one frequent approach to capturing local temporal context is called sliding-window temporal modeling. The length of the window is crucial to the sensitivity and stability of the systems used to detect anomalies [10 ],[11]. Shorter windows focus on rapid fluctuations, capturing more noise, whereas longer windows capture more sustained trends, which are less responsive to changes12. Previous research has shown that the window size is sensitive for multivariate time-series anomaly detection, yielding robust detection performance13. Nevertheless, this sensitivity to window size is rarely examined in hydrology anomaly detection studies.

To address these gaps, this study proposes an explainable, unsupervised anomaly detection framework for flood monitoring using multivariate DWLR sensor time-series data. The framework applies sliding-window temporal modelling and an LSTM autoencoder to learn normal patterns from water level, rainfall, temperature, pH, and dissolved oxygen measurements. Anomalies are detected using reconstruction error and a statistically derived threshold, thereby avoiding dependence on manually defined flood labels or fixed water-level alert limits14. Integrated Gradients are further used to explain detected anomalies at both feature and time-step levels.

Fusing explainable AI methods within the anomaly detection pipeline serves as a primary innovation of this undertaking. Integrated Gradients is used to explain anomaly scores on specific features and time points, facilitating the detailed understanding of specific events15. The recent body of work focusing on time-series models suggests that maintaining the temporal context of explanations is especially critical in high-stakes domains16. The proposed framework, by providing a time–feature attribution map, helps practitioners identify genuinely hydrologically justified predecessors and to ignore spurious anomalies due to sensor noise or brief signal oscillation.

The framework goes beyond detection and explanation. An event-oriented validation approach assesses the temporal proximity of the detected anomalies and the critical water-level exceedance. In recent hydrological AI, this type of validation has been suggested to gauge the information content of an early warning system17. The capacity to detect anomalous window weeks in advance of the threshold exceedance demonstrates the promise of anomaly-based monitoring as an additional tool alongside flood forecasting.

It is important to note that the individual components used in this study, namely LSTM autoencoders and Integrated Gradients, are established techniques. The novelty of this work lies in their domain-specific integration for explainable, unsupervised flood-risk anomaly detection using multivariate DWLR sensor data. Unlike conventional threshold-based systems or supervised flood forecasting models, the proposed framework does not require labelled flood-risk proxy event and can identify abnormal multivariate sensor behaviour before water-level threshold exceedance. In addition, the framework provides feature-wise and time-wise explanations, which improves interpretability for operational flood-monitoring applications.

Related work

This section reviews previous studies relevant to flood early warning and water monitoring, focusing on traditional threshold-based warning systems, machine learning and deep learning for anomaly detection, autoencoder-based time-series forecasting, and new developments in explainable artificial intelligence for environmental and water monitoring.

Conventional flood early warning systems are largely based on thresholds of water level or discharge derived from historical data or design standards. When the variable of interest goes above the threshold, warnings are sent to authorities and the public. Therefore, these systems are simple to apply, supported by simple computing systems, and are widely used in national flood warning systems19. But threshold-based systems are reactive and only provide post-event warnings. Climate change, urbanization and flow regulation of upstream water bodies cause non-stationary hydrologic change that is not accounted for. Further, in a given basin, the thresholds you set for a basin may not generalize well in time and space and may cause either missed alarms or a nuisance of alarms. These challenges open space for exploring techniques that are adaptive and data driven to capture and respond to anomalies.

The application of machine learning techniques in flood analysis and hydrological monitoring has been growing due to their ability to understand non-linear relationships in large, complex environmental systems. The first studies used statistical learning methods, alongside support vector machines and clustering, to capture the most irregular hydrological patterns20. More recently, LSTMs and convolutional neural networks, two deep learning models, have outperformed previous models in studies focused on the temporal dependencies of river flow and water-level data21. While most of these models have focused on supervised flood forecasting, the early warning paradigm of anomaly detection has proven useful, especially when confidence in the labelling of the flood-risk proxy event is low. Even so, there is a lack of focus on ML-driven hydrological anomaly detection methods for handling numerous or multivariate predictors. Most remain univariate and rely on rainfall-driven predictors.

Autoencoders are among the most popular methods for unsupervised anomaly detection in multivariate time-series data. Autoencoders reconstruct normal data patterns while compressing their latent representations and assigning higher reconstruction errors to anomalous sequences that fall outside the learned manifold22. LSTM autoencoders, which can effectively capture and retain temporal dependencies in sequential data, perform well and are widely used in industrial monitoring and IoT sensor networks23. In the water domain, autoencoder-based models have also been used to detect anomalies in water distribution systems and sensor networks, demonstrating robustness to noise and missing data24. Most of the existing studies focus on detection accuracy. However, most existing studies focus on detection accuracy. Despite the focus on the detection accuracy, there is little to no explanation of the contributing variables or the time steps associated with the detected anomalies. This is a limitation in their explainability and functionality in a decision-support system.

Explainable AI has helped build trust among stakeholders and policy-makers in environmental modelling. SHAP, Integrated Gradients, and attention-based methods have recently been used in explaining deep learning model predictions in flood susceptibility mapping and hydrological forecasting25. The studies explain model predictions using feature importance and temporal influence, but primarily focus on the supervised learning paradigm. Understanding explainability in unsupervised anomaly detection, particularly across multiple dimensions of hydrological monitoring, is still developing. A few recent works have shown that attribution-based explanations enhance trust with anomaly alerts. However, the literature continues to be sparse with comprehensive frameworks that integrate anomaly detection, temporal explainability, and modelling26.

Recent studies further demonstrate the growing role of advanced computational methods in flood monitoring, prediction, and risk assessment. Roohi et al.28 reviewed flood damage assessment in mountainous regions and emphasized the importance of LiDAR, remote sensing, machine learning, and early warning systems for complex terrains. Similarly, Roohi et al.29proposed a hybrid flood prediction and assessment approach by integrating physical hydrological models, deep learning, ensemble learning, and satellite remote sensing. In addition, Roohi et al30. developed an ensemble machine-learning approach for flood damage assessment, showing the relevance of ML-based models in flood-risk applications. However, these studies mainly focus on flood mapping, damage assessment, physical-model integration, or supervised prediction. In contrast, the present work focuses on unsupervised multivariate DWLR sensor anomaly detection with feature-wise and time-wise explainability, an area that remains underexplored in the existing flood-monitoring literature.

Methodology

The proposed framework is designed to be explainable and to operate autonomously with unsupervised learning principles, allowing for the Digital Water Level Recorder (DWLR) sensor flooding data to be used for the first time to develop a preliminary flood warning system27. This approach seeks to establish the normal multivariate hydrological behaviour of the DWLR and associated sensor data streams such that deviations from “normal” behaviour can be detected to signal flood risk. The methodology incorporates the following steps for each potential anomalous event: data preparation and cleaning, windowing, reconstruction via an LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory) Autoencoder, scoring anomalies by reconstruction error, applying statistical thresholds, and driving explainability.

The data in this study were sourced from the public Digital Water Level Recorder (DWLR) Sensor Data dataset on Kaggle27. This is a time series of daily measurements taken between 1 January 2023 and 31 December 2023 at a location described as a village well in Incheon, South Korea. It contains 365 daily measurements, or samples, and five sensor measurements (water level, temperature, rainfall, pH, and dissolved oxygen). The original dataset had 20 missing values, all in the dissolved oxygen variable, representing 5.48% missingness in that variable and 1.10% missingness across the entire set of sensor data. Flooding is an event triggered by a multitude of hydrological and environmental factors, not simply a function of water levels. Water level measurement is the initial and foundational signal of flooding, as it registers the sum of the cell’s headwater inflows. Rain is the main catalyst that drives surface water runoff, and that causes a delay in the response of the watercourse (river) downstream. Temperatures influenced by seasonal hydrological cycles and variations in evapotranspiration rates are indirect factors in flooding. The presence and variation of hydrogen ions (pH) in water signal a disturbance in the environmental system, as evidenced by surface runoff, soil erosion, and the inflow of pollutants during heavy rainfall. The decomposition of organic material in the water, along with increased turbulence and seeding of the water with organic material from flood events, reduces dissolved oxygen (DO). This also occurs as a precursor to flood events. The fusion model described in this paper seeks to explain the overall interactions among these variables, including mechanisms that lead up to a flooding event, those that are transitional, and those that occur during the actual flooding event. The proposed model is expected to be more reliable than univariate models, produce fewer false positives, and trigger anomaly-based early-warning signals more accurately.

The estimated framework operates on multivariate DWLR time-series data, including water levels, rainfall, temperature, pH, and dissolved oxygen, as shown in Fig. 1. Instead of using water-level thresholds and labels for flood-risk proxy events, the framework builds a baseline model of normal water-level fluctuations. Anomalies are defined as time slices whose reconstruction errors exceed a statistically derived threshold. To maintain interpretability, the anomaly scores are analysed using feature-wise and time-wise attribution methods.

LSTM autoencoder architecture

An LSTM autoencoder consists of two components: an encoder and a decoder.

Encoder: The encoder maps an input window \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{W}_{k}$$\end{document} to a latent representation\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:{z}_{k}$$\end{document}:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{z}_{k}={f}_{enc}\left({W}_{k}\right)$$\end{document}

LSTM units are used to capture long-term dependencies and nonlinear temporal interactions among variables.

Decoder: The decoder reconstructs the input window from the latent representation:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{{W}_{k}}={f}_{dec}\left({z}_{k}\right)$$\end{document}

In a nutshell, the encoder transforms a multivariate sequence of sensor observations into a small “behavioral signature” of typical multivariate hydrology. The decoder tries to recover the original sequence. If the input is normal, reconstruction is good. When out-of-distribution dynamics are present, the reconstruction is less accurate.

The above example illustrates the underlying principle of the approach: the LSTM autoencoder can reconstruct non abnormal hydrological windows, but cannot reconstruct out-of-distribution multivariate dynamics. statistical Thresholding for Anomaly Detection

To justify the choice of the anomaly threshold, we evaluated different κ values for the rule τ = µ + κσ, where µ and σ are computed solely from the training reconstruction errors. Furthermore, thresholding based on percentiles of the training reconstruction error (95th, 97.5th, and 99th) was also considered. Smaller κ values generated more anomaly flags, leading to higher sensitivity, but also incurs more false alarms. Larger κ values were more conservative but also less sensitive. The κ value of 3 offers a good compromise between sensitivity and stability (Table 4), and was used for the final model.

Experimental setup and validation

Dataset and preprocessing

This research used real-world, multivariate Digital Water Level Recorder (DWLR) sensor data from 2023 for the experiments. The data represent five synchronised hydrologic and environmental variables: water level (m), rainfall (mm), temperature (°C), pH, and dissolved oxygen (mg/L), measured daily. To ensure numerical stability during training, all features were Z-score scaled after inconsistencies in the timestamps were removed and missing values were handled.

Given that the dataset is a chronological hydrological time series, we opted for a time-split rather than random sampling. The 365 daily observations were divided into the training, validation and test sets in a proportion of 70:10:20. To avoid data leakage, the handling of missing values, normalization, model training and anomaly-threshold estimation were all based only on the training set. The validation data were used solely for convergence monitoring and early stopping, and the test data were used only for final anomaly detection, lead-time assessment and explainability measurements.

We used a sliding-window approach with a window length of L = 7 days to segment the time series into overlapping multivariate temporal windows. Hence, each window is represented by a 7 × 5 tensor that captures short-term hydrologic dynamics and interdependencies.

Model training and hyperparameters

An LSTM autoencoder was trained unsupervisedly, using only sensor observations. The encoder was a stack of LSTM layers that encoded each input window into a low-dimensional latent space, while the decoder aimed to reconstruct the original input sequence.

The Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 5 × 10 − 4, Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss, and early stopping with validation loss were used to train the model. The model was trained for up to 40 epochs, and it converged at epoch 40, as per the training log. The hyperparameters used are summarised in Table 3.

Evaluation protocol using flood-risk proxy

Given that there are no ground-truth anomaly labels, validation was conducted using a proxy based on event-oriented and hydrological domain knowledge. Flood-risk events were labeled as the dates when the water levels stayed within the 95th percentile of the historical distribution, which is ± 4.4542 m. This system evaluates whether detected anomalies precede the predominant hydrological situations rather than being mere occurrences.

For each flood proxy event, the first anomaly detected within the specified lookback window was recorded, and the lead time (in days) between abnormality and flood-risk was calculated.

Validation Significance and Discussion

Across 19 flood-risk proxy events, the proposed framework detected anomalies before each event, with lead times ranging from 25 to 29 days. This indicates that the model can identify abnormal multivariate sensor behaviour before water-level threshold exceedance. However, because official flood-event labels are not available, these results should be interpreted as proxy-based early-warning evidence rather than confirmed flood prediction performance. The explainability results further support the anomaly decisions by identifying the sensor variables and time steps that contributed most to the reconstruction-error-based anomaly score.

The averaged explainability results show that pH is the dominant contributor to the detected anomaly windows, contributing nearly 45–47% in the reconstruction-error analysis and approximately 45–46% in the Integrated Gradients analysis as shown in Figs. 8 and 9. This indicates that abnormal pH behaviour was consistently difficult for the LSTM autoencoder to reconstruct and played a major role in anomaly formation. Dissolved oxygen was the second most influential variable, suggesting that changes in oxygen concentration may act as an early environmental disturbance signal caused by stagnation, runoff, organic matter inflow, or water-quality variation. Although water level showed a lower reconstruction-error contribution, its moderate Integrated Gradients contribution indicates that it remains relevant, especially as flood-risk conditions approach. Rainfall showed comparatively lower IG contribution, implying that it was not the strongest direct driver of anomaly decisions in the selected windows. The temporal heatmap further confirms that pH contributes across multiple time steps, particularly earlier positions such as t − 6 and t − 5 as shown in Fig. 10 showing that anomalies develop gradually over the temporal window rather than appearing as sudden one-day spikes.

Conclusion & future research

This research has developed an explainable framework that operates in an unsupervised mode for early flood warning systems using multivariate Digital Water Level Recorder (DWLR) sensor data. The threshold systems exhibited a lack of responsiveness, which motivated the development of a sliding-window LSTM autoencoder to characterise and ‘learn’ the hydrological ‘normal’ and thus capture and respond to temporal anomalies that suggest an elevated flood risk. When testing DWLR data from the previous year, the real-world framework successfully detected the abnormal system state before the critical water levels were reached. The model, using the statistically robust threshold from the training reconstruction errors (τ = µ + 3σ), successfully flagged anomalous activity windows before the proxy flood (95th percentile water level) with 25–29 day lead times and an average lead time of 27 days. This significant advantage is in time sensitive risk mitigation over conventional alert systems.

The model’s explainability has been enhanced. Integrated Gradients, paradoxically, illustrate that the anomalies were not primarily the result of abrupt changes in water level, but rather of altered water quality parameters, before, and maybe including, the dissolution of pH and the oxygen hydrodynamically. Domain-relevant temporal heat maps and contribution feature bar charts demonstrated the ability to recognise patterns and enhance the model’s explainability. The ability of the model to explain the prediction removes the anxiety associated with the model becoming a black-box. This is critical for environmental monitoring.

The results are overall indicative of the successful and practical implementability of the early flood prediction framework that has brought together the explainable Artificial Intelligence and the unsupervised temporal modelling paradigms.

Several extensions can be made to the current framework in future studies. To begin with, the addition of more meteorological variables and sensor data of higher temporal resolution (e.g., hourly data) may further enhance temporal sensitivity. The second proposed extension involves the use of adaptive window sizing and online threshold updating, which may improve flexibility and robustness in the context of shifting non-stationary climate conditions. The use of graph-based and spatio-temporal deep learning models to integrate and analyze spatial correlations across multiple DWLR stations is the third proposed extension.

A limitation of the present study is that the experiments were conducted using one year of data from a single DWLR dataset. Therefore, the model’s generalizability across different basins, climatic conditions, seasonal regimes, and interannual hydrological variability cannot be fully established. Future work will extend the validation to multi-year and multi-site DWLR datasets, include officially verified flood-event records, and compare the proposed framework with additional classical hydrological and statistical baselines.
