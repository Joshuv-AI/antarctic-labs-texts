Based on Raman spectroscopy and clinical laboratory tests: Developing a fusion model to predict neoadjuvant therapy response in breast cancer
Authors: Xueyi Chen, Zhenlong Li, Yanjun Li, Zongshun Chen, Jie Song, Hongyu Wu, Shan Liu, Lintao Li, Junjie Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Integrating two modalities to overcome the limitations of single-modality information.•The fusion model achieved an AUC value of 0.790, outperforming both univariate models.•Identified lipase as top predictor; Ki-67, HER2, HFR% and albumin as core biomarkers.•Significantly enhance the interpretability of fusion models through the AFS module.•Provides a non-invasive dynamic monitoring tool for clinical personalised treatment.

Introduction

In recent years, breast cancer (BC) has become the most common malignant tumour among women [1]. Neoadjuvant Therapy (NAT), as a crucial therapeutic modality within comprehensive BC treatment, can reduce tumour burden, lower tumour stage, and increase patients' eligibility for surgery [2]. Pathologic complete response (pCR) serves as the gold standard for evaluating NAT efficacy and is significantly correlated with favorable clinical outcomes [3]. pCR is defined as the absence of residual invasive cancer cells in histopathological specimens from the breast and axillary lymph nodes following neoadjuvant therapy (ypT0/is, ypN0) [4]. However, post-operative pathological assessment of NAT efficacy involves lengthy waiting times and cannot guide treatment during the therapeutic course [5]. Early prediction of NAT response is therefore essential to optimize therapeutic regimens and avoid unnecessary surgical or toxic interventions [6].

Imaging modalities such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and ultrasound have been extensively studied for predicting NAT efficacy [7,8]. These techniques clearly reflect changes in tumour morphology, size, texture, and density before and after treatment [[9], [10], [11]]. However, these imaging techniques cannot be dynamically repeated, and their interpretation is susceptible to clinician subjectivity. Similarly, genomic analysis remains limited in widespread clinical application due to its high cost and operational complexity [12].

The malignant transformation of cells and tissues is often accompanied by significant alterations in the internal environment, which occurs much earlier than the appearance of clinical symptoms and imaging features [13]. In this context, liquid biopsy offers significant advantages by evaluating disease status through the analysis of biomarkers in blood or other body fluids [14]. Among available methods, optical liquid biopsy, such as Raman spectroscopy, is a molecular vibrational technique that provides specific molecular "fingerprint" information of biological samples [15]. Raman spectroscopy, is non-invasive, highly sensitive, and rapid, enabling the detection and quantification of these altered molecular features [16,17]. In our previous study, we utilized raman spectroscopy to achieve breast cancer diagnosis and HER-2 status identification [18]. Despite these strengths, raman spectroscopy still faces the challenge of poor interpretability [16].

The advancement of artificial intelligence provides robust frameworks for processing high-dimensional biomedical data [19,20]. However, most current predictive models rely on single-modality data, which may fail to capture the holistic complexity of the internal environment. Integrating multimodal data into a unified fusion model is expected to enhance predictive accuracy and robustness.

This study retrospectively enrolled 406 patients with invasive BC. A predictive model was developed by integrating multimodal data—clinical laboratory tests and serum Raman spectroscopy—to evaluate the efficacy of NAT. This omics fusion strategy addresses the limitations of traditional statistical methods in handling large and complex datasets, while also compensating for the poor interpretability of Raman spectroscopy. Ultimately, this model serves to guide personalized treatment strategies and improve patient prognosis. Method

Patient selection

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Sichuan Cancer Hospital (Approval No SCCHEC-02-2022-140).

This study retrospectively enrolled patients diagnosed with invasive BC at Sichuan Cancer Hospital between July 2021 and October 2024. The inclusion criteria included: (1) Patients aged 18–80 years with a diagnosis of invasive BC; (2) Meeting the indications for NAT and having completed the treatment; (3) Absence of other malignancies; (4) Undergoing surgical treatment after NAT with available postoperative histopathological results. The exclusion criteria included: (1) Patients with ambiguous or missing pathological data; and (2) Failure to complete the NAT regimen. Ultimately, 406 patients were included, of whom 134 achieved pCR and 272 were identified as non-pCR based on postoperative pathological findings.

Acquisition of clinicopathological data

All patients received standardized NAT regimens following diagnosis. Baseline characteristics, including the expression status of ER, PR, HER2, and Ki-67, were obtained via preoperative core needle biopsy (CNB). Surgery was performed after the completion of NAT to obtain postoperative pathological results, which served as the basis for evaluating therapeutic efficacy. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) protocols followed the 2022 American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) guidelines. ER and PR positivity were defined as ≥1% nuclear staining. HER2 positivity was defined as IHC 3+ or IHC 2+ with a positive FISH result. All pathological assessments were reviewed by experienced pathologists to ensure diagnostic accuracy and consistency throughout the study.

Selection of clinical laboratory indicators and feature extraction

To ensure consistency of results, serum Raman spectra and laboratory tests were derived from blood samples collected at the same time point prior to the initial NAT cycle. Blood counts and biochemical tests are performed using standardized automated analyzers (Roche Diagnostics). Clinical laboratory data were standardized prior to analysis. Feature selection was conducted using seven distinct algorithms (ANOVA, ElasticNet, Random Forest, GBM, GLM, Deep Learning, Comprehensive). To ensure that the selected features were not model-specific, a multi-model intersection approach was employed for further screening. The final set of clinical laboratory features was determined by extracting the intersection of the top 20 features ranked by all seven algorithms.

Data acquisition, analysis, and processing of Raman spectroscopy

Fresh serum samples were cryopreserved for Raman analysis following established protocols [21]. A single-mode diode laser with a wavelength of 785 nm was selected for this study. The CCD camera (Andor) enabled the acquisition of Raman spectra within the wavenumber range of 400–1800 cm⁻¹. To ensure data quality, mass control was performed using the characteristic peak of phenylalanine in serum near 1000 cm⁻¹. Spectra contaminated by cosmic ray interference were excluded to guarantee the validity of the obtained spectra. Each sample underwent three rounds of testing, with five measurements per round. Each measurement had an integration time of 1 s, ultimately yielding 15 spectra per sample to ensure data stability.

Raman data processing involved preprocessing, feature extraction, and modeling. Preprocessing included baseline correction, fluorescence removal, smoothing, and normalization, with wavenumbers calibrated using ethanol standards. Inter-cohort variances were assessed via F-tests. For feature extraction, unsupervised analysis and ANOVA ensured intra-group homogeneity. Subsequently, a modified VGG-16 framework was employed for deep feature extraction. To reduce complexity and prevent overfitting, a shallow architecture was implemented, consisting of two 1D-convolutional layers and one max-pooling layer (kernel size = 5, stride = 2).

Attention module

To enhance classification performance and visualize the correlation between intra-module features and clinical categories, an Attention-based Feature Selection (AFS) module was integrated prior to the dual-modal learning modules. This module utilizes a fully connected network to extract intrinsic relationships from the raw input X while eliminating outliers. Individual attention networks were constructed for each feature to assign selection probabilities. Let pk and nk denote the scores for the k-th feature being selected or unselected, respectively, calculated as follows:pk=wpkhLk+bpknk=wnkhLk+bnk

Here, hLk represents the output of the l-th hidden layer in the k-th attention network. wpk, bpk denote the learnable parameters for pk. wnk, bnk denote the learnable parameters for nk. A softmax function then maps the attention scores to the interval (0, 1) to amplify the probability differential between selected and unselected features. Finally, the attention weigh sk is derived by calculating the weighted average of all feature selection probabilities.sk=1m∑i=1mexp⁡(pk)exp(pk)+exp⁡(nk)

The sk represents the association between individual biochemical indicators and NAT response. Ultimately, by concatenating diverse learning modules, features are ranked according to their significance, thereby providing corresponding interpretability analysis for various models.

Multi-modal information fusion in fusion model and training details

This study developed a Transformer-based fusion model for classification. The model uses a multimodal module to integrate information from different modalities into a unified representation. Finally, a multilayer perceptron with a Softmax activation function is used for response classification. First, serum Raman spectroscopy and biochemical features are extracted. In this module, linear layers transform token segments into queries Q, keys K, and values V the three core components of the Transformer. The linear projections are expressed as follows:Qi=LinearQ(Fictoken)Ki=LinearK(Firtoken)Vi=LinearV(Firtoken)

Ki and Vi are derived from serum Raman spectroscopy features Firtoken, while Qi is mapped from biochemical features Fictoken. The cross-attention layer computes attention weights through the interaction between queries $Q$ and keys $K$, and performs weighted aggregation on $V$ accordingly to fuse the two modal representations. This mechanism enables the model to focus on the most relevant parts of one input relative to another, bridging the gap between different data types, as shown below:OFVi=softmax(QiKiTdK)Vi dk is a scaling factor that prevents the softmax function from converging to regions of vanishing gradients as dot products increase. The cross-attention layer integrates biochemical features based on Raman spectra to form features that distinguish different IHC categories. Additionally, it re-evaluates the importance of biochemical features. The Adam optimizer and cross-entropy loss are employed. To prevent overfitting, a Dropout layer with a 20% rate is introduced, randomly deactivating 20% of neurons during each iteration. We performed 5-fold cross-validation. During loss calculation, sample weights are adjusted to ensure equal contribution from all classes, using weighted balancing to mitigate potential data imbalance.

In this study, the data were divided into a model development set (80%) and an independent test set (20%). The independent test set comprised the 20% of patients with the latest enrolment dates, ensuring that model evaluation reflected clinical applicability in a temporally distinct cohort. The remaining 80% were used for model development and internal validation. Within the model development set, a 7:3 random split was applied combined with 5-fold cross-validation for model training and hyperparameter tuning. The independent test set was not involved in any stage of model training and was reserved solely for final performance evaluation.

Comparison of predictive model performance

To evaluate the efficacy of the fusion model in predicting pCR following NAT, we systematically compared the performance of Clinlabomics-only, Raman-only, and the Fusion model. Performance metrics for the three models in the validation set are detailed in Table 3. The fusion model achieved the highest AUC value (0.790), significantly surpassing both the Clinlabomics-only model (AUC= 0.759) and the Raman-only model (AUC= 0.669). Specifically, the fusion model demonstrated an accuracy of 0.752, sensitivity of 0.682, specificity of 0.787, positive predictive value (PPV) of 0.612, and negative predictive value (NPV) of 0.834. The Clinlabomics-only model achieved an accuracy of 0.709, sensitivity of 0.657, specificity of 0.735, PPV of 0.550, and NPV of 0.813 on the validation set. The Raman-only model achieved an accuracy of 0.675, sensitivity of 0.341, specificity of 0.839, PPV of 0.511, and NPV of 0.721. Fusion model’s interpretability

To enhance clinical interpretability, an Attention Feature Selection (AFS) module was employed to rank and analyse key features within the fusion model (Fig. 6B). The top ten features by importance included lipase, Ki-67, HER2 status, high-fluorescence reticulocyte fraction (HFR), albumin (ALB), creatine kinase (CK), total bile acid (TBA), oestrogen receptor (ER) status, progesterone receptor (PR) status, and the total number of neoadjuvant cycles. The inclusion of HER2, ER, PR, and Ki-67 aligns with current BC guidelines. Notably, lipase ranked first in importance, consistent with the multiple lipid-related peaks observed in Raman spectra, suggesting that systemic metabolic status may correlate with treatment response. These findings elucidate the multifactorial drivers of pCR, enhancing the model's interpretability for clinical decision-making.

To further improve model interpretability, we performed SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) analysis to quantify the contribution of each feature to the model’s predictions (Fig. 9). The SHAP summary scatter plot revealed that PR status, HER2 status, albumin, age, plateletcrit, and Ki‑67 represented the top predictors driving model output. Discussion

Raman spectroscopy enables direct analysis of unprocessed biological samples, aligning seamlessly with existing clinical workflows. However, its limited interpretability has hindered clinical adoption. This study innovatively integrates serum Raman spectra with clinical laboratory tests, achieving robust predictive performance and providing novel insights into potential biomarkers.

The fusion model effectively integrated Raman spectra and clinical laboratory data, significantly enhancing performance (AUC increased from 0.669 to 0.759 to 0.790). Combined with the AFS module, the model identified high-impact features that ranked top in discriminating pCR from non-pCR. While key features such as ER, PR, HER2, and Ki-67 have established associations with therapeutic efficacy and prognosis, it is noteworthy that no statistical differences were observed among the remaining features.

Within the fusion model, our findings partially align with prior research. HR positive BC exhibits reduced sensitivity to NAT compared to other molecular subtypes, correlating with lower pCR rates [23]. Higher Ki-67 indicates active cellular proliferation and greater sensitivity to chemotherapeutic agents [24]. Furthermore, while HER2-positive BC is characterized by high aggressiveness, the implementation of dual HER2-targeted therapy has significantly improved its pCR rates [25].

Lipase (LPS) yielded the highest contribution in our fusion model. The serum lipase measured in this study is primarily pancreatic lipase [26]. Although the serum lipase measured in this study differs in origin and biological significance from tumour-specific lipases expressed locally (such as LIPG and ATGL), systemic metabolism is closely intertwined with the tumour microenvironment (TME). Ringel et al. demonstrated that systemic metabolic disorders (e.g., obesity) drive metabolic reprogramming in tumor cells by upregulating molecules associated with fatty acid uptake (SLC27A1), binding (FABP5), and mitochondrial β-oxidation (CPT1A, ACSM3), allowing them to sequester fatty acids within the TME [27]. Gao et al. further revealed that caloric restriction triggers HILPDA downregulation, which reduces lipid accumulation in tumor-infiltrating neutrophils, thereby inhibiting tumor growth and bolstering anti-tumor immunity by restricting lipid transfer to both tumor and immune effector cells [28].

Our analysis indicates that patients with elevated baseline HFR, MFR, hemoglobin, and plateletcrit are more likely to achieve pCR following chemotherapy. Residual intracellular RNA in reticulocytes can be quantified by fluorescence intensity; higher intensity signifies increased immaturity, serving as the most sensitive real-time indicator of erythropoietic activity [29].Robust bone marrow reserve and hematopoietic recovery reflect superior physiological tolerance to chemotherapy, enabling patients to withstand systemic toxicity and more readily achieve pCR [30,31].

Albumin is the predominant serum protein, with extensive evidence linking its levels to prognosis in various cancers [32]. Albumin's reduced thiol groups are primary scavengers of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [33,34], which promote cancer cell survival, proliferation, and genomic instability [35]. Additionally, malignancies often trigger systemic inflammation, characterized by elevated interleukin-6 (IL-6) [36]. Through the canonical IL-6/JAK/STAT3 pathway, IL-6 drives tumor proliferation and invasion while inhibiting apoptosis, sustaining a pro-tumorigenic inflammatory cycle [37,38]. High IL-6 levels further downregulate albumin expression via tyrosine kinase activation [39]. Moreover, Albumin mediates the transport of multiple chemotherapeutic agents, thereby influencing drug uptake and therapeutic efficacy [40].

Creatine kinase (CK) is typically associated with intracellular energy metabolism, muscle contraction, and ATP regeneration [41]. Cytoplasmic CK isoenzymes include CK-MM, CK-BB, and CK-MB [42]. CK-BB expression has been shown to enhance T-cell activation, proliferation, and cytokine secretion [43]. Studies have found that serum CK levels in BC patients are significantly lower than in those with benign breast disease, and serum CK levels are negatively correlated with tumour size [44,45]. Consequently, low CK levels may reflect a suboptimal immune response status in patients.

Despite its strengths, this study has limitations. Firstly, due to constraints in research objectives, the model was not validated externally using multicentre datasets. Future work will validate the model through larger mult-icentre cohorts to enhance its generalisability. Secondly, integrating additional modalities—such as genomics or radiomics—could further improve predictive accuracy, offering a more comprehensive solution for predicting response to neoadjuvant therapy. Furthermore, our study primarily focused on constructing a multimodal fusion framework integrating Raman spectroscopy with clinical laboratory tests to address specific clinical challenges; consequently, a systematic benchmarking against conventional machine learning models was not performed. These will be further refined in our future studies.

Conclusion

This study developed a fusion model combining serum Raman spectroscopy with clinical laboratory tests, demonstrating its efficacy in predicting response to NAT in BC. The model achieved an AUC of 0.79, outperforming any model based on a single modality. Analysis identified several features with potential predictive value: lipase, Ki-67, HER2, HFR, albumin, creatine kinase. This approach provides a non-invasive and cost-effective tool for personalised NAT decision-making. Future research will focus on expanding multi-centre sample sizes and incorporating additional modalities to further enhance predictive accuracy and clinical applicability.
