OCT4-driven PER1 activation promotes odontogenic differentiation in senescent SCAP spheres
Authors: Changfang Li, Zibin Zhang, Zhuoxin Yang, Tingjie Liu, Xiaoning He, Bei Li, Wei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) hold significant promise for applications in regenerative medicine, yet their therapeutic potential is often limited by replicative senescence. Identifying effective strategies to reverse replicative senescence in MSCs and elucidating the underlying molecular mechanisms are essential steps in advancing their clinical use. Here, this study demonstrated that the pluripotency regulator octamer-binding transcription factor 4 (OCT4) promoted odontogenic differentiation by activating period circadian regulator 1 (PER1) in replicative senescent stem cells from apical papilla (SCAP) spheres. Specifically, OCT4 overexpression significantly alleviated cell cycle arrest, reduced senescence-associated β-galactosidase activity, and downregulated the expression of senescence-related markers, including CDKN2A/P16, CDKN1A/P21, and TP53/P53. Moreover, this approach markedly enhanced the proliferation and odontogenic differentiation potential of SCAP spheres in vitro and promoted the formation of regenerative pulp-like tissue in vivo. Mechanistically, we demonstrated that OCT4 transcriptionally activated PER1 through direct binding to its promoter, thereby restoring the odontogenic differentiation capacity of replicative senescent SCAP. Collectively, our findings establish the OCT4-PER1 axis as a critical regulatory pathway that counteracts replicative senescence in SCAP. These insights suggest new therapeutic strategies targeting senescence-associated signaling pathways to enhance MSC-based regenerative outcomes.Subject terms: Regeneration, Stem-cell differentiation

Introduction

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) hold a crucial position in tissue repair and regenerative medicine, offering unique self-renewal ability and multidirectional differentiation potential, which provides new strategies for treating various diseases.1 However, clinical applications of MSCs require a substantial number of cells, ranging from millions to hundreds of millions. For instance, the dose of MSCs used for the prevention of chronic graft-versus-host disease can reach 1×106 to 2×106 cells per kilogram of body weight.2 Given the limited availability of stem cell sources, obtaining a sufficient number of cells for therapeutic applications often requires more than 25 doublings from a single cell. This extensive proliferation significantly depletes the replicative potential of MSCs. Once cells have exhausted their replicative potential, they enter a stage of replicative senescence, and cell division stops irreversibly.3 Importantly, as MSCs reach later passages, they tend to lose their stemness properties and homing capacity.4,5 Transplantation of such senescent cells would not only fail to produce the desired therapeutic outcomes but may also be harmful to the recipient. Therefore, exploring effective strategies to reverse the replicative senescence of MSCs and elucidating the underlying molecular mechanisms are crucial in accelerating their clinical application.

Pulp regeneration aims to replace damaged dental tissue structures through biological means to obtain the biological function of the pulp-dentin complex, ultimately enhancing tooth retention.6–8 Stem cells of the apical papilla (SCAP) are an extensively studied source in the field of regenerative medicine due to their accessibility and multilineage differentiation potential.9,10 However, replicative senescence also significantly impaired their regenerative capabilities, including DNA damage, genomic instability, cell cycle arrest, and alterations in gene expression.11–13 Three-dimensional (3D) culture has increasingly gained widespread acceptance as a superior in vitro model, offering enhanced biological and physiological relevance. This system more faithfully recapitulates the in vivo microenvironment by providing extracellular matrix (ECM) components and cytokine cues that closely mimic native tissue conditions, thereby better supporting the maintenance of stem cell proliferation and differentiation capacity.14–16 Consequently, 3D cell culture models represent a promising platform for investigating cellular behavior under conditions that more closely approximate physiological complexity, compared with conventional two-dimensional (2D) monolayer cultures.17 A previous study demonstrated that 3D culture attenuated senescence of SCAP through UQCRC2-mediated mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation.18 However, whether 3D culture can reverse the replicative senescence SCAP remains unclear. In this study, we initially employed a 3D spheroid culture system to attempt to reverse replicative senescence of SCAP. Although this approach did not significantly enhance proliferation, differentiation, or cell cycle arrest in vitro, it did elevate the expression of the pluripotency-associated transcription factor octamer-binding transcription factor 4 (OCT4) in senescent cells. OCT4 is essential for maintaining stem cell pluripotency and self-renewal.19–21 Recent research showed OCT4 was closely associated with stem cell replicative senescence. In senescent MSCs, OCT4 overexpression reduced cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 1A (P21) transcriptional activity, delaying typical G1 phase cell cycle arrest in cellular senescence.22 Therefore, it is plausible to transfect SCAP in 3D culture with OCT4 to further reverse the senescence process.

Increasing evidence has highlighted a close relationship between circadian rhythm and cellular senescence.23 Circadian rhythm exerts its influence on cellular senescence through specific proteins such as BMAL1 and CLOCK, which are involved in cell cycle regulation and DNA repair.24–26 For instance, BMAL1 deficiency disrupted reactive oxygen species homeostasis and metabolism, thereby affecting cellular senescence.27 Additionally, CLOCK has been shown to reverse the decline in the proliferation and multi-differentiation potential of replicative senescent MSCs, thus promoting cartilage regeneration.28 Moreover, the circadian clock gene period circadian regulator 1 (PER1) has been identified as a key regulator in myogenic and osteogenic differentiation.29,30 During stem cell differentiation, the progressive downregulation of the pluripotency factor OCT4 alongside the upregulation of circadian clock components suggests that the relationship between OCT4 and the circadian rhythm in the odontogenic differentiation of replicative senescent SCAP warrants further investigation.31

In this study, we found that overexpression of OCT4 in senescent SCAP spheres significantly alleviated cell cycle arrest, enhanced the proliferative and differentiation capacity of senescent SCAP. Intriguingly, SCAP spheres with OCT4 overexpression promoted the formation of regenerative pulp-like tissue with abundant vascular-like tissue in vivo. Mechanistically, OCT4 activated PER1 by binding to its promoter, which effectively rescued PER1 downregulation and enhanced the odontogenic differentiation of senescent SCAP spheres. Collectively, our research uncovered an unrecognized mechanism that pluripotency factor OCT4 regulated PER1 expression, which promoted the odontogenic differentiation of replicative senescent SCAP.

Results

3D spheroid culture failed to reverse senescence-associated phenotypes in replicative senescent SCAP

To investigate how different culture microenvironments influence cell behavior, SCAP at passage 5 (P5) and passage 15 (P15) were separately cultured in both 2D and 3D conditions. Following approximately 7 days of cultivation in the 3D environment, spontaneous sphere formation was observed in SCAP, maintaining a round shape with cell density gradually decreasing over time (Supplementary Fig. 1a). These spheres exhibited relatively smooth surfaces under scanning electron microscope (SEM) (Supplementary Fig. 1a) and had diameters ranging from 20 to 100 μm (Supplementary Fig. 1b). Furthermore, cell morphology exhibited variations across different passages and culture conditions. In 2D culture, cells at P5 displayed a fibroblast-like morphology characterized by a spindle shape and alignment. In contrast, cells at P15 presented larger and flatter morphological characteristics with an increased number of pseudopods (Supplementary Fig. 1c). Conversely, SCAP at P5 and P15 were harvested and re-cultured in six-well plates for 24 h after 3D culture. Similar elongated spindle forms were observed that consistently preserved their characteristic MSCs morphology (Supplementary Fig. 1c, d).

To evaluate the senescence characteristics of SCAP in both 2D and 3D conditions, cells were stained with senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal). As anticipated, P5 cells under both culture conditions showed minimal SA-β-gal activity. In contrast, P15 cells cultured in 2D condition exhibited prominent blue staining indicative of senescence, whereas the proportion of SA-β-gal-positive cells remained unchanged in 3D culture (Fig. 1a, f). Furthermore, senescence-associated markers such as cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 2A (P16), P21, and tumor protein 53 (P53) showed a significant increase in P15 cells under both culture conditions, as demonstrated by quantitative Reverse Transcription-Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-qPCR) and immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 1a, b, Supplementary Fig. 1f).

Cellular replicative senescence is marked by cell cycle arrest. We employed flow cytometry to assess the cell cycle and the distribution of various cultured cells under identical treatment conditions. The results showed that in P15 SCAP, about 90% cells were arrested in the G1 phase. Moreover, compared to P5 SCAP, the ratio of proliferating S-phase cells in P15 SCAP accounted for a small proportion across different culture microenvironments (Supplementary Fig. 1e, Fig. 1c). To assess the proliferation capacity, we employed 5-ethynyl-20-deoxyuridine (EdU) assay to label replicative DNA. The P5 cells exhibited a significantly higher positive labeling ratio (red staining) in both culture formats, whereas the P15 cells displayed markedly fewer positively labeled cells (Fig. 1d, e).

3D spheroid culture failed to restore odontogenic differentiation capacity in replicative senescent SCAP

To assess the odontogenic differentiation capabilities of P5 and P15 cells cultured in both 2D and 3D conditions, we performed RT-qPCR, immunofluorescence staining and Western Blot. The results (Fig. 2a–c) demonstrated that the expressions of pluripotency factor OCT4, SOX2, and NANOG were downregulated in P15 cells cultured in 2D condition. In contrast, in 3D-cultured P5 and P15 cells, only OCT4 expression was significantly up-regulated. Moreover, P5 cells had a significantly higher level of OCT4 expression than P15 cells. In 3D-cultured P5 cells, the expressions of odontogenic differentiation-related genes (RUNX2, ALP at 7 days; OCN, DMP-1, DSPP at 14 days) were significantly upregulated compared to cells cultured in 2D condition (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). Protein expression of RUNX2 and DSPP also markedly increased at 14 days. However, no significant differences were observed between 3D-cultured P15 cells and 2D-cultured P15 cells (Fig. 2d, Supplementary Fig. 2c). Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) (Supplementary Fig. 2e, f) and Alizarin Red S (ARS) (Fig. 2e, f) staining demonstrated enhanced mineralization in 3D-cultured P5 cells compared to their 2D counterparts, with quantitative assays confirming this disparity. Conversely, P15 cells showed no statistically significant differences in mineralization potential between culture modalities. In 3D-cultured P5 cells, we further observed a significant upregulation of the vasculogenic gene VEGF, whereas P15 cells exhibited no discernible differences in VEGF gene expression across any of the culture conditions tested (Supplementary Fig. 2d).

OCT4 restored the impaired odontogenic differentiation capacity of replicative senescent SCAP under 3D spheroid culture conditions

Although 3D culture could partially rejuvenate the replicative senescence of SCAP through the re-expression of the OCT4 gene, it did not enhance their proliferation and odontogenic differentiation capabilities in P15 cells. At this stage, SCAP in 3D culture were transfected with OCT4, in an attempt to assess their proliferation and differentiation potential. P5 cells transfected with GFP were employed as the positive control and named P5GFP+, whereas P15 cells transfected solely with GFP served as the negative control and named P15GFP+. And SCAP at passage 15 were transfected with OCT4 (Pou5f1), which were named P15OCT4+ (Supplementary Fig. 3a–d). Lentiviral vector-mediated ectopic expression of OCT4 in P15OCT4+ cells significantly alleviated senescence phenotypes compared to P15GFP+ cells, as evidenced by a reduction in the percentage of SA-β-gal-positive cells (Fig. 3a, f). Moreover, the overexpression of OCT4 in P15 cells led to the downregulation of senescence-associated genes, including P16, P21, and P53 (Supplementary Fig. 3f). Immunofluorescence analysis further confirmed that the overexpression of OCT4 led to a significant decrease in the expression of corresponding senescence-associated protein (Fig. 3a, b). Consistent with our previous findings, to further validate these observations, we performed OCT4 knockdown experiments in 3D-cultured P5 SCAP (Supplementary Fig. 5a–c). The results demonstrated that downregulation of OCT4 significantly elevated the percentage of SA-β-gal-positive cells. (Supplementary Fig. 5d, e).

To assess the impact of overexpression of OCT4 on the cell cycle, we performed flow cytometry to evaluate the cell cycle. The results indicated that OCT4 alleviated the G1 phase block in P15 cells and enhanced the progression through the S phase (Supplementary Fig. 3e, Fig 3c). Additionally, overexpression of OCT4 led to an increased ratio of EdU-positive cells in P15 cells (Fig. 3d, e). While following OCT4 knockdown in 3D-cultured P5 SCAP, cellular proliferation was found to be simultaneously suppressed, as determined by EdU staining (Supplementary Fig. 5n, o).

RT-qPCR and immunofluorescence results indicated that overexpression of OCT4 in P15 cells enhanced the gene and protein expression of SOX2 and NANOG (Fig. 4a–c). This upregulation of pluripotency factors suggested that the senescent stem cells may have regained their multi-lineage differentiation potential. Hence, genes and proteins associated with odontogenic differentiation were detected. OCT4 overexpression triggered temporal gene activation in P15 cells (RUNX2, ALP at 7 days; OCN, DMP-1, DSPP at 14 days) (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). To further validate these observations, we performed OCT4 knockdown experiments in 3D-cultured P5 SCAP. The results demonstrated OCT4 knockdown markedly attenuated the expression of odontogenic differentiation-associated genes (RUNX2, ALP, and OCN at 7 days; RUNX2, OCN, DMP-1, DSPP at 14 days) (Supplementary Fig. 5f, g). Western blot analysis further confirmed that overexpression of OCT4 elevated the expression of proteins related to odontogenic differentiation (RUNX2, ALP, OCN, DMP-1, and DSPP) (Fig. 4d, Supplementary Fig. 4c). Reciprocally, a significant inhibition of odontogenic differentiation-related protein expression (RUNX2, ALP, OCN, DMP-1, and DSPP) was observed upon OCT4 downregulation in 3D-cultured P5 cells (Supplementary Fig. 5h, i). ALP (Supplementary Fig. 4e, f) and ARS (Fig. 4e, f) staining demonstrated that overexpression of OCT4 enhanced mineralization capability in 3D-cultured P15 cells. In contrast, OCT4 downregulation markedly attenuated the formation of mineralized nodules in 3D-cultured P5 SCAP (Supplementary Fig. 5j–m). Additionally, OCT4 overexpression significantly up-regulated the pro-angiogenic factor VEGF in both P5 and P15OCT4+ SCAP spheres, underscoring its capacity to enhance vasculogenic differentiation (Supplementary Fig. 4d). These findings collectively indicated that the overexpression of OCT4 may effectively reverse replicative senescent SCAP.

OCT4 enabled replicative senescent SCAP to regenerate pulp-like tissues in vivo under 3D spheroid culture conditions

To assess the in vivo regenerative potential of replicative senescence SCAP with OCT4 overexpression, we implanted three groups of SCAP spheres and subcutaneously transplanted them into immunocompromised mice. Specimens at 12 weeks were subjected to histological and immunofluorescence analysis. H&E staining revealed that newly formed dentin-like tissue was present in the P5GFP+ group, as indicated by the yellow arrows (Fig. 5a). Additionally, vascular-like structures were observed in both P5GFP+ and P15OCT4+ groups as indicated by the black arrows, in contrast to the P15GFP+ group (Fig. 5a). Masson’s trichrome staining further confirmed a higher degree of collagen fiber regeneration in the P15OCT4+ and P5GFP+ groups, while the P15GFP+ group showed haphazard regenerated collagen (Fig. 5b). The expression of odontogenic differentiation-related proteins (DSPP and DMP-1) (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 4g) and vascularization-associated proteins (CD31 and VEGF) (Fig. 5d, Supplementary Fig. 4h) was confirmed through immunofluorescent staining. The regenerated tissues in the P15OCT4+ and P5GFP+ groups exhibited enhanced staining for these markers, indicating a significant improvement in odontogenic differentiation and neovascularization. Conversely, tissues in the P15GFP+ group displayed weaker staining for these proteins.

OCT4 upregulated PER1 expression in replicative senescent SCAP under 3D spheroid culture conditions

To elucidate the potential molecular mechanisms underlying odontogenic differentiation among the three groups, we employed RT-qPCR to analyze the expression profiles of key circadian genes, including PER1 (Fig. 6a, b), PER2 (Fig. 6c, d), CLOCK (Supplementary Fig. 6c, d), BMAL1 (Supplementary Fig. 6e, f), CRY1 (Supplementary Fig. 6g, h), and CRY2 (Supplementary Fig. 6i, j). Our results indicated that the expression levels of the peripheral clock activators CLOCK and BMAL1, as well as the inhibitors CRY1 and CRY2, did not exhibit significant rhythmic oscillations. Interestingly, the expression of PER1 and PER2 was found to be significantly higher following OCT4 overexpression and appeared to display rhythmic oscillations (Fig. 6a–d). Western blot analysis further revealed that the expression of PER1 gradually increased over time, reaching a peak at 18 hours post-synchronization and declined at 24 hours, while there was no discrepancy of PER2 expression (Fig. 6e–g). Immunofluorescence staining results corroborated these findings, showing that after synchronization, PER1 expression was low from 0 to 6 hours, increased significantly at 12 hours, and peaked at 18 hours. Notably, higher expression levels of PER1 were observed in the cytoplasm compared to the nucleus (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b).

PER1 downregulation attenuated OCT4-mediated restoration of odontogenic differentiation in replicative senescent SCAP

To further investigate the role of PER1 in odontogenic differentiation, we specifically knocked down PER1 using siRNA in the P15OCT4+ group (Supplementary Fig. 7a–d). Our results demonstrated that the knockdown of PER1 significantly impaired the odontogenic differentiation capacity of SCAP both in vitro and in vivo. Specifically, the expression of odontogenic differentiation-related genes, including RUNX2, ALP, and OCN at 7 days (Supplementary Fig. 8a), and OCN, DSPP, and DMP-1 at 14 days (Supplementary Fig. 8b), was substantially reduced. Consistent with these findings, Western blot analysis revealed that the expression levels of odontogenic differentiation-related proteins (RUNX2, ALP, OCN, DMP-1, and DSPP) were also significantly decreased following PER1 knockdown (Fig. 7a, Supplementary Fig. 8c). Additionally, ALP (Supplementary Fig. 8d, e) and ARS (Fig. 7b, c) staining further demonstrated that PER1 knockdown markedly impaired the odontogenic differentiation and mineralization potential of P15OCT4+ cells. Conversely, SA-β-gal staining and immunofluorescence jointly indicated that PER1 silencing paradoxically down-regulated the expression of senescence-associated markers (P16, P21, and P53) (Supplementary Fig. 7e–h). EdU staining revealed that PER1 knockdown unexpectedly enhanced the proliferative capacity of P15OCT4+ cells. (Supplementary Fig. 7i, j). Moreover, Cell cycle analysis further revealed that PER1 knockdown facilitated G1-S transition and accelerated cell cycle progression, manifested by elevated populations in S phase and subsequent G2 phase accumulation (Supplementary Fig. 7k, l). In vivo findings revealed that shRNA-mediated downregulation of PER1 severely impaired the odontogenic differentiation of P15OCT4+ cells. Regenerated tissues further exhibited a significant reduction in vascular-like structures (Fig. 7d, e), which were corroborated by significantly lower fluorescence intensity of both odontogenic (Fig. 7f, Supplementary Fig. 8f) and angiogenic differentiation markers (Fig. 7g, Supplementary Fig. 8g).

OCT4 activated PER1 through direct promoter binding

To elucidate the mechanism by which OCT4 drove the oscillation of PER1, we conducted a detailed analysis of the PER1 gene sequence. We identified an octamer motif sequence (ATGCAAA) within the PER1 promoter (Fig. 8a). To assess the role of this motif in PER1 promoter activity, we constructed two luciferase reporter plasmids: one containing the wild-type PER1 promoter (WT) and another with a mutated octamer motif sequence (MUT). These constructs were transfected into 293 T cells, and the promoter activities were evaluated. Our results showed that the activity of the wild-type PER1 promoter was significantly higher than that of the mutant promoter (Fig. 8b), suggesting that the octamer motif was crucial for PER1 promoter activity.

a OCT4 promoted the odontogenic differentiation potential of replicative senescent SCAP spheres by directly activating the PER1 promoter. The OCT4 recognition motif was identified using the JASPAR database. b Two PER1 promoter luciferase reporter constructs were employed, and luciferase activity was subsequently measured. c ChIP assays were conducted in 293 T cells using anti-OCT4 antibodies or IgG, and quantified by RT-qPCR. d Schematic representation showing that overexpression of OCT4 restores the odontogenic differentiation and proliferative capacity of replicative senescent SCAP spheres via activation of PER1 transcription. (n = 3 biologically independent samples, analysis by T test: ns: not significant, ***P < 0.001, **P < 0.01, *P < 0.05)

To further investigate whether OCT4 bound to the putative overlapping binding site within the PER1 promoter, we performed a chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay. The results demonstrated that a specific band corresponding to a 200-bp fragment of the PER1 promoter could be amplified from the immunoprecipitated DNA samples using an anti-OCT4 antibody. Notably, the enrichment of OCT4 binding to the PER1 promoter was more than 10-fold higher compared to the negative control (Fig. 8c). This finding indicated that OCT4 specifically bound to the predicted motif within the PER1 promoter, thereby directly regulating PER1 transcription and expression (Fig. 8d). Beyond this, we conducted ChIP assay to confirm endogenous OCT4–PER1 interactions in both P5 and P15 SCAP cultured in 3D conditions. While OCT4 binding to the PER1 promoter was confirmed in 3D-cultured P5 cells, this specific interaction was notably absent in 3D-cultured P15 cells (Supplementary Fig. 5p).

Discussion

Stem cell technology is pivotal in tissue repair and regenerative medicine, facilitates the repair of tissues such as skin, bone, cartilage, heart, nerves, and teeth.32–37 Any decline in proliferation and differentiation abilities of stem cells can significantly impair the efficiency of tissue repair and regeneration processes. As stem cells experience replicative senescence, their proliferative capacity and differentiation potential, along with other stemness properties, are diminished.38 Additionally, stem cells exhibit immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory functions.39,40 Replicative senescence may impact these functions, thereby influencing the inflammatory response and immune balance during tissue regeneration. Although studies have reported that culture microenvironment regulation, drug treatment, gene editing, and epigenetic modifications can effectively delay or resolve this issue,41–43 further in-depth exploration is still required to investigate the underlying regulatory mechanisms prior to the realization of its clinical application.

The microenvironment is a primary determinant of stem cell fate, with alterations in this niche being a key driver of stem cell senescence during in vitro culture.44 Stem cells can only optimally maintain their stemness and functionality within a specific microenvironment. To address the challenge of replicative senescence, we initially sought to simulate an in vivo culture environment. Our investigation specifically focused on the impact of a methylcellulose-based 3D culture system on the replicative senescence of SCAP. Our findings were in line with previous studies, demonstrating that 3D culture has a positive effect on restoring cell morphology and stemness. This was evidenced by the upregulation of OCT4 expression in both non-senescent and senescent cells.45,46 However, despite these improvements, our results did not significantly ameliorate cell cycle arrest or decrease the expression of senescence markers such as P16, P21, and P53. Moreover, the expression of SA-β-gal remained unchanged despite the prolonged duration of 3D culture. We further demonstrated that after 7 days of 3D culture, the odontogenic differentiation capacity of replicative senescent SCAP did not exhibit significant improvement compared to non-senescent cells. This discrepancy may be attributed to variations in the culture system and culture durations. Future work should focus on optimizing the culture conditions and exploring additional factors that could enhance the reversal of senescence and restore the full functionality of SCAP in vitro.

Research has indicated that partial reprogramming can rejuvenate the functions and molecular characteristics of senescent stem cells, thereby enhancing their capacity to contribute to tissue regeneration.47–49 This process involves partial reversal or restoration of cellular function without fully reverting cells to a totipotent or pluripotent state by modifying their epigenetic status and gene expression patterns. This methodology is particularly applicable in research and therapeutic contexts where there is a desire to restore cellular vitality and mitigate age-related damage without the need for cells to return completely to a pluripotent state.50–52 OCT4 is crucial for maintaining pluripotency and self-renewal of embryonic stem cells, recognizing and binding to DNA regulatory regions, either independently or in cooperation with other transcription factors, to regulate the transcription of downstream target genes.21,53,54 Our results revealed that overexpression of OCT4 in a 3D culture system significantly upregulated the expression of pluripotency-associated genes SOX2 and NANOG. This finding was consistent with other reports demonstrating that such interventions could restore certain youthful characteristics of cells.22 Specifically, we observed that overexpression of OCT4 not only reduced the expression of SA-β-gal but also downregulated the expression of senescence-related genes, including P16, P21, and P53. It has been demonstrated that OCT4 directly binds to the Cyclin D1 promoter as an upstream regulator. This binding event activated Cyclin D1 expression, thereby promoting cell cycle progression and enhancing cellular proliferation.54 Furthermore, elevated OCT4 activity accelerated somatic cell reprogramming.55 Consistent with these established findings, our results demonstrated that OCT4 overexpression effectively promoted cell proliferation and alleviated cellular senescence in replicative senescent SCAP. Furthermore, our findings indicated that overexpression of OCT4 effectively promoted odontogenic and angiogenic differentiation of replicative senescent SCAP in vitro. These results were corroborated by in vivo experiments, which showed that OCT4 enhanced the regeneration of pulp-like tissue. The regenerated tissue exhibited robust upregulation of odontogenic differentiation markers and displayed substantial angiogenic potential. Notably, OCT4 functions as a direct upstream transcriptional regulator of Ang1, bFGF, HGF, VEGF, Bcl2, and survivin, whose concerted actions collectively drove a pro-angiogenic phenotype in engrafted PBMSCs.56 In related work, dynamic hydrogel’s viscoelastic 3D microenvironment has been shown to orchestrate vascular patterning and arteriolar differentiation by amplifying Notch3 signaling in MSCs.57 while 3D culture systems played a pivotal role in driving vascularization during tissue regeneration.56 Herein, we propose that OCT4-mediated partial reprogramming of replicative senescent SCAP synergizes with 3D-culture-induced angiogenic cues to robustly drive vascular differentiation. Collectively, these findings underscore the therapeutic potential of OCT4 overexpression for rejuvenating senescent stem cells and promoting tissue regeneration, particularly within the context of dental pulp tissue.

The circadian clock coordinates physiological processes by regulating cellular functions and signaling pathways.58 The evolutionarily conserved circadian clock synchronizes mammalian physiological/behavioral rhythms with the 24-hour light-dark cycle, ensuring tissue and cellular homeostasis.23 Lu et al. conducted comparative transcriptomics and uncovered that circadian factors and the pluripotency network are co-regulated in species with varying maximum lifespans.59 Research demonstrated that the reprogramming factor KLF4 enhanced rhythmic transcription in aging mouse macrophages, linking pluripotency networks and circadian regulation during aging.60 We propose that exploring the interrelation between pluripotency and circadian rhythm dysregulation is a promising avenue for this research. Understanding whether and how these processes are intertwined could provide novel insights into the mechanisms underpinning cellular senescence and tissue regeneration. In this context, the pluripotency factor OCT4 may be intricately connected to circadian rhythms during the process of replicative senescence. Our study, for the first time, uncovered that overexpression of OCT4 upregulated the expression of the circadian genes PER1 and PER2, as well as PER1 protein expression in replicative senescent SCAP cultured in a 3D environment. This restoration of circadian gene expression appeared to play a crucial role in reversing replicative senescence. In recent years, circadian rhythms have garnered increasing attention due to their close relationship with numerous physiological and biochemical activities.61 High-amplitude circadian rhythms are associated with health and longevity.62 However, normal aging is associated with a progressive attenuation of circadian rhythm amplitude, frequently accompanied by phase shifts.28 Our findings indicated that overexpression of OCT4 may alleviate the disruption of circadian rhythms associated with replicative senescence.

The precise mechanism underlying OCT4-mediated upregulation of PER1 expression remains to be fully elucidated. Although there is extensive research on the regulation of OCT4 itself, including its interactions with other pluripotency factors like NANOG and SOX2, and its regulation by various factors such as orphan nuclear receptors, miRNAs, and lncRNAs,63–66 specific information on how OCT4 directly influences PER1 expression is not clearly documented in the previous studies. When the expression of PER1 was suppressed, the odontogenic differentiation ability of SCAP spheres was significantly inhibited. This indicated that the circadian gene PER1 may play a crucial role in promoting odontogenic differentiation. This finding is supported by previous studies demonstrating that m6A modification of PER1 enhanced osteogenic differentiation.29,30 Moreover, Per1 and Per2 have been shown to promote muscle regeneration by upregulating Igf expression, thereby facilitating dynamic histone modifications at the Igf2 enhancer locus.29 Another study revealed that core circadian clock genes, such as Per1, rhythmically regulated the expression of two critical ameloblast differentiation markers Amelx and Klk4 in a circadian manner within ameloblasts, thereby modulating ameloblast differentiation and enamel formation.67 Moreover, we firstly demonstrated that OCT4 can transcriptionally activate PER1 expression by binding to its promoter region, suggesting that OCT4 acts as a transcription factor to promote circadian rhythmic transcription. This mechanism aligns with OCT4’s known role in regulating gene expression in stem cells and highlights its potential to influence circadian rhythms and differentiation processes. This finding may be crucial for linking pluripotency networks with circadian rhythms, ultimately influencing cellular functions such as odontogenic differentiation.

However, this study has several limitations that warrant acknowledgment. Specifically, our animal model primarily employed an ectopic transplantation strategy. We recognize that incorporating an in situ regeneration model in future studies will be crucial to comprehensively delineate OCT4’s pivotal role in genuine pulp regeneration within its native physiological microenvironment, thereby providing more robust evidence regarding the clinical translatability of our findings. Furthermore, several critical parameters remain to be elucidated, including the precise expression threshold of OCT4 required in senescent cells and the extent to which its upregulation is sufficient to reverse replicative senescence. Addressing these technical considerations will be essential for optimizing the therapeutic efficacy of OCT4-mediated rejuvenation strategies.

Materials and methods

Cells in 2D culture

Dental papilla tissues were procured from immature third molars of human patients, under a protocol sanctioned by the Clinical Research Ethics Board of the Medical Ethics Committee of the Fourth Military Medical University (IRB-REV-2022195). The teeth were thoroughly rinsed with Phosphate Buffered Saline (PBS, Gibco, California, USA) multiple times to ensure the surfaces were clean. Subsequently, the dental papilla tissues were sectioned into approximately 1 mm fragments and subjected to enzymatic digestion in a solution containing 3 mg/mL collagenase type I for 30 minutes at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere. The resultant tissue fragments were cultured in alpha minimal essential medium (α-MEM, Invitrogen, California, USA), supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Invitrogen, California, USA), 2 mmol/L glutamine, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Invitrogen), within a humidified incubator maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. The culture medium was carefully replaced after 3 days in the primary cell culture. The cells were cultured until dense growth islets or a monolayer formed, at which point they were passaged. Cells at passages 5 and 15 were utilized for subsequent experiments in this study.

293 T cells were cultured in high-glucose dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (H-DMEM, Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS and 2 mmol/L glutamine, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C and in the presence of 5% CO2.

Cells in 3D culture

For the 3D culture, cells were seeded onto six-well ultralow attachment plates (Corning, USA) at a density of 1×105 cells per cm² using basal Epithelial Cell Medium (Sciencell, California, USA) supplemented with methylcellulose (Stemcell Technologies, Vancouver, Canada). The Epithelial Cell Medium and methylcellulose were thoroughly mixed at a 3:2 (v/v) ratio through gentle pipetting to minimize cell aggregation, hereafter referred to as MC culture. Fresh medium, amounting to one-tenth of the original volume, was carefully added to the culture medium from the edge of the plate every two days. The size and number of spheres were recorded over a period of 1 to 7 days. After 7 days, the spheres and culture medium were transferred to a 50 mL conical tube, diluted with 3 volumes of ice-cold PBS, and centrifuged at 1 200 r/min for 5 minutes. The supernatant was aspirated and the wash step was repeated once. The pellet was then incubated with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA for 5 minutes at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ humidified incubator to dissociate spheres into single cells. Enzymatic digestion was terminated by adding an equal volume of 10% FBS-supplemented α-MEM. The resulting cell suspension was collected, centrifuged, and re-seeded in fresh 3D culture medium for subsequent passaging.

Overexpression of OCT4 by lentiviral vectors

The gene sequences were obtained from NCBI (gene ID: 5460), lentiviral vectors CMV-MCS-GFP-Pou5f1-SV40-Puromycin and CMV-MCS-GFP-SV40-Puromycin were obtained from Genechem (Shanghai, China). After reaching 30% confluence, cells were transfected with lentiviral vectors (the MOI = 5). The medium was then replaced by α-MEM with 0.25% puromycin (Biosharp, Hefei, China) after 48 hours culture. The culture medium was changed every 3 days in 10-day culture.

Small interfering RNA (siRNA) for PER1

Small interfering RNA targeting PER1 (siPER1) and scrambled siRNA (siCON) genes were constructed by Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Cells were cultured in 3D culture for 7 days and transferred to 2D plastic plates. The cells, upon reaching 60% confluence, were synchronized using 200 nmol/L dexamethasone for 2 hours. The medium was then replaced with dexamethasone-free α-MEM, and then cells were transfected with these siRNAs using Invitrogen™ Lipofectamine™ 2000 Transfection Reagent (Thermofisher, Waltham, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocols at 18 hours. The final concentrations of siRNAs were 30 pmol/L. After removing the medium and replacing it with α-MEM, the cells were cultured for an additional 48 hours for further experiments. In order to examine the expression of odontogenic differentiation-related genes and proteins, siRNAs were re-administered every 72 hours to maintain the downregulation of PER1 expression.

Lentiviral-delivered shRNA for OCT4 and PER1 knockdown

To achieve OCT4 knockdown in P5 cells cultured in 3D conditions, specific short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) (#VB250812-1086wwg) were designed by VectorBuilder (Shaanxi, China) based on the RNAi Consortium (TRC) library. To achieve PER1 knockdown in P15OCT4+ spheres, specific short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) (#VB250815-1129yxp) were designed by VectorBuilder. P5 cells and P15OCT4+ cells were then transduced with the concentrated viral particles. Polybrene (4 µg/mL final concentration) was added to enhance viral infection. After 18 hours post-transduction, the viral medium was replaced with fresh culture medium, and transduced cells underwent selection using puromycin (2.5 µg/mL). The efficiency of PER1 knockdown was evaluated 3 days after shRNA transfection using RT-qPCR. Total RNA was isolated from shRNA-transduced cells, reverse-transcribed, and amplified using gene-specific primers (Supplementary Table 1).

SA-β-gal staining

Cells from both 2D and 3D cultures were seeded into six-well plates at a density of 1×105 cells per well and incubated overnight. On the following day, the cells were fixed for 30 minutes at room temperature using 4% formaldehyde and then rinsed twice with PBS. Subsequently, cells were incubated with a freshly prepared SA-β-gal staining solution (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) overnight at 37 °C, strictly according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. At least 400 cells were examined in randomly selected, non-overlapping fields by three independent observers to assess the expression of SA-β-gal positive cells, which were stained blue, and were enumerated in three randomly chosen fields under a microscope (Leika, Wetzlar, Germany). The experiment was conducted three times, and the mean percentage of SA-β-gal-positive cells was calculated from five random fields of vision.

Cell cycle and EdU staining

Cells from both 2D and 3D cultures were seeded into six-well plates at a density of 5×105 cells per well and allowed to grow to the exponential phase. Cells were fixed in 70% ethanol (-20 °C, 24 h), rinsed twice with PBS, and stained with PI/RNase (Beyotime) for 30 minutes at 37 °C in the dark. Flow cytometry (Beckman Coulter) captured ≥20 000 events per sample. For EdU staining, cells in the exponential phase were assessed using BeyoClick™ EdU Cell Proliferation Kit with Alexa Fluor 647 (Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. EdU relative fluorescence intensity was quantified and analyzed using ImageJ software.

RT-qPCR

For the analysis of senescence and pluripotency-related genes, cells cultured in both 2D and 3D conditions were transferred to α-MEM and maintained for 72 hours prior to collection. For the assessment of odontogenic differentiation potential, cells cultured in both 2D and 3D conditions were transferred to an odontogenic induction medium (α-MEM containing 10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 5 mmol/L β-glycerophosphate, 50 μg/mL vitamin C, and 10 nmol/L dexamethasone) for 7 days or 14 days prior to collection. For circadian clock gene detection, cells were first cultured in 3D medium for 7 days following established protocols. The cultured cells were then transferred to plastic plates containing α-MEM medium and maintained for another 2 days. Cell synchronization was performed through a 2-hour exposure to 200 nmol/L dexamethasone in serum-free α-MEM. Following synchronization, the culture medium was replaced with fresh dexamethasone-free α-MEM, and cell samples were systematically collected at 6-hour intervals over a 24-hour period for subsequent analysis. According to the manufacturer’s instructions, total RNA was extracted from SCAP using TRIzol (Takara, California, USA) reagent, and 1.0 μg of RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA using HiScript II Q RT SuperMix. The power up SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) was used for RT–qPCR, and the data were evaluated using the 2−Ct technique. All data was based on GAPDH as an endogenous control based on previous studies.25 The list of RT‒qPCR primer sequences was provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Western blot analysis

For the odontogenic differentiation assay, cells were cultured in odontogenic induction medium for 14 days prior to harvesting. For analysis of circadian rhythm proteins, cells were cultured and synchronized using the previously described method. Cell samples were then collected at 6-hour intervals over a 24-hour period for subsequent analysis. The specific experimental methods were referenced from the previous studies.68 The primary antibodies were as follows: ALP (1:200, sc-79840), DMP-1 (1:200, sc-6551), and DSPP (1:200, sc-73632) were obtained from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Texas, USA). OCN (1:1 000, 59757) and RUNX2 (1:1 000, 12556S) were obtained from Cell Signaling Technology (Massachusetts, USA). PER1 (1:1 000, 13463-1-AP) and PER2 (1:1 000, 13463-1-AP) were obtained from Proteintech Group, Inc (Chicago, USA). GAPDH (1:5 000, 30201ES20) was obtained from Yeasen (Shanghai, China). The secondary antibodies were as follows: anti-rabbit (1:10 000, ab175733, Abcam) and anti-mouse (1:10 000, 7076, Cell Signaling Technology).

ARS and ALP assay

To evaluate odontogenic differentiation potential, P5/P15 cells cultured under both 2D and 3D conditions, along with sphere-derived cells (P5GFP+, P15GFP+, and P15OCT4+), were transferred to 2D tissue-culture-treated plastic plates and subjected to odontogenic induction medium. For ALP staining, after 7 days, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 minutes at room temperature, then rinsed three times with PBS (5 minutes each), and incubated with ALP staining solution (Beyotime) for 1 hour. The reaction was stopped by washing with distilled water, and stained samples were imaged under a light microscope. Semi-quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ software. For ARS staining, matrix mineralization was assessed after 21 days. Cells were fixed with 60% isopropanol for 1 minute, rehydrated with distilled water for 2–3 minutes, and stained with Alizarin Red S solution (Sigma-Aldrich) for 3 minutes. Stained samples were imaged under a light microscope. For quantification, 10% cetylpyridinium chloride (1 mL per well, diluted to 2 mL) was added, and absorbance was measured at 562 nm (200 µL aliquots in quadruplicate).

Subcutaneous transplantation in nude mice

For the preparation of transplantable treated dentin matrix (TDM), human premolars were surgically extracted and processed through sequential treatments. Root canal segments (4-5 mm in length) were ultrasonicated three times, followed by 15-minute demineralization in 17% EDTA solution. Multiple deionized water rinses (5-minute cycles) were subsequently performed. Terminal sterilization was achieved through autoclaving (121 °C, 15 psi, 20 minutes) before storage in α-MEM medium supplemented with 2-mmol/L L-glutamine and 100 U/mL penicillin at 4 °C until transplantation. Cell-Matrigel suspensions (1 × 10⁶ cells per 20 μL) were introduced into prepared TDM and allowed to adhere for 30 minutes prior to implantation. Thirty BALB/c nude mice were randomly allocated into five experimental cohorts: P5GFP+, P15GFP+, P15OCT4+, siCON (siCON-P15OCT4+) and siPER1 (siPER1-P15OCT4+) groups. All animal protocols were conducted in compliance with the Medical Ethics Committee of the Fourth Military Medical University Institutional Animal Care guidelines (IACUC-20241446). The specimens were harvested at 12 weeks post-transplantation. They were subsequently fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 hours at 4 °C and decalcified using a fast decalcification solution (Phenovision Bio, Beijing, China) for 1 month. Following decalcification, the specimens were embedded in OCT (Optimal Cutting Temperature compound) were cut into 7 μm thickness for immunofluorescence staining for DSPP, DMP1, CD31, and VEGF. For histological analysis, paraffin-embedded tissue sections were cut into 5 μm thickness, stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) or Masson’s trichrome staining.

Immunofluorescence staining

The samples, including cells (fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 minutes in room temperature) and OCT-embedded frozen tissue sections (7 μm), were washed with PBS three times and permeated with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 15 minutes. After blocking with the goat serum for 1 hour, the samples were incubated with the primary antibody at 4 °C overnight. After three PBS washes to remove unbound primary antibody, samples were incubated with fluorescent secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 hour. Finally, the nucleus was stained with DAPI (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole). All samples were observed and imaged under a confocal microscope (Nikon A1+, Japan). The fluorescence data analysis was performed by ImageJ. The primary antibody was as follows: OCT4A (1:200, 4903S), SOX2 (1:200, 2840S), NANOG (1:200, 3579S) and P53 (1:200, 2527T) were obtained from Cell Signaling Technology (Massachusetts, USA). DSPP (1:50, sc-73632) and DMP-1 (1:100, sc-6551) were obtained from Santa Cruz Biotechnology. CD31 (1: 200, ab119339) and VEGF (1:200, ab2350) were obtained from Abcam (Cambridge, UK). P16 (1:200, 10883-1-AP), P21 (1:200, 10355-1-AP), and PER1 (1:200, 13463-1-AP) were purchased from Proteintech Group, Inc. (Chicago, USA). The secondary antibodies used were anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (1:200, A11008) and anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (1:200, A11001), both sourced from Invitrogen (California, USA).

H&E and Masson’s Trichrome staining

Following fixation and paraffin embedding, sagittal sections (5 μm) were prepared and histologically analyzed using Solarbio H&E (Beijing, China) and Masson’s trichrome stain kits per manufacturer’s protocols.

ChIP assays

ChIP assays were performed utilizing the BeyoChIP™ ChIP Assay Kit (Beyotime). Chromatin was isolated from 1×10⁶ cells for each experimental condition. The DNA was sheared to an average size of 200 base pairs (bp), and fragmentation was verified through 2% agarose gel electrophoresis in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol (Thermo Fisher). For the immunoprecipitation, a total of 5 µg of anti-OCT4 antibody for ChIP (Abcam) was employed per sample. Additionally, an anti-IgG antibody (Millipore) served as a negative control. The specific primers used for amplifying the promoter fragments of PER1 are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Dual luciferase reporter assays

Dual luciferase reporter constructs containing either the wild-type PER1 promoter (WT-Luc: nucleotides 2501-3178, GenBank Accession No. 5187) or its mutated counterpart (MUT-Luc) were custom-synthesized by Vigene Biosciences (Jinan, China). The mutant variant was generated through site-directed mutagenesis of the core promoter region (-701 to -721), specifically deleting the critical “ATGCAAA” motif in the wild-type sequence. Wild-type and mutant reporter constructs were co-transfected with pRL-TK Renilla luciferase control vector using Lipofectamine 2000 transfection reagent. Following a 48-hour incubation period, transfected cells were lysed with passive lysis buffer. Firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were quantified using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (GLPBIO, Montclair, CA, USA) according to manufacturer specifications. Experimental data were normalized to Renilla luciferase activity and expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD) from three independent experiments performed in technical triplicate.

Statistical analysis

All procedures and quantitative analyses in this study were conducted in a blinded fashion to minimize bias. Each experiment was repeated at least three times to ensure reliability and reproducibility. Data were presented as means±standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (version 19.0; IBM, USA). Parametric analysis was preceded by normality and homoscedasticity verification. If these assumptions were violated, the data were nonlinearly transformed to meet the required assumptions. For comparisons between two groups, Student’s t-test was used. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed. Post hoc multiple comparisons were conducted using Tukey's or Dunnett’s test to determine the significance between groups. These assessments were carried out using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, USA).
