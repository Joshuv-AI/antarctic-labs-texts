The impact of cash and near-cash social policies on substance use outcomes: a scoping review
Authors: Maria Olivia Pozzolo Pedro, Nidhi Binoy, Dylanis López Ruiz, Sam D Gardner, Shota W Hasui, Megan E Marziali, Emilie Bruzelius, Priscila D Gonçalves, Esther Rowan, Pia M Mauro, Jamie R Daw, Morgan M Philbin, Silvia S Martins
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe impact of government financial assistance and substance use is contested by 2 competing hypotheses: the “income effect” posits that cash transfers could facilitate substance consumption, while the “stress-relief hypothesis” posits that cash transfers alleviate poverty-related stress that contributes to substance use.MethodsWe conducted a scoping review of 46 studies (2000-2025) evaluating US cash and near-cash safety net programs on substance use outcomes, following PRISMA-ScR guidance.ResultsEvidence was most robust for tax credits, the Earned Income Tax Credit and Child Tax Credit, reducing tobacco use, particularly among lower-educated mothers. Evidence was suggestive but design-limited for Unemployment Insurance buffering overdose mortality in contexts of job loss, as most studies were ecological. Findings on alcohol and opioid outcomes were generally limited or null. Restrictions in near-cash supports, including drug-felony bans on the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, were associated with higher rates of opioid misuse and substance use disorders. Disbursement design also mattered: lump-sum disability payments and synchronous COVID-era stimulus disbursements were linked to short-term spikes in overdose mortality (“check effect”), while stable, smaller benefits supported treatment retention.ConclusionsThe US safety net plays a meaningful role in substance-related outcomes, with evidentiary strength varying considerably by program and substance.Keywords: substance-related disorders, public assistance, social determinants of health, scoping review

Introduction

Substance use and overdose mortality represent a critical public health crisis in the United States.1,2 Although overdose deaths declined for the first time since 2018 in 2023 and dropped substantially in 2024,3,4 substance-related morbidity and mortality remain historically high and concentrated among populations experiencing greater socioeconomic disadvantage.5 Economic hardship, food insecurity, and housing instability are increasingly recognized as upstream determinants that may influence substance use trajectories and substance-related morbidity and mortality.5-7

Government cash and near-cash benefit programs serve individuals living in poverty or experiencing economic insecurity (eg, job loss). Two competing pathways have been proposed.8,9 Under the “stress-relief hypothesis,” income support reduces material deprivation and financial strain, potentially lowering stress-related coping through alcohol or drug use and improving access to treatment and protective resources.10 Under the “income effect” hypothesis, in contrast, increases in disposable income, particularly when delivered as lump-sum payments, could temporarily increase consumption for some individuals or coincide with short-term increases in substance-related adverse events.10,11 Empirical findings have been mixed, with effects likely varying by policy design, population subgroup, and substance outcome.

The US social safety net comprises “cash” transfers (eg, Earned Income Tax Credit [EITC], Temporary Assistance for Needy Families [TANF], Unemployment Insurance [UI]) and “near-cash” or in-kind assistance (eg, Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program [SNAP], Housing Assistance, and the Special Supplemental Nutrition Program for Women, Infants, and Children [WIC]).12,13 Although many programs have been evaluated individually, the collective evidence on their impact on substance use remains fragmented across substance types, policies, populations, and methodological approaches. Disentangling causal effects from selection bias, given that populations eligible for safety net programs often face elevated baseline substance use risks, is a particular methodological challenge across this literature.

Although recent research has increasingly leveraged quasi-experimental designs to address these complexities, evidence remains dispersed across programs, outcomes (tobacco, alcohol, cannabis, opioids/overdose, other drugs), and study designs, making it difficult to draw integrated conclusions about where and for whom cash and near-cash policies may be protective, neutral, or potentially harmful. To address this gap, we conducted a scoping review of the impact of major US cash and near-cash social safety net programs.

Methods

Study design and objectives

We conducted a scoping review to identify and synthesize existing research on the impact of cash and near-cash social safety net programs on substance use-related outcomes in the United States. The review followed the methodological guidance provided by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) and Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist (Table S1).14,15 The protocol was registered on the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/wj6ye/overview).

Search strategy and information sources

For the purposes of this review, and consistent with existing frameworks,12 “cash” and “near-cash” assistance were defined as programs intended to directly address income instability and material hardship through monetary transfers or in-kind subsidies for essential needs.

We included quantitative and mixed-methods studies published in English between 2000 and July 2025 in both peer-reviewed journals and grey literature (eg, government and think tank reports). Eligible studies had to evaluate at least one US cash or near-cash social safety net policy (eg, SNAP, TANF, EITC, Child Tax Credit [CTC], WIC, UI, and SSI) and assess one or more relevant substance use outcomes, including use, misuse, or substance use disorders (SUDs) related to alcohol, opioids, cannabis, tobacco, cocaine, or prescription drugs (eg, benzodiazepines, stimulants).

We excluded studies that did not assess a social safety net policy as an exposure or did not include substance use outcomes; descriptive policy summaries without evaluation; exclusively qualitative articles; review articles; and conference abstracts or posters lacking full methodological detail.

We searched 4 databases: PubMed, Web of Science, PsycINFO and National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER), using a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text keywords corresponding to 2 conceptual blocks: social policies (exposure) and substance use (outcomes). For social policies, we combined specific program names (eg, SNAP, EITC, UI), broader policy concepts (eg, “guaranteed income,” “cash transfer”), and broader MeSH terms (“Social Welfare,” “Government Programs,” “Food Assistance”). For substance use, we constructed parallel sub-blocks for alcohol, tobacco, opioid (including heroin and opiates), cocaine, and prescription drug misuse (including benzodiazepine and stimulants), as well as a general block capturing substance-related disorders. The complete search strategy for the PubMed database is detailed in appendix (Table S2).

Data extraction and synthesis

Two reviewers (S.G. and D.L.R.) independently screened titles and abstracts. We conducted a pilot test using a random sample of 25 titles and abstracts to ensure consistency in applying inclusion and exclusion criteria. Two independent reviewers (E.R. and N.B.) then assessed the full texts of potentially relevant studies for final eligibility, with disagreements resolved by a third reviewer (M.O.P.P.).

Data extraction was performed in Covidence using a form piloted on a random sample of included studies (n = 10). We systematically extracted information on study design and data sources, populations studied, the specific social safety net policy evaluated, the substance use outcomes measured, and the core findings. We additionally extracted information on study design quality, including whether outcomes were measured at the individual or ecological level and whether the study reported design-validity checks. The complete data extraction template is provided in Table S3.

Results

Screening and selection process

The initial search yielded 10 693 records from databases and registers; imported into Covidence for systematic review management. After removal of 2966 duplicates (2955 identified automatically and 11 manually), 7727 records were screened.

Title and abstract screening excluded 7590 studies, and 137 full-texts were assessed for final eligibility. Of these, 91 were excluded for the reasons shown in Figure 1. The final review included 46 studies: 8 assessed outcomes after the program ended, and 38 assessed individuals while they were receiving benefits. To facilitate comparison across the heterogeneous evidence base, results are grouped by program category. Table 1 summarizes the direction of findings by program category. Table 2 provides detailed characteristics for each of the 46 included studies, including indicators of design quality, and complete statistical evidence is provided in Table S4. In-text effect sizes are reported selectively when magnitudes inform the synthesis or derive from quasi-experimental designs.

Tax credits (EITC & CTC)

We identified 9 studies evaluating tax credits. Evidence was strongest for tobacco use, particularly among low-income single mothers,16,17 more limited for alcohol, and largely null for other SUDs and overdose mortality. A natural experiment exploiting the timing of short-term refund disbursement found EITC receipt was associated with lower cotinine levels (a biomarker for nicotine) in men and lower marijuana use, with no evidence of increased alcohol consumption.18 In contrast, an interrupted time series analysis in Washington, D.C., observed an increase in maternal smoking specifically when the EITC benefit expanded from 10% to 25%.20

Evidence on alcohol was more sparse. A quasi-experimental analysis using Pregnancy Risk Assessment Monitoring System (PRAMS) data found that increased state EITC generosity was associated with lower prevalence (PR: 0.96; 95% CI: 0.93-0.99) of heavy drinking before pregnancy.19

Studies focusing on SUDs and overdose mortality consistently reported null findings. State-level difference-in-differences (DiD) analysis of EITC programs21,22 found no significant association with overall substance use, SUD prevalence, or fatal overdose rates. Dow et al. (2020) similarly found no effects of state EITC supplements on drug- or alcohol-related mortality in event-study and DiD models.23

Regarding the CTC, advanced monthly payments introduced in 2021 were associated with a 4.3%-point decline in tobacco use among parents (a 24% reduction relative to the pre-period mean) in DiD analysis.24

Unemployment insurance

Four studies evaluated the impact of UI benefits. The evidence base is largely ecological and design-limited: most studies estimate associations between state- or county-level UI generosity and population-level outcomes rather than tracking outcomes among individuals who lost employment.

Two ecological analyses suggested protective signals on overdose mortality. A state-level fixed-effects analysis of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) mortality data found that higher UI wage replacement rates were associated with lower opioid and stimulant overdose mortality before and during the COVID-19 pandemic.25 A county-level DiD analysis similarly found that more generous UI attenuated the association between mass layoffs and county-level opioid overdose mortality.26

Two studies used individual-level data. Higher UI maximum benefit levels were associated with increased smoking cessation among unemployed job losers in Current Population Survey data, with stronger effects among less-educated workers.27 During the COVID-19 pandemic, UI receipt buffered economic stress among low-income households but had null effects on average substance use, with non-recipients actually showing larger reductions in smoking.28 Overall, signals of UI buffering overdose mortality remain design-limited, while quasi-experimental individual-level studies indicate increased smoking cessation among people experiencing unemployment.

Nutrition and housing assistance programs (WIC/SNAP)

Seven studies evaluated nutrition and housing assistance. WIC findings consistently pointed to protective associations during pregnancy, particularly with early enrollment; SNAP findings were mixed and largely shaped by study design; evidence on housing assistance was too limited for inferences.

WIC enrollment during the first trimester of pregnancy was associated with a higher likelihood of decreasing or quitting cigarette smoking compared to later enrollment in observational analyses of administrative WIC records and pregnancy surveillance data.29,30 Specifically, observational cohort data showed early enrollees were 4.5% points more likely to quit smoking 3 months before delivery.30 A cross-sectional analysis of birth certificate data found that while WIC enrollment was associated with higher odds of smoking throughout pregnancy (AOR = 1.16; 95% CI: 1.14-1.18), it was also associated with 1.09 (95% CI: 1.07-1.11) higher odds of quitting compared to non-participants.31 Regarding alcohol, quasi-experimental matching analysis linked WIC participation to a 1.2%-point reduction in the probability of consuming any alcohol during pregnancy (a 22% relative decrease) among WIC participants.32

Cross-sectional analyses of SNAP generally indicated a higher prevalence of substance use among beneficiaries: SNAP participation was associated with a 3.1%-point higher likelihood of a low-income household containing at least one person who smoked cigarettes33 and with 79% higher odds of individual smoking (AOR = 1.79; 95% CI 1.42-2.26).34 A single ecological state-level analysis found that states that removed the SNAP felony-drug conviction ban recorded reduced rates of opioid misuse (aRR: 0.94) and SUDs (aRR: 0.91), suggesting—though from a single ecological design—that punitive eligibility restrictions may impede recovery.35

Evidence on housing assistance was limited and derived largely from secondary analyses: in a study primarily evaluating UI benefits, Jeong et al. (2023)28 noted that housing assistance receipt was associated with increased spending on alcohol and recreational drugs among low-income households.

TANF and welfare

Eleven studies examined TANF and traditional welfare. Findings varied significantly by study design: cross-sectional studies typically identified a higher prevalence of substance use among recipients, likely reflecting selection rather than program effects. Longitudinal studies documented declines in use over the course of program enrollment, though these observational designs also cannot fully isolate program effects from secular trends or other time-varying factors. Cross-sectional comparisons consistently reported higher substance use among recipients than non-recipients. A logistic regression analysis of National Household Survey on Drug Abuse (NHSDA) data found past-year drug use was more common in households receiving any assistance (AOR = 1.49; 95% CI: 1.28-1.73) and specifically food stamps (AOR = 1.60; 95% CI: 1.35-1.91).36 Other cross-sectional analyses reported TANF recipients had nearly double the rate of illegal drug use compared to non-recipients (22.3% vs 12.8%) and significantly higher odds of needing drug treatment (AOR = 2.31; 95% CI: 1.02-5.24).37 Within a criminal justice context, welfare-receiving arrestees were more likely to test positive for opiates (13% vs 6%) and benzodiazepines (16% vs 7%) than non-recipients living in poverty.38 More recent repeated cross-sectional analyses showed that cannabis use rose substantially among adults in TANF families,39 and women in TANF families had 1.90 times higher odds of past-year SUD, with particularly elevated odds for heroin (OR = 3.38) and prescription pain reliever misuse (OR = 2.62).40 Among adolescents in public assistance households, higher illegal drug use was offset, in part, by engagement in prevention activities such as school-based education and parent–child conversations.41

Longitudinal data, in contrast, suggested that substance use often declines during enrollment. In a 2-year longitudinal path model, illegal substance use among female TANF recipients dropped from 30% at intake to 19% at 24 months,42 and engagement in work activities was inversely associated with drug use in longitudinal analysis.43 Observational cohort data indicated that women receiving TANF cash benefits were less likely to complete residential drug treatment compared to those not receiving cash assistance (RR for non-completion: 1.37); the comparison group, however, included women ineligible for TANF due to drug felony convictions, complicating direct attribution.44 A longitudinal analysis of unconditional cash transfers provided by a tribal family assistance program found no significant differences in parental substance use problems compared to non-recipients.45

Longitudinal outcomes: benefit termination and life-course effects

Eight studies evaluated outcomes following benefit termination or long-term exposure. Termination of disability benefits did not trigger immediate relapse but was strongly associated with treatment dropout, while life-course effects of childhood welfare exposure on adult substance use varied by methodological approach. In studies of SSI termination, multi-site prospective cohorts of former SSI Drug Addiction and Alcoholism beneficiaries reported declines in alcohol and illegal drug use over 2 years.46,47 A longitudinal analysis similarly found that retaining benefits did not increase cocaine or opioid use.48 However, former beneficiaries who were unemployed and lost benefits had higher odds of drug dependence one year later (OR ∼ 5.0),49 and treatment dropout rates reached 67% following the termination mandate.50

Regarding broader life-course effects, observational longitudinal analyses associated childhood welfare receipt with higher adult substance use: adults who received welfare or food stamps during childhood had a 30% lower probability of remaining cannabis-free in young adulthood (OR = 0.70; 95% CI: 0.60-0.82),51 and persistent welfare receipt of 5 or more years was associated with higher likelihood of cannabis and cocaine use in adulthood.52 In contrast, a quasi-experimental instrumental variable analysis isolating the causal effect of income via the EITC found no significant association between higher post-tax household income and the prevalence of alcohol or tobacco use in a longitudinal panel.53

Timing of receipt and unintended consequences (“check effect”)

We identified 4 studies investigating the timing of benefit disbursement within structural safety net programs. Check effects on adverse outcomes were detected for large, lump-sum monthly disability payments, but not for the EITC tax refund. In California, drug-related hospital admissions were 23% higher during the first 5 days of the month,54 and a multi-site longitudinal study using urinalysis found that while SSI receipt was not associated with increased overall drug use, beneficiaries were 28% more likely to test positive for cocaine during the first 10 days of the month.48 A retrospective spatial analysis identified a similar pattern in mortality, with higher rates of accidental overdose fatalities involving opioids and cocaine during the first 7 days of the month (rate ratio: 1.17; 95% CI: 1.04-1.38).55 A quasi-experimental analysis of the EITC found no “check effect” for alcohol or tobacco following tax refunds.56

Heterogeneity across subpopulations and settings

We identified distinct patterns of heterogeneity across demographic subgroups and specific vulnerable settings. Protective effects were most pronounced among individuals with lower educational attainment, sex-stratified findings tentatively favored men, and findings in vulnerable subpopulations were context-dependent. Studies frequently stratified analyses by education level as a proxy for program eligibility, with protective effects concentrated among individuals with lower educational attainment, including reduced smoking with EITC expansions among mothers,16,17 and lower binge drinking with state EITC generosity19 in low-educated subgroups, stronger smoking cessation effects of UI benefits among less-educated unemployed workers.28 Findings stratified by sex pointed in a consistent direction toward worse outcomes among women: women in TANF families had higher odds of SUDs than non-recipients,39 while EITC receipt was associated with lower biomarkers for smoking among men but not among women.18

Five studies focused on vulnerable subpopulations or settings. Among unhoused and marginally housed adults, those receiving cash subsidies had lower odds of injection drug use (OR = 0.69; 95% CI: 0.52-0.91) in cross-sectional analysis,57 and an observational cohort of unhoused individuals with mental illness found that newly acquiring SSI benefits was not associated with changes in substance use.58 In a clinical setting, observational analysis using machine learning found that linkage to SNAP benefits was associated with a higher probability of successful treatment completion.59 In a criminal justice context, welfare-receiving arrestees were more likely to test positive for opiates and benzodiazepines than non-recipients.38

Among adults receiving federal housing assistance, those who also participated in disability programs (DI/SSI) had lower odds of current smoking (OR = 0.69; 95% CI: 0.51-0.93), with no statistically significant difference for heavy alcohol use (OR = 0.60; 95% CI: 0.33-1.10).60 A separate cross-sectional analysis found that disability status was independently associated with higher odds of alcohol dependence (OR = 2.30) and illicit drug dependence (OR = 3.07).61

Discussion

This scoping review synthesized evidence from 46 studies regarding the impact of cash and near-cash social safety net programs on substance use outcomes in the United States. The collective findings reveal that the relationship between government assistance and substance use is not monolithic; rather, it is highly dependent on the specific effect being measured (eg, acute liquidity shocks vs long-term stability), the timing of disbursement, the beneficiary population, and the methodological rigor of the evaluation. While earlier cross-sectional literature frequently identified a positive correlation between benefit receipt and substance use prevalence,36,37 recent quasi-experimental and longitudinal evidence provides a more nuanced narrative. The strength of evidence varied considerably by program and by substance: it was strongest for tax credits reducing tobacco use, suggestive but largely ecological for UI buffering overdose mortality,25,26 and limited or null for most alcohol and opioid outcomes across programs. A recent randomized controlled trial of an unconditional cash transfer of $1000/month over 3 years among low-income adults further reinforced this pattern, finding mixed and predominantly null effects on alcohol and drug use.62

Although tax credits (EITC/CTC) and Unemployment Insurance both deliver cash to working-age beneficiaries, they target different populations and operate through distinct delivery mechanisms. Tax credits are conditional on employment or parenthood and reach low-income working families; UI, in contrast, is conditional on involuntary job loss and provides time-limited wage replacement during unemployment. Despite these differences, evidence relevant to the stress-relief hypothesis converges most strongly on tobacco use across both programs: EITC expansions were associated with reduced maternal smoking,16,17 Child Tax Credit payments with reduced parental tobacco use,24 and UI benefits with increased smoking cessation among people experiencing unemployment.27 Evidence on alcohol misuse is more limited,19 and signals of UI buffering overdose mortality emerge primarily from ecological analyses.25,26

More substantial evidence suggests a detrimental “check effect” for disability programs (SSI/DI), characterized by abrupt spikes in drug-related hospital admissions48,54 and overdose mortality immediately following benefit issuance.54,55 Experimental evidence supports staggered disbursement as a mitigation strategy: a randomized controlled trial found that staggering payments significantly reduced drug use escalation on payment days (aOR = 0.38) compared to synchronized payments.9 Recent analyses of one-time COVID-19 Economic Impact Payments—although outside our inclusion criteria—converge with this pattern, identifying synchronous spikes in overdose mortality aligned with each Economic Impact Payment round.3,63 Broader work on income receipt and mortality suggests that income may drive short-term increases in economic activity across multiple causes of death, beyond substance-specific harms,64,65 indicating that staggering may redistribute rather than reduce these acute harms in aggregate. In contrast, studies on the EITC found no check effect on substance use or hospitalizations following refund receipt,22,56 suggesting that the population (working families vs disability beneficiaries) and benefit framing (tax refund vs monthly subsistence) moderate acute consumption risk.

Among “near-cash” programs, WIC emerged as a unique, effective program. WIC enrollment was robustly associated with smoking cessation and reduced alcohol consumption during pregnancy, particularly when enrollment occurred in the first trimester.30-32 Cross-sectional data showing higher smoking prevalence likely reflect selection rather than causal program effects. Studies adjusting for selection (eg, highly motivated mothers) have confirmed WIC's protective effects.31,32 Recent evidence supports a cash-vs-in-kind contrast: emergency department visits for drug and alcohol use tended to increase following SSI receipt but showed no response to SNAP receipt,66 strengthening the case that the structure of benefits may shape acute substance-related harms.

Similarly, for TANF, a cross-sectional analysis showing higher drug use among recipients,39,40 likely reflects adverse selection, while longitudinal evidence shows that substance use declines over time following enrollment, particularly when combined with employment activities.42,43 This suggests that workforce engagement and benefit stability may facilitate reductions in substance use rather than perpetuating it.42,43,58

Critically, this review highlights the role of the safety net in supporting treatment retention, harm reduction, and recovery capital. The termination of benefits can have deleterious effects on individuals in recovery: termination of disability benefits significantly undermines treatment retention50 and heightens the risk of dependence among those unable to secure employment.49 Beyond cessation, stable benefits also appear to support reduced substance-related harms in vulnerable populations: cash subsidies among unhoused adults were associated with lower odds of injection drug use,57 acquiring new SSI benefits was not associated with increased substance use among unhoused individuals with mental illness,58 and SNAP linkage was associated with successful treatment completion in clinical settings.59 These findings underscore the structural role of the safety net, though this evidence base remains limited.

Conflicting longitudinal findings on childhood welfare exposure and adult substance use,51,52 likely reflect the difficulty of disentangling benefits from the cumulative disadvantages of poverty, while quasi-experimental designs have generally exonerated cash transfer programs from causing substance use harms.41

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations merit consideration. First, much of the evidence, particularly for TANF and SNAP, relies on cross-sectional designs susceptible to selection bias, as observed correlations may reflect the elevated baseline vulnerabilities of safety net-eligible populations rather than program effects. Evidence on UI is similarly constrained: most studies of overdose-related mortality are ecological, measuring associations at the state or county level rather than tracking outcomes among individuals who actually lost employment.

Second, outcome measurement varied substantially. While some studies used administrative mortality records25,26,54 or biological specimens18,38,48 to validate substance use, most relied on self-reported data from national surveys.24,28,40 Self-reported measures may underestimate prevalence among beneficiaries fearing legal repercussions or loss of eligibility.38,48 Conversely, mortality and hospital records capture only the most severe consequences of use.

Third, the structural complexity of the US safety net complicates the isolation of specific policy effects. Beneficiaries often participate in multiple programs simultaneously (eg, SNAP and TANF), creating interaction effects difficult to disentangle in evaluations. The included studies also span over 3 decades, capturing different policy eras and drug epidemics; older observational data on welfare programs36-38 may have limited generalizability to the contemporary landscape of synthetic opioid use and overdose mortality.

Several priorities for future research follow. First, updated outcome measurement is needed: studies on WIC and maternal health should include vaping and e-cigarettes, and evaluations of cash assistance should address synthetic opioids, which were largely absent in earlier studies.39,40 Second, future research should extend beyond the populations studied to date. Because earlier TANF studies were limited to samples of female recipients with dependent children, reflecting the program's historical focus on families with dependent children, research is needed on male beneficiaries and other less-studied subgroups, including adults without dependent children, racial and ethnic minorities, and people with disabilities. Third, program uptake mechanisms warrant attention, including why eligible unemployed workers fail to access benefits28 and whether administrative burdens themselves exacerbate substance use. Finally, while short-term “check effects” are well-documented,54,55 future work should evaluate the cumulative, long-term impacts of poverty alleviation through quasi-experimental and longitudinal designs that can isolate causal effects.

Conclusion

The synthesis of 46 studies challenges the narrative that government assistance subsidizes substance use. Income support programs show varying effects by program and substance: tax credits are most consistently associated with reduced tobacco use, while signals of UI buffering overdose mortality remain design-limited. While specific disbursement mechanisms, such as lump-sum disability payments, may carry short-term risks, emerging evidence suggests that restrictive eligibility policies (eg, drug-felony bans on SNAP) may be associated with higher rates of substance use harms, though replication is needed. Ultimately, the US safety net appears to play a structural role in supporting treatment retention, reducing substance-related harms, and promoting recovery. Future policy should prioritize the stabilization of these resources as a structural intervention for public health.
