Deep learning–based quantification of muscle volume and attenuation and their associations with preinjury ambulatory status in patients with hip fractures
Authors: Tomoki Asano, Keisuke Uemura, Sotaro Kono, Ryo Higuchi, Yi Gu, Hirokazu Mae, Tetsuro Tani, Kazuma Takashima, Tatsuhiko Kutsuna, Yoshito Otake, Masaki Takao, Seiji Okada, Hidetoshi Hamada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesUse a deep learning model on computerized tomography images to explore how muscle volume and attenuation relate to preinjury ambulatory levels in patients with intertrochanteric or femoral neck fractures.MethodsThis retrospective study involved 170 female patients with intertrochanteric or femoral neck fractures who had preoperative hip computed tomography (CT) scans. Using a deep learning–based model, muscles on the unfractured side were automatically segmented, and the muscle volume and attenuation were measured for four groups: gluteal muscles, hip adductors, quadriceps, and hamstrings. Preinjury walking ability was categorized into three groups based on the Koval index: Group 1 (Koval index 1–3; walking outdoors), Group 2 (Koval index 4–6; walking indoors), and Group 3 (Koval index 7; chairbound or bedridden). Finally, associations between preinjury ambulatory status and muscle parameters, patients’ age, body mass index (BMI), hip bone mineral density, geriatric nutritional risk index, and Charlson comorbidity index (CCI) were analyzed using ordinal logistic regression models.ResultsAmbulatory status was associated with muscle attenuation, CCI, and BMI across all four muscle groups. Other factors did not reach statistical significance. Patients with poorer ambulatory function had lower muscle attenuation, higher CCI, and lower BMI.ConclusionsMuscle attenuation on CT images correlated with preinjury ambulatory status, whereas muscle volume did not. This indicates that evaluating muscle quality via CT may more accurately reflect functional decline in older adults than assessing muscle quantity alone.

Introduction

As populations age, ensuring that older adults maintain their functional independence is a key global health challenge, crucial for preserving quality of life and reducing the gap between overall and healthy life expectancy [1]. Decline in activities of daily living (ADL) is associated with increased risk of injuries and mortality [2,3]. Sarcopenia, characterized by age-related loss of skeletal muscle mass and function, and osteoporosis, characterized by decreased bone mineral density (BMD), are significant contributors to functional decline. Both conditions are closely linked to hip fractures, which greatly increase the risk of mortality among older adults [[4], [5], [6]].

In clinical practice, muscle atrophy has traditionally been evaluated by assessing reductions in muscle mass using modalities such as bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) and dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) [6]. However, these measurements rely on global assessments of the upper and lower extremities, and evaluation of individual muscle groups remains limited. Furthermore, growing attention has focused not only on quantitative muscle loss but also on qualitative changes in muscle composition. In particular, decreased muscle attenuation on computed tomography (CT), reflecting fatty infiltration within muscle tissue (myosteatosis), has been associated with impaired ambulatory status and ADLs [7,8].

Patients with hip fractures represent a frail population in whom osteoporosis, reduced muscle quantity, and impaired muscle quality coexist and collectively influence functional outcomes and prognosis [9]. Therefore, it is clinically important to clarify how muscle quantity and quality relate to functional status in this population. This study aimed to (1) quantify muscle volume, muscle attenuation, and BMD using CT imaging, and (2) examine their associations with preinjury ADLs in patients with hip fractures.

Methods

Ethics approval

This retrospective study received approval from our institution's Institutional Review Board (IRB number: 22099-11). It was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments, or similar ethical guidelines. Informed consent was obtained concerning the study content in an opt-out fashion.

Participants

A consecutive series of 348 patients with intertrochanteric or femoral neck fractures who were treated between July 2023 and June 2025 at a single institution was initially screened. Among these, 88 male patients were excluded because sex-related differences in body composition and muscle characteristics may confound the analyses [10,11]. Subsequently, patients with implants (N = 46), high-energy injuries (N = 26), CT scans without a calibration phantom (N = 9), motion artifacts during CT (N = 2), inadequate lower limb positioning during CT (N = 2), no CT examination performed (N = 1), or a previous fracture on the analyzed side (N = 1) were excluded. Finally, 173 cases were included in the analysis (Fig. 1). The median (interquartile range [IQR]) values for age, height, body weight, and body mass index (BMI) were 87 (82–91) years, 150 (145–153) cm, 45.3 (38.6–50.8) kg, and 20.0 (18.1–23.0) kg/m2, respectively. Patient demographics, including the presence of dementia, diabetes mellitus, and osteoporosis treatment status, were collected.Fig. 1Flowchart for identification and enrollment of patients with intertrochanteric fractures (ITF) or femoral neck fractures (FNF). For these 173 patients, preoperative hip CT images obtained for fracture evaluation and surgical planning were used for analysis.

CT imaging

CT images were captured from the iliac crest to the proximal tibia following a standardized protocol (Revolution Maxima; GE Healthcare Japan, Tokyo, Japan; tube voltage: 120 kV; slice thickness: 1.25 mm). A calibration phantom (B-MAS200, Kyoto Kagaku, Kyoto, Japan) was routinely placed beneath the patient to convert CT attenuation values (Hounsfield units [HU]) into hydroxyapatite concentrations (g/cm3) for BMD evaluation. The images were reconstructed using either a standard or a bone kernel and resampled to a 1 mm slice interval, resulting in a voxel size of (0.625–0.977 mm) × (0.625–0.977 mm) × 1 mm.

Muscle volume, attenuation, and BMD measurements

Bone and muscle structures on the unfractured side were automatically segmented from CT images using a deep learning–based system (Bayesian U-Net) that we previously developed and validated [12,13]. Briefly, the model was trained on manually segmented bone and muscle images and can perform automated segmentation with high accuracy (Dice coefficient: 0.95 [13]). After automated segmentation, a board-certified orthopedic surgeon (TA) reviewed and confirmed the accuracy of the segmented structures, and cases with segmentation errors were excluded.

Pelvic and thigh muscles were classified into four groups based on their function [14]: the gluteal muscles (gluteus maximus, medius, and minimus), hip adductors (pectineus, adductor major, longus, brevis, and gracilis), quadriceps (vastus lateralis, medialis, intermedius, and rectus femoris), and hamstrings (semitendinosus, semimembranosus, and biceps femoris) (Fig. 2). Hip BMD was measured using a deep learning–based method that we previously developed and validated [16]. Briefly, the femur was segmented from CT images, and the segmented femur was rotated to a neutral position using landmarks on the proximal femur identified by a deep learning model. After HU was converted into g/cm3 using the information from the calibration phantom segmented by another deep learning model [17]. The rotated images were projected in the anteroposterior direction, and BMD was measured in the region between the neck landmarks and a point 2 cm distal to the lesser trochanter. This measurement closely correlates (r = 0.94) with DXA-derived total hip BMD [16]; the BMD obtained in this study is referred to as CT-derived areal BMD (CT-aBMD). The number and proportion of patients with a T-score ≤ −2.5 were also calculated based on the standards for Japanese females [11].

Assessment of preinjury ambulatory status and its associations with muscle parameters and BMD

Preinjury ambulatory status was assessed using the Koval index, which classifies individuals according to ambulatory ability [18,19]. Patients were divided into three groups according to the Koval index: Group 1 included those with a Koval index of 1–3, capable of walking outdoors with or without aids; Group 2 consisted of individuals with a Koval index of 4–6, able to walk indoors with or without aids; and Group 3 encompassed patients with a Koval index of 7, who were chairbound or bedridden. The study compared muscle volume, muscle attenuation, and hip BMD across these preinjury ambulatory statuses using a univariate analysis.

Assessment of preinjury ambulatory status with muscle parameters and patient background (multivariate analysis)

To clarify the factors linked to ambulatory status, a multivariate analysis was performed using variables like the geriatric nutritional risk index (GNRI) [20] and the Charlson comorbidity index (CCI) [21]. Specifically, GNRI was determined using the formula: GNRI = (1.489 × serum albumin [g/L]) + (41.7 × present body weight/ideal body weight), as defined in 2005 [20]. Patients were divided into four groups based on GNRI levels (Group 1: GNRI < 82 [major nutrition-related risk]; Group 2: 82 ≤ GNRI < 92 [moderate nutrition-related risk]; Group 3: 92 ≤ GNRI ≤ 98 [low nutrition-related risk]; and Group 4: 98 < GNRI [no risk]), according to previous studies [22]. The CCI was calculated based on the presence or absence of specific comorbidities, including dementia and diabetes mellitus with complications. Patients were categorized into four groups based on their CCI (Group 0: CCI = 0, Group 1: CCI = 1, Group 2: CCI = 2, and Group 3: CCI ≥ 3), according to previous studies [23].

All data needed to calculate GNRI and CCI were obtained from medical records, including laboratory results and anthropometric measurements (eg, height and body weight) obtained at admission. Dementia was identified based on nursing staff documentation in the admission questionnaire at hospitalization. Diabetes mellitus was defined by either documentation in the admission records or the use of antidiabetic medications. Using these variables, the study examined the relationship between preinjury ambulatory status and factors such as muscle status, BMD, and patient background through multivariate analysis.

Statistical analysis

The normality of continuous variables was evaluated with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Data that are not normally distributed are reported as medians. For univariate analysis, group comparisons were made using the Kruskal–Wallis test, with the Steel–Dwass test for multiple comparisons.

Multivariable analyses using ordinal logistic regression were performed for each muscle group, with Koval groups as the dependent variable. Muscle volume and muscle attenuation were included as independent variables, while age, BMI, BMD, GNRI, and CCI were included as covariates.

All analyses were performed using JMP (Student Edition Version 18; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). A P-value less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Results

Of the 173 eligible cases, three cases were excluded due to segmentation errors identified during manual review by a board-certified orthopedic surgeon, leaving 170 cases for final analysis.

Muscle volume and attenuation

The median (IQR) volumes of the gluteal muscles, hip adductors, quadriceps, and hamstrings were 674.3 (580.4–759.2) cm3, 440.7 (368.7–496.8) cm3, 650.0 (534.2–763.9) cm3, and 284.2 (234.4–335.1) cm3, respectively. The median (IQR) muscle attenuation values were 21.9 (13.5–29.7) HU for the gluteal muscles, 32.9 (28.9–36.9) HU for the hip adductors, 43.7 (38.8–46.7) HU for the quadriceps, and 27.7 (18.7–33.8) HU for the hamstrings.

BMD analysis

The median (IQR) CT-aBMD was 0.526 (0.464–0.605) g/cm2, corresponding to a median T-score of −3.5. A total of 133 patients (78.2%) had T-scores ≤ −2.5.

Discussion

We used deep learning to measure muscle volume and attenuation from preoperative hip CT scans and determine how these metrics relate to preinjury ambulatory status, particularly among patients with intertrochanteric or femoral neck fractures. Our results indicated that ambulatory ability is more strongly associated with muscle attenuation than with muscle volume, suggesting that muscle attenuation is a more sensitive imaging biomarker of functional decline than muscle volume in this patient population.

Preinjury ambulatory status

In this case series, 68.9% of patients were classified as ambulatory status group 1 (corresponding to Koval index levels 1–3). This proportion is within previously reported ranges (64%–81% [24,25]). Although this study was conducted at a single institution, the similarity to previously reported distributions suggests some generalizability; nevertheless, multicenter studies will be required to confirm that our findings apply to the broader hip fracture cohort.

Muscle volume and attenuation

While CT imaging provides detailed insights into both muscle volume and attenuation, traditional analysis methods that rely on manual measurements are time-consuming and limit clinical use. For example, previous studies have used one or a few axial slices for measurements [[26], [27], [28]]. However, these approaches may lead to discrepancies between single-slice measurements and volumetric analyses based on segmentation of all slices [29,30]. Recent progress in artificial intelligence (AI) has facilitated the automated and consistent extraction and measurement of skeletal muscle from CT scans [12,13,31], and we employed a deep learning model in this study to automatically segment muscles from CT scans and assess volumetric and attenuation parameters for each muscle group. Segmentation was performed successfully, with only three cases excluded due to errors, which are unlikely to have affected the overall results given the number of cases. Recent research has demonstrated the high accuracy of AI-driven muscle segmentation and its potential to predict postoperative recovery and inform rehabilitation after hip arthroplasty [14]. Therefore, we believe our study is novel because it combines fully automated muscle segmentation with assessment of preinjury ambulatory status in patients with hip fractures.

BMD analysis

Our analysis showed that 78.2% of patients had T-scores ≤ −2.5, with this rate higher in group 2 than in group 1. Despite the high median age of our cohort at 87 years, this proportion exceeds the osteoporosis prevalence of 56.2% to 68.3% reported in population-based studies of Japanese women over 80 years old [32,33]. These findings indirectly support a strong association between low BMD and hip fracture incidence.

Assessment of preinjury ambulatory status with muscle parameters, BMD, and patient background

In the multivariate analysis, muscle attenuation showed a significant association with ambulatory status, whereas muscle volume did not. Muscle attenuation on CT reflects intramuscular fat infiltration, commonly referred to as myosteatosis, which has been recognized as an important indicator of muscle quality [15,34]. In particular, the accumulation of fat within muscles is linked to reduced muscle strength and mitochondrial dysfunction, both of which impair muscle performance [5,35,36]. Unlike muscle volume, which primarily reflects the amount of muscle, muscle attenuation reflects qualitative changes in muscle tissue that may occur before noticeable muscle loss. This could explain why muscle attenuation showed a more robust and consistent association with ambulatory status than muscle volume in the current study.

The associations of CCI and BMI with ambulatory status were expected, as previous studies have linked poorer functional status with comorbidities such as dementia [37] and low BMI [38]. In contrast, BMD was not significantly associated with ambulatory status in the multivariate analysis, possibly reflecting selection bias in this hip fracture cohort, where BMD is already substantially reduced.

Overall, our findings are consistent with previous reports showing that lower muscle attenuation is associated with poorer mobility, slower gait speed, and increased risk of disability in older adults [[39], [40], [41]]. These results support the potential value of muscle attenuation as an imaging-based indicator of functional decline.

Clinical application

Traditionally, the assessment of sarcopenia has centered on skeletal muscle mass, with current diagnostic criteria primarily based on quantitative measures such as BIA and DXA [6]. However, growing evidence indicates that muscle quality is also a critical determinant of physical performance and functional status [34]. For example, the phase angle obtained from BIA has attracted attention as a surrogate marker of muscle quality and cellular health [42,43]. Our findings indicate that simply assessing muscle quantity may not fully capture the relationship between skeletal muscle and daily activities. Instead, measuring muscle attenuation from CT scans, which reflects intramuscular fat infiltration, may provide additional insights into muscle quality and its functional impact.

This study examined factors related to preinjury ambulatory status in patients with hip fractures, including those with intertrochanteric and femoral neck fractures. However, some previous studies have reported demographic differences between these fracture subtypes [44]. Therefore, an additional post hoc analysis was performed based on fracture subtypes. Similar results were observed in the intertrochanteric fracture group (N = 87; Supplementary Table 1). In the femoral neck fracture group (N = 83), a specific association between muscle volume and ambulatory status was found for the gluteal muscles. In contrast, findings for other muscles were consistent with the overall analysis (Supplementary Table 2). This finding may suggest potential differences among fracture subtypes and underscores the need for further research. Nonetheless, heterogeneity in muscle characteristics has also been reported within femoral neck fracture subtypes [31]. The subtype analysis results should be interpreted carefully, and additional studies involving larger cohorts are necessary.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, all cases were female patients with proximal femoral fractures. Therefore, the generalizability of our findings to men or individuals without hip fractures remains unknown. Our next step would be to expand the research to men, as studies have shown sex-related differences in muscle status. Second, muscles were categorized by function and evaluated in groups; individual muscles, and muscles in other regions (eg, the lower leg), were not assessed. Future, well-powered studies are warranted to clarify the relationships among individual muscles over broader anatomical regions and ambulatory status.

Conclusions

Using a deep learning approach, we showed that muscle attenuation in hip CT images is strongly associated with ambulatory status in patients with intertrochanteric or femoral neck fractures. In contrast, the relationship between muscle volume and ambulatory status was not found. These results suggest that CT-based muscle attenuation, which reflects muscle quality, could be a sensitive imaging biomarker for functional decline in this group. Although assessing skeletal muscle mass with BIA or DXA remains valuable, our findings highlight the need to also focus on muscle quality when evaluating functional status.
