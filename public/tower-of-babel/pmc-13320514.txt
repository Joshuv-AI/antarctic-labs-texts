Social inequalities in climate change concern and their relationship with health and wellbeing in adolescents, Barcelona 2021
Authors: Esther Sánchez-Ledesma, Mariona Ferrer-Fons, Gemma Serral, Carlos López-Muley, Paula Santiá, Katherine Pérez, María José López, Laura Oliveras
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionClimate change (CC) is a major global challenge with detrimental environmental, social and health impacts. Adolescents and youth are particularly vulnerable to the adverse emotional and mental health effects of CC. However, concern about CC can prompt them to engage in climate action. This study aimed to assess social inequalities in CC concern and their relationship with the health and wellbeing of adolescents in Barcelona in 2021.Materials and methodsA cross-sectional study was performed using data from the 2021-Secondary Education Risk Factors Survey (FRESC), among students in 8th, 10th, and 12th grades and vocational training. CC concern was measured by agreement with the statement "I'm concerned about climate change" . Health outcomes included: self-perceived health; mental health (via Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire); depression (via Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Wellbeing Scale); and life satisfaction. Demographic variables included gender, grade, contextual socioeconomic status and school ownership. Poisson regression models with robust variance estimated prevalence ratios (PR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI).ResultsOf 3,290 adolescents surveyed, 73.3% reported CC concern revealing gender inequalities (80.4%: girls; 65.8%; boys; 75.8%: non-binary). CC concern was most prevalent among 12th-grade and non-binary vocational training students. In 8th and 10th grades, concerned girls showed better health outcomes (poor health: prevalence ratio (PR) [95%CI]= 0.70[0.57–0.86]; poor mental health: PR [95%CI] = 0.68[0.48–0.97]; probable depression: PR [95%CI] = 0.75[0.58–0.95]), while the opposite trend was found in older students.ConclusionCC concern was more prevalent among girls and non-binary students. In lower grades, concerned girls showed better health outcomes but this reversed in higher grades. Adolescents must be included in policymaking, with a focus on climate justice to protect their health and wellbeing.

Introduction

Climate change (CC) is a tangible reality with significant environmental and social impacts [1,2]. Recent studies show a global surge in concern about its effects [3,4]. The health effects of CC can be classified as direct – such as heat and extreme weather events, which contribute to malnutrition, infectious diseases, and mortality [5]—and indirect effects, including concern and perceived future risks that affect mental health and wellbeing [6]. Exposure to climate-related information shapes individuals' and communities' risk perception, influencing their mental health and wellbeing [3]. Several studies have identified an association between CC concern and subjective distress, often referred to as eco-anxiety, solastalgia, or eco-grief [[7], [8], [9]].

Adolescents and young people are recognized as being particularly vulnerable to CC and its effects on mental health [10,11]. A study conducted across 10 different countries [12] revealed that 59% of individuals aged 16–25 in these countries were either very or extremely worried about CC, with 45% of them reporting that CC concern negatively affected their daily functioning. Several factors can explain youth vulnerability to CC threats. First, adolescence is a critical stage of identity formation, marked by emotional variability due to physical, emotional and social changes [13,14]. Secondly, uncertainty regarding future educational paths, employment, and opportunities for their independence can intensify emotional stress and vulnerability [15]. Concurrently, exposure to uncertainty about future climate conditions may exacerbate their emotional distress [8,16]. Thirdly, younger generations are expected to bear the greatest burden of CC [5], despite older generations being responsible for poor mitigation policies. Paradoxically, CC concern can also motivate youth engagement in climate action [17,18], as seen in their participation in protests and social movements such as Fridays for Future, which express generational grievances while fostering mutual support and collective resilience [18].

CC can exacerbate health inequalities as individuals or communities with fewer social and economic resources are often more exposed to its impacts and face greater difficulty in coping with it [5,19]. Vulnerable populations are among those most likely to experience the effects of CC more intensely—particularly older adults (especially women) [6,16], individuals lacking resources to adapt their homes to CC, communities in severely affected areas facing forced migration, and younger generations [10]. However, evidence remains limited regarding how concern about CC may vary across different dimensions of inequality such as gender, social class or geographical areas. This study aimed to assess inequalities in CC concern and its relationship with health and wellbeing among adolescents in Barcelona in 2021.

Methods

Design, information source and study population

We conducted a cross-sectional study using data from the 2021 Secondary School Risk Factors Survey (FRESC) [20], a population-based survey that collects health-related information from adolescents attending schools in Barcelona. The survey includes a representative sample of students enrolled in the 8th year of compulsory education (ages 13–14 years), the 10th year (15–16 years), and the 12th year of non-compulsory education and vocational training (17–19 years). Data were anonymously collected through self-administered questionnaires completed in the classroom during school hours. The FRESC survey is part of Catalonia’s Annual Statistical Action Program (https://www.idescat.cat/pec/ ;registration number 09 01 03). As this study involves secondary analysis of publicly available anonymized data, it was exempt from ethics committee approval.

Study variables

Climate change perceptions

The 2021 FRESC survey incorporated five items from the Climate Change Attitude Survey, measuring beliefs about CC [21]. These are Likert-type items using 1 to 5 scale: 1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = undecided, 4 = agree and 5 = strongly agree. The items were: 1) I believe our climate is changing; 2) I am concerned about climate change; 3) Human activities cause climate change; 4) Climate change has a negative effect on our lives; and 5) I can do my part to make the world better for future generations. The main indicator used in this study was "I am concerned about climate change", dichotomized into “yes” (agree/strongly agree) and “no” (undecided/ disagree/ strongly disagree). Analysis of the remaining indicators is available in the Supplementary Material (Table S.1).

Health and wellbeing

To assess general health, we used a self-reported health indicator dichotomized into "good health" (excellent/very good) and "poor health" (good/fair/poor) following the methodology of previous studies, as most children and adolescents tend to report optimal health values [22]. Mental health was assessed using the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ), with scores dichotomized into "good mental health" (normal/borderline) and "poor mental health" (abnormal) [23]. Depression was measured using the Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Wellbeing Scale (WEMWBS) [24], with responses dichotomized into "yes" (likely depression) and "no" (high and medium emotional wellbeing). Finally, adolescents rated their life satisfaction on a scale from 0 (not satisfied) to 10 (very satisfied), with scores from 0 to 4 indicating low life satisfaction [25].

Sociodemographic and socioeconomic data

Variables included gender (girl, boy and non-binary), grade (8th, 10th, 12th, and vocational training), household socioeconomic position, neighbourhood income level and school ownership. Household socioeconomic position was measured using the Family Affluence Scale (FAS), categorized into high, medium, and low [26]. Neighbourhood income level was assessed using the household disposable income index, with the 73 city neighbourhoods grouped into quintiles (1* being the lowest) [27]. School ownership was dichotomized as either public or charter/private.

Data analysis

First, we categorized the variables as detailed above and described the sample according to these variables. We calculated the percentage of young people concerned about climate change (CC) with 95% confidence intervals (CI), by sociodemographic and socioeconomic variables. To assess differences in CC concern within each variable’s categories, we used the Pearson chi-square test and the Fisher’s exact test. Next, we calculated the prevalence of health and wellbeing indicators among young people, both concerned and not concerned about CC. To analyse the association between CC concern and health and wellbeing outcomes, we calculated prevalence ratios (PR), and their CI'were estimated using Poisson regression models with robust variance. All analyses were stratified by gender. Analyses of health variables were also stratified by grade. Non-binary youth were excluded due to insufficient sample size. We used the R software (version 3.4.4, R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

As a sensitivity analysis, we repeated the health variable analyses stratifying and adjusting by neighbourhood of residence to ensure it did not confound or modify the relationship between CC concern and health and wellbeing. The results, which should be interpreted cautiously because of the number of missing values, are available in Supplementary Material (Table S.2).

Results

Table 3 shows the prevalence of health outcomes among adolescents with and without CC concern, along with the corresponding PR, stratified by gender and grade. Among girls, health indicators were more favourable in 8th- and 10th-grade students who expressed CC concern. This association was statistically significant in 8th-grade students for poor self-perceived health (PR [95%CI]: 0.70 [0.57;0.86]), poor mental health (PR [95 %CI]:0.68 [0.48;0.97]), and likely depression (PR [95%CI]: 0.75 [0.58;0.95]). In contrast, although not statistically significant, point estimates suggested a reversed pattern among 12th-grade and vocational training students, where girls concerned about CC showed worse health indicators. For instance, 12th-grade girls concerned about CC had 1.36 times poor mental health (95%CI [0.66; 2.79]) compared to their not-concerned peers. Among vocational training students, the PR was 1.83 (95%CI [0.89;3.76]).

Among boys, the overall results suggested better health among those concerned about CC across all grades. Discussion

This study assessed concern about CC and its relationship with health and wellbeing through a population-based survey of adolescents in the city of Barcelona in 2021. High levels of CC concern were observed, with differences by gender, educational grade and contextual socioeconomic status. CC concern was more prevalent among girls - particularly those from higher-income neighbourhoods - and among non-binary adolescents, and increased with grade level. Significant health differences were observed only among younger girls concerned about CC, who reported better mental health and emotional wellbeing compared to their not-concerned peers. Furthermore, findings suggest a reverse pattern among students in higher grades, where girls concerned about CC reported worse health indicators.

Our study reveals a high prevalence of CC concern among adolescents in Barcelona, with 73.3% expressing such concern, consistent with previous research identifying adolescents as particularly vulnerable to climate-related impacts [8,12]. For example, a recent survey of 9,000 Spanish young people (aged 16–30) revealed that 97% were concerned about CC [28]. Similarly, a special edition of Eurobarometer on CC found that 96% of young Europeans (aged 15–25) considered CC to be a serious problem affecting them [29]. Both studies also indicated that concern increases with age.

Our findings also revealed differences in CC concern by gender, school grade and socioeconomic status among adolescents.

Social inequalities in climate change concern

Girls and non-binary adolescents expressed greater concern about CC than boys. These findings confirm previously documented gender inequalities in CC perceptions, with girls consistently reporting higher levels of CC concern and a higher willingness to engage in climate action [[30], [31], [32]]. Gender inequalities in CC concern - and environmental issues in general - can be attributed to socialization processes that define gender norms, shaping different behaviours, values and attitudes for women and men. These norms, along with inequalities in power, access and control over resources, lead to different opportunities for both genders from early childhood; girls are typically socialized to care for others, while boys are often encouraged to assert dominance. A similar pattern occurs in non-binary adolescents, who may distance themselves from normative masculinity and adopt values associated with femininity—such as empathy, care and collective responsibility—increasing awareness of social and environmental issues. Women, girls and non-binary individuals with greater awareness of CC, potentially due to their caregiving roles, tend to perceive it as a threat to collective wellbeing [31], a perspective that may be linked to the rise of ecofeminist movements, particularly among younger girls [18,33]. Ecofeminism critiques how Western societies historically have enabled both the oppression of women and the exploitation of nature, providing a theoretical framework for understanding the mechanisms underlying gender and environmental inequalities and advocating for climate justice and equity [33]. A study conducted among Spanish youth (aged 15–29) in 2020 found that girls were more interested in issues such as CC and gender equity than boys, identifying CC as the most significant phenomenon of the last 30 years [34].

Differences also were observed by educational grade. A grade-based gradient was observed in all students, particularly among girls, with CC concern increasing from lower to higher compulsory education levels and peaking in 12th grade. This trend suggests that the secondary school curriculum may enhance students’ understanding of CC over time, thereby increasing concern in higher grades. However, this pattern was not observed among vocational training students. Significant differences in CC concern were found between 12th-grade students and those in vocational training, with the former expressing greater concern. To our knowledge, no previous studies have reported this difference, making our findings novel in identifying inequalities in CC concern within post-compulsory secondary education. These differences may reflect variation in curricular content between the Baccalaureate and vocational training courses, as well as university-oriented interests among Baccalaureate students. Educational trajectories are also shaped by social inequalities, including socioeconomic status and parental education [35]. These findings warrant further investigation, highlighting the need for targeted CC education in vocational training programs.

Our results also suggest a socioeconomic gradient in CC concern, with adolescents in higher-income neighbourhoods reporting greater levels of concern. This pattern may reflect the influence of material conditions on environmental awareness: young people from lower-income areas, often exposed to more immediate socioeconomic challenges, may prioritize urgent daily needs over broader or more abstract issues such as CC and its long-term consequences [16,30]. By contrast, adolescents from higher socioeconomic backgrounds report greater climate concern, reflecting better access to educational resources, climate information and opportunities for pro-environmental engagement, even after controlling for educational attainment [36,37].

Climate change concern and health and wellbeing

Our study identified high levels of poor mental health among adolescents, potentially attributable to a complex interplay of factors including academic demands, identity development, social relationships, family expectations and increased social media [15]. These findings should also be interpreted in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic, which had significant psychosocial impacts on this population group [38]. Lockdowns, school closures, mobility restrictions or fear of illness disrupted their opportunities for peer socialization and leisure – critical activities for their physical and social development - contributing to higher anxiety, depression and loneliness in this group [39].

A weak association was observed between CC concern and health indicators. While CC concern may influence mental health, its effects can be attenuated by factors such as low family income, poverty and precarious living conditions [12]. Furthermore, existing research suggests that CC may affect mental health through physical and existential threats over time [40], with adolescents reporting diverse emotional responses, including fear, anxiety, frustration, and hope [41]. It is also important to note that CC concern is a subjective indicator, reflecting general perceptions and may exhibit low specificity and sensitivity in distinguishing between different health outcomes [11].

Among girls aged 13 to 16, CC concern was associated with better general and mental health indicators. Although CC concern does not inherently reflect adverse health outcomes, it may exacerbate pre-existing health conditions or contribute to the onset of new ones [30]. Conversely, such concern can also motivate pro-environmental behaviours at both individual and collective levels, which can enhance social cohesion and empowerment, potentially mitigating distress [42]. A report by the World Health Organization highlights the physical and mental risks posed by CC and emphasizes the need for policies addressing mental health [43]. In our study, adolescents who did not express CC concern had poorer health indicators. This finding aligns with expectations related to the negative impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on living conditions [38]. Furthermore, adolescents from socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds may prioritize immediate material needs, which can limit engagement with broader environmental concerns such as CC [12]. Among non-binary adolescents, poorer health outcomes compared to girls and boys are partly because they often start from a more vulnerable health position, particularly regarding mental health. Experiences of discrimination, stigma, and violence increase their risk, which can be further amplified by CC concerns. Globally, these overlapping vulnerabilities contribute to worse overall and mental health in gender-diverse populations [39].

Limitations and strengths

The study has several limitations. First, the data analysed were derived from a general health questionnaire not specifically designed to assess concern about CC. Nonetheless, to our knowledge, this is the first European adolescent health survey to incorporate climate-related indicators, highlighting the growing relevance of this topic in this population. Secondly, the small sample size of the non-binary students limited the detection of differences in health indicators although it did reveal social inequalities in CC concern, allowing a gender-based analyses rather than by sex. The small size of this subgroup, however, did not allow for reliable pairwise post hoc comparisons with Bonferroni correction after the initial chi-square tests, as these analyses would not have sufficient statistical power and could lead to unstable or misleading estimates. Thirdly, the sample size of vocational training students was small and the classification of educational institutions was heterogeneous. Therefore, results should be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, the sample was representative of secondary school students at the city level, which may have mitigated this limitation. Lastly, the questionnaire was administered during the COVID-19 pandemic, potentially influencing mental health and wellbeing outcomes. The impact of the pandemic on emotional distress cannot be ruled out, particularly among students from lower socioeconomic backgrounds. These students reported greater concern about their precarious economic and family situations, which may have led them to prioritize immediate material needs over CC concerns. However, collecting data during such a historical period offers valuable insights into the effects of the pandemic on this population at that time.

Despite these limitations, this study is the first to examine concern about CC and its effects on health and wellbeing in a representative sample of adolescents from a Southern European city. The findings reveal notable social inequalities in CC concern, suggesting an emerging trend that warrants monitoring. These results underscore the need for policies and interventions aimed at promoting health equity and climate justice.

Conclusions and recommendations

CC represents one of the major challenges of the 21st century. This study identifies a high prevalence of CC concern among adolescent girls and non-binary individuals, particularly those in higher school grades, which may predispose them to mental ill-health. Understanding the impact of CC on youth health is essential for designing interventions aimed at strengthening emotional resilience, especially in response to climate-related concerns.

In a context of growing climate apprehension, further research employing qualitative methodologies is needed to better understand youth perceptions. Preventive interventions should be universal and gender-sensitive, supporting coping skills, collective agency and addressing gender norms that may inhibit emotional expression, particularly among boys. Young people play a critical role in climate action, and their concerns should prompt policymakers to develop measures addressing the associated health impacts. Consequently, involving young people in decision-making processes, with a focus on social and climate justice, is imperative.
