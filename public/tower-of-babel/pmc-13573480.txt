Impact of dynamic blood glucose trajectories on mortality in critically ill non-traumatic intracranial hemorrhage patients: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Mingkun Yang, Yanwen Lu, Yijing Liu, Yifeng Chen, Xindian Pan, Xiaofeng Zhang, Weihang Hu, Jing Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDysglycemia is frequently observed in patients with non-traumatic intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) and has been linked to adverse clinical outcomes. However, the prognostic impact of longitudinal blood glucose trajectories in critically ill non-traumatic intracranial hemorrhage remains incompletely understood.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study using the MIMIC-IV database (United States, 2008–2019), including 2,260 critically ill non-traumatic ICH patients. Latent class mixed modeling (LCMM) was employed to identify distinct longitudinal blood glucose trajectory subtypes, utilizing blood glucose data collected during the initial 72 h of ICU admission. Associations between trajectory groups and mortality were assessed using multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression.ResultsFour glucose trajectories were identified: Trajectory 1 (persistent low), Trajectory 2 (moderate stable), Trajectory 3 (progressive decline), and Trajectory 4 (progressive increase). Significant differences in baseline characteristics were observed across trajectories. Even after adjusting for baseline blood glucose and other potential confounders, the 28-day mortality risk remained significantly elevated in Trajectories 2–4 compared to Trajectory 1, with hazard ratios of 1.71 (95% CI: 1.33–2.20), 1.87 (95% CI: 1.25–2.80), and 2.41 (95% CI: 1.64–3.54), respectively. Consistent findings were observed for 180-day and 1-year mortality.ConclusionFour distinct blood glucose trajectories observed during the first 72 h of ICU admission among critically ill patients with non-traumatic ICH were independently associated with 28-day and longer-term mortality. These findings may support early risk stratification and inform future prospective studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12883-026-05351-0.Keywords: Non-traumatic intracranial hemorrhage, Blood glucose, Dynamic trajectories, Mortality

Introduction

As a critical subset of stroke, non-traumatic intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) is a life-threatening neurological event [1]. Non-traumatic ICH causes nearly half of all stroke-related disability-adjusted life years, and remains a global public health crisis because of its high fatality and disability rates [2–4]. Critically ill non-traumatic intracranial hemorrhage patients in the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) have prominent clinical complexity. Beyond surgery, their prognosis relies largely on precise management guided by dynamic pathophysiological indicators, including blood glucose [5], blood pressure [6], and intracranial pressure [7].

Blood glucose dysregulation is a core prognostic factor for non-traumatic ICH. Its complex mechanisms involve oxidative stress, inflammation and secondary neuronal injury [8, 9]. Numerous previous studies have demonstrated that hyperglycemia independently increases the early mortality risk in acute spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage [10–12]. In addition, several glucose-related derived indices, such as glucose variability [13] and the stress hyperglycemia ratio [9], have been shown to correlate with the prognosis of non-traumatic ICH.

Most existing studies only adopt admission glucose and ignore dynamic physiological changes in critically ill non-traumatic intracranial hemorrhage patients. In contrast, longitudinal trajectory analysis based on repeated measurements reveals individual and population differences for comprehensive prognostic evaluation [14]. This approach has been widely applied in critical care research to investigate disease heterogeneity, including sepsis [15], acute kidney injury [16], and heart failure [17]. Recently, latent class mixed modeling (LCMM)-based glucose trajectories have also been associated with 30-day mortality in patients with ischemic stroke [18]. However, these findings cannot be directly extrapolated to non-traumatic ICH, which elicits stronger neuroendocrine stress responses involving massive catecholamine release and severe intracranial hypertension and further induces distinct metabolic derangements [19].

Therefore, this study aimed to identify distinct blood glucose trajectory subtypes based on serial monitoring data during the first 72 h of ICU admission. Furthermore, we analyzed the associations between these dynamic patterns and mortality to optimize early risk stratification in critically ill non-traumatic ICH patients.

Method

Data source

This retrospective cohort study used data from the MIMIC (Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care)-IV database [20]. MIMIC-IV is a publicly available database containing clinical data from ICU patients at the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston, USA, hospitalized between 2008 and 2019. The database included demographic information, laboratory test results, vital signs, and treatment data.

Study population and definitions

Inclusion criteria for this study were: patients diagnosed with non-traumatic ICH based on ICD-9 or ICD-10 codes (detailed in Table S1). Exclusion criteria were: (1) age < 18 years; (2) multiple hospitalizations or repeated ICU admissions; (3) ICU stay < 3 days; (4) insufficient longitudinal glucose data. To mitigate surveillance bias, the initial 72 h of ICU admission were divided into six 12-hour windows, with all readings within a window averaged. Inclusion required valid mean glucose values in at least two distinct windows.

Variable extraction

Clinical data were extracted from the MIMIC-IV database using structured query language, including the following variables: (1) demographic characteristics: age, sex, race, weight; (2) comorbidities: congestive heart failure, cancer, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, diabetes, hypertension, renal failure, and sepsis. The diagnoses of these comorbidities were identified based on the international classification of diseases codes; (3) laboratory and physiological indicators: blood glucose, prothrombin time, activated partial thromboplastin time, white blood cell count, platelet, hemoglobin, sodium, potassium, calcium, chloride, blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, heart rate, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, respiratory rate, body temperature, and oxygen saturation (SpO2); (4) disease severity scores: simplified acute physiology score II (SAPS II) [21], sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA) score [22], Glasgow coma scale (GCS) [23]; (5) treatment: use of insulin, renal replacement therapy, mechanical ventilation, and vasopressors. For all laboratory tests and vital signs, the average values during the first 24 h of ICU admission were calculated and used as baseline measurements. The primary outcome was 28-day mortality. Secondary outcomes included mortality at 180 days and 365 days. As described in the MIMIC-IV documentation, the date of death was derived from hospital admission records and Massachusetts State death records; deaths occurring more than one year after hospital discharge were censored, so the maximum follow‑up was one year after the last discharge [20].

Trajectory clustering analysis

LCMM was applied to model blood glucose trajectories based on repeated measurements within the first 72 h after ICU admission, implemented via the hlme function of the lcmm package in R. Time was modeled as a continuous quadratic polynomial (intercept, linear, and quadratic terms) for both fixed and class-specific mixture effects to capture non-linear dynamics. The model incorporated random effects on the intercept and linear slope with an unstructured covariance matrix, and permitted class-specific variances. We compared models with 2 to 6 latent trajectories and selected the best model using statistical criteria, including maximum log-likelihood, Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). The average posterior probability (AvePP) was calculated, with values greater than 0.70 considered indicative of adequate classification. Additionally, we ensured that each group contained at least 5% of the sample to maintain representativeness.

Machine learning–based phenotype identification

We employed the XGBoost algorithm to identify clinical indicators measured at ICU admission that predicted memberships of the blood glucose trajectory subtypes. XGBoost was selected for its superior ability to capture complex non-linear interactions and handle inherent sparsity in clinical data [24, 25]. Patients were randomly partitioned into a training set (70%) and an independent testing set (30%). Hyperparameter tuning was performed exclusively on the training set using grid search coupled with 5-fold cross-validation. To address class imbalance without inducing data leakage, internal ROSE (Random Over-Sampling Examples) resampling was applied strictly within the cross-validation folds. The raw output probabilities were calibrated using Platt scaling (logistic regression) to ensure clinical reliability. The predictive performance of the classifiers on the independent testing set was primarily evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC), which assessed the model’s ability to classify specific trajectory groups by plotting sensitivity against 1-specificity. Additional evaluation metrics included sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive values (PPV and NPV), F1-score, and balanced accuracy. Furthermore, the model predictions were subsequently interpreted via the Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) framework to quantify the contribution of each feature.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were described as medians (interquartile ranges [IQRs]) for continuous variables with skewed distributions and as numbers (percentages) for categorical variables. Intergroup comparisons for continuous variables were performed using one-way ANOVA or the Kruskal-Wallis test, as appropriate for the data distribution. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. Missing clinical data (Table S2) were imputed using the random forest-based missForest R package to minimize bias and maximize sample size for all analyses.

To visualize survival probabilities and evaluate the unadjusted differences in survival across the four blood glucose trajectory subgroups, Kaplan-Meier survival curves were constructed and compared using log-rank tests. To further investigate the association between different blood glucose trajectory subgroups and patient outcomes, multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were used. Variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated to assess multicollinearity. The proportional hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals; the global test showed no significant violation, indicating the assumption was satisfied. The crude model was unadjusted for covariates, while Model 1 adjusted for demographic variables, comorbidities and disease severity scores. Model 2 further adjusted for laboratory and physiological indicators, and therapeutic interventions.

Subgroup analyses were conducted based on pre-specified factors, including age, sex, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and insulin use, to evaluate the robustness of the associations. Multiplicative interactions between trajectory groups and subgroup variables were tested by incorporating interaction terms into the multivariable Cox models, with P for interaction calculated via likelihood ratio tests. Sensitivity analyses were performed by excluding patients with subarachnoid, subdural, or epidural hemorrhages, and those with pre-existing diabetes. Additionally, a complete case analysis was conducted by restricting the cohort to individuals with no missing data to address potential bias from data imputation. All analyses were performed using R statistical software (version 4.2.2), with a two-sided P value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Identification of optimal blood glucose trajectories

From the MIMIC-IV database, 2,260 adult patients with non-traumatic ICH were enrolled. To capture early changes in blood glucose, we analyzed serial blood glucose measurements taken over the first 72 h of ICU admission (Fig. 1). As shown in Table S3 and Figure S1, while models with 5 or 6 categories improved information criteria (lower AIC/BIC), they fell short of our prespecified minimum class size criterion (≥ 5%). Consequently, we selected the 4-trajectory model as the optimal solution. This model not only satisfied our a priori minimum class size criterion but also demonstrated high classification certainty, with AvePP values for all four categories exceeding 0.80 (well above our predefined 0.70 threshold). The final model identified four distinct trajectories of blood glucose: Trajectory 1 (persistent low), Trajectory 2 (moderate stable), Trajectory 3 (progressive decline), and Trajectory 4 (progressive increase) (Figure S2).

Baseline characteristics of patients

As shown in Table 1, the median age of the enrolled patients was 66.4 years, with 53% male. Significant differences were observed between the four blood glucose trajectory groups in terms of demographic variables, comorbidities, therapeutic interventions, laboratory and physiological indicators, and disease severity scores. Specifically, compared to trajectory 1, patients in trajectory 3 and 4 were older, heavier, and had a higher prevalence of comorbidities, including diabetes, as well as a higher rate of insulin use. Laboratory tests showed higher levels of white blood cell count, potassium, blood glucose, blood urea nitrogen, and creatinine, with prolonged prothrombin time, while hemoglobin levels were lower. In terms of vital signs, patients in trajectory 3 and 4 had higher heart rates, systolic blood pressure, temperature, and SOFA and SAPS II, while diastolic blood pressure was lower. Prediction and Interpretation of blood glucose trajectory

We developed independent XGBoost binary classifiers to predict each specific subtype of blood glucose trajectories in patients with non-traumatic ICH. These models demonstrated robust discriminatory power, with the AUC-ROC for each subtype exceeding 0.7 (Figures S3). The detailed performance metrics for each trajectory-specific predictive model are summarized in Table S5. Furthermore, SHAP analysis was employed to quantify the contribution of each feature to the predictions. The top 10 most important features for each model are presented in Figure S4. Furthermore, the SHAP swarm plot (Figure S5) provides interpretable insights into the specific impact and directionality of key variables across the study population. Notably, a history of diabetes exhibited a positive SHAP value for trajectories 3 and 4, indicating that patients with pre-existing diabetes were more likely to be classified into these two blood glucose trajectory groups.

Association between blood glucose trajectories and mortality

Kaplan-Meier survival curves (Figure S6) showed significant differences in 28-day, 180-day, and 1-year mortality rates among the four groups (log-rank test, P < 0.0001), with trajectory 4 having the lowest survival rate, followed by trajectory 3. Cox proportional hazards regression analysis further confirmed this trend (Table 2). The Schoenfeld residual test indicated that the proportional hazards assumption was not violated. The multicollinearity analysis revealed that the VIFs for all variables in the model were below 10, indicating the absence of multicollinearity(Table S6). In the unadjusted crude model, the hazard ratios (HR) for 28-day mortality were 2.21 (95% CI: 1.76–2.79) for trajectory 2, 3.28 (95% CI: 2.41–4.45) for trajectory 3, and 3.47 (95% CI: 2.55–4.73) for trajectory 4. After adjusting for all selected covariates, including baseline blood glucose levels, the mortality remained significantly higher in trajectories 2–4 compared with trajectory 1, with HR of 1.71 (95% CI: 1.33–2.20), 1.87 (95% CI: 1.25–2.80), and 2.41 (95% CI: 1.64–3.54), respectively. The mortality analysis at 180 days and 1 year showed consistent results (Table 2).

Subgroup analyses

When analyzing potential interactions by pre-specified factors, a significant interaction was observed only in the age subgroup (Fig. 2): in patients aged < 65 years, the effect of blood glucose trajectories on prognosis was more pronounced, though the direction of effect remained consistent with the overall findings. No significant interactions were observed in subgroup analyses by the other selected covariates.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of our primary findings. The associations between glucose trajectories and mortality remained consistent after excluding patients with subarachnoid, subdural, or epidural hemorrhages, or pre-existing diabetes. Furthermore, a complete case analysis restricted to individuals with no missing data yielded similar results (all detailed in Table S7).

Discussion

This study is the first to investigate the prognostic association between early blood glucose dynamics and mortality in critically ill non-traumatic ICH patients. Using serially measured blood glucose values collected during routine ICU care over the first three days of admission, we applied LCMM to identify four distinct blood glucose trajectory subtypes: trajectory 1 (persistent low), trajectory 2 (moderate stable), trajectory 3 (progressive decline), and trajectory 4 (progressive increase). Compared with trajectory 1, trajectories 2, 3, and 4 were significantly associated with higher 28-day, 180-day, and 1-year mortality rates, and these associations remained significant after adjusting for baseline glucose.

Previous studies have consistently shown a close relationship between blood glucose levels and poor prognosis in non-traumatic ICH. For instance, hyperglycemia (serum glucose > 7 mmol/L) is associated with higher in-hospital mortality (51.2% vs. 26.2%, OR = 2.3, 95% CI 1.2–7.6, p = 0.02), even after adjusting for age, hemorrhage volume, and bleeding location [26]. A meta-analysis of 17 studies further demonstrated that hyperglycemia was significantly associated with mortality in patients with primary intracerebral hemorrhage (RR = 2.36, 95% CI 1.79–3.12). Subgroup analysis revealed that hyperglycemia increased both short-term (RR = 3.97, 95% CI 2.13–7.43) and long-term (RR = 1.53, 95% CI 1.14–2.05) mortality risk [10]. However, most prior studies focused on single baseline assessments and therefore failed to capture dynamic glycemic fluctuations. In contrast, our longitudinal approach using only the first 72 h of routine glucose data revealed that patients with adverse trajectories—especially the progressive decline in Trajectory 3 and progressive increase in Trajectory 4—were associated with a higher risk of mortality. Importantly, these 72‑hour trajectories provided prognostic information incremental to baseline glucose, underscoring that dynamic patterns are more informative than a single admission value. Interpretability analysis using XGBoost-SHAP suggested that a history of diabetes may be a primary driver of these volatile glycemic patterns, which also came with elevated markers of systemic stress and disease severity. Furthermore, these abnormal trajectories predicted worse outcomes, particularly in patients under 65. This phenomenon may be explained by the fact that acute stress‑induced hyperglycemia can severely disrupt homeostasis in younger individuals despite their robust baseline physiological reserves.

Our findings are biologically plausible. In patients with non-traumatic ICH, acute stress activates the sympathetic nervous system and hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, releasing catecholamines and cortisol [27, 28] that promote hyperglycemia via hepatic glucose production and peripheral insulin resistance [29, 30]. Under persistent hyperglycemia, the NF-κB pathway upregulates pro-inflammatory cytokines, intensifying perilesional inflammation and secondary brain injury [31, 32]. Concurrently, hyperglycemia increases reactive oxygen species generation through mitochondrial dysfunction and NADPH oxidase [33], leading to endothelial damage, reduced nitric oxide bioavailability, and cerebral vasospasm [34, 35]. Hyperglycemia also disrupts blood–brain barrier integrity by modulating tight junction proteins, promoting cerebral edema [36]. Conversely, a subsequent decline from initial high glucose levels might induce detrimental glycemic variability, which is thought to exacerbate oxidative stress [37, 38]. Furthermore, this reduction could potentially precipitate relative neuroglycopenia, depriving the vulnerable, injured brain of essential energy substrates, particularly during aggressive glycemic correction [39, 40]. These interconnected mechanisms may explain why both trajectory 4 (progressive increase) and trajectory 3 (progressive decline) were associated with the highest mortality, whereas trajectory 1 exhibited the best prognosis.

This study has several limitations. First, residual confounding may remain due to missing radiological parameters (e.g., hematoma volume). Second, requiring a minimum 3‑day ICU stay potentially introduces survivorship bias, as patients with early death or rapid discharge were excluded. Third, while our analysis primarily focused on glucose trajectories, future research should incorporate other dynamic physiological parameters; multi-parameter joint trajectory analysis could better elucidate complex prognostic interactions. Fourth, causality cannot be established from observational data. Fifth, our reliance on a single-center database from the United States may limit generalizability to other healthcare systems.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that early blood glucose trajectories were significantly associated with the prognosis of critically ill non-traumatic ICH patients. Both progressive decline and progressive increase blood glucose trajectories were independently associated with significantly higher mortality risks compared to the persistent low glucose trajectory. These findings may support the prognostic value of blood glucose trajectories in critically ill non-traumatic ICH patients.
