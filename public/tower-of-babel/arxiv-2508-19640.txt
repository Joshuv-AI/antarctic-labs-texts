Optimal Cox regression under federated differential privacy: coefficients and cumulative hazards
Elly K. H. Hung, Yi Yu

Abstract
We study two foundational problems in distributed survival analysis: estimating Cox regression coefficients and cumulative hazard functions, under federated differential privacy constraints, allowing for heterogeneous per-sever sample sizes and privacy budgets. To quantify the fundamental cost of privacy, we derive minimax lower bounds along with matching (up to poly-logarithmic factors) upper bounds. In particular, to estimate the cumulative hazard function, we design a private tree-based algorithm for nonparametric integral estimation. Our results reveal server-level phase transitions between the private and non-private rates, as well as the reduced estimation accuracy from imposing privacy constraints on distributed subsets of data. To address scenarios with partially public information, we also consider a relaxed differential privacy framework and provide a corresponding minimax analysis. To our knowledge, this is the first treatment of partially public data in survival analysis, and it establishes a no-gain in accuracy phenomenon. Finally, we conduct extensive numerical experiments, with an accompanying R package FDPCox , validating our theoretical findings. These experiments also include a fully-interactive algorithm with tighter privacy composition, which demonstrates improved estimation accuracy.

Introduction

Privacy concerns have become increasingly prominent in recent years, fuelled by the widespread collection of personal data and the growing number of incidents involving unauthorised access, misuse and re-identification. These concerns are particularly acute in high-stakes domains such as healthcare, biomedical research, social sciences and revenue management, as datasets in these areas often contain highly sensitive information about individuals. In such settings, ensuring data confidentiality is not only an ethical imperative but also a regulatory requirement
(
European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2016
;
Congress, 1996
, e.g.)
.

A common characteristic of data in the aforementioned domains is the presence of censoring, which typically occurs when follow-up on study subjects is discontinued before the event of interest is observed. The backbone of statistical analysis to address censoring is survival analysis, a well-established area with decades of development, much of which has traditionally assumed an environment where privacy was not a central concern. We provide a literature review on existing results of survival analysis without and with privacy concerns in
Section

1.2
.

A variety of techniques have been explored for preserving data privacy while enabling meaningful statistical analysis. Classical methods such as data anonymisation or restricting access to summary statistics have been shown to be vulnerable to re-identification attacks
(
Narayanan and Shmatikov, 2008
;
Rocher et al., 2019
;
Abowd et al., 2023
, e.g.)
. More recently, modern frameworks, particularly differential privacy
(
Dwork, 2006
;
Dwork et al., 2014
, DP,)
, have gained increasing attention, offering not only mathematically rigorous guarantees but also practical feasibility. Notably, DP was adopted in the 2020 US Census
(
U.S. Census Bureau, 2021
, e.g.)
and has been deployed by other leading organisations to safeguard sensitive data
(
Apple Inc., 2021
, e.g.)
.

Statistical analysis on DP has been soaring in the last few years, spanning a wide spectrum of topics and approaches from theoretical and applied perspectives. As for private survival analysis, despite the broad range of potential applications, it seems to have received little rigorous statistical attention. In this paper, we study two canonical problems: estimating the regression coefficients and the cumulative hazard function of the Cox proportional hazards model, in a distributed data setting and subject to a federated DP constraint, which will be explicitly defined in
Section

2.2
, to reflect the decentralised nature of contemporary data analysis in healthcare
(
Choi et al., 2024
, e.g.)
, financial risk management
(
Dhanawat et al., 2024
, e.g.)
, and many other areas.

1.1
List of contributions

We summarise the contributions of this paper.

•

Firstly, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first paper providing a systematic analysis on differentially private Cox model analysis, with a particular focus on distributed data under heterogeneous privacy constraints. We study both the regression coefficients
\beta_{0}
and the cumulative hazard function
\Lambda_{0}(\cdot)
, establishing matching upper and lower bounds (up to poly-logarithmic factors) on their estimation errors. These results quantify the fundamental cost of federated DP, with the central DP setting as a special case. By addressing this semi-parametric problem, our work lays the foundation for rigorous private survival analysis, making a substantial contribution to the survival analysis literature, while also enriching the privacy literature.

•

Secondly, we propose computationally-efficient and theoretically-optimal algorithms for estimating both
\beta_{0}
and
\Lambda_{0}(\cdot)
. For
\beta_{0}
, we develop a private stochastic gradient descent-based algorithm that carefully handles gradients normalised by
at-risk
sets averages, rather than overall averages, which pose additional technical challenges due to their dependence across all entries in the dataset and their random nature. For
\Lambda_{0}(\cdot)
, we propose a novel private tree-based nonparametric estimator. This method is specifically constructed to circumvent repeated privacy composition in an integral estimation. An immediate byproduct of our results is a private minimax analysis on the survival function, which is especially important in healthcare applications.

•

Thirdly, we address scenarios where part of the the survival data are publicly available, for example when demographic information are known. We consider a relaxed version of DP tailored for such settings. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first rigorous treatment for partially public data in survival data. By establishing matching upper and lower bounds (up to poly-logarithmic factors), we show that the availability of public covariates does not improve estimation accuracy in estimating the Cox regression coefficients. We further provide a discussion of other types of partially public information in survival analysis and their potential impact on private estimation.

•

Lastly, we conduct systematic numerical experiments to evaluate our proposed methods, and release an accompanying
R
package
FDPCox

In particular, we implement a tighter privacy composition scheme with fully interactive mechanisms, demonstrating improved accuracy for the Cox regression coefficients. 1.2
Related works

We provide a literature review focused on the two central themes of this paper: differential privacy, in particular federated DP, and survival analysis.

Differential privacy

Differential privacy has been studied extensively in the statistics literature. In terms of statistical inference tasks, studies have addressed tasks such as multivariate mean estimation
(
Huang et al., 2021
, e.g.)
, density estimation
(
Lalanne et al., 2023a
, e.g.)
, function estimation
(
Hall et al., 2013
, e.g.)
, networks analysis
(
Jiang et al., 2023
, e.g.)
, one-sample
(
Berrett and Butucea, 2020
, e.g.)
and two-sample
(
Kent et al., 2025
, e.g.)
testing, limiting distribution derivation
(
Cai et al., 2023b
, e.g.)
and sub-sampling based techniques
(
Chadha et al., 2024
, e.g.)
, to name but a few. In terms of integration into other statistical areas, DP has been adopted by causal inference
(
Kusner et al., 2016
, e.g.)
, change point analysis
(
Cummings et al., 2019
;
Berrett and Yu, 2021
, e.g.)
, conformal prediction
(
Angelopoulos et al., 2022
, e.g.)
, optimisation
(
Avella-Medina et al., 2023
, e.g.)
, robust statistics
(
Li et al., 2023b
, e.g.)
, transfer learning
(
Auddy et al., 2024
;
Li et al., 2024
, e.g.)
, among many others.

The DP framework has evolved to include a range of variants tailored to address the specific constraints of different applications. Some widely used examples include local DP – only data owners have access to their own data
(
Duchi et al., 2018
;
Acharya et al., 2020
, e.g.)
, central DP – a central data curator is trusted to aggregate the private data
(
Cai et al., 2021
;
Acharya et al., 2021
, e.g.)
, user-level DP – the privacy guarantee holds for all of a user’s records
(
Levy et al., 2021
;
Kent et al., 2024
, e.g.)
, and label DP – only the labels of the dataset need to be private
(
Chaudhuri and Hsu, 2011
;
Zhao et al., 2025
, e.g.)
.

In this paper, we focus on federated DP, where distributed data are subject to different DP constraints at different levels
(
Wei et al., 2019
;
Li et al., 2024
;
Cai et al., 2024c
, e.g.)
. Federated learning is a subfield of distributed learning where the training data are decentralised between multiple clients, which coordinate on a joint learning problem. Rather than sending their raw data, each server sends updates in the form of summary statistics, see e.g.
Kairouz et al., 2021
for a survey. However, it has been shown that user information can be reconstructed even from these updates
(
Wang et al., 2019
;
Zhu et al., 2019
, e.g.)
, highlighting the need for more rigorous privacy guarantees. Federated DP has therefore been considered as a rigorous DP guaranteed federated learning methodology and a means to bridge the privacy/utility gap between the local and central models of DP.

There have been a few proposed definitions of federated DP in the literature, corresponding to variations on the privacy guarantees. Some methods, e.g. those proposed in
Jayaraman et al., 2018
, still rely on a trusted central server to aggregate private information before releasing a DP version of the shared model. Inter-silo record-level DP
(
Lowy and Razaviyayn, 2023
)
requires that each silo’s communications satisfy item-level DP for its data, to allow for a setting where there is no trust between the silos or a central aggregator. The shuffle model of DP
(
Cheu et al., 2019
, e.g.)
can be seen as an intermediate trust model requiring only a trusted shuffler such that the shuffled outputs satisfy central DP. The federated DP definition (
Definition

2
) we adopt in this paper has been considered in
Li et al., 2024
for mean estimation and linear regression,
Xue et al., 2024
and
Cai et al., 2024a
for functional data analysis,
Cai et al., 2024c
for nonparametric regression and
Cai et al., 2024b
for nonparametric hypothesis testing.

Survival analysis without privacy constraints

Survival analysis dates back to at least the 1600’s, with the development of cross-sectional life tables for early demographic research. The Kaplan–Meier estimator
(
Kaplan and Meier, 1958
)
was one of the earliest statistical breakthroughs in survival analysis, providing a nonparametric estimator of the survival function that could accommodate for right-censored data. In the following decades, more methodologies for censored data emerged, including estimators for cumulative hazard functions
(
Nelson, 1972
;
Aalen, 1978
, e.g.)
and models for competing risks
(
Prentice et al., 1978
, e.g.)
, multi-state processes
(
Aalen and Johansen, 1978
, e.g.)
, recurrent events
(
Lawless and Nadeau, 1995
, e.g.)
and more. A range of nonparametric rank tests for censored data were also proposed, including a log-rank test
(
Mantel, 1966
)
, the Gehan–Breslow–Wilcoxon test
(
Gehan, 1965
;
Breslow, 1970
)
, and generalisations by
Peto and Peto, 1972
and
Harrington and Fleming, 1982
.
The applications for survival analysis have expanded beyond healthcare data into diverse areas ranging from manufacturing
(
Meeker and Escobar, 1998
, e.g.)
to labour economics
(
Lancaster, 1990
, e.g.)
to cybersecurity
(
Bradley et al., 2023
, e.g.)
.

Cox’s landmark paper on the proportional hazards model
(
Cox, 1972
)
introduced a semi-parametric framework that models the effect of covariates without needing to specify the baseline hazard function. Its theoretical guarantees were subsequently established in
Cox, 1975
,
Tsiatis, 1981
and
Andersen and Gill, 1982
, among others, as well as extensions to frailty models, which include a latent multiplier on the hazard to model unexplained individual-or group-level heterogeneity
(
Hougaard, 1995
)
. As an alternative to the proportional hazards assumption, the accelerated failure time model is a direct regression of the logarithm of the survival time on the covariates:
Prentice, 1978
formulated linear rank test statistics for the coefficients of such models and
Buckley and James, 1979
introduced an version of the least-squares estimator based on imputing censored observations using the estimated distribution.

Building upon developments in modern statistics, methods for Cox regression have been extended to cost-efficient sampling designs
(
Tao et al., 2020
;
Keret and Gorfine, 2023
, e.g.)
, penalised estimators for the high-dimensional setting
(
Tibshirani, 1997
;
Fan and Li, 2002
;
Bradic et al., 2011
;
Huang et al., 2013
, e.g.)
, as well as more flexible estimators in the form of neural networks
(
Kvamme et al., 2019
;
Katzman et al., 2018
;
Zhong et al., 2022
, e.g.)
.

Of particular relevance to this paper are the methods for distributed datasets, i.e. when the data are stored at multiple locations due to privacy or storage constraints. One of the first methods for distributed Cox regression is the WebDISCO algorithm by
Lu et al., 2015
. There have since been numerous other studies on distributed Cox regression; of these, the main papers with theoretical results for convergence rates include those by
Li et al., 2023a
, who develop an algorithm that only requires each server to make a single file transfer;
Bayle et al., 2025
, who consider the high-dimensional setting and adapt the lasso penalised method from
Jordan et al., 2019
; and
Zhang et al., 2025
who consider how to pool local estimators constructed from sub-sampled data to reduce computational costs.

Private survival analysis

The privacy guarantees of the aforementioned distributed survival methods are, however, not clear, especially for those that require sending summary statistics based on risk sets that consist of only a few data points. More broadly in private survival analysis, one of the first papers to consider privacy in Cox regression is
Yu et al., 2008
, who propose fitting the Cox model to a lower-dimensional projection of the covariates. There are a few works that consider estimation under DP constraints.
Nguyên and Hui, 2017
study discrete-time Cox regression problems and propose several central DP mechanisms based on methods from
Chaudhuri et al., 2011
and
Li et al., 2016a
.
Rahimian et al., 2022
consider a federated setting with a trusted central server injecting noise to satisfy DP constraints.
Niu et al., 2025
develop a method for client-level DP updates for estimating Cox model parameters with neural networks.

Looking beyond regression coefficients to cumulative hazard estimation,
Gondara and Wang, 2020
present a central DP Kaplan–Meier estimator, using similar constructions to DP-histograms, and derive related statistics using the post-processing property of DP.
Veeraragavan et al., 2024
propose a central DP Kaplan–Meier estimator where the sensitivity is controlled by clipping the survival probabilities. These previous works have provided empirical results for their methods.
Egéa and Escobar-Bach, 2025
focus on the case where only the censoring indicators need to satisfy local DP, and propose a minimax optimal estimator for the cumulative hazard function based on the Beran estimator.

In view of these existing results, characterising the fundamental cost of privacy in survival analysis under CDP or FDP constraints remains uncharted.

2
Problem formulation

In short, we consider estimating the regression coefficients and the cumulative hazard function in the Cox proportional hazards model, with a distributed dataset and subject to the federated DP constraint. In this section, we will formalise the problem, provide the necessary background and state the required assumptions.

2.1
Distributed Cox regression models

The Cox proportional hazards model
(
Cox, 1972
)
assumes that, at time
t\in[0,1]
, conditional on the covariate
Z(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the conditional hazard rate of the survival time
\widetilde{T}
is of the form

\lambda(t):=\lambda_{0}(t)\exp\{\beta_{0}^{\top}Z(t)\},

(1)

where
\lambda_{0}(\cdot)
is an unknown baseline hazard function with its cumulative version denoted by
\Lambda_{0}(\cdot):=\int_{0}^{\cdot}\lambda_{0}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s
, and
\beta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an unknown regression coefficient vector. In the right-censored setting, we further consider a censoring time
C
, and let
T:=\min\{\widetilde{T},\,C\}
and
\Delta:=\mathbbm{1}\{\widetilde{T}\leq C\}
. The observed data
\{(T_{s,i},\Delta_{s,i},\{Z_{s,i}(t):\,t\in[0,1]\})\}_{s,i=1}^{S,n_{s}}
are independent and identically distributed copies of the generic triple
(T,\delta,\{Z(t):\,t\in[0,1]\})
. In the distributed setting, we assume that each server
s\in[S]
holds
n_{s}
triples. The statistical inference tasks lie in the estimation of
\beta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\{\Lambda_{0}(t):\,t\in[0,1]\}
.

Following the counting process representation from
Andersen and Gill, 1982
, for
s\in[S]
,
i\in[n_{s}]
and
t\in[0,1]
, define
N_{s,i}(t):=\mathbbm{1}\{T_{s,i}<t,\,\Delta_{s,i}=1\}
,
Y_{s,i}(t):=\mathbbm{1}\{T_{s,i}\geq t\}
and
\mathcal{F}_{t}:=\sigma(\{N_{s,i}(u),Y_{s,i}(u),Z_{s,i}(u)\}_{i=1}^{n_{s}},u\in[0,t])
as the natural filtration up to time
t
. With the counting process notation in hand, for
s\in[S]
, the partial likelihood of the Cox model
(
Cox, 1975
)
based on the dataset in server
s
can be written as

\displaystyle\ell_{s}(\beta):=\ell(\beta;\{T_{s,i},\Delta_{s,i},\{Z_{s,i}(u),\,u\in[0,1]\}\}_{i=1}^{n_{s}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{s}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}\int_{0}^{1}\beta^{\top}Z_{s,i}(t)\,\mathrm{d}N_{s,i}(t)-\frac{1}{n_{s}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}\int_{0}^{1}\log\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n_{s}}Y_{s,j}(t)\exp\left\{\beta^{\top}Z_{s,j}(t)\right\}\right]\,\mathrm{d}N_{s,i}(t).

(2)

The gradient and the Hessian of
\ell(\beta)
are subsequently written as

\dot{\ell}_{s}(\beta):=\frac{\partial\ell_{s}(\beta)}{\partial\beta}=\frac{1}{n_{s}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}\int_{0}^{1}\left\{Z_{s,i}(t)-\bar{Z}_{s}(t,\beta)\right\}\,\mathrm{d}N_{s,i}(t)

(3)

and

\ddot{\ell}_{s}(\beta):=\frac{\partial^{2}\ell_{s}(\beta)}{\partial\beta\partial\beta^{\top}}=\frac{1}{n_{s}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}\int_{0}^{1}V_{s}(t,\beta)\,\mathrm{d}N_{s,i}(t),

(4)

where

S^{(k)}_{s}(t,\beta):=\frac{1}{n_{s}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}Z_{s,i}(t)^{\otimes k}Y_{s,i}(t)\exp\{\beta^{\top}Z_{s,i}(t)\},\,\,k\in\{0,1,2\},\quad\bar{Z}_{s}(t,\beta):=\frac{S^{(1)}_{s}(t,\beta)}{S^{(0)}_{s}(t,\beta)}

and

V_{s}(t,\beta):=\frac{1}{n_{s}S^{(0)}_{s}(t,\beta)}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}Y_{s,i}(t)\exp\{\beta^{\top}Z_{s,i}(t)\}\left\{Z_{s,i}(t)-\bar{Z}_{s}(t,\beta)\right\}^{\otimes 2}=\frac{S^{(2)}_{s}(t,\beta)}{S^{(0)}_{s}(t,\beta)}-\bar{Z}_{s}(t,\beta)^{\otimes 2}.

Remark 1
.

Throughout this paper, we assume the time horizon to be
[0,1]
for simplicity. Extending to general compact time horizon is straightforward, whereas extension to an open time horizon presents substantial challenges.
The compact time horizon reflects the finite interval of real-world studies; in this case, the actual observations would be
(T_{i},\Delta_{i}):=(\min(\tilde{T}_{i},\Delta_{i},1),\mathbbm{1}\{\tilde{T}_{i}\leq\min(1,C_{i})\})
, but the formulation detailed in (
2
), (
3
) and (
4
) is unchanged since the integration is on
[0,1]
.

2.2
Federated differential privacy

At a high level, federated DP is a version of DP tailored towards distributed data, where central DP is considered within each server and only private information is allowed to be communicated across servers. For completeness, we define central and federated DP in Definitions
1
and
2
, respectively.

Definition 1

(Central differential privacy, CDP)
.

For
\epsilon>0
and
\delta\geq 0
, a privacy mechanism
M
is an
(\epsilon,\delta)
-CDP mechanism, if it is a conditional distribution on a space
\mathcal{R}
that satisfies

M(R\in A\mid D)\leq e^{\epsilon}M(R\in A\mid D^{\prime})+\delta,

(5)

for all measurable set
A\in\sigma(\mathcal{R})
, and all pairs of datasets
(D,D^{\prime})
differing by at most one data point, denoted as
D\sim D^{\prime}
.

Definition

1
was originally proposed in
Dwork et al., 2006
and implicitly assumes that there is a trusted central server which aggregates the information to release a privatised output. Since its debut, other variants have been proposed for other types of privacy constraints and communication architecture, including a version of federated DP defined below. A very similar form has been used in
Li et al., 2024
,
Cai et al., 2024c
,
Xue et al., 2024
, and
Cai et al., 2024a
.

Definition 2

(Federated differential privacy, FDP)
.

For
S\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, let
\epsilon_{s}>0
and
\delta_{s}\geq 0
,
s\in[S]
, be privacy parameters. For
K\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, we say that a privacy mechanism
Q:=\{Q_{s}^{(k)}\}_{s,k=1}^{S,K}
satisfies
(\{\epsilon_{s},\delta_{s}\}_{s=1}^{S},K)
-FDP, if for any
s\in[S]
and
k\in[K]
, the data
R_{s}^{(k)}
shared by the server
s
satisfies
(\epsilon_{s},\delta_{s})
-CDP, i.e.

Q_{s}^{(k)}(R_{s}^{(k)}\in A_{s}^{(k)}\mid M^{(k-1)},D_{s}^{(k)})\leq e^{\epsilon_{s}}Q_{s}^{(k)}(R_{s}^{(k)}\in A_{s}^{(k)}\mid M^{(k-1)},(D_{s}^{(k)})^{\prime})+\delta_{s},

for any measurable set
A_{s}^{(k)}
,
M^{(k-1)}:=\cup_{l=1}^{k-1}\cup_{s=1}^{S}R_{s}^{(l)}
, and pairs of datasets
D_{s}^{(k)}\sim(D_{s}^{(k)})^{\prime}
, where for each
s\in[S]
,
\cup_{k=1}^{K}D_{s}^{(k)}
forms a partition of the dataset at server
s
.

Definition

2
specified a
K
-iteration mechanism, which involves independent batches of data at each iteration. At the end of each iteration, private data
M^{(k-1)}
obtained from all servers are allowed to communicate across servers for the next iteration.

In both of the aforementioned definitions, the privacy constraints are quantified by the parameters
\epsilon
and
\delta
. The parameter
\epsilon
controls the indistinguishability of two conditional distributions, conditional on a pair of data differing by at most one data point. The parameter
\delta\in(0,1)
can be interpreted as the probability of privacy leakage, usually taken to be polynomial functions of the sample sizes. The smaller the privacy budgets are (
\epsilon
and/or
\delta
), the more stringent the privacy constraint is, and therefore the harder the statistical inference tasks are.

Remark 2
.

It is important to note that
\{(\epsilon_{s},\delta_{s})\}_{s=1}^{S}
are pre-specified as the privacy constraints, while
K
serves as a tuning parameter of an algorithm and reflects communication constraints across servers. The special case
K=1
corresponds to non-interactive mechanisms, where no between-server interaction is allowed. General
K
cases correspond to a broad class of popular interactive mechanisms, though they do not extend to fully interactive ones.

One common way to quantify the hardness of a statistical inference task is through the minimax risk. For a private statistical inference task, the private minimax risk
(
Duchi et al., 2018
, e.g.)
is formalised via
\mathcal{P}
, the family of data generating distributions, and
\mathcal{Q}
, the collection of all mechanisms satisfying the required DP constraints. The private minimax risk is defined as

\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\inf_{\widehat{\theta}}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{P,Q}\big\{L\big(\theta(P),\widehat{\theta}\big)\big\},

where
\theta(P)
is the quantity of interest,
L(\cdot,\cdot)
is a loss function, the inner infimum is taken over all private estimators, the outer infimum is over all possible privacy mechanisms and the expectation is with respect to both the data generating distributions and the conditional distributions generating the privatised data.

Unless otherwise specified, we focus on the challenging, high-privacy regime where
\epsilon
is upper bounded by an absolute constant, say
\epsilon<1
. While the theoretical guarantees derived in this paper for our proposed methods hold for general
\epsilon
, the minimax lower bounds require this upper bound condition on
\epsilon
.

2.3
Assumptions

In this subsection, we collect all the assumptions needed in the sequel.

Assumption 1

(Model)
.

Assume that
\{(\widetilde{T}_{s,i},C_{s,i},\{Z_{s,i}(t):\,t\in[0,1]\})\}_{s,i=1}^{S,n_{s}}
are mutually independent copies of
(\widetilde{T},C,\{Z(t):\,t\in[0,1]\})
, where the conditional hazard of
\widetilde{T}
given the covariate process
\{Z(t):\,t\in[0,1]\}
is specified in (
1
).

a

(Conditional independence). The event time
\widetilde{T}
and the censoring time
C
are conditionally independent given the covariate process
\{Z(t):\,t\in[0,1]\}
.

b

(Bounded covariate processes). The process
\{Z(t):\,t\in[0,1]\}
is predictable and there exists an absolute constant
C_{Z}>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\|Z(t)\|_{2}\leq C_{Z}\right)=1.

A canonical problem in the Cox model is to estimate the regression coefficients

Theorem 1

We show in

using an application of the van Trees inequality

To better understand this result, we first recall the condition
1

eigenvalue rate leads to an extra

in the squared-
\ell_{2}

Consider a homogeneous setting where
n_{s}=n
and
\epsilon_{s}=\epsilon
, for all
s\in[S]
.

1
leads to the minimax rate of

\frac{d^{2}}{\min\{Sn,\,Sn^{2}\epsilon^{2}/d\}}=\max\left\{\frac{d^{2}}{Sn},\,\frac{d^{3}}{Sn^{2}\epsilon^{2}}\right\},

which can be seen as the maximum of the non-private and federated DP rates, echoing the general observations from the existing privacy literature. The non-private rate characterises the inherent difficulty of estimating a
d
-dimensional regression problem, and the private rate quantifies the cost incurred by a federated DP constraint. The cost of privacy can be seen as

\frac{\mbox{dimension}^{3}}{\mbox{no. of servers}\times\mbox{(server size)}^{2}\times\mbox{(privacy budget)}^{2}};

this form has also appeared in other settings under federated DP, including multivariate mean estimation
(
Li et al., 2024
)
, low-dimensional linear regression
(
Li et al., 2024
)
and function mean estimation
(
Xue et al., 2024
;
Cai et al., 2024a
)
.

Whilst the censoring could be dealt with similar techniques, unlike these works, using a semi-parametric model due to the unspecified hazard poses additional challenges, making

1
a genuinely new contribution not reducible to the existing literature.

Theorem

1
is fundamentally linked to the central DP rate. First, when there is only one server, i.e.
S=1
, the federated DP constraint degenerates to the central DP constraint and the rate becomes

\max\left\{\frac{d^{2}}{n},\,\frac{d^{3}}{n^{2}\epsilon^{2}}\right\},

which aligns with known results for other regression problems under central DP
(
Cai et al., 2021
, e.g.)
, given the normalisation in
1

d^{2}
to
d^{3}
. Second, the denominator in the rate of
Theorem

is the sum of per-server contributions, where each server’s effective contribution is the minimum of its non-private sample size
n_{s}
and its central DP effective sample size
n_{s}^{2}\epsilon_{s}^{2}/d
, reflecting that the central DP constraint is imposed within each server. Allowing for a fully heterogeneous setting, depending on the local sample size and privacy budget, we see that each server exhibits its own phase transition between the private and non-private rates.

3.2
Label differential privacy

So far, we have treated a survival data point as the full triple
(T,\Delta,Z)
, upon which the privacy constraints are imposed. In practice, however, this is not always possible. In some applications, such as healthcare datasets, covariate such as demographic information may already be publicly available
(
Bussone et al., 2020
, e.g.)
, and only the time and censoring indicators require privacy protection. In this subsection, we aim to answer the question:

Does having public covariates lead to an accuracy improvement in estimating the Cox regression coefficients?

To formalise this relaxation, we note the label DP constraint
(
Chaudhuri and Hsu, 2011
)
which is proposed as a relaxation of the
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP constraint in a non-survival setting. We formalise a label-CDP definition tailored for survival data in
Definition

3
.

Definition 3

(Label-CDP)
.

For
\epsilon>0
and
\delta\geq 0
, a privacy mechanism
M
is an
(\epsilon,\delta)
-label-CDP mechanism for survival data, if it is a conditional distribution on a space
\mathcal{R}
that satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{M(\{(T_{i},\Delta_{i},Z_{i})\}_{i\in[n]}\})\in A\mid\{(T_{i},\Delta_{i},Z_{i})\}_{i\in[n]}\})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{\epsilon}\mathbb{P}\{M(\{(T_{i}^{\prime},\Delta_{i}^{\prime},Z_{i})\}_{i\in[n]}\})\in A\mid\{(T_{i}^{\prime},\Delta_{i}^{\prime},Z_{i})\}_{i\in[n]}\})+\delta,

for all measurable set
A\in\sigma(\mathcal{R})
, all possible
\{Z_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
and all possible
\{(T_{i},\Delta_{i},T_{i}^{\prime},\Delta_{i}^{\prime})\}_{i\in[n]}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}\{(T_{i},\Delta_{i})\neq(T_{i}^{\prime},\Delta_{i}^{\prime})\}\leq 1
.

We can consequently define label-federated DP by replacing the inclusion of the central DP (
Definition

4
Optimal estimation of the cumulative hazard function

The Cox regression model, in essence, is a linear regression, where the logarithm of the hazard ratio serves as the response variable. The regression coefficients
\beta_{0}
quantify how different covariates influence the survival time, but in order to fully characterise the distribution of the event time, it is necessary to estimate the baseline hazard function. Recalling the notation in
Section

, conditional on its covariate process

S_{\widetilde{T}}(t)=\exp\left\{-\int_{0}^{t}\exp\{\beta^{\top}_{0}Z(s)\}\,\mathrm{d}\Lambda_{0}(s)\right\}.

(9)

To fully exploit the Cox model, in this section, we investigate the private analysis of the baseline hazard function
\Lambda_{0}(\cdot)

\Lambda_{0}(\cdot)

(
Breslow, 1972
)
. Originally proposed as a discussion contribution to the seminal work
Cox, 1972
, the Breslow estimator is a nonparametric maximum likelihood estimator. It was later extended to the counting process framework in
Andersen and Gill, 1982
, resulting in the form

\widehat{\Lambda}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\mathrm{d}N_{i}(s)}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}Y_{j}(s)\exp\{\widehat{\beta}^{\top}Z_{j}(s)\}},\quad t\in[0,1],

and they further showed that
\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\Lambda}-\Lambda_{0})
converges to a zero-mean Gaussian process and provided a consistent estimator for its covariance function.

[0,1]
into equal-sized intervals and privatise the Breslow estimator of the cumulative hazard within each interval using the Gaussian mechanism. The interval width introduces approximation bias, and is therefore chosen to match the minimum of the non-private and private rates for integral estimation. Since releasing cumulative estimates up to a time point
t\in[0,1]
would otherwise aggregate noise across all preceding intervals, we instead employ a
(\epsilon_{s},\delta_{s})
-CDP binary tree structure. This allows for any cumulative estimator to be reconstructed from only a logarithmic number of privatised interval estimators.

Tree-based private algorithms are previously studied in
Chan et al., 2011

Kulkarni, 2019

Chan et al., 2011
consider counting-based queries under central DP and
Kulkarni, 2019

Our setup has the additional complication where changing a data point can change multiple nodes per tree-level due to the involvement of at-risk sets in the Breslow estimator. In addition, to accommodate for federated learning with heterogeneous data, we adopt a weighted average of the cumulative hazard estimated across the servers, where the weights are based on the the variance of each server’s estimator as in
Algorithm

1

Different from the FDP-Cox algorithm, the privacy mechanism in

4.1
Minimax optimality of the FDP-Breslow algorithm

In this section, we establish the minimax optimality of Algorithm

Proposition 6

Let
\mathcal{H}
be the set of cumulative hazard functions that satisfy
2

. Fix privacy parameters
\{\epsilon_{s},\delta_{s}\}_{s=1}^{S}
such that
\delta_{s}\leq n_{s}\epsilon_{s}^{2}
for all

\mathcal{Q}

(\{(\epsilon_{s},\delta_{s})\}_{s\in[S]},1)

Proposition

6
establishes the minimax near-optimality of the FDP-Breslow estimator proposed in
Algorithm

2

Theorem

5
, this result reveals a two-level phase transition phenomenon. At the within-server level, the per-server contribution exhibits a private-non-private transition. At the global level, in a homogeneous setting, the overall rate transitions between the private and non-private regimes: from
(nS)^{-1/2}
to
(n^{2}S\epsilon^{2})^{-1/2}
.

The proof of
Proposition

6
, deferred to
Section

C.3
, proceeds via the Le Cam lemma
(
Yu, 1997
, e.g.)
combined with data processing inequalities. The data processing inequalities upper bound the total variation distance between two privatised distributions by that of the non-privatised counterparts. The key ingredients are: 1) introducing a pair of distributions close to the original ones to convert approximate DP constraints to the pure versions
(
Karwa and Vadhan, 2017
, e.g.)
, and 2) using the coupling method
(
Acharya et al., 2021
;
Lalanne et al., 2023b
;
Lowy and Razaviyayn, 2023
;
Karwa and Vadhan, 2017
, e.g.)
to provide a central DP version of the total variation data processing inequalities. A similar technique appears in
Cai et al., 2024c
, to provide a minimax lower bound on the pointwise risk of estimating a function subject to federated DP without censoring.

Our setting presents two unique features: the presence of censoring and the focus on the cumulative hazard functions. For the former, we consider identical censoring distributions in the lower bound construction. For the latter, the cumulative hazard function introduces additional subtleties: whereas the total variation distance (or the Kullback–Leibler divergence) between two distributions can be directly characterised in terns of their density functions, the nonlinear transformation to the difference between two cumulative hazard functions imposes difficulties in where the density functions should differ. The details can be found in
Section

C.3
.

6
Conclusions

This paper is the first to systematically study the Cox model under DP constraints. In particular, we establish the minimax rates for estimating both the regression coefficients and the cumulative hazard functions, in a distributed data setting subject to federated DP constraints. By laying the foundation for rigorous private survival analysis, our work also opens up several promising directions for future research. We highlight a few below.

Firstly, in our distributed setting, we assumed that the observations at each server are generated from the same distribution. While the stratified Cox partial likelihood allows for different baseline hazards across the different servers when estimating coefficients, more complicated methods would be needed to estimate heterogeneous regression coefficients.
Li et al., 2016b
propose a transfer learning-based method that uses a
\ell_{2,1}
-norm penalty to try to share the sparsity representation across the source and target. Both transfer learning and variable selection under DP constraints would be interesting directions for further investigation.

Secondly, if we allow for recurrent events, then an individual may hold multiple observations. This setting may need to be studied under user-level differential privacy. A particular challenge would be that some users may hold many more data points than others
(
Cummings et al., 2022
)
. Deriving optimal estimators for censored data under local DP constraints would also be an interesting challenge as the standard non-private estimators use a reweighting based on all the other data to account for censoring. For example, this seems to turn the Nelson–Aalen estimator to be as difficult as a
k
-ary distribution estimation problem, leading to worse than parametric rates.

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\sum_{s=1}^{S}v_{s}W_{s}^{(k)}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]\lesssim\sum_{s=1}^{S}v_{s}^{2}\frac{d\log(1.25/\delta_{s})\log(b_{s})^{2}}{b_{s}^{2}\epsilon_{s}^{2}}\lesssim\frac{\max_{s\in[S]}\{\log(1/\delta_{s})\log(b_{s})^{2}\}}{\sum_{s=1}^{S}\min(b_{s},b_{s}^{2}\epsilon_{s}^{2}/d)}.

Putting these together, we have

\mathbb{E}[\|\beta^{(k+1)}-\beta_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\mid\beta^{(k)}]\lesssim(1-\eta\mu)\|\beta^{(k)}-\beta_{0}\|_{2}+\eta^{2}\frac{\max_{s\in[S]}\{\log(1/\delta_{s})\log(b_{s})^{2}\}}{\sum_{s=1}^{S}\min(b_{s},b_{s}^{2}\epsilon_{s}^{2}/d)},

provided that
\eta<1/L
. By recursion, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\beta^{(K)}-\beta_{0}\|^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(1-\frac{c_{1}\eta}{d}\right)^{K}\|\beta_{0}\|_{2}^{2}+\eta^{2}\frac{\max_{s\in[S]}\{\log(1/\delta_{s})\log(n_{s}/K)^{2}\}}{\sum_{s=1}^{S}\min(b_{s},b_{s}^{2}\epsilon_{s}^{2}/d)}\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\left(1-\frac{c_{1}\eta}{2d}\right)^{k}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(1-\frac{c_{1}\eta}{d}\right)^{K}\|\beta_{0}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{d\eta}{c_{1}}\frac{K\max_{s\in[S]}\{\log(1/\delta_{s})\log(n_{s}/K)^{2}\}}{\sum_{s=1}^{S}\min(n_{s},n_{s}^{2}\epsilon_{s}^{2}/(Kd))}.

Setting the number of iterations to be

K=\frac{\log(d^{2}/\sum_{s=1}^{S}n_{s})}{\log(1-\eta\mu)}\asymp\frac{1}{\eta\mu}\log\left(\sum_{s=1}^{S}n_{s}/d^{2}\right),

the first term is the same order as the non-private rate. Therefore, we have a matching upper bound (up to poly-logarithmic factors) if
\eta\asymp d
.
∎

Lemma 11
.

Suppose that we have
n
observations of
(T_{i},\Delta_{i},Z_{i})\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}P\in\mathcal{P^{\prime}}
, where
\mathcal{P^{\prime}}
is as constructed in the proof of
Proposition

10
. If
R
is the random output of a mechanism that satisfies
(\epsilon,\delta)
-CDP, then

\mathrm{Tr}(I_{R}(\beta))\lesssim\min(n,n^{2}\epsilon^{2}/d).

Proof.

This proof is adapted from the proofs of Theorem 4.1 and Lemma 4.2 of
Cai et al., 2024c
. The first term in the minimum can be obtained by the data processing inequality for Fisher information and (
26
). The second term arises by considering

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[S_{\beta}(D)\mid R=r]

\displaystyle=\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{+},\{0,1\},\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\log f_{\beta}(D)\right)f_{\beta}(D\mid R=r)\,\mathrm{d}D

\displaystyle=\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{+},\{0,1\},\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}}\frac{\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}f_{\beta}(D)}{f_{\beta}(D)}\frac{f_{\beta}(D,r)}{f_{\beta}(R=r)}\,\mathrm{d}D=\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\log f_{\beta}(r)

(27)

which implies
I_{R}(\beta)=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[S_{\beta}(D\mid R)]^{\otimes 2}]
.

By the linearity of expectation, we have that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[S_{\beta}(D\mid R)]^{\otimes 2}])

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbb{E}[S_{\beta}(D\mid R)]^{\otimes 2})]=\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathbb{E}[S_{\beta}(D\mid R)]\|_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[\langle S_{\beta}(D_{i}),\mathbb{E}[S_{\beta}(D\mid R)]\rangle]=:\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}G_{i}

where the third equality is from the fact that for any random variables
A
and
B
, the quantities
A-\mathbb{E}[A\mid B]
and
\mathbb{E}[A\mid B]
are uncorrelated. For
i\in[n]
, let
\breve{G}_{i}:=\langle S_{\beta}(\breve{D}_{i}),\mathbb{E}[S_{\beta}(D\mid R)]\rangle
, where
\breve{D}_{i}
is an independent copy of
D_{i}
and denote
(\cdot)_{+}=\max(0,\cdot)
and
(\cdot)_{-}=-\min(0,\cdot)
. By the
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP constraint, we have that for any
W>0
,

\mathbb{E}(G_{i})_{+}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}((G_{i})_{+}\geq t)\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\mathbb{E}(\breve{G}_{i})_{+}+C_{\epsilon}\epsilon\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}((\breve{G}_{i})_{+}\geq w)\,\mathrm{d}w+W\delta+\int_{W}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}((G_{i})_{+}>w)\,\mathrm{d}w

and similarly that

\mathbb{E}(G_{i})_{-}\geq\mathbb{E}(\breve{G}_{i})_{-}-C_{\epsilon}\epsilon\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}((\breve{G}_{i})_{+}\geq w)\,\mathrm{d}w-W\delta-\int_{W}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}((\breve{G}_{i})_{-}\geq w)\,\mathrm{d}w

Putting the last two equations together, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[G_{i}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[(G_{i})_{+}-(G_{i})_{-}]

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathbb{E}[\breve{G}_{i}]+\epsilon\mathbb{E}|\breve{G}_{i}|+2W\delta+\int_{W}^{\infty}\{\mathbb{P}((G_{i})_{+}\geq w)+\mathbb{P}((\breve{G}_{i})_{-}\geq w)\}\,\mathrm{d}w.

(28)

By independence, we have
\mathbb{E}[\breve{G}_{i}]=0
. For the second term of (
28
), by Jensen’s and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|\check{G}_{i}|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}[S_{\beta}(D)\mid R]^{\top}\mathrm{Var}(S_{\beta}(\breve{D}_{i}))\mathbb{E}[S_{\beta}(D)\mid R]\right]}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}\left(I_{\breve{D}_{i}}(\beta)\right)}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left\|\mathbb{E}[S_{\beta}(D)\mid R]\right\|_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle\asymp\sqrt{1/d}\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}\left(I_{R}(\beta))\right)}

where the last line follows by the construction of
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
and
(
B.4
)
.

To obtain the tail bound for the final term on the right-hand side of (
28
), we use the boundedness assumptions to obtain

\displaystyle\|S_{\beta}(T_{i},\Delta_{i},Z_{i}))\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|Z_{i}\left(\Delta_{i}-\int_{0}^{T_{i}}\lambda_{0}(u)\exp(\beta^{\top}Z_{i}(u))\,\mathrm{d}u\right)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{Z}\left(1+e^{C_{\beta}C_{z}}\Lambda_{0}(1)\right)=:C_{S}.

(29)

By (
29
), we have that

\mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda|G_{i}|)]\leq\exp(\lambda C_{S}^{2}n)

and therefore by a Chernoff bound that

\mathbb{P}(|G_{i}|\geq w)\leq\exp\left(1-\frac{w}{C_{S}^{2}n}\right).

Applying similar arguments for a tail bound on
|(\breve{G}_{i})_{-}|
and taking
W=\log(1/\delta)C_{S}^{2}n
, we have

\int_{W}^{\infty}\left\{\mathbb{P}((G_{i})_{+}\geq w)+\mathbb{P}((G_{i})_{+}\geq w)\right\}\,\mathrm{d}w\lesssim C_{S}^{2}\delta n

By summing over
\mathbb{E}[G_{i}]
over
i\in[n]
, we have

\mathrm{Tr}(I_{R}(\beta))\lesssim n\left(\epsilon\sqrt{1/d}\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}(I_{R}(\beta))}+\log(1/\delta)\delta n\right),

which implies that
\mathrm{Tr}(I_{R}(\beta))\lesssim(n\epsilon)^{2}/d
as long as
\delta\log(1/\delta)\lesssim\epsilon^{2}/d
.
∎

Lemma 12
.

Suppose that we have observations
\{(T_{s,i},\Delta_{s,i},Z_{s,i})_{i=1}^{n_{s}}\}_{s=1}^{S}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}P\in\mathcal{P^{\prime}}

10

is the random output of a mechanism that satisfies

Proof.

We use the arguments in the proof of Theorem 4 of
Xue et al., 2024
, where the assumption of independent batches of data allows us to follow similar steps to the proof of Lemma
11
. By the chain rule for Fisher information, we have

I_{R}(\beta)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{s=1}^{S}I_{R_{s}^{(k)}\mid M^{(k-1)}}(\beta),

(30)

where
M^{(k-1)}:=\cup_{l=1}^{k-1}\cup_{s=1}^{S}R_{s}^{(l)}
. An analogous result to (
B.4
) may be obtained as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[S_{\beta}(D_{s}^{(k)})\mid R^{(k)}_{s}=r,M^{(k-1)}\right]

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}f(x\mid M^{(k-1)})}{f(x\mid M^{(k-1)})}\frac{f(x,R^{(k)}=r\mid M^{(k-1)})}{f(R^{(k)}=r\mid M^{(k-1)})}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{f(R^{(k)}=r\mid M^{(k-1)})}\int f(r\mid x,M^{(k-1)})\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}f(x\mid M^{(k-1)})\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\log f(R^{(k)}=r\mid M^{(k-1)}),

where the first equality is due to the use of independent batches of data. Hence, we have

I_{R^{(k)}_{s}\mid M^{(k-1)}}(\beta)=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}_{R^{(k)}_{s}\mid M^{(k-1)}}\left\{\mathbb{E}\left[S_{\beta}(D_{s}^{(k)})\mid R^{(k)},M^{(k-1)}\right]^{\otimes 2}\right\}\right].

Defining
G_{s,i}^{(k)}:=\left\langle S_{\beta}(D_{s,i}^{(k)}),\mathbb{E}[S_{\beta}(D_{s}^{(k)})\mid R_{s}^{(k)},M^{(k-1)}]\right\rangle
and

\breve{G}_{s,i}^{(k)}:=\left\langle S_{\beta}(\breve{D}_{s,i}^{(k)}),\mathbb{E}[S_{\beta}(D_{s}^{(k)})\mid R_{s}^{(k)},M^{(k-1)}]\right\rangle,

where
\breve{D}_{s,i}^{(k)}
is an independent copy of
\breve{D}_{s,i}^{(k)}
, we obtain the conditional version of (
31

\mathbb{E}[G_{s,i}^{(k)}]\lesssim\mathbb{E}[\breve{G}_{s,i}^{(k)}]+\epsilon\mathbb{E}|\breve{G}_{s,i}^{(k)}|+2W\delta+\int_{W}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}((G_{s,i}^{(k)})_{+}\geq w)+\mathbb{P}((\breve{G}_{s,i}^{(k)})_{-}\geq w)\,\mathrm{d}w,

for any
W>0
; this can be shown using that for random variables
A,B,C
, the variables
A-\mathbb{E}[A\mid B,C]
and
\mathbb{E}[A\mid B,C]
are uncorrelated. By similar arguments and using the bound on

from (
29
), we have

\mathrm{Tr}(I_{R_{s}^{(k)}\mid M^{(k-1)}}(\beta))\lesssim(\epsilon_{s}b_{s}^{(k)})^{2}/d,

provided that
\delta_{s}\log(1/\delta_{s})\lesssim\epsilon_{s}^{2}/d
, where
b_{s}^{(k)}
is the number of observations used by server
s
at iteration

. Since
\{b_{s}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{K}
such that

, we have that
\sum_{k=1}^{K}(b_{s}^{(k)})^{2}\leq n_{s}^{2}
, which implies for all

\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathrm{Tr}(I_{R_{s}^{(k)}\mid M^{(k-1)}}(\beta))\lesssim\min(n_{s},n_{s}^{2}\epsilon_{s}^{2}/d),

where the first term in the minimum is from the the data processing inequality for Fisher information and (
26

∎

Definition 4

(Label federated differential privacy, label-FDP)
.

For

, let
\epsilon_{s}>0
and
\delta_{s}\geq 0
,
s\in[S]

-label CDP, i.e.

X_{s}^{(k)}
and any measurable set
A_{s}^{(k)}
,
M^{(k-1)}:=\cup_{l=1}^{k-1}\cup_{s=1}^{S}R_{s}^{(l)}
, and pairs of dataset labels
Y_{s}^{(k)}\sim(Y_{s}^{(k)})^{\prime}
, where for each
s\in[S]
,
\cup_{k=1}^{K}(X_{s}^{(k)},Y_{s}^{(k)})
forms a partition of the dataset at server
s
.

13

4

We will show how some steps of the proof of

A
and
B

, where
\breve{Y}_{i}
is an independent copy of
Y_{i}\mid X_{i}
. By the
(\epsilon,\delta)
-label DP constraint, we have that for any
W>0

\mathbb{E}(G_{i})_{+}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}((G_{i})_{+}\geq t)\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\mathbb{E}(\breve{G}_{i})_{+}+C_{\epsilon}\epsilon\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}((\breve{G}_{i})_{+}\geq w)\,\mathrm{d}w+W\delta+\int_{W}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}((G_{i})_{+}>w)\,\mathrm{d}w

and similarly that

\mathbb{E}(G_{i})_{-}\geq\mathbb{E}(\breve{G}_{i})_{-}-C_{\epsilon}\epsilon\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}((\breve{G}_{i})_{+}\geq w)\,\mathrm{d}w-W\delta-\int_{W}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}((\breve{G}_{i})_{-}\geq w)\,\mathrm{d}w.

Putting the last two equations together, we have

\mathcal{S}:=\{s\in[S]:\epsilon_{s}^{2}<1/n_{s}\}\text{ and }h^{2}\asymp\frac{1}{\sum_{s=1}^{S}\min(n_{s},n_{s}^{2}\epsilon_{s}^{2})}.

Therefore under the assumption of
\delta_{s}<n_{s}\epsilon_{s}^{2}\,\forall s\in[S]
, we have that
D_{\mathrm{TV}}(RP_{1},RP_{2})\lesssim 1
.

Application of Le Cam’s lemma.

Since
\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|\Lambda_{1}(t)-\Lambda_{2}(t)|=h
, applying Le Cam’s two point method leads to the proposition:

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{\Lambda}\in\mathcal{M}}\sup_{\Lambda_{0}\in\mathcal{H}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|\widehat{\Lambda}(t)-\Lambda_{0}(t)|\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|\Lambda_{1}(t)-\Lambda_{2}(t)\right|\left(1-D_{\mathrm{TV}}(RP_{1},RP_{2})\right)\gtrsim h.

∎

Lemma 22
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be the set of cumulative hazard functions that satisfy

\mathcal{Q}

(\{(\epsilon_{s},\delta_{s})\}_{s\in[S]},K)
-FDP and at each iteration

For

P_{j}^{M^{(K)}}
denote the push-forward of the collection of private transcripts when the data is generated from i.i.d draws from
P_{j}
, and similarly
P_{j}^{R^{(k)}\mid M^{(k-1)}}
for the
k
-th round mechanism conditional on the past transcripts
M^{(k-1)}

By the data-processing inequality, we can upper bound

D_{\mathrm{TV}}(P_{0}^{M^{(K)}},P_{1}^{M^{(K)}})\leq D_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(P_{0}^{\otimes\sum_{s=1}^{S}n_{s}},P_{1}^{\otimes\sum_{s=1}^{S}n_{s}}\right)\leq\left(\frac{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{0},P_{1})}{2}\sum_{s=1}^{S}n_{s}\right)^{1/2}.

Therefore, by a similar approach to the proof of
Proposition

6
, we obtain the non-private lower bound in the lemma.

To account for the private rate, we consider a second bound

D_{\mathrm{TV}}(P_{0}^{M^{(K)}},P_{1}^{M^{(K)}})\leq D_{\mathrm{TV}}(P_{0}^{M^{(K-1)}},P_{1}^{M^{(K-1)}})+\mathbb{E}_{M_{0}^{(K-1)}}\left[D_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mathbb{P}_{0}^{R^{(K)}\mid M^{(K-1)}},\mathbb{P}_{1}^{R^{(K)}\mid M^{(K-1)}}\right)\right].

(55)

Fix any
k\in[K]
and
M^{(k-1)}
, and let

\epsilon_{s}^{{}^{\prime}(k)}:=6b_{s}^{(k)}\epsilon_{s}D_{TV}(P_{0},P_{1})\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\delta_{s}^{{}^{\prime}(k)}:=\exp(\epsilon^{\prime(k)}_{s})b_{s}^{(k)}\delta_{s}D_{TV}(P_{0},P_{1})

where
b_{s}^{(k)}
is the number of observations used by server
s\in[S]
at iteration
k
.
Lemma

23
and Lemma C.4 in
Cai et al., 2024c
imply that for all
s\in[S]
, there exists
\tilde{R}_{0,s}
and
\tilde{R}_{1,s}
such that

D_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(P_{0}^{R_{s}^{(k)}\mid M^{(k-1)}},\tilde{R}_{0,s}\right)\leq\delta_{s}^{\prime(k)},\quad D_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(P_{1}^{R_{s}^{(k)}\mid M^{(k-1)}},\tilde{R}_{1,s}\right)\leq\delta_{s}^{\prime(k)},

and
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\tilde{R}_{0,s},\tilde{R}_{1,s})\leq\epsilon_{s}^{\prime(k)}(\exp(\epsilon_{s}^{\prime(k)})-1)
.

Denote
\tilde{R_{j}}:=\otimes_{s=1}^{S}\tilde{R}_{j,s}
for
j\in\{0,1\}
. The next part of the proof follows similar arguments to the proof of Lemma C.7 in
Cai et al., 2024c
. Due to the independence between
(R_{1}^{(k)},\dots,R_{s}^{(k)})=:R^{(k)}
conditional on
M^{(k-1)}
, by the triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(P_{0}^{R^{(k)}\mid M^{(k-1)}},P_{1}^{R^{(k)}\mid M^{(k-1)}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq D_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(P_{0}^{R^{(k)}\mid M^{(k-1)}},\tilde{R}_{0}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+D_{\mathrm{TV}}(\tilde{R}_{0},\tilde{R}_{1})+D_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\tilde{R}_{1},P_{1}^{R^{(k)}\mid M^{(k-1)}}\right).

From the conditional independence, we have

and likewise for the third term in (
56
). We also have that

D_{\mathrm{TV}}(\tilde{R}_{0},\tilde{R}_{1})\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s=1}^{S}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\tilde{R}_{0,s},\tilde{R}_{1,s})\right)^{1/2}\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\epsilon_{s}(e^{\epsilon_{s}}-1)\right)^{1/2}.

D_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(P_{0}^{R^{(k)}\mid M^{(k-1)}},P_{1}^{R^{(k)}\mid M^{(k-1)}}\right)\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\epsilon_{s}^{\prime(k)}(\exp(\epsilon_{s}^{\prime(k)})-1)\right)^{1/2}+2\sum_{s=1}^{S}\delta_{s}^{\prime(k)}

for all

and

\displaystyle\lesssim KD_{\mathrm{TV}}(P_{0},P_{1})\left(\left(\sum_{s=1}^{S}(n_{s}/K)^{2}\epsilon_{s}^{2}\right)^{1/2}+\sum_{s=1}^{S}\delta_{s}n_{s}/K\right)\lesssim 1.

Lemma 23
.

For all
s\in[S]
and
M^{(k-1)}
,
k\in[K]
, we have

D_{\infty}^{\delta^{\prime}}\left(\mathbb{P}_{0}^{R^{k}_{s}\mid M^{(k-1)}},\mathbb{P}_{1}^{R^{k}_{s}\mid M^{(k-1)}}\right)\leq\epsilon^{\prime}_{s},\quad\epsilon^{\prime}_{s}:=6n_{s}\epsilon_{s}D_{TV}(P_{0},P_{1}),\,\delta^{\prime}_{s}:=e^{\epsilon^{\prime}_{s}}n\delta_{s}D_{TV}(P_{0},P_{1}).

Proof sketch.

The key idea of the proof is to modify Lemma 6.1 of
Karwa and Vadhan, 2017
to be for the private transcript from a server
s\in[S]
on the
k
-th round, conditional on transcripts from the previous rounds. We will follow their notation in the rest of this proof.

We construct the same coupling between
P_{0}
and
P_{1}

Karwa and Vadhan, 2017

H:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}H_{i}
at any server
s\in[S]

,

\displaystyle q_{j,k}(h)

\displaystyle=\int_{x}Q_{k}(E\mid x,M^{(k-1)})\,\mathrm{d}P_{j}(X\mid M^{(k-1)},H_{1}=h_{1},\dots H_{n}=h_{n})

)

Lemma 32

(Corollary of Theorem 1.5 in
