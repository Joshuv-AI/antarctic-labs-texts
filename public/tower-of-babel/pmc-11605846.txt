Can high-sensitivity C reactive protein (hsCRP) be used as a prognostic marker of functional disability after acute ischaemic stroke? A cross-sectional study at a tertiary care centre in Eastern India
Authors: Kotha Vamshikrishnapatel, Ratnadeep Biswas, Vijay Kumar, Vishnu Shankar Ojha
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study was conducted with the aim of assessing the correlation between highly sensitive C reactive protein (hsCRP) levels and indicators of disability and functional impairment in individuals presenting with acute ischaemic stroke (AIS).DesignCross-sectional study.SettingDepartment of General Medicine at the All India Institute of Medical Sciences, Patna, Eastern India from August 2022 to August 2023.ParticipantsThe study population comprised a total of 125 patients aged 18 years or more, presenting within 48 hours of symptom onset or time last known well and having confirmed ischaemic stroke through CT or MRI of the brain. Individuals with a history of prior stroke, other neurological disorders, active infections, known coronary artery disease, chronic kidney disease, chronic liver disease and abnormal blood cell counts were excluded.Primary outcome measuresModified Rankin scale (mRS) and Barthel Index (BI) scores on day 2 and day 5 were the primary outcome measures.ResultsThe hsCRP levels on day 2 and day 5 were significantly positively correlated with the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale scores with Spearman’s rho 0.303 (p=0.001) and 0.386 (p<0.001), respectively. The hsCRP on day 2 exhibited a significant association with mRS scores (p=0.04), indicating disability, but this significance was not sustained on day 5 (p=0.125). A negative correlation was observed between hsCRP and BI scores on both day 2 (Spearman’s rho=−0.265, p=0.003) and day 5 (Spearman’s rho=−0.297, p=0.002). While the hsCRP levels on day 2 did not differ significantly between fatal and non-fatal cases (p=0.110), the hsCRP levels on day 5 were significantly higher in fatal cases (p<0.001).ConclusionsThis study underscores the potential utility of hsCRP as a prognostic indicator in patients with AIS. Elevated hsCRP levels correlated with increased stroke severity and functional disability. Regular assessments of hsCRP in the early phase of AIS could contribute to predicting prognosis and guiding optimal patient management.

Introduction

Strokes are the second most prevalent cause of mortality in the world and the third largest cause of functional disabilities.1 They are characterised by disruption of the blood flow to a specific region of the brain, with two primary categories: ischaemic and haemorrhagic, where ischaemic stroke accounts for 87% of cases.2 3

An acute ischaemic stroke (AIS) is a sudden and severe reduction of blood flow causing a lack of nutrients and oxygen supply to the brain tissue due to blockage or obstruction of vessels supplying the brain. This reduction in blood flow leads to tissue damage because of energy deficit caused by intracellular ionic imbalance and activation of apoptotic enzymes. Once the blood supply is restored, the area surrounding the ischaemic core comes under scrutiny due to inflammation and excitotoxicity. Thus, the pathophysiology of ischaemic stroke involves a complex interplay of factors, including cerebral blood flow disruption, tissue hypoxia and subsequent inflammatory responses. Inflammation, in particular, has emerged as a critical determinant of stroke outcome, contributing to neuronal damage, oedema formation and functional impairment.4

Traditionally, prognostic factors for functional stroke outcomes have encompassed demographic characteristics (age and sex), stroke severity (National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS)) and vascular risk factors (hypertension, hyperlipidaemia, diabetes and smoking). However, while these factors provide valuable information, they often lack sufficient predictive accuracy for individual patients.5 Consequently, there is a pressing need for novel biomarkers to enhance risk stratification and guide therapeutic interventions.

C reactive protein (CRP), a well-established marker of systemic inflammation, has been implicated in various cardiovascular diseases, including stroke.6 However, the standard CRP assay may be limited in its ability to detect subtle inflammatory changes associated with stroke. Highly sensitive CRP (hsCRP) offers superior sensitivity in quantifying low-grade inflammation, making it a potentially promising biomarker for stroke prognosis.7

The role of hsCRP as a marker of inflammation has been explored in patients who had a stroke previously, with some studies reporting its association with recurrent strokes. However, there are only a handful of studies investigating its role in predicting functional disability and overall prognostication in stroke patients. While some investigations have suggested an association between elevated hsCRP levels and adverse outcomes, including functional disability, others have failed to demonstrate such a link.8 9 The heterogeneity in study populations, methodologies and outcome measures may contribute to these discrepancies.

It is hypothesised that hsCRP, as a sensitive marker of inflammation, may reflect the severity of brain injury and the subsequent inflammatory response in patients who had a stroke. By identifying patients at higher risk for poor functional outcomes, hsCRP could potentially aid in early prognostication and tailored treatment strategies. However, the precise role of hsCRP in predicting functional disability following ischaemic stroke remains to be fully elucidated.

Furthermore, several factors can influence hsCRP levels, including age, sex, drug intake, infections and concomitant medical conditions. It is essential to consider these potential confounders when assessing the association between hsCRP and stroke outcomes.

Therefore, this study was conducted with an aim to assess the correlation between hsCRP levels and indicators of disability and functional impairment in individuals presenting with AIS.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a cross-sectional study conducted over a period of 12 months (August 2022 to August 2023) in the Department of General Medicine at the All India Institute of Medical Sciences (AIIMS), Patna, a tertiary care medical centre in Eastern India.

Study participants

Patients who presented to the Emergency Medicine Department experiencing their first episode of AIS and seeking medical attention at AIIMS Patna were included in the study.

Inclusion criteria

Patients aged 18 years or more, presenting within 48 hours of symptom onset or time last known well and having confirmed ischaemic stroke through non-contrast CT (NCCT)/MRI of the brain.

Exclusion criteria

Patients who did not provide consent, those who had a history of prior stroke, those who had other neurological disorders, such as meningitis, head injury, brain abscess, brain tumours, etc, those suffering from other active infections like pneumonia, urinary tract infection, etc, known cases of chronic diseases such as coronary artery disease (CAD), chronic kidney disease (CKD) or chronic liver disease (CLD), and those having abnormal blood cell counts were excluded through detailed history taking, basic blood tests (including complete blood count, liver function test and kidney function test) and chest radiography.

Sample size and sampling technique

Considering the proportion of patients with AIS with high hsCRP levels (>3 mg/L) as 62%,10 a CI of 95%, and 15% precision relative to the expected proportion, a minimum sample size of 105 patients who had a stroke was calculated. A random systematic sampling technique was employed to minimise sampling bias.

Assuming a total estimated population of 355 patients with AIS (based on the previous year’s hospital data) and a required sample size of 105, every third eligible patient (355/105=3.38) presenting with AIS was included in the study during the study period.

Study procedures

Patients diagnosed with AIS, who met inclusion and exclusion criteria, were included after obtaining informed consent. Detailed clinical examinations were conducted to calculate the NIHSS score as a marker of stroke severity on the day of admission.11 Venous blood sampling for hsCRP levels was done on days 2 and 5. The samples were processed using the RX-Suzuka Analyser through an immunoturbidimetric assay at a central core laboratory. Neurological assessments were conducted, and modified Rankin Scale (mRS) scores and Barthel Index (BI) scores were calculated on days 2 and 5 by clinicians who were blinded to the hsCRP levels to avoid observer bias.

Study tools

The mRS is a prevalidated, standard scale ranging from a score of ‘0’ signifying no symptoms at all to a score of ‘6’ signifying death, with higher scores representing worse global disability in patients who had a stroke.12 The BI is a prevalidated, standard ordinal scale that categorises and assesses an individual’s capacity to carry out 10 common activities of daily living, including basic mobility.13 14 The scores range from 0 to 100, with higher scores signifying better functionality.

Statistical analysis

The collected information was entered in Microsoft Excel, and statistical analysis was done using IBM SPSS V.26.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, New York, USA). A descriptive analysis was carried out, and the categorical variables were expressed as proportions, and the continuous variables were reported as median and IQR after checking for normality. The participants were divided into two categories, high and low hsCRP groups, based on the median baseline (day 2) hsCRP levels to compare the demographic characteristics between them using the χ2 test of association. An independent-samples Kruskal-Wallis test was performed to look for any association between hsCRP and mRS scores. Spearman’s rank correlation test was employed to look for a correlation between hsCRP levels with NIHSS scores and BI scores. A p value less than 0.05 was taken as significant. An independent-samples Mann-Whitney U test was performed to determine if hsCRP levels differed between fatal and non-fatal cases.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the study design, conduct, or reporting of this research. We intend to disseminate the key findings of this study to the general public through the local media and open-access publications.

Discussion

Our study aimed to investigate the potential of hsCRP as a prognostic marker for functional outcomes in the early phase of AIS. By analysing data from 125 patients, we observed a significant positive correlation between hsCRP levels and NIHSS scores, as well as a negative correlation with Barthel’s Index on days 2 and 5. Notably, hsCRP levels showed a progressive increase with rising mRS scores, specifically on day 2. These findings suggest that elevated hsCRP levels are associated with increased stroke severity and poorer functional outcomes in the acute phase.

Stroke remains a major global health burden, emphasising the need for accurate prognostic tools. The NIHSS is a widely used scale to assess stroke severity.15 Our study found a significant positive correlation between hsCRP levels and NIHSS scores on days 2 and 5, indicating that higher hsCRP levels are associated with more severe stroke. These findings align with previous research demonstrating the association of hsCRP with stroke severity.16

A key strength of our study lies in demonstrating the potential of hsCRP as a prognostic marker in the very early stages of stroke. This early predictive capability is crucial for timely intervention and improved patient management. By identifying patients with elevated hsCRP levels, clinicians may be able to stratify patients into high-risk groups and implement more intensive monitoring and therapeutic strategies such as aggressive blood pressure control, early initiation of antithrombotic therapy or consideration of novel neuroprotective agents.

While the precise mechanisms underlying this relationship require further elucidation,17 several potential pathways have been proposed. Inflammation, a key component of the stroke pathophysiology, is known to contribute to neuronal damage, oedema formation and blood–brain barrier disruption. According to Pedersen et al, elevated levels of CRP after a stroke may play a role in the increase of the terminal complement complex, a marker of complement system activation.18 However, certain studies have indicated that CRP might not play a contributory role in the inflammatory response.19

Studies conducted by Karlinski et al20 and Topakian et al,21 have reported no discernible association between CRP and early-phase functional outcomes in AIS. This discrepancy may be attributed to the universal application of intravenous thrombolysis treatment in their patient cohorts, as this intervention has been shown to mitigate inflammation by reducing neuronal damage.22 Additionally, the choice of CRP instead of hsCRP in their studies may contribute to the observed disparity, considering the lower sensitivity of CRP as compared with hsCRP as a diagnostic marker for inflammation.

The mRS is a widely used clinical tool designed to evaluate the level of disability or dependence in daily activities among individuals who have experienced a stroke or other neurological conditions. It is often used in clinical settings to assess poststroke functional outcomes and correlate with the patient’s expected recovery and quality of life.12 In our study, a significant association with the mRS was noted at day 2, but this significance was not sustained at day 5. In contrast to these findings, a study conducted by Gu et al,23 demonstrated a positive correlation between hsCRP levels and mRS scores, extending up to day 90. It is possible that we did not observe a statistically significant correlation on day 5 due to the relatively smaller sample size. The hsCRP, being an acute-phase reactant, demonstrates inflammation in the brain, and elevated levels of hsCRP may indicate increasing neuronal damage leading to an increase in cerebral ischaemia. The level of cerebral ischaemia is a key factor associated with the level of functional disability. Furthermore, CRP is also proven to have a role in atherosclerosis, complement system activation and fibrinolytic system inhibition, which can act as a possible contributor to increasing inflammation in the penumbra region of stroke.18 24 This role of CRP can be detrimental to the functional outcome of stroke, and elevated levels of CRP can lead to a poor prognosis in patients with AIS. Similar to our study, various studies report the association of hsCRP with functional outcomes in the early phase of AIS.8 25 26 Consequently, it can be concluded that hsCRP can serve as a marker for predicting functional disability in the early phases of AIS.

Methodological considerations and study limitations

The choice of hsCRP as our primary marker of inflammation was based on its widespread use as a reliable and easily measurable indicator of systemic inflammation, particularly in cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases. Furthermore, the cross-sectional design allowed us to quickly assess the association between hsCRP levels and functional outcomes within the acute phase of stroke, which is a critical window for early intervention and prognostication. Our primary objective was to establish the relevance of early inflammatory response as a prognostic tool during the acute phase.

While hsCRP is a well-established marker of systemic inflammation, it is worth acknowledging certain limitations inherent to its use. First, hsCRP can be influenced by a variety of factors, including age, gender, comorbid conditions and drug intake, which may confound its association with stroke outcomes. Although we attempted to minimise these confounders by excluding patients with significant comorbidities, residual confounding cannot be completely ruled out.

Moreover, the cross-sectional design limits the temporal relationship between hsCRP levels and long-term outcomes. A longitudinal approach would be better suited to ascertain the prognostic value of hsCRP over extended follow-up periods, allowing for the observation of dynamic changes in inflammatory markers and functional outcomes over time. This limitation should be addressed in future studies to validate our findings.

The statistical methods used, including Spearman’s correlation and Kruskal-Wallis tests, are robust for assessing relationships between non-parametric data. However, these methods do not account for potential interactions between multiple covariates that might influence both hsCRP levels and stroke outcomes. More sophisticated multivariate analyses, such as Cox proportional hazards models or multiple regression, may provide additional insights by adjusting for these interactions. Despite this, the use of non-parametric tests was justified in our study, as they are suitable for handling the non-normal distribution of data, particularly in a relatively small sample size. Future research with larger sample sizes should consider these approaches to provide a more comprehensive analysis.

Additionally, our study did not include other inflammatory markers, such as interleukin-6 or tumour necrosis factor-α, which might have provided a more holistic view of the inflammatory response post-AIS. A multibiomarker approach could improve the predictive power for functional outcomes and should be explored in future research.

Lastly, our study focused solely on the early phase of stroke (days 2 and 5). While this allows for early prognostication, it limits our ability to generalise findings to the chronic or rehabilitation phases of AIS, where hsCRP’s relevance may differ. Long-term studies would be beneficial to explore the utility of hsCRP beyond the acute phase.

Conclusion

Elevated levels of hsCRP were observed to exhibit a positive correlation with NIHSS scores, indicating stroke severity. Additionally, hsCRP levels were found to correlate with the mRS and BI scale, reflecting functional disability post-AIS. Therefore, conducting successive assessments of circulating hsCRP, particularly in the early phase of AIS, may prove valuable in predicting prognosis and functional disability in patients with AIS.
