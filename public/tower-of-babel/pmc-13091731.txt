Patient profiles and disease characteristics of the IMSRN cohort: a multicentre study (2021–2025)

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundData of Central Nervous System (CNS) demyelinating disorders from India has been published from limited centres. The Indian Multiple Sclerosis And Allied Demyelinating Disorders Registry and Research Network (IMSRN) is a multicentric database for multiple sclerosis and allied demyelinating disorders in the Indian subcontinent. This study aimed to describe the demographic, clinical, laboratory, treatment, and follow-up details of patients in the IMSRN and summarise the distribution of major disease phenotypes in a real-world cohort.MethodsThis was a prospective, observational, registry-based analysis of patients with CNS demyelinating disorders enrolled in the IMSRN between 16 August 2021 and 25 October 2025. Data were collected in a predefined case record form at recruitment and periodically every six months on a secure database. We performed descriptive and comparative analyses, including temporal trends, treatment patterns, and longitudinal follow-up.FindingsAs of 25th October, 2025, 4976 patients have been recruited including radiologically isolated syndrome (RIS), 15 (0.30%); clinically isolated syndrome (CIS), 200 (4.02%); multiple sclerosis (MS), 2479 (49.82%); neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD), 793 (15.94%); myelin oligodendrocyte antibody associated disease (MOGAD), 698 (14.03%); acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM), 76 (1.53%); chronic relapsing inflammatory optic neuritis (CRION), 34 (0.68%); chronic lymphocytic inflammation with pontine perivascular enhancement responsive to steroids (CLIPPERS), three (0.06%), and others, 562 (11.29%). The mean (SD) age of the entire cohort at enrolment is 34.62 (12.16) years, at disease onset was 29.12 (11.80) years and median (IQR) disease duration at recruitment is 2.79 (0.51–7.22) years; longest for MS. There were 65.33% females and 34.67% males, with a female-to-male ratio of 1.9:1; highest in NMOSD (3.58:1). Most patients are from urban areas and educated. Optic nerve and spinal cord involvement were the dominant areas at first presentation. The median (IQR) number of relapses per patient over follow-up was 2 (1–3) highest in the NMOSD population; dominated by optic nerve and spinal cord symptoms. The median (IQR) EDSS was 2 (1–4.5). Rituximab was the commonest disease modifying therapy (DMT) used. Longitudinal trends reflect improvement in time-to-diagnosis and significant shift in the pattern of DMT use in MS towards oral DMTs and B-cell inhibitors.InterpretationThe paper describes a cohort of patients with MS and allied disorders. The key disease characteristics of the MS population seem similar to those reported in international MS registries. The data adds to the existing literature to ascertain disease patterns, response to treatment, and long-term outcome.FundingThe IMSRN registry is funded by the 10.13039/501100001411Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) vide Grant number 5/4-5/192/NeuroTF/2019-NCD-1.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched major databases, including PubMed, EMBASE, SCOPUS, Google Scholar, and the Cochrane Library using the MeSH terms “multiple sclerosis” OR “neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorders” OR “NMOSD” OR “MOG antibody disease” OR “demyelinating diseases” AND “registry” (last search: 27 August 2025). The search yielded 2780 articles. After removal of duplicates and exclusion of publications not focused on registries, 398 articles were relevant to registries of multiple sclerosis and other demyelinating disorders. Several established registries were identified, including NARCOMS (USA), MSBase (global), OFSEP (France), the Big MS Data Network, and national registries from Denmark, Sweden, Switzerland, Germany, Norway, Italy, Poland, Canada, China, Argentina and Iran, as well as the UK MS Register, Australian MS Longitudinal Study, Spanish REEM, and Czech ReMuS. Regional collaborations such as MENACTRIMS in the Middle East and North Africa also contribute valuable data. Collectively, these registries have provided high-quality real-world evidence on epidemiology, natural history, treatment outcomes, and long-term prognosis of multiple sclerosis and related demyelinating disorders. In contrast, studies from India have been limited to single-centre cohorts of patients with multiple sclerosis, neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder, or MOG antibody associated disease and there is no large prospective data from a nationwide multicentric registry of patients.

Added value of this study

The Indian Multiple Sclerosis and Allied Demyelinating Disorders Registry and Research Network (IMSRN) is a, multicentric, prospective registry for demyelinating disorders in India. It systematically captures demographic, clinical, imaging, laboratory, treatment, and outcome data across a diverse population that has been underrepresented in global research. IMSRN thus provides a prospective dataset from South-East Asia, enabling comparisons with international registries and offering insights into regional disease patterns, diagnostic practices, and therapeutic approaches.

Implications of all the available evidence

Collectively, evidence from global registries demonstrates how systematic, prospective data collection can clarify the natural history of demyelinating disorders, evaluate long-term treatment outcomes, and guide treatment decisions. Until now, this evidence has largely come from Europe, North America, and other high-income regions. The IMSRN fills a gap by providing prospective data from India, a country with unique ethnic, environmental, and socioeconomic contexts. When integrated with existing international networks, these data will enable meaningful cross-population comparisons, highlight regional variations in disease burden and treatment access, and support the development of equitable and contextually relevant health policies.

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS), neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorders (NMOSD), myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody associated disease (MOGAD), acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM) etc., are some of the key central nervous system (CNS) demyelinating disorders. These conditions show substantial heterogeneity in clinical presentation, disease course, and treatment response, making region-specific epidemiological and real-world data essential for guiding diagnosis and management. To date, only limited studies from India have described the clinical features, disease course, and treatment patterns in these patients, and most have been restricted by small sample sizes and short follow-up.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 Consequently, there remains a critical gap in evidence, underscoring the need for large-scale, nationwide data with longitudinal follow-up to more accurately define disease characteristics, guide clinical management, and add to the existing world literature. The Indian Multiple Sclerosis And Allied Demyelinating Disorders Registry and Research Network (IMSRN) is a database for MS and allied demyelinating disorders (https://imsrn.icmr.org.in/).6 The registry was initiated in August 2021 and prospectively collects standardised longitudinal data from 22 collaborating centres across the country, including demographics, clinical phenotypes, relapse details, treatments, laboratory evaluation, biobanking, pregnancy outcomes, and imaging to characterise long-term disease behaviour and outcomes. The present study describes the demographic, clinical, laboratory, treatment characteristics, and follow-up data of patients enrolled in the IMSRN and summarises the relative distribution and key features of major disease phenotypes (MS, NMOSD, MOGAD, and others) recruited until 25 October 2025, thereby providing an overview of real-world data in this cohort.

Methods

The IMSRN is a hospital-based registry that currently includes 22 sites. The sites were selected based upon region, patient volume and presence of a neurologist with a special interest in these disorders. These are tertiary referral institutes situated in the northern, southern, western, and eastern parts of India. Patients of all ages, including both paediatric and adult patients, were eligible for inclusion in the registry. Following informed consent, patients with CNS demyelinating disorders are recruited into the registry. An outline of the registry, data collection methods and follow up has been published previously.1

The current study is a prospective, observational registry-based analysis of patients recruited between 16 August 2021 and 25 October 2025.

The diagnosis of MS was made according to the 2017 revisions of the McDonald criteria.7

NMOSD was diagnosed using the 2015 International Panel for NMO Diagnosis (IPND) criteria.8 MOGAD was diagnosed according to the International MOGAD Panel proposed criteria9; for patients enrolled before the publication of these criteria, the diagnosis was based on compatible clinical and radiological features in conjunction with MOG-IgG seropositivity. ADEM was diagnosed according to the International Pediatric Multiple Sclerosis Study Group (IPMSSG) criteria.10 Across all participating sites, AQP4-IgG and MOG-IgG testing was performed using a fixed cell-based assay (Euroimmun) in accredited laboratories with external quality assurance. Antibody testing data were recorded from serum samples in the registry. Patients who did not meet the diagnostic criteria for NMOSD (seropositive and seronegative), MS, or MOGAD were categorised under “other” demyelinating disorders. The “other” group includes double seronegative myelitis, double seronegative optic neuritis, double seronegative longitudinally extensive transverse myelitis (LETM), seronegative LETM with other CNS involvement, double seronegative opticospinal syndrome, double seronegative brainstem demyelination, tumefactive demyelinating lesions (TDL), and other unclassified CNS demyelinating disorders.

Details of patients’ demographics, family history, clinical disease history, including disease onset, relapses, investigative work-up done, medication use (current and previous), assessment on various scales, disability status, and imaging details are recorded both in a print version of the case record form and online on the IMSRN database portal. Gender is self-reported using a structured questionnaire with response options of male, female, and others. Socioeconomic status was evaluated using Kuppuswamy scale.11 The details are filled as per the standard operating procedure manual outlining all definitions of variables. The anonymised imaging is uploaded in a DICOM format on an OSIRIX enabled database portal (https://www.osirix-viewer.com/), and details on predefined variables of imaging are entered on the database. The clinical diagnosis as recorded is based on the decision of the treating neurologist judged by the clinical features, imaging, and laboratory details using standard diagnostic criteria. Follow-up data is collected every six months or earlier as indicated. Each patient is enquired about the clinical status, medication-related issues, any relapse or disease worsening, pregnancy, and assessment on various scales, some being mandatory and others optional for a six-month or 12-month follow-up. Follow-up details on imaging, if done in the interim, are also recorded. The study is monitored by a task force committee of the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR), which periodically reviews the progress of the registry (Supplementary Appendix 1).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were performed and are presented as mean ± SD or median (interquartile range) for continuous variables, and as count with percentages for categorical variables. Group comparisons were performed using the Student's t test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, and the chi-square test for categorical variables. Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of continuous variables. Temporal trend analyses were performed by stratifying patients into four symptom onset-year cohorts: before 2010, 2010–2014, 2015–2019, and after 2019. Diagnostic delay was assessed by calculating the duration (in days) between first symptom onset and confirmed diagnosis for each cohort, using Kaplan–Meier curves. DMT utilisation patterns were evaluated across the same cohorts by computing the proportion of patients receiving each DMT. These analyses were descriptive in nature. Longitudinal follow-up data were analysed in patients with MS to assess disease stability using EDSS scores, and within-patient changes between baseline and 12- and 24-month follow-up were evaluated using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. All statistical analyses were performed using STATA version 14, with a two-sided alpha level of <0.05 considered statistically significant.

Following informed consent, patients with CNS demyelinating disorders are recruited into the registry.

The various key demographic variables are outlined in Tables 1 and 2. The mean (SD), median (range) age of the cohort at recruitment is 34.62 (12.16) and 33 (3.0–85.0) years, respectively (Table 1). 300 patients (6.11%) were aged <18 years. There are 3251 female participants and 1725 male participants, with an overall female-to-male ratio of 1.9:1. NMOSD has the highest female-to-male ratio of 3.58:1, and MOGAD is equally distributed between two sexes (Table 2). Majority were urban residents and in the higher economic strata, especially among the MS cohort. Only 36.71% patients were involved in regular exercise. Majority of patients had never smoked, vaped, consumed tobacco or taken alcohol. Most were non-vegetarian and consumed a mixed diet. Among available data 61.19% patients were married and 2647 patients reported having children.

Family history of neuroimmunological illness was observed in 1.65% of patients and was highest in the MS population (2.23%). Comorbidities included asthma (3.11%), hypertension (7.83%), diabetes (6.77%), epilepsy (2.50%), psychiatric comorbidity (2.57%), cardiac illness (0.69%) and stroke (0.59%). Past history of infection reported were measles (7.66%), mumps (3.76%), any immunological illness (1.5%), and COVID-19 (13.2%). Many of the patients were not aware of their childhood vaccination status. Where vaccination history was certain, it was reported in majority (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2). A limited number of patients (292; 6.16%) were taking alternative medication.

The disease characteristics are outlined in Tables 1 and 2. The mean (SD) age of disease onset in the current cohort is 29.12 (11.80) years (Table 1). The median (IQR) duration of disease at the time of recruitment was 2.79 (0.51–7.22) years (Table 1) and was the longest for MS; 4.91 (1.82–9.51) years (Table 2). The median (IQR) duration between disease onset and diagnosis (calculated in days and from time of onset of symptoms to a formal diagnosis given to the patient) was 85 (20–588) days and was highest in patients with MS followed by CRION, and shorter for ADEM, MOGAD and NMOSD (Table 2). Overall, the two commonly involved systems at presentation were optic nerves and spinal cord across all major disease phenotypes other than ADEM where supratentorial involvement was the most common. Multifocal presentation at onset was observed in 622 patients. The median (IQR) relapse rate was 2 (2–4) and similar across all groups and the most common region of involvement in relapses was spinal cord followed by the visual pathway (Table 2). The median (IQR) EDSS of the overall cohort was 2 (1–4.5) respectively, and was higher for NMOSD and ADEM.

There was a clear reduction in the time from symptom onset to diagnosis across successive onset-year cohorts for all disease groups (Fig. 1). Among patients with onset prior to 2010, the median (IQR) onset-to-diagnosis duration was longest for all phenotypes. This progressively decreased for those with onset between 2010 and 2014. Laboratory characteristics are outlined in the Table 2 and Supplementary Table S3. Among available data, antinuclear antibody (ANA) was positive in 351 (11.65%) patients, extractable nuclear antigen (ENA) profile was positive in 86 (9.41%), and the majority were seronegative for any concomitant antibody. Among the NMOSD cohort, aquaporin-4 IgG was positive in 69.8%. Among 4217 patients who underwent the test, MOG IgG was positive in 16.29% patients. It was positive in all patients diagnosed with MOGAD, and two patients with MS. One NMOSD patient was double seropositive for AQP4-IgG and MOG-IgG; given that testing was performed using fixed cell-based assays as part of routine clinical care, this may represent a rare double positivity or a potential false-positive MOG-IgG result.

In the seronegative NMOSD group, all patient tested negative for both AQP4-IgG and MOG-IgG. In the ADEM group, most patients underwent AQP4-IgG and MOG-IgG testing, with all tested patients being negative.

Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) was tested in 3317 (74.12%) patients, and the details are outlined in Table 2 and Supplementary Table S3. Highest prevalence of positive OCB (oligoclonal band) test was present in patients with MS, although it was observed across the spectrum (Table 2).

A total of 8709 MRI scans have been contributed to the central database from participating centres. Imaging frequency is determined according to each site's study protocol. Both baseline and follow-up scans were uploaded via a secure portal, and all images were anonymised and coded sequentially for each patient based on the order of MRI acquisition.

The various current and previous DMTs are outlined in Table 3 and Supplementary Table S4. Among 3768 patients where data was available, Rituximab was the most common DMT in use or previously prescribed, followed by dimethyl fumarate (DMF). Azathioprine and mycophenolate mofetil were the most common oral immunosuppressants in use among patients with non-MS phenotypes. Some of the patients were also on maintenance prednisolone as indicated at the time of recruitment. Data of 4560 follow-ups is available across various time points (6–54 months), collected every six months. Data for the disease course was available for 4423 patients. The majority of patients were stable (63.05%) or had improved (29.93%), and a small proportion (7.02%) worsened (Tables 1 and 2). “Improved” was defined as a decrease in EDSS by ≥ 0.5 points along with the patient's subjective improvement in functional status (self-care or ambulation); “Stable” was defined as no meaningful change in EDSS and clinical status; and “Worsening” was defined as an increase in EDSS by ≥ 0.5 points along with subjective worsening in functional status. Most patients (91.53%) continued on previous DMTs, and in a few, it was either stopped, switched or temporarily withheld due to various reasons.

Among available data (n = 3613), 2566 (71%) patients were in clinical no evidence of disease activity (NEDA) based on the absence of relapses and any disability worsening. Infections were observed in 160 (3.79%) patients in the cohort as per available data (n = 4226) at last follow-up. Among MS patients, the highest infection rate was observed at 24 months (Supplementary Figure S2). Among 64 MS patients who developed infections while on disease-modifying therapies (DMTs), most were receiving rituximab (32.25%; n = 20), followed by dimethyl fumarate (18.75%; n = 11). A smaller proportion of infections occurred in patients on interferon beta 1a (3.12%; n = 2), azathioprine (1.56%; n = 1), teriflunomide (1.56%; n = 1), and natalizumab (1.56%; n = 1).

Relapse proportion in MS patients remained low across follow-up visits (Supplementary Figure S3). EDSS status among MS patients showed overall stability, with a greater proportion demonstrating improvement than worsening at each follow-up (Supplementary Figure S4). EDSS score in PPMS and SPMS showed no significant difference while in RRMS, improvement in disability was observed in disability assessment at 12 and 24 months (Supplementary Figure S5).

We compared some key characteristics of patients with MS versus NMOSD and MOGAD (Supplementary Table S5). Mean age at onset differed significantly across groups, being highest in NMOSD (30.85 [SD 12.61] years), followed by MOGAD (28.50 [14.57] years) and MS (27.87 [9.35] years; p < 0.001). The median duration between onset and diagnosis was longest in MS compared with NMOSD and MOGAD with a much higher female predominance in NMOSD. CSF-restricted oligoclonal bands were more frequent in MS, optic pathway involvement was more frequent in MOGAD and median EDSS was higher in NMOSD.

Many international MS registries have been collecting data prospectively, including NARCOMS (USA), the Swedish MS registry, the Danish MS registry, Italian MS registry, OFSEP (France), UK MS Register, MSBase, German, Polish, Swiss, MENACTRIMS, Norwegian MS registries and the newly established Big MS database.12, 13, 14 The majority of published data are available for MS. We compared key demographic and clinical indicators with the IMSRN MS cohort (Supplementary Table S6). The predominant disease phenotype across registries is RRMS (IMSRN: 82.6%; Danish: 78%; Swedish: 62%; Italian: 71%; MSBase: 81.5%), with a similar female predominance (IMSRN F:M ratio 2:1; range 2–4:1 across registries). Age at onset was slightly younger in the IMSRN cohort (median 27.9 years) compared with Danish (35.6 years), Swedish (34.4 years), Italian (32.5 years), and MSBase (31.4 years) cohorts. Disease duration was shorter in IMSRN (median 5.48 years for MS) relative to long-standing registries (e.g., UK 14.5 years, MSBase 12.4 years), reflecting the relatively recent establishment of the registry. Time from onset to diagnosis was comparable or shorter in IMSRN (median 0.55 years) versus some European cohorts (e.g., MSBase 1.73 years). Follow-up duration was also shorter in IMSRN when compared with established registries with >10 years of follow-up. Overall, demographic patterns, clinical characteristics, and disease phenotypes in IMSRN are comparable to international registries, while the shorter follow-up reflects early-phase registry maturity.

Discussion

IMSRN is an Indian multicentric registry to prospectively collect information about various facets of the common CNS demyelinating disorders, including demographics, clinical profile, laboratory data, imaging and follow-up.6 Registries are important in understanding large demographic datasets, real-world disease behaviour, long-term outcomes and therapeutic responses.2,12, 13, 14 It provides an opportunity for observing the similarities and differences between disease characteristics worldwide.

The incidence, prevalence, possible risk factors and disease behaviour of these disorders may vary across the globe. MS, the most common among the primary demyelinating disorders, is also reflected in the present registry with proportionately much higher numbers, although the exact incidence and prevalence are not well known from India.1 As per the last Atlas of MS, even though the disease has increased over time, a gap regarding understanding the incidence and prevalence of MS continues to exist.14, 15, 16

The higher relative frequency of NMOSD and MOGAD observed in our cohort is consistent with prior epidemiological data from Asia. Although MS remains the most prevalent demyelinating disorder across Asian populations, large population-based studies have shown that the relative proportion of NMOSD is higher in Asia than in Western countries. In East Asia, NMOSD has a higher prevalence (4/100,000) than in predominantly Caucasian populations (1–1.5/100,000), and MOGAD constitutes a substantial proportion of cases previously classified as antibody-negative NMOSD or isolated optic neuritis, including cohorts from Japan, China, Sri Lanka, and India.17 Population-based data from Singapore show prevalence rates of MS (6.6/100,000), AQP4-positive NMOSD (3.8/100,000), and MOGAD (1.3/100,000), indicating that NMOSD and MOGAD together approach the prevalence of MS, in contrast to Western populations.18

The female predominance and age of onset in the present cohort are consistent with published data worldwide. In our NMOSD cohort, the male-to-female ratio was 1:3.58. Consistent with prior Asian data, this represents a less extreme female predominance than the 9:1 ratio typically reported in Western AQP4-positive NMOSD, with Asian population-based studies reporting female:male ratios ranging from 2:1 to 6:1 in Japan, Korea, China, and Taiwan, and even lower ratios in some South Asian cohorts.17 We observed longer delays in diagnosis of MS compared to other disorders. This observation has also been reported in the past and is likely multifactorial with patient, physician and investigative delays being responsible.15,16,19,20 In a large survey conducted for the MS Atlas of MSIF (Multiple Sclerosis International Federation), at least one major barrier for diagnostic delay was reported by majority of coordinators from 107 countries.15 Since NMOSD and MOGAD present with more severe and acute symptoms, and serological tests have improved sensitivity of diagnosis, the diagnostic time delay is probably shorter. However, a marked improvement in diagnostic timelines across successive onset-year cohorts for all disease groups was observed in the IMSRN registry, with steadily reducing delays after 2010 likely reflecting greater clinical awareness, availability of more trained professionals, earlier and wider availability of MRI and antibody testing, more efficient referral pathways, and simplification of diagnostic criteria; collectively enabling faster diagnosis and treatment initiation.

A study on optic neuritis, however, reported that cost was an impediment to timely diagnosis and management.21 MS may present with mild symptoms, which may be non-specific, and patients may visit non-neurologists for symptomatic relief, leading to a delay in diagnosis, although revision of diagnostic criteria has possibly led to an earlier diagnosis by experts.20,22 A recent extensive report discusses the various facets of timely evaluation, diagnosis and management for patients with MS, NMOSD and MOGAD.22 There is an unmet need for improving this gap, which is likely to be improved with public awareness, education, training of health care professionals, and improvement of resources.15,20,22

A higher socioeconomic status (SE), education level, and lifestyle may affect the incidence and prevalence of MS, although the exact mechanisms are not certain.1,23 This is also reflected in the higher prevalence of MS in developed countries, as was observed in the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2016 study, suggesting a strong relationship between MS and socio-demographic index (SDI).24 The IMSRN data also reflected a higher SE status in the MS cohort. We used a specific SE scale to assess socioeconomic status, which has its inherent limitations.2,25 The increasing burden of MS globally may be a reflection of changing epidemiology and lifestyle, which seem relevant to India as well. Socioeconomic status may also drive inequitable care, limited access to treatments, increase in comorbidities, and suboptimal patient outcomes.1,22,26,27 A recent study also observed a relationship between higher mortality and low socioeconomic status amongst patients with MS.26

Differences in educational level and socioeconomic status across residential settings may influence healthcare-seeking behaviour and referral patterns and could therefore act as potential confounders in this registry-based analysis.

Employment status was assessed in adults 18 years and above. Exact observations are limited due to a significant number of subjects being homemakers or still pursuing education and not in formal employment. A large number of patients were employed, but a proportion were previously employed for which we didn't collect further information about specific reasons that led to unemployment. Many factors may influence employment in these disorders, including disease type, severity, disability, fatigue, coping mechanisms, psychosocial causes and silent disability, etc.26, 27, 28, 29, 30 Comorbidities could exist among patients with demyelinating disorders as observed in the present study. These could affect diagnostic delays, cognition, mortality, and quality of life.31,32 A recent study found significant psychiatric comorbidities in patients with NMOSD,33 and another study suggested the influence of comorbidity on both the occurrence and outcome of optic neuritis among patients with NMOSD and MOGAD.34 Population and longitudinal studies suggest direct and indirect association of MS with the presence of disorders like cancer, ischaemic heart disease, but an uncertainty on stroke.31 The observed pattern is likely multifactorial, potentially reflecting underlying genetic susceptibility, while also being influenced by conventional risk factors such as smoking and hypertension, together with the growing burden of non-communicable diseases; although the occurrence of MS in the young population weakens the conventional risk factor theory, as observed in the present study, with only a small proportion of patients having them.

The majority of patients had mild disability, although there was a range of disability depending upon the disease severity, duration and type of illness. Although follow-up is not too long in the present cohort, the majority of patients remained stable. Early diagnosis, DMT exposure and timely transition to more effective agents likely contribute to the reduction of cumulative disability presently when compared to historical cohorts.35 Among the available data of current DMT use in the population, both immunomodulators and immunosuppressive therapy use were observed across the spectrum of drugs available. Rituximab was the most commonly used DMT, followed by dimethyl fumarate, azathioprine and mycophenolate mofetil.36,37 Although currently used for treatment of non-MS phenotypes, azathioprine and other immunosuppressive medications have been used to treat MS due to the high cost of DMTs.38,39 With the availability of low-cost generic formulations of medications like dimethyl fumarate and teriflunomide, access to treatment has significantly improved over the past few years, even though cost may still be a limiting factor for treatment for patients.40 Our analysis of first-line DMT use trends in MS over time demonstrates a clear shift from interferon-based therapies toward oral agents, particularly dimethyl fumarate, which has seen a marked rise in use since 2015. A parallel increase in rituximab use is also observed, reflecting its growing acceptance as a cost-effective and highly efficacious therapy. Overall, it reflects a trend towards more accessible and convenient therapies driven by the introduction of affordable generics and biosimilars in the country. Other highly effective medications, including natalizumab, ocrelizumab, and ofatumumab, were used in a limited number of patients. Ocrelizumab has recently been approved for use in India and expands the availability of anti-CD20 medications. Complement inhibitors are currently unavailable for the treatment of NMOSD. Cost is a major limiting factor for treatment with these medications. Rituximab is cost-effective, has a long interdosing interval and an acceptable safety profile, making it a frequent choice of medication for MS patients, both for induction or switching therapy in India and also in other countries.36,37 Cheaper Rituximab biosimilars have been available in the country for the past few years, providing an effective and more affordable treatment option. Due to its worldwide use and efficacy, rituximab has now also been included in the essential medication list of the WHO for the treatment of MS in 2024.41

Strengths of this study is the prospective and standardised data collection with longitudinal follow-up and country-wide representation, enabling to build the first large database of these disorders.

Limitations of the registry are the missing data on some elements. There may be some variability in data collection, although we have ensured standardised data collection method is followed by all sites. As this is a hospital-based, referral centre registry and not a population-based cohort, true incidence and prevalence may not be reliably estimated. Treatment strategies and availability of therapies have also evolved over the study period, which may have influenced observed treatment patterns and outcomes.

Further analysis from the IMSRN registry will help understand specific disease phenotypes, pregnancy outcomes, imaging patterns and biomarkers. The IMSRN also aims to expand its long-term goal towards population-based estimates of incidence, prevalence and disease characteristics, as well as patient-reported-outcomes to enable more detailed longitudinal, phenotype-specific, and treatment-outcome analyses.

In conclusion, data collected from IMSRN presents a unique opportunity to study disease characteristics and longitudinal follow-up of a wide spectrum of CNS demyelinating disorders, including MS, NMOSD and MOGAD. The comparison of Indian data to that reported from other countries8,9,42,43 suggests that the core elements do seem to be quite similar and presents an opportunity to understand potential disease characteristics that could be related to various factors. Further analysis of the IMSRN database on individual disorders and multiple facets will enhance our understanding and foster patient care.
