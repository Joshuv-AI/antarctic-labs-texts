An Accessible Algorithm for the Quick and Precise Interpretation of Atrioventricular Blocks in Electrocardiograms
Authors: Ali A Ashkanani, Ali H Ashkanani, Mohammad F Alkandari, Malak A Ashkanani, Maraheb M Alkhalidi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe study investigated an innovative algorithm to improve the diagnosis of different atrioventricular (AV) block types and to improve patient treatment by expanding knowledge in the field of accurate AV‐block identification. Implementing an innovative algorithm holds great potential to assist junior healthcare professionals and those with less expertise in electrocardiogram (ECG) interpretation.MethodsIn this quantitative study, 40 junior doctors and medical students were randomly assigned to a training group (n = 20) and a control group (n = 20). The training group received a 1‐h teaching session on the algorithm and subsequently interpreted standardized ECGs for AV‐block diagnosis. Data were analyzed using SPSS software. Diagnostic accuracy between groups was compared using an independent t‐test; effect sizes were assessed using Cohen's d. A p value of < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.ResultsThe implementation of the novel ECG interpretation method improved the accuracy of diagnosing AV blocks owing to its reliable and practical application, high accuracy, and impact on the grouped beating in AV‐block diagnosis.ConclusionsThe suggested algorithm can improve AV‐block detection in clinical practice owing to its high accuracy and structured approach. The research findings will enhance the existing knowledge base on the diagnosis of AV block and positively impact overall patient treatment.Keywords: algorithm, atrioventricular, AV blocks, electrocardiogram, idioventricular, Wenckebach

Introduction

Electrocardiography (ECG) is a diagnostic tool used in healthcare to provide heart‐related essential information, including heart rhythm and coronary artery status. It is noninvasive in nature and a quick utility in providing various clues regarding the electrical activity and structure of the heart. Hence, it is one of the most prescribed investigations in the emergency department (ED) (Zègre‐Hemsey et al. 2016). An ECG informs heart functioning and helps quickly detect and monitor heart problems, particularly atrioventricular (AV) blocks, thereby assisting in clinical decision‐making to save lives. AV block is an impairment or blockage of the conduction system in the heart that results in a disruption of electrical transmission from the atria to the ventricles. A normal ECG typically shows that each P‐wave is followed by a QRS complex with a fixed PR interval. This typical pattern is lost when there is a disruption in AV transmission. Complexities can arise, often leading to substantial challenges in the accurate interpretation of such ECGs (Acosta Velez et al. 2022).

AV blocks are classified according to the blockage degree and include first degree, second degree (types I and II), and third‐degree AV blocks. In some individuals, AV blocks may be asymptomatic, whereas in others, they may be life‐threatening. As disease occurrence is relatively low, large population‐based studies examining this phenomenon are lacking (Kashou, Goyal, et al. 2023). Accurate AV block diagnosis is challenging, particularly for junior doctors who may struggle to recognize the urgency of specific diagnoses. In most cases, AV blocks can mimic other cardiac conditions on ECG, such as myocardial infarction, ischemia, hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy, drug‐induced arrhythmias (e.g., amiodarone and beta‐blockers), and electrolyte imbalances, or may even present as benign findings in some athletes (Mitchell 2023).

One source of confusion associated with AV block diagnosis using ECG is that AV blocks may cause grouped beatings, repetitive patterns of heart signals visualized on an ECG that resemble arrhythmia (Chuang et al. 2021). They consist of two or more beats owing to intermittent AV conduction. Hence, they are largely associated in particular with the Wenkebach AV block, the second‐degree type I AV block (Chuang et al. 2021). The differentiation between the second‐degree AV block types, particularly in the absence of grouped beatings, may be achieved by distinguishing between Mobitz type I (Wenckebach) and Mobitz type II, as their prognostic and management implications differ significantly. For instance, Mobitz type II AV block is a more serious condition requiring urgent intervention as it carries a higher risk of progression to complete heart block and sudden cardiac events (Kusumoto et al. 2019). In contrast, Mobitz type I (Wenckebach) block arising at the supra‐Hisian (AV nodal) level is less critical in terms of hemodynamic compromise (Surawicz and Knilans 2019). The most effective way to differentiate between these subtypes is through QRS complex morphology analysis of lead V1 in the ECG. Wide QRS complex indicates likeliness for a Mobitz type II block. This condition is characterized by infranodal pathology that requires immediate pacing. Conversely, a narrow QRS complex more likely indicates a Mobitz type I block, characterized by typical benign AV nodal involvement. Complete heart block (third‐degree AV block) is a life‐threatening emergency requiring immediate recognition and intervention.

Moreover, further classification is necessary as the etiology and management strategies differ depending on whether the escape rhythm is junctional or idioventricular. The junctional escape rhythm that originates from the AV junction is characterized by a narrow QRS complex and a heart rate of 40–60 bpm. This heart rate indicates a relatively stable conduction block. Conversely, the ventricular escape rhythm originating from the ventricles presents with a wide QRS complex and a heart rate of < 40 bpm. Similarly, this heart rate suggests distal conduction failure with a higher risk of cardiac arrest (Kusumoto et al. 2019). Therefore, clinicians must assess QRS morphology and ventricular rate to distinguish between these rhythms and stratify urgency accordingly. For instance, bradycardia due to idioventricular escape rhythm necessitates immediate pacing, whereas junctional escape rhythms may allow for temporary pharmacological management in stable patients.

Taken together, these phenomena and their various differentials increase the complexity of precisely diagnosing AV blocks. Therefore, there is a need to refine the diagnostic accuracy of ECG interpretation so that clinicians can rapidly and precisely identify cardiac conditions and ensure timely, appropriate intervention for patients with second‐degree and complete AV blocks. The current study aims to introduce a novel algorithm that offers a systematic approach to accurately and quickly diagnose various types of AV blocks. Junior healthcare professionals and those lacking experience in ECG interpretation may find this algorithm highly useful and beneficial. Our findings contribute to the knowledge base for accurate AV block diagnosis.

Methods

“Type I” and “Type II” second‐degree AV block have been used throughout this manuscript to refer to the physiological classification of conduction behavior described by Mobitz: progressive PR prolongation until a non‐conducted P wave occurs (Type I/Wenckebach) versus a fixed PR interval with sudden conduction failure (Type II). This physiological classification differs from the anatomical localization of the block, which concerns whether conduction failure occurs at the AV node or below it.

Application of the Algorithm

We developed an algorithm to facilitate the diagnosis of various types of AV block/dissociation. The algorithm was divided into two pathways based on the presence or absence of grouped beating on the rhythm strip. One pathway is followed when grouped beating is present, whereas the other is followed when grouped beating is absent. The algorithm was intentionally designed as a targeted diagnostic tool for clinicians who have already identified an ECG as potentially abnormal. Therefore, the presence or absence of grouped beating represents the most clinically meaningful and efficient initial discriminator.

No Grouped Beating Present

The algorithm followed in the absence of grouped beating is shown in Figure 1a. The general approach for ECGs without grouped beating involved three steps.

Step 1: Examination of QRS regularity. The QRS complexes were assessed for regularity, morphology, and duration. QRS morphology was evaluated to confirm uniformity in shape and size across the rhythm strip, and QRS duration was assessed to ensure that it remained within the normal range of 0.06–0.12 s (Kusumoto et al. 2019). Irregular QRS complexes indicated the need to investigate potential atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, or ectopic beats (Surawicz and Knilans 2019). A regular R–R interval allowed progression to assessment of the rhythm strip according to the subsequent diagnostic steps.

Underlying atrial arrhythmias were also assessed. One P Wave Present Per QRS Complex (Assessing PR Interval)

The assessment of the PR interval in the presence of one P wave per QRS complex is illustrated in Figure 1b. The PR interval was measured by counting the number of small squares between the beginning of the P wave and the beginning of the QRS complex (Surawicz and Knilans 2019). The PR interval duration was then interpreted. A PR interval of < 0.12 s suggested Wolff–Parkinson–White syndrome, Lown–Ganong–Levine syndrome, or junctional rhythm; a PR interval of 0.12–0.20 s indicated normal sinus rhythm; and a PR interval of > 0.20 s indicated first‐degree AV block. For PR intervals > 0.20 s, further assessment of the P‐wave axis was performed. Upright P waves in leads II, III, and aVF confirmed true first‐degree AV block, whereas inverted P waves in these leads, reflecting a superior axis and retrograde conduction, redirected the diagnosis toward junctional rhythm. This included further subclassification into junctional escape rhythm at 40–60 bpm and accelerated junctional rhythm at 60–100 bpm. If the PR interval varied, AV dissociation or ventricular tachycardia was considered, particularly when the ventricular rate exceeded 120 bpm (Kusumoto et al. 2019).

More Than Two P Waves Present Between QRS Complexes

The assessment used when more than two P waves were present between QRS complexes is shown in Figure 1c. Escape rhythm classification was based on QRS width in lead V1 and ventricular rate. Narrow QRS complexes with rates of 40–60 bpm indicated junctional escape rhythm, rates of 60–100 bpm indicated accelerated junctional rhythm, and rates > 100 bpm indicated junctional tachycardia. Wide QRS complexes with rates of 20–40 bpm indicated idioventricular escape rhythm, rates of 40–100 bpm indicated accelerated idioventricular rhythm, and rates > 100 bpm indicated ventricular tachycardia. The PR interval was measured using the P wave closest to the QRS complex. A fixed PR interval throughout the rhythm strip indicated the need for further classification. The presence of two P waves per QRS complex was classified as 2:1 AV block, whereas the presence of three or more P waves per QRS complex represented high‐grade AV block. Type I and Type II AV block were differentiated using lead V1. A narrow QRS complex (< 100 ms) suggested Type I AV block, which is usually AV nodal and often benign, whereas a wide QRS complex (> 100 ms) suggested Type II AV block, which is usually infranodal and may require pacing (El Hajjaji et al. 2018). Third‐degree, or complete, AV block was considered when the PR interval varied. In complete AV block, there was no association between P waves and QRS complexes. Further differentiation was based on QRS duration in lead V1, with narrow QRS complexes (< 100 ms) indicating junctional rhythm (Das et al. 2008) and wide QRS complexes (> 100 ms) indicating idioventricular rhythm (Kusumoto et al. 2019). The ventricular rate was then determined to further classify escape rhythms.

Final Diagnostic Considerations

Type I AV block and junctional escape rhythm are usually stable and require observation unless the patient is symptomatic (Kusumoto et al. 2019). In contrast, Type II AV block and idioventricular rhythm may require pacing because of the higher risk of progression to asystole (Kusumoto et al. 2019). Lead V1 was used to assess QRS duration because it provides a clear view of ventricular depolarization, which facilitates differentiation between supraventricular and ventricular rhythms and helps identify conduction abnormalities such as idioventricular rhythm (El Hajjaji et al. 2018; Kusumoto et al. 2019). The rhythm in which the change occurred was always analyzed.

Grouped Beating Present in ECG

The algorithm followed when grouped beating was present is shown in Figure 2. Grouped beating refers to a repetitive pattern of QRS complexes characterized by a temporary pause (absence of electrical activity) for a few milliseconds before the appearance of a new P wave. Grouped beats consist of two or more QRS complexes and require further classification based on whether two or three QRS complexes are present per group. This distinction is important because of the potential differences in sinoatrial node disruption (Kusumoto et al. 2019). The process involved three steps.

Step 1: Assessment of the number of QRS complexes in grouped beating. Initially, the number of QRS complexes within each group was determined. R–R interval regularity within each group was then evaluated and compared with that of the next group on the rhythm strip. Calipers or the two‐dot marking method were used to ensure measurement accuracy (Surawicz and Knilans 2019). Irregular R–R intervals indicated the need for further evaluation of an underlying rhythm abnormality, whereas regular R–R intervals prompted progression to the next step.

Step 2: Assessment of P–P interval regularity. P–P interval variability was assessed while recognizing that substantial P–P variation may itself be a feature of Type I second‐degree AV block rather than an indicator of a separate atrial arrhythmia. This includes marked lengthening of the pause containing the blocked complex. Wenckebach block remained in the differential diagnosis, and the algorithm proceeded to PR interval analysis when P–P variability followed a regularly progressive pattern that reset with each grouped cycle. Irregular and non‐cyclical P–P variability raised suspicion for non‐conducted premature atrial complexes.

Step 3: PR interval analysis. PR intervals were analyzed using calipers or the two‐dot marking method to assess conduction stability. Progressive PR prolongation until a dropped QRS complex indicated Mobitz Type I second‐degree AV block, or Wenckebach block (Kusumoto et al. 2019). Mobitz Type II second‐degree AV block was diagnosed when the PR interval remained fixed despite occasional non‐conducted P waves, which may progress to complete AV block (El Hajjaji et al. 2018). Notably, although progressive PR prolongation until a dropped QRS complex is characteristic of Mobitz Type I block, the associated R–R interval behavior is variable and does not always follow a pattern of progressive shortening. In addition, R–R and P–P intervals may show regular progressive variability within each Wenckebach cycle rather than monotonic shortening (Lange et al. 1988).

Evaluating the Efficacy of the Algorithm

The study recruited 40 participants, who were randomly and equally assigned to a control group and a training group. Participants included final‐year medical students completing their clinical rotations and junior doctors within their first two postgraduate years. They were recruited from various departments, including dermatology, internal medicine, orthopedics, and plastic surgery, to ensure diversity. Participants were also at equivalent stages of clinical training to ensure comparable baseline exposure to clinical ECG interpretation.

Randomization was performed using a simple random allocation method, whereby participants were sequentially assigned to either the training or control group. The training group received a 1‐h teaching session and had access to the algorithm before ECG interpretation. The control group did not receive training and did not have access to the algorithm. Both groups were provided with eight standardized ECGs that were entirely different from the eight standardized ECGs used in the subsequent assessment. These ECGs were selected by Ashkanani et al. for interpretation using the algorithm, and the participants' interpretations were compared with those of an experienced senior emergency medicine specialist.

A supplementary analysis involving two junior physicians interpreting 150 ECGs was also conducted but is not reported here because the available data were insufficient to support conclusions within the scope of the present study.

Statistical Analyses

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The statistical significance level was set at α = 0.05. All variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests. Descriptive variables were expressed as counts (n) and percentage (%) for categorical variables and as mean and standard deviation (mean ± SD) for continuous variables. The chi‐square (ꭓ 2) test was used to assess whether the frequency of correct identification of diagnosis for the AV block conditions differed between the control and training groups. Independent sample t‐tests were used to assess differences in the mean accuracy of correct diagnoses. Participants in the control and training groups were evaluated according to their ability to interpret different types of AV block on ECG. Effect size was calculated as the standardized mean difference using Cohen's d: d = (x 1 − x 2)/s.

Results

To evaluate the application of the algorithm designed to improve the diagnostic accuracy of different types of AV block, the study used a quantitative research methodology.

Frequency of Correct Diagnosis for Different Types of ECG

Table 1 compares the rates of correct diagnosis between the training and control groups for the different ECG types. In most cases, the differences between the control and training groups were statistically significant (p < 0.05). Eight ECGs were analyzed. For ECG1, which showed third‐degree AV block with junctional escape rhythm, the overall diagnostic accuracy was 87.5%. The correct diagnosis rates were 80% in the control group and 95% in the training group; however, this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.151). ECG2, which showed Type I 2:1 AV block, demonstrated a significant improvement with training, with correct diagnosis rates of 55% in the control group and 90% in the training group (p = 0.013). ECG3, which showed Type I second‐degree AV block, showed no significant between‐group difference, with correct diagnosis rates of 70% and 85% in the control and training groups, respectively (p = 0.256).

Mean Accuracy of Correct Diagnosis in Trained and Control Groups

Significant differences in diagnostic accuracy between the two groups were observed for five of the eight ECGs. No statistically significant differences in mean diagnostic accuracy were observed between the training and control groups for ECGs showing third‐degree AV block, Type I second‐degree AV block, and first‐degree AV block. However, statistically significant mean differences were found between the training and control groups for ECGs showing 2:1 AV block, isorhythmic AV dissociation, high‐grade AV block, third‐degree AV block with accelerated idioventricular rhythm, and Type II second‐degree AV block.

Association Between Training and the Likelihood of a Correct Diagnosis of AV Blocks

The results showed a strong association between training and the probability of a correct diagnosis (p = 0.007). This finding indicates that participants who received training were more likely to accurately diagnose AV blocks than those who did not. Trained participants were more than three times more likely to reach the correct diagnosis than untrained controls. Therefore, training appears to be an important factor in improving diagnostic accuracy.

Evaluating the Efficacy of the Algorithm

Comparability between groups was supported by enrolling participants at similar stages of clinical training, including final‐year medical students and junior doctors within their first two postgraduate years, and from diverse departmental backgrounds. No formal pretest of baseline ECG proficiency was administered before group assignment, which is acknowledged as a limitation below.

During the testing phase, the training group interpreted ECGs from memory without access to the algorithm flowcharts. Similarly, the control group had no access to the algorithm during testing. Therefore, the control group's performance reflected baseline ECG interpretation skills without algorithmic guidance. The eight standardized test ECGs were selected exclusively for evaluation and were distinct from any ECGs used during the 1‐h teaching session.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that the proposed algorithm for detecting and diagnosing AV blocks is accurate, structured, and user‐friendly. Trained participants achieved correct diagnosis rates of up to 95% across several AV block types, highlighting the potential value of the algorithm in clinical education and practice.

Enhancing ECG Interpretation Among Healthcare Professionals

ECG interpretation remains a challenging skill across healthcare disciplines, with considerable variability in proficiency and confidence among professionals with different levels of experience. Several studies have shown that systematic approaches, such as the algorithm proposed in this study, can improve diagnostic accuracy and efficiency, particularly among less‐experienced practitioners (Abdalla and Khanra 2022; Kashou, Noseworthy, et al. 2023). A structured algorithmic method may therefore reduce diagnostic errors and support the learning curve for ECG interpretation.

Potential for Integration With Artificial Intelligence

With the rapid development of artificial intelligence and machine learning in medicine, there is increasing interest in AI‐powered ECG diagnostics (Attia et al. 2021; Nagarajan et al. 2021). Integrating AI technology with the proposed algorithm may further improve diagnostic precision, reduce human error, and assist junior doctors and trainees in complex ECG assessments (Guo et al. 2023). The combination of human clinical expertise and AI‐supported interpretation could improve the efficiency and reliability of ECG diagnosis in clinical settings.

Redefining the Role of Grouped Beating in AV Block Diagnosis

Grouped beating has traditionally been strongly associated with Wenckebach, or Mobitz Type I, AV block (Chuang et al. 2021). However, the present algorithm highlights that grouped beating may also be observed in Mobitz Type II block and non‐conducted premature atrial complexes. Therefore, grouped beating should not be automatically interpreted as Wenckebach syndrome. Instead, it should be evaluated as a broader diagnostic marker that may indicate multiple rhythm abnormalities. This finding supports the need to refine existing AV block classification frameworks and consider grouped beating as a more versatile diagnostic feature.

Study Limitations and Future Improvements

Despite its promising potential, the algorithm has some limitations. First, the study did not directly compare participants' ECG interpretations before and after learning the algorithm, which would have provided stronger evidence of its effectiveness. Second, a larger cohort including participants with different levels of clinical experience would strengthen the evaluation of algorithm performance across different skill levels. Third, some participants required additional clarification of specific algorithm steps, suggesting that further refinement and more structured training may improve usability. Nevertheless, a brief 1‐h training session significantly improved participants' diagnostic performance. Although participants were encouraged to use calipers for ECG measurements, calipers may not always be available in clinical settings. Therefore, future versions of the algorithm should include alternative measurement strategies to improve accessibility.

Another important limitation relates to the use of lead V1 for QRS duration assessment. Although lead V1 is widely used, previous evidence suggests considerable variability and poor interobserver agreement, particularly when QRS duration exceeds 100 ms (Danzig et al. 1976). In addition, using QRS duration in lead V1, with a 100‐ms partition value, as the primary surrogate for the anatomical localization of AV block has limitations. The association between Mobitz Type II physiology and infranodal anatomy is not absolute, as Type II physiological behavior can occur at the AV nodal level. Moreover, infranodal block does not always produce wide QRS complexes. The 100‐ms threshold also has limited specificity because QRS prolongation may reflect a coexisting bifascicular conduction defect rather than the level of AV block itself.

Future studies should include a pretest of baseline ECG proficiency and extend training across multiple sessions. They should also evaluate performance both with and without the algorithm available as a visual reference. In addition, future research should expand the algorithm's diagnostic capabilities to include other forms of AV dissociation and validate its effectiveness in larger and more diverse cohorts. Continuous refinement and adaptation of this algorithm may help address current gaps in ECG interpretation training and improve clinical accuracy, efficiency, and patient outcomes.

Conclusion

This study shows that the proposed algorithm has strong potential to improve the detection and diagnosis of AV blocks in clinical practice. The algorithm provides a structured and efficient approach that may help both experienced and less‐experienced healthcare professionals interpret ECGs more accurately. One of the most important findings is that grouped beating is not exclusive to Wenckebach, or Mobitz Type I, AV block, as traditionally believed. Instead, grouped beating may also occur in Mobitz Type II block and non‐conducted premature atrial complexes, suggesting that it may serve as an important initial diagnostic feature in the assessment of different arrhythmias. Although the results are promising, larger and more controlled studies are required to confirm the effectiveness of this algorithm in real‐world settings. With further refinement and possible integration with AI, this tool could become a valuable component of ECG interpretation, helping clinicians make faster and more accurate diagnoses and ultimately improve patient outcomes.
