Leveraging design for collective phototaxis in morphological swarm robotics
Authors: Jeremy Fersula, Nicolas Bredeche, Olivier Dauchot
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMorphological computing-the use of a robot’s physical design to facilitate task execution-has emerged as a promising strategy in swarm robotics. Here, through experiments and simulations, we show that its success critically depends on both the robots’ control policy and the details of their physical design. We study a swarm of Kilobots embedded in an exoskeleton whose geometry controls the propensity of robots to align or anti-align with the external force they experience. Experimentally, the contrast between the two morphologies in the success rate of a simple phototactic task becomes dramatic when stopping is forbidden and robots can only slow down. Using a faithful physical model of the robots’ self-aligning dynamics, numerical simulations reveal that efficient phototaxis requires fine tuning of the self-alignment strength around a sweet spot. More broadly, varying this parameter unlocks a rich repertoire of collective behaviors.Subject terms: Applied physics, Mechanical engineering, Nonlinear phenomena

Introduction

Many different aspects contribute to the design of a robot: its morphology (shape, structure, materials), sensory apparatus, motor system, control architecture, etc. 1,2. All of these aspects interact and jointly determine the robot’s behavior. By designing the morphology to take advantage of certain physical properties, one can alleviate the need for computation to achieve a desired behavior. The laws of physics “work" in favor of achieving the task3. This design principle of using morphology to facilitate control has been proven effective in achieving complex tasks for many different robotic systems4,5. In the specific context of swarm robotics, prior work has demonstrated that locomotion or aggregation can be achieved using a single actuation parameter per robot6–8, or even a single control input applied to the entire swarm9.

As this design principle heavily relies on physics, a great source of inspiration lies in the field of active matter. Conversely, the systems of interest in that field of research look more and more alike to swarm robotic systems10. In both cases, one takes interest in the collective behaviors that emerge from local interactions in large assemblies of mobile units. There are, however, important paradigm differences. In swarm robotics, the mobile units are robots usually equipped with sensors and the ability to perform numerical computation to make decisions about their future behavior. The focus lies on designing proper policies and collective strategies to control the swarm11–15. In the context of active matter, the focus is on the spontaneous emergence of symmetry broken phases16–19. Diverse behaviors emerge through reactive interactions, such as collisions, rather than through interactions that require decision-making or cognitive processes. This is in sharp contrast with the context of swarm robotics, where collisions, for instance, are typically avoided12,20.

In recent years, there has nevertheless been a convergence of interest. Not only are there more works in active matter dealing with smart agents21–25, but also works in swarm robotics demonstrating, for instance, that swarm aggregation benefits from collisions between robots7–9. More specifically, it was shown in ref, 8 that a specific re-orientation response of the robots to an external force, the so-called self-alignment19, can improve or degrade the realization of a simple phototactic task. In this task, robots explore their environment to find a light region the size of which is too small to fit the entire swarm. The resulting crowding causes frequent collisions and re-orientations, hence the role of self-alignment. In this set of experiments, the robots' controller allows for a complete stop. This has two consequences : (i) each robot can individually achieve the task, (ii) when stopping at the boundary of the light region, the robots build up a wall preventing the remaining robots from accessing the light region. The success of the aggregation in the light region precisely depends on the way these remaining robots morphologically respond to this repulsive barrier.

Here we address a more subtle question. We probe the possibility of a collective effect of the morphological design in achieving the task. To do so, we require the robots to maintain a finite velocity. This prevents a single robot from achieving phototaxis. Experimentally, we consider two populations of robots with opposite self-alignment: the so-called aligners align with the force they experience; the so-called fronters anti-align in the opposite direction. We observe that the contrast in task achievement is even more dramatic: while the aligners achieve no phototaxis at all, the fronters leverage a collective effect, called motility-induced phase separation (MIPS)17 in the realm of active matter, to successfully aggregate in the light. We further investigate the role of self-alignment in aggregating in the light using numerical simulations. By tuning the self-alignment continuously, we first show that minute changes in the design actually drastically alter the performance in achieving phototaxis. This is a direct consequence of the effect of self-alignment on MIPS26. We further show that exploring a wider range of self-alignment strength provides an opportunity for designing highly expressive swarms in terms of the collective behavior they have access to.

Results

Real robots experiments

We use the experimental setup originally described in ref. 8, and we only recast here its main features. A swarm of N = 64 Kilobots27, equipped with a 4.8 cm-wide 3D-printed exoskeleton replacing their native legs, evolves in a circular arena of diameter ⊘ = 1.5m (Supplementary Fig. 1). They perform a simple phototactic task: aggregating in a light region located at the center of the arena and covering σ = 6% of the total area. The boundary between the light and dark regions is sharp, and no light gradients can guide the robots. The light region is made purposely too small to fit the entirety of the swarm (N∘max≈54) in order to favor collisions.

Each robot continuously monitors the local light intensity. When in the dark area, defined as an intensity below a predefined threshold, the robot executes a run-and-tumble motion with a constant velocity v•. Upon entering the light region, where the intensity is above the threshold, the robot switches to a stop-and-run type of motion, producing an effective velocity v∘ (see METHODS). A typical trajectory of a single robot is illustrated in Fig. 1A. The effective velocities in the dark and light regions are extracted from the tracking of the trajectories. More specifically, we compute the mean square displacement MSD(τ)=r(t+τ)−r(t)2, where the brackets denote an average over the running time t, inside and outside the light region (Fig. 1B). Averaging over many trajectories, we find a mean effective velocity ratio of one third: v∘/v• ≈ 1/3.

Two types of exoskeletons have been designed in order to obtain two opposite self-aligning behaviors (Fig. 1C). The asymmetry in the leg positions and mass distribution leads to an asymmetric drag on the contact points with the floor. This in turn induces a re-orienting torque on the robot when it experiences a force that is not aligned with its propelling direction19. For the so-called aligner morphology, two flexible legs are placed symmetrically at the rear of the robot, and a single rigid leg is placed centered at the front. The mass is distributed toward the front. For the fronter morphology, the two flexible legs are placed symmetrically at the front of the robot and the single rigid leg at its rear. The mass is distributed toward the back. All 3D mesh files are publicly available in the associated repository28. As a result, the aligner re-orients in the direction of the force it experiences. Conversely, the fronter re-orients against the direction of the force it experiences (Supplementary Video 1).

When two robots enter a “collision event", they typically experience repeated tapping29 and repeated re-orientation. At an effective level, the collision can be described as follows (Fig. 1C): the two robots feel a force pointing outwards from the collision point. As two aligners collide, they re-orient partially away from the contact point, more so according to the strength of morphological effects. This produces a tendency to share a common direction after a collision event. On the contrary, as two front ends collide, they re-orient toward the contact point, which may in turn trigger another collision. Provided that the strength of morphological effects is high enough, this cycle becomes hard to break, and two colliding robots can be stuck until vibrational noise eventually leads them to escape the collision (see Supplementary Video 2 and the statistics of collisions provided in Supplementary Fig. 2). Altogether, the interplay between activity and morphology promotes the dispersion of aligners and the aggregation of fronters.

At the beginning of each experiment, robots are placed randomly in the dark region of the arena with varying initial positions and orientations. A time series of image snapshots taken from a typical experiment is shown in Fig. 2A, for the fronters (top) and the aligners (bottom). A side-by-side movie of two experiments, one with fronters, the other with aligners, is provided as Supplementary Video 3. As robots explore the space of the arena, the local density quickly increases in the light region as compared to the dark one, simply because of the difference in effective velocity in the two regions. After this brief transient phase, one observes two clearly distinct behaviors. In the case of the fronters, the initial increase in density rapidly triggers the formation of a cluster of up to 25 robots. This cluster is subject to evaporation at the boundary, with robots leaving the cluster due to orientational or positional noise. However, this evaporation is compensated by new robots randomly colliding with the existing cluster, triggering the force re-orientation response which drives the incoming robot toward the crowd30,31. In the case of the aligners, nothing of the sort happens. The data show no clear evolution in time, but a distribution centered around N∘/N = 0.16, a value close to the expected proportion obtained as if there was no collisions and robots would explore the arena independently (σv∙v∘=0.18). There is thus no sign of aggregation for the aligners. Repeating 4 independent runs for each morphology and monitoring the fraction of robots inside the light region as a function of time confirms the above observation (Fig. 2B, C). In particular, in the case of the fronters, one observes an exponential relaxation to a plateau taking values around N∘/N = 0.4, highlighting their capacity to aggregate (Fig. 2B).

The illuminated area (not visible on the images) is surrounded by a red circle. The positive self-alignment of the aligners prevents them from aggregating in the light region, while the fronters succeed in clustering in and around the illuminated region. b Fraction of the swarm in the light region as a function of time for 4 independent experiments, quantifies the lack of aggregation for the aligners (blue), with no significant evolution in time and a mean N∘/N around 0.16. Conversely, the fronters (green) manage to hold a consistent filling of the light region following an exponential-like convergence to a plateau value around N∘/N = 0.4.

This effect can only be attributed to the role of morphology: aggregation is made possible through the force re-orientation response granted by the exoskeleton design. When two fronters collide and face each other, their real speed decreases to almost zero, even if their respective motors keep on running. This dramatic decrease in velocity with local density is the basic mechanism at the origin of the well-known motility-induced phase separation17: density increases where velocity slows down; if velocity slows down strongly enough with density, this positive feedback leads to a phase separation between a low-density and a high-density phase. In other words, the transient increase of the density induced by the “slowing down in the light" policy, coupled to the fronters’ strong tendency to decrease their effective velocity during collisions, induces the seeding of the phase separation, hence the aggregation in the light region. This cannot take place for the aligners, which barely slow down during a collision.

The above result demonstrates the possible cross-fertilization between the field of active matter and that of swarm robotics. It also stresses the importance of the morphological design for the realization of the given task. Finally, it shows that the mechanism by which the morphology helps in the task realization may be strongly dependent on the accessible set of policies. In the case where the robots could stop in the light, the difference in success rate in the phototaxis between the fronters and the aligners results from the barrier built by the arrested robot at the boundary between the light and the dark regions8. In the present case, where the robots can only slow down, we just saw that the dynamics responsible for the aggregation of the fronters is analogous to nucleation: a large enough seed cluster initiates the growth of the aggregate.

Tuning self-alignment in silico

Optimizing the morphology in view of a given task is in general a very tedious and time-consuming task, because of the highly non-linear dependence of the output on many parameters. In the present case, the mechanical rules linking the morphology (friction and mass distribution) to the self-alignment strength are known (see ref. 19-appendix), and we can concentrate on the numerical evaluation of the right amount of self-alignment needed to optimize aggregation.

The dynamics of self-propelled agents, such as the robots, taking their momentum from an external32,33 or an internal34 vibration source, including self-alignment19, is well described by a set of stochastic equations for the center of mass r and for the body orientation encoded in a unit vector n35. Time and length have been rescaled using the robot diameter d as the unit length and τ = d/va as the unit time, where va is the typical active velocity, chosen here as the velocity in the dark v•. As in the real robot experiment, we perform a phototactic aggregation task, keeping the same ratio σ = 6% for the area of the light region to the arena size, which here has periodic boundary conditions. The ratio v∘/v• is chosen to mimic the experimental value of 1/3, we set D = 0.01, and we concentrate on varying ϵ/τn, where ϵ sets the sign of the self-alignment, ϵ = + 1 for aligners and ϵ = − 1 for fronters, and 1/τn sets the strength of the self-aligning torque. In the limit where τn → + ∞, the self-aligning torque vanishes and the dynamics is that of standard Active Brownian Particles38.

We run simulations lasting 1000τ, uniformly varying the parameter ϵ/τn in the range [ − 5, 5]. We record the fraction of the swarm in the light region averaged over the last 100τ, as shown in red in Fig. 3. The most salient feature in these data is the presence of a sweet spot of values for the parameter ϵ/τn for which aggregation in the light takes place. In this regime, the slowing down of the dynamics inside the light region leads to a density increase, which triggers the formation of a cluster in a motility-induced phase separation-like scenario: once a cluster seed is large enough, the probability of wandering robots coming into contact and aggregating with it exceeds that of robots leaving the cluster. This maintains the growth of the aggregate and is the same effect as the one observed in our real-robot experiments. And indeed, this sweet spot takes place for negative values of ϵ/τn, that is, for a fronter behavior. Also in line with our observations, we find that aligners can never achieve aggregation, and the fraction of agents in the light matches that of a random exploration of space, σv∙v∘=0.18, for a light fraction of space σ = 6% and a velocity ratio v∘/v• = 1/3.

Even more surprising is the fact that increasing the magnitude of the negative self-alignment rapidly turns counterproductive. As a matter of fact, we even observe reverse phototaxis: the fraction of agents inside the light ( ≃ 0.1) is smaller than expected from the random exploration of space ( ≃ 0.18). Similarly, increasing ϵ/τn > 0 does not improve and even slowly deteriorates the performance. The reasons for these features can be inferred from a closer look at the late-stage state of the system (see insets of Fig. 3), and match the conclusions of a recent numerical work26, where the stability of the motility-induced phase separation with respect to self-alignment is analyzed. For large and positive self-alignment, the collisions together with the self-alignment lead to collective, unidirectional motion35. This is further evidenced by measuring the swarm alignment over the last 100τ <Ψ>=∣∑i=064ni∣, as shown in blue in Fig. 3. For ϵ/τn > 0, particles start to align, and the value of < Ψ > grows up to a plateau for which the alignment is maximal with respect to the orientational noise D and the total density. For large, but negative self-alignment, micro-clusters form everywhere, even in the low-density dark region: the robots entering in contact quickly lock in a very stable binary or ternary configuration. These two emergent behaviors act against aggregation in the light. We have verified that increasing the overall density by a factor of three does not modify the above conclusions (Supplementary Fig. 3). Finally, we have simulated a 50/50 mixture of fronters and aligners, keeping all other parameters identical to the above experiments (Supplementary Fig. 4). Although the flocking dynamics is destroyed, we still observe a sweet spot value of the absolute self-alignment close to 1/τn = 1.5. The fronters and the aligners rapidly segregate, respectively, in and out of the light region, resulting in a degraded performance as compared to that of the population of fronters only.

The results of these simulations are thus two-fold. First, comparing with the real robot experiments, one clearly sees that the morphologically rooted force-reorientation observed in the real robot systems is well-replicated by the self-alignment term. The parameter ϵ/τn entirely drives the large-scale dynamics and the success or failure of the aggregation in the light region. Second, by increasing the range of this parameter, we have observed new large-scale collective behaviors, ranging from local clustering to collective motion, suggesting a richer expressivity of the control parameter than initially expected.

Discussion

This work demonstrates that morphological design does not merely modulate individual robot responses to environmental cues, but can fundamentally reshape the collective mechanisms through which a swarm achieves a task. By constraining robots to maintain a non-zero velocity, we remove the possibility of single-agent phototaxis and force the swarm to rely on collective effects. In this regime, the two morphologies exhibit radically divergent behaviors: aligners fail to accumulate in the light region, while fronters trigger a phase-separation mechanism akin to motility-induced phase separation, driven by collision-induced velocity reduction. Morphology thus acts not simply as a facilitator of control, but as an enabler of alternative collective pathways to task success. Importantly, the mechanism enabling aggregation differs qualitatively from previous observations under stop-and-run control policies, where aggregation is limited by boundary effects, and morphology modulates how remaining agents negotiate the crowd barrier. This highlights that the benefits of morphological computation depend critically on the space of available behavioral policies, suggesting that morphology cannot be optimized independently of control, and may even compensate for policy limitations.

Our numerical results further reveal that successful aggregation requires tuning self-alignment within a narrow “sweet spot”: too weak, and collisions are insufficient to seed clustering; too strong, and either velocity alignment or stable micro-clusters outside the light region hinder aggregation. This sharp sensitivity underscores the high-dimensional trade-offs inherent in morphology design: robustness may require morphologies that adapt dynamically rather than static, single-purpose exoskeletal structures.

More broadly, these results position morphology as a control parameter enabling a richer design vocabulary for swarm robotics. Exploring a continuous space of self-alignment strengths yields a spectrum of collective behaviors, from global flocking to jamming into localized cluster formation. This suggests that morphological tuning could be leveraged not merely for performance optimization, but for programmable expressivity. This resonates with recent theoretical developments in smart active matter, where physical interactions provide implicit computation and decision-making capacity at the collective level.

Finally, our findings raise several engineering and scientific perspectives. (i) Toward adaptive or reconfigurable morphology: dynamically tuning mass distribution, friction, or contact asymmetry could allow swarms to switch between collective modes in response to context, much as biological collectives modulate adhesion or polarity. (ii) Learning morphology-policy couplings: combining morphological effects with decentralized learning or embodied evolution may enable robots to autonomously discover how to exploit physical interactions rather than treat them as constraints39–41. (iii) Bridging active matter theory and swarm design: the observed behaviors provide a real-robot platform for testing predictions from kinetic theories of self-aligning active particles42,43.

Methods

Experimental setup

Kilobot robot

The Kilobot robot is a 33mm wide robot consisting of a circular board mounted on 3 rigid metal legs and supporting a cylindrical battery, making it approximately 34 mm tall. On top of the board are placed a light intensity sensor, an RGB LED, two vibration motors, and the removable 3.7 V lithium-ion battery. On the bottom of the circuit board, the robot is equipped with a close-range infrared emitter/receiver, used for both communication between Kilobots and computer to Kilobot.

Aligner and fronter exoskeletons

Exoskeletons are tripod-based designs printed using a photopolymer-based 3D printer, Stratasys Connex3 Objet260. Each print comes embedded in a soft material, the support, which has to be removed mechanically. Materials used for both morphologies are “Support 706B" and “VeroClear". Once the support has been removed, exoskeletons are gently washed with water and a black-and-white detection pattern is glued on top.

Arena

The whole arena rests on a thick slab of chipboard wood of 5 × 200 x 200cm on which the arena floor, walls, and overall structure are screwed in. The floor consists of a 2 mm thick black PMMA slab, and the walls are made of a 0.2 mm thick circular aluminum sheet of 1.5 m in diameter. A main beam structure is fixed along a bearing wall of the experimentation room; two beams are placed on the opposite sides to hold black fabric covering the whole arena. The inside of the arena is lit via LEDs light strips attached to the inner part of the circular wall. About 2 meters higher than the floor, a black-and-white camera is set to film the entirety of the arena, and a small video projector allows for projection on part of the arena. The projected zones are all set to use only the green channel, as it matches the Kilobot sensor intensity peak better than the color blue. In front of the camera, we placed a red long-pass filter, cutting wavelengths shorter than 645 nm. Doing so, the contrast between the lit and unlit regions disappears on the film; the overall light intensity is enough to track the robots, and the real contrast in light intensity is maintained high enough to allow for phototaxis.

Algorithms

The algorithm used is based on a sense-act cycle, in which the robot senses its environment (light intensity) and acts by driving its motor vibration intensity according to a predefined Run-And-Tumble behavior. After each cycle, an assessment is made to switch from a high-speed Run-And-Tumble to a low-speed Run-And-Tumble. In the light integration phase (sense), the robot performs batches of light intensity measurements to average out sensor noise over the course of ≈ 1s. In the motion phase, the robot decides its velocity for the Run-And-Tumble. A high-speed Run-And-Tumble is performed by setting both motors of the Kilobot to 130/255 for a fixed duration of 6s, and tumbling by applying a less powerful vibration intensity to one motor only, drawn at random (110/255). The tumbling duration is drawn from a long-tail distribution with 6 possibilities, from 1 to 7 s. The low-speed Run-And-Tumble on the other hand, cannot be implemented by reducing motor intensity only, as motors have a starting threshold below which they do not vibrate. Instead, the motion is interrupted quickly, following phases of Run-And-Tumble and complete stop with a ratio in duration of 1 to 15. At the end of the sense-act cycle, a flag is updated according to the measured light intensity to switch from one behavior to the other.

Numerical simulations

Simulations are run using LAMMPS44, an open-source software designed for molecular dynamics simulation. This software has been extended with our equations of motion, using code available publicly on Github28. Collisions are soft and computed with a truncated and shifted Lennard-Jones potential (WCA) cut at 1.122. Initially, particles are placed randomly in the simulation box. A first phase of Brownian motion with increasing potential cutoff from 0 to the final value is performed to avoid interpenetration of particles. Only then is the true simulation started.
