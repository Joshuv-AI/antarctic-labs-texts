The kinases Hsl101 and Urk1 regulate metabolism of Cryptococcus neoformans
Authors: Yuanyuan Ma, Yetong Shen, Lei Zhang, Wenjie Fang, Weihua Pan, Wanqing Liao, Xin Ye, Xinyao Liu, Yanyan Xu, Xiaojing Li, Peng Xue, Liuyang Cai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cryptococcus neoformans is a significant human fungal pathogen, particularly concerning for immunocompromised individuals. Key kinases, Hsl101 and Urk1, are critical for crossing the blood-brain barrier, although the specific mechanisms by which they do so remain undefined. In this study, we systematically investigate the impact of HSL101 or URK1 deletion on the proteomic and metabolomic profiles of C. neoformans. By generating a deletion mutant for HSL101, we observed profound alterations in the expression levels of 366 proteins and 421 metabolites, highlighting significant disruptions in key biological processes, such as oxidative phosphorylation and ATP synthesis, which are vital for energy production in the cell. Similarly, the deletion of URK1 resulted in significant changes in the expression of 236 proteins and 366 metabolites, particularly affecting pathways related to steroid biosynthesis and starch and sucrose metabolism, which are critical for cellular function and adaptation. These findings shed light on the regulatory roles of Hsl101 and Urk1 in the metabolic pathways of C. neoformans.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-28785-x.

Introduction

Cryptococcus neoformans is an opportunistic fungal pathogen that poses a significant risk to immunocompromised individuals, particularly those with HIV/AIDS1–4. This fungus is a major contributor to cryptococcal meningoencephalitis, leading to around 200,000 deaths each year among individuals with HIV/AIDS2,3,5. The pathogenesis of C. neoformans begins with the inhalation of airborne spores, which colonize the lungs and can subsequently disseminate to the central nervous system, leading to severe neurological complications6–8. The effective management of cryptococcal infections is increasingly challenging due to the rising incidence of antifungal resistance and the limited availability of effective therapies and vaccines9–11. The virulence of C. neoformans is closely associated with its complex regulatory networks, particularly those involving protein kinases12–14.

Kinases are essential for signal transduction, allowing the fungus to adapt to various environmental stresses and enhance its pathogenicity. Previous studies have identified 34 kinases as core virulence factors critical for the infectivity of C. neoformans in both pulmonary and central nervous system infections13. Among these, four kinases—Hsl101, Urk1, Irk2, and Irk5—were identified as critical for crossing the blood-brain barrier, although their specific pathways remain undefined13. Our recent research revealed the roles of Irk2 and Irk5 in regulating ATP and metabolism in C. neoformans15. Building on this foundation, our study focuses on further examining Hsl101 and Urk1. We systematically investigated the roles of Hsl101 and Urk1 in the proteomic and metabolomic profiles of C. neoformans through targeted gene deletion experiments. Using quantitative proteomics and untargeted metabolomics, we observed changes in protein expression and metabolite concentrations linked to the absence of these kinases. Preliminary results show that Hsl101 significantly impacts the levels of proteins and metabolites related to mitochondria, which are essential for ATP production, underscoring its probable role in in energy metabolism and mitochondrial function. Additionally, the deletion of URK1 led to substantial changes in 236 proteins and 366 metabolites, suggesting its potential involvement in vital metabolic pathways such as steroid biosynthesis and starch and sucrose metabolism. These findings emphasize that Hsl101 and Urk1 may serve as important key regulators of metabolic pathways in C. neoformans.

Materials and methods

Construction of deletion strains and grow condition

We used the wild-type (WT) strain Cryptococcus neoformans var. grubii H99, along with the deletion mutants hsl101Δ and urk1Δ. The primers used for the construction of deletion strains can be found in Table S1. The gene sequence for Hsl101 (CNAG_02675) was sourced from the C. neoformans var. grubii serotype A genome database (https://www.broadinstitute.org/fungal-genome-initiative/cryptococcus-neoformans-serotype-genome-project). To create the hsl101Δ mutant, we replaced the genetic locus containing the 2894-base pair (bp) open reading frame through homologous recombination using a gene-specific deletion cassette that included a neomycin resistance (NEOr) marker. The NEOr gene was introduced into this cassette, which was amplified using the primers Hsl101-UP-F, Hsl101-UP-R, neoF, neoR, Hsl101-Down-F, and Hsl101-Down-R (Table S1). This deletion cassette was introduced into the WT strain via biolistic transformation, following established protocols16–18. Positive transformants were confirmed using YPD medium plates (1% yeast extract, 2% dextrose, 2% peptone, 2% agar) supplemented with neomycin (200 mg/ml) and through polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analysis (Fig S1). To check for the mutant hsl101Δ, we employed the following primers: for long PCR amplification, we used the reverse primer Hsl101-UP-R (TGAGTCGTATTACAATTCACTGGCCGTCGTTTTAC) and the forward primer Hsl101-Down-F (ATGGTCATAGCTGTTTCCTG). For RT-PCR amplification, we utilized the forward primer Hsl101-RT-F (ATCAAGGTTCTTCTGTTGGGTCG) and the reverse primer Hsl101-RT-R (ACGCCAATGCTGAGTAGTATTTTCC). The PCR was conducted in 25 µl reactions that included NEB Taq DNA Polymerase and 15 ng of template DNA. The PCR conditions were as follows: an initial cycle at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 30 cycles of 95 °C for 20 s, 53 °C for 60 s, and 68 °C for 300 s, concluding with a final cycle at 68 °C for 300 s. After the reaction, a 0.5% or 2% agarose gel was utilized to analyze the PCR products. The gene sequence for the Urk1 (CNAG_03367) was similarly obtained from the C. neoformans var. grubii serotype A genome database. For the generation of the urk1Δ mutant, we replaced the genetic locus containing the 1681 bp open reading frame through homologous recombination with a gene-specific deletion cassette that included a NEOr marker. This cassette was amplified using the primers Urk1-UP-F, Urk1-UP-R, neoF, neoR, Urk1-Down-F, and Urk1-Down-R (Table S1). This cassette was introduced into the WT strain via biolistic transformation, as previously described16–18. Confirmation of positive transformants was achieved through YPD medium plates (1% yeast extract, 2% dextrose, 2% peptone, 2% agar) containing neomycin (200 mg/ml) and via PCR analysis. To verify the mutant urk1Δ, long PCR amplification was performed using the reverse primer Urk1-UP-R (CAATTCACTGGCCGTCGTTTTAC) and the forward primer Hsl101-Down-F (ATGGTCATAGCTGTTTCCTG). For RT-PCR amplification, we employed the forward primer Urk1-RT-F (CATGACAATACTTCGAGATCGTACC) and the reverse primer Urk1-RT-R (CATCTGCGAATCCAACAGCAAA). The PCR reactions were set up in a total volume of 25 µl, which included NEB’s Taq DNA Polymerase and 15 ng of template DNA. The PCR cycling conditions consisted of an initial cycle at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 30 cycles of 95 °C for 20 s, 52 °C for 60 s, and 68 °C for 300 s, concluding with a final extension at 68 °C for 300 s. After completion of the reaction, a 0.5% or 2% agarose gel was employed to analyze the resulting PCR products. To differentiate between the long PCR products of the urk1Δ and WT strains, we performed ApaI enzyme digestion. The long PCR product for the WT strain does not contain an ApaI restriction site, yielding a single product of 4965 bp (Fig S1). In contrast, the long PCR product for urk1Δ contains one ApaI site, producing expected band sizes of 3771 bp and 1200 bp (Fig S1). The digestion reaction was prepared with 5 µl of the long PCR product, 1 µl of ApaI enzyme, 2.5 µl of 10X rCutSmart™ buffer, and 16.5 µl of dH2O, for a total volume of 25 µl. The mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. The rCutSmart™ buffer composition included 50 mM Potassium Acetate, 20 mM Tris-acetate, 10 mM Magnesium Acetate, and 100 µg/ml Recombinant Albumin (pH 7.9 at 25 °C). The digested PCR products were then analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis (Fig S1). The 4.0 × 105 fungal cells were cultured overnight in 30 mL of YPD medium (1% yeast extract, 2% dextrose), with shaking at 150 rpm at 30 °C for 16 h. Following cultivation during the logarithmic phase, the cells designated for proteomic and metabolomic analyses were harvested, washed with PBS, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and preserved at −80 °C for subsequent analyses. We performed three biological replicates for both the proteomic and metabolomic analyses.

Proteome studies

Initially, frozen cells were pulverized in liquid nitrogen to yield a fine powder. This powder was then subjected to a 3-minute sonication on ice in a lysis buffer containing 10 mM dithiothreitol, 1% Triton X-100, and 1% protease inhibitor cocktail, 2 mM EDTA, 3 µM TSA, 50 µM PR-619, 50 mM NAM, and 1% phosphatase inhibitor to maintain phosphorylation. Next, Tris-saturated phenol (pH 8.0) (Sigma-Aldrich) was incorporated into the mixture, followed by vortexing for 5 min. After centrifuging for 10 min at 4 °C, the phases were separated, and the upper phenol layer was subsequently transferred with care to a fresh tube. Next, the mixture was combined with a minimum of four volumes of methanol saturated with ammonium sulfate, and the sample was placed in incubation for at least 6 h at −20°C. After centrifugation, the protein pellet was washed with 100% ice-cold methanol, followed by three washes with 100% ice-cold acetone. The resulting protein was subsequently re-dissolved in 8 M urea, and its concentration was assessed using a BCA kit (Beyotime). 100% (w/v) Trichloroacetic acid was slowly introduced to the mixture to achieve a final concentration of 20% (m/v) for protein precipitation. Following this, the mixture was vortexed and incubated for 2 h at 4 °C. The collected precipitated protein was washed and dried for one minute following centrifugation. The dried protein sample was then re-dissolved in 200 mM TEAB (Sigma-Aldrich) and subjected to ultrasonic dispersion. For overnight digestion, trypsin (1 µg/µl in 50 mM acetic acid) was added at a 1:50 mass ratio relative to the protein. The reduction of the mixture was performed using 5 mM dithiothreitol (Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min at 56 °C, after which alkylation was carried out with 11 mM iodoacetamide in the dark for 15 min. The final step involved desalting the peptides through a Strata X SPE column (Phenomenex). Tryptic peptides were reconstituted in solvent A prior to loading onto a custom-designed reversed-phase analytical column that was 25 cm long and had an inner diameter of 100 μm. The mobile phase consisted of two solvents: solvent A, made up of 0.1% formic acid and 2% acetonitrile in water, and solvent B, which contained 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile. Using a gradient program, peptide separation was achieved as follows: the concentration of solvent B increased from 6% to 24% between 0 and 14 min, further to 35% from 14 to 16 min, then surged to 90% from 16 to 18 min, and was held at 90% from 18 to 20 min. This procedure was carried out at a constant flow rate of 500 nL/min on an Easy-nLC1000 UHPLC system (Bruker Daltonics). Utilizing a capillary source connected to a timsTOF Pro mass spectrometer, the eluted peptides underwent analysis at an electrospray voltage of 1.75 kV. The TOF mass detector was responsible for the detection of both precursor ions and their fragments. In dia-PASEF mode, the timsTOF Pro captured 20 PASEF-MS/MS scans per cycle, with the full MS scan range established between 300 and 1500 m/z. For the MS/MS scans, the range was defined from 400 to 850 m/z, incorporating an isolation window of 7 m/z. The data processing utilized the DIA-NN search engine (version 1.8). The Cryptococcus neoformans var. grubii serotype A strain H99 235,443 PR 20,240,409.fasta database, which consists of 7429 entries and includes a reverse decoy database, was used to match the tandem mass spectra. Trypsin/P was designated as the cleavage enzyme, permitting a maximum of one missed cleavage. Fixed modifications addressed the excision of N-terminal methionine and the carbamidomethylation of cysteine residues. Notably, we stringently controlled the false discovery rate (FDR) to ensure it remained below 1%. The Gene Ontology (GO) annotation process initiates with the application of eggnog-mapper software v2.1.12 to retrieve GO IDs for the identified proteins, utilizing information from the EggNOG database. After this, a functional classification analysis is conducted, categorizing the proteins into three main areas: cellular components, molecular functions, and biological processes. To annotate the protein pathways, we turn to the KEGG pathway database19 and perform BLAST (BLAST + 2.13.0) comparisons (specifically blastp, with an e-value threshold of ≤ 10− 4)20 to identify the proteins involved. The annotation for each sequence is based on the highest scoring comparison result. Additionally, we utilized the WolF PSORT software21 to predict the subcellular localization of eukaryotic cells. To evaluate the significance of functional enrichment among differentially expressed proteins, Fisher’s exact test was conducted with the identified proteins serving as the background. Functional terms that exhibited a fold enrichment greater than 1.5 and a p-value below 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Metabolomics studies

The sample preparation process for metabolomics studies begins with the careful thawing of cell samples that have been stored at −80 °C, ensuring they remain on ice during this time. Once thawed, the samples are homogenized for 20 s using a grinder. They are subsequently combined with 400 µL of a 4:1 (V/V) methanol-water solution containing an internal standard. This mixture is then vortexed for 3 min to achieve thorough mixing. To further improve the extraction, the samples are subjected to three freeze-thaw cycles, alternating between liquid nitrogen and dry ice, followed by additional vortexing. The next step involves centrifuging the samples at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, which allows for the collection of 300 µL of supernatant. After this, the supernatant is stored at −20 °C for 30 min before the second centrifugation process. For LC-MS analysis, a 200 µL aliquot is extracted from the supernatant. Each sample undergoes examination using two separate LC-MS techniques. The first sample is analyzed in positive ionization mode, employing a T3 column (Waters ACQUITY Premier HSS T3 Column, 2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.8 μm). The mobile phase is composed of 0.1% formic acid in water (solvent A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (solvent B). The gradient schedule begins at 5% B, progressing to 20% B over 2 min. It then increases to 60% B during the next 3 min, followed by a rapid rise to 99% B within 1 min. This concentration is maintained for 1.5 min before returning to 5% B in just 0.1 min, concluding with a 2.4-minute hold at this initial concentration. In this analysis, the column temperature is set at 40 °C, while the flow rate is maintained at 0.4 mL/min, and the injection volume is 4 µL. For the second sample, we employ negative ionization conditions, applying the same elution gradient used in the positive mode. Data is collected through information-dependent acquisition (IDA) mode, and the processing is carried out using Analyst TF 1.7.1 software (Sciex, Concord, ON, Canada). The ion source parameters are set as follows: Ion source gas 1 (GAS1) and gas 2 (GAS2) are both maintained at 50 psi, while the curtain gas (CUR) is set at 25 psi. The temperature (TEM) is configured to 550 °C. For the positive mode, the declustering potential (DP) is established at 60 V, whereas for the negative mode, it is calibrated to −60 V. Additionally, the ion spray voltage floating (ISVF) is adjusted to 5000 V for positive ionization and − 4000 V for negative ionization conditions. Regarding the Time-of-Flight (TOF) MS scan parameters, a mass range of 50–1000 Da is defined, with an accumulation time of 200 ms, and dynamic background subtraction is enabled. In the case of product ion scans, the parameters include a mass range of 25–1000 Da, an accumulation time of 40 ms, and collision energies set at 30 V for positive mode and − 30 V for negative mode. The collision energy spread is 15, and the resolution is set to UNIT. Furthermore, the charge state is limited to 1, and the intensity is configured to 100 counts per second (cps). Isotopes within 4 Da are excluded, with a mass tolerance of 50 ppm, and a maximum of 18 candidate ions are monitored per cycle. The data file originally acquired from LC-MS was transformed into mzXML format through the use of ProteoWizard software. The XCMS program was utilized for peak extraction, alignment, and retention time correction. To correct the peak area, the “SVR” method was applied. Peaks that exhibited a detection rate below 50% in each sample group were removed. Following this, information for metabolic identification was sourced from the laboratory’s custom database22, along with integrated public databases such as HMDB, Metlin, MassBank, Mona, and KEGG, as well as from AI databases23 and metDNA24. Differential metabolites are detected by assessing Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) values (where VIP > 1) in combination with P-values (P < 0.05, Student’s t-test) through the method of Orthogonal Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA). The annotation of identified metabolites was performed using the KEGG Compound database (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/compound/), after which these annotated metabolites were mapped to the KEGG Pathway database (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/pathway.html). Pathways that are significantly enriched were determined using the P-value from a hypergeometric test applied to the specified list of metabolites. To explore the connections between differentially expressed proteins and metabolites within specific pathways, distinct KEGG pathway annotation analyses are performed for both differentially expressed proteins and metabolites. This approach ultimately reveals pathways that contain both differentially expressed proteins and metabolites, facilitating a deeper understanding of their interactions.

Evaluation of intracellular ATP levels

Intracellular ATP levels were assessed by culturing cells overnight in YPD medium (1% yeast extract, 2% dextrose, 2% peptone) at 30 °C with shaking at 150 rpm. ATP concentrations were measured using the BacTiter-Glo Microbial Cell Viability Assay Kit (Promega, USA) and evaluated with a microplate reader (TECAN Infinite E Plex). Each experimental condition was performed in triplicate for statistical robustness. Statistical analysis was conducted using the relative ATP levels compared to the WT, calculated as the average ± standard deviation (SD) of the triplicate measurements, with the WT average normalized to 1. A t-test was employed to assess significance.

Results

Proteome and metabolomics analyses of mutant hsl101Δ

The Hsl101 kinase plays a pivotal role in the virulence of C. neoformans, particularly in lung and brain infections13. This study investigates the effects of HSL101 deletion on the proteomic and metabolomic profiles of C. neoformans. We employed data-independent acquisition (DIA)-based quantitative proteomics to compare the protein expression profiles between the WT and hsl101Δ mutant strains, successfully identifying differentially expressed proteins. In addition to proteomic analysis, we performed untargeted metabolomics to evaluate changes in metabolite levels resulting from the deletion. Our proteomic analysis identified a total of 4931 proteins from the 742925 present in C. neoformans. Significant changes in protein expression were determined based on expression ratios lower than 0.67 or higher than 1.5, with a significance threshold set at t-test probabilities of less than 0.05. An overview of these findings is provided in Table S2. The deletion of HSL101 led to differential expression of proteins corresponding to 366 genes, comprising 209 upregulated and 157 downregulated proteins (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Data 1). GO analysis indicated that these regulated proteins are involved in a variety of biological processes, such as cellular metabolic processes, organic compound metabolism, primary metabolic activities, and nitrogen compound metabolism. Further categorization of the proteins was based on their cellular components and molecular functions (Fig. 1B). The subcellular localization of the differentially expressed proteins, including those associated with mitochondria, plasma membranes, nuclei, cytoplasm, extracellular spaces, and cytoskeletons, is depicted in Fig. 1C. KEGG pathway analysis demonstrated that these regulated proteins are involved in several key metabolic pathways, including oxidative phosphorylation (Fig. 1D). On the metabolomics front, Fig. 1A summarizes the observed changes in metabolite levels following HSL101 deletion. This deletion resulted in distinct metabolic profiles characterized by 421 individual alterations, with 124 metabolites showing increased levels and 297 exhibiting decreased levels. The KEGG enrichment analysis for the differentially expressed metabolites is presented in Fig. 1E, reinforcing the involvement of regulated proteins in various metabolic pathways, including oxidative phosphorylation. These findings indicate that Hsl101 significantly influences both the expression of mitochondrial proteins and the levels of associated metabolites. Figure 1C and F, along with Table S3, illustrate the subcellular localization of these differentially expressed proteins and the corresponding metabolite levels, particularly those related to mitochondria. Figure 1F emphasizes mitochondrial proteins and metabolites that are differentially expressed and are crucial for key cellular processes such as oxidative phosphorylation, purine metabolism, and the TCA cycle. Mitochondria are recognized as the primary sites for ATP synthesis within cells, with ATP generation closely linked to oxidative phosphorylation, purine metabolism, and the TCA cycle. Oxidative phosphorylation serves as the key mechanism for ATP production, while the TCA cycle generates reducing equivalents (NADH and FADH₂). Additionally, purine metabolism plays an essential role in ATP synthesis necessary for various cellular functions. The interplay among these components is critical for efficient cellular energy metabolism. Our experiments revealed a substantial increase in ATP levels following the knockout of HSL101, as demonstrated in Fig. 2. This increase suggests a complex relationship between Hsl101 and mitochondrial function. Overall, the knockout of HSL101 significantly affects both protein expression and metabolite profiles in C. neoformans, suggesting that it may regulate the levels of these proteins and metabolites.

Proteome and metabolomics analyses of mutant urk1Δ

Urk1, much like Hsl101, is integral to key signaling pathways linked to virulence in C. neoformans13. To gain deeper insights into the regulatory functions of this kinase, we conducted extensive proteomic and metabolomic analyses on the urk1Δ mutant. Our proteomic assessment identified a total of 4,931 proteins from the 7,429 proteins25 present in C. neoformans. Significant changes in protein expression were established with ratios falling below 0.67 or exceeding 1.5, using a significance threshold defined by t-test probabilities of less than 0.05. A summary of the differences observed is provided in Table S2. The disruption of URK1 resulted in differential expression of proteins corresponding to 236 genes, of which 134 proteins were upregulated while 102 were downregulated (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Data 1). GO analysis of these proteins indicated their participation in a variety of biological processes, including cellular metabolic processes, organic substance metabolism, primary metabolic activities, and nitrogen compound metabolism. Furthermore, the proteins were classified based on their cellular components and molecular functions (Fig. 3B). The subcellular localization of the differentially expressed proteins—spanning the nucleus, mitochondria, cytoplasm, plasma membranes, and cytoskeletons—is illustrated in Fig. 3C. KEGG pathway analysis indicated that the regulated proteins are involved in several metabolic pathways, including steroid biosynthesis, the pentose phosphate pathway, and starch and sucrose metabolism (Fig. 3D). In terms of metabolomic changes, Fig. 3A provides a summary of the alterations in metabolite levels following URK1 deletion. This knockout resulted in distinctive metabolic profiles characterized by 366 individual changes, with 184 metabolites exhibiting increased levels and 182 showing decreased levels. KEGG enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed metabolites is depicted in Fig. 3E and F, highlighting pathways such as starch and sucrose metabolism, and steroid biosynthesis. Moreover, our experiments did not detect any significant changes in ATP levels following the knockout of URK1 (Fig. 2). Overall, the knockout of URK1 profoundly influences both protein expression and metabolite levels in C. neoformans, implying that it may play a role in regulating the levels of these proteins and metabolites.

Discussion

In this study, we examined the alterations in the proteomic and metabolomic profiles of C. neoformans following the deletion of the kinase Hsl101 or Urk1. Our findings indicate that the absence of these kinases leads to significant changes in protein expression and metabolite levels, suggesting that they may play a role in regulating the levels of these proteins and metabolites. However, it is important to note that the observed changes in proteins and metabolites may not be directly caused by the lack of these kinases, as compensatory mechanisms could also be at play.

The regulatory functions of Hsl101 may influence C. neoformans infection by affecting mitochondrial dynamics. This aligns with previous studies that implicate mitochondrial metabolism in the virulence of pathogenic fungi11,26,27. The significant increase in ATP levels observed upon HSL101 gene deletion supports the notion that this kinase may help fine-tune mitochondrial function and energy production under stress conditions. Additionally, the involvement of Urk1 in regulating steroid biosynthesis and carbohydrate metabolism correlates with prior research emphasizing the importance of these pathways in the pathogenicity of C. neoformans. Steroids are vital for maintaining membrane integrity and facilitating the fungal response to host environments28–30, while carbohydrate metabolism provides the necessary substrates for energy and biosynthetic processes31–33. Understanding the metabolic pathways regulated by these kinases may help to understand the molecular mechanisms of the C. neoformans pathogenicity. Hsl101, a protein kinase belonging to the CAMK/CAMKL/GIN4 family25, and Urk1, an uridine kinase25, play crucial roles in regulating the virulence of the fungal pathogen C. neoformans13,34. C. neoformans employs complex signaling pathways that govern various biological processes essential for brain infection13. Notably, Hsl101 is involved in cell cycle regulation, which is vital for the fungal ability to penetrate the blood-brain barrier13. Both Hsl101 and Urk1 are also key components of yet-to-be-defined pathways that are critical for BBB penetration. In terms of survival within the brain parenchyma, Hsl101 not only regulates the cell cycle but also influences cellular morphology13. Our findings indicate that Hsl101 impacts various metabolic pathways at the protein level, including steroid biosynthesis, phenylalanine metabolism, starch and sucrose metabolism, tyrosine metabolism, and oxidative phosphorylation, ultimately affecting intracellular ATP levels. These alterations in metabolic pathways may provide further insight into the mechanisms by which Hsl101 regulates cell cycle dynamics and morphology. In parallel, Urk1 contributes to the pyrimidine ribonucleotide salvage pathway13, which is essential for nucleotide synthesis and overall cellular function. Our results demonstrate that Urk1 plays a significant role in several metabolic pathways, including steroid biosynthesis, the pentose phosphate pathway, and starch and sucrose metabolism.

In a systematic analysis conducted by Lee and colleagues, the functions of Hsl101 and Urk1 were further elucidated by examining various phenotypic changes following gene deletion34. They assessed traits such as growth at different temperatures, capsule and melanin production, urease activity, and responses to oxidative stress and drug sensitivity. The mutant hsl101Δ displayed increased capsule production and reduced urease production, alongside sensitivity to certain stressors, indicating complex stress response pathways. The mutant urk1Δ exhibited heightened sensitivity to elevated temperatures and oxidative agents, yet increased resistance to 5-flucytosine, reflecting potentially conflicting roles in stress adaptation and drug resistance34. These inconclusive results underscore the necessity for further investigation into the intricate functions of these kinases.

We acknowledge that there are limitations to this study. Firstly, we did not conduct phosphorylation experiments, which restricts our understanding of the relevant signaling pathways and their regulatory mechanisms. Additionally, we have not yet identified proteins that interact with the kinases Hsl101 or Urk1, leaving significant directions for future research. Future studies should focus on performing phosphorylation experiments to explore the specific roles of these mutant strains in signal transduction. Additionally, methods such as mass spectrometry could be employed to identify other proteins that interact with Hsl101 or Urk1. This would help us gain a more comprehensive understanding of their biological functions and potential regulatory mechanisms. Such experiments will aid in further unraveling the complexity of these kinases’ roles within the cell. Moreover, future research efforts should also focus on successfully creating complemented strains, as our previous attempts were unsuccessful.

Conclusion

Our study enhances the understanding of the complex regulatory networks of C. neoformans. By elucidating the proteomic and metabolomic changes associated with HSL101 and URK1 deletion, we emphasize the importance of these kinases in metabolism. Targeting these pathways may offer promising strategies to improve outcomes for patients with cryptococcal infections as research progresses.
