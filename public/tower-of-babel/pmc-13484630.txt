Nuclear Factor Erythroid 2-Related Factor 2 in Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease Progression: Regulation of Macrophage Polarization and Hyaluronic Acid Metabolism
Authors: Ge Bai, Jiaqi Wang, Qianmei Wen, Yuning Huang, Haiqing Li, Yanhui Xu, Yiyi Liu, Erkang Yi, Fan Wu, Jieda Cui, Xinyue Mei, Yuxia Zhang, Yumin Zhou, Ruiting Sun, Pixin Ran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlveolar microenvironment-driven macrophage polarization critically influences chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) progression. Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2), a key transcription factor, regulates macrophage polarization, suppresses inflammation, and promotes cellular repair. However, its role in macrophage-mediated inflammation and metabolic regulation, particularly hyaluronic acid (HA)-related remodeling, remains incompletely defined. This study investigates a novel dimension of NRF2 in COPD pathogenesis through macrophage-specific modulation, aiming to uncover its previously unrecognized mechanism.MethodsMacrophage-specific Nrf2 knockout (KO) and control mice were chronically exposed to cigarette smoke (CS) to induce COPD-like pathology. Lung function, alveolar damage, macrophage polarization (M1/M2), and inflammatory cytokines were analyzed by respiratory function tests, histology, immunohistochemistry, and chemokine profiling, respectively. Proteomics approaches were used to identify dysregulated pathways.ResultsNrf2 KO mice displayed aggravated CS-induced lung dysfunction and alveolar destruction compared with control mice, accompanied by heightened M1 polarization and elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines (C–C motif chemokine ligand [CCL]2, CCL7, CXCL16). Proteomics revealed dysregulation of immune, oxidative stress, and metabolic pathways in the lungs of KO mice, including HA metabolism/uptake-associated proteins and increased CD44/CD74 expression. These findings suggest an association between NRF2 deficiency, macrophage polarization imbalance, and HA-related remodeling.ConclusionMacrophage-specific NRF2 protects against CS-induced COPD-like lung injury by restraining inflammatory activation and preserving macrophage polarization-associated balance. Proteomic and validation analyses further nominate HA metabolism-related remodeling, particularly the CD44/CD74-associated response, as a biologically plausible feature of NRF2-deficient disease progression. These findings define a macrophage NRF2-linked inflammatory-remodeling axis in COPD-like pathology while highlighting the need for functional studies to establish whether HA/CD44 signaling is a causal downstream mechanism.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), a highly prevalent and heterogeneous respiratory disorder, is primarily characterized by persistent airflow limitation.1,2 Smoking is unanimously acknowledged as a dominant risk factor for COPD.3 Extended exposure to cigarette smoke (CS) damages the lung extracellular matrix (ECM), a fundamental component of the pulmonary structure.4 From a histological perspective, ECM damage gives rise to two typical manifestations: emphysema, caused by the destruction of alveoli and the subsequent reduction in surface area, and chronic bronchitis, stemming from continuous airway inflammation.4,5 These phenomena not only illustrate the intricate pathophysiology of COPD, but they also underscore the critical role of smoking in its etiology.

Macrophages are of utmost importance in COPD progression.6 Their functional capacity and polarization states are intricately regulated by the alveolar microenvironment.7 In the context of COPD, macrophages trigger the inflammatory cascade and mediate lung tissue injury, participate in subsequent repair processes, and maintain the delicate equilibrium of the lung microenvironment.8,9 In the initial stages of COPD, macrophages predominantly adopt the M1 phenotype.10 This is marked by the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-6, and IL-12, accompanied by oxidants like reactive oxygen species and proteolytic enzymes, such as matrix metalloproteinase (MMP)-9 and MMP-12.11 These factors work in concert to intensify the inflammatory response in the lungs, facilitating the elimination of invading microbial pathogens. As COPD progresses, there is a distinct shift in macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype.12 M2 macrophages exert an anti-inflammatory effect, engaging in the phagocytosis of pathogenic microorganisms and apoptotic cellular debris and actively contributing to lung tissue repair.13 M2 macrophages secrete anti-inflammatory cytokines, particularly IL-10 and transforming growth factor-β, along with biosynthetic enzymes, such as MMP-12.14–16 Moreover, they express T helper 2-associated chemokines, such as C–C motif chemokine ligand (CCL) 17, CCL22, and CCL24, which are instrumental in immune response regulation and tissue remodeling.17–19

Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2) serves as a pivotal transcription factor, playing a central role in coordinating cellular responses to oxidative stress and inflammation.20,21 During COPD progression, NRF2 exerts a multi-faceted effect. It regulates macrophage polarization, suppresses inflammatory cascades, promotes cellular repair mechanisms, and exquisitely controls the immune response.22–24 These diverse functions collectively contribute to modulating the pathogenesis of COPD.

Beyond its canonical antioxidant function, NRF2 is increasingly viewed as a regulatory node linking cigarette smoke-induced oxidative stress to macrophage activation, inflammatory amplification, and tissue-remodeling responses in COPD. Experimental studies have shown that loss of Nrf2 increases susceptibility to cigarette smoke-induced emphysema-like injury, supporting a protective role for NRF2 in maintaining pulmonary homeostasis under chronic oxidative challenge.25 In the injured lung, extracellular matrix components are not only structural substrates but also dynamic immune signals.26,27 Hyaluronic acid (HA), a major nonsulfated glycosaminoglycan of the pulmonary extracellular matrix, is particularly sensitive to oxidative and enzymatic fragmentation. Its biological activity is highly size-dependent: high-molecular-weight HA generally supports tissue hydration, epithelial barrier integrity, and repair, whereas fragmented low-molecular-weight HA can function as a matrix-derived danger signal that promotes inflammatory responses through receptors such as CD44 and Toll-like receptors. CD44-dependent HA handling is also implicated in the resolution of lung inflammation, suggesting that disturbed HA turnover may contribute to persistent inflammatory remodeling.28 Clinically, serum HA levels have been associated with COPD severity and survival, and altered HA metabolism has been reported in COPD airway disease.29,30 However, the mechanisms by which HA homeostasis becomes dysregulated in COPD, and whether macrophage NRF2 participates in this process, remain incompletely defined. This provides the rationale for examining HA metabolism-related pathways in the context of macrophage-specific Nrf2 deficiency.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Animals

All animal procedures were conducted with approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Guangzhou Medical University. Age-matched macrophage-specific Nrf2 KO (Lyz2-Cre Nrf2f/f) and littermate control mice (Lyz2-Cre background) were obtained from the laboratory of Dr. Yuxia Zhang (Guangzhou Medical University). The mice were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions at the Animal Center of Yuexiu Campus, Guangzhou Medical University, with controlled environmental parameters, including a temperature of 22°C ± 1°C, humidity of 55% ± 10%, and 12-h/12-h light/dark cycle. At the conclusion of the experiments, all mice were humanely euthanized. Euthanasia was carried out via intraperitoneal injection of 1.25% Avertin (17.5 µL/g body weight, Meilunbio, China) to induce profound anesthesia, followed by cervical dislocation to confirm death. Every effort was made to minimize animal suffering throughout the study.

Cigarette Smoke (CS) Exposure System

Lyz2-Cre and Lyz2-Cre Nrf2f/f mice (planned n = 5 per genotype/group unless otherwise indicated in the figure legends) were randomized into filtered air or whole-body CS exposure groups. The air group was housed in HEPA-filtered isolators for 12 weeks. CS exposure was performed using Hongmei filters (China Tobacco Yunnan Industrial Co. Ltd, Yuxi City, Yunnan Province, China) with standardized emissions (10 mg tar, 12 mg carbon monoxide, and 0.8 mg nicotine per cigarette). A habituation protocol was used to progressively increase the exposure intensity over 2 weeks (weeks 1–2: 5 cigarettes/session, 30-min exposure, twice daily; weeks 3–12: 10 cigarettes/session, 60-min exposure, twice daily). These sessions were performed twice daily—once in the morning and once in the afternoon—with a 20-min break between the sessions and a 3-h gap between the morning and afternoon sessions. This routine was followed 6 days per week, with one day of rest. Post-exposure (12 weeks), lung tissues were harvested for downstream analyses. This protocol was used to model CS-induced emphysema-like COPD pathology in mice rather than the full clinical spectrum of human COPD.

Pulmonary Function Tests

Pulmonary function evaluation was conducted using the BUXCO FinePointe™ PFT Small Animal Respiratory System (Data Sciences International, USA) following standardized procedures. The mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal administration of Avertin (17.5 µL/g body weight) to achieve surgical plane anesthesia. After confirming adequate anesthesia through pedal reflex testing, a midline cervical tracheotomy was performed under aseptic conditions. The mice were then positioned in the supine orientation on a temperature-controlled surgical platform (37°C) and stabilized using non-constrictive silk sutures. Endotracheal intubation was meticulously executed using a 20-G angiocatheter connected to the system’s pressure-controlled ventilator. Real-time respiratory parameters were continuously monitored using FinePointe software (v.3.2) with data acquisition at 200 Hz. Three consecutive measurement cycles were performed for each animal following system calibration with reference standards.

Bronchoalveolar Lavage Fluid (BALF) Collection and Processing

BALF was obtained under controlled aseptic conditions following a standardized protocol. After euthanasia, the thoracic cavity was surgically exposed, and the left lung was ligated at the bronchial junction using polypropylene sutures (Ethicon, China) to isolate the right lung for lavage. A 22-G polyethylene catheter (BD Insyte™, China) was inserted into the trachea in a retrograde manner. The right lung was lavaged with six sequential aliquots of 0.4 mL ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Gibco, USA) pre-cooled to 4°C. Recovered lavage fluid was immediately transferred to pre-chilled 15 mL polypropylene tubes (Corning, USA) and centrifuged at 400 × g for 10 min at 4°C (ThermoFisher, USA). The supernatants were aliquoted and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, while cellular pellets were resuspended in 1 mL PBS for differential cell counting using the Cellometer AUTO1000 Bright Field Cell Counter (Nexcelom, USA) and Cell counting chambers (Nexcelom). All procedures were completed within 15 min post-euthanasia to minimize analyte degradation.

Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) Staining and Mean Linear Intercept (MLI) Analysis

Lung tissue processing was performed using standardized histomorphometric protocols. Following tracheal cannulation, the left lung was inflation-fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) in PBS (pH 7.4) under constant hydrostatic pressure (15 cmH2O) via a perfusion system. The fixed lung was ligated at the main bronchus, excised, and post-fixed in fresh 4°C PFA for 24 h. The tissues were then dehydrated using an ethanol gradient (70%–100%), cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin (Leica, Germany) using a tissue processor (Leica) with three wax infiltration cycles (60°C, 2 h per cycle). Serial sections (4 μm thickness) were cut using a rotary microtome (Leica) and mounted on charged slides.

For histopathological assessment, the sections were deparaffinized and stained using Mayer’s hematoxylin (10 min) and eosin Y (5 min). Whole-slide imaging was performed at 20× magnification (0.25 μm/pixel resolution) using the automatic digital panoramic scanning feature (Model PRECICE500B, UNIC, China). Using ImagePro Plus 6.0 software, we drew four horizontal and six vertical lines across each image, carefully recording the length and alveolar count along each line. The MLI was calculated as MLI (μm) = test line length (μm) ÷ number of alveolar intercepts. Vascular and bronchiolar regions were excluded by manual annotation.

Multiplexed Immunofluorescence Staining

Paraffin-embedded lung sections (4 μm) underwent multiplex staining using the PANO 4-plex IHC kit (Cat# 10079100100, Panovue, China). Sequential antigen retrieval was performed using microwave irradiation in citrate buffer (pH 6.0; BOSTER, China), followed by blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin (ThermoFisher). Primary antibodies targeting acetylated NRF2 (Cat# 14596S, Cell Signaling Technology, USA) and F4/80 (Cat# 70076T, Cell Signaling Technology) were applied with tyramide signal amplification (TSA) (horseradish peroxidase [HRP]/TSA system) between cycles. The nuclei were counterstained using DAPI. High-resolution three-dimensional imaging was performed using the Zeiss LSM880 confocal microscope (Zeiss Axiovert, Germany) with ZEN software (v.3.5), using 405/488/561/633 nm laser lines and the 20×/0.8 numerical aperture objective.

Cytokine Assays

BALF supernatants, obtained by centrifugation (400 × g, 10 min, 4°C), were analyzed using the Bio-Plex Pro Mouse Chemokine 31-Plex Assay (Cat# 12009159, Bio-Rad, USA). Aliquots (60 μL) and serially diluted standards were loaded into 96-well plates with 50 μL pre-mixed magnetic beads. After automated washing (Bio-Plex Wash Station), the samples and blanks (50 μL/well) were transferred to the assay plate and incubated under agitation (60 min, room temperature). Detection antibodies (25 μL/well, 30 min) and streptavidin-phycoerythrin (50 μL/well, 10 min) were sequentially added. Fluorescence signals were quantified using a Bio-Plex 200 system (Luminex xMAP®), and the analyte concentrations were calculated using Bio-Plex Manager™ v.6.2 using five-parameter logistic regression.

RNA Extraction and Reverse Transcription Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-qPCR) Analysis

Total RNA was isolated from snap-frozen murine lungs using the EZB Total RNA Kit (EZBioscience, USA). RNA quality (260/280 and 260/230) was verified using the NanoDrop 2000 (ThermoFisher, Germany). Reverse transcription was performed using 1 μg RNA with the PrimeScript™ RT Kit (Cat# AG11728, AG, China). SYBR Green qPCR was performed using the CFX Real-Time PCR Detection System (Model 1855200, Bio-Rad) with the following settings: 95°C for 30s, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 5s and 60°C for 30s. Melting curves (65°C–95°C, 0.5°C increments) confirmed specificity. The reactions (20 μL), which were performed in triplicate, contained 2× SYBR Premix Ex Taq™ (Cat# AG11701, AG), 0.2 μM primers, and 2 μL complementary DNA. The data were analyzed using the ΔΔCT method normalized to 18S rRNA. The primers (Supplementary Table 1) were designed using Primer-BLAST (amplicons: 80–150 base pairs; efficiency: 90%–110%) and analyzed using CFX Maestro™ Software v.2.3 (Bio-Rad).

Western Blot Analysis

Snap-frozen murine lung tissues were homogenized in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer (CWBIO, China) containing a protease/phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (ThermoFisher) using a cryogenic homogenizer (Model LUKYM-I/II/III, LUKA, China) at 70 Hz for four 20-s cycles (−20°C). The lysates were incubated on ice for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min (4°C) to collect the supernatants. The protein concentrations were determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (ThermoFisher). Proteins (20 μg per lane) were denatured in loading buffer (CWBIO) (95°C, 10 min), separated by 10% SDS-PAGE, and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Bio-Rad). The membranes were blocked using 5% non-fat milk/Tris-buffered saline with Tween-20 (TBST) and incubated overnight (4°C) with primary antibodies against NRF2 (Cat# 14596S, Cell Signaling Technology) and β-actin (Cat# 66009-1-Ig; Proteintech, USA) After washing with TBST, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology) for 2 h at room temperature. The protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence substrate (Model Tanon 5200, Tanon, China) and quantified using Image J software (NIH).

Flow Cytometry Analysis

Cells were isolated from the BALF by centrifugation (400 × g, 10 min, 4°C) and resuspended in RPMI 1640 (ThermoFisher) and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, USA). The cells were stained using fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies for 30 min at 4°C in the dark. The antibodies targeted CD45 (Cat# 103111, BioLegend, USA), CD11b (Cat# 552850, BD Biosciences, USA), CD11c (Cat# 117343, BioLegend, USA), CD64 (Cat# 139304, BioLegend, USA), CD24 (Cat# 101806, BioLegend, USA), and major histocompatibility complex class II (MHC-II) (Cat# 107622, BioLegend, USA). After washing with PBS, the cells were analyzed by flow cytometry (Model BD LSRFortessa™ X-20, BD Biosciences, USA) with fluorescence compensation using UltraComp eBeads (ThermoFisher). Live cells were gated by forward scatter/side scatter to exclude debris and doublets. Leukocytes were identified as CD45+, myeloid cells as CD11b+, monocytes as CD11c−/CD64+, and macrophages as CD11c+/CD64+. Subpopulations were further stratified by CD24/MHC-II expression. The data were processed using FlowJo v.10.8 (BD Biosciences) and expressed as a percentage of CD45+ leukocytes (mean ± standard error of the mean [SEM]).

Immunohistochemistry

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue sections (4 μm) were deparaffinized in xylene (Newprobe, China) and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Heat-mediated antigen retrieval was performed using citrate buffer (pH 6.0; BOSTER) in a microwave oven. After endogenous peroxidase blocking with 3% hydrogen peroxide, the sections were incubated with 5% normal serum to prevent non-specific binding. Incubation with primary antibodies against CD206 (Cat# 24595S, Cell Signaling Technology) and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) (Cat# 13120S, Cell Signaling Technology) was conducted overnight at 4°C in a humidified chamber. After washing with PBS, the sections were treated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Chromogenic detection was achieved using 3,3’-diaminobenzidine substrates, followed by hematoxylin (Solarbio, China) counterstaining. All slides were dehydrated using a graded alcohol series, cleared in xylene, and permanently mounted for bright-field microscopy analysis.

In vitro Experiment

THP-1 monocytes were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin under standard conditions (37°C, 5% carbon dioxide). The cells were maintained in suspension culture (2 × 105–1 × 106 cells/mL) and passaged every 2–3 days. For macrophage differentiation, 5×105 cells/mL were plated into 6-well plates and exposed to 100 ng/mL phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) for 48 h. After PMA removal, the cells were incubated for an additional 24 h to stabilize macrophage morphology. Successful differentiation was validated through adherent morphology observation. Differentiated macrophages were challenged with CS extract for 48 h. CS extract was prepared by bubbling CS through RPMI 1640 medium using a smoke-collection apparatus, followed by filtration (0.22 μm pore size) and standardization of absorbance at 320 nm (adjusting to 0.8 ± 0.05 optical density units). Total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer for Western blot analysis.

Proteomics Analysis

Lung tissues from four groups of experimental mice (n = 3 biological replicates/group) were homogenized in lysis buffer. Protein concentrations were quantified using a BCA assay (Pierce™, Thermo Fisher). The samples were digested using trypsin (1:50 w/w, 37°C, 16 h) and desalted using C18 StageTips. Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry was performed at Shenzhen BGI Tech Co. Ltd. (China) using the Q-Exactive HF-X system (Thermo Fisher) coupled with an EASY-nLC 1200 HPLC system. A data-dependent acquisition (DDA) approach first generated a spectral library (2 μg sample load, 300–1800 m/z, 60-min gradient). Subsequent data-independent acquisition (DIA) runs (40 variable windows) enabled quantitative profiling. DIA data were processed using Spectronaut™ 15 (Biognosys AG, Switzerland) with the DDA-derived spectral library. The MSstats package of R was used to normalize the intensity values and apply linear mixed-effects modeling with comparative group contrasts. Differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were defined by |log2(fold change)|>1 and Benjamini–Hochberg-adjusted P < 0.05. Functional annotation of DEPs was performed using STRING (v.11.5) using the Gene Ontology (GO) and Reactome pathway databases.

Statistical Analyses

All analyses were performed using SPSS, v.29.0 (SPSS Inc, USA). Continuous data are expressed as the mean ± SEM, where n represents biological replicates. Multiple-group comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for pairwise comparisons when the overall ANOVA was significant. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05, with asterisks denoting specific P-values, as follows: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001. For proteomics, differentially expressed proteins were defined by |log2(fold change) | > 1 and Benjamini-Hochberg-adjusted P < 0.05.

Results

Macrophage-Specific Nrf2 Deficiency Exacerbates CS-Induced Pulmonary Dysfunction and Alveolar Destruction in a Mice COPD-Like Model

We established a CS-induced mice COPD-like model through standardized chronic CS exposure (Figure 1a). To investigate the macrophage-specific role of NRF2 in COPD pathogenesis, we utilized two genetic models: Lyz2-Cre mice as controls and macrophage-specific Nrf2 KO mice (Lyz2-Cre Nrf2f/f). Both groups were subjected to identical CS exposure protocols to induce emphysema-like pathology. Following chronic CS exposure, macrophage-specific Nrf2 KO mice displayed worse lung function than CS-exposed controls, as evidenced by spirometric measurements (Figure 1b). This functional impairment supports a role of NRF2 in preserving pulmonary homeostasis during CS-induced lung injury. Consistent with these findings, histological evaluation using H&E staining demonstrated exacerbated alveolar wall destruction in Nrf2-deficient mice compared with controls (Figure 1c and d). Collectively, these data suggest that NRF2 contributes to macrophage-associated protection against CS-induced lung functional decline and structural damage.

Macrophage-Specific Nrf2 Deficiency Suppresses Phase II Detoxification and Antioxidant Enzyme Expression via Antioxidant-Response Element (ARE) Signaling

To elucidate the regulatory role of macrophage NRF2 in detoxification pathways, we assessed the expression of canonical ARE-regulated genes encoding phase II detoxification enzymes and antioxidant proteins. Immunofluorescence and qPCR analyses revealed that CS exposure significantly reduced both the mRNA and protein expression of NRF2 in lung tissues, particularly within alveolar macrophages, compared with air-exposed controls (Figure 2a and b). This observation was corroborated in CS extract-treated THP-1 macrophages, which showed diminished NRF2 protein expression (Figure 2c). qPCR revealed significant downregulation of canonical ARE-regulated genes in CS-exposed lungs, including phase II detoxification enzymes (Gstm1, Gstm2, Ugtla6a, Ugtla6b) and antioxidant mediators (Aox3). Macrophage-specific Nrf2 KO did not exacerbate these transcriptional changes, with no significant differences observed between KO and control mice following CS exposure (Figure 2d). This paradoxical observation suggests two potential mechanistic explanations. First, compensatory NRF2 activation in non-macrophage pulmonary cells (eg, epithelial cells) may maintain ARE-driven gene expression.22 Second, non-canonical regulatory mechanisms may mediate the effects of NRF2 on macrophage function, potentially through post-translational enzyme stabilization or redox-sensitive signaling crosstalk. Macrophage-Specific Nrf2 Deficiency Dysregulates M1/M2 Polarization Balance in COPD Pathogenesis

In this CS-induced mice COPD-like model, macrophage-specific Nrf2 KO did not alter pulmonary macrophage infiltration (Figure 3a–d), but it disrupted polarization-associated marker patterns. Using multiplex immunohistochemical analysis of lung sections from four groups, we quantified polarization markers, including iNOS+ for M1-associated macrophages and CD206+ for M2-associated macrophages. Chronic CS exposure increased both iNOS+ and CD206+ populations in control mice, which may reflect mixed activation states and microenvironment-driven phenotypic plasticity rather than a simple linear M1-to-M2 transition. Macrophage-specific Nrf2 deficiency enhanced M1-associated markers while suppressing M2-associated markers (Figure 3e–g), accompanied by upregulated M1-polarization signature genes (Nos2) and downregulated M2-polarization signature genes (Arg1) compared with the CS-exposed control group (Figure 3h and i). These findings support a role for NRF2 in maintaining macrophage polarization-associated balance under CS exposure. Macrophage-Specific Nrf2 Deficiency Exacerbates CS-Driven Pulmonary Cytokine and Chemokine Imbalance in Mice COPD-Like Pathology

To delineate the regulatory effects of macrophage Nrf2 on inflammatory mediators in COPD-like pathogenesis, we systematically quantified cytokines and chemokines in BALF from CS-exposed mice. Targeted profiling showed that macrophage-specific Nrf2 knockout amplified the pulmonary inflammatory response, with increased CCL2, CCL7, CXCL16, CCL3, CCL4, GM-CSF and IL-6 in KO_Sm mice compared with smoke-exposed controls, while the heatmap captured broader cytokine remodeling across the four groups (Figure 4a and b). The observed imbalance between pro-inflammatory chemotactic factors and immunomodulatory cytokines supports a role for NRF2 in maintaining pulmonary immune homeostasis during environmental insult. Nrf2 Deficiency Alters the Pulmonary Proteome in Macrophage-Specific KO Mice

To investigate the effects of macrophage Nrf2 deficiency on pulmonary protein profiles in CS-exposed mice, we performed quantitative proteomics. Principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated group-level separation among control, KO_Con, Sm and KO_Sm samples (Figure 5a). Comparative analysis identified 203 DEPs associated with macrophage Nrf2 deficiency under CS exposure (Figure 5b), comprising 114 upregulated and 89 downregulated proteins (Figure 5c). GO enrichment revealed that these DEPs predominantly localized to lysosomes, ECM, and MHC-II complexes (cellular component), exhibiting molecular functions, including hydrolytic enzyme activity, glutathione/iron ion binding, and MHC-II-mediated antigen processing (Supplementary Figure 1). Reactome pathway enrichment analysis identified pathways including biological oxidation, glycosphingolipid metabolism, hyaluronic acid (HA) metabolism/uptake and degradation, and neutrophil degranulation (Figure 5d). In COPD-related lung remodeling, high-molecular-weight HA is generally considered protective or anti-inflammatory, whereas fragmented low-molecular-weight HA can promote inflammatory signaling.31,32 Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis of Nrf2-centered DEPs identified candidate nodes linked to oxidative, inflammatory and metabolic responses (Figure 5e). Intersection of the Nrf2-centered PPI core with HA metabolism-related proteins identified CD44 as a candidate node linking NRF2 deficiency with HA-related pathway changes (Figure 5f). Consistently, multiplex immunofluorescence and qPCR validation showed increased CD44/CD74 signals and Cd44/Cd74 expression after CS exposure, with the strongest response in KO_Sm mice (Figure 5g–i). These findings indicate an association between macrophage Nrf2 deficiency and HA receptor-related remodeling, but additional functional studies are needed to establish causality. Discussion

In this study, we used mice models to clarify the previously unsubstantiated role of NRF2 in modulating macrophage function and its subsequent impact on COPD progression. Our results highlight the diverse and far-reaching influence of NRF2 as a key regulator of macrophage polarization and inflammation within the lung microenvironment. The Nrf2 gene regulates protein expression in lung tissues, which is essential for various biological processes, including the immune response, oxidative stress adaptation, and metabolic regulation. Notably, our study produced a potentially novel discovery regarding the role of HA metabolism in macrophages and its implications in the context of COPD.

Through the generation of macrophage-specific Nrf2 KO mice exposed to CS, this study demonstrated that NRF2 plays a pivotal role in CS-induced lung function impairment and alveolar wall destruction. Nrf2-deficient mice exhibited more severe deterioration in lung function indicators and increased alveolar wall damage than control mice under CS exposure. These findings support a protective function of NRF2 in maintaining lung integrity and function under COPD-like pathological conditions.

Our study provides insight into macrophage-specific NRF2 signaling in CS-induced COPD-like pathology and highlights candidate pathways for future mechanistic work. Future research should determine whether NRF2 regulates macrophage polarization through non-canonical mechanisms, including NF-kB p65 nuclear translocation, protein-protein interactions, redox-independent signaling, or metabolic reprogramming, and should test whether HA fragmentation or CD44/CD74 signaling functionally mediates the observed inflammatory phenotype.

As a pivotal transcriptional regulator, NRF2 could modulate the transcription of over 250 genes.33 It exerts its functions by orchestrating macrophage oxidative metabolic reprogramming, enhancing the efficiency of bacterial clearance,23,34 and inhibiting macrophage polarization toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype. Conversely, NRF2 promotes macrophage polarization toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype35–37 and alleviates the inflammatory response of macrophages by suppressing the transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines.22

This study, through the generation of CS-induced murine COPD-like models with Nrf2 KO specifically in macrophages, demonstrated that Nrf2 plays a crucial role in CS-induced lung function impairment and alveolar wall destruction. Macrophage-specific Nrf2 KO mice showed significantly deteriorated lung function indicators and increased alveolar wall damage compared with control mice, highlighting the protective function of NRF2 in maintaining lung integrity and function under COPD-related pathological conditions. Our results suggest that NRF2 deficiency in macrophages accelerates the progression of inflammation, not by changing the total number of lung macrophages, but by promoting the transformation of macrophages to the M1 phenotype upon CS exposure. This leads to the secretion of more pro-inflammatory factors and a reduction in anti-inflammatory factors. HA, a linear, unbranched polysaccharide, is essential for maintaining ECM hydration, regulating tissue homeostasis and stress resistance, and forming pericellular coatings.38 In these coatings, HA acts as a signaling molecule, interacting with specific binding proteins to modulate cell adhesion, migration, and proliferation.39 Notably, HA has different inflammatory effects depending on its molecular weight; high-molecular-weight HA is pro-inflammatory, while low-molecular-weight HA is anti-inflammatory.40,41

Recent studies have emphasized the significant role of HA and its degradation products in pulmonary pathophysiology and airway remodeling in COPD. HA and its degrading enzyme, hyaluronidase-1, are associated with the severity and clinical outcomes of COPD.29 The present study suggests that macrophage-specific Nrf2 deficiency is associated with HA metabolism/uptake pathway changes and increased Cd44/Cd74 expression. NRF2 may influence macrophage polarization by affecting HA receptor-related signaling involving CD44 and CD74.42,43 However, this proposed relationship has not been functionally validated in the current study, and causal interpretation will require HA quantification, molecular-weight profiling, pathway blockade, rescue experiments, or in vitro functional assays.

Study limitations should be acknowledged. First, this work used a CS-induced murine COPD-like model and therefore may best reflect emphysema-like injury rather than the full heterogeneity of human COPD. Second, the applicability of these findings across sex, COPD severity, and non-cigarette exposures such as air pollution or occupational pollutants remains uncertain. Third, macrophage polarization was inferred mainly from tissue marker staining and qPCR rather than single cell flow cytometric polarization profiling. Fourth, the HA-related mechanism remains circumstantial because HA abundance, molecular-weight distribution, HA synthesis/degradation enzymes, and HA fragment bioactivity were not directly measured.

Conclusion

In a CS-induced mice COPD-like model, macrophage-specific Nrf2 deficiency aggravated lung dysfunction, alveolar destruction, macrophage polarization-associated imbalance, and inflammatory mediator dysregulation. Proteomic and qPCR findings further suggest an association between NRF2 deficiency and HA metabolism/uptake-related pathways, including Cd44/Cd74 expression. These data support NRF2 as a protective regulator of macrophage-associated lung injury while identifying HA-CD44/CD74 signaling as a candidate mechanism that requires direct functional validation.
