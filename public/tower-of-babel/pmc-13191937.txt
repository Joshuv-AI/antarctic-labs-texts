Organizational readiness and program sustainability within juvenile justice and community mental health: the mediating role of cross-system collaboration
Authors: Lauren M. O’Reilly, Allyson Dir, Katherine Schwartz, Yoonsang Kim, Mateusz Borowiecki, Bruce Taylor, Zachary W. Adams, Tamika Zapolski, Leslie Hulvershorn, Matthew C. Aalsma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEvidence-based system-level intervention is needed to address the problem of poor connection to behavioral health (BH) services among justice-involved youth. System preparedness to implement evidence-based practices (EBP) or interventions, i.e. “organizational readiness,” is theoretically predictive of the sustainability of these practices. Cross-system collaboration—crucial for the connection of justice-involved youth between juvenile justice (JJ) to BH agencies—is theoretically related to organizational readiness and EBP sustainability, as the process of EBP implementation requires individual and shared efforts from involved systems. Despite strong theoretical justification, there has been minimal quantitative investigation into the relationships among organizational readiness, program sustainability, and cross-system collaboration. The current paper aimed to examine a) the association between organizational readiness and EBP sustainability, and b) the mediational role of cross-system collaboration in that association.MethodsData were derived from a hybrid type II effectiveness-implementation trial in eight counties in one Midwest state of partnered JJ and community mental health center (CMHC) agencies. Throughout the study, JJ and CMHC personnel participated in five waves of survey data collection measuring organizational readiness (Organizational Readiness to Implement Change; ORIC), sustainability (Program Sustainability Assessment Tool; PSAT), and cross-system collaboration (Cultural Exchange Inventory process and output scales; CEI). The final analyzable dataset included 274 participants with 529 observations. Linear mixed-effects models were conducted to account for the cluster-randomized, stepped-wedge design and repeated participant measurement.ResultsOrganizational readiness was significantly positively associated with program sustainability (B = 0.79, 95% Confidence Interval [CI] 0.68–0.90), and the association was weakened when including the mediator CEI process subscale (B = 0.69, 95% CI 0.58–0.80). CEI process was significantly associated with program sustainability (B = 0.21, 95% CI 0.15–0.28) and accounted for 17.4% of the association between organizational readiness and sustainability. Results were comparable when examining the second mediating subscale, CEI output, which accounted for 17.2% of the observed association between organizational readiness and sustainability.ConclusionsResults supported the findings that organizational readiness was positively associated with sustainability, with cross-system collaboration partially mediating the association. Although our study is observational, cross-system collaboration may serve as a promising intervention target to rigorously examine in future implementation studies between JJ and BH agencies.Trial RegistrationThe study was preregistered as a clinical trial (Clinicaltrials.gov identifier: NCT04499079) on 30 July 2020.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s43058-026-00919-w.

Background

Substance use among justice-involved youth

Justice-involved youth exhibit significantly higher rates of substance use disorders (SUD) compared to peers who are not involved in the legal system (lifetime prevalence of 62% compared to 29%) [1, 2]. A large randomized controlled trial of 33 sites designed to reduce gaps in SUD care access for justice-involved youth found that half of youth referred to the justice system were indicated as “in need” of SUD treatment [3]. Early SUD identification and intervention is critical, as earlier onset, heavier substance use, and polysubstance use are associated with SUD persistence into adulthood [4–6]. Substance use among justice-involved populations is also associated with greater risk for recidivism [7].

Despite the well-documented need for addiction services among justice-involved youth, significant barriers to accessing treatment exist. Among justice-involved youth who are identified as needing SUD treatment, fewer than half were referred to treatment (31–47%) or initiated treatment (21–52%) [3]. Such gaps in care are not unique to SUD treatment. Approximately one third of justice-involved youth with psychiatric symptoms reported using psychiatric treatment services [8]. The low rates of referrals for behavioral health (BH; substance use or mental health) treatment from the juvenile justice (JJ) system disproportionately affect youth of color, with racial and ethnic disparities consistently documented in the literature [9]. There is a pressing need for research aimed at improving access to and use of evidence-based services for justice-involved youth guided by implementation science theories and frameworks. Given these pronounced disparities and the multifaceted nature of service delivery for justice-involved youth, addressing SUD requires not only effective clinical interventions but also a system-level framework that ensures organizational preparedness and inter-agency collaboration. This premise underlies the importance of examining organizational readiness and cross-system partnerships in improving youth outcomes.

Organizational readiness and implementation effectiveness

In JJ and BH agencies, issues such as high staff turnover, fluctuating funding, and competing priorities can pose major obstacles to sustained implementation of new practices. Cultivating organizational readiness—where staff share a sense of commitment, efficacy, and capacity for change—may be key to overcoming these systemic barriers and adopting evidence-based interventions. The Theory of Organizational Readiness posits that within an organization, individuals' commitment to and perceived efficacy of the implemented innovation, or evidence-based practice (EBP), constitute organizational readiness. Thus, these individuals are both psychologically and behaviorally ready to implement a given innovation. The theory hypothesizes that greater organizational readiness will lead to more effective implementation, mediated by their efforts to initiate implementation, maintain implementation, and work together [10]. Readiness to change has also been identified by other researchers, such as in the conceptual process model of program change. Simpson and colleagues [11] outline factors of motivation and institutional resources that promote organizational readiness. The Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR)—a comprehensive framework that organizes factors influencing implementation into various domains to promote the development and testing of theories and hypotheses related to implementation science—identifies constructs relevant within an organization (i.e., inner setting) to be important for implementation uptake [12]. A systematic review of implementation research studies supports that the field considers the inner setting constructs to overlap with organizational readiness to implement an EBP [13]. Taken together, organizational readiness is theoretically and conceptually related to and considered crucial to successful implementation [14]. Organizational readiness has most commonly been studied descriptively to understand the context of an organization and/or to identify factors associated with organizational readiness for EBP implementation [15].

Within the field of SUD intervention, prior research found that lower organizational readiness was associated with more barriers to implementation of SUD EBP, resulting in more EBP adaptations [16]. A systematic review of EBP adoption in SUD treatment programs found that only one study examined how organizational readiness was associated with sustained EBP use [17]. This study found that staff from nearly 60 SUD treatment programs who endorsed greater readiness prior to an intervention training reported greater use of intervention components at follow-up [18]. Given the theoretical importance of organizational readiness and implementation outcomes, research has also targeted interventions to improve organizational readiness. In a study examining extended-release naltrexone for alcohol use disorders in primary care settings, provider attitudes toward naltrexone improved following an intervention aimed at increasing organizational readiness [19, 20]. However, minimal research has examined the association between organizational readiness and implementation success, including sustainability; to our knowledge, no studies have specifically addressed this issue in the context of justice-involved youth.

Cross-system collaboration

While poor access to and use of SUD services is multifaceted, one potential reason is an inadequate partnership between systems, namely JJ and BH systems. This is codified in the Juvenile Justice Behavioral Health Care Cascade, which is both a conceptual framework and descriptive tool to address service needs for youth moving through systems of care. The Cascade outlines steps of screening, assessment, determination of need for services, referral, treatment initiation, treatment engagement, and continuation of care [21]. In a nationally representative study of community supervision (i.e., probation) agencies and BH agencies, 11% of community supervision agencies and 97% of BH agencies reported providing SUD treatment [22]. Therefore, given the needed “hand-off” of youth between systems (from identification and referral to treatment), the connection between systems is crucial. Solutions—and the implementation of these solutions—to address access to and engagement in substance use care is critically needed. One potential solution is improving the collaboration and connection between systems. Cross-system collaboration encompasses a range of coordinated behaviors and structures (e.g., shared referral protocols, co-training initiatives, routine data exchange, and joint performance monitoring) that facilitate seamless transitions for youth across JJ and BH services. When these practices are integrated, we would expect that youth would be more likely to be successfully screened, referred, and engaged in needed treatment, ultimately improving outcomes.

Cross-system collaboration is not only crucial for addressing the service gaps faced by justice-involved youth with SUD, but it is also theoretically linked to organizational readiness and program sustainability. As discussed above, the Theory of Organizational Readiness proposes that greater organizational readiness will lead to implementation effectiveness through individual and collective efforts [10]. In a review of studies examining organizational readiness, Weiner and colleagues (2008) found that prior studies have explored organizational readiness as both a general factor within an organization needed to promote change broadly (e.g., organizational climate, resources) and a specific factor needed for a given intervention (e.g., certain protocols, collaboration across agencies/organizations). Therefore, while organizational readiness is related to the level of pre-existing cross-system collaboration, the process of collaboration across systems (JJ and BH) can be conceptualized as a mediator between organizational readiness and implementation effectiveness.

The complexities of cross-system collaboration have been well documented, notably between the child welfare, JJ, and BH care systems [23–30]. Collaboration among these systems has been associated with improved youth outcomes [23]. For example, Barth, Rozeff [27] aimed to improve the coordination between child welfare and BH systems through, but not limited to, shared training of child welfare and BH professionals and ongoing consultation meetings. Throughout the study, youth demonstrated improved mental health outcomes since baseline. While the specific mechanism of action could not be determined, the results offer a promising avenue for further research into collaboration across systems that are traditionally siloed yet work with youth in need of BH interventions. Qualitative results from the same project identified facilitators and barriers to implementation, particularly concerning the quality of relationships and the organizational context in which these relationships exist [31].

Compared to research on the child welfare system and other agencies, less research has been conducted between JJ and BH care systems. A nationally representative survey of community supervision and BH agencies revealed that only 25% of paired agencies were considered to have a high quality of collaboration (i.e., based on a median scale split of collaboration activities), while nearly 40% were considered to have low quality collaboration [22]. Qualitative results from JJ and community mental health workers demonstrated that, although individuals from both systems expressed interest and enthusiasm for implementing EBPs related to SUD, numerous barriers hindered the formalization and support of their collaboration, thereby affecting EBP implementation [32, 33]. Together, while limited, research examining the JJ-BH collaboration highlights the need to further examine the role of cross-system collaboration, given its potential to facilitate improved youth outcomes [28, 34].

Current study

According to Weiner’s Theory of Organizational Readiness (2020), collective engagement across units or organizations mediates the path from initial readiness to ultimate implementation success. Cross-system collaboration represents this collective action, making it a logical mediator that explains how readiness translates into sustained practice. Past literature has heavily theorized the role of organizational readiness in promoting implementation effectiveness; however, there has been minimal quantitative investigation into this association within the context of innovative strategies for youth interacting with JJ and BH systems. Additionally, the mediational role of cross-system collaboration, while theorized to be important, has not been systematically examined.

To address these gaps, we aimed to use data collected from a hybrid type II effective-implementation trial to improve access to evidence-based SUD interventions for justice-involved youth. The current study aims are to examine: 1) the association between organizational readiness and program sustainability, and 2) the mediational role of cross-system collaboration. We hypothesized that organizational readiness would be positively associated with program sustainability, and that cross-system collaboration would partially mediate this association.

Methods

Sample

Data were derived from the Alliances to Disseminate Addiction Prevention and Treatment (ADAPT) project, which aimed to improve the identification of need for and connection to SUD care among justice-involved youth. ADAPT was a cluster-randomized, stepped-wedge, hybrid type II effectiveness-implementation trial testing implementation of a learning health system, which is a quality improvement approach to iteratively collect, interpret, and generate solutions from localized data [35]. In the current study, the learning health system entailed representatives from JJ and community mental health centers (CMHCs) meeting quarterly to review county-level Cascade data to drive solutions for Cascade improvement; learning health systems utilized county-level data dashboards developed to illustrate Cascade outcomes and have been described elsewhere [36]. Solutions generated to address substance use service needs for justice-involved youth varied by county; however, examples included the use of standardized, evidence-based substance use screening measures during the JJ intake appointment; reservation of weekly appointment intake slots for justice-referred youth at CMHCs; and submission of grant applications to increase service coverage for treatment of mild-moderate SUDs [37]. For a detailed description of the study protocol, refer to Aalsma, Aarons [38]. Effectiveness results from the ADAPT trial (i.e., Cascade step completion) can be found in Aalsma, Schwartz [39].

Eight counties from one Midwest state enrolled and were randomly assigned to one of three cohorts, which were stepped in at nine-month intervals between 2020–2024. Each cohort underwent an active implementation period lasting 18 months. Primary implementation strategies utilized to promote uptake and sustainment included initial training in learning health system principles at the beginning of implementation and ongoing monthly technical assistance meetings with each site throughout implementation. Such strategies are consistent with the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) [40].

Throughout the study, JJ and CMHC staff employed within the agencies involved in the research study were identified via publicly available websites and staff contact lists provided by agency leadership. Personnel were then invited via email to participate in five waves of survey data collection. A unique link to REDCap was emailed to each eligible participant, which prompted an explanation of informed consent prior to survey initiation. CMHC personnel were paid $15 via electronic gift card for survey completion; JJ personnel could not be compensated due to State law. During the four subsequent waves of survey collection, personnel who were no longer employed by the target agencies were removed from the list of eligible participants and no longer contacted. Surveys were administered to personnel based on calendar time, not respective position in the intervention. Therefore, all surveys were administered to personnel at the same time regardless of their cohort. No harm was documented during the study. A CONSORT diagram of eligible participants recruited to participate in the ADAPT study is detailed in Appendix 1.

A total of 307 individuals participated in the survey across five waves. One participant was excluded due to a lack of information regarding their county and cohort. We further excluded 30 participants who did not respond to the instruments measuring program sustainability and cross-system collaboration (see Measures below) and then excluded 2 who did not provide their age and time at a specific agency. As a result, this study included 274 participants with a total of 529 observations across five waves. Response rates ranged from 72.3% (wave 5) to 83.7% (wave 1). By county, the number of individuals who responded ranged from 5–64 for CMHCs and 4–22 for JJ. Organizational readiness

Grounded in the Theory of Organizational Readiness for Change, the Organizational Readiness to Implement Change (ORIC) is a 12-item measure assessing the extent to which individuals perceive that their organization is ready to implement a specific evidence-based intervention. ORIC has demonstrated adequate interrater reliability, agreement, and content validity [41]. Example items include, “People who work here are committed to implementing ADAPT” and “People who work here feel confident that they can coordinate tasks so that implementation goes smoothly.” Response options were measured on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “Disagree” (1) to “Agree” (5). The items were averaged, with higher scores indicating greater perceived organizational readiness to implement the ADAPT intervention. The alpha coefficient for the 12 items was 0.98, indicating high internal consistency.

Sustainability

The Program Sustainability Assessment Tool (PSAT) version 2 is a 15-item measure designed to evaluate factors that are theoretically proposed to affect program sustainability and, thus, is considered a measure of sustainability capacity. The original PSAT, which consisted of 40 items comprising eight domains, demonstrated high internal consistency [42]. To reduce the burden on survey length for participants, we included three of the domains (five questions each for a total of 15 items): environmental support (“ADAPT has leader support from within the larger organization”), partnerships, (“The ADAPT teams communicates with community leaders”) and organizational capacity (“Organizational systems are in place to support the various ADAPT needs”) due to relevance to the ADAPT learning health system intervention.

Participants were provided with the following prompt: “The primary goal of ADAPT is to increase access to and use of evidence-based addiction services for youth involved in the juvenile justice system. The ADAPT team will work with participating counties to improve the alliance between the juvenile justice system (JJ) and community mental health centers (CMHCs). The alliances should result in improved communication and opportunities to develop local solutions to connect justice-involved youth to substance use treatment in the community.” Response options are measured on a 7-point Likert scale, ranging from “To little or to no extent” (1) to “To a very great extent” (7). All 15 items were averaged, with a higher score indicating greater sustainment. The alpha coefficient for the 15 items was 0.97, indicating high internal consistency.

Cross-system collaboration

The Cultural Exchange Inventory (CEI) is a 15-item measure indexing the exchange of information, practices, and attitudes between two agencies. The CEI has demonstrated strong internal consistency, convergent validity, and discriminant validity [43]. The CEI includes and theoretically distinguishes two subscales: process (perceived quality of interactions; seven items) and output (perceived changes in knowledge, attitudes, or behaviors due to interactions; eight items). Process items include “I feel like we respect one another” and “I feel like we understand one another.” Output items include “I feel like they are changing their opinions about something because of me” and “I feel like they are changing their practices because of this collaboration.” Response options were measured on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from “Not at all” (1) to “A great deal” (7). The items for each subscale were averaged, with both process and output items demonstrating high internal consistency (α = 0.94 and 0.96, respectively).

Analyses

Linear mixed-effects models were used to analyze sustainability in relation to ORIC (Aim 1), while accounting for a cluster-randomized stepped wedge design [44, 45]. Note that ORIC, PSAT, and CEI were measured at each data collection wave. We included a randomly varying intercept for the interaction between county and system to account for the clustering by county and system, and another randomly varying intercept for participants nested within the county and agency to account for lack of independence across repeated measures within individuals. We also included fixed effects for the intervention implementation stage, continuous time (measured as the number of days between the first recorded survey completion on record, October 16, 2020, and the participant’s survey completion date), and the interaction between these two variables.

We conducted a mediation analysis to assess whether cross-system collaboration mediates the relationship between sustainability and ORIC (Aim 2) [46, 47]. Three models (six total comparisons) were used to estimate the total effect, the direct effect, and the indirect effect of ORIC: 1) model of sustainability on ORIC; 2) model of sustainability on ORIC and CEI; 3) model of CEI on ORIC. We estimated these models separately for CEI process and CEI output. The indirect effect of ORIC was calculated by the product method, and the asymptotic confidence interval was calculated [48]. We also calculated the proportion of the total effect due to the mediator by dividing the indirect effect by the total effect. The models included covariates as fixed effects, including system, age, gender, race/ethnicity, time at the specific agency, education, and job satisfaction.

A set of sensitivity analyses were run, in which the three subscales of program sustainability (support, partnerships, and organizational) were analyzed as separate outcomes using the same modeling approach described above. For incomplete longitudinal data (due to skipping one or more waves), the mixed-effects models produce unbiased estimates under the missing at random assumption (Hedeker & Gibbons, 2006). All analysis was done using the MIXED Procedure in SAS Windows version 9.4 [49].

Results

A summary of the demographic characteristics of 274 participants included in this study is presented in Table 1. Nearly two-thirds of participants (65.3%) were staff at community mental health clinics, the majority were female (78.8%) or non-Hispanic White (86.5%), 46.3% had postgraduate degrees, and the vast majority were satisfied (57.3%) or very satisfied (29.6%) with their job.

Organizational readiness was significantly positively associated with program sustainability (Table 2). One more score on ORIC was associated with a 0.79 (95% Confidence Interval [CI] 0.68, 0.90) higher score in program sustainability (p < 0.001). When the mediator CEI process was included in the model, the direct effect of ORIC on program sustainability was 0.69 (95% CI 0.58, 0.80). From the model of CEI process on ORIC (Appendix 3), ORIC was significantly positively associated with CEI process (0.64, 95% CI 0.50, 0.78). We estimated the indirect effect of ORIC on program sustainability to be 0.14 (95% CI 0.09, 0.19) based on the three models, indicating that 17.4% of the ORIC effect was mediated through CEI process. Similarly, when the mediator CEI output was included in the model, the direct effect of ORIC on program sustainability was 0.69 (95% CI 0.59, 0.80). From the model of CEI output on ORIC (Appendix 3), ORIC was positively associated with CEI output (0.59, 95% CI 0.44, 0.75). Sensitivity analyses including the sustainability measure’s three subscales (PSAT support, partnerships, and organizational) were analyzed as separate outcomes (Appendix 4). The extent to which CEI process or output mediated the association between ORIC and PSAT subscales ranged from 15.1% to 19.2%. Note that while sensitivity analyses conducted 18 comparisons (three tested pathways x two measures of CEI x three PSAT subscales), results remained significant at p < 0.05 after Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate correction.

Discussion

The aims of the current study were to examine the association between organizational readiness and program sustainment, as well as the mediational role of cross-system collaboration, within the context of a hybrid type II effectiveness-implementation study to promote SUD treatment among justice-involved youth. Results from the study supported our hypotheses: first, that organizational readiness was positively associated with program sustainability, and second, that cross-system collaboration partially mediated this association.

The association between organizational readiness and program sustainability is theoretically consistent with the Theory of Organizational Readiness [10], such that greater preparedness by individuals within an organization would facilitate more effective implementation and sustainment. As elucidated by Weiner and colleagues [15] in a review of the current state of organizational readiness research, organizational readiness is most often defined as psychological preparedness prior to implementation, including individuals’ beliefs in their ability and/or their commitment to implement given innovations. This psychological conceptualization of organizational readiness aligns with the measure used in the current study (ORIC), which encompasses items such as confidence in others, organizational support, confidence and determination to maintain momentum, and commitment to the project (often described as “doing whatever it takes”). The promotion of strong organizational readiness may overcome potential implementation barriers, such as low resources [50]. For example, in a qualitative study evaluating the implementation of a Regional Prevention System in the state of Indiana, rural communities endorsed valuing implementation but reported slower implementation process [51]. Understanding the role of contextual factors, such as community resources and rurality, was outside the scope of the current investigation, but we acknowledge that such factors may promote organizational readiness. Despite the theoretical support that organizational readiness is important to implementation success, few studies have prospectively examined its role in the process and sustainment of implementation [15]. While we did not experimentally manipulate organizational readiness, organizational readiness was measured in tandem with program sustainability across five waves and prior to any intervention implementation. Therefore, our research begins to observationally clarify the longitudinal role of organizational readiness with other measures, such as sustainability. As we cannot elucidate directionality, reverse causality between our measures of organizational readiness and program sustainability may be possible. Despite this, the current results are promising to further examine the potential predictive role of organizational readiness within the context of substance use care for justice-involved youth.

Cross-system collaboration partially mediated the association between organizational readiness and program sustainability—approximately 17% of the association was attributed to cross-system collaboration. Notably, both subscales of cross-system collaboration (CEI process and CEI output) were comparable in their mediational effect (17.4% versus 17.2%, respectively). When evaluating program sustainability by subscale, the mediational effect ranged from 15.1% (CEI output to environmental support) to 19.8% (CEI process to organizational capacity). Cross-system collaboration has been the growing focus of health services research, especially due to multisystem involvement of many youth, which requires effective cross-system collaboration. The current study’s measure of cross-system collaboration—Cultural Exchange Inventory (CEI)—indexes individuals' perceptions of the process of interactions with the other system, as well as their perceived output of such interactions. Our findings demonstrated that attitudes about the collaboration (e.g., mutual understanding, respect, and effort made and received) mediated the relationship to a similar degree as perceived output from the relationship (changing practices, policies, and opinions because of the collaboration). While we cannot establish a causal relationship, it is possible that both process and output domains are important in the facilitation of program development and sustainment. It is also possible that process and output, while theoretically distinct [43], measure a similar underlying construct within the current sample, such as general positive attitudes about the other agency/system. We retrained the subscales as separate mediators, but future work would benefit from examining the reliability of the CEI and examining both subscales as mediators simultaneously.

These findings also align with the CFIR’s emphasis on the “Inner Setting,” wherein organizational culture, readiness for implementation, and networks/communication are key determinants of success. Specifically, our observation that cross-system collaboration partially mediated the link between readiness and sustainability underscores the importance of interorganizational relationships—an element sometimes classified under the CFIR “Outer Setting” or cross-organization domain. Future research can leverage these results by measuring climate, culture, and leadership (key CFIR constructs) in tandem with readiness to more thoroughly predict and optimize sustainment.

At the practice level, these results suggest that agencies should not only build internal readiness for implementing new interventions but also intentionally foster interagency trust and shared decision-making processes. For instance, structured joint staff trainings or co-located services could enhance the “process” component of collaboration, while data-sharing agreements and coordinated policies could strengthen the "output" dimension. Policymakers could further incentivize cross-system partnerships by offering grants or performance-based funding contingent on demonstrated collaboration across JJ and BH agencies.

The type and quality of interactions between individuals across systems was not measured in the current study. Qualitative interviews with staff will help enrich the understanding of perceptions of cross-system collaboration; however, we draw upon other research to identify potential collaborative strategies. Within a national survey of community supervision workers and BH providers, the most common collaborative interaction was sharing client information endorsed by 92% and 100% of BH and community supervision personnel, respectively. Other examples of collaborative strategies included creating protocols for sharing client information, collaboratively developing and sharing agreements on intervention program eligibility, jointly training staff, pooling funding, sharing office space, and developing joint procedure manuals [22]. Notably, community supervision agencies endorsed more collaborative efforts than BH agency providers. Past qualitative research has noted differences by system [32]. We did not explore differences in measures by system and rather adjusted for system as a fixed effect in the current study due to these potential differences. System was significantly associated with program sustainability in Aim 1 (community mental health employees endorsed greater program sustainability than juvenile justice) and remained significant when including cross-system collaboration as a mediator (Aim 2). Future research is needed to examine whether cross-system collaboration interventions need to be tailored by system and how this may impact youth outcomes.

We note that the current study was not aimed at investigating the impact of cross-system collaboration; the intervention randomized in the ADAPT study was the implementation of a learning health system, of which cross-system collaboration was likely impacted [52]. Ongoing research is aimed at improving cross-system collaboration through collaboration strategies [29]. Considering how to address differences in perceptions or approaches toward cross-system collaboration may be valuable future research directions. Researchers investigating cross-system collaboration between the child welfare and BH system, for example, identified cross-system collaboration strategies [30]. Applying these strategies to juvenile justice and BH may prove valuable next steps.

Given that cross-system collaboration partially mediated the association between organizational readiness and program sustainability, other potential mediators may include initiation and persistence of behavior [14]. Organizational readiness psychologically prepares one for action, however individuals must then behaviorally prepare (i.e., have behavioral intention to act), initiate, and maintain such change actions, consistent with the Theory of Planned Behavior [15, 53]. Therefore, measures of behavioral intentions or change behaviors may be avenues for future research to examine other mediators between organizational readiness and sustainment. Prior research examining collaboration between the child welfare and juvenile justice system found that when one agency was held accountable for connecting youth to services, youth had increased odds of receiving BH services compared to when accountability was not clearly established between agencies [54]. Therefore, part of organizational readiness may be role definition, which may then lead to greater cross-system collaboration and behavioral intention. Future research that examines role delineation between juvenile justice and community mental health would be a benefit to the field, especially as it prepares systems for EBP implementation.

Strengths and limitations

As previously discussed, the current study is strengthened by the prospective and longitudinal assessment of organizational readiness, program sustainability, and cross-system collaboration. Specifically, mixed-effects modeling allowed for the simultaneous analysis of both between- and within-individual changes of readiness and cross-system collaboration on sustainability. While results are derived from observational assessment, the results are an addition to the literature that moves organizational readiness beyond descriptive analyses. We examined the potential mediating role of cross-system collaboration, strongly grounded in theoretical rationale. Additionally, there is minimal cross-system collaboration research between juvenile justice and mental health collaboration, significantly adding to the field.

The strengths of the study should be considered in light of a few key limitations. First, although our sample provides valuable insight into how readiness and collaboration relate to sustainability, our focus on a single Midwestern state may limit generalizability, especially in settings with different policy or funding environments (outer setting). Moreover, our reliance on self-reported measures means we did not directly assess EBP fidelity (a key implementation outcome) nor confirm sustained EBP usage rates. Future studies should incorporate objective fidelity metrics and a more diverse range of organizational contexts to verify these mediational pathways across varying outer settings. Looking ahead, research should examine whether increased organizational readiness and collaboration also improve critical implementation outcomes such as acceptability (e.g., how staff and youth perceive EBP utility), feasibility (e.g., capacity for consistent service delivery), and cost-effectiveness. Examining implementation outcomes in relation to effectiveness outcomes (i.e., youth BH care connection) is also a critical need for the field. A more holistic understanding of these outcomes will guide system-level decisions on resource allocation and policy.

Second, the current study measured sustainability using the Program Sustainability Assessment Tool (PSAT) version 2, a tool commonly used to identify factors that may influence sustainability of EBP implementation. While greater sustainability as indicated on the PSAT would theoretically predict greater program sustainment, we did not directly measure the sustainment of the main effectiveness or implementation outcomes of the study (i.e., greater access to and engagement in substance use interventions, and increased acceptability and feasibility of using learning health systems). Expanding measures of sustainability may require trials to extend follow-up periods or revisit trial sites with the primary purpose of measuring outcomes after trial completion. Other measures have been developed to identify both determinants and outcomes of sustainment, such as the Sustainment Measurement System Scale [55], which may be more appropriate when considering sustainment as an outcome.

Third, we did not examine organizational climate, which has often been cited as a component or determinant of organizational readiness [51]. Prior research supports the correlation between cross-system collaboration and organizational climate ratings between child welfare and BH systems [56]. Organizational climate has also been associated with implementation of substance use screening and brief intervention in primary care [57]. Organizational climate may be bidirectionally related to cross-system collaboration. Future research would benefit from examining the role of organizational climate in relation to cross-system collaboration as predictors of program sustainability. While not necessarily a limitation of the current study, we do note the field’s ongoing investigation into the differences between individual- and organizational-level perceptions of organizational readiness and cross-system collaboration, as differences by levels may affect the observed associations. Our measures do not explicitly examine differences by level. Multilevel frameworks have been proposed by researchers [58] and work is needed to examine such frameworks within the context of EBPs for youth substance use.

Conclusions

Within the context of improving use of and access to BH services for justice-involved youth, the current investigation examined the association between organizational readiness and program sustainability and the mediating role of cross-system collaboration. Results supported both an association between organizational readiness and program sustainability, as well as partial mediation (approximately 17% of the association due to the indirect role) of cross-system collaboration between JJ and CMHC personnel. Future research would benefit from examining program sustainability based on behavior change (e.g., change in practices, changes at the youth-outcome level). Also, by empirically demonstrating that cross-system collaboration partially mediated the link between organizational readiness and perceived sustainability, this study contributes to the broader implementation science literature on how multi-agency contexts can foster EBP adoption and sustainment. Our findings underscore the need for intentional strategies that build and maintain inter-organizational relationships to ensure EBP reach and benefit for justice-involved youth.
