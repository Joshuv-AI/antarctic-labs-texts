The Zariski Topology on Homeomorphism groups
L Elliott

Abstract
. The Zariski topology ℨ G \mathfrak{Z}_{G} on a group G G is the coarsest topology such that all sets of the form { x ∈ G ∣ 1 G ≠ g 0 ​ x k 0 ​ g 1 ​ … ​ g l − 1 ​ x k l − 1 ​ g l } \{x\in G\mid 1_{G}\neq g_{0}x^{k_{0}}g_{1}\dots g_{l-1}x^{k_{l-1}}g_{l}\} are open. Originally introduced by Bryant as the “verbal topology,” it serves as a fundamental tool for investigating the topological structure of infinite groups and is always a T 1 T_{1} topology with continuous shifts and inversion. Since the Zariski topology is coarser than every Hausdorff group topology on G G , it provides a natural starting point for topologizing groups; specifically, for countable or abelian groups, it is known that the Zariski topology coincides with the Markov topology—the intersection of all Hausdorff group topologies on G G . In this paper, we analyze the Zariski topology on various homeomorphism groups. We demonstrate that for the Thompson groups F F and T T , the Zariski (and thus Markov) topology coincides with the standard compact-open topology derived from their respective actions on [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] and S 1 S^{1} . In contrast, we show that the Zariski (and thus Markov) topology on Thompson’s group V V is irreducible, and therefore neither Hausdorff nor a group topology. As V V acts highly transitively on each of its orbits, this result stands in notable opposition to a theorem by Banakh et al, which establishes that the Zariski topology on any permutation group containing all finitely supported elements is a Hausdorff group topology. We also extend these investigations to Homeo ⁡ ( [ 0 , 1 ] ) \operatorname{Homeo}([0,1]) , Homeo ⁡ ( S 1 ) \operatorname{Homeo}(S^{1}) , and Homeo ⁡ ( 2 ω ) \operatorname{Homeo}(2^{\omega}) . We conclude by providing a classification of manifolds M M for which the homeomorphism group Homeo ⁡ ( M ) \operatorname{Homeo}(M) admits a Hausdorff Zariski topology.

A

*:G\times G\to G
and
{}^{-1}:G\to G

A

is then a group equipped with a group topology.
Having a topology on a group is often a very useful tool for analyzing its group structure, particularly if the topology is in some sense intrinsic.
Topologies on homeomorphism groups in particular have seen special attention in the literature, see for example
[

,

The compact-open topology on the homeomorphism group of a space

is open, is perhaps the most standard.

A fundamental tool for studying topologies on a group is the
Zariski topology

\mathfrak{Z}_{G}
, introduced by Bryant
[
6
]
as the “verbal topology.”
The Zariski topology on groups and semigroups has been extensively studied in the literature. See for example

,

,

Defined by the non-solutions to equations (

), it provides a lower bound for the Hausdorff group topologies on any group

.
As such, studying the Zariski topology informs us about all Hausdorff group topologies and, in the case of a countable or abelian group, finding a set which is not Zariski-open amounts to finding a Hausdorff group topology on our group which does not include the set.

In this paper, we investigate the Zariski topology on various homeomorphism groups.
We start by focusing on the famous Thompson groups

and

We demonstrate that for

If

If

Suppose that

2.
Preliminaries

Here we give the standard definitions, conventions, and facts used throughout the paper.
In this paper, we use right actions and compose functions from left to right. We also adopt the convention that
0\in\mathbb{N}
.
We begin with the definition of the Zariski topology.

Definition 2.1

(Zariski Topology)
.

Suppose that
G
is a group with identity
1_{G}
. Then the group Zariski topology
\mathfrak{Z}_{G}
is the topology with subbasis consisting of the sets

\{x\in G\mid 1_{G}\neq g_{0}x^{k_{0}}g_{1}\dots g_{l-1}x^{k_{l-1}}g_{l}\}

where
l\in\mathbb{N}
,
g_{0},\ldots,g_{l}\in G
, and
k_{0},\ldots,k_{l-1}\in\mathbb{Z}
.

The following is perhaps the most standard topology to consider for a homeomorphism group. We will show that in many cases it is the smallest Hausdorff topology.

Definition 2.2

(Compact-open topology)
.

Suppose that
X
is a topological space. Then the compact-open topology on
\operatorname{Homeo}(X)
is the topology with subbasis consisting of the sets

\left\{f\in\operatorname{Homeo}(X)\;\middle|\;\begin{tabular}[]{@{}l@{}}$(K)f\subseteq U$\end{tabular}\right\}

where
K\subseteq X
is compact and
U\subseteq X
is open.

This definition of the compact-open topology, while standard, is not always the easiest to use.
Consequently, we provide an alternative characterization.
The following fact is well-known
(see for example Theorem 46.8 of
[
21
]
and section 9B example 8 of
[
16
]
).

Proposition 2.3
.

If
(X,d)
is a compact metric space then
\operatorname{Homeo}(X)
is a topological group with the compact-open topology.
Moreover this topology is induced by the metric

d_{\infty}(f,g)=\sup_{x\in X}d((x)f,(x)g).

Our results on non-Hausdorff topologies in this paper actually show a much stronger condition. This condition prevents any pairs of non-empty disjoint open sets from existing at all.

Definition 2.4

(Irreducible)
.

A topological space
X
is said to be
irreducible
(also known as hyperconnected) if whenever
K_{1},K_{2}\subsetneq X
are closed, we have
K_{1}\cup K_{2}\neq X
.
Equivalently, all non-empty open subsets of
X
are dense.

The following hypotheses will be needed for one of our core lemmas,
in particular
Lemma

3.1
.

Definition 2.5

(Support)
.

If
g
is a permutation of a set
X
, then the support of
g
is the set

\left\{x\in X\;\middle|\;\begin{tabular}[]{@{}l@{}}$(x)g\neq x$\end{tabular}\right\}.

If
f,g
are permutations of a common set, then we denote by
f^{g}
the conjugate
g^{-1}fg
of
f
by
g

Crucially, the support of
f^{g}
is always the image of the support of
f
under the map

.

Definition 2.6

(Highly Transitive)
.

We say that a group
G
of permutations of a set
X
is

if for every bijection
\sigma
between finite subsets of
X
, there is
g\in G
extending
\sigma
.

The Thompson groups
F_{n},T_{n}
, and
V_{n}
are the homeomorphism groups defined as follows:

•

F_{n}
is the group of piecewise linear homeomorphisms of

that are orientation-preserving, have all slopes in the set

, and have breakpoints in the

.

•

T_{n}
is the group of piecewise linear homeomorphisms of the circle

.

•

V_{n}
is the group of homeomorphisms

of the Cantor space

that are piecewise defined by prefix replacements. That is to say, for all

and

(x_{0}x_{1}x_{2}\ldots x_{i}z_{0}z_{1}\ldots)f=y_{0}y_{1}y_{2}\ldots y_{j}z_{0}z_{1}\ldots

If

3.
The Cantor Space

The following lemma will be our main tool for showing that Zariski topologies are irreducible.
It closely resembles Theorem 1.12 of
[
4
]
, which shows that the semigroup Zariski topology on a group is irreducible under weaker hypotheses.
These weaker hypothesis are not sufficient for the group Zariski topology as can be seen by considering the group
\operatorname{Sym}(\mathbb{N})
and Theorem 2.1 of
[

]
or indeed Thompson’s group

The stronger assumptions however are still satisfied for a large family of examples.

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose that
X
is an infinite set and
G

In this case the Zariski topology on

Proof.

$1_{G}\neq x^{k_{1,0}}a_{1,1}\ldots a_{1,l_{1}-1}x^{k_{1,l_{1}-1}}a_{1,l_{1}}$\\
\quad\quad\vdots\\

(i^{\prime})d_{\beta}\neq l_{i^{\prime}}
, we have
(p_{i^{\prime}})f_{i^{\prime},\beta}\notin\operatorname{dom}(\beta)\cup\operatorname{im}(\beta)
.
Combining this with the case
i^{\prime}=i
displayed above, we have now shown for all
i^{\prime}<m
with
(i^{\prime})d_{\beta}\neq l_{i^{\prime}}
, that

(p_{i^{\prime}})f_{i^{\prime},\beta}\notin\operatorname{dom}(\beta)\cup\operatorname{im}(\beta).

If
i^{\prime}<m
and
(i^{\prime})d_{\beta}\neq 0
, then we either have
i^{\prime}\neq i
, in which case
(i^{\prime})d_{\sigma}=(i^{\prime})d_{\beta}\neq 0
and by induction
(p_{i^{\prime}})f_{i^{\prime},\beta}=(p_{i^{\prime}})f_{i^{\prime},\sigma}\neq p_{i}
, or we have
i^{\prime}=i
.
The point
(p_{i})f_{i,\beta}
was chosen not to belong to
C_{\sigma}
, so in particular this point is not
p_{i}
.

In summary, we have now shown each of

(1)

if
i^{\prime\prime}\neq i^{\prime}<m
, then
(p_{i^{\prime\prime}})f_{i^{\prime\prime},\beta}\neq(p_{i^{\prime}})f_{i^{\prime},\beta}

(2)

if
i^{\prime}<m
and
(i^{\prime})d_{\beta}\neq l_{i^{\prime}}
, then
(p_{i^{\prime}})f_{i^{\prime},\beta}\notin\operatorname{dom}(\beta)\cup\operatorname{im}(\beta)
,

(3)

if
i^{\prime}<m
and
(i^{\prime})d_{\beta}\neq 0
, then
(p_{i^{\prime}})f_{i^{\prime},\beta}\neq p_{i}
,

and
d_{\beta}
is obtained from
d_{\sigma}
by increasing one of the image points.
We can therefor complete our inductive step by redefining
\sigma
to be
\beta
.
This completes the proof.
∎

We can now finish the proof of
Theorem

1.4
.
Moreover
Theorem

1.3
is shown as a special case.

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose that
X
is a Hausdorff topological space with no isolated points and
G
acts highly transitively on
X
by homeomorphisms.
In this case the group Zariski topology on
G
is irreducible.

Proof.

To apply
Lemma

3.1
, we need only show that every non-trivial element has infinite support.
This follows as the support of any homeomorphism of a Hausdorff space is open, and a Hausdorff space with no isolated points has no non-empty finite open subsets.
∎

Theorem 3.3
.

The group Zariski topologies on Thompson’s group
V
(or any of the Thompson groups
V_{n}
with
n\geq 2
) and the group
\operatorname{Homeo}(2^{\omega})
are irreducible.

Proof.

The group
V_{n}
does not act highly transitively on the Cantor set but it does act faithfully and highly transitively on any one of its orbits.
Moreover, the orbits are subspaces with no isolated points.
Thus we may fix any orbit and apply
Theorem

3.2
.

On the other hand, the group
\operatorname{Homeo}(2^{\omega})
does act highly transitively on the Cantor space so this is an immediate application of
Theorem

3.2
.
∎

4.
The Interval and the Circle

In this section, the main tool is
Lemma

4.2
so we start by establishing the conventions needed there.
For convenience, we view the topological space
S^{1}
as the set
[0,1)
with the topology induced by the metric

d(x,y)=\operatorname{min}(|x-y|,1-|x-y|).

Under the compact-open topology, the topological group
\operatorname{Homeo}([0,1])
is topologically isomorphic to the stabilizer of
0
in the topological group
\operatorname{Homeo}(S^{1})
.
As such, we will for simplicity work entirely in the group
\operatorname{Homeo}(S^{1})
.
Given
x\neq y\in S^{1}=[0,1)
, we write
(x,y)
for the
open interval from
x
to
y
using the cyclic order on
S^{1}
. That is to say

(x,y):=\left\{z\in[0,1)\;\middle|\;\begin{tabular}[]{@{}l@{}}$x<z<y$ or $y<x<z$ or $z<y<x$\end{tabular}\right\}.

We refer to an arbitrary set of this form as simply an
open interval
. Note that we consider neither the empty set nor the entire space to be an open interval.
As a subspace of
S^{1}
, each open interval is open, connected, and homeomorphic
\mathbb{R}
.
It is well-known that every homeomorphism of
S^{1}
either preserves or reverses the cyclic order. In particular the following proposition will be useful to us.

Proposition 4.1
.

If
h\in\operatorname{Homeo}(S^{1})
and
I
is an open interval of
S^{1}
, then
(I)h
is also an open interval of
S^{1}
.

In our arguments we will need to keep track of the size of these intervals and their unions.
We denote by
\lambda
the usual Lebesgue measure on
[0,1)=S^{1}
. For example,
\lambda(\varnothing)=0
,
\lambda(S^{1})=1
,
\lambda(\frac{1}{5},\frac{3}{5})=\frac{2}{5}
and
\lambda(\frac{3}{5},\frac{1}{5})=\frac{3}{5}
. A key observation will be that if any interval
I
contains points of distance at least
\varepsilon
, then the measure of the interval is at least
\varepsilon
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose that
G\leq\operatorname{Homeo}(S^{1})
and there is a dense subset
D
of
[0,1)
such that for all
p,q\in D
with
p<q
, there is
t_{p,q}\in G
with support
(p,q)
.
In this case
\mathfrak{Z}_{G}
is the compact-open topology.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{co}
be the compact-open topology on
G
.
By
2.3
,
\mathcal{T}_{co}
is a group topology induced by the uniform metric. In particular, as a Hausdorff group topology,
\mathcal{T}_{co}\supseteq\mathfrak{Z}_{G}
. We need only show the reverse containment.
As shifting by elements of
G
is a homeomorphism with respect to
\mathfrak{Z}_{G}
, we only need to show the each neighbourhood of
1_{G}
with respect to
\mathcal{T}_{co}
is a
\mathfrak{Z}_{G}
-neighbourhood of
1_{G}
.
The
\mathcal{T}_{co}
-neighbourhoods of
1_{G}
have a basis consisting of the sets

B_{d_{\infty}}(1_{G},\varepsilon):=\left\{f\in G\;\middle|\;\begin{tabular}[]{@{}l@{}}for all $x\in S^{1}$, $d(x,(x)f)<\varepsilon$\end{tabular}\right\}

where
\varepsilon>0
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. We may assume without loss of generality that
\varepsilon<\frac{1}{64}
.
As
D
is dense, we can find
p_{1},\ldots,p_{k-1}\in D\backslash\{0\}
such that for all
i<k
, we have
0<p_{i+1}-p_{i}<\frac{\varepsilon}{64}
,
0<p_{1}<\frac{\varepsilon}{64}
and
\frac{63\varepsilon}{64}<p_{k-1}<1
.
Define
p_{0}=0
and
p_{k}=1
.
For each
0<i<k
, also let
p_{i,l},p_{i,r}\in D
be such that for all
0\leq j<l
we have
0<p_{i,r}-p_{i,l}<\frac{\varepsilon(p_{j+1}-p_{j})}{64k}
and
p_{i,l}<p_{i}<p_{i,r}
.
We define

U_{i,\varepsilon}:=\left\{g\in G\;\middle|\;\begin{tabular}[]{@{}l@{}}$[t_{p_{i},p_{i+1}}^{g},t_{p_{i,l},p_{i,r}}]\neq 1_{G}$ and $[t_{p_{i},p_{i+1}}^{g},t_{p_{i+1,l},p_{i+1,r}}]\neq 1_{G}$\end{tabular}\right\}

for
0<i<k-1
.
As the support of
t_{p_{i,l},p_{i,r}}
is
(p_{i,l},p_{i,r})
, the conjugate of
t_{p_{i,l},p_{i,r}}
by
t_{p_{i},p_{i+1}}
has support which is an interval between

(p_{i,l})t_{p_{i},p_{i+1}}
and
(p_{i,r})t_{p_{i},p_{i+1}}
. As
t_{p_{i},p_{i+1}}
fixes
p_{i,l}
and does not fix
p_{i,r}
, it follows that
t_{p_{i},p_{i+1}}
and
t_{p_{i,l},p_{i,r}}
do not commute.
Similarly,
t_{p_{i},p_{i+1}}
and
t_{p_{i+1,l},p_{i+1,r}}
do not commute. Thus
1_{G}\in U_{i,\varepsilon}
for all
0<i<k
.
Let
U_{\varepsilon}:=\cap_{0<i<k-1}U_{i,\varepsilon}
. We need only show that

1_{G}\in U_{\varepsilon}\subseteq B_{d_{\infty}}(1_{G},\varepsilon).

We have already shown that
1_{G}\in U_{\varepsilon}
.
Let
g\in U_{\varepsilon}
and let
x\in S^{1}
. We need only show that
d((x)g,x)<\varepsilon
.
Let
i<k
be fixed such that
x\in[p_{i},p_{i+1}]
.
Note that the intervals
(p_{0},p_{1}),(p_{1},p_{2})\ldots(p_{k-1},p_{k})
are disjoint and contain all but finitely many points of
S^{1}
.
Thus by
4.1
, the sets

(p_{0},p_{1})g,(p_{1},p_{2})g\ldots(p_{k-1},p_{k})g

are disjoint open intervals which contain all but finitely many points of
S^{1}
. It follows that

\sum_{0\leq j<k-1}\lambda((p_{j},p_{j+1})g)=1.

As, by the choice of
g
,
t_{p_{j},p_{j+1}}^{g}
does not commute with
t_{p_{j,l},p_{j,r}}
for
0<j<k-1
, it follows that they cannot have disjoint supports.
Thus for
0<j<k-1
, the interval
(p_{j},p_{j+1})g
intersects the interval
(p_{j,l},p_{j,r})
.
By the same reasoning,
(p_{j},p_{j+1})g
intersects the interval
(p_{j+1,l},p_{j+1,r})
for all
0<j<k-1
. From the choice of
\varepsilon
, we must have
d(p_{j,r},p_{j+1,l})<d(p_{j,l},p_{j+1,r})
for all
0<j<k-1
.
It follows that the interval
(p_{j},p_{j+1})g
contains two points of distance at least
|p_{j,r}-p_{j+1,l}|
for all for
0<j<k-1
. Thus, for
0<j<k-1
, we have

\lambda((p_{j},p_{j+1})g)\geq|p_{j,r}-p_{j+1,l}|\geq p_{j+1}-p_{j}-2\frac{\varepsilon}{64k}=p_{j+1}-p_{j}-\frac{\varepsilon}{32k}.

Combining our two displayed equations we have

\displaystyle\lambda((p_{i},p_{i+1})g)

\displaystyle=1-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq j<k\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}\lambda((p_{j},p_{j+1})g)

\displaystyle\leq 1-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<j<k-1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}\lambda((p_{j},p_{j+1})g)

\displaystyle\leq 1-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<j<k-1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}(p_{j+1}-p_{j}-\frac{\varepsilon}{32k})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{32}+1-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<j<k-1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}(p_{j+1}-p_{j})

\displaystyle\leq(p_{i+1}-p_{i})+(p_{1}-p_{0})+(p_{k}-p_{k-1})+\frac{\varepsilon}{32}+1-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq j<k\end{subarray}}(p_{j+1}-p_{j})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3\varepsilon}{64}+\frac{\varepsilon}{32}<\frac{\varepsilon}{16}+\frac{\varepsilon}{32}<\frac{\varepsilon}{8}

The above argument also shows that
\lambda((p_{1},p_{2})g),\lambda((p_{k-2},p_{k-1})g)<\frac{\varepsilon}{8}
.
We define a value
i^{\prime}<k
depending on cases:

(1)

If
i=0
, let
i^{\prime}=1
,

(2)

if
i=k-1
, let
i^{\prime}=k-2
,

(3)

if
i\notin\{0,k-1\}
, let
i^{\prime}=i
.

In each case we know that

d(x,p_{i^{\prime}})\leq d(x,p_{i})+d(p_{i},p_{i^{\prime}})\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{64}+\frac{\varepsilon}{64}=\frac{\varepsilon}{32}

and
\lambda((p_{i^{\prime}},p_{i^{\prime}+1})g)<\frac{\varepsilon}{8}
so

d((x)g,(p_{i^{\prime}})g)\leq d((x)g,(p_{i})g)+d((p_{i})g,(p_{i^{\prime}})g)<\frac{\varepsilon}{8}+\frac{\varepsilon}{8}=\frac{\varepsilon}{4}.

Moreover, we have shown earlier that there is a point
b\in(p_{i^{\prime}},p_{i^{\prime}+1})g\cap(p_{i^{\prime},l},p_{i^{\prime},r})
.
In conclusion

\displaystyle d(x,(x)g)

\displaystyle\leq d(x,p_{i^{\prime}})+d(p_{i^{\prime}},b)+d(b,(p_{i^{\prime}})g)+d((p_{i^{\prime}})g,(p_{i})g)+d((p_{i})g,(x)g)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{32}+\frac{\varepsilon}{64}+\frac{\varepsilon}{8}+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}<\varepsilon

as required.

∎

Theorem

1.1
and
Theorem

1.2
now follow from the Lemma.

Theorem 4.3
.

If
G\leq\operatorname{Homeo}(S^{1})
is any group containing Thompson’s group
T
(or any of the Thompson groups
T_{n}
with
n\geq 2
), then the group Zariski topology on
G
is the compact-open topology. In particular it is a Hausdorff group topology.

Proof.

Taking
D=\mathbb{Z}[1/n]\cap[0,1)
, the Thompson group
T_{n}
contains the elements required by
Lemma

4.2
.
The compact-open topology is a Hausdorff group topology by
2.3
.
∎

Theorem 4.4
.

If
G\leq\operatorname{Homeo}([0,1])
is any group containing Thompson’s group
F
(or any of the Thompson groups
F_{n}
with
n\geq 2
), then the group Zariski topology on
G
is the compact-open topology. In particular it is a Hausdorff group topology.

Proof.

For this proof we identify
\operatorname{Homeo}([0,1])
with the stabilizer of the point
0
in the group
\operatorname{Homeo}(S^{1})
.
Note that the compact-open topology is the same whether
\operatorname{Homeo}([0,1])
acts on
[0,1]
or
S^{1}
.
Taking
D=\mathbb{Z}[1/n]\cap[0,1)
, the Thompson group
F_{n}\leq\operatorname{Homeo}([0,1])\leq\operatorname{Homeo}(S^{1})
contains the elements required by
Lemma

4.2
.
The compact-open topology is a Hausdorff group topology by
2.3
.
∎

5.
Manifolds

In this section we prove
Theorem

1.5
. Most of the proof for this has been established in previous sections so we can go straight to the main result.

Theorem 5.1
.

Suppose that
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
M
is an
n
-manifold with exactly one connected component. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

the group Zariski topology on
\operatorname{Homeo}(M)
is Hausdorff,

(2)

the group Zariski topology on
\operatorname{Homeo}(M)
is a group topology,

(3)

n\leq 1
.

Proof.

(2)\Rightarrow(1):
The group Zariski topology is always a
T_{1}
topology.
It is well known that every
T_{1}
topological group is Tychonoff. In particular every
T_{1}
topological group is Hausdorff.

(1)\Rightarrow(3):

By
Theorem

1.4
+
2.7
, the Zariski topology is not Hausdorff when
n\geq 2
.

(3)\Rightarrow(2):

By
2.8
, the only manifolds of dimension at most
1
with exactly one connected component are
\mathbb{R}
,
S^{1}
, and a single point space.
In the case of a point,
\operatorname{Homeo}(M)
is the trivial group so is discrete.
In the case of
\mathbb{R}
, we have
\operatorname{Homeo}(M)\cong\operatorname{Homeo}([0,1])
so we apply
Theorem

1.1
.
The case of
S^{1}
follows from
Theorem

1.2
.
∎

6.
Questions

By
Theorem

1.3
, the group Zariski topology on
V
is not Hausdorff and so
[
19
]
implies that the Markov topology on
V
is not Hausdorff either.
The intersection of the compact-open topology it inherits from
\operatorname{Homeo}(2^{\omega})
and the pointwise topology it inherits from
\operatorname{Sym}(2^{\omega})
contains the Hausdorff subspace topology induced by the product topology on
(2^{\omega})^{(2^{\omega})}
where
2^{\omega}
has its usual topology.
It follows that
V
admits more Hausdorff group topologies which do not contain any of these. This leads us to ask if we can describe the remaining topologies.

Question 6.1
.

Are any of the remaining Hausdorff group topologies on Thompson’s group
V
metrizable (other than the pointwise topology, the compact-open topology, their join, and the discrete topology).

Question 6.2
.

How many Hausdorff group topologies does
V
admit?

The results of
[
19
,
10
]
imply that the Zariski and Markov topologies agree for countable groups and abelian groups.
The group
\operatorname{Homeo}(2^{\omega})
is in neither of these classes. As such, given
Theorem

1.3
, the following are natural questions.

Question 6.3
.

Is the Markov topology on
\operatorname{Homeo}(2^{\omega})
equal to the Zariski topology?

Question 6.4
.

Is the Markov topology on
\operatorname{Homeo}(2^{\omega})
Hausdorff?

If we give
2^{\omega}
the lexicographic order and the induced circular order from this total order, then one can show that the subgroup of
\operatorname{Homeo}(2^{\omega})
which preserves or reverses this circular order is isomorphic to
\operatorname{Homeo}(S^{1})
.
As such,
Theorem

1.3
in a sense says that all sufficiently large subgroups of
\operatorname{Homeo}(2^{\omega})
which preserve the circular order have a Hausdorff Zarsiki topology.
Conversely,
Theorem

1.4
says that all highly transitive subgroups of
\operatorname{Homeo}(2^{\omega})
do not have a Hausdorff Zariski topology. This leads us to the following question.

Question 6.5
.

Can one classify which “sufficiently large” subgroups of
\operatorname{Homeo}(2^{\omega})
have a Hausdorff Zariski topology (for some reasonable notion of sufficiently large).

Theorem

1.1
and
Theorem

1.2
show that every Hausdorff group topology on
F
and
T
contains the compact-open topology.
They also admit the pointwise topology inherited from
\operatorname{Sym}([0,1])
and
\operatorname{Sym}(S^{1})
respectively as well as the discrete topology. This leads us to the following questions.

Question 6.6
.

Are the compact-open topology, the pointwise topology, and the discrete topology the only Hausdorff group topologies on Thompson’s group
F
?

Question 6.7
.

Are the compact-open topology, the pointwise topology, and the discrete topology the only Hausdorff group topologies on Thompson’s group
T
?
