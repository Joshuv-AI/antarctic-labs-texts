Mortality risk in osteoarthritis patients: a meta-analysis of observational studies
Authors: Jingbo Pan, Yue Zou, Qi Zhang, Dan Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study aimed to evaluate the relationship between osteoarthritis (OA) and all-cause mortality, as well as cause-specific mortality.MethodsThis study was registered with PROSPERO (ID: CRD42024542643). A comprehensive literature search was conducted using Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and keywords across PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library databases, from inception to May 16, 2024. Study quality was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) and the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) criteria. Funnel plots and Egger’s test were used to assess publication bias. Statistical analyses were performed using STATA software version 14.0.ResultsTwenty-four observational studies (2003–2023) involving 465,555 OA patients were included. The pooled analysis revealed no significant association with all-cause mortality based on pooled estimates, although with very high heterogeneity [OR = 1.14, 95% CI (0.97, 1.35), I² = 98.2%, P = 0.119], but appeared to be associated with increased (CVD) mortality [OR = 1.15, 95% CI (1.07, 1.24), I² = 73.5%, P < 0.001]. There was no increased risk of cancer mortality [OR = 1.02, 95% CI (0.94, 1.11), I² = 69.4%, P = 0.000]. Subgroup analysis indicated no significant gender-based differences in all-cause mortality, nor in radiographic OA. However, symptomatic OA and knee/foot OA were associated with a markedly higher risk of all-cause mortality. Regional analysis showed no significant variation across Europe, Asia, or North America. Sensitivity and subgroup analyses confirmed the results, with no evidence of publication bias.ConclusionsThis meta-analysis suggests that osteoarthritis is not associated with all-cause mortality but is but is associated with a modest increase in CVD mortality. There is no relationship between OA and cancer mortality. These findings emphasize the importance of focused care and lifestyle modifications to mitigate cardiovascular risks in OA patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-025-09321-4.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a prevalent and debilitating condition characterized by chronic musculoskeletal pain. It primarily affects the knees, hips, spine, big toes, and hands, and is closely associated with mobility impairments and functional decline [1, 2]. In 2017, the age-standardized point prevalence and annual incidence rate of OA were approximately 3754.2 and 181.2 per 100,000, respectively [3, 4]. This marks an increase of 9.3% and 8.2% from 1990, highlighting a significant upward trend in the global incidence of OA over recent decades. The economic burden of OA is substantial, with costs ranging from 1.0% to 2.5% of the gross domestic product (GDP) in developed countries [5]. In the United States, the estimated total annual cost per patient varies between $1,442 and $21,335 [6].

Several risk factors for OA have been identified, including genetics, age, gender, diet, obesity, and joint-level factors such as injury, malalignment, and abnormal joint loading [7]. A previous meta-analysis comparing the risks of osteoporosis and OA showed no statistically significant difference between the two conditions [8]. Moreover, physical inactivity has been independently associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) and mortality. Evidence from a meta-analysis has also shown a link between OA and atherosclerosis, suggesting that OA increases the risk of both subclinical atherosclerosis and cardiovascular diseases (CVD), although it does not appear to increase the risk of cardiovascular death [9]. Furthermore, another meta-analysis found that individuals with OA were nearly three times as likely to suffer from heart failure or ischemic heart disease, with CVD pathology affecting 38.4% of people with OA [10].

Historically, OA has rarely been associated with mortality in the elderly, as it is often considered a physiological condition related to aging. However, more recent research has focused on the relationship between OA and the risk of all-cause mortality. While two meta-analyses have reported no significant difference in the association between OA and all-cause mortality [11, 12], Xing et al. [11] found osteoarthritis increases all-cause mortality, particularly cardiovascular death, but lacked consideration of confounding factors and generalizability. Veronese et al. [12] also identified a mortality risk, especially in the elderly, but had limited data on disease severity and missed subgroup analyses for osteoarthritis types. Despite these findings, existing studies remain limited and incomplete due to insufficient sample sizes in prior meta-analyses and a lack of subgroup analyses stratified by OA anatomical sites or symptom types, underscoring the need for further investigation. To provide a more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between OA and both overall and cause-specific mortality, we conducted this meta-analysis.

Methods

This meta-analysis was conducted in adherence to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [13]. The study protocol was pre-registered on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) platform, with the registration number CRD42024542643.

Data sources

We systematically searched PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library from their inception dates up to May 16, 2024. The search strategy included a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and keywords, with no restrictions on language. Key search terms included various forms of “osteoarthritis” and “mortality.” Detailed search strategies are outlined in Supplementary Tables 1–3. In addition to database searches, we manually screened the reference lists of relevant systematic reviews to ensure comprehensive identification of studies [11, 12].

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria for the studies were as follows: observational study design; investigation of all-cause and/or cause-specific mortality in patients diagnosed with osteoarthritis and reporting adjusted hazard ratios (HR), odds ratios (OR), or standardized mortality ratios (SMR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). We utilized the PEO (Population - Exposure - Outcome) framework, and the PEO framework of our systematic review are described below:P (Population): Adults with or without osteoarthritis.E (Exposure): Diagnosis of osteoarthritis (any anatomical site).O (Outcome): All-cause and cause-specific mortality (e.g., cardiovascular and cancer-related).

We excluded conference abstracts, study protocols, letters to the editor, and studies without relevant outcomes. Studies were excluded if they did not report adjusted HR, OR, or SMR with corresponding 95% CI, and SMR and RR will be approximated as OR. In cases where multiple studies utilized the same dataset, preference was given to studies with the largest participant cohort or the longest duration of follow-up. Additionally, conference abstracts, study protocols, letters, and studies that did not report relevant outcomes of interest were excluded from the analysis.

Study selection

Study selection was performed by two independent reviewers (J.B.P. and D.L.), who screened articles based on predefined eligibility and exclusion criteria. Initially, duplicate and irrelevant articles were excluded based on title and abstract screening. Full texts of the potentially eligible studies were then retrieved and assessed to confirm eligibility. Any discrepancies or disagreements in study selection were resolved through consensus or, when necessary, through consultation with a third reviewer (Y.Z.).

Data extraction

Data extraction was carried out independently by the two reviewers mentioned above, following established guidelines for systematic reviews and meta-analyses [14]. Pre-designed data extraction forms were utilized to collect key study characteristics, including: first author, publication year, sample size, age distribution, gender ratio, duration of follow-up, geographical region, study period, and effect size estimates. The primary outcome was the the association of osteoarthritis with all-cause mortality. Secondary outcomes were the the association of osteoarthritis with type specific mortality (including cardiovascular and cancer-related). Discrepancies in data extraction were resolved through discussion, and final consensus was reached with the involvement of a third reviewer (Qi Z).

Risk of bias

For cohort and case-control studies, the quality was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), which assigns a maximum of 9 points per study. A higher score reflects better study quality, with the scores categorized as follows: 0–3 for low quality, 4–6 for moderate quality, and 7–9 for high quality.

For cross-sectional studies, the quality was evaluated using the American Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) tool. This scale includes eleven items, each scored as ‘1’ for a ‘Yes’ response or ‘0’ for ‘Unclear’ or ‘No.’ The total scores were then categorized into low quality (0–3), moderate quality (4–7), and high quality (8–11) [15].

Statistical analysis

We analysed data using Stata version 14.0 (Stata Corp, College Station, TX, USA), choosing DerSimonian and Laird random-effects models to pool data. Study-specific effect sizes, including HR, OR, or SMR, were extracted and standardized into OR with corresponding 95% CI for both all-cause and cause-specific mortality outcomes to facilitate the data synthesis for this meta-analysis. This approach is justified because, when the mortality rate ratio (θ) between the exposed group and the reference population remains constant across strata, the SMR provides an unbiased estimate of the RR [16]. Furthermore, since the incidence of death was relatively low in individuals with OA, RR and HR can reasonably approximate OR. The heterogeneity of study results was assessed using the χ² test (i.e., Cochran’s Q test) and the I² statistic. A random-effects model was applied to calculate pooled OR with 95% CI for the association between osteoarthritis and risk of mortality, indicating substantial heterogeneity. In contrast, a fixed-effects model was used when the P-value exceeded 0.1 and I² was less than 50% [17, 18].

To test the robustness of the findings, sensitivity analyses were performed. Publication bias was assessed visually through funnel plots and further tested using Egger’s regression test. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on gender and the type of osteoarthritis (e.g., osteoarthritis of the knee, hip, etc.).

Results

Literature search

A total of 3,840 records were identified through the initial search. Following screening of titles and abstracts, seventy-five articles were deemed potentially relevant. Subsequently, twenty-four studies [19, 12, 20–41] were included after a full-text review, which reported data on both all-cause and cause-specific mortality. Risk of all-cause mortality in osteoarthritis

The association between osteoarthritis and the risk of all-cause mortality was explored using 20 data from 15 observational studies [12, 13, 21, 23, 24, 26, 27, 29, 31, 33–36, 39, 40]. The pooled analysis found no significant increase in all-cause mortality among patients with osteoarthritis [OR = 1.14, 95% CI (0.97, 1.34), I2 = 98.2%, P = 0.119, Fig. 2]. The high heterogeneity somewhat limits the robustness of this estimate. However, the sensitivity analysis results offered some reassurance regarding robustness and supported the main finding (Supplementary Table 5). Exclusion of Swain S et al. (2023) rendered the previously non-significant pooled all-cause mortality result significant (OR = 1.11, 95% CI: 1.02–1.20, P = 0.002 vs. OR = 1.14, 95% CI: 0.97–1.35, P = 0.119) and substantially reduced heterogeneity (I²=85.6% vs. 98.2%). This study was a primary source of initial non-significance and heterogeneity, attributable to its large sample size, unique diagnostic methods, thorough confounding adjustment, and regional characteristics. Its divergent, higher OR with non-overlapping CI contributed significantly to the observed heterogeneity.

Discussion

Main findings

Our meta-analysis, which incorporated 24 observational studies involving 465,555 individuals with OA, provides a comprehensive evaluation of the relationship between OA and mortality risk. The findings indicate that, overall, osteoarthritis does not significantly increase all-cause mortality. Nevertheless, the very high heterogeneity observed warrants caution regarding the robustness of this conclusion. However, the risk of CVD mortality was a little higher, while no significant association with cancer mortality was observed. Subgroup analysis revealed no significant differences in the risk of all-cause mortality between male and female patients with OA. Additionally, radiographic OA was not associated with increased all-cause mortality risk, whereas symptomatic OA showed a significant increase. Among specific OA types, knee and foot OA were linked to a substantial increase in all-cause mortality, while hip and hand OA showed only a marginal increase. Geographically, no significant differences in all-cause mortality risk were found across studies from Europe, Asia, and North America.

Interpretation of findings

An earlier meta-analysis that included nine prospective cohort studies also found no significant difference in all-cause mortality risk between symptomatic and radiographic OA patients [42]. Another review of six studies indicated an increased risk of death due to CVD in OA patients but no significant increase in all-cause mortality [43]. We manually reviewed references from these systematic reviews, identifying 11 duplicate studies for inclusion and excluding one due to unreported 95% CIs. In addition to these studies, we included 13 other eligible studies, resulting in 24 studies being analyzed in our current research.

Recent meta-analyses have shown that knee OA patients face an increased risk of all-cause mortality [44]. Subgroup analysis in these studies revealed a significant increase in mortality for radiographic and symptomatic knee OA patients. However, no significant increase was found in patients with radiographic or symptomatic knee OA alone. Our meta-analysis, in contrast, covered all OA types, including wrist/hip, hand, knee, spine, ankle/foot, and other peripheral joints. We also converted study-specific effect sizes such as HR, OR, and SMR into ORs with corresponding 95% CIs for consistency.

Unlike previous studies, which primarily focused on all-cause mortality, our analysis also assessed cause-specific mortality, including CVD and cancer mortality. Furthermore, we performed subgroup analyses based on gender, OA type, anatomical location, and geographic region, which strengthens the reliability of our findings. Despite non-significant regional subgroup results, substantial global disparities in healthcare systems—particularly in treatment access (e.g., surgical wait times), clinical practice patterns, and integrated comorbidity management—lead us to hypothesize that specific systemic features (such as prolonged joint replacement delays, inadequate primary foot care, or fragmented chronic disease management) may amplify site-specific OA mortality risk. The modest increased risk of CVD mortality in OA patients was consistent with previous studies, potentially due to factors such as poor physical function and chronic inflammation [45, 46]. OA pain may lead to sedentary behavior, cardiovascular complications, and early mortality [47, 48]. Several mechanisms underlie this association. Established mechanisms encompass shared risk factors between OA and CVD, such as aging, obesity, inflammation, and metabolic dysfunction, as well as the downstream consequence of reduced physical activity due to lower limb joint involvement [49, 22]. Additional proposed mechanisms requiring further validation include oxidative stress, chondrocyte senescence, and endothelial dysfunction [49]. Furthermore, adipose tissue inflammation has been hypothesized as a potential common pathway driving the development of both conditions [50].

In contrast to the previous literature, our study found no increased risk of cancer mortality in OA patients. This finding remains unexplained, but it may be linked to inflammation in OA, which involves processes like cellular senescence, mitochondrial dysfunction, and dysbiosis [51]. These mechanisms might foster elevated oxidative stress and pro-inflammatory pathways, which have been implicated in cancer pathogenesis [52, 53]. The role of oxidant-damaged DNA in tumorigenesis and subsequent mortality is a potential avenue for future research [54, 55].

Our meta-analysis included recent and larger cohort studies and employed comprehensive subgroup analyses to further validate the reliability of the results regarding all-cause and cause-specific mortality risks in OA patients. The findings suggest that while OA is not associated with all-cause mortality, it is significantly associated with a modest increased risk of CVD mortality but not cancer mortality.

Implications and limitations

Our findings highlight that OA does not significantly increase all-cause mortality risk in general, regardless of gender or the presence of radiographic OA. However, knee and foot OA patients exhibit a significantly increased risk of mortality, while hip and hand OA patients show only a slight increase. Our regional analysis indicates that this risk is not significantly affected by geographic location (Europe, Asia, or North America). The increased risk of CVD mortality in OA patients underscores the importance of managing comorbidities, such as cardiovascular disease, to reduce mortality risk in OA patients. This calls for greater attention to the prevention, treatment, and daily management of these comorbidities in clinical settings.

Despite the strengths of this meta-analysis, there are several limitations. Primarily, causal inference is limited by the exclusive inclusion of observational studies susceptible to residual confounding; future investigations employing prospective cohort designs with enhanced sample sizes are imperative to substantiate mortality associations. Secondly, key confounding variables were inconsistently reported across primary studies, introducing potential selection and measurement biases. Thirdly, subgroup analyses could only address gender, clinical presentation (symptomatic versus radiographic OA), anatomical site, and geographic region due to data scarcity. Fourthly, the restriction to CVD and cancer mortality precludes examination of associations with other mortality determinants (respiratory, gastrointestinal, neurodegenerative, etc.) highly pertinent to elderly populations with mobility impairment. Fifthly, substantial heterogeneity likely originates from heterogeneous osteoarthritis diagnostic criteria. Finally, while sensitivity analyses corroborated primary outcomes, the detection of publication bias necessitates circumspect interpretation, particularly regarding all-cause mortality. Subsequent research must incorporate expanded mortality endpoints and cause-specific subgroup analyses.

Conclusions

This meta-analysis suggested that osteoarthritis is not associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality. However, OA appears to be associated with increased CVD mortality, with no significant increase observed in cancer mortality risk. These findings emphasize the importance of focusing on cardiovascular health in OA patients, although the mechanisms underlying this association require further elucidation.
