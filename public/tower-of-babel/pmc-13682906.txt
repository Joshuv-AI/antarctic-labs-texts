Intensivists’ Perspectives and Practices on Palliative Care in the ICU: A Nationwide Survey in Korea
Authors: Tae Jung Kim, Yoon Sun Jung, Ye Sul Jeung, Woo Hyun Cho, Shin Hye Yoo, Jae Young Moon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhile many countries have made progress in integrating palliative care into intensive care units (ICUs), implementation remains highly context-dependent. In Korea, palliative care integration within ICUs remains limited, and intensivists’ perspectives on its role and applicability have not been well studied.MethodsWe conducted a nationwide, cross-sectional online survey of certified intensivists and fellows registered with the Korean Society of Critical Care Medicine between April and May 2024. The questionnaire explored knowledge and confidence in palliative care, current ICU practices, referral patterns, perceived barriers, and preferred strategies for integration.ResultsA total of 241 intensivists responded (response rate, 23.9%). Most agreed that palliative care should be incorporated into ICU care (97.1%) and were willing to make referrals (98.3%). Advance care planning, symptom management, and family decision-making were the most expected domains of support. However, fewer reported involvement in psychological support, end-of-life care, or bereavement services. Referral practices were more frequent in institutions with palliative care teams, while lack of resources, unsupportive institutional culture, and unclear processes were commonly cited barriers. Clinical guidelines, professional education, and reimbursement reform were identified as priority strategies for integration.ConclusionKorean intensivists widely recognize the need for palliative care in the ICU but apply it within a limited scope. To strengthen integration, consensus on definitions, clear referral criteria, and supportive institutional and policy frameworks are required.

INTRODUCTION

Critically ill patients admitted to intensive care units (ICUs) frequently experience severe symptoms, functional decline, and psychological distress, creating a need for comprehensive, multidimensional care.123 Palliative care, which aims to relieve suffering and support patients and families facing serious illness, has been increasingly recognized as a key component of high-quality ICU care.4

International guidelines recommend the early integration of palliative care in the ICU, reporting benefits such as improved symptom control, better communication, and more goal-concordant decision-making.356789 However, the degree and mode of integration differ considerably across countries,710 influenced by variations in healthcare systems and cultural contexts.11

In Korea, nearly 300,000 patients are admitted to ICUs each year, where care is commonly provided for those with advanced cancer, cardiovascular disease, and other life-threatening conditions.12 Since the enactment of the Life-Sustaining Treatment Decisions Act in 2018, awareness of the need for palliative care has grown.1314 Nevertheless, ICU-based palliative care services remain underdeveloped.15 The distinction between palliative care and hospice care is often unclear in practice,16 and barriers such as insufficient reimbursement, limited institutional support, and shortages of specialized teams continue to hinder effective implementation. As a result, ICU physicians frequently deliver selected aspects of palliative care themselves, leading to wide variability in practice across hospitals.17

Although previous studies have examined perceptions of palliative care among oncologists and general physicians in Korea,1819 little is known about the perspectives of intensivists, who play a pivotal role in treatment planning and decision-making in the ICU. Their views are essential for understanding current practices and identifying barriers to the integration of palliative care in critical care settings.11

This nationwide survey was therefore conducted to investigate intensivists’ familiarity with and confidence in palliative care, current practices, referral patterns, perceived barriers, and preferred strategies for integration. By analyzing these perspectives, the study aims to provide baseline evidence that can inform institutional frameworks, educational programs, and policy initiatives to strengthen palliative care delivery in Korean ICUs.

METHODS

Study design and participants

A nationwide cross-sectional survey was conducted between April and May 2024 through an online questionnaire distributed by the Korean Society of Critical Care Medicine (KSCCM) to 1,010 certified intensivists and fellows in training. In Korea, intensivists are physicians who have completed subspecialty board certification in critical care through designated training programs.20 All responses were anonymous and non-identifiable.

Questionnaire

The questionnaire was developed based on a comprehensive literature review1271021 and refined through expert discussions involving palliative care specialists and intensivists. The final version was reviewed and formally agreed upon by the Ethics Committee of the KSCCM. The survey used the World Health Organization’s (WHO) definition of palliative care.4 The term end-of-life care was used only in specific survey items to refer to care provided during the dying phase, typically encompassing the last days or weeks of life, rather than as a separate overarching construct.

The survey included 17 core items covering knowledge and confidence in palliative care, current ICU practices, referral patterns and triggers, perceived barriers, and preferred strategies for integration. Items were measured on a 4-point Likert scale. The draft questionnaire was pilot-tested by three intensivists and two palliative care clinicians to ensure clarity and relevance. Demographic information—including age, sex, clinical, and ICU experience, specialty, institutional type, hospital volume, and geographic location—was also collected. The survey assessed the presence of specialty palliative care services at the institutional level without distinguishing specific service models.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are summarized as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables are reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). The frequency and proportion of responses were calculated for multiple-response and Likert-scale questions. For descriptive analysis, Likert responses were dichotomized into “Agree” (combining agree and absolutely agree) and “Not agree” (combining not agree and absolutely not agree). All statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 18.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

Ethics statement

This study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Seoul National University Hospital (IRB No. H-2404-015-1525). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their participation in the survey.

RESULTS

Characteristics of the participants and their institution

A total of 241 individuals responded to the survey, yielding a response rate of 23.9%. The demographic characteristics of respondents were broadly similar to those of the overall KSCCM membership, except for a higher proportion of respondents in their 30s and a slightly lower representation of anesthesiology (Supplementary Table 1). Most respondents were male (68.5%) and in their 30s or 40s (70.6%). The median clinical experience was 17 years, and the median ICU experience was 8 years. The majority were affiliated with tertiary hospitals (73.4%) and worked in metropolitan areas (60.6%). Specialty palliative care services were available in 47.3% of the institutions, unavailable in 34.4%, and unknown in 18.3% (Table 1).

Perceived necessity and expectations for ICU palliative care

Nearly all respondents agreed that palliative care should be integrated into ICU care (97.1%) and expressed willingness to refer patients (98.3%). The most commonly cited reasons for integration (Fig. 1) were alleviation of patient anxiety and depression (65.8%), management of physical symptoms (65.4%), and reduction of potentially inappropriate medical interventions (60.7%). Additional anticipated benefits included improved communication and decision-making support, greater satisfaction and understanding among patients and families, and relief of family burden. In contrast, fewer respondents expected palliative care to address socioeconomic challenges (38.9%) or to shorten ICU or hospital stays (22.2%).

When asked about the specific domains in which they sought support from palliative care services (Fig. 1), respondents most frequently identified advance care planning, symptom management, and family decision-making support. Expectations were relatively lower for psychological support, socioeconomic assistance, legal and ethical consultations, and bereavement care.

Conceptual understanding and self-confidence

Most respondents were familiar with the terms “palliative care” (97.5%) and “hospice care” (99.2%). Their self-rated knowledge level was moderately high, with a median score of 7 (IQR, 5–8). As shown in Fig. 2, confidence was greatest in pain and symptom management (88.0%), illness education and counseling (83.8%), and family decision-making support (75.5%). In contrast, fewer respondents felt confident in addressing legal and ethical issues (47.7%) or providing bereavement support (28.2%).

Actual provision of palliative care in the ICU

The most commonly reported elements of ICU-based palliative care were symptom management (83.4%) and assessment of socioeconomic needs (68.9%) (Fig. 2). Support for family decision-making (66.0%) and illness-related communication (60.6%) was also frequently provided. In contrast, psychological support for patients (46.9%) and families (42.7%), advance care planning (39.8%), and end-of-life care (27.8%) were less often delivered, while bereavement care was least common (27.8%). These services were performed mainly by ICU physicians (69.3%) and nurses (45.2%), with palliative care specialists and non-medical personnel involved in about 20% of cases.

Referral practices and underlying reasons

Of the 241 respondents, 127 (52.7%) had referred patients to a palliative care specialist or team, while 114 (47.3%) had not (Fig. 3A). The most frequent reasons for referral were advance care planning and discussions on life-sustaining treatment (61.4%), support for surrogate decision-making (48.0%), and assistance with socioeconomic issues (42.5%) (Fig. 3B). In contrast, the main barriers to referral were the lack of available specialists (72.8%), unfamiliarity with referral processes (26.3%), and uncertainty regarding appropriate timing (19.3%) (Fig. 3C). Referral was more common among respondents working in institutions with specialty palliative care services (70.2%) compared with those without such services (41.0%). In addition, intensivists with more than 10 years of ICU experience reported higher referral rates (60.4%) than those with less experience (44.9%).

Triggers for initiating ICU palliative care

Respondents showed high levels of agreement regarding appropriate triggers for palliative care referral in the ICU. Commonly endorsed clinical conditions included advanced organ failure (95.4%), advanced cancer (94.6%), multiple organ failure (91.7%), and cardiac arrest with neurological compromise (91.3%). In contrast, agreement was lower for ICU readmission (41.1%) and repeated hospitalizations (50.2%). Non-clinical triggers were also recognized, particularly unrealistic goals of care (78.4%) and conflicts in decision-making (72.6%) (Table 2).

Perceived barriers to palliative care integration

Respondents identified several barriers to implementing palliative care in the ICU at both the institutional and patient–family levels. The most frequently reported obstacles were patient and family preferences for aggressive treatment (89.2%) and the lack of reimbursement policies (88.8%). Other barriers included limited institutional support for low-revenue services (84.7%), prognostic uncertainty (83.4%), and an unsupportive institutional culture (76.4%) (Supplementary Table 2).

Preferred models and strategies for integration

Respondents were also asked to identify their preferred models of ICU palliative care delivery and strategies to support implementation. The integrated model was most frequently selected (47.7%), followed by the consultative (40.7%) and multidisciplinary (11.6%) models (Supplementary Table 3). The strategies most often endorsed were the development of clinical guidelines, targeted education for ICU professionals, and reforms in the reimbursement system. Less frequently cited strategies included expanding the specialty palliative care workforce and enhancing patient and family education (Supplementary Table 4).

DISCUSSION

Our study revealed that while Korean intensivists widely recognize the necessity of palliative care in the ICU, their understanding of its scope remains fragmented. Most respondents associated palliative care with symptom management and decision-making support, while far fewer engaged in or expected support for psychosocial, end-of-life, or bereavement care. This selective view indicates that palliative care in Korean ICUs is still perceived as an adjunct for acute symptom control and decision making support2223 rather than as a comprehensive, interdisciplinary approach as defined in global frameworks. Despite providing the WHO definition in the survey, responses demonstrated the lack of a shared conceptual foundation, underscoring the need for national consensus on the essential components of ICU palliative care.24 This narrow scope likely reflects multiple, overlapping factors.2526 Limited formal training in psychosocial, spiritual, and bereavement care may contribute to lower self-confidence in these non-clinical domains. In addition, ICU-specific structural constraints—such as heavy workloads, time pressure, and limited manpower—may further limit the feasibility of providing such care in routine practice. These different mechanisms may have distinct implications for how palliative care integration is supported in ICU settings.

The conceptual ambiguity observed in this study is consistent with international findings,2721252728 but contrasts in important ways with reports from other clinical specialties. Nationwide surveys of Korean oncologists18 suggest a relatively clear conceptual understanding of early palliative care, with broad recognition of its role in advance care planning, end-of-life care, and symptom management. However, these studies also demonstrate a persistent gap between favorable attitudes and actual practice. In contrast, intensivists in our study demonstrated a more restricted conceptualization of palliative care itself, with a predominant focus on symptom control and life-sustaining treatment decisions and less engagement in broader psychosocial or bereavement care. These differences likely reflect ICU-specific features, including substantial prognostic uncertainty, rapidly evolving clinical trajectories, and time-pressured decision-making. In the Korean context, these challenges are further compounded by structural and policy-level constraints. Unlike some high-resource countries that have implemented structured ICU palliative care programs, Korea lacks standardized service models and specialist workforce capacity. The distinction between hospice and palliative care also remains unclear in daily practice, shaped in part by the implementation of the Life-Sustaining Treatment Decisions Act in 2018. This has led many intensivists to equate palliative care primarily with discussions around life-sustaining treatment withdrawal,1415 while broader domains such as psychosocial or bereavement support remain underdeveloped. Furthermore, the absence of dedicated reimbursement mechanisms and limited institutional investment in non-revenue–generating services have created structural disincentives for integration.2527 These contextual barriers may explain why intensivists continue to rely heavily on individual judgment rather than standardized triggers or collaborative frameworks.29

Referral practices in this study highlight differences in perception among Korean intensivists. Korean intensivists in this study endorsed advanced cancer, multi-organ failure, and neurological compromise as referral triggers, but showed less agreement on repeated hospitalizations or ICU readmissions, which are commonly recommended in international guidelines.9 This suggests that, in Korea, referrals are often reactive and individualized rather than guided by systematic protocols.2728 Developing locally relevant, evidence-informed triggers would help ensure that ICU patients receive more timely and equitable access to palliative care, even in ambiguous clinical scenarios.223031

Respondents identified the development of clinical guidelines, targeted education, and improved financial and institutional support as top priorities. These strategies are particularly relevant in Korea, where institutional culture often prioritizes life-prolonging interventions and where palliative care teams remain scarce outside tertiary centers. Clinical guidelines can provide intensivists with clearer criteria for referral and delineation of roles,39 while educational initiatives are needed to build confidence in underrepresented areas such as psychosocial care, bereavement support, and legal–ethical issues.2832 Such educational initiatives may include structured communication skills training focused on discussing prognosis, uncertainty, and emotional distress with families3334; case-based learning on psychosocial support35; and targeted education on legal and ethical frameworks governing life-sustaining treatment decisions in the ICU.36 Interdisciplinary education involving palliative care specialists, social workers, and legal or ethics consultants may further help ICU clinicians translate these competencies into routine practice.37

At the policy level, reimbursement reform will be essential to incentivize hospitals to invest in sustainable ICU palliative care programs. Such reform requires clearer delineation of the core palliative care activities delivered in the ICU, many of which involve substantial time, coordination, and professional effort but are not readily visible within routine care structures. Time-intensive family counseling constitutes a substantial and often underappreciated component of ICU palliative care that extends beyond routine bedside communication.363839 It provides psychosocial support, facilitates complex decision-making, and often requires dedicated professional involvement. In addition, multidisciplinary involvement in palliative care represents another high-burden activity, taking different forms such as interdisciplinary family meetings and team-based discussions.404142 At present, these essential activities are delivered without dedicated reimbursement mechanisms, highlighting a critical mismatch between clinical practice and existing payment structures. Leadership engagement and interprofessional collaboration will also be critical to overcoming cultural resistance and embedding palliative care into routine ICU practice.11242729

This study has several limitations. First, the low response rate (23.9%) raises concerns about selection and non-response bias, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to the broader intensivist population across diverse practice settings. It is plausible that intensivists with greater interest in or more favorable attitudes toward palliative care were more likely to participate, which may have led to an overestimation of overall support for palliative care integration. Second, reliance on self-reported data may not reflect actual practice, and predefined survey items may have restricted nuanced responses. Third, the analysis was limited to descriptive statistics without adjustment for potential confounders, limiting interpretation of subgroup differences. Finally, many participants worked in institutions without established palliative care services, so some responses may reflect theoretical understanding rather than direct experience. Despite these limitations, this study provides critical insights into the current perceptions and practices surrounding ICU palliative care and highlights areas for targeted improvement. Future research should incorporate qualitative methods to explore clinicians’ conceptualizations in greater depth and evaluate pilot programs aimed at improving care delivery in diverse ICU settings.

In conclusion, although Korean intensivists broadly recognize the value of palliative care in the ICU, their understanding remains fragmented and shaped by structural, cultural, and policy constraints. Bridging this gap through shared definitions, context-specific referral guidelines, targeted education, and reimbursement reform is essential to achieve meaningful and sustainable integration of palliative care into Korean critical care practice.
