Linear and Nonlinear Optical Properties of Molecules from Real-Time Propagation Based on the Bethe-Salpeter Equation
Štěpán Marek, Jan Wilhelm

Abstract
We present a real-time propagation method for computing linear and nonlinear optical properties of molecules based on the Bethe-Salpeter equation. The method follows the time evolution of the one-particle density matrix under an external electric field. We include electron-electron interaction effects through a self-energy based on the screened exchange approximation. Quasiparticle energies are taken from a prior GW calculation to construct the effective single-particle Hamiltonian and we represent all operators and wavefunctions in an atom-centered Gaussian basis. We benchmark the accuracy of the real-time propagation against the standard linear-response Bethe-Salpeter equation using a set of organic molecules. We find very good agreement when computing linear-response isotropic polarizability spectra from both approaches, with a mean absolute deviation of 30 meV in peak positions. Beyond linear response, we simulate second harmonic generation and optical rectification in a non-centrosymmetric molecule. We foresee broad applicability of real-time propagation based on the Bethe-Salpeter equation for the study of linear and nonlinear optical properties of molecules as the method has a similar computational cost as time-dependent density functional theory with hybrid functionals.

Introduction

Recent advances in the optical microscopy
Siday et al. (2024)
;
Yang et al. (2020)
,
scanning tunneling microscopy
Cocker et al. (2016)
and
laser pulse control
Corkum and Krausz (2007)
;
Krausz and Ivanov (2009)

have significantly increased the amount of experimentally accessible information
about the light-driven excitations of matter. In many investigations,
non-linear optical response
Herrmann et al. (2025)
;
Schmid et al. (2021)
;
Martín-Hernández
et al. (2025)
;
Itatani et al. (2005)
properties
become important.
This calls for theory of non-linear optical responses which could help to gain further insights
into the mechanism behind the observed nonlinear optical phenomena.

Commonly used methods for the theoretical description of optical excitations and absorption spectra
from first principles include time-dependent density functional theory
Martin (2020)
;
Ullrich (2012)
;
Li et al. (2020)
;
He et al. (2022)
;
Attaccalite et al. (2019)

(TDDFT) and the
GW
-Bethe-Salpeter equation (
GW
-BSE) approach
Martin (2020)
;
Onida et al. (2002)
;
Rohlfing and Louie (1998)
.
However, these methods are subject to certain limitations.
For TDDFT, when using local and semilocal approximate exchange-correlation potentials,
the excitonic effects, stemming from the screened Coulomb interaction between the excited charge carriers, are
described inacurrately or may be missing entirely
Ullrich (2012)
. This limitation in TDDFT can be improved by using more advanced functionals, such as tuned hybrid functionals
Martin (2020)
. However, developing a single functional that reliably describes excited-state phenomena across a broad range of matter, including metals, atomically thin semiconductors, and molecules, remains a major challenge.
Furthermore, the computational
cost of hybrid functionals is significantly higher than the cost of local or semilocal functionals.

Compared to TDDFT with local or semilocal functionals, the
GW
–BSE approach provides a more accurate description of
excitonic effects and optical spectra, as
GW
-BSE naturally includes the screened Coulomb interaction between the excited charge carriers
Onida et al. (2002)
;
Blase et al. (2020)
.
Common implementations of
GW
-BSE
Liu et al. (2020)
;
Holzer and Klopper (2019)
;
Bruneval et al. (2015)
;
Blase et al. (2020)
;
Förster and Visscher (2022)
;
Tölle et al. (2021)
;
Sangalli et al. (2019)
;
Zhou et al. (2025)
employ the Casida framework to study electronic excitations in the linear response limit, so for relatively weak driving electric fields.
Due to advances in laser spectroscopy
Boyd (2008)
, also nonlinear optical excitations are commonly studied, which go beyond this Casida framework.
Specific nonlinear optical properties, in particular low-order responsenses in second and third order, can be computed in frequency domain in
GW
-BSE
Taghizadeh and Pedersen (2019)
;
Rauwolf et al. (2024)
;
Ruan et al. (2024)
,
but a general analytical expression to arbitrary order in the response remains elusive at present.

An alternative approach for studying electronic excitations propagates the electronic many-body system in real time and Fourier transforms the electric dipole to obtain nonlinear optical responses.
In a Green’s function framework, the exact propagation can be described by the Kadanoff-Baym equations
Kadanoff and Baym (1962)
;
Stefanucci and van Leeuwen (2025)
;
Perfetto and Stefanucci (2023)
;
Semkat et al. (1999)
;
Tuovinen et al. (2021)
;
Reeves et al. (2025)
(KBEs).
The self-energy in KBEs is time non-local, which increases the computational effort considerably.

In order to reduce the computational effort of KBEs, we apply the real-time Bethe-Salpeter equation approach
Attaccalite et al. (2011)
;
Attaccalite et al. (2018)
;
Chan et al. (2021)
;
Perfetto et al. (2015)
;
Hou et al. (2025)
;
Sangalli (2021)

(RT-BSE), which uses time local approximations
for the self-energy
to arrive at algorithms with computational cost
comparable to TDDFT with hybrid functionals. Specifically, the screened
exchange
Hedin (1965)
;
Casida and Chong (1989)
;
Betzinger et al. (2015)
(SEX) approximation is applied.

RT-BSE allows for evaluation
of non-linear effects, such as second harmonic generation or
optical rectification
Boyd (2008)
;
Chan et al. (2021)
. In the time propagation scheme, the
non-linear effects are present to arbitrary order. For these effects,
description of excitons is again crucial for accurate determination of the absorption/emission spectra.

We report an implementation of the RT-BSE approach for molecular systems in the CP2K

Kühne et al. (2020)

program. We
performed a benchmark on standard test set of organic molecules
Schreiber et al. (2008)
comparing excitation energies from
the real time (RT) approach to the ones from the linear response
Attaccalite et al. (2011)
;
Onida et al. (2002)
;
Rohlfing and Louie (2000)
;
Blase et al. (2020)
(LR) approach.
Note that LR-BSE and RT-BSE approaches are equivalent in the linear response limit (see Ref.
1
or Supporting Information 1
(SI 1)).
Going beyond the linear response limit, we study non-linear
optical phenomena in molecules, specifically we
investigate the emergence of second harmonic generation
Boyd (2008)
and
optical rectification in the
non-centrosymmetric cysteine molecule.

2
Equation of Motion for the Density Operator within the Real-Time Bethe-Salpeter Approach

To study the interaction of a many-electron system with light, we follow the RT-BSE approach
described in Ref.
1
, which employs the von Neumann equation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\hat{\rho}(t)}{\partial t}=\frac{-\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}[\hat{H}^{\text{eff}}(t),\hat{\rho}(t)]

(1)

to compute the time evolution of the one-particle density operator
\hat{\rho}(t)
.
The time-dependent effective one-particle Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{\text{eff}}(t)=

\displaystyle\;\hat{h}^{G_{0}W_{0}}+\hat{U}(t)+\hat{V}^{\text{H}}[\hat{\rho}(t)]-\hat{V}^{\text{H}}[\hat{\rho}(0)]+\hat{\Sigma}^{\text{SEX}}[\hat{\rho}(t)]-\hat{\Sigma}^{\text{SEX}}[\hat{\rho}(0)]

(2)

where
\hat{h}^{G_{0}W_{0}}
is the static effective one-particle Hamiltonian operator obtained from a preceding DFT plus
G_{0}W_{0}
calculation
Reining (2018)
;
Golze et al. (2019)
,

\displaystyle\hat{h}^{G_{0}W_{0}}=\sum_{n}\left|\psi_{n}\right\rangle\epsilon^{G_{0}W_{0}}_{n}\left\langle\psi_{n}\right|

(3)

where
\left|\psi_{n}\right\rangle
is a Kohn-Sham DFT (KS-DFT) orbital and
\epsilon^{G_{0}W_{0}}_{n}
is the corresponding
G_{0}W_{0}
quasiparticle energy.
Note that the RT-BSE scheme can also be started from
GW
schemes including self-consistency, for example from
GW
with eigenvalue-selfconsistency in
G
(ev
GW_{0}
) which might align better with quasiparticle energies from higher-level theories
Golze et al. (2019)
;
Véril et al. (2018)
;
Schambeck et al. (2024)
;
Knysh et al. (2024)
.

\hat{V}^{\text{H}}

is the Hartree mean-field operator
and
\hat{\Sigma}^{\text{SEX}}
is the SEX self-energy operator, which are both specified
in more detail when rewriting the equation of motion in an atomic Gaussian basis.
In contrast to Ref.
1
, the Coulomb-hole
self-energy is not included, since it is exactly canceled in (
2
) for static screened potential fixed
at
G_{0}W_{0}
or ev
GW_{0}
screening.
The dynamics starts at
t=0
, and
\hat{\rho}(0)
is the initial density operator,

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(0)=\sum_{n}\left|\psi_{n}\right\rangle f_{n}\left\langle\psi_{n}\right|\,,

(4)

where
f_{n}\in\{0,1\}
is the occupation of the Kohn-Sham orbital
n
from the KS-DFT calculation.

\hat{U}(t)
is the time-dependent external field operator. In order to excite the system, we implement two options. First option is to apply an external time
dependent potential
\hat{U}(t)
in form of electric field in length gauge
Mattiat and Luber (2022)
;
Ditler et al. (2023)

\displaystyle\hat{U}(t)=eE(t)\bm{\epsilon}\cdot\hat{r}

(5)

where
E(t)
is the time-dependent electric-field amplitude,

\bm{\epsilon}
is the electric field polarization direction and
\hat{r}

is the position operator.

The second option is the delta kick
Attaccalite et al. (2011)
;
Mattiat and Luber (2022)
;
Yabana et al. (2006)
, which is
described in more detail in the SI 2.
In essence, the delta kick transforms the equilibrium density matrix
\hat{\rho}(0)
to a
non-equilibrium one via unitary transformation

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(0)\to\hat{\rho}^{\prime}=\mathrm{e}^{(-\mathrm{i}e/\hbar)I\bm{\epsilon}\cdot\hat{r}}\,\hat{\rho}(0)\,\mathrm{e}^{(\mathrm{i}e/\hbar)I\bm{\epsilon}\cdot\hat{r}}

(6)

where
I
is the scale of the kick and
\bm{\epsilon}
is the polarisation of the kick.
This transformation is associated with electric field with profile

\bm{E}(t)=I\bm{\epsilon}\delta(t)
, where
\delta(t)
is the Dirac delta function, hence
the name of the excitation scheme.

We compute the observables from the Fourier transform of the time-dependent dipole moment associated with the dynamics of the density matrix
Yabana et al. (2006)
;
Müller et al. (2020)

(see SI 3 for details). The Fourier transform
of the dipole moments is refined using the Padé approximant interpolation
Bruner et al. (2016)
;
Mattiat and Luber (2018)
;
Kick et al. (2024)
;
Leucke et al. (2025)
,
which allows the use of a fine frequency grid.

3
Real-time Bethe-Salpeter in a Gaussian Basis

We represent all operators appearing in the effective one-particle Hamiltonian (
2
) as matrices in the basis of atom-centered Gaussian orbitals
\left|\phi_{\mu}\right\rangle
.
Since this is a nonorthonormal basis set, the overlap matrix
\bm{S}
with elements
S_{\mu\nu}=\left\langle\phi_{\mu}\right|\left.\phi_{\nu}\right\rangle

enters the equation of motion (see SI 4 for details) as follows

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{\rho}}{\partial t}=\frac{-\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}\left(\bm{S}^{-1}\bm{H}^{\text{eff}}(t)\bm{\rho}(t)-\bm{\rho}(t)\bm{H}^{\text{eff}}(t)\bm{S}^{-1}\right)

(7)

where
\bm{H}^{\text{eff}}
is the matrix with elements of the respective operators introduced in Eq. (
2
),

\displaystyle H^{\text{eff}}_{\mu\nu}(t)=\left\langle\phi_{\mu}\right|\hat{H}^{\text{eff}}(t)\left|\phi_{\nu}\right\rangle=h^{G_{0}W_{0}}_{\mu\nu}+U_{\mu\nu}(t)+V^{\text{H}}_{\mu\nu}[\bm{\rho}(t)-\bm{\rho}(0)]+\Sigma^{\text{SEX}}_{\mu\nu}[\bm{\rho}(t)-\bm{\rho}(0)]

(8)

and
\bm{\rho}(t)
is the time-dependent density matrix with elements

\rho_{\mu\nu}(t)
that satisfy

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(t)=\sum_{\mu,\nu}\rho_{\mu\nu}(t)\left|\phi_{\mu}\right\rangle\left\langle\phi_{\nu}\right|\>.

(9)

These definitions ensure that time-dependent observables
X(t)
of an operator
\hat{X}
have
expectation values represented by traces without the presence of the overlap matrix, i.e.

\displaystyle X(t)=\trace\left(\hat{\rho}(t)\hat{X}\right)=\trace\left(\bm{\rho}(t)\bm{X}\right)\>,

(10)

where
\bm{X}
has matrix elements
X_{\mu\nu}=\left\langle\phi_{\mu}\right|\hat{X}\left|\phi_{\nu}\right\rangle
as discussed in more detail in the SI 4.

An initial density at time
t
is propagated using the enforced time reversal symmetry (ETRS)
scheme
Castro et al. (2004)

\displaystyle\bm{\rho}(t+\Delta t)=\exp\left(-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}\bm{S}^{-1}\bm{H}^{\text{eff}}(t+\Delta t)\frac{\Delta t}{2}\right)\bm{\rho}\left(t+\frac{\Delta t}{2}\right)\exp\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}\bm{H}^{\text{eff}}(t+\Delta t)\bm{S}^{-1}\frac{\Delta t}{2}\right)\>,

(11)

where

\displaystyle\bm{\rho}\left(t+\frac{\Delta t}{2}\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}\bm{S}^{-1}\bm{H}^{\text{eff}}(t)\frac{\Delta t}{2}\right)\bm{\rho}(t)\exp\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}\bm{H}^{\text{eff}}(t)\bm{S}^{-1}\frac{\Delta t}{2}\right)\>.

(12)

The matrix exponential in the equations above can be determined using the
Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff
Castro et al. (2004)
;
O’Rourke and Bowler (2015)

formula or by exact diagonalisation, more information is given in SI 5.

As can be seen from (
11
), ETRS is an implicit scheme - the effective Hamiltonian at
t+\Delta t

depends on
\bm{\rho}(t+\Delta t)
that we aim to compute. In our implementation, the implicit
dependence is solved by self-consistent (SC) iterations of the density matrix. Specifically, in iteration
n
,
we evaluate the effective Hamiltonian based on density matrix from previous iteration
\bm{\rho}_{n-1}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\bm{H}^{\text{eff}}_{n}(t+\Delta t)=\bm{H}^{\text{eff}}_{n}[\bm{\rho}_{n-1}(t+\Delta t)](t+\Delta t)

(13)

with initial guess
\bm{\rho}_{0}(t+\Delta t)
using
\bm{H}^{\text{eff}}[\bm{\rho}(t+\Delta t/2)](t+\Delta t)

for the propagation from
\bm{\rho}(t+\Delta t/2)
. The SC criterion is checked as follows: For consecutive
density matrices in the SC iterations, element-wise difference is evaluated. The maximum absolute value of the
difference is noted. If this maximum is smaller than the SC threshold, the iterations are considered converged.
Importantly, ETRS scheme conserves the idempotency of the density matrix, see SI 6 for more details.

The numerically most expensive part of the ETRS loop is to recalculate the effective Hamiltonian,
specifically to compute the SEX self-energy and Hartree matrix. In Gaussian orbitals,
these are determined as (see SI 7 for details)

\displaystyle V^{\text{H}}_{\mu\nu}[\bm{\rho}(t)]=\sum_{\lambda,\sigma}(\mu\nu|\sigma\lambda)\rho_{\lambda\sigma}(t)\>,

(14)

where

\displaystyle(\mu\nu|\sigma\lambda)=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}r\mathrm{d}^{3}r^{\prime}\phi^{*}_{\mu}(\bm{r})\phi_{\nu}(\bm{r})\frac{e^{2}}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}|\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime}|}\phi^{*}_{\sigma}(\bm{r}^{\prime})\phi_{\lambda}(\bm{r}^{\prime})\>,

(15)

and

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\text{SEX}}_{\mu\nu}[\bm{\rho}(t)]=-\sum_{\lambda,\sigma}W_{\mu\lambda,\sigma\nu}\rho_{\lambda\sigma}(t)\>,

(16)

where

\displaystyle W_{\mu\lambda,\sigma\nu}=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}r\mathrm{d}^{3}r^{\prime}\phi^{*}_{\mu}(\bm{r})\phi_{\lambda}(\bm{r})W(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega{=}0)\phi^{*}_{\sigma}(\bm{r}^{\prime})\phi_{\nu}(\bm{r}^{\prime})

(17)

and
W(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega{=}0)
is the static screened Coulomb interaction, determined from
G_{0}W_{0}
.
In both (
14
) and (
16
), we employ the resolution of identity (RI) to separate the 4-center kernels into 3-center ones
Wilhelm et al. (2021)
;
Graml et al. (2024)
.
Details are given in SI 8.

Note that the RT-BSE framework discussed in this work is closely related to real-time Hartree-Fock (RT-HF), which forms a fundamental component of real-time TDDFT when hybrid functionals are employed.
In RT-HF, the unscreened Coulomb interaction
V
is used in the exchange self-energy, in contrast to the screened Coulomb interaction
W
utilized in the screened-exchange self-energy (
16
).
Importantly,
W
only needs to be computed once at the beginning of the simulation.
As a result, the computational cost of the time propagation is the same in RT-BSE, RT-HF, and real-time TDDFT with hybrid functionals.

3.1
Observables

The main observable recovered from the time propagation shown in this work is the time-dependent dipole moment

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}(t)=\text{Tr}\left(\hat{\rho}(t)\hat{\mu}\right)=-e\text{Tr}\left(\hat{\rho}(t)\hat{r}\right)

(18)

The absorption spectrum of the molecules is proportional to the isotropic part of the electric polarizability
Wu et al. (2016)

\alpha(E)

\displaystyle\alpha_{jk}(\omega)=\frac{\mu_{j}(\omega)}{E_{k}(\omega)}

(19)

where
\mu_{j}(E)
is the Fourier transform of the component
j
of
\bm{\mu}(t)
and
E_{k}(E)
is the k-component
of the Fourier transform of electric field
\bm{E}(t)
. The isotropic part is then recovered by a trace

\displaystyle\alpha^{\text{iso}}(\omega)=\frac{1}{3}\text{Tr}\left(\bm{\alpha}(\omega)\right)=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{j\in\{x,y,z\}}\alpha_{jj}(\omega)

(20)

The polarizability determined by the RT-BSE method is compared with the LR-BSE polarizability, which is determined
from
Ullrich (2012)

\displaystyle\alpha^{\text{iso}}(\omega)=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{n,j\in\{x,y,z\}}\frac{2\Omega_{n}|\left\langle\Psi_{n}\right|\hat{r}_{j}\left|\Psi_{0}\right\rangle|^{2}}{(\hbar\omega+i\eta)^{2}-\Omega_{n}^{2}}\>,

(21)

where
n
enumerates LR-BSE excitation energies
\Omega_{n}
,
\hat{r}_{j}
is the position operator for position component
j
,
\eta

is small artificial broadening,
\left|\Psi_{0}\right\rangle
is the equilibrium state and
\left|\Psi_{n}\right\rangle
is the excitation state.

4.2
Fourier Transform and Padé Approximant Refinement

To make meaningful comparisons between the chosen molecules in both LR-BSE and RT-BSE, we
identified clear peaks present in both of the refined spectra obtained from the methods, fitted the peaks with Lorentzian
function
Ullrich (2012)
and compared the peak positions between the two methods.

The initial spectra are obtained by fast Fourier transform of the time trajectory of the dipole moment
\bm{\mu}(t)

of each molecule. We propagate the dynamics for 20 fs, using 20 000 steps
of 1 as (timestep of 1 as is shown to be converged for cysteine molecule in SI 11).
Since the value of the dipole moment at the end is different from the initial value, we apply
artificial exponential damping
\gamma
Yabana et al. (2006)
;
Müller et al. (2020)
during post-processing,

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}(\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\omega+\mathrm{i}\gamma)t}\bm{\mu}(t)\>,

(22)

which acts as a window function of the Fourier transform and effectively broadens the spectrum. A value of
\gamma=0.2\>\text{fs}^{-1}
was chosen for the damping in order
to reduce the amplitude of dipole moment oscillations by
\mathrm{e}^{-4}
by the end
of the 20 fs propagation.
Further details are given in the SI 3.

We obtain refined spectra by fitting a Padé approximant to
\bm{\mu}(E)
and interpolating
on a grid of energies from 0 to 20 eV, with 20 meV steps. The refinement uses
N/2
Padé parameters,
where
N
is the total number of the points considered for the fit,
consistently with previous work
Bruner et al. (2016)
. Points of energy up to 200 eV from the initial
spectra are used for the fitting.

6
Nonlinear-Optical Effects in Cysteine

In linear optics, the response
\bm{\mu}(t)
of the molecule to light is proportional to the electric field amplitude
E_{0}
of the incoming light wave.
In nonlinear optics, the response
\bm{\mu}(t)
includes higher-order terms of
E_{0}
that become significant for large
E_{0}
.
Our chosen molecule to study nonlinear effects is cysteine, an amino acid, see molecular structure sketched in the inset of Fig.
3
.
Cysteine lacks inversion symmetry and therefore allows for second harmonic generation
Boyd (2008)
.
Furthermore, being an amino acid, our choice of cysteine demonstrates the applicability of our method to
a molecule present in proteins.
The linear absorption spectrum of cysteine is shown in Fig.
3
with an absorption peak at 6.0 eV.
We study nonlinear effects in the electric field amplitude using an electric-field pulse

7
Conclusions

We have implemented the real-time Bethe-Salpeter equation approach via propagation of the density
matrix with screened-exchange self-energy in CP2K. We benchmarked the accuracy of the implementation with
respect to linear response Bethe-Salpeter equation approach, showing an mean average absolute deviation of
peak positions in the isotropic polarizability of only 30 meV.
The application potential of the method was demonstrated by showing the emergence of the
second harmonic peak and optical rectification in the dipole moment magnitude of the amino acid cysteine.
The RT-BSE method is an important alternative approach to LR-BSE,
expanding it to capture non-linear optical effects
and allowing for explicit control of the pulse shape, both of which are inaccessible in
standard LR-BSE.

LR-BSE and RT-BSE Equivalence

Before continuing to the specifics of the implementation, let us quickly show the equivalence of
the RT-BSE approach with the LR-BSE method in the linear regime.
In Ref.
1
, authors show that the real-time
propagation of density matrix with COHSEX
self-energy in linear regime leads to equations are formally equivalent to standard Bethe-Salpeter equation.
Here, we briefly show a slightly less general way of deriving the Bethe-Salpeter equation in the
explicit form shown for example in Ref.
2
,
again based on the COHSEX equation of motion (1) in the main text.

Suppose that in equilibrium, the density matrix describing the effective single-particle states
is
\hat{\rho}^{0}

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}^{0}=\sum_{m}f_{m}\left|m\right\rangle\left\langle m\right|\>,

(1)

where
\left|m\right\rangle
are the equilibrium Hamiltonian (
\hat{H}^{0}
) eigenstates

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{0}\left|m\right\rangle=\hbar\omega_{m}\left|m\right\rangle\>,

(2)

where
\hbar\omega_{m}
is the energy eigenvalue, the eigenvectors are orthonormal

\left\langle m\right|\left.n\right\rangle=\delta_{mn}
and
f_{m}
is a limit of the
Fermi distribution for zero temperature - if the highest occupied molecular orbital of the system
is
M
, then

\displaystyle f_{m}=\begin{cases}1\>\mathrm{if}\>m\leq M\\
0\>\mathrm{if}\>m>M\\
\end{cases}\>.

(3)

Now, suppose that the state of the system is changed so that the state is now described by density
matrix
\hat{\rho}(t)=\hat{\rho}^{0}+\Delta\hat{\rho}(t)
.
Inserting into the equation of motion leads to

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Delta\hat{\rho}}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial\hat{\rho}}{\partial t}=\frac{-i}{\hbar}\left[\hat{H}^{\text{eff}}(t),\hat{\rho}^{0}+\Delta\hat{\rho}(t)\right]\>.

(4)

Without the external field
\hat{U}(t)
, the
only time-dependence in the Hamiltonian is present due to the changes in the density matrix, i.e.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Delta\hat{\rho}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\frac{-i}{\hbar}\left(\left[\hat{H}^{0},\hat{\rho}^{0}\right]+\left[\hat{H}^{0},\Delta\hat{\rho}(t)\right]+\left[\hat{V}^{\text{Hartree}}[\hat{\rho}(t)]-\hat{V}^{\text{Hartree}}[\hat{\rho}^{0}],\hat{\rho}^{0}+\Delta\hat{\rho}(t)\right]+\right.

\displaystyle+\left.\left[\hat{\Sigma}^{\text{COHSEX}}[\hat{\rho}(t)]-\hat{\Sigma}^{\text{COHSEX}}[\hat{\rho}^{0}],\hat{\rho}^{0}+\Delta\hat{\rho}(t)\right]\right)\>.

(5)

The first commutator is zero. For the resolution of the rest, it is useful to transform to the
basis of eigenstates of
\hat{H}^{0}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Delta\rho_{mn}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\Biggl(\left[\bm{H}^{0},\Delta\bm{\rho}(t)\right]_{mn}+

\displaystyle+\sum_{p,q,r}(mp|rq)\Delta\rho_{qr}(t)(\bm{\rho}^{0}+\Delta\bm{\rho}(t))_{pn}-(\bm{\rho}^{0}+\Delta\bm{\rho}(t))_{mp}(pn|rq)\Delta\rho_{qr}(t)-

\displaystyle-\left.\sum_{p,q,r}W_{mq,rp}\Delta\rho_{qr}(t)(\bm{\rho}^{0}+\Delta\bm{\rho}(t))_{pn}-(\bm{\rho}^{0}+\Delta\bm{\rho}(t))_{mp}W_{pq,rn}\Delta\rho_{qr}(t)\right)\>.

(6)

Now, we restrict ourselves to linear regime, i.e., we neglect higher than linear powers of

\Delta\bm{\rho}(t)
. Furthermore, we substitute for
\bm{\rho}^{0}
from (
1
), which
leads to

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Delta\rho_{mn}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\Biggl(\hbar(\omega_{m}-\omega_{n})\Delta\rho_{mn}(t)+

\displaystyle+\sum_{p,q}(mn|qp)\Delta\rho_{pq}(t)f_{n}-f_{m}(mn|qp)\Delta\rho_{pq}(t)-

\displaystyle-\left.\sum_{p,q}W_{mp,qn}\Delta\rho_{pq}(t)f_{n}-f_{m}W_{mp,qn}\Delta\rho_{pq}(t)\right)\>.

(7)

After some reordering, the equation of motion in linear response regime
for the perturbation
\Delta\bm{\rho}(t)
is

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Delta\rho_{mn}}{\partial t}=i(\omega_{n}-\omega_{m})\Delta\rho_{mn}(t)-\frac{i}{\hbar}(f_{n}-f_{m})\sum_{p,q}\left((mn|qp)-W_{mp,qn}\right)\Delta\rho_{pq}(t)

(8)

By applying the Fourier transform, we obtain

\displaystyle\hbar\omega\Delta\rho_{mn}(\omega)=-\hbar(\omega_{n}-\omega_{m})\Delta\rho_{mn}(E)+(f_{n}-f_{m})\sum_{p,q}\left((mn|qp)-W_{mp,qn}\right)\Delta\rho_{pq}(\omega)

(9)

This is as far as we can get for a general form of
\Delta\rho_{mn}
. We now choose a specific ansatz
for the structure of
\Delta\rho_{mn}
- we allow for it to only contain off-diagonal terms in
the occupied+unoccupied blocks. Specifically, we write

\displaystyle\Delta\hat{\rho}=\sum_{mn}\left(X_{nm}f_{n}(1-f_{m})+Y_{mn}f_{m}(1-f_{n})\right)\left|m\right\rangle\left\langle n\right|

(10)

1.1
Linear Response Density via Delta-Kick

In order to justify the ansatz (
10
), consider that the initial
non-equilibrium part of the density can result from applying the delta-kick,
described later on. The effect of the delta kick can be described as

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(t=0^{+})=\mathrm{e}^{-i\hat{A}}\hat{\rho}^{0}\mathrm{e}^{i\hat{A}}=\hat{\rho}^{0}+\Delta\rho(t=0)\>,

(11)

where
\hat{A}
is a Hermitian operator. Evaluating
\hat{\rho}(t=0^{+})
in the basis of
original Hamiltonian eigenstates
\left|m\right\rangle
leads to

\displaystyle\left\langle m\right|\hat{\rho}(t=0^{+})\left|n\right\rangle=\sum_{p,q}\left\langle m\right|\mathrm{e}^{-i\hat{A}}\left|p\right\rangle\left\langle p\right|\hat{\rho}^{0}\left|q\right\rangle\left\langle q\right|\mathrm{e}^{i\hat{A}}\left|n\right\rangle

(12)

Approximating the exponential and resulting matrix products to linear order in
\hat{A}
leads to

\displaystyle\left\langle m\right|\hat{\rho}(t=0^{+})\left|n\right\rangle=\left\langle m\right|\hat{\rho}^{0}\left|n\right\rangle+\sum_{p,q}(-i)\left\langle m\right|\hat{A}\left|p\right\rangle\left\langle p\right|\hat{\rho}^{0}\left|q\right\rangle\delta_{qn}+i\delta_{mp}\left\langle p\right|\hat{\rho}^{0}\left|q\right\rangle\left\langle q\right|\hat{A}\left|n\right\rangle

(13)

Substituting from (
1
)

\displaystyle\left\langle m\right|\hat{\rho}(t=0^{+})\left|n\right\rangle=f_{m}\delta_{mn}+\sum_{p}(-i)A_{mp}f_{p}\delta_{pn}+if_{m}\delta_{mq}A_{qn}=f_{m}\delta_{mn}+i(f_{m}{-}f_{n})A_{mn}

(14)

First of all, we note that the perturbed density matrix remains Hermitian

\displaystyle\left\langle n\right|\hat{\rho}(t=0^{+})\left|m\right\rangle^{*}

\displaystyle=(f_{n}\delta_{nm}+i(f_{n}-f_{m})A_{nm})^{*}=

\displaystyle=f_{m}\delta_{mn}+i(f_{m}-f_{n})A_{mn}=\left\langle m\right|\hat{\rho}(t=0^{+})\left|n\right\rangle

(15)

since
\hat{A}
is Hermitian.
The
f_{m}\delta_{mn}
term corresponds to equilibrium density element
\left\langle m\right|\hat{\rho}^{0}\left|n\right\rangle
.
The second term (corresponding to
\Delta{\rho}(0)
) is non-zero only if one of
m,n
belongs to the
occupied subspace while the other belongs to the unoccupied subspace. We can change the second term
further

\displaystyle\left\langle m\right|\Delta\hat{\rho}(0)\left|n\right\rangle

\displaystyle=iA_{mn}(f_{m}-f_{n})=iA_{mn}(f_{m}-f_{m}f_{n}+f_{m}f_{n}-f_{n})=

\displaystyle=iA_{mn}f_{m}(1-f_{n})-iA_{mn}f_{n}(1-f_{m})

(16)

We can see that this form of density perturbation follows the ansatz (
10
)
(with
Y_{mn}=iA_{mn}
and
X_{mn}=-iA_{nm}
).
To summarize, the off-diagonal occupied/unoccupied block structure of the density matrix perturbation
is a general feature of weak (linear response regime) unitary transformation of the density matrix
away from the equilibrium density matrix.

Casida Formalism

Substituting to (
9
) from (
10
), we get

\displaystyle\hbar\omega(X_{nm}(\omega)f_{n}(1-f_{m})+Y_{mn}(\omega)f_{m}(1-f_{n}))=

\displaystyle-\hbar(\omega_{n}-\omega_{m})(X_{nm}(\omega)f_{n}(1-f_{m})+Y_{mn}(\omega)f_{m}(1-f_{n}))+

\displaystyle+(f_{n}-f_{m})\sum_{p,q}((mn|qp)-W_{mp,qn})(X_{qp}(\omega)f_{q}(1-f_{p})+Y_{pq}(\omega)f_{p}(1-f_{q}))

(17)

We now notice
that only the values of
m,n
for occupied + unoccupied orbital pairs lead to non-trivial equations.
Specifically, setting
i,j
to stand for indices of occupied orbitals and
a,b
for indices of

\displaystyle\hbar\omega X_{ia}(\omega)=\hbar(\omega_{a}-\omega_{i})X_{ia}(\omega)+\sum_{j\>\text{occ.},b\>\text{unocc.}}((ai|jb)-W_{ab,ji})X_{jb}+((ai|bj)-W_{aj,bi})Y_{jb}

(18)

\displaystyle\hbar\omega Y_{ia}(E)=-\hbar(\omega_{a}-\omega_{i})Y_{ia}(E)-\sum_{j\>\text{occ.},b\>\text{unocc.}}((ia|bj)-W_{ij,ba})Y_{jb}+((ia|jb)-W_{ib,ja})X_{jb}

(19)

This is equivalent to the Bethe-Salpeter approach via the Casida equation. Specifically,
defining

\displaystyle A_{ia,jb}=\hbar(\omega_{a}-\omega_{i})\delta_{ij}\delta_{ab}+(ia|jb)-W_{ij,ab}

\displaystyle B_{ia,jb}=(ia|bj)-W_{ib,aj}\>,

(20)

one can write

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\bm{A}&\bm{B}\\
-\bm{B}^{\dagger}&-\bm{A}^{\dagger}\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{X}\\
\bm{Y}\\
\end{pmatrix}=\hbar\omega\begin{pmatrix}\bm{X}\\
\bm{Y}\\
\end{pmatrix}

(21)

which is consistent for example with notation in
Liu et al.
Liu et al. (2020)

Delta Kick for Homogeneous Excitation

Instead of a time resolved field pulse
\bm{E}(t)
, we might choose to apply a delta-like pulse

\displaystyle\bm{E}(t)=I\bm{\epsilon}\delta(t)

(22)

where
I
is the scale of the delta pulse and
\bm{\epsilon}
is the polarization
Mattiat and Luber (2022)
.
We resolve the effect of the delta pulse
on the density of states via explicitly integrating the equation of motion. Let
\hat{\rho}^{-}
be the
density matrix operator before the application of the pulse and
\hat{\rho}^{+}
the density matrix operator
immediately after the application, i.e.

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}^{\pm}=\lim_{\Delta t\to 0^{+}}\hat{\rho}(\pm\frac{1}{2}\Delta t)

(23)

Before the application of the pulse,
\hat{\rho}^{-}
corresponds to the starting
\hat{\rho}_{0}
provided by the
initial guess from DFT+
G_{0}W_{0}
. The equation of motion to zeroth order in
I
is solved as

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}^{+}=\hat{\rho}^{-}-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{-\Delta t/2}^{\Delta t/2}dt[\hat{H}_{0},\hat{\rho}(t)]\approx\hat{\rho}^{-}-\frac{i}{\hbar}\Delta t[\hat{H}_{0},\hat{\rho}^{-}]

(24)

where
\hat{H}_{0}
is the time-independent DFT+
G_{0}W_{0}
Hamiltonian. To the first order in
I

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}^{+}=\hat{\rho}^{-}-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{-\Delta t/2}^{\Delta t/2}dt[\hat{H}_{0}+I\delta(t)\bm{\epsilon}\cdot\hat{r},\hat{\rho}(t)]\approx

(25)

\displaystyle\approx\hat{\rho}^{-}-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{-\Delta t/2}^{\Delta t/2}dt\left[\hat{H}_{0}+I\delta(t)\bm{\epsilon}\cdot\hat{r},\hat{\rho}^{-}-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{-\Delta t/2}^{t}dt^{\prime}[\hat{H}_{0}+I\delta(t^{\prime})\bm{\epsilon}\cdot\hat{r},\hat{\rho}^{-}]\right]={}

(26)

\displaystyle{}=\hat{\rho}^{-}-\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat{H}_{0},\hat{\rho}^{-}]\Delta t+\left(\frac{-i}{\hbar}\right)^{2}[\hat{H}_{0},[\hat{H}_{0},\hat{\rho}^{-}]]\underbrace{\int_{-\Delta t/2}^{\Delta t/2}dt\int_{-\Delta t/2}^{t}dt^{\prime}}_{(\Delta t)^{2}/2}+

(27)

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{-i}{\hbar}\right)^{2}[\hat{H}_{0},[I\bm{\epsilon}\cdot\hat{r},\hat{\rho}^{-}]]\Delta t-\frac{i}{\hbar}[I\bm{\epsilon}\cdot\hat{r},\hat{\rho}^{-}]+

(28)

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{-i}{\hbar}\right)^{2}[I\bm{\epsilon}\cdot\hat{r},[\hat{H}_{0},\hat{\rho}^{-}]]\underbrace{\int_{-\Delta t/2}^{\Delta t/2}dt\delta(t)\int_{-\Delta t/2}^{t}dt^{\prime}1}_{\Delta t/2}

(29)

Now, we can notice that only the terms
\rho^{-}
and
(-i)/\hbar[I\bm{\epsilon}\cdot\hat{r},\hat{\rho}^{-}]

are independent of
\Delta t
and will therefore be finite even for
\Delta t\to 0
.

Similarly, we derive that only term independent of
\Delta t
to the second order in
I
will be

\displaystyle\left(\frac{-i}{\hbar}\right)^{2}[I\bm{\epsilon}\cdot\hat{r},[I\bm{\epsilon}\cdot\hat{r},\hat{\rho}^{-}]]\int_{-\Delta t/2}^{\Delta t/2}dt\delta(t)\int_{-\Delta t/2}^{t}dt^{\prime}\delta(t^{\prime})={}

(30)

\displaystyle{}=\left(\frac{-i}{\hbar}\right)^{2}[I\bm{\epsilon}\cdot\hat{r},[I\bm{\epsilon}\cdot\hat{r},\hat{\rho}^{-}]]\int_{-\Delta t/2}^{\Delta t/2}dt\delta(t)\theta(t)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{-i}{\hbar}\right)^{2}[I\bm{\epsilon}\cdot\hat{r},[I\bm{\epsilon}\cdot\hat{r},\hat{\rho}^{-}]]

(31)

and equivalently for higher orders, leading to expression replicating the
BCH theorem
Castro et al. (2004)

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}^{+}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\left[\left(\frac{-i}{\hbar}\right)I\bm{\epsilon}\cdot\hat{r},\hat{\rho}^{-}\right]_{n}=e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}I\bm{\epsilon}\cdot\hat{r}}\hat{\rho}^{-}e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}I\bm{\epsilon}\cdot\hat{r}}

(32)

The expression in atomic basis follows exactly the BCH/exact diagonalisation scheme discussed later,
with substitution of
\bm{H}(t)

3
(Dipole) Polarizability from Dipole Moment Time Series

The dipole polarizability
\alpha_{mn}(\omega)
(
m,n\in\{x,y,z\}
are Cartesian directions)
is recovered from the time series of the dipole operator
\hat{\mu}_{m}
expectation value
and the time series of the applied field
E_{n}(t)
as

\displaystyle\alpha_{mn}(\omega)=\frac{\mu_{m}(\omega)}{E_{n}(\omega)}=\frac{\int_{t_{0}}^{\infty}dt\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega(t-t_{0})}\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma(t-t_{0})}\left\langle\hat{\mu}_{m}(t)-\hat{\mu}_{m}(t_{0})\right\rangle}{\int_{t_{0}}^{\infty}dt\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega(t-t_{0})}E_{n}(t)}

(33)

where
t_{0}
is a reference time before which the density matrix is stationary (before application of
the excitation),
\hat{\mu}_{m}(t)
is the dipole moment operator for Cartesian direction

m\in\{x,y,z\}
,
\gamma
is an artificial damping which
stabilizes the numerical Fourier transform
Müller et al. (2020)

and
E_{n}(t)
is the external field along direction
n
.

3.1
Optical Spectra from Real Time Moment Traces

The absorption is given (quasi-classically) by the acceleration of the dipole moment, which introduces an extra
factor of
\omega
. The absorption of the field is associated with decreasing amplitude of the
resulting field, i.e. with the imaginary value of
\alpha
. Specifically,
Müller et al. (2020)
;
Mattiat and Luber (2022)
;
Cistaro et al. (2022)
;
Wu et al. (2016)
;
Yabana et al. (2006)
;
Martin (2020)

\displaystyle S(\omega)\propto\frac{1}{3}\omega\text{Tr}\left(\text{Im}\left(\bm{\alpha}(\omega)\right)\right)\>.

(34)

3.2
Spectrum Post-Processing

The discrete Fourier transform is used to get the transform of the dipole moment oscillations. In
order to reduce numerical noise, several post-processing steps are applied.

Firstly, a damping envelope is applied to the real time signal.
For majority of the results, the
chosen envelope is the exponential damping, as shown in (
33
),
which leads to effective broadening of the peaks of the oscillation, as can be seen from FT of

\displaystyle\int dte^{i\omega t}\theta(t)\sin(\omega_{0}t)e^{-\gamma t}=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{i(\omega+i\gamma)t}\sin(\omega_{0}t)=

(35)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2i}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\left[e^{i(\omega+\omega_{0}+i\gamma)t}-e^{i(\omega-\omega_{0}+i\gamma)t}\right]=\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{1}{\omega+\omega_{0}+i\gamma}-\frac{1}{\omega-\omega_{0}+i\gamma}\right]

(36)

The sine was chosen as an oscillatory function that has 0 value at
t=0
. Besides numerical stabilization of the
peak positions by broadening, the exponential damping also leads to coinciding initial and final value of the moments, which removes some abnormal frequencies otherwise present in the FT.

The imaginary part of the FT is than the imaginary part of the expression in the brackets above,
which results in a Lorentzian peak

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{-\gamma}{(\omega+\omega_{0})^{2}+\gamma^{2}}+\frac{\gamma}{(\omega-\omega_{0})^{2}+\gamma^{2}}\right]

(37)

The polarizability depends also on the transform of the applied field. Therefore,
we set
t=0
at the symmetry center of non-oscillatory envelope of the field - this leads to a purely
real Fourier transform. Numerically, this is achieved by wrapping the sample around certain
index of the signal data array.

3.3
Other Possible Window Functions

Note that one can also choose symmetric window functions such as Gaussian and symmetric exponential (Poisson) window function. For a general signal
f(t)
with Fourier transform

\displaystyle f(\omega)=\sum_{i}c_{i}\delta(\omega-\omega_{i})\>,

(38)

the effect of the window function
h(t)
is understood through the convolution in Fourier space.
Specifically,

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(t)h(t)e^{i\omega t}dt

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega^{\prime}e^{-i\omega^{\prime}t}e^{-i\omega^{\prime\prime}t}e^{i\omega t}f(\omega^{\prime})h(\omega^{\prime\prime})=

(39)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega^{\prime\prime}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega^{\prime}f(\omega^{\prime})h(\omega^{\prime\prime})\frac{1}{2\pi}\delta(\omega^{\prime}+\omega^{\prime\prime}-\omega)=

(40)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int d\omega^{\prime}f(\omega^{\prime})h(\omega-\omega^{\prime})=

(41)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{i}c_{i}h(\omega-\omega_{i})\>,

(42)

i.e. the Fourier transform of the window function replaces the delta peaks in the
spectrum
f(\omega)
of original signal
f(t)
. For even more specific case of sine signal

\displaystyle f(t)=\sum_{i}a_{i}\sin(\omega_{i}t)

(43)

we have

\displaystyle f(\omega)=i\pi\sum_{i}a_{i}(\delta(\omega-\omega_{i})-\delta(\omega+\omega_{i}))

(44)

For exponential window
h(t)=e^{-\gamma|t|}
, so

\displaystyle h(\omega)=\frac{2\gamma}{\omega^{2}+\gamma^{2}}

(45)

and hence

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(t)h(t)e^{i\omega t}dt=i\gamma\sum_{i}\frac{a_{i}}{(\omega-\omega_{i})^{2}+\gamma^{2}}-\frac{a_{i}}{(\omega+\omega_{i})^{2}+\gamma^{2}}

(46)

For Gaussian window function
h(t)=e^{-t^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})}
,
h(\omega)=\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}e^{-\sigma^{2}\omega^{2}/2}
, so the sine signal produces

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(t)h(t)e^{i\omega t}dt=i\sigma\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\sum_{i}a_{i}e^{-\sigma^{2}(\omega-\omega_{i})^{2}/2}-a_{i}e^{-\sigma^{2}(\omega+\omega_{i})^{2}/2}

(47)

In summary, both the exponential and the Gaussian window function produce a positive semi-definite imaginary spectrum in the positive frequency range for sine signal.

4
Working in Overlapping Basis - Covariant and Contravariant Operator Representations

In an overlapping basis of state vectors, we have a set of vectors
\left|\phi_{\mu}\right\rangle

(we shall assume finite) which satisfy

\displaystyle\left\langle\phi_{\mu}\right|\left.\phi_{\nu}\right\rangle=S_{\mu\nu}

(48)

where
S_{\mu\nu}
is the so-called overlap matrix. Assume now that a new set of vectors

\left|\psi_{m}\right\rangle
is constructed as linear combinations of
\left|\phi_{\mu}\right\rangle
,

\displaystyle\left|\psi_{m}\right\rangle=\sum_{\mu}C_{\mu m}\left|\phi_{\mu}\right\rangle,\left\langle\psi_{m}\right|\left.\psi_{n}\right\rangle=\delta_{mn}\>,

(49)

where
\delta_{mn}
is the Kronecker delta. The condition on coefficients
C_{\mu m}
can be then formulated
as matrix equation

\displaystyle\bm{C}^{\dagger}\bm{S}\bm{C}=\bm{I}\>,

(50)

where
\bm{I}
is the identity matrix, or alternatively

\displaystyle\bm{S}^{-1}=\bm{C}\bm{C}^{\dagger}\>.

(51)

For representation of quantum mechanical operators as matrices, one usually employs the completeness relation
to resolve the matrix elements. In orthogonal basis, this takes form
\sum_{m}\left|\psi_{m}\right\rangle\left\langle\psi_{m}\right|=\hat{I}
,
where
\hat{I}
is the identity operator. In the overlapping basis, this can be expressed as

\displaystyle\sum_{m}\left(\sum_{\mu}C_{\mu m}\left|\phi_{\mu}\right\rangle\right)\left(\sum_{\nu}C_{\nu m}^{*}\left\langle\phi_{\nu}\right|\right)=\sum_{\mu,\nu}\left|\phi_{\mu}\right\rangle\left(\sum_{m}C_{\mu m}C_{\nu m}^{*}\right)\left\langle\phi_{\nu}\right|=\sum_{\mu,\nu}\left|\phi_{\mu}\right\rangle S^{-1}_{\mu\nu}\left\langle\phi_{\nu}\right|

(52)

We crucially note that the last expression is independent of the choice of the orthonormal basis
and therefore represents the identity operator and associated completeness relation in the overlapping basis.

The presence of the overlap matrix in the completeness relation leads to difference in the representation of
operators, which we call covariant and contravariant representation. In the covariant representation, we represent
the operators by matrices

\displaystyle A_{\mu\nu}=\left\langle\phi_{\mu}\right|\hat{A}\left|\phi_{\nu}\right\rangle\>,

(53)

while in the contravariant representation, we use

\displaystyle\hat{A}=\sum_{\mu,\nu}\left|\phi_{\mu}\right\rangle A_{\mu\nu}\left\langle\phi_{\nu}\right|\>.

(54)

The two representations are related by transformation

\displaystyle\bm{A}_{\text{cov}}=\bm{S}\bm{A}_{\text{contra}}\bm{S}\>.

(55)

4.1
Reasoning Behind Representation Naming

When the basis is transformed by a unitary transformation
\hat{U}
, such that

\displaystyle\left|\phi^{\prime}_{\mu}\right\rangle=\hat{U}\left|\phi_{\mu}\right\rangle

(56)

the covariant representation of the operator transforms as

\displaystyle(\bm{A}^{\prime})_{\mu\nu}=\left\langle\phi^{\prime}_{\mu}\right|\hat{A}\left|\phi^{\prime}_{\nu}\right\rangle=\left\langle\phi_{\mu}\right|\hat{U}^{\dagger}\hat{A}\hat{U}\left|\phi_{\nu}\right\rangle=\bm{U}^{\dagger}\bm{S}^{-1}\bm{A}\bm{S}^{-1}\bm{U}

(57)

while the contravariant representation transforms as

\displaystyle\hat{A}=\sum_{\mu,\nu}\left|\phi^{\prime}_{\mu}\right\rangle(\bm{A}^{\prime})_{\mu\nu}\left\langle\phi^{\prime}_{\nu}\right|=\sum_{\mu,\nu}\hat{U}\left|\phi_{\mu}\right\rangle(\bm{A}^{\prime})_{\mu\nu}\left\langle\phi_{\nu}\right|\hat{U}^{\dagger}=\sum_{\mu,\nu}(\bm{S}^{-1}\bm{U}\bm{A}^{\prime}\bm{U}^{\dagger}\bm{S}^{-1})_{\mu\nu}\left|\phi_{\mu}\right\rangle\left\langle\phi_{\nu}\right|

(58)

which can be rewritten as

\displaystyle(\bm{A}^{\prime})_{\mu\nu}=(\bm{S}^{-1}\bm{U}^{\dagger}\bm{A}\bm{U}\bm{S}^{-1})_{\mu\nu}\>.

(59)

Here, we used
\left\langle\phi_{\mu}\right|\hat{U}^{(\dagger)}\left|\phi_{\nu}\right\rangle=(\bm{U}^{(\dagger)})_{\mu\nu}
and unitarity condition
\bm{U}\bm{S}^{-1}\bm{U}^{\dagger}=\bm{S}
.

The covariant representation transforms with
\bm{S}^{-1}
directly next to operator components, which is the same as in
the completeness relation, while the contravariant representation transforms with
\bm{U}/\bm{U}^{\dagger}
directly next to components. We note that in an orthonormal basis, overlap matrices become identity matrices, and hence the
representations become identical.

4.2
Mixed Operator Representation in CP2K

In CP2K, most operators are represented covariantly, while the density matrix is represented contravariantly.
This has an important effect on the numerical calculation of the trace of operators - in the orthonormal
basis, expectation values of operators are often evaluated as

\displaystyle\left\langle\hat{A}\right\rangle=\text{Tr}\left(\hat{\rho}\hat{A}\right)=\sum_{m}\left\langle\psi_{m}\right|\hat{\rho}\hat{A}\left|\psi_{m}\right\rangle

(60)

Expressing the orthonormal vectors in terms of the non-orthogonal basis leads to

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\left(\hat{\rho}\hat{A}\right)=\sum_{\mu,\nu,m}\left\langle\phi_{\mu}\right|C^{*}_{\mu m}\hat{\rho}\hat{A}C_{\nu m}\left|\phi_{\nu}\right\rangle=\sum_{\mu,\nu,\mu^{\prime},\nu^{\prime},m}C_{\nu m}C^{*}_{\mu m}S_{\mu\mu^{\prime}}\rho_{\mu^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}}\left\langle\phi_{\nu^{\prime}}\right|\hat{A}\left|\phi_{\nu}\right\rangle\>,

(61)

where we substituted for the contravariant representation of
\hat{\rho}
. We now substitute from (
51
),
which leads to

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\left(\hat{\rho}\hat{A}\right)=\sum_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}\rho_{\nu\nu^{\prime}}\left\langle\phi_{\nu^{\prime}}\right|\hat{A}\left|\phi_{\nu}\right\rangle\>,

(62)

meaning that using the covariant representation for
\hat{A}
will lead to operator trace being calculated
as numerical trace of the matrix representation.

4.3
Propagation of Matrices in Overlapping Basis

The equation of motion ((1) in the main text) can be
represented in the overlapping Gaussian basis using the contravariant representation
for the density operator and the covariant representation for the
Hamiltonian operator as

\displaystyle\bm{S}\frac{\partial\bm{\rho}}{\partial t}\bm{S}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\left(\bm{H}(t)\bm{\rho}(t)\bm{S}-\bm{S}\bm{\rho}(t)\bm{H}(t)\right)

(63)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{\rho}}{\partial t}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\left(\bm{S}^{-1}\bm{H}(t)\bm{\rho}(t)-\bm{\rho}(t)\bm{H}(t)\bm{S}^{-1}\right)

(64)

An approximate solution to this differential equation would predict a density matrix at time
t+\Delta t
using density matrix
at time
t
as

\displaystyle\bm{\rho}(t+\Delta t)=e^{-(i/\hbar)\bm{S}^{-1}\bm{H}(t)\Delta t}\bm{\rho}(t)e^{(i/\hbar)\bm{H}(t)\bm{S}^{-1}\Delta t}

(65)

i.e. by neglecting the variation of
\bm{H}
via approximation
\forall t^{\prime}\in[t,t+\Delta t]:\bm{H}(t^{\prime})=\bm{H}(t)
.

The variation of
\bm{H}
with time can be included by self-consistent schemes such as the
enforced time reversal symmetry scheme (ETRS), as described in the main text.

5
Exponentiation Methods

5.1
Exact Diagonalisation

In the exact diagonalisation scheme, Hamiltonian matrix
\bm{H}(t)
is diagonalized at each time
t
, which
results in eigenvectors
\bm{C}(t)

\displaystyle\bm{H}(t)\bm{C}(t)=\bm{S}\bm{C}(t)\bm{\Lambda}(t)

(66)

where
\bm{\Lambda}(t)
is the diagonal matrix of eigenvalues. Using (
51
)

\displaystyle\bm{H}(t)=\bm{S}\bm{C}(t)\bm{\Lambda}(t)\bm{C}^{\dagger}(t)\bm{S}

(67)

and therefore

\displaystyle e^{-(i/\hbar)\Delta t\bm{S}^{-1}\bm{H}(t)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{-i\Delta t}{\hbar}\right)^{n}\frac{1}{n!}(\bm{C}(t)\bm{\Lambda}(t)\bm{C}^{\dagger}(t)\bm{S})^{n}=

(68)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\bm{C}(t)\left(\frac{-i\Delta t}{\hbar}\bm{\Lambda}(t)\right)^{n}\bm{C}^{\dagger}(t)\bm{S}=\bm{C}(t)e^{-(i/\hbar)\Delta t\bm{\Lambda}(t)}\bm{C}^{\dagger}(t)\bm{S}

(69)

where the exponential of diagonal matrix
-(i/\hbar)\Delta t\bm{\Lambda}(t)
is trivial to evaluate.

5.2
BCH Scheme

Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula
Castro et al. (2004)
;
O’Rourke and Bowler (2015)
establishes that for two operators
\hat{A}
and
\hat{B}
, the
product

\displaystyle e^{\hat{A}}\hat{B}e^{-\hat{A}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}[\hat{A},\hat{B}]_{n}

(70)

where

\displaystyle[\hat{A},\hat{B}]_{n}=[\hat{A},[\hat{A},\hat{B}]_{n-1}],\>[\hat{A},\hat{B}]_{0}=\hat{B}

(71)

In non-orthogonal basis, the commutators are resolved as

\displaystyle([\hat{A},\hat{B}]_{n})_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=(\left\langle\phi_{\mu}\right|\hat{A}[\hat{A},\hat{B}]_{n-1}\left|\phi_{\nu}\right\rangle-\left\langle\phi_{\mu}\right|[\hat{A},\hat{B}]_{n-1}\hat{A}\left|\phi_{\nu}\right\rangle)=

(72)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu^{\prime},\nu^{\prime}}\left(A_{\mu\mu^{\prime}}S^{-1}_{\mu^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}}([\hat{A},\hat{B}]_{n-1})_{\nu^{\prime}\nu}-([\hat{A},\hat{B}]_{n-1})_{\mu\mu^{\prime}}S^{-1}_{\mu^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}}A_{\nu^{\prime}\nu}\right)

(73)

where

\displaystyle([\hat{A},\hat{B}]_{n})_{\mu\nu}=\left\langle\phi_{\mu}\right|[\hat{A},\hat{B}]_{n}\left|\phi_{\mu}\right\rangle

(74)

The mathematics is again simplified if we choose a mixed representation - we shall represent
\hat{A}
as covariant
and the commutators as contravariant, i.e. we define
C_{n,\mu\nu}

\displaystyle[\hat{A},\hat{B}]_{n}=\sum_{\mu,\nu}C_{n,\mu\nu}\left|\phi_{\mu}\right\rangle\left\langle\phi_{\nu}\right|

(75)

\displaystyle([\hat{A},\hat{B}]_{n})_{\mu\nu}=(\bm{S}\bm{C}_{n}\bm{S})_{\mu\nu}

(76)

For the simple propagation scheme, we can identify
\hat{A}=-(i/\hbar)\Delta t\hat{H}(t)
and in the atomic
orbital basis

\displaystyle\bm{S}\bm{\rho}(t+\Delta t)\bm{S}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\bm{S}\bm{C}_{n}\bm{S}

(77)

\displaystyle\bm{\rho}(t+\Delta t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\bm{C}_{n}

(78)

with

\displaystyle\bm{S}\bm{C}_{n}\bm{S}=\frac{-i}{\hbar}\Delta t\left(\bm{H}(t)\bm{C}_{n-1}\bm{S}-\bm{S}\bm{C}_{n-1}\bm{H}(t)\right)

(79)

\displaystyle\bm{C}_{n}=\frac{-i}{\hbar}\Delta t\left(\bm{S}^{-1}\bm{H}(t)\bm{C}_{n-1}-\bm{C}_{n-1}\bm{H}(t)\bm{S}^{-1}\right)

(80)

and with

\displaystyle\bm{C}_{0}=\bm{\rho}(t)

(81)

More specifically, one would typically define
\bm{A}=\frac{-i}{\hbar}\Delta t\bm{S}^{-1}\bm{H}(t)
and then write

\displaystyle\bm{C}_{n}=\bm{A}\bm{C}_{n-1}+\bm{C}_{n-1}\bm{A}^{\dagger}

(82)

since both
\bm{H}(t)
and
\bm{S}^{-1}
are Hermitian.

In practice, we need to introduce a convergence parameter
\epsilon
which truncates the series at smallest
n
for which
||\bm{C}_{n}||<\epsilon
,
assuming uniform convergence.

6
Conservation of Idempotency

As long as the effective Hamiltonian is hermitian, the density operator is idempotent at all times
if it is idempotent initially. For generally non-hermitian Hamiltonian, the equation of motion for the density operator would read

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\hat{\rho}}{\partial t}=\frac{-i}{\hbar}\left(\hat{H}(t)\hat{\rho}(t)-\hat{\rho}(t)\hat{H}^{\dagger}(t)\right)

(83)

From this, we can construct equation of motion for
\hat{\rho}^{2}(t)

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(t)\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}\hat{\rho}(t)=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\left(\hat{\rho}(t)\hat{H}(t)\hat{\rho}(t)-\hat{\rho}^{2}(t)\hat{H}^{\dagger}(t)+\hat{H}(t)\hat{\rho}^{2}(t)-\hat{\rho}(t)\hat{H}^{\dagger}(t)\hat{\rho}(t)\right)

(84)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial(\hat{\rho}^{2})}{\partial t}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\left(\hat{H}(t)\hat{\rho}^{2}(t)-\hat{\rho}^{2}(t)\hat{H}^{\dagger}(t)\right)-\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat{\rho}(t)\left(\hat{H}(t)-\hat{H}^{\dagger}(t)\right)\hat{\rho}(t)

(85)

i.e. if at time
t
,
\hat{\rho}^{2}(t)=\hat{\rho}(t)
, then such condition is true at all times,
as the equation of motion is the same for
\hat{\rho}(t)
and
\hat{\rho}^{2}(t)
at every instant,
as long as the Hamiltonian is hermitian.

In the overlapping basis, the form of idempotency condition is given by overlapping resolution of identity (
52
)

\displaystyle\left\langle\phi_{\mu}\right|\hat{\rho}\left|\phi_{\nu}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=(\bm{S}\bm{\rho}\bm{S})_{\mu\nu}=\left\langle\phi_{\mu}\right|\hat{\rho}^{2}\left|\phi_{\nu}\right\rangle=

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu^{\prime},\nu^{\prime}}\left\langle\phi_{\mu}\right|\hat{\rho}\left|\phi_{\mu^{\prime}}\right\rangle S_{\mu^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}}\left\langle\phi_{\nu^{\prime}}\right|\left|\phi_{\nu}\right\rangle=(\bm{S}\bm{\rho}\bm{S}\bm{S}^{-1}\bm{S}\bm{\rho}\bm{S})_{\mu\nu}

(86)

\displaystyle\bm{\rho}=\bm{\rho}\bm{S}\bm{\rho}

(87)

In the code, we track the idempotency deviation by outputting

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\left(\hat{\rho}^{2}\right)-\text{Tr}\left(\hat{\rho}\right)=\sum_{\mu}(\bm{\rho}\bm{S}\bm{\rho}\bm{S})_{\mu\mu}-(\bm{\rho}\bm{S})_{\mu\mu}=\sum_{\mu}(\bm{\rho}\bm{S}(\bm{\rho}\bm{S}-\bm{I}))_{\mu\mu}

(88)

7
Hartree and COHSEX Terms in the Gaussian overlapping basis

7.1
Hartree Term

In the position basis, Hartree term is given as

\displaystyle\left\langle\bm{r}\right|\hat{V}^{\text{Hartree}}(t)\left|\bm{r}^{\prime}\right\rangle=V^{\text{Hartree}}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},t)=\delta(\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime})\int d^{3}r^{\prime\prime}\frac{e^{2}\rho(\bm{r}^{\prime\prime},t)}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}|\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime\prime}|}

(89)

where
\rho(\bm{r}^{\prime},t)=\left\langle\bm{r}^{\prime}\right|\hat{\rho}(t)\left|\bm{r}^{\prime}\right\rangle
. Substituting for covariant
representation of the Hartree term in the atomic orbital basis and contravariant representation
of the density matrix in the atomic orbital basis, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu,\nu,\mu^{\prime},\nu^{\prime}}\phi_{\mu}(\bm{r})S^{-1}_{\mu\nu}V^{\text{Hartree}}_{\nu\mu^{\prime}}(t)S^{-1}_{\mu^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}}\phi^{*}_{\nu^{\prime}}(\bm{r}^{\prime})=\left\langle\bm{r}\right|\left.\bm{r}^{\prime}\right\rangle\int d^{3}r^{\prime\prime}\frac{e^{2}}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}|\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime\prime}|}\sum_{\mu,\nu}\phi_{\mu}(\bm{r}^{\prime\prime})\phi_{\nu}^{*}(\bm{r}^{\prime\prime})\rho_{\mu\nu}(t)

(90)

Multiplying by
\left\langle\phi_{\lambda}\right|\left.\bm{r}\right\rangle\left\langle\bm{r}^{\prime}\right|\left.\phi_{\sigma}\right\rangle
and integrating in
\bm{r}
and
\bm{r}^{\prime}
,
we obtain

\displaystyle V^{\text{Hartree}}_{\lambda\sigma}(t)=\int d^{3}rd^{3}r^{\prime}d^{3}r^{\prime\prime}\left\langle\phi_{\lambda}\right|\left.\bm{r}\right\rangle\left\langle\bm{r}\right|\left.\bm{r}^{\prime}\right\rangle\left\langle\bm{r}^{\prime}\right|\left.\phi_{\sigma}\right\rangle\frac{e^{2}}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}|\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime\prime}|}\sum_{\mu\nu}\phi_{\mu}(\bm{r}^{\prime\prime})\phi_{\nu}^{*}(\bm{r}^{\prime\prime})

(91)

Resolving the completeness relation in
\bm{r}^{\prime}
leads to
Attaccalite et al. (2011)

\displaystyle V^{\text{Hartree}}_{\lambda\sigma}(t)=\sum_{\mu,\nu}\int d^{3}rd^{3}r^{\prime\prime}\phi^{*}_{\lambda}(\bm{r})\phi_{\sigma}(\bm{r})\frac{e^{2}}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}|\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime\prime}|}\phi^{*}_{\nu}(\bm{r}^{\prime\prime})\phi_{\mu}(\bm{r}^{\prime\prime})\rho_{\mu\nu}(t)=\sum_{\mu,\nu}(\lambda\sigma|\nu\mu)\rho_{\mu\nu}(t)

(92)

For real orbitals then also
V^{\text{Hartree}}_{\lambda\sigma}=\sum_{\mu\nu}(\lambda\sigma|\mu\nu)\rho_{\mu\nu}
.

7.2
COHSEX Term

The Coulomb hole term has the following position base representation
Attaccalite et al. (2011)

\displaystyle\left\langle\bm{r}\right|\hat{\Sigma}^{\text{COH}}(t)\left|\bm{r}^{\prime}\right\rangle=-\frac{1}{2}W(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega=0)\delta(\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime})

(93)

The screened exchange is represented as
Attaccalite et al. (2011)

\displaystyle\left\langle\bm{r}\right|\hat{\Sigma}^{\text{SEX}}(t)\left|\bm{r}^{\prime}\right\rangle=i\hbar W(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega=0)G^{<}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},t)

(94)

with

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}^{\text{COHSEX}}(t)=\hat{\Sigma}^{\text{COH}}(t)+\hat{\Sigma}^{\text{SEX}}(t)

(95)

where
G^{<}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},t)
is the time-diagonal lesser Green’s function
Attaccalite et al. (2011)
.

Under the approximation that the screened exchange
W
does not change as time passes, the
Coulomb hole part of the self-energy is constant, and hence does not contribute to the dynamics
Attaccalite et al. (2011)
.

The screened exchange part can be expressed in terms of the density matrix, since

\displaystyle G^{<}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},t)=\frac{i}{\hbar}\rho(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},t)

(96)

leading to

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu,\nu,\mu^{\prime},\nu^{\prime}}\phi_{\mu}(\bm{r})S^{-1}_{\mu\nu}\Sigma^{\text{SEX}}_{\nu\mu^{\prime}}S^{-1}_{\mu^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}}\phi_{\nu^{\prime}}^{*}(\bm{r}^{\prime})=-W(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega=0)\left\langle\bm{r}\right|\hat{\rho}(t)\left|\bm{r}^{\prime}\right\rangle

(97)

Similarly as before, multiplying by
\left\langle\phi_{\lambda}\right|\left.\bm{r}\right\rangle\left\langle\bm{r}^{\prime}\right|\left.\phi_{\sigma}\right\rangle
and integrating in
\bm{r}
and
\bm{r}^{\prime}
,
Attaccalite et al. (2011)

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\text{SEX}}_{\lambda\sigma}(t)=-\int d^{3}r\int d^{3}r^{\prime}\sum_{\mu,\nu}\phi_{\lambda}^{*}(\bm{r})\phi_{\mu}(\bm{r})W(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega=0)\phi_{\sigma}(\bm{r}^{\prime})\phi_{\nu}^{*}(\bm{r}^{\prime})\rho_{\mu\nu}(t)

(98)

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\text{SEX}}_{\lambda\sigma}(t)=-\sum_{\mu,\nu}W_{\lambda\mu,\nu\sigma}\rho_{\mu\nu}(t)

(99)

where we used the
W_{\lambda\mu,\nu\sigma}
notation for the screened four-center integral.

RI Approximation

Resolution of identity approximation can be used to reduce the scaling of electron repulsion integrals
with number of basis functions. The basic idea is that the basis pair products are
substituted by summations over auxiliary basis functions which minimize difference from true ERIs, specifically
Graml et al. (2024)

\displaystyle\phi_{\mu}(\bm{r})\phi_{\nu}(\bm{r})=\sum_{P}B^{P}_{\mu\nu}\phi_{P}(\bm{r})

(100)

where
B^{P}_{\mu\nu}=\sum_{Q}m_{PQ}^{-1}(Q][\mu\nu)
, where
(Q][\mu\nu)
is
a 3-center integral with
m(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})
as the metric.

This simplifies the calculation of Hartree term to

\displaystyle V^{\text{Hartree}}_{\lambda\sigma}=\sum_{P,Q}(\lambda\sigma][P)(\bm{m}^{-1}\bm{V}^{\text{H,A}}\bm{m}^{-1})_{PQ}\sum_{\mu,\nu}(Q][\nu\mu)\rho_{\mu\nu}(t)

(101)

where

\displaystyle V^{\text{H,A}}_{PQ}=\int d^{3}rd^{3}r^{\prime}\phi^{*}_{P}(\bm{r})\frac{1}{|\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime}|}\phi_{Q}(\bm{r}^{\prime})

(102)

Similar approximation can be made for the screened exchange self-energy, with

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\text{SEX}}_{\lambda\sigma}(t)=-\frac{1}{\hbar}\sum_{P,Q,\mu,\nu}(\lambda\mu][P)(\bm{m}^{-1}\bm{W}^{\text{A}}\bm{m}^{-1})_{PQ}(Q][\nu\sigma)\rho_{\mu\nu}(t)

(103)
