Balloon Rupture After Subannular Slippage During Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement in Extremely Calcified Anatomy

Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has become the standard treatment for patients with severe aortic stenosis across all surgical risk categories. Direct valve implantation without balloon predilatation has gained popularity to simplify procedures and reduce embolic risk. Severe and asymmetric aortic valve calcification, particularly when extending into the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT), represents a major mechanical challenge and may predispose to rare but serious complications, including balloon slippage and rupture.

Case Presentation

Clinical history

A 77-year-old man was referred for TAVR because of symptomatic severe calcific aortic stenosis. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (68%), a mean transaortic gradient of 56 mm Hg, and an aortic valve area of 0.67 cm2. Coronary angiography showed nonobstructive coronary artery disease.

Preprocedural computed tomography

Multidetector computed tomography (CT) demonstrated a tricuspid aortic valve with extreme calcification (Agatston calcium score: 9,026) (Figures 1C and 1D). Calcification was markedly asymmetric, predominantly involving the antero-left and antero-right cusps, with bulky nodular calcifications extending below the annular plane into the left ventricular outflow tract, whereas the noncoronary cusp showed minimal subannular extension, resulting in a highly heterogeneous mechanical substrate (Figures 1A to 1D, Videos 1 and 2).

The aortic annulus measured 595 mm2 in area, with a perimeter of 87.1 mm and derived diameters of 27.5 mm (area) and 27.7 mm (perimeter). Two nodular calcifications were identified approximately 3 mm below the annular plane, opposite the antero-left and antero-right cusps. (Figure 1A, Videos 1 and 2).

The LVOT area measured 579.9 mm2, with an area-derived diameter of 27.2 mm, and exhibited similar nodular calcifications, confirming significant subannular extension (Figure 1B, Videos 1 and 2).

Coronary heights measured 15.5 mm (left) and 21.6 mm (right). The sinuses of Valsalva measured 25.3 mm in height, with sinotubular junction dimensions of approximately 33 mm (Videos 1 and 2).

Peripheral access assessment showed moderate bilateral iliac tortuosity without calcification, with minimal diameters of 8.6 mm (right) and 8.3 mm (left).

Procedural course

The procedure was performed via a right transfemoral approach under conscious sedation. A 29-mm Edwards Sapien 3 Ultra balloon-expandable valve was selected. Direct implantation without predilatation was chosen.

Systemic anticoagulation was initiated at femoral access and was maintained throughout the procedure with continuous activated clotting time monitoring, targeting values >250 seconds; this was continued until surgical transfer.

Predeployment angiography confirmed correct valve positioning (Video 3). Progressive balloon inflation was performed after a control contrast injection. Early asymmetric balloon expansion was observed, with increased constraint at the valvular level and preferential inflation toward the LVOT (Figure 1E). During late inflation, the balloon slipped downward into the LVOT (Figure 1F) and ruptured during the final seconds of inflation (Figure 1G, Video 3).

Despite rupture, the valve was fully expanded and well seated. Final angiography showed no paravalvular regurgitation (Figure 1H, Video 4). A transient intraprosthetic regurgitation was attributed to the presence of the guidewire and ruptured balloon catheter.

Management and outcome

After rupture, the balloon catheter was withdrawn proximally and maintained within the ascending aorta, while the guidewire remained in place. Immediate transthoracic echocardiography showed no pericardial effusion, a mean transprosthetic gradient of 5 mm Hg, and no paravalvular regurgitation.

Because balloon deflation was impossible, the patient was transferred immediately to the operating room. Surgical extraction was performed via median sternotomy under cardiopulmonary bypass, allowing safe removal of the ruptured balloon catheter while preserving the transcatheter valve. The femoral access was closed intraoperatively. The patient was extubated on the same day.

Postoperatively, the course was marked by new-onset left bundle branch block and first-degree atrioventricular block. On postoperative day 2, atrial fibrillation occurred, prompting initiation of oral anticoagulation with warfarin. No high-grade atrioventricular block occurred, and permanent pacing was not required.

The remainder of the hospital stay was uneventful, and the patient was discharged to hospital-at-home care on postoperative day 8.

Five-month follow-up

At 5 months postprocedure, the patient reported marked clinical improvement. The sternotomy scar was well healed, and the sternum was stable. Echocardiography showed preserved left ventricular function (ejection fraction: 60%), a mean transprosthetic gradient of 9 mm Hg, an effective orifice area of 1.76 cm2, and mild paravalvular regurgitation (grade 1) localized at the 3 o'clock position on parasternal short-axis view (Figure 2, Video 5).

Discussion

Balloon rupture during TAVR is an exceptionally rare complication, reported only in isolated case reports and small series. Contemporary registries of balloon-expandable TAVR report device success rates exceeding 95%, with major complications primarily related to vascular injury, conduction disturbances, and paravalvular regurgitation rather than balloon failure.1

In the present case, preprocedural CT provided critical mechanistic insight. Beyond the extreme overall calcium burden exceeding 9,000 Agatston units, CT analysis revealed a markedly asymmetric distribution of calcification, with bulky nodular deposits involving the antero-left and antero-right cusps and extending below the annular plane into the LVOT, approximately 3 mm beneath the annulus. In contrast, calcification of the noncoronary cusp was confined to the leaflet level without subannular extension. This highly heterogeneous calcific architecture created a nonuniform mechanical environment at both the annular and subannular levels by generating uneven resistance during balloon inflation.2,3

During valve deployment, this asymmetric resistance resulted in early asymmetric balloon expansion, with increased constraint at the valvular level and preferential inflation toward the ventricular side. As inflation progressed, the valve opened in a relatively harmonious fashion. During the final phase of inflation, axial forces likely exceeded anchoring resistance, leading to downward balloon slippage into the LVOT. Contact between the balloon and sharp, protruding subannular calcific nodules at peak inflation pressure most plausibly explains the acute balloon rupture. Importantly, at the time of rupture, valve expansion was already complete, accounting for the absence of immediate hemodynamic compromise or significant paravalvular regurgitation on final angiography.

Management of ruptured transcatheter valve delivery balloons remains poorly standardized because this complication is extremely rare and is reported mainly in isolated case reports and small series.4, 5, 6 In balloon-expandable systems, rupture usually prevents adequate balloon re-collapse, as torn balloon segments may behave like opposing parachutes, preventing safe withdrawal into the introducer sheath and resulting in a bulky and irregular structure.

In selected stable situations, a percutaneous retrieval strategy may be considered. Reported techniques include contralateral femoral arterial access with advancement of a large-bore sheath into the aorta and snare capture of the distal balloon component or guidewire.6 Once secured, femorofemoral externalization improves device control, after which the proximal shaft of the delivery catheter is typically transected at the primary access site, allowing controlled sequential retrieval of the ruptured balloon components under sheath protection. Successful endovascular retrieval requires favorable iliofemoral anatomy, the ability to mobilize the ruptured balloon into a safe aortic segment, and availability of appropriate retrieval equipment.

Balloon size also plays an important role. Large delivery balloons used for 29-mm transcatheter valves may create bulky ruptured structures that are difficult to collapse or sheath, increasing the risk of vascular trauma during traction across the iliofemoral axis. In such situations, surgical bailout may represent the safest strategy, allowing controlled extraction under direct visualization while minimizing vascular injury.

In the present case, the patient remained hemodynamically stable, and the transcatheter valve was well positioned and fully expanded. Although contralateral iliofemoral access was technically feasible, with minimal vessel diameters of approximately 8 mm bilaterally and no significant calcification or tortuosity, the ruptured balloon corresponded to a large delivery system for a 29-mm valve. Given the potential risk of vascular injury during endovascular traction, the heart team elected to perform immediate surgical extraction, which allowed controlled removal of the ruptured device and resulted in a favorable clinical outcome.

A simplified management algorithm for ruptured TAVR balloon retrieval is proposed (Figure 3). Initial assessment should confirm hemodynamic stability, preserved valve function, absence of tamponade, and feasibility of contralateral iliofemoral access. Percutaneous retrieval may be attempted if the balloon can be safely mobilized into the aorta, whereas large balloon profile or high vascular risk should favor early surgical bailout.

This case highlights the importance of meticulous preprocedural CT assessment beyond annular sizing, including calcium distribution, subannular extension, and LVOT involvement. In patients with extreme or asymmetric calcification, balloon predilatation may improve annular compliance and reduce axial forces during valve deployment. Finally, prompt recognition of balloon rupture is essential; while percutaneous retrieval may be attempted, large noncollapsible valve balloons may favor early surgical extraction, as summarized in the simplified management algorithm (Figure 3).

Conclusions

Balloon rupture during balloon-expandable TAVR is a rare but potentially catastrophic complication. Extreme and asymmetric valvular and subannular calcification plays a key mechanistic role by promoting balloon slippage and rupture during valve deployment. Meticulous preprocedural computed tomography analysis, judicious procedural strategy, and readiness for percutaneous retrieval or immediate surgical bailout are essential to ensure patient safety.
