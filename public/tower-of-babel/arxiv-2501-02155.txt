ItsDEAL: Inexact two-level smoothing descent algorithms for weakly convex optimization
Alireza Kabgani, Masoud Ahookhosh

Abstract
This paper addresses nonconvex optimization problems via a two-level smoothing framework, where the high-order Moreau envelope (HOME) provides a smooth approximation of weakly convex cost functions. We study the differentiability and weak smoothness of HOME. Building on the inexact two-level smoothing optimization framework (ItsOPT), we propose an inexact two-level smoothing descent algorithm (ItsDEAL) by: (i) approximately solving the proximal subproblem to obtain an inexact first-order oracle for HOME at the lower level; and (ii) applying an inexact first-order method at the upper level. We derive computable sufficient conditions for lower-level inexactness, enabling approximate high-order proximal-points to be certified without access to the exact proximal-point or HOME value. In particular, we study a parameter-free Hölderian inexact gradient method and an inexact descent method with Armijo line search, both exploiting the weak smoothness of HOME. We establish subsequential convergence under mild inexactness assumptions, and global and linear convergence under the Kurdyka–Łojasiewicz (KL) property. Preliminary experiments on ridge-regularized hinge-loss classification, a high-dimensional MCP-regularized classification model, and robust matrix completion illustrate the numerical behavior of the proposed methods and the role of the HOME order.

Introduction

Let us consider the optimization problem

\mathop{\boldsymbol{\min}}\limits_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\ \varphi(x),

(1.1)

where
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}:=\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is a proper, lower semicontinuous (lsc), and
weakly convex
function (Definition
2
) that is possibly nonsmooth and admits at least one minimizer
x^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the corresponding minimal value
\varphi^{*}
.
The class of
weakly convex functions
appears in a variety of problems in many application domains such as machine learning, signal and image processing, statistics, optimal control,
and beyond; see, e.g.,
Böhm and Wright (2021)
;
Goujon et al. (2024)
;
Kungurtsev and Rinaldi (2021)
;
Liao et al. (2024)
;
Montefusco et al. (2013)
;
Pougkakiotis and Kalogerias (2023)
;
Yang et al. (2019)
.
Compared with convexity, weak convexity allows a controlled amount of negative curvature,
accommodating a broader class of functions including many interesting nonconvex functions; see, e.g.,
Atenas et al. (2023)
;
Böhm and Wright (2021)
;
Davis et al. (2018)
;
Rahimi et al. (2026)
. As an interesting example, a composition of a convex Lipschitz continuous function with a smooth mapping whose Jacobian is Lipschitz is a weakly convex function
Davis et al. (2018)
;
Rahimi et al. (2026)
. Consequently, the development of theoretically strong and numerically efficient algorithms for this broad class of functions is of significant practical relevance.

A major challenge in addressing the weakly convex optimization problem of the form (
1.1
) lies in handling the nonconvexity and the nonsmoothness of the cost function. The classical methods for dealing with such functions are
subgradient methods
; see, e.g.,
Atenas et al. (2023)
;
Davis et al. (2018)
;
Davis and Drusvyatskiy (2019)
;
Li et al. (2020)
;
Rahimi et al. (2026)
;
Yang et al. (2019)
. An alternative approach
employs
smoothing techniques
, which approximate the original nonsmooth cost function by a smooth surrogate and thereby enable the use of
first-order methods
. Such techniques have received considerable attention over the last few decades; see, e.g.,
Ahookhosh et al. (2021)
;
Beck and Teboulle (2012)
;
Ben-Tal and Teboulle (1989)
;
Bertsekas (1975)
;
Boț and Hendrich (2015)
;
Boţ and Böhm (2020)
;
Moreau (1965)
;
Nesterov (2005)
;
Patrinos and Bemporad (2013)
;
Stella et al. (2017)
;
Themelis et al. (2018)
;
Themelis et al. (2019)
;
Themelis and Patrinos (2020)
.

Among all smoothing techniques, the Moreau envelope is particularly interesting due to its continuity and differentiability properties under suitable conditions; see, e.g.,
Moreau (1965)
.
Indeed, it can generate a smooth approximation of the original cost function by employing a proximal operator. Specifically, the
proximal-point operator
and the
Moreau envelope
with parameter
\gamma>0
are defined as

\boldsymbol{{\rm prox}}_{\gamma\varphi}(x):=\mathop{\boldsymbol{\arg\min}}\limits_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\varphi(y)+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|x-y\|^{2}\right),\quad\varphi_{\gamma}(x):=\mathop{\boldsymbol{\inf}}\limits_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\varphi(y)+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|x-y\|^{2}\right).

(1.2)

Evaluating the Moreau envelope requires computing the proximal operator. Although this operator is available in closed form for various convex (e.g.,
(
Beck, 2017
, Chapter 6)
) and weakly convex (e.g.,
Böhm and Wright (2021)
) functions, its exact evaluation can be challenging for more general models. In such cases, the proximal subproblem (
1.2
) is typically solved only approximately, and consequently the Moreau envelope and its gradient are also available only through
inexact evaluations. This naturally leads to inexact variants of proximal-point and proximal-gradient methods; see, e.g.,
Khanh et al. (2025)
;
Salzo and Villa (2012)
and the references therein.

Recently, an
inexact two-level smoothing optimization framework
(ItsOPT) was introduced in
Kabgani and Ahookhosh (2026)
, which leverages the
high-order proximal operator
(HOPE) and its corresponding
high-order Moreau envelope
(HOME);
see Definition
3
and Algorithm
1
.
ItsOPT consists of two levels:
(i) at the lower-level, the high-order proximal auxiliary problems are solved inexactly to produce an inexact oracle for HOME; (ii) at the upper-level, an inexact zero-, first-, or second-order method is developed to minimize HOME. This offers a flexible framework for developing inexact two-level smoothing methods, where the numerical experiments indicate that using regularization powers different from two can lead to more effective algorithms; cf.
Ahookhosh et al. (2026a)
;
Kabgani and Ahookhosh (2026)
;
Kabgani and Ahookhosh (2025)
.
While
Kabgani and Ahookhosh (2025)
studies fundamental properties of HOPE and HOME in a general nonconvex setting and
Kabgani and Ahookhosh (2026)
develops the general ItsOPT framework, the present paper focuses specifically on weakly convex optimization. In particular, we exploit weak convexity to study the properties of HOME needed for the analysis and to develop concrete inexact descent methods within the ItsOPT framework.

A practical issue in this two-level setting is that lower-level inexactness conditions are naturally stated in terms of the exact HOPE point and the exact HOME value, which are generally unavailable in an implementation. We show that weak convexity also provides computable sufficient conditions for these requirements. In particular, a stationarity residual, or alternatively a valid lower bound for the proximal model, can be converted into explicit distance and value-error bounds. These certificates allow the lower-level solver to be stopped using quantities that can be evaluated numerically and are used directly in the experiments of Section
5
.

Since this paper focuses on minimization problems, our numerical experiments are conducted on lower-bounded weakly convex functions. However, to study fundamental properties of HOME such as differentiability and weak smoothness and properties of HOPE, including boundedness, we work with the broader class of high-order prox-bounded weakly convex functions. This class includes, in particular, all convex functions and all lower-bounded weakly convex functions; see Definition
4
and Remark
2
.

1.1
Contribution

Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

(i)

(
Further properties of high-order Moreau envelope
) We further study the fundamental and differential properties of the high-order Moreau envelope (HOME) as a tool for constructing smooth approximations of nonsmooth weakly convex functions. Unlike the traditional Moreau envelope via regularization with power two (see (
1.2
)), HOME incorporates arbitrary-order regularization terms (see Definition
3
with
p>1
), leading to a much more flexible smoothing approach that can be adapted to the geometry of the original cost function. In particular, we establish the boundedness (see Proposition
1
and Corollary
1
), the differentiability, and the weak smoothness (see Theorems
3.1
and
3.2
, and Corollary
2
). As such, leveraging the weak convexity, this ensures a weakly smooth approximation of the cost on a prescribed ball, i.e., if
x^{0}
is located in this ball, one can develop first-order methods generating a sequence of iterations
that remain inside this ball.

(ii)

(Inexact two-level smoothing descent algorithm)

We introduce an Inexact two-level smoothing DEscent ALgorithm (ItsDEAL; see Algorithm
2
), which serves as a generic algorithmic strategy for developing first-order descent methods via HOME. At the lower-level, solving high-order proximity problems approximately, we provide an inexact oracle for HOME, and in the upper-level, we employ this inexact information to construct inexact descent methods.
We also derive computable sufficient conditions for the lower-level inexactness requirements using either a stationarity residual or a valid lower bound for the proximal model; see Proposition
2
and
Corollary
3
.
In particular, we generate descent directions for HOME in Lemma
3
via relationships between these directions and the inexact gradient. Building on the Hölderian smoothness of HOME, we propose a Hölderian inexact gradient descent algorithm (HiGDA) in Algorithm
3
and its parameter-free version in Algorithm
4
, which does not require knowledge of the Hölderian parameters, thereby enhancing its practicality for real-world scenarios. Additionally, we introduce an Inexact DEscent Armijo Line Search (IDEALS) in Algorithm
5
, a robust strategy that dynamically adapts the step-size to ensure convergence. Besides well-definedness, the subsequential convergence of the sequence generated by ItsDEAL is investigated under some reasonable assumptions on the inexactness of the proximity operator. Moreover, the global and linear convergence are verified under the Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz (KL) property.

(iii)

(Numerical behavior of the HOME order)

We investigate the role of the regularization order
p
in three numerical settings with certified lower-level solves. For ridge-regularized hinge-loss classification, the relative performance of the subquadratic and quadratic orders depends on the upper-level method and the requested accuracy. We also consider a high-dimensional MCP-regularized hinge-loss model, which is weakly convex and nonconvex. Under a short CPU budget, the subquadratic choices preserve held-out classification performance better than the quadratic order, while
p=2
decreases the training objective more aggressively. For nonsmooth robust matrix completion, IDEALS with
p=1.75
gives the best early-update objective and recovery values among the tested IDEALS realizations. These experiments show that the HOME order is an algorithmic parameter whose effect depends on the problem and computational regime.

Organization

Further properties of high–order Moreau envelope

This section recalls the high-order proximal operator (HOPE) and the high-order Moreau envelope (HOME), together with the properties needed in our analysis; see, e.g.,
Kabgani and Ahookhosh (2026)

Definition 3

(High-order proximal operator and Moreau envelope)
.

Let

,
\gamma>0

The

(

\boldsymbol{{\rm prox}}_{\gamma\varphi}^{p}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}

\boldsymbol{{\rm prox}}_{\gamma\varphi}^{p}(x):=\mathop{\boldsymbol{\arg\min}}\limits_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\varphi(y)+\frac{1}{p\gamma}\|x-y\|^{p}\right),

and the
high-order Moreau envelope
(

) of
\varphi
with parameter
\gamma

\varphi_{\gamma}^{p}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\pm\infty\}

is given by

\varphi_{\gamma}^{p}(x):=\mathop{\boldsymbol{\inf}}\limits_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\varphi(y)+\frac{1}{p\gamma}\|x-y\|^{p}\right).

and let
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be a proper lsc function that is high-order prox-bounded with a threshold
\gamma^{\varphi,p}>0
. Then

\boldsymbol{{\rm prox}}_{\gamma\varphi}^{p}(x)=\mathop{\boldsymbol{\arg\min}}\limits_{\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\|y\|\leq\tau\}}\left(\varphi(y)+\frac{1}{p\gamma}\|x-y\|^{p}\right),

for each
\gamma\in\left(0,4^{1-p}\widehat{\gamma}\right)
,

\widehat{\gamma}\in(0,\gamma^{\varphi,p})
,
and for any
r>0
,
x\in\mathbf{B}(0;r)
,
and

\tau:=\left(\frac{2r^{p}+p\gamma(\varphi(0)-\ell_{0})}{2^{1-p}-\ell p\gamma}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}
,
where

\ell=\frac{2^{p-1}}{p\widehat{\gamma}}
and
\ell_{0}
is a lower bound of
\varphi(\cdot)+\ell\|\cdot\|^{p}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proof.

By
(
Kabgani and Ahookhosh, 2025
, Proposition 9)
,
\varphi(y)+\ell\|y\|^{p}\geq\ell_{0}
for all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Let
x\in\mathbf{B}(0;r)
and
y\in\boldsymbol{{\rm prox}}_{\gamma\varphi}^{p}(x)
. Then,

-\ell\|y\|^{p}+\ell_{0}+\frac{1}{p\gamma}\|x-y\|^{p}\leq\varphi(y)+\frac{1}{p\gamma}\|x-y\|^{p}=\varphi_{\gamma}^{p}(x)\leq\varphi(0)+\frac{1}{p\gamma}\|x\|^{p}.

(3.1)

Since
\|y\|^{p}\leq 2^{p-1}\left(\|x\|^{p}+\|y-x\|^{p}\right)
, it is deduced from (
3.1
) that

-\ell\|y\|^{p}+\frac{1}{p\gamma}\left(2^{1-p}\|y\|^{p}-\|x\|^{p}\right)\leq\frac{1}{p\gamma}\|x\|^{p}+(\varphi(0)-\ell_{0}).

Thus,
(2^{1-p}-\ell p\gamma)\|y\|^{p}\leq 2\|x\|^{p}+p\gamma(\varphi(0)-\ell_{0})
.
Hence
\|y\|<\tau
, which proves the claim.
∎

Remark 3
.

A related localization result in Banach spaces is given in
(
Kecis and Thibault, 2015
, Theorem 3.1)
, under different assumptions. Proposition
1
provides an explicit radius
\tau
in the present setting. The auxiliary parameter
\widehat{\gamma}
is chosen strictly below
\gamma^{\varphi,p}
because the prox-boundedness threshold need not itself belong to the admissible set, whereas
(
Kabgani and Ahookhosh, 2025
, Proposition 9)
requires a value for which HOME is finite.

For lower-bounded functions, the localization bound simplifies and holds for every
\gamma>0
.

Corollary 1

(Boundedness of HOPE: lower boundedness)

Let
p>1
and let
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be a proper lsc function that is bounded from below by
\ell_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then

\boldsymbol{{\rm prox}}_{\gamma\varphi}^{p}(x)=\mathop{\boldsymbol{\arg\min}}\limits_{\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\|y\|\leq\tau\}}\left(\varphi(y)+\frac{1}{p\gamma}\|x-y\|^{p}\right),

for any
\gamma>0
,
r>0
,

x\in\mathbf{B}(0;r)
,
and

\tau:=2r+\left(2^{p-1}p\gamma(\varphi(0)-\ell_{0})\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}
.

Proof.

Let
y\in\boldsymbol{{\rm prox}}_{\gamma\varphi}^{p}(x)
. Similar to the proof of Proposition
1
, we obtain

\frac{1}{p\gamma}\left(2^{1-p}\|y\|^{p}-\|x\|^{p}\right)\leq\frac{1}{p\gamma}\|x\|^{p}+(\varphi(0)-\ell_{0}),

leading to
\|y\|^{p}\leq 2^{p-1}\left(2\|x\|^{p}+p\gamma(\varphi(0)-\ell_{0})\right)
.
From (
2.1
), we obtain

\displaystyle\|y\|\leq\left[2^{p-1}\left(2\|x\|^{p}+p\gamma(\varphi(0)-\ell_{0})\right)\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq 2\|x\|+\left(2^{p-1}p\gamma(\varphi(0)-\ell_{0})\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Since
\|x\|<r
, the result follows.
∎

3.1
Differentiability and weak smoothness of HOME

This subsection establishes differentiability and weak smoothness of HOME for weakly convex functions. We show that, for any prescribed radius
r>0
, HOME is continuously differentiable on
\mathbf{B}(0;r)
when

\gamma>0
is sufficiently small, and we subsequently derive a Hölder continuity estimate for its gradient. The analysis is restricted to
p\in(1,2]
; Example
1
indicates that differentiability may fail for
p>2
.

We first recall a characterization of differentiability of HOME.

{fact}
[Characterization of differentiability of HOME]
(
Kabgani and Ahookhosh, 2025
, Proposition 20)
(see also
(
Kecis and Thibault, 2015
, Proposition 3.1)
)
Let
p>1
, and let
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be proper lsc that is high-order prox-bounded with a threshold
\gamma^{\varphi,p}>0
. Then, for each
\gamma\in(0,\gamma^{\varphi,p})
and any open subset
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the following statements are equivalent:

(a)

\varphi_{\gamma}^{p}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(U)
;

(b)

\boldsymbol{{\rm prox}}_{\gamma\varphi}^{p}
is nonempty, single-valued, and continuous on
U
.

Under these conditions, for any
x\in U
and
y=\boldsymbol{{\rm prox}}_{\gamma\varphi}^{p}(x)
, we have
\nabla\varphi_{\gamma}^{p}(x)=\frac{1}{\gamma}\|x-y\|^{p-2}(x-y)
.

In general, when
\varphi
is a weakly convex function and
p>2
, there may not exist any
\gamma>0
such that
\varphi_{\gamma}^{p}

is differentiable. The following example, taken from
(
Kabgani and Ahookhosh, 2025
, Example 27)
, illustrates this possibility.

Example 1

(Nondifferentiability of HOME for
p>2
)

Consider
\varphi(x)=x^{4}-x^{2}
on
\mathbb{R}
, which is weakly convex. For every

and
\gamma>0
, the set

\boldsymbol{\arg\min}_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{\Phi(y):=y^{4}-y^{2}+\frac{1}{p\gamma}|y|^{p}\right\}

(
Kabgani and Ahookhosh, 2025
, Example 27)
.
Hence,
\boldsymbol{{\rm prox}}_{\gamma\varphi}^{p}(0)
is not single-valued.
By Fact
3.1
,
\varphi_{\gamma}^{p}
is not differentiable at
\overline{x}=0
.

In view of Example

, we restrict throughout to

.

Remark 4

4
ItsDEAL: Inexact two-level smoothing descent algorithms

We here introduce the Inexact two-level smoothing DEscent ALgorithm (ItsDEAL), a generic framework based on HOME for solving weakly convex optimization problems of the form (
1.1
). ItsDEAL follows the inexact two-level smoothing optimization framework
(ItsOPT) introduced in
Kabgani and Ahookhosh (2026)
. ItsOPT consists of two levels. At the upper level, a zero-, first-, or
second-order method is applied to a smooth approximation of the original cost function. At the lower level, the associated proximal problem is solved inexactly to generate an inexact oracle for the upper-level
method. The proximal subproblem can be solved approximately using, for example, subgradient methods
Davis et al. (2018)
;
Rahimi et al. (2026)
, Bregman gradient methods
Ahookhosh and Nesterov (2024)
, or BELLA
Ahookhosh et al. (2021)
. For completeness, we summarize the ItsOPT framework below.

Algorithm 1

ItsOPT (Inexact two-level smoothing OPTimization algorithm)

1:

Smoothing technique
Choose an envelope for generating a smooth variant of
\varphi
;

2:

Initialization
Start with
x^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\gamma>0
, and set
k=0
;

3:

while
the stopping criterion is not satisfied
do

4:

Find an approximate solution for the proximal auxiliary problem;
\triangleright
lower-level

5:

Generate the inexact zero-, first-, or second-order oracle
\mathcal{O}(\varphi_{\gamma},x^{k})
;
\triangleright
lower-level

6:

Choose a direction
d^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
using the inexact oracle
\mathcal{O}(\varphi_{\gamma},x^{k})
;
\triangleright
upper-level

7:

Find the step-size
\alpha_{k}>0
and update
x^{k+1}:=x^{k}+\alpha_{k}d^{k}
;
\triangleright
upper-level

8:

k=k+1
;

9:

end

while

In this section, we specialize ItsOPT by using HOME as the smoothing approximation and developing inexact first-order descent methods at the upper level, while the corresponding high-order proximal subproblem is solved approximately at the lower level. We consider a Hölderian inexact gradient descent method, its parameter-free variant, and an inexact descent method using the Armijo line search.

4.1
Inexact oracle for HOME

We note that the exact computation of HOPE may be expensive, and we therefore generate approximate proximal points to construct an inexact first-order oracle for HOME. The following assumptions are used throughout this section.

{assumption}
Let us consider problem (
1.1
). We assume that

(a)

p\in(1,2]
, and
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is proper, lsc, coercive, and
\rho
-weakly convex. Let
x^{*}
be a minimizer of
\varphi
.

(b)

\{\varepsilon_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
and
\{\delta_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
are non-increasing sequences in
[0,+\infty)
such that
\overline{\varepsilon}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\varepsilon_{k}<\infty
and
\delta_{k}\downarrow 0
;

(c)

Fix
r>0
and
\gamma>0
such that Corollary
2
applies on
\mathbf{B}(0;r)
.
For every
x^{k}\in\mathbf{B}(0;r)
, we can compute
\boldsymbol{{\rm prox}}_{\gamma\varphi}^{p,\varepsilon_{k}}(x^{k})
such that, for some fixed
\mu\in(0,1)
,

\left\|\boldsymbol{{\rm prox}}_{\gamma\varphi}^{p,\varepsilon_{k}}(x^{k})-\boldsymbol{{\rm prox}}_{\gamma\varphi}^{p}(x^{k})\right\|\leq\boldsymbol{\min}\left\{\delta_{k},\,\mu\left\|x^{k}-\boldsymbol{{\rm prox}}_{\gamma\varphi}^{p,\varepsilon_{k}}(x^{k})\right\|\right\},

(4.1)

and

\varphi(\boldsymbol{{\rm prox}}_{\gamma\varphi}^{p,\varepsilon_{k}}(x^{k}))+\frac{1}{p\gamma}\|x^{k}-\boldsymbol{{\rm prox}}_{\gamma\varphi}^{p,\varepsilon_{k}}(x^{k})\|^{p}\leq\varphi_{\gamma}^{p}(x^{k})+\varepsilon_{k}.

(4.2)

The two terms in
4.1
provide, respectively, an absolute error bound that vanishes asymptotically and a relative error bound with respect to the approximate proximal displacement.

The inexact function value of
\varphi_{\gamma}^{p}(x^{k})
is defined as

\varphi_{\gamma}^{p,\varepsilon_{k}}(x^{k}):=\varphi(\boldsymbol{{\rm prox}}_{\gamma\varphi}^{p,\varepsilon_{k}}(x^{k}))+\frac{1}{p\gamma}\|x^{k}-\boldsymbol{{\rm prox}}_{\gamma\varphi}^{p,\varepsilon_{k}}(x^{k})\|^{p}.

By Fact
3.1
, if
\varphi_{\gamma}^{p}
is differentiable, then

\nabla\varphi_{\gamma}^{p}(x^{k})=\frac{1}{\gamma}\|x^{k}-\boldsymbol{{\rm prox}}_{\gamma\varphi}^{p}(x^{k})\|^{p-2}(x^{k}-\boldsymbol{{\rm prox}}_{\gamma\varphi}^{p}(x^{k}))
. Motivated by this fact, if
\varphi_{\gamma}^{p}
is differentiable, we define the corresponding
inexact gradient

\nabla\varphi_{\gamma}^{p,\varepsilon_{k}}(x^{k})
of
\nabla\varphi_{\gamma}^{p}(x^{k})
as

\nabla\varphi_{\gamma}^{p,\varepsilon_{k}}(x^{k}):=\frac{1}{\gamma}\|x^{k}-\boldsymbol{{\rm prox}}_{\gamma\varphi}^{p,\varepsilon_{k}}(x^{k})\|^{p-2}(x^{k}-\boldsymbol{{\rm prox}}_{\gamma\varphi}^{p,\varepsilon_{k}}(x^{k})).

(4.3)

The pair
\left(\varphi_{\gamma}^{p,\varepsilon_{k}}(x^{k}),\nabla\varphi_{\gamma}^{p,\varepsilon_{k}}(x^{k})\right)
defines the inexact first-order oracle for HOME at
x^{k}
.

Remark 5
.

Let us consider Assumption
4.1
.

(a)

Both (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) imply that if
\delta_{k}=0
or
\varepsilon_{k}=0
,
then
\boldsymbol{{\rm prox}}_{\gamma\varphi}^{p,\varepsilon_{k}}(x^{k})=\boldsymbol{{\rm prox}}_{\gamma\varphi}^{p}(x^{k})
.

(b)

Using
(
Rodomanov and Nesterov, 2020
, Theorem 6.3)
, and analogous to the proof of Theorem
3.2
, we can show

\left\|\nabla\varphi_{\gamma}^{p,\varepsilon_{k}}(x^{k})-\nabla\varphi_{\gamma}^{p}(x^{k})\right\|\leq\frac{2^{2-p}}{\gamma}\left\|\boldsymbol{{\rm prox}}_{\gamma\varphi}^{p,\varepsilon_{k}}(x^{k})-\boldsymbol{{\rm prox}}_{\gamma\varphi}^{p}(x^{k})\right\|^{p-1}.

(4.4)

Combining (
4.1
) and (
4.4
) leads to

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla\varphi_{\gamma}^{p,\varepsilon_{k}}(x^{k})-\nabla\varphi_{\gamma}^{p}(x^{k})\right\|\leq\frac{2^{2-p}}{\gamma}\delta_{k}^{p-1}.

(4.5)

Moreover, it follows from (
4.1
), (
4.3
), and (
4.4
) that

\left\|\nabla\varphi_{\gamma}^{p,\varepsilon_{k}}(x^{k})-\nabla\varphi_{\gamma}^{p}(x^{k})\right\|\leq\frac{2^{2-p}\mu^{p-1}}{\gamma}\|x^{k}-\boldsymbol{{\rm prox}}_{\gamma\varphi}^{p,\varepsilon_{k}}(x^{k})\|^{p-1}\leq c_{p}\|\nabla\varphi_{\gamma}^{p,\varepsilon_{k}}(x^{k})\|,

(4.6)

where
c_{p}:=2^{2-p}\mu^{p-1}>0
.
This inequality has been extensively studied in the context of inexact gradient methods; see
Byrd et al. (2012)
;
Carter (1991)
;
Khanh et al. (2026)
;
Lan and Monteiro (2016)
and references therein.

(c)

Using Fact
2
and Corollary
2
, we get

\varphi_{\gamma}^{p}(y)\leq\varphi_{\gamma}^{p}(x)+\langle\nabla\varphi_{\gamma}^{p}(x),y-x\rangle+\frac{2\mathcal{L}_{p}}{p+1}\|x-y\|^{\frac{p+1}{2}},\qquad\forall x,y\in\mathbf{B}(0;r),

(4.7)

Consequently, from (
4.2
) and (
4.7
), for any
x,y\in\mathbf{B}(0;r)
and
\varepsilon_{y},\varepsilon_{x}\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\varphi_{\gamma}^{p,\varepsilon_{y}}(y)-\varepsilon_{y}\leq\varphi_{\gamma}^{p,\varepsilon_{x}}(x)+\langle\nabla\varphi_{\gamma}^{p}(x),y-x\rangle+\frac{2\mathcal{L}_{p}}{p+1}\|x-y\|^{\frac{p+1}{2}}.

(d)

Inequality (
4.6
) also yields a computable stopping criterion for the upper-level methods. Indeed,

\left\|\nabla\varphi_{\gamma}^{p}(x^{k})\right\|\leq\left\|\nabla\varphi_{\gamma}^{p}(x^{k})-\nabla\varphi_{\gamma}^{p,\varepsilon_{k}}(x^{k})\right\|+\left\|\nabla\varphi_{\gamma}^{p,\varepsilon_{k}}(x^{k})\right\|\leq(1+c_{p})\left\|\nabla\varphi_{\gamma}^{p,\varepsilon_{k}}(x^{k})\right\|,

i.e., for any prescribed tolerance
\tau_{\rm out}>0
, the computable condition

\left\|\nabla\varphi_{\gamma}^{p,\varepsilon_{k}}(x^{k})\right\|\leq\frac{\tau_{\rm out}}{1+c_{p}},

(4.8)

guarantees
\left\|\nabla\varphi_{\gamma}^{p}(x^{k})\right\|\leq\tau_{\rm out}
.
Equivalently, using (
4.3
), condition (
4.8
) can be written as

\left\|x^{k}-\boldsymbol{{\rm prox}}_{\gamma\varphi}^{p,\varepsilon_{k}}(x^{k})\right\|\leq\left(\frac{\gamma\tau_{\rm out}}{1+c_{p}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}.

Thus, the upper-level stopping test requires neither the exact HOPE point nor the exact HOME gradient.

The conditions in Assumption
4.1
are stated in terms of the exact HOPE point and HOME value. We next derive computable certificates for these conditions.

Proposition 2

(Strong convexity of the proximal model)

Let
p\in(1,2]
, let
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be a proper, lsc,
\rho
-weakly convex function, and fix
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\gamma>0
. Define the proximal model

Q_{p,\gamma}(y;x):=\varphi(y)+\frac{1}{p\gamma}\|y-x\|^{p}.

Then the following statements hold.

(a)

If
p=2
and
0<\gamma<1/\rho
, then
Q_{2,\gamma}(\cdot;x)
is globally
m_{2}
-strongly convex with

m_{2}:=\frac{1}{\gamma}-\rho>0
.

(b)

Let
p\in(1,2)
and
R>0
, and set
\mathcal{B}_{R}(x):=\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\|u-x\|\leq R\}
.
If
m_{R}:=\frac{p-1}{\gamma R^{2-p}}-\rho>0
,
then
Q_{p,\gamma}(\cdot;x)
is
m_{R}
-strongly convex on
\mathcal{B}_{R}(x)
.

Proof.

(a)
This is standard; see, e.g.,
Böhm and Wright (2021)
and
Davis and Drusvyatskiy (2019)
.

(b)

For any
u,v\in\mathcal{B}_{R}(x)
, Lemma
1
applied to
u-x
and
v-x
gives

\left\langle\frac{1}{\gamma}\|u-x\|^{p-2}(u-x)-\frac{1}{\gamma}\|v-x\|^{p-2}(v-x),u-v\right\rangle\geq\frac{p-1}{\gamma}R^{p-2}\|u-v\|^{2}.

Hence
u\mapsto\|u-x\|^{p}/(p\gamma)
is
m^{\rm reg}_{p,R}
-strongly convex on
\mathcal{B}_{R}(x)
where

m^{\rm reg}_{p,R}:=\frac{p-1}{\gamma}R^{p-2}=\frac{p-1}{\gamma R^{2-p}}.

Together with the
\rho
-weak convexity of
\varphi
, this yields the asserted modulus
m_{R}=m^{\rm reg}_{p,R}-\rho
.
∎

The preceding proposition makes the distance and value requirements in Assumption

computationally accessible. The following result gives two useful certificates.

Corollary 3

(Computable sufficient conditions for an inexact HOPE element)

Let the assumptions of Proposition

\|y-z\|\leq\frac{\|r_{\gamma,p}(y;x)\|}{m},

(4.9)

and

(4.10)

Consequently, if

\frac{\|r_{\gamma,p}(y;x)\|}{m}\leq\boldsymbol{\min}\{\delta_{k},\mu\|x-y\|\},\qquad\frac{\|r_{\gamma,p}(y;x)\|^{2}}{2m}\leq\varepsilon_{k},

satisfies (

)–(
4.2
).

(b)

Suppose that a computable lower bound

\underline{Q}\leq\varphi_{\gamma}^{p}(x)=Q_{p,\gamma}(z;x)

\widehat{\varepsilon}:=Q_{p,\gamma}(y;x)-\underline{Q},\qquad\widehat{\delta}:=\sqrt{\frac{2\widehat{\varepsilon}}{m}}.

(4.11)

Hence, if
\widehat{\varepsilon}\leq\varepsilon_{k}
,
\widehat{\delta}\leq\boldsymbol{\min}\{\delta_{k},\mu\|x-y\|\}

satisfies (

)–(
4.2
).

Proof.

(a)
The first-order optimality condition for

In light of the hypomonotonicity inequality (

which proves (

\mathcal{S}
is compact. Hence
\{x^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
has cluster points. Combining (
4.3
) with

leads to
\|x^{k}-\boldsymbol{{\rm prox}}_{\gamma\varphi}^{p,\varepsilon_{k}}(x^{k})\|\to 0
.
Additionally, from (

) and

, it holds that

\|\boldsymbol{{\rm prox}}_{\gamma\varphi}^{p,\varepsilon_{k}}(x^{k})-\boldsymbol{{\rm prox}}_{\gamma\varphi}^{p}(x^{k})\|\to 0
.
Let

be an arbitrary cluster point, and let

be a subsequence such that

.
For this subsequence, we obtain

\|\boldsymbol{{\rm prox}}_{\gamma\varphi}^{p}(x^{j})-\widehat{x}\|\leq\|x^{j}-\widehat{x}\|+\|x^{j}-\boldsymbol{{\rm prox}}_{\gamma\varphi}^{p,\varepsilon_{j}}(x^{j})\|+\|\boldsymbol{{\rm prox}}_{\gamma\varphi}^{p,\varepsilon_{j}}(x^{j})-\boldsymbol{{\rm prox}}_{\gamma\varphi}^{p}(x^{j})\|\to 0.

Therefore,
\boldsymbol{{\rm prox}}_{\gamma\varphi}^{p}(x^{j})\to\widehat{x}
.
By Fact
3

(b)
, it follows that

\widehat{x}\in\boldsymbol{{\rm prox}}_{\gamma\varphi}^{p}(\widehat{x})
, which implies
\widehat{x}\in\boldsymbol{\rm Fix}(\boldsymbol{{\rm prox}}_{\gamma\varphi}^{p})
.
∎

The hinge-loss experiment is convex and nonsmooth and uses a primal–dual certificate for the lower problem. The matrix-completion experiment is nonconvex and weakly convex and uses the residual certificate of Corollary
3
to verify every accepted lower-level point. In both cases, parameters are calibrated first and then fixed for separate confirmation instances.
All experiments are conducted in Python on a laptop equipped with a 12th Gen Intel
\circledR

5.1
Classification with hinge-loss

We first consider the ridge-regularized hinge-loss problem

\mathop{\boldsymbol{\min}}\limits_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\ \Phi(x):=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\boldsymbol{\max}\left\{0,1-b_{i}a_{i}^{\top}x\right\}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|x\|^{2},

where
a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
b_{i}\in\{-1,1\}
. This is the standard primal
\ell_{2}
-regularized support vector machine formulation with hinge loss; see, e.g.,
Chapelle (2007)
;
Shalev-Shwartz et al. (2011)
.
The objective is nonsmooth and
\lambda
-strongly convex, and hence belongs to the convex subclass of the weakly convex setting considered in this paper.

We use
m=192
,
n=24
,
\lambda=10^{-2}
, and
x^{0}=0
. For each random seed, the rows of
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
are the feature vectors
a_{i}^{\top}
. We generate

A=\sqrt{m}\,U\operatorname{diag}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})V^{\top}
with

s_{j}=30^{(j-1)/(n-1)}
,
where
U
has orthonormal columns, and
V
is orthogonal. A Gaussian vector
w_{\star}
is generated,
the scores
q=Aw_{\star}
are rescaled to have standard deviation
3
, and the labels are sampled independently according to the model

\mathbb{P}(b_{i}=1\mid a_{i})=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-q_{i})}.

For a center
c
, the lower-level problem is

Q_{p,\gamma}(y;c)=\Phi(y)+\frac{1}{p\gamma}\|y-c\|^{p}.

(5.1)

Since
\Phi\geq 0
, its exact minimizer
z
belongs to
\mathbf{B}(c;R)
, where
R=(p\gamma\Phi(c))^{1/p}
. On this ball,
Q_{p,\gamma}(\cdot;c)
is strongly convex with the modulus

m_{R}=\lambda+\frac{p-1}{\gamma R^{2-p}}
. We solve (
5.1
) by the accelerated primal–dual method
Chambolle and Pock (2011)
. If
D(v)
is a feasible dual lower bound and

\Delta=Q_{p,\gamma}(y;c)-D(v)

Q_{p,\gamma}(y;c)-\mathop{\boldsymbol{\min}}\limits_{u}Q_{p,\gamma}(u;c)\leq\Delta,\qquad\|y-z\|\leq\widehat{\delta}:=\sqrt{\frac{2\Delta}{m_{R}}},

where
z
is the exact lower minimizer.
A lower solution at the outer iteration
k
is accepted whenever

\Delta\leq\varepsilon_{k},\qquad\widehat{\delta}\leq\boldsymbol{\min}\{\mu\|y-c\|,(k+1)^{-2}\},\qquad Q_{p,\gamma}(y;c)\leq\Phi(c),

with

\varepsilon_{k}=\frac{\varepsilon_{\rm sc}\boldsymbol{\max}\{1,\Phi(x^{0})\}}{(k+1)^{2}},

where
\varepsilon_{\rm sc}
is a scaling parameter for the accuracy at the lower-level.
These conditions provide the value and distance guarantees required in Assumption
4.1
; see Corollary
3
.

In all selected ItsDEAL configurations,
\varepsilon_{\rm sc}=10^{-3}
. The initial primal and dual step-sizes of the lower solver are
t_{0}=\frac{r_{0}\sqrt{m}}{\|A\|}
and
\sigma_{0}=\frac{0.99}{t_{0}\|A\|^{2}}
,
where
r_{0}
is calibrated together with
\gamma
.
Let us set

g^{k}=\frac{1}{\gamma}\|x^{k}-y^{k}\|^{p-2}(x^{k}-y^{k}),

be the inexact HOME gradient. We choose
\mu
so that
c_{p}:=2^{2-p}\mu^{p-1}=0.95

for both orders, giving
\mu=0.45125
for
p=1.5
and
\mu=0.95
for
p=2
.
For the parameter-free HiGDA (PFHiGDA) in Algorithm
4

.
The backtracking starts from
L_{0}=10^{-4}
and uses the factor
2
. For IDEALS in Algorithm
5

d^{k}=-D_{p}\|g^{k}\|^{(2-p)/(p-1)}g^{k},\qquad D_{p}=0.75\,\gamma^{1/(p-1)},

so that

. The Armijo parameter and the backtracking factor are both
1/2
.
The parameters are selected on seeds

–

–

\Phi(x)\leq L+\tau\bigl(\Phi(x^{0})-L\bigr),\qquad\tau\in\{10^{-2},10^{-5}\},

Let us now turn to an MCP-regularized hinge-loss problem. Nonconvex penalties, such as MCP and SCAD, are commonly used in sparse high-dimensional SVMs because they can reduce the estimation bias associated with convex sparsity penalties while performing variable selection

5.2
Robust matrix completion

We consider a nonsmooth robust low-rank matrix-completion problem. Let
\Omega\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}^{2}
denote the set of observed indices,
and let
Y_{ij}
be the observed value at
(i,j)\in\Omega
. The variable
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the matrix to be estimated. We consider

\Phi(X)=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{(i,j)\in\Omega}|X_{ij}-Y_{ij}|+\lambda R_{a}(X),

(5.4)

where

R_{a}(X)=\frac{a^{2}}{2}\sum_{\ell}\log\left(1+\frac{\sigma_{\ell}(X)^{2}}{a^{2}}\right),

\sigma_{\ell}(X)
denotes the
\ell
-th singular value of
X
, and
a>0
controls the shape of the rank surrogate. The first term in (
5.4
) is convex and nonsmooth. Related nonsmooth
\ell_{1}
formulations are used in robust low-rank recovery; see, e.g.,
Li et al. (2020)
. The spectral term is a smooth nonconvex log-det-type rank surrogate motivated by
Fazel et al. (2003)
.

Let us set

h_{a}(t)=\frac{a^{2}}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{t^{2}}{a^{2}}\right).

Since

h_{a}^{\prime\prime}(t)=\frac{a^{2}(a^{2}-t^{2})}{(a^{2}+t^{2})^{2}}\geq-\frac{1}{8},

the function
t\mapsto h_{a}(t)+t^{2}/16
is convex and nondecreasing on
[0,\infty)
. Hence
R_{a}(X)+\frac{1}{16}\|X\|_{F}^{2}
is convex. Therefore,
R_{a}
is
1/8
-weakly convex, and (
5.4
) is
\rho
-weakly convex with
\rho=\frac{\lambda}{8}
.

We generate
20\times 20
matrices of rank
4
with singular values
8,6,4,3
, and rescale each planted matrix to have unit standard deviation. Each entry is observed independently with probability
0.4
. Gaussian noise with standard deviation
0.02
is added to the observations, and approximately
15\%
of the observed entries receive an additional Student-
t_{2}
perturbation with scale
4
. We set
\lambda=0.05
and
a=1.2
.

All methods use the same deliberately rank-underestimated warm start. Let
Q_{0.6}
denote the empirical
60\%
quantile of the absolute observed entries and set

\tau_{\rm clip}=\max\left\{0.5,\,1.5Q_{0.6}\bigl(\{|Y_{ij}|:(i,j)\in\Omega\}\bigr)\right\}
.
The observed entries are clipped at
\tau_{\rm clip}
, and the resulting sparse matrix is scaled by the inverse realized observation fraction. Its rank-
2
truncated SVD is used to initialize a rank-
2
factorization. We then refine this factorization for at most
80
L-BFGS-B iterations using a pseudo-Huber data term and ridge coefficient
0.02
. The resulting rank-
2
matrix is the common initial point
X^{0}
.

At an outer center
C
, the lower problem is

Q_{p,\gamma}(Y;C)=\Phi(Y)+\frac{1}{p\gamma}\|Y-C\|_{F}^{p}.

We solve this problem by forward–backward splitting. The smooth part is
\lambda R_{a}
, while the nonsmooth proximal part is

\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{(i,j)\in\Omega}|Y_{ij}-Y_{ij}^{\rm obs}|+\frac{1}{p\gamma}\|Y-C\|_{F}^{p}.

For a fixed radial coefficient
\beta=\gamma^{-1}\|Y-C\|_{F}^{p-2}
, the corresponding proximal step reduces to entrywise soft-thresholding on the observed entries. The
coefficient
\beta
is obtained from a one-dimensional scalar equation.

The internal stopping test of the lower solver is not used as a certificate. Let
Z
be an exact minimizer of the lower model. Since
\Phi\geq 0
,

Q_{p,\gamma}(Z;C)\leq Q_{p,\gamma}(C;C)=\Phi(C),

and therefore

\|Z-C\|_{F}\leq R_{C}:=\bigl(p\gamma\Phi(C)\bigr)^{1/p}.

We also require the candidate
Y
to satisfy
Q_{p,\gamma}(Y;C)\leq\Phi(C)
. By Proposition
2
, a valid strong-convexity modulus
of the lower model on this ball is

m_{C}=\frac{p-1}{\gamma R_{C}^{2-p}}-\rho,\qquad 1<p<2,

and
m_{C}=1/\gamma-\rho
for
p=2
. The certificate is used only when
m_{C}>0
. This condition was verified at every accepted outer call in the reported experiments.
For a candidate
Y
, we choose
\xi_{Y}\in\partial\Phi(Y)
so that the norm of

r_{\gamma,p}(Y;C)=\xi_{Y}+\frac{1}{\gamma}\|Y-C\|_{F}^{p-2}(Y-C),

is minimized. The separability of the absolute-loss term makes this choice explicit on the observed entries. Corollary
3

gives the computable bounds

\widehat{\delta}=\frac{\|r_{\gamma,p}(Y;C)\|_{F}}{m_{C}},\qquad\widehat{\varepsilon}=\frac{\|r_{\gamma,p}(Y;C)\|_{F}^{2}}{2m_{C}}.

The candidate is accepted only if

6
Concluding remarks

In this work, we introduced ItsDEAL, an Inexact two-level smoothing DEscent ALgorithm, to address weakly convex optimization problems. Indeed, this framework leverages the high-order Moreau envelope (HOME) to create a suitable smooth approximation of such a nonsmooth and nonconvex cost function. Crucially, new results to address the differentiability and weak smoothness of HOME under weak convexity assumptions were derived, paving the way toward developing first- or second-order optimization methods. By solving the underlying proximity problem approximately, an inexact oracle for HOME is provided. Further, a novel descent condition using this inexact oracle was established that is particularly well-suited to select proper descent directions for the minimization of weakly convex functions.

A key strength of the ItsDEAL framework is its versatility. We have demonstrated its applicability by developing specific inexact first-order methods, namely, a Hölderian inexact gradient descent algorithm (HiGDA) with both fixed and dynamically adaptive step-sizes, and an inexact descent algorithm with Armijo line search (IDEALS). These algorithms are specifically tailored to minimize the smooth approximations generated by HOME using its inexact oracle.
The numerical behavior of the ItsDEAL framework was examined on ridge-regularized hinge-loss classification, high-dimensional MCP-regularized classification, and robust matrix completion. The experiments show that the HOME order can materially affect the behavior of the proposed methods. In the convex ridge experiment, IDEALS with
p=1.5
is more competitive at the higher-accuracy target. In the nonconvex MCP experiment, the quadratic order decreases the training objective more aggressively, while subquadratic orders preserve better held-out classification performance under the short common CPU budget. The matrix-completion experiment shows an early-update advantage for
p=1.75
, although direct subgradient updates remain much cheaper. These results do not establish uniform CPU-time superiority over subgradient methods. They instead illustrate that the HOME order provides a meaningful algorithmic degree of freedom, while the main contribution of the paper remains the theoretical framework and the computable lower-level certification.
