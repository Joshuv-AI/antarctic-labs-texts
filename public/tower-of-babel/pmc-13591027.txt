Genomic characterization of antifungal resistance patterns in Candida auris clade I: A large-scale analysis of 647 global genomes
Authors: Ayhan Tosunoglu, Ozleyis Konyali, Mehmet Demirci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
OBJECTIVECandidozyma auris has emerged as a global “urgent threat” characterized by multidrug resistance and high mortality. While six distinct lineages have been identified, Clade I (South Asian) is the primary driver of global nosocomial outbreaks and exhibits the most profound resistance profiles. This study aims to provide a high-resolution genomic characterization of Clade I, focusing on the consolidation of resistance mechanisms and virulence factors that support its global dominance.METHODSWe performed a comprehensive genomic analysis focusing on a cohort of 647 Clade I isolates, selected from a total of 662 global C. auris genomes available in public repositories. Using a standardized bioinformatic pipeline, we conducted core-genome single-nucleotide polymorphisms-based phylogenetic reconstruction, non-synonymous mutation profiling of key resistance genes (TAC1B, FKS1, ERG11/6/3), and functional mapping of virulence-related pathways (ALS4, secretable aspartyl proteases [SAP5], LIP1). The remaining isolates from Clades II to VI were utilized as comparative reference groups to identify clade-specific signatures.RESULTSAnalysis of the Clade I cohort (n=647; 97.73% of the total dataset) revealed a significant consolidation of resistance markers. The Y132F and K143R substitutions in ERG11 were near-ubiquitous, often co-occurring with specific TAC1B variants (A640V, V742A). Notably, 24.32% of the Clade I isolates demonstrated a highly synchronized “genomic armor,” characterized by the simultaneous presence of TAC1B (A:YTDQ/A:GSVG), FKS1 (S:SL), and deletions in ERG6/ERG3. Virulence profiling showed high-frequency conservation of biofilm-associated (ALS4) and proteolytic (SAP5) genes, suggesting a synergistic evolution of resilience and pathogenicity.CONCLUSIONThis study delineates the genomic landscape of C. auris Clade I, highlighting how the consolidation of multiple resistance and virulence markers contributes to its clinical success. The high frequency of multidrug-resistant genotypes within this lineage mandates a transition toward genome-led surveillance and personalized antifungal stewardship.

Global public health networks have been on high alert since the 2009 identification of Candidozyma auris in Japan, as this pathogen has shown an extraordinary capacity for rapid expansion [1, 2]. Characterized by its unique ability to colonize human skin, persist on inanimate surfaces for prolonged periods, and develop rapid resistance to all major classes of antifungal agents, this pathogen has been reported in over 60 countries across six continents [3, 4]. With clinical mortality rates reaching as high as 60%, both the centers for disease control and prevention (CDC) and the World Health Organization now categorize C. auris as an “urgent threat” to global public health [5, 6]. What distinguishes C. auris from most Candida counterparts is its exceptional capacity to anchor onto medical hardware. Beyond mere adherence, it deploys a diverse array of hydrolytic enzymes, particularly secretable aspartyl proteases (SAPs) and lipases, which drive both tissue infiltration and the bypassing of host immunity [7].

Phylogenomic investigations have established that C. auris consists of six geographically clustered but genetically distinct lineages (clades): South Asian (Clade I), East Asian (Clade II), African (Clade III), South American (Clade IV), Iranian (Clade V), and the recently described Indomalayan (Clade VI) [8–10]. These clades are separated by thousands of single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), yet they exhibit high clonality within each lineage, suggesting that multidrug-resistant (MDR) C. auris emerged independently and nearly simultaneously across diverse geographic regions under similar selective pressures [1, 9]. Among these, Clade I has achieved the widest global dissemination and serves as the primary driver for major nosocomial outbreaks worldwide, exhibiting the most complex and robust resistance profiles [11, 12].

Pan-drug resistance (PDR) has emerged as a major clinical hurdle; it renders standard therapies obsolete and leaves clinicians with few, if any, viable treatment routes for C. auris. Genomic surveillance has elucidated that mutations in gene families such as TAC1B, FKS1, ERG11, ERG6, and ERG3 confer a “genomic armor” against azoles, echinocandins, and polyenes [13, 14]. Recent evidence suggests that these resistant lineages can evolve and persist without incurring traditional evolutionary fitness costs, maintaining high virulence while resisting the most potent antifungal therapies [15]. This dangerous synergy between high-level resistance and a robust virulence arsenal, comprised of adherence and invasion genes such as ALS4, HYR3, SAP5, and LIP1, defines C. auris as a uniquely resilient “super-pathogen” [7, 13]. While whole-genome sequencing (WGS) remains the gold standard for deciphering these patterns, most existing research focuses on localized outbreaks, often leaving the broader patterns of genomic consolidation within the dominant lineages under-explored [16].

Local outbreak reports have multiplied recently, yet we still lack the high-resolution datasets needed to map how resistance markers stabilize across a truly global cohort. While all six clades represent the phylogenetic breadth of the species, the overwhelming clinical burden is currently driven by Clade I. In this study, I present a large-scale genomic characterization focusing on 647 Clade I isolates within a total cohort of 662 global C. auris genomes. By integrating mutation profiling with functional Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway mapping, this study aims to delineate the patterns of genomic consolidation that drive multidrug resistance and metabolic fitness within this globally dominant lineage. The findings provide a high-resolution map of the pathogen’s evolutionary trajectory, offering critical insights for the development of targeted clinical interventions.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data Acquisition and Quality Control

A comprehensive genomic dataset was curated by retrieving WGS data from public repositories, including the NCBI sequence read archive and the European Nucleotide Archive. Following a strict quality control workflow, our final analytical cohort was narrowed down to 662 high-quality genomes. To ensure high-quality assemblies, we set a strict threshold for each genome, requiring at least 30x average depth of coverage and a completeness level exceeding 95%. This cohort primarily consists of 647 Clade I isolates, providing high-resolution genomic depth for this globally dominant lineage, while isolates from Clades II to VI (n=15) were included as comparative reference groups to identify lineage-specific and pan-clade genomic signatures [6, 17].

Bioinformatic Pipeline and Variant Calling

Raw sequence reads were processed using FastQC (v0.11.9) and trimmed with Trimmomatic (v0.39) (Phred score <20). High-quality reads were aligned to the C. auris B8441 reference genome (GenBank: GCA_002759435.2) using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA-MEM v0.7.17) [10]. To refine the alignments, we utilized SAMtools (v1.9) for coordinate-based sorting, while Picard Tools (v2.23.4) was employed specifically to identify and strip polymerase chain reaction duplicates.

Variant calling was performed using Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) (v4.1.8.1) HaplotypeCaller following “best practices” for germline short variant discovery. To ensure high-quality variant calls and minimize false positives, stringent hard filtering was applied using GATK VariantFiltration with the following parameters: QualByDepth <2.0, FisherStrand >60.0, RMSMapping Quality <40.0, MappingQualityRankSumTest <−12.5, and ReadPosRankSumTest <−8.0 [11, 14]. Annotation of identified variants was conducted using SnpEff (v5.0), focusing on non-synonymous mutations in gene families associated with antifungal resistance (TAC1B, FKS1, ERG11, ERG6, and ERG3) and virulence (ALS4, SAP5), as characterized in recent pathogenesis updates [7, 13, 14].

To ensure that the identified structural deletions in ERG3 and ERG6 were not bioinformatic assembly artefacts, a high-depth coverage mapping analysis was performed. To validate the structural variants, we performed a targeted re-alignment of raw reads against the reference template, followed by manual evidence-tracking at specific genomic loci. Only regions with zero-read coverage across the entire gene length, despite a minimum average genome-wide coverage of ×30, were classified as true deletions.

Delineating Metabolic Resilience: Functional Annotation and KEGG Mapping

To translate genomic data into biological roles, we integrated our findings into the KEGG framework for functional annotation. Protein sequences were predicted and mapped to KEGG Orthology (KO) identifiers using the GhostKOALA and KAAS pipelines [18, 19]. This facilitated the systematic reconstruction of essential metabolic pathways, including biofilm formation (map05111), ABC transporters (map01501), and steroid biosynthesis (map00100). The conservation of each pathway was quantified to identify genomic consolidation patterns that facilitate the highly resilient MDR phenotype [7, 13].

Phylogenetic Reconstruction and Visualization

Phylogenetic relationships among the 662 isolates were reconstructed based on a high-resolution core-genome SNP alignment. To reconstruct the evolutionary relationships, we employed RAxML (v8.2.12) to build a maximum-likelihood (ML) tree, applying the GTRGAMMA substitution model across 1,000 bootstrap replicates for robust branch support [6, 9]. The resulting phylogenetic data were visualized and annotated using the interactive tree of life v6 [20]. Metadata, including clade assignment, mutation profiles (TAC1B, FKS1, ERG), and KEGG functional categories, were mapped onto the tree to visualize the evolutionary trajectory of the Clade I lineage.

Lineage Assignment and Clade Identification

Isolates were assigned to clades using a two-step validation process. First, core-genome SNP clustering was performed against validated reference strains: B8441 (Clade I), B11220 (Clade II), B11221 (Clade III), B11245 (Clade IV), B15643 (Clade V), and Indomalayan reference markers for Clade VI [6, 8, 9]. Second, genetic distances were verified; inter-clade variation exceeded the established threshold of >3,000 SNPs, while intra-clade variation remained consistent with lineage-specific clonality [1, 10, 16].

Clade assignment was performed through a two-step validation process. First, isolates were clustered using an ML approach based on the core-genome SNP alignment. Second, the genetic distance between each isolate and the reference strains was calculated to verify that the inter-clade variation exceeded the established threshold of >3,000 SNPs, while intra-clade variation remained consistent with lineage-specific clonality [1, 10]. Any isolate showing ambiguous clustering was further cross-verified using the “CLADE-FINDER” logic and the most recent Indomalayan lineage markers to ensure the integrity of the Clade VI assignments [16].

Statistical Analysis and Data Processing

Custom Python scripts were developed to automate the integration of genomic datasets and metadata. High-throughput manipulation of mutation profiles and KEGG assignments was conducted using the NumPy and Pandas libraries [21]. For statistical visualization, Matplotlib and Seaborn were utilized to generate distribution plots and heatmaps illustrating the conservation of metabolic pathways. The analytical scripts have been made available in a public repository to ensure the reproducibility and robustness of the phylogenomic findings.

Antifungal Mutational Landscape and the “Genomic Armor” of Clade I

The large-scale genomic screening of 647 Clade I isolates revealed a profound level of mutational synchronization, defining a consolidated “genomic armor.” In the ERG11 gene, the Y132F and K143R substitutions were identified in 99.24% (n=642/647) of the Clade I isolates. Furthermore, the FKS1 gene exhibited highly conserved hotspot mutations at the S639 position, correlating with an MDR profile.

The phylogenomic analysis provides definitive evidence of the global transition of C. auris Clade I into a “highly resilient MDR pathogen.” As illustrated in Appendix 1, this dominance is inextricably linked to a standardized “multi-locus genetic armor,” where synergistic combinations of TAC1B variants (notably A: YTDQ) and FKS1 mutations co-occur with structural deletions in ERG6 and ERG3. The fact that the Reference (B8441-like) genotype has been reduced to a marginal 0.76% (n=5) of the population underscores that PDR is no longer a transient adaptation but a fixed genomic baseline in Clade I (Table 3).

Lineage-Specific Distribution of Virulence Factors

Beyond antifungal resistance, genomic screening identified a robust repertoire of virulence-associated genes consistently maintained. Key adhesins and invasins, including the Agglutinin-like sequence family (ALS4) and HYR3, were detected in 99.24% of the Clade I isolates. The enzymatic arsenal required for tissue degradation, specifically SAP5 and lipases (LIP1), showed high conservation, particularly in the dominant Clade I and the emerging Clade VI.

Subtelomeric deletions observed across the cohort primarily affected genes involved in iron acquisition (SIT1), glucose transport (HGT), and cell-surface adhesion. The high conservation of these determinants in Clade I suggests that the pathogen has reached a state of evolutionary maturity, where high-level resistance is integrated into its core genomic architecture without compromising biological fitness (Table 2).

Delineating the Pan-metabolic Framework via KO Mapping

To translate genomic variants into functional insights, we projected the identified orthologous groups onto the KEGG database. By doing so, we reconstructed the metabolic architecture that underpins C. auris resilience, pinpointing the specific pathways that likely facilitate its persistence under diverse clinical pressures. This step allowed us to visualize the specific metabolic pathways that may drive C. auris survival in clinical environments. Functional profiling of the Clade I cohort reveals a profound level of genomic stabilization across essential biological modules. Critical pathways, including antifungal resistance, biofilm formation (map05111), steroid biosynthesis (map00100), and adhesion, were found to be highly conserved in 99.24% of the Clade I isolates. This near-universal prevalence suggests that C. auris has reached an evolutionary plateau where these traits are fixed components of its core genome (Fig. 2).

Correlation between Resistome and Metabolic Fitness

A significant finding was the strong statistical association (99.24%) between the presence of PDR-associated mutations (in TAC1B and FKS1) and the maintenance of high-order virulence pathways (KEGG map05111). This functional stability indicates that the evolution of pan-resistance in C. auris Clade I does not impair its pathogenic potential. The metabolic pathways for carbohydrate metabolism and energy production remained highly conserved, identifying Clade I as a uniquely resilient “super-pathogen” that avoids fitness costs by maintaining efficient functional genomic potential even under extreme drug-induced stress.

DISCUSSION

The global emergence of C. auris represents a significant shift in fungal epidemiology, characterized by independent lineage evolution and an alarmingly rapid progression toward multidrug resistance [1, 10]. This study, focusing on a robust cohort of 647 Clade I isolates within a 662-genome dataset, provides a high-resolution phylogenomic and functional mapping of the globally dominant lineage. By bridging the gap between genomic variation and metabolic fitness, it is demonstrated that C. auris Clade I has reached a state of genomic consolidation, where resistance mechanisms and virulence factors are functionally synchronized to ensure survival under extreme clinical and environmental pressure. While early foundational studies established a four-clade paradigm [1, 11], the present analysis of 662 genomes confirms that this rapid diversification is driven by both core-genome SNPs and highly dynamic subtelomeric regions, which contribute to significant karyotype plasticity and clinical adaptation [22].

In this global cohort, Clade I remains the overwhelmingly dominant lineage at 97.73%, a finding that aligns with recent epidemiological surveys [12, 15]. However, the global landscape is becoming increasingly complex. For instance, recent studies from Turkiye and Europe have documented independent introductions where genomic epidemiology revealed “star-like” transmission patterns, emphasizing the pathogen’s ability to establish endemicity rapidly [23, 24]. The most critical revelation of this analysis is the near-total prevalence (99.24%) of combined antifungal resistance markers within Clade I. This 662-genome analysis confirms that mutations such as Y132F/K143R in ERG11 are part of a broader “genetic armor” that includes synergistic co-occurrence with TAC1B and FKS1 variants [13, 24]. The detection of Clades IV and VI across diverse geographic regions confirms that the global landscape of C. auris is no longer defined by strict geographic isolation, but by transcontinental expansion and multiple introductions [13, 14, 25–27].

The present large-scale screening confirms that the synergistic co-occurrence of mutations in TAC1B, FKS1, and the ERG gene family is now a standardized feature of the global C. auris population. This “genetic armor” is likely orchestrated by complex epigenetic mechanisms, such as the recurrent deletion in the epigenetic regulator GCN5 identified in recent European isolates. This inherent plasticity likely serves as a regulatory tool for C. auris, enabling the pathogen to adaptively reshape its cell wall pathways to survive intense drug selection. This dynamic reconfiguration appears to offset the fitness costs typically linked to high-level antifungal resistance [24]. This lack of fitness cost is further validated by the consistent maintenance of high-order virulence genes such as ALS4, SAP5, LIP1, and the core regulator RBF1 [7, 16, 25]. Our findings suggest that C. auris has successfully uncoupled drug resistance from fitness defects, allowing it to maintain full pathogenic potential even under extreme antifungal pressure [22, 24].

Furthermore, the implementation of KEGG pathway mapping allowed for a transition from descriptive mutation lists to a deeper functional understanding. The discovery that 99.24% of the isolates possess fully integrated genomic modules for both biofilm formation (map05111) and ABC transporter-mediated drug efflux (map01501) indicates that these traits have been optimized as a single functional unit. Notably, the role of the urinary tract has emerged as an anatomical hotspot for therapy-induced resistance, where subtherapeutic drug concentrations facilitate the rapid intra-patient emergence of FKS1 mutations [25, 28]. The clinical emergence of non-canonical variants, such as the G32E substitution in CDC10 or S237Y in CRZ1, points toward a sophisticated, multi-layered cell wall integrity network [24, 26].

Given the scale of this mutational synchronization, existing global management protocols may no longer be sufficient, requiring an urgent shift toward more targeted intervention strategies. The global dominance of combined mutations targeting both azoles (ERG11 Y132F/K143R) and echinocandins (FKS1 S639P) across nearly the entire cohort suggests that empirical therapy may soon reach a point of significantly decreased efficacy [29]. The fact that pan-resistance signatures are already present in the recently emerged Clade VI suggests that new lineages are entering the clinical landscape with pre-existing adaptive advantages. Moving forward, our results prioritize the search for therapeutic interventions that disrupt the entire metabolic network of C. auris, moving beyond the traditional narrow focus on isolated resistance markers [30].

Despite the global reach of our genomic dataset, certain structural constraints in the sampling process require careful consideration. First, the genomic data were primarily sourced from public repositories, which results in a lack of detailed longitudinal clinical metadata for some isolates, limiting the ability to correlate genomic markers with direct patient outcomes. Second, while KEGG pathway mapping provides a robust computational framework, further in vitro transcriptomic and proteomic studies are required to confirm the expression levels of these consolidated pathways. Finally, the extreme rarity of Clade II, III, IV, V, and VI isolates in global databases, while included here for comparative purposes, means that these lineages do not yet have the same statistical depth as the dominant Clade I. Given the current global data distribution, a formal power calculation for rarer lineages remains constrained by sample availability. Consequently, the genomic signatures identified for non-Clade I lineages are presented here as preliminary observations rather than definitive population-level trends.

Conclusion

Nevertheless, the unprecedented scale of this study, encompassing 662 genomes under a standardized bioinformatic pipeline, represents a significant advancement. By providing a high-resolution map of Clade I, I have delineated a universal pattern of “genomic consolidation” that defines the modern C. auris landscape. The strong statistical association between pan-resistance markers and core virulence pathways provides definitive evidence that this pathogen has evolved into a “highly resilient multidrug-resistant pathogen” capable of bypassing traditional fitness trade-offs.
