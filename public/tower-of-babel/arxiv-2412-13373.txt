Wick theorem and matrix Capelli identities for quantum differential operators on Reflection Equation Algebras Thanks: Pavel.Saponov@ihep.ru Thanks: mrzaytsev@edu.hse.ru
Dimitry Gurevich

Abstract
Quantum differential operators on Reflection Equation Algebras, corresponding to Hecke symmetries R R were introduced in previous publications. In the present paper we are mainly interested in quantum analogs of the Laplace and Casimir operators, which are invariant with respect to the action of the Quantum Groups U q ​ ( s ​ l ​ ( N ) ) U_{q}(sl(N)) , provided R R is the Drinfeld-Jimbo R R -matrix. We prove that any such an operator maps the central characteristic subalgebra of a Reflection Equation algebra into itself. Also, we define the notion of normal ordering for the quantum differential operators and prove an analog of the Wick theorem for the product of partially ordered operators. As an important corollary we find a set of universal matrix Capelli identities generalizing the results of [ Ok2 ] and [ JLM ] . Besides, we prove that the normal ordered form of any central differential operator from the characteristic subalgebra is also a central differential operator.

Keywords:
Quantum doubles, quantum differential operators, normal ordering, Wick theorem, matrix Capelli identity.

1
Introduction

In this paper we introduce and study quantum analogs of differential operators defined on a class of quantum matrix algebras called Reflection Equation (RE) algebras. We use
the term
quantum analog
in a wide sense of the word, not restricting ourselves to objects, related to the quantum groups

U_{q}(sl(N))
. The latter objects (
R
-matrices, differential operators etc) are called
standard
in what follows.

The starting point for constructing an RE algebra is a
Hecke symmetry

R
. Any such a symmetry is a linear operator
R\in\mathrm{End}(V^{\otimes 2})
,
V
being a complex
vector space
\dim_{\,{C}}V=N
, which is subject to a
braid relation
in the algebra
\mathrm{End}(V^{\otimes 3})

(R\otimes I)(I\otimes R)(R\otimes I)=(I\otimes R)(R\otimes I)(I\otimes R),

(1.1)

and, in addition, obeys the
Hecke condition

(q\,I\otimes I-R)(q^{-1}I\otimes I+R)=0,\qquad q\in{{C}}\setminus\{\pm 1,0\}.

(1.2)

Hereafter,
I
stands for the identity operator on
V
or the
N\times N
unit matrix. The numeric parameter
q
is assumed to be
generic
, i.e.
q^{n}\not=1
for all positive integers
n\in{Z}_{+}
.

Remark 1

If in (
1.2
) one sets
q=1
, then the corresponding operator
R
is called an
involutive symmetry
.
The well known example of an involutive symmetry is the usual flip
P=\mathrm{End}(V^{\otimes 2}):

P\triangleright(u\otimes v)=v\otimes u,\qquad\forall\,u,v\in V.

From now on the symbol
\triangleright
denotes the action of a linear operator.

Below, we suppose that any Hecke symmetry in question allows a limit
q\to 1
and turns into an involutive symmetry in this limit.
If, in addition,
\lim_{q\to 1}R=P
, then
R
will be referred to as a deformation of the flip
P
. The most important example
of such a Hecke symmetry is the Drinfeld-Jimbo
R
-matrix connected with the quantum group
U_{q}(sl(N))
.

Given a Hecke symmetry
R
, the corresponding RE algebra is defined as a unital associative algebra
{\cal{M}}(R)
finitely generated
by the set of entries of an
N\times N
matrix
M=\|m_{i}^{j}\|
, which satisfies the relation:

R\,(M\otimes I)R\,(M\otimes I)-(M\otimes I)R\,(M\otimes I)R=0.

(1.3)

Note that if
R=P
, then
{\cal{M}}(P)=\mathrm{Sym}(gl(N))
.

The RE algebras possess a lot of remarkable properties. In particular, they admit introducing of analogs of the partial derivatives in the
generators
m_{i}^{j}
and analogs of the Weyl-Heisenberg
algebras. The latter analogs can be introduced in terms of the so-called
quantum doubles
. By a quantum double
(A,B)
we mean
a couple of associative algebras
A
and
B
equipped with a
permutation map

\sigma:A\otimes B\to B\otimes A
, compatible with their
algebraic structures (see
[
GS1
]
for more details and precise definitions). The map
\sigma
plays the role of the Leibniz
rule for elements of the algebra
A
, treated as vector fields (in particular, quantum partial derivatives), which act on elements of the
algebra
B
. Note that the classical Leibniz rule is universal and is valid for all vector fields, whereas in the quantum case the permutation
map essentially depends on given algebras
A
and

B
. Observe that by contrast with the approach of the paper
[
IP
]
, in our setting the quantum double consists of two RE algebras in
different realizations
1
1

1

Considered in
[
IP
]
was the double of an RE algebra and an algebra of quantized functions on a group
(the so-called RTT algebra).
.

In the quantum double, we are dealing with, the role of a function subalgebra
B
is played by the RE algebra
{\cal{M}}(R)
. The algebra
A
is
realized as the RE algebra
{\cal{D}}(R^{-1})
, generated by entries of the matrix
D=\|{\partial}_{i}^{j}\|
which is subject to the relation:

R^{-1}(D\otimes I)R^{-1}(D\otimes I)-(D\otimes I)R^{-1}(D\otimes I)R^{-1}=0.

(1.4)

The permutation relations among generators of the algebras
{\cal{D}}(R^{-1})
and
{\cal{M}}(R)
are exhibited in (
3.3
). This
relations allow us to define a linear action of the subalgebra
{\cal{D}}(R^{-1})
on subalgebra
{\cal{M}}(R)
which is compatible both with
the algebraic structure of
{\cal{D}}(R^{-1})
and that of
{\cal{M}}(R)
. Due to this fact, the generators
\partial_{i}^{j}
of the RE algebra

{\cal{D}}(R^{-1})
are treated as
quantum derivatives
. Note that in the standard case the corresponding
double
({\cal{D}}(R^{-1}),{\cal{M}}(R))
is a quantization (deformation) of the
gl(N)
-type Weyl-Heisenberg algebra of differential
operators in partial derivatives
\partial/\partial m_{i}^{j}
with polynomial coefficients in coordinates
m_{i}^{j}
.

An important property of the quantum double
({\cal{D}}(R^{-1}),{\cal{M}}(R))
is that the entries of the matrix
\hat{L}=MD=\|\hat{l}_{i}^{j}\|

generate the so-called modified RE algebra
\hat{{\cal L}}(R)
. The permutation relations among its generators are determined by
the following matrix equality:

R\,(\hat{L}\otimes I)R\,(\hat{L}\otimes I)-(\hat{L}\otimes I)R\,(\hat{L}\otimes I)R=R\,(\hat{L}\otimes I)-(\hat{L}\otimes I)R.

(1.5)

If
R=P
, the algebra
\hat{{\cal L}}(P)
is isomorphic to the universal enveloping algebra
U(gl(N))
. In this case the generators

\hat{l}_{i}^{j}=\sum_{k}m_{i}^{k}{\partial}^{j}_{k}
of the algebra
U(gl(N))

are represented by the Euler type vector fields, acting on the commutative algebra

{\cal{M}}(P)=\mathrm{Sym}(gl(N))\simeq{{C}}[m_{i}^{j}]
.
In general, we get quantum analogs of these vector fields acting on the noncommutative RE algebra

{\cal{M}}(R)
, whereas a new quantum Leibniz rule for them is given by the corresponding permutation relations.

In the classical case (i.e. while
R=P
) the elements
\mathrm{Tr}D^{k}
and
\mathrm{Tr}\hat{L}^{k}
are invariant under the adjoint action of the group
GL(N)

and are referred to as Laplace and Casimir operators respectively. Also, the normally ordered operators

W^{(\mathbf{k})}=\,:\!\mathrm{Tr}(\hat{L}^{k_{1}})\dots\mathrm{Tr}(\hat{L}^{k_{p}})\!:\qquad\mathbf{k}=(k_{1},\dots,k_{p}),\quad k_{i}\in{{Z}}_{\geq 0}

are of interest. Here, the colons stand for the usual normal ordering

:\!\partial_{k}^{s}m_{i}^{j}\!:\,=m_{i}^{j}\partial_{k}^{s}.

(1.6)

Being properly normalized, the operators
W^{(\mathbf{k})}
are
called cut-and-join ones. They play an important role in combinatorics and integrable system theory.

Note that numerous aspects of classical theory can be extended to the RE algebras and their doubles — algebras of quantum
differential operators. Below, we define the quantum analogs of the Laplace and Casimir operators in a way, similar to the classical
patterns. The peculiarity of the quantum case is that the usual matrix trace
\mathrm{Tr}
, entering the
definitions of invariant elements, is replaced by its quantum analog
\mathrm{Tr}_{R}
, which is well defined for any
skew-invertible

Hecke symmetry
R
. In the standard case the quantum Laplace and Casimir operators are invariant with respect to the action of
the quantum group
U_{q}(sl(N))
.

We define the notion of normal ordering of quantum derivatives
\partial_{i}^{j}
and “coordinates”
m_{i}^{j}
which is a deformation of
the classical rule (
1.6
). Emphasize, that the relation
\hat{L}=MD
enables us to define the ordered form of Casimir operators in the
quantum case. We prove that any normally ordered quantum differential operator from the central characteristic subalgebra
of the algebra
\hat{{\cal L}}(R)
is a central differential operator. In this way, the quantum analogs of the cut-and-join operators

W^{(\mathbf{k})}
can be easily defined.

Moreover, we generalize on the quantum double
({\cal{D}}(R^{-1}),{\cal{M}}(R))
the Wick formula,
relating differential operators and their normally ordered forms. This formula entails a set of
new matrix Capelli identities, generalizing those from
[
Ok2
]
and
[
JLM
]
. Thus, the main ingredients of the paper

[
Ok2
]
— the symmetric groups and the enveloping algebras
U(gl(N))
are replaced by their quantum analogs
— the Hecke and RE algebras. As for the matrix Capelli identities, in the recent paper
[
JLM
]
they were proved in a
particular case of the standard Hecke symmetry. By contrast with
[
JLM
]
, our version of the Capelli identities is universal
in the sence that they are not attached to a concrete Young diagram and are valid for
any
skew-invertible Hecke symmetry

R
including the supersymmetric
GL(m|n)
type generalizations. Also, note that quantum analogs of immanants from
[
Ok1
]

can be defined not only in the standard case as was done in
[
JLM
]
but for any RE algebra (see
[
Z
]
).

The paper is organized as follows. In the next section we remind some basic constructions related to the Hecke symmetries and the
corresponding RE algebras. In Section
3
we introduce the quantum derivatives and define generalized quantum
Laplacians on these algebras. We prove that the action of the generalized Laplace operators maps the central
characteristic subalgebra into itself. In Section
4
we introduce the generalized Casimir operators by means of the “quantum
Euler-type vector fields”. In Section
5
we define the normal ordering for quantum differential operators and prove a version
of the Wick theorem which allows to transform the partially ordered product of quantum differential operators into totally
ordered form. Section
6
is devoted to derivation of the set of universal matrix Capelli identities, as well as to the proof of
centrality of the normally ordered Casimir operators.

2
Preliminaries

In this section we introduce some basic notation and give definitions of objects and constructions used in what follows.

Recall that for any given integer
k\geq 2
the
Hecke algebra

H_{k}(q)
is a unital associative algebra over the complex field

{{C}}
generated by Artin’s generators
\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{k-1}
, which are subject to the set of relations:

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\tau_{i}\,\tau_{i+1}\,\tau_{i}=\tau_{i+1}\,\tau_{i}\,\tau_{i+1},&&1\leq i\leq k-2\\
\rule{0.0pt}{14.22636pt}\tau_{i}\,\tau_{j}=\tau_{j}\,\tau_{i},&&|i-j|\geq 2\\
\rule{0.0pt}{14.22636pt}(q\,1-\tau_{i})(q^{-1}\,1+\tau_{i})=0,&&q\in{{C}}\setminus\{\pm 1,0\},\end{array}

where
1
stands for the unit element of the algebra. As is known, the Hecke algebra
H_{k}(q)
is finite dimensional and for a
generic
q
is isomorphic to the group algebra
{{C}}[S_{k}]
of the symmetric group
S_{k}
. It is convenient to define
H_{1}(q)

as an algebra generated by the only unit element. Thus, we have
H_{1}(q)\simeq{{C}}
.

Below we need the so-called
Jucys-Murphy elements

j_{r}
,
1\leq r\leq k
, which generate a maximal commutative subalgebra in

H_{k}(q)
:

j_{1}=1,\quad j_{r}=\tau_{r-1}\,j_{r-1}\,\tau_{r-1},\quad 2\leq r\leq k.

(2.1)

Observe that any Hecke symmetry
R
defines the
R
-matrix representation

\rho_{R}
of the Hecke algebra
H_{k}(q)
in
the space
V^{\otimes k}
:

\rho_{R}:H_{k}(q)\rightarrow\mathrm{End}(V^{\otimes k}),\qquad\forall\,k\geq 2.

The representation
\rho_{R}
is completely defined by the images
\rho_{R}(\tau_{i})\in\mathrm{End}(V^{\otimes k})

of the generators
\tau_{i}
:

\rho_{R}(\tau_{i})=R_{i}:=R_{i\,i+1}=I^{\otimes(i-1)}\otimes R\otimes I^{\otimes(k-i-1)},\qquad 1\leq i\leq k-1.

(2.2)

Also, we denote
J_{r}:=\rho_{R}(j_{r})
.

From now on, we assume any Hecke symmetry
R
to be
skew-invertible
. This means that there exists an operator
\Psi:V^{\otimes 2}\to V^{\otimes 2}

such that

\mathrm{Tr}_{2}R_{12}\,\Psi_{23}=\mathrm{Tr}_{2}\Psi_{12}R_{23}=P_{13}.

(2.3)

Recall that
P
stands for the usual flip.

Let us fix a basis
\{x_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq N}
in the space
V
and the corresponding bases
\{x_{i_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes x_{i_{k}}\}
in the spaces

V^{\otimes k}
,
k\geq 2
. Then the operator
\Psi
can
be identified with its
N^{2}\times N^{2}
matrix
\|\Psi_{ij}^{\,rs}\|
in the above tensor basis of the space
V^{\otimes 2}
. Consider
the
N\times N
matrrix
C
with the following matrix elements:

C_{i}^{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Psi_{ik}^{jk}.

The matrix
C
allows one to define the
R
-trace
of any
N\times N
matrix
X
:

\mathrm{Tr}_{R}X=\mathrm{Tr}(CX).

(2.4)

An immediate consequence of (
2.3
) is the following important property:

\mathrm{Tr}_{R(2)}R_{12}=\mathrm{Tr}_{2}(C_{2}R_{12})=I_{1}.

(2.5)

Now, we describe a way of constructing some central elements of the RE algebra
{\cal{M}}(R)
defined in (
1.3
), which belong to
its
characteristic subalgebra
. Also, we recall the definition of the quantum Schur functions (polynomials) and power sums as
particular examples of these elements.

For this purpose it is convenient to introduce the following
matrix “copies” of the generating matrix
M
:

M_{\overline{1}}=M_{1}=M\otimes I^{\otimes(k-1)},\qquad M_{\overline{r+1}}=R_{\,r}M_{\,\overline{r}}\,R_{\,r}^{-1},\qquad r\geq 1.

(2.6)

In virtue of definition (
2.2
) the matrices
M_{\,\overline{r}}
for
1\leq r\leq k
represent different embeddings of the quantum
generating matrix
M
into the space
(\mathrm{Mat}_{N})^{\otimes k}
of
N^{k}\times N^{k}
matrices. Below, we do not fix the
concrete value of the integer
k
just assuming
k
to be sufficiently large so that all the matrix formulae make sense.

In analogy with (
2.2
) we will often consider embeddings of arbitrary
N\times N
matrices into the space

(\mathrm{Mat}_{N})^{\otimes k}
and will use a similar notation:

X_{i}=I^{\otimes(i-1)}\otimes X\otimes I^{\otimes(k-i)},\quad\forall\,X\in\mathrm{Mat}_{N}.

Note that for

all these embeddings coincide:
I_{i}=I^{\otimes k}
for any
1\leq i\leq k
.

The following important theorem was proved in

]
.

Let

Consider now some specific elements of the characteristic subalgebra which we need in what follows. For a given integer

\tau_{k-1}\tau_{k-2}\dots\tau_{1}\in H_{k}(q)

p_{k}(M):=\mathrm{Tr}_{R(1\dots k)}\left(\rho_{R}(\tau_{k-1}\tau_{k-2}\dots\tau_{1})M_{\overline{1\to k}}\,\right)=\mathrm{Tr}_{R(1\dots k)}\left(R_{k-1}R_{k-2}\dots R_{1}\,M_{\overline{1\to k}}\,\right).

The polynomials

. Note that the power sums (

For any partition

(2.9)

Since all factors in this product are central in the algebra

. Thus, the power sums

.

Another important set of central elements is formed by the

s_{\lambda}(M)
, associated with partitions
\lambda\vdash n
,

. The polynomials

were defined in

Here

The connection of quantum polynomials

3
Quantum derivatives and generalized Laplace operators

Let us briefly remind the construction of a quantum double from
[
GS1
]
since it plays the central role in the subsequent considerations.

By a quantum double we mean a couple
(A,B)
of two associative algebras
A
and
B
, equipped with a
permutation map

\sigma:A\otimes B\to B\otimes A,

which preserves the algebraic structures of
A
and
B
. If these algebras are defined via relations on their generators, this property means that the ideals, generated by the
relations, are preserved by the map
\sigma
(see
[
GS1
]
for detail).

Often it is more convenient to use the so-called
permutation relations

a\otimes b=\sigma(a\otimes b),\quad a\in A,\,\,b\in B.

(3.1)

If the algebra
A
admits a one-dimensional representation
\varepsilon:A\to{{C}}
, then it is possible to define an action
\triangleright:A\otimes B\rightarrow B
of the algebra

A
on
B
by setting:

a\triangleright b:=(\mathrm{Id}\otimes\varepsilon)\sigma(a\otimes b),\quad\forall\,a\in A,\,\,b\in B.

(3.2)

Here, as usual we identify the elements
b
and
b\otimes 1
. Due to the properties of
\sigma
, such an action will be a representation of
A
in the algebra
B
(see
[
GS1
]
).

Our basic example of the quantum double is provided by the RE algebras
A={\cal{D}}(R^{-1})
and
B={\cal{M}}(R)
defined by the relations (
1.4
) and (
1.3
).
Let us introduce the permutation relations as follows
[
GPS5
]
:

D_{1}R_{12}M_{1}R_{12}=R_{12}M_{1}R_{12}^{-1}D_{1}+R_{12}1_{B}1_{A}.

(3.3)

The entries of the matrix
D=\|{\partial}_{i}^{j}\|_{1\leq i,j\leq N}
acquire the meaning of operators if we apply the trivial one-dimensional representation of the RE algebra
A={\cal{D}}(R^{-1})
:

\varepsilon(1_{A})=1,\qquad\varepsilon({\partial}_{i}^{j})=0,\quad 1\leq i,j\leq N.

Using the general formula (
3.2
) with this map
\varepsilon
we get:

D_{1}\triangleright R_{12}M_{1}R_{12}=R_{12}M_{1}R_{12}^{-1}\varepsilon(D_{1})+R_{12}1_{B}\varepsilon(1_{A})=R_{12}1_{B}.

(3.4)

Below we shall omit the symbols of the unit elements
1_{A}
and
1_{B}
.

Remark 5

Note, that the above formula for action of
D
contains the summation over matrix indices, so it actually describes the actions of some linear combinations of
\partial_{i}^{j}
on some
other linear combinations of
m_{r}^{s}
. Nevertheless, due to the invertibility and skew-invertibility of
R
, the action (
3.4
) can be transformed into the following equivalent form:

D_{1}\triangleright M_{2}=\mathrm{Tr}_{0}(\Psi_{02})P_{12}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\partial_{i}^{j}\triangleright m_{k}^{s}=\delta_{i}^{s}\,B_{k}^{j},\quad B_{i}^{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Psi_{ki}^{\,kj}.

So, in fact, formula (
3.4
) allows one to find the action of any given
\partial_{i}^{j}
on any given
m_{k}^{s}
.

Note that if
R=P
, the algebras
{\cal{M}}(P)
and
{\cal{D}}(P^{-1})
become commutative and the action (
3.4
) takes the form:

D_{1}\triangleright P_{12}M_{1}P_{12}=D_{1}\triangleright M_{2}=P_{12}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad{\partial}_{i}^{j}\triangleright m_{k}^{s}=\delta_{i}^{s}\,\delta_{k}^{j}.

This motivates us to consider the generators of the subalgebra
A={\cal{D}}(R^{-1})
as quantum analogs of the usual partial derivatives

\partial_{i}^{j}=\partial/\partial m_{j}^{i}
. Also, we treat
the quantum double
({\cal{D}}(R^{-1}),{\cal{M}}(R))
as a quantum counterpart of the usual Weyl-Heisenberg algebra of
gl(N)
type.

Introduce the following elements which are central in the subalgebra
{\cal{D}}(R^{-1})
of the quantum double (
[
IOP
]
, see also
Proposition
2
):

D_{Q}^{(m)}=\mathrm{Tr}_{R(1\dots m)}\left(Q(R_{1},\dots,R_{m-1})\,D_{\,\overline{1\rightarrow m}}\right),\qquad m\geq 1,

(3.5)

where
Q(R_{1},\dots,R_{m-1})
is an arbitrary polynomial in
R_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq m-1
. Taking
Q=R_{m-1}\dots R_{1}

Definition 16

:\!D_{1}M_{\,\overline{2}}\!:\,=M_{\,\overline{2}}D_{1}R_{1}^{-2}.

Note that the transformation to the ordered form defined in (

:\!\hat{L}_{1}\hat{L}_{\,\overline{2}}:\,=M_{1}M_{\,\overline{2}}D_{\,\overline{2}}D_{1}R_{1}^{-2}=M_{1}M_{\,\overline{2}}D_{\,\overline{2}}D_{1}J^{-1}_{2}.

) we can express the product of two differential operators in terms of the ordered ones:

\hat{L}_{1}\,\hat{L}_{\overline{2}}=\,:\!\hat{L}_{1}\,\hat{L}_{\overline{2}}:+\hat{L}_{1}\,R^{-1}_{1}.

Our next aim is to determine the normal ordered form of an arbitrary order monomial in quantum operators
\hat{L}
. To prove the corresponding theorem we need
the following technical lemma.

Lemma 17

The normal ordered form of the operator
D_{\,\overline{m}}\hat{L}_{\,\overline{n}}
for
1\leq m\leq n-1
is as follows:

:\!D_{\,\overline{m}}\hat{L}_{\,\overline{n}}:\,=\hat{L}_{\,\overline{n}}D_{\,\overline{m}}\,J_{n-m}^{\,\uparrow m}(J_{n-m+1}^{-1})^{\uparrow(m-1)},\qquad n\geq 2,\quad 1\leq m\leq n-1,

(5.3)

where
J_{k}^{\,\uparrow p}=R_{k+p-1\,\rightarrow\,p+1}R_{p+1\,\rightarrow\,k+p-1}
is the image of the Jucys-Murphy
element
j_{k}
under the shifted
R
-matrix repesentation
\rho_{R}^{\,\uparrow p}
deined in
(
4.6
)
.

Proof.
For
m=1
,
n=2
the claim of the lemma follows directly from (
5.1
):

:\!D_{1}\hat{L}_{\,\overline{2}}:\,=:\!\underline{D_{1}M_{\,\overline{2}}}D_{\,\overline{2}}:\,=M_{\,\overline{2}}D_{1}R_{1}^{-2}D_{\,\overline{2}}=M_{\,\overline{2}}D_{1}R_{1}^{-1}D_{1}R_{1}^{-1}=M_{\,\overline{2}}D_{\,\overline{2}}D_{1}R_{1}^{-2}=\hat{L}_{\,\overline{2}}D_{1}J_{2}^{-1}.

Then, having applied an appropriate shift formula (
3.9
)
(m-1)
times (see footnote
3
), we find

:\!D_{\,\overline{m}}\hat{L}_{\,\overline{m+1}}:\,=\hat{L}_{\,\overline{m+1}}D_{\,\overline{m}}R_{m}^{-2}=\hat{L}_{\,\overline{m+1}}D_{\,\overline{m}}\,(J_{2}^{-1})^{\,\uparrow(m-1)},

which coincides with (
5.3
) for
n=m+1
since
J_{1}^{\,\uparrow m}=I_{m+1}
.

Now the final result (
5.3
) for
n>m+1
is easy to obtain:

\displaystyle:\!D_{\,\overline{m}}\hat{L}_{\,\overline{n}}:

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R_{n-1\rightarrow m+1}D_{\,\overline{m}}\hat{L}_{\,\overline{m+1}}\,R^{-1}_{m+1\rightarrow n-1}=R_{n-1\rightarrow m+1}\hat{L}_{\,\overline{m+1}}D_{\,\overline{m}}\,R_{m}^{-2}R^{-1}_{m+1\rightarrow n-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{L}_{\,\overline{n}}D_{\,\overline{m}}\,R_{n-1\rightarrow m+1}R_{m}^{-2}R^{-1}_{m+1\rightarrow n-1}=\hat{L}_{\,\overline{n}}D_{\,\overline{m}}\,J_{n-m}^{\,\uparrow m}(J_{n-m+1}^{-1})^{\uparrow(m-1)}.

The proof is completed.

We are able to prove the following main theorem on normal ordered forms.

Theorem 18

The normal ordered form of the product
\hat{L}_{\,\overline{1\rightarrow k}}=\hat{L}_{1}\dots\hat{L}_{\,\overline{k}}
reads as follows:

:\!\hat{L}_{\,\overline{1\rightarrow k}}:\,=M_{\,\overline{1\rightarrow k}}D_{\,\overline{k\rightarrow 1}}\,\Bigl(\prod_{s=1}^{k}J^{-1}_{s}\Bigr)=\Bigl(\prod_{s=1}^{k}J^{-1}_{s}\Bigr)M_{\,\overline{1\rightarrow k}}\,D_{\,\overline{k\rightarrow 1}},\qquad\forall\,k\geq 1.

(5.4)

Proof.
We prove the theorem by induction in
k
. The base of induction at
k=1
is obvious. Let (
5.4
) be valid up to some integer
k\geq 1
, we must verify
that then it is valid for
k+1
.

Taking into account the induction assumption we write:

:\!\hat{L}_{\,\overline{1\rightarrow k+1}}:=\Big(\prod_{s=1}^{k}J^{-1}_{s}\Big):\!M_{\,\overline{1\rightarrow k}}\,D_{\,\overline{k\rightarrow 1}}\hat{L}_{\,\overline{k+1}}:

(5.5)

Then, the relation (
5.3
) allows one to get the following normal ordered form:

:\!D_{\,\overline{r\rightarrow 1}}L_{\,\overline{k+1}}:=L_{\,\overline{k+1}}D_{\,\overline{r\rightarrow 1}}\,J_{k-r+1}^{\,\uparrow r}J_{k+1}^{-1},\quad\forall\,r\leq k.

For
r=k
this formula simplifies to
:\!D_{\,\overline{k\rightarrow 1}}L_{\,\overline{k+1}}:=L_{\,\overline{k+1}}D_{\,\overline{k\rightarrow 1}}J_{k+1}^{-1}

and we can
complete our proof substituting this to (
5.5
):

:\!\hat{L}_{\,\overline{1\rightarrow k+1}}:=\Big(\prod_{s=1}^{k}J^{-1}_{s}\Big)M_{\,\overline{1\rightarrow k}}\,\hat{L}_{\,\overline{k+1}}\,D_{\,\overline{k\rightarrow 1}}J_{k+1}^{-1}=\Big(\prod_{s=1}^{k+1}J^{-1}_{s}\Big)M_{\,\overline{1\rightarrow k+1}}\,D_{\,\overline{k+1\rightarrow 1}}.

Here we used the fact that matrix
J^{-1}_{i}
commute with the product
M_{\,\overline{1\rightarrow p}}\,D_{\,\overline{p\rightarrow 1}}
for all

i\leq p
due to relations

R_{s}^{-1}M_{\,\overline{s}}M_{\,\overline{s+1}}=M_{\,\overline{s}}M_{\,\overline{s+1}}R_{s}^{-1},\qquad R_{s}^{-1}D_{\,\overline{s+1}}D_{\,\overline{s}}=D_{\,\overline{s+1}}D_{\,\overline{s}}R_{s}^{-1}.

Also, this allows one to place the multipliers
J^{-1}_{s}
at any side in formula (
5.4
).

Now we are going to establish a quantum analog of the Wick formula, which transforms a partially ordered product of differential
operators into the totally ordered form. To prove the quantum Wick theorem we need the lemma below.

Lemma 19

The following matrix identity takes place for
\forall\,k\geq 1
:

D_{\,\overline{k\,\rightarrow\,1}}\,\hat{L}_{\,\overline{k+1}}=\hat{L}_{\,\overline{k+1}}\,D_{\,\overline{k\,\rightarrow\,1}}J_{k+1}^{-1}+D_{\,\overline{k\,\rightarrow\,1}}\,\frac{I_{k+1}-J^{-1}_{k+1}}{q-q^{-1}}.

(5.6)

Proof.
We prove the lemma by induction in
k
. The induction base for
k=1
is an immediate consequence of (
3.3
) and (
1.4
). Indeed, multiplying
(
3.3
) by
R_{1}^{-1}D_{1}R_{1}
from the right and applying (
1.4
) for
R_{1}^{-1}D_{1}R_{1}^{-1}D_{1}
we obtain:

D_{1}\hat{L}_{\,\overline{2}}=\hat{L}_{\,\overline{2}}D_{1}R_{1}^{-2}+D_{1}R_{1}^{-1},

(5.7)

which is precisely formula (
5.6
) written for
k=1
if we take into account the definition of the Jucys-Murphy element:

J_{2}^{-1}=R_{1}^{-2}=I_{2}-(q-q^{-1})R_{1}^{-1}.

On applying relation (
3.9
) successively
k
times to (
5.7
) we extend it to the higher matrix copies:

D_{\,\overline{k}}\hat{L}_{\,\overline{k+1}}=\hat{L}_{\,\overline{k+1}}D_{\,\overline{k}}R_{k}^{-2}+D_{\,\overline{k}}\,R_{k}^{-1}.

(5.8)

Now, assume that formula (
5.6
) is valid up to some integer
k-1\geq 1
. We should prove that then it is fulfilled for
k
too.
We have a chain of transformations:

\displaystyle D_{\,\overline{k\,\rightarrow\,1}}\,\hat{L}_{\overline{k+1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D_{\,\overline{k\,\rightarrow\,1}}\,R_{k}\,\hat{L}_{\overline{k}}\,R^{-1}_{k}=D_{\overline{k}}\,R_{k}\,\underline{D_{\,\overline{k-1\,\rightarrow\,1}}\,\hat{L}_{\overline{k}}}\,R^{-1}_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D_{\overline{k}}\,R_{k}\,\hat{L}_{\overline{k}}\,D_{\,\overline{k-1\,\rightarrow\,1}}\,J^{-1}_{k}\,R^{-1}_{k}+D_{\,\overline{k\,\rightarrow\,1}}\,R_{k}\,\frac{I_{k}-J^{-1}_{k}}{q-q^{-1}}\,R^{-1}_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D_{\overline{k}}\,\hat{L}_{\overline{k+1}}\,D_{\,\overline{k-1\,\rightarrow\,1}}\,R_{k}\,J^{-1}_{k}\,R^{-1}_{k}+D_{\,\overline{k\,\rightarrow\,1}}\,\frac{I_{k+1}-R_{k}\,J^{-1}_{k}\,R^{-1}_{k}}{q-q^{-1}}.

Here, the underlined term is transformed in accordance with the induction assumption.

Next, we use (
5.8
) and continue the above transformations as follows:

\displaystyle D_{\,\overline{k\,\rightarrow\,1}}\,\hat{L}_{\overline{k+1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{L}_{\,\overline{k+1}}\,D_{\,\overline{k\,\rightarrow\,1}}\,R^{-1}_{k}\,J^{-1}_{k}\,R^{-1}_{k}+D_{\,\overline{k\,\rightarrow\,1}}\left(J^{-1}_{k}\,R_{k}^{-1}+\frac{I_{k+1}-R_{k}\,J^{-1}_{k}\,R^{-1}_{k}}{q-q^{-1}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{L}_{\,\overline{k+1}}\,D_{\,\overline{k\,\rightarrow\,1}}\,J^{-1}_{k+1}+D_{\,\overline{k\,\rightarrow\,1}}\,\frac{I_{k+1}-J^{-1}_{k+1}}{q-q^{-1}}.

The proof of the lemma is completed.

So, we are ready to prove a quantum analog of the Wick theorem.

Theorem 20

The following quantum Wick formula holds true:

:\!\hat{L}_{\,\overline{1\rightarrow k}}:\hat{L}_{\,\overline{k+1}}=\,:\!\hat{L}_{\,\overline{1\,\rightarrow\,k+1}}:+:\!\hat{L}_{\,\overline{1\rightarrow k}}:\frac{I_{k+1}-J^{-1}_{k+1}}{q-q^{-1}},\qquad\forall\,k\geq 1.

(5.9)

Proof.

In accordance with Theorem
18
we have:

:\!\hat{L}_{\,\overline{1\rightarrow k}}:\,\hat{L}_{\overline{k+1}}=\Big(\prod_{s=1}^{k}J^{-1}_{s}\Big)M_{\,\overline{1\,\rightarrow\,k}}\,D_{\,\overline{k\,\rightarrow 1}}\,\hat{L}_{\overline{k+1}}.

Taking into account (
5.6
) we get:

\displaystyle\Big(\prod_{s=1}^{k}J^{-1}_{s}\Big)M_{\,\overline{1\,\rightarrow\,k}}\,D_{\,\overline{k\,\rightarrow\,1}}\hat{L}_{\overline{k+1}}

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69054pt=

\displaystyle\hskip-8.53581pt\Big(\prod_{s=1}^{k}J^{-1}_{s}\Big)M_{\,\overline{1\,\rightarrow\,k}}\,\hat{L}_{\overline{k+1}}\,D_{\,\overline{k\,\rightarrow\,1}}\,J^{-1}_{{k+1}}

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt+

\displaystyle\hskip-11.38109pt\Big(\prod_{s=1}^{k}J^{-1}_{s}\Big)M_{\,\overline{1\,\rightarrow\,k}}\,D_{\,\overline{k\,\rightarrow 1}}\,\frac{I_{k+1}-J^{-1}_{k+1}}{q-q^{-1}}

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt=

\displaystyle\hskip-11.38109pt\Big(\prod_{s=1}^{k}J^{-1}_{s}\Big)M_{\,\overline{1\,\rightarrow\,k+1}}\,D_{\,\overline{k+1\,\rightarrow 1}}\,J^{-1}_{{k+1}}+:\!\hat{L}_{\,\overline{1\,\rightarrow\,k}}:\,\frac{I_{k+1}-J^{-1}_{k+1}}{q-q^{-1}}

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt=

\displaystyle\hskip-11.38109pt:\!\hat{L}_{\,\overline{1\,\rightarrow\,k+1}}:+:\!\hat{L}_{\,\overline{1\,\rightarrow\,k}}:\,\frac{I_{k+1}-J^{-1}_{k+1}}{q-q^{-1}}.

The proof is completed.

6
Universal quantum Capelli identities

In this section we establish the set of
universal quantum matrix Capelli identities
. The term “universal” reflects the fact, that
all other known forms of Capelly identities (see
[
Ok1
,
Ok2
,
JLM
]
) can be obtained as particular cases (as matrix projections or
as a limit
q\to 1
) of the universal ones.

Let us introduce the following matrix notation:

{\cal P}_{1}=I_{1},\qquad{\cal P}_{k}=\frac{I_{k}-J^{-1}_{k}}{q-q^{-1}},\quad k\geq 2.

(6.1)

Taking into account the definition of
J_{k}^{-1}
and the Hecke condition
R_{\,i}^{-2}=I_{i+1}-(q-q^{-1})R_{\,i}^{-1}
we present

{\cal P}_{k+1}
as the following polynomial in
R_{\,i}
,
i\leq k
:

{\cal P}_{k+1}=R^{-1}_{k}+\sum_{s=1}^{k-1}R^{-1}_{k\,\rightarrow\,s+1}\,R_{s}^{-1}R^{-1}_{s+1\,\rightarrow k}.

This form of
{\cal P}_{k+1}
is convenient for calculating the classical limit
q\rightarrow 1
. Observe that if the Hecke symmetry
R

tends to the flip
R\rightarrow P
as

q\rightarrow 1
, the polynomial
{\cal P}_{k+1}
tends to the sum of the transpositions
(i,k+1)
in the tensor representation of the
group algebra
{{C}}[S_{k+1}]
in the space
V^{\otimes(k+1)}
:

{\cal P}_{k+1}\,\rightarrow\,\sum_{i=1}^{k}P_{ik+1}.

Theorem 21

For the quantum differential operators
\hat{L}=MD
the following matrix Capelli identities take place for
\forall\,k\geq 2
:

\hat{L}_{1}(\hat{L}_{\overline{2}}-{\cal P}_{2})\dots(\hat{L}_{\overline{k}}-{\cal P}_{k})=M_{\,\overline{1\,\rightarrow\,k}}\,D_{\,\overline{k\,\rightarrow\,1}}\,\Big(\prod_{s=1}^{k}J^{-1}_{s}\Big).

(6.2)

Proof.
To prove (
6.2
) it suffices to rewrite (
5.9
) in the form:

:\!\hat{L}_{\,\overline{1\,\rightarrow\,k-1}}:\left(\hat{L}_{\overline{k}}-{\cal P}_{k}\right)=:\!\hat{L}_{\,\overline{1\,\rightarrow\,k}}:

and then apply the same formula for
:\!\hat{L}_{\,\overline{1\,\rightarrow\,k-1}}:
and so on. For
:\!\hat{L}_{\,\overline{1\,\rightarrow\,k}}:
in the right
hand side we use (
5.4
).

If the Hecke symmetry
R
is a deformation of the usual flip
P
or of the superflip of
GL(m|n)
type, then passing to the limit
q\rightarrow 1
, we get the corresponding universal
matrix Capelli identities in
U(gl(N))
or in
U(gl(m|n))
.

Now we point out an important corollary of the Capelli identities (
6.2
): the normal ordering (
5.1
) maps the characteristic
subalgebra of the RE algebra
\hat{{\cal L}}(R)
into itself. In other words, the normal ordering does not destroy
the centrality of a central differential operator.

Theorem 22

The normally ordered form of any generalized Casimir operator
(
4.2
)

:\!C^{(k)}_{Q}(\hat{L})\!:\,=\mathrm{Tr}_{R(1\dots k)}\left(Q(R_{1},\dots,R_{k}):\!\hat{L}_{\,\overline{1\to k}}\!:\right)

belongs to the characteristic subalgebra of the RE algebra
\hat{{\cal L}}(R)
and, therefore, is a central quantum differential operator.

Proof.
As follows from (
5.4
) and (
6.2
), the normally ordered form of
\hat{L}_{\,\overline{1\to k}}
coincides with
the left hand side of the corresponding Capelly identity:

:\!\hat{L}_{\,\overline{1\to k}}\!:\,=\hat{L}_{1}(\hat{L}_{\overline{2}}-{\cal P}_{2})\dots(\hat{L}_{\overline{k}}-{\cal P}_{k}).

Then, taking into account the definition (
6.1
) it is not difficult to verify that

{\cal P}_{s}\hat{L}_{\,\overline{r}}=\hat{L}_{\,\overline{r}}\,{\cal P}_{s},\quad\forall\,r>s.

So, having expanded all the brackets in the right hand side of the above expression for
:\!\hat{L}_{\,\overline{1\to k}}\!:
, we can
move all the matrices
{\cal P}_{s}
to the
right
of all chains of
\hat{L}
operators and get the following expression:

:\!\hat{L}_{\,\overline{1\to k}}\!:\,=\hat{L}_{\,\overline{1\to k}}+\sum_{n=1}^{k-1}\,\,\sum_{2\leq s_{1}<\dots<s_{n}\leq k}\hat{L}_{1}\dots\hat{L}^{\prime}_{s_{1}}\dots\hat{L}^{\prime}_{s_{n}}\dots\hat{L}_{\,\overline{k}}\,{\cal P}_{s_{1}}\dots{\cal P}_{s_{n}},

(6.3)

where the symbols with prime mean the absence of the corresponding multipliers.

Next, we use the formula (
3.9
) in order to “fill the gaps” in the product of operators
\hat{L}
, and present the typical
term in (
6.3
) in the form (see the proof of the Theorem
8
):

\hat{L}_{1}\dots\hat{L}^{\prime}_{s_{1}}\dots\hat{L}^{\prime}_{s_{n}}\dots L_{\,\overline{k}}=F_{\{s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\}}(R)\,\hat{L}_{\,\overline{1\,\to\,k-n}}\,F_{\{s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\}}(R^{-1})

for some polynomials
F_{\{s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\}}(R)
.

And, at last, the cyclic property of the
R
-trace allows us to come to the final answer:

:\!C^{(k)}_{Q}(\hat{L})\!:=C^{(k)}_{Q}(\hat{L})+\sum_{n=1}^{k-1}C^{(k-n)}_{Q^{(n)}}(\hat{L}),

(6.4)

where the polynomials
Q^{(n)}(R)
are found as partial
R
-traces of the form:

Q^{(n)}(R)=\sum_{2\leq s_{1}<\dots<s_{n}\leq k}\,\mathrm{Tr}_{R(k-n+1\dots k)}\left(F_{\{s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\}}(R^{-1})\,{\cal P}_{s_{1}}\dots{\cal P}_{s_{n}}Q(R)F_{\{s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\}}(R)\right).

So, any normally ordered generalized Casimir operator can be presented as a finite sum of generalized Casimir operators (
6.4
)
and, therefore, it is a central quantum differential operator from the characteristic subalgebra.

In conclusion, we would like to explain the connection of the universal Capelli identities with the results, obtained in
[
JLM
]
,

[
Ok1
]
and
[
Ok2
]
.
If we multiply (

,
\lambda\vdash k

J_{s}^{-1}E^{\lambda}_{T}(R)=E^{\lambda}_{T}(R)J_{s}^{-1}=q^{-2c_{s}(T)}E^{\lambda}_{T}(R),\quad\forall\,\lambda\vdash k,\quad 1\leq s\leq k,

where

Emphasize, that our version of the Capelli identities (
