Mapping DNA glycosylase binding across lesion sequence contexts reveals extended sequence and structural recognition logic
Authors: Noga Levy, Vered Levin Salomon, Sharon N. Greenwood, Matthew Wang, Naama Kessler, Omer Erez, Brian P. Weiser, Ariel Afek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
DNA repair of mutagenic lesions is imperfect, allowing mutations to accumulate unevenly across the genome. In base excision repair, glycosylases must locate rare damaged bases embedded in diverse sequence contexts, yet how these contexts shape recognition and mutational outcomes remains unresolved. Here, we introduce a high-throughput approach that quantifies glycosylase binding across thousands of lesion-containing sequences. Focusing on the cytosine deamination pathway, we map the recognition landscapes of human UDG, TDG, and MBD4. Binding depends strongly on sequence context, extending several bases beyond the lesion and including non-additive interactions between neighboring positions. Structural analyses and molecular dynamics simulations implicate DNA-shape features, including minor groove width, as determinants of recognition. Nearest-neighbor preferences resemble deamination-related cancer mutational signatures, whereas broader-context preferences track variation in cytosine–thymine balance across matched human genomic contexts. Together, these findings establish a versatile and generalizable platform for decoding glycosylase recognition and linking repair specificity to mutational patterns.

Introduction

Genomic DNA is continuously challenged by spontaneous chemical reactions that generate thousands of lesions per cell per day1, including uracil from cytosine deamination and T:G mismatches from 5-methylcytosine deamination (Fig. 1a). Cellular DNA repair pathways protect genomic integrity, with base excision repair (BER) providing the first line of defense against these frequent yet subtle forms of damage2,3. This protection is mediated by several partially overlapping glycosylases that recognize and excise lesions arising from cytosine deamination. Uracil in DNA is removed primarily by Uracil DNA Glycosylase (UDG), which provides broad surveillance of cytosine deamination products and can additionally act on single-stranded substrates4. Other uracil-processing glycosylases, including Single-strand-selective Monofunctional Uracil DNA Glycosylase (SMUG1), also contribute in specific cellular contexts4. Thymine DNA Glycosylase (TDG) and Methyl-CpG-Binding Domain 4 (MBD4) act mainly on double-stranded DNA and recognize T:G mismatches arising from 5-methylcytosine deamination5. Recent studies suggest that, under physiological conditions, MBD4 is the dominant glycosylase for repair of these lesions at methylated CpG sites, whereas TDG has a major role in the excision of oxidized 5-methylcytosine derivatives during active DNA demethylation6–8. If left unrepaired, these lesions are mutagenic, leading to genomic alterations that can be either creative or destructive. While mutations contribute to cancer, aging, and genetic disease, they also drive evolution and immunity development9–11. The efficiency with which BER enzymes recognize and excise lesions is therefore a key determinant of mutational outcomes12–14. Importantly, mutational burden is not uniform across the genome but reflects a balance between damage formation and repair efficiency, both of which vary with local sequence context15–17 (Fig. 1a, b). The mutational consequences of inefficient repair are likely to be most striking for highly frequent lesions, since incomplete removal can rapidly lead to accumulation of mutations1. Yet even relatively infrequent lesions can leave a substantial genomic imprint over longer timescales.

Evidence indicates that flanking bases can modulate repair efficiency16,18, though these observations are based on a limited number of studied sequence contexts. Mutational signatures in cancer genomes further suggest that context-dependent repair contributes to mutational biases19–22. Therefore, understanding mutational patterns requires understanding not only whether a lesion is present, but how its local sequence and structural context influence its recognition by BER enzymes. Still, in contrast to transcription factors—where high-throughput assays have defined sequence rules, DNA shape preferences, and cooperative interactions23–25—no comparable maps exist for DNA repair enzymes. The general principles by which BER glycosylases interpret sequence context remain unresolved (Fig. 1b).

Repair involves base flipping and local DNA distortion18,26, and crystallographic and mechanistic studies have revealed highly bent DNA conformations in the bound state27,28 (Fig. 1c). Crystal structures of UDG, TDG, and MBD4 reveal a shared recognition framework in which lesion detection is coupled to base extrusion and stabilization of a strongly distorted DNA intermediate, with protein–DNA interfaces extending several base pairs beyond the lesion site29–31. At the same time, this common strategy is realized through distinct modes of DNA engagement, differing in interaction footprint, backbone contacts, and the magnitude and geometry of DNA deformation (Supplementary Fig. 1). However, these insights have largely come from a limited number of substrate examples, and a comprehensive, systematic framework for understanding how local DNA sequence and structure influence lesion recognition remains lacking.

Here, we systematically define how the local DNA environment guides damage recognition by BER enzymes. We developed a high-throughput on-chip approach extending the principles of the protein-binding microarray32 and saturation mismatch-binding assay33 to measure the binding of UDG, TDG, and MBD4 to uracil- and T:G-containing duplexes across thousands of sequence contexts, and to monitor catalytic activity through detection of the resulting apurinic/apyrimidinic (AP) sites (also known as abasic sites)34–36. These three enzymes were chosen for their complementary roles in repairing cytosine deamination products and preventing C → T mutation accumulation.

Conventional high-throughput binding assays often rely on polymerase- or enzymatic-based methods, which limit precise damage incorporation and the sequence contexts that can be tested. To circumvent this limitation, in our method, we directly synthesize damaged substrates on-chip using a custom hairpin design, enabling precise placement of lesions, including T:G mismatches for TDG and MBD4 and site-specific uracil lesions for UDG, within a double-stranded region in any desired sequence context (Fig. 1d).

The resulting measurements revealed that repair enzymes read information extending several bases beyond the lesion, integrating both flanking sequence and local DNA-structural features to guide recognition. Nearest-neighbor sequence preferences resembled deamination-associated mutational signatures, while broader flanking context preferences correlated with local cytosine–thymine balance in matched human genomic contexts. These observations suggest that context-dependent recognition is linked to mutational biases. Together, our results connect molecular recognition with genome-scale sequence patterns and provide a general framework for understanding how DNA repair enzymes interpret sequence- and structure-encoded signals surrounding DNA lesions.

Results

A high-throughput approach for systematic mapping of base excision repair recognition

To exhaustively probe the influence of local sequence context on DNA damage recognition, we developed BER-Map (Base Excision Repair Enzyme Recognition Mapping), a high-throughput approach that embeds lesions into thousands of custom-designed DNA sequences with single-nucleotide precision (Fig. 1d). We previously developed the saturation mismatch-binding assay (SaMBA)33 to measure the effects of mismatches on protein–DNA interactions; here, we extend this approach to include chemically modified nucleotides. In all BER-Map libraries, a lesion is fixed at a defined position while the surrounding sequence is systematically varied (e.g., 5′-NNNXNNN-3′, where N represents any nucleotide and X denotes the fixed damaged base). Multiple replicates per sequence context are included to ensure statistically robust measurements. Lesion-free sequences are always included as negative controls. All oligonucleotides were designed with a self-complementary segment that folds into a stem–loop hairpin conformation upon hybridization, ensuring that the lesion is presented in a double-stranded context (see Methods).

Protein-binding measurements were performed directly on the microarrays by incubating the relevant repair enzyme—human UNG2 (the nuclear isoform, hereafter referred to as UDG) on uracil-containing libraries, in which uracil was paired with its physiological partners guanine (U:G) or adenine (U:A), His-tagged human TDG (hereafter referred to as TDG), and His-tagged human glycosylase domain MBD4 (hereafter referred to as MBD4) on T:G libraries. Binding levels were quantified from fluorescence signals, either directly from fluorophore-labeled protein or indirectly via fluorescent antibody detection (Fig. 1d, Methods). Subsequently, we assayed enzymatic cleavage by detecting the excised product, the AP site generated upon base excision, using Aldehyde Reactive Probe (ARP) labeling34–36 (see Methods and Supplementary Fig. 2 for the AP detection workflow). This platform provides a versatile framework for systematically dissecting how sequence environments on the 5′ side, the 3′ side, or both, modulate repair enzyme binding. The BER-Map binding signal is highly reproducible between replicate experiments (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 3) and shows overall agreement with gel-based activity assays conducted for selected sequences spanning different binding levels, as well as with data reported in the existing literature18,37 (Fig. 1f).

Comprehensive mapping of flank preferences reveals that damage recognition extends beyond adjacent positions

BER-Map libraries provide a comprehensive framework for interrogating how sequence environments shape repair recognition. Using this platform, we first sought to elicit how bases flanking the lesion influence repair enzyme binding (Fig. 2). We began by systematically evaluating the effects of single-nucleotide substitutions at each position by comparing binding to otherwise identical sequences differing only at the base and position of interest. For each enzyme, we selected a mid-affinity target sequence and systematically substituted each flanking base with the three alternative nucleotides (Fig. 2a). The resulting profile revealed that substitutions at multiple sites substantially altered binding, with clear preferences for specific bases at defined positions. TDG and MBD4 showed a dominant preference for G at position +1 and a secondary preference for A (Fig. 2a, c), consistent with previous lower-throughput biochemical studies18,37–39. This pattern is also compatible with the prominence of methylated CpG sites, and of CpA sites in cellular contexts where non-CpG methylation is enriched, as major sources of deamination-derived damage40,41. Unlike these earlier studies, however, our high-throughput dataset extends well beyond nearest-neighbor effects, revealing previously uncharacterized contributions from more distal flanking positions and exposing differences between TDG and MBD4 across the broader sequence landscape. To move beyond individual examples, we leveraged the full BER-Map dataset to quantify the influence of each flanking position across all contexts. Specifically, we computed, for each position, the fraction of single-base substitutions that produced a statistically significant change in binding (statistical test and FDR correction; see figure legend and Methods). This analysis demonstrated that flanking substitutions were frequently significant (Fig. 2b). The strongest effects were observed at lesion-adjacent positions, particularly +1, but distal flanks up to ±3—which are typically not considered in current studies—also exerted a measurable and significant influence on recognition.

To capture these effects in a unified framework, we next developed an analytical model that estimates the contribution of each base at each position across the full combinatorial sequence space (Methods). The resulting coefficients were used to construct position weight matrices (PWMs), which summarize additive base preferences, under the assumption of positional independence, and can be visualized as sequence logos25,42 (Fig. 2c). These models explained most of the observed binding variation (R² = 0.78 for UDG, 0.88 for TDG, and 0.79 for MBD4; see Supplementary Fig. 4a–c for performance and Supplementary Tables 1–2 for model coefficients), indicating that additive sequence features account for much of the measured specificity.

All enzymes were sensitive to bases up to position ±3, the outermost range of our initial library. To test whether this influence extends further, we designed an additional library varying positions +4 and +5. Even these distal bases measurably affected binding (Supplementary Fig. 5), suggesting that flanking sequence elements beyond the immediate lesion can modulate repair enzyme recognition, under certain contexts.

Together, these results demonstrate that base-excision repair recognition extends well beyond the lesion itself, with distal flanks—up to five bases away—exerting measurable, systematically quantifiable effects.

Recognition specificity differs across distinct steps of the repair reaction pathway

Because glycosylase activity involves multiple steps, including lesion binding, catalytic excision, and product release, we extended our measurements beyond binding. As an initial extension, we quantified product formation on the array by detecting abasic sites generated during the reaction using the ARP workflow (Supplementary Fig. 2, Methods). Remarkably, for TDG and MBD4, binding signals correlated strongly with excised product formation detected on the array (Fig. 3a; see Supplementary Fig. 6 for specific correlations), indicating that the sequence preferences observed in binding largely track catalytic output. To further dissect this difference, we next measured binding in the absence of catalytic activity. To this end, we used uncleavable substrates containing the 2′-fluoroarabino analogue of deoxyuridine (UF), which has previously been shown to preserve glycosylase binding while strongly inhibiting N-glycosidic bond hydrolysis by UDG and TDG30,43,44 (Fig. 3b). Binding measured with this substrate is referred to hereafter as UF binding. Under these conditions, TDG binding profiles closely matched those observed with the native substrate (Fig. 3c), indicating that the sequence preferences observed in the non-fluorinated assay are largely independent of catalytic turnover, either because they are set at the initial recognition step or because substrate- and product-bound states share similar sequence dependence. In contrast, UDG binding preferences differed markedly in the absence of catalysis (Fig. 3c), suggesting that its sequence specificity in the native substrate assay is shaped in part by downstream catalytic steps, such as product binding or release. For MBD4, the strong agreement between binding and product signals suggests behavior similar to TDG, although this could not be tested directly with UF-containing probes, which failed to yield a detectable binding signal for MBD4, consistent with previous suggestions that UF may be poorly accommodated in the MBD4 active site45.

Together, these complementary readouts—binding to native substrates, detection of excised products, and, where possible, binding to uncleavable substrates—provide a multi-stage view of glycosylase activity, revealing distinct points along the reaction pathway at which sequence context can exert influence.

Context-dependent damage recognition includes non-additive and structural components

Having profiled glycosylase sequence-context preferences across distinct reaction steps, we next asked whether the positional independence assumption underlying traditional PWMs limits this characterization. Indeed, inspection of our data suggested that the contribution of individual bases cannot be fully understood in isolation from neighboring bases. Specifically, to capture multi-base contributions, we constructed a global representation of preferences across all 3-mer contexts. A circular recognition map (Fig. 4a for TDG and Supplementary Fig. 7 for UDG and MBD4) summarizes these effects, where within each half, corresponding to the 5′ or 3′ flank, the inner rings encode the first two bases of each 3-mer, while the outer ring reports the average contribution of the third base, providing a compact visualization of thousands of measurements. Patterns in the circular recognition map revealed conditional effects between positions: for example, on the 3′ side, base C is favored at position +3 when +2 is A/G/T, but this preference shifts to G when +2 is C. To systematically assess such dependencies, we extended our predictive models to include interaction terms between adjacent positions (see Methods). Incorporating these interactions improved model performance across all enzymes (UDG: R² 0.78 → 0.83; TDG: 0.87 → 0.94; MBD4: 0.78 → 0.89; Supplementary Fig. 4d, Supplementary Data 1–2), and revealed multiple context-dependent shifts in base preference that were predicted by the model (Fig. 4b) and confirmed in direct comparisons (example in Fig. 4c, more in Supplementary Fig. 8). These findings demonstrate that repair enzyme recognition is not strictly additive, but instead reflects higher-order sequence context effects.

Such dependencies often arise when proteins read the local structural properties of DNA, rather than simply the chemical signature of the individual bases alone. These observations raised the key question of whether DNA shape directs recognition of damage by repair enzymes, in an analogous manner to that demonstrated for several transcription factors46,47. Indeed, our comprehensive BER-Map data now provides the breadth and resolution needed for this systematic dissection of DNA shape contributions to repair enzyme recognition, removing the limitation of previous low-throughput studies, which focus on a small number of specific sequence contexts.

We therefore examined whether computationally calculated DNA shape features may account for the observed binding patterns in our analysis. To this end, we correlated predicted base step parameters (Tilt, Slide, Shift, Roll, Rise, and Helical Twist) from flanking sequences (excluding the lesion site, for which shape values are undefined)48, with UDG, MBD4, and TDG binding measurements from our BER-Map libraries, averaged over dinucleotide steps (see Methods). This analysis revealed that multiple structural features across distinct positions contribute to binding with either strong positive (red) or negative (blue) correlations observed (Fig. 5a). Strikingly, some shape features exhibited strong standalone correlations with binding, as indicated by the more intense colors in Fig. 5a—an unexpected result, as many structural features are normally intertwined. One such example is the strongly significant relationship of the structural parameter Slide with UDG binding at the +2/ + 3 base step relative to the damage site (Fig. 5b), which was observed for both the standard binding readout and the UF-based measurement (Supplementary Fig. 9a, b). Slide describes the lateral displacement of adjacent base pairs, a feature that can reshape groove geometry and promote local DNA deformation.

Analysis of four UDG–DNA complexes from the Protein Data Bank (PDB), selected for having the same flanking sequences surrounding the lesion, using DNAproDB49 revealed the consistent presence of marked deformation at this site, with narrowing of the minor groove width (MGW) by up to 2.6 Å compared to the +1 base, reaching levels below those defined by the B-DNA envelope (Fig. 5c, and Supplementary Fig. 9c; see Methods). Notably, an arginine residue is positioned adjacent to this site (Arg276 in the hUNG-DNA complex, PDB: 1SSP50; corresponding to residue 195 in structure coordinates).

These observations were consistent with a role for groove narrowing in damage recognition. Given prior evidence that transcription factors often exploit narrow minor grooves for recognition through arginine-mediated interactions24, we investigated whether the UDG repair enzyme shows a similar trend. Indeed, analysis of the narrowest and widest 10% MGW sequences in our library revealed stronger average binding to narrow grooves at this position (Fig. 5d; see Methods).

To further test the role of minor groove geometry, we used mismatches (non-canonical base pairs) adjacent to the damage site as controlled perturbations of local groove width (Methods). Here, mismatches served as local structural perturbations that alter DNA shape with minimal sequence change, rather than as physiologically relevant lesions. Consistent with this hypothesis, pyrimidine: pyrimidine mismatches, which are predicted to narrow the minor groove51, showed elevated UDG binding relative to all other mismatch types (Fig. 5e). This dependency, which implicates narrow-groove recognition in UDG binding, was not observed for TDG or MBD4 (Supplementary Fig. 10a).

To investigate the mechanistic basis of the observed relationship between minor groove geometry and UDG binding, we performed a 1 µs atomistic molecular dynamics simulation of UDG bound to DNA using the crystal structure of the complex (PDB: 1EMH29) as the starting model (see Methods). During the simulation, only two residues, Leu272 and Arg276, engaged the DNA minor groove in the region where we observed shape-dependent effects on binding (Supplementary Fig. 10b–d). Leu272 formed stable contacts while inserting its side chain into the DNA helix to fill the space vacated by the flipped uracil base. In contrast, the positively charged Arg276 dynamically sampled multiple positions (>5 bases) within the minor groove in this shape-sensitive region, rather than remaining confined to a single base pair (<50% occupancy per contact) (Supplementary Fig. 10e). These transient interactions involved both the uracil-containing strand and the complementary strand and primarily contacted bases that define the minor groove (Fig. 5f). This behavior aligns well with the established mechanism of narrow minor-groove recognition, in which positively charged residues are attracted to the enhanced negative electrostatic potential generated by groove narrowing24.

Together, these results show that repair enzyme specificity arises from an interplay of base readout and local DNA structure, and that high-throughput approaches such as BER-Map enable these correlations to be systematically revealed.

Comparing nucleotide preferences at the −1 and +1 positions between COSMIC signatures and our BER-Map measurements revealed position-specific agreement. Specifically, we aggregated mutational frequencies by the identity of the base at each position (−1 or +1), and compared these rankings to the corresponding base preferences derived from our binding measurements. The −1 preference of UDG matched SBS2, while the +1 preferences of TDG and MBD4 aligned with SBS1 (Fig. 6a, significant Spearman ρ for full triplet correlations = 0.76, 0.54, and 0.81 for UDG, MBD4, and TDG, respectively; UDG and TDG based on UF binding; see Supplementary Fig. 11a). Notably, independent genome-wide uracil mapping in mammalian genomes, which reports damage patterns rather than mutation outcomes and primarily reflects uracils arising from misincorporation events54, also revealed agreement with the −1 base preferences observed in UDG UF binding (Supplementary Fig. 11b). These correlations are consistent with repair enzymes contributing to the mutational landscape, though they are also compatible with an alternative interpretation in which enzymes have evolved to preferentially recognize sequence contexts that are inherently more prone to damage.

We next examined glycosylase-deficient systems to explore these possibilities further, including knockout cell lines and MBD4-deficient tumors6,55,56. In these settings, the resulting mutational patterns can help reveal how damage formation and repair interact when a major glycosylase is missing. Loss of MBD4 function is known to produce a characteristic signature (SBS96)6,7,56 which is consistent with the +1 base preferences observed in our binding data (Fig. 6b, middle; Spearman ρ = 0.68; Supplementary Fig. 11c). However, agreement at the −1 position was weaker, and analogous agreement was not observed for UDG- or TDG-deficient systems6,55 (Fig. 6b), underscoring that mutational patterns reflect the combined effects of damage formation, repair, and other cellular processes rather than the intrinsic binding preferences of any single glycosylase alone.

Together, these observations support a connection between the binding specificity of the BER glycosylase and genomic mutational patterns. However, given the complexity of in vivo systems and the indirect nature of these comparisons, future studies integrating larger mutational datasets and direct cellular perturbation approaches are needed to further establish this link.

We next asked whether our measurements relate to broader patterns of genomic sequence variation beyond cancer mutational signatures, which are largely confined to nearest-neighbor contexts. To address this, we analyzed genomic C/T ratios across sequence contexts, reasoning that long-term accumulation of C → T mutations should shape cytosine abundance in a context-dependent manner57 (Fig. 6c). Grouping extended sequence contexts by BER-Map binding strength revealed that TDG binding was positively associated with genomic C/T ratios across 12 of 16 central triplet contexts (Fig. 6d–f), a pattern unlikely to arise by chance (empirical P < 1 × 10-4; Methods). This is consistent with the possibility that lesions in efficiently recognized contexts are less prone to C → T accumulation. The four exceptions all corresponded to NXG contexts (CpG sites), consistent with the distinct mutational dynamics of CpGs and the prominent contribution of MBD4 in repair of these lesions6,7. By contrast, UDG and MBD4 did not show comparable associations (Supplementary Fig. 12). Together, these findings suggest that context-dependent TDG recognition may contribute to fine-scale variation in mutational outcome across the genome.

Discussion

We developed BER-Map to define how base excision repair glycosylases recognize lesions across diverse sequence contexts. Our results show that lesion recognition depends not only on the identity of the damaged base itself, but also on extended flanking sequence, higher-order dependencies between positions, and local DNA structural features. In this view, the sequence environment surrounding a lesion functions as an active recognition signal rather than a passive context: bases several nucleotides away measurably influence binding, and these effects were not fully explained by additive contributions of individual positions. Instead, dependencies between neighboring flanking bases indicate that recognition reflects not only the identity of individual bases, but also how their combinations shape the local substrate environment.

Beyond sequence identity, our data suggest that DNA structure provides an additional layer of recognition. Structural features, including base-step parameters, correlated with binding across all three enzymes. For UDG, this relationship was characterized most extensively, with the base-step parameter Slide at the +2/ + 3 position showing a strong association with binding, in a region that also exhibits pronounced minor groove narrowing. Further structural analysis, groove-geometry perturbation based on mismatches, and molecular dynamics simulations support a key role for minor groove width in UDG recognition.

The combination of three complementary readouts, binding to native substrates, ARP-detected product formation, and binding to uncleavable UF substrates, revealed how sequence preferences manifest at different stages of the glycosylase reaction pathway. For TDG, and likely also for MBD4, the strong agreement between binding and product readouts, together with the correspondence between native and UF binding, indicates that sequence preferences established during initial lesion recognition are largely preserved through subsequent steps. For UDG, by contrast, divergence across readouts indicates that distinct stages of the reaction pathway carry different sequence preferences, such that the overall sequence dependence measured on the array reflects contributions from multiple mechanistic steps rather than initial recognition alone. These differences are consistent with the known kinetic behavior of these enzymes: TDG and MBD4 are slow-turnover glycosylases with substantial product retention58,59, whereas UDG undergoes rapid catalytic turnover4, rendering its measured binding signal more sensitive to downstream steps. More broadly, these observations suggest that different glycosylases deploy distinct recognition strategies that are well matched to their biological functions, from broad lesion surveillance to more context-sensitive repair.

Comparison of our damage-binding measurements with deamination-associated COSMIC mutational signatures revealed position-specific agreement in nearest-neighbor preferences across all three enzymes. This concordance is consistent with two non-mutually exclusive interpretations: BER glycosylases may shape mutation patterns through sequence-dependent differences in repair efficiency, and they may also be adapted to recognize sequence contexts that are intrinsically more prone to damage. Analysis of glycosylase-deficient systems6,55 provided further, although not definitive, insight into these possibilities and supports a framework in which mutational outcome reflects a balance between context-dependent lesion formation and context-dependent repair.

This distinction is important for interpreting the genomic analyses presented here. The positive association between TDG binding and genomic C/T balance across non-CpG triplet contexts suggests that sequence-dependent repair can, in principle, contribute to fine-scale variation in mutational susceptibility. By contrast, the absence of a corresponding relationship in canonical CpG contexts emphasizes that repair specificity must be interpreted together with lesion propensity and the identity of the glycosylase that predominates in vivo. MBD4 provides an important point of comparison in this context. Recent studies support a prominent role for MBD4 in repair of canonical CpG-associated deamination products in several physiological settings6,7, whereas TDG has major additional roles in active DNA demethylation8. Our results, therefore, support the view that TDG and MBD4 share overlapping biochemical preferences in some contexts, but should not be interpreted as making equivalent contributions in vivo. In particular, the absence of a TDG-associated correlation in canonical CpG contexts is consistent with a setting in which lesion formation is especially frequent and MBD4-dependent repair has a dominant influence on mutational outcome. At the same time, TDG sequence preferences may remain more relevant in non-CpG methylation contexts or in cellular states in which such methylation is more prevalent.

Our comparison to genomic sequence patterns should therefore be viewed as suggestive rather than exhaustive. Long-term mutational outcome is shaped not only by lesion chemistry and glycosylase recognition, but also by methylation state, replication timing, chromatin environment, protein occupancy, and contributions from other repair enzymes not examined here, including SMUG112,13,60,61. BER-Map does not capture all of these determinants. Its strength instead is to provide a controlled biochemical framework in which the intrinsic sequence and structural logic of lesion recognition can be quantified directly.

In summary, BER-Map reveals that BER glycosylases interpret lesion context through a combination of extended sequence readout, positional dependencies, and local DNA structural features. By resolving these effects across thousands of lesion-containing substrates, it establishes a quantitative framework for decoding the rules of lesion recognition. This framework provides a foundation for future models linking DNA sequence and structure to repair efficiency, mutational susceptibility, and genome instability.

Methods

Protein expression and purification

Human TDG (hTDG) was expressed in E. coli BL21(DE3) from the pET28-hTDG plasmid (#70758, Addgene) and purified following a previously described protocol62, with minor modifications (see Supplementary Methods).

Fluorescein-labeled human UNG2 was produced as described previously63. Briefly, an N-terminal cysteine was introduced to enable site-specific labeling with fluorescein-5-maleimide, while endogenous cysteines were mutated to alanine to ensure selective labeling (Supplementary Methods). This construct has been shown to retain activity comparable to wild-type UNG263.

Human MBD4 (residues 427–580 + X6 His) with a C-terminal His6 tag was expressed from a pET-29 plasmid as template for PURExpress® In Vitro Protein Synthesis kit (NEB) according to the manufacturer’s recommendations.

Damaged sequences library design

Uracil and UF-containing libraries were synthesized by LC Sciences using custom phosphoramidites, whereas T:G mismatch libraries composed of canonical bases were synthesized by Agilent Technologies using standard DNA synthesis protocols. To accommodate non-complementary pairing (U:G and T:G), we designed self-annealing oligonucleotides that form intramolecular hairpin structures, enabling presentation of the lesion within a double-stranded DNA context. In this design, the lesion (uracil or thymine) is embedded within a variable flanking region positioned in the stem, allowing systematic interrogation of sequence-context effects. Libraries were constructed to cover all possible 5–7-mers flanking sequences around the lesion, with each sequence represented by multiple replicates and accompanied by non-damaged control probes. Details of library architecture and design variants are provided in Supplementary Methods. A schematic of the hairpin oligo design is shown in Fig. 1d.

BER glycosylase measurements

To create the double-stranded hairpin structure, the microarray was incubated in annealing buffer (IDT catalog no. 11-05-01-03) at 85 °C for 5 min and then gradually cooled down to room temperature. The binding step was performed as described previously32. Briefly, hybridized microarrays were blocked with 2% (w/v) nonfat milk for 1 h at room temperature. Following the blocking step and washes, the microarray was incubated with the repair enzyme of interest. Uracil libraries were incubated with 400nM or 800nM UDG for 0.5h or 1h, respectively, at room temperature in a reaction buffer containing 20mM Tris-HCl, pH 8, 1mM DTT, 1mM EDTA. T:G-containing libraries were incubated either with 1 µM TDG for 2 h at room temperature in reaction buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT, and 5% glycerol, or with in vitro-translated MBD4, added at a 1:30 (v/v) dilution of the transcription-translation reaction into the final reaction mixture, for 1 h at room temperature in reaction buffer containing 20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8, 1 mM DTT, 1 mM EDTA, 50 mM NaCl, and 5% glycerol. To detect the binding of TDG or MBD4, the relevant microarray was incubated with either Anti-His Alexa Fluor 647 (Qiagen, catalog no. 35370) or Anti-His Alexa Fluor 488 (Qiagen catalog no. 35310) for 1 h at room temperature. The antibody stock solutions, as obtained from the supplier, were diluted 1:18 in the respective reaction buffer. Fluorescein-labeled UDG was imaged directly after the binding step. The fluorescent signals were measured using a GenePix 4400 A microarray scanner and extracted by GenePix Pro analysis software.

Activity measurements using Aldehyde Reactive Probe (ARP) reagent

Following scanning of protein binding, bound protein was removed from the microarray using the stripping protocol, as described previously32. To detect the formation of abasic (AP) sites, the microarray was incubated with 2 mg/mL ARP-biotin (GERBU Biotechnik GmbH, catalog No. A305-10) in 2% (w/v) nonfat milk for 45 min at room temperature. ARP reacts specifically with the aldehyde group of the open-chain form of abasic sites via its hydroxylamine moiety, forming a stable covalent oxime linkage34–36. The slide was then washed for 3 min in 0.5% Tween-20 in PBS (1×), followed by 2 min in 0.01% Triton X-100 in PBS (1×). Subsequently, the microarray was incubated with 2 µg/mL streptavidin–Alexa Fluor 488 (Thermo Fisher, catalog No. S11223) in 2% nonfat milk for 45 min at room temperature. After incubation, the slide was washed twice for 3 min each in 0.05% Tween-20 in PBS (1×), and once for 2 min in PBS (1×). The arrays were scanned using a GenePix 4400 A microarray scanner at an excitation wavelength of 488 nm.

Microarray data preprocessing

Binding data for all probes in each experiment were normalized using MASLINER and spatial-normalization functions based on Berger et al. 32. Each sequence was represented by multiple replicate probes, allowing calculation of a reliable median fluorescence intensity across replicates, yielding one value per unique sequence. To correct for non-specific binding, we then subtracted the signal of the highest-intensity damage-free probe from all sequences, which defines the upper limit of non-specific interactions for the repair enzyme. This correction yields an intensity value of zero for non-specific binding. Because a logarithmic transformation was applied, a constant of +1 was added to all values to avoid undefined logarithms of zero. The resulting log-transformed values were used as normalized binding signals.

Statistical analysis

After preprocessing, all analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.2.2). Unless otherwise specified, sequence-level comparisons were performed using two-sided Wilcoxon tests (Mann–Whitney U test), and replicate-level comparisons using two-sided t-tests. When applicable, multiple comparison correction was applied using the method indicated in the Results section. P-values are reported either explicitly or using the specified convention in the figure legend or the following one: **** P < 0.0001; *** P < 0.001; ** P < 0.01; * P < 0.05; ns, not significant.

Positional sensitivity analysis of flanking bases

To quantify how individual flanking positions influence enzyme binding, we assessed positional sensitivity by testing whether changes at a single position significantly alter binding signals while holding all other positions constant. Sequences were grouped by shared flanking context, and binding differences between bases at the tested position were evaluated using pairwise statistical comparisons. The fraction of contexts showing significant differences at each position was used as a measure of positional sensitivity (Fig. 2b). Details of statistical testing and multiple-comparison correction are provided in Supplementary Methods.

Validation of microarray data using gel-based excision assays

DNA oligonucleotides were purchased from IDT Technologies (see Supplementary Tables 3–4 for sequences). Substrates were fluorescently labeled and annealed by heating to >95 °C followed by cooling to room temperature.

UDG assay

Uracil-containing oligonucleotides carrying a 3′ fluorescein label were purified by denaturing urea–TBE PAGE and buffer-exchanged into assay buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 8, 100 mM NaCl, 0.1 mM EDTA). Duplex substrates were formed by annealing the labeled strand with an 8% molar excess of unlabeled complementary strand. Reactions were performed at 22 °C for 3.5 min using 3 nM UDG and 0.5 µM DNA. Conditions were chosen to enable detection across substrates, targeting <50% turnover for high-activity sequences, although the broad variability in UDG activity across contexts precluded strict steady-state conditions (<10–15% turnover).

TDG assay

Self-complementary hairpin oligonucleotides labeled with 5′ fluorescein were used to generate intramolecular duplex substrates. Reactions were performed at room temperature for 20 min in buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT, and 5% glycerol, using 600 nM TDG and 80 nM DNA ( ~ 7-fold enzyme excess). These conditions approximate single-turnover kinetics, such that the observed rate constants (kobs) primarily reflect the catalytic step and provide an estimate of the maximal excision rate (kmax)18.

Reactions were quenched with 200 mM NaOH and heat to cleave abasic sites. Products and substrates were separated by denaturing urea–TBE PAGE and visualized using a fluorescence gel imaging system. Band intensities were quantified using Fiji/ImageJ, and the fraction of substrate processed was calculated as the ratio of product to total signal. For UDG, we determined the enzyme activity rate as (percent of substrate processed × µM substrate in the reaction) / reaction time. The rate was divided by the enzyme concentration used in the assay to calculate kobs values. Each assay was measured in two to four independent experiments, and resulting values were min–max normalized prior to comparison with BER-Map measurements seen in Fig. 1f.

Validation with orthogonal methods

To benchmark BER-Map measurements against independent biochemical data, we compared our binding signals to previously reported kinetic parameters for TDG and MBD4 on matched sequence contexts (Fig. 1f). For TDG, binding intensities were correlated with published single-turnover rate constants (kmax) measured for a subset of sequences18. Reported kmax values were log-transformed (natural logarithm, as in the original study), and min–max normalized prior to correlation analysis. For MBD4, published excision activities (kobs) at TpN sites37 (where T represents the damage and N is each of the bases) were used for comparison with average levels of values of the same TpN sites from BER-Map. These measurements were min–max normalized prior to correlating with the average BER-Map binding intensities aggregated at the corresponding dinucleotide level.

Data modeling by multiple linear regression

To quantify sequence determinants of enzyme binding, we modeled binding signals using multiple linear regression with one-hot encoded DNA sequence features. Sequence contexts (5–6 bases flanking the lesion) were encoded at the mononucleotide level to capture additive effects, and extended to include adjacent dinucleotide features to account for non-additive interactions. Models were first fitted to capture additive effects using linear regression, and subsequently extended with dinucleotide interaction features using regularized (Lasso) regression. Model performance was evaluated on held-out data, and the resulting coefficients were used to estimate the contribution of individual bases and pairwise interactions to binding specificity. Details of feature encoding, model selection, and validation are provided in Supplementary Methods.

Base step DNA shape features and MGW analysis

To investigate whether local DNA structural features contribute to differential repair enzyme binding, we analyzed predicted DNA shape parameters for sequence flanks surrounding the lesion. Structural features were obtained from the Deep DNAshape webserver48 for all possible 3-mer contexts and assigned to flanking sequences (positions −3 to −1 and +1 to +3) relative to the damaged base. Shape values were aligned by position and aggregated at the dinucleotide level, enabling comparison of mean structural features with corresponding mean binding signals across sequence contexts to assess structure–binding relationships.

To further examine the role of minor groove width (MGW) in UDG binding, we used an additional asymmetric library design (XNNNNN, X = U:A) and MGW predictions from the DNAshape database64. MGW values were assigned based on pentamer contexts 3′ to the lesion, and integrated with binding measurements for corresponding flanking sequences. To perturb MGW, we introduced defined mismatches at the +1 position (3′) relative to the UF:G damage site, while keeping all other bases fixed, following the same hairpin library design principles. Binding signals for each mismatch were then associated with corresponding MGW values derived from Rossetti et al. 51. Details of feature assignment, data aggregation, and analysis are provided in Supplementary Methods.

Structural analysis of UDG–DNA complex crystal structures

To assess local DNA distortions around the lesion site, we analyzed four available crystal structures of uracil-bound UDG–DNA complexes: 1EMH, 1EMJ29, 1SSP, and 2SSP50. These structures were selected based on their identical flanking sequence surrounding the lesion site, allowing for a controlled comparison of structural features. DNA structural parameters, MGW and Slide, were extracted using the DNAproDB pipeline49, which integrates output from CURVES + . To visualize the distortions, DNA sequences from the structures were aligned according to the uracil position.

Molecular dynamics simulation of UDG–DNA complex

The.pdb file 1EMH was entered into CHARMM-GUI’s solution builder and placed in a cubic box before hydrating with TIP3P water molecules65,66. The length of the system box was 87 Å, and KCl was added to neutralize the system and achieve a concentration of 150 mM. The system contained approximately 62,000 atoms. The CHARMM36m force field was used67, and simulations were run with NAMD3.0b768. The simulation step size was 2 fs, the pH was 7.4, and the temperature was 293 K. The simulation was run for 1 µs, and we analyzed 10,000 frames. Interaction of residues with DNA bases was quantified by identifying frames where they were within 4 Å of each other. Simulations were analyzed with VMD69.

Genomic analysis (C/T ratio analysis)

To calculate the C/T ratio per context in the genome, we used the Homo sapiens genome assembly GRCh38.p14 (hg38) from UCSC, using sequences extracted via the BSgenome.Hsapiens.UCSC.hg38 package from Bioconductor70. We scanned the genome (forward strand) for all 6-mer sequences with either a cytosine (C) or thymine (T) at the third position (i.e., NNCNNNN and NNTNNNN, respectively). The C/T ratio for each sequence context was calculated as the ratio of counts of matched 6-mers: NNCNNNN counts/NNTNNNN counts = C/T ratio per context. To account for local sequence effects, we grouped our ratio results into triplets (e.g., CXA, GXC). For each triplet, unique 6-mers were ordered by binding signal levels and divided into 16 equally sized bins. Then, for each bin, the median C/T ratio and mean normalized binding signal were calculated. Pearson correlation tests were performed for each triplet on the binned data and corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. More details are provided in Supplementary Methods.

To test whether the observed C/T correlations with TDG binding levels could arise by chance, we generated a null distribution using simulated genomes. Random genomes of 10 Mb in length were generated by sampling nucleotides according to their frequencies in the human hg38 reference genome, thereby matching its overall composition. For each of 10,000 simulations, the C/T ratio was calculated per sequence context and correlated with the measured TDG binding levels across triplets following the same procedure described above. We then compared the number of significant triplet-level correlations in the real genome (12) to this null distribution. The empirical P-value was calculated as the proportion of simulations with as many or more significant triplets than observed.
