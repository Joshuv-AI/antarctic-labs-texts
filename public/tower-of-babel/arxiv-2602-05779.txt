How Controlling the Variance can Improve Training Stability of Sparsely Activated DNNs and CNNs
Emily Dent, Jared Tanner

Abstract
The intermediate layers of deep networks can be characterised as a Gaussian process, in particular the Edge-of-Chaos (EoC) initialisation strategy prescribes the limiting covariance matrix of the Gaussian process. Here we show that the under-utilised chosen variance of the Gaussian process is important in the training of deep networks with sparsity inducing activation, such as a shifted and clipped ReLU, CReLU τ , m ​ ( x ) = min ⁡ ( max ⁡ ( x − τ , 0 ) , m ) \text{CReLU}_{\tau,m}(x)=\min(\max(x-\tau,0),m) . Specifically, initialisations leading to larger fixed Gaussian process variances, allow for improved expressivity with activation sparsity as large as 90% in DNNs and CNNs, and generally improve the stability of the training process. Enabling full, or near full, accuracy at such high levels of sparsity in the hidden layers suggests a promising mechanism to reduce the energy consumption of machine learning models involving fully connected layers.

Training of DNNs and CNNs with activations that induce high degrees of sparsity, e.g. 85% and greater, raises some novel initialisation challenges and nonlinear activation designs

The phenomena of training instability occurs for other similar activations which are zero around the origin. Difficulties in training occur for the sparsifying activation functions as a result of instability in the empirical
\tilde{q}^{(\ell)}
. The EoC theory typically advocates that
\mathbb{E}\left[{h_{i}^{(\ell)}}^{2}\right]
converges independently to a non-zero limit
q^{*}

q^{*}

Experiments are shown whereby increasing

1.1
Related Work

There is a range of related work which mathematically characterises the behaviour of the hidden layers and proposes methods to improve stability and training of large neural networks. As mentioned previously
28
pioneered the results extended here. The remainder of this section reviews other methods to further stabilize information propagation on the Edge-of-Chaos.

Traditional EoC theory presumes a rapid and stable convergence of
\mathbb{E}\left[{h_{i}^{(\ell)}}^{2}\right]
to
q^{*}
in depth and then focuses on higher order phenomena. In particular, through concentration of
all
singular values of the input-output Jacobian towards 1, see
24
;
25
;
32
. Specifically, these works analyse the variance of the spectrum of the input-output Jacobian
JJ^{T}
, denoted as
\sigma_{JJ^{T}}
. To better concentrate the spectrum of
JJ^{T}
they propose methods so that
\sigma_{JJ^{T}}\rightarrow 0
. The first approach was in
25
where they propose taking
q^{*}\rightarrow 0
to cause
\sigma_{JJ^{T}}\rightarrow 0
. In
16
;
29
;
19
they also advocate taking
q^{*}\rightarrow 0
, for certain activation functions. Another methodology proposed in
19
;
16
shows that information propagation is improved through a linearized region around the origin of the activation function. These mentioned works are to the best of our knowledge the only other cases to consider varying
q^{*}
, all of which only consider the limit of a decreasing, or arbitrarily chosen
q^{*}
. A further in depth analysis of the EoC and additional finite-width corrections can be found in
34
;
29
;
9
.

Extensions to the EoC initialisation have been explored for other architectures including ResNets
35
and Recurrent Neural Networks
33
. The perhaps natural extension of this work to ResNets is not explored for the precise reason that the skip connections would negate the induced sparsity in the activation function, and therefore the proposed efficiency improvements. Transformer architectures are also not considered here, as they are a separate line of analysis with additional complexity.

There are a range of motivations for the particular case of activation sparsity, one such property is the immediate computational reducibility for entire rows in the matrix multiplication. An incentivizing example for this efficiency improvement comes from the identification of naturally occurring sparsity leading to brain efficiency improvements in neuroscience,
1
. Separately, sparsity is also shown to improve the ability of models to generalize and learn
8
;
20
;
2
; a phenomenon colloquially known as the
lottery ticket
. Due to this intuition, some recent work considers activation sparsity in attention, see
38
;
13
;
36
; in
36
they find competitive activation sparsity up to 92%, however these works are largely empirical in nature.

There is also some parallel literature which is related, but not the main focus of this work. One of these is the study of high variance weight initialisation leading to faster training, as a
lazy training
method, this was first mentioned in
5
, and has further been explored in
6
;
7
; in this work, as a result of increasing
q^{*}
on the EoC, the initial variance of the weights is increased. Another linked line of work is gradient clipping, as the exemplar activation function in this work,
\text{CReLU}_{\tau,m}
, could be interpreted as a method for naturally encouraging gradient clipping; however it should be noted that
\text{CReLU}_{\tau,m}

37
;
14
;
15
;
4

Here, we focus on the mathematical motivations behind the parameter choices, and show that increasing
q^{*}
is a technique to achieve stable and highly sparsifying activation functions - experiments then show proof of concept for this work.

\chi^{\prime}_{1,\text{CReLU}_{\tau,m}}(q^{*})
can be reduced at the fixed point
q^{*}
by increasing this fixed point, allowing the use of
q^{*}
as a parameter to reduce the sensitivity to perturbations of
\chi_{1,\text{CReLU}_{\tau,m}}
where
\chi^{\prime}_{1,\text{CReLU}_{\tau,m}}(q^{*})>0
.

2.2
Summary of Findings

Increasing
q^{*}
improves the symmetry of the variance map around the fixed point, the fluctuation of the next-leading-order term, and further reduces the sensitivity of
\chi^{\prime}_{1,\phi}(q^{*})
for our chosen
\phi
. In fact, Figures
4
,
5
and
6
show that even small increases in
q^{*}
to
2
or
3
allow for great improvements across all three considered control metrics. To this endeavor, the largely ignored parameter
q^{*}
is shown to be a valuable tool for activations where
V^{\prime}(q^{*})
approaches 1, especially where dynamical isometry is ruled out by not being approximately linear around the origin.

Particularly for our activation functions, there is an important relationship that appears between
V^{\prime}_{\phi}(q)
and
\chi_{1,\phi}(q)
, found in
28
,

\displaystyle\chi_{1,\text{CReLU}{\tau,m}}(q)

\displaystyle=V^{\prime}_{\text{CReLU}_{\tau,m}}(q)+\frac{\sigma_{w}^{2}m}{\sqrt{2\pi q}}\text{exp}\left(-\frac{(\tau+m)^{2}}{{2q}}\right).

(25)

Equation (
25
) shows the direct relationship between
\chi_{1,\phi}(q)
and
V^{\prime}_{\phi}(q^{*})
for the exemplar choice of
\phi=\text{CReLU}{\tau,m}
.

Then see the relationship between
\chi^{\prime}_{1,\phi}(q)
and
V^{\prime\prime}_{\phi}(q)
,

\displaystyle\chi^{\prime}_{1,\text{CReLU}{\tau,m}}(q^{*})

\displaystyle=V^{\prime\prime}_{\text{CReLU}_{\tau,m}}(q^{*})+\frac{\sigma_{w}^{2}m}{\sqrt{2\pi q^{*}}}\text{exp}\left(-\frac{(\tau+m)^{2}}{{2q^{*}}}\right)\left(-\frac{1}{2q^{*}}+\frac{(\tau+m)^{2}}{{2{q^{*}}^{2}}}\right).

(26)

This relationship shows that by increasing
q^{*}
, both
V^{\prime\prime}_{\text{CReLU}_{\tau,m}}(q^{*})
and
\frac{d\chi_{1,\text{CReLU}_{\tau,m}}}{dq}\left(q^{*}\right)
can be reduced,
Figure

6
. Assuming
V^{\prime\prime}_{\phi}(q^{*})>0
is decreasing, this secondary behaviour of the sensitivity reduction of
\chi_{1,\phi}
should similarly apply to any activation function where you can write
\chi^{\prime}_{1,\phi}(q)=V^{\prime\prime}_{\phi}(q)+f(q)
, given
f(q)>0
is decreasing on
q>0
.

4
Conclusion and Further Extensions

Previous Edge-of-Chaos analysis has primarily focused on how the correlation of nearby points evolves through the layers of a deep neural network,
26
. For unitary weight matrices and activations that are approximately linear around the origin these correlation dynamics can be improved by having
q^{(\ell)}
decreasing towards zero; the dynamic isometry property
16
;
29
;
19
. Dynamic isometry isn’t possible for activations that are zero around the origin and new issues of training instability arise
27
. This motivates revisiting the role of the Gaussian process variance and showing for the first time that this parameter is a valuable tool for improving the training dynamics of DNNs and CNNs with such sparsifying nonlinear activations. Faster training, less hyper-parameter sensitivity, and greater overall accuracy are obtained for even greater 90% hidden layer sparsities.

This line of research has multiple natural extensions, the most obvious of which are to the more complex architectures such as transformers and state-space models. Both cases include MLP type layers which could similarly benefit from the computational gains suggested by the hidden layer sparsity. Moreover, experiments demonstrating the interplay of our approach with pruned, quantized, and low-rank weight matrices is needed. One can also expect that while
\text{CReLU}_{\tau,m}
is a particular simple example, that other sparsity inducing activation designs might show further improvements. Lastly, software engineering to demonstrate real world time and energy reductions are needed to encourage the deployment of the theory developed here.

Appendix A
Further on
\chi_{1}

To further understand the importance of
\chi_{1}
consider, as in
32
, the input-output Jacobian,

J=\frac{\partial x^{(L)}}{\partial x^{(0)}}=\prod_{l=1}^{L}D^{l}W^{l}.

(27)

The Jacobian is the product of the linear operators used in computing each layer’s error vector in backpropagation, and therefore is closely related to training.

Assuming a normalized input so that
q^{(1)}=q^{*}
, the moments of
D^{(\ell)}
are given by,

\mu_{k}=\int\left(\phi^{\prime}\left(\sqrt{q^{*}}z\right)\right)^{2k}\gamma(dz).

(28)

(Note:
\sigma_{w}^{2}\mu_{1}=\chi_{1}
)
The quantities
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
are then related to the first two moments of the spectrum of
JJ^{T}
,
m_{1},m_{2}
, by the following,

\displaystyle m_{1}

\displaystyle=\left(\sigma_{w}^{2}\mu_{1}\right)^{L},

(29)

\displaystyle m_{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\sigma_{w}^{2}\mu_{1}\right)^{2L}L\left(\frac{\mu_{2}}{\mu_{1}^{2}}+\frac{1}{L}-1-s_{1}\right),

(30)

where
s_{1}
is the first moment of
WW^{T}

S-
transformed, and so we have identified
m_{1}=(\chi_{1})^{L}
. For our case of initialisation,
W
is Gaussian with mean
0
and variance
\sigma_{w}^{2}/N_{\ell-1}
,
s_{1}=-1
.

Since
m_{1}=\left(\chi_{1}\right)^{L}
, we can interpret
\chi_{1}
as the growth of the error vector per layer.
The motivation of concentrating
\chi_{1}
around
1
is therefore to ensure for large
L
the growth of the error vectors doesn’t explode or collapse in expectation.

To further stabilize our network, we can concentrate the variance of
JJ^{T}
,

\sigma_{JJ^{T}}=m_{2}-m_{1}^{2}=L\left(\frac{\mu_{2}}{\mu_{1}^{2}}-1-s_{1}\right).

(31)

As the variance
\sigma_{JJ^{T}}^{2}
increases with depth
L
this may become problematic for large depth. Methods have been proposed to control
\sigma_{JJ^{T}}^{2}
, for example in
25
they proposed allowing
q^{*}\rightarrow 0
at a rate to ensure
\sigma_{JJ^{T}}
converges to a nonzero constant in the large depth limit.
Further studies show in the case of
\phi=\text{ReLU}
, there is only one EoC point and so
\sigma_{JJ^{T}}
is fixed with no way to improve it
25
. If instead
\phi\left(\cdot\right)
is approximately linear around the origin, with orthonormal activations then
s_{1}=0
, and it is possible to have
\frac{\mu_{2}}{\mu_{1}^{2}}\geq 1
approach
1
in order to mitigate the growth of
\sigma_{JJ^{T}}

19
, such as
\frac{\mu_{2}}{\mu_{1}^{2}}\sim L^{-1}

16
.

In our case, for
\phi\left(\cdot\right)
where
\phi\left(z\right)=0
with
|z|\leq\tau
, no such
L
independence is possible; however
\frac{\mu_{2}}{\mu_{1}^{2}}-1-s_{1}
remains a function of
m,\tau,q^{*}
.

Appendix B
Derivation of Further Derivatives for
\text{CReLU}_{\tau,m}
and
\text{CST}_{\tau,m}

We know,

\displaystyle V^{\prime}_{\text{CReLU}_{\tau,m}}(q)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{w}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}\text{erf}\left(\frac{\tau+m}{\sqrt{2q}}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\text{erf}\left(\frac{\tau}{\sqrt{2q}}\right)-\frac{m}{\sqrt{2\pi q}}\text{exp}\left(-\frac{(\tau+m)^{2}}{2q}\right)\right)

(32)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{w}^{2}\left(a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}\right),

(33)

which we write as,

\displaystyle V^{\prime}_{\text{CReLU}_{\tau,m}}(q)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{w}^{2}\left(a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}\right),

(34)

defining,

\displaystyle a_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\text{erf}\left(\frac{\tau+m}{\sqrt{2q}}\right),

(35)

\displaystyle a_{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\text{erf}\left(\frac{\tau}{\sqrt{2q}}\right),

(36)

\displaystyle a_{3}

\displaystyle=-\frac{m}{\sqrt{2\pi q}}\text{exp}\left(-\frac{(\tau+m)^{2}}{2q}\right).

(37)

It follows that,

\displaystyle\frac{da_{1}}{dq}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\tau+m}{\sqrt{8\pi q^{3}}}e^{-\left(\frac{\tau+m}{\sqrt{2q}}\right)^{2}},

(38)

\displaystyle\frac{da_{2}}{dq}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tau}{\sqrt{8\pi q^{3}}}e^{-\left(\frac{\tau}{\sqrt{2q}}\right)^{2}},

(39)

\displaystyle\frac{da_{3}}{dq}

\displaystyle=\frac{m}{\sqrt{8\pi q^{3}}}\left(1-\frac{(\tau+m)^{2}}{q}\right)\text{exp}\left(-\frac{(\tau+m)^{2}}{2q}\right).

(40)

Hence,

\displaystyle V^{\prime\prime}_{\text{CReLU}_{\tau,m}}(q)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{w}^{2}}{\sqrt{8\pi q^{3}}}\left({\tau}e^{-\left(\frac{\tau}{\sqrt{2q}}\right)^{2}}-\left(\tau+m\right)e^{-\left(\frac{\tau+m}{\sqrt{2q}}\right)^{2}}\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 128.0374pt\left.+{m}\left(1-\frac{(\tau+m)^{2}}{q}\right)\text{exp}\left(-\frac{(\tau+m)^{2}}{2q}\right)\right).

(41)

Now since
V^{\prime}_{\text{CST}_{\tau,m}}(q)=2V^{\prime}_{\text{CReLU}_{\tau,m}}(q)
,
V^{\prime\prime}_{\text{CST}_{\tau,m}}(q)=2V^{\prime\prime}_{\text{CReLU}_{\tau,m}}(q)
.

Further, we can calculate
\chi^{\prime}_{1,\text{CReLU}_{\tau,m}}
and
\chi^{\prime}_{1,\text{CST}_{\tau,m}}
since,
\chi_{1,\text{CReLU}_{\tau,m}}=\sigma_{w}^{2}\left(a_{1}+a_{2}\right)
, we can immediately see,

\displaystyle\chi^{\prime}_{1,\text{CReLU}_{\tau,m}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{w}^{2}}{\sqrt{8\pi q^{3}}}\left({\tau}{}e^{-\left(\frac{\tau}{\sqrt{2q}}\right)^{2}}-(\tau+m)e^{-\left(\frac{\tau+m}{\sqrt{2q}}\right)^{2}}\right).

(42)

Again
\chi^{\prime}_{1,\text{CST}_{\tau,m}}=2\chi^{\prime}_{1,\text{CReLU}_{\tau,m}}
, so

\displaystyle\chi_{1,\text{CST}{\tau,m}}(q)

\displaystyle=V^{\prime}_{\text{CST}_{\tau,m}}(q)+\frac{\sqrt{2}\sigma_{w}^{2}m}{\sqrt{\pi q}}\text{exp}\left(-\frac{(\tau+m)^{2}}{{2q}}\right).

(43)

Appendix C
Variance Map Analysis for
\text{CST}_{\tau,m}

It was shown in
28
that

\displaystyle V^{\prime}_{\text{CST}_{\tau,m}}(q)=2V^{\prime}_{\text{CReLU}_{\tau,m}}(q),

Appendix D
Finite Dimensional Correction

To show the precise appearance of the second derivative in the finite dimensional correction we can look to the work of Chapters 4 and 5,
29
, they find the following equivalent recursions,

\displaystyle q^{(\ell+1)}

\displaystyle=V\left(q^{(\ell)}\right)

(46)

\displaystyle r^{(\ell+1)}

\displaystyle=\left(V^{\prime}\left(q^{(\ell)}\right)\right)^{2}r^{(\ell)}+\sigma_{w}^{4}\left(\left\langle\phi^{4}(z)\right\rangle_{q^{(\ell)}}-\left\langle\phi^{2}(z)\right\rangle^{2}_{q^{(\ell)}}\right)

(47)

\displaystyle\tilde{q}^{\{1\}(\ell+1)}

\displaystyle=V^{\prime}\left(q^{(\ell)}\right)\tilde{q}^{\{1\}(\ell)}+\frac{1}{2}V^{\prime\prime}\left(q^{(\ell)}\right)r^{(\ell)}.

(48)

We know from
29
that the first-layer pre-activation distribution is exactly Gaussian, hence
\tilde{q}^{\{1\}(1)}=0
and
r^{(1)}=0
. Since we initialise at the fixed point
q^{(1)}=q^{*}
, such that
q^{*}=V\left(q^{*}\right)
, immediately
q^{(\ell)}=q^{*}
for
l\geq 1
.

The following
Lemma

D.1
,
Lemma

D.2
will prove useful.

Lemma D.1
.

Where the following recursive relation holds,

r^{(\ell+1)}=V^{\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)^{2}r^{(\ell)}+\sigma_{w}^{4}\left(\left\langle\phi^{4}(z)\right\rangle_{q^{*}}-\left\langle\phi^{2}(z)\right\rangle^{2}_{q^{*}}\right),

(49)

assuming
V^{\prime}(q^{*})\neq 1
and
r^{(1)}=0
,

r^{(\ell)}=\sigma_{w}^{4}\left(\left\langle\phi^{4}(z)\right\rangle_{q^{*}}-\left\langle\phi^{2}(z)\right\rangle^{2}_{q^{*}}\right)\frac{1-V^{\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)^{2(\ell-1)}}{1-V^{\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)^{2}},

(50)

for
l\geq 2
.

Proof.

We have a recursive formula for
r^{(\ell)}
of the form
r^{(\ell+1)}=ar^{(\ell)}+b
, where
a=V^{\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)^{2}
and
b=\sigma_{w}^{4}\left(\left\langle\phi^{4}(z)\right\rangle_{q^{*}}-\left\langle\phi^{2}(z)\right\rangle^{2}_{q^{*}}\right)
, hence for
l\geq 2
,

\displaystyle r^{(\ell)}=\sigma_{w}^{4}\left(\left\langle\phi^{4}(z)\right\rangle_{q^{*}}-\left\langle\phi^{2}(z)\right\rangle^{2}_{q^{*}}\right)\sum_{0\leq i\leq l-2}V^{\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)^{2i}.

(51)

Since
V^{\prime}(q^{*})\neq 1
, we can simply apply the geometric series formula to find the result,

r^{(\ell)}=\sigma_{w}^{4}\left(\left\langle\phi^{4}(z)\right\rangle_{q^{*}}-\left\langle\phi^{2}(z)\right\rangle^{2}_{q^{*}}\right)\frac{1-V^{\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)^{2(\ell-1)}}{1-V^{\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)^{2}}.

(52)

∎

Lemma D.2
.

Where the following recursive relationship holds,

\tilde{q}^{\{1\}(\ell+1)}=V^{\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)\tilde{q}^{\{1\}(\ell)}+\frac{1}{2}V^{\prime\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)r^{(\ell)},

(53)

and we initialise such that
\tilde{q}^{\{1\}(1)}=0
and
r^{(1)}=0
, then

\tilde{q}^{\{1\}(\ell)}=\frac{1}{2}V^{\prime\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)\sum_{0\leq i\leq\ell-3}V^{\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)^{i}r^{(\ell-i-1)},

(54)

for
\ell\geq 3
.

Proof.

We prove this result by induction. To begin, we identify
\tilde{q}^{\{1\}(2)}=V^{\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)\tilde{q}^{\{1\}(1)}+\frac{1}{2}V^{\prime\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)r^{(1)}=0
. Now for
\ell=3
,

\displaystyle\tilde{q}^{\{1\}(3)}

\displaystyle=V^{\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)\tilde{q}^{\{1\}(2)}+\frac{1}{2}V^{\prime\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)r^{(2)}=\frac{1}{2}V^{\prime\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)r^{(2)}.

(55)

Let us then assume the result holds for
\ell=n
so,

\displaystyle\tilde{q}^{\{1\}(n)}=\frac{1}{2}V^{\prime\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)\sum_{0\leq i\leq n-3}V^{\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)^{i}r^{(n-i-1)}.

(56)

Hence for
l=n+1
,

\displaystyle\tilde{q}^{\{1\}(n+1)}

\displaystyle=V^{\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)\tilde{q}^{\{1\}(n)}+\frac{1}{2}V^{\prime\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)r^{(n)}

(57)

\displaystyle=V^{\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)\frac{1}{2}V^{\prime\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)\sum_{0\leq i\leq n-3}V^{\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)^{i}r^{(n-i-1)}+\frac{1}{2}V^{\prime\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)r^{(n)}

(58)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}V^{\prime\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)\sum_{0\leq i\leq n-2}V^{\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)^{i}r^{(n-i)},

(59)

as required. This completes our proof.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

2.1
.

We can substitute our equation for
r^{(\ell)}
from
Lemma

D.1
into our result for
\tilde{q}^{\{1\}(\ell)}
found in

D.2
. Since
V^{\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)\neq 1
, for

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{w}^{4}}{2}V^{\prime\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)\left(\left\langle\phi^{4}(z)\right\rangle_{q^{*}}-\left\langle\phi^{2}(z)\right\rangle^{2}_{q^{*}}\right)\sum_{0\leq i\leq\ell-3}V^{\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)^{i}\frac{1-V^{\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)^{2(\ell-i-1)}}{1-V^{\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)^{2}}

(60)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{w}^{4}}{2}\frac{V^{\prime\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)}{1-V^{\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)^{2}}\left(\left\langle\phi^{4}(z)\right\rangle_{q^{*}}-\left\langle\phi^{2}(z)\right\rangle^{2}_{q^{*}}\right)\sum_{0\leq i\leq\ell-3}V^{\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)^{i}-V^{\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)^{2\ell-i-2}

(61)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{w}^{4}}{2}\frac{V^{\prime\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)}{1-V^{\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)^{2}}\left(\left\langle\phi^{4}(z)\right\rangle_{q^{*}}-\left\langle\phi^{2}(z)\right\rangle^{2}_{q^{*}}\right)\times

\displaystyle\hskip 142.26378pt\left[\frac{1-V^{\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)^{\ell-2}}{1-V^{\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)}-V^{\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)^{2\ell-2}\frac{1-V^{\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)^{2-\ell}}{1-V^{\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)^{-1}}\right]

(62)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{w}^{4}}{2}\frac{V^{\prime\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)}{1-V^{\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)^{2}}\left(\left\langle\phi^{4}(z)\right\rangle_{q^{*}}-\left\langle\phi^{2}(z)\right\rangle^{2}_{q^{*}}\right)\times

\displaystyle\hskip 142.26378pt\left[\frac{1-V^{\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)^{\ell-2}+V^{\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)^{2\ell-1}-V^{\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)^{\ell+1}}{1-V^{\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)}\right]

(63)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{w}^{4}}{2}\frac{V^{\prime\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)}{1-V^{\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)^{2}}\left(\left\langle\phi^{4}(z)\right\rangle_{q^{*}}-\left\langle\phi^{2}(z)\right\rangle^{2}_{q^{*}}\right)\times

\displaystyle\hskip 142.26378pt\left[\frac{\left(1-V^{\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)^{\ell-2}\right)\left(1-V^{\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)^{\ell+1}\right)}{1-V^{\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)}\right].

(64)

Further, considering our assumption

and taking the absolute value, we find,

\displaystyle\left|\tilde{q}^{\{1\}(\ell)}\right|\leq\frac{\sigma_{w}^{4}}{2}\frac{\left|V^{\prime\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)\right|}{\left(1-V^{\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)\right)^{2}\left(1+V^{\prime}\left(q^{*}\right)\right)}\left|\left\langle\phi^{4}(z)\right\rangle_{q^{*}}-\left\langle\phi^{2}(z)\right\rangle^{2}_{q^{*}}\right|,

(65)

for
\ell\geq 3
. This completes our proof.
