Tranexamic acid in proximal femoral megaprosthetic reconstruction for metastatic bone disease
Authors: Eren Yalçin, Hüseyin Sünbül, Mehmet Çağlar Torunlar, Yüksel Topkaya, Ozgur Uslu, Ahmet Kaya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This retrospective comparative study evaluated the effect of tranexamic acid (TXA) on perioperative transfusion requirements and clinically documented thromboembolic events in patients undergoing proximal femoral tumor resection and megaprosthetic reconstruction for metastatic bone disease. A total of 32 patients who underwent proximal femoral tumor resection and megaprosthetic reconstruction between 2019 and 2026 were included. The patients were divided according to perioperative TXA administration (TXA group, n = 15; control group, n = 17). TXA was administered intravenously at 10 to 15 mg/kg prior to skin incision. Primary outcomes included transfusion requirements and the number of transfused erythrocyte units. The secondary outcomes included perioperative hemoglobin change, length of hospital stay, postoperative complications, and mortality. Perioperative blood transfusion was required in 60.0% and 82.4% of the patients in the TXA and control groups, respectively (P = .243). The transfusion requirement was numerically lower in the TXA group, corresponding to an absolute difference of 22.4 percentage points (relative risk, 0.73). The mean number of transfused units was similar between groups (1.27 ± 1.22 vs 1.35 ± 0.99, P = .829). Hospital stay was numerically shorter in the TXA group (9.53 ± 4.73 vs 13.94 ± 10.52 days, P = .202). No clinically documented symptomatic thromboembolic events were observed in either group of patients. In this small retrospective cohort, TXA use was associated with a numerically lower perioperative transfusion requirement, although the difference was not statistically significant. These findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in larger, adequately powered prospective studies.

1. Introduction

The proximal femur is a common site of both primary bone sarcomas and metastatic bone disease. The management of bone tumors may involve diagnostic and treatment-planning challenges, particularly when radiological and pathological findings overlap in selected lesions.[1] Limb-salvage surgery with wide tumor resection followed by megaprosthetic reconstruction has become the standard treatment, offering improved functional outcomes and quality of life compared with amputation.[2] However, these procedures are technically demanding and associated with substantial perioperative blood loss due to extensive soft tissue dissection, large bone resections, and intramedullary canal preparation.[3] Reported intraoperative blood loss ranges from 500 to 2000 mL, frequently necessitating perioperative blood transfusion.[3,4]

Although often unavoidable, perioperative blood transfusion is associated with several adverse outcomes, including an increased risk of infection, immunosuppression, transfusion-related complications, prolonged hospitalization, and higher healthcare costs.[5] In oncologic patients, who often have limited physiological reserves, perioperative anemia and transfusion requirements may further compromise recovery, extend hospital stay, and negatively impact short-term outcomes, including mortality.[5]

Tranexamic acid (TXA), a synthetic lysine analogue, exerts its antifibrinolytic effect by inhibiting the conversion of plasminogen to plasmin.[6] Its efficacy in reducing perioperative blood loss and transfusion requirements has been well established in elective orthopedic procedures such as total hip and knee arthroplasty, without a significant increase in thromboembolic complications.[7,8] However, the use of TXA in orthopedic oncology remains controversial. Patients with malignancy are known to have a hypercoagulable state, raising concerns about the potentially increased risk of venous thromboembolism in this population.[9]

Despite the widespread adoption of TXA in orthopedic practice, evidence regarding its safety and effectiveness in patients undergoing proximal femoral tumor resection with megaprosthetic reconstruction remains scarce. Existing studies have predominantly focused on perioperative blood loss and transfusion rates, whereas data on hemoglobin dynamics, length of hospital stay, and survival outcomes are limited. Importantly, the effect of TXA on clinically relevant outcomes has not been comprehensively evaluated in this high-risk oncological population.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the impact of TXA on erythrocyte transfusion requirements, perioperative hemoglobin decline, length of hospital stay, postoperative complications, including clinically documented thromboembolic events and mortality in patients undergoing proximal femoral tumor resection and megaprosthetic reconstruction.

2. Materials and Methods

This retrospective observational study was conducted at a single tertiary referral center. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (Non-Interventional Research Ethics Committee; approval No: 2026/01-09; date: 02/02/2026). This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Owing to the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee.

Patients who underwent proximal femoral tumor resection followed by megaprosthetic reconstruction between January 2019 and January 2026 were reviewed retrospectively. The inclusion criteria were as follows: patients aged ≥ 18 years, a diagnosis of metastatic bone disease involving the proximal femur, and treatment with megaprosthetic reconstruction. The exclusion criteria were as follows: incomplete medical records; missing perioperative hemoglobin or transfusion data; revision procedures; patients with known coagulopathy or ongoing anticoagulant therapy that could not be discontinued preoperatively; and intraoperative mortality.

The study size was determined by the number of eligible patients who underwent proximal femoral tumor resection and megaprosthetic reconstruction for metastatic bone disease during the study period. No formal sample size calculation was performed because of the retrospective design and the rarity of the condition. The patients were divided into 2 groups according to perioperative administration of TXA. Before 2022, TXA was not routinely administered in these patients because of concerns regarding its potential thromboembolic risk in the setting of malignancy, and patients treated during this period constituted the non-TXA (control) group (n = 17). From 2022 onward, institutional clinical practice evolved toward the perioperative use of TXA in these procedures, and patients treated thereafter constituted the TXA group (n = 15). No major documented changes in surgical techniques or perioperative management protocols were identified during the study period.

In the TXA group, TXA was administered intravenously at a dose of 10 to 15 mg/kg prior to skin incision. Additional doses were not routinely administered. All patients received standard perioperative care, including thromboprophylaxis, according to institutional guidelines. Thromboprophylaxis consisted of low-molecular-weight heparin administered postoperatively in accordance with institutional protocols.

All data were obtained from the institutional electronic medical records, and patient identifiers were removed before analysis to ensure anonymization. The demographic variables included age and sex. Clinical variables included the primary malignancy diagnosis. All patients included in the final analysis had metastatic bone disease involving the proximal femur. To reduce selection bias, all consecutive eligible patients treated during the study period were included. Patients with missing key perioperative data were excluded from the analysis. No imputation was performed because of the retrospective design and small sample size. Potential confounding was explored using multivariable regression analyses adjusted for age, sex, and preoperative hemoglobin level. Given the small sample size and limited number of outcome events, these models were considered exploratory and were not intended to provide definitive estimates of independent treatment effects.

Hematological parameters included preoperative hemoglobin levels and postoperative hemoglobin values at 24, 48, and 72 h, as well as the hemoglobin level at discharge. Postoperative hemoglobin change was calculated as the preoperative minus the postoperative hemoglobin level; negative values reflected post-transfusion hemoglobin levels exceeding baseline. Perioperative hemoglobin decline was defined as the difference between the preoperative hemoglobin level and the lowest recorded postoperative hemoglobin level during hospitalization. Blood transfusion data included whether erythrocyte transfusion was administered (yes/no) and total number of transfused units per patient. The indication for transfusion was based on institutional clinical practice (generally a hemoglobin level <8 g/dL or the presence of anemia symptoms). Additional variables included length of hospital stay (defined as the number of days from surgery to discharge), postoperative complications, administration of TXA, mortality, and survival time. Survival time was calculated as the duration from the date of surgery to the date of death or last follow-up. Postoperative complications included wound-related complications and thromboembolic events such as deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE). Thromboembolic events were recorded if clinically documented during hospitalization or follow-up; routine screening for asymptomatic thrombosis was not performed.

The primary outcomes were perioperative erythrocyte transfusion requirement and the total number of transfused erythrocyte units. Secondary outcomes included perioperative hemoglobin decline, length of hospital stay, postoperative complications, mortality, and survival time.

Statistical analyses were performed using the IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 24; IBM Corp., Armonk). The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), depending on the data distribution. Comparisons between the TXA and control groups were performed using the independent samples t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed to evaluate overall survival, and differences between groups were assessed using the log-rank test. Statistical significance was set at P < .05.

3. Results

During the study period, 47 patients underwent proximal femoral tumor resection and megaprosthetic reconstruction. Of these, 6 were excluded because of primary bone tumors, 3 because of revision procedures, 2 because of incomplete records, and 4 because of missing perioperative hemoglobin or transfusion data. Finally, 32 patients with metastatic bone disease were included in the analysis. A representative case of proximal femoral tumor resection and megaprosthetic reconstruction is shown in Figure 1. The mean age was 60.5 ± 9.9 years, and 20 patients (62.5%) were male. TXA was administered to 15 patients (46.9%), whereas 17 patients (53.1%) did not receive TXA. Perioperative blood transfusion was required in 23 patients (71.9%). Postoperative mortality occurred in 8 patients (25%), and the median overall survival time was 61 months (Table 1).

The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were comparable between the TXA and non-TXA groups. There were no statistically significant differences in age (59.5 ± 11.4 vs 61.4 ± 8.8 years, P = .636) or sex distribution (male/female: 10/5 vs 10/7, P = .726). Although the preoperative hemoglobin level was higher in the TXA group (11.72 ± 1.61 g/dL vs 10.84 ± 1.53 g/dL), this difference was not statistically significant (P = .104). Similarly, the length of hospital stay was numerically shorter in the TXA group (9.53 ± 4.73 days vs 13.94 ± 10.52 days), although the difference did not reach statistical significance (P = .202).

Postoperative mortality rates were similar between the groups (20.0% vs 29.4%, P = .691). The median survival times were 61 and 54 months in the TXA and non-TXA groups, respectively, with no statistically significant difference between the groups (log-rank P = .584; Table 2).

Postoperative hemoglobin change at 24 hours was 1.23 ± 1.36 g/dL in the TXA group and 0.99 ± 0.84 g/dL in the non-TXA group (P = .677). At 72 hours, hemoglobin change was −2.18 ± 2.01 g/dL and −1.35 ± 1.77 g/dL, respectively (P = .374). In the exploratory multivariable logistic regression analysis, the point estimate for TXA suggested lower odds of transfusion after adjustment for age, sex, and preoperative hemoglobin level; however, the association was not statistically significant (adjusted odds ratio 0.43, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.06–3.09; P = .403). Higher preoperative hemoglobin was associated with lower odds of transfusion (adjusted odds ratio 0.36 per g/dL, 95% CI: 0.16–0.85; P = .020).

Numerical differences between the groups included a lower transfusion requirement, shorter hospitalization, lower mortality, and longer survival in the TXA group; however, none of these between-group differences reached statistical significance. No clinically documented symptomatic thromboembolic events, including DVT or PE, were observed in either group during follow-up.

4. Discussion

In the present study, numerically lower transfusion requirements and a shorter hospital stay were observed in the TXA group; however, none of the between-group differences reached statistical significance. Mortality and survival outcomes were also comparable between groups. Accordingly, these numerical differences should be interpreted as exploratory observations rather than evidence of a statistically demonstrated treatment benefit.

The absence of statistically significant differences despite the observed numerical trends may reflect the limited sample size and consequently low statistical power.[10] Similar limitations have been reported in orthopedic oncology studies, in which relatively small cohorts may preclude the detection of clinically relevant between-group differences.[10] Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than as evidence of definitive efficacy.

Although the difference in transfusion requirement did not reach statistical significance, the absolute numerical difference between the groups may be clinically relevant and is consistent in direction with the established blood-sparing effect of TXA reported in arthroplasty and oncological surgery.[7,11,12] However, given the limited sample size, this observation should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of treatment efficacy. Park et al similarly reported that the implementation of a patient blood management protocol reduced transfusion rates in limb-salvage surgery for osteosarcoma, emphasizing the clinical importance of perioperative blood conservation strategies in oncologic orthopedic surgery.[13]

In our study, a single intravenous dose of TXA (10–15 mg/kg) was administered perioperatively, consistent with commonly used regimens in orthopedic practice.[14,15] The numerical trend toward a lower transfusion requirement is consistent with previous reports demonstrating reduced perioperative blood loss and transfusion rates with TXA.[11,12,16] Nevertheless, the present data do not establish a statistically significant benefit of TXA in this population.

Regression analyses should also be interpreted cautiously. Although the point estimate for TXA suggested lower odds of transfusion after adjustment for age, sex, and preoperative hemoglobin level, the association was not statistically significant and the wide CI indicates substantial imprecision. Therefore, the model does not provide definitive evidence of an independent TXA effect on transfusion requirement. In contrast, higher preoperative hemoglobin was independently associated with a lower transfusion requirement, although this finding should also be interpreted in the context of the limited sample size.

Hospital stay was numerically shorter in the TXA group, although the difference was not statistically significant. Previous studies have suggested that reduced perioperative blood loss may facilitate postoperative recovery[10,17]; however, the present study cannot establish whether TXA contributed to the observed difference in length of stay. This finding should therefore be considered exploratory. Beyond perioperative outcomes, standardized functional assessment is also important in musculoskeletal oncology. Validated tools such as the Musculoskeletal Tumor Society (MSTS) scoring system may provide complementary information regarding postoperative functional outcomes.[18] Functional outcome scores were not systematically available in our retrospective cohort and should be considered in future prospective studies.

Survival was evaluated as a secondary exploratory outcome. No statistically significant difference in survival was observed between the groups; however, only 8 deaths occurred, resulting in limited statistical power and substantial imprecision. Therefore, no conclusions regarding a potential effect of TXA on survival can be drawn from the present data, and the primary clinical focus of this study remains perioperative transfusion outcomes.

One of the major concerns regarding TXA use in patients with cancer is the potential risk of thromboembolic complications because of the hypercoagulable state associated with malignancy. In the present study, no clinically documented symptomatic thromboembolic events, including DVT or PE, were observed in either group. Previous studies and meta-analyses have similarly reported no significant increase in thromboembolic events associated with TXA use in oncological surgery.[9,11,12] However, our cohort was small, and thromboembolic events were assessed clinically without routine screening for asymptomatic thrombosis. Therefore, the absence of observed events in this study should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of thromboembolic safety, and asymptomatic events cannot be excluded.

An important strength of the present study was the relatively homogeneous patient population, as all cases consisted of metastatic bone disease. This reduces the heterogeneity related to tumor biology and treatment strategies that may be encountered when primary bone tumors are included. However, the inclusion of various metastatic tumor types may introduce some degree of variability in clinical outcomes. Additionally, the consistent surgical indication and reconstruction method (megaprosthetic reconstruction) further strengthened the internal validity of this study. However, this clinical homogeneity also restricts the external applicability of the findings. The present results should therefore not be directly extrapolated to patients with primary bone sarcomas, pelvic tumors, or other anatomical sites and types of megaprosthetic reconstruction.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design and small sample size limited statistical power and increased the risk of type II error. Second, the historical-control design may have introduced temporal bias, as subtle changes in transfusion practices, perioperative care, anesthesia management, or surgical experience over time cannot be excluded. Third, several potentially relevant perioperative variables, including estimated blood loss, operative duration, preoperative embolization, postoperative drainage volume, and extent of soft-tissue resection, could not be reliably and uniformly retrieved from the retrospective records and may have contributed to residual confounding. Fourth, the multivariable regression analyses were susceptible to model instability and overfitting because of the limited number of outcome events, particularly in the survival analysis, which included only 8 deaths; therefore, these estimates should be considered exploratory. Finally, the single-center design and highly selected cohort of patients with metastatic proximal femoral disease treated with megaprosthetic reconstruction limit the generalizability of the findings to other musculoskeletal oncology populations and reconstructive settings.

5. Conclusion

In patients undergoing proximal femoral tumor resection and megaprosthetic reconstruction for metastatic bone disease, TXA use was associated with a numerically lower perioperative transfusion requirement, although the difference did not reach statistical significance. Given the small sample size, limited statistical power, retrospective design, and use of a historical control group, these findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Larger prospective and multicenter studies are warranted to determine the efficacy and safety of TXA in this setting.
