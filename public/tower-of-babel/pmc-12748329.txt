Prognostic and microenvironmental analysis of pancreatic cancer by tumor location
Authors: Zedian Chen, Zichang Dai, Shaomei Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPancreatic cancer exhibits distinct biological and clinical features according to tumor location. Differences in survival and molecular programs among head (PH), body (PB), and tail (PT) tumors remain unclear.MethodsClinical data from the SEER database (n = 7302) and transcriptomic profiles from the TCGA cohort (n = 160) were analyzed. Patients were grouped by tumor site as PH, PB or PT. Overall survival was compared using Kaplan–Meier and Cox regression analyses, and location-specific nomograms were constructed. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using edgeR, followed by GO, KEGG, and GSEA enrichment to explore molecular mechanisms.ResultsOverall survival differed significantly by tumor site (P < 0.0001), with tail tumors showing the best prognosis, followed by PB, and PH tumors the poorest. Tumor location was an independent prognostic factor, and the nomograms demonstrated good predictive performance. Transcriptomic analysis revealed enrichment of lipid and steroid metabolism in tail versus FH tumors, and neuroactive ligand–receptor and endocannabinoid signaling in body–tail comparisons. GSEA identified apical junction, epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), and hypoxia as key pathways associated with location-specific outcomes.ConclusionsIntegrating large-scale epidemiologic and transcriptomic data, this study demonstrates that pancreatic tumor location independently influences survival and reflects distinct metabolic and microenvironmental programs. Recognition of these differences may aid individualized prognostic assessment and guide tailored therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer, one of the deadliest solid malignancies, has increased from 43,140 to 60,430 cases per year worldwide in the last decade [1, 2]. Regardless of the pathological type of pancreatic cancer, the survival rate of 5-year pancreatic cancer patients remains low [3]. Pancreatic cancer can be classified into head (PH), body (PB), and tail (PT) depending on the site of origin. Pancreatic cancer in different sites is highly heterogeneous in clinical manifestation, genetics, morphology, and biological behavior. Some studies have shown that pancreatic head cancer causes hyperbilirubinemia due to obstructive jaundice, which makes pancreatic head cancer easier to identify early in the disease. The incidence of the pancreatic body cancer and pancreatic tail cancer is more insidious, and most patients are found to have lymph node metastasis at the time of diagnosis. Nevertheless, interestingly, in previous research data, the survival rate of 5-year pancreatic head cancer seems to be higher than that of the pancreatic body cancer and the pancreatic tail cancer [4, 5].

Based on the SEER and TCGA databases, patients with pancreatic cancer were divided into three groups in this study: head, body, and tail, according to their primary sites. We compared the overall survival of patients in these groups and tried to find the difference in their molecular mechanisms, providing a theoretical basis for clinicians to make clinical decisions. Unlike previous studies that mainly described clinical or pathological disparities among pancreatic tumor sites, this work uniquely integrates large-scale epidemiological data from SEER with transcriptomic profiling from TCGA to reveal how site-specific molecular programs influence prognosis. By linking population-level outcomes with underlying biological pathways, our study provides a comprehensive framework connecting tumor location, molecular heterogeneity, and clinical behaviours in pancreatic cancer.

Materials and methods

Data source

Data for this study were from The Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results [6] (SEER) Program of the National Cancer Institute (the SEER-program) and The Cancer Genome Atlas database [7] (TCGA).

The SEER data set

Data from the SEER database was obtained using the site code classifications C34.0–34.9 and site recode “ICD-O-3 Pancreatic” from SEER*STAT 8.3.9 software. The cases with autopsy report (Autopsy only) and death report (Death certificate only) were excluded. So does cases with incomplete information.

The cancer genome atlas (TCGA) date set

The TCGA website was used to download clinical, transcriptomic, and mRNA expression data related to pancreatic cancer. R software was used to organize and summarize clinical data.

Statistical analysis: main outcome measures

Eleven clinical characteristics features of different primary lesions in the SEER database were compared by chi-square test, including Age, Sex, Race, Grade, AJCC Stage Group 6th, AJCC T 6th, AJCC N 6th, AJCC M 6th, Tumor Size, Radiation, Chemotherapy. KM curves were established using the R survival package, and log-rank tests were to assess the differences between groups to explore the overall survival of different primary lesions [8]. Univariate Cox regression was used to screen survival-related variables, and factors with P < 0.05 were entered into a multivariate model to identify independent prognostic indicators. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to estimate relative risk. HR >1.0 indicated increased risk compared with the reference group. For this study, all reported P values were bilateral.

Development and evaluation of the nomogram

To integrate multiple predictors, we constructed a characteristic Nomogram based on the site of the primary lesion. All independent prognostic factors were included in the analysis. Modelling with as few variables as required without affecting the predictive outcome, we used stepwise regression to obtain the optimal subset of features when the AIC was minimized. R version 4.0.3 was used to build and validate the location-specific Nomogram model. The concordance index (C-index) was for the validity of the Nomogram.

DEGs identified and functional annotation and pathway enrichment analysis

We divided the patients in the TCGA database into head pancreatic cancer group (Ph), body pancreatic cancer group (Pb), and tail pancreatic cancer group (Pt) according to the primary site of pancreatic cancer and selected three groups of pancreatic cancer patients expressing differential genes. Gene expression matrices were generated between the three groups using R package ‘edgeR’ to identify and select differentially expressed genes (DEGs) [9]. The false discovery rate (FDR) threshold was 0.05, and the |log2-fold change| (|log2FC|) minimum threshold was set at 2. And the mean of gene expression values was taken when the same gene was measured with multiple probes. We assessed differences between the two groups in Student’s t-test, adjusted P-values for multiple testing by the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method, then presented the results as volcano plots in R language. Differential gene functions, pathway enrichment analysis functions, enrichment analysis of molecular functions (MF), biological processes (BP), and cellular components (CC) are run employing the R language statistical package ‘ClusterProfiler’ [10]. The ClusterProfiler package was also used to run the KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) pathway enrichment analysis function to identify vital biological pathways.

GSEA

GSEA was a kind of computational method that can analyse whether there are statistical differences between two biological states within a given gene set. The gene set “h.all.v7.5.1.symbols.GMT” was downloaded from the MSigDB database for GSEA analysis of DEGs and then subject to enrichment analysis using the default weighted enrichment method [11]. Random combinations were set to 1000; FDR < 0.25, NOM p-values < 0.05 and |NES| >1 were considered significantly enriched. The GSEA results were then visualized employing the R package ‘ggplot2’ [12].

Statistical analysis

R (version 4.0.2; The R Foundation for Statistical Computing) was used for data analysis and statistics. The significance of normally distributed variables could be estimated by the test of Student t, while the Mann-Whitney U test analyzed the differences between non-normally. Absolute values of R greater than 0.25 were considered relevant, and p-values < 0.05 were considered significant.

Survival rate

In the SEER database, overall survival differed significantly among tumor locations (P < 0.0001). The five-year survival rates were 16.3% for head, 18.4% for body, and 28.8% for tail tumors (Fig. 2a). In the TCGA cohort, a consistent pattern was observed: patients with tail tumors had the best prognosis, followed by those with body tumors, while head tumors showed the poorest outcomes (Fig. 1a, b).

Identify survival rate-related factors

Univariate Cox proportional hazards regression was performed on the three groups with different primary tumor locations. The results showed that Sex had no significant effect on the pancreatic head or pancreatic body; Race, Chemotherapy on the pancreatic body; Radiation on the pancreatic tail had no significant overall survival (P. Value > 0.05); the rest all affected the overall survival of the corresponding primary lesions (Fig. 2a) (P < 0.05). We included variables with an effect of each group on survival in multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression. In order to build a model with as few variables as possible without affecting the prediction results, we use a stepwise regression method to obtain the optimal feature subset when the AIC is minimum. Finally, use Age, Race, Grade, Stage, Satge T, Tumor Size, Radiation, and Chemotherapy to build a pancreatic head model; Age, Grade, Stage, Tumor Size, Radiation builds a pancreatic body model; Age, Race, Stage, Satge N, Tumor Size to build a pancreas tail model (Fig. 2b).

Nomogram establishment

Based on the above Cox models, site-specific nomograms were constructed to predict 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall survival (Fig. 3a, b, c). The C-index values for the head, body, and tail models were 0.692, 0.684, and 0.715, respectively (95% CI, P.value < 0.001), indicating good discriminative ability. Calibration plots also showed excellent agreement between predicted and observed survival (Fig. 4a, b, c).

Discussion

Patients with pancreatic cancer usually show no obvious symptoms in the early stage, resulting in a poor 5-year survival rate. Although TNM staging remains the key reference for treatment decision [13, 14], the primary tumor site also has a major impact on surgical planning. The surgical approaches for pancreatic head and body/tail cancer are very different. Head cancer requires a pancreaticoduodenectomy (Whipple procedure), while tail cancer requires a distal pancreatic resection and a complete splenectomy. The American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) Cancer Staging Manual and the International Study Group on Pancreatic Surgery (ISGPS) suggest different regional lymph node dissection options which depend on the primary site [15, 16]. Therefore, we believe there is a wide variation in pancreatic cancer at different sites and tried to investigate its possible reasons.

In the study, the pancreatic tumors were divided into three groups based on the location of the primary lesion: head (PH), body (PB), and tail (PT). Our results showed that a significant difference was in overall survival among the three groups in the SEER database, with the 5-year survival rate of the primary lesion located in the tail of the pancreas being significantly higher than that of the other two groups, while the survival rate of the head of the pancreas was the lowest. This result was also confirmed in the TCGA database. However, this differs from the study by Mirang Lee et al., which indicated that the survival rate of pancreatic head tumors was significantly higher than that of the pancreatic body or tail tumors [17]. This difference may be due to the ethnic and geographic differences between the SEER, TCGA database, which mainly included American data, and the study by Mirang Lee et al. [17] which had data from Korea. After analyzing the clinical characteristics of the 7302 patients included in the study and excluding the factors that did not affect survival, we further constructed and validated the primary lesion-specific clinical characteristics nomograms. as seen in the results, the nomograms of the three groups showed differences. Furthermore, the reliability of the nomograms was verified by C-index.

We next explored biological differences among the three tumor locations using GO and KEGG analyses. The PB and PT groups mainly differed in cell differentiation and ion-channel transport pathways, which appeared less related to pancreatic cancer prognosis. The differences between the PH and PB groups were mainly related to endopeptidases. Endopeptidases are closely associated with metabolic regulation, and their aberrant activation may promote pancreatic tumor aggressiveness through disordered protein turnover and hormone signaling. Prolyl endopeptidase (PREP) regulates glucose-induced neuronal activation, and its knockdown leads to insulin resistance and hyperglucagonemia, both of which are recognized contributors to pancreatic cancer progression [18]. And fasting insulin levels are causally associated with an increased risk of pancreatic cancer [19]. Qiang Yan et al. demonstrated that asparagine endopeptidase enhances pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma cell invasion in an exosome-dependent manner and is associated with poor prognosis [20]. These findings suggest that proteolytic activity–related metabolic remodeling may underlie the site-specific aggressiveness of pancreatic cancer.

In contrast, the differential genes between the PT and PH groups were focused on the metabolic-related pathways of lipids, which are strongly associated with pancreatic cancer prognosis. Lipids include fats and lipids. Saturated and monounsaturated fatty acids are thought to promote the growth of pancreatic cancer cells, while the effects of polyunsaturated fatty acids appear to exhibit duality [21]. Statins, which affect lipid metabolism, are associated with prolonged survival in pancreatic cancer patients [22]. In addition, it has been shown that atorvastatin delays PI3/AKT by modulating p48Cre/+ LSL-KrasG12D/+ mouse signaling delays the progression of pancreatic lesions to cancer [23]. Our transcriptomic results further support the notion that lipid and steroid metabolic pathways differ among tumor locations, implying distinct metabolic dependencies. Such site-specific metabolic signatures could represent actionable vulnerabilities, and metabolic interventions (e.g., statins, fatty acid synthase inhibitors) may be more effective for metabolically active subtypes.

And we performed GSEA to analyze the differences between the three groups. Genes in the PB and PT groups were enriched in apical junction, epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and hypoxia. The cancer progression is strongly associated with EMT, which is associated with three significant steps of cancer development: invasion, dissemination, and metastasis, which remain consistent in pancreatic cancer [24]. Several studies have shown that the EMT activator ZEB1 is a critical factor in promoting metastasis in pancreatic cancer [25]. Mingyang Liu et al. [26] demonstrated that the Hippo pathway effector YAP1 forms a complex with ZEB1 and activates ITGA3 transcription through the YAP1/TEAD binding site in human pancreatic cancer KPC-derived mouse cells. In addition, EMT also plays an essential role in drug resistance in pancreatic cancer. In drug-resistant pancreatic cancer cells, targeted silencing of ZEB1 restored E-cadherin expression and led to an overall increase in drug sensitivity [27]. In pancreatic cancer, the hypoxic environment was uncovered 20 years ago (Duffy et al. 2003). Studies have been conducted on this feature of hypoxia in recent years. Hypoxia-inducible factor 1 alpha (HIF1A) deletion increases PPP1R1B expression and p53 degradation to promote pancreatic tumor invasion and metastasis [28]. LIMS1 promotes pancreatic cancer cell survival under hypoxia-glucose deprivation conditions by enhancing HIF1A protein translation [29]. Hypoxia regulates ABCG2 activity through activation of ERK1/2/HIF-1α and leads to chemoresistance of pancreatic cancer cells [30]. Collectively, these findings indicate that EMT and hypoxia activation are not only markers of biological aggressiveness but also potential therapeutic targets. Targeting TGF-β or HIF1A signaling may improve sensitivity to chemotherapy and immunotherapy in hypoxic or mesenchymal pancreatic cancer subtypes.

Together, these transcriptomic features demonstrate that primary tumor location is associated with distinct metabolic and stress-response programs that shape disease progression, treatment response, and prognosis. Integrating such molecular insights into clinical management may help refine risk stratification, guide adjuvant therapy selection, and support the development of site-specific targeted strategies for pancreatic cancer.

These hypoxia- and EMT-related alterations may partially explain the poorer outcomes observed in head tumors compared with body and tail lesions. Moreover, we expanded the discussion to highlight their clinical relevance. Site-specific nomograms derived from SEER data can help stratify patients before surgery, informing the extent of resection, lymph-node dissection, and perioperative risk assessment. Postoperative surveillance strategies may also be refined using nomogram-based risk scores to identify patients at higher recurrence risk who could benefit from closer imaging follow-up or adjuvant therapy. At the molecular level, pathways such as EMT and hypoxia signaling represent potential targets for combination treatment or biomarker-guided therapy, providing a translational bridge between prognostic modeling and precision oncology. Nevertheless, here a new pathway KRAS emerged. Oncogenic KRAS mutations are the main event in pancreatic cancer [31]. RAS signaling mediated by KRAS plays a crucial role in disease onset, progression, and drug resistance [32]. KRAS plays a vital role in disease development, progression, and resistance through lipase HSL, NIX-mediated mitochondrial autophagy, and many other aspects that affect the progression of pancreatic cancer [33, 34]. The PT group was only enriched with the PB group. This is in keeping with the less pronounced clinical differences between PB and PT.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. Although the SEER database provides extensive epidemiologic information, it lacks detailed data on surgical margins, chemotherapy, and targeted or immunotherapy regimens, which may influence survival outcomes. The TCGA cohort also includes a limited number of pancreatic cancer cases, leading to relatively small sample sizes for site-specific subgroup analyses. Moreover, TCGA transcriptomic data are derived from bulk sequencing and therefore cannot fully capture intratumoral heterogeneity. These constraints should be considered when interpreting the results, and future studies with larger, multi-center cohorts and single-cell or spatial transcriptomic profiling are warranted to validate and extend our findings.

Conclusions

Studies have shown that the primary site of cancer has irreplaceable value in predicting cancer outcomes recently. In pancreatic cancer, there is a non-negligible difference in the 5-year survival rate in different primary locations. This difference may be related to the fact that tumors in the head of the pancreas compress the gallbladder and undertake more glucose and lipid metabolism than the body and tail of the pancreas.
