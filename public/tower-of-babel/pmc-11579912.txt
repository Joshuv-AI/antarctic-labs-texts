Middle fossa approach for a petrous apex bony spur causing trigeminal neuralgia: illustrative case

Trigeminal neuralgia (TN) is defined as paroxysmal, lancinating pain in the distribution of one or more branches of the trigeminal nerve. It has an incidence of 12.6–27.0 per 100,000 person-years and has an average age of onset between 53 and 57 years.1–3 TN frequently arises from trigeminal nerve compression at the root entry zone, which is believed to cause demyelination and resultant aberrant nerve signaling.4 The trigeminal root entry zone is found within the cisternal segment of the trigeminal nerve. In most individuals, this location marks the transition from central to peripheral myelination, where oligodendrocytes and Schwann cells meet, and is therefore thought to be more prone to producing aberrations in nerve signaling.5

Neurovascular conflict of the trigeminal nerve is often the result of compression from the superior cerebellar artery. However, in addition to pathological processes such as tumors, several observational and cadaveric studies have revealed alternative structural causes, including the periosteum and venous plexus of the foramen ovale, the inferolateral edge of the mouth of Meckel’s cave, the cavernous segment of the internal carotid artery, the medial wall of the foramen rotundum, the suprameatal tubercle, and the petrous bone.6–9 Given the relative rarity of TN in younger patients, considering alternative structural causes of TN is of particular importance in this population.

Illustrative Case

History

A 21-year-old woman with no significant prior medical history presented with worsening right-sided TN predominantly involving the V2–3 distributions. She experienced electric shock–like pain with burning pain in the right corner of her mouth, cheek, and upper and lower gums, which lasted seconds to minutes. Her symptoms were triggered or exacerbated by physical contact, speaking, brushing her teeth, eating, and drinking. Oxcarbazepine, buspirone, amitriptyline, and baclofen failed to relieve her symptoms.

During her assessment and workup, axial T2 sampling perfection with application optimized contrast using different flip angle evolution (SPACE) magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) did not reveal any vascular compression of the trigeminal nerve; however, sagittal reformats demonstrated a prominent bony ridge causing compression of the right trigeminal nerve at the porus trigeminus (Figs. 1A and 2). It was determined that a middle fossa craniotomy would serve as an ideal approach to achieve decompression of the trigeminal nerve.

Left: Preoperative three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction of the course of the trigeminal nerve revealed a prominent bony ridge (red circle) in contact with the patient’s right trigeminal nerve. Right: Postoperative 3D reconstruction showed the results of our targeted surgical approach after selective reduction of the prominence of the bony ridge (red circle) and successful decompression of the trigeminal nerve.

Sagittal T2 SPACE MRI revealing the right trigeminal nerve with obvious prominence of the petrous apex (arrowhead) and resultant deformation of the trigeminal nerve.

Surgery

The patient was placed in a left lateral decubitus position on the operating table with the head turned to the left and secured in a 3-pin Mayfield head clamp. Neuromonitoring leads were placed for intraoperative facial nerve and brainstem auditory reflex monitoring, and intraoperative neuronavigation was registered. A curvilinear incision was planned overlying the root of the zygoma, extending to the superior temporal line.

The curvilinear incision was made, and then the temporalis fascia was exposed in a separate layer and opened sharply. The underlying bone, including the anterior and posterior portions of the zygomatic root, was then exposed. Neuronavigation was used to confirm the floor of the middle fossa. A 3 × 3–cm craniotomy centered over the root of the zygoma was performed, and the inferior edge of the craniotomy was further drilled until it was flush with the floor of the middle fossa. The temporal dura was retracted superiorly by first exposing the middle meningeal artery as it exited the foramen spinosum. This vessel was then cauterized and incised to aid in further retraction. The temporal lobe was retracted to reveal the petrous ridge. The dissection was carried out anteriorly to ensure protection of the greater superficial petrosal nerve. Once this was identified, the V3 branch of the trigeminal nerve was identified and followed posteriorly to the trigeminal ganglion and trigeminal impression in the middle fossa floor. Significant scar tissue was noted between the temporal dura and the trigeminal ganglion. With gentle retraction of the trigeminal ganglion, the bony spur was identified. After the bony pathology was confirmed with neuronavigation, a 2-mm diamond burr was used to remove the spur (Video 1).

Postoperative imaging revealed satisfactory decompression of the trigeminal nerve and reduction of the bone spur believed to be contributing to the patient’s symptoms (Figs. 1 and 3). After surgery, the patient reported complete resolution of her preoperative neuralgia, with some numbness isolated to her right lower lip. Her neurological examination findings were otherwise normal, and she was pain free at her last follow-up, 10 months after surgery.

Discussion

Observations

Neurovascular conflict of the trigeminal nerve is believed to be the most common cause of TN, but it is important to consider nonvascular compressive etiologies, especially in younger patients. Ko and colleagues10 demonstrated that for patients who lack imaging findings consistent with neurovascular compression, the onset of TN tends to occur earlier in life. In their study, the age distribution for this subset of patients with TN was bimodal, with many patients presenting in the 3rd decade of life. Demyelinating changes are often present on microscopic and histopathological analysis of the trigeminal nerve in patients with vascular compression, whereas those without compression show no obvious abnormalities.11 Patients who have minimal (Sindou grade I) vascular compression on imaging are more likely to have unfavorable operative outcomes after microvascular decompression.12 Those without neurovascular conflict more commonly undergo neuroablative procedures or internal neurolysis, which tend to produce less durable results.13, 14 Given the differences in presentation, microscopic findings, and operative outcomes between patients with and without neurovascular conflict, it is important to distinguish between a nonvascular compressive cause amenable to surgical decompression, as in the case presented here, and those cases without neurovascular compression that may require ablative procedures.

Several nonvascular anatomical structures have been presented as potential contributors to TN in cadaveric studies. Among these are the porus trigeminus,7 the suprameatal tubercle,9 the inferolateral edge of Meckel’s cave,6 the lateral wall of the cavernous internal carotid artery,6 the medial wall of the foramen rotundum,6 and the periosteum and venous plexus of the foramen ovale.6 Among the cases of TN arising from bony compression, some have been associated with pathological osseous growth, such as Paget’s disease15 and endostosis of the petrous bone.16 Hirata et al.8 reported a case of left petrous bone thickening that contributed to trigeminal nerve compression. After surgical reduction of the prominent petrous bone ridge, the patient experienced durable relief from TN. Brinzeu et al. and Zhong et al. demonstrated that in patients with TN, the angle of the petrous ridge was often more acute than in patients without TN, highlighting the role of nearby bony structures either causing compression or exacerbating contact with the superior cerebellar artery.17, 18 Branstetter et al.19 noted that in a subset of patients with TN without vascular compression, the segment of trigeminal nerve in the porus trigeminus formed a more acute angle on sagittal MRI than in asymptomatic patients. In their control group, the average angle created by the nerve was 170°, and the subset of patients with TN without vascular compression had a sagittal angle of 143°. Branstetter et al. argued that some cases of TN may arise from the nerve’s aberrant course at the porus trigeminus and patients may therefore benefit from an alternative surgery targeted to their anatomical variation. In our patient, the sagittal angle of the trigeminal nerve in the porus trigeminus on MRI was 134° (Fig. 3).

The discrepancy in surgical outcomes between TN patients with and without significant neurovascular conflict highlights the need to thoroughly review patient imaging in axial, sagittal, and coronal planes. The current literature demonstrates many cases in which patients without neurovascular conflict undergo microvascular decompression or neuroablative procedures with comparably less durable results than their counterparts with obvious vascular compression. In addition, there are few cases and cadaveric reports that reveal areas of potential bony compression that conceivably contribute to TN, with or without associated neurovascular compression, presenting a clear surgical target. In our case, the patient’s sagittal imaging revealed a bony spur along the petrous apex, decreasing the angle of the nerve along this segment and producing trigeminal nerve compression. Although compression in this region can be seen on imaging preoperatively and under direct visualization intraoperatively, surgical manipulation of the affected nerve may conceivably contribute to an effectual and therapeutic rhizotomy. We predicted that by reducing the size of the bony spur in this region, the patient would experience a reduction in nerve compression or tension and ameliorated symptoms. Our preoperative imaging and assessment prompted a middle fossa craniotomy for the removal of the bony spur, thus decompressing the transiting trigeminal nerve and successfully relieving the patient’s facial pain.

Lessons

A thorough preoperative imaging assessment in a young patient with TN can reveal areas of nonvascular compression that can be treated with targeted surgical decompression. Prominent and compressive osseous structures along the course of the trigeminal nerve can be evaluated most reliably on sagittal imaging typically performed during the TN workup.
