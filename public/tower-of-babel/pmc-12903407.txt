Effect of vitamin D supplementation on skeletal muscle function and quality of life in patients with chronic intestinal failure: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Xuejin Gao, Sirui Liu, Yupeng Zhang, Yufei Xia, Sitong Liu, Xin Qi, Yaqin Xiao, Minyi Zhu, Guangming Sun, Tingting Gao, Li Zhang, Xinying Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVitamin D deficiency is common in patients with chronic intestinal failure (CIF); however, the effectiveness of high-dose vitamin D supplementation remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate the effects of high-dose vitamin D supplementation on skeletal muscle mass, function, and quality of life (QoL) in patients with CIF.MethodsThis was a single-center, prospective, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial conducted at a tertiary hospital in China. Participants included individuals aged 18–70 years with chronic intestinal failure and vitamin D deficiency (serum 25(OH)D < 30 ng/ml). Eighty-four adult patients were randomly assigned (1:1) to either the vitamin D group (intramuscular injection of 600,000 units of vitamin D2 every two weeks for 10 weeks) or the control group (matched placebo). The primary outcomes were appendicular skeletal muscle mass index (assessed by bioelectrical impedance), handgrip strength, 6-meter walking speed, and quality of life (SF-36 scales) from the date of randomization to the end of the 12-week follow-up period. The analysis was performed on an intention-to-treat principle.ResultsEighty-four patients were randomized to the control group (n = 42; mean age, 52.8 ± 15.6 years) or the vitamin D group (n = 42; mean age, 52.9 ± 16.2 years). At the 12-week follow-up, serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D levels were significantly higher in the vitamin D group than in the control group (96.7 ± 5.8 vs. 23.8 ± 4.1 ng/mL; p < 0.001). Compared with the control group, the vitamin D group showed clinically meaningful improvements in the appendicular skeletal muscle mass index, handgrip strength, 6-meter walking speed, and the SF-36 physical component summary score (all p < 0.05 after Bonferroni correction). No other clinically significant differences were observed between the groups in the remaining secondary clinical and safety endpoints.ConclusionHigh-dose intramuscular vitamin D2 supplementation for 10 weeks was significantly associated with improved skeletal muscle mass, function, and QoL in patients with CIF.This trial registrationClinicalTrials.Gov Identifier: NCT06283615. Registered 11 March 2024, https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06283615.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07607-8.

Introduction

Intestinal failure (IF) is characterized by reduced gut function below the minimum necessary for the absorption of macronutrients and/or water and electrolytes, such that intravenous supplementation (IVS) is required to maintain health and/or growth [1]. Individuals with intestinal failure are highly susceptible to a deficiency in Vitamin D, which is essential for maintaining skeletal muscle mass and function [2]. The serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25[OH]D) level is considered to be the best biomarker of vitamin D status, with a serum 25(OH)D level ≥ 30 ng/ml considered adequate [3]. The Endocrine Society Guidelines recommend an adequate intake of vitamin D from sun, food, and supplements for all ages [3, 4]. This is necessary to maintain a circulating 25(OH)D concentration of at least 30 ng/ml, which is considered optimal for maximum bone health [3, 4].

Numerous clinical observational studies have shown that vitamin D deficiency is common in patients with chronic intestinal failure (CIF) [5–8]. It is considered an important risk factor for osteoporosis [9], which can result in reduced muscle strength and a decreased quality of life. CIF is most often secondary to short bowel syndrome (SBS) [1].SBS results in nutrient malabsorption, particularly fat malabsorption, and may play a role in vitamin D deficiency. A lack of adequate ultraviolet exposure from sunlight due to chronic illness and medications that affect vitamin D metabolism are additional risk factors found in patients with CIF [10]. International guidelines recommend routine monitoring of fat-soluble vitamin levels in patients with CIF [11, 12], and supplementation of vitamin D through oral, enteral, or parenteral routes, including regular intramuscular injection [12], may be beneficial in these patients. In clinical practice, enteral vitamin D supplementation is generally preferred; however, conventional enteral replacement therapy for vitamin D may be insufficient in patients with CIF [13]. Therefore, when patients fail to achieve optimal vitamin D levels through enteral supplementation, intramuscular vitamin D administration is recommended. However, these recommendations are primarily based on expert opinion and lack robust scientific evidence.

Vitamin D is recognized as essential for calcium homeostasis and bone metabolism, and it also has important direct effects on skeletal muscle function [14]. Evidence indicates that vitamin D has direct and indirect effects on skeletal muscle through the vitamin D receptor, which has been shown to be present in muscle cells [14–16]. Deficient serum 25(OH)D levels have been shown to impair muscle function and lead to sarcopenia as well as decreased muscle strength [17–20]. Furthermore, some studies have suggested that serum 25(OH)D is associated with physical performance [21–23]. Low 25(OH)D levels are associated with decreased skeletal muscle mass and reduced physical performance and function [24–26]. This association is particularly relevant in patients with chronic intestinal failure, in whom vitamin D deficiency has been linked to adverse clinical outcomes [27, 28]. However, evidence regarding the impact of intramuscular high-dose vitamin D supplementation on skeletal muscle mass, function, and quality of life in patients with chronic intestinal failure remains scarce.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate the effects of intramuscular administration of high-dose vitamin D2 on skeletal muscle mass, muscle function, and quality of life in patients with chronic intestinal failure.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a prospective, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial at Jinling Hospital in China from March 2024 to March 2025. The Ethics Committee of Jinling Hospital approved the study (approval number: 2023ZYLLSL143-KY112) and each participant provided written informed consent before randomization. The trial was registered prior to patient enrollment at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06283615) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The trial protocol and statistical analysis plan are included in Supplement 1. This study adhered to the guidelines of the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) [29].

Patients

Participants were eligible for inclusion in the study only if they met al.l of the following criteria: Participants were aged between 18 and 70 years with CIF and serum 25(OH)D levels < 30.0 ng/ml. The diagnostic criteria for chronic intestinal failure were established in accordance with the 2016 ESPEN guidelines on intestinal failure [1].

Participants were excluded from this study if they met any of the following criteria: they were unable to read and sign the written informed consent form; their screening serum 25(OH)D level was > 30.0 ng/ml; they regularly used vitamin D supplements (excluding those contained in parenteral or enteral nutrition preparations) within the past 6 months; had primary hypothyroidism or parathyroidism; suffered from allergic diseases, allergies, or had a history of drug sensitivity similar to the structure of the study drug; had primary diabetes; had mental illness, inability to cooperate, or consciousness disorders; had contraindications for experimental drugs; had a suspected or confirmed history of substance abuse; had immune deficiency or used immune suppressants and hormones; were pregnant or lactating women; took any medication in the last 6 months that affects vitamin D metabolism (e.g., phenytoin, phenobarbital, rifampicin); participating in other clinical trials; or were sponsors or investigators directly involved in this trial or their family members.

Randomization and masking

An independent statistician generated the randomization schedule and randomly assigned participants in a 1:1 ratio to either the vitamin D group or the control group. To ensure allocation concealment, the assignments were sealed in sequentially numbered, opaque envelopes. A study nurse, who had no involvement in subsequent patient assessments or clinical care, was solely responsible for receiving the envelope and administering the intramuscular injection. The vitamin D group received 600,000 IU of vitamin D2 every 2 weeks for 10 weeks, while the control group received a visually matched placebo. The interventions were prepared by an independent pharmacy to ensure identical appearance. All personnel involved in patient recruitment, outcome assessment, data management, and statistical analysis remained blinded to group assignment until the final analysis was completed.

Interventions

The vitamin D group received intramuscular injections of vitamin D2 (600,000 IU every 2 weeks for 10 weeks) plus standard care. Specific treatment time points are detailed in Figure S1 (see Supplement 2). Vitamin D2 was purchased from Jiangxi Gannan Haixin Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. The control group received appearance-matched placebo injections plus standard care. Standard care encompassed nutritional support, dietary counseling, and maintenance of habitual sunlight exposure.

Physical activity levels and nutritional intake (in terms of energy and protein) were comparable between groups and were monitored throughout the duration of the trial. Investigators and statisticians remained blinded to group allocation until the final analysis was completed. Follow-up began at intervention initiation and continued through week 12.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were skeletal muscle mass, physical function, and quality of life from the date of randomization to the end of the 12-week follow-up period. Skeletal muscle mass and physical function were evaluated by measuring appendicular skeletal muscle mass index (assessed using bioelectrical impedance analysis), handgrip strength, and 6-m walking speed. Quality of life was assessed using the SF-36 questionnaire, including the SF-36 Physical Component Summary (PCS) and Mental Component Summary (MCS) scores [30].

Secondary outcome measures included serum levels of 25(OH)D, infection complications, liver and kidney function, bone mineral density, thyroid-related hormones (including thyroid-stimulating hormone, free tetraiodothyronine, free triiodothyronine, total triiodothyronine, and total tetraiodothyronine), parathyroid hormone, calcitonin levels, and nutritional status indicators. The safety endpoints were total calcium levels up to week 12, incidence of kidney stones up to week 12, fall-related fractures up to week 12, and bone pain up to week 12.

The baseline data including age, sex, body weight, body mass index (BMI), etiology of CIF, vitamin D-related indicators (including 25(OH)D, parathyroid hormone, calcitonin, estimated average daily vitamin D dose, total calcium, phosphorous, creatinine, and estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR]), thyroid function (including thyroid stimulating hormone, free tetraiodothyronine, free triiodothyronine, total triiodothyronine, and total tetraiodothyronine), skeletal muscle mass and function (including appendicular skeletal muscle mass index, hand grip strength, and 6-m walking speed), quality of life (SF-36 score), and other laboratory tests, were recorded.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was calculated based on the primary outcome measure, appendicular skeletal muscle mass index (ASMI). Our previous study on patients with CIF indicated that the standard deviation (SD) of ASMI was 0.76 [31]. Assuming a mean difference of 0.5 in ASMI between the two groups, with a significance level of α = 0.05 and statistical power of 80%, a total of 76 participants were required. Accounting for a 10% dropout rate, a total of 84 patients were enrolled and randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio, resulting in 42 patients per group.

Categorical data were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Quantitative data were expressed as mean (SD) or median (minimum, maximum) for variables following the normal or skewed distribution, respectively. Data were analyzed using per-protocol and intention-to-treat analyses to compare outcome measures between the two groups. Comparison of continuous variables between groups was performed using Student’s t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum (Mann–Whitney U) test. Categorical variables were compared between groups using the χ2 or Fisher’s exact test. Given that this study used a composite primary endpoint, we implemented the Bonferroni correction to control for Type I error inflation from multiple comparisons. The primary outcome comprised five key measures: ASMI, handgrip strength, 6-meter walking speed, SF-36 PCS, and SF-36 MCS. Accordingly, we applied a Bonferroni-adjusted significance threshold of α = 0.01 (0.05/5) to maintain an overall family-wise error rate (FWER) of 5%. Each individual outcome was evaluated against this more stringent threshold to minimize false positive findings.

Analyses were performed using the SPSS software (version 25.0; IBM Inc.). All reported P values were two-sided with a significance level of < 0.05.

Results

Patient characteristics

From March 2024 to March 2025, we obtained consent from 120 screened patients with CIF at Jinling Hospital. Of these, 36 (30.0%) patients did not meet the eligibility criteria and were excluded. A total of 84 patients (47 males [56.0%] and 37 females [44.0%]; mean [SD] age, 52.8 [15.9] years) were enrolled and randomized. One patients (1.2%) withdrew from the study. Overall, 42 patients were assigned to the vitamin D group, and 42 patients were assigned to the control group (Fig. 1). Baseline characteristics were comparable between the two groups in the primary analysis populations (Table 1).

Serum vitamin D levels

The mean baseline 25(OH)D level, measured by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS), was 14.9 ± 3.5 ng/mL in the vitamin D group and 14.8 ± 3.7 ng/mL in the control group (Table 1). At week 12, the mean 25(OH)D level was 96.7 ± 5.8 ng/mL in the vitamin D group and 23.8 ± 4.1 ng/mL in the control group (mean difference: 72.9; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 70.72 to 75.08; p < 0.001) (Table 2). The primary increase was observed in serum 25(OH)D2 levels (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2 and Table S1 in Supplement 2). In the vitamin D group, the majority of patients (95.2%) achieved the target 25(OH)D range of 30–120 ng/mL at week 12, while a small proportion (4.8%) had levels exceeding 120 ng/mL (Table 2). In the control group, 92.9% of patients maintained 25(OH)D levels below 30 ng/mL at week 12 (Table 2).

Primary outcomes

The vitamin D group demonstrated significant improvements in skeletal muscle mass, physical function, and quality of life scores compared to the control group (control group vs. vitamin D group: appendicular skeletal muscle mass index: 6.3 ± 0.69 vs. 6.7 ± 0.65; mean difference, 0.4; 95% CI [0.108 to 0.692]; p = 0.008; hand grip strength: 26.3 ± 3.8 vs. 28.6 ± 3.9; mean difference, 2.3; 95% CI [0.629 to 3.971]; p = 0.008; 6-meter walking speed: 1.12 ± 0.18 vs. 1.24 ± 0.20; mean difference, 0.12; 95% CI [0.037 to 0.203]; p = 0.005; SF-36 PCS: 36.4 ± 7.4 vs. 42.6 ± 7.5; mean difference, 6.2; 95% CI [2.966 to 9.434]; p < 0.001) (Table 2, and Fig. 3). Although the SF-36 MCS score increased in the vitamin D group, this change did not remain statistically significant after adjustment for multiple comparisons (p = 0.071) (Table 2). Significant between-group differences were observed in changes in skeletal muscle mass, physical function, and quality of life scores (p < 0.001) (Table S2 in Supplement 2).

Secondary outcomes

In the primary analysis, there were no significant differences between the groups with respect to infection-related complications, liver and kidney function, bone mineral density (BMD), thyroid-related hormones, parathyroid hormone levels, and nutritional status indicators (p > 0.05) (Table 2). Although the vitamin D group showed a trend toward reduced nosocomial infections, this difference was not statistically significant (7.1% [3/42] vs. 16.7% [7/42]; risk difference: -9.5%; 95% CI: -15.7% to -3.3%; p = 0.313) (Table 2).

Adverse events

Safety outcomes and adverse events are summarized in Table 2. Pre-specified vitamin D-related adverse events, including hypercalcemia, kidney stones, and fall-related fractures, were comparable between the two groups. The vitamin D group showed a slight increase in blood calcium levels compared with the control group. Serious and non-serious adverse events were uncommon and similarly distributed between the vitamin D and control groups across the study population.

Discussion

In this randomized clinical trial, high-dose intramuscular vitamin D2 supplementation administered every 2 weeks for 10 weeks significantly increased skeletal muscle mass, improved muscle function, and enhanced quality of life in patients with CIF. To our knowledge, this is the first randomized clinical trial to demonstrate these effects in the CIF population.

Observational studies have shown that lower 25(OH)D levels are associated with adverse clinical outcomes in gastrointestinal disorders [32]. Furthermore, a positive correlation has been observed between reduced 25(OH)D concentrations and an unfavorable prognosis in individuals diagnosed with CIF. In a cohort of 167 patients from Denmark with intestinal insufficiency and intestinal failure receiving home parenteral nutrition, multivariate analysis identified that body mass index (BMI) and vitamin D deficiency as independent predictors of spinal and hip bone mineral density (BMD) [33]. The present randomized clinical trial demonstrates that high-dose intramuscular vitamin D2 supplementation, administered biweekly over a 10-week period, significantly increases skeletal muscle mass, improves muscle function, and enhances quality of life in patients with CIF. These findings are broadly consistent with a recent report by Dechsupa et al., which demonstrated that vitamin D₂ supplementation improved vitamin D status, muscle mass, physical function, and quality of life in patients with degenerative lumbar spinal disease [34]. However, our study is distinguished by several critical elements that underscore its unique contribution. First, our study employed a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled design, providing a higher level of evidence than the single-arm cohort design used in the aforementioned investigation. More importantly, we focused on a fundamentally distinct patient population: individuals with CIF, who exhibit severe fat malabsorption. This pathophysiology mandates a high-dose intramuscular repletion strategy, contrasting sharply with the oral supplementation feasible in populations with normal absorptive capacity. Consequently, our findings offer direct and actionable evidence for managing vitamin D deficiency in malabsorptive conditions. Furthermore, while the previous study reported improved physical performance without significant gains in muscle strength [34], our trial demonstrated robust increases in both appendicular skeletal muscle mass and handgrip strength. This underscores the potent anabolic effect of rapid, high-dose intramuscular repletion in a deeply deficient and high-risk population. Our study provides novel, high-quality evidence to support a specific and effective short-term repletion protocol for patients with CIF.

The clinical benefits observed in this study were attributed to the ability of vitamin D2 to increase serum 25(OH)D levels. Following the intervention, a substantial proportion of patients (100.0%) in the vitamin D group achieved adequate 25(OH)D levels (≥ 30 ng/mL), compared with only 7.1% in the control group. Previous studies have demonstrated that high-dose intramuscular vitamin D supplementation can rapidly improve serum vitamin D levels in patients with various diseases and is both safe and effective [35, 36], which is consistent with the findings of the present study. In a post hoc analysis limited to patients with baseline 25(OH)D deficiency, a single high dose of vitamin D3 was effective in increasing 25(OH)D levels compared with placebo; however, no significant clinical improvement was reported [37], a finding that contrasts with the results of our trial. A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials has shown that vitamin D supplementation can improve muscle mass and function in patients with various medical conditions, and these findings are consistent with our study [38]. Previous studies have demonstrated that vitamin D status is associated with skeletal muscle function and quality of life in patients with CIF [6, 7, 39]. Our study demonstrates that high-dose intramuscular vitamin D2 supplementation rapidly increases 25(OH)D levels and is effective in improving muscle function and quality of life in patients with CIF. However, the therapeutic goal is to achieve and maintain sufficiency, not supraphysiological levels. The fact that 4.8% of patients exhibited levels > 120 ng/mL after repletion—a range that increases the risk of hypercalcemia if sustained—highlights a potential risk. It should be emphasized that this intensive regimen is for short-term repletion only. Long-term management must be individualized, employing lower maintenance doses guided by periodic serum 25(OH)D monitoring to sustain target levels and ensure safety.

The significant improvements in skeletal muscle mass, function, and quality of life observed in our trial can be attributed to several biological mechanisms. Vitamin D directly influences skeletal muscle by binding to the vitamin D receptor (VDR), which is highly expressed in muscle tissue, thereby regulating genes responsible for myocyte proliferation, differentiation, and protein synthesis [40, 41]. Furthermore, vitamin D deficiency is strongly associated with atrophy of type II (fast-twitch) muscle fibers, which are essential for strength and power [42]. Correcting this deficiency likely promoted the recovery of these fibers, explaining the marked improvements in handgrip strength and walking speed. In addition to these direct musculoskeletal effects, indirect systemic contributions, such as reduced chronic inflammation and potential benefits for neurological and mood function, may have synergistically enhanced both physical performance and self-reported quality of life.

Our findings indicate that the most immediate and pronounced benefits of correcting vitamin D deficiency in CIF patients are functional, particularly affecting skeletal muscle health and quality of life. In contrast, its impact on other parameters, such as bone mineral density, was not statistically significant, an expected finding given the slow pace of bone remodeling. An intriguing but underpowered trend toward a lower incidence of infections was observed, supporting the need for further research into the immunomodulatory effects of vitamin D in this population.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, the rigorous study design: as a prospective, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, it minimizes bias in outcome assessment while preserving real-world applicability. The low attrition rate (1.2%) further enhances the reliability of the findings. Second, the targeted population: the study specifically focuses on patients with CIF, a high-risk group for vitamin D deficiency and muscle dysfunction, thereby addressing a critical evidence gap in this under-researched population. Third, high-dose intramuscular vitamin D2 protocol: the intervention (600,000 IU every 2 weeks for 10 weeks) demonstrated rapid and robust correction of vitamin D deficiency, with 95.2% of treated patients achieving target 25(OH)D levels (30 ng/ml ≤ 25(OH)D < 120 ng/ml). This provides a practical and effective dosing strategy for clinicians. The safety of this intensive regimen (cumulatively 3 million IU over 10 weeks) is supported by three factors: no significant safety signals emerged in our trial; the intramuscular route provides slow release, reducing acute toxicity risk; and its prior success in other populations. This combination of high efficacy and a manageable safety profile supports the feasibility of this protocol as a practical short-term repletion strategy under appropriate monitoring. Fourth, comprehensive outcome measures: the study evaluated both biochemical (25(OH)D, PTH, calcium) and functional endpoints (muscle strength, walking speed, ASMI) alongside quality of life (SF-36), offering a holistic assessment of vitamin D’s clinical impact. Fifth, safety monitoring: rigorous monitoring for adverse events (e.g., hypercalcemia, fractures) confirmed the short-term safety of high-dose intramuscular vitamin D2, supporting its feasibility in clinical practice. Sixth, the study cohort included patients with CIF of various etiologies, predominantly short bowel syndrome. The robust improvements observed across the entire group suggest that vitamin D repletion is beneficial regardless of the underlying cause. This is likely attributable to the commonalities in pathophysiology—namely, vitamin D deficiency—across CIF subtypes. However, we cannot rule out that the magnitude of benefit might vary based on the severity of malabsorption or the presence of concomitant inflammation. Future studies with larger sample sizes should prospectively investigate whether treatment efficacy is modified by baseline vitamin D levels, CIF etiology, or the presence of sarcopenia, allowing for a more targeted therapeutic strategy. Finally, as the first randomized trial to assess intramuscular vitamin D2’s effects on muscle function and quality of life in CIF, it elevates evidence beyond observational data and expert opinion. These strengths underscore the study’s contribution to optimizing vitamin D repletion strategies in CIF and provide a foundation for future research.

This study also has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the short-term (12-week) nature of our study presents an important limitation: it is insufficient for evaluating the long-term sustainability of improvements in muscle function and quality of life, or for detecting potential late adverse effects. Consequently, the long-term safety and efficacy of this dosing regimen for indefinite use remain unknown, highlighting the need for future studies to determine the optimal maintenance therapy. Second, the heterogeneity in CIF etiology represents a limitation. While it improves generalizability, differences in underlying pathophysiology may influence individual treatment responses. As our trial was not powered for subgroup analyses, future large-scale studies should prospectively determine whether the efficacy of vitamin D2 is modified by the underlying cause, thereby paving the way for personalized therapeutic strategies. Third, the lack of positive controls limits comparisons to standard therapies (e.g., oral supplementation). Future studies should directly compare intramuscular vitamin D supplementation with enteral routes. Fourth, lack of mechanistic exploration: the study did not investigate molecular mechanisms (e.g., vitamin D receptor activity in muscle tissue) or potential confounders (e.g., physical activity levels, concurrent medications). Fifth, single-center design: conducted at one tertiary hospital, the findings may not be fully generalizable to broader CIF populations with varying healthcare access or nutritional practices. Sixth, potential over-supplementation risk: nearly 5% of treated patients exceeded 25(OH)D levels of 120 ng/mL, raising questions about the safety of prolonged high-dose regimens. Seventh, due to the 12-week duration of our study, the sustainability of the observed improvements beyond the intervention period cannot be determined. The long-term efficacy remains unknown and should be a focus of future research. Eighth, our study focused on total 25(OH)D as the primary biochemical outcome. Future mechanistic studies could build on this by profiling vitamin D metabolites, such as the vitamin D metabolite ratio (VMR), to provide a more comprehensive understanding of vitamin D homeostasis in this population. Finally, no assessment of muscle histology: advanced measures (e.g., muscle biopsies, electromyography) could have provided deeper insights into vitamin D’s impact on muscle physiology. These limitations highlight the need for larger, multicenter, placebo-controlled trials with extended follow-up to confirm these findings and refine clinical guidelines for vitamin D repletion in CIF.

Conclusions

The findings of this randomized clinical trial demonstrate that high-dose intramuscular vitamin D2 supplementation, administered every two weeks during a 10-week period, was significantly associated with increased skeletal muscle mass, improved muscle function, and enhanced quality of life in patients with chronic intestinal failure.
