On the kernel conditions of operators mapping atoms to molecules in local Hardy spaces
Chun Ho Lau, Claudio Vasconcelos

Abstract
. In this paper, we explore the relationship between the operators mapping atoms to molecules in local Hardy spaces h p ​ ( ℝ n ) h^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n}) and the size conditions of its kernel. In particular, we show that if the kernel of a Calderón–Zygmund-type operator satisfies an integral-type size condition and a T ∗ − T^{*}- type cancellation, then the operator maps h p ​ ( ℝ n ) h^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n}) atoms to molecules. On the other hand, assuming that T T is an integral type operator bounded on L 2 ​ ( ℝ n ) L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}) that maps atoms to molecules in h p ​ ( ℝ n ) h^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n}) , then the kernel of such operator satisfies the same integral-type size conditions. We also provide the L 1 ​ ( ℝ n ) L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}) to L 1 , ∞ ​ ( ℝ n ) L^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}) boundedness for such operators connecting our integral-type size conditions on the kernel with others presented in the literature.

1.
Introduction

The real variable theory of Hardy spaces can be traced back to the work of Fefferman and Stein in
[
8
]
, in which they provided different characterizations of the real Hardy space, denoted by
H^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for
p>0
, using maximal functions and Poisson integrals. These spaces coincide with Lebesgue spaces when
p>1
and for some applications it provides a suitable substitute for
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
when
0<p\leq 1
since Hardy spaces have nontrivial dual characterizations. Furthermore, when
0<p\leq 1
, tempered distributions belonging to
H^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
can be decomposed in terms of bounded compactly supported functions
a
satisfying vanishing moment conditions, that is

(1.1)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}a(x)x^{\alpha}dx=0,\quad\forall\,|\alpha|\leq N_{p}:=\lfloor n(1/p-1)\rfloor.

This decomposition was established for
H^{p}(\mathbb{R})
by Coifman
[
1
]
and generalized for any dimension by Latter
[
12
]
. It turned out to be very useful, since many properties of the space can be reduced by studying it over the atoms. For instance, it is well known that the boundedness of Calderón-Zygmund type operators can be established by proving that the image of the operator by an atom is uniformly bounded in the
H^{p}-
norm. Since atoms have themselves uniformly bounded norm in
H^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, one may try to show that the operator maps atoms into atoms, which is not the case since it may not preserve the compact support. This led to the study of a more general decomposition of Hardy spaces, called molecular decomposition, in which the hypothesis of compact support is relaxed.

The first discussion on molecular decompositions of Hardy spaces, using functions that are no longer compactly supported but satisfy certain Lebesgue norm size conditions and vanishing moments, was established by Taibleson and Weiss
[
18
]
. This new decomposition were extensively used over the years to show the boundedness of certain classes of linear operators on
H^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, since its boundedness follows by showing that the operator maps atoms into molecules.

Even though the classical Hardy spaces are useful, they still have some inconveniences. For instance, due to the cancellation conditions inherent in the Hardy space, they are not stable under multiplications by cutoff functions and the class of Schwartz functions
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is not contained in
H^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Motivated by this, in
[
10
]
Goldberg introduced a localized version of Hardy spaces, called local Hardy spaces and denoted by
h^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, for
p>0
. As desired,
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\subset h^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for all
0<p<\infty
and
h^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is stable under multiplication by a smooth cutoff function. This localization property allows the extension of local Hardy spaces in different settings as manifolds. From a comparison between
H^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
h^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
(see
[
10
]
*Lemma 4) one can get an analogous atomic decomposition for
h^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, except that vanishing moment conditions (
1.1
) are required only for atoms supported in balls with small radii, which we call local vanishing moment condition.

It turned out that this local vanishing moment condition is not necessarily required. For instance, in
[
6
]
, Dafni introduced atoms in
h^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, for
0<p\leq 1
, replacing the local vanishing moment condition by a control of the absolute value of their moments by the factor
r^{\beta}
, where
r
is the radius of the ball in which the support of the atom is contained and
\beta>0
. Later, Komori
[
11
]
showed that for
\frac{n}{n+1}<p<1
, one just needs to bound the absolute value of the moment by a constant; however, this is not the case when
p=\frac{n}{n+k}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
(see the
[
2
, Example 3.4]
). For the particular case
p=1
, Dafni and Yue in
[
5
]
showed an atomic decomposition for
h^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with atoms with moments being bounded above by
[\log(1+r^{-1})]^{-1}
. Later, motivated by the boundedness of inhomogeneous Calderón-Zygmund operators in
h^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the authors in
[
2
]
extended these ideas and introduced approximate atoms and molecules for all
0<p\leq 1
. In particular, they showed an atomic decomposition with atoms satisfying the inhomogeneous cancellation condition

(1.2)

\displaystyle\left|\int_{B(x_{B},r)}{a(x)(x-x_{B})^{\alpha}dx}\right|\leq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}C&\quad\text{if }|\alpha|<\gamma_{p},\\
&\\
\left[\log\left(1+\dfrac{C}{r}\right)\right]^{-\frac{1}{p}}&\quad\text{if }|\alpha|=N_{p}=\gamma_{p},\end{array}\right.

where
C>0
is a constant and
B(x_{B},r)
is the smallest ball containing the support of
a
.

To be more precise of operators we mentioned before, a Calderón–Zygmund singular integral operator is an
L^{2}
-bounded operator formally given by

Tf(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}K(x,y)f(y)dy,\quad\forall\,x\notin\supp(f)

where the kernel
K
is a continuous function away from the diagonal satisfying certain size conditions. It is well known that if
K
satisfies the Hörmander condition,
i.e.
,

\sup_{y\in B}\int_{(2B)^{c}}|K(x,y)-K(x,c(B))|dx<\infty,\quad\text{for all balls $B$},

then
T
is bounded on
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for
1<p<2
, bounded from
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
to
L^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and from
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
to
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, see
[
15
]
*Chapter I Section 5 and Chapter III Section 3.1. However, Hörmander condition is not enough to conclude that
T
is bounded on
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, even if
T^{*}(1)=0=T(1)
, as shown in
[
19
]
. The boundedness of
T
, under the condition that
T^{*}(1)=0
, on
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is guaranteed considering kernels satisfying the classical Hölder regularity

(1.3)

\displaystyle|K(x,y)|\leq C|x-y|^{-n}\ \ \text{and}\ \ |K(x,y)-K(x,z)|\leq C\frac{|y-z|^{\delta}}{|x-y|^{n+\delta}}

whenever
2|y-z|\leq|x-y|
, or some other weaker integral Hörmander-type conditions presented in
[
14
]
. However, such operator
T
associated with
K
would not necessarily be bounded on
h^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
nor from
h^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
to
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
(consider, for instance, the Hilbert transform on
\mathbb{R}
and the characteristic function on
[0,1]
). An alternative for local Hardy spaces is to consider inhomogeneous Calderón-Zygmund singular integral operators, introduced by
[
7
]
. The main difference between this latter and the classical one is that the kernel of the former has a stronger decay far away from the diagonal, namely

(1.4)

|K(x,y)|\leq C\min\{|x-y|^{-n},|x-y|^{-n-\mu}\},\quad\forall\,x\neq y\text{ and some $\mu>0$}.

Hence, the class of inhomogeneous Calderón–Zygmund singular integral operators is smaller than the class of Calderón–Zygmund singular integral operators. This kind of operator includes some of the pseudo-differential operators, see
[
2
,
7
]
. Then, if
T
is an inhomogeneous Calderón–Zygmund singular integral operator, the authors in
[
2
,
3
]
found necessary and sufficient conditions for the boundedness of such operators on
h^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for all
0<p\leq 1
, improving and extending the work
[
7
]
.

The core of proving the boundedness of a Calderón–Zygmund singular integral operator
T
on Hardy spaces or local Hardy spaces is to show that
T
maps atoms to molecules. This strategy works perfectly in
H^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, under the assumption that the operator satisfies
T^{*}(x^{\alpha})=0
, i.e.,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}Ta(x)x^{\alpha}dx=0,\quad\forall f\in L^{2}_{N_{p}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\text{ and }|\alpha|\leq N_{p}.

This condition is actually necessary and sufficient for the boundedness of such operators in
H^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. However, a local version of such cancellation condition on
T
is too strong in the local setting (for instance, for pseudodifferential operators). The key to find less restrictive necessary and sufficient conditions for the boundedness of inhomogeneous Calderón-Zygmund operators in the local setting, was to consider approximate atoms and molecules. To do this, if
a
is an atom in
h^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we show that only the conditions imposed on the kernel imply that
Ta
satisfy the size conditions of the molecule. Then, the additional cancellation condition that
T^{*}((\cdot-c)^{\alpha})
satisfy a local Campanato-type estimate will show the approximate moment condition of the molecule (see
[
2
]
*Section 5). On the other hand, if we suppose that
Ta
satisfies the size condition of molecules, then
T^{*}((\cdot-c)^{\alpha})
must satisfy the local Campanato type conditions, see
[
3
]
*Theorem 2. In a more general setting, the authors in
[
3
, Theorem 1]
also proved the necessity of this local Campanato condition for genereral linear and bounded operators in
h^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
that maps atoms into
pseudo-molecules
, a generalization of
pre-molecules
, that will be defined in Definition
3
.

Motivated by these results, the goal of this paper is to further investigate this relation between the size conditions of the kernel and the property of the operator to map atoms to molecules. This work is inspired by a related question answered in the setting of the homogeneous Triebel-Lizorkin spaces
\dot{F}^{\alpha,q}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
1<p,q<\infty
in
[
9
]
, which is related to Hardy spaces since
H^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\dot{F}^{0,2}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
h^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})={F}^{0,2}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for all
0<p\leq 1
, where
F^{\alpha,q}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
denotes the inhomogeneous Triebel-Lizorkin spaces. In particular, the authors showed in Theorem 1.16 that a linear operator
T
which is continuous from
C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with mean 0 to the distribution spaces mapping certain smooth atoms to smooth molecules, then the Schwartz kernel of the restriction on
C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
of the extension of
T
on
\dot{F}^{\alpha,q}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
indeed satisfies the classical Calderón-Zygmund estimates
|K(x,y)|\leq C|x-y|^{-n}
and
|K(x,y)-K(x,z)|\leq C|y-z|^{\delta}|x-y|^{-n-\delta}
whenever
2|y-z|\leq|x-y|
. However, the necessary conditions on the kernel of an operator to map atoms to molecules for (local) Hardy spaces are unclear.

Background and notation

Throughout this paper, we denote by
q^{\prime}
the Hölder conjugate of
q
,
i.e.

q^{-1}+(q^{\prime})^{-1}=1
, and
B(c,r)
the ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
centered at
c\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with radius
r>0
. Also, the constants
C
,
C^{\prime}
, etc., in the proofs may vary from line to line, and we use the subscript to highlight the dependence of the variables in the constants. By
\mathbb{N}
we mean the positive integers.

We start with the formal maximal definition of the Hardy space
h^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Definition 1
.

Let
p\in(0,\infty)
. We say that the tempered distribution
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
belongs to the local Hardy space
h^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
if there exists
\Phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
satisfying
\int\Phi\neq 0
such that

\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}f(x)=\sup_{0<t<1}|\Phi_{t}\ast f(x)|\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

where
\Phi_{t}(x):=t^{-n}\Phi(xt^{-1})
.

The functional
\|f\|_{h^{p}}=\big\|\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}f\big\|_{L^{p}}
defines a quasi-norm in
h^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
when
0<p<1
and a norm otherwise. We may refer it as a norm for convenience. The associated distance
d(f,g)=\|f-g\|_{h^{p}}^{p}
defines a metric on
h^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Next we provide the definition of
(h^{p},q)
atoms.

Definition 2
.

Let
0<p\leq 1\leq q\leq\infty
with
p\neq q
. We say the function
a
is an
(h^{p},q)
exact atom if there exists a ball
B=B(c,r)
such that

(1)

\supp(a)\subset B
,

(2)

\|a\|_{L^{q}(B)}\leq|B|^{\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{p}}
, and

(3)

\displaystyle\int_{B}a(x)(x-c)^{\alpha}dx=0
for all
|\alpha|\leq N_{p}
if
0<r<1
,

where
N_{p}:=\lfloor n(p^{-1}-1)\rfloor
, the largest possible integer that is at most
\gamma_{p}:=n(p^{-1}-1)
. If Condition
(3)
is replaced by (
1.2
), we call it an
(h^{p},q)
approximate atom.

Meanwhile, the molecular theory for
h^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with non-exact cancellation is studied in
[
11
]
for
\frac{n}{n+1}<p<1
and
h^{1}(\mathbb{R})
in
[
4
]
. In
[
2
]
, the authors studied the approximated molecular theory for
h^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for all
p\leq 1
. We now give the definitions of approximate molecules.

Definition 3
.

Let
0<p\leq 1\leq q<\infty
with
p\neq q
and
\lambda>n(\frac{q}{p}-1)
. We say that a function
M
is an
(h^{p},q,\lambda,C)
approximate molecule if there exist a ball
B=B(c,r)
and
C>0
(independent of
M
) such that

(M_{1})

\|M\|_{L^{q}(B)}\leq Cr^{n(\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{p})}
,

(M_{2})

\|M|\cdot-c|^{\frac{\lambda}{q}}\|_{L^{q}(B^{c})}\leq Cr^{\frac{\lambda}{q}+n(\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{p})}
, and

(M_{3})

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}{M(x)(x-c)^{\alpha}dx}\right|\leq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}C,&\quad\text{if }|\alpha|<\gamma_{p},\\
&\\
\left[\log\left(1+\dfrac{1}{Cr}\right)\right]^{-\frac{1}{p}},&\quad\text{if }|\alpha|=N_{p}=\gamma_{p}.\end{array}\right.

Following
[
3
]
*Definition 3, when the function
M
satisfies only the size conditions (
M_{1}
) and (
M_{2}
), we call it a pre-molecule.

We shall note that it is not harmful to change
B
in (
M_{1}
) and (
M_{2}
) by
kB
for some
k>1
(independent of
B
) or to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Moreover, the constant

can be written as
\lambda=n(\frac{q}{p}-1)+\varepsilon
for some
\varepsilon>0
and the upper bound of (2) becomes

.

In this paper, we only use the decomposition of
h^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
in terms of exact atoms and approximate molecules. More precisely, we will use the following.

Theorem A

(
[
10
,

]
)
.

Let
0<p\leq 1\leq q\leq\infty
with

and

(1)

The distribution

(2)

for all
|\alpha|\leq N_{p}
. Then

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|x-c|^{\alpha}|T(g)(x)|dx<\infty\quad\text{for all $|\alpha|\leq N_{p}$.}

Proof.

Note that from the
L^{2}-
boundedness of
T
we have

\int_{2B}|x-c|^{\alpha}|T(g)(x)|dx\leq C_{n}r^{\alpha+\frac{n}{2}}\|Tg\|_{L^{2}}\leq C_{n}r^{\alpha+\frac{n}{q}}\|T\|_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}\|g\|_{L^{q^{\prime}}}<\infty.

It remains to estimate the integral on
(2B)^{c}
. Note that, using the cancellation condition of
g
,

\displaystyle\int_{(2B)^{c}}|x-c|^{\alpha}|T(g)(x)|dx\leq\int_{(2B)^{c}}|x-c|^{|\alpha|}\int_{B}|K(x,y)-P_{K,B}(x,y)||g(y)|dydx

\displaystyle\leq|B|^{\frac{1}{q^{\prime}}}\|g\|_{L^{q^{\prime}}}\int_{(2B)^{c}}|x-c|^{\alpha}\|K(x,y)-P_{K,B}(x,y)\|_{L^{q}(B,dy/|B|)}dx

\displaystyle\leq|B|^{\frac{1}{q^{\prime}}}\|g\|_{L^{q^{\prime}}}\bigg\||x-c|^{n(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{s})+\frac{\varepsilon}{s}}\|K(x,y)-P_{K,B}(x,y)\|_{L^{q}(B,\frac{dy}{|B|})}\bigg\|_{L^{s}((2B)^{c},dx)}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\times\||\cdot-c|^{-n(p^{-1}-s^{-1})-\frac{\varepsilon}{s}+|\alpha|}\|_{L^{s^{\prime}}((2B)^{c})}.

Note that
-n(s^{\prime})(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{s})-\frac{s^{\prime}\varepsilon}{s}+s^{\prime}|\alpha|+n=-ns^{\prime}(\frac{1}{p}-1)-\frac{s^{\prime}\varepsilon}{s}+s^{\prime}|\alpha|<0
if and only if
|\alpha|\leq n(p^{-1}-1)+\frac{\varepsilon}{s}
, which is the case because
|\alpha|\leq N_{p}
. Therefore, the above estimate is bounded by a function of
r
and is therefore finite.
∎

is similar to the one in
[

]
*Theorem 5.3, for the sake of completeness, we will give the proof here.

Proof of Theorem

.

Let

3.2.
Proof of Theorem

The first main step in the proof of Theorem

is to describe a suitable decomposition of the kernel of

, following the analogous ideas presented in
[

*Theorem 1.16 and

]

Lemma 1
.

There exists a sequence
\{\psi_{j}\}_{j}\subset C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
of radial functions supported in
B(0,1)
such that
\int\psi_{0}=1
,
\int\psi_{j}=0
for all
j\geq 1
and

(3.1)

\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\widehat{\psi_{j}}(\xi)=1.

Moreover, if
K_{j}
denotes the kernel of
T(\cdot\ast\psi_{j})
, then
K_{j}(x,y)=T(\psi_{j}(\cdot-y))(x)
and the kernel of the operator
T|_{C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
is given by
K(x,y)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}K_{j}(x,y)
in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Proof.

We start by constructing the functions
\{\psi_{j}\}_{j}
. Let
\Phi
be a non-negative function in
C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
\supp(\Phi)\subset B(0,{1}/{2})
,
\int\Phi=1
and
\int\Phi(x)x^{\alpha}dx\neq 0
for
1\leq|\alpha|\leq 2N_{p}
. Define

\psi_{0}(x)=c_{0}\Phi(x)-\sum_{1\leq|\alpha|\leq N_{p}}c_{\alpha}x^{\alpha}\Phi(x),

for some suitable constants
c_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\int x^{\alpha}\psi_{0}(x)dx=0
for all
1\leq|\alpha|\leq N_{p}
and
\int\psi_{0}(x)dx=1
. Then, define
\psi(x)=2^{n}\psi_{0}(2x)-\psi_{0}(x)
and let
\psi_{j}(x)=2^{(j-1)n}\psi(2^{j-1}x)
for any
j\in\mathbb{N}
. By the translation property of the Fourier transform we get
\widehat{\psi_{j}}(\xi)=\widehat{\psi_{0}}(2^{-j}\xi)-\widehat{\psi_{0}}(2^{1-j}\xi)
. In this way,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{N}\widehat{\psi_{j}}(\xi)=\widehat{\psi_{0}}(2^{-N}\xi)\rightarrow\widehat{\psi_{0}}(0)=1\ \ \text{as $N\rightarrow\infty.$}

For
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
we have
f=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}f\ast\psi_{j}
in
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and from (
3.1
) and Plancherel identity it also holds in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Moreover,

(3.2)

T(f)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}T(f\ast\psi_{j})\quad\text{in $\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})$ and $L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})$.}

If
K_{j}
denotes the kernel of
T(\cdot\ast\psi_{j})
, simply by the integral representation of
T
we have that
K_{j}(x,y)=T(\psi_{j}(\cdot-y))(x)
and from (
3.2
), together with the
L^{2}
continuity of
T
, the kernel of
T
is given by
K=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}K_{j}
in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Since
K
is the kernel of
T
and such decomposition is true in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
, we have from (
3.2
)

\displaystyle\langle\langle K-\sum_{j=1}^{N}K_{j},\phi\otimes f\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle T(f)-\sum_{j=1}^{N}T_{j}(f),\phi\rangle\rightarrow 0,

where
\langle\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\rangle
denotes the paring between
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the paring between
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Since the tensor product is dense in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we have the convergence in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
∎

Since we want to establish estimates for
K_{j}(x,y)
, in which the construction was described in the previous lemma, the next step is to provide additional information on the family
\{\psi_{j}\}_{j}
, which is useful in Proposition
2
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
0<p\leq 1
and
\{\psi_{j}\}_{j}\subset C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
the family of functions constructed in the proof of Lemma
1
. Then the following are true:

(1)

The function
x\mapsto\displaystyle\frac{\psi_{0}(x)}{\|\psi_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}\,|B(0,2)|^{\frac{1}{p}}}
is an
(h^{p},\infty)
exact atom supported in
B(0,2)
;

(2)

For
N\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, the function
x\mapsto\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{\beta}\psi_{j}(x)}{2^{(j-1)(|\beta|+n)}\,(2^{n}+1)\,\|\psi_{0}\|_{C^{N}}\,|B(0,2^{-j+1})|^{\frac{1}{p}}}
is an
(h^{p},\infty)
exact atom supported in
B(0,2^{-j+1})
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
and all multi-indices
\beta
with
|\beta|\leq N
, where

\|\psi\|_{C^{N}}:=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,\max_{|\beta|\leq N}|\partial^{\beta}\psi(x)|.

(3)

If
|y-c|\leq\frac{2^{-j+1}}{3}
, then

(3.3)

\displaystyle A_{j}(x):=\psi_{j}(x-y)-\psi_{j}(x-c)-\sum_{1\leq|\alpha|\leq N_{p}}\frac{(-1)^{|\alpha|}}{\alpha!}\left(\partial^{\alpha}\psi_{j}(x-c)\right)(y-c)^{\alpha},

is also a constant multiple (with constant
c_{p,n}\,2^{j(N_{p}+1-\gamma_{p})}|y-c|^{N_{p}+1}
) of
(h^{p},\infty)
atoms supported in
B(c,2^{-j+2}).

Proof.

The proof of (1) is immediate. We show the others.

(2)

By construction,
\supp(\psi)\subset B(0,1)
and
\supp(\partial^{\beta}\psi_{j})\subset B(0,2^{-j+1})
. A direct calculation shows that

\displaystyle\bigg\|\frac{\partial^{\beta}\psi_{j}}{2^{(j-1)(|\beta|+n)}(2^{n}+1)\|\psi_{0}\|_{C^{N}}|B(0,2^{-j+1})|^{\frac{1}{p}}}\bigg\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\leq\frac{2^{(j-1)(|\beta|+n)}(2^{n}+1)\|\psi_{0}\|_{C^{N}}}{2^{(j-1)(|\beta|+n)}(2^{n}+1)\|\psi_{0}\|_{C^{N}}|B(0,2^{-j+1})|^{\frac{1}{p}}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad=|B(0,2^{-j+1})|^{-\frac{1}{p}}

for any
j\in\mathbb{N}
and
|\beta|\leq N
. To verify the cancellation condition, it suffices to check it for
\psi_{j}
. Suppose first
|\beta|=0
, and note that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}2^{(j-1)n}\psi_{j}(x)dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\psi(x)dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}[2^{n}\psi_{0}(2x)-\psi_{0}(x)]dx=0.

For
1\leq|\alpha|\leq N_{p}
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}x^{\alpha}2^{(j-1)n}\psi_{j}(x)dx

\displaystyle=2^{-(j-1)|\alpha|}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}x^{\alpha}\psi(x)dx

\displaystyle=2^{-(j-1)|\alpha|}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(2^{-n|\alpha|}-1)x^{\alpha}\psi_{0}(x)dx

\displaystyle=0.

For
1\leq|\beta|\leq N
, the cancellation conditions hold because

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}x^{\alpha}\partial^{\beta}\psi_{j}(x)dx

\displaystyle=(-1)^{|\beta|}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\partial^{\beta}(x^{\alpha})\psi_{j}(x)dx=0

and
\partial^{\beta}(x^{\alpha})
is again a monomial with non-negative power.

(3)

Let
0<|y-c|\leq\frac{2^{-j+1}}{3}
.
Note that
\supp(A_{j})\subset B(y,2^{-j+1})\cup B(c,2^{-j+1})\subset B(c,2^{-j+2})
. By Taylor’s theorem, there exists
v=ty+(1-t)c
with
t\in[0,1]
such that

\displaystyle A_{j}(x)=\sum_{|\alpha|=N_{p}+1}\frac{(-1)^{|\alpha|}}{\alpha!}\big(\partial^{\alpha}[\psi_{j}(x-v)]\big)(y-c)^{\alpha}.

Then,

\displaystyle\|A_{j}\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{|\alpha|=N_{p}+1}\frac{1}{\alpha!}c_{n}2^{(j-1)(N_{p}+1+n)}\|\psi_{0}\|_{C^{N_{p}+1}}|y-c|^{N_{p}+1}

\displaystyle\leq c_{p,n}2^{(j-1)(N_{p}+1+n)}\|\psi_{0}\|_{C^{N_{p}+1}}|y-c|^{N_{p}+1}.

Meanwhile, the cancellation conditions hold because of a change of variable and the fact that
\int x^{\beta}\partial^{\alpha}\psi_{j}=0
for all
|\beta|\leq N_{p}
. Therefore,
A_{j}[c_{p,n}2^{(j-1)(N_{p}+1+n)}\|\psi_{0}\|_{C^{N_{p}+1}}|y-c|^{N_{p}+1}|B(0,2^{-j+2})|^{p^{-1}}]^{-1}
is an
(h^{p},\infty)
exact atom.

∎

Finally, in the next proposition, we show some important estimates on the kernels

when

maps atoms into pre-molecules.

Proposition 2

Proof.

Let

,

\displaystyle\int_{|x-y|\geq 2^{-j+1}}

\displaystyle|x-y|^{n\left(\frac{q}{p}-1\right)+\varepsilon}|T(\tilde{\psi_{j}}(\cdot-y))(x)|^{q}dx\leq 2^{(-j+1)\varepsilon}

(3.5)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\int_{|x-y|\geq 2^{-j+1}}\bigg(\frac{|x-y|}{2^{-j+1}}\bigg)^{n(\frac{q}{p}-1)+\varepsilon}|T(\psi_{j}(\cdot-y))(x)|^{q}dx\leq C_{n,p,q}2^{(j-1)n(q-1)}.

3.8
) we get

\displaystyle\int_{|x-c|\geq 4r}\fint_{|y-c|\leq r}\bigg(\frac{|x-c|}{r}\bigg)^{n\left(\frac{q}{p}-1\right)+\varepsilon}|T(A_{j})(x)|^{q}dydx

\displaystyle\leq C_{n,p,q,\varepsilon}\fint_{|y-c|\leq r}\int_{|x-y|\geq 3r}\bigg(\frac{|x-y|}{r}\bigg)^{n\left(\frac{q}{p}-1\right)+\varepsilon}|T(A_{j})(x)|^{q}dxdy

\displaystyle\leq C_{n,p,q,\varepsilon}\bigg(\fint_{|y-c|\leq r}\int_{3r\leq|x-y|\leq 2^{-j+2}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+\fint_{|y-c|\leq r}\int_{|x-y|\geq 2^{-j+2}}\bigg)\bigg(\frac{|x-y|}{r}\bigg)^{n\left(\frac{q}{p}-1\right)+\varepsilon}|T(A_{j})(x)|^{q}dydx

\displaystyle\leq C_{n,p,q,\varepsilon}^{\prime}\bigg(\frac{2^{-j+2}}{r}\bigg)^{n\left(\frac{q}{p}-1\right)+\varepsilon}2^{(j-2)[q(N_{p}+1)+(q-1)n]}r^{q(N_{p}+1)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{n,p,q,\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}(2^{-j})^{q\gamma_{p}+\varepsilon-q(N_{p}+1)}r^{q(N_{p}+1)-n(\frac{q}{p}-1)-\varepsilon}.

After taking
q
-th root and summing over
j
s, we have the upper bound
C_{n,p,q,\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}r^{n(q^{-1}-1)}
using the fact that
\varepsilon<q(N_{p}+1)-q\gamma_{p}
.
∎

We now are ready to prove the main theorem.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

We may assume that
\varepsilon<s(N_{p}+1-\gamma_{p})
since an
(h^{p},s,n(\frac{s}{p}-1)+\varepsilon,C)
molecule is also an
(h^{p},s,n(\frac{s}{p}-1)+\varepsilon^{\prime},C)
molecule for any
\varepsilon^{\prime}<\varepsilon
. Moreover, since
T
maps
(h^{p},q^{\prime})
exact atoms to
(h^{p},s,n(sp^{-1}-1)+\varepsilon,C)
approximate molecules, we can express

P_{K,B}(x,y)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}P_{K_{j},B}(x,y)=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq N_{p}}\frac{1}{\alpha!}F_{\alpha,c,r}(x)(y-c)^{\alpha}

in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
, where
F_{\alpha,c,r}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}M_{j}
, with

\lambda_{j}=2^{(j-1)|\alpha|}2^{(j-1)n}(2^{n}+1)\|\psi_{0}\|_{\mathcal{S}}|B(0,2^{-j+1})|^{\frac{1}{p}}

and
M_{j}(x)=\lambda_{j}^{-1}T[(-1)^{|\alpha|}\partial^{\alpha}\psi_{j}(\cdot-c)](x)
.

For this fixed ball
B=B(c,r)
, we will show that
F_{\alpha,c,r}(x)

is a function when
x\notin B
and is in
L^{s}(B^{c})
. For simplicity, we write
\lambda=n(\frac{s}{p}-1)+\varepsilon
. By the definition of molecules, note that if
2^{-j+1}<r
,

\|M_{j}\|_{L^{s}(B^{c})}\leq r^{-\lambda/s}\|M_{j}|\cdot-c|^{\lambda/s}\|_{L^{s}(B^{c})}\leq Cr^{-\frac{\lambda}{s}}2^{(-j+1)(\frac{\lambda}{s}-n(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{s}))}

and if
2^{-j+1}\geq r
(only finitely many
j
s), then

\displaystyle\|M_{j}\|_{L^{s}(B^{c})}

\displaystyle\leq\|M_{j}\|_{L^{s}(B^{c}\cap B(c,2^{-j+1}))}+\|M_{j}\|_{L^{s}([B(c,2^{-j+1})]^{c})}

\displaystyle\leq C2^{(j-1)n(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{s})}+Cr^{-\frac{\lambda}{s}}2^{(-j+1)(\frac{\lambda}{s}-n(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{s}))}.

Then, since
\frac{\lambda}{s}=n(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{s})+\frac{\varepsilon}{s}>n(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{s})
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}\|M_{j}\|_{L^{s}(B^{c})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j:2^{-j+1}\leq r}\lambda_{j}\|M_{j}\|_{L^{s}(B^{c})}+\sum_{j:2^{-j+1}\geq r}\lambda_{j}\|M_{j}\|_{L^{s}(B^{c})}

\displaystyle\leq C_{n,p,s,\varepsilon}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}r^{-\frac{\lambda}{s}}2^{(-j+1)(\frac{\lambda}{s}-n(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{s})+\gamma_{p}-|\alpha|)}+C_{n,p,s,\varepsilon}\sum_{j:2^{-j+1}\geq r}2^{(-j)n(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{s})}

\displaystyle\leq C_{n,p,q,\varepsilon}(r^{-\frac{\lambda}{s}}+r^{-n\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{s}\right)})<\infty.

Therefore, for fixed
B=B(c,r)
,
F_{\alpha,c,r}(x)
is indeed a function when
|x-c|\geq r
, and therefore
P_{K,B}(x,y)
is a function when
|x-c|\geq r
. Finally, the desired estimate follows from Proposition
2
.

If
N_{p}\neq\gamma_{p}
, note that

\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}^{p}\simeq_{n,p,s}\sum_{j}(2^{-j})^{p(n/p-n-|\alpha|)}\simeq_{n,p,s}\sum_{j}(2^{-j})^{p(\gamma_{p}-|\alpha|)}<\infty

because
|\alpha|\leq N_{p}<\gamma_{p}
, and
M_{j}
is an approximate molecule by Lemma
2
and the hypothesis. Therefore, we can conclude that
F_{\alpha,c,r}(x)\in h^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
∎
