The role and mechanism of estrogen/estrogen receptor α in sexual identity determination of chicken somatic cells
Authors: Zidi Jin, Xiao Lin, Xiaolin Wu, Kang Wen, Shuo Li, Amaal Omara, Jing Ge, Haizhou Gong, Minmeng Zhao, Jun Zhang, Daoqing Gong, Long Liu, Tuoyu Geng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Unlike mammals, where systemic sex hormones determine somatic sexual identity, chickens primarily employ a cell-autonomous mechanism, known as the cell autonomous sexual identity (CASI) hypothesis. However, tissue sensitivity to sex hormones varies significantly. This study aimed to elucidate the specific role of the estrogen/estrogen receptor α (ERα) system in this context. We assessed protein levels of key sex hormone receptors, including ERα, estrogen receptor β (ERβ), and androgen receptor (AR), in the tissues of 6-week-old and adult chickens. Results showed that ERα protein was markedly abundant in hormone-sensitive tissues (comb and wattles) but low in insensitive ones (muscle and liver); this pattern was not observed for ERβ or AR. Functionally, in the absence of estrogen, ERα overexpression in ERα-deficient primary muscle cells affected immunity but not sexual differentiation-related pathways. In contrast, analysis on the transcriptomes of ERα-rich wattle tissues indicated that the genes differentially expressed between estrogen-treated versus untreated groups were enriched in the pathways critical for development, neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction, and glycerolipid metabolism to establish sexual phenotype. In conclusion, estrogen regulates somatic sex identity via ERα in a tissue-specific manner, with regulatory intensity dictated by local ERα abundance. This provides a crucial supplement to the CASI hypothesis.

Introduction

The chicken is an important economic animal. Its production traits are closely associated with sex. For instance, only females lay eggs. For production purposes, single-sex individuals are preferred because this improves production efficiency and animal welfare. Elucidating the mechanism of sex determination may lay a solid foundation for sex control. Moreover, the chicken is also an important model animal. Deciphering the mechanism of its sex determination will provide insights into the evolution of animal sex.

In avian species, including chickens, sex is determined by a ZW-type sex chromosome system (male: ZZ, female: ZW), which differs from the XY system in mammals (male: XY, female: XX). In mammals, gonadal sex is determined by the SRY gene on the Y chromosome. Its early expression initiates gonadal development toward testes, while its absence leads to ovarian development (Goodfellow and Lovell-Badge, 1993). Furthermore, the sexual phenotype of somatic cells (the secondary sex characteristics) is influenced by sex hormones produced by the gonads (Marshall and Tanner, 1969; Shah and Maibach, 2001; Imperato-McGinley and Zhu, 2002; Hughes et al., 2012). Estrogen secreted by ovarian tissue promotes female secondary characteristics, whereas androgen from testis promotes male secondary characteristics. In avian species, several key genes involved in gonadal sex determination and differentiation have been identified. These include genes associated with ovarian development, such as FOXL2 (Loffler et al., 2003), CYP19A1 (Jin et al., 2020), and RSPO1 (Smith et al., 2008), and genes associated with testicular development, such as DMRT1 (Hirst et al., 2017), SOX9 (Kent et al., 1996), and AMH (Nishikimi et al., 2000). The DMRT1 gene, located on the Z chromosome, has been demonstrated to be a crucial testis-determining gene; its dosage in the chicken embryo determines whether the early gonads differentiate into testes or ovaries (Ioannidis et al., 2021).

Regarding the mechanism determining the sexual phenotype of somatic cells in avian species, the currently widely accepted hypothesis is the cell autonomous sex identity (CASI) mechanism. The CASI hypothesis was initially proposed by Zhao et al. based on their study of three naturally occurring gynandromorphic chickens (Zhao et al., 2010). They compared the sex chromosome composition of the cells on the male and female sides of the chickens using in situ hybridization assays and constructed chimeric gonads via early mesoderm transplantation in chicken embryos. Evidence suggests that chicken somatic cells can autonomously determine their own sex identities. In other words, the sexual phenotypes of somatic tissues in gynandromorphic chickens are almost independent of systemic sex hormones but instead correlated with the sex chromosome type of the cells within the tissues. On this basis, Clinton et al. proposed that the sexual phenotype differences in chicken somatic cells are caused by the sex-related differential expression of genes on sex chromosomes (Clinton et al., 2012). These genes may include the female-specific genes on the W chromosome and genes with no dosage compensation effects on the Z chromosome.

Substantial evidence supports the existence of the CASI mechanism. In gynandromorphic chickens, even though the blood hormone levels are identical on both sides of the body, the wattle on the male side remains significantly larger than that on the female side (Zhao et al., 2010). Ioannidis et al. generated chickens with a hemizygous deletion of DMRT1 on the Z chromosome (ZDMRT1-) using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene editing (Ioannidis et al., 2021). In these chickens, individuals with genotypes ZDMRT1+ZDMRT1- and ZDMRT1-W all developed ovarian gonads. However, the ZDMRT1+ZDMRT1- individuals exhibited male phenotypes in their combs, wattles, and spurs, with body weights comparable to normal roosters, whereas the ZDMRT1-W individuals displayed phenotypes and body weights similar to normal hens. These findings indicate that although DMRT1 dosage influences gonadal differentiation, changes in gonadal type and sex hormones do not affect the secondary sexual characteristics of ZZ-male individuals. Furthermore, castrated roosters, despite a lack of systemic androgens, largely retain a male appearance and maintain a body weight equivalent to or even greater than that of normal roosters (the potential increase in body weight is possibly attributable to excessive abdominal fat) (Fennell and Scanes, 1992; Gesek et al., 2017). Additionally, treating chicken embryos with fadrozole before sexual differentiation can cause masculinization of the gonads in female embryos by inhibiting cortical development. Nevertheless, the growth and development of pectoral muscle in these sex-reversed females remained largely unaffected (Burke and Henry, 1999).

However, certain phenomena regarding the sexual phenotypes (secondary sexual characteristics) of chicken somatic cells cannot be fully explained by the CASI mechanism. Some studies indicate that the secondary sex characteristics are influenced by both CASI and sex hormones. Lambeth et al. created ZZ individuals with undetectable testosterone but highly elevated estradiol levels by knockin-based overexpression of the CYP19A1 gene (Lambeth et al., 2016). These individuals exhibited female characteristics in their combs, tail feathers, and wattles, but they developed rooster-specific spurs with body weights comparable to those of normal roosters. These findings suggest that in ZZ individuals overexpressing CYP19A1, a high systemic level of estradiol can induce female characteristics in some somatic tissues, while other somatic tissues maintain male characteristics. This indicates differential sensitivity to sex hormones across tissues. Furthermore, when early ovarian tissue is transplanted into the gonadal position of castrated roosters, the ovaries can develop and grow normally. These genetically male individuals, possessing functional ovaries, display female phenotypes in their combs, wattles, and feathers, yet maintain male characteristics in body weight and spurs (Goodale, 1918). In summary, tissues such as the comb, wattles, and tail feathers are regulated by both the CASI mechanism and sex hormones, whereas tissues like muscle and spurs are primarily controlled by CASI and are largely unaffected by sex hormones. To date, the scientific explanation for this tissue-specific differential response remains incomplete.

Based on these findings, we propose the following hypothesis: the differential responsiveness of various tissues to sex hormones is primarily determined by differences in the abundance of sex hormone receptors across tissues. The higher the abundance of sex hormone receptors in a tissue, the more sensitive the tissue is to sex hormones. One objective of this study is to address this hypothesis, which may provide a supplement to the CASI hypothesis.

Estrogens and androgens are the primary sex hormones. Testosterone, an androgen, can be converted into estrogen by aromatase. Both androgens and estrogens exert their regulatory effects through their respective receptors. The common sex hormone-binding receptors in chickens include the androgen receptor (AR), the estrogen receptors (estrogen receptor α (ERα) and estrogen receptor β (ERβ)). Previous research has revealed that estrogen effects can be genomic and non-genomic (Curtis Hewitt et al., 2000; Kong et al., 2003; Björnström and Sjöberg, 2005; Magnani and Lupien, 2014; Gupte et al., 2015; Hamilton et al., 2017; Klinge, 2017; Fuentes and Silveyra, 2019; Biason-Lauber and Lang-Muritano, 2022). The genomic effect primarily occurs when estrogen binds to estrogen receptors (ERα and ERβ), forming a complex that then binds to the estrogen response element (ERE) in the promoter sequence of target genes. This complex recruits co-activators or co-repressors to regulate the transcription of target genes. The genomic effect typically takes hours or even days to manifest. In contrast, the non-genomic effect of estrogen is mainly mediated by its binding to membrane-associated receptors (ERs or GPER), which usually involves the cascade activation of various protein kinases. The non-genomic effect generally occurs within seconds or minutes. The mechanism of action of the androgen receptor is similar to that of the estrogen receptor. It is currently known that the sex hormone/sex hormone receptor system (particularly the estrogen/ERα system) plays a crucial role in the differentiation of gonads in chicken embryos. However, its role and mechanism in the formation of secondary sexual characteristics in chickens have been less extensively studied. Another objective of this study is to address the mechanism underlying the role of sex hormone receptors that are crucial to the secondary sexual characteristics of chicken somatic tissues.

Materials and methods

All animal experimental procedures in this study complied with relevant guidelines for animal welfare and use, and were approved by the Animal Welfare and Use Committee of Yangzhou University (SYXK(SU)2021-0020).

Collection of tissues from normal adult chickens

Eight healthy adult chickens (four males and four females) were selected from a commercial Hy‑Line White flock at 50 weeks of age. All birds were raised under standard management conditions and euthanized by CO₂ asphyxiation. For each tissue type (pectoral muscle, liver, gonad, comb, and wattle), samples were collected from the same anatomical location. All samples were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C.

Sex hormone injection experiment

Sixty Hy-Line White commercial chicks of similar body weight from the same hatch were raised under standard conditions until 2 weeks of age. Twenty healthy male and twenty healthy female chicks (sex determined by cloacal sexing) were then selected for the hormone injection experiment. Male chicks were randomly divided into two groups of 10 birds each: a control group (injected with saline + DMSO) and a hormone-treated group (injected with β-estradiol + DMSO). Similarly, female chicks were randomly divided into a control group (injected with saline + DMSO) and a hormone-treated group (injected with testosterone + DMSO). Starting at 2 weeks of age, each chicken received an intramuscular injection of either saline or the respective sex hormone every two days. The dosage of sex hormone was 5 mg/kg body weight (determined by preliminary experiments; Supplementary Fig. 1), and injections continued until 6 weeks of age. All experimental chickens were housed together in the same pen with raised wire floors, with ad libitum access to feed and water throughout the injection period. The injected β-estradiol (E8140, Solarbio, Beijing, China) and testosterone (T8600, Solarbio, Beijing, China) were dissolved in DMSO (D8371, Solarbio, Beijing, China). Prior to the end of the injection experiment, chickens were fasted for 6 h, then weighed, and their combs and wattles were photographed and measured. Chickens were euthanized by CO₂ asphyxiation. Pectoral muscle and abdominal fat were dissected and weighed. Tissue samples collected from the same spot of each tissue (pectoral muscle, liver, gonad, comb, and wattle) were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C.

Collection of tissues from normal chicken embryos

Sixty fertilized eggs of similar size from the Hy-Line White commercial line were incubated under standard conditions. On days 6 and 12 of incubation, embryos were decapitated, and gonadal tissues were collected, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C.

Fadrozole treatment of chicken embryos

Thirty fertilized eggs of similar weight from the Hy‑Line White commercial line were incubated under standard conditions. At 2.5 days of incubation, the eggs were removed from the incubator and randomly divided into a control group and a treatment group. After disinfecting the shell surface with 75% ethanol, a small window was opened at the blunt end of each egg. Each embryo in the treatment group was injected with 0.2 mg of fadrozole (HY-14247A, MCE, NJ, USA) dissolved in 100 μL sterile PBS; the control group received 100 μL sterile PBS only. Then, 50 μL of a 100 × Penicillin-Streptomycin solution (15140122, Gibco, NY, USA) in PBS was dropped into each egg, and the window was sealed with medical tape. Eggs continued to hatch until embryonic day 12 (E12), at which time gonadal tissues were collected, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C.

PCR-based sex identification

Sex identification was performed using MightAmp™ DNA Polymerase Ver.2 (R071A, TaKaRa, Tokyo, Japan) with primers targeting the chicken CHD1 gene according to the manufacturer's instructions (Ellegren, 1996).

Isolation and culture of chicken primary muscle cells

Ten fertilized eggs of similar size were selected from the Hy-Line White commercial line were incubated under standard conditions. Primary muscle cells were isolated from E12 embryos. Briefly, muscle tissues were rinsed twice with PBS, cut into small pieces, and digestion with pre-warmed trypsin (25200072, Gibco, NY, USA) in a 37°C-water bath. After complete digestion, DMEM (11965092, Gibco, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (A5256701, Gibco, NY, USA) was added to the cells. Cells were pelleted by centrifugation, rinsed once with PBS, resuspended in complete culture medium (DMEM, 10% FBS, 50 × Penicillin-Streptomycin), filtered through a 40-μm cell strainer, counted, and seeded into 12-well plates at an appropriate density. Plates were placed in a 37°C, 5% CO₂ incubator.

Overexpression of ERα in chicken primary muscle cells

The coding sequence (CDS) of chicken ERα (GenBank: NM_205183.2) was amplified by RT‑PCR from chicken ovarian cDNA. EcoRI and HindIII restriction sites were introduced at both ends. The PCR product and pcDNA3.1(+) vector were double‑digested and ligated, then transformed into DH5α competent cells. Expression of the exogenous sequence in pcDNA3.1(+) is driven by the CMV promoter and terminated by the bGH poly(A) signal. The recombinant plasmid (named pcDNA3.1‑ERα) was verified by DNA sequencing and then used in subsequent experiments. Chicken primary muscle cells were transfected with pcDNA3.1‑ERα or empty vector using Lipofectamine™ 2000 (11668500, Invitrogen, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Culture medium was replaced 6 h post-transfection, and cells were cultured continuously in a 37°C, 5% CO₂ incubator. Cells were harvested 24 h after transfection.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from tissues using TRNzol Universal reagent (DP424, Tiangen, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using a commercial kit (R233-01, Vazyme, Nanjing, China). All quantitative PCR (qPCR) primers were designed using Primer Premier 5.0 software and synthesized by Genewiz (Suzhou, China). Primer sequences are listed in Table 1. Quantitative PCR was performed using a commercial kit (11202ES03, Yeasen, Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer's protocol. Protein extraction and Western blot

Total protein was extracted from tissues using RIPA lysis buffer (C1035, APPLYGEN, Beijing, China). Protein concentration was determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (P0010, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Immunoblotting assays were performed as previously described (Zhou et al., 2024). Briefly, protein samples were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes, blocked, and incubated with specific primary antibodies at 4°C overnight, followed by incubation with corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Immunoblots were developed using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit, and images were acquired. The antibodies used in this study, including ERα antibody (20698-1-AP), ERβ antibody (14007-1-AP), and GAPDH antibody (60004-1-Ig), were purchased from Proteintech Group (Wuhan Sanying Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). AR antibody (AB52615) was purchased from Abcam (Shanghai) Trading Co., Ltd., and Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (CW0103S) and Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (CW0102S) were purchased from Jiangsu Cowin Biotech Co., Ltd.

Transcriptome sequencing analysis

Transcriptome sequencing was performed by Shanghai Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd. as previously described (Zhou et al., 2024). Briefly, total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent, followed by DNase I treatment to remove genomic DNA. RNA integrity was assessed with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, only samples with RNA integrity number (RIN) ≥ 7.0 were used. Subsequently, mRNA was enriched using oligo(dT) magnetic beads, fragmented, and used for cDNA synthesis. After adapter ligation, sequencing libraries were constructed and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (paired‑end 150 bp).

Raw data were quality filtered, and clean reads were then aligned to the chicken reference genome (GRCg6a) using HISAT2. Gene expression quantification was performed with featureCounts. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 with thresholds of |log₂(Fold Change)| > 1 and FDR < 0.05. Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment and KEGG pathway analyses were performed on the DEG sets.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in the R programming environment. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Data were assessed for normal distribution (Shapiro‑Wilk test) and homogeneity of variances (Bartlett's test). Comparisons between two groups were made using independent samples t-test (parametric) or Mann-Whitney U test (non‑parametric). For multiple groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's HSD post-hoc test (parametric) or Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn's post-hoc test (non‑parametric) was used. Significance was set at p < 0.05. Figures were generated using the ggplot2 package in R.

Differential response of chicken tissues to exogenous hormones

Resulting changes in comb and wattle size at 6 weeks of age are shown in Fig. S1. Based on these results, a dosage of 5 mg/kg body weight was selected for the formal experiment.

In the formal experiment, hens were injected with testosterone and roosters with β-estradiol starting at 2 weeks of age. After 4 weeks of treatment, comb size, wattle size, body weight, pectoral muscle weight, and abdominal fat weight were measured. The comb and wattle sizes of testosterone-treated hens were significantly larger than those of the control hens, and their colors resembled that of control roosters. Conversely, β-estradiol treatment in roosters significantly reduced the sizes of their combs and wattles, and the colors became similar to those of control hens (Fig. 3). Discussion

Previous studies have established that estrogen and ERα play a crucial role in the feminization of chicken gonads (Clinton and Haines, 2001; Lambeth et al., 2013). The findings of this study are consistent with this conclusion. First, at E6 (prior to gonadal sex differentiation), no significant difference in either ERα mRNA expression or ERα protein expression was detected between male and female gonads. However, at E12 (after sex differentiation), a significant difference in both ERα mRNA expression and ERα protein expression was observed, with higher levels in female gonads than in males. Second, after inducing sex reversal in female chicken embryos by fadrozole treatment before gonadal differentiation, ERα protein content in the embryonic gonads was significantly reduced. The mechanism by which fadrozole induces gonadal sex reversal in female embryos involves blocking androgen-to-estrogen conversion (Burke and Henry, 1999; Vaillant et al., 2001, 2003; Lin et al., 2021). These results indicate that the sexual identity of female chicken embryonic gonads is influenced by estrogen and its receptor ERα at the protein level. Based on these findings, we propose that the sexual identity of chicken somatic cells is also closely related to sex hormones and the protein levels of their receptors.

The comb and wattles exhibit pronounced sexual dimorphism, whereas the differences in pectoral muscle and liver between sexes are less distinct. Studies have indicated that androgens can promote comb growth and maintain its size and structure (Yoshioka et al., 2010). Indeed, this study found that administering testosterone to 6-week-old hens gradually induced masculinized characteristics in the comb and wattles. Conversely, injecting β-estradiol into 6-week-old roosters caused the combs and wattles to progressively exhibit feminized characteristics. However, neither androgen nor estrogen significantly altered body weight, pectoral muscle weight, or pectoral muscle percentage (without abdominal fat weight) — with the latter calculated after removing abdominal fat weight to reflect true changes, given that sex hormone treatment significantly increased abdominal fat deposition. This indicates that muscle tissue is relatively insensitive to sex hormones. Previous research has shown that castrated roosters achieve a body weight comparable to that of intact roosters by adulthood (Fennell and Scanes, 1992; Gesek et al., 2017), and genetically male chickens with ovaries also reach a body weight similar to normal roosters (Ioannidis et al., 2021). This phenomenon, where the sexual phenotype of muscle tissue shows insensitivity to sex hormone stimulation, can be explained by the CASI mechanism (Zhao et al., 2010): the sexual phenotype of chicken muscle tissue is determined by the sex chromosome composition of its cells. Interestingly, both androgen and estrogen treatments significantly increased abdominal fat deposition. This is consistent with previous reports have indicated that β-estradiol promotes abdominal fat accumulation in animals (Chen et al., 2014). The increase in abdominal fat following testosterone injection in hens may be due to conversion of some testosterone into estradiol by aromatase (Elbrecht and Smith, 1992).

To elucidate why combs and wattles are sensitive to sex hormones while muscle tissue is not, this study examined and compared the expression levels of ERα, ERβ, and AR in the gonads, pectoral muscle, wattles, liver, and comb from both 6-week-old and adult male and female chickens. As hypothesized, ERα protein abundance was significantly higher in hormone‑sensitive somatic tissues than in insensitive ones. However, expression of ERβ and AR did not follow this pattern. Therefore, the sensitivity of somatic tissues to sex hormones primarily depends on ERα protein abundance, not on ERβ or AR. In summary, the sexual identity of hormone-insensitive muscle is primarily determined by the cell's sex chromosome composition – i.e., the CASI mechanism – and is minimally influenced by sex hormones. In contrast, the sexual identity of hormone-sensitive tissues like comb and wattles is not only governed by CASI but is also significantly modulated by the sex hormone/sex hormone receptor system, with high ERα expression being key: This conclusion provides a complementary perspective to the theory that chicken somatic tissue sex identity is determined solely by CASI. Regarding the observation that androgen injection induced masculinization of comb and wattles in hens, a possible explanation is that androgen exerts their masculinizing effect independent of AR. For instance, androgen may activate masculinization-related genes while suppressing feminization-related genes. However, further investigation is required to address the mechanism underlying the role of sex hormone receptors in somatic sex identity determination. This would necessitate the use of poultry models with tissue-specific knockout or overexpression of these receptors, which is now achievable through significant advances in avian genome-editing technology.

To investigate whether differences in ERα protein content directly cause sexual phenotype differences across tissues, we overexpressed ERα in chicken primary muscle cells and analyzed transcriptome changes. The results showed that DEGs were primarily associated with immune defense responses, with no significant link to sex phenotype determination (Fig. 5B-C). This indicates that ERα alone, in the absence of estrogen co-activation, cannot independently induce the sexual phenotype differences observed between tissues.

Furthermore, transcriptome analysis on the wattle tissues of male vs. female chickens and the estrogen-treated vs. untreated roosters revealed that estrogen influences pathways related to development, receptor signaling, neuroactive ligand-receptor interactions, and glycerolipid metabolism (Fig. 6A-B). These changes ultimately lead to phenotypic differences in tissue sexual characteristics. This demonstrates that the differential expression of ERα across tissues requires the synergistic action of estrogen to drive the formation of distinct sexual phenotypes.

Conclusion

This study reveals that ERα protein content is high in sex hormone-sensitive non-gonadal somatic tissues such as the comb and wattles, whereas ERα protein content remains low in sex hormone-insensitive non-gonadal somatic tissues like muscle and liver. The expression patterns of ERβ and AR differ from that of ERα across various non-gonadal somatic tissues, and their protein levels are not associated with the sex hormone sensitivity or sex phenotype. Thus, ERα protein abundance is a key determinant of sex hormone sensitivity and sex phenotypes in these tissues. The estrogen/ERα system likely participates in determining the sexual phenotype of non-gonadal somatic tissues by regulating pathways related to development, metabolism, and signal transduction. These findings provide an important supplement to the CASI hypothesis, which posits that the sexual phenotype of avian somatic cells is determined by sex chromosome type/genetic composition.
