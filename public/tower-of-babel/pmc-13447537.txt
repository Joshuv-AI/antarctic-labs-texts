Enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) for elective glioma craniotomy: a propensity score-matched retrospective cohort study
Authors: Rachakorn Punyasiri, Anukoon Kaewborisutsakul, Thakul Oearsakul, Thara Tunthanathip, Chin Taweesomboonyat, Chanatthee Kitsiripant, Tulaya Dissaneewate, Chaitong Churuangsuk, Sakchai Sae-heng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) has demonstrated benefits in various surgical specialties, evidence supporting its application in elective glioma craniotomies remains limited. This study compared the institutional ERAS pathway with conventional perioperative care in adults undergoing elective craniotomy for a single cerebral glioma. This retrospective cohort study (2019–2024) at a Thai university hospital enrolled patients aged ≥ 15 years with histologically confirmed gliomas. Propensity score matching (PSM; 1:1) addressed measured baseline confounding, and inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) served as a sensitivity analysis. The primary outcomes were total hospital length of stay (LOS), intensive care unit (ICU) LOS, and postoperative LOS. Of 153 eligible patients (ERAS, n = 58; conventional, n = 95), PSM yielded 53 matched pairs. The ERAS group had shorter median total (9 vs. 12 days; p = 0.004) and postoperative (7 vs. 9 days; p = 0.037) LOS, with equivalent ICU LOS, hospitalization costs, and complication rates. The results of the IPTW analysis were directionally consistent. In multivariable logistic regression analyses, 2-hour preoperative carbohydrate loading (adjusted odds ratio [95% confidence interval] 5.98 [1.08–33.02], p = 0.040) and early oral fluid intake within postoperative 8 h (5.74 [1.22–26.96], p = 0.027) independently predicted a short postoperative LOS (< 7 days). Thus, ERAS was associated with shorter hospital stay without increased complications. Preoperative carbohydrate loading and early oral fluid intake were independently associated with a short postoperative LOS, identifying them as high-priority targets for a pragmatic, stepwise implementation strategy for ERAS in elective glioma craniotomies. Introduction

Diffuse gliomas are the most prevalent malignant primary brain tumors in adults, and glioblastomas, the most aggressive subtype, continue to have a grave prognosis [1]. Despite meaningful advances in surgical techniques, intraoperative neuromonitoring, and multimodal adjuvant therapy, including concurrent radiotherapy and temozolomide chemotherapy, the median overall survival remains at approximately 11–15 months [2, 3]. The burden borne by these patients extends well beyond survival statistics: perioperative morbidity is substantial, hospitalization is prolonged, and postoperative quality of life is frequently compromised. These challenges are amplified in resource-constrained settings, where access to specialized neurosurgical and neurocritical care, together with the timely delivery of adjuvant treatment, may be inconsistent [4–6].

Enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) is a structured evidence-based perioperative care pathway that seeks to attenuate surgical stress and accelerate functional recovery through a coordinated bundle of interventions spanning the preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative phases [7, 8]. Originally developed for colorectal surgery, the ERAS principles have since been translated into an expanding range of surgical subspecialties [9]. However, in neurosurgery and particularly in elective cranial tumor surgery, the evidence base remains relatively limited, and a universally accepted procedure-specific ERAS protocol has not yet been established [10, 11]. Existing systematic reviews and meta-analyses suggest that ERAS may offer benefits to patients undergoing elective craniotomy, including a reduction in hospital stay, lower costs, and improved postoperative symptom control, without a discernible increase in short-term adverse events [12–14]. Nevertheless, the available evidence is heterogeneous and may not translate readily across different tumor pathologies.

At our institution, an ERAS pathway for elective brain tumor surgery was introduced in a progressive rather than uniformly comprehensive fashion. The pathway was informed by early feasibility and safety studies conducted at other centers and then adapted to the local workflow, staffing structures, and available resources [15–17]. In routine clinical practice, the adoption of a multidisciplinary perioperative protocol is constrained by the need for cross-team coordination, variable clinician engagement, and uncertainty about local benefit. Therefore, a staged implementation strategy prioritizing potentially high-impact elements may represent a pragmatic approach, consistent with evidence suggesting that higher adherence to ERAS pathways is associated with improved outcomes [17, 18].

Accordingly, we conducted a retrospective cohort study to compare our institutional ERAS pathway with conventional perioperative care in adults undergoing elective craniotomy for a single cerebral glioma. To address the measured baseline confounding inherent to the nonrandomized design, we applied propensity score-based methods [19] to analyze outcomes related to length of stay (LOS), hospitalization costs, and postoperative complications. We additionally explored which individual ERAS elements were independently associated with shorter hospitalization with the aim of informing prioritization during early-stage implementation.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at a tertiary referral university hospital in Thailand. Eligible patients were identified from the institutional database between January 2019 and June 2024. During the study period, the institutional ERAS pathway was applied selectively according to clinical feasibility and the preferences of the attending surgeon. The patients who did not undergo ERAS were managed using conventional perioperative care. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review board (REC.67-470-10-4), and the requirement for individual informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective observational design.

Participants and eligibility criteria

All consecutive patients who underwent elective craniotomy for a single cerebral glioma at our institution during the study period were assessed for eligibility. Patients were eligible if they were aged 15 years or older and had a histologically confirmed glioma on postoperative pathological examination. Patients were excluded if they underwent emergency craniotomy, were moribund, had uncontrolled comorbidities precluding perioperative optimization (including uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, ischemic heart disease, advanced hepatic or renal failure, or sepsis), were pregnant, had severe psychiatric illness or cognitive impairment limiting perioperative cooperation, had preoperative paralysis with complete functional dependence, or had incomplete medical records.

ERAS protocol

The institutional ERAS pathway for elective glioma craniotomy comprises 26 perioperative elements organized into preoperative, perioperative, and postoperative phases (Fig. 1).

Components of the ERAS protocol for elective glioma surgery. ERAS, enhanced recovery after surgery; NPO, nil per os; NSAID, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug; VTE, venous thromboembolism; wk, week

Preoperative phase. Preoperative elements included preoperative counseling and education, smoking and alcohol cessation (≥ 4 weeks) when applicable, nutritional assessment and supplementation when indicated, co-morbidity assessment and optimization, postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) risk assessment and prophylaxis, preoperative chlorhexidine body wash, and preoperative chlorhexidine mouthwash.

Perioperative phase. Perioperative elements included shortened preoperative fasting (nil per os [NPO], 6–8 h), preoperative carbohydrate loading (2 h before surgery), preemptive acetaminophen administration, hypothermia prevention, goal-directed fluid restriction, antibiotic prophylaxis (within 60 min before incision), antiseizure prophylaxis when indicated, minimal hair removal, scalp block, awake craniotomy when clinically appropriate, and no wound drainage.

Postoperative phase. The postoperative elements included scheduled postoperative acetaminophen use, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) use when appropriate, venous thromboembolism evaluation and prophylaxis, early oral fluid intake (< 8 h), early solid food intake (< 24 h), early urinary catheter removal (< 24 h), early mobilization (< 24 h), discharge care plan, and follow-up.

Adherence assessment

Adherence was assessed at the level of individual ERAS elements and recorded as achieved or not achieved in each patient. In the adherence calculation of elements applicable only under specific clinical circumstances (e.g., smoking cessation, antiseizure prophylaxis, and awake craniotomy), inapplicability was distinguished from nonadherence. The overall adherence of each patient was calculated as the proportion of completed elements among all applicable elements.

Data sources and variables

Data were extracted from the electronic inpatient and outpatient medical records and hospital information systems. Baseline variables included age; sex; body mass index (BMI); American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification; Apfel PONV risk score; preoperative Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS); tumor recurrence status; eloquent tumor location; and histopathological subtype, including glioblastoma status. The operative variables included operative duration, estimated blood loss, and transfusion requirements. The histopathological diagnosis was confirmed by postoperative pathological examination.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were total hospital LOS, intensive care unit (ICU) LOS, and postoperative LOS. Secondary outcomes included total hospitalization costs, postoperative clinical outcomes, and functional outcomes. Postoperative clinical outcomes included 30-day mortality, unplanned 30-day readmission, reoperation, surgical site infection, postoperative seizure, pneumonia, urinary tract infection, deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, PONV, pain intensity measured using a visual analog scale, and total opioid (fentanyl) consumption. Functional outcomes were assessed using the KPS preoperatively, at discharge, and at 1 and 6 months postoperatively; deaths occurring before a given time point were coded as a KPS score of 0. In addition, a short postoperative LOS, defined as a postoperative LOS < 7 days, was analyzed as a binary outcome to identify individual ERAS elements independently associated with shorter hospitalization.

Propensity score methods

Propensity score matching (PSM) was applied to address the measured baseline confounding arising from the nonrandomized allocation of patients to the ERAS pathway. The propensity score, representing the conditional probability of receiving ERAS, was estimated using a multivariable logistic regression model incorporating prespecified pretreatment covariates: age, sex, BMI, preoperative KPS score, ASA class, Apfel PONV risk score, tumor recurrence status, eloquent tumor location, and glioblastoma status.

Patients were matched 1:1 within propensity score strata of width 0.1 using random sampling from the larger group within each stratum; strata without patients in either treatment arm were excluded. A bin width of 0.1 was selected to ensure adequate stratum size while maintaining acceptable within-stratum balance. No additional caliper restriction was applied beyond the stratum boundaries, as the bin width inherently constrained the matching of patients with similar propensity scores. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) were calculated to assess covariate balance before and after matching; SMD values below 0.2 were considered indicative of acceptable balance. Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) using stabilized weights was performed as a sensitivity analysis for the original (unmatched) cohort. The treatment effects in the IPTW-weighted cohort were estimated using weighted linear regression for continuous outcomes with robust variance estimation.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or as median with minimum, 25th percentile, 75th percentile, and maximum values according to distributional characteristics. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. Between-group comparisons were performed using Student’s t-test for normally distributed continuous variables, the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed continuous variables, and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value of less than 0.05. Univariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to evaluate the association between individual ERAS elements and a short postoperative LOS (< 7 days). ERAS elements with identical adherence rates across both groups were excluded from the regression analyses to avoid noninformative estimates. Variables with p < 0.10 in univariable analysis or considered clinically relevant were subsequently entered into a multivariable logistic regression model to estimate adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

All analyses were performed using Stata/BE software version 17.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Patient selection and PSM

A total of 153 adult patients who underwent elective craniotomy for a single cerebral glioma during the study period were eligible for analysis. Of these, 58 patients were managed under the ERAS pathway, and 95 received conventional perioperative care. After 1:1 subclassification matching across propensity score strata, 106 patients remained in the matched cohort, with 53 patients allocated to each group; 5 patients from the ERAS group and 42 from the conventional group were excluded due to unmatched strata (Fig. 2).

Sensitivity analysis using IPTW

IPTW analyses in the full unmatched cohort corroborated the findings in the PSM cohort, with ERAS remaining independently associated with shorter total LOS (β = −4.84 days, 95% CI − 7.80 to − 1.87; p = 0.002) and shorter postoperative LOS (β = −3.64 days, 95% CI − 6.32 to − 0.96; p = 0.008). The ICU stay and hospitalization costs did not differ significantly, which is consistent with the results of the primary analysis.

Adherence to ERAS elements

Table 5 summarizes the adherence to individual ERAS elements in both PSM groups. Preoperative carbohydrate loading was administered to all ERAS patients and to none in the conventional group (p < 0.001). Scalp blocks (77.36% vs. 50.94%, p = 0.008) and awake craniotomies (41.51% vs. 9.43%, p < 0.001) were significantly more frequent in the ERAS group. Early solid food intake within 24 h was more common in the ERAS group (62.26% vs. 35.85%, p = 0.011). Other elements, including early mobilization and urinary catheter removal, did not differ significantly between the PSM groups.

Functional outcomes

Functional outcomes are shown in Supplementary Table 2. The median KPS score was 80 in both PSM groups at all time points except at discharge, where the ERAS group showed a marginally lower median KPS score (median [interquartile range]: 70 [60–90] vs. 80 [60–90], p = 0.563), most likely reflecting an earlier discharge rather than a genuine functional deficit. The KPS score recovered to 80 in both groups by 1 month and remained stable at 6 months.

Discussion

Randomized evidence for ERAS in glioma surgery remains limited, as most comparative data originate from nonrandomized studies. In this propensity score-matched cohort study, ERAS was associated with shorter total and postoperative LOS without an apparent increase in postoperative morbidity, reoperation, or readmission. The total hospitalization cost was higher in the ERAS group, but this difference was not significant. These findings were corroborated by IPTW analyses in the fully unmatched cohort, supporting the robustness of the primary results.

The published ERAS literature on elective glioma craniotomy remains relatively limited and has been generated predominantly from centers in China and the United States, with comparatively sparse evidence from other healthcare settings, including Southeast Asia [12, 14, 15, 20]. ERAS is considerably more established in degenerative spine surgery, where procedure-specific consensus recommendations for lumbar spinal fusion have already been published and are supported by structured evidence appraisal and a defined set of perioperative elements [21]. In cranial surgery, however, ERAS pathways remain less standardized, and broadly adopted procedure-specific guidance for craniotomies is yet to be established. Earlier framework studies proposed candidate ERAS elements for oncological craniotomies and highlighted the need for stronger procedure-specific evidence [20, 22]. More recently, randomized trials of elective craniotomy have demonstrated its feasibility and short-term safety, reporting a shorter postoperative LOS and lower hospital costs without increased readmission or reoperation [15, 23, 24]. However, many individual ERAS elements in elective craniotomy still have low-to-moderate certainty of evidence, and only a small number of components are supported by high-quality data, constraining the strength of element-specific recommendations [14].

Meta-analyses of elective craniotomies have generally associated ERAS with LOS reductions of approximately 2–3 days and, in many reports, with lower hospitalization costs without a clear increase in overall complication rates [12, 13]. Our observed LOS reductions were broadly consistent with these meta-analytic estimates. ERAS protocols vary substantially across studies, adherence is measured inconsistently, and outcome definitions are not standardized, which limits the generalizability of protocol-specific findings across centers [14, 18]. In this context, propensity-based analyses provide clinically useful comparative evidence. However, residual unmeasured confounders cannot be fully excluded, and the findings should be interpreted accordingly [19].

The ERAS group incurred numerically higher total hospitalization costs, a pattern corroborated by IPTW analyses (β = +9,978, 95% CI − 22,757 to 42,714 [in ฿]), which appears to contradict both the observed LOS benefit and cost savings reported in prior meta-analyses [12, 13]. Rather than indicating a true economic disadvantage, this pattern reflects the front-loading cost structure embedded in the ERAS pathway. Adherence data (Table 5) identify several high-cost drivers concentrated in the ERAS group: awake craniotomy, which entailed prolonged operative time and additional neuromonitoring resources; scalp block; universal preoperative carbohydrate loading; and more frequent NSAID use.

In Thai public hospital settings, the cost-offset capacity of LOS savings is structurally constrained. According to the national standard fee schedule, inpatient accommodation (comprising room occupancy, meals, and nursing services) costs approximately ฿1,400–4,000 per day [25]. In contrast, the average daily inpatient expense is substantially higher in Western healthcare systems, estimated at $3,297 in the United States [26] and ranging from £395 to £536 in the United Kingdom [27]. Consequently, the accommodation savings attributable to the observed three-day reduction in total LOS amount to only ฿4,200–12,000 per patient, insufficient to offset the upfront expenditure embedded in the pathway. This cost dynamic is unlikely to be unique to our setting and may help explain why cost-neutrality is more readily demonstrated in high-income healthcare systems, where bed-day costs are an order of magnitude greater. Activity-based cost accounting that disaggregates operative, pharmaceutical, and accommodation expenditures is required to fully characterize the cost implications of ERAS implementation in this setting [28].

Craniotomy induces a neuroendocrine and inflammatory stress response that carries a substantial physiological burden [29, 30]. Patients often experience pain, nausea, sleep disturbances, fatigue, and cognitive symptoms in the postoperative period, and functional recovery is frequently delayed compared to other elective surgical procedures [31]. ERAS aims to mitigate this burden through a coordinated bundle of perioperative measures so that perioperative recovery milestones (mobilization, resumption of oral intake, symptom control, and discharge readiness) can be achieved earlier than with conventional care [32, 33]. In glioma surgery, earlier recovery carries implications beyond discharge timing alone, as a prompt restoration of the functional status may facilitate the timely initiation of adjuvant chemoradiotherapy, potentially reducing treatment delays [34]. Conversely, long-term functional recovery in gliomas is predominantly determined by tumor biology and molecular profile, tumor location and eloquence, extent of resection, baseline performance status, and the effects of adjuvant treatment [35]. This multifactorial framework explains the observed dissociation between a shorter LOS and preserved long-term KPS score in our cohort. The marginally lower KPS score at discharge in the ERAS group most likely reflected earlier discharge rather than inferior functional recovery, given that the KPS score converged to 80 in both groups by 1 month.

The surgical technique is integral to the ERAS framework because the degree of operative invasiveness directly shapes the tissue injury and subsequent recovery trajectory. While awake craniotomy is frequently used for tumors involving the eloquent cortex to preserve neurological function and is often hypothesized to accelerate postoperative recovery, objective evidence supporting its role in accelerating recovery remains limited. In our cohort, awake craniotomy was performed significantly more often in the ERAS group but was not identified as an independent predictor of shorter LOS in multivariable analyses, despite being associated with a longer operative time. This finding is consistent with that of a recent study evaluating a modified ERAS protocol for awake craniotomy, which similarly reported no significant reduction in LOS [36]. Whether awake craniotomy confers a true independent recovery benefit within an ERAS pathway or whether any apparent advantage chiefly reflects patient selection and local discharge practices remains to be established.

Many neurosurgical ERAS studies have omitted the social determinants of health, which limit our understanding of whether the observed benefits are consistent across varying socioeconomic contexts [37]. This omission is clinically relevant, as discharge timing is shaped not only by clinical recovery but also by logistical and social circumstances outside the hospital [7]. Beyond socioeconomic factors, effective ERAS implementation faces multidisciplinary organizational barriers to routine clinical practice [13, 14]. Adoption requires coordinated efforts from neurosurgery, anesthesia, nursing, and hospital administration. Teams may resist altering established practices related to ICU use, perioperative routines, or discharge thresholds. In low- and middle-income settings, additional barriers, including resource limitations, health literacy constraints, and travel distance from tertiary referral centers, may further compromise protocol adherence and limit equitable access to ERAS benefits [6, 10].

Against this backdrop, a stepwise adoption strategy may be more feasible than attempting a comprehensive full-protocol implementation from the outset. In our cohort, preoperative carbohydrate loading and early oral fluid intake within 8 h were independently associated with short postoperative LOS in multivariable analyses. Therefore, a focused bundle centered on perioperative nutritional support and early resumption of oral intake represents a pragmatic starting point, with gradual expansion of the pathway as institutional experience, multidisciplinary engagement, and local capacity gradually develop. This recommendation is biologically plausible in the context of glioma surgery, in which perioperative corticosteroid administration and surgical stress may contribute to insulin resistance and catabolism, making nutritional optimization a particularly relevant and actionable target throughout the perioperative period [32, 38]. This approach is also consistent with evidence demonstrating a dose–response relationship between ERAS adherence and clinical outcomes, suggesting that even partial implementation of high-impact elements may yield meaningful benefits [17].

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, the retrospective single-center design introduced an inherent selection bias and limited external validity. ERAS elements were applied selectively according to the preferences of the attending surgeon, introducing the risk of confounding indications. Although PSM and IPTW were employed in tandem to address measured confounders, residual unmeasured confounders cannot be fully excluded, particularly with respect to surgeon experience, patient frailty, functional trajectory, and tumor characteristics not captured in clinical records, such as the extent of resection and molecular profile [19]. Furthermore, surgeons who adopted the ERAS pathway may have differed systematically from those who did not in terms of perioperative care philosophy and attention to patient optimization, introducing a form of provider-level confounding that propensity score methods cannot fully address.

Second, the modest sample size is an important methodological constraint. A post-hoc power analysis based on the observed difference in total LOS (9 vs. 12 days; pooled SD 5.5 days; Cohen’s d ≈ 0.55) estimated that the matched cohort of 53 patients per group provided approximately 82% power to detect this medium-sized effect at a two-sided α of 0.05, indicating that the primary LOS comparison was adequately powered. However, the multivariable logistic regression model identifying element-level predictors of short postoperative LOS was based on 28 outcome events among 106 matched patients across six candidate variables, yielding an events-per-variable ratio (EPV) of approximately 4.7, which is below the conventionally recommended threshold of 10. At this EPV, the model is susceptible to overfitting, and element-level OR estimates carry substantial uncertainty, as evidenced by wide CIs (e.g., for preoperative carbohydrate loading). Therefore, these estimates should be regarded as hypothesis generating rather than confirmatory. A prospective study enrolling at least 150–200 patients per arm, based on an assumed EPV of 10 with a similar event rate, is required to provide adequately powered estimates of individual ERAS elements.

Third, the study was conducted in a Thai tertiary public care hospital with a specific resource environment, staffing structure, and discharge culture; therefore, the findings may not translate directly to settings with different perioperative practices, bed management norms, or levels of neurocritical care support.

Fourth, patient-reported outcomes, health-related quality of life, and postoperative recovery experiences were not assessed. These domains are increasingly regarded as essential endpoints in contemporary ERAS research and are necessary to demonstrate patient-centered benefits beyond shorter hospitalizations [5, 39]. Additionally, functional follow-up was limited to 6 months, which may be insufficient to capture the full trajectory of functional recovery and the downstream effects on adjuvant treatment completion in patients with high-grade glioma.

Finally, although a reduction in LOS in a high-volume tertiary referral center may carry meaningful system-level benefits, such as improved surgical throughput and reduced waiting times, these downstream effects were not evaluated in the present analysis.

Future perspectives

Cranial ERAS continues to evolve, and several important knowledge gaps remain to be addressed. The present study adds to the growing evidence for ERAS in elective glioma craniotomies and highlights several priorities for future studies. The development of more standardized, procedure-specific guidelines for brain tumor surgery, analogous to existing spine recommendations [21], may improve benchmarking across centers and strengthen the synthesis of future evidence [20]. A stepwise implementation strategy beginning with potentially high-impact elements, such as perioperative nutritional interventions, may represent the most pragmatic path toward broader institutional adoption. Future studies should prospectively incorporate patient-reported outcomes using validated instruments along with functional endpoints to capture patient-centered benefits beyond LOS [5]. These studies should also focus on broader postoperative recovery domains and longer-term functional endpoints. Moreover, they should examine whether faster postoperative recovery translates into more timely initiation of adjuvant chemoradiotherapy in patients with high-grade gliomas, a clinically meaningful question with direct prognostic implications that has not yet been rigorously evaluated. Ultimately, adequately powered multicenter randomized trials and collaborative registries with standardized outcome reporting may provide robust evidence that cranial ERAS still requires.

Conclusion

In this single-center retrospective cohort study of adults undergoing elective glioma craniotomy, the ERAS pathway was associated with shorter total and postoperative LOS, without an apparent increase in postoperative complications or readmission. The total hospitalization cost was numerically higher in the ERAS group but did not reach statistical significance, most likely reflecting a front-loading cost structure rather than a true economic disadvantage. Preoperative carbohydrate loading and early oral fluid intake within 8 h were independently associated with short postoperative LOS in multivariable analyses, suggesting that these elements may represent high-priority targets for early-stage ERAS implementation. These findings should be interpreted with caution, given the retrospective design and modest sample size. Prospective studies, ideally multicenter and incorporating patient-reported outcomes, are needed to confirm whether ERAS improves perioperative recovery in this population and to identify the pathway elements that contribute the most to earlier discharge and timely initiation of adjuvant therapy.
