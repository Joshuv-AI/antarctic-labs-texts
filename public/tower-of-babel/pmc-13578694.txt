A comparative evaluation of the efficacy of traps and rodenticide baits in field studies with house mice (Mus musculus)
Authors: Nicole Klemann, Stefan Endepols
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe objective of this study was to compare the efficacy of trapping and subsequent rodenticide baiting for house mouse (Mus musculus) control under field conditions. Three field trials were conducted, with pre- and post-treatment feeding and tracking censuses used to estimate control success. Trapping alone reduced feeding census values by 69% from 159 to 49 g at Site 1, 40% (344 g/208 g) at Site 2, and 58% (194 g/82 g) at Site 3. Tracking censuses confirmed similar reductions (44–67%). Trapping was most effective in the lower infestation levels and where food availability was limited. The choice of lure strongly influenced performance: peanut butter consistently outperformed synthetic attractants. Subsequent brodifacoum baiting achieved 90–100% control of the remaining mouse populations across all sites. All trapped mice were found dead at daily checks; the majority were caught by the neck indicating generally swift kills, although some captures involved the chest or abdomen. Non-target captures were negligible. These findings demonstrate that while snap traps can substantially reduce mouse infestations, their success depends on infestation size, food availability, and bait type. Introduction

House mice (Mus musculus) are one of the most common and persistent pests in urban and rural environments, posing significant risks to human health, agriculture, and infrastructure1,2. Efficient control methods are essential in mitigating these risks, with trapping and rodenticide baiting being two of the most widely used approaches for house mouse control. Rodenticides and rodenticide products have been evaluated in laboratory and field studies for decades all over the world3–8, whereas house mouse-traps lack such history of research due to their drawbacks9.

The first control method, traps, has long been a traditional means of controlling house mouse populations. The efficacy evaluation of traps is guided by the NoCheRo guideline10 for testing traps, issued by the German Umweltbundesamt (UBA). This guideline outlines standardized procedures to evaluate the performance of different trap types, including factors such as capture efficiency, humaneness, and ease of use. By focusing on these parameters, the NoCheRo (Non Chemical Rodent Control) guideline ensures that tests are conducted in a scientifically rigorous manner, providing reliable data on the effectiveness of various trapping mechanisms.

On the other hand, rodenticide baits, mostly containing an anticoagulant rodenticide, offer a different approach, which has been proven effective for any product on the market to get regulatory authorisation. Today in the EU (European Union), the effectiveness of rodenticide baits must be assessed according to the Efficacy Guideline for Testing Biocides, published by the European Chemicals Agency11. The ECHA guidelines provide a framework for testing the performance of rodenticides in controlling house mice, including the evaluation of toxicity, palatability, and the method of application. The objective of these tests is to determine how well a particular biocide reduces mouse populations while ensuring safety and regulatory compliance. While there are studies assessing the efficacy of traps12–14 and baits15,16 individually, there appears to be a lack of peer-reviewed articles assessing the efficacy of these two methods in a controlled field setting, especially in relation to the specific guidelines mentioned (NoCheRo and ECHA).

This study aims to address a significant research gap by directly measuring the efficacy of mouse control using traps, and by comparing this with rodenticide treatments. The aim is to determine whether surviving mice can be controlled using rodenticides under practical conditions. To date, no studies have explored the efficacy of trapping, followed by subsequent attempts to achieve sufficient control using another method, particularly in accordance with established testing guidelines. In our recent field studies, we followed a standardized methodology across three trials, where mouse populations of approximately one hundred individuals were first controlled with traps according to the NoCheRo guidelines10. After the trapping phase, rodenticide baits were applied to control any surviving mice, and the bait’s efficacy was then tested in accordance with the ECHA guidelines11. These studies provide a novel opportunity to assess and compare the effectiveness of both methods in integrated pest management strategies. By examining the strengths and limitations of each approach, this research seeks to offer valuable insights into the most effective means of controlling house mice.

Methods

Three independent trials were conducted in the Münsterland area, North-West Germany. Trial #1 was conducted in a 100-year-old residential house with 300 m2 living space (half-timbered house) in the outskirts of a small town with 15,000 inhabitants. Trial #2 was conducted in an agricultural building of 600 m2 on a livestock farm. The site included three areas, a pigsty, a feed storage with feed preparation and mixing technique, and a straw storage. Trial #3 also was conducted in an agricultural building of 600 m2 on a livestock farm. The site included two areas, a barn with straw and feed storage, and a pigsty. The properties were moderately infested with house mice. A thorough inspection of the premises and of potential access points during the trial period largely ruled out the likelihood of mice being introduced. No rodent trapping or rodenticide treatment had been carried out over the past six months prior to the studies.

The materials used in the study were independently selected by the conductor of the study. Traps and boxes to house them as well as synthetic lures and non-tox baits were selected for quality criteria, such as robustness, general acceptance by pest controllers, certification as far as available, and their broad availability in the market. The field trials to assess the efficacy of traps were conducted according to the NoCheRo-guidance for the evaluation of rodent traps10. This guideline was developed by an international group of experienced scientists in order to enable the comparison of traps with rodenticides for the “Comparative Assessment” of rodenticides by the European Chemical Agency, ECHA, required for the re-registration of rodenticides. After completion of the snap trap trial, a rodenticide treatment was conducted following the ECHA guidance on the biocidal products regulation, Volume II Efficacy11. All treatments were included in the hygiene and rodent management schedules at the study sites, and they were conducted in any detail in accordance with the relevant European guideline11. Implementation of the trial/Installation of trap equipment

The entire sites were surveyed, examining for signs of rodent activity, such as droppings, burrows, damaged building fabric and smear marks. A sketch map showing all significant features of the site, including signs of infestation, was drawn. A pre-trial survey was undertaken to determine optimal position of control points with traps and bait boxes.

All census feed stations, trap boxes and tunnels were installed accordingly. All traps were placed, but not activated for a period of at least two days prior to trapping. A small amount of cereals was scattered in and around each trap position. The number of census points was similar to the number of control points, with a few less tracking plates than feeding points, because tracking plates could not be set up in all census points. The census points were not located at the same place chosen for the trap points, but nearby. The target animals could become familiar to the entire trapping equipment for a sufficient period of time to minimize neophobia10,11.

As for rodenticide bait points, trap points were installed at a distance of two to three meter in the infested areas, when possible, and in a shorter distance in accordance with the pre-trial survey. One or two traps in box or tunnel, respectively, were placed at one trap point, e.g. along walls. As trap tunnels housed two traps, and boxes housed one trap, trap boxes were used pairwise in order to place two traps in each control point in trial #1. Due to their hidden places, a few traps could be set without box or tunnel in trial site #1. For numbers of control points and number of traps on each trial site see Table 2.

Pre-treatment and post-treatment census

Indices of the house mouse infestation size were obtained both before and after the treatments using two quantitative methods:

Feeding census

Feeding census:

Census feed was offered at each census feed point in bait boxes for four days, covering the entire infestation in quantities at each census feed point which, as far as possible, exceeded the daily consumption by rodents. The census feed used (rolled oats) was likely to be readily consumed by mice, according to previous experiences with house mice in the study area. The number of census feed points and the amount taken from each point were weighed daily to the nearest one gram. We used a portable kitchen scale, which was checked for function with weights before each use. An indication of the change in weight of the bait due to moisture loss or uptake was included. Therefore, two additional census points (control points) were installed, and census feed was applied in a way house mice could not reach it, in order to measure the natural weight variation. The census feed points were topped up daily if census feed had only been partially taken. If all the census feed had been consumed, the quantity was increased to ensure an accurate pre-treatment census. At the end of the census, all census feed was removed from the trial site. The total amount of census feed consumed per time period provides a reliable quantification of the infestation size.

Tracking activity census:

Tracking activities were measured over four days, simultaneously with the feeding census, using tracking patches of 10 cm × 15 cm silver sand, purchased at a local DIY store, covering the site, and in number similar to the census feed points but not in the same locations. The patches were inspected for signs of activity and resurfaced daily. A simple scoring system was devised to assess the quantity of rodent footprints per patch. The scoring weighted the density of tracks in relation to the percentage coverage of the tracking patches in five classes:

0% (index 0),

1–5% (index 1),

5–33% (index 2),

34–66% (index 3).

> 66% (index 4) of the tracking patch covered with tracks.

The daily tracking activity index was shown as the sum of the tracking scores on all patches recorded after 24 h. When the pre-treatment assessment was complete, the tracking patches were maintained to provide supplementary information on rodent activity. Once the pre-treatment census had been conducted, there was a lag period of two to four days with no experimental interference on the site.

After the field trial, a comparison of population indices obtained before and after the treatment provided the quantitative assessment of the efficacy of the control measures. The degree of control was expressed as a percentage reduction in the pre-treatment census feed uptake and census tracking index. An evaluation of a rodent control measure, either using a rodenticide bait or a non-chemical method, is considered effective, when post-treatment census feed uptake and tracking activity index proved for a remaining infestation below 10 per-cent of the initial infestation. In case the survival rate of the treatment with traps was higher than 10 per cent, a rodenticide treatment was implemented to achieve a sufficient control level.

In case of a rodenticide treatment required, the data of the post-treatment census after trapping (post-treatment census I) should be considered as pre-treatment census of the succeeding rodenticide treatment. On the last day of the census period, the bait points (bait stations) were set in the same position as the trap points. In a few cases, the position deviated by maximum 30 cm, e.g. because of the different sizes of the safety box, tunnel and the bait station, or the use of two safety boxes at one trap point. The locations of bait points were different from those of the census feed points and tracking patches. In addition, two inaccessible bait stations (control points) were installed to measure natural weight variation.

Trapping treatment

The trap system was activated in accordance with the label at the previously defined trap points. A maximum treatment period of up to 30 days was chosen (according to NoCheRo-guidance, p.42)10. During the entire test period, the trap points remained at exactly the same locations. Tracking patches (same place as during census) were daily visited, scored, and resurfaced.

Peanut butter was used during the initial phase of trapping in trial #1 and #3. Trial #2 started with Nara® sticks, a synthetic lure, see Table 1. After two weeks of trapping, the success of each trap was evaluated and lures of all traps that had been unsuccessful for seven days were replaced by professional non-toxic baits in trial #1, and Provoke in # 2 and #3 to ensure a variety of scents, as stated in the NoCheRo-guidance10.

Rodenticide treatment

Two to four days after completion of the pre-treatment census, 20–50 g of a rodenticide bait were placed in each bait station. The use of both rodenticide products was authorised by the Federal Institute of Occupational Safety and Health (BAuA) with approval numbers DE-0018327-14 and DE-0003381-14. The rodenticide was applied in accordance with the label, for an appropriate period (28 to 29 days). Bait points were regularly replenished. The quantities consumed were recorded two times per week (every 3 to 4 days). Indication of the change in weight of the bait due to moisture loss was included (two bait points out of reach). Tracking patches (same place as in census phases) were scored according to the scoring used during the pre-treatment census. Observations and recordings were continued until no signs of rodent activity had been detected for at least three days. Then, the bait stations and the bait were removed from the site. At that point a lag period of three days was implemented.

Results

The snap trap treatments resulted in 40% to 69% control success according to the feeding census, and 44% to 67% control according to the tracking activity census. The rodenticide treatments resulted in 90% to 100% control of the house mouse infestations, which were present after the trapping period (for details of each trial see Tables 3, 4 and 5).

All trapped mice were found dead at the time of daily control checks. A majority of mice were caught by the neck, suggesting a swift and likely humane kill. Specifically, 20 mice (69%) at Site 1, 22 mice (28%) at Site 2, and 16 mice (36%) in Site 3 were captured by the neck. The remaining mice were caught across other regions, including the chest, abdomen, or lower abdomen. In all study sites, traps were triggered without catching a mouse: 35 in trial 1 (1.77% of trap nights), 128 (6.7%) in trial 2, and 41 (2.2%) in trial 3.

One wood mouse (Apodemus sylvaticus) was caught Site 1, accounting for 0.05% of trap nights. No non-target organisms were caught in Sites 2 and 3.

Subsequent brodifacoum baiting achieved 90% to 100% control of the remaining mouse populations across all sites, with bait consumption ranging from 111 to 1,635 g (Tables 5 and 6). No non-target animals were found dead or dying during any of the rodenticide treatments.

Discussion

In the presented studies, we observed a trend where the highest trapping result occurred in the lower infestation level, and the lowest trapping result occurred in the highest infestation level. However, this trend cannot be proven significant with only three replicates. Trapping success can also depend on the type of habitat where infestations are being treated. Moreover, we cannot conclude that a critical infestation size exists at which complete trapping success becomes impossible. Complete trapping success is achievable, but it depends not only on infestation size but also on the complexity of the environment.

The results suggest a relationship between infestation size and trapping success. The site with the highest initial infestation level (Site 2, with 344 g of feeding census per day) showed the greatest survival rate of 60%, compared to Site 1 (159 g, 31% survival) and Site 3 (194 g, 42% survival). This suggests that trapping success is primarily influenced by infestation level rather than trap type. However, factors such as environmental complexity and food availability likely play important roles as well.

The amount of census feed taken per trapped mouse varied across sites, which may reflect differences in the availability and accessibility of nutrition in the environment rather than merely infestation size. Both the trap and efficacy evaluation guidelines state that two independent census methods must be used before and after each treatment to calculate the effect of the control measure. As in most studies, these methods are the feeding census and the tracking census. In our study sites, the results of these two censuses are very similar, with one confirming the reliability of the other (see Tables 3 and 4 vs. Table 5). The similarity of the census results enabled us to estimate the number of mice present prior to trapping, based on the feeding census. The highest reduction in feeding census per trapped mouse was observed at Site 1 (3.79 g per mouse), conducted in a residential building with limited food sources. In contrast, Site 2, the most heavily infested site conducted in an agricultural building, offered plentiful and easily accessible livestock feed. This difference suggests that habitat type and food availability influence trapping results and should be further examined to better understand their impact on trapping success.

The traps tested in this study, GORILLA TRAP® and KNESS mouse trap, are widely recommended by practitioners with long-term experience and appear to function similarly in their basic effectiveness. The most critical factor influencing trapping success was the lure and non-tox baits, respectively, that we used in traps. Specifically, NARA® STICK, a plastic bait with some flavour, was less effective compared to edible and palatable non-tox baits like peanut butter and Provoke® Mouse. In particular peanut butter has been selected for trap baiting as one of the most attractive lure during decades of practical experimentation in pest control. Replacing NARA® STICK with such highly attractive lure considerably increased trapping success (see Fig. 2).

While recent research has explored chemical communication in house mouse behaviour17,18, and the potential of synthetic sex pheromones and acoustic signals to enhance rodent trap attractiveness10,19,20,21, our study focused specifically on evaluating established, widely used tools under field conditions. Practical rodent control in agricultural, industrial, and residential settings requires methods that are commercially available, robust, and economically viable. In our trials, trap and bait boxes were visibly marked with urine within the first night, indicating natural chemical communication was already active. The utility of synthetic pheromones under such conditions is likely limited. Moreover, acoustic attractants are impractical in noisy environments like the one we studied. While such technology may be a subject for future research, their limited availability, cost, and sensitivity to environmental conditions constrain their current applicability in field-based pest control16.

Following the trapping trials, rodenticide baiting was applied using grain bait containing brodifacoum at the same sites where traps were previously set. This baiting resulted in high success rates, achieving 90% to 100% control of the remaining mouse populations. The consistency of bait placement with previous trapping locations indicates that the traps were positioned effectively, providing the highest possible trapping success prior to baiting. These findings emphasize that rodenticide baiting is essential to achieve > 90% control in medium-sized infestations in these environments. It should also be considered, that we applied several types of bait to the traps, whereas only one type of rodenticide bait was used. This bait was a very basic formulation, obviously not containing any additional component, such as seeds, nuts or oils. We consider the rodenticide bait to be of average palatability; there may be more palatable alternatives. Switching to a different type of bait during the baiting period could have resulted in an even higher control rate.

Although we did not determine the resistance of the house mouse populations at the study sites prior to treatment, resistance to anticoagulant rodenticides is widely suspected in the region16,22–24. It is therefore likely that at least some of the treated populations carried resistance-associated alleles. However, brodifacoum is one of the most potent second-generation anticoagulant rodenticides and has consistently demonstrated high efficacy across all known resistant strains of house mice, including hybrid genotypes such as L128S and Vkorc1spr introgression strain23,24.

The apparent plateauing of trapping success could be attributed to trap aversion learned by the mice rather than inherent aversion to the traps themselves. The number of traps that were triggered without catching anything was roughly equal to the number of catches (site 1: 35 without and 29 with; site 2: 128 without and 80 with; site 3: 41 without and 44 with). Triggering a trap produces a relatively loud bang, which could condition avoidance behaviour. However, the traps can also be easily triggered by mice jumping on the trap boxes, as well as by other triggers in the absence of a mouse, such as vibrations caused by pigs and equipment. Therefore, learned aversion can only partially explain the low catch success rate.

Additionally, these findings suggest that trap type alone is not the key determinant of trapping efficiency; instead, the combination of infestation size, bait type, habitat type, and trap placement plays a critical role. The findings of comprehensive efficacy tests of traps25 support our choice of trap type, as they demonstrated that the Victor trap, which is a snap trap similar to the traps used in this study, was the most effective for house mice among 11 traps tested. This comparison reinforces the validity of using snap traps for mouse control in agricultural and residential settings.

However, our findings emphasize that trap effectiveness alone may not be sufficient to suppress rodent populations in all contexts. For example, it was reported that in rural rat infestations, snap traps captured only 13% of the present rat population over 21 days, with an 18.5% increase in the overall infestation during the trapping period. A large proportion (79%) of the rats captured were juveniles, and 37% of the captures involved body positions indicating likely inhumane deaths. The authors concluded that snap traps were both inefficient and raised serious concerns regarding animal welfare13.

In our own study, all captured mice were found dead during daily control visits, suggesting that no individuals remained alive in traps for extended periods. Among these, a majority were caught by the neck – a position typically associated with a quick and presumably humane death4. Specifically, 69% of mice at Site 1, 28% at Site 2, and 36% at Site 3 were neck-caught. The remaining captures involved the chest, abdomen or lower abdomen, which could indicate less humane outcomes. These data suggest variability in the humaness of snap traps, potentially influenced by trap position in the trap box or mouse behaviour, and highlight the need to consider both efficacy and welfare in trap evaluations.

These findings parallel the challenges observed in our own study, where trapping alone was insufficient for full control and baiting was necessary. The above referenced findings further highlight how population structure and learned avoidance by adult individuals may limit trap efficacy, and underscore the importance of evaluating not just performance, but also welfare outcomes. Similarly, the limited effectiveness was demonstrated of snap traps alone for rat control in remote island forests, observing only a reduction in rat populations but no significant effect from rodenticides on rats, while mice were temporarily suppressed by rodenticide application26.

Although traps can be an effective means of controlling mice, this study demonstrates their limitations, particularly in heavily infested areas. The findings emphasise the importance of selecting the right lure to maximise trapping efficiency. Our research into factors impacting trapping success could inform future studies, and advance current knowledge on rodent management. Future studies should explore whether changing lures, or altering trap placement, can further enhance effectiveness throughout the trapping process. The potential for learned trap aversion also warrants investigation, as does the evaluation of the potential influence of population density and environmental complexity, to improve long-term trapping success. Previous research12,13 has emphasised the importance of evaluating traps for both efficacy and humaneness. Considering both aspects is essential for developing effective and ethically responsible control strategies using traps.

When developing practical rodent management strategies, the limitations of each control strategy must be considered alongside the advantages and limiting biological factors. For example, trapping can be labour-intensive, requiring considerable time and personnel. Baiting with anticoagulant rodenticides can be limited by resistance and unintended impacts on non-target species if not implemented appropriately. Integrating trapping with rodenticide application as part of an integrated pest management strategy could offer advantages in situations where the two approaches complement each other.
