Predictors for failed same-day discharge after total shoulder arthroplasty
Authors: Jonathan A Marter, Kathleen C Smith, Mina Entessari, Alan W Reynolds, Brian R Waterman, Garrett S Bullock, Nicholas A Trasolini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSame-day discharge (SDD) after total shoulder arthroplasty (TSA) has gained popularity with expansion of TSA performed in the ambulatory setting. Prolonged post-operative length of stay remains challenging, contributing to increased health-care costs without improved outcomes. Identifying risk factors predictive of failed SDD is critical for optimizing patient selection for outpatient vs. inpatient post-operative care settings. The purpose of this study was to evaluate risk factors associated with failure of SDD after TSA.MethodsThis was a retrospective review of 808 patients who underwent primary reverse or anatomic TSA between September 2016 and August 2025 at a single institution. Patients undergoing hemiarthroplasty and revision arthroplasty were excluded. Data collected included demographics, comorbidities, pre-operative laboratory and imaging findings, physical examination metrics, and perioperative pain control. SDD was defined as discharge within 24 hours of surgery; failure was defined as discharge >24 hours after surgery. Logistic regression models were used to evaluate potential predictors of SDD failure, controlling for age, body mass index, sex, laterality, race, and type of arthroplasty. The Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was applied to control for false discovery. Odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were reported.ResultsAmong 808 patients, 377 (46%) achieved SDD. American Society of Anesthesiology (ASA) physical status classification was the only statistically significant predictor of SDD failure (P < .001). Overall, SDD failure rates included: 20% for ASA 1, 36% for ASA 2, 49% for ASA 3, and 71% for ASA 4. No other demographic or intraoperative variables (age, sex, body mass index, race, laterality, arthroplasty type, smoking status, cardiopulmonary disease, prior shoulder surgery, steroid use, and intraoperative factors) demonstrated statistically significant associations with SDD failure.ConclusionIn this cohort, ASA classification was a significant predictor of failure for SDD after TSA. In particular, ASA Class 3 and 4 patients are at significant risk for length of stay >24 hours. These findings can inform clinical decision-making to optimize outpatient surgical pathways, improve patient counseling, and reduce health-care expenditures.Keywords: Shoulder arthroplasty, Same-day discharge, Outpatient surgery, Pre-operative risk factors, Length of stay, Anesthesia score

Total shoulder arthroplasty (TSA) is a well-established surgical intervention used to manage degenerative shoulder conditions, including osteoarthritis, rotator cuff arthropathy, as well as complex proximal humerus fractures. From 2016 to 2020 approximately 30,000 patients underwent TSA annually.24 The demand for TSA is expected to rise by 235% in the coming decade.27,36 Historically, shoulder arthroplasty was performed exclusively as an inpatient procedure; however, in 2021 it was removed from the Medicare inpatient-only list for patients aged 65 and older, expanding payer coverage and enabling broader adoption of outpatient pathways, including same-day discharge (SDD).9,13 Therefore, SDD after TSA has gained increasing attention to reduce cost and improve patient outcomes.9,21,22,31

Similar to the well-documented transition of total hip and knee arthroplasty to the outpatient setting, carefully selected TSA patients have demonstrated comparable safety and clinical outcomes in the outpatient setting.2,13,21 SDD protocols have been associated with approximately a 20% reduction in total episode-of-care costs due to decreased staffing requirements and admission-related expenses.8,20,31 In addition to these economic benefits, patient-reported satisfaction scores are consistently higher in ambulatory surgery centers (ASCs), particularly among patients at low risk for post-operative complications such as uncontrolled pain, pulmonary or cardiac instability, or increased post-operative physical therapy needs.28,31,33

Despite the growing interest in SDD TSA, unplanned post-operative admissions remain problematic; contributing to increased health-care costs, higher bed utilization, and hospital related complications.28,32 Identifying factors predictive of failed SDD is critical to refine patient selection and ensure patient safety during the ongoing transition to outpatient care. Previous studies in lower extremity arthroplasty have identified risk factors for unsuccessful SDD—including advanced patient age, higher body mass index (BMI), elevated American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) Physical Status Classification System, longer operative duration, and pre-operative opioid use—but these findings have not been well characterized in the shoulder arthroplasty population.1,3,5,10

Improved patient selection can also inform insurance-based cost modeling. By integrating these predictors into a risk stratification framework, insurers can more accurately estimate the likelihood of unplanned admissions or extended post-operative care, allowing for more precise coverage determinations and reduced variability in copays.16,17 This approach benefits all parties involved: physicians can better counsel patients regarding expected recovery pathways and potential financial implications, patients gain a clearer understanding of their anticipated out-of-pocket expenses, and payers can mitigate excessive surgical costs through more efficient resource allocation.8,12,16,37

Ultimately, incorporating predictive risk assessment into both clinical and financial planning promotes transparency, enhances patient-centered care, and supports sustainable health-care utilization. The purpose of this study was to evaluate risk factors associated with failure of SDD following both anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty (aTSA) and reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA). We hypothesized that higher BMI, advanced age, elevated ASA classification, pulmonary disease, and cardiac arrhythmia would be independently associated with increased failure of SDD, and that these findings could inform the development of a pre-operative risk prediction model to optimize inpatient vs. outpatient surgical planning.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

This was an institutional review board–approved retrospective cohort study conducted at a single institution evaluating pre-operative and intraoperative factors associated with failure of SDD after TSA. The study included patients who underwent aTSA and rTSA performed by multiple fellowship-trained surgeons between September 2016 and August 2025. Inclusion criteria consisted of patients who underwent primary aTSA or rTSA for glenohumeral osteoarthritis, rotator cuff arthropathy, or proximal humerus fracture.4, 5, 6 Exclusion criteria consisted of patients who underwent hemiarthroplasty or revision shoulder arthroplasty. All patients underwent general endotracheal anesthesia for the length of the procedure, and the majority of patients received regional anesthesia using an interscalene, brachial plexus block (97.9%).

Data collection

Patient data were extracted from the electronic medical record system. The dataset included demographic characteristics such as age, sex, race, BMI, and hand dominance.27,29 Pre-operative history was reviewed for surgical laterality, primary diagnosis, radiographic findings, athletic participation, corticosteroid injection history, and prior shoulder repair. Medical comorbidities were recorded, including coagulopathies, cardiac disease (ie coronary artery disease, arrhythmias, and congestive heart failure), peripheral vascular disease, pulmonary disease (ie obstructive sleep apnea, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and interstitial lung disease), and history of tobacco use. Physical examination findings and patient-reported outcome measures were also collected, including range of motion, shoulder stability, symptom duration, and validated scores such as the visual analog scale, Single Assessment Numeric Evaluation, and American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons score.12,19,20 Pre-operative laboratory values and electrolyte panels were reviewed for abnormalities, and perioperative data included ASA classification, operative time, estimated blood loss, and use of a regional anesthesia block (Table I). All data were verified for accuracy through independent review by 2 investigators.30,34

Each patient's length of stay (LOS), discharge disposition, and any readmission or unplanned return to care within 7 days were recorded. During the study period, TSA surgical protocols required a mandatory post-operative observation period in either a surgical observation unit or inpatient unit until discharge criteria were met. Pre-operative clinical evaluation by the treating surgeon and anesthesiologist determined surgical location between 2 hospitals with inpatient admission available, which differed based on number of beds available. Institutional criteria guiding surgical site selection between Davie Medical Center (50 beds) and Atrium Health Wake Forest Baptist Hospital (855 beds) included ASA score >3, BMI >45 kg/m2, or significant cardiac history. Criteria were used to predict the level of care needed in post-operative setting.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was failure of SDD, defined as a post-operative observational period greater than 24 hours in an inpatient unit. Success of SDD was defined as LOS or post-operative observation period less than 24 hours following surgery. Primary risk factors of interest included age, sex, BMI, ASA classification, procedure type (aTSA vs. rTSA), pulmonary disease, cardiac arrhythmia, and pre-operative corticosteroid injection history. These variables were selected based on prior literature associating them with post-operative complications or failure of outpatient discharge in arthroplasty patients. Additional exploratory variables included race, hand dominance, laterality, athletic participation, smoking status, presence of cardiac or pulmonary disease, pre-operative visual analog scale, Single Assessment Numeric Evaluation, and American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons scores, laboratory and electrolyte abnormalities, use of an intraoperative nerve block, and year of surgery. These were assessed for potential inclusion in multivariate analyses if significant on univariate comparison.

Statistical analyses

All analyses were performed in R (version 4.3.1). The dplyr package was used for all cleaning and data manipulation, naniar for missing data analyses, and the Epi package for reporting model results. Prior to analysis, missing data were assessed by examining associations between missingness and observed variables and were determined to be consistent with a missing-at-random mechanism, with overall missing data prevalence of <1. Complete case analyses were performed.

Descriptive analyses were performed with continuous variables analyzed as mean (standard deviation) and tabular data as count (%). Patients were analyzed for the overall sample and grouped by success and failure. To evaluate potential prognostic factors of failure, a series of logistic regressions were performed.6,11,14,30 Models controlled for age, BMI, biological sex, limb, race, and aTSA vs. rTSA. To control for false discovery rate, the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was performed.4,5 All model results were reported as odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals. Temporal trends in SDD were assessed using annual proportions. A trend analysis was performed with year modeled as a continuous variable to evaluate changes over time. Years 2016 and 2025 were excluded due to incomplete annual case capture. The year 2020 was excluded because pandemic-related disruptions to elective surgical care limited the interpretability of discharge trends.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 808 patients were included in the analysis, of whom 377 (46%) successfully completed SDD. The mean age of the overall cohort was 68.3 years, with nearly identical ages observed in the SDD success group (68.4 ± 9.2 years) and the failure group (68.1 ± 9.2 years). Biological sex distribution differed slightly between groups, with females comprising 57.8% of the success cohort compared with 50.3% of the failure cohort; however, this difference did not reach statistical significance.

The majority of patients were classified as Caucasian (87%), followed by African American (11.5%). Racial distribution was similar between groups, although the success cohort had a slightly higher proportion of African American patients (14.6% vs. 8.8%), while the failure cohort had a slightly greater proportion of Caucasian patients (89.8% vs. 83.8%). Laterality was similarly distributed between groups. Left-sided procedures accounted for 41.5% of cases overall, with no meaningful differences observed between the SDD success and failure cohorts. Anatomic arthroplasty was performed in 26.6% of cases overall, with comparable rates between the success and failure groups (26.8% vs. 26.5%). Reverse arthroplasty accounted for the remaining 73.4% of procedures, again with no meaningful differences between groups (73.2% vs. 73.5%) (Table I).

Clinical factors and comorbidities

ASA Physical Status Classification System was the only variable demonstrating a statistically significant association with SDD outcomes (P < .001) (Table II). Increasing ASA class was associated with progressively lower probabilities of successful SDD, with observed success rates of 80% for ASA 1, 64% for ASA 2, 51% for ASA 3, and 29% for ASA 4. In trend-based regression analysis, a higher ASA class was associated with significantly reduced odds of successful SDD (odds ratio, 0.4–0.7; 95% confidence interval) (Fig. 1).

Discussion

In this study, 46% of patients achieved SDD. Among the pre-operative and intraoperative variables examined, ASA classification emerged as the only statistically significant predictor of SDD failure, with higher ASA classification predicting lower probability of successful SDD. Overall, SDD failure rates included the following: 20% for ASA 1, 36% for ASA 2, 49% for ASA 3, and 71% for ASA 4. These findings support ASA classification as a practical metric for pre-operative risk stratification in arthroplasty patients considering SDD.

Existing literature supports these results stating that ASA classification ≥3, renal insufficiency, increased age, and prolonged operative time were independent significant in predicting failure of SDD.10,23 However, Nazzal et al found no ASA classification association; reporting older age, rotator cuff arthropathy, female sex, perioperative opioid use, and prolonged anesthesia exposure as significant predictors for SDD in a 333-patient study following TSA. The heterogeneity in the literature emphasizes that a defined set of variables as predictors for failed SDD have not been consolidated, making the development of a standardized risk calculator difficult. This leaves providers to implement general screening criteria for initial risk assessment for outpatient vs. inpatient selection.26,32

Commonly cited variables for prolonged LOS after TSA—including age, gender, pulmonary disease, cardiac arrhythmia, BMI, surgical duration, perioperative opioid use, and prior shoulder interventions—were not significant in our cohort.2,3,7,10 Since these variables can factor into ASA classification, their combination creates significance, while certain factors alone may not result in meaningful prediction tools. Also, this lack of significance may be impacted by the timing of diagnosis, standardized institutional perioperative protocols, or careful pre-operative patient selection already in place. Nevertheless, our results show ASA classification can be considered as an additional predictive tool for outpatient planning.

These results have direct implications for both surgeons and administrative teams. ASA-based risk stratification enables targeted patient counseling, allowing providers to discuss the likelihood of SDD and potential need for inpatient admission. Administratively, ASA classifications can guide assignment of patients to appropriate surgical sites for low-risk patients and the main hospital for higher-risk patients requiring more intensive post-operative care.25,33,35,37 Additionally, if patients are improperly designated for outpatient procedure, hospitals and providers may receive penalty risks.18,22 Utilizing comprehensive predictor models optimizes resource utilization, reduces unnecessary admissions, improves cost-efficiency while maintaining patient safety and satisfaction, and prevents penalty accumulation.

This study has several strengths and limitations. Strengths include adequate power, through a large cohort spanning nearly nine years and involving multiple fellowship-trained surgeons. The dataset was comprehensive, incorporating detailed demographic, clinical, radiographic, perioperative, and post-operative variables, and all data underwent independent dual-review verification to minimize misclassification. Standardized anesthesia practices, consistent perioperative pain management protocols, and defined institutional criteria for ASC eligibility also strengthened internal validity and reduced variability in care delivery.

However, certain limitations must be acknowledged. As a retrospective cohort study, it is subject to inherent risks of selection bias and limitations in documentation. The single-institution setting may limit generalizability to community hospitals or ambulatory centers with different resources or care pathways. Surgeon-dependent decision-making regarding SDD eligibility and surgical site selection may introduce variability not fully captured in the dataset. Additionally, unmeasured social and environmental factors—such as home support, transportation, or medication adherence—were not available in the medical record but may influence SDD success. Finally, the nearly decade-long study period includes changes in post-operative pathways, ASC availability, and Medicare policy, which may have influenced shoulder arthroplasty practices over time. Temporal analysis demonstrated a progressive increase in the proportion of successful SDDs from 2017 through 2024, excluding years with incomplete data capture (2016 and 2025) and 2020 due to pandemic-related alterations in discharge practices. Trend analysis confirmed a significant upward trajectory in successful SDD over time.

Ultimately, these findings establish a foundation for future initiatives. Validation of ASA classification-based predictive models across multi-hospital datasets can ensure broader applicability, while incorporating patient-reported measures, such as motivation for recovery and engagement in rehabilitation, may further refine outpatient discharge predictions.13,17,18,29 Comparisons with total hip and knee arthroplasty risk stratification data may also inform institutional strategies for other elective joint replacement procedures and prove useful in other subspecialties of orthopedic surgery.

Conclusion

In this cohort, ASA classification was a significant predictor of failure for SDD after TSA. In particular, ASA 3 and ASA 4 patients are at significant risk for LOS>24 hours. These findings can inform clinical decision-making to optimize outpatient surgical pathways, improve patient counseling, and reduce health-care expenditures. Further research is needed to verify the external validity of these results and to cement the role of ASA score in SDD planning.
