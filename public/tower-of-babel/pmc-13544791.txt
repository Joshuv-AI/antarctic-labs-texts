Severe multi-site abscesses and necrotizing fasciitis in a young patient with diabetes
Authors: Hong-Jie Wen, Shou-Yan Zhu, Peng Liao, Zhong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Multifocal abscesses in young patients are uncommon and may signal underlying genetic or immune defects.Case presentation:A 20-year-old woman with maternal diabetes and consanguinity presented with hip pain and fever after a minor fall. Examination revealed short stature, cognitive impairment, and thigh masses. Imaging confirmed abscesses, and laboratory tests revealed severe infection, anemia, hypoalbuminemia, hypothyroidism, and uncontrolled diabetes. Genetic testing identified an X-chromosome deletion affecting immune, endocrine, and neurodevelopmental genes. This deletion explains susceptibility to infection, diabetes, intellectual disability, and delayed puberty. Management requires early recognition, aggressive surgery, and multidisciplinary expertise.

Introduction

Management of pelvis-thigh soft-tissue abscesses is challenging. Traditional etiologies include abdominal pathologies or local infections[1], but host genetic or immune defects may underlie atypical cases[2]. We report a 20-year-old woman who developed extensive pelvis-thigh abscesses after minor trauma, accompanied by new-onset diabetes and a ~55.45 Mb Xp22.33-p11.21 deletion affecting immune and endocrine genes. This case underscores the need for genetic evaluation and multidisciplinary management in young patients with unexplained widespread abscesses. This report adheres to the SCARE criteria [3].

Case report

A 20-year-old female presented with progressive right hip pain and fever for 2 weeks following a minor fall. Initial imaging showed no fracture, but symptoms worsened despite analgesics, prompting transfer to our hospital. Her mother has type 1 diabetes; her parents are consanguineous, with no prior diagnoses documented. On admission, she was tachycardic (120 bpm) and hypotensive (98/50 mmHg). Examination revealed short stature, short neck, facial pigmented nevi, abnormal nails, and underdeveloped secondary sexual characteristics. The right buttock and thigh had multiple fluctuant, warm masses with limited hip and knee motion; distal neurovascular function was intact. Cognitive function was below average. CT/MRI confirmed extensive soft tissue infection involving the right iliopsoas, internal iliac, inguinal, and thigh compartments (Fig. 1a–h). Genetic testing revealed a ~55.45 Mb deletion in Xp22.33-p11.21, encompassing immune, endocrine, and neurodevelopmental genes (e.g., PDHA1, PORCN, ARX, and WDR45). Laboratory findings showed leukocytosis (WBC 23.85 × 109/L, 90.8% neutrophils), elevated CRP (337.7 mg/L) and PCT (1.4 ng/mL), anemia (Hb 84 g/L), hypoalbuminemia (26.8 g/L), hypokalemia (3.02 mmol/L), coagulopathy (PT 14.9 s, fibrinogen 9.57 g/L), hypothyroidism (TSH 10.7 µIU/mL, FT3 2.25 pmol/L, TPOAb 53.98 pmol/L), and poorly controlled diabetes (HbA1c 8.7%, fasting glucose 18 mmol/L). Diagnoses included multiple abscesses with necrotizing fasciitis, sepsis, type 1 diabetes, hypothyroidism, anemia, hypoproteinemia, electrolyte imbalance, coagulopathy, intellectual disability, delayed puberty, and X-chromosome deletion syndrome. Multidisciplinary preoperative preparation included blood transfusion, albumin supplementation, nutritional support, antibiotic therapy, glycemic control, and psychological intervention. On day 3, the patient underwent initial abscess debridement and drainage with vacuum-assisted closure (VAC). Extensive pus and necrotic fascia were debrided from the lateral thigh, hip, and iliac fossa; the wound was irrigated and drained (Fig. 2a–e). Postoperatively, pain significantly improved. Empiric intravenous piperacillin-tazobactam was administered (tissue cultures were negative). A second debridement on day 5 showed reduced pus, with partial wound closure and continued drainage (Fig. 2f–h). A third debridement was performed later for persistent hip drainage, revealing only minimal purulent fluid; the wound was then closed (Fig. 2i–k). Thereafter, infection markers declined, glycemic control stabilized, anemia resolved, and right lower limb pain diminished with improved joint range of motion. The patient was discharged ambulating with crutches after 28 days. At 1-, 3-, and 6-month follow-up, the wounds were well-healed with excellent Harris Hip and Knee Society scores (Fig. 3a–c; Supplemental Digital Content Video File, available at: https://links.lww.com/IJSCR/A108). Discussion

This case describes a young female with rapid-onset extensive deep soft tissue abscesses following minor trauma, in the context of a large Xp22.33-p11.21 deletion. Several differential diagnoses were considered. Turner syndrome (SHOX involvement) typically presents with short stature and ovarian dysgenesis, not severe infection or type 1 diabetes[4]. Focal dermal hypoplasia (Goltz syndrome, PORCN) features linear skin atrophy and digital anomalies, distinct from this case[5]. BPAN (WDR45) and PDHA1-related pyruvate dehydrogenase deficiency present with developmental delay, epilepsy, or metabolic crises, rather than infection with new-onset diabetes[6]. Unlike cases linked to abdominal pathologies or pressure ulcers[7,8], our patient had no identifiable source. Concurrent new-onset diabetes and a ~55.45 Mb Xp22.33-p11.21 deletion, encompassing immune, endocrine, and neurodevelopmental genes[9], suggest a causal chain of genetic defect, immunoendocrine dysregulation, and infection susceptibility. Gene loss (e.g., PDHA1, PORCN, ARX, and WDR45) likely exerts additive multi-system effects, impairing immune function[10] and pancreatic islet development, consistent with a “autoimmune polyendocrine syndrome” that predisposes to severe infection[11]. The genetic defect compromised immune surveillance. Minor trauma then triggered infection, which the host could not contain – a vulnerability worsened by hyperglycemia from new-onset diabetes that further impaired neutrophil function. This allowed rapid fascial spread and multifocal abscess formation in a young patient without classic risk factors. These findings suggest a autoimmune polyendocrine syndrome arising from the Xp22.33-p11.21 deletion, with diabetes and severe infection as predominant manifestations.

Limitations include a lack of functional gene validation and negative cultures that preclude pathogen identification. Nevertheless, this case supports genetic workup, including chromosomal microarray and whole-exome sequencing (WES), in young patients with unexplained severe infections and developmental or multisystem involvement. Infection may be the primary manifestation of a genetic syndrome; management requires multidisciplinary coordination – surgical control, comorbidity management, and genetic counseling with long-term surveillance.

Conclusion

This case links extensive deep soft-tissue abscesses and necrotizing fasciitis to a large Xp22.33-p11.21 deletion. Unexplained disseminated infection in young patients should prompt an evaluation for underlying immunologic and endocrine genetic defects. Effective management requires early recognition, aggressive surgical debridement, and multidisciplinary care integrating infection control, endocrine support, and genetic counseling. This case highlights the clinical value of a genetic workup in complex infectious diseases.
