A predictive model for bubble–particle collisions in turbulence
Timothy T.K. Chan, Linfeng Jiang, Dominik Krug

Abstract
The modelling of bubble–particle collisions is crucial to improving the efficiency of industrial processes such as froth flotation. Although such systems usually have turbulent flows and the bubbles are typically much larger than the particles, there currently exist no predictive models for this case which consistently include finite-size effects in the interaction with the bubbles as well as inertial effects for the particles simultaneously. As a first step, Jiang and Krug 2025 proposed a frozen turbulence approach which captures the collision rate between finite-size bubbles and inertial particles in homogeneous isotropic turbulence using the bubble slip velocity probability density function measured from simulations as an input. In this study, we further develop this approach into a model where the bubble–particle collision rate can be predicted a priori based on the bubble, particle, and turbulence properties. By comparing the predicted collision rate with simulations of bubbles with Stokes numbers of 2.8 and 6.3, and particles with Stokes numbers ranging from 0.01 to 2 in turbulence with a Taylor Reynolds number of 64, good agreement is found between model and simulations for Froude number F ​ r ≤ 0.25 Fr\leq 0.25 . Beyond this range of bubble Stokes number, we propose a criterion for using our model and discuss the model’s validity. Evaluating our model at typical flotation parameters indicates that particle inertia effects are usually important. Generally, smaller bubbles, larger particles, and stronger turbulence increase the overall collision rate.

1
Introduction

Bubble–particle collisions lie at the heart of froth flotation — an industrial process which selectively recovers particles based on their hydrophobicity with applications ranging from mineral extraction to wastewater treatment
(
Rubio et al. 2002
;
Nguyen and Schulze 2004
)
. In this process, bubbles and particles are injected into a cell filled with water so they can collide with each other. As the bubbles and particles collide, the target particles (which are hydrophobic) attach to the bubbles and are floated to the surface, where they can be removed. In other words, every single particle has to collide with a bubble before it is recovered. Given the broad range of application of froth flotation, as well as the large size of flotation cells and the complex flows therein which precludes particle-resolved simulations, there is a strong incentive to model the bubble–particle collision rate. This parameter is usually reported in terms of the collision kernel

\Gamma=Z/n_{b}n_{p},

(1)

where
Z
is the collision rate per unit volume and
n_{b}
and
n_{p}
are the bubble and particle number densities, respectively, to facilitate comparison between systems with a different number of particles.

1.1
Bubble–particle collisions in still fluid

A key characteristic of bubble–particle collisions in flotation of mineral particles lies in the huge size difference between bubbles and particles, with typical bubble-to-particle radius ratios
r_{b}/r_{p}\sim\mathrm{\textit{O}}(10)

(
Rubio et al. 2002
;
Ngo-Cong et al. 2018
;
Kostoglou et al. 2020a
)
. This means that the actual collision rate is significantly affected by the distortion of the flow field due to the bubbles. Many related studies have focused exclusively on the
case of a bubble rising in a still fluid with a constant slip velocity of
w_{b}

(
Huang et al. 2012
;
Nguyen and Schulze 2004
;
Sarrot et al. 2005
;
Schulze 1989
;
Weber and Paddock 1983
;
Zheng et al. 2024
, see e.g.)
. In this limit, the problem is deterministic. As figure
1
(
a
) illustrates, only particles that are initially (i.e. in the undisturbed flow far from the bubble) located within a distance
r_{g}
from the centreline of the cylinder swept by the bubble eventually collide with the bubble (
r_{g}
is hence known as the ‘grazing radius’). The ratio of the number of particles that actually hit the bubble to the number of particles inside the cylinder swept by the bubble is the ‘collision efficiency’

E_{c}=\bigg(\frac{r_{g}}{r_{b}}\bigg)^{2},

(2)

such that when the particle number density immediately upstream of the bubble is equal to the global value, the collision kernel is given by

\Gamma=\pi r_{b}^{2}w_{b}E_{c}.

(3)

In general,
E_{c}
is determined by five nondimensional parameters, i.e.

E_{c}=f\bigg(Re_{b}=\frac{2r_{b}w_{b}}{\nu},\frac{r_{b}}{r_{p}},St_{p}^{\prime}=\frac{\tau_{p}w_{b}}{2r_{b}},\frac{v_{s}}{w_{b}},\theta_{cap}\bigg),

(4)

where
Re_{b}
is the bubble Reynolds number,
\nu
is the kinematic viscosity,
St_{p}^{\prime}
is the particle Stokes number based on the bubble slip velocity,
\tau_{p}=r_{p}^{2}(2\rho_{p}/\rho_{f}+1)/(9\nu)
is the particle response time,
\rho_{p}
is the particle density,
\rho_{f}
is the fluid density,
v_{s}
is the particle settling speed, and
\theta_{cap}
is the bubble contamination angle. In the discussion below, we restrict ourselves to the case of fully contaminated bubbles (
\theta_{cap}=\pi
) and non-settling particles (
v_{s}=0
). The former is reasonable for bubbles in flotation
(
Nguyen and Schulze 2004
;
Huang et al. 2012
)
, while the latter assumption is strictly valid when
v_{s}
is much smaller than the bubble velocity and is imposed here as a simplification of the real situation.

In the
St_{p}^{\prime}\to 0
limit particles perfectly follow the fluid flow, a collision occurs if the streamline carrying the particle comes within the collision distance
r_{c}
. This is termed the ‘interception mechanism’
(
Schulze 1989
)
and
Sarrot et al. 2005
showed that

E_{c}^{(i)}=\bigg(\frac{r_{g}}{r_{b}}\bigg)^{2}=\frac{3}{2}\bigg(\frac{r_{p}}{r_{b}}\bigg)^{2}\bigg(1+\frac{Re_{b}^{2/3}}{5}\bigg).

(5)

As
St_{p}^{\prime}
increases, the particle trajectories are less affected by the locally distorted flow field, which can cause more of the incident particles to collide with the front half of the bubble as shown in figure
1
(
b
), thus increasing the collision efficiency
(
Huang et al. 2012
)
. This ‘inertial mechanism’ can be expressed as
(
Schuch and Löffler 1978
;
Schulze 1989
)

E_{c}^{(in)}=\bigg(1+\frac{r_{p}}{r_{b}}\bigg)^{2}\bigg(\frac{St_{p}^{\prime}}{St_{p}^{\prime}+a}\bigg)^{b},

(6)

where
a
and
b
are fitting parameters (see
A
).
Schulze 1989
proposed to model the overall collision efficiency as a superposition of the interception and inertial contributions, leading to

E_{c}=E_{c}^{(i)}+E_{c}^{(in)}\bigg(1-\frac{E_{c}^{(i)}}{(1+r_{p}/r_{b})^{2}}\bigg).

(7)

Here, the term in brackets ensures that
E_{c}
scales as
(1+r_{p}/r_{b})^{2}
in the high
St_{p}^{\prime}
limit.

) Sketch of a particle with
St_{p}^{\prime}=0

1.2
Bubble–particle collisions in turbulence

The above deterministic picture, however, falls short in the presence of turbulence, which is an important factor in most applications. In these, turbulence is either induced by the rising bubbles or even generated in order to increase the bubble–particle collision rate
(
Jiang and Krug 2025
)
. Turbulence introduces characteristic time and length scales into the problem leading to the emergence of additional dimensionless numbers. The most relevant ones are the Taylor Reynolds number
Re_{\lambda}
, which indicates the turbulence intensity, the Froude number
Fr
characterising the strength of turbulence relative to gravity, and the bubble and particle Stokes numbers based on the Kolmogorov time scale
St_{b}
and
St_{p}
. These are defined as follows:

Re_{\lambda}=\sqrt{\frac{15}{\nu\varepsilon}}u^{\prime 2},\,Fr=\frac{a_{\eta}}{\mathfrak{g}}=\frac{u_{\eta}}{\mathfrak{g}\tau_{\eta}},\,St_{b}=\frac{\tau_{b}}{\tau_{\eta}},\,St_{p}=\frac{\tau_{p}}{\tau_{\eta}},

(8)

where
u^{\prime}
is the single-component root-mean-square (r.m.s.) fluid velocity,
\nu
is the kinematic viscosity,
\varepsilon
is the average rate of turbulent dissipation,
\mathfrak{g}
is the gravitational acceleration,
\tau_{b}=r_{b}^{2}(2\rho_{b}/\rho_{f}+1)/(9\nu)
is the bubble response time (the subscript ‘b’ denotes bubbles), and
\tau_{\eta}=\sqrt{\nu/\varepsilon}
and
u_{\eta}=(\nu\varepsilon)^{0.25}
are the Kolmogorov time scale and velocity scale, respectively.
Accounting for the effect of turbulence in addition to the flow distortion by the bubble is a long standing problem in the literature
(
Pyke et al. 2003
;
Nguyen et al. 2016
;
Wang et al. 2018
;
Hassanzadeh et al. 2018
;
Wang et al. 2020
;
Wang et al. 2022
)
.
One approach is to consider an extra subprocess for bubble–particle collisions: ‘geometric collisions’, which are encounters resulting purely from turbulence and buoyancy-driven bubble and particle motions neglecting any distortion to the flow field caused by the bubbles
(
Bloom and Heindel 2002
;
Bloom and Heindel 2003
;
Pyke et al. 2003
;
Koh and Schwarz 2006
)
. The total collision rate is then given by multiplying the geometric collision rate in turbulence with the collision efficiency
E_{c}
in still fluid based on an ensemble-average value of
Re_{b}
. However, this is problematic as the instantaneous and averaged values of
Re_{b}
can differ significantly. Additionally,
Pyke et al. 2003
incorrectly defines
Re_{b}
with the bubble rise velocity instead of the bubble slip velocity. In contrast, the recent model by
Kostoglou et al. 2020a
implicitly includes the collision efficiency concept in turbulence in a consistent manner. This is done by directly evaluating the particle influx around an isolated bubble that is exposed to an unsteady incident flow whose fluctuations are determined by the bubble r.m.s. slip velocity, and by incorporating the distorted flow field around the bubble using the expression given by
Nguyen and Schulze 2004
. In addition, they consider the collisions caused by small scale turbulence. Their model is based on the combined surface and velocity integral

\displaystyle\Gamma^{(Kos)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\oiint_{S_{c}}\iiint\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Lambda(-\Delta v_{r}\cdot f(w_{bo,x})f(w_{bo,y})f(w_{bo,z})f(u_{pt})

(9)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}u_{pt}\mathrm{d}w_{bo,x}\mathrm{d}w_{bo,y}\mathrm{d}w_{bo,z})\mathrm{d}S,

where
S_{c}
is a sphere with a radius of
r_{b}+r_{p}
,
\Delta v_{r}
is the radial component of the bubble–particle relative velocity, which is positive when the pair is separating,

\Lambda(x)=\left\{\begin{aligned} x\quad&\text{for $x>0$,}\\
0\quad&\text{otherwise,}\\
\end{aligned}\right.

(10)

f(\cdot)
is the probability density function (p.d.f.),
w_{bo,x,y,z}
are components of the bubble slip velocity with respect to the fluid velocity in the absence of the bubble, and
u_{pt}
is the relative bubble–particle velocity due to local velocity gradients (see
B
for the complete expression). The model is proposed for the case of uniformly distributed tracer-like particles colliding with bubbles so it has limited applicability in real-life, where most particles are inertial.
Jiang and Krug 2025
therefore developed an approach that applies to inertial one-way coupled particles. To obtain the collision kernel, they decompose the bubble trajectory into tiny fragments, for which the bubble velocity can be considered constant. During the time it takes for the bubble to travel along such a fragment, they employ a ‘frozen turbulence’ approximation (i.e. the flow field close to the bubble is considered to be steady and uniform), such that the instantaneous process is equivalent to the deterministic still fluid case (described in §
1.1
) with a bubble slip velocity corresponding to the instantaneous value. The instantaneous collision efficiency and collision kernel are hence given by (
7
) and (
3
), respectively. Integrating this over the entire bubble trajectory results in the overall collision kernel

\Gamma=\pi r_{b}^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}E_{c}(Re_{b},St_{p}^{\prime})w_{b}f(w_{b})\mathrm{d}w_{b}.

(11)

As
E_{c}(Re_{b},St_{p}^{\prime})
is defined by (
7
) and can be parametrised,
Jiang and Krug 2025
used the measured
f(w_{b})
from their simulations to validate the underlying ‘frozen turbulence’ approximation for
St_{b}=11
. The missing ingredient to turn this approach into a predictive model is therefore a suitable model that predicts the p.d.f. of the bubble slip speed
f(w_{b})
using flow and bubble parameters as inputs. To develop this is the aim of this article. We present a bubble–particle collision model which consistently accounts for turbulence and the distorted flow field around the bubble based on (
11
), and show that it is applicable for a wide range of
St_{p}
and multiple values of
St_{b}
.

This paper is organised as follows: we present our modelling approach of
f(w_{b})
and review the related models in §
2
, then we describe the simulations conducted in §
3
. The simulation results are compared with the model predictions in §
4
. This is followed by a discussion on our model’s regime of validity and its practical implications in §
5
. Finally, the conclusions are drawn in §
6
.

2
Modelling the bubble slip velocity probability density function

To model the probability density function of
w_{b}
, we consider normally distributed bubble slip velocity components
w_{b,i}
(
i=x,y,z
), i.e.

f(w_{b,i})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}_{i}}}\exp{\bigg(-\frac{(w_{b,i}-\langle w_{b,i}\rangle)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}_{i}}\bigg)},

(12)

where
\langle\cdot\rangle
denotes averaging and
\sigma_{i}
is the standard deviation of the bubble slip velocity component. This approach has also been taken by
Berk and Coletti 2024
. For the horizontal components
\langle w_{b,x}\rangle=\langle w_{b,y}\rangle=0
as buoyancy acts only along the vertical (
z
) direction and hereafter we take
\langle w_{b,z}\rangle=\langle w_{b}\rangle
for simplicity. We note that
f(w_{b,i})
is sometimes better described by a stretched exponential function
(
Masuk et al. 2021
)
. Nonetheless, as the main difference lies in the tails of the distributions, the impact on the predicted collision kernel
\Gamma
is minimal
(
Bragg and Collins 2014
;
Saffman and Turner 1956
)
. Additionally, fitting parameters have to be introduced if a stretched exponential function is employed. We therefore choose to model
f(w_{b,i})
with a Gaussian distribution. Assuming that the components are independent of each other and
\sigma_{x}=\sigma_{y}=\sigma_{z}=\sigma

(
Berk and Coletti 2024
)
, we integrate the joint p.d.f. over a spherical shell
S
with a radius of
\sqrt{w^{2}_{b,x}+w^{2}_{b,y}+w^{2}_{b,z}}
to obtain

\displaystyle f(w_{b})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\oiint_{S}f(w_{b,x})f(w_{b,y})f(w_{b,z})\mathrm{d}S

(13)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{2}w_{b}}{\sqrt{\pi}\sigma\langle w_{b}\rangle}\exp{\bigg(-\frac{w_{b}^{2}+\langle w_{b}\rangle^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\bigg)}\sinh{\bigg(\frac{w_{b}\langle w_{b}\rangle}{\sigma^{2}}\bigg)}.

We note that numerically evaluating (
13
) directly at large
\langle w_{b}\rangle
may lead to undefined values as
\lim_{x\to\infty}\sinh{(x)}/x\to\infty
and
\lim_{x\to\infty}\exp{(-x^{2})}\to 0
. This corresponds to the strong gravity regime where the slip velocity vector is dominated by its vertical component, such that
f(w_{b})
converges to a normal distribution centred at
\langle w_{b}\rangle
(see
C
). Therefore, we use

\displaystyle f(w_{b})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\,\frac{\sqrt{2}w_{b}}{\sqrt{\pi}\sigma\langle w_{b}\rangle}\exp{\bigg(-\frac{w_{b}^{2}+\langle w_{b}\rangle^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\bigg)}\sinh{\bigg(\frac{w_{b}\langle w_{b}\rangle}{\sigma^{2}}\bigg)}&\text{if $\langle w_{b}\rangle/\sigma\leq 16$,}\\
\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\exp\bigg(-\frac{(w_{b}-\langle w_{b}\rangle)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\bigg)&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(14)

Based on (
14
),
f(w_{b})
can be constructed if the mean vertical bubble slip velocity and the standard deviation of the slip velocity components are determined. The models for these quantities in literature are all based on the point-bubble approximation
(
Maxey and Riley 1983
;
Gatignol 1983
)
, which means that the bubble dynamics are governed by

\displaystyle\frac{4}{3}\pi r_{b}^{3}\rho_{b}\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{v_{b}}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 6\pi\mu r_{b}f_{b}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v_{b}})+\frac{4}{3}\pi r_{b}^{3}\rho_{f}\frac{\mathrm{D}\boldsymbol{u}}{\mathrm{D}t}+\frac{2}{3}\pi r_{b}^{3}\rho_{f}\bigg(\frac{\mathrm{D}\boldsymbol{u}}{\mathrm{D}t}-\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{v_{b}}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigg)

(15)

\displaystyle+\frac{4}{3}\pi r_{b}^{3}(\rho_{f}-\rho_{b})\mathfrak{g}\boldsymbol{e}_{z},

where
\boldsymbol{v_{b}}
is the bubble velocity,
\boldsymbol{u}
is the fluid velocity,
t
is the time,
\mu=\nu\rho_{f}
is the absolute viscosity,
f_{b}
is the correction factor that accounts for nonlinear drag and is a function of
Re_{b}
, and
\boldsymbol{e}_{z}
is the unit vector pointing vertically upwards. The terms on the right hand side of (
15
) are the drag force, pressure gradient force, added mass force, and buoyancy, respectively. (
15
) does not include the history force and the lift force as these are neglected in many models to improve tractability. We note that the lift force can play a significant role on the bubble trajectory
(
Spelt and Biesheuvel 1997
)
. Nonetheless, for predicting the magnitude of the slip velocity,
Berk and Coletti 2024
showed that neglecting the lift force does not significantly affect the result. Hence we do not consider the lift and history forces in our model.

2.1
Mean vertical slip velocity

To obtain the mean vertical slip velocity, we assume that it is approximately equal to the bubble rise velocity. This is a strong assumption since it implies that the fluid sampled by the bubbles has a negligible average vertical velocity, even though bubbles are known to cluster in certain flow regions in turbulence
(
Calzavarini et al. 2008a
;
Calzavarini et al. 2008b
)
. This assumption will be justified in §
4
.
Ruth et al. 2021
investigated the bubble rise velocity in turbulence by introducing a Reynolds decomposition of
\boldsymbol{v_{b}}=\langle v_{b,z}\rangle\boldsymbol{e}_{z}+\boldsymbol{\tilde{v}_{b}}
and in the absence of a mean flow
\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{\tilde{u}}
into (
15
), where
\tilde{\cdot}
represents fluctuations. For small
1/Fr
(i.e. weak gravity), they found that turbulence reduces the bubble rise velocity through nonlinear drag, which couples the horizontal slip velocity components with the vertical component as
Re_{b}
depends on the magnitude of the slip velocity. As a result,
\langle v_{b,z}\rangle/v_{q}=0.37/Fr_{L}
, where
v_{q}
is the bubble rise velocity in quiescent fluid,
Fr_{L}=u^{\prime}/\sqrt{2\mathfrak{g}r_{b}}
is a large-scale Froude number, and 0.37 is obtained by fitting. For the large
1/Fr
limit, they also found that turbulence reduces the bubble rise velocity. To model this, they considered
|\boldsymbol{v_{b}}-\boldsymbol{u}|\approx v_{q}
as well as
\langle|\boldsymbol{\tilde{v}_{b}}-\boldsymbol{\tilde{u}}|\rangle\propto u^{\prime}
which was observed in their simulations, and expanded
|\boldsymbol{v_{b}}-\boldsymbol{u}|
around
v_{q}
. This resulted in
\langle v_{b,z}\rangle/v_{q}=(1-\chi Fr_{L}^{2})
, where
\chi=\langle|\boldsymbol{\tilde{v}_{b}}-\boldsymbol{\tilde{u}}|\rangle/(4u^{\prime})
is a proportionality constant. We adopt these parametrisations here and model the mean vertical slip velocity with

\langle w_{b}\rangle=\left\{\begin{aligned} v_{q}(1-\chi Fr_{L}^{2})\quad&\text{for $1/Fr_{L}\geq 2.08$,}\\
0.37\frac{v_{q}}{Fr_{L}}\quad&\text{otherwise,}\\
\end{aligned}\right.

(16)

where
v_{q}
is determined through balancing the drag term with buoyancy in (
15
) taking
f_{b}=1+0.169(2r_{b}v_{q}/\nu)^{2/3}
, and
\chi=1
in accordance with
Liu et al. 2024
.

2.2
Standard deviation of the slip velocity

The other quantity that needs to be determined to construct
f(w_{b})
is the standard deviation of the bubble slip velocity
\sigma
. Several expressions for
\sigma
have been proposed over the years. One of the earliest predictions draws analogy with gravitational settling
(
Liepe and Möckel 1976
)
. In this framework, gravitational acceleration is replaced by the centripetal acceleration
a_{m}
of eddies with a size of
r_{b}
. The resultant apparent weight is then balanced by a drag force
\propto w_{b}^{3/2}
(i.e.
f_{b}\propto\sqrt{w_{b}}
) and an assumption of
w_{b}\sim\sigma_{i}
is applied. Considering eddies that lie inside the inertial subrange such that
a_{m}=2(\varepsilon r_{b})^{2/3}/r_{b}
,

\sigma_{i}^{(LM)}=\zeta\frac{\varepsilon^{4/9}r_{b}^{7/9}}{\nu^{1/3}}\bigg(\frac{\rho_{f}-\rho_{b}}{\rho_{f}}\bigg)^{2/3},

(17)

where
\zeta
is a constant whose value ranges from 0.57 to 0.83 in the literature
(
Liepe and Möckel 1976
;
Schubert 1999
;
Nguyen and Schulze 2004
)
. Among these,
Kostoglou et al. 2020b
argued that a value of 0.83 is correct which corresponds to
f_{bd}=(5/12)\sqrt{Re_{b}}
. We note that
\sigma_{i}^{(LM)}
does not necessarily have to be motivated using gravitational settling. Instead, (
17
) can be recovered in the no-gravity
St\ll 1
limit of (
15
) if the fluid acceleration is approximated by
a_{m}
, as discussed in
Chan et al. 2023
. In this case, one still needs to take
w_{b}\sim\sigma_{i}
and this assumption should not be taken for granted.

The model used in
Kostoglou et al. 2020a
does not employ this assumption and is based on the work about collisions between particles with different response times by
Yuu 1984
, which has been extended to bubble–particle collisions in
Ngo-Cong et al. 2018
. For the bubble slip velocity,
Kostoglou et al. 2020a
considers the special case where one of the particles is a tracer. As the buoyancy term is constant over time, it is not expected to directly affect
\sigma_{i}
and is therefore omitted from this analysis. In this model, the standard deviation is determined through

\sigma_{i}^{2}=\langle(\tilde{v}_{b,i}-u_{i})^{2}\rangle=\langle\tilde{v}_{b,i}^{2}\rangle+u_{i}^{\prime 2}-2\langle\tilde{v}_{b,i}u_{i}\rangle.

(18)

The bubble velocity variance
\langle\tilde{v}_{b,i}^{2}\rangle
is obtained by integrating the bubble energy spectrum
E_{b}(\omega)
, which is related to the fluid energy spectrum at the particle location
E(\omega)
such that

\langle\tilde{v}_{b,i}^{2}\rangle=\int_{0}^{\infty}E_{b}(\omega)\mathrm{d}\omega=\int_{0}^{\infty}H^{2}(\omega)E(\omega)\mathrm{d}\omega,

(19)

where the response function

H^{2}(\omega)=\frac{1+(\beta\omega\tau_{p}/f_{b})^{2}}{1+(\omega\tau_{p}/f_{b})^{2}}

(20)

and

E(\omega)=E^{(Kos)}(\omega)=u^{\prime 2}\frac{T_{L}}{1+(\omega T_{L})^{2}}.

(21)

\beta=3\rho_{f}/(2\rho_{b}+\rho_{f})
, and
T_{L}
is the Lagrangian time scale. The procedure for evaluating
\langle\tilde{v}_{b,i}u\rangle
is the same as (
19
) apart from a slightly different
H^{2}(\omega)
. For both terms,
H^{2}(\omega)
is obtained by expanding
\boldsymbol{v_{b}}
and
\boldsymbol{u}
with a Fourier integral in the point-bubble equation. Crucially,
\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}t=\mathrm{D}/\mathrm{D}t
was taken in the point-bubble equation (
15
), which implies that the resulting expression

\sigma_{i}=\frac{2u^{\prime}}{\sqrt{1+T_{L}f_{b}/\tau_{b}}}

(22)

is strictly true only when
St_{b}\ll 1
. Substituting
T_{L}/\tau_{\eta}=0.181Re_{\lambda}
and
f_{b}=1+0.169(2r_{b}\sigma_{i}/\nu)^{2/3}

(
Kostoglou et al. 2020a
;
Nguyen and Schulze 2004
)
, (
22
) becomes

\sigma_{i}^{(Kos)}=2u_{i}^{\prime}\bigg(1+8.897\frac{u_{i}^{\prime 2}\sqrt{\nu\sigma_{i}^{(Kos)}}}{\varepsilon r_{b}^{3/2}}\bigg)^{-1/2}.

(23)

Note that as the expression of
f_{b}
used tacitly implies
Re_{b}=2r_{b}\sigma_{i}/\nu
, so (
23
) should only apply for
1/Fr=0
.

Most recently,
Berk and Coletti 2024
used an alternative approach to derive an expression for
\sigma_{i}
by considering the variance of (
15
):

\sigma_{i}^{2}=\frac{\tau_{b}^{2}}{f_{b}^{2}}\bigg\langle\bigg(\frac{\mathrm{d}v_{b,i}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigg)^{2}\bigg\rangle+\frac{\tau_{b}^{2}\beta^{2}}{f_{b}^{2}}\bigg\langle\bigg(\frac{\mathrm{D}u_{i}}{\mathrm{D}t}\bigg)^{2}\bigg\rangle-2\frac{\tau_{b}^{2}\beta}{f_{b}^{2}}\bigg\langle\frac{\mathrm{d}v_{b,i}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{\mathrm{D}u_{i}}{\mathrm{D}t}\bigg\rangle,

(24)

where using (
15
) the last term is expressed as

\bigg\langle\frac{\mathrm{d}v_{b,i}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{\mathrm{D}u_{i}}{\mathrm{D}t}\bigg\rangle=\frac{f_{b}}{\tau_{b}}\bigg\langle w_{b,i}\frac{\mathrm{D}u_{i}}{\mathrm{D}t}\bigg\rangle+\beta\bigg\langle\bigg(\frac{\mathrm{D}u_{i}}{\mathrm{D}t}\bigg)^{2}\bigg\rangle.

(25)

The bubble acceleration variance
\langle(\mathrm{d}v_{b,i}/\mathrm{d}t)^{2}\rangle
and fluid acceleration variance
\langle(\mathrm{D}u_{i}/\mathrm{D}t)^{2}\rangle
are then obtained by integrating the particle (
\omega^{2}H^{2}(\omega)E(\omega)
) and fluid acceleration spectra (
\omega^{2}E(\omega)
), respectively, where
H^{2}(\omega)
is given by (
20
) and
E(\omega)
is
a model energy spectrum with two time scales
(
Sawford 1991
)

E(\omega)=E^{(BC)}(\omega)=u^{\prime 2}\frac{T_{L}+T_{2}}{(1+(\omega T_{L})^{2})(1+(\omega T_{2})^{2})}.

(26)

Here,
T_{L}/\tau_{\eta}=2(Re_{\lambda}+32)/(\sqrt{15}C_{0})
,
T_{2}=C_{0}/2(a_{0})
,
C_{0}=7
, and
a_{0}=5/(1+110/Re_{\lambda})
. The short time scale

allows (
26
) to faithfully capture high-frequency behaviour by constraining the slope of the corresponding velocity correlation function to zero at zero time lag. Note that we take
C_{0}=7
instead of the expressions listed in
Berk and Coletti 2024
in view of the better agreement with the values of
T_{L}
reported in
Zaichik et al. 2003
.
On the other hand, the covariance term
\langle w_{b,i}(\mathrm{D}u_{i}/\mathrm{D}t)\rangle
in (
25
) is evaluated by assuming
\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{v_{b}}/\mathrm{d}t=\mathrm{D}\boldsymbol{u}/\mathrm{D}t
in (

) such that

\bigg\langle w_{b}\frac{\mathrm{D}u_{i}}{\mathrm{D}t}\bigg\rangle=\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}f_{b}}{\tau_{b}(1-\beta)}.

(27)

Finally, using (
25
) – (
27
) in (

\sigma_{i}^{(BC)}=u^{\prime}(\beta-1)\frac{\tau_{b}/f_{b}}{\sqrt{(T_{L}+\tau_{b}/f_{b})(T_{2}+\tau_{b}/f_{b})}},

(28)

where
f_{b}=1+0.169(2r_{b}\langle w_{b}\rangle/\nu)^{2/3}

(
Nguyen and Schulze 2004
)
with
\langle w_{b}\rangle
defined by (
16
).

Throughout the derivation of (
28
), particle accelerations are always related to the fluid accelerations using either (
20
) or (
27
), both of which are valid for the
St\ll 1
limit. Hence, this technically implies that
\sigma_{i}^{(BC)}
is only applicable for
St\ll 1
. In fact, (

) reduces to (

) when
\beta=3
(corresponding to
\rho_{b}=0
) and

. Despite their similarities, there are two key differences between

and

. First,

that enters

does not include the mean settling velocity. Second,

employs an expression for

with a single time scale. As will be shown in §

and will be the one we select to model the bubble slip velocity p.d.f. as displayed in (

3
Numerical methods

3.1
Equations and solvers

In order to assess the quality of our model predictions, we perform direct numerical simulations of interface-resolved bubbles and point-particles in homogeneous isotropic turbulence.
The fluid flow is governed by the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations and the continuity equation, which read:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\textbf{u}}{\partial t}+\textbf{u}\cdot{\nabla}\textbf{u}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\rho_{f}}{\nabla}p+\nu\ \nabla^{2}\textbf{u}+\textbf{f}+\textbf{f}_{b},

(29)

\displaystyle{\nabla}\cdot\textbf{u}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

(30)

where
\mathbf{u}
is the fluid velocity,
p
denotes the pressure, and
\mathbf{f_{b}}
is the force exerted by the bubble on the fluid which will be discussed later in this section.

\mathbf{f}
is an external random large-scale volume force that sustains the background turbulence
(
Perlekar et al. 2012
)
.
This volume force term
\mathbf{f}
is statistically homogeneous and isotropic with a time-independent global energy input, such that the resulting turbulence is statistically stationary, homogeneous and isotropic. To obtain the flow field, (
29
) and (
30
) are solved on a uniform Eulerian grid using the lattice Boltzmann code developed in
Calzavarini 2019
and
Jiang et al. 2022
.

To simulate the bubbles, we consider them as two-way coupled buoyant spheres with zero slip on the surface. This is a reasonable assumption for bubbles in flotation processes since a significant amount of surfactants is usually present in the liquid, such that the bubble surface can be considered fully contaminated and the no-slip condition can be applied
(
Nguyen and Schulze 2004
;
Huang et al. 2012
)
. We note that surfactants can reduce the surface tension and promote bubble deformation. As an indicator of the deformation, we calculate the Weber number,
We=2r_{b}\rho_{f}\langle v_{b,z}\rangle^{2}/\gamma
, based on the surface tension
\gamma
of water and the measured bubble rise velocity in turbulence
\langle v_{b,z}\rangle
, and find that it is
\mathrm{\textit{O}}(0.1)
for our simulations meaning bubble deformation is small (
We
is even smaller if the turbulent fluctuation is considered instead of
\langle v_{b,z}\rangle
). We furthermore focus on bubbles with an average
Re_{b}\lesssim 200
, which according to
Clift et al. 2005
are spherical in shape. Consequently, we model the bubbles as rigid spheres whose translational and rotational dynamics are governed by the Newton–Euler equations

\displaystyle m_{b}\frac{{\rm d}\mathbf{v}_{b}}{{\rm d}t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\oiint_{S_{b}}\bm{\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{n}\ {\rm d}S-V_{b}(\rho_{b}-\rho_{f})\mathfrak{g}\mathbf{e}_{z},

(31)

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}\bm{\mathbf{I_{b}}}\mathbf{\Omega}_{b}}{{\rm d}t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\oiint_{S_{b}}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{b})\times(\bm{\sigma}\cdot\textbf{n})\ {\rm d}S.

(32)

Here,
\mathbf{\Omega}_{b}
is the angular velocity of the bubble at position
\mathbf{x}_{b}
with a volume
V_{b}
and a surface
S_{b}
,
m_{b}=\rho_{b}V_{b}
is the bubble mass,
\bm{\mathbf{I_{b}}}
is its moment of inertia tensor,

n
is a unit vector pointing outward normal to the bubble surface, and
\mathbf{x}
is the position vector.
The fluid stress tensor
\bm{\sigma}=-p\boldsymbol{I}+\mu(\nabla\boldsymbol{u}+\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{T})
, where
\boldsymbol{I}
is the identity tensor, is solved by the immersed boundary method (IBM)
(
Verzicco 2023
;
Uhlmann 2005
)
. This method imposes a volume force
\mathbf{f}^{ibm}
on the bubble–liquid interface that couples the bubble with the liquid, and vice versa. To compute the bubble motions and determine
\mathbf{f}^{ibm}
, we use the semi-implicit method described in
Tschisgale et al. 2017
, instead of the conventional explicit method which is numerically unstable when the bubble density is low
(
Uhlmann 2005
)
.

A potential artefact of taking
\mathbf{f}_{b}=\mathbf{f}^{ibm}
in our present case is the formation of a mean upward flow driven by the rising motion of the bubbles.
To avoid such a situation, we require the net force exerted on the fluid by the bubble to be zero
(
Höfler and Schwarzer 2000
;
Chouippe and Uhlmann 2015
)
. This is achieved by subtracting from
\mathbf{f}^{ibm}
its spatial average

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{f}^{ibm}\rangle_{\Pi}(t)=\frac{1}{||\Pi||}\int_{\Pi}\mathbf{f}^{ibm}(\mathbf{x},~t)\rm{d}\mathbf{x}

(33)

at every time step, where
\Pi
denotes the entire computational domain,
\langle\cdots\rangle_{\Pi}
indicates spatial averaging over
\Pi
, and
||\Pi||
is the volume of
\Pi
. Hence, the force exerted by the bubble on the fluid is

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}_{b}(\mathbf{x},t)=\mathbf{f}^{ibm}(\mathbf{x},t)-\langle\mathbf{f}^{ibm}\rangle_{\Pi}(t).

(34)

In contrast to the bubbles, the size of the mineral particles in the flotation processes are comparable to the Kolmogorov length scale
\eta
, meaning they can be modelled as point-like particles. Here, we consider one-way coupled non-settling particles that can overlap with each other and only experience the drag, pressure gradient, and added mass forces, which is consistent with the modelling assumptions in
Jiang and Krug 2025
. The particle dynamics is therefore governed by (
15
), except all bubble quantities are replaced by those of the particles, the gravity term is ignored, and
f_{p}=1+0.15Re_{p}^{0.687}
.
The particle equation is solved using a finite-difference scheme where the spatial derivatives are discretised using a second-order central finite-difference method and time marching is performed with a second-order Adams–Bashforth scheme using the same time step size as the fluid solver. Since the terms involving
\mathbf{u}
need to be evaluated at particle positions, their values are interpolated from the Eulerian grid using a tri-linear scheme.

Standard deviation of the bubble slip velocity component

We next consider the other quantity needed to construct

) in our model tacitly assumes that far upstream where the flow field is undistorted, the fluid velocity can be considered as uniform across the surface area bounded by the grazing trajectories (refer to figure
1
). This assumption is more likely to be violated with larger bubbles in the same background turbulence. We emphasise that the above has no bearing on
\Gamma^{sim}
because it is determined by directly counting the number of collisions. These explanations are furthermore consistent with the fact that
\Gamma^{sim}
in the present simulations agrees very well with
\Gamma^{f(w_{b})}
when
St_{p}=0
(shown only for
1/Fr\geq 4
in figure
5
), where
\Gamma^{sim}
takes a higher value than when
St_{p}\approx 0.01
. The slight reduction of
\Gamma^{sim}
with increasing
St_{p}
suggests that for the tested values of
Re_{\lambda}
,
St_{b}
and
1/Fr
, turbulence activates a ‘negative’ inertia effect at small
St_{p}
because the collision angle
\theta
defined relative to the bubble slip velocity is increased
(
Jiang and Krug 2025
)
. When the particles close to the bubble are advected by the local flow field at larger polar angles, they are slung away from the bubble by a centrifugal force. As this centrifugal force increases with particle inertia, the collision efficiency hence the collision rate in turbulence may decrease with increasing
St_{p}
for particles in the very small
St_{p}
regime
(
Dai et al. 1998
;
Huang et al. 2012
)
.

To put the accuracy of our model into perspective, we include the prediction by
Kostoglou et al. 2020a
,
\Gamma^{(Kos)}
, in figures
5
(
a
–
b
). Although their model was developed for particles with small
St_{p}
, it still overestimates the collision kernel even for tracer particles, with
\Gamma^{(Kos)}
showing a discrepancy at least ten times larger than that of our model. In view of the excellent agreement of our model prediction with simulation data, we use our model prediction as a benchmark and plot
\Gamma^{(Kos)}/\Gamma^{model}
for
St_{p}=0
in figure
5
(
e
). The figure shows that the overprediction persists for all
1/Fr\in[1,10]
. We note that in contrast with our model,
Kostoglou et al. 2020a
considers the relative motion driven by small-scale shear. This is done by adding to the (radial) collision velocity a contribution
u_{pt}
that is uniform over all positions on the sphere with radius
r_{c}
(
u_{pt}=r_{p}/2\cdot\langle|\partial u_{i}\partial x_{i}|\rangle=r_{p}/2\cdot\sqrt{2/(15\pi)}\sqrt{\varepsilon/\nu}
in (
9
), where the factor 1/2 is to account only for the influx). Remarkably, despite not including this effect in our model, our predictions agree well with the data for tracer particles, suggesting that this shear mechanism likely plays a relative minor role in determining the collision kernel at the present parameters. We remove this mechanism from
\Gamma^{(Kos)}
to obtain
\Gamma^{(Kos)}_{nS}
. As figure
5
(
e
) shows,
\Gamma^{(Kos)}_{nS}<\Gamma^{(Kos)}
as expected, yet
\Gamma^{(Kos)}_{nS}
still significantly and consistently overpredicts the collision rate. We therefore consider another key difference with
Kostoglou et al. 2020a
: the position of collisions.
\Gamma^{(Kos)}_{nS}
includes a significant number of collisions in the bubble wake, which is attributed to the inward branches of the bubble wake vortex pair (and is enhanced by the now-neglected small-scale fluid shear effect). Such collisions are very rarely observed in the simulations in this study as well as those in
Jiang and Krug 2025
and
Tiedemann and Fröhlich 2025
because most of the incident particles collide with the upstream hemisphere of the bubble and are subsequently reseeded, such that the particle concentration is especially low in the bubble wake. This collision scheme corresponds to the scenario where particles attach to bubbles after collision, meaning similar behaviour might be expected in reality. Removing these collisions in the wake (i.e. setting
\theta_{d}=\pi
in (
39
)) gives
\Gamma^{(Kos)}_{nSnW}
in figure
5
(
e
). Since the values of
\Gamma^{(Kos)}_{nSnW}
are still larger than those of
\Gamma^{model}
, collisions in the bubble wake alone cannot fully account for the overprediction of the model by
Kostoglou et al. 2020a
.

Taken together, this indicates that the discrepancy arises from the slip velocity magnitude and the representation of the flow distortion around the bubble in the model of
Kostoglou et al. 2020a
.
Specifically, this distortion is modelled in
Kostoglou et al. 2020a
using a fit for the radial component of the fluid velocity adopted from
Nguyen and Schulze 2004
given by

\frac{u_{r}^{(Kos)}(r_{c})}{u_{\infty}}=-(2X\cos\theta+3Y\cos^{2}\theta-Y)F,

(35)

where
X
,
Y
, and
F
are defined in (
44
),
\theta
is the polar angle with
\theta=0
pointing upstream,
u_{\infty}
is the freestream velocity, and positive values of
u_{r}^{(Kos)}
correspond to flow away from the bubble. This expression is directly tested in figure
5
(
f
) for the case of a bubble in a uniform incident flow with
Re_{b}^{\infty}=2r_{b}u_{\infty}/\nu=120
and
r_{p}/r_{b}=1/30
(see
A
for simulation details).
Re_{b}^{\infty}=120
is chosen as it is the approximate value of
\langle Re_{b}\rangle
for the turbulent cases with
(St_{b},1/Fr)=(2.8,10)
and
(6.3,4)
. Figure
5
(
f
) shows that
-u_{r}^{(Kos)}(r_{c})
slightly overpredicts the radial fluid velocity in the bubble wake, where the actual number of collisions is low due to the low particle concentration as discussed above. The radial fluid velocity on the upstream (
\theta<$$
) side of the bubble, where most of the collisions occur in the simulations, shows significant deviation between the fit and the data.
Surprisingly, the values of
-u_{r}^{(Kos)}/u_{\infty}
are much lower in this region compared to the simulation results. This implies that the overprediction of
\Gamma^{(Kos)}_{nSnW}
, at least for
(St_{b},1/Fr)=(2.8,10)
and
(6.3,4)
at the present value of
Re_{\lambda}
, is driven by a significant overprediction of the freestream bubble slip velocity in turbulence, which we partly attributed further to the modelling of the vertical component in their case. There, the mean vertical slip velocity results from a simple force balance in still fluid without considering the reduction in turbulence as discussed in §
2.1
.

Two main conclusions can be drawn from the above analysis. First,
\Gamma^{(Kos)}
consistently overestimates the collision kernel in the present parameters, partly because of collisions in the wake which become more frequent in the presence of small-scale shear. However, even when these two effects are excluded,
\Gamma^{(Kos)}_{nSnW}
still overpredicts the collision kernel since the freestream slip velocity and the distorted flow field are not accurately modelled. For
(St_{b},1/Fr)=(2.8,10)
and
(6.3,4)
at the present value of
Re_{\lambda}
, the freestream bubble slip velocity is overpredicted, though this is partially compensated by the poor agreement between the fit of the distorted flow (
35
) and the data. Therefore, caution is advised when using (
35
), as its accuracy may be limited under certain flow conditions.
Second, our model, in spite of its simplicity, captures the essential phenomena and produces predictions that are much closer to the simulated values compared to the those by
Kostoglou et al. 2020a
, even in the tracer particle limit.

5
Discussion

5.1
Model validity for the cases with lower bubble Stokes number

In this study, we did not perform (heavy) particle-laden simulations for the cases with lower
St_{b}
bubbles in view of the computation cost. Here, the validity of our model at these parameters is discussed. A key assumption in our model is the flow field around the bubble is steady during a bubble–particle collision, which can be assessed by comparing the bubble slip velocity integral time scale

T_{L}^{w}=\int_{0}^{\tau_{\infty}}\rho_{w}(\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau

(36)

with the fluid time scale based on the measured value of the bubble vertical velocity
\langle w_{b}\rangle

\tau_{f}=\frac{2r_{b}}{\langle w_{b}\rangle},

(37)

where
\rho_{w}(\tau)=\langle w_{b}(t)w_{b}(t+\tau)\rangle_{t}/\langle w_{b}^{2}(t)\rangle_{t}
is the autocorrelation function of the bubble slip velocity,
\langle\cdots\rangle_{t}
denotes averaging over time, and
\tau_{\infty}
is defined as the value of
\tau
where
\rho_{w}(\tau)
first decays to 0.01.
T_{L}^{w}/\tau_{f}\gtrsim 1
would suggest that the frozen turbulence assumption is valid and the model can be applied.
The measured values of
T_{L}^{w}/\tau_{f}
is shown in figure
6
.
T_{L}/\tau_{f}
generally increases with
1/Fr
and is far less sensitive to
St_{b}
, such that for all
St_{b}
considered here the frozen turbulence assumption can be applied when
1/Fr\geq 4
. For
St_{b}=0.5
and 1, we expect that modelling the slip velocity p.d.f.
f(w_{b})
by (
16
) and (
28
) might slightly underpredict the collision kernel, since the mean value of the slip velocity magnitude is underpredicted as shown in figures
4
(
a
–
b
). This discrepancy in
f(w_{b})
for
1/Fr\geq 4
is not due to using the mean vertical rise velocity instead of the magnitude of the bubble rise velocity in the bubble drag correction factor
f_{b}
in (
28
), because the bubble velocity at high
1/Fr
is dominated by its vertical component. As evidence, we plot
f(w_{b})
with
\sigma_{i}^{(BC)}
determined using the average
f_{b}
obtained from the simulations as dashed green lines in figure
4
. Only at small
1/Fr
does using the actual value of
f_{b}
noticeably improve the quality of the predicted slip velocity p.d.f.. If desired for these cases, a more accurate estimate of
\sigma_{i}^{(BC)}
can be obtained
a priori
by solving (
28
) iteratively using
f_{b}=1+0.169(2r_{b}\langle w_{b}\rangle/\nu)^{2/3}
as an initial guess. We additionally note that particles with
St_{p}\sim 1
preferentially sample flow regions in turbulence which can reduce
\Gamma
by up to 20%
(
Jiang and Krug 2025
)
. Similarly preferential sampling occurs for bubbles with
St_{b}\sim 1
but they cluster at different regions, which means that bubbles and particles segregate spatially when
(St_{b},St_{p})\sim(1,1)
unless
1/Fr\gtrsim 10
where segregation becomes negligible under strong gravity
(
Chan et al. 2025
)
. This segregation effect is not included in our model and reduces the collision kernel, hence for
(St_{b},St_{p})\sim(1,1)
at intermediate values of
1/Fr
the predicted value of the collision kernel may be closer to the actual value than anticipated.

5.2
Implications for practice

5.2.1
Relevant parameter space

In order to evaluate the model for practically relevant conditions, we consider
r_{b}=0.05\,$\mathrm{mm}$
,
0.5\,$\mathrm{mm}$
, and
2\,$\mathrm{mm}$

(
Ngo-Cong et al. 2018
;
Ahmed and Jameson 1985
)
;
r_{p}\in[1,200]\,$\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$

(
Ngo-Cong et al. 2018
)
;
\varepsilon\in[0.1,100]\,$\mathrm{W}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{g}$

(
Li et al. 2021
)
; and
Re_{\lambda}=100
for sulphide minerals in water (
\rho_{b}=1.2\,$\mathrm{k}\mathrm{g}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{m}^{3}$
,
\rho_{p}=5000\,$\mathrm{k}\mathrm{g}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{m}^{3}$
,
\rho_{f}=998\,$\mathrm{k}\mathrm{g}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{m}^{3}$
,
\nu=1.002\times 10^{-6}\,$\mathrm{m}^{2}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{s}$
and
\mathfrak{g}=9.81\,$\mathrm{m}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{s}^{2}$
). Note that here we assume
Re_{\lambda}=\textrm{const.}
, in contrast to
Ngo-Cong et al. 2018
where
u^{\prime}
is fixed. Our choice implies that
u^{\prime}
increases with
\varepsilon
, and corresponds to the more physical scenario where a higher rotor speed drives stronger turbulence at both small and large scales. Furthermore,
Re_{\lambda}=100
was selected such that when
\varepsilon=100\,$\mathrm{W}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{g}$
, then
u^{\prime 2}\approx 0.25\,$\mathrm{m}^{2}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{s}^{2}$
, which falls within the range of values of
u^{\prime 2}
considered in
Kostoglou et al. 2020a
.

Depending on the specific value within the broad range of possible dissipation rates
\varepsilon
, the maximum stable bubble size varies because bubbles tend to break up when the local fluid shear becomes sufficiently large
(
Kolmogorov 1949
;
Hinze 1955
)
. Quantitatively, this behaviour is captured by a critical bubble Weber number
We_{ISR}=2.13\rho_{f}(2r_{b}\varepsilon)^{2/3}(2r_{b})/\gamma
beyond which a bubble cannot remain stable
(
Masuk et al. 2021
)
. Here, the velocity scaling in the inertial subrange is used and
\gamma=73\,$\mathrm{m}\mathrm{N}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{m}$
is the surface tension of water.
The nominal value for
We_{crit}
reported in the literature vary
(
Hinze 1955
;
Risso and Fabre 1998
)
,
which is indicated in figure
7
(
a
) along with the values of
We_{ISR}
for the combinations of
r_{b}
and
\varepsilon
investigated here. Figure
7
(
a
) shows that while most of the combinations can stably exist when accounting for the spread in
We_{crit}
, bubbles with
r_{b}=2\,$\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}$
would break up when
\varepsilon=100\,$\mathrm{W}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{g}$
. This is further consistent with the very high average bubble Reynolds number,
\langle Re_{b}\rangle=2r_{b}\langle|\mathbf{w_{b}}|\rangle/\nu
, estimated by our model for this case. As shown in figure
7
(
b
),
\langle Re_{b}\rangle
exceeds 6000. Based on these results, we will not evaluate our model predictions for the
(r_{b},\varepsilon)=(2\,$\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}$,100\,$\mathrm{W}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{g}$)
case.

Figure 7:
(
a
) The bubble Weber number based on the fluid velocity difference across the bubble. The shaded area indicates the critical Weber number (
We_{crit}\in[1.25,7.8]
) beyond which bubbles break up. The data markers show the selected values of
\varepsilon
. Note that the lower limit of
We_{crit}
is slightly different from the value reported by

since a coefficient of 2.13 instead of 2 is used here for the inertial subrange scaling of the second-order structure function. (

) are guides for the eye.

In addition to the physical limit imposed on
r_{b}
by the background turbulence, there is an inherent maximum floatable particle size since the buoyancy of the bubble–particle aggregate must be larger than its weight for the aggregate to surface. A simple force balance gives
r_{p}/r_{b}<((1-\rho_{b}/\rho_{f})/(\rho_{p}/\rho_{f}-1))^{1/3}
. This requirement is satisfied for all
r_{p}\leq 200\,$\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$
when
r_{b}\geq 0.5\,$\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}$
and for
r_{p}\leq 31\,$\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$
when
r_{b}=0.05\,$\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}$
(which is indicated by open circles in the figures in §
5.2.2
). We acknowledge that the actual upper limit on
r_{p}
is more stringent since it is also influenced by factors such as particle detachment by the background turbulence
(
Nguyen et al. 2016
)
. These complications are not considered here as they lie beyond the scope of this study and do not affect the discussion below.

5.2.2
Collision rate predictions and implications

Figure 8:
(
a
) The predicted collision kernel and (
b
) its value when normalised by the high-inertia limit. The legends apply to both panels (
a
) and (
b
); the open circles indicate the maximum floatable particle size. (
c
) The bubble Weber number based on the bubble slip velocity. The lines in panel (

) are guides for the eye.

Figure
8
(
a
) shows that the predicted collision kernel
\Gamma
increases monotonically with
r_{p}
for the selected parameters.
The behaviour of
\Gamma
as a function of
r_{p}
can be divided into three distinct regimes. We discuss these in detail for the case
(r_{b},\varepsilon)=(0.5\,$\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}$,0.1\,$\mathrm{W}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{g}$)
, noting that similar trends are observed across other parameter configurations.
For small particle sizes (
r_{p}\lesssim 7\,$\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$
),
\Gamma\propto r_{p}^{2}
because the instantaneous particle Stokes number
St_{p}^{\prime}
is sufficiently low, such that the interception mechanism
E_{c}^{(i)}\propto r_{p}^{2}
dominates (see (
5
)). In contrast, when
r_{p}
is large (
r_{p}\gtrsim 100\,$\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$
),
\Gamma\propto(1+r_{p}/r_{b})^{2}
. This scaling is more clearly demonstrated in figure
8
(
b
) by the plateau in the normalised quantity
\Gamma/(1+r_{p}/r_{b})^{2}
. This corresponds to the fully-inertial large-
St_{p}^{\prime}
limit, where the collision efficiency
E_{c}\approx E_{c}^{(in)}
and
(St_{p}^{\prime}/(St_{p}^{\prime}+a))^{b}\to 1
, such that
E_{c}\propto(1+r_{p}/r_{b})^{2}
. In both extremes,
E_{c}
no longer depends on
St_{p}^{\prime}
, such that the integrand in (
11
) becomes independent of
r_{p}
.
In between these extremes (
7\,$\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$\lesssim r_{p}\lesssim 100\,$\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$
), both interception and inertial mechanisms play a role (hereafter referred to as the ‘transition regime’), so
E_{c}
is a function of
St_{p}^{\prime}
and must remain inside the integral. Hence, no simple scaling with
r_{p}
is discernible. Notably, in this regime,
\Gamma
can exceed the pure interception scaling by an order of magnitude, highlighting the significance of inertial effects. This qualitative trend with
r_{p}
holds for all the values of
r_{b}
considered, though for
r_{b}=0.05\,$\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}$
the fully inertial limit is not reached even for the maximum floatable values of
r_{p}
at the smaller values of
\varepsilon
. Quantitatively, increasing
r_{b}
leads to larger values of
\Gamma
, partly because
\Gamma\propto r_{b}^{2}
by geometry (see (
3
)).

Higher values of
\Gamma
are also predicted by our model for larger values of
\varepsilon
. The increase in
\Gamma
is driven by multiple factors
since
f(w_{b})
as well as
Re_{b}
, and hence also
St_{p}^{\prime}
all vary. Furthermore, the boundaries of the transition regime clearly shifts towards smaller values of
r_{p}
at larger values of
\varepsilon
. This is because
\varepsilon
and
\langle|\mathbf{w_{b}}|\rangle
are positively correlated for the parameters considered, which can be inferred from the increase of
\langle Re_{b}\rangle=2r_{b}\langle|\mathbf{w_{b}}|\rangle/\nu
with
\varepsilon
in figure
7
(
b
) (note that the increase is driven by enhanced turbulent fluctuations of the bubble slip velocity, while the mean vertical slip velocity
\langle w_{b}\rangle
is reduced in accordance with (
16
)). Therefore, particles of a particular size would have a larger
St_{p}^{\prime}
on average for larger values of
\varepsilon
, such that the inertial mechanism is activated and the fully inertial limit is reached at lower values of
r_{p}
. Additionally, this increase in
\langle|\mathbf{w_{b}}|\rangle
raises the associated bubble Weber number defined with the bubble slip velocity
We_{s}=2\rho_{f}r_{b}\langle|\mathbf{w_{b}}|\rangle^{2}/\gamma
. As figure
8
(
c
) shows,
We_{s}\gtrsim 1
for
\varepsilon\gtrsim 1\,$\mathrm{W}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{g}$
when
r_{b}\gtrsim 0.5\,$\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}$
, which suggests that the spherical bubble assumption may no longer hold, hence the quantitative predictions in this parameter range should be used with caution. Nonetheless, deviations from the spherical bubble assumption are likely higher-order effects. In general, our model prediction at typical flotation parameters indicate that inertial effects are not negligible and need to be taken into account. This is true even for
r_{p}=7\,$\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$
which is encountered in fine particle flotation
(
Lynch et al. 1981
)
as long as
\varepsilon
is sufficiently large (
\varepsilon\gtrsim 5\,$\mathrm{W}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{g}$
for
r_{b}=0.05\,$\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}$
).

While the collision kernel facilitates comparison between scenarios with different bubble and particle number densities, it is not the most direct comparison in practice when
r_{b}
varies since it implies that the gas holdup
\varepsilon_{g}\propto r_{b}^{3}
, which may not be the case
(
Yianatos et al. 2010
)
. This is an important detail, especially for the flotation of fine particles. In this context, the mineral recovery rate is low due to reduced collision efficiency at small
r_{p}
, so smaller bubbles are employed to mitigate this problem
(
Ahmed and Jameson 1985
;
Miettinen et al. 2010
)
. The collision kernel then takes a lower value as has already been observed in figure
8
(
a
). However, considering only
\Gamma
in this scenario ignores the positive effect on the overall collision rate due to the increase in the bubble number density assuming a constant gas volume flow rate. Therefore, in figure
9
we plot
\Gamma n_{b}
(i.e. the collision rate per particle) for constant
\varepsilon_{g}
at a typical value of 10%
(
Yianatos et al. 2010
)
. As expected, the shape of the curves are identical to those in figure
8
(
a
); though crucially,
\Gamma n_{b}
increases when
r_{b}
decreases. This shows that using smaller bubbles has a net positive effect on the collision rate, and our model prediction supports the usage of smaller bubbles for flotation, which is consistent with results in the literature
(
Ahmed and Jameson 1985
;
Miettinen et al. 2010
)
.

6
Conclusion

In this study, we develop a model that predicts
a priori
the bubble–particle collision rate of inertial particles in homogeneous isotropic turbulence given the bubble, particle, and turbulence properties: namely, the bubble radius, the bubble density, the particle radius, the particle response time, the liquid kinematic viscosity, the liquid density, the Taylor Reynolds number, the dissipation rate, and gravity. This is achieved by adopting the frozen turbulence approximation proposed by
Jiang and Krug 2025
, such that the existing parametrisations of the bubble–particle collision efficiency in still fluid can be applied. The main input required for this approach is the bubble slip speed p.d.f.. We assume independent bubble slip velocity components and model their probability density functions using normal distributions with the mean of the vertical component given by
Ruth et al. 2021
and
Liu et al. 2024
(i.e. (
16
)), and the standard deviation given by
Berk and Coletti 2024
(i.e. (
28
)). The resulting bubble slip speed p.d.f.s are compared with finite-size bubble simulations over
1\leq 1/Fr\leq 10
with
0.5\leq St_{b}\leq 6.3
and
Re_{\lambda}=64
. Good agreement is observed for
1/Fr\geq 4
. For
St_{b}=2.8
and
6.3
, we additionally calculate the bubble–particle collision rates from the simulations and find satisfactory agreement with the model predictions. This result is consistent with our proposed requirement that
T_{L}^{w}/\tau_{f}\gtrsim 1
for the frozen turbulence approximation, which suggests that the model should be valid for
1/Fr\geq 4
when
0.5\leq St_{b}\leq 6.3
and
Re_{\lambda}=64
. Applying our model to typical flotation parameters confirms that the collision rate can be raised by using larger particles, smaller bubbles, or stronger turbulence. Importantly, we find that even for fine particles, particle inertia can play a significant role in determining the overall collision rate.

In summary, our model includes the particle inertia effect on the collision rate and provides significantly better predictions of the bubble–particle collision rate in homogeneous isotropic turbulence than existing theories, even at the
St_{p}\to 0
limit. Considering the high inhomogeneity of the turbulence in actual flotation cells, our model can be used on a subgrid-scale level in industrial computer fluid dynamics simulations to predict the local collision rate, at least for smaller bubble and particle Stokes numbers where the behaviour of the dispersed phases is determined by the local flow field.
Potential future improvements of the model include a more accurate representation of
E_{c}^{(in)}(St_{p})
, to more faithfully capture the dependence of the collision rate on
St_{p}
;
as well as incorporating the turbulence-induced spatial segregation of bubbles and particles
(
Calzavarini et al. 2008a
;
Chan et al. 2023
;
Chan et al. 2025
)
, which can reduce the collision rate especially when
St_{p}\approx 1

(
Jiang and Krug 2025
)
.
