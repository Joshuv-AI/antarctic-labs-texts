Laparoscopic cholecystectomy and common bile duct exploration for gallstone and common bile duct stone in a patient with a left-sided gallbladder: a case report
Authors: Takeshi Ueda, Tetsuya Tanaka, Yuki Kirihataya, Chisato Hara, Atsushi Yoshimura
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractLeft-sided gallbladder is a rare finding that is mostly discovered incidentally during surgery and is often associated with anatomic anomalies. We herein report a case in which laparoscopic cholecystectomy and common bile duct exploration were achieved for an 89-year-old female patient with left-sided gallbladder. Surgery was carried out using our usual trocar position. Calot triangle was covered by the body of the gallbladder and could not be detected. We dissected the gallbladder from the fundus towards the neck. The cystic duct joined the common bile duct from the right side, and common bile duct exploration was performed routinely without perioperative comorbidities. Although the preoperative diagnosis rate is low and the risk of intraoperative bile duct injuries in patients with left-sided gallbladder is high, laparoscopic cholecystectomy and common bile duct exploration can be safely performed by understanding the location and bifurcation of the cystic duct.Keywords: Left-sided gallbladder, Common bile duct stones, Laparoscopic cholecystectomy

Introduction

Left-sided gallbladder (LSG) is a rare congenital malposition in which the gallbladder is situated against the left lobe of the liver; the reported incidence is 0.4% to 1.2 % [1–3]. LSG is often associated with right-sided ligamentum teres (RSLT) due to a remnant right fetal umbilical vein instead of a left fetal umbilical vein [4,5]. LSG is a rare finding that is often incidentally discovered during laparoscopic cholecystectomy. When performing hepatic resection in patients with LSG, surgeons should be aware of vessel branch variations. In laparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC) and laparoscopic common bile duct exploration (LCBDE), there are variations of the cystic duct and common bile duct (CBD) [6]. We report a case in which LC and LCBDE for gallstones and CBD stones were successfully performed for a patient with LSG and RSLT.

CASE REPORT

An 89-year-old female patient was referred to our hospital because of right upper quadrant pain, which was suspected to be due to cholecystitis and cholangitis. A gallstone and CBD stone were pointed out more than 1 year previously during endoscopic treatment for early gastric cancer, but treatment was not performed because it was asymptomatic. Her other comorbidities included hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and gastric ulcer. A physical examination showed normal vital signs without jaundice. An abdominal examination demonstrated moderate tenderness in the right quadrant area without Murphy sign. The laboratory data showed an elevated white blood cell count (11,700/μL; normal range, 3,300–8,600 μL), and C-reactive protein level (9.25 mg/dL; normal range, <0.30 mg/dL). Her hepatobiliary enzyme levels were slightly elevated (aminotransferase, 58 IU/L [normal range, 13–30 IU/L]; γ-glutamyltranspeptidase, 62 IU/L [normal range, 9–32 IU/L]), whereas her bilirubin level was within normal limit (total bilirubin, 0.59 mg/dL [normal range, <1.50 mg/dL]). Abdominal computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography showed a small gallstone and CBD stone (sized 10 mm), with slight CBD dilatation to 10 mm in diameter (Fig. 1). We tried to remove the CBD stone endoscopically, but could not perform endoscopic sphincterotomy due to edema of the papilla of Vater. After treating the cholangitis conservatively, we performed LC and LCBDE under an assumed diagnosis of gallstone and CBD stone.

Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography showed a 10-mm common bile duct (CBD) stone and slight dilatation of the CBD. The cystic duct joined the CBD from the right side.

Four ports were placed in the typical French configuration for LC, and an additional port was placed for the cholangioscope. The gallbladder was located left of the ligamentum teres of the liver (Fig. 2), and Calot triangle was covered by the gallbladder body and could not be detected. We dissected the gallbladder from the fundus towards the neck to maintain the distance between the cystic duct and the CBD. When the cystic duct was dissected circumferentially, we recognized that the cystic duct joined the CBD from the right side (Fig. 3). LCBDE was performed with longitudinal incision of approximately 1.0 cm. A CBD stone was removed by cholangioscopy and a C-tube was subsequently placed. The postoperative cholangiogram was normal, and the C-tube was removed. The patient was uneventfully discharged on postoperative day 10. Upon retrospective interpretation of abdominal CT, we recognized that the gallbladder was close to the lateral segments of the liver and located to the left of the ligamentum teres (Fig. 4).

The gallbladder (GB) was located left of the ligamentum teres (LT) of the liver.

The cystic duct joined the common bile duct (CBD) from the right side. Laparoscopic CBD exploration was performed with a longitudinal incision of approximately 1.0 cm. A C-tube was placed from the cystic duct.

DISCUSSION

LSG has been defined as the displacement of the gallbladder to the left side of the ligamentum teres without situs inversus [1]. LSG is a rare congenital malposition, in LC, and approximately 70% of cases of LSG are discovered during surgery [7]. Pereira et al. [7] described five variants of cystic duct union; in 65.6% of patients, the cystic duct passed over the CBD and connected to the CBD on the right side. Other variants of union were left of the CBD (9.5%), left hepatic duct (9.5%), right hepatic duct (7.6%), and a branch of the right hepatic duct (2.4%). CBD injury occurred in 4.4% of patients due to variants of cystic duct union. Surgeons should be aware that the cystic duct lies anterior to the CBD in most patients with LSG. When a critical view of safety is not secured, it may be acceptable to change the direction of dissection from the fundus to the neck of the gallbladder or subtotal cholecystectomy. Even if the LSG is diagnosed preoperatively, placement of the usual trocar position may be acceptable, as the critical aspect is the procedure of the anterior CBD. Regarding LCBDE, as approximately 70% of patients have a connection to the right side of the CBD and the CBD anterior surface has already been dissected, the typical technique for LCBDE can be used in most cases. Even with other types of connection in the CBD, if the anterior surface of the CBD could be carefully identified, the typical procedure seemed to be performed.

LSG without malposition of the ligamentum teres is considered extremely rare [1]. As a pathogenesis of LSG that is unrelated to RSLT, it has been proposed that the gallbladder is located on the left side during organogenesis or develops from the left hepatic duct [1]. Some articles reported that the LSG may not represent a true displacement of the gallbladder, but may occur along with RSLT due to a remnant right umbilical vein [4,5,8,9]. RSLT was reported to occur in 0.45% of approximately 10,000 cases, and LSG occurred in 55% of cases with RSLT [4]. RSLT is classified into four types; bilateral ligamentum teres (BLT), bifurcation type, trifurcation type, and independent posterior branch type [4,5]. The BLT is extremely rare; Terasaki et al. [10] reported one case. In RSLT, the volume of the right hepatic lobe is larger than normal, but there is no branching abnormality. Thus, the cystic duct bifurcation is most likely to branch from the right side of the CBD. At this point, surgeons should pay alert for the CBD injury as the cystic duct is above the CBD during LC. If the cystic duct can be safely dissected, LCBDE may be safely performed for patients with LSG.

In conclusion, most LSGs are malpositioned by RSLT. Although the preoperative diagnosis rate is low, with an understanding of the location and bifurcation of the cystic duct, LC and LCBDE can be performed safely for patients with LSG with the usual trocar position.
