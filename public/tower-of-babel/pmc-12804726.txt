Hybrid 3D-printed/electrospun scaffolds drive myogenic differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs)
Authors: Silvia Pisani, Stefania Marconi, Valeria Mauri, Beatrice Rossetti, Aleksandra Evangelista, Giovanna Bruni, Marco Benazzo, Ferdinando Auricchio, Bice Conti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe development of functional scaffolds that support cell differentiation and tissue regeneration remains a major challenge in regenerative medicine. In this study, we designed and fabricated hybrid scaffolds (HS) composed of poly(lactic-co-caprolactone) (PLA-PCL) by integrating 3D extrusion-based printing and electrospinning techniques. Two distinct 3D-printed architectures were explored—aligned filaments and grid patterns—which were subsequently coated with a layer of electrospun nanofibers to better mimic the anisotropic and hierarchical structure of native skeletal muscle tissue. Bone marrow porcine mesenchymal stem cells (p-MSCs) were seeded onto the scaffolds and cultured under myogenic conditions. To evaluate the progression of myogenic differentiation, we assessed the expression of early and late myogenic markers, MyoD and Myogenin respectively, at 14, 21 and 28 days. A comprehensive physical-mechanical characterization was performed, including morphological analysis, porosity measurements, and uniaxial tensile testing. The results demonstrated that the HS provided a biomimetic microenvironment that supported p-MSC attachment, viability, and differentiation. Notably, the HS aligned architecture enhanced the expression of myogenic markers compared to the grid design, suggesting a role of topographical cues in directing lineage commitment. These findings highlight the potential of dual-fabricated PLA-PCL scaffolds as a promising platform for guiding myogenic differentiation and may serve as a foundation for promoting functional skeletal muscle regeneration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-31586-x.Keywords: Hybrid scaffolds, 3D-Printing: electrospinning: tissue engineering, Myogenic differentiation, Mesenchymal stem cellsSubject terms: Biotechnology, Cell biology, Materials science, Stem cells

Introduction

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are adult multipotent stromal cells with a well-documented capacity to modulate regenerative microenvironment and support tissue repair through both paracrine signaling and differentiation1. MSCs exert significant trophic effects via the secretion of cytokines, growth factors, and extracellular vesicles that contribute to angiogenesis, immunomodulation, and the inhibition of apoptosis2,3. Notably, their immunoregulatory properties include the downregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin (IL)−1β, IL-6, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and interferon-α (IFN-α), alongside the upregulation of anti-inflammatory mediators like IL-10. This immunomodulatory profile makes MSCs promising candidates for therapeutic applications in various inflammatory and degenerative diseases4.

In addition to their secretome-mediated effects, MSCs are capable of differentiating into multiple cell lineages of mesodermal origin, including osteoblasts, chondrocytes and adipocytes5. This intrinsic plasticity has positioned MSCs at the forefront of regenerative medicine strategies aimed at restoring the structural and functional integrity of damaged tissues. However, successful differentiation and integration of MSCs in vitro are highly dependent on the surrounding microenvironment, including biochemical cues and biophysical stimuli6,7.

Three-dimensional (3D) scaffolds have emerged as critical tools in tissue engineering, providing not only mechanical support but also a biomimetic architecture that guides cell behavior8. Essential scaffold features such as high porosity, interconnectivity, and tunable mechanical properties ensure adequate oxygen and nutrient diffusion, as well as efficient waste removal. These features collectively support cell viability and metabolic activity while mimicking aspects of the native extracellular matrix (ECM). Modulating stem cell differentiation through the tailored design of scaffold properties offers a sophisticated strategy in tissue regeneration, with the potential to reduce or even replace the need for external differentiation stimuli such as viral vectors or pharmacological agents9,10.

Among the various scaffold fabrication techniques, electrospinning has gained prominence due to its ability to produce nanofibrous networks that closely resemble the fibrillar architecture of the ECM11. Electrospun nanofibers can be engineered to display specific surface topographies, alignment, and diameters, that have profound effects on cell adhesion, morphology, and fate decisions. Recent advances have combined electrospinning with 3D printing technologies, leading to the development of hybrid scaffolds that integrate nanoscale features with customizable macroscale geometries. These composite constructs enable the precise spatial control of scaffold architecture while maintaining a high degree of biological relevance12–14.

Importantly, it has become increasingly evident that the biophysical properties of scaffolds—such as stiffness, topography, and fiber orientation actively influence stem cell fate. These cues are transduced through mechano-transduction pathways, leading to lineage-specific gene expression programs. Several studies have demonstrated that by modulating scaffold stiffness and structure, it is possible to direct MSC differentiation toward specific lineages, including osteogenic, chondrogenic, and myogenic pathways. Therefore, scaffold design plays a pivotal role not only in cell support but also in the modulation of stem cell behavior and differentiation potential15–17.

Skeletal muscle regeneration, under physiological conditions, relies on a population of tissue-resident stem cells known as satellite cells. First described by Mauro in 1961, satellite cells reside in a unique niche between the sarcolemma and the basal lamina of muscle fibers18. In response to stimuli such as trauma, exercise, or pathological damage, these quiescent cells become activated and upregulate key myogenic regulatory factors including Pax7, MyoD, and Myf5, initiating a cascade of cell viability, proliferation and differentiation. Activated satellite cells give rise to myoblasts, which subsequently fuse to form multinucleated myotubes, a process critically dependent on the expression of myogenin (Myf4)19. A portion of the activated satellite cells returns to quiescence to replenish the stem cell pool, ensuring long-term regenerative capacity20. Despite this intrinsic regenerative ability, volumetric muscle loss (VML)—resulting from severe trauma, surgical excision, or pathological conditions—often exceeds the repair capacity of the endogenous satellite cell population. In such cases, the damaged muscle fails to regenerate effectively, leading to chronic functional impairment and fibrosis21.

To overcome these limitations, several studies have investigated the use of MSCs for skeletal muscle repair, capitalizing on their immunomodulatory and regenerative properties. For instance, bone marrow-derived MSCs (BM-MSCs) were shown to enhance muscle regeneration in preclinical models of acute injury. Their therapeutic efficacy is attributed to a combination of anti-inflammatory actions and pro-regenerative effects, including improved myofiber regeneration and neovascularization22.

Although MSCs show intrinsic potential for myogenic differentiation, this process is inefficient in standard two-dimensional culture systems and often requires external cues or genetic manipulation23,24. Recent efforts have focused on leveraging engineered microenvironments to enhance the myogenic potential of MSCs. In particular, scaffolds with specific mechanical and topographical properties can emulate the native muscle niche and provide instructive cues that facilitate myogenic lineage commitment. Goldshmid et al., investigated how the mechanical properties of the hydrogel influence the differentiation of human mesenchymal stromal cells (hMSCs). Specifically, myogenic differentiation was found to be optimal in materials with an intermediate shear storage modulus (G’ ≈ 500 Pa). Under these conditions, hMSCs showed gene and protein expression profiles consistent with commitment toward the muscle lineage. This indicates that a moderate matrix stiffness in the 3D environment promotes myogenic differentiation, suggesting that tuning the mechanical modulus of the scaffold is a key parameter for directing muscle tissue formation in tissue engineering applications25. Several studies demonstrated the effectiveness of polymeric nanofiber-based scaffolds in promoting myogenic differentiation of MSCs derived from various sources, including bone marrow, adipose tissue, and umbilical cord26–29. These scaffolds, which often replicate key features of the native extracellular matrix, not only provide a biomimetic structural support but also exhibit mechanical properties—such as an average ultimate tensile strength of 13.22 ± 1.90 MPa, elastic modulus of around 105.14 MPa, and elongation at break of 33.14%— that can influence stem cell fate through mechanotransductive signaling. In parallel, these systems are frequently combined with external biochemical inducers, including transforming growth factor-beta 1 (TGF-β1), dexamethasone, and hydrocortisone, to further enhance myogenic gene expression and commitment.

In this study, we investigate the role of hybrid scaffolds combining 3D printing and electrospinning technologies in directing the myogenic differentiation of porcine MSCs. By integrating the architectural advantages of 3D printing with the biomimetic features of electrospun nanofibers, we aim to develop a platform that supports MSC viability, enhances lineage-specific differentiation, and ultimately contributes to functional muscle tissue engineering. This platform may serve as a foundation for next-generation solutions in muscle repair and regeneration.

Results

Hybrid scaffolds manufacturing

Hybrid scaffolds (HS) were fabricated by combining 3D-printed architectures—either aligned filaments or grid-like patterns—with a coating of electrospun nanofibers to enhance biomimicry. Figure 1a, b shows the HS aligned, while Fig. 1c, d shows the HS grid configuration. The table in Fig. 1e summarizes structural parameters for each scaffold type, including distance between printed filaments, filament width, overall weight, and thickness. Addition of the electrospun layer, led to a moderate increase in weight (from 23.58 ± 1.97 mg to 26.2 ± 2.22 mg for aligned scaffolds; from 22.14 ± 2.15 mg to 24.6 ± 3.25 mg for grid scaffolds), with thickness remaining consistent. The HS aligned scaffolds exhibited narrower inter-filament spacing (1013 ± 19 μm) and greater filament width (336 ± 67 μm) compared to the grid version (1159 ± 54 μm spacing; 271 ± 41 μm width), reflecting their more compact and anisotropic architecture. These structural features contribute to the biomechanical and biological performance observed in downstream analyses.

Distance between infills, filament width, weight and thickness of 3D and hybrid (HS) aligned and grid scaffolds.

Electrospun layer addition led to an average increase in scaffold weight of approximately 2.6 mg for the aligned configuration and 2.5 mg for the grid configuration. Thickness values remained relatively stable following HS coating, with only minor variations within the margin of error. The relatively modest variation in weight and thickness across all groups indicates good control over scaffold fabrication, both before and after electrospinning. The similar trends observed in both aligned and grid scaffolds following HS coating support the consistency and reproducibility of the 3D printing and electrospinning processes across different geometries. This consistency is further explained by the fact that the 3D-printed scaffold architecture predominantly determines the total thickness of the hybrid construct, as the electrospun fiber layer represents only a thin coating relative to the bulk of the structure.

Hybrid scaffolds characterization

The fiber diameter, surface porosity and predominant orientation of the electrospun fibers, as assessed through ImageJ analysis of SEM images, are summarized in Fig. 2. To enhance the accuracy of porosity measurements, the threshold function in ImageJ was applied to improve contrast and clearly delineate fiber boundaries from the red background (Fig. 1b and e).

HS aligned scaffold (a) SEM images of electrospun side, threshold (b) and orientation (c) and HS grid scaffolds (d) SEM images of electrospun side, threshold (e) and orientation (f). In (g) are reported values of fiber’s diameter, pore’s area, fiber’s density and orientation.

The data indicate that electrospinning onto 3D-printed scaffolds with a 20% grid infill resulted in significantly thinner fibers (0.93 ± 0.13 μm) compared to aligned scaffolds (1.6 ± 0.21 μm), as well as a more compact matrix, reflected by the reduced pore area (3.59 ± 1.07 μm² vs. 6.21 ± 1.55 μm², respectively). Fiber density was comparable between the two scaffold types, with 46.56% for aligned and 46.09% for grid scaffolds. Fiber orientation analysis showed a difference in mean fiber angles between aligned and grid scaffolds (16.42° vs. 43.10°), suggesting distinct deposition patterns. These results indicate that scaffold geometry influences fiber deposition, potentially affecting the microstructure, porosity, and topographical cues critical for cell behavior.

Mechanical characterization

Results of mechanical characterization, schematized in Figs. 3, show a clear distinction between the two geometries. Aligned scaffolds exhibited significantly higher mechanical strength, with an average peak stress of 5.39 ± 1.60 MPa and an average Young’s modulus of 137.80 ± 18.20 MPa. In contrast, grid scaffolds demonstrated a markedly lower mechanical performance, with an average peak stress of 1.62 ± 0.38 MPa and an average Young’s modulus of 50.08 ± 12.05 MPa. Additionally, grid scaffolds exhibit greater elongation at break.

(a) Stress-strain curves for aligned (green data) and grid (red data) scaffolds; (b) mechanical parameters for grid and aligned scaffolds, namely peak stress, Young Modulus and Elongation at Break.

These findings can be attributed to the intrinsic differences in structural alignment. In aligned scaffolds, the printed filaments are oriented predominantly in the direction of applied tensile force. This unidirectional architecture enhances load transfer along the tensile axis, thereby increasing stiffness and resistance to deformation. Conversely, the grid scaffolds present a more isotropic, multi-directional fiber distribution. While this configuration may improve flexibility and isotropic mechanical behavior, it also leads to a lower modulus and stress resistance under uniaxial tensile loading due to the inefficient load transmission across non-aligned filaments. The higher variability observed in the modulus of aligned scaffolds (standard deviation = 18.20 MPa) compared to the grid scaffolds (standard deviation = 12.05 MPa) may be due to subtle inconsistencies in print quality or electrospun fiber deposition affecting the load-bearing axis more significantly in the aligned configuration.

Overall, these results underscore the importance of scaffold geometry in tuning mechanical properties, with aligned architectures offering superior tensile performance that may be advantageous in applications where directional strength is required, such as tendon or ligament tissue engineering.

Scaffold biologic characterization

Metabolic activity and cell viability of porcine mesenchymal stem cells (p-MSCs) cultured in myogenic medium (MM) were assessed on both traditional two-dimensional (2D) surfaces and three-dimensional (3D) hybrid scaffolds (HS) with aligned and grid geometries. Evaluations were conducted at 14, 21 and 28 days using the MTT assay (Fig. 4a) and resulted in clear differences in metabolic activity across experimental conditions. The 2D control groups (MSCs cultured in standard medium and in myogenic medium) displayed relatively stable absorbance values over time, suggesting that p-MSCs maintain metabolic activity and viability but show limited changes in metabolic rate under conventional flat culture conditions, regardless of the differentiation stimuli.

(a) Results of cell viability (ABS) test performed by MTT on: MSCs grown on standard 2D culture methods (MSCs Ctrl) used as control, MSCs culture on 2D culture method in myogenic medium (MSCs in MM), MSCs culture on 3D hybrid scaffolds (HS) aligned and grid, incubated with MM medium, after 14, 21 and 28 days at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Statistical analysis reports p values < 0.05 (*). (b) Live/Dead staining of HS aligned scaffolds at 21 (a) and 28 (b) days; HS grid scaffolds at 21 (c) and 28 (d) days.

In contrast, both 3D hybrid scaffolds (HS aligned and HS grid) showed a significant increase in metabolic activity from day 14 to day 28. This progressive rise indicates that the 3D architecture supports not only sustained viability but also enhanced cellular metabolic activity over time. At 28 days, absorbance values for both HS scaffolds were notably higher than for 2D cultures, with no significant difference between aligned and grid geometries, as confirmed by overlapping standard deviations. These findings demonstrate that both scaffold types provide a similarly supportive environment for p-MSC survival and growth under myogenic induction conditions.

The improved cell viability and metabolic activity on 3D scaffolds compared to 2D surfaces can be attributed to the biomimetic microarchitecture of the electrospun layer combined with the volumetric support of the printed substrate. This configuration likely facilitates better cell–matrix interactions, spatial organization, and diffusion of nutrients and oxygen, all of which contribute to increased metabolic activity. Moreover, the fibrous structure more closely mimics the extracellular matrix (ECM) of native tissue, offering topographical and mechanical cues that are absent in standard plastic wells.

Overall, these results confirm the cytocompatibility of the hybrid scaffolds and underscore their potential to enhance cell expansion and viability in a 3D microenvironment—an essential feature for tissue engineering applications.

Live/Dead staining at 21 and 28 days (Fig. 4b) confirmed the viability of p-MSCs cultured on both HS aligned and HS grid scaffolds. In all conditions, a predominant green fluorescence signal—indicative of viable cells stained by calcein-AM—was observed, while red fluorescence (propidium iodide, dead cells) was minimal or nearly absent. This suggests that cell death was negligible and that the scaffolds were highly cytocompatible across time points. At both 21 and 28 days, the HS aligned scaffolds (panels a and b) appeared to support a more uniform and denser distribution of live cells compared to the HS grid scaffolds (panels c and d), where cell density seemed lower and more sparsely distributed. However, consistent with the MTT data (Fig. 4), no substantial difference in overall viability was noted between the two scaffold types at later time points, especially considering potential image depth limitations in 3D structures.

These results support the conclusion that both scaffold designs maintain high cell viability over prolonged culture and are suitable for supporting MSCs metabolic activity. The combination of minimal cytotoxicity and stable metabolic activity (as seen in the MTT assay) confirms the biocompatibility and functional suitability of the hybrid scaffolds for long-term culture and potential use in tissue engineering applications.

In a cell differentiation protocol involving MSCs, observing alterations in cell morphology during 2D culture conditions is crucial for understanding how the cells are responding to the differentiation stimuli30. After 4 weeks of incubation, MSCs treated with MM (Fig. 5e, f, g) showed distinct morphological changes compared to the control group (Fig. 5b, c, d). MSCs grown in MM medium demonstrated a spindle-like shape that seemed thicker, possibly due to the formation of multinucleated cells. Additionally, it can be observed that the cells tend to align with one another, a characteristic feature of myofibers. Although not all differences reached statistical significance, the more pronounced upward trend and reduced variability observed in the aligned group suggest that this architecture provides more effective guidance cues. Such directional organization likely facilitates MSC orientation and promotes progression toward a myogenic phenotype, whereas the grid structure appears less supportive of this process.

Altogether, these findings indicate that substrate topography influences timing and extent of myogenic marker expression, with aligned microenvironments in 3D scaffolds offering a more favorable setting for MSC myogenic differentiation.

Discussion

In this study, we developed and evaluated hybrid 3D-printed/electrospun scaffolds made of PLA-PCL (70:30) to support the myogenic differentiation of porcine mesenchymal stem cells (p-MSCs). The scaffolds were fabricated in two distinct 3D configurations—aligned and grid—which were subsequently coated with electrospun nanofibers to better mimic the anisotropic microenvironment of native skeletal muscle. A comprehensive structural, mechanical, and biological characterization confirmed the cytocompatibility of both scaffold types and highlighted their potential in guiding lineage-specific differentiation.

From a structural standpoint, ImageJ-based analysis of SEM micrographs revealed notable differences in fiber morphology and orientation between aligned and grid scaffolds. Electrospun fibers deposited onto the aligned 3D architecture had a larger average diameter (1.60 ± 0.21 μm) and wider pore area (6.21 ± 1.55 μm²), whereas fibers on the grid scaffolds were thinner (0.93 ± 0.13 μm) and formed a denser network with smaller pores (3.59 ± 1.07 μm²). Fiber density was comparable (≈ 46%) in both configurations, but fiber orientation differed substantially (mean angle 16.4° vs. 43.1°), suggesting that topographical features were effectively modulated by the scaffold geometry—a parameter known to influence cell alignment and differentiation31,32.

Mechanical characterization further emphasized these differences. Aligned scaffolds displayed superior tensile properties, with a peak stress of 5.39 ± 1.60 MPa and a Young’s modulus of 137.80 ± 18.20 MPa, compared to 1.62 ± 0.38 MPa and 50.08 ± 12.05 MPa, respectively, for grid scaffolds. These values are particularly relevant when benchmarked against the mechanical properties of native skeletal muscle, which has been reported to exhibit a Young’s modulus around 105 MPa and ultimate tensile strength of approximately 13.2 ± 1.9 MPa33,34. Although our aligned scaffolds did not reach the native tensile strength, they demonstrated stiffness within the physiologically relevant range—suggesting mechanical suitability for muscle tissue engineering applications. These findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating the importance of fiber orientation in scaffold performance. For example, Sooriyaarachchi et al. (2019) developed a hybrid scaffold composed of a 3D-printed PLA structure integrated with aligned or random polycaprolactone (PCL) nanofibers. Their results showed that aligned nanofibers significantly enhanced the scaffold’s compressive strength and promoted directional cell growth and metabolic activity. These outcomes, coupled with the correlation between fiber morphology and cytoskeletal organization, further support the role of anisotropic topographical cues in guiding musculoskeletal tissue regeneration—reinforcing the relevance of the aligned configuration adopted in our system35.

Cell viability and metabolic activity, evaluated through MTT and Live/Dead assays, confirmed the cytocompatibility of both scaffold types. Absorbance values from the MTT assay showed a marked increase over time (from day 14 to day 28) in both 3D conditions, while 2D cultures (with or without myogenic medium) maintained stable and lower metabolic activity. Notably, at day 28, both HS aligned, and grid scaffolds reached similar absorbance values with overlapping standard deviations, indicating comparable levels of long-term viability. This enhanced cell viability in 3D compared to 2D culture likely reflects the biomimetic architecture of the scaffold, which promotes cell–matrix interactions, better nutrient diffusion, and spatial organization—critical parameters for cellular expansion in regenerative contexts. Similarly, Choi et al. (2008) observed robust cell viability and proliferation of skeletal muscle cells on electrospun PCL/collagen nanofiber meshes, with no significant metabolic differences between aligned and random configurations, reinforcing the suitability of electrospun substrates for muscle regeneration36.

In terms of differentiation, the expression of MyoD and Myogenin was analyzed by immunofluorescence and quantified at day 14, 21, and 28. Both markers showed a progressive increase over time, indicating a sustained activation of the myogenic program throughout the culture period. Notably, Myogenin—a marker of late-stage differentiation—was more strongly expressed at day 28, confirming advanced myogenic commitment at this stage. Among the tested configurations, the HS-aligned scaffolds exhibited the highest Myogenin signal intensity, suggesting that their anisotropic architecture provides a more favorable microenvironment for promoting myogenic maturation compared to the HS-grid scaffolds. Despite some comparisons lacking statistical significance, the coherent increase over time and lower inter-sample variability corroborates the notion that aligned topography more effectively supports terminal myogenic differentiation. This is consistent with findings from Yeo & Kim, who demonstrated that hierarchical scaffolds fabricated via combined electrospinning/3D printing induced enhanced myoblast alignment and differentiation within scaffold layers, directly linking fiber orientation to improved Myogenin and Myosin Heavy Chain expression in C2C12 culture37.

In contrast, the grid-patterned scaffolds—while still supporting cell viability and differentiation—exhibited less consistent trends and slightly delayed expression of Myogenin. SEM observations corroborated these findings only partially, as cell morphology appeared more disorganized on grid scaffolds; however, due to depth-of-field limitations and qualitative nature, these data were not deemed central in interpreting biological outcomes. Altogether, our results reinforce and expand upon existing literature on hybrid electrospun/3D-printed scaffolds. While previous works have explored the benefits of combining these fabrication techniques, our study provides a direct comparison between two macro-architectures using the same material platform, highlighting the superior mechanical and myogenic performance of aligned constructs.

The present findings demonstrate that the 3D hybrid scaffold supports p-MSC viability and metabolic activity over long-term culture, suggesting a favorable microenvironment for muscle tissue engineering applications. However, as MTT and Live/Dead assays mainly reflect metabolic activity and cell viability, further analyses are required to confirm the proliferative capacity and myogenic commitment of p-MSCs within the 3D construct.

Future studies will therefore focus on the quantitative assessment of cell proliferation, for instance through EdU incorporation or Ki-67 immunostaining, and on the molecular evaluation of myogenic differentiation by RT-PCR analysis of specific markers such as MyoD, Myogenin, and Desmin. These complementary approaches will provide a deeper and more robust validation of the 3D hybrid scaffold as a platform to promote and sustain myogenic differentiation.

Conclusion

This work highlights the potential of hybrid 3D-printed/electrospun PLA-PCL scaffolds in promoting myogenic differentiation of porcine mesenchymal stem cells. By combining the tunable mechanical properties of extrusion-based printing with the biomimetic surface characteristics of electrospinning, we created anisotropic microenvironments that closely mimic the structural and functional features of native skeletal muscle tissue. Through the integration of structural, mechanical, and biological analyses, we showed that both aligned and grid scaffold configurations maintain high cell viability and metabolic activity over prolonged culture periods. Importantly, the aligned scaffolds—characterized by directional fiber orientation, physiologically relevant mechanical stiffness (137.8 ± 18.2 MPa), and anisotropic topography—proved more effective in promoting temporal progression of myogenic differentiation markers, particularly Myogenin at later stages (day 28). These findings reinforce the importance of scaffold architecture in modulating stem cell fate and offer a promising platform for skeletal muscle tissue engineering. Future studies will aim to validate these results in vivo, assess long-term differentiation into mature myotubes, and explore the effects of mechanical stimulation and bioactive molecule incorporation to further enhance myogenic maturation and functional tissue regeneration.

Materials and methods

Hybrid scaffolds manufacturing

Hybrid scaffolds were fabricated by combining Fused Filament Fabrication (FFF) 3D printing and electrospinning techniques to integrate macro- and micro-scale features suitable for supporting mesenchymal stem cell differentiation. FFF technology involves the layer-by-layer extrusion of thermoplastic materials in the form of filaments through a heated nozzle. In this process, the filament is fed by a toothed wheel connected to a stepper motor and pushed into a heating chamber, where it is brought to the extrusion temperature and extruded through a nozzle on a printing platform. For the manufacturing of the 3D printed scaffolds, a LeapFrog HS® printer (Dutch LeapFrog Group) and a custom-made poly(lactic-co-caprolactone) (PLA-PCL) 70:30 copolymer filament with a diameter of 1.75 mm produced by TreeD Filaments (https://treedfilaments.com/) were employed. The machine was equipped with a 0.35 mm diameter nozzle, a heated bed and a closed printing chamber, with no active environmental heating. Scaffold geometries were designed using the software Autodesk Inventor 2023, while machine instructions (Gcode file) were generated using the slicing software UltiMaker Cura 5.7.2. The slicing software allows the setting of all the printing parameters required to instruct the machine. Key parameters are listed in the following, along with the actual value used for the manufacturing: layer thickness (0,1 mm), width of the extruded filament (0,4 mm), infill density (20%), infill pattern (grid or rectilinear aligned), number of perimeters (1), extrusion temperature (190 °C) and bed temperature (60 °C).

For the electrospun coating, a PLA-PCL copolymer (Resomer® LC 703 S, Mw 160,000 Da; Evonik Nutrition & Care GmbH, 64275 Darmstadt, Germany) was dissolved in a solvent mixture of methylene chloride (MC) and N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF) in a 70:30 volume ratio. Solvents were purchased by Sigma-Aldrich (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany), and (from Carlo Erba, Milan, Italy). The final polymer concentration was adjusted to 20% w/v. MC was selected for its high volatility and strong solubilizing power, while DMF contributed to fiber elongation due to its high dielectric constant. Electrospinning was performed using a NANON-01 A electrospinning apparatus equipped with a dehumidifier (MEEC Instruments, MP, Pioltello, Italy). The optimized electrospinning conditions were as follows: an applied voltage of 30 kV, a flow rate of 0.5 mL/h, a needle-to-collector distance of 15 cm, a temperature of 25 ± 3 °C, and a relative humidity of 30 ± 4%. Electrospun fibers were directly deposited onto 3D-printed scaffolds positioned on a 14 × 25 cm grounded metal plate, forming hybrid constructs. The electrospinning process was carried out for 30 min, with the spinneret moving at 70 mm/s over a 70 mm traverse width. Rectangular scaffolds with dimensions of 15 × 15 × 0.4–0.5 mm was 3D printed and then coated by electrospinning the PLA-PCL copolymer, as explained here above, for cell seeding/biological experiments, while 60 × 15 × 0.4–0.5 mm electrospun scaffolds were fabricated for mechanical testing.

Hybrid scaffolds characterization

The morphological features of the hybrid scaffolds were characterized using Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), a technique that provides high-resolution imaging of surface microstructures through the interaction of a focused electron beam with the sample. Before analysis, all samples were sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold to enhance conductivity and minimize charging effects under the electron beam.

SEM analyses were performed using a Zeiss EVO MA10 instrument (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). The scaffolds were examined at magnifications of 1.00 kX and 2.50 kX to evaluate surface morphology, fiber architecture, and structural organization at the micro- and nanoscale levels.

Quantitative morphometric analysis of the electrospun matrices was conducted using ImageJ software (version 1.53k; National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). Parameters assessed included fiber diameter (nm), matrix porosity (nm²), surface porosity (%), and fiber orientation (°). Image data were acquired from multiple fields per sample and averaged to ensure representative values.

This structural characterization provided critical insights into the microstructural organization of the hybrid scaffolds, validating the efficacy of the combined 3D printing and electrospinning approach in reproducing features relevant for tissue engineering applications.

Scaffold biologic characterization

Porcine mesenchymal stem cells (p-MSCs) were isolated from bone marrow, provided by the Cell Factory, Fondazione IRCCS Policlinico San Matteo (Pavia, Italy). The biological evaluation aimed to assess the ability of porcine mesenchymal stem cells (p-MSCs) to adhere, survive, proliferate, and penetrate within the 3D hybrid scaffolds. Specifically, we investigated cellular viability and scaffold colonization through a combination of viability assays (MTT), Live/Dead staining, and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) imaging. For viability assays, a Live/Dead cell double staining kit and an MTT assay kit [3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)−2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide] were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany).

p-MSCs were isolated from bone marrow aspirates collected from 6-month-old piglets under general anesthesia (3% isoflurane) via posterior iliac crest puncture. Mononuclear cells (MNCs) were separated by density gradient centrifugation using Ficoll and cultured in uncoated polystyrene flasks at an initial seeding density of 160.000 cells/cm². The cells were maintained in complete medium at 37 °C with 5% CO₂, with medium changes twice weekly. Upon reaching ≥ 80% confluence, cells were passaged via trypsinization and reseeded at 4.000 cells/cm² up to passage 4.

Phenotypic characterization at passage 4 was performed using flow cytometry (Navios, Beckman Coulter, Milan, Italy) with FITC- or PE-conjugated antibodies against porcine CD90, CD105, CD29 (positive markers), and CD45, CD11b (negative markers), including appropriate isotype controls. Multipotency was confirmed by inducing osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation. For osteogenesis, cells were cultured in Alpha Minimum Essential Medium (α-MEM) supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum (FCS), 10⁻⁷ M dexamethasone, 50 µg/mL ascorbic acid, and 5 mM β-glycerol phosphate, with Alizarin Red staining used to detect calcium deposition. For adipogenesis, cells were cultured with insulin (10 mg/mL), indomethacin (0.25 M), and 1-methyl-3-isobutylxanthine (50 mM), and stained with Oil Red O to visualize lipid droplets.

Prior to cell seeding, the scaffolds were sterilized by gamma irradiation following European Pharmacopoeia (EU.PH., Vol. III, Ch. 5.1) protocol. Samples were individually sealed in sterile, hermetically closed polyamide/aluminum/polyester peel pouches (VWR) and irradiated at a total dose of 25 kGy using a Cobalt-60 source at the Laboratorio di Energia Nucleare Applicata (LENA), University of Pavia, Italy. All biological assays were performed on sterilized scaffolds.

MTT assay

To evaluate the cytocompatibility, viability and metabolic activity of MSCs cultured on the electrospun scaffolds, a colorimetric MTT assay was performed using 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)−2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide. The assay is based on the enzymatic reduction of MTT by mitochondrial dehydrogenases, which are active only in viable cells, converting the yellow tetrazolium salt into insoluble violet-blue formazan crystals. These crystals accumulate in the cytoplasm and, upon disruption of cellular membranes, can be solubilized in organic solvents, allowing for spectrophotometric quantification. The amount of formazan produced is directly proportional to the number of metabolically active cells and is measured at 570 nm.

The scaffolds were positioned in CellCrown™ inserts to ensure stable placement and optimal exposure to culture medium during in vitro cultivation. Before seeding, each scaffold was equilibrated in culture medium to allow proper wetting of the material and to promote initial cell adhesion. For cell loading, p-MSCs were resuspended at a defined concentration (density of 1 × 10⁵ cells per scaffold) and carefully dispensed onto the upper surface of each scaffold in a small volume of medium. Using a limited volume (50 µL) allows the cells to remain in close contact with the three-dimensional structure during the initial adhesion phase, favoring their infiltration within the pores and the establishment of uniform cell distribution. After the seeding suspension was applied, the constructs were kept under standard culture conditions to permit the first attachment phase before adding additional medium.

The culture medium consisted of DMEM (low glucose, GlutaMAX™ supplement, pyruvate) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. All media components were sourced from Gibco™, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc. (Waltham, MA, USA).

The MTT assay was conducted at 7, 14, and 30 days post-seeding. Positive controls (CTRL+) consisted of cells cultured directly on the well plate surface, while negative controls (CTRL−) consisted of unseeded scaffolds to assess potential background interference between the polymer and MTT reagent.

For each time point, scaffolds were washed with PBS to remove non-adherent or dead cells. Then, 300 µL of a 5 mg/mL MTT solution was added to each well to ensure scaffold coverage. After 2 h and 30 min incubation at 37 °C and 5% CO₂, the solution was carefully removed to avoid losing the formed formazan crystals. Samples were washed with PBS, and formazan was solubilized by transferring the scaffolds into glass vials containing tetrahydrofuran (THF) and magnetically stirred for 1 h. This process allowed complete dissolution of the polymer matrix and release of intracellular formazan. For the CTRL + condition, the formazan was solubilized in DMSO. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a UV/Vis spectrophotometer (JENWAY 6705, single quartz cell holder), with THF used as the blank. The results provided quantitative insights into the viability of p-MSCs cultured on the electrospun scaffolds over time.

Live/dead assay

The LIVE/DEAD assay is a widely employed method to evaluate cell viability by differentiating live from dead cells using two fluorescent dyes: calcein-AM and propidium iodide (PI). Calcein-AM is a cell-permeant dye that penetrates viable cells, where intracellular esterases convert it into fluorescent calcein, emitting green fluorescence typically between 515 and 530 nm. This enzymatic conversion occurs exclusively in live cells, as dead cells lack active esterases. Conversely, propidium iodide is a membrane-impermeant dye that can only enter cells with compromised membranes, characteristic of dead or dying cells. Upon binding to DNA, PI emits red fluorescence around 617 nm when excited. When concurrently applied, live cells exhibit green fluorescence due to calcein, while dead cells fluoresce red owing to PI, enabling straightforward distinction and quantification via fluorescence microscopy or flow cytometry.

In this study, cell viability was assessed using the LIVE/DEAD assay kit (Sigma Aldrich, Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The cells were incubated with calcein-AM and propidium iodide to label live and dead populations, respectively. Fluorescent images were captured using a Leica DM IL LED-FLUO microscope (Leica Microsystems Srl, Buccinasco, Italy) equipped with appropriate filters for green and red fluorescence detection. Subsequent image analysis was performed using ImageJ software, where contrast enhancement and background subtraction were applied to improve image clarity and enable quantitative evaluation of cell viability.

MSCs differentiation

Pig bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (p-MSCs) were utilized to investigate the influence of three-dimensional electrospun scaffolds on myogenic differentiation. For the two-dimensional (2D) control, p-MSCs were cultured in a myogenic differentiation medium (MM) composed of low-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; GlutaMAX™ Supplement, pyruvate; Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 5% horse serum (HS), 0.1 µM dexamethasone, 50 µM hydrocortisone, and 1% antibiotic26. Sterile electrospun scaffolds were cut into circular discs (15 mm diameter) and placed into CellCrown™ systems (CellCrown™ 12 NX, Scaffdex, Tampere, Finland) housed in 12-well plates. Each scaffold was seeded with 1 × 104 p-MSCs suspended in 50 µL of medium. Following 10 min of incubation at 37 °C in 5% CO₂ to facilitate cell attachment, 2 mL of the respective culture medium was added to each well, with medium changes every 48 h. For comparative purposes, additional scaffolds were seeded and maintained in standard MSC medium (low-glucose DMEM with 10% FBS and 1% antibiotic). Myogenic differentiation was assessed by immunofluorescence staining of key myogenic markers MyoD1 and myogenin. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized using 0.1% Triton X-100, and blocked with 2% BSA serum. Primary antibodies against MyoD1 (polyclonal, Invitrogen) and myogenin (monoclonal, Invitrogen) were applied at 1:500 dilution for 3 h at 37 °C. Secondary detection involved Alexa Fluor-conjugated antibodies (Alexa Fluor 594 goat anti-rabbit IgG for MyoD1; Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-rabbit IgG for myogenin), phalloidin conjugates for F-actin visualization, and DAPI for nuclear staining. The samples were examined using a Leica DM IL LED fluorescence microscope, and images were processed via ImageJ for contrast enhancement and background subtraction. Immunostaining was performed at 14, 21, and 28 days post-seeding.

Four experimental groups were analyzed: (1) negative control consisting of MSCs cultured in standard MSC medium, (2) positive control with MSCs in myogenic differentiation medium (MM), (3) MSCs seeded on hybrid scaffolds with aligned or grid geometry in MM and (4) MSCs seeded on hybrid scaffolds with aligned or grid geometry in standard MSC medium. Controls without primary antibodies and secondary-only controls were included to validate staining specificity and exclude autofluorescence artifacts. MATLAB R2021b (The MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA, USA) was used to quantify the expression of MyoD and Myogenin in different samples. The script is reported in the Supplementary data.

The following protocol was carried out to evaluate cell orientation and penetration within electrospun nanofibrous scaffolds. At prefixed timing points (14, 21, and 28 days), samples were first rinsed twice with 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer (SCB, pH 7.4) for 7 min to remove residual culture medium, following cells fixation in a solution made from 2% glutaraldehyde and 2% paraformaldehyde prepared in SCB for 30 min at room temperature. Following fixation, the samples underwent two additional washes with SCB. Gradual dehydration was performed through a graded ethanol series of 30%, 70%, 80%, and 100% (v/v). Subsequently, the scaffolds were soaked in a 1:1 mixture of 100% ethanol and hexamethyldisilazane (HMDS) for 10 min to enhance drying and preserve ultrastructure. Samples not immediately analyzed were stored refrigerated. Prior to SEM imaging, scaffolds were sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold under an argon atmosphere to improve electrical conductivity. SEM images were acquired at a magnification of 1,000× to visualize cellular morphology and scaffold interactions.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise stated. A two-way analysis of variance (two-way ANOVA) was used to evaluate the effects of treatment and time, as well as their interaction, across experimental groups. When significant effects were detected, multiple comparisons were performed using Tukey’s post hoc test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Significance levels were indicated in the figures as follows: p < 0.05 (*), and p ≥ 0.05 (ns, not significant).
