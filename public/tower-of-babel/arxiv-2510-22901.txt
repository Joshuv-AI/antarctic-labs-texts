Abstract
. We study the Lusternik-Schnirelmann category and topological complexity of 1-dimensional spaces. We define both invariants as lengths of suitable closed filtrations, as opposed to a more common definition based on open covers. Our main results provide a precise description of 𝐜𝐚𝐭 ⁡ ( X ) \mathbf{cat}(X) and 𝐓𝐂 ⁡ ( X ) \mathbf{TC}(X) of a 1-dimensional Peano continuum X X in terms of the wildness rank of X X . A surprising consequence is that 𝐜𝐚𝐭 ⁡ ( X ) \mathbf{cat}(X) and 𝐓𝐂 ⁡ ( X ) \mathbf{TC}(X) of a general 1-dimensional space X X can be arbitrarily high, which is in stark contrast with the analogous results for 1-dimensional CW-complexes.



1.
Introduction

Is there a continuous rule that, to every pair of points
x,x^{\prime}
in a space
X
, assigns a path in
X

starting at
x
and ending at
x^{\prime}
? This question, motivated by applications in robot motion planning,
was a starting point for Michael Farber’s theory of topological complexity. Farber observed that a
continuous rule valid for all pairs
(x,x^{\prime})\in X\times X
exists if, and only if
X
is
contractible. He thus defined the topological complexity
\mathbf{TC}(X)
of motion planning in
X
as the
minimal integer
n
for which
there exists an open cover
U_{0},\ldots,U_{n}
of
X\times X
such that each
U_{i}
admits a continuous
motion plan, i.e. a continuous choice of a path connecting
x
and
x^{\prime}
for every
(x,x^{\prime})\in U_{i}
. (Note the
so-called
normalized
indexing with respect to which contractible space
X
has
\mathbf{TC}(X)=0
.) Farber
proved that
\mathbf{TC}(X)\leq 2\dim(X)
if
X
is a connected CW-complex. However, if
X
is not locally
simply-connected, then the requirement that the domains
U_{i}
in the covering of
X\times X
are open implies
that
\mathbf{TC}(X)
is infinite. Thus, in order to discuss motion planning on more general spaces
like fractals or 1-dimensional continua, we are led to work with an alternative definition of topological
complexity
based on closed filtrations. This definition yields the same value of
\mathbf{TC}(X)
for CW-complexes
but is more suitable for spaces with intricate local features. The resulting theory is very satisfactory
and we were able to obtain precise values for the topological complexity of certain 1-dimensional Peano continua.

To describe our results, we first recall that a point of
x\in X
is said to be
wild
if every open
neighborhood of
x
contains a loop that is essential in
X
. The subspace of all wild points of
X
is denoted

\operatorname{w}(X)
. The wild set
\operatorname{w}(X)
may have its own wild points, so we may consider the descending sequence of closed
subspaces

X=\operatorname{w}^{0}(X)\supseteq\operatorname{w}^{1}(X)\supseteq\operatorname{w}^{2}(X)\supseteq\cdots
, where
\operatorname{w}^{k+1}(X)=\operatorname{w}(\operatorname{w}^{k}(X))
.
We will say that
X
is
\operatorname{w}
-stable
if
\operatorname{w}^{k}(X)
is locally path-connected for all
k
.
Furthermore, we define the
wildness rank

\mathbf{wrk}(X)
of
X
as the smallest integer
n
such
that
\operatorname{w}^{n}(X)=\emptyset
(and
\mathbf{wrk}(X)=\infty
if there is no such
n
).

The following are our principal results about the Lusternik-Schnirelmann category
\mathbf{cat}(X)
and the topological complexity
\mathbf{TC}(X)
of 1-dimensional Peano continua. Note that a path-connected one-dimensional space is simply-connected if, and only if, it does not contain a simple closed curve.

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume
X
is a
w
-stable one-dimensional Peano continuum and
\mathbf{wrk}(X)=n<\infty
. Then

\mathbf{cat}(X)=\begin{cases}n-1&\text{ if }\operatorname{w}^{n-1}(X)\text{ contains no simple closed curve,}\\
n&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Theorem 1.2
.

Assume
X
is a
w
-stable one-dimensional Peano continuum and
\mathbf{wrk}(X)=n<\infty
. Then

\mathbf{TC}(X)=\begin{cases}2n-2&\text{ if }\operatorname{w}^{n-1}(X)\text{ contains no simple closed curve,}\\
2n-1&\text{ if }\operatorname{w}^{n-1}(X)\text{ contains one simple closed curve,}\\
2n&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

For comparison, if
X
is a one-dimensional CW-complex, then
\mathbf{cat}(X)\in\{0,1\}

and
\mathbf{TC}(X)\in\{0,1,2\}
.

The organization of the paper is as follows. In the next section we introduce the relevant definitions
of the Lusternik-Schnirelmann category and topological complexity and establish their properties.
In Section 3, we discuss some special properties of one-dimensional spaces, define the wild set and
describe some standard decompositions. In Section 4, we study iterated wild sets, define wildness rank,
and use it to prove Theorem
1.1
. Finally, in Section 5, we give estimates for topological
complexity of one-dimensional spaces and prove Theorem
1.2
.

2.
Lusternik-Schnirelmann category and topological complexity

Standard references for LS-category and topological complexity are respectively

[
7
]
and
[
12
]
. We will use freely the results from those monographs and we will try to use compatible notation whenever possible.

Throughout, all spaces will be assumed to be Hausdorff. Given a space
X
we will denote by

PX
the space of paths
[0,1]\to X
, endowed with the compact-open topology. The evaluation maps
at the points 0,1 or both 0 and 1 are Hurewicz fibrations and are denoted respectively

\operatorname{ev}_{0},\operatorname{ev}_{1}\colon PX\to X
and
\operatorname{ev}_{0,1}\colon PX\to X\times X
.
It is well-known that
\operatorname{ev}_{0}
is a homotopy equivalence with a homotopy inverse given by the map

c\colon X\to PX
that to each
x\in X
assigns the constant path
c_{x}
. The fibre of
\operatorname{ev}_{0}
over a point

x\in X
consists of all paths in
X
that begin at
x
and will be denoted
P_{\ast}X
. The paths
in
P_{\ast}X
can be continuously deformed to their initial point, therefore
P_{\ast}X
is contractible.

Our definitions of LS-category and topological complexity are based on closed filtrations and
are stated as follows.
A subspace
A\subseteq X
is said to be
categorical in
X
if the inclusion map
A\to X
is
homotopic to a constant map or equivalently, if the projection
ev_{1}\colon P_{\ast}X\to X

admits a section over
A
. By default, the empty set is categorical in
X
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
X
be a path-connected space. The
(normalized) Lusternik-Schnirelmann category

\mathbf{cat}(X)
of
X
is the smallest integer
n
for which there exists a filtration

\emptyset=F_{-1}\subset F_{0}\subset F_{1}\subset F_{2}\subset\cdots\subset F_{n}=X

by closed subsets of
X
such that the difference
F_{j}\backslash F_{j-1}
is categorical in
X

for all
j\in\{0,1,2,\dots,n\}
. If no such
n
exists, then
\mathbf{cat}(X)=\infty
.

A subspace
A\subseteq X\times X
is said to
admit a motion plan
in
X
if the
projections
p_{1},p_{2}\colon A\to X
are homotopic, or equivalently, if the projection

ev_{0,1}\colon PX\to X\times X
admits a section over
A
.
By default, the empty set admits a motion plan.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
X
be a path-connected space. The
(normalized) topological complexity

\mathbf{TC}(X)
of
X
is the smallest integer

n
for which there exists a filtration

\emptyset=F_{-1}\subset F_{0}\subset F_{1}\subset F_{2}\subset\cdots\subset F_{n}=X\times X

by closed subsets of
X\times X
such that
F_{j+1}\backslash F_{j}
admits a motion plan in
X
for all

j\in\{0,1,2,\dots,n\}
. If no such
n
exists, then
\mathbf{TC}(X)=\infty
.

The standard definitions of
\mathbf{cat}(X)
and
\mathbf{TC}(X)
are somewhat different and are given in terms of
suitable open covers instead of closed filtrations (cf.
[
7
]
and
[
12
]
).
However, it is clear that an open neighbourhood of a wild point in
X
cannot be categorical in
X
, so
if
X
has wild points, then LS-category and topological complexity of
X
are infinite.
A similar difficulty appears in the study of the topological complexity relative to a map
[
19
]

and in geodesic motion planning
[
21
]
. In both cases a way out is to base the definitions on
closed filtrations. Note that if
X
is a CW-complex (or more generally, an Absolute Neighbourhood Retract –
see
[
15
,
23
]
) both variants give the same value of
\mathbf{cat}(X)
and
\mathbf{TC}(X)
, while for
more general spaces the values may differ.

Since our definitions are based on closed filtrations we must carefully verify which properties
of
\mathbf{cat}(X)
and
\mathbf{TC}(X)
that appear in the literature are still valid. We take care of this
in the following theorems.

Theorem 2.3
.

The following statements are valid for arbitrary path-connected spaces
X
and
Y
.

(1)

\mathbf{cat}(X)=0
if, and only if,
X
is contractible.

(2)

If
X\simeq Y
, then
\mathbf{cat}(X)=\mathbf{cat}(Y)
.

(3)

If
X
contains a closed path-connected subspace
A
such that
X\backslash A
can be deformed to
a discrete subspace of
X
, then

\mathbf{cat}(X)\leq\mathbf{cat}(A)+1
.

(4)

\mathbf{cat}(X\times Y)\leq\mathbf{cat}(X)+\mathbf{cat}(Y)
.

(5)

If
X
is a locally compact subspace of some Euclidean Neighbourhood Retract, then
\mathbf{cat}(X)\geq\text{cup-length}\big(\operatorname{Ker}\check{H}^{\ast}(X)\to\check{H}^{\ast}(\ast)\big)
.

In the last statement,
\check{H}^{\ast}
denotes the Čech-cohomology functor, and the cup-length of
an ideal
I
in a cohomology ring is, by definition, the maximal number of factors in a non-zero
product of elements of
I
.

Theorem 2.4
.

The following statements are valid for arbitrary path-connected spaces
X
and
Y
.

(1)

\mathbf{TC}(X)=0
if, and only if,
X
is contractible.

(2)

If
X\simeq Y
, then
\mathbf{TC}(X)=\mathbf{TC}(Y)
.

(3)

\mathbf{cat}(X)\leq\mathbf{TC}(X)\leq\mathbf{cat}(X\times X)
.

(4)

\mathbf{TC}(X\times Y)\leq\mathbf{TC}(X)+\mathbf{TC}(Y)
.

(5)

If
X
is a locally compact subspace of some Euclidean Neighbourhood Retract, then
\mathbf{TC}(X)\geq\text{cup-length}\big(\operatorname{Ker}\Delta^{\ast}\colon\check{H}^{\ast}(X\times X)\to\check{H}^{\ast}(X)\big)
.

We will prove both sets of statements together.

Proof.

Statement
2.3
(1) follows directly for the definition and
statement
2.4
(1) is proved as Theorem 1 in
[
11
]
.

The standard proofs (see for example
[
7
, Theorem 1.30]
and
[
11
, Theorem 3]
)
of homotopy invariance statements
2.3
(2) and
2.4
(2)
are easily adapted to closed filtrations so we leave the details to the readers.

Toward the proof of
2.3
(3) observe that a discrete subspace
D
of a path-connected
space
X
is categorical in
X
. In fact, the paths connecting points of
D
to a point
x_{0}\in X

define a continuous deformation of
D
to
x_{0}
. Clearly, if
X\backslash A
can be deformed to a discrete subspace
of
X
, then
X\backslash A
is categorical in
X
. Therefore, by adding
X\backslash A
to
A
we may increase
\mathbf{cat}(A)

at most by one. Note that
2.3
(3) generalizes the classical estimate of LS-category
of a mapping cone, cf.
[
7
, Theorem 1.32]

Estimate
2.4
(3) can be proved along the same lines as in the usual proof for open coverings
- see for example
[
11
, Theorem 5]
.

The proofs of product inequalities
2.3
(4) and
2.4
(4)
based on closed filtrations are simpler than the proofs of analogous statements based on open coverings.
Assume
\mathbf{cat}(X)=n
and
\mathbf{cat}(Y)=m
, so that there exist closed filtrations

\emptyset\subset F_{0}\subset F_{1}\subset\cdots\subset F_{n}=X
and

\emptyset\subset G_{0}\subset G_{1}\subset\cdots\subset G_{m}=Y
, such that the
differences
F_{i}\backslash F_{i-1}
and
G_{j}\backslash G_{j-1}
are categorical in
X
and
Y
, respectively. For

k=0,1,\ldots,m+n
define

H_{k}:=\bigcup_{i+j=k}F_{i}\times G_{j}

yielding a closed filtration
H_{0}\subset H_{1}\subset\cdots\subset H_{m+n}
of
X\times Y
.
The difference of successive terms in the filtration is given as

H_{k}\backslash H_{k-1}=\bigcup_{i+j=k}(F_{i}\backslash F_{i-1})\times(G_{j}\backslash G_{j-1}).

Clearly, each product
(F_{i}\backslash F_{i-1})\times(G_{j}\backslash G_{j-1})
is categorical in
X\times Y
. Moreover, the
terms in the union are separated (the closure of each of them does not intersect the others), so the
deformation of the individual terms to a point combine into a continuous deformation of

H_{k}\backslash H_{k-1}
to a point. We may thus conclude that
\mathbf{cat}(X\times Y)\leq m+n
as claimed. The proof of
the product inequality for topological complexity is analogous (cf. also
[
18
, (3.8)]
).

The cohomological lower bound for topological complexity defined using closed filtrations
stated as
2.4
(5) was proved in
detail in
[
20
, Section 3.5]
(note that the relevant proof was added after the publication of
the homonymous article
[
19
]
). The analogous statement for LS-category is proved analogously
(e.g. as a direct consequence of
[
20
, Corollary 3.20]
).
∎

Example 2.5
.

Let
G
be a connected 1-dimensional CW-complex (i.e. a connected multigraph).
Since
G
can be obtained by adding a discrete set of open segments to its maximal tree,
Theorem
2.3
(3) implies that
\mathbf{cat}(G)\leq 1
.
By taking into account Theorem
2.3
(1) and
2.3
(2) as well, we obtain

\mathbf{cat}(G)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{ if }G\text{ is a tree,}\\
1,&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

By combining the above result with estimates Theorem
2.4
(3) and
Theorem
2.3
(4) we obtain
\mathbf{TC}(G)\leq\mathbf{cat}(G\times G)\leq 2\,\mathbf{cat}(G)\leq 2
.
By Theorem
2.4
(1),
\mathbf{TC}(G)=0
if, and only if
G
is a tree.
Let us now consider the circle
S^{1}
viewed as the subspace of the complex plane. We may filtrate

S^{1}\times S^{1}
by taking the anti-diagonal
F_{0}:=\{(x,-x)\mid x\in S^{1}\}

and
F_{1}:=S^{1}\times S^{1}
. The motion plans (i.e. sections of the projection

\operatorname{ev}_{0,1}\colon PS^{1}\to S^{1}\times S^{1}
) can be defined explicitly in complex notation as

\alpha_{0}(x,-x)(t):=e^{it}\cdot x\text{ for }(x,-x)\in F_{0}

and

\alpha_{1}(x,y)(t):=\frac{(1-t)x+ty}{|(1-t)x+ty|}\text{ for }(x,y)\in F_{1}-F_{0}.

As a consequence, if
G
is a graph with exactly one cycle, then
G\simeq S^{1}
and so

\mathbf{TC}(G)=\mathbf{TC}(S^{1})=1
. If
G
has more than one cycle, then one can show that the
cup-length estimate of
2.4
(5) gives
\mathbf{TC}(G)\geq 2
, see
[
12
, Proposition 4.42]

for details of the computation. Summarizing,

\mathbf{TC}(G)=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }G\text{ is a tree}\\
1&\text{ if }G\text{ has exactly one cycle},\\
2&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

We conclude this section with relative versions of LS-category and topological complexity, which are
particularly useful when dealing with disconnected subspaces.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
A
be a subspace of a path-connected space
X
. The
(normalized) relative Lusternik-Schnirelmann
category of
A
in

X
, denoted
\mathbf{cat}_{X}(A)
, is the smallest integer
n
for which there exists a filtration

\emptyset=F_{-1}\subseteq F_{0}\subseteq F_{0}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq F_{n}=A

by closed subsets of
A
such that
F_{j}\backslash F_{j-1}
is categorical
in
X
for all
j\in\{0,1,2,\dots,n\}
. If no such
n
exists, then
\mathbf{cat}_{X}(A)=\infty
.

Similarly, given a subspace
A\subseteq X\times X
we define the
(normalized) relative topological complexity of
A
in
X
, denoted
\mathbf{TC}_{X}(A)
, as the smallest integer
n
for which there exists
a filtration

\emptyset=F_{-1}\subseteq F_{0}\subseteq F_{0}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq F_{n}=A

by closed subsets of
A
such that
F_{j}\backslash F_{j-1}
admit motion plans in
X
for all

j\in\{0,1,2,\dots,n\}
. If no such
n
exists, then
\mathbf{TC}_{X}(A)=\infty
.

Observe that
\mathbf{cat}_{X}(X)=\mathbf{cat}X
and
\mathbf{TC}_{X}(X\times X)=\mathbf{TC}(X)
. We will later need the following
estimates of LS-category in terms of relative category.

Proposition 2.7
.

For any subspace
A\subseteq X
, we have
\mathbf{cat}_{X}(A)\leq\mathbf{cat}(A)
. Moreover, if
A
is closed in
X
, then
\mathbf{cat}(X)\leq\mathbf{cat}_{X}(A)+\mathbf{cat}_{X}(X\backslash A)+1
.

Proof.

The first claim is clear. Assume that
A
is closed with
\mathbf{cat}_{X}(A)=n
and
\mathbf{cat}_{X}(X\backslash A)=m
, so that there exist closed filtrations

\emptyset=F_{-1}\subseteq F_{0}\subseteq F_{1}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq F_{n}=A,
and
G_{0}\subseteq G_{1}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq G_{m}=X-A,

with all differences
F_{i}\backslash F_{i-1}
and
G_{j}\backslash G_{j-1}
categorical in
X
.
Note that the sets
F_{i}
are closed in
X
as well, while the sets
G_{j}
may not be closed in
X
in general.
Set
F_{n+j+1}:=A\cup G_{j}
for
0\leq j\leq m
and observe that
F_{j}
are closed in
X
for all
0\leq j\leq m+n
and that
F_{j}\backslash F_{j-1}
are
categorical in
X
for all
0\leq j\leq m+n+1
. Thus
\mathbf{cat}(X)\leq m+n+1
.
∎

Proposition 2.8
.

If
X
is path-connected and
A\subseteq X
is a finite disjoint union of closed path-connected subspaces
A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{k}
, then
\mathbf{cat}_{X}(A)=\sup\{\mathbf{cat}_{X}(A_{i})\mid 1\leq i\leq k\}
.

Proof.

That
\mathbf{cat}_{X}(A_{i})\leq\mathbf{cat}_{X}(A)
for each
i
is clear. If
\sup\{\mathbf{cat}(A_{i})\mid 1\leq i\leq k\}=n<\infty
, then for each
i
, there is a closed filtration
F_{i,0}\subseteq F_{i,1}\subseteq\cdots F_{i,n}=A_{i}
such that
F_{i,j}\backslash F_{i,j-1}
is categorical in
X
for all
0\leq j\leq n
. Then
G_{j}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}F_{i,j}
,
0\leq j\leq n
defines a closed filtration of
A
. Each
G_{j}\backslash G_{j-1}
is categorical in
X
since it is a finite disjoint union of sets which are categorical in
X
. Hence,
\mathbf{cat}_{X}(A)\leq n
.
∎

The proof of the next proposition is analogous to that of Theorem
2.3
(2) and is thus omitted.

Proposition 2.9
.

If
(X,A)
and
(Y,B)
are homotopy equivalent pairs of spaces, then
\mathbf{cat}_{X}(A)=\mathbf{cat}_{Y}(B)
.

3.
One-dimensional spaces and wild sets

The “dimension” of a space will refer to the Lebesgue covering dimension. A
Peano continuum
is a connected, locally path-connected compact metrizable space. By the Hahn-Mazurkiewicz Theorem, a continuous image of a Peano continuum in a Hausdorff space is a Peano continuum.

A Peano continuum
D
is a
dendrite
if it contains no simple closed curve (equivalently, if it is uniquely arcwise connected). It is well-known that dendrites are one-dimensional and contractible
[
17
, Chapter X]
.

Since one-dimensional spaces will be our primary focus, we recall some important facts about them.
First, we note that one-dimensional Hausdorff spaces are aspherical
[
8
]

and have contractible generalized universal coverings in the sense of Fischer-Zastrow
[
14
, Example 4.14]
.
The following lemma plays a key role in one-dimensional homotopy theory. It was first proved in
[
13
]

and a nice proof may also be found in
[
5
, Theorem 3.7]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

A loop
\alpha\colon S^{1}\to X
in a one-dimensional Hausdorff space
X
is null-homotopic if there exists a dendrite
D
, a surjective loop

\beta\colon S^{1}\to D
and a map
f\colon D\to X
such that
\alpha=f\circ\beta
.

It follows that every null-homotopic loop in a one-dimensional Hausdorff space contracts in its own image.

Lemma 3.2
.

[
5
, Lemma 3.1]

Let
A\subseteq X
be one-dimensional Peano continua. Then
A
is a retract of
X
.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
X
be any space. A point
x\in X
is a
wild point of

X
if for every neighborhood
U
of
x
, there exists a based
loop
\alpha\colon S^{1}\to U
, which is essential in
X
. We let
\operatorname{w}(X)
denote the subspace of
X
consisting of all wild points of
X
.

Remark 3.4
.

It is clear from the definition of
\operatorname{w}(X)
that
X\backslash\operatorname{w}(X)
is open in
X
and so
\operatorname{w}(X)
is closed.
In particular, if
X
is a compact metric space, then so is
\operatorname{w}(X)
.
If
X
is locally path-connected, then
\operatorname{w}(X)
is precisely the subspace of
X
consisting of points at which
X

fails to be semi-locally simply connected (the latter being the usual definition of the “wild set” or “bad set”
in the literature on one-dimensional Peano continua).

The following basic observations about wild sets are analogues of the results about (sequential) based-wild sets in
[
3
]
.
Since the arguments are nearly identical to those in
[
3
]
so we just sketch the proofs. Recall that a map
f\colon X\to Y

is
\pi_{1}
-injective
if for every
x\in X
, the induced homomorphism
f_{\#}\colon\pi_{1}(X,x)\to\pi_{1}(Y,f(x))
is injective.

Lemma 3.5
.

If
f\colon X\to Y
is a
\pi_{1}
-injective map, then
f(\operatorname{w}(X))\subseteq\operatorname{w}(Y)
.

Proof.

Let
x\in\operatorname{w}(X)
and
U
be a neighborhood of
f(x)
. Since
x\in\operatorname{w}(X)\cap f^{-1}(U)
, there exists a loop
\alpha:S^{1}\to f^{-1}(U)
, which is essential in
X
. Since
f
is
\pi_{1}
-injective,
f\circ\alpha:S^{1}\to U
is essential in
Y
. Thus
f(x)\in\operatorname{w}(Y)
.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

If
X
is a one-dimensional Hausdorff space and
A\subseteq X
, then the inclusion of
A
in
X
is
\pi_{1}
-injective. In particular,
\operatorname{w}(A)\subseteq\operatorname{w}(X)
.

Proof.

The first statement is Corollary 3.3 of
[
5
]
. The second statement then follows from Lemma
3.5
.
∎

When wild points are absent, a “deforestation” process developed in
[
6
]
implies the following.

Theorem 3.7
.

If
X
is a non-simply connected one-dimensional Peano continuum with
\operatorname{w}(X)=\emptyset
, then there exists a unique finite graph
G\subseteq X

with no free edges, which is a deformation retract of
X
.

For example, if
X
is a one-dimensional Peano continuum with a cyclic fundamental group, then
X
deformation retracts onto a unique simple closed curve in
X
.

When wild points are present, they play a particularly important role in the theory of one-dimensional spaces. For instance, if
X
is a one-dimensional Peano continuum, then
\operatorname{w}(X)
is the set of fixed-points by the action of self-homotopy equivalences on
X

[
6
, Theorem 3.3]
. Strict rigidity of the wild set under continuous deformation is vital to Eda’s homotopy classification of one-dimensional Peano continua
[
9
]
, which states that one-dimensional Peano continua are homotopy equivalent if and only if they have abstractly isomorphic fundamental groups.

M. Meilstrup
[
16
]
proved that every one-dimensional Peano continuum
X
is homotopy equivalent to a one-dimensional Peano
continuum
Y
where
\operatorname{w}(X)=\operatorname{w}(Y)
and
Y\backslash\operatorname{w}(Y)
is a disjoint union of a sequence of open arcs
O_{1},O_{2},O_{3},\dots

where
\overline{O_{n}}
is a closed arc or simple closed curve and
\overline{O_{n}}\backslash O_{n}\subseteq\operatorname{w}(Y)
. A modified proof appears
in
[
9
]
. The homotopy equivalence
q:X\to Y
is a quotient map, which collapses to a point each member of countable collection of dendrites. Since we are interested in filtrations of spaces to compute topological complexity, we require the actual decomposition of
X
determined by
q
. In particular, the following can be proved by combining the results in
[
16
]
. We give a brief outline of the proof and refer to Meilstrup’s Thesis for the details.

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
X
be a one-dimensional Peano continuum with
\operatorname{w}(X)\neq\emptyset
. Then
\operatorname{w}(X)
is contained in a closed subset
Y\subseteq X
such that

(1)

X\backslash Y
is a disjoint union of open contractible sets;

(2)

\operatorname{w}(X)
is a strong deformation retract of
Y
;

(3)

The closure of every connected component of
Y\backslash\operatorname{w}(X)
is a dendrite that meets
\operatorname{w}(X)
at a single point.

Moreover, the statement holds if
X
is a finite disjoint union of one-dimensional Peano continua.

Sketch of Proof.

Following
[
16
]
, one identifies a “core” Peano continuum
C\subseteq X
, which is a strong deformation retract of
X
, contains
\operatorname{w}(X)
, and has
the property that if
U_{1},U_{2},U_{3},\dots
are the connected components of
X\backslash C
, then
E_{n}=\overline{U}_{n}
is a dendrite that meets

C
at a single point
e_{n}
(this “deforestation” process also appears in
[
6
]
). One then proceeds to identify a
(possibly finite) sequence of dendrites
D_{1},D_{2},D_{3},\dots\subseteq C
such that

(1)

D_{j}\cap\operatorname{w}(X)=\{d_{j}\}
for each
j
,

(2)

(D_{j}\backslash\{d_{j}\})\cap(D_{i}\backslash\{d_{i}\})=\emptyset
if
i\neq j
.

(3)

if
W=\operatorname{w}(X)\cup\bigcup_{j}D_{j}
, then
C\backslash W
is a (possibly finite) disjoint union of open arcs
A_{1},A_{2},A_{3},\dots
, whose closures are closed arcs disjoint from
\operatorname{w}(X)
.

(In
[
9
]
, Eda uses brick partitions to construct such a sequence of dendrites
D_{j}
). Set
Y=W\cup\bigcup\{E_{n}\mid e_{n}\in W\}
. Since adjoining a null sequence of dendrites along a sequence of points in a dendrite gives a dendrite, (2) and (3) follow. Note that a component
V
of
X\backslash Y
consists of either a set
U_{n}
or an open an open arc
A_{m}
potentially with some of the dendrites
E_{n}
attached (if
e_{n}\in A_{m}
). In particular,
V
is a connected subset of a dendrite and is therefore contractible.

The statement regarding finite disjoint unions of Peano continua follows by applying the main statement to each connected component.
∎

Corollary 3.9
.

If
X
is a one-dimensional Peano continuum, then

\mathbf{cat}(X)\leq\mathbf{cat}_{X}(\operatorname{w}(X))+1\leq\mathbf{cat}(\operatorname{w}(X))+1.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.8
, there is a closed set
Y\subseteq X
that strongly deformation retracts onto
\operatorname{w}(X)
and such that
X\backslash Y
is a disjoint union of open contractible sets. Thus
\mathbf{cat}_{X}(X\backslash Y)=0
. By the second statement of Proposition
2.7
, we have
\mathbf{cat}(X)\leq\mathbf{cat}_{X}(Y)+1
. Since the identity map gives a homotopy equivalence of pairs
(X,\operatorname{w}(X))\to(X,Y)
, we have
\mathbf{cat}_{X}(Y)=\mathbf{cat}_{X}(\operatorname{w}(X))
by Proposition
2.9
. The last inequality follows from the first part of Proposition
2.7
.
∎

The following theorem covers the case where the wild set of a one-dimensional Peano continuum is zero-dimensional, e.g. homeomorphic to the Cantor set (see Figure
3
). We note that this case is not covered by our main results because
0
-dimensional continua are not locally path connected unless they are finite and discrete and thus the
w
-stable condition may not be met.

Theorem 3.10
.

If
X
is a one-dimensional Peano continuum with
\operatorname{w}(X)\neq\emptyset
and there exists a dendrite
D
such that
\operatorname{w}(X)\subseteq D\subseteq X
, then
\mathbf{cat}(X)=1
and
\mathbf{TC}(X)=2
. In particular, this holds if
\operatorname{w}(X)
is zero-dimensional.

Proof.

Since
X
is not contractible
1\leq\mathbf{cat}(X)
. Corollary
3.9
gives
\mathbf{cat}(X)\leq\mathbf{cat}_{X}(\operatorname{w}(X))+1
. Since
D
is contractible and
\operatorname{w}(X)\subseteq D
,
\operatorname{w}(X)
is categorical in
X
. Thus
\mathbf{cat}_{X}(\operatorname{w}(X))=0
. We conclude that
\mathbf{cat}(X)=1
.

By the results of Section
2
, we have
\mathbf{TC}(X)\leq\mathbf{cat}(X\times X)\leq 2\mathbf{cat}(X)=2
. Since
\operatorname{w}(X)\neq\emptyset
, there exists a finite graph
G\subseteq X
with at least two cycles and thus
TC(G)=2
by Example
2.5
. By Lemma
3.2
,
G
is a retract of
X
, giving
\mathbf{TC}(G)\leq\mathbf{TC}(X)
. Thus
\mathbf{TC}(X)=2
.

For the second statement, we note that Katsuya Eda has proved that if
Y
is a one-dimensional Peano continuum with
\operatorname{w}(Y)
zero-dimensional, then
Y
is homotopy equivalent to a one-dimensional Peano continuum
X
and with the property that there exists a dendrite
D\subseteq X
with
\operatorname{w}(X)\subseteq D

[
10
, Lemma 2.4]
. By the homotopy invariance of LS-category and the first part of the theorem, we have
\mathbf{cat}(Y)=\mathbf{cat}(X)=1
and
\mathbf{TC}(Y)=\mathbf{TC}(X)=2
.
∎

4.
Iterated Wild Sets and LS-Category

Definition 4.1

(iterated wild sets)
.

Let
X
be a space. Set
\operatorname{w}^{0}(X)=X
. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, set
\operatorname{w}^{n}(X)=\operatorname{w}(\operatorname{w}^{n-1}(X))
. We refer to
\operatorname{w}^{n}(X)
as the
n
-th iterated wild set
of
X
.

Note that
X\supseteq\operatorname{w}(X)\supseteq\operatorname{w}^{2}(X)\supseteq\operatorname{w}^{3}(X)\supseteq\cdots
is a nested sequence of closed subspaces of
X
. It is possible that this sequence never stabilizes or that it stabilizes at a non-empty space. For example,
\operatorname{w}(S)=S
if
S
is the Sierpinski Carpet.

Definition 4.2
.

The
wildness rank
of a non-empty space
X
is the smallest positive integer
\mathbf{wrk}(X)=n
such that
\operatorname{w}^{n}(X)=\emptyset
. If no such
n
exists, we say the wildness rank is infinite and write
\mathbf{wrk}(X)=\infty
.

Remark 4.3
.

Our definition of wildness rank mimics the definition of familiar set-theoretic ranks since it, in many respects, behaves like the Cantor-Bendixson rank. However, since we show that
\mathbf{cat}(X)
and
\mathbf{TC}(X)
are infinite whenever
\mathbf{wrk}(X)
is not a finite ordinal, we do not continue the definition of iterated wild sets into transfinite ordinals here.

Remark 4.4
.

It follows from the main result in
[
3
]
that for any (one-dimensional) compact metric space
Y
, there exists a (one-dimensional) Peano continuum
X
such that
\operatorname{w}(X)=Y
. Hence, if
X
is a one-dimensional Peano continuum, then
\operatorname{w}(X)
may be an arbitrary compact metric space of dimension
\leq 1
. Moreover, iterating the construction
X\mapsto Y
, one may construct (one-dimensional) Peano continua of rank
n
for any
n\geq 0
.

A one-dimensional Peano continuum
X
(left) whose wild set
\operatorname{w}(X)
(right) is homotopy equivalent to the infinite earring space and thus
\mathbf{wrk}(X)=3
.

Definition 4.5
.

A space
X
is
w
-stable
if the
n
-th iterated wild set
\operatorname{w}^{n}(X)
is locally path-connected for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proposition 4.6
.

If
X
is a
w
-stable Peano continuum, then for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\operatorname{w}^{n}(X)
is a disjoint union of finitely many Peano continua whenever it is non-empty.

Proof.

Since
X
is
w
-stable, each path component of
\operatorname{w}^{n}(X)
is open in
\operatorname{w}^{n}(X)
. Moreover, since
X
is compact,
\operatorname{w}^{n}(X)
is also compact. Thus
\operatorname{w}^{n}(X)
must have finitely many path components. This implies that the path components of
\operatorname{w}^{n}(X)
are also closed in
X
. It follows that each path-component of
\operatorname{w}^{n}(X)
is a Peano continuum.
∎

The proof of the next lemma is straightforward and is left as an exercise.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
X
and
Y
be spaces. Then
\operatorname{w}(X\times Y)=X\times\operatorname{w}(Y)\cup\operatorname{w}(X)\times Y
.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
X
be a one-dimensional Hausdorff space. Then for all
n\geq 0
, we have
\bigcup_{i+j=n}\operatorname{w}^{i}(X)\times\operatorname{w}^{j}(X)\subseteq\operatorname{w}^{n}(X\times X)
.

Proof.

Before proving the statement in the lemma, we make a general observation. Fix
i,j
and
\operatorname{w}^{i}(X)\times\operatorname{w}^{j}(X)\subseteq Z\subseteq X\times X
. It follows from Lemma
3.6
that each inclusion map
\operatorname{w}^{i}(X)\to X
is
\pi_{1}
-injective. Thus the product embedding
\operatorname{w}^{i}(X)\times\operatorname{w}^{j}(X)\to X\times X
is
\pi_{1}
-injective for all
i,j
. Moreover, the inclusion
\operatorname{w}^{i}(X)\times\operatorname{w}^{j}(X)\to Z
must be
\pi_{1}
-injective and we have
\operatorname{w}(\operatorname{w}^{i}(X)\times\operatorname{w}^{j}(X))\subseteq\operatorname{w}(Z)
by Lemma
3.6
.

The case of
n=0
is clear and the case
n=1
is one inclusion of the equality in Lemma
4.7
. The cases
n\geq 2
are proved by induction on
n
. Supposing
\operatorname{w}^{i}(X)\times\operatorname{w}^{j}(X)\subseteq\operatorname{w}^{n}(X\times X)
, we have

\operatorname{w}^{i}(X)\times\operatorname{w}^{j+1}(X)\cup\operatorname{w}^{i+1}(X)\times\operatorname{w}^{j}(X)=\operatorname{w}(\operatorname{w}^{i}(X)\times\operatorname{w}^{j}(X))\subseteq\operatorname{w}^{n+1}(X\times X)

where the equality is an application of Lemma
4.7
and the inclusion follows from the observation made in the first paragraph.
∎

Theorem 4.9
.

If
f:X\to Y
and
g:Y\to X
are inverse homotopy equivalences, then
f|_{\operatorname{w}(X)}:\operatorname{w}(X)\to\operatorname{w}(Y)
and
g|_{\operatorname{w}(Y)}:\operatorname{w}(Y)\to\operatorname{w}(X)
are inverse homotopy equivalences. In particular,
(X,\operatorname{w}(X))
and
(Y,\operatorname{w}(Y))
are homotopy equivalent pairs.

Proof.

The restricted functions are well-defined by Lemma
3.5
. If
H:X\times[0,1]\to X
is a homotopy from
id_{X}
to
g\circ f
, then
H
is
\pi_{1}
-injective and
H(\operatorname{w}(X\times[0,1]))=H(\operatorname{w}(X)\times[0,1])\subseteq\operatorname{w}(X)
using Lemma
4.7
for the first equality. Thus
H
restricts to a map
\operatorname{w}(X)\times[0,1]\to\operatorname{w}(X)
that is a homotopy from
id_{\operatorname{w}(X)}
to
g|_{\operatorname{w}(Y)}\circ f|_{\operatorname{w}(X)}
. The other composition follows from the same argument.
∎

The next corollary follows by combining Theorems
2.9
and
4.9
.

Corollary 4.10
.

If
X
and
Y
are homotopy equivalent spaces, then
\mathbf{cat}_{X}(\operatorname{w}(X))=\mathbf{cat}_{Y}(\operatorname{w}(Y))
.

Corollary 4.11
.

Wild rank is an invariant of homotopy type.

Proof.

Suppose
X\simeq Y
. Then Theorem
4.9
implies that
\operatorname{w}^{n}(X)\simeq\operatorname{w}^{n}(Y)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Since a non-empty space cannot be homotopy equivalent to an empty space, it follows that
\operatorname{w}^{n}(X)=\emptyset
if and only if
\operatorname{w}^{n}(Y)=\emptyset
. Thus
\mathbf{wrk}(X)=\mathbf{wrk}(Y)
.
∎

Lemma 4.12
.

If
X
is a one-dimensional Hausdorff space and
H:X\times[0,1]\to X
satisfies
H(x,0)=x
for all
x\in X
, then
H(x,t)=x
for all
(x,t)\in\operatorname{w}(X)\times[0,1]
.

Proof.

It suffices to show
H(x,1)=x
for all
x\in\operatorname{w}(X)
. Fix
x\in\operatorname{w}(X)
and suppose
H(x,1)=y\neq x
. Define
f(x)=H(x,1)
. Let
U
and
V
be disjoint open neighborhoods of
x
and
y
respectively. Find a neighborhood
W
of
x
such that
H(W\times\{0\})\subseteq U
and
H(W\times\{1\})\subseteq V
. Since
x\in\operatorname{w}(X)
, we may find an essential loop
\alpha:S^{1}\to W
. Now
\alpha
and
f\circ\alpha
are freely homotopic loops in the one-dimensional Peano continuum
H(\operatorname{Im}(\alpha)\times[0,1])
having disjoint images. However, it is well-known that freely homotopic loops with disjoint images do not exist in one-dimensional metric spaces (see the proof of
[
4
, Theorem 9.13(b)]
).
∎

Applying Lemma
4.12
to the homotopies in the proof of Theorem
4.9
, we obtain the following.

Corollary 4.13
.

If
f:X\to Y
and
g:Y\to X
are inverse homotopy equivalences of one-dimensional Hausdorff spaces, then
f|_{\operatorname{w}(X)}:\operatorname{w}(X)\to\operatorname{w}(Y)
and
g|_{\operatorname{w}(Y)}:\operatorname{w}(Y)\to\operatorname{w}(X)
are inverse homeomorphisms.

Lemma 4.14
.

Suppose
X
is a
w
-stable one-dimensional Peano continuum such that
\mathbf{wrk}(X)=n<\infty
. Then there exists closed sets
\emptyset=Y_{n}\subseteq Y_{n-1}\subseteq\cdots Y_{2}\subseteq Y_{1}\subseteq Y_{0}=X
such that

(1)

Y_{j+1}\subseteq\operatorname{w}^{j}(X)\subseteq Y_{j}
for all
1\leq j\leq n-1
,

(2)

Y_{j}
strongly deformation retracts onto
\operatorname{w}^{j}(X)
for all
1\leq j\leq n-1
,

(3)

If
U
is a connected component of
Y_{j}\backslash\operatorname{w}^{j}(X)
, then
\overline{U}
is a dendrite meeting
\operatorname{w}^{j}(X)
at one point.

(4)

Y_{j-1}\backslash Y_{j}
is a disjoint union of contractible sets that are open in
Y_{j}
for all
1\leq j\leq n-1
,

(5)

Y_{j}
is a finite disjoint union of Peano continua for
1\leq j\leq n-1
.

Proof.

Since
X
is
w
-stable, it follows from Proposition
4.6
that
\operatorname{w}^{j}(X)
is a finite disjoint union of Peano continua for all
j\geq 0
. Applying Theorem
3.8
to
Y_{0}=X
, we obtain
Y_{1}
satisfying conditions (1)-(4) for
j=1
. Since
\operatorname{w}(X)
is a finite disjoint union of Peano continua and
Y_{1}
consists of
\operatorname{w}(X)
and a null-sequence of attached dendrites,
Y_{1}
is a finite disjoint union of Peano continua that deformation retracts onto
\operatorname{w}(X)
. Hence, (5) holds for
j=1
. Since the inclusion
\operatorname{w}(X)\to Y_{1}
is a homotopy equivalence, we have
\operatorname{w}(Y_{1})=\operatorname{w}^{2}(X)
by Corollary
4.13
. For all following steps we recursively apply the second statement of Theorem
3.8
(for finite disjoint unions of Peano continua) to the space
Y_{j-1}
to obtain
Y_{j}
. Altogether, we obtain a sequence
Y_{1},Y_{2},Y_{3},\dots,Y_{n-1}
of spaces satisfying (1)-(5) noting that the equality
\operatorname{w}(Y_{j})=\operatorname{w}^{2}(Y_{j-1})=\operatorname{w}^{j+1}(X)
holds for all
j
within the recursion.
∎

Lemma 4.15
.

Suppose
X
is a
w
-stable one-dimensional Peano continuum such that
\mathbf{wrk}(X)=n<\infty
. Then
\mathbf{cat}(X)\leq n
. Moreover, if
\operatorname{w}^{n-1}(X)
contains no simple closed curve, then
\mathbf{cat}(X)\leq n-1
.

Proof.

Lemma
4.14
gives a filtration of closed sets
Y_{n-1}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq Y_{2}\subseteq Y_{1}\subseteq Y_{0}=X
each of which is a finite disjoint union of Peano continua, such that each set
Y_{j-1}\backslash Y_{j}
is a topological sum of contractible sets, and such that
Y_{j}
strongly deformation retracts onto
\operatorname{w}^{j}(X)
for
1\leq j\leq n-1
.

If
\operatorname{w}^{n-1}(X)
contains no simple closed curve, then
Y_{n-1}
contains no simple closed curve. In this case, it follows that
Y_{n-1}
is a finite disjoint union of dendrites and is therefore categorical in
X
. Set
Y_{n}=\emptyset
. The filtration
\{Y_{n-j}\}_{1\leq j\leq n}
proves that
\mathbf{cat}(X)\leq n-1
.

If
\operatorname{w}^{n-1}(X)
contains a simple closed curve, then so does
Y_{n-1}
. In this case, let
P_{1},P_{2},\dots,P_{m}
denote the connected components of
Y_{n-1}
. Since
\operatorname{w}^{n}(X)=\operatorname{w}(Y_{n-1})=\emptyset
and
Y_{n-1}
deformation retracts onto
\operatorname{w}^{n-1}(X)
, each
P_{i}
is a Peano continuum with no wild points. Theorem
3.7
implies the existence of a finite graph
G_{i}\subseteq P_{i}
that is a strong deformation retract of
P_{i}
(take
G_{i}
to be a point if
P_{i}
is simply connected). Choose a maximal spanning tree
T_{i}
in
G_{i}
for each
i
and let
Y_{n}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}T_{i}
. The set
Y_{n-1}\backslash Y_{n}
is a topological sum of sets that contain no simple closed curves and is therefore categorical in
X
. The set
Y_{n}
is a finite disjoint union of finite trees and is therefore categorical in
X
. Set
Y_{n+1}=\emptyset
. The filtration
\{Y_{n-j}\}_{0\leq j\leq n}
proves that
\mathbf{cat}(X)\leq n
.
∎

Here we use iterated wild sets to obtain lower bounds on the category of a one-dimensional space.

Lemma 4.16
.

Assume
A\subseteq B\subseteq X
where
X
is one-dimensional Hausdorff and
B\backslash A
is categorical in
X
. Then
Y\subseteq B
implies
\operatorname{w}(Y)\subseteq A
.

Proof.

Suppose
x\in\operatorname{w}(Y)\backslash A
. Since
Y\backslash A
is an open neighborhood of
x
in
Y
, there exists an essential loop
\alpha:S^{1}\to Y\backslash A
. Since
X
is
1
-dimensional, the inclusion map
Y\backslash A\to X
is
\pi_{1}
-inective (by Lemma
3.6
) and thus
\alpha:S^{1}\to X
is essential. Since
B\backslash A
is assumed to be categorical in
X
,
\alpha
cannot have image in
B
. Thus there exists a point
y\in\operatorname{Im}(\alpha)\backslash B\subseteq Y\backslash B
.
∎

Lemma 4.17
.

If
X
is a one-dimensional Hausdorff space and
\mathbf{cat}(X)=m<\infty
, then
\mathbf{wrk}(X)\leq m+1
. Moreover, if
\operatorname{w}^{m}(X)
has a non-simply connected path-component, then
\mathbf{wrk}(X)\leq m
.

Proof.

If
\mathbf{cat}(X)=m<\infty
, then there exists closed sets
\emptyset=F_{-1}\subseteq F_{0}\subseteq F_{1}\subseteq F_{2}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq F_{m}=X
such that
F_{j}\backslash F_{j-1}
is categorical in
X
for
0\leq j\leq m
. Applying Lemma
4.16
in the case where
Y=X
,
A=F_{m-1}
,
B=F_{m}
, we have
\operatorname{w}(X)\subseteq F_{m-1}
. Applying Lemma
4.16
in the case where
Y=\operatorname{w}(X)
,
A=F_{m-2}
, and
B=F_{m-1}
, we have
\operatorname{w}^{2}(X)\subseteq F_{m-2}
. Proceeding recursively, we eventually obtain
\operatorname{w}^{j}(X)\subseteq F_{m-j}
for
0\leq j\leq m+1
. In particular,
\operatorname{w}^{m+1}(X)\subseteq F_{-1}=\emptyset
, which gives
\mathbf{wrk}(X)\leq m+1
.

For the second statement, suppose
\operatorname{w}^{m}(X)
has a non-simply connected path-component and, to obtain a contradiction, that
\mathbf{wrk}(X)>m
. The recursion from the first paragraph gives
\emptyset=F_{-1}\neq\operatorname{w}^{m}(X)\subseteq F_{0}
where
F_{0}
is categorical in
X
. Since
\operatorname{w}(\operatorname{w}^{m}(X))\neq\emptyset
,
\operatorname{w}^{m}(X)
admits loops that are essential. Applying Lemma
3.6
to the inclusions
\operatorname{w}^{m}(X)\to F_{0}\to X
, we see that
F_{0}
admits loops that are essential in
X
. However, this contradicts the fact that
F_{0}
is categorical in
X
.
∎

Corollary 4.18
.

Let
X
be a one-dimensional Hausdorff space. If
\mathbf{wrk}(X)=\infty
, then
\mathbf{cat}(X)=\infty
and
\mathbf{TC}(X)=\infty
.

Proof.

Given
\mathbf{wrk}(X)=\infty
,
\mathbf{cat}(X)=\infty
follows from the previous lemma. Since
\mathbf{cat}(X)\leq\mathbf{TC}(X)
(recall Theorem
2.4
), we also have
\mathbf{TC}(X)=\infty
.
∎

Example 4.19
.

If
X
is a one-dimensional Hausdorff space and there exists a non-empty subspace
A\subseteq X
such that
\operatorname{w}(A)=A
, e.g. if it contains a copy of the Sierpinski Carpet, then no finite motion plan exists for
X
even with our modified definitions. Indeed, for such a space, Lemma
3.6
implies that
A=\operatorname{w}^{n}(A)\subseteq\operatorname{w}^{n}(X)
for all
n\geq 0
and thus
\mathbf{wrk}(X)=\infty
. Corollary
4.18
then gives
\mathbf{cat}(X)=\infty
and
\mathbf{TC}(X)=\infty
.

At this point, we obtain exact values for LS-category in the
w
-stable case.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Suppose
X
is a
w
-stable one-dimensional Hausdorff space with
\mathbf{wrk}(X)=n<\infty
. The upper bound
\mathbf{cat}(X)\leq n
(and
\mathbf{cat}(X)\leq n-1
if
\operatorname{w}^{n-1}(X)
contains no simple closed curve) is given in Lemma
4.15
. The lower bound
n-1\leq\mathbf{cat}(X)
(and
n\leq\mathbf{cat}(X)
if
\operatorname{w}^{n-1}(X)
contains a simple closed curve) is given in Lemma
4.17
.
∎

5.
Topological complexity of one-dimensional spaces

In this section we prove Theorem
1.2
, which generalizes neatly the computation of the topological
complexity of graphs described in Example
2.5
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
X
be a one-dimensional Peano continuum and
G\subseteq X
be a (not necessarily connected) finite graph with more than one cycle. If
\emptyset\subseteq A_{0}\subseteq A_{1}\subseteq X\times X
are closed sets and
G\times G\subseteq A_{1}
, then at least one of
A_{0}
or
A_{1}\backslash A_{0}
does not admit a motion plan in
X
.

Proof.

Suppose to obtain a contradiction that
A_{0}
and
A_{1}\backslash A_{0}
admit a motion plan in
X
. If
G
has a connected component
H
, which has more than two cycles, then
H\cap A_{0}
and
H\cap(A_{1}\backslash A_{0})
admit a motion plan in
X
. However,
H
is a retract of
X
by Lemma
3.2
. Thus
H\cap A_{0}
and
H\cap(A_{1}\backslash A_{0})
admit a motion plan in
H
. However, this contradicts our computation in
Example
2.5
.

We now consider the case where each connected component of
G
has either no cycles or one cycle. Let
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
be two
disjoint components of
G
each containing one cycle. Set
C=C_{1}\cup C_{2}
,
K_{0}=(C\times C)\cap A_{0}
, and

K_{1}=(C\times C)\cap A_{1}=C\times C
. Then
K_{0}
and
K_{1}\backslash K_{0}
admit motion plans in
X
. Let

\alpha:[0,1]\to X
be an injective path with
\alpha(0)\in C_{1}
and
\alpha(1)\in C_{2}
. Since
C_{1}
and
C_{2}

are closed, we may assume that the arc
A=\alpha([0,1])
meets
C_{1}
only at
\alpha(0)
and
C_{2}
only at

\alpha(1)
. Note that
\widetilde{C}=C_{1}\cup A\cup C_{2}
is a connected graph with exactly two cycles.
Since
\widetilde{C}
is a retract of
X
by Lemma
3.2
,
K_{0}
and
K_{1}\backslash K_{0}

admit motion plans in
\widetilde{C}
. In other terms, the relative topological complexity

\mathbf{TC}_{\widetilde{C}}(C\times C)
is at most 1. However, we will show that by a cup-length argument

\mathbf{TC}_{\widetilde{C}}(C\times C)\geq 2
.

First observe, that
\mathbf{TC}_{\widetilde{C}}(C\times C)
can be equivalently described as the topological
complexity of the inclusion map
i\colon C\hookrightarrow\widetilde{C}
in the sense of

[
22
, Example 3.3]
.
Then
[
22
, Theorem 3.10]
provides a cohomological estimate for relative topological complexity
as
\mathbf{TC}_{\widetilde{C}}(C\times C)\geq\text{ cup-length }\big((i\times i)^{*}(\operatorname{Ker}\Delta^{*})\big),

where
\Delta^{*}\colon H^{*}(\widetilde{C}\times\widetilde{C})\to H^{*}(\widetilde{C})
is induced by the
diagonal map
\Delta\colon\widetilde{C}\to\widetilde{C}\times\widetilde{C}
and

(i\times i)^{*}\colon H^{*}(\widetilde{C}\times\widetilde{C})\to H^{*}(C\times C)
is induced by the inclusion

i\times i\colon C\times C\hookrightarrow\widetilde{C}\times\widetilde{C}
.
(Note that
C
and
\widetilde{C}
are ENRs, so we may use singular cohomology in our computations.)
Since the homomorphism

(i\times i)^{*}
is clearly injective, it follows that

\text{cup-length }\big((i\times i)^{*}(\operatorname{Ker}\Delta^{*})\big)\geq\text{ cup-length }(\operatorname{Ker}\Delta^{*}).

It is well-known (see for example
[
12
, Prop. 4.42]
) that

\text{cup-length }(\operatorname{Ker}\Delta^{*})=2
, which concludes our proof.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

Suppose that
A\subseteq X\times X
. If there exists
n\geq 1
, an essential loop
\alpha:S^{1}\to X
, and
a point
x\in X
such that
\operatorname{Im}(\alpha)\times\{x\}\subseteq A
or
\{x\}\times\operatorname{Im}(\alpha)\subseteq A
, then

A
does not admit a motion plan in
X
.

Proof.

If
\alpha
and
x
exists such that
\operatorname{Im}(\alpha)\times\{x\}\subseteq A
, let
\gamma:S^{1}\to A
be given by
\gamma(t)=(\alpha(t),x)
. Since the projections
p_{1},p_{2}:A\to X
are homotopic, the loops
f_{1}=p_{1}\circ\gamma:S^{1}\to X
and
f_{2}=p_{2}\circ\gamma:S^{1}\to X
are homotopic. However,
f_{1}=\alpha
is essential and
f_{2}
is the constant map at
x
; a contradiction. The other case follows using a symmetric argument.
∎

Lemma 5.3
.

Suppose
X
is a one-dimensional Hausdorff space and
A\subseteq B\subseteq X\times X
are closed. If
B\backslash A
admits a motion plan in
X
, then
\operatorname{w}(Y\times Z)\subseteq A
for all
Y\times Z\subseteq B
.

Proof.

Suppose that
B\backslash A
admits a motion plan in
X
. Suppose to the contrary that
Y,Z\subseteq X
are such that
Y\times Z\subseteq B
and such that
\operatorname{w}(Y\times Z)
meets
B\backslash A
at a point
(y,z)
. Recall from Lemma
4.7
that
\operatorname{w}(Y\times Z)=Y\times\operatorname{w}(Z)\cup\operatorname{w}(Y)\times Z
. Then it must be the case that either
y\in\operatorname{w}(Y)
or
z\in\operatorname{w}(Z)
. Suppose without loss of generality that
y\in\operatorname{w}(Y)
. Since
A
is closed, we may find open sets
U,V\subseteq X
such that
(y,z)\in(U\times V)\cap B\subseteq B\backslash A
. Since
y\in\operatorname{w}(Y)
, we can find a loop
\alpha:S^{1}\to Y\cap U
that is essential in
X
. Since
\operatorname{Im}(\alpha)\times\{z\}\subseteq(Y\cap U)\times\{z\}\subseteq(U\times Z)\cap B\subseteq B\backslash A
, we obtain a contradiction of Lemma
5.2
.
∎

Lemma 5.4
.

If
X
is a one-dimensional Hausdorff space and
\mathbf{wrk}(X)=n<\infty
, then
\mathbf{TC}(X)\geq 2n-2
. Moreover,

(1)

if
\operatorname{w}^{n-1}(X)
contains a simple closed curve, then
\mathbf{TC}(X)\geq 2n-1
;

(2)

if
X
is a Peano continuum and
\operatorname{w}^{n-1}(X)
contains two distinct simple closed curves, then
\mathbf{TC}(X)\geq 2n
.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathbf{TC}(X)=m
and
\emptyset=A_{-1}\subseteq A_{0}\subseteq A_{1}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq A_{m}=X\times X
are closed subsets such that
A_{j+1}\backslash A_{j}
admits a motion plan in
X
for all
j
. Note that if
\operatorname{w}^{i}(X)\times\operatorname{w}^{j}(X)\subseteq A_{k}
, then Lemmas
4.7
and
5.3
imply that

\operatorname{w}^{i}(X)\times\operatorname{w}^{j+1}(X)\cup\operatorname{w}^{i+1}(X)\times\operatorname{w}^{j}(X)=\operatorname{w}(\operatorname{w}^{i}(X)\times\operatorname{w}^{j}(X))\subseteq A_{k-1}.

Starting with
X\times X=\operatorname{w}^{0}(X)\times\operatorname{w}^{0}(X)\subseteq A_{m}
and recursively taking the wild set in the first coordinate
(n-1
)-times, we have
\operatorname{w}^{n-1}(X)\times X\subseteq A_{m-n+1}
. We continue by recursively taking the wild in the second coordinate
(n-1)
-times to obtain
\operatorname{w}^{n-1}(X)\times\operatorname{w}^{n-1}(X)\subseteq A_{m-2n+2}
. Since
\operatorname{w}^{n-1}(X)\times\operatorname{w}^{n-1}(X)\neq\emptyset
, we have
m-2n+2\geq 0
. Thus
m\geq 2n-2
.

For (1), suppose
\operatorname{w}^{n-1}(X)
contains a simple closed curve. Then there exists an essential loop
\alpha:S^{1}\to\operatorname{w}^{n-1}(X)
based at a point
x\in\operatorname{w}^{n-1}(X)
. By Lemma
3.6
,
\alpha
is also essential in
X
. Now
\operatorname{Im}(\alpha)\times\{x\}\subseteq\operatorname{w}^{n-1}(X)\times\operatorname{w}^{n-1}(X)\subseteq A_{m-2n+2}
and thus by Lemma
5.2
,
A_{m-2n+2}
cannot admit a motion plan. In particular,
m-2n+2\geq 1
and thus
m\geq 2n-1
.

For (2), suppose
X
is a Peano continuum and
\operatorname{w}^{n-1}(X)
contains two distinct simple closed curves. Then there exists a (not necessarily connected) finite simplicial graph
G\subseteq\operatorname{w}^{n-1}(X)
with at least two cycles. Since
G\times G\subseteq A_{m-2n+2}
, Proposition
5.1
ensures that
m-2n+2\geq 2
. Thus
m\geq 2n
.
∎

Lemma 5.5
.

If
X
is a
w
-stable one-dimensional Peano continuum with
\mathbf{wrk}(X)=n<\infty
, then

\mathbf{TC}(X)\leq\begin{cases}2n-2,&\text{if }\operatorname{w}^{n-1}(X)\text{ contains no simple closed curve},\\
2n-1,&\text{if }\operatorname{w}^{n-1}(X)\text{ contains one simple closed curve},\\
2n,&\text{ otherwise},\\
\end{cases}

Proof.

By Lemma
4.14
, there exists closed sets
F_{1}\subseteq F_{2}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq F_{n}=X
such that
F_{j}\backslash F_{j-1}
is categorical in
X
(re-indexing from the lemma by
F_{j}=Y_{n-j}
) and where
F_{1}
deformation retracts onto
\operatorname{w}^{n-1}(X)
. Since
X
is
w
-stable,
\operatorname{w}^{n-1}(X)
is a finite disjoint union of one-dimensional Peano continua (Proposition
4.6
) with no wild points. Mimicking the proof of the inequality
\mathbf{cat}(X\times X)\leq 2\,\mathbf{cat}(X)
(see Section
2
), we define
H_{k}=\bigcup_{i+j=k}F_{i}\times F_{j}
for
k=2,3,4,\dots,2n
. For each
k\in\{3,4,\dots,2n\}
,
H_{k}\backslash H_{k-1}
is categorical in
X\times X
and therefore admits a motion plan in
X
.

Case (1): If
\operatorname{w}^{n-1}(X)
admits no simple closed curve, then both
\operatorname{w}^{n-1}(X)
and
F_{1}
are finite disjoint unions of dendrites. In this case,
F_{1}
is categorical in
X
and
H_{2}=F_{1}\times F_{1}
admits a motion plan in
X
. Hence
\mathbf{TC}(X)\leq 2n-2
.

For Cases (2) and (3), Theorem
3.7
ensures that
\operatorname{w}^{n-1}(X)
strongly deformation retracts onto a subspace
G\subseteq\operatorname{w}^{n-1}(X)
, which is a finite disjoint union of finite connected graphs
G_{1},G_{2},\dots,G_{r}
with no free edges and where at least one of these components is not simply connected. Thus
F_{1}
strongly deformation retracts onto
G
. If
\phi:F_{1}\to G
is a retraction and a homotopy equivalence, then so is
\phi\times\phi:F_{1}\times F_{1}\to G\times G=\coprod_{1\leq l,m\leq r}G_{l}\times G_{m}
. Thus, if
A\subseteq G\times G
admits a motion plan in
G
, then
(\phi\times\phi)^{-1}(A)
admits a motion plan in
F_{1}
(and thus in
X
). With this set-up we prove these cases separately.

Case (2): In this case there exists a unique
m_{0}
such that
G_{m_{0}}
is a circle and
G_{m}
is a point when
m\neq m_{0}
. Using the standard construction for the circle in Section
2
, may find
\emptyset\subseteq K^{m_{0},m_{0}}_{1}\subseteq K^{m_{0},m_{0}}_{2}=G_{m_{0}}\times G_{m_{0}}
where
K^{m_{0},m_{0}}_{1}
and
K^{m_{0},m_{0}}_{2}\backslash K^{m_{0},m_{0}}_{1}
admit a motion plan in
G_{m_{0}}
(and thus in
X
). Whenever
(l,m)\neq(m_{0},m_{0})
,
G_{l}\times G_{m}
is a point or a circle and thus we can find
\emptyset\subseteq K^{l,m}_{1}\subseteq K^{l,m}_{2}=G_{l}\times G_{m}
where
K^{l,m}_{1}
and
K^{l,m}_{2}\backslash K^{l,m}_{1}
admit a motion plan in
X
. Set
H_{1}=(\phi\times\phi)^{-1}(\bigcup_{1\leq l,m\leq r}K^{l,m}_{1})
and note that
H_{2}=(\phi\times\phi)^{-1}(\bigcup_{1\leq l,m\leq r}K^{l,m}_{2})
. Since
H_{1}
and
H_{2}\backslash H_{1}
are finite disjoint unions of sets that admit motion plans in
X
, they both admit motion plans in
X
. Thus
\mathbf{TC}(X)\leq 2n-1
.

Case (3): For each
1\leq m\leq r
, let
T_{m}
be a maximal spanning tree in
G_{m}
. For each pair
(l,m)
, let
K^{l,m}_{0}=T_{l}\times T_{m}
,
K^{l,m}_{1}=G_{l}\times T_{m}\cup T_{l}\times G_{m}
, and
K^{l,m}_{2}=G_{l}\times G_{m}
. Note that the set
K_{l,m}^{0}
,
K^{l,m}_{1}\backslash K^{l,m}_{0}
, and
K^{l,m}_{2}\backslash K^{l,m}_{1}
(for
l\neq m
) are all categorical in
X\times X
and therefore admit a motion plan in
X
. Define
H_{0}=(\phi\times\phi)^{-1}(\bigcup_{1\leq l,m\leq r}K^{l,m}_{0})
and
H_{1}=(\phi\times\phi)^{-1}(\bigcup_{1\leq l,m\leq r}K^{l,m}_{1})
. Then
H_{0}\subseteq H_{1}\subseteq H_{2}
where
H_{0}
,
H_{1}\backslash H_{0}
and
H_{2}\backslash H_{1}
admit motion plans in
X
since they are finite disjoint unions of sets that admit motion plans in
X
. Thus
\mathbf{TC}(X)\leq 2n
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

The theorem follows directly by combining the lower bounds in Lemma
5.4
and the upper bounds in Lemma
5.5
.
∎
