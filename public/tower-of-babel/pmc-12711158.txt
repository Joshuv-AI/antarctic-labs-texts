Scaffold‐Based Libraries Versus Make‐on‐Demand Space: A Comparative Assessment of Chemical Content
Authors: Léonard Bui, Teodora Djikic‐Stojsic, Guillaume Bret, Frédéric Bihel, Esther Kellenberger
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Chemical libraries are essential in drug discovery, providing a vast variety of compounds for screening and exploration. Previously, through collective efforts of chemoinformaticians and chemists, the group has created two libraries: the essential eIMS containing 578 in‐stock compounds on plates ready for high throughput screening and a companion virtual library vIMS, containing 821.069 compounds derived from the scaffolds of the eIMS compounds, and decorated with substituents from customized collection of R‐groups. In this article, validation of this library design approach is aimed, which is built on scaffold‐based structuring and decoration guided by chemists’ expertise. Specifically, its effectiveness is evaluated in comparison to the widely adopted reaction‐ and building block‐based approach. Using Enamine REAL Space library, two scaffold‐focused datasets are developed and the make‐on‐demand chemical space containing the same scaffolds are systematically compared. The results showed similarity between the two, but with limited strict overlap. Interestingly, a significant portion of the R‐groups are not identified as such in the make‐on‐demand library. Synthetic accessibility analysis of the compound sets indicated overall low to moderate synthetic difficulty. These findings confirm the value of the scaffold‐based method for generating focused libraries, offering high potential for lead optimization in drug discovery.

Introduction

The concept of the chemical scaffold has long been central to the fields of chemoinformatics and drug discovery. Serving as the core structure around which molecular diversity is built, scaffolds provide a crucial framework for analyzing libraries and classifying compounds.[ 1 ] In drug discovery, the scaffold has been viewed as the structural framework that determines the molecular activity.[ 2 ] Inspired by combinatorial chemistry, scaffold‐based design consists in substituting scaffolds with diverse R‐groups, enabling medicinal chemists to systematically investigate structure–activity relationships (SAR) and optimize bioactive compounds.[ 3 ] This methodology is particularly valued for its ability to identify key chemical features driving biological activity and refine molecular properties. Scaffolds play a critical role in intellectual property rights protection, exemplified by Markush structures in chemical patents.[ 4 ] These representations capture a scaffold along with a broad range of allowable substituents, ensuring comprehensive coverage of chemical space. Recently, we applied scaffold‐driven approach to chemical library design, combining cheminformatics and synthetic chemistry to create a small, diverse, and original panel from in‐stock compounds synthesized within the laboratory, while further enhancing the collection's pharmacophoric diversity through strategic scaffold modification.[ 5 ] The virtual library, named vIMS, was built on the expertise of chemists, who designed the scaffolds and substitution points. Data mining through chemoinformatics identified rules for compound generation, encompassing common decorations among our in‐stock compounds and drug‐like compounds,[ 6 ] their combinations based on the number of substitution points, and filters to exclude irrelevant chemistries.

At the same time, computational technologies have significantly advanced, enabling the evolution of chemical library design from scaffold‐based methods to innovative approaches that expand the possibilities for exploring chemical space. Artificial intelligence has emerged as a promising tool for generating novel compounds[ 7 ] showing significant potential despite not yet being fully mature to supersede more classical rule‐based approaches.[ 8 ] Currently, the state‐of‐the‐art in library design relies on on‐demand virtual libraries constructed from predefined reactions and building blocks. Pioneering work, such as Niklaus's development of the synthetically accessible virtual inventory library, which in its 2016 version included over 283 million easily synthesizable organic molecules with proposed synthetic routes, exemplifies this approach by enabling systematic exploration of chemical space while ensuring synthetic accessibility.[ 9 ] The Pan‐Canadian Chemical Library integrates innovative chemical reactions from Canadian academic research and reagents from the ZINC database to generate over 148 billion synthesizable compounds, including 128 million cost‐effective, druglike molecules.[ 10 ] Chemistry suppliers have seized on this technology, and combined with their practical know‐how, provide access to billions of compounds. This diversity is ensured by a large variety of building blocks associated with a small number of reactions.[ 11 , 12 ]

Within this evolving landscape, library design strategies can be categorized into approaches that prioritize focus or breadth, respectively, the decoration method based on scaffold and R‐groups,[ 13 ] or the enumeration method based on reaction and building block[ 14 ] (Figure 1 ). Although different, the two methods follow strict rules for building compounds and filtering unrealistic chemistry. Importantly, they are both guided by the chemists’ expertise, to define scaffold including attachment points or to prioritize building blocks depending on the reaction, focusing on the synthesizability of the virtual compounds.

The two compared library design strategies.

In this study, we addressed two key questions: How similar or different are the chemical spaces generated by the two library design strategies, and what is the predicted synthetic accessibility of the compared compounds sets? Our investigation was based on the REAL Space library, focusing on two principal reactions (amide and sulfonamide reaction) and 180,000 building blocks. This ultralarge space, accounting for over 60 billion virtual compounds, was explored using a dedicated tool designed for navigating this nonenumerated library. We generated a virtual ensemble from over 1700 frequent scaffolds in this set using the Scaffold‐based methodology developed from vIMS and systematically compared the resulting chemical spaces with REAL Space compounds.

Results

Scaffold‐Based Library Preparation

The first phase of the study involved the development of a virtual compound library using the scaffold‐based approach, consisting of two main steps. Initially, frequent scaffolds were identified from the make‐on‐demand REAL Space library,[ 15 ] followed by the decoration of these scaffolds to generate diverse molecular structures (Figure 2 ).

Pipeline for generation of scaffold‐based library.

Specifically, a first dataset was created based on the amide reaction,[ 16 ] with 2,000 molecules generated from two randomly picked types of building blocks, one containing a carboxylic acid group and another containing an amine group. For each of the in silico synthesized products, the corresponding Bemis and Murcko[ 17 ] scaffold was identified and its frequency assessed in the make‐on‐demand library. If a scaffold appeared in at least 10,000 structures of the REAL Space, it was considered frequent and selected for further exploration. Each frequent scaffold was then analyzed for substitution sites and common properties of substituents at each site. A total of 1,136 frequent scaffolds were selected from the 2,000 examined. For each of these scaffolds, a focused virtual compound library was created by varying the decorations while preserving the type of substitution. For example, if mainly halogen and small substituents were encountered on a specific position, only halogen or small R‐groups were attached to the scaffold at this position. The process yielded a total of 1,663,000 virtual molecules, out of which 1,129,158 passed filters to remove duplicates and irrelevant chemistry.

Similarly, a second dataset was prepared based on the sulfonamide reaction. A total of 2,000 different molecules were generated from random picking of suitable building blocks, including 1,007 sulfonyl chloride and 49,704 secondary amines. Among the corresponding Bemis and Murcko scaffolds, only 581 scaffolds were frequently retrieved in the REAL Space. Their decoration resulted in the generation of 721,720 molecules, reduced to 449,560 after removal of duplicates and irrelevant chemistry. All the structures and the scaffolds used can be found in Supporting Information 1–4 as smiles strings.

Interestingly, the two datasets differ significantly in size, even though they were built using the same pipeline. The in silico products of the sulfonamide reaction are more often missing compared to those of the amide reaction in the REAL Space. As a consequence, the sulfonamide dataset is about twice smaller than the amide dataset.

The two generated datasets display broadly comparable distributions with respect to the number of scaffold substitution points, as well as the ranges of molecular weights and logP values covered. In both datasets, the majority of compounds comprise between two and four rings (Figure 3 ).

Comparison of Scaffold‐Based and Make‐on‐Demand Libraries

The generated sulfonamide and the generated amide datasets were compared to the make‐on‐demand REAL Space library. For both datasets, each molecule was searched in the REAL chemical space, and the best match, that is, the most similar molecule, was identified. A maximum common substructure (MCS) score is used to assess the similarity, with a value of 1 indicating an exact match.

For both the generated amide and generated sulfonamide datasets, the distribution of MCS‐ similarity values exhibits a unimodal shape centered around 0.84, with truncation at the upper bound of 1 (Figure 4 ). Overall, MCS‐values are relatively high, indicating that the molecules generated by the scaffold‐based approach are close to those generated from reaction and building blocks. Several examples of scores are provided in Figure 5 to show that a MCS‐similarity of 0.84 corresponds to only a minimal change in structure, corresponding for example to one extra atom. Finally, when focusing on the strictly identical portion between the compared datasets, it corresponds to only 7% and 6% of the generated amide and generated sulfonamide datasets, respectively.

On Figure 5, different pairs of generated compounds and their closest analog in the REAL space library are shown at different MCS‐similarity value. For similarity of 0.85 or higher, the structural changes are minimal. As the MCS‐similarity decreases, we observe that the generated compounds are larger than their closest analog. Another clear trend is difference in scaffold increasing upon MCS‐similarity decrease. Typically, while 85% of the pairs with MCS‐similarity above 0.85 share exactly the same scaffold, this proportion decreases to 61%, 47%, and below 40% if MCS‐similarity is comprised in the 0.85–0.60, 0.60–0.45, or 0.45–0.00 intervals, respectively.

Validation of the R‐Group Collection

We then questioned the relevance of the R‐groups used for the scaffold‐based construction of molecules. These groups were derived from the analysis of ≈10,000 physical molecules synthesized in three laboratories specialized in organic chemistry, biological chemistry, and medicinal chemistry over the last decade. A total of 667 R‐groups were identified, each associated with a frequency of occurrence. This set was complemented by 23 R‐groups recommended in the literature and 8 types of R‐groups from the Enamine building blocks catalog (carboxylic, sulfonyl, amino acids, and ethers), resulting in a total of 338 R‐groups. The entire set of 1,028 R‐groups was then divided into seven categories depending on the physicochemical properties: alkyl, aromatic, hydrophilic, heterocyclic, sulfonyl, small substituents containing up to four atoms, and bulky substituents with more than 12 heavy atoms (Figure 6 ).

A) Molecular weight distribution for our collection of R‐groups. B) Distribution of the R‐group types using the unique collection of R‐groups.

Each of the 1,028 R‐groups was searched against the REAL Space library. 80% of these R‐groups were retrieved as substituent linked to the rest of the molecule by a single bond. Noteworthy, among the 20% of R‐groups that were not retrieved as substituents, many are relatively simple and common chemical groups, yet connected by more than one bond, either as part of a multiring system or functioning as linkers. Approximately half of them (78 in total) are completely absent from the REAL Space as R‐groups or substructures. Figure 6B shows the types distribution of present and absent R‐groups from REAL Space. A direct search through the Enamine Store website using the R‐groups without indication of substitution point identified three additional R‐groups as superstructure in REAL Space. Moreover, 39 of the R‐groups absent from REAL Space were found to be substructures of building blocks, either coming directly from the Enamine catalog (23 out of 39), comprising ca. 300,000 building blocks, or from MADE set (16 out of 39), comprising ca. 800 million building blocks for make‐on‐demand design. Overall, our analysis suggests that not all building blocks available at Enamine are used to build the REAL Space, likely due to synthesizability issues or pricing. A recap of the R‐groups completely absent from the REAL Space can be found in Supporting Information 6.

Synthetic Accessibility

Both scaffold‐based and make‐on‐demand libraries include compounds that are considered synthetically accessible, though they exist in a virtual format. Computational approach allows for predicting the synthetic feasibility of compounds either by exploring retrosynthetic rules, which can be time‐consuming in terms of computational resources, or by using empirical rules for a more efficient estimation.[ 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 – 22 ] Here, we used a complexity‐based method, SAScore, which provides an output value on a scale from 1 to 10, ranging from easy to very difficult.[ 23 , 24 ]

Overall, the compounds of the generated amide and sulfonamide datasets are generally easy to synthesize (Figure 7 A–C). Additionally, the synthetic accessibility scores suggest that compounds exactly identified in the on‐demand library are slightly easier to synthesize. A 0.3 increase in the distribution mean score is observed when considering compounds library with close analogs in make‐on‐demand library (MCS‐similarity > 0.9), and an additional 0.5 increase is observed when considering the rest of the dataset. Despite these observations from the mean values of the subsets, it is not meaningful to compare them directly. Indeed, running a Kolmogorov–Smirnov test shows that all the subsets do not follow the same distribution, making it difficult to draw a definitive conclusion. As such, scaffold‐based libraries are not necessarily harder to synthesize than make‐on‐demand libraries.

Discussion

Ultralarge chemical libraries, encompassing billions of compounds, have become crucial in modern drug discovery, facilitating the exploration of vast chemical spaces.[ 11 ] A notable example of ultralarge library is the REAL Space database, which offers a virtual collection of over 60 billion compounds, including 80% of compounds highly feasible. This database integrates with computational tools, such as SpaceMACS, used in this study for substructure search.

The REAL Space database is built from 167 validated synthesis protocols[ 16 ] and about 170,000 building blocks. In this work, we focused on the 2‐component reactions, and selected two of the most commonly used reactions, specifically for the formation of amide and sulfonamide products. Unlike other reactions, products from amide and sulfonamide reactions are obtained through their respective reactions (i.e. rarely synthesized through different pathways), justifying our choice.

The amide and sulfonamide products generated here were based on scaffolds from REAL Space database and decorations derived from preliminary work.[ 5 ] The comparison with REAL Space database reveals that the two construction methods, scaffold‐based and reaction‐based converge, exhibiting similar chemical spaces, despite a limited strict overlap. Additionally, compounds generated from scaffold‐based approach showed high‐to‐medium predicted synthetic accessibility, similarly to the related on‐demand compounds.

Ultralarge libraries offer clear advantages in hit discovery, including broadening chemical diversity, revealing novel chemotypes, improving docking scores, and increasing the number of active compounds identified.[ 25 , 26 ] As libraries grow, they also introduce challenges, such as scoring artifacts and a dilution of bio‐like chemical space[ 27 ] increased computational demands, both in terms of processing time[ 28 ] and the computational tools, and hardware required to navigate and analyze such vast chemical spaces. These strengths and limitations are most evident during the early discovery phase, where the impact of ultralarge libraries has been most pronounced. When it comes to progressing hits into leads, optimization still relies heavily on classical approaches. Here, medicinal chemists play a central role, using SAR and expert knowledge to guide synthesis and refinement. This traditional, more focused strategy—capitalizing on nuanced chemistry and known compound series—remains essential for advancing initial hits toward drug‐like candidates. Scaffold‐based virtual library offers a balanced approach, enabling exploration of chemical diversity through different decoration of a core structure, while still using compounds that are synthetically accessible and suitable for further medicinal chemistry work.[ 29 , 30 – 31 ]

Conclusion

In conclusion, the scaffold‐based and the reaction‐based approaches to virtual library creation, analyzed here using ≈1.5 million amide and sulfonamide products, generate distinct ensembles when considering strict identity. However, they exhibit strong chemical similarities and predicted synthetic accessibility. These findings further support the relevance of the scaffold‐based approach for generating focused virtual libraries during the optimization phases of drug discovery.

Experimental Section

Scaffold‐Based Construction Method

For each scaffold, a total of 1,000 combinations of R‐groups were generated as follows. Each substitution point in a scaffold is filled with R‐group according to the flagged type. The different types are: hydrogen, hydrophilic, halogen, sulfur, heterocyclic, aromatic, and bulky. The R‐groups are selected randomly, with a higher probability of selection determined by their frequency, as previously analyzed in in‐stock set of ≈10,000 compounds synthetized in the laboratory.[ 5 ]

Note that the maximum number of R‐group of each type is determined by different rules, depending on the number of substitution points in the scaffolds (Table 1 ).

The generated datasets were filtered using the Filter Software (OpenEye Scientific Software, ver. 5.1.0). Additional filters were also applied to remove unwanted substructures and to limit the number of certain functional groups within the molecule, such as sulfonamide. The filtering rules (fully detailed in Supporting Information 5), tailor‐made for our in‐house scaffolds but broad enough for Enamine, fall into two groups: pregeneration filters, which limit redundant R‐groups to boost diversity, and post‐generation filters, which remove compounds with undesirable properties (e.g., MW > 600, logP > 10) or excessive structural redundancy, such as multiple anhydrides or any epoxide.

Scripts

All descriptors were computed using RDKit package in python: molecular weight, number of rings with the Descriptors module; logP with the Crippen module.

Perception of Bemis and Murcko scaffolds relied on the module MurckoScaffold.

Similarity Comparison

The generated compounds were searched in REAL Space database (70 billion, 2024–09) using SpaceMACS software (ver. 1.1.0). Using SpaceMACS similarity mode, the closest analog of each query was retrieved based on the MCS‐similarity computed by the software. Structures were inputted as SMARTS string, generated by a python script using RDKit packages.

The in‐house collection of R‐groups was also searched using SpaceMACS with the R‐group mode, allowing to input fragments with one substitution point.

Synthetic Accessibility

The synthetic accessibility score (SAscore) was used to assess how easy a virtual compound is to synthesize. SAscore assigns a value from 1 (easy) to 10 (difficult), based on fragment frequency in the PubChem database‐frequent fragments lower the score, while rare or complex fragments increase it. The algorithm also penalizes molecular complexity, such as large size or unusual ring systems, but does not use machine learning. SAscore calculations were performed using RDKit in Python.
