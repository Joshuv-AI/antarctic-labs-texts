Age-Related Associations between Reactive-Balance Stepping Responses, Self-Induced Stepping, and Gray-Matter Volume
Authors: Inbal Paran, Moti Salti, Hadas Nachmani, Yuliya Berdichevsky, Ilan Shelef, Itshak Melzer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe distinction between reactive and proactive balance control mechanisms in terms of age-related structural neural correlates is still scarce. From a biomechanical perspective, reactive stepping is a rapid response to sudden loss of stability, whereas proactive self-induced stepping requires anticipatory postural adjustments and longer duration. This study aims to explore how cortical and subcortical gray-matter volume correlates with variables of reactive and proactive stepping responses among older and young adults; and whether these stepping responses can be distinguished from one another in terms of their structural neural correlates (i.e., cortical and subcortical gray-matter volume).MethodsTwenty-six older adults and nine young adults underwent structural MRI brain scanning. Self-induced stepping variables were derived from ground reaction force data, while kinematic parameters of reactive stepping, including step thresholds, were obtained using a three-dimensional motion capture system. Age-related differences in ground reaction force measures, stepping kinematics, and gray-matter volume in ten regions of interest were examined, followed by partial correlation analyses.ResultsAge-related impairments in reactive and proactive stepping performance were accompanied by significantly smaller gray-matter volume among older adults across all regions of interest (p ≤ 0.003), except for the brainstem (p = 0.026; post-correction significance level: p < 0.005). Partial correlation analyses including both older and young adults revealed significant associations whereby longer balance recovery durations and lower stepping thresholds were associated with lower gray-matter volume in prefrontal and cortical regions, and in the putamen and amygdala (r = −0.41 to −0.60, p ≤ 0.037; and r = 0.38 to 0.43, p ≤ 0.048, respectively). In self-induced stepping performance, longer preparation and step durations were significantly associated with lower precentral, cerebellar and amygdala gray-matter volume (r = −0.51 to −0.56, p ≤ 0.006).ConclusionAge-related differences in reactive stepping deficits were primarily associated with lower gray-matter volume in prefrontal, cortical, putamen, and amygdala regions, whereas self-induced stepping impairments were associated with precentral, cerebellar, and amygdala gray-matter volumes. These findings suggest distinct neural substrates underlying reactive versus self-initiated balance control. Further investigation needs to explore whether intervention programs in older adults may change gray-matter volume in these regions.

Introduction

Among older adults above 65 years of age in the USA, falls are the leading cause of injury such as head injuries, hip and wrist fractures and even death. Centers for Disease control and prevention report that more than one in four older adults fall each year [1]. Among the common causes of falls are unexpected external perturbations, such as a slip or a trip, or self-induced perturbations caused by self-induced voluntary movements [2].

To prevent balance loss resulting from self-induced perturbations our “proactive balance control” is activated. Due to the multi-link structure of the human body where one segment is connected to another, any voluntary movement by itself will impose a perturbation of posture. To compensate for this perturbation our voluntary movements are preceded by so-called anticipatory postural adjustments (APAs). These proactive “automatic” APAs are smoothly incorporated into our movement repertoire to ensure accurate and harmonious motion [3]. However, when balance is lost unexpectedly following an external balance perturbation, the perturbation triggers an automatic reflex-like reactive balance response that act to recover equilibrium. A reactive-balance response is generated very quickly, well ahead of anticipated and self-induced movements (<100 milliseconds vs. 150–200 milliseconds, respectively) [4]. Moreover, the APAs are absent in reactive-balance responses [3].

It has been suggested that the first phase of the reactive-balance response (<50 ms) involves mono- or oligo-synaptic spinal loops, or brainstem circuits and is considered automatic. The later phases (around 100 ms) are presumed to involve the cerebellar-cortical loop and the basal ganglia-cortical loop [5]. Recently, a study with older adults with mild cognitive impairment demonstrated that lower gray-matter volume in the brainstem and in the right cerebellum was associated with reduced stability during reactive stepping responses [6]. They also examined the associations with self-induced movement performance and found that reduced performance was associated with reduced gray-matter volume in the cerebellum, brainstem and thalamus. Associations with white-matter tracts were examined as well and findings indicated that reduced integrity in tracts that originate in the supplementary motor area (SMA), pre-SMA, and primary motor cortex were also associated with reduced performance of self-induced movements [6]. Another study found that APAs generation was associated with brain activation in the SMA and primary motor cortex (M1) [7].

Although the underlying neural correlates of balance control have been studied extensively [8, 9], less have focused on the distinction and direct comparison between proactive and reactive-balance control [6], and on the neural correlates of self-induced and reactive stepping responses’ properties [6, 7, 10]. Furthermore, previous studies have identified functional neural correlates of both self-induced [7] and reactive [10] stepping responses; however, the corresponding structural correlates remain largely unclear, particularly in the context of aging.

As we age, natural aging processes involving declines in sensory, muscular, visual, and vestibular systems lead to diminished balance control [11]. Also, central neural age-related changes are known to be associated with reduced balance performance [12]. Interestingly, these central neural age-related changes are more pronounced in prefrontal cortical brain regions, which exhibit greater gray-matter atrophy compared with subcortical deep brain structures [12, 13]. Nevertheless, there is a known age-related shift from lower level motor control to higher level cognitive motor control, which relies on the prefrontal, cortical and basal-ganglia systems [12].

Consequently, there are significant age-related differences in the performance of self-induced stepping [14] and reactive stepping responses [3, 15] which represent two distinct mechanisms of balance control. Older adults demonstrate longer self-induced step initiation duration, longer preparation, and swing durations [14]. As for reactive stepping, older adults respond with a reactive step at lower perturbation magnitudes (i.e., stepping thresholds) [15], use more steps to recover balance [3, 15], exhibit more foot collisions, and perform unsuccessful balance recovery maneuvers (i.e., fall into a harness system) [3, 15]. In contrast to these age-related differences, evidence from kinematic analysis of reactive stepping responses show that the first reactive step initiation duration is similar between older and young adults [16, 17].

The evidence regarding age-related associations between gray-matter volume changes and spatiotemporal parameters of proactive and reactive stepping responses is scarce. In the present study, we aimed to explore: (1) age-related differences in reactive and self-induced stepping responses parameters, by directly comparing young adults and older adults self-induced and reactive stepping performances. In the current investigation, we focused on lateral loss of balance. This focus is particularly relevant because lateral stepping differs biomechanically from forward or backward stepping and has been shown to constitute a more challenging balance recovery response [18]. Moreover, falls account for the majority of hip fracture-related deaths (83%) and hospitalizations or emergency department visits (88%) in older adults, with lateral falls frequently involved [1]; (2) age-related differences in gray-matter volume which will be examined and compared in specific regions of interest (ROIs) in young and older adults; and (3) the associations between gray-matter volume in chosen ROIs and the parameters of reactive stepping and self-induced stepping responses will be explored using partial correlation analysis.

We hypothesized that: (1) older adults will exhibit reduced performance in both reactive and self-induced stepping compared to young adults. Specifically, in reactive stepping performance, older adults will demonstrate lower step-threshold and longer time to fully recover balance from unexpected balance perturbations. In self-induced stepping, step duration will be prolonged in older adults; (2) based on previous reports on a lower gray-matter volume in prefrontal and cortical brain regions of older adults, we hypothesized that older adults will demonstrate lower gray-matter volume in all chosen ROIs, with more pronounced differences in prefrontal and cortical brain regions compared to subcortical areas; (3) reflecting the age-related shift toward higher level motor control [12], reduced balance recovery performance will be associated with lower gray-matter volume in prefrontal, sensorimotor, and basal-ganglia regions rather than subcortical structures as the brainstem. As for self-induced stepping, we hypothesized that a diminished stepping performance will be associated with lower gray-matter volume in areas governing motor planning, specifically the prefrontal, sensorimotor, and cerebellar regions.

Methods

The analyses in this study are based on, and focus exclusively on, the pretest procedures and measures of a prospective randomized controlled trial approved by the Helsinki Committee of Soroka University Medical Center in Beer-Sheva, Israel (ClinicalTrials.gov Registration number NCT04455607, ID Numbers: Sor 396-16 CTIL; First Posted: 02/07/2020). The aim of this randomized controlled trial is to examine whether, among older adults, perturbation-based balance training leads to improvements in proactive and reactive balance, and whether these functional changes are accompanied by alterations in gray-matter brain volume, including identification of the specific brain regions involved.

Participants

Thirty-seven older adults (age range 70–90 years old, average age: 75.4 ± 5.4) and 10 younger adults (age range 22–36 years old, average age: 27.90 ± 4.3) were recruited for this study. Recruitment of the younger adults was performed via advertisements and personal contacts. The inclusion criteria for the young group were healthy 20–40-year-old adults. Recruitment of older adults was performed by advertising in retirement homes and the Ben-Gurion University retirees committee, as well as through personal contacts and word of mouth. The inclusion criteria were age of at least 70 years, excluding those who reported any vestibular impairments, recurrent dizziness, any active neurological diagnosis (e.g., stroke, Parkinson’s disease, multiple sclerosis, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, severe peripheral neuropathies), blindness, symptomatic orthostatic hypotension, respiratory diseases, active cancer treatment, total hip or knee arthroplasty, or acute lower limb trauma in the past year. We also excluded older adults who used a walker. Finally, the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) was administered as an exclusion screening tool in the older adults group. Older adult participants were excluded if their MMSE score was <24 [19].

Experimental Setup

Participants performed a structural MRI brain scan, then completed the reactive-balance assessment and the proactive balance test. Reactive balance assessment was performed while standing on the BaMPer system, a mechatronic system that provides controlled unexpected surface translations aimed to trigger a reactive stepping response [20] (Fig. 1). No handrails were mounted on the system so that arm movements were unconstrained. A full-trunk safety harness was used, designed to allow free motion but prevent ground contact in case of a fall.

Reactive balance assessment setup note. a Setup before unexpected mediolateral balance perturbation. b Setup during unexpected mediolateral balance perturbation.

The perturbations protocol was based on a previous study by Paran et al. [18], which was designed to identify the perturbation magnitude that required a change in postural strategy, from fixed-BoS to a change in BoS strategy (i.e., step threshold) in four directions. Therefore, perturbation directions were presented in each of the six gradually increasing magnitudes, i.e., from low to high, in the following order: (1) backward surface translation, (2) lateral-left surface translation, (3) forward surface translation, and (4) lateral-right surface translation, for a total of 24 perturbed trials (i.e., 4 directions × 6 perturbation magnitudes). In the current analyses, however, we focused on mediolateral balance perturbations, as these were found to be more challenging than anterior-posterior perturbations [18].

The perturbation characteristics were based on platform acceleration (accel), velocity (vel), and displacement (displ) (see online suppl. Table 1). To increase the unpredictability, the intervals between the perturbations were 15–30 s. To maintain consistency and to increase the postural challenge, the participants stood with their feet together (toes and heels touching), hands at the sides of their body, and they were instructed to “react naturally to avoid falling.” The trial was stopped if a participant fell into the harness system or requested at any point to stop.

After completing the reactive-balance assessment on the BaMPer system, participants performed a proactive balance test while standing on a Kistler 9287 force platform (Kistler Instrument Corp., Winterthur, Switzerland). Proactive balance was examined using the self-induced voluntary step execution test [14]. Participants stood barefoot on a single force platform with their feet abducted at 10° and their heels separated mediolaterally by 6 cm as marked on the force platform. They were instructed to voluntarily step forward as quickly as possible following a somatosensory tap cue given manually by the examiner on the heel of the stepping foot [14]. The average step times of 6 trials were calculated. Figure 2 illustrates the examination setup of the step execution test. Generative AI-based image creation tools (i.e., chat GPT plus, version 5.2 and Google Gemini 3) were used to produce this illustrative image. The figure was created from textual prompts only to improve visual clarity and does not represent primary study data or affect the experimental results, analyses, or conclusions.

Descriptive and Kinematic Analyses of Reactive Stepping Responses

A 3D Vicon Motion Systems (Oxford, UK) with 16 infrared cameras operating at 120 Hz was employed to record the kinematic data for reactive stepping responses. The system’s 16 camera views were integrated into a 3D coordinate framework by the Vicon Nexus System software (version 2.5) using an internal direct linear transformation algorithm. Thirty-nine retroreflective markers (14 mm radius) were affixed to anatomical landmarks on a full-body suit to track a 12-segment kinematic model. The placement of these markers complied with the guidelines outlined by the Vicon Motion System [21, 22]. To monitor the initiation of surface translation motions, two additional reflective markers were positioned on the perturbation system. The analysis focused on approximately 2 s before perturbation to observe any pre-perturbation postural adjustments, and about 3 s after perturbation to assess reactive stepping responses. Data exported from the Vicon system were validated and analyzed offline using custom Matlab (MathWorks Inc.; Cambridge, MA, USA) code.

As spatiotemporal kinematic parameters of the reactive stepping responses have been shown to be affected by the magnitude of the perturbation [23] and given that older adults tend to initiate a reactive step at lower perturbation magnitudes compared to young adults [15], we sought to minimize magnitude-related confounding when examining age-related differences in the kinematic parameters. Therefore, spatiotemporal kinematic analyses focused on the first reactive stepping response elicited at an equivalent mediolateral perturbation magnitude, at which the majority of both young and older adult participants executed a reactive step (perturbation magnitude of 12-cm displacement).

Participants who did not perform a stepping response at this perturbation magnitude were excluded from the analyses (see details in the Results section). In addition, participants who performed a reactive leg abduction response were excluded from the analyses as well. A reactive leg abduction response was defined as an abduction movement of one leg while the participant remained supported on the contralateral leg, followed by a return of the abducted leg to the original narrow-base stance, without an actual change in the base of support (BoS). These reactive abduction responses resulted in negligible step length and prolonged step duration and were used almost exclusively by young adults. Because this response does not alter the BoS, it was not considered a recovery stepping response.

We first extracted the step threshold in the mediolateral perturbations during perturbed standing, defined as the minimum perturbation magnitude that consistently elicited at least one reactive step. The above parameter was found to be highly reliable (interobserver reliability ICC2, 1 = 0.978; p < 0.001) [15]. Then, the following spatiotemporal parameters were calculated for mediolateral reactive stepping responses: first step initiation duration (ms), first step duration (ms), first step length (cm), estimated margins of stability (cm) and the total balance recovery duration. Specifically, the first step initiation duration was measured from the initial deviation of the perturbation system marker (i.e., perturbation onset) to foot-lift off from the ground to initiate stepping. First step duration (ms) was measured as the time from perturbation onset until foot contact, completing the first reactive step. First step length was calculated as the Euclidean distance between ankle markers from foot lift-off to foot contact on the ground, completing the first reactive step. Estimated margins of stability (eMoS) were measured as the distance between the estimated center of mass and the BoS boundaries as defined by the feet at step initiation; larger eMoS indicated smaller CoM displacement at the initiation of the first reactive step. Finally, we also extracted the average of the total balance recovery duration (ms) which was defined as the duration from the lift-off of the first mediolateral reactive step to the foot contact of the last step, when multiple steps were performed.

Analysis of the Self-Induced Step Execution Test

Center of pressure (CoP) movement and ground reaction force data were collected from a Kistler 9287 force platform, sampled at a frequency of 100 Hz. The following temporal events were extracted from the self-induced step execution data using a code written in Matlab (Math Works Inc, Cambridge, MA, USA): (1) Step initiation duration; (2) Step duration (i.e., foot contact time); and (3) Preparation phase duration [14]. Higher values in each of these parameters represent reduced performance. The ICC values for intra-tester reliability for the older adults were good to excellent for these step parameters (0.62–0.88) [14].

Structural Brain Image Acquisition and Analyses

Structural brain images for morphometric analysis were acquired with a 3.0 T Philips Ingenia magnetic resonance imaging scanner (Philips Healthcare, The Netherlands) with a 32-channel head coil. A high-resolution T1-weighted image was taken (repetition time, 8.3 s; echo time, 3.8 ms; flip angle, 8; 170 slices; voxel resolution, 1 × 1 × 1 mm; matrix, 256 × 240). Images were examined by an experienced neuroradiologist (Prof. I.S., MD), and extracerebellar pathology was excluded.

Extraction of Gray-Matter Volume

ROIs gray-matter volume extraction was conducted using the standard procedures of the FreeSurfer image analysis suite (Version 7.2; https://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu) as described in detail in previous studies [24–26]. After standard preprocessing procedures, a manual quality check was carried out. The Desikan-Killiany atlas [26] was used for cortical ROI analysis. Subcortical segmentation and labeling were based on the volumetric segmentation atlas described by Fischl et al. [25]. We focused on ten cortical and subcortical ROIs that have been previously linked to postural abilities: the superior-frontal gyrus (including SMA), caudal and rostral middle frontal gyrus (including the premotor cortex and the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex [DLPFC]), paracentral gyrus (BA1, 2, 3, 4, and part of BA5), precentral gyrus (primary-motor cortex), postcentral gyrus (BA1,2,3; somatosensory area), amygdala (as part of the limbic system), putamen, cerebellum, and brainstem. Taki et al. [27] demonstrated that there are no significant differences in gray-matter volume between hemispheres in the above brain regions; therefore, we added the left and right volumes of brain regions that are bilateral (e.g., superior-frontal gyrus or putamen) to a single bilateral volume. FreeSurfer’s ROI analysis (i.e., cortical labeling and subcortical segmentation procedures) also provide the estimated total intracranial volume (eTIV) [28] which we used as a covariate to account for head size differences between participants.

Results

Participants

Of the 37 older adults that were eligible for participation, five performed the stepping tests but were not interested in performing the MRI scan. One participant was excluded from the analysis since the spatiotemporal parameters of the reactive stepping response were considered as outliers (±3SD above the mean). Another participant was excluded due to missing data of the voluntary stepping test. An additional four participants were unable to reach the perturbation magnitude required for the analysis (i.e., 12-cm displacement) and therefore could not complete the trial. Consequently, the final analysis included 26 older adults. Of the 10 young adults who were included in the study, one was excluded because at the perturbation magnitude used for the analysis, he used a reactive abduction response which is not considered as a reactive stepping response. Thus, 9 young adults were included in the study. The differences in demographic characteristics between the younger and older adults are described in online supplementary Table 2. Older adults were significantly older in age.

Age-Related Differences in Reactive Stepping and Self-Induced Stepping Performance

Age-related differences in kinematic parameters of reactive stepping performance are shown in Table 1. Older adults needed a significantly longer time to fully recover balance compared to young adults (1,116.6 ms ± 318.6 vs. 791.2 ms ± 203.2, p = 0.006), and had a significantly lower mediolateral step threshold (5.7 cm ± 2.5 vs. 8.3 cm ± 2.0, p = 0.006). The differences between older and young adults in self-induced stepping performance are shown in Table 2. Significantly longer self-induced step initiation and step duration were found among older adults compared with young adults (226.7 ms ± 51.8 vs. 183.2 ms ± 36.03, p = 0.024 and 1,011.7 ms ± 173.5 vs. 870.2 ms ± 132.7, p = 0.026; respectively). In addition, older adults tended to have a longer self-induced preparation phase duration compared to young adults (455.3 ms ± 80.7 vs. 392.8 ms ± 74.3, p = 0.07).

In the reactive step kinematic parameters, longer total balance recovery duration was moderately correlated with post-central gyri (r = −0.50, p = 0.007), and the amygdala (r = −0.6, p = 0.001). Lower yet significant correlations were found between longer total balance recovery duration and lower gray-matter volume in superior-frontal gyrus (r = −0.48, p = 0.011), precentral (r = −0.46, p = 0.018), paracentral (r = −0.44, p = 0.023), and the putamen (r = −0.41, p = 0.037). In addition, lower step threshold was correlated with lower gray-matter volume in the in the superior frontal (r = 0.43, p = 0.027) and paracentral gyri (r = 0.41, p = 0.038), and in the putamen (r = 0.41, p = 0.036). Low yet significant correlations were found between lower step-threshold and lower gray-matter volume in the postcentral gyrus (r = 0.38, p = 0.058), and in the amygdala (r = 0.39, p = 0.048). Other kinematic parameters of balance recovery, however, showed no correlations with any of the cortical and subcortical ROIs.

Regarding self-induced step parameters, Figure 3 shows that longer self-induced step duration was moderately associated with lower gray-matter volume in the precentral gyrus (r = −0.56, p = 0.002), the amygdala (r = −0.55, p = 0.003), the putamen (r = −0.53, p = 0.004), and the cerebellum (r = −0.51, p = 0.006). Lower yet significant associations were found in the superior-frontal gyrus (r = −0.49, p = 0.009) and postcentral gyrus (r = −0.42, p = 0.031). Longer preparation phase duration was found to be moderately associated with lower gray-matter volume in the cerebellum (r = −0.56, p = 0.002), the amygdala (r = −0.52, p = 0.005) and the precentral gyrus (r = −0.52, p = 0.006). In addition, low negative associations were found between longer preparation phase duration and lower gray-matter volume in the putamen (r = −0.45, p = 0.022), the superior-frontal gyrus (r = −0.43, p = 0.029) and the postcentral gyrus (r = −0.41, p = 0.037). Finally, low negative associations were found between longer self-induced step initiation duration and lower gray-matter volume in the precentral (r = −0.41, p = 0.038) and postcentral gyri (r = −0.39, p = 0.047).

The subsample analysis of young adults showed some moderate-to-high associations (above |0.5|), yet none were statistically significant. The correlations data are reported in detail in online supplementary Table 3. In the subsample of older adults, longer total balance recovery duration showed a trend toward being associated with lower amygdala gray-matter volume (r = −0.51, p = 0.071). In the self-induced stepping parameters, longer preparation phase duration was moderately associated with lower gray-matter volume in the cerebellum (r = −0.62, p = 0.017) and longer step duration showed a trend toward being associated with lower cerebellar gray-matter volume (r = −0.53, p = 0.066). All other correlations were not statistically significant and are reported in detail in online supplementary Table 4.

Discussion

This study applied an ecologically valid assessment of balance recovery from an unexpected loss of balance, along with an evaluation of self-induced stepping performance. Age-related differences were examined and associations between stepping parameters and gray-matter volume in cortical and subcortical brain regions were explored.

Age-Related Differences in Reactive Stepping and Self-Induced Stepping Performance

In line with previous findings, and with our first hypothesis, age-related declines in balance control mechanisms were confirmed as older adults demonstrated longer self-induced step initiation and longer step duration [14]. Also as hypothesized, older adults showed longer balance recovery times, and lower mediolateral step thresholds [3, 15]. In the present cohort, no age-related differences were found in the first recovery step initiation duration, and in contrast to our hypothesis, also in first step duration, first step length, and eMoS. The similar first step initiation duration possibly reflects a more automatic phase of the response. Past investigations have produced conflicting results regarding recovery step initiation duration. Maki and McIlroy [3] observed small delays (40 milliseconds) in step initiation among older adults, while Luchies et al. [17] reported earlier foot lift-off (100 milliseconds) in older participants. This discrepancy may be due to the different instructions given to the participants: “try not to step” versus “step as quickly as possible.” In our study, we adopted unconstrained instructions, specifically, “react naturally to avoid falling,” to enhance the ecological validity of real-life balance loss situations, possibly better reflecting reflex-like automatic responses.

Age-Related Differences in Gray-Matter Volume

Our investigation highlighted age-related differences in gray-matter volume across various brain regions (Table 3), including the frontal cortex, putamen, and amygdala, with more modest reductions observed in the cerebellum and brainstem. These findings generally agree with our second hypothesis and also with previous reports [13, 27], however, regarding the cerebellum, previous studies have shown conflicting results, indicating either a substantial reduction [33] or no significant reduction [27].

Associations between Reactive Stepping Parameters, Self-Induced Stepping Performance and Gray-Matter Volume

Our findings regarding age-related structural differences in gray-matter volume were strongly correlated with impaired performance in reactive stepping responses and self-induced stepping and are in partial agreement with our third hypothesis. Longer total balance recovery duration was linked to lower gray-matter volume in prefrontal brain regions, the putamen, and the amygdala, underscoring the involvement of higher level motor control processes including planning and somatosensory processing [34]. Specifically, the prominent association between longer balance recovery duration with lower gray-matter volume in the postcentral gyrus reflects the importance of providing and processing sensory and proprioceptive feedback on foot location during movement and balance recovery. Further, in agreement with Jacobs and Horak [5] basal-ganglia-cortical loop involvement is indicated as the putamen outputs to the primary-motor cortex, SMA, and premotor cortex [35].

Consistently, we found age-related associations between lower mediolateral step threshold and lower gray matter in the putamen and SMA (i.e., in the superior-frontal gyrus), which emphasize the critical role of these regions in automating balance recovery responses execution. More specifically, the putamen is known to take part in motor automatic processing and in developing automatic motor skills as necessary for changing balance recovery strategy from feet-in-place to change-in-BoS during an abrupt loss of balance [36]. Additionally, this association manifests the role of the basal ganglia as a mediator between the cerebral cortex and the brainstem, where postural synergies are stored [5]. Lower mediolateral stepping threshold showed a trend toward an association with lower gray-matter volume in the brainstem (shown in Fig. 3).

Interestingly, a key region in the proactive balance mechanism was the cerebellum, showing prominent associations with prolonged self-induced step duration and prolonged preparation phase duration (i.e., APAs phase). These associations, observed in the whole sample analysis, were even stronger in the subgroup analysis of older adults, affirming the aging effect on the associations between the structural changes in the cerebellum and diminished proactive balance function. Prolonged APAs duration and lower gray-matter volume in the cerebellum have been previously demonstrated in cerebellar syndromes as well [37]. Our findings also highlight the network connecting the cerebellum with the SMA, and the putamen, which has been suggested to be active when performing a familiar motor task (such as stepping), as it relies on motor-memory based learning strategies [38].

Finally, the moderate associations between gray-matter reductions in the amygdala and impaired reactive and self-induced stepping performance, point on the role of emotional processing in balance control and its connection to higher cortical brain regions. The amygdala is well known as a crucial brain region in processing threatening stimuli [39]. Thus, the age-related associations with the amygdala may reflect the effect of threatening conditions on postural performance, especially during balance recovery from unexpected loss of balance. Moreover, it has been previously suggested that heightened postural threat such as during unexpected balance perturbations may increase attentional demands, potentially interfering with automatic postural mechanisms [40].

Limitations and Conclusion

The results of this exploratory study are novel and should be treated in caution and viewed in consideration of its limitations. First, the results are based on a fairly small sample, in which the older adult participants were drawn from an independent community-dwelling older adult population with moderate-to-high functional levels (i.e., capable of independent walking). This restricts the generalizability of our conclusions to more frail older populations.

Second, the small sample size made detecting associations between gray-matter volume and parameters of balance-reactive stepping responses challenging. Nevertheless, this study aimed to explore specific ROIs encompassing both cortical and subcortical brain areas to investigate the interactions between higher and lower level balance control and how these interactions change with age. Additionally, a broad set of kinematic variables was intentionally included to improve analytical resolution and examine phase-specific associations in reactive and self-induced stepping responses. By doing so, we sought to lay the foundation for future research with larger and more diverse cohorts, enabling the formulation of more precise hypotheses in subsequent studies.

Third, the spatiotemporal kinematic analyses focused on the first reactive step elicited at an equivalent mediolateral perturbation magnitude (12 cm) common to most young and older adult participants. This meant that for some participants, usually young adults, it represented an easy postural challenge (stepping threshold), while for others it was the highest perturbation achieved, usually older adults, possibly affecting fear-of-falling and alertness.

We also note that although participants were screened for exercise habits and everyday physical activity, we did not obtain a detailed quantification of these parameters and they were not included in our analysis. Therefore, we cannot exclude residual confounding related to these factors.

In conclusion, age-related structural differences in gray-matter volume were associated with impaired reactive and proactive balance control. Our findings suggest that reactive stepping relies on age-related higher level motor control involving the basal-ganglia-cortical loop. In this context, lower gray-matter volume prefrontal, cortical, and basal-ganglia regions were associated with diminished balance recovery performance, and reduced automaticity in balance recovery. Self-induced anticipatory stepping performance seems to rely more on cerebellar-cortical loops and interconnections with the putamen. Also, associations between reduced postural performance and lower amygdala volume indicate that emotional processing, potentially related to postural threat, may adversely affect balance. Overall, these results highlight the structural neural correlates underlying age-related declines in reactive and proactive stepping performance, providing insight into the neural mechanisms of balance control in older adults. Further investigation needs to explore whether intervention programs may affect gray-matter volume in these regions.
