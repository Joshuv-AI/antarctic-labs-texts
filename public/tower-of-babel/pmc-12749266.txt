Behaviour of ewes and lambs in response to human emotional odours
Authors: Izïa Larrigaldie, Fabrice Damon, Dylan Brimaud, Léa Lansade, Benoist Schaal, Alexandra Destrez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigated whether sheep (Ovis aries) discriminate the body odour of unfamiliar humans subjected to a contrastive emotion-inducing procedure. Axillary secretions were collected from 38 humans after they watched fearful or joyful films. The behavioural responses of adult ewes (n = 35) and 6-day-old lambs (n = 22) to these odours were assessed using a habituation-dishabituation paradigm validated in this species. Both ewes and lambs exhibited habituation (reduced tactile-olfactory investigation), but no significant dishabituation, limiting strong conclusions about odour discrimination. However, ewes displayed ear postures suggestive of differential emotional reactivity to assumed valence, but these effects were subtle and potentially influenced by test conditions. The lambs exposed to fear odour (FO), relative to those exposed to joy odour (JO), showed increased escape attempts and oral grasping of the odour pad, a conflicting pattern of responses that complicates interpreting FO as aversive. In sum, these results suggest that lambs may react to FO as a distinct stimulus, but they may not clearly associate it with negative valence. Ewes showed behavioural differences in response to odours, yet the absence of clear dishabituation makes it difficult to confirm discrimination ability. Further research including physiological measures is needed to clarify ovine perception of human emotional odours.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-29186-w.

Introduction

By encoding a wide range of potential meanings in odour cues and signals from their bodily secretions and excretions, animals do control their individual behaviour and modulate interactions with their conspecifics1,2. However, these chemical signals do not only contribute to communication between members of a same species, but also to interactions among members of different species, promoting adaptive responses in various polyspecific contexts (e.g., inter-species mutualisms, wariness of predators, selection of preys, avoidance from parasites3–6. Acquired aptitudes to communicate with heterospecifics are particularly obvious in domesticated animals, in which heterospecific partners comprise humans as well as other domesticated or wild species. Through millennia of cultural history, human domesticators have indeed exploited the sensory, affective and cognitive propensities of several species in shaping their socialization in favour of humancentric cooperation (e.g7,8.).

During this long domestication process, generations of animals were exposed to the layered sensory cues underlying human identity (e.g., individuality, sex, age, health, genetic relatedness, familiarity). Accordingly, they may have acquired ways to detect their keepers’ identity and interpret his/her behaviour, emotional states and possibly physiological stages9. Dogs, cats and horses can indeed perceive human emotions conveyed in human vocal cues (dogs10–13; cats14; horses15–17, as well as in visual cues including behaviour patterns and facial expressions (dogs12,18; cats19; horses20–22. Beyond these most studied visual and auditory informative channels, humans also transmit chemical cues, which can carry a wealth of information perceivable to humans themselves23–33, but also to their accompanying animal receivers who are often super-smellers.

Growing empirical evidence shows indeed that emotional cues transmitted via human body odours can influence the behaviour of multiple domesticated species. This is true for dogs, which can detect and discriminate human odours of fear and joy34,35. Similarly, human fear odour induces greater stress responses in cats, as compared to mere physical activity-related odours or neutral stimuli36. Horses do also react differentially to emotionally-distinct human odours, as attested by both physiological37 and behavioural variables38,39. Although research on odour communication of emotions between humans and animals has so far mostly focused on “man’s best friends” – i.e., horses, dogs and cats12,34–39, other domestic or laboratory species should have evolved homologous cognitive abilities9. Being closely and regularly exposed to humans caretakers40–42, such animals are expected to attend to the whole range of their sensory features, including body odours and related fluctuations caused by mood or emotion changes. Lab mice were indeed found to strongly react to the odour of human males43, and mice and cows to distinguish human stress odours from odours collected in a neutral emotional context44. Even wild animals do selectively use odour cues to categorise humans depending on whether they are preys45–47. This was clearly shown in wild African elephants48. Taken together, the aforementioned findings highlight the keen sensitivity of animals to human olfactory cues and the possibility of potential human-to-animal emotional contagion based on odours.

Given the importance of chemosensation in the regulation of ovine social behaviours49,50, and given their stress sensitivity and affective reactivity, domestic sheep are an interesting model for studying animal emotions in the context of interactions with humans51–53. In addition, their gregarious way of life makes them a good model to assess emotional contagion in a collective of individuals. Their physical proximity with humans for the past 10,000 years54 through breeding, feeding, health care, and protection55 may engage ovines’ acute sensitivity and reactivity to human odour cues. Domestication should indeed exacerbate the chances to reveal whether sheep are able to detect human emotion-related body odours and discriminate them from such odours resulting from emotionally “neutral” contexts. An exploratory study56 suggested indeed that 6-month-old sheep react to the scent of unfamiliar humans, although without clear distinction of the assumed valence of the odours. Building upon these findings, the present study first aimed to examine whether adult sheep, like adult horses and dogs34,35,37–39, can distinguish the valence of body odours sampled in humans exposed to fear- or joy-inducing conditions. A habituation-dishabituation (H-D) paradigm57,58 was used to further confirm whether (i) sheep habituate to repeatedly presented human fear or joy odours, and (ii) dishabituate when exposed to one of these odours after being habituated to the other, leading to the diagnostic of olfactory discrimination. In addition, behaviour items known to express ovine affective states were coded to evaluate the animals’ ability to sense hedonic polarity in the body odour of humans subjected to negative or positive emotions. In line with previous research on ovines55 and other domestic species23–33, we expect adult sheep will discriminate (based on the H-D test) and differentiate the emotional content of human odours obtained after contrastive emotion-induction contexts.

As dogs do variably respond to human emotional odours as a function of age59, we secondly studied whether lambs could also discriminate fear- and joy-related human odours. The direction of outcomes with lambs is less confident than with adult sheep as they typically have little direct exposure with humans and may thus have had limited olfactory awareness of them. However, in the present study, lambs were raised artificially in close physical contact with humans as they were intensively tutored to suckle an artificial nipple in their first days of life, a period when they are specially receptive to social stimuli from conspecifics60 as well as from heterospecifics61,62. As found in early olfactory learning in other mammalian infants63–66, such artificially-raised lambs might thus (i) be exposed to human odour cues in highly reinforcing conditions and (ii) be reactive to them. The present study explores this possibility.

Participants, materials and methods

Ethics statement

The present study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the French Institute for medical research and Health for the elicitation of emotions in humans and sampling of related body odours (Inserm authorization #24–1103). All experiments were performed in accordance with these guidelines and regulations and consent was obtained from all participants.

For the testing of sheep, the present study was approved by the Dijon Animal Experimentation Ethics Committee (CEEA authorization #105). All experiments were performed in accordance with these guidelines and regulations, and we complied with the ARRIVE guidelines. The testing of sheep took place in two waves (13/11 to 21/11/2023 and 21/03 to 29/03/2024) in a commercial farm located in northern Burgundy (Francheville, France). The experiment was conducted with the permission of the farm owner where the animals were housed, with his agreement to carry out olfactory behavioral tests without invasive measures. The study was conducted in the background of the farmer’s management practices concerning breeding, lambing and the artificial rearing of lambs. French farmers are strictly enforced to follow the rules of the Rural Code that regulate animal welfare (Code rural et de la pêche maritime, chapter IV, Articles L214–1 to L214–23). The experimenters were thus under the constant supervision of the farmer and his associates, ensuring that animal handling was never invasive.

Emotional odour collection in human donors

The sampling of emotional odours was run in two waves, one destined to the testing of ewes, the other to the tests with lambs.

Odour stimuli for testing adult ewes

The odour stimuli used with ewes were sampled from participants following procedures adopted from conceptually similar investigations24,39,67. Due to donors’ schedule constraints, we were unable to collect axillary odours from all participants within a single session of emotion-inducing films. Participants attended separate groups of 3–5 individuals in morning sessions lasting 3 h (9:00–12:00). In this way, 22 participants (16 women, 6 men; Mage= 35.8 years, SDage = 17.2 years, range: 20–65 years) donated the odours from both of their axillae. Consenting participants were required to donate axillary odours twice on a same day in the following conditions of sampling. First, the day before and during axillary sweat sampling, they were instructed (i) not to consume foods known to influence body odour (i.e., garlic, onion, leek, cabbage68,69, (ii) not to smoke, (iii) not to use scented hygiene products (deodorant, perfume, soap), (iv) to shower only with clear water, and (v) to wear a clean, freshly laundered T-shirt washed with their own washing powder (on the day of odour sampling). Second, participants were supplied with two cotton pads (8 × 9.5 cm, 100% cotton, Tetra Médical, Annonay, France) to be secured under each armpit with strips of scentless tape (Micropore, 3 M, St Paul, USA). Third, on the day of sampling, the participants attended a 28-min film which was assumed to induce an emotional state of happiness assumedly correlated with the emission of an axillary “joy odour” (JO). This film consisted of excerpts adapted from de Groot and his colleagues29: the song scene “The Bare Necessities” (4 min 41 s) from The Jungle Book, the opera scene from Intouchables (2 min 51 s), the short film Validation by Kurt Kuenne (14 min 46 s)29,38 and Sarah Hughes’ performance and victory at the 2002 Olympics (6 min 1 s). Following this film watching, participants were given a 10-minute break to firstly remove the pads and secondly rinse each of their armpits with clear water. After this break, the participants applied themselves fresh cotton pads in their armpits before attending another 29-min film which was assumed to induce a state of horror or fear, translated into an axillary “fear odour” (FO). This film sequence consisted in 5 excerpts used in a previous study70; films f3, f4, f10, f11, f13). The video clips used as stimuli were short films (3–11 min in duration) representing different subgenres of horror cinema, namely found footage, supernatural and monster-based narratives. Both emotion-inducing film sessions were always in the same order (joy-fear) and were run over three morning hours (9:00–12:00).

After each film session, the cotton pads were removed by the donors themselves, put in a supplied plastic bag and immediately carried on ice to the lab to be frozen (−20 °C).

The human odour donors self-rated their perceived levels of stress and joy after each film session using 10-point Likert scales36,38,56,71–73 in the form of the following questions: “What was your stress/joy level during this session?”; the lowest and highest possible stress/joy score was 1 and 10, respectively.

The odour donors also filled-in a questionnaire assessing their compliance with the dietary and hygiene instructions. The odour samples were discarded in case of a failure to comply with those instructions or if the difference of the self-perceived stress between the emotionally joyful and negative events was below 2 points. As previous studies28,29,38 did not specify participant selection based on Likert responses, we applied a 2-point threshold to ensure observable differences in self-reported emotion while maintaining a sufficient number of participants. Due to the small number of volunteers among all those surveyed (22 vs. 202 for this session), we were unable to apply a higher selection criterion. Following the application of above-mentioned exclusion criteria, 3 female and 2 male donors were dropped from the study. At the end, the odour samples from 17 participants were retained (13 women, 4 men; Mage= 35.1, SDage = 16.4 years, range: 20–65 years). The self-reported emotional scores differences of the donors for each film were: 5.9 ± 1.2 for the joy-inducing film (mean difference between joy and stress scores) and 5.5 ± 2.3 for the stress-inducing film (mean difference between stress and joy scores).

The cotton pads from several donors in the same emotion condition were pooled to attenuate interindividual variability in axillary odours29,34,37,72,74,75. To that aim, each donor’s pad was cut into four pieces (4 × 4.75 cm) to be pooled with those of two other individuals of the same sex. The individual samples were mixed in a controlled manner, ensuring that the same individuals contributed to both the FO and JO pools presented to the animal. Accordingly, 37 axillary odour pools from both axillae76, including 5 pools from male participants, were created and stored at −20 °C until testing, which happened within a month (a period shorter than the one during which frozen axillary samples were reported to remain stable after sampling)77.

Odour stimuli for testing lambs

Human odours were collected from additional donors and stored using a procedure similar to that used for ewes, with a few variations. Here, we were able to collect axillary odours of all participants within a single film session (8:00–11:00 am). Thus, the odour stimuli used with lambs were sampled from 16 participants (12 women, 4 men; Mage= 23.3 years, SDage = 2.9 years, range: 21–34 years), with one participant reporting being a smoker but stating he had stopped smoking 24 h prior to sample collection, in accordance with the instructions.

Following the application of above-mentioned exclusion criteria, 4 female and 2 male donors among 16 were dropped from the study, due to their failure to comply with dietary instructions. In the end, 10 participants were retained (8 women, 2 men; Mage= 23.8, SDage = 3.64 years, range: 21–34 years). Due to the small number of volunteers among all those surveyed (26 vs. 148 for this session), we were unable to apply a higher selection criterion Each donor’s cotton pads were also cut into four pieces (4 × 4.75 cm) to be pooled with those of three other individuals of the same sex and emotion condition, ensuring that the same individuals contributed to both the FO and JO pools presented to the animals. Accordingly, 22 axillary odour pools from both axillae76 were created and stored at −20 °C until testing, which happened within a month. The totality of pools was used for this study, including 1 pool from male participants and 21 from female participants.

Animals and housing

The ewes [Suffolk (n = 150) and Romane breeds (n = 460)] were typically raised on pasture. One month before lambing, they were housed in a closed barn under natural light and with a concrete floor lined with straw. Following delivery, ewes and lambs were kept separately in lambing pens (1.70 × 1.70 m) for 24 h to facilitate the lambs’ intake of colostrum. After this initial period, all lambs were weighed, ear-tagged, and had their umbilical cord disinfected. They were then directed toward either natural or artificial rearing.

Due to risk of insufficient milk yield in dams bearing more than two lambs, surnumerary (i.e., > 2) lambs were removed from their mother, kept in small groups in separate pens to be fed artificially and followed-up for health and growth. By the end of the lambing period, 62 lambs were assigned artificial rearing. They were housed in two open-space nurseries (16 m2) within the barn, equipped with an artificial milk dispenser (EOO 32 heated mixer, Förster-Technik GmbH, Engen, Germany) delivering milk replacer ad libitum. The feeder automatically diluted the milk powder (Agnodour Tradition+, Bonilait, Chasseneuil-du-Poitou, France; gross composition: 22% proteins, 23% lipids, 0.05% cellulose, and 8.0% cinders) at 42 °C, delivering the resulting liquid to the lambs at 38 °C through three rubber nipples (diameter: 0.8 cm, length: 5 cm; between-nipple distance: 18 cm). Lambs also had unrestricted access to water and hay, provided through a feeder device, as well as to clay, supplied in a dedicated pan.

The experimenters involved in both experiments refrained from smoking prior to the test or wearing any scent. They wore the same standardized green overall over their clothes, as well as violet nitrile gloves (Kimtech), from the beginning to the end of the tests. During the various trials in the test pens, the behaviour of the ewes and the lambs was recorded using silent cameras (GoPro Hero8 Black).

Testing adult Ewes (Experiment 1)

Odour stimuli

Before the behavioural test, the pools of axillary odour were thawed at room temperature for 30 min in airtight bags. They were then affixed to the end of a plastic stick (lengths: 42.3 cm) according (Fig. 1a) to a protocol already validated in a previous study78. In order to avoid mixing odours, these stimuli sticks were dedicated to carry only one odour stimulus (JO or FO) during the study. The odour stimuli were changed between each animal, and the sticks were cleaned with water every day.

Habituation -dishabituation test

The HD test was run on postparturient ewes selected randomly 24 h after lambing. They were then installed in lambing pens with visual, auditory, and olfactory contact with conspecifics (Fig. 1d). The ewes were first familiarized to one experimenter presenting a stick for 3 min. The experimenter presenting the odour stimuli, blind to the odour condition, was the same throughout the experiment. During the tests themselves, the odour-presenting experimenter remained outside the test pen, kneeling to display the odour stimuli through the slatted barriers of the lambing pen.

Thirty-seven ewes were involved (Suffolk; Mage = 2.8, SDage = 1.5 years, range: 1–8 years). The ability of ewes to discriminate between JO and FO was assessed using the H-D56,79,80. During the habituation phase, ewes were presented with an odour (habituation stimulus, O1) in three 30-sec trials (O1.1, O1.2, and O1.3), with 10-s inter-trial intervals. Then, in the dishabituation phase, a different odour (dishabituation stimulus O2) was presented during 30 s. A decrease in behavioural responsiveness was expected during the repetitive habituation phase, whereas an increase of responsiveness was expected to the dishabituation stimulus79,80. This procedure has previously been validated in ewes, where it successfully detected habituation-dishabituation pattern between odours of opposite valence (e.g., dog faeces vs. wool from conspecifics78;. To meet potential fatigue in recently-parturient ewes and in accordance with an earlier study78, habituation stimuli were repeated 3 times (instead of 4 times in lambs). For each individual ewe, the H-D test lasted approximately 2,5 min. Ewes exhibiting signs of excessive stress (continuous high-pitched bleating, jumping, escape attempts) were removed from the study (n = 2). After this screening, 35 ewes were involved in the testing phase. Finally, odour presentation order was counterbalanced across ewes (Joy-Fear order (i.e. JO in habituation vs. FO in dishabituation), n = 15; Fear-Joy order: n = 20).

Dependent variables

Two independent coders who were blind to the odour condition analysed the video-recorded tests frame-by-frame on a computer using the BORIS software81, one of them analysing the ewes’ general behaviours, while the other assessed their ear positions. Behavioural analyses proceeded from the most general reactions to the most specific responses toward the stimuli. The ewes’ attention and attraction to the stimulus were first assessed based on visuo-olfactory exploration, muzzle contact with, and oral grasping of, the stick and pad (see Table 1 for definitions). Ewes were tested in presence of their lambs, thus low-pitched bleats (with closed mouth) and tactile and olfactory interactions (sniffing) with lambs were coded to assess whether human SO and JO did differentially affect ewe-lamb interactions (see Table 1). At last, the ewes’ ear positions were coded as indicators of their emotional state82–84 (Table 2). Since forward, backward, and asymmetrical ear positions were shown to indicate a negative affective state in sheep, we created a “Negative ear position” variable by summing their occurrences/duration56. All behavioural items were coded as occurrences and durations during the four 30-sec odour presentation trials.

Statistical analyses

As noted above, the H-D test consisted of 1/a habituation phase consisting in the repetition of the same stimulus 3 times, from trial O1.1 (1st habituation trial) to trial O1.3 (3th trial), and 2/a dishabituation phase induced with trial O2 (dishabituation trial). Following previous studies96,97, the data were analyzed separately for both phases of the test. Specifically, data from trial O1.1 were compared with those of trial O1.3, to assess whether a repeated odour stimulus results in decreased responsiveness; then, data from trials O1.3 were compared to those of trial O2 to evaluate the rebound of interest to a novel stimulus, indicating discrimination between the habituated and novel stimuli.

No outliers were detected for the H-D test. Both the occurrences and durations of behavioural observations were analysed using a mixed model with restricted maximum likelihood estimation (REML), incorporating animals as random factor, and stimulus order in the H-D test (JO-FO vs. FO-JO) and odours (JO vs. FO) as fixed factors for the analyses of the habituation and dishabituation responses, with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. A potential age effect was explored in adding ewes’ age in the model as a continuous covariate. Given the highly unbalanced ratio of human male/female odour pools, we did not statistically evaluate the effect of donor sex in order to preserve the statistical power of our model. Some variables showed deviations from normality in model residuals. We nevertheless used a linear mixed model to analyse the data as recommended by Knief and Forstmeieir98, as (i) linear mixed model are fairly robust to non-normality, (ii) deviations from normality usually do not bias regression coefficients, (iii) and non-normality of residuals does not impair hypothesis testing (type I error rate is kept as the desired low rate). All analyses were made on Jamovi (α = 0.05, Jamovi project, 2022, version 2.3; R Core Team, 2021, version 4.1, including the GAMLj module). All data in the Results section are presented as mean ± SE (Standard Error).

Testing lambs (Experiment 2)

Odour stimuli

As for the ewes above, before the behavioural test, the pools of human odours were thawed at room temperature for 30 min. They were then affixed on a stick used throughout the HD test to present only one type of odour stimulus (JO or FO) to avoid odour mixing. Odour stimuli were changed between each animal, and the sticks were washed with water.

Habituation -dishabituation (H-D) test

Prior to testing, all lambs were familiarized with the test pens, stimulus sticks and experimenters. When they were 2 days old, 24 lambs were individually introduced into the test pen and positioned on the lap of the experimenter (Fig. 1c), gently constrained with one arm/hand and stroked with the other hand for 7 min99,100. The stroking was performed at a slow pace, from the top of the head between the ears down to the lower back of the lamb, at the level of the rump. This procedure was repeated once per day until the test, including on the day of testing when the lambs were 6 days old, for a total of five sessions. When being tested for the first time, all lambs had been familiarized to the experimenter for a total of 35 min (for a similar procedure see78. Two experimenters conducted the testing. Experimenter 1 prepared the odour stimuli, affixed them on the sticks, brought the lambs, and operated the camera, while Experimenter 2 received the lamb onto her lap and presented the odour sticks to it (Fig. 1c). Experimenter 2, the same throughout the experiment, was thus blind to the odour condition. During testing, Experimenter 1 remained silent at 3 m from the test pen. Between the tests, urine or faeces-contaminated straw was removed as necessary, and the pen re-groomed with fresh straw. Sticks were cleaned with water between each lamb.

The study included 24 6-days-old lambs (of Romane breed; mean age Mage = 6, SDage = 0 days; 13 females), males and females being housed together. They were tested individually, separated from the flock by slated barriers to minimize isolation stress, while allowing potential contact with peers through visual, auditory, tactile, and olfactory stimuli. The test pen was set up in the animals’ familiar enclosures (see “Test H-D pen” Fig. 1b). Lambs exhibiting signs of excessive stress (continuous high-pitched bleating, jumping, escape attempts) were removed from the study (n = 2). After this screening, 22 lambs (11 females) were included in the testing phase.

The ability of lambs to discriminate between JO and FO was assessed applying the H-D test described above, with an additional habituation trial. During habituation, the lambs were thus exposed to a given odour (habituation stimulus, O1) for four 30-sec trials (O1.1, O1.2, O1.3 and O1.4), with 10-sec inter-trial intervals. The dishabituation trial consisted in exposing lambs to a different odour (dishabituation stimulus O2) presented during 30 s. As in adult ewes, lambs were expected to reduce their behavioural odour responsiveness during the habituation phase, and to increase it in response to the dishabituation stimulus. This expectation was further supported by our previous study, in which the same protocol successfully detected behavioural differences between dog faeces and conspecific wool odours in lambs78. The H-D test lasted approximately 5 min. The odour presentation order was counterbalanced across lambs (Joy-Fear order; n = 11; 5 females, 6 males; Fear-Joy order; n = 11; 6 females, 5 males).

Dependent variables

The behaviour of the lambs was recorded on video for subsequent frame-by-frame analyses using the BORIS software. Four independent coders who were blind to the odour condition analysed these video-records. The Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) was calculated to assess inter-rater reliability for all behavioural variables, based on their ratings on 2 videos. The 4 coders coded these two videos until they achieved a suitable ICC score: 5 training sessions in all with an ICC of 0.81 (95% CI [0.68–0.90], F(27,54) = 13.9, p <.001) indicates good inter-rater reliability, suggesting that the coders consistently evaluated the same behavioural events with minimal variability between them.

As for the ewes, different behaviour patterns were coded, following criteria outlined in previous studies (Table 3). These patterns ranged from the most general reactions to the most specific responses toward the target odour and stick. We also measured a behaviour item that was displayed only by lambs, i.e. the protrusion of the tongue, characterized by a partial or complete lingual extra-oral extension, with antero-posterior (licking-like) and sometimes lateral movements, the tip of the tongue contacting the upper lip but without elevation of it or of the nostril region (such as in the flehmen motion) (see Supplementary Fig. S1). Such tongue protrusions were not associated with head extension toward the stimuli. They occurred either before or after sniffing/touching a pad, but never during.

Finally, several reactions indicative of the animals’ emotional state were timed to assess their relative attraction or aversion to FO or JO, including high-pitched vocalizations, jolts, urination, and defecation (positions of ear pinnae being biased by the experimenter holding the lambs, they were was not recorded). All these behavioural items were quantified as occurrences and durations during the five 30-sec odour trials composing a H-D test.

Statistical analyses

As explained above for ewes, the data were analyzed separately for both phases of the H-D test. Habituation was assessed in comparing data from trials O1.1 and O1.4, while dishabituation was assessed in comparing data from trials O1.4 and O2. Again, no outliers were detected for the H-D test. Both the occurrences and durations of behavioural observations were analysed using a mixed model with restricted maximum likelihood estimation (REML), incorporating animals as random factor, and stimulus order in the H-D test (JO-FO vs. FO-JO), odours (JO vs. FO) as fixed factor for the analyses of the habituation and dishabituation responses, with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. The lambs’ sex was also incorporated in the analyses as a fixed factor. Given the highly unbalanced ratio of male and female human odour pools, and as this variable was not part of our initial hypotheses, we chose not to include donor sex in the statistical analyses in order to preserve the model’s statistical power. All analyses were made on Jamovi (α = 0.05, Jamovi project, 2022, version 2.3; R Core Team, 2021, version 4.1, including the GAMLj module). All data in the Results section are presented as mean ± SE (standard Error).

Results

Experiment 1: adult Ewes

Human participants’ emotions

Human donors reported an average stress score of 1.35 ± 0.17 after watching the films conveying a joyful emotional content, and of 7.12 ± 0.45 after watching films conveying a fearful emotional content. As expected, the donors self-reported a significantly lower average stress level after attending the joyful films than after the stressful films (Wilcoxon test, α = 0.05, n = 17, p <.001). They reported also a higher average joy score of 6.0 ± 1.99 after the joyful film and of 2.0 ± 0.8 in the fearful film (Wilcoxon test, α = 0.05, n = 17, p <.001). The selected film excerpts induced thus contrasted emotional feeling in the donors, which should be correlated with perceptually differentiable axillary odours73. Whether such human body odour cues convey sensory/affective contrasts differentiable to adult ewes is assessed below.

Habituation

First, a general habituation effect to the testing materials was noted in the habituation phase. As repeated presentations proceeded, the ewes sniffed the odour stick significantly longer during trial 1 than during trial 3 (O1.1: 5.39 ± 0.82 s vs. O1.3: 2.93 ± 0.67 s, F(1,31) = 12.75, p =.0012). Likewise, the time spent in nuzzling the pad decreased between trials 1 and 3 (O1.1: 1.33 ± 0.29 s vs. O1.3: 0.35 ± 0.10 s, F(1,31) = 11.47, p =.0019), as did the frequency of these contacts (O1.1: 2.25 ± 0.42 times vs. O1.3: 0.85 ± 0.15 times, F(1,31) = 9.22, p =.004), but regardless of the habituation odour. Ewes also spent less time facing the stimulus device (stick and pads) (O1.1: 6.23 ± 0.86 s vs. O1.3: 3.80 ± 0.67 s, F(1,31) = 12.20, p =.0014). Finally, one variable increased from trial 1 to 3, in that ewes held their ears back longer during the latter than the former trial (O1.1: 6.54 ± 1.36 s vs. O1.3: 9.28 ± 1.51 s, F(1,31) = 5.08, p =.031), regardless of the type of odour presented or the odour to which they had been habituated.

Secondly, a general effect of the odour was observed only on ear position during the habituation phase. Indeed, across the two trials of the habituation phase (O1.1 + O1.3), ewes presented with FO were in the ‘negative ear position’ longer than those presented with JO (FO: 26.79 ± 1.05 s vs. JO: 21.81 ± 1.71 s, F(1,31) = 4.66, p =.038, Fig. 2a). A significant odour effect based on the order of presentation was also noted (F(1,31) = 7.65; p =.009). Post hoc analyses with Bonferroni correction revealed that, during trial 1, ewes exposed to JO spent significantly more time with ears positioned horizontally (8.92 ± 9.51 s) compared to those exposed to FO (2.17 ± 0.97 s, p =.024, see Fig. 2b). No further effects of the odour condition, the presentation order of the odours during the H-D test or age were found on other affective response items or on interaction behaviour with lambs.

Dishabituation

The odour stimulus, presentation order of odours, and age did not appear to significantly affect the ewes’ investigative responses (approaching, sniffing, nuzzling) during the dishabituation phase (Fig. 3). However, some ear position-related responses indicated differential processing of the habituation and dishabituation stimuli: the frequency of backward ear position increased (O1.3: 1.37 ± 0.18 times vs. O2: 1.85 ± 0.25 times, F(1,31) = 4.64, p =.038); but independently of the nature of habituating or dishabituating stimuli. No significant change was noted in the duration of backward ear position (O1.3: 9.28 ± 1.54 s vs. O2: 10.56 ± 1.50 s, F(1,32) = 1.71, p =.020). Similarly, the ewes changed ear positions more frequently during the dishabituation trial than during the last habituation trial (O1.3: 5.37 ± 0.38 times vs. O2: 6.42 ± 0.49 times, F(1,31) = 4.96, p =.033). No further effects of the odour condition, the presentation order of the odours during the H-D test or age were found for the other affective response items or for interaction behaviour with lambs.

In sum, three presentation trials of human odours to adult ewes sufficed to induce relative habituation of investigative behaviour toward FO and JO. However, one affective item (backward ear position) increased during the habituation procedure, following a sensitization rather than a habituation trend. Then, at the dishabituation stage, ewes’ ear postures indicated subtle signs of discrimination of the novel odour, whether it was of FO or JO type. However, ear posture data during the habituation phase suggest both odours are differentiable to ewes.

Experiment 2: lambs

Differential emotional responses of human odour donors

Human donors reported an average stress score of 1.4 ± 0.16 in their emotional state following joyful films, and of 6.60 ± 0.40 in the fearful context. As expected, the donors self-reported significantly lower stress levels after seeing the films assumed to convey a joyful content than after seeing the films assumed to conveyed a fearful content (Paired t-test, t(9) = 14.48, p <.001, d = 4.58). They also reported an average joy score of 7.90 ± 0.31 after seeing the films assumed to convey a joyful content, and of 2.60 ± 0.56 after watching fearful film scenes (Paired t-test, t(9) = 8.37, p <.001, d = 2.65). It may thus be assumed that the selected fearful and joyful films induced distinctive emotional states potentially reflected in contrasted volatiles in axillary secretions. Below, neonate lambs were tested for their ability to discriminate these odour cues and to value them differentially in terms of valence.

Habituation

First, a general habituation effect to the testing material was noted. Indeed, in repeatedly presenting either FO or JO, the lambs investigated more the stimuli at 1 st than at 4th trial. Thus, they spent more time sniffing the odour pad during trial 1 than during trial 4 of the habituation phase (O1.1: 1.55 ± 0.37 s vs. O1.4: 0.48 ± 0.23 s, F(1,18) = 7.06, p =.016, Fig. 4a), and exhibited a significant higher frequency of sniffing (O1.1: 1.80 ± 0.40 times vs. O1.4: 0.55 ± 0.15 times, F(1,18) = 11.65, p =.003). Also, regardless of the odour condition, the duration of muzzle contact with the odour pad decreased between trials 1 and 4 (O1.1: 1.73 ± 0.43 s vs. O1.4: 0.35 ± 0.14 s, F(1,35) = 0.06, p =.005), as did the frequency of contact with them (O1.1: 1.50 ± 0.35 times vs. O1.4: 0.50 ± 0.18 times, F(1,18) = 6.72, p =.018). Lambs also sniffed the odour-presentation stick more frequently during trial 1 than during trial 4 (O1.1: 1.50 ± 0.48 times vs. O1.4: 0.60 ± 0.15 times, F(1,18) = 5.22, p =.034). Lambs’ responses to the odour pads involved also lingual actions: they spent less time protruding their tongue on trial 1 than on trial 4 (O1.1: 0.79 ± 0.19 s vs. O1.4: 0.38 ± 0.19 s, F(1,18) = 5.03, p =.037) and did it less frequently (O1.1: 1.35 ± 0.30 times vs. O1.4: 0.45 ± 0.13 times, F(1,18) = 11.79, p =.003). No effect of lambs’ sex was detected for these or other behavioural variables.

Secondly, a general odour effect on several behaviours was noted during the habituation trials. Indeed, when items were summed across the two trials of the habituation phase (O1.1 + O1.4), lambs exposed to FO orally grasped the odour-presentation stick longer than those exposed to JO (FO: 3.75 ± 0.94 s vs. JO: 1.08 ± 0.62 s, F(1,17) = 6.20, p =.023, Fig. 4b), and they did it more frequently (FO: 1.39 ± 0.30 times vs. JO: 0.27 ± 0.24 times, F(1,17) = 9.39, p =.007). Those exposed to FO relative to those exposed to JO, also showed more frequent (FO: 1.28 ± 0.32 times vs. JO: 0.59 ± 0.17 times, F(1,17) = 4.70, p =.044) and longer tongue protrusion (FO: 0.93 ± 0.26 s vs. JO: 0.30 ± 0.10 s, F(1,17) = 6.20, p =.023). These responses were modulated by sex as male lambs showed longer lingual actions than females (0.91 ± 0.24 s vs. 0.26 ± 0.10 s, respectively; F(1,18) = 6.59, p =.020), yielding a significant sex by odour interaction (F(1,16) = 6.70, p =.019). Post-hoc analyses with Bonferroni corrections found males exposed to FO “tonging” longer than males exposed to JO (FO: 1.72 ± 0.41 s vs. JO: 0.37 ± 0.17 s, p =.008) and than females exposed to FO (males: 1.72 ± 0.41 s vs. females: 0.30 ± 0.18 s, p =.007). Finally, FO-exposed lambs attempted to escape more insistently from experimenter’s arms than those exposed to JO, both in terms of frequency (FO: 0.66 ± 0.24 times vs. O1.4: 0.09 ± 0.06 times, F(1,17) = 5.48, p =.031) and duration (FO: 0.81 ± 0.26 s vs. JO: 0.15 ± 0.12 s, F(1,17) = 4.99, p =.039). This effect was observed regardless of sex or odour presentation order.

Dishabituation

No meaningful effects on sniffing behaviour (Fig. 5) or other investigative responses were noted as a function of the odour condition, the odour presentation order, and sex of lambs at the dishabituation stage. But the assumedly novel odour introduced after habituation led to a notable reactivity change for one behavioural variable: the lambs orally grasped the stick longer during the third habituation trial compared to the dishabituation trial (O1.3: 2.70 ± 1.09 s vs. O2: 1.40 ± 0.59 s, F(1,18) = 5.28, p =.033).

In sum, as expected, the lambs showed signs of habituation to the odour stimuli or the presentation-stick conveying them over 4 repetitions. Then, at the dishabituation stage, instead of displaying the expected increase of investigation toward the novel odour, the lambs further lessened investigating it, suggesting that, at least by this behavioural criterion, they did not discriminate human FO from JO. However, several other behavioural variables (such as escape attempts and tongue protrusion) were significantly influenced by the odour during the habituation phase. Taken together, these findings suggest that, although they do not show olfactory dishabituation in the H-D test, lambs show some evidence of differentiation of both odours.

Discussion

The present study examined whether sheep discriminate the axillary odours of humans who have been subjected to contrasted emotion-inducing procedures. Based on previous studies using similar methods to induce contrastive human emotions70,101 and on the present self-reports of stress vs. joy by the donors, we assumed that the emotion-inducing procedure was effective enough to elicit the release of olfactorily differentiable axillary secretions. The potential perceptual contrast between FO and JO was put to the test with adult ewes and neonate lambs using a H-D paradigm and measuring responses that were indicative of valenced responses.

As expected, adult ewes showed significant habituation of investigative actions (approaching, sniffing, nuzzling) toward the odour stimuli, although without differential effect of FO vs. JO. But the dishabituation stimulus did not cause any significant rebound in investigative reactivity, contrary to our predictions, indicating that both odour stimuli were not clearly discriminated. However, data of emotional behaviour during the habituation phase were differentiable between FO or JO, in that ewes exposed to FO showed more negative ear displays (i.e., forward, backward and asymmetric) interpretable as indicative of a negative emotional state, while those exposed to JO evinced more ear displays indicative of neutral or positive emotional state82,83. Although these differences were statistically significant and in line with previous findings on sheep reactivity to human emotional odours56, we must admit that they are rather subtle.

However, the progressive increase in the backward ears’ display throughout the habituation phase of the test may also reflect increasing stress caused by the testing procedure rather than a response to odour valence. As ewes are highly neophobic102–107 (although less so right after delivery in presence of their lamb), any stress induced by the test itself may have confounded our results: one cannot exclude that they may have reacted to an unfamiliarity confound of the human odours rather than to an hypothetical FO vs. JO difference. This potential stress of the ewes could also have mitigated their ability to express more overt behavioural responses with the advancing H-D test. Thus, although these elusive differences of ewes’ reactions to both human odours are of interest, they may not fully capture their emotional impact. Like horses, whose heart rate variability differs depending on whether they are exposed to FO or JO37, it would be valuable to incorporate physiological measures, such as heart rate or cortisol levels, to further assess the emotional impact of these odours in sheep. Finally, replicating our previous experiment in which ewes were exposed to a human stress-related odour versus a neutral stimulus56 would provide further insights into the ability of ewes to perceive these different types of odours when presented singly or in pairs.

Like ewes, neonate lambs showed a clear habituation pattern to the repeated odour stimuli. Over successive trials, they reduced both number and duration of investigative actions on the stimulus (e.g., sniffing, touching), but did so irrespective of whether it was a FO or a JO. Dishabituation was only noted for oral actions on the stick, as lambs grasped it longer with the habituation odour than with the dishabituation odour, suggesting decreased interest for, or avoidance to, the whole device. This apparent shift of focus toward the stick rather than the odour stimuli implies that the dishabituation phase may not have been sensitive enough to reveal a clear discrimination between FO and JO.

But during the habituation phase, lambs exposed to FO attempted to escape more frequently and more insistently from the experimenter’s lap than those of lambs exposed to JO. This suggests that lambs might have distinguished FO as a potentially aversive stimulus relative to JO. On the other side, they also mouthed the pads more often and longer, which may be interpreted as exploratory nuzzling/mouthing to any object, an activity regularly reported in artificially-reared lambs [e.g., 108]. It cannot be excluded that FO is not linked with any negative emotional valence in artificially-raised lambs, but rather with learning to suck an artificial nipple in physical contact with a farmer who himself is under great stress (lambing being a very stressful and labour-intensive period109,110 and emitting stress-induced body odours bearing similarity to those conveyed in the experimental stimuli. This conflicting pattern of lambs’ responses complicates the interpretation of FO as aversive, as oral investigation may indicate interest rather than avoidance. This hypothesis is further supported by the observation that FO-exposed lambs protruded their tongue more frequently during the first habituation trial than those exposed to JO.

The increased rate of tongue protrusion observed in male lambs exposed to FO may be reminiscent of the flehmen response of adult sheep111–114. However, the lambs tonguing does not fully correspond to the typical flehmen response, which is characterized by a distinctive elevation of the upper lip combined with an upward extension of the head. Nevertheless, this behaviour could reflect either an immature form of the flehmen pattern or a general exploratory response involving both olfaction (and taste despite the tongue does never enter into contact with the odour stimuli). Further research with larger sample sizes and including lambs raised under their mother may yield more reliable insights into these behaviours.

In both above experiments, a non-significant dishabituation response should typically lead to conclude that the dishabituating odour is not discriminated from the habituating odour. Another possibility would be that that the paradigm used here may not be sensitive enough to evidence olfactory discrimination in sheep when using unfamiliar human odours. This interpretation is however unlikely as the behavioural paradigm used in the current study has already been validated in sheep in a previous study78. The findings showed that both ewes and lambs exhibited the typical habituation-dishabituation pattern for stimulus exploration variables – and notably, for olfactory exploration– when presented with odours of opposite valence (i.e., dog faeces versus wool from unknown conspecifics)78. These results therefore indicate that the paradigm allows the demonstration of olfactory discrimination in ewes, provided that the animals are able to perceive the difference between stimuli. Thus, the absence of differential responses to human fear and joy odours in the present study is unlikely to reflect a methodological limitation of the test itself, but rather provides informative evidence regarding the nature of sheep processing of these particular stimuli. It remains possible that the H-D test, when applied to human emotional odours, is not the most appropriate paradigm for assessing sheep’s ability to discriminate between them. Indeed, as noted by Slotnick and Coppola115, “the absence of a dishabituation effect must be interpreted with caution” (p. 276), especially if the habituating odour resembles the dishabituating odour115,116. In the present case, habituating and dishabituating stimuli were probably similar to ovines as they derived from the axillary secretions from same human donors, although these declared being in different emotional states (self-reports of stress). Either both odour stimuli were not perceptually different enough in quality or intensity, fear and joy films leading to similar activation of axillary processes despite different levels of self-reported stress; further, the donors’ axillary outputs may be blurred by carry-over effects between emotional states as they were exposed to the fear- and joy-inducing films within a same, relatively short session. Indeed, it must be acknowledged that although previous work has reported chemical differences between axillary secretions associated with happiness and fear73, the same study also showed that “happy sweat” partly overlaps chemically with both fearful and neutral states. This raises the possibility that the odour samples collected in our study, although associated with different self-reported emotions, were not sufficiently distinct at the chemical or perceptual level to be consistently differentiated by sheep. Such partial overlap may thus contribute to the absence of a clear dishabituation effect observed here. Although the films primarily aimed to induce fear or joy, we acknowledge that other co-occurring emotions may have been elicited29,73, potentially blurring the emotional signal conveyed by the odour samples. It is worth noting however that in ecological context a similar “emotional noise” is also probably present when the ewes are exposed to a “happy” or “anxious” farmer.

Another possibility could be that both odour stimuli carried a same higher-level meaning that is crucial for strongly neophobic animals such as sheep in terms of human donors’ unfamiliarity that overshadowed any subtler emotional note. Our failure to find dishabituation could thus be explained as stimulus generalization rather than a true inability to discriminate between both odours103. Finally, it is possible that sheep are unable to discriminate between these two types of unfamiliar human emotional odours.

However, in both experiments, some response items known to reflect ovine affect were discriminative of both types of odours when presented during the habituation phase, suggesting that adult ewes and lambs could in fact sense some perceptual contrasts in human FO and JO. However, these reactive items were few among the long list of those which were coded and involved rather subtle (to humans) behaviours (such as ear movements in ewes or erratic tongue protrusion in lambs). These results may reflect inter-individual variability, with certain animals exhibiting heightened sensitivity compared to others. Such differences could also plausibly explain the absence of a clear habituation-dishabituation pattern in our study. Including a third scent that was more extreme, such as a predatory odor78, may produce a high H-D effect and serve as a control. A related study using the H-D or other paradigms but with more intense and contrasted odorants should assess whether sheep do express more robust behavioural indicators of affect such as increased avoidance or escape responses.

In conclusion, the H-D paradigm used in the present study does not provide compelling evidence that adult ewes and neonate lambs do discriminate human odours assumed to cue positive (joy) vs. negative (fear) emotions. This non-significant dishabituation effect in both ewes and lambs may be due to either perceptual similarity of the FO and JO sampled in the present conditions or to the animals’ extraction of a cue conveyed by both odours, possibly a common higher-level meaning such as the unfamiliarity of the human donors. The seemingly contradictory behavioural responses of lambs indicate that further research is needed to confirm their discrimination ability. While their responsiveness in the habituation trials appeared differential in term of emotional valence sensing of FO and JO, their responses at dishabituation trials did not reveal any clear discrimination. Adult ewes displayed reactivity to odour valence, yet whether this reflects true discrimination or is influenced by factors such as test-induced stress remains unclear. Given these limitations, more studies are needed to refine our understanding of ovine olfactory perception of human emotions. Specifically, future research should explore whether sheep can differentiate FO and JO in associating behaviour with physiological variables. Additionally, more attention to the role of experience in shaping responses to human odours could help clarify whether and how ovine reactivity to human emotional odours develops over time. Instead of studying “lambda sheep” sampled randomly within a flock, future studies might focus on specific individuals (leader sheep117 that have experienced intensive exposure to particular humans in optimal learning conditions (imprinting, conditioning) and do not shy away from unfamiliar stimuli.
