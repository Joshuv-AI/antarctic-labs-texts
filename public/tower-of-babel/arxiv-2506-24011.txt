The Lifespan of our Universe
and S.-H. Henry Tye

Abstract
The Dark Energy Survey (DES) and the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) measurements claim that the dark energy equation of state w ≠ − 1 w\neq-1 . This observation can be explained by the axion Dark Energy (aDE) model of an ultralight axion plus a cosmological constant Λ \Lambda . Despite a relatively large degeneracy, there is a high probability that Λ < 0 \Lambda<0 . This negative Λ \Lambda leads the universe to end in a big crunch. Using the best-fit values of the model as a benchmark, we find the lifespan of our universe to be 33 billion years.

2
The aDE Model

The aDE model starts with the potential for the ultra-light axion field
\phi=f\theta
,

V(\theta)=m_{\phi}^{2}f^{2}\left[1-\cos\left(\theta\right)\right]\;,

(2.1)

where
f
is the so called decay constant and
\pi>\theta\geq 0
.
If the universe starts at a random initial value
\theta=\theta_{i}\neq 0
, inflation will then lead to
\theta(\bm{x})=\theta_{i}=\phi_{i}/f
everywhere.
When the Hubble parameter
H\gg m_{\phi}
, the universe is essentially frozen at the misaligned initial
state
\phi_{i}
and
V(\theta_{i})
contribute to the dark energy,
i.e.
, the equation of state (EoS)
w=-1
. As
H\lesssim m_{\phi}
,
\phi
starts to roll down along the potential towards
\phi=0
and deposits the
\phi
vacuum energy density into matter density (which drops like
a^{-3}
where
a
is the scale factor). The current dark energy is a combination of the cosmological constant
\Lambda
and the axion field.
The cosmological evolution is governed by Friedmann equations, which are

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{2}

\displaystyle=g(a)+\Omega_{\Lambda}+\Omega_{\phi}\;,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{dg(a)}{d\log a}-\frac{3}{2}\left(\Omega_{\Lambda}+\Omega_{\phi}\right)\left(w_{\rm DE}+1\right)\;,

(2.2)

where the dimensionless Hubble parameter is defined as
\mathcal{H}=H/H_{0}
, and
H_{0}
is the Hubble constant today.
The prime indicates the derivative with respective to the dimensionless cosmic time
H_{0}t
and contributions with constant EoS
w_{i}\neq-1
are collectively expressed as

g(a)=\sum_{i}\frac{\Omega_{i}}{a^{3(1+w_{i})}}\;.

(2.3)

Here the dimensionless energy density is
\Omega_{i}=\rho_{i,0}/3H_{0}^{2}M_{\rm Pl}^{2}
, where
\rho_{i,0}
is the energy density today at
a=1
; so
\Omega_{\Lambda}
is the energy density due to
\Lambda
.
For our purposes here, we consider only
g=\Omega_{\rm m}/a^{3}
, which includes both the baryon and the dark matter densities.
(It is straightforward to include the radiation or other possible energy content.)
The axion (dimensionless) energy density
\Omega_{\phi}
is

\Omega_{\phi}=\frac{1}{6}\beta^{2}\theta^{\prime 2}+\overline{V}(\theta)\;,\quad\overline{V}(\theta)=\frac{\beta^{2}m_{\phi}^{2}}{3H_{0}^{2}}(1-\cos\theta)\;,

(2.4)

where
\beta=f/M_{\rm Pl}
, with
M_{\rm Pl}=2.43\times 10^{18}
GeV. Today at
a=1
, we have

\Omega_{\rm m}+\Omega_{\phi,0}+\Omega_{\Lambda}=1\;.

(2.5)

The axion field evolution is determined by its equation of motion, which is

\theta^{\prime\prime}+3\mathcal{H}\theta^{\prime}+\frac{m_{\phi}^{2}}{H_{0}^{2}}\sin\theta=0\;.

(2.6)

Accordingly, the formula for the EoS of the dark energy
(\Omega_{\Lambda}+\Omega_{\phi})
is

w_{\rm DE}\equiv\frac{\beta^{2}\theta^{\prime 2}/6-\overline{V}(\theta)-\Omega_{\Lambda}}{\beta^{2}\theta^{\prime 2}/6+\overline{V}(\theta)+\Omega_{\Lambda}}\;.

(2.7)

Note that
w_{\rm DE}=-1
if the axion is absent, or if
H>m_{\phi}
when the axion field is frozen.
As proposed in ref.
[
24
]
,
it is well approximated by a 2-parameter formula for the
a\leq 1
,

w_{\rm DE}(a)=\begin{cases}-1+w_{1}(a-a_{1})^{2}&1\geq a\geq a_{1}\\
-1&a<a_{1}\end{cases}\;,

(2.8)

Note that
w\geq-1
throughout.
Parameters
a_{1}
and
w_{1}
can be extracted from data.

Towards the future

One can predict the future of our universe based on current observations.
Here we investigate the cosmic future based on the best-fit parameters (
3.1
) of the aDE model.

4.1
An approximate model

It is useful to first consider an approximate but analytic version of the aDE model. Here, the picture is clear and the result yields a good estimate of the lifespan.

For small
\theta
or
\phi
, the potential
V(\theta)
(
2.1
) reduces to
V(\phi)=m_{\phi}^{2}\phi^{2}/2
.
Multiplying (
2.6
) by
\theta^{\prime}
and averaging over a period of the coherent
\theta
oscillation,
\langle{\theta^{\prime}}^{2}\rangle=3\Omega_{\phi}
,
we have

\Omega_{\phi}^{\prime}=-3\mathcal{H}\Omega_{\phi}\quad\to\quad\Omega_{\phi}\sim a^{-3}\;.

So
\Omega_{\phi}
behaves like matter (although in the actual situation,
\theta
is not small and will not reach a full cycle).
We propose an approximate model, which describes the axion energy density as

\Omega_{\phi}(a)\simeq\begin{cases}\Omega_{\phi,0}&a\leq 1\\
\Omega_{\phi,0}a^{-3}&a>1\end{cases}\;,

(4.1)

where
\Omega_{\phi,0}
is a constant.
Starting from
a=1
, we consider the effective
\Lambda
CDM model, where
{\bar{\Omega}}_{\rm m}
now includes baryon matter, dark matter as well as the axion matter.

The first Friedmann equation is then
\mathcal{H}^{2}={\bar{\Omega}}_{\rm m}/a^{3}+\Omega_{\Lambda}
. So
the consistency relation
\mathcal{H}^{2}(a=1)=1
indicates that
{\bar{\Omega}}_{\rm m}+\Omega_{\Lambda}=1
.
One can combine it with the second Friedmann equation (
2.2
) and obtain a phase space relation for the Hubble parameter,
which is
3
3

3

It is straightforward to include a radiation or other constant-EoS energy content.

\mathcal{H}^{\prime}=-\frac{3}{2}\left(\mathcal{H}^{2}-\Omega_{\Lambda}\right)\;.

(4.2)

The sign of
\Omega_{\Lambda}
is crucial for the future. As shown in figure
2
, there are 3 possibilities:

•

For dS vacuum (
\Omega_{\Lambda}>0
), one has a stable point where
\mathcal{H}=\sqrt{\Omega_{\Lambda}}>0
and
a\sim e^{Ht}
.

•

For the Minkowski limit (
\Omega_{\Lambda}=0
), the universe will approach the point
\mathcal{H}=0
with a vanishing
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
. This process will take infinite cosmic time and
a
continues to grow without bound.

•

For the AdS (
\Omega_{\Lambda}<0
) case, there is no such stable configuration. Here, when the Hubble parameter reaches zero (and
a
reaches its maximum), its derivative
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
is negative. This means that the Hubble parameter will become negative, where
a
begins to collapse and starts the crunching phase. It is easy to see that
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}<0
in the actual aDE model with
\Omega_{\Lambda}<0
.

For
\Omega_{\Lambda}<0
in this approximate model, the evolution of the scale factor can be analytically solved from
a^{\prime}=\mathcal{H}a
and eq. (
4.2
), where the age of the universe
t_{0}=13.8
Gyr,

a(t)^{3}=\frac{\Omega_{\Lambda}-1}{\Omega_{\Lambda}}\sin^{2}\left[\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{-\Omega_{\Lambda}}H_{0}\left(t-t_{0}\right)+\arctan(\sqrt{-\Omega_{\Lambda}})\right]\;,

(4.3)

Here

a(t=t_{0})^{3}=(|\Omega_{\Lambda}|+1)\sin^{2}\left[\arctan(\sqrt{|\Omega_{\Lambda}|})\right]/|\Omega_{\Lambda}|=1

and we shall take
a(t_{0})=1
as the starting point.
The scale factor
a
reaches a maximum

a_{\rm max}=\left(\frac{\Omega_{\Lambda}-1}{\Omega_{\Lambda}}\right)^{1/3}\;

(4.4)

and then approaches zero.

Note that in this approximation, we treat the axion as matter density for
t>t_{0}
(before which it is dark energy) till the end (
4.1
). Therefore, the crunching phase is not simply time reversal of the expanding phase.

The lifespan can be estimated as one cycle of eq. (
4.3
), when
a\to 0
,

T(\Omega_{\Lambda}<0)=\frac{1}{H_{0}}\left(\frac{2\pi-2\arctan{\sqrt{|\Omega_{\Lambda}|}}}{3\sqrt{|\Omega_{\Lambda}|}}\right)+t_{0}\;.

(4.5)

By taking the best-fit data
\Omega_{\Lambda}=-1.61
(
3.1
), one obtains
a_{\rm max}=1.17
and a universe of lifespan
T\simeq 30.9
Gyr.
The crunching phase takes up
\sim 39\%
of the total lifespan.

Estimating the lifespan

As indicated in the best-fit parameters (
3.1
),
\Omega_{\Lambda}=-1.61
and the mass of the axion is roughly
m_{\phi}\simeq 2.04H_{0}

a=1

With the initial angle at
\theta_{i}=0.82\pi

\Omega_{\phi}
cannot be simply approximated as matter-like. One has to numerically solve the dynamics.
Taking the
a(H_{0}t_{0}=0.952)=1

we fix the initial conditions from
the consistency of the Firedmann equation
\mathcal{H}^{2}(a=1)=1

One obtains, from eq. (
2.2

\displaystyle\overline{V}(\theta_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(1-\Omega_{\rm m})\left(1-w_{0}\right)-\Omega_{\Lambda}\;,

\displaystyle\theta_{0}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\pm\sqrt{\frac{3}{\beta^{2}}(1-\Omega_{\rm m})(1+w_{0})}\;,

(4.6)

where
\theta_{0}=\theta(a=1)

Here, the sign of the derivative depends on the position

Since the axion in our scenario is still rolling down towards its minimum for the first time, we take

\theta_{0}^{\prime}<0

The initial conditions determine the evolution of the axion via eq. (

Meanwhile, the evolution of the scale factor is governed by

\displaystyle a^{\prime}

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-\frac{3}{2}\frac{\Omega_{\rm m}}{a^{3}}-\frac{1}{2}\beta^{2}\theta^{\prime 2}\;.

, where
a(t_{\rm max})\simeq a_{\rm min}

Discussion

We give some discussion in the following.

•

As shown in Table 1 and Figure 4 in the extended version of ref.
[
24
]
. Because of the large degeneracy and the highly skewed probability distribution, the best-fit value
\Omega_{\Lambda}=-1.61
is way outside the
1\sigma
range of the mean value

\displaystyle\langle\Omega_{\Lambda}\rangle=-0.69^{+0.70}_{-0.20}\;.

(5.1)

This also shows that, although not favored,
\Omega_{\Lambda}=0
is consistent with the DESI data
[
20
]
. With the corresponding average mass
\langle m_{\phi}\rangle=2.51\times 10^{-33}
eV and
\langle\phi_{i}\rangle=5.22\times 10^{18}
GeV, the lifespan will be

\displaystyle\langle T\rangle=40.3\,{\rm Gyr}\quad{\rm at}\quad\langle w_{0}\rangle=-0.763

(5.2)

which is longer than
T
in (
4.8
). This result is shown as a blue dot in figure
4
.

•

Since both a radiation and a positive curvature term are negligible today, including them will make little difference to the analysis. However, including a negative curvature term will shorten the lifespan. In fact, a negative curvature term will render the lifespan finite even if the cosmological constant term is absent.

•

For smaller decay constant
f\lesssim M_{\rm Pl}
, which corresponds to
\beta<1
, it only affects the axion evolution through its effect on the Hubble flow. As shown in the equation of motion (
2.6
), there is no explicit
\beta
dependence. However, it enters the Hubble flow via
\Omega_{\phi}(\beta)=\beta^{2}\Omega_{\phi}(\beta=1)
. Since the axion is still in its early rolling period, the energy density is still dominated by the potential energy, where
\beta
enters in the combination
m^{2}_{\phi}\beta^{2}
(
2.4
), reflecting a degeneracy in data fitting. In any case, because of the degeneracy already present, a small lowering of the value of
f
will make little difference to the fit.

•

Note that throughout the evolution in the aDE model,
H^{2}\geq 0
(on-shell). If the universe starts in an AdS space,
H^{2}=-|\Lambda|<0
, or more generally starts with
H^{2}<0
(off-shell), which may be approached via a tunneling process
[
36
]
, its evolution will be very different.

•

The Hubble tension is lurking at the background of our analysis as well as the DES/DESI analaysis
[
37
]
. If we bring in the axi-Higgs model
[
38
,
39
]
,
H_{0}
can be uplifted by
2\%
–
3\%
, relieving some of the Hubble tension. This will shorten the lifespan by a comparable amount.

•

If there is another ultra-light axion with mass
m\leq 10^{-34}
eV, its energy is still contributing to the dark energy density today. As a result, its presence will further lower the value of
\Lambda
. That is, the big crunch will still happen, though the lifespan of our universe will depend on the properties of this additional axion.

6
Remarks

Here, a few remarks on the overall picture are in order.

String Theory Implications
— String theory suggests that constructing a meta-stable vacuum with a positive cosmological constant can be challenging, while constructing vacua with a negative cosmological constant is generic
[
40
,
41
,
42
]
. Presumably, non-supersymmetric vacua are unstable or meta-stable, so they will eventually evolve towards supersymmetric vacua, which have negative
\Lambda
.

It is clear that SUSY is broken today, as not a single super-partner has been discovered, suggesting that the super-partners have masses of order of TeV.
A generic mass splitting term of TeV scale will shift the standard model particle masses, but cosmological observations such as the CMB measurements shows that the masses of the electron and nucleons should not have been shifted by more than a few percent since big bang nucleosynthesis time. This suggests, in addition to the standard model particle masses in the supersymmetric vacuum, there is an additional SUSY-breaking mass term of the form,

V(\phi)(1-p_{R})\;,\quad p_{R}=(-1)^{3(B-L)+2s}

where the super-partners have odd R-parity,
p_{R}=-1
, while the standard model particles have
p_{R}=1
.
This raises only the masses of the super-partners until
V(\phi)=0
at
\phi=0
, when the super-partner spectrum matches that of the standard model particle spectrum.
Irrespective on whether the AdS ground state is supersymmetric or not, the universe will pass it at roughly
H_{0}t\simeq 2.1
(see the right panel of Fig.
3
) before reaching the crunch.

Small
|\Lambda|
— Earlier the grand puzzle is the smallness of
\Lambda
in de Sitter space. Now, the puzzle is to explain the smallness of the negative
\Lambda
in AdS space.
Note that the explanation for these 2 cases can be very different. For example, the statistical explanation for an exponentially small positive
\Lambda

[
43
,
44
]
does not work when
\Lambda<0
. Interestingly, in a set of Calabi-Yau orientifold models in Type IIB string theory, one finds that
\Lambda
can be exponentially small, only if it is negative in a supersymetric vacuum
[
45
,
46
]
.
This provides a plausible explanation why the negative
\Lambda
is so small. However, with broken SUSY today, normal quantum corrections to
\Lambda
that are absent in a SUSY vacuum are in general present now.

Axiverse
— As noted before, ultralight axions (or axion-like particles) are ubiquitous in string theory
[
47
,
10
]
. They pick up their exponentially small masses via non-perturbative dynamics (cf. ref.
[
48
]
). There is tremendous freedom to introduce any number of ultra-light axions with a spectrum of masses and couplings (i.e., decay constants). Of course, it is much more interesting when the introduction of a specific ultra-light axion resolves some outstanding puzzles in astrophysics and/or cosmology.

The best known case is the fuzzy dark matter (FDM) model, where an axion of mass around
10^{-22}~{\rm eV}
is the source of the dark matter
[
49
,
50
]
. To resolve some issues with the diversity of ultra faint dwarf galaxies, the introduction of a second axion of mass around
10^{-20}
eV seems to be necessary
[
51
]
. Another even lighter axion of mass around
10^{-29}
eV introduced in the so-called axi-Higgs model
[
38
,
39
]
helps to explain the
7
Li puzzle in BBN, the Hubble tension and the isotropic cosmic birefringence
[
52
]
. The aDE model introduces one with mass
10^{-33}
eV.
These 4 ultra-light axions have comparable decay constant
f\lesssim M_{\rm Pl}
. Presumably, they are Ramond-Ramond modes in string theory under the compactification to 3 spatial dimensions. If their existences are confirmed, these axions together with their properties will shed valuable insight into the string theory compactification.

Astrophysical/Cosmological Implications
— In view of the dynamical dark energy impact on the evolution of the universe, it is interesting to re-analyze some of the dynamics involving astrophysical phenomena. As
a\to 0
, matter is being squeezed together. This is expected to enhance the formation of black holes, in particular the merging of black holes. Eventually, it is plausible that the universe ends in a single giant black hole. Including a growing radiation density as
a\to 0
will make little difference.

Note that the scale factor
a
has a periodic behavior and we consider only one cycle for our universe. As mentioned earlier, because of the nature of the axion field, the process is irreversible. Although very unlikely, one cannot rule out that there is a way for the universe to transition to the next cycle. However, this reincarnation will lead to a very different universe from our present one, not to a cyclic universe.

On the local level, the possible collision of our Milky Way with its largest neighbor, the Andromeda galaxy, is predicted to occur in about 4 to 10 billion years
[
53
,
54
]
. It is interesting to re-estimate the time of their collision (or no collision) before the big crunch.

7
Summary

We start with

\Omega_{\rm m}+\Omega_{\phi,0}+\Omega_{\Lambda}=1
,
where the matter density
\Omega_{\rm m}=0.28
includes both the ordinary matter and the dark matter densities, while the dark energy density includes the axion energy density and the cosmological constant:
\Omega_{\phi,0}+\Omega_{\Lambda}=0.72
. Here, introducing a radiation density makes little difference. For the dark energy EoS
w=-1
,
\Omega_{\phi}=0
. For
w>-1
and varying, the ultralight axion in the aDE model can explain its varying behavior, provided that
w\geq-1
. In fitting the DES/DESI data plus the CMB data, we determine the axion mass to be
m_{\phi}\simeq 2.93\times 10^{-33}
eV and
\Omega_{\phi,0}\simeq 2.33
, so
\Omega_{\Lambda}\simeq-1.61
. This negative cosmological constant means that our universe contains the matter and the
\phi
energy in an AdS space, resulting in its eventual contraction, ending in a big crunch. Defining the big crunch as the end of our universe allows us to estimate the lifespan of our universe to be about 33.3 billion years.

We emphasize that the determination of the lifespan of our universe depends on the recent observation that
w>-1
and the validity of the aDE model. It is crucial that the DES/DESI observation is confirmed and the aDE model is rigorously tested. Fortunately, a number of projects measuring different aspects of the dark energy are forthcoming in the near future. We look forward to a more precise determination of the universe’s lifespan.
