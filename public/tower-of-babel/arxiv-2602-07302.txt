Failure of the Invariant Cycle Theorem over ℤ \mathbb{Z}
Donu Arapura, François Greer, Yilong Zhang

Abstract
. We construct a counterexample to both the local and global invariant cycle theorems with integral coefficients. The example is a semistable one-parameter family of elliptic surfaces with p g = q = 1 p_{g}=q=1 and constant period map. The smooth fibers have the smallest possible discriminant, and are associated with Vinberg’s most algebraic K3 surface. Our construction generalizes the Shioda–Inose construction for rational double covers of K3 surfaces.

Introduction

The classical local invariant cycle theorem, stated below, was conjectured by Griffiths
[
Gri70
]
in 1970 and proved by Clemens and Schmid
[
Cle77
]
[
GS75
, pp 121-122]
shortly thereafter.
Professor Griffiths kindly informed us that many of these ideas were worked out in a seminar at Berkeley in the 1960’s. We note that a different proof was found by Deligne
[
Del80
, théorème 3.6]
. Both proofs are nontrivial; the first relies on the existence of limit mixed Hodge structures and the second on the Weil conjectures.

Theorem 1.1

(LICT
Q
)
.

Let
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{X}\to\Delta
be a flat family of complex projective varieties over a disk. Suppose
\mathcal{X}
is smooth and
f
is smooth over the punctured disk
\Delta^{*}
. Let
X_{t}
be a general smooth fiber, then for each
n
, the restriction map

H^{n}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{Q})\twoheadrightarrow H^{n}(X_{t},\mathbb{Q})^{\pi_{1}(\Delta^{*})}

surjects onto the invariant part.

For semistable families (i.e., the central fiber
\mathcal{X}_{0}
is a reduced normal crossing divisor and the total space is smooth), the theorem follows from the exactness of the Clemens–Schmid sequence
[
Cle77
,
Mor84b
]

H^{n}(X_{0},\mathbb{Q})\to H^{n}(X_{t},\mathbb{Q})\xrightarrow{N}H^{n}(X_{t},\mathbb{Q}),

where
N=\log(T)
is the logarithm of the monodromy operator
T
, and
\ker(N)=\ker(T-I)
is precisely the invariant part
H^{n}(X_{t},\mathbb{Q})^{\textup{inv}}
(see Section
8
). More generally, a much stronger form of Theorem
1.1
follows from the decomposition theorem
[
BBD82
, Theorem 6.2.5]
.

However, the previous arguments only work rationally. A natural question to ask is

Question 1.2

(LICT
Z
)
.

Does the theorem still hold with integral coefficients? In other words, is the restriction map

r\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}H^{n}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{Z})\to H^{n}(X_{t},\mathbb{Z})^{\pi_{1}(\Delta^{*})}

surjective?

The answer to this question is no. A straightforward counterexample can be constructed as follows. Let
\mathcal{X}\to\Delta
be a local elliptic surface with a multiple fiber of multiplicity
m>1
over
0
. Any class in the image of
H^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{Z})\to H^{2}(X_{t},\mathbb{Z})
must be divisible by
m
. In particular, the generator of
H^{2}(X_{t},\mathbb{Z})
, which is invariant, cannot lift to
\mathcal{X}
. One can preclude this sort of counterexample by working with semistable families. However, even with this assumption, we will see that the answer is no.

This type of question was first studied by Friedman and Scattone
[
Fri84
,
FS86
]
, where they showed that LICT
Z
holds for a semistable family of K3 surfaces. Recently, Friedman and Griffiths showed it also holds for I-surfaces
[
FG24
]
. In general, we show that LICT
Z
for
H^{2}
holds for any semistable family of surfaces with
q=0
(see Proposition
8.2
).

1.1.
Failure of local ICT
Z

In this paper, we provide a negative example in which the family is semistable, and
q=1
for the general fiber:

Theorem 1.3
.

The local invariant cycle “theorem” (
\textup{LICT}_{\mathbb{Z}}
) fails over
\mathbb{Z}
for a semistable family. More precisely,
there exists a semistable degeneration of smooth elliptic surfaces with
p_{g}=q=1
and torsion-free cohomology
\mathcal{X}\to\Delta
, such that the restriction map

H^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{Z})\to H^{2}(X_{t},\mathbb{Z})^{\pi_{1}(\Delta^{*})}

is not surjective.

The counterexample comes from a one-parameter family of
p_{g}=q=1
elliptic surfaces. We refer to such surfaces as elliptic-elliptic surfaces. They have K3-type Hodge structures on their second cohomology
H^{2}
, and nonzero first cohomology
H^{1}
. We construct a family of such surfaces over a complete curve with finite monodromy on
H^{2}
, and infinite monodromy on
H^{1}
. The family has several degenerations. Restricting to a neighborhood of a specific singular fiber, and then passing to a semistable model yields a family
\mathcal{X}\to\Delta
with a constant VHS on
H^{2}
, such that the image
of
H^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{Z})
is a proper subgroup of
H^{2}(X_{t},\mathbb{Z})
.
To prove this, we show that specialization map induces an injection on transcendental parts

T_{X_{0}}\hookrightarrow T_{X_{t}},

which preserves the cup product pairings.
We find that the central fiber
X_{0}
has a component birational to a specific elliptic K3
surface with discriminant
48
, and so does
T_{X_{0}}
.
However, we show that the discriminant
of
T_{X_{t}}
is
3
.
Hence, the image of the restriction map is only an index
4
subgroup on transcendental lattices, so LICT
Z
fails. On the other hand, for divisor classes, LICT
Z
holds for this family (cf. Corollary
8.5
).

In the previous example, the maps in the Clemens–Schmid sequence are defined integrally. The following is a direct consequence of Theorem
1.3
.

Corollary 1.4
.

The Clemens–Schmid sequence fails to be exact over
\mathbb{Z}
for the semistable family in Theorem
1.3
.

1.2.
Failure of global ICT
Z

For a proper surjective family
\mathcal{X}\to B
over a projective base

, Deligne proved the global invariant cycle theorem over
\mathbb{Q}

[
Del71
]
.
See also
[
Voi03

for a clear exposition.

Theorem 1.5

(GICT
Q

[
Del71
]
)
.

Let
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{X}\to B
be a proper surjective morphism between smooth projective varieties. Let
U\subseteq B
be the largest Zariski open subset over which

H^{n}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{Q})\to H^{n}(X_{t},\mathbb{Q})

One can also ask if the global invariant cycle theorem holds integrally. It is a classical result that it holds for a Lefschetz pencil arising from hyperplane sections of a smooth projective variety
[

We show that it can still be false in general:

Theorem 1.6
.

The global invariant cycle "theorem" (
GICT
Z
) fails over
\mathbb{Z}
for a one-dimensional semistable family. More precisely,
There is a smooth projective 3-fold
\mathcal{X}
, a smooth projective curve
C
, and a proper surjective morphism
\mathcal{X}\to C

H^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{Z})\to H^{2}(X_{t},\mathbb{Z})^{\pi_{1}(U,t)}

Note that in our example, the Leray spectral sequence still degenerates over
\mathbb{Z}
for the smooth fibration
\mathcal{X}_{U}\to U
because
U
is an open Riemann surface homotopy equivalent to a 1-dimensional CW complex, so the invariant cycles lift to
H^{2}(\mathcal{X}_{U},\mathbb{Z})

1.3

1.3.
The construction

The family
\mathcal{X}\to\Delta
used in the proof of Theorem
1.3
has constant period map.
Each smooth fiber is Hodge-theoretically associated with a K3 surface
Y
, which is one of Vinberg’s “most algebraic” K3 surfaces
[
Vin83
]
with discriminant
d=3
, in the sense that there is an Hodge isometry
T_{X_{t}}\cong T_{Y}
. They are related by the following diagram, formally analogous to a Shioda–Inose structure
[
SI77
,
Mor84a
]
:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{t}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Z}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{2}}

Shioda and Inose
[
SI77
]
showed that
Y
admits a rational map of degree two onto the Kummer surface
Z=\textup{Kum}(E_{\omega}\times E_{\omega})
associated to the self-product of the elliptic curve
E_{\omega}=\mathbb{C}/(\mathbb{Z}+\mathbb{Z}\omega)
with
j=0
.
The rational map
\pi_{2}
arises from a quadratic base change of an elliptic fibration on
Z
branched at two special fibers (
IV^{*},I_{0}^{*}
).

We show that the quadratic base change branched at
three

II^{*},IV^{*},I_{0}^{*}
), plus an additional smooth fiber lying over
t
produces an elliptic-elliptic surface

. As

varies, the Hodge structure

remains constant. That is, the period map for this family sends all values of

to a point.
The three singular members of the family occur when

collides with each of the special values lying under singular fibers of

. Each of these singular surfaces contains a reduced component birational to a K3 surface; one of these K3 surfaces is

. This forces

. On the other hand, when
t
collides with the special value lying under the

fiber, the limiting K3 is different; it has discriminant

. This allows us to deduce Theorem
1.3
. A second counterexample can be constructed similarly, beginning instead with Vinberg’s second most algebraic K3, which has discriminant

Preliminaries

2.1.
Transcendental Hodge structures

In this paper, the word
surface
, without any further qualification, will mean
a smooth projective surface over
\mathbb{C}
. If
S
is a surface, the Néron–Severi group
NS(S)
is the subgroup of divisor classes in
H^{2}(S,\mathbb{Z})
. The intersection pairing on
NS(S)/\textup{torsion}
is nondegenerate, so it carries a lattice structure. Its orthogonal complement
T_{S}
in
H^{2}(S,\mathbb{Z})
is called the transcendental lattice.

T_{S}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=NS(S)^{\perp}\subset H^{2}(S,\mathbb{Z}).

By Wu’s formula,
T_{S}
is always an even lattice, meaning that for every
x\in T_{S}
,
x\cdot x\in 2\mathbb{Z}
. One has
|\det(NS(S))|=|\det(T_{S})|
, and this positive integer is called the
discriminant
of
S
and is denoted by
\mathrm{disc}(S)
.

The complexification
T_{S}\otimes\mathbb{C}
contains
H^{2,0}(S,\mathbb{C})
, and it carries a Hodge filtration induced from
H^{2}(S,\mathbb{C})
. Hence,
T_{S}
(resp.
T_{S,\mathbb{Q}}
) carries a weight two
\mathbb{Z}
-Hodge structure (resp.
\mathbb{Q}
-Hodge structure) induced from
H^{2}(S,\mathbb{Z})
. We call
\phi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}T_{S}\to T_{S^{\prime}}
a Hodge isometry if
\phi
is an isometry as a lattice and preserves the Hodge filtration.

Lemma 2.1
.

The transcendental Hodge structure
T_{S,\mathbb{Q}}\subset H^{2}(S,\mathbb{Q})
is the smallest
\mathbb{Q}
sub-Hodge structure whose complexification contains
H^{2,0}(S)
. Furthermore

T_{S}=T_{S,\mathbb{Q}}\cap H^{2}(S,\mathbb{Z})/\textup{torsion}.

Proof.

By the Lefschetz
(1,1)
theorem,
NS(S)_{\mathbb{Q}}
is the maximal Hodge structure contained in
H^{1,1}(S)
. hence the first statement follows from the fact that
H^{2,0}(S)
is orthogonal to
H^{1,1}(S)
. The second statement follows from the fact that
T_{S}
is primitive in
H^{2}(S,\mathbb{Z})/\text{torsion}
.
∎

Given a dominant rational map
\pi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}S\dashrightarrow S^{\prime}
between surfaces. There is a pullback
\pi^{*}
and a pushforward
\pi_{*}
(of free abelian groups)

\pi^{*}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}T_{S^{\prime}}\to T_{S},\ \pi_{*}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}T_{S}\to T_{S^{\prime}}.

To see this, one can resolve the indeterminacy of
\pi
:

S\xleftarrow{\sigma}\tilde{S}\xrightarrow{p}S^{\prime},

where
\sigma
is birational. We can define
\pi^{*}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sigma_{*}\circ p^{*}
and
\pi_{*}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=p_{*}\sigma^{*}
on transcendental lattices. It is well-defined since the transcendental lattice is birationally invariant (both
\sigma_{*}
and
\sigma^{*}
induce isomorphisms
T_{S}\cong T_{\tilde{S}}
), and the definition is independent of the resolution
\sigma
. We remark that
\pi^{*}
and
\pi_{*}
do not preserve the intersection pairings in general.

When
p_{g}(S)=p_{g}(S^{\prime})
, then both
\pi^{*}
and
\pi_{*}
are injective and the image is a finite index sublattice. Thus there is an isomorphism of (unpolarized)
\mathbb{Q}
-Hodge structures:

T_{S}\otimes\mathbb{Q}\cong T_{S^{\prime}}\otimes\mathbb{Q}.

2.2.
Elliptic surfaces

An elliptic surface is a smooth projective surface
S
together with map
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}S\to C
to a smooth projective curve
C
, whose general fiber is an elliptic curve. We call an elliptic surface relatively minimal if there is no (
-1
)-curve contained in a fiber of
f
. If
f
has a section, then the set of sections of
f
form an abelian group
MW(S/C)
, called the Mordell–Weil group, which is finitely generated as long as
f
has a singular fiber. The rank of this group is called the Mordell–Weil rank.
There is a
\mathbb{Q}
-lattice structure on the torsion-free part of the Mordell–Weil group, called the Mordell–Weil lattice. One refers to
[
SS10
,
SS19
]
for details.

Without further qualification, we will assume that all elliptic surfaces are relatively minimal and admit a section. The possible singular fibers in an elliptic surface were classified by Kodaira, Néron, and Tate
[
SS19
, Section 5.4]
into the following types:

II,II^{*},III,III^{*},IV,IV^{*},I_{n},I_{n}^{*}\ \textup{with}\ n\geq 0.

These singular fibers occur as the Kodaira–Néron models for any one-parameter family of elliptic curves over
\Delta^{*}
. Among all the singular fiber types, we are particularly interested in the following subset featuring the superscript
∗
:

Definition 2.2
.

The singular fiber types
II^{*},III^{*},IV^{*},I_{n}^{*}
,
n\geq 0
are called
star fibers
.

They are all reducible and non-reduced; the dual graph encoding the incidence of all components not meeting the zero section
is a Dynkin diagram of type
\mathrm{E}_{8},\mathrm{E}_{7},\mathrm{E}_{6}
, and
\mathrm{D}_{4+n}
, respectively. These fiber types play an important role in the study of quadratic base change of elliptic surfaces. We record the following useful observation for later use:

Lemma 2.3
.

In each star fiber
F
, there are exactly four components with odd multiplicity, and they are disjoint.

Proof.

This can be directly verified from
[
SS19
, Theorem 5.12]
.
∎

The canonical bundle of an elliptic surface
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}S\to C
is given by

\omega_{S}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=f^{*}(\omega_{C}\otimes\mathbb{L}_{S}),

where
\mathbb{L}_{S}=f_{*}(\omega_{S/C})
is the
fundamental line bundle
of degree
\chi(S)
on
C
.

Lemma 2.4
.

The degree of fundamental line bundle of an elliptic surface satisfies

\deg\mathbb{L}_{S}=\frac{1}{12}e(S)=\frac{1}{12}\sum_{c\in\Delta}e(f^{-1}(c)),

where
e(\cdot)
denotes the topological Euler characteristic, and
\Delta
is the subset of
C
that corresponds to the singular fibers.

In particular,
\deg\mathbb{L}_{S}>0
if and only if there is at least one singular fiber.

Lemma 2.5
.

The geometric genus
p_{g}(S)
of an elliptic surface satisfies

p_{g}(S)=\deg\mathbb{L}_{S}+g(C)-1.

Remark 2.6
.

There are exactly two types of elliptic surfaces
S\to C
with
p_{g}=1
that have positive
\deg\mathbb{L}_{S}
: one has
g(C)=0
and
\deg\mathbb{L}_{S}=2
, and is an elliptic K3 surface; the other has
g(C)=1
and
\deg\mathbb{L}_{S}=1
, and is called an elliptic-elliptic surface.

An elliptic-elliptic surface
S\to C
is an elliptic surface with
p_{g}=q=1
together with a section
[
EGW24
,
GZ24
]
. Equivalently, the base curve has genus one, and the degree of the fundamental line bundle is one also. The cohomology group
H^{2}(S,\mathbb{Z})
carries a weight two Hodge structure
with Hodge numbers
h^{2,0}=h^{0,2}=1,h^{1,1}=12
.
Therefore,
S
has a unique nonzero holomorphic 2-form
\omega
up to a scaling. It vanishes along a unique fiber, which is called the canonical fiber.

Lemma 2.7
.

[
GS85
, Lemma D]
If an elliptic surface
S
has a section and at least one singular fiber, then its second cohomology
H^{2}(S,\mathbb{Z})
is torsion-free.

Shioda and Inose

A K3 surface with maximal Picard number is uniquely determined by its transcendental lattice.

3.
Quadratic base change and variations of Hodge structure

In this section, we investigate certain special base changes of elliptic surfaces, in which the degree of the fundamental line bundle is smaller than expected, and study their Hodge structures. Our main application is to construct elliptic-elliptic surfaces as rational double covers of elliptic K3 surfaces.

-Hodge structures

(7)

Proposition 8.1
.

Let
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{X}\to\Delta
be a semistable degeneration of complex projective varieties, with

smooth. If

and

Z
holds for

Proof.

Given a class
\alpha\in\ker(T-I\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}H^{n}(X_{t},\mathbb{Z})\to H^{n}(X_{t},\mathbb{Z}))
, it lifts to a class
\tilde{\alpha}\in H^{n}(\mathcal{X}^{*},\mathbb{Z})
. By the exactness of the Clemens–Schmid sequence over
\mathbb{Q}
, an integer multiple

lifts to

. Hence
r(\beta)-m\tilde{\alpha}\in\ker(s)
, so it lifts to
H^{n-1}(X_{t},\mathbb{Z})

r(\beta)=m\tilde{\alpha}\textup{ mod torsion}
, so the image
\delta(\tilde{\alpha})\in H^{n+1}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}^{*},\mathbb{Z})
is torsion.

Using excision and Lefschetz duality, we have isomorphisms

H^{n+1}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}^{*},\mathbb{Z})\cong H^{n+1}(\mathcal{X},\partial\mathcal{X},\mathbb{Z})\cong H_{n+1}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{Z}).

Since there is a deformation retract of

onto

]

[

The second assumption implies that

is zero, so
\tilde{\alpha}
lifts to

∎

The above is essentially the same argument as in

, Lemma 3.5]
and
[

Proposition 8.2
.

Let

The assumption

implies that

Lemma 8.3
.

The top dimensional cohomology of an SNC variety is torsion-free. More precisely, let
Z=\cup_{i}Z_{i}
be an SNC variety with pure dimension
n
, then

H^{2n}(Z,\mathbb{Z})\cong\oplus_{i}H^{2n}(Z_{i},\mathbb{Z})

is generated by the fundamental classes of the irreducible components.

Proof.

Let
Z_{i_{0},\ldots,i_{p}}=Z_{i_{0}}\cap\cdots\cap Z_{i_{p}}
. There is a Mayer-Vietoris spectral sequence

E_{1}^{p,q}=\bigoplus_{i_{0}<\cdots<i_{p}}H^{q}(Z_{i_{0},\ldots,i_{p}},\mathbb{Z})\Rightarrow H^{p+q}(Z,\mathbb{Z}).

However, by dimension reason,
E_{1}^{p,q}=0
unless
q\leq 2(n-p)
. Hence the only nonzero term with
p+q=2n
is

E_{1}^{0,2n}=\oplus_{i}H^{2n}(Z_{i},\mathbb{Z}),

and there is no nonzero differential out of
E_{r}^{0,2n}
for

H^{2n}(Z,\mathbb{Z})\cong E_{\infty}^{0,2n}=E_{1}^{0,2n}=\oplus_{i}H^{2n}(Z_{i},\mathbb{Z}).

∎

Since our counterexamples to the LICT

Lemma 8.4

Let

The assumption
