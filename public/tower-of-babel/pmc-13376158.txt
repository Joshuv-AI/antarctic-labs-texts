Development of Human Conjunctival Goblet Cell Segmentation Datasets to Improve Quantitation
Authors: Fredrik Andreas Fineide, Jeffrey Bair, Tor Paaske Utheim, Michael Alexander Riegler, Darlene A. Dartt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dry eye disease is an inflammatory disease of the ocular surface and one of the most common pathologies in medicine. This multifactorial disease can cause significant morbidity and visual disturbance. The ocular tear film consists of an outer lipid layer, an underlying aqueous layer and an innermost mucus layer produced by goblet cells interspersed in the conjunctiva. This inner mucus layer is vital for tear film stability, immunoregulation and ocular surface health. With dry eye disease and several other ocular surface pathologies the goblet cells can be affected, with decreased density and function. Much laboratory research is being performed on goblet cells necessitating manual counting and evaluation. This work presents the first comprehensive, publicly available dataset of semantically segmented goblet cells consisting of more than 65,000 instances. Moreover, we include versions of the dataset compatible with several state-of-the-art computer vision models and source code for training and testing. The current dataset can be used to train local models, as a basis for transfer learning on similar datasets, to streamline laboratory workflow, and thus save time and resources by reducing manual effort. Moreover, the dataset can play an important role in the development of improved computer vision algorithms in cellular detection.

Background & Summary

Stemming from multiple etiologies, dry eye disease (DED) can be characterized by hyperosmolarity and ocular surface inflammation with a resultant loss of tear film homeostasis and tear film stability1,2. This disease, despite being highly prevalent and affecting up to 50% of the population, is also one of the most underdiagnosed and poorly treated disorders in ophthalmology1,3.

Dry eye disease targets and disrupts the ocular tear film, which is reported to be 2–5.5 µm thick4. While it features a thin outer layer of lipids, the majority of the tear film is composed of the inner mucoaqueous layer. The lipid layer is predominantly produced by modified sebaceous glands situated in the upper and lower eyelids5. Transmembrane mucins expressed on corneal and conjunctival epithelial cells help ensure that the tear film spreads evenly and adheres properly across the ocular surface6–8. Goblet cells secrete large, soluble mucins that help shield the ocular surface from environmental exposure. These mucins are high-molecular-weight glycoproteins, classified as either transmembrane or secretory mucins9. Propelled by blinking, the mucin layer slides over the ocular surface towards the nasolacrimal duct, gathering pathogens, foreign bodies and debris on its way10.

Goblet cells are columnar epithelial cells found in mucosal tissues such as the conjunctiva, as well as the reproductive, gastrointestinal, and respiratory tracts10. Human goblet cells, situated among stratified-squamous epithelial cells, occur mainly as solitary cells dispersed throughout the tissue, with their highest density on the nasal side of the inferior palpebral conjunctiva11. Studies indicate that human conjunctival stem cells are bipotent, capable of differentiating into either stratified squamous epithelial cells or goblet cells. Growing evidence also suggests that goblet cells contribute to mucosal immunity by producing immunoregulatory factors and by presenting antigens from the ocular surface to phagocytic cells within the underlying stroma12.

Ocular surface disease may involve either elevated or reduced mucin levels, and tight regulation of mucin production is crucial for maintaining ocular surface homeostasis4. Reduced numbers of mucin-producing goblet cells have been reported in DED, Sjögren’s syndrome, vitamin A deficiency, after refractive and other ocular surgeries, and following the use of topical eye drops containing the preservative benzalkonium chloride10.

Although much insight concerning goblet cell structure and function has been gained, considerable basic and clinical research is still needed. In many cases the goblet cells can be marked with dyes and counted with automated cell counters. This is, however, not always possible, as the currently used antibodies and histochemical stains identify the secretory product contained only in the secretory granules. Furthermore, some experimental approaches require unstained specimens and in those cases the number of goblet cells must be counted manually. As the number of goblet cells per unit area varies over the ocular surface, the area chosen for counting must be carefully chosen and consistent or larger areas must be evaluated. Moreover, goblet cells display varying morphology and can be completely or partially layered with overlapping cytoplasmic areas, further complicating the already difficult task of differentiating the cell borders of unstained cells. This is a tedious, time-consuming and error-prone task.

We investigate, herein, if artificial intelligence (AI) and its subgroup machine learning (ML) can improve the determination of goblet cell identity and number in culture without staining or fluorescence. Artificial intelligence and ML have been acquiring scientific and clinical momentum within the medical fields, including ophthalmology13. Computer vision models are dependent on large datasets of properly labeled images to train the ML models14. Construction of medical datasets is time-consuming and requires expertise, especially concerning semantic segmentation. The multiple publicly available datasets containing images of biological cells are shown in Table 1. We have not, however, been able to discover any dataset with annotated goblet cells. For this reason, we developed the proposed Goblet Cell Segmentation (GCS) dataset (10.5281/zenodo.18517381) to help develop improved algorithms for the segmentation and counting of goblet cells15. There are several datasets containing images of various cell types and corresponding semantic segmentation masks that have been published and are summarized in Table 1.

Among previously published cellular datasets, only three datasets include cells without staining or fluorescence16–18. Moreover, one of these contain images of yeast16, and one is based on segmentation of the nuclei18. As a result, the LIVECell dataset is the only previously published dataset including whole-cell annotation without staining or fluorescence17. Several differences between LIVECell images and our own dataset exist. First, the LIVECell dataset contains images of eight different cell types while the herein proposed dataset only includes goblet cells. Most importantly, images in the LIVECell dataset have a homogenous background making the cells easily differentiable. In our GCS dataset, however, both the shapes and sizes of the cells as well as the background are very heterogeneous, obfuscating cellular boundaries. The global standard deviation of pixel intensities in the GCS dataset is 43.98, almost four times that of LIVECell (11.58), indicating significantly greater background heterogeneity. These differences make the GCS dataset the first of its kind, representing a specialized dataset of human conjunctival goblet cells. The heterogenous nature of this kind of cellular images makes training and prediction difficult. Thus, the GCS dataset will be valuable both for other goblet cell models and as a basis for transfer learning to models for different cell lines of similar appearance.

Methods

The proposed GCS dataset consists of images of cultured human goblet cells15. The images are without fluorescence or staining. The cells were grown from human conjunctival explants obtained from the Eye Bank for Sight Restoration (120 Wall Street New York, NY 10005) in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Cells were grown for 12–14 days at 37°C and 5% CO2, then photographed in phase contrast by an EVOS XL Core imaging system at 200x magnification, at Schepens Eye Research Institute, Boston, Massachusetts. The Massachusetts Eye and Ear Institutional Review Board (IRB) reviewed the study protocol and determined that the project does not involve human tissues and therefore does not constitute human subjects research. As such, formal IRB approval was not required. Conjunctival explants were obtained in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, with appropriate informed consent from all donors; all samples were anonymized prior to use. Images were chosen based on their location relative to the explant rather than for high cell purity. This approach was used to repeatedly sample representative regions of the culture and to capture inherent biological and technical variability present in primary conjunctival explants. Dataset development

First, images were annotated using a semi-automated human-in-the-loop approach with the Cellpose 2.0 graphical user interface (GUI) by the first author (FAF)19. Following the initial annotation process, all images and annotations were reviewed by the second author (JB). Approximately 17% of annotations were revised by the second author, with the main reasons for initial disagreement being placement of cell-cell junctions and cell area. Upon second revision by the first author, agreement was made in all cases.

The original images are in JPEG file format. Segmentation masks from the Cellpose GUI were exported as PNG files. The images and corresponding segmentation masks were originally large with a 2048 × 1536-pixel resolution. A Python script was written that patched the images and masks to a resolution of 256 × 256 pixels. This size was selected based on the average goblet cell diameter (25–30 µm), goblet cell density (24–2226 goblet cells/mm2) and imaging resolution (roughly 0.5 µm/pixel)20,21, ensuring that most patches contain complete cells with sufficient background context. Additionally, 256 × 256 is a standard input size for several relevant convolutional networks, enabling efficient training and compatibility with segmentation architectures such as Unet and Segment Anything Model (SAM). Furthermore, segmentation masks in PNG format are not compatible with many ML models. We therefore converted the PNG masks to You Only Look Once (YOLO) compatible text files and SAM compatible Common Objects in Context (COCO) run-length encoding (RLE) JSON format.

In the original unpatched dataset images and masks were named fbs{image_number}. In the patched dataset the images and masks were named fbs{image_number}_{patch_number}, there were 48 patches per image numbered 0–47. The masks were annotated in gray scale with data type uint16 and each pixel value >0 represented a goblet cell instance. Model development

To demonstrate the technical quality of the GCS dataset we performed several experiments with different ML models to obtain various baseline metrics. Experiments were run on both unpatched and patched datasets. The ML models included were a Unet++ from Segmentation models PyTorch22, Ultralytics YOLOv-1123 and SAM224. The UNet architecture was chosen because it is commonly used and performs well in biomedical imaging17,18,25,26. We chose YOLO models due to their effectiveness and speed in object detection27,28, and SAM2 as a foundation, promptable large language model24. Transfer learning

Training computer vision models from scratch often requires large amounts of data and prolonged training times32. To mitigate this, a model can be trained on similar data before fine-tuning the model on the dataset in question, referred to as transfer learning. In the current project, we also experimented with pretraining the models on the TissueNet dataset33,34. TissueNet was selected for being large-scale, easily accessible, including a cytoplasm channel, diversity of cell types and a predominance of 256 × 256 pixel sized images corresponding to our patched dataset. The dataset was downloaded from the official website (https://datasets.deepcell.org/) as NPZ files organized in train, val and test partitions34. A Python script was written to extract the images and masks as PNG files. The masks were extracted with the cytoplasmic channel. The train and val partitions were combined into a train_val partition for training, the test partition was used unaltered as a test set. The included models were then trained on the train_val set prior to fine-tuning on the GCS dataset.

When pretraining Unet++ on the TissueNet dataset we used an initial learning rate of 0.001 and the PyTorch StepLR learning rate scheduler with a step_size of 50 and a gamma of 0.5. The model was trained for 300 epochs. When pretraining YOLOv-11 we used the YOLO11m-seg model as a baseline model representing a compromise between speed and model complexity, a learning rate of 0.001 and trained for 300 epochs. For SAM2 we used the baseplus model with base_lr: 5e-6 and vision_lr: 3e-6 and trained for 300 epochs. Following training on the TissueNet dataset, the models were trained for 500 epochs on the GCS dataset before testing.

Class imbalance in segmentation tasks might lead to overstated performance when using pixel accuracy as a metric. This is because large background regions may dominate the calculation and mask poor performance on minority classes such as goblet cells. To mitigate this, IoU is presented were available as it provides a more balanced assessment. The strong performance of SAM2 on the GCS dataset can be attributed to its extensive pretraining on large-scale, diverse datasets. This enables robust generalization capabilities and performance in domains it was not trained on. These results, however, do not necessarily mean the model has learned goblet cell specific features as it could reflect model capacity to leverage general visual priors such as edge detection and texture cues to delineate cell boundaries.

The observed differences in model performance after pretraining on TissueNet can be attributed to resolution mismatch, variation in instance density across datasets and model architectures.

The Unet++ model improves on the unpatched dataset and to a lesser extent on the patched dataset. This difference could be a result of the model architecture with nested and dense skip connections favoring many small objects over fewer larger ones.

The YOLO models demonstrate deterioration of results with transfer learning on both datasets with the exception of mask precision on the patched dataset. On the unpatched dataset this might be due to TissueNet images consisting of fewer and relatively larger cells on the comparatively smaller images, resulting in the model missing smaller, densely packed instances. Moreover, even though resolutions match, pretraining on TissueNet led to slightly decreased mean average precision on the patched dataset. This might be due to different number of positive instances per image or differences in background. Also, because the YOLO architecture is optimized for efficiency on small inputs and real-time object detection, rather than accurate biomedical segmentation, pretraining might not yield noticeable gains.

The SAM2 model performance on the unpatched dataset was marginally decreased after transfer learning. On the patched dataset, however, we see some improved metrics. This plateau on the unpatched images and improvement on patched images could be due to the SAM2 model being heavily pretrained on natural images typically containing few, distinct instances with low instance density rather than many tightly packed smaller instances.

Usage Notes

We present here an open-source dataset containing human ocular goblet cells with corresponding semantic segmentation masks. The main objective is to provide an expertly annotated dataset as a resource to the scientific community. We believe the dataset has the potential to serve as a multidisciplinary resource. First, it is a challenge to the ML and computer vision community to improve the baseline results. This is difficult due to the heterogeneity of human goblet cells and absence of dyes and fluorescence, which makes segmentation reliant on more subtle morphological cues. Because of this, the dataset encourages the development of more robust computer vision algorithms.

Second, the dataset can be used to train or fine-tune local models in any laboratory working on human goblet cells or cells of similar size and morphology in need of counting or cell volume calculation. As such, we propose that ML models based on the GCS dataset will help to streamline laboratory workflow, save time and resources by reducing manual effort, introduce the possibility to quantify cellular volumes in addition to cell numbers and remove bias, thus significantly advancing science.

Users should be aware, however, of certain limitations. The dataset is derived from a small number of unique fields of view and was acquired using a single imaging setup. This may constrain generalizability across different tissue preparations, anatomical regions and imaging modalities on otherwise similar cells. Moreover, the donor demographic data demonstrates that 3/4 donors where male and 2/4 donors were of African American descent, biological factors that may influence cell morphology and density. Additionally, when using the patched dataset, the image-level split is recommended for benchmarking to avoid information leakage. We encourage future users to consider these factors when interpreting results, adapting models or using the dataset for transfer learning.
