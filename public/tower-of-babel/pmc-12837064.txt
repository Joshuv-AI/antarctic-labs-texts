Prevalence of autoantibody responses in COVID-19 patients: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Dan Xu, Minjie Zhang, Ying Zhao, Weiwei Liang, Yi Zhang, Lijiang Fang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAn infection with severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus (SARS-CoV-2) may significantly contribute to the pathogenesis of autoimmune rheumatic diseases; interactions between the virus and defence mechanisms may promote the development of autoimmune processes. Studies have reported elevated levels of autoimmune antibodies in patients with Coronavirus Disease-19 (COVID-19), however, the exact prevalence remains poorly undocumented. This study aims to evaluate the prevalence of autoantibodies in COVID-19 patients compared to unaffected individuals.MethodsElectronic searches were conducted using the Cochrane Library, PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Chinese Biological Medicine Database (CBM), China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), WANFANG, and Chinese Weipu (VIP). The case-control studies investigating the presence of autoantibodies in the serum of COVID-19 patients and control subjects published were included in this meta-analysis. The search covered November 2019, to December 2025. Studies were rigorously screened based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Quality assessment was performed using a modified version of the Newcastle-Ottawa scale (NOS). The odds ratios (ORs) for autoantibody seropositivity were calculated using RevMan 5.3. Sensitivity analysis was conducted to evaluate the stability results, and publication bias was assessed using Egger’s test and funnel plot.ResultsA total of 17 studies involving 1652 COVID-19 patients and 1455 control subjects met eligibility criteria for inclusion in the meta-analysis. The overall OR for antinuclear antibodies (ANAs) was 2.90 [95% confidence interval (CI): 1.36–6.18], for anti-cardiolipin antibodies (ACAs) was 3.34 (95% CI: 2.14–5.21), for anti-β2-glycoprotein 1 antibodies (anti-β2GP1) was 1.87 (95% CI: 1.00–3.49) and that for anti-cytoplasmic neutrophil antibodies (ANCAs) was 8.49 (95% CI: 3.38–21.33). Among the 11 studies assessing ANAs, the quality varied widely, resulting in considerable heterogeneity in the meta-analysis results. Heterogeneity across studies on ANAs may partially stem from differences in assay manufacturers as well as variations in the working cut-off dilution used for IIF. Egger’s test reports indicated statistically significant publication bias among the included studies on ANAs.ConclusionsThis study suggests a higher seroprevalence of autoantibodies, including ANAs, ACAs, anti-β2GP1, and ANCAs in COVID-19 patients compared to control subjects. The findings identified a possible association between SARS-CoV-2 infection and autoantibody positivity, contributing to the understanding of COVID-19’s role in autoimmune processes.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12407-y.

Introduction

Infection with severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus (SARS-CoV-2) often leads to varying degrees of illness, with Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19) potentially causing immune system dysregulation and the development of autoimmune phenomena [1]. The consequences of such immune dysregulation range from the production of autoantibodies to the onset of rheumatic autoimmune disease. A growing body of research has explored the association between COVID-19 infection and autoimmune conditions. SARS-CoV-2 infection manifests in a variety of clinical manifestations, which may result from either direct viral tissue damage or indirect effects mediated by the host’s antiviral immune response. As COVID-19 may act as a predisposing factor for autoreactivity and contribute to the mechanisms that initiate autoimmunity, exploring this association is of substantial interest. Previous studies have demonstrated a higher seroprevalence of autoantibodies in COVID-19 patients, suggesting a possible link between COVID-19 infection and the development of autoimmunity [2]. Early reports indicated that antinuclear antibodies (ANAs) were detected in approximately a quarter of COVID-19 patients when assessed using indirect immunofluorescence (IIF). Furthermore, anti-phospholipid antibodies (aPLs) have been identified in a significant of COVID-19 patients, potentially contributing to thromboembolic complications [3].

Autoantibodies, a hallmark of most but not all autoimmune disorders, have been identified as a symbol of autoimmune disorders and are frequently considered a clinical marker of these disorders. Autoimmune diseases are characterized by chronic, systemic, excessive immune activation and inflammation, affecting almost all body tissues [4]. Similar to other complex diseases, autoimmune disorders often develop over several years. Monitoring autoantibody-positive COVID-19 patients and preventing their progression to inflammatory autoimmune conditions are quite necessary. Biomarkers predicting this transition would have a significant clinical impact, as SARS-CoV-2 infection may accelerate progression from asymptomatic autoimmunity to inflammatory autoimmune conditions. This study aims to inspire further research to define and target autoimmune pre-disease. Here, we conducted a meta-analysis to evaluate published evidence regarding the influence of SARS-CoV-2 infection on critical environmental factors that trigger autoimmune pathogenesis. Similar to other viral infections, SARS-CoV-2 exhibits features associated with an immune-mediated mechanism. The immune response appears to contribute to disease severity with direct tissue damage caused by the virus. Recent findings suggested that both viral effect and immune-mediated mechanisms play roles in the pathogenesis of severe acute respiratory syndrome-related coronavirus infection [5]. Shah et al. proposed that SARS-CoV-2, like in many other viral infections, could lead to various rheumatological and autoimmune manifestations through mechanisms such as molecular mimicry, bystander killing, epitope spreading, viral persistence, and the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) [6]. Similarly, Vahabi et al. suggested that molecular mimicry allows SARS-CoV-2 to disrupt self-tolerance in a host’s immune system. Additionally, the hyperactivation of the immune system during attempts to eliminate the virus may trigger an autoimmune response [7]. In this meta-analysis, we systematically reviewed and analyzed the current data on the prevalence of autoantibodies in COVID-19 patients compared to unaffected individuals. Our study aims to uncover significant immunological properties of several autoantibodies, which could be valuable in future clinical studies aimed at managing this pandemic.

Materials and methods

Search strategy

Relevant Studies were identified through a comprehensive search of multiple databases, including the Cochrane Library, PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Chinese Biological Medicine Database (CBM), China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), WANFANG and Chinese Weipu (VIP) databases. The search terms used were (“SARS-CoV-2” OR “severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2” OR “COVID” OR “COVID-19” OR “2019-nCoV” OR “coronavirus disease 2019 virus” OR “2019 novel coronavirus”) AND (“autoantibodies” OR “antinuclear antibodies” OR “anti-cytoplasmic neutrophil antibodies” OR “antiphospholipid antibodies” OR “anti-cytoplasmic neutrophil antibodies” OR “ANA” OR “ANCA” OR “ACA”). The literature search was confined to articles published either in Chinese or English, covering the period from November 2019 to December 2025.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We included studies that examined the autoantibodies in COVID-19 patients compared to control subjects. Meta-analysis will be conducted if 3 or more studies are available for each outcome. We defined our eligibility criteria based on the PICO framework: (P) Population: COVID-19 patients (diagnosis of COVID-19 using reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) as the reference standard, supplemented by clinical symptoms and imaging diagnosis). (I) Intervention/Exposure: Autoantibody measures. (C) Control: Pre-COVID-19 blood samples from healthy blood donors or blood samples from individuals with confirmed non-COVID-19 infection. (O) Outcome: The presence of at least one autoantibody was assessed.

Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) The studies in which COVID-19 patients were diagnosed without at least one positive RT-PCR test being carried out. (2) The studies that quantified the autoantibody and results were presented as mean ± standard deviation/median (IQR, interquartile range) or presented results only as a graph without numerical data. (3) Utilized in-house developed protein arrays instead of a commercial assay for autoantibody measurement.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two authors (Yi Zhang and Ying Zhao) independently viewed and assessed articles for inclusion. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (Minjie Zhang). Data from eligible studies were extracted using a modified version of the Newcastle-Ottawa scale (NOS), covering patient selection, performance of the index test, performance of the reference test, and flow and timing (for risk of bias only). Extracted data included the first author’s name, publication year, source/type, sample size, gender ratio, age, type of autoantibodies, manufacturer, testing method, working dilution, and Cut-off values.

Statistical analysis

The meta-analysis was performed by Review Manager 5.3 (Cochrane Collaboration). The odds ratios (ORs) of seropositivity for each autoantibody were calculated. The Cochrane-Q statistic and the I2 statistic were used to assess heterogeneity between studies. I2 values ≤50% were considered indicative of low heterogeneity, whereas I2 values > 50% indicated high heterogeneity. Statistical significance for heterogeneity was defined as p < 0.05 for the Cochrane-Q test. In case of significant heterogeneity, a random effects model was applied for data synthesis; otherwise, a fixed-effect model was used. Subgroup analysis compared ORs of ANAs to identify potential sources of heterogeneity. Additionally, sensitivity analysis was also conducted to assess the impact of each individual study on the overall results. The Egger’s test and funnel plot were employed to evaluate potential publication bias.

Results

Study characteristics

A PRISMA flow chart Fig. 1 illustrates the selection process. Our search identified 1,430 records after excluding duplicates, a total of 674 articles were identified. Following a full-text review, 29 studies were assessed for eligibility, and 17 articles were included in the systematic review [8–24]. These studies encompassed data from 1652 COVID-19 patients and 1455 control subjects. Quality assessment

The 17 included studies were non-randomized, and assessed using NOS. The quality assessment checklist comprises three sections: participant selection, group comparability and exposure assessment. The criteria assign a maximum of four stars for the selection domain, two stars for the comparability domain, and four stars for the exposure domain. Based on quality rating criteria, nine studies were rated as “High quality”, seven as “Moderate quality”, and one as “Low quality.” Detailed quality assessment scores for each included study are presented in Table S2. Any Discrepancies between the reviewers in the risk of bias assessment were resolved through discussion, and a third senior reviewer was consulted when consensus could not initially be reached.

Data synthesis and meta-analysis

11 studies provided data on ANA seropositivity, encompassing a total of 1133 COVID-19 cases and 1161 controls. The overall percentage of COVID-19 patients testing positive for ANAs was higher compared to controls (24.80% and 11.54%, respectively). Due to significant heterogeneity, a random-effects model was applied. The overall OR of 2.90 (95% CI:1.36–6.18, Z = 2.75, p < 0.01), indicating a significantly higher seroprevalence of ANAs in COVID-19 patients compared to controls (Fig. 2). However, significant high heterogeneity was observed across the 11 studies with I2 = 85% (p < 0.01).Fig. 2Random effects meta-analysis of seroprevalence of ANAs between COVID-19 patients and controls

Random effects meta-analysis of seroprevalence of ANAs between COVID-19 patients and controls

Eight studies examined ACA seroprevalence. The prevalence of ACAs was markedly higher in COVID-19 patients compared to controls (17.33% and 8.66%, respectively). The overall OR from these studies was 3.34 (95% CI: 2.14–5.21, Z = 5.33, p < 0.01) (Fig. 3). Significant heterogeneity was detected (I2 = 51%, p = 0.06). Sensitivity analysis was conducted to identify the potential source of heterogeneity. Omitting a single study did not significantly affect the overall OR. However, omitting the study of “Zheng Bokun 2022” reduced heterogeneity among seven studies for ACAs (I2 = 24%, p = 0.25). The overall OR from these seven studies was 2.38 (95% CI: 1.43–3.97, Z = 3.33, p < 0.01).Fig. 3Fixed effects meta-analysis of seroprevalence of ACAs between COVID-19 patients and controls

Fixed effects meta-analysis of seroprevalence of ACAs between COVID-19 patients and controls

Eight studies measured anti-β2GP1, involving a total of 756 COVID-19 patients and 406 controls. The overall percentage of COVID-19 patients positive for anti-β2GP1 was higher than controls (5.03% and 3.45%, respectively). While Blickstein et al. [9] and Karahan et al. [10] observed a low prevalence of anti-β2GP1 in COVID-19 patients compared to controls, Anaya et al. [8] reported a significantly higher prevalence in COVID-19 patients. Meta-analysis revealed an overall OR of 1.87 (95% CI: 1.00–3.49, Z = 1.96, p = 0.05), indicating no statistically significant difference in the seroprevalence of anti-β2GP1 (Fig. 4). Omitting individual studies significantly affected the overall OR (OR = 1.21, 95% CI: 0.56–2.63, Z = 0.49, p = 0.63). “Anaya 2021” was identified as the primary source of the heterogeneity, and its exclusion eliminated heterogeneity for anti-β2GP1 (I2 = 0%, p = 0.60, omitting “Anaya 2021”).Fig. 4Fixed effects meta-analysis of seroprevalence of anti-β2GP1 between COVID-19 patients and controls

Fixed effects meta-analysis of seroprevalence of anti-β2GP1 between COVID-19 patients and controls

6 studies reported data on anti-cytoplasmic neutrophil antibody (ANCAs) seropositivity, including 640 COVID-19 cases and 439 controls. The overall percentage of COVID-19 patients positive for ANCAs was notably higher than in controls (8.75% versus 0.91%, respectively). All 6 studies showed a higher ANCAs seroprevalence in COVID-19 patients. The meta-analysis showed an overall OR = 8.49 (95% CI: 3.38–21.33, Z = 4.55, p < 0.01), with no significant heterogeneity observed between studies (I2 = 0%, p = 0.68) (Fig. 5).Fig. 5Fixed effects meta-analysis of seroprevalence of ANCAs between COVID-19 patients and controls

Subgroup analysis

A subgroup analysis was conducted based on the manufacturer (categorized into the “EUROIMMUN group”, “Inova Diagnostics group”, and “Non-IIF group”) and by the cut-off dilution of IIF (classified into “1:80 dilution group” and “1:100 dilution group”) to identify the potential source of heterogeneity among the 11 studies on ANAs. The analysis revealed that Inova Diagnostics group exhibited higher heterogeneity (I2 = 71%, p = 0.03) compared to EUROIMMUN group (I2 = 0%, p = 0.49) with no heterogeneity observed in the EUROIMMUN subgroup (Fig. 6). However, substantial heterogeneity was observed in the subgroup non-IIF group (I2 = 70%, p = 0.04). Similarly, subgroup analysis based on the cut-off dilution showed that 1:80 dilution group (I2 = 89%, p < 0.01) had a significantly higher heterogeneity compared to the 1:100 dilution group (I2 = 0%, p = 0.45). These findings suggest that the observed heterogeneity could partially be attributed to variations in ANA assays produced by different manufacturers and differences in the working cut-off dilution for screening of ANAs via IIF. Furthermore, a subgroup analysis (divided into “with AID group” and “without AID group”) was performed. The results showed that the overall percentage of COVID-19 patients without AID group (OR = 4.06, 95% CI: 0.99–16.65) positive for ANAs was higher compared to COVID-19 patients with AID group (OR = 1.78, 95% CI: 0.97–3.27) (Fig. 6).Fig. 6Subgroup analysis forest plot of or with 95% CIs for ANAs

Subgroup analysis forest plot of or with 95% CIs for ANAs

Meta-regression analysis

Due to the high heterogeneity observed among the 11 studies reporting ANA prevalence, we performed a meta-regression analysis to explore potential sources of variability. A univariate meta-regression was performed using the same stratifying variables applied in the subgroup analyses. The analysis examined the association between the ANA prevalence in COVID-19 patients and several potential moderators, including the manufacturer (b = 1.41, 95% CI −0.09 to 2.91, p = 0.062), the cut-off dilution of IIF (b = 0.85, 95% CI −0.62 to 2.32, p = 0.223), the autoimmune disease history (b = 1.21, 95% CI −0.09 to 2.50, p = 0.064), and the ANAs testing method (b = 0.91, 95% CI −0.28 to 2.10, p = 0.119). The results indicated that none of the individual moderators accounted for a significant proportion of the heterogeneity across the included studies.

Risk of bias assessment

A funnel plot and Egger’s test were performed to evaluate publication bias. Significant publication bias was detected among the studies reporting ANAs (t = 4, p < 0.01). In contrast, no significant publication bias was observed for ACAs (t = 0.26, p = 0.803) (Fig. 7B), anti-β2GP1 (t = −0.56, p = 0.604) (Fig. 7C) and ANCAs (t = −0.19, p = 0.859) (Fig. 7D). The funnel plot indicates the absence of statistically significant bias among the included studies except for ANAs (Fig. 7A).Fig. 7Funnel plot for spotting publication bias among the included studies

Discussion

In the five years since the onset of the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic, several remarkable studies have found a significant occurrence of autoantibodies related to systemic autoimmune diseases in COVID-19 patients [25]. However, it remains uncertain whether a causal relationship exists between SARS-CoV-2 infection and the development of autoimmunity. Viral infections have long been recognized as a critical environmental factor affecting the production of autoantibodies with various viruses implicated in the pathogenesis of autoimmune diseases. ANAs, a diverse group of autoantibodies, play an indispensable role in the pathogenesis and diagnosis of various rheumatological disorders. Numerous studies have confirmed the presence of autoantibodies related to systemic autoimmune diseases in COVID-19 patients. The current study demonstrated SARS-CoV-2, like other viral infections, such as Epstein-Barr virus, acts as an important environmental factors that trigger the pathogenesis of autoimmune diseases [26]. If the relationship between SARS-CoV-2 and autoimmunity were causal, the prevalence of autoantibodies should be higher in COVID-19 patients compared to controls. Our review identified a significantly higher seroprevalence of ANAs in COVID-19 patients compared to controls (OR = 2.90, 95% CI: 1.36–6.18, p < 0.01). However, despite the overall percentage of ANAs in COVID-19 patients was significantly higher, no significant differences in ANA positivity were observed in the studies conducted by Pascolini et al. [14] and Taeschler et al. [18]. Conversely, the studies by Anaya et al. [8] and Kozłowski et al. [11] reported a significantly lower seroprevalence of ANAs in COVID-19 patients compared to controls. In Anaya et al.‘s study, the majority of COVID-19 patients received antibiotics (97.5%), anticoagulation with heparins (95%), and corticosteroids (90%). COVID-19 patients are affected by multiple pathways of autoimmunity, highlighting the role of immunomodulatory therapies in hospitalized individuals. The observed lower frequency of ANAs may be attributable to the early administration of these therapies, which could mitigate the inflammatory response and consequently reduce ANA positivity in this context. Participants who were COVID-19 convalescents for at least 6 months (with asymptomatic, mild-to-moderate disease) were enrolled in the study of Kozłowski et al. Their results indicated that COVID-19 with mild to moderate symptoms in a generally healthy population does not induce the development of ANA.

To explore the sources of heterogeneity, subgroup analysis was conducted based on the key factors to evaluate the source of heterogeneity among the 11 studies of ANAs. However, many original articles lacked sufficient information on age and gender, precluding subgroup analysis for these variables. The timing for the serological autoantibody testing following COVID-19 infection was identified as an important factor in understanding the persistence of autoantibodies. While ten studies enrolled hospitalized patients with acute COVID-19, only a few examined the persistence of autoantibodies over time. Further research involving larger patient cohorts and long-term autoantibody measurement is needed to clarify the persistence of autoantibodies after COVID-19. Lee et al. [27] reported that a significant proportion of COVID-19 patients with oxygen demand showed the presence of autoantibodies, many of which persisted for several months after symptom onset. Whether these autoantibodies contributed to long-term sequelae in COVID-19 patients requires further investigation. Copple et al. [28] highlighted variations in the sensitivity and specificity of different assays. The sensitivity of ELISA ranged from 90% to 97% compared to 80% for IIF in SLE serum samples. The ELISA demonstrated specificities ranging from 36% to 94%, whereas the IIF exhibited a specificity of 99%. These variations depend on the specific assay and protocol used by the laboratories. ANAs IIF testing is influenced by several variables, including the characteristics of the secondary antibody, the substrates provided on slides from different manufacturers, and the cut-off dilution used. Subgroup analysis by the manufacturer revealed that the Inova Diagnostics group (I2 = 71%, p = 0.03) exhibited higher heterogeneity compared to EUROIMMUN group (I2 = 0%, p = 0.49) where no heterogeneity was observed. Subgroup analysis by the cut-off dilution of IIF revealed that ANAs IIF testing conducted and reported at 1:80 dilution exhibited higher heterogeneity, where studies utilizing a 1:100 dilution showed low heterogeneity. Additionally, variations in the time of sample collection and potential changes in the presence of autoantibodies may have contributed to the observed heterogeneity. Although significant progress has been made in studying autoantibodies following COVID-19, few have examined the persistence over time. Further research involving larger, well-defined patient cohorts and long-term follow-up is necessary to elucidate the persistence of autoantibodies and to investigate various factors contributing to heterogeneity in these studies.

Patients with COVID-19 who had a previous clinical history of autoimmune disease may exhibit differences in the prevalence of autoantibodies. In this study, ten original articles recruited COVID-19 patients who explicitly excluded individuals with a history of autoimmune diseases. In three original articles, COVID-19 patients with a prior history of autoimmune diseases comprise a small proportion of the cohort. However, no detailed description of the autoimmune disease history of the participants was provided in the Pascolini’s study [14]. To analyze the impact of the autoimmune disease history on the prevalence of autoantibodies, a subgroup analysis was performed, dividing participants into “with AID group” and “without AID group” to evaluate the discrepancies in ANA seropositivity. The subgroup analysis results showed that the overall percentage of COVID-19 patients without AID group positive for ANAs was higher compared to COVID-19 patients with AID group. Due to the exclusion of autoimmune disease history in most original articles and the limited number of studies that included such patients, this meta-analysis could not definitely determine whether a history of autoimmune disease affects the prevalence of autoantibodies in COVID-19 patients. Infection with SARS-CoV-2 can significantly impact the human immune system. Interactions between the virus and host defense mechanisms may promote the development of autoimmune processes, manifested in the synthesis of ANAs. Numerous studies have reported a strong association between acute COVID-19 and the presence of ANAs, with many investigating their potential role in predicting disease severity and clinical outcomes. However, substantial variability exists across these studies in terms of COVID-19 patient characteristics and control group composition. This heterogeneity can be attributed to several factors, including age, sex, timing of blood sample collection, assay manufacturer, and specific patient subgroups (e.g., asymptomatic cases, acute COVID-19 patients, six-month follow-up cases, and symptomatic convalescents). These factors likely account for the observed residual heterogeneity across subgroups.

Thrombosis is a cardinal feature of antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) and critical COVID-19 infection. Although the mechanisms contributing to thrombosis in APS and COVID-19 are relatively similar, this remains an open subject. The mechanisms underlying the development of aPLs in COVID-19 were associated with neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) release, reduced oxygenation efficiency, and disease severity [29]. Taken together, patients with COVID-19 who were positive for aPLs suggest a potential role in the autoimmune thrombosis inflammatory disease and COVID-19-associated thrombosis [30]. However, the relationship between newly generated aPLs during SARS-CoV-2 infection and thrombosis remains uncertain. Regular monitoring of aPLs positivity over time is recommended to further investigate the relation between the aPLs occurrence and thrombotic events in COVID-19. We have investigated the presence of aPLs in COVID-19 patients, this analysis showed a significantly higher seroprevalence of ACAs in COVID-19 patients compared to controls (OR = 3.34, 95% CI: 2.14–5.21, p < 0.01). However, no statistically significant difference was observed in the seroprevalence of anti-β2GP1 antibodies. The literature presents divergent findings regarding the prevalence of aPLs in COVID-19 patients. Borghi et al. [31] reported a low prevalence of aPLs in COVID-19 patients and found no association between aPLs and major thrombotic events. Similarly, Zhang et al. [32] cautioned that the presence of aPLs in COVID-19 patients might be incidental. Another important consideration is that most of the studies suggest that aPLs in COVID-19 patients could be transient. Indeed, aPLs may lead to thrombotic events that are challenging to differentiate from other causes of multifocal thrombosis in critically ill COVID-19 patients, such as disseminated intravascular coagulation. Consistent with this, Pavoni et al. [33] observed no significant association between aPLs positivity and thrombosis in critically ill COVID-19 patients. While the prevalence of aPLs in COVID-19 patients varies across studies, other factors are likely responsible for the rate of thromboembolic events in COVID-19. Overall, aPLs in COVID-19 patients seem to not be predictive of vascular events. However, a high prevalence of aPLs possibly linked to the presence of thrombotic events must be carefully evaluated.

ANCAs were another group of autoantibodies recognized by their presence against the neutrophil’s proteins, including proteinase-3 (c-ANCA), and myeloperoxidase (p-ANCA). ANCA-associated vasculitides (AAVs) are systemic autoimmune diseases. This analysis showed a higher prevalence of ANCAs in COVID-19 patients (OR = 8.49, 95% CI: 3.38–21.33, Z = 4.55, p < 0.01). The development of ANCAs has been reported previously [34]. The mechanisms which SARS-CoV-2 infection can act as ‘trigger factor’ for vasculitis may be related to the formation of NETs [7]. The occurrence of AAVs-like symptoms in COVID-19 patients and the fact that AAVs can mimic COVID-19 in terms of pulmonary involvement highlight the ability of SARS-CoV-2 to trigger autoimmunity phenomenon, which can lead to the onset or flare-up of AAVs.

This meta-analysis has several limitations that should be acknowledged. Notably, the absence of protocol registration may increase the risk of bias in study selection and introduce greater analytical flexibility. The included studies differed in terms of method, manufacturer, and period of blood collection. Moreover, many studies had small sample sizes and did not match participants for age and gender. Another limitation was the considerable heterogeneity observed for ANAs. Subgroup analysis by manufacturer was performed to identify possible sources of heterogeneity among the 11 studies on ANAs. Several studies have suggested a correlation between autoantibodies detected in patients and the severity of the COVID-19 disease [8, 14, 17, 19]. It was also found that patients with autoantibodies had a worse prognosis, leading to more severe consequences compared to patients without autoantibodies [35]. Previous literature on the increased seroprevalence of autoantibodies after SARS-CoV-2 infection is largely limited to case reports and a few cohort studies. In a prospective study that included participants across the spectrum of acute COVID-19 severity and systematically identified and confirmed both COVID-19 and systemic autoimmune rheumatic diseases (SARDs), a substantial impact on SARDs after acute COVID-19 was found [36]. Another retrospective observational multicenter cohort study found that 6% of patients with systemic lupus erythematosus experienced disease flare thought to be triggered by COVID-19, SARS-CoV-2 infection determined autoimmune flares in a small number of patients [37]. It remains difficult to provide an accurate estimate of increased SARDs after infection or to prove a causal relationship between COVID-19 and increased SARDs. The long-term humoral autoantibody responses following SARS-CoV-2 infections in patients with SARDs should be studied to better understand the development of autoimmunity in these patients. Further prospective studies involving participants with SARDs after COVID-19 are needed to establish causality between SARS-CoV-2 infection and autoimmunity. More research needed to recognize COVID-19-associated autoimmune manifestations, which will increase awareness of the value of autoantibody testing in these patients. Although this study confirmed a higher seroprevalence of autoantibodies in heterogeneous COVID-19 patients . further investigations were warranted to determine the exact mechanisms underlying the autoantibody responses.
