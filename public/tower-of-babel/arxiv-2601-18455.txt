1
Introduction

As a cornerstone of modern combinatorics, extremal graph theory is concerned with investigating the limits of structural properties in graphs and hypergraphs under various constraints. Given a family
{\mathcal{F}}
of (hyper)graphs, a (hyper)graph is called
{\mathcal{F}}
-free if it contains no copy of any member of
{\mathcal{F}}
.
One of the central problems in extremal graph theory is the Turán problem: for a family
{\mathcal{F}}
of forbidden subgraphs, determine the maximum number
\mathrm{ex}(n,{\mathcal{F}})
of (hyper)edges in an
n
-vertex
{\mathcal{F}}
-free (hyper)graph.
This problem is well-understood for complete graphs, starting with Mantel’s theorem for
K_{3}

[
17
]
and Turán’s theorem for
K_{t}

[
20
]
, and asymptotically for general graphs by the Erdős–Stone–Simonovits theorem
[
12
,
13
]
.

A (hyper)graph is
{\mathcal{F}}
-saturated
if it is
{\mathcal{F}}
-free but the addition of any new (hyper)edge creates a copy of some element of
{\mathcal{F}}
.
In contrast to Turán problem, the saturation problem asks for the minimum number of edges in an
{\mathcal{F}}
-saturated (hyper)graph.
In particular, the
r
-uniform saturation number of hypergraph
{\mathcal{F}}
is denoted by

\mathrm{sat}_{r}(n,{\mathcal{F}})=\min\left\{|E({\mathcal{H}})|:|V({\mathcal{H}})|=n,{\mathcal{H}}\text{ is }r\text{-uniform and }{\mathcal{F}}\text{-saturated}\right\}.

Saturation numbers and their variants have wide applications in combinatorics and are also relevant to other areas, such as bootstrap percolation
[
1
]
and topology
[
5
,
19
]
.

The study of saturation numbers began with graphs was initiated independently by Zykov in 1949
[
22
]
and by Erdős, Hajnal, and Moon in 1964
[
11
]
, determining
\mathrm{sat}_{2}(n,K_{p})=\binom{n}{2}-\binom{n-p+2}{2}
and characterizing the unique extremal graph.
Later, Kászonyi and Tuza
[
16
]
established the linear upper bound
\mathrm{sat}_{2}(n,\mathscr{F})=O(n)
for any graph family
\mathscr{F}
and solved the problem for several specific families (e.g., stars, paths, matchings).
For further graph saturation results, we refer readers to
[
2
,
6
,
21
]
and subsequent works.

For hypergraphs, Bollobás
[
3
]
generalized the complete graph result to the
r
-uniform complete hypergraph
K_{p}^{r}
in 1965, proving an exact formula
\mathrm{sat}_{r}(n,K_{p}^{r})=\binom{n}{r}-\binom{n-p+r}{r}
and showing the uniqueness of extremal hypergraph.
Pikhurko
[
18
]
later showed the general upper bound
\mathrm{sat}_{r}(n,\mathscr{F})=O(n^{r-1})
for any finite family
\mathscr{F}
of
r
-uniform hypergraphs. Additional exact results for specific hypergraph families appear in
[
4
,
6
,
8
,
10
]
.

Determining exact saturation numbers remains challenging even for graphs, and is considerably harder in the hypergraph setting, where only a few precise values are known.
A particularly active area of research within this field concerns
Berge hypergraphs
, a notion introduced by Gerbner and Palmer
[
14
]
that has attracted considerable interest.
Given a graph
F
, a hypergraph
{\mathcal{H}}
is called a
Berge-
F
if there exists a
bijection

\phi:E(F)\to E({\mathcal{H}})
such that
\{u,v\}\subseteq\phi(uv)
for every edge
uv\in E(F)
.
The saturation number for Berge-
F
was first systematically studied by English, Gordon, Graber, Methuku, and Sullivan
[
7
]
.
They established several bounds and exact results when
F
is a path, cycle, or matching, and conjectured that
\mathrm{sat}_{r}(n,\text{Berge-}F)=O(n)
for any fixed graph
F
.
Recently, English, Kritschgau, Nahvi, and Sprangel
[
9
]
determined the asymptotic saturation number for Berge cliques, showing that

\mathrm{sat}_{r}(n,\text{Berge-}K_{p})\sim\frac{p-2}{r-1}\,n.

Despite this asymptotic progress, exact values are known only for the smallest case
p=3
. This gap naturally leads to the problem of determining the exact saturation number
\mathrm{sat}_{r}(n,\text{Berge-}K_{p})
for
p\geq 4
.
Based on an extremal construction, English et al. posed the following concrete problem.

Problem 1.1

(
[
9
]
)
.

Determine
\mathrm{sat}_{3}(n,\text{Berge-}K_{4})
exactly. In particular, is
\mathrm{sat}_{3}(n,\text{Berge-}K_{4})=n
when
n
is odd?

In this paper, we resolve the problem for all integers
n
with
5\leq n\leq 8
and
n\geq 96
. Using parallel computation (see
Appendix A
), we determine all extremal hypergraphs for
n=5,6,7,8
, and for
n\geq 96
we exhibit many non-isomorphic families of extremal hypergraphs. Our main result is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

For
n=5,7,8
and
n\geq 96
, we have

\mathrm{sat}_{3}(n,\mathrm{Berge}\text{-}K_{4})=n,

while
\mathrm{sat}_{3}(6,\mathrm{Berge}\text{-}K_{4})=5
.
Moreover, there exist many non-isomorphic extremal hypergraphs.

We remark that the constant
96
is not optimal; a more careful analysis could lower it, though the proof would become more tedious. Nevertheless, the remaining gap cannot be easily closed with current methods.

1.1
Definitions and notations

Let
{\mathcal{H}}=(V({\mathcal{H}}),E({\mathcal{H}}))
be a hypergraph, we denote by
v({\mathcal{H}})
and
e({\mathcal{H}})
the number of vertices and hyperedges of
{\mathcal{H}}
, respectively. The minimum degree of
{\mathcal{H}}
is denoted by
\delta({\mathcal{H}})
.
A hypergraph is called an
r
-uniform hypergraph (or
r
-graph for short) if all its hyperedges have cardinality
r
. Assume that
{\mathcal{H}}
is a
3
-graph, and let
v\in V({\mathcal{H}})
be a vertex, we set
N^{d}_{{\mathcal{H}}}(v)=\{(v_{i},v_{j}):vv_{i}v_{j}\in E({\mathcal{H}})\}
,
N_{{\mathcal{H}}}(v)=\left\{v_{i}:\{v,v_{i}\}\subset e\mbox{ for some }e\in E({\mathcal{H}})\right\}
, and
d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(v)=|\{e\in E({\mathcal{H}}):v\in e\}|
. For a pair of vertices
(u,v)\in V(H)\times V(H)
, we set
N_{{\mathcal{H}}}(u,v)=\{w\in V({\mathcal{H}}):uvw\in E({\mathcal{H}})\}
and
d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(u,v)=|N_{{\mathcal{H}}}(u,v)|
.

Recall that
{\mathcal{H}}
is a Berge-
F
if there exists a bijection
\phi:E(F)\to E({\mathcal{H}})
such that
\{u,v\}\subseteq\phi(uv)
for every edge
uv\in E(F)
. The
core
of
{\mathcal{H}}
is the set of vertices that correspond to the vertices of
F
.
For
u,v\in V({\mathcal{H}})
, we say that the pair
(u,v)
is
\mathbf{good}
if
{\mathcal{H}}+uv
creates a new Berge-
K_{4}
; otherwise, the pair is
\mathbf{bad}
.
Consequently, if
(u,v)
is good, then the core of the newly formed Berge-
K_{4}
must contain both
u
and
v
.

For an integer
\ell\geq r
, the
r
-uniform tight cycle
of length
\ell
, denoted
{\mathcal{C}}_{\ell}^{r}
, is the
r
-graph with vertex set
V({\mathcal{C}}^{r}_{\ell})=\{v_{1},v_{2},\cdots,v_{\ell}\}
and hyperedge set
E({\mathcal{C}}^{r}_{\ell})=\left\{v_{i}v_{i+1}\cdots v_{i+r-1}:i\in[\ell]\right\}
, where indices are taken modulo
\ell
.

|e_{1}\cap e_{2}|=|\{v_{1},v_{2}\}|=2
.

We first claim that
v_{2}\not\in e_{3}
. Indeed, if not, then we have
e_{1}\cap e_{2}\cap e_{3}=\{v_{1},v_{2}\}
. Since
(v_{1},v_{2})
is good, Fact
3.1
(i) implies that
d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(v_{1},v_{2})\leq 2
, which contradicts
d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(v_{1},v_{2})\geq 3
. Hence
|e_{1}\cap e_{3}|\leq 2
.

In the following of the proof, we proceed by analyzing the sizes of the intersections
|e_{1}\cap e_{3}|
and
|e_{2}\cap e_{3}|
.
If
|e_{1}\cap e_{3}|=|e_{2}\cap e_{3}|=1
, then there exist two vertices
v_{5},v_{6}\in V({\mathcal{H}})\setminus\{v_{1},v_{2},v_{3},v_{4}\}
such that
e_{2}=v_{1}v_{2}v_{5}
and
e_{3}=v_{1}v_{4}v_{6}
. A contradiction follows easily, since
(v_{4},v_{6})
is good and the three edges containing
v_{1}
are
e_{1}
,
e_{2}
, and
e_{3}
.

If
|e_{1}\cap e_{3}|=|e_{2}\cap e_{3}|=2
, then we have
e_{2}=v_{1}v_{2}v_{4}
and
e_{3}=v_{1}v_{3}v_{4}
. Since
(v_{2},v_{4})
is good and
\{v_{2},v_{4}\}=e_{2}\cap e_{4}
, the core of the new Berge-
K_{4}
in
{\mathcal{H}}+v_{2}v_{4}
must contain
v_{1}
and the vertex in
e_{4}\setminus\{v_{2},v_{4}\}
. As
N_{{\mathcal{H}}}(v_{1})=\{v_{2},v_{3},v_{4}\}
, the core must be
\{v_{1},v_{2},v_{3},v_{4}\}
, which implies
e_{4}=v_{2}v_{3}v_{4}
. Similarly, by
(v_{3},v_{4})
is good, we have that
e_{5}=v_{2}v_{3}v_{4}=e_{4}
, which is a contradiction.

We only need to consider the cases where either
|e_{1}\cap e_{3}|=2
,
|e_{2}\cap e_{3}|=1
, or
|e_{1}\cap e_{3}|=1
,
|e_{2}\cap e_{3}|=2
.
Without loss of generality, we may assume that
|e_{1}\cap e_{3}|=2
,
|e_{2}\cap e_{3}|=1
and there is vertex
v_{5}\in E({\mathcal{H}})
such that
e_{2}=v_{1}v_{2}v_{5}
and
e_{3}=v_{1}v_{3}v_{4}
.
Let
E_{1}=E({\mathcal{H}})\setminus\{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}\}
. If there are three hyperedges in
E_{1}
that form a Berge-
K_{3}
with core in
\{\{v_{2},v_{3},v_{4}\},\{v_{2},v_{3},v_{5}\},\{v_{2},v_{4},v_{5}\},\{v_{3},v_{4},v_{5}\}\}
, then one can easily find a Berge-
K_{4}
in
{\mathcal{H}}
, which is a contradiction. Henceforth, we assume that no such triple of hyperedges in
E_{1}
exists. Since
(v_{1},v_{2})
is good and
v_{1}v_{2}v_{3},v_{1}v_{2}v_{5}\in E({\mathcal{H}})
, there are two hyperedges
e_{6},e_{7}\in E_{1}
such that
v_{2},v_{5}\in e_{6}
and
v_{3},v_{5}\in e_{7}
.
Since
(v_{3},v_{4})
is good and
N_{{\mathcal{H}}}(v_{1})=\{v_{2},v_{3},v_{4},v_{5}\}
, the core of the new Berge-
K_{4}
should be
\{v_{3},v_{4},v_{1},v_{2}\}
or
\{v_{3},v_{4},v_{1},v_{5}\}
, which implies that there are two hyperedges
e_{8},e_{9}\in E_{1}
such that
v_{3},v_{2}\in e_{8}
and
v_{4},v_{2}\in e_{9}
or
v_{3},v_{5}\in e_{8}
and
v_{4},v_{5}\in e_{9}
.
If
v_{3},v_{2}\in e_{8}
, we claim that
e_{8}\in\{e_{4},e_{5}\}
and
e_{8}\in\{e_{6},e_{7}\}
, this implies that
e_{8}=v_{2}v_{3}v_{4}=v_{2}v_{3}v_{5}
, which is a contradiction. Indeed, if
e_{8}\notin\{e_{4},e_{5}\},
then
e_{4},e_{5},e_{8}\in E_{1}
can form a Berge-
K_{3}
with core
\{v_{2},v_{3},v_{4}\}
, and if
e_{8}\notin\{e_{6},e_{7}\},
then
e_{6},e_{7},e_{8}\in E_{1}
can form a Berge-
K_{3}
with core
\{v_{2},v_{3},v_{5}\}
, both will induce contradictions.
Thus we have
v_{3},v_{5}\in e_{8}
and
v_{4},v_{5}\in e_{9}
. Since
E_{1}
does not contains a Berge-
K_{3}
with core in
\{\{v_{2},v_{4},v_{5}\},\{v_{3},v_{4},v_{5}\}\}
,
e_{4},e_{6},e_{9}
can not be three distinct hyperedges. Hence we have
v_{2}v_{4}v_{5}\in E_{1}
. We also have that
e_{5},e_{8},e_{9}
can not be three distinct hyperedges, which implies
v_{3}v_{4}v_{5}\in E_{1}
. Hence
{\mathcal{H}}[\{v_{1},v_{2},v_{3},v_{4},v_{5}\}]
contains a
{\mathcal{C}}^{3}_{5}
with hyperedges
\{v_{1}v_{2}v_{5},v_{2}v_{4}v_{5},v_{3}v_{4}v_{5},v_{1}v_{3}v_{4},v_{1}v_{2}v_{3}\}
, which completes the proof of Lemma
3.2
.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
n\geq 7
be an integer and
{\mathcal{H}}
be a Berge-
K_{4}
-saturated
3
-graph on
n
vertices with the minimum number of hyperedges, then we have
\delta({\mathcal{H}})\geq 2
.

Proof.

By Constructions
2.1
and
2.2
, we have
e({\mathcal{H}})\leq n
.

We first prove that
\delta({\mathcal{H}})\geq 1
. Suppose for the contrary that there is a vertex
v_{0}\in V({\mathcal{H}})
with
d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(v_{0})=0
, then
v_{0}v_{i}v_{j}\notin E({\mathcal{H}})
implies that
(v_{i},v_{j})
is good for every pair
(v_{i},v_{j})\in(V({\mathcal{H}})\setminus\{v_{0}\})\times(V({\mathcal{H}})\setminus\{v_{0}\})
. Let
{\mathcal{H}}_{0}={\mathcal{H}}-\{v_{0}\}
. Since every pair
(v_{i},v_{j})\in V({\mathcal{H}}_{0})\times V({\mathcal{H}}_{0})
is good, we have
\delta({\mathcal{H}}_{0})\geq 2
.
Moreover, for any vertex
v_{i}\in V({\mathcal{H}}_{0})
, let
v_{i}v_{j}v_{k}\in E({\mathcal{H}})
be a hyperedge incident to
v_{i}
. Since
(v_{j},v_{k})
is good in
{\mathcal{H}}
, the core of any Berge-
K_{4}
in
{\mathcal{H}}+v_{j}v_{k}
must contain
v_{i}
, which implies
d_{{\mathcal{H}}_{0}}(v_{i})=d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(v_{i})\geq 3
, and hence
\delta({\mathcal{H}}_{0})\geq 3
.
We claim that
\delta({\mathcal{H}}_{0})=3
. Indeed, if
\delta({\mathcal{H}}_{0})\geq 4
, then we have
e({\mathcal{H}})=e({\mathcal{H}}_{0})\geq\left\lceil\frac{4(n-1)}{3}\right\rceil\geq n+1
for
n\geq 5
, which contradicts that
e({\mathcal{H}})\leq n
.

Let
v_{1}\in V({\mathcal{H}}_{0})
be the vertex with
d_{{\mathcal{H}}_{0}}(v_{1})=3
. Since any pair
(v_{i},v_{j})\in V({\mathcal{H}}_{0})\times V({\mathcal{H}}_{0})
is good, Lemma
3.2
implies that there are four vertices
v_{2},v_{3},v_{4},v_{5}\in V({\mathcal{H}})
such that
v_{1}v_{2}v_{3},v_{2}v_{3}v_{4}
,
v_{3}v_{4}v_{5},v_{4}v_{5}v_{1},v_{1}v_{2}v_{5}\in E({\mathcal{H}})
. In other words,
v_{1},v_{2},v_{3},v_{4},v_{5}
form a
{\mathcal{C}}^{3}_{5}
.
If
n=7
, then there is a vertex
v_{6}\in V({\mathcal{H}}_{0})\setminus\{v_{1},v_{2},v_{3},v_{4},v_{5}\}
. Since
d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(v_{6})\geq\delta({\mathcal{H}}_{0})\geq 3
, there are at least three hyperedges in
E({\mathcal{H}})\setminus\{v_{1}v_{2}v_{3},v_{2}v_{3}v_{4},v_{3}v_{4}v_{5},v_{4}v_{5}v_{1},v_{1}v_{2}v_{5}\}
, thus
e({\mathcal{H}})\geq 5+3=n+1
, a contradiction.
If
n=8
, then there are two vertices
v_{6},v_{7}\in V({\mathcal{H}}_{0})\setminus\{v_{1},v_{2},v_{3},v_{4},v_{5}\}
. Since
(v_{6},v_{7})
is good, by Fact
3.1
(i), we have
d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(v_{6},v_{7})\leq 2
. Thus
d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(v_{6}),d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(v_{7})\geq\delta({\mathcal{H}}_{0})\geq 3
implies that there are at least four hyperedges that contain
v_{6}
or
v_{7}
. Hence
e({\mathcal{H}})\geq 5+4=n+1
, a contradiction.
If
n=9
, then there are three vertices
v_{6},v_{7},v_{8}\in V({\mathcal{H}}_{0})\setminus\{v_{1},v_{2},v_{3},v_{4},v_{5}\}
. Since
(v_{6},v_{7})
,
(v_{7},v_{8})
and
(v_{6},v_{8})
are good, by Fact
3.1
(i), we have
d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(v_{6},v_{7}),d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(v_{7},v_{8}),d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(v_{6},v_{8})\leq 2
. Moreover, if
d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(v_{6},v_{7})=1
, then there are at least five hyperedges that contain
v_{6}
or
v_{7}
, which implies that
e({\mathcal{H}})\geq 5+5=10=n+1
, a contradiction. Thus
d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(v_{6},v_{7})=d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(v_{7},v_{8})=d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(v_{6},v_{8})=2
and there are at most four hyperedges which contain at least one vertex in
\{v_{6},v_{7},v_{8}\}
and moreover, we have
v_{6}v_{7}v_{8}\in E({\mathcal{H}})
. Otherwise there are at least six distinct hyperedges which contain exact two vertices in
\{v_{6},v_{7},v_{8}\}
, a contradiction. Since
(v_{6},v_{7})
is good and
v_{6}v_{7}v_{8}\in E({\mathcal{H}})
, the core of Berge-
K_{4}
must contain
v_{6}
,
v_{7}
and
v_{8}
, which means there are at least five hyperedges which contain at least one vertex in
\{v_{6},v_{7},v_{8}\}
, a contradiction.
If
n\geq 10
, by the previous analysis we know that
(v_{1},v_{i})
is good for any
v_{i}\in V({\mathcal{H}}_{0})\setminus\{v_{1},v_{2},v_{3},v_{4},v_{5}\}
. Since
d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(v_{1})=3
and
v_{1}v_{2}v_{3},v_{4}v_{5}v_{1},v_{1}v_{2}v_{5}\in E({\mathcal{H}})
, the core of any new Berge-
K_{4}
in
{\mathcal{H}}+v_{1}v_{i}
must be
\{v_{1},v_{i},v_{j_{1}},v_{j_{2}}\}
for some
v_{j_{1}},v_{j_{2}}\in\{v_{2},v_{3},v_{4},v_{5}\}
. Thus there are two hyperedges
e_{1},e_{2}\in E({\mathcal{H}})
such that
v_{i},v_{j_{1}}\in e_{1}
and
v_{i},v_{j_{2}}\in e_{2}
, which means that there are at least

|V({\mathcal{H}}\setminus\{v_{0},v_{1},v_{2},v_{3},v_{4},v_{5}\})|=n-6

distinct hyperedges outside the
{\mathcal{C}}^{3}_{5}
with at least one vertex in
\{v_{2},v_{3},v_{4},v_{5}\}
.
In particular, we have
\sum_{j=2}^{5}d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(v_{j})\geq\sum_{j=2}^{5}d_{{\mathcal{C}}^{3}_{5}}(v_{j})+n-6\geq 3|\{v_{2},v_{3},v_{4},v_{5}\}|+n-6
, which implies that
\sum_{v\in V(H_{0})}d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(v)\geq 3|V({\mathcal{H}}_{0})|+n-6
. Hence
e({\mathcal{H}})\geq\left\lceil\frac{3(n-1)+n-6}{3}\right\rceil\geq n+1
, where the last inequality holds when
n\geq 10
, which a contradiction to
e({\mathcal{H}})\leq n
.

Next, we prove
\delta({\mathcal{H}})\geq 2
. Suppose for the contrary that there is a vertex
u_{1}\in V({\mathcal{H}})
with
d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(u_{1})=1
, let
u_{1}u_{2}u_{3}\in E({\mathcal{H}})
be the hyperedge incident to
u_{1}
. Clearly,
(u_{1},u_{i})
is bad for every
u_{i}\in V({\mathcal{H}})\setminus\{u_{1}\}
. Thus for any pair
(u_{i},u_{j})\neq(u_{2},u_{3})
with
u_{i},u_{j}\in V({\mathcal{H}})\setminus\{u_{1}\}
,
u_{1}u_{i}u_{j}\notin E({\mathcal{H}})
implies that
(u_{i},u_{j})
is good, and hence
d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(u_{i})\geq 2
for every
u_{i}\in V({\mathcal{H}})\setminus\{u_{1}\}
.
Moreover, for every
u_{i}\in V({\mathcal{H}})\setminus\{u_{1}\}
, there is at least one pair
(u_{j},u_{k})\neq(u_{2},u_{3})
such that
u_{i}u_{j}u_{k}\in E({\mathcal{H}})
. Since
(u_{j},u_{k})
is good, it follows that
d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(u_{i})\geq 3
.
Furthermore, there are at most two vertices have degree more than
3
, otherwise, if there are three vertices having degree at least
4
, then
e({\mathcal{H}})\geq\left\lceil\frac{1+3(n-1-3)+3\times 4}{3}\right\rceil=\left\lceil\frac{3n+1}{3}\right\rceil=n+1
, which contradicts that
e({\mathcal{H}})\leq n
.
Similarly, we have that for any two vertices

,
d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(u_{i})+d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(u_{j})\leq 8
. Otherwise,
e({\mathcal{H}})\geq\left\lceil\frac{1+3(n-1-2)+9}{3}\right\rceil=n+1
, which also contradicts our assumption that
e({\mathcal{H}})\leq n
.

Claim 3.4
.

There is no 3-uniform tight 5-cycle in
{\mathcal{H}}
.

Proof.

Suppose that there is a 3-uniform tight 5-cycle
{\mathcal{C}}
with
V({\mathcal{C}})=\{w_{1},w_{2},w_{3},w_{4},w_{5}\}
and
E({\mathcal{C}})=\{w_{1}w_{2}w_{3},w_{2}w_{3}w_{4},w_{3}w_{4}w_{5},w_{4}w_{5}w_{1},w_{5}w_{1}w_{2}\}
in
{\mathcal{H}}
. Since there are at most two vertices have degree more than 3, we may assume that
d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(w_{2})=d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(w_{3})=d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(w_{4})=3
.

If
u_{2},u_{3}\in\{w_{1},w_{2},w_{3},w_{4},w_{5}\}
, then
{\mathcal{C}}+u_{1}u_{2}u_{3}
contains a Berge-
K_{4}
, a contradiction to
{\mathcal{H}}
is Berge-
K_{4}
-free.
Thus
|V({\mathcal{C}})\cap\{u_{2},u_{3}\}|\leq 1
.

If
V({\mathcal{C}})\cap\{u_{2},u_{3}\}=\emptyset
, the previous analysis tells us that
(w_{2},u_{2})
and
(w_{2},u_{3})
are good. Since
N_{{\mathcal{H}}}(w_{2})=\{w_{1},w_{3},w_{4},w_{5}\}
, the core of any Berge-
K_{4}
in
{\mathcal{H}}+w_{2}u_{2}
must composed of
w_{2},u_{2}
and two vertices from
\{w_{1},w_{3},w_{4},w_{5}\}
. Assume that a core is
\{w_{2},u_{2},w_{i_{1}},w_{i_{2}}\}
for some
w_{i_{1}},w_{i_{2}}\in\{w_{1},w_{3},w_{4},w_{5}\}
. Then there are two hyperedges
e_{3},e_{4}
such that
w_{i_{1}},u_{2}\in e_{3}
and
w_{i_{2}},u_{2}\in e_{4}
, thus
d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(w_{i_{1}}),d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(w_{i_{2}})\geq 4
. It implies that
(w_{i_{1}},w_{i_{2}})=(w_{1},w_{5})
.
On the other hand, since
(w_{2},u_{3})
is also good, there are two hyperedges
e_{5},e_{6}
such that
u_{3},w_{1}\in e_{5}
and
u_{3},w_{5}\in e_{6}
. Then we claim that
e_{3}=e_{5}=w_{1}u_{2}u_{3}
,
e_{4}=e_{6}=w_{5}u_{2}u_{3}
, and
d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(w_{1})=d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(w_{5})=4
, and any vertex in

has degree
3
. Otherwise, one can easily obtain that
3e({\mathcal{H}})\geq 3(n-1)+1+3=3n+1
, which implies that
e({\mathcal{H}})\geq n+1
, a contradiction to
e({\mathcal{H}})\leq n
.
Furthermore, since
(w_{1},u_{2})
is good and

, the core of any new Berge-
K_{4}
in

must be
\{w_{1},u_{2},u_{3},w_{5}\}
. But
w_{5}u_{2}u_{3}
can not be mapped to both
w_{5}u_{2}
and
w_{5}u_{3}
, a contradiction.

Therefore,
|V({\mathcal{C}})\cap\{u_{2},u_{3}\}|=1
. We may assume that
u_{2}\in V({\mathcal{C}})
, and clearly we have
d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(u_{2})\geq 4
. Moreover,
d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(w_{2})=d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(w_{3})=d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(w_{4})=3
implies that none of
w_{2}
,
w_{3}
, or
w_{4}
can be adjacent to
u_{3}
. Without loss of generality, we assume
w_{1}=u_{2}
. Since
(w_{2},u_{3})
is good, the core of the Berge-
K_{4}
in
{\mathcal{H}}+w_{2}u_{3}
must contains
w_{1}=u_{2}
and
w_{5}
. Hence
d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(w_{5})=d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(u_{2})=4
and there is a vertex

such that

. On the other hand, since
(w_{5},u_{3})
is good and

, the core of any Berge-
K_{4}
in

must be
\{u_{3},w_{5},x,u_{2}\}
. By considering

, we have

. Together with

, it follows that

, which contradicts

. Thus, there is no

in

.
∎

Recall that
(u_{i},u_{j})
is good for any pair
(u_{i},u_{j})\neq(u_{2},u_{3})
with
u_{i},u_{j}\in V({\mathcal{H}})\setminus\{u_{1}\}
, thus if there is a vertex
w\in V({\mathcal{H}})\setminus\{u_{1},u_{2},u_{3}\}
such that

and

, then every pair

is good, and
(w,v_{k})
is good for every

. Hence Lemma
3.2
implies that there is a

in
{\mathcal{H}}
. In the following, we prove that there is a such vertex in

, which is a contradiction to Claim
3.4
.
As shown before, we have
d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(u_{i})\geq 3
for
u_{i}\in V({\mathcal{H}})\setminus\{u_{1}\}
, and the number of vertices with degree more than

is at most
2
. Moreover, we have

, which implies
d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(u_{2},u_{3})\leq 4
.
If
d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(u_{2},u_{3})=4
, then by

, there is a vertex
u_{7}
with

and

, a contradiction.
If

, then for

, there is a vertex

such that

and

, a contradiction. For

, suppose that

and assume that

, then one can easily obtain a contradiction in the case where

. Thus we assume

. In this case, it is easy to see that

, which implies that

, a contradiction.
If

, then by

, there is a vertex

such that

and

, a contradiction.
This completes the proof of Lemma
3.3
.
∎

3.2
Proof of the lower bound for
n\geq 96

Equipped with the tools above, we now prove the lower bound. Let
n\geq 96
be an integer and
{\mathcal{H}}
be an
n
-vertex
3
-uniform Berge-
K_{4}
-saturated hypergraph with the minimum number of edges.

We define the following vertex partition:
X=\{v\in V(\mathcal{H}):d_{\mathcal{H}}(v)\geq 3\}
,
A=\{v\in V(\mathcal{H})\setminus X:\mbox{every edge containing }v\mbox{ intersects X}\}
and
B=V(\mathcal{H})\setminus(X\cup A)
.
By Lemma
3.3
,
\delta({\mathcal{H}})\geq 2
, thus every vertex in
A\cup B
has degree exactly
2
. Moreover, by the definition of
A
, there is no hyperedge in
{\mathcal{H}}[A]
.
The proof proceeds in two steps.
First we prove that
B
is empty.
Then we perform a case analysis according to the types of hyperedges between
X
and
A
to get the desired lower bound of
e({\mathcal{H}})
.

Lemma 3.5
.

B=\emptyset
.

Proof.

Suppose for the contrary that there is a vertex

(ii), we have
(v,v_{1})
,
(v,v_{2})

(v_{1},v_{2})

(v_{1},u)
and
(v_{2},u)
are bad for every

. Hence for every

{\mathcal{H}}
is Berge-
K_{4}
-saturated implies that

∎

Note that Lemma

. If

and we are done. So we may assume that

All hyperedges between

and

can be divided into two types:

Type 1
consists of those containing exactly two vertices from

and one vertex from

Type 2
consists of those containing exactly one vertex from

and two vertices from

Consider a vertex
a_{1}\in A
such that the two hyperedges containing
a_{1}
, say
e_{1}
and

Then
(e_{1},e_{2})
must fall into one of the following three patterns:

(a)\ e_{1}=a_{1}a_{2}x_{1}\ \text{and}\ e_{2}=a_{1}a_{2}x_{2};(b)\ e_{1}=a_{1}a_{2}x_{1}\ \text{and}\ e_{2}=a_{1}a_{3}x_{1};(c)\ e_{1}=a_{1}a_{2}x_{1}\ \text{and}\ e_{2}=a_{1}a_{3}x_{2},

where
a_{2},a_{3}\in A
and
x_{1},x_{2}\in X
.

The next lemma shows that whenever a vertex
a_{1}\in A
has both of its incident hyperedges of Type 1, we may restrict our attention to Case (c).

Lemma 3.6
.

Suppose there is a vertex
a_{1}\in A
such that both of its incident hyperedges are of Type 1.
If Case (a) occurs, then
e({\mathcal{H}})\leq n.
Moreover, Case (b) can never occur.

Proof.

For the first statement, if Case (a) occurs, we may assume there are vertices
a_{2}\in A
,

3.1
(ii), since

(a_{1},a_{2})
,
(a_{1},x_{1})
, and
(a_{1},x_{2})

(a_{1},v)
or
(a_{2},v)
is good. If
(a_{1},v)
(resp.
(a_{2},v)
) is good, then the core of any new Berge-
K_{4}
in
{\mathcal{H}}+a_{1}v
(resp.
{\mathcal{H}}+a_{2}v
) must be
\{a_{1},v,x_{1},x_{2}\}
(resp.
\{a_{2},v,x_{1},x_{2}\}

,
x_{2},v\in e_{v}^{2}

and

2

and
x_{2}

If

x_{1}
and

Combining with
3e({\mathcal{H}})=\left(\sum_{x\in X\setminus\{x_{1},x_{2}\}}d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(x)\right)+d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(x_{1})+d_{{\mathcal{H}}}(x_{2})+2|A|

Since

(a_{1},x_{1})
and
(a_{1},x_{2})
are bad implies that
(x_{1},x_{2})
is good. And the core of every new Berge-
K_{4}

.

For the second statement, if Case (b) occurs, we may assume that there are vertices

By Fact

5
Concluding Remarks

In this paper, we have proved that
\mathrm{sat}_{3}(n,\text{Berge-}K_{4})=n
for
n=5,7,8
and
n\geq 96
, while
\mathrm{sat}_{3}(6,\text{Berge-}K_{4})=5
.
The constant
96
in our result could be reduced through a more detailed case analysis in the proof, especially when handling the case there is a pair of vertices in
A
is good. However, pushing this down to
n=9
and thereby settling the problem for all
n
appears to require substantially new ideas.
We conjecture that, in fact,
\mathrm{sat}_{3}(n,\text{Berge-}K_{4})=n
holds for every
n\geq 7
.

We have also developed an algorithmic framework for generating
k
-uniform Berge-
K_{\ell}
-saturated hypergraphs on a given number of vertices. This allowed us to determine the exact saturation numbers and to enumerate all extremal hypergraphs for
n=5,6,7,8
. The same approach can be applied to study saturation numbers for other uniformity parameters
k
and clique sizes
\ell
.

Finally, we observed that many extremal hypergraphs for small
n
can be extended to larger extremal hypergraphs by attaching the small configuration
\mathcal{T}
. This suggests a possible recursive structure: for sufficiently large
n
, every extremal hypergraph may be obtained by adding copies of
\mathcal{T}
to an extremal hypergraph on fewer vertices.
