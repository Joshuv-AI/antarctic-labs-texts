Facility-level determinants of infection prevention and control monitoring in Somalia: evidence from the 2024 Harmonized Health Facility Assessment
Authors: Abdikarim Abdi Adam, Mohamed Hassan Mohamed, Nor Haji Osman, Felix Emeka Anyiam, Kassim Abdi Jimale, Yahye Sheikh Abdulle Hassan, Shafie Abdulkadir Hassan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSomalia lacks empirical data on routine infection prevention and control (IPC) monitoring at the health-facility level, despite ongoing gaps in IPC worldwide. Reducing healthcare-associated infections, preventing antibiotic resistance, and safeguarding healthcare personnel, especially in fragile health systems, all depend on effective IPC systems. This study examines IPC monitoring practices and associated facility-level determinants in Somalia using nationally representative data from the 2024 Harmonized Health Facility Assessment (HHFA).Methods1,219 health facilities were evaluated using a cross-sectional analytical approach. Data were collected using the standardized WHO. Harmonized Health Facility Assessment (HHFA) instrument, including facility-reported evaluations of standard IPC monitoring procedures using structured questionnaire items. The data were verified using field supervision and digital quality standards. To investigate relationships between IPC monitoring and facility characteristics, descriptive statistics and chi-square tests were applied, with a significance level of p < 0.05. ResultsRoutine IPC monitoring based on standardized monitoring approaches in the preceding six months was reported in only 6.6% of facilities. While there was no significant variation by the state level, monitoring varied considerably by managing authority (p = 0.047) and facility type (p < 0.001). Primary health units, private clinics, TB centers, and privately run facilities demonstrated the lowest levels of monitoring performance, whereas higher-level facilities and those run by the government or non-governmental organizations showed higher monitoring levels. There was no significant correlation found between facility ownership and IPC monitoring.ConclusionsThe IPC system in Somalia is still limited by managerial, structural, and capacity-related issues. It is crucial to prioritize resources for lower-tier and privately run facilities, strengthen governance, and standardize audit-feedback systems. To lower healthcare-associated infections and support antimicrobial resistance control, these findings provide practical evidence for national IPC scale-up, targeted oversight, and policy. Building capacity in privately run, primary-level facilities needs to be a top priority for the national IPC scale-up.Clinical trial registrationNot applicable.

Introduction

Organizational, individual, and resource-related factors affect IPC monitoring and compliance [1–6]. Higher IPC compliance was linked to good IPC knowledge, training accessibility, a sufficient supply of PPE and IPC materials, a manageable workload, and less job stress, social recognition at work, appropriate working hours (less than eight hours per day), and no substance misuse [6]. Furthermore, compliance was predicted by institutional support (leadership, management backing), critical care work experience, and perception/attitude toward IPC [7]. In addition, important IPC monitoring factors include the presence of active IPC committees, leadership support and prioritization, PPE and IPC supplies (supply chain) availability, regular auditing and monitoring, and employee training [8]. Furthermore, better IPC adherence was associated with in-service training, access to IPC rules and guidelines, operational IPC committees, and the availability of necessary materials [9].In contrast, organizational and structural flaws, excessive workloads, imprecise or poorly communicated guidelines, inadequate or ineffective training, and a lack of control and oversight tools [10].The two main cornerstones of successful IPC programs are monitoring and audit-feedback [11–13].Strong infection prevention and control (IPC) programs prevent avoidable patient harm, safeguard healthcare personnel, slow the spread of antimicrobial resistance (AMR), and improve outbreak preparedness in fragile settings [14, 15]. IPC is essential to safe, effective healthcare. According to recent global evaluations, well-executed IPC interventions can significantly lower healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) and the morbidity and death that go along with them [16]. Furthermore, such interventions can lessen the burden of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) and safeguard the capacity of the health system during epidemics [17]. Globally, infection prevention and control (IPC) is still essential for patient safety. In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where the cost of healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) is disproportionately high, IPC is especially urgent. A recent meta-analysis of point-prevalence studies conducted throughout Africa revealed that the pooled prevalence of HAIs was substantially higher than in high-income settings, highlighting the region’s ongoing deficiencies in IPC infrastructure and practice [18].Likewise, only 15.2% of all healthcare institutions assessed satisfied the basic IPC requirements, including low compliance in low-income countries, according to the World Health Organization’s (WHO) first worldwide assessment utilizing the Infection Prevention and Control Assessment Framework (IPCAF) [19]. These regional and global IPC deficiencies offer a crucial backdrop for Somalia, as the country’s weak health system and scant empirical data support a more thorough analysis of routine IPC monitoring [20]. A recent national analysis of Somalia’s HHFA 2022–2023 data reported that only 18.46% of healthcare facilities had undergone accreditation and concluded that accreditation and quality assurance systems remain weak and poorly institutionalized, highlighting broader gaps in facility-level regulation, monitoring, and quality improvement [21]. The average IPCAF score in a nationally representative survey of 307 healthcare facilities was just 136/800, classified as “inadequate,” with particularly poor scores in the areas of multimodal methods, monitoring/audit feedback, and IPC program governance [20]. In African contexts, interventions to enhance IPC have produced positive, if inconsistent, outcomes. Multimodal techniques (e.g., combining training, audit/feedback, and system reform) consistently produced decreases in HAIs or improvements in hand-hygiene compliance, according to a comprehensive assessment of IPC interventions across African healthcare facilities [16]. Despite significant evidence that IPC and hand-hygiene interventions are highly cost-effective and scalable, implementation and monitoring remain inconsistent, especially in low-resource and fragile environments. However, a critical evaluation of the variables that might account for these interventions’ success in certain contexts but failure in others would, however, enhance the conversation. Effectiveness, for instance, may be impacted by variations in local implementation settings, personnel training, supervision, leadership support, and resource availability. By incorporating this viewpoint, the precise research need that the current study seeks to fill would become clearer [22]. Wide variations in national and facility-level IPC capacity are documented in the World Health Organization’s IPC reports, which were first released in 2022 and updated globally in 2024. The reports stress that monitoring, including the use of standardized frameworks such as the WHO IPC Assessment Framework and the Hand Hygiene Self-Assessment Framework, is essential for improvement because it enables targeted investments, performance feedback, and accountability [17]. Hand hygiene is a fundamental, evidence-based IPC practice. Meta-analyses and implementation studies demonstrate that multimodal hand-hygiene programs can significantly reduce preventable illnesses, and that one of the most successful aspects of these programs is direct monitoring with feedback. However, many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) still have low hand hygiene compliance and limited routine monitoring, which makes it difficult to translate guideline recommendations into long-term behavior change and infection reduction [23]. IPC implementation is hampered by particular structural obstacles in fragile and conflict-affected states, such as poor governance, disruptions to supply chains (for example, for consumables like alcohol-based hand rub), a lack of qualified personnel, and disjointed facility management models. In fragile health systems, routine and consistent monitoring is crucial for finding implementation gaps, assessing differences among healthcare facilities, and generating data to direct focused corrective activities. The adoption and usage of standardized IPC frameworks for continuous monitoring and quality improvement are significantly predicted by facility-level governance and ownership (public vs. private, NGO-run, or mixed), according to recent research [24]. These difficulties and the pressing need for trustworthy, facility-level data are exemplified by Somalia. Over the past five years, there has been an acceleration of national efforts to strengthen public health functions and adopt AMR and IPC goals. However, facility-level assessments reveal ongoing gaps in routine IPC monitoring and hand-hygiene surveillance across regions and facility types. Low overall IPC preparation, uneven adoption of standardized monitoring frameworks, and distinct differences by region, facility type, and management are all revealed by recent IPCAF-style and HHFA analyses from Somalia; these findings indicate specific goals for program and policy intervention [20]. Studying the factors that influence IPC monitoring at the state and facility levels is valuable from a research and policy standpoint for three reasons. First, facilities that routinely employ a standardized framework to monitor IPC and hand hygiene are more likely to have the leadership, resources, and data-driven procedures required to lower HAIs and manage AMR. Monitoring provides a quantifiable proxy for effective IPC systems. Second, multilevel analyses that connect monitoring practices to facility ownership, management, and geographic factors yield actionable evidence for targeted investments (e.g., giving priority to supervisory visits, strengthening the supply chain, or providing training in areas or facility types that are falling behind). Third, by identifying modifiable system-level obstacles to routine surveillance and feedback, a crucial step in fulfilling WHO recommendations and the Somalia National Action Plan on AMR, such evaluations directly influence national IPC scale-up plans and donor priorities [17]. Evidence about the factors influencing IPC monitoring in Somalia at the facility level is scarce. In order to close this gap, this study examines factors that influenced IPC monitoring during the previous six months using HHFA data, paying close attention to facility type, ownership, management, and regional context. The results are meant to guide specific and useful policy measures to improve IPC, lower illnesses linked to healthcare, and aid in the management of antibiotic resistance in Somalia and other fragile settings [17].Monitoring, auditing, and feedback were identified as a major problem in a recent study that evaluated IPC fundamental components in Guinea using WHO’s IPCAF framework [25]. This study aimed to assess the facility and state-level factors associated with IPC monitoring in Somalia using the Harmonized Health Assessment Data 2024. This study offers practical insights to improve hand hygiene compliance, fortify IPC systems, and raise overall patient safety by examining infrastructure, human resources, training, and organizational practices. The results are intended to assist international partners, health system planners, and policymakers in developing focused initiatives to lower HAIs and support resilient healthcare delivery in Somalia.

Methods

Study design and participants

The 2024 Harmonized Health Facility Assessment (HHFA), a standardized nationwide survey created by the World Health Organization (WHO), provided secondary data for this study’s cross-sectional analytical design. All facilities in the 2024 HHFA sample frame that met the survey eligibility criteria and were successfully assessed during data collection were included in the study. Hospitals, clinics, primary health units, health centers, and other governmental, private, and community-based service delivery locations were among them. All qualified facilities that were successfully surveyed in the HHFA are referred to in this study as “healthcare facilities assessed.” The term “IPC monitoring” describes the evaluation of infection prevention and control procedures using a specified framework or monitoring methodology as recorded in the survey instrument.

Sampling design

The HHFA used a stratified random sampling design, with the Ministry of Health master facility list serving as the sampling frame. In the first stage, health facilities were selected using simple random sampling from the national Ministry of Health facility registration, ensuring that each facility had an equal chance of being chosen. Stratification was implemented at the state level in the second step, treating the Banadir region and each federal member state as distinct strata. To guarantee sufficient representation of facilities across all strata, disproportionate allocation was employed. Facilities were further categorized by facility type, ownership, and location (urban/rural) in order to further capture variance across important health system characteristics. Within each stratum, facilities were then chosen at random. Facilities that were discovered to be inoperable or unavailable during field verification were not included. There were 1,219 facilities in the final analytical sample: Benadir (n = 209), Galmudug (n = 146), Hirshabelle (n = 118), Jubaland (n = 160), Puntland (n = 316), and South West State (n = 270).

Data collection procedure and measurements

Under the direction of the Ministry of Health and WHO, trained field teams used standardized HHFA instruments to collect data between March and August of 2024. Indicators of the availability of IPC guidelines/SOPs, IPC focal persons or committees, staff IPC training, hand hygiene materials, PPE, cleaning and disinfection supplies, sterilization/decontamination capacity, healthcare waste management practices, and environmental cleaning/monitoring systems were among the variables used in this study from the IPC module of the HHFA. Direct observation, structured interviews, and record review were used to collect data. At the time of the survey, IPC monitoring was defined as whether or not a health facility reported using a specified framework to monitor infection prevention and control throughout the previous six months. A binary outcome (Yes/No) was applied to this variable.

Data quality assurance

To improve the validity and reliability of the data, a number of quality assurance procedures were put into place. Prior to data collection, supervisors and data collectors had standardized training on field protocols, interview techniques, ethics, and HHFA tools. Supervisors performed spot checks and back checks in addition to daily inspections for consistency and completeness throughout fieldwork. The digital data-gathering technology incorporated real-time validation algorithms to minimize entry errors. Prior to analysis, the Ministry of Health worked with WHO and technical partners to clean and validate the dataset following data collection. Data consistency and reliability were strengthened by the use of standardized instruments, consistent training, and structured supervision. Before the survey, pilot testing of the HHFA tools was carried out.

Data analysis

Whether a health facility reported adopting a particular framework for IPC monitoring over the preceding six months (Yes/No) was the main outcome variable examined. The explanatory variables, including state, facility type, ownership category, and management authority, were the primary facility-level characteristics analyzed in relation to IPC monitoring in the current analysis. Microsoft Excel was used to arrange and clean the raw data. In comparison, the Chi-square test evaluated relationships between categorical variables, descriptive statistics, such as frequencies and percentages, described facility features. STATA Version 16 was used for all analyses, and statistical significance was defined at p < 0.05.

IPC monitoring varied significantly by management category (χ² = 7.95, p = 0.047), but not by ownership category (χ² = 7.68, p = 0.104), according to Table 4. In this study, management refers to the organization responsible for the facility’s daily operations, while ownership refers to the facility’s legal owner. IPC monitoring was comparatively higher in publicly managed and NGO-managed facilities, lower in privately managed facilities, and lowest in facilities under other management structures. Although a higher proportion of facilities reporting IPC monitoring were government-owned or NGO-owned, the absence of a statistically significant association by ownership suggests that operational management may have a greater influence on routine IPC implementation than legal ownership alone. These results suggest that IPC monitoring may be supported more by organizational capacity, daily governance, and structured supervisory practices than by formal ownership structure. Overall IPC monitoring coverage in the study sample was very low, especially in primary health units, private clinics, TB centers, and privately managed facilities. This underscores significant systemic deficiencies in IPC implementation and readiness.

Discussion

This study adds three significant pieces of information to the body of knowledge regarding IPC monitoring in Somalia. First, just 6.6% of assessed facilities reported routine IPC monitoring using a stated methodology in the preceding six months, demonstrating how seldom such monitoring is done. Second, it shows that IPC monitoring varied considerably by facility type and management category but not by state or ownership, indicating that operational governance and facility-level organization may have a greater influence on routine IPC implementation than location or legal ownership. Third, it identifies the settings with the lowest IPC monitoring coverage, primary health units, private clinics, TB centers, and privately managed facilities, highlighting the concentration of the most important system inadequacies.

These results are in line with data from Somalia and other low- and middle-income nations demonstrating that lower-level institutions frequently have significant IPC limitations, such as poor infrastructure, a shortage of qualified staff, and poor-quality monitoring systems [3, 20, 26]. Previous observations that frontline and specialty outpatient institutions are frequently less prepared for routine IPC implementation than higher-level hospitals are supported by the particularly poor monitoring seen in PHUs, private clinics, and TB centers in our study [20, 27].Similarly, the study’s overall low monitoring level is consistent with larger LMIC data showing that monitoring, audit, and feedback continue to be some of IPC systems’ weakest elements, especially in contexts that are fragile and resource-constrained [20, 28, 29].

An additional significant operational perspective is provided by the discovery that IPC monitoring differed by management category but not by ownership. Compared to privately managed institutions, publicly and NGO-run facilities demonstrated comparatively better IPC monitoring performance, indicating that organizational capability, implementation support, and daily supervision may have a greater impact than formal ownership status alone. This tendency is consistent with earlier research from Somalia and other LMICs, which has demonstrated that IPC performance is typically higher in facilities with stronger governance, more transparent accountability frameworks, and more reliable technical assistance [20, 24]. When combined, these results show that the primary weaknesses in Somalia’s IPC monitoring system are found in both lower-level institutions and those that may have the least regular managerial oversight and support.

Conclusion

Only 6.6% of the evaluated health facilities in Somalia reported using a particular framework during the preceding six months, indicating a very low level of routine IPC monitoring. The lowest coverage was found in primary health units, private clinics, TB centers, and privately managed facilities. Monitoring varied considerably by facility type and management, but not by state or ownership. These results show significant gaps in IPC monitoring throughout Somalia’s healthcare system.

Policy implications

The study’s conclusions point to three urgent policy goals for strengthening IPC in Somalia. First, because of their exceptionally low monitoring coverage, lower-level facilities, particularly primary health units and TB centers, should receive priority IPC monitoring support. Second, because privately run facilities have repeatedly shown poorer IPC monitoring performance, they need more robust regulatory control and accountability procedures. Third, to enhance uniformity in IPC implementation across facility types, the health system should fortify regular supervisory structures and standardized monitoring frameworks.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study provides one of the most comprehensive nationwide assessments of IPC readiness and monitoring in Somalia, drawing on data from over 1,200 healthcare facilities across the country. This study has a number of shortcomings. Actual IPC monitoring practices may have been overstated or understated due to reporting bias created by self-reported facility data. In addition to limiting causal inference, the cross-sectional approach fails to record changes over time. Furthermore, even though standardized tools were used, consistency may have been impacted by differences in data collection between locations. Additionally, contextual elements that could affect IPC implementation were not evaluated in the study. To further understand trends and contextual factors influencing IPC monitoring in Somalia, future research should take into account longitudinal designs and mixed-method techniques.
