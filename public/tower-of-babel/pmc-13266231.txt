Formation mechanism and regulation strategies of off-flavors in meat and meat products - An overall perspective
Authors: Lijun Pan, Dexin Wang, Fang Li, Yun Liu, Huan Kan, Jiahua He, Changwei Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMeat is an important part of the human diet, providing the body with abundant protein, fat, vitamins, and various minerals, and plays an irreplaceable role in ensuring the nutritional needs of the human body. However, the off-flavors limits the production and consumption of meat products. Systematically clarifying the key substances of off-flavors and their formation mechanisms is crucial for controlling this off-flavors and enhancing the production and consumption of meat products. Therefore, this paper summarizes the core flavor components responsible for the off-flavors, including aldehydes, ketones, sulfides, and amines, as well as the formation mechanisms of off-flavors such as bio-enrichment, protein oxidation and degradation, microbial action, and lipid oxidation. Furthermore, the research and application progress of off-flavors regulation technologies based on physical, chemical, biological, and composite strategies was analyzed, aiming to provide references for the production, processing, and quality improvement of meat products.Keywords: Meat products, Off-flavors components, Formation mechanism, Regulatory strategy, Quality improvement, Animal breeding and rearing

Introduction

Meat is a fundamental component of the human diet, supplying essential nutrients such as high-quality protein, dietary fats, vitamins, and a variety of minerals, thereby playing an indispensable role in fulfilling human nutritional requirements (global meat production reached 373 million tons in 2024 (FAO, 2025). Its unique flavor is one of the key factors influencing consumer acceptance (Ren et al., 2024). The flavor of meat is a complex sensory experience composed of hundreds of volatile compounds, with only a small portion contributes to its characteristic aroma. However, during the production, processing, transportation and storage of meat and meat products, unpleasant odors, namely “fishy smell” or “off-flavors”, often occur, such as the “boar taint” of pork and the “muttony smell” of mutton. These odors seriously affect the quality of the products and consumers' purchasing intentions (Zhou et al., 2024). Solving these odor problems is crucial for enhancing the quality of meat and the market competitiveness of meat products.

The formation of flavor in meat and meat products involves a series of complex reactions of non-volatile precursor substances under the effects of heating, oxidation and enzymatic hydrolysis (Muhammad et al., 2015). The main precursor substances include sugars, lipids, thiamine, amino acids and peptides, etc. Among them, the Maillard reaction, lipid oxidation, Strecker degradation and thiamine degradation are the main pathways for the generation of volatile compounds in meat (Bleicher et al., 2022). For instance, the Maillard reaction generates a variety of volatile organic compounds during the cooking and storage of meat, making significant contributions to the flavor and aroma of the meat (Feng et al., 2024). However, these reactions may also produce off-flavors substances under specific conditions. The flavor of meat largely depends on volatile organic compounds and is influenced by pre-slaughter factors such as species, breed, gender, age, and feed, as well as post-slaughter conditions such as refrigeration and maturation, cooking conditions, curing, and fermentation (Bleicher et al., 2022). For meat off-flavors, the “fecal” odor from the animal rearing phase, “bloody” smell from the slaughter process, “mutton” smell from cooking process, “decay” odor caused by microorganisms, etc. These malodorous odors impact the sensory attributes of meat products and consumer acceptance, and hinder the advancement and diversification of the meat processing industry (Pu et al., 2022). Moreover, they may also pose potential risks to both the environment and human health (Zatta et al., 2023). For instance, the skatole (SKA) which is a kind of odor nuisance produced from tryptophan by anaerobic bacteria in the gut and environment of animals may contribute to the pneumotoxicity to rumen animals and humans (Ma et al., 2021). Therefore, controlling the off-flavors in meat products is a challenge faced by the meat industry.

To overcome the challenge of off-flavors in meat and meat products, researchers are exploring a variety of regulatory techniques. These technologies are designed to control or remove off-flavors from the source of odorous substances or during the processing. It has been confirmed that the off-flavors of aquatic products can be removed or mitigated through various physical, chemical and biological methods such as yeast adsorption, zeolite and powdered activated carbon adsorption, addition of antioxidants, use of aroma masking agents, microencapsulation and biological fermentation (Dai et al., 2024). The latest research has also proposed innovative strategies such as ozone treatment, cold plasma, edible films/coatings, molecular sieves, photocatalysis and probiotics to reduce the off-flavors of farmed freshwater fish (Zhou et al., 2024). In livestock products, the addition of natural spices is also widely used to enhance flavor. For instance, strong-flavored natural spices such as star anise, cinnamon, fennel and cloves play a significant role in seasoning and flavoring in both braised and fermented meat products (Takakura et al., 2014). Even with the rise of plant-based foods and cultivated meat, the problem of off-flavors remains prominent. The off-flavor compounds in soy isolate protein are described as “grassy”, “beany” and “bitter”, which limits consumers' acceptance of plant-based meat (Nagassa et al., 2025). Systematically elucidating off-flavors compounds, the mechanisms of off-flavors formation, and developing targeted control technologies holds significant importance for promoting the healthy development of the meat, meat products, and alternative meat industries.

Therefore, this article comprehensively overview covers the possible sources and potential mechanisms off-flavors generated in livestock and poultry products from pre-slaughter handling, slaughter, cooking processing, packaging to storage. Furthermore, the existing physical, chemical and biological deodorization technologies were summarized, and an in-depth analysis and discussion were conducted on the application prospects, current challenges, and future development directions of these deodorization technologies. These will provide references for the exploration of potential, reduction of loss, improvement of quality and expansion of the consumer market of meat and meat products.

Composition and influencing factors of meat off-flavors substances

Composition of meat off-flavors substances

The off-flavors in meat refers to an unpleasant smell that develops in raw meat or meat products, significantly impairing consumers' sensory perception and purchase intention (Bryhni et al., 2002), and is determined by the characteristics of the raw materials, processing methods, and external environment. The off-flavors compounds are broadly classified into volatile and non-volatile substances. Volatile off-flavors compounds are the principal contributors to olfactory perception, with key constituents including aldehydes, ketones, sulfides, and amines, which impart sensory notes such as “rancid”, “fishy”, “musty-nutty”, “rotten cabbage”, and “sewer-like”. These compounds are volatile at ambient temperature, possess low odor thresholds, and can influence flavor even at trace concentrations. Non-volatile compounds act as “hidden modulators” of off-flavors, influencing odor expression through taste-odor interactions or serving as precursors to volatile compounds. Their mechanisms are complex and primarily involve peptides and free amino acids (Rey-Salgueiro et al., 2018). Generally, the lowest concentration at which a person can perceive a certain chemical substance is referred to as the “detection threshold”. When the concentration is constant, the compound with a lower detection threshold is more likely to be perceived; when the detection threshold is constant, the compound with a higher concentration is more likely to be perceived. However, the OAV value, also known as the odor activity value, is a method for evaluating the contribution rate of volatile compounds to the flavor based on their olfactory thresholds. It is calculated by dividing the detected concentration of the volatile compound by its olfactory threshold. If the OAV value of a compound is greater than 1, it is generally considered that the compound has a significant contribution to the flavor of the sample (Wang et al., 2019). Currently, qualitative and quantitative analyses of these volatile compounds are predominantly conducted using techniques such as gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS), gas chromatography-olfactometry (GC-O), and gas chromatography-ion mobility spectrometry (GC-IMS), enabling the identification of key odorants in various meats and meat products (Liu et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2024).

In livestock and poultry meat, major off-flavors components include volatile fatty acids, aldehydes, ketones, sulfur-containing compounds, and pyrazines. These compounds originate from lipid oxidation, protein degradation, and microbial activity (Ren et al., 2024). For example, 1-octen-3-ol and hexanal are recognized as key contributors to the off-flavors in most livestock and poultry meat (Wang, 2023). Some researchers have detected that the off-flavors in duck meat mainly come from volatile substances such as hexanal, heptanal, 1-octen-3-ol and 2-pentylfuran (Zhong et al., 2024). In pork, skatole and androstenone (AND) are closely associated with off-flavors, which tend to accumulate with animal age and are more pronounced in intact (non-castrated) males. Additionally, feed composition will influence animal lipid profiles and thereby affect odor development (Bone and Squires, 2021). Influencing factors of meat off-flavors

The off-flavors of meat is influenced by a combination of factors, including the characteristics of raw materials, processing methods, external environmental conditions, and slaughter practices (Fig. 1).

Factors affecting off-flavors in meat and meat products.

Before slaughter

Before the slaughter, volatile off-favor compositions of livestock and poultry meat are infuenced by various factors, such as species, breed, gender, age, muscle type, and as well as feeding conditions (Bleicher et al., 2022). The odors of meat and meat products from different breeds of livestock and poultry vary due to the different characteristics of the volatile compounds they contain. Studies have found that beef mainly presents a herbal flavor (such as hexanal, heptanal, 1-butene-3-ol, etc.), while pork is rich in boar taint flavors (such as skatole, androstenone, 3-methylindole, etc.); chicken has a strong bloody odor (such as prop-2-ene and 1,4-benzenesulfonic acid), while duck meat has a strong fatty and earthy odor ((E)-but-2-enonitrile, phenol, 3-methylbut-3-enonitrile) (Park et al., 2025). Furthermore, the flavors of meat and meat products from different breeds of the same species can also vary. Studies have shown that the types and contents of flavor substances in different breeds of chickens (Wang et al., 2015) and ducks (Xie et al., 2026) are significantly different. For the gender of animals, the research has found that gender has little impact on the nutritional quality of rabbit meat, but the off-flavors of male rabbits is stronger than that of female rabbits in terms of sensory perception (Carrilho et al., 2008). Chen et al. (2014) detected that the content of volatile flavor substances in female rabbits was significantly higher than that in male rabbits. Moreover, esters, ethers, phenols, and acids are unique components of female rabbit meat, while substances related to the off-flavors characteristics (such as hexanal) in male rabbit meat are significantly higher than female, which also proves that the pungency of male rabbit meat is higher than that of female rabbit meat. The meat of uncastrated boars has an unpleasant odor, known as boar skunk odor, which is mainly attributed to two compounds: 3-methylindole (SKA) and AND (Iniesta et al., 2023). SKA is a volatile compound that is formed through the degradation of tryptophan by intestinal microorganisms and gives pork a distinct fecal odor. Meanwhile, AND (5α-androst-16-3one) is a sex pheromone produced by testicular interstitial cells, which causes pork to emit a noticeable urine or sweat odor (Burgeon et al., 2023). Regarding the age of animals, the flavor of meat and meat products from the same breed but at different ages can also vary. A study has found that as the age increases, the pungent off-flavors substances in the meat of the Italian rabbit show a trend of first increasing and then decreasing (Xie, 2016).

In addition, the feeding method and environment also affect the flavor of livestock and poultry meat. The feeding method is closely related to the diet and significantly influences various quality characteristics of the meat. An unreasonable diet will eventually lead to the production of off-flavors in the meat after slaughter. Luo's (2019) study found that the total volatile substances in grazing sheep were higher than those in confined feeding, while the aldehydes in grazing sheep were lower than those in confined feeding. Among them, the hexanal content in confined feeding sheep was significantly higher than that in grazing sheep, and the contents of hexanal and nonenal in confined feeding sheep were much higher than those in grazing sheep. This might have an adverse effect on the flavor of the sheep meat, resulting in a poorer taste of the confined feeding sheep. Li et al. (2024) research shows that the breed has the greatest impact on the volatile flavor of yak meat, followed by the feeding method, and then the part. However, consumers from different regions, cultures and dietary habits have different preferences, and they also have different definitions and acceptance levels for the odors produced by the sheep themselves (Pavan et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2022). Furthermore, the type of muscle also plays a certain role in determining the flavor of meat and meat products. As reported by Sun et al., the flavors and odors of beef from different parts after frying show significant differences. The flavor quality of the eye meat is the highest, while the odor quality is poorer. There are significant differences in characteristic volatile substances among the beef from different parts. The benzaldehyde in the cow tendon, 3-methylbutanol in the eye meat, 2,3-pentanedione and z-3-hexenol in the hindquarter, butyraldehyde in the brisket, 2-propanol, 1-hexanol, 2-pentanone and 3-methyl-3-butanenol in the top loin, are all significantly higher than those in the other four parts (Sun et al., 2025). The reason is the difference in muscle fat content and composition. Unsaturated fatty acids are more prone to hydrolysis or oxidation under certain conditions, resulting in the formation of volatile odor substances (Zhao et al., 2024).

Slaughter

The slaughtering process may lead to the production of off-flavor (Abd El-Rahim et al., 2023). Slaughter-induced stress serves as the primary initiating factor, triggering postmortem enzymatic activation in muscle tissue, which in turn accelerates the oxidation of fatty acids. For example, the slaughtering process contributes to the characteristic bloody odor in beef, while lipid oxidation in poultry such as chickens and ducks leads to the formation of aldehydes, resulting in off-flavor (Grosch, 2001). At the same time, the microorganisms present in the water, on the surfaces of machines, workers and tools are also important factors contributing to the off-flavor of the meat. Studies have shown that the microbial load on each hand of slaughterhouse workers ranges from 107 to 1012 CFU/hand and identified 124 different types of bacteria have been detected (Sui et al., 2023). de Alba et al. (2022) reported that immediately after the skin was removed, thermophilic bacteria (such as Yersinia species, Serratia species) and thermophilic clostridium species were detected in the flesh of the gluteal muscles and the upper part of the posterior body. Even after the slaughtering process is completed, the massive proliferation of microorganisms can lead to meat deterioration, resulting in discoloration, off-odors, and the formation of mucus.

Furthermore, radiation used for sterilizing the surface of meat after slaughter (such as ultraviolet radiation) may also have a certain negative impact on the olfactory characteristics of meat. Although many studies have reported that irradiation is a safe and effective method for preserving and maintaining the freshness of meat (Indiarto et al., 2023; Jia et al., 2021), there are also reports that unreasonable irradiation doses may exacerbate the oxidation of meat, thereby causing the off-flavors associated with irradiation (Brewer, 2008). For example, low-dose irradiation can moderately reduce meat odor, but when the dose exceeds a certain threshold (7 kGy), it may generate off-flavors and promote lipid oxidation (Li et al., 2017). Yao et al. (2024) reported that pork irradiated with doses of 3 kGy and 5 kGy received lower sensory scores, and irradiation changed the color of the pork, promoted fat oxidation, and increased volatile odor substances such as hydrocarbons, aldehydes, and alcohols, resulting in a change in the flavor of the pork.

Cooking

Cooking is a crucial step in the flavor formation of meat products. Through heating, the precursor substances in the meat undergo a series of complex physical and chemical changes, generating a large number of volatile compounds, thereby endowing meat products with their unique flavors and aromas. Meanwhile, cooking can also alter the content and type of off-flavor substances in the meat through processes such as Maillard reaction, lipid oxidation, and the transformation of sulfur-containing compounds, thereby influencing the overall flavor of the meat (Bleicher et al., 2022). Therefore, factors such as cooking methods, cooking temperatures, cooking times, and even cooking utensils all affect the off-flavor of the meat. Deng (2018) research found that steaming, baking, microwaving, and frying all had varying degrees of impact on the contents of the rabbit meat's off-flavors substances caproic acid and hexanal; among them, the content of both substances decreased sharply throughout the frying process, and the reason might be related to the high temperature promoting the volatilization and degradation of the pungent substances. Compared to boiling, the duck meat baked by electricity has a lower off-flavors (Xu, 2025).

Furthermore, the cooking temperature and duration are also crucial factors influencing the off-flavor of meat. A study shows that the off-flavor of duck meat increases after being treated at different temperatures (90 °C, 100 °C, and 105 °C) and after reheating (Wang, 2023). The reason is that the increase in heating temperature and reheating increase the degree of lipid oxidation in duck meatafter heat treatment and reheating, the degree of lipid oxidation in the duck meat increases. Key precursor lipid molecules such as phosphatidylcholine (PC), phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), arachidonic acid (C20:4), oleic acid (C18:1), and linoleic acid (C18:2) in phospholipids undergo oxidation and degradation, generating odor-causing substances such as nonaldehyde, (E)-2-octenaldehyde, and hexanal, thereby intensifying the off-flavors of the duck meat (Grosch, 2001). After being reheated and subjected to high-temperature treatment, the beef undergoes enhanced lipid oxidation, resulting in the accumulation of volatile aldehyde compounds such as hexanal, (E)-2-octen-1-al, decanal, dodecanal, pentadecanal, hexadecanal, and (E,E)-2,4-decadienal, significantly enhancing the warming flavor (Li et al., 2026). Meanwhile, the dry beef baked at 150 °C has a higher intensity of off-flavors (Lee et al., 2021). Overall, the influence of cooking on the intensity of meat off-flavors is the result of the combined effect of multiple factors, multiple stages, and multiple chemical reactions.

Storage

Storage is an important process in meat and meat product production, involving lipid oxidation, protein degradation, microbial activity, and changes in volatile compounds. These factors work together to cause changes in the flavor of meat during storage, which may result in unpleasant odors or the formation of new flavor characteristics (Dang et al., 2021). Meat from slaughterhouses tends to exhibit a more pronounced bloody odor compared to that obtained from supermarkets and retail outlets, likely due to differences in handling, chilling efficiency, and freshness maintenance (Gagaoua et al., 2022; Imrich et al., 2018). During storage, temperature is the main factor affecting the sensory and quality of meat and meat products. For instance, the lamb that was rapidly cooled at −35 °C after slaughter contains more aroma and umami compounds compared to that cooled at 4 °C or −20 °C. Moreover, the content of the off-flavors compound 1-octen-3-ol is lower. This is because rapid cooling can delay the hydrolysis of adenosine triphosphate, slow down protein degradation, fat decomposition and fat oxidation (Xiang et al., 2021).

Of course, storage time and packaging also affect the flavors of meat and meat products. It has been reported that when cooked duck meat is stored for 7 days and then reheated, the content of volatile flavor substances will significantly increase, and the unpleasant smell will also become more severe (Zhao et al., 2024). Studies have shown that as the storage time increases, the content of aldehyde and acetic acid substances in different parts of rabbit meat undergoes significant changes. The content of aldehyde in the abdominal muscle significantly increases, while the contents in the longissimus dorsi muscle and hind leg muscle change less and are always lower than that in the abdominal muscle; the content of acetic acid in all three parts shows a trend of significant decrease followed by an increase, but only the hind leg muscle shows that the final value is significantly higher than the initial value (Zhou et al., 2019). Fresh meat stored under modified atmosphere packaging (70% oxygen and 30% carbon dioxide) at 4 °C, as the storage time increases, the contents of alcohols, aldehydes, ketones and acids will continue to increase. The stickiness, greasiness, cheese-like flavor, vomit-like smell, sweat-like smell and yogurt-like smell of the meat will significantly intensify, while the fruit-like flavor will significantly weaken (Mohsen et al., 2018). For packaging, Reis et al. (2016) reported that in vacuum-packed lamb meat, 3-methylbutanal, 3-hydroxy-2-butanone, and sulfur dioxide were positively correlated with “packaging odor”, and these substances are the final products or by-products of bacterial glucose fermentation and amino acid decomposition. High-oxygen modified gas packaging will damage the flavor and texture of steaks during storage, and may production unpleasant substances. Therefore, it is a better choice to store and age the product in an airtight packaging (Vierck et al., 2019). Furthermore, due to the differences in the composition and processing methods of the packaging materials, their inherent off-flavors compounds may migrate into the meat, or the differences in the barrier properties (such as gas barrier, light barrier, etc.) of packaging materials, the off-flavors in the meat products may increase during storage (Bai et al., 2024).

Formation mechanism of off-flavors compounds in meat

The formation of meat off-flavors is a complex process mediated by multiple reaction pathways, such as bioaccumulation, protein oxidation and degradation, lipid oxidation and degradation, as well as microbial action, etc (Fig. 2). Systematically clarifying these pathways is of vital importance for the targeted development of meat off-flavor control technologies.

Formation mechanism of meat off-flavors components.

Biological enrichment

Bioaccumulation refers to the process by which animals absorb and accumulate odor-causing compounds from their environment or feed during growth. When these substances reach a certain concentration in tissues, they can impart undesirable fishy or off-flavors to meat products. The types and levels of accumulated odorants vary across species and are influenced by multiple factors, including biological characteristics, rearing conditions, and feed composition. Aquatic organisms represent the most extensively studied group regarding the bioenrichment of fishy odorants. For instance, geosmin and 2-methylisoborneol—metabolic byproducts of algae and actinomycetes in aquatic environments—are readily absorbed by aquatic species through respiration and ingestion. Due to their extremely low olfactory thresholds, these compounds are easily detectable by humans and contribute to earthy off-flavors in seafood (Xiao et al., 2025). Although aquatic products are the most extensively studied area in bioaccumulation research, since most studies do not classify aquatic products as meat products, this article does not provide detailed explanations.

In livestock and poultry, the bioaccumulation of odor-causing compounds is primarily linked to dietary formulation and husbandry practices. Research indicates that caged mutton exhibits a more pronounced off-flavors compared to free-range mutton (Luo, 2019). A well-documented example is the accumulation of skatole (3-methylindole) and androstenone in intact male pigs. Skatole is produced through the anaerobic microbial degradation of L-tryptophan in the porcine hindgut, followed by systemic absorption and deposition in adipose tissue. Androstenone is a steroid hormone secreted by the testicles and has a strong urine odor. Its accumulation in uncastrated males results in meat with characteristic off-flavors commonly described as boar taint (Meng et al., 2013). The main source of the off-flavors smell of sheep is the volatile 4-alkyl branched fatty acids: 4-methyl-octanoic acid, 4-methyl-valeric acid, and 4-ethyl-octanoic acid. The rumen of ruminants metabolizes L-tryptophan into 3-methylindole through intestinal microorganisms. This metabolite, like indole, has a similar fecal odor and accumulates in livestock products, leading to the formation of some unpleasant flavors. 4-methylphenol is also produced by rumen bacteria acting on tyrosine, and it may follow the blood and deposit in adipose tissues, causing the sheep meat to have the off-flavors (Iniesta et al., 2023).

Excessive oxidation of lipids

Lipid oxidation is one of the key processes in the formation of meat flavor, but excessive lipid oxidation is also one of the core mechanisms for the formation of meat off-flavors. This process is a complex free-radical-mediated chain reaction that primarily consists of three stages: initiation, transport, and termination. During the initiation phase, unsaturated fatty acids generate free radicals upon exposure to pro-oxidative factors such as metal ions, light, oxygen, and enzymatic activity. These radicals initiate a cascade of chain reactions, leading to fatty acid peroxidation and the subsequent formation of volatile compounds—such as aldehydes, ketones, and alcohols—with low odor thresholds, which significantly contribute to the meat off-flavors (Zhang et al., 2018). This process is influenced by multiple factors such as fatty acid composition, oxidation pathways, and myoglobin catalysis. For example, polyunsaturated fatty acids like linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid in meat are highly susceptible to both enzymatic and non-enzymatic oxidation under the influence of light, oxygen, metal ions, or lipases, resulting in the formation of unstable hydroperoxides. These hydroperoxides readily decompose into volatile aldehydes, ketones, and carboxylic acids, which are primarily responsible for the development of rancid or fishy odors in meat products (Zhao et al., 2024). Lipid hydrogen peroxides are the main products of lipid oxidation. Although these hydrogen peroxides have neither odor nor taste, they will further undergo a series of complex reactions under the influence of heat, light or metal ions, decomposing into compounds that may cause unpleasant flavors, such as aldehydes, alcohols and ketones, leading to spoilage odor, off-odors, rancid smell, color change, reduced nutritional value and shortened shelf life of meat and meat products (Lai, 2021).

Similarly, enzymatic oxidation is also a very important pathway for lipid oxidation. This process is mainly caused by the catalytic action of endogenous lipoxygenase (LOX), which promotes the reaction between oxygen and unsaturated fatty acids. It can also catalyze the formation of volatile flavor substances similar to auto-oxidation. In photo-sensitive oxidation processes, photosensitizers play a crucial role because they can promote the excitation of oxygen, generating singlet oxygen (1O2), which will accelerate lipid oxidation (Xiong et al., 2020). In addition, meat contains transition metals (such as Cu and Fe etc.) and heme proteins, which are all oxidants that can accelerate the oxidation of unsaturated lipids (Lai, 2021). Yao et al. (2025) reported that aldehydes such as hexanal, octanal, and nonanal, generated through excessive lipid oxidation, are closely associated with the off-flavor in braised pork. Similarly, during the heating process, phosphatidylcholine (PC) and phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) in the phospholipids of duck meat undergo lipid oxidation, resulting in the production of volatile off-flavors, which enhances the odor of the heated duck meat (Wang, 2023). These findings suggest that targeting the inhibition of Lipid oxidation may be a way to reduce the off-flavors of meat and meat products.

Protein oxidation and degradation

Protein degradation plays a crucial role in meat flavor formation. Through enzymatic and non-enzymatic processes, proteins are broken down into amino acids, peptides, and other compounds that serve as precursors for flavor formation. During the storage and processing of meat products, endogenous enzymes such as calpains and cathepsins, along with exogenous proteases secreted by microorganisms, catalyze the hydrolysis of proteins into polypeptides and free amino acids. These degradation products provide precursors for the generation of volatile odorants associated with spoilage (Zhao et al., 2023). Under enzymatic catalysis, these precursors undergo deamination and decarboxylation reactions, yielding volatile compounds responsible for off-flavors, including aldehydes, alcohols, organic acids, and sulfur-containing compounds. Specifically, sulfur- and nitrogen-containing amino acids are transformed via decarboxylation or deamination into malodorous substances. For example, dimethyl sulfide is considered a volatile sulfur compound formed through the interaction of unsaturated fatty acids and sulfur-containing amino acids (Chandra et al., 2020). Additionally, histidine decarboxylation leads to the formation of histamine, while lysine decarboxylation produces cadaverine—both biogenic amines closely associated with meat spoilage. Throughout the spoilage process, protein degradation also gives rise to various nitrogen- and sulfur-containing volatile compounds, such as hydrogen sulfide and ammonia, which contribute significantly to undesirable odors (Zhao et al., 2023).

Moreover, during the storage of meat and meat products, the antioxidant capacity of the muscle decreases, leading to an increase and accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive nitrogen species (RNS). These substances will increase the oxidation level of proteins, thereby altering their structure and function, including the structure of enzymes. Eventually, it may result in the production of sulfides or other irritating substances and compounds, causing the meat products to undergo spoilage, deterioration, or other unpleasant odors, and reducing their edible quality (Zhang et al., 2013). Furthermore, protein oxidation is closely related to lipid oxidation and interacts with it, further influencing the formation of volatile compounds (Hadidi et al., 2022). Wang et al. (2022) summarized the main pathways through which irradiated yak meat develops off-flavors, which are related to the metabolism of cysteine and methionine as well as the metabolism of linoleic acid. The oxidation and decomposition of sulfur-containing amino acids and unsaturated fatty acids may be the causes of the off-flavors in irradiated meat. Essentially, the olfactory characteristics of meat and meat products are largely influenced by protein oxidation and degradation. Regulating these processes to control the flavor characteristics of meat products is also an important approach.

Microbial action

Microorganisms play a crucial role in the formation and change of the flavor and quality of meat products. They break down proteins and fats in the meat, metabolize carbohydrates, and even directly produce volatile compounds, thereby endowing meat products with unique flavor characteristics (Zhao et al., 2024). However, the products resulting from the growth and reproduction of these pathogenic microorganisms usually have unpleasant odors. These microorganisms mainly include Pseudomonas species, Enterobacteriaceae bacteria, Lactobacillus, and Carnobacterium. For instance, Pseudomonas strphytica, Putrefactive pseudomonas, Lactobacillus sakei, and Enterorhabdus lumikogenes can break down the sulfur-containing amino acids (such as methionine and cysteine) present in sterile pork, generating volatile sulfur compounds with rotten or rotten egg-like odors, such as hydrogen sulfide, methanethiol, and dimethyl sulfide (Papadopoulou et al., 2020); Some pathogenic bacteria can decarboxylation amino acids, generating biogenic amines (such as cadaverine, putrescine, histamine, etc). These substances are often associated with the foul smell of rotten meat (Ashaolu et al., 2021). For fat degradation, strains with lipase activity can hydrolyze the fat in meat, releasing free fatty acids. These free fatty acids may then undergo oxidation, generating unpleasant aldehydes (such as hexanal, nonanal) and ketones (such as 2-butanone, 2-pentanone), as well as short-chain fatty acids with rancid or sweaty odors like butyric acid and caproic acid (Wu et al., 2017; Papadopoulou et al., 2020). Although the carbohydrate content in meat is relatively low, the metabolism of microorganisms in it can also affect the flavor. Some spoilage bacteria, when fermenting sugars under anaerobic conditions, produce organic acids such as acetic acid and propionic acid, which can cause the meat to develop an acidic taste or a pungent odor (Papadopoulou et al., 2020). Studies has shown that during the pickling and fermentation process of ham, Enterobacteriaceae, Micrococcus, and mycetes produce odor components such as benzaldehyde, 3-methylbutanal, 3-methylhexanal, 2-methylpropanal, biogenic amines, and sulfur-containing compounds through Streck degradation, deamination, and decarboxylation reactions, leading to the deterioration of ham (Zhou et al., 2022). Under the action of rumen microbial lipases, lipids are converted into unsaturated fatty acids. Some of these hydrogenated unsaturated fatty acids undergo isomerization reactions to form branched-chain fatty acids, which become the main source of the off-flavors in mutton (Zhou et al., 2022; Lourenço et al., 2010).

To prevent the unpleasant odors in meat caused by microorganisms, hygiene control, cold chain management, packaging techniques, as well as food additives or preservation technologies that inhibit the growth of spoilage bacteria are usually employed (Ashaolu et al., 2021). For fermented meat products, by precisely controlling the fermentation microorganisms and fermentation conditions, it is possible to effectively inhibit the growth of harmful microorganisms and promote the formation of beneficial flavor compounds (Zhao et al., 2024). If specific types of staphylococcus are inoculated, it can enhance the sensory properties of Chinese cured meat. This is mainly achieved by coordinating the composition of flavor compounds, especially amino acid metabolites (Yang et al., 2024). The current research is beginning to utilize technologies such as metagenomics, metatranscriptomics and metabolomics to more comprehensively analyze the complex relationship between microorganisms and meat flavor. This is aimed at improving the flavor quality of meat products by regulating the microbial community, while ensuring food safety (Zhan et al., 2022).

Strecker degradation

The Strecker degradation is a chemical reaction involving α-amino acids and carbonyl compounds, representing a crucial step in the Maillard reaction. Under thermal conditions, carbonyl compounds react with amino acids to produce aldehydes or ketones, carbon dioxide, and amines. The resulting aldehydes and ketones contribute significantly to flavor development, whereas partial aldehydes may negatively affect meat flavor (Luo et al., 2025). For example, methyl butyraldehyde and 3-methylthiopropanal,generated through the Strecker degradation, are associated with meat spoilage (Nagassa et al., 2025); some carbonyl compounds may undergo undesirable reactions under specific conditions, producing volatile compounds such as acrolein and diacetyl, which impart off-flavors odors (Ren et al., 2024). Cysteine undergoes the Strecker degradation reaction to produce sulfur compounds like hydrogen sulfide and methanethiol, as well as aldehydes like acetaldehyde, which have distinctive odors. These substances significantly impact the flavor of meat products. Strecker degradation reaction is affected by factors such as temperature, reaction time, pH, and water activity. Excessive reaction will lead to the overproduction of sulfur- and nitrogen-containing heterocyclic compounds and aldehydes, potentially contributing to off-flavors and intensifying undesirable fishy odors. Research indicates that uncontrolled Maillard reaction conditions during high-temperature roasting, particularly when accompanied by excessive Strecker degradation, may result in burnt flavors and strong off-flavors odors that mask the characteristic meaty flavor (Lan et al., 2025). By optimizing processing parameters, adding flavor enhancers, and employing antimicrobial agents and antioxidants, etc., the extent and outcomes of the Strecker degradation can be effectively modulated, thereby mitigating the formation of undesirable odors in meat products.

Technological for controlling the off-flavors of meat

To eliminate or reduce the off-flavors in meat products and enhance overall product quality for consumers, a comprehensive approach involving both upstream interventions at the animal breeding stage and downstream processing improvements is employed. This includes physical, chemical, and biological strategies aimed at effectively controlling undesirable odors in meat (Fig. 3).

Techniques for regulating the off-flavors in meat products.

Physical deodorization

Physical deodorization rely on the structural or physical characteristics of deodorizing agents to eliminate off-flavors from livestock, poultry, and aquatic products through mechanisms such as adsorption, encapsulation, masking, heating, extraction, and vacuum volatilization. These methods are simple and straightforward but are limited in effectiveness and cannot fundamentally remove odor-causing volatile compounds due to the limitations of the deodorizing agents (Ren et al., 2024).

Seasoning masking

Seasoning masking is the most traditional and simplest way to remove the off-flavors from meat products. Research has demonstrated that extracts from spices such as ginger, garlic, angelica dahurica, fennel, rosemary, nutmeg, cardamom, cinnamon, star anise, and bay leaves can significantly reduce the off-flavors intensity in silver carp. Among these, rosemary extract exhibits the most effective deodorizing properties, due to polyphenolic play a major role in deodorization (Wang et al., 2022). A study has found that adding 0.2% star anise to stewing duck legs can significantly reduce the concentrations of off-flavors compounds such as hexanal, heptanal, 1-octen-3-ol, and 2-pentylfuran, and give duck meat a unique floral and herbal notes (Zhong et al., 2024). Phenolic compounds can interact with lipoxygenase and myofibrillar proteins, inducing conformational changes via hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and van der Waals' forces, thereby suppressing off-flavors. Furthermore, the deodorizing efficacy of tea polyphenols is superior to that of resveratrol and rosmarinic acid (Li et al., 2025). Instant tea can increase the floral, roasted and grassy components in duck meat, while reducing the fishy and fatty flavors (Liu et al., 2024). Marinating lamb with spices such as rosemary, ginger, turmeric, garlic and basil leaves can enhance the overall flavor and reduce the off-flavors (Choi et al., 2024). Niu et al. (2025) reported that adding porcini mushroom powder and sea mushroom powder could reduce the content of undesirable aldehydes (including pentanal, hexanal and heptanal) in pork patties during high-temperature processing (roasting). Adding chopped chili peppers (SPC) and six-yang fermented soybeans (SPD) could reduce the intensity of the “greasiness” and “fishy smell” of steamed pork loin by 45.16% to 50.57% respectively, while endowing pork with a strong spicy and sauce like taste (Zhang et al., 2025). Polyphenols and flavoring extracts, especially tea polyphenols, also exhibit strong deodorizing effects in poultry, effectively lowering thiobarbituric acid reactive substance (TBARS), overall odor intensity, and concentrations of six major odorants including 1-octene-3-ol, heptanal, and hexanal (Ning et al., 2025). Although seasoning masking is widely used, its effect is limited. Further optimization of processing parameters or combination with other techniques is necessary to achieve enhanced results.

Thermal evaporation and dissolution

Thermal evaporation or dissolution is a deodorization method that uses high temperatures or steam to dissolve malodorous compounds into water or facilitate their removal via steam volatilization. This technique can effectively removes fishy substances while better retaining polyunsaturated fatty acids. Yan (2020) applied steam deodorization to treat horse oil, which significantly reduced the levels of volatile odor-causing substances and free fatty acids, as well as lowered the acid value—thereby enhancing the oil's storage stability. In addition, conventional thermal processing methods such as frying and boiling are widely used for odor removal. During these processes, high-temperature heating promotes the volatilization or thermal decomposition of malodorous components. Frying, in particular, has been shown to efficiently reduce fishy odors in meat. Similarly, during boiling, certain odor-causing substances dissolve into the cooking water, contributing to deodorization (Wei et al., 2024). Washing with 40 °C warm water multiple times and boiling with auxiliary materials for 50 min can effectively remove the unpleasant flavor substances of deodorized sheep liver powder and significantly increase the volatile aromatic compounds (Wu et al., 2025). Microwave heating enables rapid volatilization or breakdown of these compounds through targeted thermal effects. Blanching not only removes a portion of the undesirable odors but also helps retain the original flavor and nutritional quality of the food materials (Sun et al., 2023). Cui et al found that the processing by microwave absorption disc cooking method could enhance the flavor of Kung Pao chicken and reduce off-flavors, and the content of volatile substances (such as hexanal, heptanal, (E)-2-octen-1-al, (E)-2-decanal, (E)-2-nonenal, and 2,4-decadienal) was appropriate(Cui et al., 2021).

Vacuum deodorization

Vacuum treatment removes odorous compounds by reducing environmental pressure, thereby enhancing the volatility of odor-causing substances (Xia et al., 2024). This method is typically integrated with other technologies to improve deodorization efficiency and preserve food quality. The underlying principles are as follows: first, under vacuum conditions, the boiling points of volatile odor compounds—such as trimethylamine, geosmin, and 2-methylisoborneol—are lowered, increasing their volatility; second, the reduction in system pressure accelerates the migration of odorants substances from the interior to the surface of food, thereby promoting their evaporation and removal (Orozco et al., 2023); third, a vacuum environment minimizes contact between food and oxygen, suppressing oxidative reactions and consequently reducing the formation of oxidation-derived odor compounds (Dai et al., 2024). In practical applications, vacuum technology is often combined with complementary techniques or employed in oil processing engineering. In meat products, vacuum steam distillation can remove 67.6% of the off-flavors in dried pork liver, but its deodorizing effect is not as good as that of supercritical carbon dioxide (Abril et al., 2023). The semitendinosus muscle of beef subjected to vacuum low-temperature cooking exhibits improved tenderness, enhanced texture, and superior sensory qualities. Furthermore, samples cooked under vacuum conditions at 70 °C demonstrate increased concentrations of umami-related compounds, specifically inosinic acid and guanylic acid, along with a reduced perception of off-flavor (Sangrok et al., 2025). Jiang et al. (2025) reported that the optimal deodorization process for yak meat is: vacuum degree of −0.08 MPa, curing time of 22 min, and injection volume of 52%; The yak meat marinated under this condition has the lowest off-flavors, and potential odor-causing substances such as N-heptanal, hexanal, decanal, benzaldehyde, hexanoic acid, and decanoic acid are significantly reduced. In fact, a single vacuum treatment has limited effectiveness in removing off-flavors from meat products. It is usually used as an auxiliary technology for cooking, pickling, and other processes to enhance the overall flavor quality of meat products such as duck meat (Chen, 2022).

Adsorption deodorization

Adsorption deodorization technology represents a significant approach for eliminating undesirable odors from food products. The underlying mechanism primarily involves physical and chemical adsorption. Physical adsorption is driven by intermolecular forces, such as van der Waals interactions, and is influenced by the specific surface area and pore structure of the adsorbent. This process is typically reversible, allowing for desorption and regeneration of the adsorbent. In contrast, chemical adsorption occurs through the formation of chemical bonds, wherein active sites on the adsorbent surface react with odor-causing compounds to generate stable complexes. Chemical adsorption offers advantages including high selectivity and substantial adsorption capacity; however, it is generally irreversible and presents challenges in terms of regeneration (Cansado and Mourão, 2021; Yamamoto et al., 2014). In practical applications, adsorption-based odor removal technologies have been widely employed for the treatment of oily, lipid-based substrates, and meat. For instance, packaging materials containing activated carbon have been proven to be effective in reducing the levels of volatile organic compounds and off-odor in irradiated pork meat (Kim et al., 2008). Mineral adsorbents - bentonite, diatomite, waste filtering aids and hydrated sodium calcium aluminum silicate - can selectively adsorb androstenone and fecal odor substances in pork, reducing the off-flavors smell of the pork (Park and Squires, 2025). Jen and Squires (2011) demonstrated that adsorbents such as cholestyramine, activated carbon, Tween-60, bentonite, and polyvinylpyrrolidonecan effectively remove the off-odor substances (androstenone fecal odor) in male pork products, and improve its unpleasant taste. Although adsorption-based deodorization is cost-effective, highly efficient, and easy to operate, it may concurrently remove proteins and other essential nutrients from the product. Furthermore, since deodorization primarily occurs at the food surface, careful selection of adsorbents and precise control of operational parameters are crucial.

Embedding/microencapsulation

Encapsulation deodorization, also known as microencapsulation technology, is one of the current research hotspots in the food industry. Its core principle is to encapsulate substances with unpleasant odors within specialized carrier materials to mask, isolate, or eliminate undesirable odors. Widely used encapsulating agents include polysaccharides, proteins, lipids, and natural polymers, while common encapsulation methods encompass spray drying, emulsification, extrusion, and fluidized bed coating. Application-wise, encapsulating fish oil using microencapsulation and adding β-cyclodextrin (β-CD) and other flavor-modifying agents, the oil structural will be restructured, and significantly reducing its characteristic off-flavors while enhancing aroma profiles and extending shelf life (Serfert et al., 2010). Notably, β-CD-based encapsulation has proven effective in deodorizing gelatin derived from pigskin and fish skin (Yang et al., 2022); Using β-CD to treat beef by-products (beef liver, beef tongue, rumen, and beef heart) can remove the pungent off-flavors substances and improve the food quality. (Deng et al., 2025); the use of yeast β-glucan can effectively reduce the content of volatile aldehydes such as (E)-2-decanal, hexanal, 2-butylpyridine, nonanal and 1-octen-3-ol in duck meat products, significantly reducing the off-flavor (Xu, 2025). Additionally, starch represents a promising green and cost-effective solid-phase adsorbent for deodorization. Dai (2024) prepared flexible V-shaped starch, which demonstrated significant efficacy in mitigating the off-flavors of oyster peptides. Mechanically, the V-shaped starch could selectively adsorb off-flavor substances such as heptanal and 1-octen-3-ol, forming complexes to encapsulate these off-flavor substances, and different fishy components could also promote the combination of the V-shaped starch with other fishy substances. Surface coating techniques also contribute to odor control during fish storage by inhibiting the accumulation of aldehydes, alcohols, and odor-active fatty acids. In particular, coatings incorporating ginger essential oil in combination with gelatin exhibit superior deodorizing performance compared to gelatin-only coatings (Li et al., 2022). Despite the encapsulation-based deodorization is simple and suitable for descaling industrial products, limitations remain, particularly regarding the inefficient encapsulation of macromolecular compounds, which restricts overall deodorization efficiency.

Irradiation

Irradiation deodorization primarily employs radiation sources such as 60Co, 137Cs, and gamma rays to ionize odor-causing molecules, thereby altering their molecular structure and achieving effective deodorization. Key factors influencing the irradiation efficacy include radiation dose and the type of irradiation source. Low-dose irradiation generally has minimal impact on the sensory attributes of food, whereas high-dose irradiation may induce significant changes (Brewer, 2008). Research indicates that low-dose irradiation can moderately reduce meat odor; however, when the dose exceeds a certain threshold (7 kGy), it may generate off-flavors and promote lipid oxidation (Li et al., 2017). Studies on irradiation treatment of chicken powder seasonings suggest that electron beam irradiation offers advantages over gamma rays, as it better preserves original volatile compounds, reduces undesirable flavors, and mitigates lipid oxidation (Zheng et al., 2022). When irradiating fresh chicken, turkey, sheep, beef and pork products at a temperature range of 0 - 4 °C using a dose of 3 kGy, it was found that the irradiation treatment could reduce the number of microorganisms and extend the shelf life of the meat; after irradiation, the level of thiobarbituric acid in the meat increased, the amount of fat oxidation increased, but the odor of the meat could be eliminated to varying degrees (Kim et al., 2002). Although irradiation deodorization has been explored in meat products, concerns regarding safety, efficacy, and optimal dosage selection remain critical barriers to its widespread application in meat deodorization.

Other physical deodorization techniques

In addition to the above-mentioned physical technologies, ultrasonic waves, supercritical carbon dioxide, ultra-high pressure and cold plasma technologies are also used in the field of food deodorization. For instance, ultrasound defrosting has shown potential in eliminating off-flavors. Using 400 W ultrasonic-assisted thawing can significantly reduce the off-flavors of duck meat and enhance its overall eating quality characteristics such as color, water retention ability and tenderness. The reason is that ultrasound inhibits fat oxidation and the degradation of free amino acids (Sun et al., 2023). Studies have shown that moderate-intensity cold plasma treatment can improve the flavor of meat products, but excessive treatment intensity may have adverse effects (Qiu et al., 2024).Wu et al reported that ultra-high pressure treatment reduced the types of volatile flavor substances in tropical sea cucumbers, thereby reducing the fishy smell to a certain extent. Supercritical carbon dioxide technology is regarded as an effective method for removing volatile flavor components (Wu et al., 2026). The supercritical CO2 extraction treatment for removing the fishy smell from pork liver is superior to the traditional vacuum distillation method, and it can completely eliminate three characteristic fishy compounds in pork liver raw materials: 1-octen-3-ol, 1-nonanol, and (E,E)-2,4-heptadienal (Abril et al., 2023). However, at present, supercritical carbon dioxide is mainly used for removing the fishy odor from oils. There is relatively little research on its application in removing the fishy odor from meat products, and further evaluation of its feasibility is needed.

Chemical method

Acid, alkali, and salt treatments

Acid-base-salt deodorization involves the treatment of meat products with acids, bases, or salts to eliminate undesirable odors. This process induces protein denaturation and structural expansion, enabling fishy compounds to bind with proteins and form insoluble complexes that precipitate and separate from the matrix. Concurrently, lipids and pigments are solubilized, facilitating both odor removal and decolorization, while lipid oxidation is effectively inhibited (Aaslyng and Koch, 2018). Organic acids such as lactic acid, peracetic acid, and sorbic acid, can suppress the formation of trimethylamine by inhibiting microbial activity or chelating metal ions, thereby reducing the generation of volatile off-flavors in meat products. For instance, treatment of chicken leg meat with various concentrations of lactic acid was shown to significantly reduce odor intensity; similar effects were observed with other organic acids (González-Fandos et al., 2009). After soaking with a 1.0% NaCl solution for 60 min, the odor substances such as (E)-2-penten-1-ol dimer, (E)-3-penten-2-one, and methanethiol in the beef liver were significantly reduced, and the off-flavors value decreased significantly (Liu et al., 2022). Saline soaking also enhances the release of odorants from bovine liver, with optimal deodorization achieved at 1.0% salt concentration and 60 min of immersion (Duan et al., 2023). Treating beef by-products (beef liver, beef tongue, rumen, and beef heart) with NaCl can remove the pungent off-flavors substances and improve the products quality (Deng et al., 2025). In practical applications, acid-base-salt deodorization offers high efficiency and contributes to improved product quality by inhibiting microbial growth and reducing metabolically derived odorants. However, its implementation requires careful evaluation of production costs, safety, economic feasibility, and potential environmental impacts.

Antioxidants

Antioxidants eliminate off-flavors by neutralizing or inhibiting free radicals and oxidative reactions responsible for the development of such odors, thereby effectively reducing the characteristic off-flavors. On one hand, antioxidants mitigate the formation of volatile odor-causing compounds—such as aldehydes and ketones—by scavenging free radicals, thus delaying or preventing lipid peroxidation. On the other hand, through redox reactions, they transform malodorous compounds like amines and sulfides into odorless or less volatile substances. Additionally, antioxidants can chelate metal ions, thereby reducing their catalytic activity and slowing down oxidation processes. Natural antioxidants include vitamin C, terpenoids, tea polyphenols, among others. For example, the thymol extract can reduce the content of key volatile compounds such as 4-methyl-octanoic acid, 4-methyl-nonanoic acid, octanoic acid, decanoic acid, hexanal, heptanal, (E)-2-octen-1-al, and (E)-2-nonen-1-al in cooked sheep fat, thereby reducing the ‘sheep odor’ (Wu et al., 2023). Soriano et al. (2018) investigated the effects of antioxidant extracts from oak wood on the antioxidant activity, antibacterial activity, and volatile components of pork patties. They found that after treatment with this extract, the antioxidant capacity of the pork increased, the off-flavors caused by lipid oxidation was significantly reduced, and the pork cake had a slight sweetness. Adding mulberry polyphenols to Guangdong-style sausages can reduce the production and accumulation of aldehyde substances in the flavor components of the sausages by delaying the lipid degradation and secondary oxidation processes during storage (Hou et al., 2022). Soaking pigeon meat pieces in the tea juices of black tea, green tea and oolong tea for 40 min can significantly reduce the sensory off-flavors of the pigeon meat. The GC×GC-QTOF-MS analysis shows that after the tea juice treatment, the contents of alcohols, hexenol and heptenol in the pigeon meat have decreased to varying degrees (Yu et al., 2025). Ma et al. (2024) reported that soaking beef tripe for 30 min in a solution composed of 0.17 g/100 mL rosemry extract, 2.25 g/100 mL trehalose, and 0.64 g/100 mL sodium chloride significantly reduced the off-flavor. After treatment, the contents of off-flavors compounds such as hexanal, n-octanal, nonanal, and 1-octen-3-ol in the beef tripe were significantly reduced. Antioxidant-based deodorization is operationally simple and highly effective. However, limitations include partial nutrient loss in treated products, variable efficacy across different processing methods and product types, and compromised product stability. This approach is primarily suitable for deodorization and flavor modulation of oxidation-prone materials, especially in meat product processing.

Oxidizing agent

Oxidant-based deodorization involves the application of strong oxidizing agents to chemically react with volatile organic compounds responsible for off-flavors in meat, converting these malodorous substances into odorless or less perceptible volatile compounds with higher odor thresholds, thereby effectively reducing undesirable smells. Commonly employed oxidants include ozone and hydrogen peroxide. Ozone treatment has been shown to efficiently eliminate earthy and off-flavors from bighead carp meat. While ozone water treatment offers a milder oxidative deodorization approach compared to ozone gas flotation, it may still induce oxidation of myofibrillar proteins in fish tissue (Liu et al., 2021). A 20-min ozone treatment significantly reduces key off-flavor compounds such as 1-octen-3-ol, octanal, and nonanal in egg white protein powder, while increasing the concentration of terpene compounds with odor-masking properties by 213.81%, indicating a favorable deodorization effect (Xin et al., 2024). Qian et al. (2022) reported that an optimal gaseous ozone concentration of 1 or 3 mg/m3, applied for 10 min, is effective for salmon treatment. The deodorizing mechanism of ozone may be attributed to the decomposition of fatty acids and the suppression of phototrophic bacterial abundance. In surimi gel processing, rinsing with hydrogen peroxide can reduce the binding affinity between protein molecules and odor-causing odors molecules such as octanal and 1-octen-3-ol, facilitating the release of these volatile compounds and achieving deodorization (Wang et al., 2025). Although oxidants offer rapid and effective deodorization, residual oxidant levels may cause food safety concerns, and excessive treatment may exacerbate off-flavor development. Therefore, precise control over oxidant dosage and processing duration is essential to ensure both efficacy and product quality.

Maillard reaction

The Maillard reaction is a chemical process that generates flavor compounds such as pyrroles and pyridines, which contribute to masking undesirable odors and enhancing the color and taste of food products (Lee et al., 2025). The Maillard reaction is an effective method for improving the flavor quality of chicken bone extract. During the Maillard reaction process, the proportion of flavor amino acids in the chicken bone extract increases, and the volatile flavors undergo significant changes, resulting in an increase in the umami flavor and a reduction in the bitterness of the extract (Sun et al., 2014). After oxidation at 160-180 °C and Maillard reaction processing, the acidity and peroxide value of butter increased, while the off-flavor and acidity decreased. The meat, roasted, and burnt flavors were enhanced, and 2-hexenal, trans-2-heptenal (E,E)-2, 4-Heptadienal (E,E)-2, 4-Nondienal and other odorous aldehydes were not detected (Li et al., 2025). Wang et al. (2024) found that the present lentinan significantly enhanced the Maillard reaction degree of chicken liver hydrolysate. Moreover, in the Maillard reaction products, the contents of alcohols and heterocyclic compounds increased by 166.71% and 53.37% respectively, while the content of aldehydes decreased by 73.12%. The off-flavors of the products were significantly reduced. Adding an appropriate amount of yam polysaccharide (0.5%) can significantly increase the relative content of volatile compounds such as heterocyclic compounds and ketones by promoting the production of free amino acids, Maillard reaction, and Strecker degradation in sugar reduced pork jerky (SDMS), and inhibit the production of the fishy substance trimethylamine in SDMS, significantly improving the flavor of SDMS (Xiao et al., 2024). While the Maillard reaction is effective in improving sensory attributes such as flavor and color, it may also lead to the degradation of certain amino acids, proteins, and other essential nutrients.

Biological method

Biological deodorization is a method that utilizes microbial fermentation or enzymatic hydrolysis to transform and modify the molecular structure of malodorous compounds, thereby effectively eliminating undesirable odors.

Microbial fermentation

Microbial fermentation is a method in which microorganisms produce metabolic byproducts during the fermentation process that exhibit deodorizing properties. These metabolites can modulate environmental factors such as CO2 and O2 levels, and pH, thereby inhibiting the growth of putrefactive bacteria. Additionally, metabolic process generates enzymes or consumes the odor-containing groups, thereby reducing the production of off-flavors. Some metabolic products also possess aroma-enhancing and odor-masking capabilities, further improving sensory characteristics. Moreover, studies have demonstrated that specific microorganisms have special structures that can adsorb odor-causing substances. For example, yeast exhibits a loose, porous cell structure that enables effective adsorption of volatile fishy compounds (Zhao et al., 2025). When plant lactobacillus (Lactiplantibacilllus plantarum), pentosacoccus (Pediococcus pentosaceus) and staphylococcus carnosus (Staphylococcus carnosus) are added to the lamb meat and undergo appropriate fermentation treatment, the contents of odor-causing fatty acids such as 4-methyl-octanoic acid, 4-ethyl-octanoic acid and 4-methyl-nonanoic acid can be reduced, thereby minimizing the off-flavors (Zhou et al., 2022). It is reported that by fermenting the pig liver with yeast, the off-flavors can be effectively removed and its nutritional value can be enhanced (Lei and Wang, 2019). Zhao et al. (2018) used Bulgarian lactobacillus and Angel yeast to ferment pork and found that fermentation reduced the pH value of pork. During the fermentation process, the protease produced degraded some of the proteins in the pork, and also increased the content of free amino acids and fatty acids, significantly improving the meat quality and flavor. Yu and Tong. (2016) also demonstrated that yeast fermentation can effectively reduce the off-flavor of porcupine liver. After 35 °C fermentation with 1.2% yeast for 45 min, the types and contents of major volatile flavor substances such as aldehydes and ketones in the pig liver were significantly reduced. The contents of off-flavor components such as n-hexanal, 2-octen-1-al, 2-hepten-1-al, 1-octen-3-one, 1-octen-3-ol, and 2-n-propylfuran also decreased to varying degrees. Another study used lactic acid bacteria (Lactobacillus plantarum) and yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) to ferment duck leg meat. Their results indicate that lactic acid bacteria have a significant inhibitory effect on lipid peroxidation and produce more volatile flavor compounds; Saccharomyces cerevisiae breakdown protein to produce glutamic acid, which enhances umami taste, while lactic acid bacteria release alanine, significantly improving sweetness (Cai et al., 2020). The synergistic fermentation process employing lactic acid bacteria (Pediococcus pentose L7) and yeast (Candida parapsilosis Y22) effectively reduces the characteristic mutton odor in mutton sausages (Lan, 2023). The microbial fermentation-based deodorization method demonstrates a notable deodorizing efficacy; however, its application is limited to liquid and fermentable substrates. The prolonged fermentation process may alter the original flavor profile of the by-products, making it unsuitable for products requiring flavor preservation. Additionally, the instability of the resulting products poses potential food safety risks, thereby limiting its widespread industrial applicability.

Enzymatic deodorization

Enzymatic deodorization employs bioactive enzymes to specifically target and degrade malodorous compounds such as aldehydes, ketones, and alcohols into odorless byproducts, or to inhibit the formation of these volatile organic compounds responsible for undesirable odors. Flavor proteases can selectively hydrolyze peptide segments enriched with hydrophobic amino acids (e.g., leucine and valine) in myofibrillar proteins, thereby reducing the generation of bitter peptides and simultaneously releasing umami-enhancing amino acids such as glutamic acid, which help mask undesirable odors. Lipase inhibitors function by binding to serine residues within the active site of lipase, thereby suppressing the release of free fatty acids and minimizing oxidative substrates at the source (Zhu et al., 2015). Wang et al. (2019) investigated a deodorization method for chicken soup involving blanching combined with the addition of CaCl2 and a CaCl2-complexed lipase. The deodorization efficacy was evaluated based on concentrations of heptanal and (E)-2-nonenal, as well as sensory evaluation scores. Their results demonstrated that double blanching following pretreatment with CaCl2-complexed lipase effectively reduced the off-flavors and improved the overall sensory quality of the soup, outperforming single CaCl2 treatment. The chicken bone hydrolysate obtained by hydrolyzing compound flavor protease and neutral protease (in a ratio of 2.0:1) at 53.0 °C and pH 6.1 for 5 h has no bitter or off-flavors, and has excellent freshness and meat aroma. (Jiang et al., 2025). Furthermore, treatment with flavor protease, papain, and neutral protease has been shown to markedly decrease fishy-compound content in mackerel flakes, while increasing pyrazine and furan derivatives, thereby enhancing the overall aroma profile and yielding mackerel flakes with reduced fishiness (Yang et al., 2025).

Compound deodorization

Due to the diversity of volatile compounds responsible for off-flavors in meat products and the inherent limitations of individual deodorization techniques, single methods are often insufficient to achieve effective odor removal. In practice, a combination of multiple approaches is typically employed to enhance overall deodorization performance. For instance, the yeast, combined ultrasonic waves and chitosan composite deodorization method can effectively remove aldehyde substances from chicken feet. However, soaking in ozone water and soaking in ginger, garlic and cilantro extract solution will introduce new flavor substances to mask the off-flavors (Zhang et al., 2025). It is reported that duck meat was treated with an ethanol solution concentration of 46%, grape seed extract concentration of 0.35%, sodium chloride concentration of 4.3%, and a soaking time of 41 min. This treatment can significantly reduce the content of key aldehyde substances (such as nonaldehyde, heptaldehyde, pentaldehyde, decaldehyde, 2-methylbutanal, 3-methylbutanal, (E)-2-octen-1-ol, (E)-2-hepten-1-ol) and alcohols (such as 1-octen-3-ol) in the duck meat, thereby improving the flavor of the duck meat (Wang, 2023).

Utilizing the cavitation effect of ultrasound to alter protein hydrophobicity can prevent the binding of odorant compounds to myofibrillar proteins. Ultrasound-assisted chitosan treatment significantly reduced concentrations of 1-nonanol and 3-heptanone in bovine liver (Luo et al., 2022). Adding 1.00% yeast and fermenting at 30 °C, combined with soaking in a 5.6% salt solution for 78.00 min, can effectively remove the fishy smell from beef liver (Wang et al., 2018). The off-flavors substances in beef liver may include hexanal, (E,E)-2,4-nonadienal, E−2-octenal, nonanal, decanal, dodecanal, and nonanol. Luo et al. found that the combined treatment with ultrasound and chitosan was most effective in removing the off-flavor from beef liver (Luo et al., 2023). By enzymatically breaking down beef protein and combining with the Maillard reaction to prepare the base material for beef essence, the aroma of the product can be enhanced, the bitterness and off-flavors can be reduced, and the overall acceptance of the product can be improved (Chen, 2024).

Animal breeding and rearing

Animal breeding and rearing strategies are essential for controlling the off-flavors in meat at the source. Through precise nutritional management during the breeding process, the flavor profile of meat products can be effectively optimized. Studies have demonstrated that adding appropriate functional components, such as fatty acids, vitamins, and amino acids, in animal feed can regulate metabolic pathways within the animal body and reduce the synthesis and accumulation of off-flavors compounds. Luo (2019) found that by adding lactic acid bacteria and flaxseed to the daily diet of captive sunnite sheep, the formation of muttony odor such as 4-methyloctanoic acid and 4-ethyloctanoic acid in the sheep meat could be reduced, resulting in improved meat quality and a flavor profile more closely resembling that of grass-grazed mutton. Tannin is a compound that can form complexes with proteins, thereby inhibiting their rumen degradation. As a result, it can suppress the synthesis of some odor-active compounds. When tannin extracts (4%) such as Acacia mearnsii (CT), Castanea sativa (HT), or Caesalpinia spinosa (HT) are added to the diet and fed for 75 days, it is found that the “pastoral” odor in the perirenal fat of lambs is reduced (Silvia et al., 2020). Relevant studies have shown that fermented feed, through the metabolism and reproduction of microorganisms, decomposes or converts the macromolecular substances and anti-nutritional factors in the feed into substances that are beneficial for the growth and reproduction of livestock and poultry and are easily digestible and absorbable. Adding lactic acid bacteria fermented feed to the diet can effectively reduce the content of characteristic flavor substances such as hexanal, nonanal, 1-octen-3-ol, 2,3-octanedione, and 2-methyl furan in duck meat, and reduce the off-flavors (Xu, 2023). Sinapine in double-low rapeseed meal serves as a precursor to off-flavors in eggs. When laying hens are fed diets containing double-low rapeseed meal, the occurrence of fishy-smelling eggs may arise. However, this phenomenon is also influenced by the genotype of the laying hens and the inclusion level of rapeseed meal. When the inclusion level of double-low rapeseed meal is below 4.63%, the incidence rate of fishy-smelling eggs can be effectively reduced (Long et al., 2016). In addition, there are numerous factors influencing the flavor of meat, especially the intestinal microbiota and genetic factors. The genetic background may affect the colonization pattern of the intestinal microbiota, and the intestinal microbiota can regulate the host's muscle metabolism. Therefore, by regulating the fatty acid and amino acid metabolism of the duck's intestinal microbiota, the flavor of duck meat can be improved (Xie et al., 2026).

Advancements in breeding technologies have opened new avenues for developing low-odor meat products. By integrating traditional crossbreeding methods with modern molecular marker-assisted selection, it is possible to select animal breeds with excellent flavor genes. Take pig breeding as an example, marker-assisted selection targeting genes involved in the metabolism of androstenone and skatole has proven effective in reducing boar taint (Wood et al., 1996). Zhang (2024) employed multi-omics integration analysis to identify specific mutations in the ACSS2 and ACSF3 genes, as well as critical roles genes of MMUT and FASN, which may be closely associated with the development of muttony odor in goat milk. Additionally, the branched-chain amino acid degradation pathway has been implicated in the formation of characteristic off-flavors in goat milk. The transcription factor ZNF281 may negatively regulate ECHDC1 gene expression, thereby influencing the accumulation of odor-causing substances—a finding that offers potential targets for breeding goats with reduced or absent muttony odor. In Hulun Buir sheep, developmental changes in ruminal volatile fatty acid profiles and microbial composition have been observed, which may influence the deposition of muttony fatty acids in subcutaneous adipose tissue (Zhao et al., 2024). Chu et al. (2019) The researchers conducted molecular marker-assisted selection on the mutant sites of the fishy odor gene FMO3 in four breeding lines of Beijing oil chicken. They successfully eliminated the unfavorable sites of the fishy odor gene in these four lines. Through two detection methods - TaqMan probe and KASP - on the breeding chickens and commercial chickens from the hybrid offspring, no individuals carrying the T site were found. The results clearly indicated that the Beijing oil chicken population has completely eliminated the unfavorable sites of the off-flavors gene. Conclusions and future prospects

The formation of off-flavors in meat products is a complex process involving multiple pathways and factors, including bioaccumulation, metabolic transformation, chemical degradation, and processing-induced reactions. Although, in recent years, some progress has been made in the identification, formation mechanisms, and control strategies of off-flavors compounds. However, current research still exhibits notable limitations. For instance, there is more research on the mechanisms and regulation of off-flavors in aquatic products, but insufficient research on meat products from livestock and poultry. Moreover, most of the technologies focus on the processing stage, less attention has been paid to “reducing off-flavors from the source”. Furthermore, the basic research in the field of animal breeding and genetics is rather weak. The regulation of off-flavor through animal breeding is almost at an entirely unexplored stage, which makes it difficult to achieve long-term control of off-flavor at the genetic level.

Future research should focus on the accurate identification of characteristic compounds of off-flavors in livestock and poultry, and deeply explore the regulatory mechanisms of the biological synthesis of these odor substances by the breeding factors. By screening low off-flavors candidate genes to develop specialized animal breeds, complemented by the use of functional feeds that reduce off-flavors precursors at the source, and integrating post-slaughter green processing technologies, a comprehensive, full-process strategy for off-flavors mitigation can be achieved. Furthermore, emphasizing the application of natural ingredients, optimizing processing techniques, and leveraging omics-based approaches and intelligent equipment will enhance the precision and efficiency of off-flavors control. Ultimately, this integrated approach will facilitate a transformation in the meat industry from “passive deodorization” to “proactive odor control,” delivering meat products with superior flavor profiles and consistent quality to consumers.
