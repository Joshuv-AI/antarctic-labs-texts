Confirmed Treponema pallidum pleural effusion in patient with known syphilis
Authors: Rachel Shadbolt, Matthew Ing, Michaela Donaghy, Krist Ewe, Y. C. Gary Lee, Edward Fysh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPulmonary and pleural involvement in Treponema pallidum infection is uncommon. Previously reported syphilis‐related pleural effusions have been described largely based on clinical features combined with positive syphilis serology rather than the detection of T pallidum in pleural fluid or pleural biopsy. We report a case of secondary syphilis and confirmed syphilitic pleural effusion via thoracentesis.

INTRODUCTION

Syphilis is a sexually transmitted infection caused by the spirochete, Treponema pallidum. Involvement of the lung and pleura is uncommon, though rare pulmonary manifestations of secondary and tertiary syphilis have been described in case reports with the diagnosis based on clinical suspicion. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 We report a case of a Caucasian male in his twenties with known secondary syphilis and confirmed syphilitic pleural effusion on pleural fluid analysis.

CASE REPORT

A 22‐year‐old male presented to our hospital with a two‐week history of an erythematous maculopapular rash over his upper extremities and torso, dry cough and subjective fevers. He had recently been diagnosed with secondary syphilis (syphilis enzyme immunoassay (EIA) detected, TPPA positive, RPR 1:128) by his General Practitioner (GP) and started on weekly intramuscular (IM) benzathine penicillin 2.4 million units. A chest x‐ray demonstrated a small left‐sided pleural effusion.

The patient had no significant medical history and took no regular prescription medications. However, he reported current substance use including cigarette and marijuana smoking, previous intravenous methamphetamine and prescription opioid addiction. He also reported multiple female sexual partners over the preceding 6 months.

On physical examination he was afebrile, and his other vital signs were normal. There was a decrease in breath sounds and dullness to percussion at the left lung base. A mildly erythematous maculopapular diffuse rash was present over his chest, arms, back and abdomen.

Investigation results included a full blood picture, showing a white cell count of 11.61 × 109/L, platelets 451 × 109/L, neutrophils 7.93 × 109/L, eosinophils 0.94 × 109/L, with an elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate of 84 mm/h, and C reactive protein of 11 mg/L. Autoimmune screening was negative. Investigation of his peripheral eosinophilia showed normal total IgG, Aspergillus‐specific IgG and Strongyloides IgG. Hepatitis B, hepatitis C and HIV serology were also negative.

A chest x‐ray confirmed a moderate unilateral left‐sided pleural effusion (Figure 1), and subsequent CT chest showed normal underlying lung parenchyma. Bedside ultrasound, diagnostic thoracentesis and Abrams pleural biopsy were performed. Ultrasound revealed a simple, left‐sided pleural effusion with no loculations. The pleural fluid analysis confirmed a lymphocytic exudative effusion with glucose 4.2 mmol/L, LDH 458 U/L, pH 7.4, and protein 53 g/L. Routine culture‐based techniques did not grow any bacteria or fungi. Pleural fluid cytopathology reported numerous lymphocytes and organisms consistent with spirochetes. Treponema pallidum DNA was detected on the pleural fluid sample. The pleural biopsy confirmed mild pleural thickening associated with a lymphocytic infiltrate; however, T. pallidum DNA was not detected by PCR. Post‐pleural biopsy, an asymptomatic hydropneumothorax was noted on chest x‐ray, with an apical measurement of 7 mm (Figure 2 ). This was conservatively managed, and no other complications occurred.

The initial management included mandatory reporting, contact tracing, and preventative education. He was recommenced on weekly IM benzathine penicillin for 3 weeks. At the two‐week follow‐up, a repeat chest x‐ray showed improvement in the pleural effusion. He remained asymptomatic during a 10‐week virtual consultation.

DISCUSSION

We provide a case of a rare and atypical manifestation of secondary syphilis. With over 50 causes of pleural effusions being recognized, we entertained several differential diagnoses, including a drug‐induced, parapneumonic and malignancy‐related effusion. This case highlights the need for a systematic approach with patient‐focused interventions to guide the diagnosis of a unilateral pleural effusion. Thorough consideration and investigation of more common causes should precede testing for rarer conditions, based on individual patient presentations and risk factors. In settings where molecular diagnostics are available, syphilis should be considered as a differential after excluding more common causes, especially in patients with recently diagnosed syphilis.

Previous pulmonary syphilis cases have been described mostly based on a clinical diagnosis, less commonly on confirmation of T. pallidum detection in pleural fluid, bronchial alveolar lavage washings or biopsies. 1 , 2 , 3 Diagnoses have been based largely on Coleman et al. criteria combining typical clinical features, positive syphilis serology, no alternative cause and response to treatment. 1 , 2 T. pallidum cannot be cultured on artificial media. Therefore, the diagnosis of syphilis relies on a combination of microscopy, serological and molecular methods, depending on the stage of disease, clinical presentation and type of specimen obtained. 4 In secondary syphilis, peripheral serology is often reactive, and a real‐time PCR assay is useful for detecting T. pallidum DNA from clinical specimens when dark‐field microscopy is not available. Pleural fluid analysis from thoracentesis confirmed the diagnosis in our case with positive syphilis PCR. However, pleural biopsy was negative.

When considering the possibility of syphilis as a differential diagnosis for pleural effusion, it is essential to consider the patient's lifestyle, comorbidities and geographical risk factors. Previous pulmonary manifestations of secondary syphilis have been more frequently observed in patients with known HIV infection compared to those without, although there have been reports of cases where the HIV status of the patient was unknown. 3

In Australia, the incidence of syphilis infection has been on the rise over the past decade, with 5867 new cases reported in the 12 months leading up to September 2022. 5 This trend could lead to a greater occurrence of rare manifestations, emphasizing the importance of reporting such cases to improve our understanding of their presentation, diagnosis, and management going forward.

In summary, this case highlights the importance of considering syphilis as a potential rare differential diagnosis in patients presenting with pleural effusion and positive serology for syphilis. It also underscores the diagnostic value of thoracocentesis in conjunction with clinical features.
