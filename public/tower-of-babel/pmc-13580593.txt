Nonresonant SABRE provides an additional versatile hyperpolarization approach in magnetic resonance
Authors: Loren L. Smith, Warren S. Warren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hyperpolarization approaches in magnetic resonance overcome sensitivity limitations imposed by thermal magnetization and are integral to many modern applications. We begin with an overview of hyperpolarization. We then focus on one method, SABRE, which uses parahydrogen to form hydride transition metal complexes, followed by reversible exchange to polarize target molecules in solution. SABRE has produced large signal enhancements (up to 104) on many different molecules, cheaply and rapidly. Here, we demonstrate an unconventional SABRE strategy, applicable to a wide range of targets, that produces field-independent spin order in target molecules which efficiently converts to magnetization. The observed signal is even independent of field direction, and hyperpolarization can be achieved on a lab bench with no field control. We show that this signal arises from creation of two-spin order in targets and discuss ways this strategy should expand SABRE generality and efficiency. We also show that, often, the standard assumption that low-field SABRE requires a singlet-only starting state leads to incorrect results.

INTRODUCTION

Over eight decades after the first observations of bulk spin signals (1, 2), nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) have evolved into powerful and versatile tools with broad applications in chemistry, physics, biology, materials science, and medicine. However, these applications are almost always limited by sensitivity. Sometimes, that limitation is subtle: In MRI, for example, achievable resolution is many orders of magnitude finer than the exciting wavelength but, in practice, limited by the magnetization in each individual volume element. Sometimes, the limitation is more direct: NMR spectra characterize organic molecules much more cleanly than mass spectra do, but metabolomics is dominated by mass spectroscopy because the required concentrations are much smaller. The low sensitivity at thermal equilibrium arises because the energy difference between spin states is almost always very small compared to the Boltzmann factor kT for accessible magnetic fields and room temperatures, which implies that the detected signal (proportional to the bulk magnetization) arises from only a small fraction of the spins.

This sensitivity challenge has, for decades, driven the development of methods to enhance nuclear magnetization/polarization (the terms are used interchangeably) far past its equilibrium value. This is commonly called “hyperpolarization,” and in many cases, the produced magnetization is 100 to 10,000 times the room temperature thermal value. This paper begins with a broad overview of multiple methods, including current and projected strengths and limitations. We then focus on one of the newer hyperpolarization methods, variants of SABRE (signal amplification by reversible exchange), and identify a substantial omission in the general understanding of this method. Correcting this omission should lead to signal enhancements and new experimental strategies.

The starting point for discussing hyperpolarization is the fractional magnetization, which is tanh(ℏγBo2kT) at thermal equilibrium for a spin-1/2 system. For 1H at 30 T and T = 300 K, this is ≈10−4; for lower fields or isotopes with lower γ (13C and 15N), it can be much worse (see Fig. 1). The most obvious way to enhance the magnetization is drastic cooling (millikelvin to kelvin) in a strong magnetic field [“brute-force” hyperpolarization (3)], but this is slow; achieving equilibrium at these temperatures (as measured by the spin-lattice relaxation time T1) can take months (4). Instead, most hyperpolarization technologies share a common theme: transfer in nuclear spin order from something else, usually unpaired electron spin order. One way to get electron spin order is to introduce a paramagnetic species, sometimes by ionizing (5) or ultraviolet radiation (6), more often by doping the target of interest with a radical. Electrons have vastly larger fractional magnetization than nuclei (γe/γH ≈ 658), and magnetization is transferred by irradiating at or near electron resonance frequencies. These methods, first demonstrated in 1953 (7–9), are generally called dynamic nuclear polarization (DNP) (7, 10–15). Even if the total number of unpaired electrons is relatively small (millimolar concentration), electron T1 values are commonly far shorter than their nuclear counterparts, so a single electron can be reused to polarize many nuclear spins.

Sources of spin order for nuclear hyperpolarization.

This figure shows the usable spin order as a function of temperature at equilibrium in a 10-T magnet. For spins, this is the population difference between spin-up and spin-down. For parahydrogen (p-H2), as discussed in the text, this is the difference between singlet population (the even J rotational states) and one-third of the triplet population (the odd J rotational states). The difference between the three spin cases arises solely because of the different gyromagnetic ratios; the p-H2 differences arise because the rotational spacing effectively controls the spin populations.

Room temperature DNP, limited by γe/γH to a gain of several hundred, has recently become commercially available and is becoming popular in solid-state and protein NMR. It is worth noting that the many-decade delay between discovery and wide applications is partly because the original postulated mechanisms scale very unfavorably to modern high fields. Only with a better understanding of different mechanisms (which led, for example, to biradicals with electron line splittings comparable to nuclear spin frequencies) did high-field experiments become practical. Today, DNP can be done directly in the same magnet used for acquiring an NMR spectrum, although modern proton resonance frequencies above 1 GHz (hence electron resonance frequencies approaching 1 THz) present severe technical challenges, so often lower fields are used. A major frontier will be the transition from continuous-wave DNP to pulse methods, which can take advantage of the astonishing versatility of pulse sequences inherited from NMR and likely improve performance (16).

T1 for electrons typically remains <<1 s even at low temperatures, and near-unity electron spin magnetization can be achieved in a modest field. This means DNP can create very large magnetization enhancement in cryogenic samples without the problems facing brute-force magnetization discussed earlier. In a typical application, a glassy matrix of the target molecule, solvent, and a free radical polarizing agent is cooled to about 1 K and irradiated for many minutes. The cold sample can then be rapidly dissolved into a body temperature (310 K) solution without depolarization (this is called dissolution DNP or d-DNP) (15). Hundreds of different molecules have been polarized (17–25), and commercial instruments have been available for over two decades. The first clinical DNP application (26), metabolism of 13C-labeled pyruvate to alanine or lactate, has been validated as a prostate cancer marker and is currently in clinical trials, and other molecules such as HCO3− also show promise (27–29). In common applications, samples with roughly 10 mmol of polarized pyruvate are produced in a few tens of minutes (30, 31). The most important limitation is that polarization is costly per sample because of the required drastic cooling, rapid heating, and long microwave irradiation, and further scalability is limited because millimeter-wave fields penetrate bulk samples poorly.

Nuclear spin polarization can also be produced by optical pumping, mediated by electron spin states. For example, in spin-exchange optical pumping (SEOP) (32–37), a circularly polarized laser polarizes electron spins in Rb vapor, which then collide to polarize nuclear spins in 3He, 83Kr, or 129Xe (38–43). The origins of this field are even older than DNP (32, 33), but again what was needed was decades of careful experimental and theoretical work, for example, to develop gas mixtures (including N2) that increased the efficiency and high-power solid-state lasers at the right frequency. The outcome was that 129Xe lung imaging has actually received Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval (44), but no molecular targets are possible. It is also possible to pump nitrogen vacancy (NV) centers in diamond (45) or triplet states in organic molecules (photochemically induced DNP or photo-CIDNP) (46–51) and take advantage of intrinsic differences in relaxation rates to create electron (and ultimately nuclear) polarization. Although these methods are subjects of active research, the range of targets is somewhat restricted, and they are generally limited in producing large magnetization by the einstein (E) (the traditional name for a mole of photons). For visible light (λ = 600 nm), 1 E is 200,000 joules, and almost all of which is lost in the polarization process as the energy difference between nuclear spin states is trivial. For SEOP, where the sample is a mixture of gases, this problem is surmounted by massive powers (often >100 W) and long exposure times, leading to typical delivered volumes of 100 mmol of 129Xe at >50% polarization in an hour (52). The other optical methods cannot use comparable powers without sample destruction and are restricted to much smaller numbers of excited spins.

The focus of this paper is on yet another source of spin order, parahydrogen [p-H2; the singlet-state S≡(∣αβ⟩−∣βα⟩)/2 of gaseous H2]. 1H nuclei are fermions, thus antisymmetric with respect to exchange. Even rotational states are symmetric, so the ground rotational state J = 0 is purely singlet spin state. Odd J states are antisymmetric, so they pair with the “ortho” nuclear states T+≡∣αα⟩,T0≡(∣αβ⟩+∣βα⟩)/2,T−≡∣ββ⟩ (o-H2). The energy separation between J = 0 and J = 1 is E/k = 175 K, 104 times the 1H spin splitting in a large magnetic field, so p-H2 dominates at equilibrium at low temperatures. Figure 1 shows the temperature dependence of the spin order (the total singlet population minus one-third of the total triplet population as the triplet states interconvert readily), which is far higher than even electron magnetization at moderately low temperatures and is still larger than 1H nuclear polarization at room temperature. With a suitable catalyst to break the symmetry and allow singlet-triplet interconversion (53–56) (even rust works well), a system of 40 K can create liters per hour of 95% p-H2. Industrial-scale preparation of p-H2 has been extensively demonstrated as conversion to the para state is essential for stability of liquid H2 rocket fuel.

However, the feature which makes p-H2 truly interesting is that, at room temperature without catalyst or oxygen, excess p-H2 can be stable for months (57). Thus, p-H2 is an extraordinary “quantum reagent” in a metastable, pure spin state, which can be made in large quantities at high spin purity and low cost (58, 59). Rotational Raman shows that the ortho-para separation is retained for H2 in solution (60), which is fundamental for the NMR methods below to work.

Until recently, p-H2 was mostly used in NMR by addition across inequivalent carbon double or triple bonds, and then pulse sequences converted singlet order into nuclear polarization (61–66). This so-called parahydrogen–induced polarization (PHIP) initially lagged DNP in generality because each target molecule requires different optimization (often including a different catalyst), but again this is a rapidly evolving field. For example, pyruvate can now be hyperpolarized by synthesizing a “side arm” derivative with a double bond, adding p-H2 across that bond, relaying magnetization, and cleaving the side arm (67). Target molecules are limited to those which have a double or triple bond, and optimization of hyperpolarization parameters is time and material intensive.

This paper focuses on a different method, SABRE (Fig. 2) introduced by Duckett and co-workers in 2009 (68–72), sometimes called nonhydrogenative PHIP. SABRE works by reversibly binding p-H2 and target ligands (such as pyridine in Fig. 2) to an octahedral transition metal complex, usually iridium-based. Under the right circumstances (see Fig. 2), as discussed later, para order in the hydrides is converted to negative ligand (and positive hydride) magnetization. After both p-H2 and ligand exchange (typically ligand exchange is much faster), the Ir atom can catalyze another transfer. SABRE directly polarizes 1H on the ligand target, typically at a field around 6.5 mT. Methods for direct heteronuclear polarization such as SABRE-SHEATH (73) go by the general name X-SABRE and typically use fields around 0.6 μT, about 1/100 of Earth’s field. There are now at least a dozen such methods with a wide range of applications. This paper will show that those field constraints can be readily overcome.

Comparison of different SABRE variants.

In all cases, p-H2 and a target ligand (here pyridine) bind reversibly in the equatorial positions of an Ir complex. Typically, one axial position has an N-heterocyclic carbene, as illustrated here. In SABRE, four-bond couplings between the hydride and target 1H atoms create nuclear spin polarization, commonly 1000 to 3000 times larger than what is observed at thermal equilibrium in even a large magnet (only one proton is highlighted for simplicity). In X-SABRE methods, the much larger two-bond couplings between the hydride and 15N create polarization. Here, we demonstrate a third mechanism, nonresonant SABRE, which directly creates heteronuclear two-spin order in the ligands. This has vastly different properties from the first two approaches.

An “ideal” hyperpolarization method would be simple, rapid, readily scalable, and completely general. For real methods, Table 1 summarizes the currently demonstrated (and projected) advantages and disadvantages. At the moment, DNP leads the other molecular methods in terms of quantity produced, but PHIP and SABRE are often within an order of magnitude of DNP and should in principle be more scalable. The cost difference between the p-H2 systems and DNP systems is substantial (today, 100 to 1000 cost ratio). This vastly limits routine DNP applications, and it is hard to see that changing anytime soon due to continued reliance on high frequency microwave irradiation and temperature cycling. DNP is a very general polarization strategy with relatively few constraints as to target. A double/triple bond and appropriate catalyst (PHIP) or lone pairs (SABRE) are needed in the p-H2 methods, but those appear widely. In addition, side-arm hydrogenation for PHIP (67) and relayed magnetization through exchangeable protons for SABRE (74) further expand the range of interesting molecular targets. NV-based approaches might eventually end up becoming quite general if the challenges with getting polarization out of the diamond sample can be overcome. PHIP and SABRE often produce nearly maximum polarization in seconds, whereas the other methods commonly use minutes to hours.

Thus, there is no single best hyperpolarization method, but SABRE and X-SABRE (where we focus from now on) avoid all cooling, can hyperpolarize in seconds, are not as limited in sample volume as DNP, and have been applied to a wide variety of molecules. Today, many groups work on different aspects including methods that use shaped three-dimensional (3D) magnetic fields or work in a high-field magnet using radio frequency (rf) pulses (75–92). However, the “workhorse” experiment since the beginning has used a constant magnetic field and no rf irradiation. There are two basic regimes: the traditional SABRE case, where this constant field is matched to make the hydride scalar coupling comparable to the frequency difference between hydride protons and target protons (≈6.5 mT), and the SABRE-SHEATH case, where the field is matched to the frequency difference between hydride protons and target heteronuclei such as 15N and 13C [≈0.5 μT, achieved in a mu-metal shield (73)]. In both cases, the matching condition is generally justified as an example of level anticrossings, although this does not quite work in the limit of large couplings as has been detailed elsewhere (93). Still, as Fig. 3 shows, well-validated simulations (93, 94) reproduce the experimental field dependence well. SHEATH experiments are constrained by inconvenient field strengths that require a mu-metal shield to achieve, and the high-field experiments are very sensitive to experimental complications such as resonance frequency difference between the two hydride spins.

Here, we show (to be honest, to our surprise) that this picture is an oversimplification. For a wide range of target ligands that have both protons and heteronuclei (including the most heavily studied molecules in the field), there is a highly efficient path to two-spin order in the target, which can be quantitatively converted to observable magnetization competitive with SABRE and X-SABRE. Over the field regime between SABRE and X-SABRE, this produces magnetization independent of field strength and direction—in other words, inverting the field has no effect on the magnetization. For this reason, we call this effect nonresonant SABRE. Magnetization can even be produced by bubbling directly on a tabletop with no external magnet or shield, further simplifying the SABRE method. Although antiphase line shapes resulting from hyperpolarized heteronuclear two-spin order have been observed in 13C, 15N, and 19F in studies as early as 2009 (69, 73, 83, 95), previous conceptualizations of this signal treat the two-spin order as a field-dependent by-product of the hyperpolarization process, rather than a potential target. To our knowledge, the mechanisms which lead to this two-spin order have never been considered in detail, and strategically generating two-spin hyperpolarization may lead to many options for SABRE improvement in other regimes.

RESULTS

Experimental demonstration of nonresonant SABRE

We start by presenting experimental evidence for nonresonant SABRE. Figure 4 shows hyperpolarized free induction decays (FIDs) and spectra for 320 mM 15N-pyridine at two specific fields: one which matches the well-known SABRE-SHEATH condition and one that is about a factor of 100 away. As the field strength increases, the spectrum transitions smoothly from in-phase to antiphase and then remains constant over a wide variety of field strengths. Far from resonance, the FID is zero immediately after a 90 pulse (implying there is no one-spin z magnetization), but the FID grows in at later times. The first maximum occurs at time 1/(4JNH) where JNH is the coupling between the pyridine 15N and the ortho protons (≈−10 Hz).

Experimental one-scan 15N signal from 320 mM 15N-pyridine after excitation at the peak of the SABRE-SHEATH resonance (0.55 μT) and a factor of 100 away from that resonance (50.0 μT).

The off-resonance signal is nearly zero immediately after the pulse, implying that no z magnetization was directly produced. However, substantial signal grows in at later times. The amplitude and phase of the FID of the off-resonance signal are nearly independent of field over the range of 5 to 500 μT, including inverting the field. 1H or 15N π/2 pulses applied after the polarization time produce nearly equal 1H or 15N fractional magnetization, respectively (see the Supplementary Materials).

Figure 5 shows this field dependence over about three orders of magnitude. As shown in the Supplementary Materials, detection of 1H magnetization instead of 15N magnetization at fields far from the SHEATH condition reveals signal comparable to the 15N magnetization, with similar antiphase behavior.

We note several experimental observations which contribute to the analysis below:

First, we stopped the bubbling for a variable period before pulsing the sample after moving it from the polarizing field to the 1-T measurement magnet and saw much faster attenuation of the off-resonance signal (T1 = 12.46 ± 0.26 s after polarization buildup at 50 μT) than was observed for the conventional SABRE-SHEATH signal (T1 = 96.91 ± 5.86 s after polarization at 0.5 μT). When left in the polarizing field for variable time before transferring to the 1 T, the two signals showed fast and roughly comparable relaxation, with the 50-μT signal relaxing with a time constant of 7.82 ± 0.50 s and the 0.5-μT signal with 4.95 ± 0.10 s.

Second, we performed SABRE at three different points in the lab, without a shield, all with roughly 80-μT field aligned substantially away from the z axis of the magnet used to detect the magnetization, and all gave comparable antiphase signal.

Last, as seen in Fig. 5, inverting the direction of the field has no effect on the off-resonance FID.

In all of the pathways, the first step is creation of three-spin order with both hydride protons and one of the ligand nuclei. The net effect of the next two steps is to transform this into three-spin order with only the coupled hydride proton and two of the ligand nuclei, with either a two-spin or a four-spin state as the intermediate. Evolution of an AX2 model, which is a better match for pyridine, has a different time dependence and is described later in the text.

A qualitative way to understand nonresonant SABRE is that the newly added couplings 2πJHSIˆ1zSˆz+2πJLSLˆzSˆz perturb the local environment of I1 and L depending on the spin state of S. When spin S is up, a positive J coupling will result in a larger local field experienced by L (or I). So long as JHS ≠ JLS, this creates a resonance frequency difference between spin I1 and spin L, just as the term ΔωHL did in the original SABRE derivation. Thus, the coupling plays the same role as the magnetic field did in the SABRE case. If JHS-JLS > 0 and spin S is up, the hydride spin is at the higher frequency; if spin S is down, the hydride spin is at the lower frequency and the produced L magnetization is inverted. This means that the order is two-spin rather than one-spin.

Antiphase magnetization is not directly observed in magnetic resonance. In normal NMR experiments, only transverse magnetization Lˆx, Lˆy, Sˆx, or Sˆy induces a voltage in the detection coil. Such a signal is created immediately after a π/2 pulse from Lˆz or Sˆz and gives an absorptive line shape (nonzero area after Fourier transformation of the FID). In contrast, Eq. 24 shows that these two-spin operators only create signal after a time comparable to 1/(2JLS) and the area of the peak is zero (see Fig. 4). This can be fixed by delaying the start of acquisition to the maximum signal time, but this is not an issue as long as that time is short compared to the homogeneous relaxation time T2*. This is commonly the case for most SABRE imaging targets (13C-pyruvate and its derivatives, for example, show relaxation on the order of 500 ms or longer). Inhomogeneous broadening can be eliminated by simply inserting simultaneous π pulses on both L and S at time 1/(4JLS), similar to a refocused INEPT experiment.

It is important to note that, although many multispin operators will be produced in a fourth-order commutator, the only multispin operators that are productive for SABRE are those that are contained on the ligand and thus are preserved when chemical exchange occurs. In addition, only the operators that do not evolve under the applied magnetic field will coherently build up. Therefore, we focus on the three operators that fit those criteria: Sˆz, Lˆz, and LˆzSˆz.

This derivation reproduces the experimental features in Figs. 4 and 5. It is independent of the applied field magnitude and direction (subject to the constraint above, which we are only considering the secular part of the JLS coupling). Inverting the field, or pointing it in an arbitrary direction, makes no difference. This mechanism is equally effective at producing L or S magnetization. In the regime where neither SABRE nor SABRE-SHEATH is efficient (e.g., 10 to 1000 μT), this pathway will dominate; in other regimes, it competes. Last, because it arises from two-spin order, the nonresonant pathway does not share one important advantage SABRE-SHEATH has over SABRE: the ability to store magnetization on a longer-lived spin. In the 10- to 1000-μT regime, 1H relaxation is much faster than 13C or 15N relaxation, so in practice, the two-spin order decays away with essentially the 1H relaxation time. At lower fields, couplings connect different spins, and heteronuclear magnetization leaks into neighboring protons, so the buildup and decay dynamics are often different.

There are a few nuances to how this magnetization may be used experimentally. Note from Eq. 24 that, at time t=0, there will be no observable signal in the FID. This indicates that the integral of the resulting Fourier-transformed NMR spectrum will be zero, so either the signal must be converted to in-phase or an alternative metric must be used to quantify it. There are several methods that have been applied historically to convert antiphase magnetization to net magnetization, including the use of a refocused INEPT sequence in many common 2D NMR techniques, so we do not repeat the work here.

The 20 pathways (in the Supplementary Materials) from I⇀1·I⇀2 to LˆzSˆz include 8 terms of the form JHL2JHHJLS (all of which lead to positive LˆzSˆz) and 12 terms of the form JHL2JHHJHS (six leading to positive LˆzSˆz and six to negative LˆzSˆz), so the expected functional dependence is JHL2JHHJLS. This functional dependence reveals another interesting feature of the nonresonant mechanism. The eight pathways from Iˆ1zIˆ2z to LˆzSˆz in Eqs. 16 to 23 include four terms of the form JHL2JHHJLS (all of which lead to positive LˆzSˆz) and four terms of the form JHL2JHHJHS (all of which lead to negative LˆzSˆz), which is this exact functional dependence. This strongly suggests that the only term that matters in the initial density matrix for the long-time behavior is the Iˆ1zIˆ2z term, which is equivalent to a 50:50 mixture of singlet (αβ-βα) and triplet (αβ+βα). This was verified numerically by replacing the initial condition in Eq. 1 withρˆ0=(141ˆ−Iˆ1zIˆ2z)⊗1ˆ(27)and replenishing magnetization with the same 50:50 mixture, which, as Fig. 9 shows, has virtually no impact on the predicted signal. This is also true to a lesser extent in normal SABRE-SHEATH (data shown in the Supplementary Materials). In effect, what the simulation is showing is that, when ligand exchange is much more rapid than hydride exchange, the effect of the JHLIˆ1zLˆz and JHSIˆ1zLˆz couplings (which commute with Iˆ1zIˆ2z but not Iˆ1xIˆ2x or Iˆ1yIˆ2y) is to convert the hydride Iˆ1xIˆ2x and Iˆ1yIˆ2y order into three-spin terms (e.g., Iˆ1yIˆ2xLˆz), which do not survive ligand dissociation. Thus, in the usual limit where the hydride dissociation is at least an order of magnitude slower than the ligand dissociation, only the Iˆ1zIˆ2z term is an effective hyperpolarization source most of the time. This is also a known issue at high fields due to para-ortho interconversion (96, 97).

DISCUSSION

Inclusion of the additional pathways that create two-spin magnetization has several practical consequences that have not yet been fully explored but are likely meaningful for SABRE applications:

The pathways are independent of magnetic field over a broad range, but additional terms come in at frequencies lower than the SABRE-SHEATH or higher than the SABRE condition. This will lead to additional optimal frequencies, particularly in the SABRE case. For example, the C─H coupling in a methyl group 13CH3 is typically about 130 Hz. This implies that, if the carbon is spin-down, the methyl protons have an intrinsic 130-Hz difference between spin up and spin down, which is the same as the frequency difference those protons would have from the hydride protons at 120 mT [assuming a chemical shift difference of 25 parts per million (ppm)]. In the normal SABRE condition, the frequency difference of 25 ppm between the hydride and methyl protons gives about a 7-Hz frequency difference at 6.5 mT. However, this suggests an additional resonance would exist offset by this amount from 120 mT, where this field cancels out the JCH coupling. In this case, spin-down 13C nuclei allow polarization of 1H (because the ligand-hydride resonance frequency difference is matched to the traditional SABRE condition) but spin-up 13C nuclei produce a large frequency difference so they are inactive. Mathematically, this implies normal 1H and antiphase 1H-13C magnetization are produced in equal quantities. This resonance will move with the chemical shift difference (so it provides some selectivity) and is near the range of commercially available low-field MRI scanners (89). This effect is also likely responsible for anomalous observations of SABRE resonances near 100 mT in 13C-labeled pyridines (83).

Although we have only explicitly considered the case of 1H plus a heteronucleus, there are likely extensions to two heteronuclei. One example is the 15N-77Se case, which has recently been explored experimentally (98) and to direct pumping of homonuclear singlets such as in diazirine (99, 100). In the latter case, direct fourth-order pathways from hydride singlet order to ligand singlet order (e.g., I⇀1·I⇀2→JHLIˆ1zIˆ2yLˆx(cyc)→JHHI⇀1·L⇀→JLSIˆ1xLˆySˆz(cyc)→JHLS⇀·L⇀) are also field independent.

Most of the terms in the Hamiltonian in Eq. 14 are readily controlled, inverted, or cancelled by rf pulses in the standard ways known in NMR for over half a century. This provides a different strategy for doing high-field SABRE than has been commonly explored. Bubbling a SABRE sample at high fields, say 1 T, will not result in observable nonresonant signal because the resonance frequency difference between the hydride protons and the ligand proton (over 1 kHz at 1 T) dominates the system and truncates the transverse components of the I⇀·L⇀ coupling. This may easily be circumvented by using multiple echo sequences on both the proton and the heteronucleus during SABRE buildup to remove chemical shift evolution while preserving the full scalar coupling. Note that these 180° pulses must be applied to I, L, and S to preserve the LˆzSˆz being built up while averaging out the ΔωHL term. This brings us back into the regime where the rest of the nonresonant SABRE mechanism may proceed as usual.

There are likely substantial advantages to this strategy for high-field SABRE experiments because most existing methods are highly sensitive to the population difference between hydride singlet (αβ-βα) and the triplet state (αβ+βα). For example, the first such experiment (LIGHT-SABRE) (101) showed explicitly that polarization is created by weak irradiation at the nitrogen frequency, interchanging states with those two different hydride characters. As noted earlier, the fragility of the Iˆ1xIˆ2x and Iˆ1yIˆ2y order implies a strong tendency to equalize those populations (Iˆ1zIˆ2z has an equal population of αβ-βα and αβ+βα), which cancels the LIGHT-SABRE effect. Thus, nonresonant SABRE works fine with the Iˆ1zIˆ2z order, whereas most existing high-field SABRE approaches do not. This has been addressed in some recent papers (97, 102, 103) with relatively complex pulse sequences, but using the nonresonant mechanism will likely improve pulse sequence strategies.

Last, extensive optimization of SABRE-SHEATH approaches by computer has led to robust, 3D fields that are accessible in the laboratory (104). To our knowledge, none of these calculations have ever tried to optimize anything other than magnetization (⟨Sˆz⟩). Feeding two-spin as well as one-spin order into optimization approaches is likely to improve results as the pathways are largely independent of one another.

We have demonstrated a hitherto unappreciated parahydrogen–based hyperpolarization mechanism, which involves direct creation of two-spin zz order in the target ligand. Although here we have presented data mostly for pyridine, the mechanism should be accessible for essentially every molecule that has been hyperpolarized by SABRE and has a heteroatom near the hydrogen atoms, and we have found experimental signals in other common molecules such as acetonitrile (see the Supplementary Materials). One reason this may have gone undetected until now is relatively simple. In normal hyperpolarization experiments, magnetization is detected by the signal directly after a 90 pulse, so inhomogeneous broadening in the spectrometer is not important. Thus, SABRE testing is often done without removing the bubbling apparatus (which causes susceptibility effects). This is a critical problem for detecting the antiphase magnetization in nonlinear SABRE, particularly if (unlike the pyridine case) the coupling JLS is small as the signal disappears before it can refocus. This is of course easily solved in the laboratory; it was just that it was not realized that it might be important.

The nonresonant mechanism has multiple desirable features. These include near independence of external field strength over multiple orders of magnitude (it can be done quite well in an uncontrolled lab setting in Earth’s field) and low sensitivity to the coupling-induced interconversion between hydride singlet and triplet order. It also suffers from a few drawbacks, including fast relaxation (compared to traditional X-SABRE experiments) and the need for an echo pulse to counteract inhomogeneous broadening in imaging applications. Overall, this mechanism likely provides multiple new strategies for SABRE optimization at a wide variety of fields. This amplifies the advantages of SABRE (a cheap and simple source of spin order as well as simple and low-cost apparatus) as the current best candidate for a truly general and versatile hyperpolarization strategy.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample preparation

15N-Pyridine SABRE samples were prepared with 160 mM 15N-pyridine (Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, NLM 305-0.5, CAS 34322-45-7) and 10 mM Ir-IMes catalyst [IMes = 1,3-bis(2,4,6-trimethylphenyl)imidazol-2-ylidene; synthesized by a collaborator] in methanol-d4 (Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, DLM-24-50, CAS 811-98-3) solvent. For the representative spectra shown in Fig. 4, samples additionally contained 10% (v/v) methanol (Sigma-Aldrich, 34860-4L-R, CAS 67-56-1) so that the measurement magnet could be shimmed on the sample to help reduce inhomogeneity resulting from the presence of the H2 gas bubbler in the NMR tube. The sample tube was a Wilmad 5 mm sapphire NMR tube (WG-507-7) custom fitted to adapt to the H2 gas line which was pressurized to 120 psi. The Ir-IMes catalyst was activated by bubbling H2 gas through the sample tube at a rate of 16.15 cm3/min for 1 hour at 40°C. Roughly half of the sample volume was lost to evaporation during activation, causing the final pyridine concentration to be ∼320 mM.

Procedure for nonresonant SABRE experiments

For each SABRE experiment, the sample was bubbled with 80 to 90% p-H2 gas (produced with a Bruker BPHG 90 generator) at a rate of 43.46 cm3/min for 5 s in the presence of the polarizing field. After this polarization buildup period, bubbling was shut off, and the electromagnet was triggered to output a field with a magnitude of ≈50 μT to control the transfer into the lab field. The sample was then transferred into a Magritek Spinsolve benchtop 1-T spectrometer and 15N and 1H spectra were acquired back-to-back. A repetition time of 200 s was allowed between SABRE experiments to allow for hyperpolarized 15N magnetization to fully relax.

A thermal reference sample containing 2.72 M 15N-pyridine in methanol-d4 was created, and a 250-scan-averaged reference spectrum was recorded using the same NMR tube and measurement magnet as the presented experiments. The signal intensity from the thermal reference was scaled down by a factor of 8.5 to control for concentration differences. Enhancement values were obtained by dividing the SABRE sample signal by this concentration-scaled thermal value.

Simulation methodology

The simulation framework used here to model SABRE dynamics has been reported in ref. (94). The 15N-pyridine SABRE complex was modeled as either a four-spin (Fig. 7 and figs. S6 to S8) or a five-spin (Figs. 3 and 9, and figs. S2 and S4) system, which includes two 1H (H) from bound p-H2 as well as one 15N (S) and one or two ortho 1H (L) from a bound pyridine. The SABRE complex was treated as a Y system, although it is known that pyridine creates an X geometry in which it binds both available equatorial positions of the SABRE catalyst. Simulations that accounted for the X geometry showed analogous dynamics to the Y geometry (just with twice the efficiency because pyridine is bound to both positions), so Y geometry was preferred for computational efficiency. Unless otherwise specified, this system used the following J-coupling values: JHH=−7 Hz, JHS=19.6 Hz, JHL=1.2 Hz, JLS=−10.06 Hz, and JLL=−0.16 Hz. Simulations included a chemical shift of 30.75 ppm between H and L. The longitudinal relaxation times were assumed to be: T1,H=1 s, T1,L=4 s, and T1,S=40 s. As stated in the main text, the simulations accounted for a ligand exchange rate of 16 s−1 a hydride exchange rate of 2 s−1, a 1:20 catalyst to ligand ratio, and assumed that p-H2 flowed continuously ([Ir]/[H2] is negligible). Unless otherwise specified, SABRE performance was evaluated for a given test condition by allowing the system to evolve in the polarizing field for 5 s before a 90y pulse was applied to either 15N or 1H and a simulated FID was acquired by allowing a further 5 s of evolution to proceed at B0=1 T.
