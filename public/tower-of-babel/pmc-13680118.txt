Safety Reassured: Macrocyclic GBCA Shows No Increased Risk of Parkinsonism
Authors: Won-Jin Moon
Published: 2026

Commentary on Kanda T, et al. High signal intensity in the dentate nucleus and globus pallidus on unenhanced T1-weighted MR images: relationship with increasing cumulative dose of a gadolinium-based contrast material. Radiology 2014;270:834-841 (https://doi.org/10.1148/radiol.13131669).

SUMMARY

Ever since brain accumulation of gadolinium-based contrast agent (GBCA), especially in globus pallidus, was reported by Kanda et al. (1), the relationship between GBCA and neurodegeneration disease—in particular, Parkinson disease—has attracted significant academic attention in the field of radiology. Welk et al.’s 2016 (2) large population study showed no association between GBCA exposure and the incidence of parkinsonism. However, the effects of different types of GBCA on the risk of parkinsonism remain underexplored.

Shin et al. (3) analyzed patients aged 40 years or older who underwent their first MRI examination between 2011 and 2014. Patients were stratified into a contrast-enhanced group—further subdivided into a linear GBCA group and a macrocyclic GBCA group—and a non-contrast group. Those undergoing brain or spine MRI were excluded to minimize selection bias from underlying neurological disease. The primary outcome was defined as all-cause parkinsonism to capture potential gadolinium-induced toxic parkinsonism. The incidence of all-cause parkinsonism, Parkinson’s disease (PD), atypical parkinsonism, and secondary parkinsonism was assessed. Using the non-contrast MRI group as the reference, hazard ratios (HRs) for each contrast agent group were calculated using a multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression model.

A total of approximately 220,000 health insurance claims records were analyzed. Exposure to linear GBCAs was associated with a 13% increased risk of all-cause parkinsonism {adjusted HR 1.13, (97.5% confidence interval [CI], 1.07–1.19)}, and this elevated risk was particularly pronounced in patients aged 65 years or older. Sex did not influence the relationship between linear GBCA exposure and all-cause parkinsonism. In contrast, no increase in risk was observed in the macrocyclic GBCA group (adjusted HR 1.00, [97.5% CI, 0.95–1.05]). Similar trends were identified for treatment-requiring all-cause parkinsonism, PD, and secondary parkinsonism, whereas no statistically significant difference was observed for atypical parkinsonism.

In conclusion, the authors suggest that linear GBCAs may modestly increase the risk of developing parkinsonism, whereas macrocyclic GBCAs appear to be unrelated to parkinsonism.

COMMENT

This well-designed, large-scale retrospective study using the Korean National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) claims database strengthens the evidence that macrocyclic GBCAs are not associated with parkinsonism risk.

However, the 13% increased risk observed with linear GBCAs should be interpreted with caution. The linear GBCA-exposed group was, on average, 3.6 years older than the non-contrast controls, and because the incidence of neurodegeneration such as PD rises exponentially with age, this age gap alone may substantially account for the observed risk difference (45). Moreover, the pathophysiological mechanism by which gadolinium retention might induce parkinsonism remains unknown, warranting cautious interpretation. Although gadolinium deposition occurs predominantly in the globus pallidus, this structure is not the primary site of neurodegeneration in PD (6).

Earlier this year, another nationwide NHIS analysis reported contradictory findings (7). However, that study presents critical methodological flaws. Foremost, the control group was not restricted to patients undergoing non-enhanced MRI, and the anatomical region of the MRI was ignored. Including brain and spine MRIs likely captured patients with prodromal PD, thereby overestimating the GBCA-associated risk. Additionally, the study limited its outcome solely to PD and failed to adjust for essential lifestyle covariates. However, Shin et al. (3) strengthened their study design by restricting the control group strictly to non-enhanced MRI patients, significantly minimizing bias. Excluding initial brain and spine MRIs effectively prevented reverse causality. Furthermore, they comprehensively adjusted for various lifestyle factors and applied rigorous outcome definitions using specific medication and strict registration codes.

A fundamental limitation of this study is that insurance claims data cannot provide key information such as GBCA dose, route, or cumulative exposure (5). Nevertheless, this study provides significant evidence to alleviate unwarranted anxiety regarding contrast-enhanced MRI, and to support the rational application of clinically indicated examinations.
