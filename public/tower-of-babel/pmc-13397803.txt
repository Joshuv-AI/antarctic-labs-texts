Functional potential of the APETALA2 transcription factors StAP2-13/18 in modulating tuberization under long-day (LD) conditions in potato
Authors: Zefeng Zhai, Haicai Li, Jinfu Lyu, Zhen Guo, Yongguang Liu, Honglian Gu, Chengfeng Wang, Yun Zhang, Weihong Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTuberization represents the most critical biological process during the growth and development of potato (Solanum tuberosum L.), as it directly dictates the final yield and quality of the crop. The APETALA2 (AP2) family of transcription factors (TFs) serves as key regulators of plant development, orchestrating diverse processes including organogenesis, hormone signaling, and environmental adaptation. Nevertheless, the precise functions of StAP2 genes in potato tuberization remain elusive, limiting our understanding of the molecular regulatory network underlying this agriculturally important trait.ResultsA total of 18 StAP2 genes, designated StAP2-1 to StAP2-18 according to their chromosomal positions in the potato genome, were identified. Herein, we present a comprehensive genome-wide systematic analysis of these StAP2 genes, encompassing their phylogenetic relationships, conserved motif characteristics, and tuberization-related expression profiles. Our expression data demonstrated that StAP2-13 and StAP2-18 are most likely involved in potato tuberization, as evidenced by their marked upregulation in tuberization-related leaves and developing tubers, respectively. In addition, both StAP2 genes exhibit distinct circadian rhythmic expression patterns - a regulatory feature known to modulate potato tuberization via crosstalk with photoperiod signaling. Yeast two-hybrid experiment verified that StAP2-13 and StAP2-18 physically interact with the key tuberization regulatory factor StCDF1. Furthermore, yeast one-hybrid experiment and dual-luciferase reporter assays confirmed that StAP2-13 and StAP2-18 directly bind to the promoter of the tuber-inducing gene StSP6A and activate its transcriptional activity. More importantly, transgenic overexpression of StAP2-13 or StAP2-18 markedly promoted tuber initiation in potato plants under long-day conditions, a non-favorable photoperiod for tuberization in most potato cultivars.ConclusionsThese results provide a comprehensive genome-wide characterization of the StAP2 gene family in potato, and identify StAP2-13 and StAP2-18 as promising candidate targets for genetic improvement of tuberization-related traits in potato, particularly for enhancing productivity under long-day growing environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12993-4.Keywords: Potato, AP2, Tuberization, Photoperiod, Overexpression, Long-day condition

Background

Potatoes (Solanum tuberosum L.), ranking as the third most important food crop globally after wheat and rice, develop starch- and energy-dense storage tubers from stolon [1, 2]; These tubers constitute a major dietary staple, sustaining approximately 1.3 billion people worldwide [3]. Given that the tuber is the primary harvested and edible organ that directly determines the final crop yield, deciphering the molecular genetic basis underlying tuberization constitutes a prerequisite for the targeted modulation of this critically developmental process in potato.

Tuber induction and formation are coordinately regulated by endogenous hormones and exogenous environmental cues. Among these factors, photoperiod represents a pivotal determinant of tuberization [4–6]. Photoperiod-mediated tuberization encompasses three sequential processes: signal perception in leaves, intracellular transduction, and long-distance signaling cascades that trigger tuber initiation. Upon sensing light signals through leaf photoreceptors, potato plants transduce these cues to the circadian clock and associated regulatory genes, which subsequently modulate expression of downstream tuberization factors to govern initiation [7–9]. Within this network, StCDF1 (CYCLING DOF FACTOR 1) functions as a central hub connecting the circadian clock component StGI (GIGANTEA), blue-light receptor StFKF1 (FLAVIN-BINDING KELCH REPEAT F-BOX PROTEIN 1), and tuberization initiator StSP6A (SELF-PRUNING 6 A). Under long-day conditions, the StGI-StFKF1 complex accumulates markedly and physically interacts with StCDF1, thereby triggering its ubiquitin-proteasome-mediated degradation [10–13]. The depletion of StCDF1 relieves the repression of StCO (CONSTANS), which subsequently activates StSP5G, an FT paralog that negatively regulates StSP6A, ultimately suppressing tuberization [14–16]. Conversely, under short-day conditions, StGI-StFKF1 complex accumulation is significantly reduced, enabling StCDF1 to maintain stable intracellular levels. Sustained StCDF1 abundance represses StCO activity, leading to decreased StSP5G expression and consequent de-repression of StSP6A. Subsequently, StSP6A protein translocates from leaves to underground stolons, where it promotes tuber formation [17–22]. Notably, direct binding of either StCDF1 or StCO to the StSP6A promoter has not been demonstrated. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that long-day photoperiods suppress tuberization, whereas short-day conditions promote this process.

APETALA2 (AP2) constitutes a core member of the AP2/Ethylene Response Factor (AP2/ERF) transcription factor superfamily, whose members regulate diverse biological processes in plants [23–25]. This superfamily comprises four subfamilies (AP2, ERF, RAV, and Soloist) based on AP2 domain sequence similarity [26, 27]. The AP2 subfamily, distinguished by two tandem AP2 domains, is further divided into euAP2, euANT, and basalANT lineages according to amino acid sequences and nuclear localization patterns [28, 29]. Initially identified in Arabidopsis [30], AP2 genes have been functionally characterized across multiple species. For instance, loss-of-function mutants of Arabidopsis ANT exhibit complete loss of floral organ identity [31]. In soybean, downregulation of AP2 family member GmNNC1 significantly increases grains per pod [32]. In rice, loss-of-function mutations in OsAP2 alter grain size, length, and yield traits [33]. Furthermore, AP2 genes participate in photoperiodic flowering regulation. In Arabidopsis, the AP2 family member TOE1 acts as a pivotal regulator of the photoperiod-mediated flowering pathway; functional studies have demonstrated that TOE1 directly binds to the promoter of the florigen-encoding gene FT, thereby modulating flowering [34, 35]. In maize, the AP2 family members IDS1 (indeterminate spikelet1) and SID1 (sister of indeterminate spikelet1) synergistically govern floral meristem initiation and specify meristem cell fate [36]. In tomato, modulation of SlAP2a expression impacts floral morphology, development, and fruit ripening [37]. Although AP2 family genes remain characterized in potato, evidence indicates that miR172, an established upstream repressor of AP2 genes, regulates tuberization, suggesting that potato AP2 genes potentially mediate tuber formation [38].

Here, we mined potato genomic resources to identify AP2 family members implicated in tuberization. Phylogenetic analysis, gene structure characterization, and expression profiling identified StAP2-13 and StAP2-18 as candidate regulators. We then aimed to investigate whether these AP2 candidates physically interact with StCDF1, the central tuberization regulator, and examine their potential role in modulating the expression of StSP6A. Furthermore, we sought to elucidate the functional significance of StAP2-13 and StAP2-18 in promoting tuberization under long-day conditions. Ultimately, this study intends to uncover the functional mechanisms by which StAP2s regulate potato tuberization and to identify promising genetic targets for improving potato adaptation to long-day environments.

Results

Identification of AP2 genes in potato

To identify AP2 family members in potato, Arabidopsis AP2 protein sequences were used as queries for BLAST searches against the potato protein database. We validated the BLAST results by verifying the presence of AP2 domains in each candidate protein via HMMER analysis. Following removal of redundant proteins lacking conserved characteristic amino acid residues, a total of 18 putative AP2 genes were identified. These genes were subsequently designated StAP2-1 to StAP2-18 according to their chromosomal positions in the potato reference genome. Detailed characteristics of the StAP2 genes are listed in Table 1, including gene loci, protein names, amino acid length, molecular weight (MW), and isoelectric point (pI). Remarkably, all StAP2 proteins harbored two AP2 domains, with amino acid lengths ranging from 232 to 487 residues. Furthermore, their molecular weights predicted using the ExPASy server ranged from 35.8 to 71.8 kDa, while their pI values varied from 5.14 to 9.31. Collectively, these variations in the structural properties of StAP2 proteins suggest their multiple functions in potato.

AP2 genes identified in potato

Phylogenetic analysis and multiple sequence alignments of the StAP2 gene family members

To further elucidate the evolutionary relationships and functional divergence of StAP2 family members, a rooted phylogenetic tree encompassing 18 StAP2 genes and other species was constructed using MEGA 6.0 software with the maximum likelihood (ML) algorithm. As shown in Fig. 1A, phylogenetic analysis revealed that StAP2 genes could be clustered into three distinct groups: euAP2, euANT, and basalANT. Notably, within the phylogenetic tree, the euANT group represented the largest clade (containing the highest number of genes), while basalANT group was the smallest. More importantly, StAP2-13, StAP2-14 and StAP2-18 in euAP2 group were phylogenetically closely related to the Arabidopsis AP2 gene AtTOE1 (At2g28550.1), indicating that these three StAP2 proteins might share similar functions to AtTOE1 in mediating photoperiodic responses. Furthermore, multiple sequence alignments performed via the Clustal X program demonstrated that all StAP2 genes contained highly conserved AP2 domains (Fig. 1B). In addition, chromosome localization analysis showed that the 18 StAP2s were localized to 11 potato chromosomes, exhibiting an uneven distribution: each chromosome harbored one to four genes. Chromosome 11 contained the maximum number of StAP2 members (four), followed by three genes on chromosomes 2 and 3, while the remaining eight chromosomes each carried one StAP2 gene (Figure S1).

Gene structure, conserved motif and cis-elements analysis of StAP2s

Subsequently, gene structure analysis of StAP2 family members was conducted with TBtools software, revealing that StAP2 genes within the same phylogenetic clade exhibited conserved exon-intron architectures-exemplified by StAP2-13 and StAP2-18 in group Ⅱ (FigureS2). To further analyze the conserved motifs in the amino acid sequences of StAP2s, online tool MEME was used. As shown in Fig. 2, eight conserved motifs are almost enriched in all StAP2s were identified.

Conserved motifs of StAP2 proteins. The 8 motifs are depicted using different color ranges

Genes with similar expression profiles tend to harbor shared regulatory cis-elements in their promoter regions. Accordingly, we performed cis-acting element enrichment analysis by querying the promoter sequences of StAP2 genes against the PlantCARE database (Fig. 3). A total of 12 putative cis-elements were identified in these promoter regions. Strikingly, the majority of StAP2 genes contained light-responsive elements, highlighting the critical role of photoperiod (day length) in regulating their transcriptional expression. Furthermore, the remaining enriched cis-elements included defense-related responsive elements, MYB binding sites, and other phytohormone-responsive elements (e.g., auxin, methyl jasmonate (MeJA), gibberellin (GA), and salicylic acid (SA). Collectively, the diverse distribution of cis-acting elements suggests that StAP2 family members may participate in multiple signaling pathways, including biotic stress responses, abiotic stress adaptation, and hormone-mediated regulatory networks.

Identification of StAP2 genes regulating photoperiod-mediated tuberization in potato

To further explore the potential functions of StAP2 genes in potato tuberization, we investigated the expression profiles of all StAP2 genes across different plant organs using RNA-seq data. As shown in Fig. 4A, StAP2 family members exhibited diverse expression patterns. Strikingly, StAP2-18 showed specifically high expression in tubers and stolons, suggesting its specific involvement in regulating tuber initiation and development. StAP2-13 was highly expressed in leaves, given that leaves serve as the primary photoperiod-perceiving organs, implying a potential association with photoperiodic regulation. Furthermore, StAP2-4 exhibited the highest expression level in flowers among all StAP2 genes, indicating its probable role in floral development. We further examined the circadian expression patterns of StAP2 genes under long-day (LD) conditions in potato leaves, which act as the photoperiodic receptor organ. As illustrated in Fig. 4B, most StAP2 genes lacked obvious circadian rhythms in their expression, with the exception of StAP2-13 and StAP2-18. Notably, StAP2-13 displayed higher expression during the daytime and a significant decrease at night. In contrast, StAP2-18 exhibited elevated expression at night and reduced levels during the day.

Overexpression of StAP2-13 and StAP2-18 promotes tuberization initiation under LD conditions

Given that StCDF1 is a central regulator of photoperiod-mediated potato tuberization, we sought to verify whether StAP2-13 and StAP2-18 are involved in the StCDF1-mediated regulatory pathway. To this end, a yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) assay was performed to investigate the protein-protein interaction between StAP2-13/StAP2-18 and StCDF1. The full-length cDNAs of StAP2-13/StAP2-18 and StCDF1 were fused to the pGADT7 and pGBKT7 vectors, respectively. The resultant pGAD-StAP2-13/18 and pGBK-StCDF1 plasmids were co-transformed into yeast cells. Our results demonstrated a direct interaction between StAP2-13/StAP2-18 and StCDF1 proteins (Fig. 6A). To rule out experimental errors arising from self-activation of yeast fusion proteins, we additionally constructed pGAD-StCDF1 and pGBK-StAP2-13/18 plasmids; these reciprocal Y2H assays further validated the interaction (Fig. 6B).

Subsequently, we performed yeast one-hybrid (Y1H) assays to verify whether StAP2-13 and StAP2-18 could directly regulate the tuberization-related gene StSP6A. A total of seven AP2-binding sites (TTTGTT) were found in the promoter of StSP6A (Figure S3). As illustrated in Fig. 7, yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) co-transformants carrying the prey constructs pGADT7-StAP2-13/StAP2-18 and bait constructs containing the promoter fragments of StSP6A exhibited robust growth on medium supplemented with 300 ng·mL⁻¹ aureobasidin A (AbA). In contrast, the negative controls showed no growth under the same conditions, indicating that StAP2-13 and StAP2-18 directly bind to the promoter region of StSP6A in yeast.

Ectopic overexpression of StAP2-13/StAP2-18 markedly enhanced the luciferase activity driven by the StSP6A promoter (Fig. 8). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that StAP2-13 and StAP2-18 are implicated in potato tuberization regulation by directly modulating the transcriptional activity of StSP6A.

To verify the biological functions of StAP2-13 and StAP2-18 in potato, we generated transgenic overexpression (OE) lines of these two genes in Solanum tuberosum L. cultivar ‘01–58’, which inherently displays extremely late tuberization initiation under long-day (LD) conditions. As shown in Fig. 9A, quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis confirmed that StAP2-13 expression in transgenic lines OE13-1 and OE13-2 was ~ 20-fold higher than that in the wild-type (WT) control, while StAP2-18 expression in OE18-1 and OE18-2 was elevated by ~ 6-fold relative to WT. Notably, overexpression of StAP2-13 and StAP2-18 significantly increased the transcriptional level of StSP6A in the transgenic lines, further validating the direct regulatory effect of StAP2-13/StAP2-18 on StSP6A. Consistently, we grew WT and transgenic plants in an artificial climate chamber under long-day (LD) conditions (16 h light/8 h dark). Phenotypic observation revealed that overexpression of StAP2-13 or StAP2-18 significantly promoted tuber initiation (Fig. 9B). The premature tuberization phenotype of the StAP2-overexpressing lines underscores the critical role of StAP2-13 and StAP2-18 in mediating potato tuber formation.

Discussion

In the present study, we identified 18 AP2 family genes in the potato genome. The number of AP2 family members varies across plant species: 14 in Arabidopsis thaliana (Arabidopsis) [39], 26 in Oryza sativa subsp. indica (indica rice) [40], and 62 in Triticum aestivum (wheat) [41]. These discrepancies likely reflect differences in genome size and complexity among species. Furthermore, all StAP2 genes were classified into three subfamilies: euAP2, euANT, and basalANT. This classification is consistent with previous findings in various plant species [39–42], indicating that the evolution and classification of AP2 genes are highly conserved across plant lineages. This study establishes a robust basis for subsequent functional dissection and characterization of the potato AP2 transcription factor gene family (Fig. 1A). Significantly, AtTOE1, a regulator of photoperiodic signaling that antagonizes CONSTANS (CO) and modulates flowering time [35], clustered with StAP2-13 and StAP2-18 (Fig. 1A), suggesting that these two genes may have the potential to mediate photoperiodic response. Multiple sequence alignment revealed that all StAP2 proteins contain two conserved AP2 domains (Fig. 1B), consistent with the structural features of AP2 subfamily proteins [25]. Potato tuberization is governed by multiple factors, among which photoperiod and phytohormones serve as critical external and internal cues, respectively [6]. Specifically, potato requires sufficient dark exposure to initiate tuberization [7]; gibberellin (GA) inhibits tuber formation, while abscisic acid (ABA) and auxin are considered tuberization promoters [43]. In this study, promoter analysis revealed the presence of light-responsive elements in all StAP2 genes, together with phytohormone-responsive cis-elements specific to ABA, auxin, and GA (Fig. 3), suggesting that StAP2 transcription factors may function as integrators of photoperiodic and hormonal signals during tuberization.

Accumulating evidence indicates that AP2 subfamily members are involved in photoperiod-mediated flowering. For example, overexpression of miR172 in tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) repressed SlAP2a expression, thereby altering floral morphology, development, and maturation [37]. In Arabidopsis thaliana, mutation of TOE1 (a euAP2 subfamily member) resulted in earlier flowering, and this phenotype was enhanced in the toe1toe2 double mutant, confirming that both TOE1 and TOE2 function as flowering repressors [44]. In Dendrobium officinale, DoAP2 genes exhibit diverse roles in floral development, with expression patterns analogous to those of NsAP2 in floral organs of water lily (Nymphaea sp. cv. ‘Yellow Prince’) [45]. Critically, altering miR172 expression in potato modulates the transcript level of its downstream AP2 family gene RAP1, which in turn regulates photoperiod-mediated tuberization [38]. These reports support the hypothesis that AP2 genes mediate photoperiod-regulated developmental processes such as tuberization, although direct functional evidence in potato has been lacking. Here, we analyzed tissue-specific and circadian expression patterns of StAP2 genes. StAP2-13 was predominantly expressed in leaves-the primary photoperiod-perceiving organs-whereas StAP2-18 showed peak expression in tubers and stolons (Fig. 4A). Importantly, both genes exhibited robust circadian rhythms (Fig. 4B), validated by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) (Fig. 5). It is noteworthy that the circadian expression data (Fig. 4B) were obtained from leaves, whereas StAP2-18 is predominantly expressed in stolons and tubers (Fig. 4A). Whether StAP2-18 exhibits similar circadian rhythms in its primary expression tissues remains to be determined. Furthermore, StAP2-4 exhibited constitutively elevated expression under long-day (LD) conditions (Fig. 4B). Nevertheless, considering its preferential expression in floral tissues (Fig. 4A) and phylogenetic distinctness from the TOE1-clade, we postulate that StAP2-4 likely serves a primary role in floral organogenesis instead of tuberization. Future investigations will be required to dissect its exact biological functions. Together with the phylogenetic analysis (Fig. 1A), these findings suggest that StAP2-13 and StAP2-18 may participate in the photoperiodic regulation of tuber formation. Subsequently, we demonstrated that StAP2-13 and StAP2-18 proteins interact with StCDF1, a central regulator of photoperiod-mediated potato tuberization, representing a previously unidentified interaction (Fig. 6). In Arabidopsis, TOE1 directly binds to the promoter of the florigen gene FT to regulate photoperiodic flowering, and this direct regulation of FT by TOE1 is modulated by the antagonistic interaction between CO and TOE1 [34]. Consistent with this regulatory paradigm, we validated via yeast one-hybrid (Y1H) and dual-luciferase reporter assays that StAP2-13 and StAP2-18 are capable of directly binding to the promoter of StSP6A, the potato FT homolog, and thereby activating its transcriptional activity (Figs. 7 and 8). These findings reveal a regulatory mechanism that is distinct from the canonical photoperiodic tuberization pathway. In the established model, StCDF1 indirectly regulates StSP6A through the StCO-StSP5G module: StCDF1 stabilizes under short days to repress StCO, thereby reducing StSP5G expression and relieving its suppression on StSP6A [17–22]. Our results indicate that StAP2-13/18 can bypass this indirect hierarchy by directly activating StSP6A transcription, providing a plausible explanation for how their overexpression promotes tuberization even under long-day conditions when the StCO-StSP5G pathway is active. The physical interaction between StAP2-13/18 and StCDF1 (Fig. 6) raises intriguing questions regarding potential crosstalk among these regulators. We speculate that this interaction may modulate the protein stability or transcriptional activity of StAP2-13/18, which acts in concert with the direct activation of StSP6A by StAP2s to promote tuberization. The precise functional relevance of this interaction remains to be elucidated. Moreover, whether StAP2 proteins antagonize StCO activity analogously to Arabidopsis TOE1, or whether they sequester StCDF1 to interfere with its downstream function, warrants further investigation.

To functionally validate the roles of StAP2-13 and StAP2-18 in photoperiodic tuberization, we generated overexpression (OE) lines. Wild-type (WT) and transgenic plants were grown under controlled long-day (LD) conditions (16 h light/8 h dark), an environment that inherently inhibits potato tuberization. Phenotypic analysis revealed that overexpression of StAP2-13 or StAP2-18 significantly promoted tuber initiation compared to WT (Fig. 9B). Concordantly, the transcriptional level of StSP6A characterized long-range “tuberigen” that directly governs potato tuberization was significantly upregulated in the OE lines (Fig. 9A). Interestingly, StAP2-13 and StAP2-18 exhibit anticorrelated circadian rhythms (Fig. 5B), yet both exert an identical regulatory function in promoting potato tuberization, implying potential differences in their respective molecular pathways mediating tuberization. Notably, OE-18 plants appeared to produce fewer above-ground leaves relative to wild-type (WT) and OE-13 lines (Fig. 9B). Given that StAP2-18 is predominantly expressed in stolons and tubers (Fig. 5A), this phenotype likely reflects enhanced source-sink partitioning toward early tuber development, rather than defective leaf growth. The possibility of pleiotropic effects on leaf development remains to be fully considered. Future systematic analyses of leaf number, photosynthetic efficiency, and final tuber yield are warranted to assess the agronomic impact of StAP2-13/18 overexpression.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study provides a comprehensive characterization of the AP2 family genes in potato. A total of 18 StAP2 genes were identified and classified into three subfamilies (euAP2, euANT, and basalANT). Significantly, we demonstrate for the first time that StAP2-13 and StAP2-18 directly bind to the promoter of StSP6A (the potato FT homolog) and activate its transcription, thereby promoting potato tuberization even under long-day (LD) conditions that inherently inhibit tuber formation. These results establish a genome-wide landscape of the potato StAP2 gene family and identify StAP2-13 and StAP2-18 as valuable candidate genes for the genetic improvement of tuberization-related traits in potato-especially for optimizing tuber yield in LD cultivation systems.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and treatments

The potato variety ‘01–58’, which tuberizes extremely late under long-day (LD) conditions, was used for tissue-specific expression analysis, circadian expression profiling of the StAP2 family, and functional characterization of StAP2-13/18.

For circadian expression profiling, plants were grown under long-day (LD) conditions (16 h light/8 h dark, 25 °C, 60% relative humidity, light intensity 200 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹) in artificial climate chambers for 3 weeks after tissue-cultured seedlings were transplanted into soil. The third fully expanded leaves from the top of the plant were harvested at Zeitgeber time (ZT) 0, 8, 16, and 20 h for RNA extraction. Three biological replicates were performed at each time point, with three seedlings per replicate.

For tissue-specific expression analysis, plants were grown under LD for 4 weeks, then shifted to short-day (SD) conditions (8 h light/16 h dark, 25 °C, 60% relative humidity, light intensity 200 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹) in artificial climate chambers for an additional 4 weeks to induce tuberization. Total RNA was extracted from roots, stems, leaves, flowers, tubers, and stolons. Three biological replicates were performed for each tissue, with three seedlings per replicate.

For phenotypic and gene expression analyses of transgenic lines, tissue-cultured wild-type and transgenic plantlets were transplanted into soil and grown under LD conditions for 3 weeks in artificial climate chambers. Tuberization initiation was defined as the onset of stolon tip swelling. Total RNA was extracted from leaves for qRT-PCR analysis.

All samples used for RNA extraction were collected and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C.

Mining of the AP2 gene family in potato genome

The deduced amino acid sequences of 14 Arabidopsis AP2 protein sequences [46] were used as query sequences to perform BLASTP searches (E-value ≤ 1e-5, identity ≥ 50%, query coverage ≥ 60%) against the potato (Solanum tuberosum L.) genome assembly DMv6.1 (accession number: PRJNA636376) obtained from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) [47]. The candidate genes were screened and identified via HMMER analysis (E-value ≤ 1e-10) against the Pfam database (http://pfam.xfam.org/search) to confirm the presence of the AP2 domain (PF00847). The candidates of StAP2 genes that contained the two conserved AP2 domains were regarded to be StAP2 genes. The molecular weights, and isoelectric points (PI) were calculated using the ExPASy (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/).

Phylogenetic analysis, multiple sequence alignment, genomic structure, chromosomal location, and conserved motif analysis

The phylogenetic tree was constructed using the neighbor-joining (NJ) method with 1,000 bootstrap replicates with MEGA 6.0 software [48]. Multiple sequence alignments were conducted with Clustal X software and the online ESPript program (http://espript.ibcp.fr/). Gene structures were visualized via TBtools software based on the GFF3 file of the potato (Solanum tuberosum L.) genome. Chromosomal locations of the genes were retrieved from the SpudDB potato genome database (https://spuddb.uga.edu/), which were subsequently mapped to chromosomes using TBtools software [49]. Conserved motifs were predicted using the local version of MEME Suite (v5.0.5).

Cis-element prediction in the StAP2 gene promoters

The promoter sequences (2 kb upstream of the ATG start codon) of StAP2 genes were extracted from the potato (Solanum tuberosum L.) genome assembly DMv6.1 (accession number: PRJNA636376) obtained from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) [47]. Cis-acting elements within these promoters were predicted using the online PlantCARE program (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html), and their distribution was visualized with TBtools [49].

RNA-seq analysis

Twelve cDNA libraries were constructed from leaf samples of potato variety 01–58 harvested at ZT 0, 8, 16, and 20 h under LD conditions, with three biological replicates per time point. Total RNA from each sample was isolated using the Plant Total RNA Isolation Kit Plus (Foregene Biotech, Chengdu, China).

Library construction was carried out with the TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina) in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, mRNA was enriched via oligo(dT) magnetic beads, fragmented, and reverse-transcribed into first-strand complementary DNA (cDNA) using random hexamers. Subsequently, second-strand cDNA synthesis, end repair, A-tailing, adapter ligation, and PCR amplification were performed. The constructed libraries were quantified using a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer and quality-validated by an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer.

Paired-end sequencing (2 × 150 bp) was conducted on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw sequencing reads were processed with Trimmomatic v0.39 [50] to filter out adapter sequences and low-quality reads, with the parameters set as follows: LEADING:3, TRAILING:3, SLIDINGWINDOW:4:15, MINLEN:36. Clean reads were aligned to the potato reference genome DMv6.1 using HISAT2 v2.2.1 [51] under default parameters. Gene-level read counts were generated using FeatureCounts v2.0.1 [52] according to the corresponding gene annotation file (GFF3 format). Fragments per kilobase of exon model per million mapped fragments (FPKM) values were computed with the following formula: FPKM = (total exon fragments/mapped reads (millions))/exon length (kb). These FPKM values were then used to quantify gene transcriptional abundance. Heatmap visualization was implemented using TBtools software [49].

For tissue-specific expression analysis of the StAP2 family, FPKM values were retrieved from previous studies [53] (NCBI BioProject: PRJNA641265) and similarly visualized as heatmaps with TBtools [49].

RT-qPCR

For quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis, total RNA was isolated from various tissues and organs of potato using the Plant Total RNA Extraction Kit (Huayueyang Biotechnology, Beijing, China). First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of DNase I-treated total RNA using the PrimeScript™ Reverse Transcription Kit (TaKaRa Biotechnology, Dalian, China). All gene-specific primers used for qRT-PCR were listed in Table S1. qRT-PCR was performed using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (Tli RNaseH Plus) (Cat. No. RR820A/B; TaKaRa Biotechnology, Dalian, China) on a StepOnePlus™ Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA). The StElF3e gene was used as the internal reference (housekeeping gene) for sample normalization. All experiments included three biological replicates and three technical replicates per biological replicate. The relative expression levels of each gene were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Y2H

Y2H assays were conducted strictly in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions (Clontech). The full-length coding sequences (CDSs) of StAP2-13/18 and StCDF1 were amplified from potato variety 01–58 and cloned into the bait vector pGBKT7 (BD) and the prey vector pGADT7 (AD) respectively, resulting in two sets of recombinant plasmids: (1) pGADT7-StAP2-13/18 (prey) and pGBKT7-StCDF1 (bait); (2) the reciprocal combinations pGADT7-StCDF1 (prey) and pGBKT7-StAP2-13/18 (bait). The recombinant plasmids were constructed for Y2H interaction analysis. Appropriate combinations of prey and bait vectors were co-transformed into competent cells of the yeast strain AH109, which were then incubated at 28 °C on synthetic defined (SD) medium lacking leucine and tryptophan (SD/-Leu/-Trp). Positive transformants were assayed for growth on SD medium lacking leucine, tryptophan, histidine, and adenine (SD/-Leu/-Trp/-His/-Ade) supplemented with 40 mg/L X-α-gal to detect protein–protein interactions. Transformants harboring empty BD or AD vectors were included as negative controls. The primers used for plasmid construction were listed in Table S1.

Y1H

The full-length CDSs of StAP2-13 and StAP2-18, with EcoRI/BamHI restriction sites incorporated at their termini, were amplified from potato variety 01–58 and inserted into the linearized pGADT7 vector (digested with the corresponding restriction enzymes). The promoter region of StSP6A, which harbors the AP2-binding motif (TTTGTT), was amplified from genomic DNA of potato variety 01–58 and cloned into the KpnI/SalI restriction sites of the pAbAi vector. The yeast one-hybrid (Y1H) assay was conducted using the Matchmaker Gold Yeast One-Hybrid Library Screening System (Clontech Laboratories, Inc., Mountain View, CA, USA), strictly following the manufacturer’s protocol. The primers used for plasmid construction were listed in Table S1.

Dual-luciferase reporter assays

The promoter region of StSP6A was amplified from genomic DNA of potato variety 01–58 and cloned into the BamHI/NcoI restriction sites of the reporter vector pGreen II 0800-LUC. The full-length CDSs of StAP2-13/18, with BamHI/KpnI restriction sites incorporated at their termini, were inserted into the effector vector pGreen II 0029 62-SK. Agrobacterium-mediated infiltration and dual-luciferase (LUC/REN) promoter activity assays were conducted as described previously [54]. The primers used for plasmid construction were listed in Table S1.

Overexpression of StAP2-13/18 in potato

The full-length CDSs of StAP2-13 and StAP2-18 were cloned from cDNA of potato variety 01–58 and inserted into the binary expression vector pCAMBIA2300, linearized with XbaI/BamHI restriction enzymes, under the control of the Cauliflower mosaic virus (CaMV) 35 S promoter. The recombinant plasmids were sequence-verified and then transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101. Agrobacterium-mediated transformation was performed on three-week-old plantlets of potato variety 01–58 following a previously described method [55]. The relative expression levels of StAP2-13 and StAP2-18 in the resulting transgenic overexpression (OE) lines were determined by RT-qPCR. The primers used for plasmid construction were listed in Table S1.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 20.0 (Windows; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Significant differences were determined by one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan’s multiple range test or Student’s t-test. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.
