Detecting relevant deviations from the white noise assumption for non-stationary time series
Patrick Bastian

Abstract
We consider the problem of detecting deviations from a white noise assumption in time series. Our approach differs from the numerous methods proposed for this purpose with respect to two aspects. First, we allow for non-stationary time series. Second, we address the problem that a white noise test, for example checking the residuals of a model fit, is usually not performed because one believes in this hypothesis, but thinks that the white noise hypothesis may be approximately true, because a postulated models describes the unknown relation well. This reflects a meanwhile classical paradigm of Box, (1976) that ”all models are wrong but some are useful”. We address this point of view by investigating if the maximum deviation of the local autocovariance functions from 0 exceeds a given threshold Δ \Delta that can either be specified by the user or chosen in a data dependent way. The formulation of the problem in this form raises several mathematical challenges, which do not appear when one is testing the classical white noise hypothesis. We use high dimensional Gaussian approximations for dependent data to furnish a bootstrap test, prove its validity and showcase its performance on both synthetic and real data, in particular we inspect log returns of stock prices and show that our approach reflects some observations of Fama, (1970) regarding the efficient market hypothesis.



Keywords: White Noise, Autocorrelation, Relevant Hypotheses, Bootstrap, Time Series

Introduction

In the last decades the analysis of time series has received an enormous amount of attention on account of its numerous applications including subjects such as economics, hydrology, climatology, engineering, genomics and linguistics to name but a few. One key characteristic of such data that plays both a huge role in theory as well as in applications is their temporal dependence structure, there are two main approaches to address these dynamics. One in the spectral domain, based on the spectral density and one in the time domain, based on the autocovariance function. Independently of the approach a key problem is the testing for a white noise assumption which is important when exploring the serial dependence structure of a time series and when diagnosing whether or not a time series model is a good fit for the data at hand. We refer the interested reader to
Li, (2003)
,
Francq and Zakoian, (2010)
and
Kim et al., (2023)
for surveys on this topic for univariate, vector valued and functional time series, respectively.

In this paper we are chiefly concerned with a time domain approach to univariate time series, although the results we present can be extended to multivariate or functional data in a fairly straightforward manner. Our central endeavor is to extract information about the autocovariance function
\gamma:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
from a given sample
X_{1},...,X_{n}
, in particular one is interested in ascertaining if the sequence
\{\gamma(h)\}_{h\in\mathbb{N}}
is zero, i.e. if the serial correlation structure is trivial. This is, for instance, related to the estimation of the order of linear processes (see
Xiao and Wu, (2014)
and the references therein) and to the goodness of fit of a time series model (see chapter 8 in
Box et al., (2015)
). There is a large amount of statistical procedures available for this testing problem, such as the Box Pierce portmanteau test (and modifications thereof, see
Box and Pierce, (1970)
and
Ljung and Box, (1978)
) which employs a sum of squares test statistic. Another approach consists of analysing the maximal deviation of the empirical autocovariance from its population version, i.e.

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\max_{1\leq h\leq d_{n}}|\hat{\gamma}(h)-\gamma(h)|

(1.1)

for some
d_{n}
that may tend to infinity. In the stationary case results in this direction can be found in
Hannan, (1974)
,
Jirak, (2011)
,
Xiao and Wu, (2014)
and
Braumann et al., (2021)
among others.

One common thread that unites all the hitherto cited references is that they assume stationarity of the time series, an assumption that is not always valid in practical applications but that significantly simplifies technical considerations. One way of extending the scope of autocovariance analysis that has gained a lot of traction in the literature is the notion of locally stationary time series. We refer the reader to
Zhou, (2010)
and
Dahlhaus, (2012)
and the references therein for additional literature. In this framework the autocovariance function
\gamma
depends on two parameters
h\in\mathbb{N}
and
t\in[0,1]
and is defined by

\displaystyle\gamma(h,t)=\lim_{k/n\rightarrow t}\mathbb{E}[X_{k+h}X_{k}]\quad h\in\mathbb{N},t\in[0,1]

(1.2)

where existence is ensured by the definition of locally stationary time series. As mentioned earlier the principal statistical problem we are concerned with in this paper are white noise tests, i.e. testing the hypotheses

\displaystyle H_{0}:\sup_{h\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|\gamma(h,t)|=0\quad\text{ vs }\quad H_{1}:\sup_{h\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|\gamma(h,t)|>0

(1.3)

Note that for locally stationary time series one may have, for all
h
,
\gamma(h)=0
for the classical autocovariance while
H_{0}
is violated. To the best of our knowledge research on the local autocovariance function and hypotheses of the form
1.3
are, compared to their stationary counterparts, still in their nascent stages. We refer the interested reader to the three works
Killick et al., (2020)
,
Cui et al., (2021)
and
Ding and Zhou, (2024)
. The first two works define estimators for
\gamma(h,t)
which are shown to be consistent under Gaussian and non Gaussian assumptions, respectively.
Ding and Zhou, (2024)
defines a Box-Pierce type statistic, derives its asymptotic (data dependent) distribution, defines a bootstrap procedure and establishes its validity.

We now come to the first key point of this paper, while the assumption of a white noise process is technically convenient it is rare to be satisfied exactly in applications. For instance, ”Since all models are wrong” (
Box, (1976)
), it seems overly optimistic to apply hypotheses of the form (
1.3
) to residuals when diagnosing goodness of fit of a time series model. In many cases it might be more reasonable to expect the autocovariances to be negligibly small but not zero and one might still be interested in working under a white noise assumption (or diagnosing the model fit to be acceptable) as long as the deviation from that assumption is not too large.

An important example occurs when considering the efficient market hypothesis which implies independence of log returns in certain scenarios,
Fama, (1970)
notes that ”Looking hard, though, one can probably find evidence of statistically ”significant” linear dependence in Table 1 […]. But with samples of the size underlying Table 1 […] statistically ”significant” deviations from zero covariance are not necessarily a basis for rejecting the efficient markets model” (see also
Campbell et al., (1997)
for a discussion about testing the efficient market hypothesis that comes to similar conclusions - ”Although many of the techniques covered in these pages are central to the market-efficiency debate […] we feel that they can be more profitably applied to measuring efficiency rather than to testing it.”).

In these cases a consistent test for (
1.3
) will, with sufficiently high sample size, detect arbitrary small deviations from the null - rejecting them even when the deviation might be negligible for the application at hand. Motivated by this we propose to instead consider relevant hypotheses, i.e. hypotheses of the form

\displaystyle H_{0}(\Delta):\max_{h\in\mathbb{N},t}|\gamma(h,t)|\leq\Delta\quad\text{ vs }\quad H_{1}(\Delta):\max_{h\in\mathbb{N},t}|\gamma(h,t)|>\Delta

(1.4)

where
\Delta>0
that signifies an acceptable amount of deviation from the white noise assumption that can be pre-specified by the user. Of course one may also choose
\Delta
in an automatic fashion by the sequential rejection principle (see remark
2.5
for a data adaptive procedure that chooses
\Delta
), suggesting the use of
\Delta
as a measure of evidence against a white noise assumption. Similar perspectives have been taken in
Bücher et al., (2023)
and
van Delft and Dette, (2024)
and we refer to the discussions therein for additional details. In section
3
we showcase the utility of this approach by inspecting the daily log returns of the S&P500, to be precise we show that
H_{0}(\Delta)
is rejected only for very small
\Delta
indicating that the efficient market hypothesis might be reasonable at least for this particular data set. This stands in contrast to the results of the usual methods like the Box-Pierce test which reject
H_{0}(0)
with very small p values.

Forgoing the hypotheses (
1.3
) in favor of testing (
1.4
) brings with it substantial technical complications. Deriving the asymptotic distribution of an appropriate test statistic under (
1.4
) is more complicated than under (
1.3
) as the data is neither stationary nor uncorrelated. In fact it is not clear if a limit distribution exists at all.

Our Contributions:

We extend the relevant hypothesis approach for white noise testing proposed in
Bücher et al., (2023)
and
van Delft and Dette, (2024)
to the supremum distance, i.e. we propose a supremum type statistic for hypotheses of the form (
1.4
). To be precise we establish a testing procedure that additionally allows for non-stationarity under the null and a diverging number of lags. To the best of our knowledge there are as of yet no results on supremum type statistics in the non-stationary setting and no results on supremum type statistics in a relevant hypothesis framework even for stationary data. This work therefore can be understood as complementary to
Ding and Zhou, (2024)
in two senses:

1.

We propose and analyse a supremum type statistic instead of a sum of squares type statistic for the autocovariance of locally stationary data.

2.

We investigate relevant instead of ”classical” hypotheses.

From a technical perspective we establish the validity of a block bootstrap procedure for high dimensional
\beta
-mixing time series that is similar to the one provided in
Chernozhukov et al., (2018)
but only requires the choice of one instead of two block size parameters. Additionally our conditions on the coordinate-wise variances of the data that is to be bootstrapped are substantially less strict.

(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}},(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

a_{n}\lesssim b_{n}

a_{n}\leq Cb_{n}

C

a_{n}\simeq b_{n}

\mathcal{A}

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A})=1-o(1)
.

2
Univariate Time Series

In this section we consider a real valued mean zero time series
X_{1},...,X_{n}
and want to test if is close to a white noise sequence, i.e we are interested in detecting relevant deviations of their local autocovariances from zero. To that end we assume that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{j+h}X_{j}]=\gamma(h,j/n)+O(d_{n}/n)

(2.1)

for some function
\gamma(h,\cdot)
and sequence
d_{n}\rightarrow\infty
. We define

\displaystyle d_{\infty}=\sup_{h\in\mathbb{N},t\in[0,1]}|\gamma(h,t)|

(2.2)

as a measure of deviation of our time series from a white noise process.
We thus consider the hypotheses

\displaystyle H_{0}(\Delta):d_{\infty}\leq\Delta\quad\text{ vs }\quad H_{1}(\Delta):d_{\infty}>\Delta

(2.3)

where
\Delta>0
is a given constant.

Remark 2.1
.

1)

The assumption that
X_{1},...,X_{n}
have mean zero is purely for convenience’s sake as including estimation of the means does not change the proofs beyond some cumbersome but straightforward calculations. One may use local (linear) regression to obtain an estimate for the possible time-varying mean function and the results of this paper will apply without changes.

2)

A large class of time series fulfilling these assumptions is given by piecewise locally stationary time series as given in
Zhou, (2013)
.

3)

One may instead of course consider the local autocorrelation function

\displaystyle\rho(h,t)=\gamma(h,t)/\gamma(0,t)

(2.4)

and the associated quantities analogous to (
2.2
) and (
2.3
). The methods presented in this paper can be extended to this setting by the obvious modifications of the test statistics and the bootstrap procedure we present.

To construct a test statistic we will require an estimator for
\gamma(h,t)
. For this purpose let
K
denote a kernel function and define
K_{h}(\cdot)=K(\cdot/h)
for
h>0
. For fixed
t\in[0,1]
and
h\in\mathbb{N}
the local linear estimator
\hat{\gamma}_{h}^{h_{n}}
of the function
\gamma_{h}
at the point
t
with positive bandwidth
h_{n}=o(1)
as
n\rightarrow\infty
is defined by the first coordinate of the
minimizer

\displaystyle(\tilde{\gamma}_{h}^{h_{n}}(t),(\tilde{\gamma}_{h}^{h_{n}})^{\prime}(t))=\underset{c_{0},c_{1}\in\mathbb{R}}{\arg\min}\sum_{j=1}^{n-h}(Y_{jh}-c_{0}-c_{1}(j/n-t))^{2}K_{h_{n}}(j/n-t).

(2.5)

In the following we will use a bias corrected version (see
Schucany and Sommers, (1977)
) of the local linear estimator given by

\displaystyle\hat{\gamma}_{h}^{h_{n}}=2\tilde{\gamma}_{h}^{h_{n}/\sqrt{2}}-\tilde{\gamma}_{h}^{h_{n}}~.

(2.6)

When the choice of
h_{n}
is clear from the context we will suppress the dependence of
\hat{\gamma}_{h}=\hat{\gamma}_{h}^{h_{n}}
on
h_{n}
. Theorem
4.1
in the appendix describes the theoretical properties of the resulting estimate.

With these estimators at our disposal we can now define, for some sequence
d_{n}\rightarrow\infty
the test statistic

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{n,\Delta}=\sqrt{nh_{n}}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq h\leq d_{n}\\
t\in I_{n,h}\end{subarray}}(|\hat{\gamma}(h,t)|-\Delta):=\sqrt{nh_{n}}(\hat{d}_{\infty,n}-\Delta)

(2.7)

where

\displaystyle I_{n,h}=[h_{n},1-h_{n}]\cap[0,1-h/n]~.

(2.8)

The restriction to the sets
I_{n,h}
serves two purposes - avoidance of boundary effects in local linear regression and ensuring well definedness of the random variables
Y_{j}(h)
.

Clearly we want to reject
H_{0}(\Delta)
for large values of
\hat{T}_{n,\Delta}
. Obtaining critical values for this statistic is not a trivial matter even in the stationary case. To wit neither the existence of a limit distribution is evident nor, in the event of its existence, is it clear what the appropriate scaling is. We also observe that the asymptotic distribution of statistics of the form (
4.66
) usually depends on certain extremal sets which are data dependent, further complicating the issue.

We will propose a solution to these issues by means of a bootstrap procedure which we define in section
2.2
and whose assumptions are stated in the next Section.

2.1
Assumptions

We now proceed with the necessary definitions and assumptions that facilitate the theoretical analysis that will follow.

For modeling the dependence structure of the time series
X_{1},...,X_{n}
we will use the concept of
\beta
-mixing as introduced in
Bradley, (2005)
. To be precise, assume that all random variables are defined on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})
, consider two sigma fields
\mathcal{F}
,
\mathcal{G}\subset\mathcal{A}
and define

\displaystyle\beta(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G})=\sup\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{I}\sum_{j=1}^{J}|\mathbb{P}(F_{i}\cap G_{j})-\mathbb{P}(F_{i})\mathbb{P}(G_{j})|

(2.9)

where the supremum is taken over all partitions
\{F_{1},...,F_{I}\}
and
\{G_{1},...,G_{J}\}
of
\Omega
such that
F_{i}\in\mathcal{F}
and
G_{i}\in\mathcal{G}
. Next we denote by
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{k^{\prime}}
the sigma field generated by the process
\{X_{j}\}_{k\leq j\leq k^{\prime}}
and define the
\beta
mixing coefficients of the sequence
\{X_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
by

\displaystyle\beta(k)=\sup_{l\in\mathbb{N}}\beta(\mathcal{F}_{1}^{l},\mathcal{F}_{l+k}^{\infty})

(2.10)

The sequence
\{X_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is called
\beta
-mixing if
\beta(k)\rightarrow 0
as
k\rightarrow\infty
.

(M)

The time series
\{X_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
has bounded moments of order
2J+\delta
for some
J\geq 8
and
\delta>0
, i.e.

\sup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}[X_{j}^{2J+\delta}]\leq K<\infty.

(D)

\{X_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is
\beta
-mixing, with mixing coefficients
\beta(j)
satisfying

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(j+1)^{J-1}\beta(j)^{\frac{\delta}{2J+\delta}}<\infty~,

for
\delta,J
from (A1).

(K1)

We have a kernel
K
that is a symmetric and twice differentiable function, supported on the interval
[-1,1]
, that satisfies
\int_{-1}^{1}K(x)dx=1
as well as
K(0)>0
. Further assume that the bandwidth
h_{n}
satisfies
h_{n}\sim n^{-\gamma_{1}}
where
1/7<\gamma_{1}\leq 1/5

(K2)

The partial derivatives
\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}\gamma_{h}(t)
of the functions
\gamma_{h}:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
exist on
[0,1]
and are uniformly bounded and Lipschitz continuous, i.e.

\displaystyle\Big|\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}\gamma_{h}(t)-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}\gamma_{h}(t^{\prime})\Big|

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}|t-t^{\prime}|~

(2.11)

\displaystyle|\gamma_{h}(t)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}

(2.12)

where the constants
C_{1},C_{2}
do not depend on
h
. In particular we have that the
\gamma_{h}
are Lipschitz with Lipschitz constant
H
that does not depend on
h
or
n
.

Assumptions
(M)
and
(D)
are standard moment and mixing assumptions,
J\geq 8
is mostly required to make statements easier to read, it can be relaxed at the cost constraints on other parameters which are difficult to state in an organized manner due to mutual influences on their constraints. Assumptions
(K1)
and
(K2)
are standard assumptions for local linear regression (compare to
Bücher et al., (2021)
for instance).

2.2
A bootstrap based on a stochastic expansion for
\hat{T}_{n,\Delta}

To obtain a bootstrap procedure that produces asymptotically valid quantiles for the statistic
\hat{T}_{n,\Delta}
defined in (
2.7
) we require a suitable stochastic expansion of
\hat{d}_{\infty,n}
. We will need a number of additional definitions, beginning with a population version of
\hat{d}_{\infty,n}
given by

\displaystyle d_{\infty,n}=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq h\leq d_{n}\\
t\in I_{n,h}\end{subarray}}|\gamma(h,t)|~,

(2.13)

as well as the (discretized) extremal sets associated to
d_{\infty,n}
and
\hat{d}_{\infty,n}
which are defined as

\begin{split}\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{\pm}_{\rho}&=\big\{(h,t)\big|\pm\hat{\gamma}(h,t)\pm\rho\geq\hat{d}_{\infty,n},1\leq h\leq d_{n},t\in\{1/n,...,1\}\cap I_{n,h}\big\}\\
\mathcal{E}^{\pm}_{\rho}&=\big\{(h,t)\big|\pm\gamma(h,t)\pm\rho\geq d_{\infty,n},1\leq h\leq d_{n},t\in\{1/n,...,1\}\cap I_{n,h}\big\}~,.\end{split}

(2.14)

Repeated use also makes the following abbreviations useful

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}:=\mathcal{E}^{+}_{0}\cup\mathcal{E}^{-}_{0}

(2.15)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{E}}:=\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{+}_{0}\cup\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{-}_{0}

(2.16)

\displaystyle s(h,t)=\text{sign}(\gamma(h,t))

(2.17)

\displaystyle\hat{s}_{n}(h,t)=\text{sign}(\hat{\gamma}(h,t))~.

(2.18)

The desired stochastic expansion of
\hat{T}_{n,\Delta}
is then given as follows.

Theorem 2.2
.

Assume that (M), (D), (K1), (K2) are satisfied. Denote by
\rho_{n}
any sequence such that
\rho_{n}^{-1}=o\big((nh_{n})^{1/2}d_{n}^{-1/J}h_{n}^{\frac{J+1}{J^{2}}}\big)
. Further assume that
d_{\infty}>0
and that
d_{n}\lesssim n^{-\gamma}\sqrt{nh_{n}^{-1}}
. We then have with probability converging to
1
that

\displaystyle\hat{d}_{\infty,n}-d_{\infty,n}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{(h,t)\in\mathcal{E}_{\rho_{n}}}\Big(\frac{s(h,t)}{nh_{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n-h}\epsilon_{jh}K^{*}_{h_{n}}(j/n-t)\Big)+o_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-\gamma}(nh_{n})^{-1/2})

(2.19)

\displaystyle\leq\hat{d}_{\infty,n}-d_{\infty,n}+O(\rho_{n})~,

where

\displaystyle\epsilon_{jh}=X_{j+h}X_{j}-\gamma_{h}(j/n)

(2.20)

We now define a block multiplier bootstrap; let
\nu_{j}
be a sequence of iid standard normal variables and define for some
l_{n}\rightarrow\infty
the multiplier blocks

\displaystyle Y_{jh}(t)=l_{n}^{-1/2}\nu_{j}\sum_{k=0}^{l_{n}-1}\hat{\epsilon}_{(j+k)h}K^{*}_{h_{n}}(j/n-t)~.

(2.21)

where
\hat{\epsilon}_{jh}=X_{j+h}X_{j}-\hat{\gamma}_{h}(j/n)

Now the bootstrap version of the partial sum (
2.19
) is given as

\displaystyle T_{n}^{*}:=\sup_{(h,t)\in\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\rho_{n}}}(nh_{n})^{-1}\hat{s}_{n}(h,t)\sum_{j=1}^{n-(l_{n}\lor h)+1}Y_{jh}(t)~.

(2.22)

for some sequence
\rho_{n}
that satisfies
\rho_{n}^{-1}=o\big((nh_{n})^{1/2}d_{n}^{-1/J}h_{n}^{\frac{J+1}{J^{2}}}\big)
.

We also require one more additional assumption for the bootstrap to work.

(V)

Let
q=q_{n}
be any sequence such that
q_{n}\rightarrow\infty,q=o(\sqrt{n})
. We assume that for all such sequences we have

\displaystyle\min_{I}\max_{(h,t)\in\mathcal{E}_{\rho_{n}}}\frac{1}{mh_{n}q}\sum_{J\in\{I_{1},...,I_{m}\}}\text{Var}\Big(\sum_{i\in J}X_{i}X_{i+h}K^{*}_{h_{n}}(i/n-t)\Big)\gtrsim 1

(2.23)

where
\max_{I}
is taken over all collections consisting of
m=\lfloor n/q\rfloor
disjoint subsets of
\{1,...,n\}
of the form
\{k+1,...,k+q\}
and
K^{*}(x)=2\sqrt{2}K(\sqrt{2}x)-K(x)
.

Remark 2.3
.

(1)

To gain some insight into the nature of this assumption let us assume that
K^{*}
is a constant function supported on
[-1,1]
. Then, letting

\displaystyle L=\big\{\lfloor nt_{n}\rfloor-\lfloor\tfrac{nh_{n}}{2}\rfloor+1,\lfloor nt_{n}\rfloor-\lfloor\tfrac{nh_{n}}{2}\rfloor+2,\ldots,\lfloor nt_{n}\rfloor+\lfloor\tfrac{nh_{n}}{2}\rfloor+1\big\}

(2.24)

denote the set of consecutive indices “centered at
nt_{n}
and of size
nh_{n}
” we then obtain for any
l\in\{1,...,m\}

\displaystyle\text{Var}\Big(\sum_{i\in I_{l}}X_{i}X_{i+h}K^{*}_{h_{n}}(i/n-t)\Big)

\displaystyle\simeq\text{Var}\Big(\sum_{i\in I_{l}\cap L}X_{i}X_{i+h}\Big)

(2.25)

For all but two indices
l\in\{1,...,m\}
we have that that
I_{l}\cap J
is either empty or equal to
J_{l}
. In particular we have that the number of such sets that is nonempty is proportional to
mh_{n}
. Consequently we obtain that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{mh_{n}q}\sum_{J\in\{I_{1},...,I_{m}\}}\text{Var}\Big(\sum_{i\in J}X_{i}X_{i+h}K^{*}_{h_{n}}(i/n-t)\Big)\simeq\frac{1}{mh_{n}}\sum_{l:I_{l}\cap J\neq\emptyset}\frac{1}{q}\text{Var}\Big(\sum_{i\in I_{l}\cap L}X_{i}X_{i+h}\Big)

(2.26)

In the case of a second order stationary sequence this reduces to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q}\text{Var}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{q}X_{i}X_{i+h}\Big)

(2.27)

which, in the case of strict stationarity, is asymptotically bounded from below whenever

\displaystyle\text{Var}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n-h}X_{i}X_{i+h}\Big)\rightarrow\infty

(2.28)

by Lemma 1 from
Bradley, (1997)
.

(2)

One can further weaken this assumption to only concern two particular sequences and two particular collections of subsets - for details regarding this see the statement of Theorem
5.3
(the two sequences are given by
q
and
r
therein, the associated collections of subsets are defined at the beginning of the associated Section).

In the following we denote by
\hat{q}^{*}_{1-\alpha}
the
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of the statistic (
2.22
). We again recall our test statistic

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{n,\Delta}=\sqrt{nh_{n}}(\hat{d}_{\infty,n}-\Delta),

(2.29)

and propose to reject the null hypothesis in (
2.3
) whenever

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{n,\Delta}\geq\hat{q}^{*}_{1-\alpha}~.

(2.30)

The main result of this section shows that this decision rule defines a consistent and asymptotic level
\alpha
test for the hypotheses in (
2.3
).

Theorem 2.4
.

Assume that (M), (D), (K1), (K2), (V) are satisfied. Denote by
\rho_{n}
any sequence such that
\rho_{n}^{-1}=o\big((nh_{n})^{1/2}d_{n}^{-1/J}h_{n}^{\frac{J+1}{J^{2}}}\big)
. Additionally, assume that there exists a constant
\delta>0
such that

\displaystyle n^{0.325}\lesssim l_{n}\lesssim n^{1/3}

(2.31)

\displaystyle d_{n}\lesssim n^{-\gamma}\sqrt{nh_{n}^{-1}}

(2.32)

Then the following statements are true.

(i)

Under
H_{0}(\Delta)
we have

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\hat{T}_{n,\Delta}>q^{*}_{1-\alpha})\leq\alpha~.

(2.33)

(ii)

Under
H_{1}(\Delta)
we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\hat{T}_{n,\Delta}>q_{1-\alpha}^{*})=1.

(2.34)

Remark 2.5
.

1)

An important question from a practical point of view is the choice of the threshold
\Delta>0
, which depends on the specific application under consideration and in some cases might be difficult to determine. In those cases one can also determine a threshold from the data which can serve as measure of evidence against a white noise assumption.

To be precise, note that the hypotheses
H_{0}(\Delta)
in (
2.3
) are nested, that the test statistic (
2.7
) is monotone in
\Delta
and that the quantile
\hat{q}^{*}_{1-\alpha}
does not depend on
\Delta
. Consequently, rejecting
H_{0}(\Delta)
for
\Delta=\Delta_{1}
also implies rejecting
H_{0}(\Delta)
for all
\Delta<\Delta_{1}
. The sequential rejection principle then yields that we may simultaneously test the hypotheses (
4.66

Consequently, one may postpone the selection of

2)

The restrictions on

3
Finite Sample Properties

In this section we will investigate the finite sample properties of the proposed procedure by means of a simulation study. We also illustrate the new methods by an application to a real data set.

3.1
Simulation study

For the simulation study we consider a stationary and a locally stationary AR(1) process. To be precise we will consider time series of the form

\displaystyle X_{i}=\phi(i/n)X_{i-1}+\epsilon_{j}\quad i=1,...,n

(3.1)

where
\{\epsilon_{j}\}
is an iid sequence of standard normal variables and
\phi:[0,1]\rightarrow[-1,1]
is a smooth function. For concreteness we consider the choices

\displaystyle\phi_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=0.2\sin(2\pi t)

(3.2)

\displaystyle\phi_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=0.2

(3.3)

for the locally stationary and stationary case, respectively.

Regarding the parameter choices (i.e.
l_{n},h_{n},\rho_{n}
) we proceed as follows:

(1)

We utilize the b.star function from the ”np” R package
Hayfield and Racine, (2008)
to choose the block length
l_{n}
for each lag and then average over all lags. This method employs the methods from
Andrew Patton and White, (2009)
to determine the optimal block length for each lag. Averaging isn’t strictly necessary but we do it to keep the method in line with the notation in the paper which uses only a single common block length for all lags.

(2)

We utilize the dpill method from the ”KernSmooth” R package separately for each lag and then average over all
d_{n}
lags. This method employs a plugin method to determine the optimal bandwidth for estimating each lag. Averaging isn’t strictly necessary but we do it to keep the method in line with the notation in the paper which uses only a single common bandwidth for all lags. We have also tried using cross validation which yielded similar results but was computationally more expensive.

(3)

We choose
\rho_{n}=0.1\hat{\sigma}\sqrt{\frac{\log(nh)}{nh}}

where

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}^{2}=\frac{1}{d_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{n}}\frac{1}{n-i}\sum_{j=1}^{n-i}(\hat{\gamma}(h,j/n)-\bar{\gamma}(h))^{2}

(3.4)

where
\bar{\gamma}(h)
is the mean of
\hat{\gamma}(h,j/n),j=1,...,n-h
. This follows similar choices as in
Dette et al., (2020)
and
Bücher et al., (2021)
but also incorporates an additional factor that accounts for data specific variations. One could refine this factor further, possibly adjusting the threshold
\rho_{n}
used in the estimation of the extremal sets to vary for different
(t,h)
, but this choice yielded reasonable results in simulations. We leave refinement in this direction as a future avenue of research.

We display the results of the simulations in Figure

below where we show the
empirical rejection probabilities of the test (
2.30

\alpha=0.1
. The vertical line corresponds to the true value
d_{\infty}
, which is

\displaystyle d_{\infty}=0.2

(3.5)

both in the stationary and the locally stationary case. We choose
n=600
. To obtain the empirical rejection rates we generated 1000 data sets each. We performed our test with
d_{n}=3,10,20
lags for each of the functions (

).

We observe that the rejection probabilities are decreasing with an increasing threshold

Real data example

We apply the proposed method to the daily closing prices of the S&P500 stock index which is available on Yahoo and can be accessed via the quantmod R package
Ryan and Ulrich, (2024)
. As is classical in financial data analysis we consider the log returns of the closing prices. We consider the data from 01.01.1980 to 31.12.1999 (i.e. n=5054) whose log returns and (classical) autocorrelation function are displayed in figure

Visual inspection immediately yields that the lag correlations are very small, nonetheless standard tests like the Box-Ljung test reject a white noise hypothesis with p values significantly smaller than 0.01 when considering lags up to size 100 and smaller than 0.05 for lags up to size 500. yields that

that ”statistically ”significant” deviations from zero covariance are not necessarily a basis for rejecting the efficient markets model.” Further we also provide a measure of efficiency (the size of

\displaystyle\bar{\sigma}(q):=\max_{1\leq j\leq p}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{I\in\{I_{1},...,I_{m}\}}\text{Var}\left(q^{-1/2}\sum_{i\in I}X_{ij}\right)

Theorem 5.1
.

Suppose that

\displaystyle\bar{\sigma}(q)

\displaystyle\gtrsim 1

For the remainder of this proof

denotes a generic positive constant that may change from line to line that only depends on

as well as

\displaystyle\Big|\mathbb{P}\Big(\max_{1\leq j\leq p}n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}B_{ij}\leq t\Big)-\mathbb{P}\Big(\max_{1\leq j\leq p}n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{B}_{ij}\leq t\Big)\Big|

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{1\leq j\leq p}\Big|n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{S}_{ij}\Big|\right]\lesssim\sqrt{r/q\bar{\sigma}(r)\log(p)}+n^{-1/2}rD_{n}\log(p)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\max_{1\leq j\leq p}n^{-1/2}\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{S}_{ij}\Big|>t\Big)

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\sqrt{r/q\bar{\sigma}(r)\log(p)}+n^{-1/2}rD_{n}\log(p)\right)/t

(4.66)

∎

Let

(4.67)
