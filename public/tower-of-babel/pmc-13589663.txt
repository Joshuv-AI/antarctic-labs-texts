Post‐ICU Psychotropic Medication Initiation in Nonagenarian Sepsis Survivors
Authors: Yuichiro Shimoyama, Noriko Kadono, Osamu Umegaki
Published: 2026

Introduction

The American Geriatrics Society (AGS) Beers Criteria strongly advise against prescribing benzodiazepines to older adults due to severe risks of delirium, falls, and cognitive impairment [1]. Consequently, intensive care unit (ICU) and primary care practices have widely adopted strict benzodiazepine avoidance strategies. However, the severe psychiatric sequelae of Post‐Intensive Care Syndrome (PICS) remain a significant clinical challenge [2]. We examined patterns of post‐ICU psychotropic medication initiation in patients aged ≥ 90 years following sepsis, in the context of contemporary geriatric prescribing recommendations.

Methods

Using the National Database of Japan (NDB), which captures > 98% of national health insurance claims [3], we conducted a retrospective cohort study. The Ethics Committee of Osaka Medical and Pharmaceutical University approved this study and waived the requirement for informed consent.

This study is a prespecified secondary analysis of a nationwide administrative cohort. We identified a cohort of previously independent and medication‐free nonagenarian (aged ≥ 90 years) sepsis survivors admitted to ICUs between October 2015 and March 2020. “Medication‐free” was defined as having no dispensed psychotropic medications in the 6 months prior to admission. To eliminate baseline confounding, we also excluded patients with a history of home medical care or rehabilitation during this period.

We evaluated the new initiation of psychotropic medications within 6 months post‐ICU. Central neuromodulators were specifically defined as gabapentinoids (e.g., pregabalin, mirogabalin). Analyses were descriptive and unadjusted. Administrative claims data preclude distinguishing between scheduled and pro‐re‐nata (PRN) use, assessing symptom severity, or identifying end‐of‐life contexts.

Results

Among 269 previously independent and medication‐free nonagenarian sepsis survivors (median age category, 90–94 years; sex distribution masked per privacy regulations), the incident prescribing rate for anxiolytics (benzodiazepines) was 0% (0/269). Among the 5.9% (16/269) of patients initiating new hypnotics, all received orexin receptor antagonists, with zero prescriptions for benzodiazepine receptor agonists (Z‐drugs).

Within 6 months post‐ICU, 21.9% (59/269) of patients were newly prescribed antipsychotics, and 21.9% (59/269) initiated antidepressants (including overlapping prescriptions). Additionally, 5.9% (16/269) received central neuromodulators (Figure 1).

Among the 83 patients (30.9%) initiating any new psychotropic medication, 41.0% (34/83) concurrently experienced a severe decline of physical independence, necessitating the new initiation of home medical care (a concurrent decline in physical and mental autonomy).

Discussion

Our findings describe post‐ICU psychotropic prescribing patterns in nonagenarian sepsis survivors. We observed a complete absence of incident benzodiazepine prescribing, alongside new initiations of antipsychotics, antidepressants, and central neuromodulators. These newly initiated medications carry their own warnings for mortality and falls in older adults [4].

This study is limited by the use of administrative claims data, which cannot determine the exact clinical indications or intent behind prescriptions. Furthermore, this observational design cannot establish a causal relationship between psychotropic medication initiation and loss of autonomy. A plausible explanation is that severe acute illness and its sequelae independently contributed to both the initiation of psychotropic medications—for a range of potential indications—and subsequent functional decline. Mitigating post‐ICU psychiatric and physical exhaustion requires addressing the root causes of iatrogenic sequelae during the acute phase and implementing robust, non‐pharmacological delirium management.
