Impact of Indoor Environmental Quality on Student Behavior: A Case Study Using AI-Powered Computer Vision
Authors: Alma Mena-Martinez, Danilo Valdes-Ramirez, Genaro Zavala, Jesús Alfonso Beltran-Sanchez, Dafne Jacques Pulgarin, Viktor Sobrino Caamal, Jose Ignacio Huertas Cardozo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe role of Indoor Environmental Quality (IEQ) factors in shaping student behavior and emotional states in the classroom, which have been observed as potentially diminishing performance, necessitates objective and continuous assessment to overcome the limitations of subjective methods. This study addressed this need by utilizing a case study approach. We deployed an AI-powered behavioral observation system to anonymously estimate aggregate student behavior metrics (Engagement, Attention, Interaction) in real-time, synchronized with data collected from a custom-built multi-sensor device monitoring IEQ factors, including temperature, humidity, equivalent carbon dioxide (eCO), total volatile organic compounds (TVOCs), air quality index (AQI), light variations, and oxygen volume (O). Comprehensive statistical and causality analyses included nonparametric correlations, Cross-Correlation Function (CCF) analyses to assess lagged effects, Time-Varying Granger Causality (TV-GC) tests, and categorical analysis with Chi-squared tests. The results revealed that thermal and humidity extremes correlate with increased behavioral volatility. Temperature is the most consistent predictor of student attention; Chi-squared and violin plot analyses demonstrated that attention levels are significantly higher at slightly lower temperatures, specifically below 30.9C, within the reported range [29.62 - 31.35C]. Besides, a significant association between humidity and attention was observed, although it was significant at the level rather than the more stringent threshold applied to core findings. Additionally, the study identified a critical TV-GC relationship between O volume and engagement, pinpointing specific causal bursts that global correlation measures failed to capture. Standard CCF analyses suggested that lower light levels may be associated with higher interaction levels; however, this pattern was not statistically significant after pre-whitening and bootstrapping the CCF, nor was it supported by the TV-GC analyses. These findings advocate for responsive, automated classroom systems that dynamically adjust IEQ parameters to synchronize with the temporal demands of the learning process.Keywords: Indoor Environment Quality, Learning Environment Engineering, Engineering for education, Classroom behavior analysis, Environmental Sensing Systems, Higher educationSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Indoor Environmental Quality (IEQ) factors have been widely studied in relation to student behavior and emotions, particularly in face-to-face lessons where multiple individuals share the same physical classroom space1–10. IEQ refers to a suite of environmental variables relevant to such settings, including indoor pollutants (biological, chemical, or particulate), thermal conditions (temperature and humidity), as well as noise, lighting, and odor1. These parameters can adversely affect student performance by inducing discomfort or distraction, or by altering thermal, visual, acoustic, or olfactory conditions, thereby diminishing achievement, well-being, and overall student engagement1,11–13.

Engagement is central to understanding students’ learning experiences in schools. Higher engagement correlates with improved learning outcomes and lower dropout rates14. Student engagement also serves as an important metric for evaluating the effectiveness of pedagogical practices15. Beyond academic metrics, it provides insight into developmental processes linked to classroom interactions and offers actionable feedback on students’ interactive behaviors14,15. This construct is multifaceted and is commonly divided into cognitive, emotional, and behavioral engagement16,17. Cognitive engagement pertains to students’ intellectual effort and includes both underlying psychological attributes (e.g., elaboration) and skills developed through academic work (e.g., problem-solving, critical thinking, and self-regulation)16,18. Emotional engagement captures students’ affective responses toward learning, encompassing positive emotions (enjoyment) and negative emotions (such as boredom or anxiety) triggered during classroom experiences14,16. Behavioral engagement focuses on observable conduct and includes dimensions such as attendance, attention, interaction, compliance, focus, persistence, hard work, and initiative in academic activities14,19.

Student engagement is typically measured through surveys completed by students or teachers, which capture valuable self-reported or observer-reported data16. However, limitations of self-assessment (e.g., students concealing their true feelings or experiences) raise concerns about the accuracy of these measures15. To address this issue, some research employs physiological or neurological measures, including electroencephalography (EEG) and electrocardiography (ECG), to better characterize students’ states20. Nevertheless, these methods can be uncomfortable due to the devices used. Standardized observations are another common approach, but are constrained by the observer’s attention and subjective judgment15. In recent years, computer vision approaches have emerged, enabling automated and scalable behavioral analysis in classroom settings, with significant potential for high-resolution, time-sensitive classroom observations19–22.

Numerous studies have explored the relationship between environmental variables and diverse student learning metrics2–10. Many investigations target higher education settings and utilize commonly recognized IEQ variables such as temperature, humidity, or illumination2,3,5,23–25. The standard approach relies on questionnaires and tests to assess student satisfaction, performance, cognitive effort, and learning outcomes2,4–7,25. Such reliance on subjective instruments introduces perceptual and reporting biases, limiting the objectivity of outcome measurement. Furthermore, very few studies have investigated oxygen volume as a relevant IEQ variable or systematically analyzed student interaction during live, face-to-face lessons.

In recent years, there has been a surge of interest in understanding the multifaceted nature of student engagement within classroom contexts. While research increasingly views engagement as encompassing attention, interaction, and emotional involvement, relatively few studies directly consider the influence of IEQ across these dimensions, despite its plausible role in shaping cognitive and behavioral outcomes.

A seminal contribution by Murakami et al.2 evaluated the impact of air quality and thermal environment on both objective (e.g., standardized quiz) and subjective (e.g., self-report) learning measures, monitoring variables such as CO, dust, temperature, humidity, air velocity, sound, illumination, ventilation rates, chemical pollutants, and fungal concentrations. Findings indicated a logarithmic association between ventilation rates and quiz performance, supporting the conclusion that improved classroom ventilation elevates student achievement. Building on this, Lee et al.3 examined air temperature, humidity, air speed, radiant temperature, CO, sound, and illumination in air-conditioned classrooms, reporting that suboptimal IEQ conditions were strongly and negatively associated with academic performance, chiefly due to discomfort and complaints about air quality.

Other works further examined specific IEQ aspects. For example, Mongkolsawat et al.4 analyzed the effects of CO, ventilation rates, and odor on cognition, mood, and perceived performance, observing that students reported lower perceived learning in less comfortable, fan-ventilated spaces. Jamaludin et al.5 applied a mixed-methods framework to study the effects of volatile organic compounds, particulates, lighting, and noise, highlighting a discrepancy between physical IEQ conditions, which often failed to meet standards, and students’ subjective reports, which suggested limited impairment.

Expanding this scope, Ramprasad and Subbaiyan6 established that IEQ significantly determines perceived classroom support for learning, with downstream effects on satisfaction and achievement. Kuhlenengel et al.26 utilized surveys and structural equation modeling to show that multiple IEQ features, including indoor air quality, thermal comfort, lighting, and acoustics, jointly predict test outcomes. Another study stressed the role of adequate ventilation and temperature management, finding substantial benefits for cognition when mechanical ventilation and CO control are implemented8.

Lighting and acoustics have also been focal areas. For instance, Aliakbari et al.7 found that poor humidity and inadequate window design correlated with health complaints, while Brink et al.27 validated systematic IEQ measurement frameworks for comfort and short-term academic success. Interestingly, Paschoalin et al.23 reported that performance remains resilient even as students notice changes in physical conditions; however, another study did detect declines in engagement, memory, and processing as IEQ deteriorates25. Recent literature continues to reveal complex linkages among thermal comfort, acoustics, lighting, and cognitive processes, particularly working memory and sustained attention9,10,24. Despite these advances, research gaps remain. Most prior investigations focus on cognitive metrics and self-reported satisfaction, with less emphasis on observable behaviors like interaction and real-time engagement cues. Temperature and CO arise as the most common IEQ variables in studies about the relationship between IEQ factors and student performance, satisfaction, and learning outcomes. However, few studies have considered the O concentration impact on these student behavioral variables, which may be a potential driver of fresh air exchange and improved student performance in face-to-face lessons28. Besides, many findings in previous work derive from cross-sectional and laboratory settings, challenging their generalizability to authentic classroom contexts. Previous work also considered long-term effects, measuring the results by the end of the lessons as learning outcomes, quizzes, questionnaires, or students surveys, omitting shorter-term relationships between IEQ and students manifestations of fatigue, aversion, sleepiness, or active participation, interactions, and relevant attention. Crucially, the field lacks comprehensive studies that integrate continuous, objective behavioral measures with real-time short-term IEQ monitoring, including fresh air indicators (such as O, TVOCs, eCO), thermal (such as temperature and humidity), and lighting conditions. This limits the ability to establish robust causal inferences. Addressing these limitations will be vital to forging strong, evidence-based recommendations linking IEQ and the behavioral facets of student performance.

Given these research gaps, our study addresses the following question: To what extent are IEQ variables, specifically, air temperature, relative humidity, carbon dioxide equivalent (eCO), total volatile organic compounds (TVOCs), oxygen volume (O), air quality index (AQI), and ambient light (Lux), correlated with students’ engagement, attention, and interaction during face-to-face lessons? Our main objective is to evaluate whether these IEQ factors shape behavioral engagement. Leveraging an AI-driven behavioral observation system19,21,22, the study systematically analyzes engagement, attention, and interaction using multi-sensor environmental monitoring for high-fidelity data capture.

To address the lack of studies conducted in real-life, non-laboratory settings, this research is designed as an exploratory in-situ case study. Our goal is to capture IEQ and student behavior variables as they occur during in-person lessons. We have deliberately avoided manipulating the teaching and learning environment or the teaching strategy. Instead, we have discreetly implemented our systems to sample all variables every five seconds. This short sampling interval was chosen based on two previous case studies where longer intervals (10 and 15 seconds) missed behavioral changes among students. For example, capturing observable cues of engagement, attention, and interaction, such as students raising their hands or talking to peers for less than 10 seconds, proved challenging with longer sampling periods. In contrast, the 5-second sampling frequency enables us to capture high-resolution temporal dynamics, providing more insight than the long-term seasonal trends typically studied.

The principal contributions of this research are: (I) a comprehensive statistical analysis of IEQ impact on students’ engagement, attention, and interaction in face-to-face lessons without altering indoor environmental conditions, (II) a collection of more than 1600 measurements of IEQ and estimated students’ behavior variables (engagement, attention, and interaction) measured simultaneously every five seconds during two lessons in a classroom setting of a higher education institution, (III) and actionable insights on optimizing environmental conditions to foster student achievement.

Section Results details our empirical findings on IEQ and student behavior during in-person lessons. Section Discussion explores interpretations, implications, and study limitations. Section Conclusion presents final remarks and implications for policy and practice.

Materials and methods

This study examines the relationship between IEQ factors, specifically air temperature, relative humidity, TVOCs and their equivalent derived eCO, O, AQI, ambient light, and their impact on student engagement, attention, and interaction in the classroom. The research is designed as an exploratory in-situ case study following the methodology proposed by Kin29. The case study consists of two sessions, aiming to capture both IEQ measurements and student behavior in real-time during in-person lessons. To achieve a comprehensive analysis, it is crucial to synchronize the collection of IEQ data and student behavioral indicators during live classroom activities. This requires the deployment of specialized hardware and software systems that can unobtrusively gather data while preserving the natural dynamics of teaching and learning. This section outlines the devices utilized, the measurement methods employed, the data collected, and the statistical analyses performed throughout the research.

Data Collection

An AI-powered system was deployed to estimate aggregate student engagement, attention, and interaction metrics19,21,22 (Patent application number 190196)30. The system implements a real-time, privacy-preserving edge computing pipeline using an AI-powered computer vision system. A wide-angle HD camera captures the students’ behavior, which is processed locally by an NVIDIA Jetson Xavier NX device working at a rate of 15 frames per second (FPS). To adhere to ethical requirements and data protection standards, the video stream is analyzed in real-time and immediately discarded; only anonymized, aggregate floating-point metrics are transmitted to the web server every five seconds. This architecture ensures that no individual identities or raw visual data are stored, providing a non-intrusive method for capturing high-frequency behavioral dynamics during live instruction21.

The system quantifies student dynamics through a suite of Deep Neural Networks (DNN) designed for group-level analysis. Interaction is measured using a fine-tuned DNN using the architecture You Only Look Once (YOLO) that identifies “interaction bounding boxes” for no-interaction, student-object, student-student, or student-professor clusters, while a pipelined YOLO model determines the number of individuals within these two last groups; interaction level is then computed in the range 0-100 as the sum of students interacted divided the number of students detected and penalized with the number of students not interacting22. Simultaneously, two other fine-tuned YOLO models detect faces and bodies to classify face orientations and body actions. The face orientation model is pipelined with an EfficientNet DNN for facial emotion recognition, computing a pleasant ratio as the number of students active and happy divided by the number of students detected in each frame19. The model for body actions identifies “positive actions” (such as raising a hand and taking notes) and “negative actions” (such as sleeping and averted gaze).

These raw detections are then fused into three primary indicators: an interaction level, representing the proportion of students in active exchange22; an attention level, computed as a weighted sum of facial orientation, emotional valence, and body actions19; and an engagement level, calculated as a composite score including interactions, body actions, attendance, emotions, and face orientations, but dynamically adjusting the weights of behavioral and emotional indicators based on the specific pedagogical strategy, such as traditional lecturing, collaborative teamwork, or individual work30. These three “real-time” indicators, computed for each frame, are aggregated every five seconds and sent as logs to a web server coupled with synchronized IEQ readings. As a result, we created the core dataset with 1,652 synchronized temporal measurements, which provides robust statistical power for time-series analysis31,32.

IEQ was monitored using a custom-built device incorporating low-power sensors. Such a device was built as described by Valdes-Ramirez et al.21. This device transmitted readings every five seconds over WiFi to the video analytics unit, ensuring precise temporal alignment between IEQ and behavioral metrics.

The ENS160 sensor uses advanced on-chip processing algorithms to derive IEQ indicators from measurements of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) and hydrogen. The sensor uses metal oxide technology with four independent sensing elements. Each of these elements has independent heater control, enabling the detection of various gases, such as VOCs, hydrogen, and nitrogen dioxide, with high selectivity. The calculation of eCO, expressed in ppm, is based on the proportional correlation between the presence of human bio-effluents (VOCs and reducing gases) and CO levels in confined spaces, providing a standardized estimate aligned with HVAC ventilation standards.

Likewise, the AQI is calculated internally from the TVOCs signal. TVOCs represent the combined presence of various reducing and oxidizing gases in the environment. To ensure the calculated index remains accurate, the algorithms perform automatic baseline management (to compensate for sensor drift and aging) and apply humidity and temperature compensation to correct for changes in the physical environment. This index follows the guidelines of the German Federal Environmental Agency (Umweltbundesamt-UBA)33, classifying air quality on a 1 (very good), 2 (good), 3 (moderate), 4 (poor), 5 (very poor) scale.

Following the manufacturer’s instructions, we placed the SEN0322 Oxygen sensor outdoors, where the oxygen concentration is a known constant of . Once the readings were stabilized for more than 10 minutes, we pressed and held the calibration button on the board for 2 seconds. Likewise, we calibrated the DFR0026 light sensor for classroom use after assembling, employing a light-tight dark box with a stabilized warm-white LED light source. Although the manufacturer of the light sensor reported inter-device variability of , it maintains high intra-device stability and a linear response to lighting changes after calibration. Since this study focuses on the temporal correlation and causal impact rather than absolute values, the sensor’s precision in capturing relative variations is sufficient for robust time-series analysis.

The ENS160 required an initial one-hour start-up period after its first-ever power-on and a one-minute warm-up before the lesson began for calibration. Conversely, the AHT21 sensor is calibrated and tested by the manufacturer; thus, we do not perform additional steps. During data preprocessing, we dropped the first 10 minutes of data because they were used for sensor warm-up and camera calibration before the lessons started.

Experiment Descriptions and Data Preprocessing

The case study was conducted in Classroom A7-334-A at Tecnológico de Monterrey (Nuevo León, Mexico) during two consecutive face-to-face sessions held from 16:02 to 18:46 in September 2024. The outdoor temperature was C, slightly higher than the mean maximum temperature for September between 2001 and 2023 (C); the humidity was , lower than the mean humidity , all these data according to the data of the National Commission for the Water (CONAGUA). The classroom, shown in Figure 1, encompasses an area of 94 m with a maximum capacity of 28 students. Key features included a Heating, Ventilation, and Air Conditioning (HVAC) system, seven desks, one computer, two projectors, three whiteboards, six glass windows distributed across the front and rear walls near the ceiling, and a glass entrance door. The centralized HVAC system was designed solely to control the temperature, which was set at C. The lighting system was not adjustable but operated using switches.

The participants included 22 undergraduate students, one instructor, and a member of the research team who remained outside the camera’s field of view. These 22 students represented nearly of the room’s total capacity, ensuring that the environmental and behavioral data reflect a classroom with high occupancy rather than an under-populated setting. In many exploratory case studies, the classroom group, rather than the individual student, serves as the primary unit of analysis. Research involving 20 to 30 students is standard for exploratory case studies or pilot studies, as it facilitates the observation of nearly all individuals in a naturalistic environment29,34. This approach prioritizes ecological validity over the larger sample sizes required for experimental research, which often demand highly controlled conditions and researcher intervention29. Furthermore, the classroom area provided approximately per person, which significantly exceeds the minimum requirements of many educational standards (typically per student)34. This density is ideal for research as it prevents overcrowding from becoming a confounding variable.

Two breaks ( minutes each) were scheduled to allow students to rest. Throughout the sessions, a few small windows near the ceiling at the back side of the classroom remained open, and the HVAC operated. This setup represents a hybrid ventilation scenario (mechanical HVAC combined with natural ventilation)8. In many educational settings, temporarily opening windows is a common practice used to ensure a high rate of fresh air exchange, especially in post-pandemic contexts7,8. In our case study, the professor and students decided to keep these few small windows at the rear of the classroom open, and the research team did not intervene in the classroom dynamics. Artificial lighting was switched on six minutes before the second session ended.

For student behavioral data collection, the computer vision system19,21,22 was deployed in the classroom, including a camera adjacent to the whiteboard (orange box in Figure 1), with the environmental sensor placed on a front desk near the students (blue box in Figure 1).

Rigorous data preparation was performed to ensure the quality of the analysis. Raw files containing student performance and IEQ measures were exported from the processing unit in JSON format to the WEB server, then parsed, merged, and stored into unified data logs indexed by timestamp. During pre-processing, missing values were systematically detected and quantified. Sensor readings from initialization and warm-up periods were excluded due to potential instability. For additional statistical analyses, environmental and student metrics were categorized to facilitate comparative analysis. Data Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using Python 3.8.10, the statsmodels 0.14.1 library31 with a significance threshold at , and the scipy library (version 1.13). Graphs were generated using the Python libraries matplotlib (version 3.10.8) and plotly (version 6.6.0) in Jupyter Notebook. An initial exploratory analysis characterized distributional properties, trends, and anomalies within the dataset35.

Distributional properties were further evaluated using skewness (acceptable range ) and kurtosis (ideal value 3)36,37. Levene’s test assessed homogeneity of variances38, and the Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) test evaluated the stationarity of the time series data32. Stationarity was confirmed if p-value.

To visualize behavioral distributions across environmental strata, we draw violin plots to map engagement, attention, and interaction levels using three-level categorizations for each variable. For all continuous IEQ factors, the low/mid/high categories were defined using the and percentile thresholds of each variable’s distribution.

The Chi-squared () test of independence was employed to identify non-linear dependencies and probabilistic associations between categorical states. Under this framework, a statistically significant deviation from the null hypothesis of independence indicates a meaningful association between specific IEQ levels and behavioral outcomes39. To mitigate the risk of Type I errors arising from the inherent temporal dependence and high autocorrelation of IEQ variables (as detailed in Table 4), we used a 5-minute subsampled dataset. This enabled us to approximate an independent dataset, thereby ensuring the validity of the distributional assumptions.

Relationship analysis used Spearman’s (nonparametric) and was corroborated with Kendall’s tests, which are suitable for monotonic, ordinal, or non-normally distributed variables40. Correlation coefficients ranging from +1 (perfect positive) to -1 (perfect negative), with 0 indicating no linear relationship, were interpreted in context. Additionally, Crammer’s V coefficients were employed to analyze the AQI variable, which had categorical values 1 and 2 in our dataset.

To assess the temporal relationships and potential lead-lag effects between IEQ factors and student behavioral indicators, we employed a two-stage CCF analysis. While a standard CCF evaluates the linear relationship between two time series by computing Spearman’s correlation coefficients () across varying temporal offsets41, this method is susceptible to spurious correlations when series exhibit high internal persistence or non-stationarity. To address these limitations and ensure statistical robustness, we implemented a Pre-whitened Bootstrapped CCF method. This refined approach involved three critical steps:

We fitted an AutoRegressive (AR) model to each IEQ variable with a maximum search limit of 1 lag, equivalent to 5 minutes in a 5-minute subsampled dataset, and accounting for the autocorrelation values in Table 4. The resulting AR coefficients were used to filter both the IEQ and student behavior series, effectively transforming them into residuals that represent sudden changes rather than slow-moving trends.

To determine statistical significance without assuming a specific data distribution, a Moving-Block Bootstrap was performed with 2,000 replicates (n_bootstrap = 2000). Using a block_length of 10 samples (50 minutes), we preserved local temporal dependencies while generating a null distribution. A confidence interval (CI) was then constructed using the and percentiles of the bootstrapped replicates. A relationship was considered significant only if the observed Spearman’s exceeded the boundaries of the bootstrap-derived confidence interval.

To capture the dynamic and potentially transient nature of the relationships between IEQ factors and student behaviors, we implemented a Time-Varying Granger Causality (TV-GC) analysis42. This approach extends standard Granger causality by utilizing a rolling-window framework, allowing for the detection of causal influences that may fluctuate or emerge only during specific periods of the monitored duration. We employed the grangercausalitytests function from the statsmodels Python library, which implements a Vector Autoregression (VAR) model. In this model, the dependent variable is regressed on its own lagged values and the lagged values of the independent variable. By including the history of the behavioral variable itself, the model explains away the portion of the current value attributable to its own autocorrelation43. Hence, the test only assigns a significant p-value if the IEQ variable provides predictive power above and beyond what the behavioral variable’s own history already explains. Therefore, we employed the first 5-second dataset with the following methodological steps:

We employed a rolling window of 360 samples (window_size = 360) with a step size of 1 (step = 1). Given our first 5-second interval, each window represented a 30-minute continuous observation.

For each window and every IEQ-behavior pair, bivariate Granger causality tests were performed in both directions (IEQ Behavior and Behavior IEQ). This was implemented using the Granger causality tests function from the statsmodels library, which evaluates whether the past values of a potential cause variable significantly improve the prediction of a result variable beyond what is provided by the result variable’s own history.

Results

This section presents the findings from a classroom-based case study analyzing the relationship between IEQ variables and student behavior during face-to-face lessons, without altering the IEQ conditions. It describes the statistical characterization of student engagement, attention, and interaction, alongside IEQ measures of temperature, humidity, eCO, TVOCs, AQI, O volume, and light. Through descriptive statistics, correlation, causality tests, and categorical association analyses, the section details how specific environmental factors relate to students’ behavioral responses during live instruction.

Statistical data acquisition

After the mandatory sensor warm-up period ( minutes), which introduced additional stabilization artifacts in temperature and humidity readings, the system yielded a raw dataset with 1,968 observations (rows) and 29 features (columns), including student engagement, attention, interaction, auxiliary group indicators used to compute these metrics, time-related variables, and IEQ variables. Raw data were preprocessed to retain classroom behavior variables (engagement, attention, and interaction) and IEQ variables (temperature, humidity, eCO, TVOCs, AQI, O, and light).

The sensor device missed 91 measurements during the sessions ( of the data). These observations were removed due to their scarcity and to avoid introducing spurious values. Three records corresponding to zero values of the students’ behavioral variables were also removed. Likewise, observations during the break periods (222/variable) were excluded because the sensor device produced IEQ measurements, but no teaching-learning activities were taking place. The final analytic dataset44 comprised 1,652 curated observations ( of the raw dataset).

Correlation between IEQ variables

The correlation analysis between IEQ variables, conducted using non-parametric Spearman’s and corroborated with Kendall’s coefficients (see Figure 5), served as a pre-screening step to detect multicollinearity and ensure the independence of the predictor variables used in the study. It utilized the 5-minute interval to mitigate the influence of high-frequency autocorrelation. The results reveal a near-perfect positive correlation between TVOCs and eCO. This dependency is a direct consequence of the sensor’s technical architecture, where eCO is not a direct measurement of carbon dioxide but an indirect estimate derived algorithmically from TVOCs. Consequently, to prevent multicollinearity and redundant analysis, eCO was excluded in favor of the primary measured variable, TVOCs. Furthermore, temperature and humidity exhibit a moderate negative correlation. This trend is consistent with established thermodynamic principles, as the relative humidity typically decreases as temperature rises51,52. Contrastingly, O and TVOCs showed a positive moderate correlation. Since some small windows were open, the classroom did not have a fully closed environment but rather a hybrid ventilation system. As a result, fluctuations in gas levels were partially influenced by external fresh air entering the room, rather than solely by internal metabolic processes. When gusts of fresh air flowed in through the open windows, they brought O at outdoor ambient levels ()28 and TVOCs used to calculate eCO at outdoor ambient levels ( ppm)53. This explains the moderate positive correlations observed between O and TVOCs, as well as between O and eCO.

Cross Correlation Function Analysis

To evaluate the dynamic relationship between IEQ and students’ behavioral responses, the standard CCF analysis was performed using Spearman’s rank correlation across temporal offsets ranging from -100 to +100 minutes. The CCF analysis suggests a temporal link between thermal conditions and student attention (see Figure 7 and Table 7). Temperature shows a peak positive correlation with attention at a +75-minute lag. This suggests a delayed physiological or cognitive response where temperature increases are followed by shifts in attention levels over an hour later. Similarly, humidity exhibits a strong negative peak with attention at a +80-minute lag. These findings expand upon the static results, where temperature and humidity were already identified as primary correlates of attention, by defining the specific 75- to 80-minute window of maximum impact. However, most p-values fall within the confidence intervals, indicating non-significant relationships.

The CCF results largely validate and extend the findings from the static Spearman and Kendall analyses. Hence, the subsequent comparison with the pre-whitened and bootstrapped CCF is critical to determine if these 60- to 85-minute leads represent true causal-like innovations or are artifacts of the slow-moving nature of both IEQ and behavioral trends.

Pre-whitened and Bootstrapped Cross Correlation Function Analysis

The application of pre-whitening and 2000-iteration bootstrapping provides a critical correction to the initial findings. By removing the internal memory of the time series, the previously observed correlations do not persist after pre-whitening and appear to be artifacts of shared temporal trends and high autocorrelation. Under the confidence interval, no IEQ–behavior pair exhibits a correlation between residuals that reaches statistical significance (see Figure 8). This indicates that sudden changes in the IEQ factors do not result in a statistically detectable immediate or delayed behavioral response. Discussion

Our findings contribute to the ongoing discussion regarding the relationship between key IEQ variables and students’ behavioral responses during in-person lessons2–10. This study provides high-frequency data collected during two face-to-face classes at a higher education institution, with 1,652 measurements taken every five seconds, bridging the gap of studies on short-term temporal dynamics between IEQ and behavioral variables during face-to-face lessons. Such a sampling period was sufficient to capture significant thermal and oxygen fluctuations necessary to test the statistical models, as previously presented in our results. Larger sampling periods (10 and 15 seconds) were previously tested in two case studies, noticing that the AI-based algorithms missed relevant students’ behaviors indicating engagement, interaction, or their absence.

The in-situ case study design of our research29 allowed us to capture the real dynamics during the two lessons. The hybrid ventilation selected by the professor and students likely helped maintain stable oxygen levels and resulted in a low pollutant load, with low levels of TVOCs and eCO recorded. This created a clean baseline for our behavioral analysis. Additionally, the lighting conditions managed by the professor and students did not appear to affect the students’ behavior, indicating no impact of lighting conditions.

Our approach integrates correlational, CCF and pre-whitened CCF, time-varying Granger causality, and categorical analyses to provide a comprehensive examination of the data while reducing the influence of artifacts arising from time-series autocorrelation. The Chi-squared analysis identifies temperature as the most robust predictor of attention (, ). Contribution analysis reveals that “Low temperature” predominantly corresponds to “High attention” (contribution = 6.01), consistent with the violin plots. This suggests that slightly lower temperatures -at high temperatures around 30C- generally facilitate focus, whereas thermal extremes increase behavioral volatility in engagement. The TV-GC test identified a brief causal relationship around 16:55 between temperature and attention (p-value) in of the windows. This finding indicates that thermal fluctuations had a localized impact on student attention and helps explain the non-significant p-values reported by the pre-whitened bootstrapped CCF test for this pair of variables. Together, the temperature results, the humidity–attention relationship, and the strong contribution of “Low humidity” to “Low attention” (3.72) support the need for simultaneous climate control to mitigate potential cognitive impairment.

The correlation analysis, complemented by CCF, revealed a strong positive delayed association between O volume and student behavior, peaking between 70 and 85 minutes. Crucially, the TV-GC test identified a specific causal effect from O volume to engagement occurring between 17:30 and 17:45. The time series in Figure 2 shows moderate peaks in O levels followed by corresponding peaks in engagement during this period. The discrepancy between the significant temporal causality indicated by the TV-GC test and the non-significant global Chi-squared result ( for engagement) suggests that oxygen volume has a transient causal influence rather than a constant effect. In addition, the non-significant p-values reported by the pre-whitened bootstrapped CCF test support the notion of a time-localized effect. Practically, these findings highlight the importance of ventilation as a strategy to support performance, particularly during periods of high cognitive demand, as also recommended by Chung and Lim28.

We built upon the work of Pradhan et al.10 to investigate how lighting relates to students’ behaviors. Our findings, illustrated through violin plots, indicate that lower illumination levels were associated with a more pronounced clustering around higher behavioral scores. This observation was also reflected in a strong negative correlation in the standard CCF. However, analyses using pre-whitened and bootstrapped CCF and TV-GC indicated that this relationship was not statistically significant and lacked evidence of a causal effect. Consequently, these results may reflect students’ adaptation to decreasing light levels, as shown in Figure 2, alongside changes in instructional activities. Therefore, we plan to further explore the impact of lighting in in-person higher education lessons where multiple digital screens are incorporated into teaching strategies.

To address the relationship between environmental stimuli and student response, it is essential to distinguish between safety thresholds, as defined by international standards11,45–49, and temporary performance/behavioral thresholds, which describe the subtle levels at which IEQ begins to influence cognitive processes. Our findings suggest that students act as biological sensors, exhibiting sensitivity to environmental micro-fluctuations even when conditions remain within clinically safe or stable ranges. For instance, despite a narrow thermal variance (C), the statistically significant association between temperature and attention () indicates that even small shifts can correlate with observable changes in student focus. Conversely, TVOCs and AQI did not yield significant correlations or causal impacts in our analyses, likely due to their concentrations remaining below the threshold of human perception or behavioral influence during the monitored sessions. This reinforces the necessity of “real-time” IEQ measurements in educational facilities.

Implications and limitations

The findings of this research offer a roadmap for shifting from static classroom management to a more dynamic, responsive educational environment. The identification of specific “causal bursts” via TV-GC analysis suggests that IEQ management should not be treated as a background constant. Instead, facility managers should prioritize high-sensitivity periods. For example, the causal link between O volume and engagement during peak lesson hours implies that automated ventilation should be synchronized with high-cognitive-demand phases of a lecture rather than operating on a fixed timer. This “precision ventilation” could bolster student engagement.

Our results demonstrate that students exhibit a degree of behavioral resilience to environmental stressors, such as high temperatures or lower lighting conditions, when teaching strategies are effective. However, the strong link between lower temperatures and closer attention, even with a few C difference around 30C, provides an actionable guideline for instructors: maintaining a lower thermal baseline (the lower end of the comfort range) is objectively superior for fostering sustained focus. Teachers can use these insights to adjust their lesson plans by scheduling complex, high-attention tasks during periods when the thermal and air conditions are at their peaks.

By showing that atmospheric components (like OVol) and thermal states (temperature/humidity) exert distinct temporal and categorical pressures on students, this work advocates for an integrated sensing policy. Educational policy could move toward certifying smart environments in higher education institutions that use real-time feedback loops. These systems would monitor both the environment and anonymized behavioral cues, allowing the building to “self-correct” (e.g., boosting oxygen or adjusting temperature) before student attention levels reach a critical decay point.

The findings and contributions in this work should be read considering the following limitations. The investigation was conducted during two continuous sessions in the same classroom and with the same group of students, limiting generalizability. Future studies will involve larger cohorts across multiple classrooms to further validate these findings across different demographic and pedagogical contexts. Moreover, student behavior variables were estimated using an AI-powered system, which is susceptible to errors, similar to human observations.

Another limitation of this study is the reliance on a single sensor, which likely restricts the spatial resolution of the IEQ data. Given the spatial variability that can occur in a classroom, we positioned the device near the student group (as indicated by the “blue box” in Figure 1). This placement ensures that the data reflects the immediate micro-environment experienced by the students. However, future research should consider using synchronized sensor networks to more accurately characterize environmental variability and its relationship to student behavior.

Conclusion

This case study analyzes the role of measurable and dynamic indoor environmental conditions in shaping student behavior in the classroom setting. By integrating AI-based computer vision with high-resolution environmental sensing, this work implemented a novel framework to capture both immediate and time-dependent interactions between Indoor Environmental Quality (IEQ) and the engagement, attention, and interaction of a group of students, yielding a high-resolution database with IEQ and students’ behavior measurements every five seconds. Among the evaluated IEQ variables, thermal conditions, particularly temperature, emerged as the most consistent factor associated with students’ attention. The findings suggest that maintaining stable and moderate thermal environments supports sustained attention, while deviations from such stable conditions may introduce variability in students’ attention.

This case study also reveals that the influence of IEQ is not always constant over time. Certain environmental factors, such as oxygen levels, appear to affect students’ engagement during specific temporal windows rather than through continuous effects. This highlights the need to move beyond static interpretations of indoor environments for teaching and learning and consider their dynamic nature. In contrast, some commonly emphasized factors, including lighting and TVOCs, showed limited or context-dependent effects in this case study. These results suggest that environmental interventions should be carefully tailored to specific classroom conditions rather than based on generalized assumptions.

Overall, this research contributes to the growing body of evidence supporting the role of IEQ in educational outcomes and introduces a data-driven approach for its assessment. This research advocates for a shift toward “precision environmental management” in educational facilities. By leveraging real-time sensing and AI, classrooms can be transformed into responsive ecosystems that dynamically adjust IEQ parameters to nurture student well-being and maximize academic performance.

Future research should validate these findings across diverse educational contexts, incorporate direct measures of academic performance, and explore controlled interventions to better establish causal relationships. Also, we will explore the impact of lighting in in-person higher education lessons, considering the more frequent use of digital screens.
