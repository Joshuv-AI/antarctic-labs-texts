Mapping prefrontal afferents along development
Authors: Anne Günther, Malte Stelzer, Andreas Franzelin, Annette Marquardt, Thomas G. Oertner, Ileana L. Hanganu-Opatz, Jastyn A. Pöpplau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) serves as a central hub for cognitive, emotional, and social processes by integrating inputs from numerous brain regions. Understanding the mechanisms underlying the formation and myelination of prefrontal afferents is essential for elucidating the emergence, maturation, and refinement of mPFC-dependent behaviors. However, despite extensive characterization of long-range projections to the mPFC in the adult brain, their developmental trajectories remain poorly understood.MethodsIn this study, we combine retrograde labeling, light-sheet imaging, and automated cell segmentation to quantitatively map afferent projections to the mPFC from neonatal stages to adulthood.ResultsWe show that ipsi- and contralateral afferent densities undergo marked, region-specific developmental changes. Quantitative laterality analysis revealed that many afferent pathways develop an increasing ipsilateral bias after neonatal age, arising through distinct combinations of ipsilateral and contralateral changes rather than a uniform loss of contralateral inputs. In addition, we identify region-specific developmental patterns, including increased projection densities from limbic regions during early adolescence and a progressive decline in inputs from the mediodorsal thalamus toward adulthood. Notably, myelination of interhemispheric prefrontal afferents begins only after the reduction of neonatal contralateral projections.ConclusionTogether, these findings reveal distinct, region-specific developmental dynamics of prefrontal afferents, with important implications for the maturation of mPFC-dependent functional and behavioral outputs.

Introduction

The mPFC, a central hub of integrative processing in the brain, is involved in a wide range of cognitive, emotional, and social functions. It integrates direct axonal inputs from numerous cortical and subcortical regions through local and long-range circuits to guide behavior (Hoover and Vertes, 2007; Ährlund-Richter et al., 2019; Hanganu-Opatz et al., 2023). Within the PFC, functions are anatomically and physiologically compartmentalized, with medial subdivisions [i.e., anterior cingulate (ACA), prelimbic (PL), and infralimbic (ILA) areas] primarily contributing to sensorimotor functions, action-planning, and decision making, while ventral regions [i.e., orbitofrontal cortex (ORB)] as well as adjacent cortices [i.e., secondary motor cortices (MOs)] control emotional and motivational behaviors, respectively (Heidbreder and Groenewegen, 2003; Laubach et al., 2018; Anastasiades and Carter, 2021). In contrast, long-range inputs to the mPFC provide essential information on environmental context and internal state from other regions (Spellman et al., 2015; Burgos-Robles et al., 2017). For instance, inputs from the ventromedial thalamus (VM) to the mPFC regulate arousal states (Honjoh et al., 2018), while hippocampal projections contribute to working, recognition, and episodic memory (MacDonald et al., 2011; Takehara-Nishiuchi, 2014; Chao et al., 2017, 2020).

However, not only ipsilateral projections shape prefrontal circuits. Interhemispheric connectivity has been shown to increase the complexity of prefrontal networks (Floresco and Ghods-Sharifi, 2007; Chao et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2023). Numerous studies have investigated projections crossing the corpus callosum (CC) in rodents and humans (Yorke and Caviness, 1975; van der Knaap and van der Ham, 2011; Innocenti et al., 2022). While callosal connectivity has traditionally been assumed to be predominantly homotopic (i.e., mPFC to mPFC) (Yorke and Caviness, 1975; De Benedictis et al., 2016), more recent studies have revealed substantial heterotopy of these projections (Szczupak et al., 2023). Notably, specific functions of homotopic and heterotopic callosal connections have been described in rodents. At the homotopic level, sensory processing has been shown to be influenced by contra- and ipsilateral ACA projections (Wang et al., 2023), whereas disruptions of heterotopic pathways between the basolateral amygdala (BLA) and the ACA led to impaired decision making (Floresco and Ghods-Sharifi, 2007).

In contrast to this detailed dissection of adult prefrontal afferents, little is known about their development. Projections from other brain regions reach the mPFC during development and are thought to undergo dynamic reorganization during early life to promote maturation of higher-order cognitive (e.g., working memory) and emotional functions (e.g., fear learning) (Chini and Hanganu-Opatz, 2021). For example, projections from the mediodorsal thalamus (MD) and from the BLA progressively increase in density during development and modulate the maturation of local prefrontal circuits (Cunningham et al., 2002; Maren and Quirk, 2004; Rios and Villalobos, 2004; Parnaudeau et al., 2018; Klune et al., 2021). However, prior studies along development have focused on isolated pathways and time points, precluding a large-scale age-dependent mapping of afferents.

In this study, we addressed this knowledge gap by systematically characterizing the development of axonal projections to the mPFC from birth to adulthood. First, we combined retrograde viral tracing with light-sheet microscopy to generate a comprehensive atlas of regional afferent projection patterns to the developing mPFC. Based on these results, we used the same viral tracing strategy, combined with confocal microscopy and automated cell segmentation, to quantify projecting neurons across the brain throughout development. Additionally, we assessed the degree of myelination of retrogradely labeled inputs using immunohistochemistry. Our data provide a comprehensive developmental overview of prefrontal afferents across a broad range of brain regions, including the agranular insular cortex (AI), piriform cortex (PIRI), basolateral amygdala (BLA), basomedial amygdala (BMA), retrosplenial cortex (RSP), entorhinal cortex (ENT), ventral and intermediate CA1 (CA1v, CA1i), ventral and dorsal subiculum (SUBv, SUBd), claustrum (CLA), anterior thalamic nuclei (ATN), ventromedial thalamus (VM), mediodorsal thalamus (MD), supramammillary nucleus (SUM), periaqueductal gray (PAG), and ventral tegmental area (VTA), together with local prefrontal subdivisions. This brain-wide analysis reveals distinct region- and age-specific trajectories of mPFC afferents and provides insight into the evolving architecture of prefrontal circuits.

Materials and methods

Animals

All experiments were performed in compliance with German law and guidelines of the European Union for the use of animals in research (European Union Directive 2010/63/EU) and were approved by the local ethical committee (Behörde für Gesundheit und Verbraucherschutz Hamburg). Experiments were carried out in C57BL/6J mice of both sexes. Animals were housed in a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle and were given access to water and food ad libitum. The day of birth was considered postnatal day (P) 0.

Virus injections

Viral vectors were injected unilaterally into the PFC of the right hemisphere of C57BL/6J mice. Two viral vectors were applied simultaneously: rAAV9rg_mCaMKII-mCherry (#114469-AAVrg, Addgene, Watertown, MA, United States) for retrograde labeling of excitatory projection neurons and rAAV9_CAG-eGFP (#37825 Addgene, Watertown, MA, United States) for local labeling of cells at the injection site. Viral vectors were prepared in a genome copy ratio of 1:2.5 and injected at the same time using a stereotax (Kostka and Bitzenhofer, 2022; Figure 1A). Viral vectors were injected at four different time points, for which volumes and coordinates were adjusted to compensate for brain volume to result in a comparable virus spread across development, i.e., P0 (neonatal, 130 nl at a rate of 120 nl/min, 0.3 mm posterior to the inferior cerebral vein, 0.2 mm lateral to the midline and 0.4–0.8 mm ventral from the brain surface), P10 (pre-juvenile, 170 nl at a rate of 120 nl/min, 1.6 mm anterior to Bregma, 0.2 mm lateral to midline and 0.8–1.2 mm ventral from the brain surface), P20 (juvenile, 190 nl at a rate of 120 nl/min, 1.8 mm anterior to Bregma, 0.2 mm lateral to midline and 1–1.4 mm ventral from the brain surface), and P50 (adult, 210 nl at a rate of 120 nl/min, 1.9 mm anterior to Bregma, 0.2 mm lateral to midline and 1.1–1.5 mm ventral from the brain surface). Mice were transcardially perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) 10 days after virus injection, i.e., at P10 (neonatal, n = 6), P20 (pre-juvenile, n = 6), P30 (juvenile, n = 6), and P60 (adult, n = 6). To further account for differences in viral spread at the injection site, brains were manually categorized as low spread, normal spread, or high spread. Low spread was defined as expression in only one mPFC subdivision or a very low degree of labeled cell bodies in more than one subdivision (neonatal n = 1, pre-juvenile n = 1). High spread was defined as labeled cell bodies in all three subdivisions of the mPFC, while also slightly exceeding mPFC boundaries along the dorsal-ventral or anterior-posterior axis (neonatal n = 1, pre-juvenile n = 1, juvenile n = 1, adult n = 1). Accordingly, normal spread was defined as expression in at least two subdivisions without exceeding mPFC boundaries (neonatal n = 4, pre-juvenile n = 4, juvenile n = 5, adult n = 5). Virus spread was included in the statistical analysis (see Statistical analysis, Supplementary discussion, and Statistics Supplementary Tables 1, 2).

Tissue clearing and light-sheet imaging

Light-sheet imaging on an open-source mesoSPIM (Voigt et al., 2019) was used to generate brain-wide overviews of mPFC-projecting brain regions at neonatal (injected at P0, perfused at P10) and pre-juvenile (injected at P10, perfused at P20) age. Brains were fixed by perfusion before being cleared with SHIELD reagent (Park et al., 2019) to preserve endogenous fluorescence and imaged in EasyIndex solution (RI = 1.465, Life Canvas Technologies) with an Orca Flash 4.0 camera (Hamamatsu).

Cell quantification

For confocal microscopy, brains were prepared in coronal 100 μm sections and mounted on slides using Vectashield with DAPI (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, United States). For cell quantification, images were obtained using a confocal microscope (LSN700, Zeiss, Germany) with a 20× objective (2,048 × 2,048 pixels; 319.5 × 319.5 μm). Areas were demarcated according to the coronal P56 mouse reference atlas from the Allen Brain Atlas1. Additionally, images of mice injected at P0 were also compared to a developing mouse brain atlas (Paxinos, 2007). Depending on the size of the targeted brain region, between 3 and 6 images were acquired per mouse. Images were segmented with Cellpose 2 in Anaconda (Python 3.8) using both the standard model algorithm “cyto2” for the images of cells transduced by rAAV9rg_CaMKII-mCherry and a self-trained algorithm as described for the human-in-the-loop approach (Pachitariu and Stringer, 2022) for rAAV9_CAG-eGFP-transduced cells. After automatic cell segmentation, all images were validated by visual inspection. Confocal images from 22 prefrontal and long-range projecting brain sections with mCherry-labeled neurons were used to quantify the density of retrogradely labeled mPFC-projecting neurons across development. Images of eGFP-labeled cells were acquired from prefrontal areas at the injection site (ACA, PL, ILA, ORB, MOs) for normalization of quantification of retrogradely labeled projections. Immunohistochemistry

For immunohistochemistry, brains were sectioned coronally at 50 μm thickness. Staining of myelin basic protein (MBP) at the CC was performed on the corresponding sections of injected brains after perfusion at P10 (n = 4), P20 (n = 3), P30 (n = 3), and P60 (n = 3) as previously described (Günther et al., 2026; Parbst et al., 2025). Briefly, brain slices were permeabilized using 0.8% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA) and blocked with 5% normal goat serum and 5% normal donkey serum (Jackson Immuno Research, PA, USA) for 1 h. Samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C with an MBP antibody (1:500, Abcam ab40390; Cat# ab40390, RRID:AB_114152). As a secondary antibody Goat-Anti-Rabbit IgG (H&L) DyLight 633 (1:1000, Novus, NBP1-76057; Cat# NBP1-76057, RRID:AB_11059121) was used.

Myelin quantification

For myelin quantification, images were obtained using a confocal microscope (F1000, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) with a 60× objective and 2× zoom (1024 × 1024 pixels; 105.47 × 105.47 mm). Immunohistochemically stained samples from injected brains were used to image axonal fibers and myelin at the CC. Confocal image stacks were consistently acquired at the midline of the corpus callosum from anatomically comparable rostrocaudal sections across animals (i.e., P10 +0.6 AP, P20 +0.7 AP, P30 +0.7 AP, and P60 +1.0 AP). For every animal, 3–4 stacks from different brain slices (10–30 single images, 150 nm interval) were acquired. After preprocessing and thresholding in ImageJ, colocalization of MBP-positive and mCherry-labeled structures was calculated in Matlab R2023a using a custom-written algorithm similar to Pöpplau et al. (2024). Values were normalized for stack depth.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was carried out in R using linear mixed effect models (LME) of the “lme4” package in R 4.4.1. We assessed the impact of age on NCCs while including sex and viral spread as additional fixed effects. Animal identity was included as a random intercept to account for multiple image-based measurements obtained from the same individual animal. Analyzed image stacks of myelinated structures, labeled axonal fibers, and their colocalization were similarly analyzed with LME models including age, sex, viral spread, and the number of images within each analyzed stack as fixed effects, and animal identity as a random effect (full model: NCC ∼ age + viral spread + sex + (1 | animal)). The number of images within each stack was included as an additional covariate to account for potential variation in the amount of tissue sampled between image stacks and significantly influenced the measured values (see Statistics Supplementary Table 1). Briefly, likelihood ratio tests were performed to compare log-likelihoods between the full and the reduced model. To test for a significant effect of age group, sex, viral spread, or, for the myelination analysis, the number of stack images, each fixed effect was individually removed from the full model, and the resulting reduced model was compared with the full model using the “anova” function. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons between age groups were conducted using Tukey-adjusted pairwise contrasts implemented with the “multcomp” package. P-values were adjusted using the Holm method “holm” to account for multiple comparisons (see Statistics Supplementary Table 1).

Results

mPFC afferents show age-dependent trajectories

Resolving the developmental dynamics of long-range projections to the mPFC from different brain regions and between hemispheres is the basis for understanding how prefrontal circuits gain and develop their functions. In a first step, we used light-sheet microscopy on cleared brain tissue to obtain a whole-brain overview of mPFC afferents during early postnatal development. We focused this exploratory analysis on the earliest developmental stages because the organization of adult mPFC afferents has already been comprehensively characterized (Ährlund-Richter et al., 2019). For the qualitative light-sheet overview, one mouse received unilateral mPFC injections of rAAV9rg_mCaMKII-mCherry and rAAV9_CAG-eGFP at P0, and a second mouse received the same injections at P10, following a previously established protocol for assessing afferent development in the olfactory bulb (Kostka and Bitzenhofer, 2022). Tissue was perfused and cleared 10 days after injection (P10 and P20, respectively; Figures 1A, B and Supplementary Figure 1). This qualitative whole-brain screening was used to identify candidate afferent regions for subsequent quantitative confocal analysis across the full developmental range. Based on this, we selected 22 brain regions with direct axonal projections to the mPFC during early development and adulthood for detailed analysis. We also included the PAG and VTA, which showed prominent glutamatergic afferent projections during development, which is not reported for the adult brain (Hoover and Vertes, 2007; Ährlund-Richter et al., 2019). For quantification, we used confocal microscopy on slices from injected brains across the entire developmental period (i.e., neonatal P0, pre-juvenile P10, juvenile P20, adult P50). We then performed cell segmentation (Figures 1C–E) to quantify the density of retrogradely labeled mPFC-projecting neurons in prefrontal subdivisions (ACA, PL, ILA, ORB, MOs) as well as in cortical (AI, PIRI, BLA, BMA, RSP, ENT, CA1v, CA1i, SUBv, SUBd) and subcortical areas (CLA, ATN, VM, MD, SUM, PAG, VTA), projecting ipsi- and contralaterally to the mPFC. We calculated the NCC (normalized cell count: calculated as the ratio of mCherry-labeled cells from each brain area vs. all eGFP- and mCherry-labeled cells, see section “2 Materials and methods”) to normalize for differences in local viral spread across age groups (Figure 1F, see Statistics Supplementary Tables 1, 2, and Supplementary discussion).

mPFC afferent organization increases along development

In order to assess local prefrontal afferent labeling, we quantified retrogradely labeled cells in prefrontal subdivisions of the ipsilateral hemisphere (marked as i for all regions). The density of labeled cells in mPFC and adjacent subdivisions changed with age. ORBi labeling was influenced by sex and viral spread (see Figures 2A–E and Statistics Supplementary Table 1, 2). For the ACAi and PLi the NCC increased from neonatal to pre-juvenile age and remained stable afterward (Figures 2A,B). Solely ILAi local afferent labeling peaked at juvenile age (Figure 2C). In contrast, the NCC in adjacent MOsi and ORBi did not significantly change with age (Figures 2D,E). Taken together, these data indicate that retrogradely labeled inputs originating from ACAi, PLi, and ILAi become more prominent during development.

Cortical long-range afferents to the mPFC peak at (pre-)juvenile age

Next, we quantified the density of long-range afferents originating from ipsilateral cortical regions (RSPi, PIRIi, AIi, ENTi, SUBvi, SUBdi, CA1ii, and CA1vi) as well as adjacent regions (BLAi, BMAi, and CLAi). While viral spread had no significant effect on labeling, sex significantly influenced labeling in the CLAi, RSPi, and PIRIi, with female mice showing lower labeling across age groups (see Statistics Supplementary Tables 1, 2). Except for AIi, which exhibited consistently high afferent density across development (Figure 3A), most regions showed distinct age-dependent afferent labeling patterns. Projections from BMAi and CLAi increased markedly from neonatal to pre-juvenile age and changed only modestly thereafter. A similar, although less pronounced, developmental pattern was observed for ENTi and SUBvi. Notably, BLAi-to-mPFC afferents were nearly absent at neonatal age, emerged by pre-juvenile age, and reached their highest density at juvenile age. In contrast, RSPi afferent density peaked at pre-juvenile age, whereas CA1ii and CA1vi projections peaked at juvenile age and remained comparatively low at the other developmental stages (Figures 3B–I,K). SUBdi displayed a distinct developmental trajectory, with dense projections to the mPFC at neonatal age that progressively declined and were nearly absent in adulthood (Figure 3J).

Together, these findings reveal distinct region-specific developmental dynamics of ipsilateral inputs to the mPFC, with labeling of long-range mPFC-projecting populations being particularly dense during the pre-juvenile and juvenile periods.

Prefrontal projections from thalamic and hypothalamic nuclei develop in an inverse manner

Next, we analyzed long-range prefrontal afferents arising from ipsilateral subcortical brain regions (ATNi, VMi, MDi, SUMi, PAGi, and VTAi). For the ATNi, we quantified the interanterodorsal nucleus as well as ventral and dorsal sections of the AM. VTAi-to-mPFC projections remained relatively stable throughout development (Figure 4A). In contrast, afferent densities from VMi and ATNi increased from neonatal to pre-juvenile age and remained largely stable thereafter, although neonatal projection densities were low in VMi but already high in ATNi (Figures 4B,C). MDi afferent density strongly decreased with age, indicating a progressive sparsification of prefrontal inputs from this region (Figure 4D). SUMi-to-mPFC projections showed a similar decline, although this decrease emerged only from pre-juvenile age onward (Figure 4E). Notably, projections from both VMi and SUMi were influenced by sex. Consistent with the pattern observed in several cortical regions, SUMi afferent densities were lower in females across age groups. In contrast, females showed higher VMi afferent densities than males after neonatal age (see Statistics Supplementary Tables 1, 2 and Supplementary discussion). PAGi-to-mPFC projections were generally sparse but followed a developmental pattern similar to that observed in several cortical regions, with afferent density peaking at pre-juvenile age (Figure 4F).

In summary, most prefrontal afferents from subcortical regions reach high densities at neonatal or pre-juvenile age, yet their dynamics, either increasing or decreasing with age, are region-specific.

Increasing ipsilateral dominance of prefrontal afferents

Adult mPFC has been reported to receive afferents from numerous contralateral regions (Anastasiades and Carter, 2021). To clarify how these projections evolve along development, we monitored contralateral prefrontal afferents from birth to adulthood alongside ipsilateral projections (marked as c for all regions). No age-dependent changes of prefrontal afferent density from contralateral ACAc, PLc, ILAc and MOsc were detected (Supplementary Figures 2A,B). In contrast, the number of labeled cells in the ORBc decreased from neonatal to juvenile and adult age, following a similar trend as observed for the ORBi (Supplementary Figure 2B). Based on their developmental trajectories, we classified contralateral long-range afferents into four distinct patterns. First, afferents were prominent early in development and strongly declined thereafter. This pattern was observed for projections from AIc, ATNc, MDc, SUMc, SUBdc, PIRIc, VTAc, and RSPc (Figures 5A,C,E and Supplementary Figures 2C,D). Among these regions, VTAc and RSPc were unique in that retrogradely labeled cells were present at neonatal and pre-juvenile age before subsequently declining (Supplementary Figure 2D). Second, some contralateral afferents were already present at neonatal age and remained relatively stable throughout development, as observed for projections from CLAc and ENTc (Figures 5B,D and Supplementary Figure 2E). Third, BLAc and BMAc projections displayed a distinct developmental profile, with afferent densities peaking at juvenile age (Supplementary Figure 2F). Fourth, several regions showed little to no contralateral prefrontal labeling throughout development, including VMc, PAGc, CA1vc, CA1ic, and SUBc (Figure 5D and Supplementary Figures 2G,H). Notably, labeling in several contralateral regions was significantly influenced by viral spread (see Statistics Supplementary Tables 1, 2 and Supplementary discussion), whereas among ipsilateral regions such an effect was detected only in the ORB. This difference may arise because contralateral projections are generally sparser and more topographically restricted, making their quantification more sensitive to variation in the viral spread at the injection site.

To further assess the hemispheric bias of mPFC afferent organization across development, we calculated an ipsilateral-contralateral laterality index, with positive values indicating a predominance of ipsilateral labeling. Despite distinct developmental trajectories of ipsilateral and contralateral afferents, the laterality index generally increased after neonatal age, indicating a developmental shift toward greater ipsilateral bias. For example, AIi and ATNi afferent densities remained relatively stable across development, whereas their contralateral counterparts declined after neonatal age, resulting, as expected, in an increased ipsilateral bias (Figures 5A,C). Conversely, CLAc and VMc showed little developmental change, whereas their ipsilateral counterparts increased with age, resulting in a progressively stronger ipsilateral bias across development (Figures 5B,D). Finally, the MD showed age-dependent decreases in labeling in both hemispheres. However, the decline was more pronounced contralaterally, similarly resulting in an increasing ipsilateral bias across development (Figure 5E).

Together, these findings reveal pronounced region-specific developmental dynamics of contralateral prefrontal afferents and demonstrate a general shift toward greater ipsilateral predominance of afferent labeling after neonatal age. Notably, the balance between ipsilateral and contralateral inputs develops in a region-specific manner, resulting in distinct patterns even between closely related structures such as different thalamic nuclei.

Myelination of contralateral prefrontal afferents emerges after neonatal age

In addition to monitoring the density of contralateral prefrontal afferents, we also assessed their myelination. For this, we focused on the cortico-cortical projections crossing the CC. The CC represents the largest white matter tract in the mammalian brain (Fitsiori et al., 2011; De León Reyes et al., 2020; Szczupak et al., 2021), and it has been extensively studied at adult age (Yorke and Caviness, 1975; van der Knaap and van der Ham, 2011; De León Reyes et al., 2020; Innocenti et al., 2022). We characterized the developmental dynamics of myelination of contralateral prefrontal afferents in the previously defined age groups (i.e., neonatal, pre-juvenile, juvenile, and adult) (Figure 6A). Overall, myelin expression increased with age (Figure 6B). Retrogradely labeled axons that colocalized with myelin were sparse in neonatal mice, but their density strongly increased at pre-juvenile age and remained elevated until adulthood (Figure 6C). Similarly, the portion of myelin colocalizing with labeled axons increased from neonatal to adult age (Figure 6D).

Discussion

The PFC is a higher-order cortical region that is essential for complex cognitive and affective processes such as decision making, working memory, and emotional regulation. Its maturation is protracted when compared to primary sensory cortices (Chini and Hanganu-Opatz, 2021), suggesting a prolonged time window for structural and functional refinement of prefrontal circuits. In this study, we unraveled the developmental dynamics of local and long-range ipsilateral and contralateral projections to the mPFC, with a specific focus on developmental changes in the density and hemispheric distribution of retrogradely labeled mPFC-projecting neurons from different brain regions (i.e., ACA, PL, IL, ORB, MOs, AI, PIRI, BLA, BMA, RSP, ENT, CA1v, CA1i, SUBv, SUBd, CLA, ATN, VM, MD, SUM, PAG, and VTA) as well as the onset of myelination. Our findings reveal regionally distinct maturational dynamics of prefrontal afferents that might underlie functional and behavioral development.

Developmental shift toward ipsilateral dominance of projections to the mPFC

At neonatal age, afferent projections to the mPFC show a comparatively strong bilateral organization. However, hemispheric refinement of prefrontal inputs emerges through distinct region-specific developmental trajectories rather than through a uniform loss of contralateral afferents. The most commonly observed dynamic of contralateral projections was a decline in projection density after neonatal age (i.e., ORBc, AIc, PIRIc, SUBdc, ATNc, MDc, SUMc, VTAc, and RSPc). While postnatal pruning of perinatal contralateral projections for the ATN and MD has been reported (Minciacchi and Granato, 1989; De León Reyes et al., 2019), our laterality analysis extends these observations by demonstrating that the balance of prefrontal afferents shifts toward the ipsilateral hemisphere after neonatal age across cortical and subcortical pathways. This suggests that the refinement of interhemispheric afferent labeling represents a widespread, yet regionally heterogeneous, feature of mPFC maturation.

Our data further show that myelination of CC-crossing prefrontal afferents is sparse at neonatal age and strongly increases from pre-juvenile age onward, coinciding with the emergence of contralateral afferent refinement. In adult animals, the CC, is the largest white matter tract in the mammalian brain (Fitsiori et al., 2011; De León Reyes et al., 2020) and contains mostly cortico-cortical fibers (Szczupak et al., 2021), although evidence also suggests the presence of cortico-subcortical connections (Milardi et al., 2015; De Benedictis et al., 2016). In line with previous studies (Sturrock, 1980), we detected low amounts of myelin in the CC and found that mPFC axonal fibers are largely unmyelinated at neonatal age. As has been previously shown (Paus et al., 1999; Fornari et al., 2007), myelination of prefrontal afferents increases strikingly during the second postnatal week, co-occurring with weaning and the onset of more complex behavioral abilities (Klune et al., 2021). As oligodendrocyte engagement and axonal wrapping are promoted by activity-dependent mechanisms (de Faria et al., 2019), the absence of myelin in early-stage interhemispheric fibers supports the idea that these projections might lack the necessary activity for functional embedding into mature circuits. In line with this hypothesis, the emergence of activity follows a protracted development in the mPFC (Brockmann et al., 2011). This transient bilateral connectivity may serve as a scaffolding mechanism for the maturation of local prefrontal microcircuits, facilitating early synchronization across hemispheres. The question of whether early contralateral prefrontal projections are functionally active remains to be addressed. Alternatively, these projections could reflect developmental remnants of cerebral ontogenesis that, in the absence of a specific function, are pruned with ongoing maturation.

Developmental refinement was not restricted to contralateral afferents. Ipsilateral inputs followed similarly heterogeneous trajectories: local ACA and PL afferent labeling as well as inputs from VM and ATN increased from neonatal to pre-juvenile age and subsequently stabilized, whereas other projections were already established early or underwent a progressive reduction. Most strikingly, MDi and SUBdi afferents decreased substantially with age. This result partially contradicts earlier qualitative, not statistically validated reports that suggested a rebound of MD input in adulthood (Rios and Villalobos, 2004). Given the well-established role of the MD in working memory and cognitive functions (Parnaudeau et al., 2018), developmental pruning of its projections to the mPFC appears paradoxical. However, pruning of excessive, non-informative, or redundant projections might improve signal fidelity. Our data suggest that this refinement of projections to the mPFC continues into adulthood, potentially facilitating the emergence of precise, goal-directed behavior.

Notably, these developmental trajectories should not necessarily be interpreted as reflecting the initial arrival of afferent axons in the mPFC. Several major afferent systems are already established during early postnatal life and undergo substantial subsequent refinement. For example, MD afferents are present from birth and are reorganized during the first postnatal weeks (Rios and Villalobos, 2004), whereas amygdala-prefrontal projections continue to expand during juvenile and adolescent development (Cunningham et al., 2002). Hippocampal-prefrontal projections are likewise present early in life and undergo prolonged structural and functional maturation (Song et al., 2022). Thus, age-dependent changes in retrograde labeling likely reflect growth, stabilization, or pruning of established pathways rather than simply their initial emergence.

Afferent labeling to the mPFC peaks during early adolescence

One of the most salient observations in our data is the pronounced maturation of multiple long-range projections to the mPFC during the pre-juvenile and juvenile periods, coinciding with the emergence of higher-order cognitive functions (Klune et al., 2021; Thies et al., 2025). The developmental increase was not uniform across mPFC-projecting regions. Some inputs, such as those from BMA, CLA, ENT, SUBv, VM, and ATN, increased strongly by pre-juvenile age. RSPi projections peaked at pre-juvenile age, while afferents from CA1i, CA1vi, and BLAi, as well as contralateral BLA and BMA projections, reached their highest densities at juvenile age. This maturation of afferents during adolescence is notable given their roles in memory, rewards, and adaptive behavior. For example, the SUBv is known to be involved in the rewards circuit of the brain (Ding et al., 2020) and a role of the CA1 in anticipation of consequences has been proposed (Fyhn et al., 2002; Numan, 2015). Thus, this temporal alignment of projections to the mPFC supports behavioral studies that implicate adolescence as a sensitive window for risk-taking and cognitive flexibility in humans (van Duijvenvoorde et al., 2016; McCormick and Telzer, 2018) and in rodents (Johnson and Wilbrecht, 2011; Klune et al., 2021; Thies et al., 2025). Similarly, BLAi-to-mPFC afferents were nearly absent at neonatal age, emerged during the pre-juvenile period, and reached their highest density at juvenile age. Contralateral BLA and BMA afferents likewise peaked at juvenile age, indicating pronounced maturation of bilateral amygdala-prefrontal connectivity during this developmental window. This developmental trajectory has been previously described (Cunningham et al., 2002) and might be essential for the consolidation of fear memories. Notably, the BLA has also been shown to play a role in decision-making paradigms, in which effort and rewards values have to be evaluated (Floresco and Ghods-Sharifi, 2007). Moreover, the developmental increase in AM-derived (as part of the ATNi) mPFC-projecting neurons could facilitate dopamine-dependent rewards processing, as its role in reinforcing goal-directed behaviors has been shown in adult mice (Yang et al., 2022). Since the density of dopaminergic receptors in the mPFC peaks during adolescence (Larsen and Luna, 2018), the maturation of these inputs might contribute to the anatomical foundation for adaptive decision-making and behavioral flexibility.

While some brain areas maintained relatively stable densities of ipsilateral mPFC-projecting neurons after initial increase, other regions showed a decline of projections after early adolescence or even across the entire developmental period assessed here. RSPi afferents peaked at pre-juvenile age before declining, SUMi projections decreased after pre-juvenile age, and SUBdi displayed an especially distinct trajectory, with dense neonatal projections that were nearly absent in adulthood. The RSP and SUM are involved in both prefrontal and hippocampal networks and play a role in spatial navigation as well as episodic memory (Vann et al., 2009; Ito et al., 2018; Loureiro et al., 2019).

Together with the transient developmental profiles of other cortical and limbic afferents, these findings raise the possibility that the mPFC receives a particularly broad and dense set of long-range inputs during the transition of young mice toward independent exploration. Such transient connectivity may facilitate developmental adaptations before being refined into the more selective organization characteristic of the adult prefrontal network.

Concluding remarks

Our data reveal that the development of mPFC afferents is neither linear nor uniform but characterized by region-specific developmental dynamics of afferent emergence, maintenance, and refinement (see Supplementary discussion). Moreover, quantitative comparison of ipsilateral and contralateral afferent labeling reveals a developmental shift from comparatively bilateral neonatal afferent labeling toward increasing ipsilateral dominance, which emerges through distinct mechanisms across individual afferent pathways. This refinement is accompanied by increasing myelination of interhemispheric prefrontal axons after neonatal age and by a particularly dense organization of several long-range inputs during the pre-juvenile and juvenile periods. These anatomical changes closely parallel known milestones of behavioral and functional maturation, suggesting structural development as a framework for functional circuit integration and adult-like cognitive functions.

At the same time, these developmental trajectories need to be interpreted within the methodological constraints of the present approach (see Supplementary discussion). Although injection coordinates and volumes were adjusted according to developmental brain size, injection spread was categorized, and viral spread was included as a covariate in the statistical models, these measures cannot fully exclude age-dependent differences in effective infection volume, viral uptake, retrograde transport efficiency, transgene expression, tissue properties, or the relative proportion of individual mPFC subdivisions targeted. Moreover, retrograde AAV efficiency has not been calibrated across the complete developmental range investigated here. Consequently, age-dependent differences in labeling should be interpreted as relative changes in the anatomical organization of mPFC afferents under matched experimental conditions rather than as absolute measures of connectivity or synaptic strength.

Within these interpretative boundaries, the present dataset nevertheless provides a quantitative framework for understanding how local, long-range, and interhemispheric afferents are reorganized during mPFC maturation and highlights developmental periods and pathways that may be particularly susceptible to perturbation. Knowledge about mPFC afferent development might help to understand how early afferent maturation shapes the integrative functions of the mPFC and how disruptions may result in lifelong phenotypes, such as those associated with neurodevelopmental disorders (Pöpplau and Hanganu-Opatz, 2024; Bitzenhofer and Günther, 2026).
