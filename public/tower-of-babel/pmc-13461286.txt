Expanding the Phenotype: Myoclonus–Dystonia as a Novel Manifestation of 47,XXX Syndrome
Authors: Lakshmi Priya Lalitha, Asish Vijayaraghavan, Kiren George Koshy, Sayooja Sachithanandan, Divya Kalikavil Puthenveedu, Syam Krishnan
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Triple X syndrome (TXS), or 47,XXX, is a sex chromosome aneuploidy first described by Jacobs in 1959 and resulting from non-disjunction during meiosis with an approximate incidence of 1 in 1,000 live-born females [1]. The phenotype of this disorder is hypothesized to result from the overexpression of genes that escape X inactivation, and the phenotypes of only approximately 10% of the patients are diagnosed as mild [2]. The reported features include developmental delay, intellectual disability, seizures, subtle facial and skeletal dysmorphisms, small head size, tall stature, joint hypermobility, amenorrhea, and premature ovarian failure. In addition, intention tremors have been reported in approximately 6%–20% of patients with TXS, but other movement disorders are rarely reported [1]. We report the first case of a patient with TXS who presented with myoclonus-dystonia syndrome (MDS) without any detectable pathogenic single-gene variants on whole-exome sequencing (WES), thereby expanding the phenotypic spectrum of this underrecognized chromosomal disorder.

A 27-year-old woman, born of nonconsanguineous parents with a normal perinatal and developmental history, presented with an insidious onset of jerky movements of both hands that had gradually progressed since 13 years of age, which resulted in a gradual impairment of her ability to write and feed herself. She had no behavioral problems or intellectual disability. Clinical examination revealed dystonic posturing and myoclonus of both hands in the outstretched and batwing positions. She had significant dystonic posturing of the right hand while writing, with overflow dystonia to the right shoulder. She also had dystonic posturing of the left hand while holding a glass of water. Myoclonic jerks in the right upper limb were also clearly appreciable while trying to empty a glass of water, suggestive of action myoclonus (Supplementary Video 1). No dysmorphic facies, pyramidal, other extrapyramidal, or cerebellar features were present on examination. The family history was negative. Clinical presentation was suggestive of MDS. Differential diagnoses included MDS related to pathogenic variants in SGCE, ANO3, and KCTD17. Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain and electroencephalogram were normal, and evaluation for Wilson disease with serum ceruloplasmin and copper levels was unremarkable. WES revealed no pathogenic single-gene variants but suggested possible duplication of the X chromosome, which was confirmed on karyotyping as 47,XXX, establishing a diagnosis of TXS. Her detailed neuropsychological assessment and abdominal ultrasonography were normal. She was treated with zonisamide and clonazepam, which resulted in mild subjective improvement with regard to eating and writing.

We report the first documented case of MDS in a patient with TXS confirmed by karyotyping, who notably lacked classical associated features such as dysmorphic facies, behavioral disturbances, or psychiatric manifestations. This unique presentation broadens the spectrum of chromosomal abnormalities possibly associated with MDS, a genetically heterogeneous condition characterized by nonepileptic myoclonus and dystonia as the sole or prominent feature. The prototypical cause of MDS is a pathogenic variant in epsilon-sarcoglycan (SGCE) gene on chromosome 7q21-q31 [3]. Non-SGCE mutations resulting in MDS in the first and second decades of life include mutations in ANO3, KCTD17, ADCY 5, RELN, GCH-1, TMEM240, NKX2-1, 22q11.2 deletion, CYP2A1, and PRKCG [4,5].

MDS has been reported to be associated with maternal uniparental disomy 7, and it was hypothesized that this was related to the lack of expression of the SGCE gene on chromosome 7 [6]. Other chromosomal aberrations, such as Klinefelter’s syndrome (XXY) and fragile X premutation, have been associated with tremors and ataxia. Necpál et al. [7] reported a young girl who presented with dystonia and tremors and had dual genetic abnormalities (47,XXX and SATB1 mutation), but the phenotype could not be conclusively attributed to either mutation individually by the authors. Our patient had only sex chromosome aneuploidy, thereby expanding the genotypic spectrum of MDS. A typical female has a 46,XX karyotype, and one X chromosome is active, but the other is inactivated by DNA methylation throughout the chromosome and the accumulation of a histone variant. X-chromosome inactivation begins in blastogenesis and is controlled by the X chromosome inactivation center, which counts the number of X chromosomes in a diploid cell and inactivates all except one copy. The selected copy is silenced through expression of the XIST and Tsix genes. However, few regions on the X chromosome, called pseudoautosomal regions (PAR1 and PAR2), in addition to approximately 5%–10% of adjacent genes, escape X inactivation and remain genetically active. In TXS, these regions in all three X chromosome copies remain active and affect phenotypic expression; however, the specific genes involved remain to be elucidated [1].

In most cohorts for which clinical or prenatal WES is employed for single-gene diagnoses; sex chromosome aneuploidies such as 47,XXX are usually picked up incidentally only when dedicated copy number variants/aneuploidy pipelines are used.

This case implicates MDS as one of the phenotypic expressions associated with TXS, although a rare possibility of coincidental association cannot be completely ruled out. This case also highlights the pivotal role of dedicated chromosomal detection studies—particularly karyotyping and chromosomal microarray—in confirming such rare genotype–phenotype correlations, as standard WES, which targets single-gene variants and may not reliably detect 47,XXX or other chromosomal aneuploidies.
