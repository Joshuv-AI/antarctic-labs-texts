Stress recovery effects of visual exposure to bamboo landscapes in urban parks: Evidence from physiological and psychological responses
Authors: Meiyun Huang, Ruiyi Jiang, Zhiwen Deng, Meixia Lin, Jiannan Lin, Lingyan Chen, Yushan Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInteractions with bamboo forests and bamboo parks are known to support human health, yet the stress-recovery effects of bamboo landscapes in urban parks remain insufficiently explored. This study examined psychophysiological responses to bamboo landscapes compared with an urban block landscape and evaluated how different landscape configurations influence stress recovery. Using photographic simulations, 280 university students were exposed to seven conditions: an urban block landscape (control) and six bamboo landscape types combining bamboo with terrain, architecture, environmental furniture, pavement, water, or plants. Physiological responses were assessed using skin conductance level (SCL), blood pressure, and heart rate variability indices, including high-frequency (HF) power and the low-frequency/high-frequency ratio (LF/HF), while psychological responses were measured using the Chinese revised version of the abbreviated Profile of Mood States. Compared with the urban control, visual exposure to bamboo landscapes was associated with significant stress-recovery effects, indicated by decreased SCL, LF/HF, and blood pressure, alongside increased HF and improved mood states (P < 0.05), whereas the control showed opposite trends. Landscapes integrating water or plant elements showed significantly greater stress-recovery effects than those incorporating built components (e.g., pavement and environmental furniture), with consistent differences observed across physiological and psychological measures (P < 0.05). No significant differences were found between bamboo–water, bamboo–plant, and bamboo–architecture landscapes. These findings indicate that while bamboo landscapes generally supported stress recovery, their effectiveness varies with landscape configuration. Introduction

With the acceleration of urbanization, an increasing proportion of individuals are experiencing mental stress1. Substantial evidence indicates that stress poses a serious threat to human health and contributes to a range of disorders2,3. Chronic stress without adequate relief may lead to physical conditions, including gastrointestinal, cardiovascular, neurological, and immunological diseases4, as well as psychological disorders such as depression and anxiety5. Consequently, effective stress management among urban residents is essential for maintaining overall well-being. Exposure to natural environments has been widely recognized as an effective approach to alleviating stress6. Among various forms of urban nature, urban parks are particularly important, as they provide accessible opportunities for daily interaction with natural environments and serve as valuable outdoor spaces for promoting health in densely populated cities7–9.

The health-promoting effects of natural environments have been extensively interpreted through theoretical frameworks such as attention restoration theory (ART)10 and stress recovery theory (SRT)11. ART proposes that natural settings facilitate the restoration of voluntary attention by engaging involuntary attention processes12, whereas SRT suggests that non-threatening natural environments elicit positive affective responses and reduce physiological arousal11. Consistent with these frameworks, a large body of empirical evidence demonstrates that, relative to urban environments, exposure to natural landscapes enhances parasympathetic nervous system (PNS) activity while suppresses sympathetic nervous system (SNS) activity. These effects are reflected in physiological responses such as reduced skin conductance level (SCL)13, lower blood pressure (BP)14, and favorable changes in heart rate variability (HRV), including decreased low-frequency/high-frequency (LF/HF) ratio and increased high-frequency (HF) components15. Parallel improvements have been observed at the psychological level, with widely used instruments—including the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory13,14, Semantic Differential Method14,16, and Profile of Mood States16,17—consistently indicating reduced negative affect and enhanced emotional well-being following exposure to natural environments.

Building on this theoretical and empirical foundation, increasing attention has been directed toward examining whether indirect exposure to nature can elicit comparable stress-recovery effects18. Although mediated experiences are unlikely to completely substitute direct contact, they may provide meaningful benefits for individuals with limited access to outdoor environments19. To investigate these effects, prior research has adopted a range of methodological approaches spanning both field and laboratory contexts. Field-based studies have examined psychophysiological responses under real-world or quasi-natural conditions (e.g., window views or in situ exposure), whereas laboratory experiments have employed controlled designs using standardized stimuli such as photographs, videos, and virtual reality environments, often combined with physiological and psychological measurements20–23. Such methodological frameworks have been widely applied to examine stress recovery processes across different landscape settings. In particular, the use of visual stimuli has become a dominant experimental paradigm for isolating the effects of environmental characteristics on human psychophysiological responses24–26.

Bamboo landscapes represent a distinctive type of natural environments, especially in Chinese urban parks. Existing studies have primarily focused on bamboo forests, demonstrating their capacity to regulate microclimate, improve air quality, and enhance environmental conditions27, thereby indirectly supporting human health. Moreover, several studies have reported direct psychophysiological benefits associated with exposure to bamboo forests14,28,29 or bamboo parks30, including changes in brain activity14,28,30, reductions in blood pressure14,28,29, pulse rate28–30, and heart rate31, increases in HF and decreases in LF/HF ratio29, as well as improvements in mood states14,29,31. Despite these encouraging findings, empirical evidence specifically addressing bamboo landscapes in urban parks remains limited. In practical landscape design, bamboo is frequently combined with various landscape elements, creating diverse spatial configurations. To identify representative bamboo landscape types, eleven studies on bamboo landscape applications published between 2019 and 2024 were retrieved from the China National Knowledge Infrastructure database. These application types were synthesized alongside recent bamboo landscape classification studies, and the landscape element classification framework proposed by Norman K. Booth32 was adopted and extended to establish a conceptual basis for categorizing combinations of bamboo and other landscape elements (Table 1). However, despite their widespread application, systematic evidence regarding how different configurations of bamboo landscapes influence human stress recovery remains scarce. In particular, it remains unclear whether bamboo landscapes incorporating natural elements (e.g., terrain, water, plants) provide greater stress-recovery effects than those integrating built components (e.g., architecture, pavement, environmental furniture).

To address these gaps, the present study investigates the psychophysiological responses of visual exposure to bamboo landscapes in urban parks, with a particular focus on stress recovery effects. Specifically, the study compares stress recovery after viewing bamboo landscapes versus one urban block landscape with minimal natural elements, and further examines differences among six bamboo landscapes, each featuring a distinct element: terrain, architecture, environmental furniture, pavement, water, and plants. Based on previous evidence, we hypothesized that bamboo landscapes in urban parks would provide greater stress-recovery effects than the urban block landscape (H1), and that bamboo landscapes integrating natural elements would demonstrate greater stress-recovery benefits than those incorporating built components (H2). The findings are expected to advance understanding of the role of bamboo landscapes in promoting stress recovery and to inform the design of health-supportive urban green spaces.

Materials and methods

Research region

Fujian Province is a major region in China with a high concentration of bamboo plants27. Its distinct geographic and climatic conditions have promoted the growth of a wide range of bamboo species. In Fuzhou, the capital city of Fujian Province, bamboo has been extensively integrated into urban green spaces, particularly in urban parks, as a common landscape element. Among the 44 urban parks surveyed in this study, 43 parks (except Xihe Intelligent Sports Park) incorporate bamboo in various forms, representing commonly used landscape application modes. This widespread use provides a consistent and ecologically valid basis for selecting experimental stimuli.

Moreover, Fuzhou has experienced continuous urban development and population growth in recent years, accompanied by increasing urban density and environmental pressures. Such conditions may contribute to elevated stress levels among urban residents, increasing the need for accessible natural environments that support stress recovery and emotional well-being. In this context, urban parks may serve as important spaces for everyday contact with nature. Therefore, selecting bamboo landscapes in Fuzhou’s urban parks ensures alignment between regional environmental characteristics and the study’s focus on psychophysiological indicators of stress recovery, while enhancing the real-world relevance of the findings.

Experimental stimuli

In studies examining stress recovery in natural environments, short-term stress induction13,17 is often used to initially elevate participants’ stress levels, followed by exposure to nature to assess subsequent recovery responses.

Stress induction

Earlier studies have induced stress through a range of methods, including taking a spoken English exam13 or administering a mental arithmetic test44. In this study, a three-minute timed mock spoken-English exam was employed as the stressor task, chosen for its appropriateness for the student population. Research has shown that when students speak in English or other foreign languages, they usually feel stressed and anxious45, making this an effective and practical stress-induction method. The 50 spoken-English questions used in the exam were drawn from part 2 of the International English Language Testing System (IELTS) speaking test. A random number generated by Microsoft Excel 2016 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA) was used to select the question for each participant, with the question number corresponding to the random number. Each participant had 1 min to review the selected question and 2 min to respond. The spoken-English exam question number and spoken-English exam questions are presented in Supplementary Table S1 and Table S2.

Stress-recovery stimuli

To investigate stress recovery, visual simulation methods were employed, as commonly adopted in studies examining psychophysiological responses to natural environments. Compared with field-based experiments, this approach allows for greater control over external disturbances, such as human activity and meteorological conditions. Previous studies have supported the validity and reliability of such simulated stimuli46,47. In the present study, participants were exposed to photographs of bamboo landscapes in urban parks, with an urban block landscape used as a non-vegetated baseline control condition. The landscape categories included B0 (urban block landscape with little or no natural elements, control), B1 (bamboo–terrain landscape), B2 (bamboo–architecture landscape), B3 (bamboo–environmental furniture landscape), B4 (bamboo–pavement landscape), B5 (bamboo–water landscape), and B6 (bamboo–plant landscape) (Fig. 1). Each category consisted of 10 photographs, which were presented to the corresponding participant group. All photographs used for visual stimulation are provided in Supplementary Figure S1-S7.

The photographs were captured using an iPhone 11 (Apple Inc., Cupertino, CA, USA) under standardized sunny summer conditions. Image acquisition was conducted during two fixed time windows (9:00–11:00 a.m. and 2:30 − 4:30 p.m.) to minimize variation in lighting conditions and no post-processing was applied. During the experiment, participants were seated and exposed to a 180-second visual stimulus presented via a PowerPoint slideshow on a computer. The images were presented in randomized order. Each image was displayed for 15 s, with a 3-second interval between consecutive images. The computer screen was positioned approximately 41 cm from the participants’ eyes and adjusted according to individual height.

Measurement

The selected physiological and psychological indices were used to evaluate stress-related psychophysiological responses before and after visual exposure.

Physiological indices

Skin conductance level

SCL is a widely used physiological indicator of stress-related autonomic arousal12,48, reflecting sweat gland activity regulated by the SNS49. Increased SNS activity is associated with elevated SCL and is typically linked to stress states, whereas decreased SNS activity during relaxation leads to reduced SCL50. SCL was measured using an electrodermal activity sensor (ErgoLAB V1.0, Beijing Kingfar Science & Technology Co., Ltd, Beijing, China) (Fig. 2a). Electrodes were attached to the middle and index fingers of the left hand to record skin electrical signals. Each participant underwent two 3-min recordings (before and after visual exposure). The mean SCL value from each recording was used for statistical analysis.

Experimental instruments used in this study: (a) a physiological recorder for measuring skin conductance level; (b) a physiological recorder for measuring heart rate variability; (c) an electronic sphygmomanometer for measuring blood pressure. Notes: These photographs were taken by Meiyun Huang. The brand identifiers on the blood pressure monitor and cuff shown in panel (c) have been removed.

Heart rate variability

HRV refers to the variability in R-R intervals and is widely used to assess autonomic nervous system (ANS) activity51. HRV was monitored via a photoplethysmography sensor (ErgoLAB V1.0, Beijing Kingfar Science & Technology Co., Ltd, Beijing, China) (Fig. 2b). The low-frequency (LF, 0.04–0.15 Hz) and high-frequency (HF, 0.15–0.40 Hz) components were extracted. The HF component corresponds to PNS activity, whereas the LF/HF ratio is positively associated with SNS activity52. Signals were recorded using an ear clip attached to the middle of right earlobe. Each participant underwent two 3-min recordings (before and after visual exposure), and the mean HRV indices were used for statistical analysis.

Blood pressure

Blood pressure, including systolic and diastolic blood pressure, reflects ANS activity. Increased SNS activity is associated with elevated blood pressure, while PNS activation is related to decreased blood pressure53. Blood pressure was measured with an Omron HEM-7127 J upper-arm electronic sphygmomanometer (Omron Healthcare Co., Ltd., Kyoto, Japan) (Fig. 2c). Three consecutive measurements were taken before and after visual exposure, with a 30-s interval between measurements. The mean of the three readings was used for statistical analysis.

Psychological indices

The Chinese version of the abbreviated Profile of Mood States (POMS), revised by Zhu Beili54 based on the scale developed by Robert Grove et al.55, was used to assess participants’ mood states before and after viewing landscape photographs. The POMS is a widely recognized, analytically derived instrument for assessing emotional distress, with proven reliability and validity. In this study, the Chinese POMS consisted of 35 adjectives rated on a 4−0 scale (4 = extremely, 3 = quite a bit, 2 = moderately, 1 = a little, 0 = not at all), which were categorized into six dimensions: Tension (T), Fatigue (F), Vigor (V), Anger (A), Depression (D), and Confusion (C). Scores for each dimension were obtained by summing the ratings of all items belonging to the corresponding subscale. The translated scale is presented in Supplementary Table S3. Lower ratings for T, F, A, D, and C indicate lower levels of negative mood states, while higher ratings for V represent a more positive mood state. Total mood disturbance (TMD) score was calculated according to Formula56(1). A higher TMD score reflects greater overall mood disturbance. A total of 280 completed POMS questionnaires, corresponding to seven landscape types, were collected for analysis in the present study.

Participant

An a priori power analysis using G*Power (version 3.1.9.7; Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany) determined the minimum required sample size (see Supplementary Methods S1 for parameter settings and output results). To account for potential attrition, 280 healthy university students in China (50% male; age 18–34 years, mean ± SD: 21.7 ± 2.3) from Fujian Agriculture and Forestry University (FAFU) were recruited via posters and social media platforms (e.g., WeChat). Among participants, 142 were undergraduates, 120 were master students, and 18 were doctoral students. They were randomly assigned to seven groups, each comprising 40 participants with an equal male-to-female ratio (Supplementary Table S16). All participants had normal vision and no history of cardiovascular, psychiatric, or neurological disorders. Participants were instructed not to consume alcohol on the day before the experiment. Prior to participation, all participants were fully informed about the study objectives and procedures and provided written informed consent. Participants arrived individually at the laboratory at scheduled times. This study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the College of Landscape Architecture and Art, FAFU, China.

Procedure

Before the experiment, participants received a concise explanation of the experimental procedures and instrument functions to minimize potential nervousness. Each participant then provided demographic information, including gender, age, educational background, and major. The experimenter attached the physiological recording devices, and a mock spoken-English exam was administered to induce stress once the participant had returned to a calm state. Immediately after the exam, physiological measurements (SCL, HRV, and blood pressure) were recorded as pre-exposure data. Participants then completed the POMS questionnaire to obtain psychological pre-exposure data. Next, participants viewed a set of landscape photographs for 180 s. After the slideshow, physiological measurements were recorded again to obtain post-exposure data, followed by completion of the POMS questionnaire for post-exposure psychological assessment. Upon completion of all measurements, the experimenter removed the physiological recording devices. All experimental procedures were conducted in a quiet environment, with the same procedure applied to all treatments (Fig. 3).

Statistical analysis

To examine psychophysiological responses associated with stress recovery across various bamboo landscapes in urban parks, Wilcoxon signed-rank test and paired-samples t-test were utilized to compare psychological and physiological indicators before and after visual exposure to each landscape type, with statistical significance defined as P < 0.05 (two-tailed). The normality of the data was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. When the change scores (post-exposure minus pre-exposure) were non-normally distributed, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was employed57; otherwise, the paired-samples t-test was applied58.To compare differences among the seven landscape types, the Kruskal–Wallis H test was applied because the change scores for all indicators violated the assumptions of normality or homogeneity of variance, as indicated by the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests. When significant differences were detected, Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed. Changes in physiological and psychological indicators were interpreted as reflecting psychophysiological responses associated with stress recovery following visual exposure. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 25.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

Physiological and psychological responses to bamboo landscapes and the urban block landscape

To examine psychophysiological responses associated with stress recovery in bamboo landscapes relative to the urban block landscape (H1), physiological and psychological indicators were compared before and after visual exposure across the seven landscape scenarios. Depending on data normality, paired-samples t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were applied.

SCL

Participants’ SCL decreased significantly after visual exposure to five bamboo landscape types (B1, B2, B3, B5, and B6; all P < 0.001). No significant change was observed for B4 (P = 0.100). In contrast, SCL increased significantly after exposure to the urban block landscape (B0) (P < 0.001) (Table 2).

HRV

HF

HF increased significantly after visual exposure to all bamboo landscape types (B1–B6; all P ≤ 0.004). Conversely, HF decreased significantly following exposure to the urban block landscape (B0) (P = 0.002) (Table 2).

LF/HF

LF/HF ratio decreased significantly after visual exposure to all six bamboo landscapes (B1–B6; all P < 0.001), whereas exposure to the urban block landscape (B0) resulted in a significant increase (P < 0.001) (Table 2).

Blood pressure

Both systolic and diastolic blood pressure decreased significantly after visual exposure to the six bamboo landscapes (B1–B6). Systolic blood pressure declined significantly across all bamboo scenarios (all P < 0.001), while diastolic blood pressure also showed significant reductions (all P ≤ 0.027). In contrast, both systolic and diastolic blood pressure increased significantly following exposure to the urban block landscape (B0) (both P < 0.001) (Table 2).

POMS

Psychological responses measured using the POMS varied across landscape conditions. Scores for negative mood states showed significant reductions after visual exposure to bamboo landscapes. Specifically, Tension (all P ≤ 0.018), Depression (all P ≤ 0.032), and Confusion (all P ≤ 0.002) decreased significantly across all bamboo landscape types (B1–B6), whereas these scores increased significantly following exposure to the urban block landscape (B0) (all P < 0.001 ) (Table 3).

SCL

SCL changes differed significantly among the seven landscape types (H = 102.382, P < 0.001). Post hoc comparisons showed that all bamboo landscape scenarios (B1–B6) were associated with significantly greater reductions in SCL than the urban block landscape (B0) (all P ≤ 0.003). Among the bamboo landscapes, B6 and B5 exhibited significantly larger SCL reductions than B4 (P = 0.004 and P = 0.049, respectively) (Fig. 5a).

Discussion

This research investigated differences in stress recovery between exposure to bamboo landscapes and the urban block landscape, and examined whether bamboo landscapes incorporating natural elements offer greater stress-recovery benefits than those integrated with built elements.

Consistent with Hypothesis 1, bamboo landscapes in urban parks provided greater stress-recovery effects than the urban block landscape. Physiologically, after exposure to bamboo landscapes, participants exhibited higher HF values accompanied by reduced SBP and DBP, together with lower SCL and LF/HF ratio. These changes reflect augmented parasympathetic nervous system (PNS) activity and diminished sympathetic nervous system (SNS) activity. As the two major divisions of the autonomic nervous system (ANS), the PNS and SNS jointly regulate the body’s stress–relaxation balance59; therefore, visual exposure to bamboo landscapes was associated with a clear physiological relaxation response. In contrast, exposure to the urban block landscape showed opposite trends in these indicators, suggesting that such environments may be less conducive to stress recovery. These findings support previous research indicating stress-reducing effects linked to bamboo landscapes14,28.

In terms of psychological responses, visual exposure to bamboo landscapes reduced negative mood states—including Fatigue, Tension, Anger, Confusion, and Depression—while increasing Vigor. These results reinforce the potential role of bamboo landscapes in promoting positive mood states and echo earlier studies suggesting that such environments can alleviate negative emotions and enhance positive moods31,53. Conversely, exposure to the urban block landscape was accompanied by increased negative mood states and reduced Vigor. This pattern was further reflected in TMD scores, which were consistently lower after viewing bamboo landscapes (B1–B6) and higher after exposure to the urban block landscape (B0). As higher TMD scores indicate a poorer psychological state60, these findings further support prior evidence that bamboo landscapes may provide greater effects for mood improvement than urban settings31,53.

The contrasting psychophysiological responses observed between the simulated bamboo landscapes and the urban block landscape may be explained by humans’ long evolutionary history of adaptation to natural environments, which has fostered an inborn inclination to affiliate with nature and other living organisms61. Consistent with stress recovery theory (SRT), exposure to natural environments facilitates psychophysiological stress recovery through rapid affective and physiological responses. Bamboo landscapes may be evolutionarily associated with safe and resource-rich environments, potentially contributing to positive affect, reduced SNS activity, and enhanced PNS activity. These processes are reflected in decreases in SCL, LF/HF ratio, and blood pressure, alongside increases in HF. Collectively, these mechanisms suggest that exposure to bamboo landscapes may contribute to stress reduction and improved mood states. The present findings therefore provide empirical support for the potential of visual exposure to bamboo landscapes in urban parks to promote psychophysiological stress recovery under the tested conditions.

Beyond the general stress-recovery effects of bamboo landscapes, notable variations were observed among different landscape types. Overall, the findings provide partial support for Hypothesis 2. Specifically, bamboo landscapes incorporating natural elements (e.g., water and plants) generally exhibited greater stress-recovery benefits than those integrating certain built components (e.g., pavement and environmental furniture). Physiological indicators showed that bamboo–water (B5) and bamboo–plant (B6) landscapes were associated with significantly greater stress reduction than the bamboo–pavement landscape (B4), as evidenced by larger decreases in SCL and greater increases in HF. Psychological responses followed a similar pattern, with B5 and B6 outperforming bamboo–environmental furniture (B3) and bamboo–pavement (B4) landscapes in reducing negative moods and enhancing Vigor. However, no significant differences were observed between bamboo–water (B5), bamboo–plant (B6), and bamboo–architecture (B2) landscapes in either physiological or psychological responses. Likewise, the bamboo–terrain landscape (B1) did not differ significantly from landscapes incorporating built components. Although reductions in TMD scores did not differ significantly between B5 and B6, both were significantly more effective than B3 and B4 (P = 0.006 and 0.017 for B3; P < 0.001 and 0.001 for B4). The slightly stronger effect observed for B5 suggests that bamboo landscapes incorporating water may provide marginally greater stress-recovery benefits than those featuring plants alone.

These findings align with existing theories and empirical studies suggesting that exposure to scenes incorporating natural elements elicits favorable physiological reactions in the ANS and improves psychological well-being10,11,13,61–65. In particular, previous research has shown that stress-reducing effects are strongly associated with the visible amount of natural elements such as plants62,63, and that natural scenes containing water features provide greater benefits than those without water64. Eye-tracking research further indicates that trees and shrubs, and water contribute positively to stress reduction65. The relatively strong effects observed for water landscapes may be partly rooted in evolutionary preferences shaped through long-term human–environment interactions66, although this explanation cannot be directly verified. Additionally, specific visual characteristics of water landscapes may be aesthetically appealing and support stress reduction. Water, for instance, can reflect light in visually engaging way, with certain patterns and lines of light potentially enhancing these effects67. In the current study, water landscapes may have exhibited a greater diversity of light reflections and patterns, which could help explain their relatively greater stress-recovery benefits and warrant further investigation. The absence of significant differences between B5 and B6, and B2 may be explained by the strong cultural and artistic attributes of architectural elements in bamboo landscapes. Previous studies have suggested that built features associated with artistic and cultural attributes can facilitate stress recovery by conveying cultural meaning68,69. In this study, the integration of bamboo with traditional Chinese-style garden architecture created a spatial setting that combines cultural and aesthetic qualities, which may account for the comparable stress-recovery benefits observed across B2, B5, and B6. Similarly, the bamboo–terrain landscape (B1) did not differ significantly from those incorporating built components. This pattern may be attributed to the relatively low perceptual salience of terrain features. Compared with more visually distinctive elements such as water or diverse vegetation, terrain variations in B1 are subtle and thus less likely to provide strong stress-recovery cues. Moreover, the relatively enclosed spatial structure and limited visibility within dense bamboo stands may reduce perceived safety, thereby limiting stress-reduction responses. By contrast, certain built components (e.g., pavement or environmental furniture) may offer clearer spatial organization and environmental affordances, contributing to comparable levels of perceived comfort and stress reduction. Consequently, although B1 represents a natural landscape type, its overall capacity to support stress reduction may not be substantially greater than that of landscapes incorporating certain built elements.

From a practical perspective, these results provide evidence-based guidance for the design and management of bamboo landscapes in urban parks. Specifically, the integration of natural elements—particularly water and plant—may be prioritized to enhance stress-reduction responses associated with bamboo landscapes. At the same time, spatial configuration appears to play a critical role, as excessive enclosure and limited visibility may reduce the positive psychophysiological responses associated with these environments, highlighting the importance of maintaining visual openness and perceptual salience in landscape design. Notably, the comparable responses observed between bamboo–architecture landscapes and those incorporating natural elements suggest that culturally meaningful landscape design does not necessarily compromise stress-reduction effects. Instead, integrating bamboo with traditional architectural elements may offer a balanced approach that combines cultural expression with health benefits. Collectively, these findings highlight the potential of bamboo landscapes as a promising nature-based approach associated with stress reduction and emotional well-being, with important implications for urban public health in increasingly dense and stressful urban environments.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the sampling framework may limit the generalizability of the present findings. Due to logistical constraints, participants were restricted to university students aged 18 to 34. Although they were drawn from diverse academic majors, the sample remained relatively homogeneous. Individuals from different age groups, social classes, and professions may exhibit distinct physiological and psychological responses to bamboo landscapes. Second, limitations related to the photographic stimuli should be considered. To minimize potential confounding influences, photographs were selected to reduce the presence of unrelated landscape elements. However, this approach also resulted in variations in bamboo size, visual prominence, and viewing scale across the six bamboo landscape types. In addition, the use of real-world photographs limited the ability to fully control coexisting landscape elements (e.g., pavement and architecture) and broader visual characteristics of the scenes, which may have influenced participants’physiological and psychological responses. Moreover, although the study focused on bamboo landscapes, the observed stress-reducing effects may not be entirely attributable to the visual characteristics associated with bamboo landscapes. Broader visual attributes of the scenes, such as overall greenness and perceived naturalness, may also have contributed to the psychophysiological responses. Therefore, the current findings should be interpreted as reflecting the overall effects of bamboo landscape settings under the tested visual conditions. Furthermore, the selected images represented only a limited range of bamboo landscapes from urban parks in Fuzhou, China, which may constrain the representativeness of the stimuli. Future studies could employ simulated or computer-generated environments to achieve greater experimental control over visual landscape characteristics while incorporating a broader range of bamboo landscapes across different regions. Third, this study focused exclusively on visual perception and did not incorporate other sensory modalities. Given that human-environment interactions are inherently multisensory, future research should examine the combined effects of visual, auditory, and tactile stimuli to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the stress-reducing potential of bamboo landscapes.

Conclusions

This study investigated the stress-recovery effects of visual exposure to bamboo landscapes in urban parks in comparison with an urban block landscape. By integrating physiological indicators and psychological measures, this study provides a comprehensive assessment of stress-related responses across different landscape types.

The key findings are summarized as follows. First, visual exposure to bamboo landscapes in urban parks was associated with clear stress reduction, as reflected in decreased SCL, LF/HF ratio, and blood pressure, along with increased HF and improved mood states, whereas the urban block landscape demonstrated opposite trends. Second, among bamboo landscape types, those incorporating natural elements—particularly water (B5) and plant (B6)—showed significantly greater stress-reduction benefits than landscapes integrating built components, such as pavement (B4) and environmental furniture (B3), with statistically significant differences observed in both physiological indicators (e.g., SCL and HF; P < 0.05) and psychological measures (e.g., TMD; P < 0.05). Notably, no significant differences emerged between bamboo–water (B5), bamboo–plant (B6), and bamboo–architecture (B2) landscape. Similarly, bamboo–terrain (B1) landscape did not differ significantly from those incorporating built elements. These results suggest that different natural elements may vary in their associations with stress reduction, and that specific landscape configurations may influence stress-related responses.

Overall, these findings offer evidence-based insights for urban park design. The incorporation of natural elements—especially water and plant—may be prioritized to enhance stress-reduction benefits, while excessive enclosure and limited visibility should be avoided, as they may reduce perceived safety. Furthermore, the comparable performance of bamboo–architecture landscape indicates that culturally meaningful design can be successfully integrated with health-oriented landscape strategies. Collectively, these results suggest that bamboo landscapes may serve as a potentially effective nature-based approach for reducing stress and promoting positive emotional states, with important implications for urban public health and green-space planning.
