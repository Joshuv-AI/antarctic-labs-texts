Revisiting Randomized Smoothing: Nonsmooth Nonconvex Optimization Beyond Global Lipschitz Continuity
Jingfan Xia, Zhenwei Lin, Qi Deng

Abstract
Randomized smoothing is a widely adopted technique for optimizing nonsmooth objective functions. However, its efficiency analysis typically relies on global Lipschitz continuity, a condition rarely met in practical applications. To address this limitation, we introduce a new subgradient growth condition that naturally encompasses a wide range of locally Lipschitz functions, with the classical global Lipschitz function as a special case. Under this milder condition, we prove that randomized smoothing yields a differentiable function that satisfies certain generalized smoothness properties. To optimize such functions, we propose novel randomized smoothing gradient algorithms that, with high probability, converge to ( δ , ϵ ) (\delta,\epsilon) -Goldstein stationary points and achieve a sample complexity of 𝒪 ~ ​ ( d 5 / 2 ​ δ − 1 ​ ϵ − 4 ) \tilde{\mathcal{O}}(d^{5/2}\delta^{-1}\epsilon^{-4}) . By incorporating variance reduction techniques, we further improve the sample complexity to 𝒪 ~ ​ ( d 3 / 2 ​ δ − 1 ​ ϵ − 3 ) \tilde{\mathcal{O}}(d^{3/2}\delta^{-1}\epsilon^{-3}) , matching the optimal ϵ \epsilon -bound under the global Lipschitz assumption, up to a logarithmic factor. Experimental results validate the effectiveness of our proposed algorithms.



1
Introduction

In this paper, we consider the following optimization problem

\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\quad f(\mathbf{x}),

(1.1)

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is a locally Lipschitz continuous function. Nonsmooth nonconvex problems formulated as (
1.1
) have been prevalent in the machine learning and engineering areas
(
Jain et al. 2017
;
Cui and Pang 2021
)
.
We assume that first-order information is unavailable. Instead, we can only query a zeroth-order oracle that returns function values.
This is motivated by numerous real-world applications where computing the exact (sub)gradient is difficult or even intractable, such as adversarial attacks on learning models
(
Chen et al. 2017
)
, simulation modeling
(
Rubinstein and Kroese 2016
)
, and fine-tuning large language models
(
Malladi et al. 2023
)
.

Randomized smoothing
(
Nesterov and Spokoiny 2017
)
has emerged as a promising approach for solving our target problem. The key idea is to replace the standard (sub)gradient of
f(\mathbf{x})
with an unbiased estimate derived from function values at randomly perturbed points. This estimate corresponds to the gradient of a smooth approximation of
f(\mathbf{x})
via convolution operators.
Compared to classical derivative-free and black-box optimization methods
(
Larson et al. 2019
;
Nelder and Mead 1965
)
, randomized smoothing is more scalable to high-dimensional data and offers stronger theoretical convergence guarantees. A substantial body of work has explored the development of randomized smoothing methods for convex, nonconvex, and stochastic optimization
(
Nesterov and Spokoiny 2017
;
Ghadimi and Lan 2013
;
Duchi et al. 2012
;
Balasubramanian and Ghadimi 2022
;
Lin et al. 2022
)
.
Despite this progress, most existing analyses rely heavily on the global Lipschitz property, which assumes the existence of a constant
L_{f}>0
such that

|f(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{y})|\leq L_{f}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|

(1.2)

holds for any
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
While this assumption simplifies convergence analysis, it is often unrealistic in practice. For instance, piecewise nonlinear functions (e.g., quadratic functions) may have unbounded subgradient norms, thereby violating (
1.2
).

This raises a natural question:
Can we develop randomized smoothing algorithms for general nonsmooth, nonconvex problems when global Lipschitz continuity does not hold?

Addressing this question introduces two primary challenges: First, we must appropriately model the local continuity properties of
f(\mathbf{x})
and understand how they affect the randomized smooth approximation functions. Second, we need to design efficient algorithms capable of solving the smooth approximation problem under these more relaxed assumptions.

In this paper, we aim to address these two difficulties and thereafter summarize our main contributions as follows.

First, we introduce a subgradient growth condition that implies a generalized form of Lipschitz continuity, where the parameter can vary as a general function of the variable. Our condition is strictly weaker than global Lipschitz continuity, a widely used setting in nonsmooth nonconvex optimization. Furthermore, we demonstrate that applying randomized smoothing to a generalized Lipschitz continuous function yields a differentiable function that exhibits certain generalized smoothness properties. This provides the foundation for developing gradient-free algorithms and conducting complexity analysis.

Second, we propose and analyze several gradient-free algorithms based on randomized smoothing, specifically designed for optimizing the proposed generalized Lipschitz continuous functions.
In the Randomized Smoothing-based Gradient-Free (RS-GF) method, we introduce a step size that adapts to local smoothness and noise levels. We show that this algorithm achieves a convergence rate of
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(d^{5/2}\delta^{-1}\epsilon^{-4})
for reaching
(\delta,\epsilon)
-Goldstein stationary point.
We further propose a Randomized Smoothing-based batched Normalized Gradient-Free (RS-NGF) method, which employs a normalized stepsize to improve the complexity dependence on
d
.
Finally, using variance reduction techniques, we develop the Randomized Smoothing-based Normalized Variance Reduction Gradient-Free (RS-NVRGF) method, which, with high probability, achieves a
(\delta,\epsilon)
-Goldstein stationary point with a complexity of
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(d^{3/2}\delta^{-1}\epsilon^{-3})
. This result on the
\delta,\epsilon
term, is nearly optimal for nonsmooth, nonconvex problems with global Lipschitz continuity, differing only by logarithmic factors from the best-known complexity bounds.

Related works

Randomized smoothing

The efficiency of randomized smoothing-based gradient methods have been extensively studied over the past decade
(
Nesterov and Spokoiny 2017
;
Duchi et al. 2012
;
Flaxman et al. 2005
;
Ghadimi and Lan 2013
)
, primarily focusing on two cases: (1) convex optimization and (2) nonconvex but smooth optimization problems. For a comprehensive review, we refer the readers to
Liu et al. 2020
.
The extension of randomized smoothing to more general Lipschitz continuous functions has been explored in recent work
(
Lin et al. 2022
;
Cui et al. 2023
)
.
Specifically,
Lin et al. 2022
demonstrated that, with spherical smoothing, a
(\delta,\varepsilon)
-Goldstein stationary point can be obtained, using at most
\mathcal{O}(d^{3/2}L_{f}^{4}\varepsilon^{-4}+d^{3/2}\Delta L_{f}^{3}\delta^{-1}\varepsilon^{-4})
calls of the zeroth-order oracle. Subsequently,
Chen et al. 2023a
employed variance reduction techniques to further improve the complexity to
\mathcal{O}(d^{3/2}L_{f}^{3}\varepsilon^{-3}+d^{3/2}\Delta L_{f}^{2}\delta^{-1}\varepsilon^{-3})
. In the later work,
Kornowski and Shamir 2023
further reduced dimensionality dependence from
\mathcal{O}(d^{3/2})
to
\mathcal{O}(d)
, bringing the complexity bound in line with results for smooth or convex optimization.

Local Lipschitz continuity
: The challenge of handling functions that lack global Lipschitz continuity has been primarily addressed in the context of convex optimization
(
Grimmer 2019
;
Lu 2019
;
Mai and Johansson 2021
)
or nonconvex optimization under certain regularity conditions, such as weak convexity
(
Zhu et al. 2023
;
Gao and Deng 2024
)
. Typically, these works assume that the function or the norm of its subgradient satisfies specific growth conditions.
Closely related to our work,
Lei et al. 2024
focus on the case where the growth condition of the subgradient follows a polynomial function under the quadratic growth condition. With Gaussian smoothing, they demonstrate the convergence rate to achieve the Goldstein stationary point in expectation, whereas we focus on the high-probability bound.

Other related works

The technique of gradient normalization and variance reduction has been playing an important role in our algorithms. Most work on gradient normalization
has been focusing on the smooth problem or generalized smooth problem
(
Cutkosky and Mehta 2020
;
Chen et al. 2023b
)
.
Very recently,
Hübler et al. 2024
extended normalized stochastic gradient descent (NSGD) to problems with heavy-tailed noise, eliminating the need for clipping, and provided a convergence analysis in both expectation and high probability. Additionally,
Liu and Zhou 2024
;
Sun et al. 2024
also demonstrated that NSGD can effectively handle heavy-tailed noise and the generalized smooth setting without requiring clipping.

Variance reduction techniques are widely used to improve the convergence rate by reducing the variance in gradient estimates. While
Allen-Zhu and Hazan 2016
first introduced the variance reduction technique to nonconvex optimization,
Fang et al. 2018
;
Zhou et al. 2020
improve the convergence rate by sampling a large batch periodically.
Cutkosky and Orabona 2019
proposed a momentum-based variance reduction technique, which avoids generating a very large batch of samples. While most of the work on variance reduction has considered the case where the objective function is smooth,
Reisizadeh et al. 2023
tailored clipping and variance reduction techniques under the generalized smooth condition, achieving order-optimal complexity.

Organization

The paper is organized as follows.
In Section 2, we present our new concept of generalized Lipschitz continuity and analyze the properties of smooth approximation. Section 3 describes three proposed algorithms along with their convergence properties. Section 4 provides numerical results to validate our approach. Finally, Section 5 concludes the paper and discusses potential directions for future work.

\mathbf{x}

\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
to denote the sphere of

\mathbb{X}
.

\operatorname{Vol}

The Clarke subdifferential

\partial f(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\operatorname{conv}\{\lim\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k}):\mathbf{x}_{k}\rightarrow\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{k}\notin S,\mathbf{x}_{k}\in D\}

.

2
Generalized Lipschitz continuity

A globally Lipschitz‑continuous function enjoys a uniform bound on the norm of every subgradient. For non-Lipschitz functions, it is therefore natural to describe their local Lipschitz continuity through a local subgradient growth condition.
Thus, we begin with a class of functions for which the upper bound and variation of the subgradient with respect to can be characterized as follows.

Let
\alpha:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be continuous, and let
\beta:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be continuous and assume
\beta(\mathbf{x},r)
to be non‑decreasing in
r
.
The continuous function
f(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies an
(\alpha,\beta)
subgradient growth condition if

\sup_{\zeta\in\partial f(\mathbf{x})}\|\zeta\|\leq\alpha(\mathbf{x}),\quad\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

and the variation of
\alpha(\cdot)
can be bounded by

|\alpha(\mathbf{x})-\alpha(\mathbf{y})|\leq\beta(\mathbf{x},\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|),\quad\forall\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(2.1)

The first condition provides a general local subgradient upper bound controlled by
\alpha(\mathbf{x})
, while the second condition controls the variation between the subgradients using the distance between points. Putting them together, we give the local Lipschitz continuity of
f(\mathbf{x})
in the following lemma. The proof can be found in Appendix
C
.

Lemma 1
.

Suppose that
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}

has an
(\alpha
,
\beta
) subgradient growth condition. Then, we have

\|f(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{y})\|\leq(\alpha(\mathbf{x})+\beta(\mathbf{x},\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|))\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|.

Remark 1
.

Although we show that the subgradient growth condition implies generalized Lipschitz continuity, one may first start from a generalized Lipschitz assumption and derive the corresponding subgradient bounds. See Appendix
B
for more discussion.
We emphasize the subgradient upper‑bound formulation here because it is easy to estimate from first‑order information, which will facilitate a cleaner characterization of the gradient‑Lipschitz constants of the smoothed objective.

We now present several examples to illustrate that the proposed subgradient growth condition can accommodate a wide range of important functions.

Example 1
.

If
f(\mathbf{x})
is
L
-Lipschitz continuous, then it has
(\alpha,\beta)
subgradient growth condition with
\alpha(\cdot)=L
and

\beta(\cdot,\cdot)=0
.

Example 2
.

If
f(\mathbf{x})
is differentiable and its gradient
\nabla f(\mathbf{x})
is
L
-Lipschitz continuous, then
f(\mathbf{x})
has
(\alpha,\beta)
subgradient growth condition
with
\alpha(\mathbf{x})=\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\|
and

\beta(\mathbf{x},r)=Lr
.

Example 3
.

If
f(\mathbf{x})
satisfies the generalized smoothness condition
\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})-\nabla f(\mathbf{y})\|\leq\phi(\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\|,\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|)\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|
where
\phi(x,y):\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is non-decreasing on both
x
and
y
as defined in
(
Chen et al. 2023b
;
Li et al. 2024
;
Tyurin 2024
)
, then
f
has
(\alpha,\beta)
subgradient growth condition with
\text{$\alpha(\cdot)$=}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\|

and
\text{$\beta(\mathbf{x},r)$=}\phi(\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\|,r)r.

Example 4
.

If
\sup_{\zeta\in\partial f(\mathbf{x})}\|\zeta\|\leq\gamma(\|\mathbf{x}\|)

where
\gamma
is non-decreasing on
\|\mathbf{x}\|
and is finite
on any bounded set of
\|\mathbf{x}\|
, then
f
has
(\alpha,\beta)
subgradient growth condition with
\beta(\mathbf{x},r)=

|\alpha(\|\mathbf{x}\|)+\alpha(\|\mathbf{x}\|+r)|
.

Example 5
.

Consider the composite problem
f(x)=g(\mathbf{x})+h(\mathbf{x})
where
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}

has
(\alpha_{g},\beta_{g})
subgradient growth condition and
h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}

has
(\alpha_{h},\beta_{h})
-subgradient growth condition, then

f
has
(\alpha_{f},\beta_{f})
-subgradient growth condition with
\alpha_{f}(\mathbf{x})=\alpha_{h}(\mathbf{x})+\alpha_{g}(\mathbf{x})

and
\beta_{f}(\mathbf{x},r)=\beta_{g}(\mathbf{x},r)+\beta_{h}(\mathbf{x},r)
.

Non-polynomial growth

Compared to the definitions presented in existing works, our definition is more general and flexible. For instance, it encompasses all polynomial subgradient bounds (see Example
4
) that have been proposed in the literature
Zhu et al. 2023
;
Lei et al. 2024
. Furthermore, our framework extends beyond these cases to include more general, non-polynomial growth functions.
To illustrate this, consider the function
f(x)=\exp(|x|)-x^{3}
, whose subdifferential is given by:

\partial f(x)=\begin{cases}\operatornamewithlimits{sign}(x)\exp(|x|)-3x^{2}&x\neq 0,\\
{}[-1,1]&x=0.\end{cases}

In this case, let
\alpha(x)=\exp(|x|)+3x^{2}
. Clearly, the subgradient can not be bounded by a polynomial of
\|\mathbf{x}\|
. This choice satisfies the condition in Example
4
.

The composite function

Consider the composite problem

f(x)=g\circ\mathbf{\mathbf{h}}(\mathbf{x})

where
g:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}

and
\mathbf{\mathbf{h}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}

are Lipschitz continuous.
Since
f(\mathbf{x})
is not Clarke regular, the standard chain rule in subdifferential computation does not hold
(
Bagirov et al. 2014
)
.
Fortunately, by exploiting the local Lipschitzness of
g
and
\mathbf{\mathbf{h}}
, we can still apply the chain rule to estimate the local Lipschitz continuity of
f(\mathbf{x})
.

Proposition 1
.

Suppose that
g:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}

has
(\alpha_{g},\beta_{g})
subgradient growth condition and
\mathbf{\mathbf{h}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}

has
(\alpha_{\mathbf{\mathbf{h}}},\beta_{\mathbf{\mathbf{h}}})
subgradient growth condition,
then
f
has
(\alpha_{f},\beta_{f})
subgradient growth condition with
\alpha_{f}(\mathbf{x})=\alpha_{\mathbf{\mathbf{h}}}(\mathbf{x})\alpha_{g}(\mathbf{\mathbf{h}}(\mathbf{x}))

and
\beta_{f}(\mathbf{x},r)=(\alpha_{\mathbf{\mathbf{h}}}(\mathbf{x})+\beta_{\mathbf{\mathbf{h}}}(\mathbf{x},r))\beta_{g}(h(\mathbf{x}),(\alpha_{\mathbf{\mathbf{h}}}(\mathbf{x})+\beta_{\mathbf{\mathbf{h}}}(\mathbf{x},r))r)+\beta_{\mathbf{\mathbf{h}}}(\mathbf{x},r)\alpha_{g}(h(\mathbf{x}))
.

3
Randomized smoothing

Randomized smoothing uses a convolution kernel to construct a smooth approximation of the original nonsmooth function. We consider spherical smoothing:

f_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}\sim\mathbf{U}(\mathbb{\mathbb{B}}_{1}(0))}f(\mathbf{x}+\delta\mathbf{w}).

(3.1)

When
f(\mathbf{x})
is globally Lipschitz continuous as in (
1.2
),
Lin et al. 2022
have shown that the smoothing function
f_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})
is globally Lipschitz smooth.
However, this result is not applicable when (
1.2
) fails to hold.
In this subsection, we will establish some useful properties, including some new generalized smoothness conditions of
f_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})
. The omitted proof can be found in the Appendix
C
.

First, we will show that the smoothed function, as well as its gradient, is well-defined. Furthermore, we will show the relationship between optimizing the surrogate function and optimizing the original function.

Lemma 2
.

Suppose that
f
has
(\alpha,\beta)
subgradient growth condition. Then
f_{\delta}
, defined by (
3.1
), is continuously differentiable,
and
\nabla f_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}}[\nabla f(\mathbf{x}+\delta\mathbf{w})]
.
Furthermore, we have
\nabla f_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})\in\partial_{\delta}f(\mathbf{x})
.

The following proposition establishes the properties of
f_{\delta}
, especially the smoothness of
f_{\delta}
.

Proposition 2
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
have
(\alpha,\beta)
subgradient growth condition, and
f_{\delta}
be given by (
3.1
). Then, we have the following results:

1.
\left|f_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{x})\right|\leq\delta\left(\alpha(\mathbf{x})+\beta(\mathbf{x},\delta)\right)
.

2. The function
f_{\delta}
has
(\tilde{\alpha},\tilde{\beta})
subgradient growth condition with
\tilde{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})=\alpha(\mathbf{x})+\beta(\mathbf{x},\delta)
and
\tilde{\beta}(\mathbf{x},r)=\beta(\mathbf{x},r+\delta)
. Furthermore,

\left|f_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})-f_{\delta}(\mathbf{y})\right|\leq\left(\alpha(\mathbf{x})+\beta(\mathbf{x},\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|+\delta)\right)\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|.

3.
f_{\delta}
is locally Lipschitz smooth, i.e.

\begin{split}&\left\|\nabla f_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})-\nabla f_{\delta}(\mathbf{y})\right\|\\
&\leq\frac{c\sqrt{d}}{2\delta}\Big(2\alpha(\mathbf{x})+\beta(\mathbf{x},\delta)+\beta(\mathbf{x},\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|+\delta)\Big)\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|.\end{split}

Consequently, we have the following useful descent property.

Lemma 3
.

Under the assumptions of Proposition
2
, for any
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the following inequality holds:

f_{\delta}(\mathbf{y})\leq f_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})+\langle\nabla f_{\delta}(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\rangle+\frac{\ell(\mathbf{x},\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|)}{2}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|^{2},

(3.2)

where
\ell(\mathbf{x},r)=\frac{c\sqrt{d}(2\alpha(\mathbf{x})+\beta(\mathbf{x},\delta)+\beta(\mathbf{x},r+\delta))}{2\delta}.

Remark 2
.

Proposition
2
implies that
f_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})
satisfies some non-standard local Lipschitz smoothness. Notably, this condition also differs from the generalized smoothness (
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smoothness) framework
(
Zhang et al. 2019
;
Chen et al. 2023b
;
Li et al. 2024
;
Tyurin 2024
)
, which typically bounds the gradient Lipschitz continuity as a function of the gradient norm. In contrast, our bound depends more directly on
\mathbf{x}
, providing a different source of non-Lipschitz smoothness.
Moreover, our local smoothness condition can be seen as a computable and concrete instance of directional smoothness as studied in
(
Mishkin et al. 2024
)
, where
\ell(\mathbf{x},\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|)
serves as the directional smoothness function.

In the following part, we use the notation
\ell(\mathbf{x},r)
to show the local smoothness of
f_{\delta}
.
We always estimate the gradient of
f_{\delta}
at the point
\mathbf{x}
with a batch of i.i.d. samples
S_{B}=\{\mathbf{w}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{B}
, where
\mathbf{w}_{i}
is drawn from
\mathbf{U}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
, we estimate the gradient as

\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},S_{B})=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\frac{(F(\mathbf{x}+\delta\mathbf{w}_{i})-F(\mathbf{x}-\delta\mathbf{w}_{i}))d}{2\delta}\mathbf{w}_{i}.

(3.3)

When
B=1
, we denote it as
g(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w})
. In the Appendix
C
, we show the detailed properties of this gradient estimator.

While we primarily focus on central difference gradient estimators, it is also possible to use forward or backward difference estimators, which can reduce the number of function evaluations by nearly half. The subsequent analysis remains the same in either case. However, a key advantage of using central difference estimators is their ability to significantly mitigate the dependence of estimation variance on the dimensionality
d
(see
Shamir 2017
).

4
Algorithms and convergence analysis

In the standard complexity analysis for nonsmooth optimization, the bound on the convergence rate typically involves some Lipschitz parameters over the solution sequence.
When the Lipschitz continuity varies with
\mathbf{x}
in an unbounded domain,
a major challenge is to ensure the boundedness of
\{\mathbf{x}_{t}\}
in the trajectory.
To address this issue, it is natural to impose some relation between
f(\mathbf{x})
and the growth of
\|\mathbf{x}\|
.
Specifically, we require the following level-boundedness assumption.

Assumption 1
.

f_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})
is level-bounded, namely, for any
\nu\in\mathbb{R}
, the level set

\mathcal{L}_{\nu}=\{\mathbf{x}:f_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})\leq\nu\}
is bounded.

The level-boundedness is essential in developing iteration complexity for nonsmooth nonconvex optimization. It implies that by bounding
f_{\delta}(\cdot)
, we can control the Lipschitz terms involving
\alpha(\mathbf{x})
and
\beta(\mathbf{x},\delta)
.
Level-boundedness is satisfied in the coercive function, which exhibits high nonlinearity and hence is natural in our setting.
For example,
Lei et al. 2024
assumes a quadratic growth condition, which can be seen as a special case of Assumption
1
. Some sufficient conditions about the assumption can be found in Appendix
D
.

The RS-GF method

First, we present the randomized-smoothing-based gradient-free method (RS-GF method) in Algorithm
1
. Similar to
Lei et al. 2024
, we employ a stepsize adapted to the local Lipschitz continuity.
For brevity, we state an informal convergence result, and leave the formal theorem and its proof in Appendix
D
.

\mathbf{x}_{1}
, parameters
T\in\mathbb{N}
, and stepsize
\{\eta_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}

for

t=1,\ldots,T

do

Draw

and compute

.

Theorem 1

(Informal)
.

Under Assumption

, let
T=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(d^{\frac{5}{2}}\delta^{-1}\epsilon^{-4})
and set the stepsize

with

and

\displaystyle C_{t}=

Remark 3
.

The stepsize (

) is employed to account for local Lipschitz continuity in algorithm design.
The rationale behind this choice is to adapt dynamically to local gradient estimation errors (see Lemma

) and local smoothness variations (see Lemma

), both of which evolve with the sequence
\{\mathbf{x}_{t}\}
.

Remark 4
.

Another challenge in the convergence analysis is ensuring the boundedness of the iterates. Without this condition, the sequence
\{\mathbf{x}_{t}\}
may become unbounded, leading to a growing Lipschitz parameter and, consequently, an arbitrarily small stepsize (

To address this issue, we employ a high-probability convergence analysis introduced by

;
Chezhegov et al. 2024

is bounded, then with high probability,
f_{\delta}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
remains bounded as well. This guarantees that the entire sequence
\{\mathbf{x}_{t}\}
stays bounded with high probability.
For brevity, we defer the technical details to Appendix
D
.

6
Conclusion and future work

This paper defines a new class of functions to address the restrictive global Lipschitz continuity assumption in nonsmooth optimization. We propose three algorithms—RS-GF, RS-NGF, and RS-NVRGF, that align with optimal convergence rates for globally Lipschitz problems. Specifically, while RS-GF and RS-NGF achieve
(\delta,\epsilon)
-Goldstein stationarity with
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\delta^{-1}\epsilon^{-4}d^{\frac{5}{2}})
and
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\delta^{-1}\epsilon^{-4}d^{\frac{3}{2}})
complexity, RS-NVRGF improves the rate to
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\delta^{-1}\epsilon^{-3}d^{\frac{3}{2}})
, matching the optimal
\epsilon
-bound under the global Lipschitz assumption, up to a logarithmic factor.
In the theoretical analysis, our result relies on the assumption of knowing
\alpha
and
\beta
.
A possible direction is to design a parameter-free method.

Discussions about the definiton in Section 2: Genetalized Lipschitz continuity

Our definition begins from the growth condition of the norm of the subgradient. However, since our main contribution is to relax the global Lipschitz continuity assumption, a natural question is why don’t we start from the definition of local Lipschitz continuity, such as the one in Lemma
