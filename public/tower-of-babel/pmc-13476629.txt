Cerebral Palsy and Postoperative Outcomes After Distal Radius Fracture Repair: A Retrospective Analysis
Authors: David B Frumberg, Julian Smith-Voudouris, Alexander J Kammien, Michael J Gouzoulis, Ally Ae Lim Yang, David L Colen, Jonathan N Grauer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo identify if patients with cerebral palsy (CP) are at increased risk of postoperative complications and repeat surgery following open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) for distal radius fractures both acutely and in the long-term following their surgery.MethodsUsing the Mariner 161 PearlDiver dataset, adult patients undergoing ORIF following a DRF were identified. Patients with CP were then matched 1:4 with a non-CP cohort based on age, sex, and the Elixhauser Comorbidity Index (ECI). The incidence of 90-day postoperative adverse events and readmissions were determined and compared using univariate and multivariate analyses. The 5-year repeat surgery rate of each cohort was compared using log-rank testing.ResultsPrior to matching, there were 300,071 patients who underwent ORIF for distal radius fractures identified, of which 654 (0.2%) had CP. On multivariate analysis, the CP cohort had increase odds of all aggregated event groups: any adverse event (OR 4.06, 95% confidence interval [CI] 3.25–5.08), severe adverse event (OR 2.93, 95% CI 2.11–4.05), and minor adverse event (OR 4.32, 95% CI 3.41–5.48). Additionally, the CP cohort had increased odds of the following individual adverse events (in descending order): urinary tract infection (OR 5.32, 95% CI 4.01–7.06), myocardial infarction (OR 5.29, 95% CI 2.37–12.08), pneumonia (OR 4.15, 95% CI 2.83–6.07), sepsis (OR 4.08, 95% CI 2.41–6.90), pulmonary embolism (OR 3.73, 95% CI 1.60–8.58), transfusion (OR 3.71, 95% CI 1.59–8.57), acute kidney injury (OR 3.20, 95% CI 2.08–4.93), and deep vein thrombosis (OR 3.18, 95% CI 1.78–5.67).There was no difference in reoperation rate between the CP and non-CP cohort (2.3% vs 2.8%, P = 0.60).ConclusionsPatients with CP who underwent ORIF were at increase odds of postoperative adverse events immediately following surgery bur had no difference in reoperation rate over 5 years.Type of study/level of evidenceRetrospective cohort study III.Key words: Cerebral palsy, Complications, Database, Distal radius fracture, Open reduction internal fixation

Cerebral palsy (CP) is one of the most common neurodevelopmental disorders in the United States with a prevalence of 1.5 to 3.0 per live births.1 Patients with CP commonly have motor dysfunction, with some presenting with spastic upper extremity leading to significant posturing. With the patient population with CP in the United States aging overall, a larger portion of the population are at risk of fragility injuries, such as distal radius fractures and, thus, open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF).2,3

Distal radius fractures are one of the most common fractures in the United States, with 600,000 a year.4 Overall, there is often inconclusive evidence of how to best manage these fractures despite how common they are.3,5,6 ORIF has been becoming more common form of treatment, and it has been shown to have a low incidence of complications.3,7, 8, 9 However, to date, there have been no studies investigating how patients with CP perform following this increasingly common surgical intervention.

The present study aims to use a large national dataset to determine rates of 90-day postoperative adverse events and 5-year repeat surgery rates in patients with CP undergoing ORIF for distal radius fractures. It was hypothesized that patients with CP would demonstrate significantly higher rates of postoperative adverse events in the 90-day period following ORIF compared to matched controls. Additionally, it was hypothesized that long-term reoperation rates over 5 years would be worse for the CP cohort.

Materials and Methods

Patient cohort

The present study used the 2010 to Q1 2022 M161Ortho PearlDiver Mariner Patient Claims Database (PearlDiver Technologies), a large multi-insurance national administrative claims database that contains both inpatient and outpatient information from across the United States. This dataset has frequently been used for the hand literature.10, 11, 12 Our Institutional Review Board (IRB) has exempted studies using this dataset from review as all data from it is obtained in a deidentified/aggregated form. This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

All patients who underwent ORIF for their distal radius fracture were identified using the following Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes: CPT-25607, CPT-25608, and CPT-25609. Patients were excluded from the study if they had any other fracture proximal to the distal radius to ensure a homogenous patient cohort and exclude polytrauma patients. Other exclusion criteria included patients who were 18 years of age or younger, if they did not have 90 days of follow-up, or those who presented with concurrent neoplasm or infection.

All patient characteristics were tabulated and separated based on whether the patients had a diagnosis of cerebral palsy or did not. The following characteristic variables was included for analysis: patient’s age, sex, and the Elixhauser Comorbidity Index (ECI). The ECI is a common index used in the orthopedic literature to determine the relative comorbidity burden of a patient, and prior work has demonstrated summary comorbidity measures such as ECI perform comparably to adjustment by individual component comorbidities in statistical models.13, 14, 15

Patients with CP were identified using International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-9/10 codes. Patients were divided into two groups based on presence of CP diagnosis versus patients without CP. Patients with CP who underwent ORIF were matched to those who did not have CP at a ratio of 1:4 based on age, sex, and ECI.

Ninety-day postoperative outcomes

Ninety-day postoperative outcomes were identified using ICD codes (Table S1, available online on the Journal’s website at https://www.jhsgo.org). All adverse events were determined and analyzed independently, as well as aggregated into the following groups: any adverse events (AAEs), severe adverse events (SAEs), and minor adverse events (MAEs).

AAEs included all adverse events analyzed in this study. SAEs included surgical site infection, sepsis, deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, myocardial infarction, and cardiac arrest. MAEs included wound dehiscence, pneumonia, urinary tract infection, acute kidney injury, transfusion, and hematoma. Additionally, the incidence of readmissions and ED visits within 90 days following surgery was also analyzed.

The incidence and timing of return to the operating room over 5 years was tracked using CPT codes following the primary index surgery.

Statistical analysis

Univariate analysis of the patient characteristics (age, sex, and ECI) was performed comparing both the nonmatched cohort and the matched (Table 1). Age and ECI were compared using a Welch t test, while sex was compared using a chi-square test. All 90-day postoperative events were also compared using univariate analysis with a chi-square test.

Multivariate logistical regression was also performed to determine odds ratios (OR) for all 90-day postoperative outcomes. The multivariate logistical regression controlled for diagnosis of CP, age, sex, and ECI.

The incidence of return to the operating room was tracked and the matched groups were then compared using a Kaplan–Meier survival curve over the course of 5 years. A log rank (Mantel–Cox) test was used to determine significance between the two groups.

All statistical analysis was performed using the R statistical software in the PearlDiver Mariner Database (PearlDiver Technologies). All figures were created using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software). Significance for analyses of adverse events was defined as P < .003 after Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Significance for reoperation analyses was defined as P < .05.

Results

Patient cohort

There was a total of 300,071 DRF patients who underwent ORIF identified, of which 654 (0.2%) were noted to have cerebral palsy. Patients with cerebral palsy were younger (55.3 vs 59.9 years of age), less likely to be women (77.7% vs 79.4%), and had a higher comorbidity burden (7.5 vs 3.5 ECI; P < .001 for each). After matching, there were no significant differences among age, sex, or ECI between the matched cohorts.

Postoperative adverse events

The incidence of 90-day postoperative adverse events are shown in Table 2. Compared to the non-CP cohort, patients with CP had a significantly higher incidence of AAEs (30.6% vs 10.7%), SAEs (10.9% vs 4.2%), MAEs (26.6% vs 8.5%), and ED visits (39.0% vs 16.2%; P < 0.05 for each).

Five-year repeat surgery

Over 5 years, there were 62 patients in the non-CP cohort who underwent reoperation (2.8%) compared to 10 patients in the CP cohort who underwent reoperation (2.3%). There were no significant differences over the 5 years in rate of reoperation (P = .60; Fig. 1).

Discussion

Distal radius fractures remain one of the most common fractures in the United States, most often caused by a fall. ORIF for distal radius fractures is a common procedure performed in adults most often in their fifth decade or older.16 With improved care, patients with CP have been living significantly longer, making fragility injuries more relevant for this cohort.2 The present study looked at a matched cohort comparison to determine how short- and long-term postoperative outcomes vary between non-CP and CP cohorts. Of note, prior to matching, patients with CP did have a significantly higher comorbidity burden (7.5 vs 3.5 ECI), suggesting a significantly higher prevalence of chronic conditions in this cohort. The prior literature supports that patients with CP often have a significantly higher rate of chronic comorbidities.17,18 As a result, these individual comorbidities would need to be treated appropriately; however, the study matched the two cohorts to minimize the confounding effect that this might have.

Overall, patients with CP experienced a significantly higher rate of complications within the 90 days following surgery compared to the general cohort. There was an over four-fold increased odds of any adverse events, a nearly three-fold increased odds of a severe adverse event, and over four-fold increased odds of a minor adverse event.

Amongst severe adverse events, there were significantly increased odds of sepsis (OR 4.08). This can likely be attributed to the increased odds of other localized infections, such as pneumonia and urinary tract infections, which can in some cases lead to a systemic infection. A pertinent negative is that these patients were not at an increased risk of surgical site infection (P = 0.07), which has similarly been shown in other orthopedic procedures, such as total knee arthroplasty (TKA).19,20 The CP cohort did have significantly increase odds of several cardiovascular events (deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, and myocardial infarction). A possible explanation for this is due to lack of mobility following surgery because of CP. Patients with more severe CP might be particularly at risk because of their motor limitations; and the prior literature has suggested that increasing Gross Motor Functional Classification System (GMFCS) level, a measure of disease severity, might be independently associated with increased complications such as pulmonary embolism.21 Additionally, other retrospective database analyses have demonstrated that adults with CP carry a substantially elevated burden of postfracture cardiovascular disease and mortality compared to adults without CP, likely reflecting the chronic disease burden in this vulnerable population.22

Amongst minor adverse events, there was a significantly higher incidence of urinary tract infections (OR, 5.32) and pneumonia (OR, 4.15). Increased incidence of urinary tract infections in this cohort could be attributed to neurogenic bladder and urinary incontinence in this population, which has been attributed to increasing one’s risk.23, 24, 25 Similarly, the increased odds of acute kidney injury (OR, 3.20) is at least partially due to the elevated odds of urinary tract infections. The increased odds of pneumonia is likely due to respiratory and swallowing dysfunction present in some degree of the CP cohort.26 Swallowing dysfunction in particular is a known significant risk factor for developing aspiration pneumonia in the postoperative period, and postfracture pneumonia has been associated with a nearly six-fold increase in 30-day mortality among adults with CP.27,28 Lastly, the CP cohort also received significantly more transfusions during their surgery and in the postoperative period (OR, 3.71). The prior literature has suggested that patients with CP often require increased red blood cell transfusions, possibly to being more complex surgeries with varied musculoskeletal deformities.29

Overall, the CP cohort made significantly higher usage of the ED (OR, 3.61) immediately following their surgery; however, they were not readmitted at a higher rate than the non-CP cohort (P = 0.31). This finding may in part reflect a methodological limitation of the PearlDiver database as readmission is identified by the presence of an inpatient procedure code, rather than by a discrete admission event. Therefore, hospitalizations for observation for example may not generate an inpatient procedural code, and thus may not be captured. As a result, the elevated rate of ED visits in the CP population is likely a more accurate measurement of post-operative healthcare utilization.

Although the previous literature examining upper extremity orthopedic procedures in this population is sparse, the current study’s findings are consistent with that reported following other surgeries in adults with CP. A recent PearlDiver study found CP patients following TKA were at substantially increased odds of pneumonia, urinary tract infection, transfusion, and ED utilization.30 This observed consistency across different orthopedic procedures suggests that the elevated perioperative risk in CP patients may be driven less by procedure-specific factors, and more so by systemic vulnerabilities inherent to adults with CP.

In our matched comparison, there was no significant difference in 5-year reoperation rate between the non-CP and CP cohort (97.2% vs 97.7%, P = .60). Overall, our low incidence of reoperations across both cohorts is in line with the rest of literature examining return to the operating room following this procedure.7,9 Despite the significantly elevated medical complications described above, overall, patients with CP do not appear predisposed to requiring further surgical intervention. Although there is a lack of other literature examining ORIF in this cohort, the reoperation rate following other orthopedic procedures has been studied, including TKA, and patients with CP consistently have similar outcomes compared to their non-CP counterparts.30

The current study’s findings has several implications for the perioperative management of DRF ORIF in patients with CP. Given the disproportionate risk of cardiopulmonary and infectious complications, preoperative evaluation should include pulmonary function testing, assessment of swallowing and aspiration risk, and assessment of urinary tract function with multidisciplinary input from primary care, pulmonology, or rehabilitation medicine.31 Furthermore, as up to 38% of patients with CP have malnutrition, nutritional assessments and optimization should be prioritized in the perioperative period to reduce surgical complications.32,33 Additionally, given the elevated odds of thromboembolism in the present study, individualized VTE risk assessment using the Caprini score, with consideration of mechanical or chemical prophylaxis, is warranted.34 This is particularly in patients with limited baseline mobility as the background rate of VTE has been shown to be approximately two-fold higher in CP patients compared to the general population.35 Postoperatively, the finding of an over three-fold increase in ED utilization suggests that patients and their caregivers are likely to encounter unmet needs during the postoperative period. Proactive discharge planning with early follow-up and access to outpatient rehabilitation should be emphasized to reduce postoperative complications and unplanned health care utilization.

This study is not without its limitations. It is reliant on retrospective data and thus cannot draw causative conclusions. More granular clinical variables that may meaningfully influence perioperative outcomes such as fracture characteristics (comminution, articular involvement), fixation strategy, and functional or patient-reported outcomes are not captured by coding systems and could not be incorporated into the analysis. Relatedly, injury severity, such as the distinction between open and closed fractures was not adjusted for. Additionally, there are no ICD-10 codes for GMFCS level, therefore any analysis or stratification by level of motor impairment was precluded from the study. Prior work has demonstrated GMFCS level to be independently associated with several complications observed here, including pulmonary embolism.21 The odds ratios reported in the current study are therefore population level averages for all GMFCS levels. It is likely then that lower GMFCS levels carry similar risks to the general population, whereas GMFCS IV or V are more strongly associated with adverse events than reported. Providers should incorporate GMFCS status when counseling individual patients. This poses an important question for further study, as future work should incorporate GMFCS levels to better refine estimates of risk. Geographic typing to determine if the fracture occurred on an affected side was also not possible. The PearlDiver database reports ECI only as an aggregate score and does not provide a breakdown of individual component comorbidities, precluding direct comparison of each comorbidity between the matched cohorts. Although balance across the aggregate ECI was achieved, residual imbalance in specific comorbidities cannot be excluded.

Overall, this national cohort examined 631 patients with CP following ORIF for DRF and found significantly worse medical complications and more ED visits, but no difference in surgical complications or additional surgery within 5 years following their surgery. The immediate postoperative period boar’s special consideration for surgical planning to minimize these medical complications; however, patients with CP should have no other additional complication over the general populace.
