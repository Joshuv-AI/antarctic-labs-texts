Hemoglobin concentration sensing via a high-Q-factor Q-BIC-excited heterogeneous dielectric terahertz metasurface biosensor
Authors: Lingyu Liu, Zhongmin Wang, Weilin Shi, Guozhong Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryA high quality factor (Q factor) heterogeneous all-dielectric terahertz metasurface biosensor, excited by quasi-bound states in the continuum (Q-BIC), was designed and fabricated, and its application for high-sensitivity detection of hemoglobin solutions was demonstrated. Benefiting from the inherently low ohmic losses of dielectric materials, the experimentally measured Q factor of this all-dielectric metasurface biosensor reached 134, revealing a significantly high Q factor (Q >100). In practical applications for detecting hemoglobin solutions, the tri-band all-dielectric terahertz metasurface exhibited a notable redshift phenomenon and showed linear response characteristics relative to changes in concentration. Moreover, this study experimentally verifies the strong localized field confinement capability of bound states in the continuum (BIC). Compared with the non-Q-BIC-governed resonances f1 and f3, the Q-BIC-driven resonance f2 demonstrated significantly enhanced sensing sensitivity. This superior sensing sensitivity of Q-BIC, stemming from its strong field confinement, provides an important reference for the application of Q-BIC-based metasurface biosensing.Subject areas: sensor, physics, biomolecules, electrical engineering, nanotechnology fabrication, photonics, metamaterials

Introduction

Hemoglobin is a protein found in red blood cells that is responsible for transporting oxygen. Measuring it is vital for diagnosing anemia and related disorders. Currently, the main methods employed for hemoglobin assays in clinical laboratories include the hemoglobin cyanide method (HiCN), an internationally standardized approach with high precision that, however, relies on highly toxic potassium cyanide1; the sodium lauryl sulfate hemoglobin method, a nontoxic, easy-to-perform approach that, however, requires calibration curves2; the hemoglobin azide method, this method offers reduced toxicity compared with HiCN while still presenting environmental disposal challenges3; and the alkaline hematin method (AHD575), which is a safe alternative but has limited applicability owing to its wavelength incompatibility.4 Metasurfaces are a type of artificial material that allow for the arbitrary manipulation of light through the spatial arrangement of subwavelength-scale units‌. One prominent application area for metasurfaces is biosensing, which is characterized by label-free and reagent-free operation, rapid response, and high sensitivity.5 When combined with terahertz waves, known for their low photon energy,6,7 this approach offers a safe and feasible approach for the rapid and precise detection of hemoglobin concentration.

The quality factor (Q factor) is a crucial performance metric for metasurface biosensors, as it directly affects their sensitivity and detection accuracy. Metasurfaces are mainly categorized into two types: metallic and dielectric, based on the materials used. Metallic metasurfaces suffer from inherent ohmic dissipation, which results in significant energy loss and low Q factors, which limit their applicability in biosensing scenarios.8 In the early stages of research, the Q factor of metallic terahertz metasurface biosensors remained relatively low. As reported by Gupta et al.9 and Zhang et al.,10 the Q factors of their respective devices reached 9.6 and 15.9. However, a major advance came in 2023 when Liu et al. introduced a quasi-bound state in the continuum (Q-BIC)-enabled terahertz metasurface biosensor for cysteine detection. They successfully achieved an experimental Q factor of 58.11 To enhance the Q factor of metallic metasurfaces, researchers have proposed various innovative strategies: transitioning resonant modes from quasi-isolated localized resonances to extended resonances involving strong interaction among neighboring structure metaunits. This tailors the resonant modes from local to nonlocal regimes, significantly improving the Q factor of metallic metasurfaces.12 Another study designed an anisotropic metasurface composed of arrays of vertically oriented double-pillar meta-molecules, covered by a thin layer of gold. By exciting the Q-BIC resonances near the perfect absorption regime, the optimal coupling condition is achieved, enabling high-Q resonances in metallic metasurfaces.13 In 2025, Basharin et al. developed a dual-layer free-standing metallic metamaterial structure, which ingeniously minimized ohmic dissipation through optimized structural design. In the sub-terahertz range, this structure achieved an astonishing Q factor of up to 3,570. This groundbreaking result shattered the previous perception that metallic metamaterials could only achieve low Q factors.14

Dielectric metasurfaces, owing to their extremely low free carrier concentration, effectively suppress ohmic losses while maintaining strong electromagnetic field localization, thereby enabling high Q factors and establishing themselves as an ideal platform for biosensing applications. In simulation studies, the lithium tantalate (LiTaO3) tetramer structure on a quartz substrate achieved a Q factor of 105 (Wang et al., 202115), while the silicon-based BIC structure attained a Q factor of 3,250 (Xue et al., 202316). An increasing variety of terahertz all-dielectric metasurface biosensors has been extensively designed and simulated in recent years, further demonstrating the great potential of dielectric platforms in achieving ultrahigh Q factors.15,16,17,18,19,20,21,22

Experimental studies of dielectric metasurface biosensors in the terahertz regime remain scarce due to formidable fabrication challenges, particularly on low-refractive-index substrates.23 Although experimental Q factors of 1,049 have been achieved in all-silicon metasurfaces (Wang et al., 2022),24 and BIC modes on a glass (BF33) substrate reach Q = 371 (Sun et al., 2025),25 neither of these metasurfaces was specifically designed for sensing applications and thus lacked the corresponding sensing analysis. In terms of experimentally studied for sensing applications, the all-silicon metasurfaces and glass (BF33)-substrate/silicon-structural-layer dielectric metasurface, exhibited the measured Q factors of 40 and 35, respectively.26,27 A significant advancement was achieved in 2024 by Cui et al., who reported an all-silicon metasurface biosensor with an experimental Q factor of 272.28 Current research on metasurfaces for biosensing applications indicates that targeted experimental validation in the context of biosensing remains limited, and further investigation into high-Q biosensors and related sensing applications is still necessary.

As mentioned earlier, the experimentally verified dielectric metasurface biosensors mainly include two types: all-silicon and BF33 substrate with silicon as the structural layer. It is difficult to achieve high sensitivity using all-silicon metasurface biosensors. In contrast, a low-refractive-index substrate paired with a high-refractive-index structural layer promotes stronger interaction with light.29 For the latter type, the presence of metal ion impurities in the BF33 substrate introduces additional absorption losses, thereby reducing the Q factor of metasurfaces.30 Low-refractive-index quartz serves as an ideal substrate for dielectric metasurfaces; however, there have been no experimental verification reports of high-Q-factor all-dielectric metasurface biosensors fabricated on quartz substrates so far.

In this study, we developed a high-Q-factor heterogeneous all-dielectric terahertz metasurface biosensor by leveraging the low ohmic loss of dielectric materials and the unique ability of BIC to generate extremely sharp resonances. Metasurfaces that incorporate different dielectric materials as the substrate and structural layer are referred to as heterogeneous dielectric metasurfaces. The developed metasurface biosensor achieved an impressive experimental Q factor of up to 134. Simulations indicated a sensitivity of 361 GHz/RIU. When used for sensing hemoglobin concentration, the tri-band resonance peaks demonstrated significant redshifts, highlighting the superior sensing performance of the proposed heterogeneous all-dielectric metasurface biosensor. The limit of detection (LoD) is 0.05 mg/mL for the hemoglobin solution analysis. As a macromolecule, hemoglobin presents a complex structure and uneven charge distribution, resulting in sluggish and insufficient polarization response under an applied electric field, making synchronized polarization difficult. Additionally, its large molecular volume restricts packing density, leading to a lower concentration of polarizable units compared to small molecules. As a consequence, metasurface biosensors tend to exhibit reduced sensitivity and LoD when targeting macromolecules rather than small molecules. This study introduces the Q-BIC metasurface, leveraging its high-Q resonance to achieve efficient electromagnetic field localization at subwavelength scales. This significantly enhances the light-matter interaction and effectively amplifies the minute refractive index changes induced by hemoglobin, thus opening new pathway for macromolecular biosensing.

This article is organized into the following sections: section Results describes the designed dielectric metasurface structure and presents the results of the simulation analysis. It investigates the physical mechanisms underlying the Q-BIC resonances through multipole decomposition, analysis of the electromagnetic field distribution, and energy band theory. This section also details the fabrication procedures and key process parameters for the realized heterogeneous all-dielectric metasurface, utilizing micro/nano processing technology. Section Discussions evaluates the sensing performance of the Q-BIC-driven all-dielectric metasurface biosensor using hemoglobin solutions at various concentrations.

Results

The resonances of a dielectric metasurface are geometry-dependent, and the unit cells exhibit characteristic sizes d∼ λ0/n, where d denotes the geometric size of a unit cell, λ0 is the wavelength in free space, and n is the refractive index of the dielectric particles. By utilizing Mie theory, a high-Q-factor heterogeneous all-dielectric metasurface biosensor was developed.

Metasurface design and simulation

Figures 1A and 1B illustrate the schematics of the proposed heterogeneous all-dielectric terahertz metasurface, while Figure 1C presents an electron microscope photo of the fabricated structure. The unit cell of the heterogeneous all-dielectric metasurface consists of two rectangular silicon columns positioned on a quartz substrate, with the right column rotated to create a distortion in structural symmetry. Both silicon columns share identical geometric dimensions: a length (L) of 84 μm, a width (w) of 25 μm, and a height (t) of 120 μm. Prior to rotation, these columns exhibit mirror symmetry about both the x and y axes. The center-to-center distance between the columns in the xy plane cross-sectional projections is d=41 μm. Additionally, the periods in the x and y directions are px = 106 μm and py = 92 μm, respectively. The thickness (h) of the quartz substrate is 500 μm. By rotating the right column, the symmetry of the unit cell is distorted, which excites Q-BIC-driven resonance. This rotation occurs around an axis that is perpendicular to the xy plane and passed through the geometric center of the right column’s xy cross-section, with a specific rotation angle of 20°. The relative permittivity values are 3.75 for the quartz substrate and 11.9 for the silicon structural layer. The transmission mode was employed in the characterization and sensing experiments.

The transmission electromagnetic characteristics of the designed heterogeneous all-dielectric metasurface were analyzed using the finite element method with the CST Microwave Studio 2023. The right column of the metasurface rotates from 0° to 20° around an axis that is perpendicular to the xy plane and passing through the geometric center of its xy cross-section. The corresponding transmission spectra and their 2D maps are shown in Figures 2A and 2B, respectively. The terahertz wave ‌illuminating the all-dielectric metasurface‌ is linearly polarized along the x axis‌. As shown in Figure 2, ‌the all-dielectric metasurface unit maintains C2 symmetry at a rotation angle of 0°, allowing for ‌complete energy confinement within the resonant cavity, which is a hallmark of BIC with ‌no energy leakage. However, as the right column begins to rotation, this C2 symmetry is broken, leading to energy leakage and the emergence of two Q-BIC-driven resonance peaks in the transmission spectra. As the rotation progresses, increased structural asymmetry results in a broadening of the extremely narrow Q-BIC resonance linewidth, accompanied by a decline in their Q factors. As illustrated in Figure 2A, when the right column is rotated to an angle of 20°, the two Q-BIC mode resonances merge at a frequency of 1.261 THz, referred to as f2, while a new resonance, designated as f3, appears at 1.322 THz. The resonance peak at 1.166 THz is defined as f1. For the fabricated sample with the right column rotated to 20°, Figure 3 compares the simulated and experimental transmission spectra of the heterogeneous all-dielectric metasurface biosensor. The discrepancies between the simulated and experimental transmission spectra can be attributed to several factors, including calibration errors in silicon layer permittivity, manufacturing deviations in lateral geometric dimensions, non-uniformity in silicon thickness, approximations made during the simulation processes, and defects that may have been introduced during the micro/nanostructure fabrication. The experimental Q factors for the three resonances are measured to be 48, 61, and 134, respectively. The Q factor is defined as Q=fr/FWHM, where fr is the resonant frequency, and FWHM is the full width at half-maximum.

The interaction of terahertz waves with metasurface results in the localization and enhancement of electromagnetic energy, leading to localized field intensities that significantly surpass the incident field. This local field enhancement significantly enhances light-matter interactions, enabling the detection‌ of minute amounts of analytes. Sensitivity Sn quantifies the capability of metasurface-based biosensors to detect minor changes in the refractive index of the surrounding medium. It is defined as Sn=Δf/Δn, where Δf represents the resonant frequency shift and Δn indicates the refractive index variation. The unit of sensitivity is GHz/RIU (gigahertz per refractive index unit). If the change in solution concentration is Δc, which causes the resonance frequency shift Δf, then the sensitivity related to concentration Sc=Δf/Δc, with units of GHz/mg·L-1.

A 120-μm-thick analyte was deposited on the asymmetric dimer cluster heterogeneous all-dielectric metasurface to investigate its sensing performance across a refractive index range of 1.0–1.1 (step size, 0.1). The transmission spectra corresponding to the change in refractive index from 1 to 1.1 are shown in Figure 4A. By monitoring the frequency shifts of tri-band resonances, a linear correlation was observed between the spectral displacement and the change in refractive index (see Figure 3B). Linear fitting revealed the sensitivities of 142 GHz/RIU, 361 GHz/RIU, and 136 GHz/RIU for resonances f1, f2, and f3, respectively. Notably, f2 exhibited the highest sensitivity, demonstrating its potential for higher-precision biosensing. Furthermore, a general figure of merit (FoM) was introduced for a comprehensive performance assessment. Mathematically expressed as FoM = Sn/FWHM, this index combines both the sensor’s sensitivity and the width of its resonance response into a single quantitative value. The calculated FoM for resonances f1, f2, and f3 were 5.9, 14.4, and 13, respectively.

Physical mechanism

To investigate the fundamental physical mechanisms underlying the resonance, we analyzed the scattered field from the heterogeneous all-dielectric metasurface by decomposing it into five fundamental multipole modes within a Cartesian coordinate framework: electric dipole (ED), magnetic dipole (MD), electric quadrupole (EQ), magnetic quadrupole (MQ), and toroidal dipole (TD). These modes are recognized as the primary sources of scattering power.10,34,35,36 From Figure 5, it is clear that f1 and f2 are primarily originate from the EQ and MD, with the EQ contributing the most among the five multipoles, followed by the MD. In contrast, the contributions of the ED, TD, and MQ to scattering power are relatively minor. For resonance f3, the EQ again plays the dominant role, while the contributions from the ED, MD, MQ, and TD are comparatively limited. The total scattering power from all five fundamental multipole modes is represented by the black solid curve labeled TOT. Resonance f2 exhibits the highest total scattering power, leading to the greatest sensitivity to changes in refractive index.

Figures 6A–6C illustrate the intensity distributions of the electric field (E-field) and magnetic field (H-field) in the x-y plane at the mid-height of the columns for f1, f2, and f3, respectively. The black arrows indicate the vector directions of the electromagnetic field, while the red regions highlight areas of localized near-field enhancement. Figures 6D and 6 E illustrate the E-field and H-field intensity distributions in the x-y plane at the mid-height of the columns for BIC1 and BIC2. Figure 6 shows that the electric and magnetic field intensities of BIC1 and BIC2 are significantly weaker than those of f2. This occurs because BICs are ideal bound states whose energy is entirely confined within the continuum, preventing radiation leakage to the external environment. Consequently, at external boundaries or in the far field, their electromagnetic field intensities are drastically reduced, manifesting as weak-field characteristics. In contrast, f2 operates as a Q-BIC, which allows for partial radiation leakage‌. This radiation leakage enables the Q-BIC mode to exhibit stronger observable electric and magnetic field intensities externally.

For a deeper understanding of the properties of the heterogeneous all-dielectric metasurface, a plot of wave vector versus mode frequency (band diagram) is shown in Figure 8A. The Q-factor distribution of the BIC mode is shown in Figure 8B. Figures 8C and 8D are the 3D band structures of band 2 and band 3, respectively. The occurrence of symmetry-protected BICs is observed at the Γ point (the center of the Brillouin zone).37,38 This type of resonance mode is orthogonal to the odd-symmetric radiation mode, thus decoupling from the far-field radiation, that is, forming a BIC. As shown in Figure 8B, the Q factors of band 2 and band 3 at the Γ point reach 106, tending to infinity. This is due to the mismatch between the symmetric mode at the Γ points and any external propagation modes,39 proving that the eigenmode corresponding to the Γ point is BIC. Once the symmetry of the structure is broken, the BIC will couple with the continuum, resulting in radiation leakage, thus forming a Q-BIC. The modes are localized in the vicinity of the silicon. When in the air, the modes have imaginary k⊥, corresponding to fields that decay exponentially (are evanescent) away from the silicon. These are index-guided modes.

As symmetry-protected BICs, these non-radiative states serve as vortex centers in the polarization direction of far-field radiation. This vortex behavior stems from destructive interference of radiative channels, forming a topological singularity where the polarization vector degenerates to a null state at the point Γ (k = 0) in the k space. Vortices are characterized by their topological charges (q), a property of a BIC that is defined by the number of times the polarization vectors wind around the vortex center (BIC). Here, ϕ(k) is the angle of the polarization vector, and C is a closed simple path in k space that goes around the BIC in the counterclockwise direction. The total angular change must be an integer multiple of 2π, ensuring that q is an integer.40

Both the polarization vectors of BIC1 and BIC2, visualized in Figures 9A and 9D, traverse a counterclockwise closed loop around the Γ point in k-space. They accumulate a 2π phase change, corresponding to a topological charge q = +1. When the symmetry of the metasurface unit cell is broken, by rotating the right silicon column, the perfectly localized BIC at the Γ point transitions into a radiative state, as its previously closed radiation channels are reactivated. This symmetry breaking triggers the migration of the vortex from the Γ point to a k≠0 region, ultimately resulting in the formation of a Q-BIC, as shown in Figures 9A–9C and 9D–9F. This migration evolves continuously with increasing asymmetry and remains reversible.

Fabrication

The fabricated heterogeneous all-dielectric metasurface employed a 500 ± 10 μm quartz wafer as the substrate and a 120 ± 10 μm intrinsic silicon wafer (⟨100⟩ orientation, resistivity ≥10 kΩ cm) as the structural layer. This choice was made due to intrinsic silicon’s high refractive index and low ohmic loss in the terahertz frequency band. During the inductively coupled plasma (ICP) etching of standalone silicon wafers, both intrinsic silicon and N-type silicon demonstrate virtually identical etch rates and thermal dissipation characteristics. However, for the ICP etching of silicon wafers bonded to quartz substrates, the quartz substrate decreases the heat dissipation efficiency. Under this condition, the N-type silicon was more prone to failure in sample fabrication compared to intrinsic silicon, primarily due to inadequate heat dissipation.

The manufacturing processes involved several critical steps, including material bonding, photolithographic patterning, and ICP etching. The preparation was conducted at the Guilin University of Electronic Technology, and the fabrication flowchart is illustrated in Figure 10. The high-resistivity silicon was bonded to quartz substrate using Optical Adhesive 74 from Norland Products Inc. This bonding was followed by 8 min of ultraviolet lamp irradiation and a one-week aging period to allow the adhesive to fully cure. The photolithography process transferred the pattern from the photomask onto the photoresist-coated silicon wafer through exposure and development. The photoresist used was AZ4620, a positive-tone resist from Merck designed for thick-film lithography. The photomask acted as the master template for replicating the desired pattern. The subsequent ICP etching process precisely transformed the two-dimensional planar pattern on the masking layer into a three-dimensional silicon structure with defined depth, achieved by carefully controlling the etching time. The heterogeneous all-dielectric metasurface biosensor was successfully fabricated after the final cleaning step to remove polymer residues generated during the etching process.

The development process is critical for controlling the fine features that are printable by selectively dissolving exposed photoresist areas to form micro-nanostructures. This process is predominantly influenced by variables such as development time, development temperature, soft bake duration, exposure dose, photoresist thickness, and developer concentration.41,42 The width of the development trench (Dx) is proportional to the product of the dose (d) and the square root of the development time (t),43

Discussions

The sensing experiment was conducted on the self-built terahertz time-domain spectroscopy (THz-TDS), and its schematic diagram is shown in Figure 12. The femtosecond laser (Chameleon Ultra II, Coherent Inc., Santa Clara, California) with a pulse width of 100 fs, a repetition rate of 80 MHz, and a total output power of 1.6 W was used to generate and detect terahertz (THz) radiation. The laser beam was split into a pump beam (1.02 W) and a probe beam (13 mW). The pump beam, modulated by an 1,800 Hz chopper, was incident on a ⟨100⟩-oriented InAs crystal to generate THz radiation. The THz radiation was then detected using the electro-optic sampling technique. The linearly polarized probe beam, combined with the THz beam after passing through the sample, was incident on a ⟨110⟩-oriented ZnTe crystal, converting the polarization to elliptical. The beam then passed through a quarter-wave plate and a Wollaston prism, resulting in unequal intensities of the horizontal and vertical polarization components. The differential current detected by the differential photodetector was proportional to the electric field strength of the THz wave. The system demonstrated a dynamic range of 2,950 and a signal-to-noise ratio of 645, with a spectral coverage spanning 0.1–2 THz. The frequency resolution of THz-TDS is 7 GHz. When applying the fast Fourier transform to convert the time-domain signal into frequency-domain data, zero-padding interpolation was performed on the acquired time-domain signal to improve the frequency resolution to 0.35 GHz. To minimize water vapor interference, the relative humidity in the experimental environment was dynamically maintained at 3% through continuous dry-air purging. In the experiment, the biosensor position was fixed to ensure complete overlap between the sample deposition area and the spectral acquisition area for each measurement.

The hemoglobin used in the sensing testing was sourced from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. The step-by-step workflow for sample preparation is laid out in Figure 14. First, a solution at twice the target concentration was prepared using water as the solvent. Hemoglobin has low water solubility but dissolves in water when heated, so the solution was placed in a 50°C drying oven to ensure full dissolution. At pH 7.0, denaturation and aggregation of oxyhemoglobin occur above 55°C, while these processes are initiated above 60°C for methemoglobin. Cyanomethemoglobin, however, retains structural stability up to 60°C.44 Right before testing, an equal volume of alcohol was added to dilute the solution to the target concentration, as illustrated in Figure 14C. Next, 10 μL of hemoglobin solution was deposited onto the surface of the all-dielectric superstructure biosensor. Finally, the biosensor was placed in a 50°C drying oven to evaporate the solvent and achieve complete drying, as depicted in Figure 14D. Once fully dried, the metasurface biosensor was transferred to the THz-TDS system illustrated in Figure 12 for transmission spectrum characterization.

The Pearson correlation coefficients between the measured values and the corresponding linear-fit values are 0.83, 0.89, and 0.84, respectively, indicating a significant positive correlation and confirming that quantitative detection of hemoglobin solution concentration using the all-dielectric metasurface biosensor is accurate and reliable. The frequency shifts of the tri-band resonance demonstrate the superior sensing performance of the all-dielectric metasurface biosensor with a LoD of 0.05 mg/mL for the hemoglobin solution. The Q-BIC-driven resonance f2 exhibited the highest sensing sensitivity. This enhancement may have originated from the prolonged electromagnetic energy confinement within the Q-BIC resonant cavity, which dramatically amplified the localized light field and consequently generated strong light-matter interactions.

Table 2‌ compares the ‌performance metrics‌, specifically the ‌Q factor‌ and ‌refractive index sensitivity‌, of our proposed ‌all-dielectric metasurface biosensor‌ with those of recently reported metasurface sensors, highlighting their experimental validation status. Previous studies reported ultrahigh Q factors; however, these values originated from design simulations‌ rather than experimental measurements.16,17,45,46 References experimentally obtained Q factors as high as 1,049 and 371, but the metasurfaces were not specifically designed for sensing applications‌, and thus lacked the corresponding sensing experimental analysis.24,25 The highest experimentally validated Q factor‌ reported is ‌70.11,19,26,27,47 Lin et al. reported an all-silicon metasurface sensor with experimentally measured Q factors of 125 and 63, which was employed for L-proline sensing detection.48 After drying 10 μL of a 1 mg/mL L-proline solution, the two resonance peaks of the sensor exhibited frequency shifts of 6 GHz and 5 GHz, respectively. The sensing results are comparable to those of this study, though the Q factor achieved in this work is somewhat higher.

Performance comparison of different sensors

Current research on quartz-silicon metasurface sensors is primarily focused on design and simulation.16,17,45,46,51 While Wang et al. achieved a high experimental Q factor of 265, their study was not aimed at biosensing applications and thus lacked corresponding sensing simulation analysis and experimental validation.52 Liu et al. conducted sensing test analysis on L-proline, achieving a LoD of 0.1 mmol/L.53 In this study, through experimental validation, we not only obtained a high experimental Q factor but also completed comprehensive sensing experimental analysis, and conducted sensor detection on the biological macromolecule hemoglobin solution. Protein macromolecules have significant application value. The study achieved commendable sensing performance. Compared with the quartz-silicon metasurface sensors of references listed in Table 1, this study demonstrates higher sensing sensitivity.16,45,46,51

By utilizing the advantage of the inherent low ohmic loss of dielectric materials and the BIC concept, a heterogeneous all-dielectric metasurface biosensor with experimental high Q factors was developed. The measured Q factor reached 134, exceeding that of traditional metamaterials. When applied to the sensing of hemoglobin solution concentration, the tri-band resonance peaks exhibited significant redshifts. ‌Resonance f2, excited via Q-BIC, demonstrated superior sensitivity relative to non-Q-BIC resonances f1 and f3. The sensing experiments showed that the proposed heterogeneous all-dielectric metasurface biosensor exhibited excellent sensing performance, with a sensitivity of up to 5.4 GHz/mg·L-1. The results demonstrated that the proposed heterogeneous all-dielectric metasurface biosensor provided a safe and feasible approach for rapid, label-free, and high-precision detection of hemoglobin concentration and showcased significant application potential in medical diagnostics.

Limitations of the study

In this study, we fabricated an all-dielectric metasurface consisting of two rectangular silicon columns on a quartz substrate, with the right column rotated to distort structural symmetry. However, the discrepancy between metasurface transmission spectra simulation results and experimental data indicates that the fabrication process of the structure still requires improvement.
