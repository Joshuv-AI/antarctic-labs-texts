Integrative genomic and transcriptomic analysis to identify potential candidate genes influencing plumage color variation in mule ducks
Authors: Hongxiang Liu, Yifei Wang, Shiheng Zhou, Wei Zhou, Huiqing Zhu, Zhicheng Wang, Weitao Song, Zhiyun Tao, Wenjuan Xu, Haotian Gu, Shuangjie Zhang, Huifang Li, Chunhong Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlumage color is a distinctive and economically important trait in poultry; however, its genetic architecture in hybrid species, such as the mule duck, remains poorly characterized. Traditional mapping approaches often fail to accurately establish causal genes, particularly for traits likely governed by major-effect loci. In this study, we used an integrated multi-omics approach to obtain a deeper understanding of the genetic basis of plumage color in mule ducks. Following whole-genome resequencing of 114 mule duck individuals, a combined analysis involving genome-wide association studies and genome scans for genetic differentiation (FST/π) was conducted, which collectively highlighted the EDNRB2 genomic region. Comparative transcriptomics of black versus white feather follicles confirmed that EDNRB2 and key melanogenesis genes were significantly upregulated. This convergence of genomic and transcriptomic evidence was further confirmed by experimental validations. Quantitative polymerase chain reaction confirmed differences in EDNRB2 expression levels, while competitive allele-specific PCR (KASP) genotyping in an independent population revealed a significant association between specific upstream SNPs of EDNRB2 and plumage color scores. Our study revealed EDNRB2 as a pivotal candidate gene regulating plumage color variation in mule ducks. The multi-tiered approach, from population-level genomics to molecular validation, identified a key genetic mechanism, and also provided functionally validated KASP markers, establishing a direct foundation for further developing mule duck breeding in the poultry industry.Keywords: Mule duck, Plumage color, GWAS, RNA-seq, EDNRB2

Introduction

In traditional Fujian cuisine, Ginger Duck has been historically prepared using Muscovy duck meat. However, the slow growth rate and increasing husbandry costs of Muscovy ducks have led to a preference for using mule ducks in contemporary culinary practices. These mule ducks, produced by the hybridizing of Muscovy ducks and domestic ducks, exhibit enhanced hybrid vigor, accelerated growth rates and improved meat quality (Arroyo et al., 2017). Despite these advantages, the current production of mule ducks is predominantly characterized by mottled plumage and the presence of colored hair follicles in poultry with black or brown plumages post-slaughter, which negatively impact the visual appeal of culinary dishes prepared using these ducks (Dickens et al., 1994). Consequently, the identification of the molecular mechanisms underlying the white feather rate in mule ducks is an important research topic to ensure that the product quality meets the aesthetic preferences of market consumers.

The marketability of poultry, particularly within the context of contemporary livestock production, is heavily influenced by the visual features of the carcass, where feather pigmentation plays a pivotal role in determining consumer preference (Baéza et al., 2022; Karawita et al., 2023). Currently, at the genomic level, genome-wide association studies (GWAS) are extensively used to reveal key genes or genetic markers (Zhang et al., 2018; Guo et al., 2023). Concurrently, transcriptomic analysis is the predominant technique for identifying candidate genes through differential gene expression (Zhu et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023). Elucidation of the genetic mechanisms underlying avian plumage coloration has been substantially advanced by the analysis of the pathways and gene expression patterns associated with melanin synthesis (Yang et al., 2017; Zheng et al., 2020). Contemporary research has delineated the molecular intricacies of plumage pigmentation in animals, emphasizing the pivotal regulatory functions of melanogenesis-associated genes in determining feather color phenotypes (Xi et al., 2020a). The transition of melanocyte precursors into fully differentiated melanocytes is a multifaceted developmental process contingent upon the coordinated action of various signaling cascades and transcriptional regulators (D’Mello et al., 2016). Although current research has extensively explored the pigmentation of poultry feathers, studies assessing plumage coloration in mule ducks are still in their early stages. Despite this situation, critical discoveries have been made regarding the genetic mechanisms associated with plumage coloration in these ducks.

In the present study, we used an integrated multi-omics approach combining population genomics, transcriptomics, and experimental validation [reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)/competitive allele-specific PCR (KASP)], and aimed to identify potential key genetic regulators of plumage color in mule ducks. Thus, this study provided mechanistic insights regarding plumage coloration regulation in mule ducks, which could serve as a tool for advancing molecular breeding of these ducks.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

The experimental protocol was reviewed and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Jiangsu Institute of Poultry Sciences, and the study was conducted in strict accordance with the guidelines established by the China Council on Animal Care and the Ministry of Science and Technology of the People's Republic of China. The procedures involving animals adhered to the guidelines for the care and use of experimental animals set forth by the Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (IAS2022-103).

Animals, phenotype recording, and sample collection

All mule ducks used in this study were provided and raised by Jiangsu Guiliu Livestock Group Co., Ltd (Xuzhou City, China) under standardized commercial conditions on a single farm. Plumage color of 114 individuals was systematically quantified. The scoring system was based on black plumage distribution, wherein a weight of 1 to the head, neck and tail, and a weight of 2 to the back, abdomen, and wings (Fig. 1). Each weight was scored up to 4 points, yielding a maximum total score of 36 points. This plumage color score (PCS) served as the phenotypic value for subsequent analysis.

The presence of black feathers on the abdomen is a rare occurrence. If the abdomen is also covered with black feathers, the individual is entirely black and receives the maximum PCS of 36.

At 1 day of age, blood was drawn from the jugular vein using citrate-coated syringes during routine health checkup and used to obtain DNA. To investigate the basis of plumage color variation, 10 individuals with black dorsal feathers (PCS > 12) and 10 with no black dorsal feathers (PCS < 3) were selected for feather follicle collection from the back at 6 weeks of age. All collected tissues were rapidly immersed in liquid nitrogen to prevent degradation and preserve RNA integrity, followed by long-term storage at -80 °C until RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis.

DNA extraction, whole genome resequencing, and quality control

Genomic DNA was isolated from blood samples of all 114 mule ducks using a standard phenol-chloroform protocol (Chomczynski and Sacchi, 2006), and its integrity was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis. Sequencing libraries were prepared and subjected to whole-genome sequencing on an DNBSEQ-T7 platform to generate paired-end reads, with an average sequencing depth of 10 × .

Raw sequencing data were processed through a standardized bioinformatics pipeline. Briefly, adaptors and low-quality sequences were trimmed using Picard software (http://picard.sourceforge. net/) (version 2.28). Because mule ducks are non-reproductive hybrids derived from Muscovy ducks and domestic ducks, no species-specific reference genome is available for them. Consequently, due to the absence of a mule duck-specific reference genome and the higher quality of the domestic duck genome assembly, all clean reads from whole-genome resequencing were aligned to the domestic duck reference genome (ZJU1.0, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/GCF_015476345.1/) by using the Burrows-Wheeler alignment (BWA v0.7.17) (Li and Durbin, 2009). The resulting Sequence Alignment Map (SAM) files were converted to Binary Alignment Map (BAM) format, sorted according to coordinates, and indexed using SAMtools (v1.12) (Li et al., 2009). Genome Analysis Toolkit HaplotypeCaller was used to detect SNPs (McKenna et al., 2010).

Stringent quality control was performed using PLINK (v1.90) software (Anderson et al., 2010). We excluded SNPs and individuals with a genotype missing rate of > 5%, a SNP call rate of ≤ 90%, a minor allele frequency of < 0.05, and a significant deviation from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (P < 1 × 10−5). Sex-chromosome-related SNPs were also eliminated. Following this filtering process, a high-quality dataset from all 114 individuals was retained for further analysis.

GWAS analysis

A GWAS analysis was conducted to identify genetic variants associated with PCSs. Principal component analysis (PCA) was initially performed using plink software (v1.90) to account for population stratification, and the top principal components (PCs) were included as covariates in the subsequent GWAS analysis (Ringnér, 2008). Association testing was conducted using a mixed linear model (MLM) implemented in GEMMA (v0.98.4), which incorporated a kinship matrix to control for genetic relatedness among individuals (Kang et al., 2010; Zhou et al., 2018b). The Wald test was used to select SNPs associated with PCSs.

We used the parameters of plink software –indep-pairwise 25 5 0.2 to infer the effective independent test. Considering the over-conservation of the 5% or 1% Bonferroni correction method, we adjusted the threshold of genome-wide significant P-values based on the number of independent SNPs (Tao et al., 2020; Ding et al., 2022). The results of the GWAS were visualized using the CMplot package in R program. Linkage disequilibrium (LD) patterns surrounding significant loci were visualized and analyzed using LDBlockShow (Dong et al., 2021).

Genome scans for genetic differentiation

We used complementary population genetic approaches to map genomic regions associated with extreme plumage color. The population was divided into a high-scoring group (scores ≥ 10, n=30) and a low-scoring group (scores ≤ 3, n=30). First, genetic differentiation was assessed by calculating Wright's fixation index (FST) in 50-kb sliding windows with a 25-kb step by using VCFtools (v0.1.13) (Danecek et al., 2011). Windows with FST values in the top 1% genome-wide scans were selected and merged into candidate regions. Second, to refine the search, we performed a per-site analysis genome-wide, calculating per-site FST and nucleotide diversity (π) for each group to derive a log2πratio. We defined significance thresholds as the top 5% of per-site FST values (FST threshold) and the top/bottom 5% of log2πratio values (high and low log2πratio thresholds, respectively). Finally, we identified high-confidence candidate variants by extracting SNP sites within the EDNRB2 gene and its 5-kb flanking regions that simultaneously exceeded the FST threshold and either the high or low log2πratio threshold.

RNA extraction and sequencing

Tissue samples, including black and white plumage follicle tissue from the back of 10 mule ducks with white plumages and 10 mule ducks with black plumages, were subjected to total RNA extraction using a TRIzol kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA); the extracted RNA was then reverse transcribed to cDNA. Finally, 20 libraries were obtained for RNA-seq experiments using the Illumina Hiseq X Ten sequencing platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA) with 150-bp reads at both ends. FastQC (v0.11.9) (Andrews, 2014) was used to validate the quality of the raw sequence data for each sample. The trimmed transcriptome data were aligned to the reference genome of duck (GCF_015476345.1) using HISAT 2.1.0 (Kim et al., 2015).

Validation of candidate genes through RNA-seq and RT-qPCR

Gene expression levels were quantified and normalized. Differential expression analysis between the black and white feather groups was conducted using the DESeq2 software (v1.48.1) (Love et al., 2014). Genes with an absolute fold change of ≥ 2 and an adjusted P-value (false discovery rate, FDR) of < 0.05 (Benjamini-Hochberg method) were identified as significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was subsequently performed on the identified DEGs by using clusterProfiler package in R program (v4.16.0) (Wu et al., 2021).

To validate the transcriptomic data, nine candidate genes were selected for RT-qPCR analysis. These genes included EDNRB2, five genes from the melanogenesis pathway (TYR, KIT, TYRP1, MC1R, WNT8A), and three randomly selected genes from the GWAS candidate list (NSDHL, RAB39B, SPRY3). For each biological sample (collected from the same 20 individuals used for RNA-seq), first-strand cDNA was synthesized from total RNA by using a reverse transcription kit. Next, RT-qPCR was performed using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) on a quantitative PCR instrument. The relative expression levels of target genes were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method (Ohan and Heikkila, 1993), with stable reference genes for normalization. Statistical significance of expression differences between the black and white groups was determined using an unpaired t-test.

Validation of signals by KASP genotyping

To independently validate the GWAS findings, we performed genotyping using the KASP assay (Higentec, Changsha, China) on an additional population of 142 individuals. Based on the surrounding sequence information of the significant SNP site, specific KASP primers were designed and used as genotyping markers (Table S1).

The PCR reaction system (10.14 μL) was comprised 5 μL KASP Master Mix, 0.14 μL KASP Marker Mix, and 5 μL template DNA (50 ng/μL). The thermal cycling protocol was as follows: initial hot-start activation at 94 °C for 15 min, 10 touchdown cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 20s and annealing at 60 °C for 60s (with a decrease in the annealing temperature by 0.6 °C per cycle), followed by 26 additional cycles of denaturation 94 °C for 20s and annealing at 55 °C for 60s. The final holding stage was set at 30 °C. The amplified product was detected by a fluorescent microplate detector, and detection data were read with the SNP viewer 2.0 software (LGC Company) (Zhang et al., 2020).

Genome analysis of plumage phenotype in mule ducks

We conducted a GWAS using phenotypic data from 114 mule ducks. The genome-wide significance threshold was calculated as -log10(0.05 / 646,594) = 7.11, and the suggestive significance threshold as -log10(1 / 646,594) = 5.81. Manhattan and Q-Q plot of association results from the genome-wide association analysis of PCS are shown in Fig. 3A-B. This analysis revealed 1, 1 and 1,102 suggestive significant SNPs across chromosomes 3, 6, and 10, respectively, with a threshold for significant level at -log10(P) > 5.81 (Table S4). Notably, chromosome 10 featured 648 significant SNPs with -log10(P) values greater than 7.11 (Table S5). The estimated genomic inflation factor λ value was 0.967, suggesting no population stratification in the studied population after accounting for kinship matrix and PCA as covariates.

A total of 44 genes (Table S6) were identified as candidate genes based on their location within a 100 kb window around the significant SNPs. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were performed on these 44 genes. In the GO analysis, the “endothelin receptor signaling pathway” was the significantly enriched term in the biological process category (Table S7), with EDNRB2 being the only gene enriched in this pathway. In the KEGG analysis, the same gene, EDNRB2, was found to be enriched in the “the melanin biosynthetic pathway” (Table S8). A total of 78 SNP loci were identified within the EDNRB2 gene and its flanking regions spanning 5 kb upstream and downstream, with their specific distribution detailed in Table S9. Fig. 3C presents an LD heatmap illustrating the pairwise correlations (r2 values) among these SNPs. The heatmap showed distinct LD blocks with a high correlation surrounding the region of EDNRB2, suggesting this region may have contained co-inherited variants crucial for melanin synthesis.

Based on the sliding-window approach using vcftools, we calculated FST values across 33,518 windows (Table S10) by comparing 30 individuals with low PCSs (2.96±0.19) and 30 individuals with high PCSs (5.1±4.40). Based on the threshold of 0.15, two high-FST regions were identified on chromosome 10, both flanking the EDNRB2 gene within a 200-kb genomic window (Fig. 4A). Genome-wide scans of per-site FST and log2πratio values revealed that the genomic region near EDNRB2 contained multiple sites where both metrics exceeded the set thresholds (FST > 0.0954; log2πratio > 1.4683 or < -1.8539). Within the EDNRB2 gene and its 5 kb flanking regions, 240 SNPs exceeded the per-site FST threshold, 183 SNPs exceeded the log2πratio thresholds, and 164 SNPs simultaneously exceeded both sets of thresholds (Fig. 4B, Table S11). These findings provided additional evidence that EDNRB2 was likely a key candidate gene influencing the genetic variation underlying plumage color in mule ducks.

Combined transcriptomic analysis and RT-qPCR validation

Transcriptomic analysis comparing the black and white feather groups identified 551 DEGs comprising 232 upregulated and 319 downregulated genes (Fig. 5A, Table S12). The KEGG enrichment analysis of these DEGs revealed "Melanogenesis" among the top 10 significantly enriched pathways (Fig. 5B). This pathway included six key genes: TYR, KIT, TYRP1, MC1R, WNT8A, and EDNRB2. Among them, WNT8A was significantly downregulated, while the other five genes were notably upregulated. We hypothesized that these genes play a pivotal role in the genetic inheritance of feather color in mule ducks.

Intriguingly, when these findings were integrated with the 44 candidate genes derived from our previous GWAS analysis, we found that EDNRB2 alone overlapped and was also significantly upregulated in black feathers. To further validate the reliability of our omics data, we conducted RT-qPCR on a panel of nine selected genes, including EDNRB2, three randomly chosen genes (NSDHL, RAB39B, SPRY3) from the GWAS candidate set, and five other significantly altered genes (TYR, KIT, TYRP1, MC1R, WNT8A) from the melanogenesis pathway. The results of RT-qPCR exhibited a high concordance with the transcriptomic data (Fig. 6), thereby confirming the accuracy of our sequencing-based expression profiles. In summary, the convergent evidence from both genomic (GWAS and genome scan) and transcriptomic analyses, coupled with experimental validation, strongly suggests that the EDNRB2 gene likely played a critical regulatory role in feather color determination in mule ducks.

Results of KASP detection of color trait-related genes

Five KASP markers (labeled KASP01 to KASP05) were designed for the following SNP loci on chromosome 10 within and near the EDNRB2 gene: 6451606, 6452579, 6453116, 6455947, and 6458629. All five markers are located within the EDNRB2 genomic region and, according to the genome scan analysis, each simultaneously exceeds the per-site FST and log2πratio thresholds (Table S11). These markers were used to genotype the corresponding five SNPs in an additional, independent cohort of 142 mule ducks. Whereas only one genotype was detected at KASP01 and KASP02 (AA and CC, respectively) and KASP03 showed two (AA and AC), both KASP04 and KASP05 revealed all three possible genotypes (Table S13).

Analysis of variance indicated that at the KASP04 locus, individuals with the GG genotype had a significantly lower mean PCS (3.54) than those with the GT or TT genotypes. Similarly, at the KASP05 locus, the GG genotype was associated with a significantly lower score (3.53) compared to the GC and CC genotypes (Table 2, Fig. 7A). These results suggest a potential role of the KASP04 and KASP05 loci in the inheritance of plumage color in mule ducks (Fig. 3A). A further haplotype analysis combining KASP04 and KASP05 identified nine different haplotypes, with highly significant differences in PCSs among them. The GG/GG haplotype had the lowest mean score (3.05), followed by GT/GG (4.60) (Table 3, Fig. 7B). All other haplotypes were associated with scores greater than 8, indicating that individuals carrying these haplotypes likely exhibit a larger area of black plumage.

Discussion

In the contemporary breeding landscape, the integration of multi-omics approaches has emerged as a highly efficient strategy for breeders. Plumage color, a crucial packaging trait, has substantial practical and economic implications for breed consistency in the production of mule ducks (Wang et al., 2025b). Specifically, the white-feathered mule ducks are favored by consumers because of their aesthetically pleasing carcasses post-slaughter. By using a combination of genomic and transcriptomic analysis and KASP validation techniques, our study successfully identified the EDNRB2 gene as a key determinant of plumage color in mule ducks. This discovery provided novel insights into the genetic mechanisms underlying plumage color in mule ducks, and also invigorated the development of breeding programs of mule duck parents.

By comparing the transcriptome data of mule ducks with different plumage colors, we observed that the EDNRB2 gene expression level was significantly higher in individuals with black feathers than in those with white feathers. Similar findings have been reported in studies on poultry plumage, for example, in geese, the EDNRB2 gene expression level is notably elevated in the dorsal skin of individuals with melanin primarily distributed in this area (Xi et al., 2020b). In pigeons, EDNRB2 gene expression is also significantly higher in individuals with black feathers (Maclary et al., 2023). In present study, GWAS of mule ducks revealed that SNP markers associated with plumage color were concentrated in the chromosomal region where the EDNRB2 gene was located. This finding further supports the crucial role of the EDNRB2 gene in plumage color formation. The advantage of GWAS was its ability to identify gene regions associated with target traits across the entire genome, thereby overcoming the limitations of traditional mapping methods. However, the interpretation of GWAS results requires validation through other experimental approaches. Here, we used the KASP technique to validate the key SNP loci identified in the GWAS. The results revealed that the distribution frequency of these SNP loci varied significantly among mule duck populations with different plumage colors, further confirming the association between the EDNRB2 gene and plumage color. The application of KASP validation technology enhanced the reliability of the study, and also provided technical support for subsequent marker-assisted breeding. By using KASP technology, we could rapidly screen individuals carrying specific plumage-related SNP loci, thereby accelerating the selection process for plumage traits in mule ducks.

Although the sample size of 114 individuals in our GWAS was below the conventional recommendation for studying complex traits, it was sufficient for a Mendelian-like trait such as plumage color, which exhibited minimal environmental influence and a relatively simple genetic architecture governed by a few major-effect loci. The requirement for adequate sample size in genetic mapping studies is intrinsically linked to the underlying genetic architecture of the trait. Statistically, large samples are not necessary to detect a biologically meaningful quantitative trait locus (Wang and Xu, 2019), particularly when a small number of loci with substantial effects can explain the phenotypic variance. This principle is empirically supported by the successful localization of the MITF gene associated with plumage color in Beijing ducks by using a cohort of only 106 individuals (Zhou et al., 2018a). Moreover, the robustness of the initial GWAS signal for EDNRB2 was confirmed through a convergent multi-omics validation pipeline, including genome scan, transcriptomics, and KASP genotyping in an independent cohort, which significantly strengthens the evidence beyond the scope of the initial association study.

In the melanogenesis pathway, endothelin-3 (EDN3) signaling plays a pivotal role in melanocyte development. The secretory peptide EDN3 acts as a crucial upstream regulator by binding to and activating its specific G protein-coupled receptor, EDNRB2 (endothelin B2 receptor) (Saldana-Caboverde and Kos, 2010; Bondurand et al., 2018). Previous research has established that the EDN3/EDNRB2 axis is fundamentally involved in the migration, proliferation, and differentiation of neural crest cells, which are the embryonic precursors of melanocytes (Lee et al., 2003; Liu et al., 2019). Specifically, EDN3 promotes the survival and expansion of melanoblast populations (Lahav et al., 1996). EDNRB2 expression is also highly specific to the melanocytic lineage in avians, with strong upregulation in neural crest cells committed to the dorsolateral pathway (the melanoblast route) and remaining persistent in differentiated melanocytes (Lecoin et al., 1998). This receptor is essential for orienting cell migration towards the skin and for promoting melanocyte differentiation. Therefore, the EDN3-EDNRB2 signaling pathway forms a critical genetic module regulating the formation and distribution of melanocytes, which directly underpins plumage pigmentation.

The association between EDNRB2 and plumage color is well established in diverse poultry species, such as Japanese quail (Miwa et al., 2006, 2007), chicken (Kinoshita et al., 2014), goose (Xi et al., 2020b; Yang et al., 2022, 2024), domestic duck (Li et al., 2015; Xi et al., 2021; Guo et al., 2022) and Muscovy duck (Wu et al., 2017). FST analysis between white and gray domestic geese identified EDNRB2 as the key gene for plumage variation, with a 14-bp insertion in its third exon causing premature transcript degradation and loss of function (Xi et al., 2020b). In Japanese quail, a missense mutation (c.995G→A) in the EDNRB2 gene leads to an amino acid substitution (Arg332His), resulting in the “panda” phenotype characterized by an almost complete absence of melanin deposition across the body, with only minimal pigmented patches remaining on the back (Miwa et al., 2007). An orthologous mutation in ducks similarly causes recessive white plumage (Li et al., 2015). In domestic chickens, recessive white plumage is also associated with a mutation in the EDNRB2 gene, though this mutation is located in exon 5 and causes a different amino acid substitution (Cys244Phe) (Kinoshita et al., 2014). Additionally, a definitive mechanistic link was recently demonstrated in blue peacocks, where a specific nonsense mutation (g.4:12583552G>A) in the fifth coding sequence of EDNRB2 has been identified as the causal variant for albinism, preventing the colonization of feather by melanocytes (Wang et al., 2025a). While these studies on plumage color have focused on purebred species, research on the hybrid mule duck (a cross between Muscovy duck and domestic duck) remains unreported. Our findings in mule ducks aligned with those in the purebred species, identifying EDNRB2 as a key candidate gene influencing plumage variation. Preliminary KASP validation further suggests that two specific loci within the 5 kb upstream region of EDNRB2 may have critically influenced its expression activity, thereby affecting melanocyte migration, differentiation, and ultimately, the plumage phenotype. However, existing research indicates that while mutations in EDNRB2 can cause recessive white plumage, mutant individuals consistently retain residual pigmentation in limited areas. This observation suggests that the identified mutations may not completely abolish the function of the endothelin-endothelin receptor pathway. Given the high sequence and functional similarity among various endothelins and their receptors, it is plausible that other family members may induce compensatory effects (Liu et al., 2019), although this hypothesis requires further in-depth investigation for confirmation. Future studies should focus on functional validation of the upstream SNPs and the EDNRB2 gene itself, including analyses of their regulatory effects on gene expression, role in melanocyte development, and causal relationships with plumage color phenotypes in vivo.

Conclusion

This study identified the genetic basis of plumage color in mule ducks through an integrated analytical approach. By combining GWAS with genome-wide scans for genetic differentiation between plumage color groups, together with comparative transcriptomics and RT-qPCR, we bridged population-level genetic variation with differences in gene expression, collectively nominating EDNRB2 as the key candidate gene. This finding was further validated by KASP genotyping in an independent population, which confirmed the significant association between specific upstream variants of EDNRB2 and PCSs. Collectively, our present work identified a major genetic determinant for a key trait, and also provided validated molecular tools that establish a foundation for future breeding strategies.
