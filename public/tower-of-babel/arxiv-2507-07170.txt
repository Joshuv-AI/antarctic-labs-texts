Holomorphic bootstrap for RCFT: signs and bounds for quasi-characters
and Sunil Mukhi

Abstract
Quasi-characters are solutions of simple modular differential equations that form an explicit basis for the space of all admissible characters that can potentially describe rational CFT. They were classified for rank- 2 2 in arXiv:1810.09472 [hep-th], where it was conjectured that their q q -series has coefficients of alternating sign that stabilise to a fixed sign at order c 12 \frac{c}{12} and sometimes undergo more exotic behaviour – where c c is the central charge. Here we prove some of these conjectures and estimate the growth of coefficients near the stabilisation point. We also explain what role the signs play in the construction of admissible characters from quasi-characters.

Introduction

The partition function
Z(\tau,{\bar{\tau}})
of a rational conformal field theory (RCFT) can be expanded over a finite set of (generalised) characters
\chi_{i}(\tau)
that are holomorphic in the interior of the torus moduli space parametrised by
\tau
:

Z(\tau,{\bar{\tau}})=\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}\chi_{i}(\tau)M_{ij}{\overline{\chi}}_{j}({\bar{\tau}})

(1.1)

The characters count descendants under the full chiral algebra. Let
r
, called the rank, denote the number of primary fields
\phi_{i}
of the theory. There is one character for each primary field, though they are not necessarily linearly independent – for example, a field
\phi_{i}
and its complex conjugate
\phi_{\bar{i}}
will have the same character, even though they can be distinct as primaries. Hence, the total number
p
of characters satisfies
p\leq r
.

The full set of characters (counting repeated ones) makes up a vector-valued modular form (VVMF), which transforms under SL(2,Z) as:

\chi_{i}\left(\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d}\right)=\sum_{j}\varrho_{ij}\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\chi_{j},\quad\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\hbox{SL(2,Z)}

(1.2)

where the modular representation matrix
\varrho
provides an
r
-dimensional unitary representation of SL(2,Z) and satisfies
\varrho^{\dagger}M\varrho=M

1
1

1

As explained in
[
2
,
3
]
, Eq. (
1.2
) remains true for the – in general smaller – set of

1&~0\end{pmatrix}
only satisfies

and is no longer unitary. Instead, it preserves a diagonal matrix

where

and the non-unitary

The components
\chi_{i}
of the VVMF have a series expansion in terms of

of the form:

\chi_{i}(\tau)=q^{\alpha_{i}}\sum a_{i,n}q^{n}

(1.3)

A VVMF will be called

a_{0,0}=1

i,n
. This is a necessary but not sufficient condition for them to correspond to a sum over descendants under a chiral algebra.

Quasi-characters are several infinite families of VVMF. They were adapted in
[
1
]
from an infinite family of solutions to a Modular Linear Differential Equation (MLDE) discussed by Kaneko and Zagier
[
4
]
and in subsequent works by Kaneko et al
[
5

6

, motivated by the application to supersingular curves. However their adapted version turns out to be a useful tool in understanding admissible characters for rational conformal field theory. In this context they are characterised by a weaker requirement than admissibility: integrality of the coefficients

is still required, but they need not all be positive. Also we no longer insist on
a_{0,0}=1
, so there only has to exist
some

[

, they form a linear basis for admissible characters of arbitrary Wronskian index. Hence they are an extremely useful construction, and our purpose in the present work is to study and derive some of their intriguing aspects.

We will focus on VVMFs that have precisely two independent components,
p=2
in the above notation. However, these can arise in the context of RCFT with rank

or
4
. In the latter two cases there are symmetries that identify two or three characters, respectively, with each other

Our goal here is to provide proofs of these properties for the

Survey of quasi-characters

2.1
Review of quasi-characters

Quasi-characters are non-admissible but integral solutions to Modular Linear Differential Equations (MLDE). The most general second-order MLDE has the form:

\Big(D_{\tau}^{2}+{\textstyle\frac{\ell}{6}}\phi_{2}(\tau)D_{\tau}+\phi_{4}(\tau)\Big)\chi=0

(2.1)

where:

D_{\tau}\equiv\frac{1}{2\pi i}\frac{d}{d\tau}-\frac{w}{12}E_{2}(\tau)

(2.2)

when acting on a weight-
w
modular function and
E_{2}(\tau)
is the second Eisenstein series, which transforms as a connection on moduli space. The function
\phi_{2},\phi_{4}
are modular of weight 2 and 4 respectively under SL(2,Z). In general they are meromorphic and can have
{\textstyle\frac{\ell}{6}}
poles in
\tau
, where the fractional number comes from the singular points in torus moduli space (see
[
1
]
for details) and
\ell
is an integer called the Wronskian index. The solutions
\chi
of the equation are of course holomorphic in
\tau
, except at
\tau\to i\infty
.

The solutions of Eq. (
2.1
) have an expansion of the form:

\chi_{i}(q)=q^{\alpha_{i}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{i,n}q^{n}

(2.3)

where
i=0,1
for the identity and non-identity character respectively. The
\alpha_{i}
are called the exponents of the solution.
They satisfy the valence formula:

\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}=\frac{1-\ell}{6}

(2.4)

It is useful to label the exponents by
\alpha_{0}=-\frac{c}{24},\alpha_{1}=-\frac{c}{24}+h
. In the case where MLDE solutions describe a CFT,
c
and
h
are the (chiral) central charge and conformal dimension for that CFT, otherwise this is just a choice of notation. In terms of
c,h
, the valence formula becomes:

\ell=1-6h+\frac{c}{2}

(2.5)

For the special case of second-order MLDE, it has been shown that the Wronskian index is even
[
24
]
(see also
[
11
]
).

The central result of
[
1
]
is that quasi-characters with
\ell=0,2,4
provide a basis for all admissible VVMFs with Wronskian index
\ell=6n,6n+2,6n+4
respectively, where
n
is any non-negative integer. Given that MLDEs with
\ell<6
are relatively easy to solve explicitly, this is a useful simplification and in principle permits a complete classification of admissible characters.

For
\ell=0
, the quasi-characters are solutions to the MMS equation
[
2
]
:

(D_{\tau}^{2}+\mu E_{4})\chi=0

(2.6)

Here
\mu
is a real parameter, related to the central charge of the solution by:

\mu=-\frac{c(c+4)}{576}

(2.7)

The construction of these quasi-characters was inspired by explicit results in the theory of second-order Modular Linear Differential Equations
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
. It should be noted that these references only address the case of vanishing Wronskian index
\ell=0
, which in the mathematical literature is sometimes called the “non-zero Wronskian condition” (because
\ell=0
means that the Wronskian determinant, which appears in the denominator of
\phi_{2}
and
\phi_{4}
, is everywhere non-vanishing).

For Wronskian index
\ell=2
, the quasi-character solutions were again constructed in
[
1
]
. Next, it was argued that all
\ell=4
quasi-characters take the form
\chi_{{\mathsf{E}}_{8,1}}
multiplied by the
\ell=0
quasi-characters. This means that using only solutions of the MLDEs with
\ell=0
and 2, we can construct all admissible characters for any allowed
\ell

In the present work we focus exclusively on
\ell=0

\ell=2
family for future work
[
25
]
. Let us now provide the details of the quasi-character solutions. The

2

3
3

3

Now as shown in

of the

2.3
Frobenius recursion relation

The Modular Linear Differential Equation Eq. (
2.6
) which determines the quasi-characters can be solved by the Frobenius method in terms of the Eisenstein series coefficients and the central charge. The solution given here will be used later on for approximations as well as proofs by induction. The relevant Eisenstein series appearing in this equation are given by:

\begin{split}E_{2}(\tau)&=1-24\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{1}(i)q^{i}\\
E_{4}(\tau)&=1+240\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{3}(i)q^{i}\end{split}

(2.11)

where
\sigma_{m}(i)\equiv\sum_{d|i}d^{m}
is the generalised divisor function. The resulting recursion relation for the identity character is
[
2
]
:

a_{0,n}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{0,n-i}\left(-4\big(n-i-\frac{c}{24}\big)\sigma_{1}(i)+\frac{5c(c+4)}{12}\sigma_{3}(i)\right)}{n(n-\frac{c+2}{12})}

(2.12)

For the non-identity character we have instead:

a_{1,n}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{1,n-i}\left(-4\big(n-i+\frac{c+4}{24}\big)\sigma_{1}(i)+\frac{5c(c+4)}{12}\sigma_{3}(i)\right)}{n(n+\frac{c+2}{12})}

(2.13)

Now we use the parametrisation of
c
in Eqs (
2.8
), (
2.9
) and re-write the Frobenius recursions as:

a_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{M}(n,i)\,a_{n-i}

(2.14)

where the
f_{M}(n,i)
are:

\begin{split}f_{M}(n,i)&\equiv\frac{-4\big(n-i-M-\frac{j}{24}\big)\sigma_{1}(i)+\frac{5(24M+j)(24M+j+4)}{12}\sigma_{3}(i)}{n(n-2M-\frac{j+2}{12})}\qquad\qquad\hbox{(identity)}\\
f_{M}(n,i)&\equiv\frac{-4\big(n-i+M+\frac{j+4}{24}\big)\sigma_{1}(i)+\frac{5(24M+j)(24M+j+4)}{12}\sigma_{3}(i)}{n(n+2M+\frac{j+2}{12})}\qquad\hbox{(non-identity)}\end{split}

(2.15)

2.4
Modular transformations

It was noted above that with the quasi-characters being labelled by
c=24M+j
, the
M=0
member of each series is in the MMS family
[
2
]
. It has

and the modular

[
3
]
. We have:

\begin{split}j=\frac{14}{5},\frac{26}{5}:~~S&=\sqrt{2}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{5+\sqrt{5}}}&~\frac{1}{\sqrt{5-\sqrt{5}}}\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{5-\sqrt{5}}}&-\frac{1}{\sqrt{5+\sqrt{5}}}\end{pmatrix}\\[5.69054pt]
j=1,7:~~S&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&~1\\
1&-1\end{pmatrix}\\[5.69054pt]
j=2,6:~~S&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\begin{pmatrix}1&~2\\
1&-1\end{pmatrix}\\
j=4:~~S&=~~\,\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&~3\\[5.69054pt]
1&-1\end{pmatrix}\end{split}

(2.16)

In the first two lines, the given
S
-matrix is real, symmetric and unitary (hence it squares to 1). In the remaining two lines it is real but neither symmetric nor unitary, but still squares to 1. In the latter two cases it is not the true
S
-matrix. As explained in
[
3
]
, in these cases one has more than two primary fields (hence they correspond to rank
>2
MTC). In fact the
{\mathsf{A}}_{2},{\mathsf{E}}_{6}
series have three primaries, with two of them corresponding to complex conjugates of each other and thus having the same character, while the
{\mathsf{D}}_{4}
series has four primaries of which three (the

representations of
{\mathsf{D}}_{4}
) are related by triality and thus have the same character. Thus the true
S
-matrices should have rank 3 and 4 respectively. The ones listed above then follow by “collapsing” the rows and columns corresponding to the same characters. The true
S
-matrices for these cases were constructed in
[
3
]
. They coincide with the known
S
-matrices for the corresponding MTC
[
7
]
, however we do not need them as the collapsed matrices are sufficient for our purpose here.

Now although the above matrices are obtained for the admissible characters for

2.16
) gives the (collapsed)
S
-matrix for each quasi-character series.

2.5
Combining quasi-characters

The utility of quasi-characters is that they form a convenient basis for constructing admissible characters. Each term in the basis solves the same MLDE (but with different parameters), and taking linear combinations of them can potentially generate admissible characters that solve a different -- and more difficult -- MLDE

8

8

Such MLDEs are said to have “movable poles” and were recently studied from first principles in
[
11
]
.
. It has been shown in several examples that admissible characters can be found in this way
[
1
,
11
]
and also that there exist genuine RCFT corresponding to many of these examples
[
15
,
11
]
. Here we first explain how these linear combinations are taken and what are the basic properties (

and
\ell

The basic idea is that, since all quasi-characters in a sub-series transform in the same way under SL(2,Z), we can add them to construct more general VVMFs. Each quasi-character considered here solves Eq. (
2.6
) but, as emphasised above, each term actually solves the equation with a different value of the parameter
\mu

not
satisfy the same equation. Rather, with each successive term in the sum one finds that
\ell

for any positive integer

1
]
that this procedure generates all admissible VVMFs with these values of

A generic linear combination of quasi-characters in any one of the sub-series with
c=24M+j
(for fixed

\chi_{i}(\tau)=\sum_{M=M_{\rm min}}^{M_{\rm max}}{\cal N}_{M}\,\chi_{i}^{[24M+j]}(\tau)

\begin{split}\chi_{0}(\tau)&={\cal N}_{M_{\rm max}}q^{-M_{\rm max}-\frac{j}{24}}+{\cal N}_{M_{\rm max}-1}q^{-M_{\rm max}+1-\frac{j}{24}}+\cdots+{\cal N}_{M_{\rm min}}q^{-M_{\rm min}-\frac{j}{24}}\\

As long as
M_{\rm max}>M_{\rm min}

From Eq. (

Notice that if

Another important point is that if

Thus we have the following results:

•

The central charge of the sum is entirely determined by

•

The conformal dimension

•

.

•

When

Since

When

The first of the above results of course follows also from the well-known (many-to-one) identification of RCFT with MTC, but the second one is also useful.

2.6
Admissible characters from quasi-characters

Now we look more closely at admissibility. The general problem is not solved (and we hope to report on it in the future), so we provide two examples that illustrate the key issues. The points that these examples will highlight are: (i) quasi-characters can be used to construct admissible characters, (ii) the signs of the coefficients of the quasi-characters, the main theme of the present work, play an essential role in this construction.

3
Asymptotic behaviour and signs of
a_{n}

In this section we consider the simplest limits of the Frobenius recursion relations of sub-section
2.3
in which the behaviour of quasi-characters can be extracted. These give us some confirmation of the properties that were guessed based on examples. In the following section we analyse the intermediate region between the limits considered here, which will enable us to prove the desired results completely.

3.1
n\gg c

We start with the asymptotic behaviour for large
n
and fixed
c
, of the coefficients
a_{n}
of different quasi-characters. This can be obtained from the Rademacher formula (see
[
1
]
for more details):

a_{j,n}\sim\sum_{i=0}^{1}\sum_{\alpha_{i}+m<0}\Big(S_{ji}^{-1}e^{4\pi\sqrt{|\alpha_{i}+m|(\alpha_{j}+n)}}+\cdots\Big)a_{i,m}

(3.1)

where
S_{ij}
is the modular (collapsed)
S
-matrix.

We want to find the asymptotic growth in terms of the leading coefficients of the two characters. Hence we choose
m=0
in Eq. (
3.1
), to get:

a_{j,n}\sim\sum_{i=0}^{1}\sum_{\alpha_{i}<0}\Big(S_{ji}^{-1}e^{4\pi\sqrt{|\alpha_{i}|(\alpha_{j}+n)}}+\cdots\Big)a_{i,0}

(3.2)

Recall that just after Eq. (
A.3
) we showed that one of the exponents
\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1}
is positive and the other negative. In fact for
c>0
we have
\alpha_{0}<0,\alpha_{1}>0
while for
c<0
we have
\alpha_{0}<0,\alpha_{1}>0
. Thus in the former case, the leading contribution to the RHS of Eq. (
3.2
) comes from
i=0
, while in the latter, the leading term comes from
i=1
. We learn that:

\begin{split}c>0:\qquad~a_{0,n}&\sim\exp\left(4\pi\sqrt{\frac{c}{24}\Big(-\frac{c}{24}+n\Big)}\,\right)a_{0,0}\\[5.69054pt]
a_{1,n}&\sim\exp\left(4\pi\sqrt{\frac{c}{24}\Big(\frac{c+4}{24}+n\Big)}\,\right)a_{0,0}\\[5.69054pt]
c<0:\qquad a_{0,n}&\sim\exp\left(4\pi\sqrt{\left|\frac{c+4}{24}\right|\Big(-\frac{c}{24}+n\Big)}\,\right)a_{1,0}\\[5.69054pt]
a_{1,n}&\sim-\exp\left(4\pi\sqrt{\left|\frac{c+4}{24}\right|\Big(\frac{c+4}{24}+n\Big)}\,\right)a_{1,0}\end{split}

(3.3)

The last line tells us that asymptotically
a_{1,n}
is negative if we normalise the non-identity character to start with a positive value.
It follows that for
\ell=0
, all the
c>0
quasi-characters are of type I, while all the
c<0
quasi-characters have a non-identity component of type II. These predictions are confirmed in Table
2
and easily verified for the other quasi-character families as well.

3.2
c\gg n

Here we consider the opposite limit where
c
is very large compared to
n
the order in the
q

Empirically, as seen in Table
2
, the identity character for
c=24M+j>0
has exactly

negative coefficients, namely

while the non-identity character is completely positive. On the other hand for
c=24M+j<0

, always assuming that we have chosen

M>0
and in the non-identity character for
M<0
. We now prove part of the above result: namely that for
n\ll c
, the alternating signs indeed arise as seen above. Later we will examine the crossover region. In the following discussion we set
a_{0}=1
.

Identity character

The Frobenius recursion relation for the identity character was written in Eq. (

\begin{split}a_{0,n}&\sim\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{5c^{2}}{12}\sigma_{3}(i)}{\left(-\frac{cn}{12}\right)}a_{0,n-i}\\
&=-\frac{5c}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{3}(i)a_{0,n-i}\end{split}

(3.4)

This implies that each

scales to leading order as

, so in the above sum one can neglect all but the

\sigma_{3}(1)=1
, this leads to:

a_{0,n}\sim\left(-\frac{5c}{n}\right)^{n}+{\cal O}\left(c^{n-1}\right)

(3.5)

Thus for
c>0
, the sign of

alternates, with negative signs for odd

. This is consistent with the behaviour seen in explicit examples in Table
2
(top left quadrant). On the other hand for

2
(bottom left quadrant).

Non-identity character

Here we take the large-

) to get:

\begin{split}a_{1,n}&\sim\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{5c^{2}}{12}\sigma_{3}(i)}{\left(\frac{cn}{12}\right)}a_{1,n-i}\\
&\sim\frac{5c}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{3}(i)a_{1,n-i}\end{split}

(3.6)

At large

the recursion is dominated as before by the highest power of

so we get:

a_{1,n}\sim\left(\frac{5c}{n}\right)^{n}+{\cal O}\left(c^{n-1}\right)

(3.7)

This time we see that all coefficients are positive for

while they alternate in sign for

, confirming the remaining quadrants of
2
(top right and bottom right).

As emphasised from the beginning, the results in this section can only be trusted for

is not accessible to the analysis here. To study this region, we introduce a new approach in the following section.

4
Behaviour for
n\sim|{\textstyle\frac{c}{12}}|\gg 1

Recall that we have parametrised
c
as
24M+j
where
1\leq j\leq 8
. Thus we are now considering the limit
n\sim|2M|\gg 1
. In explicit examples, we saw that the alternating component of a
\ell=0
quasi-character exhibits the following features: in some cases there is a crossover from alternating behaviour to a definite sign at
n=2|M|
. The previous asymptotic analyses were carried out for
n\gg c
or
n\ll c
and in these limits one obviously cannot see such crossovers. Hence we are interested in the regime
n\sim|c|
. Of course the coefficients can be explicitly computed for any small, finite value of these quantities, but we now consider the limit where both
c
and
n
are large and of comparable order. This will permit us to find approximate solutions to the Frobenius recursion in general, and use those to predict the observed behaviour.

4.1
Recursion relation in the limit

Recall that in Eqs. (
2.14
), (
2.15
) we rewrote the Frobenius recursion by defining the quantity
f_{M}(n,i)
and replacing
c
by
24M+j
.
We start by taking the limit of large
M
where
n\lesssim 2|M|
. For these values of
n
the second term in the numerator of Eq. (
2.15
) dominates over the first as was seen in Eq. (
3.4
). But this time we do not neglect
n
relative to
c
in the denominator, so (assuming for
n\neq 2|M|
) we get:

\begin{split}f_{M}(n,i)~&{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\simeq}\limits^{|M|\to\infty}}}~\frac{240M^{2}}{n(n-2M)}\sigma_{3}(i)\qquad\hbox{(identity)}\\[5.69054pt]
f_{M}(n,i)~&{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\simeq}\limits^{|M|\to\infty}}}~\frac{240M^{2}}{n(n+2M)}\sigma_{3}(i)\qquad\hbox{(non-identity)}\end{split}

(4.1)

The cases
n=2M,M>0
for the identity character, and
n=-2M,M<0
for the non-identity, must be considered separately – here the expression Eq. (
4.1
) is not valid, and must be replaced by:

\begin{split}f_{M}(2M,i)~&{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\simeq}\limits^{|M|\to\infty}}}~-\frac{1440M}{j+2}\sigma_{3}(i)\qquad\hbox{(identity, $M>0$)}\\
f_{M}(2|M|,i)~&{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\simeq}\limits^{|M|\to\infty}}}~~~~\frac{1440|M|}{j+2}\sigma_{3}(i)\qquad\hbox{(non-identity, $M<0$)}\end{split}

(4.2)

Putting everything together, we have:

\begin{split}f_{M}(n,i)<0\hbox{ for: }&M>0\hbox{ and }n\leq 2M\qquad\hbox{(identity)}\\
&M<0\hbox{ and }n<2|M|\quad\hbox{(non-identity)}\\
f_{M}(n,i)>0\hbox{ for: }&M<0\quad\hbox{(identity)}\\
&M>0\quad\hbox{(non-identity)}\end{split}

(4.3)

One direct consequence is that the identity character is positive for
M<0
while the non-identity is positive for
M>0
. Also, both are completely positive for
n>2|M|
. This is because at every step of the recursion, the relevant functions
f_{M}(n,i)
are positive.

Before considering the other cases, let us obtain some bounds.
In Eq. (
4.1
), for fixed
n
the coefficient multiplying
\sigma_{3}(i)
grows linearly with
|M|
. On the other hand if
n
is of order
|M|
, say
n=\alpha|M|
where
\alpha
is a constant of order 1, then – for the identity character –
f_{M}(n,i)
tends to a constant:

\begin{split}f_{M}(n,i)&\simeq\frac{240}{\alpha(\alpha-2)}\sigma_{3}(i),\quad M\geq 0\\
&\simeq\frac{240}{\alpha(\alpha+2)}\sigma_{3}(i),\quad M<0\end{split}

(4.4)

The situation is reversed for the non-identity character.

Thus for large
|M|
and all
n\lesssim 2|M|,~i\leq n
, we have the lower bound:

\begin{split}|f_{M}(n,i)|&~>~\frac{240}{\alpha|\alpha-2|},\quad M>0\\
&~>~\frac{240}{\alpha(\alpha+2)},\quad M<0\end{split}

(4.5)

The lower bound over the full range
\alpha\in(0,2)
is therefore:

\begin{split}|f_{M}(n,i)|&~>~240,\qquad M>0\\
&~>~30,~\qquad M<0\end{split}

(4.6)

Now we proceed to determine the signs and estimate the magnitudes of
a_{n}
for
n=\alpha|M|
. For this, we first write down a formal solution to the recursion relation Eq. (
2.14
) as follows:

\begin{split}a_{n}&=\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n}f_{M}(n,i_{1})a_{n-i_{1}}\\
&=f_{M}(n,n)a_{0}+\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n-1}\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{n-i_{1}}f_{M}(n,i_{1})f_{M}(n-i_{1},i_{2})a_{n-i_{1}-i_{2}}\\
&=\Big(f_{M}(n,n)+f_{M}(n,n-1)f_{M}(1,1)\Big)a_{0}\\
&\qquad+\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n-2}\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{n-i_{1}-1}\sum_{i_{3}=1}^{n-i_{1}-i_{2}}f_{M}(n,i_{1})f_{M}(n-i_{1},i_{2})f_{M}(n-i_{1}-i_{2},i_{3})a_{n-i_{1}-i_{2}-i_{3}},\quad\hbox{etc.}\end{split}

(4.7)

Continuing until only
a_{0}
appears on the RHS, we see the following structure. The RHS is a sum of
p
products of
f_{M}
factors with
1\leq p\leq n
. The term of order
p
has sums over all
i_{k},1\leq k\leq p
with the constraint
\sum_{k=1}^{p}i_{k}=n
. Every term multiplies
a_{0}
, which we henceforth set to 1.

We can classify the terms by ordered partitions of
n
. For example the term with partition
(n)
corresponds to the first term
f_{M}(n,n)
in the second line of Eq. (
4.7
), while the term with partition
(1^{n})
corresponds to
i_{1}=i_{2}=\cdots i_{n}=1
and can be written explicitly as:

\prod_{r=1}^{n}f_{M}(r,1)

(4.8)

Next, the ordered partition
(2.1^{n-2})
corresponds to
p=n-1
and we have
i_{1}=2;i_{2},i_{3},\cdots,i_{n-1}=1
. The associated term is:

f_{M}(n,2)\prod_{r=1}^{n-2}f_{M}(r,1)

(4.9)

By summing over all orderings, we can replace ordered by un-ordered partitions. For example the un-ordered partition
(2.1^{n-2})
corresponds to:

\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}f_{M}(k+1,2)\prod_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{r=1}{r\ne k,k+1}}^{n}f_{M}(r,1)

(4.10)

which can more conveniently be written as:

\left(\prod_{r=1}^{n}f_{M}(r,1)\right)\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{f_{M}(k+1,2)}{f_{M}(k,1)f_{M}(k+1,1)}

(4.11)

These examples suggest that a useful way to organise all terms in the sum is to first factor out:

P_{M}(n)\equiv\prod_{r=1}^{n}f_{M}(r,1)

(4.12)

In the example above, we are left with a ratio
\frac{f_{M}(k+1,2)}{f_{M}(k,1)f_{M}(k+1,1)}
wherever there is a 2 in the partition replacing two 1s. More generally, we define:

g_{M}(k,m)\equiv\frac{f_{M}(k+m-1,m)}{\prod_{r=1}^{m}f_{M}(k+r-1,1)}

(4.13)

Note that
g_{M}(k,1)=1
. Intuitively, the combinatorial meaning of each
g_{M}(k,m)
is “start with a chain of
n
symbols each equal to 1, then at the
k
th position in the chain, delete the next
m
factors of 1 and replace them by a single factor of
m
”.

Now the subset of terms on the RHS of the recursion relation corresponding to all un-ordered partitions of the form
(m,1^{n-m})
, for any
m\geq 2
, is (after factoring out
P_{M}(n)
) given by:

\sum_{m=2}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n-m+1}g_{M}(k,m)=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}g_{M}(k,2)+\sum_{k=1}^{n-2}g_{M}(k,3)+\cdots+g_{M}(1,n)

(4.14)

These are all the terms linear in
g_{M}
. Quadratic terms in
g_{M}
correspond to partitions of the form
(m_{1}.m_{2}.1^{n-m_{1}-m_{2}})
with
m_{1},m_{2}\geq 2
. These terms take the form:

\begin{split}&\sum_{k_{1}=1}^{n-3}\sum_{k_{2}=k_{1}+2}^{n-1}g_{M}(k_{1},2)g_{M}(k_{2},2)+\sum_{k_{1}=1}^{n-5}\sum_{k_{2}=k_{1}+3}^{n-3}g_{M}(k_{1},3)g_{M}(k_{2},3)\\
&\qquad\quad+\sum_{k_{1}=1}^{n-4}\sum_{k_{2}=k_{1}+3}^{n-1}g_{M}(k_{1},3)g_{M}(k_{2},2)+\sum_{k_{1}=1}^{n-4}\sum_{k_{2}=k_{1}+2}^{n-1}g_{M}(k_{1},2)g_{M}(k_{2},3)+\cdots\\
&=\sum_{m_{1}m_{2}\geq 2}\sum_{k_{1}=1}^{n+1-m_{1}-m_{2}}\sum_{k_{2}=k_{1}+m_{1}}^{n+1-m_{2}}g_{M}(k_{1},m_{1})g_{M}(k_{2},m_{2})\end{split}

(4.15)

The generalisation to higher powers of
g_{M}
is straightforward.

In this way we organise the recursion relation Eq. (
2.14
) into contributions of increasing orders in
g_{M}
. For fixed
n
, the maximum order in
g_{M}
is achieved when every term is a
g_{M}(k,2)
for some
k
(because
g_{M}(k,1)=1
as noted above). The corresponding order is
\frac{n}{2}
and corresponds to the partition
2^{\frac{n}{2}}
(assuming
n
is even).

Implicit in our discussion above is the notion that
g_{M}
is small and orders in
g_{M}
can be used to develop a perturbative expansion. In fact, in the approximation leading to Eq. (
4.1
), we have:

\begin{split}g_{M}(k,m)&\simeq\frac{\sigma_{3}(m)}{(240M^{2})^{m-1}}\prod_{r=1}^{m-1}(k+r-1)(k+r-1-2M)\qquad\hbox{(identity)}\\
&\simeq\frac{\sigma_{3}(m)}{(240M^{2})^{m-1}}\prod_{r=1}^{m-1}(k+r-1)(k+r-1+2M)\qquad\hbox{(non-identity)}\end{split}

(4.16)

Let us now see what this tells us about the two characters of our solution.

Identity character

Let us now pick the identity character and estimate the value of the product
P_{M}(n)
as defined in Eq. (
4.12
) for large
|M|
. For
M>0
we use the first line of Eq. (
4.1
). Because the last argument of each
f_{M}
is 1, each of the
f_{M}
factors in
P_{M}
is proportional to
\sigma_{3}(1)=1
and hence that factor can be ignored. Then we get:

\begin{split}P_{M}(n)&\simeq\prod_{r=1}^{n}\frac{240M^{2}}{r(r-2M)}\\
&=(-1)^{n}\frac{(240M^{2})^{n}(2M-n-1)!}{n!(2M-1)!}\qquad\hbox{ for }n<2M\end{split}

(4.17)

We will be particularly interested in the value at
n\simeq 2|M|
.
However the answer is apparently divergent at this point, since – as we already explained – the approximation Eq. (
4.1
) to
f_{M}
is invalid at this value. So the last term in the product Eq. (
4.12
) that defines
P_{M}(n)
has to be replaced by Eq. (
4.2
), giving:

P_{M}(2M)\simeq\frac{1440M}{j+2}\frac{(240M^{2})^{2M-1}}{\big((2M-1)!\big)^{2}},\quad M>0

(4.18)

On the other hand, for
M<0
we get:

\begin{split}P_{M}(n)&\simeq\prod_{r=1}^{n}\frac{240M^{2}}{r(r+2|M|)}\\
&=\frac{(240M^{2})^{n}(2|M|)!}{n!(2|M|+n)!}\end{split}

(4.19)

which is true for any
n\lesssim 2|M|
and even
n
larger than
|M|
as long as it is of the same order.

Non-identity character

For the non-identity case the roles of
M>0
and
M<0
are reversed. Noting that the sign of Eq. (
4.2
) changes as a result, we have in particular:

\begin{split}P_{M}(2M)&\simeq\frac{(240M^{2})^{2M}}{(4M)!},\quad M>0\\
P_{M}(2|M|)&\simeq-\frac{1440|M|}{j+2}\frac{(240M^{2})^{2|M|-1}}{\big((2|M|-1)!\big)^{2}},\quad M<0\end{split}

(4.20)

Now we can proceed to use the recursion to find the sign and estimate the magnitude of the coefficients
a_{n}
for
n\simeq 2|M|
.

4.2
A crude approximation

Identity character

Consider the identity character, and start with
M>0
. Because
|f_{M}(n,i)|\geq 240
for
n<2M
we can, as an initial approximation, solve the recursion Eq. (
2.14
) by keeping only the
i=1
term on the RHS at every step. Then we have:

a_{n}\simeq\prod_{r=1}^{n}f_{M}(r,1)\equiv P_{M}(n)

(4.21)

Then for
n<2M
, every factor in the above product is negative. Hence in this approximation the
a_{n}
are alternating in sign for all
n<2M
and have the same sign as
(-1)^{n}
. This precisely confirms the prediction from explicit calculations.

It remains to consider
n=2M
as well as
n>2M
. We saw above that at
n=2M
, the term
n-2M
in the denominator of Eq. (
2.15
) vanishes and therefore we must use Eq. (
4.2
) instead. Thus we have:

a_{2M}\simeq\left(-\frac{1440M}{j+2}\right)\prod_{r=1}^{2M-1}f_{M}(r,1)

(4.22)

The terms inside the product are all negative for
\alpha<2
, so the RHS is a product of
2M
negative numbers and hence positive. Thus we conclude that
a_{n}
has the same sign as
(-1)^{n}
for all
n
up to
and including

n=2M
.

Finally, for
n>2M
(but not too large) we get the expression:

a_{n}\simeq\prod_{r=2M+1}^{n}f_{M}(r,1)\left(-\frac{1440M}{j+2}\right)\prod_{r=1}^{2M-1}f_{M}(r,1)

(4.23)

Now all terms in the first product are positive and we already argued that the rest of the expression is positive, so the final answer is positive. We see that once
n
crosses
2M
all terms turn positive, in agreement with explicit calculations. Extrapolating to very large
n
, this also matches on correctly to the Rademacher formula Eq. (
3.3
).

For
M<0
things are even simpler. The above expressions are replaced by the result:

a_{n}\simeq\prod_{r=1}^{n}f_{M}(r,1)

(4.24)

for
n\lesssim 2|M|
, and all factors are positive, in perfect agreement with the explicit calculations. However in this case we have
|f_{M}|>30
and this lower bound is almost an order of magnitude smaller than for
M>0
, where it was 240. So we may expect the crude approximation to be less accurate for
M<0
. This will indeed turn out to be the case.

To check the validity of our approximations, let us estimate the growth of both
a_{2|M|}
and
P_{M}(2|M|)
with
|M|
. First let
M>0
. Extrapolating from explicit computations

which is extremely close.

As a check of these results, we can find an upper bound for

Next consider

\begin{split}P_{M}(2|M|)&\sim\frac{e^{2M\ln 240M^{2}}}{e^{4M\ln(4M/e)}}\\

Comparing Eqs (

To summarise, for the identity character the crude approximation Eq. (

Non-identity character

From Eq. (

a_{n}
is verified for
M>0
. One small twist is that for
M<0

a_{2|M|}
is:

a_{2|M|}\simeq\left(\frac{1440|M|}{j+2}\right)\prod_{r=1}^{2|M|-1}f_{M}(r,1)

(4.31)

Here we have an odd number of negative terms, so
a_{2|M|}
is negative. After this point the series stops oscillating and therefore remains negative for
n>2|M|
. This correctly matches onto the prediction from the Rademacher formula valid for
n\gg 2|M|
.

4.3
A better approximation

We now examine corrections to Eq. (
4.21
), which take the forms in Eqs (
4.14
), (
4.15
) and so on. These terms are organised by the number of
g_{M}
factors in each one. We will try to extract the relevant corrections for large
n\sim 2|M|
. One striking discovery will be that, despite the excellent agreement between Eqs (
4.25
) and
4.26
), the crude approximation of the previous sub-section is quite misleading – there are correction terms of opposite sign that can (and do) overcome the leading terms retained in the crude approximation. However by summing a partial set of corrections we will see that the original predictions are retained.

Identity character

Starting out with the identity character and
M>0
, we define:

\begin{split}G_{M}(m)&\equiv\sum_{k=1}^{2M-m+1}g_{M}(k,m)\\
&\simeq\frac{\sigma_{3}(m)}{(240M^{2})^{m-1}}\sum_{k=1}^{2M-m+1}\prod_{r=1}^{m-1}(k+r-1)(k+r-1-2M)\end{split}

(4.32)

where we used Eq. (
4.16
). Thus the corrections linear in
g_{M}
, Eq. (
4.14
), become:

1+\sum_{m=2}^{n}G_{M}(m)

(4.33)

Now it is easily shown (using Mathematica) that:

\begin{split}\lim_{M\to\infty}\frac{G_{M}(m+1)}{G_{M}(m)}&\sim\frac{\sigma_{3}(m+1)\Gamma\left(-m-\frac{1}{2}\right)\Gamma(m+1)}{240\,\sigma_{3}(m)\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}-m\right)\Gamma(m)}\\
&=-\frac{m\,\sigma_{3}(m+1)}{240(m+\frac{1}{2})\sigma_{3}(m)}\end{split}

(4.34)

For large
m
this tends to
-\frac{1}{240}\frac{\sigma_{3}(m+1)}{\sigma_{3}(m)}
. Since the generalised divisor function
\sigma_{3}(m)
grows as
m^{3}
, this ratio tends to
-\frac{1}{240}\frac{(m+1)^{3}}{m^{3}}\sim-\frac{1}{240}
for large
m
, and the series
\sum_{m=2}^{2M}G_{M}(m)
is convergent. Moreover it is well-approximated by:

\sum_{m=2}^{2M}G_{M}(m)\simeq G_{M}(2)\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(-\frac{1}{240}\right)^{j}=\frac{240}{241}G_{M}(2)

(4.35)

which shows that the linear corrections can be well-approximated by just
G_{M}(2)
.

As a check, we compute
\frac{G_{M}(3)}{G_{M}(2)}
. For such small values of
m
one needs to use the second line of Eq. (
4.34
) and with this one finds:

\begin{split}G_{M}(2)=\sum_{k=1}^{2M-1}g_{M}(k,2)&\simeq-\frac{M}{20}\\
G_{M}(3)=\sum_{k=1}^{2M-2}g_{M}(k,3)&\simeq\frac{7M}{13500}\end{split}

(4.36)

Thus for
n=2M
, both
G_{M}(2)
and
G_{M}(3)
grow linearly for large
M
. However an encouraging sign is that
G_{M}(3)
is much smaller. Their ratio is approximately:

\frac{G_{M}(3)}{G_{M}(2)}\simeq-0.01037

(4.37)

The calculated value of the same ratio is in fact found to settle down to
-0.0104
for sufficiently large
M
(the error arises from the fact that we neglected the first term in the numerator of Eq. (
2.15
) and also neglected some small terms along the way). This is a useful confirmation of our estimates in Eq. (
4.1
). It disagrees with
-\frac{1}{240}
by a factor
\sim 2.4
, but that was expected due to
m
being small. This discrepancy will only affect the first few terms in Eq. (
4.33
) and will not significantly affect Eq. (
4.35
).

Now let us see what this discussion tells us about the sign and magnitude of the answer.
We have previously discussed the signs of the functions
f_{M}
defined in Eqs (
2.15
) from which we may deduce the signs of the
g_{M}
of Eq. (
4.13
). For the identity character and with positive
M
, we find that the sign of
g_{M}(k,m)
is given by
(-1)^{m-1}
, so it is negative for even
m
and positive for odd
m
. Hence the single-
g_{M}
series in Eq. (
4.14
) has terms of alternating signs for
M>0
. Then the magnitude of the corrections becomes important since they can affect the overall sign. On the other hand for negative
M
, all the
g_{M}
functions are positive and they cannot change the sign of the answer.

Now from Eq. (
4.32
) we find that for
M>20
the magnitude of
G_{M}(2)
becomes greater than 1 at
n\simeq 2M
, showing that it can potentially change the sign of the full sum. Moreover as Eq. (
4.35
) shows, adding the remaining linear terms
G_{M}(m)
makes little difference. Thus it is necessary to consider terms involving higher than linear powers of the
g_{M}
.

As a start we consider only those terms involving multiple powers of
g_{M}(k,2)
, such as the first term in Eq. (
4.15
), which we recall is:

\sum_{k_{1}=1}^{n-3}\sum_{k_{2}=k_{1}+2}^{n-1}g_{M}(k_{1},2)g_{M}(k_{2},2)

(4.38)

Now we add the corresponding terms with cubic and higher powers of
g_{M}(k,2)
. We will find that all such terms can be approximately summed. To start with, since we are taking
n
large, we replace the upper limits of both sums in the above expression by
n
. Now since
k_{2}>k_{1}
, the sum covers half the space
k_{1},k_{2}\in\{1,n\}
. So we approximate the quadratic term above by:

\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}g_{M}(k,2)\right)^{2}

(4.39)

Similarly the contribution of terms with
p
powers of
g_{M}(k,2)
becomes at large
n
:

\frac{1}{p!}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}g_{M}(k,2)\right)^{p}

(4.40)

As a result, the series exponentiates. Of course there are corrections that we expect (but have not rigorously proven) to be small. So we get:

a_{n}\simeq P_{M}(n)\,e^{\sum_{k=2}^{n}g_{M}(k,2)}

(4.41)

Using the first line of Eq. (
4.36
) we thus have:

\begin{split}a_{2M}&\simeq P_{M}(2M)\,e^{-\frac{M}{20}}\end{split}

(4.42)

This exponentiation immediately solves our sign problem: the sign of
a_{2M}
, and more generally
a_{n}
for
n<2M
as well as
n
slightly above
2M
, will be the same as that of
P_{M}(n)
. This cements the agreement with explicit calculations.

As for the magnitude, the exponential factor leads to a small correction to the “crude” approximation based only on Eq. (
4.26
). Using that equation in Eq. (
4.42
) we now get:

a_{2M}\simeq e^{(12.19-0.05)M}=e^{12.14M}

(4.43)

This brings the answer even closer to the result extrapolated by calculating up to
M=1800
, which was
12.13
. This is very satisfying

11

11

In fact, for

and the better approximated value as

where the percentage error in the

.

The above discussion was for
M>0
. Repeating the same steps for
M<0
, we get:

\begin{split}G_{M}(m)&\equiv\sum_{k=1}^{2|M|-m+1}g_{M}(k,m)\\
&\simeq\frac{\sigma_{3}(m)}{(240M^{2})^{m-1}}\sum_{k=1}^{2|M|-m+1}\prod_{r=1}^{m-1}(k+r-1)(k+r-1+2|M|)\end{split}

This is an apparently minor change with respect to Eq. (

a_{2|M|}\simeq P_{M}(2|M|)\,e^{\frac{M}{4}}

(4.45)

This time the correction in the exponential is positive, and the growth in Eq. (

Non-identity character

Like the leading estimate, the corrections in this case are found essentially by exchanging

5
Inductive approach to signs of
a_{n}

In this section we present an alternative approach to the Frobenius recursion relation, based on proofs by induction. We will be able to prove the desired alternation of signs without making any approximations, overcoming some of the limitations of the previous section. While this proves our key result regarding signs, it does not provide estimates for the growth of coefficients similar to Eq. (
4.43
). However, it enables us to find the following growth patterns of the coefficients:

\begin{split}&a_{n}\geq R\,a_{n-1},\quad\text{with }R>1\,\,\text{and }\,1\leq n\leq 2|M|\end{split}

(5.1)

which we dub as the
super-geometric
growth to contrast it to the geometric growth which happens for
R=1
. Note that from the Rademacher formula in Eq. (
3.3
), we have a geometric growth of coefficients in the regime of
n\gg c
(where Rademacher approximation is valid). Thus, the necessary condition for Rademacher behaviour to kick in is
\left|\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_{n}}\right|\simeq 1
. From numerics we have: for the identity character with
M=50\,(c\sim 1200)
with
j=1
and
n=50M^{2}=125000
, the ratio being
\left|\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_{n}}\right|\simeq 1.134
. We further observed that this ratio keeps on decreasing and asymptoting towards unity as we increase
M
in the above numerics.

From the above discussion, we see that if we consider the crude approximation as in Sec.
4.2
and compute the ratio
\left|\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_{n}}\right|
for the identity character with
M>0
, then it doesn’t hold beyond
n\sim 16M
since around this point, we get from Eq. (
4.12
):
\frac{P_{M}(n+1)}{P_{M}(n)}=f_{M}(n+1,1)\simeq 1
for large
M
. Also, for any scaling of the kind
n=c_{1}M^{2}
with
c_{1}>1
,
\frac{P_{M}(n+1)}{P_{M}(n)}=f_{M}(n+1,1)\to 0
for large
M
. Thus, we actually have very different behaviours in the regimes:
n\ll|M|
,
n\sim{\cal O}(|M|)
and
n\gg M
like
n\sim{\cal O}(|M|^{2})
.

5.1
Bounds on the divisor function

To carry out the inductive proof we first need to find bounds on various functions that arise in it. Here we bound
\sigma_{1}(i)
and
\sigma_{k}(i),k\geq 2
.

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}(i)=\sum_{d|i}d=\sum_{e|i}\frac{i}{e}=i\sum_{e|i}\frac{1}{e}<i\sum_{k=1}^{i}\frac{1}{k}

(5.2)

with
e\equiv\frac{i}{d}
. Now
\sum_{k=1}^{i}\frac{1}{k}\leq 1+\int_{1}^{i}\frac{dx}{x}\leq 1+\log(i)
. Thus, we get,

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}(i)\leq i(1+\log(i))

(5.3)

For
\sigma_{k}(i),k\geq 2
, we have:

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}(i)=\sum_{d|i}d^{k}=\sum_{e|i}\left(\frac{i}{e}\right)^{k}<i^{k}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{k}}=i^{k}\zeta(k)

(5.4)

Since
\zeta(2)\simeq 1.6
and
\zeta(k)\to 1
monotonically as
k\to\infty
, we have
\zeta(k)<2
for all integral
k\geq 2
. Combining this with the obvious lower bound
\sigma_{k}(i)\geq i^{k}
, which is saturated at
i=1
, we get:

i^{k}<\sigma_{k}(i)<\zeta(k)i^{k}<2i^{k},\qquad k\geq 2

(5.5)

5.2
Bounds on the functions
f_{M}(n,i)

In this section, we shall find bounds on the numerator and denominator of
f_{M}(n,i)
as in (
2.15
). This, in turn, will bound
f_{M}(n,i)
itself. For simplicity, we will outline the procedure for
M>0
and for the identity character only, though some equations below will hold generically for
M<0
and for the non-identity character too.

Using the bounds of the previous section, let us find out where the numerator
N_{n,i}
and denominator
D_{n}
change signs, where:

f_{M}(n,i)\equiv\frac{N_{n,i}}{D_{n}}

(5.6)

where
f_{M}(n,i)
was defined in Eq. (
2.15
). For the identity character, we get:

\displaystyle N_{n,i}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{5}{12}(24M+j)(24M+j+4)\sigma_{3}(i)-4\left(n-i-M-\frac{j}{24}\right)\sigma_{1}(i)\equiv A\sigma_{3}(i)-B\sigma_{1}(i)

\displaystyle D_{n}

\displaystyle\equiv n\left(n-2M-\frac{j+2}{12}\right)

(5.7)

We see that
N_{n,i}
vanishes at:

\displaystyle n=\frac{A\sigma_{3}(i)}{4\sigma_{1}(i)}+i+M+\frac{j}{24},

(5.8)

6
Conclusions

We have proved that all quasi-characters of vanishing Wronskian index have alternating signs following the pattern in Table
2
. Two types of proofs were given: one that involves an approximation where the central charge is taken large along with
n
, and the other without any approximation, using induction. The approximation allows us to estimate the growth of the coefficient
a_{2|M|}
, which is the one where the alternating sign stabilises to a positive or negative value. This is a region outside the conventional limit in which the Rademacher formula can be applied. The estimate is particularly accurate for the identity character at
M>0
, as seen in Eq. (
4.43
).

For apparently technical reasons, the case of Wronskian index
\ell=2
seems more difficult to study than
\ell=0

The admissible ones, first identified as CFTs in
[
17
]
and studied further in
[
28

, are also considerably more complicated than the MMS solutions at
\ell=0
.
. The alternating signs of quasi-characters follow a slightly different pattern – in examples, one finds alternation until
n=|M|
, then the reverse alternation (i.e. with one extra negative sign) until
n=2|M|
, followed by stability. If proofs for this case can be found on the lines of what we have presented here

15

We are currently working on this in an upcoming work
[
25
]
and at this stage the results seem quite promising.
, it will amount to a complete understanding of the quasi-characters that generate all rational CFTs regardless of Wronskian index.

The regime where the alternating signs stabilise,
n\sim 2|M|\sim\frac{|c|}{12}
, seems to have the following significance. Suppose we take one particular quasi-character and attempt to make a non-chiral “partition function”
\sum_{i=0}^{1}\chi_{i}{\overline{\chi}}_{i}
from it (this is not admissible and does not correspond to a CFT, but considerations of modular invariance do not know this). This partition function contains a term:

. Now for

[
29
]
) as the boundary between “light” and “medium” states. It is intriguing that the “crossover” takes place at this boundary, perhaps one can find an intuitive reason for this.

Pushing further on the above connections to the irrational case, we note that the:
c\text{ fixed}
and
E\to\infty

[
29
]
(see Eq. (1.3) therein) which is the regime where Cardy growth holds, is equivalent – under the identifications considered in the above paragraph – to the regime of
M\text{ fixed}
and

Additonally, it is interesting to note that, for
1\leq n\lesssim\frac{|c|}{12}
, we get a super-geometric growth indicating a sparseness in the distribution of Fourier coefficients
a_{n}
in this regime. On the other hand, when
n\gg c
(where Rademacher kicks in) we have a geometric growth, implying a denseness of the Fourier coefficients
a_{n}
. This behaviour could potentially shed some light about sparseness/denseness of states in genuine rational CFTs.

One reason for all of the above connections to the irrational case could simply be modularity. To test this, one can compare the behaviour of these
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
quasi-characters to the quasi-characters which were obtained in
[
30
]
as solutions of MLDEs to more generic groups like the Fricke groups of prime level – of which
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
is a sub-group.

Conventional modular forms do not, as far as we know, exhibit the property that the sign of coefficients alternates up to a point where it stabilises, which makes quasi-characters interesting modular objects. But beyond their mathematical interest they are of practical importance because they are a basis for admissible characters that populate the space of solutions to the holomorphic modular bootstrap. Knowledge of signs and growths is crucial in mapping out this space, since in the sum Eq. (
2.17
), specific negative contributions to the coefficient of
q^{n}
have to be overcome by positive contributions from other terms. The general structure of this space of solutions is currently under investigation.

Generalisation to more than two characters is an open problem. In fact, quasi-characters for the three-character case were studied in
[
12
]
, where infinite families of examples were provided. A complete classification should not be too difficult, and it can then be subjected to the same sort of analysis that was carried out here for the case of two characters. The space of three-character admissible VVMF is very different from that of the two-character case – already at
\ell=0
there are infinitely many examples
[
3
]
as against just 7 examples with two characters. Thus, the picture could be quite different in the two cases.

A different approach to finding admissible characters for
\ell\geq 6

[
11

[

[
