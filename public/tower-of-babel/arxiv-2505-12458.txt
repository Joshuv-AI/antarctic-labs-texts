Spectral Decomposition of Euler-Mellin Integrals
Martin Helmer 1 \,{}^{1}, Felix Tellander 2 \,{}^{2}

Abstract
. We consider the spectral decomposition of singularities of integrals and their integrands. Our results apply to any integral of Euler-Mellin type, and thus especially to every scalar Feynman integral. Specifically we provide for both the integrand and integral respectively; two explicit constructions of the characteristic variety and characteristic cycle of the constructible function and D D -module they are associated with. From this we also obtain the singular locus or Landau singularities of the integral. En route we give a simple procedure to compute the local Euler obstruction function of a variety, and using this, to compute the Euler characteristic of the complex link of a Whitney stratum.

Introduction

Feynman integrals are a fundamental object underpinning a wide array of topics and calculations in modern physics
[
1
]
. As such there is significant interest in both understanding their mathematical structures and in computing them effectively. More broadly these integrals fall into the family of integrals of
Euler-Mellin
type, a generalization of the integrals studied in
[
2
,
3
]
; that is integrals of the form

(1)

\mathcal{I}(z):=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}}\frac{x_{1}^{\nu_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{\nu_{n}}}{f_{1}(x,z)^{\lambda_{1}}\cdots f_{p}(x,z)^{\lambda_{p}}}\frac{dx_{1}}{x_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\frac{dx_{n}}{x_{n}}

for any polynomials
f_{i}(x,z)
and fixed (but generic) constants
\lambda_{i}
and
\nu_{j}
where we treat
x
as integration variables and
z
as parameters.

In particular we seek to understand the singularities of the integral (
1
), that is the regions in
\mathbb{C}^{m}\ni z
where the function (or distribution)
\mathcal{I}(z)
fails to be analytic. More precisely we seek to calculate the characteristic variety and cycle of the
D
-module which annihilates
\mathcal{I}(z)
in a practical and effective manner.

In physics integrals of the form (
1
) with a single denominator polynomial
f(x,z)
correspond to scalar Feynman integrals in the Lee-Pomeransky
[
4
]
representation. The singularities of these integrals are called
Landau singularities

[
5
]
and studied in detail in e.g.
[
6
,
7
]
. Knowing these singularities in advance may significantly aid the evaluation of the integrals, for example with the canonical differential equations approach
[
8
]
: They may constrain or even fully predict
[
9
]
the analytic building blocks or (symbol)
letters

[
10
]
of this approach. This turns the derivation of the differential equations from a symbolic, to a much simpler numerical problem. Thanks to their utility, also in other aspects of Feynman integration
[
11
]
, Landau analysis is currently experiencing a revival, see for example
[
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
,

,
9
,

,

,

]
and many others.

The physicist’s Landau singularity is directly related to the characteristic variety of the
D
-module annihilating (
1
) by simply removing the zero-section and projecting onto the space of
z
-variables. In this case, Theorem
A
below gives us the Landau singularities and Theorem
C
(see also Section
5
) implies that the singular locus coincides with the so called
Euler discriminant
at codimension one, something that was recently proved in
[
21
, Theorem 5.3]
.

In this note we will give two recipes which can be used to obtain this characteristic variety, and from it, the characteristic cycle. The first works by computing the blow-up of (a compactification of) the ambient space of the hypersurface defined by
F=f_{1}\cdots f_{p}
along a Jacobian ideal derived from a certain hypersurface associated to
F
. The second employs a coarsening of the Whitney stratification of the union of the (homogenization of the) hypersurface defined by
F
with the coordinate hyperplanes
x_{i}=0
; the coarsening is obtained from certain multiplicity calculations for each strata.

We begin by considering the homogenization
F_{h}
of the product
F=f_{1}\cdots f_{p}
in (
1
) as a polynomial in
x
with respect to the new variable
x_{0}
; we then consider
F_{h}(x,z)
as a polynomial in the ring
\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n},z_{1},\dots,z_{m}]
and define
G=x_{0}\cdots x_{n}F_{h}
.

Consider the blow-up
{\rm Bl}_{{\rm Jac}(G)}(\mathbb{P}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{m})
of
\mathbb{P}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{m}
along the ideal

{\rm Jac}(G)=\langle\frac{\partial G}{\partial x_{0}},\dots,\frac{\partial G}{\partial x_{n}},\frac{\partial G}{\partial z_{1}},\dots,\frac{\partial G}{\partial z_{m}}\rangle,

and let
I_{\rm Bl}
be the ideal defining
{\rm Bl}_{{\rm Jac}(G)}(\mathbb{P}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{m})
in

\mathbb{C}[x,z,\xi,\zeta]=\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n},z_{1},\dots,z_{m},\xi_{0},\dots,\xi_{n},\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{m}].

Then, considering
{\rm Jac}(G)
as an ideal in
\mathbb{C}[x,z,\xi,\zeta]
, the exceptional divisor of the blow-up is defined by the ideal
I_{E}={\rm Jac}(G)+I_{\rm Bl}
.

Theorem A

(Characteristic Variety of Integral via Blow-up)
.

With the notations above, and letting
\mathscr{M}
be the
D
-module which annihilates the integral (
1
), if we set

I=I_{E}\cap I_{\mathbf{Con}(Y)}\cap I_{\mathbf{Con}(0)}\;{\rm and}\;I_{\mathrm{Char}}=(I+\langle\xi_{0},\dots,\xi_{n}\rangle)\cap\mathbb{C}[z,\zeta]

then the characteristic variety of
\mathscr{M}
is given by

\mathrm{Char}(\mathscr{M})=\mathbf{V}(I_{\mathrm{Char}}).

Consider a Whitney stratification
\{S_{\gamma}\}
of a variety
Y\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
of dimension
d
and suppose our chosen stratification has a total of
r
strata with
d_{\alpha}=\dim(S_{\alpha})
. For a pair of strata
S_{\alpha}
,
S_{\beta}
, with
{S}_{\alpha}\subset\overline{S}_{\beta}
, let
\mathbf{Eu}_{\overline{S}_{\beta}}:\overline{S}_{\beta}\to\mathbb{Z}
denote the
local Euler obstruction
function associated to
S_{\beta}
and let
\mathbf{Eu}_{\overline{S}_{\beta}}(S_{\alpha})
be the (constant) integer value
\mathbf{Eu}_{\overline{S}_{\beta}}(x)
for any point
x
in
S_{\alpha}
. If
S_{\alpha}\not\subset\overline{S}_{\beta}
then
\mathbf{Eu}_{\overline{S}_{\beta}}(S_{\alpha})=0
.

Associated to the constructable function
\mathbb{1}_{Y}
we have the
characteristic cycle
which is the cycle class given by the formal sum

CC(\mathbb{1}_{Y})=\sum_{i=1}^{r}m_{\alpha_{i}}\mathbf{Con}(S_{\alpha_{i}}),

where
\mathbf{Con}(S_{\alpha})
is the Zariski closure of the conormal bundle to
S_{\alpha}
and where the integers

are computed by solving the following explicit linear system:

(2)

\hskip-8.5359pt\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\vdots\\
1\end{pmatrix}=\mathbf{Eu}(Y)\begin{pmatrix}m_{\alpha_{1}}\\
\vdots\\
m_{\alpha_{r}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}(-1)^{d-d_{1}}\mathbf{Eu}_{\overline{S}_{\alpha_{1}}}(S_{\alpha_{1}})&\cdots&(-1)^{d-d_{r}}\mathbf{Eu}_{\overline{S}_{\alpha_{r}}}(S_{\alpha_{1}})\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
(-1)^{d-d_{1}}\mathbf{Eu}_{\overline{S}_{\alpha_{1}}}(S_{\alpha_{r}})&\cdots&(-1)^{d-d_{r}}\mathbf{Eu}_{\overline{S}_{\alpha_{r}}}(S_{\alpha_{r}})\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}m_{\alpha_{1}}\\
\vdots\\
m_{\alpha_{r}}\end{pmatrix},

with
d_{i}=d_{\alpha_{i}}=\dim(S_{\alpha_{i}})
. We emphasize that the above is, in fact, an explicit linear system with the entries of the

integer matrix
\mathbf{Eu}(Y)
being computed from the multiplicities of subvarieties in certain

as in (
11
), see Remark
4.2

is monotonically increasing with the index

is upper triangular.

Theorem B

(Characteristic Variety of Integral via Whitney Stratification)
.

Using the notation above consider
G=x_{0}\cdots x_{n}F_{h}
. Let
Y=\mathbf{V}(G)
, take
\{S_{i}\;|\;i=1,\dots,r\}
to be a Whitney stratification of
Y
, and let
m_{1},\dots,m_{r}
denote the corresponding multiplicities obtained by solving the integer linear system (
2
) using the matrix
\mathbf{Eu}(Y)
resulting from this choice of Whitney stratification for
Y
. Set
\Theta\subset\{1,\dots,r\}
to be the subset of indices for which
m_{i}
is non-zero. With
I_{E}
and
I=I_{\rm Bl}\cap I_{\mathbf{Con}(Y)}\cap I_{\mathbf{Con}(0)}
as in Theorem
A
we have that

\mathbf{V}{(I)}=\bigcup_{i\in\Theta}{\mathbf{Con}(S_{i})}\cup\mathbf{Con}(0).

Or in other words, the characteristic variety may be obtained from the parts of the Whitney stratification with non-zero multiplicity.

Our final result employs either one of the two results above and gives an explicit recipe to obtain the characteristic cycle of the integral (
1
). As above consider the polynomial
G=x_{0}\cdots x_{n}F_{h}
in
\mathbb{C}[x,z]

is the homogenization of the product

) with respect to the new variable

, let

be the projection, and let
\mathscr{M}
denote the
D
-module which annihilates the integral (
1

A
or Theorem
B

\mathrm{Char}^{*}(\mathscr{M})
the characteristic variety with the zero-section removed. Considering the projection map

is given by

are evaluated at a generic point

\begin{pmatrix}c(G)+\chi(Y\cap\varphi^{-1}(z_{1}))\\
\vdots\\

\vdots\\
\mu_{\rho}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}(-1)^{d-d_{1}}\mathbf{Eu}_{\overline{W}_{1}}(W_{1})&\cdots&(-1)^{d-d_{\rho}}\mathbf{Eu}_{\overline{W}_{\rho}}(W_{1})\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
(-1)^{d-d_{1}}\mathbf{Eu}_{\overline{W}_{1}}(W_{\rho})&\cdots&(-1)^{d-d_{\rho}}\mathbf{Eu}_{\overline{W}_{\rho}}(W_{\rho})\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mu_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\mu_{\rho}\end{pmatrix},

where

B

Theorem C

(Characteristic Cycle of Integral via Whitney Stratification)
.

Let
\mathscr{M}
be the
D
-module which annihilates the integral (
1
) and let
\mathrm{Char}(\mathscr{M})\subset\mathbb{C}_{z}^{m}\times\mathbb{P}^{m-1}_{\zeta}
be its characteristic variety with the associated projection map
\pi_{z}:\mathbb{C}_{z}^{m}\times\mathbb{P}^{m-1}_{\zeta}\to\mathbb{C}_{z}^{m}
. If
\{W_{i}\;|\,i=1,\dots,\rho\}
is a Whitney stratification of the algebraic variety
Z:={\rm Sing}(\mathscr{M})=\overline{\pi_{z}(\mathrm{Char}^{*}(\mathscr{M}))}\subset\mathbb{C}_{z}^{m}
then the characteristic cycle of
\mathscr{M}
is given by

CC(\mathscr{M})=\mu_{0}\mathbf{Con}(0)+\sum_{i=1}^{\rho}\mu_{i}W_{i},

with
\mu_{0}=c(G)+n+1
,
\mu_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,\rho
are obtained by solving the linear system (
3
) and
\mathrm{Char}(\mathscr{M})
is obtained via Theorem
A
or Theorem
B

2.
Whitney Stratifications and Euler Obstructions

In this section we briefly review several important geometric constructions which will play a key role in our results in later sections.

Let
Z
be a variety of dimension
d
. A
Whitney stratification
of
Z
is a subdivision into a finite number of smooth connected manifolds
S_{\alpha}
, called
strata
, such that
Z=\bigcup_{\alpha}{S_{\alpha}}
and such that
Whitney’s condition
B holds for all pairs
M=S_{\alpha}
,
N=S_{\beta}
, of these manifolds. A pair of strata,
M,N
, is said to be
incident
, denoted
M\preceq N
if
M\subseteq\overline{N}
. Incident strata
M,N
satisfy Whitney’s condition B
[
25
, Section 19]
at a point
x\in M
if for every sequence
\{p_{\ell}\}\subset M
and
\{q_{\ell}\}\subset N
with
\lim p_{\ell}=\lim q_{\ell}=x
,
the limit of secant lines between
p_{\ell},q_{\ell}
is contained in the limit of tangent planes to
N
at
q_{\ell}
. We say the pair
M,N
satisfies condition B if condition B holds at all points
x\in M
. For any
M\not\subset\overline{N}
condition B is vacuously true. When computing Whitney stratifications, and representing them on a computer, it will often be convenient (as in e.g.
[
26
,
22
]
) to represent the Whitney stratification as a flag
Z_{\bullet}
of varieties
Z_{0}\subset\cdots\subset Z_{d}=Z
where the strata are then the connected components of the successive differences
Z_{i}-Z_{i-1}
.

Given a variety
X=\mathbf{V}(f_{1},\dots,f_{m})\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
\dim(X)=d
, with
f_{i}\in\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
, we let
X_{\rm Sing}
denote the singular locus of
X
and let
X_{\rm reg}=X-X_{\rm Sing}
denote the (open) manifold of smooth points in
X
. The
conormal variety
of a variety
X\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
is the subvariety of
X\times\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
given by

(4)

\displaystyle{\rm\bf Con}(X)=\overline{\left\{(p,\xi)\;|\;p\in X_{\rm reg}\text{ and }T_{p}X_{\rm reg}\subset\xi^{\perp}\right\}};

as noted in the introduction
{\rm\bf Con}(X)
can also be thought of as the Zariski closure of the conormal bundle to the smooth part of a variety (where we hence consider the direction vectors as projective to get a unique representative, up to projective equivalence, for each direction).
We note that we can compute equations for
\mathbf{Con}(X)
in a straightforward manner. Work in the ring
\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n},\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}]
and set

\mathfrak{K}=\begin{pmatrix}\xi_{1}&\cdots&\xi_{n}\\
\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial x_{1}}&\cdots&\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial x_{n}}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\frac{\partial f_{m}}{\partial x_{1}}&\cdots&\frac{\partial f_{m}}{\partial x_{n}}\\
\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathfrak{J}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial x_{1}}&\cdots&\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial x_{n}}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\frac{\partial f_{m}}{\partial x_{1}}&\cdots&\frac{\partial f_{m}}{\partial x_{n}}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

Let
K
be the ideal generated by all
(n-d+1)\times(n-d+1)
minors of the matrix
\mathfrak{K}
, and let
J
be the ideal defined by the
(n-d)\times(n-d)
minors of the matrix
\mathfrak{J}
. The conormal variety
{\rm\bf Con}(X)=\mathbf{V}(I_{{\rm\bf Con}(X)})
is defined by the radical ideal given by the saturation
I_{{\rm\bf Con}(X)}=(I_{X}+K):J^{\infty}
. Note also that
X_{\rm Sing}=\mathbf{V}(J)
. Conormal varieties are naturally connected to Whitney stratification, see e.g.
[
26
]
, where an algorithm to compute Whitney a stratification using conormal varieties is given.

A related concept is that of the
polar varieties
of
X
; these are defined as follows. Consider a flag
L_{\bullet}
of length
d

L_{\bullet}=\left(L_{d}\supset L_{d-1}\supset\cdots\supset L_{1}\right),

where each
L_{i}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
is an
i
-dimensional linear subspace.
For each
i
in
{\left\{{0,1,\ldots,d-1}\right\}}
, the dimension
i
polar variety of
X
along the flag
L_{\bullet}
is defined as the closure

P_{i}(X;L_{\bullet}):=\overline{{\left\{{p\in X_{\text{reg}}\mid\dim(T_{p}X_{\text{reg}}\cap L_{i+1})>d-n+i+1}\right\}}}.

For
i=d
we set
P_{d}(X;L_{\bullet})=X
. We may write down the defining equations for the polar varieties as follows. Take

\mathfrak{K}_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}c^{(0)}_{1}&\cdots&c^{(0)}_{n}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
c^{(i)}_{1}&\cdots&c^{(i)}_{n}\\
\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial x_{1}}&\cdots&\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial x_{n}}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\frac{\partial f_{m}}{\partial x_{1}}&\cdots&\frac{\partial f_{m}}{\partial x_{n}}\\
\end{pmatrix},

where the
c^{(i)}_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
are general constants for
i\in{\left\{{0,\dots,d-1}\right\}}
.
Let
K_{i}
be the ideal generated by all
(n-d+i+1)\times(n-d+i+1)
minors of the matrix
\mathfrak{K}_{i}
. Then

(5)

P_{i}(X;L_{\bullet})=\mathbf{V}((K_{i}+I_{X}):J^{\infty}),

where
L_{i+1}
is the linear space spanned by the first
i+1
rows of
\mathfrak{K}_{i}
.

Polar varieties contains a lot of geometric information. For example, they can be used to effectively calculate Whitney stratifications
[
22
]
and reduce them to a canonical coarsest stratification. Also note that the similarity in the equations of the polar and conormal varieties is not co-incidental, and in fact if we take
\pi_{X}:\mathbf{Con}(X)\to X
to be the natural projection, the polar variety
P_{i}(X;L_{\bullet})
is the image of the (closure of the) intersection of
\mathbf{Con}(X)\cap L_{i+1}
under the projection
\pi_{X}
where we treat
L_{i+1}
as an
i
dimensional projective liner space.

Let
Z\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a complex variety, defined by the ideal
I_{Z}
and let
W\subset Z
be an irreducible variety defined by a prime ideal
I_{W}
. Recall that the
multiplicity
of
Z
along

W
, denoted
{\rm mult}_{W}(Z)
, is defined as the Hilbert-Samuel multiplicity
(see e.g.
[
27
, Chapter 12]
) of the local ring

(\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]/I_{Z})_{I_{W}}
at the prime ideal
I_{W}
; note that if
p
is a general point in
W
then
{\rm mult}_{W}(Z)={\rm mult}_{p}(Z)
. If
W\cap Z=\emptyset
we define
{\rm mult}_{W}(Z):=0
. These multiplicities can be computed efficiently using Segre classes
[
23
]
.

In
[
28
, Chapter V, Theorem 1.2]
Teissier shows that two open strata
M,\,N
with
M\subset\overline{N}
satisfy Whitney’s condition B if and only if the sequence of Hilbert-Samuel multiplicities

\mathrm{mult}_{\bullet}(\overline{N},z)=\{\mathrm{mult}_{z}P_{0}(\overline{N};L_{\bullet}),\ldots,\mathrm{mult}_{z}P_{d-1}(\overline{N};L_{\bullet}),\,\mathrm{mult}_{z}\overline{N}\}

is constant for every
z\in M
. Given any Whitney stratification, strata with the same sequence of polar multiplicities can be joined to obtain a unique canonical coarsest Whitney stratification.

Another geometric construction that can be obtained from the polar varieties is the
local Euler obstruction
. The local Euler obstruction (or Euler obstruction) was introduced by MacPherson in his generalization of the Chern class to singular varieties
[
29
]
, an object we now call the Chern-Schwartz-MacPherson (CSM) class. In this context, for a variety
Y
, it is thought of as a constructible function
Y\to\mathbb{Z}
and is shown to take a constant value on each Whitney stata of
Y
.

The same invariant was also introduced by Kashiwara a year or so earlier
[
30
]
and we will use his recursive definition of this invariant. Let
Y
be a variety of dimension
d
, fix a Whitney stratification
Y=\cup S_{\alpha}
, with
d_{\alpha}=\dim(S_{\alpha})
, and denote the Euler obstruction of
\overline{S}_{\gamma}
along
S_{\alpha}
as
\mathbf{Eu}_{\overline{S}_{\gamma}}(S_{\alpha})
; as noted above the Euler obstruction takes a constant value on strata hence
\mathbf{Eu}_{\overline{S}_{\gamma}}(x)=\mathbf{Eu}_{\overline{S}_{\gamma}}(S_{\alpha})
for all
x\in S_{\alpha}
. We define
\mathbf{Eu}_{\overline{S}_{\alpha}}(S_{\alpha})=1
and for non-incident strata
S_{\alpha}
and
\overline{S}_{\beta}
we have
\mathbf{Eu}_{\overline{S}_{\beta}}(S_{\alpha})=0
. Recursively we now define

(6)

\mathbf{Eu}_{\overline{S}_{\gamma}}(S_{\alpha})=\sum_{S\alpha\prec S_{\beta}\preceq S_{\gamma}}\mathbf{Eu}_{\overline{S}_{\gamma}}(S_{\beta})c(S_{\alpha},\,S_{\beta}),\qquad c(S_{\alpha},\,S_{\beta})=\chi(B(x,\epsilon)\cap S_{\beta}\cap H_{\eta})

where
c(S_{\alpha},\,S_{\beta})
is the Euler characteristic of a ball centered at
x\in S_{\alpha}
with radius
\epsilon
intersected with
S_{\beta}
intersected with a generic linear affine space of codimension
\mathrm{dim}S_{\alpha}+1
at a distance
\eta
from
x
, where
\epsilon>>\eta>0
. This space is closely related to the
complex link
of Goresky-MacPherson
[
31
, Part I, Sec. 2.2]
, the crucial difference is that the complex link is the intersection with an algebraic variety and not an open strata as above:

(7)

CL_{\overline{S}_{\beta}}(S_{\alpha}):=B(x,\epsilon)\cap\overline{S}_{\beta}\cap H_{\eta}.

Using a special case of Kashiwara’s index theorem
[
30
,
32
,
33
]
, which we will describe in Section
4
, we have

(8)

\mathbb{1}_{Y}(S_{\alpha})=\sum_{S_{\alpha}\preceq S_{\beta}}(-1)^{d-d_{\alpha}}m_{\beta}\mathbf{Eu}_{\overline{S}_{\beta}}(S_{\alpha})

which we can invert to

(9)

(-1)^{d-d_{\alpha}}m_{\alpha}=1-\sum_{S_{\alpha}\prec S_{\beta}}c(S_{\alpha},\,S_{\beta})

and using inclusion/exclusion relations for the Euler characteristic we obtain finally

(10)

(-1)^{d-d_{\alpha}}m_{\alpha}=1-\chi(CL_{Y}(S_{\alpha})).

It is well-known in stratified Morse theory that the complex link is constant on Whitney strata. The sign convention here is chosen to guarantee that
m_{\alpha}\geq 0
for perverse sheaves.

We know from the work of Teissier that the multiplicities of the polar varieties of
\overline{S}_{\beta}
are constant on
S_{\alpha}

[
28
]
. Let
P_{l}(\overline{S}_{\beta})
be the polar variety of dimension
l
with respect to a generic linear flag
L_{\bullet}
. We denote the multiplicity of this polar variety at a general point
p
in
S_{\alpha}
as
{\rm mult}_{\alpha}(P_{l}(\overline{S}_{\beta})):={\rm mult}_{p}(P_{l}(\overline{S}_{\beta}))
. For pair of strata,
S_{\alpha},S_{\beta}
, with
S_{\alpha}\subset\overline{S_{\beta}}
, the polar multiplicity
{\rm mult}_{\alpha}(P_{l}(\overline{S}_{\beta})=0
for
l\leq d_{\alpha}
. The Euler obstruction can now be calculated from the polar multiplicities
[
34
, Corollary 5.1.2]

(11)

\mathbf{Eu}_{\overline{S}_{\beta}}(S_{\alpha})=\sum_{k=d_{\alpha}+1}^{d_{\beta}}(-1)^{d_{\beta}-k}{\rm mult}_{\alpha}(P_{k}(\overline{S}_{\beta})).

If
\cup S_{\alpha}
is a Whitney stratification of the hypersurface
Y=\mathbf{V}(f)
we denote by
p_{x}(k)
the multiplicity at
x
of the
relative
polar variety
P_{k}(f)
which are also constant along Whitney strata so we can label the multiplicity as
p_{\alpha}(P_{k}(f))
. These numbers are also recursively defined by the normal polar multiplicities:

Proposition 2.1

(
[
35
, Proposition 5.1.1]
)
.

For
k\leq d_{\alpha}
we have
p_{\alpha}(P_{k}(f))=0
and for
k>d_{\alpha}

(12)

p_{\alpha}(P_{k}(f))=(-1)^{n-k}+\sum_{S_{\alpha}\prec S_{\beta}:k\leq d_{\beta}}p_{\beta}(P_{d_{\beta}+1}(f))\left(\sum_{l=k}^{d_{\beta}}(-1)^{k-l}{\rm mult}_{\alpha}(P_{l}(\overline{S}_{\beta}))\right).

It follows by direct substitution that in the special case
k=d_{\alpha}+1
we have
m_{\alpha}=p_{\alpha}(P_{d_{\alpha}+1}(f))
.

3.
Microlocal Analysis of
D
-modules and Annihilator Ideals

The Weyl algebra
D
on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
is given by

(13)

D:=R\left<\partial_{1},\ldots,\partial_{n}\right>,\quad R=\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]

where
[x_{i},\,x_{j}]=[\partial_{i},\,\partial_{j}]=0
and
\partial_{i}x_{j}=x_{j}\partial_{i}+\delta_{ij}
for all
1\leq i,j\leq n
. To replace
\mathbb{C}^{n}
with a general manifold
X
, the ring
R
is replaced by the sheaf of regular functions
\mathscr{O}_{X}
and
D
is replaced by
\mathscr{D}_{X}
, the sheaf of
\mathbb{C}
-linear differential operators on
\mathscr{O}_{X}
. A system of partial differential equations
P_{1}u=0,\ldots,P_{k}u=0
is encoded as the left-ideal
I=\left<P_{1},\ldots,P_{k}\right>
and generates the
D
-module
M=D/I.

For a non-zero element
p
in the Weyl algebra we define the
principal symbol
as the following initial form (cf.
[
36
, Chapter 1]
):

(14)

\sigma(p):=\mathrm{in}_{(0,1)}(p).

This is an element in the associated graded ring
\mathrm{gr}_{(0,1)}(D)=\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}]
which is a commutative ring in
2n
variables. For any left-ideal
I
in a Weyl algebra we can similarly define the
characteristic ideal
as the ideal containing all principal symbols of
p\in I
:

(15)

\mathrm{in}_{(0,1)}(I):=\{\sigma(p)\,|\,p\in I\}.

The
characteristic variety
describes regions where the solutions to the system of PDEs (partial differential equations) associated to
I
may be singular and is simply the variety defined by the characteristic ideal above, i.e.

\mathrm{Char}(I):=\mathbf{V}(\mathrm{in}_{(0,1)}(I)).

The characteristic ideal can directly be calculated using non-commutative Gröbner bases in e.g.
Macaulay2

[
24
]
and it is a theorem of Ōaku
[
37
]
that this definition coincides with the analytic
D
-module definition using good filtrations for sheaves of modules. A
D
-ideal (or module) is said to be
holonomic
if
\dim(\mathrm{Char}(I))=n
. All
D
-modules of interest in this paper are of an even nicer type called
regular holonomic
, however, we do not give the technical definition here, see e.g.
[
36
, Definition 2.4.1]
or
[
38
, Definition 5.2]
for two different but equivalent definitions.

Let
\{\Lambda_{\alpha}\}
denote the irreducible components of
\mathrm{Char}(I)
, or the irreducible components of the characteristic variety of any
D
-module
\mathscr{M}
. We the construct the algebraic cycle

(16)

CC(\mathscr{M})=\sum_{\alpha}m_{\alpha}\Lambda_{\alpha}

called the
characteristic cycle
where the multiplicities
m_{\alpha}
are unique positive integers. For an intrinsic
D
-module definition of
m_{\alpha}
see
[
39
, Proposition 1.8.2]
. For holonomic modules
CC(\mathscr{M})
is a Lagrangian cycle. For a variety
Y
one may also define the characteristic cycle of the constructable function
\mathbb{1}_{Y}
, in this case the multiplicities
m_{\alpha}
above are given by (
10
); see Section
4
for further discussion.

3.1.
Characteristic Varieties and Spectral Properties of Distributions

The spectral decomposition of a distribution
u\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
gives complete microlocal information on its singularity structure. This decomposition is called the
wave front set
and describes not only where a distribution is singular but which components in the cotangent space are causing this behavior. We call a set
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus 0

conic
if
(x,\xi)\in\Lambda\implies(x,\lambda\xi)\in\Lambda
for all real
\lambda\neq 0
. This means that we could think of
\Lambda
as being in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
. For the construction of the analytic wave front set we follow the presentation by Treves
[
40
, Section 3.5]
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
v
be a distribution with compact support in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We say that
v
is
microanalytic
at a point
(x^{\prime},\xi^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus 0
if there is a conic neighborhood
\Lambda\ni(x^{\prime},\xi^{\prime})
and positive real numbers
\kappa,\,c
such that

(17)

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{i\xi\cdot(x-y)-\kappa|\xi||x-y|^{2}}\nu(y)\Delta_{\kappa}(x-y,\xi)\,dy\right|\lesssim e^{-c|\xi|}

where
\Delta_{\kappa}(x,\xi):=1+i\kappa(x\cdot\xi)/|\xi|
holds for all
(x,\xi)\in\Lambda
with
|\xi|
sufficiently large.

For a distribution
u\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
, with not necessarily compact support, we say that it is microanalytic at a point
(x^{\prime},\xi^{\prime})\in\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus 0
if there is a distribution
v
with compact support in
\Omega
equal to
u
in some neighborhood of
x^{\prime}
and microanalytic at
(x^{\prime},\xi^{\prime})
.

Definition 3.2
.

For a distribution
u\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
we define the
analytic wave front set

WF_{A}(u)
as the closed subset of
\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus 0
consisting of points
(x^{\prime},\xi^{\prime})
at which
u
is
not
microanalytic.

The image of
WF_{A}(u)
under the canonical projection
\pi:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus 0\to\Omega:(x,\xi)\mapsto x
is precisely the set where
u
fails to be an analytic function.

We will now see that the analytic wave front set of a distribution is closely related to the characteristic variety of the differential operators that annihilate it. Let
P
be a differential operator of order
m
with analytic coefficients defined in an open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. It has the form

P=P(x,\partial)=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}a_{\alpha}(x)\partial^{\alpha}.

The principal symbol of
P
, denoted
\sigma_{m}
is defined by

(18)

\sigma_{m}(x,\xi)=\sum_{|\alpha|=m}a_{\alpha}(x)\xi^{\alpha}.

For polynomial coefficients this coincides with (
14
). The following result is by Hörmander
[
41
, Theorem 8.6.1]
.

Theorem 3.3
.

If
P
is a differential operator of order
m
with analytic coefficients, then

(19)

WF_{A}(u)\subset\mathrm{Char}^{*}(P)\cup WF_{A}(Pu),\quad u\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega),

where
\mathrm{Char}^{*}(P)=\{(x,\,\xi)\in T^{*}\Omega\setminus 0\,|\,\sigma_{m}(x,\xi)=0\}
.

In particular if
P
annihilates
u
, i.e.
Pu=0
, we have
WF_{A}(u)\subset\mathrm{Char}^{*}(P)
. This directly generalizes to the following result.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
\mathscr{M}
be the
\mathscr{D}_{\Omega}
-module consisting of all elements annihilating
u
, then
WF_{A}(u)\subset\mathrm{Char}(\mathscr{M})\setminus 0\cap(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus 0)
.

3.2.
Annihilator Ideals

Extending the coefficient field of the Weyl algebra from
\mathbb{C}
to polynomials
\mathbb{C}[s]
in a new commuting variable
s
, we get the algebra
D[s]:=D\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{C}[s]
. Denote by
\mathrm{Ann}_{D[s]}(f^{s})
the left-ideal

(20)

\mathrm{Ann}_{D[s]}(f^{s}):=\{Q\in D[s]\,|\,Q\bullet f^{s}=0\}

for any
s\in\mathbb{C}
and any
x
with
f(x)\neq 0
, this is the
parametric annihilator
. It is a result by Bernstein
[
42
]
that for a non-zero polynomial
f(x)
there exists a non-zero polynomial
b(s)
and differential operator
P(s)\in D[s]
such that

(21)

b(s)f(x)^{s}=P(s)\bullet f(x)^{s+1}.

When
b(s)
is chosen to be the monic polynomial of lowest possible degree, it is called the
Bernstein-Sato
polynomial.

The actual problem we are interested in is finding the annihilators of
f^{\lambda}
with
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
being treated as a fixed number. Replacing
s\to\lambda
in
\mathrm{Ann}_{D[s]}(f^{s})
give elements that annihilates
f^{\lambda}
, however, these elements might not fully generate
\mathrm{Ann}_{D}(f^{\lambda})
. The following theorem fully classifies when direct replacement provides the correct answer.

Theorem 3.5

(
[
36

5.1.
Integration of Distributions

In this section we are interested in the integration of regular holonomic distributions.

Definition 5.2
.

A distribution

is said to be

if its annihilator ideal defines a regular holonomic
D
-module.

As every regular holonomic distribution is tempered (cf.
[
63

), they can always be extended to projective space
\mathbb{R}_{x}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{z}^{m}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}_{x}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{z}^{m}
so that the projection
\varphi:\mathbb{P}_{x}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{z}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}_{z}^{m}\,:\,(x,z)\mapsto z
is proper. If
u(x,z)
is a regular holonomic distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
, there exists a sheaf of left ideals
\mathscr{I}
of
\mathscr{D}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{m}}
such that
\mathscr{D}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{m}}/\mathscr{I}
is a sheaf of regular holonomic
\mathscr{D}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{m}}
-modules. If
H
is any hyperplane and we identify
\mathbb{C}^{n}
with
\mathbb{P}^{n}-H
, then
\mathscr{I}|_{\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{m}}
is equal to the sheaf of left ideals in
\mathscr{D}_{\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{m}}
annihilating
u
. Using
[
64
, Proposition 5]
there is a global section
u_{h}
in
\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathscr{D}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}}}(\mathscr{D}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{m}}/\mathscr{I},\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{P}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{m}))
extending
u
. This means that we identify the integration of
u
against the measure
dx/x
with the integration of
u_{h}
against the canonical one-form
\Omega
given by

\Omega=\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-1-i}dx_{0}/x_{0}\wedge\cdots\wedge\widehat{dx_{i}}/x_{i}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx_{n}/x_{n}.

This identification is well-known in the physics literature where it goes under the Cheng-Wu theorem
[
1
, Chapter 2, Theorem 1]
. We therefore make the identification between the following pairings

\left<\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}u\frac{dx}{x},\,\phi(x,z)\right>:=\left<\int_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}u_{h}\,\Omega,\,\phi_{h}(x_{0},x,z)\right>:=\left<\varphi_{*}(u_{h}),\phi_{h}\right>=\left<u_{h},\,\varphi^{*}(\phi_{h})\right>

where
\phi
is a function with rapid decrease in the Schwartz space
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}_{x}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{z}^{m})
,
\phi_{h}
its homogenization in
x
and
u_{h}
contains a factor
x_{0}^{\nu_{0}}
where

is fixed by projective invariance.

As our objective is to make sense of the integral (
1
) and in particular the Feynman integral in Lee-Pomeransky form:

(51)

\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}}\frac{x_{1}^{\nu_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{\nu_{n}}}{(f(x,z)-i0)^{\lambda}}\frac{dx_{1}}{x_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\frac{dx_{n}}{x_{n}},

we have to make sure that the integrand can be understood as a regular holonomic distribution so that the above construction applies.

We first change the integration domain from
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by defining the the tempered distribution

x_{+}^{\nu}=x^{\nu}\ \mathrm{for}\ x>0,\quad x_{+}^{\nu}=0\ \mathrm{for}\ x\leq 0.

In the Feynman integral the meaning of

is as follows. Let

be a polynomial with real coefficients. For a complex parameter

we then define the distributions

and similarly

For

the right-hand side in (

and

Distributions can famously not always be multiplied. A sufficient condition for multiplication to be allowed is given by Hörmander’s product theorem
[

, this condition states that the product of two distributions

(the colloquial term for this phenomenon is that the wave fronts

(53)

\displaystyle WF_{A}((x\pm i0)^{\nu})

\displaystyle\subseteq\{(0;\,\xi)\,|\,\xi\gtrless 0\},\qquad WF_{A}(x_{\pm}^{\nu})\subseteq\{(0;\xi)\,|\,\xi\in\mathbb{R}\setminus 0\},

(54)

\displaystyle WF_{A}\left(\frac{1}{(f\pm i0)^{\lambda}}\right)

\displaystyle\subseteq\bigcup_{\alpha}\mathbf{Con}(S_{\alpha}),

where

is the subset of the Whitney stratification of the hypersurface
\mathbf{V}(f)
appearing with non-zero multiplicity in (

). It is not hard to construct examples where the analytic wave front sets of the integrand in (

to be the polynomial defining the Whitney umbrella, see Example

It is clear from Theorem

it was claimed without proof that this product is well-defined. A more general theorem with a proof was given in

\mathbf{Eu}(Z)
. It remains to compute the integers
c(G)
and
\chi(Y\cap\varphi^{-1}(z_{i}))
. We first note that the Euler characteristics of affine or projective complex varieties (and of Zariski open subsets of affine or projective varieties via the inclusion/exclusion property of Euler characteristics) may be computed with any algorithm to compute the Euler characteristic of a projective variety (e.g.
[
70
]
, as implemented in
[
71
]
). Hence the main practical question remaining is how to deal with picking random
z_{i}
of the required type which can be effectively represented on a computer (i.e. most practical sampling methods deliver floating point numbers as output which can then not be used as input into the Euler characteristic calculation in a straightforward way).

First consider the integer
\chi((\mathbb{C}^{*})^{n}-\mathbf{V}(x_{1}\cdots x_{n}f_{1}(x,z_{0})\cdots f_{p}(x,z_{0})))
appearing in
c(G)
; in this case, since we seek a general element
z_{0}
of
\mathbb{C}^{m}
not in
Z
we may simply choose a random vector of rational numbers (since
Z
is a proper subvariety of
\mathbb{C}^{m}
). Next consider the integers
\chi(Y\cap\varphi^{-1}(z_{i}))
, where
z_{i}
is a general element in
W_{i}
. Let
I_{W_{i}}
denote the radical ideal defining
\overline{W_{i}}.
One remedy for this is as follows:

(1)

Take
d_{i}=\dim(W_{i})
general linear equations
\ell_{j}
defining the idea
L_{i}=\langle\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d_{i}}\rangle
in
\mathbb{C}[z]
, so that
\mathbf{V}(L_{i})
is a general linear space of codimension
d_{i}
in
\mathbb{C}^{m}_{z}
. In practice we can construct
\ell_{j}
by taking random rational coefficients, and hence can represent
L_{i}
on a computer.

(2)

Compute the Euler characteristic of
Y\cap\mathbf{V}(I_{W_{i}}+L_{i})
where we abuse notation and consider the ideals
I_{W_{i}}
and
L_{i}
both as ideals in
\mathbb{C}[z]
and in
\mathbb{C}[x,z]
. Note that by construction
\mathbf{V}(I_{W_{i}}+L_{i})\subset\mathbb{C}^{m}_{z}
, considered as an variety in
\mathbb{C}^{m}_{z}
, consists of exactly
\deg(\overline{W_{i}})
many points; let
\Theta_{i}
denote this finite set of
\deg(\overline{W_{i}})
many general points in
W_{i}
. It follows that
Y\cap\mathbf{V}(I_{W_{i}}+L_{i})=Y\cap\varphi^{-1}(\Theta_{i})
.

(3)

Hence we have

\chi(Y\cap\varphi^{-1}(z_{i}))=\frac{\chi(Y\cap\mathbf{V}(I_{W_{i}}+L_{i}))}{\deg(\overline{W_{i}})}.
