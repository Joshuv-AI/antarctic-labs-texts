Taylor’s hypothesis and its impact on flux measurements in a forest clearcut


Abstract
Taylor’s hypothesis is the backbone to convert observations done over time to spatial information of the flow while carrying out turbulence measurements on a micrometeorological tower. To address its validity over a highly heterogeneous forest clearcut surface, we utilize an extensive Distributed Temperature Sensing (DTS) and Eddy Covariance (EC) datasets. The DTS measured space-time correlation curves of temperature fluctuations are used to compute the bulk convective speeds ( U C U_{C} ) of temperature structures in buoyant conditions at a height of ≈ \approx 3.1 m above the clearing. These convective speeds are compared with the mean wind speed ( U U ) and turbulent intensities of streamwise velocities ( σ u / U \sigma_{u}\textfractionsolidus U ) obtained from the EC system at the middle of the clearcut. Depending on if U U is parallel or perpendicular to the forest edge, the relationships between U C / U U_{C}\textfractionsolidus U and σ u / U \sigma_{u}\textfractionsolidus U are significantly different. However, irrespective of the wind direction, the convective speeds of temperature structures at inertial subrange scales behave in a power-law fashion with increasing wavenumbers. The exponent of this power-law ( − 0.8 -0.8 ) differs from a homogeneous atmospheric surface layer flow ( − 1 / 3 -1/3 ), thereby pointing towards the effects of heterogeneity. The scale-dependent convective speeds non-linearly transform the temporal frequencies to streamwise wavenumbers, which, eventually, impacts the properties of turbulence (co) spectra. More importantly, this non-linear transformation yields a critical frequency limit, beyond which the eddy length scales derived from frequencies are smaller than the physical dimension of the sonic anemometers, and therefore, cannot be faithfully resolved. This critical limit questions the EC flux estimates beyond this frequency.

\statement

The two major components of the Earth’s surface energy budget are sensible and latent heat fluxes. Sans spatial information, the accuracy of these flux measurements is tied to Taylor’s hypothesis, which states that the space-time conversion of any turbulence dataset can be accomplished through the local mean wind speed. We test Taylor’s hypothesis over a highly heterogeneous forest clearcut surface by using fiber-optic cables that acquired air temperatures at high spatial and temporal resolutions. From this data, we estimate the speeds of turbulent eddies at each spatial scale of the flow and derive a critical frequency limit beyond which the space-time conversion is highly uncertain. This critical limit questions the turbulent flux estimates beyond this frequency.

1
Introduction

The quantification of vertical turbulent transport of heat, matter (e.g. moisture,
\rm CO_{2}
) and momentum are important for understanding the land-atmosphere interaction, energy budget of the Earth’s surface, and carbon and water exchanges between the ecosystem and atmosphere. Under the assumptions of homogeneity and stationary conditions, these vertical transports can be interpreted as surface fluxes, which eventually serve as boundary conditions for the Earth System Models. Given such various applications, the micrometeorological towers at different locations across the globe routinely monitor these fluxes under the aegis of various programs such as FLUXNET, AmeriFlux, NEON, and ICOS
(
Baldocchi et al. 2001
;
Novick et al. 2018
;
Metzger et al. 2019
;
Heiskanen et al. 2022
)
. Although the turbulent eddies facilitating the vertical transport exist physically in space, spatial information of atmospheric turbulent flows are rarely available and thus the instruments mounted on micrometeorological towers register the turbulent variables over time
(
Hilland and Christen 2024
)
. To circumvent this issue, Taylor’s frozen turbulence hypothesis is used to convert the temporal information to spatial ones. This hypothesis states that the turbulent structures of any spatial dimension move past the measurement location with a convection velocity in a frozen state, i.e. their characteristics change slowly compared to the speed at which the mean flow transports them along. In the absence of spatial information, it remains experimentally challenging to verify the accuracy of the Taylor’s hypothesis (TH) under various land-surface conditions.

Despite so, a handful of previous studies attempted to verify TH either through theoretical formulations or by conducting novel field experiments that provided spatial information. From the theoretical side, the early study of
Wyngaard and Clifford 1977
used a model developed by
Lumley 1965
to investigate the effects of TH on the turbulence statistics at smaller scales of the flow. In particular,
Wyngaard and Clifford 1977
provided theoretical formulations that accounted for the deviation from the assumption of constant convective speeds across all scales of the flow. Later on,
Tong 1996
experimentally verified their results by using two spatially separated ultrasonic anemometers. Except a few experimental set-ups, spatially explicit sonic anemometer measurements are not readily available
(
Han et al. 2019
)
. Some studies deployed thermocouples to test TH but their spatial resolution was rather limited
(
Deardorff and Willis 1982
;
Everard et al. 2021
)
. However, with the advent of novel instrumentation, spatial data of turbulent variables can be obtained at high enough resolution either from remote sensing or through fiber optic cables. In that regard,
Higgins et al. 2012
used a Raman Lidar to investigate the convective speeds of temperature and moisture structures over a landscape surrounding a water body. On the other hand,
Cheng et al. 2017
used distributed temperature sensing (DTS) measurements over a grassland to verify the hypothesis for different atmospheric stability conditions.

The DTS measurement technique uses fiber optic cables and relies on Raman scattering principle to convert the back-scattered energy from the emitted light pulses to temperature values at specific locations in space
(
Peltola et al. 2021
)
. By laying out these cables either horizontally or vertically, it is therefore possible to obtain temperature data at a fine enough spatial (of the order of tens of cm) and temporal resolutions (of the order of seconds).
Cheng et al. 2017
used this spatial temperature data from a horizontal configuration to evaluate scale-dependent convective speeds of turbulent structures. For their experimental dataset over a flat grass surface, they found that at smaller scales of the flow, the turbulent structures moved at speeds that scaled with the streamwise wavenumbers (
\kappa
) as
{(\kappa)}^{-1/3}
. To reach this conclusion,
Cheng et al. 2017
adopted the technique of
Del Alamo and Jiménez 2009
, where differences in Fourier phases of spatial temperature signals at two subsequent instant of time were used to estimate the wavenumber-dependent convection speeds.
Cheng et al. 2017
also provided theoretical support to this
-1/3
exponent by borrowing formulations from the literature of homogeneous and isotropic turbulence. They hypothesized that these scale-dependent convective speeds could enhance the turbulent fluxes by 10-20% at smaller scales of the flow, which, eventually, could solve the crisis of surface energy imbalance.

In recent times, DTS measurements were used over a flat and homogeneous Namib desert surface to verify TH
(
Hilland and Christen 2024
)
.
Hilland and Christen 2024
found that the TH was almost never satisfied in atmospheric surface layer flows under various stability conditions (ranging from buoyancy- to shear-dominated flows), and the measurement height was an important parameter in that regard. Contrary to
Cheng et al. 2017
,
Hilland and Christen 2024
used a correlation-cube methodology to estimate the scale-dependent convective speeds. In their method, the definition of scales is not related to streamwise wavenumbers in a Fourier domain. In spite of this,
Hilland and Christen 2024
found that the convective speeds of smaller-scale turbulent structures were indeed lower than the bulk convective speeds of larger-scale structures. Be that as it may,
Hilland and Christen 2024
did not report any power-law variations in their estimates of scale-dependent convective speeds.

All these studies described above were conducted over homogeneous atmospheric surface layer flows. However, in reality, one mostly encounter strong heterogeneity in land-surface conditions, which presents a significant challenge to the atmospheric community
(
Bou-Zeid et al. 2020
)
. One of such examples is a forest clearcut. Clearcutting is a widely utilized forest management practice to collect timber and in some cases also to restrict the spread of wildfires by limiting the access to the fuel
(
Ortega et al. 2024
)
. From a fluid dynamical perspective, clearcutting introduces additional complexities to the air flows. For instance, when the air blows directly from the forest canopy sub-layers to clearcut location, it encounters a significant change in surface roughness, which causes the flow to deviate from being in equilibrium with local surface characteristics. The laboratory experiments of
Chung and Koseff 2021
over an artificial heterogeneous canopy showed that these deviations are often caused by the canopy-scale eddies extending their footprints on the clearcut surface depending on the distance from the canopy edge. On the other hand, the field-experimental results of
Detto et al. 2008
provided evidence that the flow near the forest edge shares several attributes with a backward-facing step flow in which a recirculation zone dominates the turbulence statistics.

From a climate science standpoint, the forests are strong sources and sinks of water vapor and carbon dioxide, respectively, thereby influencing the water- and carbon-cycles between the atmosphere and the ecosystem. Forest clearcutting clearly impacts these exchange processes and the climatic impact of such forest management practices can be observed with the eddy covariance (EC) technique
(
Tikkasalo et al. 2025
)
. However, due to the aforementioned complexities, the turbulent behavior of the air flow in forest clearcuts pose challenges while interpreting the observed flux values. One of such aspects is regarding the applicability of TH. For instance, if the convective speeds of turbulent structures are indeed scale-dependent then it can have serious consequences on the EC flux measurements as hypothesized by
Cheng et al. 2017
. Furthermore, the relation derived by
Cheng et al. 2017
to describe the scale-dependent convective speeds was under the assumption of homogeneous and isotropic turbulence. A recent study by
Chowdhuri and Banerjee 2024
revealed that the turbulence is hardly isotropic at smaller scales of the flow corresponding to the heights closer to the surface. Moreover, it is uncertain whether the assumption of homogeneity would remain valid in highly heterogeneous conditions, such as in a forest clearcut. To address these pertinent issues, our objectives in this study are primarily threefold:

1.

Estimate the bulk convective speeds of turbulent structures from the DTS data and use that information to validate TH.

2.

Investigate how the convective speeds vary with increasing wavenumbers.

3.

Quantify the impact of scale-dependent convective speeds on turbulence statistics and discuss its implications for EC flux measurements and surface energy balance.

To tackle these objectives, we employ an extensive dataset encompassing both DTS and EC measurements, collected during a five-month field campaign in a forest clearcut. In our analysis, we specifically consider two scenarios depending on whether the direction of the mean wind was perpendicular or parallel to the forest edge. In the latter scenario, when the air blows parallel to the forest edge, it does not encounter sharp changes in surface roughness, and therefore, is expected to behave differently than a forest-edge flow. To estimate the convective speeds of the turbulent structures, the space-time correlation curves are constructed from the DTS temperature measurements. This method has been adopted previously for laboratory
(
Deardorff and Willis 1982
)
, large eddy simulation
(
Su et al. 2000
)
, and field-experimental
(
Han and Zhang 2022
)
datasets. In addition to that, we introduce a novel concept of conditional space-time correlation curves to separately estimate the convective speeds of positive and negative temperature structures. More importantly, we develop a comprehensive framework that accounts for deviations from TH on turbulence spectra and cospectra. This framework, in general, can be applied to situations other than a forest clearcut. The rest of this article is organized in three different sections. In Sect.
2
we describe our measurement set up and methodologies being adopted to verify TH. In Sect.
3
, we present and discuss our results, and in Sect.
4
future research directions are outlined.

2.2
Measurement setup

The spatial and temporal variability of air temperature above the clearcut was measured with the distributed temperature sensing (DTS) technique
Thomas and Selker 2021
. The DTS instrument (Ultima-S, 5 km variant, Silixa Ltd., Hertfordshire, UK) was housed in a temperature controlled cabin in the middle of the forest clearcut (see Fig.
1
b). The measurements were conducted using double-ended configuration and signals from both ends of the fiber-optic cable (forward and reverse signals) were stored for later processing (Sect.
2.3
). The DTS instrument provided observations with 1.2 s temporal and 12.7 cm spatial resolution along the attached cable. Note that in double-ended configuration both ends are measured sequentially and hence after processing the temperature observations were available with 2.3 s resolution.

Two calibration locations were created next to the DTS instrument and both ends of the cable were guided through them providing altogether four calibration sections in the data. The calibration sections were at minimum 10 meters long. Cold calibration point was created by digging the cable to 0.7 m depth in the peat soil. This depth was below the soil water level and hence the temperatures at that depth did not vary rapidly. Typically, the temperatures were below the air temperature and varied between 2.9
{}^{\circ}C
and 10.7
{}^{\circ}C
during the campaign. Warm calibration point was created with 50-l calibration bath filled with water and it was located in the temperature controlled cabin. Water heater was installed on June 28 and it was set to keep the water at 30
∘
C. Prior to that the water temperature followed indoor air temperature in the temperature controlled cabin. Temperatures at the calibration locations were monitored with reference thermometers (PT-100) supplied with the DTS instrument.

A white, thin (outer diameter 0.9 mm) aramid reinforced 50
\mu
m multimode fiber-optic cable (AFL Telecommunications, Duncan, SC 29334, US) was utilized in this study. The cable was arranged in six sections and along each section the temperature values were doubly measured. This was because the measurements were made with a single continuous cable traversing back and forth the different sections (Fig.
1
). Sections 1 to 5 were aligned with the forest edge located in South-West direction of the clearcut and the section lengths were between 97 m and 103 m. The cable sections 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 were approximately at a 20 m, 47 m, 75 m, 115 m and 137 m distance from the forest edge in SW direction, respectively. The sixth section was perpendicular to the other cable sections and to the forest edge and its length was 164 m, out of which 27 m was within the forest. Therefore, the total cable length was 1478 m.

The cable was fastened to wooden support structures and kept at a constant height throughout the measurement domain. The cable locations were determined with a handheld GNSS device (Geo 7X, Trimble Inc., USA). This height was around 3.1 m above the soil, which is similar to the height of the location where eddy covariance (EC) measurements were made (see Fig.
1
b). However, due to the heterogeneity in the surface conditions (see Sect.
2.1
), local DTS measurement height varied and was approximately between 2 to 4 m above the soil and between 0.5 to 2.5 m above the local roughness elements (e.g. vegetation). Moreover, occasionally the cable was also surrounded by the few isolated trees left at the clearcut.

To quantify the turbulent fluxes over such a complex surface, the EC equipment consisted of a 3-D sonic anemometer (uSonic-3 Cage MP, METEK GmbH, Germany) and an Infrared gas analyzer (LI-7200RS, LI-COR Biosciences, NE, USA). These together measured the three wind velocity components, sonic temperature, and carbon dioxide and water vapor mixing ratios at the same height as DTS, i.e. 3.1 m above the soil. The infrared gas analyzer sampled the flow next to the sonic anemometer and all the EC data were logged with 10-Hz frequency. See more details on the EC measurement setup in
Tikkasalo et al. 2025
.

2.3
DTS data processing

DTS data were post-field calibrated with Python (3.11.9) scripts and the processing followed

Van De Giesen et al. 2012
and
Peltola et al. 2022

(

)

T(s,t)=\frac{\gamma}{\ln{\frac{P_{s}(s,t)}{P_{as}(s,t)}}+C(t)+\int_{0}^{s}\alpha(s^{\prime})ds^{\prime}},

(1)

where
T=T(s,t)
is the calibrated temperature at location
s
along the fiber-optic cable at time step
t
,
\gamma
and
C(t)
are calibration parameters, and
\int_{0}^{s}\alpha(s^{\prime})ds^{\prime}
describes the differential attenuation of
P_{s}
and
P_{as}
along the fiber. This integral was estimated from the forward and reverse signals for each location
s
separately, corresponding to individual 30-min periods
(
Van De Giesen et al. 2012
)
. Thereafter, at each time step, the calibration parameters
\gamma
and
C
were estimated for the forward and reverse signals by fitting Eq.
1
to
P_{s}
and
P_{as}
observed with the DTS instrument and
T
measured with the reference sensors. Although, in theory,
\gamma
should be constant, here it was treated as a fitting parameter varying in time. These parameters were used to calculate separate estimates of
T(s,t)
from the forward (

) and reverse (

) signals. Since

and
T_{r}
were measured sequentially,

Noise estimates (

, 1-

variability related to random noise) of

and
T_{r}
observations were computed for each 30-min period as,

\sigma_{T}^{2}(s)=\frac{\overline{\left(T(s,t)-T(s+\Delta s,t)\right)^{2}}}{2},

(2)

where
\overline{()}
denotes temporal averaging and

is the spatial step along the cable (0.127 cm). In this equation, we assume that the variability between consecutive data points in space is dominated by noise. The calibrated temperature
T

and

where inverse of noise estimates were used as weights
(
des Tombe et al. 2020
)
. More importantly, the noise estimates for

were calculated following noise propagation of weighted averaging. The spatial resolution of the data were reduced to 30 cm with spatial aggregation using weighted averaging as above.

After this calibration procedure, we obtained calibrated

along the cable. The

). Since there were double cabling at each physical location, the double measurements made at each location were further averaged using weighted averaging as above. By utilizing this procedure, we obtained calibrated

2.5
Scale-dependent convection speed

To compute the scale-dependent convection speeds of temperature structures, we follow the same procedure as outlined in
Cheng et al. 2017
. By borrowing the concept of phase-dependent convection speeds from
Del Alamo and Jiménez 2009
,
Cheng et al. 2017
defined the convection speed at a wavenumber
\kappa
(
U_{c}(\kappa)
) as,

U_{c}(\kappa)=\frac{\Delta\varphi}{\kappa\times\Delta t}\

(5)

Here,
\Delta\varphi
indicates the Fourier phase differences between the spatial temperature signals at time instants
t
and
t+\Delta t
,
\kappa
is the spatial wavenumber directly evaluated from the DTS measurements (
\kappa=2\pi/\lambda
, where
\lambda
is the wavelength), and
\Delta t
is the temporal lag (2.3 s for our DTS data). Unlike
Cheng et al. 2017
, we used a wavelet based approach
(
Torrence and Compo 1998
)
to compute the phase differences between the spatial temperature signals at times
t
and
t+\Delta t
, i.e.
\Delta\varphi
. For our purposes, Morlet was used as the mother wavelet and upon comparison with a Fourier based approach, both yielded similar results (see Fig. S4a in the Supplementary material). Specifically, a wavelet based approach allowed us to estimate the scale-dependent convection speeds separately for the positive and negative temperature structures. To do so, corresponding to a particular wavelet scale, the wavelet coefficients of the spatial temperature signals at a time
t
, were conditionally averaged only over those spatial locations where the temperature fluctuations were positive. An analogous operation was carried out for the negative counterparts and for a time
t+\Delta t
. We, therefore, defined the scale-dependent connection speeds of positive and negative structures as,

U^{+}_{c}(\kappa)=\frac{\Delta\varphi_{\rm P}}{\kappa\times\Delta t},U^{-}_{c}(\kappa)=\frac{\Delta\varphi_{\rm N}}{\kappa\times\Delta t},

(6)

In Eq.
6
, the phase differences were only evaluated conditionally over the locations where temperature fluctuations were either positive or negative. This is denoted by the subscripts P and N, respectively.

3
Results and discussion

We begin with demonstrating the relationship between the convective speed estimations from the DTS measurements and the mean wind speed from the EC system at the clearcut. Corresponding to the EC data,
u
and
w
indicate the streamwise and vertical velocities, while
T,q,c
indicate temperature, moisture, and
\rm CO_{2}
concentrations. After employing a double-coordinate rotation, the turbulent fluctuations in these quantities are computed through linear detrending. By using both DTS and EC datasets, the impact of scale-dependent convection speeds on the turbulence statistics are discussed. We end by pointing out some potential limitations of the DTS measurements and how our findings influence in general the flux measurements from the eddy covariance systems.

3.1.2
Conditional convection speeds

Moving on, to see whether the positive and negative temperature structures move at the same speeds, we computed the ratio
U^{+}_{C}/U^{-}_{C}
, where
U^{+}_{C}
and
U^{-}_{C}
were estimated from the conditional space-time correlation curves (Figs.
3
b–d). From a statistical perspective, the asymmetry between positive and negative temperatures is related to the skewness of temperature fluctuations. As shown in Fig.
9
a of Appendix
A
, for our selected periods, the distributions of temperature fluctuations are positively skewed. In Fig.
4
d, the ratios
U^{+}_{C}/U^{-}_{C}
were plotted against the skewness of temperature fluctuations (
\mathcal{S}(T)
) computed from the EC measurements. This was done to check if the statistical asymmetry could explain any differences between
U^{+}_{C}
and
U^{-}_{C}
. Note that in Fig.
4
d there are fewer data-points corresponding to the perpendicular-wind cases. This is because the peaks (
\tau^{\pm}_{p}
) of the conditional space-time correlation curves did not move nicely with
r
in order to have robust estimates of
U^{+}_{C}
and
U^{-}_{C}
. For the along-wind cases,
U^{+}_{C}/U^{-}_{C}
values were averaged over all the five sections and the spread between them is shown as error-bars with one standard deviation. From Fig.
4
d it appears that, for both along- and perpendicular-wind cases, the ratios
U^{+}_{C}/U^{-}_{C}
remain at a constant value of 0.8 irrespective of temperature skewness.

Since
U^{+}_{C}/U^{-}_{C}<1
, the up-plumes tend to move slower than the down-plumes. In essence, this result suggests that the temporal statistics are based on a biased sample of turbulent flow when compared against sampling over spatial domain, which, however, is not in practical terms possible. In terms of boundary-layer physics, the down-plumes bring colder air from aloft to the layer close to the surface and since these plumes carry the signatures of the large-scale structures, they appear to move faster
(
Li et al. 2018
)
. We now use this critical information to compute the averaged sizes of up- and down-plumes from the EC measurements. To do that, we first estimate the auto-correlation functions conditioned upon whether the temperatures are positive or negative. Mathematically, these functions can be defined as,

R^{+}_{TT}(\tau)=\frac{\overline{[T^{\prime}(t)|>0][T^{\prime}(t+\tau)|>0]}}{\sigma^{2}_{T}},

(8)

where
R^{+}_{TT}(\tau)
indicate the autocorrelation functions of temperature fluctuations conditioned on their positive values only.
R^{-}_{TT}(\tau)
is defined in a similar way but for the negative ones. By integrating
R^{+}_{TT}(\tau)
and
R^{-}_{TT}(\tau)
up to their first zero crossing, one can estimate the integral time scales of the positive (
\gamma^{+}_{T}
) and negative (
\gamma^{-}_{T}
) temperature structures. The functions
R^{+}_{TT}(\tau)
and
R^{-}_{TT}(\tau)
are compared with the original autocorrelation functions (
R_{TT}(\tau)
) in Fig. S3 of the Supplementary Material. By multiplying
\gamma^{+}_{T}
and
\gamma^{-}_{T}
with their respective convection speeds (
U^{+}_{C}
and
U^{-}_{C}
), the physical dimensions of the up- and down-plumes can be calculated.

In Fig.
4
e, we show a scatter plot between
(\gamma^{+}_{T}U^{+}_{C})/z
and
(\gamma^{-}_{T}U^{-}_{C})/z
, where
z
is the measurement height (
z=3.1
m). We only show this comparison for the along-wind cases and one can clearly see that the sizes of the down-plumes can be as large as 12 times the measurement height. In a statistical sense, the down-plumes remain nearly 2.5 times larger than the up-plumes (see the blue dash-dotted line in Fig.
4
e). This information is important e.g. from the perspective of choosing the domain size for the large eddy simulation models, since the down-plumes would require considerably larger area for their statistics to converge.

Figure

\kappa z<1
,
U_{C}(\kappa z)
values tend towards
U_{C}
, irrespective of the wind directions. On the other hand, at larger wavenumbers,
\kappa z>1
,
U_{C}(\kappa z)/U_{C}
values decrease in a power-law fashion with the exponent being equal to
{(\kappa z)}^{-0.8}
(Fig.

a). This is surprisingly different from the result of
Cheng et al. 2017
who reported this exponent to be
{(\kappa z)}^{-1/3}
. We replotted the results of
Cheng et al. 2017
and indeed for different atmospheric stability conditions (near-neutral to highly-convective) their data agree more closely with
{(\kappa z)}^{-1/3}
instead of
{(\kappa z)}^{-0.8}
(see Fig. S4b in the Supplementary material). However, compared to ours, the data of
Cheng et al. 2017
exhibited a significant amount of scatter.

Cheng et al. 2017
theoretically derived this
-1/3
exponent for atmospheric surface layer flows by exploiting the advection-diffusion equation and a power-law dependence of the diffusion coefficient under the assumption of homogeneous and isotropic turbulence at the inertial subrange scales. For our case, the apparent disagreement with their results could be attributed to two factors. First, we consider highly-heterogeneous flow conditions such as a forest clearcut. Second, the recent results of
Chowdhuri and Banerjee 2024
have demonstrated that, for a wide range of both atmospheric and laboratory datasets, significant anisotropy can persist even at smaller scales of the flow. This is true for our EC dataset, as the flow remains anisotropic at the inertial subrange scales when computed through the intermittency-anisotropy framework of
Chowdhuri and Banerjee 2024
(not shown).

For atmospheric surface layer and sparse canopy flows, a couple of previous studies have shown that an elliptic model of space-time correlation curves to compute the convection speeds is a better approach as opposed to a linear one adopted here
(
Han and Zhang 2022
;
Everard et al. 2021
)
. We describe this elliptical model in Appendix
B
. In this model, the convective speeds of the turbulent structures are estimated as
\sqrt{U_{e}^{2}+V_{e}^{2}}
, where
U_{e}
and
V_{e}
are convection and sweeping velocities, respectively. In Fig.
5
b,
\sqrt{U_{e}^{2}+V_{e}^{2}}
is used to normalize
U_{C}(\kappa z)
and the results demonstrate that under this scaling the scale-dependent convection speeds do not exactly approach the bulk convective speeds (
\sqrt{U_{e}^{2}+V_{e}^{2}}
) at lower wavenumbers. Furthermore, the collapse among different sections is poorer as compared to Fig.
5
a. Therefore, this questions the applicability of an elliptic model in these heterogeneous flow conditions.

To understand the origin of
-0.8
exponent in scale-dependent convection speeds, we conditioned them based on the instances when the spatial temperature fluctuations were either positive or negative (see Sect.
2.5
). For consistency, both
U^{+}_{C}(\kappa z)
and
U^{-}_{C}(\kappa z)
values were scaled with
U_{c}
. From Figs.
5
c–d, it is clear that the
-0.8
exponent is connected to the convective speeds of the down plumes (
U^{-}_{C}(\kappa z)
), contrary to the up ones (
U^{+}_{C}(\kappa z)
). In fact, at lower wavenumbers (
\kappa z<1
),
U^{-}_{C}(\kappa z)
remain larger than
U^{+}_{C}(\kappa z)
values and at larger wavenumbers (
\kappa z>1
) the convective speeds of the up plumes decrease as fast as
{(\kappa z)}^{-1.5}
. Therefore, as per the terminology of
Cheng et al. 2017
, the down-plumes diffuse slower than the up-plumes while carried along with the flow. We next discuss the implications of the scale-dependent convection speeds on the estimations of turbulence spectra and cospectra from EC measurements conducted in a temporal domain.

3.2
Impact on turbulence spectra

3.2.1
Frequency to wavenumber conversion

Under the assumption that the turbulent structures of different scales advect past the measurement location at similar speeds, one could essentially transform the frequencies (
f
) to wavenumbers (
\kappa
) using
\kappa=2\pi f/U
, where
U
is the mean wind speed estimated from the sonic anemometer. Irrespective of whether one uses
U
or
U_{C}
to convert the frequencies to wavenumbers, it is a linear transformation. However, if the convective speeds of turbulent structures are scale-dependent, it introduces additional complexities to the transformation. From our DTS observations, it is clear that
U_{C}(\kappa z)
is equal to
U_{C}
at scales
\kappa z<1
, while it varies as
{(\kappa z)}^{-0.8}
at scales
\kappa z>1
(see Fig.
5
a). One could express this dependence through the following functional form,

\frac{U_{c}(\kappa z)}{U_{c}}=\frac{1}{{(1+\kappa z)}^{4/5}},

(9)

which satisfies the aforementioned conditions. When compared against the observations, this functional form fits the data exceptionally well (see the yellow dash-dotted line in Fig.
5
a). Now, if this relationship is used to convert the frequencies to wavenumbers, one could write,

\kappa z=\frac{2\pi fz}{U_{C}(\kappa z)}.

(10)

By replacing Eq.
9
in Eq.
10
, and after some algebraic manipulations, one could rewrite Eq.
10
as,

{(\kappa z)}^{5}=a{(1+\kappa z)}^{4},

(11)

where
a=(2\pi fz/U_{C})^{5}
. By solving the polynomial in Eq.
11
and restricting its roots to positive values, one could essentially convert the frequencies to wavenumbers by using a scale-dependent convection speed.

Before we discuss the impact of this transformation on turbulence spectra and cospectra, it is important to notice that since Eq.
11
is a non-linear function, the wavenumbers generated from Eq.
11
will not be spaced equally in a linear space. Accordingly, the wavenumber increments (
d\kappa
) themselves would depend on the values of
\kappa
. In fact, by differentiating Eq.
11
, one can show,

d\kappa=\frac{10\pi}{U_{C}}\frac{{(1+\kappa z)}^{9/5}}{5+\kappa z}df.

(12)

Eq.
12
dictates that
d\kappa=f(\kappa)
at a fixed
z
, meaning that constant
df
does not convert to constant
d\kappa
. This creates an issue while estimating the spectral densities (
S_{xx}(\kappa)
) as those are defined as
S_{xx}(\kappa)=E_{xx}(\kappa)/d\kappa
, where
E_{xx}(\kappa)
are the spectral powers of a turbulent signal
x
and
d\kappa
is the wavenumber increment independent of
\kappa
.

Therefore, to solve this paradox, we exponentially bin the scaled wavenumbers (
\kappa z
) generated from Eq.
11
and then compute the bin-averaged spectral powers (
E_{xx}(\kappa z_{\rm bin})
) corresponding to those wavenumber ranges, bounded between
\kappa z_{\rm bin}(i)<\kappa z<\kappa z_{\rm bin}(i)+d\log(\kappa z_{\rm bin})
, where
i
spans from 1 to
N
with
N
being the number of exponential bins being used. Here, we use
N=50
for our application. To get an equivalent estimate in the linear space, the spectral density at a wavenumber of
\kappa z_{\rm bin}(i)
is expressed as,
S_{xx}(\kappa z_{\rm bin}(i))=E_{xx}(\kappa z_{\rm bin}(i))/[\kappa z_{\rm bin}(i)d(\log(\kappa z_{\rm bin})]
, where
\kappa z_{\rm bin}(i)
itself appears in the denominator. This is necessary since
d(\log(\kappa))=d\kappa/\kappa
. The entire procedure described above is a standard one while dealing with empirical estimations of the probability density functions of stochastic variables that are known to display power-law variations
(
Chowdhuri et al. 2020a
)
. Henceforth, while reporting the spectral results derived from the scale-dependent convection speeds, we omit the subscript from
\kappa z_{\rm bin}
.

(see the blue squares in Fig.

)
. For the scalars, especially moisture and

slope, which could be due to attenuation of the high frequency fluctuations in the gas analyzer sampling tube and instrument noise. However, under the transformation with Eq.

(

In fact, Eq.

-5/3

Upon applying the Kolmogorov’s similarity model on these transformed spectra, one could essentially recompute the dissipation rates of turbulence kinetic energy and scalar variances. This is in accord with previous studies, demonstrating that the dissipation rate estimations from temporal turbulence spectra are sensitive to whether the Taylor’s hypothesis holds
(
Dahm and Southerland 1997
;

)
.

Unlike scalars, for the vertical velocity, scale-dependent convective speeds modify the
-5/3
spectral slope. We explain this anomaly in Appendix

. If we turn our attention towards the flux cospectra (heat, moisture, momentum, and

(Fig.

is consistent with the dimensional result of

. For momentum and
\rm CO_{2}
transport we show their absolute cospectral densities, since their original values are negative. This is evident from the premultiplied cospectra shown in Fig. S6 of the Supplementary Material. Now, if the conversion to wavenumbers is carried out using Eq.

-5/3
scaling instead of
-7/3
(Fig.

(
Cheng et al. 2020
)
. These same results appear to be true even for the perpendicular-wind cases (Figs.
7
e–h). Inspite of such interesting findings, the estimations of the revised
\kappa z
values and subsequently the spectra are hinged upon how accurate the DTS measurements are. While we discuss this aspect next, we also provide a recipe on how to correct the EC flux measurements based on the results presented so far.

3.3
Implications for EC fluxes

3.3.1
Limitations of DTS

Peltola et al. 2021

+1

\sigma^{2}_{T}
) estimated from the EC measurements and the frequencies are scaled with the standard surface layer scaling (

) of
Kaimal et al. 1972
. Here, we focus only on the along-wind periods although the conclusions remain same for the perpendicular-wind periods too. From Fig.
8

2.1\ \rm s
) is lower than the EC one (
0.1\ \rm s
), it does not fully capture the fine scale features. Contrary to
Peltola et al. 2021
, we do not find any evidence of a white noise since there is no presence of a
+1
spectral slope at larger frequencies. This is likely since our analyses focused on periods with high temperature variance and hence on periods with high signal-to-noise ratio.

3.3.2
A critical frequency limit

Despite the limitations of DTS measurements, we test one hypothesis of
Cheng et al. 2017
where they proposed that by using scale-dependent convection speeds one can increase the flux contributions from smaller time scales. This eventually could serve as a correction for improving the imbalance in surface energy components as commonly being observed in micrometeorological measurements
(
Mauder et al. 2020
)
. In contrast to ours,
Cheng et al. 2017
found that the scale-dependent convection speeds of temperature structures vary as
{(\kappa z)}^{-1/3}
. If we use that information to convert the frequencies to wavenumbers, then after some algebraic manipulation, one can show that the wavenumbers (
\kappa z
) can be estimated from frequencies (
f
) by solving the polynomial,

{(\kappa z)}^{3}=a(1+\kappa z),

(13)

where
a=(2\pi fz/U_{C})^{3}
. To derive Eq.
13
, we have assumed that the convection speeds can be expressed as,

\frac{U_{c}(\kappa z)}{U_{c}}=\frac{1}{{(1+\kappa z)}^{1/3}},

(14)

which appears to be supported by the plot shown in Fig. S4b of the Supplementary Material.

In Figs.
8
c-d, we show the premultiplied heat and moisture cospectra (
fS_{wx}(f)
, where
x=T,q
) where the frequencies are converted to wavenumbers using mean wind speed (
U
, red line), bulk convective speed (
U_{C}
, magenta line), and scale-dependent convective speeds (
U_{C}(\kappa z)
) from our observations (pink line) and from
Cheng et al. 2017
(blue line). We adopt the premultiplied form since the area under this log-linear coordinate system is directly related to the flux contributions across different scales
(
Kaimal and Finnigan 1994
)
. The results from Figs.
8
c-d clearly demonstrate that using our scale-dependent convective speeds (Eq.
9
) cause an appreciable increase in the heat and moisture fluxes at smaller scales of the flow. More importantly, the differences between the cospectral shapes when using either
U
,
U_{C}
, or
U_{C}(\kappa z)
relationship from
Cheng et al. 2017
to convert the frequencies to wavenumbers are small.

The transformations between wavenumbers and frequencies (Eqs.
11
and
13
) also yield an unrealistic outcome since the wavenumbers estimated from both Eqs.
11
and
13
are strong non-linear functions of frequency. If these functions are plotted for a specific 30-min period, as done in Fig.
8
e, it is apparent that the wavenumbers estimated from Eq.
11
can be up to
10^{6}\rm\ m^{-1}
when converting the EC sampling frequency (10 Hz) to wavenumber. This cannot be true as it suggests that the EC systems can measure eddies comparable to the dissipation scales, which are clearly smaller than the path length (distance between the transducers) of the sonic anemometers themselves. For our sonic anemometer system, the path length is around 16 cm, and hence the wavenumbers that can be realistically resolved is around
2\pi/0.3\rm\ m^{-1}
. To avoid the aliasing effect, the minimum wavelength being resolved is assumed to be twice the path length (i.e. 30 cm). This limit is shown as a horizontal dash-dotted line in Fig.
8
e. If we call this minimum wavelength being set by the physical dimension of the sonic anemometer as
\kappa_{\rm cr}
, then from Eq.
11
, one can convert
\kappa_{\rm cr}
to a frequency limit (
f_{\rm cr}
) in the temporal space. Accordingly, one can estimate

as,

f_{\rm cr}=\frac{\kappa_{\rm cr}U_{C}}{2\pi{(1+\kappa_{\rm cr}z)}^{4/5}}.

(15)

For our set up,
z=3.1
m,
\kappa_{\rm cr}=2\pi/0.3\rm\ m^{-1}
, and
U_{C}
can be found from the space-time correlation curves (see Fig.
4
). For the along-wind cases,
U_{C}
values are averaged over all the five sections. By replacing all these values in Eq.
15
,
f_{\rm cr}
appears to be
\approx
0.3 Hz (for both along- and perpendicular-wind periods), with some run-to-run variations since
U_{C}
values are not fixed.

3.3.3
High-frequency flux uncertainties

If we consider the
U_{C}(\kappa z)
values and therefore the derived wavenumbers from Eq.
11
to be correct, then it is unclear what the EC observations represent at frequencies above
f_{\rm cr}
(i.e.
f\geq f_{\rm cr}
). This is because, beyond
f_{\rm cr}
, the eddy length scales are smaller than the physical dimension of the instrument itself. Of course this problem is not that severe if the wavenumbers are estimated from Eq.
13
, i.e. using the
Cheng et al. 2017
relationship (see the blue line in Fig.
8
e). By following the same procedure,
f_{\rm cr}
values for the
Cheng et al. 2017
relationship can be derived as,

f_{\rm cr}=\frac{\kappa_{\rm cr}U_{C}}{2\pi{(1+\kappa_{\rm cr}z)}^{1/3}}.

(16)

If one substitutes all the values from our set up in Eq.
16
,
f_{\rm cr}
appears to be
\approx
2 Hz. In general,
f_{\rm cr}
values are dependent on the power-law exponent (
\alpha
) of the scale-dependent convection speeds,
U_{C}(\kappa z)/U_{C}=1/(1+\kappa z)^{\alpha}
, with
0<\alpha<1
. Here,
\alpha=1
corresponds to a limit for the randomly-shuffled samples while
\alpha=0
indicates no scale-dependency.

The existence of
f_{\rm cr}
ultimately results in a situation where the fluxes associated with EC systems at higher frequencies are largely uncertain. In a fractional form, this high-frequency flux uncertainty can be expressed as,

1-\frac{\int_{0}^{f_{\rm cr}}S_{wx}(f)\,df}{\overline{w^{\prime}x^{\prime}}},

(17)

where
S_{wx}
is the cospectral density between
w
and
x
. Here
x
can be any constituent, e.g.
u
,
T
, H
2
O or CO
2
. For any EC system, this fraction can be estimated by computing
f_{\rm cr}
from either Eq.
15
or Eq.
16
, depending on the flow situations. In the absence of any concrete evidence, we hypothesize that Eq.
15
is valid for highly-heterogeneous flows (e.g., forest clearcut) while Eq.
16
can be used for homogeneous surface layer flows. For highly-heterogeneous conditions,
U_{C}
can be obtained from Eq.
7
.

For our case, the fraction of fluxes within the frequency range
0<f<f_{\rm cr}
is around 85-90% of the total flux being resolved. Thus, between 10-15% of the flux is related to the frequency range that is questionable given the physical dimension of the sonic-anemometer (see above). This has significant impact on the long-term estimations of ecosystem gross primary productivity, net ecosystem exchange, evapotranspiration, sensible heat and momentum fluxes from the EC systems. Potentially, Eq.
17
could be extended to reevaluate the estimations of velocity and scalar variances and turbulence kinetic energy, and therefore has implications for surface layer parameterizations. One other impact of Eq.
17
is on the surface energy balance.

At our measurement site, we estimate the imbalance among the surface energy components. To do so, in Fig.
8
f, we show a scatter plot between the sensible (SHF) and latent heat fluxes (LHF) and
\rm R_{n}-G_{0}
for all the periods utilized in this study. Here,
\rm R_{n}
and
\rm G_{0}
are the net-radiation and soil heat flux components. The blue dash-dotted line in Fig.
8

\rm R_{n}-\rm G_{0}
(black dash-dot line in Fig.
8
f). The slope is around
0.76
, which translates to a 24% imbalance in the surface energy budget for our measurement periods. Given this high-frequency uncertainty in Eq.
17
, we plot the pink squares in Fig.
8
f, which represent the values after multiplying the sensible and latent heat fluxes with the factor
({\int_{0}^{f_{\rm cr}}S_{wx}(f)\,df})/{\overline{w^{\prime}x^{\prime}}}
, where
x=T,q
and
f_{\rm cr}
is computed from Eq.
15
. It is clear that the pink squares lie below the original red ones, which indicates the worsening of the surface energy balance. This is tied to the failure of Taylor’s hypothesis and a limitation associated with the physical dimension of the instrument itself.

From a practical standpoint, since all our results are from a single height, we cannot establish whether
z
is the ideal scaling variable to describe
U_{c}(\kappa)
. If
z
indeed is the correct scaling parameter, then depending on the observation height,
f_{\rm cr}
values will change (see Eqs.
15
and
16
). Accordingly, the high-frequency flux uncertainty associated with the EC systems would also change, and possibly will increase at higher heights since
f_{\rm cr}
is inversely proportional to
z
. We present our conclusions in the next section.

4
Conclusion

In this study, we have developed a comprehensive framework to test the validity of Taylor’s hypothesis and its impact on turbulence statistics over a highly heterogeneous forest clearcut surface. To execute this objective, an extensive dataset encompassing both Distributed Temperature Sensing (DTS) and Eddy Covariance (EC) measurements were used. These datasets were collected during a five-month field campaign (May-September 2024) and were separated into two different categories. First, when the mean wind speed was parallel to the forest edge, and second, when it was perpendicular to the edge. For the former, our results demonstrated that the Taylor’s hypothesis remained invalid as the turbulent intensities of the streamwise wind fluctuations exceeded 0.5. An empirical relationship is proposed to estimate the convective speeds in such extreme conditions from the sonic anemometer observations. Corresponding to the latter scenario, no such relationship was apparent since depending on whether the wind approached from the forest or from the clearcut the results were different.

Despite this discrepancy, for both along- and perpendicular-wind cases, the ratios of the convective speeds of positive and negative temperature structures remained at a constant value of 0.8, irrespective of the temperature skewness measured by the EC system. We used this information to compute the sizes of up- and down-plumes in a convective boundary layer. We found that the sizes of the down-plumes can be as large as 12 times the measurement height and therefore would require considerably larger area for their statistics to converge. This result informs about what should be the appropriate horizontal domain size while setting up the large eddy simulations of convective boundary layers. Furthermore, the different convection speeds for the positive and negative temperature structures suggests that an EC tower sampling the flow over time produces a biased sample of the flow when compared to sampling taken over space.

We demonstrated that the convective speeds of temperature structures at inertial subrange scales behave in a power-law fashion with increasing wavenumbers. These wavenumbers were directly computed from the DTS spatial data. Contrary to a previous study over a homogeneous atmospheric surface layer flow
(
Cheng et al. 2017
)
, the exponent of this power law was
-0.8
instead of
-1/3
. The
-1/3
exponent was theoretically derived under the assumption of homogeneous and isotropic turbulence at smaller scales of the flow. For the present case in hand, we hypothesize that the heterogeneity in the surface conditions violate these assumptions even at smaller scales of the flow. We evaluated the impact of this revised scale-dependent convection speed on the turbulence spectra of velocity and scalars (temperature, moisture, and
\rm CO_{2}
), estimated from the temporal EC measurements. The results displayed an extensive
-5/3
spectral slope (over two decades of wavenumbers) for the streamwise velocity component and the three different scalars when the frequencies were converted to wavenumbers using our scale-dependent convective speeds. For the flux cospectra, instead of a
-7/3
slope, a
-5/3
slope was recovered under such conversion.

The appearance of a slope flatter than
-7/3
indicates an enhancement of turbulent fluxes at smaller scales of the flow. However, the non-linear conversion of frequencies to wavenumbers through scale-dependent convective speeds generates unrealistically large wavenumber values. The physical dimension of the sonic anemometers sets a strict constraint on the wavenumbers that can be resolved by the EC systems. In a temporal domain, this eventually results in a critical frequency limit beyond which the EC measurements cannot be trusted. This critical limit depends on the measurement height and on the power-law exponent of the scale-dependent convective speeds. The existence of this limit dictates that there is a high-frequency flux uncertainty associated with EC systems, which, in turn, has an adverse effect on the estimation of surface energy budget. Before we outline our future research directions,
Hilland and Christen 2024
asked whether the Taylor’s hypothesis is ever satisfied in atmospheric flows. For a flat desert surface,
Hilland and Christen 2024
found that the Taylor’s hypothesis appeared to be valid only between spatial scales 5 to 16 m. Over a forest clearcut, our results suggest that for the periods when the wind approached the clearcut location from the forest, the Taylor’s hypothesis remained valid at scales
\kappa z<1
, where
\kappa
is the streamwise wavenumber and
z
is the measurement height. This was realized notwithstanding the fact that during such periods the flow was disturbed as it encountered abrupt changes in the surface roughness.

At present, these results are representative of buoyancy-driven flows in a heterogeneous location and are also limited by a single observation height. One another drawback is, the estimations of the convective speeds are based on an imperfect temperature signal and we inherently assume that the same could be extended to other scalars and velocity components. It is not immediately apparent whether the structures in all these different turbulent variables move at same speeds. Therefore, for future studies, more extensive spatial data are needed to verify our framework, which ideally should span multiple heights, stability conditions (stable, neutral, and convective), surface roughness (forest canopies, grasslands, arid deserts, water-bodies, etc.), and signal types (velocity and scalars). To achieve this ambitious goal, the observations alone will not suffice and large eddy simulations would be needed to complement the DTS measurements.

As opposed to a linear model to estimate the convective speeds of turbulent structures, there also exists an elliptical model. A couple of previous studies, pertaining to atmospheric surface layer and sparse canopy flows, found the elliptical model to be more appropriate while estimating the convective speeds of turbulent structures
(
Han and Zhang 2022

Everard et al. 2021

R_{TT}(r,\tau)
appear to be of elliptical shape. To quantify this aspect, the elliptical model computes two velocities
U_{e}
and
V_{e}
, which are referred to as convection and sweeping velocities, respectively.

For all practical purposes, to estimate these two quantities one needs to use two different timescales
(
Han and Zhang 2022
)
. One of those timescales is
\tau_{0}
and are calculated from the condition,
R_{TT}(r,0)=R_{TT}(0,\tau_{0})
. The second one is
\tau_{p}
, which represents the peak positions of the space-time correlation curves at different values of
r
. The concepts of
\tau_{0}
and
\tau_{p}
are illustrated in Fig.
10
b. The blue square represents the peak of the space-time correlation curve at a specific value of
r
, thereby denoting
\tau_{p}
. On the other hand,
\tau_{0}
represents that timescale where
R_{TT}(r,0)
is equal to
R_{TT}(0,\tau_{0})
. For this specific example,
r=21\ \rm m
. After defining those, one fits straight lines to quantities
\tau_{0}
and
r
, and
\tau_{p}
and
r
(see Fig.
10
c). If the slopes of these lines are
\alpha_{0}
(
\tau_{0}=\alpha_{0}r
) and
\beta_{p}
(
\tau_{p}=\beta_{p}r
), respectively, then
U_{e}
and
V_{e}
are computed as,

U_{e}=\frac{\beta_{p}}{\alpha^{2}_{0}},V_{e}=\frac{\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}_{p}/\alpha^{2}_{0}}}{\alpha_{0}}.

(19)

In Figs.
10
d-e, we compare both
U_{e}
and
V_{e}
with the mean wind speed (
U
) from the EC measurements. This comparison is done for the along-wind cases where the gray-colored squares represent the five different sections of the DTS cable. It is clear from Fig.
10

is weakly correlated to
U
, which is in stark contrast with Fig.
4
a. The sweeping velocities, on the other hand, are consistently smaller than

(Fig.
10

\sqrt{U_{e}^{2}+V_{e}^{2}}
and if this ratio
\sqrt{U_{e}^{2}+V_{e}^{2}}/U
is compared against the turbulent intensities of the streamwise velocity component (

), a significant scatter is observed (Fig.
10

7
, cannot fully explain these variations. We, therefore, conclude that for our situation the performance of the elliptical model remains poor. It is, however, unclear whether this is a general feature of heterogeneous flows or a feature specific to our site. More experimental validation is needed to confirm this aspect, which is beyond the scope of the present work.
