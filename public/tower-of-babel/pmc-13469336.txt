Psychometric Properties and Factor Structure of the Persian Version of the Sexual Health Outcomes in Women Questionnaire Among Iranian Women With Pelvic Disorders: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Leila AliAghdami, Fatemeh Nasiri‐Amiri, Sobhan Rahimi Esbo, Afsaneh Bakhtiari, Mahjoobeh Betyar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsTo accurately assess women's sexual health, psychometrically sound and culturally adapted instruments must be used. Although the Sexual Health Outcomes in Women Questionnaire (SHOW‐Q) is widely used among Western populations, a validated Persian version has yet to be developed. This study aimed to translate the SHOW‐Q into Persian and evaluate its psychometric properties among Iranian women with pelvic disorders.MethodsA cross‐sectional methodological study was conducted from February to November 2024. The SHOW‐Q was translated into Persian using the standard forward–backward method. A total of 416 women diagnosed with pelvic disorders completed the Persian version of the questionnaire. The psychometric evaluation included face and content validity (qualitative and quantitative), as well as construct validity via exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses, and convergent and discriminant validity. Internal consistency and test–retest reliability were also assessed.ResultsThe Persian SHOW‐Q comprises 13 items, and factor analysis supports a three‐factor structure considering the physical and psychological influences on sexual function, satisfaction, and experience. Model fit indices were satisfactory. Cronbach's alpha (α = 0.823) indicated strong internal consistency and test–retest reliability showed excellent stability (ICC = 0.993).ConclusionThe Persian version of the SHOW‐Q demonstrates satisfactory psychometric properties and can be used to reliably and validly evaluate sexual health outcomes among Iranian women with pelvic disorders.

Introduction

Sexual function is a multifaceted aspect of women's health, encompassing biological, psychological, emotional, and social factors. Disruptions in this area can have a significant impact on intimacy, self‐confidence, and quality of life [1]. From a clinical perspective, female sexual dysfunction (FSD) is characterized by consistent disturbances in one or more phases of the sexual response cycle [2], resulting in reduced sexual satisfaction and an impaired ability to experience pleasure [3].

FSD is one of the most prevalent and distressing health concerns among women of reproductive age worldwide. Its multifactorial etiology involves biological, psychological, relational, and sociocultural factors [4]. A comprehensive systematic review and meta‐analysis, published in 2016 and including data from over 215,000 women, estimated the global prevalence of FSD to be around 41% [5]. National‐level investigations in Iran have reported prevalence rates close to 31% [6], and evidence suggests that these rates are even higher among women with gynecological or pelvic conditions than in healthy populations [7].

While psychological and relational factors are well‐established contributors to FSD, pelvic floor disorders are another key determinant of sexual difficulties. Millions of women globally experience these conditions, yet stigma and embarrassment often hinder open discussion and access to timely medical care. Early recognition and management are crucial for protecting women's sexual and reproductive health [8]. Indeed, numerous studies demonstrate a strong association between pelvic pain and sexual dysfunction. Over two‐thirds of affected women report experiencing sexual problems related to pain [9].

However, accurately estimating the prevalence of FSD is challenging due to methodological inconsistencies such as differences in diagnostic criteria, measurement tools, study design, and cultural context. Many existing instruments inadequately capture the impact of pelvic disorders on sexual outcomes, while others fail to effectively assess treatment response [9]. In response to this, Learman and colleagues developed the Sexual Health Outcomes in Women Questionnaire (SHOW‐Q) in 2008—a standardized tool designed to measure sexual desire, frequency, satisfaction, orgasm, and discomfort related to pelvic conditions [10].

Adapting and validating the SHOW‐Q for non‐Western populations is essential to ensure its cultural relevance and expand its utility in clinical and research settings. Currently, no Persian‐language version has undergone rigorous psychometric evaluation. Therefore, the aim of this study was to translate the SHOW‐Q into Persian and evaluate its validity, reliability, and factor structure among Iranian women with pelvic disorders. This may facilitate a more accurate assessment of sexual health outcomes and support the development of culturally tailored interventions to enhance women's quality of life.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Participants

This cross‐sectional study was conducted between February and November 2024 at hospitals affiliated with Babol University of Medical Sciences in northern Iran. A total of 416 married women of reproductive age presenting with gynecological pelvic complaints at the gynecology clinics of Rouhani and Yahya Nejad hospitals were recruited via convenience sampling. Data were collected using two instruments: a form to record demographic and clinical characteristics (including variables such as participant age, educational level, occupation, number of pregnancies and children, spouse's age, and duration of marriage); and the Persian version of the Sexual Health Outcomes Questionnaire (SHOW‐Q). Participants completed the paper‐based questionnaires under the supervision of a trained study midwife in a private setting within the participating hospitals. This approach ensured that participants could respond confidentially and without interruption. To maintain privacy and confidentiality, all completed questionnaires were kept securely, and participants' identities were anonymized during data entry and analysis.

Eligibility criteria: women aged 15–49 years who reported at least one pelvic disorder were eligible. Target conditions included abnormal uterine bleeding, menorrhagia, chronic pelvic pain, dysmenorrhea, dyspareunia, symptomatic leiomyoma, and premenopausal status. Participants were excluded if they were sexually inactive or did not have a pelvic disorder.

Sample Size Determination

An adequate sample size is essential for psychometric studies. Recommendations suggest a minimum of 3–10 participants per item, although generally, at least 100 participants are considered sufficient [11]. Gorsuch proposed five participants per item, whereas Nunnally and Bernstein [12], whereas Nunnally and Bernstein [13] recommended at least 10 participants and Comrey and Lee [14] deemed 3 participants per variable acceptable. Following these guidelines, 416 women were recruited to ensure robust construct validity. Of these, 213 were randomly assigned to the exploratory factor analysis (EFA) group and 173 to the confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) group. A further 30 participants completed the questionnaire twice, 2 weeks apart, to assess test–retest reliability.

Translation Procedure

Translation procedure: The English version of the SHOW‐Q comprises 12 items across 4 dimensions. In this study, the initial step involved securing permission from the SHOW‐Q's original designer. The Persian version of the questionnaire was then developed in accordance with the standard protocol outlined by the World Health Organization [15]. Participants were excluded if they were sexually inactive or did not have a pelvic disorder.

In addition to the standard translation procedure, particular attention was paid to items addressing sensitive aspects of sexual health. To ensure linguistic accuracy and cultural appropriateness, a multidisciplinary expert panel consisting of a gynecologist, a reproductive health specialist, a clinical psychologist, and two bilingual researchers reviewed all items. Items containing culturally sensitive wording were discussed in detail to ensure that they conveyed the original clinical meaning while remaining respectful and acceptable within the Iranian cultural context. Where necessary, minor adjustments to the wording were made to replace potentially ambiguous or uncomfortable phrasing with culturally neutral alternatives without altering any item's underlying construct.

To further evaluate the cultural relevance and clarity of the pre‐final Persian version, it was pilot‐tested with a group of 30 married women with pelvic disorders. Participants were asked whether any items were confusing, offensive, or culturally inappropriate. The research team and expert panel then reviewed the feedback and made additional refinements where needed.

Throughout the adaptation process, no items were removed in order to maintain conceptual equivalence with the original instrument.

Psychometric Properties

The psychometric properties of the Persian version of the SHOW‐Q were evaluated in this phase. This assessment included a thorough analysis of the following aspects: –face validity, which was assessed both qualitatively and quantitatively;–content validity was evaluated using qualitative and quantitative methods;–construct validity; and–reliability.

Face Validity

Face validity was evaluated using qualitative and quantitative methodologies. For the qualitative approach, the questionnaire was distributed to 30 married women with pelvic disorders. The women provided feedback on the difficulty, relevance, and clarity of the items. Suggestions for modifying the items were incorporated based on this feedback. In the quantitative method, the same 30 women were asked to rate the importance of each item. They used a scoring system where 5 = extremely important, 4 = moderately important, 3 = somewhat important, 2 = slightly important, and 1 = not important at all. The impact score for each item was then calculated using a specified formula. Items with a minimum impact score of 1.5 were retained [16]. ImpactScore=Frequency(%)×Importance.

Impact Score = Frequency (%): The number of individuals who assigned scores of 4 or 5 to the item.

Importance: The mean importance score based on the Likert scale.

Content Validity

Both qualitative and quantitative methods were used to assess the content validity of the Persian version of the SHOW‐Q questionnaire. For the qualitative analysis, the questionnaire was reviewed by 16 midwifery experts with experience in translating and evaluating psychometric instruments. The experts assessed the questionnaire in terms of grammar, wording, item allocation, and scaling. They also provided feedback that led to specific revisions of individual items. In the quantitative analysis, the content validity ratio (CVR) was calculated. The same 16 experts rated the necessity of each item using a 3‐point Likert scale (1 = not necessary, 2 = useful but not necessary, 3 = necessary). The CVR was then computed using the formula [n e − (N/2)]/(N/2), where “n e” is the number of experts who rated the item as 3 (necessary) and “N” is the total number of experts. The minimum acceptable CVR value was determined using Lawshe's table [17], employing a strict method that considered only items deemed necessary.

Subsequently, the content validity index (CVI) was calculated for the items. The same 16 experts assessed the items using a Likert scale ranging from “completely relevant” to “irrelevant.” A minimum CVI value of 0.78 was set as the threshold for excellence, as per Polit et al. [18], requiring input from at least six experts [18]. The study also computed the Scale‐level Content Validity Index (S‐CVI/UA) and the Average Content Validity Index (S‐CVI/Ave), aiming for minimum acceptable values of 0.8 and 0.9, respectively [19, 20].

Item Analysis

Prior to assessing construct validity, an item analysis was conducted to identify any potential issues with the items. This analysis involved calculating the corrected item‐total correlation for each item. Items with correlation coefficients below 0.32 or above 0.9 were flagged for potential deletion based on established criteria [21].

Construct Validity

An EFA was conducted on a sample of 213 participants using maximum likelihood extraction with ProMax rotation. Sampling adequacy was confirmed via a Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) value greater than 0.7 and a significant result for Bartlett's test of sphericity (p < 0.001) [22, 23, 24]. In this study, factors were extracted based on eigenvalues and the scree plot in order to determine the number of factors. Factors with eigenvalues above 1 were considered suitable and retained in the study. A factor loading of at least 0.3 was considered appropriate for assigning each item to a factor. This value was determined using the formula CV = 5.152 ÷ √(n – 2), where CV is the minimum factor loading and n is the sample size. Items with communalities below 0.2 were excluded from the EFA. CFA was then performed on a sample of 173 participants using AMOS with maximum likelihood estimation. Model fit was evaluated using the following indices: Root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) (< 0.08), chi‐squared to degree of freedom ratio (χ 2/df) (< 3), goodness‐of‐fit index (GFI) (> 0.9), adjusted goodness‐of‐fit index (AGFI) (> 0.8), incremental fit index (IFI) (> 0.9), and parsimony chi‐square fit index (PCFI) (> 0.5) [25, 26].

Convergent and Discriminant Validity

Convergent validity was established when the average variance extracted (AVE) was greater than 0.50 and the composite reliability (CR) was greater than 0.70 [27]. Discriminant validity was confirmed when the maximum shared variance (MSV) was less than the AVE. [28]

Reliability

Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach's α and McDonald's Ω, with values of at least 0.70 being considered acceptable [29]. Test–retest reliability was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC).

Absolute reliability was evaluated through the standard error of measurement (SEM) formula : SEM=PooledSD×(1−ICC) where ICC is the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient reflecting stability over time [30].

Responsiveness was examined using the minimum important change (MIC) and minimum detectable change (MDC) criteria. Responsiveness was confirmed if the MIC was less than the MDC [21].

At the baseline assessment, each participant was assigned a unique study identification code and asked to write it on the questionnaire. They were also given a sealed reminder card containing only their code and the scheduled retest date. Two weeks later, the same participants were given the same questionnaire and asked to write their unique code on the retest form only. No names or personal identifiers were collected at any stage. They were given a sealed reminder card containing their unique code and the rescheduled test date. Two weeks after that, they were given the same questionnaire and asked to write their code on the retest form only. No names or personal identifiers were collected at any stage. This procedure enabled us to match test–retest responses while maintaining full anonymity.

Ceiling and Floor Effects

Ceiling and floor effects were defined as present if more than 15% of participants achieved the maximum or minimum score [31].

Data Normality and Outliers

Univariate and multivariate normality were evaluated using skewness (±3) and kurtosis (±7) [32]. Multivariate outliers were identified using Mahalanobis distance (p < 0.001), and Mardia's coefficient (< 8) was used to confirm multivariate normality [33].

Measurements

Demographic and Reproductive Questionnaire

This seven‐item, researcher‐designed questionnaire collected the following data: age; age of husband; years of marriage; occupation; number of pregnancies; education level; and number of children.

Sexual Health Outcomes Questionnaire (SHOW‐Q)

The original 12‐item English SHOW‐Q, developed by Learman and colleagues in 2008, covers four domains: sexual satisfaction, orgasm, desire, and interference from pelvic conditions [10].

Data Analysis

Descriptive statistics, EFA, and reliability testing were performed using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). CFA was conducted using AMOS version 24.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed continuous variables, and as frequency (percentage) for categorical variables. Normality was assessed using skewness and kurtosis values within the acceptable ranges of ±3 and ±7, respectively. Independent‐samples t‐tests were used to compare SHOW‐Q scores across demographic variables for group comparisons. All statistical tests were two‐sided, with an a priori significance level of α = 0.05. All statistical methods and results were reported in accordance with the SAMPL (Statistical Analyses and Methods in the Published Literature) guidelines to ensure transparency and reproducibility [34].

Ethical Considerations

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Babol University of Medical Sciences (IR.MUBABOL.REC.1402.156). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and confidentiality and anonymity were strictly maintained in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Test–retest reliability was excellent, with ICCs ranging from 0.967 to 0.993. The small standard error of measurement (SEM = 0.829) supports the precision of individual scores (see Table 6). Final Scale Structure and Scoring

The finalized Persian version of the Sexual Health Outcomes Questionnaire (SHOW‐Q) consisted of 13 items, which were grouped into 3 domains: physical and psychological influences on sexual function, sexual satisfaction, and sexual experience. Scoring procedures were standardized, with reverse coding applied to items related to interference. The total score ranged from 11 to 64, with higher values indicating better sexual functioning. Subscale scores were rescaled to a range of 0–100 to enable more specific interpretation of each domain.

Discussion

This study presents the first comprehensive evaluation of the psychometric properties of the Persian version of the Sexual Health Outcomes Questionnaire (SHOW‐Q) among Iranian women with pelvic disorders. The findings suggest that the Persian SHOW‐Q is valid and reliable and could be used in clinical and research settings with this population. These results are consistent with the satisfactory psychometric properties previously demonstrated by the original English version [10].

Face and content validity were rigorously examined through participant feedback and expert review to ensure item clarity, cultural appropriateness, and relevance. No items fell below the acceptability threshold, demonstrating the instrument's cultural adaptability. These findings are consistent with those of previous cross‐cultural adaptations of sexual health instruments, which emphasize the importance of integrating qualitative and quantitative validation methods [35, 36].

Reliability

The Persian SHOW‐Q demonstrated acceptable internal consistency, with a Cronbach's α of 0.823 for the total scale and subscale alphas ranging from 0.778 to 0.902. All of these values exceed the acceptable threshold of 0.70 [37]. These results are consistent with those of the original English SHOW‐Q (α ≈ 0.86) [10] and the Turkish version [38], which reported α values between 0.735 and 0.892.

Test‐retest reliability over a 2‐week interval indicated excellent temporal stability, with ICC values ranging from 0.967 to 0.993. This demonstrates the tool's ability to detect genuine changes in sexual function over time [39]. These values are comparable to those of other validated Persian instruments, including the APFQ‐IR (α = 0.85–0.92, ICC = 0.88), the PFDI‐20 (α = 0.85–0.91, ICC > 0.80), the FSFI (α = 0.87–0.94), and the PISQ‐12 (α = 0.84) [40, 41, 42, 43].

Construct Validity

EFA and CFA factor analyses supported a three‐factor structure, which accounted for 59.09% of the total variance and comprised the following factors: (1) physical and psychological factors affecting sexual function, (2) sexual satisfaction, and (3) sexual experience. Although the original SHOW‐Q was developed with four conceptual domains, the three‐factor structure of the Persian version emerged during cultural adaptation. The original 12‐item instrument was modified to create a 13‐item version by splitting one compound item into two separate items to improve clarity for Persian‐speaking women.

The emergence of a three‐factor structure reflects Iranian women's cultural perception of sexual health, as well as the interconnected nature of physical symptoms and sexual function, among those with pelvic disorders. This finding is not uncommon in cross‐cultural psychometric studies, where linguistic and sociocultural contexts can influence the grouping of items. Importantly, CFA confirmed the adequacy of the three‐factor model, yielding the following satisfactory fit indices: CFI = 0.933, χ 2/df = 2.825, RMSEA = 0.093, and NFI = 0.90 [44, 45].

Compared with other Persian instruments that assess sexual health and pelvic floor disorders, the Persian SHOW‐Q demonstrated excellent performance. While the PFDI‐20 and PFIQ‐7 are widely used to assess symptom distress and quality of life, they lack the SHOW‐Q's specific depth in evaluating sexual outcomes. The PISQ‐12 provides a complementary assessment of prolapse and incontinence; however, the SHOW‐Q offers a more targeted evaluation of the direct influence of pelvic problems on desire, orgasm, satisfaction, and discomfort [43]. This specificity establishes the Persian SHOW‐Q as a valuable addition to comprehensive urogynecology and pelvic floor physiotherapy evaluations.

Convergent and Discriminant Validity

Convergent validity was established as all factor loadings exceeded 0.50. CR ranged from 0.793 to 0.903 and AVE ranged from 0.495 to 0.758 [46]. These values exceed the recommended thresholds [27, 46]. Regarding discriminant validity, the Fornell–Larcker criterion was only partially met. For the Sexual Satisfaction and Sexual Experience factors, the AVE values exceeded their respective MSV values. However, for the first factor (“Physical and Psychological Factors on Sexual Function”), the MSV (0.548) slightly exceeded the AVE (0.527), indicating some conceptual overlap with the “Sexual Satisfaction” factor. This partial overlap is not unexpected given the interrelated nature of physical symptoms, psychological factors, and sexual satisfaction in women with pelvic disorders [9, 10].

Overall, the three‐factor model demonstrated generally acceptable discriminant validity, although some conceptual overlap was observed between the physical/psychological factors and the sexual satisfaction domains [38].

Clinical and Practical Implications

The availability of culturally appropriate and validated instruments, such as the Persian SHOW‐Q, alongside tools like the PFDI‐20, APFQ‐IR, and PISQ‐IR, enables a more accurate, multidimensional assessment of sexual function in women with pelvic floor disorders. This can guide targeted interventions (e.g., pelvic floor physiotherapy or surgical outcomes evaluation), improve communication between patients and clinicians, and facilitate the evaluation of treatment efficacy. Using symptom‐focused tools alongside instruments that are specific to sexual outcomes provides a comprehensive framework for clinical practice and research in Iranian settings [40, 42, 47].

Strengths and Limitations

The key strengths are the rigorous methodology, which covers face, content, construct, convergent, and discriminant validity, the robust reliability testing, and the relatively large sample size, which enhances statistical power and representativeness. Recruiting from multiple teaching hospitals ensured the inclusion of women from diverse socioeconomic backgrounds, thereby strengthening the study's generalizability.

However, only married women of reproductive age were included, which limits generalizability to unmarried women, women outside the reproductive age range, and populations with different cultural and demographic characteristics. Potential recall bias was minimized by using a 4‐week recall period. Future studies should include more diverse populations to enhance external validity.

Conclusions

This study successfully translated and validated the SHOW‐Q for Iranian women with pelvic disorders. The Persian version demonstrated satisfactory psychometric properties, including valid indicators, a stable three‐factor structure, and reliable indices. These results highlight the clinical relevance and cultural appropriateness of the Persian SHOW‐Q for evaluating sexual health outcomes in this population. The validated instrument provides healthcare providers and researchers with a practical and reliable means of assessing the impact of pelvic disorders on sexual health and well‐being. However, future studies should examine its applicability among postmenopausal women and in different cultural and regional contexts to enhance generalizability and broaden its clinical and research utility.
