The impact of preoperative regular exercise on mental health in patients undergoing Type II incision surgery
Authors: Xiwen Yang, Sibin Yi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the effect of preoperative regular exercise on postoperative mental health in patients undergoing Type II incision surgery. Explore the correlations between preoperative exercise and postoperative mental health in this patient group.MethodsA total of 480 patients who underwent Type II incision surgery at a tertiary Grade A hospital in Pingxiang from July 2024 to June 2025 were enrolled. Preoperative physical activity was assessed via the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) short form, and patients were divided into an exercise group (EH, n = 133) and a nonexercise group (NEH, n = 347) on the basis of the "sports population" criteria in China. Mental health was evaluated via the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) and the 12-Item Short-Form Health Survey (SF-12) on the 14th postoperative day.ResultsCompared with the NEH group, the EH group had significantly lower postoperative HADS-Anxiety (HADS-A) scores (5.3 ± 2.0 vs. 8.5 ± 3.3) and higher SF-12 Mental Component Summary (MCS, 49.1 ± 7.5 vs. 42.1 ± 8.3) and Physical Component Summary (PCS, 47.3 ± 7.1 vs. 42.8 ± 7.6). Correlation analysis revealed that preoperative regular exercise in the EH group was negatively associated with postoperative anxiety (r = -0.55) and positively associated with the SF-12 PCS (r = 0.72) and MCS (r = 0.60). No significant correlations were observed in the NEH group for anxiety or depression.ConclusionPreoperative regular exercise significantly alleviates postoperative anxiety and improves both physical and mental function in patients undergoing Type II incision surgery, with these effects remaining significant after adjusting for confounders. However, its effect on improving postoperative depression is not significant. Additionally, preoperative exercise is closely associated with better postoperative psychological status in the EH group, while the NEH group shows no significant correlations between preoperative activity and postoperative anxiety/depression, and weaker correlations with physical/mental function—suggesting that establishing preoperative exercise habits may be a key factor in promoting postoperative psychological recovery.Keywords: Preoperative exercise, Type II incision, Postoperative mental health, Anxiety, Physical function

Introduction

Each year, approximately 280 million individuals worldwide undergo surgical procedures, making surgery a cornerstone of modern healthcare (Weiser et al., 2008). Postoperative mental health (e.g., anxiety, depression) is not only a subjective burden but also a key predictor of objective recovery outcomes. For example, Oteri et al., (2021) found that preoperative anxiety in brain surgery patients was associated with longer hospital stays and higher rates of postoperative delirium, while depression after major surgery increases the risk of non-adherence to rehabilitation and 6-month readmissions (Hartej et al., 2019). In Type II incision patients specifically, psychological distress is further amplified by concerns about wound infection (a common complication in clean-contaminated surgeries) and long-term functional limitations—factors that can delay return to work and reduce quality of life (Jiao et al., 2021; Qu and Li 2004).

Nonpharmacological interventions are increasingly recognized as essential for mitigating postoperative psychological distress, given the potential side effects of psychiatric medications (e.g., sedation, drug interactions) in surgical patients. Preoperative exercise, as a cost-effective intervention, has shown promise in improving perioperative outcomes (Levett and Grimmett 2019). Current evidence on preoperative exercise has focused largely on physiological benefits: Jensen et al., (2016) showed that exercise prehabilitation reduced postoperative pulmonary complications in radical cystectomy patients, and a meta-analysis by Benjamin et al., (2020) confirmed that preoperative exercise improves cardiorespiratory fitness and reduces surgical site infection rates. However, data on psychological outcomes remain sparse, and existing studies often exclude Type II incision patients—who face unique stressors related to contamination risk (Blathnaid et al., 2019). For instance, Ashdown-Franks et al., (2019) reviewed physical activity and mental health in major mental illnesses but did not address surgical populations, while Larina et al., (2022) studied exercise and mood in glioma patients but focused on high-grade tumors rather than clean-contaminated surgeries. Notably, previous studies often assess single psychological factors (e.g., anxiety alone) rather than comprehensive mental and physical functions (Blathnaid et al., 2019), failing to capture the holistic interplay between physical recovery and psychological well-being after surgery.

Specific psychological sequelae after Type II incision surgery include persistent anxiety about infection, guilt related to prolonged caregiving needs, and depression due to delayed functional recovery (Jiao et al., 2021). Yet, no studies have explicitly tested whether preoperative exercise can mitigate these surgery-specific psychological stressors. This gap limits the development of targeted perioperative care strategies for this large and high-risk patient group.

This study aimed to (1) evaluate the effects of preoperative regular exercise on postoperative anxiety, depression, and physical/mental function in Type II incision patients; (2) explore correlations between preoperative exercise and postoperative mental health. By addressing these aims, we seek to inform perioperative care strategies for this high-risk population.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This prospective observational study was conducted at Pingxiang Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine (a tertiary Grade A hospital) from July 2024 to June 2025. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Pingxiang Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine (Approval No. PXTCM-IRB-2024—008) and registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400081234). All participants provided written informed consent.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) aged ≥ 18 years; (2) scheduled for Type II incision surgery (defined as surgical incisions involving clean-contaminated tissues, e.g., colorectal, urological, or orthopedic procedures with potential exposure to mucosal surfaces) (Qu and Li 2004); (3) able to complete questionnaires independently; and (4) no cognitive impairment (assessed via the Mini-Mental State Examination [MMSE] score ≥ 24).The Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) is a validated 30-point questionnaire used to assess global cognitive function, including orientation to time/place/person, attention, memory, language, and visuospatial skills (Folstein et al., 1975). For this study, a cutoff score of ≥ 24 was used to exclude patients with cognitive impairment: scores of 24—30 indicate normal cognitive function, while scores < 24 suggest mild-to-severe cognitive impairment (18—23: mild; 10—17: moderate; < 10: severe). This cutoff was selected based on its use in surgical populations to ensure participants could independently complete study questionnaires and comprehend the informed consent process.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) severe cardiovascular, respiratory, or neurological diseases (e.g., heart failure, COPD, or stroke); (2) a history of psychiatric disorders (e.g., major depressive disorder or generalized anxiety disorder) confirmed by medical records; (3) inability to engage in physical activity due to musculoskeletal limitations; and (4) postoperative complications requiring intensive care (e.g., sepsis) within 14 days.

Assessment of preoperative regular exercise

Preoperative physical activity was assessed via the Chinese version of the IPAQ short form,which was selected for several reasons: (1) It has demonstrated good reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.82) and validity in Chinese surgical populations (Maria et al., 2019), including patients undergoing clean-contaminated surgeries (e.g., colorectal, urological procedures) (Qu and Li 2004). (2) Unlike the Duke Activity Status Index (DASI)—which focuses on functional capacity (e.g., climbing stairs)—the IPAQ captures real-world exercise habits (e.g., leisure, work, transportation), making it more relevant for assessing "regular exercise" as defined by public health guidelines (National 2022). (3) While the MET-REPAIR questionnaire (Jaeger et al. 2022) is validated for surgical patients, it is not available in a Chinese version, limiting its use in our study population. The questionnaire captures physical activity across four domains (leisure, work, transportation, housework) in the 7 days prior to surgery—a time point chosen to capture recent, stable exercise habits while avoiding potential changes in activity due to preoperative anxiety or hospital admission.

"Regular exercise" was defined on the basis of China’s "sports population" criteria (National 2022), with specific parameters for activity type, intensity, duration, and frequency:

Type: Moderate-intensity activities included brisk walking, cycling, and water aerobics (3—6 metabolic equivalents [METs]); high-intensity activities included running, swimming, and high-intensity interval training (HIIT, ≥ 6 METs).

Assessment of mental health after surgery

All postoperative assessments were conducted by trained research nurses (blinded to group allocation) on the 14th postoperative day. This time point was selected because previous studies indicate that postoperative mental states (e.g., anxiety, mood fluctuations) stabilize by 2 weeks postoperatively, reducing the influence of acute surgical stress (e.g., pain, sleep disturbance) on psychological evaluations (Karlsson et al., 2019).

Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS)

A 14-item scale with two subscales HADS-Anxiety (HADS-A) and HADS-Depression (HADS-D), each scored 0–3 (total 0–21). Higher scores indicate more severe symptoms. The Chinese version of the HADS has good reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.81 for HADS-A; 0.79 for HADS-D) in surgical populations (Ware et al., 1996). Maria et al., (2019) validated the HADS in cancer patients undergoing surgery, showing that HADS-A scores > 8 accurately predict clinical anxiety, and Guan et al., (2018) used the HADS to measure anxiety in PCI patients, confirming its sensitivity to postoperative changes in mood. No validation gaps were identified for use in Type II incision patients, as the scale focuses on general anxiety/depression (rather than surgery-specific distress) and is widely used across surgical specialties.

12-Item Short-Form Health Survey (SF-12)

A validated tool assessing physical (PCS) and mental (MCS) component summaries, with scores normalized to 0–100 (higher = better function). The Chinese version of the SF-12 has strong validity in postoperative patients, with a correlation coefficient of 0.89 for the PCS and 0.87 for the MCS when compared to the longer SF-36 (Ozemek and Arena 2019)—a gold standard for health-related quality of life. While most validation studies focus on spine surgery (Ware et al., 1996), recent work by Liu et al., (2020) confirmed its validity in patients after metabolic weight loss surgery (a clean-contaminated procedure), with SF-12 scores strongly correlated with objective functional measures (e.g., 6-min walk test).

Primary endpoint: Postoperative anxiety, measured by the HADS-Anxiety (HADS-A) subscale on the 14th postoperative day. This endpoint was prioritized because anxiety is the most prevalent postoperative psychological symptom in Type II incision patients and is strongly associated with delayed recovery (Oteri et al., 2021).

Statistical analysis

The sample size was calculated a priori using G*Power 3.1.9.7 software. Based on a previous study reporting a medium effect size (d = 0.5) for preoperative exercise on postoperative anxiety (Ashdown-Franks et al., 2019), we set α = 0.05 (two-tailed) and power = 0.90. To account for potential attrition (e.g., postoperative complications requiring intensive care, loss to follow-up), we enrolled 483 patients, resulting in 480 valid participants (EH: n = 133; NEH: n = 347) after excluding ineligible cases.

Normality was tested via Shapiro‒Wilk tests and Q‒Q plots. Normally distributed data are presented as the mean ± SD; nonnormally distributed data are presented as the median (IQR). Between-group comparisons were performed via independent samples t tests (normal distribution, equal variance) or Welch’s t tests (unequal variance). Categorical variables (e.g., gender, surgical type) are presented as frequency (percentage, n [%]) and compared between groups using Pearson’s χ2 test. For small expected frequencies (n < 5), Fisher’s exact test was used instead of the χ2 test. Correlations between preoperative exercise and postoperative outcomes were analyzed via Pearson’s r. Multivariable linear regression was used to adjust for confounders (age, sex, surgical type, operative time) and identify independent associations. A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

In terms of depression level, the mean HADS-D score of the EH group was slightly lower (6.8 ± 1.8) than that of the NEH group (7.3 ± 2.7), but the t test revealed that the difference was not significant (t = −3.56, P = 0.341), which implies that adherence to exercise preoperatively could not effectively improve postoperative depression.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that preoperative regular exercise significantly alleviated postoperative anxiety and improved physical and mental function in patients undergoing Type II incision surgery, with these effects remaining significant after adjustment for confounders. Regular preoperative exercise significantly relieves patients' postoperative anxiety and improves mental health; although its ability to improve depressive symptoms is unclear, the overall positive effect on postoperative physical and mental recovery is confirmed to be effective. Additionally, patients’ ability to exercise regularly preoperatively was closely related to improvements in their postoperative psychological status: preoperative exercise was negatively associated with postoperative anxiety (r = −0.55, P = 0.04) and positively associated with SF-12 PCS (r = 0.72, P = 0.02) and MCS (r = 0.60, P = 0.01) in the EH group. In contrast, the NEH group showed no significant correlations between preoperative activity and postoperative anxiety or depression, and weaker positive correlations with SF-12 PCS (r = 0.38, P = 0.03) and MCS (r = 0.25, P = 0.04). These findings advance current knowledge by highlighting the benefits of exercise beyond physiological outcomes, particularly in high-risk populations prone to psychological stress.

The effect of regular exercise on postoperative mental health

The results showed that patients in the EH group had better postoperative HADS and SF-12 scores than those in the NEH group. This aligns with previous studies and confirms that exercise benefits mental health (Blathnaid et al., 2019; Benjamin et al., 2020; Chelsia et al., 2022; Brendon et al., 2023; Ashdown-Franks et al., 2019; Qingyuan et al., 2024). In terms of the mechanism of action, one reason for this benefit is improved physiological function. Preoperative exercise boosts cardiorespiratory fitness, muscle strength, and metabolic flexibility. This reduces the risk of postoperative complications like lung infections and longer hospital stays, which in turn lowers patients’ anxiety about their health (Benjamin et al., 2020; Blathnaid et al., 2019). As noted by Brendon et al., (2023) and two narrative reviews (Benjamin et al., 2020; Chelsia et al., 2022), there is a strong correlation between cardiorespiratory fitness and surgical prognosis. Higher preoperative peak oxygen uptake maintains the body's stability and reduces the psychological burden of respiratory dysfunction during the surge in metabolic demand in the postoperative period. Strength training not only improves respiratory muscle function but also reduces depressive mood by enhancing the sense of somatic mastery directly (Ashdown-Franks et al., 2019; Brendon et al., 2023).

In addition, the effects of different exercise types on postoperative psychology significantly differ. Endurance training (e.g., running, swimming) improves mood indirectly by enhancing cardiorespiratory function, and in high-grade glioma patients, both endurance training and active control(e.g., yoga) reduce negative moods like depression and stress in EH-like groups, but patients with high-grade gliomas, individual strength training failed to significantly reduce negative emotions due to limited physical strength and low training compliance (Eliana et al., 2021); In adolescents or general surgical patients with good physical foundation, strength training can effectively reduce anxiety by improving muscle strength and self-efficacy, combined with social support through group training (Larina et al., 2022). Group strength training can also reduce anxiety through social support (Eliana et al., 2021). High-intensity interval training (HIIT) is beneficial for patients with psychiatric disorders, but its high dropout rate limits its application (Levett and Grimmett 2019). Active control exercises such as yoga and meditation directly regulate mood by increasing the sense of self-control (Larina et al., 2022). This suggests that exercise programs need to be adjusted according to the type of surgery and individual patient characteristics; for example, aerobic + resistance training can be used to improve the quality of life of lung cancer patients (Jin-Hee et al., 2019), and cardiovascular surgery is more suitable for low-intensity respiratory muscle training to reduce intraoperative stress (Kian et al., 2019).

Notably, the effects of exercise on mental health do not rely solely on physiological changes. Cai et al. reported that physical activity during pregnancy directly moderates mood by lowering the levels of stress hormones, such as cortisol (Neumann et al., 2021), and Luo et al. reported that exercise-induced release of neurotransmitters, such as endorphins, is key to alleviating anxiety and depression (Chenxi et al., 2022). A deeper mechanism lies in the fact that prolonged exercise improves mitochondrial function and neuroplasticity in the hippocampus, a mechanism that has been demonstrated in posttraumatic stress disorder models (Qingyuan et al., 2024). In this study, we hypothesized that regular preoperative exercise may enable the body to establish a stable neurotransmitter regulatory mechanism, thus maintaining better emotion regulation even under postoperative stress.

Correlations between regular exercise and postoperative mental health

The correlation analysis of this study revealed that regular preoperative exercise was significantly positively correlated with postoperative mental health indicators, which were negatively correlated with the frequency of exercise and significantly positively correlated with exercise intensity. The effect of preoperative exercise on postoperative psychological well-being is not a simple “quantitative-effect relationship” (Ware et al., 1996); rather, there is a more complex path of action. From a mechanistic point of view, on the one hand, preoperative exercise can mediate the process of psychological improvement by enhancing self-efficacy. Neumann et al. reported that muscle strength, especially respiratory muscle strength, is positively correlated with stress resilience and that self-efficacy plays a key intermediary role in this process (Jolanda et al., 2022), i.e., preoperative exercise improves a patient's confidence in his or her own ability to recover and results in “physiological improvement → self-efficacy enhancement → psychological adaptation” (Neumann et al., 2021). That is, preoperative exercise enhances patients' confidence in their ability to recover, resulting in a virtuous cycle of “physiological improvement → self-efficacy enhancement → psychological adaptation enhancement”. This is particularly evident in ACL reconstruction patients, where the risk of a second ACL injury is greater in patients with a lower level of psychological preparedness than in those with a higher level of psychological preparedness, which is related to injury-related fear, which reduces the likelihood of returning to exercise by 17% (Guan et al., 2018).

On the other hand, regular exercise in the preoperative period may have a “behavioral inertia” effect, as regular exercisers have better preoperative baseline characteristics, such as lower BMI, diabetes mellitus, and surgical risk scores (Kian et al., 2019), and the continuity of this pattern of healthy behaviors makes it easier for patients to adopt positive coping strategies in the postoperative period. Zhang Z and Chen W concluded from a systematic review of the relationship between physical activity and well-being that there is a positive association between physical activity and well-being (Zhang and Chen 2018). From a neurobiological perspective, long-term exercise alters the secretion patterns of neurotransmitters (e.g., serotonin and dopamine), extends the duration of their stimulation, enhances the activation of remote neural populations associated with motivated behavior, and thus continues to play a positive role in emotion regulation during postoperative stress (Guangding et al., 2018; Li and Jasanoff 2020; Zhang and Chen 2018). Whereas the relationship between exercise frequency and psychological well-being is not a simple linear relationship, studies have shown that even only 10 min of physical activity per week or one exercise session per week may lead to increased well-being (Ware et al., 1996), but high-frequency exercise (e.g., ≥ 5 sessions per week) may lead to psychological burnout due to fatigue accumulation, manifesting as depressed mood and sleep disturbances (Valerian et al., 2019). The positive correlation between exercise intensity and psychological well-being suggests that a reasonable range of elevated exercise intensity may be more conducive to psychological regulation. High-intensity exercise (e.g., one high-intensity interval training session per week) can increase hippocampal volume, increase learning and memory capacity, and indirectly improve mood regulation (Ozemek and Arena 2019); moderate-intensity (50%−60% of maximal heart rate) and high-intensity (70%−85% of maximal heart rate) exercise can significantly improve depressive symptoms (Chen et al., 2021). Therefore, the load and type of exercise should be individualized, especially for special groups.

In addition, social support plays an important role in this association. Social support has a positive effect on promoting mental health recovery in postoperative patients. A quantitative study revealed a moderate positive association between social support and posttraumatic growth (PTG), which was more pronounced among caregivers, the Chinese community, and older individuals (Jing et al., 2022). A qualitative study revealed that family support and healthcare guidance were key to narrowing patients’ “willingness-to-exercise-behavior gap”. A qualitative study revealed that family support and healthcare guidance are key to narrowing the “exercise intention‒behavior gap” (Karlsson et al., 2019; Natali et al., 2023) and that effective communication between clinicians and patients has a direct impact on their participation in treatment (Ozemek and Arena 2019). Such social support not only alleviates postoperative loneliness but also enhances confidence in rehabilitation through the modeling effect.

In conclusion, preoperative regular exercise significantly reduces postoperative anxiety and improves physical and mental function in Type II incision patients. These findings support preoperative exercise as a feasible perioperative intervention. Future research should explore optimal exercise regimens and the mechanisms underlying their effects on depression.

Conclusion

Preoperative regular exercise can significantly improve postoperative anxiety and overall mental health in patients undergoing Type II incision surgery, but its effect on improving postoperative depression is limited. Additionally, regular preoperative exercise was closely related to better postoperative psychological status in the EH group: it was negatively associated with postoperative anxiety and positively associated with SF-12 PCS and MCS. In contrast, the NEH group showed no significant correlations between preoperative activity and postoperative anxiety or depression, and weaker positive correlations with physical and mental function. This suggests that establishing exercise habits before surgery may be a key factor affecting postoperative psychological recovery.

However, this study has limitations. The single-center design limits generalizability, so multicenter studies are needed. We only assessed exercise within 7 days preoperatively and did not measure long-term exercise habits, This study did not collect data on other meaningful clinical outcomes, such as postoperative return to function (e.g., time to walk independently) or days alive and at home at 30 days (DAH30). These outcomes are critical for translating psychological benefits into real-world clinical value, and future studies should include them to fully evaluate the impact of preoperative exercise on perioperative recovery. Mechanism analysis is exploratory; future studies should measure biomarkers (e.g., cortisol, endorphins) to confirm neurobiological paths. The lack of effect on depression may be due to low baseline HADS-D scores or insufficient exercise duration, so longer interventions (e.g., 8 weeks preoperatively) should be tested.
