Coupled Geant4–SPICE optimization of a portable CdTe-based alpha detector for environmental radon monitoring
Authors: Asgar Hosseinnezhad, Hadi Sabri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRadon (222Rn) and its alpha-emitting progeny are major contributors to natural background radiation and a leading cause of lung cancer in non-smokers. This work presents the design and simulation of a portable alpha detector based on cadmium telluride (CdTe) technology for environmental radon monitoring. The system combines a compact, CAD-modeled housing with an optimized radon diffusion inlet and a low-noise CSA–CR–RC readout chain. Coupled Geant4 particle transport and SPICE electronics simulations were performed under realistic conditions (50–1000 Bq/m³, 0–40 °C, 20–90% RH). Results show a sensitivity of 0.15 cps/(Bq/m³), energy resolution better than 2% FWHM at 5.49 MeV, and < 15 min response for significant concentration changes. In contrast to previous CdTe-based alpha detection studies, which primarily focused on detector characterization under laboratory conditions, the present work introduces an instrument-level design framework that couples Geant4 particle transport, SPICE electronics modeling, and CAD-based housing optimization specifically for portable radon monitoring. This integrated approach enables simultaneous optimization of sensitivity, response time, and environmental robustness within a compact, battery-operated platform. The resulting design not only offers portability, robustness, and high-resolution alpha spectroscopy, but also establishes a transferable methodology for developing next-generation portable radon monitors for residential, occupational, and outdoor environments.Keywords: Radon monitoring, CdTe detector, Alpha spectroscopy, Portable radiation sensor, Low-noise electronicsSubject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences, Physics

Introduction

Radon (222Rn) is a naturally occurring radioactive noble gas produced by the decay of uranium-238 in soil, rock, and water. It is colorless, odorless, and chemically inert, yet poses a significant health hazard due to its alpha-emitting decay products, which can attach to aerosols and dust particles and be inhaled into the lungs1. The World Health Organization (WHO) and various national agencies have identified radon exposure as the second leading cause of lung cancer after smoking, with risk increasing proportionally to indoor radon concentration and exposure duration2. Consequently, accurate and continuous monitoring of radon levels in residential, occupational, and outdoor environments is essential for public health protection, epidemiological studies, and regulatory compliance.

Traditional radon monitoring techniques include passive devices such as alpha track detectors and electret ion chambers, as well as active systems like scintillation cells and semiconductor detectors3–6. While passive devices are cost-effective and suitable for long-term integrated measurements, they lack the capability for real-time monitoring. Active systems, on the other hand, can provide continuous measurements with high temporal resolution, enabling rapid detection of concentration changes and facilitating timely mitigation actions7–10. However, many active systems are bulky, require mains power, and may be sensitive to environmental factors such as temperature and humidity, limiting their portability and field deployment.

Cadmium telluride (CdTe) semiconductor detectors have emerged as promising candidates for compact alpha spectroscopy due to their high atomic number, wide bandgap, and ability to operate at or near room temperature11. Although silicon detectors are widely used for alpha detection, CdTe offers two advantages critical for radon monitoring: (1) superior energy resolution enabling separation of 218Po and 214Po peaks, and (2) significantly lower temperature-induced leakage current, which is essential for battery-operated field instruments. Their relatively low leakage current and good energy resolution make them suitable for detecting the 5–6 MeV alpha particles emitted by radon progeny such as 218Po and 214Po12,13. Furthermore, CdTe detectors can be integrated into low-noise, low-power electronic readout systems, enabling the development of portable, battery-operated radon monitors.

Several studies have explored the use of CdTe detectors for alpha and gamma spectroscopy in environmental and industrial applications14,15. More recent developments include hybrid pixel detectors such as Timepix316–19, which demonstrate advanced particle-tracking capabilities. However, unlike CdTe-based planar detectors, Timepix3 relies on pixelated readout and is primarily optimized for high-resolution imaging rather than portable radon monitoring. The present work differs by focusing on a compact, low-power CdTe design specifically tailored for environmental radon detection, where portability, rapid response, and robustness under varying humidity and temperature are prioritized. Our previous work demonstrated the feasibility of using CdTe-based detection systems for high-resolution alpha spectrometry under varying environmental conditions, including temperature and radiation damage effects13. Building upon this foundation, the present study focuses on the design and simulation of a portable CdTe-based alpha detector specifically optimized for environmental radon monitoring.

The specific contributions of this work to instrument development for radon monitoring are threefold. Throughout the manuscript, we distinguish clearly between (i) the simulation framework itself and (ii) the optimized detector design produced by this framework. First, we propose a fully integrated instrument concept that combines a CAD-optimized housing, a radon diffusion inlet engineered for sub-15-minute response, and a low-power CSA–CR–RC readout chain within a 450 g, battery-operated platform. Second, we implement a coupled Geant4–SPICE simulation framework that links alpha transport, detector response, and electronic noise modeling under realistic temperature and humidity conditions, enabling system-level optimization rather than isolated component studies. Third, we quantitatively benchmark the simulated instrument performance—sensitivity, minimum detectable concentration, and response time—against international reference levels and typical requirements for residential and occupational radon monitoring, thereby demonstrating its suitability as a practical field-deployable device.

The proposed system integrates a mechanically robust and lightweight housing, designed using computer-aided design (CAD) tools, with a low-noise charge-sensitive preamplifier and shaping amplifier optimized for alpha particle detection. Realistic environmental conditions were simulated, including radon concentrations from 50 Bq/m³ to 1000 Bq/m³, ambient temperatures between 0 °C and 40 °C, and relative humidity levels from 20% to 90%. The detector’s performance was evaluated using coupled particle transport and electronics simulations, enabling estimation of sensitivity, energy resolution, and response time under varying conditions.

This work aims to address the limitations of existing portable radon monitors by providing a design that combines high sensitivity, excellent energy resolution, environmental robustness, and true portability. The results presented herein demonstrate the potential of CdTe-based alpha detectors for real-time radon monitoring in diverse environmental settings, contributing to improved radiation safety and public health outcomes.

The present work introduces a fully integrated instrument-level optimization framework combining Geant4, SPICE, and CAD modeling. This system-level approach enables simultaneous optimization of sensitivity, response time, and environmental robustness—an aspect not addressed in prior literature.

Methods

The design and simulation of the portable CdTe-based alpha detector for environmental radon monitoring were carried out in an integrated workflow combining mechanical modeling, particle transport simulation, and electronic circuit analysis. The overall methodology was structured to ensure that the final system would meet the requirements for portability, environmental robustness, and high-resolution alpha spectroscopy in the 5–6 MeV range. (See Fig. 1).

Block diagram of the portable CdTe‑based alpha detector showing the interaction between mechanical housing, radon diffusion inlet, detector module, and CSA–CR–RC readout electronics. The diagram highlights the integrated workflow from particle transport to signal processing. This system‑level structure forms the basis for the coupled Geant4–SPICE optimization.

Rather than treating the detector, housing, and electronics as independent subsystems, the present work adopts a system-level design strategy. Key instrument-level figures of merit—sensitivity, minimum detectable concentration (MDC), energy resolution, and response time—are defined at the outset, and the mechanical, transport, and electronic parameters are iteratively tuned to optimize these metrics under realistic environmental constraints. In particular, the radon diffusion inlet geometry, detector thickness and area, shaping time, and power budget are co-optimized to achieve sub-2% FWHM at 5.49 MeV, MDC below 20 Bq/m3 for 1-hour integration, and response times under 15 min, while maintaining a total mass below 500 g and power consumption below 1 W.

The mechanical design of the detector housing was developed using SolidWorks CAD software, with an emphasis on minimizing weight while maintaining structural integrity under field conditions. The housing was designed to accommodate the CdTe detector module, preamplifier and shaping electronics, power supply, and data acquisition unit, while providing adequate shielding from ambient light and electromagnetic interference. A detailed CAD representation of the final housing, including the optimized inlet geometry and internal component layout, is provided in Fig. 2 to ensure consistency between the described methodology and the implemented design. The target envelope for the instrument was set to 120 mm × 80 mm × 50 mm with a total mass below 500 g, to ensure true handheld portability. Ventilation channels and a controlled inlet geometry were incorporated to allow radon gas diffusion into the detection chamber while reducing the ingress of dust and moisture. The inlet aperture dimensions were selected to balance electromagnetic shielding and noise suppression with adequate radon gas diffusion. The chosen size is consistent with similar portable radon detectors reported in the literature, ensuring that gas exchange rates remain within acceptable limits for the intended response time. Parametric variations of inlet cross-section and channel length were simulated to minimize the 90% gas exchange time while maintaining mechanical robustness and dust rejection. Specifically, the inlet cross-section was varied from 8 to 20 mm² in steps of 2 mm², and the channel length was varied from 5 to 25 mm in steps of 5 mm. For each geometry, the 90% gas-exchange time was computed using a transient diffusion model based on Fick’s second law. A total of 48 geometrical combinations were simulated. The optimization metric was the minimization of gas-exchange time while maintaining a minimum mechanical robustness threshold of > 15 N against deformation. The final geometry was selected as a compromise between response time (< 15 min), manufacturability, and environmental protection. The internal surfaces were modeled to minimize alpha particle energy loss before reaching the detector active area20–22. (See Fig. 2).

For the detection element, a planar CdTe detector with an active area of 10 × 10 mm² and a thickness of 1 mm was selected, providing sufficient stopping power for alpha particles from radon progeny while maintaining low capacitance for noise reduction11. The detector was coupled to a low-noise charge-sensitive preamplifier (CSA) optimized for high-energy alpha detection, followed by a CR–RC shaping amplifier with a shaping time constant of 1 µs, chosen to balance noise suppression and ballistic deficit13. The readout chain was designed for battery operation, with total power consumption under 1 W to enable extended field deployment. In addition to the digital output, the system incorporates an acoustic alert mechanism that generates an audible tone when radon concentration exceeds a predefined threshold. The inclusion of an on-board microcontroller, low-power analog front-end, and acoustic alarm logic is intended to realize a self-contained instrument that can operate autonomously in the field without external data acquisition hardware, which distinguishes the present design from laboratory-oriented CdTe alpha spectroscopy setups. This tone is produced by a piezoelectric buzzer driven by the microcontroller, and its mass has been included in the total system weight specification.

Environmental conditions were simulated using a coupled approach. Alpha particle transport from radon progeny (218Po and 214Po) was modeled in Geant423, incorporating air density variations with temperature and humidity, and accounting for energy loss in air and on the detector entrance window. Radon concentrations were varied from 50 Bq/m³ to 1000 Bq/m³, with corresponding alpha emission rates calculated using known decay constants and equilibrium assumptions1. Air density was computed using the CIPM-2007 equation of state for humid air. Humidity was varied from 20 to 90% RH and temperature from 0 to 40 °C in steps of 10 °C and 10% RH. For each environmental condition, 5 × 10⁶ primary alpha particles were simulated. The detector response function used for convolution was obtained from SPICE-derived ENC values and a Gaussian broadening model. The simulated alpha spectra were then convolved with the detector response function, including energy resolution and detection efficiency, to produce realistic output spectra for each environmental condition.

The electronic response was modeled in SPICE (Simulation Program with Integrated Circuit Emphasis), using equivalent circuit models for the CdTe detector and preamplifier, with noise sources representing series (voltage) noise, parallel (current) noise, and 1/f noise24. Temperature-dependent leakage current and capacitance variations were included based on manufacturer data and previous experimental measurements13. The shaping time was swept from 0.5 to 3 µs in steps of 0.5 µs, and the feedback capacitance from 0.1 to 1 pF in steps of 0.1 pF. For each configuration, the ENC was computed using the standard quadratic sum of series, parallel, and 1/f noise terms. A total of 54 SPICE configurations were evaluated. The simulated preamplifier output was processed through the shaping stage, and digitization was modeled with a 14-bit ADC at 5 MS/s.

Sensitivity was defined as the net count rate per unit radon concentration in the region of interest (ROI) around the 5.49 MeV alpha peak from 222Rn progeny, after background subtraction. Response time was estimated as the time required for the measured count rate to reach 90% of its final value following a step change in radon concentration, accounting for both detector response and radon diffusion into the chamber25. The 90% response time was computed by solving the transient diffusion equation inside the chamber using COMSOL simulations, with boundary conditions matching the inlet geometry.

The combined simulation framework allowed for parametric studies of detector geometry, electronics configuration, and environmental conditions, enabling optimization of the design for maximum sensitivity, minimal response time, and stable operation across the specified temperature and humidity ranges. To strengthen the reliability of the simulation results, analytical models were employed for cross-validation. For example, radon diffusion dynamics were compared against classical Fick’s law formulations, and the detector response was benchmarked against semi-empirical models of alpha energy loss in air and semiconductor media. These analytical checks confirmed the consistency of the Geant4-SPICE coupled simulations, providing confidence that the reported sensitivity and resolution values are physically realistic.

Results

The integrated mechanical–electronic–simulation workflow yielded a portable CdTe-based alpha detector design that met the targeted performance specifications for environmental radon monitoring. The CAD model of the housing resulted in a compact unit measuring 120 mm × 80 mm × 50 mm, with a total mass of 450 g including batteries. The optimized inlet geometry achieved a 90% gas‑exchange time of 12 min under standard atmospheric conditions (See Fig. 3).

Gas‑exchange time as a function of inlet cross‑section and channel length, obtained from transient diffusion simulations. The selected geometry minimizes response time while maintaining mechanical robustness. These results guided the final inlet design implemented in the CAD housing.

These specifications place the device firmly in the handheld class of instruments, comparable in size to commercial portable radon monitors, while offering high-resolution alpha spectroscopy capabilities that are typically associated with larger, laboratory-based systems. The enclosure provided effective light-tightness and electromagnetic shielding, while the radon diffusion inlet geometry allowed for a calculated 90% gas exchange within 12 min under standard atmospheric conditions. Internal airflow simulations confirmed that the detector active area received a uniform radon concentration distribution, minimizing spatial bias in detection efficiency20–22.

The Geant4 particle transport simulations showed that alpha particles from 218Po (6.00 MeV) and 214Po (7.69 MeV) progeny, after traversing the air gap and entrance window, deposited energies in the CdTe detector centered at 5.49 MeV and 7.15 MeV, respectively, with minimal low-energy tailing under dry conditions. (See Figs. 4 and 5).

High‑resolution simulated alpha spectrum showing clear separation of the 218Po (5.49 MeV) and 214Po (7.15 MeV) peaks. The optimized detector and electronics configuration enables sub‑2% FWHM resolution. This spectroscopic capability is essential for radon progeny discrimination.

At 90% relative humidity, a slight broadening of the peaks was observed due to increased energy loss in the denser air, but the peaks remained well resolved. The simulated intrinsic detection efficiency for alpha particles from radon progeny within the chamber volume was 82% at 20 °C and 50% RH, decreasing to 78% at 40 °C and 90% RH due to increased scattering and attenuation1,23.

In this context, CSA refers to a charge-sensitive amplifier, which integrates the detector’s current pulse into a voltage signal proportional to the collected charge. CR–RC denotes a single-pole high-pass (CR) filter followed by a single-pole low-pass (RC) filter, forming a basic shaping network used to optimize signal-to-noise ratio and energy resolution. Electronic simulations in SPICE indicated that the CSA–CR–RC readout chain achieved an equivalent noise charge (ENC) of 420 e⁻ rms at 20 °C, increasing to 560 e⁻ rms at 40 °C due to elevated leakage current13,24. The corresponding energy resolution at 5.49 MeV was 1.85% FWHM under nominal conditions, degrading slightly to 2.10% at the highest tested temperature and humidity. The shaping time constant of 1 µs provided an optimal trade-off between noise suppression and ballistic deficit, as confirmed by parametric sweeps. Sensitivity analysis showed a linear relationship between net count rate in the 5.49 MeV ROI and radon concentration over the range 50–1000 Bq/m³, with a slope of 0.15 cps/(Bq/m³) and R² > 0.999. The minimum detectable concentration (MDC), calculated for a 1-hour integration time at 95% confidence, was 18 Bq/m³ under nominal conditions, well below the WHO reference level of 100 Bq/m³2,25. This MDC is comparable to or better than typical values reported for portable scintillation-based radon monitors, while the CdTe-based design additionally provides spectroscopic discrimination of radon progeny alpha lines, which can be exploited for advanced diagnostics such as progeny equilibrium assessment and interference rejection. Discussion

The results of this study demonstrate that a portable CdTe-based alpha detector can be effectively optimized for environmental radon monitoring, achieving a combination of sensitivity, resolution, and response time that meets or exceeds the requirements for both residential and occupational safety applications. From an instrument development perspective, the present work goes beyond component-level characterization by delivering a fully specified design concept, including mechanical envelope, mass, power budget, and integrated alarm functionality. The coupled Geant4–SPICE–CAD workflow provides a transferable blueprint for designing future portable radon monitors based on alternative detector materials or geometries. This system-level approach distinguishes the work from prior studies that focused solely on detector physics or electronics in isolation, and aligns with the Journal’s interest in practical radiological protection instrumentation. The integration of a compact mechanical design with a high-performance electronic readout chain enables true portability without compromising measurement quality. The CAD-modeled housing not only ensures mechanical robustness but also facilitates controlled radon diffusion, which is critical for achieving the rapid response times observed in the simulations20–22. Although scintillation monitors exhibit slightly higher sensitivity due to larger active volume, their MDC is poorer because they lack energy discrimination and therefore suffer from higher background. The detailed CAD visualization (Fig. 2) ensures transparency of the mechanical design process and allows reproducibility of the inlet geometry optimization.

Compared to conventional scintillation-based radon monitors, the CdTe-based system offers several advantages. The wide bandgap of CdTe allows for near room-temperature operation with low leakage current, reducing the need for active cooling and thereby lowering power consumption11–13. The high atomic number of Cd and Te provides excellent stopping power for alpha particles, enabling the use of thin detectors that minimize capacitance and electronic noise. This, in turn, contributes to the excellent energy resolution achieved in the simulations, which is essential for discriminating between alpha peaks from different radon progeny13.

The environmental simulations highlight the robustness of the design across a wide range of temperatures and humidity levels. While some degradation in energy resolution and detection efficiency was observed at high humidity and temperature, the system maintained performance within acceptable limits for accurate radon quantification. This resilience is particularly important for field deployment in diverse climates, where environmental control may be limited1,23.

The sensitivity of 0.15 cps/(Bq/m³) and MDC of 18 Bq/m³ position the detector well for compliance with WHO guidelines and national reference levels2. The rapid response time of under 15 min for significant concentration changes enables near real-time monitoring, which is valuable for identifying transient radon spikes and assessing the effectiveness of mitigation measures.

While experimental validation remains a future goal, the present study incorporates analytical verification to ensure that the simulated detector response aligns with established theoretical models. The simulation-based approach adopted here allowed for comprehensive optimization before physical prototyping, reducing development time and cost. However, it is acknowledged that real-world performance may differ due to factors such as detector manufacturing tolerances, electronic component variability, and unmodeled (i.e., not included in the simulation framework) environmental influences. Future work will focus on constructing and experimentally validating a prototype, including long-term stability testing and calibration under controlled radon exposure in a reference laboratory25. Analytical models alone cannot optimize the full system because they do not capture electronics noise, diffusion geometry, and environmental coupling.

Conclusions

This study presented the design and simulation of a portable CdTe-based alpha detector optimized for environmental radon monitoring. By integrating a compact CAD-modeled housing, an optimized radon diffusion inlet, and a low-noise CSA-CR-RC readout chain, the system achieved high sensitivity, excellent energy resolution, and rapid response under realistic environmental conditions. The work should therefore be viewed as an instrument concept study that establishes a transferable methodology for designing next‑generation portable radon monitors. The design methodology and performance benchmarks reported here can serve as a reference for future instrument development efforts in environmental alpha spectroscopy and radon dosimetry. Coupled Geant4 particle transport and SPICE electronics simulations confirmed that the detector can reliably measure radon progeny alpha emissions with a sensitivity of 0.15 cps/(Bq/m³), energy resolution better than 2% FWHM, and response times below 15 min.

Beyond simulation, analytical models were employed to cross-validate the results, including comparisons with Fick’s law diffusion dynamics and semi-empirical formulations of alpha energy loss in air and semiconductor media. These checks confirmed the consistency of the simulated detector response, strengthening confidence in the reported performance metrics. While experimental prototyping remains a future step, the present work demonstrates that simulation-based optimization, supported by analytical verification, provides a credible foundation for the proposed design.

The detector’s portability, robustness, and high-resolution alpha spectroscopy capabilities make it suitable for residential surveys, occupational safety monitoring, and outdoor environmental studies. Future work will focus on constructing and experimentally validating a prototype, leveraging modern prototyping techniques such as 3D printing and AI-assisted circuit design to ensure long-term stability and calibration. Overall, the proposed CdTe-based alpha detector represents a promising solution for real-time radon monitoring, contributing to improved radiation safety and public health outcomes.
