A first case of subdural empyema due to Staphylococcus saccharolyticus
Authors: Masahiko Kaneko, Tomoki Shinohara, Yuya Masuda, Kenichi Ishikawa, Hisaharu Shikata, Chie Sakisuka, Daisuke Syoda, Akira Fukui, Kiichirou Zenke
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Staphylococcus saccharolyticus, an anaerobic coagulase-negative staphylococcal species, is a member of the normal skin microbiota. It can be a rare cause of human infectious disease and is usually considered a contaminant, but some rare reports have described deep-seated infections caused by S. saccharolyticus. Intracranial subdural empyema, a life-threatening condition that requires early diagnosis and emergency intervention, can be caused by various pathogens. A 54-year-old man with no pre-existing medical conditions other than hypertension was transferred to our emergency department because of progressively worsening consciousness impairment. As non-contrast head computed tomography revealed hematoma-like collection in the subdural space, urgent drainage was performed via multiple burr holes. Perioperative tissue culture yielded monomicrobial growth of S. saccharolyticus after 4 days. Meropenem was administered in the immediate postoperative period, and later replaced with Penicillin G for six weeks. The patient is now in good clinical condition more than 18 months after treatment. This represents the first reported case of subdural empyema caused by S. saccharolyticus in an immunocompetent adult patient with a review of the pertinent literature. S. saccharolyticus should be added to the list of anaerobic microorganisms that can to cause subdural empyema. Prolonged anaerobic culture is critical to improve the yield of this possibly underestimated pathogen.

Introduction

Staphylococcus saccharolyticus (formerly known as Peptococcus saccharolyticus), a member of the normal skin microbiota, is a rarely encountered coagulase-negative staphylococcal species and the only anaerobic species of the genus Staphylococcus [1]. Recently it has been clarified that this organism is the third most abundant coagulase-negative staphylococcal species on human skin, but its role on normal human skin is unknown [2]. When correctly identified, it has often been regarded as a contaminant originating from the skin. In recent years, widespread adoption of molecular methods including 16S rRNA gene sequencing and matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization-time of flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) has improved detection [3]. Some isolated reports have described deep-seated infectious diseases in humans caused by S. saccharolyticus, but – as yet – no cases of intracranial subdural empyema.

Intracranial subdural empyema is a serious condition with significant morbidity and mortality. Early diagnosis, emergency surgery, extended antibiotic therapy, and concurrent source control employing a multidisciplinary approach are essential for management. The most common microorganisms involved are anaerobes, aerobic streptococci, staphylococci, Haemophilus influenzae, Streptococcus pneumoniae, and other gram-negative bacilli. Many infections are polymicrobial [4]. Here we describe the first confirmed case of intracranial subdural empyema caused by monomicrobial S. saccharolyticus infection in an immunocompetent adult patient with no underlying diseases as a source of infection.

Case report

A 54-year-old Japanese man with no pre-existing medical conditions other than hypertension was found lying on the street near his home, exhibiting impaired consciousness, vomiting, and incontinence. He was brought to a local hospital’s emergency room. Colleagues from his company confirmed that he had been drinking with them until five hours earlier and had been well. Non-contrast head computed tomography (CT) revealed a significant hypodense collection on the right convexity of the brain, along with a small amount of bleeding and associated mass effect causing midline shift (Fig. 1A, B). The patient was immediately transferred to our hospital emergency room two hours after the incident. On arrival, his Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score was 11 (E2, V3, M6). Vital sign data included an axillary temperature of 37.8 °C, a pulse rate of 80 bpm with a regular rhythm, blood pressure reading of 177/101 mmHg, respiration rate of 15 breaths/min, and oxygen saturation of 97 % (upon administration of 3 liters of oxygen via a nasal cannula). Neurological examination revealed a pupil diameter of 3.5 mm with normal light reflex bilaterally, but left hemiparesis was evident. Findings of other physical examinations were normal. Initial blood tests showed a slightly elevated white blood cell count of 9100/μL, while the hemoglobin level and platelet count were within the normal ranges. The results of additional laboratory tests, including serum levels of C-reactive protein, liver function tests, clotting screen, hemoglobin A1c, and arterial blood gases, were all within the normal ranges. However, elevated levels of aminotransferase (56 U/L; normal range 13–33 U/L), lactate dehydrogenase (236 U/L; normal range 100–200 U/L), creatine kinase (1115 U/L; normal range 62–287 U/L), and serum amylase (457 U/L; normal range 33–120 U/L) were evident. Fifteen minutes post-admission, the patient’s consciousness deteriorated significantly (GCS score of 3; E1, V1, M4) and he developed anisocoria with a dilated right pupil. A contrast-enhanced imaging study was deemed unsafe. With a preliminary diagnosis of chronic subdural hematoma exerting significant mass effect, an intravenous bolus of 1 g cefazolin was administered as prophylaxis. Urgent single burr-hole drainage was performed. Intraoperatively, a thick outer membrane was noted over the hematoma. Upon incising this membrane, a small quantity of altered blood was aspirated. The posterior inner membrane was thickened and adherent, limiting further dissection. Hematoma cavity irrigation and drainage tube placement were completed. Following surgery, the patient’s level of consciousness improved, although left-sided hemiparesis remained. Postoperative CT demonstrated a substantial residual subdural collection posterior and inferior to the initial hematoma site, with ongoing midline shift (Fig. 1C). Suspecting an infected subdural hematoma due to the presence of a thickened and adherent inner membrane, a second burr-hole drainage, tube placement, and saline washout were performed 4 cm posterior to the original incision, two hours after the initial procedure. The blood clot collected intraoperatively was sent for microbiological evaluation. Empirical antimicrobial treatment with meropenem was promptly initiated. Although Gram staining of the specimen was negative, visible growth of Gram-positive cocci was observed in HK semi-solid medium after 80 h of incubation in ambient air. This bacillus was identified correctly (99.9％ confidence) as S. saccharolyticus by matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization-time of flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS)-based VITEK MS (Sysmex bioMerieux, Tokyo, Japan). The 16S gene sequence of this strain was analyzed to nearly full length (1476 bp) at TechnoSuruga Laboratory (Shizuoka, Japan). This sequence best matched, at 99.9％−99.7 % (one to five nucleotide mismatches), those of the S. saccharolyticus type strain and strain ATCC14953 (GenBank accession no. L37602). Therefore, we identified this organism as S. saccharolyticus. The isolate showed susceptibility to beta-lactam antibiotics, including penicillins, cephalosporins, and carbapenems, as well as clindamycin. Based on the results of susceptibility testing, the antimicrobial agent was then switched to penicillin G. Gadolinium-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging on postoperative day 4 demonstrated thick residual subdural fluid collection with no surrounding edema. T1-weighted imaging revealed iso-to-high signal intensity with rim enhancement (Fig. 2A), while diffusion-weighted imaging showed hypointense to partially hyperintense areas (Fig. 2B). Systemic examinations, including otolaryngology and dental evaluations, revealed no infection sources. The patient completed six weeks of antimicrobial therapy and was discharged with no neurological deficits. Discussion

S. saccharolyticus is rarely associated with human disease, and diagnosing infections caused by this bacterium presents specific challenges. These challenges stems from its specific requirement for anaerobic growth conditions, extended incubation times (>3 days), and fastidious growth that are uncommon compared to nearly all other coagulase-negative staphylococcal species known to date [1], [2]. Additionally, S. saccharolyticus may be easily outcompeted by fast-growing species such as S. epidermidis on standard growth media [5]. These factors may have contributed to the underrecognition of S. saccharolyticus in culture-based studies. Although typically considered a contaminant, S. saccharolyticus has been occasionally associated with foreign body-related infections such as prosthetic joint infection and structural heart valve disease [6], [7]. Nevertheless, infections directly attributable to S. saccharolyticus remain infrequent, with only a few clinical cases reported in the literature, including spondylodiscitis, endocarditis, pneumonia, low-grade infections of the shoulder, bone marrow infection, pyomyositis, empyema and a hospital outbreak of bacteremia [8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15].

In this case, the patient presented with symptoms consistent with an intracranial subdural empyema (SDE), such as impaired consciousness and focal neurological deficits. Radiological findings on gadolinium-enhanced MRI further corroborated the presence of SDE, showing features typical of this condition. The intraoperative discovery of a thickened inner membrane and a well-defined outer membrane encapsulating the hematoma strongly suggested that the subdural collection had progressed from a hematoma to an empyema, likely due to infection. The monomicrobial growth of S. saccharolyticus from the aseptic collection and culture of subdural specimen, combined with the patient's positive response to targeted antimicrobial therapy, provided further support for the bacterium's pathogenic role in this case.

Intracranial SDE refers to a localized collection of pus within the subdural space, situated between the dura mater and arachnoid mater. Intracranial SDE is often characterized by signs and symptoms of infection and increased intracranial pressure; however, it can also remain asymptomatic in its early stages. Typically, the symptoms of intracranial SDE are progressive and may worsen if left untreated. In some cases, patients can deteriorate rapidly, experiencing drowsiness, stupor, coma, or even death. The etiology of SDE can be multifaceted; certain predisposing factors linked to its development include prior cranial surgery, head trauma with open skull fracture or penetrating injury, and untreated ear or sinus infections. The infection can disseminate from nearby sources or through distant blood pathways [16]. In rare instances, as hypothesized in our case, intracranial SDE can arise following hematogenous seeding of a pre-existing intracranial subdural hematoma, converting the hematoma from a relatively indolent pathologic process into a rapidly progressive one [17]. Given the potential for extremely atypical presentations, any delay in diagnosis in this context can result in higher mortality rates. Our hypothesis is that the hematoma in this patient became infected during a transient episode of S. saccharolyticus bacteremia, possibly originating from the skin, as no other infection sources were identified. The absence of prior imaging to document a chronic subdural hematoma limits definitive conclusions; however, several findings support this hypothesis. First, intraoperative observations of encapsulating membranes indicate a chronic process, as acute subdural fluid collections generally do not display such organized boundaries. Furthermore, the lack of brain edema adjacent to the subdural collection, along with the high viscosity of the blood, implies that the hematoma was not recent. Chronic subdural hematomas are thought to grow through episodic microhemorrhages within the hematoma cavity [18], providing a substrate that can harbor infections if bacteremia occurs.

In conclusion, while S. saccharolyticus is considered a part of the normal skin flora, it has the potential to cause significant infections, as demonstrated in this first reported case of intracranial SDE in an immunocompetent host. Our findings suggest that the infection likely resulted from hematogenous seeding. This case underscores the importance of considering S. saccharolyticus as a potential pathogen in cases of intracranial empyema and highlights the need for timely surgical intervention and prolonged antimicrobial therapy to achieve successful outcomes. More research is needed to better understand the clinical risk factors and pathogenesis associated with S. saccharolyticus infections.
