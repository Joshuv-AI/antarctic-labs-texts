Association between prediabetes and nephropathy: a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Raheleh Karimi, Roya Riahi, Mojgan Mortazavi, Setayesh Sindarreh, Ashraf Aminorroaya, Mohammad Reza Maracy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study examines the association between prediabetes and nephropathy, focusing on chronic kidney disease and key indicators such as glomerular filtration rate and urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio.MethodsA systematic review of studies published before November 2024 included 26 articles, 15 cohort studies, and 11 cross-sectional studies, covering 350,546 individuals with normal glucose tolerance and 146,547 with prediabetes. A random-effects restricted maximum likelihood model was used to assess chronic kidney disease, glomerular filtration rate, and urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio. The risk of chronic kidney disease was reported using relative risk (RR) with 95% confidence intervals, while mean difference (MD) was used for glomerular filtration rate and urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio. Publication bias and methodological quality were assessed using the Egger regression test and the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute quality assessment tools. Sensitivity was tested using a leave-one-out method.ResultsData from 19 studies showed that individuals with prediabetes had a higher risk of developing chronic kidney disease (RR: 1.53; 95% CI: 1.30–1.81; P < 0.001), rising to 1.21 (95% CI: 1.78–2.73) when all three diagnostic methods were used. Across 31 observations, glomerular filtration rate declined by (MD: − 2.85; 95% CI: − 4.57 to − 1.13; P = 0.001). Urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio increased in 21 observations (MD: 0.87; 95% CI: 0.19–1.55; P = 0.012). Stronger associations were observed in Asia than in Europe or the United States.ConclusionPrediabetes is linked to lower glomerular filtration rate and higher urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio, indicating increased risk of chronic kidney disease. Early intervention may be beneficial.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12882-026-05212-5.

Introduction

The global increase in obesity and lifestyle changes are the main drivers of the increasing prevalence of prediabetes mellitus (Pre-DM) and type 2 diabetes [1–3]. Prediabetes means an increased risk of developing diabetes, characterized by elevated blood sugar levels that are not yet high enough to be diagnosed with diabetes. Individuals with prediabetes are defined by impaired fasting glucose (IFG) with fasting plasma glucose levels between 100 and 125 mg/dL, impaired glucose tolerance (IGT) with 2-hour plasma glucose values between 140 and 199 mg/dL, or hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) levels ranging from 5.7% to 6.4% [4, 5].

The prevalence of prediabetes is alarmingly high, with millions worldwide unaware of their condition [6–8]. This undiagnosed state increases the risk of progressing to overt diabetes and associated complications, such as the incidence of pre-diabetes increases, the risk of diabetes-related complications rises, including both macrovascular and microvascular issues such as cardiovascular disease (CVD) and nephropathy [9–12]. Evidence suggests that early intervention during prediabetes can significantly delay or prevent the onset of diabetes and its complications [13]. Also, Research indicates that diabetic nephropathy can develop in the early stages of the disease, even during the prediabetes phase [14, 15].

Nephropathy refers to kidney damage or disease that affects the normal functioning of the kidneys and can lead to a range of complications, such as ultimately chronic kidney disease (CKD) or kidney failure. CKD is diagnosed based on the presence of urine albumin creatinine ratio (UACR) > 30 mg/g and/or glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60, persisting for at least 3 months [16]. This emphasizes the importance of early screening for kidney health in prediabetic individuals.

Some studies indicate that early treatment of diabetes can prevent the onset of diabetic kidney disease [17]. Therefore, early diagnosis during the prediabetes stage, before irreversible nephropathy develops, is crucial for effective intervention. Management of prediabetes and nephropathy involves lifestyle interventions like dietary changes, regular exercise, and smoking cessation to regulate blood sugar and reduce kidney strain [18, 19]. Pharmacological treatments such as metformin, SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists, and RAS blockers help manage blood sugar, reduce albuminuria, and protect kidney function [20, 21]. These medications not only improve glycemic control but also have renal protective effects. For diabetic kidney disease, regular monitoring of fasting plasma glucose (FPG), HbA1c, and kidney function tests like eGFR and UACR are essential for early detection and management [22, 23].

Early identification enables the timely implementation of therapies that can slow disease progression and prevent further renal damage. Despite the significant burden of CKD among prediabetic populations, little is known about its true prevalence in undiagnosed cases, highlighting gaps in screening and awareness [24]. Given the varying results from studies on diabetic nephropathy and kidney diseases in individuals with prediabetes, a structured review can systematically identify and evaluate all relevant studies, offering a more objective critique.

This review highlights the importance of early detection and management of prediabetes to prevent CKD. For clinicians, it offers insights into diagnostic markers like eGFR and UACR for risk stratification and monitoring. Researchers can use the comprehensive data synthesis to guide future studies on prediabetes and nephropathy. Patients benefit from increased awareness of the potential kidney risks associated with prediabetes, emphasizing the need for early intervention and lifestyle modifications to reduce disease progression and improve long-term health outcomes.

Materials and methods

Protocol

The review follows the PRISMA guidelines for systematically assessing the existing literature. The study protocol was registered in PROSPERO (Registration Number: CRD42021255587).

Search strategy

To identify relevant published articles, we systematically searched PubMed (n = 746), Web of Science (n = 939), Scopus (n = 2073), and Google Scholar (n = 50) until November 1, 2024. Search terms included " prediabetic state”, “impaired fasting glucose”, “chronic kidney disease”, “renal insufficiency, chronic”, “Glomerular Filtration Rate”, and “hyperfiltration.” We also reviewed the references of relevant articles to identify additional studies.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This review includes published English-language case-control, cross-sectional, and cohort studies using human samples. Articles such as reports, reviews, editorials, and summaries lacking the required results were excluded. The PECO strategy defined inclusion: Population (prediabetes patients), Exposure (prediabetes vs. controls), Comparator (mean differences/odds ratios), and Outcome (CKD, eGFR, UACR).

Data screening procedure

After removing duplicates, two authors independently screened abstracts and full-text articles based on predefined criteria, with a kappa score of 0.85. Disagreements were resolved through discussion. The screening process is detailed in the PRISMA flow chart (Fig. 1). Data was extracted into Microsoft Excel spreadsheets, including (1) Basic details (first author, year, study design); (2) Study population (sample size, age, gender, follow-up, location); (3) Exposure details (prediabetes diagnosis/definition, outcome measurements); and (4) Data analysis requirements (frequency and mean of nephropathy indicators: eGFR, CKD, UACR in prediabetes and control groups).Information on baseline comorbidities such as hypertension and pre-existing kidney disease was inconsistently reported across included studies and therefore could not be uniformly accounted for in the pooled analyses.

Outcomes and exposure measurements

The outcome variables measured for the review included CKD, eGFR, and UACR, which are widely recognized biomarkers for assessing kidney function and detecting diabetic nephropathy [25]. eGFR is considered a reliable and valid measure of renal filtration capacity, with standardized calculation methods such as the Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation [26] or Modification of Diet in Renal Disease (MDRD) formulas [27], which are clinically validated indicators of kidney health [28]. Similarly, UACR is a validated marker for detecting microalbuminuria and early kidney damage, with high sensitivity and specificity in identifying patients at risk for CKD progression [29]. CKD was defined according to the KDIGO guidelines as an eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² and/or UACR ≥ 30 mg/g persisting for at least three months [16] [30]. Prediabetes, defined across studies based on FPG, oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), and HbA1c levels, is also validated for assessing associations with nephropathy [5].

Medication use, including of renin angiotensin aldosterone system (RAAS) inhibitors, sodium glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors, and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MR) antagonists were not systematically reported and their use was unclear in most included studies, preventing incorporation into subgroup or adjustment analyses.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included articles was independently assessed by two reviewers (RR and SS) using NHLBI quality assessment tools for observational studies [31]. The cross-sectional and cohort tool consists of 14 questions, while the case-control tool has 12 questions, each scoring 0 or 1 to identify potential flaws in study methods or implementation. Each article’s total score was categorized as follows: low quality (≤ 50% of the overall score), moderate quality (50–70% of the overall score), and high quality (≥ 70% of the overall score).

Statistical analysis

All studies reported the number of CKD events and sample size for prediabetes and control groups; some also provided adjusted effect sizes. Relative risk (RR) with a 95% confidence interval (CI) was used as the pooled effect size from crude and adjusted results. Mean and standard deviation were extracted for continuous outcomes (eGFR and UACR), and the mean difference (MD) was reported as the pooled effect size. A random-effects restricted maximum-likelihood (REML) model was applied due to substantial heterogeneity (I² > 50%, Cochran’s Q test p < 0.05) [32]. Heterogeneity was further assessed using Galbraith plots, and sensitivity analysis evaluated the robustness of associations using the leave-one-out method. In this approach, one study is removed at a time, and the analysis results are recalculated. This helps evaluate the impact of specific observations on the results; if the findings remain stable, it indicates their reliability. A sensitivity analysis was conducted by stratifying studies according to the eGFR estimation equation (CKD-EPI vs. MDRD) to assess potential bias from estimation methods. Subgroup analysis explored heterogeneity based on prediabetes diagnostic methods, definition type, study design, and geographic region. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots, Egger’s, and Begg’s tests. Trim-and-fill analysis estimated the number of missing studies due to publication bias. All analyses were performed using Stata 17.

Results

Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of the 25 reviewed studies: six studies examined all three outcomes (CKD, eGFR, and UACR), eight focused on eGFR and UACR, three on eGFR and CKD, six exclusively on eGFR, and two reported CKD. Among these, fifteen studies were cohort, while the rest were cross-sectional; Fifteen studies were conducted in Asia, with the remaining in Europe or America. The total sample size included 350,546 individuals in the control group (normal glucose tolerance or normal glucose regulation) and 146,547 in the prediabetes group. Definitions of prediabetes varied across studies: four studies reported only on IFG, four only on IGT, two on both, and three on IGT or IFG. Additionally, fifteen studies mentioned prediabetes without differentiation, and two included HbA1c outcomes. Diagnostic methods for assessing prediabetes included: five studies using all three methods (FPG, HbA1c, and 2 h-PG), ten using two methods (HbA1c and FPG or 2 h-PG and FPG), two using only HbA1c, and seven using only FPG. eGFR was measured with different formulas, with ten studies utilizing the CKD-EPI formula and six the MDRD formula, and CKD was defined in all studies as an ACR > 30 mg/g and/or an eGFR < 60 ml/min/1.73 m².

Quality study

Quality assessment using NIH guidelines (Appendix Table 1) indicated a low risk of information bias for exposed and unexposed groups in most studies. Quality varied across studies for other domains, such as the definition of prediabetes and outcomes. Overall, most studies were of high quality, with the remainder being of moderate quality.

Prediabetes and chronic kidney disease

The results from 19 observations indicated that the relative risk (RR) of developing CKD in individuals with Pre-DM was 1.53 (95% CI: 1.30–1.81), with a statistically significant difference (P < 0.001, I² = 97.96) (Fig. 2). The absence of significant publication bias, shown by the funnel plot (Appendix), the Begg test (P = 0.496), and the Egger test (P = 0.468), enhances the reliability of the findings. Subgroup analysis showed that the use of FPG, HbA1c, and their combinations with OGTT had different levels of risk for CKD. The HbA1c criterion had a relative risk of 2.02 (95% CI: 1.63–2.50), and the combination of FPG & OGTT & HbA1c showed the highest association with a relative risk of 2.21 (95% CI: 1.78–2.73). Geographically, the association between Pre-DM and CKD was strongest in Asia (RR = 1.57, 95% CI: 1.28–1.92, P < 0.001). In contrast, the association was weaker in Europe (RR = 1.77, 95% CI: 0.99–3.18, P = 0.054) and the USA (RR = 1.19, 95% CI: 0.92–1.84, P = 0.131). Additionally, based on study design, both cohort studies (RR = 1.47, 95% CI: 1.17–1.83, P = 0.001) and cross-sectional studies (RR = 1.68, 95% CI: 1.34–2.11, P < 0.001) demonstrated a significant association between Pre-DM and CKD. Prediabetes and eGFR

The results from 31 observations showed a significant decrease in eGFR among individuals with.

Pre-DM, with a mean difference (MD) of -2.85 (95% CI: -4.57 to -1.13, P = 0.001) (Fig. 3). This suggests that individuals with prediabetes may experience a reduction in kidney function relative to healthy individuals. While substantial heterogeneity was present among the included studies (I² = 99.04%, p < 0.001), the funnel plot (Appendix), Begg’s test (P = 0.292), and Egger’s test (P = 0.119) provided no evidence of publication bias, suggesting the findings are robust. Subgroup analysis revealed variations in the impact of different diagnostic criteria on eGFR (Table 3). The HbA1c criterion demonstrated the greatest decrease in eGFR (MD = -8.09, 95% CI: -15.78 to -0.45, P = 0.038). Conversely, the combination of FPG, OGTT, and HbA1c showed a non-significant decrease (MD = -4.51, 95% CI: -10.05 to 1.04, P = 0.111). From a geographical perspective, the reduction in eGFR was more pronounced in Asia (MD = -4.13, 95% CI: -7.15 to -1.10, P = 0.008) compared to Europe (MD = -1.47, 95% CI: -3 to 0.06, P = 0.060) and the USA (MD = -0.83, 95% CI: -2.40 to 0.73, P = 0.297). Regarding study design, cross-sectional studies indicated a more substantial decrease in eGFR (MD = -4.90, 95% CI: -8.49 to -1.31, P = 0.007) than cohort studies (MD = -1.25, 95% CI: -2.29 to -0.20, P = 0.020). Sensitivity analysis based on estimation methods showed that the using only CKD-EPI method had a significant association with decreased eGFR (MD = -3.21, 95% CI: -5.15 to -1.27, P = 0.001), similar to the main analysis (–2.85). However, the MDRD method did not show a significant reduction (MD = -1.68, 95% CI: -4.98 to 1.61, P = 0.317).

Prediabetes and UACR

The overall analysis of 21 observations indicated a significant increase in UACR among individuals with Pre-DM, with a mean difference (MD) of 0.87 (95% CI: 0.19 to 1.55, P = 0.012, I² = 97.77%) (Fig. 4). Significant publication bias was indicated by the Egger test (P < 0.001) and funnel plot (Appendix). Subgroup analysis showed that the diagnostic criterion of HbA1c had the highest association with an increase in UACR (MD = 1.30, 95% CI: 0.79 to 1.80, P < 0.001). The combination of FPG, OGTT, and HbA1c also showed a significant increase (MD = 4.63, 95% CI: 0.29 to 8.97, P = 0.037). However, the FPG criterion alone did not show a significant change (MD = -0.13, 95% CI: -0.42 to 0.17, P = 0.401). Geographically, the association between Pre-DM and UACR was significant in Asia (MD = 1.94, 95% CI: 0.44 to 3.44, P = 0.011). In contrast, no significant association was observed in Europe (MD = -0.01, 95% CI: -0.42 to 0.39, P = 0.947) or the USA (MD = 0, 95% CI: -0.43 to 0.43, P = 1.000). Regarding study design, cross-sectional studies showed a significant increase in UACR (MD = 1.85, 95% CI: 0.24 to 3.46, P = 0.024), while cohort studies did not indicate a significant association (MD = 0.33, 95% CI: -0.47 to 1.12, P = 0.418). Discussion

This study pooled results from 26 studies, providing updated evidence that prediabetes is associated with an increased risk of CKD and a decline in kidney function as indicated by lower eGFR and higher UACR. Observed publication bias related to UACR may stem from several factors. Since UACR is often regarded as a secondary outcome, it might be underreported when findings are inconclusive. Additionally, methodological inconsistencies such as variations in urine collection methods, assay techniques, or timing can lead to measurement variability and deter authors from publishing nonsignificant results.

In our study, we observed a significant association between prediabetes and glomerular hyperfiltration, consistent with emerging evidence that intermediate hyperglycemia can initiate early renal hemodynamic changes. Although glomerular hyperfiltration is a well-described early renal hemodynamic change in prediabetes, it is unlikely by itself to explain a marked decline in eGFR. The observed reduction in eGFR in this meta-analysis may therefore partly reflect the contribution of concomitant conditions, particularly hypertension and hypertensive nephrosclerosis [33]. Prediabetes frequently coexists with elevated blood pressure, which can independently accelerate renal structural damage and lead to progressive eGFR decline [34]. Since not all included studies adequately adjusted for hypertension status or duration, residual confounding cannot be excluded and may have contributed to the observed associations. Geographical differences in CKD risk may arise from ethnic-specific factors, such as genetic susceptibility, dietary patterns (high-carb diets in Asia exacerbating hyperfiltration), and socioeconomic disparities in healthcare [35]. In Asian cohorts, higher relative risk aligns with greater insulin resistance and glomerular hyperfiltration [36].

The weaker association observed in studies conducted in the United States may partly reflect population heterogeneity. Several In US studies did not report outcomes stratified by race; they only provided the number of prediabetic individuals in racial groups, precluding subgroup analysis for racial effects. These factors may explain attenuated US associations and highlight the need for race-stratified analyses.

Our sensitivity analysis based on eGFR estimation equations showed that the association between prediabetes and lower eGFR remained significant when using the CKD-EPI equation, similar to the main analysis, but not when using the MDRD equation. This discrepancy likely reflects the known superiority of CKD-EPI over MDRD, particularly at higher GFR levels, with less bias and greater accuracy [37]. The MDRD equation tends to underestimate GFR in the normal to mildly reduced range, which is typical in prediabetic populations. Therefore, the choice of eGFR equation may influence study findings, and we recommend using CKD-EPI in future research. Nevertheless, several included studies did not report which eGFR equation they used, and this lack of reporting limits the robustness of our sensitivity analysis; therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution and considered exploratory rather than definitive.

A meta-analysis was published in 2016 by Echouffo-Tcheugui et al., which included 9 studies and demonstrated that prediabetes is modestly associated with an increased risk of chronic kidney disease (CKD) [38]. This study included a significantly larger number of studies (26 vs. 9) than previous meta-analyses, and incorporated updated evidence up to November 2024, along with subgroup analyses by diagnostic method, geography, and study design. This allows for a more comprehensive understanding of the prediabetes-nephropathy relationship. Notably, previous meta-analyses primarily focused on individuals with metabolic syndrome and assessed only CKD outcomes. In contrast, we examined two CKD-related outcomes, such as eGFR and UACR, both directly associated with CKD.

In a community-based prospective cohort study conducted in 2020 involving 7,015 participants, three glycemic indices of fasting plasma glucose (FPG), postprandial glucose (PPG), and HbA1c were used to evaluate the association between prediabetes and the risk of renal dysfunction [39].

Two prospective cohort studies by Lucove (2008) and Watanabe (2010) found that prediabetes was associated with a 40% and 80% increased risk of kidney disease, respectively [40, 41]. A study by Baydar et al. (2024) also showed that prediabetes is associated with nephropathy, as shown in the study where 28.9% of prediabetes patients had nephropathy [42]. Early detection and management are crucial for preventing complications. However, some studies reported that prediabetes did not pose a significant risk for renal dysfunction. These findings suggest that, in certain populations or under specific conditions, prediabetes may not directly contribute to the deterioration of kidney function [43–46]. In this analysis of a large cohort of older German adults, the incidence of reduced kidney function was higher in individuals with prediabetes compared to those with normal FPG and/or HbA1c levels. However, after adjusting for cardiovascular risk factors, the elevated relative risk for reduced kidney function was no longer significant. HbA1c and FPG were strongly linked to reduced kidney function only in participants with diagnosed diabetes mellitus [44]. The lack of association in these studies highlights the need for further research to clarify the conditions under which prediabetes impacts renal health and to explore potential factors that may mitigate this risk.

The mechanisms underlying these findings are primarily related to metabolic and vascular changes during the prediabetic phase, which progressively impair kidney function [47]. Specifically, prediabetes drives CKD risk through insulin resistance (leading to elevated intraglomerular pressure and albuminuria, as in Tsuda et al., 2018), glomerular hyperfiltration, oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and inflammation, amplifying vascular damage and reducing eGFR over time [14, 48].

One key factor is “insulin resistance”, a characteristic of prediabetes, which hinders cells’ ability to properly utilize glucose, resulting in hyperglycemia (high blood sugar levels) [49]. Even moderately elevated glucose levels, below the diabetic threshold, can damage the small blood vessels in the kidneys (glomeruli), leading to glomerulosclerosis, a condition that reduces the kidneys’ filtration ability and increases albuminuria. Additionally, hyperglycemia triggers the production of advanced glycation end-products (AGEs), which cause structural changes and inflammation in the kidneys, further contributing to the decline in kidney function [50, 51]. From another point of view, in prediabetes, elevated blood glucose levels trigger oxidative stress, a state where excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) overwhelms the body’s antioxidant defenses. This oxidative stress can directly damage renal tissues, leading to a loss of kidney function. Chronic low-grade inflammation is also commonly seen in prediabetes and contributes to kidney damage. Pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) can promote fibrosis in the kidneys and exacerbate vascular damage, leading to a decline in eGFR and elevated UACR [52–54]. Hypertension is also frequently associated with prediabetes [55] and is a significant risk factor for CKD. Elevated blood pressure exerts extra strain on the kidneys’ filtering system, damaging the glomeruli and impairing kidney function over time. This damage manifests as both reduced eGFR and elevated UACR. In addition, prediabetes can lead to altered renal hemodynamics, such as increased glomerular hyperfiltration in the early stages, followed by glomerular hypertension and damage as the condition progresses, contributing to a decline in kidney function [56, 57]. Prediabetes is associated with endothelial dysfunction, where the inner lining of blood vessels fails to function properly. This dysfunction affects kidney microcirculation, impairing the regulation of blood flow and filtration in the kidneys, leading to both a reduction in eGFR and increased leakage of elevated UACR [58–60]. Prediabetes may activate the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), which plays a critical role in regulating blood pressure and fluid balance. The overactivation of RAAS contributes to glomerular hypertension, kidney inflammation, and fibrosis, worsening CKD risk, reducing eGFR, and increasing UACR [61, 62]. Our findings are along with findings from studies in which nephropathy in diabetic patients was assessed. For instance, Cho et al. (2022) categorized CKD risk based on eGFR and UACR levels, highlighting that a decline in eGFR and an increase in UACR are associated with higher risks of kidney failure, cardiovascular disease, and mortality in diabetic patients. Both eGFR and UACR directly measure biological processes affected by hyperglycemia and hypertension, which are key in diabetic nephropathy pathogenesis. Effective management of blood glucose, blood pressure, and use of renoprotective medications can positively influence these markers, leading to improved outcomes. Monitoring these measures allows for early detection, timely intervention, and adjustment of therapies, ultimately improving disease prognosis and kidney health.

This meta-analysis is one of the most comprehensive studies on the association between prediabetes and nephropathy-related outcomes. Strengths of this study include the large sample size, which increases statistical power, and the assessment of multiple nephropathy-related outcomes, such as eGFR, CKD, and UACR, which provide a broad understanding of the impact of prediabetes on kidney function. In addition, subgroup analyses examined the influence of factors such as prediabetes assessment methods, different definitions of prediabetes, and eGFR measurement approaches and provided different insights.

This study has several limitations that must be acknowledged. First, substantial heterogeneity across studies may impact the accuracy of effect estimates. The quality of evidence for certain outcomes, particularly estimated eGFR and UACR, was low, primarily due to variability in measurement methods and differences in diagnostic criteria for prediabetes and kidney function. Second, the presence of background conditions such as hypertension or early kidney damage, which can influence both eGFR and UACR, was not consistently reported across studies. As a result, the potential confounding effect of these conditions could not be fully addressed.

Third, the potential influence of medications, including RAAS inhibitors, SGLT2 inhibitors, and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, was largely unavailable. Given their known effects on eGFR and albuminuria, medication use may have influenced the observed associations.

Furthermore, publication bias was identified in studies related to UACR, which may have resulted in an overestimation of effects. Additionally, the lack of detailed data on potential confounding factors, such as lifestyle and environmental influences, limits the accuracy of the results.

Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable insights into the renal outcomes of prediabetes and lays the groundwork for further research. To improve the accuracy and generalizability of future systematic reviews, it is crucial to control study heterogeneity, use advanced statistical methods, and rigorously assess data quality. Managing publication bias and considering confounding factors like lifestyle and environmental influences can enhance result reliability. Additionally, incorporating diverse populations and employing innovative study designs, such as AI-driven meta-analyses, can yield deeper insights. By addressing these aspects, future studies can overcome existing limitations and enhance our understanding of the relationship between prediabetes and renal health.

Conclusion

This comprehensive systematic review highlights the association between prediabetes and increased risk of chronic kidney disease, lower estimated glomerular filtration rate, and higher urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio. The interplay between insulin resistance, oxidative stress, inflammation, hypertension, endothelial dysfunction, and dysregulated lipid metabolism in prediabetes creates a harmful environment for kidney health. Over time, these factors promote kidney damage, increasing the risk of chronic kidney disease, reducing estimated glomerular filtration rate, and elevating urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio.

Early detection and management of prediabetes may help mitigate these risks and slow the progression of kidney disease. Additionally, monitoring kidney function in individuals with prediabetes can aid in timely intervention, potentially reducing the burden of chronic kidney disease on healthcare systems. Identifying vulnerable populations and developing tailored prevention strategies may further enhance outcomes and minimize the progression of chronic kidney disease.
