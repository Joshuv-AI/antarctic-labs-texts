Recent trends in hand surgery fellowship applicants and programs
Authors: Christopher P Bellaire, Emily M Graham, Lauren M Bracey, Harrison R Ferlauto, Caleigh Weliver, Shaun D Mendenhall, Eitan Melamed
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHand surgery is a competitive fellowship that draws applicants from orthopedic, plastic, and general surgery. In 2010, recommendations from the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) Board of Specialty Societies Match Oversight Committee and the American Society of Plastic Surgeons (ASPS) Plastic Surgery Workforce Task Force led to significant reforms to their respective workforces and training. This study characterizes trends in hand fellowship applicants and programs since these recommendations (2010–2023).Materials and methodsWe queried hand fellowship applicant and program data from the American Society for Surgery of the Hand (ASSH), the National Resident Matching Program (NRMP), and the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME).Results, discussion and conclusionsFrom 2010 to 2023, the number of ACGME-accredited hand fellowship programs increased across specialties (orthopedic surgery, 58 to 74 (27.6 % increase, R = 0.97, p < 0.001); plastic surgery, 14 to 19 (35.7 % increase, R = 0.91, p < 0.001); general surgery, 1 to 2 (50 % increase, R = 0.71, p = 0.004). The number of available fellowship positions commensurately increased by 38.8 % over the same time period. The number of applicants did not significantly change between 2010 (155 applicants) and 2023 (198 applicants) (R = 0.32, p = 0.27). The ratio of applicants-to-positions significantly decreased from 2010 (1.12 applicants per position) to 2023 (1.04 applicants per position) (R = -0.74, p = 0.003). Yet, applicants ranked nearly twice as many programs in 2023 (14.9 programs per applicant) compared to in 2010 (7.5 programs per applicant) (R = 0.98, p < 0.001). In light of the American Orthopaedic Association endorsing signal preferencing in the 2022–2023 residency Match, strategies to optimize rank lists in the fellowship application process should be further explored.Keywords: Hand surgery, Hand fellowship, Match, Applicants, Programs, Medical Education

Introduction

Dr. Asa Sterling Bunnell, the father of hand surgery in the United States, envisioned a hand surgeon to be a “regional surgeon”, applying best practices from various surgical sub-specialties, with subject matter expertise in bones and joints from orthopedists, soft tissue and skin from plastic surgeons, and nerve repairs and grafts from neurosurgeons [1]. When embraced, these differences would produce synergies and a healthy tension to advance the field of hand surgery [2]. This interdisciplinary ideal, however, has not been without its territorial disputes and follies over the years. Often forgotten is that multiple academic institutions initially denied Dr. Bunnell's protégé, Dr. Raymond Curtis, in his pursuit of starting a hand clinic out of concern it would infringe on other surgical services [3].

Today, these sub-specialty differences quietly endure, manifesting over the course of a hand surgeon's training and career. Despite efforts by the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) to standardize hand fellowships in recent years, program directors still disagree over the core competencies of fellowship and the relative importance of elbow/shoulder surgery versus burns, soft tissue reconstruction, and microsurgery [4,5]. In addition to differences of opinion, operative case logs during fellowship are drastically different depending on the department sponsoring the fellowship (orthopedic versus plastic surgery) [6]. Years after fellowship, a hand surgeon's residency background continues to significantly impact the surgeon's practice setting, patient profile, and caseload [7]. There is even evidence that orthopedic and plastic surgeons on hand trauma call assume procedural differences for similar injuries, with orthopedists using predominantly closed reductions, splinting and casting and plastic surgeons preferring percutaneous and open methods [8].

In 2010, recommendations from the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) Board of Specialty Societies Match Oversight Committee and the American Society of Plastic Surgeons (ASPS) Plastic Surgery Workforce Task Force contributed to reforms to their respective workforces and training [9,10]. This article reviews the implementation and impact of changes to the hand surgery fellowship match since 2010, using data from 2010 to 2023. The present study investigates qualitative and quantitative trends in the Match for hand surgery fellowship, analyzing both applicant and program data. In doing so, we highlight the shifting equilibrium between orthopedic, plastic, and general surgeons in hand surgery. We apply an econometric approach to quantify supply and demand for fellowship positions using the number of fellowship positions available and the number of applicants, respectively.

Our findings intend to support decision-making at the ACGME and National Resident Matching Program (NRMP), which administers the hand fellowship Match, as well as for hand surgery societies, including the American Society for Surgery of the Hand (ASSH) and the American Association for Hand Surgery (AAHS). This study also informs individual fellowship applicants and program directors about factors that may implicitly or explicitly impact the training and practice of hand surgery at their institutions. Lastly, this review of fellowship data and demographics provides an initial look at the next generation of hand surgeons in the United States.

Methods

Hand fellowship applicants and programs

The NRMP publishes annual reports on the Match, including metrics on the number of applicants [11]. This publicly available data pools together statistics from all surgical sub-specialties (orthopedic, plastic, and general surgery) to calculate the total number of hand fellowship applicants each year. NRMP also publishes data on the number of hand fellowship programs and number of fellowship positions offered at those programs each year. When combined, these two sources of data show the relative demand and supply for hand surgery fellowships (Table 1). The strictly quantitative ratio of applicants-to-positions, which Morzycki et al. coined the “competitiveness ratio”, has been used as an imperfect proxy for scarcity and selectivity, indicating the relative difficulty of matching into a sub-specialty [12,13]. We queried this database and extracted data on the total number of programs, positions offered, applicants and the number of ranked programs per applicant between the years 2010 and 2023.

Hand fellowship applicant demographics

To add context to the quantitative approach of the study, we wrote to the ASSH and requested applicant demographic data for the years available, which included the 2019–2021 application cycles. Prior years were unavailable. Demographic information collected included age, sex, location of residency program, and residency specialty. We also quantified the number of ACGME-accredited orthopedic, plastic, and general surgery fellowships to which each applicant applied. The number of applications to non-accredited hand fellowships was also recorded.

Statistical analyses

Pearson's correlations and linear regressions were used to analyze trends over time. Independent t-tests, Mann-Whitney U tests, and Fisher's exact tests were performed to compare applicant demographics and application trends.

Results

Hand surgery fellowship programs

According to ACGME data, from 2010 to 2023, the number of hand fellowship programs increased by 30.1 % across specialties (orthopedic surgery, 58 to 74; plastic surgery, 14 to 19; general surgery, 1 to 2), with 72.7 % of the total increase attributed to orthopedic programs. Orthopedic surgery fellowship programs were most common (77.9 % of hand programs in 2023) and grew in number since 2010 (R = 0.97, p < 0.001) (Fig. 1).

Trends in the Number of Hand Fellowship Programs by Specialty Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education for years 2010–2023. Pearson's Correlations were used to determine strength and significance of correlation.

Hand surgery fellowship positions

Though the current number of hand fellowships applicants has exhibited minimal, statistically non-significant variation (R = 0.32, p = 0.27), a remarkable escalation has occurred in the availability of positions offered. This increase, marked as 139 positions in 2010 to 193 positions in 2023, shows a growth of 38.3 % (R = 0.99, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2). The surge in fellowship positions can be attributed, at least in part, to the aforementioned proliferation of hand fellowship programs, particularly among departments of orthopedic surgery. In 2023, there were 193 positions available with a total of 198 certified applicants entering the match. There were 189 positions filled (97.9 %), meaning 4 positions went unfilled and 9 applicants did not match. There were 93 certified programs offering hand surgery positions and 90 programs were filled (96.8 %), resulting in a total of 4 unfilled positions among the 3 programs that did not fully match their respective fellowship programs. These figures indicate a relatively stable equilibrium between the number of positions offered and the number of applicants seeking to match [11].

Trends in the Number of Applicants and Positions for Hand Surgery Fellowship, using data from the National Resident Matching Program from 2010 to 2023. Pearson's Correlations were used to determine strength and significance of correlation.

Hand surgery fellowship applicants

Between 2010 to 2023, there were non-significant changes in the total number of hand surgery applicants (R = 0.32, p = 0.27), ranging between a minimum of 155 applicants in 2010 and a maximum of 205 applicants in 2014, and most recently 198 applicants in 2023. Using the 2019–2021 applicant data from the ASSH, the majority of hand fellowship applicants were from orthopedic residency programs, with 417 (68.5 %) applicants from orthopedic surgery residency programs, 166 (27.2 %) applicants from plastic surgery residency programs, and 26 (4.3 %) applicants from general surgery residency programs (Table 3). However, there were more prospective hand surgery fellowship applicants to draw from orthopedic surgery residency programs, as there are more orthopedic surgery residents nation-wide compared to the number of plastic surgery residents. In 2023, there were 899 post-graduate year one orthopedic surgery positions available, whereas there were only 207 integrated plastic surgery residency positions, according to NRMP data [9].

Hand surgery fellowship competitiveness

From 2010 to 2023, the significant expansion (38.8 % increase from 2010 to 2023) in fellowship positions (R = 0.99, p < 0.001) exceeded the non-significant growth (27.7 % increase from 2010 to 2023) in applicants (R = 0.32, p = 0.27). As a result, the ratio of applicants-to-positions significantly decreased from 2010 to 2023 (R = −0.74, p = 0.003) (Fig. 3).

Trends in Hand Fellowship Applicants Per Position Offered, using data from the National Resident Matching Program from 2010 to 2023. Pearson's Correlations were used to determine strength and significance of correlation.

As a result, in 2023, there were relatively more fellowship positions available per applicant than at any time in the past decade; yet, prospective hand fellows, on average, ranked more programs than ever before. The mean number of ranked programs increased by 98.7 % from 2010 to 2023 (7.5 to 14.9, respectively) (R = 0.98, p < 0.001) (Fig. 4).

Trends in the Number of Ranked Programs Per Applicant, using data from the National Resident Matching Program from 2010 to 2023. Pearson's Correlations were used to determine strength and significance of correlation.

The influx in of ranked programs by hand fellows has resulted in a massive increase in total rankings submitted by fellowship applicants into the Match, a 153.8 % increase from 1163 programs ranked in 2010 to 2952 programs ranked in 2023 (R = 0.91, p < 0.001) (Fig. 5).

Consequently, this upsurge in rankings and signaling has placed a heightened burden on fellowship programs, necessitating a more extensive screening process and an increased number of rankings per program. The mean number of rankings per program increased by 85.4 %, from 17.1 in 2010 to 31.7 in 2023 (R = 0.87 p < 0.001) (Fig. 6).

Based on the 2023 hand fellowship match data, US applicants had significantly higher rates of matching into hand surgery fellowship programs than their international counterparts. Among allopathic and osteopathic graduates, 96.7 % matched in 2023, in comparison with 77.8 % of international medical graduates (p = 0.007). While far fewer in number than allopathic graduates certified for the match (165 applicants), all 15 osteopathic graduates (100 %) matched in 2023 [11].

Discussion

Hand fellowship match

Historically, hand surgery fellowship is among the most popular and competitive fellowships, with previous studies finding 96.6 % of hand fellowship positions were filled between 2011 and 2017 [9]. We similarly found that 97.1 % of all hand fellowship positions were filled between 2010 and 2023. At the same time, our econometric analysis of relative supply and demand suggested a more favorable “marketplace” for fellowship applicants, with a down-trending applicants-to-positions ratio.

From strictly a quantitative perspective of supply and demand, this finding suggests a decrease in relative scarcity for matching into hand fellowship despite an absolute increase in the number of applicants. The down-trending ratio of applicants-to-positions is especially noteworthy, as the 2020–2021 application cycle was the first time in over a decade when the number of fellowship positions offered exceeded the total number of applicants, which is defined as an applicants-to-positions ratio < 1.

Despite less relative scarcity, applicants in 2023 ranked nearly twice as many programs compared to applicants from 2010. This increase in the number of ranked programs likely correlated with an increase in expenses associated with interviews and application fees. This trend may disproportionately impact applicants from disadvantaged socioeconomic backgrounds. At the same time, however, this rise in the mean number of ranked programs may also suggest improvements and efficiencies in the application process that made it easier for applicants to apply and interview more widely. Our data established an increased number of total rankings and resulting increase in rankings per program which serve as an increased burden for fellowship program directors during the process of screening and reviewing fellowship applications. Further investigation in this area is warranted, and strategies to optimize rank lists and Match likelihood should be explored, with attention to the potential disparate impact on students from socioeconomically disadvantaged and under-represented backgrounds.

The American Orthopaedic Association instituted signal preferencing for the Orthopaedic Surgery Residency Match in 2022, and this policy has continued in the current 2023–2024 application cycle. Signal preferencing only applies to the match for orthopaedic surgery residency programs, and this policy does not apply to the fellowship match process, to include hand surgery fellowship. However, given the significant rise in the number of rankings per applicant over the past decade, with the number of rankings per applicant nearly doubling from 2010 to 2023 (R = 0.98, p < 0.001), professional organizations including the ACGME and NRMP may consider instituting a similar policy for fellowship programs to better optimize the hand surgery application and ranking process [11].

Core competencies of hand fellowship

Hand surgery requires proficiency in treating complex issues of bones and soft tissues. As such, concerting the philosophies and technical strengths of orthopedic and plastic surgery are key to preparing the next generation of hand surgeons. Ideally, trainees should have strong mentors and experiences in both fields. As recently as the 2020–2021 application cycle, we found 63.7 % of applicants were orthopedic-trained and 33.1 % were plastic surgery-trained. This finding may reflect a recent balancing in specialty representation, as one prior study showed that roughly 72 % of hand surgeons were orthopedic-trained and 18 % were plastic surgery-trained [15].

Despite this slight increase in plastic surgery representation, cross-pollination between orthopedic and plastic surgery residencies and fellowships remains limited. According to our data, roughly 90 % of the orthopedic residents matched into an orthopedic-based fellowship whereas 75 % of plastic surgery residents matched into an orthopedic-based fellowship. These findings echo Nies et al. in their 2014 report, which suggested an orthopedic residency background was a positive predictor of matching [16]. Interestingly, our study also showed that many orthopedic applicants only applied to orthopedic based fellowships. In 2023, a small minority (3.7 %) of hand fellowship positions were filled by general surgery residency applicants. This proportion is similar to the previous 2021–2022 application cycle (p = 0.47), where 5.3 % of filled positions were from general surgery residency backgrounds. Of the 189 filled positions in 2023, seven were from general surgery residency programs; of the 181 filled positions in 2022, 10 were from general surgery residency programs. The two most recent cycles indicate a small, but stable, number of general surgery applicants in the hand surgery fellowship match [11]. This finding may highlight a need for increased collaboration across specialties during residency and fellowship to ensure the next generation of hand surgeons is well-rounded and capable of taking care of complex upper extremity problems. Hand fellowship traditionally has emphasized teaching from both orthopedic and plastic surgery faculty to open opportunities traditionally siloed within each specialty. The present study presents this trend so that program directors and fellows can review case logs and curricula to ensure that there is appropriately balanced and comprehensive training in the core competencies of hand surgery.

Our analysis of fellowship programs provides insight into the training of the next generation of hand surgeons applying to hand fellowships in the United States. Recent studies show that the fellowship's sponsoring department (orthopedic or plastic surgery) significantly impacts the operative experiences and case logs of hand fellows [6]. These differences during fellowship can have long-term consequences for a hand surgeon's career trajectory. Compared to orthopedic trained hand surgeons, plastic trained hand surgeons perform more replantations, free flaps, and other microsurgical procedures [17]. In addition to differences in clinical practice, most plastics-trained hand surgeons entered academic practice, whereas most orthopedic-trained hand surgeons entered private practice [7,18]. Complex reconstructive cases often reside in tertiary referral centers, which may in part explain why plastics-trained hand surgeons are drawn to academic practice. However, orthopedic-trained hand surgeons may achieve similar proficiencies by matching into plastic surgery-based fellowships. Ultimately, program directors should consider applicant career goals and resident case logs over residency specialty background when determining whether an applicant would be a good fit. Hand fellowship programs should also avoid excluding applicants solely based on their residency specialty.

Interdisciplinary training during residency and fellowship is vital, especially as microsurgical case volume for hand fellows has declined in recent years [19]. Fewer opportunities may adversely affect residents’ interest in the field. According to a survey of first-year orthopedic and plastic surgery residents, exposure to hand surgery was a more important factor in deciding to pursue hand surgery fellowship than lifestyle considerations, such as work hours and reimbursement rates [20]. As a result, this has led some to argue for a two-year fellowship; however, a majority of fellows and fellowship directors still favor the one-year curriculum [5,21,22]. This debate further underscores the need for collaboration between specialties with diverse experiences and opportunities for trainees.

Future directions

According to a 2015 survey of applicants and program directors, the prototypical successfully matched hand surgery fellow was an orthopedic resident, Caucasian male in his early thirties [23]. Our results, however, showed that as recently as the 2020–2021 cycle, there was increased female representation among hand fellowship applicants, which is consistent with similar trends in orthopedic and plastic surgery residency programs [24], [25], [26]. Additionally, although we provided an updated analysis of residency background, gender, and age, data regarding race and ethnicity was not provided by ASSH. This limitation represents an opportunity for further research and advocacy. Additional studies are needed to monitor trends and explore how diversity of fellows and faculty impacts hand surgery training, patient outcomes, and career trajectories.

Neither the NRMP, which collects data on applicants, nor the ACGME, which collects data on programs, distinguishes between orthopedic and plastic surgery applicants in their open-source data. This limitation was mitigated by soliciting applicant information directly from ASSH, but the present study was only able to acquire three years of applicant data from 2019 to 2021. NRMP has overseen the hand fellowship Match since 1990 and has longitudinal data on the absolute number of applicants, but does not publish sub-group analyses based on residency training backgrounds (orthopedic, plastic, and general surgery) or applicant demographics [27]. Furthermore, our analysis does not examine hand fellowship programs that were not accredited by the ACGME. Future studies should monitor how applicant demographics, rank lists, and overall match competitiveness evolve with time.

Conclusions

Surgical subspecialty training may contribute to nuanced differences in hand surgery practice despite recent attempts to standardize competencies. Our study highlights qualitative and quantitative trends in hand surgery fellowship, analyzing applicants and programs using an econometric model of supply (number of fellowship positions) and demand (number of applicants). Applicants, residency and fellowship program directors, and hand surgery societies can use this information to gain insight into national trends. The recent expansion in hand surgery fellowship programs and positions offered has corrected a previously extremely competitive and selective application process. Prospective fellows, however, are submitting more rankings-per-applicant than ever before, suggesting that applicants have not changed their behavior in response to the recent expansion in fellowship opportunities. In light of the orthopedic surgery residency Match implementing signal preferencing in the 2022–2023 application cycle, strategies to optimize rank lists and match likelihood in the fellowship application process should be further explored.
