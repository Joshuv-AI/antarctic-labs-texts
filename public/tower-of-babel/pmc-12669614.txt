AMP-activated protein kinase–Caspase 6 axis regulation alleviates the combined apoptotic damage caused by deoxynivalenol and fumonisin B1 in mammary glands
Authors: Liguang Cao, Guojin Li, Kai Liu, Wenrui Gao, Yifei Dong, Zifeng Yang, Jianchun Luo, Lin Lei, Xiliang Du, Yuxiang Song, Xinwei Li, Wenwen Gao, Guowen Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Deoxynivalenol (DON) and fumonisin B1 (FB1) are prevalent mycotoxins commonly found together in dairy cow feed, posing significant risks to cow health and food safety. This study investigates combined toxic effects of DON and FB1 on bovine mammary epithelial cells (BMECs), and role of AMPK-Caspase 6 axis in mycotoxin-induced apoptosis in BMECs and mouse model. We showed that even subcytotoxic doses of DON, combined with FB1, induced synergistic or additive toxic effects, reducing cell viability, increasing LDH release, and promoting apoptosis. This combination also impaired milk triglyceride synthesis and downregulated genes involved in milk fat and protein production. Silencing Caspase 6 via siRNA, inhibiting with Z-VEID-FMK, or activating AMPK with AICAR alleviated apoptosis. Conversely, overexpressing Caspase 6 diminished AMPK’s protective effects. In vivo, inhibiting Caspase 6 alleviated apoptosis in mammary tissues. These findings highlight therapeutic potential of targeting the AMPK-Caspase 6 pathway to mitigate combined toxicity of DON and FB1.

Introduction

Fusarium toxins, secondary metabolites produced by certain Fusarium fungi, occur naturally in various foods and feeds and can cause adverse effects in vertebrates1. Among these toxins, deoxynivalenol (DON) and fumonisin are two prominent contaminants, particularly in cereal crops such as wheat, maize, and barley2,3. DON, which is primarily produced by Fusarium graminearum, is one of the most prevalent mycotoxins found in animal feed worldwide2. Fumonisin, which is produced primarily by Fusarium verticillioides and Fusarium proliferatum, often cooccurs with DON in animal diets, prompting intensified research into their concurrent exposure4,5. The presence of DON and fumonisin in feed adversely affects milk yield and quality, metabolic parameters, immunity, and diet digestibility in dairy cows, as evidenced by previous studies on cows fed mycotoxin-contaminated diets6,7. The biological mechanisms underlying these toxic effects are not fully understood, yet indications suggest that DON alone can induce increased apoptosis and cell death in bovine mammary epithelial cells (BMECs), impair tight junctions in the mammary epithelium, and damage the gut epithelium8–10. Although ruminants exhibit tolerance to fumonisin B1 (FB1), as this mycotoxin is hardly metabolized by the rumen microbiota or the liver, changes in liver enzymes and metabolic markers have been reported11,12. Moreover, severe toxic effects may occur due to the interactions between these toxins, potentially resulting in additive and synergistic toxicity in susceptible organisms13. Previous studies have confirmed the occurrence of synergistic toxic effects on the viability of various cell types, such as human hepatocytes, porcine small intestinal epithelial cells, and human colon cancer cells14,15. Since DON and fumonisin are likely to coexist and their combined presence adversely impacts milk production and quality in dairy cows, investigating their combined cytotoxicity in BMECs is critical.

Apoptosis usually underlies the toxic potential of these compounds. DON has been reported to induce apoptosis in many cell lines through multiple mechanisms. For example, it can trigger autophagy induction, subsequently leading to apoptosis in prostate epithelial cells16. Additionally, oxidative stress induced by DON has been shown to be associated with apoptosis in BMECs17. Research on fumonisin-induced apoptosis has focused primarily on kidney cells18,19. Despite the detection of fumonisin in dairy milk20, there is a paucity of research investigating their effects on mammary glands, which may be attributed to their inconsequential toxicity to BMECs, as demonstrated by our study. In addition, a previous study suggested that noncytotoxic doses of FB1 can worsen ochratoxin A-induced apoptosis in renal cells, highlighting the potential for FB1 to induce apoptosis in an additive or synergistic manner21.

Caspases, a family of cysteine proteases, serve as key mediators of inflammation (Caspases 1, 4, 5, and 12) and apoptosis22. Apoptotic caspases are categorized into initiator caspases (Caspases 2, 8, 9, and 10) and executioner caspases (Caspases 3, 6, and 7) according to their function and sequence homology in apoptotic signaling pathways23. Caspase proteases are widely involved in mycotoxin-induced apoptosis, which is characterized by increased expression and enhanced activity of caspase proteins upon toxin exposure24–26. Caspase 6 is typically activated by Caspase 3 rather than by initiating caspases and can be autoactivated even in the absence of Caspase 3 activity27. Furthermore, Caspase 6 exerts a positive feedback effect on apoptosis, thereby facilitating sustained apoptotic responses, which might correspond with the underlying mechanisms implicated in subclinical mycotoxin poisoning28. Adenosine monophosphate (AMP)–activated protein kinase (AMPK) is a key regulator of the cellular energy status and has been shown to phosphorylate procaspase-6, repressing its activation28. However, the role of the AMPK-Caspase 6 pathway in the apoptosis of BMECs induced by mycotoxin combinations remains unclear.

The aim of this study was to investigate the effects of noncytotoxic concentrations of DON in combination with increasing concentrations of FB1 on the viability, apoptosis, and milk composition of BMECs. Furthermore, we sought to elucidate the role of the AMPK-Caspase 6 pathway in regulating apoptosis induced by combined exposure to DON and FB1 in BMECs and in a mouse model. By addressing these questions, this study aimed to provide new insights into the mechanisms of mycotoxin-induced toxicity in mammary epithelial cells and identify potential therapeutic targets for mitigating the adverse effects of mycotoxins on dairy production.

Results

Effects of DON and FB1, individually and in combination, on cell viability and LDH release in BMECs

To investigate the potential synergistic cytotoxicity of DON and FB1 in vitro, we conducted assays to assess cell viability and LDH release. BMECs were treated with individual or combined concentrations of DON and FB1 to evaluate dose‒response relationships and interactive effects. The results indicated that DON exposure caused a concentration-dependent decrease in BMEC viability, with significant reductions at 1, 2, 4, 8, and 16 μM DON (Supplementary Fig. 1A). The calculated IC50 value for DON was 1.5 μM. Correspondingly, LDH release increased significantly at 2, 4, 8, and 16 μM DON (Supplementary Fig. 1D). FB1 alone did not significantly affect cell viability (Supplementary Fig. 1B) but induced substantial LDH release at 16 and 32 μM (Supplementary Fig. 1E), indicating the occurrence of membrane damage without immediate metabolic effects. Compared with 0.5 μM DON alone, cotreatment with 0.5 μM DON and increasing FB1 concentrations (4, 8, 16, and 32 μM) progressively reduced cell viability and increased LDH release (Supplementary Fig. 1C, F). These results demonstrate that exposure to subtoxic DON increases FB1-induced cytotoxicity, suggesting complementary mechanisms of action.

Individual and combined effects of DON and FB1 on BMEC apoptosis

Annexin V-FITC/PI dual-staining revealed that DON induced a concentration-dependent increase in the number of apoptotic cells (Fig. 1A, B). In contrast, FB1 alone did not significantly promote apoptosis at any tested concentration (Fig. 1C, D). Notably, cotreatment with 0.5 μM DON and 4 μM FB1 significantly increased apoptosis compared with that in the control group (Fig. 1E, F). Compared with that in the group treated with 0.5 μM DON alone, the ratio of cleaved-Caspase 3 to Caspase 3 was elevated in the groups cotreated with 0.5 μM DON and 4 μM, 8 μM, or 32 μM FB1 (Fig. 1G, H). Similarly, the ratio of cleaved-PARP to total PARP increased in the groups treated with 0.5 μM DON combined with 4 or 32 μM FB1 (Fig. 1G, H). Casp3 mRNA expression was upregulated in the groups cotreated with 0.5 μM DON and higher FB1 concentrations (16 and 32 μM) (Fig. 1I). Furthermore, TUNEL staining revealed increased cell death in the group treated with 0.5 μM DON + 4 μM FB1 (Fig. 1J, K). Effects of DON and FB1, individually and in combination, on milk composition and the expression of relevant genes in BMECs

The combined effects of DON and FB1 on milk composition and gene expression in BMECs were investigated. ELISA revealed a slight reduction in the β-casein content in cells treated with 0.5 μM DON + 4 μM FB1 (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Cotreatment with DON and FB1 significantly decreased the ratios of phosphorylated to total mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR), Janus kinase 2 (JAK2), and signal transducer and activator of transcription 5 (STAT5), as well as the β-casein level, compared with DON alone (Supplementary Fig. 2D–H), indicating disruption of milk protein synthesis pathways. Milk fat synthesis was also impaired, with reduced intracellular triglyceride (TG) content in the 0.5 μM DON + 4 μM FB1 group (Supplementary Fig. 2B). This was associated with decreased sterol regulatory element-binding protein (SREBP1) protein levels (Supplementary Fig. 2I) and downregulated expression of lipogenic genes peroxisome proliferator activated receptor gamma (PPARG), fatty acid synthase (FASN), diacylglycerol O-acyltransferase 1 (DGAT1), and SREBP1 (Supplementary Fig. 2J–M). The lactose content remained unchanged in the 0.5 μM DON + 4 μM FB1 group (Supplementary Fig. 2C). However, β-1,4-Galactosyltransferase 1 (B4GALT1) expression was upregulated with increasing FB1 concentration, whereas glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1), Lactalbumin Alpha (LALBA), and solute carrier family 2 member 12 (SLC2A12) levels were unaffected (Supplementary Fig. 2N–Q). These findings demonstrate that combined DON and FB1 exposure disrupts milk protein and fat synthesis in BMECs but has minimal effects on lactose production.

Caspase 6 participated in combination toxin-induced BMEC apoptosis

To investigate the role of Caspase 6 in combined toxin-induced apoptosis, we first examined its activation in BMECs treated with DON and FB1. Compared with 0.5 μM DON alone, coexposure to 0.5 μM DON and increasing FB1 concentrations (4, 8, 16, and 32 μM) significantly elevated the ratio of cleaved-Caspase 6 to total Caspase 6 (Fig. 3A). This activation coincided with increased apoptosis, suggesting a potential role for Caspase 6 in mediating cell death. To confirm the involvement of Caspase 6, we silenced Casp6 using siRNA. Caspase 6 knockdown significantly reduced the ratios of cleaved-Caspase 3 to Caspase 3 and cleaved-PARP to PARP in the BMECs treated with 0.5 μM DON + 4 μM FB1 (Fig. 3B, C). Additionally, Annexin V-FITC/PI staining and TUNEL assays revealed that Caspase 6 silencing markedly decreased the rates of apoptosis and cell death (Fig. 3D–G). To further validate the role of Caspase 6, we used the Caspase 6 inhibitor Z-VEID-FMK. Z-VEID-FMK treatment attenuated the activation of Caspase 6, Caspase 3, and PARP in combined toxin-treated BMECs (Fig. 4A, B). It also significantly reduced the rates of apoptosis and cell death, as evidenced by the results of the Annexin V-FITC/PI staining and TUNEL assays (Fig. 4C–F). AMPK-Caspase 6 mediated DON-FB1-induced BMEC apoptosis

Previous studies have shown that AMPK regulates apoptosis via Caspase 6 in other pathological contexts, prompting us to investigate its role in DON-FB1-induced BMEC apoptosis. Consistent with these findings, we observed reduced AMPK phosphorylation during toxin-induced apoptosis (Fig. 5A, B), suggesting that AMPK may modulate Caspase 6 activity during this process. Treatment with AICAR, an AMPK activator, significantly increased AMPK phosphorylation and suppressed Caspase 6 activation in BMECs exposed to DON and FB1 (Fig. 5C, D). This effect was accompanied by reduced cleavage of Caspase 3 and PARP, as well as decreased apoptosis rates and cell death (Fig. 5E, F). Caspase 6 inhibition mitigates mammary gland damage induced by coexposure to DON and FB1 in lactating mice

To address translational relevance, we employed a mouse model as a feasible in vivo system to evaluate whether Caspase-6 inhibition mitigates mycotoxin-induced mammary gland damage. We assessed histopathological changes and apoptotic markers in mammary tissue to functionally support our in vitro findings (Fig. 7A). Mice exposed to DON and FB1 presented a significant decrease in body weight starting in the 2nd week postpartum, with further deterioration by the 3rd week; however, intraperitoneal injection of Z-VEID-FMK (5 mg/d) for 1 week effectively rescued body weight loss (Fig. 7B). In mammary tissues, the protein ratios of cleaved-Caspase 6 to Caspase 6, cleaved-Caspase 3 to Caspase 3, and cleaved-PARP to PARP were significantly elevated, whereas P-AMPK levels were markedly reduced compared with those in the control group (Fig. 7C, D). Discussion

The present study revealed a previously unrecognized synergistic interaction between DON and FB1 in mammary epithelial cells, revealing novel molecular targets for mitigating mycotoxin-induced lactation impairment. Our findings establish three pivotal advances in understanding mycotoxin toxicology: (1) the identification of Caspase 6 as a master regulator of combined DON-FB1 toxicity, (2) the discovery of AMPK-Caspase 6 crosstalk as a metabolic checkpoint controlling apoptosis, and (3) the demonstration that subclinical mycotoxin coexposure disrupts milk biosynthesis pathways. These insights bridge fundamental cell biology with practical challenges in dairy production, offering actionable strategies for addressing feed safety issues.

Recent advancements in analytical techniques have revealed that humans and animals are frequently exposed to multiple mycotoxins simultaneously29. In dairy cows, DON and FB1 frequently coexist in corn-based feeds, contaminating diets and leading to additive or synergistic toxic effects, as observed in this study30–32. We found that the combination of DON and FB1 reduced BMEC viability, increased apoptosis, and altered milk components more severely than individual exposure. DON was more cytotoxic, whereas FB1 alone did not significantly impact cell viability, even at higher concentrations. However, when DON and FB1 are combined, their toxicity is increased, raising concerns about the health risks of low-level mycotoxin mixtures33. These findings emphasize the importance of studying mycotoxin combinations, as their toxic effects cannot be predicted by examining individual toxins alone.

Our results demonstrated that the combined toxins significantly reduced the TG content, whereas β-casein and lactose secretion remained unaffected. Mechanistically, DON-FB1 notably downregulated key genes related to milk fat synthesis and casein production, whereas some genes involved in lactose synthesis were largely unaffected. Although milk protein synthesis was influenced by the combination of toxins, protein secretion might not be affected, possibly due to the sensitivity limitations of the assay or the requirement for higher toxin concentrations to observe an effect. Interestingly, lactose production appeared resistant to the antagonistic effects of these toxins. This resistance may be attributed to the unaltered availability of substrates necessary for lactose synthesis, supported by unchanged expression levels of GLUT1 and SLC2A12. Overall, this differential impact highlights the possibility of developing targeted interventions that mitigate toxic effects by modulating specific molecular pathways.

One of the most intriguing findings of this study is the identification of Caspase 6 as a central mediator of DON-FB1-induced apoptosis. While previous studies have implicated Caspase 6 in the regulation of apoptosis in other pathological contexts, such as neurodegenerative diseases and nonalcoholic steatohepatitis, its role in mycotoxin-induced toxicity has not been previously explored28,34,35. Studies have demonstrated that DON treatment increases the mRNA expression of the genes Casp3, Casp8, and Casp9 in intestinal porcine epithelial cells (IPEC-J2 cells)36 and leads to the activation of Caspase 3, Caspase 8, Caspase 9 and PARP in the mouse thymic epithelial cell line 137. Similarly, FB1 induces apoptosis by increasing the expression of cleaved-Caspase 3 in HK-2 cells38 and facilitating the cleavage and expression of Caspase 3 and Caspase 9 in IPEC-J2 cells39. These findings align with our own results, which demonstrate that both DON and FB1 are capable of activating specific caspases in BMECs, suggesting a similar apoptotic mechanism. Our findings expand the understanding of mycotoxin-induced apoptosis and suggest that Caspase 6 may play a more prominent role in chronic, low-dose exposure scenarios.

While AMPK is traditionally known as a metabolic regulator, its involvement in regulating apoptosis is context dependent40,41. In our study, DON-FB1 exposure led to AMPK inactivation, which in turn derepressed Caspase 6 activity and triggered apoptosis. This contrasts with studies showing that AMPK activation can either promote or inhibit apoptosis depending on the cellular context. For example, in hepatocytes, AMPK activation suppresses apoptosis42, whereas in astrocytoma cells, it induces apoptosis43. Previous studies have shown that mycotoxins can activate AMPK through multiple mechanisms. Mycotoxins such as Aflatoxin B1 (AFB1) and DON stimulate AMPK activation by inducing excessive intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, which subsequently leads to mitochondrial damage and ATP depletion44,45. As an energy sensor, AMPK responds to changes in the cellular ATP/ADP ratio and regulates stress adaptation46. Moreover, Liver kinase B1 (LKB1) is recognized as one of the primary upstream kinases of AMPK. It has been reported that AFB1 can inhibit the LKB1-AMPK pathway, thereby disturbing lipid metabolic homeostasis47. In addition, DON suppresses Sirtuin-3 (SIRT3) expression and activates AMPK to mitigate cellular damage48. Recent studies have also demonstrated that AMPK directly phosphorylates Caspase 6 at the Ser257 residue, a modification that inhibits its activation by effector caspases such as Caspase 3 or 7, particularly in the context of nonalcoholic steatohepatitis28. Moreover, pharmacological activation of AMPK has been shown to confer hepatoprotective effects. For example, Wang, Dong49 reported that the administration of an Non-mitogenicrecombinanthumanFGF4 (rFGF4△NT) analog designed to activate AMPK signaling led to the phosphorylation of Caspase 6 at Ser257 in a Non-Alcoholic Steatohepatitis (NASH) model induced by a Western-like diet. This intervention effectively reduced hepatocyte apoptosis, reinforcing the regulatory link between AMPK and Caspase 6 in metabolic liver diseases. However, whether AMPK-mediated phosphorylation of Caspase 6 occurs under exposure to mycotoxins in mammary epithelial cells requires further investigation.

Despite these advances, our study has several limitations that also point to unresolved questions. First, while we used a mouse model to simulate the effects of DON and FB1 on mammary gland tissue, the translation of these findings to dairy cows remains uncertain. Future studies should consider the use of bovine mammary gland perfusion models or in vivo experiments in dairy cows to better replicate the physiological conditions of lactating animals. Second, the doses of DON and FB1 used in this study, while relevant to subclinical exposure scenarios, may not fully capture the variability in mycotoxin concentrations encountered in field conditions. This raises questions about the dose‒response relationship and whether higher or lower doses might produce different outcomes.

Our study demonstrated that the combination of DON and FB1 exerts synergistic toxic effects on BMECs, primarily through the AMPK-Caspase 6 axis. These findings not only advance our understanding of mycotoxin toxicology but also provide a foundation for developing targeted interventions to mitigate their effects. On the basis of our results, we propose that Caspase 6 inhibition could be a promising strategy to protect mammary gland function in dairy cows exposed to mycotoxin-contaminated feed. Furthermore, our findings suggest that current safety thresholds for mycotoxins may need to be reevaluated to account for their combined effects.

Methods

Primary cell culture

Primary bovine mammary epithelial cells (BMECs) were isolated from three multiparous healthy lactating Holstein cows at similar lactation stages (DIM = 100 ± 5) as previously described50,51. Mammary glands were divided into four quadrants along the midline, and tissue samples were aseptically excised from the center of each segment. After the surrounding adipose and connective tissues were removed, the tissues were minced into ~0.5-mm pieces. The minced tissue was then digested with 0.5% collagenase III (C8490, Solarbio) at 38 °C for 3 h. The solution was subsequently filtered through a 74-μm cell strainer and centrifuged at 150 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The collected cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium/nutrient mixture F-12 (DMEM/F-12) (11330032, Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, 16140071, Gibco), 500 ng/mL hydrocortisone (3867, Sigma‒Aldrich), 1 μg/L prolactin (869039, Sigma‒Aldrich), 10 ng/mL epidermal growth factor (AF-100-15, Peprotech), and 100 IU/mL penicillin/streptomycin and cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. The residual fibroblasts were removed by repeated digestion with 0.25% trypsin (15050065, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 3‒4 cycles at 37 °C. After 3 passages, the cells were plated on six-well plates with DMEM containing 100 IU/mL penicillin/streptomycin and 10% FBS. The medium was changed every 24 h.

The morphology of the isolated mammary epithelial cells were evaluated via immunofluorescence staining for cytokeratin 18 (CK-18). The microscope image revealed a characteristic ‘cobblestone-like’ morphology, and immunofluorescence confirmed positive CK-18 expression, indicating high purity of the mammary epithelial cells (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Cell treatment

Deoxynivalenol (DON, purity ≥ 98%) was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare a 10 mM stock solution (D0156, Sigma‒Aldrich). FB1 (purity ≥ 98%) was dissolved in sterile water to a concentration of 20 mM (F135527, Aladdin). These stock solutions were then diluted in culture medium to produce the desired concentration for use. BMECs were treated with DON (0, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, or 4 μM) or FB1 (0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, or 32 μM) for 24 h. In addition, BMECs were exposed to a combination of 0.5 μM DON with increasing concentrations of FB1 (0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, or 32 μM) for 24 h. To inhibit Caspase 6 activity, the cells were pretreated with 3 μM Z-VEID-FMK (ALX-260-143-R020; Enzo Life Sciences) for 1 h. To activate AMPK, BMECs were treated with 500 μM AICAR (S1515-50 mg, Beyotime) for 4 h before further treatment.

For Caspase 6 silencing, BMECs were transfected with negative control small interfering RNA (si-control, si-ctrl) or siRNA targeting Caspase 6 (si-Caspase 6, si-Casp6) using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA). The siRNAs were designed and synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China) on the basis of the bovine Caspase 6 mRNA sequence. The sequence of the Caspase 6-siRNA primer used was as follows: sense, GGCCUCGGUUUAUACACUUTT; antisense, AAGUGUAUAAACCGAGGCCTT. The scrambled negative control siRNA primer sequence was as follows: UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT, and the antisense sequence was ACGUGACACGUUCGGAGAATT. A concentration of 10 nM si-Casp6 was sufficient to significantly reduce the protein and mRNA levels of Caspase 6, and this concentration was used in subsequent experiments (Supplemental Fig. 4). For Caspase 6 overexpression, primary BMECs were infected with empty adenoviral vectors (Ad-vector, 1 × 1010 PFU/ml) or recombinant adenoviral vectors expressing Caspase 6 (Ad-Casp6, 1.26 × 1010 PFU/ml) constructed by Hanbio Biotechnology Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The cells were infected at different multiplicities of infection (MOIs of 10, 20, and 40) for 10 h in serum-free media, followed by replacement with neurobasal media. The transfected cells were incubated for 48 h under 5% CO2. The optimal MOI was determined on the basis of infection efficiency (>80%) and the cell survival rate (>80%). The transduction efficiency was confirmed by qRT‒PCR and western blotting (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Immunofluorescence assay

Primary BMECs were seeded on glass-bottom dishes and cultured until ~90% confluence. The cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature. After additional washes with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), the cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 1 h. Following additional washes with PBS, the cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with an anti-CK-18 antibody (1:100; cat. no. bs-2043R; Bioss) diluted in PBS containing 5% bovine serum albumin. The cells were then washed and incubated with an anti-rabbit secondary antibody conjugated with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) (1:200; cat. no. ab6785; Abcam) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. After additional washes, the cells were stained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; cat. no. C0060; Solarbio) for 5 min. Immunofluorescence was observed using a confocal laser microscope (FluoView FV1200, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and immunohistochemical (IHC) staining

H&E and IHC staining were performed on paraffin-embedded mammary glands. Briefly, the fourth pair of mammary glands was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight, dehydrated, cleared, and embedded in paraffin. The waxes were sectioned serially at 3–5 μm thickness. After deparaffinization and rehydration, standard H&E staining was performed. H&E-stained sections of mammary gland tissues were reviewed manually first, and representative sections from each group were selected and processed further for immunohistochemical analysis. For immunohistochemical (IHC) staining, deparaffinized and rehydrated sections were heated in EDTA antigen retrieval buffer (pH = 8.0) (G1206, Servicebio, China) at 100 °C for 15 min for antigen retrieval and then blocked in TBS containing 3% BSA for 60 min. We then incubated them with anti-cleaved-Caspase 3 rabbit pAb (1:500, GB11532, Servicebio, China) and anti-cleaved-Caspase 6 rabbit pAb (1:100, 341036, Zenbio, China) primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Afterward, the sections were incubated with FITC-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:200, AS011, ABclonal, China) and stained with DAPI to label the nucleus. Histological features were observed and captured under a light microscope (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan).

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, ST1007, Sunbao Biotech) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well. After treatment, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific Instruments Inc., Shanghai, China). Cell viability was calculated using the following formula: (OD of treatment group—OD of blank group)/(OD of control group—OD of blank group) × 100%, where OD represents the optical density.

Determination of lactate dehydrogenase activity

LDH activity was measured using a commercial assay kit (A020-2-2, Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well. After treatment, 20 μL of the supernatant from each well was transferred to a new 96-well plate. The supernatant was mixed with 20 µL of 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine and incubated at 37 °C for 15 min. Subsequently, 250 µL of NaOH solution (0.4 M) was added, and the mixture was incubated at room temperature for 5 min in a light-protected environment. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Multiskan FC microplate reader (Thermo Scientific Instruments Inc., Shanghai, China).

Apoptosis assay

Apoptosis was detected using an Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit (C1062M; Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The cells were seeded into 6-well plates and treated for 24 h, after which the culture supernatants were collected. The cells were washed with PBS and digested with trypsin, and the digestion was terminated by adding the collected supernatants. Following centrifugation at 1000 × g for 5 min, the supernatant was discarded, and the cells were resuspended in a mixture containing 195 μL of binding buffer, 5 μL of Annexin V-FITC, and 10 μL of propidine iodide (PI) solution. The cells were incubated for 15 min at room temperature in the dark, and Annexin V-FITC and PI staining were analyzed by flow cytometry (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ). The cells were classified into live, necrotic, and apoptotic categories, and the percentages of apoptotic cells in each group were compared.

TUNEL assay

Mammary tissues and cells were processed for TUNEL staining using a TUNEL Apoptosis Detection Kit IV, CY3 (MK1016, Boster Biological Technology, Ltd., Wuhan, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The cells were treated with proteinase K for 10 min, fixed, and permeabilized at room temperature. After rinsing, the cells were incubated with the TUNEL reaction mixture at 37 °C for 60 min. Coverslips were mounted, and the samples were visualized using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus). Cells with bright red fluorescence in the nucleus were considered apoptotic. Quantitative analysis was performed using Image-Pro Plus (Media Cybernetics).

Measurement of triglyceride (TG)

The TG content in the cells was measured using a commercial kit (E1013, Applygen Technologies, Beijing, China). The cells were lysed in 100 µL of lysis buffer (Sangon Biotech Co. Ltd.) and incubated on ice for 5 min. The lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 × g at 4 °C for 5 min, and a portion of the supernatant was used to determine the total protein concentration using a bicinchoninic acid assay (P1511, Applygen Technologies, Inc.). The TG content was determined by mixing 10 μL of the supernatant with 190 μL of chromogenic reagent, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 15 min. The absorbance was measured at 550 nm using a Multiskan FC microplate reader (Thermo Scientific Instruments Inc., Shanghai, China).

Measurement of the β-casein concentration

The concentration of β-casein in the cell culture medium was determined using a bovine CSN2 ELISA Kit (ml036568-J, Shanghai, mlbio) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The cells were seeded into six-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/mL. After treatment, the medium was collected and centrifuged. Subsequently, 50 μL of each sample was added to a 96-well plate precoated with a capture antibody for 1 h, followed by incubation with a detection antibody for 1 h and avidin-HRP for 30 min. After incubation with a chromogenic reagent for 15 min at 37 °C, the reaction was terminated by the addition of 100 μL of stop solution. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm. A standard curve was generated, and the β-casein concentration in the samples was calculated on the basis of the standard curve.

Measurement of the lactose concentration

The lactose concentration in the cell culture medium was determined using a Lactose Content Detection Kit (JL-T1072; JiangLai Biotech) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The cells were seeded into six-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/mL. After treatment, the medium was collected and centrifuged. A 10-μL sample from each well was mixed with 20 μL of distilled water, 10 µL of Reagent 1, and 30 µL of Reagent 2, followed by incubation at 25 °C for 20 min. Subsequently, 170 µL of Reagent 4 and 10 µL of Reagent 5 were added, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. The absorbance was measured at 510 nm, and the lactose concentration was calculated using the provided formula.

Total RNA isolation and Real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol reagent (TaKaRa Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using PrimeScript Reverse Transcriptase (TaKaRa Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a K5500 Micro Spectrophotometer (Beijing Kaiao Technology Development Ltd., Beijing, China), with an OD260/OD280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.0 considered acceptable. In this study, the OD260/OD280 ratio was determined to be 1.9, indicating sufficient purity. One microgram of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA, and relative mRNA levels were quantified using a 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems Inc., Waltham, MA) and a SYBR Green QuantiTect RT‒PCR Kit (TaKaRa Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). The PCR conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min. The PCR efficiency (E) was calculated as E (%) = (10(−1/slope) − 1) × 100. The primers used and their efficiencies are listed in Supplementary Table 1. Relative expression values were normalized to those of β-actin (ACTB) and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), and fold changes in gene expression were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. ACTB and GAPDH were stably expressed across the treatment groups (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Protein extraction and western blotting

Western blot analysis was performed as previously described52,53. The total protein was extracted from mammary tissue or cells, prepared for western blotting and measured using the bicinchoninic acid assay (P1511; Applygen Technologies). A total of 30 μg of protein from each sample was separated by 12% SDS‒PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. The membranes were blocked with 3% BSA in Tris-buffered saline with Tween-20 (TBS-T) for 4 h. Primary antibodies against Caspase 3 (1:1000; A19654; ABclonal), PARP (1:1000; 9542; Cell Signaling Technology), Caspase 6 (1:1000; A1784; ABclonal), cleaved-Caspase 6 (1:1000; 9761; Cell Signaling Technology), P-AMPK (1:1000; 2535; Cell Signaling Technology), AMPK (1:1000; 10929-2-AP; Proteintech), P-mTOR (1:1000; AP0115; ABclonal), mTOR (1:1000; 2972; Cell Signaling Technology), P-JAK2 (1:1000; YP0306; ImmunoWay), JAK2 (1:1000; sc-390539; Santa Cruz Biotechnology), P-STAT5 (1:1000; 9359; Cell Signaling Technology), STAT5 (1:1000; 94205; Cell Signaling Technology), SREBP1 (1:1000; A1586; ABclonal), and β-actin (1:2000; 60008-I-Ig; Proteintech) were incubated with the membranes overnight at 4 °C The membranes were washed with TBS-T and incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (Boster, Wuhan, China) for 45 min at room temperature. Immunoreactive bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL, Millipore, Bedford, MA). The intensity of the protein bands was quantified using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 (Media Cybernetics) and normalized to that of β-actin. The ratios of cleaved-Caspase 6/Caspase 6, cleaved-Caspase 3/Caspase 3, and cleaved-PARP/PARP were calculated to assess protein abundance.

Animals

Seventy pregnant C57BL/6 female mice (12–13 weeks old) were purchased from Liaoning Changsheng Biotechnology Co. After delivery, each group of ten pups was housed with their respective mothers in individual cages. The day following the birth of the pups was designated Lactation Day 1. Lactating mice were randomly divided into four groups on the basis of exposure duration (n = 10 per group) with low-dose coexposure to DON and FB1 (45 μg/kg DON, 20 μg/kg FB1): (1) DON + FB1 7-day exposure group (DF-7); (2) DON + FB1 14-day exposure group (DF-14); (3) DON + FB1 21-day exposure group (DF-21); and (4) DON + FB1 + Z-VEID-FMK group, in which lactating mice were exposed to DON + FB1 for 14 days followed by intraperitoneal injection of either vehicle or the Caspase 6 inhibitor Z-VEID-FMK for 1 week while continuing exposure to DON + FB1. The control groups (7 days, 14 days, and 21 days) consisted of lactating mice that were orally gavaged with distilled water for 7, 14, and 21 days, respectively. All the mice were housed under controlled conditions (23–25 °C, 12-h light/dark cycle) with free access to water and food. At the end of the experiment, the mice were sacrificed, and mammary tissues were collected. All procedures were performed in accordance with the Laboratory Animal Welfare Guidelines (GB/T 35, 892–2018, China).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software) or SPSS 23.0 (IBM Corp.). All the data were tested for normality and homogeneity of variance using the Shapiro‒Wilk and Levene tests, respectively. For data that conformed to a normal distribution, one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test, was performed for multiple group comparisons; for data that did not conform to a normal distribution, a nonparametric test was used. All the data conformed to a normal distribution. The results are presented as the means ± SEMs. Different superscript lowercase letters in the bar charts indicate statistically significant differences (P < 0.05). All experiments were repeated at least three times with independently prepared cell samples from different animals.
