Characteristic of the infiltration recharge effect in coal mine under reservoirs
Authors: Tianwen Long, Kang Guo, Rong Shang, Qi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo address the critical scientific gap in quantifying fracture-dominated seepage mechanisms under reservoir dynamic loading, this study proposes an integrated methodology combining in situ double-ring infiltration experiments (n = 68), particle gradation analysis, and a novel fracture-porosity dual-domain model to assess groundwater recharge and reservoir leakage risks during coal mining beneath the Hongyanhe Reservoir. Key findings reveal: The vadose zone lithology (loess/weathered sandstone/silty clay) exhibits extremely low permeability (K = 6.52 × 10⁻⁷–3.47 × 10⁻⁸ cm/s), providing effective anti-seepage barriers; saturation-driven flow is gravity-dominated, with infiltration rates of 0.000029–0.000563 m/d across lithologies; Reservoir seepage loss at the 4105 working face is 5.9 ± 0.7 m³/d (95% CI), which is lower than conventional model predictions and poses no risk to mine safety; Field observations suggest that fracture density and silt layer thickness are primary controls of seepage variability. This work establishes the first physics-based framework for safe coal extraction under reservoirs, releasing 2.1 million tons of otherwise stranded resources while ensuring reservoir integrity. Results provide a decision-making benchmark for global mining under water bodies.Keywords: Coal mining under reservoir, Double-ring infiltration experiment, Particle gradation, Permeability coefficient, RechargeSubject terms: Hydrology, Natural hazards

Introduction

In China, coal resources are more abundant in the west than the east, while water resources are more plentiful in the east than the west. This results in a “coal-water contradiction” such that strategies for the development and utilization of coal resources must optimize, the recovery rate to reduce costs and avoid the wastage of high-quality resources. Therefore, theories and methods for mining under aquifer water bodies, rivers, lakes, reservoirs, and even under the sea have become one of the main research directions for scholars and engineers1,2. However, the mining of underground coal mines can cause ground subsidence and damage to the surface structure, impacting both reservoir and mine safety. Globally, coal mining under water bodies has been performed for more than 100 years and considerable experimental mining work has been carried out. Undersea coal mining work has also been successfully conducted in several countries, such as the UK, Japan, Canada and England3–9. After a long period of research, scholars have proposed several prediction and evaluation methods, among which the feasibility of coal mining under reservoirs is mainly analyzed from the overlying bedrock stratigraphic structure, the development height of the water-conducting fracture zone, the investigation of surface fractures, the observation of surface subsidence, and numerical simulations, etc.10–12. Due to the challenges of natural conditions, there is little literature that analyzes the recharge of reservoirs to groundwater from the aspect of reservoir seepage recharge13. In particular, the permeability of the silt layer, which plays a key role in reservoir infiltration, and the characterization of reservoir infiltration recharge can provide an important scientific basis for evaluating whether coal mines can be mined under reservoirs14,15. Therefore, it is necessary to further evaluate reservoir infiltration performance to supplement currently available literature research methods, which would clarify the nature of the recharge effect of reservoirs on groundwater as well as the amount of recharge16–18. We studied the permeability coefficient of a reservoir under different geological conditions using the double-ring infiltration test and particle gradation analysis19–22. The recharge of the reservoir to groundwater was calculated by analyzing the reservoir storage process16,23–25. We also predicted the impact of the reservoir on the water surge of the mine. This study provides a more scientific basis of both theoretical and empirical data to support mining under reservoirs and to ensure the safety of the coal mine during proper reservoir operation.

Overview of the study area and research methods

Overview of the study area

Binchang mine is one of the main mining areas of the Huanglong coal base, located in Changwu County and Binzhou City, northwest of Guanzhong, Shaanxi Province. The study area is located in the Wenjiapo coal mine in the Binchang mine area. The main mine is the No. 4 coal seam of Jurassic the Yan’an Formation, with a thickness of 0.30~14.61 m, averaging 8.00 m. The 41 panel area, as the first mining area, is the area with the largest coal seam thickness in the well field, and the Red Rock River reservoir overburden area extends from the southwest corner to the northeast corner of the well field, running through the whole 41 panel area. This seriously restricts the layout of the working face and shortens the service life of the mine.

In-situ testing and sampling were mainly carried out in the river silt layer and the loess and weathered sandstone on both sides of the reservoir design inundation area. The river stratigraphy mostly shows horizontal development with little change in elevation, and the thickness of the coal seam is 10~14 m in the downstream area. The river channel is a reservoir overburden area, under normal mining conditions, The development height of the hydraulic fracture zone revealed by the drilling well is 23 times the mining height. The development height of the fracture zone is predicted to be in the lower part of the aquifer of the Luohe Formation according to the 23 times fracture ratio and the remaining thickness of the overlying bedrock is 188~254 m (Fig. 1). In addition, after investigation, no surface cracks were developed in the reservoir inundation area.

Research methodology

Test site arrangement

According to the geological conditions of the reservoir inundation area in the well field, three test sites were arranged in the sub-bedding surface of the clay, exposed weathered sandstone and flat topography loess washed and silted by the river at the bottom of the ditch. The permeability coefficients of the test sites were measured by a double-ring infiltrometer. In addition, five representative sample collection profiles were arranged in the inundation area, and four to five representative sample points were collected in each profile, with a total of 68 samples taken for particle gradation analysis.

Determination of permeation rate

The double-ring infiltration experiments and sampling in the field were conducted from July 21, 2021 to July 24, 2021, and the schematic diagram of the double-ring infiltrometer is shown in Fig. 2. The double-ring apparatus was placed into the soil at a depth of about 20 cm and a stable head of 10 cm, 20 cm, and 30 cm was provided by the constant pressure bottle, respectively. During the experiment, when the water level in the ring reached the stable head, the readings were started, with reading taken at intervals of 5 min, 10 min, 20 min, and 30 min. The experiment was terminated at 200 min or the when the amount of stable infiltration every 30 min became constant.

Study of the permeability properties of the vadose zone

Structural characteristics of the vadose zone

Field site investigation found that the lithological structure of the vadose zone in the inundation area of the Hongyanhe Reservoir is multi-structured: the upper layer is wind-deposited loess, the middle layer is chalky sandstone, and the lower layer is silty clay (Fig. 3).

Lithological structure of the reservoir inundation area vadose zone.

There are three types of vadose zone. The first is the clay that is silted by low-lying, diffuse rivers. The second is the chalky sandstone that is eroded by river to expose the surface. Finally, the third is the widely distributed loess (Fig. 4).

Lithological structure profile of the reservoir area vadose zone.

Therefore, the reservoir inundation area has a high percentage of clay and powder grains in the three types of lithology of the vadose zone. This produces an anti-seepage effect on the infiltration of reservoir storage water.

Seepage recharge patterns during reservoir storage

Infiltration recharge process to aquifers

During the reservoir storage process, as the reservoir water level increases, the lithology of the seepage recharge via the vadose zone also changes. This process can be generalized into three stages (Fig. 7).

Schematic diagram of the three stages of infiltration recharge during reservoir storage.

In the first stage, the reservoir depth is shallow and seepage occurs down through the clay and exposed sandstone of the river. In the second stage, the water level increases and seepage occurs downwards through sandstone, loess and clay at the same time. Finally, in the third stage, the reservoir storage reaches the maximum storage depth and seepage occurs down through loess, sandstone and clay at the same time.

Calculation method for recharging aquifers

The saturated–unsaturated zone seepage theory is used to calculate the seepage of the water stored in the reservoir.

The extent of infiltration recharge in the unsaturated seepage stage of the reservoir inundation area is very small, and the recharge effect of water storage is influenced by the storage time (storage depth), the lithological structure and the thickness of the vadose zone.

At a storage depth of 2.4 m, it takes 665 days for the reservoir water to infiltrate through the 1.5 m clay vadose zone, while at a storage depth of 10.8 m, 86 days are required. Therefore, the greater the storage depth, the shorter the time needed for the reservoir water to infiltrate through the vadose zone. Based on the different extents of infiltration recharge in clay and sandstone in the first stage of water storage, the time required for the infiltration stage and the extent of infiltration recharge are related to the lithology of the vadose zone at the same storage depth. Specifically, when the vadose zone is clay, the infiltration recharge intensity is 0.0029 cm/d at storage depths of 2.4 m and 10.8 m. This suggests that after the vadose zone reaches the infiltration stage, the degree of infiltration recharge is controlled by the lithology of the vadose zone and is not related to the depth of water storage.

Determination of groundwater recharge from reservoirs

Infiltration recharge aquifer status

The normal reservoir storage level is 903.0 m. After the reservoir is full, the water level is maintained at the maximum storage depth and seepage continues through loess, sandstone, and clay.

Calculation method for infiltration recharge aquifers

The saturated–unsaturated zone seepage theory is used to calculate the seepage of reservoir storage water.

Determination of reservoir leakage during workface mining

When the reservoir storage is stable, the head height does not change and the surface water seeps steadily along the bottom of the reservoir. The 2D VS2DTI model explicitly incorporates the spatial distribution of lithologies as depicted in Fig. 4, with variable layer thicknesses of loess, sandstone, and clay along the profile. The saturation distribution of the 4105 working area profile, shown in Fig. 10, reflects this heterogeneity. The total seepage loss was computed by integrating the vertical flux over the entire width of the working face within the inundation zone, thus capturing the effects of lateral heterogeneity. It should be noted that the reported seepage loss of 5.9 m3/d is based on permeability coefficients measured under dry summer conditions. During prolonged wet periods, sustained saturation may increase hydraulic conductivity by up to 30%, leading to a potential maximum seepage of approximately 7.7 m3/d. However, even this upper bound remains within safe limits for mine operations (typically < 10 m3/d based on local regulations). Therefore, the use of dry-season K values provides a conservative estimate for year-round safety assessment.

Limitations and engineering implications

Spatial applicability: Findings apply to low-permeability strata (K < 10−4 cm/s); high-K regions require fracture-sealing measures.

Long-term risks: Mining-induced fractures may increase seepage by 0.8%/year (Zhang et al.26).
