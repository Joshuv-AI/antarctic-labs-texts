The Efficacy of Whole Blood Exchange-Lymphoplasmapheresis Combined with Transfusion in Sepsis with Anemia
Authors: Run Yao, Danyang Yan, Xiangjie Fu, Huan Li, Siya Pei, Wenyu Yin, Cheng Mei, Xinying Zhang, Wanting Huang, Bijuan Li, Ning Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Sepsis progression is driven by a dysregulated immune response and persistent leukocyte activation. Inflammation-induced hemolysis also places septic patients at high risk for anemia. Whole blood exchange has shown efficacy in severe autoimmune hemolytic anemia. Consequently, whole blood exchange-lymphoplasmapheresis combined with transfusion (WLCT) is hypothesized as a potential adjunctive therapy for sepsis complicated by anemia.Method:This retrospective study analyzed data from 219 anemic septic patients (49 WLCT vs. 156 conventional therapy) treated between January 1, 2020, and September 30, 2023. After 1:2 propensity score matching, 38 WLCT patients were compared with 76 conventional patients. Associations between WLCT and clinical outcomes were assessed using regression analyses, with robustness verified by sensitivity analyses. A generalized additive mixed model was used to evaluate the trends in sequential organ failure assessment scores, vital signs, and laboratory indexes in two groups.Results:Postmatching, WLCT was significantly associated with decreased risks of in-hospital mortality (26.32% vs. 48.68%, odds ratio: 0.38, 95% confidence interval: 0.16–0.88, P = 0.024) and incidence of liver failure (16.13% vs. 41.27%, odds ratio: 0.27, 95% confidence interval: 0.09–0.81, P = 0.019). Sensitivity analysis consistently supported these findings. Compared with conventional group, WLCT group demonstrated significant decreases over time in sequential organ failure assessment scores, activated partial thromboplastin time, bilirubin levels, alongside increased in mean arterial pressure, oxygen partial pressure, hemoglobin, red blood cell count, and plasminogen levels. Compared with pre-WLCT, levels of interleukin-6 (18.81 ± 22.63 vs. 116.86 ± 277.53 pg/mL, P = 0.041), C-reactive protein (37.90 ± 41.30 vs. 72.77 ± 65.59 mg/L, P = 0.020), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (18.46 ± 22.97 vs. 50.44 ± 45.79 mm/h, P = 0.048) were significantly lower post-WLCT. After treatment, the WLCT group demonstrated significantly lower CD3+ T lymphocytes % (53.63 ± 16.31 vs. 66.14 ± 14.94, P = 0.041) and CD3+CD4+ T lymphocytes % (28.08 ± 8.90 vs. 39.18 ± 10.70, P = 0.004), but higher CD19+ B lymphocytes % (36.09 ± 18.06 vs. 19.23 ± 12.30, P = 0.007) compared with the conventional group.Conclusions:This study evaluated the impact of WLCT therapy on mortality and other clinical outcomes in sepsis with anemia. WLCT might be an effective adjunctive treatment for managing sepsis with anemia, which was significantly associated with reduced mortality. Furthermore, WLCT might exhibit the potential to ameliorate outcomes by improving organ function, coagulation, anemia, and inflammatory status. Our study provides a potential treatment option for sepsis.

INTRODUCTION

Sepsis is a life-threatening immune dysregulation and organ dysfunction caused by the body’s abnormal response to infection. It is one of the leading causes of death and increased healthcare costs in intensive care unit (ICU) patients, with an in-hospital mortality rate of approximately 26.7% (1). A dysregulated immune response and persistently activated white blood cells play a pivotal role in sepsis progression (2). Studies have indicated that removing activated white blood cells from the circulation can help mitigate distant organ damage induced by sepsis (3). An animal study found that selectively adsorbing granulocytes and monocytes with cellulose acetate beads significantly improved sepsis caused by cecal ligation and puncture. This treatment enhanced survival rates and organ function (4). However, current new therapies for sepsis focus on blocking excessive plasma inflammatory mediators or abnormal activation of the coagulation system. These methods have shown limited impact on the disease process in clinical trials (5). Therefore, the exploration of new treatment strategies aimed at limiting the excessive activation of immune cells caused by immune dysregulation has become a key focus of current research (6).

In addition, inflammation-induced hemolytic mechanisms often place sepsis patients at high risk of anemia (7,8). Anemia is associated with worse clinical outcomes in patients with sepsis. For instance, among patients with septic shock, the 28-day and 90-day mortality rates were 59% and 66% in those with hemoglobin levels of 6.8–7.9 g/dL, compared with 39% and 43% in those with hemoglobin levels of 8.9–11.0 g/dL (9). Another study demonstrated that the 28-day and 90-day mortality rates were 35% and 46.9% in patients with hemoglobin <7.0 g/dL, 26.7% and 46.5% in those with 7.0–7.9 g/dL, 27.3% and 43.0% in those with 8.0–8.9 g/dL, and 19.1% and 29.1% in those with hemoglobin ≥9.0 g/dL, respectively (10). Furthermore, it has been documented that each 1 g/dL increase in hemoglobin level was associated with a 17% reduction in in-hospital mortality among septic patients (11). Inflammatory mediators, such as interleukin (IL)-6 and IL-1β, can affect erythropoiesis and promote red blood cell destruction, thereby exacerbating lung and kidney injury in patients (12). Autoantibodies frequently appear in sepsis patients and sensitize red blood cells, which increases the risk of hemolysis (13). A positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) is associated with hemolysis, with hemolysis severity strongly correlating with DAT reaction strength (more severe hemolysis leads to more severe anemia) (14). Additionally, sepsis treatment often involves antibiotics, and drug-induced immune hemolysis is a common cause of positive DAT (15); residual drugs in such patients may render red blood cell transfusion ineffective or even trigger immune hemolysis (16). Therefore, optimizing the condition that support red blood cells survival and effectively managing sensitized red blood cells are crucial for improving patient prognosis. Based on these findings, comprehensive therapy targeting plasma inflammatory mediators and blood cell components, including activated immune cells and red blood cells, appears promising for opening new therapeutic avenues for sepsis patients with anemia.

Whole blood exchange (WBE) is a therapy that mechanically eliminates hemolyzed erythrocytes, plasma, and complement bound to sensitized red blood cells from the circulation, replacing them with normal erythrocytes and frozen plasma (17,18). Early studies have confirmed that WBE is effective in treating severe autoimmune hemolytic anemia with strongly positive antihuman globulin tests, providing rapid improvement in clinical prognosis (17,19,20). And research has indicated that WBE may enhance outcomes in critically ill patients, yet its specific effects on sepsis-related anemia have not been extensively studied (21). This may be because WBE predominantly targets erythrocytes, leading to limited removal of inflammatory mediators and immune-active cells in sepsis. WBE-lymphoplasmapheresis combined with transfusion (WLCT) is a novel blood replacement technique that removes a portion of the patient’s whole blood and replaces it with healthy red blood cells and fresh frozen plasma. This procedure combines exchanging plasma, exchanging red blood cells, and removing pathogenic cells. Compared with plasma exchange, WLCT can not only eliminate soluble factors such as cytokines and immune complexes but also reduce sensitized T and B cells in the peripheral blood, thereby removing soluble pathogenic factors and disease-related over-activated immune cells. It also eliminates sensitized and fragmented red blood cells, while supplementing fresh red blood cells. Therefore, WLCT may become a promising adjunctive treatment for sepsis patients with anemia.

This study aims to address a critical knowledge gap regarding WLCT therapy in septic patients suffering from anemia. By systematically retrospectively analyzing the clinical data, it seeks to provide insights into the efficacy of WLCT as a treatment strategy, potentially leading to improved management protocols for this high-risk group. The findings may contribute to a better understanding of how WLCT can be integrated into routine clinical practice for septic patients and inform future research directions in this area. As sepsis continues to pose significant challenges in critical care settings, innovative approaches such as WLCT therapy may hold promise for enhancing patient outcomes and reducing the burden of this complex condition.

METHODS

Study design and patients

Septic patients admitted to Xiangya Hospital of Central South University between January 1, 2020, and September 30, 2023, who fulfilled the following inclusion criteria were recruited: 1) meeting the diagnostic criteria for Sepsis-3.0; 2) meeting the diagnostic criteria for anemia, defined as hemoglobin <12 g/dL for adult males and <11 g/dL for adult females; and 3) who received conventional treatments during hospitalization, including antimicrobial therapy, supportive care, and red blood cell transfusion. Exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) age <18 years old; 2) nonfirst admission; 3) hospital stay <24 hours; 4) pregnant or lactating patients; and 5) patients with end-stage diseases. Patients were divided into the WLCT group and the conventional group based on whether they had received WLCT therapy during hospitalization. This observational retrospective study was performed in compliance with the Helsinki Declaration II and the Chinese Standards for Good Clinical Practice. Individual consent was waived owing to the study’s retrospective nature. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xiangya Hospital (202103418).

Data collection

We retrospectively retrieved clinical data of patients through the information management system of Xiangya Hospital, Central South University. Baseline data collected for all patients included: 1) demographic information: age and gender; 2) clinical scores on the first day of admission, including the sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA) score and the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) II score; 3) interventions on the first day of ICU admission: mechanical ventilation (MV), vasopressors, and renal replacement therapy (RRT); 4) septic shock: whether the patient presented with septic shock; 5) comorbidities: hypertension, diabetes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, heart failure, connective tissue diseases, chronic kidney diseases, and malignancy. Connective tissue diseases refer to conditions affecting connective tissues, including systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis, scleroderma, dermatomyositis, polyarteritis nodosa, Wegener’s granulomatosis, and giant cell arteritis. Chronic kidney diseases refer to structural or functional abnormalities of the kidneys or unexplained decline in glomerular filtration rate for more than 3 months, such as glomerulonephritis, nephrotic syndrome, diabetic nephropathy, and hypertensive nephropathy; 6) laboratory parameters obtained from the first blood sample collected upon admission included platelet count (PLT), international normalized ratio, white blood cell count (WBC), hemoglobin (Hb), serum creatinine (SCr), and total bilirubin (TBIL); 7) infection sites: pulmonary infection, urinary tract infection, or intraabdominal infection; 8) infectious pathogens: bacterial infection, fungal infection, mixed infection, or negative culture results; 9) organ failure status in the patient; 10) DAT results and other anemia-related indicators, including red blood cell count (RBC), mean corpuscular volume, mean corpuscular hemoglobin, mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration, red blood cell distribution width (RDW), and hematocrit (HCT).

The longitudinal data included the following parameters: 1) SOFA score; 2) vital signs: mean arterial pressure (MAP), partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PCO2), and partial pressure of oxygen (PO2); and 3) laboratory parameters: Hb, RBC, lactate (Lac), D-dimer, plasminogen (PLG), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), TBIL, direct bilirubin (DBIL), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST). For patients in the WLCT group, the time of the first WLCT was defined as “day 0,” whereas for patients in the conventional group, the time of the first conventional treatments was defined as “day 0.” Longitudinal data were recorded from day 0 to day 7. Additionally, we collected inflammation-related indicators and lymphocyte subgroup data within 3 days before and 3 days after treatment (22). Inflammatory markers included IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, C-reactive protein (CRP), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR). The lymphocyte subgroup data included the percentage of T lymphocytes (CD3+%), helper T lymphocytes (CD3+CD4+%), cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CD3+CD8+%), B lymphocytes (CD19+%), NK cells (CD16+CD56+%), as well as the ratio of helper T cells to cytotoxic T cells (TH/TS).

WLCT procedures

Patients in the WLCT group received additional WLCT alongside standard sepsis care, whereas those in the conventional group received standard therapy alone. The therapy protocol involved performing WBE in the initial phase, followed by transitioning to lymphoplasmapheresis mode once the target hemoglobin level of 9 g/dL was reached (23). The WLCT procedure included system setup, vascular access establishment, semiautomatic operation with parameter adjustment, and targeted treatment monitoring; detailed operational steps are illustrated in Supplementary Figure 1, https://links.lww.com/SHK/C823. In brief, the key distinction between WLCT and lymphoplasmapheresis was that WLCT initially replaced the patient’s whole blood with donor plasma and red blood cells, followed by removal of plasma and immune cells, whereas lymphoplasmapheresis removed plasma and immune cells throughout the procedure (22). Similar to previous reports (24), WLCT was performed by the Department of Blood Transfusion at our hospital, with the decision for therapeutic WLCT made via a multidisciplinary approach. Patients who met the inclusion criteria and had a DAT ≥ 2+ were considered candidates. The attending intensivist initiated a WLCT request, followed by transfusion team consultation and individual evaluation. WLCT was only performed if both parties agreed on potential benefits; otherwise, it was not included in the treatment plan. WLCT was initiated in patients with an Hb level <7.0 g/dL. For patients with an Hb level of 7–10 g/dL, WLCT was initiated if any one of the following conditions was present: persistent hyperlactatemia, impaired tissue perfusion, high vasopressor requirement, active myocardial ischemia, or severe hypoxemia. Adverse reactions occurring during the operation were recorded and handled by professional physicians according to the specific symptoms of patients.

Definition and clinical outcomes

Sepsis was defined as a life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection. The diagnostic criteria included suspected or documented infection and an acute increase in total SOFA score ≥2 points as a proxy for organ dysfunction. Septic shock was a subset of sepsis characterized by circulatory and cellular/metabolic dysfunction, clinically identified by the need for vasopressors to maintain MAP ≥65 mmHg and lactate >2 mmol/L (18 mg/dL) despite adequate fluid resuscitation (25).

The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality. Secondary outcomes included length of hospital stay (LOS hospital) and length of ICU stay (LOS ICU), duration of MV, duration of vasopressor therapy, duration of RRT, and the incidence of respiratory failure, circulatory failure, renal failure, hepatic failure, coagulation failure, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) (26), and disseminated intravascular coagulation (27). Organ dysfunction was diagnosed based on the SOFA score, with a threshold of ≥3 points for the respective organ system (28). For example, liver failure was defined as a SOFA score ≥3 in the hepatic subscale, corresponding to a serum total bilirubin level ≥6.0 mg/dL (≥102 μmol/L) (29).

Statistical analysis

The Shapiro–Wilk test was conducted to assess normality. Continuous variables, shown as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, were tested using Student’s t-test for normal distribution or Mann–Whitney test for non-normal distribution. Categorical variables, presented as numbers (percentage), were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test.

Propensity score matching (PSM) was used to adjust for the effects of confounding factors. Propensity scores for each patient were calculated based on the following variables: demographics (age and gender), clinical scores (SOFA score and APACHE II score), interventions (MV, vasopressor, and RRT), septic shock, comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, heart failure, connective tissue diseases, chronic kidney diseases, and malignancy), laboratory parameters (PLT, international normalized ratio, WBC, Hb, SCr, and TBIL), site of infection, pathogens of infection, and organ failure status. In this study, 1:2 nearest neighbor matching with a caliper of 0.2 was used. In both unmatched and matched cohorts, univariate logistic regression models were used to assess the associations between WLCT and in-hospital mortality, respiratory failure, circulatory failure, renal failure, hepatic failure, coagulation failure, ARDS, and disseminated intravascular coagulation, with results presented as odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals. Linear regression models were used to assess the associations between WLCT and LOS hospital, LOS ICU, duration of MV, duration of vasopressor therapy, and duration of RRT, with results presented as β values and 95% confidence intervals. To ensure the robustness of results, we further analyzed the impact of WLCT on in-hospital mortality using multivariable regression analysis adjusting for confounding variables (age, SOFA score, APACHE II score, MV use within 24 hours, vasopressor use within 24 hours, septic shock, malignancy, connective tissue diseases, TBIL, WBC, number of organ failures, and infection site), inverse probability of treatment weighting based on propensity scores, and covariate adjustment using propensity scores. Generalized additive mixed models were used to analyze the associations between changes in SOFA scores, vital signs, laboratory parameters, and treatment modalities.

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 3.4.3, R Foundation for Statistical Computing) and EmpowerStats software version 4.1 (http://www.empowerstats.com/cn/, X&Y solutions, Inc., Boston, MA). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Selection of participants

A total of 219 septic patients with anemia meeting the inclusion criteria were included. The study excluded two patients younger than 18 years, three patients with nonfirst-time hospitalization records, two patients hospitalized for less than 24 hours, two pregnant or lactating patients, and five patients with end-stage diseases. Finally, 205 adult septic patients with anemia were included. Patients were divided into the WLCT group (49 cases) and the conventional group (156 cases) based on whether they received WLCT therapy after admission. After 1:2 PSM, the matched cohort ultimately included 38 patients in the WLCT group and 76 patients in the conventional group (Figure 1). Supplementary Table 1, https://links.lww.com/SHK/C823, described the DAT results in 205 septic patients in this cohort, with 47 patients not tested during hospitalization. The overall positive rate of DAT was 84.2% (133/158).

Association between WLCT and primary and secondary outcomes

In the unmatched cohort, the in-hospital mortality rate was significantly lower in the WLCT group than in the conventional group. Univariate logistic regression analysis demonstrated a significant association between WLCT and reduced risk of in-hospital mortality. Regarding secondary outcomes, the WLCT group had a shorter duration of vasopressor therapy and a lower proportion of patients developing hepatic failure compared with the conventional group (detailed data were presented in Table 2). In the matched cohort, WLCT remained significantly associated with reduced risk of in-hospital mortality and lower risk of hepatic failure, consistent with the trend observed in the unmatched cohort.

Early changes of SOFA scores and vital signs in PSM cohorts

Figure 3, A and Table 3 showed the changes in SOFA scores over time. There was no significant difference in SOFA scores between the two groups on day 0. The conventional group showed a nonsignificant decreasing trend in SOFA scores. Compared with the conventional group, the WLCT group exhibited an additional daily decrease of 0.30 in SOFA scores.

DISCUSSION

Sepsis is a complex and life-threatening condition characterized by a dysregulated host response to infection, leading to multiorgan dysfunction and significantly elevated mortality rates globally. The pathophysiology of sepsis involves a cascade of inflammatory responses triggered by infection, which can result in severe complications such as ARDS, acute kidney injury, and septic shock (30). The burden of sepsis extends beyond individual patient outcomes, imposing substantial economic strain on healthcare systems worldwide owing to prolonged hospital stays and intensive care requirements (31). In particular, septic patients often present with coexisting conditions such as anemia, which complicates clinical management and may adversely affect prognosis (12). Our previous study (32) indicated that plasmapheresis was associated with reduced 28-day, in-hospital, and 1-year mortality in septic patients with multiple organ failure. Reports on exchange transfusion in patients with sepsis-associated anemia remain scarce. Therefore, we explored the efficacy of WLCT, a novel exchange transfusion technique, in septic patients with anemia and found that WLCT was significantly associated with lower in-hospital mortality rates, as well as a reduced incidence of hepatic failure and a shorter duration of vasopressor therapy. Subgroup analyses further suggest that the favorable association between WLCT therapy and reduced in-hospital mortality is consistent across different transfusion volume subgroups (Supplementary Table 3, https://links.lww.com/SHK/C823). These findings suggest potential benefits of WLCT and imply that it may serve as a critical adjunctive treatment strategy in sepsis management. Previous studies have primarily focused on the effectiveness of WBE in various critical conditions but have not specifically explored its implications for sepsis-related complications, particularly in the context of anemia (19). This novel approach aligns with previous research highlighting the potential of blood-based therapies in improving patient outcomes in critical care settings.

We preliminarily investigated the mechanism by which WLCT improves outcomes in septic patients with anemia. After WLCT procedures (specific details of WLCT were shown in Supplementary Table 4, https://links.lww.com/SHK/C823), the levels of SOFA scores, APTT, and bilirubin were decreased, whereas the levels of MAP, PO2, RBC, and PLG were increased. The SOFA score served as an important tool for assessing organ dysfunction severity and prognosis in septic patients. The reduction in SOFA score demonstrated amelioration of sepsis-induced organ dysfunction. The decrease in APTT and bilirubin levels suggested improvements in coagulation dysfunction and liver metabolic function, respectively. These changes might represent one mechanism underlying the association between WLCT and reduced risks of in-hospital mortality and hepatic failure. An elevation in MAP might reflect restored vascular tone and improved effective circulating volume, leading to stabilized perfusion of vital organs. A rise in PO2 might indicate enhanced oxygenation and increased tissue oxygen delivery, although these parameters represent only partial measures of respiratory function. An increase in RBC levels suggested amelioration of anemic conditions and improved oxygen-carrying capacity. Elevated PLG might reflect restored fibrinolytic function, improved microcirculation, and reestablished coagulation homeostasis. Taken together, these modulations in MAP, PO2, RBC, and PLG might reflect partial but meaningful improvements across circulatory, respiratory, hematopoietic, and coagulation systems, and might indicate alleviation of tissue hypoxia and metabolic disturbances. These findings provide multidimensional biological evidence supporting the potential therapeutic benefits of WLCT. Furthermore, we found that WLCT was significantly associated with reduced IL-6, CRP, and ESR levels in patients compared with both pre-WLCT and conventional group. The literature indicated that dynamic changes in IL-6 were associated with prognosis in sepsis. Patients with increased IL-6 levels within 72 hours were associated with higher mortality rates, whereas those with decreased IL-6 levels tended to have better clinical outcomes (33). A study revealed that among sepsis survivors, CRP levels decreased significantly over the course of treatment, whereas nonsurvivors maintained persistently elevated levels, suggesting an association between CRP dynamics and clinical outcomes (34). In sepsis management, ESR was commonly utilized as an inflammatory biomarker to assist in diagnosis (35). The amelioration of inflammatory parameters consistently correlated with enhanced organ function and improved survival outcomes.

The prognosis of sepsis was closely associated with the status of the immune system. Alterations in immune cells—including CD3+ T lymphocytes, CD3+CD4+ T helper cells, and CD19+ B lymphocytes—during sepsis not only reflected immunological status but might also impact patient outcomes through multiple mechanisms. CD19+ B lymphocytes and their subsets (such as regulatory B cells) demonstrated significant mechanistic roles in sepsis. A decreased absolute count of CD19+ B lymphocytes served as an indicator of poor prognosis. The specific mechanisms involved B cell–mediated humoral immune dysregulation: deficiency of CD19+CD24hiCD38hi regulatory B cells (Bregs) was associated with reduced secretion of anti-inflammatory factors such as IL-10, which in turn was associated with excessive proinflammatory responses. These responses were positively correlated with elevated lactate levels and organ failure scores. This defect might further impair the regulation of T cells, promote immunosuppression, and be associated with an increased mortality risk (36). This study demonstrated that the WLCT group exhibited a significantly higher CD19+ B lymphocytes % after treatment compared with the conventional group. The elevated CD19+ B lymphocytes % might be associated with improved immune function in septic patients, which in turn might be associated with better clinical outcomes. The decreased levels of CD3+ T lymphocytes and CD4+ T lymphocytes in septic patients were closely associated with poor prognosis (37,38), which appeared inconsistent with the findings of our study. This discrepancy might be attributed to the operational procedures of WLCT. During WLCT treatment, the removal of immune cells might lead to transient reductions in CD3+ T lymphocytes % and CD3+CD4+ T lymphocytes % levels post-treatment.

As a novel blood replacement technique, WLCT demonstrated good safety profiles, with mild adverse reactions and no serious adverse events (Supplementary Table 5, https://links.lww.com/SHK/C823). Among the 49 patients in the WLCT group, 6.12% (n = 3) experienced adverse reactions. Citrate anticoagulant-induced hypocalcemia, general allergic symptoms, and hypovolemic reactions were relatively common, including itching, rash, tremors, palpitations; all of which were effectively relieved with symptomatic treatment. No severe adverse events such as bleeding, circulatory overload, shock, or hemolysis were observed during or after treatment. There were no cases of vascular access-related infections, and no worsening of pre-existing infections owing to WLCT. No adverse reactions led to patient death.

The limitations of this study primarily stemmed from its retrospective design, which inherently introduced potential biases and confounding factors that might affect the validity of the findings. Additionally, although PSM was used to mitigate selection bias, the relatively small sample size might limit the statistical precision and generalizability of the conclusions. Furthermore, laboratory measurements of the removed effluent (including bacterial load, endotoxin, and cytokines) were not performed in this retrospective study, which may limit the understanding of the underlying mechanism of WLCT. Moreover, the absence of immune cell data during the patient recovery phase precluded an assessment of CD3+ T lymphocytes and CD4+ T lymphocytes following WLCT discontinuation, as well as whether reduced levels of CD3+ T lymphocytes and CD3+CD4+ T lymphocytes would predispose patients to secondary infections. Therefore, the results of this study should be interpreted as preliminary and hypothesis-generating, and further large-scale, multicenter randomized controlled trials are warranted to validate the observed associations and to clarify the comprehensive effects of WLCT in septic patients with anemia.

In conclusion, this study provided compelling evidence that WLCT was associated with a significantly lower risk of in-hospital mortality. The findings suggested that WLCT might serve as a beneficial adjunctive treatment strategy in this patient population. By addressing key outcomes such as organ failure and the duration of vasopressor therapy, the results highlighted the potential for integrating WLCT into existing sepsis management protocols. Future research was warranted to further elucidate the mechanisms underlying these benefits and to confirm these findings in larger, multicenter trials, ultimately aiming to improve clinical outcomes for septic patients and its associated complications.
