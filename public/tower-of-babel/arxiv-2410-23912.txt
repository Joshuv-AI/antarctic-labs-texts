RL-STaR: Theoretical Analysis of Reinforcement Learning Frameworks for Self-Taught Reasoner
Fu-Chieh Chang fc-mark.chang@mediatek.com, Yu-Ting Lee r14942088@ntu.edu.tw, Hui-Ying Shih hys114061583@gapp.nthu.edu.tw, Yi Hsuan Tseng r12227115@ntu.edu.tw, Pei-Yuan Wu peiyuanwu@ntu.edu.tw

Abstract
The reasoning abilities of large language models (LLMs) have improved with chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting, allowing models to solve complex tasks stepwise. However, training CoT capabilities requires detailed reasoning data, which is often scarce. The self-taught reasoner (STaR) framework addresses this by using reinforcement learning to automatically generate reasoning steps, reducing reliance on human-labeled data. Although STaR and its variants have demonstrated empirical success, a theoretical foundation explaining these improvements is lacking. This work provides a theoretical framework for understanding the effectiveness of reinforcement learning on CoT reasoning and STaR. Our contributions are: (1) criteria for the quality of pre-trained models necessary to initiate effective reasoning improvement; (2) an analysis of policy improvement, showing why LLM reasoning improves iteratively with STaR; (3) conditions for convergence to an optimal reasoning policy; and (4) an examination of STaR’s robustness, explaining how it can improve reasoning even when incorporating occasional incorrect steps. We also run RL-STaR on GPT-2, Qwen2.5-0.5B and Phi-3-mini, and the measured return curves follow the ones the analysis predicts. This framework bridges empirical findings with theoretical insights, advancing reinforcement learning approaches for reasoning in LLMs.



1
Introduction

With the advancement of large language models (LLMs), their reasoning capabilities have become crucial to their success. This progress is mainly attributed to chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting
(
Wei et al., 2022
)
, which allows LLMs to go beyond pattern matching and handle more complex reasoning problems by providing step-by-step guidance. GPT4-o1
(
OpenAI, 2024
)
exemplifies this success, achieving high scores on various mathematical and programming benchmarks.
However, to train models with CoT capabilities, training data must include detailed reasoning steps
(
Malach, 2024
;
Prystawski et al., 2024
;
Xiao and Liu, 2024
)
, which are often absent. To address this challenge, the self-taught reasoner (STaR) approach
(
Zelikman et al., 2022
)
was proposed, leveraging reinforcement learning to automatically discover reasoning steps. Numerous improvements to STaR have since been introduced
(
Hosseini et al., 2024
;
Zelikman et al., 2024
;
Lin et al., 2025
;
Andukuri et al., 2024
;
Xiang et al., 2025
)
, demonstrating empirically that LLMs can effectively learn reasoning steps via reinforcement learning without human intervention.
Although some theoretical research exists on CoT techniques (e.g.,
(
Prystawski et al., 2024
;
Malach, 2024
;
Feng et al., 2024
;
Xiao and Liu, 2024
;
Hu et al., 2024
)
), these studies are primarily focused on supervised and auto-regressive learning settings that require detailed reasoning steps included in training data. They do not show how reinforcement learning techniques can enhance reasoning steps. Furthermore, while there are existing reinforcement learning frameworks for theoretical analysis (e.g.,
(
Jin et al., 2018
;
Ayoub et al., 2020
;
Jin et al., 2021
;
Jin et al., 2020
;
Bhandari and Russo, 2021
;
Chen et al., 2022
;
Yeh et al., 2023
;
Lai et al., 2025
)
), none are designed to analyze the self-improvement of LLMs through reinforcement learning. As a result, no theoretical framework explains how LLMs can enhance their reasoning capabilities via reinforcement learning. A detailed literature review is given in Sec.
2
.

1.1
Our Contributions

In this work, we develop a theoretical framework to analyze how the Self-Taught Reasoner (STaR) enhances Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning under a simplified setup such as Markov properties (see Sec.
6.1
). In this context, we address the following questions:

1.

Conditions of Pre-trained Models for STaR:
How competent does the pre-trained LLM need to be to bootstrap the discovery of reasoning steps in the first iteration?

We show that a pre-trained LLM can initiate effective reasoning improvement if one of the following holds when inferencing on the problems in STaR’s training set.

•

The pre-trained LLM performs better than a randomly initialized model at each reasoning step (i.e., its probability of producing the correct step exceeds that of random guessing).

•

The pre-trained LLM is on par with a randomly initialized model at exactly one reasoning step but exceeds it at all other steps.

2.

Policy Improvement:
Can LLMs improve their reasoning capabilities iteratively through STaR?

We demonstrate that if the pre-trained model satisfies the conditions we mentioned previously, within each iteration of the STaR algorithm, LLMs can consistently improve the correctness of their reasoning trajectories.

3.

Convergence to the Optimal Policy:
If an optimal reasoning model exists, can STaR eventually find this optimal reasoner?

Given sufficient iterations, we prove that if the pre-trained model satisfies the previously mentioned conditions, LLMs can converge to the optimal reasoner, achieving the highest probability of generating correct reasoning trajectories that lead to correct answers.

4.

Existence of Incorrect Reasoning Steps in STaR:
Is it possible for a sub-optimal model to generate incorrect reasoning steps that are included in the next iteration of training, while still arriving at the correct final answer?

We show that even when incorrect reasoning steps are included in the training data for a given iteration, the probability of these incorrect reasoning steps being included in the training data will diminish as the number of STaR iterations increases.

Although our theoretical setup simplifies real-world LLM settings, we also discuss how our theoretical results could be applied to more realistic scenarios. In Sec.
4.5
we explain why the convergence guarantees of policy-gradient methods do not already answer these questions.

2
Related Works

2.1
Approaches to Improve Chain-of-Thought

Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting improves reasoning by encouraging models to generate intermediate steps
(
Wei et al., 2022
)
. However, scaling model size alone does not strengthen causal understanding
Bao et al. (2025)
, motivating research on selecting effective CoT prompts and correcting flawed reasoning traces (e.g.,
Jiang et al. (2024)
). Recent work has explored multiple strategies for enhancing CoT. Retrieval-Q-CoT
(
Zhang et al., 2023
)
selects prompts by grouping questions via cosine similarity, while Process Supervision
(
Lightman et al., 2024
)
trains reward models that stabilize step-by-step reasoning. Other studies focus on discovering optimal instructions
(
Zhou et al., 2023
)
or improving Zero-Shot CoT through automated prompting strategies
(
Wang et al., 2023
)
. Importantly,
Huang et al. (2024)
shows that external feedback is critical for self-correction, highlighting the need for explicit feedback-driven mechanisms. Overall, these approaches illustrate a growing effort to refine CoT prompting, enhance reasoning robustness, and reduce reliance on model scale alone.

2.2
Self-Taught Reasoner (STaR) for Boosting Chain-of-Thought

The Self-Taught Reasoner (STaR) framework
(
Zelikman et al., 2022
)
improves LLM reasoning by using reinforcement learning to iteratively refine self-generated chain-of-thought (CoT) traces. STaR first produces reasoning steps via in-context learning, retains only those leading to correct answers, and continually expands its training set with successful CoTs. Numerous extensions enhance this paradigm: Quiet-STaR
(
Zelikman et al., 2024
)
adds token-level rationales; V-STaR
(
Hosseini et al., 2024
)
trains a verifier using DPO to assess both correct and incorrect solutions; Lean-STaR
(
Lin et al., 2025
)
introduces informal pre-proof reasoning; STaR-GATE
(
Andukuri et al., 2024
)
rewards insightful question generation; and Meta-STaR
(
Xiang et al., 2025
)
incorporates meta-cognitive CoT. Other variants include B-STaR
(
Zeng et al., 2025
)
, which balances exploration and exploitation, and rStar-Math
(
Guan et al., 2025
)
, showing strong reasoning gains even for small models. Despite these advances, the theoretical basis for why STaR enables autonomous reasoning improvement remains largely unexplored.

2.3
Theories of Reinforcement Learning

Reinforcement learning theory aims to explain how algorithms iteratively improve a policy toward optimality. Foundational results on Tabular Q-learning, such as
(
Jin et al., 2018
)
, establish polynomial-time convergence, with extensions to settings involving linear reward and transition structures
(
Jin et al., 2020
;
Yang and Wang, 2019
;
He et al., 2021
)
and kernel-based function approximation
(
Yang et al., 2020
;
Yeh et al., 2023
)
. Additional analyses characterize convergence for policy gradient methods
(
Phansalkar and Thathachar, 1995
;
Bhandari and Russo, 2021
;
Bhandari and Russo, 2024
)
, human-in-the-loop RL
(
Chen et al., 2022
;
Kong and Yang, 2022
)
, model-based RL
(
Osband and Van Roy, 2014
;
Ayoub et al., 2020
)
, and offline RL
(
Hu et al., 2023
;
Jin et al., 2021
;
Lai et al., 2025
)
. Collectively, these works provide deep insight into classical RL algorithms and their sample-efficiency guarantees. However, they do not directly address the distinct challenges involved in improving large language model reasoning—where policies correspond to multi-step generative processes rather than state-action mappings. This gap motivates the need for new theoretical frameworks tailored to RL-driven enhancement of chain-of-thought reasoning in LLMs.

2.4
Theories of Chain-of-Thought

Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting
(
Wei et al., 2022
)
enables LLMs to solve complex tasks by decomposing solutions into stepwise reasoning. Several theoretical works attempt to explain its effectiveness.
Prystawski et al. (2024)
models CoT as Bayesian networks linking questions, answers, and intermediate steps, while
Xiao and Liu (2024)
introduces length generalization, showing that models can extend simple patterns to harder cases. In a PAC auto-regressive framework,
Malach (2024)
proves that CoT allows learning linear threshold circuits. CoT also increases computational reach:
Feng et al. (2024)
demonstrates transformer ability to emulate dynamic programming;
Li et al. (2024)
and
Kim and Suzuki (2025)
show enhanced expressiveness via sequential intermediate states. Additional analyses address sample complexity
(
Hu et al., 2024
;
Li et al., 2025
)
, OOD generalization
(
Yao et al., 2025
)
, and the role of positional embeddings
(
Wang et al., 2025
)
. While these studies form the theoretical basis of CoT, they do not explain why reinforcement learning further improves CoT, nor how to reduce reliance on extensive reasoning-step supervision.

3
Theoretical Framework

3.1
Problem Formulation

In our problem formulation, we consider a chain of thought (CoT) reasoning process composed of
N
steps where
N>1
. Let
s_{0}
denote the initial input string, and
s_{n}
represent the resulting string after the
n
-th CoT step, where
1\leq n\leq N
. We assume that the chain-of-thought steps satisfy the Markov property: each string
s_{n}
contains sufficient information to derive the next string
s_{n+1}
, without relying on information from any of the preceding strings
s_{i}
where
0\leq i<n
. For instance, in the addition problem
1+2+3+4
, the chain-of-thought steps can be expressed as:

\displaystyle s_{0}=\texttt{1+2+3+4}\Rightarrow s_{1}=\texttt{3+3+4}~\Rightarrow s_{2}=\texttt{6+4}\Rightarrow s_{3}=\texttt{10}.

In this example, obtaining
s_{2}=\texttt{6+4}
requires only the information in
s_{1}=\texttt{3+3+4}
and does not depend on
s_{0}=\texttt{1+2+3+4}
.
Under this Markov assumption, the chain-of-thought (CoT) process can naturally be cast as a Reinforcement Learning (RL) problem, which can be described by a Markov decision process (MDP). Formally, let
\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},N,r,\mathcal{P})
represent the MDP, where:

•

\mathcal{S}
is the space of all possible initial inputs, CoT steps, and final answers, denoted
\{s_{i}\mid 0\leq i\leq N\}
. In this formulation,
s_{0}
is the CoT input,
s_{1},\ldots,s_{N-1}
are the subsequent reasoning steps, and
s_{N}
is the final answer.

•

\mathcal{A}
is the action space. Because each action corresponds uniquely to selecting the next state, we identify
\mathcal{A}
with
\mathcal{S}
. That is, choosing
a
is equivalent to setting
s_{n+1}
as the unique next step associated with
a
.

•

\mathcal{P}
is the transition function. Given that an action
a
uniquely determines the next state
s_{n+1}
, this transition is deterministic:

\mathcal{P}\bigl(S_{n+1}=s_{n+1}\big|A=s_{n+1},S_{n}=s_{n}\bigr)=1.

•

r(s_{0},s_{N})
is the reward function, yielding a nonzero reward solely at the final step if
s_{N}
matches the correct answer. Concretely, consider a dataset
\mathcal{D}
composed of question-answer pairs. For each instance
(s_{0},s_{N}^{\star})\in\mathcal{D}
, where
s_{0}
represents the question and
s_{N}^{\star}
the correct final answer, define

r(s_{0},s_{N})=\begin{cases}1,~\text{if $s_{N}=s_{N}^{\star}$ and $(s_{0},s_{N}^{\star})\in\mathcal{D}$},\\
0,~\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Thus, the agent earns a reward of 1 exclusively when it terminates on
s_{N}^{\star}
, and 0 otherwise. Note that we fixed the number of CoT steps to be
N
; intermediate states
s_{n}
(for
1\leq n<N
) do not produce any positive reward.

Given the RL formulation above, a policy
\pi(A\mid S_{n}=s_{n})
specifies how the next action
A=s_{n+1}
is selected based on the current state
S_{n}=s_{n}
. Although the transition
\mathcal{P}\bigl(S_{n+1}\mid A=s_{n+1},S_{n}=s_{n}\bigr)
is deterministic (that is, once
A=s_{n+1}
is chosen,
S_{n+1}=s_{n+1}
is uniquely determined), the policy
\pi(A\mid S_{n}=s_{n})
itself can be stochastic if there is uncertainty about which next step to choose given
S_{n}=s_{n}
.
To streamline notations, we define a stochastic transition
P(S_{n+1}\mid S_{n})
by combining the stochastic policy
\pi(A\mid S_{n})
with the deterministic transition
\mathcal{P}(S_{n+1}\mid A,S_{n})
:

P\bigl(S_{n+1}\mid S_{n}\bigr)\;=\;\mathcal{P}\bigl(S_{n+1}\mid\pi(A\mid S_{n}),S_{n}\bigr).

In this setup, the LLM serves as the transition function
P(S_{n+1}\mid S_{n})
, producing the subsequent CoT step
s_{n+1}
based on the current CoT step
s_{n}
. After the final step
s_{N}
is reached, the reward function
r(s_{0},s_{N})
measures correctness by comparing
s_{N}
to the ground truth answer
s_{N}^{\star}
.

To guide LLMs toward selecting a final state
s_{N}
that maximizes the reward upon completing CoT, we use a modified version of STaR
(
Zelikman et al., 2022
)
, termed RL-STaR, as outlined in Algorithm
1
.
This algorithm takes a training dataset
\mathcal{D}
and a pre-trained LLM as transition
P_{0}
as input, and outputs a trained LLM as transition
P_{T}
, where
\mathcal{D}
consists of
K
instances.
In each iteration
t
, we repeat the following procedure
L
times: we uniformly sample a pair
\bigl(s_{0}^{(\ell)},s_{N}^{\star(\ell)}\bigr)
from
\mathcal{D}
and generate a trajectory
\tau^{(\ell)}
by sequentially sampling states from the current transition
P_{t-1}
. Specifically, starting from
s_{0}^{(\ell)}
, we iteratively draw
s_{n}^{(\ell)}\sim P_{t-1}\bigl(S_{n}\!\mid\!S_{n-1}=s_{n-1}^{(\ell)}\bigr)
for
n=1,\dots,N
. If the final state
s_{N}^{(\ell)}
matches the ground truth
s_{N}^{\star(\ell)}
, we add the entire trajectory
\tau^{(\ell)}
to
\mathcal{D}_{t}
. After completing these
L
samplings, we update
P_{t-1}
to
P_{t}
by retraining on
\mathcal{D}_{t}
. In particular,
\operatorname{Train}(P_{t-1},\mathcal{D}_{t})
adjusts the transition probabilities
P_{t-1}(S_{n}=s_{n}\!\mid\!S_{n-1}=s_{n-1})
to better align with the transitions observed in the successful trajectories within
\mathcal{D}_{t}
. After
T
iterations, this procedure outputs the final transition model
P_{T}
.
In Sec.
4
, we show that RL-STaR aims to maximize the total expected return
J(P_{t})
, defined by

\displaystyle J(P_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{(s_{0},s_{N}^{\star})\sim\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{E}_{(s_{1},\cdots,s_{N})\sim P_{t}(\tau|S_{0}=s_{0})}r(s_{0},s_{N}),

\displaystyle\text{where}~P_{t}(\tau|S_{0}=s_{0})=P_{t}(S_{1}=s_{1}|S_{0}=s_{0})\times(\Pi_{n=2}^{N}P_{t}(S_{n}=s_{n}|S_{n-1}=s_{n-1})).

3.2
Settings of Our Theoretical Analysis

Markov Assumption of Language Models’ Input

For simplicity in analyzing the RL-STaR algorithm, we impose the Markov assumption by allowing the LLM to accept only the current step
s_{n-1}
as input, rather than the complete history
\bigl(s_{0},s_{1},\dots,s_{n-2}\bigr)
. This contrasts with standard CoT methods, which include every previous token in the context. Nevertheless, the Markov property remains essential for CoT generalization. Several studies (e.g.,
(
Yao et al., 2025
;
Teng et al., 2025
;
Wang et al., 2025
;
Li et al., 2025
;
Xiao and Liu, 2024
;
Prystawski et al., 2024
)
) show that CoT succeeds by breaking complex tasks into simpler, reusable subtasks whose outcomes depend only on a limited recent context, thus preserving Markovian structure.
The details are discussed in Sec.
6.1
.

Simplification of STaR Algorithm

For simplicity in our theoretical analysis, we exclude the
rationalization
from STaR—that is, we do not correct an LLM’s incorrect outputs using hints derived from the final answer. As noted in
(
Zelikman et al., 2022
)
, omitting rationalization may reduce STaR’s convergence rate. However, based on their result, rationalization is not a necessary condition for policy improvement.
Hence, we accept this trade-off here since our focus lies on preliminary theoretical examination rather than achieving state-of-the-art performance on reasoning benchmarks.

Assumption of the Ground-Truth Reasoner
\bar{\pi}

We assume that a ground-truth transition
\bar{P}(S_{n+1}|S_{n})
exists, which produces the correct sequence
s_{1},\ldots,s_{N-1}
leading to
s_{N}=s_{N}^{\star}
given
s_{0}
for every instance
(s_{0},s_{N}^{\star})
in
\mathcal{D}
. This property enables us to apply the analysis of RL-STaR which approximates
\bar{P}(S_{n+1}|S_{n})
with an estimated transition
P_{T}(s_{n+1}|S_{n})
. It is clear that if an estimated transition
P_{T}
can perfectly match the ground-truth reasoning step transitions
\bar{P}
, it would be the optimal estimated transition
P^{\star}
which maximizes
J(P^{\star})
,
namely,

\displaystyle P^{\star}(S_{n+1}=s_{n+1}|S_{n}=s_{n})=\bar{P}(S_{n+1}=s_{n+1}|S_{n}=s_{n}),

\displaystyle\text{\quad for all $(s_{n+1},s_{n})\in\operatorname{support}{(S_{n+1})}\times\operatorname{support}{(S_{n})}$,\, where }n\in\{0,1,\dots,N-1\}.

4.2
The Family
P_{n}[\delta]
and Standing Assumptions

Throughout Sec.
4
, a CoT step
n
has
M
states. We write
\bar{P}_{n}
for the ground-truth transition at that step, which puts all of its mass on the correct next state, and
P_{u,n}
for the uniform transition, which puts mass
\frac{1}{M}
on every state.

Definition 4.1

(
\delta
-accurate transition)
.

For
\delta\in[0,1)
, let

P_{n}[\delta]\;:=\;\delta\,\bar{P}_{n}+(1-\delta)\,P_{u,n},

so that
P_{n}[\delta]
assigns probability
\frac{1+(M-1)\delta}{M}
to the correct next step and
\frac{1-\delta}{M}
to each of the
M-1
incorrect ones. Hence
P_{n}[0]=P_{u,n}
is the uniform model, and
P_{n}[\delta]
approaches
\bar{P}_{n}
as
\delta\rightarrow 1
, so
\delta
measures how far the model has moved from random guessing toward the ground-truth reasoner.

The results in this section rest on two assumptions about RL-STaR.

Assumption 4.2

(Training set)
.

\mathcal{D}=\{(s_{0,m},s_{N,m})\mid m\in[M]\}
, that is, the training set holds one question–answer pair per ground-truth trajectory.

Assumption 4.3

(Exact fitting)
.

\operatorname{Train}(P_{t-1},\mathcal{D}_{t})
returns the transition that matches the conditional distribution of
\mathcal{D}_{t}
exactly. That is, for every step
n
,
P_{t,n}(S_{n}\mid S_{n-1})
equals the conditional probability of observing
(s_{n-1,m},s_{n,m})
and
(s_{n-1,m},s_{n,m^{\prime}\neq m})
among the trajectories
\tau\sim\mathcal{D}_{t}
.

4.3
A Toy Example

We first illustrate our theoretical results with a toy example. In this scenario, we consider a CoT process with two reasoning steps (i.e.,
N=2
), and each step has two possible states (i.e.,
M=2
). Here,
S_{0}
is a random variable representing the initial state,
S_{1}
the intermediate state, and
S_{2}
the final state. We assume their supports are
\operatorname{support}(S_{0})=\{s_{0,1},s_{0,2}\}
,
\operatorname{support}(S_{1})=\{s_{1,1},s_{1,2}\}
, and
\operatorname{support}(S_{2})=\{s_{2,1},s_{2,2}\}
. The ground-truth reasoning trajectories are defined as
\tau_{0}^{\star}=(s_{0,1},s_{1,1},s_{2,1})
and
\tau_{1}^{\star}=(s_{0,2},s_{1,2},s_{2,2})
, giving the ground-truth transition
\bar{P}_{n}(S_{n}|S_{n-1})
at step
1\leq n\leq 2
as

\bar{P}_{n}(S_{n}|S_{n-1})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }(S_{n},S_{n-1})=(s_{n,m},s_{n-1,m})\text{ for all }n,m\in[2],\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

We define
P_{u,n}
as a uniform distribution such that

4.4
Main Theorem

After introducing a toy example, we now present our main theorem, which can be applied to an arbitrary number of reasoning steps
N
and an arbitrary number of states
M
for each step. In this scenario,
S_{0}
is a random variable that represents the initial state,
S_{1},\dots,S_{N-1}
represent the intermediate states of steps
1
to
N-1
respectively, and
S_{N}
represents the final state. Each step
n\in[N]
contains
M
possible states, so
\operatorname{support}(S_{n})=\{s_{n,1},s_{n,2},\dots,s_{n,M}\}
.
Assuming there are
M
ground-truth reasoning trajectories, we denote these trajectories as
\{\tau_{m}|m\in[M]\}
where each trajectory
\tau_{m}
has the form of
(s_{0,m},s_{1,m},\dots,s_{N,m})
. The transition function for step
n
, denoted as
\bar{P}_{n}(S_{n}|S_{n-1})
, is defined as

\bar{P}_{n}(S_{n}|S_{n-1})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $(S_{n},S_{n-1})=(s_{n,m},s_{n-1,m})$}\text{ for all $m\in[M]$},\\
0&\text{if $(S_{n},S_{n-1})=(s_{n,m^{\prime}\neq m},s_{n-1,m})$ }\text{ for all $m,m^{\prime}\in[M]$},\end{cases}

and the uniform transition at step
n
, denoted as
P_{u,n}(S_{n}|S_{n-1})
, is defined as

P_{u,n}(S_{n}|S_{n-1})=\frac{1}{M}\quad\text{for all }(S_{n},S_{n-1}).

We now address the questions listed in Sec.
1.1
. In response to the first question, Theorem
4.9
indicates that a condition for the pre-trained models to bootstrap the RL-STaR algorithm is that the pre-trained LLM outperforms a randomly initialized model at every reasoning step.

Theorem 4.9

(
Sufficient Conditions of Pre-trained Models
)
.

Given the scenario defined in the previous paragraph, let Assumptions
4.2
and
4.3
hold, and assume that the pre-trained LLM estimates
P_{0,n}=P_{n}[\delta_{0,n}]
at every CoT step
n\in[N]
, for some
0\leq\delta_{0,n}<1
.
If, at the start of RL-STaR iteration
t
, there exist
0\leq\delta_{t-1,n}<1
with
P_{t-1,n}=P_{n}[\delta_{t-1,n}]
for every CoT step
n\in[N]
, then
P_{t,n}=P_{n}[\delta_{t,n}]
for all
n\in[N]
, where

\delta_{t,n}=\frac{(M-2)\prod_{k=1}^{N}\delta_{t-1,k}+\prod_{k\neq n}\delta_{t-1,k}+\delta_{t-1,n}}{(M-1)\prod_{k=1}^{N}\delta_{t-1,k}+1}.

Besides, based on the values of
\delta_{0,n}
, we have the following cases:

(a)

If
0<\delta_{0,n}<1
for each

, then for all

\delta_{t-1,n}<\delta_{t,n}<1.

(b)

\delta_{1,n}=\prod_{n^{\prime}\neq n}\delta_{0,n^{\prime}}>0\quad\text{and}\quad\delta_{1,n^{\prime}}=\delta_{0,n^{\prime}}>0,

\delta_{t-1,n}<\delta_{t,n}<1.\quad\text{for all $n\in[N]$}.

such that

\delta_{t,n}=\delta_{t-1,n}\quad\text{for all $n\in[N]$}.

.

The above theorem establishes three key points:

(a)

If

for all

is strictly increasing in

(b)

There can be at most one step

4.5
Comparison with the Convergence Analysis of Policy-Gradient Methods

At first glance, STaR
(
Zelikman et al., 2022
)
might appear to be an offline variant of policy-gradient methods in reinforcement learning, suggesting that classical convergence guarantees
(
Phansalkar and Thathachar, 1995
;
Bhandari and Russo, 2021
;
Bhandari and Russo, 2024
)
could be directly applied, rendering STaR’s convergence analysis trivial.
However,
to provide a clear comparison between STaR and RL-style policy-gradient methods, we first revisit the
distinguishing characteristics of STaR’s convergence:

1.

STaR exploits the inductive biases of a pretrained LLM to bootstrap improved reasoning traces.

2.

The reward signal is sparse, depending solely on the final correctness of the answer.

3.

Iterative self-training enables progressive refinement of intermediate reasoning steps.

Although both STaR and policy-gradient methods can operate under similar settings—such as receiving a reward only at the final step, or being initialized from a pre-trained model—their training dynamics differ fundamentally. Besides, from a theoretical perspective, the convergence guarantees of these policy-gradient algorithms focus on the maximization of the expected return,
\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}[v_{t}]
. These guarantees ensure that the parameter
\theta
converges to a local (or global) optimum of the expected return, but do not provide assurances about the improvement of intermediate reasoning steps.
This distinction creates different learning dynamics—STaR may gradually suppress incorrect intermediate reasoning steps if the base model is biased toward correct ones, even without stepwise rewards. Our analysis formalizes this, showing how, under specific conditions, STaR can refine reasoning paths through this iterative process.

6
Limitations and Possible Extensions

While our theorem provides useful theoretical guarantees, it also has limitations when applied to real-world LLM settings. These limitations include the
Markov assumption of state transitions
, the
uniformity of
\delta_{t,n}
, and the
fixed state count
M
. Below, we discuss these restrictions and outline how our framework could be extended to better align with practical LLM behavior.

6.1
Markov Assumption of State Transitions

We simplify our analysis by modeling the reasoning process as Markovian, even though this assumption may not strictly hold for real-world LLMs, whose context windows can span the entire trajectory and thus violate Markov independence. In this section, we first show that the Markov assumption is essential for filtering out incorrect reasoning steps, and we discuss how practical LLMs can approximate this behavior.

Toy Example without Markov Assumption

Suppose the pre-trained transition at step
2
of the toy example in Sec.
4.3
reads both
S_{1}
and
S_{0}
instead of
S_{1}
alone. Working through one iteration of Algorithm
1
(Appendix
A.3
), the transition
P_{1,2}
learned in the first iteration is already deterministic, so
\mathcal{D}_{T}=\mathcal{D}_{T-1}=\cdots=\mathcal{D}_{1}
. Trajectories of the form
\tau=(s_{0,m},s_{1,m^{\prime}\neq m},s_{2,m})
, which contain an incorrect reasoning step, therefore stay in the dataset at every iteration and RL-STaR cannot remove them.

How Real-world LLMs Approximate Markov Property

Although real-world Chain-of-Thought trajectories include the entire context
(S_{0},S_{1},\dots,S_{n})
, predicting
S_{n+1}
typically relies only on a few recent steps. Several studies (e.g.,
(
Yao et al., 2025
;
Teng et al., 2025
;
Wang et al., 2025
;
Li et al., 2025
;
Xiao and Liu, 2024
;
Prystawski et al., 2024
)
) demonstrate that CoT generalization stems from decomposing complex tasks into simpler, reusable subtasks. Each subtask’s outcome depends on a limited window of prior reasoning steps, so the training data effectively exhibits a Markovian structure. Consequently, when LLMs focus on these local dependencies and ignore unnecessary earlier context, they approximate the Markov property in practice.

6.2
Uniformity of
\delta_{t,n}

We assume that
\delta_{0,n}
is uniform across all states at step
n
, but real pre-trained LLMs rarely satisfy this idealization. Allowing
\delta_{0,n,m,m^{\prime}}
for each transition from
s_{n-1,m}
to
s_{n,m^{\prime}}
to take various values complicates the analysis substantially. However, we will show that it is possible to extend our theorem to non-uniform cases.

Non-Uniform
\delta_{0,n}

In this section, we show that even though our theorem does not directly apply, we can still derive a valid lower bound by comparing the original, non-uniform transition
P_{0,n}
to a worst-case uniform model
\underline{P}_{0,n}
. We begin by defining

\begin{cases}P_{0,n}\bigl(S_{n}=s_{n,m^{\prime}}\mid S_{n-1}=s_{n-1,m}\bigr)=\frac{1-\delta_{0,n,m,m^{\prime}}}{M}\text{for all $m^{\prime}\neq m$, and $m,m^{\prime}\in[M]$,}\\
P_{0,n}\bigl(S_{n}=s_{n,m}\mid S_{n-1}=s_{n-1,m}\bigr)=\frac{1+\sum_{m^{\prime}\in[M]~\text{and}~m^{\prime}\neq m}\delta_{0,n,m,m^{\prime}}}{M}.\end{cases}

Let

\underline{\delta}_{0,n}=\min_{m,m^{\prime}\in[M]}\delta_{0,n,m,m^{\prime}}
,
and define a uniform transition

\begin{cases}\underline{P}_{0,n}\bigl(S_{n}=s_{n,m^{\prime}}\mid S_{n-1}=s_{n-1,m}\bigr)=\frac{1-\underline{\delta}_{0,n}}{M}\quad\text{for all $m^{\prime}\neq m$, and $m,m^{\prime}\in[M]$},\\
\underline{P}_{0,n}\bigl(S_{n}=s_{n,m}\mid S_{n-1}=s_{n-1,m}\bigr)=\frac{1+(M-1)\,\underline{\delta}_{0,n}}{M}.\end{cases}

By construction,
\underline{P}_{0,n}
is no more accurate than
P_{0,n}
at selecting the correct step but does satisfy the uniform-
\delta
condition. Applying Theorem
4.10
to
\underline{P}_{0,n}
gives the convergence rate of
\delta_{t,n}
under uniform transitions, which therefore serves as a lower bound on the convergence speed for the original, non-uniform case. We validate this lower bound with the experimental results shown in Sec.
5.4
.

6.3
Fixed Number of States
M

In our framework, each reasoning step is assumed to take one of
M
possible states. While this finite-state assumption is common in theoretical analyses of Chain-of-Thought reasoning (e.g.,
(
Xiao and Liu, 2024
;
Feng et al., 2024
;
Yao et al., 2025
;
Wang et al., 2025
)
), it does not always hold in practice. Large language models can freely generate arbitrary strings rather than being confined to a fixed state set. Even if the model’s outputs are drawn from a bounded vocabulary, the effective number of states available at each step may still vary substantially.

LLMs Generate Arbitrary Strings

In real-world deployments, LLMs can emit any text, not just tokens from a fixed set. For example, in an arithmetic task, the model might output Python code instead of a numerical expression. A plausible solution for this scenario is to introduce an extra state
s^{\prime}
that represents all out-of-set outputs and redefine the state count as
M^{\prime}=M+1
. However, since
s^{\prime}
does not lead to a valid terminal state, it complicates convergence and correctness guarantees. Extending the theoretical framework to accommodate this setting is an interesting direction for future work.

Different Number of States at Each Reasoning Step

Some reasoning tasks may feature a different number of candidate states at each step. Let
M_{n}
denote the number of states at step
n\in[N]
. We consider two scenarios:

•

M_{n}
is nonincreasing:

M_{0}\geq M_{1}\geq\cdots\geq M_{N}.

•

There exist indices
n<n^{\prime}
such that

M_{n}<M_{n^{\prime}}
.

First, we focus on the first scenario. In this setting, we reformulate the problem by creating composite states: each state in the step
n
of ground-truth trajectory as

\displaystyle s_{n,m_{n}}\,\in\,\operatorname{support}(S_{n}),\text{where}~|\operatorname{support}(S_{n})|=M_{n},~\text{and}~m_{n}\in[M_{n}],

is paired with its initial state
s_{0,m_{0}}
to form a new state

\bigl(s_{0,m_{0}},\,s_{n,m_{n}}\bigr).
This construction is illustrated in the diagram below:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{s_{0,m_{0}}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{s_{n,m_{n}}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{s_{0,m_{0}}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{(s_{0,m_{0}},s_{n,m_{n}})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{s_{0,m^{\prime}_{0}}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{s_{0,m^{\prime}_{0}}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{(s_{0,m^{\prime}_{0}},s_{n,m_{n}})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{s_{0,m^{\prime\prime}_{0}}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{s_{n,m^{\prime\prime}_{n}}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{s_{0,m^{\prime\prime}_{0}}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{(s_{0,m^{\prime\prime}_{0}},s_{n,m^{\prime\prime}_{n}})}}

Before concatenation, step 0 has
M_{0}
states and step
n
has
M_{n}
. After concatenation, each composite state
(s_{0,m_{0}},s_{n,m_{n}})
yields exactly
M_{0}
states at step
n
, so we set
M=M_{0}
for all
n
. By applying Theorem
4.10
to this constant-
M
setting, we obtain a convergence rate that we conjecture approximates that of the original varying-
M
problem. In Sec.
5.5
, we empirically compare this approximated rate with the convergence behavior observed in the original task. However, owing to the challenges of analyzing the non-constant
M
case, we present only empirical results and leave the theoretical analysis as future work.

For the second scenario, the ground-truth reasoning paths form a DAG. In this case, one state at step
n
leads to multiple ground-truth transitions with non-zero probability at step
n+1
, which complicates the analysis. However, to illustrate how our framework can handle certain forms of trajectory multiplicity, consider the case where multiple correct intermediate steps exist but ultimately converge to the same final answer. For instance, suppose we are given two input questions,
s_{0,0}=1+2+3
and
s_{0,1}=2+3+4
, each with multiple valid reasoning paths leading to their respective correct answers. In the following, we show that these intermediate trajectories can be merged into single abstract states
s_{1,0}
and
s_{1,1}
, allowing our framework to operate over equivalence classes of states. In this example, we define the ground-truth reasoning steps and final answers as follows:

\displaystyle s_{0,0}=1+2+3\Rightarrow s_{1,0,0}=3+3\Rightarrow s_{2,0}=6

\displaystyle s_{0,0}=1+2+3\Rightarrow s_{1,0,1}=1+5\Rightarrow s_{2,0}=6

\displaystyle s_{0,1}=2+3+4\Rightarrow s_{1,1,0}=5+4\Rightarrow s_{2,1}=9

\displaystyle s_{0,1}=2+3+4\Rightarrow s_{1,1,1}=2+7\Rightarrow s_{2,1}=9

And the transition probability is as follows:

\displaystyle P(S_{1}|S_{0})=\begin{cases}\frac{1+\delta_{2}}{4}&\text{if $S_{1}\in\{s_{1,m,0},s_{1,m,1}\}$ and $S_{0}=s_{0,m}$},\\
\frac{1+\delta_{2}}{4}&\text{if $S_{1}\in\{s_{1,m,0},s_{1,m,1}\}$ and $S_{0}=s_{0,m^{\prime}\neq m}$},\\
\end{cases}

and

\displaystyle P(S_{2}|S_{1})=\begin{cases}\frac{1+\delta_{1}}{2}&\text{if $S_{2}=s_{2,m}$ and $S_{1}\in\{s_{1,m,0},s_{1,m,1}\}$},\\
\frac{1+\delta_{1}}{2}&\text{if $S_{2}=s_{2,m^{\prime}\neq m}$ and $S_{1}\in\{s_{1,m,0},s_{1,m,1}\}$}.\\
\end{cases}

In the above case, we can merge
s_{1,1,0},s_{1,1,1}
into
s_{1,1}
and
s_{1,0,0},s_{1,0,1}
into
s_{1,0}
, and the ground-truth trajectories with merged states become:

\displaystyle s_{0,0}=1+2+3\Rightarrow s_{1,0}=(3+3,1+5)\Rightarrow s_{2,0}=6

\displaystyle s_{0,1}=2+3+4\Rightarrow s_{1,1}=(5+4,2+7)\Rightarrow s_{2,1}=9

And the transition probability becomes:

\displaystyle P(S_{1}|S_{0})=\begin{cases}\frac{1+2\delta_{2}}{2}&\text{if $S_{1}=s_{1,m}$ and $S_{0}=s_{0,m}$}\\
\frac{1+2\delta_{2}}{2}&\text{if $S_{1}=s_{1,m}$ and $S_{0}=s_{0,m^{\prime}\neq m}$}\\
\end{cases}

and

\displaystyle P(S_{2}|S_{1})=\begin{cases}\frac{1+\delta_{1}}{2}&\text{if $S_{2}=s_{2,m}$ and $S_{1}=s_{1,m}$}\\
\frac{1+\delta_{1}}{2}&\text{if $S_{2}=s_{2,m^{\prime}\neq m}$ and $S_{1}=s_{1,m}$}\\
\end{cases}

This setting can still be accommodated within our framework. However, when the ground-truth trajectories differ in their number of reasoning steps
N
—for instance, when certain solution paths omit intermediate steps—our current analysis no longer applies directly. Such scenarios are more complex to handle and are left for future work.

7
Conclusion

We propose RL-STaR, a theoretical framework for analyzing the core properties of the Self-Taught Reasoner (STaR) algorithm. Assuming suitably bootstrapped pre-trained models, we prove that RL-STaR yields policy improvement and converges toward the optimal policy—even when the training data include erroneous reasoning trajectories. Although our analysis abstracts away many complexities of real-world LLM deployments, it offers a rigorous account of why STaR works and lays the groundwork for extending this theory to more realistic settings in future work.

A.2.2
Proof of Theorem
4.5

Proof.

Let
f(x)=\frac{2x}{1+x^{2}}
. Using Taylor’s theorem to expand about
x=1
, one has

f(\delta_{t})-1=f^{\prime}(1)(\delta_{t}-1)+\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(1)}{2!}(\delta_{t}-1)^{2}+\frac{f^{(3)}(\xi_{t})}{3!}(\delta_{t}-1)^{3},

for each
t\geq 0
and some
\xi_{t}\in(\delta_{t},1)
. It’s straightforward to see that
f^{\prime}(1)=0
,
f^{\prime\prime}(1)=-1
and
f^{(3)}(x)=-\frac{12\left(x^{4}-6x^{2}+1\right)}{\left(x^{2}+1\right)^{4}}
. Hence,

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{|\delta_{t+1}-1|}{|\delta_{t}-1|^{2}}=0.5,

which shows quadratic convergence.

To find its closed-form expression, put
x_{t}=\delta_{t}^{-1}
and rewrite our recurrence as
x_{t+1}=({1+x_{t}^{2}})/{2x_{t}}.
Observe that

\displaystyle x_{t+1}+1=\frac{(x_{t}+1)^{2}}{2x_{t}},\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle x_{t+1}-1=\frac{(x_{t}-1)^{2}}{2x_{t}}.

One now sees that

\frac{x_{t}+1}{x_{t}-1}=\left(\frac{x_{0}+1}{x_{0}-1}\right)^{2^{t}}.

Directly solving for
x_{t}
and
\delta_{t}
, we obtain

x_{t}=\frac{\left(\frac{x_{0}+1}{x_{0}-1}\right)^{2^{t}}+1}{\left(\frac{x_{0}+1}{x_{0}-1}\right)^{2^{t}}-1}\quad{\text{and}}\quad\delta_{t}=\frac{\left(\frac{\delta_{0}^{-1}+1}{\delta_{0}^{-1}-1}\right)^{2^{t}}-1}{\left(\frac{\delta_{0}^{-1}+1}{\delta_{0}^{-1}-1}\right)^{2^{t}}+1}.

∎

A.2.6

Proposition A.1
.

Consider a non-homogeneous Markov sequence
S=\left(S_{n}\right)_{n=0}^{N}
with state space
\mathcal{S}=[M]
, initial distribution
\mu
, and transition matrices
P_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
. More specifically, for each
n\in[N]

S_{0}\sim\mu,\quad\left[P_{n}\right]_{i,j}=P\left[S_{n}=j\mid S_{n-1}=i\right].

Denote by
\mathcal{C}=\left\{\left(s_{n}\right)_{n=0}^{N}\in\mathcal{S}^{N+1}:{s_{0}=s_{N}}\right\}
the set of correct trajectories, let
\mu_{i}=\mu(\{i\})
, and

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} {\big[\tilde{P}_{n}\big]_{i,j}}&=P\left[S_{n}=j\mid S\in\mathcal{C},S_{n-1}=i\right]\\
&=\frac{\sum_{m=1}^{M}P\left[S_{0}=S_{N}=m,S_{n-1}=i,S_{n}=j\right]}{\sum_{m=1}^{M}P\left[S_{0}=S_{N}=m,S_{n-1}=i\right]}\\
&=\frac{\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mu_{m}\left[\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}P_{k}\right]_{m,i}\left[P_{n}\right]_{i,j}\left[\prod_{k=n+1}^{N}P_{k}\right]_{j,m}}{\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mu_{m}\left[\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}P_{k}\right]_{m,i}\left[\prod_{k=n}^{N}P_{k}\right]_{i,m}}.\end{aligned}

Suppose
\mu
is uniform and that
\big[P_{n}\big]_{i,j}=\alpha\left(\delta_{n}\right)\mathbb{I}\{i=j\}+\beta\left(\delta_{n}\right)\mathbb{I}\{i\neq

j\}
with
\delta_{n}\in[0,1]
for each
n\in[N]
, where

\alpha(\delta)=\frac{1+(M-1)\delta}{M},\beta(\delta)=\frac{1-\delta}{M}.

Then
\big[\tilde{P}_{n}\big]_{i,j}=\alpha(\tilde{\delta}_{n})\mathbb{I}\{i=j\}+\beta(\tilde{\delta}_{n})\mathbb{I}\{i\neq j\}
satisfies

\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}=\frac{1-\prod_{k\neq n}\delta_{k}}{1+(M-1)\prod_{k=1}^{N}\delta_{k}}\epsilon_{n},

(4)

where
\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}=1-\tilde{\delta}_{n}
and
\epsilon_{n}=1-\delta_{n}
.

Proof.

Let
\left\{u_{m}\right\}_{m=1}^{M}
be an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{M}
where
u_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\mathbf{1}
. Then

\displaystyle P_{n}

\displaystyle=\delta_{n}I_{M}+\tfrac{1-\delta_{n}}{M}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\delta_{n}\sum_{m=1}^{M}u_{m}u_{m}^{\top}+\left(1-\delta_{n}\right)u_{1}u_{1}^{\top}

\displaystyle=u_{1}u_{1}^{\top}+\delta_{n}\sum_{m=2}^{M}u_{m}u_{m}^{\top}=U\Lambda_{n}U^{\top},

where
U=\left[u_{1}\cdots u_{M}\right]
and
\Lambda_{n}=\operatorname{diag}\left(1,\delta_{n},\cdots,\delta_{n}\right)
. Denote
\delta_{k:l}=\prod_{t=k}^{l}\delta_{t}
and
\Lambda_{k:l}=

\prod_{t=k}^{l}\Lambda_{t}=\delta_{k:l}I_{M}+\left(1-\delta_{k:l}\right)e_{1}e_{1}^{\top}
. One has

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &{\big[\tilde{P}_{n}\big]_{i,j}=\left[P_{n}\right]_{i,j}\frac{\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mu_{m}e_{m}^{\top}U\Lambda_{1:n-1}U^{\top}e_{i}e_{j}^{\top}U\Lambda_{n+1:N}U^{\top}e_{m}}{\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mu_{m}e_{m}^{\top}U\Lambda_{1:n-1}U^{\top}e_{i}e_{i}^{\top}U\Lambda_{n:N}U^{\top}e_{m}}}\\
&=\left[P_{n}\right]_{i,j}\frac{\sum_{m=1}^{M}e_{m}^{\top}\left(\delta_{1:n-1}I_{M}+\left(1-\delta_{1:n-1}\right)u_{1}u_{1}^{\top}\right)e_{i}e_{j}^{\top}\left(\delta_{n+1:N}I_{M}+\left(1-\delta_{n+1:N}\right)u_{1}u_{1}^{\top}\right)e_{m}}{\sum_{m=1}^{M}e_{m}^{\top}\left(\delta_{1:n-1}I_{M}+\left(1-\delta_{1:n-1}\right)u_{1}u_{1}^{\top}\right)e_{i}e_{i}^{\top}\left(\delta_{n:N}I_{M}+\left(1-\delta_{n:N}\right)u_{1}u_{1}^{\top}\right)e_{m}}\\
&=\left[P_{n}\right]_{i,j}\frac{\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left(\frac{1-\delta_{1:n-1}}{M}+\delta_{1:n-1}e_{m}^{\top}e_{i}\right)\left(\frac{1-\delta_{n+1:N}}{M}+\delta_{n+1:N}e_{j}^{\top}e_{m}\right)}{\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left(\frac{1-\delta_{1:n-1}}{M}+\delta_{1:n-1}e_{m}^{\top}e_{i}\right)\left(\frac{1-\delta_{n:N}}{M}+\delta_{n:N}e_{i}^{\top}e_{m}\right)}.\end{aligned}

(5)

Note that

\displaystyle\big[\tilde{P}_{n}\big]_{i,j}=\beta\left(\delta_{n}\right)\frac{(M-2)\beta\left(\delta_{1:n-1}\right)\beta\left(\delta_{n+1:N}\right)+\beta\left(\delta_{1:n-1}\right)\alpha\left(\delta_{n+1:N}\right)+\alpha\left(\delta_{1:n-1}\right)\beta\left(\delta_{n+1:N}\right)}{(M-1)\beta\left(\delta_{1:n-1}\right)\beta\left(\delta_{n:N}\right)+\alpha\left(\delta_{1:n-1}\right)\alpha\left(\delta_{n,N}\right)},

are identical for all
i\neq j
. Hence there uniquely exists a
\tilde{\delta}_{n}\in[0,1]
such that
\big[\tilde{P}_{n}\big]_{i,j}=\beta\big(\tilde{\delta}_{n}\big)
and
\big[\tilde{P}_{n}\big]_{i,i}=\alpha\big(\tilde{\delta}_{n}\big)
for all
i\neq j
. Moreover, (cf. (
5
))

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \alpha\big(\tilde{\delta}_{n}\big)&=\alpha\left(\delta_{n}\right)\frac{(M-1)\beta\left(\delta_{1:n-1}\right)\beta\left(\delta_{n+1:N}\right)+\alpha\left(\delta_{1:n-1}\right)\alpha\left(\delta_{n+1,N}\right)}{(M-1)\beta\left(\delta_{1:n-1}\right)\beta\left(\delta_{n:N}\right)+\alpha\left(\delta_{1:n-1}\right)\alpha\left(\delta_{n,N}\right)}\\
&=\alpha\left(\delta_{n}\right)\frac{\left(1-\alpha\left(\delta_{1:n-1}\right)\right)\beta\left(\delta_{n+1:N}\right)+\alpha\left(\delta_{1:n-1}\right)\alpha\left(\delta_{n+1,N}\right)}{\left(1-\alpha\left(\delta_{1:n-1}\right)\right)\beta\left(\delta_{n:N}\right)+\alpha\left(\delta_{1:n-1}\right)\alpha\left(\delta_{n,N}\right)}\\
&=\alpha\left(\delta_{n}\right)\frac{\beta\left(\delta_{n+1:N}\right)+\alpha\left(\delta_{1:n-1}\right)\delta_{n+1:N}}{\beta\left(\delta_{n:N}\right)+\alpha\left(\delta_{1:n-1}\right)\delta_{n:N}}\\
&=\alpha\left(\delta_{n}\right)\frac{1+(M-1)\delta_{1:n-1}\delta_{n+1:N}}{1+(M-1)\delta_{1:N}}.\end{aligned}

Hence,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &(M-1)\beta\big(\tilde{\delta}_{n}\big)=1-\left(1-(M-1)\beta\left(\delta_{n}\right)\right)\frac{1+(M-1)\delta_{1:n-1}\delta_{n+1:N}}{1+(M-1)\delta_{1:N}}\\
&=\frac{(M-1)\left(\delta_{1:N}-\delta_{1:n-1}\delta_{n+1:N}\right)+(M-1)\beta\left(\delta_{n}\right)\left(1+(M-1)\delta_{1:n-1}\delta_{n+1:N}\right)}{1+(M-1)\delta_{1:N}}\\
&=\frac{(M-1)\left(-M\beta\left(\delta_{n}\right)\delta_{1:n-1}\delta_{n+1:N}\right)+(M-1)\beta\left(\delta_{n}\right)\left(1+(M-1)\delta_{1:n-1}\delta_{n+1:N}\right)}{1+(M-1)\delta_{1:N}}\\
&=(M-1)\beta\left(\delta_{n}\right)\frac{1-\delta_{1:n-1}\delta_{n+1:N}}{1+(M-1)\delta_{1:N}}.\end{aligned}

Note that
\epsilon_{n}=M\beta\left(\delta_{n}\right)
and
\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}=M\beta\big(\tilde{\delta}_{n}\big)
yield Eq. (
4
).

Proof.

(a)

Because
0\leq\delta_{k}\leq 1
for all
k\in[N]
, we can use a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mu)
to show
\vartheta\leq\theta
. Let
\{E_{1},E_{2},\dots,E_{N}\}\subset\mathcal{F}
be events with
\mu(E_{k})=\delta_{k}
. Then the complements
E_{k}^{c}
satisfy
\mu(E_{k}^{c})=1-\delta_{k}
. We can define

\vartheta=\mu\!\Bigl(\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}E_{k}^{c}\Bigr)\quad\text{and}\quad\theta=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mu(E_{k}^{c}).

If
E_{1}^{c},E_{2}^{c},\dots,E_{N}^{c}
are disjoint,
\vartheta=\theta
by countable additivity. In general,
E_{k}^{c}
may overlap, so

\mu\Bigl(\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}E_{k}^{c}\Bigr)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mu(E_{k}^{c}),

implying
\vartheta\leq\theta
.

(b)

From the proof of Proposition
A.1
, we can derive

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}=\frac{\theta-\delta_{1:N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{\epsilon_{k}}{1-\epsilon_{k}}}{1+(M-1)\delta_{1:N}}\leq\frac{\theta-(1-\vartheta)\frac{N\theta}{N-\theta}}{M-(M-1)\vartheta}=\theta\frac{(N\vartheta-\theta)/(N-\theta)}{M-(M-1)\vartheta}\leq\frac{\vartheta\theta}{M-(M-1)\vartheta},

where the inequalities follow by noting that
\vartheta\leq\theta
and

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\theta^{2}=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{\epsilon_{k}}{\sqrt{1-\epsilon_{k}}}\cdot\sqrt{1-\epsilon_{k}}\right)^{2}\leq\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{\epsilon_{k}^{2}}{1-\epsilon_{k}}\right)\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(1-\epsilon_{k}\right)\right)=(N-\theta)\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{\epsilon_{k}^{2}}{1-\epsilon_{k}}\\
\Rightarrow&\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{\epsilon_{k}}{1-\epsilon_{k}}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(\epsilon_{k}+\frac{\epsilon_{k}^{2}}{1-\epsilon_{k}}\right)\geq\theta+\frac{\theta^{2}}{N-\theta}=\frac{N\theta}{N-\theta}.\end{aligned}

(c)

Since
\vartheta_{t}\leq\vartheta_{0}
, it is clear that
\theta_{t+1}\leq\gamma\theta_{t}
and the rest is trivial.

(d)

Denote
f:x\in[0,1]\mapsto\frac{x^{2}}{M-(M-1)x}
and
g:x\in[0,1)\mapsto\left(\varphi\circ h\circ\varphi^{-1}\right)(x)
where

\displaystyle\varphi:x\in[0,\infty)\mapsto\frac{Mx}{Mx+1},\quad h:x\in[0,\infty)\mapsto x^{2},\quad\varphi^{-1}:y\in[0,1)\mapsto\frac{y}{M(1-y)}.

Note that

\displaystyle g(x)=\frac{M\left(\frac{x}{M(1-x)}\right)^{2}}{M\left(\frac{x}{M(1-x)}\right)^{2}+1}=\frac{x^{2}}{M-2Mx+(M+1)x^{2}}\geq\frac{x^{2}}{M-2Mx+(M+1)x}=f(x),

for all
x\in[0,1)
. Since
\vartheta_{t}\leq\theta_{t}<1
, one has
\theta_{t+1}\leq f\left(\theta_{t}\right)\leq g\left(\theta_{t}\right)
, which further implies
\theta_{t}\leq g^{(t)}\left(\theta_{0}\right)
due to
g
being monotonically increasing. Thus

\theta_{t}\leq g^{(t)}\left(\theta_{0}\right)=\left(\varphi\circ h^{(t)}\circ\varphi^{-1}\right)\left(\theta_{0}\right)=\frac{M\left(\frac{\theta_{0}}{M\left(1-\theta_{0}\right)}\right)^{2^{t}}}{M\left(\frac{\theta_{0}}{M\left(1-\theta_{0}\right)}\right)^{2^{t}}+1}.

We complete the proof by noting that (provided
t\geq 0
)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} g^{(t)}\left(\theta_{0}\right)\leq\varepsilon&\Leftrightarrow\left(\varphi\circ h^{(t)}\circ\varphi^{-1}\right)\left(\theta_{0}\right)\leq\varepsilon\Leftrightarrow\left(\varphi^{-1}\left(\theta_{0}\right)\right)^{2^{t}}=\left(h^{(t)}\circ\varphi^{-1}\right)\left(\theta_{0}\right)\leq\varphi^{-1}(\varepsilon)\\
&\Leftrightarrow 2^{t}\log\left(1/\varphi^{-1}\left(\theta_{0}\right)\right)\geq\log\left(1/\varphi^{-1}(\varepsilon)\right)\Leftrightarrow t\geq\log_{2}\left(\frac{\log\left(1/\varphi^{-1}(\varepsilon)\right)}{\log\left(1/\varphi^{-1}\left(\theta_{0}\right)\right)}\right).\end{aligned}

(e)

Take

\displaystyle\tau_{1}=\left\lceil\frac{\log\frac{M+1}{M\gamma}+\log\left(\theta_{0}\right)}{\log(1/\gamma)}\right\rceil_{+}\!\!,~\tau_{2}=\left\lceil\log_{2}\left(\frac{\log M+\log\frac{1-\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}}{\log\frac{M+1-M\gamma}{\gamma}}\right)\right\rceil_{+}\!\!.

Then (a) implies
\theta_{\tau_{1}}\leq\frac{M\gamma}{M+1}
, and (b) further implies
\theta_{\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}}\leq\varepsilon
.

∎

(6)

(b)

\displaystyle\delta_{1,n^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{0+0+\delta_{0,n^{\prime}}}{1+0}=\delta_{0,n^{\prime}}>0.

After the first iteration, apply the analysis in

(c)

A.2.10
Proof of Corollary
4.12

Proof.

Theorem
4.10
implies that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\delta_{t,n}=1\quad\text{for all $n\in[N]$}.

By Proposition
A.1
, we know that
P_{t,n}
has diagonal elements
\alpha(\delta_{t,n})
and off-diagonal elements
\beta(\delta_{t,n})
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\alpha(\delta_{t,n})

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1+(M-1)\delta_{t,n}}{M}=1,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\beta(\delta_{t,n})

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1-\delta_{t,n}}{M}=0,

for all
n\in[N]
.
Every row of

has one entry equal to
\alpha(\delta_{t,n})-1
and

, so

\|P_{t,n}-I_{M}\|_{\infty}=\left(1-\alpha(\delta_{t,n})\right)+(M-1)\beta(\delta_{t,n})

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\|P_{t,n}-I_{M}\|_{\infty}=0\quad\text{for all}\quad n\in[N].

A.3
Toy Example without the Markov Assumption

In the toy example of Sec.
4.3
, let
P_{0,n}
denote the pre-trained LLM’s estimated transition at step
n
, which fails to satisfy the Markov property. Specifically, we assume

\displaystyle P_{0,1}(S_{1}|S_{0})=\begin{cases}\frac{1+\delta_{0,n}}{2}&\text{if }(S_{1},S_{0})=(s_{1,m},s_{0,m})\text{ for all }n,m\in[2],\\
\frac{1-\delta_{0,n}}{2}&\text{otherwise. }\end{cases}

And that

\displaystyle P_{0,2}(S_{2}|S_{1},S_{0})=\begin{cases}\frac{(1+\delta_{0,1})^{2}}{4}&\text{if }(S_{2},S_{1},S_{0})=(s_{2,m},s_{1,m},s_{0,m})\text{ for all }m\in[2],\\
\frac{(1+\delta_{0,1})(1-\delta_{0,2})}{4}&\text{if }(S_{2},S_{1},S_{0})=(s_{2,m^{\prime}\neq m},s_{1,m},s_{0,m})\text{ for all }m^{\prime},m\in[2],\\
\frac{(1-\delta_{0,1})(1+\delta_{0,2})}{4}&\text{if }(S_{2},S_{1},S_{0})=(s_{2,m},s_{1,m},s_{0,m^{\prime}\neq m})\text{ for all }m^{\prime},m\in[2],\\
\frac{(1-\delta_{0,1})^{2}}{2}&\text{if }(S_{2},S_{1},S_{0})=(s_{2,m},s_{1,m^{\prime}\neq m},s_{0,m})\text{ for all }m^{\prime},m\in[2].\end{cases}

To derive
P_{1}(S_{1}\mid S_{0})
and
P_{2}(S_{2}\mid S_{1},S_{0})
, we follow the same procedure as in the proof of Theorem
4.4
in
A.2.1
. In the inner loop
\ell=1,\dots,L
of Algorithm
1
, we sample trajectories
\tau
, and
each occurring with probability
p(\tau)
as

p(\tau)=\begin{cases}.125\left({1+\delta_{0,1}}\right)\left({1+\delta_{0,2}}\right)&\text{if }\tau=(s_{0,1},s_{1,1},s_{2,1}),\\
.125\left({1+\delta_{0,1}}\right)\left({1-\delta_{0,2}}\right)&\text{if }\tau=(s_{0,1},s_{1,1},s_{2,2}),\\
.125\left({1-\delta_{0,1}}\right)\left({1-\delta_{0,2}}\right)&\text{if }\tau=(s_{0,1},s_{1,2},s_{2,1}),\\
.125\left({1-\delta_{0,1}}\right)\left({1+\delta_{0,2}}\right)&\text{if }\tau=(s_{0,1},s_{1,2},s_{2,2}),\\
.125\left({1-\delta_{0,1}}\right)\left({1+\delta_{0,2}}\right)&\text{if }\tau=(s_{0,2},s_{1,1},s_{2,1}),\\
.125\left({1-\delta_{0,1}}\right)\left({1-\delta_{0,2}}\right)&\text{if }\tau=(s_{0,2},s_{1,1},s_{2,2}),\\
.125\left({1+\delta_{0,1}}\right)\left({1-\delta_{0,2}}\right)&\text{if }\tau=(s_{0,2},s_{1,2},s_{2,1}),\\
.125\left({1+\delta_{0,1}}\right)\left({1+\delta_{0,2}}\right)&\text{if }\tau=(s_{0,2},s_{1,2},s_{2,2}).\\
\end{cases}

Conditioned on
\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{1}
, the sampling probability is

p(\tau\mid\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{1})

as

p(\tau|\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{1})=\begin{cases}\frac{(1+\delta_{0,1})(1+\delta_{0,2})}{4(1+\delta_{0,1}\delta_{0,2})}&\text{if }\tau=(s_{0,1},s_{1,1},s_{2,1}),\\
\frac{(1-\delta_{0,1})(1-\delta_{0,2})}{4(1+\delta_{0,1}\delta_{0,2})}&\text{if }\tau=(s_{0,1},s_{1,2},s_{2,1}),\\
\frac{(1-\delta_{0,1})(1-\delta_{0,2})}{4(1+\delta_{0,1}\delta_{0,2})}&\text{if }\tau=(s_{0,2},s_{1,1},s_{2,2}),\\
\frac{(1+\delta_{0,1})(1+\delta_{0,2})}{4(1+\delta_{0,1}\delta_{0,2})}&\text{if }\tau=(s_{0,2},s_{1,2},s_{2,2}).\\
\end{cases}

If the LLM perfectly learns these conditional transitions from
\tau\sim p(\tau\mid\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{1})
, then

\displaystyle P_{1,1}(S_{1}|S_{0})=\begin{cases}\frac{(1+\delta_{0,1})(1+\delta_{0,2})}{2(1+\delta_{0,1}\delta_{0,2})}&\text{if $(S_{1},S_{0})=(s_{1,m},s_{0,m})$ for all $m\in[2]$},\\
\frac{(1-\delta_{0,1})(1-\delta_{0,2})}{2(1+\delta_{0,1}\delta_{0,2})}&\text{if $(S_{1},S_{0})=(s_{1,m^{\prime}\neq m},s_{0,m})$ for all $m^{\prime},m\in[2]$}.\end{cases}

And that

\displaystyle P_{1,2}(S_{2}|S_{1},S_{0})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $(S_{2},S_{1},S_{0})\in$}\text{$\left\{(s_{2,m},s_{1,m},s_{0,m}),(s_{2,m},s_{1,m^{\prime}\neq m},s_{0,m})\right\}$}\text{ for all $m^{\prime},m\in[2]$},\\
0&\text{if $(S_{2},S_{1},S_{0})\in$}\text{$\left\{(s_{2,m\neq m^{\prime}},s_{1,m},s_{0,m}),(s_{2,m},s_{1,m},s_{0,m^{\prime}\neq m})\right\}$}\text{ for all $m^{\prime},m\in[2]$}.\\
\end{cases}

Based on these equations, since
P_{1,2}
is deterministic, repeating the outer loop of Algorithm
1
for
T
iterations yields

\mathcal{D}_{T}=\mathcal{D}_{T-1}=\cdots=\mathcal{D}_{1}
.
As a result, the trajectories
