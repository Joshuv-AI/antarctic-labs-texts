Moment-based approach for two erratic KPZ scaling limits
Shalin Parekh

Abstract
. A recent paper of Tsai [ Tsa24 ] shows how the first few moments of a stochastic flow in the space of measures can completely determine its law. Here we give another proof of this result for the particular case of the one-dimensional multiplicative stochastic heat equation (mSHE), and then we investigate two corollaries. The first one recovers a recent result of Hairer on a “variance blowup” problem related to the KPZ equation [ Hai24 ] , albeit in a much weaker topology. The second one recovers a KPZ scaling limit result related to random walks in random environments from [ Par24 ] , but in a weaker topology. In these two problems, we furthermore explain why it is hard to directly use the martingale characterization of the mSHE, the chaos expansion, or other known methods. Using the moment-based approach avoids technicalities, leading to a short proof.



1.
Introduction

The (1+1)-dimensional multiplicative-noise stochastic heat equation is a stochastic partial differential equation (SPDE) written formally as

(mSHE)

\partial_{t}\mathcal{U}(t,y)=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{y}^{2}\mathcal{U}(t,y)+\mathcal{U}(t,y)\xi(t,y),\;\;\;\;\;\;\;t\geq 0,y\in\mathbb{R},

where
\mathcal{U}(0,y)=\mathcal{U}_{0}(y)
and
\xi
is a Gaussian space-time white noise, that is, the Gaussian field with covariances
\mathbb{E}[\xi(t,x)\xi(s,y)]=\delta(t-s)\delta(x-y)
. Such
\xi
will be a tempered distribution that is not a function, thus one needs some work to make sense of this object. Using stochastic calculus it is possible to define a natural notion of solution started from any nonnegative measure-valued initial condition
\mathcal{U}_{0}
of at-worst-exponential growth at infinity, see
[
Wal86
]
. This is called the Itô solution or Itô-Walsh solution of the SPDE given by (
mSHE
).

It turns out that the Itô-Walsh solution
\mathcal{U}
is a random function that remains strictly positive at all positive times almost surely, as long as the initial data
\mathcal{U}_{0}
is a non-negative and nonzero Borel measure. This random function
\mathcal{U}

-
and its logarithm, which solves the so-called Kardar-Parisi-Zhang (KPZ) equation
-
has been the subject of intense research in recent decades due to its connection to a number of active areas, including exactly solvable systems, interface growth models, interacting particle systems, directed polymers, KPZ universality, and stochastic analysis. We refer to
[
FS10
,
Qua11
,
Cor12
,
QS15
,
CW17
,
CS20
]
for some surveys on this topic.

A recent work of Tsai
[
Tsa24
]
studies the (2+1)-dimensional analogue of (
mSHE
), which is more difficult to define because it is not known to be the strong solution of an SPDE like the (1+1)-dimensional case, see
[
CSZ23a
,
CSZ23b
]
. The Tsai paper shows that the (2+1)-dimensional analogue of (
mSHE
) is
uniquely characterized in law as a measure-valued stochastic flow using only its first four moments.
That result is remarkable in the sense that it makes rigorous various “moment-based replica tricks” which physicists have long used, but which were thought to be non-rigorous from a mathematical perspective due to the extremely fast growth of the moments. Inspired by
[
Tsa24
]
, we use a similar methodology for the
one-dimensional
object (
mSHE
) in order to study two “unusual” problems in KPZ scaling limit theory that exhibit very erratic behavior. The two corresponding main results are Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
below.

First let us introduce some notations. We let
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
denote the set of locally finite and non-negative Borel measures on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. We equip
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
with the vague topology, that is, the weakest topology under which the maps
\mu\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f\;d\mu
are continuous, for all
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. We also denote
\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}:=\{(s,t,x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}:s<t\}
, and
C(\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow})
the set of functions from
\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}\to\mathbb{R}
that are continuous. Equip
C(\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow})
with the topology of uniform convergence on compacts.
\mathbb{Q}
will denote rational numbers, but in all results and proofs it could be replaced by any countable dense subset of
\mathbb{R}
as desired. The following result is due to Tsai
[
Tsa24
]
, and we state a less optimized (1+1)-dimensional version of it that will suffice for our purposes.

Proposition 1.1

((1+1)-dimensional variant of Theorem 1.3 in
[
Tsa24
]
)
.

Let
Z_{s,t}
be a family of
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
-valued random variables indexed by
-\infty<s\leq t<\infty
with
s,t\in\mathbb{Q},
all defined on some complete probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
. Suppose that the following are true:

(1)

Z_{s_{j},t_{j}}
are independent under
\mathbb{P}
whenever
(s_{j},t_{j})
are disjoint intervals.

(2)

For all
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and all
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
with
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\psi=1
, we have that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}\delta^{-1}\psi(\delta^{-1}(y-w))\cdot f(x,z)Z_{t,u}(dy,dz)Z_{s,t}(dx,dw)\to\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(x,z)Z_{s,u}(dx,dz)

in probability as
\delta\to 0
, whenever
s<t<u
.

(3)

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\phi,\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}

\displaystyle\phi(x)\psi(y)Z_{s,t}(dx,dy)\bigg)^{n}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\phi(x_{k})\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{BM}^{\otimes n}}^{(x_{1},...,x_{n})}\bigg[\prod_{k=1}^{n}\psi(B^{k}_{t-s})e^{\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}L_{0}^{B^{i}-B^{j}}(t-s)}\bigg]dx_{1}\cdots dx_{n}.

The expectation on the right side is with respect to a
n
-dimensional Brownian motion
(B^{1},...,B^{n})
started from
(x_{1},...,x_{n})
, and
L_{0}^{X}
denotes the local time at 0 of the semimartingale
X
.

Then there exists a
C(\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow})
-valued random variable
\tilde{Z}
defined on the same probability space, such that
(Z_{s,t},f)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(x,z)\tilde{Z}_{s,t}(x,z)dxdz
almost surely, for all
(s,t)\in\mathbb{Q}^{2}
(with
s<t
) and
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

Furthermore, there exists a space-time white noise
\xi
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, defined on the same probability space, with the property that
\tilde{Z}_{s,t}
are the
propagators
of (
mSHE
) driven by
\xi
: for all
s,x\in\mathbb{R}
the function
(t,y)\mapsto\tilde{Z}_{s,t}(x,y)
is a.s. the Itô-Walsh solution of the equation (
mSHE
) started from time
s
with initial condition
\delta_{x}(y)
. In particular, the finite-dimensional marginal laws
(Z_{s_{1},t_{1}},...,Z_{s_{m},t_{m}})
are
\mathrm{uniquely\;determined}
by Conditions (1) - (3), for any finite collection of indices
s_{j}\leq t_{j}
.

Although the above result is not the main focus of this paper, we will give a novel proof of it in Section
2
for the sake of completeness. However, our proof is specific to the one-dimensional case and it is not as robust as the proof in
[
Tsa24
]
. The proof is based on stochastic calculus techniques rather than the Lindeberg replacement method of
[
Tsa24
]
, and shows that these three conditions actually imply the
martingale problem
for the equation (
mSHE
)
[
SV97
,
KS88
,
BG97
]
, and also that the noise can be reconstructed via a stochastic integral formula. In Condition (3), we only need the equality to hold for
1\leq n\leq 19
as opposed to all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, as the proof will show (see Remark
2.6
). Optimally it should be 4 moments, as shown for the (2+1)-dimensional analogue in
[
Tsa24
]
. The weaker version above has a short proof and suffices for the two main problems that we want to study, see Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
below.

From the perspective of scaling limit theory, the value of a result like Proposition
1.1
is that it can be used to identify limit points quite easily in cases where the moments are known. This now leads to the question of whether or not there are any interesting problems in scaling limit theory that can be solved using the above approach, which cannot be done directly using some other well-established methods such as the martingale problem for the equation (
mSHE
). The following theorem was first proved by Hairer in
[
Hai24
]
, and we now recover the result using Proposition
1.1
, albeit in a much weaker topology.

Theorem 1.2

(Scaling limit of the SHE with derivative noise)
.

Fix some
p\in\mathbb{N}
. Consider the SPDE

(dSHE)

\partial_{t}z^{\epsilon}(t,y)=\tfrac{1}{2}\partial_{y}^{2}z^{\epsilon}(t,y)+\epsilon^{p-\frac{1}{4}}z^{\epsilon}(t,y)\partial_{y}^{p}\eta^{\epsilon}(t,y),\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;t\geq 0,y\in\mathbb{R},

where
\eta^{\epsilon}:=\varphi_{\epsilon}*\eta
for a standard Gaussian space-time white noise
\eta
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}
and where the convolution is in space only, and where
\varphi_{\epsilon}(y):=\epsilon^{-1}\varphi(\epsilon^{-1}y)
for some fixed smooth even mollifier
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
.

Let
Z^{\epsilon}_{s,t}(x,y)
denote the associated family of propagators given by the Itô-Walsh solution
z^{\epsilon}
to the equation (
dSHE
). Then
Z^{\epsilon}
converges in law as
\epsilon\to 0
to the family of propagators given by the Itô-Walsh solution of the SPDE

\partial_{t}\mathcal{U}(t,y)=\tfrac{1}{2}\partial_{y}^{2}\mathcal{U}(t,y)+\sigma_{p}\mathcal{U}(t,y)\xi(t,y),\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\sigma_{p}^{2}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\varphi*\varphi^{(2p-1)}(x))^{2}dx

where
\varphi^{(k)}
is the
k^{th}
derivative and
\xi
is another space-time white noise. Convergence is in the sense of finite-dimensional laws
(Z^{\epsilon}_{s_{1},t_{1}},...,Z^{\epsilon}_{s_{m},t_{m}}),
where each coordinate has the topology of
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, for any finite collection of indices
s_{j}\leq t_{j}
.

We prove Theorem
1.2
in Section
3
. We remark that the result of
[
Hai24
]
was on a circle as opposed to ours on the full line, and moreover it was restricted to more regular initial data. However, that result was stronger in many ways. First of all, that result can consider
non-Markovian approximations,
such as mollifications of
\eta
in both space and time. But more crucially, that paper proves convergence in law with a much stronger topology of Hölder continuous functions as opposed to just measure-valued stochastic flows. This question of topology is very important here, because one of the most striking aspects of Proposition
1.1
is the fact that
Z_{s,t}
is not assumed to be very regular at all, in particular there is no
a priori
assumption that it is continuous or even function-valued. This makes tightness fairly easy to prove in our case. In contrast, obtaining tightness of (
dSHE
) in a stronger topology seems very difficult to do directly, because of the fact that there is a derivative when we try to calculate moments of local Hölder norms by writing out the Duhamel formula.

It is not possible to improve Theorem
1.2
so that convergence occurs a.s. or in probability. Let us explain why this is the case, and why it is difficult to directly use established methods such as the martingale problem or the chaos expansion to solve this problem. A direct application of the martingale problem would require us to prove tightness in a space of continuous functions, which as pointed out above is difficult. But even then, obtaining nice expressions for the quadratic variations of the relevant martingales seems to be extremely tedious. On the other hand, the chaos expansion method of
[
AKQ14b
,
CSZ17
]
would be difficult because as pointed out in
[
Hai24
]
, the “correct driving noise” in (
dSHE
) to see the limit is
not

\eta^{\epsilon}
itself, but rather it is
\epsilon^{2p-\frac{1}{2}}((\partial_{x}K\star\partial_{y}^{p}\eta^{\epsilon})^{2}-C_{\epsilon})
for some large constant
C_{\epsilon}\uparrow\infty,
where
K
is the heat kernel and
\star
is space-time convolution. To form a chaos expansion with
this
noise seems to be a gargantuan task, since it is non-Gaussian and has nonzero correlations in both space and time.

The paper
[
Hai24
]
proves the result by using the theory of BPHZ renormalization
[
HS23
,
CH18
,
BH24
]
to automatically find canonical
probabilistic lifts
of the driving noise (which consist of some large but finite number of homogeneous chaoses over
\eta^{\epsilon}
), together with the
analytic theory
of regularity structures
[
Hai14
]
to construct a continuous map from the lifted noise to the solution. Those structural properties of the solution map are extremely powerful, and the reason why the result of
[
Hai24
]
is more robust. Hairer calls the scaling of Theorem
1.2
an example of “variance blowup” because if one naively tries to set up the regularity structure based on just
\eta^{\epsilon}
without
separately
keeping track of
\epsilon^{2p-\frac{1}{2}}((\partial_{x}K\star\partial_{y}^{p}\eta^{\epsilon})^{2}-C_{\epsilon})
, then the stochastic part of the construction of some of the trees fails to converge in
L^{2}
due to an unbounded variance (despite the
scaling subcriticality
of the equation for
z^{\epsilon}
in the sense of
[
Hai14
]
), similar to what happens if one tries to construct iterated integrals with respect to fractional Brownian motion with
H\leq 1/4
. These variance blowup issues cause
[
Hai24
]
to have to slightly modify the BPHZ theory before applying it, which makes it somewhat technical compared to the more elementary approach here.

Next, we consider another application of Proposition
1.1
, which is unrelated to Theorem
1.2
despite the apparent similarity. The following result was first conjectured in
[
BW22
]
and then proved in
[
Par24
]
.

Theorem 1.3

(Scaling limit of the SHE with advective noise)
.

Let
\eta
be a space-time white noise on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}
, and let
\eta^{\epsilon}:=\varphi_{\epsilon}*\eta
where the convolution is in space only, and where
\varphi_{\epsilon}(y):=\epsilon^{-1}\varphi(\epsilon^{-1}y)
for some fixed smooth even mollifier
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}<1
(equivalently
\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\varphi*\varphi(x)<1
).

Denote by
\mathcal{Z}^{\epsilon}_{s,t}(x,y)
the propagators given by the Itô-Walsh solution of the SPDE

(aSHE)

\partial_{t}u(t,y)=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{y}^{2}u(t,y)+u(t,y)\eta^{\epsilon}(t,y)+\epsilon^{1/2}\partial_{y}\big(u(t,y)\eta^{\epsilon}(t,y)\big),\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;t\geq 0,y\in\mathbb{R}.

Then
\mathcal{Z}^{\epsilon}
converges in law as
\epsilon\to 0
to the family of propagators given by the Itô-Walsh solution of

\partial_{t}\mathcal{U}(t,y)=\tfrac{1}{2}\partial_{y}^{2}\mathcal{U}(t,y)+\gamma_{\mathrm{ext}}\mathcal{U}(t,y)\xi(t,y),\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\gamma_{\mathrm{ext}}^{2}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\varphi*\varphi(a)}{1-\varphi*\varphi(a)}da.

Here
\xi
is another space-time white noise. The convergence is in the sense of finite-dimensional laws
(\mathcal{Z}^{\epsilon}_{s_{1},t_{1}},...,\mathcal{Z}^{\epsilon}_{s_{m},t_{m}}),
where each coordinate has the topology of
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

We prove Theorem
1.3
in Section
4
. The scaling of the equation (
aSHE
) exhibits even more erratic properties than the one in Theorem
1.2
, as we will explain just below. Nonetheless, it is physically relevant due to its appearance in problems related to the topic of KPZ universality in models of random walk in random environments, see e.g.
[
LDT17
,
TLD16
,
BLD20
,
BC17
,
CG17
,
BR20
,
BW22
,
DDP23b
,
DDP23a
,
Par24
]
for more discussion. In much greater generality it was first proved in
[
Par24
]
that as
\epsilon\to 0
, (
aSHE
) converges in law to the solution of (
mSHE
), in the stronger topology of
C([0,T],\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}))
. See Section 6.3 of that paper for the particular case of (
aSHE
). That result did not consider the full family of propagators, but rather just
s=x=0
. Combined with Theorem
1.3
, that result now extends to the full family of propagators.

As with Theorem
1.2
, the convergence of Theorem
1.3
does not occur a.s. or in probability, and in fact extra noise will be generated in the limit that is independent of the original driving noise
\eta
(as is suggested by the fact that
\gamma_{\mathrm{ext}}>1
). This was explained in detail in
[
DDP23a
, Theorem 1.4]
, which studied a discrete version of this model (
aSHE
). A related pathological phenomenon here is that (unlike Theorem
1.2
) convergence of
\mathcal{Z}^{\epsilon}
will
not
occur in a space of continuous functions such as
C(\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow})
. This is not just a technicality. Indeed, if one assumes that such a convergence holds in the space of continuous functions, then one can derive a contradiction, see the discussion in
[
DDP23a
]
for a discrete analogue.

This suggests that unlike Theorem
1.2
, the scaling limit problem in Theorem
1.3
cannot even be studied using regularity structures (at least not directly). Indeed, one can check that the equation of Theorem
1.3
is not
scaling subcritical
in the sense of
[
Hai14
]
. To see that, one can consider the “naive” regularity structure where the model space
T
is generated by two abstract noise symbols
\Xi_{1},\Xi_{2}
and an integration operator
\mathcal{I}
of order 2 (representing convolution with the heat kernel in space and time). The rule for
T
is that
1\in T
, and
\tau\in T
implies
\mathcal{I}(\tau\Xi_{1})+\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(\tau\Xi_{2})\in T
. Here
\Xi_{1}
stands for
\eta^{\epsilon}
and
\Xi_{2}
stands for
\epsilon^{1/2}\eta^{\epsilon}
. One wants to solve a fixed point problem of the form
x=\mathcal{I}(x\Xi_{1})+\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(x\Xi_{2})
, where
\Xi_{1}
would have order
-3/2
while
\Xi_{2}
would have order
-1
thanks to the multiplying factor
\epsilon^{1/2}
. Now the reason that local subcriticality fails is that orders of products are the sum of the orders, thus
\Xi_{2}\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(\Xi_{2})
has order
-1
again, thus
\Xi_{2}\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(\Xi_{2}\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(\Xi_{2}))
has order
-1
once again, and so on. Thus it is a
scaling critical model
, since the regularity remains bounded below but fails to improve under successive iterations of the rules for this regularity structure. Thus Theorem
1.3
is not a “variance blowup” problem in the same sense as described in
[
Hai24
]
, because the problem occurs at the level of the
analytic theory
and not just the
probabilistic lifts
. Still, it exhibits a similar flavor in the sense that putting the vanishing factor
\epsilon^{1/2}
on
\eta^{\epsilon}
appearing in the advective term leads to a nontrivial limit in distribution but not pathwise.

The chaos expansion also fails for the equation of Theorem
1.3
, due to some of the
L^{2}
-mass of
\mathcal{Z}^{\epsilon}
escaping into the tails of the
\eta^{\epsilon}
-chaos expansion (very high-order chaos) as
\epsilon\to 0
, see the discussion in the introduction of
[
DDP23a
]
. In (
aSHE
), the “correct prelimiting noise” does not live in any fixed-order chaos as was the case for Theorem
1.2
.
Conjecturally
it should be
\eta^{\epsilon}\cdot F^{\epsilon}
where
F^{\epsilon}
is the space-time stationary solution to
\partial_{t}v=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{y}^{2}v+\epsilon^{1/2}\partial_{y}(v\eta^{\epsilon})
. More intuitively in terms of the regularity structure discussion,
F=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\Xi_{2})^{\circ k}
where
(\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\Xi_{2})^{\circ k}
denotes the
k
-fold iteration of the operation
\tau\mapsto\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(\tau\Xi_{2})
. Ultimately in
[
Par24
]
and related problems
[
DDP23b
,
DDP23a
]
, the method of
martingale problem
was successfully used for this type of model, but the resultant effort was long and technical. Here the result is weaker, but nonetheless we can successfully identify the limit points
uniquely
with a calculation of only five pages, thus illustrating the power of the weaker moment-based formulation above.

Having discussed the benefits, let us discuss some of the major limitations of Proposition
1.1
in (1+1)-dimensional KPZ-related problems. First, there are many interesting systems (for example the WASEP of
[
BG97
]
) in which calculating the moments is not so easy, where the classical martingale problem would actually be simpler than the moment method applied here. Second, one always suspects tightness in a better topology than just finite-dimensional laws in
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, but the moment-based approach of Proposition
1.1
by itself will never be able to show the required tightness in any better topology, thus illustrating another limitation (although the moment method
does
identify the limit point uniquely if the tightness has already been shown in a stronger topology such as
C(\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow})
).
Third, the method of Proposition
1.1
is limited to systems that are
linear as a function of their initial data
, see the discussion in Subsection 2.1. Fourth, Proposition
1.1
is unable to calculate
joint
limits in distribution. For example in the setting of Theorem
1.2
, the method of
[
Hai24
]
actually shows that
(\eta^{\epsilon},Z^{\epsilon})
converge in law jointly to two independent processes with the correct marginals. In the setting of Theorem
1.3
, the result of
[
DDP23a
, Theorem 1.4]
shows how to calculate the
joint
limit in distribution of
(\eta^{\epsilon},\mathcal{Z}^{\epsilon})
which has a nontrivial answer. Proposition
1.1
cannot do this.

Proof of Proposition

2.1.
Elementary discussion

Before proving the proposition, we first explain the main idea by considering a toy model given by the “geometric Brownian flow,” which is the same example given in
[
Tsa24
]
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Consider a two-variable process
(G_{s,t})_{-\infty<s\leq t<\infty}
with
s,t\in\mathbb{Q}
, defined on some complete probability space, and satisfying

(1)

G_{s_{j},t_{j}}
are independent if
(s_{j},t_{j})
are disjoint intervals.

(2)

G_{s,t}G_{t,u}=G_{s,u}
whenever
s<t<u
.

(3)

For
n=1,2,3,4,5,6
, we have that
\mathbb{E}\big[G_{s,t}^{n}\big]=e^{\frac{n^{2}}{2}(t-s)}.

These three conditions uniquely determine the finite-dimensional distributions of the process
(G_{s,t})_{s<t}
as being the same as those of
(e^{B(t)-B(s)})_{s<t}
for a standard Brownian motion
B
, and moreover
B
can be defined on the same space so that the relation holds pathwise on
\mathbb{Q}^{2}
.

Proof.

[
Tsa24
]
proves this using the Lindeberg exchange principle, so let us apply a different method. Define the process
X_{t}:=e^{-\frac{t}{2}}G_{0,t}.
First we note that
X
admits a continuous modification, meaning that there exists a
C[0,T]
-valued random variable
\tilde{X}
defined on the same probability space such that
\tilde{X}_{t}=X_{t}
almost surely for all
t\in\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,T]
. This is because one has the bound

\mathbb{E}[|G_{0,t}-G_{0,s}|^{3}]^{1/3}=\mathbb{E}[|G_{0,s}|^{3}|G_{s,t}-1|^{3}]^{1/3}\leq\mathbb{E}[|G_{0,s}|^{6}]^{1/6}\mathbb{E}[|G_{s,t}-1|^{6}]^{1/6}\leq C_{T}|t-s|^{1/2},

uniformly over
s,t\in[0,T]\cap\mathbb{Q}
for any
T>0
. Now note that
\tilde{X}_{t}
is a martingale using (1), (2), and (3) with
n=1
. For
t>0
, we claim that for any deterministic sequence of partitions of
[0,t]
with mesh tending to 0 and having rational endpoints, one has

(2.1)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{N}(X_{t_{i+1}}-X_{t_{i}})^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{t_{i}}^{2}(t_{i+1}-t_{i})\bigg)^{2}\bigg]=0.

To prove this, note that the claim is true
1
1

1

For any continuous
L^{2}
-martingale, the partition approximations of the quadratic variation process always converge
in probability
to the quadratic variation process
[
RY99
, Theorem (1.8) in Chapter IV]
. Under the stronger assumption that the martingale is in
L^{p}
for some
p>4
, this can be automatically upgraded to
L^{2}
convergence by a uniform integrability argument, using e.g. the lower bound of BDG and then Doob’s inequality. This will be used later as well.
if
X
was replaced by the process
e^{B(t)-\frac{t}{2}}
. But since the first four moments of
every multiplicative increment
of the latter process match those of
X
, the claim is true for
X
as well. In particular the quadratic variation is given by
\langle\tilde{X}\rangle_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{X}_{s}^{2}ds.
But if
\tilde{X}_{t}
and

The proof can be modified slightly so that the assumption of six moments can be relaxed to four moments in Item (3), but then one needs to work with discontinuous processes, see Remark
2.6
. We also remark that in order to be able to use this method, it is important for the object of interest to be a
linear function of its initial data
. This type of moment-based approach would never work for more complicated nonlinear multiplicative forcing terms, such as SDEs of the form

Proposition 2.2
.

In the setting of Proposition

, there exists a random four-parameter function

(2)

If

are independent.

(3)

If
s<t<u
and

, then we have the a.s. relation

(2.2)

\tilde{Z}_{s,u}(x,z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\tilde{Z}_{s,t}(x,y)\tilde{Z}_{t,u}(y,z)dy.

For
x,y\in\mathbb{Q}
, consider

such that
\phi_{n}\to\delta_{x}
and
\psi_{n}\to\delta_{y}
weakly as

. Then we have that
(Z_{s,t},\phi_{n}\otimes\psi_{n})
form a Cauchy sequence in

, since the

-norm of
(Z_{s,t},\phi_{n}\otimes\psi_{n})-(Z_{s,t},\phi_{m}\otimes\psi_{m})

L^{2}
-norm of
(U_{s,t},\phi_{n}\otimes\psi_{n})-(U_{s,t},\phi_{m}\otimes\psi_{m})
where

are the propagators for (
mSHE
) which is known to be a continuous field satisfying
(U_{s,t},\phi_{n}\otimes\psi_{n})\to U_{s,t}(x,y)
a.s. and in
L^{2}
as
n\to\infty
.

By letting
\tilde{Z}_{s,t}(x,y)
denote the
L^{2}
-limit of these random variables for

, for
n<19
(

we obtain that for all

(2.3)

\mathbb{E}[\prod_{j=1}^{n}\tilde{Z}_{s,t}(x_{j},y_{j})]=\mathbf{E}[\prod_{j=1}^{n}U_{s,t}(x_{j},y_{j})],

where

are the propagators for (
mSHE
), not necessarily defined on the same probability space. Now by expanding out all powers, this implies that
\mathbb{E}[|\tilde{Z}_{s,t}(x,y)-\tilde{Z}_{s,t}(x^{\prime},y)|^{18}]^{1/18}\leq C|x-x^{\prime}|^{1/2}

\mathbb{E}[|\tilde{Z}_{s,t}(x,y)-\tilde{Z}_{s,t}(x,y^{\prime})|^{18}]^{1/18}\leq C|y-y^{\prime}|^{1/2}
, with

uniform over all
(x,x^{\prime},y,y^{\prime})
lying in some compact set of
\mathbb{R}^{4}
. These bounds hold because by (

) we can replace
\tilde{Z}_{s,t}
with

which is known to satisfy these bounds, see e.g.
[

]
or alternatively
[
AJRAS22
, Lemma 3.4 - Lemma 3.9]
for a more explicit calculation.

Thus by the multiparameter Kolmogorov-Chentsov criterion, for each
fixed

(s,t)\in\mathbb{Q}^{2}
there exists a field
\tilde{Z}_{s,t}(x,y)
with
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
which is a.s. Hölder continuous on compact sets of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. We need to verify that
(Z_{s,t},f)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\tilde{Z}_{s,t}(x,y)f(x,y)dxdy
almost surely for all
s<t
and all

. To prove this, we have the following general fact: let
\mu
be a random variable in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
such that for
some
smooth even (deterministic)

one has

\sup_{a,\varepsilon}\mathbb{E}[(\mu,\phi^{a}_{\varepsilon})^{2}]<\infty,\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathbb{E}[(\mu,\phi_{\varepsilon}^{a})^{2}]=0,\text{ for all }a\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

where for

and

the rescaled mollifier is given by
\phi_{\varepsilon}^{a}(x):=\varepsilon^{-1}\phi(\varepsilon^{-1}(x-a)).
Then we claim

(\mu,\psi)=0\text{ almost surely for all }\psi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{2}).

To prove this, define
\mu^{\varepsilon}(x):=\mu*\phi_{\varepsilon}(x)

(\mu,\phi^{a}_{\varepsilon})=\mu^{\varepsilon}(a).
Given some smooth
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}

(\mu^{\varepsilon},\psi)\to(\mu,\psi)
a.s. as
\varepsilon\to 0
. This is a purely deterministic statement. Thus it suffices to show that

Since by assumption
\mathbb{E}[\mu^{\varepsilon}(a)^{2}]

of compact support. For general

Finally, to verify that

almost surely for all

and all

, apply (

) with

Simply by construction, we have the two bounds written above. The reason that

is because of the fact that

[

[

Next, we need to verify (
2.2
). To do this, we of course need to use Item (2) in the assumptions of Proposition

. Using the fact that Item (1) of the proposition has already been proved, we can write

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}f(x,z)\epsilon^{-1}\psi

\displaystyle(\epsilon^{-1}(y-w))Z_{t,u}(dy,dz)Z_{s,t}(dx,dw)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}f(x,z)\delta^{-1}\psi(\delta^{-1}(y-w))\tilde{Z}_{t,u}(y_{1},z)\tilde{Z}_{s,t}(x,y_{2})dxdydwdz.

As

Thus for

Now take

which converges weakly to

and use the continuity of
\tilde{Z}
to obtain (
2.2
).

So far, this argument shows that for each

(s,t)\in\mathbb{Q}^{2}
with

such that the desired properties are respected. However, we still need to show the continuity in

]

Proposition 2.3

(Solving the martingale problem)
.

Let
\tilde{Z}_{s,t}(x,y)
be the continuous modification from Proposition
2.2
. Fix some
x,s\in\mathbb{R}
. Then for all
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
the process

M_{t}(\phi):=(\tilde{Z}_{s,t}(x,\bullet),\phi)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{s}^{t}(\tilde{Z}_{s,u}(x,\bullet),\phi^{\prime\prime})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}du,\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;t\geq s

is a continuous martingale in the filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{s,t})_{t\geq s}
where
\mathcal{F}_{s,t}:=\sigma(\tilde{Z}_{a,b}(x,y):s<a<b<t,x,y\in\mathbb{R}\}
. The quadratic variations are furthermore given by

\langle M(\phi)\rangle_{t}=\int_{s}^{t}(\tilde{Z}_{s,u}(x,\bullet)^{2},\phi^{2})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}du.

Proof.

Without loss of generality we can assume
s=x=0
. Let
z_{t}(y):=Z_{0,t}(0,y).
Applying (
2.2
) and using independence of
\tilde{Z}_{0,s}
and
\tilde{Z}_{s,t}
, one obtains that

\mathbb{E}[z_{t}(y)|\mathcal{F}_{0,s}]=p_{t-s}*z_{s}(y).

where
p_{t}(x):=(2\pi t)^{-1/2}e^{-x^{2}/2t}
is the standard heat kernel. This means that if
s<t
then
\mathbb{E}[(z_{t},\phi)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}|\mathcal{F}_{s}]=(z_{s},p_{t-s}*\phi)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}
, and from here the martingality of
M_{t}(\phi)
is an easy and direct consequence. Now we need to compute the quadratic variations. For this, we claim that for any deterministic sequence of partitions of
[0,t]
with mesh tending to zero, one has

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{N}(z_{t_{i+1}}-z_{t_{i}},\phi)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}(z_{t_{i}}^{2},\phi^{2})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}(t_{i+1}-t_{i})\bigg)^{2}\bigg]=0.

Indeed if
(t,x)\mapsto\mathcal{U}_{t}(x)
solves (
mSHE
) with Dirac initial condition, then notice from the assumptions of Proposition
1.1
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg(

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}(z_{t_{i+1}}-z_{t_{i}},\phi)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}(z_{t_{i}}^{2},\phi^{2})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}(t_{i+1}-t_{i})\bigg)^{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\bigg[\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\mathcal{U}_{t_{i+1}}-\mathcal{U}_{t_{i}},\phi)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\mathcal{U}_{t_{i}}^{2},\phi^{2})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}(t_{i+1}-t_{i})\bigg)^{2}\bigg].

This follows from a calculation, expanding everything out algebraically and then using the propagator equation (
2.2
) together with the moment equality (
2.3
) repeatedly, to show that replacing
z_{t}
by
\mathcal{U}_{t}
does not change the above expectation. But the desired limit is indeed true for the process
\mathcal{U}_{t}
since
\mathcal{U}_{t}
does solve the martingale problem for (
mSHE
), and because the associated quadratic variation of the martingale for
\mathcal{U}
is known to be given by
\int_{0}^{t}(\mathcal{U}_{s}^{2},\phi^{2})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}ds,
see e.g.
[
BG97
, Proposition 4.11]
. The claim thus follows immediately.
∎

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
\tilde{Z}_{s,t}(x,y)
be as in Proposition
2.2
. Then for all fixed
s,x\in\mathbb{R}
, the law of the process
(t,y)\mapsto\tilde{Z}_{s,t}(x,y)
agrees with that of the solution to (
mSHE

.

Again we can assume
s=x=0
, so let

The martingale problem from Proposition
2.3
identifies the law of the process

Par18

Proposition 2.5

(Identifying the joint noise and defining it on the same probability space)
.

Let
\tilde{Z}_{s,t}
be as in Proposition
2.2
. On can construct a space-time white noise
\xi
on the same probability space, such that if
s,x\in\mathbb{R}
, then
(t,y)\mapsto\tilde{Z}_{s,t}(x,y)
almost surely solves (
mSHE
) driven by that same noise
\xi
. Thus we have completed Proposition
1.1
.

Proof.

Throughout this proof, fix
s\in\mathbb{R}
, and let
\{x_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
be some enumeration of
\mathbb{Q}
. Let
z_{i}(t,y):=\tilde{Z}_{s,t}(x_{i},y).
We will argue that the driving noise
\xi_{i}
of the process
z_{i}
is an unambiguous notion, and furthermore that all
\xi_{i}
are the same noise, i.e.,
\xi_{i}=\xi_{1}.

Assume without loss of generality that
s=0
. By using the result of Corollary
2.4
, we now argue that each
\xi_{i}
can be recovered measurably from the process
z_{i}
itself. This will be a consequence of the Walsh theory of stochastic integrals
[
Wal86
]
. More precisely, we let
M^{i}_{t}(\phi):=(z_{i}(t,\bullet),\phi)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}(z_{i}(s,\bullet),\phi^{\prime\prime})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}ds
denote the martingales from above but corresponding to the respective initial data
\delta_{x_{i}}
. These are
orthomartingales
in the sense of
[
Wal86
]
, thus we can define stochastic integrals against them. Then as observed in
[
KS88
]
, Walsh’s theory guarantees that we can measurably recover the driving noises by the stochastic integral formula

(\xi_{i},\phi\otimes 1_{[0,t]})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R})}=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}z_{i}(s,y)^{-1}\phi(y)M^{i}(ds,dy).

This formula implies that
(\xi_{i},\phi\otimes 1_{[0,t]})
is a martingale in
t
with quadratic variation
\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}t
, which implies that each
\xi_{i}
is a Gaussian space-time white noise (defined on the same probability space) by Levy’s criterion. A similar calculation as above in Proposition
2.3
yields
\langle M^{i}(\phi),M^{j}(\phi)\rangle_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}(z^{i}_{s}z_{s}^{j},\phi^{2})ds
. It follows that
\xi_{i}=\xi_{j}
for the respective noises, since the above formula yields the relation
\mathbb{E}[(\xi_{i},\phi\otimes 1_{[0,t]})(\xi_{j},\phi\otimes 1_{[0,t]})]=t\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}
which in turn yields
\mathbb{E}[(\xi_{i}-\xi_{j},\phi\otimes 1_{[0,t]})^{2}]=0.

From
\mathbb{Q}
one can extend the result to
\mathbb{R}
simply because
\tilde{Z}
is already known to be continuous in all four variables. Now if
s_{1}<s_{2}
and
x_{1},...,x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
and one wishes to show that the driving noise of
(t,y)\mapsto Z_{s_{i},t}(x_{j},y)
are the same for all
i,j
, note that by the Markov property that is guaranteed by Proposition
2.4
, one can restart the process at time
s_{2}
, which reduces us back to the case where
s_{1}=s_{2}
which has already been covered above.
∎

Remark 2.6
.

Notice that we only used Item (3) of Proposition
1.1
up to
n=19
rather than all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. This is because we used an
18^{th}
moment bound in the proof of Proposition
2.2
. This
19^{th}
moment assumption could be further relaxed, but the proof would become longer.

Let us illustrate how to weaken the moment assumptions in the simpler context of the “Geometric Brownian flow” of Proposition
2.1
. We claim that in Item (3) of that proposition, the assumption of 6 moments can be weakened to 4 moments. To prove it, note that
\tilde{X}_{t}:=e^{-\frac{t}{2}}G_{0,t}
is a martingale indexed by
\mathbb{Q}
, thus it admits a cadlag extension to
\mathbb{R}_{+}
by the fundamental regularization lemma, see
[
RY99
, Chapter II, Theorem (2.5)]
. Then (
2.1
) still implies that the (optional) quadratic variation of the martingale
\tilde{X}
is given by
[\tilde{X}]_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{X}_{s}^{2}ds.
Continuity of
[\tilde{X}]
implies continuity of
\tilde{X}
itself, thanks to the identity
\delta[Y]=(\delta Y)^{2}
for any semimartingale
Y
, where
(\delta Y)_{s}:=Y_{s}-Y_{s-}.
Thus
\tilde{X}_{t}
and
\tilde{X}_{t}^{2}-\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{X}_{s}^{2}ds
are both continuous martingales, completing the proof exactly as before. But Item (3) has only been used up to
n=4
rather than
n=6
, since we avoided the use of Kolmogorov-Chentsov to construct the continuous modification.

This illustrates the fact that that when we assume less moments, we can no longer keep the proof within the realm of
continuous
processes, thus needing to think about how to deal with jumps or other irregularities. Very similar considerations apply in the SPDE case of Proposition
1.1
: we can assume less than 19 moments, but in that case the proof will become so much longer that we have not pursued it in this paper. The reason is that it would involve introducing various other topologies that are weaker than that of
C(\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow})
and harder to define, then showing retroactively that the resultant four-parameter process
\tilde{Z}
ends up being an element of
C(\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow})
anyways. Alternatively, one can use Tsai’s exchange method to avoid this
[
Tsa24
]
. The version with all moments will suffice for us.

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

Henceforth we consider the propagators

Proposition 3.1

(Moment formula)
.

Let
\Phi_{\epsilon}:=\varphi_{\epsilon}*\varphi_{\epsilon}
, with
\varphi_{\epsilon}
as in Theorem
1.2
. If
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is bounded and continuous, and

, we have that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathbb{E}[\prod_{j=1}^{n}Z_{0,t}^{\epsilon}(x_{j},y_{j})]\prod_{j=1}^{n}\phi(y_{j})dy\cdots dy_{n}=\mathbf{E}^{(x_{1},...,x_{n})}_{\mathrm{BM}^{\otimes n}}[e^{\epsilon^{2p-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\int_{0}^{t}\Phi_{\epsilon}^{(2p)}(W^{i}_{s}-W^{j}_{s})ds}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\phi(W^{j}_{t})].

Here the expectation on the right side is with respect to a
n

W
started from
(x_{1},...,x_{n})
, and
\Phi^{(k)}
denotes the
k^{th}
derivative of
\Phi
.

The proof follows from using the Feynman-Kac representation of
Z^{\epsilon}
, see
[
BC95
, Section 2]
for an identical calculation (neither the factor
\epsilon^{2p-\frac{1}{2}}
nor the
(2p)^{th}
derivative on
\Phi_{\epsilon}
appear there, since their driving noise is just
\eta^{\epsilon}
as opposed to
\epsilon^{p-\frac{1}{4}}\partial_{y}^{p}\eta^{\epsilon}

\varphi_{\epsilon}
by
\epsilon^{p-\frac{1}{4}}\partial_{y}^{p}\varphi_{\epsilon}
, which precisely leads to the replacement
\Phi_{\epsilon}\to\epsilon^{2p-\frac{1}{2}}\Phi_{\epsilon}^{(2p)}
in the above moment formula). By Itô’s formula, note that we can rewrite this as

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\bigg[\prod_{j=1}^{n}Z_{0,t}^{\epsilon}(x_{j},y_{j})\bigg]\prod_{j=1}^{n}\phi(y_{j})dy\cdots dy_{n}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{BM}^{\otimes n}}^{(x_{1},...,x_{n})}\bigg[e^{\epsilon^{2p-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\Phi_{\epsilon}^{(2p-2)}(W^{i}_{t}-W^{j}_{t})-\int_{0}^{t}\Phi_{\epsilon}^{(2p-1)}(W^{i}_{s}-W^{j}_{s})d(W^{i}-W^{j})_{s}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\phi(W^{j}_{t})\bigg]

(3.2)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}^{(x_{1},...,x_{n})}_{\mathrm{Diff}(\epsilon)}\bigg[e^{\mathbf{T}^{\epsilon}_{t}(X)+\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\epsilon^{2p-\frac{1}{2}}\Phi_{\epsilon}^{(2p-2)}(X^{i}_{t}-X^{j}_{t})+\epsilon^{4p-1}\int_{0}^{t}\Phi_{\epsilon}^{(2p-1)}(X^{i}_{s}-X^{j}_{s})^{2}ds}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\phi(X^{j}_{t})\bigg]

where by Girsanov’s theorem the latter expectation is with respect to the
diffusion process
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, started from initial condition
(x_{1},...,x_{n})
, with dynamics given by

dX^{i}=\epsilon^{2p-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{j\neq i}\mathrm{sign}(i-j)\Phi_{\epsilon}^{(2p-1)}(X^{i}-X^{j})ds+dW^{i},

and where

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}_{t}^{\epsilon}(X)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{4p-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},j_{2}\neq i\\
j_{1}\neq j_{2}\end{subarray}}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{sign}(i-j_{1})\mathrm{sign}(i-j_{2})\prod_{k=1,2}\Phi^{(2p-1)}_{\epsilon}(X^{i}_{s}-X^{j_{k}}_{s})ds.

Definition 3.2
.

We say that a family of functions
u_{\epsilon}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{[}0,\infty)
with
0<\epsilon\leq 1
is an approximate Dirac mass if
u_{\epsilon}(y)=\delta^{-1}\psi(\delta^{-1}y)
for some continuous function
\psi
of compect support with
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\psi=1
.

Notice that the function
\epsilon^{4p-1}(\Phi_{\epsilon}^{(2p-1)})^{2}
appears in the exponential in (
3.2
). This family of functions is an approximate Dirac mass centered at the origin, multiplied by the deterministic constant
\sigma_{p}^{2}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\varphi*\varphi^{(2p-1)}(x))^{2}dx.
Meanwhile the process
\mathbf{T}^{\epsilon}_{t}(X)
will be shown to be irrelevant in the limit, which is because it only depends on “triple intersections” between the different coordinates
X^{1},...,X^{n}
. These are the the crucial observations in proving Theorem
1.2
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{BM}}^{x}
denote expectation with respect to a standard Brownian motion on
\mathbb{R}
, started from
x\in\mathbb{R}
. There exists some universal constant
C>0
, such that uniformly over all smooth functions
\phi\geq 0
with
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi=1,
and all
s,t,q\geq 0
, one has

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{E}^{x}_{\mathrm{BM}}\bigg[\bigg|\int_{s}^{t}\phi(B_{s})ds\bigg|^{q}\bigg]^{1/q}

\displaystyle\leq Cq^{1/2}|t-s|^{1/2}

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{E}^{x}_{\mathrm{BM}}[e^{q\int_{0}^{t}\phi(B_{s})ds}]

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{Cq^{2}t}.

Proof.

First note the trivial bound
\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{E}^{x}_{\mathrm{BM}}[\phi(B_{t})]\leq c\|\phi\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}t^{-1/2},
where
c=1/\sqrt{2\pi}
is universal. Thus if
\|\phi\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}=1
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we can iteratively apply the Markov property of Brownian motion
m
times to see that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}^{x}_{\mathrm{BM}}\bigg[\bigg(\int_{s}^{t}\phi(B_{s})ds\bigg)^{m}\bigg]

\displaystyle=m!\int_{s\leq s_{1}\leq s_{2}...\leq s_{m}\leq t}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\prod_{j=1}^{m}\phi(B_{s_{j}})\bigg]ds_{1}\cdots ds_{m}

\displaystyle\leq m!c^{m}\int_{s\leq s_{1}\leq s_{2}...\leq s_{m}\leq t}\prod_{j=0}^{m-1}(s_{i+1}-s_{i})^{-1/2}ds_{1}\cdots ds_{m}

\displaystyle\leq C^{m}(t-s)^{m/2}\Gamma(m/2),

where
\Gamma
is the Gamma function, and
C\geq c
is some larger but still universal constant. From here, the first bound is immediate. For the second bound, take
s=0
, then divide both sides by
m!
and sum over all
m
to obtain the required bound.
∎

Proposition 3.4

(Limit of the moments)
.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be bounded and continuous. For all
n\in\mathbb{N},
and all
(x_{1},...,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\prod_{j=1}^{n}Z_{0,t}^{\epsilon}(x_{j},y_{j})\bigg]\prod_{j=1}^{n}\phi(y_{j})dy\cdots dy_{n}=\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{BM}^{\otimes n}}^{(x_{1},...,x_{n})}\bigg[e^{\sigma_{p}^{2}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}L_{0}^{W^{i}-W^{j}}(t)}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\phi(W^{j}_{t})\bigg].

Here

with
\varphi
as in Theorem

. The above convergence is uniform on compact subsets of

Proof.

Using the diffusion representation explained after Proposition
3.1
above, we just need to calculate the limit as
\epsilon\to 0
of the expectation

(3.3)

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\mathbf{E}^{(x_{1},...,x_{n})}_{\mathrm{Diff}(\epsilon)}\bigg[e^{\mathbf{T}_{t}^{\epsilon}(X)+\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\epsilon^{2p-\frac{1}{2}}\Phi_{\epsilon}^{(2p-2)}(X^{i}_{t}-X^{j}_{t})+\epsilon^{4p-1}\int_{0}^{t}\Phi_{\epsilon}^{(2p-1)}(X^{i}_{s}-X^{j}_{s})^{2}ds}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\phi(X^{j}_{t})\bigg].

On one hand, one has a deterministic bound

, thus the first term in the exponential

is completely inconsequential in the limit, and will be ignored henceforth. To take the limit of the remaining parts, it is clear that we require a joint invariance principle for the entire collection of processes given by

where

For any continuous martingale

Thus if we let
\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{BM}}^{x}
denote expectation with respect to a standard Brownian motion

\mathbb{R}

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\epsilon\in(0,1]\\

where

Letting

\displaystyle M^{i}_{t}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}f(x,z)\delta^{-1}\psi(\delta^{-1}(y-w))Z^{\epsilon}_{t,u}(dy,dz)Z^{\epsilon}_{s,t}(dx,dw)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(x,z)Z^{\epsilon}_{s,u}(dx,dz)\bigg)^{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}

\displaystyle f(x,z)\delta^{-1}\psi(\delta^{-1}(y-w))Z^{\epsilon}_{t,u}(dy,dz)Z^{\epsilon}_{s,t}(dx,dw)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(x,z)Z^{\epsilon}_{s,u}(dx,dz)\bigg)^{2}

We can say therefore that (

Definition 4.1

Lemma 4.2

Let

Here the expectation on the right side is with respect to a diffusion process on

Note here that the mechanism that will give rise to the KPZ limit is completely different than that of Theorem

sing

Lemma 4.4

For any bounded measurable

Proposition 4.5

(Limit of the moments)
.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be bounded and continuous. For all
n\in\mathbb{N},
and all
(x_{1},...,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{Z}_{0,t}^{\epsilon}(x_{j},y_{j})\bigg]\prod_{j=1}^{n}\phi(y_{j})dy\cdots dy_{n}=\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{BM}^{\otimes n}}^{(x_{1},...,x_{n})}\bigg[e^{\gamma_{\mathrm{ext}}^{2}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}L_{0}^{W^{i}-W^{j}}(t)}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\phi(W^{j}_{t})\bigg].

Here
\gamma_{\mathrm{ext}}^{2}
is as in Theorem
1.3
. The above convergence is uniform on compact subsets of
(x_{1},...,x_{n})
.

The proof will be done using the diffusion representation of the moments from Lemma
4.2
. Section 2 of
[
BW22
]
suggests one method of calculating the
\epsilon\to 0
behavior of that diffusion using previously known results and Taylor expansions, though they only consider

and they do not write out the proof explicitly. We give a different ODE-based argument here (based on simplified ideas from
[

DDP23a
]
) that also keeps the discussion more self-contained.

Proof.

It is clear by Lemma
4.2
that we require a joint invariance principle for the entire collection of processes given by

\bigg(\big(X^{1}_{t},...,X^{n}_{t}\big),\big(\int_{0}^{t}\Phi_{\epsilon}(X^{i}_{s}-X^{j}_{s})ds\big)_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\bigg)_{t\in[0,T]},

under the measures
\mathbf{E}^{(x_{1},...,x_{n})}_{\mathrm{Sing}(\epsilon)}
as
\epsilon\to 0
. Specifically, we will now show that these processes have a
joint
limit in distribution given by

\bigg(\big(W^{1}_{t},...,W^{n}_{t}\big),\big(\gamma_{\mathrm{ext}}^{2}L_{0}^{W^{i}-W^{j}}(t)\big)_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\bigg)_{t\in[0,T]},

where
(W^{1},...,W^{n})
is a standard Wiener process and
L^{X}_{0}
is the local time process of the semimartingale
X
. To prove this, the main idea will be to first use Girsanov to “reduce” estimates for
\mathbf{E}^{(x_{1},...,x_{n})}_{\mathrm{Sing}(\epsilon)}
to estimates for the simpler process
\mathbf{E}^{(x_{1},...,x_{n})}_{\mathrm{Cen}(\epsilon)}
, then in turn use the rescaling property of Lemma
4.4
to reduce all estimates to the
\epsilon
-independent process
\mathbf{E}^{(x_{1},...,x_{n})}_{\mathrm{Cen}(1)}
, for which Lemma
4.3
already yields very strong bounds.

Apply Girsanov, and from the explicit expressions for the generators
L^{(n,\epsilon)}_{\mathrm{Sing}}
and
L^{(n,\epsilon)}_{\mathrm{Cen}}
we obtain the existence of a martingale
D
such that

(4.5)

\mathbf{E}^{(x_{1},...,x_{n})}_{\mathrm{Sing}(\epsilon)}[F(X)]=\mathbf{E}^{(x_{1},...,x_{n})}_{\mathrm{Cen}(\epsilon)}\big[e^{D_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\langle D\rangle_{t}}F(X)\big]

for all bounded measurable
F:C([0,t],\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\mathbb{R},
where one can check that the martingale
D
satisfies

(4.6)

\langle D\rangle_{t}\leq C\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\int_{0}^{t}\delta_{\epsilon}(X^{i}_{s}-X^{j}_{s})ds

for some absolute constant
C>0
and some function
\delta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
with
\int\delta=1
and
\delta_{\epsilon}(y):=\epsilon^{-1}\delta(\epsilon^{-1}y).

For any continuous martingale
G

\mathbb{E}[e^{2\langle G\rangle_{t}}]<\infty
for all

, then by Novikov’s condition
e^{2G_{t}-2\langle G\rangle_{t}}
is a martingale, thus we have the bound

\mathbb{E}[e^{G_{t}}]=\mathbb{E}[e^{G_{t}-\langle G\rangle_{t}+\langle G\rangle_{t}}]\leq\mathbb{E}[e^{2G_{t}-2\langle G\rangle_{t}}]^{1/2}\mathbb{E}[e^{2\langle G\rangle_{t}}]^{1/2}=\mathbb{E}[e^{2\langle G\rangle_{t}}]^{1/2}.

By Lemmas
4.3

4.4

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\epsilon\in(0,1]\\
(x_{1},...,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\end{subarray}}\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{Cen}(\epsilon)}^{(x_{1},...,x_{n})}\big[e^{q\int_{0}^{T}\delta_{\epsilon}(X^{i}_{s}-X^{j}_{s})ds}\big]\leq Ce^{q^{2}CT}<\infty,

where
C
is some universal constant. By Hölder’s inequality together with (
4.5
) and (
4.6
) and (
4.7
), the last expression easily implies that if

is any measurable path functional, then

\mathbf{E}^{(x_{1},...,x_{n})}_{\mathrm{Sing}(\epsilon)}[F(X)]\leq C\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{Cen}(\epsilon)}^{(x_{1},...,x_{n})}[F(X)^{2}]^{1/2}

where
C
is an
absolute constant
independent of
\epsilon\in(0,1]
and
F
(specifically

is an upper bound, uniform over all
\epsilon
, for the
L^{2}
norm of the Radon-Nikodym derivative of
\mathbf{E}^{(x_{1},...,x_{n})}_{\mathrm{Sing}(\epsilon)}
with respect to
\mathbf{E}^{(x_{1},...,x_{n})}_{\mathrm{Cen}(\epsilon)}
). This will be a very useful fact going forward.

Now notice that under
\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{Cen}(\epsilon)}^{(x_{1},...,x_{n})},
the process
X
is a martingale and by explicit calculation one has
\partial_{t}\langle X^{i}\rangle_{t}\leq C
for some deterministic constant
C
, thus by Burkholder-Davis-Gundy, one has the estimate
\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{Cen}(\epsilon)}^{(x_{1},...,x_{n})}[|X_{t}^{i}-X_{s}^{j}|^{q}]^{1/q}\leq C|t-s|^{1/2},
from which (
4.8
) immediately implies

(4.9)

\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{Sing}(\epsilon)}^{(x_{1},...,x_{n})}[|X_{t}^{i}-X_{s}^{j}|^{q}]^{1/q}\leq C|t-s|^{1/2}.

Now notice that Lemmas
4.3
and
4.4
imply the bound
\mathbf{E}^{(x_{1},...,x_{n})}_{\mathrm{Cen}(\epsilon)}\big[\big|\int_{s}^{t}\Phi_{\epsilon}(X^{i}_{u}-X^{j}_{u})du\big|^{q}\big]^{1/q}\leq C|t-s|^{1/2}

(4.10)

\mathbf{E}^{(x_{1},...,x_{n})}_{\mathrm{Sing}(\epsilon)}\big[\big|\int_{s}^{t}\Phi_{\epsilon}(X^{i}_{u}-X^{j}_{u})du\big|^{q}\big]^{1/q}\leq C|t-s|^{1/2}.

First let us show that

by Levy criterion.

It remains to investigate the process-level behavior of
\big(\int_{0}^{\bullet}\Phi_{\epsilon}(X^{i}_{s}-X^{j}_{s})ds\big)_{1\leq i<j\leq n}

\mathbf{E}^{(x_{1},...,x_{n})}_{\mathrm{Sing}(\epsilon)}

\epsilon\to 0

denote its generator. Let

It is immediate (e.g. from L’Hôpital’s rule) that

\lim_{y\to+\infty}y^{-1}\Psi(y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Psi^{\prime\prime}(u)du=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\varphi*\varphi(u)}{1-\varphi*\varphi(u)}du=\gamma_{\mathrm{ext}}^{2},

which is the key observation. In particular
\Psi_{(\epsilon)}(y):=\epsilon\Psi(\epsilon^{-1}y)
converges uniformly on compacts to the function
x\mapsto\gamma_{\mathrm{ext}}^{2}\max\{0,x\}.
Define
\Gamma^{ij}_{\epsilon}(x_{1},...,x_{n}):=\sum_{k\neq\ell}\Phi_{\epsilon}(x_{k}-x_{\ell})\cdot\partial_{k}\big(\Psi_{(\epsilon)}(x_{i}-x_{j})\big).
Using Dynkin’s formula, one checks that in the prelimit the process

N^{ij}_{t}:=\Psi_{(\epsilon)}(X^{i}_{t}-X^{j}_{t})-\int_{0}^{t}\Phi_{\epsilon}(X^{i}_{s}-X^{j}_{s})ds-\epsilon^{1/2}\int_{0}^{t}\Gamma^{ij}_{\epsilon}(X^{1}_{s},...,X^{n}_{s})ds

is a
\mathbf{E}^{(x_{1},...,x_{n})}_{\mathrm{Sing}(\epsilon)}
-martingale for
1\leq i<j\leq n
, where the middle term on the right side comes from the second-order part of the generator
L^{(n,\epsilon)}_{\mathrm{Sing}}
because
G_{\mathrm{diff}}\Psi_{(1)}=\Phi_{1}
, and the last term comes from the first-order (drift) part of
L^{(n,\epsilon)}_{\mathrm{Sing}}
. Notice that
\sup_{\epsilon\in(0,1]}|\Psi_{(\epsilon)}^{\prime}|\leq C
for some absolute constant
C
, thus by (
4.10
) one sees that the last term goes to zero in law in the topology of
C[0,T]
, under
\mathbf{E}^{(x_{1},...,x_{n})}_{\mathrm{Sing}(\epsilon)}
as
\epsilon\to 0
.

Let
(W^{1},...,W^{n},(\mathscr{V}^{ij})_{1\leq i<j\leq n})
be a joint limit point of the full tuple of
\frac{1}{2}n(n+1)
processes given by
\big((X^{1}_{t},...,X^{n}_{t}),(\int_{0}^{t}\Phi_{\epsilon}(X^{i}_{s}-X^{j}_{s})ds\big)_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\big)_{t\in[0,T]}
under the measures
\mathbf{E}^{(x_{1},...,x_{n})}_{\mathrm{Sing}(\epsilon)}
as
\epsilon\to 0
. We already proved that the marginal law of
(W^{1},...,W^{n})
must be a Brownian motion. Since martingality is preserved by limit points as long as uniform integrability holds, the martingality of
N^{ij}
in the prelimit implies that
2\gamma_{\mathrm{ext}}^{2}\max\{0,W^{i}_{t}-W^{j}_{t}\}-\mathscr{V}^{ij}_{t}
is a continuous martingale for the limit point. By Tanaka’s formula, this forces
\mathscr{V}^{ij}_{t}=\gamma_{\mathrm{ext}}^{2}L_{0}^{W^{i}-W^{j}}(t)
, for
all

1\leq i<j\leq n,
thus uniquely identifying the law of the full tuple of
\frac{1}{2}n(n+1)
processes and completing the proof of the invariance principle.

Finally, we take the limit in the proposition statement. Using the invariance principle, and the uniform integrability guaranteed by Lemma
4.3
and Equation (
4.8
), one has

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\mathbf{E}^{(x_{1},...,x_{n})}_{\mathrm{Sing}(\epsilon)}\bigg[e^{\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\int_{0}^{t}\Phi_{\epsilon}(X^{i}_{s}-X^{j}_{s})ds}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\phi(X^{j}_{t})\bigg]=\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{BM}^{\otimes n}}^{(x_{1},...,x_{n})}\bigg[e^{\gamma_{\mathrm{ext}}^{2}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}L_{0}^{W^{i}-W^{j}}(t)}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\phi(W^{j}_{t})\bigg].

The convergence is uniform on compacts because allowing
x_{i}=x_{i}^{\epsilon}
to vary within some bounded set as
\epsilon\to 0
will not affect the invariance principle or the uniform integrability in any way.
∎
