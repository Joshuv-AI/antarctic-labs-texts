Late finding of a dermoid cyst in a posterior fossa intra-axial lesion: A rare case report
Authors: Rohadi Rohadi, Kadek Dede Frisky Wiyanjana, Agung Bagus Sista Satyarsa, Dewa Putu Wisnu Wardhana, Nyoman Golden, Januarman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Intracranial dermoid cysts are rare congenital inclusion cysts derived from ectodermal elements and account for a very small proportion of intracranial lesions. Although they are usually slow-growing and may remain clinically silent for years, they can become symptomatic once they exert mass effect or obstruct cerebrospinal fluid pathways. Posterior fossa involvement is uncommon, and delayed clinical presentation may occur despite long-standing lesion growth. This report describes a posterior fossa dermoid cyst causing obstructive hydrocephalus in an adolescent patient and highlights the radiological, histopathological, and surgical considerations relevant to its management.Case Description: The patient, a 16-year-old female, presented with a 3-year history of progressive visual impairment, daily vomiting, fatigue, and loss of appetite. Radiological studies, including computerized tomography and magnetic resonance imaging scans, revealed a midline cystic lesion within the fourth ventricle, leading to active obstructive hydrocephalus. Clinical manifestations included signs of increased intracranial pressure and fourth ventricle narrowing. Histopathological examination of tissue fragments from the cyst wall demonstrated a lining of squamous epithelium with evidence of parakeratosis. Furthermore, the necessity for careful consideration of surgical techniques is underscored, as dermoid cysts could exhibit adherence to arachnoid tissue. Intraoperatively, the lesion was approached through a posterior fossa craniotomy, and cyst decompression/ excision was performed with careful microsurgical dissection because the capsule was adherent to surrounding arachnoid and neural structures.Conclusion: This report emphasizes the significance of early diagnosis and surgical intervention in managing the intricate challenges posed by posterior fossa dermoid cysts.

INTRODUCTION

The dermoid cyst is an embryonic anomaly that results from the entrapment of ectodermal elements during formation. It is lined with multilayered squamous epithelium and contains mature skin layers, keratin, hair, and various ectodermal derivatives, such as apocrine sweat glands, sebaceous glands, hair follicles, squamous epithelium, and potentially teeth.[1,17] Dermoid cysts typically appear within the 1st year of life, exhibiting slow growth, and with several cases occurring in the head and neck.[4,17] Intracranial dermoid cysts are uncommon congenital inclusion cysts composed of ectodermally derived squamous epithelial lining and dermal appendages. They should be distinguished from epidermoid cysts, which are lined by squamous epithelium but lack dermal appendages, and from teratomas, which contain tissues derived from more than one germ cell layer.[4] The etiology of intracranial dermoid cysts remains largely undetermined, and the causative factors for these congenital developmental anomalies have not been conclusively established.[18] Dermoid cysts within the cranial cavity are exceedingly rare, constituting <1% of all intracranial tumors.[5] Commonly congenital in nature, 70% of dermoid cysts are found in children aged 5 years or younger, although cases in adults have also been reported.[11,15,20] Due to their slow growth, some lesions may remain undiagnosed until adolescence or adulthood, particularly when symptoms evolve gradually. In addition, no previous significant gender dominance has been observed. There has been no known apparent racial predilection, as the majority of cases are documented in individuals of Caucasian descent.[7,17] Dermoid cysts are predominantly found within the cisternal spaces, particularly in the cerebellopontine angle and parasellar cisterns.[21] Malignant transformation into squamous cell carcinoma is exceedingly rare, but it has been reported in the literature to occur in approximately 5% of cases, primarily in slowly growing lesions that typically manifest in young adults.[19]

Dermoid cysts typically remain asymptomatic but may manifest a variety of different symptoms, especially when rupture occurs. It is believed that the secondary rupture of dermoid cysts results from the production of hair and oil by internal skin elements, leading to increased pressure. However, cyst rupture can also be preceded by trauma.[16,23] There are two primary reasons for clinical symptoms caused by dermoid cyst rupture. One is the impact of the lesion on surrounding structures, and the other is aseptic meningitis, vasospasm, and nerve injury induced by the contents of the ruptured cyst affecting the meninges, blood vessels, and adjacent nerves.[23] The incidence of ruptured intracranial dermoid cysts remains unclear, and the exact cause is unknown. Over 12 years, ruptured dermoid cysts accounted for only 0.18% of all tumors resected from the central nervous system (CNS).[10] Focal erosion of the calvarial bone may occur in proximity to dermoid tumors on the scalp or orbit. Specifically, dermoid tumors within the skull can lead to local bone expansion or erosion, resulting in round, intradiploic lytic lesions with thin sclerotic borders. Parasellar dermoid tumors can cause sellar deformity. Spinal dermoid tumors may lead to focal vertebral bone erosion, widening of the spinal canal, and flattening of pedicles and laminae.[4] Large calvarial dermoids are visible on plain skull radiographs, but computerized tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) provide precise intracranial lesion localization and imaging features. The CT scans are useful for initial CNS dermoid tumor diagnosis. They appear as well-defined, predominantly cystic masses with decreased attenuation due to fat content. The MRI shows a unilocular cystic mass near the cranial midline with fat-like signal intensity. Most dermoid tumors have fat-like signal intensity characteristics on MRI, but fat-suppression techniques confirm their fat content. Ruptured dermoids may cause fat droplets in the subarachnoid space or ventricles.[10,12] Radiologically, dermoid cysts are often considered in the differential diagnosis together with epidermoid cysts and teratomas; however, the presence of fatty components and histopathological confirmation remains essential for definitive diagnosis. In addition, histological diagnosis is obtained through craniotomy and cyst excision. Dermoid tumors are round masses with a hard outer capsule, filled with sebaceous material, keratin, and hair, lined with multilayered squamous epithelium and subepithelial skin components.[11]

We present a case of a posterior fossa intracranial cerebellar dermoid cyst that was discovered at a later age. The educational value of this case lies not solely in its rarity but in the diagnostic challenge posed by the imaging findings, the need to differentiate dermoid cyst from other inclusion cysts and teratomatous lesions, and the operative nuances required for safe resection in the posterior fossa.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 16-year-old female patient was referred to the Neurosurgery Outpatient Clinic at RSUD Provinsi Nusa Tenggara Barat due to progressive visual impairment experienced over the course of 3 years. Initially, the visual disturbance was limited to long-distance vision but gradually encompassed near vision, significantly affecting her academic performance. The symptoms were not influenced by any specific triggers and were resistant to amelioration. Concurrently, the patient reported chronic headaches persisting for 2 years, with an intensity rating of 7–8 on the Visual Analog Scale (VAS). Over the recent 3 months, the patient began experiencing daily vomiting, accompanied by sensations of fatigue, primarily following her school attendance. Importantly, the patient had a documented history of obstructive hydrocephalus attributed to a cerebellar tumor and had undergone ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt placement on August 4, 2023. The patient denied any family history of similar conditions. As a high school student, the patient had noted limitations in her daily activities, citing persistent blurry vision, frequent dizziness, and a reduced appetite since the onset of her symptoms. A general physical examination revealed results within normal limits. The neurological examination demonstrated a Glasgow Coma Scale score of E4V5M6, indicating the patient’s consciousness and responsiveness. Ocular examination of Cranial Nerve II revealed severe visual impairment in both eyes (1/60) with features of papilledema, including round papillae, blurred margins, and hyperemia. Cranial Nerves III, IV, and VI examinations showed limitations in eye movement, while no reflex, motor, or sensory deficits were identified.

In terms of MRI evaluation, axial T1-weighted without contrast [Figure 1a], T1-weighted with contrast [Figure 1b], T2-weighted, fluid attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), and diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), along with Coronal T2-weighted, Sagittal T2-weighted sequences, both with and without contrast, revealed a well-defined hypointense lesion with regular peripheral margins on T1, T2, FLAIR, and hyperintense nodules within it on DWI [Figure 1c]. No restricted diffusion areas were noted. The lesion, measuring approximately 7 cm × 6.4 cm × 5.8 cm in the posterior fossa, led to ventricular compression and obstructive hydrocephalus. Heterogeneous contrast enhancement was evident within the nodules. Bilateral lateral ventricles displayed hyperintensity, while sulci and gyri appeared normal. No midline structural deviations were noted, and the pons appeared unremarkable. Other regions, including the mastoid, orbits, right paranasal sinuses, and subcutaneous scalp lesions, displayed no abnormal findings. The imaging also indicated the presence of a VP shunt, with its tip positioned in the right lateral ventricle. A primary brain tumor located in the posterior fossa, highly suspicious of hemangioblastoma, was identified. This tumor, measuring 7 cm × 6.4 cm × 5.8 cm, exerted significant pressure on the fourth ventricle, leading to active obstructive hydrocephalus. An epidermoid inclusion cyst was detected in the left occipital region. Nevertheless, the circle of Willis appeared patent, with no evidence of aneurysms or vascular malformations.

Dermatoid cyst from magnetic resonance imaging examination. Axial head slices, (a) T1-weighted both without contrast, (b) T1-weighted with contrast, and (c) T2-weighted.

The patient subsequently underwent a posterior fossa craniotomy for tumor removal. After dural opening, the lesion was identified and internally decompressed to reduce mass effect and facilitate dissection. The cyst contents were evacuated carefully, followed by gradual capsular dissection under microscopic visualization. Particular attention was paid to the interface between the capsule and surrounding arachnoid/neurovascular structures because of adhesiveness in the operative field. Copious irrigation was performed to minimize spillage of cyst contents. Resection was pursued as safely as possible while avoiding injury to critical posterior fossa structures. The specimen was then submitted for histopathological examination.

Histopathological findings revealed that macroscopically, the received specimen weighed 17 g and consisted of irregularly shaped tissue fragments with dimensions ranging from 0.5 cm × 0.2 cm × 0.1 cm to 2.5 cm × 2.5 cm × 1.8 cm [Figure 2]. The tissue displayed a greyish-white color, a solid elastic consistency, and was prone to fragility. Microscopically, the tissue fragments comprising the cyst wall were lined with squamous epithelium showing evidence of parakeratosis. The stroma exhibited a loose structure and contained keratin flakes. These findings led to the definitive diagnosis of a dermoid cyst. No immature elements or tissues derived from multiple germ layers were identified in the examined specimen, supporting the diagnosis of dermoid cyst rather than teratoma.

DISCUSSION

Dermoid cysts account for a mere 0.1–0.7% of all intracranial tumors and develop during the closure of the neural tube, typically between the 3rd and 4th week of embryonic life.[8] These cysts are lined with keratinizing squamous epithelium, representing a rare, benign, and slow-growing neoplasm originating from ectodermal elements. They evolve through the aggregation of desquamation products and sebaceous secretions within a cystic cavity, rather than cell division, distinguishing them from true neoplasms.[2]

The 16-year-old patient experienced recurring headaches for 2 years without seizures. Although an intracranial dermoid cyst is an uncommon entity, the present case should be interpreted primarily as an instructive diagnostic and surgical case rather than as an exceptionally unique presentation. Its relevance lies in the delayed diagnosis, posterior fossa mass effect with obstructive hydrocephalus, and the preoperative radiological differential diagnosis. The late discovery of dermoid cyst cases is uncommon, as they typically present with detrimental symptoms as they reach a significant size. Nevertheless, this patient managed to survive until the age of 16 despite the condition. Furthermore, the presence of intracranial dermoid cysts located in the posterior fossa is rare. The patient has previously been treated for obstructive hydrocephalus due to a cerebellar tumor with VP shunt placement on August 4, 2023. The patient has also experienced vomiting for the past 3 months, likely related to hydrocephalus. As previously mentioned, dermoid cysts in the posterior fossa may lead to hydrocephalus, causing increased intracranial pressure with associated symptoms, including headaches, vomiting, blurred vision, and impaired eye movement.[21] One possible explanation for their presence in the posterior fossa is that intracranial dermoid cysts tend to be located at the extremes of the neuraxis, typically in proximity to the midline.[7] Radiologically, a head CT scan revealed a 5 cm × 4 cm inhomogeneous hyperdense mass in the fourth ventricle, while a brain MRI showed characteristic features, with a subcutaneous scalp lesion in the left occipital region. The MRI findings in this case also display an atypical pattern. It exhibits mixed signal intensities with a predominant hyperintense quality. Such atypical radiological features may be indicative of the varied content within dermoid cysts, which can lead to diverse signal characteristics on imaging. In addition, histopathology confirmed the diagnosis as a dermoid cyst.

A major point in the differential diagnosis is the distinction from epidermoid cyst and teratoma. Epidermoid cysts are also ectodermal inclusion cysts, but they classically lack dermal appendages and usually contain desquamated keratinaceous material. Teratomas, in contrast, contain tissues derived from multiple germ layers and therefore represent a different pathological category. In the present case, the histopathological finding of squamous epithelial lining with keratinous material, together with the absence of immature tissues or heterologous elements from multiple germ layers, supported the diagnosis of dermoid cyst rather than teratoma. Therefore, definitive diagnosis in such lesions should not rely on imaging alone, especially when preoperative radiology suggests other posterior fossa tumors.

Dermoid cysts are generally benign and carry a favorable prognosis. Surgical intervention is indicated when the cyst exerts a significant mass effect or results in severe neurological deficits. In this case, the patient underwent cyst excision via craniotomy on September 7, 2023, and a histopathological examination of the specimen confirmed the diagnosis as a dermoid cyst. In cases of intracranial dermoid cysts, recurrence rates of up to 9% have been reported following lesion excision.[13] While the present case encompasses a benign intracranial lesion, it can lead to compression of nearby neurological structures and blood vessels.[6] Furthermore, if left unaddressed, it may culminate in intracranial hypertension when expanding,[9] underscoring the critical importance of timely diagnosis and intervention. Histologically benign, dermoid cysts can grow and erode the skull, making them potentially susceptible to epidural extension. In cases of intracranial or intraspinal expansion, the overall prognosis remains favorable with timely and appropriate surgical intervention. However, in rare instances when dermoid cysts become symptomatic due to localized mass, effects, rupture, infection, or even, in rare cases, cause brain compression, the prognosis may turn unfavorable.[4,6,17]

Dermoid cysts typically exhibit a slow growth tendency, potentially causing bone deformities and intracranial or intraspinal extension. Intracranial or intraspinal extension may cause meningitis or abscess formation. Dermoid cysts of small size and asymptomatic nature may not require immediate excision since they may remain stable for years or even regress over time.[6,11,20] In most cases, dermoid cysts grow over time, requiring a complete surgical excision before complications arise. An accurate direct surgical approach at the site where a dermoid cyst adheres to bone is the most effective method for removing dermoid cysts. If the cyst wall ruptures during removal surgery, any remaining tissue should be extracted through curettage and irrigation.[6,3] The surgical approach for dermoid cysts in the posterior fossa necessitates careful consideration due to their inherent adhesive properties to the arachnoid tissue. These lesions often infiltrate and adhere to the surrounding neural and vascular structures, making their complete resection challenging. In cases where the cyst wall attaches to vital structures, partial excision may be performed. Recurrence of dermoid cysts has been observed in cases of incomplete excision. In instances of intracranial expansion of the cyst, craniotomy may still be necessary. Total resection is a reasonable consideration if the location is favorable to prevent seizures and send samples for histopathological diagnosis.[14] However, in cases where the tumor is located in suprasellar or cerebellopontine angle areas, severe adhesions between the tumor, cranial nerves, brain stem, and blood vessels may complicate total tumor resection, and in such situations, excessive resection should not be an option.[22] Excessive caution must be exercised during intraoperative cyst manipulation to prevent the spread of cyst contents and postoperative chemical meningitis. Clinical and radiological diagnoses are crucial for accurate intracranial dermoid cyst diagnosis, with surgical resection as the treatment of choice.[4,23] Successful surgical outcomes depend on a comprehensive understanding of the lesion’s location and its relationship to adjacent neurovascular structures, enabling the neurosurgeon to navigate the complexities of resection while preserving neurological function.

CONCLUSION

Dermoid cysts are non-neoplastic, congenital ectodermal inclusion cysts that contain various ectodermal derivatives, including apocrine, sweat, and sebaceous glands, hair follicles, squamous epithelium, and possibly even teeth. Despite their benign nature, these lesions can still lead to several serious complications. The author reports this case to enable early diagnosis and better management. The MRI is superior to CT in diagnosing these lesions as it provides comprehensive information regarding their extent. Surgical resection is the primary treatment option, involving the removal of a sample for diagnosis. However, the potential risk of injury to vital surrounding structures must be carefully considered. Complications related to the procedure should be closely monitored during the perioperative period. The recurrence rate after resection is very low, but cases of incomplete resection require regular follow-up.
