A Candidate Syndromic Triad of Acne Keloidalis Nuchae, Dissecting Cellulitis, and Secondary Cutis Verticis Gyrata
Authors: Sanusi Umar, Ochanya Ogah, Jason Yang, Belinda H Tan, Nancy Aiead, Paul K Shitabata
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeAcne keloidalis nuchae (AKN) affects the posterior hairline/nape; dissecting cellulitis of the scalp (DCS) is a suppurative scarring alopecia; and cutis verticis gyrata (CVG) describes scalp folds that may arise from chronic inflammation. Introduction

Acne keloidalis nuchae (AKN) is a chronic folliculocentric inflammatory disorder typically localized to the posterior hairline and nape and may progress from papules and pustules to hypertrophic plaques or tumorous scarring masses.1 Dissecting cellulitis of the scalp (DCS) is a relapsing inflammatory disorder characterized clinically by boggy nodules/abscesses, drainage, and scarring alopecia and is classically grouped among follicular occlusion–associated disorders.2–4 In contrast, AKN is classified as a primary cicatricial alopecia (PCA) and is not included in the classic follicular occlusion triad/tetrad.

Cutis verticis gyrata (CVG) describes cerebriform scalp folding with ridges and furrows. Secondary CVG may occur in chronic inflammatory or infiltrative conditions. In a prior cohort of AKN, extensive CVG occurred in 3.7% of patients and was associated with more aggressive and extensive AKN when CVG developed within two years of AKN onset.5 Conversely, furrows and gyri have been described as a prominent presenting morphology in DCS.4

AKN may overlap with other PCAs, including folliculitis decalvans (FD), supporting a possible clinical continuum in some patients.6 Prior AKN cohorts have occasionally reported comorbid DCS or chronic scalp folliculitis (approximately 1–6%), but standardized whole-scalp examination, scalp-zone mapping, and histopathologic confirmation were often absent.1,7,8 Consequently, published estimates may be vulnerable to misclassification among DCS, FD, and severe posterior AKN and may not distinguish localized occipital involvement from extensive multisite scalp disease. Conversely, large DCS series emphasize acne conglobata and hidradenitis suppurativa and have not consistently documented AKN, suggesting that posterior hairline phenotypes may be overlooked when assessment is centered on suppurative scalp lesions.2,3

Here, we describe three men with a clinicopathologically corroborated constellation of AKN, scalp-wide DCS, and extensive secondary CVG identified within a consecutive DCS-evaluated cohort. Unlike prior reports, this series combines standardized full-scalp and posterior hairline/nape examination, scalp-zone mapping, trichoscopy, and multisite biopsy in the same patients. We propose the constellation as a candidate syndromic triad and highlight perifollicular infundibulo-isthmic lymphocytic inflammation with fibrosis (PIILIF) in clinically normal-appearing scalp (cNAS).9–15 Recognition may prompt reciprocal screening: full-scalp evaluation for DCS in patients with AKN and deliberate posterior hairline/nape examination for AKN in patients with DCS.

Patients and Methods

Study Design and Setting

This retrospective, hypothesis-generating case series was performed at a single hair disorders clinic in Los Angeles, California.

Case Identification and Definitions

This retrospective case series included consecutive men evaluated for suspected or confirmed DCS at a single hair-disorders clinic (Los Angeles, California) between December 2022 and November 2024. The Western Institutional Review Board (WIRB, Copernicus Group) granted exemption from full review because the analysis used de-identified data derived from routine clinical care with no intent to re-identify or contact participants. All patients provided written informed consent, including permission to use and publish anonymized clinical photographs. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (1964) and its later amendments.

All 12 consecutive men evaluated for suspected or confirmed DCS during the study interval underwent standardized full-scalp and posterior hairline/nape evaluation as part of routine care, including trichoscopy. The three cases reported here constituted the prespecified subset that met all triad criteria. The full-cohort clinicopathologic analysis is reported in a published companion article in Clinical, Cosmetic and Investigational Dermatology.16

Triad cases were defined a priori as patients meeting all of the following: (1) clinicopathologically confirmed AKN in the posterior hairline/nape; (2) clinicopathologically confirmed scalp-wide DCS involving at least 3 scalp zones (frontal, vertex, parietal, or occipital); and (3) clinically extensive scalp furrowing and gyri consistent with secondary CVG involving at least 3 scalp zones. A patient was not classified as a triad case if any required component lacked the specified confirmation or if a more persuasive alternative scalp diagnosis explained the findings. Race and ethnicity were self-reported.

Clinical Assessment

All patients underwent standardized examination of the entire scalp and posterior neck. Trichoscopy was performed in representative lesional and cNAS zones to identify perifollicular abnormalities (eg, erythema, scale or casts, and tufting) and guide exact site selection. AKN was staged using the 3-tier classification system described by Umar et al.1 CVG was assessed clinically based on diffuse scalp ridging and furrowing. History and examination were used to consider alternative contributors to secondary CVG, including long-standing inflammatory dermatoses, scalp trauma, and grooming-related mechanical factors.

Clinicopathologic Adjudication

Because sinus tracts are sampling-dependent and may be absent in limited biopsy sections, the diagnosis of DCS did not require histologic demonstration of sinus tracts. DCS was diagnosed when (a) the clinical phenotype showed fluctuant boggy nodules or abscesses with clinically evident drainage openings and scarring alopecia across at least 3 scalp zones and (b) lesional scalp histology showed suppurative, neutrophil-rich folliculitis with follicular occlusion or rupture, keratinaceous debris, foreign-body reaction, and associated dermal fibrosis, supporting DCS rather than isolated AKN at the posterior hairline.

Histopathology and Immunohistochemistry

The standardized sampling framework prespecified 3 diagnostic compartments: (1) a representative nuchal lesion for AKN confirmation, (2) a representative inflammatory scalp lesion for DCS confirmation, and (3) a cNAS site to assess subclinical perifollicular changes. Within each compartment, the exact 6-mm punch biopsy site was selected by clinician judgment using clinical morphology and trichoscopy. cNAS sites were 2–3 cm from visible inflammatory lesions and lacked gross erythema, pustules, drainage, or tenderness. Specimens were sectioned vertically and transversely.17,18 cNAS denotes scalp without visible alopecia, scarring, or inflammation on inspection. Two board-certified dermatopathologists reviewed all specimens, and discordances were resolved by consensus. When perifollicular fibrosis was subtle, alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) immunohistochemistry was used to highlight early fibroblast activity.19 CD4, CD8, and CD117 immunostaining was performed to characterize T-cell subsets and mast-cell prominence. Representative triad-case panels adapted from the published companion article are shown in Supplementary Figure S1, and detailed cohort-wide comparisons among cNAS, clinically inactive nodules, and active DCS lesions are reported in that article.16

Data Collection and Analysis

For triad cases, chart review captured demographic variables, comorbid follicular occlusion-associated disorders (eg, hidradenitis suppurativa), patient-reported chronology by anatomic site (nape vs scalp), DCS and CVG distribution by scalp zone, and available follow-up. Analyses were descriptive. No inferential statistical testing or formal power calculation was performed. The 3-of-12 source-cohort observation reflects case ascertainment within a small referral-enriched cohort and was not interpreted as a prevalence estimate.

Ethics

Ethical approval/exemption and consent to publish for clinical details and images are described in the Ethics and Consent section.

Results

Three patients met the prespecified triad criteria among the 12 consecutive men evaluated for suspected or confirmed DCS. This descriptive case-ascertainment observation from a small, single-center, referral-enriched cohort should not be interpreted as a population or clinic prevalence estimate. The patients were aged 30–40 years; 2 self-identified as Hispanic and 1 as African American. All had clinically extensive CVG, scalp-wide inflammatory boggy nodules and scarring alopecia consistent with DCS, and clinicopathologically confirmed AKN at the posterior hairline/nape. All 3 reported posterior hairline disease before recognition of diffuse scalp inflammation and furrowing by approximately 1–2 years. Key features are summarized in Table 1.

Source-Cohort Context and Immunophenotype

The companion 12-patient report identified PIILIF in all cNAS biopsies (12/12) and all clinically inactive nodules (3/3), while active lesions showed suppurative inflammation and remodeling superimposed on PIILIF.16 It also identified CD4-predominant T-cell infiltrates with prominent CD117-positive mast cells in DCS lesions and cNAS/PIILIF.16 Within the 3 triad cases, a similar immunophenotype was observed across AKN, DCS, and cNAS specimens; representative triad-case panels are shown in Supplementary Figure S1.

Case Presentations

Patient 1

A 31-year-old Hispanic man presented with a 15-year history of pruritic papules and pustules at the posterior hairline/nape, followed by diffuse scalp pain, fluctuant boggy nodules with intermittent drainage, and progressive scalp folding (Figure 4A–C). Comorbidities included severe facial acne, pseudofolliculitis barbae, prediabetes, hypertension, and obesity (BMI, 44.0). His father had severe cystic acne, chronic inflammatory scalp disease with dispersed boggy lesions, diffuse scalp furrowing consistent with CVG, and a posterior hairline linear plaque suggestive of AKN (Figure 5A–C). Because histologic confirmation was unavailable, the father’s presentation was considered a clinically suspected but unconfirmed triad-like phenotype. Examination of Patient 1 showed class II tumorous, positive AKN1 at the nape, diffuse scalp nodules, and extensive CVG. He also had bilateral discharging sinuses over the upper shoulders near the neck fold, consistent with hidradenitis suppurativa. Nape and scalp biopsies confirmed AKN and DCS, respectively; cNAS biopsy demonstrated PIILIF.

Discussion

In this small retrospective, referral-enriched case series, three men demonstrated a reproducible constellation of clinicopathologically confirmed AKN, scalp-wide DCS, and extensive secondary CVG. The main clinical implication is reciprocal: new diffuse boggy or draining scalp nodules and furrowing in AKN should prompt full-scalp assessment for DCS, whereas posterior hairline/nape papules, plaques, or masses in DCS should prompt evaluation for AKN. Because the source cohort was small and selected in a specialty clinic, the observation cannot establish prevalence or broad generalizability.

Secondary CVG has been associated with severe AKN and high disease burden.5 Furrows and gyri have also been described in DCS.4 Coexistent DCS has been reported infrequently in AKN cohorts, but retrospective ascertainment, limited scalp mapping, and absent histopathologic confirmation constrain interpretation.1,7,8 Conversely, major DCS series have not consistently documented AKN, possibly because attention is centered on suppurative scalp lesions.2–4 Standardized examination of the full scalp and posterior hairline/nape, trichoscopy, scalp-zone mapping, and multisite biopsy may reduce this anatomic-focus bias. Misclassification among DCS, FD, and severe posterior AKN remains possible, reinforcing the need for deliberate clinicopathologic adjudication.20

Histologic sampling is an additional challenge. Sinus tracts may be missed in limited punch sections and were not identified in this series. DCS adjudication therefore required the combination of a classic clinical phenotype, defined by fluctuant boggy nodules with drainage and scarring alopecia across at least 3 zones, and supportive lesional histology showing suppurative neutrophilic folliculitis, follicular rupture, and foreign-body reaction. AKN lesions were confined to the posterior hairline/nape and showed fibrosing folliculitis with follicular effacement and foreign-body granulomatous reaction.

The reproducibility of the clinicopathologic constellation across three patients supports consideration of AKN-DCS-CVG as a candidate syndromic triad. However, the present series does not establish a distinct syndrome, and secondary CVG may represent downstream fibrotic remodeling in patients with severe overlapping AKN and DCS. The designation is therefore provisional and requires validation in larger, independently ascertained cohorts.

All 3 triad cases demonstrated PIILIF-pattern changes in cNAS. Considered with the source-cohort findings in cNAS, clinically inactive nodules, and active DCS lesions, this recurring upper-follicle lymphocytic-fibrotic pattern is compatible with a scalp-wide inflammatory-fibrotic field effect.16 However, the cross-sectional data cannot determine whether PIILIF precedes, follows, or coexists with clinically apparent AKN or DCS.9–16 The source cohort also found CD4-predominant T-cell infiltrates and prominent CD117-positive mast cells in DCS lesions and cNAS/PIILIF.16 Within the triad cases, a similar pattern was observed in AKN specimens. These findings provide adjunct biologic coherence but are not disease-specific and remain hypothesis-generating.

Patient 1 also reported a first-degree relative with a clinically suspected but unconfirmed triad-like phenotype. The relative had severe cystic acne, chronic inflammatory boggy scalp lesions suggestive of DCS, diffuse scalp furrowing consistent with CVG, and a posterior hairline plaque suggestive of AKN (Figure 5). Histologic confirmation was unavailable, so a diagnosis of the triad could not be assigned. Familial DCS, including affected siblings and monozygotic twins,21,22 and familial AKN23 have been reported. This single observation raises the possibility of shared susceptibility but does not establish heritability and warrants systematic evaluation.

Proposed Pathobiology (Hypothesis-Generating)

The shared PIILIF pattern raises the possibility of a broader inflammatory-fibrotic scalp substrate on which site-specific AKN and DCS phenotypes develop. The source-cohort report provides a more detailed hypothesis-generating framework in which chronic upper-follicle lymphocytic-fibrotic inflammation may coexist with episodic neutrophilic activation in DCS.16 Potential amplifiers include androgen-associated cytokine induction and profibrotic wound-healing programs,5,24–26 neutrophil chemotaxis relevant to suppurative activity,27 mast cell-associated fibrosis,28 and microbial or innate immune amplification.29 These pathways were not measured in either study and are presented only as a framework for future investigation. Taken together with prior reports across PCAs,9–16 these observations support studying PIILIF as a possible earlier-stage therapeutic target. No treatment effect or influence on established AKN, DCS, or secondary CVG can be inferred from the present data.

Clinical Implications

In AKN, new scalp furrowing or diffuse boggy or draining nodules should prompt examination of the entire scalp for DCS. In DCS, posterior hairline/nape papules, plaques, or masses should prompt focused evaluation for AKN. Extensive CVG in folliculocentric scarring disease may signal high inflammatory burden and fibrotic remodeling. When morphology is atypical or diagnoses overlap, trichoscopy-guided multisite biopsy can improve clinicopathologic adjudication. Recognition should also support early referral to a hair-disorders specialist and longitudinal monitoring. We do not propose a treatment algorithm because therapeutic efficacy and outcomes were not evaluated.

Limitations include the small sample, single-center design, referral and spectrum bias, and reliance on patient recall for chronology. Standardized longitudinal follow-up was unavailable, limiting assessment of disease sequence and outcomes. The findings are exploratory and may not generalize to broader populations. Because the three triad cases are contained within the 12-patient source cohort, the companion report provides broader clinicopathologic context but not independent validation. Systematic laboratory or radiologic evaluation for alternative causes of CVG was not performed, although history and examination supported secondary CVG.

Conclusion

We describe a candidate syndromic triad comprising AKN, scalp-wide DCS, and extensive secondary CVG. Recognition of this constellation may improve identification of coexisting disease across scalp regions and facilitate earlier evaluation of patients at risk for extensive scarring and fibrotic remodeling. These preliminary observations require validation in larger, prospectively phenotyped, multicenter longitudinal cohorts.
