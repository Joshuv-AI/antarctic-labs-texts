IL20RB as a potential biomarker for prognosis in clear cell renal cell carcinoma
Authors: Li Yuehua, Xingyou Dong, Li Guiqiang, Ren Feiqiang, Xu Bin, Tan Wei, Wang Jie, Guo Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) is the most common subtype of renal cell carcinomas and is known for having the worst prognosis. It features a complex array of histone deacetylases (HDACs), which play varied roles in tumor progression and patient outcomes. Exploring the role and significance of HDACs in ccRCC can provide effective prognosis and treatment methods for ccRCC.MethodsWe investigated the expression patterns of HDAC family genes in renal cancer and stratified the disease into distinct subtypes based on their expression profiles using consensus clustering. For each subtype, we conducted differential expression analysis, pathway enrichment analysis, and drug sensitivity evaluation. We then developed and validated a prognostic model utilizing genes associated with the HDAC-based subtypes across multiple independent cohorts. Finally, we collected clinical ccRCC samples and validated the expression of key genes, including IL20RB, using real-time quantitative PCR.ResultsWe identified three clusters linked to distinct HDAC expression patterns that demonstrate varied prognosis and drug sensitivities in ccRCC. Multi-omic analysis revealed distinct mutation and CNV profiles among these clusters, highlighting unique molecular characteristics. Additionally, an 8-gene model risk score, derived from HDAC expression patterns, has proven reliable as a prognostic marker in both training and validation cohorts for ccRCC. Survival analysis also showed that IL20RB is a dependable prognostic factor for ccRCC. Moreover, a pan-cancer analysis suggested that IL20RB is associated with poor prognosis and the activation of metastasis pathways in various types of cancer. Then, compared with tumor surrounding normal tissues, IL20RB expression was significantly up-regulated in in ccRCC tissues at all stages.ConclusionOur study delineates the role of HDACs in ccRCC, shedding light on their influence on tumor heterogeneity, immune modulation, drug response, and molecular profiles. Notably, IL20RB is identified not only as a promising therapeutic target but also as a prognostic indicator, thereby advancing the development of targeted therapies and precision medicine for this disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-15755-3.Keywords: Clear cell renal cell carcinoma, Histone deacetylase, Consensus clustering, Prognostic signature, IL20RB

Introduction

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) accounts for 2% of all global cancer cases, and its incidence is steadily increasing. RCC encompasses various histological subtypes, each with distinct pathological features [1]. Clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC), the most predominant subtype representing approximately 75% of all RCC cases, originates from the proximal tubular cells of the nephron [2]. While early surgical intervention is highly beneficial for patients with localized ccRCC, about 30% experience recurrence or metastasis after surgery. Although antiangiogenic therapies (e.g., Sunitinib and Pazopanib) are effective for metastatic RCC [3], challenges such as treatment resistance and recurrence persist, with some patients exhibiting intrinsic resistance [4]. Therefore, identifying early prognostic biomarkers is crucial for improving ccRCC treatment outcomes.

Histone deacetylases (HDACs) are enzymes that play a vital role in gene expression regulation by removing acetyl groups from histone proteins, thus altering chromatin structure and gene transcription [5]. Through their epigenetic control, HDACs are involved in various cellular functions such as cell cycle regulation, apoptosis, DNA repair, and differentiation. Dysregulated HDAC activity is linked to the development of numerous diseases, including cancer, where abnormal HDAC expression and activity are often noted [6–8]. In clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC), there is growing evidence that HDACs are crucial in disease onset, progression, and resistance to therapy. Changes in HDAC expression and activity have been observed in ccRCC compared to normal kidney tissue, suggesting a role in renal cancer development [9, 10]. Specifically, altered HDAC expression is associated with tumor growth, invasion, metastasis, angiogenesis, and immune escape, underscoring the complex roles of HDACs in ccRCC progression. Research has also explored the prognostic value of HDAC levels in ccRCC, finding that higher HDAC expression correlates with worse clinical outcomes, including increased tumor recurrence, disease progression, and lower overall survival [11, 12].

The interleukin-20 receptor subunit beta (IL20RB) forms heterodimers with either the alpha subunit IL-20RA or the interleukin-22 receptor (IL-22R). This heterodimeric complex specifically binds cytokines of the IL-20 subfamily, including IL-19, IL-20, and IL-24. Ligand binding activates the intracellular JAK1/STAT3 signaling pathway [13, 14]. Recent studies have highlighted the role of IL-20 signaling in cancer progression. Evidence suggests that IL-20 can promote the advancement and spread of prostate, oral, and breast cancers. Abnormal expression of IL-20RB has also been observed in multiple cancer types [15, 16]. Furthermore, IL20RB is implicated in modulating the tumor microenvironment (TME) by influencing immune cell infiltration and immune checkpoint expression, thereby contributing to tumor immune evasion and therapy resistance [12, 17]. Investigating the role of IL20RB in clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) biology may lead to the development of targeted therapies and prognostic biomarkers, ultimately improving clinical management and patient outcomes.

Tumor prognostic models are essential for predicting patient outcomes and guiding individualized treatment plans in cancer care. These models integrate a range of clinical, molecular, and pathological factors to provide prognostic scores that support informed clinical decisions. In clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC), characterized by its variability, such models are crucial for developing customized treatments [18, 19]. By incorporating both clinicopathological and molecular markers, these models categorize patients into risk groups based on their likelihood of recurrence, progression, and survival [20]. Recent advances in molecular profiling techniques, such as genomics and transcriptomics, have greatly enhanced the sophistication of prognostic models for ccRCC. This progress has led to the discovery of new biomarkers and the refinement of existing models, improving their accuracy and reliability. Machine learning algorithms have further advanced these predictions by processing extensive datasets and identifying complex relationships between molecular characteristics and clinical outcomes [21–23]. The use of tumor prognostic models in ccRCC is promising for improving risk stratification, choosing appropriate treatments, and guiding patient discussions. For instance, Zhang et al. established a prognostic model based on copper-death-associated LncRNA in LUAD, which demonstrated excellent performance across multiple datasets [24]. Furthermore, the establishment of a prognostic model based on genes associated with post-translational modifications of proteins in a large-scale clinical sample of LUAD patients can be utilised for individualised prognostic assessment and immunotherapy stratification in LUAD and multiple cancer patients [25]. By accurately forecasting disease progression and pinpointing high-risk individuals, these models enable personalized treatment options, including chemotherapy, immunotherapy, or targeted therapies [26–28]. They also aid in selecting patients for clinical trials, promoting the assessment of innovative treatments and personalized care strategies. Ultimately, the incorporation of these prognostic models into routine clinical practice is likely to enhance patient management and outcomes in ccRCC.

In our study, the landscape of histone deacetylases (HDACs) in ccRCC reveals intriguing correlations and prognostic implications. Initially, examination of HDAC family genes in ccRCC and adjacent normal kidney tissue revealed a similar pattern, with predominantly positive correlations between HDAC genes in tumor tissue and normal tissue. Furthermore, most HDAC family genes showed upregulation in ccRCC compared to adjacent normal kidney tissue. Through differential analysis and consensus clustering, we identified three distinct gene clusters. HDAC cluster 3 was associated with poorer overall survival and displayed lower immune cell infiltration and reduced expression of genes targeted by immunotherapy, suggesting implications for treatment responses. Analysis of drug responsiveness indicated increased resistance to PI3K inhibitors in HDAC cluster 3, highlighting its clinical relevance. Multi-omic analyses revealed differences in mutational profiles and copy number variations across the clusters, with HDAC cluster 3, linked to a worse prognosis, showing chromosomal amplifications at several oncogenic loci. We developed a prognostic model based on HDAC-related genes, identifying a high-risk group characterized by poor survival and aggressive tumor features. IL20RB emerged as a critical gene within this model, strongly correlating with adverse survival outcomes, aggressive tumor behavior, and diminished immune infiltration. Additionally, pan-cancer analysis emphasized IL20RB's importance, noting its upregulation in various cancers and its association with worse survival rates. In summary, the detailed study of HDACs in ccRCC highlights their complex involvement in cancer development, prognosis, and response to treatment. The discovery of specific gene clusters and prognostic indicators such as IL20RB offers potential for improving risk assessment and tailoring treatment approaches in the management of ccRCC.

Methods

Dataset source and data pre-processing

This study analysed patients from three renal cell carcinoma cohorts (E-MTAB-1980, Braun ccRCC 2020 and TCGA-KIRC). Patients lacking survival information or RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data were excluded from our analysis. Clinical and transcriptomic expression data for the E-MTAB-1980 cohort were sourced from cBioPortal (https://www.cbioportal.org/), This dataset contains RNA sequencing and survival data for 101 cases of clear cell renal cell carcinoma (KIRC), specifically comprising 23 positive cases (deceased) and 78 negative controls (survivors), with survival times ranging from 0.83 to 12.08 years and a median of 4.25 years. The transcriptomic data and clinical details from the 2020 Braun clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) study originate from the original publication. This dataset comprises RNA sequencing data from 311 clear cell renal cell carcinoma (KIRC) samples, alongside clinical information including survival duration and status. Specifically, it contains 231 positive samples and 80 negative controls, with survival times ranging from 0.024 to 6.015 years and a median survival of 1.73 years. It further incorporates immunotherapy and response data, specifically comprising 237 non-responding samples and 44 responding samples. Analysis in this study excluded 30 samples with missing immunotherapy response data. For the TCGA-KIRC cohort, transcriptomic data expressed as transcripts per million (TPM) values alongside clinical information (comprising RNA sequencing data from 522 renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC) samples and 71 adjacent tumour tissues). Specifically, the 522 KIRC samples comprise 171 positive cases and 351 negative controls, with survival times ranging from 0.005 to 12.4 years and a median survival of 3.28 years. Notably, we retrieved this data from the Genomic Data Commons (GDC, https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) using the R package “TCGAbiolinks(v2.36.0)”. For sequencing data, we employed normalised TPM values for subsequent analysis; for microarray data, we performed batch normalisation using the limma package prior to analysis, with the expression matrix undergoing normalisation before subsequent analysis. In the survival analysis, deceased and surviving patients served as positive and negative controls, respectively.

Acquisition of HDAC-related clusters and gene related clusters

The "ConsensusClusterPlus (v1.72.0)" package [29] in R was employed to classify the TCGA-KIRC cohort based on the expression levels of 18 genes from the HDAC family. The operating parameters are set as follows: maxK = 10,reps = 500,pItem = 0.8,pFeature = 1,clusterAlg = pam and the seed was 123,456. This family includes 4 Class I HDACs (HDAC1, HDAC2, HDAC3, and HDAC8), 6 Class II HDACs (HDAC4, HDAC5, HDAC6, HDAC7, HDAC9, and HDAC10), 7 Class III HDACs (SIRT1, SIRT2, SIRT3, SIRT4, SIRT5, SIRT6, and SIRT7), and one Class IV HDAC (HDAC11). The optimal number of clusters (K) was established based on consensus values and cumulative distribution function analysis. It is worth noting that we initially select the optimal number of clusters by choosing the K value representing the inflection point on the CDF curve. Subsequently, we assess the average silhouette coefficient under this clustering condition, observing whether this value exceeds 0.7. If it does, this value is adopted as the optimal number of clusters; if it falls below 0.7, the clustering process is repeated. Ultimately, we selected k = 3 as the optimal number of clusters. Following this, univariate Cox analysis (Supplementary Table 1) was conducted to assess the prognostic significance of genes differentially expressed between HDAC cluster 3—which showed the poorest prognosis—and the other two clusters, identifying 814 significant prognostic genes. The Nearest Template Prediction (NTP) method was utilized for classification predictions using a signature gene list and a test dataset, enhancing the assessment of predictive confidence for each patient's gene expression profile. In this study, we used the top 50 upregulated genes from each gene-related cluster in the TCGA-KIRC cohort to predict cluster membership within the E-MTAB-1980 cohort using the 'MOVIC(v0.99.17)' package in R [30].

Gene set variation analysis (GSVA)

The gene set variation analysis (GSVA) was performed using the 'GSVA(v2.2.0)' package in R to generate gene set enrichment scores [31]. These scores facilitated the identification of differing pathway activities between groups with high-risk and low-risk scores by highlighting upregulated and downregulated pathways. The gene sets employed in this analysis were sourced from MSigDB. We quantified immune cell infiltration in the TCGA-KIRC cohort using the CIBERSORT algorithm on normalized gene expression data.

Subtype chemotherapeutic response prediction

We forecasted chemotherapeutic responses for each cluster using data from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database, the largest pharmacogenomics resource available. These predictions were generated using the 'oncoPredict(v1.2)' package in R [32], employing ridge regression to estimate the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) for the drugs in the patient samples. The precision of these predictions was evaluated through tenfold cross-validation using the training dataset from GDSC. All settings were kept at default, with the exception of specifying the tissue type as 'kidney'.

Genomic alterations

Somatic mutations and somatic copy number alterations (CNAs) were retrieved from the GDC using the R package 'TCGAbiolinks' [33]. Subsequently, the 'Maftools' package [34] in R was employed to both visualize and analyze the data related to somatic mutations and CNAs, specifically interpreting GISTIC output. GISTIC 2.0 was utilized to pinpoint significant copy number amplifications and deletions. To identify differences in mutated genes and copy number variations, Chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests were conducted.

Establishment of HDAC gene-related prognostic model

We constructed a prognostic model using genes from the HDAC family with data from two renal cancer cohorts: TCGA-KIRC as the training set and E-MTAB-1980 for validation. Initially, from a list of 2662 prognosis-linked genes, pivotal genes related to the HDAC family and patient outcomes were identified using the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) method. And the purpose of employing the LASSO model is to enhance the model's generalisation capability to a certain extent while mitigating the risk of overfitting. The selection of nine prognostic genes was performed using the "glmnet(v4.1–10)" package in R, utilizing LASSO regression with tenfold cross-validation. The family parameter has been set to binomial, and the remaining parameters shall be processed according to their default settings. These genes were then integrated into a multivariate Cox regression model constructed using the stepwise regression method. The risk score was formulated as follows: 0.225*(EIF4EBP1 expression)—0.101*(PDK4 expression) – 0.157*(CLDN4 expression) + 0.062*(IL20RB expression)—0.102*(SEMA3G expression)—0.093*(ERICH5 expression)—0.213*(SLC2A9 expression) + 0.226*(PABPC1L expression). Risk scores for both the TCGA-KIRC and the validation cohort were calculated using this formula, and patients were stratified into low-risk or high-risk groups based on the median of these scores. The differences in overall survival (OS) between these groups were analyzed using the "survival(v3.7–0)" package in R.

Pan-cancer analysis

The normalized mRNA expression data and clinical information for the TCGA pan-cancer cohorts were sourced from the UCSC Xena Browser (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/). The prognostic relevance of IL20RB across various cancer types was assessed using univariate Cox regression analysis and Kaplan–Meier survival modeling. IL20RB expression, treated as a continuous variable, was included in the univariate Cox regression analysis. For Kaplan–Meier survival analysis, IL20RB expression was dichotomized using the "surv-cutpoint" function from the "survminer(v0.5.0)" R package. Both the log-rank p-value from the Kaplan–Meier analysis and the hazard ratio (HR) with a 95% confidence interval (95%CI) were calculated. The findings were visually displayed in a heatmap.

RNA extraction, reverse transcription, and real-time quantitative PCR

Cancerous and para-cancerous tissues from patients with ccRCC (12 patients in stage 1, 12 patients in stage 2 and 8 patients in stage 3) were collected from the Chongqing Traditional Chinese Medicine Hospital and The First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Chongqing Traditional Chinese Medicine Hospital (approval NO.: CTCMH20230410) and conducted following the Helsinki Declaration. Written informed consent was obtained from individual or guardian participants. Total RNA was extracted from all tissues using Trizol (Invitrogen, 15,596,026). The SYBR Green One-Step qRT-PCR kit (Invitrogen, 11,736,059) was used to measure total RNA (100 ng). PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Shenggong, Shanghai, China) was used for cDNA synthesis. The PCR primers are listed below: IL20RB, forward: 5′-AGGCCCAGACATTCGTGAAG-3′; IL20RB, reverse: 5′-CGACCACAAGGATCAGCATGA-3′; GAPDH, forward:5′-GGAGCGAGATCCCTCCAAAAT-3′; GAPDH, reverse: 5′-GGCTGTTGTCATACTTCTCATGG-3′; The 2-ΔΔCt method was conducted to calculate the RNA expression. Student's t-test was used to compare the expression level of each RNA between different groups.

Statistical analysis

We analyzed survival differences between groups using Kaplan–Meier curves and the log-rank test. To determine correlation coefficients, Pearson and Spearman analyses were used. For data that followed a normal distribution, we applied the student’s t-test, whereas the Mann–Whitney U test was used for non-normally distributed data. In cases where comparisons involved more than two groups, the Kruskal–Wallis test was utilized for nonparametric data, and one-way ANOVA was used for parametric data. All these statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1), with P values less than 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Landscape of HDACs and identification of HDAC gene subtypes in renal cancer

First, we analyzed the correlation of HDAC family genes between tumor tissues and paired adjacent liver tissues. In both adjacent and tumor tissues, correlations among HDAC family genes were predominantly positive (Fig. 1A). Next, we examined the expression patterns of HDAC family genes between tumor tissues and paired adjacent kidney tissues, finding that most HDAC family genes are upregulated in renal cancer tumor tissues compared to adjacent tissues (Fig. 1B). We then conducted univariate Cox regression analysis to assess the prognostic significance of HDAC family genes. The results of the Cox analysis for all HDAC-related genes are presented in Supplementary Table 1. In renal cancer, high expressions of SIRT7, HDAC8, and HDAC10 were negatively correlated with overall survival, while higher expressions of other HDAC family genes were associated with improved overall survival (Fig. 1C). We also used consensus clustering to classify renal cancer into 2 to 10 subtypes based on HDAC expression profiles, identifying three clusters as the most suitable (Fig. 1D, Figure S1). PCA results indicated distinct expression profiles among the different HDAC clusters (Fig. 1E). Survival analysis showed that HDAC cluster 3 had the poorest prognosis (Fig. 1F). Subtype GSEA demonstrated that in HDAC cluster 3, inflammatory, cancer-related, and metastatic pathways were present, potentially linked to poor prognosis (Fig. 1G). It is noteworthy that we assessed differences in immune infiltration between distinct subtypes, with results presented in Fig S3A-B. Findings indicate that the cluster1 subtype exhibits heightened characteristics of antigen presentation, ECM formation, complement activation, and macrophage M1 polarization.

Different characteristics of the three clusters

Recognizing the pivotal role of cancer immunity in tumor progression, we hypothesized that the tumor microenvironment (TME) could vary across HDAC subtypes. To investigate this, we analyzed immune cell infiltration in samples from the TCGA-KIRC cohort, assessing 24 distinct cell types and examining immune checkpoint expression in renal cancer samples. Our gene expression analysis indicated a significantly higher level of immune cell infiltration in the HDAC cluster 2 subtype compared to the lower levels in HDAC cluster 3 (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, HDAC cluster 3 showed reduced expression of several genes related to immunotherapy targets, including PDCD1 (PD1), CD247 (CD3), CD274 (PDL1), PDCD1LG2 (PDL2), CTLA4 (CD152), TNFRSF9 (CD137), TNFRSF4 (CD134), and TLR9 (Fig. 2A). We then confirmed the presence of these three clusters in an external renal cancer cohort (E-MTAB-1980) using the Nearest Template Prediction (NTP) algorithm (Fig. 2B). Consistent with the TCGA cohort findings, patients in the HDAC cluster 3 of the E-MTAB-1980 cohort also exhibited poorer prognosis compared to other clusters (Fig. 2C). In addition, based on the results of the previous analysis, we observed the differential expression of SIRT7, HDAC8, and HDAC10 (genes with significantly high expression and poor prognosis) in different clusters. The results are shown in Figure S5B. As expected, from cluster 1 to cluster 3, the expression of these genes showed a significantly increasing trend. Considering the potential drug resistance in ccRCC patients, we evaluated the drug responsiveness of these clusters. We developed a predictive model using ridge regression based on the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) cell line dataset and validated its accuracy through tenfold cross-validation. Using this model, we calculated the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of drugs on samples from the TCGA-KIRC dataset. Interestingly, as shown in Fig. 2D, HDAC cluster 2 was more resistant to PI3K inhibitors like Apitolisib and Pilaralisib.

Multi-omic differences between three clusters

To analyze mutations within each cluster, we used a waterfall plot to showcase the top 10 significantly mutated genes (SMGs) in both clusters. While the majority of SMGs were common between the three clusters, there were notable differences. HDAC cluster 1 uniquely harbored SMGs such as FBN2 (6%), DNAH3 (6%) and DST (6%). HDAC cluster 2 uniquely harbored SMGs such as AHNAK2 (8%), LRP1B (8%) and ESPL1 (7%). In contrast, HDAC cluster 3 had its own unique SMGs, including ATM (7%), DNAH9 (7%), KDM5C (7%) and KMT2C (6%) (Fig. 3A). We explored the variations in copy number (CNV) across different clusters by utilizing the TCGA-KIRC cohort. Compared to other clusters, the HDAC cluster 3, which is associated with poorer prognosis, was characterized by distinct chromosomal alterations: amplifications at oncogenic loci including MYC, and deletions at tumor suppressor loci such as PTEN (Fig. 3B).

Construction and validation of HDAC gene-related prognostic model

Considering the risk of multicollinearity, we employed LASSO Cox regression analysis to refine the gene selection for building our risk model from all genes related to HDAC clusters. The LASSO analysis pinpointed 18 genes closely associated with overall survival in renal cancer (Figure S2A). We then constructed a multivariate Cox risk model using these 18 genes, ultimately retaining 8 genes in the final model (Figure S2B). Survival analysis indicated that patients in the TCGA cohort with high-risk scores experienced significantly poorer overall survival (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, the AUC (Area Under the Curve) values at 1, 2, 3, and 5 years demonstrated that the risk score was a strong predictor of overall survival in the TCGA-KIRC cohort (Fig. 4B). Notably, ccRCC patients with higher tumor grades and clinical stages tended to have higher risk scores (Fig. 4C-D). To validate the prognostic significance of the risk score, we applied the same calculation to the E-MTAB-1980 validation cohort. The results confirmed that the risk score retained its prognostic value in the validation cohort (Fig. 4E-F). Additionally, genes such as SEMA3G, PDK4, ERICH5, SLC2A9, and CLDN4 were found to be more highly expressed in the low-risk group, whereas IL20RB, PABPC1L, and EIF4EBP1 exhibited higher expression in the high-risk group. Moreover, the high-risk group experienced a higher incidence of deaths compared to the low-risk group (Fig. 4G-H). We further validated the model using external datasets, with results shown in Supplementary Figure S4A-C. These demonstrate that our model performs excellently across multiple datasets when compared to several published external cohorts. Furthermore, through immune infiltration analysis, we observed that patients in the high-risk group exhibited heightened expression of the M1 macrophage phenotype, increased stromal scores, and greater infiltration of T cells and their subtypes(Fig S3C-D). We also calculated the risk score in the Braun ccRCC 2020 cohort. The results showed that patients with high-risk scores were predominantly those resistant to immunotherapy, and these patients had higher risk scores compared to those responsive to immunotherapy (Fig. 4I-J).

Establishment of the nomogram model based on risk score

To enhance the predictive accuracy of the risk scores previously discussed, we constructed a nomogram model by incorporating both the risk score and age using multivariable Cox regression analysis (Fig. 5A). Calibration curves for disease-specific survival (DSS) at 5 and 8 years demonstrated high concordance between the nomogram's predicted survival probabilities and the actual outcomes, validating the model's effectiveness in survival prediction (Fig. 5B). Additionally, the nomogram's predicted AUC was shown to surpass the standalone risk score's AUC in the TCGA-KIRC cohort (Fig. 5C-D). For drug sensitivity analysis, we employed two different approaches using data from the CTRP and PRISM databases to identify drug candidates that demonstrated increased sensitivity in patients with high nomogram scores. We initially performed a differential drug response analysis between the high and low nomogram score groups, searching for drugs with significantly lower estimated AUC values in the high-risk group. Further, we identified drugs that showed a negative correlation between their AUC values and the Treg infiltration-related risk scores by calculating the Spearman correlation coefficient (Spearman’s r < −0.50 for CTRP and −0.5 for PRISM). This analysis yielded 8 compounds from PRISM (including lovastatin, PI-103, fluvastatin, dasatinib, RITA, mitomycin, leptomycin B, and rigosertib) and 9 from CTRP (including mitoxantrone, GSK461364, NVP-AUY922, litronesib, tosedostat, LY2606368, romidepsin, epothilone-b, and ispinesib), all of which demonstrated lower estimated AUC values in the high-nomogram group and a negative correlation with the nomogram score (Fig. 5E).

IL20RB was a key gene among the prognostic genes related to the HDAC cluster

To determine the key genes among the eight prognostic genes selected through LASSO, we ranked them based on their average functional similarity within the interactome network. IL20RB, SEMA3G, and EIF4EBP1 were identified as the top three genes, potentially playing pivotal roles. IL20RB stood out as the only gene with a cutoff value greater than 0.75, a threshold commonly used to discern between significant and non-significant correlations. In addition, the diagnostic ROC curve indicated that IL20RB had the highest diagnostic efficacy (AUC = 0.957, Figure S5A). Hence, we selected IL20RB for subsequent functional investigation (Fig. 6A). Notably, ccRCC patients with higher tumor grades and clinical stages tended to have higher IL20RB expression (Fig. 6B-C). Survival analysis revealed that patients with high IL20RB expression had a much worse overall survival in TCGA cohort and E-MTAB-1980 cohort (Fig. 6D-E). We explored the differences in pathway enrichment between high-IL20RB and low-IL20RB groups using the GSVA and differential gene enrichment analysis methods, focusing on hallmark pathways. As the result showed, high-IL20RB group mainly enriched in tumor-promoting Hallmark pathways, such as EMT, hypoxia, IL6-JAK-STAT3 signaling up and etc. (Fig. 6F). Next, we analyzed the correlation between IL20RB and immune cell infiltration levels in ccRCC patients. The result showed that IL20RB expression was negatively correlated with naïve B cells and NK cells (Fig. 6G). Based on IL20RB expression grouping, differential relationships with HDAC family gene expression were observed. Results are presented in Fig S3F, demonstrating that samples with high IL20RB expression significantly upregulate HDAC10 and SIRT7 whilst downregulating HDAC1, HDAC2, HDAC4, HDAC5, SIRT1, SIRT2, SIRT3, SIRT4, SIRT5, and HDAC11. In addition, an examination of IL20RB expression under different immunotherapy response conditions revealed that compared to the non—responder group of immunotherapy, the expression of this gene was significantly elevated in the responder group and was significantly associated with disease remission (Figure S3E).

Pan-cancer analysis of IL20RB

Next, the expression of IL20RB in tumor versus normal tissues for 20 cancer types within the TCGA cohort was also investigated, revealing that RCN1 was upregulated in 30% of the cancers, such as UCEC, KIRP, LUSC, KIRC, KICH, THCA and LUAD (Fig. 7A). Moreover, we examined the association between IL20RB and the Hallmark pathways across the TCGA pan-cancer cohort, finding that IL20RB positively correlated with several pathway and EMT, including G2M checkpoint pathway and Mtorc1 pathway, across various cancer types (Fig. 7B). Furthermore, the study assessed the impact of IL20RB expression on the survival prognosis in 32 cancer types, indicating that high IL20RB expression correlated with poorer survival outcomes in over 10 cancer types, including ACC, COAD, KIRC, KIRP, KICH, LUAD, PAAD, READ, SKCM, UCEC and UVM (Fig. 7C).

IL20RB was highly expressed in ccRCC tissues and vesicles

In our study, real-time quantitative PCR analyses were conducted on ccRCC tissues across different tumor stages—stage 1, stage 2, and stage 3. The results consistently demonstrated that IL20RB was highly expressed in ccRCC tissues compared to the surrounding non-tumor tissues in all stages examined and showed a tendency for higher expression at higher stages (Fig. 8). This uniform overexpression of IL20RB across various stages of ccRCC suggests its potential role in the early onset and progression of the disease, underscoring its utility as a biomarker for both diagnosis and potential therapeutic targeting. Additionally, we analysed the cellular sublocalisation of IL20RB. Due to the current data availability in the Human Protein Atlas (HPA), we utilized HaCaT, SiHa, and hTCEpi cell lines as models to investigate the fundamental spatial distribution of this protein. The results shown in Fig S4D indicate that IL20RB exhibits a consistent vesicular localization across these multiple cell lines. This suggests that its subcellular distribution may be a conserved biological property in human cells, which is consistent with its functional role as a receptor subunit involved in cytokine signaling and intracellular trafficking.

Discussion

Clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC), the most common type of kidney cancer, accounts for approximately 75% of all cases [35]. It exhibits complex and variable biological behavior, with clinical manifestations ranging from localized lesions to highly invasive systemic disease. Although significant progress has been made in clinical treatment over the past few decades [36], the variability in treatment response and resistance mechanisms across different biomarkers requires further research to optimize therapeutic strategies and improve survival rates. Traditional treatments like surgical resection, radiation therapy, and chemotherapy remain cornerstones. However, the development of targeted therapies and immunotherapies has provided new hope for patients with advanced or refractory ccRCC [37, 38]. Notably, the approval of various anti-angiogenic drugs and mTOR inhibitors has shifted treatment paradigms and improved patient prognosis. Furthermore, immune checkpoint inhibitors targeting the PD-1 and CTLA-4 pathways have demonstrated significant potential in clinical trials, opening new avenues for ccRCC treatment [39, 40].

In mammalian genomes, there are 11 recognized HDAC family members, organized into four distinct "phylogenetic" classes: Class I is composed of HDAC1, 2, 3, and 8; Class IIa includes HDAC4, 5, 7, and 9; Class IIb features HDAC6 and 10; and Class IV is represented solely by HDAC11. In addition, a separate group known as SIRTUINS (SIRTs), which comprises SIRT1-7, constitutes Class III of the deacetylase superfamily [41, 42]. In the context of cancer, dysfunctional HDAC activity is tightly linked with cancer initiation, progression, and metastasis. Research indicates that elevated levels of HDACs contribute to the enhanced growth, invasion, and chemotherapy resistance observed in various cancers [43]. Dysfunctional HDAC activity can lead to inappropriate chromatin condensation, which in turn represses tumor suppressor genes and fosters cancer cell development. Consequently, targeting HDACs has become a pivotal strategy in cancer treatment [44]. For example, in breast cancer, increased expression of HDAC1 and HDAC6 is linked to tumor aggressiveness and recurrence, while HDAC2 levels are associated with patient prognosis. Moreover, high levels of SOX9 and HDAC5 expression correlate with poorer survival outcomes in breast cancer patients treated with tamoxifen, emphasizing the relevance of these findings to patient prognosis [8, 45, 46]. In colorectal cancer, HDAC3 is commonly found to be overexpressed and is regarded as a potential contributor to tumor development [47]. In renal cancer, class I HDAC isoforms 1 and 2 are expressed at high levels, whereas HDAC 3 is less frequently expressed and its presence varies depending on the tumor histology. In ccRCC, both class I and II HDACs, particularly HDAC1 and HDAC6, are overexpressed and significantly enhance tumor cell invasion and motility. HDAC1 increases invasiveness through upregulation of matrix metalloproteinases and its expression is further elevated under hypoxia, likely regulated by HIF-2α. HDAC6 promotes cell motility by reducing acetylated α-tubulin levels, an effect that can be inhibited both biochemically and pharmacologically. Moreover, higher levels of HDAC1 mRNA are linked to poorer overall survival in ccRCC patients, indicating its potential role as a prognostic marker. In contrast, SIRT4 expression in ccRCC is significantly reduced compared to normal tissues. Additionally, bioinformatics analysis and Western blot results indicate that lower SIRT4 expression is associated with poorer overall survival and more advanced tumor stages in ccRCC patients [11, 12, 48]. The variability in HDAC expression implies that different regulatory mechanisms, influenced by oncogenic signals, may drive clonal evolution, leading to drug resistance and treatment failures in ccRCC. In our study, except for Class IV and SIRTs, most of the HDAC family genes were up-regulated in tumor tissues compared with normal tissues.

The integration of consensus clustering—an advanced methodology that combines multiple clustering algorithms to identify stable and consistent patterns—has significantly advanced the study of cancer heterogeneity. This approach allows for the classification of patients into distinct groups based on prognostic outcomes, treatment responses, and risk profiles, thereby deepening our understanding of tumor biology. It also facilitates the discovery of novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets by uncovering molecular signatures associated with specific cancer subtypes [49–51]. For instance, in muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC), Woonyoung Choi and colleagues identified three molecular subtypes—basal, luminal, and p53-like—which resemble well-established breast cancer subtypes. Their findings highlight the biological diversity of MIBC and its implications for prognosis and treatment strategies. By classifying tumors into these subtypes, the authors suggest that MIBC therapy could be personalized to target molecular pathways specific to each subtype, potentially improving treatment efficacy and patient outcomes [52, 53].

In ccRCC, Rose Brannon and colleagues integrated data from 480 tumor samples across multiple public gene expression databases. Through consensus clustering, they confirmed the presence of two major subtypes, ccA and ccB, which were initially defined in earlier studies and exhibit significantly different survival rates and molecular characteristics. Their analysis also suggested a potential role of gender in ccRCC biology, revealing notable differences in gene expression between male and female tumors that may influence tumor behavior and treatment response [48, 54]. In our study, we applied consensus clustering to HDAC family gene expression data from the TCGA-KIRC cohort, successfully categorizing patients into three distinct groups: HDAC cluster 1, 2, and 3. Principal component analysis and survival analysis revealed unique expression profiles and differential survival outcomes across these clusters, with HDAC cluster 3 associated with the poorest prognosis. Immune profiling indicated that HDAC cluster 3 exhibited reduced immune cell infiltration and elevated expression of immune checkpoint genes compared to the other clusters—a finding validated in external cohorts. Although HDAC cluster 2 showed the highest total amount of immune cell infiltration, our analysis found that the HDAC cluster 1 subtype exhibited more prominent features in specific immune functions, such as antigen presentation, ECM formation, complement activation, and M1 macrophage polarization. This difference emphasizes the heterogeneity of the tumor microenvironment (TME) in ccRCC, indicating that the quantity of immune cells (a characteristic of Cluster 2) and their functionally activated states (a characteristic of Cluster 1) may not be fully consistent. The high M1 macrophage polarization and antigen presentation features of Cluster 1 suggest that the TME of this subtype undergoes a stronger process of inflammatory activation and immune function remodeling. In contrast, Cluster 3 had the poorest prognosis, characterized by the lowest infiltration level and reduced expression of immunotherapy targets. Therefore, our findings suggest that insufficient immune infiltration (Cluster 3) is a factor more directly associated with poor prognosis, while the distinction between Cluster 1 and Cluster 2 represents different patterns of immune activation and tissue remodeling. Drug response analysis further showed that HDAC cluster 2 was associated with increased resistance to PI3K inhibitors. Multi-omics analysis also suggested differences in copy number variation and mutational landscapes among the three clusters.

Prognostic models for tumors are essential for tailoring individualized treatment approaches in renal cancer, integrating clinical, molecular, and pathological information to predict patient outcomes and inform therapeutic decisions. Given the complexity of renal cancer, accurate models are required that account for tumor stage, grade, and molecular markers. Enhancements in molecular profiling techniques, including genomic and transcriptomic analyses, have improved these models, enabling more precise risk evaluation and therapeutic selections. These advancements facilitate personalized treatments such as chemotherapy and immunotherapy, guide the inclusion of patients in clinical trials, and promote the development of targeted therapies, thereby enhancing patient care and outcomes [55–58]. In our study, Utilizing an FDR (False Discovery Rate) of less than 0.05 as the threshold, our differential analysis distinguished 2662 genes between the poor prognosis group, HDAC cluster3, and other subtypes. Subsequent univariate Cox analysis pinpointed 831 genes that are significant for prognosis. To further refine this list, the LASSO (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) method was employed, selecting 18 genes most closely associated with prognostic outcomes. Next, these 18 genes were selected to build a ccRCC risk model, which incorporated eight genes. Survival analysis indicated that high-risk patients had significantly poorer outcomes in the TCGA cohort. Validation in E-MTAB-1980 and Braun ccRCC 2020 cohorts confirmed the model's predictive accuracy for overall survival and immunotherapy resistance.

Regarding the immune paradox in the high-risk group, our research results show that patients with high risk scores are mainly concentrated in the group non-responsive to immunotherapy, which directly confirms the association between the high-risk group and immunotherapy resistance. This resistance, despite the enhanced expression of the M1 macrophage phenotype, increased infiltration of T cells and their subtypes, and a higher stromal score in the high-risk group, can be explained by "dysfunctional inflammation". On the one hand, the increased infiltration of T cells and M1 macrophages (usually regarded as the "immune hot" phenotype) indicates a strong immune response in the tumor microenvironment (TME). On the other hand, the high stromal score in the high-risk group predicts significant remodeling and a barrier effect of the extracellular matrix (ECM). The dense ECM may form a physical barrier, isolating immune cells from tumor cells, leading to the accumulation of immune cells around the tumor periphery and resulting in ineffective "immune exclusion". In addition, the high expression of the key oncogene IL20RB in the high-risk group also provides a molecular mechanism for immune resistance. The high expression of IL20RB is significantly associated with the up-regulation of tumor-promoting pathways, such as EMT, hypoxia, and the IL6-JAK-STAT3 signaling pathway. The activation of these pathways is usually closely related to tumor aggressiveness, immune escape, and increased resistance to treatment, further explaining the mechanism by which tumors can evade immune clearance despite the presence of immune cell infiltration.

IL20RB is implicated in several types of cancers, where it often acts as a biomarker for tumor aggressiveness and prognosis. For example, IL20RB expression is markedly increased in pancreatic cancer tissues and is associated with poor prognosis. This receptor enhances stemness and chemoresistance in pancreatic cancer through STAT3 phosphorylation, both in vitro and in vivo. Interleukin-19 from the tumor microenvironment acts as the primary ligand for IL20RB, driving these effects, which can be mitigated by inhibitors of STAT3 phosphorylation [59]. Moreover, IL-20RB mediates a direct tumor response to osteoclasts and is linked to bone metastasis in lung cancer, promoting metastatic growth by stimulating tumor cell proliferation in bone. This occurs as tumor cells induce osteoclasts to release IL19, which activates JAK1/STAT3 signaling in IL-20RB-expressing tumor cells. Blocking IL20RB with a neutralizing antibody significantly reduces bone metastasis, highlighting the potential of IL20RB-targeted therapies in treating metastasis [60]. In cccRCC, IL20RB is significantly overexpressed in ccRCC tissues and cells, correlating with poor overall survival, high tumor grade, and advanced TNM stage; its knockdown notably reduces cell proliferation [11]. In our study of the TCGA-KIRC cohort, we found that higher IL20RB expression was linked to poorer overall survival, advanced tumor stages, and greater metastatic potential, indicating that IL20RB promotes tumor aggressiveness and alters the tumor microenvironment to facilitate cancer progression. Functional enrichment analyses showed that in renal cancer, IL20RB is closely related to key oncogenic pathways such as hypoxia, Jak-Stat signaling, and EMT, all essential for tumor proliferation and metastasis. Moreover, IL20RB's activation of pathways like EMT and hypoxia underscores its role in enhancing tumor invasiveness, and its negative correlation with the infiltration of immune cells like B cells and NK cells underlines its contribution to immune evasion, emphasizing the need for further research on IL20RB as a therapeutic target to improve the efficacy of immunotherapies in renal cancer. Our study on IL20RB has certain limitations that future research should address. Firstly, although our sample size from independent datasets was adequate, we suggest further validation using larger, multi-center datasets that include a broader range of populations. Secondly, more in-depth in vitro and in vivo studies are necessary to establish the therapeutic potential of IL20RB in renal cancer. Thirdly, the related mechanistic insights from the research necessitate further experimental validation. Furthermore, the spatial dynamics of HDAC clusters and IL20RB⁺ immune cells remain to be further elucidated.

Conclusion

Our research has developed a prognostic model using HDAC family genes to predict overall survival in renal cancer, examining the prognostic significance of IL20RB in this setting. We aim to deepen insights into the influence of HDACs on the biology and prognosis of renal cancer. Furthermore, we propose that IL20RB could serve as a new biomarker for predicting clinical outcomes in renal cancer patients.
