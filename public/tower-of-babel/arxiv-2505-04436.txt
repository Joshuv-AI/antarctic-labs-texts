Introduction

The
d

The study of the percolated hypercube

[

, with subsequent work by Bollobás, Kohayakawa, and Łuczak

whp

1.1
Notation

For real numbers
a,b,c
with
c>0
, we write
a=b\pm c
to mean that
a\in[b-c,b+c]
. For positive integers
k
, we write
[k]=\{1,\ldots,k\}
.
Throughout the paper, all logarithms are taken with respect to the natural base. Any asymptotic notation is used with respect to the parameter
d
.
We further write
f(C,x)=o_{C}(x)
if
\lim_{C\to\infty}f(C,x)/x=0
, and
f(C,x)=\omega_{C}(x)
if
\lim_{C\to\infty}f(C,x)/x=\infty
. With a slight abuse of notation, we sometimes write
a\gg b
to indicate that
a=\omega(b)
, and
a\ll b
to indicate that
a=o(b)
.
Rounding is ignored where irrelevant for better readability.

We introduce some notation specific to high-dimensional hypercubes.
For simplicity, we write throughout
V=V(Q^{d})
.
For a vertex
v\in V
, we write
\mathds{1}(v)
for the set of coordinates of
v
equal to 1 and call it the
support
of
v
. Given
S\subseteq V
, we let
\mathds{1}(S)
be the
joint support
of
S
, that is,
\mathds{1}(S)=\bigcap_{v\in S}\mathds{1}(v)
, and
\mathbb{T}(S)
be that
total support
of
S
, that is,
\mathbb{T}(S)=\bigcup_{v\in S}\mathds{1}(v)
.

Moreover, for an integer
i\in[0,d]
, we denote by
L_{i}\subseteq V
the set of vertices with exactly
i
coordinates equal to 1, that is,
L_{i}\coloneqq\{v\in V\colon|\mathds{1}(v)|=i\}
.
We often call this set
layer
L_{i}
or, with a slight abuse of notation, layer
i
of the (hyper)cube.
For integers
i_{1},i_{2}\in[0,d]
with
i_{1}<i_{2}
, we also write
L_{i_{1},i_{2}}\coloneqq L_{i_{1}}\cup L_{i_{1}+1}\cup\ldots\cup L_{i_{2}}
.
Further, for subsets
U\subseteq V
, we write
L_{i}[U]\coloneqq L_{i}\cap U
and
L_{i_{1},i_{2}}[U]\coloneqq L_{i_{1},i_{2}}\cap U
. A path in
Q^{d}
is called
monotone
if it visits every layer of the hypercube at most once.
Given two vertices
u,v\in V
such that
\mathds{1}(u)\subseteq\mathds{1}(v)
, let
Q[u;v]
be the induced subcube of
Q^{d}
whose vertex set is given by
V(Q[u;v])\coloneqq\left\{w\in V\colon\mathds{1}(u)\subseteq\mathds{1}(w)\subseteq\mathds{1}(v)\right\}
. Note that
Q[u;v]
is isomorphic to a hypercube of dimension
|\mathds{1}(v)|-|\mathds{1}(u)|
.

Given
p,q\in[0,1]
, we denote by
Q^{d}_{p}
the random subgraph obtained by retaining every edge of
Q^{d}
independently and with probability
p
, and we further write
Q^{d}_{p}(q)
for the random (vertex) subgraph of
Q^{d}_{p}
obtained by retaining every vertex
v\in V
independently and with probability
q
.
Finally, we denote by
Q_{0}
(resp.
Q_{1}
) the subcube of
Q^{d}
of dimension
d-1
obtained by fixing the first coordinate to be
0
(resp.
1
).

If
\mathcal{P}
is a family of vertex-disjoint subgraphs of
Q^{d}
, then we write
V(\mathcal{P})=\bigcup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}V(P)
.

1.2
Proof outline

In this section, we present an overview of the proof of Theorem
1
. For ease of presentation, we will describe the strategy
deterministically
, although in reality some of the statements will hold instead with (sufficiently) high probability.

We will start by partitioning
V(Q^{d})
into a ‘main’ set
V_{1}
, containing all but a vanishing in
C
proportion of the vertices of
Q^{d}
, and some small random ‘reservoirs’
V_{2},V_{3}
, which are sufficiently ‘well-distributed’ in
V(Q^{d})
, and within each layer
L_{i}
, to preserve the ‘typical’ degrees of most vertices in each partition class to adjacent layers.

On a high-level, our strategy will be to first cover almost all of
V_{1}
with a large family of paths, where by ‘almost all’, both here and in the rest of this section, we mean all but a vanishing in
C
proportion. We then use the vertices in the reservoirs to ‘merge’ these paths into smaller and smaller families of longer and longer paths, still covering almost all of
V_{1}
, until they are eventually merged into a single, nearly spanning cycle.

After the initial step, in which we build a family of paths covering almost all the vertices of
V_{1}
, there are four main merging steps. Since the merging process will rely heavily on the ‘layered’ structure of
Q^{d}
, it will be useful to keep track of the vertices in or between certain layers. Later on, we will fix layers
m_{1}\leq m_{2}\leq m_{3}\leq d/2\leq m_{4}
such that almost all vertices in
Q^{d}
lie in
L_{m_{3},m_{4}}
and
m_{1}=\Theta(\log d)\ll m_{2}\ll m_{3}
.

The main steps are then as follows.

1.

We first construct a family
\mathcal{P}_{1}
of paths of length
\omega_{C}(1)
covering almost all vertices in
L_{m_{3},m_{4}}[V_{1}]
(Section
3
).

2.

We then use the vertices in
L_{m_{3},m_{4}}[V_{2}]
together with a novel variant of the Depth-First-Search algorithm on an auxiliary unbalanced bipartite graph in order to merge most of the total volume of these paths into a family
\mathcal{P}_{2}
of paths of length
\omega_{C}(d)
(Section
4
).

3.

We then introduce a process, which we call Merge-Or-Grow (MOG), in which we iteratively grow expanding trees down from the initial and terminal segments of the paths in
\mathcal{P}_{2}
one layer at a time. When the trees corresponding to different paths meet, we will use them to merge two paths. We first run MOG using the vertices in
L_{m_{2},m_{4}}[V_{3}]
and show that any tree which does not merge during this process grows to have
many
leaves in
L_{m_{2}}[Q_{0}]
(Section
5.1
).

4.

We then run MOG using the vertices of
L_{m_{1},m_{2}}[Q_{0}]
, and analyse this process using an innovative coupling with a simpler process on a mixed percolated hypercube. This allows us to construct a family
\mathcal{P}_{3}
of at most six paths, each of whose initial and terminal segments are connected by a tree to
many
vertices in
L_{m_{1}}[Q_{0}]
(
Section

5.2
).

5.

Finally, we use some self-similarity properties of
Q^{d}
to find
many
disjoint subcubes of dimension
\Theta(d)
in
L_{0,m_{2}}[Q_{1}]
, whose edges we did not reveal yet, which can be used to merge the paths in
\mathcal{P}_{3}
. Since
pd\gg 1
, it is very likely that these merges can be performed inside the giant component of one of these subcubes (
Section

6
).

We note that, for Steps
4
and
5
, it will be important that we have some control over the supports of the leaves in the trees constructed via MOG (see, in particular, Proposition
5.4
).

Preliminaries

We collect several lemmas used throughout the paper. The first one is a simplified version of the Kruskal-Katona theorem due to Lovász
[
24
]
.

Theorem 2.1

(Lovász’ version of the Kruskal-Katona theorem)
.

Fix
i\in[d]
, a set
A\subseteq L_{i}
and a real number
x
such that
|A|=\prod_{j=0}^{i-1}\tfrac{x-j}{i-j}=:\tbinom{x}{i}
. Then,
A
has at least
\tbinom{x}{i-1}
neighbours in
L_{i-1}
within
Q^{d}
.

The second one is a technical lemma used together with Theorem
2.1
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Consider
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
i\in[d]
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
such that
i=\Theta(d)
and
\binom{x}{i}=\Theta\left(d^{\alpha}\right)
.
Then,
x\leq i+\frac{3}{2}\alpha
and, in particular,

\frac{i}{3\alpha}\binom{x}{i}\leq\binom{x}{i-1}.

Proof.

Note that, for
x\geq i
,
\binom{x}{i}
is increasing as a function of
x
, and if
y\geq i+\frac{3}{2}\alpha
, then

\displaystyle\binom{y}{i}

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=0}^{i-1}\frac{y-j}{i-j}\geq\prod_{j=0}^{i-1}\bigg(1+\frac{3\alpha/2}{i-j}\bigg)\geq\exp\bigg(\left(\frac{3}{2}+o(1)\right)\alpha\sum_{j=0}^{i-\log i}\frac{1}{i-j}\bigg)

\displaystyle\geq\exp\left(\left(\frac{3}{2}+o(1)\right)\alpha\log i\right)=\Omega\left(d^{(3/2+o(1))\alpha}\right),

using the fact that
1+z\geq\mathrm{e}^{(1+o(1))z}
for
z=o(1)
and that
\sum_{j=0}^{i-\log i}\frac{1}{i-j}=(1+o(1))\log i
.
In particular, it follows that
x\leq i+\frac{3}{2}\alpha
and, hence,

\frac{i}{3\alpha}\binom{x}{i}=\frac{x-i+1}{3\alpha}\binom{x}{i-1}\leq\binom{x}{i-1}.\qed

The following lemma allows one to find monotone paths in hypercubes after mixed percolation. It generalises
[
5
, Lemma 4.1]
(which addresses edge-percolation) and is proved similarly. We include a full proof here for the sake of completeness.

Lemma 2.3
.

Fix
\alpha>\mathrm{e}
,
q\in(\mathrm{e}/\alpha,1]
, let
D\in\mathbb{N}
be sufficiently large, and let
\rho=\alpha/D
.
Then, the probability that
Q^{D}_{\rho}(q)
contains a monotone path of length
D
is at least
D^{-5}
.

Proof.

Let
X
be the random variable counting the number of monotone paths of length
D
in
Q^{D}_{p}(q)
.
An application of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives

\mathbb{P}(X\geq 1)\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{X\geq 1}]\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]\geq\mathbb{E}[(\mathbf{1}_{X\geq 1}X)^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[X]^{2}.

(1)

Since
\mathbb{E}[X]=D!\rho^{D}q^{D+1}
, where we account for the
D+1
vertices and the
D
edges on a monotone path, it remains to bound
\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]
from above.

Denote by
\Pi
the set of all monotone paths of length
D
in
Q^{D}
.
Define
\textnormal{id}=\{v_{0}v_{1},\ldots,v_{D-1}v_{D}\}\in\Pi
, where
v_{i}\in L_{i}
is the vertex whose first
i
coordinates are 1 (and the last
D-i
coordinates are 0).
Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\in\Pi}\mathbb{P}(\{\pi_{1}\subseteq Q^{D}_{\rho}(q)\}\wedge\{\pi_{2}\subseteq Q^{D}_{\rho}(q)\})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\in\Pi}q^{|V(\pi_{1}\cup\pi_{2})|}\rho^{|E(\pi_{1}\cup\pi_{2})|}=D!\sum_{\pi\in\Pi}q^{|V(\textnormal{id}\cup\pi)|}\rho^{|E(\textnormal{id}\cup\pi)|}.

For a number
k\in[D]
, we restrict our attention to paths
\pi\in\Pi
such that
\textnormal{id}\cap\pi
contains
k
edges, and let
\ell
be the number of maximal segments of the path
id
which are edge-disjoint from
\pi
. Note that, given these quantities, we have that
|V(\textnormal{id}\cup\pi)|=2D+1-k-\ell
and
|E(\textnormal{id}\cup\pi)|=2D-k
.

Fix
k,\ell
as above. Then, there are at most
\binom{D}{2\ell}
ways to choose where the maximal segments lie.
Indeed, choosing the first and the last edge of the segments (which must be different) determines its position.
For every
j\in[\ell]
, by writing
x_{j}
for the number of edges in the
j
-th segment, we have that
x_{1}+\ldots+x_{\ell}=D-k
.
Given where these maximal segments lie, there are at most
x_{1}!\cdot\ldots\cdot x_{\ell}!
ways to recover
\pi
. Further, note that, for every
j\in[\ell]
, we have that
x_{j}\geq 2
and thus
\ell\leq(D-k)/2
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq D!\sum_{k=0}^{D}\sum_{\ell=0}^{(D-k)/2}\binom{D}{2\ell}q^{2D+1-k-\ell}\rho^{2D-k}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell}\geq 2,\\
x_{1}+\ldots+x_{\ell}=D-k\end{subarray}}\left\{\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}x_{j}!\right\}

\displaystyle\leq D!q^{D+1}\rho^{D}+D!\sum_{k=0}^{D-2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{(D-k)/2}\binom{D}{2\ell}q^{2D-k-\ell}\rho^{2D-k}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell}\geq 2,\\
x_{1}+\ldots+x_{\ell}=D-k\end{subarray}}\left\{\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}x_{j}!\right\},

(2)

where the first summand corresponds to the case where
k=D
(and
\ell=0
). We now turn to estimate the second summand. By the convexity of the Gamma function, the maximum in (
2
) is attained when
x_{j}=2
for all
j\in[\ell]
except one, which is equal to
D-k-2(\ell-1)
. Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{D-2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{(D-k)/2}\binom{D}{2\ell}

\displaystyle q^{2D-k-\ell}\rho^{2D-k}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell}\geq 2,\\
x_{1}+\ldots+x_{\ell}=D-k\end{subarray}}\left\{\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}x_{j}!\right\}

\displaystyle\leq(q\rho)^{2D}\sum_{k=0}^{D-2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{(D-k)/2}\left(\frac{\mathrm{e}D}{q\ell}\right)^{2\ell}\left(\frac{D}{q\alpha}\right)^{k}(D-k-2(\ell-1))!\,,

(3)

where we used the inequality
\tbinom{D}{2\ell}\leq(\mathrm{e}D/2\ell)^{2\ell}
.
By changing the order of summation, and then by changing variables, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{D-2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{(D-k)/2}

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\mathrm{e}D}{q\ell}\right)^{2\ell}\left(\frac{D}{q\alpha}\right)^{k}(D-k-2(\ell-1))!\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{D/2}\left(\frac{\mathrm{e}D}{q\ell}\right)^{2\ell}\sum_{k=0}^{D-2\ell}\left(\frac{D}{q\alpha}\right)^{k}(D-k-2\ell+2)!

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{D/2}\left(\frac{\mathrm{e}D}{q\ell}\right)^{2\ell}\left(\frac{D}{q\alpha}\right)^{D-2\ell}\;\sum_{m=0}^{D-2\ell}\left(\frac{q\alpha}{D}\right)^{m}(m+2)!\,.

(4)

Moreover, by using the inequality
(m+2)!\leq D^{3}(m/\mathrm{e})^{m}
for
m\in[0,D-2]
, the inner sum is bounded from above by

D^{3}\sum_{m=0}^{D-2\ell}\left(\frac{q\alpha m}{D\mathrm{e}}\right)^{m}\leq D^{4}\cdot\left(\frac{q\alpha}{\mathrm{e}}\right)^{D-2\ell}.

(5)

Now, (
4
) together with (
5
) give

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{D-2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{(D-k)/2}

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\mathrm{e}D}{q\ell}\right)^{2\ell}\left(\frac{D}{q\alpha}\right)^{k}(D-k-2(\ell-1))!\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{D/2}\left(\frac{\mathrm{e}D}{q\ell}\right)^{2\ell}\left(\frac{D}{q\alpha}\right)^{D-2\ell}D^{4}\cdot\left(\frac{q\alpha}{\mathrm{e}}\right)^{D-2\ell}

\displaystyle=D^{4}\cdot\left(\frac{D}{\mathrm{e}}\right)^{D}\sum_{\ell=1}^{D/2}\left(\frac{\mathrm{e}^{2}}{q\ell}\right)^{2\ell}.

(6)

Hence, putting together (
2
), (
3
), and (
6
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]\leq D!q^{D+1}\rho^{D}+D!(q\rho)^{2D}D^{4}\cdot\left(\frac{D}{\mathrm{e}}\right)^{D}\sum_{\ell=1}^{D/2}\left(\frac{\mathrm{e}^{2}}{q\ell}\right)^{2\ell}\leq D^{5}(D!q^{D+1}\rho^{D})^{2},

For the rest of the paper, we fix

\displaystyle m_{1}=50\log d,\quad m_{2}=d/2-d^{0.7},

Lemma 3.3
.

Fix even

(d)

We now turn to the analysis of DFS-Aux. We will first show that, independently of the history of the process, in each phase, it is quite likely that a long path is added to

Lemma 4.2

At the end of each phase of

Proof.

Recall that, by definition of the DFS-Aux algorithm, each phase ends when either the set

and

We now consider the two stopping conditions separately and show that, in each case, with probability at least

Case 1.
Assume

However, by the Chernoff bound, the number of successful queries during the current phase is smaller than

If this event does not hold, then, since

∎

Lemma 5.3
.

Hence, by the Chernoff bound, with probability

∎

.

5.2
Connecting almost all paths in
\mathcal{P}^{m_{2}}
in the subcube
Q_{0}

We now turn to the proof of Proposition
5.5
. For this, we will analyse how the trees in
\mathcal{F}^{m_{2}}
continue to grow as we iterate MOG down from
L_{m_{2}}
to
L_{m_{1}}
.
To this end, we will introduce a simpler process where the growth of trees is stochastically dominated by the growth of the random trees in MOG.
We call it the
alternative growth procedure
(AGP for short). The AGP grows graphs in a mixed-percolated hypercube
(Q_{0})_{p}(1/2)
.

By coupling the AGP with the trees grown using MOG from a fixed pair of segments, we show that the conclusion of
Proposition

5.5
holds with high enough probability to deduce the result via a union bound.

Alternative growth procedure for a graph
T
:

Fix connected graphs
T_{1},T_{2}\subseteq L_{m_{2},d}
and a mixed-percolated hypercube
(Q_{0})_{p}(1/2)
.
Define
H
to be the restriction of
(Q_{0})_{p}(1/2)
to
L_{m_{1},m_{2}}
, and let
K\subseteq L_{m_{1},m_{2}}
.
Then, we write
\mathrm{AGP}(T_{i},K)
for the connected subgraph of
(H\cup T_{i})\setminus K
induced by the vertices which can be reached from
T
, by following monotone decreasing paths in
H
. Similarly, we write
\mathrm{AGP}(T_{1},T_{2},K)=\mathrm{AGP}(T_{1},K)\cup\mathrm{AGP}(T_{2},K)
. When
K=\varnothing
, we will simply write
\mathrm{AGP}(T)=\mathrm{AGP}(T,\varnothing)
and
\mathrm{AGP}(T_{1},T_{2})=\mathrm{AGP}(T_{1},T_{2},\varnothing)
.

We first motivate why coupling AGP with the trees grown using MOG is, in fact, quite natural.
Note that, if
T\in\mathcal{F}^{m_{2}}
and
T
was not used in a merge during MOG, then, at each iteration of MOG, every vertex which was revealed to be adjacent in
Q^{d}_{p}
to
T
and was processed according to
2
or
3
is appended to the tree
T
with probability at least
1/2
.
Hence, putting aside vertices adjacent in
Q^{d}_{p}
to
T
and processed according to
1
(of which, since
T
was not used in a merge, by
Lemma

5.3
there are typically few),
one can see that the tree in
\mathcal{F}^{m_{1}}
which extends
T
will dominate the graph obtained by gradually attaching each neighbour of
T
in
Q^{d}_{p}
to
T
with probability
1/2
.
At the same time, AGP is actually significantly easier to analyse than MOG due to the presence of only one or two trees in the process.

More formally, the connection between AGP and MOG is exhibited by the following coupling lemma.

Lemma 5.8
.

Fix a mergeable pair of segments
S,S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{m_{2}}
with corresponding trees
T_{S},T_{S^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{F}^{m_{2}}
. Then, there exists a coupling
\mathcal{C}
of the AGP (that is, a pair
((Q_{0})_{p}(1/2),K)
) with the iterations of MOG on the layers in
L_{m_{1},m_{2}-1}
such that, under the coupling

, exactly one of the following holds.

(a)

At least one of
S,S^{\prime}
is not in
\mathcal{S}^{m_{1}}
, that is, at some point a tree corresponding to
S
or
S^{\prime}
is used for a merge.

(b)

S,S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{m_{1}}
are not a mergeable pair, that is, there is some

such that

.

(c)

S,S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{m_{1}}

such that,
for the corresponding trees
\hat{T}_{S},\hat{T}_{S^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{F}^{m_{1}}

and

V(\mathrm{AGP}(T_{S^{\prime}},K))\subseteq V(\hat{T}_{S^{\prime}})
.
Furthermore, there exists an event
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}(S,S^{\prime})
which holds with probability

such that, conditionally on

has size at most

.

Let
(Z_{v})_{v\in V(Q_{0})}
be a sequence of independent Bernoulli
(1/2)
random variables.
We run the iterations of MOG on the layers

, where, whenever a vertex

is processed according to
2
and is adjacent to the tree extending
T_{S}
but not the tree extending

if and only if

. Let us write

for the set of vertices processed in this manner. Similarly, if
v
is processed according to
2
and is adjacent to the tree extending
T_{S^{\prime}}
but not the tree extending
T_{S}
, then we append it to
T_{S^{\prime}}
if and only if

. Let us write
\hat{K}(S^{\prime})
for the set of vertices processed in this manner.

Denote by
K
the set of vertices which are processed by MOG according to
1
and are adjacent to the (growing) trees extending
T_{S}
or

. We couple

with the process described above by identifying edge-percolation in

with edge-percolation in

processed by MOG, and by including each vertex in

in
(Q_{0})_{p}(1/2)
if

, and all other vertices according to Bernoulli(

) random variables, independent from the current construction. This completes the description of our coupling

.

Clearly, it cannot happen that two among

,
(b)
and

simultaneously occur.
In the sequel, we suppose that neither
(a)
nor
(b)
occurs — if some of them take place, we abandon the coupling automatically.
In this case, we show that

occurs.

We claim inductively that, at the iteration of MOG on

for an even

contains

For the induction step, any neighbour

of

. For neighbours processed according to

, since we assumed that

and

do not occur, at this moment

Hence, if

It remains to prove that
