Application of convolutional neural networks in focused ion beam scanning electron microscopy image denoising
Authors: Wen Xu, Dong Zhao, Baoding Zhu, Xiaopan Kou, Ying Zhou, Chuanrui Sun, Xin Nie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Focused Ion Beam Scanning Electron Microscopy (FIB-SEM) technology is a crucial method for obtaining three-dimensional (3D) nanoscale digital rock images. However, due to surface topography or compositional differences in rock samples, “curtain noise” appears in FIB-SEM images, degrading their quality. Traditional image filtering and denoising methods can improve FIB-SEM image quality but are ineffective at fully removing “curtain noise”. To address the issue, this paper proposes two Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) for curtain noise denoising: U-Net: Convolutional Networks for Biomedical Image Segmentation (U-Net) and Denoising Convolutional Neural Network (DnCNN). Experiments evaluate the denoising performance of these models using digital rock images obtained from FIB-SEM equipment. The results show that both models significantly enhance image quality. Based on the updated values, the Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR) for the U-Net and DnCNN models are 29.10dB and 27.28dB, respectively. The Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM) values are 0.73 and 0.38, and the Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity (LPIPS) values are 0.31 and 0.45. After denoising with both models, the estimated porosity of the digital rock images is closer to the true value. For a rock image with a manually segmented porosity of 6.3%, the threshold-segmented porosity decreases from 7.9% to 6.1% and 6.0% following curtain noise removal by U-Net and DnCNN network, respectively. The U-Net model demonstrates strong denoising performance while preserving texture and details, whereas the DnCNN model significantly reduces the curtain effect but introduces noticeable smoothing and blurring. Moreover, the generalization ability of the DnCNN model is relatively weaker than that of the U-Net, making it less effective when applied to unseen FIB-SEM images. This research provides a foundation for the accurate reconstruction of 3D nanoscale digital rock models.

Introduction

Three-dimensional (3D) digital rocks – digital representations of rocks in three dimensions – are widely used to analyze the micro-pore structure of rocks at the pore scale1. Rock physical property simulations based on digital cores are referred to as Digital Rock Physics (DRP). Compared to traditional rock physical experiments, DRP offers a faster and more cost-effective approach, enabling the simulation of different rock physical properties using the same 3D digital core. This also facilitates the analysis of correlations between various physical properties. Digital rock modeling methods can be broadly divided into physical experimental methods and numerical reconstruction methods2,3.

The physical experimental approach involves capturing or scanning a series of two-dimensional (2D) images of core samples using specialized instruments. These 2D images are then stacked and reconstructed into a 3D digital rock using computational modeling programs or software. Common physical experimental techniques include sequential slice imaging4, X-ray Computed Tomography (X-CT) scanning5,6, and Focused Ion Beam Scanning Electron Microscope (FIB-SEM)7. Sequential slice imaging was the earliest method used to construct digital rock models, but it is labor-intensive due to experimental constraints. The process requires continuous cutting, polishing, imaging, and finally stacking to reconstruct the pore structure. In contrast, X-CT offers a non-destructive, relatively low-cost, and time-saving method for generating 3D digital rock models. It provides a resolution in tens of nanometers, accurately characterizing pore structures, but it cannot depict micropores smaller than the instrument’s resolution.

The FIB-SEM dual-beam system is a critical technique for studying micro- and nano-scale pore structures. In this system, a Focused Ion Beam (FIB) accelerates and directs ion beams generated by an ion source onto the sample surface, enabling ion imaging, injection, etching, and deposition. During cross-sectional processing, the target position is precisely located, and the sample surface is tilted perpendicular to the ion beam for material removal. Simultaneously, a Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) captures images of the cross-section prepared by FIB. These 2D images are then reconstructed into a 3D digital core. Due to its high resolution, FIB-SEM is particularly useful for studying fine pore structures and integrating multiple physical quantities to construct digital cores, thereby providing a more comprehensive representation of rock properties.

A common challenge in FIB is that during the etching process, factors such as surface topography and compositional differences in rock samples lead to varying etching rates across different locations. This results in vertical stripe noise during SEM imaging8,9(as shown in Fig. 1). The presence of this noise directly impacts the accuracy of cross-sectional imaging and, consequently, the reliability of digital rock modeling. A common mitigation strategy involves using lower ion beam currents for cross-sectional refinement or depositing protective layers on the sample surface to smooth irregularities. While reducing the ion beam current can effectively minimize the curtain effect of FIB processing, it significantly increases processing time and does not fully eliminate the issue. Depositing protective layers helps counteract surface unevenness but does not address noise caused by compositional differences within the sample. Traditional image denoising methods, typically based on filters or statistical methods, lack specificity in handling complex noise patterns. With advancements in information technology, Artificial Intelligence (AI) techniques have demonstrated significant potential in image denoising. AI-based denoising methods leverage neural networks based on large datasets to identify and suppress specific noise characteristics, offering a more effective approach to handle complex noise types and distributions.Fig. 1Curtain effect caused by FIB cutting.

Curtain effect caused by FIB cutting.

Burger et al. introduced the use of multi-layer perceptron (MLP) for image denoising10, setting a new benchmark at the time. In addition, Xie et al. combined sparse coding, pre-trained deep neural networks, and denoising autoencoders11, while Mao et al. proposed deep convolutional encoding networks with symmetric encoder-decoder structures and jump connections between convolutional layers.12 Furthermore, Zhang et al. developed the Denoising Convolutional Neural Network (DnCNN) model, combining residual learning and batch normalization for improved denoising performance13. In the context of digital rock imaging using CNNs, Münch et al. proposed a curtain removal algorithm that applies discrete wavelet transforms to separate vertical components of the image at multiple scales8,14,15. The stripes are then filtered in Fourier space before applying an inverse wavelet transform, recursively processing the image to eliminate the curtain effect. This algorithm has proven highly effective in removing vertical extending stripes across the entire image and is now incorporated into professional software plugins. Moreover, Giannatou et al. explored deep learning for SEM image denoising, demonstrating its ability to detect noise variations and perform effective denoising16. Added to that, Ahuja introduced a deep learning-based superresolution model, Siamese-SR, which improves the resolution of digital rock images while preserving textures and reducing noise17. In addition, Sato et al. proposed a deep learning-based SEM image denoising technique for pattern measurement, evaluating it using a 256-frame average image as a pseudo-correction reference18. Finally, Ma described the application of stable diffusion models in digital rock images to improve resolution, denoise, and deblur19. From traditional image denoising methods to AI-based image denoising algorithms, AI has become an important tool for image denoising.

To mitigate the curtain effect in SEM imaging during the FIB cutting process, this study evaluates the denoising performance of two CNN models–an enhanced U-Net architecture with an integrated attention mechanism and the representative deep learning–based denoising model DnCNN–on a FIB-SEM digital core dataset13,20,21. The attention mechanism is introduced to strengthen the network’s ability to capture fine pore-scale features while suppressing noise, thereby improving the structural fidelity of the restored pore morphology. DnCNN is selected as a baseline due to its widespread use in image denoising tasks and its proven effectiveness across a variety of noise types, allowing for a representative comparison.

The models are assessed using image-based evaluation metrics: Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR), Structural Similarity Index (SSIM)22, and Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity (LPIPS). In addition, porosity–an essential physical property of the rock–is used to evaluate the effectiveness of denoising. Finally, the study examines whether denoised 2D images enhance the precision of porosity calculations in the construction of 3D digital rock models6,23.

The PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS metrics are commonly used to evaluate denoising performance, where higher PSNR and SSIM values and lower LPIPS values indicate better image restoration quality. In our dataset, the grayscale values of noise are very similar to those of the pore regions, leading to misclassification of noise as pores when a fixed threshold is applied. After denoising, applying the same threshold results in a reduced detected porosity that more closely matches the manually segmented ground-truth value. Experimental results show that the enhanced U-Net and DnCNN achieve PSNR values of 29.10 dB and 27.28 dB, SSIM values of 0.73 and 0.38, and LPIPS values of 0.31 and 0.45, respectively. Correspondingly, the porosity decreases from 7.9% to 6.1% after U-Net denoising and to 6.0% after DnCNN denoising, both approaching the true porosity of 6.3%. These results confirm that both models effectively suppress noise and improve image quality, especially in the highlighted orange regions (as shown in Fig. 2).Fig. 2Key steps in constructing the pore network model from FIB-SEM images.

Hyperparameters of the DnCNN network.

DnCNN utilizes a custom data loader to load the training data in batches by reading configuration files. During training, data augmentation techniques, flipping and rotation are applied to enhance data diversity. To ensure reproducibility and improve generalization, a random seed is set for random shuffling of data and noise generation. In this study, Gaussian noise with a standard deviation of 25 was added to the clean image patches in the residual learning framework to simulate realistic noisy conditions. The model parameters were updated through continuous forward propagation, loss calculation, and backward propagation.

The DnCNN network employs the Adam optimizer to efficiently adjust network weights in the complex space of noisy images. The learning rate is set to 0.0001, with MultiStepLR learning rate decay strategy to gradually reduce the learning rate during training, improving the model convergence. Epsilon and momentum parameters of BatchNorm2D are set to 0.0001 and 0.9, respectively, to improve training stability and accelerate model convergence. The model is saved and tested every 5000-training step.

Experiment

Dataset construction

The research data are primarily obtained from the FIB-SEM system, which provides nanoscale, high-resolution grayscale images that clearly reveal the microstructural characteristics of the samples, such as pore morphology, connectivity, and mineral interfaces. These images serve as a reliable foundation for further analysis of pore structures and deep learning model training. To construct the data set for the denoising research, a subset of representative images is selected from the raw data as the noisy data set (as shown in Fig. 5), and the corresponding noise-free data set is generated through manual processing. The specific procedure is as follows: first, the original FIB-SEM images are acquired; then, ImageJ software is used to manually create pore masks to accurately delineate the pore regions; next, a band-pass filter is applied to remove vertical stripe noise and improve the overall texture continuity; finally, Photoshop is used to eliminate residual white artifacts and further enhance the visual and structural quality of the images. Each processed image has a resolution of 1300\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}2000 pixels, with pixel intensity values ranging from 0 to 255, providing precise input data for subsequent model training and validation.Fig. 5Training dataset. Training dataset. (a) Original FIB-SEM image. (b) Image with manually created pore mask using ImageJ. (c) Denoised image obtained by applying a band-pass filter. (d) Image after removing white artifacts using Photoshop.

For the U-Net network, the dataset comprises 241 paired clean and noisy images, of which 90% are used for training and 10% for validation. The network is trained directly on these full-size images, allowing it to learn spatial context and fine structural details from the high-resolution data.

For the DnCNN network, the same 241 paired images are used. 40\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}40 patches are extracted from each image with a step size of 10, producing paired noisy and clean patches for training. The network predicts the residual image, and the clean patch is recovered by subtracting the predicted residual from the noisy input. Data augmentation, including horizontal and vertical flipping and rotations, is applied to the patches to increase the number of training samples and improve model generalization. No separate validation set was used during training; only a test set was employed to evaluate the model performance. The peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) on the test set was recorded throughout training, and the training process was manually terminated based on the observed performance.

Method

Attention mechanism

To enhance the U-Net model’s focus on regions affected by striped noise, an attention mechanism is introduced at the skip connections25–28. Residual learning

Residual learning is employed for image denoising by learning the difference between the noisy image and the clean image29,30. After predicting the noise, it is subtracted to restore the clean image from the input image. The equation can be expressed as follows:10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} y = F(x) + x \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where x is the input, y is the output, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{F}(x)$$\end{document} is the residual mapping learned by the network. Instead of directly learning \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y = H(x)$$\end{document}, the network learns \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{F}(x) = H(x) - x$$\end{document}, which represents the difference between the input and the desired output. This approach enables the network to more effectively capture subtle differences between the input and output. It is particularly beneficial when the difference is small, as residual learning can significantly accelerate the convergence of the network. Furthermore, residual learning is typically implemented using residual blocks, in which the output of each block consists of the original input added to the residual learned by the network. A typical residual block structure is defined as follows:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} y = F\left( x, \{W_i\}\right) + x \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{F}(\textrm{x})$$\end{document} is the residual obtained through several convolution layers, activation functions, and other operations, and x is the original input passed via skip connections.

FFT-based stripe noise removal

Stripe noise was removed using a frequency-domain filtering method based on the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) (as shown in Fig. 6, implemented in ImageJ). Image I(x, y) was first transformed from the spatial domain to the frequency domain using FFT:12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} F(u,v) = \sum _{x=0}^{M-1} \sum _{y=0}^{N-1} I(x,y) \, e^{-j 2 \pi \left( \frac{ux}{M} + \frac{vy}{N} \right) } \end{aligned}$$\end{document}In the frequency domain, the stripe noise appeared as distinct and sharp bright lines corresponding to high-intensity frequency components. These lines were manually masked in ImageJ by painting them black using the brush tool, which effectively set the corresponding frequency values to zero. The filtering process can be expressed as:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} F_{\text {filtered}}(u,v) = F(u,v) \cdot H(u,v) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where H(u, v) is a binary mask equal to 0 at the noise frequencies and 1 elsewhere. Model evaluation metrics

This study evaluates image quality based on two aspects: the first one is based on image processing-related evaluation metrics –PSNR and SSIM– for quantitative image assessment, while the second is based on the practical application of the image. This leads to introduce porosity as an evaluation metric17, which better reflects the practical value of image denoising in this study.

PSNR is a widely applied image quality evaluation metric that measures the similarity between the denoised and the original images. The higher the PSNR value is, the better the image quality and the more significant the denoising effect will be. PSNR is calculated as follows:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {PSNR} = 10 \log _{10} \left( \frac{\text {MAX}^2}{\text {MSE}} \right) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where MAX is the maximum pixel value of the image.

SSIM measures the similarity between the denoised and the reference images in terms of three components: luminance, contrast, and structure. Value range between 0 to 1, and the closer they are to the unit, the more similar the two images are, indicating better denoising performance.

LPIPS is a technique for measuring image similarity based on CNNs. It works by mapping the image into feature spaces at different layers and calculating features’differences. Unlike directly comparing pixel values, LPIPS focuses on high-level features, such as texture and shape, as it is more aligned with human visual perception. In our experiment, the LPIPS evaluation method utilizes a pre-trained deep network, AlexNet, to extract image features, and then calculates the distance between these features to evaluate the perceptual similarity between images. A low LPIPS value indicates that both images are more similar, while a high value denotes greater differences between these images.

The Euclidean distance is defined for the feature maps at each layer as follows:16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} d_l(x, y) = \left\| F_l(x) - F_l(y) \right\| _2^2 \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_1(x)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_1(y)$$\end{document} are the feature maps of the image of x and y at the 1-th layer.

Regarding generalization performance, the model’s ability to remove curtain effect noise depends on the noise characteristics in the training dataset. If the noise type or texture features of the test images differ significantly from the training data, the model’s generalization capability may be insufficient, thereby affecting the final denoising results. To verify the model’s generalization capability for the same noise type, this study applied two network models to previously unseen core images. Discussion

In the porosity segmentation process of original images, noise points are often mistakenly identified as part of the porosity (refer to Fig. 13). To mitigate noise influence, porosity is typically set lower than the actual value. In this experiment, both the U-Net and DnCNN models can effectively remove noise, improving the accuracy of porosity segmentation. However, the DnCNN model exhibits poor generalization capability on unseen data, while the U-Net model maintains robust performance across different images.Fig. 13Comparison of image threshold segmentation effects where (a) before U-Net denoising, (b) after U-Net denoising.

Comparison with FFT-based denoising

Through evaluating U-Net and FFT denoising methods, we found that after applying the Fourier transform, some stripe noise was significantly removed, but the overall grayscale values changed considerably. The U-Net model, utilizing skip connections and feature fusion, demonstrated superior denoising performance on core images. It effectively removed noise while preserving texture edges and maintained clear pore boundaries (as shown in Fig. 14). Due to the relatively weaker generalization ability of the DnCNN model, we only performed a comparison between U-Net and FFT.

Using evaluation metrics to assess the denoising effects of the models, PSNR measures the difference between the denoised and the original images, where a higher PSNR value indicates better denoising performance. The U-Net model achieved the highest PSNR value compared to FFT. SSIM, which ranges closer to one, indicates better image restoration, and the U-Net model also achieved the highest SSIM value. Finally, the LPIPS value of the U-Net model was lower than that of FFT (refer to Table 5), suggesting that the perceptual quality of the U-Net denoised images is superior. U-net network application effect

In this study, the U-Net model was used to denoise digital core images. A comparison of the results of the threshold segmentation before and after denoising (Fig. 15) confirmed that the curtain noise was effectively removed. The denoised images were then used to reconstruct a 3D digital core model2,7,31, providing a continuous pore-scale representation of the rock sample. Porosity analysis revealed a slight decrease from 5.22% (before denoising) to 5.06% (after denoising), indicating that noise-induced false pores were effectively suppressed.

To further characterize the pore structure, an important parameter for quantitative pore structure analysis, the equivalent pore diameter is commonly used to describe the size of pores in porous materials. It is obtained by comparing the pore volume (or area) distribution with that of ideal cylindrical (or circular) pores. The equivalent pore diameter reflects the size and distribution of pores in the material and serves as a key indicator for evaluating the performance of porous materials.

In this study, the aperture statistic was employed to quantify the size distribution of the segmented pore regions. For each identified pore, a connected-component labeling algorithm was applied to extract its corresponding set of pixels. The equivalent pore diameter was then calculated as:17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} d_{eq} = 2\sqrt{\frac{A_{pore}}{\pi }} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_{pore}$$\end{document} is the area of the pore in the 2D slice.

This statistic allows classification of micropores, mesopores, and macropores, providing information on the pore size distribution and structural heterogeneity of the rock sample. By analyzing the distributions of equivalent pore radii and the areal porosity along different core depths (Figs. 16 and 17), the distribution is dominated by pores in the 100–300 nm range, with the largest number in the 200–300 nm bin. After denoising, the number of very small pores (0–100 nm) decreases, indicating that noise points have been effectively removed. The main pore structure is well preserved, and most pores fall within the macropore range (>50 nm), consistent with the characteristics of the original FIB-SEM images.

These observations, combined with the threshold segmentation results (Fig. 15), demonstrate that the U-Net model significantly improved the accuracy of porosity calculations32, effectively reducing pore analysis errors. Conclusion

The study implemented a denoising method for FIB-SEM images using convolutional neural networks, specifically U-Net and DnCNN. The trained models were applied to denoise FIB-SEM images, and their performance was evaluated using PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS, and porosity segmentation. In the U-Net architecture, an attention mechanism was integrated to enhance the network’s ability to focus on noise during feature extraction. Meanwhile, the DnCNN network leveraged residual learning and incorporated Gaussian noise for training. The experimental results led to the following conclusions:Regarding PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS, U-Net demonstrated superior denoising performance compared to DnCNN. The U-Net model was able to retain image details and textures, whereas the DnCNN model resulted in smooth images with some loss of detail. Moreover, U-Net exhibited strong generalization ability across different FIB-SEM images, while DnCNN showed relatively poor generalization and was more sensitive to variations in the dataset.Regarding porosity segmentation, denoised images processed by both DnCNN and U-Net networks produced porosity values closer to the real porosity than those of noisy images or images processed using traditional denoising methods, with smaller error values.In practical digital core construction applications, such as using 2D images denoised by the U-Net network, the 2D denoising results were extended to a 3D structure. As a result, the porosity at different depths significantly decreased following denoising, validating the effectiveness of the model.

Regarding PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS, U-Net demonstrated superior denoising performance compared to DnCNN. The U-Net model was able to retain image details and textures, whereas the DnCNN model resulted in smooth images with some loss of detail.
