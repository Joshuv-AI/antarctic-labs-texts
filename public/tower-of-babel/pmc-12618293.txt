Balancing yield and stability: optimizing leaf pigment extraction to minimize chlorophyll degradation
Authors: Monika Sitarek-Andrzejczyk, Jakub Dobrzyński, Paweł Orliński, Jarosław L. Przybył
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Main conclusionMethanol proved to be the most efficient solvent for pigment extraction using pressurized liquid extraction, while extraction temperature and static time critically influenced both the yield and stability of the pigments.AbstractPhotosynthetic pigments, particularly chlorophylls and carotenoids, are central to plant physiology and are increasingly valued for their nutritional and functional benefits in the human diet. However, accurate quantification of these compounds remains challenging due to their sensitivity to degradation during sample preparation. The present study aimed to optimize extraction protocols for chlorophylls (a, b) and two major leaf carotenoids (β-carotene and lutein) from fresh parsley leaves, with a focus on balancing yield and stability. The effects of solvent type, extraction temperature, static extraction time, and number of extraction cycles were systematically evaluated using pressurized liquid extraction (PLE) and compared with conventional solvent extraction. High-performance liquid chromatography with diode-array detection was employed for quantitative analysis. Methanol consistently outperformed acetone as a solvent, yielding significantly higher recoveries of both chlorophylls and carotenoids. Optimal performance was observed under two distinct PLE conditions: three 5-min cycles at 100 °C, which provided a balanced recovery of pigments with low chlorophyll a degradation, and a single 5-min extraction at 125 °C, which maximized carotenoid yields but accelerated chlorophyll a breakdown. Extractions at 150 °C and prolonged dimethyl sulfoxide treatment resulted in substantial chlorophyll a degradation, highlighting the need for carefully controlled conditions. Compared with conventional solvent extraction, PLE reduced extraction time and offered superior yields for β-carotene and lutein. The results underscore the trade-offs inherent in pigment extraction and demonstrate that PLE, when optimized, provides a robust and efficient tool for evaluating the nutritional quality of fresh leafy vegetables. These findings may serve as a methodological foundation for both research applications and the development of more reliable, industry-relevant quality assessment protocols.

Introduction

Photosynthetic pigments such as chlorophylls and carotenoids are essential for plant function and are increasingly recognized for their health-promoting properties. Chlorophyll a and carotenoids are universally present in photosynthetic organisms, while chlorophyll b is specific to vascular plants and some algae (Scheer 2003). These pigments not only determine the green and yellow coloration of vegetables, important traits influencing consumer preference, but also serve as indicators of freshness and nutritional quality.

The chlorophyll a/b ratio, typically around 3:1 in higher plants, changes during leaf senescence as chlorophyll a degrades more rapidly, exposing carotenoids such as lutein and β-carotene (Lichtenthaler et al. 1981; Gross 1991). These carotenoids support human health through their roles as antioxidants, provitamin A precursors, and protectants against photooxidative stress (He et al. 2011; Fiedor and Burda 2014; Darvin et al. 2022; Terao 2023; Bufka et al. 2024).

Accurate quantification of these pigments in leafy vegetables is essential for assessing their nutritional potential. However, chlorophylls are chemically unstable and prone to degradation during sample preparation, forming derivatives such as pheophytins and chlorophyllides. These transformations can occur naturally or as artifacts during extraction and, if not properly controlled, may compromise analytical accuracy and lead to an overestimation of pigment degradation (Fiedor et al. 2003; Scheer 2003; Hu et al. 2013; Chazaux et al. 2022; Kratzer et al. 2022).

Traditional extraction methods often require long processing times and large volumes of solvents, limiting their utility in food research. Pressurized liquid extraction (PLE), a rapid and automated method using elevated temperature and pressure to enhance solvent efficiency, offers a promising alternative. PLE has been applied to extract carotenoids from various sources, including microalgae such as Chlorella vulgaris and Dunaliella salina (Cha et al. 2010; Yaiche Achour et al. 2023) and root vegetables like carrot (Mustafa et al. 2012; Tumbas Šaponjac et al. 2021). It has also been used with leafy matrices for pigment extraction, for example, wild nettle (Urtica dioica) (Repajić et al. 2020), and with aromatic herbs such as Myrtus communis, Pistacia lentiscus, Thymus vulgaris, Salvia officinalis, and Laurus nobilis (Cvitković et al. 2021) and bitter melon (Momordica charantia) (Rocha et al. 2025). However, these applications have mainly involved lyophilized or dried materials and typically relied on a single solvent system. To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first attempt to optimize the simultaneous extraction of chlorophylls and carotenoids from fresh leaves using PLE, addressing a largely unexplored area in the field. The present study aimed to optimize PLE conditions including solvent type, temperature, and extraction time for isolating chlorophylls and key carotenoids (β-carotene and lutein) from fresh parsley leaves. The attention was given to minimizing chlorophyll a degradation to ensure analytical reliability. Our study focused on the chlorophyll a derivative that could be reliably quantified and was present in all samples.

This work primarily contributes to advancing more accurate and efficient protocols for assessing the nutritional quality of green leafy vegetables and may serve as a foundation for the development of future industry-oriented protocols.

Material and methods

Sample preparation

Fresh leaves of parsley (Petroselinum crispum [Mill.] Fuss) were used as a model matrix. Parsley leaves (Petroselinum crispum var. tuberosum) were purchased from the local shop and prepared for the analysis in the laboratory of Department of Vegetable and Medicinal Plants. Lamina were separated from the petioles and immediately frozen under -80 °C in 1 ± 0.001 g aliquots.

Experimental design

The efficiency of two solvents, methanol and acetone, was evaluated at extraction temperatures of 50, 100, and 150 °C, using a single extraction cycle with three different static times (1, 3, and 5 min) to determine their effect on pigment yield. The effect of temperature on the extraction yield was examined across ten temperature points—room temperature, 40, 50, 60, 70, 80, 90, 100, 125, and 150 °C—using a single extraction cycle with a static time of 5 min. In addition, the influence of the number of extraction cycles (1, 2, and 3) and static times (1, 2, 5, and 10 min) on pigment recovery was assessed at 100 °C. Finally, four conventional solvent extraction methods were compared with two methanol-based PLE procedures conducted at 100 °C (three cycles) and 125 °C (single cycle with a 5-min static time), respectively.

Pigment extraction

Accelerated Solvent Extractor—ASE™ 350 (Thermo Scientific™ Dionex™, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) was used for PLE. Samples were placed in a 10 ml stainless-steel extraction cell with cellulose or glass fiber filter in the cell out. Extractions were performed at ~ 10.3 MPa, with a 5 min heat-up time, 50% flush volume and a 30 s purge time. Extraction solvent, temperature, static time, and number of static cycles were varied depending on the combination. The extracts were collected in 60 ml glass sample vials fitted with PTFE-coated rubber caps.

Conventional extraction procedures were carried out by shaking (45 min, dark, ambient temperature) the samples with three types of solvents (80% aqueous acetone, 96% acetone, 99.8 methanol) or incubation with dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) for 2 h at 55 °C in a water bath. Each extract was transferred to a 50-ml volumetric flask and filtered through 0.20-μm PTFE syringe filter prior to HPLC analysis. Triplicate extractions were carried out for each combination.

HPLC analysis

Chlorophyll a (CID 6477652), chlorophyll b (CID 11593175), β-carotene (CID 5280489), and lutein (CID 5281243) were quantified. Commercially available standard solutions of chlorophyll a, b, β-carotene, and lutein (Niagen—ChromaDex, Los Angeles, CA, USA) were used as standard stock solutions. Further calibration levels were prepared by diluting these solutions with methanol in 10-ml volumetric flasks (injected volumes ranges: 0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5 and 1 ml). The working solutions were injected (0.001 ml) on a column in six replicates (n = 6) using SIL-20AC HT to generate a six-point calibration curve for each standard compounds separately using LC Solution chromatography software. The peak table and spectra library (190–800 nm) of individual compounds were created. Standard curve parameters were calculated with MS Excel. Signal-to-noise ratio approach was used to determine LOD (S/N of 3:1) and LOQ (S/N of 10:1). The analyses were performed using a Shimadzu chromatograph equipped with auto-sampler SIL-20AC HT, photodiode array detector SPD-M20A PDA, and LC Solution chromatography software. C-18 reversed-phase column (Phenomenex Kinetex®, Torrance, CA, USA; 2.6 μm, C18, 100 Å, 100 × 4.60 mm i.d.) was used as solid phase. As a mobile phase, pure methanol was used. The following conditions were applied: isocratic elution, flow rate 2.0 ml × min−1, oven temperature 30 °C, injection volume 0.01 ml, total time of analysis 5 min. Peak identification was conducted by comparison of retention time and UV-spectra recorded between 190 and 800 nm of standards. The content of the determined compounds was calculated in mg per 100 g of fresh weight (FW). Chlorophyll a derivative determination: This compound was not formally identified because no mass spectrometry (MS) analysis was performed. Identification was based solely on retention time and UV–Vis spectra (190–800 nm); although its spectra matched chlorophyll a, its retention time differed, indicating that it is a distinct derivative. The chlorophyll a derivative was quantified using a chlorophyll a calibration curve and is reported as chlorophyll a equivalent per 100 g of FW.

Statistical analysis

All data were subjected to multi-way ANOVA, and significant differences among means were determined using Tukey’s HSD test at a significance level of α = 0.05. Analyses were performed using Statistica 12 (TIBCO Software Inc., Palo Alto, CA, USA).

Results

The influence of the solvent, temperature, and time on the extraction yield

All tested factors had a significant impact on the efficiency of pigments extraction. Both in the case of chlorophylls and carotenoids, methanol proved to be a better solvent than acetone (Fig. 1a–e). Extending the extraction time to 5 min markedly improved the extraction efficiency of all pigments at 100 °C. In acetone, yield’s range was from 22 to 57%, whereas in methanol, enhancements exceeded 90% for carotenoids and was higher for chlorophyll than it was observed in acetone. Prolonged extraction time at 50 °C did not significantly enhance extraction efficiency, indicating that the temperature was suboptimal for effective release of pigments. In contrast, elevated temperatures of 100 °C and 150 °C facilitated significantly higher yields with increasing extraction time. The highest concentrations of chlorophyll b and β-carotene were observed in methanolic extracts at 150 °C with a 5-min static time. A pronounced increase in chlorophyll a derivative content was observed in methanol extracts following static extraction at 100 °C for 5 min, indicating that short-term exposure to high temperatures promotes pigment degradation or transformation into derivative forms in parsley leaves (Fig. 1e). This effect was not observed in acetone extracts at 100 °C, where the levels of chlorophyll a derivatives remained relatively stable over different extraction time.

The highest concentrations of chlorophyll a derivatives were detected at 150 °C in both solvents, with no significant difference between methanol (7.44 ± 0.59 mg × 100 g⁻1 FW) and acetone (7.69 ± 3.46 mg × 100 g⁻1 FW).

The effect of temperature on the extraction yield

A marked decline in chlorophyll stability was observed at extraction temperatures above 125 °C. The influence of the number of cycles and their static time on the extraction yield

Both, the number of extraction cycles and their static duration, had a significant effect on the efficiency and reproducibility of plant pigment extraction from parsley leaves. The optimal condition was three extraction cycles with a static time of 5 min each (Tables 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5). While a single 5-min cycle was also effective, particularly for β-carotene extraction, it yielded less consistent results for lutein and chlorophyll b, suggesting that repeated solvent–matrix interactions improved extraction reproducibility. In contrast, shorter cycles of 1 or 2 min, regardless of their number, proved to be insufficient to fully extract the pigments, likely due to limited contact time between the solvent and plant matrix. The most efficient β‑carotene extraction was achieved using PLE1. This condition also provided excellent lutein yields, comparable to those obtained from conventional extraction with 99.8% methanol (C3). C3 extraction method gave the highest chlorophyll a and b contents (103.83 and 36.50 mg × 100 g⁻1 FW, respectively). However, despite its efficiency for chlorophylls, C3 extraction resulted in the lowest β‑carotene yield. C2 method was the least effective for chlorophyll a recovery.

PLE significantly improved β‑carotene extraction in methanol. PLE1 proved to be optimal for the simultaneous extraction of the two major leaf carotenoids, though it was accompanied by substantial chlorophyll a degradation. In contrast, PLE2 reduced the chlorophyll a loss, providing a more reliable estimation.

Discussion

The influence of the solvent, temperature, and time on the extraction yield

Solvent choice, extraction temperature, and extraction time influenced pigment yield and stability. Although acetone and its aqueous solutions are widely used due to sharp chlorophyll absorption peaks and established spectrophotometric equations (Jeffrey and Humphrey 1975; Lichtenthaler and Wellburn 1983). Recent evidence, including this study, suggests that methanol, ethanol, and their mixtures provide superior extraction efficiency (Zapata et al. 2000; Carter and Knapp 2001; Ritchie 2006). For example, Kitada et al. (2009) reported that ethanol as a co-solvent in supercritical CO₂ extraction yielded over threefold higher chlorophyll levels and nearly twice the amount of lutein and β-carotene compared to acetone. Zeaxanthin extraction from Chlorella ellipsoidea was also optimized with ethanol (Koo et al. 2012). Conversely, Denery et al. (2004) found acetone and ethanol equally effective for lutein and astaxanthin extraction from Haematococcus pluvialis.

The improvement in extraction efficiency at elevated temperatures observed in this study supports the idea that heat helps overcome diffusion barriers in plant tissues—an effect less relevant for microalgae or phytoplankton, which are more susceptible to pigment degradation at high temperatures (Sartory and Grobbelaar 1984). Differences in tissue composition and structural characteristics explain the species-dependent extraction efficiency (Minocha et al. 2009). The increase in chlorophyll a derivatives in methanol at 100 °C suggests that short-term thermal exposure promotes pigment degradation or transformation, an effect not observed in acetone extracts. The highest chlorophyll a derivative concentrations at 150 °C indicate that thermal stress plays a key role in pigment breakdown, even as overall extraction efficiency improves. These findings highlight the interaction between solvent polarity, extraction temperature, and tissue structure in optimizing pigment recovery while minimizing degradation.

The effect of temperature on the extraction yield

Temperature plays a decisive role in determining pigment extraction efficiency and stability, influencing both solubility and degradation pathways. Understanding its impact is essential for optimizing PLE conditions, as variations in thermal exposure can markedly alter pigment recovery across different plant matrices.

Sánchez et al. (2014) reported minimal losses of β-carotene and lutein under high-pressure (625 MPa) thermal treatments, whereas chlorophyll a degraded substantially, even at 70 °C, while chlorophyll b was more stable. Weemaes et al. (1999) found that chlorophyll a and b in broccoli juice degraded above 60 °C, with chlorophyll a more heat-sensitive than b (activation energies ~ 71 kJ mol⁻1 and ~ 67 kJ mol⁻1, respectively).

Compared to these studies, our observations show less severe chlorophyll degradation, with significant losses only at 150 °C, likely due to the lower pressure used in PLE. This highlights the importance of optimizing both temperature and pressure for pigment preservation.

Temperature appears to be the dominant factor influencing PLE efficiency, affecting solvent viscosity, solubility of target compounds, and enzyme activity related to pigment degradation. Previous studies in Chlorella species reported optimal carotenoid extraction between 115–117 °C and chlorophyll stability up to 174 °C, partly due to chlorophyllase inactivation at elevated temperatures (Cha et al. 2010; Koo et al. 2012). Mustafa et al. (2012) also emphasized the influence of temperature on carotenoid recovery from carrot, noting poor extraction at 180 °C and optimal β-carotene yield (89%) at 60 °C, indicating the need to evaluate intermediate temperatures for better efficiency and repeatability.

The influence of the number of cycles and their static time on the extraction yield

Beyond solvent and temperature, the duration and repetition of extraction cycles markedly influenced pigment recovery. Our findings showed that three extraction cycles with a 5-min static time provided the highest and most reproducible pigment yields, particularly for β-carotene and chlorophylls. The results demonstrate that extraction efficiency and reproducibility are strongly influenced by both cycle number and duration, with repeated short cycles improving solvent penetration and solute release. The formation of chlorophyll a derivatives during longer static periods highlights the risk of thermal degradation, emphasizing the need to balance extraction time with pigment stability.

Previous studies suggest the matrix- and compound-specific nature of PLE optimization. For example Cha et al. (2010) showed in Chlorella vulgaris that chlorophyll b reached a maximum yield after 3.4 min at 169.9 °C, while chlorophyll a required 14.7 min at 173.4 °C, and carotenoids needed significantly longer times. Denery et al. (2004) observed in Haematococcus pluvialis that increased PLE temperatures slightly reduced lutein and astaxanthin concentrations. Three 5-min cycles at only 20 °C using acetone were sufficient for effective carotenoid extraction. These observations highlight that pigment stability and extraction behavior are highly dependent on both the chemical nature of the compound and the plant or algal matrix, as well as the applied temperature and pressure. Differences in solvent–matrix interactions and thermal sensitivity must be considered to optimize PLE protocols.

Comparison of conventional extraction methods to PLE

The results highlight that solvent choice, temperature, and pressure strongly influence pigment extraction efficiency and selectivity. The high formation of chlorophyll a derivative in DMSO (C3) aligns with earlier studies (Hiscox and Israelstam 1979; Tait and Hik 2003), which showed that DMSO provides good pigment stability and comparable yields to aqueous acetone, with efficiency depending on tissue type and extraction conditions. While DMSO was effective for chlorophyll a and carotenoids, it performed poorly for chlorophyll b, illustrating compound-specific solvent interactions.

In contrast, acetone-based methods (C1, C2) underperformed for carotenoids, especially β-carotene, emphasizing that conventional solvents may not efficiently extract all target pigments simultaneously. PLE methods demonstrate that high pressure improves carotenoid extraction, particularly β-carotene, by enhancing solvent penetration and mass transfer. PLE1 optimized carotenoid recovery but caused chlorophyll a degradation, indicating a trade-off between carotenoid yield and pigment stability. PLE2 mitigated chlorophyll a loss, making it more suitable for studies requiring accurate chlorophyll a/b ratios or rapid chlorophyll quantification.

These observations underscore the importance of tailoring extraction protocols to target pigments, plant tissue characteristics, and analytical goals, balancing yield, selectivity, and pigment stability.

Conclusion and limitations

The present study highlights pressurized liquid extraction (PLE) as a powerful alternative to conventional methods for isolating chlorophylls and carotenoids from fresh leafy vegetables. Methanol proved to be the most efficient solvent, while extraction temperature and static time critically influenced pigment yield and stability. Based on our results, we recommend either PLE at 125 °C for 5 min or PLE at 100 °C for three 5-min cycles for simultaneous quantification of chlorophylls and carotenoids. Potential distortions in chlorophyll a content can be corrected by including its main degradation product in the calculation. If the objective is to study chlorophyll derivative formation prior to extraction, shaking in 99.8% methanol at ambient temperature in the absence of light is recommended. For industrial applications, PLE offers the best balance of efficiency, time, and yield. When high β-carotene and lutein recovery is the priority, PLE at 125 °C for 5 min is recommended, accepting some degradation of chlorophyll a. If chlorophyll preservation is essential, PLE at 100 °C for 3 × 5 min provides a better balance between pigment stability and carotenoid extraction. For maximum chlorophyll b yield, conventional methanol extraction remains the preferred method, though safety protocols and solvent recovery systems are essential for large-scale use. DMSO, despite good pigment recovery in research, is unsuitable for industrial production due to food safety concerns and poor scalability.By reducing solvent use and extraction time while improving analytical accuracy, PLE addresses key limitations of traditional approaches. Although methanol itself is unsuitable for food processing on an industrial scale, the optimized methodology establishes a benchmark for the design of safer, food-grade alternatives. The findings not only improve reliability in nutritional quality assessment but also contribute to a deeper understanding of chlorophyll a stability during extraction. Ultimately, this work may guide future research toward green extraction technologies and standardized methodologies that ensure accurate evaluation of the health-promoting compounds in leafy vegetables.This study was conducted exclusively on fresh parsley leaves, and preliminary trials should be performed before applying these methods to other plant species. The focus on fresh leaves is both a limitation and an advantage—while results may differ for dried material, avoiding drying steps can significantly reduce costs in industrial processing, where drying is one of the most energy intensive operations. Future research should focus on developing edible, eco-friendly solvents that match the efficiency of PLE while ensuring product safety. In addition, process optimization studies could explore hybrid extraction methods that combine PLE with greener solvents to enhance yields while minimizing pigment degradation.
