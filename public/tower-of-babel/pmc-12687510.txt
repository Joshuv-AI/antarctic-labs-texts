Predictive power of socio-demographic attributes of healthcare workers on data quality in Tanzanian regional referral hospitals
Authors: Rogate Phinias, Mikidadi Muhanga, Joshua Malago
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHealthcare data quality is essential towards effective decision-making. Data of high quality are accurate, complete, consistent, and timely. Any mistake in patient information is likely to result in serious life-threatening issues. Monitoring and evaluation systems have been introduced to address these data quality issues. These systems are affected by human inputs which can subsequently introduce errors that lower the quality of data. This study examined the influence of socio-demographic attributes of healthcare workers on data quality in selected regional referral hospitals in Tanzania.MethodologyA cross-sectional study design was conducted from August to October 2024 across eight regional referral hospitals in Tanzania. Using simple random sampling, 336 healthcare workers involved in regular patient data entry were recruited. The sample size was determined using Cochran’s formula. Data were collected through structured questionnaires, cleaned, and analyzed using IBM SPSS v27, applying both descriptive statistics and multiple linear regression to examine the relationship between socio-demographic factors and healthcare data quality.ResultsThe study identified several significant predictors of data quality. Age had a positive relationship with accuracy (β = 0.241, p <.001), completeness (β = 0.159, p <.001) and timeliness (β = 0.202, p <.001), while sex was positively associated with all outcomes, particularly completeness (β = 0.428, p <.001), and consistency (β = 0.561, p <.001). Education positively impacted accuracy (β = 0.161, p <.01), and timeliness (β = 0.240, p <.01), but negatively affected completeness (β=-0.166, p <.01), and consistency (β=-0.144, p <.01). Marital status significantly predicted completeness (β = 0.139, p =.044), while experience showed a negative relationship with timeliness (β=-0.369, p <.001). All models were statistically significant (all p <.001).ConclusionThis study highlights the significant influence of socio-demographic factors, particularly age and sex, on the accuracy and completeness of healthcare data. These findings suggest that the characteristics of patient data collectors can either enhance or hinder data quality. Therefore, it is crucial to implement targeted training programs for healthcare workers, emphasizing their role in maintaining high-quality healthcare data.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13219-1.

Introduction

In the rapidly evolving healthcare sector, quality data is crucial for improving patient health outcomes, enhancing decision-making, fostering innovation, and delivering effective health services [1, 2]. Healthcare data are used for both primary purposes (direct patient care and clinical decision-making) and secondary purposes (such as research, quality improvement, public health surveillance, and policy-making). Globally, healthcare data quality is assessed across four dimensions: accuracy, completeness, consistency, and timeliness [3]. While all four dimensions support both primary and secondary uses, timeliness is especially critical for real-time clinical decisions, whereas completeness and consistency are foundational for reliable secondary analyses.

Accuracy ensures that recorded data correctly reflects a patient’s health status, some minor errors can threaten patient safety. Completeness involves capturing all relevant patient information, some missing data can hinder understanding of a patient’s medical history. Consistency guarantees that data is uniformly provided, preventing issues like delayed treatments and misdiagnoses. Timeliness is essential for real-time decision-making, ensuring that data reflects the most current patient information. In absence of these dimensions, healthcare data can lack significance and quality [4, 5].

Globally, Healthcare data quality remains dynamic, and countries are actively working to enhance its consistency and reliability, these efforts are pioneered by the World Health Organization (WHO), notably the WHO has developed and implemented the healthcare digital strategy to ensure all countries in the world collect, store, process and disseminate quality healthcare data [6].

In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), a growing body of literature highlights significant challenges related to the healthcare data quality. Such studies indicate that healthcare data in these regions often suffers from various issues, including inaccuracies, incompleteness, and inconsistencies, which undermine the effectiveness of health interventions and policy-making [4, 7]. Similarly, many African nations face similar challenges regarding healthcare data quality [4, 5]. These studies indicate that the completeness, accuracy, and timeliness of health data in these countries vary significantly, with reported rates ranging from 34 to 72%. Such variability suggests that while some regions have made steps to improve their data systems, others continue to struggle with some issues that hinder their ability to collect and use healthcare data effectively.

Healthcare Data Quality (HDQ) must be collected and stored at every level of the healthcare system (i.e. Primary, secondary and tertiary) to support informed decision-making [8]. In Tanzania, monitoring and evaluation (M&E) systems have been introduced in all regional referral hospitals (RRH) to ensure the production of quality data [9]. These systems, including Afyacare, Government of Tanzania Health Operations Management Information System (GoTHOMIS), District Health Information System 2 (DHIS2), and Afya Electronic Health Management System (Afya-EHMS) among others, have been designed to collect, store, process, and share patient information. The government has also developed policies, strategies and guidelines to ensure that HDQ is generated through these systems. These include Monitoring and Evaluation Strategic Framework (MESF) 2020–2025, Health Sector Strategic Plan July 2021– June 2026 (HSSP V)-Leaving No One Behind, and Health Information Management System Guidelines (HISG) [10, 12]. Furthermore, the health sector is making good progress in digitalizing its services, it has introduced the Digital Health Strategy July 2019-June 2024 which needs all levels of healthcare systems use and provide digital health services along with digital M&E systems [13, 14]. However, despite these efforts, producing quality healthcare data remains a challenge due to notable inaccuracies, incompleteness, delays, and inconsistencies. Human errors are often cited as a major factor [15, 18]. This is because, healthcare workers may unintendedly enter wrong data or overlook a critical data point in the documentation process.

In addressing data quality issues, it is good to investigate the role of human differences in the healthcare context by considering their socio-demographic attributes. Socio-demographic factors such as age, sex, job roles, experience, marital status, and education have been reported to influence health behaviors, performance of healthcare workers, access to healthcare services, and the accuracy of data reporting [19]. Literature indicates that socio-demographic characteristics contribute to performance of healthcare workers hence resulting to inaccuracies, delays, and inconsistencies in healthcare data. For instance, Chebureti and Odhiambo [19] argue that marital status influences routine data quality, noting that married civil servants often prioritize social matters over official tasks, while single staff may work longer hours due to fewer social obligations. Similarly, Belita et al. [20] report that marital status influence absenteeism in various ways due to family responsibilities, these marital challenges leads to delays in data collection/entry, rushing to family matters hence impact in the completeness and accuracy of data. In contrast, Yendemye and Nimubona [21] argue that stable home lives and strong emotional support motivate married staff to value their jobs more, translating into greater attention to data completeness and accuracy. Tuna et al. [22] found that the variability in the type of department affects job satisfaction, while age was reported to associate with increase job stress, ultimately reducing healthcare worker performance. They claim that younger workers are generally more eager and productive; however, this contrasts with Kujala [23], who posited that younger individuals may perform poorly as well. Additionally, variations in education lead to differences in how data is collected and reported [24, 25], further complicating the issue of data quality.

Despite the evident need for studies on socio-demographic and healthcare data quality, there is scant empirical evidence from the literature indicating that this aspect has been studied in Tanzania. This study, therefore, addresses this knowledge gap by assessing how the socio-demographic characteristics of healthcare workers predict the production of quality healthcare data in regional referral hospitals in Tanzania. It is important to understand how these factors interplay with data quality to develop strategies to improve healthcare data collection. By identifying the specific challenges faced by different groups within the healthcare workforce, targeted training programs can be designed to address gaps in knowledge and skills.

Methods

Study setting, period and design

The cross-sectional research design was employed in this study from August to October 2024 in eight selected RRH in Tanzania. The country has 28 RRHs, in this study, multi-stage sampling technique was employed to get eight RRH. In the initial stage, all the 28 RRH were identified. In the second stage, the eight administrative zones were recognized, followed by random selection of one RRH from each zone, as shown in Fig. 1., the selected RRH were Mount Meru (Northern zone), Mwalimu Nyerere Memorial (Lake zone), Maweni (Western zone), Singida (Central zone), Morogoro (Eastern zone), Mbeya (South-West highland zone), Sokoine (Southern zone) and Iringa RRH (Southern Highland zone). This was done to ensure randomization and generalizability of the study. The study population comprised all healthcare workers involved in regular patient data entry activities from patients’ arrival at the hospital to their departure.

The 336 individuals were identified using simple random sampling technique and proportionately allocated to all the 8 RRH with 39 to 45 respondents in each RRH. To manage attrition, we over-sampled by 10%, inviting 370 healthcare workers via simple random sampling (including alternates) across the eight RRHs. Two reminder visits were made, yielding 324 usable questionnaires (87.6% of those approached). The 12 non-responders were then replaced by their pre-selected alternates to reach a final sample of 336 (90.8% response rate).

Study personnel

The research team comprised two core members who visited all eight RRHs to coordinate and oversee data collection activities. In accordance with ethical clearance guidelines issued by the Ministry of Health, the team was required to collaborate with staff from each study site during data collection. At every facility, two healthcare workers who were willing to assist were identified and temporarily integrated into the research team. These local collaborators supported questionnaire administration and helped ensure adherence to facility protocols, while the core team-maintained consistency and quality control across all sites.

Data collection and measurement

The questionnaire used in this study was specifically developed for this research. Data were collected through a self-administered questionnaire designed to assess how socio-demographic attributes such as age, sex, marital status, education, and years of experience, predict the quality of healthcare data. The quality of data was evaluated based on four key factors: accuracy, completeness, consistency, and timeliness of data entry. Table 1 presents the measurement of the dependent variable, while Table 2 outlines the measurement of the independent variables. For additional data on the measurement instruments used in the study, see Supplementary Material.

Healthcare data quality level was measured based on percentiles, dividing the data into three distinct quality levels: Low, Moderate, and High. The classification was based on the three percentile ranges as shown in Table 3: Data quality was considered low when it fell below the 33.33rd percentile, it was categorized as moderate when ranged between the 33.33rd and 66.66th percentiles, and a high when it exceeded the 67.00th percentile. Similarly, studies by [27] and [28] employed this approach to categorize levels.

Data processing and analysis

Data entry and cleaning were done in Microsoft excel and analyzed using IBM SPSS version 27. Descriptive analysis used frequency, percentage to describe the socio-demographic factors and the dependent variable. Subsequently, Likert scale responses were converted into mean values for inclusion in multiple regression analysis. Age, education, and experience were treated as continuous variables, whereas Sex, and marital status were dummy variables which were transformed using dummy coding. Thereafter, diagnostic tests of dataset and multiple linear regression analysis were done to establish the relationship between the socio-demographic factors and production of high-quality healthcare data.

Diagnostic and reliability test of the dataset

Table 6 indicates the diagnostic and reliability test of the dataset. Five diagnostic tests were conducted to evaluate the assumptions of multiple linear regression analysis. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test yielded a P-value of 0.394, indicating that the dataset follows a normal distribution, thus confirming the normality assumption. ANOVA showed insignificant deviations from linearity, leading to the acceptance of the null hypothesis that no deviations exist. Also, Tolerance and Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) metrics confirmed that multicollinearity was not a concern, as Tolerance was above 0.1 and VIF below 10. The Breusch–Pagan test produced a significance value of 0.215, above the acceptable level, confirming no heteroscedasticity in the dataset. The Durbin-Watson results were 1.789 which confirmed the assumption of no auto-correlation in the dataset. In addition, Table 6 illustrates the Cronbach’s alpha results exceeding the common threshold of 0.7.

Discussion

This study examined the relationship between various socio-demographic factors of healthcare workers and perceived healthcare data quality from selected eight regional referral hospitals. The findings indicate that all studied socio-demographic factors (age, sex, education, marital status and years of experience) have positive correlation with the overall perceived quality of healthcare data. Notably, age and Sex emerged as significant predictors, demonstrating a strong association with healthcare data quality in healthcare settings, specifically data accuracy and completeness. These findings are similar to a number of empirical literature [19, 22, 29, 30].

The findings indicate that while age does not significantly impact data consistency, it does positively affect data quality. Young individuals tend to report more accurate, complete, and timely data. This finding is consistent with existing literature suggesting that age does significantly affect job-related stress, satisfaction, performance, and ultimately data quality in the healthcare sector [21]. Younger workers often face less stress and have more enthusiasm early in their careers, which boosts their job satisfaction and performance, leading to better data entry. Additionally, young individuals are frequent users of digital technologies even in their personal lives [31, 32], this makes them more familiar with electronic M&E systems too. On the other hand, older healthcare workers may struggle with electronic M&E systems due to less familiarity with technology, which can result in more errors. However, providing adequate training can help bridge the technological gap, enabling healthcare workers of all ages to produce high-quality data [33].

The study also found that sex plays a significant role in the perceived production of quality healthcare data, especially in data accuracy, completeness, and consistency, indicating that male workers influence higher reporting in these areas. Existing literature presents mixed findings on this issue. Some research indicates that female healthcare workers spend more time engaging with electronic health systems, which may enhance the accuracy of data entry [34]. Others argue that this increased time often comes at a cost, as female workers frequently report higher workloads, job burnout, and stress [35]. These contrasting perspectives highlight the complex relationship between Sex and data quality. While the additional time women spend on health information systems can lead to more accurate data entry, the stress and burnout associated with their workloads may ultimately hinder their ability to produce quality data. This calls for efforts by healthcare institutions to address these challenges for optimizing data quality across sex groups.

The study found no significant association between marital status and the perceived quality of healthcare data, with only a weak relationship noted in data completeness. This suggests that unmarried healthcare workers do not necessarily produce quality data, which contrasts with the assumption that personal situations like marital status would influence professional performance. In contrast, Yendemye and Nimubona [21] argue that married workers may perform better in enhancing data quality for two key reasons: their stable home life and emotional support, which contribute positively to data management, and the additional family responsibilities that may lead them to value their jobs more, resulting in greater attention to data quality. These factors suggest that, for some workers, marriage may provide both practical and emotional stability, which could improve their focus and performance in tasks such as data entry. On the other hand, these studies [19] and [36] found that marital status does not significantly affect healthcare data quality. They offer three explanations for this: first, family responsibilities can introduce additional stress for married workers, which may reduce their focus on data entry compared to single workers who have fewer personal obligations. Second, married individuals often have fewer flexible schedules, limiting the time they can dedicate to thorough data entry. Third, while marital happiness might increase focus and attention to detail, those experiencing marital difficulties may struggle with concentration, leading to errors in data entry. These differing viewpoints highlight the complexity of the relationship between marital status and work performance. While the current study suggests no strong connection, other research offers sensible reasons for how marital status could either enhance or hinder data quality. These variations in findings could be influenced by cultural, environmental, or individual differences, and future studies may need to explore these factors more deeply.

The study also found mixed effects between healthcare workers’ education levels and healthcare data quality, where education level positively affects timeliness of data while negatively affects data completeness and consistency, indicating that higher education may lead to timely reporting but complicate other aspects of data quality. This finding opposes some literature that emphasizes the importance of education level in enhancing data quality. For instance, Solomon et al. [24] argue that higher education correlates with better data outcomes due to increased confidence, similarly Ndishaa et al. [25] suggest that higher education improves skills and attention to detail, leading to better data management. However, other studies, including those by Theofileo [30] and Glèlè et al. [37], argue that education alone is insufficient, instead, factors like work engagement, satisfaction, and organizational support are critical. Ndishaa et al. [25] also propose that organizational culture and support can outweigh individual education levels. Admittedly, despite the fact the current study found high education level is important in the production of data quality, a comprehensive approach that includes training and supportive environments is necessary for improving healthcare data quality.

In addition, the association between years of experience and healthcare data quality is complicated. Research often shows that experienced workers know the health information system well and can use it more quickly than those with less experience [38]. This familiarity can lead to more accurate and complete data, fewer mistakes, and better timeliness. However, the current study questions the common belief that years of experience alone determine data quality. There are three main reasons for this. First, a worker with extensive experience might not have received proper training in data management or in using newer system modules. Second, the work environment, including IT infrastructure robustness, system interoperability, and user-support services can significantly moderate performance [39]. For instance, even experienced staff may struggle in facilities with isolated systems that require duplicate entry or lack real-time integration. Third, demographic factors such as digital literacy, age, and educational background interact with these environmental conditions: younger or more digitally literate workers tend to adapt quickly to new interoperability standards, whereas others may experience frustration or make more errors when infrastructure is poor [40, 41].

Indeed, Komba and Lwoga [40] found that in hospitals with high system interoperability, staff with lower baseline digital skills achieved data-entry accuracy rates comparable to their more experienced peers, provided sufficient on-site IT support was available. Similarly, Zheng et al. [41] showed that older healthcare workers’ familiarity with clinical workflows only translated into high-quality data when core infrastructure such as stable networks, and integrated electronic records minimized manual workarounds. Taken together, these findings suggest that enhancing both the technological environment and tailored training rather than relying on tenure alone may be the most effective route to consistently high-quality healthcare data.

Implication for Tanzania’s health system

Our findings underscore the need for a coordinated national strategy that aligns digital-health investments with workforce diversity. At the policy level, the Ministry of Health should integrate socio-demographic-tailored data-quality training into its Health Information System strategy, ensuring both pre-service curricula and in-service programs address age, sex, and digital-literacy gaps. Resources must be prioritized to upgrade IT infrastructure especially interoperability and real-time integration across all regional referral hospitals to eliminate fragmented systems and reduce redundant data entry. Facility managers should establish on-site IT support teams and flexible staffing models to mitigate burnout and influence the strengths of both younger, tech-adept staff and experienced workers. Finally, embedding regular, sociodemographically sensitive audits and feedback circles will help track progress, fine-tune interventions, and foster a culture of continuous improvement; together, these measures will strengthen Tanzania’s health information backbone and ultimately enhance patient care outcomes.

Study limitation and area for further research

This study has some limitations. First, it uses a cross-sectional design, which means it collects data at a single point in time. This approach only shows a snapshot of the relationship between the variables. Future research should consider longitudinal studies to track how socio-demographic factors relate to the quality of healthcare data over time. Second, the study focuses on the general socio-demographic characteristics of healthcare workers, giving a general overview of their characteristics related to healthcare data quality. Future studies could use mixed methods and more in-depth analysis to explore these factors further. For example, examining marital status could reveal how being single, cohabiting, separated, divorced, or widowed affects data quality. Similarly, comparing the impact of different education levels, like certificates versus bachelor’s degrees, on data quality could provide valuable insights. This complex relationship might not be fully captured by multiple linear regression, even though it effectively identifies associations. This study did not include measures of occupational stress, excitement, or job satisfaction, focusing solely on socio-demographic predictors. Future research should incorporate these psycho-social factors to more comprehensively understand their influence on healthcare data quality.

Even with these limitations, the study methodology is strong, using a solid sampling strategy and trustworthy data collection tools, which backs up the findings. The sample accurately reflects the healthcare workers, making the results more applicable. In general, the findings offer important information that can guide practice and policy, adding to the existing research and pointing out areas for future study.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that among healthcare workers in eight Tanzanian regional referral hospitals, age and sex significantly predict data accuracy and completeness, while education, marital status, and experience showed mixed or non-significant effects. All conclusions are drawn strictly from those socio-demographic variables; we did not measure potential confounders such as occupational stress, job satisfaction, or ingrained technology-use habits, so broader inferences should be made with caution. Future research incorporating these psycho-social and behavioral factors will help validate and extend our findings. Ultimately, recognizing and addressing socio-demographic influences on data quality is essential for strengthening Tanzania’s health information system and improving patient care.
