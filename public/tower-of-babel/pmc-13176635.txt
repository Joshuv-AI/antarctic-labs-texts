Mass Spectrometry‐Driven Proteomic Biomarkers for Serum‐Based Detection of Pancreatic Ductal Adenocarcinoma and Intraductal Papillary Mucinous Neoplasm‐Associated Invasive Carcinoma
Authors: HyunGyo Jung, Namyoung Park, Jaihwan Kim, Min‐Jung Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPancreatic cancer remains a highly lethal malignancy due to late diagnosis and the lack of effective non‐invasive biomarkers for detection, further complicated by biological heterogeneity, including intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm (IPMN) and IPMN‐associated invasive carcinoma (IPMC).MethodsSerum samples from a discovery cohort (n = 60), including patients with IPMN, IPMC, and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), were analyzed using LC–MS/MS‐based serum proteomic profiling to identify candidate biomarkers. Feature selection was performed using ANOVA, receiver operating characteristic analysis, and permutation feature importance. The selected markers were further evaluated in an independent validation cohort (n = 40).ResultsIn the discovery cohort, the model demonstrated robust performance, with leave‐one‐out cross‐validation AUROC values ranging from 0.814 to 1.000 across classifications of healthy controls and disease groups (IPMN, IPMC, and PDAC). In the independent validation cohort, the six‐marker panel (CALR, FCN1, MBL2, SPP1, TAGLN2, and VWF) achieved an AUROC of 0.962 with a specificity of 95.0% for distinguishing healthy controls from IPMC, and an AUROC of 0.856 with a specificity of 90.0% for healthy controls vs. PDAC.ConclusionsThese findings demonstrate the robustness and clinical potential of the six‐protein panel as a non‐invasive diagnostic tool for pancreatic cancer.Keywords: IPMN‐associated invasive carcinoma, non‐invasive biomarker, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, proteomics

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer (PC) remains one of the most lethal malignancies, with a 5‐year survival rate of approximately 13%. Its global burden is projected to increase substantially, and PC‐related mortality is expected to become one of the leading causes of cancer‐related death by 2050. Although outcomes are significantly improved when the disease is detected at an early stage, most patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage because early disease is typically asymptomatic and progresses insidiously [1, 2, 3].

Currently, the diagnosis of PC relies primarily on imaging modalities. However, imaging‐based approaches have limited sensitivity for detecting malignant transformation and do not fully reflect tumor biology. Tissue biopsy, while informative, is invasive and unsuitable for repeated assessment due to procedure‐related risks such as bleeding and pancreatitis [3, 4, 5]. Carbohydrate antigen 19–9 (CA 19–9) remains the only U.S. Food and Drug Administration‐approved serum biomarker for PC, with a reported pooled sensitivity of 72%, specificity of 86%, and an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.85 [5, 6]. However, CA 19–9 has well‐recognized limitations, including a lack of diagnostic utility in Lewis antigen‐negative individuals and reduced sensitivity in early‐stage disease. Other circulating biomarkers, such as carcinoembryonic antigen and carbohydrate antigen 125, show even lower diagnostic performance [6]. To overcome these limitations, extensive efforts have been made to develop non‐invasive approaches. A plasma cfDNA‐based machine learning (ML) model using end‐motif patterns distinguished pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) with an AUROC of 0.954 [7]. A three‐miRNA signature (let‐7i‐5p, miR‐130a‐3p, and miR‐221‐3p) achieved an AUROC of 0.974 [8], while hsa‐let‐7f‐5p was significantly overexpressed in the metastatic stage (p = 0.007) [9]. A seven‐marker exosomal protein panel (ERBB3, FGF2, FTL, LEP, MUC‐1, PRL, and ST6GALNAC6) showed an AUROC of 0.971 [10]. In addition, serum THBS2 combined with CA 19–9 yielded an AUROC of 0.92 [11], and a panel including PIGR and VWF reached an AUROC of 0.893, which increased to 0.980 when combined with CA 19–9 [12]. Nevertheless, translation into clinically applicable assays remains limited, largely due to issues related to reproducibility, validation, and disease heterogeneity.

Pancreatic tumorigenesis is biologically heterogeneous. While the majority of pancreatic cancers are PDAC, these tumors can arise from pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia (PanIN) [13]. In addition, intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasms (IPMNs) are also a well‐known precursor lesion that can progress to invasive carcinoma, referred to as IPMN‐associated invasive carcinoma (IPMC) [14, 15, 16]. Emerging evidence suggests that IPMN‐derived carcinomas may exhibit molecular and biological features distinct from those of conventional PanIN‐derived PDAC, reflecting differences in tumor evolution and microenvironmental interactions [16, 17]. Despite this heterogeneity, most biomarker studies have focused exclusively on PDAC without incorporating IPMC, thereby potentially limiting their clinical applicability across the spectrum of pancreatic neoplasia.

In this study, we aimed to develop a non‐invasive, serum‐based diagnostic model for PC using univariate and ML‐based multivariate approaches. Importantly, both PDAC and IPMC were included to better capture the biological diversity of pancreatic tumorigenesis and to enhance the clinical relevance of the identified biomarker panel.

Materials and Methods

Sample Cohort

Participants were prospectively enrolled into a discovery cohort and an independent validation cohort between January 1, 2018, and August 31, 2025. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The experimental plan was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (SNUBH; B‐2412‐940‐302). The study population included patients with PDAC, IPMC, IPMNs with varying malignant risk, and healthy controls (HC). Participants with PC (PDAC and IPMC) were primarily included based on pathologically confirmed diagnoses. In cases where histologic confirmation was not feasible, enrollment was based on a clinicoradiologic diagnosis supported by characteristic imaging findings. Notably, in the discovery cohort, only a single IPMC case lacked histologic confirmation due to advanced age and poor general condition; however, the diagnosis was considered highly reliable based on rapidly progressive imaging features.

Patients with IPMN were stratified according to the 2017 International Consensus Guidelines (Fukuoka guidelines) [17]. Specifically, IPMNs were categorized into a high‐risk group (HRG), defined as IPMN with main pancreatic duct involvement or branch duct IPMN with high‐risk stigmata, and a low‐risk group (LRG), defined as branch duct IPMN without high‐risk stigmata.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) diagnosis of PDAC or IPMN based on clinical evaluation, imaging findings, and/or histopathologic assessment at Seoul National University Bundang Hospital; and (2) availability of prospectively collected serum samples from the Human Bioresource Center. Patients with other pancreatic cystic lesions, including inflammatory fluid collections, non‐neoplastic pancreatic cysts, serous cystic neoplasms, and indeterminate cysts, were excluded.

The discovery cohort included 60 participants (6 PDAC, 6 IPMC, 11 HRG, 25 LRG, 12 HC). The independent validation cohort comprised 40 participants (16 PDAC, 4 IPMC, 20 HC), and all PDAC and IPMC participants in the validation cohort were pathologically confirmed. The validation cohort was not used for feature selection or model training. Clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

PRM Target Proteins

Parallel reaction monitoring (PRM) target proteins were selected from 107 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) identified across all comparisons, according to the criteria described in the Statistical analysis section. Among DEPs, the top 14 abundant serum proteins were excluded. In addition, one of the 14–3–3 proteins and periostin isoform were excluded due to low label‐free quantification (LFQ) quality. As additional candidates, ST6GALNAC6, CEACAM5, and CEACAM6 were added from the literature [18, 19, 20]. TNC was also included as a PRM target because it was uniquely identified in a single group with high‐quality spectra. For PRM method development, one precursor m/z value per protein was selected based on the following criteria: (1) a clear fragment ion spectrum with minimal co‐elution interference, (2) high fragment ion intensity, and (3) high precursor intensity.

Statistical Analysis

DEPs were defined as proteins with an adjusted p‐value < 0.05 (one‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey–Kramer post hoc test) and an absolute fold change ≥ 2.5. Fold changes for DDA data were calculated as ratios of grouped mean LFQ abundances obtained from Proteome Discoverer‐normalized values (group maximum normalization followed by averaging across replicates). LFQ abundance values were used for generating Z‐score‐scaled heatmaps. For PRM data, fold changes were calculated as ratios of grouped mean peptide peak areas.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of Gene Ontology (GO) molecular function (MF) terms was performed using the DDA dataset (proteins with ≥ 2 peptides and FDR ≤ 1%, excluding keratins and the 14 most abundant proteins). A ranked gene list based on fold change values was used as input. Reactome pathway over‐representation analysis was conducted using the 22 candidate proteins selected by at least two of the three feature selection methods (ANOVA, ROC analysis, and permutation feature importance). The Reactome network plot was made for proteins associated with the top 15 significantly enriched (p < 0.05) pathways. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 for both analyses.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated, AUROC was calculated. The 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for AUROC were estimated by bootstrap resampling with 2000 iterations.

Results

Identification of Differentially Expressed Proteins in the Discovery Cohort

The overall study workflow is illustrated in Figure 1A. Sixty serum samples in the discovery cohort were analyzed using DDA LC–MS/MS. A total of 884 proteins were identified (Table S1), of which 640 met the filtering criteria (FDR ≤ 1% and ≥ 2 unique peptides). Among these, 593 proteins were detected across all groups (Figure 1B). A total of 107 DEPs, defined as absolute fold change ≥ 2.5 and ANOVA p < 0.05, were identified across all comparisons (Table S2). Volcano plots and hierarchical clustering heatmap revealed distinct and progressive proteomic changes across groups (Figure 1C,D). VWF was distinctly upregulated in both IPMC and PDAC, while GRN, GGT1, CDHR2, CRP, SAA1, and SAA2 showed marked upregulation in PDAC. In contrast, pancreatic enzymes such as CPA1 and CPB1 were enriched in IPMC rather than PDAC. In addition, DAG1 and TREML1 were observed in a stage‐specific manner for LRG and HRG, respectively.

Study workflow and serum proteome profiling. (A) Schematic overview of the overall workflow. (B) Upset plot of 640 identified proteins with peptide count ≥ 2. (C) Volcano plots showing the number of differentially expressed proteins (DEPs; absolute fold change ≥ 2.5 and ANOVA p < 0.05) across comparisons (gray: Non‐significant). (D) Hierarchical clustering heatmap of DEPs based on Z‐score–transformed Label‐free quantification (LFQ) intensities (n = 90). LFQ values were averaged for proteins mapped to the same gene.

GSEA showed that the top enriched terms were shared between HC vs. IPMC and HC vs. PDAC, with the top five terms being consistent across both comparisons (Figure S1A,C). Cytoskeletal protein binding was the most significant term, while cell adhesion molecule binding involved the largest number of genes, indicating shared proteomic alterations. Network plots further illustrated the interconnected relationships among proteins within these enriched terms (Figure S1B,D).

Univariate ANOVA and ROC Analyses of PRM Data

Information on PRM target proteins and corresponding peptides is provided in Table S3. Boxplots of PRM peak areas for all 94 proteins are shown in Figure S2, with results summarized in Table S4. ANOVA‐based univariate analysis identified 32 proteins with significant differences across comparisons (Figure 2A), with a greater number of significant proteins observed in PDAC‐related comparisons than in IPMC‐ or HRG‐related comparisons. ROC‐based univariate analysis, using AUROC criteria (≥ 0.9 for HC vs. HRG, HC vs. IPMC, and HC vs. PDAC; ≥ 0.75 for HC vs. LRG), identified 23 proteins (Figure 2B), with results summarized in Table S5. Among these proteins, CALR, IGFBP2, LBP, and VWF met the selection criteria in both univariate approaches (ANOVA and ROC) in either the HC vs. IPMC or HC vs. PDAC comparisons and also showed relatively high absolute fold‐change values. In contrast, CRP exhibited the highest fold‐change but did not meet the ROC criteria because only a subset of individuals showed extremely elevated CRP levels in PDAC (Figure S2). This suggests that high fold‐change values do not always correlate with diagnostic utility.

PFI‐Based Multivariate Feature Selection

Detailed methods for permutation feature importance (PFI) are provided in the Supplementary Methods. To identify proteins critical for class differentiation, PFI was applied in a multiclass classification framework (Figure 3A). Top‐ranked features were selected for each class and classifier, resulting in a total of 13 candidate biomarkers (Figure 3B). Notably, FCN1 was consistently identified as the most important feature for distinguishing LRG across all classifiers, while TAGLN2 was identified as the top feature for PDAC in two classifiers.

Bars indicate median permutation importance, and error bars represent the interquartile range. PFI, permutation feature importance.

Integrated Feature Selection and Pathway Enrichment Analysis

Integration of ANOVA‐, ROC‐, and PFI‐based feature selection identified six proteins (CALR, SPP1, FCN1, TAGLN2, MBL2, and VWF) that were consistently selected by all three approaches and were therefore defined as the final candidate panel for subsequent validation (Figure 4A). A total of 22 proteins were supported by at least two methods and were used for Reactome pathway enrichment analysis. Figure 4B showed proteins (B4GALT1, CEACAM6, CTSD, GRN, ICAM1, IGFBP2, LAMC1, LBP, MRC1, PGLYRP1, SAA1, ST6GALNAC6, YWHAZ) associated with the top 15 significantly enriched (p < 0.05) pathways, including six selected proteins. The identified protein network was primarily enriched in pathways related to extracellular matrix organization, modification of glycosylation, and neutrophil degranulation, indicating involvement in tissue remodeling, recognition of glycan on the cell surface, and regulation of immune responses. Additional enrichment in pathways associated with platelet activation, complement cascade, and insulin transport pathways further suggests roles in hemostasis, triggering of immune responses, and insulin resistance processes. Notably, key proteins such as FCN1, VWF, SPP1, and ICAM1 act as central hubs connecting these diverse biological processes.

Model Comparison for Binary Classification Using the Final Candidate Panel

The workflow for model development and evaluation is summarized in Figure S3A and described in the Supplementary Methods. Model performance was evaluated using leave‐one‐out cross‐validation (LOOCV), with hyperparameter optimization performed through inner cross‐validation. Four classifiers—logistic regression (LR), support vector classifier (SVC), random forest (RF), and linear discriminant analysis (LDA)—were evaluated. Although no single model consistently outperformed the others across all comparisons, SVC demonstrated consistently high and well‐balanced performance across sensitivity, specificity, and AUROC. In terms of AUROC, SVC showed relatively narrow 95% confidence intervals across different classification tasks, whereas other models exhibited wider confidence intervals depending on the comparison (Figure S3B). The LOOCV results confirmed the robust performance of SVC, with high sensitivity, specificity, and AUROC values ranging from 0.814 to 1.000 (Figure 5A–D). Based on these results, SVC was selected as the final classifier for subsequent model construction and validation.

Boxplots of predicted probabilities from leave‐one‐out cross‐validation and ROC curves for binary classification of HC vs. LRG (A), HRG (B), IPMC (C), and PDAC (D). The blue zone represents the 95% confidence interval of the AUROC.

Validation of PRM Assay and Analytical Reproducibility

Prior to biomarker validation, the analytical performance of the PRM assay was validated. In addition to the six selected markers, a total of 19 candidate proteins that met the ANOVA and ROC criteria were measured. Representative extracted ion chromatograms demonstrating consistent peptide detection are shown in Figure S4A. To evaluate intra‐ and inter‐batch reproducibility, pooled serum samples were independently processed and analyzed. The PRM assay demonstrated good reproducibility, with coefficients of variation (CVs) below 30% for both intra‐ and inter‐batch measurements across all targets (Figure S4B,C).

Validation of the Multi‐Marker Panel in an Independent Cohort

The performance of the multi‐marker panel was further evaluated in an independent validation cohort (n = 40). Binary classification models were applied to distinguish IPMC and PDAC from HC. In the validation cohort, the SVC model showed an AUROC of 0.962 with a specificity of 95.0% for HC vs. IPMC, and an AUROC of 0.865 with a specificity of 90.0% for HC vs. PDAC (Figure 6A,B). The sample size of IPMC and PDAC was small in both the training and validation cohorts, particularly for IPMC in the validation set (n = 4). Therefore, these results should be interpreted with caution, as the limited sample size may increase the risk of overfitting and potential overestimation of model performance. Therefore, further large‐scale validation studies are required to confirm the robustness and generalizability.

Classifier performance in an independent validation cohort. (A) Box plots of predicted probabilities and ROC curves for HC vs. PDAC (HC, n = 20; PDAC, n = 16), and for HC vs. IPMC (B; HC, n = 20; IPMC, n = 4). The blue zone represents the 95% confidence interval of the AUROC.

Discussion

In this study, using ANOVA, Tukey–Kramer post hoc test, univariate ROC, and multiclass classification, we identified six serum proteins with diagnostic potential for PC. Among these, VWF, SPP1, and MBL2 have been previously reported as PDAC‐associated serum biomarkers [12, 21, 22], whereas CALR, FCN1, and TAGLN2 have not yet been reported as serum biomarkers. We developed an ML‐based multi‐marker panel, which showed enhanced diagnostic accuracy, and its robustness was confirmed in the validation cohort. These findings highlight the promise of combining complementary biomarkers reflecting diverse biological mechanisms for more accurate detection of PC.

The six proteins in our multi‐marker panel are involved in diverse processes in PC pathogenesis, including inflammation, glycosylation, extracellular matrix remodeling, and immune surveillance. CALR is an endoplasmic reticulum‐resident chaperone protein that has been reported to be upregulated in pancreatic cancer tissue and cell lines and to play a role in tumor progression and immune modulation [23, 24, 25]. In the present study, serum CALR levels progressively decreased in IPMC and PDAC patients, suggesting a stage‐dependent alteration during disease progression.

Both FCN1 and MBL2 are key initiators of the lectin complement pathway [26]. In the Reactome network plot (Figure 4B), three pathways (initial triggering of complement, complement cascade, creation of C4 and C2 activators) were enriched. FCN1 was elevated only in the LRG group in the screening cohort, whereas MBL2 was significantly increased only in PDAC. This discrepancy may reflect several factors: Sequestration or consumption of FCN1 at the tumor site [27], confounding by FCN1 genetic variants affecting expression [28], or distinct temporal roles in established cancer.

SPP1 is linked to oncogenic processes, including proliferation, invasion, and epithelial–mesenchymal transition, in PDAC [29]. In this study, SPP1 was elevated in PDAC, and Reactome analysis supported its role in extracellular matrix organization and degradation, highlighting it as a mechanistic biomarker and potential therapeutic target.

TAGLN2 is an actin‐binding protein that has been reported to be upregulated in PDAC tissues and to function as a downstream effector of KRAS–ERK signaling, promoting tumor cell proliferation and tumorigenesis [30]. In addition, experimental studies in pancreatic cancer cell lines have shown that TAGLN2 enhances cancer progression via activation of the MEK/ERK pathway [31]. In the present study, serum TAGLN2 levels were increased in PDAC patients significantly. But the secreting mechanism of TAGLN2 has not been studied, and further investigation is necessary for clinical utility.

VWF, a hemostatic factor, is implicated in cancer inflammation and vascular dynamics, promoting angiogenesis, permeability, and tumor cell adhesion [32]. Elevated serum VWF and integrin cell surface interactions have been reported in PDAC [12, 33, 34]. We have observed that VWF was significantly higher in both IPMC‐ and PDAC‐related comparisons, suggesting it as a valuable biomarker.

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single institution with a relatively small sample size, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. In particular, the number of PDAC and IPMC cases was limited, which may increase the risk of model overfitting and affect the stability of the results. To mitigate this, we employed relatively simple and interpretable machine learning models and limited the extent of hyperparameter optimization. Second, potential confounding factors may have influenced the serum proteomic profiles. Specifically, three patients had concomitant malignancies, and one patient underwent blood sampling on the day after surgery, which may have affected protein expression patterns. The sensitivity analyses were performed by repeating the entire analysis pipeline after excluding three IPMC samples and one LRG sample. Despite this exclusion, for the HC vs. IPMC comparison, the AUROC showed only a minimal decrease from 0.962 to 0.950, while all other performance metrics remained unchanged. These findings suggest that the inclusion of samples with concomitant cancers or perioperative blood collection had a negligible impact on the overall results. Third, stratification into HRG and LRG was based on clinical and imaging findings rather than histopathologic confirmation, and therefore may not fully reflect the true degree of dysplasia. However, most cases of pancreatic malignancy (PDAC and IPMC) were pathologically confirmed. In the single case without histologic confirmation, the diagnosis of IPMC was considered highly reliable based on characteristic clinical and imaging features, suggesting that any potential misclassification is unlikely to have materially influenced the overall findings. Despite these limitations, the proposed machine learning‐based multi‐marker panel was evaluated in an independent validation cohort, supporting the robustness of the findings. Further validation in larger, multi‐center populations, as well as studies investigating tissue‐level expression and longitudinal dynamics of circulating proteins, will be necessary to establish clinical applicability.

Conclusions

We developed a multi‐marker diagnostic panel comprising six proteins associated with PDAC and IPMC. A panel combining biomarkers that reflect diverse biological mechanisms may improve diagnostic accuracy. With further validation in larger cohorts, this panel may provide clinical utility for diagnosis.
