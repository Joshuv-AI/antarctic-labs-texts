A Note on Eigenvalues of Perturbed Hermitian Matrices This paper originally published in Linear Algebra and its Applications , 395 (2005), 183–190. The main results are well received since. Unfortunately, the published version contains quite a number of typos. This arXiv version serves two purposes: 1) fix typos, and 2) add an appendix on how to best use the main results for sharper error bounds than straightforward applications in certain situations, e.g., approximations from Krylov subspaces.
Chi-Kwong Li, Ren-Cang Li

Consider a partitioned Hermitian matrix

\widetilde{A}=\bordermatrix{&\scriptstyle m&\scriptstyle n\cr\scriptstyle m&H_{1}&O\cr\scriptstyle n&O&H_{2}\cr}.

\widetilde{A}

1.

(1.4)

Example 1

(1.5)

Main Result

Theorem 2

Let

A=\bordermatrix{&\scriptstyle m&\scriptstyle n\cr\scriptstyle m&H_{1}&E^{*}\cr\scriptstyle n&E&H_{2}\cr}\quad\hbox{ and }\quad\widetilde{A}=\bordermatrix{&\scriptstyle m&\scriptstyle n\cr\scriptstyle m&H_{1}&O\cr\scriptstyle n&O&H_{2}\cr}

be Hermitian matrices with eigenvalues

\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{m+n}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}\geq\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\widetilde{\lambda}_{m+n},

(2.1)

respectively.
Define

\displaystyle\eta_{i}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm{def}}}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\min\limits_{\mu_{2}\in\lambda(H_{2})}|\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}-\mu_{2}|,&\mbox{if $\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}\in\lambda(H_{1})$},\\
\min\limits_{\mu_{1}\in\lambda(H_{1})}|\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}-\mu_{1}|,&\mbox{if $\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}\in\lambda(H_{2})$},\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\eta

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm{def}}}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\min_{1\leq i\leq m+n}\eta_{i}=\min_{\mu_{1}\in\lambda(H_{1}),\,\mu_{2}\in\lambda(H_{2})}|\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}|.

(2.5)

Then for
i=1,2,\cdots,m+n
, we have

\displaystyle|\lambda_{i}-\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2\|E\|^{2}}{\eta_{i}+\sqrt{\eta_{i}^{2}+4\|E\|^{2}}}

(2.6)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2\|E\|^{2}}{\eta+\sqrt{\eta^{2}+4\|E\|^{2}}}.

(2.7)

Proof.
Suppose
U^{*}H_{1}U
and
V^{*}H_{2}V
are in the diagonal form with
their diagonal entries arranged in the descending order, respectively. We may assume
that
U=I_{m}
and
V=I_{n}
. Otherwise, replace

A
by

(U\oplus V)^{*}A(U\oplus V).

We may perturb the diagonal of
A
so that all entries are distinct,
and apply continuity argument for the general case.

We prove the result by induction on
m+n
. If
m+n=2
, the result
is clear (from our Example). Assume that
m+n>2
, and the result is
true for Hermitian matrices of size
m+n-1
.

First, refining an argument of Mathias
[
5
]
, we show that
(
2.6
) holds for
i=1
.
Assume that the
(1,1)
st entry of
H_{1}
equals
\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}
.
By the min-max principle
[
1
,
7
,
8
]
, we have

\lambda_{1}\geq e_{1}^{*}Ae_{1}=\widetilde{\lambda}_{1},

where
e_{1}
is the first column of the identity matrix.
No proof is necessary if
\lambda_{1}=\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}
. Assume

\lambda_{1}>\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}
and let

X=\pmatrix{I_{m}&0\cr-(H_{2}-\lambda_{1}I_{n})^{-1}E&I_{n}}.

Then

X^{*}(A-\lambda_{1}I)X=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}M(\lambda_{1})&0\\
0&H_{2}-\lambda_{1}I_{n}\end{array}\right),

where

M(\lambda)=H_{1}-\lambda I_{m}-E^{*}(H_{2}-\lambda I_{n})^{-1}E.

Since
A
and
X^{*}AX
have the same inertia,
we see that
M(\lambda_{1})

has zero as the largest eigenvalue.
Notice that the largest eigenvalue of
H_{1}-\lambda_{1}I
is

\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}-\lambda_{1}\leq 0
. Thus,
for
\delta_{1}=|\lambda_{1}-\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}|=\lambda_{1}-\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}
,
we have (see
[
7
, (10.9)]
)

\lambda_{1}\leq\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}+\|E\|^{2}_{2}/(\delta_{1}+\eta_{1}),

and hence

\delta_{1}\leq\|E\|^{2}/(\delta_{1}+\eta_{1}).

Consequently,

\delta_{1}\leq{2\|E\|^{2}\over\eta_{1}+\sqrt{\eta_{1}^{2}+4\|E\|^{2}}}

as asserted.
Similarly, we can prove the result if the
(1,1)
th entry of
H_{2}
equals

\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}
. In this case, we will apply the inertia arguments to

A
and
YAY^{*}
with

Y=\pmatrix{I_{m}&0\cr-E(H_{1}-\lambda_{1}I_{m})^{-1}&I_{n}}.

Applying the result of the last paragraph to
-A
, we see that
(
2.6
) holds for
i=m+n
.

Now, suppose
1<i<m+n
. The result
trivially holds if
\lambda_{i}=\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}
.
Suppose
\lambda_{i}\neq\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}
.
We may assume that
\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}>\lambda_{i}
. Otherwise,
replace
(A,\widetilde{A},i)
by
(-A,-\widetilde{A},m+n-i+1)
.
Delete the row and column of
A
that contain the diagonal entry

\widetilde{\lambda}_{m+n}
.
Suppose the resulted matrix
\widehat{A}
has
eigenvalues
\nu_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\nu_{m+n-1}
.
By the interlacing inequalities
[
7
, Section 10.1]
, we have

\lambda_{i}\geq\nu_{i}\qquad\hbox{ and hence }\qquad\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}-\lambda_{i}\leq\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}-\nu_{i}.

(2.8)

Note that
\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}
is the
i
th largest diagonal entries in

\widehat{A}
.
Let
\widehat{\eta}_{i}
be the minimum distance between
\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}

and the diagonal entries in the diagonal block
\widehat{H}_{j}

in
\widehat{A}
not containing
\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}
,
where
j\in\{1,2\}
. Then

{\widehat{\eta}}_{i}\geq\eta_{i}

because
\widehat{H}_{j}
may have one fewer diagonal entries than
H_{j}
.
Let
\widehat{E}
be the off-diagonal block of
\widehat{A}
. Then

\|\widehat{E}\|\leq\|E\|
.
Thus,

\displaystyle|\lambda_{i}-\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}-\lambda_{i}\hskip 72.1972pt\hbox{ because }\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}>\lambda_{i}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\widetilde{\lambda}_{i}-\nu_{i}\hskip 73.354pt\hbox{ by }(\ref{eq:2})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{2\|\widehat{E}\|^{2}\over\widehat{\eta}_{i}+\sqrt{\widehat{\eta}_{i}^{2}+4\|\widehat{E}\|^{2}}}\hskip 15.17719pt\hbox{ by induction assumption }

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{2\|\widehat{E}\|^{2}\over\eta_{i}+\sqrt{\eta_{i}^{2}+4\|\widehat{E}\|^{2}}}\hskip 15.17719pt\hbox{ because }\widehat{\eta}_{i}\geq\eta_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{1\over 2}\sqrt{\eta_{i}^{2}+4\|\widehat{E}\|^{2}}-\eta_{i}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{1\over 2}\sqrt{\eta_{i}^{2}+4\|E\|^{2}}-\eta_{i}\quad\hbox{ because }\|\widehat{E}\|\leq\|E\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{2\|E\|^{2}\over\eta_{i}+\sqrt{\eta_{i}^{2}+4\|E\|^{2}}}

as asserted.

3
Application to Singular Value Problem

In this section, we apply the result in Section 2 to study singular
values of matrices. For notational convenience in connection to our
discussion, we define the sequence of singular
values of a complex
p\times q
matrix
X
by

\sigma(X)=(\sigma_{1}(X),\dots,\sigma_{k}(X)),

where
k=\max\{p,q\}
and

\sigma_{1}(X)\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{k}(X)
are the
nonnegative square roots of the eigenvalues of
the matrix
XX^{*}
or
X^{*}X
depending on which
one has a larger size. Note that the nonzero eigenvalues of

XX^{*}
and
X^{*}X
are the same, and they give rise to the
nonzero singular values of
X
which are of importance.
We have the following result concerning the nonzero
singular values of perturbed matrices.

Theorem 3

Let

B=\bordermatrix{&\scriptstyle k&\scriptstyle\ell\cr\scriptstyle m&G_{1}&E_{1}\cr\scriptstyle n&E_{2}&G_{2}\cr}\quad\hbox{ and }\quad\widetilde{B}=\bordermatrix{&\scriptstyle k&\scriptstyle\ell\cr\scriptstyle m&G_{1}&O\cr\scriptstyle n&O&G_{2}\cr}

be complex matrices with singular values

\sigma_{1}\geq\sigma_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{\max\{m+n,k+\ell\}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\widetilde{\sigma}_{1}\geq\widetilde{\sigma}_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\widetilde{\sigma}_{\max\{m+n,k+\ell\}},

(3.1)

respectively, so that
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are non-trivial.
Define
\epsilon=\max\{\|E_{1}\|,\|E_{2}\|\}
, and

\displaystyle\eta_{i}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm{def}}}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\min\limits_{\mu_{2}\in\sigma(G_{2})}|\widetilde{\sigma}_{i}-\mu_{2}|,&\mbox{if $\widetilde{\sigma}_{i}\in\sigma(G_{1})$},\\
\min\limits_{\mu_{1}\in\sigma(G_{1})}|\widetilde{\sigma}_{i}-\mu_{1}|,&\mbox{if $\widetilde{\sigma}_{i}\in\sigma(G_{2})$},\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\eta

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm{def}}}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\min_{1\leq i\leq\min\{m+n,k+\ell\}}\eta_{i}=\min_{\mu_{1}\in\sigma(G_{1}),\,\mu_{2}\in\sigma(G_{2})}|\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}|.

(3.5)

Then
for
i=1,2,\cdots,\min\{m+n,k+\ell\}
, we have

\displaystyle|\sigma_{i}-\widetilde{\sigma}_{i}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2\epsilon^{2}}{\eta_{i}+\sqrt{\eta_{i}^{2}+4\epsilon^{2}}}

(3.6)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2\epsilon^{2}}{\eta+\sqrt{\eta^{2}+4\epsilon^{2}}},

(3.7)

and
\sigma_{i}=\widetilde{\sigma}_{i}=0
for
i>\min\{m+n,k+\ell\}
.

Proof:

By Jordan-Wielandt Theorem
[
8
, Theorem I.4.2]
, the eigenvalues
of

\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}cc@{}}O&B\\
B^{*}&O\end{array}\right)

are
\pm\sigma_{i}
and possibly some zeros adding up to

m+n+k+\ell
eigenvalues. A similar statement holds for
\widetilde{B}
.
Permuting the rows and columns appropriately, we see that

\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}cc@{}}O&B\\
B^{*}&O\end{array}\right)\quad\mbox{is similar to}\quad\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}cc|cc@{}}O&G_{1}&O&E_{1}\\
G_{1}^{*}&O&E_{2}^{*}&O\\
\hline\cr O&E_{2}&O&G_{2}\\
E_{1}^{*}&O&G_{2}^{*}&O\end{array}\right),

and

\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}cc@{}}O&\widetilde{B}\\
\widetilde{B}^{*}&O\end{array}\right)\quad\mbox{is similar to}\quad\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}cc|cc@{}}O&G_{1}&&\\
G_{1}^{*}&O&&\\
\hline\cr&&O&G_{2}\\
&&G_{2}^{*}&O\end{array}\right).

Applying Theorem
2
with

H_{i}=\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}cc@{}}O&G_{i}\\
G_{i}^{*}&O\end{array}\right)\quad\hbox{ and }\quad E=\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}cc@{}}O&E_{2}\\
E_{1}^{*}&O\end{array}\right),

we get the result.

One can also apply the above proof to the degenerate cases when
G_{1}

or
G_{2}
in the matrix
B
is trivial, i.e., one of the parameters

m,n,k,\ell
is zero. These cases are useful in applications.
We state one of them, and one can easily extend it to other
cases.

Theorem 4

Suppose
B=\pmatrix{G\ E\cr}
and

\tilde{B}=\pmatrix{G\ O\cr}
are

p\times q
matrices with
singular values

\sigma_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{\max\{p,q\}}\quad\hbox{ and }\quad\widetilde{\sigma}_{1}\geq\dots\geq\widetilde{\sigma}_{\max\{p,q\}},

respectively.
Then for
i=1,\dots,\min\{p,q\}
,

|\sigma_{i}-\widetilde{\sigma}_{i}|\leq{2\|E\|^{2}\over 2\widetilde{\sigma}_{i}+\sqrt{\widetilde{\sigma}_{i}^{2}+4\|E\|^{2}}}.

Appendix A
Discussions
(August 10, 2025)

We have noticed that some recent articles on the similar topic use the main results of this paper in such a way that
the induced bounds are magnitude worse than the “new” bounds in the articles. It turns out that those discoveries
are misleading at best, if not anything else. In this appendix, we discuss how the main result in section 2 can
and should be used in those articles for sharper error bounds than straightforward applications.
The same can be said about the result in section 3, but we omit the detail.

An equivalent form of Theorem 2 in terms of the eigen-residuals can be gotten as follows. Suppose that we have computed an
m
-dimensional approximate invariant subspace
{\cal X}_{1}
of Hermitian matrix
A\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
and let
X_{1}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times m}
be an orthonormal basis matrix of
{\cal X}_{1}
, i.e.,
{\rm span}(X_{1})={\cal X}_{1}
, and
X_{1}^{*}X_{1}=I_{m}
. Let
H_{1}=X_{1}^{*}AX_{1}
, the Rayleigh quotient matrix
of
A
with respect to subspace
{\cal X}_{1}
. As usual, we define the associated residual
as

R=AX_{1}-X_{1}H_{1}.

(A.8)

Now expand
X_{1}
to a unitary matrix
X:=[X_{1},X_{2}]\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
. We have

X^{*}AX=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}X_{1}^{*}AX_{1}&X_{1}^{*}AX_{2}\\
X_{2}^{*}AX_{1}&X_{2}^{*}AX_{2}\end{array}\right)=:\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}H_{1}&E^{*}\\
E&H_{2}\end{array}\right)

(A.9)

which is in the form of (
1.1
). Theorem 2 now immediately applies to yield error bounds in terms of
\|E\|
.
We claim that

\|E\|=\|R\|

(A.10)

and hence Theorem 2 also yields error bounds in terms of
\|R\|
.
To see (
A.10
), we notice that
\|R\|=\|X^{*}R\|=\|E\|
because

X^{*}R=X^{*}AX_{1}-X^{*}X_{1}H_{1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}X_{1}^{*}AX_{1}\\
X_{2}^{*}AX_{1}\end{array}\right)-\left(\begin{array}[]{c}H_{1}\\
0\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
E\end{array}\right).

We observe that error bounds by Theorem 2 use the whole
E
(or the whole
R
for that matter) for all eigenvalues although in (
2.6
) they do use individual
gaps
\eta_{i}
.
It raises a question if somehow
E
could be broken apart so that different pieces go with
different eigenvalues for sharper bounds. Indeed, that is possible and advantageous to do sometimes.
Without loss of generality, the basis matrix
X_{1}
can be chosen so that

H_{1}={\rm diag}(\widetilde{\lambda}_{j_{1}},\widetilde{\lambda}_{j_{2}},\ldots,\widetilde{\lambda}_{j_{m}}),

in which case each column of

X_{1}\equiv[\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{m}]

can be regarded as an approximate eigenvectors of
A
, and

j_{1}<j_{2}<\cdots<j_{m}
in terms of earlier notation.
We still have (
A.9
). Define

\boldsymbol{r}_{i}=A\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\,\widetilde{\lambda}_{j_{i}}\quad\mbox{for $1\leq i\leq m$}.

(A.11)

Introduce
\widehat{H}_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{(N-1)\times(N-1)}
standing for the matrix from striking out the
i
th row and column of

X^{*}AX
in (
A.9
) and write

E=[\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{1},\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{2},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{m}].

Theorem 2 says, for
1\leq i\leq m
,

|\lambda_{j_{i}}-\widetilde{\lambda}_{j_{i}}|\leq\frac{2\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}\|^{2}}{\widehat{\eta}_{i}+\sqrt{\widehat{\eta}_{i}^{2}+4\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}\|^{2}}}=\frac{2\|\boldsymbol{r}_{i}\|^{2}}{\widehat{\eta}_{i}+\sqrt{\widehat{\eta}_{i}^{2}+4\|\boldsymbol{r}_{i}\|^{2}}},

(A.12)

where

\widehat{\eta}_{i}=\min_{\mu\in\lambda(\widehat{H}_{i})}|\widetilde{\lambda}_{j_{i}}-\mu|\quad\mbox{for $1\leq i\leq m$}.

Essentially, those individual error bounds are simply gotten by letting
m=1
to begin with, for each eigenvalue of
H_{1}
.

The errors bounds by (
A.12
) use both individual gaps and different portions of
E
(or different portions of
R
for that matter). They
are particularly advantageous over the ones by Theorem 2 when
{\cal X}_{1}
is computed by
certain subspace methods for eigenvalue computations such as the Lanczos method, where
often those
\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}\|=\|\boldsymbol{r}_{i}\|
corresponding to a few largest and smallest eigenvalues of
H_{1}
are
magnitude
smaller than the ones corresponding to other eigenvalues of
H_{1}
, yielding magnitude smaller error bounds
for the few largest and smallest eigenvalues, whereas error bounds straightforwardly from Theorem 2 may have similar orders
of magnitude
because of the use of the whole
E
(or the whole
R
for that matter).

How do the error bounds by Theorem 2 compare against those in (
A.12
)? It is a tradeoff between mathematical elegance and practical sharpness, to put it simply. Yet, mathematically, (
A.12
) is no more general than
(
2.6
). In fact, (
2.6
) is the stronger one in mathematics because it implies
(
A.12
), even though the latter delivers much smaller error bounds in those articles referred to at the beginning of this appendix.
