Digital twin for the analog scoliometer: advancements in telehealth for paediatric spine deformity care
Authors: Sinduja Suresh, Annabelle Stubbs, Grace Pulling, Amir Amiri, Maree Therese Izatt, Robert David Labrom, Geoffrey Noel Askin, Judith Paige Little
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn scoliosis care, a spine specialist will measure the lateral curvature of the spine from a radiograph and measure the torso rotation non-radiographically with an analog scoliometer directly on the patient at every surveillance appointment. While radiographs can be taken at any imaging centre and transferred to the treating spine clinic, the patient must visit in-person for monitoring the progression of structural rotation. Until now, distance measurement of the Angle of Trunk Rotation (ATR) has not been possible as no “virtual” scoliometer exists. This study describes the development of the first digital twin for the analog scoliometer to enable fast, gravity-independent, reliable and accurate digital ATR measurements from patient-specific 3D virtual models. It mimics the physical measurement process of the ATR using an analog scoliometer in a virtual environment. Validation of the tool showed excellent intra-user (< 0.95) and inter-user (< 0.95) reliability. The digital measurements had a high positive correlation (0.897) and agreement (92.7%) with the analog measurements made clinically. At 6° cut-off, the tool showed a high sensitivity of 90.24% and specificity of 92.31%. Larger device width was not shown to be an advantage. Introduction

Scoliosis is a complex 3D deformity of the spine characterised by an abnormal lateral curvature of the spine combined with the loss of normal sagittal curves1. It is also accompanied by a fixed vertebral and ribcage rotation component2–4. This results in an externally observable torso distortion which affects normal posture5,6. Scoliosis is the most common spine deformity7,8, often presenting in childhood, and its prevalence varies depending on the aetiology, gender, age of onset, and geographical region8,9. It drastically diminishes quality of life in adulthood if left untreated10, and in severe cases can cause back pain and negatively impact pulmonary and cardiac function2,11.

Scoliosis is clinically diagnosed and monitored using two key metrics - the Cobb angle and the Angle of Trunk Rotation (ATR). The Cobb angle is a measure of the lateral curvature, calculated from radiographic imaging in standing posture, and is the primary metric that is used for diagnosis, monitoring of scoliosis progression, conservative or surgical treatment choices and finally to measure the effect (if any) of any treatment that was prescribed12,13. The ATR is a measure of the rotational component of scoliosis and is non-radiographically inferred by quantifying the maximum rib/lumbar musculature prominence in the posterior torso, typically measured in the Adam’s Forward Bend Test (AFBT)14 using an analog scoliometer. Its use is depicted in Fig. 1. It is the primary method of identifying possible scoliosis in children before confirmation with radiography and is used throughout the patient’s care trajectory to track deformity progression, plan treatment, and determine the effect of various interventions and surgical success alongside the Cobb angle15. While the two metrics are related and rotation is often present along with lateral curvature, the direct correlation between the two are weak and they must still be analysed separately16. It is important to note that the two metrics are always assessed in conjunction with other factors such as age, menarche, gender, family history and skeletal maturity to determine the risk of scoliosis progression in a patient.

The progression of scoliosis can be effectively controlled when diagnosed early, consistently monitored by a spine specialist and treated in a timely manner8,17. While radiographs can be taken at any imaging centre and transferred to the treating spine clinic, the patient must visit a tertiary spine clinic in person for assessing the progression of structural rotation. In Australia, for children throughout Queensland, northern New South Wales and Northern Territory, spinal care is provided by the Queensland Children’s Hospital Spine Outpatient Clinics (QCHSOC). The current status quo of telehealth appointments at QCHSC involves phone consultations in combination with radiography (Cobb angle), providing the specialist with no information on the outward physical deformity of the patient. Other clinics across the world have attempted to extablish video conferencing, especially to assess gait18. However, neither of these strategies allow for numerical measurements of the ATR and in-person visits are still required for this.

Easy access to a centrally located tertiary spine clinic is a convenience not easily afforded to those living in remote or rural areas19–22. Lengthy travel to these clinics places significant temporal, financial and emotional stress on patients and their families in addition to the costs incurred by the health service. These challenges lead to delayed diagnoses and limit conservative ‘early intervention’ treatment options, resulting in increased rates of avoidable surgical correction23,24.

The analog scoliometer is a simple inclinometer device developed by Bunnell for clinical use in 1948 25 (current cost AUD 345). While the scoliometer was first adopted to reduce radiation exposure to young AIS patients, it is now used ubiquitously in clinics as a simple and inexpensive method of diagnosing and monitoring scoliotic rotational deformity progression alongside traditional radiographic imaging25. A measure of 7° or more on the scoliometer is generally indicative of a scoliotic curve and a change of more than 5° is considered significant for a scoliosis patient25.

The assessment of structural rotation in a patient’s spine requires a specialist understanding of spinal anatomy. The scoliometer must be run down a patient’s torso when in AFBT following the trajectory of the spinous processes with a slow and steady hand. The specialist must visually determine the maximum ATR value on the scoliometer. While making a scoliometer measurement, a spine specialist will also note the approximate vertebral level at which the maximum ATR is recorded, the length of the spine that appears rotated and the side of torso prominence (left or right). A thoracic and a lumbar ATR recording is a clinical standard, with notes on whether it is left or right prominent. This detailed information is integral in clinical decision making. For example, the magnitude of the lumbar ATR and its location may assist in deciding the vertebral level to which the spine is fused in surgery. The measurement of the ATR is therefore not solely a number, but a process through which the spine specialist understands the rotational deformity as a whole in the patient’s body, in conjunction with their lateral deformity. While the ATR value is simply recorded during screening, this level of detailed assessment is only performed post scoliosis diagnosis throughout the duration of care at a tertiary specialist spine clinic.

The reliability and accuracy of scoliometer measurements have been described controversially in the literature with the average error ranging from 5–8° with a variety of error sources15,16,26–30. Commonly cited sources of error include variability in examiner experience and patient positioning16. The most cited limitation of the device is the moving analog display and associated parallax error16. In addition, there are some practical constraints. Firstly, there is some concern that the scoliometer does not span the full rib prominence in adults, larger males and/or high BMI patients. Secondly, the 30° scale limit is sometimes exceeded in patients with severe torso rotations. In these cases, clinicians will at times use the digital inclinometer on their smartphones as a proxy scoliometer. A smartphone is even shorter than the scoliometer and has no central notch to allow for the prominent spinous processes when in AFBT (see Fig. 1)31, and may require a custom acrylic sleeve27 for accurate readings.

Despite the widespread acknowledgement of its measurement variability15,16,32, the analog scoliometer remains the gold standard method for ATR measurement in scoliosis centres across the world. Its concept and use have remained relatively unchanged since its invention, with only minimal effort invested into its digitisation and automation. The development of digital scoliometer devices33–37 and smartphone apps27,31,38,39 have mitigated the difficulties associated with the limited scale and parallax-prone analog readings. However, the clinical translation of such devices has been limited given their minimal incremental value over the analog device. They have limited utility for telehealth as these devices must still be placed directly on the patient’s back. In addition, a smartphone app or digital scoliometer does not automatically give the maximum angle value of rotation (which is the ATR). The user must still decide this as the device is carefully run down the patient’s back, going back and forth to determine the maximum value, similar to how an analog scoliometer is operated.

To our knowledge, no digital twin or virtual representation of the analog scoliometer has been explored to date. While the need for competent remote health management for rural and remote communities has always been evident40, the Covid-19 pandemic has provided much needed impetus for clinics to adopt the use of “digital twins” (a functioning virtual representation of a real-world physical object) in telehealth protocols41. However, as rightly noted in the review by Navarro et al. 42, while there has been an effort from the scientific community in developing telehealth alternatives for postural assessment in scoliosis, there is a lack of quantitative tools that allow direct measurement by a clinical professional and a paucity of quality studies showing detailed validation (repeatability, accuracy, etc) of the developed methods in the context of the clinic. There is a need to fill this gap in the literature.

With recent advances in digital imaging technology and computing power, there is now a renewed interest in using virtual data for the remote assessment of scoliosis, particularly in children. 3D surface scanning (3DSS) is one such imaging modality that uses structured light to produce 3D virtual surface topography information of a subject43. It has garnered significant interest within the medical community in recent years because of its demonstrated simplicity, accuracy, and reliability in obtaining the 3D shape of a patient’s torso and extracting useful information from it 43. In particular, handheld 3DSS devices are gaining popularity because of their portability and ease of use43–46. 3DSS has, to date, predominantly only been used to estimate lateral spine curvature from a standing posture and overall trunk balance from surface topography, using deviation maps and manual measurement techniques43–46.

This paper describes the first development and clinical validation of a digital twin for the analog scoliometer using patient-specific 3D models created by 3DSS. We investigate the reliability, accuracy, sensitivity and specificity of the tool, and its potential for future clinical use in telehealth initiatives. The use of a generative design algorithm that allows parametric user control is an effective way to introduce automation into the measurement process and streamline the analysis of patient-specific virtual data, potentially increasing the accuracy of the measurements and decreasing the variance between users. The development of such a digital twin is expected to have significant clinical value in rapidly emerging digital health management technologies for remote spine deformity care.

Methods

The digital scoliometer tool was developed within a graphical coding environment using generative 3D modelling to replicate the physical measurement process of ATR with the analog scoliometer. It is packaged into a simple graphical user interface for user-friendliness and validated using 3D virtual models of patients with Adolescent Idiopathic Scoliosis (AIS) and non-scoliotic, healthy developing adolescents. Details of the recruited participants, tool development and verification/validation of the tool are described below.

Datasets

Three cohorts were considered in the current study - a group of non-scoliotic, healthy developing adolescents (control), a group of AIS patients selected from a historical database of AIS patients treated at the Queensland Children’s Hospital Spine Clinic (QCHSC), and a group of operatively treated AIS patients also selected from this same historical database, for whom both pre- and post-operative clinical data was available. Patient positioning and scan protocol

The existing historical database of 3DSS data for AIS patients followed an optimised scan capture protocol. Before each 3D scan, the patient’s height, weight, and date of birth were recorded. Males were scanned shirtless while females were asked to change into crop tops provided to them in clinic by the researcher.

Once appropriately clothed, all patients were asked to stand on a wooden calibration board designed and fabricated in-house, which provided a reliable co-ordinate frame for later alignment of the digital scan data. The patients were positioned in the standard AFB position for analog scoliometer readings as well as 3D scanning. The patients were asked to align their heels with the back of the calibration board and bend forward 90o to align their torso parallel to the ground. Further to this, they were also instructed to extend their arms down towards the floor (palms close together), keep their knees in full extension and relax their shoulders, neck and head. Some patients (either due to post-op stiffness or short Hamstring length) were unable to bend to 90o, so they were instructed to bend as far as comfortable. Standardised patient positioning in all cases was done either by a senior surgeon in the clinic or by an experienced physiotherapist, all of whom have at least two decades of clinical experience in spine deformity care. The same specialist recorded the analog ATR using a scoliometer before the 3D scan.

A rectangular box support was then provided in front of the patients for stability, on which the patient could ‘rest’ their hands to prevent them from swaying during the scan. Patients were scanned at 22 frames per second using the Artec Leo scanner (Artec Group Inc., Luxembourg). The scan path was kept consistent, following a 360o path, with the feet and board captured first, followed by the legs, torso and finally the shoulders. Each scan roughly takes a minute. We replicated this protocol for the participant group of healthy developing adolescents, to ensure consistency between scanning and the resultant 3D virtual models.

Scan processing

The raw scans were stitched in the associated software Artec Professional (Version 17), and the fused, watertight 3D models were exported in a binary stereolithography (.stl) format to be further processed in Geomagic Wrap 2021 (Oqton, USA) using a standardised workflow developed in-house. Here the models were aligned to the world coordinate system using the co-ordinate frame that was captured on the wooden calibration board on which the patients stood to be scanned. The models were then cropped to only include the torso (head and limbs were removed) and any unwanted items captured in the background were deleted. Next, the model was globally smoothed. Excessive smoothing and noise reduction can result in loss of detail, so this operation was only performed once per model. The inbuilt “Mesh Doctor” tool was used to repair errors (such as non-manifold edges, self-intersections, highly creased edges, spikes, small components and small holes) if any, in the polygon surface mesh. The mesh was then conservatively decimated to reduce the file size. In preparation for further processing with the digital tool (detailed below), the anterior torso was cropped to a flat surface, leaving only the posterior torso from shoulder to just below the left/right posterior superior iliac spines (PSIS), as this is the region of primary interest when measuring ATR using a scoliometer.

Algorithm development

A virtual digital scoliometer tool was developed to measure ATR from 3DSS data using the 3D modelling and graphical programming software Rhino3D-Grasshopper (Robert McNeel and associates, USA). The tool is of a parametric design, allowing a user to make semi-automated measurements in a controlled and streamlined manner, while at the same time manipulating the orientation, perspective view and position of the 3D model in the Rhino workspace44. User inputs to the algorithm can be made through the graphical user interface (GUI) on the Grasshopper canvas. The user must follow three successive, simple steps detailed below to calculate ATR values:

Step 1 – Import the patient mesh model into Rhino by dragging the.stl file into the workspace. Place a minimum of 5 points (there is no maximum) on the spine, from which the algorithm will automatically estimate the spine curve.

Step 2 – Use the number sliders to define a region of interest (length of the spine that shows rib/musculature prominence) on the estimated spine curve within which to measure the ATR.

Step 3 – Using the two number sliders, indicate the lateral torso limits across which the ATR will be measured.

An example measurement on the Rhino viewport and Grasshopper user interface is shown in Fig. 2. ‘Scoli MP’ shows the calculated ATR value when the virtual scoliometer has the same dimensions as the analog device. ‘Scoli MP Ext’ works similarly but has an extended device length to span the full torso of the patient.

Virtual digital scoliometer tool developed on the Rhino7-Grasshopper software. A 3DSS torso model of a scoliosis patient in the AFBT is displayed in the Rhino viewport showing an example measurement (left) with the corresponding GUI on the Grasshopper canvas (right). The output boxes (green) display the calculated ATR. The user need not interact with the cluster of grey boxes in the lower half of the Grasshopper canvas – only the inputs in the upper half.

The logic of the algorithm is pictorially depicted in Fig. 3. The user is required to place a minimum of five points on the input mesh to indicate the approximate spine trajectory. A non-uniform rational B-spline (NURBS) curve with degree 4 and equally weighted points is created from the points placed by the user. The algorithm divides this curve into 1 mm segments, outputting a point at each division location, and resulting in a large number of “division points”. A search function is then deployed where the algorithm finds the closest point on the input mesh to each division point on the curve. A new high-resolution curve is then generated by the algorithm with these points, which forms the base on which the user defines the region of interest (ROI) for ATR measurement.

Step by step description of the algorithm logic starting from point placement by the user, scoliometer trajectory determination, creation of multiple planes along this trajectory within the defined ROI, creation of intersection curves, determination of extreme points on either side of the spine and ending in displaying the line (created with pairs of extreme points) with the maximum angle. The model showing the last step of the process is made partially transparent for viewing clarity of the lines.

Once the ROI is defined, the algorithm fits an arc with a curvature that approximates that of the ROI, providing a trajectory for the “virtual scoliometer” to follow. Within this ROI, it also creates multiple planes, one centred at each point on the high-resolution curve, perpendicular to the curve trajectory at that specific point. A Mesh-Plane intersection is solved for every plane, which creates an intersection curve every 1 mm along the ROI. The intersection curves are then recursively trimmed to create sub-curves according to the length of the analog scoliometer (‘Scoli MP’) and according to the user input of the torso limits (‘Scoli MP Ext’). In both versions, an allowance for the ridge caused by the spinous processes is provided, measuring the same length as the central notch found on the analog scoliometer.

On each intersection curve, on both sides of the spine, the most extreme point is identified, a line is drawn at each level joining the two extreme points, the angle of each line is measured, and the line with the maximum angle is extracted, all automatically. This angle is the ATR measurement displayed in the output box.

The same process can be repeated for another portion of the spine by simply adjusting the ROI, enabling a separate thoracic and lumbar ATR measurement as is clinically relevant.

Measurement and analysis protocol

Figure 4 gives an overview of the datasets used for the analyses listed in this section, details of the four users who participated in the study and their measurement attempts.

Chart showing dataset usage for four sets of analyses, details of the users who participated in the study and their measurement attempts.

Absolute difference - This analysis aimed to investigate what is gained by increasing the length of the virtual device. The absolute difference was calculated between ‘Scoli MP’ and ‘Scoli MP Ext’ (for all attempts of all users in all datasets, n = 780) and graphed in a histogram to determine whether increasing the length of the digital scoliometer to span the entire torso produced significantly different values to those obtained from the standard device length.

Reliability - This analysis aimed to investigate measurement repeatability when ATR was calculated by a single user and by multiple users. Measurements were completed over the span of a few weeks to mitigate any memory bias.

For intra-user reliability, all three measurement attempts for each User A, B and C were used. For inter-user reliability, the first measurement attempt of Users A, B, C and D were used.

In addition, the mean absolute error within each user was calculated by subtracting the ATR values obtained in each measurement attempt (A1-A2, A2-A3, A3-A1, and similarly for Users B and C) and averaging the results. The mean absolute error between users (A1-B1, B1-C1, C1-A1) was similarly calculated.

Accuracy - This analysis aimed to compare how close the digital measurements were to the clinically reported analog measurements. For the successful clinical uptake of such a virtual digital scoliometer tool, it is important to show that the measurements are comparable to those obtained with current clinical practices. For the scoliosis cohorts, the analog measurement was made by the QCH clinical staff during outpatient spine clinic appointments on the same day as their 3DSS scan. For the healthy non-scoliotic cohort, the analog measurement was made at the time of their 3DSS by a highly experienced QCHSC physiotherapist specialising in AIS.

A correlation matrix was calculated to obtain the relationship between the digital and analog scoliometer measurements. To further assess agreement in the values themselves, a Bland-Altman analysis was carried out by denoting the analog ATR values as the ‘reference’.

Sensitivity and Specificity - This analysis aimed to test the robustness of the algorithm when measuring ATRs of low magnitude and its suitability as a screening tool. The Scoliosis Research Society recommends a screening cutoff of 5–7° as a threshold for radiography referrals50. Sensitivity and specificity were therefore calculated for clinically relevant cutoff levels 5°, 6° and 7°. A measurement below the cutoff was marked negative (scoliosis unlikely), and a measurement equal to or greater than the cutoff was marked positive (scoliosis likely). In keeping with traditional clinical practice, and methods in other studies15,29,32, true positives and negatives were determined by whether a participant was formally diagnosed for scoliosis at the QCHSC based on the radiographic Cobb angle.

Statistical analysis

Simple descriptive statistics for absolute differences, means and standard deviations were calculated on Microsoft Excel (Microsoft, USA). Other statistical analyses were performed on R statistical computing software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria)51.

A precision-based sample size calculation was performed for each of the following statistical tests to ensure sufficient power in the analysis52. For reliability analysis, the minimum required sample size was determined to be n = 36, calculated with an expected reliability of 0.8, a precision of ± 0.1, a 95% confidence level and a minimum of three repetitions/raters per subject. For accuracy analysis, the minimum required sample size was determined to be n = 104, calculated with an expected correlation of 0.7, a precision of ± 0.1 and a 95% confidence level. For sensitivity and specificity analysis, the minimum required sample size was determined to be n = 70, calculated with an expected sensitivity of 0.9, an expected specificity of 0.9, a precision of ± 0.1 and a 95% confidence level.

Intra- and inter-user reliabilities were calculated using a two-way, random effects, absolute agreements, single rater Inter-Class Correlation Coefficient (ICC) according to the McGraw and Wong classification53. Significance level was set at 0.05 (two-tailed) at a power of 80%. ICC values of 0.5 and above indicate that the method is reliable. In detail, poor reliability is indicated by values less than 0.5, moderate reliability is indicated by values between 0.5 and 0.75, good reliability is indicated by values between 0.75 and 0.9, and excellent reliability is indicated by values greater than 0.90 54. Error bars in the ICC graphs represent 95% confidence interval (CI).

Spearman correlation coefficient was used to create the correlation matrix to account for non-linearity in the data. Significance levels were denoted as * (p < 0.05), ** (p < 0.01) and *** (p < 0.001). Limits of Agreement (LoA) for the Bland Altman analysis were calculated at a significance level of 0.95 as is standard. Sensitivity, specificity and test usefulness were calculated using standard formulae. In general, the utility of a test ranges from 1 (not useful) to 2 (perfectly useful)54.

Results

Qualitative user feedback

The time taken to complete one measurement was around 30 s for User A and 40 s for User B. User C took 2–3 min to complete one measurement in the first attempt but easily reduced this duration to around 45 s by the third attempt. User D took approximately 1 min to complete each measurement.

All users indicated excellent user-friendliness of the tool with respect to ease of input and low measurement time per scan. Users A and B were very comfortable using the tool and had no difficulty interacting with the 3D model, placing points on the spine or using the number sliders. Users C and D both initially had difficulty rotating the 3D model in a controlled manner, given their inexperience with 3D digital tools. However, they both reported a shallow learning curve, noting that it takes very little time and effort to get comfortable with the interface and make fast measurements.

Absolute difference

The mean absolute difference between ‘Scoli MP’ and ‘Scoli MP Ext’ measurements was 0.03 ± 0.24° with values ranging from a minimum of 0° to a maximum of 4.48°. A histogram of absolute differences is shown in Fig. 5.

Discussion

Over many years, there have been several attempts at developing a wide range of both radiographic as well as non-radiographic methods for quantifying the intrinsic vertebral rotation that is a key feature of scoliosis55. Using radiography, several mathematical approaches like the Nash-Moe method56, the Perdriolle method57, the Stokes method58, the Raimondi method59, and the Rib Vertebra Angle Difference method (for infants)60 have been developed, among others. However, these methods suffer from limited reliability as they require the difficult identification of 3D vertebral landmarks on a 2D radiograph taken in the standing posture61.

Non-radiographic methods cannot directly measure intrinsic vertebral rotation but are instead used to infer the severity of rotational deformity from surface features. Most notable of these include the scoliometer paired with AFBT14, 2D analysis of photographs62,63, Moiré topography64, rasterstereography65–68, spinal ultrasound69, depth sensors70,71 and infrared thermography72. Except for the scoliometer, all other methods require the installation of fixed equipment and cannot easily be translated to a digital environment. These methods also require calculations and/or complex interpretations of measurement outputs. As such, the most widely used tool to measure axial rotation in scoliosis or for scoliosis screening continues to be the analog scoliometer or a smartphone mimicry of it.

The work presented here is the first development of a digital twin framework for the analog scoliometer and a full validation of its reliability, accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity. The ATR can be measured digitally with simple user inputs and a patient-specific virtual 3D model. The tool was developed on the Rhino3D-Grasshopper commercial software which is affordable (AUD 300 for an educational license and AUD 1500 for a commercial license for lifetime use) and easily usable in a generic computer. Rhino3D-Grasshopper was developed primarily for use in the architecture field to enable the parametric design of buildings but is now gaining popularity in other fields due to its ability to create 3D workflows44,73. The digital twin developed on this software shows promise for enabling remote assessment of trunk rotation in paediatric scoliosis. It uses novel generative 3D modelling techniques to measure trunk rotation on any patient 3D model using a set of pre-programmed rules and boundary conditions to automatically perform certain functions.

The digital tool was tested for reliability and found to produce highly repeatable measurements both for a single user and multiple users. The commonly cited measurement variability of 5–8° and ICC 0.81–0.95 of the analog scoliometer in the literature is always inclusive of the error caused by patient positioning27. A study conducted at the Queensland Children’s Hospital, Australia in 2012 used plaster-casts instead of real patients for repeated measurements27, reducing the impact of patient positioning error, and reported an intra-user variability of under 2° and inter-user variability of under 4° for the analog scoliometer. In comparison, the digital twin presented here has a lower variability of under 1° in both intra- and inter-user conditions. In addition, we report high reliability results (ICC > 0.97) that compare well against those reported by Izatt et al. (0.92–0.95)27 for the analog scoliometer. A limitation of this study is that we did not explicitly consider the influence of patient positioning variability when capturing scans. We expect that the reliability of our experienced staff in positioning a child in AFBT and measuring the ATR using an analog scoliometer is similar to those reported in other studies15,26,28,29, but we did not quantify this.

The ATR measurements produced on the digital twin showed a strong positive correlation with the clinically measured scoliometer ATR values, indicating that it would be a good digital alternative to the current analog measures. In addition, the digital ATR values were shown to have excellent agreement (92.7%) with the analog ATR values (having 95% points within LoA usually indicates that the two methods being tested are interchangeable74. The average bias of less than 1° between the digital and analog methods is negligible. The proportional bias trend line shows that the digital twin tends to slightly underestimate the ATR values below 19° and slightly overestimate the ATR values above it. However, this finding must be interpreted with caution as ATR values above 19° were only found in the pre-op cohort and only comprise a small proportion of the tested datasets. This trend may change if the composition of the tested dataset is altered to have more high-value ATRs. In saying this, we also note that the current proportional bias is very small and entirely contained within the LoA – small trend variations are unlikely to be clinically relevant, but large variations may have some clinical relevance.

The sensitivity and specificity of the analog device was on par with those found in the literature16,63,75, providing a reasonable baseline for comparison. The digital twin demonstrated an equal or higher sensitivity and specificity compared to the analog scoliometer at all three threshold values. It shows evidence of satisfactory performance as a screening tool in all three cutoff levels with test usefulness above 1.5 (a value above this threshold generally indicates that the test method is useful54. A 6° cutoff yielded the highest test usefulness for both devices.

In addition to the validation presented in this paper, it is worth noting that the developed virtual digital tool is not constrained by gravity as is the case for the analog scoliometer. It can measure ATR accurately regardless of the positioning of the torso relative to the ground, enabling the quantification of torso distortion with the patient in a more upright position if required. This is key for making ATR measurements on stiff post-operative patients who find it difficult to correctly position their bodies for the AFBT. Reduction in flexibility is expected post spinal fusion surgery, and its extent is dependent on the type of surgery, the number of fused vertebrae levels and the location of the fusion76,77. Whether this loss of flexibility is long term or not is also dependent on a variety of factors78–80. ATR measurement in a slightly more upright position is also relevant for those with limited Hamstring muscle length, which depends on the general fitness and activity level of the patient.

ATR measurements may additionally be taken in the standing position should this be of clinical relevance in determining cosmetic deformity and overall trunk balance. Evidence of the ability of the tool to measure the ATR regardless of the bending angle of the patient’s torso (bending angle calculations (Supplementary Fig. 1) using a small measurement algorithm add-on (Supplementary Fig. 2)) is shown in Supplementary File 1.

Further, the results show that increasing the length of the digital tool to span the full breadth of the patient’s torso did not significantly alter the measurements. Since the ATR measurement is calculated from the “most extreme point” within the ROI on both sides of the torso, the two measures will only differ if the Scoli MP width is not wide enough to capture the real “most extreme point” on the torso. But as seen in the results, this is rarely the case. For nearly all patients, Scoli MP and Scoli MP ext measure the same points, giving the same value. While the extended length is a useful feature of the tool, its practical relevance is not evident, as the original length of the analog scoliometer device was not shown to be a disadvantage.

In this study we have used the Artec Leo (current cost AUD 38,400), a high-end 3D scanner with real-time frame registration capabilities, reliably producing high-quality scans even under poor scanning conditions, be it environmental (e.g., improper lighting) or operator-related (e.g., inconsistent distance to object, shaky handling). While it was necessary to validate the performance (accuracy and reliability) of the digital twin in a controlled low-error environment with skilled personnel and high-quality 3DSS acquisitions, we envision the eventual usage of this tool in conjunction with low-cost, easy-to-use smartphone photogrammetry. Photogrammetry can now be done by any smartphone using commercially available photogrammetry apps (free and paid apps are both available). In the last five years, iPhones also come equipped with high quality LIDAR capability which some apps leverage for 3D scanning. There are also several photogrammetry reconstruction software available (both free and paid) which enable users to generate 3D models on any standard computer from a set of photographs which can be taken by any smartphone. Smartphone photogrammetry applications are, therefore, starting to gain popularity in the medical field, allowing for new methods of remote healthcare management81–83. 3D captures of a patient taken in a remote clinic or in their own home can be transferred to the treating tertiary spine clinic where the 3D models can be reconstructed, and surface metrics can be measured.

The ability of a clinician to reliably measure the ATR on a high-quality 3D model using the digital twin has been investigated in this paper. However, future work is required to estimate the reliability of a non-expert image capture using smartphone photogrammetry and test it in conjunction with the digital twin. Further research comparing ATR measurement on 3D captures taken by an expert and a novice is required. In addition, there is currently no study in the literature that invesigates the relibaility of a lay person to instruct a child into a correct AFB position. While the position can be achieved by a simple forward bending motion, small details such as relaxing the shoulders and keeping the arms and knees straight need to be conveyed effectively to the patient. Future work in this space is required for deployment of at-home scanning protocols.

It is important to note that it is still necessary for a patient to visit a specialist spine clinic in person for new treatment interventions (for e.g. the first time a patient is prescribed a spine brace or 1 week before surgery). However, a telehealth appointment where the specialist can effectively “see” the body of the patient (3D model), measure the ATR with a virtual scoliometer, measure the Cobb angle from a radiograph as well as speak to the patient over a phone call can potentially replace regular (every 3–6 months) in-person visits when monitoring the progression of scoliosis during the adolescent growth period. A future non-inferiority randomised clinical trial is necessary to evaluate concordance in patient outcomes between such a telehealth appointment and a standard in-clinic face to face consultation.

Furthermore, an implementation feasibility study using a co-design methodology, with data analysed using a qualitative content analysis in a NASSS (Nonadoption, Abandonment, Scale-up, Spread & Sustainability) framework84 is required to understand barriers to adoption of this digital technology in a rural setting. For the successful implementation of this tool within a telehealth framework, much future work is also required in other parts of the pipeline such as the development of culturally responsive education pieces to enable at-home image capture, establishment of secure data transfer of sensitive health-related information and protocols for management of 3D models within hospital documentation systems.

In conclusion, new remote health technology is needed to connect regional, remote and rural patients more effectively with spinal specialists. As a first step towards this complex goal, this proof-of-concept work has established a streamlined digital twin for the analog scoliometer that uses a semi-automated algorithm to measure ATR from patient-specific 3D models. The digital twin mimics the process of manually following the spine curve with an analog scoliometer to find the position of maximum asymmetry in a scoliotic torso. The tool is fast, reliable, accurate, sensitive, specific, not limited by gravity, can measure angles higher than 30°, and is not affected by parallax errors. While it was necessary to validate the tool on high accuracy 3DSS data for the purpose of the study, it can potentially be used to measure the ATR on a 3D model of a patient obtained from any source. This study represents an emerging and significant area of research, with this technology development having future impact and relevance in telehealth initiatives for remote diagnosis and management of scoliosis and many other areas of musculoskeletal medicine.
