Unraveling the implications of ULBP1 expression from pan-cancer to head and neck squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Yu Peng, Ye Zhou, Cong Xu, Lanzhen Cui, Lijun Zhang, Bin Di, Xiaoming Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
UL-16 binding protein 1 (ULBP1), a ligand for the NK cell-stimulatory receptor NKG2D, is expressed as a cell-surface protein on malignant cells. While accumulating evidence links ULBP1 to cancer progression, its pan-cancer immunological and clinical significance remains underexplored. Through systematic bioinformatics analyses of 33 malignancies, we uncovered that widespread ULBP1 dysregulation across cancers was associated with advanced tumor staging and patient survival. ROC curve analysis further identified ULBP1 as a robust diagnostic biomarker. Subsequent exploration demonstrated ULBP1’s epigenetic regulation through DNA/RNA methylation and its influence on tumor microenvironment remodeling. Functional validation in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) confirmed that ULBP1 overexpression promoted cellular migration, invasion, proliferation in vitro and in vivo, while inhibiting apoptosis, suggesting NK cell-independent oncogenic mechanisms. We discovered that ULBP1 protein was upregulated in HNSCC tissue, induced epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), as well as activation of the KRAS signaling pathway. Intriguingly, elevated ULBP1 was correlated with improved clinical outcomes following PD-1/PD-L1 blockade therapy, indicating its potential as a predictive biomarker for checkpoint inhibitor response. This study comprehensively delineates ULBP1’s functional mechanisms across multiple cancers and experimentally validates its oncogenic role in HNSCC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51832-0.

Introduction

Ligands of the natural killer group 2D (NKG2DL) family exhibit highly restricted expression in healthy tissues but are widely expressed in tumors1. In humans, the NKG2DL family consists of MHC class I chain-related protein A/B (MICA/B) and UL16 binding protein 1–6 (ULBP1-6) molecules which are also known as retinoic acid early transcript (RAET). Among the human NKG2D ligands, MICA, MICB, ULBP4-5 are type I transmembrane proteins, whereas ULBP1-3 and ULBP6 are glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchored proteins2. MICA/B is primarily involved in the DNA damage response by serving as key ligands for natural killer (NK) cells and cytotoxic T lymphocytes, thereby mediating the recognition and elimination of DNA-damaged cells3,4. ULBP exerts its core function by specifically binding to the NKG2D receptor on the surface of NK cells, CD8⁺ T cells, and γδ T cells, thereby activating downstream signaling pathways to trigger the cytotoxic activity of these immune cells and mediate the recognition and lysis of stressed cells5. Nevertheless, serum NKG2D ligands have been shown to impair T cell function6, reducing NK cell activity by decreasing surface expression of NKG2D receptor7, and mediate tumor evasion by reducing peripheral NK cell numbers8. Together, NKG2DL exhibit dual immunoregulatory functions: activating immune responses to eliminate infected or malignant cells, while also facilitating immune evasion to circumvent immune surveillance.

UL16 binding protein 1 (ULBP1), recognized as a NKG2D ligand, has been previously implicated in modulating NK cell-mediated innate immune responses. Among several known NKG2D ligands, human cytomegalovirus infection was shown to induce ULBP1, ULBP2, and ULBP3 in human foreskin fibroblasts9. HIV-1 infection similarly induces the surface expression of ULBP1 and ULBP2 in primary CD4+ T-cells through the DNA damage response10. HCV induces the surface expression of ULBP1 in human hepatocytes, enabling NK cells to recognize and attack HCV-infected hepatocytes9. Notably, ULBP1 is highly expressed in tumor tissues, as reported in previous studies, which is associated with poor prognosis in breast cancer and early tumor recurrence in liver cancer3,11. The potential role and function of ULBP1 in pan-cancer is unexplored systematically.

This study comprehensively analyzed the expression and prognostic value of ULBP1 in human cancers, investigated the biological functions and mechanisms of ULBP1 across various cancers, and explored the relationships between ULBP1 levels and tumor-infiltrating immune cells. We placed a focus on head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC)—a heterogeneous disease encompassing oral, pharyngeal, and laryngeal cancers that rank as the sixth most prevalent cancer type12. Despite advancements in diagnosis and treatment, the 5-year survival rates for advanced HNSCC remain low, at around 50–60%13. Our study aimed to determine whether ULBP1 serves as a potential biomarker for multiple cancers and its association with tumor immune responses. Moreover, we validated the function of ULBP1 in HNSCC in vitro and explored the impact of ULBP1 on cell-killing efficacy of NK cells. Finally, we performed a comprehensive analysis of ULBP1-regulated signaling networks in HNSCC and evaluated the association between differential ULBP1 expression and immune checkpoint inhibitor efficacy.

Results

Expression of ULBP1 and its association with clinicopathologic characters in pan-cancer

Through group comparisons and subsequent paired T-tests, we concluded that ULBP1 expression is elevated in various types of tumors, predominantly in BLCA, BRCA, CHOL, COAD, ESCA, HNSCC, LIHC, LUSC, PARD, READ, STAD, THCA, and UCEC (Fig. 1A, B). By combining data from the TCGA and GTEx databases, we visualized the expression level of ULBP1 across 33 types of cancers and found that it is overexpressed in 20 malignancies (Fig. 1C). Further pan-cancer analyses showed that the expression level of ULBP1 was significantly elevated in advanced stages in BLCA, HNSCC, KIRC, KIRP, LIHC, MESO, TGCT (Fig. 1D–J). ULBP1 overexpression correlates with poor survival in pan-cancer

The impact of the variation in gene expression on patient prognosis is a critical standard to measure whether the gene is an important biomarker for this kind of cancer. The forest plots of single-factor Cox regression analysis showed that ULBP1 overexpression is a risk factor for short overall survival (OS) in 10 types of cancers, including ACC, BRCA, HNSCC, KIRC, KIRP, LAML, LGG, PRAD, THYM, UVM (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Fig. S1). Further analyses showed that ULBP1 overexpression is markedly associated with short disease-specific survival (DSS) in 9 malignancies: ACC, ESCA, GBM, KIRC, KIRP, LGG, PRAD, UCEC and UVM (Fig. 2B). In addition, ULBP1 overexpression is significantly associated with short progression-free Interval (PFI) in 10 malignancies: ACC, BLCA, BRCA, ESCA, KIRC, KIRP, LGG, MESO, PRAD and UVM (Fig. 2C) and poor disease-free interval in BRCA, KIRP, LGG and PRAD (Fig. 2D). Prognostic value of ULBP1 for OS, DSS, PFI and DFI were demonstrated by Kaplan–Meier (KM) survival curves, with similar results of univariate Cox regression analyses. In patients with ACC, BRCA, COAD DLBC, HNSCC, KIRC, KIRP, LAML, LGG, SARC and UVM, ULBP1 overexpression is significantly associated with short OS (Supplementary Fig. S1). The DSS of patients with ACC, BLCA, KIRC, KIRP, LGG, and UCEC tumors is significantly shortened (Supplementary Fig. S2). Moreover, KM survival analyses showed that ULBP1 overexpression is significantly associated with short PFI in patients with ACC, BLCA, ESCA, KIRC, KIRP, LGG, MESO, PRAD and UVM (Supplementary Fig. S3), and is significantly associated with short DFI in patients with KICH, KIRP and PRAD (Supplementary Fig. S4). Implications of ULBP1 expression in cancer prediction and diagnosis

The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was used to assess the discriminative power in identifying tumors from normal cells across cancers. The expression of ULBP1 had AUC values of 0.942, 0.998, 0.963, 0.937, and 0.942 in CESE, CHOL, COAD, ESCA and READ respectively, which indicated high accuracy. The expression of ULBP1 had AUC values of 0.874, 0.809, 0.795, 0.71, 0.758, 0.819, 0.871, 0.875, 0.783, and 0.894 in BLCA, BRCA, HNSCC, KICH, KIRP, PRAD, SARC, STAD, THCA, and UCEC respectively, which indicated a certain accuracy (Fig. 3A–O). Taking HNSCC as an example, this cancer type was prioritized for in-depth investigation based on three key observations from our pan-cancer analysis. Firstly, HNSCC exhibited one of the most pronounced stage-dependent increases in ULBP1 expression. Additionally, ULBP1 overexpression demonstrated prognostic significance in HNSCC, with a statistically significant hazard ratio for overall survival. Furthermore, the value of ULBP1 in the prediction and diagnosis of tumors, including HNSCC, provides a basis for further exploring its potential mechanisms in regulating immune responses within the tumor microenvironment, thereby offering new insights into tumor progression.Fig. 3Diagnostic value of ULBP1 overexpression in pan-cancer. ULBP1 expression and its association with antitumor immunity across human cancers

The immune response is the most crucial mechanism by which our body defends itself against the development of tumor cells, which plays a vital role in identifying and eliminating abnormal cells, thereby preventing the formation and progression of tumors14. In order to explore whether ULBP1 is involved in tumor immunity in 33 kinds of cancers, we performed co-expression analysis of ULBP1 with a series of immune-related genes from TISIDB database. The heatmap of co-expression analysis demonstrated that the expression of ULBP1 was closely associated with immune checkpoint genes (Fig. 5A, B), chemokines (Fig. 5C), chemokine receptors (Fig. 5D), and major histocompatibility complex (MHC) (Fig. 5E) in almost all types of cancer, except for in ACC, BLCA, GBM, OV, PAAD and UCS. Interestingly, we found that the expression of ULBP1 was significantly positively correlated with immune responses in BRCA, DLBC, KICH, LIHC, LGG, LUAD, PCPG, PRAD and UVM, and significantly negatively correlated with immune responses in CESC, COAD, ESCA, HNSCC, LAML, LUSC, STAD, TGCT and UCEC (Fig. 5A–E). In HNSCC, ULBP1 exhibited a unique expression pattern characterized by negative correlations with chemokine receptors and major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecule. This inverse relationship suggests a potential immunosuppressive role of ULBP1 in HNSCC. We employed cBioPortal to inspect the mutation frequency of ULBP1 in the TCGA database. The alteration frequency with several mutation types in different tumors is displayed in Fig. 6A. We found that ocular melanoma and mature B-cell neoplasms shared deep deletion level with the ULBP1 alteration frequency exceeding 6%, and sarcoma exhibited high amplification of ULBP1 (Fig. 6A). Deficiencies lead to microsatellite instability (MSI), increasing mutation loads in cancer genes and tumor mutation burden (TMB), contributing to tumor initiation and serving as independent predictors of immune checkpoint blockade efficacy16. Correlation analysis of ULBP1 expression with methylation modification

Epigenetics is a discipline that modulates heritable gene expression without DNA sequence changes. 5-methylcytosine (m5C) DNA methylation is a major DNA epigenetic modification, catalyzed by DNA methyltransferases that transfer a methyl group from S-adenosylmethionine to the cytosine residues in genomic DNA, most frequently in CpG dinucleotide regions. m6A RNA methylation is the most abundant internal modification in eukaryotic mRNAs, which is regulated by specific regulators including writers, erasers, and readers and participates in the regulation of RNA splicing, translation, and degradation17. Abnormal m5C hypermethylation can silence tumor suppressor genes, while dysregulated m6A modification can affect the stability and translation of oncogenes or tumor suppressor mRNAs18. The results demonstrated that ULBP1 was positively correlated with the majority of DNA and RNA methylation-related genes mainly, writers, readers and erasers (Fig. 6E, F). To address the epigenetic regulation of ULBP1 gene transcription, we assessed the promoter DNA methylation levels of ULBP1 in various tumors based on UALCAN database. The promoter of ULBP1 exhibits hypomethylation in various cancers, such as BLCA, BRCA, CHOL, CESC, HNSCC, KIRP, LUAD, PRAD, PCPG, TGCT and UCEC (Supplementary Fig. S5A–K). The aforementioned results indicate that the ULBP1 gene may exert a functional influence by modulating gene methylation, thereby regulating gene expression levels in tumors.

Effect of ULBP1 on NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity in HNSCC cells

To investigate the impact of ULBP1, as a ligand of NKG2D, on NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity against HNSCC cells, we designed a co-culture system as illustrated in Fig. 10A, oe-NC and oe-ULBP1 HNSCC cells were cultured either alone or co-cultured with NK-92 cells. After 6 h of co-culture, NK-92 cells were removed, and the HNSCC cells were collected for apoptosis assay and colony formation assay. Flow cytometry analyses indicated that NK-92 cells demonstrate a potent cytotoxic effect against HNSCC cells; however, ULBP1 overexpression did not appear to enhance NK-92-mediated cytotoxicity (Fig. 10B, C). It can be observed that ULBP1 overexpression enhanced the clonogenic potential of TU686 and Cal-27 cells (Fig. 10B–E, Fig. 9C). These findings suggest that ULBP1 plays an oncogenic role in HNSCC, potentially through mechanisms independent of NK cell-mediated immune surveillance. ULBP1 promotes tumor growth in vivo and is upregulated in HNSCC tissues

To investigate the role of ULBP1 in in vivo tumor growth and its expression pattern in HNSCC, we conducted tumorigenesis assays. Fadu cells stably overexpressing ULBP1 were injected subcutaneously into the flank of nude mice, showed enhanced tumor formation compared to the negative control (Fig. 11A–C). IHC staining of excised tumors from xenograft models revealed increased expression of ULBP1 and the proliferation marker Ki67 in the oe-ULBP1 group (Fig. 11D). Conversely, the stable knockdown of ULBP1 showed reduced tumorigenicity and lower Ki67 expression. (Fig. 11E–H). To explore the clinical relevance of ULBP1, we performed univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses on HNSCC patients from TCGA database (Fig. 11I). Both ULBP1 and pathologic stage were identified as independent prognostic factors in HNSCC. In the GSE58911 dataset, ULBP1 mRNA expression was significantly higher in HNSCC tissues (Fig. 11J). We collected 40 pairs of HNSCC tissues and their corresponding adjacent normal tissues from patients. IHC staining of peri-tumor (n = 40) and HNSCC tissues (n = 40) showed that ULBP1 is mainly localized in the cytoplasmic/membranous region, revealed stronger ULBP1 staining in HNSCC tissues (Fig. 11K), and quantification of IHC scores confirmed a significant upregulation of ULBP1 in HNSCC tissues (Fig. 11L). Additionally, the expression of ULBP1 protein is upregulated in most tumor in the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database (Fig. 11M–R). ULBP1 predicts efficacy of immunotherapy response

Immunophenoscore (IPS), a known predictor of response to immune checkpoint therapy, is determined by evaluating key immune-related gene expressions and composed of scores from four components: MHC molecules, effector cells (primarily T cells), suppressor cells, and immune checkpoints. A high IPS score indicates enhanced immune activity. Our analysis revealed that the ULBP1-high group exhibited lower IPS scores, suggesting that ULBP1 may inhibit T cells to create an immunosuppressive microenvironment in HNSCC (Fig. 12I, J). Notably, patients with high ULBP1 expression had poorer responses to anti-PD-L1/PD-1 blockade therapy than those with low ULBP1 expression (Fig. 12K, L). Combined with our previous findings, ULBP1 may drive therapeutic resistance to anti-PD-L1/PD-1 blockade by shaping an immunosuppressive microenvironment, impairing T cell function and inhibiting immune cell infiltration. These findings highlight the importance of ULBP1 expression as a potential prognostic biomarker for predicting therapeutic sensitivity to anti-PD-1/PD-L1 blockade.

Discussion

The activating receptor NK cell group 2 member D (NKG2D) has been well established to mediate antitumor immunity in experimental animal models, by recognizing and binding to ligands on the surface of tumor cells. However, whether NKG2D ligands contribute to tumor suppression or progression clinically remains controversial. We first systematically analyzed ULBP1 mRNA expression patterns in human cancers through TCGA databases and found that ULBP1 was dysregulated in most cancer types compared to normal tissues. In addition, high ULBP1 expression was associated with advanced clinical stage in 7 cancers (Fig. 1). ULBP1 is minimally expressed or undetectable under normal conditions but upregulated during stress, infection, or cancer. Studies have revealed that epithelial cells (such as the intestinal and skin cells) exhibit low-level constitutive or localized expression of ULBP1 in normal tissues20,21. This implies that NKG2D ligands may exert distinct roles in various tissues or cell types. HNSCC, as an epithelial-derived malignant tumor, is likely to be closely associated with the regulation of ULBP1 expression in its biological behavior.

Moreover, we found ULBP1 is closely connected to patients’ survival, and was a risk factor for poor prognosis in most cases (Figs. 2 and 3). Consistent with our findings, previous studies reported that patients with breast cancer (BRCA) with high ULBP1 expression had a poorer prognosis11.The ULBP1 gene has a high diagnostic and prognostic value in COAD, up-regulated ULBP1 gene of patients with COAD predicts a worse prognosis22. McGilvray et al.23 also indicated that patients with ovarian cancer with high expression of ULBP1 had a worse survival than those patients with no expression of ULBP1. Similarly, T cell leukemia less often expressed MICA and more often expressed ULBP15. Weaver et al. demonstrate that esophageal cancer stem-like cells were resistant to NK cell cytotoxicity, likely due to downregulation of ULBP1 expression24. These conflicting observations highlight the tissue-specificity of ULBP1 function, further supporting our conclusion that ULBP1 expression is a significant biomarker for predicting prognosis.

The tumor microenvironment, composed of various cell types such as immune cells, stromal cells, cancer-associated fibroblasts, and endothelial cells, exerts a significant impact on tumor therapeutic efficacy and prognosis25. Our results indicated that ULBP1 expression was significantly associated positively correlated with M0/M1 macrophages, Th2 cells —known to suppress antitumor immunity, while negatively correlating with resting mast cells, Th17 cells —key producers of pro-inflammatory cytokines that enhance antitumor immunity (Fig. 4). Our results suggested that high ULBP1 expression may help shape an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment by inhibiting pro-inflammatory Th17 cell differentiation while promoting anti-inflammatory/regulatory Th2 cell differentiation. The expression of ULBP1 was associated with intratumoral infiltration and distribution of most immune cells, including NK cells, most NK T cells, γδ T + and CD8 T + cells22,26.A correlation analysis revealed significant associations between ULBP1 and various subsets of immune cells including DC cells, T-cells, macrophages, Th2 cells, Treg cells and Th1 cells. However, a negative association was observed with CD8 + T-cells and NK cells11. Importantly, our study extends these observations to HNSCC: we found that ULBP1 expression was negatively correlated with infiltration of key antitumor immune cells such as CD8 T cells, cytotoxic cells, dendritic cells (DC), NK CD56dim (primarily responsible for cytotoxicity), γδ T cells, Th1, and Th17. This suggests that the upregulation of ULBP1 in HNSCC may reflect a weaker immunoactivity, making it more susceptible to immune evasion.

In most malignancies, ULBP1 exhibited significant covariation with immune checkpoint genes, immunosuppressive checkpoints, chemokines and their receptors, and antigen presentation machinery (HLA genes) (Fig. 5). Notably, ULBP1 expression negatively associated with MHC molecules and chemokine receptors in HNSCC, suggesting a context-dependent immune suppression. The rise in ULBP1 along with the decline in antigen presentation pathways may enable tumors to evade the immune system in two ways: suppresses chemokine receptors to reduce immune cell recruitment, and decrease MHC class I to lower tumor antigen visibility. ULBP1’s relationship with MSI and TMB suggests it may be implicated in the oncogenesis and progression of cancers with high mutation loads, potentially serving as a biomarker for predicting responses to immunotherapy (Fig. 6). The positive correlations between ULBP1 and methylation-related genes imply that ULBP1 may influence gene expression through epigenetic modifications (Fig. 6). Collectively, ULBP1 may function as a significant player in tumor immunology and epigenetics, influencing both tumor behavior and immune interactions.

Our analyses and assays showed that elevated ULBP1 expression is associated with enhanced activity of the KRAS pathway and EMT signaling, which could potentially contribute to immune evasion in HNSCC (Fig. 12). Previous studies have shown that increased activity of the ERK signaling pathway in myeloma cells induces elevated expression of the NKG2D ligands MICA/B and ULBP227. It was reported that EMT signaling facilitates immune evasion in circulating gastric cancer cells by modulating the expression of ULBP128. In summary, ULBP1 may play a critical role in anti-tumor activity of immune cells, however, the current experiments can only demonstrate a correlation between ULBP1 and KRAS pathway activation, but cannot prove that ULBP1 is a direct or upstream regulator of KRAS. Further mechanistic studies are needed to validate this direct relationship.

In recent years, NKG2DLs were shown to be shed into the bloodstream, and the presence of their soluble form in patient serum samples has been associated with poor prognosis for other cancers29,30. Easom et al.31 reported that ULBP1 elevated above 2000 pg/ml is a predictor of poor prognosis in HCC, and the level of expression of MICA/B and ULBP1/2 is higher in HCCs with an early tumor recurrence3. Mechanistically, β-catenin signaling represses NKG2D ligands like MICA/B and ULBP1/2, creating an “immunoevasive niche” linked to advanced HCC features27. In circulating gastric tumor cells (CTCs), TGF-β1 induces EMT, leading to ULBP1 suppression via SMAD3/4-dependent transcriptional inhibition. This allows CTCs to evade NK cell detection while gaining motility27. Elevated serum levels of soluble NKG2D ligands (sNKG2DLs), including sULBP1 and sULBP3, are significantly increased in breast cancer patients, with particularly pronounced elevations observed in ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) cohorts. This early-stage ligand shedding may reflect a pre-invasive immune evasion strategy32. Leukemia cells from patients variously express MIC and ULBP molecules and release proteolytically cleaved soluble MICA fragments into the tumor microenvironment, which competitively bind to NKG2D receptors on NK cells, impairing their immunosurveillance capacity and promoting immune evasion33. Taken together, these findings confirm the dual modulatory role of NKG2DLs in tumor immunity: they enhance immunosurveillance via NK cell activation while promoting immune evasion via ligand shedding-mediated NKG2D receptor exhaustion.

While previous studies demonstrate that surface NKG2D ligand expression renders tumor cells susceptible to NK-mediated cytotoxicity through receptor engagement34, our findings reveal that ULBP1 may exhibit oncogenic functions that transcend its canonical role as an NKG2D ligand in Uncini our study, ULBP1 was upregulated in HNSCC tissues compared with normal tissues, as well as in the TCGA and GEO databases. The overexpression of ULBP1 facilitated the proliferation, migration, and invasion of HNSCC cells (Fig. 9). Moreover, our results indicate that the overexpression of ULBP1 did not enhance the tumor-killing effect mediated by NK-92 cells (Fig. 10). The importance of NKG2D is underscored by observations that tumors arise more frequently in NKG2D-deficient mice35 and that many viruses and tumors have evolved mechanisms to escape NKG2D recognition2. These differences between canonical NKG2DL function and our observations in HNSCC highlight the need for further investigation into the mechanisms by which ULBP1 regulates tumor growth and immune evasion in this disease.

HNSCC is etiologically linked to viral infections, with human papillomavirus (HPV) was recognized as a predominant oncogenic driver in oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma36. Human cytomegalovirus (HCMV) specifically targets MICA using its viral glycoprotein US9, blocking its interaction with the NKG2D receptor, thus providing a new mechanism for viral immune evasion37. However, our analysis indicated that HPV status is not correlate with ULBP1 mRNA expression. The association between ULBP1 and immune responses of other viral infections requires further investigation. In our findings, high ULBP1 expression predicted a poorer response to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade therapy, suggesting its potential as a biomarker for immunotherapy (Fig. 12). This implies that ULBP1 creates an immunosuppressive TME characterized by T cell inhibition, which may synergize with PD-1/PD-L1 signaling to inhibit antitumor immunity.

Collectively, our analysis establishes ULBP1 as a multi-cancer oncogenic driver and reveals its clinical relevance in HNSCC progression, potentially through modulating oncogenic pathways. Notably, ULBP1’s dual function as both a carcinogenic factor and immune modulator reshapes our understanding of the NKG2D ligand-receptor axis, proposing a therapeutic strategy to concurrently target malignant cells and reverse immunosuppression. Although we conducted a comprehensive analysis of ULBP1, there were certain limitations in this study. The role of ULBP1 in tumor progression across different cancer types requires further in vivo and in vitro validation. The potential role of soluble ULBP1 as a circulating biomarker in HNSCC warrants further investigation. Additionally, while ULBP1 appears linked to immune regulation, direct evidence of its impact on immunotherapy is lacking. Besides, the roles of ULBP1 in NK cells, macrophages, and T cells remain to be explored. Further studies are necessary to confirm the value of ULBP1 in prognosis and immunotherapy.

Materials and methods

Pan-cancer data acquisition

The pan-cancer samples were downloaded from TCGA (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) and partial normal samples were downloaded from GTEx (https:// commonfund.nih.gov/GTEx/). The log2 [TPM (Transcripts per million) + 1] transformed expression data were applied. The data from TCGA and GTEx were integrated including mRNA sequencing data for ULBP1 in 33 different types of tumor tissues and 31 normal tissues with relevant clinical data (survival status, clinical and pathological stage). During this process, cancers with fewer than 3 samples were discarded. In addition, R packages “limma”, “ggplot2” and “ggpubr” were used to analyze the expression of ULBP1 in cancer tissues. The Wilcoxon rank sum test was used for unpaired group comparisons between TCGA and GTEx datasets, while the Wilcoxon signed rank test was employed for paired sample analyses. The abbreviations for different tumor types are shown (Supplementary Table S1). GSE58911 and GSE40774 datasets along with their corresponding platform annotation files were downloaded from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The expression data of ULBP1 and sample grouping information were extracted for comparative analysis.

Assessment of ULBP1 expression and clinicopathological context

To evaluate the association between ULBP1 mRNA expression and pathological stages across TCGA tumors, the Kruskal–Wallis test was initially applied. For visualization in Fig. 1D–J, stages I and II were combined as “early stage”, while stages III and IV were combined as “advanced stage”. The difference in ULBP1 expression between these two groups was then assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test. The relationship between ULBP1 expression and other clinicopathological contexts was analyzed using R packages “limma” and “ggpubr”.

Analysis of prognostic and diagnostic value of ULBP1

Prognostic data were collated by excluding samples with incomplete pan-cancer expression data or clinical information. Patients were dichotomized into ULBP1-high and ULBP1-low expression groups based on the median expression level within each cancer type. Cox regression model was constructed by using the R package “survival” and the Kaplan–Meier method was used to assess prognostic factors including overall survival, disease-specific survival, progression-free interval and disease-free interval. The expression data of the ULBP1 gene were employed in the construction of the ROC curve, with the area under the curve serving as a metric to assess its accuracy. Prognostic indicators, including hazard ratios (HRs), 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) and p values, were calculated under standard conditions. A result was considered significant when p < 0.05.

Immune microenvironment characterization linked to ULBP1

Correlation analysis was performed between ULBP1 gene expression and immune infiltration data in the pan-cancer dataset, with the results visualized using ggplot2 package. The R package “estimate” was utilized to calculate the stromal and immune scores for the corresponding cloud data. Based on the ssGSEA algorithm provided by the R package “GSVA”, immune infiltration levels were assessed using markers for 24 types of immune cell38. Additionally, immune infiltration levels were calculated using the algorithm of CIBERSORT, utilizing markers for 22 types of immune cells provided by the CIBERSORTx website (https://cibersortx.stanford.edu/). Statistical analysis was conducted using the Spearman method.

Analysis of tumor immune microenvironment features

The features of the tumor immune microenvironment were mainly reflected based on the expression levels of immunomodulators, including chemokine, MHC, receptor, immunoinhibitors, and immunostimulators obtained from TISIDB database (http://cis.hku.hk/TISIDB/). Analysis of the relationship between ULBP1, tumor mutational burden (TMB), microsatellite instability (MSI), and tumor stemness data were obtained from the TCGA database (downloaded from UCSC Xena). The correlation between the expression of ULBP1 and TMB, MSI was analyzed by Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient.

Analysis of gene methylation correlations

We utilized the University of Alabama at Birmingham Cancer Database (http://ualcan.path.uab.edu/) to investigate the DNA methylation status of the ULBP1 promoter39. The associations between ULBP1 and genes associated with m5C and m6A modifications were assessed through a heatmap analysis. The heatmap of DNA methylation at CpG s of ULBP1 gene was acquired from MethSurv database (https://biit.cs.ut.ee/methsurv/).

Protein–protein interaction network analysis and enrichment analysis of gene functions

The protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis was carried out with the help of the online STRING website (https://string-db.org/) for the sake of exploring the potential genes related to ULBP1. We conducted STRING to search 20 ULBP1-interacted genes and constructed PPI networks with a medium confidence (0.400). Pathway annotations were download from Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database40,41 (https://www.kegg.jp/kegg/kegg1.html). Gene ontology (GO) enrichment and KEGG pathway analyses of genes were performed by the “Cluster Profiler” package and visualized by the “ggplot2” package.

Cell lines culture

The HNSCC cell lines Fadu and Cal-27 were acquired from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA). Tu686 and NK-92 cell line were purchased from Keycell Biotechnology (Wuhan, China). The HPV status of the Tu686 cell line has not been definitively reported. Cal-27 and Fadu cell lines are HPV-negative. The cells were authenticated by short tandem repeat identification (STR) analysis. Fadu and Cal-27 were cultured in DMEM basal medium and Tu686 were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (P/S) solution. NK-92 were cultured in MEMα + 0.2mM Inositol + 0.1mM β-mercaptoethanol + 0.02mM folic acid + 100-200U/mL recombinant IL-2 + 12.5% HS + 12.5% FBS + 1% P/S. The cell culture environment was set at 37 °C 5% CO2, in appropriate humidity.

RNA interference and virus infection

SiRNAs and lentivirus were construct by GenePharma (Suzhou, China); siRNAs were transfected using Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. The transfection efficiency was verified by Western blot and qRT‑PCR at 48 h post-transfection. The siRNA sequences of ULBP1 were provided in Supplementary Table S2. Recombinant lentivirus for ULBP1 overexpression (oe-ULBP1) and lentiviral vector for negative control (oe-NC) were used to infect HNSCC cells, along with polybrene. Moreover, 1 µg/mL of puromycin was incubated for 48 h to screen the infected cells.

Quantitative real time PCR (qRT-PCR)

The total RNA of the three groups of Fadu, Tu686 and Cal-27 cells was extracted and synthesized with reverse transcription kit to obtain cDNA (PrimeScript RT reagent Kit, RR047A, TaKaRa). ULBP1 mRNA expression was detected by real-time quantitative RCR kit (SYBR® Premix Ex Taq™ II, RR820A, TaKaRa), in which ACTIN was used as an internal reference gene. The sequences for primers were provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Western blotting

HNSCC cells were lysed using RIPA lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors. Proteins were separated on a 12% SDS-PAGE gel and transferred onto a methanol-activated PVDF membrane. The membrane was blocked with a quick blocking solution at room temperature for 15 min and then incubated with the primary antibody at 4 °C overnight. Subsequently, the PVDF membrane was incubated with an HRP-labeled Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG secondary antibody at room temperature for 1h. Protein signals were visualized using ECL chemiluminescence reagent and detected with the Amersham Imager 600 system. The information of primary antibody was provided in Supplementary Table S4. Original blots are presented in Supplementary Figs. S9, S10 and S11.

Immunohistochemical (IHC)

3–5μm thick paraffin sections of HNSCC tissues were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated in graded alcohols. Following deparaffinization in xylene and rehydration through a graded alcohol series, endogenous peroxidases were quenched with 0.3% H2O2. Antigen retrieval was then carried out using EDTA buffer. The sections were blocked with 10% goat serum for 1 h at 37 °C and subsequently incubated with primary antibodies (listed in Supplementary Table 4) at 4 °C overnight. After washing with PBST for three times, sections were incubated with corresponding secondary antibodies. Immunostaining was performed using an EnVision Detection Rabbit/Mouse Kit.

Cell proliferation assay

Fadu cells were plated in 96-well plates at 3 × 103 cells/well and cultured in DMEM containing 5% FBS. The number of metabolically active cells was determined using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Solarbio, China). The absorbance at OD450 was recorded using a microplate reader.

Wound healing assay

TU686 cells infected with SiRNAs or lentivirus are seeded in a 6-well plate and grown to 90% confluence. A scratch is created using a pipette tip to simulate a wound. After removing debris, cells are incubated with serum-free medium. Images are captured at 0 and 24 h to monitor wound closure, followed by analysis of the wound area.

Cell Invasion assay

In vitro migration and invasion assays were performed using transwell chambers (Corning, USA) with Matrigel. 5 × 104 Fadu cells or Cal-27 cells in serum-free DMEM were seeded in the upper chamber, and 500 μl DMEM supplemented with 10%FBS was added into the lower chamber. After incubation for 48–72 h at 37 °C, the invaded cells were fixed and stained. Five fields of cells were photographed and counted to estimate cell density. Image-Pro Plus 6.0 was used to measure the stained area.

Colony formation assay

TU686 cells were inoculated with 2000 cells in 6-well plates. The next day, they were incubated with 1 × 104 NK-92 cells or the corresponding medium for 6 h. After removing the NK cells, the TU686 cells were cultured for an additional 10 days. The cells were rinsed were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and subsequently stained with 0.1% crystal violet. They were then washed using ddH2O, dried, and photographed. The colonies were counted using ImageJ software.

Apoptosis assay

Tu686 and Cal27 cell lines were seeded in a 6-well plate at a density of 1 × 106 cells and then incubated for 24 h. They were incubated with 5 × 106 NK-92 cells or the corresponding medium for 8 h. After removing the NK cells, the TU686 cells were cultured for 12 h and then collected in flow-through tubes and washed with PBS buffer; they were stained with 5 µL of Annexin V-APC (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Then, the apoptosis rate of the cells was detected by flow cytometry to evaluate the cytotoxicity of NK-92 cells against TU686 cells. The results were acquired on the MACS flow cytometer and analyzed using FlowJo software.

Animals

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee at the Bethune International Peace Hospital (approved number: 2024-KY-69). All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. All methods are reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines. A total of 24 male BALB/c nude mice were purchased from Beijing Sibeifu Biotechnology Co., Ltd. After a one-week acclimatization period, the mice were randomly assigned to the ULBP1 overexpression group (oe-ULBP1), ULBP1 knockdown group (shULBP1), and negative control groups (oe-NC, shNC) using a random number table method, with 6 mice per group. 2 × 106 Fadu cells infected with ULBP1- overexpressing lentivirus, ULBP1- knockdown lentivirus, or control lentivirus were subcutaneously injected into the flank of BALB/c nude mice. The tumor volume was measured every 6 days; tumor volume was determined using the formula: volume = (length × width2)/2. The growth curves were plotted based on the gathered data. The mice were weighed, and the excised tumors were weighed and collected for further analysis.

Patients and clinical specimens

In this study, all patients were diagnosed with HNSCC by postoperative pathology, and HNSCC tissues were collected from the Bethune International Peace Hospital. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. All experimental protocols were approved by the Ethics Committee of Bethune International Peace Hospital (approved number: 2024-KY-69). Informed consent was obtained from all subjects.

Statistical analysis

The differences in ULBP1 expression levels in tumor tissues and normal tissues were evaluated by Wilcoxon rank sum test. In survival analysis, the Kaplan–Meier method was used to visualize survival curves, and the differences between groups stratified by ULBP1 expression levels were assessed using the Log-rank test. Additionally, univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was employed to calculate Hazard ratios (HRs) with corresponding p-values. The correlation of gene expression was evaluated using Spearman’s correlation analysis. Prior to statistical analysis, normality of the expression data was assessed. For data with equal variances and normal distribution, Student’s t-test was used. For data without normal distribution or equal variances, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied. Results are shown as mean ± SEM from at least three independent experiments. p < 0.05 was set as the significance threshold for all statistical analyses.
