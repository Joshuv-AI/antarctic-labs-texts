Human stem cell models to unravel brain cancer
Authors: Biren Dave, Jignesh Tailor
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPre-clinical animal models of human brain tumors have been invaluable tools for studying cancer pathogenesis and exploring novel treatment modalities. Such models recapitulate important aspects of the human disease such as the stem-progenitor-differentiated cell hierarchy. Although powerful, we argue that animal models are inherently limited in their ability to phenocopy certain important aspects of human brain tumor biology. We specifically highlight the inability of mouse models to generate certain forms aggressive pediatric medulloblastoma likely owing to cellular, anatomic, and genetic differences between the human and mouse brains. Additionally, we review some limitations of human brain tumor derived cell lines and outline why they are a sub-optimal system for purposes of pre-clinical modeling. Below, we present the case for human stem cell-based models of brain tumors, focusing mainly on glioblastoma and medulloblastoma. Drawing on several recently published studies, we review the exciting progress that has been made towards modeling human brain tumors using two-dimensional adherent stem cell cultures and three-dimensional organoids. We identify the important advances arrived at using these human stem cell-based models and suggest opportunities for future work in this direction. In this review article, we aim to highlight the utility and promises of human stem cell-based models of brain tumors as a complementary system to traditional transgenic animal and cell line systems.Keywords: Human, Brain tumor, Stem cell, Model systems

Background

It has been over a decade since the genomic atlas of a brain tumor was published [1, 2]. Since then, there has been an explosion of genomic data from next generation sequencing that has changed the way we look at brain cancer [3–6]. We have learnt that brain cancer types, such as glioblastoma (GBM), medulloblastoma (MB) or ependymoma are not single entities but are heterogeneous and are made up of different biological subtypes with different clinical trajectories [7–9]. The genetic make-up of the tumor has a large influence on why some patients do well following tumor resection, and others relapse. Genomics has provided opportunity for accurate diagnosis and precise risk stratification, so that treatment plans can be tailored to the individual’s risk profile [10]. Importantly, it has also provided opportunity to create more accurate pre-clinical models, either with genetically engineered mice and/or with human stem cells.

With the explosion of knowledge on the genetic landscape of brain tumors, it is probably now more important than ever to invest in humanized pre-clinical models. To make progress in drug development, we need to understand the functional consequences of gene mutations and their downstream growth-promoting pathways within the cell in a human context. Whilst brain tumors may harbor half a dozen or so driver gene mutations, there may be hundreds of other passenger mutations that are already present in a cell before it turns malignant [3, 11, 12]. It is difficult to know for certain who the key players are in the initiation and propagation of these tumors without functional data. In addition, tumor suppressor gene-inactivating mutations usually predominate over oncogene-activating mutations in brain tumors [3]. This provides difficulty for drug targeting, since drugs generally interfere with protein function but do not, in general, replace the function of defective genes resulting from mutations in tumor suppressor genes. The greater knowledge of players that are activated downstream of tumor suppressor genes and the ways in which they function is a pressing need in basic cancer research.

Pre-clinical models also provide innovative insight into brain cancer at the cellular and organ level. For example, the stem cell hierarchy within tumors, the role of differentiation failure, the cellular origins, differences between quiescent and proliferating cells, the role of the microenvironment in promoting tumorigenesis and the mechanisms of brain tumor metastasis. These broader perspectives are fundamental to understanding how brain tumors operate, and how best to tackle them. Especially for more relentless tumors such as GBM or MB, we may only start to see improvement in overall survival if and when these different dimensions are addressed simultaneously.

With recent advances in stem cell technology, there has been an expansion of human stem cell models of brain tumors, including fresh approaches to engineering brain tumors using pluripotent and neural stem cells, both in organoid cultures and following xenotransplantation in mice (Fig. 1). Here we discuss the current approaches to modelling brain cancers. While brain cancers comprise a broad set of disease entities, we focus on GBM and MB, as these represent the most common adult and pediatric malignant brain tumor types, respectively. We examine human stem cell-based modeling approaches and discuss the biological questions that are being addressed using these state-of-the-art tools. We do not try to provide an exhaustive review of brain cancer biology. Instead, we aim to present examples of the current human stem cell-based approaches and discuss their advantages and limitations, alongside those of transgenic mouse models, xenograft models and tumor derived cell lines. We discuss how human stem cell-based modeling approaches may complement studies in genetically engineered mouse models to address fundamental questions in tumor biology.

What have we learnt from transgenic mice?

Studies in transgenic mice have been the cornerstone of tumor biology for many years. This is primarily due to their ease of genetic manipulation, short breeding times, and shared organ systems and physiology. The earliest mouse models were based on familial cancer syndromes such as Gorlin syndrome (Ptch1+/−)13 and Li-Fraumeni syndrome (Trp53+/−) [14]. Mice with heterozygous loss of the SHH receptor Ptch1 became the archetypal model for SHH medulloblastoma. In this model, MB was found to arise from the external granule layer of the developing cerebellum [15], and from overactivity of SHH signaling in granule cells [16, 17]. Approximately 20% of Ptch1+/− mice develop MB [13], but this increases to 80% if the pups are irradiated at postnatal day (P) 0 [18], suggesting that brain tumor initiation probably requires co-operating genetic or epigenetic events induced by the environment in addition to genetic predisposition. Mice are useful for understanding the relationship between cell of mutation and cell of origin. Engineering conditional driver mutations (either Ptch1 loss or activation of its downstream effector Smoothened (Smo) at different points of the granule neuron lineage results in convergence of the MB phenotype, suggesting that a pre-neoplastic cell (‘cell of mutation’) still must progress into a certain developmental state (‘cell of origin’) before transformation occurs [19, 20]. Similarly, studies in Trp53 heterozygous mice with conditional Nf1 knockout has revealed that GBM can be initiated from neural progenitor populations [21] and that more differentiated neuronal populations were more resistant to transformation [22]. Using this fully penetrant mouse model and combining it with various Cre drivers, this group has been able to explore, using elegant mouse genetics, the candidate cell of origin for GBM [22–24], and the importance of the quiescent GBM stem cell-like population in driving relapse [25].

Why we can’t just rely on mice

Although the human and mice share similar organ systems, the human brain is probably the one organ that most drastically differs from mouse. The human brain has a striking anatomical diversity that is not matched by the mouse. The human cortex has a secondary germinal zone (outer subventricular zone (oSVZ)) that does not exist in mouse [26–29], and unique neuron subtypes (e.g. rose hip neuron) [30]. Although cerebellar development has long been considered to be similar between mice and humans, recent studies have challenged this dogma [31, 32]. Specifically, the germinal zone for all cerebellar glutamatergic neurons, known as the rhombic lip, contains a human specific sub-ventricular compartment not present in mice [31]. Exciting new work has shown that progenitor cells from this compartment represent the cellular origins of group 3 and 4 medulloblastoma, providing an elegant explanation of why these cancers have proved notoriously difficult to model in transgenic mice [33, 34]. Additionally, although the rhombic lip has disappeared by birth in mice, it persists through the first two years of human life, broadening the temporal window during which genetic and/or environmental insults may hijack stem and progenitor cells within this germinal zone to seed tumorigenesis. In addition, the mouse brain has large areas fueled by neural stem cell activity but dedicated to functions (e.g. olfaction) that are much more rudimentary in humans. These anatomic and temporal differences suggest that testing genetic changes in mouse brain, in some instances, will have very different developmental and functional consequences from human. Furthermore, fundamental differences in human and mouse cells can be found in cancer susceptibility to carcinogens and genetic mutation, capacity to be immortalized, telomere maintenance, and responses to various injuries. For a more comprehensive discussion of this topic, we direct readers to a review paper by Rangarajan and Weinberg [35].

Recent genomic and transcriptomic comparisons of medulloblastoma models derived in genetically engineered mice have displayed fundamental differences with their human counterparts. Mice with Ptch1 or Smo mutations selectively resembled SHH medulloblastoma for adulthood, although these mutations are found at all ages [36]. None of the mouse models accurately resemble infantile SHH subtype medulloblastoma. Moreover, there are additional mutations that are repeatedly found in adult SHH subtype medulloblastoma, such as DDX3X (41%), CREBBP (14%) and MLL2 (14%) that are never seen in the murine counterparts [36]. These findings suggest there are additional players other than genetic make-up that are important in the formation of human medulloblastoma, which may not be accurately modelled in mice, such as the cell of origin and/or the evolution of secondary hits within the tumor.

Another critical issue also lies with differences in chromosomal organization. Malignant brain tumors such as GBM are usually characterized by amplifications and deletions of large chromosomal regions. Due to differences in chromosomal synteny, experimental mouse tumors will not share the same cooperating structural events as human cells [35]. So, for example, losses in whole chromosome 10 in human GBM, which is classic in about 50% of patients, would require alterations in matching DNA material from 5 different mouse chromosomes to recapitulate the human mechanisms, which is very unlikely. This issue demands that cell of origin for human brain tumours must also be studied in a human context.

Xenograft brain tumor models

Another widely used approach to model brain tumor biology has been the xenograft method, first described by Kaye et al., in 1986 [37]. This method involves engraftment of human brain tumor cells into the brain of an immunocompromised host animal, often mouse. Xenograft approaches are well suited to studying cell-autonomous mechanisms of human brain tumor growth, because they are not hampered by issues of interspecies cellular and genetic differences as transgenic models are. Therefore, xenograft experiments remain powerful techniques for estimating cancer stem cell frequency and functionally demonstrating cancer stem cell properties [37, 38]. Another strength of the xenograft approach may lie in its ability to prevent the phenotypic drift of tumor cells from their parental tumors, as observed in some instances when brain tumour cells are propagating in vitro [39, 40]. Furthermore, xenografting human tumor initiating cells into the neonatal mouse brain has been shown to be useful in modeling pediatric tumors such as MB [41]. However, outside of these use-cases, xenograft models are limited by their inability to non-cell autonomous (i.e. microenvironmental) mechanisms of tumor growth. As described above, the xenograft assay necessitates use of immunocompromised host animals, most commonly nude or NOD-SCID mice. Nude mice lack mature T lymphocytes [42], while NOD-SCID mice lack mature B and T lymphocytes, and have impaired NK cells [43, 44]. Considering the important functions of these cell types in host anti-cancer defence, and more specifically in the brain tumor microenvironment [45], their absence may alter tumor growth kinetics. Additionally, the process of cell engraftment causes a stab wound at the injection site. Such wounds are known to promote local inflammatory reactions which may also alter brain tumor growth dynamics, and therefore need to be taken into consideration when designing experiments and interpreting results [46]. For these reasons, parallel human systems to complement mouse systems are required to have access to critical factors that may be unique to human brain tumorigenesis.

‘Traditional’ brain tumor cell lines

In vitro cell lines derived from human brain tumors provide a tractable, cost-effective human model system to interrogate the intrinsic operations within a cancer cell. Cell lines derived from brain tumors can be easily manipulated by genetic or chemical approaches and massively expanded for drug screening, transcriptomic or proteomic profiling. There is an abundance of in vitro cell lines to choose from, and it is important for the researcher to understand the differences between them. There is a choice between ‘traditional’ cell lines versus newer models that are propagated in neural stem cell culture conditions. The ‘traditional’ models such as DOAY, D283 and ONS-76 derived from medulloblastoma, or the U87MG, U251 and T98G cell lines from glioblastoma are grown in serum-supplemented media. In serum, neural stem cells differentiate away from their parental state into astrocytes [47]. This effect is also seen in brain tumor stem cells, whereby the cells are forced into a transcriptional and epigenetic program that no longer reflects the neural stem/progenitor pathways that underlie glioblastoma stem cells (GSCs) [48]. Moreover, cells may no longer depend on the signaling pathways that promote their growth and tumorigenicity. For example, cells isolated from human SHH-MB downregulated the SHH pathway when cultured in serum supplemented conditions, indicating that their growth was no longer dependent on SHH activity [40]. This suggests profound phenotypic divergence from parental tumors, which has major implications when using these cells in pre-clinical drug testing or in vivo tumor modelling.

Brain cancer stem cells

More recent cell lines derived from brain cancers have used stem cell culture systems to propagate brain cancer cells in vitro in a similar fashion to in vitro fetal neural stem cells (NSC). These systems originated from studies in rodents in the 1990s that showed primary NSCs from fetal brain and adult SVZ could be expanded in suspension culture, as neurospheres, in the absence of serum and in the presence of basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF) [49–53].

In the early 2000s, researchers began dissecting the cellular hierarchy of brain tumors. Peter Dirks’ lab and others found that similar cells with neural stem cell-like properties could be isolated in these suspension cultures from both glioblastoma and medulloblastoma [54–57]. Brain cancer stem cells could be maintained as neurospheres in medium containing epidermal growth factor (EGF) and bFGF. In the absence of growth factors, they would differentiate into neurons and glia. Serial transplantation of the cells in xenograft assay led to recapitulation of the parental brain tumor, providing evidence of self-renewal in the brain tumor initiating fraction of cells [54]. These findings suggested that brain cancers such as glioblastoma consist of a hierarchy of cells with corrupted neural stem cells at the apex of the hierarchy fueling the rest of the tumor through self-renewal and differentiation. Moreover, the cancer stem cell component appears to be most resistant to therapy [58, 59], and may promote angiogenesis through vascular endothelial growth factor [60]. Cancer stem cells provided a platform to interrogate intrinsic mechanisms such as self-renewal, differentiation failure and quiescence that may underpin the growth of brain tumors. Important aspects of tumor biology that we couldn’t reliably tackle with more traditionally used tumor cell lines.

Suspension vs. adherent systems

Growth in suspension culture systems is not a requirement for neural stem cells [61, 62] and in fact, may have inherent limitations [63, 64]. Not all cells within a neurosphere are equally exposed to growth factors in the culture media, which may cause spontaneous differentiation and loss of self-renewal [65, 66]. Working with cells in adherent monolayer cultures may offer experimental advantages, particularly with regards to culture homogeneity, imaging approaches, clonal propagation, transfection, drug screening and cell quantitation [67]. The field has generally moved away from using neurosphere culture systems for the propagation of NSCs for these reasons. Cancer stem cells derived from GBM in adherent monolayer are more homogeneous when cultured side-by-side with cells in suspension and accurately recapitulate parental tumor biology [68]. Highly efficient derivation of GBM stem cell lines can be achieved on adherent monolayer systems, with > 90% success for IDH wild-type GBM [66, 69].

Questions we can’t address with brain cancer stem cells

Cancer stem cells have allowed us to address fundamental questions in tumor biology, including cell intrinsic mechanisms that drive tumorgenicity [70, 71], self-renewal [72, 73], and the origins of intra-tumoral functional heterogeneity [74]. It has provided strong evidence for a hierarchical model for glioblastoma growth that is rooted in corrupted stem cells with neural progenitor/stem-like properties. Yet, there are some drawbacks of this approach. Firstly, stem cell lines cannot be derived from all malignant brain tumors. For example, although stem cell populations are seen in medulloblastoma [54, 75], the culture conditions for long-term self-renewal in vitro have still not been optimized. Most MB cultures cannot be passaged in vitro for more than 3–5 passages and this is a major limitation in the field. The reasons for this are unclear. One possibility is that bFGF, which is an important organizing signal secreted at the midbrain-hindbrain boundary suppresses the proliferation of SHH-dependent granule cells and medulloblastoma cells that rely on aberrant SHH-signaling for growth [76–78]. Perhaps other mitogens or elements of the tumor microenvironment are needed to capture MB cells in a long-term self-renewing state in vitro that are to date unknown.

Although cancer stem cells bear resemblance to neural stem cells, these cells are derived from the tumor endpoint. Thus, it is difficult to infer the precise mechanisms of tumor initiation and co-operative events leading to transformation of the normal cell, or its true cell-of-origin. These fundamental questions are probably better studied by engineering tumors in putative cells of origin, such as forebrain radial-glia like neural stem cells for glioblastoma, or hindbrain neuroepithelial stem cell for medulloblastoma. These genetically engineered human stem cell models can be studied both in organoid cultures or in xenograft models, which have their own unique pros and cons.

Modelling brain tumors with organoids

Organoid generation relies on the remarkable ability of stem and progenitor cells to self-organize into complex tissue structures. In 2013, Lancaster et al., showed that pluripotent stem cells can be directed towards the neural lineage in a 3D Matrigel matrix and that the progenitors would self-organize into structures that were architecturally similar to the human brain [79]. These structures, known as cerebral organoids, contain areas resembling diverse areas of the human brain [79–81], and also recapitulate the development of human specific cell types such as the outer ventricular zone radial-glial cells [82]. In similar 3D culture systems, embryonic stem cells have also been directed towards self-organizing cerebellar tissue [83, 84]. For a more comprehensive discussion on the generation of cerebral organoids and their potential applications in modeling human neurodevelopment and disease, we direct readers to the cited review papers [85–87]. Some labs have explored the possibility of using these structures to mimic the process of brain tumor initiation, by transfection of CRISPR-Cas9 constructs targeting GBM driver mutations in brain organoids [88, 89]. Others have shown that GBM cells from patient tumors can self-organize in Matrigel matrix to form brain tumor organoids [90, 91]. This allows the modelling of hypoxic gradients and within a human tumor which cannot be obtained in monolayer culture systems. Moreover, such models may allow the integration of non-cell intrinsic components of tumor growth, such as the interaction with immune and endothelial cells or other factors in the tumor microenvironment [92]. Exciting recent progress has been made to generate cerebral organoids containing microglia [93, 94] and vascular structures [95, 96], and efforts are surely underway to model brain tumors in these cutting-edge systems are surely underway. However, generation of cerebral organoids is time consuming and difficult to scale. Further, inter-organoid heterogeneity may affect reproducibility of experimental findings. Cerebral organoids are emerging as powerful models to study both cell autonomous and microenvironmental determinants of human brain tumorigenesis. As these models continue to be improved and refined, they will surely be important tools in furthering our understanding of tumor biology and serving as pre-clinical platforms for drug development.

Engineering brain tumors from normal stem cells

Instead of focusing on end-stage brain cancer, several groups including ours are now taking an alternative approach to understanding the pathogenesis of brain cancer by starting from a normal human stem cell population that are putative cells of origin. This approach has become popular due to the recent development of more accurate human neural stem cell models that retain regional and stage-specific properties of specific progenitor populations in the developing brain [62, 97], and the genetic tractability of these cells by methods such as CRISPR-Cas9. Stem cell-based approaches are well suited to studying pediatric brain tumors that likely arise because of aberrant developmental processes [98]. Interestingly, recent work suggests that GBMs, predominantly an adult tumor, may also arise from developmentally immature cell types that resemble fetal neural stem cell populations [99]. Therefore, stem cell-based approaches may be amenable to modeling both pediatric and adult tumor types.

Medulloblastoma is thought to arise when cerebellar precursors derived from rhombomere 1 deviate away from their normal developmental trajectories [17]. Progenitors from human rhombomere 1 can be captured in vitro as neuroepithelial stem (NES) cells and expanded long term in culture as self-renewing stem cells [97]. These cellular models, which are very similar to lt-NES cells derived from pluripotent cells in vitro [100, 101], retain specific properties of the rostral hindbrain neuroepithelium. They retain hindbrain identity markers GBX2, EN2, KROX20 and rostral HOX genes, and responsiveness to extrinsic developmental cues. For example, BMPs will induce the expression of the rhombic-lip marker ATOH1, and subsequent differentiation towards rhombic lip derivatives, including cerebellar granule cells [97, 102]. In this way, NES cells, and perhaps their immature precursor-cell dependents, represent likely cells of origin for medulloblastoma that can be genetically manipulated in culture.

As a proof of principle, NES cells were derived in Austin Smith’s lab from induced pluripotent stem (iPS) cells generated from patients with Gorlin syndrome [103]. These cells harboring germline mutations in PTCH1+/− showed normal growth and differentiation dynamics in culture. However, orthotopic transplantation of these cells into the mouse brain led to the development of pre-neoplastic lesions after 20 weeks. The introduction of a second hit, such as the expression of dominant-negative TP53 led to full blown cancers with histology that represented medulloblastoma. These initial observations set the stage for the testing of novel candidate driver mutations in NES cells. Overexpression of N-MYC reliably leads to SHH subgroup MB from NES cells with transcriptomic profiles that are more reminiscent of the patient tumor than SHH-MB generated in the Ptch1+/− mouse model [103]. Moreover, the precise editing of novel gene mutations found in human MB such as DDX3X or GSE1 has been shown to accelerate tumorigenesis following xenotransplantation of these cells.

This relatively simple model provides an important look into previously inaccessible aspects of the human disease, the early events, using a functional approach. By studying the evolution of genetically engineered neural stem cell populations, and with comparison to data on patient tumors, one can now probe into the molecular processes that kick-start brain cancers. A similar approach has been taken to engineer DIPG and ATRT from embryonic stem cell derived progenitors [104, 105] CRISPR-Cas9 editing methods are also being applied to engineer multiplex inducible GBM drivers in radial glia like NS cells [47].

These approaches may help to identify the core machinery that drives the malignant state and allow the discovery of new therapeutic targets (Fig. 2). Knowing the stages of brain cancer evolution from normal to malignancy would allow the application of small molecule screens to identify agents that inhibit proliferation of early and late transformed cells, probing whether full blown transformation could be inhibited, thus, setting the stage for prevention of full-blown tumors if and when diagnostic technologies identify early lesions.

Future directions

Brain cancer models are constantly evolving as new biological questions emerge. The future will see the advent of more complex and creative model systems. One of the major gaps in our understanding of brain cancer is its interaction with the host microenvironment, including the human immune system. Current xenograft models involve transplantation of tumor initiating cells in immunosuppressed mice which do not recapitulate the same cellular microenvironment as the human brain. Mice models with humanized immune systems are being developed [106]. Furthermore, differentiation protocols from human embryonic stem (ES) or iPS cells are continuously improving. In future, it may be possible to study the direct interaction between tumor cells and human microglia, macrophages, or endothelial cells either in co-culture in vitro systems, tumor organoid systems or xenograft models following orthotopic transplantation of humanized cellular cocktails.

In addition, there are many unanswered questions on how brain tumors are initiated. Is the cellular context of the neural progenitor relevant for mutation permissiveness? How does a pre-disposing factor within a cell cooperate with driver mutations to initiate full-blown tumors? How might non-cell autonomous factors such as mutations in immune cells promote tumorigenesis? There is now growing evidence that pediatric brain cancer is initiated in the developing brain in utero. For example, medulloblastoma subgroups share genetic signatures with distinct progenitor populations in the mouse cerebellum [107]. It will be important to test this functionally through the advent of cerebellar progenitor populations from human pluripotent stem cells or hindbrain neuroepithelial stem cells engineered with reporter constructs and inducible driver mutations.

On the clinical front, oncology is entering an era of precision medicine whereby treatment is individualized based on specific gene alterations within the tumor [108]. Modelling brain cancers with human stem cells has the potential to enhance precision medicine paradigms (Fig. 2). As our ability to recapitulate brain tumors in the laboratory improves, it may be possible to replicate tumors with the precise genetic make-up seen in the individual. This may allow personalized pre-clinical drug screening to enrich treatment options in case targeted drug therapy were to fail, or, if the tumor were to relapse.

Conclusions

Malignant brain tumors are driven by multiple errors in developmental or cancer signaling pathways in the context of neural stem or progenitor states. Capturing these human stem cell or progenitor states in culture has created a powerful hierarchical model of brain cancer growth, which has allowed us to address fundamental questions concerning stemness, quiescence and differentiation failure, amongst others. Advances in the isolation and maintenance of specific neural stem cell populations from iPS cells and human fetal brain have now opened up fresh approaches to brain cancer, looking at the early events of tumorigenesis and clonal evolution in both organoid and xenograft systems. There is no perfect model, but some approaches are better suited to answer certain biological questions. The emergence of unique human stem cell systems for functional assays is complementary to current mammalian models and together, may provide the next wave of discoveries, molecular targets and therapeutic strategies. The brain cancer community is on fertile ground for cross-collaboration and should collectively embrace these new human-based tools to help unravel brain cancer.
