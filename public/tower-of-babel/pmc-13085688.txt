Baseline alignment and resuscitation exposure in hemodynamic phenotyping of septic shock
Authors: Yiqi Qian, Jincheng Zhang, Jialin Chen, Wei Wu
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with interest the study by Ko et al. on vasopressor timing and hemodynamic phenotyping in septic shock [1]. The study addresses an important clinical question. However, two methodological issues may affect how the results are interpreted.

First, the timing of phenotype assignment is not fully clear. The abstract states that patients were stratified by “pre-vasopressor diastolic blood pressure (DBP).” In the Methods, however, blood pressure was recorded at time zero, defined as emergency department (ED) triage, and time-to-vasopressor was measured from triage to norepinephrine initiation. The study also excluded patients without DBP measurements before vasopressor administration. It therefore remains unclear whether phenotyping was based on triage DBP or on the last DBP measured before norepinephrine initiation. This matters because, in patients who deteriorated later during the ED stay, triage DBP may not reflect the hemodynamic state at the time vasopressors were started [2].

The baseline data support this concern. In the preserved DBP group, the median triage mean arterial pressure (MAP) was 83.4-83.9 mmHg and the median triage systolic blood pressure (SBP) was 112.6-112.8 mmHg (Table 1). These values suggest that many patients in this group were unlikely to need norepinephrine immediately at triage. The sensitivity analysis restricted to patients with MAP < 65 mmHg at time zero is helpful, but it does not resolve the main issue that triage may still precede shock onset. In Supplementary Table 3, the preserved DBP subgroup becomes much smaller under this restriction (n = 98) than in the overall cohort (n = 889). This suggests that, for many patients with preserved DBP, triage did not coincide with overt hypotension and may not have been the most relevant time point for phenotype assignment.

Second, early resuscitation was not captured in a way that fully matched the exposure used in this study. The adjusted analyses included completion of the 3-hour Surviving Sepsis Campaign bundle, but this is not the same as measuring fluid exposure before norepinephrine initiation [3]. Because this process measure extends well beyond the 25-minute threshold, it may reflect later or overlapping care rather than the actual amount and timing of pre-vasopressor fluids. This is relevant because DBP and pulse pressure (PP) are sensitive to volume status, and the meaning of a given DBP-PP profile may change after fluid administration. Baseline arterial stiffness may also affect DBP and PP, particularly in an older cohort, and may further complicate interpretation [4]. More precise reporting of pre-vasopressor resuscitation would therefore help clarify whether the observed associations relate to vasopressor timing itself or to differences in early resuscitation.

We also noted that the subgroup description in the text does not fully match Fig. 2. The direction of effect for age appears opposite to the wording in the Results, and the figure presents Charlson comorbidity index rather than chronic kidney disease. Since the interaction tests are not significant, these subgroup findings may be better described as exploratory.

The reported 25-minute threshold was derived within this cohort and should be viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than as a general clinical decision threshold.

Clarification of whether phenotyping was based on triage DBP or on measurements obtained immediately before norepinephrine initiation, together with more precise reporting of pre-vasopressor resuscitation, would help readers interpret the current findings. More broadly, future studies should align phenotype measurement, shock onset, and treatment timing to the same clinical decision point [5].
