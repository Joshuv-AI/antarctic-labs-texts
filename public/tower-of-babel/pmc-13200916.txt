Sequential Paramedian Forehead Flaps for Nasal Salvage Reconstruction After Failure of a Folded Microvascular Forearm Flap
Authors: Khaled Al Tabaa, Tom Cosset, Esthelle Ngom Minka, Benoît Faucon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary:Subtotal nasal amputation poses significant reconstructive challenges, particularly in complex traumatic composite defects. The gold-standard Burget–Menick technique combines a radial forearm free flap (RFFF) for lining, rib cartilage framework for structural support, and paramedian forehead flap (PFF) for coverage. Although single-stage RFFF–PFF combinations have reduced operative time, the literature lacks clear salvage protocols for microvascular flap failure. We present a 43-year-old patient with a history of smoking with near-total nasal amputation following human bite trauma, in whom initial reconstruction using a folded RFFF, costal cartilage grafts, and a left PFF was complicated by partial RFFF necrosis due to venous thrombosis. The salvage strategy used sequential bilateral PFFs: the original left PFF was thinned and converted to a vascularized hinged lining, whereas a contralateral right PFF provided external coverage after Doppler confirmation of supratrochlear artery patency. Rib cartilage grafts reconstructed the structural framework, including alar struts, spreader grafts, and a septal extension graft. Despite requiring a skin graft at the second PFF donor-site (tissue expansion declined by the patient), 1-year outcomes demonstrated satisfactory aesthetic and functional results, with patent airways, natural contour, and no donor-site morbidity. This case illustrates that sequential PFFs can effectively salvage failed microvascular nasal reconstruction while preserving the original reconstructive goals. Key technical considerations include preservation of a vascular base during PFF conversion, preoperative Doppler assessment of contralateral flap perfusion, and strategic use of hinged flaps to optimize lining. This novel sequential PFF approach offers a viable alternative when primary reconstruction fails, particularly when tissue expansion is not feasible.

On postoperative day 21, venous thrombosis led to partial necrosis of the RFFF, requiring debridement (Fig. 2). The necrosis involved the entire right-sided portion of the RFFF, which had been used for lining of the right lateral dorsum and hemi-tip.

Three weeks later, salvage reconstruction began. The viable left-sided RFFF was preserved (Figs. 2, 3). The key maneuver was designing a hinged flap from the right side of the original PFF. The PFF was divided; its right segment was turned over to reconstruct the missing internal lining of the right hemi-tip, at the expense of its external skin. The left PFF was elevated in full thickness. Its left-sided segment (not used for the hinge) was then thinned on its deep surface, along with the adjacent preserved RFFF.

The inset of the hinged flap is shown reconfiguring the right nostril, with the resulting external skin defect outlined. Coverage was provided by the contralateral right PFF.

A new costal cartilage framework was secured. After Doppler confirmation of right supratrochlear artery patency, a contralateral right PFF was harvested and inset to cover the resultant external defect. Primary forehead closure was not possible, requiring a skin graft (tissue expansion was declined). Nasal conformers were placed.

The second stage (3 wk later) involved division and insetting of both PFFs. At 6-month follow-up, the patient was satisfied with the aesthetic and functional result, with unimpaired social and professional activities and no donor-site morbidity (Supplemental Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E850).

DISCUSSION

Our case of subtotal nasal reconstruction using consecutive combined flaps demonstrated excellent aesthetic and functional outcomes with low donor-site morbidity. Although the double PFF technique is well described, our approach differed fundamentally from a preplanned bilateral procedure; it was an unplanned, sequential salvage requiring on-table redesign of available flaps without compromising vascularity, ensuring reconstruction of missing lining and cover. This technique has not previously been reported in such a salvage context. Farias and Moore,1 in a retrospective review of 7 patients, concluded that combining an ipsilateral pericranial flap with a contralateral PFF represents an effective strategy for reconstructing full-thickness defects involving multiple nasal subunits.

Among total reconstruction techniques, the Burget method—using a chimeric RFFF combined with a forehead flap—remains the gold standard.2 This approach provides both aesthetic contour and functional support, maintaining patent nasal airways without vestibular collapse.2 However, the procedure is time-consuming. In our case, we combined preliminary free tissue transfer for nasal lining with a single-stage PFF for external coverage to streamline the process. Yen et al3 reported a mean reconstruction time of only 2.2 months (without complications) in a 10-patient cohort. Unfortunately, we encountered partial necrosis of the microvascular flap.

Given the ample skin paddle of our initial PFF, principles of total nasal reconstruction by Menick were adapted.4 As demonstrated by Salibian et al,4 the RFFF skin paddle—rather than being fully excised during the second stage—can be hinged to reconstruct the nasal vestibules, preventing collapse. Following the required healing period, the resulting tissue contracture of the necrotic zone created a new, well-demarcated composite defect. We specifically applied the hinged-flap principle to our PFF to address internal lining loss in this contracted area. This maneuver yielded excellent results. However, use of skin for the hinge flap reduces the available external lining. This deficit, compounded by the tissue loss from contracture, must be accounted for when planning cartilage framework projection for the alae and tip. It is precisely this resultant external skin deficit that the contralateral PFF was designed to resurface.

We adhered to principles described by Menick5 by using the contralateral side as a template for shaping the secondary PFF. Thus, the salvage strategy involved a 2-step redesign: first, repurposing available tissue for lining in the contracted zone, and second, importing new tissue to replace the external cover that had been lost. Critical to this approach is preoperative Doppler assessment of the contralateral supratrochlear artery, which may be compromised during prior interventions.1,6,7

An alternative to avoid contralateral PFF donor-site skin grafting is forehead tissue expansion. Balaji8 (14-patient retrospective review) reported a mean expansion period of 32.14 ± 6.31 days. Although we proposed this option to optimize donor-site closure, the patient declined due to the prolonged timeline. Earlier initiation of expansion might have altered this outcome.

CONCLUSIONS

Subtotal nasal reconstruction remains particularly challenging when managing complications. When using a combined PFF–RFFF strategy, forehead tissue expansion should be considered to address donor-site deficits—especially after microvascular flap failure. For salvage, sequential contralateral PFF with ipsilateral hinged-flap modification offers a viable solution.
