Assessing the Fidelity of Depression Screening Implementation in Coronary Heart Disease Patients: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Chinelo Onyilofor, Baoyi Feng, Jungang Zou, Jennifer Mizhquiri Barbecho, Andrea T Duran, Siqin Ye, Othanya Garcia, Jessica Singer, Qixuan Chen, Nathalie Moise
Published: 2024

Guidelines recommend cardiologists screen coronary heart disease (CHD) patients for depression, which confers cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. 1 Few implementation science‐informed screening evaluations exist.

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. We conducted a cross‐sectional study of screening fidelity in integrated behavioral primary care (PC) and cardiology settings serving Medicaid patients in Upper Manhattan. During outpatient visits, the electronic health record (EHR) prompts bilingual medical assistants to administer the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ)‐2, which is read aloud in a open/nonprivate area, and if positive, a paper PHQ‐9. We conducted an EHR query for patients with CHD (International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision [ICD‐10] I21–I25, excluding severe mental illness/dementia) with ≥1 PC or cardiology visit between July 2018 to June 2019. Informed by a fidelity framework, 2 we assessed content (PHQ‐2 or PHQ‐9), coverage (% with ≥1 screen/year), and frequency (median screens/individual/year). We excluded social work/behavioral/mental health visits.

To measure quality, we prospectively enrolled patients with CHD from April 2019 to September 2019. Bilingual clinical staff called patients before upcoming PC/cardiology visits, obtained their consent for the preimplementation/nonintervention phase of a patient activation trial, and administered high‐fidelity PHQ‐9s (e.g., confidential, no paraphrasing, regular staff training/quality checks). We conducted structured observations (n=4 cardiology clinic affiliated PC clinics) and tracked quality of PHQ‐2/PHQ‐9 screening. We employed descriptive statistics, reporting count (percentage) for discrete and mean/median (interquartile range [IQR]) for continuous measures. Sensitivity analyses employed Fisher's exact to compare independent proportions and McNemar exact tests for paired proportions.

The Table compares demographics and results of real world versus high‐fidelity administered PHQs. Of the 1170 eligible patients with CHD, 867 (74.1%) were screened ≥1 time over 2115 visits (i.e., PC, cardiology/vascular, neurology); mean age 69.5 years (IQR: 62.0–77.0) and 49.5% female. In the most recent visit, 818 (94.4%) patients had standard PHQ‐2 = 0 and 22 (2.5%) had a positive PHQ‐2 (PHQ‐2 ≥ 3 or yes to ≥1 PHQ‐2 item), of whom 16 (76.2%) received a PHQ‐9 within the same visit. The mean number of screens/patient/year following a zero versus non‐zero initial PHQ‐2 was 2.43 (IQR: 1.0–3.0) versus 2.30 (IQR: 1.0–3.0). Of the 50 patients with a recent visit PHQ‐9 (not necessarily preceded by standard PHQ‐2 screens), the mean PHQ‐9 score was 11.5 (IQR: 7.0–15.8), and 31 patients (62.0%) had a PHQ‐9 ≥ 10.

Of the 171 high‐fidelity screens, 69 (40.4%) had a PHQ‐2 = 0, 46 (26.9%) a positive PHQ‐2, and 51 (29.8%) a PHQ‐9 ≥ 10. Our observations revealed that medical assistants routinely administer PHQ‐2s with vitals, but raters identified suboptimal screening quality (e.g., “Have you been feeling depressed?” prompts in lieu of validated questions), lack of privacy, and lack of standardization (e.g., PHQ‐2s yearly, every visit, or PHQ‐9 only depending on clinic; positive PHQ‐2s infrequently followed by PHQ‐9s). The percentage of PHQ‐2 = 0 was higher in the EHR versus high‐fidelity screens (94.5% versus 39.7%,P < 0.0001; n=73 patients in both cohorts) and similar over time (EHR screens 92.9% July 2018–March 2019 versus 95.5% April 2019–June 2019, P=0.106; high‐fidelity screens 37.9% April 2018–June 2019 versus 44.4% July 2019–September 2019, P=0.419).

We found high rates of false negative depression screens among patients with CHD seen in low socioeconomic status settings (94% had a PHQ‐2=0 whereas high‐fidelity screening identified nearly 30% with moderate/severe depressive symptoms). Our findings suggest that even when widely implemented, real‐world depression screening may see quality decays, particularly in historically marginalized populations with low‐English proficiency; this may contribute to missed opportunities to equitably deliver evidence‐based treatment (i.e., fewer opportunities to administer PHQ‐9s and identify depressive symptoms), prognosticate cardiac events, and uncover behavioral risk factors (e.g., nonadherence) in patients with CHD who experience some of the highest depression rates among medical conditions. 1

Our study suggests that suboptimal real world screening fidelity (perhaps due to low‐quality, nonconfidential screening) and overscreening (>2 screens/patient/year) are additional considerations for detractors of systematic screening concerned about resource allocation and lack of evidence base. 3 Patient portals may facilitate accurate depression screening but will need to overcome low use, mistrust, and stigma in low socioeconomic status settings. 4 Ongoing depression screening training/standardization may ensure accuracy and fidelity, and machine learning algorithms that identify true positive patients with CHD most likely to benefit from treatment should be explored.

A strength of our approach was our ability to distinguish low depression rates from quality gaps. Limitations include unmeasured temporal effects between high‐fidelity and EHR groups (though sensitivity analyses revealed consistent results, and high‐fidelity results mirrored observational screen positive rates of 15%–40% in cardiac populations 1 ); sampling bias (i.e., in‐person clinic versus consented phone screening), though the PHQ is validated for multiple modalities and both used bilingual clinical staff 5 ; inclusion of noncardiology/PC screens to assess overscreening; and inaccurate or missing ICD‐10, race, and ethnicity EHR variables.

Nonetheless, our results should prompt screening fidelity interventions in PC/cardiology settings, comparison of quality gaps between patients with CHD and general populations, and investigation of the impact of overscreening in populations with cardiac conditions who by virtue of multiple visits/comorbidities may be more likely to be overscreened.
