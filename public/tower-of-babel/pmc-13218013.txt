Female sexual function, urinary incontinence, and sexual position use 5 years after bariatric surgery: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Magdalena Sternau, Mateusz Czajkowski, Agata Błaczkowska, Monika Proczko-Stepaniak, Marcin Matuszewski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFemale sexual dysfunction and urinary incontinence are common among women with obesity. Bariatric surgery may improve sexual health, but long-term data are limited, and evidence on the use of sexual positions is lacking. We prospectively assessed female sexual function, urinary incontinence, and selected sexual positions before and 5 years after bariatric surgery.MethodsIn this single-centre prospective cohort study, women who underwent bariatric surgery completed the Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI) and the International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire–Urinary Incontinence Short Form (ICIQ-UI SF) preoperatively and at the 5-year follow-up. A study-specific questionnaire was used to assess the frequency and perceived pleasure of eight predefined coital positions. Paired comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank and exact McNemar tests. Predictors of changes in total FSFI (ΔFSFI) were examined using robust linear regression.ResultsSeventy women were enrolled in this study, and a total of 40 patients provided paired baseline and 5-year data. The mean age at the time of surgery was 39.8 years, and the mean body mass index (BMI) decreased from 39.1 to 30.7 kg/m². The proportion of patients who met the study definition of urinary incontinence decreased from 67.5% to 37.5% (p = 0.004). The total FSFI score increased modestly from 25.83 ± 7.96 to 27.16 ± 7.38, but the change was not statistically significant (p = 0.107), and no FSFI domain showed a significant within-subject change. In exploratory regression analyses, a lower baseline FSFI and a lower postoperative body mass index were associated with greater improvement in the FSFI. In the correlation analysis, urinary incontinence severity showed a negative association with the FSFI score, but did not reach statistical significance. Sexual position frequency and pleasure ratings were largely stable over time, with no significant changes in any position.ConclusionsFive years after bariatric surgery, overall FSFI scores showed only modest, nonsignificant improvement. Urinary incontinence improved, but the negative correlation with FSFI scores was not significant. Women with poorer baseline sexual function and lower postoperative body mass index appeared more likely to experience improvement. These findings should be interpreted in light of the modest sample size and the substantial loss to follow-up.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-026-04461-8.Keywords: Bariatric surgery, Obesity, Female, Sexual health, Sexual dysfunction, Urinary incontinence, Longitudinal studies

Introduction

Female sexual dysfunction (FSD) affects quality of life and relationship well-being. In population-based research, commonly reported difficulties include reduced sexual desire and orgasm-related problems [1]. Female sexual function is multifactorial and influenced by biological, psychological, interpersonal, and sociocultural factors [2, 3]. Consequently, sexual dysfunction may contribute to distress, impaired mental health, and relationship difficulties [4].

Obesity is a significant global health concern because it increases the risk of various diseases, including musculoskeletal disorders, metabolic conditions, and cardiovascular diseases [5]. It is also associated with a higher risk of several cancers and can have a profound impact on mental health [5]. The impact of obesity on sexual function remains a topic of ongoing debate. Although several studies contend that the relationship between body weight and sexual function is not conclusively established, it is posited that obesity, particularly when accompanied by comorbidities, may contribute to FSD [6, 7].

Bariatric surgery is an established intervention for the management of obesity. Several studies have examined this topic. Most published studies report improved sexual outcomes after bariatric surgery, particularly within the first postoperative year. However, long-term prospective data remain limited, and the results are not fully consistent across studies. It is important to highlight that most existing studies are characterised by a limited observation period, which constrains the potential for long-term evaluation [8–13]. In addition, evidence regarding changes in coital position use after bariatric surgery is very limited [14].

Therefore, we conducted a prospective cohort study to evaluate changes in female sexual function, measured using the FSFI urinary incontinence, measured using the ICIQ-UI SF and the frequency and perceived pleasure of eight predefined coital positions, comparing baseline assessments with outcomes at a 5-year follow-up after bariatric surgery.

Study design, setting, and ethics

This single-centre prospective cohort study was conducted at the Medical University of Gdańsk (Department of General, Endocrine, and Transplant Surgery and Department of Urology). Women were recruited between January 2018 and December 2019 and were reassessed 5 years after bariatric surgery (final follow-up completed in June 2025). At the 5-year follow-up, the questionnaires were self-administered in the presence of a study team member who provided clarification when needed. The study protocol was approved by the Bioethics Committee for Scientific Research at the Medical University of Gdańsk on 8 December 2017 (NKBBN/521/2017), and an extension was granted on 9 December 2022 (NKBBN/792/2022). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent.

Participants

The inclusion criteria were: women aged ≥ 18 years who met eligibility criteria for bariatric surgery and provided written informed consent [15]. The exclusion criteria were pharmacological or surgical treatment of urinary incontinence (UI) before or after bariatric surgery, neurological diseases affecting lower urinary tract function, pregnancy during the observation period, and reoperations.

Clinical data and surgical procedures

At preoperative admission, a standardised medical interview was performed, and height and weight were measured to calculate the body mass index (BMI). The choice of surgical procedure (laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy (LSG), one-anastomosis gastric bypass (OAGB), or Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB)) was made by the operating surgeon based on clinical indications and routine practice.

Outcomes and questionnaires

Urinary incontinence was assessed using the International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire Urinary Incontinence Short Form (ICIQ-UI SF; range 0–21) [16]. Consistent with prior research, urinary incontinence was defined using a cut-off score of ≥ 1 [17].

Female sexual function was assessed using the Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI; range 2–36), which covers six domains (desire, arousal, lubrication, orgasm, satisfaction, pain) over the preceding 4 weeks [18]. A total FSFI score ≤ 26.55 was used as a threshold indicating risk of clinically relevant sexual dysfunction, based on published cut-offs [19].

A study-specific questionnaire was used to collect information on relationship status, education, sexual orientation, sexual activity status (and reasons for inactivity), and frequency of intercourse. The questionnaire also included eight predefined, easily distinguishable coital positions (A–H; Fig. 1) selected by the research team. For each position, the participants rated the frequency of use and perceived pleasure. Frequency was rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = never; 2 = once or twice in a lifetime; 3 = fewer than half of the intercourse episodes; 4 = half or more than half of the episodes; and 5 = every time or almost every time). Pleasure was rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = painful, 2 = neither painful nor pleasurable, 3 = slightly pleasurable, 4 = moderately pleasurable, and 5 = very pleasurable). The sexual activity status was recorded separately. Analyses of coital position, coital position frequency, and pleasure were restricted to participants with paired available responses for the respective items, and no imputation was performed.

Statistical methods

Statistical analyses were performed using Python 3.11 (Python Software Foundation, USA) with the standard scientific libraries. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Because FSFI domain scores showed non-normal distributions, non-parametric tests were applied. Paired comparisons between baseline and the 5-year follow-up were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Paired binary outcomes (e.g. urinary incontinence status and sexual activity) were compared using McNemar’s exact test. Between-group comparisons (presence vs. absence of urinary incontinence) were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. The effect size r was calculated as Z/√N.

To identify factors associated with changes in sexual function, we modelled the change in the total FSFI score (ΔFSFI = FSFI-total at 5 years minus FSFI-total at baseline) using robust linear regression (RLM; Huber T M-estimation). Two-sided p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Exploratory baseline associations between urinary incontinence severity and sexual function were assessed using Spearman’s correlation and non-parametric subgroup comparisons. Any procedure-specific comparisons were considered exploratory because procedure allocation was not randomised and subgroup sizes were small.

Results

Participants and clinical characteristics

Seventy women were enrolled in this study. However, 14 were lost to follow-up, 12 became pregnant, and four underwent reoperation. Baseline characteristics of women who completed the 5-year follow-up and those who did not were broadly similar, although moderate standardised differences were observed for urinary incontinence-related variables. (Table S1) Ultimately, 40 women provided paired baseline and 5-year follow-up questionnaires (Fig. 2). The mean age at the time of surgery was 39.8 years. At baseline, 36/40 (90.0%) participants reported being in a relationship, and 36/40 (90.0%) were sexually active. At the 5-year follow-up, 34/40 (85.0%) reported being in a relationship, and 33/40 (82.5%) were sexually active. The baseline and follow-up characteristics are summarised in Table 1.

The mean BMI decreased from 39.1 kg/m² at baseline to 30.7 kg/m² at 5 years (p < 0.001). The procedures included LSG in 23 (57.5%), OAGB in 15 (37.5%), and RYGB in 2 (5.0%) women. Based on the prespecified ICIQ-UI SF cut-off, UI was present in 27 women (67.5%) preoperatively and in 15 women (37.5%) at follow-up (p = 0.004).

Female sexual function

At baseline, 16 women (40.0%) had a total FSFI score ≤ 26.55; at 5 years, 13 women (32.5%) remained below this threshold. The total FSFI score increased from 25.83 ± 7.96 (median 28.65 [IQR 22.30–31.70]) at baseline to 27.16 ± 7.38 (median 29.00 [IQR 24.50–31.92]) at 5 years. However, this change was not statistically significant (p = 0.107; r = 0.26; mean change Δ = 1.33, 95% CI: -1.79 to 4.33). No statistically significant within-subject changes were observed in the total FSFI score or in any individual FSFI domain over the 5-year follow-up period. (Table 2; Fig. 3).

Predictors of change in FSFI

In robust linear regression (n = 38 complete cases), lower baseline FSFI (p < 0.001) and lower postoperative BMI (p = 0.015) were independently associated with greater improvement in total FSFI. Preoperative BMI (p = 0.164) and age at surgery (p = 0.160) were not significant predictors. In exploratory models, after adjusting for hypothyroidism, hypertension, and type 2 diabetes, none of these comorbidities were significantly associated with ΔFSFI. (Table 3).

FSFI by baseline urinary continence

Baseline urinary continence status was not associated with the baseline FSFI scores (Table S2). Spearman correlation analysis at baseline showed a consistent negative association between urinary incontinence severity (ICIQ-UI SF total score) and sexual function (FSFI total and domain scores). However, none of these correlations reached statistical significance. These analyses were cross-sectional and should not be interpreted as evidence of the longitudinal effects.

Sexual positions

Eight coital positions (A–H; Fig. 1) were assessed for frequency and perceived pleasure before and after surgery. All 40 participants completed the baseline and 5-year follow-up assessments. Analysable paired responses for specific sexual position items were only available for subsets. For the coital position questionnaire, paired frequency ratings were provided by 26 of the 40 women (65%). Paired pleasure ratings were available for 11–19 women, depending on the position; the denominators are reported in Tables S3 and S4. No imputation was performed on the data. Although the medians suggested minor shifts for selected positions, no statistically significant within-subject changes were observed for frequency or pleasure in any position (all p > 0.05) (Figs. 4 and 5; Tables S3–S4).

Median frequency of sexual positions before and after surgery. The median frequency of sexual positions used before and after the procedure indicated that most positions were characterised by relatively stable levels of use. The most frequently used position was position A, with a median of 4.0 before and after the procedure. For position B, the median decreased from 4.0 to 3.0, whereas for position G, it increased from 2.0 to 3.0. For the remaining positions (C–F, H), no significant differences in the medians were observed (they remained the same or fluctuated slightly). The median subjective pleasure ratings for individual sexual positions were largely stable between baseline and the 5-year follow-up (median=4.0 for most positions). Discussion

In this single-centre prospective study with a 5-year follow-up after bariatric surgery, we observed substantial long-term weight loss and a significant reduction in the prevalence of urinary incontinence. In contrast, sexual function showed only modest improvement overall. Neither the total FSFI score nor any individual FSFI domain changed significantly within subjects over time. The final paired sample was modest, and the observed change in the total FSFI score was small. Although the confidence interval around the mean change was wide, no statistically significant within-subject improvement in the FSFI was detected. Therefore, both a genuinely modest long-term effect and a type II error remain plausible explanations for this finding. Exploratory regression analyses suggested that women with poorer baseline sexual function and lower postoperative BMI were more likely to show improvement. Baseline urinary incontinence status was not clearly associated with FSFI scores, although cross-sectional analyses suggested a non-significant inverse relationship between urinary incontinence severity and sexual function. Preferences regarding the assessed sexual positions and perceived pleasure have remained largely stable over time. Efthymiou et al. assessed 50 women using the FSFI questionnaire before surgery and 1, 6, and 12 months after surgery. The most visible improvement was present 6 months after the surgery, indicating significant improvement in FSD [20]. Moreover, de Almeida Menzes et al. prospectively evaluated 40 women who underwent RYGB 6 and 12 months postoperatively. The patients completed the Sexual Quotient – Female Version, and their androgenic hormone levels were measured. The study revealed no statistical improvement after 6 months and a statistically significant improvement after 12 months in sexual function. However, in this group of patients, only 10% had FSD [21]. Furthermore, Gokalp et al. included 26 patients who underwent LSG in their study. The FSFI questionnaire was administered before and one year after surgery. Patients experienced significant improvements in their sexual function. Consistent with our study, the individuals who derived the greatest benefit were those with poorer sexual function prior to the surgery, as opposed to those who exhibited better sexual function [10]. Law et al. highlighted the multifactorial nature of female sexual function, encompassing factors such as self-image, mental health, and relationship status. This complexity may elucidate why improvements are frequently observed approximately 12 months post-surgery, followed by a decline or stabilisation over time [12].

In a comprehensive five-year study conducted by Steffen et al. across ten hospitals in the United States, data were collected from 1,600 women at a one-year follow-up. The authors reported enhanced patient-reported satisfaction with sexual life following bariatric surgery. This improvement was evaluated using measures such as frequency of sexual activity, sexual desire, physical limitations during sexual activity, and overall satisfaction, with the most significant improvements observed within the first postoperative year. However, between one and five years post-surgery, the enhancements in sexual desire and satisfaction were no longer statistically significant. The early benefits were more pronounced in women with preoperative sexual functioning impairments. Notably, the FSFI questionnaire was not employed for the patients [9]. Additionally, in a meta-analysis by Gao et al., which considered 20 articles, it was concluded that bariatric surgery improves FSFI in women with obesity, but not in women with pelvic floor disorders [11]. In accordance with this trajectory, our analysis did not reveal a statistically significant long-term improvement, which may be attributed to the limited power resulting from the small sample size. However, with a five-year follow-up and in alignment with previous reports and the article by Lopez et al., who analyzed 118 women using the FSFI questionnaire with a six-year follow-up, it appears that sexual dysfunction may exhibit less pronounced longitudinal improvement beyond the first postoperative year. This finding underscores the necessity for adequately powered, long-duration studies [13].

Oliveira et al. conducted a study involving 62 women who underwent Roux-en-Y gastric bypass and assessed them both before and six months after surgery. The study analysed the FSFI score and the frequency of 12 specific sexual positions. The researchers observed improvements across all six FSFI domains and an increased frequency in three out of the 12 positions. However, a significant limitation in interpretability is that pleasure per position was not assessed, and the high number and detail of positions could have compromised objectivity during follow-up. Oliveira et al. reported that changes in BMI were not significantly correlated with improvements in sexual function. It is worth noting that in the analysed group, the stability of sexual positions may also reflect partner habits, relationship dynamics, and comfort rather than physical limitations alone [14]. In our multivariable analysis, a lower postoperative BMI was independently associated with greater improvement in the FSFI. Any apparent differences between procedures should be interpreted with great caution because procedure allocation was not randomised, subgroup sizes were small, and residual confounding was likely. Larger studies with prespecified between-procedure analyses are needed before drawing any procedure-specific conclusions.

The association between body weight and sexual function is likely multifaceted and may not be adequately represented by BMI. A similar complexity has been noted in studies of urinary incontinence after bariatric surgery [22, 23]. This highlights not only the limitations of BMI as a measurement but also the intricate nature of sexual dysfunction and urinary incontinence. Furthermore, urinary incontinence adversely impacts sexual function in women. Although a review by Bezerra et al. supports this hypothesis, the precise mechanism remains unidentified. It is unclear whether factors such as body self-image, self-confidence, or sensuality are affected, or if the primary concern is the fear of incontinence during sexual intercourse. In the analysed article, an increased impact is made on the psychological side of sexual functioning [24]. Interestingly, Kasyan et al. analysed the presence of single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNP) in collagen type 3A1 and 1A1 in women with pelvic organ prolapse (POP) and stress urinary incontinence (SUI). They tested positive in 10% and 2% of patients with POP/SUI, respectively. The healthy control group tested negative for each genotype. The relationship between urinary incontinence and sexual function likely involves not only psychological mechanisms but also pelvic floor support and connective tissue integrity. Pelvic floor disorders and urinary incontinence may share biological determinants, including collagen-related mechanisms, which may partly influence the sexual well-being of affected women. This broader pathophysiological context should be considered in future studies exploring the link between continence and sexual function after bariatric surgery [25].

Finally, McNabney et al. analysed sexual dysfunction in individuals with obesity, focusing on a broader perspective and considering both metabolic and psychological aspects. The multidimensionality of sexual functioning may influence the ambiguity of the results of different studies, emphasising the need for more complex studies. The importance of psychological influence might be underestimated [3].

This study has several strengths, including its prospective design with a long (5-year) follow-up, focus on female sexual health after bariatric surgery, use of validated instruments (FSFI and ICIQ-UI SF), and attempt to identify predictors of change in sexual function using robust regression modelling. Moreover, the assessment of urinary incontinence provides a clinically relevant context, and the structured evaluation of sexual positions adds a novel, patient-centred dimension. Nevertheless, the findings should be interpreted in light of these limitations. First, the final paired sample was modest, and attrition over the 5-year follow-up was substantial, which may have introduced selection bias and reduced external validity. Second, the absence of intermediate follow-up assessments precluded the evaluation of the trajectory of change over time. In addition, no formal a priori sample-size calculation for long-term FSFI outcomes was performed. Therefore, the study may have been underpowered to detect small changes, particularly in individual FSFI domains. Third, procedure allocation was not randomised, and subgroup sizes were small. Therefore, any between-procedure comparisons should be considered exploratory only. Fourth, all outcomes were self-reported, and the study-specific sexual position questionnaire was not formally validated. Fifth, not all participants were sexually active at both time points, and paired data for coital position ratings were available only for subsets of participants. Sixth, important potential confounders, including psychological status, body image, partner-related factors, hormonal changes, and medication changes were not systematically assessed. Finally, the lack of a conservatively treated control group limits our ability to distinguish surgery-specific effects from the impact of weight loss and aging over time. Overall, our results should be viewed as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, supporting the need for larger, multicentre studies with validated tools and repeated follow-up time points.

Conclusions

Five years after bariatric surgery, women experienced substantial weight loss and a significant reduction in the prevalence of urinary incontinence. Overall, female sexual function showed only modest, non-significant improvement, and no significant within-subject change was observed in the total FSFI score or its individual domains. Women with poorer baseline sexual function and lower postoperative BMI appeared more likely to improve. Sexual position preferences and perceived pleasure have remained largely unchanged. These findings should be interpreted cautiously because of the modest sample size, substantial loss to follow-up, and the absence of intermediate assessments.
