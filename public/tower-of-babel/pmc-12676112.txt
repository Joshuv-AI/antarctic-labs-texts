Mild photothermal-mediated neuroimmunomodulatory composite hydrogel ameliorates bone healing microenvironment for enhanced osteoporotic bone defect regeneration
Authors: Qingcheng Song, Shuo Zhang, Jiahao Yu, Yiran Zhang, Chunxu Fu, Xin Xing, Jianhua Wu, Yanbin Zhu, Wei Chen, Yingze Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe treatment of osteoporotic bone defects represents a substantial clinical challenge in contemporary orthopedics due to the abnormal accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and elevated inflammatory levels. Despite advances in bone graft materials for enhancing bone regeneration, developing an ideal therapeutic strategy capable of orchestrating the multidimensional biological processes underlying bone healing, such as immunomodulation, angiogenesis, osteogenesis and innervation, remains a formidable challenge in orthopedic biomaterial design. In our study, a multifunctional composite hydrogel (Ce@Mo/GC) was developed through the incorporation of CeO2@Mo2Ti2C3 MXene (Ce@Mo) heterojunction structures into gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA)/carboxymethyl chitosan (CMCS) for optimizing the osteogenic microenvironment in osteoporotic bone defects. The Ce@Mo system integrates multiple enzyme-like activities with NIR-responsive photothermal effects, enabling effective ROS scavenging at defect sites through mild hyperthermia-enhanced Ce3+/Ce4+ valence transitions in CeO2, while promoting macrophage polarization to M2 phenotype to modulate immune responses. Additionally, localized administration of Ce@Mo/GC can promote bone healing by inducing neurogenesis in the defect site and triggering the spatiotemporal release of neuropeptides through mild photothermal stimulation. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated that the Ce@Mo/GC hydrogel exhibits excellent biocompatibility while effectively modulating inflammatory responses, promoting osteogenic differentiation, angiogenesis and achieving nerve immune regulation to promote bone healing.

Introduction

Against the backdrop of the global population aging, the incidence of osteoporotic bone injuries is witnessing a continuous upward trend [1,2]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that patients with osteoporotic bone defect exhibit compromised osteogenic potential and a persistent local inflammatory state, both of which significantly impair bone regeneration efficiency and deteriorate quality of life [3]. Although current bone repair strategies based on autologous bone, allograft bone and bone substitute materials are effective methods for the treatment of osteoporotic bone defects, they are often limited by supply limitations, risk of immunogenicity, and difficulties in achieving precise morphological and functional matching between the implant and the defect area [4,5]. Therefore, the development of innovative bone repair strategies capable of satisfying the multifaceted biological demands of the regenerative microenvironment, including innervation, immune regulation, angiogenesis, and osteogenic differentiation, represents an important research direction in the field of bone repair.

In contrast to the well-orchestrated process of physiological bone healing, osteoporotic bone defects are typically characterized by elevated local inflammation, excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), ultimately forming a pathological microenvironment that severely compromises bone regeneration [6,7]. In particular, macrophage, as an important innate immune cell in the inflammatory phase after bone injury, are deeply involved in the regulation of the entire healing process by polarizing into different phenotypes [8]. Unfortunately, macrophages tend to polarize into the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype under the stimulation of multiple pathological signals, inducing the formation of granulomas and excessive fibrosis, which can hinder bone regeneration [9]. Even worse, elevated local ROS levels may, in turn, trigger excessive oxidative stress and further exacerbate inflammation to maintain the M1 phenotype of macrophages, ultimately hindering osteogenesis and angiogenesis [10]. Therefore, the development of therapeutic strategies based on bone tissue engineering to break the ROS-inflammation cascade cycle and regulate the macrophages polarization to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, accompanied by the secretion of various anti-inflammatory cytokines is conducive to constructing an immune microenvironment that promotes bone healing.

In recent years, a variety of enzyme-mimicking biomaterials have received extensive attention in the treatment of various diseases. Among them, cerium oxide (CeO2) nanoparticles (NPs) exhibit desired catalase-like (CAT-like) and superoxide dismutase-like (SOD-like) activities by virtue of the reversible conversion between Ce3+ and Ce4+, making them an effective tool for regulating ROS levels and alleviating inflammation [[11], [12], [13]]. Leveraging the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, CeO2 NPs have demonstrated remarkable therapeutic potential in facilitating multi-tissue regeneration, and have been successfully applied in the treatment of chronic wound and inflammatory bowel disease [14,15]. It is worth noting that CeO2 NPs modified on the titanium substrate surfaces by magnetron sputtering can be served as an effective functional material to enhance osteogenic capability of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) and M2 polarization of macrophages, thus conducive to early osseointegration [16]. Therefore, as a metal with variable valence states, CeO2 NPs demonstrates great potential in the field of bone tissue engineering due to its excellent properties of effectively alleviating pathological inflammation and reprogramming the osteoimmune microenvironment at the injury site.

Moreover, the pivotal role of neural network in facilitating bone regeneration remains frequently overlooked in bone tissue engineering, resulting in delayed bone healing [17]. Anatomically, neural networks extensively distributed throughout bone tissues such as the periosteum, bone marrow, and cortical bone, and regulate osteogenic differentiation, bone regeneration and bone homeostasis by transmitting neurotrophins or neurotransmitters [18,19]. In particular, emerging evidence reveals that sensory nerves can positively regulate the reconstruction of vascular networks and facilitate the proliferation and differentiation of MSCs by secreting various neuropeptides [20,21]. Therefore, considering the regulatory role of sensory nerves on bone metabolism and homeostasis, biomaterial-mediated sensory nerve regeneration at defect site has emerged as a promising therapeutic modality for bone regeneration. In recent years, two-dimensional (2D) transition metal carbides and nitrides (MXenes) have demonstrated significant potential for neural regeneration owing to their exceptional conductivity, hydrophilic surface properties, and outstanding mechanical performance [22]. Among them, MXenes containing the transition metal molybdenum (Mo2Ti2C3) exhibit superior surface area and enhanced electrical conductivity than common Ti3C2Tx through the substitution of the Ti-Ti with the Mo-Ti structure, which makes them a valid candidate for promoting neurogenesis [22]. Therefore, the heterojunctions (Ce@Mo) prepared by loading CeO2 nanoparticles onto Mo2Ti2C3 MXene are capable of promoting bone regeneration via multiple pathways, including antioxidation, anti-inflammation, and the facilitation of neurogenesis.

Additionally, benefiting from the exceptional near-infrared (NIR) light absorption properties, Mo2Ti2C3 MXene based mild photothermal therapy (MPTT) can serve as an effective approach to synergistically enhance osteogenic efficiency, owing to its precise switching control, high spatiotemporal selectivity, and minimal tissue invasiveness [23]. Related studies have confirmed that MPTT with the temperature maintained at around 42 °C can accelerate bone regeneration through heat stress-induced upregulation of heat shock proteins followed by extracellular regulated protein kinase (ERK) pathway activation, while simultaneously inducing cytoskeletal alteration [24,25]. Moreover, MPTT has also been confirmed to be closely related to the optimization of the bone healing microenvironment, as relevant studies found that the combination of an antioxidant nanotherapeutic platform with MPTT can alleviate local inflammatory responses and induce M2 macrophage polarization to promote bone healing [26,27]. Inspired by this therapeutic approach, it is reasonable to believe that locally administered Ce@Mo heterojunctions at bone defect sites can be NIR-responsively activated to address various critical challenges such as persistent inflammation, oxidative stress, and compromised neurogenesis, thereby orchestrating a pro-regenerative microenvironment.

Based on the above introduction, to tackle the challenge of promoting bone repair under pathological microenvironment disorders, we developed an injectable multifunctional hydrogel (Ce@Mo/GC) with mild photothermal activity by incorporating Ce@Mo heterojunctions into GelMA/CMCS matrix, which can synergistically enhance osteoporotic bone repair through alleviating inflammation and inducing neurogenesis. As shown in Scheme 1, the Ce@Mo/GC composite hydrogel can be precisely delivered to bone defect sites via simple injection and subsequently achieves minimally invasive filling through in situ crosslinking, which is beneficial to local mechanical support and continuous release of therapeutic reagents. Then, under 808 nm NIR laser irradiation, the mild photothermal effect of Ce@Mo/GC hydrogel can synergistically enhance the CAT-like and SOD-like enzyme activities of CeO2 NPs to eliminate excess ROS, while inducing macrophage polarization toward anti-inflammatory phenotype and promoting various pro-healing factors secretion for osteoimmune microenvironment optimization. More important, we further demonstrated that local administration of Ce@Mo/GC hydrogel combined with MPTT can also promote the reconstruction of sensory nerve network at bone defect site and effectively regulate the process of new bone formation by stimulating the secretion of relevant neuropeptides. Collectively, this study demonstrated that the hydrogel system capable of spatiotemporally orchestrating multifaceted biological processes in bone healing exhibits remarkable repair efficacy in osteoporotic bone defects, which not only provides a new paradigm of multi-mechanism collaborative treatment for bone regeneration in complex pathological environment, but also provides a reference for the subsequent development of intelligent responsive bone repair materials.

Materials and methods

Materials

All chemical reagents were obtained from commercial sources and used without further purification unless otherwise specified. Hydrofluoric acid (HF), tetrapropyl ammonium hydroxide (TPAOH) and MXene (Mo2Ti2AlC3) were purchased from Foshan Xinxi Technology Co., Ltd. Cell Counting Kit-8, Calcein-AM/PI double staining kit, Actin-Tracker Green-488 were acquired from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). Gelatin methacryloyl and carboxymethyl chitosan were obtained from Engineering for Life (Suzhou, China).

Preparation of Ce@Mo nanocomposites

The monolayer MXene synthesis was performed through a modified chemical exfoliation approach. Briefly, 1 g Mo2Ti2AlC3 was subjected to etching in 10 mL HF under continuous stirring at 55 °C for 72 h. The resulting suspension underwent sequential centrifugation and deionized (DI) water washing until neutral pH. Then, the collected sediment was mixed with 20 mL of 25 wt% TPAOH aqueous solution, followed by continuous stirring for 6 h at room temperature. After complete removal of excess TPAOH, the product was redispersed in 30 mL of DI water and ultrasonicated for 30 min in an ice bath. Finally, the dispersion underwent centrifugation at 3500 rpm for 20 min to separate unexfoliated aggregates, after which the monolayer Mo2Ti2C3 MXene was obtained for further application.

The Ce@Mo heterojunction was synthesized via a hydrothermal method based on established protocols [14,28]. Initially, 100 mg of MXene was uniformly dispersed in 20 mL DI water. Subsequently, 20 mL of 0.1 M cesium nitrate hexahydrate (Ce (NO3)3·6H2O) was introduced dropwise into the MXene suspension, followed by continuous magnetic stirring at 40 °C for 2 h to facilitate the electrostatic adsorption of cerium ions onto the highly electronegative surface functional groups of Mo2Ti2C3 MXene nanosheets. Subsequently, 30 mL of sodium hydroxide solution (NaOH, 10 M) was added to the mixed solution, and the mixture was continuously stirred at room temperature for 3 h. Then, the resulting mixture was transferred to a Teflon-lined pressure reactor and reacted at 180 °C for a certain period. Finally, the precipitate was collected by high-speed centrifugation, and Ce@Mo heterojunction was obtained after lyophilization.

Preparation of Ce@Mo/GC hydrogels

For the preparation of GC hydrogel, 50 mg of CMCS was dissolved in 5 mL of DI water under continuous stirring at 50 °C to obtain a CMCS aqueous solution. Meanwhile, 500 mg of GelMA was dissolved in the photoinitiator LAP (0.25 % w/v) at 60 °C. Then, the two prepared hydrogel precursor solutions were mixed at a volume ratio of 1:1 and magnetically stirred for 30 min to obtain the GC hydrogel precursor solution. Subsequently, the precursor solution was injected into a customized circular mold and cross-linked to form a gel under 405 nm ultraviolet (UV) irradiation. The composite hydrogel was prepared by adding the Ce@Mo aqueous solution to the GC hydrogel precursor solution, followed by the aforementioned experimental procedure to obtain the 0.2 wt% Ce@Mo/GC composite hydrogel.

Characterization

The morphologies and microstructures of different samples were observed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and EDS elemental mapping. The thickness of the nanosheets was measured by atomic force microscopy (AFM). The zeta potential was measured by a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS90. The chemical composition and valence states of different samples was detected by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) using a Thermo ESCALAB 250XI.

Swelling ratio

The swelling behavior of different hydrogels was evaluated according to established protocols [29]. Briefly, lyophilized hydrogels were weighed (W0) and subsequently immersed in PBS (pH 7.4) at 37 °C. After careful removal of surface moisture with filter paper, the swollen hydrogels were reweighed (Wt). The swelling ratio was calculated using the following equation: swelling ratio (%) = (Wt – W0)/W0 × 100 %.

In vitro degradation

The in vitro degradation profiles of hydrogels were assessed by monitoring mass changes in PBS containing 2 U mL−1 collagenase at 37 °C. Lyophilized hydrogels (initial weight W0) were immersed in the solution under constant shaking (100 rpm). At predetermined time points, samples were retrieved, gently rinsed with DI water, lyophilized, and reweighed (Wt). The residual mass ratio was calculated as: weight remaining (%) = (Wt/W0) × 100 %.

Evaluation of photothermal properties of Ce@Mo/GC hydrogels

To evaluate the photothermal performance of different hydrogels in vitro, the prepared GC and Ce@Mo/GC hydrogels were placed in a 24 well plates and irradiated by 808 nm laser with a power density of 1 W cm−2 for 5 min. The temperature changes and corresponding thermal images were recorded in real time by an IR thermal imaging camera. The photothermal stability of the Ce@Mo/GC composite hydrogel was further evaluated by monitoring temperature changes during five ON/OFF cycles of 808 nm laser irradiation, each consisting of 5 min of laser exposure followed by natural cooling phase, with real-time temperature recording via an IR thermal imaging camera at 30 s intervals.

In vitro cytocompatibility testing

The cytocompatibility of different hydrogels was evaluated using MC3T3-E1 and HUVEC cells, both of which are closely associated with bone regeneration. All hydrogel samples were sterilized by immersion in 75 % ethanol followed by 1 h UV irradiation in a 48-well plate. After PBS washing, MC3T3-E1 cells or HUVECs were seeded at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well and cultured at 37 °C with 5 % CO2. The cell viability was assessed by CCK-8 assay at 24, 48, and 72 h after co-culture. Meanwhile, the live/dead status of cells was observed with the Calcein-AM/PI double staining kit according to the manufacturer's protocol. Furthermore, after 72 h of co-culture, cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 15 min and permeabilized with 0.1 % Triton X-100. Subsequently, the samples were stained with Hoechst 33342 (for nuclei) and phalloidin (for cytoskeleton) at room temperature for 20 min, followed by fluorescence microscopy observation of the cell cytoskeletons.

Macrophage polarization in vitro

Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were isolated from 8-week-old C57 mice according to the method reported in previous literature [30]. Briefly, femurs and tibias of mice were carefully separated, after removing the epiphyses, the bone marrow cavities were thoroughly rinsed with α-MEM medium 3–4 times until the bones turned white. Subsequently, the obtained cell suspension was centrifuged and resuspended in a culture medium containing 20 ng mL−1 M-CSF, followed by culturing for 6 days. Then, BMDMs were seeded into a 48 well plate containing different hydrogels at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well and subjected to 808 nm laser irradiation (1 W cm−2, 5 min) with temperature maintained at 42 ± 0.5 °C. After removing the medium, the cells were incubated with 10 μM DCFH-DA for ROS detection and subsequently imaged using a fluorescence microscope.

Next, the BMDMs were co-cultured with different hydrogels for 3 days, in which the laser-irradiated group received the aforementioned treatment, and macrophage polarization was evaluated by immunofluorescence staining. The cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.1 % Triton X-100 and blocked with 10 % goat serum for 1 h. Then, the cells were incubated with CD206 primary antibody (1:100, Abcam) overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h, and finally counterstained with Hoechst 33342 nuclear dye for 15 min before CLSM observation.

Furthermore, the polarization of macrophages and the expression of inflammation-related genes were evaluated via quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR). Briefly, total RNA was extracted from macrophages in each group using Trizol (Takara, China) reagent, followed by reverse transcription into cDNA. The relative expression levels of target genes were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method and normalized to the expression level of the actin gene. The primer sequences used for RT-qPCR are listed in Table S1.

RNA-seq analysis

The gene expression of macrophages after co-culture with the composite hydrogels was detected by RNA sequencing. BMDMs were inoculated into a 6 well plate containing composite hydrogels at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well and subjected to the aforementioned mild photothermal treatment. Total RNA was extracted using Trizol (Takara, China) reagent according to the manufacturer's protocol, with subsequent sequencing and analysis conducted by CapitalBiotech Co., Ltd. Differential gene expression was visualized through heatmap and volcano plot. Functional annotation of transcriptional alterations was systematically performed through KEGG and GO analysis to identify key signaling cascades and molecular mechanisms associated with gene expression variations.

In vitro evaluation of immunomodulatory effect on osteogenesis and angiogenesis

To further evaluate the impact of macrophage phenotype transition on osteogenic and angiogenic capacity, the supernatants of BMDMs after 3-day co-culture with various hydrogels were collected, followed by centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 10 min and then filtration of the resulting supernatant through 0.22 μm filters. The conditioned medium was obtained by mixing filtered supernatants with osteogenic induction medium (OriCell, MUXMT-90021) at a 1:1 ratio for osteogenic differentiation induction. For alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity assessment, MC3T3-E1 cells were seeded in a 48 well plate with conditioned medium replenishment every two days. The cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde on the seventh day and stained with a BCIP/NBT Alkaline Phosphatase Color Development Kit (Beyotime, C3206) according to the manufacturer's protocol. For alizarin red S (ARS) staining, the cells cultured under the aforementioned conditions for 21 days were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde and stained with ARS solution to visualize calcium nodules. Quantification of mineralization was performed by adding acetic acid solution to each well followed by 75 °C incubation until complete dissolution of calcium deposits, and the absorbance at 450 nm was determined by a microplate reader.

The angiogenic potential of HUVECs was evaluated through tube formation assay according to established methodologies [31]. The conditioned medium was prepared by mixing serum-free high glucose DMEM with the collected supernatants at a 1:1 vol ratio. Subsequently, 150 μL of Matrigel was carefully added to each well of 48 well plate using pre-chilled pipette tips without introducing air bubbles. After incubating the plate at 37 °C for 30 min until gel formation, HUVECs were suspended in 200 μL of diverse conditioned medium and seeded into each well. After 24 h of co-incubation, the formation of tubular structures was observed using an inverted microscope. The total length of tubules, the number of tubules and the number of connections were quantified by Image J software. For wound healing assay, HUVECs were seeded into a 6 well plate (3 × 105 cells per well) and cultured at 37 °C with 5 % CO2 in a humidified atmosphere until reaching 90 % confluence. Then, a uniform scratch was created using a sterile 200 μL pipette tip. After washing three times with PBS to remove cell debris, HUVECs were incubated with different conditioned medium and photographed using an inverted microscope at predetermined time points. The wound healing ratio was determined by Image J.

Hemolysis assay

Briefly, 1.0 mL of anticoagulated blood was collected from healthy mice and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. The upper serum was discarded to obtain red blood cells (RBCs), which were then resuspended in 1.5 mL of PBS to prepare an RBC suspension. Subsequently, 200 μL RBC suspension was added to 800 μL DI water (positive control), PBS (negative control), and different hydrogel samples, respectively. After incubation in a constant temperature environment at 37 °C for 2 h, the mixtures were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. The absorbance of each supernatant at a wavelength of 570 nm was measured using a microplate reader, and the hemolysis rate was calculated according to the formula: (A1 - A0)/(A2 - A0) × 100 %, in which A0, A1, and A2 represent the absorbance of negative control, hydrogel sample, and positive control, respectively.

Animal studies

All animal experimental procedures were strictly performed in accordance with the ethical guidelines approved by the Ethics Committee of the Fourth Hospital of Hebei Medical University (Approval No. IACUC-4th Hos Hebmu-2024733-3). In our study, 20-month-old C57BL/6 mice (male) were used to establish a distal femoral defect model. Under continuous isoflurane anesthesia, monolayer cortical bone defect with a diameter of 1.0 mm was created below the growth plate using a trephine drill. Subsequently, 20 μL of the hydrogel precursor solution was injected and in-situ crosslinking was achieved under UV irradiation (30 s) at the defect site. For mild photothermal therapy, the implanted hydrogels were subjected to 808 nm laser irradiation (1 W cm−2) for 5 min every 2 days post-surgery, with real-time temperature monitoring via an IR thermal imaging camera. After 7 days of treatment, the mice were sacrificed and the femoral specimens were collected for micro-CT analysis, histological examination and immunofluorescence staining.

Histological and immunofluorescence staining

For histological staining, the harvested femurs were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde for 48 h at 4 °C, followed by decalcification in 10 % EDTA solution (Sigma Aldrich) with daily solution changes for 4 weeks. After complete decalcification, specimens were processed through graded ethanol dehydration, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 4 μm thick sections for subsequent H&E and Masson staining to evaluate morphological alterations.

For immunofluorescence staining, the decalcified specimens were dehydrated in 30 % sucrose solution at 4 °C for 48 h, and then embedded with OCT. 20 μm thick frozen sections were prepared by a freezing microtome at −30 °C. After rewarming, the sections were permeabilized with 0.5 % Triton X-100 and blocked with 10 % goat serum. Subsequently, they were incubated with the corresponding primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with the secondary antibodies with fluorescence labeling at room temperature for 1 h. After staining the nuclei with DAPI, images were captured using a fluorescence microscopy.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were statistically analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software and presented as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD) from three independent experiments. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey's test was performed for multiple comparisons, with statistical significance set at *p < 0.05 (**p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001 and ****p < 0.0001).

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of Ce@Mo heterojunctions

Initially, according to the preparation process illustrated in Fig. 1A, the Al layers in Mo2Ti2AlC3 MXene were selectively etched with hydrofluoric acid (HF), and then Mo2Ti2C3 MXene nanosheets were fabricated through ultrasonic-assisted stripping. Subsequently, to endow the synthesized Mo2Ti2C3 with CAT-like and SOD-like activities, the in-situ growth and firm anchoring of CeO2 NPs on the surface of MXene were achieved via a hydrothermal method, resulting in the formation of Ce@Mo heterojunctions. The morphology of the synthesized Mo2Ti2C3 was initially characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). As shown in Fig. 1B, the Mo2Ti2AlC3 precursor was successfully converted into Mo2Ti2C3 MXene through HF etching, and its SEM image clearly exhibited a typical two-dimensional multilayer morphology with an accordion-like structure. Subsequently, Mo2Ti2C3 exhibited a single-layer geometric morphology after ultrasonic treatment, which provides sufficient active sites and a suitable growth substrate for the formation of CeO2 (Fig. 1C). The lamellar characteristics of Mo2Ti2C3 and Ce@Mo heterojunctions were further observed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). As shown in Fig. 1D and E, all the synthesized nanomaterials presented a nanosheet morphology with a lateral size of up to 400 nm. The energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping results showed that the constituent elements (Ce, Ti, O, C, Mo) were all homogeneous distributed in the lamellar structure, which provided strong evidence for the successful formation of heterojunctions (Fig. 1F). Additionally, atomic force microscopy (AFM) characterization revealed that the monolayer MXene nanosheets exhibited a thickness of approximately 1.8 nm, which is consistent with theoretical predictions, thereby confirming the precision of the monolayer MXene preparation process. After the successful in-situ growth of CeO2 NPs, the AFM analysis revealed that the loading of CeO2 did not significantly alter the thickness of the MXene nanosheets (Fig. 1G and H). Collectively, the effective synthesis of MXene nanocomposites incorporating evenly dispersed CeO2 NPs offers a viable solution to the issue of CeO2 aggregation.

Additionally, the synthesis process of the Ce@Mo heterostructure was systematically monitored through zeta potential measurement and DLS analysis. As shown in Fig. 1I, the zeta potential of Mo2Ti2C3 shifted from −37.21 ± 0.59 mV to −26.17 ± 0.43 mV after CeO2 loading, which can be attributed to charge screening effect induced by the positively charged CeO2 surface. Meanwhile, DLS analysis confirmed that the particle size distribution remained virtually unchanged before and after the loading process, indicating the preservation of structural integrity during heterojunction formation (Fig. S1). Next, to further evaluate the multi-enzyme mimetic activities of the Ce@Mo, the mixed-valence properties of Ce were investigated through X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra, in which the coexistence of Ce3+/Ce4+ redox pair was analyzed due to their crucial roles in enzyme-like catalytic processes. As shown in Fig. 1J, compared with the XPS spectrum of Mo2Ti2C3, a distinct Ce 3d peak appears in the XPS spectrum of Ce@Mo. This clear signal serves as direct evidence for the presence of cerium in the Ce@Mo heterojunction, indicating that CeO2 has been successfully loaded onto the surface of Mo2Ti2C3. The high-resolution XPS spectrum of Ce exhibited four distinct peaks at 902.6, 900.5, 884.5, and 882.0 eV, corresponding to Ce 3d3/2 and Ce 3d5/2, in which the fitting peaks located at 902.6 and 884.5 eV were ascribed to Ce3+, while the other two peaks at 900.5 and 882.0 eV suggest the presence of Ce4+. These finding demonstrated the feasibility of Ce@Mo with mixed-valence state of CeO2 could serve as a multifunctional nanozyme catalyst to relieve oxidative stress (Fig. 1K).

Preparation and characterization of Ce@Mo/GC hydrogels

In recent years, to promote the clinical translation of bone regeneration research, various biological scaffold materials have been developed to optimize the bone repair process. For instance, the sandwich-like hybrid surface-functionalized beta-tricalcium calcium phosphate (β-TCP) scaffold and bioinspired redwood-like scaffold developed by Wu et al. have demonstrated unique advantages in regulating the osteoimmune microenvironment and promoting the adhesion, spreading, proliferation, and migration of endogenous stem cells and endothelial cells. These findings inspire the development of therapeutic platforms with multidimensional microenvironment-regulating capabilities, which hold critical significance for efficiently promoting the repair of bone defects [32,33]. In this study, we developed an injectable CMCS-based therapeutic platform for localized delivery of Ce@Mo heterojunctions to modulate bone regeneration microenvironment. As a water-soluble chitosan (CS) derivative, CMCS not only exhibits more favorable biocompatibility, biodegradability and superior bioactivity compared to CS, but also demonstrates greater formulation versatility for developing various delivery systems including scaffolds, hydrogels and composites, making it an exceptionally promising material for bone tissue engineering applications [34,35]. However, the inadequate mechanical strength and poor tissue adhesion of CMCS significantly limit their clinical application. To overcome these obstacles, we designed a photopolymerizable hydrogel based on GelMA and CMCS loaded with Ce@Mo heterojunctions, which can adhere to the tissue surface by forming hydrogen bonds and electrostatic interactions with the extracellular matrix through its abundant hydroxyl and amino groups, thus serving as an effective local drug delivery platform for bone defect repair (Fig. 2A).

As shown in Fig. 2B, all hydrogel samples could rapidly form hydrogels after 30 s of UV irradiation. In addition, all samples exhibited excellent shape retention, maintaining their original structures in tilted bottles without flowing or collapsing, which confirmed the formation of a stable 3D crosslinked network. The FTIR analysis exhibited that the characteristic peaks of GelMA and CMCS mainly appeared in the GelMA-CMCS gel, in which the characteristic C-N peak at 1629 cm−1 served as confirmation of the reaction between the -COOH groups of CMCS and the -NH2 groups of GelMA, thereby verifying the successful conjugation of the two hydrogels (Fig. 2C). Moreover, we systematically evaluated the injectability, shape adaptability, and tissue adhesion properties of Ce@Mo/GC. The results demonstrated that the hydrogel can be smoothly extruded through an injection needle, self-adapt to complex shapes, and adhere to the tissue surface (Fig. S2), which provide significant advantages for local precise drug delivery and tissue integration. Subsequently, SEM was employed to examine the microscopic morphological features of the fabricated hydrogels, and the results are illustrated in Fig. 2D. All lyophilized hydrogel samples exhibited uniform and highly interconnected 3D porous structure with an average pore size of approximately 110 μm, while the incorporation of Ce@Mo demonstrated negligible influence on the porosity characteristics (Fig. 2E). This ideal pore architecture not only provides sufficient space for cell proliferation and migration but also facilitates efficient nutrient diffusion and metabolic waste removal. Additionally, elemental mapping analysis (EDS) demonstrated the highly uniform spatial distribution of Mo element throughout the hydrogel matrix, indicating the successful loading of heterojunctions within the hydrogel network (Fig. 2D).

Next, to evaluate the swelling properties, the hydrogels were immersed in PBS to monitor their dynamic swelling behavior. The results indicated that GelMA exhibited rapid initial liquid absorption within 120 min and then tended to stabilize in the subsequent time. Upon the incorporation of CMCS to fabricate GC and the subsequent introduction of Ce@Mo heterojunctions for preparing Ce@Mo/GC, although the swelling ratio exhibited a marginal reduction in comparison with that of GelMA, no statistically significant difference was observed among the samples (Fig. 2F). Meanwhile, as potential implant material, the appropriate biodegradability of hydrogel is of crucial importance. Therefore, the in vitro degradation behavior of Ce@Mo/GC hydrogels was evaluated by monitoring the residual mass in a simulated physiological solution containing collagenase. As shown in Fig. S3, GelMA underwent rapid degradation within 4 days and degraded by more than 50 % by the 8th day. In contrast, the incorporation of CMCS and the loading of Ce@Mo heterojunctions could reduce the degradation rate of the therapeutic system to a certain extent, which might be attributed to the enhanced molecular interactions within the system. This appropriate degradation rate provides sufficient time for the adhesion, proliferation and differentiation of osteoblasts, while gradually releasing the contained active components to create a favorable microenvironment for bone tissue regeneration.

In vitro evaluation of photothermal performance and antioxidant activity

Recently, composite hydrogels based on mild photothermal therapy have exhibited excellent potential in the field of bone defect repair, which can not only regulate the osteoimmune microenvironment by virtue of mild photothermal effects, but also integrate multiple functions such as antibacterial and antioxidant properties to synergistically promote bone healing [36,37]. Therefore, we investigated the photothermal performance of Ce@Mo/GC composite hydrogel under 808 nm NIR laser irradiation. As shown in Fig. 2G and H, compared with negligible temperature increase in GC, the Ce@Mo/GC rapidly heated up to approximately 42 °C within 5 min under 808 nm NIR laser irradiation (1.0 W cm−2), aligning with the optimal range for MPTT. Subsequently, the photothermal stability of Ce@Mo/GC was investigated by subjecting it to multiple cycles of laser irradiation. Following five consecutive irradiation cycles, the Ce@Mo/GC could maintain consistent temperature elevation profiles as evidenced in Fig. S4, demonstrating the exceptional photothermal stability of the MXene-based nanostructure. These results confirmed the promising applicability of prepared Ce@Mo/GC hydrogel for sustained photothermal therapy in clinical applications.

Next, considering the ideal photothermal properties of Ce@Mo/GC, we further investigated the effects of MPTT on the CAT-like and SOD-like activities of its antioxidative component Ce@Mo, which are essential for alleviating inflammation [38]. The hydroxyl radicals (•OH) scavenging capacity of Ce@Mo was first evaluated by electron spin resonance (ESR) spectra. As shown in Fig. 2I, the characteristic 1:2:2:1 •OH pattern generated by the Fenton reaction was significantly attenuated upon addition of Ce@Mo, with additional signal reduction achieved under 42 °C thermal conditions simulating mild photothermal therapy. In addition, the scavenging property of Ce@Mo towards superoxide anion (O2•-) was consistent with the ESR results of •OH, and the treatment with Ce@Mo + NIR can significantly reduce the characteristic peak of O2•- (Fig. S5). Subsequently, two commonly used probes, ABTS and TMB, were employed to further evaluate the antioxidant capacity of Ce@Mo. As shown in Fig. 2J, the cationic radical ABTS+• has an obvious absorption peak at 738 nm, while the addition of Ce@Mo and the implementation of mild photothermal therapy can reduce its characteristic absorption peak at 738 nm. Similarly, Ce@Mo can also significantly decrease the absorbance of TMB + •OH at 652 nm, which can be further reduced under mild photothermal stimulation (Fig. 2K). These free radical scavenging experiments and ESR spectroscopy strongly demonstrate that Ce@Mo has an ideal ROS scavenging capacity under mild photothermal conditions. Collectively, this mild photothermal-enhanced CAT-like and SOD-like activities make Ce@Mo a potentially effective therapeutic agent for promoting bone repair by reducing oxidative stress and inflammation.

Cytocompatibility of Ce@Mo/GC hydrogels

Excellent cytocompatibility is recognized as a crucial prerequisite for the in vivo application of biomedical implants [39]. Therefore, MC3T3-E1 cells and HUVECs were employed to investigate the biological safety of the Ce@Mo/GC composite hydrogel combined with MPTT, as they are closely associated with bone regeneration and angiogenesis [40]. As shown in Fig. 3A and C, the results of the CCK-8 assay demonstrated that both types of cells exhibited varying degrees of proliferation as the incubation time extended. Notably, the most significant increase in OD values was observed on the third day of co-incubation, indicating the synergistic effect of Ce@Mo/GC combined with mild thermal stimulation in promoting cell proliferation. Furthermore, the cellular viability and proliferative capacity were evaluated using live/dead cell staining and EdU incorporation assay. As shown in Fig. 3B and D, after three days of co-culture, MC3T3-E1 cells and HUVECs in all groups exhibited a favorable growth state and maintained their proliferative activity even under the combination of Ce@Mo and laser irradiation. Moreover, fluorescence images revealed a significantly higher proportion of EdU-positive cells in the Ce@Mo/GC + NIR group, further demonstrating the pro-proliferative effect of this strategy.

Given recent studies highlighting the pivotal role of cellular morphology in phenotype regulation, we further performed cytoskeleton staining to characterize cell adhesion and spreading morphology. As shown in Fig. 3E, after three days of co-culture, MC3T3-E1 cells adhered to Ce@Mo/GC exhibited desirable spreading morphology with well-developed cytoskeletal architecture and cell clusters. Notably, MC3T3-E1 cells in Ce@Mo/GC + NIR group displayed a typical elongated spindle shape and osteoblast-like morphology, extending numerous branched filopodia that intertwined closely with each other. Quantitative analysis results revealed that, compared with other groups, the synergistic effect of Ce@Mo/GC and mild photothermal stimulation could significantly promote cell adhesion and greatly increase cell spreading area, revealing a strong interaction between cells and the material surface (Fig. 3F and G). Meanwhile, similar to the cytoskeleton staining results of MC3T3-E1 cells, HUVECs in the Ce@Mo/GC + NIR group also exhibited a favorable growth state and an excellent stretched morphology (Fig. 3H–J). In conclusion, these results indicate that the mild photothermal therapy based on Ce@Mo/GC can effectively induce cytoskeleton rearrangement and promote cell proliferation, migration, which collectively provide a robust biological foundation for accelerating bone tissue regeneration.

In vitro evaluation of immunomodulatory performance

Related studies have confirmed that macrophage polarization plays a crucial role in regulating bone regeneration, in which M1 macrophages with pro-inflammatory properties can trigger local inflammatory responses at inflammation stage while M2 macrophages are capable of secreting a variety of anti-inflammatory factors, effectively promoting the neogenesis of bone tissues [41,42]. Unfortunately, osteoporotic bone defects are pathologically characterized by extensive infiltration of pro-inflammatory cytokines and excessive ROS accumulation, which could suppress osteogenic marker expression and matrix mineralization, inevitably increasing the risk of delayed bone healing [43]. In this study, BMDMs were stimulated with LPS to establish an acute inflammatory model characterized by localized ROS overproduction, followed by culturing on various hydrogels to evaluate the responses of macrophage (Fig. 4A). The cellular ROS levels after different treatments were detected through DCFH-DA staining. As shown in Fig. 4B and Fig. S6A, compared with the intense green fluorescence in the control group after LPS stimulation, the fluorescence signal in the Ce@Mo/GC group was significantly reduced, which could be further decreased after receiving mild photothermal stimulation. This antioxidant property is of great significance for regulating macrophage function, as excessive ROS can affect macrophage polarization and activity. The effective scavenging of ROS by Ce@Mo/GC may facilitate the reprogramming of macrophages toward beneficial phenotypes, thereby playing an active role in bone tissue repair.

(C) CD206 immunofluorescence staining and cytoskeleton staining of BMDMs after different treatments (scale bar: 50 μm). (D) Relative mRNA expression of CD206, IL-4 and IL-10 in BMDMs. (E) Differential gene expression analysis of BMDMs after different treatments. (F) Volcano plots presenting the upregulated and downregulated genes of BMDMs after treatment with Ce@Mo/GC + NIR. (G) Heatmap of DEGs with upregulated and downregulated patterns in the Ce@Mo/GC + NIR and control group. (H) GO and (I) KEGG enrichment analysis of DEGs of Ce@Mo/GC + NIR and control group.

Based on this, to further explore the effect of the combination of Ce@Mo/GC and MPTT on macrophage polarization, immunofluorescence staining and cytoskeleton staining were employed to investigate the phenotype markers and morphological changes of macrophages. As shown in Fig. 4C and S6B, a significant enhancement in the fluorescence intensity of CD206 (M2 macrophages marker) was observed in both Ce@Mo/GC and photothermal treatment groups, with the most pronounced effect achieved in the Ce@Mo/GC + NIR group. Moreover, the BMDMs treated with Ce@Mo/GC + NIR exhibited a distinct morphological transformation toward an M2 phenotype, characterized by elongated and flattened spindle-shape with extended pseudopodia. Meanwhile, real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) results further confirmed that the expression levels of M2 phenotype-related genes (CD206, IL-4, IL-10) in BMDMs were significantly upregulated after Ce@Mo/GC + NIR treatment (Fig. 4D). These findings confirmed that Ce@Mo/GC can not only promote M2 macrophage activation but also exhibit thermal-enhanced immunomodulatory effects.

More importantly, to investigate the molecular mechanism underlying macrophage polarization induced by Ce@Mo/GC combined with MPTT, RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis was employed to detect changes in the expression levels of messenger RNA (mRNA) in BMDMs under different treatment conditions. The results indicated that there were 1965 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the Ce@Mo/GC + NIR group and the control group, in which 1041 genes were upregulated and 924 genes were downregulated (Fig. 4E and F). Next, the differential gene expression changes were visualized via a gene expression heatmap, in which Ce@Mo/GC + NIR treatment enhanced the expression of M2 macrophage-associated phenotypic genes such as Retnla and Arg-1, suggesting that Ce@Mo/GC + NIR treatment can regulate the transcriptional program of BMDMs to skew them toward the M2 phenotype (Fig. 4G). Subsequently, gene ontology enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes revealed significant enrichment of “regulation of inflammatory response” and “negative regulation of immune system process” in biological processes, indicating that Ce@Mo/GC + NIR treatment modulates the inflammatory cascade and suppresses immune activation, consistent with inflammation resolution mediated by M2 macrophage polarization (Fig. 4H). KEGG pathway analysis of DEGs highlighted major signaling pathways related to immune system and inflammatory expression, such as the MAPK signaling pathway and the PPAR signaling pathway (Fig. 4I). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) confirmed the negative regulation of the immune response in the Ce@Mo/GC + NIR treatment group, which was consistent with the previous research results on M2 macrophage polarization and the regulation of local inflammatory responses (Fig. S7). Collectively, these results confirmed that the combination of Ce@Mo/GC hydrogel and MPTT exhibited reliable efficacy in inducing M2 macrophage polarization and significantly alleviating local inflammatory responses.

In vitro evaluation of immunomodulatory effects on osteogenesis and angiogenesis

During bone healing processes, the establishment of an optimized immune microenvironment could facilitate osteogenesis and angiogenesis through the secretion of various anti-inflammatory cytokines and growth factors by activated M2 macrophages [44,45]. Therefore, the conditioned medium of BMDMs treated with different regimens were collected to evaluate the impact of macrophage polarization on osteogenesis and angiogenesis. First, the proliferative capacity of endothelial cells was evaluated by detecting the expression level of Ki-67, a nuclear antigen closely associated with cell proliferation. As shown in Fig. 5A and S8A, the fluorescence intensity of Ki-67 in the Ce@Mo/GC + NIR was significantly enhanced, indicating the promotion effect of optimized immune microenvironment on angiogenesis. Then, the conditioned medium was further used in wound healing and tube formation assays. As shown in Fig. 5B, cell migration of HUVECs was observed in all experimental groups, with the most pronounced migration occurring in the Ce@Mo/GC + NIR group. Quantitative analysis further revealed that, after 24 h of culture, the wound healing rate of this group was significantly higher compared to other experimental groups (Fig. 5D). Furthermore, the tube formation assay further revealed that both Ce@Mo/GC and Ce@Mo/GC + NIR groups exhibited significantly enhanced angiogenic potential, as evidenced by more extensive and structurally intact vascular-like network formation, including a greater number of nodes, junctions, meshes, as well as a larger total meshes area (Fig. 5C and 5E–H).

Quantitative analysis of tube formation assay including (E) number of nodes, (F) number of junctions, (G) number of meshes and (H) total meshes area. (I) RUNX2 immunofluorescence staining of MC3T3-E1 cells after different treatments (scale bar: 50 μm). (J) ALP and ARS staining of MC3T3-E1cells after different treatments (scale bar: 500 μm).

Subsequently, immunofluorescence staining for the osteogenic marker RUNX2 was performed to investigate the impact of macrophage polarization on osteogenesis. As shown in Fig. 5I and S8B, the expression of osteogenic protein RUNX2 progressively increased with the introduction of Ce@Mo in the therapeutic strategy and the application of NIR treatment, as evidenced by the gradually enhanced red fluorescence. Additionally, to further evaluate the osteogenic activity of MC3T3-E1 cells, the activity of alkaline phosphatase (ALP), an early osteogenic differentiation marker, was assessed. As illustrate in Fig. 5J and S9A, compared with control group, the ALP staining in Ce@Mo/GC + NIR treatment group exhibited a more intense blue color, suggesting that the early osteogenic differentiation process was effectively promoted. Meanwhile, the late-stage osteogenic mineralization was detected by alizarin red (ARS) staining. It was found that the Ce@Mo/GC + NIR group formed a large number of dark red mineralized nodules with a significant increase in absorbance at 562 nm, demonstrating the outstanding advantages of this treatment protocol in inducing cell mineralization and promoting bone matrix formation (Fig. 5J and S9B). Taken together, the in vitro results of this study align well with prior findings, confirming that inducing macrophages M2 polarization play a pivotal positive role in angiogenesis, osteoblast differentiation and bone tissue regeneration.

In vivo assessment of immunomodulation, neovascularization and bone regeneration capability

Before investigating the in vivo therapeutic effect, to eliminate potential risks of the implant regarding hemocompatibility and ensure the reliability of subsequent animal experiments, the biosafety of the hydrogel was evaluated through a hemolysis assay. As shown in Fig. S10, the supernatant of the positive control group appeared bright red due to massive RBC lysis and hemoglobin release. In contrast, only negligible hemolysis was detected in the negative control and other groups, which confirms the favorable biosafety of prepared materials. Subsequently, building on the well-established efficacy of Ce@Mo/GC combined with NIR treatment in optimizing the osteoimmune microenvironment and promoting osteogenesis and angiogenesis from the in vitro studies, mice osteoporotic bone defect model was further employed to investigate the practical effectiveness of this treatment regimen in promoting bone regeneration in vivo. The Ce@Mo/GC composite hydrogels were implanted into the bone defect site at the distal femur in mice, followed by irradiation with an 808 nm laser. The results indicated that this therapeutic strategy can maintain the local temperature at approximately 42 °C, which can not only effectively promote bone regeneration, but also prevent tissue thermal damage caused by high temperature (Fig. 6A and S11). Relevant studies have confirmed that regulating macrophage polarization toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype during the initial stage of biomaterial implantation is pivotal for establishing a favorable immunomodulatory microenvironment, ultimately determining bone healing outcomes [46,47]. Therefore, immunofluorescence staining on femoral defect sites was performed to characterize the phenotype of macrophages recruited to the injury microenvironment under different treatment conditions. As shown in Fig. 6B–E, with the implantation of Ce@Mo/GC and the application of MPTT, the expression level of iNOS, a marker representing M1 macrophages was gradually decreased, while the immunofluorescence staining intensity of CD206 was increased significantly, indicating the effectiveness of this treatment strategy in regulating macrophage polarization in vivo. Furthermore, the anti-inflammatory properties of the implanted hydrogels were evaluated by detecting the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokine TNF-α and anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10. As shown in Fig. 6F and G, extensive infiltration of TNF-α was observed in the control group, demonstrating the intense inflammatory state following bone injury. In contrast, there was a significant downregulation of TNF-α expression and a substantial upward trend in IL-10 expression in the Ce@Mo/GC and Ce@Mo/GC + NIR treatment groups, in which the strongest red fluorescence signal representing IL-10 expression was observed in the Ce@Mo/GC + NIR group (Fig. 6F and H). These results demonstrated that Ce@Mo/GC combined with MPTT can reshape the inflammatory microenvironment at bone injury sites by suppressing pro-inflammatory factors and enhancing anti-inflammatory expression, thereby establishing an optimal immune foundation for bone regeneration.

Immunofluorescence staining of (B) iNOS (scale bar: 200 μm) and (D) CD206 (scale bar: 200 μm) in mice femoral defects and (C, E) quantitative analysis of corresponding positive staining areas. (F) Immunofluorescence staining of TNF-α (scale bar: 200 μm) and IL-10 (scale bar: 200 μm) in mice femoral defects and (G, H) quantitative analysis of corresponding positive staining areas.

Accumulating evidence has revealed that M2 macrophages play a pivotal role in orchestrating a regenerative environment by enhancing the expression of BMP-2 and VEGF, which act as powerful mediators to support the coordinated formation of bones and blood vessels [48]. Therefore, we further evaluated the expression of pro-regenerative factors at bone defect sites following different treatments through immunofluorescence staining. As expected, the expression levels of BMP-2 and VEGF exhibited a progressive upward trend across treatment groups, with the most substantial upregulation observed in the Ce@Mo/GC + NIR group (Fig. S12A and C). The quantitative analysis further corroborated this observation, demonstrating higher expression of these two pro-regenerative factors within the Ce@Mo/GC + NIR group (Fig. S12B and D). Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining for CD31, a specific marker for vascular endothelial cells, was performed to investigate neovascularization within the defect region. As shown in Fig. S12E and F, the Ce@Mo/GC + NIR group exhibited a remarkable increase in neovascular infiltration within the bone defect area, significantly surpassing that of the control group. Abundant CD31 positive endothelial cells formed a dense and well-organized microvascular network, confirming the enhancement of angiogenesis, which is conducive to providing the necessary nutrients and oxygen for subsequent bone regeneration.

In vivo evaluation of neurogenesis bioactivity

Recently, immunomodulatory strategies targeting bone microenvironment have been widely recognized as effective approaches for enhancing bone regeneration. However, the critical role of the nervous system in bone tissue engineering has largely been overlooked [49]. Emerging evidence indicated that sensory denervation may contribute to osteopenia and delayed fracture healing, while enhancing the release of calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) from peripheral sensory nerves can effectively promote bone regeneration [50,51]. Moreover, immunofluorescence staining of CGRP and RUNX2 in mouse embryo revealed a significant co-localization between sensory innervation and osteogenic marker, as evidenced by the presence of numerous CGRP+ nerves within the ossification center (Fig. 7A and B). These results not only underscore the potential crosstalk between neural and skeletal cells during bone development but also demonstrate that promoting nerve regeneration at bone defect sites is an effective strategy for enhancing bone regeneration. Given the extensive advancements of MXene in the field of nerve tissue engineering and regeneration as well as the ideal antioxidant capacity of CeO2, the potential of Ce@Mo/GC + NIR to induce nerve growth was further explored, aiming to reveal whether this innovative therapeutic strategy can contribute to sensory nerve regeneration and thereby establish a more comprehensive and synergistic approach for bone healing. As shown in Fig. 7C and D, the content of sensory nerves marked by CGRP and Nav1.8 remained persistently low in the control group, while the optimization of bone regeneration microenvironment by Ce@Mo/GC and the intervention of MPTT, the sensory innervation in the Ce@Mo/GC and Ce@Mo/GC + NIR group was significantly enhanced. These results demonstrated that the Ce@Mo/GC-based treatment strategies can actively promote neural remodeling at the injury site and creating a more favorable microenvironment for bone regeneration (Fig. 7E).

In vivo evaluation of bone regeneration

Inspired by this multi-modal synergistic bone repair strategy, the in-situ bone repair capacity of Ce@Mo/GC was further evaluated using micro-CT, histological staining, and immunofluorescence staining according to the procedure illustrated in Fig. 8A. The micro-CT 3D reconstruction images of the distal femur bone defect sites following various treatments demonstrated superior bone regeneration in all treatment groups compared to control group (Fig. 8B). Notably, the osteogenic effect of the Ce@Mo/GC group was significantly better than that of GC group, which highlights the crucial role of inducing macrophage M2 polarization, alleviating excessive oxidative stress, and facilitating neurogenesis in creating a microenvironment conducive to bone regeneration. Furthermore, the most significant bone formation was observed in the Ce@Mo/GC + NIR group, characterized by extensive trabecular bone generation within the defect region, indicating the synergistic enhancement effect of MPTT on bone regeneration. Additionally, quantitative analysis of micro-CT scanning revealed that the Ce@Mo/GC + NIR group not only achieved the highest bone volume fraction (BV/TV, 45.2 % ± 3.72 %), approximately 2.45 times that of the control group, but also exhibited optimal trabecular number (Tb.N), the greatest trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), the smallest trabecular separation (Tb.Sp) and the highest bone mineral density (BMD) (Fig. 8C–F and S13). The results of these bone morphological parameters demonstrated excellent osteogenic performance of the Ce@Mo/GC + NIR treatment strategy and provide strong evidence for the effectiveness of this strategy in optimizing the microenvironment for bone regeneration. Subsequently, the histomorphology and regeneration efficacy of the newly formed bone at the defect site were analyzed using H&E staining and Masson's trichrome staining. As shown in Fig. 8G and H, compared with the abundant fibrous tissues in the control group, more trabecular bone structures were observed in Ce@Mo/GC and Ce@Mo/GC + NIR group, which is consistent with the results of micro-CT analysis. Furthermore, the osteogenic potential of this therapeutic strategy was evaluated through immunofluorescence staining of the osteogenic markers RUNX2 and Sp7. As shown in Fig. 8I–K, the expression of osteogenic markers in the bone defect area treated with Ce@Mo/GC + NIR was significantly higher than those in control group and GC group, indicating the desirable effect of the combination of Ce@Mo/GC and MPTT in enhancing the expression of key proteins related to bone formation and accelerating the deposition of bone matrix. Collectively, the in vivo results demonstrated that Ce@Mo/GC + NIR can not only alleviate inflammation by reducing oxidative stress and inducing macrophages to polarize towards an anti-inflammatory phenotype, thereby creating favorable conditions for tissue regeneration, but also promote neurogenesis, which is conducive to the establishment of crucial nerve-bone crosstalk and further enhance the osteogenic effect.

Conclusion

In summary, in this study, a multifunctional hydrogel (Ce@Mo/GC) capable of coordinating multidimensional biological processes involved in bone regeneration has been designed to promote the healing of osteoporotic bone defects. Leveraging the photothermal conversion capability of MXene, the Ce@Mo heterojunction-based therapeutic platform can achieve a locally mild photothermal-enhanced valence state transition of Ce3+/Ce4+ under NIR irradiation, which could effectively alleviate the excessive oxidative stress at the bone injury site and induce the polarization of macrophages towards the M2 phenotype. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments have demonstrated that the combination of Ce@Mo/GC with NIR irradiation can not only exert potent immunomodulatory effect by stimulating the secretion of various anti-inflammatory cytokines and growth factors, thereby significantly accelerating osteogenesis and angiogenesis, but also demonstrate the ability to promote sensory neurogenesis. This multifaceted functionality underscores the unique value of our therapeutic system in orchestrating neuro-bone crosstalk and achieving high-quality tissue regeneration. In summary, the development of this multi-modal synergistic therapeutic strategy not only establishes a new paradigm for treating refractory bone defects by orchestrating the complex biological processes involved in bone regeneration, but also suggests a promising path for developing advanced intelligent biomaterials capable of coordinating the intricate interactions within the bone repair microenvironment.
