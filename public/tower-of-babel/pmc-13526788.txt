Rumen-derived Pichia membranifaciens modulates the rumen microbiome and metabolome and mitigates methane emissions in dairy cows
Authors: Jichao Li, Xu Liang, Pengyu Liu, Weiyun Zhu, Wei Jin, Shengyong Mao, Fei Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMethane emissions from ruminants represent a significant environmental challenge and dietary energy loss. While yeasts are potential rumen modulators, specific methane-mitigating species remain poorly characterized. Here, we screened 73 rumen-derived strains in vitro, identifying Pichia membranifaciens M12 as the most effective candidate, reducing methane output by 17.1%. Subsequently, a randomized block trial with 36 dairy cows compared a control group with P. membranifaciens M12 supplementation at 2.5 and 5 × 1011 CFU/cow/day. Methane yield per unit of dry matter intake significantly decreased in the high-dose group (18.7%, P = 0.003), without compromising lactation performance and animal health. Multi-omics analyses revealed that M12 suppressed hydrogenotrophic methanogens (e.g., Methanobrevibacter) and hydrogen-producing bacteria (e.g., Ruminococcus and Fibrobacter), while enriching specific eukaryotic taxa like Orpinomyces and Entodinium. Metabolomic profiling indicated a significant dose-dependent accumulation of metabolites. Metagenomic function analysis demonstrated the decreased abundance of key methanogenesis genes (e.g., mcrABCDG) and increased abundance of hydrogenase (hyaABC), lactate-forming (ghrB), and propionate-forming (mcmA1 and lcdB), suggesting a redirection of reducing equivalents from methanogenesis toward propionate synthesis, alongside enhanced butyrate production. These findings demonstrate that P. membranifaciens M12 mitigates methane emissions via coordinated ecological and metabolic modulation, highlighting its potential as a sustainable strategy for low-carbon ruminant production.Subject terms: Biotechnology, Microbiology

Introduction

Methane (CH4) is a potent greenhouse gas with a substantial global warming potential1. Rumen fermentation in ruminant livestock is a major CH4 source, accounting for approximately 30% of global anthropogenic emissions and representing up to 12% of dietary energy loss2,3. Therefore, developing strategies to mitigate rumen CH4 production is critical for reducing the environmental impact while improving feed efficiency. The rumen microbiome has a vast taxonomic diversity and plays a central role in methanogenesis4–7. Among various intervention strategies, microbial feed additives, particularly yeasts, have garnered significant interest for their potential to modify rumen fermentation and directly or indirectly suppress methanogenic activity8–10. Yeasts are regarded as promising additives due to their favorable safety profile, ecological adaptability, and multifunctional benefits8,11,12. The most widely studied yeast, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, demonstrates multiple beneficial modes of action, including rumen pH stabilization, promotion of fibrolytic and lactate-utilizing bacteria, and enhanced feed efficiency and milk production10,13,14. While its stimulation of fibrolytic activity may increase hydrogen (H2) production, it concurrently promotes propionate-producing bacteria that compete with methanogens for H2, thereby diverting reductants away from methane production15–17. However, the efficacy of such commercial yeast strains in reducing CH4 emissions is highly variable. This inconsistency is often attributed to species-specific properties and the poor ecological adaptation of non-rumen yeasts, which typically fail to achieve sustained effects in the rumen8,18,19. In contrast, rumen yeasts possess inherent ecological adaptability, enabling colonization and interaction with the resident microbiota20,21. These traits position them as excellent candidates for rumen CH4 mitigation. Nevertheless, research dedicated to these indigenous, methane-reducing yeasts remains limited, and a comprehensive understanding of their impact on rumen ecology and metabolism, particularly through integrated multi-omics approaches, is still lacking.

To address this gap, we isolated and characterized native rumen yeasts and performed functional screening in an in vitro rumen fermentation system. The most promising isolate, Pichia membranifaciens M12, was then evaluated in a dairy cow trial to assess its effects on methane emissions, animal health, and productivity. We further integrated a multi-omics approach, including 16S/18S rRNA gene sequencing, metagenomics, and non-targeted metabolomics. The results provided insights into the function of rumen-derived yeasts for CH4 mitigation, supporting the development of novel strategies for sustainable livestock production.

Results

Isolating rumen yeast species and evaluating its effects on rumen fermentation in vitro

Through gradient dilution methodology, we isolated and purified 73 yeast strains from cow rumen fluid (Fig. 1A; Table S1). Taxonomic analysis based on ITS sequencing classified all isolates into the phylum Ascomycota, spanning 7 genera (Pichia, Saccharomyces, Cyberlindnera, Wickerhamomyces, Trichomonascus, Galactomyces, and Candida) and 11 species (P. membranifaciens, P. kudriavzevii, S. cerevisiae, S. mikatae, Cy. fabianii, Cy. jadinii, W. anomalus, T. asahii, G. pseudocandidus, Ca. tropicalis, and Ca. catenulata) (Fig. 1B). The growth curve of representative strains from each species showed sustained proliferation, with most yeasts reaching peak growth within 48 h (Fig. 1C).

To preliminarily screen their effects on rumen fermentation, we performed an in vitro fermentation assay using 11 representative yeast species (Fig. 1D; Table S1). Compared with the CON group, most yeasts significantly increased pH (P < 0.001), with Ca. tropicalis B13 showing the most pronounced increase (Fig. 1D). Analysis of volatile fatty acid (VFA) profiles revealed species-specific variations, with Cy. fabianii B49 exhibited the highest concentration of total VFAs. P. membranifaciens M12 showed no significant differences in the concentrations of total VFAs and acetate, while significantly decreasing butyrate concentration (P < 0.001) (Fig. 1D; Table S2). Ca. catenulata Y9 significantly increased acetate concentration (P = 0.011) (Table S2). With the exception of T. asahii B2, most species significantly increased both concentration and molar proportion of propionate (P < 0.05), with P. membranifaciens M12 showing the highest molar proportion (Table S2). Several species significantly decreased the concentration of isobutyrate, and G. pseudocandidus B17 showed the strongest decrease (P < 0.001) (Table S2). Similarly, multiple species lowered valerate concentration (P < 0.05), with Ca. tropicalis B13 exhibiting the greatest reduction (P < 0.001) (Table S2). These results indicated that the influence of yeast species on rumen fermentation is highly strain-specific.

We further evaluated the potential of these yeast species to mitigate CH4 production through gas chromatographic analysis of fermentation gas (Fig. 1D; Table S3). Total gas production was significantly reduced by several species: W. anomalus Y3 (P = 0.021), Cy. jadinii M7 (P = 0.014), Ca. catenulata Y9 (P = 0.002), P. membranifaciens M12 (P < 0.001), Ca. tropicalis B13 (P = 0.024), and G. pseudocandidus B17 (P = 0.025). Among these, P. membranifaciens M12 showed the greatest inhibition, reducing total gas output by 13.5% compared to the CON group (Fig. 1D; Table S3). Significant reductions in H2 production were observed with P. kudriavzevii Y4 (P = 0.006), Cy. jadinii M7 (P = 0.016), and P. membranifaciens M12 (P = 0.008) (Fig. 1D; Table S3). CH4 production was markedly suppressed by P. membranifaciens M12 (P = 0.003), P. kudriavzevii Y4 (P = 0.008), Cy. jadinii M7 (P = 0.031), and G. pseudocandidus B17 (P = 0.01), while T. asahii B2 significantly increased CH4 output (P = 0.01) (Fig. 1D; Table S3). Notably, P. membranifaciens M12 showed the greatest efficacy, achieving a 17.1% reduction in methane production relative to the CON group, followed by P. kudriavzevi Y4 with a 15.5% reduction (Fig. 1D; Table S3). Compared to the CON group, both P. membranifaciens M12 and P. kudriavzevi Y4 significantly decreased relative methane production (see methods) by 15.5% (P = 0.007) and 16.4% (P = 0.004) (Table S3), respectively. Given the higher molar proportion of propionate in the M12 group, we selected it as the optimal candidate.

Effects of P. membranifaciens M12 supplementation on methane production, productivity, and health in dairy cows

We next conducted a feeding trial to evaluate the effects of P. membranifaciens M12 on lactating dairy cows (Fig. 2A). An assessment of gaseous emissions revealed that the PM5 treatment significantly reduced several key methane-related parameters, including daily methane production by 15.4% (P = 0.015) compared to the CON group (Table S4), methane yield per unit of dry matter intake (DMI) by 18.7% (P = 0.003), and methane intensity per unit of energy-corrected milk (ECM) by 17.7% (P = 0.013), whereas the PM2.5 treatment showed no significant effects on these parameters (Fig. 2B; Table S4). Supplementation with P. membranifaciens M12 did not significantly affect DMI or the apparent degradability of dry matter (DM), crude protein (CP), and acid detergent fiber (ADF) compared to the CON group (Fig. 2B; Table S5). However, the PM5 group exhibited significantly higher ether extract (EE) degradability and lower neutral detergent fiber (NDF) degradability compared to the CON group (P = 0.008 and P = 0.036, respectively) (Fig. 2B; Table S5). Lactation performance, including milk production, 4% fat-corrected milk (FCM), and ECM, was not significantly affected by P. membranifaciens M12 supplementation compared to the CON group (Fig. 2B; Table S6). Milk composition (fat, lactose, urea nitrogen, and total solids) did not differ significantly by P. membranifaciens M12 supplementation compared to the CON group (Table S6). However, the milk protein percentage was significantly lower in the PM2.5 group than in the CON group, while the absolute milk protein yield remained unaffected (Table S6). To assess potential metabolic effects, serum biochemical parameters were analyzed, and no significant differences were observed in protein metabolism indicators, including total protein, albumin, globulin, and albumin-to-globulin ratio, in either of the P. membranifaciens M12 treatment groups compared to the CON group (Table S7). Serum urea nitrogen and creatinine levels, which reflect protein metabolism and nitrogen utilization, also did not differ between the treatment groups and the CON group (Table S7). Similarly, energy metabolism and lipid homeostasis markers, including glucose, free fatty acids, triglycerides, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, were not significantly affected by either treatment compared to the CON group (Table S7). Hepatic function parameters, including activities of alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, alkaline phosphatase, and total bilirubin concentration, remained unchanged in both treatment groups relative to the CON group (Table S7). Collectively, these results demonstrated that supplementation with P. membranifaciens M12 at the tested doses mitigates methane emissions without compromising production performance and animal health.

Effects of P. membranifaciens M12 supplementation on rumen fermentation in dairy cows

To investigate the effects of P. membranifaciens M12 on rumen fermentation function, we evaluated basic physicochemical parameters and found no significant differences in pH, redox potential, ammonia nitrogen concentration, and lactate concentration among the groups (Fig. 2C; Table S8). Similarly, the concentrations of total VFAs, propionate, butyrate, isobutyrate, isovalerate, and valerate, as well as the acetate-to-propionate ratio, exhibited no significant differences (Fig. 2C; Table S8). In contrast, the PM2.5 treatment significantly decreased the concentration of acetate (P = 0.013) compared to the CON group, with its molar proportion remaining unchanged. Furthermore, the PM5 group exhibited a significantly higher molar proportion of butyrate (P = 0.004), while the proportions of propionate, isobutyrate, isovalerate, and valerate remained consistent between the CON group and the PM2.5 or PM5 groups (Fig. 2C; Table S8). To determine whether these fermentation changes affected microbial protein synthesis, we assessed urinary excretion of purine derivatives (PD), and found no significant differences among the groups in the excretion of allantoin, uric acid, and endogenous PD, estimated PD absorption, as well as microbial protein (MCP) yield (Table S9). We further evaluated the efficacy of P. membranifaciens M12 in mitigating rumen methane emissions. Gas chromatographic analysis of rumen fluid revealed significant dose-dependent alterations in dissolved gas profiles (Table S4). Supplementation with different doses of P. membranifaciens M12 significantly reduced dissolved methane concentrations (P < 0.001). Notably, dissolved hydrogen concentrations were also significantly decreased in the PM2.5 group (P = 0.017) (Table S4). In summary, supplementation with P. membranifaciens M12 modulated rumen fermentation patterns and dissolved gas profiles without adversely affecting nitrogen metabolism.

P. membranifaciens M12 supplementation altered the rumen microbial biomass, structure, and composition in dairy cows

To elucidate the microbial mechanisms underlying the alterations in rumen fermentation induced by P. membranifaciens M12, we quantified the absolute abundances of bacteria, archaea, fungi, and protozoa using quantitative PCR and profiled their communities across all groups via 16S and 18S rRNA gene sequencing. Quantification results revealed no significant differences in absolute abundance among the CON, PM2.5, and PM5 groups (Table S10). After quality filtering, a total of 860,457 bacterial, 850,110 archaeal, and 830,990 eukaryotic high-quality sequences were obtained, with per-sample averages of 35,852, 35,272, and 34,625, respectively (Table S11). Clustering of these sequences yielded 5865 bacterial amplicon sequence variants (ASVs), 3523 archaeal ASVs, and 73,686 eukaryotic ASVs (Table S11).

Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) based on ASV abundances revealed a significant separation of protozoal communities among all groups (P = 0.022), while the overall structures of bacterial, archaeal, and fungal communities were not significantly changed (Fig. 3A). Alpha diversity indices for bacterial, fungal, and protozoal communities also showed no significant differences among the groups (Fig. 3B, C; Table S12). In contrast, archaeal alpha diversity was significantly higher in the PM2.5 group than in both the CON and PM5 groups, as indicated by increases in the number of observed ASVs (P = 0.039), ACE (P = 0.043), and Chao1 (P = 0.039) indices, whereas the Shannon and Simpson indices showed no significant differences among the groups (Fig. 3B, C; Table S12). Because significant shifts in observed ASVs were detected exclusively within the archaeal community, a subsequent Venn analysis focused on these populations showed that a total of 705 archaeal ASVs were shared among all groups, while 488 and 648 ASVs were unique to the PM2.5 and PM5 groups, respectively (Fig. S1A). Taxonomic annotation revealed that unclassified taxa dominated the unique ASVs of all treatments at the order level, with Methanosarcinales and Methanomicrobiales being the most represented classified orders. At the genus level, the composition of unique ASVs varied among treatments: the CON group and the PM5 group were dominated by Methanosaeta, while the PM2.5 group was dominated by Methanobrevibacter (Fig. S1B–D).

Taxonomic profiling revealed that bacterial communities were dominated by Bacteroidota (57.1%), Bacillota (26.6%), and Pseudomonadota (10%), with abundant genera including Xylanibacter (37.6%), Rikenellaceae RC9 gut group (5.2%), and Succinivibrionaceae UCG-002 (4.7%) (Fig. 4A). Archaeal sequences were exclusively affiliated with Euryarchaeota, encompassing seven major orders. Among these, Methanobacteriales (25.7%), Methanosarcinales (24.1%), and Methanomicrobiales (13.5%) were predominant. At the genus level, Methanobrevibacter (23.6%), Methanimicrococcus (15%), Methanosaeta (7.4%), and Methanoculleus (7.7%) represented the dominant taxa (Fig. 4B). Fungal communities were dominated by Chytridiomycota (66.8%), Ascomycota (22.4%), and Basidiomycota (10.8%), with Piromyces (28.5%) and Neocallimastix (23.6%) as key genera (Fig. 4C). Protozoal taxa mainly belonged to Entodiniomorphida (55.7%) and Vestibuliferida (44.2%), dominated by Entodinium (52.1%), Isotricha (35.3%), and Dasytricha (6%) (Fig. 4D). Differential abundance analysis indicated that the abundances of Spirochaetota (P = 0.046) and Fibrobacterota (P = 0.021) were decreased in the PM5 group compared to the CON group. At the genus level, compared to the CON group, Ruminococcus (P = 0.005), Treponema (P = 0.041), Saccharofermentans (P = 0.021), and Fibrobacter (P = 0.024) were significantly decreased in the PM5 group, whereas Lacrimispora (P = 0.026) and Lachnospiraceae FCS020 (P = 0.01) were significantly increased in the PM2.5 group (Figs. 4A and S2A). Within the archaea, the PM2.5 group suppressed Methanobacteriales (P = 0.041), Methanococcales (P = 0.016), Methanobrevibacter (P = 0.031), and Methanocaldococcus (P = 0.018), while enriching Methanomassiliicoccales Group 12 (P = 0.014) and Methanolinea (P = 0.024) compared to the CON group (Figs. 4B and S2B). Compared to the CON group, fungal profiles revealed that Ceramothyrium was significantly decreased in both the PM2.5 (P = 0.014) and PM5 groups (P = 0.031). Additionally, significant reductions were observed for Orpinomyces in the PM5 group (P = 0.041) and Aspergillus (P = 0.026) in the PM2.5 group (Figs. 4C and S2C). Protozoal profiles revealed an enrichment of Entodinium (P = 0.031) in the PM5 group and an increase of Ophryoscolex (P = 0.023) in the PM2.5 group compared to the CON group (Figs. 4D and S2D).

Correlation network analysis revealed a multi-kingdom structure with dense interconnections among bacteria, archaea, fungi, and protozoa (Fig. 4E, F). Central nodes included bacteria (Ruminococcus and Fibrobacter), methanogens (Methanobrevibacter and Methanocaldococcus), fungi (Orpinomyces), and protozoa (Entodinium and Ophryoscolex). Positive associations were predominant, indicating cooperative metabolic interactions. Methanobrevibacter was positively correlated with CH4 production and the concentration of total VFAs, whereas Methanolinea showed negative correlations with CH4 production and the concentration of propionate. Ruminococcus, Treponema, and Fibrobacter were positively associated with the concentrations of acetate and total VFAs. Aspergillus exhibited strong positive correlations with the concentration of total VFAs, while Ceramothyrium was positively correlated with the concentrations of propionate and dissolved methane. In addition, Entodinium and Ophryoscolex were negatively correlated with the concentrations of multiple VFAs, including acetate, propionate, and total VFAs.

To assess the occurrence of P. membranifaciens in the rumen ecosystem, we examined 18S rRNA sequencing, which revealed that the genus Pichia accounted for 3.1% of the total fungal community in the CON group (Fig. S3A). Metagenomic data further confirmed the presence of P. membranifaciens in all CON samples (n = 8) (Fig. S3C), with a mean relative abundance of approximately 0.3% among total fungal reads, supporting its ecological adaptation to the rumen environment. Combined analysis of 18S rRNA and metagenomic data showed no significant differences in the relative abundance of the genus Pichia or species P. membranifaciens among treatments (Fig. S3B, D), suggesting that the yeast supplementation did not alter their overall population levels.

P. membranifaciens M12 supplementation induced modifications in the rumen metabolome

We next conducted metabolomic profiling of rumen fluid to characterize the functional metabolic consequences. A total of 3836 metabolites were shared among the CON, PM2.5, and PM5 groups, while 28, 20, and 15 unique metabolites were identified in each group, respectively (Fig. 5A). PCA and PLS-DA did not reveal clear separation among the three groups (Fig. 5B). Comparative analysis identified 136 differentially abundant metabolites (46 upregulated and 90 downregulated) between the PM2.5 and CON groups, 615 metabolites (164 upregulated and 451 downregulated) between the PM5 and CON groups, and 255 metabolites (124 upregulated and 131 downregulated) between the PM2.5 and PM5 groups (Figs. 5C and S4A). Compared to the CON group, the PM2.5 group exhibited a marked increase in creatinine and decreases in metabolites related to amino acid and alkaloid metabolism, such as tyrosyl-alanyl-glycine (Fig. 5C). The PM5 group showed significant enrichment of an aromatic amide compound and reduction of metabolites involved in terpenoid and flavonoid biosynthesis, such as kadethrin (Fig. 5C). Furthermore, the PM5 and PM2.5 comparison revealed dose-dependent differential metabolites primarily associated with nitrogen and secondary metabolite metabolism (Fig. 5C).

Further comparison analysis identified that seven metabolites (cuminaldehyde, 1,4-ipomeadiol, 3,8-dihydroxy-6-methoxy-7(11)-eremophilen-12,8-olide, stigmasterol, viridin, 3-methyloxindole, and 2-mercaptobenzothiazole), which are known for their antimicrobial, anti-protozoal, and redox-modulating activities, were significantly enriched in the PM2.5 group (Fig. 5D). Pathway enrichment analysis indicated that the PM2.5 group was significantly differed in arginine biosynthesis, linoleic acid, biotin, sphingolipid, and pyrimidine metabolism (Fig. 6A). In contrast, the PM5 group was primarily differed in linoleic acid, vitamin B6, tryptophan, purine, and nicotinate/nicotinamide metabolism (Fig. 6B). Direct comparison between the PM5 and PM2.5 groups revealed alterations in amino acid and vitamin B6 metabolism (Fig. 6C). Two-dimensional fold-change analysis identified commonly upregulated metabolites (e.g., N2-acetylornithine and 3’-deoxy-3’-fluorothymidine) and downregulated metabolites (e.g., L-nicotine) across the groups (Fig. S4B–D). Dose–response analysis further revealed accumulation of 2,6-diaminopimelic acid, cholic acid, 7-hydroxy-5-methoxyflavan, and sakuranetin, alongside depletion of raffinose and maltotetraose (Fig. 7A). Random forest analysis identified that the top discriminant metabolites were plant-derived alkaloids and monoterpenoids with recognized anti-methanogenic potential, including nigellicine, (1S,2S,4R,8R)-p-menthane-1,2,9-triol, and (1R,2R,4R)-1,8-epoxy-p-menthane-2,4-diol (Fig. 7B). Collectively, these findings indicated that supplementation with P. membranifaciens M12 triggered metabolic reprogramming in the rumen.

P. membranifaciens M12 supplementation altered rumen microbial methanogenesis

We performed a comparative analysis of functional gene abundance based on metagenomic sequencing and found alterations in metabolic pathways related to methanogenesis, VFA generation, and amino acid metabolism (Fig. 8). In the PM2.5 group, the abundance of genes involved in key methanogenesis (mcrABCDG, hdr, mvh, and mch) was significantly decreased, while the abundance of genes involved in formate-dependent and methylotrophic methanogenesis, such as fdol, mtmB, and mtbC, was increased (Fig. 8), suggesting a compensatory shift toward alternative methanogenic substrates. Furthermore, the abundance of genes involved in amino acid and pyruvate metabolism (vorAD, HMGCS, and pycB) was decreased in the PM2.5 group, whereas the abundance of mcmA1 and lcdB was increased (Fig. 8). In the PM5 group, compared to the CON group, the abundance of genes essential for hydrogenotrophic and methylotrophic methanogenesis, such as mtd, mtrAD, fwdE, mvhD, cofDE, and mauAB, was decreased (Fig. 8). The abundance of genes involved in central carbon metabolism and VFA precursor formation, including porABCD, adhA, fumA, argH, EC:4.1.2.13 (fructose-bisphosphate aldolase), EC:3.1.3.11 (fructose-bisphosphatase), mfnF, puuE, DAK, AGXT, fdhA, and mtdB, was also decreased in the PM5 group (Fig. 8). Conversely, the abundance of torZ, eutE, and ghrB genes was increased in the PM5 group (Fig. 8). Compared to the CON group, the PM2.5 group exhibited the highest abundance of hyaA, hyaB, and hyaC genes that encode subunits of a hydrogenase which primarily catalyzes the oxidation of hydrogen (Fig. S5). These hydrogenase genes were mainly associated with Bacillota_C, particularly with genera such as Selenomonas_A, RUG12372, and Selenomonas_B. The abundance of these associated taxa significantly increased in the PM2.5 group compared with the CON group (P < 0.05). Specifically, the increased abundance of hyaA and hyaC genes was significantly associated with the enrichment of Selenomonas_A and Selenomonas_B, while the increased abundance of the hyaB gene was significantly associated with the enrichment of Selenomonas_A (Fig. S5). Collectively, supplementation with P. membranifaciens M12 suppressed rumen microbial methanogenic metabolism, redirecting hydrogen flux to alternative fermentation routes.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the effects of rumen-derived yeasts via an integrated approach involving strain isolation, in vitro fermentation, in vivo trials, and multi-omics analyses. Our results demonstrated the ability of P. membranifaciens M12 to mitigate rumen methane emissions without compromising host productivity and health. Unlike S. cerevisiae, which typically exhibits no anti-methanogenic activity8,22,23, P. membranifaciens M12 markedly inhibited methane production, with the greatest reduction observed at a dosage of 5 × 1011 CFU/day. Notably, supplementation at 2.5 × 1011 CFU/day significantly decreased the concentrations of dissolved methane and hydrogen, although methane emissions were not significantly reduced.

In a preliminary comparative selection in vitro, P. membranifaciens M12 redirected rumen fermentation toward propionate and reduced methane output by 17.1%. While P. membranifaciens M12 significantly decreased hydrogen production, it did not compromise total gas or total VFA concentrations. Instead, the concurrent increases in propionate concentration and its molar proportion indicate a highly efficient rerouting of metabolic hydrogen from methanogenesis toward propionogenesis19. Therefore, P. membranifaciens M12 was selected as the candidate for the in vivo evaluation. The in vivo performance of P. membranifaciens M12 confirmed its function, though future studies should include a wider range of assays to verify broad validity. As individual strains possess distinct preferences that include enzymatic efficiencies, nutrient utilization, and hydrogen-scavenging capacities, their effects on the rumen microbiome may vary16. The strain M12 likely exhibits superior niche adaptation within the rumen, allowing it to effectively interact with other rumen microbes24. This strain-level variation is crucial for developing targeted and reliable microbial feed additives for ruminant methane mitigation.

In vivo supplementation also confirmed that P. membranifaciens M12 does not negatively affect milk production, milk composition, and systemic metabolism, aligning with prior reports on other yeast probiotics25–27. The observed reduction in NDF digestibility likely reflects a disruption of the specific syntrophic associations between methanogens and cellulolytic microbes, rather than thermodynamic inhibition resulting from hydrogen accumulation. This energy deficit from reduced fiber degradation could potentially be offset by the conservation of gross energy from reduced methane emissions and an enhanced ether extract degradability. Moreover, although the absolute concentration of acetate decreased, its molar proportion remained unchanged, suggesting a reduction in fermentation intensity, consistent with the in vitro observations. Unchanged microbial protein synthesis supported the maintenance of nitrogen metabolism28, indicating that P. membranifaciens M12 selectively reduces methanogenesis. These findings provide a basis for the targeted anti-methanogenic effects of P. membranifaciens M12 and motivate further investigation into its influence on the rumen microbial community.

The supplementation of P. membranifaciens M12 selectively regulated certain microbial taxa, with the PM5 group suppressing key hydrogen-producing fibrolytic bacteria such as Ruminococcus and Fibrobacter29–31. In contrast, the PM2.5 group enriched carbohydrate-fermenting bacteria (Lacrimispora and Lachnospiraceae FCS020)32, potentially supporting VFA production. Furthermore, the PM2.5 group reduced the abundance of major hydrogenotrophic methanogens (Methanobrevibacter)33 and enriched the methyl-dependent lineage Methanomassiliicoccales Group 1234, as well as Methanolinea (another hydrogenotrophic methanogen)35. This shift in methanogenic pathways may account for the marked decrease in dissolved methane at this dosage, whereas gaseous methane remained unchanged. Notably, a large proportion of the methanogen sequences in our amplicon analysis remained unclassified, highlighting a widely recognized limitation of current archaeal reference databases. In addition, increased cellulolytic fungi (Orpinomyces) and selective stimulation of protozoal taxa (Entodinium in PM5; Ophryoscolex in PM2.5)36,37 support multi-kingdom ecological restructuring. The detection of unexpected environmental taxa such as Aspergillus is likely attributable to the universal primers and the database limitation. These rumen eukaryotes promote methanogenesis through interspecies hydrogen transfer to methanogens38. However, because P. membranifaciens M12 suppressed the dominant hydrogenotrophic methanogens, this process was likely influenced.

Metabolomic profiling revealed dose-dependent changes in cholic acid, which is reported to suppress methane formation39,40, and 2,6-diaminopimelic acid, whose accumulation suggests enhanced microbial growth and hydrogen utilization41,42. Concurrent depletion of fermentable carbohydrates (raffinose and maltotetraose) suggests an increased microbial utilization of these substrates43. Several enriched bioactive metabolites, including the flavonoids 7-hydroxy-5-methoxyflavan and sakuranetin, the antibiotic viridin, and the sterol stigmasterol, exhibited known antimicrobial or antioxidant properties, suggesting their potential role in microbial modulation. Additionally, terpenoids such as 3,8-dihydroxy-6-methoxy-7(11)-eremophilen-12,8-olide and (1R,2R,4R)-1,8-epoxy-p-menthane-2,4-diol, along with the aromatic aldehyde cuminaldehyde, were also enriched. These compound classes have previously been linked to anti-methanogenic effects in studies involving plant essential oils and cinnamaldehyde44–50. By disrupting microbial cell membrane integrity and energetic metabolism51, they may exert selective pressure against dominant hydrogenotrophic methanogens52. However, these compounds should not be over-interpreted as isolated ones, but they most likely act as synergistic mediators within the rumen ecosystem. Notably, the detection of atypical xenobiotics (e.g., 2-mercaptobenzothiazole) likely reflects either exogenous contaminants from the diet or uncharacterized endogenous metabolites annotated as synthetic structural analogs. At the pathway level, low-dose supplementation primarily influenced amino acid and lipid metabolism (e.g., arginine biosynthesis, linoleic acid, and sphingolipid pathways), consistent with improved nitrogen utilization53. In contrast, high-dose supplementation triggered broader metabolic reprogramming, affecting vitamin B6, tryptophan, purine, and nicotinate metabolism. For example, reduced pyridoxal levels may influence amino acid metabolism54, while accumulation of 5-hydroxy-L-tryptophan suggests potential roles in immune modulation and microbial homeostasis55. These shifts indicate that P. membranifaciens M12 remodels rumen metabolism to mitigate methane emissions.

Shifts in rumen microbial gene abundance further supported the methane-mitigating role of P. membranifaciens M12. In the PM2.5 group, the reduced abundance of key methanogenesis genes (mcrABCDG, hdr, mvh, and mch) indicated impaired methane biosynthesis. Suppression of the methyl-coenzyme M reductase complex (mcrABCDG), the terminal step in methanogenesis and a biomarker of methanogenic activity56,57, along with genes involved in electron transfer (hdr and mvh), suggests limited reducing equivalents for methanogenesis. The concurrent increased abundance of fdhB, mtmB, and mtbC implies a shift toward formate utilization and methylotrophic methanogenesis. As the dominant hydrogenotrophic pathways were suppressed, surviving methyl-dependent lineages (e.g., Methanomassiliicoccales) likely filled the niche58. Enrichment of hydrogenase genes (hyaA, hyaB, and hyaC) further indicates an enhanced hydrogen oxidation capacity, as these Group 1 [NiFe]-hydrogenases may divert electron flow from methanogenesis to alternative acceptors59,60. Taxonomic assignment linked these genes primarily to Selenomonas spp., which oxidize hydrogen and support propionate formation as a hydrogen sink59,61. Collectively, these gene shifts could explain the observed discrepancy between concentrations of dissolved H2 and CH4 and overall CH4 emissions, likely driven by hydrogen depletion into alternative sinks and a compensatory transition in methanogenic pathways.

Similarly, in the PM5 group, the decreased abundance of multiple hydrogenotrophic and methylotrophic methanogenesis genes (mtd, mtrAD, fwdE, mvhD, cofDE, and mauAB) indicates a suppression of archaeal methane formation62. Furthermore, the significantly decreased abundance of carbohydrate-metabolizing enzymes (EC:4.1.2.13, fructose-bisphosphate aldolase; EC:3.1.3.11, fructose-bisphosphatase) supports the suppression of H2-producing fibrolytic bacteria (Ruminococcus and Fibrobacter). Notably, the increased abundance of ghrB, a key gene involved in lactate formation, suggests that the reduction of pyruvate to lactate directly incorporates reducing equivalents rather than releasing them as free H2. The enrichment of mcmA1 and lcdB indicates the concurrent activation of the succinate and acrylate pathways for propionogenesis. While concentrations of propionate remained unchanged, the increased abundance of related genes potentially reflects a redirection of metabolic hydrogen flow. Furthermore, the increased genetic potential for lactate synthesis might indirectly support butyrate production63. The increased abundance of eutE may enhance acetyl-CoA availability, which further promotes butyrate biosynthesis. Consistent with findings in low-CH4-yield sheep, the role of lactate as an intermediate for butyrate formation might be enhanced under conditions of reduced methanogenesis64, which warrants further investigation. Collectively, this study elucidated the microbial and metabolic shifts responsible for rumen methane mitigation by P. membranifaciens M12, and provided new insights into developing microbial strategies that modulate hydrogen flux to achieve low methane emissions in ruminants.

Methods

Animal ethics statement

All procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Nanjing Agricultural University (Nanjing, China) and conducted in accordance with the Regulations on the Administration of Laboratory Animals issued by the National Science and Technology Commission of China (1988). The ethical approval numbers were NJAU.No20230416N06 for donor animals used in in vitro experiments and NJAU.No20240302N18 for animals used in feeding trials. No anesthesia or euthanasia was performed during this study.

Isolation and identification of rumen-derived yeasts

Rumen fluid was collected via oral stomach tubing from three healthy Holstein cows before morning feeding and immediately transferred into pre-warmed (39 °C) bottles filled with carbon dioxide to maintain anaerobic conditions. All culture media were prepared under aerobic conditions. Serial dilutions (10−1–10−6) were prepared in sterile saline (0.9%) and plated on yeast malt (YM) agar (Qingdao Hi-Tech Industrial Park Hope Bio-Technology Co., Ltd., Shandong, China), which contained (per liter) 3 g yeast extract, 3 g malt extract, 5 g peptone, 10 g glucose, and 20 g agar. The plates were incubated under aerobic conditions at 30 °C for 48 h to minimize thermal stress and maximize the viability of diverse yeast isolates21,65. While our initial isolation at 30 °C aimed to maximize viable yeast recovery, the performance of these strains under rumen physiological conditions (39 °C) confirms their ecological adaptation. Distinct colonies were purified and cultured aerobically in YM liquid medium for 48 h with continuous monitoring of growth. Genomic DNA was extracted using a fungal DNA extraction kit (Soleibao, Beijing, China), and the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region was amplified with universal primers ITS1 (5′-TCCGTAGGTGAACCTGCGG-3′) and ITS4 (5′-TCCTCCGCTTATTGATATGC-3′). Sequencing was performed by Qingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China), and the resulting sequences were analyzed by BLAST against the NCBI-NT database (E-value threshold 1e−5, word size 28, match/mismatch scores +1/−2). Sequences sharing ≥97% identity with reference sequences were considered to represent the same species. Multiple sequence alignment was carried out in MEGA (v.11.0)66 using ClustalW, and phylogenetic trees were constructed using the neighbor-joining method with the Kimura 2-parameter model and 1000 bootstrap replications to assess node support.

In vitro functional screening of yeast isolates

Eleven representative strains from distinct species were selected based on growth performance. Cells were enumerated by methylene blue staining, washed, and resuspended to 1 × 108 CFU for in vitro fermentation assays. Prior to in vitro inoculation, the viability of the yeast suspension was assessed using 1% methylene blue staining (Fig. S6A). The yeast suspension was pre-incubated at 39 °C before supplementation. Each fermentation bottle contained 500 mg of basal substrate (forage: concentrate = 6:4) and 1 mL yeast suspension, with four replicates per strain. Control (substrate + saline) and blank (saline only) groups were also included. Rumen fluid collected before feeding was filtered and mixed with synthetic saliva (1:2 v/v) prepared according to Menke et al.67, then 50 mL of the mixture was dispensed into each bottle under a CO2 atmosphere. The incubations were conducted anaerobically at 39 °C with continuous shaking for 48 h, following established procedures68. This single-batch in vitro assay served as a preliminary screening step to identify the most promising candidate for subsequent in vivo validation. All isolates were evaluated simultaneously within the same experimental run to ensure consistent fermentation conditions. Cumulative gas production was recorded at 2 h to 48 h. Methane (CH4) and hydrogen (H2) concentrations were determined using gas chromatography (Agilent 8860, USA) equipped with Porapak Q and MolSieve 5 A columns (column temperature: 80 °C; injector/detector temperature: 200 °C; carrier gas: N2). After incubation, pH was immediately measured, and supernatants were analyzed for volatile fatty acids (VFAs) according to Jin et al.69. Briefly, 1 mL fermentation supernatant was mixed with 0.2 mL of a deproteinization-acidification solution [25% (w/v) metaphosphoric acid, 0.65% (w/v) crotonic acid] and analyzed by gas chromatography (GC-14B, Shimadzu, Japan) under the following conditions: column temperature, 135 °C; injector temperature, 180 °C; detector temperature, 210 °C. Crotonic acid was used as the internal standard, and nitrogen served as the carrier gas. Relative methane production was expressed as the ratio of methane production to total VFAs concentration.

In vivo experimental design and animal feeding

P. membranifaciens M12, isolated from rumen fluid, was used for the feeding trial. Thirty-six healthy mid-lactation Holstein dairy cows were assigned to a randomized block design with six blocks of six cows each. Blocking was based on similar parity (2.7 ± 0.9), days in milk (154 ± 11day), and milk yield (38.5 ± 6.2 kg/day) measured during the pre-trial period. Within each block, cows were randomly assigned to one of three groups (n = 12/group): control group (CON, basal diet), PM2.5 (basal diet + 2.5 × 1011 CFU/cow/day), and PM5 (basal diet + 5 × =1011 CFU/cow/day). The supplementation levels were determined based on an assumed dry matter intake (DMI) of approximately 24 kg/day70. The two levels were selected based on Li et al.71 to assess potential dose-dependent effects. The experiment consisted of a 1-week adaptation followed by a 4-week experimental period. All cows were individually housed with ad libitum access to feed and water, fed a total mixed ration three times daily (06:10, 13:10, and 20:20), and milked three times daily (06:10, 13:20, and 20:30). Prior to daily in vivo administration, the viability of the prepared yeast culture was verified using 1% methylene blue staining (Fig. S6B). The yeast was administered three times daily alongside regular feeding. To ensure complete intake, each dose was pre-mixed with a small amount of TMR and was offered immediately before the remaining bulk diet. The ingredient composition and nutrient levels of the basal diet are presented in Table S13.

Sample collection and analysis

During each week of the experimental period, DMI was recorded over two consecutive days by weighing offered feed and refusals (maintained at more than 5% of offered feed). DMI was calculated as: DMI (kg/day) = (feed offered − feed refused) × dry matter content (%). During the fourth experimental week, feed samples were dried at 65 °C for 48 h (GZX-9246MBE, Shanghai Boxun, Shanghai, China), ground, and sieved (40-mesh) for nutrient analysis. Fecal (200 g/cow) and urine samples (20 mL/cow) were collected via spot sampling over three days at specified intervals: day 1 (05:00, 17:00, and 23:00), day 2 (08:00, 14:00, and 20:00), and day 3 (02:00 and 11:00). Immediately after collection, samples were mixed with 10% sulfuric acid (10:1, w/w) and stored at −20 °C. Feed and feces were analyzed for dry matter (DM) following GB/T 6435-2014, crude protein (CP) according to GB/T 6432-2018, amylase-treated neutral detergent fiber (NDF) according to Van Soest et al.72, acid detergent fiber (ADF) according to NY/T 1459-2022, ether extract (EE) according to GB/T 6433-2006, and crude ash according to GB/T 6438-2007. Apparent nutrient digestibility was determined using acid-insoluble ash (AIA) as an internal marker: apparent nutrient digestibility (%) = 100–100 × [(AIA in feed × nutrient in feces)/(AIA in feces × nutrient in feed)], where nutrient and AIA contents are expressed as percentages. Daily urinary purine derivative (PD) excretion (mmol/day) was estimated from urine volume and PD concentration. Animal body weight (BW) was estimated from heart girth and oblique length over two consecutive days. Daily urine volume was then calculated by dividing the assumed daily creatinine excretion (29 mg/kg BW)73 by the spot urinary creatinine concentration74,75. Duodenal microbial PD absorption and microbial nitrogen flow were calculated according to Verbic et al.76 and Chen and Gomes77. Milk yield was recorded daily for each cow over two consecutive days during each week during the experimental period. Milk samples (50 mL/cow) were collected at each milking session and pooled in a 4:3:3 ratio based on milk yield. Milk composition was analyzed using a FOSS MilkoScanTM 7RM automated milk analyzer (Foss Electric, Denmark). On the final experimental day, blood samples (5 mL) were collected before feeding, centrifuged (4000 rpm, 15 min), and serum was stored at −20 °C for biochemical analysis. Serum biochemical parameters were quantified using a CLS880 automatic biochemical analyzer (Jiangsu Zecen Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China). The measured parameters included markers for protein/nitrogen metabolism (urea nitrogen and creatinine), energy and lipid homeostasis (glucose, free fatty acids, triglycerides, total cholesterol, high-density and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol), and hepatic function (alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, alkaline phosphatase, and total bilirubin). Rumen fluid was obtained via an oral stomach tubing at 3 h post-feeding78, and both the pH and oxidation-reduction potential (ORP) were measured immediately after sampling using a pH/ORP meter (PHSJ-6L, INESA, Shanghai, China). Filtered rumen fluid was then stored at −20 °C for analysis of VFAs69. Lactate concentration was analyzed using an assay kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Jiancheng Bioengineering Research Institute, Nanjing, China), and ammonia nitrogen (NH3-N) was determined using the phenol-hypochlorite method79. An additional 15 mL aliquot of rumen fluid was preserved for microbial analysis.

Measurement of gas emissions

Methane emissions were monitored using an automated head chamber system. Following a 7-day acclimation period, cows were divided into two blocks of 18 animals each. Methane concentration was recorded over 6 consecutive days, with each block measured on two consecutive days at alternating time points: block 1 at 07:00, 13:00, 19:00, and 01:00, and block 2 at 10:00, 16:00, 22:00, and 04:00 on Day 1; schedules were swapped on Day 2. The entire measurement cycle was repeated three times. Each sampling session consisted of 5 min of methane recording per cow, followed by 2 min of background gas measurement, according to the methods described by Wang et al.80. Dissolved methane and hydrogen concentrations were immediately quantified from fresh rumen fluids as described by Wang et al.81.

Microbial DNA extraction and sequencing

Microbial genomic DNA was extracted from rumen fluid samples of eight randomly selected cows per group using the E.Z.N.A.® Soil DNA kit (Omega Bio-tek, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The V3-V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified using primers 341F (5′-CCTAYGGGRBGCASCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACNNGGGTATCTAAT-3′). Archaea 16S rRNA genes were amplified with primers Arch519F (5′-CAGCCGCCGCGGTAA-3′) and Arch915R (5′-GTGCTCCCCCGCCAATTCCT-3′). Eukaryotic 18S rRNA genes were amplified with primers TAReuk454FWD1 (5′-CCAGCASCYGCGGTAATTCC-3′) and TAReukREV3 (5′-ACTTTCGTTCTTGATYRA-3′), which are broad-spectrum eukaryotic primers designed to capture both fungi and protozoa82. Amplicon libraries were sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq PE300 platform (BIOZERON Biotech. Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Raw sequences were processed in QIIME2 (v.2021.08)83 for merging paired-end reads and removing chimeras, and the DADA2 (v.1.18)84 algorithm was subsequently used to generate amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Taxonomy assignment was performed using a naive Bayes classifier85 against the SILVA database (v.138.2) for bacteria, the RIM (Rumen and Intestinal Methanogen) database for archaea, and the NCBI-NT database for eukaryotes.

Quantification of ruminal microbial populations

Bacteria, archaea, fungi, and protozoa were quantified using a real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems 7300, California, USA) with previously validated primers86–89. The detailed information of primer sequences is listed in Table S14. The reactions were performed with SYBR® Premix Ex Taq™ (TaKaRa, Dalian, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. External standards were constructed using recombinant plasmids containing the target amplicons of each microbial group, and standard curves were generated based on serial tenfold dilutions. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate, and the mean value was used for subsequent analysis. The results were expressed as the copy number of the corresponding marker gene per milliliter of rumen liquid.

Metabolomic analysis

Rumen metabolites were extracted from 100 µL of samples using methanol-water (4:1, v/v) as an internal standard. Samples were homogenized with zirconium beads and subjected to ultrasonic extraction at 5 °C for 30 min, followed by centrifugation. The supernatants were reconstituted for subsequent LC–MS analysis. Quality control (QC) samples were prepared by pooling equal volumes from all samples and were analyzed every 5–15 injections to ensure system stability. Metabolite profiling was performed using a UHPLC-Exploris 480 system (Thermo Fisher, USA) equipped with an HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.8 µm; Waters, USA) in both positive and negative ionization modes. Raw data were processed with Progenesis QI (Waters, USA) for peak alignment, retention time correction, and metabolite annotation against HMDB, Metlin, and Majorbio database. Data were filtered (80% rule) and normalized to total signal intensity, and then log10-transformed. Metabolites with a relative standard deviation (RSD) > 30% in the QC samples were excluded. Multivariate statistical analyses, including principal component analysis (PCA) and partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), were performed, and model validity was assessed using cross-validation.

Metagenomic sequencing and analysis

Metagenomic DNA libraries were prepared following the Illumina protocol and sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw reads were quality-filtered and adapter-trimmed using Trimmomatic (v.0.33)90. Host, human, and feed-derived contaminants were removed by aligning the reads to a comprehensive reference database using BWA-MEM (v.1.1.1)91. This database included the host genome (Bos taurus GCA_002263795.2), the human genome (Homo sapiens GCA_000001405.28), and a curated panel of plant genomes representing the primary components of the experimental diet, such as corn (Zea mays GCA_003185045.1 and GCA_000005005.6), soybean (Glycine max GCA_000004515.4), wheat (Triticum aestivum GCA_002220415.3), barley (Hordeum vulgare GCA_900075435.2), and alfalfa (Medicago truncatula GCA_000219495.2 as a reference model), among other common agricultural crops. Clean reads were assembled using MEGAHIT (v.1.1.1)92 with the following parameters: -min-contig-len 500 -presets meta-large. Open reading frames (ORFs) were predicted with Prodigal (v.2.6.3; parameters: -p meta)93 and clustered using CD-HIT (v.4.6.7)94 at 95% nucleotide sequence identity to generate a non-redundant gene catalog. The representative gene catalog was then translated into protein sequences using EMBOSS Transeq (v.6.6.0.0)95. Taxonomic and functional annotations were performed with DIAMOND (v.0.9.22)96 based on BLASTP searches against the NCBI-NR and KEGG (v.111.0)97 databases (parameter: -evalue 1e−5 -max-target-seqs 10). Taxonomic assignments were determined based on the NCBI-NR alignment results. Gene abundances were estimated by mapping clean reads to the gene catalog using BWA-MEM (v.1.1.1)91 and calculated in transcripts per million (TPM), with corrections for gene length and total mapped reads per sample. Finally, the relative abundances of specific taxonomic levels and functional categories were calculated by summing the TPM values of their respective annotated genes.

Statistical analysis

In vitro fermentation data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) in SPSS 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with yeast treatment as a fixed effect. Duncan’s multiple range test was performed for post hoc pairwise comparisons. For in vivo data, milk yield, milk composition, and DMI data were analyzed using a linear mixed-effects model in R (v.4.4.3) with treatment as a fixed effect and block as a random effect. Significance of fixed effects was assessed using lmerTest with Satterthwaite’s method, and pairwise comparisons were adjusted with Tukey’s method. Other phenotypic data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA in R (v.4.4.3), with treatment as the fixed effect. Pairwise comparisons among treatments were performed using Tukey’s test. Microbial community data were analyzed using Kruskal-Wallis tests, followed by comparisons with the Wilcoxon pairwise comparisons, and ANOSIM on Bray-Curtis dissimilarities. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients and corresponding P values were calculated using the psych package in R (v.4.4.3) to evaluate associations between variables. Based on significant Spearman correlations (P < 0.01), microbial co-occurrence networks were constructed at the genus level and visualized using Cytoscape software (v.3.5.0). Furthermore, the specific correlations between microbial genera and rumen fermentation parameters were analyzed and subsequently visualized as heatmaps utilizing the pheatmap package in R (v.4.4.3). Differential metabolites were identified using OPLS-DA (VIP > 1) and Student’s t-test (P < 0.05). Random forest analysis was used to identify key microbial taxa and metabolites, with feature importance ranked by mean decrease in accuracy. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.
