Cost-utility analysis of sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors on chronic kidney disease progression in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus
Authors: Sukanya Siriyotha, Amarit Tansawet, Oraluck Pattanaprateep, Tanawan Kongmalai, Panu Looareesuwan, Junwei Yang, Suparee W. Boonmanunt, Gareth J. McKay, John Attia, Ammarin Thakkinstian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMany trials have highlighted the benefits of sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2is) in terms of chronic kidney disease (CKD) progression. However, SGLT2is are high-cost medication, and their cost- effectiveness varies by setting across countries.MethodsThis study aimed to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2is) in terms of chronic kidney disease (CKD) progression by utilizing data from the type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2D) cohort collected from 2010 to 2022 to estimate transitional probabilities and costs. Markov models were constructed from the following states: CKD stage 3, 4, 5, and death. Cost and quality-adjusted life year (QALY) were estimated for SGLT2i and non-SGLT2i groups from both hospital and societal perspectives.ResultsFrom a societal perspective, the incremental cost was US$3,485.28 (US$80,245.48 (non-SGLT2i) and US$83,730.76 (SGLT2i)), and US$2,971.00 (US$80,913.73 (non-SGLT2i) and US$83,884.73 (SGLT2i)) in patients with renal replacement therapy (RRT) and without RRT, respectively. A one-way sensitivity analysis suggested that the admission cost per day had the biggest impact on the ICER. Although SGLT2is are more expensive than alternatives, they remain cost-effective at the Thai willingness to pay threshold of US$4,869 per QALY.ConclusionThis finding was robust in multiple sensitivity analyses, showing consistent net benefit regardless of RRT status. Background

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2D) increases the risk of micro- and macro-vascular complications [1], including chronic kidney disease (CKD), that can lead to significantly impaired quality of life and reduced socioeconomic status. Kidney failure, the most advanced stage of CKD requiring renal replacement therapy (RRT such as hemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, kidney transplant), affects approximately 0.1% of the global population, with access to RRT sometimes limited, especially in lower-income countries [2, 3]. Strategies to slow CKD progression are therefore crucial.

Evidence from multiple clinical trials has demonstrated the clinical benefits of sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2is) in CKD prevention (i.e., 29% − 40% relative risk reduction, compared with placebo) [4–8]. Furthermore, real-world data [9–12] also confirms the benefits of SGLT2i use in a Thai setting [13] demonstrating a 14.2% reduction in CKD compared with sulfonylurea use [13]. Current guidelines therefore recommend SGLT2is for T2D patients with CKD [14]. Based on our cohort study [15], progression of CKD to more advanced stages could be delayed in CKD stage 3 (CKD3) patients by prescribing SGLT2is.

However, the high cost of SGLT2is may limit their accessibility, despite recommendations from economic evaluations highlighting their cost-effectiveness [16–18]. In Thailand, public health coverage is supported by three main insurance schemes i.e., Civil Service Medical Benefits Scheme (CSMBS) for government officials, Social Security Scheme (SSS) for all employees, and National Health Security (NHSO) for the general population., all of which provide varying levels of care and support. As such, cost-effectiveness is context-specific, influenced by healthcare systems and willingness to pay (WTP), which vary considerably across countries. While recent economic evaluations of SGLT2is in Thailand indicated cost savings for dapagliflozin and empagliflozin in CKD patients when compared to standard care [19, 20], these findings were based on efficacy data and transitional probability from a randomized control trial (RCT) [7, 8] conducted outside Thailand. Therefore, this study aims to conduct a cost-utility analysis of SGLT2is vs. non-SGLT2is on CKD progression in T2D patients using real-world data from Thailand that includes cost and transition probabilities for CKD progression to provide more realistic and relevant evidence to inform policy decisions.

Methods

This study was employed a cost-utility economic evaluation of CKD progression (CKD4, CKD5, death) utilizing data from the T2D cohort, Faculty of Medicine Ramathibodi Hospital, Mahidol University (COA-MURA2022/474). Briefly, this is a retrospective cohort of patients diagnosed with T2D in Ramathibodi Hospital between 2010 and 2019, with follow-up until 31st December 2022. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine Ramathibodi Hospital, Mahidol University (COA‐MURA2021/522). Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine Ramathibodi Hospital, Mahidol University waived the need for informed consent and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Clinical outcomes and cost data (i.e., direct medical and non-medical costs) were retrieved from the hospital data warehouse from 2012 to 2022 (billing data from 2010 to 2011 was incomplete). Because this study focused on the cost-effectiveness of SGLT2is in terms of delaying CKD progression, only CKD3 was included, see Fig. 1. Our treatment of interest was SGLT2is (i.e., Dapagliflozin, Canagliflozin, Empagliflozin, and Luseogliflozin) whereas the comparator included other second-line antihyperglycemic medications (i.e., sulfonylureas, thiazolidinediones, or dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors). For comparison between groups, the index date was set at the first SGLT2i prescription date (SGLT2i group) or other second-line medications (non-SGLT2i group). Clinical outcomes and cost data were aligned with the index date. Patients were assumed to receive these treatments throughout the follow-up, regardless of subsequent treatment switching, add-on-therapy, or discontinuation, until the end of the study period or loss to follow-up.

Cohort population

This cohort consists of 20,735 patients with T2D and CKD3, 2,194 patients received SGLT2i whereas 18,541 patients received other second-line antihyperglycemic medications. Patient summary characteristics are described in Supplementary Table 1 A. Mean age (standard deviation) was 70 (10.4) years and 48.31% of patients were male. Most patients were overweight with a median body mass index (interquartile range (IQR)) of 26.5 (24. 6, 28.4) kg/m2, and a median HbA1c (IQR) of 6.8% (6.3%, 7.7%). Approximately 71% of patients had abnormally low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels. Hypertension was common (92.3%), and frequency of cardiovascular disease and diabetic retinopathy were 23.5% and 9.1%, respectively. Median duration of T2D (IQR) was 2.5 (0, 6.4) years.

Transitional probabilities were obtained from our previous study, including 6,582 patients. In the previous study, RCT was emulated by matching the prescription date between SGLT2i and non-SGLT2i patients with the 3-year caliber width. In addition, covariates were balanced between groups using inverse probability (of receiving treatments of interest) weighting, see Supplementary Table 1B.

Markov model

A cost-utility analysis was performed which estimates the cost per quality-adjusted life year (QALY), considering hospital and societal perspectives. A Markov model, see Fig. 2 was used to evaluate two interventions: non-SGLT2is versus SGLT2is (monotherapy and add-on) to delay CKD progression in T2D patients.

Fig. 2Markov model (CKD, chronic kidney disease; RRT, renal replacement therapy)

Markov model (CKD, chronic kidney disease; RRT, renal replacement therapy)

A decision-analytic tool was used to determine the cost-effectiveness of health interventions. In the model, an individual patient’s possible prognosis is classified into discrete health states until the final state is reached, with costs and benefits calculated and allocated for each health state.

As an example, we start with an individual patient aged 60 years diagnosed with the health state “CKD3”. The patient could remain in the CKD3 health state or progress to other health states (CKD stage 4 (CKD4) and 5 (CKD5)) or death (absorbing state) based on the corresponding transitional probabilities, i.e., CKD3◊CKD4, CKD3◊CKD5, CKD3◊Death. Upon progressing to a transient state, patients could progress further from CKD4◊CKD5, CKD4◊Death, and CKD5◊Death. In addition, CKD5 patients may also receive renal replacement therapy (RRT). Cyclical analyses were repeated, with the 1-year cycle length with a half-cycle correction, until the patient reached an arbitrary age of 99 or death, whichever came first with 3% discount rate per year.

Model parameters

Transitional probabilities:

Transitional probabilities for the Markov model were obtained from our previously published study investigating the effects of SGLT2is on CKD progression of T2D patients [15]. Cumulative failure probabilities (years 1–10) for individual transitions were estimated using Cox proportional hazards, see Supplementary Table 2. These probabilities were used to calculate transition probabilities for each model cycle. For cycles beyond year 10, the year 10 transition probabilities were used.

Resource use:

Table 1 describes the model parameters. The lifetime treatment costs, aggregated from every year’s costs, were considered from hospital and societal perspectives, which were comprised of direct medical and non-medical costs. Indirect costs were not included separately as they were already accounted for through the productivity loss embedded in utility-based measures. This approach avoids double counting, in line with established economic evaluation [21]. For direct medical costs, resource utilization for each year of the study period was retrieved from hospital information systems, including hospitalized visit/admission and associated cost. The number of visits and admissions per patient per year and length of hospital stay (LOS) were calculated and averaged for each state (i.e., CKD3, CKD4, CKD5 with/without RRT). Each visit and admission day costs were calculated including the median value for each stage by Thai healthcare scheme (i.e., CSMBS, NHSO/SSS, and self-paid). Direct non-medical costs for a single roundtrip, including travel, food and accommodation, was retrieved from the Standard Cost List for Health Technology Assessment developed by the Health Intervention and Technology Assessment Program Foundation, Thai Ministry of Public Health (available from costingmenu.hitap.net) [22]. All costs were converted to US$ (1 US$ = 32.86 Baht, accessed on 28th January 2026) [23]. and adjusted to 2025 values based on the Thai consumer price index (accessed on 28th January 2026) [24]. Cost parameters were calculated and applied across the model stages. Only direct medical costs were considered for the hospital perspective, but both direct medical and direct non-medical costs were included for a societal perspective. Utility:

Utility data for health-related quality of life for Thai patients with CKD3-4, and CKD5 with and without RRT were based on previously published findings [25, 26]. The utility value for CKD stage 5 was adjusted by a proportional reduction relative to CKD stage 3, given the limited sample size (n = 10). Utility values for CKD stage 3 and 4 were the same, consistent with the previous study [19]. The utility for each state was multiplied by the number of life years to estimate QALY for each additional living year in the model.

Cost-utility analysis

Lifetime cost and QALY were estimated for both SGLT2i and non-SGLT2i groups from hospital and societal perspectives. The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER), which represents the additional cost incurred per additional unit of QALY gained, was then estimated as the difference in costs divided by the difference in QALYs, and then, for the societal perspective, compared with the ICER to the Thai WTP threshold of US$4,869/QALY [27].

For the societal perspective, uncertainty analysis was conducted using one-way sensitivity analysis (Tornado diagram) with the parameter intervals as shown in Table 1 and Supplementary Tables 2, and probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA). PSA included microsimulations (200 sets) with Monte Carlo (10,000 iterations) to generate cost-effectiveness acceptability curves across varying WTP thresholds. Net monetary benefit was also calculated at these WTP levels. All analyses were carried out using TreeAge® Pro 2024 (TreeAge Software, LLC, Williamstown, Massachusetts, USA) [28].

Results

Resource use

Of the 20,499 patients included, 20,378 were classified as CKD3 (8,954 hospitalizations and 20,157 out-patient-visits), 2,623 (1,163 hospitalizations and 2,554 out-patient-visits) as CKD4, and 1,032 (715 hospitalizations and 995 out-patient-visits, 343 with RRT) as CKD5. Only 10.91% of patients were prescribed SGLT2i since they were first made available in Thailand in 2015, see Supplementary Fig. 1.

The average costs per out-patient-visit for the non-SGLT2i group, for CKD3, CKD4, CKD5 without RRT and CKD5 with RRT were US$24.62, US$27.09, US$34.61, and US$64.72, with corresponding costs for the SGLT2i group of US$379.79, US$285.07, US$316.01, and US$426.90, see Table 2. For inpatients, the average length of hospitalization for the non-SGLT2i group were 7.03, 11.77, and 17.08 days for CKD3, CKD4, and CKD5, while in the SGLT2i group were 10.75, 13.04, and 17.25 days, respectively. The admission costs per day were US$265.06- US$318.84 in the non-SGLT2i group and US$327.92- US$601.28 in the SGLT2i group. Annual healthcare costs were further broken down by expenditure category including medications, laboratory tests, radiology, procedures (including surgical and interventional radiological services), hospitalization/accommodation, and other costs, see Supplementary Fig. 2. Medication costs accounted for the largest proportion of total expenditure and differed substantially between two treatment groups, whereas laboratory, procedure, and other costs were broadly comparable across groups.

Patients were covered under different health schemes including 3,879 (18.7%) with NHSO/SSS, 12,189 (58.8%) with CSMBS, and 4,667 (22.5%) who were self-paid. Costs under NHSO/SSS were US$18.73- US$44.18 per out-patient-visit for the non-SGLT2i group and US$186.49- US$214.59 per out-patient-visit for the SGLT2i group. Costs under CSMBS were US$24.62- US$57.66 per out-patient-visit for the non-SGLT2i group and US$299.07- US$537.41 for the SGLT2i group. Costs under self-paid were US$25.56- US$69.74 and US$232.08- US$379.21 for the non-SGLT2i and SGLT2i groups respectively. For inpatients, the average length of stay for NHSO/SSS patients was shorter than the others at 6.40-12.61 days compared to 7.11–23.50 days, with admission costs of US$166.17- US$679.55 for NHSO/SSS patients and US$283.35- US$962.16 for other schemes.

Base-case cost-utility analysis

Our base-case analysis reflects real-world treatment persistence and effectiveness. The lifetime QALY of patients in receipt of RRT were 13.88 and 16.61 years for the non-SGLT2i and SGLT2i groups, respectively, while for patients not in receipt of RRT they were 13.78 and 16.57 years respectively, see Table 3. In terms of cost, patients receiving RRT but not prescribed SGLT2is spent US$75,003.62, compared to US$82,690.89 for those prescribed SGLT2i from a hospital perspective. For societal perspectives, the costs were US$80,245.48 in non-SGLT2i and US$83,730.76 in SGLT2i groups for patients receiving RRT and US$80,913.73 in non-SGLT2i and US$83,884.73 in SGLT2i for patients not in receipt of RRT.

Prescription of SGLT2is increased QALY by 2.73 years in patients receiving RRT and 2.79 years in patients not receiving RRT compared to patients not prescribed SGLT2is. From a hospital perspective, patients who did or did not receive RRT and SGLT2is paid US$7,687.27 and US$7,154.03 respectively, more than comparator groups not receiving SGLT2is. As a result, ICERs for those receiving or not receiving RRT were US$2,815.85 per QALY and US$2,564.17 per QALY respectively. From a societal perspective, ICERs were lower, i.e., US$1,276.6 and US$1,064.87 per QALY in patients with and without RRT, respectively. In addition, when combined, all patients ICERs were US$2,569.57 per QALY and US$1,069.32 per QALY for hospital and societal perspectives, respectively.

Sensitivity analysis

For the societal perspective, a one-way sensitivity analysis identified the top three factors with the highest impact on ICER changes as the admission cost/day for patients receiving SGLT2is for stages CKD3, 5, and 4, as shown in the Tornado diagram in Fig. 3A and B. The fourth factor was the admission cost/day for patients not in receipt of SGLT2is for CKD4, and the fifth was out-patient visit costs for CKD3 patients receiving SGLT2i, then LOS. The model and ICER values are more affected by cost changes than by changes in transitional probabilities and utility scores. However, ICER will exceed WTP at US$4,869 per QALY when the admission cost per day for CKD3 patients receiving SGLT2i exceeds US$665 and US$661 per day for those receiving or not receiving RRT, exceeds US$908 and US$879 for CKD5 and when the admission cost per day exceeds US$425 for CKD4 receiving SGLT2i and not on RRT.

Multiple sensitivity analysis using Monte Carlo simulations as shown in the cost-effectiveness acceptability curve in Fig. 4A and B, indicated that given the Thai current WTP threshold of US$4,869 per QALY, the SGLT2i group received more net benefit compared to the non-SGLT2i patients regardless of RRT status. In addition, a counterfactual approach was applied by assigning if patients in non-SGLT2i received SGLT2i and vice versa. Results showed that the ICER changed from US$1,276.66 to US$1,468.84 when applying SGLT2i transition to non-SGLT2i group; and to US$1,927.26 under the reverse reassignment. Furthermore, structural scenario analysis imposing a strictly monotonic utility pattern across CKD stages (CKD3 > CKD4 > CKD5, with RRT assigned the lowest utility) was also performed. This indicated that the ICER changed only marginally compared with the base-case. The ICER remained well below the relevant willingness-to-pay threshold, and CKD-stage utility parameters were not among the principal drivers of uncertainty in deterministic sensitivity analysis, see Fig. 3A and B.

Discussion

This study included a cost-utility analysis that considered costs and transitional probabilities using real-world data from Thailand. A gain of 2.7 QALYs was observed in T2D-CKD patients who had received SGLT2is compared to those who had not. SGLT2i were a cost-effective option to delay CKD progression in T2D patients if the WTP threshold exceeded US$4,869/QALY.

Our findings are consistent with previous economic evaluation studies [16–21, 29–35], which have highlighted the cost-effective use of SGLT2is across several clinical conditions. However, the current study differs from previous reports in several ways. First, previous studies used efficacy data from previously published RCTs, including DAPA-CKD [7], EMPA-REG [4], EMPA-KIDNEY [8], and CREDENCE [6] trials, while the current study used clinical effectiveness generated from our own real-world cohort. Second, other studies focused specifically on a single medication (i.e., empagliflozin, dapagliflozin, or canagliflozin), whereas the current study considered the drug class and not each SGLT2i separately. Consideration of SGLT2is as a single drug class more accurately reflects real life clinical scenarios where one SGLT2i can be replaced by the others.

Dapagliflozin and empagliflozin were previously independently evaluated in studies conducted in a Thai setting [19, 20]. Both studies were sponsored by pharmaceutical companies using clinical data generated from multicenter RCTs [7, 8], with cost data obtained from other sources in Thailand [36, 37]. The current study has used clinical and cost data from the same data source. By using clinical practice data, the current study might better reflect real-world practice.

Although all studies have previously indicated the cost-effectiveness of SGLT2is with respect to kidney outcomes, several studies have addressed potential cost-savings [19–21, 30, 32, 34], i.e., both improving effectiveness and reducing overall costs. Some of these studies were conducted in Malaysia [20, 32], which, like Thailand, is classified as an upper-middle income country. Two studies also reported cost-savings associated with the use of SGLT2is in a Thai setting [19, 20]. These findings contradict ours which demonstrated higher costs associated with the SGLT2i group. Nevertheless, our findings highlight the cost-effectiveness for the use of SGLT2is, based on the recommended Thai WTP threshold.

A recent study also evaluated individual SGLT2is for the treatment of CKD3 in T2D with a one-year cycle length [26]. However, the clinical efficacy was derived from a subgroup of participants that were included in the landmark RCTs (i.e., DAPA-CKD [7], DAPA-HF [38], DELCARE-TIMI [39], EMPA-REG [4], EMPA-KIDNEY [8], and CREDENCE [6] trials) and aligned with their target population, while the transitional probabilities were extracted from previous Thai population data [40, 41]. None of the SGLT2is were cost-saving in this study [26], which were consistent with the current findings. Only empagliflozin was reported as cost-effective with respect to the Thai recommended WTP threshold. A cost reduction would be required for dapagliflozin and canagliflozin to be cost-effective.

The one-way sensitivity analysis identified costs per admission as the most impactful parameter in the current study. Transitional probabilities and utility scores had negligible effects on the model. This finding differed from previous studies in a Thai setting [19, 20, 26], which indicated that clinical information (i.e., disease progression rate) had the greatest impact on the model. As discussed previously, the manner in which model parameters were obtained might result in the differences observed.

In Thailand, basic CKD treatments and RRT are covered under all available health benefit schemes. However, RRT incidence has increased to more than 15,000 new cases per year since 2016 [42], providing an increasing cost burden to the healthcare system. Although SGLT2is have demonstrated protective effects in delaying CKD progression in various settings, it has yet to be made widely available across NHSO and SSS Thai health benefit schemes. Given the reported cost-effectiveness of this drug class in our study, NHSO/SSS benefit schemes should consider including the provision of SGLT2is to patients that meet the appropriate therapeutic need to minimize health inequity and improved clinical outcomes.

The main strength of this study is the use of real-world data from a large single hospital center, including both transitional probabilities and associated costs. Given the paucity of studies to specifically investigate the cost-effectiveness of SGLT2is in T2D-CKD patients, the current study provides additional information to inform policy practitioners. However, some limitations are notable. First, SGLT2is were collectively considered as a single drug class rather than individual agents, given the relatively small number of patients prescribed SGLT2is (~ 11%) and their high cost. Even though SGLT2is were comparable to each other in terms of their efficacy (i.e., hazard ratios between 0.61 and 0.72 compared to placebo for composite renal endpoints) [6–8], the cost of individual medications can vary significantly [26]. As such, this study was unable to provide information about the most cost-effective SGLT2i medication. Further studies for individual evaluation of the different members of this drug class are still required when more clinical practice data becomes available. Second, transition rates were obtained from the previous study, in which the SGLT2i adherence rate was relatively low (23.5%). This may have attenuated the observed effectiveness of SGLT2i, and consequently, could lead to conservative estimates of cost-effectiveness in the present model. However, the adherence in the comparator group was 68.8%, suggesting that any dilution of treatment effect due to non-persistence may have affected both groups under the intention-to-treat framework, thereby limiting substantial distortion of the ICER. Modelling a fully time-varying discontinuation or persistence would require more detailed longitudinal adherence data than were available in the current dataset and represents an important area for future research. Third, we constructed the Markov model based on our previous publication [15] which was limited by the availability of some information, including further sub-classification of CKD status. The availability of utility scores for each health state was limited. Consequently, further large-scale survey studies are warranted to validate these findings. In addition, urinary albumin to creatinine ratios are not routinely measured in Thai clinical practice, which further limits the characterization of CKD status. At the time of cohort formation, glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists were a new and high-cost drug class in Thailand. As such, few patients were prescribed this medication and follow-up time was relatively short. Therefore, it was not considered in the previous publication or the current study [15]. Finally, the clinical outcomes and cost data were derived exclusively from Ramathibodi Hospital, and healthcare services outside this hospital (including care sought in private hospitals, or other public hospitals) may not have been captured. Consequently, some healthcare utilization and associated costs may have been underestimated, particularly among patients who were lost to follow-up or who accessed care elsewhere. Nevertheless, Ramathibodi Hospital is a tertiary hospital referral center and a medical school that represents a large volume of patients from across the country, including patients with T2D and CKD, representing a real-world cohort that includes country-specific clinical and cost data, enabling the research analyses presented to inform local and national policy makers.

Conclusion

SGLT2is were associated with higher treatment costs compared to other second-line medications. Nevertheless, SGLT2is were still more cost-effective with respect to the recommended Thai WTP. Further evaluation of real-world data is needed to determine the cost-effectiveness of individual SGLT2is as a treatment option for the prevention of CKD in T2D patients.
