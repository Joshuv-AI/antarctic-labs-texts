Treatment of Genitourinary Syndrome of Menopause in Breast Cancer and Gynecologic Cancer Survivors: Retrospective Analysis of Efficacy and Safety of Vaginal Estriol, Vaginal Dehydroepiandrosterone and Ospemifene
Authors: Ermelinda Pennacchini, Roberta Dall'Alba, Silvia Iapaolo, Matilde Marinelli, Pier Luigi Palazzetti, Marzio Angelo Zullo, Mauro Cervigni, Andrea Morciano, Lorenzo Campanella, Benedetto Ferraresi, Michele Carlo Schiavi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo compare the efficacy and safety of three different treatment options (vaginal estriol, vaginal dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA), and ospemifene) for treating genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM) in breast cancer and gynecologic cancer survivors.MethodsA retrospective comparative analysis was performed among 185 cancer survivors (including breast, endometrial, ovarian, cervical, and vulvar cancer) affected by GSM. Women were divided into three groups according to the prescribed therapy (vaginal estriol, vaginal DHEA, and ospemifene). Safety and efficacy of therapies were assessed over a 6-month follow-up. Improvement in symptoms was compared using the following questionnaires: Female Sexual Function Index, Female Sexual Distress Scale, Visual Analogue Scale (VAS)-dyspareunia, VAS-vulvar pain, Short Form (36) Health Survey, and Patients' Impression of Global Improvement. Cancer recurrence was evaluated according to oncological protocol.ResultsAfter the 6-month follow-up, no significant endometrial thickening or cancer recurrence was observed in any group. The rate of sexually active women significantly increased in all groups, as well as the frequency of sexual intercourse. Scores on questionnaires assessing women's sexual function significantly improved in all patients. Women also complained of less vulvar pain and dyspareunia. Safety and efficacy of treatment were comparable between the three groups for all items except for dyspareunia. Patients taking ospemifene complained of less dyspareunia than those receiving local hormone treatment.ConclusionsVaginal estriol, vaginal DHEA, and ospemifene were effective in improving symptoms of GSM in cancer survivors and were not associated with cancer recurrence over the 6-month follow-up. Ospemifene was more effective than local hormones in treating dyspareunia.Keywords: Breast cancer, Female urogenital diseases, Gynecologic neoplasms, Hormone replacement therapy, Menopause

INTRODUCTION

Genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM) describes the symptoms and signs resulting from the effect of estrogen deficiency on the female genitourinary tract. It affects approximately 27% to 84% of postmenopausal women. Principal symptoms include vaginal dryness, painful sex, burning, and dysuria [1].

Hormone replacement therapy (HRT) is widely considered the most effective treatment for the management of menopausal symptoms [2,3]. However, its use in patients with a history of breast cancer or gynecologic cancer is controversial, because many malignancies are hormone-dependent and HRT possibly increases the risk of recurrence [1].

When considering HRT in breast cancer survivors and gynecologic cancer survivors several aspects should be taken into consideration: histological classification of the malignancy, patient's individual characteristics, as well as timing of initiation and duration of therapy, type of administered hormones, formulations, and route of administration [4,5,6].

First-line therapies for GSM include non-hormonal vulvar and vaginal lubricants with sexual activity and long-acting vaginal moisturizers used regularly (several times a week).

In breast cancer and gynecologic cancer survivors with symptoms unresponsive to non-hormonal options, low-dose vaginal hormones may be considered with shared decision-making in conjunction with patients' oncologists [2,7,8,9]. Specifically, off-label use of local estrogens and vaginal dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA) may be acceptable due to minimal systemic absorption [10].

A prospective observational cohort study based on the data from Women's Health Initiative (WHI) Observational Study, demonstrated that vaginal estrogen did not significantly increase the risk of invasive breast and endometrial cancer in postsmenopausal women [11]. Le Ray et al. [12] specifically addressed the use of local estrogen therapy (ET) in breast cancer survivors and confirmed its safety profile, demonstrating no breast cancer recurrence among treated women.

As for DHEA, clinical studies with highly sensitive assays showed a slight increase in plasma estradiol and testosterone to the lower half of postmenopause values after DHEA administration. The increase was negligible when low doses of vaginal DHEA were used (3.25 mg) [13]. The VIBRA pilot study addressed the efficacy and safety of DHEA in breast cancer survivors treated with aromatase inhibitors, and demonstrated a significant improvement in GSM with persistently low estradiol levels in treated women, therefore inferring that DHEA is a safe option in breast cancer survivors [14].

An alternative option to vaginal estrogen and DHEA is ospemifene [7]. Ospemifene is a systemically administered selective estrogen receptor modulator (SERM) approved for the treatment of moderate to severe dyspareunia associated with GSM, with favorable effects on vaginal epithelium and bone density, antiestrogenic effects on the breast, and negligible endometrial and cardiovascular safety concerns [8].

Meta-analyses in healthy postmenopausal women showed that ospemifene, compared to placebo, significantly improved genitourinary symptoms in menopause with a good safety profile [15,16]. The effects of ospemifene on breast cancer risk have not yet been systematically established.

There are no studies directly comparing the efficacy and safety profile of vaginal DHEA, vaginal estrogen and ospemifene. For this reason, there can currently be no recommendation of one over the other in survivors of breast and gynecologic cancer.

Given the lack of evidence, the choice of medication often relies on the physician's subjective preference rather than evidence-based recommendations. Our study provides evidence which can guide the prescription of the most appropriate medication for each patient.

Strong evidence supports the efficacy of hormonal therapies in improving quality of life and sexual function of postmenopausal women with GSM [17]. However, the paucity of evidence regarding their safety in breast cancer and gynecologic cancer survivors results in women's apprehension and clinician's reluctance to treat [18]. Only 34% of gynaecologists and 17% of primary care physicians feels comfortable prescribing hormonal therapies to women with a personal history of cancer [18,19,20].

Over-the-counter (OTC), non-hormonal, local vaginal products are preferred because of their safety, but insufficiently manage severe symptoms; whereas devices like vaginal laser and radiofrequency provide effective treatments avoiding estrogen exposure, but come with higher costs compared to traditional methods. Furthermore, additional trials are necessary before laser devices can be routinely recommended for treatment of GSM [21].

When treating cancer survivors for GSM, health care providers should strive to achieve a balance between efficacy and safety, improving patients' quality of life with an effective treatment, while safeguarding the patient's health profile through a case-by-case assessment of risk of oncologic recurrence.

The aim of our study is to assess the efficacy and safety profile of local hormonal therapy and ospemifene in treating GSM in breast cancer and gynecologic cancer survivors.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

From 1st January 2017 to 31st December 2023, 298 consecutive women with previous gynaecological cancer affected by GSM were referred to the Department of Gynaecology, Obstetrics and Urogynaecology of the Sandro Pertini Hospital in Rome and were enrolled into the study.

Fifty patients (16.8%) refused treatment and 63 (21.1%) were lost to follow up and were excluded from the study, so eventually 185 patients were enrolled.

All data were retrospectively evaluated from an internal urogynaecological database. Therefore, this study is a retrospective comparative analysis of 3 different treatment options for GSM.

Three groups of patients were obtained according to the prescribed therapy: the first group of patients was prescribed estriol 1 mg 1 vaginal tablet per day for 30 days then 3 tablets per week for another 5 months. The second group was prescribed DHEA ovules 1 ovule per day for 6 months. The third group had taken ospemifene 60 mg orally, 1 tablet per day for 6 months.

The Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Sandro Pertini Hospital approved the study (Approval number 987/2020). An informed written consent was obtained from all women. The research was conducted according to Good Clinical Practice Guidelines.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: menopausal patients (at least 12 months without menstruation) with genitourinary syndrome, previously treated for breast cancer or gynaecological cancer; no previous administration of other drugs for vulvovaginal atrophy; oncological treatment completed at least 5 years previously.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: current and/or suspected breast cancer or current and suspected hormone-dependent tumors endometrial hyperplasia and atypical uterine bleeding; urological tumors current or previous venous tromboembolism; chronic inflammation; pelvic organ prolapse ≥ grade 3 (according to Pelvic Organ Prolapse Quantification [POP-Q] system classification), and confirmed or suspected allergy to lactose.

The first visit included assessment of the following features: age, clinical history, and physical examination.

All patients were evaluated before initiating therapy and after a 6-month follow-up. Symptoms were compared using the following questionnaires: Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI), Female Sexual Distress Scale (FSDS), Visual Analogue Scale (VAS)-dyspareunia, VAS-vulvar pain, Short Form (36) Health Survey (SF-36) [22,23,24]. Women who engaged in sexual activity more than 2 times per month were defined as sexually active. Patients' impression of global improvement (PGI-I) 6 months after treatment was used to compare satisfaction between the 3 groups [25].

The recurrence of cancer 6 months after treatment was evaluated through clinical, biochemical (tumor markers) and instrumental examination according to the oncological protocol.

All patients refractory to non-hormonal treatment with lubricants or devices containing hyaluronic acid were evaluated.

The primary endpoint was to evaluate the effectiveness and safety of the 3 different types of treatment for genitourinary syndrome. The secondary endpoint was to evaluate quality of life and sexual function.

We analysed the incidence of every event to define its statistical significance using Fisher's exact test. Odds ratio and 95% confidence intervals were calculated for every comparison. Normality tests (D'Agostino and Pearson test) were performed to determine whether data were sampled from a Gaussian distribution. The analysis of variance test was used to compare continuous parametric and non-parametric variables. Correlations between numerical parameters were computed using the Spearman rank correlation coefficient. Matched t test was applied to determine changes in the score of questionnaires (FSFI, FSDS, VAS-dyspareunia, VAS-vulvar pain, SF-36, PGI-I). All analyses were conducted using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) 22.0 for Mac. Significance was set at a P value of < 0.05.

RESULTS

A comparative analysis was performed in 185 breast cancer and gynaecological cancer survivors affected by GSM. Women were divided into 3 groups according to the prescribed therapy: the first group (65 patients) was prescribed vaginal estriol, the second group (60 patients) was prescribed vaginal DHEA, and the third group (60 patients) took ospemifene.

The mean age of patients in the three groups was respectively 63.21 ± 10.24, 60.55 ± 11.43, and 59.10 ± 12.91 years. All patients had a history of malignant tumor, including breast cancer (26.5%), ovarian cancer (21.1%), endometrial cancer (35.1%), cervical cancer (10.8%), and vulvar cancer (6.5%). DISCUSSION

Rapid advance in oncology leads to increasing survival of oncologic patients. Breast cancer survivors and gynecologic cancer survivors often live long enough to reach the natural age of menopause or, as a side effect of their oncological treatment, experience the cessation of gonadal function, leading to premature ovarian insufficiency, with disturbing genitourinary symptoms. An ever increasing number of cancer survivors search endocrinologic help in the form of HRT. These women are a major challenge as breast cancer and gynecologic malignancies are hormone-dependent, and HRT possibly increases the risk of recurrence [1,4,5].

Estrogen is a dominant regulator of vulvar and vaginal physiology. In the setting of hypoestrogenism (either associated with menopause or consequent to the administration of SERMs, GnRH agonists or aromatase inhibitors), GSM most commonly develops. Signs of GSM include labial atrophy, vaginal dryness, introital stenosis, clitoral atrophy, and phimosis of the prepuce. Genitourinary atrophic changes increase the likelihood of trauma, pain, recurrent urinary tract infections, bleeding with or after sex, and absence of sexual activity [7].

Estrogen delivered vaginally provides sufficient estrogen to relieve genitourinary symptoms with minimal absorption. Low-dose vaginal estrogen improves symptoms such as dyspareunia, vaginal dryness, and lower urinary tract symptoms, and is also associated with a decrease in vaginal pH, an increase in the number of vaginal lactobacilli, and favorable shifts in the vaginal and/or urethral cytology [7,26].

DHEA is a steroid hormone that is an intermediate in the biosynthesis of androgens and estrogens. DHEA is transformed by vaginal mucosal cells to estrogens, including estradiol, and to androgens, including testosterone [27]. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have demonstrated the efficacy of DHEA in improving the vaginal maturation index (VMI), vaginal pH, dyspareunia, and vaginal dryness in menopausal women with GSM [28].

Ospemifene is a systemically administered SERM approved for the treatment of moderate to severe dyspareunia associated with GSM, with favorable effects on vaginal epithelium and bone density, antiestrogenic effects on the breast, and negligible endometrial and cardiovascular safety concerns [8]. RCTs have demonstrated the efficacy of ospemifene in improving the VMI, vaginal pH, dyspareunia, vaginal dryness, and genital exam findings in menopausal women with GSM [29,30].

Data specifically concerning local estrogens, DHEA, or ospemifene for the treatment of GSM in survivors of breast cancer and gynecologic cancer are lacking. Evidence to support or discourage their use is insufficient.

Our study demonstrates that, over a 6-month follow-up, local estrogens, topical DHEA and ospemifene are effective in improving genitourinary symptoms in breast cancer and gynecologic cancer survivors with a good safety profile. The majority of patients in all of the three groups of our study reported a global improvement in their sexual function, increased frequency of sexual intercourse, and a reduction of bothersome genitourinary symptoms like dyspareunia or vulvar pain. No patient had cancer recurrence during the 6-month follow-up.

Moreover, ospemifene showed superiority over local estrogens and DHEA in reducing VAS-dyspareunia. To our knowledge, this is the first study comparing different kinds of topical hormones and ospemifene in survivors of breast cancer or gynecologic cancer, and showing superiority of ospemifene in treating dyspareunia.

According to the 2022 hormone therapy position statement of The North American Menopause Society, systemic hormone therapy is generally not advised for survivors of hormone-dependent cancer [1]. However, low-dose vaginal ET or DHEA may be considered in consultation with the patients' oncologists if bothersome symptoms persist after a trial of nonhormone therapy. In fact, circulating estrogen and testosterone concentration generally remain within the menopause range with low-dose vaginal ET and DHEA [1,13,31,32,33,34].

All patients in our study were given either vaginal prescription therapies (vaginal estriol or DHEA) or ospemifene, despite being breast cancer or gynecologic cancer survivors, mostly because of refractory symptoms with nonhormone therapy. The results of our study are encouraging and consistent with current evidence. The use of local hormones and ospemifene was not associated with endometrial thickening. And no cancer recurrence occurred in any group during a 6-month follow-up.

GSM can have a great impact on the quality of life of the affected women, especially those who are sexually active. However, to this date, GSM remains extremely underdiagnosed despite its high prevalence, mostly because of the embarrassment of women in broaching the subject during consultations, or as a result of a tendency to consider it as a normal feature of natural aging [35]. Reluctance of women to volunteer concerns regarding the symptoms relating to vaginal health and sexual satisfaction is compounded by a reluctance of health care professionals (HCPs) to address these issues [36].

Moreover, the misinterpretation of the WHI Study has led to an irrational fear of female hormone replacement, both by the general population and medical professionals. It has seemed the logical and safe conclusion to many physicians to avoid HRT, supposing that this attitude definitely causes no harm [4].

Specifically, decisions about the use of vaginal hormones or ospemifene in survivors of oncologic cancer are hampered by the paucity of evidence regarding their oncologic safety [22].

Overall, difficulty in addressing the issue, lacking evidence about safety, and misconceptions concerning hormone therapy, continue to jeopardize the optimization of GSM management and its effective treatment [37].

Current evidence shows that about 62% of women discuss about genitourinary symptoms and sexual function with their HCPs, but HCPs initiate the conversation only in 10% of cases [37]. One third of women are naïve to any kind of treatment. Among women who take medications, about 62.2% use OTC, non-hormonal, local vaginal products, and only one third of patients use vaginal prescription therapies, alone or in combination with OTC products [37]. The rate of prescription of topical hormones and ospemifene in breast cancer or gynecologic cancer survivors is even lower. In fact, only 34% of gynaecologists and 17% of primary care physicians feels comfortable prescribing hormonal therapies to women with a personal history of cancer [18,19,20].

In our study, HCPs investigated perceptions, experiences and needs in term of sexual and vaginal health in all postmenopausal women with previous breast or gynecologic cancer. As a result, over 60% of patients were given either a vaginal prescription therapy or ospemifene to effectively achieve symptom reduction and improve quality of life.

As oncologic treatments improve and survivors of breast cancer or gynecologic cancers live increasingly longer, physicians should focus on interventions aimed at improving quality of life of women. The generalized approach of hormonal avoidance in all survivors of breast cancer or gynecologic cancer is both misguided and potentially harmful.

GSM impacts the quality of life of postmenopausal women, especially their sexual function, self-perception, and body image. Vaginal symptoms adversely affect women's enjoyment of sex, sexual spontaneity, ability to be intimate, relationship with partners, and overall enjoyment of life [37,38]. Women who self-identify as having sexual dissatisfaction have lower psychological general well-being. Thus, it is important to address sexual health as an essential component of women's health care [39,40].

The majority of patients in our study reported to feel better with hormonal therapy or ospemifene. Less than 10% experienced no improvement, and only one patient reported worsening of symptoms after initiating vaginal estriol. Therefore, our results firmly support the efficacy of topical hormones and ospemifene in improving the symptoms of GSM, women's sexual function and overall quality of life.

Our study has several strengths: it is a pilot study which compares 3 different treatment options for GSM in breast cancer and gynecologic cancer survivors. The study population is a medium-size sample of women with previous breast cancer or gynecologic cancer and homogeneous demographic and clinical features.

The study also has some limitations: it is a single-center non-randomized retrospective analysis, and the risk-assessment of oncologic recurrence requires a longer follow-up. Further RCTs studies are needed to confirm our results.
