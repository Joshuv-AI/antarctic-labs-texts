Human-wildlife conflict is amplified during periods of drought
Authors: Kendall L. Calhoun, Justine A. Smith, Morgan W. Tingley, Alex Heeren, Amy Van Scoyoc, Mitchell W. Serota, Justin S. Brashares, Brett J. Furnas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Climate change–induced alterations to human-wildlife interactions are recognized to pose a fundamental challenge for global conservation initiatives. However, the extent to which specific climatic disturbances influence dynamics of human-wildlife conflict across different taxonomic groups remains poorly understood. Here, we leverage an extensive dataset of community-derived human-wildlife conflict incidents to examine the influence of drought, represented by the variation in summed precipitation over the prior 12 months, on conflict reporting. We show that prolonged decreases in precipitation are associated with increased overall conflict occurrences across taxa and are significantly associated with increased conflict with carnivore species in particular. A future with increasingly severe and frequent droughts could lead to resource scarcity that not only causes conflict between humans but also between humans and the natural world around them.

INTRODUCTION

Interactions between expanding human populations and their wildlife neighbors have become increasingly prevalent and remain a fundamental challenge for global conservation initiatives (1). As antagonistic interactions between humans and wildlife persist, a deeper understanding of how climate change can affect resource availability (2, 3) and dynamics of human-wildlife conflict (4) is essential to characterizing how socioecological systems will change in the future. Conflict-related issues connected to human safety (5), damage to human livelihoods (6), and retaliatory killing of wildlife (7) may intensify as climate change alters the distribution of people, wildlife, and resources (8). The efficacy of future conservation hinges on identifying how climate change and human-wildlife conflict are interconnected, particularly as the global effects of climate change accelerate across ecosystems (9).

Drought is known to strain both human and wildlife populations and is an increasingly common manifestation of climate change (10) that is hypothesized to increase conflict among humans and wildlife by altering the distribution and abundance of species and resources (11, 12) and indirectly affecting species’ interactions with nearby human communities (13). While drought can limit the availability of resources to wildlife in wildland spaces, human infrastructure often buffers human-dominated landscapes from drought effects (14), which may, in turn, attract wildlife to anthropogenic subsidies including water (15) and agricultural (16) resources. Opportunities for conflict between wild animals and humans can therefore intensify because of increased overlap near and competition for shared resources (8, 17).

The influence of drought on human-wildlife conflict is likely variable across taxa and mediated by animal traits such as diet. Diet serves as a mechanistic driver of the mode of conflict associated with different species: Herbivores are more likely to be involved in crop raiding (18), whereas carnivores are more often associated with livestock depredation (19). As reduced precipitation directly reduces plant biomass, herbivorous species may be more sensitive to drought effects while species with more flexible diets, such as omnivores, may be more resilient. Carnivores may also be prone to conflict with humans during drought due to a reduction in prey biomass caused by drought (11), their tendency to cause property damage, such as livestock depredation, (20), and the existence of cultural norms that may vilify carnivore species (21). Investigating the theorized influence of drought on human-wildlife conflict may be key in conserving particularly sensitive animal taxa and populations from the additive impacts of future global change stressors.

Here, we investigate the effect of drought on the prevalence of human and wildlife conflict reports by leveraging an extensive dataset of human-wildlife incidents with high-resolution spatial datasets of annual precipitation (22) in a particularly drought-prone region of the world—California, USA (Fig. 1). The Wildlife Incident Reporting (WIR) database, curated by the California Department of Fish and Wildlife (CDFW), includes 7 years of reported incidents (N = 31,904 reports of conflict) across 63 animal species, spanning a range of annual drought conditions (table S1). Reported incidents comprise a broad spectrum of interaction types with four main categories: (i) depredation (i.e., the animal caused property damage; N = 18,190 reports), (ii) potential human conflict (i.e., the person reporting the incident observes an animal that they believe is likely to engage in conflict activity in the future; N = 4972 reports), (iii) general nuisance (i.e., the person reports a negative interaction with wildlife that is not associated with property damage; N = 4799 reports), and (iv) sightings (i.e., a person simply reports an observation of the animal; N = 3842 reports). The primary focus of our study was to understand how drought influenced conflict, or negative interactions, with wildlife. Thus, we constrained our main analysis to the two conflict categories most directly associated with negative interactions: “depredations” and “general nuisance” (N = 22,990, combined), hereafter referred to as the “all species” model. We performed an additional analysis to compare the effects of precipitation on reporting across each category independently, predicting that precipitation would be a strong predictor of conflict reporting associated with negative interactions (depredations and general nuisance) and a weak predictor of positive/neutral interactions (“potential human conflict” and “sightings”).

Conflict reports were aggregated within 50 km–by–50 km site-month grid cells across the entire study region. We used the sum of total precipitation over the prior 12 months of each site-month as a continuous measure of relative drought that is available across the entire study region and period to quantify the effects of drought on conflict reporting (fig. S1). We used a Bayesian hierarchical random-effects regression model to evaluate the relative influence of annual precipitation and other expected drivers including tree cover, human population density, median household income, and the month of incident reporting, on the frequency of reported conflicts, predicting that tree cover, income, and human population density would all be positively associated with conflict reporting (additional directional hypotheses are presented in table S2). We hypothesized that conflict responses would vary by species as a function of diet, predicting that conflict reporting would increase during drought for herbivores and carnivores due to their drought sensitivity and would not change for omnivores. Thus, we investigated three independent models of conflict rates by diet group (i.e., herbivores, omnivores, and carnivores).

RESULTS

Changes in overall conflict reporting and across incident types

The number of reported wildlife conflicts significantly increased in California in association with reductions in precipitation. Across the range of observed precipitation (5.8 to 4001.8 mm), the total number of reported incidents increased by 2.11% [90% confidence interval (CI), 1.41 to 2.79%] for every 25-mm (1-inch) decrease in annual precipitation (Fig. 2 and fig. S2). Lower precipitation was significantly associated with an increase in the total number of reported conflicts within our study [coefficient mean = −0.09 (90% CI, −0.12 to −0.06)], lending critical evidence to the suggested linkages between drought and human-wildlife conflict (table S3). Alongside lower precipitation, high tree cover [coefficient mean = 0.78 (0.62, 0.93)], higher median household income [coefficient mean = 0.71 (0.55, 0.86)], greater human population density [coefficient mean = 1.05 (0.86, 1.23)], and areas containing both high tree cover and greater human population density [coefficient mean = 0.26 (0.06, 0.48)] were associated with increased conflict reporting. Posterior predictive checks also suggest reliable model fit of the all species model to the conflict report data (fig. S3).

In comparing the four conflict types independently, we found that depredations [coefficient mean = −0.06 (−0.09, −0.03)], general nuisance [coefficient mean = −0.16 (−0.22, −0.11)], and potential human conflict [coefficient mean = −0.1 (−0.16, −0.04)] reports were all negatively associated with precipitation (table S4 and fig. S4). However, precipitation had a neutral effect on sighting [coefficient mean = 0.05 (−0.02, 0.11)] reports, further supporting our initial hypothesis that decreases in precipitation would be most strongly associated with increases in negative interactions with wildlife.

Changes in conflict reporting across species and diet guild

To gain insight into the mechanism of increased conflict reporting during drought periods, we tested whether this phenomenon varied by diet guild. We found that decreased precipitation had the strongest increase in reported incidents for carnivores [coefficient mean = −0.11 (−0.24, 0.01)] but was not a strong predictor of conflict reporting for herbivores [coefficient mean = −0.04 (−0.28, 0.17)] or omnivores [coefficient mean = 0 (−0.12, 0.14)] (Fig. 2). Our species-specific analysis corroborated the importance of drought on carnivore conflict reporting, with three of four carnivores and one additional omnivore—American black bear (Ursus americanus)—showing significant (90% CI does not overlap zero) increases in reported conflicts as precipitation decreased. Together, our findings suggest that although relationships between humans and carnivores may be more sensitive to rapid environmental change, species identity likely plays a more central role in defining how drought influences conflict across species. The number of reported conflicts increased for every 25-mm decrease in annual precipitation by 2.11% (90% CI, 0.84% to 3.36%) for mountain lions (Puma concolor), 2.21% (90% CI, 0.04% to 4.10%) for coyotes (Canis latrans), 2.56% (90% CI, 1.32% to 3.76%) for American black bears, and 2.97% (90% CI, 0.92% to 4.94%) for bobcats (Lynx rufus).

To examine whether reporting frequency varied with intra-annual periods of exacerbated drought conditions, we tested whether seasonality (quantified as the month of reported conflict) was a strong predictor of conflict report frequency. We found that trends in reported incidents—measured independently of interannual precipitation variation—increased sharply during the warmest and driest months of the year (May to October, in our study region) for 8 of 15 species (Fig. 3).

DISCUSSION

Amplification of human-wildlife conflict during drought

In this study, we quantify a large-scale linkage between drought and conflict using contributed conflict reporting. Our findings highlight that climate change may have important and unrealized implications for the future of human-wildlife conflict and conservation. Our result that lower precipitation is linked to conflict calls into question the exact mechanism by which drought changes behavior and space use for wildlife and human communities. Pulse disturbances like drought may elicit acute responses in either wildlife (23) or human communities (24), which lead to altered behaviors and spatial distributions, exacerbating conflict between the two groups. For instance, climate change–induced drought could trigger ecological changes in resource availability and force wildlife and people to depend on an increasingly reduced shared pool of water, food, and livable space (8). This could be modulated by human action, with wildlife adjusting to use more human-dominated areas that actively divert limited water to support human communities and industry during drought conditions (15, 25). Alternatively, changes in people’s feelings toward nature following acute disturbances may also influence whether interactions between people and surrounding wildlife communities are perceived more negatively or positively (26). Stress on human safety and livelihoods imposed by drought may push human communities to adopt more antagonistic feelings and behaviors toward overlapping wildlife communities, thus resulting in increased reported conflicts (27).

Conflict with carnivores, in particular, is a prominent conservation issue due to their ecological importance (28), common stigmatization, and the economic losses associated with their conflict. Further, increased conflict with carnivores coincidentally increases the impacts on livestock producers who may already be struggling economically because of other climate-driven effects. These conflicts stem from the tendency of carnivores to cause damage to property (20) and from the large spatial requirements many carnivore species have. Drought may also increase the likelihood of overlap between livestock producers and carnivores by pushing carnivores to further increase the range of their space use (29). Lethal removal of carnivores is a major contributor to the decline in apex predators globally (27), and intensifying climatic disturbances that amplify conflict may lead to increased pressure on already struggling predator populations.

The impact of precipitation on species conflict was similar for species that typically occur in different ecosystems, which conveys the potential applicability of our findings to ecologically variable regions globally. For example, conflict reporting increased with lower precipitation for coyotes, which are frequently associated with more urban areas (30), and with American black bears and mountain lions, which are associated with forests and the wildland-urban interface (Fig. 1) (31). Beyond species-specific responses, the exact mode of conflict, such as livestock predation versus crop raiding, may have varying levels of sensitivity to the indirect effects of drought. In our study, carnivores experience a nearly three-times stronger response to drought than herbivores, suggesting that drought may exacerbate livestock depredation and other carnivore-associated conflict over other types. Furthermore, these different types of conflict could have varying sensitivities to different categories of climate-driven disturbances, such as fire, drought, heat extremes, or floods. To best anticipate effective conservation, these categories of disturbance each warrant further research to investigate what contexts produce increased conflict.

Although our analysis leveraged an extensive dataset of reported conflicts across a variety of species, it is not comprehensive of every conflict incident that occurred during the period of our study. The true number of conflicts likely dwarves the number represented in our dataset with the difference likely driven by the willingness of individuals to report conflict. Willingness to report is potentially based on personal perceptions of wildlife and the perceived role of government institutions in wildlife management (32). Demographic data, such as income in our study, play a significant role in predicting user engagement in conflict reporting and other community science platforms (33). People whose livelihoods are directly affected by drought, such as farmers and other growers, may be particularly sensitive to the additive impacts of drought and conflict (34) and thus may be more likely to report as a means of seeking compensation where it is available. Furthermore, willingness to report and perceptions of what constitutes conflict may also be species-specific and governed by sociocultural norms (32) or government policy outside the scope of this study. However, despite these common limitations, our dataset is large enough to observe clear changes in the relative trends of reporting frequency.

Intra-annual seasonal changes in incident reporting

We found that season was a strong determinant of overall conflict reporting frequency and across numerous species. This effect of season on conflict rates is likely replicable in other regions around the world where intra-annual water availability can influence human-wildlife interactions, and we expect this effect to become even more pronounced as climate change simultaneously alters patterns of seasonality (35) and wildlife phenology (36) around the globe. In California and other Mediterranean-climate ecosystems, climate change is projected to lengthen the dry summer and shorten the wet winter (37), thereby potentially extending the peak period of intra-annual conflict. Wildlife management and conflict mitigation tools could be tailored in conjunction with broader climate mitigation policy to address intra-annual fluxes in conflict while mitigating the indirect effects of climate change on shifts in seasonality and phenology.

Drought may also have a powerful additive effect on intra-annual trends in conflict reporting by further exacerbating existing seasonal patterns of resource availability. While the influence of ongoing drought may alter animal behavior year-round, the most pronounced drought effects likely occur when precipitation is seasonally at its lowest. These compounding factors could elicit even sharper increases in reported conflict during moderate to severe droughts, especially as species’ annual and interannual exposure to drought is predicted to increase (38).

Alternatively, seasonal changes in human recreation and time spent outdoors could also explain changes in intra-annual rates of incident reporting. Outdoor recreation, already known to influence the behavior of animals (39), increases the presence of human observers during the summer months as well as the likelihood of individuals observing and reporting sightings and incidents of conflict with wildlife. Similarly, animal behavior and changes in activity over the course of a year may alter the frequency of conflict reporting. Species-specific life histories and behaviors that make certain species more active during the summer months of the year relative to other times (e.g., hibernation or dispersal) may also make these species more likely to be observed and reported by people. These points are corroborated in our additional analysis that found summer months to be a strong positive predictor of reporting regardless of conflict type, including reported sightings, suggesting that overall interactions (positive and negative) with wildlife tend to increase during these months (fig. S4). Deciphering the mechanisms underlying seasonal trends in intra-annual reporting could greatly inform future conservation planning and management efforts to mitigate the anticipated effects of seasonal resource availability, phenological shifts, and changes in outdoor recreation on incidents of conflict.

The future of human-wildlife conflict on a rapidly changing planet

In this study, we focus on the effects of a single climatic disturbance, but we anticipate that other forms and characteristics of climatic disturbance could have similar impacts on conflict reporting and that these impacts could be similarly quantified. The realized effect of climate change on local human-wildlife dynamics is likely dependent on the severity of altered disturbance regimes. Repeated disturbances or higher-severity disturbances, such as multiyear droughts, may have differing effects on human-wildlife conflict relative to isolated disturbances. Our study uses variability in precipitation to estimate the effects of drought, but access to longer term conflict datasets that allow direct comparison to broader drought indices, such as the Palmer Drought Severity Index, may better convey some of these differences. Despite these limitations, our study demonstrates that variation in annual precipitation plays a key role in modifying reported conflict, which carries important implications for dealing with associated droughts (40) and broader-scale climate change and warming (41).

The type of disturbance may also influence and compound chronic climate impacts on conflict. Here, we examined the influence of annual precipitation on trends of reported conflict across an animal community, but we expect similar effects across other types of climatic disturbance. Specifically, disturbances known to alter animal behavior such as fire (42), heat waves (43), flooding (44), and tropical storms (45) could also increase the frequency of interactions between human and wildlife communities. Similarly, disturbances that constrain quality habitat and physical space for both groups, such as fire (46) and flooding (47), are likely to force human and wildlife communities to overlap spatially more often, thus increasing the potential for human-wildlife interactions and encounters. Furthermore, we anticipate that the synergistic effects of human-wildlife conflict and climate change will present even greater challenges to the future conservation of species that share landscapes and resources with people (48). To increase the efficacy of conservation actions, these challenges should be addressed using a comprehensive framework leveraging research that explores both the additive and synergistic effects of these two looming threats to biodiversity and conservation worldwide.

People play a powerful role in defining the outcomes of human-carnivore interactions at local, national, and international scales through the implementation of conflict-mitigation management tools (49) and broader policies that promote human-wildlife coexistence (50). While conflict is often viewed as a local issue between an individual human and a particular animal, climate change is increasingly linking these issues to national and international climate policy. This echoes a general trend where climate change internationalizes conservation issues, as has been shown with introduced species (51) and zoonotic diseases (52). Alternatively, understanding how species respond to different disturbances and incorporating that knowledge into more local management and mitigation tools could produce beneficial effects by promoting nonanthropogenic resources for conflict-prone species that reduce the number of conflicts. For example, artificial water catchments could mitigate the effects of reduced precipitation on drought-induced conflict by providing alternative water sources for wildlife away from human-dominated areas during drought years (53). For predators, using prescribed burning to enhance habitat and increase the presence and abundance of natural prey (54) could decrease the likelihood of carnivore-livestock conflict in working landscapes. Furthermore, different species traits (such as body size), or a combination of different traits, may also aid in predicting how conflict across species responds to different types of disturbances.

Collectively. our findings lend important empirical evidence to the theorized amplification of human-wildlife conflict by climatic disturbance, with important ramifications for the role and priorities of future conservation under continued climate change. Intensifying droughts driven by climate change are likely to lead to worsening conflicts with certain species groups, specifically carnivores, which already face a disproportionate number of conservation threats (55) related to human-wildlife conflict (56). Understanding and addressing these interconnected mechanisms of climate-induced conflict will be increasingly pertinent for conservation work in a rapidly changing world.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

WIR data

To examine the influence of drought on human-wildlife conflict, we used a database of human-wildlife incident reports curated by the CDFW collected from 2017 to 2023 (N = 31,804 reports). Individual reports are added to the database through either self-reporting via the online WIR interface (https://apps.wildlife.ca.gov/wir) or direct input by regional CDFW biologists and specialists on behalf of individuals. Reported incidents comprise a broad spectrum of interaction types with four main categories: (i) depredation (i.e., the animal caused property damage) (N = 18,190 reports), (ii) potential human conflict (i.e., the person reporting the incident observes an animal that they believe is likely to engage in conflict activity in the future) (N = 4972 reports), (iii) general nuisance (i.e., the person reports a negative interaction with wildlife that are not associated with property damage) (N = 4799 reports), and (iv) sightings (i.e., a person simply reports an observation of the animal) (N = 3842 reports). For the purposes of our study, we focused on the two categories most likely associated with negative interactions with wildlife: depredation and general nuisance incidents.

Most reported incidents (96%) identify a specific species related to the incident. The rest of the incidents (4%) were not classified to the species level in the database (e.g., “Woodpecker or Flicker”; “Unknown”). We reclassified incidents without species-specific classifications as unknown species for the purposes of our study, thus retaining them for overall analysis of conflict but excluding them for individual species analyses. Conflict report details vary by species, but some examples include livestock depredation by mountain lions and coyotes; tree, garden, and landscaping damage caused by wild pigs, turkey, deer, and squirrels; and miscellaneous property/housing damage caused by black bears. We divided our study region of the US state of California into 50 km–by–50 km grid cells (N = 176 cells) for analysis and compared the number of reported conflicts within each cell and each month (N = 1232 site-months) to environmental covariates.

Environmental covariates

We predicted that precipitation, seasonality (month), habitat structure (tree cover), human population density, and median household income would influence the monthly number of reported incidents within each grid cell (table S1). We used summed precipitation from the 12 months preceding each site-month to represent drought as a continuous measure of water availability in each grid cell in place of categorized drought indices to more specifically quantify the effects of drought on conflict reporting. We compiled raster data of monthly precipitation at 4-km resolution for our study region and the entirety of our study period from the PRISM Climate Data database (https://prism.oregonstate.edu/). We anticipated that change in reporting effort over time may influence incident reporting across species and introduced fixed effects of reporting year in previous model iterations to account for this but found that their inclusion did not improve model fit and therefore exclude them in the final model.

To estimate tree cover, we obtained annual raster data of percent canopy cover at 30-m resolution from LANDFIRE’s Existing Vegetation Cover dataset for our study region, California, USA. We averaged the canopy cover for the first and last year of the study (2016 and 2022) to estimate canopy cover across the entire region for the duration of the study. (https://landfire.gov/vegetation/evc). For human population density, we accessed census block data from the US Census Bureau at 60 m resolution (https://data.census.gov/). We averaged two Census Block rasters, one from the beginning (“2010”) and one from the end (“2020”) of our study to estimate population density throughout the duration of our study. We obtained shapefiles of 2020 median household income (“MHI20”) drawn by census block from the California State Geoportal (gis.data.ca.gov). Shapefile income data were rasterized to 60-m resolution to be consistent with the obtained census data. We clipped all environmental variables to our study region and extracted the mean value of all covariates (precipitation, canopy cover, and human population density) to the resolution size of our study grid cells (50-km2 resolution) before performing our analysis using the sf (v1.0.2), raster (v3.4.13), and terra (v1.4.9) packages in R (57–60).

Modeling framework and implementation

We used a hierarchical Bayesian modeling framework to explore how changes in precipitation and other environmental covariates influenced the frequency of reported incidents within each annual month-cell of our study. In our first model, we treated all species incident reports agnostically and modeled them together to understand the influence of drought and the other covariates on the overall phenomenon of conflict. To explore whether these effects vary by species and diet guilds, we developed a second set of diet-specific models in which species were modeled under a common diet-group hyperparameter. The number of reported incidents within any given month-cell is zero-inflated. We therefore used a negative-binomial regression to assess the influence of explanatory covariates on the number of reported conflicts within each month-cell in our analysis (61). To investigate species-specific responses and the influence of diet on trends in reported conflicts, we ran three additional Bayesian hierarchical models, or one for each diet-group: herbivores, omnivores, and carnivores. For the species-specific analyses, we only included reports of species with more than 40 reported conflicts across the entirety of the study (N = 15 species). No species examined here is found wholly throughout California, and, therefore, certain species are not available to be reported across each of the study grid cells. We considered a species as being available to be reported in a specific grid cell if there was at least one conflict report of that species within that grid cell at any point during the time period of the study. If a species was never reported in a grid cell, we considered the number of conflicts of that species to be “NA” instead of “0” within our model.

In this iteration of the model, species were grouped into diet groups using published trait data and treated as random intercepts under a diet-group hyperparameter within each hierarchical model table S2). Species included in this analysis were classified into diet groups based on these traits as follows: herbivores (diet does not contain animal material), omnivores (diet contains <60% animal material), and carnivores (diet contains >60% animal material) (62). Across all models, we used weakly informative priors. Parameters that can take continuous, real values were given normal priors with mean of 0 and SD of 2.5. Parameters with strictly positive real values were drawn from half-Cauchy priors with a scale of 2.5. We implemented all models and estimated them with Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) using the R packages NIMBLE (v.1.1.0) and nimbleEcology (v.0.4.0) (63, 64). We ran all models for 70,000 iterations, with a 10,000-iteration burn-in across three chains and used NIMBLE custom samplers to increase the efficiency of MCMC mixing (65). Parameter chains were visually assessed for convergence, and we checked that the Gelman-Rubin statistic for all monitored parameters was calculated to be less than 1.1 across all chains (66). We interpreted coefficient estimates that did not overlap zero (90% CI) as being significant predictors of human-wildlife conflict reporting.
