Can You Tell It’s AI? Human Perception of Synthetic Voices in Vishing Scenarios
Zoha Hayat Bhatti, Bakhtawar Ahtisham, Seemal Tausif, Niklas George, Nida ul Habib Bajwa, Mobin Javed

Abstract
Large Language Models and commercial speech synthesis systems now enable highly realistic AI-generated voice scams (vishing), raising urgent concerns about deception at scale. Yet it remains unclear whether individuals can reliably distinguish AI-generated speech from human-recorded voices in realistic scam contexts, and what perceptual strategies underlie their judgments. We conducted a controlled online study in which 22 participants evaluated 16 vishing-style audio clips (8 AI-generated, 8 human-recorded) and classified each as human or AI while reporting confidence and reasoning. Participants performed poorly: mean accuracy was 37.5%, below chance in a binary classification task. At the stimulus level, misclassification was bidirectional—75% of AI-generated clips were majority-labeled as human, while 62.5% of human-recorded clips were majority-labeled as AI. Signal Detection Theory analysis revealed near-zero discriminability ( d ′ ≈ 0 d^{\prime}\approx 0 ), indicating an inability to reliably distinguish synthetic from human voices rather than simple response bias. Qualitative analysis of 315 coded excerpts revealed heavy reliance on paralinguistic and emotional heuristics, including pauses, filler words, vocal variability, cadence, and perceived emotional expressiveness. However, these surface-level cues—traditionally associated with human authenticity—were frequently replicated by AI-generated samples. Misclassifications were often accompanied by moderate to high confidence, suggesting perceptual miscalibration rather than uncertainty. Together, our findings demonstrate that authenticity judgments based on vocal heuristics are unreliable in contemporary vishing scenarios. As generative speech systems increasingly reproduce human-like paralinguistic signals, reliance on human perception alone may be insufficient for scam detection. We discuss implications for security interventions, user education, and AI-mediated deception mitigation.

Modern text-to-speech systems can now produce speech with natural pacing, emotional tone, hesitations, and subtle acoustic artifacts that were once distinctive markers of human speech

Telephone-based scams have long exploited authority cues, urgency framing, and impersonation strategies to manipulate victims

In parallel, research on audio deepfakes demonstrates that humans struggle to reliably distinguish synthetic speech from genuine recordings. Müller et al.

Despite these insights, existing deepfake detection studies typically evaluate isolated audio samples rather than synthetic speech embedded in realistic social engineering scripts. Research on scam susceptibility and research on audio deepfakes have largely evolved separately. We currently lack understanding of how AI-generated voice technology interacts with established deception tactics, and how individuals evaluate authenticity when scam scripts incorporate urgency, authority, and emotional cues.

Authenticity judgments can be conceptualized through the lens of signal detection theory

If generative systems successfully mimic traditional markers of human speech, previously reliable heuristics may no longer provide diagnostic value. In this work, we investigate how individuals evaluate AI-generated voices in simulated vishing scenarios. We design a controlled experiment in which identical scam scripts are delivered either by human speakers or AI-generated voices with manipulated paralinguistic features (e.g., urgency, pauses, disfluencies). Participants classify each sample as human or AI-generated and report their confidence in their judgments.

We address the following research questions:

•

RQ1:
Can individuals reliably distinguish between AI-generated and human-recorded voices in realistic vishing contexts?

•

RQ2:
What perceptual cues and heuristics do individuals rely upon when making authenticity judgments?

•

RQ3:
How do paralinguistic and emotional cues in AI-generated speech influence perceived realism and confidence in detection?

Across 22 participants evaluating 16 audio samples, we observe near-chance classification performance despite moderate to high self-reported confidence. Our findings suggest a growing mismatch between traditional human authenticity heuristics and the capabilities of modern voice synthesis systems. By examining AI-generated speech within realistic scam scripts rather than isolated samples, this work contributes to understanding emerging vulnerabilities in human-centered scam detection and highlights implications for authentication design and user education.

However, little empirical work examines how individuals evaluate authenticity when AI-generated voices are embedded within realistic scam scripts.

2.6
Positioning of This Work

Across these strands of research, three critical gaps emerge. First, perceptual studies rarely evaluate synthetic speech within realistic scam scripts. Second, automation studies demonstrate attack feasibility but do not examine authenticity discrimination. Third, detection research focuses on signal artifacts rather than human perceptual processes. Our work bridges social engineering, audio deepfake perception, and usable security by experimentally evaluating authenticity judgments in ecologically grounded vishing scenarios, while measuring confidence calibration and the influence of manipulated paralinguistic cues.

Methodology

This study investigates whether individuals can distinguish AI-generated vishing call audio from human-recorded scam audio under controlled manipulation of paralinguistic cues. We employed a within-subject experimental design in which participants evaluated short scam call excerpts and classified each as either AI-generated or human-produced.

3.1
Experimental Set-up

The study consisted of four stages: (1) script development, (2) AI audio synthesis, (3) human audio collection, and (4) survey-based evaluation. The final stimulus set included 16 audio clips constructed using a fully crossed design:
4 script scenarios × 2 voice sources (AI vs. human) × 2 delivery intensities (calm vs. urgent).
All participants evaluated all 16 clips in randomized order.

Figure
1
summarizes the overall study workflow. We first designed a set of vishing scripts to represent common voice-based scam scenarios, deliberately varying wording and implied intent across scripts. These scripts served as the foundation for both AI-generated and human-recorded audio samples.

We generated AI audio using Play.ht, explicitly controlling paralinguistic features such as speaking rate, pauses, filler words, and vocal urgency to approximate realistic scam delivery styles. In parallel, we collected human audio recordings via crowdsourcing. Participants recorded the same scripts using either a calm or an urgent tone, following clear instructions to ensure consistency across samples.

This process resulted in a balanced dataset containing both AI-generated and human-produced audios that shared identical scripts but differed in delivery characteristics. By holding script content constant while varying vocal style and source, the experimental design allowed us to isolate the effect of voice generation and paralinguistic cues on participants’ perception of audio authenticity.

3.1.1
Script Development

We developed four vishing scripts representing common scam typologies documented in prior social engineering research
[
28
,
24
]
, including banking fraud, tax fraud, lottery fraud, and prize notification schemes. Script construction was informed by empirical analyses of scam persuasion strategies and recurring influence principles such as authority framing, urgency induction, financial consequence threats, and reward-based incentives. Rather than replicating existing scam transcripts verbatim, we abstracted structural elements observed in prior work and reconstituted them into concise introductory call segments typical of early-stage scam interactions.

Each script was designed to last between ten and fifteen seconds when spoken at a natural conversational pace, corresponding to 42–58 words (M = 49.3). This duration reflects the opening segment of a scam call, before escalation to explicit information extraction. Scripts were contextualized for a U.S.-based audience to minimize confounds related to accent familiarity or cultural references. Two thematic classes were deliberately represented: fear-based scripts (banking and tax fraud) and reward-based scripts (lottery and prize notification). This distinction enabled examination of whether emotional framing influences perceived authenticity.

To enhance ecological realism while maintaining experimental control, we embedded controlled disfluencies—including filler words (“uh,” “um”), brief pauses, and minor repetitions—directly into the script text. These disfluencies were identical across AI and human conditions to ensure that realism cues were not confounded with voice source. Scripts were iteratively refined through pilot listening sessions to eliminate overly formal or unnaturally polished phrasing that could artificially inflate detectability.

3.1.2
AI Audio Synthesis

To generate AI-produced scam call audio, we evaluated several contemporary neural text-to-speech (TTS) systems prior to selecting Play.ht. Specifically, we assessed OpenAI’s TTS API, ElevenLabs, Google Cloud Text-to-Speech (WaveNet-based), and Amazon Polly. While all platforms produced intelligible speech, they varied substantially in controllability over paralinguistic attributes. Some systems (e.g., Amazon Polly and Google Cloud TTS) generated stable but comparatively uniform outputs with limited fine-grained manipulation of prosody, urgency, or hesitation patterns. Others, such as ElevenLabs and OpenAI’s TTS models, produced high-fidelity natural speech but provided limited deterministic control over delivery intensity and pause placement, which constrained experimental consistency across conditions.

We selected Play.ht because it offered a balance between acoustic realism and parametric control. The platform allows adjustment of vocal stability, similarity, speed, and expressiveness, enabling systematic manipulation of delivery style while maintaining reproducibility across samples. This level of control was essential for isolating the perceptual impact of paralinguistic variation on authenticity judgments.

Two male-sounding voice personas available within the platform, referred to as
Michael
and
Matt
, were selected for stimulus generation. These voices differed in baseline pitch, cadence, and expressiveness, introducing variability across AI-generated samples while holding lexical content constant. Each script was rendered using both personas to reduce the likelihood that detection performance would hinge on idiosyncratic artifacts of a single synthetic voice.

Paralinguistic manipulation focused primarily on delivery intensity, operationalized through adjustments to speech rate, prosodic contour, and stress emphasis to simulate calm and urgent scam-call styles. Because current commercial TTS systems do not autonomously generate fine-grained conversational disfluencies, filler words (e.g., “uh,” “um”), brief pauses, and minor repetitions were embedded directly into the script text to standardize realism cues across AI and human conditions. When supported by the platform, subtle background breathing or ambient noise effects were incorporated to approximate the acoustic texture of real telephone calls. No waveform-level editing beyond RMS loudness normalization was performed, ensuring that realism emerged from synthesis parameters rather than post-processing manipulation.

Audio generation followed an iterative refinement process. Preliminary outputs were reviewed for unnatural cadence, over-articulation, or exaggerated emphasis that could artificially inflate detectability. Rather than repeatedly regenerating samples to achieve stochastic variation, we adjusted textual disfluencies and synthesis parameters in a controlled manner to preserve consistency across experimental conditions. This approach allowed us to balance ecological realism with methodological control.

Unlike prior deepfake studies that rely on identity cloning or speaker-specific neural voice models trained to mimic particular individuals, the AI voices used in this study were generic commercial personas. Realism therefore derives from controlled modulation of delivery style rather than identity spoofing. This distinction isolates participants’ perceptual judgments of synthetic vocal characteristics within realistic scam scripts, independent of impersonation-based deception cues.

3.1.3
Human Audio Collection

To construct a matched human baseline, American male speakers were recruited via Prolific. The demographic alignment with AI personas was necessary to control for potential gender- or accent-based perceptual confounds. Participants recorded each script twice—once in a calm tone and once in an urgent tone—following standardized instructions regarding microphone distance, background noise minimization, and intended emotional delivery.

All participants completed an informed consent form and provided demographic information, including age, gender, education level, and occupation. We also collected information about prior voice recording experience, as familiarity with recording practices can influence delivery quality. From submitted recordings, two speakers were selected based on audio clarity and consistent execution of the specified paralinguistic cues. Each speaker recorded all four scripts under both intensity conditions, producing eight human clips. As with AI samples, all human recordings were RMS-normalized to ensure consistent amplitude and eliminate loudness as a detection cue. By holding lexical content and thematic framing constant across AI and human conditions while manipulating only vocal production, the study isolates perceptual judgments of source authenticity rather than script plausibility.

3.2
Participant Recruitment

Participant recruitment occurred in three stages corresponding to pilot testing, human audio collection, and final evaluation.

We first conducted a small pilot study with three individuals affiliated with our institution to validate survey structure, audio playback functionality, and question clarity. The pilot allowed us to identify ambiguous wording, refine response options, and estimate task duration. Minor revisions were made to improve survey flow and reduce potential confusion before broader deployment. Pilot participants were not included in the final dataset.

For the human audio recording phase, we recruited two U.S.-based male speakers via Prolific. This demographic restriction was necessary to align with the available AI voice personas, which were limited to male-sounding voices that permitted controlled manipulation of paralinguistic features. Matching speaker gender and accent between AI and human conditions minimized perceptual confounds that could arise from voice timbre or dialect differences. This alignment was implemented to preserve internal validity in comparisons between synthetic and human-produced stimuli rather than to reflect assumptions about real-world scam caller demographics.

In the final evaluation phase, we recruited 22 U.S.-based participants aged 18 and older via Prolific to complete the classification task. No gender restrictions were imposed at this stage. Participants represented diverse age groups, educational backgrounds, and occupational categories. Restricting recruitment to U.S.-based individuals reduced potential variability stemming from accent familiarity, cultural references embedded in scripts, or differing exposure to common U.S. scam typologies.

The final sample size was constrained by budgetary considerations; however, it is comparable to controlled perceptual evaluations in the audio deepfake literature. The within-subject design further increased statistical efficiency by leveraging repeated measures across stimuli, allowing robust estimation of perceptual sensitivity despite modest participant counts.

3.3
Procedure

After stimulus construction was finalized, the classification study was deployed via Prolific. Participants first completed an informed consent form followed by demographic questions and items assessing prior exposure to phishing emails and participation in cybersecurity awareness programs. These measures allowed us to characterize participants’ baseline familiarity with scam-related threats. Participants then evaluated sixteen audio clips presented in randomized order. Each clip ranged between 10 and 15 seconds and represented the introductory segment of a simulated vishing call.

Participants were permitted to replay each clip once to reduce misclassification due to momentary inattention or playback error. For each clip, participants classified the recording as AI-generated, human-generated, or selected an “unsure” option. Following each classification, participants rated their confidence using a categorical scale ranging from “Very confident” to “Not confident at all.” This structure enabled us to measure both classification accuracy and confidence calibration at the per-stimulus level. In addition to structured responses, participants provided brief open-ended explanations describing the cues that informed their judgments. Prompts encouraged them to reference specific vocal characteristics, such as tone, pauses, fluency, or emotional delivery. At the conclusion of the task, participants were asked to reflect more broadly on the difficulty of distinguishing AI-generated from human speech and on the perceived security implications of synthetic voices in scam contexts. The full task required approximately 8–10 minutes to complete. Participants received $2.50 in compensation, consistent with Prolific’s fair-pay guidelines.

3.4
Ethical Considerations

This study involved human participants and simulated scam-related audio content. All participants provided informed consent prior to participation. The consent form described the purpose of the study, the nature of the audio stimuli (including the presence of both AI-generated and human-recorded voices), the voluntary nature of participation, and the estimated task duration. No deception was employed, and participants were not exposed to real phishing attempts or requests for sensitive information.

Given the security-sensitive context of vishing scenarios, we designed the study to avoid psychological distress. Audio clips consisted solely of introductory scam segments and did not request personal data, financial details, or actionable responses. Participants were free to withdraw at any time without penalty. Data were collected anonymously via Prolific. No personally identifying information was stored alongside response data. Open-ended responses were screened to ensure that no incidental identifying information was retained. All data were stored on secure institutional servers and analyzed in aggregate. Participants were compensated $2.50 for an 8–10 minute task, consistent with Prolific’s fair-pay guidelines. Compensation was not contingent on performance. The study protocol adhered to institutional ethical research standards for studies involving human subjects.

3.5
Data Analysis

We conducted both quantitative and qualitative analyses to examine participants’ ability to distinguish AI-generated scam calls from human-recorded calls and to understand the perceptual cues underlying their judgments.

Participant-level classification accuracy was computed across all judgments (22 participants × 16 clips = 352 total decisions). Each response was compared against the ground truth label (AI or Human). “Unsure” responses were conservatively coded as incorrect classifications, reflecting failure to correctly identify the source. Overall accuracy was calculated as the proportion of correct responses across all trials. To determine whether performance differed from chance (50%), we conducted a two-sided exact binomial test comparing the observed number of correct classifications against the expected distribution under random guessing. Exact 95% confidence intervals for accuracy were computed using the Clopper–Pearson method. To examine systematic stimulus-level effects, responses were aggregated at the clip level. For each of the 16 clips, we calculated the proportion of participants labeling the clip as AI, Human, or Unsure, and identified the majority response. We then compared majority perception against the ground truth to determine whether a clip was “majority-correct” or “majority-misclassified.” This clip-level aggregation enabled analysis of whether certain stimuli consistently passed as human or AI across participants.

Because overall accuracy can obscure response bias, we additionally conducted a Signal Detection Theory (SDT) analysis. AI clips were treated as signal-present trials and human clips as signal-absent trials. We computed hit rates (correctly identifying AI clips as AI) and false alarm rates (incorrectly labeling human clips as AI). From these, we derived sensitivity (d’) and decision criterion (c) for each participant and report mean sensitivity across participants. This analysis allows us to distinguish perceptual discriminability from systematic bias toward labeling clips as human or AI. We further analyzed the relationship between confidence and accuracy. Mean confidence ratings were computed separately for correct and incorrect responses. Calibration was assessed by comparing self-reported confidence to observed accuracy and calculating overconfidence gaps (mean confidence minus mean accuracy).

Open-ended explanations provided after each classification were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis
[
2
]
. The dataset consisted of 22 participants’ responses across 16 clips. Three members of the research team independently conducted initial open coding
[
10
]
to identify recurring perceptual cues (e.g., fluency, tone, pauses, emotional intensity, perceived unnaturalness). Through iterative discussion, we developed a shared codebook that defined each code and its inclusion criteria. Following codebook finalization, each response was independently coded by at least two researchers. Inter-rater reliability was assessed using Cohen’s K on a 20% subset of responses, demonstrating substantial agreement. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus. A total of 315 codes were generated and subsequently organized into higher-level themes that captured the reasoning strategies participants used when judging authenticity. Together, the quantitative and qualitative analyses provide complementary perspectives: quantitative metrics assess detection performance and bias, while qualitative findings illuminate the perceptual heuristics driving misclassification.

4
Findings

We organize our findings around three central observations:
(1) participants exhibited below-chance performance in distinguishing AI-generated from human-recorded scam audio,
(2) perceptual confusion was bidirectional and systematic at the stimulus level, and
(3) authenticity judgments were driven by heuristic reliance on paralinguistic and emotional cues that are increasingly replicable by generative systems.

4.1
Participants Perform Below Chance in Distinguishing AI from Human Voices

Twenty-two participants evaluated 16 clips each, resulting in 352 total classification judgments. Mean participant accuracy was 6.0 out of 16 clips (37.5%), with a median of 7 and a range from 2 to 14 correct classifications. No participant correctly classified all clips, and none misclassified all clips.

Under a binary classification task (AI vs. Human), chance performance is 50%. Observed mean accuracy (37.5%) fell substantially below chance. A two-sided exact binomial test comparing observed correct classifications (132/352) against a 0.5 probability model confirmed that this deviation was statistically significant (
p<.001
). These results indicate that participants did not merely struggle — they performed reliably worse than random guessing.

To distinguish perceptual sensitivity from response bias, we conducted a Signal Detection Theory analysis. AI-generated clips were treated as signal-present trials and human clips as signal-absent trials. Hit rates (correct AI identification) and false alarm rates (human clips labeled as AI) were used to compute sensitivity (
d^{\prime}
) and response criterion (
c
). Sensitivity was approximately zero, indicating minimal perceptual discriminability between AI and human voices in this context. The criterion value indicated a mild bias toward labeling clips as human. Thus, poor performance reflects both low discriminability and systematic bias rather than purely stochastic error.

Importantly, 40.9% of participants reported prior participation in cybersecurity awareness programs. However, this subgroup misclassified an average of 9.6 out of 16 clips, suggesting that prior exposure to phishing education did not translate into reliable detection of AI-generated scam speech in this experimental setting.

4.2
Perceptual Confusion Is Bidirectional at the Stimulus Level

To examine whether misclassification was asymmetric—such that AI-generated voices consistently passed as human while human voices remained reliably identifiable—we conducted stimulus-level aggregation of responses. For each of the 16 clips, we computed the full distribution of participant classifications (AI, Human, Unsure), identified the majority label, and compared it to the ground truth. The results reveal reciprocal misattribution rather than one-sided deception. Of the 8 AI-generated clips, 6 (75%) were majority-labeled as Human. Only 2 AI clips were correctly identified by the majority of participants. Conversely, of the 8 human-recorded clips, 5 (62.5%) were majority-labeled as AI, and only 3 were correctly identified by the majority.

This pattern indicates that participants did not merely “fail to detect AI.” Instead, exposure to AI-generated speech appears to have destabilized the perceptual boundary between categories. AI voices frequently passed as human, but human voices were also reinterpreted as artificial. Although clip-level binomial tests did not reach statistical significance due to the limited number of stimuli per condition (
p=.289
for AI clips;
p=.726
for human clips), the magnitude and symmetry of misclassification are substantively meaningful. In both directions, the majority of clips were misidentified. The instability was therefore structural, not isolated to a small subset of anomalous stimuli.

Importantly, majority misclassifications were often accompanied by moderate to high confidence. Several AI clips majority-labeled as human were associated with “very confident” responses from nearly half of participants (Table
5
). Similarly, multiple human-recorded clips majority-labeled as AI elicited confident judgments. This indicates that confusion was not experienced as ambiguity; rather, it reflected conviction grounded in flawed perceptual heuristics. The bidirectional nature of error also complements the Signal Detection Theory results. Sensitivity (
d^{\prime}
) near zero suggests that AI and human voice distributions substantially overlapped in perceptual space. When discriminability collapses, categorical decisions become guided by bias and heuristic inference rather than reliable acoustic separation. The observed mild bias toward labeling clips as human explains the slightly higher rate of AI-to-human misclassification, yet the substantial human-to-AI misclassification demonstrates that bias alone cannot account for the confusion.

From a security perspective, bidirectional misattribution is particularly concerning. If AI voices consistently passed as human while human voices remained reliably identifiable, users might adjust their trust heuristics over time. Instead, reciprocal confusion suggests erosion of categorical certainty altogether. As generative speech systems approximate the acoustic and paralinguistic variability of human voices, listeners’ traditional authenticity cues lose diagnostic value, potentially increasing vulnerability in real-world vishing contexts.

4.3
Authenticity Judgments Are Structured Around Heuristic Models of Humanness

Qualitative analysis of 315 coded excerpts revealed that participants did not rely on technical knowledge of speech synthesis. Instead, authenticity judgments were guided by heuristic mental models of what “human speech” is supposed to sound like. These models were structured around five interrelated perceptual dimensions: (1) speech interruption and naturalness, (2) voice quality and tonal smoothness, (3) temporal organization and cadence, (4) emotional expressiveness, and (5) contextual assumptions about AI capability.

Rather than operating independently, these cues were often combined into holistic impressions of authenticity. Importantly, many of the cues participants treated as diagnostic were deliberately embedded into AI-generated samples, resulting in systematic misclassification.

Speech Interruption and the Imperfection Heuristic.

Participants overwhelmingly interpreted localized irregularities—such as filler words (“um,” “uh”), pauses, stuttering, minor repetitions, and breathing artifacts—as markers of humanness. These features were described as “natural,” “genuine,” and “unpolished,” suggesting that listeners equate imperfection with authenticity.

“You can hear the person pausing and using ‘umm’ or ‘hmm’.” (P20)

“He faltered in the speech at least once.” (P2)

These responses reflect what we term an
imperfection heuristic
: the belief that real-time cognitive processing produces speech errors, whereas artificial systems generate flawless output. Minor production flaws were interpreted as evidence of spontaneity and embodied cognition.

However, several AI-generated clips intentionally incorporated controlled disfluencies and breathing sounds. These clips were frequently majority-labeled as human. Notably, participants did not distinguish between naturally occurring and synthetically embedded disfluencies. Any irregularity—regardless of origin—was treated as evidence of humanness. This suggests that the imperfection heuristic operates at a surface level rather than through deeper evaluation of acoustic authenticity.

Voice Quality and Suspicion of Smoothness.

Participants also relied heavily on global assessments of vocal smoothness, clarity, and tonal consistency. Voices described as “too smooth,” “too crisp,” or “too flawless” were frequently labeled AI-generated. High production quality itself was sometimes interpreted as artificiality.

“The voice was very crisp and clear although it sounds like it was put together rather than recorded in one go.” (P20)

Interestingly, clarity—traditionally associated with professionalism—was reinterpreted as evidence of synthetic generation. This indicates a reversal of prior authenticity assumptions: technological advancement has shifted expectations such that “perfection” may now be perceived as suspicious.

At the same time, monotone delivery—regardless of whether produced by a human or AI—was labeled artificial. Participants appeared to equate tonal variability with biological speech production and tonal flatness with automation. Yet several human-recorded clips were monotone and consequently misclassified as AI-generated, demonstrating that this heuristic is unreliable.

Cadence, Rhythm, and Conversational Flow.

Temporal structure emerged as another key dimension. Participants evaluated pacing, rhythm, and the distribution of pauses across the clip. Consistent pacing was described as “robotic,” while dynamic pacing aligned with emotional emphasis was interpreted as human-like.

“The speed of the voice differed at different stages.” (P8)

However, judgments were not consistent across participants. Some interpreted abrupt pacing shifts as human error; others viewed them as software glitches. This variability suggests that cadence judgments were filtered through individual expectations about conversational spontaneity rather than stable acoustic criteria.

Rather than detecting objective markers of synthesis, participants appeared to assess whether speech aligned with an internalized model of natural conversational rhythm. When AI-generated clips approximated this rhythm, they were frequently misclassified as human.

Emotional Expressiveness as a Dominant Authenticity Signal.

Emotional nuance was one of the most heavily weighted cues in authenticity judgments. Voices described as expressive, nuanced, urgent, or affectively engaged were labeled human, while monotone or emotionally flat voices were labeled artificial.

“I discerned the voice was human from its authentic, expressive, and nuanced delivery.” (P21)

Conversely:

“The voice sounded robotic and unnatural.” (P9)

Emotionally expressive AI-generated clips were frequently misclassified as human. Conversely, emotionally restrained human recordings were labeled AI-generated. This reciprocal pattern aligns with the quantitative evidence of bidirectional confusion.

Participants appeared to treat emotional variability as a proxy for biological authenticity. However, modern text-to-speech systems can modulate urgency, pitch variation, and prosody at increasingly fine granularity. As a result, affective cues that historically differentiated machine speech from human speech are losing diagnostic reliability.

Contextual Mental Models and Assumptions About AI Capability.

Beyond acoustic cues, participants frequently invoked contextual reasoning. Some assumed that banks or institutions commonly use automated systems and therefore classified certain scripts as AI-generated regardless of vocal characteristics.

“The voice sample is definitely AI generated by that particular bank for customer services.” (P22)

Others expressed evolving awareness of AI sophistication:

“There could be nuances inserted into AI like breathing and background noises to trick people.” (P2)

These responses reveal heterogeneous mental models of generative technology. Some participants assumed AI lacks emotional depth; others recognized that AI systems may intentionally simulate human imperfections. These inconsistent beliefs likely contributed to instability in classification decisions.

Heuristic Reliance and Miscalibration.

Across all themes, participants expressed moderate to high confidence in judgments that were frequently incorrect. The persistence of confidence suggests that authenticity heuristics felt internally coherent, even when empirically invalid.

Taken together, the qualitative findings indicate that participants rely on surface-level perceptual heuristics—imperfection, emotional expressiveness, tonal variability, and conversational flow—to infer humanness. As AI-generated speech increasingly replicates these cues, the perceptual boundary between human and synthetic voices becomes increasingly unstable. The heuristics that once supported detection may now increase vulnerability.

4.4
Confidence Remains High Despite Low Detection Performance

Despite below-chance classification accuracy (37.5%), participants frequently expressed moderate to high confidence in their judgments. Forty-seven percent of participants reported high confidence (“very confident”) in at least five classification decisions. No participant consistently reported low confidence across the task, even among those whose overall accuracy was substantially below chance. Confidence ratings were not systematically aligned with correctness. Several clips that were majority-misclassified were accompanied by “somewhat confident” or “very confident” majority responses (Table
5
). In other words, errors were often made with conviction rather than hesitation. High-confidence misclassifications occurred in both directions: AI-generated clips confidently labeled as human, and human-recorded clips confidently labeled as AI.

Participants also reported dynamic shifts in confidence over time. When asked whether their confidence changed throughout the task, 54.5% indicated declining confidence, 22.7% reported increased confidence, and 22.7% reported no change. These responses suggest that repeated exposure to ambiguous stimuli generated evolving metacognitive uncertainty. However, even among those reporting declining confidence, individual trial-level ratings frequently remained moderate or high. The coexistence of low discriminability (
d^{\prime}\approx 0
) and sustained confidence indicates perceptual miscalibration. Participants appeared to rely on heuristics—such as imperfection, tonal variation, and emotional expressiveness—that felt internally diagnostic but were empirically unreliable in this setting. Because AI-generated samples intentionally replicated these cues, participants’ confidence was grounded in features that no longer reliably separated categories.

From a security perspective, this miscalibration is particularly concerning. Detection systems often assume that uncertainty accompanies ambiguity. However, our results suggest that perceptual overlap between AI and human speech does not necessarily produce subjective uncertainty. Instead, participants maintained conviction even when operating in a regime of low discriminability. As generative voice systems continue to approximate human paralinguistic variability, users may overestimate their ability to detect synthetic speech, potentially increasing susceptibility to AI-mediated vishing attacks.

5
Discussion

Our findings indicate that participants were unable to reliably distinguish AI-generated vishing calls from human-recorded calls under controlled experimental conditions. With an average accuracy of 37.5%—below the 50% chance baseline—participants demonstrated systematic perceptual confusion rather than calibrated discrimination. Importantly, this confusion was bidirectional: AI-generated clips were frequently perceived as human, while human-recorded clips were often labeled as AI (Figure
1
, Table
5
).

Qualitative analysis reveals that participants relied heavily on surface-level paralinguistic heuristics—such as filler words, pauses, vocal intensity, and perceived smoothness—when making authenticity judgments. However, these cues are increasingly manipulable by modern synthesis systems. AI clips that deliberately incorporated disfluencies (e.g., hesitation markers, breathing sounds) were often interpreted as human, whereas monotone or “overly clean” human recordings were perceived as artificial. This suggests a critical security implication: attackers do not require perfect voice cloning to succeed. Instead, perceptual plausibility under time pressure may be sufficient. As generative systems increasingly simulate human-like imperfections, traditional authenticity heuristics lose diagnostic value.

Despite below-chance accuracy, participants reported moderate to high confidence in many of their decisions. Confidence did not reliably predict correctness, indicating miscalibration. This finding is particularly concerning from a usable security perspective: users may not recognize their own vulnerability to AI-mediated deception.

Such miscalibration may increase susceptibility to real-world vishing attacks, where urgency, emotional pressure, and limited verification time further constrain analytic reasoning. Our results therefore extend prior work on phishing susceptibility by demonstrating that perceptual overconfidence may persist even when discrimination performance is objectively poor.

These findings have three implications for the design of anti-vishing interventions:

1.

Disrupt heuristic reliance.
Security warnings and educational materials should explicitly address the unreliability of vocal disfluencies and emotional tone as authenticity signals.

2.

Support confidence calibration.
Interfaces could incorporate friction mechanisms (e.g., delayed response prompts, verification nudges) that counteract intuitive but unreliable judgments.

3.

Shift authentication away from perception.
Given the declining diagnostic value of human perceptual cues, stronger system-level authentication signals (e.g., verified caller identity frameworks) may be necessary.

Collectively, our results suggest that as generative voice systems improve, human perceptual detection alone cannot be relied upon as a frontline defense against AI-driven vishing.

6
Limitations

First, the participant sample (N=22) limits statistical power, particularly for clip-level analyses. While participant-level performance was below chance, stimulus-level binomial tests were underpowered due to the small number of clips per condition (n=8). Future studies should employ larger stimulus sets to enable more robust inferential comparisons. Second, audio clips were short (10–15 seconds) and represented introductory segments of scam calls rather than interactive conversations. Real-world vishing involves dynamic exchanges, adaptive persuasion, and escalating pressure tactics. Detection performance may differ in live call contexts. Third, AI audio was generated using a commercial text-to-speech system rather than advanced speaker-cloning models. Although this reflects widely accessible attacker capabilities, results may differ with state-of-the-art voice cloning systems. Fourth, both AI and human recordings were restricted to American male voices to maintain demographic consistency. While this improved experimental control, it limits generalizability across accents, genders, and linguistic backgrounds. Finally, participants completed the study in a low-stakes online environment. Real-world vishing occurs under emotional and financial pressure, which may further impair detection. Despite these limitations, the study provides controlled evidence that authenticity judgments based on vocal heuristics are unreliable under AI-mediated manipulation.

7
Conclusion

This study examined whether individuals can reliably distinguish between AI-generated and human-recorded voices in simulated vishing scenarios. Across 16 scam-call excerpts, participants demonstrated substantial difficulty in discrimination, with mean accuracy below chance levels. AI-generated clips frequently passed as human, while human recordings were often perceived as artificial, indicating instability in listeners’ authenticity criteria. Beyond performance outcomes, our findings reveal that authenticity judgments are driven largely by heuristic reliance on paralinguistic and emotional cues—such as hesitations, pauses, tonal variation, and expressiveness. However, these cues are increasingly replicable by contemporary speech synthesis systems, diminishing their diagnostic value. Participants’ explanations further suggest that evolving and inconsistent mental models of AI capabilities shape perception, contributing to both overconfidence and misclassification. By providing a controlled dataset of matched AI and human vishing-style audio and integrating quantitative and qualitative analysis, this study offers empirical evidence that human perceptual heuristics alone are insufficient defenses against AI-mediated voice deception. As synthetic speech continues to improve, mitigation strategies must extend beyond individual detection ability and incorporate systemic, technical, and educational interventions to reduce risk from emerging vishing threats.

8
Future Work

Future work should expand the participant pool and diversify recruitment across geographic, linguistic, and cultural contexts to strengthen statistical power and generalizability. Because scam norms and caller expectations vary across regions, replication in non-U.S. settings—using culturally grounded scripts and locally relevant voice models would provide more ecologically valid insights into detection performance. Beyond vocal intensity, future research should systematically manipulate a broader range of paralinguistic features, including pitch variation, rhythm, intonation, pacing, and pause structure. Examining how combinations of these cues shape authenticity judgments can clarify which signals listeners treat as diagnostic and how those heuristics evolve as generative audio systems improve. Additionally, detection should be evaluated in more realistic vishing conditions. Rather than isolated 10–15 second clips, future studies could incorporate multi-turn interactions, adaptive persuasion strategies, and impersonation of familiar individuals. Such designs would better approximate real-world scam dynamics, where conversational pressure and urgency constrain deliberative verification. Finally, future work should investigate how contextual and psychological factors—such as cognitive load, emotional arousal, and prior exposure to AI systems—moderate detection performance. Translating these findings into evidence-based educational interventions will be critical for mitigating the growing risks posed by AI-mediated social engineering.
