Genome-wide identification and gene expression analysis of Formin homology 2 (FH2) genes in peanut
Authors: Mingfeng Che, Shan Yang, Guang Ouyang, Tingting Chen, Rui Zhang, Qing Xie, Hanqiao Hu, Yingbin Xue, Guang Chen, Ying Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Formin homology 2 (FH2) family plays a crucial role in regulating actin cytoskeleton dynamics and plant responses to abiotic stress. However, the evolutionary mechanisms and functional characteristics of the FH2 family in cultivated peanut (Arachis hypogaea) remain largely unclear, particularly under heavy metal and salt stress.ResultsIn this study, 29 AhFH2 genes were identified in the peanut genome. Phylogenetic analysis classified these genes into five major subfamilies. Synteny analysis showed that segmental duplication was the main driving force underlying expansion of the AhFH2 gene family in allotetraploid peanut, and that these genes have undergone strong purifying selection to preserve their core functions. Furthermore, quantitative analysis of AhFH2 homoeologous gene pairs revealed significant subgenome-specific expression bias, likely suggesting functional divergence and subfunctionalization during polyploid evolution. Cis-regulatory element analysis identified multiple motifs associated with hormone signaling and stress responses. In addition, qRT-PCR expression profiling and protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis showed that AhFH2 genes exhibited significant tissue specificity and transcriptional divergence under five abiotic stresses, including aluminum, cadmium, salt, selenium, and manganese. Notably, AhFH2-6 and AhFH2-25 were identified as core stress-responsive candidate genes that mediate responses to heavy metal toxicity in peanut roots.ConclusionsThis study provides the first comprehensive overview of the evolutionary landscape of the FH2 family in cultivated peanut and highlights potential subgenome-biased expression under stress conditions. These findings provide insight into Formin-mediated molecular networks underlying crop stress tolerance and identify promising candidate targets for future genetic improvement of peanut through gene editing.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09021-8.

Introduction

Eukaryotic cells possess a dynamic actin cytoskeleton that shifts between globular (G-actin) and filamentous (F-actin) forms in response to developmental and environmental stimuli, thereby regulating cell growth, division, and polarity establishment [1]. Formins are a group of proteins that have a central regulatory role in plant cytoskeletal reorganization and morphogenesis. They typically contain a proline-rich FH1 domain and a highly conserved FH2 domain that interacts with actin [2]. Based on sequence homology, plant formins are mainly classified into type I proteins with an N-terminal transmembrane (TM) domain and type II proteins with a PTEN-like domain [3]. Extensive cross-species studies have shown that most Formin homologs are highly conserved in their regulation of actin cytoskeleton dynamics [4, 5]. In the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana, multiple in vivo studies have clarified the central functions of specific members. For example, AtFH1, AtFH5, and AtFH6 are indispensable for pollen polar growth and cell plate formation [6, 7], whereas AtFH8 affects root and root hair development by altering cytoskeletal distribution [8, 9]. Furthermore, the GmFH genes of soybean are regulated by various plant hormone signals and acts as crucial “actin organizers” in plant growth and development, directly determining the structure and dynamic changes of the cytoskeleton [10]. This provides a molecular basis for the cell proliferation and enlargement of crop plant seeds during the filling stage, as well as for the establishment of storage fat and protein reservoirs [11, 12].

With advances in plant genome sequencing, the Formin gene family has been widely identified in major crops such as wheat (Triticum aestivum), cotton (Gossypium hirsutum), potato (Solanum tuberosum), and rice (Oryza sativa), and has been shown to have a key role in developmental regulation and abiotic stress responses [11–15]. In wheat, expansion of this family is closely associated with pollen cytoskeleton construction and maintenance of male fertility [11]. In addition to developmental regulation, the potential of Formin proteins to enhance crop stress tolerance has become increasingly evident [12]. For example, members of the OsFH gene family in rice respond to various abiotic stress treatments, suggesting a potential role in improving rice tolerance [12]. Formin genes identified at the whole-genome level in potato show significant differential expression under drought stress [13]. Studies in cotton have also confirmed positive responses of this gene family to multiple stress treatments [14, 15]. Together, these studies provide a basis for further investigation of Formin-mediated stress resistance mechanisms in crops.

In recent years, with the release and continuous refinement of the allotetraploid peanut genome, genome-wide identification of stress-responsive gene families has become a vital strategy for unraveling peanut’s molecular defense mechanisms. Recent systematic studies have successfully characterized several key gene families in cultivated peanut, such as the TPP, MAPK, and DUF506 families, highlighting their crucial regulatory roles in mitigating salt and drought stresses [16–18]. Beyond stress responses, the functional characterization of gene families is crucial for improving peanut nutritional quality. For instance, recent genome-wide analyses in 2025 have characterized the Oleosin (AhOle) gene family, which directly regulates the structure of oil bodies and seed oil accumulation in peanut [19]. Furthermore, large-scale genomic studies have identified extensive natural variations and hub genes controlling the oleic-to-linoleic acid (O/L) ratio, a vital determinant of peanut oil quality and shelf life [20]. However, as the core dynamic regulators of the cytoskeleton, the evolutionary mechanisms and specific functions of the Formin family in peanut remain largely unexplored.

Although the role of Formin proteins in stress resistance has been preliminarily characterized in some diploid crops, systematic mechanistic studies in major polyploid crops have only gradually emerged in recent years. Peanut (Arachis hypogaea) is a globally important legume oilseed and food crop with high nutritional and economic value, and it has an important role in sustainable agricultural development [21–23]. Recent studies further emphasize its strategic agricultural importance: global peanut production has approached 50 million tons annually, with an increasing proportion utilized as a premium plant-based protein source whose nutritional profile is highly comparable to animal proteins [24, 25]. Furthermore, as a cornerstone of global food security and sustainable agroeconomies, cultivated peanut is facing unprecedented threats from global climate change, making the exploration of stress-resilient mechanisms an urgent priority [26]. However, in actual field cultivation, the production of peanuts is severely restricted by specific soil environmental stress [27]. For instance, in the acidic red soil of the global high-quality peanut-producing area in southern China, due to the low soil pH value, aluminum (Al) and manganese (Mn) toxicity usually coexist naturally and cause significant damage to the plaant roots [28, 29]. Moreover, coastal agricultural areas are facing increasingly severe soil salinization (NaCl stress) problems [30], and long-term industrial activities and the overuse of agricultural fertilizers have also led to severe accumulation of cadmium (Cd) in some farmlands [31]. These naturally occurring field environmental stresses quickly trigger the outbreak of reactive oxygen species (ROS), interfere with seed germination, disrupt photosynthesis, and exacerbate cellular damage, thereby causing huge economic losses every year [32–35]. Recent multi-omics and physiological studies further reveal that these environmental stresses drastically perturb ion transport, hormone signal transduction, and ROS-scavenging systems in peanut, necessitating complex molecular defense networks [36, 37]. Therefore, the abiotic stress treatments designed in this study aim to precisely simulate these main limiting factors existing in the field. This enables the exploration of how peanuts regulate their intracellular skeleton network to cope with these extreme conditions, which has extremely important guiding significance for actual agricultural production and stress-resistant molecular breeding [38]. To date, the molecular mechanisms by which peanut regulates cytoskeletal dynamics to cope with these complex stresses remain unclear. In particular, peanut has a complex allotetraploid (AABB) genome, and the evolutionary retention and structural differentiation of Formin family members during polyploidization, as well as subgenomic expression bias in response to different environmental signals, remain major gaps in current knowledge [39–41]. During plant responses to abiotic stress, dynamic cytoskeletal reorganization is a core early event [42]. Previous studies have shown that heavy metal ions, such as aluminum and cadmium, directly interfere with actin polymerization by inducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) bursts and stress hormone signals, such as abscisic acid (ABA) [43, 44]. As core regulators of microfilament nucleation, Formin proteins may serve as a molecular bridge between extracellular stress signals and intracellular structural responses [45]. However, the molecular coupling mechanisms through which the AhFH2 family integrates these upstream signals and mediates heavy metal tolerance in allotetraploid peanut remain poorly understood.

Against this background, comprehensive identification of the peanut Formin gene family and detailed analysis of its expression responses under adverse conditions are both timely and of clear research interest. This study aimed to use genomic information from a polyploid crop to systematically identify members of the AhFH2 family in cultivated peanut and to analyze their expression response patterns under salt stress and multiple heavy metal stresses. This work not only helps clarify the molecular mechanisms by which peanut regulates cytoskeletal dynamics in response to complex environmental stress, but also provides valuable candidate genes for future breeding of stress-tolerant peanut varieties using gene-editing technology.

Materials and methods

Identification of AhFH2 family members

Genomic data for peanut were obtained from the Ensembl Plants database (accessed on November 28, 2025) [46]. The hidden Markov model (HMM) profile of the FH2 domain was retrieved from the Pfam database (accessed on November 28, 2025) [47] and used for a preliminary homology search with a threshold of 1e − 5. Known Formin protein sequences from Arabidopsis were then downloaded from the TAIR database (accessed on November 28, 2025) [48] for BLASTP comparison with an E-value threshold of 1e − 5. Additionally, multiple sequence alignments were performed using the homologous genes (OsCMeo_04g0016480) from the rice genome database (https://rice.uga.edu/) [49] and the genes (MsaG000593) from the Medicago sativa genome database (http://alfalfagedb.liu-lab.com/) [50]. Finally, redundant and incomplete sequences were removed on the basis of gene ID and sequence similarity, and candidate members of the peanut AhFH2 family were identified. To ensure the accuracy of the identification results, firstly, for genes with multiple transcripts, only the longest protein-coding sequence was retained to eliminate redundancy. Subsequently, all candidate sequences were submitted to the NCBI CD-search (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/wrpsb.cgi), Pfam and SMART databases for domain integrity analysis. Sequences without the conserved FH2 domain (PF02181) or with incomplete domains were manually excluded. Through the verification and cleaning of these multiple databases, 29 members of the peanut AhFH2 gene with complete functional characteristics were finally determined.

Sequence alignment and phylogenetic analysis

To elucidate the phylogenetic and evolutionary relationships of the AhFH2 family, a multi-species phylogenetic tree was constructed. The selection of reference species and outgroups was systematically designed to represent taxonomic divergence and genomic ploidy levels. Specifically, the monocot Triticum aestivum (allohexaploid) was included to serve as a functional outgroup to establish the deep evolutionary divergence boundary between dicots and monocots [11]. Within the dicot clade, Arabidopsis thaliana was selected as the basal model [48]; Solanum tuberosum (diploid) [13] and Gossypium hirsutum (allotetraploid) [14, 15] were served as crop references. Notably, the inclusion of G. hirsutum was crucial for comparative analysis of gene retention and expansion mechanisms driven by polyploidization, mirroring the complex evolutionary history of allotetraploid peanut (AABB).

For tree construction, the full-length Formin protein sequences (detailed in Table S1) were analyzed using the “One Step Build a ML Tree” module in TBtools-II (v2.472) [51]. The sequences were first aligned using MUSCLE, followed by the removal of poorly aligned regions using trimAl [52]. The phylogenetic tree was then inferred using the Maximum Likelihood (ML) method implemented in IQ-TREE [53]. The optimal amino acid substitution model was automatically determined by ModelFinder, and branch reliability was rigorously assessed using 5,000 UltraFast Bootstrap replicates [54, 55]. The final tree was visualized and annotated using the iTOL online tool [56].

Conserved motif and gene structure analysis

Genome annotation files (GFF/GTF) of peanut AhFH2 genes were imported into the “Gene Structure View” module of TBtools to analyze and visualize exon–intron structures. In addition, conserved motifs in AhFH2 protein sequences were identified using the MEME online tool ((https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/meme, accessed on November 28, 2025) [57]. The parameters were set as follows: the maximum number of motifs was 10, and the optimum motif width ranged from 6 to 50 amino acid residues.

Collinearity analysis and promoter cis-acting element analysis of AhFH2 genes

Collinearity between the peanut and Arabidopsis genomes was analyzed using the MCScanX algorithm implemented in TBtools [51, 58]. An interspecific collinearity map of the AhFH2 gene family was then generated using Circos [59], and the image was further adjusted in Adobe Illustrator. In addition, the 2,000-bp genomic sequence upstream of the start codon (ATG) of each AhFH2 gene was extracted as the promoter region. Cis-regulatory elements within this region were predicted using the PlantCARE online database (accessed on November 28, 2025) [60] and visualized using TBtools.

Prediction of the secondary and tertiary structures of AhFH2 proteins and the protein interaction network

Protein secondary structures were predicted using the SOPMA website (accessed on November 30, 2025) [61]. Subcellular localization of all FH2 protein sequences was predicted using the WoLF PSORT website (https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp/) [62]. To analyze the spatial conformations of AhFH2 proteins, the SWISS-MODEL online server (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/, accessed on November 30, 2025) [63] was used for homology modeling and tertiary structure prediction of 29 AhFH2 protein sequences. This server is based on homology modeling and automatically retrieves the optimal template from the protein database (PDB), followed by sequence alignment and three-dimensional model construction. Local quality assessment and visualization of the predicted models were exported directly from the platform. In parallel, based on the model plant A. thaliana, the protein interaction network was predicted using STRING (https://string-db.org/, accessed on November 30, 2025) [64].

Analysis of AhFH2 gene expression patterns and subgenomic expression preferences

To further elucidate the subgenomic expression preferences of the homologous genes of AhFH2 in the allopolyploid peanut, the multi-tissue developmental transcriptome sequencing data of the peanut variety ‘Tifrunner’ from the PeanutBase database (https://www.peanutbase.org/) were downloaded [41]. Based on the homology relationships determined by the whole-genome collinearity analysis, 8 pairs of direct homologous gene pairs with high confidence, which were located on the A and B subgenomes respectively were selected [40, 65]. Subsequently, the expression levels (TPM values) of these homologous genes were extracted in 10 representative tissues and developmental stages and constructed an expression matrix [41]. Using the Heatmap Illustrator module in the TBtools-II software, a heat map was generated [51]. During the drawing process, the expression matrix was standardized by rows (Row Scale) to highlight the relative expression differences between the A/B homologous copies, and a cluster analysis based on Euclidean distance was performed [51].

Cultivation and treatment of plant materials

The peanut variety used in this study was Zhanyou 75, provided by the Peanut Research Institute of the Agricultural Academy of Zhanjiang City, Guangdong Province, China. Peanut seeds were germinated in sand and grown for 10 days. Seedlings with similar growth status were then selected and transplanted into 4-L culture buckets containing hydroponic nutrient solution [66]. The composition of the hydroponic nutrient solution is listed in Table S2. Stress treatments included 50 mM sodium chloride [67], 15 µM sodium selenite [68], 20 µM cadmium chloride [69], 10 µM and 300 µM manganese sulfate [70], and 4 mM aluminum sulfate octahydrate [71]. Each treatment was conducted with three replicates. Daytime temperature was maintained at 25 to 30 °C, and nighttime temperature was maintained at 18 to 22 °C. Plants were grown under a light intensity of 240 µmol m−2 s−1. The hydroponic nutrient solution was replaced every 5 days. After 15 days of treatment, root and leaf tissues were collected. Each biological replicate was composed of the combined materials of 5 peanut seedlings grown in the same hydroponic bucket. Each treatment group had three independent biological replicates (i.e., 3 hydroponic buckets, totaling 15 seedlings).

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR analysis

Leaves and roots subjected to salt, selenium, cadmium, manganese, and aluminum treatments were used for RNA extraction. Total RNA was extracted using the MolPure Plant RNA Extraction Kit (Yisheng, China). cDNA was synthesized using the Hifair II First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Yisheng, China). qRT-PCR was performed using Hifair UNICON Universal Blue qPCR SYBR Green Premix (Yisheng, China) and an ABI7500 system (Applied Biosystems, USA) [72]. The 10 μL reaction mixture contained 0.25 μL forward primer, 0.25 μL reverse primer, 4 μL nuclease-free water, 0.5 μL cDNA template, and 5 μL Hifair UNICON Universal Blue qPCR SYBR Green Premix (Yisheng, China). The qRT-PCR cycling conditions were 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min [73]. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method [74]. Primer sequences are listed in Table S3. To ensure the accuracy and specificity of the quantitative results, a melting curve analysis was conducted for all qRT-PCR reactions to confirm that each target gene exhibited a single specific amplification peak, without primer dimerization or non-specific amplification [75]. Additionally, in this study, an AhUbiquitin (DQ887087.1), which was highly stable in the presence of multiple abiotic stresses, was selected as the internal reference gene to precisely normalize the relative expression levels of the target genes [76].

Data statistics and analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel 2016 and IBM SPSS Statistics 27. Values were presented as means ± standard deviations (SD) of three independent biological replicates [77]. Prior to parametric testing, the data were tested for normal distribution using the Shapiro–Wilk test and for homogeneity of variances using Levene’s test [77]. An independent-samples t-test was used to assess differences between control and treatment groups for data meeting these assumptions (* p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01 *** p < 0.001) [77].

Analysis of physicochemical properties showed that the 29 predicted AhFH2 proteins ranged from 682 amino acids (AhFH2-14) to 1,739 amino acids (AhFH2-13) in length, with an average length of approximately 1,017 amino acids. The predicted molecular weights ranged from 76.48 kDa (AhFH2-14) to 191.39 kDa (AhFH2-13). The isoelectric points (pI) ranged from 5.58 (AhFH2-20) to 9.49 (AhFH2-22). Subcellular localization predictions indicated that AhFH2 proteins were broadly distributed across different cellular compartments, with most localized to the plasma membrane (8) and vacuole (8), whereas the remaining members were predicted to localize to the chloroplast (6), cytoplasm (5), endoplasmic reticulum (1), and nucleus (1).

Secondary structure prediction of proteins

To further characterize the structural features of AhFH2 proteins, their secondary structures were predicted and are summarized in Table S4. The results showed that AhFH2 proteins were composed mainly of random coils and α-helices. Random coils accounted for the largest proportion in all members, ranging from 41.14% (AhFH2-19) to 70.72% (AhFH2-24), followed by α-helices, which ranged from 26.67% (AhFH2-24) to 47.65% (AhFH2-14). In contrast, extended strands and β-turns represented relatively small proportions, ranging from 1.00% to 12.43% and from 0.25% to 3.53%, respectively.

Sequence alignment and phylogenetic analysis of the AhFH2 genes

To investigate the evolutionary history and orthologous relationships of the AhFH2 gene family, 29 peanut AhFH proteins, together with full-length Formin protein sequences from representative dicot species (A. thaliana, At; S. tuberosum, St; G. hirsutum, Gh) and the monocot species wheat (Triticum aestivum, Ta), were selected to construct a multi-species phylogenetic tree.

As shown in Fig. 1, based on the Maximum Likelihood (ML) phylogenetic topology and robust bootstrap support, all Formin proteins were divided into five independent subgroups (Groups I-V). In terms of species distribution, the phylogenetic tree continued to show clear dicot-monocot differentiation. Peanut AhFH2 proteins consistently clustered with homologous proteins from dicot species, including cotton, potato, and A. thaliana, forming close orthologous relationships. By contrast, wheat Formin proteins (TaFHs) tended to form independent subclades within each group or were positioned near the basal nodes.

In addition, AhFH2 genes were unevenly distributed among the five subgroups. Groups I and III were the largest, each containing 9 AhFH2 members (e.g., AhFH2-4, AhFH2-6 in Group I; AhFH2-10, AhFH2-13 in Group III). This was followed by Group V (6 members) and Group II (4 members). Group IV was the smallest for peanut, containing only a single member (AhFH2-18).

Selection pressure analysis of the AhFH2 gene family

To investigate the evolutionary constraints acting on the AhFH2 gene family, Ka, Ks, and Ka/Ks values were calculated for 12 paralogous gene pairs (Table S5). This analysis was restricted to duplication events identified within the AhFH2 family.

The results showed that the Ka/Ks ratios of all 12 gene pairs were substantially lower than 1, ranging from 0.12 to 0.27, indicating that the AhFH2 gene family has undergone strong purifying selection during evolution to preserve conserved functions. To improve analytical reliability, gene pairs containing non-family transcripts or showing substitution saturation (Ks > 2.0) were excluded.

Discussion

Expansion pattern and evolutionary driving forces of the peanut AhFH2 gene family

Formin proteins are core nucleation factors that regulate plant cytoskeletal dynamics and have indispensable roles in plant morphogenesis, polar growth, and environmental adaptation [1]. In this study, 29 AhFH2 genes were identified at the whole-genome level in cultivated peanut, a number substantially higher than that reported in diploid model plants such as A. thaliana. The collinearity analysis confirmed that the expansion of the AhFH2 family was mainly driven by fragment duplication, and rarely by tandem duplication. This pattern is also verified in the same polyploid species, wheat and cotton [11, 14]. During the intense polyploidy and genome fusion process of peanuts (AABB), segment duplication and whole-genome replication ensure the paired retention of the Formin gene between homologous chromosomes [39]. This might have endowed tetraploid peanuts with a more abundant and redundant cellular skeleton regulatory backup compared to their diploid ancestors, which could be of significant evolutionary importance for maintaining their phenotypic stability under complex adverse conditions.

The cross-species collinearity analysis further provides important clues for the evolutionary origin and functional adaptation of this family [58]. This study found that there were up to 26 direct homologous gene pairs retained between peanuts and the model plant A. thaliana. This indicates that these AhFH2 members may have existed before the differentiation of dicotyledonous plants and may represent an ancient and highly conserved core regulatory network controlling the basic morphogenesis and cytoskeleton dynamics of plants [4, 5]. This deep cross-species conservation suggests that they are likely to have essential housekeeping functions in other leguminous plants (Housekeeping functions) [1, 4]. However, during the complex allopolyploidization (AABB) evolution of peanuts, intense chromosomal rearrangements profoundly shaped the current family structure [39, 65]. The large number of gene pairs based on fragment duplications observed in this study might actually reflect the evolutionary retention of large-scale homologous chromosomes during the fusion of the A sub-genome and B sub-genome. This genetic redundancy backup resulting from chromosomal rearrangements and fragment duplications not only may buffer the genomic oscillations in the early stages of polyploidy but also may provide valuable genetic materials for the sub-functionalization of AhFH2 genes and the evolution of ‘peanut-specific adaptations’ to specific environmental stresses (such as heavy metal toxicity) [78, 79]. This study’s quantitative expression analysis of A/B subgenomic homologous pairs further confirmed this evolutionary inference (Fig. 8). The results showed that although these homologous pairs maintained a high degree of conservation at the sequence level, their expression abundances in different tissues exhibited significant divergence. This expression bias may be one of the core features of allopolyploid evolution [78, 79]. Particularly, the extreme expression differences exhibited by gene pairs such as AhFH2-19/10 clearly revealed that the homologous copies might have undergone profound functional divergence at the transcriptional regulation level. This regulatory flexibility not only helps alleviate the functional redundancy pressure after polyploidization but also provides the possibility for peanuts to achieve more precise temporal control during complex developmental processes or environmental adaptations [78]. Although the AhFH2 family contains a relatively large number of members, the Ka/Ks analysis showed that all duplicated gene pairs had undergone strong purifying selection. This suggests that dynamic assembly and disassembly of the actin cytoskeleton are critical for plant cell viability. To preserve the core biological role of the FH2 domain in actin nucleation and elongation, this family may have remained under strong evolutionary constraint over long periods, thereby eliminating potentially deleterious mutations that could cause functional loss [2].

Subfunctionalization mediated by structural diversity and membrane-scaffold interactions

Although all AhFH2 proteins retain the highly conserved FH2 catalytic core composed of specific motif combinations to maintain the basic function of microfilament nucleation [2], different subfamilies show significant differences in the N-terminal or C-terminal domains [3]. This structural diversity, which may be caused by variations in the number or position of motifs, directly drives the functional divergence of this gene family and is likely related to the environmental adaptability of its specific evolutionary lineages. For example, this study found that specific phylogenetic branches (such as AhFH2-4, AhFH2-10, and AhFH2-23) formed a tandem PTP_DSP_cys and PTEN_C2 domain complex due to the acquisition of N-terminal specific motifs. It is known that the PTEN_C2 domain in plant cells has a strong lipid-binding ability and can specifically recognize and bind to phosphatidylinositol on the cell membrane [80, 81]. Therefore, this motif-level structural innovation enables members of this subfamily to act as molecular bridges, quickly anchoring in specific plasma membrane microdomains when plants are subjected to abiotic stresses such as salt or heavy metals, and converting external physical and chemical signals into instructions for microfilament skeleton reorganization within the cell [82, 83]. Furthermore, the exon–intron arrangement pattern of genes and the high consistency with the phylogenetic branches further confirm that the structural differences in the AhFH2 family are not random mutations but rather a stable genetic imprint of subfunctionalization that occurred during long-term evolution [78, 79]. This fine structural differentiation has endowed the polyploid peanut with more flexible and diverse cellular defense and environmental adaptation mechanisms [78, 79].

In addition, the three-dimensional structure prediction might provide intuitive structural evidence for the functional divergence of the AhFH2 protein in the response to adverse conditions. Although the FH2 core domains of all members exhibited the classic helical structure necessary for microfilament nucleation, the three-dimensional modeling in this study (Fig. 6) revealed significant differences in the proportions and spatial arrangement of random coils and flexible loops among different subfamily members. These flexible non-helical regions typically confer high conformational flexibility to the protein [53]. When plants encounter non-biological adverse conditions such as heavy metal toxicity or salt stress, the physical and chemical microenvironment within the cells (such as the levels of reactive oxygen species ROS, pH value, and local ion concentrations) undergoes drastic fluctuations [33, 84]. Variants of AhFH2 with higher conformational flexibility may be more sensitive to these microenvironmental signals and, by rapidly changing the spatial conformation of their FH2 dimer, finely regulate their binding affinity to microfilaments and the rate of cytoskeleton reorganization [85, 86]. Therefore, these local three-dimensional structural variations and fine-tuning are not accidental but are necessary to meet the evolutionary requirements of specific phylogenetic branches in the complex stress response network, as they perform highly dynamic and specific cellular defense tasks as core hubs (Hubs) [79, 87].

A putative environment-hormone-cytoskeleton cascade regulatory mechanism

Gene transcriptional responses depend strongly on cis-regulatory elements in promoter regions, which act as central sites for integration of external environmental cues and initiation of downstream gene-specific expression [88, 89]. In this study, AhFH2 promoters were enriched in cis-elements responsive to ABA, methyl jasmonate (MeJA), and multiple stress-related signals, including drought and low temperature. Many physiological studies have shown that heavy metal toxicity and salt stress rapidly induce ROS accumulation in plants, accompanied by intense fluctuations in stress hormone signals such as ABA and JA [84]. These hormone and ROS signals may regulate antioxidant enzyme expression and also act as second messengers that directly or indirectly trigger disassembly and reassembly of the actin cytoskeleton [42, 90].

The abundance of hormone-responsive motifs in AhFH2 promoters suggests that transcription of these genes may be directly controlled by classical stress hormone networks. In this study, the distinct promoter architecture of specific AhFH2 members, characterized by a high density of stress-related motifs, suggests a potential for high transcriptional sensitivity. This sensitivity might allow the peanut plant to rapidly trigger a cascade of cytoskeletal remodeling in response to fluctuating environmental signals [88, 89].On this basis, we propose that peanut may use an environment-hormone-cytoskeleton remodeling cascade to respond to complex soil abiotic stresses. Through rapid transcriptional induction, plants may accumulate specific Formin proteins in locally damaged tissues and thereby regulate dynamic actin reorganization [9, 44]. This process may alter cell polarity and reshape vesicle transport pathways, which in turn could enhance cellular tolerance to adverse environments [43, 91].

Although the numerous cis-regulatory elements (such as light response and stress response motifs) identified in the AhFH2 promoter were based on computational predictions, transcriptome and qPCR expression profiling analyses provided strong supporting evidence. For instance, elements involved in “palisade mesophyll cell differentiation” were identified in the promoters of AhFH2-6 and AhFH2-25, which precisely aligned with the strong expression and stress-induced nature of these two genes observed in peanut leaves in this study. Similarly, as shown in the sub-genome expression heatmap of this study (Fig. 8), members highly enriched with light response elements (such as AhFH2-24) exhibited dominant expression in the above-ground parts (such as vegetative branches and aerial fruit spines). The high consistency between the computer predicted promoter structure and the actual transcriptional dynamics in vivo strongly supported their regulatory roles. Nevertheless, direct molecular validations (such as promoter-reporter gene fusion expression experiments) are still needed in the future to further functionally interpret these specific cis-regulatory networks [88]. Although the qRT-PCR data in this study strongly suggested that some candidate genes (particularly AhFH2-6 and AhFH2-25) might play an active transcriptional hub role in peanut’s response to specific abiotic stresses (such as heavy metal or salt stress), it must be pointed out that the significant upregulation or downregulation of genes at the transcriptional level only indicates their participation in a broad stress response network, which is not sufficient to directly establish their biological functions [92]. The screening of expression patterns at the whole genome level provides highly valuable candidate targets for this study to uncover the core stress-resistant genes [12, 14]. To establish the exact molecular mechanism of these candidate AhFH2 genes in conferring actual environmental tolerance to peanuts, further in-depth reverse genetics and cell biology research is still needed. For example, using CRISPR/Cas9 for gene knockout or identifying stress phenotypes in overexpression lines, combined with analysis of reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation and ion content determination, and using live fluorescence microscopy to directly observe the reorganization dynamics of the cell microfilament cytoskeleton under stress conditions [43, 44] will be the key research directions for a comprehensive understanding of the function of this family in the future.

Tissue specificity and subgenomic expression bias of AhFH2 in stress responses

When exposed to different abiotic stresses, plants often deploy highly specialized defense strategies [85]. The expression profiles in this study showed that core stress-responsive candidate genes such as AhFH2-6 and AhFH2-25 were strongly upregulated in roots under Al and Cd stress. Aluminum toxicity primarily targets the root tip and can rapidly cross-link pectin in the cell wall, disrupt the dynamic balance between the plasma membrane and the actin cytoskeleton, and ultimately inhibit root growth [44]. Under such conditions, damaged root tissues may require intensive vesicle-mediated transport of pectin, hemicellulose, and related defense proteins to the plasma membrane in order to remodel and repair the cell wall barrier and restrict further entry of toxic ions [43, 93]. Because long-distance vesicle transport in plant cells depends strongly on actin filament tracks, the strong induction of AhFH2-6 and AhFH2-25 may help reconstitute and stabilize the actin network under heavy metal stress, thereby maintaining endomembrane trafficking and barrier repair [85]. By contrast, excess Mn mainly induced strong expression of specific AhFH2 members in leaves. In addition, Se and NaCl stress strongly induced expression of core stress-responsive candidate genes such as AhFH2-6 and AhFH2-25 in leaves but suppressed their expression in roots. This pattern suggests that leaves and roots may adopt different defense strategies under specific adverse conditions. For example, Mn toxicity or salt stress may mainly damage the chloroplast photosynthetic electron transport chain, thereby triggering severe photooxidative stress and irreversible cellular injury [94, 95]. We therefore infer that strong actin cytoskeleton remodeling in leaves may participate broadly in chloroplast avoidance movement, in which actin filaments drive chloroplasts toward the lateral cell wall to reduce combined damage from strong light and toxic ions and thus preserve photosynthetic activity [53, 96].

More importantly, as an allotetraploid (AABB), peanut may show substantial homoeologous gene expression bias in response to the same metal stress [40, 65]. Recent genomic studies have shown that remodeling of homoeolog expression bias under adverse conditions is a key evolutionary mechanism through which polyploid plants buffer environmental stress and maintain phenotypic stability [78]. During the long evolutionary history of allotetraploid peanut, duplicated homoeologous gene pairs in the A and B subgenomes may have undergone substantial subfunctionalization and neofunctionalization. One copy may retain housekeeping functions required for maintenance of basic cellular structure, whereas the other may acquire specific promoter elements that respond to heavy metal stress. This subgenome-level transcriptional differentiation may give allotetraploid cultivated peanut greater regulatory flexibility and environmental adaptability than its diploid ancestors [65, 79].

Synergistic effects of core hub proteins and protein interaction networks

Complex cellular adaptive responses cannot be accomplished by single genes alone, but instead depend heavily on PPI complexes [64]. Our analysis suggested that several AhFH2 members, including AhFH2-19, AhFH2-28, and AhFH2-27, may function as major hubs in the regulatory network. In network topology, hub proteins often recruit upstream signaling kinases or coordinate downstream effectors, thereby having a central role in amplification of signaling cascades [87]. Based on our network analysis, the functional roles of AhFH2 members can be categorized by their connectivity. Specifically, AhFH2-27 and AhFH2-28 likely act as central hubs that coordinate broad signaling cascades, whereas AhFH2-1 and AhFH2-2 may represent peripheral nodes engaged in specific interactions. Furthermore, AhFH2-6 and AhFH2-25, despite their lower network connectivity, might serve as fundamental structural effectors that execute the actual actin nucleation and assembly processes [64, 87]. Moreover, the predicted network showed that AhFH2 proteins were closely associated with several uncharacterized proteins, including MHD and multiple Ahy proteins. This suggests that the actin cytoskeleton may provide not only physical support, but also extensive physical and functional connections with the microtubule cytoskeleton, vesicle trafficking systems, and kinase signaling pathways, thereby forming a cellular defense network with both structural resilience and signaling capacity [86]. Functional validation of these core hub proteins and their interactors may provide important targets for future gene-editing strategies to improve peanut stress tolerance. It’s worth noting that the AhFH2 protein interaction network (PPI) constructed in this study was derived from the orthology-based projection of the direct homologous genes of the model plant A. thaliana in the STRING database [64]. Therefore, this network essentially represents a predictive and theoretical regulatory framework [64]. Although this prediction provides valuable clues for identifying potential collaborators of AhFH2 (such as actin-binding proteins or upstream kinases), the complex post-translational modifications and subcellular compartmentalization in peanuts may profoundly affect these interactions [87]. To convert these theoretical predictions into concrete biological mechanisms, future experiments must rely on in vivo and in vitro biochemical techniques such as yeast two-hybrid (Y2H), dual-molecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC), and co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) to rigorously validate these key candidate protein interactions [97].

While AhFH2-19, AhFH2-28, and AhFH2-27 may serve as central nodes in the protein–protein interaction network to maintain baseline cellular and cytoskeletal homeostasis, our transcriptional profiling likely highlights a distinct set of genes as the primary responders to environmental stimuli. Specifically, AhFH2-6 and AhFH2-25 may emerge as the core stress-responsive candidate proteins that are likely transcriptionally activated to execute dynamic cytoskeleton remodeling under heavy metal and salt stresses.

Based on the systematic analyses in this study, we propose a modular and highly collaborative working model to explain the AhFH2-mediated responses to multiple abiotic stresses and the associated mechanism of cytoskeleton remodeling (Fig. 10). Under various abiotic stresses, including salt, Cd, Al, Mn, and Se, early physiological events such as ROS bursts and accumulation of plant hormones, particularly ABA and MeJA, may be triggered. Crucially, this stress response appears to rely on the synergistic action of different family members. Signal integration at the plasma membrane is likely mediated by members containing PTEN-like domains (such as AhFH2-4, AhFH2-10, and AhFH2-23), which can anchor to the membrane and recruit interacting partners like MHD and uncharacterized Ahy proteins to form functional PPI scaffold complexes. Simultaneously, the core stress-responsive candidates, AhFH2-6 and AhFH2-25, are robustly transcribed to serve as the primary drivers of actin remodeling. Working in concert with these membrane-anchored hub complexes, or translocating to specific organelles such as chloroplasts (AhFH2-6) and vacuoles (AhFH2-25), these highly induced effectors directly promote the massive nucleation of G-actin and assembly of F-actin bundles. This collaborative, organelle-specific cytoskeletal remodeling coordinates vesicle trafficking in roots and chloroplast protective movement in leaves, ultimately helping maintain cytoskeletal homeostasis and conferring broader tolerance to multiple abiotic stresses in peanut.

Conclusion

This study systematically identified 29 AhFH2 genes and outlined a putative molecular regulatory axis involving heavy metal signals, plant hormones, and actin filament remodeling through expression profiling and interaction network analysis. However, it must be acknowledged that the observed transcriptional changes of AhFH2 genes under various abiotic stresses might simply be part of a broader stress-responsive network, rather than representing direct causal mechanisms for stress tolerance. Nonetheless, these results strongly suggest that AhFH2 proteins play active roles as candidate structural regulators and potential effectors in peanut’s adaptation to complex environments. Although AhFH2 genes have remained highly conserved during evolution, substantial divergence in domains and promoter cis-elements appears to have conferred functional diversity under complex environmental conditions. Particularly, the significant expression bias observed between A and B subgenome homoeologs may provide strong quantitative evidence for their subfunctionalization in peanut evolution. qRT-PCR analysis further suggested that AhFH2-6 and AhFH2-25, as core stress-responsive candidate genes, have important tissue-specific regulatory roles in peanut responses to heavy metal stresses such as aluminum, cadmium, and manganese. Importantly, this study proposes a tissue-specific dual-track functional model in which AhFH2-driven cytoskeleton remodeling coordinates vesicle trafficking in roots and chloroplast protective movement in leaves. These findings provide a new perspective on the spatiotemporal adaptive mechanisms by which peanut responds to multiple heavy metal and salt stresses.
