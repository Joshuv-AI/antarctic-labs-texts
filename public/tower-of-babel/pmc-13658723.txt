Pharmacokinetics, Safety, and Exploratory Efficacy of Onradivir in Children with Acute Uncomplicated Influenza: A Multicenter, Dose-Ranging, Phase 2 Study
Authors: Qiang Qin, Chunying Ma, Yimin Yang, Haijun Li, Suiqi Xu, Yongxue Chi, Zhengzhen Tang, Sanjing Li, Xiaoxin Chen, Wei Zhao, Baoping Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSeasonal influenza brings high morbidity and mortality in children, with current antivirals limited by low efficacy and rising resistance. Onradivir (ZSP1273), a polymerase basic protein 2 (PB2) cap-binding inhibitor effective in adults, remains unstudied in pediatric influenza A. This phase 2 study evaluated its safety, pharmacokinetics, and preliminary efficacy in affected children.MethodsThis multicenter randomized open-label phase 2 trial enrolled children under 18 years with confirmed influenza A at 31 Chinese sites. Participants were 1:1 randomized to 5-day high-dose or low-dose onradivir granules stratified by age and weight. Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study?Children experience a substantial burden of seasonal influenza, while currently available antivirals have limitations, including modest clinical benefit, the need for early treatment, and the emergence of antiviral resistance.Onradivir, a polymerase basic protein 2 (PB2) cap-binding inhibitor, has demonstrated efficacy in adults with influenza A; however, its pharmacokinetics, safety, and appropriate dosing had not previously been evaluated in children.This phase 2 study evaluated whether age- and weight-based onradivir regimens could achieve adult-equivalent drug exposure with acceptable safety and tolerability in children aged 2–17 years with acute uncomplicated influenza A, while also exploring clinical and virological outcomes.What was learned from the study?Among 72 randomized participants, the high-dose regimen achieved systemic exposures comparable to efficacious adult targets. No serious adverse events or treatment discontinuations occurred. Median time to symptom alleviation was 28.6 h with the high-dose regimen and 33.7 h with the low-dose regimen, while viral ribonucleic acid decreased by approximately 1.5 log10 copies/ml within 24 h and 2.4 log10 copies/ml by day 5.No treatment-emergent resistance-associated PB2 substitutions were detected, and no clear exposure–response relationship was observed across the studied exposure range. Introduction

Seasonal influenza remains a substantial cause of morbidity and mortality in children worldwide, with the highest burden observed in younger age groups. Global estimates indicate that influenza contributes to a considerable proportion of acute lower respiratory infection-related hospitalizations and deaths in children, underscoring its ongoing clinical and public health impact [1, 2].

Current antiviral options for pediatric influenza are limited by modest efficacy, narrow therapeutic windows, and the emergence of resistance. Neuraminidase inhibitors, such as oseltamivir, provide only modest reductions in illness duration (approximately 18 h overall) and require early initiation to achieve clinical benefit, a condition often unmet in routine practice [3]. Treatment-emergent resistance is also more frequently observed in young children and may prolong viral shedding. Polymerase inhibitors such as baloxavir offer single-dose convenience but are associated with the emergence of resistance-associated substitutions during treatment and have restricted use in younger age groups [4–6]. Together, these limitations highlight a persistent unmet need for antiviral agents with improved efficacy, resistance profiles, and age-appropriate dosing strategies in children.

Onradivir (ZSP1273) is a novel small-molecule inhibitor targeting the polymerase basic protein 2 (PB2) cap-binding domain of the influenza A virus ribonucleic acid (RNA) polymerase, representing a mechanistically distinct antiviral class [7]. In adult patients with uncomplicated influenza A infection, randomized trials have shown that onradivir reduces time to symptom alleviation, achieves clinical efficacy comparable to oseltamivir, and produces greater reductions in viral RNA and infectious viral titers [8, 9]. These findings led to its regulatory approval for adult use in China in 2025 [10]. However, no prospective clinical data have been available to inform its pharmacokinetics, safety, or exposure–response relationships in pediatric populations.

To address this evidence gap, we conducted a multicenter, randomized, phase 2 study to evaluate the pharmacokinetics, safety, and tolerability of onradivir in children and adolescents with acute uncomplicated influenza A infection, and to explore preliminary clinical and virological efficacy.

Methods

Study Design

This multicenter, randomized, open-label, phase 2 study evaluated the pharmacokinetics, safety, and exploratory efficacy of onradivir granules in children and adolescents with acute uncomplicated influenza A infection. Patients were enrolled at 31 clinical sites across China (Supplementary Material, pp. 1–2). The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and International Council for Harmonisation Good Clinical Practice. The protocol was approved by institutional ethics committees (approval number [2024]-Y-175-A) and was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06656026). Written informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians, with age-appropriate assent for children aged 8 years or older.

Participants

Eligible participants were 2–17 years old, weighed ≥ 10 kg, presented within 72 h of symptom onset, and had laboratory-confirmed influenza A infection at screening, detected by rapid antigen test or nucleic acid amplification test. An axillary temperature ≥ 38.0 °C and at least one respiratory symptom (cough, sore throat, nasal congestion, or rhinorrhea) were required for inclusion. Key exclusion criteria included severe influenza, history of febrile seizures, significant chronic diseases, known immunodeficiency, antiviral use within 7 days, vaccination within 14 days, or concurrent infections requiring systemic treatment (Supplementary Material, pp. 3–4).

Randomization

Participants were randomly assigned (1:1) to high-dose or low-dose onradivir granules using a stratified block randomization scheme (strata: 2–<6 years; 6–<12 years; 12–17 years < 50 kg; 12–17 years ≥ 50 kg) generated by an independent statistician. Allocation was implemented via a central interactive system.

Procedures

Onradivir granules were administered orally once daily for 5 days. Doses were adjusted by age and body weight to target systemic exposures consistent with the effective adult regimen. Patients were monitored during treatment and followed through day 28 after the first dose (Supplementary Material, pp. 5–9).

Influenza symptoms were assessed twice daily on days 1–9, and once daily on days 10–14 using a 0–3 severity scale (Table S1; Supplementary Material, p. 7). Daily activity was recorded using a 0–10 visual analog scale. Body temperature was measured three times daily on days 1–3 and twice daily on days 4–14. All assessments were recorded in patient diary cards by parents, guardians, or participants.

Nasopharyngeal swabs were collected at baseline and on days 2, 4, 5, 10, and 29 (or upon early withdrawal) for quantitative influenza A viral RNA measurement by reverse transcription quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR). Viral culture, subtype determination (H3N2/H1N1), PB2 gene sequencing, and phenotypic susceptibility testing were conducted in a subset of samples at a central laboratory.

Sparse pharmacokinetic sampling was conducted around the first and fifth doses in predefined cohorts to characterize early and late post-dose concentrations. Exact sampling and dosing times were recorded. Plasma concentrations were measured using a validated high-performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry assay.

Safety assessments included treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs), clinical laboratory tests, vital signs, physical examinations, and 12-lead electrocardiograms. Adverse events were graded according to Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0. Influenza-related complications (sinusitis, bronchitis, otitis media, pneumonia, and neurological complications) were prospectively evaluated.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were: (1) safety and tolerability, which were evaluated through TEAEs, serious adverse events, treatment-related adverse events, clinical laboratory and electrocardiographic abnormalities, and influenza-related complications; and (2) steady-state pharmacokinetic parameters, maximum concentration (Cmax), trough concentration (Cmin), and area under the 24-h concentration–time curve (AUC0–24h), which were estimated using a population pharmacokinetic model.

Exploratory efficacy outcomes included: time to alleviation of influenza symptoms (all symptoms rated 0 or 1 for ≥ 21.5h); time to fever resolution (< 37.2 °C for ≥ 12 h); time to alleviation of respiratory symptoms; time to recovery of daily activities (score of 10; baseline < 10 required); proportions achieving symptom or fever alleviation at prespecified timepoints; changes from baseline in viral RNA load; baseline-corrected viral RNA AUC to 120 h; duration of detectable viral RNA; influenza-related complications; and antibacterial use.

Palatability and mouthfeel were assessed on days 1 and 2 in participants aged 6 years or older, and PB2 sequence changes with phenotypic susceptibility shifts were evaluated in a subset.

Statistical Analysis

Because this phase 2 study was exploratory, no formal hypothesis testing or formal sample-size calculation was planned. Analyses were descriptive and performed with SAS version 9.4. Continuous variables were summarized using means, standard deviations, medians, and ranges; categorical variables were summarized using counts and percentages with 95% Clopper–Pearson confidence intervals (CIs).

Efficacy analyses used the intention-to-treat (ITT) population, defined as all randomized participants who received at least one dose, whereas virological efficacy analyses were performed in the intention-to-treat infected (ITTI) population, defined as ITT participants with RT-qPCR-confirmed influenza A infection at baseline. Safety analyses used the safety set (SS), defined as all participants who received at least one dose of study treatment and had at least one post-dose safety assessment. Pharmacokinetic and exposure analyses used the pharmacokinetic analysis set (PKS). Participants with evaluable steady-state exposure and relevant outcomes comprised the exposure–response analysis set.

Time-to-event endpoints, including time to alleviation of influenza symptoms, fever resolution, alleviation of respiratory symptoms, and recovery of daily activities, were analyzed using Kaplan–Meier methods, and median times with 95% CIs were estimated. Proportions with symptom or fever alleviation were summarized with 95% CIs. Viral RNA load and changes from baseline were summarized descriptively. Baseline-corrected viral RNA AUC to 120 h was calculated; missing tail values were imputed using last-observation-carried-forward. Population pharmacokinetic modeling and exposure–response analyses were performed using NONMEM version 7.4, with details provided in the Supplementary Material (pp. 10–14).

Results

Study Population

Between November 15, 2024, and April 7, 2025, 72 participants were enrolled and randomized to high-dose (n = 36) or low-dose (n = 36) onradivir (Fig. 1). All participants received at least one dose and were included in the intention-to-treat (ITT) and pharmacokinetic analysis sets. Two participants in the high-dose group were excluded from the intention-to-treat infected (ITTI) population due to negative baseline RT-qPCR results, leaving 70 participants for virological analyses. Three participants in the high-dose group withdrew on day 2 before a post-dose safety assessment was available and were therefore excluded from the safety set. The safety set consequently comprised 69 participants: 33 in the high-dose group and 36 in the low-dose group (Table S2; Supplementary Material, p. 17).

Baseline characteristics were balanced between groups (Table 1). The mean age was 8.0 years, and 58.3% were male. Most participants were infected with influenza A (H1N1) (95.8%), with similar baseline viral RNA loads (~ 5.1 log10 copies/ml). Symptom onset within 48 h occurred in 86% of participants. Four participants discontinued the study prematurely for reasons unrelated to treatment (Supplementary Material, p. 17).

Primary Outcomes

Pharmacokinetics

Onradivir was rapidly absorbed, with peak plasma concentrations observed within 2 h after dosing, followed by a biphasic decline (Fig. 2). Systemic exposure increased with dose. In the high-dose group, median Cmax ranged from 3.03 to 6.01 μg/ml versus 0.91 to 1.04 μg/ml in the low-dose group; Cmin ranged from 0.20 to 0.29 μg/ml versus 0.07 to 0.22 μg/ml; and AUC0-24h ranged from 17.36 to 24.23 μg.h/ml versus 6.42 to 11.19 μg.h/ml (Table S3; Supplementary Material, p. 18).

Pharmacokinetic profiles of onradivir in children. A Plasma concentration–time profiles following the first dose. B Plasma concentration–time profiles following the final dose at steady state

Population pharmacokinetic modeling supported a two-compartment model with first-order absorption and allometric scaling by body weight. Parameter estimates were stable, with acceptable precision (Table 2). Model diagnostics demonstrated good agreement between observed and predicted concentrations, and visual predictive checks indicated adequate model performance after both the first and fifth doses (Figures S1 and S2; Supplementary Material, pp. 19–20). No clinically meaningful differences in exposure were observed between fed and fasted states (Figure S3; Supplementary Material, p. 21).

Safety

TEAEs occurred in 49 of 69 participants (71.0%), more frequently in the low-dose group (80.6%) than in the high-dose group (60.6%; Table 3). Of the 156 treatment-emergent adverse events, 147 (94.2%) were grade 1 or 2. Grade 3 treatment-emergent adverse events were reported in 9 of 69 participants (13.0%). Most events resolved completely without specific medical intervention, and no serious adverse events or adverse events leading to treatment discontinuation occurred. Treatment-related events were reported in 44.9% of participants, the most common of which were decreased neutrophil count (20.3%), low white blood cell count (14.5%), increased lymphocyte percentage (14.5%), and elevated creatine phosphokinase-MB (14.5%). Gastrointestinal events were mild and infrequent.

Secondary Outcomes

Virologic Outcomes

Viral RNA declined rapidly in both groups (Table 4; Fig. 3; Table S4; Supplementary Material, p. 22). At 24 h, mean viral RNA decreased by – 1.52 log10 copies/ml in the high-dose group and – 1.51 log10 copies/ml in the low-dose group. Reductions reached approximately – 1.7 log10 by day 3 and – 2.4 log10 by day 5, with sustained suppression through day 15.

The proportion of participants with detectable viral RNA decreased over time, from 97.1% and 94.4% at day 2 to 38.2% and 27.8% at day 15 in the high- and low-dose groups, respectively (Table S5; Supplementary Material, p. 23). Median duration of detectable viral RNA was 70.73 h (95% CI, 65.55–94.03) in the high-dose group and 77.21 h (64.62–89.65) in the low-dose group (Table S6; Supplementary Material, p. 24).

Baseline-corrected viral RNA AUC₀–120 h was similar between groups (230.2 vs. 240.5 h.log10 copies/ml), as were infectious viral titer AUC values (34.6 vs. 43.6 h.log10 50% tissue culture infectious dose [TCID50]/ml) (Table S7; Supplementary Material, p. 25). Infectious viral titers declined more rapidly, with median durations of detectable infectious virus of 19.68 h and 21.58 h in the high- and low-dose groups, respectively. Fewer than 10% of participants remained culture-positive after day 4 (Table S5-S6; Supplementary Material, p. 23-24).

PB2 sequencing showed baseline genetic variability but no clustering by subgroup. Although amino acid substitutions were observed in 37.5% of post-treatment isolates, all remained phenotypically susceptible, and no treatment-emergent resistance-associated PB2 substitutions were detected.

Symptom Outcomes

Time to alleviation of influenza symptoms was similar between groups. Median time was 28.63 h (95% CI, 17.88–59.07) in the high-dose group and 33.65 h (19.38–42.73) in the low-dose group (Fig. 4; Table 4). More than 90% of participants achieved symptom alleviation.

Median time to fever resolution was 23.47 h (17.42–29.37) in the high-dose group and 26.03 h (14.52–36.72) in the low-dose group. By 48 h, 83.33% and 91.67% of participants, respectively, were afebrile.

Respiratory symptoms resolved later, with median times of 59.07 h (16.88–85.20) and 33.65 h (13.17–43.88) in the high- and low-dose groups, respectively. Approximately 80% of participants achieved overall symptom alleviation by day 4. Recovery of daily activities occurred later, with median times of 113.43 h and 114.58 h, respectively. Additional analyses are provided in the Supplementary Material (Tables S8–S13; Supplementary Material, pp. 26–31).

Exposure–Response Analyses

No meaningful associations were observed between steady-state exposure (Cmax, Cmin, AUC0-24h) and clinical or virological outcomes. Median time to symptom alleviation ranged from 20.8 to 30.6 h across Cmax quartiles (P = 0.137), with no consistent trends for fever resolution or respiratory symptom alleviation (all P ≥ 0.778).

Virological outcomes were similarly consistent across exposure levels. Median duration of detectable viral RNA ranged from 68.6 to 82.3 h (P = 0.167), and viral RNA AUC values overlapped across quartiles (P = 0.770). Linear regression analyses confirmed no association between exposure metrics and symptom alleviation (Figure S4; Supplementary Material, p. 33). Overall, exposure did not differentiate clinical or virological responses within the studied dosing range (Table S14; Supplementary Material, p. 32).

Subgroup and Sensitivity Analyses

Subgroup analyses by age showed similar patterns for viral clearance and symptom outcomes, although confidence intervals were wider in smaller subgroups (Supplementary Material, pp. 24–28). Sensitivity analyses yielded consistent results, with no evidence of age-by-treatment interaction.

Other Outcomes

Palatability was acceptable. On day 1, 37.5% of participants in the high-dose group and 72.0% in the low-dose group reported visual analog scale scores ≥ 50, increasing on day 2 (Table S15; Supplementary Material, p. 34). Refusal to take medication was uncommon (6.1%), and vomiting during dosing decreased from 6.1% on day 1 to none on day 2 (Table S16; Supplementary Material, p. 35).

Discussion

In this multicenter, randomized, phase 2 study in children and adolescents with acute uncomplicated influenza A, onradivir achieved systemic exposures comparable to efficacious adult targets and was associated with favorable safety, rapid symptom resolution, and sustained virological suppression. Across both dose groups, clinical and virological responses occurred early, no treatment-emergent resistance-associated PB2 substitutions were detected, and exploratory analyses showed no exposure–response relationship within the studied range. These findings support the adequacy of the selected pediatric dosing strategy and address an important evidence gap in antiviral development for pediatric influenza.

The safety profile observed in this study was consistent with prior adult trials [8, 9]. Most adverse events were mild or moderate, and no serious adverse events or treatment discontinuations occurred. Laboratory abnormalities, including transient reductions in neutrophil or white blood cell counts and elevations in creatine phosphokinase-MB, were generally low grade and self-limiting. Gastrointestinal events were infrequent and mild. Notably, no dose-limiting toxicities were observed, despite higher systemic exposures in the high-dose group. Together with population pharmacokinetic analyses indicating modest inter-individual variability and no exposure–toxicity relationship, these findings support the feasibility of simple, weight-based dosing strategies in pediatric populations, although continued monitoring of tolerability remains warranted.

In the context of existing antiviral therapies, the clinical course observed with onradivir appears comparable to, and in some respects favorable relative to, currently available agents. Median time to symptom alleviation was approximately 29–34 h, with more than 90% of participants achieving symptom resolution. In contrast, neuraminidase inhibitors have demonstrated more modest reductions in illness duration (approximately 0.5–1.5 days) and are highly dependent on early initiation of treatment [11]. In pediatric populations, the clinical benefit of oseltamivir is largely restricted to treatment initiated within 48 h of symptom onset, with diminishing effects thereafter [12]. In the present study, a proportion of participants initiated treatment beyond 48 h, yet symptom resolution remained rapid, suggesting that onradivir may retain activity beyond the narrow treatment window typically associated with neuraminidase inhibitors, although this observation requires confirmation in controlled comparative trials.

Virologically, onradivir induced rapid and sustained suppression of influenza A replication. Viral RNA declined by approximately 1.5 log10 copies/ml within 24 h and by approximately 2.4 log10 by day 5, with sustained suppression thereafter. The duration of detectable infectious virus was short, with fewer than 10% of participants remaining culture-positive after day 4. These kinetics are at least comparable to those reported for baloxavir marboxil and generally more rapid than those observed with oseltamivir in pediatric studies [4, 13]. However, consistent with prior observations, reductions in viral load did not clearly differentiate between dose groups, underscoring the importance of evaluating both virological and patient-centered outcomes [14, 15].

The absence of detectable resistance-associated PB2 substitutions is notable. Treatment-emergent resistance has been a key limitation of existing antivirals, particularly for polymerase inhibitors such as baloxavir, in which PA/I38 substitutions have been reported in up to approximately 20% of pediatric recipients and are associated with delayed symptom resolution and prolonged viral shedding [6, 13, 16]. Even newer polymerase inhibitors demonstrate measurable, albeit lower, rates of resistance emergence [17]. In contrast, all isolates in the present study remained phenotypically susceptible, suggesting a potentially higher genetic barrier to resistance with PB2 inhibition. This is biologically plausible, given that PB2-mediated cap binding is essential for viral transcription and is relatively conserved [7, 18, 19]. Nonetheless, the sample size and duration of follow-up limit definitive conclusions, and larger studies with extended surveillance are required.

Despite a two- to three-fold difference in systemic exposure between dose groups, no meaningful exposure–response relationship was observed for clinical or virological endpoints. Similar findings have been reported in adult dose-ranging studies of onradivir [8, 9], and in trials of other antivirals, in which increasing exposure beyond target thresholds does not confer additional clinical benefit [20]. These findings are consistent with pharmacological saturation once effective inhibitory concentrations are achieved and support the selection of dosing regimens that achieve, but do not substantially exceed, target exposure ranges.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was an open-label, non-comparative phase 2 study without a placebo or active comparator, limiting causal inference and cross-treatment comparisons. Second, the sample size was modest, and subgroup analyses were underpowered. Third, the study population primarily comprised otherwise healthy children with uncomplicated influenza A, which may limit generalizability to younger infants, high-risk populations, or severe disease. In addition, 95.8% of participants were infected with influenza A(H1N1), providing very limited evidence regarding A(H3N2) and restricting the generalizability of the clinical, virological, and resistance findings across influenza A subtypes. Fourth, follow-up duration was limited, precluding assessment of longer-term outcomes, including delayed resistance emergence or complications. Finally, exploratory efficacy endpoints were not powered for formal hypothesis testing.

Conclusions

In summary, high doses of onradivir in children and adolescents achieved adult-equivalent systemic exposure, were well tolerated, and were associated with rapid symptom resolution and robust suppression of influenza A viral replication, without evidence of treatment-emergent resistance. Although no obvious exposure–response gradient was observed, the current pediatric dosing regimen remains reasonable. These data provide a strong pharmacological and clinical rationale for confirmatory, comparator-controlled trials to establish the efficacy of onradivir in pediatric influenza and to define its role alongside existing antiviral therapies.
