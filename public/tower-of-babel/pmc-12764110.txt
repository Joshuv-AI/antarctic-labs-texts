Machine learning predicts non-thyroidal illness syndrome (NTIS) occurrence and mortality in sepsis patients
Authors: Ye Li, Yafei Li, Jundong He, Yan Wang, Yunying Cai, Yanling Li, Jinbo Luo, Zhiqing Zhang, Heng Su
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-thyroid disease syndrome (NTIS) occurs in various serious illnesses, with sepsis being a common cause. Early identification of NTIS risk in sepsis patients is crucial. This study aimed to develop machine learning-based predictive models for the NTIS occurrence and mortality in sepsis patients.MethodsClinical data were collected from 963 recruited sepsis patients, and 890 of them were retained for NTIS occurrence prediction after data selection and processing, while 797 NTIS patients were selected for mortality prediction. LASSO regression and correlation analysis identified key clinical features. We evaluated the predictive capacity on both occurrence and mortality of eight machine learning algorithms using Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) and Sensitivity and Specificity. For mortality prediction, univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed to identify risk factors and construct a nomogram model, which was further validated by decision curve and survival analyses.ResultsXGBoost performed best in NTIS occurrence prediction, while LASSO showed the highest efficiency for mortality prediction. Lower levels of T3, FT3, T4, and KAP/LAM were associated with higher NTIS risk. Mechanical ventilation, norepinephrine, abdominal infection, cardiopulmonary resuscitation with sudden cardiac arrest, multiple organ failure, novel coronavirus infection, and gender were associated with NTIS mortality. Additionally, univariate and multivariate Cox regression underscored age, mechanical ventilation, pulmonary infection, multiple organ failure, norepinephrine, UA, and FIB as independent risk factors for mortality.ConclusionsXGBoost and LASSO models showed promising performance in predicting NTIS occurrence and mortality in sepsis patients, respectively, and integrating them with clinical risk factors may improve risk stratification and support clinical decision-making. However, the findings are derived from a single-center dataset, and the use of data imputation to address missing values may introduce model instability, warranting cautious interpretation and external validation.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03629-6.Keywords: Machine learning, Sepsis, Non-thyroidal illness syndrome (NTIS), Occurrence prediction, Mortality prediction

Introduction

Sepsis is a life-threatening medical condition caused by the body’s dysfunctional response to infection [1]. The fatality rate of sepsis in intensive care unit (ICU) patients has been reported to be as high as 41.9% [2]. It is worth noting that many patients with sepsis are accompanied by changes in thyroid hormone levels. Abnormalities in thyroid hormone levels in patients with acute or chronic non-thyroidal illnesses, without any intrinsic dysfunction of the thyroid gland, are known as non-thyroidal illness syndrome (NTIS) [3]. NTIS is a common endocrine disorder characterized by dysregulation of thyroid hormone levels that occur during critical illness, particularly in patients with sepsis [4, 5]. It is associated with adverse clinical outcomes, including increased mortality rates and prolonged hospital stays [6]. The most typical hormonal changes were low triiodothyronine (T3), low or normal thyroxine (T4), and normal or slightly reduced thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels [7, 8].

Studies have provided valuable insights into the risk factors for developing NTIS. During stressful events such as infection, trauma, and surgery, the body releases inflammatory mediators, such as interleukin and tumor necrosis factor. These inflammatory mediators can inhibit the production and conversion of thyroid hormones, resulting in a decrease in serum T3 levels [9]. Numerous studies have shown that decreased T3 levels are associated with poor clinical outcomes and mortality in patients with multiple specific diseases and in the ICU [10–13]. Therefore, early identification and management of NTIS in sepsis patients are crucial for improving patient outcomes and reducing the burden on healthcare systems.

Machine learning algorithms have emerged as powerful tools for predicting and analyzing complex diseases, including sepsis [14], diabetes [15], breast cancer [16], and cardiovascular [17]. By leveraging large datasets and advancing modeling techniques, machine learning algorithms can uncover hidden patterns and relationships within patient data, enabling more accurate and timely predictions [18]. In addition, machine learning can provide patients with personalized treatment plans based on their individual characteristics and clinical status [19].

In this study, various machine learning algorithms were used to predict NTIS occurrence and mortality in sepsis patients. By utilizing clinical indicators related to sepsis, we aimed to develop machine learning models that accurately predict the occurrence of NTIS in sepsis patients for early detection and intervention, and to build models that effectively predict mortality risk in sepsis patients with NTIS, facilitating personalized treatment strategies and optimizing resource allocation.

Methods

Study population

This study was approved by the Medical Committee of the First People’s Hospital of Yunnan Province (KHLL2023-KY202). Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

A total of 963 sepsis patients were recruited in the Department of Emergency Medicine, Department of Critical Care Medicine, and Department of Infectious Diseases at the First People’s Hospital of Yunnan Province from June 2017 to October 2023. Clinical parameters related to infection and sepsis of each patient were collected. Inclusion criteria included: (1) age ≥ 18 years, and (2) diagnosed with sepsis or sepsis combined with NTIS. The diagnosis of sepsis followed the Sepsis-3 criteria [1].

Patients were further divided into four groups: (1) non-NTIS group (T3 > 1.3 nmol/L, T4 > 66 nmol/L, FT3 > 3.1 pmol/L, FT4 > 12 pmol/L, 0.27 mIU/L < TSH < 4.2 mIU/L); (2) NTIS1 group (T3 < 1.3 nmol/L and/or FT3 < 3.1 pmol/L), NTIS2 group (T3 < 1.3 nmol/L; (3) T4 < 66 nmol/L and/or FT3 < 3.1 pmol/L, FT4 < 12 pmol/L), and NTIS3 group (T3 < 1.3 nmol/L, T4 < 66 nmol/L and/or FT3 < 3.1 pmol/L; and (4) FT4 < 12 pmol/L and/or TSH < 0.27 mIU/L). The cohort comprised 963 patients admitted with sepsis, of whom 857 were diagnosed with NTIS. The remaining 106 patients, who had sepsis only, were designated as the Normal group (Supplemental Table S1).

Patient selection and data processing

After excluding 73 patients with more than 25% missing clinical characteristics, 890 sepsis patients characterized by 155 clinical attributes remained. Quality control was conducted as follows: (1) excluding 25 clinical characteristics with more than 40% missing values; (2) excluding 21 clinical characteristics with minimal variation; (3) addressing missing values using multiple imputation. Specifically, missing values in the remaining variables were imputed using the Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) method implemented in the mice package (version 3.16.0) [20] in R (v. 4.3.0) [21]. Predictive mean matching (pmm) was applied as the imputation technique, with five imputations generated under the assumption of missing at random (MAR). Finally, 890 patients with 109 variables were selected for further analysis (Supplementary Table S2).

Feature screening was performed using the Lasso regression model, and seven final key clinical features, free T3 (FT3), T3, T4, FERR, CD3, CD3, + CD8 + , and KAP/LAM, were used to build the NTIS prediction model (Supplementary Table S3). In addition, 26 key clinical features of 797 NTIS patients were used to establish a mortality prediction model (Supplementary Table S4). In both prediction models, patients were randomly divided into training and testing sets (4:1) ensuring a 1:1 ratio between the control and the NTIS group, as well as between survivors and non-survivors. To preserve this ratio, missing values were interpolated (Figure S1). The baseline characteristics of the 890 patients in the occurrence prediction model are listed in Supplementary Table S5. The baseline characteristics of the 797 patients in the mortality prediction model are listed in Supplementary Table S6.

Data splitting

In the NTIS occurrence prediction models, 890 sepsis patients were divided into training [80% (Normal = 75, NTIS = 638)] and testing [20% (Normal = 18, NTIS = 159)] sets. In the NTIS mortality prediction models, 797 NTIS patients were divided into training [80% (Alive = 529, Dead = 109)] and testing [20% (Alive = 132, Dead = 27] sets. After splitting the data, eight ML algorithms were trained and tested using the training and testing data sets, respectively. Model performance was evaluated for both phases.

Machine learning algorithms

Eight machine learning classification algorithms were employed, covering a range of modeling strategies: regularized logistic regression (regLogistic) and Lasso regression (Lasso) as linear models; Support Vector Machine with linear kernels (SVMlinear) as a kernel-based method; neural network (NNet) as a neural network model; Naive Bayes as a probabilistic model; and K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Random Forest (RF), and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) as non-linear ensemble learning methods. These algorithms were selected to capture diverse patterns in the dataset, including both linear and non-linear relationships.

The eight algorithms were implemented using the R “caret” package (version 6.0-94). The performances of machine learning algorithms were evaluated on the independent held-out test set with the following metrics: the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC AUC), accuracy, precision, recall, and balanced accuracy. ROC AUC was calculated using the R “pROC” package (v 1.18.5); the remaining four parameters (accuracy, precision, recall, balanced accuracy) were obtained using the “caret” package (v 6.0-94).

Statistical analysis

We analyzed correlations between clinical features using Pearson correlations by the Hmisc package (v 5.1-2) in R (v. 4.3.0). Lasso regression analysis was completed by the R “glmnet” package (version 4.1-8) to determine the important clinical features. Lasso is suitable for high-dimensional datasets, as it simultaneously performs variable selection and regularization, reducing overfitting while retaining important predictors. This method also relies on the sparsity assumption, meaning that only a subset of predictors has non-zero coefficients in the model [22, 23]. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were conducted to identify clinical features significantly associated with NTIS patients’ survival. Cox regression assumes proportional hazards (PH) and a linear relationship between continuous covariates and the log hazard [24, 25]. The PH assumption was assessed using the Schoenfeld residual test. Variables with potential time-dependent effects were further examined, and a stratified Cox model was constructed as a sensitivity analysis, with selected variables included as stratification factors. Non-linear effects of continuous covariates were evaluated using restricted cubic splines (RCS). All implemented tests were two-sided, with a statistical significance set as P < 0.05.

Results

The screening of important clinical features by least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (Lasso) algorithm

Lasso regression was employed to identify key clinical features associated with the occurrence and mortality of NTIS. In the NTIS occurrence prediction models, the lowest error rate was achieved at a lambda.min of 0.0085, resulting in the selection of seven critical factors influencing NTIS occurrence (Fig. 1a, b). To ensure optimal feature retention, a greedy recursive deletion strategy was applied to filter features by removing highly correlated independent variables (correlation > 0.9). Ultimately, no features were eliminated, leaving seven key factors (FT3, T3, T4, FERR, CD3, CD3 + CD8 + , KAP/LAM) for subsequent analysis (Fig. 1c).

Performance of machine learning models

We assessed the eight machine learning algorithms for predictive performance in occurrence and mortality, including XGBoost, RF, regularized logistic regression, Lasso, SVMlinear, NNet, Naive Bayes, and KNN. ROC curves, sensitivity, and specificity were employed to evaluate these algorithms.

For the NTIS occurrence prediction models, the sample was divided into training (Normal = 75, NTIS = 638) and testing sets (Normal = 75, NTIS = 638) at a 4:1 ratio. The performance of the eight models was evaluated in the training set. The XGBoost classifier exhibited the highest ROC AUC value, indicating superior predictive performance (Fig. 2a). In clinical terms, this high AUC suggests that XGBoost can reliably distinguish patients at risk of NTIS from those with normal thyroid function, which could aid early detection and intervention. The validation cohort showed that the RF model has the highest AUC value of 1 in the validation cohort, with the model prediction results perfectly matching actual results (Figure S2). However, such apparent perfection should be interpreted cautiously, as it may reflect overfitting due to the sample size and class imbalance rather than true clinical performance. Consequently, the RF model was excluded.

For other metrics, accuracy ranged from 0.780 to 1.000, sensitivity ranged from 0.722 to 1.000, specificity ranged from 0.761 to 1.000, precision ranged from 0.309 to 1.000, recall ranged from 0.722 to 1.000, and F1-score ranged from 0.466 to 1.000. XGBoost achieved the highest accuracy, sensitivity, recall, and F1-score after excluding RF in the validation cohort. Consequently, a comprehensive analysis of all parameters identified the XGBoost algorithm as the best-performing machine learning method. Sensitivity analysis through Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) of the validation set further supported XGBoost, demonstrating its potential to provide a meaningful net clinical benefit compared with other models across a range of threshold probabilities (Fig. 2b).

To further explore how individual variables in the machine learning models influence outcome predictions, we conducted a SHapley Additive exPlanation (SHAP) analysis [26]. In the NTIS occurrence prediction model, SHAP values were calculated for each feature in the XGBoost algorithm, as summarized in Fig. 3b. The results revealed that lower levels of T3, FT3, T4, and the KAP/LAM ratio were associated with an increased probability of NTIS occurrence. A detailed SHAP analysis of the XGBoost model provided additional insights (Fig. 3c). The upper section of the plot illustrated the SHAP values for all observations, highlighting the contributions of key features to the model’s predictions. These features included T3, FT3, T4, CD3, KAP/LAM, CD3 + CD8 + , and FERR. Among these, T3 (blue), FT3 (dark blue), and T4 (green) were consistently among the most significant contributors across multiple observations. The lower section of the plot focused on a subset of observations (approximately from 0 to 90), providing a detailed view of the individual contributions of each feature within this range. The influences of T3 and FT3, along with FT4 remained significant (Fig. 3c).

Overall, the SHAP force plot highlighted the top six variables, which accounted for most of the variation in the SHAP value calculations. In the NTIS mortality prediction model, a different set of features emerged as critical. Mechanical ventilation ranked highest in feature importance, followed by norepinephrine use, abdominal infection, cardiopulmonary resuscitation after sudden cardiac arrest, multiple organ failure, novel coronavirus infection, and gender (Fig. 3d). These variables collectively provided a comprehensive view of the factors contributing to mortality predictions.

Prognostic analysis of clinical features in NTIS mortality model

To evaluate the effects of clinicopathological factors on the prognosis and survival of patients with NTIS, univariate and multivariate Cox analyses were conducted. The PH assumption was assessed using the Schoenfeld residual test. In the main Cox model (cox1), including all covariates, the global test of PH was not significant (P = 0.093) (Figure S4a), indicating that the overall model generally satisfied the PH assumption. Although some individual variables, namely PulmInf (pulmonary infection) and UA (uric acid), showed marginal p-values, visual inspection of Schoenfeld residuals revealed no clear time-dependent trends, and their inclusion did not materially affect model interpretation. As a sensitivity analysis, a stratified Cox model (cox2) was constructed with Sex and PulmInf as stratification factors. In this model, the global test remained non-significant (P = 0.77) (Figure S4b), confirming that all variables satisfied the PH assumption. RCS analysis of continuous variables revealed no significant non-linear effects (P > 0.05) (Figure S5), supporting the use of linear terms in the model.

Our univariate Cox analysis of the 27 clinicopathological factors in the training set (Alive = 529, Dead = 109) showed that the 17 predictive factors selected had an impact on the prognosis and survival of NTIS patients (P < 0.05; Fig. 4a). Those factors with significant single factors were included in the multi-factor Cox analysis, and the optimal model was screened by the stepwise regression method. As shown in Fig. 4b, age, mechanical ventilation, pulmonary infection, multiple organ failure, norepinephrine, UA, FIB were independent risk factors for the prognosis of NTIS patients (P < 0.05). Based on these findings, we developed a predictive nomogram to estimate individual patient risk. In this nomogram, each risk factor is assigned a specific score, and these scores are summed to obtain a total score. This total score allows mortality rate predictions at 1, 2, and 3 months (Fig. 4c).

Discussion

In this study, we explored a predictive model for the incidence and mortality of NTIS in patients with sepsis by integrating a variety of machine learning algorithms with clinical features to enhance prediction accuracy. We found that the XGBoost model performed well in predicting the occurrence of NTIS, with the highest ROC AUC values and stable performance in both the validation and test sets. For mortality prediction, Lasso regression models showed better generalization ability and had the highest AUC values in the training and test sets. Variables that had the greatest impact on the prediction model were identified: lower levels of T3, FT3, T4, and KAP/LAM, which were associated with higher NTIS risk. Mechanical ventilation, norepinephrine, abdominal infection, cardiopulmonary resuscitation with sudden cardiac arrest, multiple organ failure, novel coronavirus infection, and gender were associated with NTIS mortality.

The thyroid gland secretes two biologically active thyroid hormones: T4 and T3, which are essential for metabolic and physiological functions of the body [27]. T3 levels can be affected by a number of factors, including thyroid function, the patient’s health, and treatment [28]. In patients with sepsis, thyroid function can be suppressed due to the inflammatory response and other pathophysiological processes, leading to a decrease in T3 levels, which triggers NTIS [29, 30]. FT4 plays an important role in human metabolism, growth and development [31]. A decrease in FT4 levels is associated with an increased incidence of NTIS [32]. Therefore, the occurrence of NTIS can be detected and intervened early by monitoring T3 and FT4 levels [26]. KAP/LAM is an immunological indicator, representing the ratio of κ light chain to λ light chain in serum, typically used to assess abnormalities in immunoglobulin light chains and plasma cell diseases [33]. KAP and LAM free light chains are produced from B cells during immunoglobulin synthesis [34]. Previous research has shown that KAP and LAM light chains are associated with malignant B-cell proliferation [33]. Therefore, we speculate that KAP/LAM may affect NTIS by influencing the immune response in sepsis patients.

Our study also highlighted mechanical ventilation and norepinephrine as the top important predictors for NTIS mortality. Mechanical ventilation is a supportive treatment used to maintain respiratory function, which is commonly used in ICU [35]. Mechanical ventilation can impact the endocrine system, including thyroid functions [36]. Actually, in critically ill adult patients who underwent cardiac surgery, the duration of mechanical ventilation was significantly decreased [36]. Thus, we hypothesized that mechanical ventilation may increase the risk of death in patients with NTIS due to a reduction in FT3 [37]. Moreover, norepinephrine is commonly used in clinical settings to treat shock and other severe circulatory failure states [38, 39]. The use of this agent often indicates a severe condition in the patient, which could suggest an association between norepinephrine and high mortality rates. These findings are consistent with the results of our study.

In addition, the risk score model based on Cox regression analysis provides a clinically actionable tool for assessing the prognosis of patients with NTIS. The univariate and multivariate Cox regression results underscored age, mechanical ventilation, pulmonary infection, multiple organ failure, norepinephrine, UA, and FIB as independent risk factors for mortality. Aging is accompanied by a gradual decline in the function of various systems in the body, including the immune system and endocrine system. The function reduction may affect the synthesis, secretion and metabolism of thyroid hormones, thereby increasing the risk of death from NTIS [40, 41]. Mechanical ventilation is often required in critically ill patients with NTIS, which itself is a contributor to higher mortality risk [42]. The need for mechanical ventilation indicates severe respiratory failure or compromise, often correlating with a worse overall prognosis. Pulmonary infections, particularly in the setting of NTIS, exacerbate the inflammatory response, further disrupting thyroid hormone metabolism, and worsening the clinical picture [43].

Multiple organ failure is a critical factor affecting survival. Studies have indicated that NTIS is closely associated with multiorgan dysfunction following surgical repair of type A aortic dissection. This association may further contribute to an increased incidence of in-hospital mortality and postoperative complications [44]. The use of norepinephrine, while essential in managing hemodynamic instability in these patients, may also reflect a higher possibility of poor outcomes [45].

By integrating these clinical and laboratory parameters into a risk score model based on Cox regression, our study not only facilitates individualized risk stratification but also underscores the multifactorial nature of NTIS-related mortality. The predictive models developed in this study may offer potential value for clinical decision-making. The XGBoost-based NTIS occurrence model could, in principle, be integrated into an electronic medical record (EMR) system to provide an automated estimate of NTIS risk upon hospital admission. Such early risk estimation may help clinicians consider closer monitoring or earlier endocrine assessment. Similarly, the nomogram derived from Cox regression provides an individualized estimate of mortality risk for patients with NTIS. In clinical settings, this tool may assist clinicians in identifying higher-risk patients and supporting discussions regarding prognosis. Compared with traditional severity scoring systems such as the Sepsis-related Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score [46] and the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Enquiry (APACHE II) score [47], which were originally developed to assess overall physiological dysfunction and critical illness severity, our models incorporate NTIS-specific clinical and biochemical characteristics. Such tailored predictive ability may provide complementary prognostic information to assessments of general critical illness severity. Despite potential applications, these models have limitations that warrant careful interpretation. First, the patient sample was relatively homogeneous, as data were only collected from the First People's Hospital of Yunnan Province. External validation studies are needed to confirm the generalizability of our method across different populations and treatment practices. Second, missing data were handled using interpolation, which may introduce model instability and potentially reduce predictive performance when applied to external datasets. Furthermore, while machine learning algorithms can provide models that predict the occurrence and mortality of NTIS, translating these models into real clinical decision support tools requires further exploration and validation.

In summary, our study critically evaluates machine learning algorithms for predicting NTIS occurrence and mortality in sepsis patients, highlighting their potential to transform clinical practice and improve patient outcomes. By harnessing the predictive capabilities of machine learning, we aim to develop models that assist clinicians in identifying at-risk patients and guiding treatment decisions.
