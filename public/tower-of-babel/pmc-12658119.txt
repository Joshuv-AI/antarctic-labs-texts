Association of World Index for Sustainability and Health Score with metabolic syndrome in Iranian adults from the PERSIAN Dena cohort study
Authors: Esmail Karami, Vahid Hadi, Farshad Amirkhizi, Soudabeh Hamedi-Shahraki, Seyed Fazel Zinat Motlagh, Javad Harooni, Saeid Hadi, Somayyeh Asghari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Metabolic syndrome (MetS) affects 20–25% of adults worldwide, with a concerning prevalence of approximately 32% observed in Iran. While various dietary patterns have been linked to MetS, the association between sustainable diets and MetS remains underexplored. This study investigates the association between the World Index for Sustainability and Health (WISH) score and MetS among Iranian adults. In this cross-sectional study, 2971 adults aged 35 to 70 years were selected from the PERSIAN Dena Cohort data. Dietary intake was assessed using validated food frequency questionnaires (FFQs). The WISH score was calculated based on thirteen food items, taking into account both health and environmental sustainability. In this study, the criteria established by the National Cholesterol Education Program (NCEP ATP III) were used to define the MetS. Individuals with the highest WISH score had 48% lower odds of having metabolic syndrome compared with those with the lowest score (adjusted OR 0.52; 95% CI 0.30–0.89). The odds of having MetS (P for trend = 0.002) and certain components, such as abdominal adiposity (P for trend = 0.001), abnormal glucose homeostasis (P for trend < 0.001), and elevated serum triglycerides (P for trend = 0.030), decrease with increasing quartiles of WISH after controlling for potential confounders.Higher adherence to environmentally sustainable and healthy dietary patterns, as measured by WISH scores, is associated with lower odds of MetS among Iranian adults. These findings underscore the importance of promoting sustainable diets for both human health and environmental benefits.

Introduction

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is a cluster of metabolic abnormalities characterized by central obesity, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and glucose intolerance. Over the past decade, it has emerged as a major global health concern. Recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses estimate that the global prevalence of MetS ranges from 20 to 25% among adults; however, there is considerable heterogeneity across different populations and definitions used1. It is particularly alarming in Iran, where recent national studies estimate the prevalence rate among adults to be approximately 32%, which is significantly higher than the global average2. It is considered a highly alarming prevalence rate, especially given that MetS increases the risk of type 2 diabetes by fivefold, cardiovascular diseases by twofold, and all-cause mortality3. Furthermore, individuals with MetS encounter a significantly greater economic burden due to elevated healthcare costs and decreased productivity4.

While its prevalence is increasing even among working-age adults, identifying modifiable risk factors for the syndrome has become increasingly important5. Diet, physical activity, and sleep are among the lifestyle-related factors considered potential targets for the prevention and management of MetS6. Of these, diet has emerged as one of the most influential modifiable factors, as dietary modifications appear to simultaneously impact all components of MetS7. There is a pressing need to identify practical and sustainable nutritional strategies that could potentially reduce the risk of MetS or halt its progression in adults8. Diet plays a crucial role in the prevention and management of MetS. High intakes of fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and legumes have been inversely associated with MetS, while refined grains, sugar-sweetened beverages, and increased consumption of processed foods have been positively associated with the components of MetS9,10. Conversely, healthy dietary patterns, such as adherence to the Mediterranean diet, have been shown to is associated with lower odds of MetS, both independently and in conjunction with unhealthy lifestyles and genetic predispositions11. Recent evidence indicates that rather than focusing solely on specific nutrients or food items, the overall dietary pattern serves as a more effective endpoint for understanding diet-disease relationships12. This approach is particularly crucial in conditions such as MetS, where multiple metabolic pathways are disrupted13. Several dietary indices have been developed and studied in relation to MetS. The Mediterranean Diet Score has consistently been found to be inversely associated with both the prevalence and incidence of MetS14. Similarly, the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) score also demonstrates an inverse association with the components of MetS15. Other indices related to MetS include the Healthy Eating Index (HEI)16, Nordic Diet Score (NDS)17, and the Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII)18. Most of these indices demonstrate significant associations with metabolic health. These dietary indices primarily emphasize the consumption of plant-based foods, whole grains, and healthy fats, while recommending minimal intake of processed foods and red meat16. Interest in sustainable diets, which consider health outcomes alongside environmental impacts, has increased in recent years19.

The World Index for Sustainability and Health (WISH) was developed based on the EAT-Lancet recommendations and uniquely combines dietary quality and environmental sustainability into a single scoring system for the first time20. This index emphasizes a higher consumption of plant-based foods and a lower consumption of animal-based foods, aligning with the goals of health promotion and environmental sustainability21,22. Although WISH was primarily developed to assess environmental sustainability, its dietary components align closely with metabolic health promotion. The index favors foods such as vegetables, fruits, whole grains, legumes, and nuts, while limiting red meat, saturated fats, and added sugars dietary patterns consistently associated with reduced MetS risk9–11. We hypothesized that adherence to WISH recommendations would confer metabolic benefits, as diets that minimize environmental impact may simultaneously improve insulin sensitivity, lipid metabolism, and systemic inflammation7,12. While some previous studies have investigated the association between WISH and various health outcomes, its relationship with MetS has not yet been explored23,24. This gap is particularly significant given that environmental sustainability, along with MetS, represents a high-priority global challenge25. Consequently, the objective of this study is to examine the association between a WISH score and the odds of MetS among Iranian adults participating in the PERSIAN Dena Cohort Study (PDCS).

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted on a large population of adults using baseline phase data from the Prospective Epidemiological Research Studies in Iran (PERSIAN) Dena Cohort Study (PDCS)26,27. The PERSIAN Cohort (http://persiancohort.com) is a comprehensive longitudinal study focused on non-communicable diseases, encompassing various geographical, ethnic, and climatic groups across 18 regional cohorts in Iran26. The Dena cohort is one of these regional cohorts and includes nearly all individuals aged 35 to 70 years residing in both urban and rural areas of Dena County, located near Yasuj city in the Kohgiluyeh and Boyer-Ahmad Province in southwestern Iran27.

Baseline phase data were collected from February 2018 to March 2020. For the Dena cohort study, the inclusion criteria comprised men and women aged 35 to 70 years who had resided in the study area for more than one year, possessed adequate physical and mental capacity to participate in the evaluation program, and provided written informed consent. The exclusion criteria included the inability to attend the clinic for a physical examination, intellectual disabilities, excessive alcohol consumption, and unwillingness to participate in the study. More detailed information regarding the study design, participants, and data collection methods has been published previously27. For the current analysis, we additionally excluded participants who reported a daily energy intake below 800 kcal or above 4500 kcal to ensure data quality, as well as pregnant or lactating women, and those with incomplete dietary or biochemical data. Ultimately, 2971 subjects met the eligibility criteria and were enrolled in the present analysis. Anthropometric and socio-demographic assessment

The data, which included demographic, anthropometric, medical, and lifestyle measurements, were collected according to the cohort protocol by trained interviewers26,27. The measurement of anthropometric indicators, including weight, height, and waist circumference (WC), was conducted in a single day for each participant by trained healthcare providers at a health center. The height of participants was measured using a stadiometer (Seca 755 1,021,994, Germany), ensuring that they stood against a wall with their heels and buttocks in contact. Weight was measured using a standing scale (Seca 755 1,021,994, Germany), which was calibrated prior to measurement. Participants were dressed in light clothing and were not wearing shoes to guarantee accurate readings. Waist circumference (WC) was measured to the nearest 0.1 cm using a flexible metric measuring tape (Seca 755 1,021,994, Germany). BMI was calculated by dividing weight in kilograms by the square of height in meters27.

Systolic and diastolic blood pressures were measured twice (i.e., after a 5-min rest in a sitting position and 15 min later, following the protocols for the right arm) using a standard mercury sphygmomanometer (Richter, MTM Munich, Germany). A minimum of 5 min elapsed between each of the two 60-s measurements of heart rate. Sleep duration was assessed using two questions from the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index27.

To document past medical history, individuals were asked whether they had ever been diagnosed with any of the following conditions: diabetes, epilepsy, multiple sclerosis (MS), head trauma, hypertension, stroke, cardiac disease, thyroid disease, gallstones, rheumatic disorders, cardiovascular diseases (CVD), fatty liver, depression, or non-depressive psychiatric disorders by a physician. Additionally, participants were inquired if they were currently receiving treatment or taking medication for any of these conditions27.

Subjects were classified as smokers if they had smoked 100 or more cigarettes in their lifetime. Tobacco use was defined as the consumption of tobacco in the form of Naas, hookah, pipe, or snuff at least once a week for a minimum of six months. Drug use was defined as the consumption of illegal drugs—including heroin, amphetamines, barbiturates, cannabis, cocaine, hallucinogens, and opioids—at least once a week for a minimum of six months. Alcohol consumption was assessed by inquiring about the amount, frequency, and duration of consumption of any alcoholic beverages (including wine, beer, and spirits) at every age27.

By using an Iranian version of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), the physical activity levels of the participants were assessed28. The translation and validation of this questionnaire were previously conducted on the adult population of Iran, where all participants were asked to report their activities based on the questionnaire. Subsequently, the results of the IPAQ were expressed in terms of Metabolic Equivalent-hours per week (MET-h/week), taking into account the type, duration, and frequency of activities performed each week. Participants were then classified into quartiles based on their MET-h/week values: active, moderately active, moderately inactive, and inactive. We defined the low-risk group as individuals categorized as active and moderately active.

Dietary assessment

The dietary intakes of participants were assessed using a validated 113-item Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ) in conjunction with a 127-item FFQ specifically designed for indigenous foods29,30. Participants were asked to report the frequency and portion sizes of food items consumed on a daily, weekly, monthly, and annual basis over the past year. All portion sizes and household measurements were converted to grams per day, and the energy and nutrient content of the foods were calculated using Nutritionist IV software (version 7.0).

World Index for Sustainability and Health (WISH) calculation

The calculation of the WISH score was performed using the method developed by Trijsburg et al.20, which assesses both the healthiness of the diet and its environmental impact. This index has been validated and applied in low- and middle-income countries31. The classification of food encompasses thirteen categories: vegetables, fruits, whole grains, fish, red meat, dairy products, chicken, eggs, nuts, legumes, saturated oils, unsaturated oils, and added sugars. Food consumption was evaluated using the previously validated FFQ. Each food group was assigned a score on a scale from 0 to 10, with higher scores indicating greater adherence to dietary recommendations. For protective food groups, scores between 0 and 10 were assigned proportionally using the formula: Score = 10 × [(reported intake—lower recommended intake) / (recommended intake—lower recommended intake)]. For vegetables, fruits, whole grains, and legumes, intake at or above recommendations scored 10. For fish, dairy, and nuts, intake within the optimal range (between recommended and upper limits) scored 10, while excess intake scored 0 due to environmental impact. For neutral food groups (eggs and chicken), intake up to the recommended amount scored 10, with scores declining proportionally to 0 at upper limits using: Score = 10 × [(upper recommended intake—recommended intake)—(reported intake—recommended intake)] / (upper recommended intake—recommended intake). For limiting food groups (red meat, saturated oils, added sugars), saturated oils (≤ 11.8 g/day) and added sugars (≤ 31 g/day) were scored as bi-variate components. Red meat intake ≤ 14 g/day scored 10, intake between 14 and 28 g/day was scored proportionally, and intake > 28 g/day scored 0. Specific thresholds based on EAT-Lancet recommendations were: vegetables ≥ 300 g/day (200–600), fruits ≥ 200 g/day (100–300), whole grains ≥ 125 g/day (100–150), legumes ≥ 75 g/day (0–100), fish 28 g/day (0–100), dairy 250 g/day (0–500), nuts 50 g/day (0–75), unsaturated oils 40 g/day (20–80), chicken 29 g/day (0–58), and eggs 13 g/day (0–25). The total WISH score is calculated as the sum of all component scores, with a theoretical range from 0 to 130, where higher scores signify healthier and more environmentally sustainable diets20.

Biochemical measurements

A 25 mL blood sample was collected in tubes containing either a clot activator or an anticoagulant, specifically one 7 mL clot tube and three 6 mL EDTA tubes, after an overnight fast. This sample was analyzed to measure various biochemical variables, including fasting blood sugar (FBS), serum cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein (LDL), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and triglycerides. Following standard laboratory protocols and utilizing Pars Azmoon kits (Pars Azmoon Co, Iran), all measurements were conducted using a biochemistry autoanalyzer (BT1500, 47,173,738, Italy) and a hematology cell counter (Sysmex, XP (B0463), Japan)27.

Definition of the metabolic syndrome

In this study, we defined MetS according to the criteria established by the Adult Treatment Panel III (ATP III). Therefore, the diagnosis of MetS requires the presence of three or more of the following components: abdominal adiposity (waist circumference > 102 cm for men and > 88 cm for women); elevated blood pressure (≥ 130/85 mm Hg or the use of antihypertensive medication); abnormal glucose homeostasis (fasting blood glucose ≥ 100 mg/dl or the use of antidiabetic medication); low serum HDL-cholesterol levels (< 40 mg/dl for men and < 50 mg/dl for women); and high serum triglyceride levels (≥ 150 mg/dl or the use of fibrate medication)32.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS version 25 (IBM Corp, Armonk, New York) and p-values of less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Participants were classified based on cut points of the WISH in quartiles categories (first, < 133; second, 133 to 144; third, 145 to 155; fourth, > 155). Percentages were used to describe qualitative variables, while mean ± standard deviation (SD) was used to describe quantitative variables. To assess differences among categories of WISH scores, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied for continuous variables. In addition, the Chi-square test was employed to compare the distribution of individuals regarding categorical variables across different categories of WISH scores. We compared sex-, age-, and energy-adjusted means for food intakes across WISH categories by analysis of covariance (ANCOVA).

To obtain the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) for MetS and its components across quartiles of WISH, binary logistic regression with adjusted models was employed. In the first model, we adjusted for sex, age, physical activity, cigarette smoking, energy intake, education level, and marital status. In Model 2, we additionally adjusted for BMI along with the variables included in Model 1.

Association of WISH with metabolic syndrome and its components

Discussion

The current study investigated the relationship between WISH scores and MetS among Iranian adults participating in the PDCS. Our findings indicate that participants with higher WISH scores had significantly lower odds of having MetS and its components. These results remained significant even after adjusting for various potential confounders, including sociodemographic, anthropometric, and lifestyle factors. The inverse associations were particularly notable for abdominal adiposity and abnormal glucose homeostasis. To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the relationship between a dietary index that simultaneously considers both health and environmental sustainability aspects and MetS. Participants in higher WISH quartiles demonstrated more favorable characteristics, including higher physical activity levels, better education, and lower BMI and waist circumference compared to those in lower quartiles. We also observed sex distribution differences across WISH quartiles (p = 0.016), with a higher proportion of females in lower quartiles. However, all statistical analyses were adjusted for sex, ensuring that the observed associations between WISH and MetS are independent of this distribution.

Our study population exhibited varying degrees of adherence to the EAT-Lancet dietary recommendations. When analyzing dietary patterns across WISH score quartiles, we found that participants with higher scores consumed significantly greater amounts of plant-based foods, particularly fruits, vegetables, and legumes. However, the consumption of whole grains remained notably below the recommended levels across all quartiles. The patterns of energy intake and food group consumption in our population reflect the ongoing nutritional transition in middle-income countries, which is characterized by an increased intake of refined grains and energy-dense foods, alongside traditional healthy dietary components. These findings indicate that, although certain aspects of the traditional Iranian diet align closely with sustainable dietary recommendations, there is significant potential for improvement in other areas, particularly in terms of whole grain consumption and the reduction of less healthy food components.

Our findings align with previous evidence regarding dietary patterns and MetS. Research has demonstrated that several dietary indices can offer protection against MetS. A systematic review and meta-analysis conducted by Bakaloudi et al.11 reported that higher adherence to the Mediterranean diet was significantly associated with lower odds of developing MetS. Furthermore, a recently published systematic review and meta-analysis by Valenzuela-Fuenzalida et al.15 indicated that the DASH dietary pattern effectively improved components of MetS. Our study anticipates a similar protective effect with WISH scores, as this index shares many components with established dietary patterns, particularly emphasizing plant-based foods while limiting processed foods19.

Several potential mechanisms may explain the inverse association between WISH scores and MetS. Diets rich in fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and legumes offer a substantial amount of dietary fiber, antioxidants, and bioactive compounds that improve metabolic parameters7. Mozaffarian’s comprehensive review highlighted that these dietary components can simultaneously influence multiple pathways associated with MetS, including inflammation, oxidative stress, and insulin sensitivity7. Furthermore, the emphasis on plant-based proteins and the limited intake of red meat in the WISH score aligns with evidence indicating that plant-forward dietary patterns can improve lipid profiles and reduce central adiposity12,13. Beyond these biological mechanisms, socioeconomic and behavioral characteristics may also contribute to the observed associations. Participants in higher WISH quartiles demonstrated higher education levels and greater physical activity, both established determinants of dietary quality and metabolic health9,10. Higher education is associated with improved nutrition knowledge and better access to diverse food choices, while physical activity often clusters with other health-promoting behaviors. These findings suggest that the inverse associations observed for higher WISH scores likely reflect both the direct metabolic benefits of dietary composition and the broader lifestyle context in which sustainable dietary patterns occur33.

However, when we compare our results with those from population-based studies, we find some discrepancies. Our study shows an inverse relationship between WISH scores and obesity, whereas the study conducted by Dhillon et al. revealed that the metabolic responses of different ethnic populations to the same dietary intervention were variable34. The significant reduction in serum lipid levels among individuals with high WISH scores strongly suggests potential genetic predispositions that influence lipid metabolism and warrants further investigation. Our results extend beyond existing dietary indices by incorporating features of environmental sustainability. This is particularly relevant given the growing awareness of the interconnectedness between human and planetary health, as eloquently highlighted by Willett et al.19. The environmental sustainability aspect of the WISH score may offer additional metabolic benefits by reducing exposure to pro-inflammatory dietary factors35,36. These findings can be interpreted through the Health Belief Model and Social Cognitive Theory, which emphasize an individual’s perception of risk and observational learning, respectively, in facilitating behavior change37,38.

Our study has several strengths that warrant mention. First, it investigated the association between WISH scores and MetS in a large population-based sample, utilizing standardized protocols for data collection to minimize biases. Second, we assessed dietary intake using validated questionnaires and measured anthropometric and biochemical markers according to standardized protocols. Third, we controlled for a comprehensive range of potential confounding variables, including socio-demographic, anthropometric, and lifestyle factors. Finally, this is the first study to examine the relationship between an index that integrates both health and environmental sustainability aspects and MetS.

Our findings must be interpreted within the context of several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and does not allow us to establish temporal relationships between WISH scores and MetS. Reverse causation and bidirectional associations cannot be ruled out. Second, although we utilized a validated FFQ for dietary assessment, self-reported dietary information is inherently subject to recall bias and measurement errors. The absence of biomarker validation may lead to misclassification of dietary intake and affect WISH score accuracy. Third, residual confounding from unmeasured or imperfectly measured factors could not be entirely eliminated, even after adjusting for most confounders. Furthermore, this study was conducted in a specific region of Iran; therefore, our findings may not be generalizable to other populations with different dietary patterns and cultural contexts. Validation across diverse populations is needed. Another limitation of our study is the potential for residual confounding due to total energy intake. The WISH diet is inherently energy-dense, and the nearly 800 kcal difference between the extreme quartiles raises the possibility that the observed associations are confounded. Although we adjusted for total energy in our models, this adjustment may be insufficient. Future studies would benefit from employing more robust methods, such as isocaloric substitution models, to isolate the effects of dietary composition from total energy intake.

Conclusion

In brief, the current study indicates that higher adherence to environmentally sustainable and healthy dietary patterns, as measured by the WISH index, is associated with lower odds of MetS among Iranian adults. These results support the dual benefits of promoting sustainable diets for both human health and environmental sustainability. Future research employing a prospective design is necessary to confirm these associations and to elucidate potential mechanistic links between sustainable dietary patterns and metabolic health outcomes.
