Global trends in adolescent and young adult female cancer burden, 1990-2021: insights from the Global Burden of Disease study
Authors: Z. Huang, J. Wang, H. Liu, B. Wang, M. Qi, Z. Lyu, H. Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe impact of breast carcinoma and genital tract malignancy on the physical and mental health, especially reproductive function, of women aged 15-39 years in the adolescent and young adult (AYA) group is significant. This research aims to analyze the burden of AYA female cancer in various regions and countries globally from 1990 to 2021.Materials and methodsEpidemiological data were sourced from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study 2021. The study analyzed data on female cancers (breast, ovarian, uterine, and cervical) to assess disease burden across different ages, years, and locations, encompassing 21 GBD regions, 195 countries, and five sociodemographic index (SDI) regions.ResultsIn 2021, AYA female cancer saw 383 241 new cases and 81 679 deaths globally, with 2 975 183 prevalent cases and 4 855 780 disability-adjusted life years. In 2021, Central Latin America recorded the highest age-standardized incidence (ASIR) and prevalence rates (ASPR), whereas South sub-Saharan Africa had the highest age-standardized mortality (ASMR) and disability rates (ASDR). Nations with high SDI typically exhibited elevated ASIR and ASPR, whereas ASMR and ASDR demonstrated inverse patterns. Over the past three decades, ASIR and ASPR of female cancers among AYA women have increased globally. In contrast, the ASMR and ASDR have demonstrated a declining trend. Notably, breast cancer emerged as the most prevalent malignancy among AYA women.ConclusionsOver the past three decades, the ASIR and ASPR of AYA female cancers have consistently increased, significantly burdening this demographic. Additionally, pronounced disparities are evident across different regions and countries, with AYA women in low SDI environments experiencing poorer prognoses compared to their counterparts in high SDI environments. Consequently, it is imperative to enhance interregional collaboration and communication to optimize the overall prognosis of AYA female cancers.

Introduction

Adolescent and young adult (AYA) cancer is generally defined as cancer diagnosed between ages 15 and 39 years.1 Among all cancer, approximately 500 000 cases belong to this specific group.2 Compared to older cancer patients, AYAs are more likely to possess genetic predispositions to cancer,1 and the physical and psychosocial sequelae following treatment tend to be more severe,2 particularly among women. In comparison to men, the side-effects of antitumor therapies disproportionately affect women, including issues such as hair loss, skin changes, fatigue, itching, diarrhea, and depression,3 directly impacting their treatment adherence.4 Female cancers (including breast, ovarian, uterine, and cervical cancers) are particularly destructive and, combined with the reproductive toxicity of cancer treatments, pose significant fertility challenges or directly cause infertility,5,6 especially in AYA women.7 As the 5-year survival rate of cancer sufferers steadily rises,8 there is a growing recognition of the psychological and physiological needs specific to AYA cancer among women.

The cancer statistics from 2022 indicated that female cancers ranked within the top 10 in terms of incidence rates of cancers among women, collectively accounting for 38.3% of all cancers among women. Among women, breast cancer was the cancer most prevalently diagnosed, with nearly 2.3 million newly diagnosed cases in 2022, ranking second only to lung cancer, and it is also a significant cause of cancer-related deaths (accounting for 15.4% of the total cancer deaths). Cervical cancer is the fourth most common cancer in incidence and mortality among women, the most prevalent in 25 countries, and a significant cause of cancer deaths in 37 nations.8 In recent years, there has been a significant increase in cancer risk among young people.9 Studies indicated a trend towards younger ages for breast and endometrial cancers, with incidence rates among young women rising year by year.10

The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 study offered comprehensive data on cancer incidence, prevalence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) from 1990 to 2021 across 204 countries and territories. Our research analyzed the incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs of breast, cervical, ovarian, and uterine cancers in AYA women from 1990 to 2021, examining the trends. Additionally, we explored the relationship between incidence or mortality rates and the sociodemographic index (SDI) to offer valuable references for the prevention and management strategies of AYA female cancers.

Materials and methods

Data sources and study population

The GBD study 2021, a publicly accessible epidemiological database, provides a detailed analysis of disease burden across 23 age categories in 204 countries and territories from 1990 to 2021. The study analyzed data on four female cancers to assess disease burden across various dimensions, including age, year, cancer type, and location (encompassing 21 GBD regions, 195 countries, and five SDI). The SDI is a comprehensive metric of national or regional development, based on an integrative assessment of total fertility rate, average educational attainment, and per capita income. It categorizes regions into five SDI levels: high, high-middle, middle, middle-low, and low.

We obtained data on the annual incidence, prevalence, mortality, DALYs, and age-standardized rates (ASRs) for breast, ovarian, uterine, and cervical carcinomas from the Global Health Data Exchange (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results/). The 95% uncertainty interval (UI) for each indicator was determined using the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the posterior distribution from 1000 draw values.

The study population includes females aged 15-39 years diagnosed with breast, ovarian, uterine, or cervical cancer. Breast, ovarian, uterine, and cervical cancers are classified as female cancers. According to prior research, the age group of 15-39 years is classified as AYA. We selected the AYA population for this study due to the distinct and severe physiological and psychological challenges faced by cancer patients in this demographic, particularly women. These challenges encompass treatment side-effects, factors influencing treatment adherence, and fertility concerns, thereby necessitating research specifically tailored to address their unique needs.

Statistical analysis

The temporal distribution characteristics of female cancer burden among AYAs were analyzed using the Joinpoint regression model which consisted of a series of statistical linear model. The ASR for the specified population was determined by employing the direct standardization method, utilizing the world standard population as delineated in the 2021 GBD study report.ASR=∑i=1Aαiωi∑i=1Aωi

The variable αi denotes the age-specific rate, while wi signifies the weight derived from the standard population corresponding to each of the respective age groups. Additionally, A represents the total number of age groups.

The 30-year trends in incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs from 1990 to 2021 were analyzed using the average annual percent change (AAPC) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) based on ASRs. AAPC is typically calculated using a log-linear regression model. Firstly, time series data (such as incidence or mortality rates) are log-transformed, and time is used as the independent variable in the regression to obtain the regression coefficient (β1). Then, the following formula is applied to convert the regression coefficient into the AAPC value:AAPC=(eβ1−1)×100%

This process quantifies the average annual rate of change for the entire study period, providing an overall trend assessment. An upward tendency was identified if both the estimated AAPC and its lower limit of the 95% CI were above zero. On the contrary, a downward tendency was observed if both the estimated AAPC and its upper limit of the 95% CI were below zero. All analyses and data visualizations were carried out using R software (version 4.2.2), with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.

Results

Female cancers burden in AYA women worldwide

In 2021, a total of 383 241 (95% UI, 344 853-423 125) AYA women worldwide were first diagnosed with female cancer, while 81 679 (95% UI, 72 928-91 126) death cases and 4 855 780 (95% UI, 4 318 491-5 419 576) DALYs were recorded. At the same time, 2 975 183 (95% UI, 2 691 760-3 278 345) prevalent cases with female cancers were documented in 2021 (Table 1). The highest incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs for female cancers in AYA women were observed in the 35-39 years age group, while the lowest were in the 15-19 years age group (Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103958). From 1990 to 2021, the age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) and age-standard prevalence rate (ASPR) for AYA women gradually increased, with an AAPC of 0.34% (95% CI, 0.22% to 0.46%) and 0.5% (95% CI, 0.39% to 0.62%), respectively. The periods 2014-2017 and 2015-2018 saw the most significant increases in ASIR [annual percent change (APC) = 1.41%, P < 0.05] and ASPR (APC = 1.55%, P < 0.05), respectively. Conversely, a notable downward trend has been observed in the age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) and the age-standardized DALY rate (ASDR). The period from 2003 to 2007 saw the most notable decline, with ASMR displaying an APC of −1.45% (P < 0.05) and an AAPC of −0.45%, while ASDR showed an APC of −1.37% (P < 0.05) and an AAPC of −0.39% The burden of female cancer among AYA women varies by country and region

Analysis of 21 GBD regions identified five regions with significantly higher ASIR: Southern sub-Saharan Africa, Southern Latin America, high-income North America, the Caribbean, and Central Latin America. Notably, Central Latin America demonstrated the highest ASIR, exceeding 40 cases per 100 000 population (Supplementary Figure S2A, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103958). Regarding ASPR, Central Latin America holds the highest ranking, while high-income North America occupies the second position, with no substantial disparity between them (Supplementary Figure S2B, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103958). In addition, Southern sub-Saharan Africa had the greatest ASMR and ASDR, with a substantial gap compared to other areas (Supplementary Figure S2C and D, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103958). Supplementary Figure S2E-H and Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103958, show that while the ASIR and ASPR have generally increased in most regions over the past three decades, the majority of regions have experienced a continuous decline in ASMR and ASDR, except for Tropical Latin America, Southern sub-Saharan Africa, and North Africa and the Middle East.

In 2021, by country, Bolivarian Republic of Venezuela exhibited the highest ASIR and ASPR among AYA female cancers as depicted in Figure 2A and B, respectively. Concurrently, Kiribati demonstrated the highest ASMR and ASDR, whereas Oman recorded the lowest values for these metrics (Figure 2C and D). In the past three decades, most countries have seen a rise in the incidence and prevalence of female cancers among AYA women. Notably, Turkey exhibited the highest increases, with ASIR rising by 3.10% (95% UI, 2.46% to 3.75%) and ASPR by 3.60% (95% UI, 2.95% to 4.25%). Conversely, it is encouraging to observe that ASMR and ASDR have been consistently declining in most countries. Saint Kitts and Nevis demonstrated the most significant declines, with ASMR decreasing by −4.44% (95% UI, −4.96% to −3.91%) and ASDR by −4.39% (95% UI, −4.91% to −3.87%). The correlation between AYA female cancer burden and SDI

The SDI was a comprehensive metric used to evaluate regional development levels and was intricately linked to the health of the local population. There is a positive correlation between ASIR (R = 0.55, P < 0.001) and ASPR (R = 0.71, P < 0.001) with the SDI, suggesting that both incidence and prevalence rates increase concomitantly with higher SDI values (Figure 3A and B). Significant regional variations were observed in Western Europe, Central Europe, High-income North America, and Australasia. ASMR and ASDR showed a decreasing trend with higher SDI (ASMR R = −0.37, P < 0.001; ASDR R = −0.35, P < 0.001) (Figure 3C and D). From 1990 to 2021, regions classified by the SDI, excluding those with a high SDI, showed an increasing trend in ASIR and ASPR. All SDI regions exhibited a decline in both ASMR and ASDR. Burden differences among female cancers in AYA women

In 2021, breast cancer exhibited the highest incidence among the four categories of female cancers, collectively surpassing 170 000 new cases. By then, 1.5 million AYA women had experienced the ordeal of breast cancer. Meanwhile, in that year, breast cancer was still the most common origin of death across AYA women with female cancers, with 41 547 (95% UI, 37 729-45 853) deaths attributed to it. Additionally, it also resulted in the highest DALYs [2 454 170 (95% UI, 2 215 872-2 711 728), Supplementary Table S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103958]. We conducted an analysis of the numerical rankings for incidence, prevalence, deaths, and DALYs pertaining to the four types of female cancers across diverse regions and countries worldwide. The findings consistently highlighted breast cancer as the top-ranked female cancer in most regions and countries (Figure 4A, Supplementary Figures S4-S8, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103958). Taken as a whole, except for cervical cancer (AAPC < 0), the ASIR and ASPR for female cancers have displayed a gradual upward tendency throughout the past 30 years (AAPC > 0). Meanwhile, there’ is a reassuring trend of gradual decrease in the ASMR and ASDR across cervical cancer and uterine cancer (AAPC < 0), but a marginal rise was observed in the ASDR and ASMR for breast cancer and ovarian cancer (AAPC > 0, Figure 4B). Significant disparities in incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs of female cancers exist among AYA women across different age groups. Among AYA females aged 15-19 years, ovarian cancer has the highest incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs. Cervical cancer exhibits the highest metrics in the 20-24 year age group. In the 25-29 year age group, cervical cancer has the highest incidence, whereas breast cancer ranks first in prevalence, mortality, and DALYs. We present a detailed overview of the global burden of various female cancers among AYA women. The rankings align with the order depicted in Figure 4A.

Breast cancer

In 2021, there were a total of 178 713 (95% UI, 164 259-195 324) new cases of breast malignancy in AYA women worldwide, and by the end of 2021, a total of 1 503 237 (95% UI, 1 385 162-1 641 821) AYA women were living with breast malignancy globally. Simultaneously, 41 547 (95% UI, 37 729-45 853) AYA women died of breast cancer, which exceeded the combined total of deaths from the other three cancers, and the DALYs affected by breast cancer amounted to 2 454 170 (95% UI, 2 215 872-2 711 728), in 2021 (Supplementary Table S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103958).

According to the 2021 GBD data, the 15-24 year age group in low-middle SDI regions exhibits the highest incidence and prevalence. For the 25-39 year age group, the middle SDI regions have the highest proportion. The 15-29 year age group exhibits the highest proportion of mortality and DALYs in low-middle SDI, whereas the 30-39 year age group shows the highest proportion in middle SDI (Supplementary Figure S10A-D, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103958). The highest ASIR, ASPR, ASMR, and ASDR for the 15-24 year age group were observed in the low-middle SDI. In the 25-39 year age group, high SDI showed the highest ASIR and ASPR, while low-middle SDI had the highest ASMR and ASDR (Supplementary Figure S10E, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103958).

In the 21 GBD regions, High-income North America exhibited the highest ASIR and ASPR, while Oceania recorded the highest ASMR and ASDR. Central sub-Saharan Africa had the lowest ASIR and ASPR, while high-income Asia Pacific had the lowest ASMR and ASDR. Over the past three decades, North Africa and the Middle East experienced the most significant increases in ASIR and ASPR, whereas Central Asia saw the most notable declines. As for ASMR and ASDR, the most substantial increases were seen in South Asia, while the most pronounced decreases were consistently in Western Europe (Supplementary Table S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103958). Among the 204 GBD countries, Monaco topped both ASIR and ASPR in 2021, whereas Niger ranked at the lowest ends, respectively. For ASMR and ASDR, Tokelau also ranked highest in 2021, while Oman ranked lowest. Over the past 30 years, Turkey has seen the most significant increases in ASIR, ASPR, ASMR, and ASDR, whereas Saint Kitts and Nevis have experienced the most notable decreases (Supplementary Table S5, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103958).

Cervical cancer

In 2021, there were 148 176 (95% UI, 133 358-163 665) new cases among AYA women globally, while the cumulative number of AYA women with cervical cancer reached 1 071 831 (95% UI, 971 245-1 181 413). Simultaneously, there were also 29 918 (95% UI, 26 618-33 612) deaths and 1 775 990 (95% UI, 1 578 354-1 993 985) DALYs recorded due to cervical cancer (Supplementary Table S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103958). In 2021, Southern Latin America exhibited the highest ASIR, ASPR, ASMR, and ASDR. North Africa and the Middle East exhibited the lowest ASIR, ASPR, ASMR, and ASDR rates. Moreover, ASPR, ASIR, ASMR, and ASDR were all greater in the low SDI group (Supplementary Table S6, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103958). In 2021, among the 204 GBD countries, Bolivarian Republic of Venezuela had the highest ASIR and ASPR, while Palestine had the lowest. Kiribati had the highest ASMR and ASDR, while Kuwait had the lowest (Supplementary Table S7, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103958). Globally, unlike the trends in the other three cancer types, the ASIR and ASPR of cervical cancer among AYA women have declined over the past three decades. From 1990 to 2021, only the ASIR of high-middle SDI exhibited an overall increase, whereas other SDI categories showed a decline. Among them, the AAPC for high SDI showed the greatest variation, reaching 1.69%. Regarding the AAPC of ASPR, high-middle SDI, middle SDI, and low-middle SDI showed a slight increase, while high SDI showed a significant decrease, reaching 1.55%.

Ovarian cancer

In 2021, there were 40 760 (95% UI, 34 470-46 150) new cases and 8290 (95% UI, 7039-9,397) deaths. The overall population affected by the illness was 270 586 (95% UI, 229 076-306 035), resulting in 508 632 (95% UI, 430 772-576 660) DALYs due to ovarian cancer (Supplementary Table S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103958). Regarding SDI, ASIR and ASPR were highest in middle SDI, in 2021. The highest ASMR and ASDR recordings were observed in the low-middle SDI. ASIR and ASPR were lowest in low SDI, while ASMR and ASDR were lowest in high SDI. Among the 21 GBD regions, Southeast Asia topped the ASIR and ASPR, whereas Western sub-Saharan Africa ranked at the bottom. Central Latin America had the highest ASMR and ASDR, while Western sub-Saharan Africa had the lowest ASMR and Oceania had the lowest ASDR (Supplementary Table S8, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103958). In 2021, Seychelles had the highest ASIR and ASPR among the 204 GBD countries, whereas Mali had the lowest. Seychelles had the highest ASMR and ASDR, whereas Palau had the lowest (Supplementary Table S9, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103958).

Over the past 30 years, the ASIR, ASPR, ASMR, and ASDR of AYA women with ovarian cancer have exhibited an overall increasing trend, distinct from the trends observed in the other three types of female cancers. Ecuador showed the highest average annual increases in ASIR, ASPR, ASMR, and ASDR. The increase in ASIR, ASPR, ASMR, and ASDR was significantly higher for lower SDI than for higher SDI.

Uterine cancer

Among the four types of AYA female cancers, uterine cancer exhibited the lowest incidence, both in terms of case count and ASR. In 2021, there were a total of 15 591 (95% UI, 12 765-17 986) new cases in AYA women recorded globally, with a total of 129 530 (95% UI, 106 276-149 076) cases reported. The number of deaths was 1924 (95% UI, 1542-2264) due to uterine cancer, resulting in 116 989 (95% UI, 93 494-137 204) DALYs attributed to uterine cancer (Supplementary Table S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103958). Eastern Europe had the highest ASIR and ASPR in 2021, whereas Western sub-Saharan Africa exhibited the lowest rate. The highest ASMR and ASDR were in the Caribbean, whereas the minimum was also in Western sub-Saharan Africa. Notably, as the SDI increased, both the ASIR and ASPR exhibited a corresponding rise. However, ASMR and ASDR did not show the same or opposite trend, with the highest rates recorded in the high-middle SDI (Supplementary Table S10, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103958).

Globally, the AAPC trends for ASIR, ASPR, ASMR, and ASDR mirrored those of female cancers. In the past 30 years, Kuwait has experienced the highest increase in ASIR, ASPR, ASMR, and ASDR, whereas Armenia has shown the greatest decrease (Supplementary Table S11, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103958).

Discussion

Female cancers (breast, cervical, ovarian, and uterine cancers) account for almost one-third of new annual cancer diagnoses in women. These malignancies represent a significant threat to women’s health and well-being, with a particularly profound impact on the reproductive health of AYA women.11 This research, for the first time, comprehensively analyzed the burden and trends of female cancer among AYA women aged 15-39 years. Our findings reveal that globally over 2.9 million AYA women were affected by female cancers in 2021, resulting in 81 679 deaths and nearly 4.9 million DALYs. Trends indicate rising ASIR and ASPR, and falling ASMR and ASDR, due to improved diagnostics and advanced treatments.12,13 In 2021, ASIR and ASPR were higher in high and high-middle SDI countries due to better diagnostics, reporting systems, and higher cancer awareness.14 ASDR and ASMR were highest in regions with low and low-middle SDI. In high-income countries, early detection and systematic treatment are common due to education and screening programs, and supportive care extends survival.15 Discrepancies in AYA female cancer incidence and mortality exist between and within SDI regions, highlighting the need to consider race, genetics, lifestyle, and environment.16 It advocates for regional collaboration and a comprehensive approach to reducing cancer burden in AYA women.

Breast cancer constitutes almost 50% of all AYA female cancer cases, ranking highest in incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs worldwide. In 2021, ASIR and ASPR were utmost in high SDI regions. This high detection rate is likely due to effective public education on breast cancer symptoms rather than screening, as women <40 years of age typically do not undergo routine screenings.17 ASMR and ASDR are highest in low and low-middle SDI nations. We believe this is because AYA women are more prone to present with symptomatic breast cancer at advanced stages due to relatively limited screening and delayed diagnosis. However, population-based data suggest that young women are more prone to progress in more invasive subtypes of breast cancer.18 Treatment options for young breast cancer patients are still in clinical trials. Low and low-middle SDI countries lack plans for such trials, impeding patient prognosis improvements. Furthermore, genetic factors are unavoidable in AYA patients. However, genetic testing is less prevalent in low-income countries.19 Our study results suggest that further education and promotion of breast cancer symptoms and self-examination methods among AYA women in low and middle-income nations can promote early diagnosis and therapy of breast cancer. Enhancing communication between SDI regions and increasing financial support can help alleviate the breast cancer burden in low and low-middle SDI regions.20,21

Cervical cancer is the most prevalent gynecological cancer during pregnancy and significantly affects fertility.22,23 Globally, the incidence rates (ASPR, ASIR, ASMR, and ASDR) of cervical cancer in AYA women are declining due to widespread human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination and cytology screening. However, many low and middle-income countries have not included HPV vaccines in their national immunization programs and lack highly sensitive and accurate cervical screening.24, 25, 26 Our research found that in 2021, low SDI countries contained the highest ASIR of cervical cancer in AYA women, reaching 13 cases, nearly twice as high as that in high SDI countries. Factors such as age of first sexual intimacy and number of sexual partners influence HPV infection rates, which are higher in AYA women compared to older females.27 Increasing participation in cervical screening and expanding HPV vaccination programs, especially in low and middle-income countries, is essential to reduce the cervical cancer burden among AYA women.28

Our study indicates a 30-year increase in ovarian cancer rates (ASIR, ASPR, ASMR, and ASDR) among AYA females, a trend not observed in other cancers. Ovarian germ-cell tumors are common in women aged 15-20 years, representing half of invasive tumors and having a good prognosis with >90% survival. However, they only make up 2%-3% of ovarian tumors. Epithelial ovarian cancer, which is more common in the 30-39 years age group and accounts for 90% of cases, has a poorer prognosis and often affects fertility.29 Additionally, early detection rates are low at 20%, leading to many late-stage diagnoses and lower survival rates.30 Interestingly, our data show the lowest ASIR and ASPR in ovarian cancer statistics in low SDI regions, which could be possibly attributed to differences in the social environments faced by AYA women in different SDI regions. Research suggests that pregnancy has a protective effect against ovarian cancer, as ceasing ovulation during pregnancy and lactation can reduce damage to the ovarian epithelium. However, in developed countries, fertility rates among AYA women are often lower.31 Additionally, AYA women in low SDI regions typically consume fewer processed foods and engage in higher levels of physical activity, which may contribute to a lower incidence of ovarian cancer. Yet, under-diagnosis due to lower medical standards remains a significant issue in these regions. Further research is needed to develop region-specific ovarian cancer prevention and treatment strategies, especially in low-income areas.

In 2021, uterine cancer exhibited the lowest incidence, prevalence, mortality, DALYs, ASMR, and ASDR among AYA female cancers. However, in high SDI regions, uterine cancer had the highest rates (ASIR, ASPR, ASMR, and ASDR) compared to other SDI regions. Over the past 30 years, the ASIR for uterine cancer has risen, especially in high-income countries.32,33 Risk factors such as high estrogen levels from obesity, diabetes, and high-fat diets contribute to its prevalence. The incidence of uterine endometrial cancer in individuals aged <40 years has doubled in the past three decades.34 Addressing obesity and promoting healthier diets in high-income areas are critical to reducing the disease burden of uterine cancer in AYA women.35

Conclusions

Based on the GBD 2021 study, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of the burden of female cancers in AYA. Over the past three decades, the incidence of female cancers in AYA women has consistently risen globally, imposing significant economic, psychological, and physical burdens on this population. High SDI countries have higher cancer incidence rates than low SDI nations; however, they benefit from systematic treatment, better health awareness, and advanced medical conditions, leading to improved prognoses. Additionally, advancements in precision medicine may further widen regional disparities. This study aims to guide prevention and treatment strategies for female cancers in AYA women across regions, foster regional cooperation, enhance cancer support in low-income countries, and reduce the disease burden on AYA women.
