An image dataset of Chinese hickory in natural orchards
Authors: Na Jia, Chengjin Fu, Kai Chen, Jiuqing Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We present CaryaData, an image dataset of Chinese hickory (Carya cathayensis Sarg.) fruit maturity acquired in natural orchards in Zhejiang Province, China. The dataset comprises 1,661 canopy images (3024 × 3024 pixels) spanning key developmental stages from fruit enlargement to harvest. Within these images, 3,211 visually discernible fruit instances are annotated with axis-aligned bounding boxes and assigned to three maturity levels (maturity1–maturity3) or an unknown class for visually uncertain cases (labelled as unknown in the released annotations), based on pericarp colour, surface blemishes and cracking status. Data construction followed a rigorous quality-control workflow, including automated image quality filtering, standardised maturity interpretation, and a two-round annotation process with double-blind cross-validation of all images. To facilitate modelling and quantitative analysis, CaryaData also provides a derived subset uniformly resized to 640 × 640 pixels, together with image-level and instance-level metadata describing illumination, maturity composition and geometric properties of targets. Physical detachment force experiments confirmed the biological consistency of the maturity grading, and benchmark experiments showed that CaryaData supports deep learning-based fruit maturity assessment, offering a reusable resource for research on maturity evaluation, yield estimation and intelligent harvesting in Chinese hickory orchards.

Background & Summary

Chinese hickory is a unique and economically important forest tree species native to China, widely cultivated in Zhejiang, Anhui, and surrounding regions, where it provides substantial ecological and economic benefits1,2. Taking Lin’an District of Zhejiang Province as an example, Chinese hickory orchards cover more than 37,850 hectares, with an annual industrial-chain output value of around 5 billion yuan, playing a pivotal role in the regional forestry economy. Fruit maturity directly affects kernel quality, oil flavour, and storage stability and is therefore a core indicator for determining harvest timing and product grading3. Under-mature nuts suffer from reduced oil content and suboptimal flavour, whereas over-mature nuts or those with cracked husks are prone to mould, insect infestation, and storage losses4,5. Consequently, obtaining objective and reliable maturity information under natural orchard conditions is a crucial foundation for precision management and intelligent harvesting in economic forests.

In practical production, maturity assessment mainly relies either on uniform harvest dates set by local management authorities or on farmers’ empirical judgments based on shell colour and cracking characteristics6,7. However, natural orchards differ substantially in topography, tree age, canopy structure, and microclimate, leading to pronounced spatial heterogeneity in fruit maturity8. Uniform harvesting strategies cannot fully accommodate maturity differences across plots and individual trees; moreover, manual visual assessments are easily affected by illumination conditions, occlusions and cluttered backgrounds, and thus exhibit strong subjectivity and variability9–11. Together, these factors hinder the standardisation of maturity data acquisition and impede decision-making and control in intelligent harvesting equipment.

With the widespread application of visual perception and deep learning technologies in agriculture, image-based fruit detection and maturity assessment have shown considerable potential in automated harvesting, orchard monitoring, and phenotypic analysis. However, compared with crops such as tomatoes, citrus, and apples12–14, research on Chinese hickory currently lacks publicly available image datasets annotated for maturity. As a result, model training lacks standardised data support, and fair comparisons between different algorithms become difficult. This shortage of data resources has emerged as a major bottleneck constraining the development of visual perception research on Chinese hickory.

To address this gap, we collected images of Chinese hickory across growth, ripening, and harvesting stages under natural orchard conditions, and constructed a standardised dataset annotated with maturity levels. This dataset has the following key characteristics: (1) It is captured in real orchard environments within a primary production region of Zhejiang Province (Lin’an District), spanning multiple acquisition dates and representative time periods of the day, and therefore covers diverse illumination, occlusions, and canopy backgrounds; (2) A three-level maturity classification system was developed based on observable external characteristics, and visually uncertain instances are marked as an unknown category (labelled as unknown in the released files); annotations were quality-controlled via a two-round procedure with an independent spot-check to ensure consistency; (3) The dataset is released with multiple annotation formats, a uniformly resized 640 × 640 counterpart, and structured metadata, enabling reusable benchmarks for maturity assessment, fruit detection, phenotypic analysis, and visual perception for orchard robots.

The dataset is released as open access, with the aim of providing a systematic, standardised and scalable resource for visual research on economic forest fruit crops and promoting the development of related algorithms and intelligent equipment.

Methods

Image Acquisition Design

To obtain images of Chinese hickory representative of natural orchard conditions, we collected data at multiple time points throughout an entire growing season, covering fruit development, ripening and harvesting stages. The collection site was a typical Chinese hickory cultivation area in Lin’an District, Hangzhou City, Zhejiang Province, China (30.0219° N, 119.15874° E). Based on the fruit growth calendar, image acquisition was scheduled on multiple dates between July and September, spanning the critical period from fruit enlargement to just before natural fruit drop.

To capture fruit appearance under different natural illumination conditions, images were taken during representative time periods—morning (7:00–9:00), noon (12:00–14:00), and evening (17:00–19:00)—using only ambient light without any supplemental lighting. The imaging device was an iPhone 14 smartphone (aperture f/1.5, equivalent focal length 26 mm). Apart from the necessary focus, aperture and resolution settings, all other parameters were set to automatic mode. During shooting, the camera was kept approximately 20–60 cm away from the target canopy to ensure sufficient fruit clarity while preserving a reasonable proportion of environmental context, resulting in compositions that range from close-ups of individual fruits to clusters of multiple fruits and scenes including foliage, branches, trunks, and sky background.

Data Cleaning

The number of images obtained during initial acquisition slightly exceeded that in the final public release. To ensure the accuracy of subsequent maturity assessment and instance annotation, we conducted manual quality checks during data curation, discarding samples that did not meet the usage requirements and retaining only high-quality images for downstream processing.

Specifically, we first computed simple no-reference quality indicators to flag candidate low-quality samples, including variance of Laplacian and Tenengrad gradient energy for blur, as well as the proportion of saturated (overexposed) or near-zero (underexposed) pixels for exposure issues. Two graduate students with a background in image processing then independently inspected the same batch of images one by one, browsing them sequentially on monitors of identical size at a fixed zoom level, and removed samples that were severely blurred, overexposed or underexposed, or whose scene content was irrelevant to the task. After this cleaning step, a total of 1,661 high-quality images with a resolution of 3024 × 3024 pixels were retained; these images constitute the Raw subset of original images in the public dataset.

Maturity Classification

The maturation process of Chinese hickory fruits is mainly manifested in observable phenotypic changes, including the pericarp colour gradually shifting from green to yellowish green and eventually to dark brown, surface blemishes evolving from scattered small spots to large irregular patches, and the pericarp transitioning from closed to dried and cracked open. Drawing on practical production experience and continuous field observations, we classified fruit maturity into three levels (maturity1– maturity3) based on an integrated assessment of pericarp browning degree, blemish pattern, and pericarp cracking status, and established corresponding visual decision criteria. Fruits whose maturity could not be reliably determined due to heavy occlusion, blur, or severe overlap were assigned to an unknown category to maintain annotation consistency and interpretability. Instance Annotation

After establishing the maturity classification scheme, we performed instance-level annotation on all images in the Raw subset, assigning a bounding box and maturity label to each visually identifiable fruit. The annotation workflow comprised three components: annotation tools, annotation rules, and quality control.

LabelImg software was used as the annotation tool, with the YOLO (TXT) format adopted as the initial annotation format. In this format, each image is associated with a single TXT file, and each line in the file records one fruit instance as a combination of class index and normalised bounding-box parameters, including the centre coordinates (cx, cy) and width and height (w, h). This annotation structure is consistent with the annotation scheme used by YOLO-series detection models. The YOLO-format annotations were subsequently converted via scripts into PASCAL VOC (XML) and COCO (JSON) formats.

Annotators examined each image sequentially, drew axis-aligned rectangular bounding boxes around every visually discernible Chinese hickory fruit, and assigned a maturity label to each instance according to the established classification criteria. In cases with occlusion, blur, or severe overlap, the following rules were applied: if the visible features were sufficient to make a reliable judgement of maturity, the fruit was assigned to one of the maturity1–maturity3 classes; however, if visual ambiguity caused by severe occlusion, motion blur, or dense overlap made the maturity attributes indiscernible, the instance was assigned to the unknown class.

In the annotated version of the dataset without any data augmentation, a total of 3,211 Chinese hickory fruit instances were obtained.

Image Preprocessing and Scaled Image Generation

To provide a fixed-size counterpart to the original images for use in mainstream detection frameworks, we generated a scaled image subset from the Raw images. Each 3024 × 3024-pixel image was proportionally resized to 640 × 640 pixels using bilinear interpolation, without cropping or padding, thereby preserving the original aspect ratio. During resizing, the bounding-box coordinates associated with each fruit instance were synchronously scaled according to the same scaling factor, ensuring geometric consistency between the images and the annotations.

No additional image enhancement, style transformation or synthetic data augmentation was applied during this preprocessing stage. The scaled images were stored in a dedicated directory and named using the same file names as their corresponding original images. The associated annotation files in YOLO format were updated with the rescaled bounding-box parameters. This scaled subset serves as an optional alternative for analysis pipelines and benchmark models that require a uniform input resolution.

Metadata Generation and Dataset Partitioning

To facilitate efficient data exploration, filtering and recombination, we generated two types of structured metadata from the Raw images and YOLO-format annotations: an image-level metadata file and an instance-level metadata file. For the image-level metadata, we parsed all annotations to count the total number of fruit instances and the number of instances in each maturity category within each image. In addition, each image was converted from RGB to HSV space, and the mean value of the V (value) channel over all pixels was computed as an indicator of global image brightness.

For the instance-level metadata, we extracted the normalised bounding-box parameters (cx, cy, w, h) from the YOLO annotations and converted them into pixel-level coordinates (bbox_xmin, bbox_ymin, bbox_xmax, bbox_ymax) in the original image coordinate system. The pixel-level bounding-box area (bbox_area_pixel) was calculated as the product of bounding-box width and height, and the normalised area (bbox_area_norm) was obtained by dividing the pixel area by the area of the full image. To characterise local illumination, we computed the mean value of the V channel within the region of interest (ROI) corresponding to each bounding box, denoted as mean_brightness_roi. All metadata fields are summarised in the Data Records section.

Based on the scaled image subset, we further constructed a dataset version suitable for direct training, validation and testing of detection models, denoted as CaryaData_YOLO. Images were randomly partitioned into training, validation and test sets with an overall ratio of 6:2:2, while accounting for the distribution of maturity categories to mitigate severe class imbalance across splits. Each image was assigned to exactly one subset, and all associated annotations and metadata entries followed the same assignment. The partitioning procedure was implemented using Python scripts, and the script used for dataset partitioning is publicly released alongside the dataset.

It should be noted that the sample sizes of the three maturity categories in CaryaData are somewhat imbalanced: maturity1 and maturity2 contain substantially more samples than maturity3. This imbalance partly reflects the fact that image acquisition was conducted mainly during the late growth and early ripening stages, and partly that relatively few fruits had reached full maturity in the orchard during the collection period. In summary, the uneven distribution of maturity levels primarily reflects the real field distribution of Chinese hickory under actual production conditions and acquisition timing, rather than being deliberately engineered.

Image Illumination Distribution

To quantify overall illumination in the images, we converted the original images from the RGB colour space to HSV (Hue–Saturation–Value) and used the V (value) channel as a brightness indicator. For each image, we computed the mean value of the V channel over all pixels to obtain the global image brightness. In parallel, we used each bounding box as a mask to extract the region of interest (ROI) corresponding to every fruit instance and calculated the mean V value within the ROI as the local fruit brightness. We then grouped the mean brightness values of all fruits according to the three annotated maturity categories (maturity1–maturity3) for subsequent statistical analysis and visualisation.

As shown in Fig. 3, we analysed both the global brightness distribution of the original orchard images and the local brightness of fruits at different maturity levels. Fig. 3(a) shows that the mean image brightness is mainly concentrated between 110 and 140, with a peak at approximately 120–130. Overall, the distribution is unimodal and approximately Gaussian, with only a small number of samples below 90 or above 150. This indicates that exposure control during data acquisition was stable, while a moderate degree of natural illumination variation was still retained. Fig. 3(b) shows the brightness distributions of fruits in the three maturity categories. Overall, maturity2 exhibits the highest median brightness, followed by maturity1, while maturity3 is slightly lower; this pattern is consistent with the gradual darkening of husk colour from green to brown during maturation. At the same time, the brightness distributions of different maturity levels overlap substantially, indicating that maturity labels are determined primarily by husk colour and surface morphology rather than brightness alone. Geometric and Spatial Distribution of Annotated Instances

To help potential users quickly understand the structural characteristics of CaryaData and the difficulty of detection tasks, we analysed the geometric attributes and spatial distribution of all 3,211 annotated instances and plotted the distributions of target aspect ratio, centre position, and area (Fig. 4).Fig. 4Statistical distributions of geometric and spatial properties of annotated instances: (a) target aspect ratio; (b) spatial distribution of target centres; (c) bounding-box area and corresponding counts.

Statistical distributions of geometric and spatial properties of annotated instances: (a) target aspect ratio; (b) spatial distribution of target centres; (c) bounding-box area and corresponding counts.

As shown in Fig. 4(a), the distribution of target aspect ratios reveals that most bounding boxes are close to 1:1, with data points clustered around the diagonal of the scatter plot, consistent with the nearly spherical natural shape of Chinese hickory. Only a small number of targets exhibit strongly elongated or flattened shapes, typically caused by shooting angle or partial occlusion. This suggests that target shapes in the dataset are generally regular, and geometric variation introduces relatively little interference, allowing researchers to focus more on challenging factors such as illumination, colour change, and surface texture.

As shown in Fig. 4(b), the distribution of target centres in normalised coordinates exhibits a pronounced central clustering pattern: the highest density occurs near the centre of the image and gradually decreases toward the periphery. This pattern reflects both the spatial positions of fruits within the tree canopy and the photographer’s tendency to place the target near the image centre. Such a spatial distribution clearly reveals human preferences in the acquisition process and provides a useful reference for follow-up studies on viewpoint shifts and compositional perturbations using this dataset.

As shown in Fig. 4(c), the normalised areas of the bounding boxes are concentrated mainly in the small-to-medium scale range, with relatively few extremely large or extremely small targets. This is consistent with the acquisition strategy of near-range shooting while retaining some surrounding background. Some fruits exhibit fine texture details in the original images but still appear as small or medium-sized targets after downscaling to 640 × 640 pixels, providing ample samples for analysing fruit discriminability across imaging scales and the impact of background interference.

Overall, the distributions of target scale, spatial location, and geometric shape in CaryaData are consistent with intuitive expectations for natural orchard scenes. On the one hand, they faithfully capture the spatiotemporal distribution characteristics of Chinese hickory in real field environments; on the other hand, they provide a representative yet moderately challenging benchmark for subsequent studies on small-object analysis, illumination variation, and background interference characterisation.

Original Images

The Raw image subset contains 1,661 high-quality images retained after data cleaning, all with a uniform resolution of 3024 × 3024 pixels. All images were captured in natural orchard environments, fully preserving the original illumination conditions, occlusions, and background structures at the time of acquisition. No artificial enhancement or visual content modification was applied.

Annotation Files

For all identifiable fruit instances in the original images, the dataset provides instance-level bounding-box annotations based on the maturity classification scheme. To enhance versatility and accommodate diverse research needs, annotations are released in three common formats. All three are derived from the same manually annotated dataset and generated via format-conversion scripts.

YOLO (TXT) format

Stored as plain-text files, with one TXT file per image. Each line records one instance and contains the category ID together with the normalised bounding-box centre coordinates (cx, cy) and width and height (w, h). File names match those of the corresponding images.

PASCAL VOC (XML) format

Stored as XML files, with each file corresponding to one image. These files include image size information, category labels, and bounding-box positions in the image coordinate system.

COCO (JSON) format

Stored as JSON files, organising all images and instances within a unified data structure. These files include image information, annotation details, and category definitions, facilitating direct integration with mainstream detection frameworks.

For each image, the annotations, instance locations, and maturity category labels are strictly consistent across all three formats.

Uniform-sized Images

To provide a fixed-size counterpart to the original images, the dataset includes a subset of 640 × 640-pixel images derived from the Raw images. In this subset, each original image has a corresponding scaled image with the same file name, and the associated annotation files are updated to match the scaled image coordinates. No additional enhancement or style transformation is applied to these images. This subset primarily serves as an optional alternative for analysis pipelines that require a uniform input resolution.

Metadata Files

To help users quickly understand the data structure without manually inspecting each raw image and annotation file, and to flexibly filter and reorganise samples, CaryaData provides two types of structured metadata files generated from the raw images and YOLO annotations. All metadata are provided as UTF-8 encoded CSV files, which can be directly read in Python, R, Matlab and common spreadsheet software.

Technical Validation

Image quality screening

To enforce consistent instance-level data quality before release, we designed an automated ROI-based screening script. The script evaluates image quality at the granularity of each YOLO bounding box by cropping the corresponding fruit region (ROI), computing sharpness/exposure-related metrics, and flagging low-quality bounding boxes according to predefined thresholds.

Script workflow

For each image, the script reads the corresponding YOLO label file, converts normalised box parameters into pixel coordinates, and crops the ROI. Screening outcome and manual audit

Using this ROI-based screening, we flagged 203 bounding boxes as low-quality and manually inspected all flagged cases. The class-wise distribution was: unknown (67), maturity3 (67), maturity1 (33), and maturity2 (36). For the unknown class, the corresponding images typically still contain other valid annotated instances; therefore, we retained these images and their valid annotations. The unknown label is used to isolate instances whose maturity cannot be reliably determined, without affecting the usability of other instances within the same image.

For maturity1-maturity3, the dominant trigger was underexposure caused by leaf occlusion, which locally reduces illumination and can violate the brightness-related thresholds. Notably, maturity3 accounts for a larger share of flagged boxes because late-maturity fruits exhibit darker pericarps, making them more susceptible to being identified as low-brightness under the same criteria. Manual inspection indicates that, despite underexposure or dark appearance, key maturity cues (browning and cracking) remain discernible in most cases; therefore, these samples reflect realistic challenging conditions rather than unusable data.

The above quality-screening script is released alongside the dataset, enabling users to reproduce the screening list and summary report on the same inputs.

Annotation quality inspection

To ensure annotation quality and consistency, we adopted a two-round quality-control procedure with automated consistency checking and a re-annotation feedback loop. In the first round, a trained annotator completed instance-level annotations for all images under a unified guideline. In the second round, another experienced annotator independently re-annotated all images to ensure comprehensive cross-validation. We then developed a validation script to automatically assess consistency between the two complete annotation sets: bounding boxes were matched using an IoU-based optimal assignment (pairs were considered matchable only when class labels agreed), and the mean IoU of matched pairs was computed as an image-level consistency score. Images with scores below 0.9 were flagged for review and re-annotation until the threshold was met. The validation script is released in our GitHub repository. This procedure helps identify and correct discrepancies in challenging cases (e.g., occlusion, severe overlap, ambiguous boundaries, and transitional maturity samples), thereby improving the reliability and reproducibility of the annotations.

Validation of the maturity category rationality

To objectively validate the rationality of the proposed three-level maturity labels, we conducted a static peduncle detachment-force experiment. A total of 120 fresh Chinese hickory fruits with intact peduncles were collected (40 for each of maturity1, maturity2, and maturity3). As detachment-force testing is destructive, each maturity group was further split into two subsets for two loading modes: axial pulling (Fig. 7(b) and radial bending (Fig. 7(c), resulting in n = 20 per maturity level for each loading mode. Usage Notes

This section provides recommendations for potential users on how to utilise CaryaData for different research tasks, helping researchers select appropriate data subsets, processing strategies, and experimental configurations according to their needs.

Recommended Task Setups

This dataset supports multiple computer-vision tasks, including but not limited to:

Object detection

Using the bounding-box annotations for the four categories (maturity1, maturity2, maturity3, and unknown), multi-class object detection models can be trained directly. We recommend using the CaryaData_YOLO subset, which already provides a complete train/val/test split.

Maturity classification

Researchers may crop individual fruit images based on detection results to construct datasets for classification tasks. For phenotypic analyses targeting specific maturity levels, the Raw subset can be combined with the metadata files to filter relevant samples.

Class imbalance and few-shot learning

Owing to the naturally long-tailed distribution of maturity levels (with maturity3 being much less frequent than the other categories), CaryaData can serve as a compact benchmark for studying class-imbalance learning and few-shot scenarios. We recommend using the original, non-augmented dataset to more faithfully reflect the true field distribution.

Image Preprocessing Recommendations

For convenience in model training, the default image size of the CaryaData_YOLO subset is 640 × 640, which can be directly used as input to mainstream detection networks. We recommend applying basic brightness and colour normalisation to mitigate the impact of inter-session lighting variations. If researchers wish to examine model robustness under strong illumination perturbations, they may combine the Raw subset or use metadata to filter samples acquired under specific lighting conditions.

Considerations

Despite providing instance-level bounding boxes and maturity labels collected in natural orchards for close-range, object-level maturity detection, CaryaData has the following limitations that should be considered when interpreting and generalising results.

(1) Data were collected under a fixed protocol within a single production region using the same mobile device, yielding consistent and reproducible imagery. For cross-region/year/device deployment, we recommend lightweight fine-tuning with a small target-domain sample set, together with colour normalisation/augmentation to reduce domain shift.

(2) Most images are taken at mid-to-close range with relatively few instances per image, making the dataset more suitable for close-range inspection and sampling. It provides limited coverage for wide-FOV whole-tree perception and long-range dense detection required by harvesting robots; future extensions may include multiple devices, multiple capture distances, and higher-density/occlusion subsets.

(3) Labels are assigned from visual cues and may remain ambiguous for transitional samples and under strong illumination/shadows or severe occlusion/overlap (isolated as unknown). The dataset provides axis-aligned boxes rather than instance masks, and exhibits class imbalance; re-sampling or class re-weighting can be used to improve robustness.
