Lung microbiota-derived deoxyinosine alleviates TBI-aggravated sepsis-induced lung injury via the S100A9/RAGE pathway
Authors: Bailun Wang, Angran Gu, Yizheng Yang, Xuan Liu, Runmeng Liu, Yuelan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Traumatic brain injury (TBI) frequently leads to severe systemic complications, with pulmonary dysfunction acting as a major determinant of poor prognosis in survivors. While the lung microbiota is increasingly recognized as a critical regulator of pulmonary immune homeostasis, the specific mechanisms by which TBI remotely remodels the lung microenvironment to exacerbate secondary insults, such as sepsis-induced acute lung injury (ALI), remain poorly understood. To investigate this mechanism, we established a murine model combining controlled cortical impact with LPS-induced sepsis and analyzed bronchoalveolar lavage fluid by 16S rRNA sequencing and untargeted metabolomics. We further conducted microbiota depletion and transplantation experiments to establish causality, alongside molecular docking, Co-IP (co-immunoprecipitation), and transgenic mouse models to elucidate molecular pathways. Our results demonstrate that TBI significantly disrupts the lung microbiota, characterized by a reduction in Corynebacterium, and decreases the levels of the metabolite deoxyinosine. Microbiota transplantation from TBI mice worsened sepsis-induced lung injury in recipients, whereas deoxyinosine administration alleviated tissue damage by promoting the polarization of alveolar macrophages from a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. Mechanistically, deoxyinosine binds directly to S100A9, competitively inhibiting its interaction with the Receptor for Advanced Glycation End Products (RAGE), which subsequently suppresses downstream NF-κB signaling. Introduction

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a leading cause of mortality and long-term disability worldwide. Survivors frequently suffer from multi-organ dysfunction, with pulmonary complications being particularly severe and detrimental to prognosis [1, 2]. Clinical and experimental evidence indicates that TBI significantly exacerbates secondary lung injury, such as that observed in sepsis, yet the underlying mechanisms remain elusive, limiting therapeutic options [3, 4]. Recently, the "microbiota-organ axis" concept has revolutionized our understanding of host physiology. The lung, specifically, harbors a unique, dynamic microbial community that is critical for maintaining pulmonary immune homeostasis [5, 6], and dysbiosis within this niche is increasingly implicated in various acute and chronic lung diseases [7].

Sepsis is a critical clinical condition, and sepsis-induced acute lung injury (ALI) and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) are major drivers of mortality [8, 9]. While ALI is traditionally attributed to uncontrolled host inflammatory responses, accumulating evidence suggests that the local lung microbiota and its metabolites are indispensable endogenous regulators of pulmonary inflammation [10]. Commensal bacteria and their metabolic byproducts fine-tune lung injury and repair by modulating the phenotype and function of immune cells, such as alveolar macrophages [11, 12]. This raises a critical question: Does TBI, acting as a "first hit," remotely remodel the distal lung microecology, thereby altering susceptibility to a subsequent septic "second hit"? Answering this connects neurotrauma, microbial ecology, and immunometabolism, offering significant potential for understanding brain-lung axis interactions and identifying targets for multi-organ protection.

Although studies have explored alterations in the gut microbiota following TBI and their impact on neuroinflammation [13, 14], it remains unclear whether and how TBI alters the lung microbiota, and what functional role these alterations play in secondary lung injury. Furthermore, the contribution of microbial metabolites, which act as key messengers in host-microbe crosstalk, remains unexplored in this potential pathway. Bridging this knowledge gap is a critical step toward unraveling the complex mechanisms of multi-organ interaction.

We hypothesized that TBI disrupts pulmonary immune homeostasis by altering the lung microbiota and its metabolic profile, thereby exacerbating sepsis-induced ALI. We first confirmed that TBI aggravates sepsis-associated ALI in a mouse model. Subsequently, 16S rRNA sequencing and untargeted metabolomics revealed specific TBI-induced alterations in lung microbiota and metabolites, identifying deoxyinosine as a key metabolite highly correlated with differential bacteria. Using lung microbiota-depleted mice and microbiota transplantation, we established a causal link between TBI-derived lung dysbiosis and aggravated ALI. Functional studies demonstrated that deoxyinosine administration mitigates TBI-exacerbated lung injury by promoting alveolar macrophage polarization from a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. Mechanistically, deoxyinosine binds directly to S100A9 without affecting its expression, competitively inhibiting the S100A9 interactions with the Receptor for Advanced Glycation End Products (RAGE). This blockade suppresses downstream NF-κB signaling, thereby modulating macrophage polarization and protecting lung tissue.

This study elucidates a novel mechanism by which TBI exacerbates sepsis-induced lung injury via the lung microbiota and its metabolites. These findings not only expand our understanding of the brain-lung axis but also suggest new microbial and metabolic interventions for preventing and treating post-TBI multi-organ dysfunction.

Methods

Animals

All procedures complied with the principles of the 3Rs (Replacement, Reduction, and Refinement) and were approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Shandong First Medical University. Male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Mice were housed under specific controlled conditions (25 ± 2 °C; 50 ± 10% humidity; 12-h light/dark cycle) with free access to food and water.

Sepsis model

A mild sepsis model was established by intraperitoneal injection of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) at 5 mg/kg.

Traumatic brain injury model

Mice in the TBI group were anesthetized with isoflurane. A cranial bone flap was removed at the junction of the right lambdoid and coronal sutures. Traumatic brain injury was induced using a controlled cortical impact (CCI) device with the following parameters: 3.5-mm tip diameter, 6 m/s impact velocity, and 2 mm impact depth. After injury, the scalp was sutured and mice were allowed to recover on a warmed pad. Sham-operated mice underwent craniotomy without CCI. Mice were euthanized by intraperitoneal injection of an overdose of anesthetic, and lung tissue and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) were collected for subsequent analyses.

Hematoxylin and eosin staining

Liver, lung, and kidney tissues were collected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h. Samples were then dehydrated, paraffin-embedded, sectioned at 4 μm, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Histological injury was scored using previously reported scoring systems for the liver, lung, and kidney [14–16].

BALF collection and analysis

BALF was collected using a 1-mL syringe by slowly instilling 0.3 mL pre-chilled sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) into the lungs over 2–3 s, followed by gentle aspiration; this procedure was repeated three times. BALF was centrifuged at 300–500 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected to quantify protein concentration using a BCA assay kit. Total cell counts were determined by loading 20 μL BALF onto an automated cell counter. The remaining BALF supernatant was used to measure inflammatory cytokines by ELISA.

Lung wet-to-dry weight ratio

Pulmonary edema was evaluated using the wet-to-dry (W/D) weight ratio. The right lung was excised and gently blotted with gauze to remove surface moisture, and the wet weight was recorded. Tissues were then dried at 60 °C for 48 h and weighed again to obtain the dry weight. The W/D ratio was calculated as wet weight divided by dry weight.

Microbiome and metabolomics analyses

BALF samples were snap-frozen and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Microbial DNA was extracted according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The targeted 16S rRNA gene region was amplified using barcode-indexed, region-specific primers, and amplicons were sequenced. After sample demultiplexing, paired-end reads were quality-filtered and merged based on overlap to generate high-quality sequences. Denoising was performed using methods such as DADA2 or Deblur to obtain amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) and their abundance profiles. Downstream analyses included taxonomic assignment, α/β diversity analyses, differential abundance testing, correlation analyses, and functional prediction, with appropriate statistical and visualization workflows.

For metabolomics, included samples were analyzed by LC–MS/MS. Raw data were processed using Progenesis QI (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA) for peak detection, deconvolution, alignment, and integration. Metabolite annotation was performed using the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB) and METLIN. To minimize technical and analytical variability, annotated data were preprocessed to generate a final data matrix for subsequent analyses. Multivariate analyses, including PCA and OPLS-DA, were performed using the ropls package (version 1.6.2) in R. Metabolites were further annotated with reference to HMDB and KEGG pathway databases.

Preparation of lung microbiota-depleted mice

To deplete endogenous pulmonary bacteria, antibiotics (10 μg ciprofloxacin plus 50 μg metronidazole; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) were administered via intratracheal instillation once every 3 days for a total of five administrations.

Pulmonary microbiota transplantation

BALF was collected from donor mice and centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The pellet containing the pulmonary microbial fraction was resuspended in 150 μL sterile PBS. Recipient mice received 50 μL of the corresponding suspension via intratracheal instillation every 5 days for 60 days. BALF from mice within the same donor group was pooled prior to transplantation to ensure consistency in microbial composition and load across recipients[17].

Deoxyinosine administration

Deoxyinosine was administered via a single intraperitoneal injection at a dose of 25 mg/kg body weight in mice, given after the LPS challenge.

Immunofluorescence staining

For lung tissue, mice were perfused intracardially with cold PBS, and lungs were harvested and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. After sectioning, slides were blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against iNOS and Arg-1. After PBS washes, sections were incubated with fluorescent secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature, counterstained with DAPI, and imaged by fluorescence microscopy. Quantification was performed using ImageJ.

For cells, macrophages grown on coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 5 min, and blocked with 5% BSA at room temperature for 1.5 h. Coverslips were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-iNOS (1:100) and anti-Arg-1 (1:200), followed by secondary antibody incubation for 2 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 15 min. Fluorescence signals were captured and mean fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ.

Western blotting

Lung tissues were homogenized by sonication, and MH-S cells were lysed using an appropriate lysis buffer. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay kit (P0012, Beyotime, China). Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE on 10% polyacrylamide gels and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate, and band intensities were quantified using ImageJ (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted using an RNA extraction kit (Fastagen, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit (R323-01, Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Quantitative PCR was performed using a CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA) with a qPCR kit (Q711, Vazyme). Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2^ − ΔΔCt method.

Cell culture and stimulation

MH-S cells were purchased from FuHeng (FH1137, Shanghai, China) and cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. Cells were seeded in six-well plates and, at ~ 90% confluence, stimulated with LPS (1 μg/mL; Sigma, USA).

Cell transfection

When cells reached ~ 70% confluence, plasmids were transfected into MH-S cells using Lipofectamine 3000 according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After 6 h, the medium was replaced with complete medium. Cells were collected 48 h post-transfection for downstream experiments, and transfection efficiency was verified by western blotting.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking was performed to evaluate the potential interaction between deoxyinosine and S100a9 (details provided in the corresponding Results section).

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

MH-S cell lysates were precleared with Protein A/G agarose beads to reduce nonspecific binding. The supernatant was incubated overnight at 4 °C with the indicated antibody and 20 μL Protein A/G agarose beads on a rotator. Beads were then washed, resuspended in loading buffer, and boiled for 10 min. Immunoprecipitated proteins were analyzed by western blotting.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way or two-way ANOVA followed by the Newman-Keuls post hoc test, as appropriate. Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t-test. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Compared with the Control group, TBI reduced the relative abundance of Comamonas and Corynebacterium. Notably, the decrease in Corynebacterium abundance represented the most discriminative taxonomic feature distinguishing the two groups (Fig. 2B-E). These findings indicate that TBI markedly reshaped the composition and diversity of the pulmonary microbiota in mice.

To further characterize metabolic alterations, we conducted untargeted metabolomic profiling to compare BALF metabolites between the two groups. Relative to the Control group, 64 metabolites were increased and 83 metabolites were decreased in the TBI group (Fig. 2F). Among these, deoxyinosine levels were significantly decreased in TBI-treated mice (Fig. 2G-I). Integrated analysis combining untargeted metabolomics with microbial sequencing further revealed a strong correlation between deoxyinosine and Corynebacterium, and both were reduced in the TBI group (Fig. 2J).

Collectively, these data suggest that deoxyinosine may represent a key microbiota-derived metabolite contributing to TBI-aggravated acute lung injury in mice.

To test whether TBI-associated microbial shifts modulate lung injury during sepsis, we transplanted BALF microbiota from untreated control mice or TBI mice into lung microbiota-depleted recipients, followed by intraperitoneal LPS administration in both groups. To determine whether deoxyinosine regulates macrophage polarization after TBI, mice were allocated to four groups (Control, Deoxyinosine, TBI, and TBI + Deoxyinosine), and macrophage phenotypes in lung tissue were evaluated. Immunofluorescence staining revealed that TBI increased iNOS expression (an M1 marker) and decreased Arg-1 expression relative to Control. Deoxyinosine administration partially reversed these changes, as indicated by reduced iNOS and increased Arg-1 expression in the TBI + Deoxyinosine group compared with the TBI group (Fig. 4G-J). Western blotting and qPCR analyses of lung tissue produced concordant results (Fig. 4K-M).

In vitro, mouse alveolar macrophage MH-S cells were divided into four groups (Control, Deoxyinosine, LPS, and LPS + Deoxyinosine). Consistent with the in vivo findings, LPS stimulation increased iNOS and decreased Arg-1 expression compared with Control. Deoxyinosine treatment counteracted these effects, evidenced by reduced iNOS and elevated Arg-1 expression in the LPS + Deoxyinosine group relative to the LPS group (Fig. 4N-Q). Western blotting and qPCR further confirmed these results (Fig. 4R-T).

Collectively, these data indicate that deoxyinosine suppresses M1 polarization while promoting M2 polarization in alveolar macrophages, thereby attenuating TBI-exacerbated SALI in mice.

These effects were absent in S100a9 KO mice, in which iNOS and Arg-1 expression did not differ between the TBI and TBI + Deoxyinosine groups (Fig. 5G-K).

In vitro, deoxyinosine similarly reversed LPS-induced increases in iNOS and decreases in Arg-1 expression in MH-S cells. This effect was abolished by siRNA-mediated knockdown of S100a9 (si-S100a9), the efficiency of which was confirmed by Western blot analysis (Fig. 5L-P, Fig. S1A). Furthermore, ELISA results demonstrated that deoxyinosine significantly reduced the production of IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α in LPS-stimulated MH-S cells, whereas this inhibitory effect was similarly abolished following si-S100a9 treatment (Fig. S2A). Notably, deoxyinosine did not change S100a9 expression in either in vivo or in vitro experiments (Fig. 5J,K,O,P).

Collectively, these data indicate that deoxyinosine regulates alveolar macrophage polarization through an S100a9-dependent mechanism, without directly altering S100a9 expression.

Discussion

TBI remains a leading cause of mortality and long-term disability, with secondary pulmonary complications representing a critical determinant of poor prognosis. In this study, we demonstrate that TBI remotely remodels the lung microenvironment by altering the pulmonary microbiota and its metabolic profile, thereby exacerbating subsequent SALI. Specifically, we identified that TBI leads to a reduction in Corynebacterium abundance and a concomitant decrease in the microbial metabolite deoxyinosine. Through a series of microbiota depletion and transplantation experiments, we established a causal link between TBI-induced lung dysbiosis and aggravated ALI. Importantly, supplementation with deoxyinosine attenuated lung injury by promoting alveolar macrophage polarization from a pro-inflammatory M1 to an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. Mechanistically, deoxyinosine directly binds to S100A9, competitively inhibiting its interaction with the receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE), which in turn suppresses downstream NF-κB signaling. These findings elucidate a previously unrecognized brain-lung axis mediated by the lung microbiota and its metabolites, highlighting potential therapeutic targets for post-TBI multi-organ protection.

Our study extends the rapidly evolving concept of the “microbiota-organ axis” into the context of neurotrauma. While prior research has predominantly focused on the gut-brain axis following TBI—demonstrating that gut dysbiosis exacerbates neuroinflammation and cognitive impairment—the role of the lung microbiota in remote organ injury has remained largely unexplored [18, 19]. Our findings align with emerging evidence that the lung harbors a dynamic microbial community capable of regulating pulmonary immune homeostasis and influencing the severity of lung injury. For instance, recent studies have shown that lung commensals can modulate macrophage function and inflammatory responses during conditions such as oxygen-induced lung injury or silica-induced fibrosis [12]. Consistent with these reports, we observed that TBI-induced depletion of Corynebacterium—a genus implicated in maintaining pulmonary immune balance—was associated with worsened sepsis outcomes.

However, our study diverges from prior work in several key aspects. First, while most research on microbiota-organ communication has focused on steady-state or primary lung insults [20], we demonstrate that a remote central nervous system injury can actively reshape the lung microbial landscape, thereby priming the lung for a secondary hit. Second, although microbial metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids and tryptophan derivatives have been widely studied in gut-organ axes [21, 22], the role of purine nucleosides like deoxyinosine in lung immunomodulation has been scarcely reported. Our integrated multi-omics approach identified deoxyinosine as a key effector linking dysbiosis to immune dysregulation, a finding that expands the known repertoire of microbiota-derived immunomodulators.

S100A9, often co-expressed with S100A8 as a calprotectin heterodimer, functions as a damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) that amplifies inflammatory responses [23, 24]. Previous studies have consistently shown that S100A9 promotes pro-inflammatory M1 macrophage polarization through multiple mechanisms. In models of sepsis and acute lung injury, S100A9 release from activated neutrophils and epithelial cells has been shown to sustain NF-κB activation and inflammasome signaling, driving TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 production [25, 26]. Our observation that deoxyinosine-mediated S100A9 binding attenuates M1 polarization aligns with these reports, yet adds the novel dimension of microbiota-derived metabolite regulation of this pathway. Notably, while most studies focus on S100A9 expression regulation, our finding that deoxyinosine inhibits S100A9 function without affecting its expression suggests a previously unrecognized layer of post-translational control.

RAGE serves as a multi-ligand pattern recognition receptor that transduces DAMPs signals into sustained inflammatory responses [27]. The S100A9-RAGE interaction has been particularly implicated in chronic inflammatory conditions such as atherosclerosis [28], arthritis [29], and neurodegenerative diseases [30]. In acute settings, RAGE activation by S100A9 promotes NADPH oxidase-dependent ROS generation and sustained NF-κB activation, creating a positive feedback loop that amplifies inflammation [31]. Our results demonstrating that RAGE overexpression partially reverses deoxyinosine's protective effects confirm the centrality of this receptor in mediating S100A9's pro-inflammatory actions in TBI-exacerbated lung injury.

The NF-κB pathway serves as the master regulator connecting S100A9/RAGE activation to macrophage polarization [32, 33]. Upon RAGE engagement, downstream signaling involves MyD88 adaptor proteins, leading to IκB kinase (IKK) complex activation, IκBα phosphorylation and degradation, and subsequent nuclear translocation of NF-κB p65 [34]. Our data showing deoxyinosine-mediated suppression of p65 and IκB phosphorylation aligns with this canonical pathway. Notably, the specificity of deoxyinosine's effect—primarily attenuating the S100A9-RAGE axis rather than globally suppressing NF-κB—suggests a targeted intervention approach. This specificity may explain why deoxyinosine promotes M2 polarization rather than causing generalized immunosuppression, as evidenced by increased Arg-1 expression alongside decreased iNOS.

From a clinical perspective, our findings may be relevant to the management of patients with moderate-to-severe TBI, who are known to be at high risk for pulmonary complications, including hospital-acquired pneumonia, ventilator-associated pneumonia, and secondary lung injury during systemic infection [35]. In this setting, our data raise the possibility that TBI does not merely coexist with pulmonary dysfunction, but may actively precondition the lung through microbiota-metabolite-immune interactions, thereby increasing susceptibility to a subsequent inflammatory insult. If confirmed in patients, this concept could have several implications. First, it supports greater attention to early pulmonary surveillance after TBI, not only in terms of gas exchange and imaging, but also with respect to inflammatory phenotyping and, potentially, airway microbial signatures. Second, our results suggest that the S100A9/RAGE axis may merit evaluation as a translational biomarker pathway in TBI patients at risk for sepsis-associated lung injury or ARDS, particularly given growing interest in these molecules in inflammatory lung disease. Third, the identification of deoxyinosine as a protective microbiota-associated metabolite raises the broader possibility that preserving or restoring pulmonary microbial homeostasis may have therapeutic value in neurocritical care. However, these implications should be interpreted cautiously. Our study used a murine TBI + LPS model and therefore cannot fully capture the complexity of human TBI, in which pulmonary outcomes are also influenced by aspiration, mechanical ventilation, antibiotic exposure, nutritional support, and inter-individual heterogeneity in injury severity and comorbidities. Moreover, whether deoxyinosine itself, or modulation of the S100A9/RAGE pathway, is feasible and effective in patients remains unknown. Thus, the present work should be viewed primarily as a mechanistic foundation for future translational studies rather than as direct evidence for immediate clinical intervention.

Despite these advances, several limitations merit consideration. First, an important question remains whether TBI already alters the pulmonary milieu at steady state, before the subsequent LPS challenge.

Although the present study was designed primarily to interrogate how prior TBI modifies susceptibility to sepsis-induced lung injury, and we therefore did not perform a full baseline assessment of pulmonary function and inflammatory indices in TBI-only mice, several lines of evidence suggest that TBI likely establishes a pre-infected “primed” lung state. Prior studies have shown that TBI alone can induce early lung inflammation, alveolar-capillary barrier disruption, and peripheral myeloid activation within hours to 1 day after injury, indicating that the lung is not immunologically quiescent after brain trauma [4, 36]. More recent work has further demonstrated that brain–lung signaling after TBI can drive emergency monopoiesis and secondary lung injury, supporting the concept that pulmonary susceptibility is altered even before a second hit occurs [1]. With respect to microbiota, direct evidence in this area is still limited, but both clinical and preclinical studies suggest that severe brain injury is associated with early changes in bronchoalveolar microbial composition and even translocation-related reshaping of the lung microbiota. In line with this concept, our microbiota transplantation results support the notion that TBI-induced alterations in the pulmonary microbial ecosystem are already biologically meaningful before LPS exposure and can subsequently influence the severity of sepsis-associated lung injury [37]. Future studies should therefore include dedicated TBI-only cohorts to systematically characterize baseline pulmonary mechanics, barrier permeability, cytokine profiles, and lung microbiota/metabolite dynamics, which will help define how TBI preconditions the lung for later inflammatory insults.

Another limitation of the present study is that we focused primarily on macrophage polarization rather than performing a comprehensive immune census of the lung after LPS challenge. Although TBI increased total BALF cellularity and enhanced inflammatory cytokine production in our model, we did not directly quantify the relative abundance of individual leukocyte subsets, such as alveolar/interstitial macrophages, recruited monocytes, neutrophils, dendritic cells, or lymphocyte populations, by flow cytometry or single-cell approaches. Therefore, our data support the conclusion that macrophage activation state is altered, but do not establish whether TBI also reshapes the broader pulmonary leukocyte landscape following LPS exposure. This distinction is important, because previous studies indicate that TBI-induced brain-lung crosstalk can involve emergency monopoiesis, neutrophil infiltration and NET formation, and neural regulation of lung macrophage-centered inflammatory programs. Thus, while our results identify macrophage polarization as a major mechanistic node in the deoxyinosine/S100A9/RAGE pathway, other innate immune populations are also likely to contribute to the amplified inflammatory response. Future studies using flow cytometry, spatial immunophenotyping, or single-cell transcriptomics will be needed to define how TBI reshapes the overall immune composition of the lung during secondary septic injury.

Third, while we identified Corynebacterium and deoxyinosine as key players, the lung microbiota comprises a complex community, and other taxa or metabolites may also contribute to the observed phenotype. Metagenomic sequencing and functional validation of additional candidates are warranted. Finally, the translational potential of deoxyinosine requires further evaluation, including pharmacokinetic studies and assessment of efficacy in larger animals or in the presence of comorbidities. Although we focused on alveolar macrophages, other immune cells, such as neutrophils and T cells, may also be influenced by deoxyinosine and warrant investigation.

Another methodological limitation concerns the microbiota transplantation protocol. Because BALF was centrifuged at 13,000 rpm before transplantation, the resulting pellet may have contained not only microbial components but also host cells, cell debris, and other host-derived materials. Therefore, although our transplantation experiments indicate that transferable BALF-derived components from TBI mice are biologically sufficient to influence recipient responses, we cannot conclude that these effects were mediated exclusively by microbiota. Host-derived components may also have contributed to the observed phenotype. Future studies should incorporate an initial low-speed centrifugation step or other purification strategies to remove host cells prior to microbial enrichment and transplantation, thereby allowing a more rigorous dissection of microbiota-specific versus host-derived effects.

Taken together, our findings support a model in which TBI reshapes the pulmonary microenvironment and primes the lung for exaggerated inflammatory injury after a secondary septic insult. More broadly, this work provides a mechanistic framework linking brain injury to pulmonary dysfunction through microbiota–metabolite–immune interactions and offers a foundation for future studies aimed at validating these pathways in clinically relevant settings.

Conclusion

This study establishes a novel brain-lung communication pathway wherein TBI-induced lung dysbiosis reduces deoxyinosine levels, leading to enhanced S100A9/RAGE signaling, macrophage M1 polarization, and aggravated sepsis-induced lung injury. Our findings not only expand the understanding of remote organ injury after TBI but also propose a microbiota-metabolite-based strategy for lung protection. Targeting the S100A9/RAGE axis with deoxyinosine or its analogs may offer a new therapeutic avenue to mitigate multi-organ dysfunction in critically injured patients.
