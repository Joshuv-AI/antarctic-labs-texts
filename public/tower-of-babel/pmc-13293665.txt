Parasitic Persistence: The Diagnostic Challenge of Strongyloidiasis in a Nonendemic Setting
Authors: Alexander Siegel, Jennifer Gamache, Kyaw Min Tun, Wuttiporn Manatsathit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Strongyloidiasis is a parasitic infection endemic to warm and moist climates, which is caused by the intestinal nematode Strongyloides stercoralis. In the United States, most cases are seen in immunocompromised patients residing in the Northeast and East South-Central regions and in travelers from endemic regions. While often underrecognized, Strongyloides infection should remain a key consideration in patients presenting with atypical gastrointestinal symptoms, with a relevant exposure history. We present a case of a 45-year-old male initially diagnosed with Helicobacter pylori whose symptoms persisted and was ultimately diagnosed with Strongyloides infection.

Introduction

Strongyloidiasis is a parasitic infection caused by the intestinal nematode Strongyloides stercoralis.1 The infection is endemic to tropical environments where warm, moist climates allow for cutaneous transmission through contaminated soil. Although this parasitic infection is estimated to affect millions worldwide, cases in the United States are uncommon and frequently underrecognized.2 In the United States, infections typically occur among immigrants or travelers from endemic areas, as well as among immunocompromised individuals.3

One unique feature of Strongyloides infection is its ability to complete an autoinfective life cycle within a human host.4 This allows the parasite to persist for years without additional exposure. While many infected individuals remain asymptomatic, clinical manifestations can range from mild gastrointestinal (GI) complaints to life-threatening hyperinfection syndrome.

Diagnosis of Strongyloides infection can be challenging, especially in nonendemic settings, due to its nonspecific symptom profile, variable laboratory findings, and overall low prevalence. Endoscopic and histopathologic evaluations are often required for definitive diagnosis. We present a case of chronic GI symptoms ultimately attributed to S stercoralis infection diagnosed on duodenal biopsy in a patient residing in a nonendemic region.

Case Report

A 45-year-old Hispanic immigrant male with no significant past medical history presented to the gastroenterology clinic with a 2-month history of episodic hematochezia associated with intermittent epigastric pain. He denied fever, weight loss, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) use, or recent travel but reported immigrating to the United States several years prior. Physical examination was significant for external hemorrhoids on digital rectal examination. Laboratory evaluation revealed a hemoglobin level of 14.6 g/dL. A Helicobacter pylori breath test was positive, and the patient was treated with standard quadruple therapy.

Four months later, he underwent outpatient upper endoscopy and colonoscopy for persistent symptoms. Upper endoscopy demonstrated nonbleeding duodenal erosions and erythematous gastric mucosa. Gastric biopsies showed normal mucosa without evidence of H pylori, but no duodenal biopsies were obtained. Colonoscopy revealed hemorrhoids, and random colon biopsies demonstrated active colitis with eosinophilic infiltrates, granulomas, and focal cryptitis. The patient did not return for follow-up due to lack of health insurance and a disconnected phone number.

Six months later, he presented to the emergency department with right upper quadrant abdominal pain and nausea. Laboratory testing was notable for leukocytosis of 22.1 K/uL with eosinophilia, elevated liver enzymes including alanine aminotransferase of 237 U/L and aspartate aminotransferase of 74 U/L, and an elevated hemoglobin of 19.9 g/dL. Repeat colonoscopy was grossly normal, and histologic evaluation again demonstrated inflammatory changes similar to those seen previously. Upper endoscopy revealed evidence of duodenitis with nonbleeding mucosal erosions in the duodenal bulb (Figure 1). Discussion

This case illustrates the diagnostic challenges associated with chronic GI symptoms in patients with parasitic infections in nonendemic settings. Because symptoms are often nonspecific and may mimic more common GI disorders, strongyloidiasis is rarely included in the initial differential diagnosis. In this patient, the initial presentation of hematochezia and epigastric pain prompted evaluation for more typical etiologies, including hemorrhoidal bleeding and H pylori infection. Although colon biopsies demonstrated eosinophilic inflammation, the absence of a clear diagnosis and eventual loss to follow-up delayed recognition of the true infectious etiology. The definitive diagnosis was only established after repeat endoscopy, with duodenal biopsy demonstrating organisms consistent with S stercoralis.

A unique feature of S stercoralis is its ability to undergo autoinfection within a human host. Unlike many other intestinal parasites that require repeated exposure for reinfection, Strongyloides larvae can penetrate the intestinal mucosa or perianal skin and re-enter the systemic circulation. This allows the infection to persist within a host for years after initial exposure. As a result, individuals who immigrated from endemic regions may present with symptoms years after they are initially infected.4 Early recognition of strongyloidiasis is particularly important because untreated infection can progress to hyperinfection syndrome or disseminated disease, especially in immunocompromised patients. Mortality rates associated with disseminated strongyloidiasis are high, and patients can present with severe GI and pulmonary manifestations. Early diagnosis and treatment with ivermectin are essential to prevent disease progression.

Eosinophilia is frequently seen on laboratory testing in patients with chronic strongyloidiasis. In this case, peripheral eosinophilia was observed during the patient’s emergency department presentation and was consistent with the eosinophilic inflammation observed on prior colon biopsies. However, eosinophilia can fluctuate in patients with chronic strongyloidiasis infection and may even be absent in more severe disease states like hyperinfection syndrome.5

Endoscopic findings associated with strongyloidiasis are often variable and nonspecific. Gross and microscopic evaluation may show evidence of mucosal erythema, erosions, duodenitis, and villous atrophy.6 Typically, definitive diagnosis requires histopathologic identification of larvae within the intestinal crypts or mucosa.4 In this case, the diagnosis was established through duodenal biopsy demonstrating parasitic structures within the crypts in the setting of eosinophilic inflammation. This highlights the importance of obtaining tissue samples when evaluating unexplained mucosal abnormalities, especially in patients with risk factors for parasitic infection.

This case underscores the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis in patients presenting with persistent GI symptoms and unexplained eosinophilia. In patients with recent travel or immigration from endemic regions, parasitic infections should remain an important consideration. Rapid recognition and tissue diagnosis may help prevent delays in care and reduce the risk of more severe complications.
