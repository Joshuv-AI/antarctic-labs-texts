Comparing postoperative reading performance and visual outcomes of a non-diffractive EDOF-IOL versus a monofocal IOL: a randomized controlled eye movement study
Authors: Sahand Amir-Asgari, Stefan Georgiev, Manuel Ruiss, Sotiris Plainis, Caroline Pilwachs, Oliver Findl
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo compare oculomotor behavior and reading performance along with conventional visual outcomes in patients following implantation of an enhanced depth of focus (EDOF) intraocular lens (IOL) versus a monofocal IOL.MethodsIn this prospective, exploratory, randomized, clinical trial patients underwent either bilateral implantation with a non-diffractive EDOF IOL DFT015 (Acrysof IQ Vivity, Alcon, USA) or an aspheric monofocal IOL SN60WF (Acrysof IQ, Alcon, USA). 106 eyes of 53 patients with bilateral age-related cataract were evaluated (EDOF IOL group: n = 25; Monofocal IOL group n = 28). At 3 months after surgery, along with visual acuity at various distances, halometry, and patient-reported quality of vision, silent reading performance and oculomotor behavior were assessed at 66 cm with an infrared eye tracker under photopic and mesopic light levels. Data analysis included computation of reading speed and a range of oculomotor indices.ResultsMedian mesopic silent reading speed in words per minute (wpm) and fixation duration in milliseconds (ms) were 205 wpm and 277 ms for the EDOF group and 168 wpm (p = 0.04) and 301 ms (p = 0.02) for the monofocal control IOL group, respectively. No significant differences were observed under photopic conditions. Binocular means expressed in logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution for postoperative corrected distance, uncorrected distance, uncorrected intermediate, and uncorrected near visual acuities were − 0.04 ± 0.07, 0.00 ± 0.07, 0.12 ± 0.09, and 0.27 ± 0.13 for the EDOF group and − 0.08 ± 0.06 (p = 0.08), -0.04 ± 0.07 (p = 0.10), 0.19 ± 0.13 (p = 0.01), and 0.41 ± 0.14 (p = < 0.001) for the control group, respectively. Postoperative halo size and visual disturbances were similar in both groups.ConclusionsSilent reading speed at 66 cm distance is found improved with EDOF compared to monofocal IOLs only at mesopic light levels, mainly due to the improvement in average fixation duration. Further studies are needed to confirm these preliminary findings.Trial registrationJan 4, 2022: NCT05194657 (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05194657).Keywords: Eye-movements, Reading, Cataract surgery, Extended depth of focus IOL, Aspheric monofocal IOL

Background

Improvements in cataract surgery have coincided with a plethora of recently introduced intraocular lens (IOL) designs to compensate for presbyopia [1, 2]. They have become a mainstay in a field with rising patient expectations for postoperative functional vision at all distances. Presbyopia correcting IOLs can be broadly categorized into the standard multifocal IOLs and the more recently developed extended depth of focus (EDOF) IOLs [2]. The former can be further subdivided into refractive concentric zonal optics, rotationally asymmetric segmented refractive designs, or refractive-diffractive designs. In multifocal designs, patients´ postoperative neuroadaptation seems to be of importance, since they demand simultaneous-image processing, i.e. superposition of near, intermediate and far distance images. While these IOLs exhibit high success rates, a non-negligible proportion of patients have shown to experience intolerable postoperative side effects such as photic phenomena and/or loss in contrast sensitivity [3].

Refractive EDOF IOLs enhance depth of focus by incorporating aspheric surfaces with aperiodic power profiles that mostly differ in the amount and polarity of spherical aberration [4]. EDOF designs are expected to reduce the incidence of visual disturbances, photic phenomena (such as halos and glare) and image contrast deterioration [5, 6], while exhibiting adequate visual acuity at intermediate distances.

While conventional visual acuity forms a rapid measure of visual capacity at various distances, utilised as clinical endpoint in IOL-based studies, it is of limited value as a measure for evaluating functional vision deficits. To address the challenges that patients face while performing real-life visual tasks, it is essential to go beyond parametrizing their high-contrast visual acuity. Reading efficiency has been established as a strong predictor of vision-related quality of life and occupational attainment, whereas reading difficulty is a frequent complaint among individuals experiencing diminished digital performance at work [7]. This is not surprising, since continuous reading in and of itself is one of the main goals when performing everyday reading tasks on a digital screen (i.e., computer screens, tablets, or smartphones) [8].

At present, sentence-level reading acuity tests or standardized passages with continuous text of fixed sizes are used to quantify reading efficiency in outcome-based clinical research [9, 10]. Nonetheless, reading is influenced by non-visual processes, including language processing capacity, learning skills, or cognitive disposition [11]. Moreover, conventional reading tests require to read the sentences/texts aloud and as fast as possible. This method, however, fails to simulate natural silent reading, which necessitates word recognition and sentence comprehension, and is essential to various daily reading tasks [12, 13].

It is well established that reading relies on efficient oculomotor behavior to execute the sequence of eye movements for effective processing. Since information is acquired during fixations, a characteristic reading signature emerges from the number of fixations (between saccades), the regressions (backward saccades), and fixation duration [11]. Based on this evidence, recent studies have been using eye movement data as a surrogate for silent reading performance, showing promising results in presbyopia correction with multifocal contact lenses [14, 15]. Patients´ reading patterns via eye-movement analysis appear to correlate with reading speed as well as overall reading performance and can be differentially used to discriminate between cognitive disposition and visuo-motor processing [11, 16, 17].

The present study extends the applicability of this novel method and its validation in EDOF IOL recipients. Therefore, the purpose of this pilot study is to assess reading speed and eye-movement based oculomotor behavior, along with conventional visual outcomes in pseudophakic patients implanted with a recently introduced commercial EDOF IOL (Acrysof IQ Vivity, Alcon Laboratories, Fort Worth, Texas, USA) compared to those implanted with its monofocal aspheric counterpart (SN60WF Acrysof IQ) [18–21].

Materials and methods

Study design

The study (NCT05194657) was conducted as a pilot, randomized, controlled, patient- and examiner-masked, single-center trial between January 2022 and September 2023 in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki, the CONSORT guidelines, and the Good Clinical Practice guidelines of the European Union. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the city of Vienna, Austria (EC number: EK-21-231-1021).

Prior to any study-related measurements, written informed consent was obtained from every patient. Inclusion criteria were age-related bilateral cataract, regular corneal astigmatism of up to 3.0 diopters (D) as measured via corneal topography/tomography, and normal findings in the medical history and physical examination. Patients had to show availability, willingness, and sufficient cognitive awareness to comply with examination procedures, as well as be native German speakers with unimpaired reading ability. Exclusion criteria were relevant other ophthalmic diseases such as pseudoexfoliation syndrome, glaucoma, macular diseases, traumatic cataract, corneal scars, any other co-morbidities that could affect visual outcomes, as well as neurological or psychiatric disorders that could affect their reading performance.

Intraocular lenses

The single piece EDOF IOL (DFT015 Acrysof IQ Vivity, Alcon Laboratories, Fort Worth, Texas, USA) consists of a hydrophobic acrylate/methacrylate copolymer, including UV and blue light filters, a refractive index of 1.55 at 35 °C, and an optical diameter of 6-mm. Available spherical powers are from + 10.0 to + 30.0 D in 0.5 D increments, with the toric version providing an astigmatism correction up to 3.75 D. The IOL features a biconvex optical design with two transitional elements with a wavefront-modulating elevation of 1 μm in the central 2.2 mm area to modulate primary and secondary spherical aberrations and enhance intermediate vision.

The aspheric monofocal IOL (SN60WF Acrysof IQ, Alcon Laboratories, Fort Worth, Texas, USA) served as control and has the identical hydrophobic acrylic single piece platform—along with UV and blue light filters, an optical diameter of 6-mm, and the same refractive index at 35°. Its posterior aspheric surface is intended to induce a spherical aberration of − 0.17 μm (6 mm pupil size) to partly compensate for the naturally present positive corneal spherical aberration based on mean population findings. Available spherical and toric powers are from + 6.00 to + 30.00 D in 0.50 D increments and from 1.00 to 6.00 D in 0.75 D increments, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Since there is no data from previous comparative studies on pseudophakic eye-movement analysis using automated eye-tracking, the sample size could only be estimated. Recent clinical results of oculomotor indices using an eye-tracker have shown that a sample size of 30 participants per group was sufficient to delineate statistically significant differences between subjects using multifocal- and single vision contact lenses, respectively [14]. On that ground, under the assumption of a 10% drop-out rate, 34 patients per group were deemed to be suitable for this prospective pilot trial to comparatively analyse oculomotor indices following IOL implantation. This should provide a foundation for future studies with larger sample sizes to confirm these findings and further investigate clinical implications. A p-value of < 0.05 being considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were computed using IBM SPSS Statistics for MAC (software version 27.0, IBM Corp.). The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, and parametric statistics were only employed in case of normally distributed parameters. In that case comparative tests were performed with the unpaired t-test; in case of non-normally distributed data the Mann Whitney-U test was employed.

Preoperative evaluation

A non-masked examiner, not involved in any masked study procedures, generated a randomization list using a 1:1 block randomization (http:/www.randomizer.org). Accordingly, patients were allocated into two groups. The study and control group were to receive bilaterally the EDOF IOL DFT015 or its aspheric monofocal SN60WF counterpart, respectively.

Baseline pre-operative measurements 1 week prior to surgery consisted of a full ophthalmic assessment, including subjective manifest refraction, autorefraction (RM 8800, Topcon Corp.), and optical biometry (IOLMaster 700, Carl Zeiss Meditec AG, Germany). Additionally, corneal tomography via Placido-combined Scheimpflug- or spectral domain optical coherence tomography (Sirius/MS39, CSO, Firenze, Italy) were employed to determine the amount and regularity of corneal astigmatism. IOL power calculations were performed with the IOLMaster 700 using the Barrett TK Universal II formula targeting for 0 D. In eyes with total corneal astigmatism > 1.0 D, the toric version of each of the IOLs was chosen to achieve a residual postoperative astigmatism of < 0.5 D.

Surgical procedure

Three experienced surgeons performed uneventful cataract surgery under topical anesthesia with 2.2 mm temporal incisions, capsulorhexis, phacoemulsification, and intracapsular IOL insertion. The surgery of the second eye was performed 1 to 2 weeks after the first eye. Routine postoperative procedures remained unaltered, including Bromfenac drops twice a day for 4 weeks and lubrication ointment 1–3 times a day.

Postoperative reading performance

Three months after cataract surgery, reading speed was initially assessed via the validated Salzburg Reading Desk (SRD Vision LLC, USA, Version 1.0) [22]. Hereby, binocular reading speed at patients´ preferred intermediate distance was first analyzed to provide comparative data to previous investigations [23]. The device displays continuous International Reading Speed Texts (IReST) German paragraphs with logarithmically scaled Colenbrander sentences on a high-resolution monitor [24]. For those measurements, chart illuminance and contrast were set to 100%. The patients read aloud, and the duration of reading was measured via a microphone that employs voice recording to quantify reading speed in words per minute (wpm). Reading speed at the preferred intermediate distance in wpm was then calculated for each text-paragraph by dividing the total number of words read by total reading time.

Subsequently, silent reading performance was evaluated binocularly at a viewing distance of 66 cm during silent passage reading. Two high contrast passages (equivalent to the highest Pelli-Robson contrast value) were used, with short breaks in-between measurements to avoid fatigue effects. Passages were displayed on a high-resolution monitor at two luminance levels: photopic (55 cd/m2) and mesopic (~ 2cd/m2), achieved with an additional neutral density filter. Eye movements from both eyes were recorded simultaneously during passage reading, at a sampling rate of 1000 Hz, using an automated eye-tracker (EyeLink 1000, SR Research Ltd, Canada). The device has been described previously in detail and consists of a high-speed infrared camera that continuously records fixations via pupil/corneal tracking [14]. Measurements were conducted with subjects seated comfortably on a chair, with their head being positioned on a chin rest to mitigate head movements. Prior to each recording, a standard 5-point calibration / validation procedure was conducted by presenting a small dot (0.3 deg) on the screen at 0 deg and at 10 deg vertically and horizontally.

During recording, reading material comprised of two IReST passages (about 140 words each), were employed. Patients were instructed to read the paragraphs at their own pace, with brief intermissions in-between to reduce fatigue effects.

Eye position data reports were initially exported from EyeLink Data Viewer (EyeLink II, SR Research Ltd, Canada) and pre-processed using custom written scripts in Matlab (R2013b). Only data from the right eye were analyzed. Silent reading performance was then assessed based on (a) measures of silent reading speed (in wpm) and (b) a set of eye-fixation based parameters during passage reading: fixation duration (in ms), number of forward fixations per word (fpw), and regressions (percentage of backward over total saccades).

Reading speed was calculated based on the number of read words divided by the total reading time measured from the first until the last fixation made on the passage area. Analysis of eye movement data included fixations with duration between 75 and 1000 ms. Fixations with duration shorter than 75 ms were merged with neighboring fixations if the latter were located within an area of 1°, in line with previous research on fixation duration during reading [25].

Postoperative visual outcomes

At the 3-month timepoint, conventional clinical parameters were assessed including binocular and monocular outcomes for uncorrected (UDVA) and corrected (CDVA) distance visual acuities at 4 m (infinity adjusted with − 0.25 D), uncorrected (UIVA) and corrected (CIVA) at 66 cm, and uncorrected (UNVA) and corrected near (CNVA) visual acuities at 40 cm expressed in logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (logMAR) via ETDRS charts (Precision Vision) [26]. In case of toric IOL implantation, postoperative rotational stability was ascertained via retroillumination photographs. Additional secondary outcomes included monocular quantification of halos via the Aston Halometer [27], along with the Rasch-tested and linearly scaled self-reported quality of vision (QoV) outcomes [28].

Silent reading speed, as calculated with the eyetracker, was found similar between the two groups under photopic light conditions (EDOF 243 ± 72 wpm; monofocal 235 ± 62 wpm, p = 0.34) as seen in Table 3; Fig. 1. However, under mesopic conditions, reading speed was statistically significantly faster in the EDOF IOL group (EDOF 205 ± 75 wpm; monofocal 168 ± 60 wpm; p = 0.04). Regarding the eye fixation parameters, statistically significant differences between the EDOF (273 ± 54 ms) and monofocal (318 ± 76 ms) IOL groups were also found at mesopic light levels (p = 0.02) (see Table 3; Fig. 1). Fixation duration was also faster at photopic levels, but the difference did not reach statistically significant difference, probably due the high inter-subject variability. Otherwise, the differences in other eye fixation parameters, i.e. number of fixations and regression, was not statistically significantly different (p > 0.05).

Discussion

The primary determinant in selecting a presbyopia-correcting IOL is the patient’s functional vision across various distances and the trade-off with potential postoperative photic phenomena. This consideration has become increasingly important due to the proliferation of new multifocal and EDOF IOLs, which have significantly raised patient expectations for spectacle-independent vision [2]. However, conventional metrics such as high-contrast visual acuity, though important, may not fully capture the challenges patients face during real-life activities, particularly reading.

The spherical aberration modulating EDOF IOL Acrysof IQ Vivity DFT015 has been recently clinically evaluated in previous studies [18–21]. The current study expands on this by assessing silent reading performance and oculomotor behavior using standardized continuous text at intermediate distance, alongside clinical visual outcomes.

Regarding standard clinical measures, both patient groups demonstrated excellent refractive predictability and very good photopic binocular distance acuity (Table 2). While the monofocal group had slightly better uncorrected distance vision on average, the EDOF group showed significantly improved uncorrected intermediate and near visual acuity. These results align with prior studies of the DFT015 IOL [18–21]. In McCabe et al.’s US registration trial (n = 435 eyes), similar outcomes were observed: mean binocular UDVA was 0.03 ± 0.10 logMAR for DFT015 and − 0.02 ± 0.10 for SN60WF; UIVA was 0.06 ± 0.08 vs. 0.14 ± 0.12 logMAR; and UNVA was 0.21 ± 0.10 vs. 0.34 ± 0.15 logMAR, respectively [18]. Furthermore, our QOV disturbance profiles closely mirrored those from the mentioned authors, though with a slightly lower proportion of DFT015 recipients reporting no blur (92%, DFT015; 80% SN60WF, McCabe et al. [18]; n = 13, 52%, DFT015; n = 18, 64%, SN60WF; present trial). Our QOV results, however, did also differ to those reported by Sheepers et al. in a recent prospective randomized study in 138 eyes [29]: their reported complete absence of starbursts (88%) and glare (88%) frequency for this EDOF IOL was higher than in our study (starburst: n = 15, 60%; glare: n = 12, 48%). Still, visual disturbance frequencies were highly comparable between IOL types. This was supported by quantitative monocular halometry: except for a slightly greater inferior halo extension with DFT015, both IOLs showed nearly identical halo profiles (Fig. 3). This is noteworthy, given that the DFT015 meets ANSI Z80.35-2018 criteria for EDOF classification—unlike many newer “advanced monofocal” lenses [18, 30]. We hypothesize that variations in patient-reported outcomes across studies may be due to differences in questionnaire methodology, reinforcing the need for standardized protocols in future benchmarking.

Our study introduced novel outcome metrics by using high-resolution eye-tracking to assess silent reading performance [12]. The simultaneous recording of eye movements during passage reading, allows for a sophisticated evaluation of visuo-motor behavior, using a range of oculomotor indices, in addition to the silent reading speed.

Our study findings indicate that differences in both reading speed and oculomotor behavior (i.e. fixation duration) between the EDOF and monofocal IOL group may be more noticeable under mesopic compared to photopic lighting conditions. Although most reading occurs in photopic conditions, this suggests the EDOF IOL may enhance reading efficiency in real-world, suboptimal lighting—possibly due to larger pupil sizes increasing the perception of blur at mesopic levels [4].

It is evident that a high inter-subject variability in silent reading speed is present, due to its comprehension component (see Table 3). Indeed, reading is differentially affected by low-level optical (pre-retinal) processes as well as higher-level cognitive processes [15, 25]. Nevertheless, despite this inter-subject variability in reading speed, fixation duration remained a reliable differentiator between groups under mesopic conditions. This metric, which is primarily influenced by optical factors, has previously shown a strong correlation with reading speed in presbyopic correction using multifocal contact lenses [14, 15, 25]. In contrast, no significant differences were observed between the two groups in the “number of fixations” and “percentage of regressions”—parameters that are influenced by cognitive processes during reading, such as comprehension level, cognitive disposition, and psychological traits [25]. These findings suggest that fixation duration may be a promising eye movement parameter for quantifying reading speed differences across various IOL types.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the targeted sample size of 60 patients was not achieved due to losses at the 3-month follow-up. Larger, adequately powered studies with sample sizes specifically calculated for eye-fixation indices are needed to confirm whether some of the observed non-significant differences are genuine. Second, postoperative wavefront aberration and pupil size analysis was not performed, despite the known inter-subject variability in ocular aberrations [31] and their influence on DOF and contrast sensitivity in refractive gradient EDOF IOLs [32]. Potential confounding factors in the monofocal IOL group may include residual DOF, given that (a) its corneal spherical aberration compensation is only partial, and (b) pupil size significantly affects pseudoaccommodation. This could explain the wide variability in binocular UIVA, which acted as a confounding factor (EDOF: − 0.10 to 0.30 logMAR; monofocal: 0.00 to 0.50 logMAR). Third, reading performance was assessed using the Salzburg Reading Desk with IReST passages in 1.25 M font size at each patient’s preferred reading distance. This approach reflects real-world conditions, such as reading newspapers or paperbacks, and aligns with previous studies: van den Berg et al. reported similar intermediate reading speeds (154 ± 50 wpm) using a mix-and-match EDOF/multifocal IOL strategy [23]. While testing at a fixed distance might have better highlighted differences between groups, it would not have accurately represented typical, everyday reading behavior. Consequently, no direct correlation could be established between Salzburg Reading Desk performance and oculomotor parameters in this study.

Future studies should include larger samples and a variety of IOL types to better elucidate relationships among optical properties, acuity, and eye movement behavior across different distances and lighting conditions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the current study indicates that EDOF IOL offer improved reading speed and performance at intermediate distance under low light conditions, although further research is needed to confirm these benefits across diverse real-world settings. Eye fixation (oculomotor) indices may provide an additional outcome measure to reading speed for functional vision recovery following IOL replacement surgery.
