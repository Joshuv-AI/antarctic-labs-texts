Spinel ferrites and their composites photocatalysts for CO2 conversion to solar fuels: A review
Authors: Ruoqiao Zhou, Haoyu Wang, Yuefa Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySpinel ferrite (MFe2O4, M= Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, Zn, Sn, Mn, Ca, and Mg) nanomaterials have attracted enormous attention for CO2 photoreduction due to their superparamagnetic properties, narrow band gap, and suitable band position. However, a review about MFe2O4 and its composites for CO2 photoconversion is still absent. In this study, the conventional preparation processes of MFe2O4 are summarized, and the recent advances in the modification of MFe2O4 catalysts for efficient CO2 photoreduction are first discussed systematically with respect to microstructure regulation, defect introduction, and MFe2O4-based composite construction. Additionally, the enhancement mechanisms, including CO2 adsorption, light harvesting, and promotion of charge separation, are analyzed in depth. Finally, the major challenges and possible perspectives for the development of MFe2O4-based heterojunctions are proposed, hoping to provide insight for designing highly efficient and selective MFe2O4-based photocatalysts for CO2 photoconversion.

Introduction

In recent years, thermochemical and electrochemical reduction strategies have been developed to convert CO2 into high-added-value chemicals.1,2 However, because of the high thermodynamic stability of CO2, combustion of a large volume of fossil fuels is usually involved in the thermochemical and electrochemical reduction process, which simultaneously generates new CO2.2 Therefore, it is desired to explore the CO2 conversion strategy with lower energy consumption and CO2 emissions. Photoconversion of CO2 to solar fuels has attracted widespread attention as an efficient route to approach this challenge.3,4,5,6,7 This technology can directly make use of the renewable and inexhaustible solar energy to convert CO2 into reusable C1(CO, CH4, CH3OH, HCOOH) or C2+(C2H5OH, CH3COOH, CH3CHO, C2H4, C2H6, C3H8, C3H7OH, and so on) products at ambient conditions. More importantly, there would be zero carbon emissions during the process, so the conversion of CO2 driven by solar light energy is an effective way to simultaneously protect the environment and supply energy.8

Owing to their strong reduction abilities TiO2,9 graphitic carbon nitride(g-C3N4),10 ZnS,11 and SrTiO312 have been extensively investigated for solar CO2 conversion. Unfortunately, these examined materials displayed very low CO2 photoreduction efficiencies due to their large band gap (more than 3.2 eV). Spinel ferrite (MFe2O4) materials have received considerable attention in expanding the absorption range to visible light due to their relatively narrow bandgap (0.10–2.51 eV).13 Other advantages of MFe2O4 and MFe2O4-based photocatalysts include room-temperature ferromagnetism and appropriate band position for redox reactions. As a result, increasing studies on MFe2O4-based photocatalysts for CO2 photoconversion have been reported in the past ten years.14,15,16,17 It is thus necessary to compare and review the published results for a better understanding of MFe2O4-based photocatalysts to inspire further novel research ideas for highly efficient CO2 photoreduction.

Up to now, there are some inspiring reviews on MFe2O4 and its composites in photocatalytic removal of contaminants and hydrogen generation.13,18,19,20 The number of publications and citation counts related to spinel ferrite-based photocatalysts for CO2 conversion in the past decade, as shown in Figure 1. However, there are no review articles regarding the overall introduction of MFe2O4 and its composites for CO2 photoreduction. In this review, we have systematically summarized the representative works from the literature regarding MFe2O4 and their composites photocatalysts for CO2 conversion to solar fuels. The study begins with the fundamentals of CO2 photoreduction, followed by an in-depth discussion on several well-known MFe2O4 photocatalysts and their synthesis methods. Next, the relationships between synthesis methods, morphology, band structure, and the photocatalytic performance of MFe2O4 and their composite photocatalysts for CO2 conversion will be systematically emphasized. In addition, the mechanism of photocatalytic CO2 conversion, stability improvement, and possible reaction routes on different MFe2O4 photocatalysts will be presented. Basic principles of CO2 photoreduction

In general, H2O can be used as the hydrogen source for the photocatalytic CO2 conversion into renewable solar fuels.6 Photoconversion of CO2 on a photocatalyst surface includes the following steps: 1) photogeneration of electron and hole (e−-h+) pairs on the surface of the photocatalyst due to photoexcitation; 2) efficient separation of the photogenerated e−-h+ pairs due to charge migration; 3) the photogenerated e− react with CO2 and hydrogen protons to yield solar fuels; 4) the photogenerated h+ react with H2O to produce O2 and hydrogen protons. Actually, one e− reduction of CO2 is difficult to proceed due to the highly negative redox potential of CO2/CO2·-(-1.90 V vs. normal hydrogen electrode (NHE), pH = 7.00).21

Instead, the photocatalytic CO2 conversion can be achieved through the proton-assisted multielectron reduction steps due to their relatively lower redox potential (vs. NHE, at pH =7.00). Crystal structures and synthesis methods of MFe2O4 photocatalysts

Crystal structure of MFe2O4 photocatalysts

The general molecular formula of MFe2O4 is {(A+2)[B+32]O−24}, where A+2 and B+3 are the divalent and trivalent atoms occupying the tetrahedral and octahedral interstitial sites of the face-centered cubic lattice formed by the oxygen ions.22 The properties of MFe2O4, especially the magnetic property, depend strongly on the distribution of the cations among the tetrahedral and octahedral sites.23 Due to their diverse magnetic and electrical properties, MFe2O4 has been studied for various applications including magnetic recording,24 microwave devices,25 and biomedical materials.26

Recently, MFe2O4 such as CaFe2O4, CoFe2O4, CuFe2O4, Fe3O4, MgFe2O4, MnFe2O4, NiFe2O4, SnFe2O4, and ZnFe2O4 have been reported for their effectiveness in removing organic pollutants from wastewater.13,27,28,29 The relatively narrow band gap and superparamagnetic property enable MFe2O4 nanomaterials for visible light-assisted photocatalyst application with easy magnetic retrieval.30 The additional advantage of MFe2O4 is that the Fe ions can react with the H2O2 molecules through Fenton processes to produce HO· and HO2· radicals, which are the most important active radicals in the oxidative removal of organic pollutants.31,32 However, the application of MFe2O4 in photocatalytic reduction of CO2 was not explored until 2011.33

Synthesis methods of MFe2O4 photocatalysts

The photocatalytic activity of MFe2O4 can be significantly influenced by the particle size, crystallinity, morphology, and surface area.13,28 A higher surface area might increase the light absorption and carrier collection, and hence enhance the performance in degradation of pollutants.34 MFe2O4 materials with different morphologies may have different band structures, which can directly affect the energy band positions.35 Various techniques of preparation, such as hydrothermal/solvothermal, sol-gel, co-precipitation, solid-state reaction, template, and microwave-assisted methods have been widely applied to improve the crystallinity and control the morphology of MFe2O4 materials.13,27,29

Hydrothermal/solvothermal method

Hydrothermal/solvothermal method is the most widely used technique for synthesis of MFe2O4 photocatalyst.36 The crystal facet, size, surface defects and morphology of MFe2O4 photocatalyst can be regulated by adjusting the pH value, solvent type, reaction time and temperature.30 Recently, our group synthesized SnFe2O4 nanoparticles through solvothermal (ST) method for the first time, which displayed superior photo-Fenton catalytic performance than SnFe2O4 nanoparticles prepared by coprecipitation (PE) method and pure anatase TiO2 (Figures 2A and 2B).31 In addition, the morphology and size of the MFe2O4 nanoparticles can be effectively regulated by adjusting the pH value during the hydrothermal/solvothermal reaction. For example, SnFe2O4 reported by Zhang’s group shows a clear evolution from nanocubes to nanodisks with different concentration of NaOH solution.35 Wang et al. synthesized NiFe2O4 nanospheres by a hydrothermal process with sizes of 7nm and 165nm when the NaOH concentration was 10mmol and 30mmol, respectively.38 Moreover, the physical and chemical properties of MFe2O4 nanomaterials obtained through solvothermal and hydrothermal methods are different. Kurian et al. reported that CuFe2O4, MgFe2O4, and ZnFe2O4 cubic particles were obtained by the hydrothermal method (Figures 2C, 2E, and 2G), whereas porous spheres composed of 5–20 nm microcrystals were obtained by the solvothermal method (Figures 2D, 2F, and 2H).37Figure 2Structure and catalytic performance of SnFe2O4 prepared by different methods(A) XRD patterns of SnFe2O4 nanoparticles synthesized by the solvothermal (ST) and the precipitation exchange (PE) methods.(B) Ct/C0 versus time curves for chlortetracycline (C0 = 50 mg L−1) solution containing SnFe2O4 (ST), SnFe2O4 (PE), TiO2, SnFe2O4 (ST)/9Mm H2O2, SnFe2O4(PE)/9Mm H2O2. Sol-gel method

MFe2O4 nanoparticles synthesized through sol-gel method usually have good crystallinity and uniform size distribution due to the high purity of raw materials and the ease of solvent removability during the treatment process.39 In addition, the calcination temperature of sol-gel method is lower than the solid reaction method.40 For example, M. Parishani et al. used such a method to prepare NiFe2O4 nanoparticles, in which NiFe2O4 particles of 42, 96, and 315 nm were synthesized at when the calcination temperatures of were 750, 850°C and 950°C, the corresponding NiFe2O4 particles were with sizes of 42, 96, and 315 nm, respectively.41 Yang et al. synthesized tetragonal phase CuFe2O4 (80nm) nanomaterials using citric acid assisted sol-gel method, which displayed a higher photocatalytic hydrogen production rate (1.72 mmol/g/h) than that of CuFe2O4 prepared by solid reaction and coprecipitation method.42

Furthermore, Zhong and co-workers prepared 10-50nm-sized ZnFe2O4 spherical particles by the sol-gel method and used them for levofloxacin degradation (Figures 3A and 3B). As displayed in Figure 3C, the defluorination rate of the ZFO-500/LED/PMS system (sintering temperatures 500°C: ZFO-500) is much higher than that of ZFO-600 and ZFO-700.43 Importantly, it is believed that combining LED with ZFO-500 had a synergistic effect on PMS activation for levofloxacin degradation (Figure 3D). Shi’s group developed CoFe2O4 powders and films by the sol-gel method.44 The obtained CoFe2O4 films with nano-scaled film thicknesses (49nm) possessed better magnetic properties than the CoFe2O4 powders, which was attributed to the relatively small grain sizes of CoFe2O4 films.Figure 3Structure and degradation performance of ZFO catalysts(A) X-ray diffraction patterns of the resulting catalysts.(B) Scanning electron microscopy image of ZFO-500.(C) Effect of calcination temperature on the degradation of levofloxacin.(D) Levofloxacin degradation efficiencies under various systems (LED power: 5W; ILED = 1.0 A; C0(levofloxacin): 10 mg/L; CPMS: 1 mM; dosage: 1 g/L).Reprinted with permission from ref.43 Copyright 2021, Elsevier.

Coprecipitation method

Coprecipitation is a simple route for MFe2O4 preparation, and this method is also suitable for large-scale production.45 Liu et al. applied the coprecipitation method to the synthesis of SnFe2O4 particles with an average grain size of 4nm.46 Zhou et al. compared the properties of SnFe2O4 nanoparticles prepared using coprecipitation and solvothermal methods. It has been reported that the coprecipitation SnFe2O4 nanoparticles showed easy aggregation into large clusters, whereas the hydrothermally prepared SnFe2O4 particles exhibited much smaller size and better dispersion.47 In addition, it is possible to achieve morphology control of MFe2O4 nanoparticles by using the coprecipitation approach. Choi’s group synthesized hierarchically structured Fe3O4 (HS-Fe3O4) nanoparticles through a chemical precipitation process using FeCl3 and FeCl2 as the starting materials (Figures 4A–4C).48 The as-obtained HS-Fe3O4 nanoparticles could be used for magnetorheological fluids. Patil and co-workers fabricated NiFe2O4 nanoparticles with an average size of 35 nm by a co-precipitation technique using cetrimide as a surfactant.49 The NiFe2O4 nanoparticles displayed a specific capacity (1103 mA h g−1) much higher than their theoretical capacity (915 mA h/g). They further demonstrated that NiFe2O4 nanoparticles exhibited good catalytic performance for the photodegradation of a mixed solution of methyl orange and Evans blue at pH = 5. Coprecipitation-prepared CoFe2O4 nanoparticles have also been reported for high-frequency applications.50 Overall, the size and morphology control of MFe2O4 nanoparticles using the coprecipitation process is a major challenge, although this synthesis technique is relatively easy.Figure 4Morphologies of HS-Fe3O4 particles(A) SEM and (B and C) TEM images of HS-Fe3O4 particles.Reprinted with permission from ref.48 Copyright 2020, American Chemical Society.

Template method

Among the various synthesis method, the template method is an effective strategy for the controlled synthesis of MFe2O4 nanoparticles with different morphologies.51 In the earlier reports, highly ordered ZnFe2O4 nanotube arrays were successfully prepared by adding porous anodic alumina templates in the sol-gel solution (Figure 5A).52 Compared to ZnFe2O4 nano-powders, the ZnFe2O4 nanotube arrays exhibited a blue shift of about 150 nm of photovoltaic response threshold (Figures 5B–5D), indicating that the photogenerated charges in the nanotubes have higher redox activity than the nano powders. Recently, Zhou and co-workers developed a 3D ZnFe2O4 nanoflower (3D-ZFO) using 3D ZnO nanoflower as a template.53 It was found that the resulting 3D-ZFO possessed an enlarged specific surface area, a large pore volume, and strong microwave absorption ability. The sedimentary rock-like Fe3O4 particles (SR-Fe3O4) were created when mesoporous silica derived from rice husk was employed as a template.54 It is shown that SR-Fe3O4 exhibited a superior As3+ adsorption capacity (1002.03 mg/g) than that of the previously reported adsorbents. Microwave-assisted method

The microwave-assisted method is mainly used together with solvothermal, hydrothermal, combustion, ball-milling, and co-precipitation synthesis.56 In comparison with the conventional synthetic methodologies, the microwave-assisted method can rapidly achieve high temperature and high pressure, and significantly shorten the preparation time. Andrade et al. demonstrated a microwave-assisted combustion approach to synthesize MnFe2O4 nanoparticulates with a size of 7-40nm.60 Such fabricated MnFe2O4 nanoparticles were explored for malachite green degradation, which exhibited 99% removal efficiency. In addition, the energy of the valence band (VB) edge position should provide enough energy for water photo-oxidation (+0.82 V vs. NHE) to generate protons.73 The hydrogen protons are essential for CO2 conversion because they can react with CO2 and e− to yield CH4 and C2+ products.3 Therefore, MgFe2O469 and CaFe2O470 satisfy these requirements and can be directly used as photocatalysts for CO2 photoreduction. NiFe2O4,65 CdFe2O4,66 CoFe2O4,67 and MnFe2O468 possess CB levels with too low energy to convert CO2 by the photoinduced e− under light irradiation, but these materials can serve as good H2O oxidation agents owing to their strong oxidation abilities. However, even though the CB edges in CuFe2O4, ZnFe2O4, and SnFe2O4 are negative enough to provide the reduction energy, their photocatalytic performances are hampered by serious surface charge recombination and their VB potentials, which are not enough for H2O oxidation.29,71,72 To be used as effective photocatalysts, these MFe2O4 should be integrated with other semiconductors to form Z-scheme systems, which not only promote H2O oxidation to release hydrogen protons but also improve the photogenerated e−-h+ separation. Additionally, it is obvious that Fe3O4 cannot induce photocatalytic CO2 reduction because neither its conduction band nor valence band can drive the redox reactions.64 The reported results regarding individual ferrite materials are discussed in the following session.

Fe3O4-based heterojunctions

When used in photocatalytic applications, Fe3O4 nanoparticles mainly work as nanomagnets to enable the easy magnetic recycling of photocatalysts. Shi and co-workers prepared a magnetically recyclable TiO2@SiO2@Fe3O4 catalyst by a sol-gel approach for the photochemical reduction of CO2.74 Similar studies include the development of magnetic Fe3O4@N-C/Cu2O nanostructures for the photoconversion of CO2 to CH3OH.75

Kumar et al. prepared magnetically separable rGO@CuZnO@Fe3O4 microspheres, which were aimed at the photocatalytic CO2 conversion into CH3OH under visible light irradiation (Figure 8A).76 In this case, Fe3O4 microspheres acted as the magnetic cores to endow good recyclability of rGO@CuZnO@Fe3O4 (Figure 8B). The authors also proposed that the Fe3O4 microspheres can provide photogenerated electrons for CO2 conversion (Figure 8C). Wei’s group presented the fabrication of CeO2 micro-flowers decorated with Fe3O4 quantum dots (QDs), which showed higher CO and CH4 formation rates than CeO2 micro-flowers. (Ni, Co)Fe2O4-based heterojunctions

Both NiFe2O4 and CoFe2O4 have been widely used as catalysts for CO2 photoreduction. Zhang et al. fabricated the NiFe2O4@N/C/SnO2 nano-photocatalysts through annealing FeNi-MOF with SnO2 in an N2 atmosphere (Figure 9A).79 Prepared at an optimal calcination temperature of 450°C, the NiFe2O4@N/C/SnO2-450 composite exhibited the best CO2 photoreduction activity (Figure 9B). As shown in Figure 9C, NiFe2O4@N/C/SnO2-450 yielded a stronger photocurrent intensity than SnO2, which indicated an improved charge transfer mechanism adjusted by the modification of NiFe2O4. Furthermore, NiFe2O4@N/C/SnO2-450 delivered a high specific surface area of 802.7633 m2/g (1.74 times that of FeNi-MOF), which is beneficial to CO2 adsorption (Figure 9D). Fascinatingly, the carbon-nitrogen layer produced by the pyrolysis of polyvinylpyrrolidone, which plays a very important role in promoting type-II electron transfer between NiFe2O4 and SnO2 (Figure 9E), thus leading to the improved photoactivity of NiFe2O4@N/C/SnO2-450 for CO2 reduction. In 2011, Xue’s group fabricated CoFe2O4 nanoparticles by the inorganic salt sol-gel strategy for CO2.33 A total of 4988.11 μmol/g of HCOOH can be produced over this catalyst after reaction for 5h. Apart from that, Santos et al. reported that the graphene/CoFe2O4 heterojunction showed much improved methanol production rates from CO2 photoreduction than CoFe2O4, graphene/CoFe2O4 (1389.9 μmol/g/h) > CoFe2O4 (1105.7 μmol/g/h).17 Li’s research group constructed Co3O4@CoFe2O4 double-shelled nanoboxes via templating and hydrothermal methods for CO2 photoconversion.80 The optimal Co3O4@CoFe2O4 displayed much higher photocatalytic activity than pristine Co3O4 in the conversion of CO2 to CO. (Ca, Mg, Cu) Fe2O4-based heterojunctions

In 1994, Matsumoto et al. reported highly active photoreduction of CO2 to CH3OH in H2O achieved over p-type CaFe2O4,81 but no further reports on the use of CaFe2O4 for photocatalytic, electrocatalytic, and photo-electrocatalytic reduction of CO2 have been published afterward. Up to 2022, Chang et al. investigated the photoreduction of CO2 on Mg2Fe2O5-decorated Ca2Fe2O5-CaFe2O4 heterojunctions (CMFO).82 The results show that the CMFO displays the best photocatalytic activity and selectivity for the conversion of CO2 to CH4 and CH3CHO. The significant increase in productivity and selectivity of CMFO was probably ascribed to the enhancements of CO2 adsorption and efficacious separation of photogenerated carriers.

Recently, Fu and co-workers used a high-temperature calcination method to synthesize MgFe2O4 with oxygen vacancies (from 650°C to 850°C, Figure 10A).83 The oxygen vacancy concentration on the surface of MgFe2O4 gradually increases with increasing calcination temperature (Figure 10B). As shown in Figure 10C, the Mg-Fe-750 sample possessed the lowest photoluminescence emission peak compared to other samples, suggesting that Mg-Fe-750 has higher charge separation efficiency. Mg-Fe-750 displayed the largest adsorption amount of CO2 (Figure 10D), ascribed to the abundance of oxygen vacancies on the surface of Mg-Fe-750. Consequently, the resulting Mg-Fe-750 displayed the best catalytic performance and stability for photoconversion of CO2 to CO and H2 (24.4 and 34.3 μmol/g/h, Figures 10E and 10F). This work demonstrated that the creation of surface oxygen vacancies in MgFe2O4 could accelerate the carrier migration and facilitate CO2 adsorption. Pristine ZnFe2O4 and ZnFe2O4-based heterojunctions

Among various MFe2O4 photocatalysts, ZnFe2O4 with a narrow band gap of ∼2.0 eV was the most widely employed for photoconversion of CO2 because its CB edge is more negative than that of the redox potentials of CO2 and its reduced products (Table 1; Figure 8). Xiao and co-workers presented the fabrication of ultrafine ZnFe2O4 nanoparticles with an average particle size of ∼10 nm through solvent-thermal method, which showed remarkably enhanced photoactivity on converting CO2 to CH3CHO (57.8 μmol/g/h) and C2H5OH (13.7 μmol/g/h).87 The great enhancement was ascribed to the synergy of high-specific surface area (∼112.9 m2/g), effective visible light harvest (Eg = 2.0 eV), and high CB level (−2.0 eV). Recently, there has been a surge of interest in the development of ZnFe2O4-based heterojunctions such as Type II, Z-scheme, P-N, and S-scheme photocatalysts to realize efficient and selective reduction of CO2. Recent reports on ZnFe2O4-based heterojunctions photocatalysts for CO2 reduction

Type-II ZnFe2O4-based heterojunctions

ZnFe2O4, when used alone as a photocatalyst, suffers from the possibility of charge recombination. Therefore, the CO2 conversion efficiency of ZnFe2O4 is far lower than what is expected. Construction of a heterojunction is a widely used approach to boost the CO2 photoconversion efficiency of single ZnFe2O4. For example, Spilarewicz et al. prepared a ZnFe2O4/TiO2 type-I heterojunction for CO2 photoreduction.88 They found that the ZnFe2O4/TiO2 can produce over 4.7 times higher yields of CO evolution than ZnFe2O4 in 6 h under 150 W xenon lamp illumination. However, the e− and h+ were preferred to accumulate in the same semiconductor for a type-I heterojunction, which is not conducive to effective charge separation.

An efficient way to accelerate CO2 photoreduction is to explore type II ZnFe2O4-based heterojunction. For example, Yan et al. constructed ZnFe2O4/FeP-CTFs heterostructure via in situ growth of ZnFe2O4 nanoparticles on iron porphyrin covalent triazine-based frameworks (FeP-CTFs).16 A distinguished contact interface between ZnFe2O4 and FeP-CTFs was formed (Figures 11A–11C), leading to effective electronic transfer within the heterojunction that has been verified by the higher photocurrent density in the ZnFe2O4/FeP-CTFs photocatalysts compared to pristine ZnFe2O4 and FeP-CTFs (Figure 11D). As shown in Figure 11E, the ZnFe2O4/FeP-CTFs heterostructure exhibited a much higher BET surface area (530.3 m2/g) in comparison to ZnFe2O4(47.9 m2/g) and FeP-CTFs (450.0 m2/g), indicating an outstanding ability of capturing CO2. The resulting ZnFe2O4/FeP-CTFs photocatalysts can reach a CO-production rate of 178 μmol/g/h, which was significantly higher than those achieved on ZnFe2O4 and FeP-CTFs (Figure 11F). It was further revealed that ZnFe2O4 and FeP-CTFs can form a Type II heterojunction due to their suitable band structures (Figure 11G). In such type II heterojunction, the holes are transported from the VB of ZnFe2O4 to the LUMO of the FeP-CTFs, whereas electrons from the LUMO of the FeP-CTFs can be injected into the CB of ZnFe2O4 to reduce CO2.Figure 11Morphology, physicochemical and photocatalytic CO2 reduction properties of ZnFe2O4/FeP-CTFs composites(A–C) (A) SEM and (B and C) TEM images of ZnFe2O4/FeP-CTFs.(D and E) (D) Photocurrent response and (E) the nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherms of the ZnFe2O4, FeP-CTFs, and ZnFe2O4/FeP-CTFs.(F) Generation rate of CO for different samples.(G) The proposed mechanism of the reduction of CO2 into CO photocatalyzed by ZnFe2O4/FeP-CTFs.Reprinted with permission from ref.16 Copyright 2021, Elsevier.

Liu et al. prepared surface oxygen vacancy-enriched ZnFe2O4@TiO2 hollow nanospheres (ZnFe2O4@H-TiO2-x) for CO2 photoreduction.89 The as-obtained ZnFe2O4@H-TiO2-x resulted in a maximum CH4 evolution rate of 21.39 μmol/g/h after the incorporation of CoOx and Au-Cu bimetallic cocatalysts. Such an improvement in photoreduction activity originated from the well-aligned, overlapping band structures of ZnFe2O4 and H-TiO2-x, forming a seamless contact heterostructure that displayed a Type II band alignment. In addition, Li and co-workers designed a type II heterostructure consisting of ZnO, ZnFe2O4, and ZnO@ZnFe2O4@CdS for CO2 photoconversion.90 The optimized ZnO@ZnFe2O4@CdS heterostructure exhibited good performance with a CO evolution rate of 197.66 μmol/g/h, which is 50.8 times that of bare ZnFe2O4. The enhanced activity of the type II ZnO@ZnFe2O4@CdS heterostructure for CO evolution is attributable to promoted CO2 adsorption, enhanced visible light absorption and efficacious separation of photoexcited charge carriers.

Z-scheme ZnFe2O4-based heterojunctions

Type II heterojunctions are promising in promoting charge separation, but their main drawback is that the redox ability of the charge carriers is weakened upon the charge transfer process.1 Therefore, it is difficult to achieve a high CO2 conversion rate and good product selectivity. On the other hand, Z-scheme systems can be effective in overcoming those disadvantages. Z-scheme systems display the following advantages: 1) a high photogenerated charge separation efficiency; 2) significantly high redox ability by maintaining a very negative CB and a high positive VB value.3 For instance, Ciocarlan et al. demonstrated that the ZnFe2O4@TiO2 exhibited a much higher activity of CO2 reduction to CO and CH4 than that of P25.91 Through linear sweep voltammetry and Mott-Schottky characterizations, it was confirmed that the Z-scheme charge transfer mode was formed between ZnFe2O4 and TiO2, thus leading to the highly promoted CO2 photoconversion efficiency. Similarly, ZnFe2O4/TiO2 and ZnFe2O4-BiOCl nanohybrids were fabricated by Song et al. for the photoreduction of CO2 to produce cyclohexanone and cyclohexyl formate.92,93 It should be emphasized that the CB edges of ZnFe2O4 and TiO2 are both more negative than the redox potential of CO2 and its reduced products. In situ irradiated X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy, Ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy, and electron spin resonance measurements may need to be carried out to clarify the charge-separating types in such composite systems. Our group has prepared Z-scheme α-Fe2O3/ZnFe2O4 and ZnFe2O4-SrTiO3 heterojunctions, which displayed outstanding photocatalytic activities for converting CO2 into CO and CH4.94,95 Eventually, the separation efficiency of photogenerated carriers and the powerful redox capacity of h+ and e− of the heterojunctions are all facilitated, which apparently enhances their catalytic behavior.

In addition to the direct Z-scheme systems, recently, Z-scheme systems with a conductive intermediate have been employed for CO2 photoreduction. For instance, Li and co-workers grew reduced graphene oxide nanosheets and octahedral ZnFe2O4 onto the surface of In2O3 microtubes (ZFO/RGO/IO) by a hydrothermal process.96 The development of high-quality heterointerfaces was indicated by the two intimate phase contacts of ZFO and IO through a well-defined boundary interface (Figure 12A). The comparison of the transient photocurrent and SPV spectra further confirmed the improved photoinduced carrier separation due to the synergistic effect between ZFO, RGO, and IO (Figures 12C and 12D). In view of the charge transfer of the 20ZFO/10RGO/IO hierarchical tubule, the holes in the VB of ZFO did not possess sufficient power to oxidize H2O to ·OH, as demonstrated by electron spin resonance data (Figure 12E). Other types ZnFe2O4-based heterojunctions

Besides the type-II and Z-scheme ZnFe2O4-based heterojunctions, S-scheme and P-N heterojunctions have also been employed for CO2 photoreduction. Zhou et al. synthesized S-scheme ZnFe2O4/Bi2MoO6 photocatalyst (ZFO-BMO) by a facile solvothermal method.98 The resulting ZFO-BMO photocatalyst could achieve a total CO2 conversion rate of 47.1 μmol/g within 5 h under simulated sunlight irradiation, which is 3.2- and 2.5-fold enhancement compared to ZFO (14.79 μmol/g) or BMO alone (19.01 μmol/g), respectively (Figures 13A and 13B). SnFe2O4-based heterojunctions

SnFe2O4 attracted widespread research attention as a promising photocatalyst candidate for CO2 photoreduction because of its room temperature magnetism, visible-light response (∼2.1 eV), and relatively negative CB potential (−1.45 V vs. NHE).101 In 2004, Liu et al. synthesized SnFe2O4 nanoparticles by the precipitation exchange method for the first time.46,102 However, the application of SnFe2O4 nanocrystals as a photocatalyst was first reported ten years later by Lee and co-workers in the photo-Fenton catalytic degradation of organic pollutant.103 Since then, there has been substantial development in the fabrication of highly efficient SnFe2O4-based heterojunctions for photocatalytic applications, including environmental remediation and hydrogen generation.29,101,104 In 2020, our research group first investigated the photocatalytic performance of SnFe2O4 for CO2 conversion. In our study, morphology-controlled SnFe2O4 was fabricated using a hydrothermal process with different pH values.105 Interestingly, octahedron-shaped SnFe2O4 presented a superior photocatalytic activity toward CO2 reduction than SnFe2O4 nanoparticles and tetrakaidecahedron SnFe2O4. It is believed that the improvement in photocatalytic performance could be ascribed to the rich oxygen vacancies on the surface of octahedron SnFe2O4, which could promote visible light absorption, improve the capture of CO2, and facilitate electron transfer. In another example, our group has deposited Au nanoparticles on H2SO4 treatment SnFe2O4 nano-octahedron (Au-N-SFO) and demonstrated that the production rates of CO, CH4, and H2 obviously higher than SnFe2O4 nano-octahedron. The considerably increased photo-activity of Au-N-SFO was due to Au nanoparticles possessing the SPR effect and the effective separation of photoinduced charge carriers across the Au-N-SFO Schottky heterojunction upon light irradiation.106 It was reported that Au could serve as a cocatalyst to improve the CH4 selectivity for photocatalytic CO2 conversion. However, the above research work focused on raising the productivity, rather than on controlling CH4 selectivity.

In addition to the Au-N-SFO Schottky heterojunction, we have constructed a Z-scheme SnFe2O4-g-C3N4(SFO-CN) heterojunction by a one-step hydrothermal route (Figure 14A).107 Such synthesized photocatalysts presented a higher concentration of oxygen vacancies in comparison to SFO (Figure 14B). As depicted in Figure 14C, the photovoltage response of SFO-CN was markedly greater than that of CN and SFO, leading to effective photoinduced charge-carrier separation. SFO-CN has a CO yield rate of 7.56 μmol/g/h under visible light irradiation, which is 2.5 and 2.2 times that of pure SFO and CN (Figure 14D). It is noted that O2 was detected in the presence of SFO-CN (3.51 μmol/g/h) and CN (1.46 μmol/g/h). In contrast, no generation of O2 was detected for bare SFO. Additionally, the resulting SFO-CN could be completely recovered from the aqueous solution (Figure 14E). Generally, the yield of solar fuels obtained by photoreduction of CO2 is closely associated with the CB levels of the semiconductor used.5 At present, the CB potentials of SnFe2O4 reported in the literature are very different (from −0.33V to −1.45 V vs. NHE),101 thus advanced characterization techniques are needed to accurately quantify the CB position of SnFe2O4. In theory, SnFe2O4 can achieve product selectivity in CO2 conversion due to its strong reduction capacity. Nevertheless, the major products of CO2 photoreduction in the presence of pure SnFe2O4 and SnFe2O4-based heterojunctions are C1 products up to now.105,107 Therefore, converting CO2 into C2+ products over pure SnFe2O4 and SnFe2O4-based heterojunctions through photocatalytic reduction is still a challenge.

Conclusions and outlook

Over the past few decades, MFe2O4 and their composite photocatalysts have been widely applied for various photocatalytic applications, and corresponding reviews have been reported. Previous studies paid little attention to CO2 photoreduction in the presence of MFe2O4 and its composites. This review presents a comprehensive overview of the synthesis methods of MFe2O4. Importantly, the essential modification strategies to enhance the photoactivity of MFe2O4 nanomaterials for CO2 conversion are discussed in detail. Although these modification strategies can significantly improve the CO2 photoreduction performance of MFe2O4 and its composites, there is still a gap compared with some traditional photocatalysts such as 3.5wt. % In-TiO2,111 TiO2 thin films,112 and g-C3N4/TiO2113(Figures 16A–16C).Figure 16Product selectivity over MFe2O4 and their composite photocatalysts(A) CO, (B) CH4, (C) CH3OH, and (D) C2+ products evolution over MFe2O4 and their composites during the CO2 photoreduction.

First of all, the CO2 adsorption and activation over MFe2O4 photocatalyst surfaces should be optimized. In this regard, the porous and ultrathin two-dimensional MFe2O4 nanomaterials with high specific surface area are expected to be designed and synthesized by novel synthetic methods. Since previous reports have demonstrated MFe2O4 nanomaterials with oxygen vacancies and wide alkaline adaptive range,114,115,116,117 surface oxygen vacancies, solid bases (NaOH, KOH, and MgO), and alkali metal (Li, Na, and K) can be introduced to promote the adsorption and activation of CO2 over MFe2O4, thus further improving the performance of MFe2O4 materials on CO2 photoreduction.

The low charge separation efficiency of MFe2O4 materials is another well-known issue to be overcome. In this respect, although crystal facet engineering has been demonstrated as a very effective approach in promoting charge separation and transfer in TiO2 and other photocatalysts,118 MFe2O4 facet junctions have not been realized for the CO2 photoconversion application. Therefore, related research can have a significant impact on future MFe2O4 nanomaterial-based photocatalysts. As for MFe2O4-based heterojunctions, the construction of Z- or S-scheme structures is more favorable than Type-II heterojunctions, which not only facilitates charge carrier separation, but also maintains the strong oxidative/reductive ability of each component. Interestingly, (Ni, Cd, Co, Mn)Fe2O4 can be used as an H2O oxidant, while (Cu, Zn, Sn)Fe2O4 can be employed as a CO2 reductant. Therefore, a seamless contact (Ni, Cd, Co, Mn)Fe2O4-(Cu, Zn, Sn)Fe2O4 heterojunction with Z- or S-scheme charge transfer mode can be constructed through an in situ procedure by sharing the Fe element. It can be expected that such a heterojunction will display a higher charge separation rate, CO2 conversion efficiency, and product selectivity. Furthermore, in situ irradiated XPS technique is the best approach to advance the current state of knowledge on charge transfer mechanisms in spinel ferrite heterojunctions during CO2 photoconversion.

As can be seen from Figure 16D, only ZnFe2O4 and their composite materials have been reported to generate C2+ products in the photocatalytic CO2 reduction83,86,87,89 In order to improve the products selectivity of MFe2O4 and their composites materials, Z or S-scheme heterojunction design, single-atom co-catalyst engineering, surface defects creating, and surface plasmon resonance operating are inevitable, with the aim of obtaining selectivity in the photocatalytic CO2 conversion to high-valued C2+ feedstocks rather than focus exclusively on C1 products with lesser commercial value. Furthermore, isotopic 13CO2 labeling was proven to be an efficient technique for demonstrating the carbon-containing products arising from CO2 photoreduction by using spinel ferrite-based photocatalysts.

The inherent room-temperature magnetism guarantees the easy recovery of MFe2O4 photocatalysts after CO2 photoreduction in the solid-liquid mode. More importantly, CO2 photoreduction over MFe2O4 in a solid-liquid atmosphere is conducive to the formation of C2+ products.4,5,7 Therefore, it is quite promising to explore MFe2O4 and related composites photocatalysts for highly efficient and selective conversion of CO2 in solid-liquid environmental systems. Moreover, the CO2 conversion rate, product selectivity, mechanisms, and possible reaction pathways should be compared with those in the solid-gas reaction.
