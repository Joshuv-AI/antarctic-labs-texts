Association of diet and physical activity with overweight and obesity in people living with HIV: a cross-sectional study in Brescia, Italy
Authors: Barbara Zanini, Giulia Gilberti, Silvia Marconi, Martina Salvi, Matteo Rota, Francesco Donato, Giorgio Tiecco, Irene Scarvaglieri, Francesco Castelli, Eugenia Quiros-Roldan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe increasing prevalence of overweight/obesity (OO) among people living with HIV (PLWH) is rising concerns. However, limited data are available on the role of modifiable lifestyle factors, particularly adherence to the Mediterranean diet, among PLWH on long-term stable ART. The aim of this study was to investigate the association between modifiable lifestyle factors, particularly dietary habits, adherence to the Mediterranean diet and physical activity, and overweight/obesity among virologically suppressed PLWH on long-term stable ART. PLWH on stable ART and with HIV-RNA suppression, followed at a tertiary care center in Northern Italy were enrolled. Data about diet (dietary recall and MediLite, a validated tool to assess adherence to the Mediterranean diet) and physical activity level (PAL) were collected. Out of 385 eligible subjects, 176 agreed to participate in the study (median age and HIV infection duration, 51 and 17 years, respectively) and categorized according to Body Mass Index (BMI) in normal weight (NW, BMI < 25 kg/m2) and OO (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2, n = 93, 52.8%). OO vs. NW participants had lower Mediterranean diet adherence scores (MediLite 10 vs. 11; p = 0.009). Beer and aperitif consumption were more frequent among OO participants (p = 0.022; p = 0.002), while PAL was comparable between groups. At multivariable analysis, BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 at the time of ART initiation was positively associated (OR = 7.61, 95% CI 3.27–19.6; p < 0.01), and each 1-point increase in the MediLite was negatively associated (OR = 0.80, 95% IC 0.67–0.94 < 0.01) with OO. Introduction

Since the advent of the modern antiretroviral therapy (ART), people living with HIV (PLWH) have dramatically improved their life quality and expectancy1. Despite effective ART, many subjects exhibit persistent low grade chronic inflammation, mainly due to persistent gut dysbiosis with microbial translocation and to activated monocytes/macrophages, with pro-inflammatory phenotype and high secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines as IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β2. This pro inflammatory state, together with both genetic and environmental factors and some antiretroviral drugs, which may promote weight gain through effects on adipogenesis, energy metabolism, and appetite regulation, may contribute to weight gain among PLWH, especially observed during the first 2 years after ART initiation3. As a consequence, the prevalence of overweight/obesity has increased among PLWH around the world, and it is now similar to that observed in the general population4,5. Among the environmental factors, unhealthy diet and the progressive shift from the Mediterranean diet, even among people living in the Mediterranean basin, are widely addressed in literature as central topics in the “obesity pandemic” in the general population6,7. PLWH appear to follow a similar increasing trend in overweight and obesity, although the underlying determinants may be more complex, involving both lifestyle-related factors and HIV- and treatment-related mechanisms8. Therefore, understanding the role of modifiable lifestyle factors, including adherence to the Mediterranean dietary pattern, may be of value to inform targeted interventions in this population for the prevention and management of overweight and obesity.

If weight gain leads to overweight or obesity, which is considered a serious multifactorial condition with increasing risk of developing metabolic and cardiovascular diseases, health span may be affected. The recent update of the European AIDS Clinical Society (EACS) guidelines for HIV infection management (October 2025) has dedicated a specific chapter to the issue of “weight gain and obesity”9. According to this update, clinicians are advised to assess weight and body mass index (BMI) among PLWH routinely, or at least on an annual basis, and to manage factors associated with an increased risk of excessive weight gain. These factors include non-modifiable determinants such as sex, older age and ethnicity, and modifiable lifestyle factors such as poor diet quality, excessive alcohol consumption, sedentary lifestyle and altered sleep patterns6,10. The exposure to specific antiretroviral agents (particularly integrase strand transfer inhibitors, INSTIs11 should be considered among modifiable factors, but according to EACS guidelines, switching ART with the goal of reducing weight is not recommended. Although exposure to INSTI-based regimens has been frequently investigated in relation to weight gain, particularly in the early phases after ART initiation, the relative contribution of long-term ART compared with modifiable lifestyle factors remains uncertain in patients on stable suppressive therapy12.

In 2023, we designed a prospective observational study, named LIFEH (LIFEstyle exploration among people living with HIV), to evaluate various lifestyle factors -including diet, physical activity, alcohol consumption, smoking habits, sleep hygiene, emotional status- among a cohort of PLWH on stable and effective ART7. Despite the well-established role of unhealthy diet and lifestyle factors in the general population, their specific contribution to overweight and obesity among people living with HIV, particularly in those on long-term stable and suppressive ART, remains insufficiently characterized, and the relative impact of lifestyle-related determinants compared with ART-related effects is still unclear. In the present analysis, we aimed to investigate the association between modifiable lifestyle factors, particularly dietary habits and adherence to the Mediterranean diet, as well as physical activity, and the presence of overweight/obesity in a cohort of virologically suppressed PLWH on long-term stable ART. By focusing on individuals with sustained virologic suppression and no recent ART changes, this study aimed to investigate lifestyle-related determinants of overweight/obesity within a more clinically homogeneous population, while limiting heterogeneity related to recent treatment modifications.

Methods

Study design

The LIFEH study protocol has been described in detail elsewhere13. It was approved by the local Ethics Committee of ASST Spedali Civili on May 23rd, 2023 (internal protocol identification number NP 5892). Briefly, the study is a prospective observational study to assess the role of lifestyle factors in the development of overweight and obesity (defined as BMI ≥ 25 Kg/m2, according to World Health Organization cut-off14 among PLWH on stable ART (defined as the absence of any change in antiretroviral regimen and sustained virologic suppression for at least 24 months prior to enrollment) and it is still ongoing. Here, as in a cross-sectional analysis, we analyze the characteristics of the enrolled subjects at the baseline.

Participants

Participants were enrolled from the cohort of PLWH routinary followed at the Outpatient Unit of Infectious Diseases of Spedali Civili Hospital and University of Brescia (Lombardy, Italy). Eligible participants were adults of Italian nationality by birth, on stable ART with HIV-RNA < 20 copies/mL for at least 24 months, enrolled between September 2023 and December 2024. Exclusion criteria included current or past opportunistic infections, AIDS dementia complex diagnosis, type II diabetes diagnosis, past or active cancer state, cardiovascular diseases including coronary heart disease, cerebrovascular disease and peripheral arterial disease (considering both past diagnosis or currently under treatment), active illicit substance use. All these data were extracted from the digital medical record of each patient.

After eligibility screening, the infectious disease clinicians further assessed, by interview, if participants adhered to a prescribed dietary plan and excluded those who declared to be followed by a dietician or a nutritionist; finally they provided all eligible participants with a detailed explanation of the study procedures, answered any questions and, upon obtaining written informed consent, referred participants to a study researcher. The researcher scheduled a subsequent visit with each participant, made all the assessments and collected all clinical and laboratory data.

Data collection and measures

Data about residence, education level, work activity, marital status and number of household members were extracted from the outpatient medical records, as well as data on HIV transmission mode (vertical transmission, accidental transmission, sexual transmission, intravenous drug use), chronic hepatitis viral infections, current ART therapy, coded as a categorical variable according to regimen class (INSTI vs. non-INSTI) and regimen structure (dual vs. triple therapy), and laboratory values (HIV-RNA level, CD4 and CD8 count, lipid profile, glycemia and liver function tests); laboratory values were extracted from digital medical records at two time points, that were at the time of ART initiation and at the time of enrollment in the study. ART regimen was included in the analysis as a potential confounder, given its known association with weight gain.

During the visit, researchers assessed weight, height, waist and hip circumferences; weight at the beginning of ART was recorded from digital medical records. Diastolic and systolic blood pressure and heart rates were measured in resting conditions. A 24-hour dietary recall (24 H recall) was administered by a trained interviewer (part of the research team) and all data were collected on a paper record before entry in the nutrition software Microdiet© (Downlee Systems, Downlee Lodge, Bankhall, Chapel-en-le-Frith, United Kingdom) for bromatological analysis. The MediLite questionnaire was administered by the same trained interviewer in order to estimate adherence to Mediterranean Diet15,16. The Medi-Lite score evaluates adherence to the Mediterranean diet across nine food domains: fruit, vegetables, legumes, cereals, fish, meat and meat products, dairy products, alcohol, and olive oil; for each domain the score ranges from 0 to 2, with total scores ranging from 0 to 18 and categorized in three level of adherence, as low (score ≤ 9), medium (10–11), or high (≥ 12). Two validated questions assessing food-insecurity17, information about use and type of food supplements and an open-ended recall question about any significant difference in dietary habits before and after the initiation of ART were administered. Physical activity level (PAL) was estimated through the short form Italian validated version of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ)18. Subjects were categorized, according to Metabolic Equivalent Task (METs) calculation, in “high physical activity level” if reaching 3000 METs/week and over, “moderate physical activity level” if reaching a minimum of 600 METs/week but less than 3000 METs/week and “low physical activity level” if not reaching 600 METs/week. As detailed elsewhere, during the visit further data about smoking habits (never/former or current smoker, duration of smoking exposure in years and average number of cigarettes per day, for former and current smokers), alcohol consumption (average numbers of alcoholic units of wine, beer, aperitifs and spirits per week) and screening for problematic alcohol use using the CAGE questionnaire, a validated tool to identify individuals at risk for alcohol use disorder, were collected (CAGE being considered positive when at least two of the four items were answered affirmatively, suggesting a possible alcohol use disorder and warranting further clinical evaluation)19.

The research team collected, stored and managed data using REDCap, a secure, web-based platform designed to support data capture for research studies. To ensure confidentiality, each participant was assigned a unique identification code, and only study investigators had access to study data and records.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs), and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Comparisons between participants with BMI < 25 kg/m² and those with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m² were performed using the t-test for continuous variables, and the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. For continuous variables, appropriate data transformations were applied to approximate normality when the assumption of normality was not met.

A multivariable logistic regression model was fitted to evaluate the association between adherence to the Mediterranean diet (as measured by the MediLite questionnaire) and physical activity (quantified as MET expenditure) with the likelihood of overweight or obesity (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m²), adjusting for age, sex, socio-economic indicators, ART regimen and BMI at ART initiation. Potential non-linear associations of the MediLite score and MET expenditure were examined using restricted cubic splines. Results are reported as Odds Ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

. Changes in BMI from ART initiation to study enrollment were calculated and modeled as a continuous outcome using linear regression. Factors potentially associated with BMI change, including demographic, clinical, and lifestyle variables, were explored.

All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

From September 2023 to December 2024 all PLWH under follow-up in our HIV outpatient center were screened for eligibility and 385 subjects met selection criteria. The flowchart of the screening and the enrollment procedures are reported in Fig. 1.

Of the 93 overweight/obese PLWH, 18 were classified as obese (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2). Overweight (25 ≤ BMI < 30 kg/m2) and obese patients differed significantly only with respect to the risk of alcohol use disorder, as assessed by the CAGE questionnaire (p = 0.005, Supplementary Table 1).

Diet

The full MediLite questionnaire results are reported in Supplementary Table 3. The median MediLite score in the overall sample was 11.00 (Supplementary Fig. 1). It was 11 (Q1-Q3 range 10–12) among normal-weight PLWH and 10 (Q1-Q3 range 9–11) among those with overweight or obesity (p = 0.002). The distribution according to MediLite categories and adherence to single MediLite items is reported in Fig. 3, Panel A and B, respectively: a higher proportion of PLWH with normal weight was in the high category of MediLite score than those with OO (62% and 41%, respectively). Among the individual items, PLWH with normal weight reported higher consumption of legumes compared with those with OO (p = 0.03), whereas differences in fruit and vegetables consumption were not statistically significant (Supplementary Table 3). Interestingly, legumes were the only component of the Mediterranean diet that differed significantly between groups. Legumes are a key source of dietary fiber and are associated with increased satiety and improved metabolic outcomes. Their lower consumption among participants with overweight/obesity may therefore reflect poorer dietary quality and could contribute to weight gain.

Physical Activity

Distribution of subjects according to IPAQ categories for activity level, expressed as METs/week, is reported in Fig. 4. A higher proportion of inactive subjects (< 600 METs/week), and a lower proportion of those doing high activity (≥ 3000 METs/week) were reported among PLWH with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 compared to the others, even if the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.197). We also observed (Supplementary Table 4) higher METs in intense physical activities among PLWH with BMI < 25 kg/m2 than PLWH with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 (median METs 720 vs. 480, IQR 0–2160 vs. 0, 0–1440; p = 0.014) and walking (median METs 924 vs. 525, IQR 210–2520 vs. 180–1260; p = 0.030).

Alcohol consumption and tobacco use

According to the MediLite item for alcohol consumption (Supplementary Table 3), 17% of the study population drink more than 2 alcoholic units per day (20%among subjects with normal weight and 14% among those with overweight or obesity, p = 0.241). Wine and spirits drinking did not different significantly between the two groups. However, drinking beer or alcoholic aperitifs three or more times per week was more frequently reported among participants with overweight/obesity compared to those without (16% vs. 8.6% for beer and 11% vs. 1.2% for alcoholic aperitifs, p = 0.022 and p = 0.002, respectively) (Supplementary Table 5).

Multivariable analysis

According to the multivariable logistic regression model (Table 2), BMI ≥ 25 kg/m² at the start of ART therapy was associated with higher odds of overweight/obesity at the study time (OR = 7.37, 95% CI 3.037–19.9; p < 0.01), and each 1-point increase in the MediLite score was associated in a linear fashion with a protective effect (OR = 0.82, 95% IC 0.68–0.98; p = 0.021) (Supplementary Table 4). Current ART regimen was also associated with overweight/obesity, with lower odds observed among participants receiving INSTI-based regimens (OR = 0.23, 95% CI 0.06–0.79; p = 0.020). (Table 2) When modeling BMI as a continuous variable, the overall pattern of associations was broadly consistent, although the association with MediLite score was no longer statistically significant (Supplementary Table 6).

Discussion

The main focus of this cross-sectional study, as part of the prospective LIFEH study, was to investigate the association between modifiable lifestyle factors, particularly dietary habits, adherence to the Mediterranean diet and physical activity, and overweight/obesity among virologically suppressed PLWH on long-term stable ART. The prevalence of overweight/obesity was 52.7% (43.1% overweight and 9.6% obesity), which is higher than what expected in Italy among the general population, especially regarding the prevalence of overweight (32% among Italian adults)19. Total caloric intake did not differ between PLWH with normal weight and those with overweight/obesity, while higher adherence to the Mediterranean diet was observed in the normal-weight group. The multivariable analysis shows a protective effect of adherence to Mediterranean diet and that BMI ≥ 25 kg/m² at HIV diagnosis was strongly associated with overweight/obesity at study time.

According to data from Istituto Superiore Sanità21, the overall prevalence of overweight and obesity among Italian adults is 43% (32.6% overweight and 10.4 obesity). To date, several reports have highlighted a global increase in overweight and obesity among PLWH5,22,23.The prevalences of overweight and obesity in our sample are like those reported in other studies among Italian PLWH. For instance, Mazzitelli et al., in a cross-sectional study of 1204 PLWH in the Veneto region - similar to Lombardy in terms of geographical location and socio-demographic characteristics-, with a median age of 53 years old and a median HIV duration of 15 years reported a prevalence of overweight/obesity of 54.4% (35.1% overweight and 19.4% obesity)24.

We found that the majority of people with BMI ≥ 25 mg/kg2 at HIV diagnosis remained in this weight category at the study time (83%) whereas almost 40% (49 of 123, 39.8%) of those with normal weight at HIV diagnosis became overweight/obese at the study time. This finding suggests that, despite the well-known detrimental health consequences of being overweight or obesity, these conditions may not have been adequately addressed either at HIV diagnosis or during follow-up. Also, BMI underestimation and weight misperception among PLWH may have contributed to the little attention to the problem of weight control, as recently described25. However, given the retrospective cross-sectional nature of the present analysis, the long duration of HIV infection and ART exposure in the study population, and the limited number of participants transitioning from normal weight to overweight/obesity, it was not possible to reliably disentangle the relative contribution of ART-related and behavioral factors to BMI changes over time. Prospective longitudinal studies are needed to better characterize these trajectories.

In our study, current INSTI-based regimens were associated with lower odds of overweight/obesity. This finding should be interpreted cautiously considering the cross-sectional design of the study and the long duration of HIV infection and ART exposure in our cohort. In this setting, current BMI is likely influenced by multiple cumulative factors, including aging, prior ART exposure, long-term metabolic changes, lifestyle-related determinants, and treatment history, rather than by the current regimen alone.

Although some studies have reported an association between INSTI-based regimens and weight gain, particularly during the first years after ART initiation3,26, more recent evidence has questioned the consistency and magnitude of this association27,28.

There is increasing interest in investigating, alongside ART-related factors, the role of modifiable lifestyle determinants, in order to understand the reasons for the increasing prevalence of overweight and obesity among PLWH, for preventing obesity-associated non-communicable chronic diseases (NCDs)29. In the general population, unhealthy diets, physical inactivity, excessive alcohol consumption, and tobacco use are considered the four most important modifiable lifestyle factors influencing NCDs development [The WHO Global Action Plan for the Prevention and Control of NCDs 2013–2030]. Overweight and obesity are considered intermediate modifiable risk factors30 which interact with the other risk factors synergistically increasing the risk for major NCDs31.

Few studies have investigated the role of diet in the development of overweight and obesity among PLWH in our country. Guaraldi and colleagues32, using a dietary assessment method similar to ours (3-day food diaries), reported no association between total caloric intake and > 5% weight gain. To our knowledge, our report is one of the few studies assessing not only the nutritional features of the diet, but also adherence to Mediterranean diet, assessed through a validated questionnaire as MediLite16, administered by a nutritionist. Our findings on adherence to the Mediterranean diet were similar to those reported in a study of PLWH in the UK, when considering data collected prior the dietary intervention33. In agreement with the study by Stradling and colleagues, we found a high prevalence of regular olive oil consumption but a low prevalence of adequate fruits, vegetables and legumes consumption in our population.

Our data showed a significant, inverse association between adherence to the Mediterranean diet pattern and overweight/obesity, when adjusting for demographic, socio-economic variables and BMI at HIV diagnosis (see supplementary Table 4). Indeed, PLWH with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m² reported lower legume consumption compared with those with BMI < 25 kg/m², while no significant differences were observed for fruit and vegetable intake. These findings partially conflict with those of a previous Portoguese cross-sectional study34, which assessed adherence to the Mediterranean diet using the MedDietScore questionnaire35 in a cohort of 571 PLWH. The Portoguese study reported that BMI < 25 kg/m2 was associated with lower MedDietScore, in comparison to patients with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2. These differences may be due to differences in dietary assessment methods, younger age of the Portuguese study population, shorter duration since HIV infection, different selection criteria of the study populations and different time of data collection (approximately 10 years earlier in the study by Policarpo et al.) compared to our cohort.

Another important dietary modifiable factor is alcohol consumption. In our study, we found a higher consumption of beer and alcoholic aperitifs among subjects with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 than those with lower BMI, in line with other studies36. Our findings about the type of alcohol consumption and overweight/obesity are partially consistent with a European survey conducted in the general population, which reported a positive association between overweight and consumption of beer and spirit, but not wine37, as well as with the UK Biobank study, which found a positive association between beer and spirits consumption and visceral adiposity38. In a further study among PLWH, alcohol consumption (particularly spirits more than beer) was associated with poorer gut barrier integrity (as indicated by elevated lipopolysaccharide binding protein levels), heightened monocyte activation (increased soluble markers as sCD14), and greater systemic inflammation (elevated IL-6)39. The study by Webel also highlights a high prevalence of overweight and obesity following antiretroviral therapy initiation (up to 50%, especially among women) and notes that alcohol exacerbates overall inflammation, which may indirectly promote metabolic disturbances and contribute to weight gain.

In our sample, we detected a high level of daily physical activity, generally exceeding the levels estimated in the adult Italian population40, likely reflecting the accurate collection of working activity hours by the nutritionist. Although no overall association between physical activity and overweight/obesity was observed in the multivariable analysis, we found that some components of physical activity differed between groups. In particular, participants with normal weight reported higher levels of intense physical activity and walking compared to those with overweight/obesity, similar to previous observations32,41. These findings suggest that specific domains of physical activity may differ across BMI categories, although their independent contribution to overweight/obesity may be limited in this population.

The lack of association between physical activity and overweight/obesity may appear unexpected. However, physical activity was assessed using a self-reported questionnaire, which may be subject to reporting bias and overestimation. In addition, the cross-sectional design does not allow causal inference, and reverse causation cannot be excluded. Finally, the relatively homogeneous and clinically stable population may have limited variability in physical activity levels.

Strengths and limitations

Our results should be interpreted with caution, due to some limitations. First of all, the cross-sectional study design does not allow interpreting an association as a cause-effect relationship. Secondly, given the voluntary participation in the study, the possibility of a selection bias cannot be excluded. As reported in Fig. 1, about half of the eligible individuals did not participate in the study, primary due to the lack of interest in the study or unwillingness to invest time. BMI underestimation and weight misperception seem common in some subgroups of PLWH25. It is plausible that PLWH who participated in the study, dedicating approximately an hour of their free time, were those more sensitive to issues of healthy lifestyle or had specific reasons for seeking dietary consultation, such as being overweight or obese. Although this selection bias cannot be completely excluded, recent data from the Swiss HIV Cohort study including 1302 unselected PLWH reported a prevalence of overweight/obesity and levels of physical activity similar to those observed in our study42. In addition, the applied eligibility criteria, including the exclusion of individuals with metabolic and cardiovascular comorbidities, may have further contributed to the selection of a relatively healthier and more homogeneous population, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings.

Moreover, our analysis focused on a single center cohort, including PLWH only with Italian nationality by birth, and these aspects may limit generalizability. In addition, the exclusion of individuals with type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease may have led to a selection of a metabolically healthier population and potentially limited the representation of overweight and obese individuals. Another limitation is that ART exposure was classified according to current regimen only; cumulative duration of exposure and switching history were not available, and residual confounding by indication or time-varying confounding cannot be excluded. We acknowledge that dietary intake was assessed using a single 24 H recall and physical activity was self-reported, according to IPAQ. Both measures could be subject to substantial measurement error. Random within-person variation in a single 24 H recall is likely to attenuate diet–outcome associations toward the null. The lower MediLite score observed in OO participants could reflect true differences in dietary patterns, post-diagnosis behavioral changes, or differential reporting. In addition, differential misclassification by BMI status cannot be excluded, as individuals with OO may underreport energy intake or overreport physical activity to a greater extent. We also acknowledge that using IPAQ to estimate PAL, could not allow us to separately analyze the effect of occupational vs. leisure physical activity. Lastly, we classified PLWH according to BMI cut-off, despite recent new proposals for defining obesity criteria43.

On the other side, a key strength of the LIFEH study is the big amount of data collected on several aspects of lifestyle, combined with the use of validated instruments for data collections. Moreover, according to recent EACS guidelines, we assessed overweight and obesity using BMI cut-off, as still recommended, but also collected additional measures including waist circumference and waist-to-hip ratio9.

Context, implications for practice and future research

Our findings are consistent with previous Italian data reporting a high prevalence of overweight and obesity among PLWH on stable ART44.

In this study, overweight and obesity were associated both with baseline BMI status at HIV diagnosis and with lower adherence to the Mediterranean diet.

Our results suggest that both ART-related and lifestyle-related factors may contribute to overweight and obesity among PLWH on long-term stable ART. In particular, adherence to the Mediterranean diet remained independently associated with OO after adjustment for ART-related variables. Our data suggest that focusing exclusively on ART optimization may be insufficient to address weight gain in this population. Interventions targeting dietary habits—particularly improving adherence to healthy dietary patterns such as the Mediterranean diet—may represent a key strategy to reduce the burden of metabolic complications. These findings support the integration of structured nutritional counseling and lifestyle interventions into routine HIV care, in line with current EACS recommendations. Systematic assessment of dietary habits, adherence to the Mediterranean diet, and alcohol consumption may help guide personalized lifestyle interventions in clinical practice. Further research is needed to define effective multidimensional strategies to address overweight and obesity among PLWH, taking into account the complex metabolic and immunological changes associated with long-term ART2.

Conclusion

Overweight and obesity are highly prevalent clinical conditions among PLWH on stable ART. Addressing this issue requires more research to develop appropriate intervention strategies focused on the specific immuno-metabolic pattern of PLWH.
