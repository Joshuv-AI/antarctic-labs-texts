Lifestyle front offices in dutch hospitals: a cross-sectional survey on similarities, differences and future needs
Authors: Florien Ham, Mariëtte R Boon, Janke F de Groot, Meike J van Boxtel, Ghislaine A P G van Mastrigt, Judith G M Jelsma, Elisabeth F C van Rossum
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSince 2019, Dutch hospitals have been developing approaches aiming to bridge the gap between hospital care and community-based lifestyle support. This has led to the introduction of the so called ‘Lifestyle Front Office” (LFO) in 2022. The LFO enables healthcare professionals to refer patients for lifestyle and (psycho)social support adjacent to medical treatment in the context of specialist medical care. After a consultation at the LFO, patients are referred to the appropriate community-based lifestyle interventions. LFOs have been quickly adopted in Dutch hospitals, leading to conceptual differences. This study aimed to evaluate similarities, differences and future needs of LFOs in the Netherlands, striving for a more unified concept.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was sent out between December 2023 and September 2024 to all Dutch hospitals with an operational LFO (n = 17 hospitals). The survey focused on referral processes, patient eligibility criteria, departmental involvement, screening methods, consultation practices, follow-up procedures, financing, and capacity. The collected data were analyzed quantitatively and qualitatively to identify common practice, challenges, and opportunities for improvement.ResultsThe survey response rate was 82% (14/17). Primary referral groups were patients with lifestyle-related conditions such as obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus and cardiovascular diseases. Most LFOs conducted pre-visit screenings by using questionnaires, employed lifestyle care coordinators with diverse professional backgrounds, utilized structured tools during consultations and used the electronic patient records for screening and communication. Future needs included the need for (long-term) funding, which was only secured in 21% of the LFOs. Furthermore, inconsistent eligibility criteria, limited capacity, and follow-up procedures were seen as priorities for future attention.ConclusionThis study highlights the similarities, differences and future needs of LFOs in the Netherlands. To achieve a more unified concept, LFOs need to better align patient-related eligibility criteria, use of structured tools, referral to community-based support, follow-up strategies and long-term funding. While the LFO concept emphasizes strong inter-organizational collaboration, further research is needed to assess its impact by investigating success rate of referrals and (long-term) health outcomes after referral. Additionally, for maintaining LFOs in the Dutch healthcare system, additional research is required to better understand the barriers and facilitators regarding structural implementation.Keywords: Integrated care, Lifestyle interventions, Specialist medical care, Referral pathways, Implementation, Chronic disease

Background

Globally, the incidence of non-communicable diseases (NCDs), such as type 2 diabetes, chronic kidney disease, cardiovascular diseases and cancer, is increasing. The burden of NCDs have a profound impact on healthcare systems worldwide, affecting healthcare spending, service utilization, and economic burdens [1]. Frequently studied and prevalent shared features of patients with NCDs are overweight and/or obesity, smoking and excessive alcohol consumption, of which a great part can be modified by adopting a healthy lifestyle [2]. Implementing lifestyle interventions into treatment care plans can therefore prevent onset, worsening and progression of NCDs.

In recent years, healthcare systems are tempting to focus more on patient-centered and prevention-oriented care [3]. This can be characterized, for example, by the development of smoking cessation services or diabetes management programs within hospital settings. However, these services were mostly not integrated into existing care pathways or healthcare professionals were limited due to experience of barriers with regard to implementation, like lack of motivation, skills, knowledge, time and organizational support [4]. In addition, several other countries have developed national prevention strategies—such as Australia’s National Preventive Health Strategy and Switzerland’s strategy to tackle non-communicable diseases [5, 6]. However, these do not integrate with hospital care [7]. In fact, most initiatives for lifestyle support start in the public or primary care setting (including general practitioners). However, patients treated in hospitals for their chronic disease encompass a different group that generally mainly encounter their medical specialist. This environment offers a unique opportunity for success. Hospital encounters often represent a “teachable moment” — a time when patients may be particularly receptive to information about lifestyle change due to the immediacy of their diagnosis or treatment. Referral to lifestyle interventions from the hospital can therefore complement primary care efforts. Additionally, by referring patients to lifestyle interventions, hospitals can complement primary care initiatives and foster continuity between acute care and long-term curative treatment that crosses health domains, including lifestyle, medical, and (psycho)social care. However, as mentioned above, this promising direction is hampered by the fact that medical specialists encounter various barriers in offering patients optimal lifestyle advise or interventions [8–10].

To bridge this gap, in 2022 in the Netherlands the Lifestyle Front Office (LFO) was introduced, as a hospital-based initiative designed to strengthen interdisciplinary collaboration and to harness hospital encounters as opportunities for lifestyle-related behavior change. This concept has been described more elaborately in the LOFIT study protocol for two randomized controlled trials aiming to evaluate the (cost-)effectiveness of the LFO [11].

In short, the LFO is a physical area or department within the hospital, where patients can be referred to by their treating physician (medical specialist, nurse specialist or physician assistant) in the context of specialist medical care. After referral, a lifestyle care coordinator (a certified professional in the field of lifestyle, such as lifestyle coach or dietician) trained in Motivational Interviewing conducts a structured assessment of patients’ lifestyle, social, and psychological factors as well as medical factors that can possibly interfere with a healthy lifestyle. Motivational Interviewing is a collaborative, person-centered communication method designed to fosters patients’ intrinsic motivation to initiate and maintain positive lifestyle changes [12]. This assessment is followed by tailored referrals to appropriate lifestyle interventions or (psycho)social services, using the referral network in the region. After consultation in the LFO, both the treating medical specialist and the treating general practitioner are informed about the assessment and referral, including close coordination about tapering medication (with potential weight inducing side effect or that is no longer needed after lifestyle improvement). As LFOs mainly serve patients with chronic conditions in specialist medical care, the medical specialist remains responsible for overall disease management and follow-up.ndar

Additionally, LFO coordinators coordinate closely with community-based lifestyle initiatives. This way, they close the gap between hospital care and community-based care. Since the introduction of the LFO in 2022, 17 out of 69 Dutch hospitals have implemented this new central concept, while adapting this idea of LFO to their local context and regional organization of care. These context-driven adaptations have led to a variety of LFOs, including one LFO predominantly run by medical students under supervision of qualified clinicians. Furthermore, healthcare professionals do often experience barriers for implementation, such limited digital resources to support referral and feedback [13]. Consequently, there is no clear understanding what key elements are essential for a unified concept of LFO, and which elements can be used for contextual adaptation. Therefore, this study aimed to provide a descriptive evaluation of similarities, differences and future needs of LFOs in the Netherlands.

Methods

Design

This study consisted of data retrieved from a cross-sectional survey to investigate the current implementation of LFOs and their organizational characteristics. This cross-sectional study was conducted between December 2023 and September 2024 in the Netherlands. The survey was developed for this study and had not been published elsewhere. An English language version can be found in the Supplementary material.

Recruitment

For this study, only hospitals in the Netherlands with an operational LFO were eligible for inclusion, as it was not possible to fill in the questionnaire without being operational. The inclusion criterium for eligibility was having at least one patient referred to the LFO, who received an intake consultation and was subsequently referred to a lifestyle or (psycho)social intervention in the community. Exclusion criteria were hospitals who were planning to start an LFO but did not have patients referred yet. In addition, given the scope of in-hospital specialist medical care, LFOs in rehabilitation centers or in the community were excluded. Data were collected from the perspective of the LFO in the hospital. The survey was completed by the LFO project lead. LFO project leaders were recruited by sending an invitation e-mail to board members of all hospitals in the Netherlands. Furthermore, a digital link to the survey was shared by LinkedIn and a digital newsletter from the Lifestyle in Healthcare Consortium. Respondents were recruited voluntary. No incentives were offered to the participants. Data was collected between December 2023 and September 2024.

Data storage and protection

The survey data were collected using Microsoft Forms, which ensured secure data transmission and storage within Microsoft’s cloud infrastructure. Access to the collected responses was restricted to one designated reviewer (FH), whom had individual login credentials and accessed the data through a secure institutional account. Only FH had access to the original dataset (raw data). For parallel review, FH prepared anonymized case summaries or coded data segments. The second reviewer accessed and reviewed those parts only. This approach aligns with data minimization principles under the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) - only giving each reviewer access to what’s necessary for their role [14]. All research data was securely stored on the Knowledge Institute of the Dutch Association of Medical Specialists’ protected Microsoft servers, which are regularly backed up and accessible only by authorized study personnel. Personal identifiers were stored in a separate protected file from the research data, linked by a participant code. All obtained data (identifiable data and anonymized data) will be archived for 15 years after study completion, in accordance with Netherlands Code of Conduct for Research Integrity [15]. Thereafter, data will be securely deleted. In accordance with these regulations, respondents had the right to access their data, delete their data and the right to object. Each participant, the representative of a hospital-bound LFO, was informed about the length of the survey, which data were stored (and for how long) and the purpose of the study by a short information section prior to the start of the survey.

Development and pre-testing

The initial draft of the survey was developed by the research team (FH, MRB, LvR, JdG). The survey included a mix of multiple-choice and open-ended questions to capture both quantitative and qualitative insights. Prior to distribution, the survey underwent a pre-testing phase to gain feedback on clarity, working and usability. The pre-test was conducted by two reviewers involved in the implementation of LFOs nationwide (including hospital representatives, LFO coordinators, board members and healthcare professionals and members of the Lifestyle in Healthcare Consortium - Team “Practice Implementation and Quality”). The Lifestyle in Healthcare Consortium is a collaboration between healthcare organizations and patient representatives, led by the Dutch national Patient Federation. Based on this feedback, the survey was revised and tested within Microsoft Forms to ensure proper functionality and logical flow. The survey included 73 questions, distributed over 10 pages with a maximum of 15 questions per page. Respondents were able to review and change their answers through a Review step which displays a summary of the responses and asks the respondents if the answers were correct.

Analysis

The survey addressed the following topics: recruitment (including referring departments, type of professionals referring, patient eligibility criteria), care delivery at the LFO (including treated patients, employment, the screening process, the intake consultation, referral, follow-up, feedback) and the context of care (including short- and long-term funding and future needs). Data were analyzed by hospital type, academic vs. general hospitals, as academic hospitals are typically frontrunners in adopting innovations such as LFOs. Academic hospitals were defined as Dutch University Medical Centers (UMCs), which formally integrate a medical faculty with a tertiary care hospital and have a statutory mandate for highly specialized care, research, and medical education. General hospitals were defined as non-UMC hospitals providing secondary care. Although many general hospitals collaborate with universities or UMCs for the training of medical interns, they were categorized as general hospitals in this study due to the absence of an institutional integration of a medical faculty and a tertiary hospital.

Qualitative research models

Open-ended responses from the survey were analyzed using qualitative research methods, specifically inductive thematic analysis following the approach outlined by Clarke and Braun [16]. Responses were read and re-read to ensure data familiarity, after which initial codes were generated without relying on pre-existing frameworks. These codes were then organized into broader themes through an iterative process of comparison and refinement. Coding and theme development were initially conducted by one researcher (FH) and subsequently validated and refined in collaboration with a second researcher (MRB) to enhance credibility and consistency. No software was used for coding. Instead, the qualitative data were analysed manually using a systematic, thematic approach. The research team independently read and reread the transcripts to identify meaningful segments, developed initial codes, and then discussed and refined these codes collaboratively to form broader themes. This manual process allowed for an in-depth engagement with the data and ensured that the coding remained closely grounded in participants’ words and contexts.

Data analysis

Data were collected in Microsoft Forms and then transported to Microsoft Excel version 16.92 (Microsoft Corporation, 2021). Data processing and visualizations were performed using R statistical software (version 4.4.3. R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna Austria). The following R packages were used: readxl (for importing Excel files), openxlsx (for data export), dplyr and tidyr (for data manipulation), ggplot2 (for generating plots), and the tidyverse meta-package to streamline data wrangling and visualization workflows. Descriptive statistics were calculated to summarize participant demographics and survey responses. The distribution of continuous variables was assessed for normality through visual inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots. No statistical analyses were performed due to low number of data points.

Results

Cross-sectional survey

Initially, 28 respondents representing 20 individual Dutch hospitals filled in the cross-sectional survey. All respondents who started the survey agreed to participate and submitted the final survey page. According to the inclusion criteria of this study, only the 20 responses derived from hospital representatives were considered for eligibility. From these 20 responses, we excluded six more due to not having patients referred yet (n = 3) and representing the same hospital (n = 3). Finally, data from fourteen respondents representing fourteen individual hospitals were included in this study, of which seven were situated in academic hospitals and the remaining seven LFOs were based in general hospitals. All academic hospitals participated in the survey, while the non-participating hospitals therefore consisted exclusively of general hospitals (n = 62). No additional organizational characteristics of non-participating hospitals were available for comparison. In case of more than one respondent from one hospital, we used the most recent response from duplicate entries, assuming it reflected the most up-to-date information. As 17 hospitals established an ongoing LFO by the time of the survey of 14 hospitals, the response rate was 82%. The three hospitals that did not participate in the survey were general hospitals. These 14 hospitals referred their first patients between January 2019 and February 2024. The median [interquartile range] time of existence of the LFOs when filling in the survey was 1.2 [0.8–2.2] years.

Recruitment: Participating departments and healthcare professionals

The number of referring departments to the LFO ranged from 3 to 19, with a median of 8 [5–10] departments per hospital. The most frequently involved departments were internal medicine (n = 12), gastroenterology (n = 12), cardiology (n = 11) and gynecology (n = 11), while the department of radiotherapy was only involved in 2 hospitals. Figure 2 provides an overview of all referring departments per hospital type (academic vs. general). Within these departments, various healthcare professionals were authorized to refer patients to the LFO. Medical specialists and nurse specialists were the most frequently involved, in 12 and 11 hospitals, respectively. They were followed by residents (in or not in training) (n = 10), physician assistants (n = 6), allied health professionals (n = 5), and nurses (n = 4). There were no notable differences in the participating departments or referring professionals between academic and general hospitals. This observation was based on the visual presentation of the data, in which we did not identify different patterns in referring departments. Every department is equally represented when comparing academic hospitals to general hospitals (Fig. 2). Similar distributions were seen in the involved healthcare professionals (figure not shown).

Recruitment: Patient Eligibility criteria for referral to the LFO

Patient eligibility criteria for referral were defined based on both disease groups and patient-related factors. The disease groups varied considerably across LFOs. Patients with cardiovascular diseases, diabetes mellitus and overweight/obesity were often referred in 13/14 hospitals. Psychiatric patients were least frequently referred (in 2/14 hospitals). Additionally, 2 out of 14 hospitals referred children to the LFO, although the originating subdiscipline was not specified. Figure 3 shows the distribution of patient groups by diseases being referred by all LFOs. In most hospitals (11 out of 14), additional patient-related criteria were specified, including mostly lifestyle-related factors (e.g. smoking), overweight or obesity and sufficient motivation for changing lifestyle. Other criteria mentioned by several hospitals included place of residence, adult population, independence in daily living, and the ability to speak Dutch.

Recruitment: In-hospital communication strategies

The following distinct methods were used for communication regarding the LFO in the hospital included presentations at staff or educational meetings, intranet posts, emails, narrow casting and printed materials.

Screening process: prior to LFO consultation

Prior to the intake consultation, 8/14 (57%) LFOs utilize surveys and tools to assess patients’ health and lifestyle needs. Among these, the Positive Health questionnaire was the most commonly used (50% of LFOs), particularly by LFOs in general hospitals, in comparison to those in academic hospitals. The Supplementary material (see Table S1) provides an overview of these surveys. Additionally, 12/14 (85.7%) hospitals answered “yes” to the question whether there was a need for (additional) online questionnaires that identify lifestyle factors and factors that may hinder lifestyle interventions (e.g. social, psychological, hormonal, medicaments with a potential weight-inducing side effect) according to the clinical practice guidelines. Furthermore, some hospitals assess medication use (n = 10), anthropometry measures (n = 2), laboratory assessments (n = 1) and additional qualitative information through questionnaires (n = 10) prior to consultation. Medical information, including medical history and medication use, is mostly retrieved from electronic patient records (resp. 10/12 and 8/12), while the additional assessments were retrieved during consultation (when applicable).

Care delivery: Human resource allocation and capacity

Since the initiation of LFOs six years ago, a total of 5,036 patients has been referred to the LFO across all 14 hospitals. The median [IQR] number of referrals per hospital was 325 [156–360], corresponding to a median of 8 [5–15] referrals per week. These estimated were derived from two separate survey questions. To accommodate these referrals, lifestyle care coordinators have been appointed, representing a median of 1.3 [0.5–2.0] full-time equivalents (FTEs) per hospital. However, only 5/14 (36%) hospitals reported having sufficient capacity to meet the referral demand. Hospitals did not all apply similar job requirements for the lifestyle care coordinator, ranging from any (para)medical education to a specific lifestyle coaching degree. In addition to the lifestyle care coordinator, 6/14 (43%) hospitals employ a physician directly affiliated to the LFO.

Care delivery: Intake consultation at the LFO

Intake consultation times were relatively consistent across all hospitals, with a median duration of 55 [45–60] minutes per intake. In contrast, the time a lifestyle care coordinator needs to find a suitable referral after the intake varied considerably from 5 up to 60 min (see Fig. 4). During intake consultations, the aim is to find a suitable referral for patients based on their personal needs. For this aim, the LFO coordinator performs an elaborate screening on lifestyle factors, social factors, medical factors and psychological factors. Intake consultations are being conducted via live consultations, digital consultations and/or phone calls. During the intake consultation, all LFOs employ various methods and/or tools to structure the conversation. A recently developed tool, the so called Lifestyle Wheel [25] was the most widely applied pragmatic tool (in 86% of LFOs) by both academic and general hospitals. Motivational Interviewing was only mentioned by 2 out of 14 LFOs. A detailed overview of the conversation and screening tools used during the intake process can be found in the Supplementary materials.

Care delivery: Referral to community-based interventions

Referral pathways to lifestyle interventions varied considerably. To find a suitable referral, 7/14 (50%) LFOs use their own regional network, while 3/14 (21%) LFOs utilize online referral systems. Still, 3/14 (29%) hospitals have not yet established a regional network, and one hospital relies on general practitioners for referring patients. Most referrals are directed to community-based interventions outside the hospital, with half of the hospitals (7/14) reporting that they refer the majority of their patients (> 80%) to community-based interventions.

Care delivery: Follow-up evaluations at the LFO

Following referral, 12 out of 14 hospitals (86%) conduct follow-up evaluations, with a maximum follow-up duration of 12 months. Follow-up contact ranges from 1 to 3 consultations (either physical, digital or by telephone). None of the hospitals use a 100% physical approach to the follow-up visits. During follow-up, all hospitals (n = 14) track whether the referral was successful (including intervention initiation).

Care delivery: Feedback to involved healthcare professionals

Following intake, referral and follow-up, 13/14 (93%) of the LFOs provide feedback about the patient towards the healthcare provider within the hospital who referred the patients to the LFO, all by using the electronic patient record system. A total of 10/14 (71%) of the LFOs additionally also provide feedback towards the patients’ general practitioner in the community. This feedback mostly includes the result of the intake consultation, the referral destination, whether the patient continued the intervention, patients’ motivation or the start date of the intervention.

Context: Funding and capacity

Currently, short-term funding is available for 12/14 (86%) of LFOs. However, long-term funding is only secured in 3/14 (21%) of the hospitals. Long-term funding involved cost coverage for several years, with one hospital providing funding for up to six years. The funding sources varied from internal budgets to external coverage by the municipality, healthcare insurances or foundations – or a combination of those.

Discussion

The introduction of LFOs in the Netherlands in 2022 represents a novel service innovation, as it explicitly integrates lifestyle and social care within specialized medical care through a dedicated connection function. When compared to other lifestyle-based initiatives internationally, the Dutch approach on LFOs is unique because of its nationwide collaborative nature linking hospitals with municipalities and private organizations. By facilitating access to appropriate, locally available services and interventions, the LFO enhances the alignment of community-based care with formal care pathways, promoting continuity rather than fragmentation of care. However, the introduction of LFOs has been accompanied by considerable heterogeneity in the concept, making it difficult to compare interventions, effectiveness and efficacy. This study offers an overview of similarities, differences and future needs of currently running LFOs across the Netherlands.

This cross-sectional nation-wide survey was conducted during the early stages of the implementation process, as reflected by the limited number of operational LFOs at the time (17 out of 69 hospital organizations within the Netherlands; 25%) and the relatively recent establishment of these LFOs. The study gathered insights from 14 of the 17 operational LFOs, with the aim of supporting policymakers in developing this new concept in a uniform and sustainable manner. The similarities observed might help finding a more detailed definition of what constitutes an LFO. Conversely, the observed differences highlight aspects that may benefit from further (inter)national development, coordination, and support. Moreover, the distinctions between academic and general hospitals provide valuable insight into acceptable variations between hospital types, reflecting differences in organizational structure, patient populations, and available resources.

The recruitment from the healthcare professional to the LFO tends to be uniform, as the type of referring department and healthcare professionals referring to the LFO were consistent across centers. More variation was found in the total number of referring departments, which could be explained by the time of existence and differences in capacity and priorities between departments. LFOs that exist longer, had more time to facilitate referral from more departments. Additionally, the most frequently involved department types were non-surgical or a combination of the two. Surgical departments may prioritize acute care over chronic care which more often may comprise long-term lifestyle modifications. This could potentially lead to less emphasis on lifestyle advice and referral to LFO [26] but does provide a valuable opportunity.

The recruitment—based on eligibility and patient-related criteria—appeared to be consistent across LFOs. The most frequently referred patient categories were those with cancer and cardiovascular diseases, likely due to their high representation among hospitalized populations in the Netherlands [27]. Moreover, these conditions are known to exert substantial pressure on hospital systems globally [28, 29]. Uniformity was also found in the patient-related criteria (with motivation, BMI thresholds, lifestyle problems and place of residency as the most cited criteria). To inform healthcare professionals within the hospitals, different dissemination strategies were used. Using a combination of strategies is an effective way to improve compliance and knowledge about new health concepts [30]. Still, implementation of a new concept requires more than knowledge sharing and dissemination only, especially in complex organizational structures and changing behavior in clinical practice. Having a clear target group (medical departments, healthcare professionals and patients) is a fundamental principle in implementation science according to the Theory of Change (ToC), improving effectivity, sustainability and scalability [31].

Unlike the recruitment phase, the screening phase shows considerable variation across the LFOs. Different questionnaires were used to collect information on health outcomes from the patients’ perspective, with the Positive Health Questionnaire being mostly mentioned. However, with the recent introduction of the online screening and monitoring tool “checkcausesobesity.com”, we do expect more uniformity in the near future. The tool has been validated in an independent study that is currently in preparation for publication, and its algorithms have been incorporated into clinical practice guidelines on overweight and obesity [32]. These developments may facilitate greater uniformity in screening and monitoring practices in the near future.

In the care delivery phase, intake consultations appear to be organized more consistently by the general use of conversation tools. Nearly all LFOs utilize the Lifestyle Wheel, a pragmatic tool designed to provide insights for guiding patients toward a healthier lifestyle. Furthermore, only two LFOs mentioned using Motivational Interviewing during LFO consultations. Given its proven effectiveness in promoting healthy behaviors [33], broader implementation of Motivational Interviewing across all LFOs is essential. However, more LFOs may use Motivational Interviewing strategies than indicated, as the open question format could have led to underreporting. Furthermore, the time allocated for consultations demonstrated minimal variation across the LFOs, supporting the conclusion that the observed range of 45 to 60 min is a realistic standard. This time frame allows for the integration of Motivational Interviewing into consultations. At the same time, it underscores the importance of offering lifestyle support outside the consultation room of medical specialists, where time is limited. The use of an online screening tool may further increase efficiency in the future, as it saves time otherwise needed to assess all lifestyle, psychological, social and specific medical factors by providing structured information in advance. This allows the consultation to focus more on the areas where problems exist, health gains can be achieved, and—importantly—what best suits the patient.

In addition, the structure of follow-up visits and care delivery methods also showed little variability between centers. Most LFOs reported conducting between one and three follow-up evaluations and routinely providing feedback regarding the care process to both the referring physician and the general practitioner.

In some hospitals, an LFO physician contributes by interpreting lab results, advising specialists, and coordinating medication adjustments—especially for complex patients. While this adds clinical value, it also raises questions about cost-effectiveness and scalability. Future research should explore when physician involvement is most beneficial—for example, in the treatment of complex patients with multimorbidity, which requires holistic assessment, as described in a theory on interdisciplinary collaboration [34].

The referral process was the only aspect of the care delivery phase that appeared to be more fragmented across the LFOs. Both, the time lifestyle care coordinators need to find a suitable referral, and the use of referral systems varied considerably. The spread in the time required to establish referral could be related to the variety in needs of different patient groups, the variety of referral options and access to referral programs. As many patient groups have access to the LFO, it may be hard for lifestyle care coordinators to provide personalized advice and guidance following intake consultation. This asks for a more demarcated approach to the patient related criteria for inclusion than most LFOs currently apply. Additionally, this fragmentation may be attributed to a lack of collaboration and integration with community-based intervention providers, municipalities, and general practitioners—an often-cited barrier to effective transdisciplinary care [35]. A better insight into the referral options was also mentioned as an important need by both general and academic hospitals that was revealed from the qualitative analysis.

Successful implementation of LFOs is highly dependent on the availability of structural funding, which had not been secured in most cases at the time of the survey. To date, all LFOs operate on short-term funding initiatives or longer-term coverage for a fixed number of years. This challenge was mainly reflected in the quantitative data and less often mentioned in the open-ended questions, possibly due to underreporting or the diversity of respondents involved in the LFOs, who might had different priorities and interests. Long-term funding is critical when it comes to embedding, scaling up and professionalize innovations in healthcare [36], which were all needs that our qualitative analysis has identified. To ensure long-term funding, organizations (such as healthcare insurance companies) and hospital boards require evidence on cost-effectiveness, business case or budget impact analyses. Preliminary evidence from Rotterdam Medical Center indicates that LFOs appear to contribute to both cost savings and improved patient outcomes. For instance, reductions in healthcare expenditures have been observed through decreases in the number and dosage of expensive medications prescribed to patients referred by LFOs.

The most frequently cited need identified through our qualitative analysis was to strengthen the connection with community-based lifestyle interventions (networking), primarily to gain better insight into available referral options. In response, nationwide initiatives are underway to develop an online platform that enables lifestyle care coordinators to identify suitable community-based referral options for their patients.

Strengths

This survey offered the first step towards a unified approach for LFOs in the Netherlands. Recently, part of these results has been used to develop a handbook including a roadmap for the implementation and organization of a LFO, in close collaboration with the Dutch Lifestyle in Healthcare Consortium (in Dutch: Coalitie Leefstijl in de Zorg) [37]. This contributes to a stronger support base from umbrella organizations and funding agencies, who play a crucial role in structural implementation. The nationwide scope of this survey enhances the generalizability of the findings across the entire country. Another key strength concerns the timing, which was conducted at an early stage of the implementation process. This enables existing LFOs to make timely adaptations and supports new LFOs to adopt sustainable structures from the start in a uniform matter.

Limitations

This study captures only a portion of the ongoing developments within LFOs and does not comprehensively represent all LFO implementations, particularly those in general hospitals. Nevertheless, given the early stage of development of LFOs in the Netherlands, academic hospitals appear to fulfil a pioneering role in initiating such innovations, from which general hospitals may subsequently draw valuable lessons. While the response rate was 14/17, offering a realistic overview of running LFOs, we did not evaluate the LFOs that were under construction by the time of the survey (n = 11). Even though this was beyond the scope of our study, it would be relevant to gain information from those initiatives. The limited sample size should be viewed in light of the early developmental stage of the LFO innovation. Because few LFOs were operational at the time of data collection, our focus was on capturing the breadth of perspectives from all available cases. Although full saturation in the traditional sense may not have been achieved, recurring themes indicated a sufficient level of conceptual saturation for an exploratory study of this nature. Moreover, we must consider that new LFOs could have been established during the study period. Another limitation includes the risk of reporting bias, as this study was based on self-reported data from different perspectives, including project leaders and lifestyle care coordinators. However, the self-report approach enabled us to capture participants’ current perspectives and contextual understanding of how LFOs are implemented in practice, which would not have been possible through registry data alone.

Future perspective

A national cohort study is currently underway to assess the (cost-)effectiveness of referral via LFOs in the Netherlands. This study aims to provide insights into patient health outcomes and health-related quality of life improvements following LFO referral. This study is in line with the need for data collection, which was revealed from our qualitative analysis. Parallel to this, the “Lifestyle Front Office For Integrating Lifestyle Medicine in the Treatment of Patients (LOFIT)” project [11] is evaluating the (cost-)effectiveness of LFOs with a specific focus on a selection of diseases. Furthermore, we will gain insight into the current barriers and facilitations for implementation from the perspective of the lifestyle care coordinators by in-depth interviews in our upcoming study. We will use the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) to guide our analysis [38]. Both the current and forthcoming studies are part of the broader project “Learning Network for Implementation and Optimization of LFOs in Hospitals (LILLo),” which promotes nationwide collaboration and the development of network strategies. The overarching goals of LILLo are to investigate the further implementation of LFOs, assess practice variation, and generate actionable recommendations for optimal implementation in Dutch hospitals. This will also include assessing perspectives of general practitioners and patients, which was not included in the current study in which we focused on the perspective of LFO project leaders. Furthermore, considering the international interest in integrating lifestyle into clinical care, the concept of an LFO may be developed abroad as well.

Conclusion

This study highlights both shared features and key differences across running LFOs. LFOs are currently showing uniformity in the duration and interpretation of LFO consultations, and the involvement of both surgical and non-surgical specialties. However, notable variation was found in referral systems, the structure of collaborative networks, and the degree of interdisciplinary cooperation. These findings underscore the need for further harmonization and knowledge exchange to optimize the development and implementation of LFOs nationwide and offer opportunities for international hospitals to adopt or adapt similar integrated care approaches. This is of great need, since LFOs reflect a growing recognition of the importance of integrating lifestyle and (psycho)social help in hospital care settings.
