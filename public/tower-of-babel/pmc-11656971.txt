Association between glottis screen location and intubation difficulty: a retrospective video laryngoscopy study
Authors: Kai-Yuan Cheng, Pang Hsu Liu, Yung-Cheng Su, Yen-Yu Chen, Ya-Ni Yeh, Jih-Chun Lin, Ming-Jen Tsai
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn emergency settings, difficult intubations often occur unexpectedly despite pre-intubation assessments. Traditional glottic view scoring systems for direct laryngoscope may not apply to video laryngoscopy. With video laryngoscopy, the vertical location of the glottis on the monitor can be clearly defined. If the glottis location is associated with intubation difficulty, it could serve as a simple indicator for anticipating intubation challenges. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between the glottis screen location during video laryngoscopy-guided intubation and the success and timing of the first-attempt intubation.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed laryngoscopy recordings from adult intubations in the emergency department of a tertiary teaching hospital in Chiayi, Taiwan, using the C-MAC video laryngoscope between March 2020 and April 2023. The vertical screen location of the vocal cords, determined by the arytenoid cartilage position after laryngeal blade engagement, was categorized into upper and lower locations for analysis. The primary outcome was first-attempt intubation success within 90 s, analyzed using Kaplan-Meier survival curves and multivariable Cox proportional hazard analysis.ResultsAmong 209 laryngoscopy records, 113 had the arytenoid in the lower field of view and 96 in the upper field. Kaplan-Meier analysis showed a significantly lower cumulative success rate for intubations with a higher arytenoid location (log-rank test, P < 0.001). Background

Since its introduction in the early 2000s, video laryngoscopy (VL) has seen increased usage due to its advantages over direct laryngoscopy (DL) [1–3]. VL incorporates a camera on the blade that transmits images to a monitor, providing a closer and wider view of the laryngeal structures compared to DL [1]. Research consistently shows that VL improves first-attempt intubation success rates across various clinical settings, making it a preferred method for both routine and difficult intubations [2–7]. Its ability to overcome anatomical limitations and provide a superior glottic view further highlights its advantages over DL [6, 8].

Traditional peri-intubation scoring systems, such as the Cormack-Lehane (CL) grading system and the percentage of glottic opening (POGO), were developed for DL to classify the glottic view and assess intubation difficulty [9–12]. However, their effectiveness for VL is not well-established. A randomized clinical trial suggests that a favorable CL grade does not always ensure easy endotracheal tube passage during VL [13]. Additionally, POGO is subjective and less accurate unless there is full visualization of the larynx, as it requires estimating the unseen portion of the glottic opening [14].

Since the VL screen view differs from DL, scoring systems like CL and POGO may not be fully applicable during VL intubation. It is also unclear where on the VL screen the glottis should be centered for optimal intubation. Some studies have analyzed VL recordings to identify intubation errors and improve technique [15–17]. For example, one study found that inadequate lifting force when advancing the blade to the vallecula was a common error, and about 25% of intubations encountered tube delivery issues despite the tube tip being positioned at the glottic opening [15]. These observations suggest that these performance errors may be related to the vocal cords appearing elevated on the screen.

By directly observing the laryngeal structure on the VL monitor, we can precisely define the vertical location of the glottis. If this location correlates with intubation difficulty or duration, it could serve as a valuable indicator for intubation challenges. This research aims to explore the relationship between the glottis’ screen location during VL intubation and the outcomes related to the success and timing of first-attempt intubations.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a retrospective review of deidentified VL recordings of adult intubations at Ditmanson Medical Foundation Chiayi Christian Hospital, a tertiary teaching hospital where the emergency department (ED) performs approximately 500 intubations annually. The ED is equipped with a C-MAC VL (Karl Storz Endoscope, Tuttlingen, Germany), which is used in about 75% of intubations, routinely with a standard-geometry Macintosh #4 blade. A hyperangulated blade is reserved for cases of failed intubation with the Macintosh blade. As a teaching hospital, we conduct biannual airway management training for emergency physicians to ensure familiarity with intubation instruments. Our intubation protocol includes at least three minutes of preoxygenation before rapid sequence induction, with a nasal cannula set at a flush rate for apneic oxygenation during the apnea phase. In most cases, a malleable stylet is routinely inserted into the endotracheal tube unless the operator specifies otherwise, typically shaped straight up to the cuff with a 25-degree bend at the distal end. Intubation decisions are made by attending physicians, with senior residents performing approximately 75% of first attempts under supervision.

The C-MAC VL system includes a video recording function for subsequent review, though no protocol specifies which ED intubations should be recorded. This study retrospectively analyzed deidentified laryngoscope recordings from emergency intubation using the C-MAC VL. The protocol was approved by the hospital’s institutional review board (IRB2023028), with informed consent waived due to the retrospective design. This study adheres to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [18].

Selection of participants

We reviewed all available C-MAC VL recordings of emergency intubations from March 2020 to April 2023, focusing on the first attempt in each video. If multiple attempts were recorded in a single intubation video, only the first attempt was included in the analysis. Videos in which the arytenoids were obscured by secretions, vomit, or blood were excluded. First-attempt success was defined as the successful placement of an endotracheal tube using a single laryngoscope insertion followed by a single endotracheal tube insertion [19].

Measurements

We recorded various intubation findings, including the glottis screen location (Supplementary Fig. 1), blade tip placement (Supplementary Fig. 2), modified CL grade, epiglottis shape (Supplementary Fig. 3), glottis swelling, foreign body obstruction, regurgitation, additional techniques used, and intubation time.

The glottis screen location was determined using the arytenoid cartilage as a reference point and classified into zones I-IV based on the vertical location of the arytenoid cartilage after blade engagement and just before tube insertion. Zones I and II represented a lower location, and zones III and IV represented an upper location (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1). Blade tip placement included engaging the midline vallecula fold, the tongue base, directly lifting the epiglottis, and downfolding the epiglottis (Supplementary Fig. 2A–D). Other techniques recorded included the use of a bougie, GlideRite stylet (Verathon Inc., Bothell, WA), external laryngeal manipulation (ELM) (observing the glottis moving downward after blade engagement), and suction or foreign body removal with Magill forceps. Intubation time was defined as the duration from blade insertion into the mouth to endotracheal tube passing through the vocal cords. For unsuccessful intubations, the intubation time was recorded from blade insertion until the endotracheal tube was removed from the field of view, with these cases classified as failure events.

Two trained emergency physicians reviewed the videos and recorded data. Discrepancies were resolved by a third reviewer. No additional patient details were recorded due to the lack of identifiable information in the videos.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were first-attempt intubation success within 90 s, overall first-attempt success (without time restriction), and intubation time for successful attempts.

Data analysis

We assessed the influence of glottis screen location on intubation duration and success by comparing lower (Zone I & II) and upper (Zone III & IV) arytenoid locations. To determine the required sample size, we conducted a preliminary analysis using the first-year videos (March 2020 to February 2021), which included 64 videos. Of these, 38 intubations (59.4%) had the arytenoid located in the lower locations, while 26 (40.6%) had the arytenoid in the upper locations. The first-attempt success rate within 90 s was 81.6% (n = 31) for intubations with lower arytenoid locations and 61.5% (n = 16) for those with upper arytenoid locations. We powered the study to detect a 20.1% difference in first-attempt success rate within 90 s between lower and upper arytenoid locations (odds ratio of 0.3602). Using a two-tailed test with a significance level of 5% and a power of 80%, we calculated that a total of 178 videos would be required to achieve statistical power [20]. To meet this requirement, we included 3 years of intubation videos in this study.

For intergroup comparisons, categorical variables were presented as numbers (percentages) and analyzed using the Chi-square test, while continuous variables were presented as medians (interquartile range) and assessed with the Mann-Whitney U test. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were used to evaluate the time to successful intubation based on arytenoid locations, with differences assessed using log-rank tests. Univariable Cox models analyzed associations between each variable and first-attempt success within 90 s. Multivariable Cox models with forward-selection, incorporating variables with P < 0.1 from the univariable analysis, assessed adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) for successful intubation within 90 s. The Schoenfeld test was used to examine the proportional hazards assumption after model fitting.

For sensitivity analyses, univariable and multivariable logistic regressions with forward-selection calculated odds ratios (ORs) for first-attempt intubation success within 90 s and overall first-attempt success across different arytenoid locations. Additional Kaplan-Meier and multivariable Cox analyses were conducted for subgroups with adequate vallecula engagement, optimal modified CL grades (1 and 2a), and those not receiving ELM to exclude performance errors due to inadequate vallecula engagement [16], correlation with glottic view [6], and the effect of ELM on glottis screen location [21, 22]. All analyses were performed using Stata 17 (StataCorp LLC), with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.

Results

During the study period, 287 intubation video records were reviewed. After excluding 73 non-first attempts (e.g., second or third attempts) and 5 instances where the arytenoid could not be identified, 209 intubation records were eligible for analysis. Of these, 113 episodes had the arytenoid in the lower location (18 in Zone I, 95 in Zone II), and 96 in the upper location (76 in Zone III, 20 in Zone IV, Fig. 2).

Notable differences in intubation findings between the groups were observed. In the lower location group, 84.7% had the blade tip engaged in the midline vallecula fold, compared to 52.08% in the upper location group. The upper location group had higher instances of direct lifting of the epiglottis (29.17% vs. 6.19%) and downfolding of the epiglottis (16.67% vs. 6.19%) (P < 0.001). Epiglottis shapes also differed significantly (P = 0.015), and techniques like bougie and GlidRite stylet use were more common in the upper location group (P = 0.044). There was no significant difference in modified CL grades between the groups (P = 0.115).

Kaplan-Meier curves for successful intubation within 90 s showed a significantly lower cumulative success rate in the upper arytenoid location group compared to the lower location group (log-rank test, P < 0.001, Fig. 3A). A dose-response trend was observed when arytenoid locations were divided into four zones (log-rank test, P < 0.001, Fig. 3B).

In univariable Cox analyses (Table 2), factors such as downfolding the epiglottis, higher modified CL grades (2b and 3), glottis swelling, foreign body obstructing, and upper arytenoid location were associated with a lower likelihood of successful intubation within 90 s. Multivariable Cox analyses confirmed arytenoid location as a significant predictor of successful intubation within 90 s, whether divided into two (model 1) or four groups (model 2). The adjusted HR for the upper arytenoid location was 0.55 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.38–0.79, P < 0.001) compared to the lower location group (model 1). The adjusted HRs for zones III and IV were 0.54 (95% CI, 0.36–0.79; P = 0.002) and 0.37 (95% CI, 0.17–0.79; P = 0.011) compared to zone II (model 2), respectively. The Schoenfeld test showed no violation of the proportional hazard assumption (P = 0.135 in model 1 and 0.134 in model 2).

Sensitivity analyses using multivariable logistic regressions showed that the upper arytenoid location group had significantly lower odds of first-attempt success within 90 s (adjusted OR, 0.41; 95% CI, 0.19–0.90; P = 0.026, Table 3) and a trend towards lower overall first-attempt success (adjusted OR, 0.50; 95% CI, 0.23–1.07; P = 0.075, Supplementary Table 1). Additional survival analyses in patient subgroups (those with adequate vallecula engagement, optimal modified CL grades, and no ELM) yielded similar findings, with the upper arytenoid location group consistently showing a lower cumulative success rate (Supplementary Fig. 4). The respective adjusted HRs for the upper arytenoid location were 0.55 (95% CI, 0.36–0.84), 0.60 (95% CI, 0.39–0.92), and 0.56 (95% CI, 0.38–0.82) compared to the lower location group (Supplementary Tables 2, 3 and 4).

Discussion

Although anesthesiology guidelines recommend comprehensive airway evaluations before tracheal intubation [23], the urgency of emergency intubation often makes thorough pre-intubation assessments impractical [2]. Previous studies, including systematic review, have shown that most difficult intubations are unanticipated despite the use of current airway examination frameworks, highlighting the limitations of pre-intubation assessments in emergency settings [4, 24]. Traditional scoring systems like CL and POGO, which were developed for DL, assess intubation difficulty by evaluating the glottic view. However, there is a need for more accurate and intuitive methods tailored to VL. While earlier research indicated that the POGO score had higher reliability than CL grade in documenting glottic view during DL and VL [12, 25], a recent study has challenged the assumptions that POGO is a quantitative and objective score. This is because the exact proportion of the glottic view cannot be reliably measured without full visualization of the laryngeal [14]. In our retrospective analysis of VL intubations, we found that the glottis screen location, determined by the arytenoid cartilage’s location after blade engagement and just before tube insertion, significantly predicts first-attempt intubation success and timing. An elevated arytenoid location correlates with a lower likelihood of successful intubation within 90 s. Sensitivity analyses, excluding cases with suboptimal glottic views (CL grade 2b and 3), ELM impact, and performance errors from inadequate vallecular engagement, yielded consistent results. These findings suggest that assessing the glottis screen location after blade engagement could provide a simple and rapid method to anticipate intubation challenges in VL.

To enhance first-attempt intubation success rates, VL is recommended [3, 5, 7]. However, even with a superior glottic view with, VL does not guarantee an easy intubation [13]. Challenges can arise from patient-related factors and operator performance errors [15, 26]. Greenland K.B.‘s three-column model categorizes difficult airways anatomically: the anterior column includes issues like an enlarged tongue or limited mouth opening; the middle column involves airway obstructions; and the posterior column relates to limited neck mobility [26]. Difficulties in the anterior and posterior columns can hinder blade tilting and lifting, potentially resulting in a higher glottis location on the screen. Weingart S.D. et al. identified key performance errors in ED intubations using VL, with inadequate blade lifting force and improper vallecula engagement being the most common, occurring in 45% and 40% of attempts, respectively [15]. These errors may also contribute to an elevated glottis location on the screen. Based on these finding, we suggest that the glottis screen location after blade engagement may reflect both patient anatomical characteristics and operator performance factors. The higher the glottis appears on the screen, the more anteriorly positioned the larynx is for the same blade tip’s position, which may necessitate a sharper curvature of the stylet-intubating tube assembly, hindering tube advancement and placement. Therefore, when an elevated glottis location is observed, it is essential to first address any performance errors. If necessary, applying measures such as ELM [21, 22], using auxiliary instruments like the GlideRite stylet [27], and preparing a rescue device (e.g. video intubation stylet) [28] are recommended, especially for patients with anatomically difficult airways.

Previous studies have indicated that engaging the midline vallecular fold with the blade tip is crucial for achieving better glottic view and higher intubation success rates [16, 17]. Since vallecular engagement can influence glottis screen location, we included this variable in our primary analysis and conducted a sensitivity analysis on patients with adequate vallecular engagement. The results were consistent. A randomized trial using the GlideScope VL found that a deliberately restricted glottic view, rather than a full view, is associated with faster and easier intubation [13]. In Table 1, we observed no significant difference in CL grades between groups with upper and lower arytenoid locations, suggesting that glottis screen location may not correlate with CL grading. In the multivariable Cox model, even after adjusting for CL grade, glottis screen location remained significantly related to intubation success rates. Our sensitivity analysis on patients with an optimal glottic view also confirmed this association. These findings suggest that, beyond assessing the degree of glottic opening, evaluating glottis screen location provides reliable guidance for anticipating intubation challenges with VL.

The application of ELM to improve the glottic view during DL intubation is well-documented [29, 30], with similar benefits reported in VL for specific patient groups. For example, ELM enhances the glottic view during tracheal intubation for thyroid surgery and reduces cervical spine motion during VL intubation under manual in-line stabilization [22, 31]. Since ELM can affect the glottis screen location, it is often applied when the vocal cords appear elevated or have a suboptimal CL grade. In this study, to minimize ELM’s impact on our outcomes, we recorded the glottis screen location after blade engagement and just before tube insertion, reflecting the post-ELM glottis position. A sensitivity analysis excluding patients who received ELM yielded results consistent with our main findings.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective study, unmeasured confounders may have influenced the results. Second, being a single-center investigation, the adequacy of the laryngeal view depends on the operator’s experience, potentially limiting the generalizability of our findings. However, all emergency physician in this study received regular training to ensure adequate laryngoscopy performance. We also conducted a sensitivity analysis excluding cases with inadequate vallecular engagement to minimize the impact of operator performance errors, and the results remained consistent. Therefore, although this factor may introduce some bias, it should not affect the overall conclusions regarding the association between glottis screen location and intubation success. Third, the study focused exclusively on intubations using the C-MAC VL, so the findings may not apply to other VL brands. Nonetheless, the method of categorizing arytenoid location by screen position should be applicable to other VLs with similar designs. Additionally, in this retrospective video review, we were unable to identify whether a Macintosh or hyperangulated blade was used, which could potentially impact glottic visibility and positioning. However, our ED protocol routinely uses a Macintosh #4 blade, reserving the hyperangulated blade as a rescue device. Further studies controlling for this variable may be warranted. Fourth, the lack of identifiable information in the laryngoscopy videos prevented analysis of additional patient data, limiting our ability to correlate specific conditions with outcomes. However, key predictors of difficult airways identified by recent machine learning models were included in our analysis [32]. Although many patient anatomical conditions, such as limited mouth opening or restricted neck mobility, can influence intubation difficulty, these variables are often difficult to accurately define. In our opinion, as previously mentioned in the discussion, these anatomical factors may already be reflected in the glottis location on the screen. Finally, not all VL intubations were recorded, which may introduce selection bias. While there’s no evidence of bias towards any glottis location group, the recorded case may represent more challenging airways, as these instances might have been selectively recorded. This limitation is similar to those in previous studies [16, 17].

Conclusions

While VL improves airway visualization and intubation success rates over DL, difficult intubations can still occur. In emergency settings, a quick, real-time assessment method is crucial for identifying and addressing intubation challenges. This study suggests that assessing glottis location on the video display after blade tip engagement may be an effective indicator of potential intubation difficulties with VL. Further research is necessary to validate these findings and to compare glottis screen location with other scale for evaluating intubation difficulty in VL.
