Chondromyxoid fibroma in a 24-year-old female: A case report highlighting its rarity, diagnostic challenges, and importance of surgical management
Authors: Jackson Kakooza, Cornerious Ssekiwala, Jama Saleban Mohamed, Musafiri Simba Lionel, Joseph Ssebamala, Arthur Serumaga, Samuel Oledo, John Dalton Masumba, Sam Kalungi, Shaban Abdullah, Catherine R Lewis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChondromyxoid fibroma (CMF) is an uncommon benign bone tumor, involving less than 1% of all bone neoplasms and is slightly more predominant in males. This case report describes a 24-year-old female patient who presented with a painless, progressively enlarging swelling on the left upper leg for six years. Radiographic imaging revealed a lytic lesion with a soap bubble appearance, well-defined margins, and endosteal scalloping in the proximal tibia. Histological analysis confirmed CMF, characterized by chondromyxoid lobules with fibroblastic proliferation and no evidence of malignancy. Surgical excision of the 6 x 8 cm tumor, involving the fibula and tibia, was performed without complications. This case highlights the rarity of CMF in females, its diagnostic challenges, and the efficacy of surgical management, emphasizing clinical, radiographic, and histopathological features.Keywords: bone neoplasm, case report, chondromyxoid fibroma, metaphysis, soap bubble appearance

Introduction

Chondromyxoid fibroma (CMF) is a benign cartilaginous-derived bone tumor, first described by Jaffe and Lichtenstein in 1948. 1 It accounts for approximately 0.5%–1% of all bone tumors and typically impacts individuals in their second to third decades, with a male-to-female predominance of approximately 1.5:1.2,3 The tumor typically develops in the metaphyseal region of long bones, principally the proximal tibia, and presents as a painless or mildly painful mass of long-standing duration. 4 Despite its benign nature, CMF has a local recurrence rate of up to 26% and a small risk of malignant transformation.3,4

Radiographically, CMF appears as a lytic lesion with a soap bubble appearance, well-defined margins, and endosteal scalloping, similar to malignant tumors such as chondrosarcoma.5,6 Computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are more reliable in making the diagnosis. Plain radiography demonstrates similarities to other more common diagnoses such as chondrosarcoma, chondroblastoma, giant cell tumor, or aneurysmal bone cysts. 7 We present a case of CMF in a 24-year-old female, detailing its clinical presentation, diagnostic workup and management, and the significance of its occurrence in a less commonly affected demographic. This case adds value to the existing CMF literature by documenting the clinical, radiographic, and histopathological features in a young African female, a demographic and geographic context that is underrepresented in the published literature, and by highlighting the outcomes of en bloc excision involving the proximal fibula without reconstruction in a resource-limited setting. The reporting of this case conforms to the CARE guidelines. 8

Case report

A 24-year-old female presented with a six-year history of an enlarging, painless swelling on the proximal third of the left leg. She reported no history of pain, trauma, joint or muscle discomfort, fever, weight loss, or night sweats. Her past medical and family history were unremarkable. Vital signs were normal. Physical examination revealed a round, firm, non-tender mass measuring 6 x 6 cm on the left leg, fixed to the underlying bone, with normal overlying skin and a longitudinal scar from a prior biopsy. Mild knee joint stiffness was present, but knee and ankle movements were preserved, and gait was normal.

Radiographic imaging of the left leg showed a lytic lesion in the proximal tibia’s metaphyseal region, with a soap bubble appearance, well-defined sclerotic margins, and endosteal scalloping without cortical breach or soft tissue extension (Figure 1). A prior biopsy revealed chondromyxoid lobules with fibroblastic proliferation, hypercellular peripheries, and no atypia or malignancy, confirming CMF.

Pre-operative radiograph demonstrating a lytic lesion in the proximal tibia. There are well-defined sclerotic margins, endosteal scalloping, and a soap bubble appearance.

Written informed consent was obtained as per institutional policies for excision of the tumor and any other indicated procedures, as well as publication of clinical findings and imaging if indicated. Advanced cross-sectional imaging was not performed preoperatively due to financial limitations. However, intraoperative assessment revealed tumor extension into the proximal fibula with preservation of the posterior neurovascular bundle. The anterior tibial artery and deep peroneal nerve remained separated from the tumor by an intact fascial plane. The common peroneal nerve was identified at the neck of the fibula prior to tumor dissection and carefully dissected free from the tumor capsule throughout the procedure; it was preserved in continuity with no evidence of intraoperative traction injury. The lateral collateral ligament (LCL) was found to be uninvolved by the tumor at its fibular attachment; it was carefully protected during resection and did not require reconstruction. Further dissection was performed, revealing a 6 x 8 cm bone mass involving the proximal fibula extending to the tibia (Figure 2). En bloc excision removed the tumor with fibular fragments, and hemostasis was achieved using bone wax. The tumor’s extraosseous component was minimal, confirmed by an intact periosteal membrane, which aligned with the radiographic absence of soft tissue involvement. A drain was placed, and the procedure was uncomplicated.

(A) Intraoperative photograph demonstrating a mass over the tibia and fibula. (B) Complete excision of the tibial tumor.

Histopathology demonstrated an encapsulated cartilaginous tumor composed of lobules separated by mononuclear spindle cells containing well-formed lacunar spaces with bland looking chondrocytes, consistent with CMF (Figure 3). Areas of variable cellularity were present, with mild pleomorphism in the peripheral zones of lobules. However, no dysplasia, increased mitotic activity, or malignant features were identified. Final pathological examination confirmed negative margins with no evidence of satellite lesions or neurovascular involvement. Immunohistochemical studies were not performed in this case as the morphological features on histopathology were diagnostic, demonstrating the characteristic lobular architecture, chondromyxoid matrix, and cellular zonation pattern pathognomonic for CMF.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. (A) 40X magnification and (B) 200X magnification demonstrating an encapsulated cartilaginous tumor composed of lobules separated by mononuclear spindle cells. There are well-formed lacunar spaces with bland-looking chondrocytes. There are areas of hypo- and hyper-cellularity with mild pleomorphism. The stroma is fibrocartilaginous to chondromyxoid.

Postoperatively, the patient was kept non-weight-bearing for 24 hours, then commenced partial weight-bearing with crutch support on postoperative day two. No postoperative immobilization was applied. A supervised physiotherapy program was initiated on day two focusing on knee range-of-motion and quadriceps strengthening exercises. Neurological assessment at discharge confirmed intact motor and sensory function with no evidence of foot drop or sensory deficit in the distribution of the common peroneal nerve. Knee stability was preserved, with no clinical laxity on varus stress testing. The drain was removed on postoperative day two, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day five with analgesia and wound care instructions. At the six week follow-up visit, the patient reported no pain, had achieved full weight-bearing status, and demonstrated full knee range of motion. Wound healing was complete and the surgical site was clean.

Discussion

CMF is a rare benign bone tumor with a male predominance.4,9 Its incidence in females is less common, making this case noteworthy for highlighting diagnostic and management considerations in this demographic. The patient’s presentation with a painless, slow-growing mass over six years is consistent with the typical clinical course of CMF, which is oftentimes asymptomatic unless associated with mechanical symptoms, joint dysfunction, or pathological fracture. 5 The absence of systemic symptoms, such as weight loss or night sweats, further supports the benign nature of the lesion.

CMF is characterized radiographically by a well-defined lytic lesion with a soap bubble appearance, sclerotic margins, and endosteal scalloping, as observed in this patient’s proximal tibia radiograph. 7 These characteristics differentiate CMF from other lesions, such as aneurysmal bone cysts, which may show fluid-fluid levels, or giant cell tumor, which more likely occurs in the epiphysis.4,10 However, the radiographic overlap with low-grade chondrosarcoma, which may also present as a lytic lesion with cortical thinning and deep endosteal scalloping, necessitates histopathological confirmation.9,11 In this case, the absence of cortical breach or soft tissue extension on X-ray further supported a benign diagnosis, though advanced imaging, such as MRI, could have provided additional details on soft tissue involvement or intramedullary extent, which is recommended in complex cases. 7

Histologically, CMF is defined by lobules of chondromyxoid matrix with multiple fibroblastic and myxoid elements and hypercellular peripheries, 2 as seen in this patient’s resected specimen. The lack of cellular atypia and increased mitotic activity distinguishes CMF from chondrosarcoma, which may show pleomorphism and invasive growth. 1 The differential diagnosis includes chondroblastoma, which typically occurs in the epiphysis containing chicken-wire calcifications, 12 and enchondroma, which lacks the myxoid component of CMF. 4

Differences in bone growth patterns or hormonal factors may be responsible for the rarity of CMF in females, though the exact etiology is unknown. 3 Genetic studies have demonstrated recurrent abnormalities in chromosome 6, particularly 6q13 and q25 in CMF cases, but these are not sex-specific. 13 Male predominance is not consistently reported, warranting further research into sex-based disparities.3,14 This case emphasizes the importance of considering CMF in females with lytic bone lesions, as delayed diagnosis could lead to complications.

Surgical management is the basis of CMF treatment, with options including curettage with or without adjuvant therapy (e.g., cryotherapy or bone cement) or en bloc resection for larger or recurrent lesions.1,14 In this case, en bloc excision was chosen due to the tumor’s size and involvement of both the fibula and tibia, ensuring clear margins and minimizing recurrence risk. Recurrence rates range from 10%-20% and are primarily associated with incomplete resection or curettage alone.2,9

Several prior case reports have described CMF involving the proximal fibula and highlight comparable surgical challenges. HemanthaKumar and Sathish 14 reported a case of CMF of the fibular head in a 38-year-old female treated with excisional biopsy, similarly confirming en bloc resection as the treatment of choice for expendable bones such as the fibula; their case demonstrated no recurrence at short-term follow-up, consistent with our findings. 14 Bhamra et al. 7 reported a series of 22 CMF cases and noted a recurrence rate of 22.7% with curettage, reinforcing the preference for en bloc excision when anatomically feasible.

A key surgical consideration in proximal fibula resection is management of the LCL and the common peroneal nerve. In the present case, the LCL’s fibular attachment was found to be uninvolved and was preserved without reconstruction; this is consistent with reports in which small or distally limited fibular head tumors permit ligament-sparing resection. 15 However, in cases where the fibular head is more extensively involved, LCL reconstruction using autograft or allograft with suture anchor fixation may be required to restore lateral knee stability, as described for proximal fibula resection in other tumors. 7 The common peroneal nerve, given its intimate anatomical relationship to the fibular neck, was at potential risk during resection in this case. It was identified proximally, meticulously dissected throughout the operative field, and preserved intact, with no postoperative motor or sensory deficit detected. This aligns with best surgical practice as described in prior CMF and proximal fibula tumor series, which consistently emphasize nerve identification and protection as a critical intraoperative step.2,9 The absence of adjuvant therapies, such as bone grafting, was appropriate in this case, given the limited structural compromise post-resection, though grafting may be necessary in larger defects to restore bone integrity. 7

Postoperative outcomes in CMF are favorable, with most patients achieving full functional recovery, as seen in this patient’s ability to bear weight on the second postoperative day and resume normal activity within weeks.7,9 The rapid recovery and lack of complications highlight the efficacy of en bloc resection for well-circumscribed tumors. However, long-term follow-up is paramount, as recurrences may occur years after initial treatment especially if residual tumor remains. 14 Regular imaging, typically with X-rays or MRI, is recommended to monitor for recurrence, with intervals of 3–6 months for the first two years and annually thereafter. 7

Conclusion

This case report details the diagnosis and management of CMF in a 24-year-old female, a demographic less commonly affected by this rare benign bone tumor. The patient’s painless, slow-growing mass, classic radiographic findings, and histological confirmation of CMF highlight the importance of comprehensive evaluation to distinguish it from malignant lesions. En bloc excision resulted in an excellent outcome, with rapid functional recovery and no complications. The infrequency of CMF in females emphasizes the need for clinical vigilance and thorough diagnostic workup. Long-term follow-up is essential to monitor for recurrence, ensuring sustained disease-free status.
