Dietary vitamin B1 intake and stroke risk in U.S. adults
Authors: Zhi Li, Min Fu, Li Wang, Qiong Chen, Guang Yao, Qianhua Dai, Lili Jiang, Yangzhou Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Stroke imposes a substantial global burden of mortality and long-term disability, and its occurrence is driven by diverse vascular risk factors. As an essential micronutrient, vitamin B1 (thiamin) has been implicated in cardiovascular protection, yet whether habitual dietary intake of this vitamin relates to stroke occurrence remains incompletely understood. The present study therefore examined the association between dietary vitamin B1 intake and stroke risk using nationally representative data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey. This cross-sectional analysis included 11,750 participants aged 20 years or older drawn from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey cycles of 2005 to 2018. Dietary vitamin B1 intake was divided into quartiles, and stroke status was ascertained from self-reported physician diagnoses. Weighted multivariable logistic regression models, adjusted sequentially for demographic, behavioral, and clinical covariates, were fitted to estimate odds ratios for stroke across intake levels, and restricted cubic spline regression was used to characterize the dose–response pattern. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression was further applied to screen predictors for a stroke risk prediction model. An inverse association was observed between dietary vitamin B1 intake and stroke. In the fully adjusted model, participants in the highest intake quartile had 44% lower odds of stroke than those in the lowest quartile (odds ratio = 0.56, 95% CI: 0.41–0.89), and this inverse gradient remained materially consistent across strata of sex, age, race/ethnicity, and major clinical conditions. Dose–response modeling revealed a nonlinear pattern in which stroke odds declined steeply until intake reached approximately 1.58 mg/d, after which the decline became more gradual. A prediction model built on least absolute shrinkage and selection operator-selected clinical variables, including vitamin B1, achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.771. In this nationally representative sample of U.S. adults, greater dietary vitamin B1 intake was independently associated with lower odds of stroke, lending support to a potential cerebrovascular benefit of thiamin. Prospective cohort and interventional studies are warranted to confirm this association and to elucidate the biological pathways involved.

1. Introduction

Stroke arises when pathological processes affecting the cerebral vasculature – including arterial embolism, small-vessel disease, and cerebral infarction – abruptly disrupt normal neurological function.[1] Clinically, strokes fall into hemorrhagic and ischemic categories, which account for roughly 20% and 80% of all events, respectively.[2] Epidemiological estimates indicate that approximately 15 million people worldwide suffer a stroke each year, and close to 6 million of these events are fatal.[3] Although advancing age remains a well-established determinant of stroke, a worrying rise in incidence has lately been documented among younger adults. Hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and tobacco use are well-recognized contributors to stroke occurrence.[4]

Members of the B-vitamin family exert indispensable physiological effects throughout the central and peripheral nervous systems.[5] When B-vitamin status is inadequate, circulating homocysteine tends to accumulate, and the resulting hyperhomocysteinemia may heighten stroke susceptibility through endothelial injury, oxidative stress, and pro-inflammatory signaling.[6] Among these micronutrients, vitamin B1 – whose biologically active form is thiamin – takes part in glucose catabolism, preserves the integrity of neuronal membranes, and supports the formation of myelin as well as the synthesis of several neurotransmitters, including acetylcholine, serotonin, and certain amino acids.[7] Thiamine has additionally been credited with reparative properties, and its putative antioxidant capacity has been hypothesized to shield neurons from oxidative damage.[8]

These biological properties point toward a plausible role of vitamin B1 in cerebrovascular protection. To date, however, epidemiological research on B vitamins and stroke has centered largely on folate, niacin, vitamin B6, and vitamin B12, whereas vitamin B1 has received comparatively little attention. Against this background, the present analysis reevaluates the association between dietary vitamin B1 intake and stroke in the general population using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), with rigorous analytical procedures adopted to safeguard data quality. The resulting evidence may inform new approaches to the early identification of stroke and deepen insight into how micronutrient intake relates to stroke prevention.

2. Methods

2.1. Study population

The NHANES program integrates household interviews, standardized physical examinations, and laboratory measurements to characterize the health and nutritional profile of the civilian, noninstitutionalized U.S. population. The present analysis was restricted to participants aged 20 years or older, and individuals with incomplete information on key variables were excluded. After applying these criteria, 11,750 adults remained eligible, comprising 449 participants with a history of stroke and 11,301 without; the participant screening workflow is summarized in Figure 1. Stroke status was based on self-report, an approach widely adopted in population-based epidemiological research despite its susceptibility to recall bias. Survey sampling weights were incorporated throughout the analysis so that the resulting estimates would be nationally representative.

Flow diagram of the participant selection process. NHANES = National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey.

2.2. Dietary vitamin B1 intake

Habitual vitamin B1 intake was estimated from the 24-hour dietary recall interviews administered within NHANES, in which each participant itemized all foods and beverages consumed during the preceding day. Nutrient values derived from these recalls were aggregated to obtain an estimate of daily vitamin B1 consumption for every participant. Participants were subsequently assigned to 4 quartile groups according to intake level, with the first quartile (Q1) denoting the lowest consumption and the fourth quartile (Q4) the highest, thereby enabling a graded comparison of stroke risk across the intake spectrum. Because single-day recalls are prone to misreporting, NHANES employs a validated multiple-pass protocol delivered by trained dietary interviewers to strengthen the completeness and accuracy of the recalled information. Even so, residual recall error cannot be entirely excluded, and future work incorporating multiple-day dietary records or food frequency questionnaires would help capture habitual intake more precisely.

2.3. Diagnosis of stroke

Stroke status was ascertained through a standardized interview in which participants were asked whether a physician or other health professional had ever informed them that they had experienced a stroke; an affirmative response was taken as evidence of prior stroke. Although self-reported ascertainment is vulnerable to recall error, it has been extensively applied in epidemiological settings and is generally regarded as a dependable indicator of population-level stroke prevalence. Given that ischemic events account for approximately 87% of strokes in the United States, the present analysis focused primarily on ischemic stroke.

2.4. Assessment of covariates

A broad panel of potential confounders was incorporated into the adjusted analyses. Demographic characteristics comprised age, sex, race/ethnicity, educational attainment, and household income; lifestyle characteristics comprised smoking status and alcohol consumption; and clinical characteristics comprised body mass index (BMI), hypertension, and diabetes mellitus. Controlling for these variables allowed the independent association between dietary vitamin B1 intake and stroke risk to be estimated with reduced confounding from established vascular risk factors.

2.5. Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted in accordance with the complex, stratified, multistage probability sampling design of NHANES, with appropriate sampling weights applied so that the estimates would reflect the U.S. population. Continuous and categorical variables were summarized descriptively, and between-group differences were examined using design-based tests. Weighted multivariable logistic regression models were then constructed to quantify the association between vitamin B1 intake and stroke risk under successive levels of covariate adjustment, and restricted cubic spline regression was fitted to probe possible nonlinearity in the dose–response relationship. Prior to multivariable modeling, collinearity among vitamin B1 and the related nutrients vitamin B6, vitamin B12, and folate was screened using the variance inflation factor (VIF); all VIF values were below 2.0, indicating that collinearity was negligible and that the estimated coefficients were stable.

3.2. Association between dietary vitamin B1 and stroke

Weighted multivariable logistic regression was applied to quantify the association between dietary vitamin B1 intake and stroke while controlling for age, sex, race/ethnicity, education level, smoking, alcohol use, hypertension, and diabetes (Table 3). Vitamin B1 intake was inversely related to stroke risk both in the unadjusted model (odds ratios = 0.61, 95% CI: 0.49–0.73) and after full covariate adjustment (odds ratios = 0.82, 95% CI: 0.68–0.99), indicating that the observed association was not explained by the measured confounders.

3.3. Construction and evaluation of the stroke risk prediction model

Candidate predictors were first screened by univariate analysis and subsequently entered into least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression for final feature selection. The retained variables comprised diabetes, hypertension, smoking, alcohol consumption, poverty income ratio, age, race, BMI, vitamin B1, total cholesterol, and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, which were jointly used to construct the stroke risk prediction model (Fig. 3A). Model discrimination was evaluated using the receiver operating characteristic curve, yielding an area under the curve of 0.771 (Fig. 3B).

4. Discussion

In this cross-sectional investigation of a nationally representative sample, we examined how dietary vitamin B1 intake relates to stroke risk. A robust inverse, approximately linear association emerged and persisted after adjustment for demographic, behavioral, and clinical covariates, implying that adults who consume more vitamin B1 tend to have lower odds of stroke. Subgroup analyses further attested to the consistency of this relationship, which remained evident across population strata defined by demographic characteristics and comorbid conditions.

Our secondary analysis drew on interview data gathered by trained CDC personnel within NHANES, providing a rich and population-representative resource. Through univariate screening followed by LASSO regression, we distilled the clinical variables most informative for stroke risk – including vitamin B1 intake – and combined them into a parsimonious prediction model. This modeling pipeline illustrates how routinely collected indicators, such as age, smoking behavior, and micronutrient intake, can be integrated to support clinical risk stratification and decision-making.

As the second most common cause of mortality globally, stroke continues to impose an expanding public health burden despite declines in age-standardized incidence, driven largely by population aging and the growing prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia. Its pathogenesis is multifactorial: oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and vascular wall injury interact to promote thrombus formation and cerebral ischemia. Age remains the dominant non-modifiable determinant, with incidence roughly doubling in both sexes beyond the age of 55.

Vitamin B1 (thiamin) is an essential water-soluble micronutrient required for energy-yielding metabolism, neuronal activity, and cardiovascular regulation, and its deficiency underlies disorders such as beriberi and Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome. Historical records of thiamin deficiency date back to the 3rd century, yet the condition reached epidemic proportions only in the 19th century, particularly among sailors subsisting on monotonous rations of polished rice. The subsequent isolation of the “anti-beriberi factor” from rice bran culminated in the structural identification of thiamin in 1936. These historical lessons underpin the current emphasis on whole grains and fortified foods as the principal dietary safeguard against deficiency-related disease.

At the cellular level, thiamin serves as an indispensable cofactor in carbohydrate oxidation, converting glucose into the energy that sustains neuronal structure and excitability. It likewise participates in the biosynthesis of nucleic acids, neurotransmitters, and myelin, thereby supporting efficient nerve conduction. Within the vasculature, thiamin restrains glucose- and insulin-driven proliferation of arterial smooth muscle cells, attenuating atherosclerotic progression. More recent work has furnished direct mechanistic evidence in cerebrovascular contexts: thiamin augments endothelial nitric oxide synthase activity, improves endothelial performance, and curbs oxidative stress.[5,6] Experimental deficiency models exhibit heightened vascular inflammation and accelerated atherosclerotic lesion formation, both of which are reversed by thiamin repletion. In parallel, thiamin sustains redox balance through the pentose phosphate pathway, limiting oxidative injury to neurons and endothelial cells. Together, these mechanisms lend biological credibility to the inverse association observed in the present study and underscore the potential neurovascular value of adequate thiamin nutrition.

Mechanistically, protection against oxidative stress is regarded as a central link between vitamin B1 and cerebrovascular health. In neural tissue, thiamin triphosphate participates directly in membrane excitability, while thiamin-dependent reactions generate reducing equivalents during glucose metabolism that help neutralize oxidative insults. By shielding endothelial and vascular wall cells from such injury, adequate thiamin status may lessen the likelihood of cerebrovascular events – a mechanism consistent with the lower stroke occurrence observed at higher intake levels in our analysis.

Vitamin B1 belongs to the 8-member B-vitamin complex (B1, B2, B3, B5, B6, B7, B9, and B12), a group of chemically distinct nutrients unified by their coenzyme functions and often described as “neurotrophic” owing to their shared importance for the central and peripheral nervous systems. Supplementation studies involving folic acid (B9), vitamin B12, and vitamin B6 have demonstrated meaningful reductions in stroke risk, an effect attributed largely to the lowering of circulating homocysteine. Evidence specific to vitamin B1, by contrast, remains scarce; the inverse association documented here suggests that a thiamin-rich diet may confer comparable benefit, although whether supplementation itself can prevent stroke awaits dedicated investigation.

Because humans, like other mammals, lack the capacity to synthesize B vitamins de novo, adequate intake must be obtained from the diet, which makes nutritional strategies a practical lever for prevention. Several design features strengthen the present findings: the use of nationally weighted survey data enhances generalizability to the U.S. adult population; covariate-adjusted regression coupled with a large sample permitted stable subgroup analyses; and the LASSO-based prediction model achieved encouraging discrimination, offering a pragmatic tool for identifying individuals at elevated stroke risk. Nevertheless, the model was developed and validated within a single dataset, so its transportability to other populations and clinical settings remains to be established. Future studies with larger, more heterogeneous, and preferably prospective cohorts are needed to confirm these observations and to delineate the contribution of vitamin B1 to stroke prevention.

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, the cross-sectional design precludes any causal inference regarding the relationship between vitamin B1 intake and stroke, and prospective cohort studies are required to establish temporality. Second, both stroke ascertainment and dietary assessment relied on self-report; although NHANES employs standardized multiple-pass interviews conducted by trained staff to improve accuracy, recalled intake may still deviate from true consumption. Third, the prediction model was validated only internally, without an independent external cohort. Future research incorporating multiple-day dietary records or biomarkers of thiamin status would permit more precise exposure assessment.

5. Conclusion

In summary, higher habitual intake of dietary vitamin B1 was independently associated with reduced odds of stroke among U.S. adults. The prediction model constructed in this analysis offered useful discrimination for stroke risk and may assist future risk assessment efforts. Prospective and mechanistic studies are needed to confirm the causality of this relationship and to clarify the pathways through which thiamine exerts cerebrovascular protection.
