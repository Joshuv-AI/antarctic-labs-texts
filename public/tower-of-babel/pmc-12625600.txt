Personal wellbeing and depression among Thai people living in poverty: evidence from the COVID-19 crisis
Authors: Rattanaporn Arsa, Natthani Meemon, Wilasinee Keetawattananon, Daraphak Yenchai, Kamonwan Kiewnin, Ponsiri Pitong, Kiattisak Jangcharoenjittkul, Orratai Waleewong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUnderstanding the multifaceted impacts of global crises on vulnerable populations remains critical. This cross-sectional study examines associations between individual and contextual characteristics, personal wellbeing, and depressive symptoms among individuals living below the national poverty line, utilizing secondary data collected in 2021 during the COVID-19 pandemic.MethodsA structural equation model was applied to examine how socio-demographic characteristics, economic conditions, and health behaviors were associated with mental health outcomes, estimating both direct and indirect pathways between personal wellbeing and depressive symptoms.ResultsAmong the 694 participants, 12.8% reported mild to moderate depressive symptoms. Older individuals and those who experienced changes in their work status during the pandemic reported lower levels of wellbeing and higher levels of depressive symptoms. These patterns may reflect heightened socioeconomic and psychosocial challenges faced by people living in poverty during crisis conditions. Additionally, individuals living in municipal areas and those with lower physical activity levels were more likely to report depressive symptoms. Although the study was conducted during the pandemic period, the findings illustrate how structural disadvantages and behavioral factors were associated with mental wellbeing among people living in poverty.ConclusionsThese findings remain relevant for informing mental health and social protection strategies in future public health or economic crises.

Background

Although the acute phase of the COVID-19 pandemic has passed, its long-term and often unequal impacts on mental health remain evident. During the pandemic, people across all socioeconomic backgrounds reported experiencing depressive symptoms linked to fears of infection and disruptions to daily life [1]. However, these effects were not evenly distributed, highlighting how mental health is profoundly shaped by social, economic, and physical environment throughout the life course. Socially disadvantaged groups, particularly those living in poverty, faced more stress and had fewer psychosocial resources, making them especially vulnerable to depression during and after the pandemic [2, 3].

Mental health and wellbeing are influenced by multiple levels of factors, ranging from individual demographic and behavioral characteristics to broader socioeconomic and contextual conditions that shape daily living environments. Although the pandemic prompted a rapid increase in research on mental health worldwide, a comprehensive understanding of the determinants of wellbeing and mental health problems during this period continues to evolve.

Before the pandemic, studies consistently identified demographic and behavioral characteristics associated with subjective wellbeing and/or susceptibility to depression. For instance, individuals living alone [4], those with lower income [5, 6], and those living in urban areas [7] were generally found to be more susceptible to depression or have lower subjective wellbeing. Conversely, being employed [8] and engaging in positive health behaviors, such as good sleep quality, physical activity, and healthy diet [8, 9], were associated with better subjective wellbeing or reduced risk of depression. Additionally, school and family environments were shown to influence children’s subjective wellbeing [10]. Exercise frequency was negatively correlated with depression, whereas alcohol and cigarette use were positively correlated [11].

A substantial body of research conducted during or after the COVID-19 pandemic has also explored these determinants, sometimes revealing differing patterns. For age, findings were inconsistent. Some pandemic-era studies reported that older age was associated with a stronger protective effect of subjective wellbeing against depression [12]. In contrast, more recent research, including data collected between December 2023 and February 2024 observed a higher prevalence of depressive symptoms among older adults [13], consistent with findings from low- and middle- income countries where older persons experienced significant psychological distress during the pandemic [14]. Conversely, research in seven Asian middle-income countries [15] and Korea [12] found that younger individuals were more likely to report depressive symptoms, whereas a study in Japan reported the opposite pattern, linking older age with higher depressive symptoms [16].

Regarding gender, most studies during the pandemic found that females were more vulnerable to mental health problems and experienced lower mental health and wellbeing than men [17]. However, Borrescio-Higa & Valenzuela (2021) found no gender differences in wellbeing among young people in Indonesia, despite citing previous research indicating lower mental health among women [18]. For education, pandemic-era studies generally showed that lower education was linked to worse mental health outcomes and higher susceptibility to depression [19, 20]. However, some more recent findings contradict this pattern, such as Liverpool et al. (2023), who reported that higher education levels were associated with increased anxiety and depression in certain student populations [21]. Finally, several studies during the pandemic also explored behavioral and contextual correlates of mental health. Internet use among older adults was found to reduce depression scores [22], while quarantine experience, especially among those with higher education, was associated with increased depressive symptoms [12, 23].

The pandemic has prompted extensive analysis of the impact of changes in work and life patterns on mental health. A consistent finding was that individuals who experienced layoffs during the pandemic reported higher level of psychological distress [23–25]. Those who lost their jobs were especially at risk of mental health problems during the early months of the pandemic [27] and reported lower life satisfaction [28]. However, the relationship between employment and mental health during the pandemic was not uniform. Some research indicated that the unemployed and self-employed had poorer mental health than government employees [29], others found that those who remained employed were more likely to have anxiety than those who were unemployed or self-employed [30], and Sato et al. (2021) reported that job suspension or loss was not significantly associated with depressive symptoms [16].

Previous literatures have confirmed that people living in poverty are particularly more vulnerable to the negative mental health impacts of COVID-19 pandemic compared to those with better economic status [12, 29]. Yet, studies focusing exclusively on this group and examining variation within the population living in poverty remain scarce. A deeper understanding of individual and contextual factors associated with wellbeing and depression within this group help identify those at greater risk of depression and inform more equitable mental health interventions.

In Thailand, the pandemic has exacerbated poverty and caused major socio-economic disruptions and impacts on employment in Thailand, including strict containment measures such as temporary and permanent closure of many businesses, curfews, and mobility restrictions that particularly affected low-income populations [26]. A recent study on the impact of the pandemic on mental health indicated a high prevalence of severe to extremely severe depression among seven Asian middle-income countries [21]. This study, therefore, explores the sociodemographic, economic, and contextual correlates of personal wellbeing and depressive symptoms among people living in poverty in Thailand. It focuses on within-group variations among individuals below the national poverty line, rather than comparisons between poor and non-poor populations.

Methods

Study design and data source

This cross-sectional study used secondary data from a 2021 survey conducted by the International Health Policy Program (IHPP) in Thailand. The original survey collected face-to-face data from 7,731 nationally representative individuals aged 15 years and above to assess the impact of the COVID-19 situation on non-communicable disease prevention and control. Ethical approval of the original data collection was granted by the Institute for the Development of Human Research Protections (IHRP), Thailand (COA No. IHRP2021002, IHRP No. 132–2563), and informed consent was obtained from all participants. For this secondary analysis, a subsample of 694 participants living below the national poverty line (defined as an income of less than 2,803 per month [31], approximately USD 87.6 or USD 2.9 per day at the 2021 exchange rate) was extracted. The lower age limit of 15 years was maintained to ensure consistency with the ethically approved national survey design which aimed to represent the working-age population.

Variables and instruments

The purpose of the study was to investigate personal wellbeing and depressive symptoms among people living under the poverty line in association with their socio-economic conditions and health-related behaviors during the COVID-19 outbreak. All study variables were retrieved from the survey data.

Personal wellbeing, modeled as a latent construct in the structural equation model, was measured using the Personal Wellbeing Index (PWI) [31]. The PWI instrument comprises eight indicators representing a participant’s satisfaction with quality of life in different domains in the past 30 days: standard of living, personal health, achievement in life, personal relationships, personal safety, community-connectedness, future security, and spirituality-religion. It also includes one question about satisfaction with the overall quality of life. Scores for each domain ranged from 0 (completely dissatisfied) to 10 (completely satisfied). Following instrument guidelines [32], a total score was summed, averaged, and converted to a standardized 0–100 scale for descriptive presentation; however, in the SEM analysis, the eight domain scores were entered separately as observed indicators of the latent construct personal wellbeing. The PWI has been widely adopted globally and in the Thai context [33].

Depressive symptoms, also modeled as a latent construct, were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9). The instrument consists of nine indicators representing common symptoms of depression, including having little interest or pleasure in doing things, feeling down, depressed or hopeless, sleep disorder, lack of energy, eating disorder, negative feelings about self, trouble with concentration, acting or moving much less or much more than usual, and having suicidal or self-harm thoughts, which occurred to the participants specifically since the COVID-19 situation [34]. Each item was scored from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day). A summed PHQ-9 score was calculated and categorized for descriptive purposes only; in the SEM, the nine items scores served as observed indicators of the latent construct of depressive symptoms. The Thai version of PHQ-9 has been validated for use in both clinical [35, 36] and non-clinical populations [37, 38]. The PHQ-9 has been widely used during the COVID-19 pandemic [10, 38].

Socio-demographic characteristics of individual participants included gender (0 = male, 1 = female), age (years), marital status (0 = not living with spouse/partner, 1 = living with spouse/partner), and educational level (0 = not completed middle school, 1 = completed middle school). Contextual and economic conditions comprised living place (0 = outside municipality, 1 = within municipality), monthly income (Thai Baht), employment status (0 = unemployed, 1 = employed) and reported change in employment as a result of the COVID-19 (0 = no change, 1 = affected). Health-related behaviors included current smoking status (0 = no,1 = yes), current drinking status (0 = no, 1 = yes), and weekly physical activity (total minutes spent on any types of physical activity).

Statistical analysis

A structural equation modeling (SEM) approach was employed to investigate the complex relationships among the latent variables (personal wellbeing and depressive symptoms) and the observed socio-economic conditions and health-related behaviors. This method was chosen for its ability to simultaneously test hypothesized relationships and accounts for measurement errors in the latent constructs. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Amos version 18.0.

Before estimating the full structural model, the reliability and validity of the two latent variables, depressive symptoms and personal wellbeing, were thoroughly examined. This involved assessing their internal consistency using Cronbach’s α and confirming their hypothesized factor structures through confirmatory factor analysis (CFA).

Model specification involved defining the measurement models for personal wellbeing and depressive symptoms, and the structural relationships hypothesized between these latent variables and the observed socio-economic and health-related behavior variables. Parameters were estimated using the Maximum Likelihood (ML) estimation method. Model fit was evaluated using the chi-square statistic, the Goodness-of-Fit Index (GFI ≥ 0.90) and the Adjusted Goodness-of-Fit Index (AGFI ≥ 0.90), the Comparative Fit Index (CFI ≥ 0.90), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA ≤ 0.08) and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR ≤ 0.08) as indicators of acceptable goodness of fit [39, 40]. Following standard SEM procedures, non-significant paths that did not contribute meaningfully to the model fit were sequentially removed, while retaining all theoretically relevant relationships. All fit indices for the final model were within these acceptable ranges, indicating that the model provided a good representation of the observed data. Standardized regression coefficients and their corresponding p-values were reported for all significant paths.

Results

Characteristics of the study sample

Of the 694 participants who reported having an income lower than the national poverty line, 59.8% were female, the average age was 42.19 years (SD = 23.62), 52.7% completed middle school as part of his or her compulsory education and 62.4% did not live with spouse/partner. A large proportion of the study sample (90.3%) were not employed at the time of data collection. The average monthly income was 995.97 THB (SD = 893.34). 65.7% of the participants were living outside of municipality area and 10.8% reported that their employment was affected by the COVID-19 situation.

Regarding health-related behaviors, participants reported an average of 608.43 min of physical activity per week (SD = 658.46). The majority did not smoke tobacco (91.6%) or drink alcohol (80.5%). The mean Personal Wellbeing Index (PWI) score was 61.26 (SD = 13.713) with domain-specific means ranging from 54.31 for personal safety to 70.97 for personal relationships. Based on PHQ-9 scores, 87.2% reported none-to-minimal depressive symptoms, 11% mild, and 1.8% moderate symptoms, corresponding to an overall prevalence of 12.8% mild-to-moderate depressive symptoms. Reliability and validity of the latent variables

Table 2 summarizes the reliability and validity test results of the latent constructs. Cronbach’ s α coefficients indicated good internal consistency for both personal wellbeing (α = 0.899) and depressive symptoms (α = 0.755). All factor loadings (λ) were statistically significant (p < 0.001). Model fit indices for the confirmatory factor analyses showed acceptable fit for both latent constructs. For personal wellbeing, the model showed χ2/df = 4.985 (p < 0.001), GFI = 0.973, AGFI = 0.940, CFI = 0.978, RMSEA = 0.076, SRMR = 0.027. Similarly, the depressive symptoms construct yielded χ2/df = 4.954 (p < 0.001), GFI = 0.961, AGFI = 0.930, CFI = 0.932, RMSEA = 0.076, SRMR = 0.050. Among the PHQ-9 items, those representing changes in physical activity or restlessness and suicidal thoughts showed relatively lower loadings, a pattern consistent with previous PHQ-9 validation studies [41], as these symptoms are less prevalent in community samples. Overall, these findings confirmed that both latent variables demonstrated good reliability and construct validity, providing a sound basis for the subsequent structural modeling.

Discussion

This study aimed to identify factors associated with personal wellbeing and depressive symptoms among a sample of individuals living in poverty in Thailand during COVID-19. Our findings add to the understanding of mental wellbeing among people living in poverty during the COVID-19 period, providing contextual evidence that complements broader population studies.

A significant finding was the slightly higher average personal wellbeing score of 61.26 (SD = 13.71) among individuals living below the poverty line, with relatively low mean scores in perceived personal safety (54.31), standard of living (57.78), and future security (57.44). According to the same IHPP national dataset, the average Personal Wellbeing Index (PWI) score for the general population was 60.09 (SD = 14.20). Although the overall wellbeing levels were comparable between the two groups, differences across domains were evident. The poverty subsample reported slightly higher satisfaction with most domains, while the national sample showed marginally higher scores only in personal health. Regarding depressive symptoms, 12.8% of individuals in the poverty subsample reported mild-to-moderate symptoms compared with 10.7% in the national sample. These descriptive contrasts suggest that, even when overall wellbeing scores appear similar or slightly higher among those with lower income, such results do not necessarily indicate the absence of vulnerability. People living in poverty may report comparable or even higher subjective wellbeing due to adaptive and social coping mechanisms, yet they continue to face structural disadvantages that affect their sense of security and stability during crisis conditions. However, the cross-sectional nature of the data limits any causal interpretation. These patterns emphasize the importance of understanding mental wellbeing within the context of poverty. Although the IHPP dataset represents the overall Thai population, the present analysis focuses specifically on individuals living below the national poverty line to explore variations within this highly vulnerable group. Socioeconomic disadvantage is widely associated with elevated risks of depression and poorer wellbeing [42], and examining this subgroup provides insights into the specific challenges and coping conditions that may be obscured in broader population analyses. Understanding the experiences of those most disadvantage remains critical for informing equitable and responsive mental health and social protection strategies.

Our study revealed several interesting findings, some of which present a different picture than what has been reported in previous literature. In many pre-pandemic studies, subjective wellbeing tended to increase with age reflecting greater emotional stability and life satisfaction among older adults [33, 43]. In contrast, our analysis showed that older individuals reported lower personal wellbeing and higher depressive symptoms. This pattern may reflect the specific circumstances of the COVID-19 pandemic period. The perceived severity of the illness among older age groups, coupled with prolonged lockdowns and restrictions on physical interactions with loved ones, likely contributed to increased psychosocial distress and depressive symptoms in this vulnerable group, a pattern observed in older persons in Thailand during the pandemic [14]. This suggests that the unique context of a large-scale crisis may be associated with changes in the typical relationships between age and wellbeing.

Changes in employment status during the pandemic period were associated with poorer personal wellbeing and higher depressive symptoms, consistent with findings from other studies [23–25]. Job loss and suspension often coincide with income loss and financial crises, which can be particularly acute for individuals in poverty [44]. This was also observed in specific occupational groups in Thailand, such as farmers [45]. This burden may be even greater among regular private employees in low-income communities, who may have fewer alternative income sources than those in irregular or self-employment [46].

Weekly physical activities and living place were associated with depressive symptoms, consistent with previous studies showing the protective role of physical activity for mental wellbeing [16]. No significant associations were found between smoking or drinking and depressive symptoms. This absence of significant relationships, although contrary to some expectations, may reflect the pre-existing habits of participants. For individuals with long-standing smoking and drinking patterns, these behaviors may not have changed substantially during the pandemic in ways that measurably affected their depressive symptoms, or they might serve as maladaptive coping mechanisms that did not reduce their distress. This finding warrants further exploration in future studies.

Although our cross-sectional analysis cannot establish causality, national data indicated that poverty declined slightly in rural areas while increasing in urban settings (from 4% to 6%) amid an estimated 18% decline in income. Government relief package during the pandemic likely helped mitigate income loss in some areas, particularly rural communities [46]. Nevertheless, the urban slum dwellers continued to face multiple challenges, including limited access to income compensation and social protections [47] and insufficient health resources such as vaccinations, healthcare staff, and hospital capacity. In contrast, rural areas generally had better access to community-level facilities and support from community health volunteers. These differences suggest that existing community infrastructure and social capital in rural settings may have offered some protection against psychosocial and economic stressors that were more evident in densely populated urban poor areas.

Monthly income was positively associated with the latent construct of personal wellbeing, which includes the domain of standard of living as one of its key components. Participants with lower income tended to report lower wellbeing and higher depressive symptoms, reflecting the close linkage between economic insecurity and psychological distress observed in previous studies [48, 49]. This association may be explained by the strain of persistent socioeconomic disadvantage, which limits access to material resources and social participation, thereby diminishing life satisfaction and increasing vulnerability to depression. In addition, a higher score on the Personal Wellbeing Index (PWI) was significantly associated with a lower score on the PHQ-9 for depressive symptoms [50, 51], reaffirming the strong inverse relationship between overall wellbeing and psychological distress and underscoring the interconnectedness of wellbeing and mental health.

Other sociodemographic factors, including gender, education and marital status, were not significantly associated with either personal wellbeing or depressive symptoms in the final model. The lack of gender differences may reflect the relatively similar levels of socioeconomic and pandemic-related stress faced by both men and women in this population. In contrast, the absence of significant relationship for education and marital status may be due to the relatively homogeneity of the sample, which consisted entirely of individuals living below the national poverty line.

This study has several limitations. First, the data were cross-sectional and collected at one point during the COVID-19 pandemic; therefore, the findings represent association observed during that period and cannot be interpreted as causal or temporal effects. Second, the sample included only individuals living below the national poverty line, which allowed examination of within-group variation but limited comparability with higher-income populations. Third, although “change in employment” was measured as being due to COVID-19, the variable did not distinguish between negative changes (e.g., job loss) and neutral or adaptive shifts to other forms of work. Future studies should clarify these distinctions to better understand the implications of employment changes for wellbeing and mental health. Finally, some potentially relevant variables, such as direct experience of COVID-19 infection, illness among family members, access to pandemic-related support services, and psycho-social support at family or community levels, were not included in the dataset. Future research using longitudinal or mixed-methods designs and including diverse income groups would help clarify the pathways linking socioeconomic conditions, wellbeing, and mental health during and beyond crisis periods.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study in Thailand to examine personal wellbeing and depressive symptoms specifically among individuals living in poverty during the COVID-19 pandemic. The findings emphasize the disproportionate burden experienced by this marginalized group. The observed variations in the relationship between age and wellbeing, as well as the differences between urban and rural settings, highlight the importance of context-sensitive analyses. Although this study did not directly measure access to psychological support or social protection, the observed associations between economic, behavioral, and contextual conditions with wellbeing suggest that efforts to promote healthy behaviors, strengthen psychological support, and ensure equitable access to social protection and healthcare can be important for supporting wellbeing and mitigating adverse mental health outcomes during times of crisis. Taking into account both individual and contextual differences, customized assistance packages are essential, particularly for those in urban poverty, unemployment, or very low incomes. Public health sectors should invest in mechanisms for identifying and reaching individuals with limited access to mental health services to ensure that the most vulnerable are not left behind in future public health emergencies.
