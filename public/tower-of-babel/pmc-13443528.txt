A chromosome-level genome assembly of the waterlily aphid Rhopalosiphum nymphaeae Linnaeus
Authors: Lijun Ma, Cheng Qu, Yun Gao, Cheng Gong, Hangwei Liu, Chen Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The waterlily aphid (Rhopalosiphum nymphaeae Linnaeus; WLA) is a polyphagous pest that inflicts considerable damage on both fruit trees and cultivated aquatic plants. In this study, we report a high-quality chromosome-scale genome assembly for WLA, produced by integrating PacBio long reads and Hi–C scaffolding. The assembled genome is 322.79 Mb in size, with a scaffold N50 of 94.06 Mb, and is anchored to four chromosomes. BUSCO completeness reached 97.2% (95.9% single-copy, 1.3% duplicated). Repeat elements account for 20.48% (67.82 Mb) of the genome, and 14,872 protein-coding genes were annotated. This high-quality assembly of R. nymphaeae serves as a valuable genetic resource for exploring the genomic basis of the molecular mechanisms of cross-host adaptation in WLA and offers valuable resources for functional research and pest control.

Background & Summary

Aphids (Hemiptera: Aphididae) are major agricultural pests, inflicting damage both directly through phloem feeding and indirectly by transmitting plant viruses1. With more than 5000 species across 23 subfamilies, aphids display substantial species diversity; the subfamily Aphidinae alone contains over half of all known species2. Among these, the waterlily aphid (Rhopalosiphum nymphaeae Linnaeus) is a polyphagous insect of agricultural significance, attacking both fruit trees and cultivated aquatic plants. It exhibits a remarkable cross-habitat life cycle, transitioning between terrestrial winter hosts, such as peach and plum (Rosaceae), where overwintering eggs are laid, and aquatic summer hosts including waterlilies and other aquatic vegetables. During spring, WLA migrates to aquatic environments where it feeds on various plants like Callitriche, Euryale, Lemna, Nelumbo, and Nymphaea3. This sap-feeding activity causes leaf rolling (Fig. 1) and honeydew secretion that reduces photosynthetic efficiency. WLA is the only aphid known to thrive in both terrestrial and aquatic habitats6, exhibiting a host range distinct from related Rhopalosiphum species. This ecological adaptability positions it as a compelling model for investigating evolutionary plasticity and host adaptation in aphids. Aphid phylogenetics commonly integrates morphological, molecular, and host-associated data7. Within the monophyletic subtribe Rhopalosiphina, relationships in Rhopalosiphina, such as the Rhopalosiphum genus, is still not resolved7,8. Molecular phylogenetic analyses group WLA within a Rhopalosiphum clade that includes R. maidis, R. padi, and R. rufiabdominale, with mitochondrial COI sequences showing 2.1–3.4% divergence from its closest relatives9. The unusual ecological niche and broad host range of WLA offer valuable opportunities to study speciation, adaptation, and evolutionary innovation in aphids.

Recent advances in high-throughput sequencing have facilitated the development of chromosome-level genome assemblies for multiple aphid species, including Acyrthosiphon pisum and Myzus persicae10,11. These resources have been instrumental in elucidating such biological features as polyphenism, symbiotic interactions with Buchnera, and detoxification mechanisms targeting plant defensive compounds12. However, the absence of a chromosome-scale genome for the WLA has limited comparative research into host adaptation and evolutionary mechanisms among aphids exhibiting complex life histories.

In this study, we present a high-quality chromosome-level genome assembly for WLA, integrating PacBio long-read sequencing with Illumina short reads and Hi–C scaffolding. We performed comprehensive genome annotation, gene prediction, and functional characterization to explore its evolutionary relationship within the superfamily Aphidoidea. This genome assembly not only advances our understanding of aphid evolution but also establishes a critical resource for future functional and pest management studies. The data generated in this work provide a foundation for exploring adaptive mechanisms in aquatic aphids and enable broader comparative genomics across aquatic insect systems.

Methods

Sample collection and genome sequencing

The samples of WLA were collected from lotus (Nelumbo nucifera) pond in the Summer Palace, Beijing, China. To reduce sequencing heterozygosity, a minimal number of individuals were selected for genomic sequencing. Sequencing used the following numbers of individuals: Illumina (50–70), PacBio (100–120), Hi-C (500–600), transcriptome (200–300 per tissue/morph sample). High-molecular-weight genomic DNA was extracted using the MagAttract HMW DNA Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), suitable for Illumina, PacBio, and Hi-C sequencing applications. DNA purity and integrity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000C spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, DE, USA) and 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis. A paired-end Illumina library with an insert size of approximately 500 bp was prepared using the VAHTS Universal DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina V2 (Vazyme, Nanning, China) and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform to generate 150 bp paired-end reads. Raw sequencing data were quality-filtered using Trimmomatic v0.3813.

For long-read sequencing, PCR-free SMRTbell libraries were constructed using the Sequel Sequencing Kit 3.0 (Pacific Biosciences, Menlo Park, CA, USA) and sequenced on a PacBio Sequel II platform at Annoroad Gene Technology (Zhejiang, China). Raw subreads were generated by removing sequencing adapters and low-quality regions from the original single-molecule data using SMRT Link v9.0 (Pacific Biosciences), resulting in high-fidelity circular consensus sequencing (CCS) reads. A total of 508.6 Gb of subreads were obtained, with 18.81 Gb in CCS read bases, a mean read length of 15.44 kb, an N50 of 15.55 kb, and an estimated coverage of approximately 60 × , which were used for contig-level genome assembly.

To facilitate chromosome-level genome assembly, Hi-C (High-throughput chromosome conformation capture) was employed to capture genome-wide chromatin interactions14. Briefly, the larvae were cross-linked with 2% formaldehyde and homogenized. Chromatin was digested overnight using the restriction enzyme MboI. The digested ends were blunted, labeled with biotin-14-dCTP, and ligated to bridge linkers. Following proteinase K digestion, samples were purified by phenol-chloroform extraction. Unligated DNA ends were debiotinylated using T4 DNA polymerase. The DNA was then sheared into 200–600 bp fragments using a Covaris S220 Focused-ultrasonicator (Covaris, USA). Biotin-labeled fragments were enriched with streptavidin C1 magnetic beads. An Illumina-compatible library was prepared from the enriched DNA using the VAHTS Universal DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina V2 (Vazyme, Nanning, China) and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform to generate 150 bp paired-end reads. After quality filtering, 46.107 million clean reads were retained, providing approximately 142.13× coverage of the genome.

RNA extraction and transcriptome sequencing

To support genome annotation with transcriptional evidence, RNA-seq was performed on a comprehensive set of developmental stages and tissues from the WLA, including 1st to 4th instar nymphs, antennae, heads, thorax-abdomen, legs, apterous adults, and alate adults. Total RNA was isolated with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration was measured on a NanoDrop 2000C spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and quality was assessed by 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis. RNA integrity was further quantified using an Agilent 5400 Fragment Analyzer (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Sequencing libraries were prepared with the VAHTSTM mRNA-seq V2 Library Prep Kit (Vazyme, NanJing, China) and subjected to paired-end 150 bp sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq platform. After quality control of raw reads with Trimmomatic v0.3813, high-quality reads were aligned to the chromosome-level genome using HISAT2 v2.2.015 and assembled into transcripts with StringTie v2.1.216. Gene expression levels were quantified as FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase per Million mapped reads) using Cufflinks v2.2.117.

Genome estimation

A genome survey is a critical first step for assessing fundamental genomic features such as genome size, repetitive content, and heterozygosity. To estimate these parameters for the WLA, we performed a k-mer-based analysis using raw PacBio HiFi reads. The frequency distribution of 21-mers was generated with Jellyfish v2.3.118 and subsequently analyzed with GenomeScope v2.019. This approach yielded an estimated genome size of 299.55 Mb, a heterozygosity rate of 0.038%, and a duplication rate of 0.15% (Figure S1).

Genome assembly

A contig-level assembly of the WLA genome was generated using PacBio long-read data. The initial assembly was constructed with HiFiasm v0.25.0-r72620 under default parameters. Following correction with Pilon v1.2421 and removal of low-quality and adapter sequences, 46.11 Gb of clean Hi-C data were aligned to the draft genome using BWA v0.7.1822. Uniquely mapped read pairs were processed with HiC-Pro v3.1.023 to filter invalid interactions (e.g., dangling ends, re-ligation, self-cycle, and dumped pairs). A total of 56,674,159 valid interaction pairs were used with LACHESIS v2e27abb24 to cluster, order, and orient contigs into chromosomes. The resulting contig-level assembly spanned 331.06 Mb across 502 contigs, with a contig N50 of 1.29 Mb and a GC content of 27.2% (Table 1). Based on interaction frequencies, 275 contigs were grouped into four linkage groups (Fig. 2), with the longest measuring 95.21 Mb and the shortest 55.24 Mb (Table 2). The scaffold N50 reached 94.06 Mb (Table 1). To evaluate base-level accuracy, we used Merqury v1.325, a k-mer-based tool, to compare Illumina reads (k = 21) with the assembly, yielding a QV of 29.42 (error rate ~ 0.00114, 99.886% accuracy). Genomic repeat annotation

Repetitive sequences in the genome consist mainly of tandem repeats and interspersed repeats, with the latter predominantly comprising transposable elements (TEs). To annotate these repeats, we employed an integrated strategy combining de novo prediction and homology-based methods. A de novo repeat library was first constructed from the genome assembly using RepeatModeler v2.0.6a26 and LTR_retriever v2.827 under default parameters. The resulting repeats were classified with PASTE Classifier v1.028 and merged with entries from the Dfam v3.829 database to generate a non-redundant, species-specific TE library. This library was then used to identify TEs within the genome via RepeatMasker v4.1.730. In total, 67.82 Mb of TE sequences were annotated, representing 20.48% of the assembled genome (Fig. 3, Table 4). Among these, long interspersed nuclear elements (LINEs) constituted the most abundant class of retroelements, comprising 1.05% of the genome. Gene modeling and prediction

Following the masking of repetitive sequences, we integrated three distinct strategies for predicting intact protein-coding genes: de novo prediction, homology-based prediction, and transcriptome-based prediction. For de novo prediction, we used Augustus v3.5.031 and SNAP32. Homology-based predictions were generated with GeMoMa v1.933 by aligning the genome to protein sequences of four related insect species—Acyrthosiphon pisum (GCA_005508785.2), Diuraphis noxia (GCA_001186385.1), Rhopalosiphum maidis (GCA_003676215.3), and Sipha flava (GCA_003268045.1)—obtained from Ensembl. For transcript-based annotation, RNA-seq reads were aligned to the genome using HISAT2 v2.2.015, and gene structures were predicted with PASA v2.3.234. The resulting gene sets from all three approaches were integrated into a consensus gene model using EVidenceModeler v1.1.135 under default parameters. Functional annotation of the predicted protein-coding genes was performed by BLAST v2.2.3136 alignment against the GO, KOG, and KEGG databases using an E-value cutoff of 1 × 10−5. In total, 14,872 protein-coding genes were annotated, with a BUSCO completeness of 94.40% (Table 3). Among these, 12,732 genes (85.61%) were functionally annotated in at least one database. Phylogenetic reconstruction

To reconstruct the evolutionary history of aphids, we performed comparative genomic analysis using protein-coding genes from the WLA and nine other high-quality aphid genomes. Protein sequences were obtained from NCBI for the following species: Acyrthosiphon pisum (GCA_005508785.2), Aphis gossypii (GCA_020184175.2), Diuraphis noxia (GCA_001186385.1), Metopolophium dirhodum (GCA_019925205.1), Myzus persicae (GCA_001856785.1), Pentalonia nigronervosa (GCA_014851325.1), Rhopalosiphum maidis (GCA_003676215.3), Rhopalosiphum padi (GCA_020882245.1), and Sipha flava (GCA_003268045.1) (Table S1). The longest transcript per gene was selected for orthogroup inference using OrthoFinder v2.5.537 with the default parameters. Single-copy orthologs were aligned with MAFFT v7.52638 and trimmed with Gblocks v0.91b39. Data Records

The sequencing reads generated in this study are available in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under BioProject accession PRJNA132721741, with run accessions SRR3533781742 (Illumina sequencing), SRR3533781643 (PacBio sequencing), SRR3533781544 (Hi-C sequencing), and SRR3757795645 (transcriptome sequencing). The chromosome-level assembly is accessible via GenBank accession JBXUNZ00000000046, and the corresponding annotation files are available on Figshare47.

Technical Validation

The quality of the WLA genome assembly was assessed using three complementary approaches. First, Illumina clean reads were mapped to the assembly with BWA, yielding an alignment rate of 94.29%. Second, third-generation sequencing data aligned to the genome at a rate of 99.18% with 99.98% coverage. Third, assembly completeness was evaluated with BUSCO v2.5 against the hemiptera_odb10 database, which confirmed the presence of 97.2% (2,439 out of 2,510) of conserved single-copy orthologs (Table 3). To evaluate chromosome-scale scaffolding, the assembly was divided into 500 kb bins and interaction frequencies from Hi-C data were visualized in a heatmap. Strong diagonal patterns across four distinct chromosomes indicated robust intrachromosomal contact signals and high contiguity. Together, these results demonstrate that a high-quality chromosome-level genome assembly was achieved for WLA.
