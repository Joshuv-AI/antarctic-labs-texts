Assessing financial toxicity with COST-FACIT: experience from a tertiary care cancer center
Authors: Mahir Khan, Elizabeth N. Dewey, Colleen Strzalka, Aarushi Bhan, Heather McKee Hurwitz, Holly Stegh, Renata Hurn, Jacqueline Tomer, Sujata Patil, Kim Bell, Betty K. Hamilton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeFinancial toxicity (FT) refers to the direct and indirect consequences of cancer diagnosis and treatment and is a key mediator of quality of life (QOL) and survival in cancer patients. To address this, financial navigation (FN) programs have been implemented in many cancer centers. At our large tertiary care cancer center, the FN program uses the Comprehensive Score for Financial Toxicity (COST) to screen patients undergoing first cancer treatment. We aimed to better understand FT, risk factors, and association with outcomes in our cancer population.MethodsWe identified 1906 patients with any cancer diagnosis and a completed COST between 6/2021 and 2/2024. Sociodemographic data including the Area Deprivation Index (ADI), disease, and treatment characteristics as well as patient-reported outcomes (PROMIS10, PHQ9 and NCCN DT) were collected. Risk factors for FT and association of FT on overall survival (OS) were analyzed.ResultsMedian age was 65 years (IQR 56–72); 54% female, 82% white, 57% married, 47% insured privately, and 35% with Medicare. Younger age, race/ethnicity, insurance type, tobacco use, and higher ADI were associated with higher FT. Higher distress (NCCN DT, N = 1001) and lower physical (N = 1474) and mental (N = 1505) health (PROMIS10) were also associated with higher FT. FT was not associated with time to treat (TTT) or OS. However, higher ADI was significantly associated with TTT (HR 0.98, 95% CI 0.96–1.00, p = 0.04) but not OS.ConclusionsOur study provides insights into risk factors for FT in a large cancer population. We did not demonstrate a significant association of FT on survival. Further analysis in specific disease groups is needed to better understand these relationships.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00520-026-11344-z.

Introduction

The cost of cancer care in the USA has significantly increased over the last several decades for patients and their families. With the rapid advancement of novel diagnostics and therapeutics, the costs of treatment continue to rise [1, 2]. Financial toxicity (FT) is a term referring to a patient’s direct treatment expenses, loss of income due to job loss and unexpected leave, and indirect costs associated with completing treatment like childcare, travel to the facility, and meals while onsite [3–5]. FT is associated with poor quality of life, symptom burden, barriers to adherence to medications, and survival [6, 7]. Given the increasing awareness of the risk of financial burden in cancer patients, the financial concerns of patients, caregivers, physicians, health systems, and payers are increasingly being investigated [4]. Thus, research in defining and measuring the consequences of cancer care has increased significantly in recent years.

Several studies have revealed cancer patients’ experiences with financial toxicity. Cancer patients experience financial debt at a significantly higher rate than the general population and are more likely to file for bankruptcy, experience foreclosures, collections, and repossessions [8, 9]. Patients with lower household income, racial/ethnic minority status, live in a rural location, or are uninsured are at an increased risk of FT. Furthermore, patients who experience high FT subsequently have a higher likelihood of medication non-adherence, delayed medical care [10–12], and ultimately, higher mortality [1, 13]. FT has also been shown to be associated with health-related quality of life (HRQOL) including increased anxiety, depression, and symptom burden [9, 14–16].

Financial navigation (FN) programs have emerged as a strategy to recognize and mitigate FT. These programs address financial toxicity at multiple levels, offering services such as case management, transportation support, financial education, and assistance with insurance and grant applications [17]. Within our cancer institute, we have an established FN team consisting of a patient financial advocate, a financial navigator, and social worker, who collaboratively assess financial and related psychosocial needs to address barriers and access to care. Since 2021, the FN team has been using the Comprehensive Score for Financial Toxicity- Functional Assessment of Chronic Illness Therapy (COST-FACIT) [18] to identify patients who are planning to receive cancer therapy and may benefit from additional resources such as grants, pharmaceutical programs, and co-pay assistance for medications. To better understand the burden of FT within our cancer population, we undertook this study to describe FT, risk factors for higher FT, and association with HRQOL in a large cohort of cancer patients.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective study of patients who were seen for a cancer diagnosis and completed a COST-FACIT survey at the Cleveland Clinic Cancer Institute from June 2021 to February 2024. The primary objectives of the study were to describe FT and severity of FT in our cancer population, and identify sociodemographic, disease, and treatment-related risk factors for FT. Secondary objectives were to analyze association of FT on time to treat and overall mortality. FT and additional patient-reported outcome measures (PROM), as well as patient demographic, disease, and treatment data were obtained from the institutional Tumor Registry Database and Electronic Health Record (EHR). The study was approved by the Cleveland Clinic Institutional Review Board.

The COST-FACIT is a validated and standardized PROM that evaluates FT using an 11-item questionnaire. The score ranges from 0 to 44 with lower scores indicating higher financial toxicity. The score was categorized into 4 grades based on previous validation on impact on quality of life: grade 0, no impact (COST-FACIT ≥ 26); grade 1, mild impact (COST-FACIT 14–25); grade 2, moderate impact (COST-FACIT 1-13); and grade 3, high impact (COST-FACIT 0) [19]. The COST-FACIT was offered to patients at the time of first outpatient treatment for cancer diagnosis. Only initial COST-FACIT data was used for analysis, as there were limited follow-up surveys. Additional PROM data were collected closest to the date of the COST-FACIT based on the last appointment where vital signs were recorded. PROMs are routinely collected at the cancer center from a patient’s initial visit and subsequently on 84-day intervals. They include the Patient Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS)−10, the National Comprehensive Cancer Network Distress Thermometer (NCCN DT), and the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ)−9. The PROMIS-10 is a 10-item questionnaire designed to assess a patient’s overall health status. It includes questions to measure physical and mental health status. Higher scores typically indicate better physical and mental health (> 58 excellent; 50–58 very good; 42–9 good; 35–41 fair; < 35 poor). NCCN DT is a validated single-item question tool for initial screening of distress with a rating scale from 0 to 10. Higher scores indicate higher distress (0–3 mild; 4–7 moderate; 8–10 severe). The PHQ-9 is a nine-item questionnaire which screens for depression. It consists of an initial 2 questions (PHQ-2), and if screening criteria are met for depression, it expands to 9 questions (PHQ-9). Higher scores also indicate more severe depression (0–4 minimal; 5–9 mild; 10–14 moderate; 15–19 moderate-severe; 20–27 severe).

Sociodemographic variables of interest included: age, sex, race/ethnicity, marital status, smoking history, body mass index (BMI), insurance information, and area deprivation index (ADI). The ADI is a validated index of neighborhood deprivation and disadvantage taking into account factors related to income, education, employment, and housing quality [20]. The ADI was derived from 2013–2017 five-year American Community Survey (ACS) estimates [21]. For analysis, ADI was grouped into quintiles based on the percentile rank in Ohio for descriptive summaries. The higher the ADI, the higher the socioeconomic disadvantage in the neighborhood/community. Although neighborhood deprivation measured by ADI is associated with socioeconomic disadvantage, it reflects community-level characteristics rather than an individual patient's perceived financial burden. We therefore evaluated ADI alongside COST-FACIT to determine whether neighborhood disadvantage and patient-reported financial toxicity demonstrated independent associations with clinical outcomes. Regression models treated ADI percentile rank as a continuous variable to maximize statistical power and estimate changes in risk per 5-percentile (Table 2) or 10-percentile (Table 3) increase. Data on patient’s disease, staging, and treatment were based on the date of diagnosis. Missing data were not imputed. PROMs were not considered appropriate for imputation because they represent individualized patient-reported outcomes. Analyses were therefore conducted using available-case data. Levels with sparse observations were combined, assigned to an “Unknown” category, or excluded when appropriate.

Statistical analysis

Standard descriptive statistics were used to describe sociodemographic, disease, and treatment factors by financial toxicity groups. Continuous factors are summarized as medians and interquartile ranges, and comparisons between groups were evaluated with log-transformed linear regression. Categorical factors are summarized using counts and percentages, and comparisons between availability responses were evaluated using Kruskal–Wallis test, Chi-Square tests, and Fisher’s Exact test, as appropriate.

Missing data was not imputed. Most of the missing data was in PROMs and was not considered suitable for imputation due to the individualistic nature of the scores. Additionally, levels in some categorical variables were combined or excluded from hypothesis testing due to low sample size or missingness.

Financial toxicity was modeled as an ordinal outcome using the four validated COST-FACIT categories (no, mild, moderate, and high financial toxicity), with cumulative logistic regression estimating the odds of belonging to a higher financial toxicity category. Odds ratios represent the odds of being in a higher financial toxicity category. Variables with univariable p < 0.20 were considered for multivariable modeling using the variable selection approach described by Hosmer and Lemeshow [22]. All odds ratios were adjusted for diagnosis.

Overall patient survival was evaluated for all patients and stratified by tumor type (hematologic malignancy or solid tumor) using Cox Proportional Hazards adjusting for patient characteristics age, BMI, sex, race/ethnicity, primary payer, marital status, smoking status, time to first treatment (from date of diagnosis), clinical stage (categorized as stage 0–I, II, III, IV, or Unknown), and chemotherapy treatment. The proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residuals.

A sensitivity analysis was performed to examine the demographic differences between populations of patients who did and did not fill out a COST-FACIT survey or a PROM. Cohorts were compared using standard descriptive statistics, and differences were evaluated with Kruskal–Wallis test, Chi-Square tests, and Fisher’s Exact test, as appropriate. Because analyses were exploratory and based on prespecified clinical hypotheses, no formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was performed. All analysis was done using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). Significance was assessed at p < 0.05.

Results

Patient and disease characteristics

We identified 16,814 unique patients seen for at least one appointment between June 2021 and February 2024. Of these, 1906 had completed at least 1 COST-FACIT assessment and met inclusion criteria (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Patient sociodemographics including PROMs are shown in Table 1. Median age of the cohort was 65 years (IQR 56–72) and N = 1033 (54.2%) were female. The majority of the cohort was non-Hispanic White, N = 1561 (81.9%). Over half of the patients reported being married, N = 1091 (57.2%) and N = 896 (47%) had private insurance. The most common malignancies were gastrointestinal (n = 347, 18.2%), breast (n = 305, 16%), lymphoma (n = 245, 12.9%), lung (n = 230, 12.1%), and myeloma (n = 196, 10.3%). The median time from diagnosis to COST-FACIT survey was 88 days, range 46 to 303; with the larger range reflective of the heterogeneity of diseases and treatment timing; and inclusion of malignancies in which treatment may have begun in the inpatient setting. Financial toxicity

Of 1906 patients, N = 815 (43%) reported no FT at initial treatment, while N = 632 (33%) reported low, N = 414 (22%), moderate, and N = 45 (2%) high FT. Several baseline characteristics were associated with higher FT categories: younger age (p < 0.001), female sex (p = 0.03), Black race (p < 0.001), Hispanic ethnicity (p < 0.001), single marital status (p < 0.001), having Medicaid insurance (p < 0.001), smoking (p < 0.001), and higher ADI (p < 0.001). Of note, although Hispanic ethnicity was noted to be associated with higher FT, this is limited by very small numbers. PROMs were also associated with higher FT: higher distress as measured by NCCN DT (p < 0.001), depression as measured by higher PHQ-9 scores (p < 0.001), and lower HRQOL as measured by lower PROMIS-10 mental and physical scores (p < 0.001) (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Financial toxicity and patient-reported outcomes

Financial toxicity and patient-reported outcomes

There was no significant association between FT and cancer diagnosis, although a relatively higher proportion of high-impact FT patients had breast (24.4%), lymphoma (22.2%), and myeloma (13.3%) diagnoses (Supplementary Table 1). Disease stage and treatment were not significantly associated with FT, except for those who received both chemotherapy and hormone therapy; there were fewer patients with no FT (11.9%) compared to mild FT (17.4%) (p = 0.01).

Sensitivity analysis

To determine whether the cohort of patients with a COST-FACIT and/or PROM was representative of our cancer population, we performed sensitivity analyses evaluating differences between patients with a COST-FACIT survey and without (Supplementary Table 2). Patients with a completed COST-FACIT survey versus no COST-FACIT were younger (median age 65 versus 69 years, p < 0.001), had lower BMI (26.9 versus 27.4, p = 0.03), were more likely to be non-Hispanic White (82% versus 79%, p < 0.001), and have Medicare insurance (35% versus 13%, p < 0.001). Patients who completed a COST-FACIT survey also reported higher median NCCN DT score (3 versus 2, p = 0.03), and in general were more likely to complete other PROMs (Supplementary Table 2).

Risk factors for increasing financial toxicity

Several factors were associated with greater financial toxicity at time of initial treatment (Table 2). In univariable analysis, younger age (p < 0.001), female sex (p = 0.02), non-Hispanic Black (NHB) and Hispanic race, and ethnicity compared to non-Hispanic White (p < 0.001), Medicaid and Medicare payer (p < 0.002), smoking (p < 0.001), single marital status (p < 0.001), lower physical/mental well-being (p < 0.001), and increased distress (p < 0.001) were all associated with increasing FT. In multivariable analysis, younger age (decreasing per 5 years), (OR 1.18, 95% CI 1.12–1.23, p < 0.001); NHB vs non-Hispanic White (NHW), (OR 1.62, 95% CI 1.19–2.20, p = 0.002); Hispanic vs NHW (OR 2.0, 95% CI 1.11–3.59, p = 0.02); Medicaid vs Private insurance (OR 2.35, 95% CI 1.68–3.29, p < 0.001); Medicare vs Private insurance (OR 1.85, 95% CI 1.47–2.33, p < 0.001); former or current history of smoking (OR 1.34, 95% CI 1.08–1.66, p = 0.007 and OR 1.41, 95CI 1.05–1.89, p = 0.02); and increasing ADI, per 5%-ranks, (OR 1.04, 95% CI 1.02–1.06, p < 0.001) all remained significantly associated with higher FT.

Discussion

Our study describes financial toxicity as measured by COST-FACIT in a large real-world cohort of cancer patients seen at a tertiary care center with a FN program. We found several associations with higher FT including younger age, minority race and ethnicity, single marital status, Medicaid/Medicare insurance, smoking status, higher ADI, as well as higher distress and lower physical/mental health, all consistent with prior literature [23–26].

Although we did not initially find an association of higher FT with survival, an additional sensitivity analysis analyzing survival from time of COST-FACIT completion did demonstrate a significant association with moderate-high FT and worse survival. The median interval between diagnosis and COST-FACIT completion was 88 days, with 25% of assessments completed within 46 days and 10% completed within 28 days of diagnosis. This timing reflects the nature of financial toxicity as a patient-reported outcome that evolves after diagnosis and initiation of cancer care, rather than a baseline characteristic measurable at diagnosis. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that requiring survival until COST-FACIT completion introduces the possibility of guarantee-time bias. Additional clinical limitations may have influenced these findings, including heterogeneity of disease, treatment and patient characteristics of the cohort, and missing data.

Our current FN program at the Cleveland Clinic identifies all patients who are undergoing first treatment at the cancer center. After patients are scheduled for treatment, financial navigators review their benefits including insurance status, deductible, and maximum out-of-pocket expense. Patients are informed of their individualized costs with each treatment. Grant opportunities and medication programs and respective eligibility are evaluated based on diagnosis and medication. Barriers to care are also discussed. While the goal for the program is that the COST-FACIT is offered every 6 months, there remains missing longitudinal data, and current work is ongoing to optimize collection of longitudinal COST data to determine change in FT over time and potential impact of FN resources. The current data set is further limited by the lack of COST-FACIT data prior to the establishment of the FN program to better determine the impact of the FN program.

Although our cancer center is geographically positioned near multiple neighborhoods associated with high ADI, we found that patients with high ADI did have longer time to first treatment. The HR for this was small (2% longer time to first treatment) but still reflects a disparity. Our cancer center does track time to treat and has committed effort to improve this metric over time [27]. We also found that in the subset of patients with hematologic malignancies, moderate/high FT was associated with survival outcomes, while this was not found in solid tumor malignancies. Patients with hematologic malignancies are often initially diagnosed and initiate first treatment in the inpatient hospital setting and thus may not have the same initial FN touchpoint as those diagnosed with solid tumor malignancies; however this finding is exploratory and needs further investigation and validation.

In order to better understand if patients with a COST-FACIT survey were representative of our cancer center population, we performed sensitivity analyses which did highlight several differences between those who had a COST-FACIT survey and those who did not. Results thus may only be applicable to the described COST-FACIT population: patients were more likely to be younger, but also more likely to complete other PROMs (less missing data), and report higher distress by the NCCN DT. Non-Hispanic White patients were also more likely to complete the COST-FACIT compared to racial and ethnic minorities. While the reasons for non-completion are unfortunately not known for the entire cohort, anecdotal reports describe that many patients are reluctant to share financial information. As well, given the volume of patients seen across multiple sites and capacity of the financial navigators, patients may just not have received a COST survey. Thus, it is important to acknowledge the potential bias of patients who completed a COST and accordingly limits the generalizability of findings. In addition, while we acknowledge that this cohort was predominantly White, and the interpretation and significance of findings related to ethnic groups are limited by small numbers, we do note that among those who completed a COST-FACIT survey, approximately 18% were from minority groups; and these subgroups typically represent about 11% of the cancer center population.

We acknowledge several other limitations to this study, primarily due to its inclusion of a broad and heterogeneous cohort of patients. Several diseases were more representative than others, specifically breast, GI, leukemia and myeloma; whereas diagnosis of genitourinary, gynecological, head and neck, and skin cancers was under-represented; and thus, this study may lack generalizability to specific diagnoses. We also acknowledge and know from existing data that FT, time-to-treat, and survival outcomes may vary based on comorbidity, disease, stage, and treatment approach; however, the purpose of this study was to describe and provide a scoping overview of the burden of FT at a large cancer center. These initial results may inform future studies within specific subgroups, particularly as we continue to gather longitudinal data. As noted previously, optimization of follow-up COST-FACIT data will better inform the impact of a FN program and FN interventions. In addition, while we hope to better understand the relationships and interplay between health-related quality of life (HRQOL) and FT, based on the timing of routine PROM data collection and COST-FACIT results, it is not possible to infer causality or directionality of FT and other PROMs. As longitudinal data is collected, we plan to analyze the dynamic aspects of HRQOL and FT and how it may fluctuate with the clinical course.

In summary, this analysis confirms a significant burden of FT in our cancer population, as well as several factors associated with FT. We did not find any association of higher FT and survival or time-to-treat outcome. Further study is needed to better understand these findings within specific disease groups and the longitudinal impact of financial navigation on FT.
