Abstract
We study the problem of preemptively scheduling jobs online over time on a single machine to minimize the total flow time. In the traditional clairvoyant scheduling model, the scheduler learns about the processing time of a job at its arrival, and scheduling at any time the job with the shortest remaining processing time (SRPT) is optimal. In contrast, the practically relevant non-clairvoyant model assumes that the processing time of a job is unknown at its arrival, and is only revealed when it completes. Non-clairvoyant flow time minimization does not admit algorithms with a constant competitive ratio. Consequently, the problem has been studied under speed augmentation (JACM’00) or with predicted processing times (STOC’21, SODA’22) to attain constant guarantees. In this paper, we consider α \alpha -clairvoyant scheduling, where the scheduler learns the processing time of a job once it completes an α \alpha -fraction of its processing time. This naturally interpolates between clairvoyant scheduling ( α = 0 \alpha=0 ) and non-clairvoyant scheduling ( α = 1 \alpha=1 ). By elegantly fusing two traditional algorithms, we propose a scheduling rule with a competitive ratio of 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 1 − α ) \mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{1-\alpha}) whenever 0 ≤ α < 1 0\leq\alpha<1 . As α \alpha increases, our competitive guarantee transitions nicely (up to constants) between the previously established bounds for clairvoyant and non-clairvoyant flow time minimization. We complement this positive result with a tight randomized lower bound.



1
Introduction

We study the following fundamental online scheduling problem: Jobs arrive online over time without prior knowledge, and an algorithm can at any time process a job on a single machine, but cannot change its past decisions. Preemption is allowed, that is, an algorithm can stop processing a job and resume it at a later point in time.
The goal is to minimize the sum of flow times, where the
flow time
of a job is the time between its release time and completion time (also known as response time or pending time).
In the
clairvoyant
setting, where the processing time of a job becomes known at its arrival, a classic result of
[
Sch68
]
says that scheduling at any time the job with the least amount of remaining work is optimal (called Shortest-Remaining-Processing-Time rule, in short SRPT). In particular, SRPT is a 1-competitive online algorithm for this problem; we say that an algorithm is
\alpha
-
competitive
if its schedule has a total flow time of at most
\alpha
times the total flow time of an offline optimum solution. The
competitive ratio
of an algorithm is the smallest
\alpha
such that it is
\alpha
-competitive.

However, practically relevant in many applications, such as scheduling tasks in computing systems, is the
non-clairvoyant
scenario, where an algorithm has no knowledge about a job’s processing time until it completes.
In contrast to the well manageable clairvoyant variant,
[
MPT94
]
showed a strong lower bound of
\Omega(n^{1/3})
on the competitive ratio of every deterministic non-clairvoyant algorithm, where
n
is the total number of jobs.

Yet, due to its practical relevance, several approaches have been considered to overcome this strong negative result:

Randomization:

If the algorithm is allowed to use randomization, a
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
-competitive algorithm is known
[
BL04
,
KP03
]
, which is best-possible
[
MPT94
]
.

Speed Augmentation:

If the algorithm’s machine runs at speed
(1+\varepsilon)
times the speed of the optimum’s machine, then a
\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})
-competitive algorithm is known, for any
\varepsilon>0

[
KP00
]
.

Predictions:

If the algorithm receives at a job’s release a prediction on its processing time such that the ratio between the predicted and actual processing time (in any direction) is bounded by some constant
\mu\geq 1
, then a
\mathcal{O}(\mu^{2}\log\mu)
-competitive algorithm is known
[
ALT21
,
ALT22
]
.
Specifically, there are
\mathcal{O}(1)
-competitive algorithms for the semi-clairvoyant model, where the power-of-2 class of a job’s processing time is known
[
Bec+04
]
.

We follow the spirit of these approaches and consider a minimal relaxation of the non-clairvoyant model for which we give a
\mathcal{O}(1)
-competitive algorithm.

The model is based on common techniques used in practice, e.g., operating systems or cloud computing: there, job lengths are commonly estimated by
profiling
techniques
[
HRB18
,
Wee+17
,
Din02
,
AK17
]
. In particular, it is expected that this information gets more accurate the longer the job has been processed
[
Cht+12
,
NF09
,
HRB18
,
Xie+23
]
.
We embed these practical approaches in the following theoretical model: For any
\alpha\in[0,1]
, every job
emits a signal
after an
\alpha
-fraction of it has been processed. Thus, an algorithm essentially learns about a job’s remaining processing time when it emits.
We call this model
\alpha
-clairvoyant. Observe that it naturally interpolates between the clairvoyant setting (
\alpha=0
) and the non-clairvoyant setting (
\alpha=1
).

The
\alpha
-clairvoyant model has previously been mentioned in an extended abstract by
[
YT17
]
, where they also presented an
\mathcal{O}(1)
-competitive algorithm if
0\leq\alpha<\frac{1}{4}
. However, they also showed that their approach is
not

\mathcal{O}(1)
-competitive for
\alpha\geq 0.5
, and it remained open whether a
\mathcal{O}(1)
-competitive algorithm for every constant
\alpha\in[0,1)
is possible.

While in general
\alpha
-clairvoyance is incomparable
to speed augmentation or predictions,
there are arguments indicating that it is not stronger than these previously studied models: Unlike in the setting with predictions, an
\alpha
-clairvoyant algorithm has no additional knowledge at the time when a job arrives, and thus, must take potentially bad decisions in order to gain additional knowledge. Given
(1+\varepsilon)
-speed augmentation, an algorithm essentially can learn about a job’s processing time when it completes the job and an optimum has only processed a
\Theta(1-\varepsilon)
-fraction of that job. This can roughly be translated to the
\alpha
-clairvoyant setting, with the difference
here the algorithm must also schedule the remaining
\Theta(\varepsilon)
-fraction to complete it.
Another reason showing that speed augmentation is stronger comes from the more general objective of total
weighted
flow time: Here, even a clairvoyant algorithm cannot have a constant competitive ratio
[
BC09
]
, but
(1+\varepsilon)
-speed augmentation suffices to achieve that
[
BD07
,
KC03
]
.

Despite these intuitive arguments that the
\alpha
-clairvoyant model is not as strong as the previously considered models, we are still able to give a
\mathcal{O}(1)
-competitive algorithm for
\alpha
-clairvoyant total flow time scheduling whenever
\alpha\in[0,1)
.

1.1
Our Results

We prove the following main theorem:

Theorem 1.1
.

For every
\alpha\in[0,1)
, there exists a
\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{1-\alpha})
-competitive
\alpha
-clairvoyant online algorithm for minimizing the total flow time on a single machine.

A consequence of this result is the following insight: for any non-constant lower bound for the non-clairvoyant setting, an adversary needs to be able to only fix a job’s remaining processing time once its value is
non-constant
, i.e., in
o(1)
, even if the total processing time of the job is in
\Omega(1)
. This is exactly the property on which the
\Omega(n^{1/3})
lower bound relies on, where the remaining processing times of all available jobs are fixed to
\Theta(n^{-1/3})

[
MPT94
]
. This is not possible in the
\alpha
-clairvoyant setting for constant values of
\alpha
. Our result shows that such a construction is indeed necessary for proving such strong lower bounds.
In other words, it implies that the classic non-clairvoyant model is exactly at the border for which no constant competitive algorithms are possible.

We complement this observation with a randomized lower bound, which essentially bridges the gap towards the previous lower bounds: the later we receive information about a job’s remaining processing time, the larger is the competitive ratio. Specifically, the dependency on
\alpha
of our analysis is asymptotically best-possible.

Theorem 1.2
.

For every constant
\alpha\in[0,1)
, every randomized
\alpha
-clairvoyant algorithm has a competitive ratio of at least
\Omega(\frac{1}{1-\alpha})
for minimizing the total flow time on a single machine.

While this lower bounds is mainly interesting when
\alpha
approaches
1
, we also investigate its behavior when
\alpha
approaches
0
. Recall that if
\alpha=0
, SRPT produces an optimal schedule.
Naturally, one would therefore expect a “PTAS-like” behavior where the competitive ratio approaches
1
when
\alpha
goes to
0
. Surprisingly, we show that this is not the case: for every
\alpha>0
, the competitive ratio of every randomized or deterministic
\alpha
-clairvoyant algorithm is at least
\frac{3}{2}
or
2
, respectively.

Further Related Work

[
MPT94
]
showed that every deterministic algorithm has a competitive ratio of at least
P

\mathcal{O}(\min\{\log W,\log P,\log D\})

AT18

\frac{w_{j}}{p_{j}}
, and builds up on earlier works
[
BD07

CKZ01

.
In the offline setting, unlike the unweighted problem, it is NP-hard
[
LRB77
]
, and a PTAS has been established very recently
[
ARW23
]
.

Under
(1+\varepsilon)
-speed augmentation, simple greedy clairvoyant strategies such as scheduling at any time the job of highest density are
\mathcal{O}(1)
-competitive for total weighted flow time, even for parallel identical machines
[
Bec+01

. For unrelated machines, where every job has a potentially different processing time on every machine, there also are

\mathcal{O}(\log(n)\cdot\log(\min\{\frac{n}{m},P\}))

There are also non-clairvoyant results with processing time predictions
[
APT22
,
ZLZ22

and with speed augmentation
[
Che+04
,

2
The Algorithm

We use standard notation for flow time scheduling. For a job
j
, we denote by
p_{j}
its processing time and by
r_{j}
its release date.
For a time
t
, we denote by
y_{j}(t)
the total amount of processing that
j
has received until time
t
, and we use
p_{j}(t)\coloneq p_{j}-y_{j}(t)
to denote its remaining processing time. Moreover, for a time interval
I=[t_{1},t_{2}]
, we write
q_{j}(I)\coloneq y_{j}(t_{2})-y_{j}(t_{1})
to denote the amount of processing that
j
receives during that interval. We denote by
C_{j}
the completion time of
j
.
For a time
t
, we use
A(t)=\{j\mid r_{j}\leq t<C_{j}\}
to refer to the set of available jobs at time
t
.

Our algorithm is a combination of the following two classic algorithms for flow time scheduling from the literature:

•

Shortest-Remaining-Processing-Time-first (SRPT):
At any time
t
, schedule the job in
A(t)
that has the smallest remaining processing time at
t
.

•

Shortest-Elapsed-Time-first (SETF):
At any time
t
, process all jobs
j\in A(t)
with smallest
y_{j}(t)
at the same rate, that is, evenly distribute the processing power of
1
among these jobs.

SRPT is 1-competitive for clairvoyant online scheduling
[
Sch68
]
, and SETF is
\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})
-competitive for non-clairvoyant online scheduling with
(1+\varepsilon)
-speed augmentation, for every
\varepsilon>0

[
KP00
]
.

For the
\alpha
-clairvoyant setting, we introduce additional notation.
For a job
j
, we denote by
s_{j}
the time at which job
j

emitted its signal
, that is, the earliest point in time
t
at which
y_{j}(t)=\alpha p_{j}
.
Moreover, at any time
t
, we partition
A(t)
into the set
N(t)\coloneq\{j\in A(t)\mid y_{j}(t)\leq\alpha p_{j}\}
of
non-clairvoyant
jobs and the set
C(t)\coloneq\{j\in A(t)\mid y_{j}(t)>\alpha p_{j}\}
of
clairvoyant
jobs. Finally, we use
D(t)
for the set of jobs that have been completed by time
t
.

Intuition

On a high-level, at any time
t
,
our algorithm either executes SETF on the set of non-clairvoyant jobs
N(t)
,
or it executes SRPT on the set of clairvoyant job
C(t)
.
Note that the latter is possible, because the algorithm can derive the remaining processing time of jobs in
C(t)
.
The crucial decision is which of both rules the algorithm should execute at time
t
. Intuitively, we design this decision such that whenever the algorithm executes SRPT, its behavior is equivalent to executing SRPT on
all
available jobs
A(t)
, and similarly, whenever the algorithm executes SETF, its behavior is equivalent to executing SETF on
all
available jobs
A(t)
.
In other words, whenever the algorithm knows that a certain job
j
has the least remaining processing time among all available jobs, it will work on
j
.

Algorithm Description

To achieve this, we define the algorithm as follows for every time
t
:

1.

If
\min_{j\in C(t)}p_{j}(t)\leq\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\cdot\min_{j\in N(t)}y_{j}(t)
, schedule the job in
C(t)
with the smallest remaining processing time at time
t
. If there are multiple such jobs, schedule the job in
C(t)
that emitted last, i.e., a job
j\in C(t)
of maximum
s_{j}
. Break all remaining ties arbitrarily.

2.

Otherwise, that is,
\min_{j\in C(t)}p_{j}(t)>\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\cdot\min_{j\in N(t)}y_{j}(t)
, process all jobs in
N(t)
that have received the smallest amount of processing so far at the same rate.

Properties

We now verify that this algorithm ensures the properties mentioned above. First, note that if
\min_{j\in C(t)}p_{j}(t)\leq\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\cdot\min_{j\in N(t)}y_{j}(t)
, we schedule a job
k
that satisfies
p_{k}(t)=\min_{j^{\prime}\in C(t)}p_{j}(t)\leq\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\cdot\min_{j^{\prime}\in N(t)}y_{j^{\prime}}(t)\leq\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\cdot y_{j}(t)\leq\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\cdot\alpha p_{j}\leq(1-\alpha)p_{j}\leq p_{j}(t)
for every job
j\in N(t)
, and
p_{k}(t)\leq p_{j}(t)
for every job
j\in C(t)
. Here,
y_{j}(t)\leq\alpha p_{j}
and
p_{j}(t)\geq(1-\alpha)p_{j}
hold by definition of
N(t)
and since
j\in N(t)
. Thus, we arrive at the following observation.

Observation 2.1
.

At any time
t
, if
\min_{j\in C(t)}p_{j}(t)\leq\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\cdot\min_{j\in N(t)}y_{j}(t)
, then the algorithm processes a job
k\in C(t)
with shortest remaining processing time, i.e.,
p_{k}(t)\leq p_{j}(t)
for all
j\in A(t)
.

Second, if
\min_{j\in C(t)}p_{j}(t)>\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\cdot\min_{j\in N(t)}y_{j}(t)
, we schedule jobs
k
that satisfy
y_{k}(t)\leq y_{j}(t)
for every
j\in N(t)
, and
y_{k}(t)=\min_{j^{\prime}\in N(t)}y_{j^{\prime}}(t)<\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\cdot\min_{j^{\prime}\in C(t)}p_{j^{\prime}}(t)\leq\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}p_{j}(t)\leq\alpha p_{j}\leq y_{j}(t)
for every
j\in C(t)
. This gives us the following observation.

Observation 2.2
.

At any time
t
, if
\min_{j\in C(t)}p_{j}(t)>\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\cdot\min_{j\in N(t)}y_{j}(t)
, then the algorithm processes jobs
S\subseteq N(t)
with minimal progress, i.e.,
y_{j^{\prime}}(t)\leq y_{j}(t)
for all
j^{\prime}\in S
and
j\in A(t)
.

Finally, the definition of our algorithm implies the following property, which will be important in the analysis by describing the structure of the algorithm’s schedule.

Observation 2.3
.

Consider a job
k
that is executed at time
t^{\prime}
with
k\in C(t^{\prime})
. Then, for every job
j
with
r_{j}<t^{\prime}<C_{j}
it holds that
j
is not executed during
[t^{\prime},C_{k}]
and
C_{k}\leq C_{j}
. In particular, this applies to a job
k
that emits at time
t^{\prime}
, that is,
t^{\prime}=s_{k}
.

Proof.

Consider a job
j
with
r_{j}<t^{\prime}<C_{j}
.
Since
k
is executed at time
t^{\prime}
, we have by the definition of the algorithm that
p_{k}(t^{\prime})\leq\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}y_{j}(t^{\prime})
if
j\in N(t^{\prime})
and
p_{k}(t^{\prime})\leq p_{j}(t^{\prime})
if
j\in C(t^{\prime})
. In any case, since
p_{k}(t^{\prime+})<p_{k}(t^{\prime})
, the algorithm would never process
j
after time
t^{\prime}
if
k
is also available. ∎

Overview of the Analysis

In this section, we give an extensive overview of our analysis. We will prove our main theorem (
Theorem

1.1
) for the algorithm presented in
Section

2
via
local competitiveness

[
Sch68
]
. To this end, we fix an instance and an optimal solution, and we denote by
O(t)
the set of available jobs at time
t
in that solution.
We assume w.l.o.g. that
\frac{1}{1-\alpha}
is integer, which does not change the bound in
Theorem

1.1
.

We prove the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

At any time
t
, it holds that
|A(t)\setminus O(t)|\leq\big(3+\frac{2}{1-\alpha}\big)\cdot|O(t)|
.

Then,
|A(t)|\leq\big(4+\frac{2}{1-\alpha}\big)\cdot|O(t)|
, and
Theorem

1.1
follows via integration over time.
Thus, it remains to prove
Theorem

3.1
. To this end, we fix any time
t
for the remainder of this section. Note that if
|O(t)|=0
, then also
|A(t)|=0
because our algorithm is non-idling. Thus, we can assume that
|O(t)|\geq 1
. Moreover, we assume that
|A(t)\setminus O(t)|\geq 1
, as otherwise the statement is trivial.
For a job
j
, we denote with
t_{j}
the latest point in time before or at
t
at which
j
is being processed.

3.1
Borrowing

We start by defining
borrowing

[
KP00
]
. The goal of this concept is to bring the algorithm’s schedule in relation to the fixed optimal schedule by describing local transformations that allow to modify the algorithm’s schedule into the optimal schedule.
We prove in the next section that such a transformation via these local operations must be possible, which will ultimately allow us to compare both schedules.
To make schedules easier to describe, we first introduce
lifetimes
.

Definition 3.2

(Lifetime)
.

Consider a fixed schedule.
For a job
j
, we refer to
I_{j}\coloneq[r_{j},\min\{C_{j},t\}]
as the
lifetime
of
j
. The
lifetime

I(Q)
of a set
Q\subseteq J
is defined as
I(Q)\coloneq\bigcup_{j\in Q}I_{j}
.

Now, observe that if a job
i
receives
\gamma
units of processing during the lifetime of another job
j\in A(t)
, we can find a similar schedule where instead of
i
we process
j
. As a result,
j
receives up to
\gamma
units of processing more until time
t
. We say that
j

borrowed
from
i
. We further distinguish whether job
i
receives processing while being non-clairvoyant or clairvoyant. Clearly, there can be both situations for some fixed
j
and
i
. In the following, we give precise definitions of this extension of the concept used in
[
KP00
]
.

Borrowing is not tailored to the schedule of our algorithm. Thus, for the remaining section, we fix any non-idling schedule
S
. For any schedule-specific quantity, we add as superscript the schedule in which we consider this quantity. E.g., we denote with
A^{S}(t^{\prime})
the set of available jobs at time
t^{\prime}
in
S
. In later sections, when the schedule is clear from the context, we will omit any such indications.

Definition 3.3

(Borrowing)
.

We say that a job
j
can
directly borrow non-clairvoyantly
from
i
if
i
is executed at any time
t^{\prime}
with
i\in N^{S}(t^{\prime})
during the lifetime of
j
, that is,
t^{\prime}\in I^{S}_{j}
, and write
j\leftarrowtail^{S}_{N}i
.
We say that a job
j
can
directly borrow clairvoyantly
from
i
if
i
is executed at any time
t^{\prime}
with
i\in C^{S}(t^{\prime})
during the lifetime of
j
, that is,
t^{\prime}\in I^{S}_{j}
, and write
j\leftarrowtail^{S}_{C}i
.

Definition 3.4

(Borrow Graph)
.

Let
E^{S}_{N}\coloneq\{(j,i)\mid j\leftarrowtail^{S}_{N}i\}
and
E^{S}_{C}\coloneq\{(j,i)\mid j\leftarrowtail^{S}_{C}i\}
. The borrow graph is the multi-graph
G^{S}_{B}=(J,E^{S})
with
E^{S}=E^{S}_{N}\cup E^{S}_{C}
.

For a job
j
, we denote by
R^{S}_{j}
the set of jobs that are reachable from
j
in
G^{S}_{B}
(including
j
).
Moreover, for every
i\in R^{S}_{j}
, we say that
j
can
borrow
from
i
.
Given a set
Q
of edges or vertices of
G^{S}_{B}
, we call a path a
Q
-path if it only uses edges or vertices in
Q
, respectively. For example, we call a path
P
in
G_{B}
that only uses edges from
E^{S}_{N}
an
E^{S}_{N}
-path
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
P
be a path in
G^{S}_{B}
. Then,
I^{S}(P)=[\min_{j\in P}r_{j},\max_{j\in P}\min\{C^{S}_{j},t\}]
.

Proof.

By definition of
I^{S}(P)
, we clearly have that
\min_{j\in P}r_{j}
is the smallest value in
I^{S}(P)
, and that
\max_{j\in P}\min\{C^{S}_{j},t\}
is the largest value in
I^{S}(P)
. Thus, it remains to argue that
I^{S}(P)
has no gaps.

To see this, assume otherwise, and let
t^{\prime}
be a value with
\min_{j\in P}r_{j}<t^{\prime}<\max_{j\in P}\min\{C^{S}_{j},t\}
and
t^{\prime}\not\in I^{S}(P)
.
Define
P_{1}\coloneq\{j_{1}\in P\mid I^{S}_{j_{1}}\subseteq[\min_{j\in P}r_{j},t^{\prime})\}
and

P_{2}\coloneq\{j_{2}\in P\mid I^{S}_{j_{2}}\subseteq(t^{\prime},\max_{j\in P}\min\{C^{S}_{j},t\}]\}
.
Observe that
P_{1}\cup P_{2}=P
, and, by definition of
I^{S}(P)
, neither
P_{1}
nor
P_{2}
can be empty.
Thus, there must exist at least one edge
e
on
P
between a job
j_{1}\in P_{1}
and
j_{2}\in P_{2}
(in some direction). However,
I^{S}_{j_{1}}\cap I^{S}_{j_{2}}=\emptyset
implies that
j_{1}
and
j_{2}
cannot directly borrow from each other; a contradiction to the existence of
e
in
G^{S}_{B}
.
∎

Lemma

3.5
immediately gives the following corollaries.

Corollary 3.6
.

For any path

If a path

in

Consider a job

Proof.

3.2
The Flow Problem

We continue with a refinement of the borrow graph. The idea is that the borrow graph only tells us which job can in principle borrow from which job, but not
how much
amount of processing. For our analysis, however, it will be important to argue about the maximum amount of work that multiple jobs can borrow via some job
i
in a certain time interval.

To this end, we introduce a refinement of the borrow graph and define a flow problem for it. A feasible flow then exactly describes how much amount of work jobs can borrow from each other. In particular, this is a non-trivial extensions to the arguments used in
[
KP00
]
.

This concept, similar to borrowing, is not tailored to the schedule of our algorithm.
Thus, throughout this section, we consider any non-idling schedule
S
.

Definition 3.9

(Flow Network)
.

Let
T^{S}
be any time discretization such that
T^{S}\supseteq\{r_{j}\mid r_{j}\leq t,j\in J\}\cup\{C^{S}_{j}\mid r_{j}\leq t,j\in J\}\cup\{0,t\}
and
\tau\leq t
for every
\tau\in T^{S}
, and denote
T^{S}=\{\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{\ell+1}\}
such that
\tau_{\ell^{\prime}}\leq\tau_{\ell^{\prime\prime}}
if
\ell^{\prime}<\ell^{\prime\prime}
.
The
flow network

G^{S}_{F}
w.r.t.
T^{S}
is defined as follows:

•

For every job
j\in J
, there is a vertex
j
.

•

For every job
i\in J
and for every
\ell^{\prime}\in\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
,
we introduce one vertex
v^{i}_{\ell^{\prime}}
and an edge
(v^{i}_{\ell^{\prime}},i)
with capacity
q^{S}_{i}([\tau_{\ell^{\prime}},\tau_{\ell^{\prime}+1}])
, and for every
j\in J\setminus\{i\}
, we introduce the edge
(j,v_{\ell^{\prime}}^{i})
with infinite capacity
if
[\tau_{\ell^{\prime}},\tau_{\ell^{\prime}+1}]\subseteq I_{j}
.
We also refer to the vertices
v_{\ell^{\prime}}^{i}
as
dummy vertices
.

•

Every vertex
j\in A^{S}(t)\setminus O(t)
has a supply equal to
p^{S}_{j}(t)
and every vertex
i\in O(t)
has a demand equal to
y^{S}_{i}(t)
. All other supplies and demands are equal to zero.

Let
f\colon V(G^{S}_{F})\times V(G^{S}_{F})\to\mathbb{R}
be a feasible flow in
G^{S}_{F}
. We use the standard convention that if
(v,w)\in V(G^{S}_{F})\times V(G^{S}_{F})
is not in
E(G^{S}_{F})
, then
f(v,w)=0
. We extend the notation for
f
as follows. For all
j,i\in J
, we denote by
f(j,i)
the total amount of flow that is directly sent from
j
to
i
in
G^{S}_{F}
, that is,
f(j,i)\coloneq\sum_{\ell^{\prime}=1}^{\ell}f(j,v_{\ell^{\prime}}^{i})
.

The main theorem of this section guarantees the existence of a feasible flow in
G^{S}_{F}
that uses the total supply.

Theorem 3.10
.

There exists a feasible flow in
G^{S}_{F}
that uses the total supply of jobs in
A^{S}(t)\setminus O(t)
.

Intuitively, this implies that there exists a set of borrow operations such that every job
j\in A^{S}(t)\setminus O(t)
can be completed until time
t
, and only the work of jobs in
O(t)
until time
t
decreases by these operations, as these jobs are the only vertices in
G_{F}^{S}
with positive demand.
The remainder of this section is devoted to proving this theorem.

We start with the observation that we arbitrarily increase the precision of the time discretization without changing
f(j,i)
for all
j,i\in J
. Note that increasing the precision essentially does not change the structure of the flow network and may only lead to more dummy vertices.

Observation 3.11
.

Let
f
be a feasible flow for
G_{F}^{S}
w.r.t.
T^{S}
, and
0\leq t^{\prime}\leq t
. Then,
there exists a feasible flow
f^{\prime}
for
G_{F}^{S}
w.r.t.
T^{S}\cup\{t^{\prime}\}
such that
f(j,i)=f^{\prime}(j,i)
for all
j,i\in J
.

The following proposition holds as we can compute a maximum flow for
G_{F}^{S}
by using a standard augmenting-path algorithm that never uses vertices in
O(t)
as inner nodes of an augmenting path.

Proposition 3.12
.

There always exists a maximum feasible flow
f
in
G_{F}^{S}
such that
\sum_{j\in J\setminus\{i\}}f(i,j)=0
for all
i\in O(t)
. That is, vertices with positive demand do not have outgoing flow in
f
.

The following lemma implies that we can find a new schedule in which the supply and demands changed according to the flow.

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
f
be a feasible flow in
G^{S}_{F}
and let
j,i\in J
be such that
f(j,i)>0
and
\sum_{i^{\prime}\in J\setminus\{j\}}f(i^{\prime},j)=0
.
Then, there exists a

, a feasible non-idling schedule

, and a feasible flow

for

such that

(a)

and

,

(b)

y_{k}^{S^{\prime}}(t)=y^{S}_{k}(t)
for all jobs

(c)

(d)

for all job pairs

Lemma 3.14
.

Let

and

. Then, there is path through jobs

with positive capacity in

if and only if there is a path through jobs

in

.

We finally prove the main theorem of this section.

Proof of

.

Fix any non-idling schedule

.
For the sake of contradiction, we assume that there exists no feasible flow in

of value

be a feasible flow of maximum value

.

Now, observe that if there exists a job with positive supply, then there also exists a job in

without any incoming flow, as otherwise we send flow on a cycle. Thus, we can iteratively and exhaustively apply
Lemma

3.13
. Let
S^{\prime}
be the resulting schedule, and

the resulting flow in

.

Note that since

and by the properties of

,
A^{S^{\prime}}(t)\setminus O(t)
cannot be empty, that is, there exists a job
j\in J\setminus O(t)
such that

. Moreover, for every job

, there cannot exist a job
i\in R^{S^{\prime}}_{j}\cap O(t)
with

.
Otherwise,

guarantees that there exists a path

of positive capacity from

to

and

and apply

to all consecutive jobs on

to find a flow of a larger value; a contradiction. Thus,
R^{S^{\prime}}_{j}\cap O(t)=\emptyset
for every job

, because a job

with
y^{S^{\prime}}_{i}(t)=0
clearly cannot be reached by any other job in
G_{B}^{S^{\prime}}
.

Let
j_{1}
be the job with minimum release date in

. Thus, for all

,

gives

, and thus,

.
Let

denote the minimum release date over the jobs in
R^{S^{\prime}}_{j_{1}}
. Note that we might have

if the minimum release date belongs to a job that completes by time

in both

and the optimal schedule.
By definition of lifetimes, we have
I(R^{S^{\prime}}_{j_{1}})=[r^{*},t]
.
Thus,

gives that every job

and completed by time

is also contained in

. In particular, this means that

only processes jobs in

during

. However, as

, the schedule

is not able to complete all of these jobs by point in time

. As

is non-idling and only works on jobs in

Since

by only working on them during

.
However, this is a contradiction to

Let

3.4
Matching Arguments

The goal of this section is to give intuition on how we use
Theorem

3.10
to bound

We first discuss a scenario where

This means that no job has emitted so far, and thus, the algorithm has only executed SETF. Consider two jobs

In this scenario, we can observe that job

For an arbitrary scenario, i.e., without the assumption that

3.5

For the sake of clarity, we use in this section the notation

Section

3.4
: If
\bm{\bar{}}{S}=\emptyset
for all
S\in\mathcal{S}
then we can use the analysis of
Section

3.4
by plugging in the truncated progress instead of the actual progress.
If
\bm{\bar{}}{S}\neq\emptyset
, then let
j^{*}(S)
be the job in
\bm{\bar{}}{S}
of minimum release date. Note that
j^{*}(S)\in S
.

Lemma 3.25
.

There is an order
S_{1},\ldots,S_{L}
of all segments
\mathcal{S}
such that
O_{S_{1}}\subsetneq\ldots\subsetneq O_{S_{L}}
.

Proof.

Let
i_{1},\ldots,i_{|O|}
denote the elements of
O
ordered by non-decreasing
\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{i}
, i.e.,
\ell\leq\ell^{\prime}
implies
\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{i_{\ell}}\leq\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{i_{\ell^{\prime}}}
. For each
j\in N\setminus O
, the set
O_{j}
is a possibly empty prefix of
i_{1},\ldots,i_{|O|}
. This implies that there must exist an order
S_{1},\ldots,S_{L}
of the segments
\mathcal{S}
such that
O_{S_{1}}\subseteq\ldots\subseteq O_{S_{L}}
. We conclude the proof by observing that the definition of a segment implies that if
O_{S_{\ell}}=O_{S_{\ell^{\prime}}}
then
S_{\ell}=S_{\ell^{\prime}}
.
∎

As an immediate corollary of
Lemma

3.25
, we can bound the number of segments by
|O|
.

Corollary 3.26
.

|\mathcal{S}|\leq|O|+1
.

For the remainder of this section, let
S_{1},\ldots,S_{L}
be the segments in
\mathcal{S}
indexed as in
Lemma

3.25
. For a segment
S
, we use the shorthand notation

S^{\prime}\coloneq\begin{cases}S\setminus\{j^{*}(S)\}&\text{ if }\bm{\bar{}}{S}\neq\emptyset\\
S&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Clearly,
|S|\geq|S^{\prime}|\geq|S|-1
for any segment
S
.

The following two lemmas are the main ingredients to the proof of
Theorem

3.20
. They bound the maximum amount of work that jobs in certain segments can borrow from certain jobs in
O
. While the first lemma is essentially just a corollary of
Theorem

3.16
, proving the second lemma will require a stronger structural understanding of the borrow graph
G_{B}
and flow network
G_{F}
regarding bottlenecks, and thus, is deferred to the subsequent sections.
Recall that
L=|\mathcal{S}|
.

Lemma 3.27

(Good borrowing)
.

For every
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,L\}
, we have

\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\sum_{j\in S_{k}}\sum_{i\in O_{S_{\ell}}}\beta(j,i)\leq\sum_{i\in O_{S_{\ell}}}y_{i}(t).

Proof.

By
Theorem

3.16
, it holds that
\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\sum_{j\in S_{k}}\beta(j,i)\leq y_{i}(t)
for every
i\in O_{S_{\ell}}
. Summing over all
i\in O_{S_{\ell}}
yields the stated bound.
∎

Lemma 3.28

(Bottleneck borrowing)
.

Let
S
be a segment such that
\bm{\bar{}}{S}\neq\emptyset
. Then,
it holds that

\sum_{j\in S}\sum_{i\in O\setminus O_{S}}\beta(j,i)\leq p_{j^{*}(S)}(t).

Lemma

3.28
ties into the intuition given in
Section

3.4
that jobs
j\in N\setminus O
can only borrow from jobs
i\in O
with
\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{i}>\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{j}
via certain bottlenecks. We will prove the lemma in the subsequent sections by first showing that there is a
single
bottleneck that needs to be part of
every
path in the borrow graph from
any

j\in S
to
any

i\in O\setminus O_{S}
, and second proving that the capacity of this bottleneck is at most
p_{j^{*}(S)}(t)
.

We can compose
Lemma

3.27
and
Lemma

3.28

to the following lemma.

Lemma 3.29
.

For every
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,L\}
, we have

\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\sum_{j\in S_{k}}\sum_{i\in O}\beta(j,i)\leq\sum_{i\in O_{S_{\ell}}}y_{i}(t)+\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}p_{j^{*}(S_{k})}(t)\cdot\mathds{1}[\bm{\bar{}}{S}_{k}\neq\emptyset]\ .

Using this, we can prove the following technical lemma, which bounds the number of jobs in every prefix of the set of ordered segments. The proof of
Theorem

3.20
is then essentially a corollary of it.

Lemma 3.30
.

For each
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,L\}
, it holds that
\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}|S_{k}^{\prime}|\leq\frac{1}{1-\alpha}|O_{S_{\ell}}|
.

Proof.

Recall that we assume that
\frac{1}{1-\alpha}
is integer. We will first split every job
i\in O
into
\frac{1}{1-\alpha}

slots
, and each slot receives a
1-\alpha
fraction of
y_{i}(t)
as progress at time
t
. Then, we will prove for each
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,L\}
that there exists a matching that matches every job in
\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}|S_{k}^{\prime}|
to some slot, which implies the lemma.

To this end, we first introduce
\frac{1}{1-\alpha}
slots
\bm{\hat{}}{O}(i)
for every
i\in O
. Let
\bm{\hat{}}{O}\coloneq\bigcup_{i\in O}\bm{\hat{}}{O}(i)
be the total set of slots. For every
j\in N\setminus O
, we define
\bm{\hat{}}{O}_{j}\coloneq\bigcup_{i\in O_{j}}\bm{\hat{}}{O}(i)
and for every segment
S
, we define
\bm{\hat{}}{O}_{S}\coloneq\bigcup_{i\in O_{S}}\bm{\hat{}}{O}(i)
. Note that
\frac{1}{1-\alpha}|O_{j}|=|\bm{\hat{}}{O}_{j}|
,
\frac{1}{1-\alpha}|O_{S}|=|\bm{\hat{}}{O}_{S}|
, and
\frac{1}{1-\alpha}|O|=|\bm{\hat{}}{O}|
. Further, we define
\bm{\hat{}}{y}_{i^{\prime}}(t)\coloneq(1-\alpha)y_{i}(t)
for every
i^{\prime}\in O(i)
and
i\in O
.
Thus, for every segment
S
, every
j\in S
and
i\in\bm{\hat{}}{O}_{j}
, we have
\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{j}\geq\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{i}
, and further,

p_{j}(t)\geq\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}y_{j}(t)=\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{j}\geq\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{i}\geq\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}y_{i}(t)\geq\bm{\hat{}}{y}_{i}(t)\ .

(1)

We now prove by induction that, for every
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,L\}
, it holds that
\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}|S_{k}^{\prime}|\leq|\bm{\hat{}}{O}_{S_{\ell}}|
. This will conclude the lemma.

We start with
\ell=1
.

Theorem

3.16
and
Lemma

3.29
imply

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in S_{1}}p_{j}(t)=\sum_{j\in S_{1}}\sum_{i\in O}\beta(j,i)

\displaystyle\leq p_{j^{*}(S_{1})}(t)\cdot\mathds{1}[\bm{\bar{}}{S}_{1}\neq\emptyset]+\sum_{i\in O_{S_{1}}}y_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=p_{j^{*}(S_{1})}(t)\cdot\mathds{1}[\bm{\bar{}}{S}_{1}\neq\emptyset]+\sum_{i\in\bm{\hat{}}{O}_{S_{1}}}\bm{\hat{}}{y}_{i}(t)\ ,

which implies
\sum_{j\in S^{\prime}_{1}}p_{j}(t)\leq\sum_{i\in\bm{\hat{}}{O}_{S_{1}}}\bm{\hat{}}{y}_{i}(t)
. Now, we can use (
1
) to conclude that every term in the left sum of the inequality is at least as large as every term in the right sum. Thus,
|S^{\prime}_{1}|\leq|\bm{\hat{}}{O}_{S_{1}}|
.

For the induction step, fix
\ell\geq 2
, and assume that for every
\ell^{\prime}\in\{1,\ldots,\ell-1\}
,
we have
\sum_{k=1}^{\ell^{\prime}}|S^{\prime}_{k}|\leq|\bm{\hat{}}{O}_{S_{\ell^{\prime}}}|
. Also recall that
\bm{\hat{}}{O}_{j}=\bm{\hat{}}{O}_{j^{\prime}}
for all
j,j^{\prime}\in S
for every segment
S
.
Thus, for every
Z\subseteq\bigcup_{k=1}^{\ell-1}S^{\prime}_{k}
, we have
|Z|\leq|\bigcup_{j\in Z}\bm{\hat{}}{O}_{j}|
.
By Hall’s Theorem, this means that for each
j\in\bigcup_{k=1}^{\ell-1}S^{\prime}_{k}
, there exists a slot
m(j)\in\bm{\hat{}}{O}_{j}\subseteq\bm{\hat{}}{O}_{S_{\ell}}
such that for every slot
i\in\bm{\hat{}}{O}_{S_{\ell}}
it holds that
|\{j\mid m(j)=i\}|\leq 1
, that is, every slot is matched at most once.
Let
M\coloneq\{m(j)\mid j\in\cup_{k=1}^{\ell-1}S^{\prime}_{k}\}
be the set of matched slots in
\bm{\hat{}}{O}_{S_{\ell}}
.

Theorem

3.16
and
Lemma

3.29
imply

\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\sum_{j\in S_{k}}p_{j}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\sum_{j\in S_{k}}\sum_{i\in O}\beta(j,i)\leq\sum_{i\in O_{S_{\ell}}}y_{i}(t)+\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}p_{j^{*}(S_{k})}(t)\cdot\mathds{1}[\bm{\bar{}}{S}_{k}\neq\emptyset]\ ,

giving

\sum_{j\in S^{\prime}_{\ell}}p_{j}(t)+\sum_{k=1}^{\ell-1}\sum_{j\in S^{\prime}_{k}}p_{j}(t)\leq\sum_{i\in O_{S_{\ell}}}y_{i}(t)=\sum_{i\in\bm{\hat{}}{O}_{S_{\ell}}}\bm{\hat{}}{y}_{i}(t)=\sum_{i\in M}\bm{\hat{}}{y}_{i}(t)+\sum_{i\in\bm{\hat{}}{O}_{S_{\ell}}\setminus M}\bm{\hat{}}{y}_{i}(t)\ .

The definition of
M
and (
1
) yield
\sum_{i\in M}\bm{\hat{}}{y}_{i}(t)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\ell-1}\sum_{j\in S^{\prime}_{k}}p_{j}(t)
. Thus, the above implies

\sum_{j\in S^{\prime}_{\ell}}p_{j}(t)\leq\sum_{i\in\bm{\hat{}}{O}_{S_{\ell}}\setminus M}\bm{\hat{}}{y}_{i}(t)\ .

By (
1
), the above implies
|S^{\prime}_{\ell}|\leq|\bm{\hat{}}{O}_{S_{\ell}}\setminus M|
. Since
|M|=|\bigcup_{k=1}^{\ell-1}S^{\prime}_{k}|
and segments are pairwise disjoint, we conclude that

\bigg|\bigcup_{k=1}^{\ell}S^{\prime}_{k}\bigg|=\bigg|\bigcup_{k=1}^{\ell-1}S^{\prime}_{k}\bigg|+|S^{\prime}_{\ell}|\leq|\bm{\hat{}}{O}_{S_{\ell}}\setminus M|+|M|\leq|\bm{\hat{}}{O}_{S_{\ell}}|\ ,

which concludes the proof of the statement.
∎

Finally, we can prove
Theorem

3.20
.

Proof of
Theorem

3.20
.

Lemma

3.30
gives
\sum_{k=1}^{L}|S_{k}^{\prime}|\leq\frac{1}{1-\alpha}|O_{S_{L}}|\leq\frac{1}{1-\alpha}|O(t)|.

Moreover, for every segment
S
, we have
|S|\leq|S^{\prime}|+1
. Thus, by combining the above with
Corollary

3.26
, we obtain

|N(t)\setminus O(t)|=\sum_{k=1}^{L}|S_{k}|\leq|\mathcal{S}|+\sum_{k=1}^{L}|S^{\prime}_{k}|\leq 1+|O(t)|+\frac{1}{1-\alpha}|O(t)|\leq\bigg(2+\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\bigg)\cdot|O(t)|\ .

This concludes the proof of the theorem.
∎

3.6
Outline: Proof of
Lemma

3.28

To complete the proof of
Theorem

3.20
, it remains to prove
Lemma

3.28
. The proof requires multiple ingredients that are shown in the subsequent sections. Here, we give a brief outline of the proof idea.

The idea of our proof is motivated by the following lemma, which we prove in
Section

C.1
.

Lemma 3.31
.

If a job
j\in A(t)
can reach a job
i
via an
E_{N}
-path, then
\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{j}\geq\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{i}
.

With respect to the segments (cf.
Definition

3.24
), this means that if a job
j\in S
for a segment
S
can reach a job
i\in O
via an
E_{N}
-path, then
\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{j}\geq\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{i}
, and thus,
i\in O_{S}
. Vice versa, a job
i\in R_{j}\setminus O_{S}

cannot
be reachable by
j
via an
E_{N}
-path.

Since
Lemma

3.28
considers exactly such jobs
j\in\bm{\bar{}}{S}
and
i\in O\setminus O_{S}
that satisfy
\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{i}>\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{j}
, we now know that there cannot be an
E_{N}
-path between any such pair
j
and
i
. In
Section

3.7
, we exploit this insight and further analyze the structure of the borrow graph, specifically in absence of
E_{N}
-paths between jobs
j\in N(t)
and
i\in O(t)
. To this end, we introduce the concept of
bottlenecks
, which are jobs that cannot be avoided on paths between jobs
j\in N(t)
and jobs
i\in O(t)
with
\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{i}>\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{j}
.

In
Section

3.8
, we show that for a fixed
j\in\bm{\bar{}}{S}
there is one unique bottleneck
b
that is the first bottleneck on
any
path from
j
to
any

i\in R_{j}\setminus O_{S}
. This means that if
j
wants to borrow from any job in
i\in R_{j}\setminus O_{S}
, then this must be via
b
. It is possible to show that the maximum amount of processing that
j
can borrow via
b
is
p_{j}(t)
, which implies
\sum_{i\in O\setminus O_{S}}\beta(j,i)\leq p_{j}(t)
. If
\bm{\bar{}}{S}
contains only a single job, then this already gives us
Lemma

3.28
.

Since
\bm{\bar{}}{S}
can contain multiple jobs, we show in
Section

3.9
that the bottleneck
b_{j^{*}(S)}
is part of
every
path from
any

j\in\bm{\bar{}}{S}
to any
i\in O\setminus O_{S}
. To finish the proof, we then prove that
all
jobs in
\bm{\bar{}}{S}
together can borrow at most
p_{j^{*}(S)}
units of processing via
b_{j^{*}(S)}
, which will imply the lemma.

3.7
The Structure of the Borrow Graph

In order to prove
Lemma

3.28
, we analyze the structure of the borrow graph.
We do this with respect to a fixed job
i\in O(t)
. Later, we will see that the relevant structures are essentially independent of the choice of
i
.
Thus, fix some
i\in O(t)
.
In this section, we always refer to the borrow graph when talking about reachability and paths.
We start with the definition of
layers
.

Definition 3.32

(layer)
.

The
layers

L_{0}^{i},\ldots,L_{\ell_{i}}^{i}
of a job
i
are defined as follows:

(i)

L_{0}^{i}
is the set of jobs that can reach
i
with an
E_{N}
-path.

(ii)

L_{x}^{i}
for
x\geq 1
is the set of jobs in
J\setminus\left(\bigcup_{0\leq y\leq x-1}L_{y}^{i}\right)
that can reach some job in
L_{x-1}^{i}
via a path in
G_{B}
that ends with an edge in
E_{C}
but otherwise only uses edges in
E_{N}
.

We write
L_{\leq x}^{i}\coloneq\bigcup_{0\leq y\leq x}L_{y}^{i}
and
L_{\geq x}^{i}\coloneq\bigcup_{x\leq y\leq\ell_{i}}L_{y}^{i}
.

In general, the layer
L_{x}^{i}
is defined to contain all jobs that can reach
i
in
G_{B}
by using
x
edges from
E_{C}
and cannot reach
i
in
G_{B}
by using less than
x
edges from
E_{C}
. As outlined in
Section

3.6
,
Lemma

3.28
is concerned with jobs
j\in N(t)\setminus O(t)
such that
i\in R_{j}
and
\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{i}>\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{j}
. By
Lemma

3.31
, such jobs must satisfy
j\in L^{i}_{\geq 1}
.

Next, we define edges and jobs (vertices) that allow paths from higher layers to lower layers; specifically from a job in
L_{x+1}^{i}
to a job in
L_{x}^{i}
.

Definition 3.33

(
\delta
-edges)
.

We define
\delta_{x}^{i}\coloneq\{(v,u)\in E_{C}\mid v\in L_{x+1}^{i}\land u\in L_{x}^{i}\}
.

Definition 3.34

(entrypoint)
.

A job
k\in L_{x}^{i}
is an
entrypoint of layer
L_{x}^{i}
if both of the following two conditions hold:

(i)

(j^{\prime},k)\in\delta_{x}^{i}
for some
j^{\prime}\in L_{x+1}^{i}
, and

(ii)

there exists a path
P
in
G_{B}
from some
j\in A(t)\cap L_{\geq x+1}^{i}
to
k
that, apart from
k
, only visits jobs in
L_{\geq x+1}^{i}
.

We use
H_{x}^{i}\subseteq L_{x}^{i}
to denote the set of all entrypoints of layer
L_{x}^{i}
.

Note that the second condition of the definition of entrypoints ensures that we only consider entrypoints that actually allow jobs in
A(t)\cap L_{\geq x+1}^{i}
to reach layer
L_{x}^{i}
. This is because these are the relevant jobs for
Lemma

3.28
.

The following definition describes paths that go from a higher layer to a lower layer.

Definition 3.35

(
x
-entry path)
.

We call a path
P
in
G_{B}

x
-entry path
if
P
starts at some job
j\in L_{\geq x+1}^{i}
, ends at some entrypoint
k\in H_{x}^{i}
, and, apart from
k
, only visits jobs in
L_{\geq x+1}^{i}
.

Having established layers and entrypoints with respect to
i\in O(t)
, we continue with the definition of
bottlenecks
, which are special entrypoints. As the name suggests, a bottleneck
k
is an entrypoint if there is some job
j\in A(t)
that can
only
borrow from
i
via transferring work through
k
. Formally, we have the following definition.

Definition 3.36
.

An entrypoint
k\in H_{x}^{i}
is a
bottleneck
of layer
L_{x}^{i}
if
there exists a job
j\in(A(t)\cap L_{x+1}^{i})\cup H_{x+1}^{i}
that can
only
reach layer
L_{x}^{i}
via paths in
G_{B}
that contain
k
.

We emphasize that a layer may have no bottleneck.
On the other side, we show that every layer has also at most one bottleneck.
We will use
b_{x}^{i}
to refer to the bottleneck of layer
L_{x}^{i}
if it has one.

Lemma 3.37
.

Every layer of job
i
has at most one bottleneck.

Next, we consider situations of the following type: Assume
L_{x}^{i}
and
L_{x+1}^{i}
both have bottlenecks
k_{x}
and
k_{x+1}
. From what we have shown so far, it is not clear that a job
j\in A(t)\cap L^{i}_{x+2}
that can reach jobs in
L_{x+1}^{i}
only via paths that contain the bottleneck
k_{x+1}
can only reach jobs in
L_{x}^{i}
only via paths that contain the bottleneck
k_{x}
. We show that this is indeed the case.

Lemma 3.38
.

Consider a layer
L_{x}^{i}
and let
j\in A(t)\cap L_{x+1}^{i}
be a job that only can reach
L_{x}^{i}
through the bottleneck of
L_{x}^{i}
. Then,
j
can only reach
i
via paths that contain
all
bottlenecks of the layers
L_{y}^{i}
with
y\leq x
.

The next lemma and its corollary show that jobs
j
that belong to a segment
S
and satisfy
i\in R_{j}\setminus O_{S}
can
only
reach
i
via paths that contain at least one bottleneck. We prove the lemma in
Section

C.2
.

Lemma 3.39
.

If there exists at least one path from
j\in A(t)
to some job
i
in
G_{B}
that does not contain any bottleneck, then
\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{j}\geq\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{i}
.

Corollary 3.40
.

Let
j\in S
for a segment
S\in\mathcal{S}
and let
i\in R_{j}\cap O
.
If
j
can reach
i
without visiting any bottleneck, then
i\in O_{S}
. Consequently, if
i\not\in O_{S}
, then any path from
j
to
i
must contain at least one bottleneck.

3.8
Fine-Grained Borrowing

In the previous section, we showed that a path from a job
j\in S
for a segment
S
to a job
i\in O(t)\setminus O_{S}
must contain at least one bottleneck of
i
. In this section, we generalize this statement and show that there is
one
bottleneck that is part of
every
path from
j
to
any

i\in O(t)\setminus O_{S}
.

To this end, we first introduce some more notation.
Fix a job
i\in O(t)
and consider a job
j\in A(t)
with
i\in R_{j}
. We use
\ell_{ji}
to denote the index of the layer
L_{x}^{i}
with
j\in L_{x}^{i}
. If every path from
j
to
i
contains at least one bottleneck of
i
, then we use
\sigma_{ji}
to denote the index of the first layer that
j
cannot reach without visiting a bottleneck, i.e., every path from
j
to some
i^{\prime}\in R_{j}\cap L^{i}_{\leq\sigma_{ji}}
visits at least one bottleneck of
i
and for every layer
L_{x}^{i}
with
\sigma_{ji}<x\leq\ell_{ji}
there exists an entrypoint
k\in H_{x}^{i}
that
j
can reach using a path without bottleneck of
i
. We use
b_{ji}

to refer to the unique bottleneck (cf.
Lemma

3.37
) of layer
L_{\sigma_{ji}}^{i}
. Intuitively,
b_{ji}
is the first bottleneck to appear on any path from
j
to
i
.

The next theorem and its corollary show that the first bottlenecks on paths from
j\in S
to any
i\in R_{j}\setminus O_{S}
are the exact same job.

Theorem 3.41
.

Let
j\in A(t)
and let
i_{1},i_{2}
be two distinct jobs that
j
can only reach via paths that contain at least one bottleneck. Then,
b_{ji_{1}}=b_{ji_{2}}
or
\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{j}\geq\min\{\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{i_{1}},\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{i_{2}}\}
.

Corollary 3.42
.

Let
j\in S
for a segment
S
and
i_{1},i_{2}\in R_{j}\setminus O_{S}
, then
b_{ji_{1}}=b_{ji_{2}}
.

To prove the theorem, we will need the following auxiliary lemma. The lemma also will be helpful later to upper bound the amount of processing that
j
can borrow from jobs in
i
with
\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{i}>\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{j}
.

Lemma 3.43
.

Let
j\in A(t)
be such that on every path from
j
to
i
is at least one bottleneck and let
\Delta=\{d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}\}=\{d\in J\mid(d,b_{ji})\in\delta^{i}_{\sigma_{ji}}\}
. For each
d_{q}\in\Delta
, let
\tau_{q}
denote the earliest point in time during
I_{d_{q}}
at which
b_{ji}
is processed.
If
j
can reach
d_{q}\in\Delta
via an
L^{i}_{\geq\sigma_{ji}+1}
-path, then
\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{j}\geq p_{b_{ji}}(\tau_{q})
.

In order to show the theorem, we also rely on the following two observations.

Observation 3.44
.

Let
j\in A(t)
be such that
j
can reach some job
i
only via paths that contain at least one bottleneck. Let
k
be such a bottleneck. Then, there is no path from
j
to any other job
j^{\prime}
that is alive at
s_{k}^{-}
that does not contain
k
.

Proof.

Since
k
is a bottleneck of some layer
L_{x}^{i}
with
k\in L_{x}^{i}
and
j
cannot reach
i
without visiting
k
, it must be the case that any path from

4
Conclusion

We have shown that any constant-factor lookahead on the job processing times is sufficient to break the deterministic lower bound for non-clairvoyant flow time minimization and to achieve a constant competitive ratio. Similar to the speed-augmented result by
[
KP00
]
, our result illustrates that non-clairvoyant flow time scheduling is
“almost”
constant competitive, as making the algorithm slightly more powerful is sufficient to achieve constant competitiveness.

For future work, we suggest to study the problem in a setting where the signals are not completely precise, for example, using learning-augmented algorithm design. Finally, the
\alpha
-clairvoyant model naturally translates to scheduling problems with different objective functions, e.g., makespan minimization.

B.1
Properties of Entrypoints

Having the layer structure and entrypoints set up, we want to more precisely analyze schedules of our algorithm that belong to a certain borrow graph structure.

First, we characterize the structure of edges

from layer

Lemma B.2

Consider two jobs

and

such that

Proof.

By the layer structure, we have that

We show the first property via proof by contradiction. To this end, assume

Since

with

Finally, we show the third property via proof by contradiction. Assume

and that

Fine-Grained Borrowing

In this section, we want to consider a fixed job

and analyze how much processing a job

can borrow from

is by showing the inequality

can reach

in

via a path that does not contain any bottleneck of

With respect to the segments (cf.

3.24
), this means that if a job

can reach a job

without visiting any bottleneck, then

. Vice versa, if

can reach a job

, then this must be via at least one bottleneck.

Afterwards, we consider jobs
j\in A(t)
that must visit at least one bottleneck of
i
in order to reach
i
and analyze how much processing
j
can borrow via that bottleneck. As argued above, for a job
j
in a segment
S
, all jobs

that

can actually reach fall into this category.
This analysis will later on be a key ingredient for proving
Lemma

3.28
.

For the remainder of this section, we fix a job
i\in O(t)
and analyze jobs

with

.

C.1

In order to show that

holds for jobs

that can reach

can reach

without visiting a bottleneck of

(Good and bad edges)
.

We call an edge

We call a path in

If a job

has a good

-path to job

If a job

In order to generalize the statement to our algorithm, we first characterize good and bad edges.

Characterization of good and bad edges.

To this end, we first show that every edge

is good. Observe that if

and

Lemma C.3

As stated above, if

Since

with

at which

is being processed.

2.2

Lemma C.4
.

Let

Assume

If

, then we must have

The goal of this section is to prove that if a job

\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{j}\geq\min\{\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{k_{1}},\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{k_{2}}\}

\Delta=\{d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}\}=\{d\in J\mid(d,b_{ji})\in\delta^{i}_{\sigma_{ji}}\}

-entry path that ends with an edge

via edges

that

can actually reach via an

For each

via an

-path. The main result of this section is the following lemma, which can be viewed as a first step towards proving

.

See
3.43

To prove the lemma, we first look at the edges

3.43
for the special case

, and will be useful for proving the full lemma.

Observation C.21
.

If

\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{d_{q}}\geq\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}y_{d_{q}}(\tau_{q})\geq p_{b_{ji}}(\tau_{q})
.

Proof.

Recall that
\tau_{q}
is the earliest point in time during

at which
b_{ji}
is processed. Since

, i.e., job

is already clairvoyant at the earliest time during

, as otherwise, that is,

.

Since we have

and

\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}y_{d_{q}}(\tau_{q})\geq p_{b_{ji}}(\tau_{q})
.
By

, we have
y_{d_{q}}(\tau_{q})\leq y_{d_{q}}(C_{b_{ji}})\leq\alpha\cdot p_{d_{q}}
. Thus,

, and we can conclude with
\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{d_{q}}\geq\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}y_{d_{q}}(\tau_{q})\geq p_{b_{ji}}(\tau_{q})

∎

Next, we show that there exists at least one job

with

. The existence of this one job will later help us to show that the inequality actually holds for

Lemma C.22

Let

be a job such that every path from

We distinguish between the two cases (1)

. In particular, the second part of the lemma gives us a job

Lemma C.23
.

Let

be a job that can reach a job

via an

-path. Let

with

be a job that is executed at some point in time

while being non-clairvoyant. Then

can reach

via an

-path.

Proof.

We distinguish between the two cases (1)

and (2)
j^{\prime}\in H_{\sigma_{ji}+1}^{i}
.

Case (1):
Assume

. Let

be the

to

to

. By assumption,

Case (2):
Assume

and let

be an

-path from

to

. Note that such a path exists by assumption of the lemma.

-entry path from some

Having established all these auxiliary lemmas, we are ready to prove

.

Proof of
Lemma

3.43
.

Assume that the elements of
\Delta=\{d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}\}=\{d\in J\mid(d,b_{ji})\in\delta^{i}_{\sigma_{ji}}\}
are indexed by non-decreasing
\tau_{h}
, i.e.,
h<h^{\prime}
implies
\tau_{h}\leq\tau_{h^{\prime}}
. In particular, this also means that
h<h^{\prime}
implies
p_{b_{ji}}(\tau_{h})\geq p_{b_{ji}}(\tau_{h^{\prime}})
. So if
\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{j}\geq p_{b_{ji}}(\tau_{h})
holds, then also
\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{j}\geq p_{b_{ji}}(\tau_{h^{\prime}})
for all
h^{\prime}>h
.

Let
h^{*}
denote the maximum index that satisfies
\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{j}<p_{b_{ji}}(\tau_{h^{*}})
. If no such index exists, then we are done as the first property of the lemma then holds for every
d_{h}\in\Delta
. Thus, assume in the following that
h^{*}
exists.

By choice of
h^{*}
, all
d_{h}
with
h>h^{*}
satisfy
\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{j}\geq p_{b_{ji}}(\tau_{h})
. Thus, it only remains to show that
j
cannot reach any
d_{h}
with
h\leq h^{*}
via an
L^{i}_{\sigma_{ji}+1}
-path.

To this end, we show that the following three properties hold, and argue that they imply that
j
cannot reach any
d_{h}
with
h\leq h^{*}
via an
L^{i}_{\geq\sigma_{ji}+1}
-path.

Property 1
.

r_{j}>\tau_{h^{*}}
.

Property 2
.

No
d_{h}\in\Delta
with
h\leq h^{*}
is executed after
\tau_{h^{*}}
. In particular,
r_{d_{h}}<\tau_{h^{*}}
.

Property 3
.

No
j^{\prime}\in L^{i}_{\geq\sigma_{ji}+1}\cap A(\tau_{h^{*}})
is executed after
\tau_{h^{*}}
.

Before we prove that the properties indeed hold, we argue that they imply that
j
cannot reach any
d_{h}
with
h\leq h^{*}
via an
L^{i}_{\geq\sigma_{ji}+1}
-path.
To see this, assume otherwise, i.e., that there exists an
L^{i}_{\geq\sigma_{ji}+1}
-path
P
from
j
to a
d_{h}\in\Delta
with
h\leq h^{*}
. Since
r_{j}>\tau_{h^{*}}
(Property 1) but
d_{h}
with
r_{d_{h}}<\tau_{h^{*}}
is not executed after
\tau_{h^{*}}
(Property 2), the path
P
must contain a job
j^{\prime}\in A(\tau_{h^{*}})
that is executed after
\tau_{h^{*}}
. However, as
P
is an
L^{i}_{\geq\sigma_{ji}+1}
-path, the job
j^{\prime}
would be in
L^{i}_{\geq\sigma_{ji}+1}\cap A(\tau_{h^{*}})
and executed after
\tau^{*}
.
The existence of such a job
j^{\prime}
is a contradiction to Property 3. Hence, we can conclude that
j
cannot reach any
d_{h}
with
h\leq h^{*}
via an
L^{i}_{\geq\sigma_{ji}+1}
-path.

To complete the proof, it remains to show that the three properties indeed hold.

Proof of Property 1.

By choice of
h^{*}
, we have
\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{j}<p_{b_{ji}}(\tau_{h^{*}})
. If we had
r_{j}<\tau_{h^{*}}
, then we would have
\tau_{h^{*}}\in I_{j}
as
j
is in
A(t)
. This would imply
(j,b_{ji})\in\delta_{\sigma_{ji}}
and, thus,
\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{j}\geq p_{b_{ji}}(\tau_{j})
for the earliest point in time
\tau_{j}\in I_{j}
at which
b_{ji}
is executed (cf.
C.21
). Since
\tau_{h^{*}}\in I_{j}
, we have
\tau_{j}\leq\tau_{h^{*}}
and, thus,
\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{j}\geq p_{b_{ji}}(\tau_{j})\geq p_{b_{ji}}(\tau_{h^{*}})
. However, this contradicts
\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{j}<p_{b_{ji}}(\tau_{h^{*}})
.

Proof of Property 2.

By
Lemmas

C.23
and
C.22
, we have
\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{j}\geq p_{b_{ji}}(\tau_{h})
for each
d_{h}\in\Delta
that is executed after
C_{b_{ji}}
while being non-clairvoyant.
Thus, by the choice of
h^{*}
, each
d_{h}\in\Delta
with
h\leq h^{*}
cannot be executed after
C_{b_{ji}}
while being non-clairvoyant; otherwise we would have
\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{j}\geq p_{b_{ji}}(\tau_{h})
for an
h\leq h^{*}
which contradicts the definition of
h^{*}
. Furthermore, by
Lemma

B.2
, each
d_{h}\in\Delta
is non-clairvoyant at
C_{b_{ji}}
, i.e.,
d_{h}\in N(C_{b_{ji}})
. This implies that each
d_{h}\in\Delta
with
h\leq h^{*}
cannot be executed after
C_{b_{ji}}
at all, because such an execution after
C_{b_{ji}}
would be non-clairvoyant.

Proof of Property 3.

Let
j^{\prime}
be a job in
L_{\geq\sigma_{ji}+1}\cap A(\tau_{h^{*}})
. Since
j^{\prime}
is alive at point in time
\tau_{h^{*}}
, we have
(j^{\prime},b_{ji})\in E_{C}
because
b_{ji}
is executed at
\tau_{h^{*}}
while being clairvoyant. Using

, this implies
(j^{\prime},b_{ji})\in\delta_{\sigma_{ji}}
. This allows us to apply
Lemma

B.2
to obtain
j^{\prime}\in N(C_{b_{ji}})
. Furthermore,
2.3
implies that
j^{\prime}
is not executed during
[\tau_{h^{*}},C_{b_{ji}}]
. Thus, if
j^{\prime}
is executed after
\tau_{h^{*}}
, then this execution must take place at a point in time

with
t^{\prime}\geq C_{b_{ji}}
and

. However, then

C.23
and

imply
\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{j}\geq p_{b_{ji}}(\tau_{j^{\prime}})
for the earliest point in time

at which
b_{ji}
is executed. Since
\tau_{h^{*}}\in I_{j^{\prime}}
, this implies
\tau_{j^{\prime}}\leq\tau_{h^{*}}
and, thus
\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{j}\geq p_{b_{ji}}(\tau_{j^{\prime}})\geq p_{b_{ji}}(\tau_{h^{*}})
. This is a contradiction to the choice of

according to which we have
\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\bm{\bar{}}{y}_{j}<p_{b_{ji}}(\tau_{h^{*}})
. Thus, we can conclude that

cannot be executed after

.
∎

For every job

Definition D.1

For every job

with

For every

.

Lemma D.3
.

The following properties hold:

(a)

For every

and for every

, it holds that
(1-\alpha)y_{i}(t)\leq p_{j}(t)
.

(b)

For every

and for every

, it holds that

.

Proof.

For (a), let
i\in N(t)
and

.
Since

, we have that
t_{j}\leq r_{i}\leq t_{i}
and

, where the latter holds as
j
is clairvoyant at point in time
t
, and thus, must have become clairvoyant the latest at time
t_{j}\leq t_{i}
.
The algorithm guarantees that at time

it holds that
\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}y_{i}(t_{i})<p_{j}(t_{i})
as

and

(1-\alpha)y_{i}(t)=(1-\alpha)y_{i}(t_{i}^{+})\leq\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}y_{i}(t_{i}^{+})\leq p_{j}(t_{i})=p_{j}(t)
.

For (b), let
i\in J\setminus N(t)
and

. Let

be the time when

emits.
Since

imply that

so

is still clairvoyant when job
i
emits the signal.
Since at time
s_{i}

i

\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}y_{i}(s_{i})<p_{j}(s_{i})
by the definition of the algorithm.
Thus,
(1-\alpha)p_{i}=\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}y_{i}(s_{i}^{+})\leq p_{j}(s_{i}^{+})=p_{j}(t)
.
∎

Appendix E
Lower Bounds

For a fixed algorithm and a fixed instance, we denote by
\delta(t)
and
\delta(t,1)
the number of unfinished jobs and the number of unfinished jobs with a remaining processing time of at least
1
at time
t
in the algorithm’s schedule, respectively, and by
\delta^{*}(t)
the number of unfinished jobs at time
t
in an optimal solution. The following technique is well-known in literature
[
MPT94
,
ALT21
,
ALT22
]
.

Proposition E.1

(Denial of Service)
.

If there exists an instance such that
\delta(t,1)\geq\rho\cdot\delta^{*}(t)
for some constant
\rho
at some time
t
, then the algorithm has a competitive ratio of at least
\Omega(\rho)
. Moreover, if for every sufficiently large integer
k
there exists an instance such that
\delta^{*}(t)=k
and
\delta(t,1)\geq\rho\cdot\delta^{*}(t)
for some constant
\rho
at some time
t
, then the algorithm has a competitive ratio of at least
\rho
.

Proof.

After time
t
, the adversary releases at each of the next
M
integer times a job of length
1
. Thus, the best strategy for the algorithm is to run such jobs to completion before any other job with remaining processing time at least
1
at time
t
.
Therefore, the algorithm has between time
t
and
t+M
at least
\delta(t)+1
unfinished jobs. Similarly, we can assume that an optimal solution has at most
\delta^{*}(t)+1
pending jobs during this time. Therefore, as
M
tends to
\infty
, the competitive ratio of the algorithm is at least
\frac{\delta(t)+1}{\delta^{*}(t)+1}\geq\frac{\rho}{2}
, proving the first part of the statement.

For the second part, we can analogously conclude that the algorithm’s competitive ratio for every instance of the family is at least
\frac{\delta(t)+1}{\delta^{*}(t)+1}\geq\frac{\rho k+1}{k+1}
, which tends to
\rho
as
k\to\infty
.
∎

E.1
Deterministic Lower Bound

Theorem E.2
.

For every
\alpha\in(0,1)
, every
\alpha
-clairvoyant deterministic algorithm has a competitive ratio of at least
\Omega(\frac{1}{1-\alpha})
for minimizing the total flow time on a single machine.

Proof.

Fix any deterministic algorithm. Initially, there are
k
jobs
J_{1}=\{1,\ldots,k\}
released at time
0
for some large constant
k
. We ensure that no job emits its signal in the algorithm’s schedule until time
k
. Thus, the algorithm has
k
unfinished jobs at time
k
.
For every
i\in J_{1}
, let
y_{i}
be the total work to job
i
in the algorithm’s schedule until time
k
, and assume w.l.o.g. that
y_{1}\leq\ldots\leq y_{k}
.
We set for every job
i\in J_{1}
its processing time to
p_{i}=\frac{1}{\alpha}y_{i}+\frac{1}{k}
and observe that
y_{i}<\alpha p_{i}
.
Note that every unfinished job at time
k
has a constant remaining processing time, and
\delta(k,1)\geq k
by scaling.

We now present an alternative strategy until time
k
.
Let
\ell=\lfloor\alpha k\rfloor-1
.
We distinguish two cases.
If
y_{\ell+1}\leq 1
, we can easily conclude that
\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}y_{i}\leq\ell
. Thus, we can complete jobs
1,\ldots,\ell
until time
k
, because

\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}p_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}y_{i}+\frac{1}{k}\right)\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\ell+1\leq k-\frac{1}{\alpha}+1\leq k\ .

Otherwise, that is,
y_{\ell+1}\geq 1
, our assumption on the jobs indices and the choice of
\ell
gives that
\sum_{i=1}^{\ell+1}y_{i}\leq\alpha k
.
We can again complete jobs
1,\ldots,\ell
until time
k
, because

\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}p_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}y_{i}+\frac{1}{k}\right)\leq k-\frac{1}{\alpha}y_{\ell+1}+1\leq k\ .

Therefore, in any case, there are at most
\delta^{*}(k)\leq k-\ell=\mathcal{O}((1-\alpha)k)
jobs unfinished at time
k
in this strategy.
Then, the lemma follows via
Proposition

E.1
.
∎

Theorem E.3
.

For every
\alpha\in(0,1)
, every
\alpha
-clairvoyant deterministic algorithm has a competitive ratio of at least
2
.

Proof.

Let
k
be an integer and
\lambda=\frac{4+\alpha}{\alpha}
. Fix any deterministic algorithm. We construct an instance of
k
consecutive phases indexed by
k,\ldots,1
, where phase
i
has length
\lambda^{i}
. Let
t
denote the end of the last phase.

At the start of every phase
i
at time
s_{i}
, the adversary releases two jobs
j^{i}_{1}
and
j^{i}_{2}
. Then, it waits until time
t_{i}=s_{i}+\alpha\lambda^{i}
while ensuring that none of both jobs emits its signal. Assume w.l.o.g. that
y_{j_{1}^{i}}(t_{i})\geq y_{j_{2}^{i}}(t_{i})
. Thus,
y_{j_{1}^{i}}(t_{i})\leq\alpha\lambda^{i}
and
y_{j_{2}^{i}}(t_{i})\leq\frac{\alpha}{2}\lambda^{i}
. At time
t_{i}
the adversary sets
p_{j_{1}^{i}}=2\lambda^{i}
and
p_{j_{2}^{i}}=\lambda^{i}
. Since
\alpha p_{j_{1}^{i}}=2\alpha\lambda^{i}>y_{j_{1}^{i}}(t_{i})
and
\alpha p_{j_{2}^{i}}=\alpha\lambda^{i}>y_{j_{2}^{i}}(t_{i})
, this choice is consistent with our assumption that none of both jobs emitted its signal at time
t_{i}
.
During phase
i
, job
j_{1}^{i}
can be processed by at most
\lambda^{i}
units and job
j_{2}^{i}
can be processed by at most
\lambda^{i}-\frac{\alpha}{2}\lambda^{i}
units.
Thus, at the end of phase
i
(at time
s_{i-1}
) the remaining processing time of each of
j_{1}^{i}
and
j_{2}^{i}
is at least
\frac{\alpha}{2}\lambda^{i}
.
Now observe that after phase
i
, no algorithm can finish any of
j_{1}^{i}
and
j_{2}^{i}
until time
t
, and moreover each of
j_{1}^{i}
and
j_{2}^{i}
has a remaining processing time of at least
\frac{\alpha}{4}\lambda^{i}=\frac{\alpha}{2}\lambda^{i}-\frac{\alpha}{4}\lambda^{i}
at time
t
.
To see this, note that

t-s_{i-1}\leq\sum_{i^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}\lambda^{i-1-i^{\prime}}=\lambda^{i-1}\sum_{i^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}\lambda^{-i^{\prime}}=\lambda^{i-1}\cdot\frac{1}{1-\lambda^{-1}}=\lambda^{i-1}\cdot\frac{4+\alpha}{4}=\frac{\alpha}{4}\lambda^{i}\ .

Therefore, the algorithm has
2k
unfinished jobs at time
t
, each with a remaining processing time of at least
\frac{\alpha}{4}\lambda^{1}>1
. Thus,
\delta(t,1)=2k
. An optimal solution has only
\delta^{*}(t)=k
unfinished jobs at time
t
, as it can complete in every phase
i
job
j_{2}^{i}
.
Then, the lemma follows via
Proposition

E.1
.
∎

E.2
Randomized Lower Bound

Theorem E.4
.

For every
\alpha\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
, every
\alpha
-clairvoyant randomized algorithm has a competitive ratio of at least
\Omega(\frac{1}{1-\alpha})
for minimizing the total flow time on a single machine.

We present a randomized instance on which every deterministic algorithm has an expected competitive ratio of at least
\Omega(\frac{1}{1-\alpha})
. The theorem then follows using Yao’s principle.

Fix any deterministic algorithm. We consider a distribution of instances where at time
0
we release
k=\lfloor 2^{1/(2-2\alpha)}\rfloor
jobs with integer processing times
p_{j}=y_{j}+1
with
y_{j}
’s drawn independently from the geometric distribution with mean
2
. Note that
\mathbb{E}[p_{j}]=3
. Let
t=\lfloor 3(k-k^{3/4})\rfloor
and let
L
denote the event that for every job
j
it holds that
p_{j}\leq\frac{1}{1-\alpha}
.

We first show that conditioned on
L
, the algorithm has many unfinished jobs at time
t
in expectation.

Lemma E.5
.

It holds that
\mathbb{E}[\delta(t,1)\mid L]\geq\Omega(k^{3/4})
.

Proof.

To prove this statement, we make the following two assumptions w.l.o.g.:

•

Every job emits its signal after
y_{j}
units have been processed. This does not weaken the algorithm, because
y_{j}=p_{j}-1\leq\alpha p_{j}
whenever event
L
occurs. Moreover, whenever a job emits, we assume that in the next time unit the algorithm also completes it by processing the final unit of processing requirement. Note that this assumption cannot increase
\delta(t)
.

•

All preemptions until time
t
occur at integer times. Since
t
is an integer, all processing times are integers and, by the previous assumption, all signals emit after integer processing amounts, we can convert any schedule with fractional time allocations to an integral schedule without increasing
\delta(t)
.

Now consider an integer time unit
t^{\prime}
between
1
and
t
. If at time
t^{\prime}
a non-clairvoyant job
j
is being processed, observe that
j
emits its signal with probability
\frac{1}{2}
if the job
j^{\prime}
that ran at time
t^{\prime}-1

did not emit its signal, because otherwise, the algorithm would finish
j^{\prime}
at time
t^{\prime}
.
This is in particular independent of the choice of
j
, because the algorithm cannot distinguish between non-clairvoyant jobs.
Therefore, we have

\mu_{t^{\prime}}:=\mathrm{Pr}\left[\text{ the job that runs at time }t^{\prime}\text{ emits }\right]=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\mu_{t^{\prime}-1}\right)\ ,

and
\mu_{0}:=\frac{1}{2}
.

Thus, the expected number of jobs that have emitted their signal until time
t
is at most

\displaystyle\sum_{t^{\prime}=0}^{t-1}\mu_{t^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t^{\prime}=0}^{t-1}\sum_{\ell=0}^{t^{\prime}}(-1)^{\ell}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\ell+1}=\sum_{t^{\prime}=0}^{t-1}\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1-(-1/2)^{t^{\prime}+1}}{1-(-1/2)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t^{\prime}=0}^{t-1}\frac{1}{3}+\frac{1}{6}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{t^{\prime}}\leq\frac{1}{3}t+\frac{1}{9}-\frac{1}{9}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{t}\leq\frac{1}{3}t+1=k-k^{3/4}+1\ .

Since every job which has not emitted until time
t
has a remaining processing time of at least
1
, we conclude that
\mathbb{E}[\delta(t,1)\mid L]\geq k-(k-k^{3/4}+1)=\Omega(k^{3/4})
.
∎

Lemma E.6
.

It holds that
\mathbb{E}[\delta^{*}(t)]\leq\mathcal{O}(\frac{k^{3/4}}{\log k})
.

Proof.

First, note that the total processing time
P
of all jobs is a sum of
k
independent random variables with mean
3k
and variance
2k
. Thus, Chebyshev’s inequality gives

\mathrm{Pr}\left[P\geq 3k+k^{3/4}\right]=\mathrm{Pr}\left[P\geq 3k+k^{1/2}\cdot k^{1/4}\right]\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{k^{1/2}}\right)\ .

Moreover, let
b=\frac{\log k}{4}
and let
B
be the number of jobs with processing time more than
b
.
Note that
B
is binomially distributed, because it can be written as the sum of
k
binary random variables
Y_{j}=\mathds{1}[p_{j}>b]
with equal success probabilities
\mathrm{Pr}[p_{j}>b]=\mathrm{Pr}[y_{j}>b-1]=(\frac{1}{2})^{b-1}=2k^{-1/4}
.
Therefore,
\mathbb{E}[B]=k^{-1/4}\cdot k=k^{3/4}
and
\mathbb{V}[B]=k^{-1/4}(1-k^{-1/4})k=O(k^{3/4})
.
Thus, Chebyshev’s inequality gives

\mathrm{Pr}\left[B\leq k^{3/4}\right]=\mathrm{Pr}\left[B\leq 2k^{3/8}\cdot k^{3/8}-k^{3/4}\right]\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{k^{3/4}}\right)\ .

Therefore, with probability of at least
1-\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{k^{1/2}})
it holds that
P<3k+k^{3/4}
and
B>k^{3/4}
.
In this case, we compute the maximum number of jobs of size at least
b
that an optimum solution cannot complete until time
t
as follows

\displaystyle\frac{1}{b}\left(P-t\right)\leq\frac{1}{b}\left(3k+k^{3/4}-3(k-k^{3/4})+1\right)\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{k^{3/4}}{\log k}\right)\ .

Note that
\mathcal{O}(\frac{k^{3/4}}{\log k})\leq k^{3/4}\leq B
, i.e., there are such many long jobs.

In total, the expected number of alive jobs in an optimum solution at time
t
is at most

\mathbb{E}[\delta^{*}(t)]\leq\left(1-\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{k^{1/2}}\right)\right)\cdot\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{k^{3/4}}{\log k}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{k^{1/2}}\right)\cdot k=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{k^{3/4}}{\log k}\right)\ ,

which concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Let
\bm{\bar{}}{L}
denote the complementary event to
L
.

Proposition E.7
.

It holds that
\mathrm{Pr}[\bm{\bar{}}{L}]\leq\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{k})
.

Proof.

Event
\bm{\bar{}}{L}
occurs if for at least one job
j
it holds that
p_{j}>\frac{1}{1-\alpha}
. Since this happens for job
j
with probability
\mathrm{Pr}[y_{j}>\frac{1}{1-\alpha}-1]=2\cdot 2^{-1/(1-\alpha)}
, we can apply the union bound and conclude that

\mathrm{Pr}(\bm{\bar{}}{L})\leq k\cdot 2\cdot 2^{-\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}\leq 2\cdot 2^{\frac{1}{2(1-\alpha)}}\cdot 2^{-\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)\ ,

which implies the stated bound.
∎

Finally, we can prove
Theorem

E.4
.

Proof of
Theorem

E.4
.

Lemmas

E.5
and
E.6
and the law of total probability imply

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}[\delta(t,1)]}{\mathbb{E}[\delta^{*}(t)]}=\frac{\mathrm{Pr}[L]\cdot\mathbb{E}[\delta(t,1)\mid L]}{\mathrm{Pr}[L]\cdot\mathbb{E}[\delta^{*}(t)\mid L]}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\mathbb{E}[\delta(t,1)\mid L]-\mathrm{Pr}[\bm{\bar{}}{L}]\cdot\mathbb{E}[\delta(t,1)\mid\bm{\bar{}}{L}]}{\mathbb{E}[\delta^{*}(t)]}

\displaystyle\geq\Omega(\log k)-\frac{\mathrm{Pr}[\bm{\bar{}}{L}]\cdot\mathbb{E}[\delta(t,1)\mid\bm{\bar{}}{L}]}{\mathbb{E}[\delta^{*}(t)]}\ .

Now, observe that
\mathbb{E}[\delta(t,1)\mid\bm{\bar{}}{L}]\leq k
and
\mathbb{E}[\delta^{*}(t)]\geq\frac{1}{2}
. The latter holds because with probability at least
\frac{1}{2}
, the total volume
P
of the
k
jobs is more than
t
, in which case no solution can finish all jobs. This can be seen via Chebyshev’s inequality because
\mathrm{Pr}[P\leq t]\leq\frac{1}{2k^{1/2}}\leq\frac{1}{2}
. Thus, using
Proposition

E.7
, we conclude that the above is at least

\Omega(\log k)-\frac{\mathcal{O}(1/k)\cdot k}{1/2}=\Omega(\log k)=\Omega\left(\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\right)\ .

The theorem now follows by applying
Proposition

E.1
to every realization of our distribution and finally using Yao’s principle.
∎

Theorem E.8
.

For every
\alpha\in(0,1)
, every
\alpha
-clairvoyant randomized algorithm has a competitive ratio of at least
\frac{3}{2}
.

Proof.

Exploiting Yao’s Theorem, we show the statement by giving a randomized instance for which each deterministic algorithm has an expected competitive ratio of at least
\frac{3}{2}
.

The randomized instance is based on the deterministic lower bound of Theorem
E.3
. We randomize the instance by, at the beginning of each phase
i
, drawing one of the jobs
\{j_{1}^{i},j_{2}^{i}\}
uniformly at random. The drawn job gets assigned the processing time of
\lambda_{i}
while the other job gets a processing time of
2\lambda_{i}
. Thus, each job in
\{j_{1}^{i},j_{2}^{i}\}
has processing time
\lambda_{i}
with probability
\frac{1}{2}
and processing time
2\lambda_{i}
with probability
\frac{1}{2}
. The rest of the instance remains deterministic as defined in the proof of Theorem
E.3
.

Consider a fixed deterministic algorithm. At any point in time during the first
\alpha\lambda_{i}
time units of a phase
i
, the probability that the algorithm executes the shorter job of
\{j_{1}^{i},j_{2}^{i}\}
is at most
\frac{1}{2}
. This is because none of the jobs can emit during the first
\alpha\lambda_{i}
time units of the phase, so the algorithm cannot distinguish the jobs.
Thus, the probability that the shorter job is processed for at most
\frac{\alpha}{2}\lambda_{i}
time units within the first
\alpha\lambda_{i}
time units of phase
i
is a least
\frac{1}{2}
. Thus, with probability at least
\frac{1}{2}
, both jobs in
\{j_{1}^{i},j_{2}^{i}\}
have a remaining processing time of at least
\frac{\alpha}{2}\lambda_{i}
at the end of phase
i
. As argued in the proof of Theorem
E.3
, each job that has a remaining processing time of at least
\frac{\alpha}{2}\lambda_{i}
at the end of its phase has a remaining processing time of at least
\frac{\alpha}{4}\lambda_{i}
at point in time
t
.
Thus, for each phase
i
, the probability that both jobs
j\in\{j_{1}^{i},j_{2}^{i}\}
satisfy
p_{j}(t)\geq\frac{\alpha}{4}\lambda_{1}>1
is at least
\frac{1}{2}
. With the remaining probability, at least the longer job of
\{j_{1}^{i},j_{2}^{i}\}
has a remaining processing time of at least
1
at time
t
.
Thus, the expected number of jobs released in phase
i
that have a remaining processing time of at least
1
at time
t
is at least
\frac{3}{2}
.
This implies that the expected number of jobs released during the
k
phases that have a remaining processing time of at least
1
at time
t
is at least
\frac{3}{2}k
.

At the same time, the same clairvoyant optimal solution as in the proof of Theorem
E.3
has only
k
jobs alive at time
t
.
By
Proposition

E.1
, this implies an expected competitive ratio of at least
\frac{3}{2}
, and thus, the statement.
∎
