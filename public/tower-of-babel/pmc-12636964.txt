Biceps lasso loop and self-locking tenodesis alongside repair of rotator cuff tears: randomised controlled trial study protocol (BLAST 1)
Authors: Igor J Shirinskiy, Dries Boulidam, Arno A Macken, Michel P J van den Bekerom, Thibault Lafosse, Geert Alexander Buijze
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPathology of the long head of the biceps tendon (LHBT) frequently accompanies rotator cuff tears, with tenotomy and tenodesis often being used to address this pathology. While meta-analyses report comparable functional outcomes between these techniques, tenotomy is linked to higher rates of Popeye deformity, whereas tenodesis is more technically demanding and might involve extra material. A novel self-locking tenodesis technique aims to reduce deformity risk while being a simpler alternative to the conventional tenodesis procedure; however, comparative evidence is currently limited.Methods and analysisThis single-centre, patient-blinded randomised controlled trial will enrol 100 patients aged ≥40 years with reparable, non-traumatic, full-thickness supraspinatus and/or infraspinatus and/or subscapularis tendon tears undergoing arthroscopic repair from January 2025 until January 2027. Key exclusion criteria include massive or irreparable tears, advanced glenohumeral osteoarthritis and prior shoulder surgery. Participants will be randomised to either 360 double lasso loop tenodesis or self-locking tenodesis. The primary outcome is the Constant score at 1 year, with a predefined non-inferiority margin of 10 points. Secondary outcomes include American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons score, simple shoulder value score, LHB score, cosmetic appearance, pain scores and radiographic tendon migration. Statistical non-inferiority will be assessed using a one-sided t-test.Ethics and disseminationThe study protocol received approval from the National Ethical Review Board in France (CPP Sud-Est V) and was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov. The results will be disseminated through publication in a peer-reviewed journal.Trial registration numberNCT06774820.Keywords: Orthopedics, Shoulder, Musculoskeletal disorders, Clinical Trial

Background

Long head biceps tendon (LHBT) pathology represents a common musculoskeletal complaint and becomes more common with advancing age, with its incidence increasing by about 4% per year.1 By the age of 85 and older, nearly all individuals with shoulder pain have both LHBT and rotator cuff pathology.1 The LHB is susceptible to various conditions, including tendinitis, subluxation and tears, which can cause pain and functional impairment. These conditions are often associated with rotator cuff tears.2 Two surgical methods, LHBT tenotomy and tenodesis, are often employed to address LHBT pathology and have been shown to be effective. The LHBT tenotomy involves cutting the LHBT without reattaching it. This procedure effectively eliminates pain by removing the symptomatic segment of the LHBT. However, it is associated with cramping pain and a higher rate of cosmetic deformity, known as Popeye sign.3 In contrast to tenotomy, LHBT tenodesis involves reattaching the transected tendon to the proximal humerus. This procedure aims to preserve the length-tension relationship of the biceps muscle, thereby maintaining its function and preventing Popeye deformity. However, LHBT tenodesis is a more technically demanding procedure that potentially requires additional materials and time compared with tenotomy. Additionally, LHBT tenodesis may be associated with a higher risk of complications and more difficult rehabilitation, and it does not provide a significant strength advantage or a guaranteed prevention of the Popeye sign.4

Currently, there is a lack of consensus regarding the use of tenodesis versus tenotomy in arthroscopic rotator cuff repair surgery. Multiple studies have investigated the clinical outcomes of LHBT tenotomy compared with LHBT tenodesis, and there is no evidence to date supporting the superiority of either technique.5,7 A recent meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials (RCTs) has shown similar functional outcomes for LHBT tenotomy and tenodesis.8 The only differences being reported were a higher prevalence of the Popeye sign after LHBT tenotomy and longer operative times for LHBT tenodesis. However, recently a novel technique known as self-locking tenodesis or auto-tenodesis has been introduced.9 This technique aims to combine the benefits of both tenotomy and tenodesis, by detaching the LHBT at its origin at the labrum where it is thicker than the bicipital groove, allowing it to lock itself in the groove. Therefore, this technique potentially reduces the incidence of the Popeye deformity associated with conventional tenotomy while offering a simpler and quicker procedure compared with tenodesis due to not reattaching the LHBT.9 However, comparative studies between self-locking biceps tenodesis and conventional biceps tenodesis are lacking.

Therefore, the aim of this proposed RCT is to compare the clinical outcomes and complications of self-locking biceps tenodesis and 360 double lasso loop biceps tenodesis for the treatment of LHBT or superior labrum anterior-to-posterior pathology during shoulder arthroscopy in patients undergoing arthroscopic rotator cuff repair. The 360 double lasso loop was chosen as the comparator because it is an established reproducible arthroscopic technique designed for secure, circumferential tendon fixation with minimal iatrogenic tendon laceration, offering a robust benchmark for suture-based tenodesis.10 This approach firmly secures the LHBT by using one perforating passage through the tendon. Therefore, helping to prevent longitudinal laceration of tendinous fibres, potentially enhancing the overall effectiveness of the procedure. This study will assess patient-reported outcomes, functional outcomes, cosmetic outcomes, operative time and direct healthcare-related costs. A RCT will be conducted, enrolling patients with LHBT pathology who are scheduled for arthroscopic rotator cuff repair. Patients will be randomly assigned to receive either self-locking tenodesis or 360-double lasso loop tenodesis, and their outcomes will be assessed at multiple time points postoperatively. This information could potentially guide future clinical practice, helping surgeons to select the most appropriate treatment for their patients with LHBT pathology.

Objectives

Primary objective

To determine the difference in postoperative patient-reported shoulder function 1 year postoperatively between 360 double lasso loop biceps tenodesis alongside cuff repair and self-locking tenodesis. The primary hypothesis is that there will be no significant difference in patient-reported shoulder function between the two tenodesis techniques at 1 year follow-up.

Secondary objectives

To evaluate clinician-reported outcomes, including shoulder function through clinical tests, range of motion, pain on palpation, clinician-assessed cosmetic appearance, radiographic findings and surgical parameters.

Methods

Trial design

This study is a single-centre, patient-blinded RCT with blinded data analysis. Patients undergoing an arthroscopic cuff repair and biceps tenodesis will be randomly assigned in equal numbers to either the group with 360 double lasso loop tenodesis or self-locking tenodesis group from January 2025 until January 2027. This trial was set up in accordance with the standard protocol items: recommendations for interventional trials checklist.11

Participants

This study will enrol patients aged 40 years and older who are candidates for surgical repair of a tear affecting the subscapularis, supraspinatus and/or infraspinatus tendons. Participants will be recruited at a private institution specialised in shoulder surgery, the Clinique Générale d’Annecy.

Enrolment

During their first hospital visit, participants meeting the eligibility criteria will be introduced to the study by the attending physician or researcher. A comprehensive explanation of the study will be provided, and patients will receive an informed consent form to review (online supplemental 1). It will be stressed that their involvement is entirely voluntary. Once a signed informed consent document has been obtained (online supplemental 1), all patients will undergo a preoperative assessment. Before surgery, patients will be randomised by the researcher using an online randomisation tool (Study Randomiser, Phase Locked Software 2021). This randomisation tool will employ a 1:1 allocation ratio between the groups and will use a permuted block method sized 4 patients per block. The randomisation will not be stratified by baseline characteristics. The study group will be revealed to the patients 1 year after surgery after final assessment. Arthroscopic 360 double lasso loop LHBT tenodesis

Patients randomised to the 360 double lasso loop tenodesis group will undergo a tenodesis procedure involving suturing the LHBT, followed by cutting the LHBT. The tendon is fastened in the bicipital groove using a 360 double lasso loop tenodesis technique (figure 2B).10 A radiographically visible 3–0 stainless steel suture (Ethicon, Somerville, NJ, USA) will be attached to the LHBT at the proximal border of the bicipital groove before cutting the tendon to later help the localisation of LHBT during imaging (figure 3). All participating surgeons in this study have substantial experience in shoulder surgeries, having performed these procedures a minimum of 100 times.

(A) Normal anatomy, (B) 360 double lasso loop tenodesis technique and (C) Self-locking tenodesis technique.

Radiographic suture placed in the long head of the biceps tendon.

Arthroscopic self-locking LHBT tenodesis

Patients randomised to the tenotomy group will undergo a self-locking LHBT tenotomy. In this procedure, the LHBT is detached from its origin at the superior labrum. The self-locking mechanism is achieved by peeling the tendon off the superior glenoid in a Y-shape fashion, including the superior labrum from the 11 to 1 o’clock position (figure 2C).9 A radiographically visible 3–0 stainless steel suture (Ethicon, Somerville, NJ, USA) will be attached to the proximal part of the LHBT at the proximal border of the bicipital groove to later help the localisation of LHBT during imaging (figure 3). All participating surgeons in this study have substantial experience in shoulder surgeries, having performed these procedures a minimum of 100 times.

Rehabilitation

Patients from both groups will all adhere to the same postoperative rehabilitation guidelines. For the first 6 weeks after surgery, patients are required to wear a sling with shoulder abduction cushion and only engage in passive range of motion exercises under the supervision of a dedicated shoulder physiotherapist. After 6 weeks, patients will start with active movement which is gradually increased. For the first 3 months, patients are advised not to lift heavy or engage in strenuous labour.

Outcomes

The single primary outcome measure is the Constant score (CS) at 1 year after surgery. The CS is a 100-point scale with four components, including a patient-reported section (pain 15 points and activity level 20 points), for a total of 35 points. Next to this is a physician-reported section (shoulder strength 25 points, range of motion 40 points) for a total of 65 points. CS results are gender-related and decline with age; therefore, these scores will be normalised in accordance with the normalisation of the CS by Katolik et al.12 Secondary outcome measures include American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons score (ASES), simple shoulder value (SSV), LHB score,13 patient-reported cosmetic appearance, physician-reported cosmetic appearance, pain score on VAS at rest, pain score on VAS during activity, pain score in the bicipital groove on VAS and MRI/CT arthrography results.

Preoperative assessment

During the initial visit after the recruitment of the patient, demographic characteristics and several outcomes will be assessed, including the CS, LHB-score,13 cosmetic appearance and findings from MRI or CT arthrography. A dynamometer will be used for the force testing necessary for the CS and LHB score. To establish a baseline for evaluating postoperative aesthetic changes, digital photographs of the flexed upper arm will be taken from anterior, left lateral and right lateral views (figure 4). Furthermore, all participants will complete a questionnaire collecting demographic data and evaluating pain characteristics (pain at rest, pain during activity and pain in the bicipital groove), as well as Patient-Reported Outcome Measures (PROMs). These PROMs will include the CS, ASES score, Subjective Shoulder Value (SSV) and a specific questionnaire assessing cosmetic appearance.

Postoperative radiological analysis

Following surgery, imaging assessments will be conducted to evaluate the LHBT position and rotator cuff integrity. Serial postoperative radiographs, which are calibrated for scale, will be used to monitor the LHBT. The distance from a radiographic marker (associated with the tenodesis) to the apex of the humeral head will be measured on each radiograph. Changes in this measured distance across time points will be used to determine any postoperative migration of the LHBT. Furthermore, an MRI examination performed 1 year after the operation will serve two purposes: final assessment of the LHBT location and evaluation of the rotator cuff repair status. The rotator cuff healing will be classified based on this MRI, applying the Sugaya classification at the final follow-up, as intact (fully healed), partially re-torn or a full-thickness recurrent rupture.

Sample size

Calculations for determining the sample size were based on the CS. The primary hypothesis is that in patients aged 40 years or older, LHBT self-locking tenodesis, when performed alongside an arthroscopic repair of a moderately sized supraspinatus/infraspinatus tear, will not yield lower functional outcomes at 1 year postoperative compared with LHBT tenodesis. Data on the minimal important change in rotator cuff surgery, published by Kukkonen et al in 2013, served as the foundation for the computation of the sample size.14 Based on this data, a difference of <10 points on the CS and a SD of 16 points across both groups is expected. To assert non-inferiority using a one-sided, two-sample t-test, an 80% power is needed and a significance level (alpha) of 0.025. This requires a sample size of 41 patients for each group. However, considering a potential dropout rate of 20%, the aim is to recruit a total of 100 patients.

Data management

Data will be entered into a password-protected database. All data will be pseudonymised using unique participant identification codes which will be stored in a separate file. Data will be regularly backed up.

Statistical analysis

The Statistical Package for the Social Sciences from Chicago, Illinois, USA will be used for our statistical analyses. We will adhere to the intention-to-treat principle in our analyses. Our main hypothesis is that both LHBT tenodesis and tenotomy have comparable functional outcomes at long-term follow-up. We will compare CSs between groups using an independent samples t-test. As this study is non-inferiority in design, a one-sided p value of <0.025 will be considered statistically significant. Longitudinal data analysis, such as mixed models or generalised estimating equations, will be used to further compare the recovery after the two surgical interventions. In the primary model, the CS will be the dependent variable, while treatment allocation and time after surgery will be the key independent variables. To account for any possible baseline imbalances in key prognostic factors, variables such as age will be included as covariates in the multivariable model. We will use similar models to analyse the secondary outcome variables (eg, CS, pain and cosmetic appearance). In our analysis, a two-tailed value of p<0.05 is considered statistically significant. We will calculate correlation coefficients for continuous outcome measures to evaluate the relation between patient-reported and clinical measures of function.

Discussion

This RCT is designed to directly compare self-locking tenodesis to the 360 double lasso loop tenodesis in patients undergoing arthroscopic rotator cuff repair. The primary objective is to determine if self-locking tenodesis is non-inferior to the 360 double lasso loop technique regarding patient-reported shoulder function at 1 year, as measured by the CS. We hypothesise that the functional outcomes will be comparable, aligning with previous findings comparing tenotomy and tenodesis.

A key strength of this study protocol is its rigorous methodology and particularly its approach to assessing cosmetic outcomes and postoperative tendon location. The inclusion of multiple outcome domains, including validated PROMs, pain scores and, importantly, both patient-reported and clinician-reported cosmetic assessments will provide a comprehensive evaluation. A novel aspect is the objective assessment of postoperative LHBT migration using serial radiographs calibrated with an intraoperatively placed radio-opaque suture marker, supplemented by definitive 1-year MRI or CT arthrography. This allows for quantification of tendon slippage, a potential mechanism underlying Popeye deformity development and functional deficits, which has been challenging to assess accurately in previous studies. Recent literature provides valuable context for this study. Samargandi et al compared ‘tomydesis’ (a self-locking T-tenotomy) with three different tenodesis techniques in a prospective multicentre study.15 Their findings showed comparable outcomes for bicipital groove pain (p=0.69), pain during resisted supination (p=0.53) and Popeye deformity (p=0.18) across all techniques. Though the tomydesis group demonstrated increased pain in resisted flexion (p=0.032). However, this finding must be interpreted cautiously given the higher prevalence of non-repairable rotator cuff tears in this cohort. Clément et al also researched the self-locking tenodesis in their retrospective study of 180 patients, finding equivalent rates of Popeye sign between their self-locking tenotomy (27.7%) and conventional tenodesis (24%) groups (p=0.616).9 Notably, bicipital groove pain was significantly less frequent following self-locking tenotomy (25.4% vs 44%, p=0.025). Additionally, Yasser et al performed a retrospective study demonstrating minimal cosmetic deformity (Popeye sign in just 5 of 53 shoulders) and exceptional patient satisfaction (>90%) after self-locking tenodesis.16 The current study will be a valuable addition to this body of evidence, by employing a randomised controlled design which provides for more precise measurements and the assessment of biceps tendon position after tenodesis.

Limitations

We acknowledge potential limitations inherent in the study design. First, the study is conducted at a single centre, which might limit the generalisability of the results, although the standardised nature of the procedures should enhance applicability elsewhere. Second, the 1-year follow-up, while common in orthopaedic trials, may not capture very late-term complications or functional decline. Additionally, the operating surgeons in this study are not blinded to the treatment allocation, which is inherent to studies comparing two distinct surgical techniques. This introduces a potential risk of performance bias. We have tried to minimise this risk by implementing robust blinding for both the patients and the outcome assessors responsible for evaluating the outcomes. Finally, adherence to the strict rehabilitation protocol might differ from real-world variability. In conclusion, the BLAST 1 trial is poised to provide valuable, high-quality evidence comparing self-locking tenodesis with 360 double lasso loop tenodesis during arthroscopic rotator cuff repair. The findings regarding functional outcomes, tendon migration and potentially costs will be crucial for informing surgeons and patients in selecting the most appropriate LHBT management strategy. The results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publication and scientific meeting presentations.

Ethics and dissemination

This study was approved by the French Committee of Person Protection South-West V with national registration number 2024-A02166-41 (table 2). Patients are not financially compensated for participation and are allowed to withdraw from the study at any time. The results of the study will be submitted for publication in a peer-reviewed journal and the abstract of this study will be submitted to international scientific meetings.
