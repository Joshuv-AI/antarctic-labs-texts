1.
Introduction: Safe Counterfactual LTR

The goal in
LTR
(
LTR
) is to find a ranking policy (
\pi
) that optimizes a given ranking metric
(
Liu, 2009
)
;
given a set of documents (
D
), a distribution of queries
Q
, and relevance function (
P(R=1\mid d)
):

(1)

U(\pi)=\sum_{q\in Q}P(q\mid Q)\sum_{d}\omega(d\mid\pi)\;P(R=1\mid d),

where
\omega(d\mid\pi)
is the weight of the document for a given policy
\pi
;
e.g., for the
DCG
(
DCG
) metric
(
Järvelin and Kekäläinen, 2002
)
:

(2)

\omega_{\text{DCG}}(d\mid q,\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi(\cdot\mid q)}\left[(\log_{2}(\textrm{rank}(d\mid y)+1))^{-1}\right].

Because the relevance function is generally unknown,
CLTR
(
CLTR
) uses clicks to estimate
U(\pi)

(
Joachims et al., 2017
;
Wang et al., 2016
;
Oosterhuis, 2020
)
.
The
policy-aware
approach
(
Oosterhuis and de Rijke, 2020
)
starts by assuming the rank-based position bias model
(
Joachims et al., 2017
;
Wang et al., 2016
;
Craswell et al., 2008
)
, where the probability of a click on document
d
at position
k
is the product of a rank-based examination probability and document relevance:

(3)

P(C=1\mid d,k)=P(E=1\mid k)P(R=1\mid d)=\alpha_{k}P(R=1\mid d).

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a set of logged interaction data:

\mathcal{D}=\big\{q_{i},c_{i}\big\}^{N}_{i=1}
,
where each of the
N
interactions consists of a query
q_{i}
and click feedback
c_{i}(d)\in\{0,1\}
.
With the policy-aware propensities:
\rho_{0}(d\mid q_{i},\pi_{0})=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{0}(q_{i})}\big[\alpha_{k(d)}\big]=\rho_{i,0}(d)

and
\omega(d\mid q_{i},\pi)=\omega_{i}(d)
for brevity,
the policy-aware
IPS
(
IPS
) estimator is:

(4)

\hat{U}_{\text{IPS}}(\pi)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{d}\frac{\omega_{i}(d)}{\rho_{i,0}(d)}c_{i}(d).

While being unbiased,
IPS

CLTR
methods can suffer from high-variance
(
Gupta et al., 2024a
;
Oosterhuis, 2023
;
Joachims et al., 2017
)
.
Specifically, if data is limited, training an
IPS
-based method can lead to an unreliable and unsafe ranking policy
(
Gupta et al., 2023
)
, a problem also known in the bandit literature
(
Thomas et al., 2015
;
Jagerman et al., 2020
;
Swaminathan and Joachims, 2015
;
Wu and Wang, 2018
;
Gupta et al., 2024b
)
.

As a solution for
CLTR
,
Gupta et al. (2023)
introduce a risk-aware
CLTR
approach that uses divergence based on the exposure distributions of policies.
They introduce normalized propensities:
\rho^{\prime}\!(d)=\rho/\sum_{d}\rho(d)
; since
\rho^{\prime}\!(d)\in[0,1]
and
\sum_{d}\rho^{\prime}\!(d)=1
, these can be treated as a probability distribution that indicates how exposure is spread over documents.
Subsequently,
Gupta et al. (2023)
use Renyi divergence to bound the real utility with probability
1-\delta
:

P\left(U(\pi)\!\geq\!\hat{U}_{\text{IPS}}(\pi)\!-\!\!\sqrt{\frac{Z}{N}\Big(\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}\Big)\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\sum_{d}\left(\frac{\rho^{\prime}(d)}{\rho_{0}^{\prime}(d)}\right)^{2}\!\!\rho_{0}^{\prime}(d)\right]\!}\right)\!\geq\!1-\delta.

Recently
Gupta et al. (2024c)
provide a similar bound for doubly-robust
CLTR

(
Oosterhuis, 2023
)
.
However, these guarantees are only proven to hold under specific click models (e.g., Eq.
3
). Therefore, they only provide a
conditional
form of safety that may not hold in practice.

2.
Proximal Ranking Policy Optimization

In the field of
RL
(
RL
),
PPO

(
PPO
) has been introduced as a method to restrict a new policy
\pi
from deviating too much from a previously rolled-out policy
\pi_{0}

(
Schulman et al., 2015
;
Schulman et al., 2017
)
.
In contrast with the earlier discussed methods,
PPO
(
PPO
) does not make use of a divergence term but uses a simple clipping operation in its optimization objective.
Inspired by this approach, we propose the first
unconditionally
safe
CLTR
method:
PRPO

(
PRPO
).
Our method is designed for practical safety by making
no assumptions
about user behavior.
Thereby, it provides the most robust safety for
CLTR
yet.

For safety, instead of relying on a high-confidence bound
(
Gupta et al., 2024c
;
Gupta et al., 2023
)
,
PRPO
guarantees safety by removing the incentive for the new policy to rank documents too much higher than the safe logging policy.
This is achieved by directly clipping the ratio of the metric weights for a given query
q_{i}
under the new policy
\omega_{i}(d)
, and the logging policy (
\omega_{i,0}(d)
), i.e.,
\frac{\omega_{i}(d)}{\omega_{i,0}(d)}
to be bounded in a fixed predefined range:
\left[\epsilon_{-},\epsilon_{+}\right]
.
As a result, the
PRPO
objective provides no incentive for the new policy to produce weights
\omega_{i}(d)
outside of the range:
\epsilon_{-}\cdot\omega_{i,0}(d)\leq\omega_{i}(d)\leq\epsilon_{+}\cdot\omega_{i,0}(d)
.
The first step to applying
PRPO
is rewriting the utility of a ranking policy as a sum over the ratios multiplied by a reward function; this can be done without loss of generality.
For example, for the
IPS
estimator:

\hat{U}(\pi)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{d}\frac{\omega_{i}(d)}{\omega_{i,0}(d)}r(d|\,q_{i}),\quad r_{\text{IPS}}(d|\,q_{i})=\frac{\omega_{i,0}(d|\,q)}{\rho_{i,0}(d)}c_{i}(d).

PRPO
applies the following clipping function to this estimator:

(5)

f(x,\epsilon_{-},\epsilon_{+},r)=\begin{cases}\min(x,\epsilon_{+})\cdot r&r\geq 0,\\
\max(x,\epsilon_{-})\cdot r&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Applying the clipping function results in the
PRPO
estimator:

(6)

\hat{U}_{\text{PRPO}}(\pi)=\sum_{q,d\in\mathcal{D}}f\left(\frac{\omega(d\mid q)}{\omega_{0}(d\mid q)},\epsilon_{-},\epsilon_{+},r(d\mid q)\right).

Additionally, we propose adaptive clipping that loosens as the number of datapoints
N
increases, e.g., through a monotonically decreasing function
\delta(N)
:
\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\delta(N)=0
;
\epsilon_{-}=\delta(N)
and
\epsilon_{+}=\frac{1}{\delta(N)}
.

Figure
1
provides a visualization of clipping that shows how it removes incentives for optimization to move documents too far from their original rank.
Appendix
B
provides a theoretical analysis.

In summary,
PRPO
provides safety in
CLTR
through clipping, without relying on assumptions about user behavior.
This makes it uniquely robust and practical for realistic ranking settings.

3.
Results and Discussion

For our experiments, we apply a semi-synthetic experimental setup common in the
CLTR
literature
(
Oosterhuis, 2023
;
Oosterhuis and de Rijke, 2021b
;
Vardasbi et al., 2020
;
Gupta et al., 2023
)
on the three largest publicly available
LTR
datasets: Yahoo! Webscope
(
Chapelle and Chang, 2011
)
, Istella
(
Dato et al., 2016
)
, and MSLR-WEB30k
(
Qin and Liu, 2013
)
.
See Appendix
A
for details of this setup.

Figure
2
shows the performance of
PRPO
(applied to a
DR
estimator
(
Gupta et al., 2024c
;
Oosterhuis, 2023
)
) and our baselines:
IPS
and
DR
estimation and their safe
CLTR
counterparts
(
Gupta et al., 2024c
;
Gupta et al., 2023
)
, in a setting where assumptions about user behavior are correct.
We see that only safe
DR
and
PRPO
have the best convergence without an initial period of poor performance.
In contrast, plain
DR
has poor performance when
N<10^{5}
but excellent performance as
N>10^{6}
; whereas
IPS
does not have poor performance when
N
is small but is outperformed by all other methods when
N>10^{4}
.
Because the performance differences between safe
DR
and
PRPO
appear marginal, we conclude that – in this setting where correct user behavior is assumed – both methods provide comparable performance.
Given the simplicity and lack of assumptions of
PRPO
, we see this as a positive result.

Figure
3
shows the performance of
DR
, safe
DR
, and
PRPO
(with a static clipping parameter
\delta
) in an adversarial setting where the assumptions about user behavior are catastrophically incorrect.
We see that all methods result in a degradation of performance compared to the production policy.
Moreover, the performance of (unsafe)
DR
is so poor that it mostly falls outside the region visualized by the graphs.
For safe
DR
, we see that performance continues to decline as more data is available (and
N
increases), a trend that continues regardless of the
\delta

Thereby, it appears that safe
DR
is unable to provide safety in this setting where its assumptions are incorrect.
In contrast, the decreases in performance of
PRPO
are clearly bounded and appear stable once
N>10^{6}
.
Thus – even in this worst case scenario –
PRPO
successfully bounds the damage that
CLTR
can do to ranking performance.

We conclude that
PRPO
provides a robust and practical form of safety that encompasses an important progression for safe
CLTR
.

Appendix A
Simulation and Evaluation Setup

We use the three largest publicly available
LTR
datasets: Yahoo! Webscope
(
Chapelle and Chang, 2011
)
, MSLR-WEB30k
(
Qin and Liu, 2013
)
, and Istella
(
Dato et al., 2016
)
. The datasets consist of queries, a preselected list of documents per query, query-document feature vectors, and manually-graded relevance judgments for each query-document pair. Following
(
Oosterhuis, 2023
;
Vardasbi et al., 2020
;
Gupta et al., 2023
)
, we train a production ranker on a
3\%
fraction of the training queries and their corresponding relevance judgments.
The goal is to simulate a real-world setting where a ranker trained on manual judgments is deployed in production and is used to collect click logs.
The collected click logs can then be used for
CLTR
.
We assume the production ranker is safe, given that it would serve live traffic in a real-world setup.

We simulate a top-
K
ranking setup
(
Oosterhuis and de Rijke, 2020
)
where only
K=5
documents are displayed to the user for a given query, and any document beyond that gets zero exposure.
To get the relevance probability, we apply the following transformation:
P(R=1\mid q,d)=0.25\cdot rel(q,d)
, where
rel(q,d)\in\{0,1,2,3,4\}
is the relevance judgment for the given query-document pair.
We generate clicks based on the trust bias click model:

(7)

P(C=1\mid q,d,k)=\alpha_{k}P(R=1\mid q,d)+\beta_{k}.

The trust bias parameters are set based on the empirical observation in
(
Agarwal et al., 2019
)
:
\alpha=[0.35,0.53,0.55,0.54,0.52]
, and
\beta=[0.65,0.26,0.15
,
0.11
,
0.08]
.
For
CLTR
training, we only use the training and validation clicks generated via the click simulation process (Eq.
7
).
To test the robustness of the safe
CLTR
methods in a setting where the click model assumptions do not hold,
we simulate an
adversarial click model
, where the user clicks on the irrelevant document with a high probability and on a relevant document with a low click probability.
We define the adversarial click model as:

(8)

P(C=1\mid q,d,k)=1-\left(\alpha_{k}P(R=1\mid q,d)+\beta_{k}\right).

Thereby, we simulate a maximally
adversarial
user who clicks on documents with a click probability that is inversely correlated with the assumed trust bias model.

Further, we assume that the logging propensities have to be estimated.
For the logging propensities
\rho_{0}
, and the logging metric weights (
\omega_{0}
), we use a simple Monte-Carlo estimate
(
Gupta et al., 2023
)
:

(9)

\hat{\rho}_{0}(d)=\frac{1}{N}\sum^{N}_{i=1:y_{i}\sim\pi_{0}\hskip-28.45274pt}\alpha_{k_{i}(d)},\quad\hat{\omega}_{0}(d)=\frac{1}{N}\sum^{N}_{i=1:y_{i}\sim\pi_{0}\hskip-22.76228pt}\left(\alpha_{k_{i}(d)}+\beta_{k_{i}(d)}\right).

For the learned policies (
\pi
), we optimize
PL
(
PL
) ranking models
(
Oosterhuis, 2021
)
using the REINFORCE policy-gradient method
(
Yadav et al., 2021
;
Gupta et al., 2023
)
.
We perform clipping on the logging propensities only for the training clicks and not for the validation set.
Following previous work, we set the clipping parameter to
10/\sqrt{N}

(
Gupta et al., 2023
;
Oosterhuis and de Rijke, 2021a
)
.
We do not apply the clipping operation for the logging metric weights.
To prevent overfitting, we apply early stopping based on the validation clicks.
For variance reduction, we follow
(
Yadav et al., 2021
;
Gupta et al., 2023
)
and use the average reward per query as a control-variate.

As our evaluation metric, we compute the NDCG@5 metric using the relevance judgments on the test split of each dataset
(
Järvelin and Kekäläinen, 2002
)
.
Finally, the following methods are included in our comparisons:

(i)

IPS
. The
IPS
estimator with affine correction
(
Vardasbi et al., 2020
;
Oosterhuis and de Rijke, 2021a
)
for
CLTR
with trust bias.

(ii)

Doubly Robust
. The
DR
estimator for
CLTR
with trust bias
(
Oosterhuis, 2023
)
.

(iii)

Safe
DR
. The recently-introduced safe
DR

CLTR
method
(
Gupta et al., 2024c
)
, which relies on a trust bias assumption.

(iv)

PRPO
. Our proposed
PRPO

(
PRPO
) method for safe
CLTR
(Eq.
6
), we apply it to the
DR
estimator (see
(
Gupta et al., 2024c
)
for a more detailed formulation).

(v)

Skyline.
LTR
method trained on the true relevance labels. Given that it is trained on the real relevance signal, the skyline performance is the upper bound on possible
CLTR
performance.

All source code to reproduce our experimental results is available at:

https://github.com/shashankg7/cikm-safeultr
.

Appendix B
Formal Proof of Safety

Theorem B.1
.

Let
q
be a query,
\omega
be metric weights,
y_{0}
be a logging policy ranking, and
y^{*}(\epsilon_{-},\epsilon_{+})
be the ranking that optimizes the
PRPO
objective in Eq.
6
.
Assume that
\forall d,\in\mathcal{D},r(d\mid q)\not=0
.
Then,
for any
\Delta\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, there exist values for
\epsilon_{-}
and
\epsilon_{+}
that guarantee that the difference between the utility of
y_{0}
and
y^{*}(\epsilon_{-},\epsilon_{+})
is bounded by
\Delta
:

(10)

\forall\Delta\!\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},\exists\epsilon_{-}\!\!\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},\epsilon_{+}\!\!\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0};\;|U(y_{0})-U(y^{*}(\epsilon_{-},\epsilon_{+}))|\leq\Delta.

Proof.

Given a logging policy ranking
y_{0}
, a user defined metric weight
\omega
, and non-zero
r(d\mid q)
, for the choice of the clipping parameters
\epsilon_{-}=\epsilon_{+}=1
,
the ranking
y^{*}(\epsilon_{-},\epsilon_{+})
that maximizes the
PRPO
objective (Eq.
6
) will be the same as the logging ranking
y_{0}
, i.e.
y^{*}(\epsilon_{-},\epsilon_{+})=y_{0}
.
This is trivial to prove since any change in ranking can only lead to a decrease in the clipped ratio weights, and thus, a decrease in the
PRPO
objective.
Therefore,
y^{*}(\epsilon_{-}=1,\epsilon_{+}=1)=y_{0}
when
\epsilon_{-}=\epsilon_{+}=1
.
Accordingly:
|U(y_{0})-U(y^{*}(\epsilon_{-}=1,\epsilon_{+}=1))|=0
directly implies Eq.
10
.
This completes our proof.
∎

Whilst the above proof is performed through the extreme case where
\epsilon_{-}=\epsilon_{+}=1
and the optimal ranking has the same utility as the logging policy ranking,
other choices of
\epsilon_{-}
and
\epsilon_{+}
bound the difference in utility to a lesser degree and allow for more deviation.
As our experimental results show, the power of PRPO is that it gives practitioners direct control over this maximum deviation.

Appendix C
Gradient Ascent and Possible Extensions

Finally, we consider how the
PRPO
objective should be optimized. This turns out to be very straightforward when we look at its gradient.
The clipping function
f
(Eq.
5
) has a simple gradient involving an indicator function on whether
x
is inside the bounds:

(11)

\nabla_{x}f(x,\epsilon_{-},\epsilon_{+},r)=\mathds{1}\big[(r>0\land x\leq\epsilon_{+})\lor(r<0\land x\geq\epsilon_{-})\big]r.

Applying the chain rule to the
PRPO
objective (Eq.
6
) reveals:

\nabla_{\!\pi}\hat{U}_{\text{PRPO}}(\pi)\!=\!\!\sum_{q,d\in\mathcal{D}}\underbrace{\!\!\Big[\nabla_{\!\pi}\frac{\omega(d|q)}{\omega_{0}(d|q)}\Big]}_{\text{\hskip-14.22636ptgrad. for single doc.\hskip-14.22636pt}}\underbrace{\nabla_{\!\pi}f\bigg(\!\frac{\omega(d|q)}{\omega_{0}(d|q)},\epsilon_{-},\epsilon_{+},r(d|q)\!\bigg)}_{\text{\raisebox{0.0pt}[4.49553pt][0.0pt]{indicator reward function}}}.

Thus, the gradient of
PRPO
simply takes the importance weighted metric gradient per document, and multiplies it with the indicator function and reward.
As a result,
PRPO
is simple to combine with existing
LTR
algorithms, especially ones that use policy-gradients
(
Williams, 1992
)
, such as PL-Rank
(
Oosterhuis, 2021
;
Oosterhuis, 2022
)
or StochasticRank
(
Ustimenko and Prokhorenkova, 2020
)
.
For methods in the family of LambdaRank
(
Wang et al., 2018
;
Burges, 2010
;
Burges et al., 2006
)
, it is a matter of replacing the
|\Delta DCG|
term with an equivalent for the PRPO bounded metric.

Lastly, we note that whilst we introduced
PRPO
for
DR
estimation, it can be extended to virtually any relevance estimation by choosing a different
r
;
e.g., one can easily adapt it for relevance estimates from a click model
(
Chuklin et al., 2015
)
, etc.
In this sense, we argue that
PRPO
can be seen as a framework for robust safety in
LTR
in general.
