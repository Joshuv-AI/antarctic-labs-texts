Physiological and psychomotor effectiveness of rapid and slow induction of nitrous oxide inhalation sedation in pediatric patients undergoing dental procedures: a randomized clinical study
Authors: Sneha Chandulal Dori, Anshula N Deshpande, Simron Baishya, Vidhi Jaiswal, Sakshi Mishra, Medha Wadhwa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNitrous oxide–oxygen (N2O:O2) inhalation sedation is a safe and effective technique for managing dental anxiety and achieving light conscious sedation. It increases the pain threshold, reduces fatigue, and improves patient cooperation. Two primary induction techniques are used: slow and rapid induction, with the latter preferred for highly anxious or uncooperative patients who require immediate sedation during dental procedures. This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of different concentrations of N2O:O2 inhalation sedation and to assess changes in physiological and psychomotor parameters, and post-sedation recovery, in pediatric patients.MethodsA total of 78 children who met the inclusion criteria were randomly divided into three groups: Group A (conventional slow induction), Group B (rapid induction with 30:70 N2O:O2), and Group C (rapid induction with 50:50 N2O:O2). Demographic and medical data were recorded. Baseline vital signs and the Trieger Dot Test were performed before treatment. Induction was carried out according to group protocol. The Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, Consolability (FLACC) pain score and the Observer’s Assessment of Alertness/Sedation (OAA/S) scale were used to assess pain and sedation levels, respectively. Vital signs were monitored during and after treatment. Post-recovery, the Trieger Dot Test was repeated, and a 24-hour follow-up call was made to assess the child’s well-being.ResultsAll groups maintained vital signs within normal limits. Differences in pain perception and psychomotor impairment were statistically non-significant. A statistically significant difference was observed in the sedation levels among the groups (P = 0.033). Light sedation was achieved in 69.23%, 57.69%, and 73.08% of participants in Groups A, B, and C, respectively, while moderate sedation was observed in 19.23% of participants in the 50:50 induction group. A 24-hour telephonic follow-up revealed that all children had resumed normal activities and daily routines.ConclusionThe results provide clinicians with evidence-based guidance on N2O:O2 titration and induction techniques. The preadjusted mixture of 30% N2O and 70% O2 offers a more efficient approach to achieving an adequate depth of sedation in a shorter period while increasing pain tolerance. Therefore, it can serve as an effective alternative to slow induction for administering N2O:O2 inhalation sedation in restless and anxious pediatric patients.

INTRODUCTION

Dental anxiety is a common issue among children and significantly affects their oral health and access to dental care. Fear is frequently triggered by dental instruments such as syringe cartridges, extraction forceps, rubber dam punches, and explorers, as well as by the stress of an initial dental visit. This anxiety often leads to the avoidance of dental treatment, resulting in untreated caries and periodontal disease [1]. A 2024 systematic review by Sun et al. reported that approximately 30% of children aged 2–6 years experience dental fear and anxiety [2]. Pediatric dentists therefore face the dual challenge of providing effective dental treatment while addressing the psychological and behavioral needs of young patients. The American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry (AAPD) [3] emphasizes “behavior guidance” as a means to facilitate communication, alleviate anxiety, ensure quality care, and promote lifelong oral health habits. Nonpharmacological techniques—such as tell-show-do, voice control, and distraction—are often effective; however, highly anxious children may require pharmacological approaches, including N2O inhalation sedation, or, in more severe cases, general anesthesia [3].

Pharmacological behavior management strategies, such as nitrous oxide–oxygen conscious sedation combined with local anesthesia (LA) when required, serve as a cornerstone of pain-free dentistry for children. Nitrous oxide inhalation sedation (NOIS) has been recognized by the AAPD as a safe and effective method for providing analgesia, alleviating anxiety, and enhancing communication between the patient and the dental practitioner [3]. It is the most commonly used sedative agent in pediatric dentistry due to its favorable safety profile and rapid onset of action. The hallmark features of N2O include its quick onset and equally rapid postoperative recovery, both achievable through titration [4].

The conventional administration method begins with 100% oxygen, followed by incremental titration of N2O (slow induction), which allows continuous monitoring and minimizes potential risks. Although effective, this technique can be time-consuming [5]. Alternatively, the rapid induction method involves the immediate administration of a preadjusted N2O:O2 mixture to anxious or uncooperative patients. Once the patient is calm, concentrations are titrated as needed. This method enables faster sedation but may increase the risk of adverse effects, including nausea, dizziness, or hypoxia [6]. According to Kaviani et al. [7], nausea and vomiting may occur with prolonged exposure to N2O:O2 for more than 2 hours or at concentrations exceeding 50% N2O, with a 0.7% risk of hypoxia.

While 20–30% N2O is generally considered safe for minimal sedation, evidence regarding its use for rapid induction in children remains limited. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of preadjusted 30:70 and 50:50 N2O:O2 mixtures in achieving rapid sedation, while monitoring physiological, psychomotor, and adverse effects.

METHODS

An in vivo, double-blinded, randomized clinical study was conducted in the Department of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry. Ethical clearance was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee prior to the commencement of the study (SVIEC/ON/Dent/BNPG22/AUG/23/1). The clinical trial was registered in the Clinical Trials Registry–India (CTRI/2023/12/060666). The inclusion criteria were as follows: patients requiring local anesthesia for dental treatment; those rated as 3 or 4 on the Frankl Behavior Rating Scale during examination; individuals classified as American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) Physical Status I or II; subjects with physiological parameters within acceptable limits; and children whose parents or guardians provided written informed consent. Exclusion criteria included any medical condition contraindicating the use of N2O:O2 sedation, known allergy to lignocaine, claustrophobia, or active upper respiratory tract infections.

1. Sample size calculation:

The total sample size was calculated using the formula: n = N x / [(N − 1)E2 + x], where N represents the population size, E the margin of error, and x the value derived from the fraction of responses at Z(c/100), which corresponds to the critical value for the confidence level c at 80% power. Based on the inclusion criteria, N was determined to be 97 patients, resulting in a calculated sample size (n) of 78 at a 95% confidence level with E = 0.05. A total of 78 children aged 5–9 years were selected after screening according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria.

This age group was chosen because children in this developmental stage can comprehend and follow breathing instructions when using a nasal mask and can benefit from the sedative and analgesic properties of nitrous oxide [4].

2. Randomization:

Participants were randomly assigned to three groups using a computer-generated randomization method (Fig. 1).

Group A: Conventional slow induction

Group B: Rapid induction using a 30:70 N2O:O2 concentration

Group C: Rapid induction using a 50:50 N2O:O2 concentration

3. Clinical procedure:

Each patient’s demographic details and brief medical history were recorded on a data sheet, and written informed consent was obtained prior to participation. Before commencing the procedure, baseline heart rate (HR) and oxygen saturation (SpO2) were recorded to assess fitness for N2O:O2 inhalation sedation. Pre-procedural psychomotor skills were evaluated using the Trieger Dot Test [8]. Each child was shown how the nasal hood and pulse oximeter would be placed and was instructed on how to respond to commands during the procedure using the tell–show–do technique. A Matrx Porter Digital Relative Analgesia Machine was used throughout the study.

Group A: Participants received N2O through slow induction, beginning with 100% O2 for 2–3 minutes to determine the appropriate flow rate. N2O administration started at 10% and was titrated in approximately 10% increments every 60 seconds, with continuous monitoring of sedation via verbal, visual, and tactile interaction. Once objective signs of sedation and patient comfort were confirmed, the dental procedure was initiated [9].

Group B: Participants received N2O:O2 via rapid induction with a pre-adjusted mixture of 30% N2O and 70% O2. If adequate sedation was not achieved at 30% N2O, the concentration was increased in 10% increments. In cases of hyper-sedation, reverse titration was performed by reducing N2O in 10% decrements until optimal sedation was achieved [4].

Group C: Participants received N2O:O2 via rapid induction with a pre-adjusted 50:50 mixture. Sedation levels at 50% N2O were recorded. If adequate sedation was not achieved, N2O concentration was increased in 10% increments; in cases of hyper-sedation, it was decreased in 10% decrements.

At the end of treatment, N2O flow was gradually tapered as the child’s need for sedation diminished. After completion of the dental procedure, each child received 100% O2 at the established flow rate for three to five minutes. Discharge was permitted once the child’s responsiveness returned to pre-sedation or near-normal levels.

4. Method of evaluation

Physiological parameters—pulse rate and oxygen saturation—were recorded at three stages: P1 (pre-treatment), P2 (post-sedation), and P3 (five minutes post-sedation oxygenation). Sedation depth was evaluated using the Observer’s Assessment of Alertness/Sedation (OAA/S) Scale [10]. Pain perception during LA administration was assessed using the Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, Consolability (FLACC) Scale [11]. Psychomotor performance was measured using the Trieger Dot Test at two time points: M1 (pre-treatment) and M2 (five minutes post-sedation). A 24-hour telephonic follow-up was conducted to assess post-treatment well-being.

5. Statistical analysis

Data were entered into Microsoft Excel (2016) and analyzed using descriptive and inferential statistics. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as numbers and percentages. Inferential analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 21. Chi-square tests were used to examine associations between categorical variables related to the induction process, enabling assessment of differences in frequency distributions across groups. Continuous variables associated with induction parameters and vital signs were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to compare group means. When ANOVA indicated statistically significant differences, Bonferroni post hoc tests were performed for pairwise comparisons while controlling for Type I error. A P-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

RESULTS

A randomized clinical study was conducted to assess the efficacy of different concentrations of N2O:O2 inhalation sedation and to evaluate changes in physiological and psychomotor parameters, as well as post-sedation recovery, in pediatric patients. The demographic data comprised a total of 78 participants, with a slight male predominance—42 males (53.8%) and 36 females (46.2%). Participants were evenly distributed across three groups: Group A (slow induction), Group B (30:70 N2O:O2), and Group C (50:50 N2O:O2), with 26 participants in each group.

Tables 1 and 2 summarize the Bonferroni post hoc comparisons of SpO2 and pulse rate between groups at different time points. For SpO2, no significant differences were found in preoperative or postoperative values among the groups. However, during treatment, Group C exhibited significantly lower SpO2 values compared with both Group A (mean difference = 1.692, P < 0.001) and Group B (mean difference = 1.231, P < 0.001), indicating a transient decrease in oxygen saturation in the 50:50 induction group.

Regarding pulse rate, Group C consistently demonstrated significantly higher values compared with Groups A and B, both preoperatively (A vs. C: −19.038, P = 0.002; B vs. C: −23.000, p < 0.001) and during treatment (A vs. C: −13.077, p = 0.002; B vs. C: −15.000, P < 0.001). Postoperatively, no significant differences were observed, although Group C maintained relatively higher pulse rates. These findings suggest that the 50:50 induction method elicited greater transient cardiovascular and respiratory variations during treatment.

Table 3 summarizes the FLACC pain scores among the three groups (A, B, and C), each comprising 26 participants. Group B exhibited the highest proportion of participants reporting mild discomfort (57.69%) and no cases of severe discomfort. Group A showed a more balanced distribution between mild (38.46%) and moderate (34.62%) discomfort, while Group C had the greatest proportion of participants who were relaxed and comfortable (34.62%) and a small percentage experiencing severe pain (3.85%). The Pearson Chi-square test yielded a value of 9.045 with a P-value of 0.171, indicating no statistically significant difference in FLACC scores among the three groups.

Table 4 presents the distribution of OAA/S scores across the three groups and the results of post hoc Bonferroni comparisons. Group A recorded the highest proportion of participants scoring 4 (69.23%) and no participants scoring 3. Group B showed a similar distribution, with 57.69% scoring 4 and 7.69% scoring 3. In contrast, Group C had the lowest proportion of participants scoring 5 (7.69%) and the highest scoring 3 (19.23%). Bonferroni post hoc comparisons revealed statistically significant differences between Group A and Group C (mean difference = 0.423, P = 0.016) and between Group B and Group C (mean difference = 0.384, P = 0.034), indicating that Group C differed significantly from both Groups A and B. No significant difference was observed between Groups A and B (P = 1.000).

The bar graph in Figure 2 illustrates Trieger Test scores (Score 0 and Score 1) across three groups. Group B (30:70) showed the highest proportion of Score 1 (96.15%) and the lowest Score 0 (3.85%), indicating better performance. Group A (Slow Ind) had 88.46% with Score 1 and 11.54% with Score 0. Group C (50:50) showed the lowest Score 1 rate (84.62%) and the highest Score 0 rate (15.38%), suggesting comparatively reduced performance after exposure.

The Table 5 presents post-procedural side effects and outcomes across three groups. No participants in Groups A or B reported vomiting, headache, or dizziness. In Group C, 1 child (3.84%) experienced vomiting, 7 (26.92%) reported headaches, and 5 (19.23%) reported dizziness. All participants in every group resumed normal activities, had no issues with eating or drinking, and experienced no unusual sensations, behavioral changes, or overall health changes. Additionally, all children in all groups reported pain relief following the procedure. Chi-square test was applied for intergroup comparison of various postoperative symptoms, and no statistically significant differences were observed across the groups for any of the symptoms.

DISCUSSION

Nitrous oxide inhalation sedation is recognized as a highly effective pharmacological behavior management technique because of its rapid onset, quick recovery, and minimal adverse effects. It enhances patient comfort and promotes psychological well-being, particularly in pediatric dental care. N2O sedation is widely used in both children and adults, and the AAPD supports individualized behavior management approaches tailored to each patient’s needs [3]. Previous studies have reported that approximately 89% of dentists use N2O, with only 2% administering concentrations exceeding 50% [12]. In the present study, 78 children aged 5–9 years were included, ensuring that participants could comprehend verbal instructions and cooperate during the procedure. Only children with Frankl Behavior Ratings of 3 or 4 were enrolled, indicating cooperative behavior suitable for nasal hood placement and adherence to nasal breathing guidance. Continuous monitoring of physiological parameters—oxygen saturation (SpO2) and pulse rate—was performed throughout sedation to ensure patient safety and enable the early detection of any adverse physiological responses. SpO2 monitoring confirmed adequate oxygenation, while pulse rate provided real-time feedback on the physiological response to sedation. None of the participants had prior exposure to N2O sedation.

The present study observed a significant decline in pulse rate across all three groups at varying N2O concentrations, indicating a calm physiological state and reduced anxiety. Importantly, heart rate reductions remained within normal physiological limits throughout the procedure. Primosch et al. reported a significant decrease in pulse rate following 40% N2O inhalation, whereas Wilson et al. found that pulse rates remained within acceptable limits during extractions using 30% N2O [1314]. In contrast, Niwa et al. [15] noted no cardiovascular changes with N2O sedation, and Takkar et al. [9] observed no significant heart rate variation over time. According to Malamed, N2O does not directly depress cardiac function but may exert a beneficial effect by reducing endogenous catecholamine release, thereby potentially lowering heart rate and blood pressure [16]. Conversely, Roberts et al. reported a slight rise in pulse rate after local anesthesia administration during pediatric dental procedures [17].

To detect early hypoxia—a potential concern with high N2O concentrations and rapid induction—SpO2 was monitored before, during, and after treatment. In the present study, all groups maintained oxygen saturation within normal limits (97–98%), except for a slight decrease during treatment (96.5%) in the 50:50 rapid induction group. No hypoxic episodes were observed. Samir PV and Khinda et al. also reported the absence of hypoxia during N2O sedation, even when postoperative oxygen was not administered [45]. Similarly, Quarnstrom et al., Jeske et al., and others found no oxygen-related adverse events with N2O concentrations up to 50% [1819]. Brodsky et al. noted that hypoxia is rare in healthy individuals with normal respiration [20]. Gupta (2022) suggested that pulse oximetry might be unnecessary during light sedation in healthy patients [21]. In contrast, Sharma S reported increased SpO2 levels and reduced heart rate and pain at 40–50% N2O [22].

Effective pain management in dentistry depends greatly on the proper administration of local anesthesia (LA). The inferior alveolar nerve block is commonly used for mandibular anesthesia but is often associated with discomfort. Although the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) is frequently employed to assess pain in children, its subjective nature limits its accuracy following sedation. Therefore, the objective and reliable FLACC scale was used in this study to assess discomfort levels. In the present study, 34% of children in the 50:50 group, 23% in the 30:70 group, and 15% in the slow induction group appeared calm. Mild discomfort during LA administration was most frequently reported in the 30:70 group (57%), followed by the slow induction (38%) and 50:50 (30%) groups. Severe discomfort was observed in 11.54% of participants in the slow-induction group and 3.85% in the 50:50 group, with no cases in the 30:70 group. Takkar et al. and Srinivasan et al. reported that N2O significantly reduced intraoperative discomfort compared with other behavior management methods [923]. Arcari et al. and Bonafé-Monzo et al. found effective pain control at 30–50% N2O concentrations, with better tolerance in older children [2425]. Pasarone observed reduced pain recall with N2O sedation [26]. Similarly, Reshi and Thomas et al. reported significant reductions in pain and FLACC scores with N2O (P < 0.001 and P = 0.0049, respectively) [2728].

In pediatric dentistry, N2O typically induces minimal sedation, though moderate sedation may occur depending on the administered concentration and individual patient response. Concentrations below 50% generally maintain patient responsiveness, making them ideal for routine dental procedures. Sedation may be achieved through slow or rapid induction techniques. Slow induction—considered safer due to gradual titration—has limited efficacy at concentrations ≤25% N2O and requires more time to achieve the desired effect. Rapid induction, on the other hand, achieves sedation more quickly, which justified its inclusion in the current comparison using the OAA/S Scale, a validated tool for assessing sedation depth [10]. Higher OAA/S scores indicate lighter sedation levels.

In this study, the slow induction group (Group A) demonstrated that 30.77% of participants had an OAA/S score of 5 and 69.23% had a score of 4, indicating minimal sedation. No subjects reached moderate sedation (score 3). These findings are consistent with those of Takkar et al. [9] and Samir PV et al. [4], who reported comparable results at 30–40% N2O concentrations. In the 30:70 rapid induction group (Group B), 34.62% of participants had a score of 5, 57.69% had a score of 4, and 7.69% achieved moderate sedation (score 3), findings similar to those reported by Samir PV [4] and Preethy et al. [29]. At the 50:50 concentration (Group C), only 7.69% of participants remained fully alert (score 5), 73.08% reached mild sedation (score 4), and 19.23% attained moderate sedation (score 3). These results are consistent with the findings of Nandinidevi et al. and Zier JL et al., who observed effective sedation with minimal side effects at 50% N2O [3031]. However, Kharouba J. suggested that a 60% N2O concentration might offer a more favorable balance between efficacy and safety in pediatric patients [32].

Psychomotor recovery following N2O sedation was evaluated using the Trieger Dot Test, which assesses fine motor coordination and cognitive integration. In the present study, most participants completed the test successfully, with no statistically significant difference between baseline and post-sedation scores after 5 minutes of 100% O2 administration. These findings are consistent with those of Shafi and Khinda, who also reported no significant psychomotor impairment following sedation [527]. In contrast, Sharma et al. observed significant differences at varying N2O concentrations (P < 0.001), while Houpt reported a mild but significant decline in motor coordination (P < 0.001) using the Bender Visual Motor Gestalt Test [22].

A 24-hour telephonic follow-up was conducted to assess post-sedation well-being. Within the first 24 hours after treatment, calm behavior was reported in 46.15% of participants in the slow induction group, 53.85% in the 30:70 group, and 53.85% in the 50:50 group. Sleepiness was more prevalent in the slow induction group (23.08%) compared with 3.85% and 7.69% in the 30:70 and 50:50 groups, respectively. Only one participant (3.85%) in the 50:50 group reported vomiting. No stumbling or major adverse effects were observed. Headache was reported in 26.92% and dizziness in 19.23% of participants in the 50:50 group, while these symptoms were minimal or absent in the other groups. These results are consistent with previous studies that documented low incidences of nausea, vomiting, and drowsiness at N2O concentrations below 70%. Overall, all children resumed normal activities after treatment without significant complications.

Limitations

• This investigation was not conducted as a blinded controlled trial; the pediatric dentist was aware of the type and concentration of sedation administered.• All observations were made by a single examiner. Incorporating multiple observers in future studies would improve the reliability and validity of the findings through inter-examiner comparison.• Further research is required to evaluate the effects of nitrous oxide in children exhibiting defiant behavior or high levels of anxiety. Special attention should be given to assessing its effects in younger children, including those as young as two years old.• The present study did not examine the long-term behavioral outcomes associated with different induction methods.

Conclusion

All three N2O:O2 sedation techniques were found to be safe and effective for pediatric dental patients, maintaining physiological parameters within normal limits and facilitating effective management of pain and anxiety. Rapid induction with a 50:50 N2O:O2 concentration demonstrated the highest sedation effectiveness and patient cooperation but was associated with a slightly higher incidence of minor side effects. In contrast, rapid induction with a 30:70 concentration provided sedation comparable to that achieved with slow induction but required less time, making it a suitable alternative for anxious or uncooperative children. Overall, nitrous oxide inhalation sedation proved to be a safe, efficient, and well-tolerated technique, supporting its continued use as a reliable behavior management strategy in pediatric dentistry.

All three induction techniques were safe and effective for pediatric dental dentistry. The 50:50 method achieved greater sedation but was associated with slightly more side effects, whereas the 30:70 method offered a balanced alternative for anxious or uncooperative children.
