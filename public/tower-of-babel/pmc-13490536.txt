A murine proof-of-concept study of polatuzumab vedotin in combination with venetoclax in experimental therapy of BCL2-positive aggressive lymphomas
Authors: Eva Pokorna, Dmitry Manakov, Kristyna Nozickova, Petra Kovarikova, Alexandra Dolnikova, Nicol Renesova, Liliana Tuskova, Magdalena Klanova, Zuzana Vreckova, Ondrej Havranek, Marek Trneny, Pavel Klener
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe evaluated the in vivo efficacy of polatuzumab vedotin (POLA), administered as a single agent or in combination with venetoclax (VEN), in relapsed/refractory (R/R) mantle cell lymphoma (MCL) and BCL2-positive diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL). In addition, we investigated the mechanisms underlying acquired resistance to POLA in vivo. Experimental therapy was assessed using a panel of 8 cell line–derived xenografts (CDXs) and 16 patient-derived xenografts (PDXs) representing MCL and DLBCL. Bulk transcriptomic profiling was performed on 7 paired tumor samples obtained from POLA-resistant tumors (generated in mice following repeated POLA-based treatments) and their corresponding untreated controls. POLA demonstrated robust single-agent antitumor activity in vivo, including in PDX models derived from patients with ibrutinib-resistant MCL. In the majority of tested PDX models, the combination of POLA and VEN produced synergistic antitumor effects without evidence of measurable toxicity. CD79B expression levels did not correlate with POLA efficacy. Following POLA treatment failure, downregulation of CD79B was observed in only a minority of models, indicating the involvement of additional mechanisms of acquired resistance. Bulk transcriptomic analysis of 7 paired POLA-resistant versus untreated tumors identified a conserved gene expression signature across all models, comprising 523 downregulated and 284 upregulated genes. This signature likely reflects core pathways associated with POLA resistance and highlights potential novel therapeutic vulnerabilities. These findings strongly support further clinical investigation of POLA in combination with VEN as a BCL2- and MCL1-targeting therapeutic strategy in patients with R/R MCL and BCL2-positive R/R DLBCL.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55062-2.Keywords: Mantle cell lymphoma (MCL), Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), Polatuzumab vedotin, Venetoclax, Patient-derived xenografts (PDX), Drug resistanceSubject terms: Cancer, Drug discovery, Oncology

Introduction

Mantle cell lymphoma (MCL) is a predominantly aggressive B-cell lymphoma characterized by a chronic relapsing course1,2. The majority of MCL cells express components of the B-cell receptor complex, including CD79A and CD79B, as well as the anti-apoptotic protein BCL23. The prognosis of patients with relapsed/refractory (R/R) MCL following failure of the Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitor (BTKi) ibrutinib remains poor, underscoring the urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies4–6. At present, brexucabtagene autoleucel represents the treatment of choice for most patients with covalent BTKi-refractory disease who are eligible for chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy. However, brexu-cel induces durable remissions in only a subset of patients, and its use is limited by high manufacturing costs, restricted global availability, and the requirement for prolonged hospitalization. Moreover, its administration is associated with substantial hematologic and non-hematologic toxicity, as well as non-relapse mortality7,8. Consequently, several alternative therapeutic approaches are currently under investigation. These include the non-covalent BTK inhibitor pirtobrutinib, the BCL2 inhibitor venetoclax (VEN), the immunomodulatory agent lenalidomide, bispecific antibodies (glofitamab, mosunetuzumab), antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs; zilovertamab vedotin, loncastuximab tesirine), and other targeted agents such as parsaclisib, palbociclib, and MALT1 inhibitors. These agents are being evaluated as monotherapies or in various combination regimens for the experimental treatment of R/R MCL following failure of covalent BTK inhibitors and/or CAR T-cell therapy9–13.

Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is a potentially curative aggressive B-cell malignancy14. Patients with relapsed or refractory disease (R/R DLBCL) after frontline chemoimmunotherapy may be treated with CAR T-cell therapy or several other innovative approaches, including polatuzumab vedotin (POLA)–based regimens15–18.

POLA is a first-in-class anti-CD79B antibody–drug conjugate that delivers the microtubule-disrupting agent monomethyl auristatin E (MMAE)19. The combination of POLA with bendamustine and rituximab (POLA-BR) has been approved for the treatment of patients with R/R DLBCL20. More recently, POLA-BR has been successfully used as a bridging therapy to CD19-directed CAR T-cell treatment in MCL, and this regimen is currently being evaluated in patients with R/R MCL after BTKi failure (NCT04913103)21. In addition, POLA combined with standard immunochemotherapy (rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, and prednisone; POLA-R-CHP) significantly improved survival outcomes in previously untreated patients with DLBCL compared with R-CHOP22. Furthermore, the combination of POLA and VEN has recently demonstrated promising anti-lymphoma activity in R/R non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL)23.

In the present study, we conducted a murine phase II–like proof-of-concept efficacy study evaluating single-agent POLA and its combination with VEN in experimental models of MCL and BCL2-positive DLBCL. The study was performed using a panel of 8 MCL cell line–derived xenografts (CDXs), 10 patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models of MCL, and 6 PDX models of DLBCL. In addition, bulk transcriptomic profiling of POLA-resistant tumors compared with untreated controls identified gene expression programs associated with acquired resistance to POLA.

Materials and methods

Cell lines

UPF1H and UPF19U cell lines were derived in our laboratory from patients with R/R MCL24. The whole exome sequencing (WES) analysis confirmed that the newly derived cell lines (designated patient-derived cell lines, PDCL) shared majority of somatic mutations with the primary lymphoma cells, from which they were derived (Supplemental Fig. S1). The WES data can be downloaded from Zenodo repository: 10.5281/zenodo.15609254.

HBL2 cells were a kind gift from prof. Martin Dreyling. The remaining, commercially available MCL cell lines (i.e., JEKO-1, REC-1, Z138, MINO, MAVER-1) were obtained from DSMZ or ATCC, and all were authenticated by Multiplexion. MCL cell lines were grown in Iscove´s modified Dulbecco´s medium (IMDM) culture medium supplemented with 15% FBS and antibiotics (Penicillin–Streptomycin). UPF1H and UPF19U were cultured in Ontario Cancer Institute (OCI) medium supplemented with 20% human AB serum, heparin and antibiotics.

Cytotoxic agents, apoptosis and proliferation measurement

Venetoclax (VEN) and monomethylauristatin E (MMAE) were purchased from MedChemExpress, polatuzumab vedotin (POLA) was provided by Roche.

For apoptosis, cells were seeded on 96-well plates and cultivated with POLA, MMAE, VEN, or indicated combinations for 24 or 48 h. Cells were stained with Annexin V FITC (Apronex, Czech Republic) and propidium iodide (Sigma), and the number of apoptotic and/or necrotic cells was determined by flow cytometry (BD FACS Canto II). Drug concentration needed for killing of 50% cells (= LD50) was calculated using Graph Pad Prism software.

For proliferation, cells were seeded on 96 well plate with the indicated concentrations of POLA. After 72 h proliferation was measured with WST-8-based cell proliferation assay using the TECAN reader according to the manufacturer’s instructions. POLA concentration needed for inhibition of 50% cells (= IC50) was calculated using Graph Pad Prism software. Combination indexes were calculated using CompuSyn By Ting Chao Chou25.

Flow cytometry and BD QuantiBrite quantitation assay

Tumors were homogenized using a 40 μM cell strainer (Falcon, Corning, NY). The homogenized samples were washed in PBS and incubated for 15 min with polyclonal IgG (1% Flebogamma, 50mg/ml, Grifols) to avoid unspecific binding. After washing in PBS, the cells were stained with the specified antibodies for 15 min at room temperature in the dark, and then twice washed with PBS. The following fluorochrome-conjugated mAb were used: CD79b PE (clone CB3-1 (RUO), BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ), IgG1κ PE (clone MOPC-21, BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ). Samples were analyzed by FACSCanto (Becton Dickinson, San Jose, CA). BD Quantibrite™ PE Phycoerythrin Fluorescence Quantitation Kit was used for the quantification of surface antigens according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The results were processed with Kaluza software, version 2.1 (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA). Isotype matched negative controls were used in all the assays to distinguish positive from negative cells. All measurements were performed in biological duplicates.

Experimental therapy of CDX and PDX-bearing mice

All PDX and PDCL models used in this study (See Supplemental Table S1) were derived in our laboratory from patients with relapsed or refractory MCL, and DLBCL as part of other research projects as previously described26,27. The whole exome sequencing (WES) analysis confirmed that all newly derived PDX models (with available primary patient tumor DNA) shared majority of somatic mutations with the primary lymphoma cells, from which they were derived (Supplemental Fig. S1). The WES data can be downloaded from Zenodo repository: 10.5281/zenodo.15609254. PDX models were established as part of other research projects approved by the Ethics Committee of the General University Hospital Prague under numbers 48/18, and 60/20, as well as by the Ministry of Agriculture of the Czech Republic under numbers MSMT-21527/2017–8, and MSMT-26147/2022–5. The experimental design of this study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee under number 708/20 and subsequently by the Ministry of Agriculture of the Czech Republic under number MSMT-37332/2019-3. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Immunodeficient NOD.Cg-Prkdcscid Il2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ mice (commonly referred to as NSG mice) were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, Maine, USA) and kept at the Center for Experimental Biomodels of the First Faculty of Medicine, Charles University, Prague, Czech Republic. All animals were bred in a pathogen-free environment in ventilated cages and were provided with sterile food and water. Briefly, NSG mice aged 6–12 weeks were subcutaneously (SC) inoculated with 5–10 million PDX cells. When all mice developed palpable tumors (= day 1, D1) the mice were stratified so that each treatment cohort contained mice with comparable tumor size and therapy was initiated (= day 1, D1). POLA was applied intra-peritoneally (i.p.) at the dose of 5 mg/kg in PBS (total volume 200 µL) on days D1 and D8. VEN was given by oral gavage at the dose of 100 mg/kg once daily on days D1-D4, and D8-D11. Tumor growth was measured daily in millimeters in three perpendicular dimensions (length, width, height) with a digital caliper. Tumor volumes were calculated using the standard formula for the calculation of ellipsoid volume from 3 perpendicular dimensions: π / 6 × length × width × height. When the tumor size in any diameter reached 20 mm, the experiment was terminated and mice euthanized. Tumors were excised and passed through a cell nylon strainer (Falcon, 40µm) for translational experiments.

Whole transcriptome analysis of CDX and PDX tumors before therapy and after failure of POLA-based (re)treatments

Total messenger RNA (mRNA) was isolated from CD45-sorted CDX (MINO, UPF1H) and PDX tumors (VFN-M1, VFN-M5R1, VFN-M8, VFN-M11, VFN-M12) obtained from untreated mice, and from mice after failure of serial re-treatments with single-agent POLA. Total mRNA was magnetically isolated, 300–400 bp fragments were selected and amplified; resulting cDNA library was sequenced on Illumina platform with PE150 mode.

Reads were mapped to hg38 using HISAT2. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2, using a model based on the negative binomial distribution; p-values were adjusted by the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Genes with adjusted p < 0.01 and |log2 fold-change|≥ log2(1.5) were defined as differentially expressed. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed using KOBAS against the KEGG database28–30. For the cross-model consensus DEG signature, we additionally performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of KEGG pathways using all expressed genes pre-ranked by log2 fold-change (POLA-R vs. CTRL), retaining pathways with p < 0.01, FDR < 0.25, |NES|> 1, and ≥ 10 core-enrichment genes. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment was performed using gseGO (clusterProfiler, R) across Biological Process, Molecular Function, and Cellular Component ontologies on the full pre-ranked gene list. Gene sets were restricted to 15–500 members; terms with Benjamini–Hochberg-adjusted p ≥ 0.05 were excluded. Redundant terms were collapsed by semantic similarity (cutoff = 0.5). Druggable targets among the 284 genes upregulated in POLA-R were identified by querying the Drug-Gene Interaction Database (DGIdb) via its GraphQL API. Interactions were retained if classified as inhibitory by directionality or interaction type (inhibitor, antagonist, blocker, or suppressor). Genes with at least one approved inhibitory drug were subsequently reviewed for biological plausibility; five entries (ATF3, ARL2, LAMC1, COPA, F2R) were excluded as annotation artifacts where the DGIdb-listed approved drugs showed no mechanistic relevance to the queried gene.

In vivo, single-agent POLA was tested on a panel of 8 CDXs of MCL, 10 PDXs of MCL, and 6 PDXs of BCL2-positive DLBCL. The primary endpoint was event-free survival (EFS) defined as an interval (in days) from the treatment initiation until the time when a calculated tumor volume of the subcutaneously engrafted lymphoma reached ≥ 1.000 mm3 or until the time when the mouse had to be euthanized due to wasting or generalized inability to thrive due to systemic lymphoma spread. Median EFSs (mEFSs) were calculated for each treatment cohort for each tested model. In vivo experiments were terminated at the latest after 6 months (183 days) from the initiation of therapy (end-of-treatment, EoT).

Excellent single-agent efficacy of POLA was observed in several tested PDX models including VFN-M5R1, VFN-M8, VFN-M12, and VFN-D5 (Fig. 1, Supplemental Fig. S2). In these models, single-agent POLA led to permanent disappearance of the majority engrafted MCL or DLBCL tumors until EoT. Resistance to POLA (defined as the failure of POLA to prolong mEFS at least twofold compared to mEFS of the untreated controls) was observed in REC-1 and MAVER-1 CDX, and VFN-M3 PDX models (Table 2A).

Cell surface density of CD79B does not correlate with sensitivity to POLA

Cell surface density of CD79B molecules in individual cell lines (grown in vitro), and CDX, and PDX tumors (analyzed ex vivo) was determined by flow cytometry (BD QuantiBrite Fluorescence Quantification Kit). The cell surface density of CD79B in the tested cell lines did not significantly correlate with LD50 or IC50 after exposure to POLA (Fig. 2A–C). Likewise, the cell surface density of CD79B in the tested CDX, and PDX tumors did not correlate with median event-free survival (mEFS) of the respective PDX or CDX bearing mice treated with single-agent POLA (Fig. 2D–G).

In addition, the cell surface density of CD79B was further analyzed in most PDX and CDX models after failure of POLA-based monotherapy. Tumors, that regrew after failure of POLA were re-engrafted into secondary mice and subjected to retreatment with POLA. After failure of POLA retreatment, the cell surface density of CD79B was analyzed again. In all tested models, progressive shortening of EFS was observed with serial retreatments. Downregulation of CD79B was observed in approximately half of the tested models (Supplemental Fig. S3).

Combination of POLA and VEN is synthetically lethal in vivo

Several studies demonstrated that anti-mitotic agents including the POLA warhead MMAE trigger downregulation of the short-lived anti-apoptotic protein MCL123,31,32. The combination of POLA and VEN was recently tested in a phase Ib clinical trial (NCT02611323) of heavily pre-treated patients with FL and DLBCL23.

In our study, the potential of the POLA and VEN combination was first tested in vitro, on a panel of 8 MCL cell lines. In most cases, a synergistic effect of the tested combination was observed (Supplemental Table S2). Most importantly, the efficacy of POLA and VEN combination was confirmed in vivo, using 2 CDX and 8 PDX models of MCL (as well as 2 PDX models of BCL2-positive DLBCL). In majority of the tested models, the POLA and VEN combination prolonged mEFS compared to the respective monotherapies (Fig. 3, Table 2B, Supplemental Fig. S2). However, the benefit of the experimental combination was observed almost exclusively in the models responsive to single-agent VEN (i.e., HBL2, UPF1H, VFN-M1, VFN-M3, VFN-M5R1, VFN-M20, VFN-D22), while the venetoclax-resistant models (VFN-M8, VFN-M11) did not profit from the treatment combination (except for VFN-M16).

Transcriptome changes associated with acquired POLA resistance

After failure of POLA, CD79B downregulation was observed inconsistently, and only in several of the tested CDX and PDX models strongly suggesting additional mechanisms of acquired resistance. Bulk transcriptome analysis of paired tumor samples obtained from POLA-R compared to CTRL MCL tumors (in case of MINO, UPF1H, VFN-M1, VFN-M5R1, VFN-M8, VFN-M11, and VFN-M12) showed differential gene expression compared to controls. RNA sequencing identified varying numbers of differentially expressed genes across models. VFN-M1 had 3218 downregulated and 3284 upregulated genes, VFN-M5R1 791 downregulated and 597 upregulated genes, VFN-M8 1148 downregulated and 1319 upregulated genes, VFN-M11 150 downregulated and 240 upregulated genes, VFN-M12 643 downregulated and 187 upregulated genes, UPF1H 3378 downregulated and 3148 upregulated genes, and MINO 275 downregulated and 279 upregulated genes (Supplemental Table S3). When analyzed collectively, the models shared 523 downregulated and 284 upregulated genes across all POLA-R samples (Fig. 4A; Supplemental Table S3B). A conserved transcriptome signature of POLA resistance was revealed across all analyzed MCL models (Supplemental Fig. S5A).

Among the most significantly upregulated protein-coding transcripts in the POLA-R compared to CTRL tumors were exportin 5 (XPO5, FC = 1.65, p = 4.2E-13), ETS Variant Transcription Factor 4 (ETV4, FC = 17.5, p = 8.1E-10), ETV5 (FC = 9, p = 7.6E-07), Gem Nuclear Organelle Associated Protein 5 (GEMIN5, FC = 1.65, p = 5.4E10-9), Pleckstrin Homology Like Domain Family A Member 1 (PHLDA1, FC = 29.1, p = 1.0E-07), PR/SET Domain 1 (PRDM1/BLIMP1, FC = 10.6, p = 1.4E-08), Ras Protein Specific Guanine Nucleotide Releasing Factor 1 (RASGRF1, FC = 4.7, p = 9.5E-07), Ras Related Protein RAB31 (FC = 26.8, p = 4.2E-04), Cadherin Related Family Member 3 (CDHR3, FC = 10.6, p = 1.8E-04), cyclin D2 (CCND2, FC = 6.7, p = 9.0E-05), caveoline 1 (CAV1, FC = 5.5, p = 6.2E-03). Of note, BCL2 gene was also significantly upregulated across the POLA-R tumors (FC 2.65, p = 2.5E-03) (Table 2B).

Among the most significantly downregulated genes in the POLA-R compared to CTRL tumors were transcription factors FOSB (FC = 0.09, p = 8.5E-08), JUNB (FC = 0.38, p = 3.4E-06), and JUND (FC = 0.48, p = 1.2E-09), Immediate Early Response 2 (IER2, FC = 0.28, p = 1.0E-08), Sin3A Associated Protein 25 (SAP25, FC = 0.27, p = 7.1E-09), transmembrane proteins TMEM91 (FC = 0.3, p = 1.4E-07), TMEM259 (FC = 0.5, p = 8.3E-07), TMEM86B (FC = 0.43, p = 3.5E-06), and TMEM256 (FC = 0.39, p = 2.6E-05), Protein Phosphatase 1 Regulatory Subunit 37 (PPP1R37, FC = 0.55, p = 3.5E-08), Trafficking Protein Particle Complex Subunit 6A (TRAPPC5, FC = 0.36, p = 3.9E-07), Suppressor Of Tumorigenicity 20 (ST20, FC = 0.43, p = 6.4E-07), Cytochrome B-245 Alpha Chain (CABY, FC = 0.39, p = 3.6E-07), Mitochondrially Encoded NADH:Ubiquinone Oxidoreductase Core Subunits 6 (MT-ND6, FC = 0.36, p = 1.2E-06), MT-ND3 (FC = 0.42, p = 2.4E-06), or RAD52 Homolog, DNA Repair Protein (RAD52, FC = 0.58, p = 1.3E-05) (Supplemental Table S3B).

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed several upregulated pathways including N-glycan biosynthesis, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, JAK-STAT signaling, or PI3K-AKT signaling. Downregulated pathways associated with POLA-R comprised oxidative phosphorylation, ribosome, or thermogenesis (Fig. 4C, Supplemental Table S3C). Consistent with the KEGG GSEA finding, expression of the 53 OXPHOS leading-edge genes was uniformly reduced across all seven models in POLA-R compared to CTRL conditions, confirming mitochondrial respiratory chain suppression as a conserved feature of acquired resistance (Fig. 4A, B).

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis confirmed and extended the KEGG GSEA findings (Supplemental Fig. S5B–D; Supplemental Tables S3D–2F). In the downregulated arm, the most significantly enriched GO terms across all three ontologies converged on mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation—with proton motive force-driven ATP synthesis (NES = − 2.49, FDR = 6.8E-09) as the top Biological Process term and respiratory chain complex (NES = − 2.62, FDR = 2.1E-12) as the top Cellular Component term. Additional suppressed programs included ribosome biogenesis, purine and nucleotide biosynthesis, and chromatin organization. In the upregulated arm, the most enriched Biological Process terms were positive regulation of cell differentiation (NES = + 1.65, FDR = 6.1E-05) and lymphocyte differentiation. The top Cellular Component terms were plasma membrane raft (NES = + 2.06, FDR = 2.5E-04) and caveola (NES = + 2.04). Cytokine-mediated signaling, MAPK cascade, and regulation of autophagy were also significantly upregulated.

To identify pharmacologically actionable targets within the 284 upregulated genes, we queried the Drug-Gene Interaction Database (DGIdb), identifying 45 genes with documented inhibitory drug interactions, of which 23 had at least one approved inhibitory drug. Five were excluded as annotation artifacts, yielding 18 genes with biologically plausible approved inhibitory interactions (Supplemental Fig. S5E; Supplemental Table S3G). The most prominent hits included PRDM1/BLIMP-1 (DGIdb score 1.49) and MALT1 (DGIdb interaction score 17.4). Additional kinase targets included PIK3CB (PI3Kβ; approved inhibitors idelalisib, copanlisib) and ABL2 (approved inhibitors dasatinib, imatinib).

Discussion

To date, phase II proof-of-concept studies have been conducted exclusively in human subjects. Several studies have demonstrated that patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models established from patients with non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) retain the majority of somatic mutations and copy number variations present in the corresponding primary lymphoma samples26,33. In the present study, we therefore conducted a murine, phase II–like proof-of-concept efficacy study evaluating polatuzumab vedotin (POLA), administered as a single agent or in combination with venetoclax (VEN), using a panel of 8 CDX models of MCL and 16 PDX models derived from patients with relapsed/refractory (R/R) MCL (n = 10) and R/R DLBCL (n = 6). Median event-free survival (mEFS) was employed as a surrogate endpoint for progression-free survival (PFS) in human studies34.

As expected, in vitro sensitivity of the tested MCL and DLBCL cell lines to POLA correlated with their sensitivity to monomethyl auristatin E (MMAE) in the majority of cases. In vivo, we demonstrated robust single-agent activity of POLA in R/R MCL and confirmed its efficacy in R/R DLBCL. Consistent with previous reports, among CD79B-expressing models, the level of CD79B expression was not predictive of either in vitro sensitivity to POLA or in vivo efficacy of single-agent POLA35,36. In contrast, complete absence of CD79B expression, as observed in MAVER-1 and VFN-M15 models, was associated with POLA resistance, which is also in concordance with so far published data37.

CD79B expression exhibited marked variability following serial POLA-based re-treatments. In several, but not all, models, reduced POLA efficacy (reflected by shortened mEFS) correlated with decreased CD79B expression (e.g., VFN-M5R1, VFN-M8, VFN-M11, and VFN-D5). These observations strongly suggest that mechanisms beyond CD79B downregulation contribute to acquired POLA resistance. Potential mechanisms may include reduced accessibility of the CD79B epitope through N-linked glycosylation of CD79B38. Other plausible mechanisms may comprise impaired intracellular trafficking and endocytosis, dysregulation of apoptotic pathways, or alterations in DNA damage response signaling. Indeed, POLA-resistant tumors displayed heterogeneous degrees of transcriptional reprogramming. The VFN-M1 and UPF1H models exhibited the most extensive gene expression changes (3218/3284 and 3378/3148 downregulated/upregulated genes, respectively), whereas the VFN-M11 and MINO models showed more limited transcriptional alterations (150/240 and 275/279), potentially reflecting alternative resistance mechanisms less dependent on global transcriptional shifts. Importantly, a conserved gene expression signature shared across all seven analyzed models—comprising 523 downregulated and 284 upregulated genes—likely represents core pathways associated with POLA resistance and reveals potential therapeutic vulnerabilities.

Uniform upregulation of BCL2 mRNA across all POLA-resistant tumors suggested a contributory role of BCL2 in mediating resistance and provided a strong molecular rationale for combining POLA with VEN in this study, in addition to the anticipated MMAE-induced downregulation of the short-lived anti-apoptotic protein MCL1.

Gene-set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of KEGG pathways identified several deregulated pathways in POLA-R tumors, including N-glycan biosynthesis (upregulated), and oxidative phosphorylation (downregulated). As mentioned above, Corcoran et al. demonstrated that glycosylation, rather than just the total among of CD79B, is a primary driver of response to POLA38. Venetoclax induces apoptosis not only through its canonical BCL2 inhibition but also by targeting mitochondrial respiration39. We may thus speculate that the observed synergy between POLA and VEN can be at least partially mediated by venetoclax-triggered non-apoptotic pathways (e.g., oxidative phosphorylation). Beyond BCL2, GO enrichment and DGIdb analyses of the conserved resistance signature revealed two additional axes of therapeutically actionable resistance. First, upregulation of PRDM1/BLIMP-1, the master transcription factor of terminal B-cell differentiation, is mechanistically linked to progressive CD79B downregulation, as PRDM1-driven plasmacytic differentiation ablates surface BCR expression40. Second, upregulation of MALT1, the catalytic component of the CARD11–BCL10–MALT1 (CBM) complex suggests NF-κB signaling as a potential escape route in POLA-R tumors. Additional druggable kinase targets within this axis include PIK3CB (PI3Kβ) and ABL2, consistent with the PI3K–AKT and JAK–STAT pathway enrichment identified by KEGG GSEA. Overexpression of Caveolin 1 (CAV1) has been repeatedly associated with increased aggressiveness and therapy resistance in many types of cancer41. Finally, enrichment of autophagy regulation among upregulated genes suggests that autophagic flux may provide an additional survival buffer by clearing MMAE-damaged organelles, complementing BCL2-mediated apoptotic resistance42.

Clinically, POLA is administered as part of combination regimens, including POLA-R-CHP and POLA-BR35,43,44. In combination with the bispecific antibody mosunetuzumab, POLA has demonstrated promising antitumor activity in patients with MCL after failure of Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitors45. Multiple studies have shown that anti-mitotic agents, including POLA, induce downregulation of the short-lived anti-apoptotic protein MCL123,31,32. Lasater et al. recently reported results of a phase Ib clinical trial (NCT02611323) in heavily pretreated patients with relapsed/refractory follicular lymphoma (FL) and DLBCL treated with POLA, VEN, and rituximab23. In that study, 25 of 33 patients (76%) with FL and 5 of 17 patients (29%) with DLBCL achieved complete or partial responses with an acceptable safety profile23.

Building on our prior work and that of others demonstrating strong antitumor synergy between MCL1 and BCL2 inhibition, we evaluated POLA as an indirect MCL1-targeting agent in combination with VEN in R/R MCL46–49. In the majority of tested MCL CDX and PDX models, the POLA–VEN combination resulted in significantly superior antitumor efficacy, as evidenced by prolonged mEFS, compared with either agent alone, without observable toxicity (including weight loss, diarrhea, or impaired general condition). In several models, clear synthetic lethality was observed between POLA and VEN, including VFN-D22, a PDX model derived from a patient with R/R DLBCL previously treated with POLA-BR. Notably, the patient achieved a partial remission following POLA-BR but relapsed within two months of the final treatment cycle. In our translational study, 4 of 6 mice bearing VFN-D22 PDX tumors and treated with the POLA–VEN combination remained free of measurable lymphoma until the end of the experiment, six months after treatment initiation.

Although beyond the scope of the present study, these findings suggest that further intensification of the POLA–VEN backbone—through the addition of agents such as the anti-CD20 antibody obinutuzumab, the non-covalent BTK inhibitor pirtobrutinib, or the bispecific antibody mosunetuzumab—may yield even greater therapeutic benefit in R/R MCL and DLBCL50.
