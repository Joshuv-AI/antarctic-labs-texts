ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic subdural hematoma (CSDH) is among the most common neurosurgical conditions, yet management strategies vary worldwide despite an expanding evidence base. To harmonize clinical practice and facilitate the establishment of transnational recommendations, the 2024 joint iCORIC/DACSUHS symposium in Copenhagen included a Delphi process on the key aspects of CSDH management.MethodsA modified Delphi process was conducted among 32 participants from 14 countries, including neurosurgeons, neurointerventionalists, and CSDH researchers. Eighteen topics were assessed across three survey rounds, based on the revised Danish national CSDH guidelines. Consensus was defined as ≥ 75% agreement and strong consensus as ≥ 95%.ResultsResponse rates were 84.3%, 100%, and 59.3% across the three rounds. Unanimous or strong consensus was achieved for several core practices: surgical evacuation for hematomas with significant mass effect, conservative management for mild cases, use of burr hole or twist-drill craniostomy as primary treatment, and postoperative drainage after evacuation. Consensus also supported irrigation with warm (37C°) Ringer’s lactate, bilateral evacuation for large bilateral hematomas, and early postoperative mobilization. Areas lacking consensus included management of antithrombotic therapy, drain duration beyond 24 h, the role of routine postoperative Computed Tomography (CT) in asymptomatic patients, and the adoption of eMMA as standard or preventive treatment.ConclusionsThe 2024 iCORIC/DACSUHS Delphi consensus process identified broad international agreement on several aspects of CSDH management while exposing persisting disagreement in others including antithrombotic reversal, imaging protocols, and embolization strategies. Knowledge of the common understanding of agreement and disagreement within CSDH treatment form a platform for future multinational collaboration and highlight priorities for further clinical research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00701-026-06854-y.

Introduction

Chronic Subdural Hematoma (CSDH) is one of the most frequent neurosurgical conditions, due to a growing, aging population and an increasing use of blood thinning agents [26, 98]. Several high-level publications address the various aspects of CSDH management [19]. Despite its high incidence and though robust clinical studies have been published, only few countries have standardized treatment, and variation persists both globally and locally [11, 12, 36, 61]. The variability in treatment regimens include differences in surgical approaches, postoperative drain placement and duration, as well as the use of pharmacological treatments and embolization strategies. Still, far from all aspects of CSDH management are thoroughly investigated [20].

Both national and international initiatives seek to standardize CSDH management for better patient outcome. This includes the Danish Chronic Subdural Hematoma Study (DACSUHS) [31, 32, 34, 48, 49, 51] and the international collaborative research initiative on chronic subdural hematoma (iCORIC) [19, 37, 52].

The 2024 joint iCORIC/DACSUHS CSDH symposium in Copenhagen, Denmark, aimed to address these variabilities in the CSDH management through a two-day symposium on several aspects of CSDH including the history of CSDH treatment, pathophysiology, anatomy, surgical approach alongside adjuvant treatments (supplementary Table 1).

iCORIC & DACSUHS

iCORIC is an international research group established at the European Association of Neurosurgical Societies (EANS) in Brussels in 2018, now including participants from 30 countries, who aims to generate transnational evidence-based guidelines for managing CSDH and establish consensus on definitions and standard data sets for CSDH research [41].

DACSUHS is a collaboration between all neurosurgical departments in Denmark, founded in 2012, aiming to establish national CSDH treatment guidelines, and to conduct, coordinate and lead CSDH research at a national level [69].

Methods

Symposium overview

The 2024 symposium was organized by iCORIC and DACSUHS from May 16th till May 17th, 2024. The 32 participants included neurosurgeons, CSDH researchers, and interventional radiologists from 14 countries.

Modified delphi process

A structured Delphi survey was employed to assess consensus between the participants at the symposium on CSDH management. The process consisted of three rounds of questionnaires addressing 18 topics in CSDH management. Sixteen of these topics were derived from the revised 10th? national CSDH guidelines created by DACSUHS [69], and two topics on embolization of middle meningeal artery (eMMA), as this topic gains increasing scientific and clinical interest.

Defining consensus

A universal definition of consensus has not been established. Though, in the development of medical guidelines, a definition of consensus is often achieved when 75% of participants reach a common answer [70]. A threshold of 95% agreement among participants may be used to indicate strong consensus.

This survey posed 18 topics, where participants could answer “strongly agree”, “agree”, “neither agree nor disagree”, “disagree” or “strongly disagree”.

In this modified Delphi approach, responses that fell under "strongly agree" or "agree" were combined, and consensus was reached if their sum surpassed 75%. Strong consensus was identified at a sum of 95% or higher, while unanimous agreement was reserved for 100% strong agreement.

Lectures were held at the symposium on the current management and pathophysiology of CSDH. Afterwards, participants reviewed the evidence and engaged in discussions facilitated by the consultant course organizers.

Percentage agreement was calculated by summing the number of responses marked “Strongly agree” and “Agree”, as well as “Disagree” and “Strongly disagree”. For the purposes of quantifying consensus, “Strongly agree” and “Agree” were given equal weight. The same approach was applied to “Disagree” and “Strongly disagree”. This ensured a representative and consistent method of measuring agreement levels. Furthermore, a sum of 100% between “Strongly agree” and “Agree” was considered as unanimous consensus.

Indication for surgery

1.1 It is recommended to operate a CSDH when it causes significant mass effect posing an immediate threat to life or mobility, with or without neurological symptoms and signs.

1.1 It is recommended to operate a CSDH when it causes significant mass effect posing an immediate threat to life or mobility, with or without neurological symptoms and signs.

1.1 It is recommended to operate a CSDH when it causes significant mass effect posing an immediate threat to life or mobility, with or without neurological symptoms and signs.

When considering surgery vs conservative treatment for CSDH, as a rule, most patients are treated with surgical drainage. However, it is generally known that CSDH can regress spontaneously without the need for surgical intervention. Two surveys of neurosurgeons in the United Kingdom and Ireland, and Austria, Germany and Switzerland have shown that conservative treatment is rarely practiced [11, 77].

A prospective study including 43 patients, of whom 16 received surgery and 27 were treated conservatively, showed that patients without midline shift did well without surgery. However, the six-month mortality for the entire cohort was about 31% [25]. In a small study including 24 patients, 5 patients were treated conservatively, as they did not have symptoms of increased intracranial pressure [27]. These patients had improvement in clinical symptoms within 7–10 days. The article concludes that conservative treatment can be attempted if there are no clinical signs of increased intracranial pressure and no focal neurological deficits. If there is no improvement within 7–10 days, these patients should be offered surgical treatment [27]. However, high-quality evidence in this area is lacking.

Consensus

By round 3, unanimous consensus was reached by 100% responding “strongly agree” supporting surgical treatment of a CSDH with significant mass effect. No studies investigate the effect of conservative versus surgical treatment of CSDH. Various reviews reserve conservative treatment for asymptomatic patients with small hematomas. It is described that patients receiving conservative treatment must have no radiological signs of increased intracranial pressure in the form of brain compression or midline shift. A generally accepted cut-off value for surgery for CSDH is hematoma thickness > 1 cm, and or midline shift > 5 mm [88, 90].

In the past surgeons tended to favor conservative treatment to the elderly (> 90 years) primarily due to the associated risk of mortality. Yet, 3 small, presumable underpowered observational studies found no increase in mortality rate among the elderly and age and should therefore not be a stand-alone factor when considering surgical intervention against conservative treatment [53, 62, 93].

Consensus

By round 3, unanimous consensus was reached with 83% responding “Strongly agree”, 17% responding “Agree”, supporting conservative treatment in mild cases with the possibility to convert to surgical intervention if symptoms worsen. The degree of consensus increased with every round is shown in Fig. 1.

Antithrombotic treatment

2. It is recommended to revert antithrombotic treatment prior to acute surgery for CSDH.

2. It is recommended to revert antithrombotic treatment prior to acute surgery for CSDH.

2. It is recommended to revert antithrombotic treatment prior to acute surgery for CSDH.

Antithrombotic medication increases the risk of CSDH and a large percentage of patients with CSDH are treated with antithrombotic medication [8]. It is therefore an important question whether this treatment should be reversed prior to an operation. No scientific literature has been found regarding the reversal of antithrombotic treatment prior to both craniostomy and craniotomy surgery for CSDH. There is good evidence for perioperative regulation of antithrombotic treatment for procedures other than treatment of CSDH [1]. The surgical treatment of CSDH is a high-risk procedure and the guidelines are clear and evidence-based regarding the reversal of antithrombotic treatment for other high-risk surgery [23]. Reverting antithrombotic treatment prior to acute surgery for CSDH may be necessary to reduce the risk of bleeding, improve surgical conditions, and prevent complications such as hematoma recurrence or rebleeding. Optimal hemostasis during recovery and an arguably improved prognosis are additional benefits [27, 81].

Contrarily, reversal of antithrombotic treatment may increase the risk of thrombosis, particularly in older CSDH patients [63]. Balancing these risks requires careful assessment and customization, which adds complexity to decision-making. Notably, some studies suggest that antithrombotic use may not significantly impact recurrence risks [73]. In a recent study published after the symposium with data from two randomized Danish CSDH trials, resuming treatment with antiplatelets, vitamin K-antagonists and direct oral anticoagulants after a median of 8, 4 and 7 days was not associated with increased risk of recurrence for neither of the blood thinners [82]. A recently published RCT by Kamenova et al. also found that discontinuation of acetylsalicylic acid did not reduce the recurrence risk within 6 months [54].

Consensus

Regarding the management of antithrombotic treatment no consensus was reached. While 16.67% strongly agreed and 50% agreed, a notable proportion (33.33%) either remained neutral or disagreed.

Choice of surgical approach

3.1 It is recommended to use burr hole(s) or twist drill craniostomy as primary treatment for CSDH.

3.1 It is recommended to use burr hole(s) or twist drill craniostomy as primary treatment for CSDH.

3.1 It is recommended to use burr hole(s) or twist drill craniostomy as primary treatment for CSDH.

Burr hole is generally considered the primary treatment for CSDH. However, it also carries with it a relatively higher recurrence rate, though with lower morbidity compared to craniotomy [51, 71].

A single drill hole is used. Twist-drill craniostomy is a smaller drill hole with a drainage screw in the skull. It can be performed “bedside” and is therefore considered more suitable for frail and/or elderly patients with multiple co-morbidities.

The evidence on this topic includes one meta-analysis of 34,829 patients in 250 studies, of which 16 were RCTs and 234 were observational studies in the period 1970–2013. Burr hole and twist-drill drainage were assessed as equivalent in terms of morbidity, mortality and recurrence rate. Craniotomy was found to be superior in terms of reducing recurrence rate, but was associated with a higher complication rate [6]. Another meta-analysis includes 16 studies, of which 4 were randomized, and a total of 1,235 patients, where burr hole and twist-drill are compared in terms of outcome. Again, no difference in outcome or complications was found between the two treatments [99]. Lastly, a RCT not included in the previous meta-analysis found numerical lower reoperation rates of 7.6%, 13.1% and 19.5% between CSDH patients operated with burr hole, craniotomy and twist-drill craniotomy (p = 0.07) [18].

Consensus

By round 3, unanimous consensus was reached on burr hole craniostomy with 66,6% responding “Strongly agree” and 33,3% responding “Agree”, as the preferred primary treatment (Fig. 1).

3.2 eMMA can be used in selected cases of recurrent CSDH.

3.2 eMMA can be used in selected cases of recurrent CSDH.

3.2 eMMA can be used in selected cases of recurrent CSDH.

Several studies indicate that the blood supply to a CSDH comes from the middle meningeal artery (MMA) [57]. It is therefore reasonable to expect that occlusion of the MMA can reduce the blood supply to a CSDH and possibly improve the prognosis including a reduces risk of recurrence. Three RCTs and were unpublished at the time of the joint iCORIC/DACSUHS symposium in 2024 but have recently been published [16, 21, 64]. Therefore, important evidence on this topic has been published after this question was answered by the participants.

Consensus

By round 3, consensus was reached to support eMMA with 26,3% responding “Strong agree” and 63,2% responding “Agree” for selected cases of recurrent CSDH (Fig. 1).

Postoperative drainage

4.1 It is recommended to use drain after burr hole evacuation of CSDH.

4.1 It is recommended to use drain after burr hole evacuation of CSDH.

4.1 It is recommended to use drain after burr hole evacuation of CSDH.

Using a drain after burr hole evacuation for CSDH facilitates the drainage of residual blood and fluids, which in several studies have shown to reduce the risk of recurrence mortality [4, 29, 49, 78]. The benefits of postoperative drainage originally shown by Santarius T et al. has been underlined in a long-term analysis of the patient from the original study regarding drain placement [30].

Consensus

By round 3, unanimous consensus was reached, with 88.9% responding “Strongly agree” and 11.1% responding “Agree”, supporting the use of a drain as an essential step in CSDH management following burr hole evacuation (Fig. 1).

4.2 A subdural or subperiosteal drain position can be used.

4.2 A subdural or subperiosteal drain position can be used.

4.2 A subdural or subperiosteal drain position can be used.

The original study on drain placement from Santarius T et al. used a subdural drain position [78]. One RCT investigating drain location did not find a different on recurrence rate between subdural and subperiosteal drain placement, but significant more hemorrhages through misplaced drains and infections was seen in patients with subdural drains [89]. It is argued that this should lead to the use of subperiosteal drains [55]. The difference in drain types and drain techniques including softness of the drain and an atraumatic insertion technique may vary between countries and making comparison between countries difficult [49]. With the current knowledge both subdural and subperiosteal drain positions may be viable options for managing CSDH [17].

Consensus

By round 3, consensus was reached with 44.4% responding “Strongly agree” and 44.4% responding “Agree”. The degree of consensus increased with every round (Fig. 1).

4.3 It is recommended not to leave the drain in place longer than 24 h.

4.3 It is recommended not to leave the drain in place longer than 24 h.

4.3 It is recommended not to leave the drain in place longer than 24 h.

The influence of drainage duration on the risk of recurrence has been investigated in a small RCT with 65 patients comparing 48 versus 96 h of drainage without a significant difference in the recurrence rate, but with increased postoperative complications in the 96-h group [40]. The DACSUHS group has published two RCTs (DRAIN TIME 1 and 2) investigating, firstly, 24 versus 48 h of subdural drainage on the risk of recurrent CSDH, and, secondly, 6 versus 12 versus 24 h of drainage [34, 50]. These studies find a similar recurrence rate with 48 h of drainage compared to 24 h with prolonged length of hospital stay, and an increased risk of recurrence when using 6 and 12 h of drainage compared to 24 h.

Consensus

By round 3, no consensus was reached. A majority of 55.6% agreed and 16.7% strongly agreed to not leave the drain in longer than 24 h (Fig. 1). This is despite high quality evidence from the DRAIN TIME 1 and 2 trials [20, 34, 48] supporting drain removal within 24 h. The reflective discussion leading to the final round of questioning revealed the disagreement relied on the specific wording of “no longer than 24 h”, as some participants argued that leaving the drain longer in cases were e.g. stadding shortage and high activity, would cause no harm to the patient and hence should not be advised against. A more favorable statement such as “it is recommended to leave the drain in 24 h” was suggested.

Irrigation

5. It is recommended to flush with 37° C Ringer’s lactate solution during evacuation of CSDH.

5. It is recommended to flush with 37° C Ringer’s lactate solution during evacuation of CSDH.

5. It is recommended to flush with 37° C Ringer’s lactate solution during evacuation of CSDH.

Several studies of varying quality have addressed this question [6, 42, 45, 65]. The results are relatively similar with the number of reoperations not being statistically different between irrigation and non-irrigation, though with one study showing better outcome for the irrigation group [45]. However, recently a Finnish non-inferiority RCT compared irrigation to no-irrigation and could not conclude a non-inferiority of burr hole drainage without irrigation. The recurrence rate was 6% higher after subdural drainage without irrigation favoring irrigation [75]. Also, a large Swedish study found that irrigation at body temperature was superior to irrigation at room temperature concerning fewer recurrences [10].

Consensus

By round 3, a unanimous consensus with 55.6% responding “Strongly agree” and 44.4% responding “Agree” was reached (Fig. 1). There was a strong consensus throughout the three rounds.

Bilateral CSDH

6. It is recommended to evacuate hematoma on both sides in larger bilateral CSDH.

6. It is recommended to evacuate hematoma on both sides in larger bilateral CSDH.

6. It is recommended to evacuate hematoma on both sides in larger bilateral CSDH.

One study directly addresses the question of surgery on one or both sides. In a national, retrospective study, it was found that the recurrence rate was twice as high with unilateral as with bilateral surgery for bilateral CSDH, while the complication rate was not significantly different for the two procedures [7]. Bilateral surgery can be performed in one procedure with the same risk of complications as with unilateral surgery. The burden on the patient and the health economy is therefore not significantly different between the two procedures, while the relatively high risk of recurrence is halved with bilateral surgery compared to unilateral.

Consensus

By round 3, unanimous consensus with 44.4% responding “Strongly agree” and 55.6% responding “Agree” was reached (Fig. 1). There was a strong consensus throughout the three rounds.

Pharmacological treatment

7. Adjuvant pharmacological treatment should not be part of the treatment of CSDH.

7. Adjuvant pharmacological treatment should not be part of the treatment of CSDH.

7. Adjuvant pharmacological treatment should not be part of the treatment of CSDH.

Adjuvant pharmacological treatment, such as systemic glucocorticoids, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACE-II-i) or statins, is generally not recommended for the treatment of CSDH [20]. The use of such agents has been proposed due to their anti-inflammatory properties – key processes in the pathophysiology of CSDH.

While several studies report lower recurrence rates following adjuvant use of glucocorticoids, there were no significant differences in favorable neurological outcome or mortality [38, 86, 87, 94, 101, 103] except for one meta-analysis reporting on higher mortality rates in the dexamethasone group [103]. Conversely, use of systemic glucocorticoids was associated with significant adverse effects such as hyperglycemia, sleep disturbances, onset of diabetes mellitus, psychiatric symptoms, gastro-intestinal bleeding, In a single multicenter retrospective study, use of ACE-II had no effect on recurrence rate [97]. Regarding the use of statins, one systematic review found limited evidence suggesting beneficial properties and a recent meta-analysis found no reduction in recurrence rate [67, 74]. A systematic review found that atorvastatin both adjuvant to surgery and as monotherapy significantly reduced recurrence rates and increased favorable clinical outcome. However, the included studies were small and designed retrospective [74].

Lastly, one meta-analysis and systematic review and two RCT’s regarding herbal medicine report no effect on recurrence rate, surgical complications or hematoma size, while a single retrospective study has implied a possible effect on reducing hematoma size [25, 33, 60, 100].

Consensus

By round 3, consensus was reached with 22.2% responding “Strongly agree” and 55.6% responding “Agree”. The degree of consensus increased with every round (Fig. 1). A notable minority (16.7% neutral and 5.6% disagreeing) in favor of adjuvant treatment reflects ongoing debate in this area. Notable, initially no consensus was found by round 1, but consensus was reached at the third round.

Postoperative mobilization

8. It is recommended to offer elevation of the head rest and early mobilisation to patients after evacuation of CSDH.

8. It is recommended to offer elevation of the head rest and early mobilisation to patients after evacuation of CSDH.

8. It is recommended to offer elevation of the head rest and early mobilisation to patients after evacuation of CSDH.

Early mobilization is generally recommended in surgery for improved postoperative recovery to prevent cardiovascular events or medical complications [3, 5, 15, 56, 76]. Both a recent RCT and a retrospective study showed that early mobilization after evacuation of a CSDH reduced medical complications and a 1.7-day shorter length of hospital stay for patients mobilized within 12 h. There were no differences regarding recurrence rate or neurological outcome [59, 91]. Additionally, a prospective study found that bedrest was associated with worse clinical outcome compared to early mobilization [13].

Elevating the head rest position postoperative is commonly applied and believed to enhance CSF-dynamics, venous drainage and intracerebral pressure control. A systematic review and meta-analysis on the surgical treatment of CSDH included, among other studies, three RCTs comparing elevated headrest to flat headrest [2, 44, 46, 68]. The meta-analysis found no difference in the recurrence rate and two of the RCTs assessing postoperative complications found no significant difference. One RCT found that length of stay was reduced using elevated head rest [44].

Consensus

By round 3, consensus was reached with 44.4% responding “Strongly agree” and 38.9% responding “Agree”, highlighting elevated head rest and early mobilization as a beneficial component of postoperative care and reaching consensus (Fig. 1). Initially no consensus was found by round 1, but consensus was reached after the third round.

Radiological follow-up

9.1 It is recommended to perform a head CT scan if the patient fails to recover after surgery.

9.1 It is recommended to perform a head CT scan if the patient fails to recover after surgery.

9.1 It is recommended to perform a head CT scan if the patient fails to recover after surgery.

Performing a head CT scan is recommended for patients who fail to recover after CSDH surgery. This allows for early detection of residual or recurrent hematomas, evaluation of the surgical site, and verification of drain position, which may guide the need for further intervention. Concerns include resource allocation, logistical challenges, and the cumulative radiation risk associated with repeated imaging.

Consensus

By round 3, unanimous consensus was reached with 83.3% responding “Strongly agree” and 16.7% responding “Agree”, supporting the importance of imaging in cases when postoperative recovery does not progress as expected (Fig. 1).

9.2 It is recommended that a control CT scan is not performed in asymptomatic patients.

9.2 It is recommended that a control CT scan is not performed in asymptomatic patients.

9.2 It is recommended that a control CT scan is not performed in asymptomatic patients.

Two retrospective studies of 202 and 697 CSDH patients, carried out routine control CT 4–8 weeks after primary surgery [24, 72]. In both studies, the majority of reoperated recurrent hematomas were identified when patients presented symptoms. Many of these patients underwent surgery prior to the planned CT scan. Only 2.5% and 1% of patients with reoperated CSDH recurrence in each study were detected on routine control CT. A comparative, multicenter study of 391 matched patients comparing American patients which were routinely scanned after surgery with Dutch patients with no planned controls found no significant difference in number of reoperations or neurological outcome [39]. In both cases, reoperations were carried out due to recurrent clinical symptoms. Lastly, a recent RCT comparing routine control CT scan 2 and 30 days after surgery found no significant difference in neurological outcome or mortality but reported that treatment costs were 18% higher in the group including routine CT-scan with a higher reoperation rate [83].

Consensus

By round 3, no consensus was reached by round 3 with 22.2% responding “Strongly agree” and 27.8% responding “Agree”, due to a notable minority disagreeing or remaining neutral. This indicates differing practices for asymptomatic patients (Fig. 1).

The utilization of routine CT scans in asymptomatic or conservatively managed patients remain debated, with concerns of abuse, cost, and radiation balanced against the risk missing complications. The literature describes a recurrence rate of 5–30% [79]. A cohort study focusing on recurrence rates presented superiority of craniotomy to burr hole, though one meta-analysis concluded that craniotomy was associated with higher morbidity than burr hole with a higher perioperative risks especially for the elderly CSDH population [6]. One review shows that craniotomy is reserved for recurrent operations in patients with membrane formation [58]. In a retrospective study including 193 patients, 151 patients were operated with craniotomy and 42 with a burr hole at the primary operation. Of these, 42 (27.8%) from the craniotomy group and 6 (14.3%) from the burr hole group required reoperation [66].

Consensus

By round 3, consensus was reached, with 5.6% responding “Strongly agree” and 72.2% responding “Agree” on considering craniotomy for recurrent CSDH cases (Fig. 1). This topic presented no consensus by round 1 but consensus was reached after the third round.

eMMA was not mentioned in the latest DACSUHS recommendation of 2022, but was included as a question, as this technique has gained substantial both clinical and scientific attention. Recently, several systematic reviews and meta-analyses have been published on eMMA both as an adjunct to surgery and as a stand-alone-treatment [14, 35, 43, 80, 85, 92, 96, 102]. Both coils, liquid materials, such as Onyx or n-butyl cyanoacrylate glue, and particles has been presented as embolization materials. Mostly composed of retrospective studies, the reviews report on significantly lower recurrence rates in eMMA both as stand-alone and as adjuvant and a similar mortality and complication rates. This has been concluded in a recent review on five systematic reviews with meta-analyses, however only supported by a weak level of evidence [47]. Another systematic review and meta-analysis focusing specifically on complications related to eMMA reported significantly lower complication rates when compared to surgical treatment and the risk of severe complications such as blindness due to closure of the ophthalmic artery or its collaterals were rare [84]. Yet, the earliest reviews were based mostly on retrospective studies or small RCTs. Contrary to their findings, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis of 3 major RCT (MAGIC-MT [64], STEM [22] and EMBOLISE [16]) report on no significant reduction in recurrence rate when comparing either stand-alone eMMA or adjuvant eMMA to surgery nor did it result in improved neurological outcome [28]. There was a reduction in hematoma progression in the conservatively treated group.

Consensus

By round 3, no consensus was reached with only 11.1% responding “Agree”, reflecting that eMMA is not yet widely accepted as a standard procedure in CSDH (Fig. 1).

Embolization, particularly as a standard treatment or for mild symptoms, raises questions regarding efficacy, safety, and resource implications. While promising, current evidence is insufficient to drive widespread adoption. Overall, practice variation, limited high-quality data, and differing institutional capacities likely contributed to the divergence in opinions. Future research should prioritize randomized trials focusing on these gray zones, including timing strategies, imaging protocols, and the role of adjuvant therapies such as embolization and pharmacological agents. The reflective discussion in the question of eMMA revealed concerns of resource-intensive procedures, and the potential sufficiency of less invasive and low-cost management in conservative treatment patients. Strengths and limitations

A strength of this study is the systematic approach of the Delphi Survey throughout the symposium. First, the latest research within the specific topic was presented and throughout the sessions, reflective discussions were held to encourage evidence-based decision-making. The symposium presents a strong consensus on several topics within the surgical and postoperative management of CSDH, such as operation on patients with mass effect CSDH, conservative treatment in patients with mild symptoms and drain placement after burr hole treatment, while also exposing several areas of clinical uncertainty. Notably, while there was strong consensus on some traditional practices, agreement was lacking within antithrombotic treatment, drain duration, whether to carry out routine postoperative CT-scans and the use of eMMA.

The study also poses some limitations, one of which is the fact that each round of questioning varied in number of participants (Round 1, n = 27, Round 2, n = 32, Round 3, n = 19). This may have influenced the stability and reliability of consensus outcomes. As the participants were anonymized the balance between medical specialties throughout the three rounds may be heterogenic, which is of importance, especially in round 3. Furthermore, since the symposium, the MAGIC-MT [64], STEM [22] and EMBOLISE [16] trials on eMMA have been published and with today’s knowledge the responds on this topic may be of limited benefit.

Conclusion

The 2024 iCORIC/DACSUHS symposium aimed to establish consensus on CSDH management through a three round Delphi Survey. Through scientific lectures and open discussions amongst participants the degree of consensus increased during the Delphi Survey on several topics thereby moving closer to a shared transnational CSDH framework.
