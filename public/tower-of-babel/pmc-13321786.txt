Caregiver burden, compassion and spiritual well-being among family caregivers of bedridden patients in Türkiye: a nursing study
Authors: Abdulmuttalip Oner, Nilufer Yildirim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe present study aims to examine the relationship between caregiver burden, compassion, and spiritual well-being in caregivers for bedridden patients.MethodA descriptive and cross-sectional study. Data were collected from 130 caregivers for bedridden patients receiving home care and healthcare services in Mus Province. The Compassion Scale, Burden Interview Scale, and Spiritual Well-Being Scale have been used.ResultThe mean scores for each of the variables were as follows: mean score for caregiver burden was 42.67 ± 13.48, mean score for compassion was 82.38 ± 13.77, and mean score for spiritual well-being was 3.70 ± 0.58. The scores for caregiver burden were significantly correlated with scores for both compassion and spiritual well-being (p < 0.001 for both). Additionally, the scores for compassion were also significantly correlated with scores for spiritual well-being (p < 0.001).ConclusionThe scores of the participants on the Burden Interview Scale, Compassion Scale, and Spiritual Well-Being Scale were found to be significantly higher than average.Clinical trial numberClinical trial number is not applicable.Keywords: Caregiver burden, Spiritual well being, Compassion, Bedridden patient, Nursing

Introduction

Care is all activities performed to maintain a person’s essentials, to maintain and develop its capabilities, and to enable the continuation of their lives [1]. Care needs have exponentially grown in past decades due to growing life expectancies, and they are still growing. This exponential growth in care needs also grows the pool of caregivers. Turkish Statistical Institute projections estimate that the average life expectancy in Türkiye will be 78 years in 2025 [2]. Home care services and home caregivers have grown increasingly important in recent years in terms of healthcare sustainability [3, 4]. This increasing importance has created awareness of caregivers’ multidimensional burden [5]. The burden of care is defined as the physical, psychological, social and financial problems of the caregiver. Caregiving is not an event that is planned. People suddenly find themselves in the position of the main caregiver. This places a significant burden on the care giver [6]. The burden of care can be divided in two types of burden. Subjective burden and objective burden. The objective burden consists of the time and effort expended by the caregiver to meet the demands of the care recipient. The subjective burden refers to the psychological, emotional and behavioral problems experienced by the caregiver [7]. Caregiving is a process in which the problems and distress occurs mutually. In this caregiving process emotional, psychological and physical changes takes place. Compassion is increasingly recognized as a fundamental component of caregiving that enables caregivers to notice suffering, respond with sensitivity, and sustain humane, person-centered care. Recent literature suggests that compassion is associated not only with better patient care but also with caregivers’ psychological resilience, professional well-being, and the capacity to maintain meaningful therapeutic relationships [8]. Caregivers of patients with long-term, high-dependency care needs, compassion can be understood as an important emotional resource that helps sustain caregiving despite ongoing physical and psychological demands. Recent evidence shows that higher compassion is associated with lower stress levels and fewer dysfunctional coping strategies among family caregivers, suggesting that compassion may support healthier adjustment during prolonged caregiving [9]. tudies focusing on caregivers of highly dependent patients also indicate that increasing caregiver burden is associated with lower spiritual well-being, whereas positive religious coping is linked to better spiritual well-being, showing that the caregiving experience is shaped not only by emotional strain but also by spiritual resources [10]. ecent palliative care research further emphasizes that recognizing and addressing the spiritual needs of family caregivers is essential for supporting their well-being and improving the overall quality of care, indicating that compassion should be considered together with spiritual well-being in caregiver-focused research [11]. This indicates that the spiritual dimension is vital to achieve holistic wellness. Spirituality is breath, the breath of life. Spiritus is the Latin word for spirituality. There is not an exact word in the Turkish language. One uses the word “maneviyat”, which is of Arabic origin, with the same meaning. Spirituality is the enjoyment of life. There is a religious aspect to it, but it is not always used in that way. As people learn spirituality, they begin to recognize it as something distinctly different from religion [12]. Yet, being well, which is spirituality and enables one to enjoy life physically, mentally, socially, and is another word for happiness, is naturally a psychological phenomenon [13].

This study aims to examine the relationship between caregiving burden, compassion and spiritual well-being among primary caregivers for bedridden patients. In alignment with the study’s aim, the following research questions will be addressed;

The three research questions are:

Is there a relationship between caregiver burden and compassion scores of caregivers?

Is there a relationship between caregiver burden and compassion scores of caregivers?

Is there a relationship between caregiver burden and spiritual well-being scores of caregivers?

Is there a relationship between caregiver burden and spiritual well-being scores of caregivers?

Is there a relationship between compassion and spiritual well-being scores of caregivers?

Is there a relationship between compassion and spiritual well-being scores of caregivers?

Methods

Type of research

A coss-sectional study.

Population and sample of the study

The population of the study was primary caregivers for bedridden patients living in the city center in Mus Province. According to data from Mus Provincial Center for 2024, the population was the 156 primary caregivers for bedridden patients that were registered with Mus State Hospital and that are currently receiving home care from Mus State Hospital in District. The sample studied was 130; the 83% of the population that agreed to participate; and sampling method used was purposive sampling.

Instruments

Four different data collection instruments were used: “Sociodemographic Information Form,” “Burden Interview Scale,” “Compassion Scale,” and “Spiritual Well-being Scale.”

Sociodemographic information form

A 19-item sociodemographic form was developed based on a literature review conducted to assess the sociodemographic characteristics of the caregivers and the patient. The items encompass variables such as the caregiver’s gender marital status, employment status, income level, educational status, relationship with the patient, and duration of care—characteristics about the patient and their care—while also including questions regarding the patient’s gender and medical diagnosis [14, 15].

Burden interview scale

The Zarit Burden Interview was developed by Zarit, Reever, and Bach-Peterson in 1980. However, İnci utilized this interview in 2008 to determine its validity and reliability for use in Turkish. The interview consists of 22 items that determine the impact that the care of an individual with care needs has upon the life of the caregiver. The items are to be answered using a Likert-type scale ranging from 0 to 4. These answers range from “never” to “almost always.” The interview can range from a minimum score of 0 to a maximum score of 88. These items relate to the social and emotional aspects of the caregiver role. Higher scores on the interview indicate that the caregiver is experiencing a higher level of burden from providing care for an individual who has care needs. Scores of 0–20 indicate that the caregiver is experiencing little to no burden from providing care for an individual with care needs. Scores of 21–40 indicate that the caregiver is experiencing mild to moderate burden from providing care. Scores of 41–60 indicate that the caregiver is experiencing moderate to severe burden from providing care. Scores of 61–88 indicate that the caregiver is experiencing severe burden from providing care. The reliability coefficient for this instrument is 0.95 [16]. In the present study, the reliability coefficient for the scale was determined to be 0.85.

Compassion scale (CS)

Data regarding compassion was obtained through the “Compassion Scale,” developed by Pommier and later translated into Turkish by Akdeniz and Deniz. The scale consists of 24 items that employ a five-point Likert-type scale for ratings (with values of 1 = Never, 2 = Rarely, 3 = Occasionally, 4 = Frequently, and 5 = Always). The items measure six sub-dimensions of compassion: Kindness (items 6, 8, 16, 24), Indifference (items 2, 12, 14, 18), Common Humanity (items 11, 15, 17, 20), Disengagement (items 3, 5, 10, 22), Mindfulness (items 4, 9, 13, 21), and Separation (items 1, 7, 19, 23). The dimensions of indifference, detachment, and disengagement use a reverse scoring method to calculate the scores for those questions. The scores from each scale are summed to calculate the total mean score for each individual that is measured with the tool. As the total score calculated from this tool increases, the level of compassion exhibited by those individuals decreases (indicating the potential for compassion) [17, 18]. In our study, the total alpha value of the internal consistency assessment of the scale was 0.843.

Spiritual well-being scale (SWBS)

The scale created by Ekşi and Karadaş in 2017 assesses adults’ spirituality and harmony with the values ​​of their lives. Validity and reliability analyzes were carried out. The scale consists of 29 items and 3 sub-dimensions. There are 15 items in the transcendence sub-dimension of the scale. The harmony with nature sub-dimension of the scale has 7 items, and the anomie sub-dimension has 7 items. In scoring the items for the total score of the scale, the anomie sub-dimension items are reverse-scored. A high score from each sub-dimension of the scale indicates that the individual possesses the feature defined by that sub-dimension. The scale provides a total well-being score. The scoring of the scale involves averaging between the sub-dimensions and total score [18]. In our study, the overall Cronbach’s Alpha internal consistency coefficient of the scale was found to be 0.838.

Ethical principles of the research

The research was ethically approved by Erzurum Technical University’s Scientific Research and Publication Ethics Committee, decision number 07; meeting number 09 on July 25, 2024. Written permission was granted by Mus Provincial Health Directorate on August 14, 2024, for permission for the research to be conducted. Caregivers for bedridden patients were contacted through homecare and the thesis intent was explained face to face. The survey was administered after the individual agreed to participate in the study and provided informed consent to complete the survey. This study was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration and in compliance with publication ethics principles.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 22.0. Descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables to determine the central tendency and dispersion of each variable. For categorical variables, percentages and frequencies were calculated. For continuous variables, means and standard deviations were calculated. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using coefficients for skewness and kurtosis. Coefficients within the range of -2 to + 2 are considered indicative of normality. Pearson’s correlation coefficients were calculated to determine the relationships between caregiver burden, compassion, spiritual well-being, and each of their subdimensions. Multiple linear regression analyses were performed to determine the extent to which each of these variables were statistically predictive of one another. All tests were performed with significance values of p < 0.05.

Discussion

In the study, it was observed that as the burden of care for the bedridden at home by family members increases, the total compassion score and spiritual well-being score of those caregivers decreases. Furthermore, a moderate, positive, and significant correlation between total compassion and spiritual well-being among the caregivers was found. Thus, it is evident that the burden of providing care for a family member who is bedridden is associated with the outcomes of the caregivers. Specifically, as the burden of providing care for the bedridden family member increases, the capacity of that family member to remain compassionate towards others, and to feel positive feelings of well-being and spirituality towards their life and beliefs towards the divine, decreases.

The negative relationship between burden of care provided to a bedridden family member and the spiritual well-being of the caregiver is in line with the findings of the current literature regarding these two concepts. For instance, Mirhosseini et al. (2024) investigated the relationship between burden of care provided to a stroke patient at home by a family member, and the spiritual well-being and religious coping methods of those caregivers [19]. Similarly, İşleyen et al. (2025) also found that there is a negative relationship between these two variables among caregivers of elderly patients with stroke [10]. Furthermore, Young et al. (2024) also investigated the impact of spirituality upon the relationship between burden of care provided to an older patient at home by an informal caregiver, and the quality of life of that caregiver [20]. Thus, each of these studies indicates that providing long-term care for a stroke patient at home to a family member is associated with lower spiritual well-being among those caregivers.

The finding that the total compassion score of the caregivers for a bedridden family member decreases as the burden of care increases is also a noteworthy finding of the study. Few studies have investigated the impact of burden of care upon compassion of a caregiver; however, many of the studies investigating the impact of burden of care upon self-compassion of a caregiver have revealed similar findings. For instance, Kalav et al. (2026) discovered that the component of self-compassion regarding mindfulness was inversely related to the burden of care provided to an adult patient with cancer [21]. Furthermore, Lloyd et al. (2019) also discovered that self-compassion was negatively associated with the burden of care provided to a family member with dementia [22]. Additionally, Xu et al. (2020) found that the level of self-compassion among a family caregiver of a patient with a chronic illness was associated with the ability of that self-compassion to mitigate the negative effect of the burden of care upon the psychological state of the caregiver [23]. Overall, these studies indicate that if the burden of care increases for a family member who is suffering from an illness, the self-compassion and compassion of that caregiver towards their own care of themselves will decrease.

In addition to the findings regarding compassion and spiritual well-being of the caregivers for the bedridden family member, a positive and significant relationship between total compassion and total spiritual well-being was also found. In other words, as the level of compassion of the caregivers for the sickly family member increases, the spiritual well-being of the caregivers increases, as well. Such findings are in line with the current knowledge of the differences in stress among caregivers based upon their levels of compassion, and how spiritual well-being is correlated with low burden in care providing responsibilities of the caregivers, quality of life of the caregivers, and the feeling of the caregivers that they have a purpose in performing care for their family member [9, 11, 20, 24]. Furthermore, the spiritual needs of family caregivers are known to be pronounced and essential for the well-being of those caregivers [11]. Thus, these two factors are positively associated with one another among the caregivers for their bedridden family member.

In addition to these findings regarding spiritual well-being and compassion of the caregivers, it was also found that these factors were still significant predictors of the outcome of the study within the regression models, though some variables were not significant among the multivariable models.

It is also appropriate to assess the family caregivers regarding their burden in caring for the patient, their level of compassion towards the patient, and their spiritual well-being [21, 24, 25]. Thus, these findings indicate that nurses are not only necessary to care for the family members regarding their burden in providing care for the bedridden patient, but also in regard to their level of compassion and their level of spiritual well-being.

Conclusion

Overall, then, the burden of care for a bedridden family member at home is associated with negative outcomes for those caregivers in relation to their levels of compassion and spiritual well-being towards their own lives; yet, these two aspects are positively associated with one another. Thus, this study helps to indicate the necessity of considering the family member as a focus of care within the management of care for the bedridden family member. Despite the non-causal relationships between each of these variables that were assessed, the study does help to indicate the need for consideration of the psychological and emotional needs of the caregiver in any care plan that is created for the bedridden patient.

Limitations of the Study

For the purpose of conducting this research, the patients were visited individually by members of the home care and health service teams. Thus, data was gathered in a time-consuming process. Many of the caregivers were not willing to complete the questionnaires provided to them. However, some of those caregivers had limited literacy skills, hence assisting those caregivers in reading the questions to them was necessary. Ultimately, though, the caregivers were successfully obtained through explaining the significance and necessity of the study in contributing to the existing literature. Finally, this study is limited to only the city center of Mus.
