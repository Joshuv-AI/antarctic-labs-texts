Introduction and importance

Tonsillectomy is the most common procedure in otorhinolaryngology. Common indications are obstructive symptoms and recurrent tonsillitis. It has been documented that tonsillectomy significantly improves the quality of life of patients who undergo this procedure[1, 2]. Often considered a low-risk procedure, post-operative complications can range from minor to major, which may occur as early complications arising within 7–14 days or as late complications occurring after 14 days. Common complications include post-operative bleeding, anesthetic agent-related issues, and, in rare cases, complications such as emphysema, tension pneumothorax, and pneumomediastinum[3].

Subcutaneous emphysema and tension pneumothorax can be life-threatening, and the etiological mechanism remains unclear. It is believed that this is due to mucosal trauma or barotrauma and also air dissection along a fascial plane. Trauma can be surgery-related, occur during the insertion of an endotracheal tube, or result from excessive positive pressure ventilation, which can breach the mucosa. Air leakage can accumulate through the loose connective tissue of the face, neck, and chest, causing subcutaneous emphysema, and it may enter the pleural space, leading to pneumothorax. Continuous accumulation of air without air escape may result in tension pneumothorax[4,5].

In this case report, we present a rare instance of head, neck, and chest subcutaneous emphysema and life-threatening bilateral tension pneumothorax with obstructive shock in a child post-tonsillectomy, discussing its clinical presentation, diagnosis formulation, management plan, and follow-up. This case emphasizes the critical need for early recognition of rare post-operative complications, providing valuable insights into the perioperative management of patients undergoing tonsillectomy to ensure timely intervention and prevent life-threatening outcomes. The work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria[6].

Case presentation

A 3-year-old male was referred from a lower facility with a history of 4 hours post-adenotonsillectomy. The patient underwent an extra-capsular adenotonsillectomy, which was conducted using cold steel instruments, followed by hemostasis using bipolar diathermy 4 hours prior to admission. The indication for surgery was adenotonsillar hypertrophy with sleep-disordered breathing, and there was no obvious muscular injury observed intraoperatively.

During extubation after anesthesia, the patient developed a sudden onset of extensive swelling of the face, neck, and chest. This was associated with difficulty breathing and bluish discoloration of the palms. Following the development of these symptoms, the patient was intubated and manually ventilated using an Ambu bag prior to referral. We suspect that excessive positive pressure ventilation during this period may have contributed to barotrauma and subsequent subcutaneous emphysema and pneumothorax. No history of drug allergy was reported.

On arrival at Emergency Medicine Department, the patient had a nasogastric tube (NGT) and was intubated with endotracheal tube size 4.5 (uncuffed), on an Ambu bag. There was swelling of the face, neck, and chest with crepitus on palpation. The patient was dyspneic (significant respiratory distress, exhibiting signs of use of accessory muscles and nasal flaring) with vital sign as follows: BP: 50/30 mmHg, RR: 51 cpm, PR: 161 bpm, SpO2: 78% on 5 L of O2, 100% with assisted ventilation. Oropharyngeal examination: The tonsillar bed was covered with slough, but there was no active bleeding. Provisional diagnosis include post-adenotonsillectomy, bilateral tension pneumothorax secondary to possible tracheal injury, and subcutaneous emphysema (Fig. 1). Urgent chest X-ray before intervention revealed bilateral tension pneumothorax with bilateral lung collapse (Fig. 2). Chest decompression was performed with a 16-gauge cannula, followed by bilateral chest tube insertion and decompression of subcutaneous emphysema. IV Ceftriaxone 1 g once daily for 3 days and IV Paracetamol 500 mg for pain were administered. After chest tube insertion and decompression, the patient was extubated and started spontaneous breathing. He was admitted to the Pediatric ICU for further observation. A control chest X-ray done on day 3 showed significant improvement in subcutaneous emphysema, and the patient was breathing spontaneously (Fig. 3). Figure 1.Physical appearance upon admission. Clinical discussion

Tonsillectomy is generally considered a safe procedure, but rare and potentially life-threatening complications, such as subcutaneous emphysema and tension pneumothorax, can occur. This case highlights the importance of early recognition and timely intervention in managing such rare postoperative complications.

Subcutaneous emphysema and pneumothorax following adenotonsillectomy are uncommon. The proposed mechanisms include barotrauma from excessive positive pressure ventilation, surgical mucosal trauma, or inadvertent tracheal injury. In this case, the rapid onset of symptoms immediately post-extubation suggests barotrauma as a likely cause, possibly exacerbated by excessive positive pressure ventilation or air dissection along fascial planes. Similar cases have been reported where improper airway management during extubation and intraoperative ventilation strategies contributed to emphysema and pneumothorax[7,8]

Clinically, tension pneumothorax is a medical emergency due to its ability to compromise cardiopulmonary function. Our patient exhibited classic signs, including respiratory distress, hypoxia, and hypotension, necessitating urgent decompression. Early recognition and rapid intervention, including needle decompression and bilateral chest tube insertion, were critical in preventing further deterioration. Postoperative monitoring with serial chest radiographs confirmed resolution of pneumothorax and subcutaneous emphysema, reinforcing the need for imaging in suspected cases.

Previous literature highlights the importance of careful perioperative airway management, particularly in pediatric patients who are more vulnerable to airway trauma due to smaller anatomical structures. Preventative measures include minimizing excessive positive pressure ventilation, avoiding forceful extubation, and promptly managing any airway injuries to prevent air dissection into deeper fascial planes[3].

This case underscores the importance of vigilance in post-tonsillectomy care. Although rare, subcutaneous emphysema and tension pneumothorax should be considered in any patient presenting with postoperative respiratory distress and swelling. Modifiable intraoperative factors, such as excessive positive pressure ventilation, aggressive airway suctioning, and traumatic intubation or extubation, are potential contributors. A multidisciplinary approach involving ENT specialists, anesthesiologists, and pediatric intensivists is crucial in ensuring prompt diagnosis and intervention to prevent fatal outcomes.

Recommendations

Gentle ventilation techniques to avoid barotrauma in pediatric patients.

Avoiding forceful intubation/extubation and ensuring adequate sedation.

Close monitoring for early signs of airway compromise postoperatively.

Use cuffed endotracheal tubes with minimal leak to prevent air escape into tissue planes when appropriate for age and weight.

Conclusion

Bilateral tension pneumothorax is a rare but life-threatening complication of tonsillectomy. Early recognition, prompt imaging, and immediate intervention are crucial for patient survival. Early signs such as facial and neck swelling, crepitus, hypoxia, and hypotension following extubation should raise suspicion for complications like subcutaneous emphysema and pneumothorax. This case underscores the importance of careful airway management, postoperative vigilance, and a multidisciplinary approach to ensure patient safety and optimal outcomes.
