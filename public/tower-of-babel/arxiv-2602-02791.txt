Plug-In Classification of Drift Functions in Diffusion Processes Using Neural Networks
Yuzhen Zhao, Jiarong Fan, Yating Liu

Abstract
We study a supervised multiclass classification problem for diffusion processes, where each class is characterized by a distinct drift function and trajectories are observed at discrete times. Extending the one-dimensional multiclass framework of 8 to multidimensional diffusions, we propose a neural network–based plug-in classifier that estimates the drift functions for each class from independent sample paths and assigns labels based on a Bayes-type decision rule. Under standard regularity assumptions, we establish convergence rates for the excess misclassification risk, explicitly capturing the effects of drift estimation error and time discretization. Numerical experiments demonstrate that the proposed method achieves faster convergence and improved classification performance compared to 8 in the one-dimensional setting, remains effective in higher dimensions when the underlying drift functions admit a compositional structure, and consistently outperforms direct neural network classifiers trained end-to-end on trajectories without exploiting the diffusion model structure.



1
Introduction

In this paper, we study a supervised multiclass classification problem for the drift functions of a time-homogeneous diffusion process using a neural-network (NN) based plug-in classifier. Specifically, we consider a filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]},\mathbb{P})
, an
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
-adapted
d
-dimensional standard Brownian motion
B=(B_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
, and an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued diffusion process
X=(X_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
defined on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]},\mathbb{P})
and solving the stochastic differential equation (SDE)

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=b_{Y}(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}B_{t},

(1)

where
X_{0}
is an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
valued random vector, and
Y
is a discrete random variable taking values in
\mathcal{Y}\coloneqq\{1,\dots,K\}
representing the class label. For each label
k\in\mathcal{Y}
, the drift
b_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the diffusion coefficient
\sigma:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
are measurable. We assume that
X_{0}
and the Brownian motion
B=(B_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
are both independent of the label
Y
.

For each fixed label
Y=k\in\mathcal{Y}
, we assume that
N_{k}
independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) sample paths are available and observed at high frequency, that is, at discrete time points on a fine temporal grid. This yields the dataset
\mathcal{D}_{N_{k}}^{[k]}
given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{N_{k}}^{[k]}=\Big\{\bar{X}^{[k],(n)}_{t_{0}:t_{M}}\!=\!\big(\bar{X}^{[k],(n)}_{t_{0}}\!\!,\dots,\bar{X}^{[k],(n)}_{t_{M}}\big),1\!\leq\!n\!\leq\!{N_{k}}\Big\},

where
t_{m}=m\Delta
with time step
\Delta=\tfrac{T}{M}\to 0
as
M\to\infty
. We denote by
\mathcal{D}_{N}=\bigcup_{k=1}^{K}\mathcal{D}_{N_{k}}^{[k]}
the full training dataset, where
N=\sum_{k=1}^{K}N_{k}
.

The objective of this paper is to construct a classifier

g^{\mathcal{D}_{N}}:(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{M+1}\to\mathcal{Y}

based on the dataset
\mathcal{D}_{N}
such that, given a new observed trajectory

\bar{Z}=(Z_{t_{0}},\dots,Z_{t_{M}}),

g^{\mathcal{D}_{N}}(\bar{Z})
predicts the corresponding label of the underlying diffusion process
Z
.

Since the class label
Y
influences the dynamics only through the drift function, we assume throughout the paper that the diffusion coefficient
\sigma
is known and that the prior class probabilities

\mathfrak{p}_{k}\coloneqq\mathbb{P}(Y=k)
are given. In contrast, the drift functions
b_{k}
,
k\in\mathcal{Y}
, are unknown and must be learned from the data. If the diffusion coefficient
\sigma
and the class priors
\mathfrak{p}_{k}
,
k\in\mathcal{Y}
, are unknown, then
\sigma
can be consistently estimated from high-frequency observations (see, e.g.,
5
), and the class priors
\mathfrak{p}_{k}
can be estimated empirically as

\widehat{\mathfrak{p}}_{k}=\tfrac{N_{k}}{N},

where
N_{k}
denotes the number of training trajectories labeled
k
.

1.1
Literature Review and Motivation

Diffusion processes of the form (
1
) constitute a fundamental class of stochastic models with a wide range of applications in physics, biology, and mathematical finance (see, e.g.,
13
;
3
;
17
). More recently, they have gained renewed attention as the continuous-time theoretical foundation of generative diffusion models, which have achieved state-of-the-art performance in image generation and related tasks (see, e.g.,
22
;
11
). The supervised classification of diffusion paths, where class information is encoded through the drift function, has been investigated in
4
;
12
;
7
;
8
;
10
. However, existing results are largely restricted to the one-dimensional setting, which limits their applicability in modern high-dimensional contexts such as diffusion-based generative models.

This paper extends this line of research to multidimensional diffusion processes. The proposed classifier is based on the nonparametric estimation of the drift function, a well-studied problem in the diffusion literature (see e.g.
15
;
6
;
9
;
23
). We note that drift estimation and classification are distinct tasks. Classification provides a statistical decision framework to map complex estimated functions, possibly learned by neural networks, onto a finite set of interpretable diffusion processes. This classification perspective not only facilitates theoretical analysis but also enhances interpretability and practical relevance in downstream applications.

1.2
Contribution and Organization of this Paper

This paper studies supervised multiclass classification for multidimensional diffusion processes, where each class is characterized by a distinct drift function. On the theoretical side, we make the following contributions:

1.

Proposition
2.4
provides a characterization of the Bayes classifier in the multidimensional setting, extending the one-dimensional result of
7
. This result also serves as the foundation for constructing plug-in classifiers based on estimated drift functions.

2.

Theorem
2.5
establishes a decomposition of the excess classification risk, defined as the gap between the risk of an arbitrary classifier and that of the Bayes classifier, in terms of the time step size
\Delta
and the drift estimation error.

3.

Theorem
2.7
derives convergence rates for the neural network based plug-in classifier using drift estimation results from
23
.

On the numerical side, we consider two simulation examples. The first involves a diffusion with locally fluctuating drift functions, where we show that the proposed neural network based classifier significantly outperforms B-spline based methods as well as a direct neural network classifier that ignores the underlying SDE structure. Moreover, even in high-dimensional settings, the observed convergence rates match the theoretical upper bounds of Theorem
2.7
and do not depend on the dimension, due to the compositional structure of the drift. The second experiment revisits the example of
8
, where our method achieves performance comparable to both their classifier and the Bayes benchmark.

2
Construction and Convergence Analysis of the Neural Network Based Plug-In Classifier

Throughout the paper, we work under the following assumptions.

Assumption 2.1
.

(a)

\min_{k\in\mathcal{Y}}\mathfrak{p}_{k}>0
,
\mathbb{E}[|X_{0}|^{4}]<+\infty
;

(b)

The coefficient functions
b_{k},\,k\in\mathcal{Y}
and
\sigma
are globally Lipschitz continuous, that is, there exist constants
L_{b},L_{\sigma}>0
such that for every
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

\displaystyle\max_{k\in\mathcal{Y}}|b_{k}(x)-b_{k}(y)|\leq L_{b}|x-y|,

\displaystyle|\sigma(x)-\sigma(y)|\leq L_{\sigma}|x-y|;

(c)

For every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the matrix
\sigma(x)
is invertible.

Assumption 2.2
.

(Novikov’s condition)
For every
k\in\mathcal{Y}
, it holds that
\mathbb{E}\big[\exp\big(\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}|\sigma^{-1}(X_{s})\,b_{k}(X_{s})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\big)\big]<+\infty
.

Assumption 2.3
.

For every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the matrix
a(x)\coloneqq\sigma\sigma^{\top}(x)
is invertible, and
a^{-1}
is globally Lipschitz continuous with respect to the operator norm
|\cdot|_{\mathrm{op}}
, with Lipschitz constant
L_{a^{-1}}
. Moreover, there exists a constant
\Lambda>0
such that
\max(|\sigma(x)|,|a(x)^{-1}|_{\mathrm{op}})\leq\Lambda,\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

2.1
Bayes classifier

Consider a diffusion process
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
that is a solution to (
1
)
and is observed continuously in time. For a given classifier

g:\mathcal{C}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathcal{Y},

its performance is measured by the misclassification risk

\mathcal{R}(g)\coloneqq\mathbb{P}\big(g(X)\neq Y\big).

(2)

The Bayes classifier
g^{*}
is defined as any classifier minimizing the misclassification risk over the class

\mathbb{G}\coloneqq\big\{g:\mathcal{C}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathcal{Y}\;\text{measurable}\big\},

that is,

g^{*}\in\argmin_{g\in\mathbb{G}}\mathcal{R}(g).

(3)

Moreover,
g^{*}
admits the characterization
g^{*}(X)\in\argmax_{k\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi_{k}^{*}(X)
(see, e.g.,
14
), where

\pi_{k}^{*}(X)=\mathbb{P}(\,Y=k\mid X\,).

(4)

The following proposition, whose proof sketch is provided in Section
5
(see Appendix
A
for the complete proof), extends
7
to a high-dimensional setting.

Proposition 2.4
.

Assume that Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
hold.
For each
k\in\mathcal{Y}
, define

\displaystyle F_{k}^{*}(X)\coloneqq\;

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}b_{k}(X_{s})^{\top}\big(\sigma\sigma^{\top}\big)^{-1}(X_{s})\,\mathrm{d}X_{s}

\displaystyle\;-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\big\lvert\sigma^{-1}(X_{s})b_{k}(X_{s})\big\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s.

(5)

Then, for each
k\in\mathcal{Y}
, the conditional probability
\pi_{k}^{*}
defined in (
4
) satisfies

\pi_{k}^{*}(X)=\phi_{k}\big(F^{*}(X)\big),\quad\mathbb{P}-\text{a.s.,}

(6)

where
F^{*}=(F_{1}^{*},\dots,F_{K}^{*})
and

\phi_{k}(x_{1},\dots,x_{K})\coloneqq\frac{\mathfrak{p}_{k}\exp(x_{k})}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\mathfrak{p}_{j}\exp(x_{j})},\;1\leq k\leq K

(7)

denote the softmax functions with prior weights
\mathfrak{p}_{k},\,1\leq k\leq K
.

Taking the Bayes classifier
g^{*}
defined in (
3
) as a reference, the performance of any classifier
g\in\mathbb{G}
is evaluated using the excess classification risk

\mathcal{R}(g)-\mathcal{R}(g^{*}).

(8)

This criterion will be used to assess the performance of our neural network based classifier in the sequel.

2.2
Construction of the NN-based plug-in classifier

Using the characterization of the Bayes classifier established in Proposition
2.4
, we describe the construction of the NN-based plug-in classifier in two steps.

Step 1: Time discretization of
F_{k}^{*}
and plug-in classification.

We begin by introducing a time-discretized version of the functional
F_{k}^{*}
defined in (
2.4
). Given a discretely observed trajectory
(X_{t_{0}},\dots,X_{t_{M}})
of the diffusion process
X
, we define

\displaystyle\bar{F}_{k}(X)\coloneqq

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}b_{k}(X_{t_{m}})^{\top}\big(\sigma\sigma^{\top}\big)^{-1}(X_{t_{m}})\,(X_{t_{m+1}}\!\!-X_{t_{m}})

\displaystyle-\frac{\Delta}{2}\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\big\lvert\sigma^{-1}(X_{t_{m}})\,b_{k}(X_{t_{m}})\big\rvert^{2}.

(9)

Since the drift functions
b_{k}
are unknown and must be estimated, we assume that, for each
k\in\mathcal{Y}
, a nonparametric estimator
\widehat{b}_{k}
of
b_{k}
is available. Plugging these estimators into (
2.2
) yields the following implementable score function:

\displaystyle\widehat{F}_{k}(X)\coloneqq

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\widehat{b}_{k}(X_{t_{m}})^{\top}\big(\sigma\sigma^{\top}\big)^{-1}(X_{t_{m}})\,(X_{t_{m+1}}\!\!-X_{t_{m}})

\displaystyle-\frac{\Delta}{2}\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\big\lvert\sigma^{-1}(X_{t_{m}})\,\widehat{b}_{k}(X_{t_{m}})\big\rvert^{2}.

(10)

The associated probabilities and the resulting plug-in classifier are then defined by

\widehat{\pi}_{k}(X)=\phi_{k}\big(\widehat{F}(X)\big),\quad\widehat{g}(X)\in\argmax_{k\in\mathcal{Y}}\widehat{\pi}_{k}(X),

(11)

where
\widehat{F}(X)=(\widehat{F}_{1}(X),\dots,\widehat{F}_{K}(X))
and
\phi_{k},\,k\in\mathcal{Y}
are softmax functions defined by (
7
).

Step 2: Drift estimation.

We now describe the construction of the nonparametric estimators
\widehat{b}_{k}
of the drift functions
b_{k}
for each class
k\in\mathcal{Y}
.
For a fixed label
k
, we estimate
b_{k}
on a compact set
\mathcal{K}
using the class-specific training dataset
\mathcal{D}_{N_{k}}^{[k]}
and neural networks defined further in (
13
). The compact set
\mathcal{K}
can be selected based on the distribution of the training data, for instance via sample coverage or a sample-splitting procedure.
Following the approach of
23
, the drift function is estimated component-wise using feedforward neural networks.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{L,\mathbf{p}}
denote the class of feedforward neural networks with
L
hidden layers and layer widths
\mathbf{p}=(p_{0},p_{1},\dots,p_{L+1})\in\mathbb{N}^{L+2}
, where
p_{0}=d
is the input dimension and
p_{L+1}=1
is the output dimension. Each function
f\in\mathcal{F}_{L,\mathbf{p}}
maps
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to
\mathbb{R}
and admits the representation

f(x)=W_{L}\sigma_{\mathbf{v}_{L}}W_{L-1}\sigma_{\mathbf{v}_{L-1}}\cdots W_{1}\sigma_{\mathbf{v}_{1}}W_{0}x,

(12)

where
W_{j}
is a
p_{j+1}\times p_{j}
weight matrix. The activation function
\sigma(x)=\max(x,0)
is the ReLU function applied component-wise, and for
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{r})\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
, the shifted ReLU operator
\sigma_{\mathbf{v}}:\mathbb{R}^{r}\to\mathbb{R}^{r}
is defined by

\sigma_{\mathbf{v}}\big((y_{1},\dots,y_{r})^{\top}\big)=\big(\sigma(y_{1}-v_{1}),\dots,\sigma(y_{r}-v_{r})\big)^{\top}.

In this paper, we consider sparse neural networks with sparsity level
s
and impose a uniform boundedness constraint with constant
F>0
. We define the class of admissible neural network estimators as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(L,\mathbf{p},s,F):=\Big\{f\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{K}}\>:\>f\in\mathcal{F}_{L,\mathbf{p}}\text{ such that }

\displaystyle\qquad\max_{j=0,\dots,L}\big(\|W_{j}\|_{\infty}\vee|\mathbf{v}_{j}|_{\infty}\big)\leq 1,\;\big\|f\big\|_{\infty}\leq F,

\displaystyle\qquad\text{ and }\sum_{j=0}^{L}\big(\|W_{j}\|_{0}+|\mathbf{v}_{j}|_{0}\big)\leq s\Big\}.

(13)

For every
i\in\{1,...,d\}
, the estimator
\widehat{b}_{k}^{i}
of the
i
-th component of
b_{k}
is obtained by minimizing the empirical loss

\mathcal{Q}^{i}_{\mathcal{D}_{N_{k}}^{[k]}}(f)\coloneqq\!\frac{1}{N_{k}M}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{k}}\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\left(Y^{[k],(n),i}_{t_{m}}-f\big(\bar{X}^{[k],(n)}_{t_{m}}\big)\right)^{2}

(14)

over the class
\mathcal{F}(L,\mathbf{p},s,F)
, where

Y^{[k],(n),i}_{t_{m}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\Delta}\left(\bar{X}^{[k],(n),\,i}_{t_{m+1}}-\bar{X}^{[k],(n),\,i}_{t_{m}}\right)

(15)

denotes the discrete-time increment of the
i
-th coordinate of the process

\bar{X}^{[k],(n)}=(\bar{X}^{[k],(n)}_{t_{0}},\dots,\bar{X}^{[k],(n)}_{t_{M}})
.

\Delta\lesssim\phi_{N}\log^{3}N\text{ and }\max_{k\in\mathcal{Y}}\Psi^{\mathcal{F}}(\widehat{b}_{k})\leq C\phi_{N}\log^{3}N,

then for any
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{4}]
, it holds

\mathcal{R}(\widehat{g})-\mathcal{R}(g^{*})\leq C_{K,\Lambda,\mathfrak{C},\widetilde{C},\mathcal{K},\varepsilon}\Big(\sqrt{\Delta}+\phi_{N}^{\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon}\log^{\frac{3}{2}-\varepsilon}N\Big).

Remark 2.8

(Compact support assumption)
.

The assumption in Theorem
2.7
that each
b_{k}
is defined on a compact support through the definition of

\mathcal{G}(q,\mathbf{d},\mathbf{t},\boldsymbol{\beta},\widetilde{K})
is not restrictive in practice.
Indeed, the drift estimator
\widehat{b}_{k}
can only be learned accurately on regions where data are observed, which are bounded with high probability. More precisely, Assumption
2.1
implies that

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathbb{E}[|X_{t}|^{4}]<\infty
(see e.g.
20
).
A simple application of Markov’s inequality yields

\forall\,t\in[0,T],\quad\mathbb{P}\big(|X_{t}|\geq R\big)\;\leq\;\frac{\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathbb{E}[|X_{t}|^{4}]}{R^{4}}.

Hence, for any prescribed accuracy level
\varepsilon>0
, one can choose
R
sufficiently large such that

\mathbb{P}(|X_{t}|\geq R)\leq\varepsilon
.
Consequently, one may assume without loss of practical generality that the true drift function
b
is also defined
on a compact set covering the observed data, without significantly affecting the statistical guarantees of the classifier.
In contrast, for the practical success of the NN-based plug-in classifier, it is more important to have sufficient data in regions where the drift functions
b_{k}
differ, since these regions determine the discriminative signal between classes.

Numerical Experiments

We illustrate the performance of the NN-based classifier defined in (
11
) through two numerical experiments.

In Section

, we consider double-layer potential drifts inspired by
23
. In the one-dimensional setting, we demonstrate that the proposed NN-based plug-in classifier outperforms the B-spline–based plug-in classifier introduced in
8

4
Conclusion

The classification task is intrinsically more challenging than drift estimation task, since the excess classification risk (
8
) is always bounded by
1
. In particular, (
18
) shows that when the drift estimator is not sufficiently accurate, the resulting classifier behaves nearly at random. This difficulty is further amplified in high-dimensional settings, as also observed in
10
.

Limitation.

A careful analysis of the proof of Theorem
2.5
shows that the constant in (
18
) grows with the dimension
d
. This dependence arises from repeated applications of the Burkholder–Davis–Gundy (BDG) inequality and Gronwall’s lemma in Lemma
A.1
of
20
, whose associated constants grow with the dimension
d
. Consequently, higher-dimensional problems require increasingly accurate drift estimation to achieve reliable classification. This phenomenon is illustrated in Figure
3
, which exhibits a clear inflection point: as the dimension
d
increases, a larger sample size
N
is required before the classification error begins to decrease significantly. Moreover, the requirement
\varepsilon>0
in Theorem
2.7
is a technical artifact of the proof. Indeed, the constant
C_{K,\Lambda,\mathfrak{C},\widetilde{C},\mathcal{K},\varepsilon}

diverges as
\varepsilon\to 0
. See the proof of Theorem
2.7
for details.

Despite these intrinsic difficulties, the proposed neural network–based plug-in classifier achieves strong performance both theoretically and empirically. By explicitly exploiting the structure of the underlying diffusion process, our approach remains effective even in high-dimensional setting, as confirmed by our theoretical guarantees and numerical experiments.

\displaystyle\Phi_{t}^{[k]}

\displaystyle\coloneqq(Z_{t}^{[k]})^{-1}=\exp\left[\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\big[\sigma^{-1}(X_{s})\,b_{k}(X_{s})\big]_{i}\mathrm{d}B_{s}^{i}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}|\sigma^{-1}(X_{s})\,b_{k}(X_{s})|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\left[\int_{0}^{t}\big[\sigma^{-1}(X_{s})\,b_{k}(X_{s})\big]^{\top}\mathrm{d}B_{s}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}|\sigma^{-1}(X_{s})\,b_{k}(X_{s})|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right],

\displaystyle=\exp\left[\int_{0}^{t}\big[\sigma^{-1}(X_{s})\,b_{k}(X_{s})\big]^{\top}\big(\sigma^{-1}(X_{s})\mathrm{d}X_{s}-\sigma^{-1}(X_{s})\,b_{k}(X_{s})\mathrm{d}s\big)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}|\sigma^{-1}(X_{s})\,b_{k}(X_{s})|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right],

\displaystyle=\exp\left[\int_{0}^{t}b_{k}(X_{s})^{\top}(\sigma\sigma^{\top})^{-1}(X_{s})\mathrm{d}X_{s}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}|\sigma^{-1}(X_{s})\,b_{k}(X_{s})|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right].

(28)

Consequently,

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{k}}{\mathrm{d}\tilde{\mathbb{P}}}=\Phi_{T}^{[k]}

(29)

(see, e.g.,
18
). It follows that

\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathfrak{p}_{k}\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathfrak{p}_{k}\Phi_{T}^{[k]}\mathrm{d}\tilde{\mathbb{P}},

Moreover, since each measure
\mathbb{P}_{k}
,
k\in\mathcal{Y}
, is mutually absolutely continuous with respect to
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}
, the same holds for
\mathbb{P}
and
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}
.
Therefore, the Radon–Nikodym theorem implies that

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{k}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{k}}{\mathrm{d}\tilde{\mathbb{P}}}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}\tilde{\mathbb{P}}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}=\frac{\Phi_{T}^{[k]}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\mathfrak{p}_{j}\Phi_{T}^{[j]}}\eqqcolon\Psi^{[k]}.

(30)

Let
\mathcal{F}^{X}
denote the
\sigma
-algebra generated by

X=(X_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
, the solution of (
1
).
Let
h:\{1,...,K\}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a bounded measurable function and let
Z
be a bounded
\mathcal{F}^{X}
-measurable random variable. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[h(Y)Z]=\mathbb{E}\big[\,h(Y)\,\mathbb{E}[Z|Y]\,\big]=\sum_{k=1}^{K}h(k)\mathfrak{p}_{k}\mathbb{E}[Z|Y=k]=\sum_{k=1}^{K}h(k)\mathfrak{p}_{k}\mathbb{E}_{k}[Z]

(31)

where
\mathbb{E}_{k}[\cdot]
denotes expectation with respect to
\mathbb{P}_{k}
.
Using (
30
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[h(Y)Z]=\sum_{k=1}^{K}h(k)\mathfrak{p}_{k}\mathbb{E}\big[Z\Psi^{[k]}\big]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{K}h(k)\mathfrak{p}_{k}\Psi^{[k]}Z\right].

(32)

By the definition of conditional expectation
(see, e.g.,
19
),
this implies

\mathbb{E}[h(Y)|X]=\sum_{k=1}^{K}h(k)\,\mathfrak{p}_{k}\Psi^{[k]},\quad\mathbb{P}-\text{a.s.,}

(33)

Finally, choosing
h(y)=\mathbbm{1}_{\{k\}}(y)
for every
k\in\mathcal{Y}

concludes the proof.
∎

where the first inequality holds because on the event

|\cdot|

The functional

\displaystyle\bar{F}_{k}(X)=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}b_{k}(X_{\eta(s)})^{\top}\big(\sigma\sigma^{\top}\big)^{-1}(X_{\eta(s)})\mathrm{d}X_{s}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\big\lvert\sigma^{-1}(X_{\eta(s)})\,b_{k}(X_{\eta(s)})\big\rvert^{2}\mathrm{d}s,

where, for every

\eta(s)\coloneqq t_{m},\text{ if }t_{m}\leq s<t_{m+1},\text{ and }\eta(T)=T.

Let

Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\big|F_{k}^{*}(X)-\bar{F}_{k}(X)\big|\big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\int_{0}^{T}\big(h_{k}(X_{s})-h_{k}(X_{\eta(s)})\big)\mathrm{d}X_{s}\right|\right]+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\big|\ell_{k}(X_{s})-\ell_{k}(X_{\eta(s)})\big|\mathrm{d}s\right]\eqqcolon I_{1}+\frac{1}{2}I_{2}

Step 1-a. Upper bound of

Let
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle|h_{k}(x)-h_{k}(y)|

\displaystyle=|b_{k}(x)^{\top}a(x)^{-1}-b_{k}(y)^{\top}a(y)^{-1}|

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\int_{0}^{T}\big(h_{k}(X_{s})-h_{k}(X_{\eta(s)})\big)\mathrm{d}X_{s}\right|\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\int_{0}^{T}\big(h_{k}(X_{s})-h_{k}(X_{\eta(s)})\big)\big(b_{Y}(X_{s})\mathrm{d}s+\sigma(X_{s})\mathrm{d}B_{s}\big)\right|\right]

Step 1-b. Upper bound of

\displaystyle|\ell_{k}(x)-\ell_{k}(y)|

\displaystyle=|b_{k}(x)^{\top}a(x)^{-1}b_{k}(x)-b_{k}(y)^{\top}a(y)^{-1}b_{k}(y)|

(I.b)

For (II.b), we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{(II.b)}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|\bar{F}_{k}(X)-\widehat{F}_{k}(X)|\right]\leq C_{\Lambda,\mathfrak{C},\widehat{b}_{\max}}\mathcal{E}(\widehat{b}_{k},b_{k})^{\frac{1}{2}}

(53)

Finally, we conclude that
\mathcal{R}(\widehat{g})-\mathcal{R}(g^{*})\leq K^{2}C_{\Lambda,\mathfrak{C},\widehat{b}_{\max}}\big(\sqrt{\Delta}+\max_{k\in\mathcal{Y}}\mathcal{E}(\widehat{b}_{k},b_{k})^{\frac{1}{2}}\big)
, which completes the proof.
∎

Appendix B
Experimental Details

B.1
Example in Section
3.1

Data Generation.

Sample paths of (
1
) are simulated using the Euler–Maruyama discretization scheme over the time interval
[0,1]
, discretized with
M=100
steps and time step
\Delta=1/M=0.01
. Experiments are performed for dimensions
d\in\{1,2,5,10,50\}
.

Recall that, in this example,
K=3
. For each dimension
d
, and for each label
k\in\mathcal{Y}=\{1,2,3\}
, we generate
N_{k}\in\{2^{5},2^{6},\dots,2^{12}\}
independent trajectories as the training set, denoted by

\mathcal{D}^{[k]}_{N_{k}}=\bigl\{\,\bar{X}^{[k],(n)}_{t_{0}:t_{M}}\,\bigr\}_{1\leq n\leq N_{k}}.

For evaluation, we independently generate 1000 trajectories for each label
k\in\mathcal{Y}
, denoted by

\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}:=\bigcup_{k\in\mathcal{Y}}\mathcal{D}^{[k]}_{\mathrm{test}}\quad\text{ with }\quad\mathcal{D}^{[k]}_{\mathrm{test}}:=\bigl\{\,\tilde{X}^{[k],(n^{\prime})}_{t_{0}:t_{M}}\,\bigr\}_{1\leq n^{\prime}\leq 1000},\quad k\in\mathcal{Y}.

NN-based plug-in classifier.

Architecture.
For the neural network estimator for
b_{k}
, we use the sparse network architecture
\mathbf{p}=(d,16,32,32,16,1)
with ReLU activation function and the sparsity ratio
s_{\text{ratio}}=0.75
inspired by
23
.

Training Procedure.

Training is performed using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of
10^{-3}
and batch size of 256. For each experimental configuration, specified by a choice of the dimension
d
, the sample size
N
, and a random seed, we randomly reserve 50% of the training samples as a validation set for early stopping. Early stopping is triggered if the validation error does not improve for 20 consecutive epochs, with a maximum of 200 training epochs. Once early stopping is activated, we retrain the model on the full training dataset for a number of epochs equal to twice the epoch at which early stopping occurred.

During training, the training samples are shuffled and processed in mini-batches. After each optimizer update, we enforce the sparsity by keeping only the top 75% parameters (by absolute value) and clip all parameters to the interval
[-1,1]
.

Random seeds for NumPy and PyTorch are fixed in each repetition to ensure reproducibility.

B-spline–based plug-in classifier.

For the B-spline estimator for
b_{k}
, we adopt the same procedure (including tuning and implementation details) as in
8
.

Direct neural network classification.

We consider a direct pathwise classification approach, where the full discretely observed path

\bar{X}^{[k],(n)}_{t_{0}:t_{M}}:=\big(\bar{X}^{[k],(n)}_{t_{0}},\dots,\bar{X}^{[k],(n)}_{t_{M}}\big)

is fed into a neural network, which directly outputs a prediction of the class label
k\in\mathcal{Y}
associated with the trajectory. This approach reduces the problem to a standard multiclass classification task and does not exploit the underlying SDE structure.

A fully connected neural network with two hidden layers and ReLU activation functions is employed.
For each experimental setting, the training data are randomly split, with 50% of the samples allocated to a validation set for hyperparameter tuning. Hyperparameters are optimized with Optuna
[
1
]
, and the configuration that maximizes the validation accuracy is selected. The hyperparameter search space is defined as follows:

•

learning rate
\in\{10^{-4},\,3\times 10^{-4},\,10^{-3},\,3\times 10^{-3}\}
,

•

weight decay
\in\{0,\,10^{-5},\,10^{-4},\,10^{-3}\}
,

•

hidden layers size
\in\{(16,16),\,(32,32),\,(64,64),\,(128,128),\,(256,128)\}
,

•

batch size
\in\{64,\,128,\,256\}
.

Early stopping based on the validation accuracy is applied, with a patience of 50 epochs and a maximum budget of 200 epochs. The model achieving the highest validation accuracy during training is retained.

Random seeds for NumPy and PyTorch are fixed in each repetition to ensure reproducibility.

Evaluation.

To account for the randomness in data generation and optimization process, for each pair
(N,d)
, we repeat the experiment 50 times with different random seeds, resulting in a series of different estimators and test set
(\widehat{g}_{N,d}^{(j)},\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}^{(j)})_{j=1}^{50}
. Let
E_{N,d}^{(j)}
denote the generalization error computed in the
j
-th run with training size
N
and dimension
d
, defined as

E_{N,d}^{(j)}:=\frac{1}{3000}\sum_{n^{\prime}=1}^{1000}\sum_{k=1}^{3}\mathbbm{1}_{\big\{\widehat{g}_{N,d}^{(j)}(\tilde{X}^{[k],n^{\prime}}_{t_{0}:t_{M}})\neq k\big\}}-\mathcal{R}(g^{*}),

For each
(N,d)
, we compute:

\bar{E}_{N,d}:=\frac{1}{50}\sum_{j=1}^{50}E_{N,d}^{(j)},\quad E_{N,d}^{\text{lower}}:=\bar{E}_{N,d}-t_{0.975}^{(49)}\cdot\tau_{N,d},\quad E_{N,d}^{\text{upper}}:=\bar{E}_{N,d}+t_{0.975}^{(49)}\cdot\tau_{N,d}.

(59)

where
\tau_{N,d}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{50}}\big(\frac{1}{49}\sum_{j=1}^{50}\big(E_{N,d}^{(j)}-\bar{E}_{N,d}\big)^{2}\big)^{1/2}
and
t_{0.975}^{(49)}
is the 97.5 percentile of the Student’s
t
distribution with 49 degrees of freedom.

We use log
2
–log
2
plots to display the average empirical error
\bar{E}_{N,d}
and the corresponding interval
[E_{N,d}^{\text{lower}},E_{N,d}^{\text{upper}}]
as functions of the training sample size
N
.
