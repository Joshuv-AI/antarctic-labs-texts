Two-dimensional kinematic analysis of futsal instep shot performance in trained and novice players
Authors: Xin Shen, Xiaomei Liu, Rahmat Hidayat, Yali Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study provides a comparative kinematic analysis of futsal instep shot performance between trained athletes and novice players, addressing limited evidence on how multijoint kinematic patterns are associated with ball velocity. Twenty trained futsal athletes and twenty novice players performed standardized instep shots recorded using high-speed cameras at 240 fps. Research procedures included camera calibration, frame-by-frame motion analysis, reliability testing, and kinematic extraction using Dartfish software. Joint angles, angular velocities, torso orientation, and ball velocity were examined using independent-sample t-tests, effect sizes, and multiple regression analysis. Trained athletes exhibited more optimized multijoint kinematic patterns, characterized by more mechanically efficient knee positioning during foot placement and backswing, improved torso stability, and effective upper-limb positioning. Significant between-group differences were detected across most kinematic variables (p < 0.05). Knee angular velocity emerged as the strongest predictor of ball velocity (R² = 0.62, p < 0.01). Optimized lower- and upper-body kinematic patterns are associated with shooting proficiency and performance, supporting targeted technical training focused on optimizing shooting mechanics and kinematic efficiency.

Introduction

Futsal is a high-intensity, small-sided variant of football characterized by rapid tempo, constrained spatial environments, and a high frequency of technical executions1–3. Among the most decisive actions in futsal is shooting, which directly contributes to scoring outcomes and match performance. Effective shooting in such dynamic contexts requires not only precision and power, but also refined biomechanical coordination to ensure consistency and efficiency under temporal and spatial constraints4,5. Among the various shooting techniques used in futsal, the instep shot is widely recognized as one of the primary techniques for generating maximal ball velocity and is particularly suitable for standardized biomechanical analysis due to its reproducibility and performance relevance6–8. As the game continues to evolve tactically and physically, there is growing interest in understanding the biomechanical determinants of successful shooting actions.

Biomechanical analyses of movement kinematics and coordination patterns have identified movement patterns that differentiate proficient from suboptimal technique across various sports. In football, experienced players demonstrate superior joint sequencing, inter-segmental coordination, and efficient segmental energy transfer during kicking and shooting tasks6,9. While this body of literature provides a strong theoretical foundation, its direct applicability to futsal remains uncertain due to contextual differences, including smaller playing surfaces, higher game density, and limited preparation time.

Despite the biomechanical complexity of futsal shooting, the existing literature remains comparatively limited, particularly when contrasted with the extensive body of research on football kicking biomechanics. Empirical studies specifically examining futsal shooting mechanics are fewer relative to football-based research, and available studies tend to emphasize elite or professional players, thereby providing limited insight into comparisons across different skill levels8,10,11. This imbalance limits understanding of how training experience shapes movement organization and kinematic patterns in futsal-specific contexts. Furthermore, many studies have prioritized outcome variables such as ball velocity or accuracy, while providing less detailed examination of the underlying kinematic mechanisms responsible for these outcomes.

Previous research suggests that trained athletes tend to display greater mechanical efficiency, characterized by more coordinated joint motion and higher segmental velocities12,13. However, most of these studies focus on football instep kicking, and may not fully generalize to futsal due to differences in ball characteristics and playing constraints. Futsal involves a smaller, heavier, and low-bounce ball played within a confined spatial environment, which imposes faster decision-making demands, shorter kicking preparation times, and distinct movement execution requirements compared to conventional football shooting. Consequently, there is limited evidence regarding how skill level influences futsal-specific biomechanical patterns, particularly among novice players.

This study specifically examined differences in joint kinematics, segmental angular velocities, and ball velocity between trained and novice futsal players during maximal instep shooting tasks. The study focused on selected lower-limb and trunk kinematic variables, including knee flexion angle, torso inclination, elbow positioning, and segmental angular velocities, to better understand how training experience influences movement organization and shooting performance. Although it is generally expected that trained athletes outperform novice players, the scientific value of comparing these groups lies not merely in confirming performance superiority, but in elucidating the underlying biomechanical mechanisms that differentiate skilled from unskilled execution.

Previous studies have primarily emphasized outcome-based measures such as ball velocity and shooting accuracy, while providing limited insight into how proximal joint kinematics and segmental movement patterns contribute to shooting performance in futsal-specific contexts. In particular, differences in joint angular displacement, segmental angular velocity, and movement organization characteristics between trained and novice futsal players have not been comprehensively investigated. Therefore, the present study aimed to provide a more detailed biomechanical characterization of skilled futsal shooting by identifying movement patterns associated with higher shooting performance. It was hypothesized that trained athletes would demonstrate more mechanically efficient movement patterns, characterized by more effective joint positioning and higher angular velocities, resulting in greater ball velocity compared to novice players.

Materials and methods

Participants

Forty male participants aged 18–24 years were recruited using stratified random sampling and divided into trained (n = 20) and novice (n = 20) groups based on futsal skill level. Trained participants were university-level athletes with ≥ 5 years of structured training, regular participation in organized futsal training sessions at least three times per week, and competitive experience in regional or inter-university tournaments during the previous two years, while novices had no formal futsal or football experience and had never participated in organized futsal competitions or structured technical training programs. All were right-foot dominant, free from lower limb musculoskeletal injuries in the past six months, and had no medical conditions affecting performance.

An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power version 3.1.9.7 (Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany) confirmed that 20 participants per group could detect a medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.7) with 80% power at α = 0.05. The analysis indicated a minimum required sample size of 34 participants, therefore the inclusion of 40 participants was considered sufficient to achieve adequate statistical power for detecting between-group biomechanical differences. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (Ethics Committee) of Wuhan Sports University (Approval No: WHSU 2022123). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to participation.

Anthropometrics and physical performance

Participants’ height, weight, and body mass index (BMI) were measured using a stadiometer and calibrated scale14. Agility was assessed via a 10-m shuttle run (six shuttles, total 60 m), recording the fastest of two trials15,16. Lower-limb power was measured with a manual vertical jump using the wall reach method, calculating the difference between standing reach and maximal jump height17,18. Muscular endurance was evaluated with 60-second push-up and sit-up tests, counting correctly executed repetitions19,20. Posterior chain flexibility was assessed with the sit-and-reach test, using the best of two trials21,22. All tests were performed under standardized conditions and supervision to ensure proper technique, providing data to interpret kinematic and biomechanical differences between trained athletes and novices.

Procedures

Participants completed a standardized 10-minute dynamic warm-up, including 3 min of jogging, dynamic stretching, and light ball touches, supervised by a certified trainer to minimize injury risk. Participants wore standardized fitted sportswear, including shorts and short-sleeved shirts, along with indoor futsal shoes. To enhance anatomical landmark visibility during motion capture, visible external markers were placed over key joint landmarks. Shooting trials were conducted on an indoor futsal court under controlled environmental conditions. All participants used the same official size-4 futsal ball throughout the testing session. Standardized maximal instep shots were specifically selected because the instep technique is widely used for powerful shooting actions in futsal and provides a reproducible movement pattern suitable for controlled biomechanical comparison across participants.

Each participant performed 10 maximal instep shots from a fixed distance of 6 m toward a target, which was positioned at the center of the goal to standardize shooting direction and target location, with the five best trials selected for analysis to represent optimal performance23,24. Only successful shots that reached the predefined target area were considered valid for analysis. The five best trials were selected based on a combination of shooting accuracy and peak ball velocity to ensure that the analyzed trials reflected both effective and biomechanically optimal shooting performance. Participants performed the shots using a consistent approach angle and natural run-up strategy to minimize movement variability between trials. This approach was adopted to capture each participant’s maximal performance capability while reducing variability associated with unsuccessful or technically suboptimal attempts. However, it is acknowledged that selecting only the best trials may introduce selection bias and may not fully reflect overall performance consistency. Verbal instructions emphasized maximal effort and natural technique without defensive pressure, aiming to enhance measurement consistency and the reliability of kinematic data.

Camera setup

Three digital cameras were strategically positioned around the athlete: one on the left, one on the right, and one at the rear. The left and right cameras recorded the shooting movement from the lateral plane (2D sagittal) of each side of the body, allowing measurement of key kinematic angles such as knee and hip flexion-extension. The lateral cameras were positioned perpendicular to the primary sagittal plane of movement to minimize out-of-plane distortion during shooting execution. The rear camera was not used for posterior landmark analysis, but rather to verify body orientation, ensure movement symmetry, and validate the positions of landmarks captured by the lateral cameras. All cameras were positioned approximately 3 m from the shooting area and mounted on tripods at a height of approximately 1.0 m to maintain a consistent field of view and minimize perspective distortion during data collection. Participants performed all shooting trials using a standardized shooting direction and approach pathway to reduce excessive rotational movement and maintain consistency within the sagittal plane during data acquisition. With cameras capable of recording at 240 fps, this setup allowed for accurate capture of fast movements, and although the analysis was performed in 2D, kinematic measurements remained precise and consistent while minimizing potential errors due to perspective distortion or body misalignment.

The high frame rate was selected to reduce motion blur and enable accurate frame-by-frame analysis of rapid lower-limb movements during ball contact and follow-through phases. A visual representation of the camera setup used to capture the futsal shooting motion is provided in Fig. 1. A 2D sagittal-plane approach was selected because the primary kinematic variables examined in the present study, including knee flexion, torso inclination, and segmental angular velocities, predominantly occurred within the sagittal plane during instep shooting. These variables primarily represented sagittal-plane dominant movements, which are considered appropriate and sufficiently robust for 2D kinematic analysis during ballistic kicking tasks. Previous biomechanical studies have demonstrated that high-speed 2D video analysis provides valid and reliable measurements for lower-limb kinematics during ballistic kicking tasks. In addition, 2D high-speed video analysis offers a practical and accessible approach for applied sport settings while maintaining adequate measurement precision for sagittal-plane movement analysis. Trials demonstrating excessive body rotation, marker occlusion, or substantial out-of-plane movement were excluded from analysis to further minimize measurement error associated with 2D motion analysis.

Kinematic variables

Nine kinematic variables were analyzed to quantify the biomechanical characteristics of futsal shooting, including right knee flexion during the backswing, left knee flexion during foot placement, torso inclination at ball contact, left and right elbow flexion, peak angular velocities of the knee, hip, and torso, and peak ball velocity. The backswing phase was defined as the interval between toe-off of the kicking limb and initiation of the forward swing motion, whereas the foot-placement phase referred to the instant when the support foot contacted the ground immediately prior to ball contact. Ball contact was identified as the frame in which the kicking foot first visually contacted the ball during the forward swing phase. Right knee flexion angle was defined as the relative angle between the thigh and shank segments during the backswing phase, while left knee flexion angle was measured during support-foot placement prior to ball contact. Torso inclination was defined as the angle between the trunk segment and the vertical axis in the sagittal plane at the moment of ball contact. Left and right elbow flexion angles were calculated as the relative angles between the upper arm and forearm segments during the shooting motion. Joint angles and segmental velocities were calculated from changes in body segment positions during the movement, with all angular measurements obtained in the sagittal plane using frame-by-frame analysis in Dartfish motion analysis software.

Flexion movements were treated as positive angular values within the sagittal-plane coordinate system. Peak angular velocities of the knee, hip, and torso were calculated from the rate of angular displacement over time and expressed in degrees per second (°/s). Peak angular velocity was defined as the highest instantaneous angular velocity recorded during the kicking motion. Peak ball velocity was calculated from frame-by-frame ball displacement relative to the calibrated spatial reference and expressed in meters per second (m/s). No additional digital filtering or smoothing procedures were applied because the high-frame-rate recordings (240 fps) and frame-by-frame tracking approach provided stable kinematic trajectories for analysis. These variables were selected for their relevance to segmental movement patterns and shooting performance, enabling clear comparisons between trained athletes and novices and providing insight into skill-related kinematic differences.

Motion analysis

Recorded video data were analyzed using Dartfish ProSuite version 9.0 motion analysis software (Dartfish SA, Fribourg, Switzerland). The software enabled frame-by-frame tracking of body segments and ball trajectories to extract key kinematic variables. Cameras were calibrated using a 1.00 m × 1.00 m grid with 10 cm divisions as the spatial reference25,26, allowing Dartfish to automatically compute a pixel-to-meter conversion factor of approximately 0.0025 m/pixel. The calibration frame was positioned within the same movement plane and approximate height as the kicking action to minimize scaling error and reduce potential out-of-plane distortion during spatial reconstruction. Calibration accuracy was verified through Dartfish’s internal validation, with the RMS error maintained below 1% of the calibration frame, demonstrating high precision in spatial reconstruction27–29.

All video recordings were analyzed under identical calibration and camera conditions to ensure consistency and reproducibility across trials. Only trials with clear anatomical landmark visibility and minimal out-of-plane body deviation were included for subsequent kinematic analysis. The primary variables analyzed, including knee flexion, torso inclination, and segmental angular velocities, predominantly represented sagittal-plane movements considered appropriate for high-speed 2D kinematic assessment during instep kicking tasks. No additional digital filtering or smoothing procedures were applied because the high-frame-rate recordings (240 fps) and frame-by-frame tracking approach provided stable kinematic trajectories for analysis. This approach provided reliable, reproducible, and valid data for subsequent biomechanical analyses.

The anatomical landmarks

Anatomical landmarks were manually digitized in the sagittal plane on a frame-by-frame basis using Dartfish motion analysis software. The selected landmarks acromion, lateral humeral epicondyle, radial styloid, greater trochanter, lateral femoral epicondyle, and lateral malleolus were chosen due to their clear visual definition and relevance for calculating segmental orientations and joint kinematics during the shooting task. All digitization procedures were performed by a trained evaluator experienced in motion analysis, following a standardized protocol for anatomical landmark identification30–32. To ensure consistency, each trial was digitized twice on separate occasions, and the mean values were used for subsequent analysis.

To quantify digitization consistency, intra- and inter-rater reliability were evaluated using intraclass correlation coefficients based on absolute agreement models. The results demonstrated high reliability across all markers, with intra-rater ICC values ranging from 0.90 to 0.95 and inter-rater ICC values from 0.88 to 0.93. In accordance with established biomechanical reliability thresholds30,33, these values indicate good to excellent agreement, supporting the reliability of the digitization procedure. In addition, quality-control measures, including repeated digitization and consistent frame selection, were implemented to minimize potential measurement error associated with manual landmark tracking. Statistical analysis

Data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection (histograms and Q–Q plots) to determine the appropriateness of parametric versus non-parametric tests. Peak ball velocity was determined using Dartfish by identifying the highest velocity along the ball’s trajectory based on frame-by-frame displacement and calibrated distance markers, with the average of five trials used for analysis. Independent samples t-tests were applied for normally distributed variables, while the Mann–Whitney U test was used for non-normal variables. Effect sizes were reported systematically Cohen’s d for parametric comparisons and rank-biserial correlation (r) for non-parametric comparisons providing both statistical and practical significance. To examine the predictive contribution of kinematic variables to shooting performance, multiple linear regression analysis was conducted with ball velocity as the dependent variable. Independent variables were selected based on their theoretical relevance in kicking biomechanics and were further screened using bivariate correlation analysis to identify variables significantly associated with ball velocity.

All selected variables were initially entered into the model using the enter method. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF) to ensure model stability. Standardized regression coefficients (β), coefficient of determination (R²), and associated significance levels (p-values) were used to evaluate the relative contribution of each predictor. In addition, regression assumptions, including normality of residuals and homoscedasticity, were assessed through visual inspection of residual plots. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). This approach enabled the identification of key kinematic factors influencing ball velocity, with particular attention to variables reflecting lower-limb angular velocity and multisegment movement patterns.

Results

Both groups were comparable in terms of age, body weight, and BMI. Athletes were slightly taller than novices. Significant differences were observed in physical performance variables. Athletes completed the agility test approximately 7% faster than novices. Vertical jump height was about 28% higher in athletes, while upper-body endurance (push-up performance) was roughly 35% greater. Core muscular endurance (sit-up performance) was nearly 30% higher in athletes, and flexibility (sit-and-reach) was moderately better. These results highlight the overall superior physical fitness of trained athletes compared to novice players.

The analysis of futsal shooting kinematics revealed clear differences between trained athletes and novice players (Table 2). Trained athletes demonstrated greater joint flexion at key phases of the kicking motion, particularly in knee positioning during the backswing and foot placement phases. In addition, trunk inclination and upper-limb positioning were more pronounced in trained athletes, indicating more coordinated whole-body involvement during shooting. Peak angular velocities of the knee, hip, and torso were consistently higher in trained athletes, reflecting more effective segmental acceleration. Ball velocity was also substantially greater in trained athletes, indicating superior shooting performance. Across most variables, significant between-group differences were observed (p < 0.05), with large effect sizes, highlighting meaningful biomechanical distinctions between skill levels. All variables met the assumptions of normality; therefore, parametric tests were applied for group comparisons.

A multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to examine the contribution of selected kinematic variables to ball velocity (Table 3). Scatter plots illustrating the relationships between selected kinematic variables and ball velocity are presented in Fig. 3. Independent variables were selected based on their theoretical relevance in kicking biomechanics and their association with ball velocity identified through preliminary correlation analyses. Other candidate variables were initially considered but were excluded due to multicollinearity or lack of significant association. The overall regression model was statistically significant (R² = 0.62, F(3, 36) = 19.57, p < 0.001), indicating that the included variables explained a substantial proportion of variance in ball velocity. Visual inspection of Fig. 3 indicates a clear positive association between knee-related kinematic variables and ball velocity, whereas weaker or less consistent relationships are observed for upper-limb and trunk variables. Multicollinearity diagnostics indicated acceptable levels (VIF < 5 for all variables), confirming model stability. Peak knee angular velocity emerged as the strongest predictor of ball velocity (β = 0.78, p < 0.001), demonstrating a significant positive contribution to shooting performance. In contrast, peak hip angular velocity (β = 0.24, p = 0.125) and peak torso angular velocity (β = 0.19, p = 0.162) did not significantly predict ball velocity after accounting for shared variance among predictors.

Discussion

The present study aimed to compare the biomechanical characteristics of futsal shooting between trained athletes and novice players, with a specific focus on joint kinematics and their contribution to ball velocity. The findings demonstrated distinct differences in preparatory and segmental movement patterns between trained athletes and novice players, particularly in knee flexion, torso inclination, and angular velocity characteristics associated with futsal shooting performance under constrained movement conditions. Specifically, trained athletes demonstrated greater right knee flexion during the backswing phase (93.1° vs. 81.2°), higher peak knee angular velocity (578.2°/s vs. 469.5°/s), and substantially greater ball velocity (21.7 m/s vs. 15.8 m/s), suggesting the use of more effective preparatory movement organization and segmental acceleration strategies during shooting execution. Rather than merely demonstrating superior performance outcomes in trained players, the present findings provide insight into how futsal-specific movement constraints may shape the organization of sagittal-plane shooting mechanics, particularly through the interaction between preparatory knee flexion, trunk positioning, and rapid lower-limb acceleration. Importantly, these results extend previous futsal and soccer biomechanics research by highlighting the preparatory and coordinative mechanisms underlying effective shot execution, rather than focusing solely on distal outcomes such as foot speed or shot accuracy.

From a kinematic perspective, trained athletes exhibited more optimized joint movements across multiple phases of the shooting action. In particular, greater knee flexion during the backswing phase may contribute to enhancing elastic energy storage and more effective movement sequencing during the subsequent forward swing. The significantly greater right knee flexion observed in trained athletes (93.1° ± 4.7°) compared with novice players (81.2° ± 6.1°) suggests that trained players may utilize a larger preparatory movement range to facilitate more efficient proximal-to-distal segmental sequencing during the kicking motion. This finding aligns with the proximal-to-distal sequencing theory of ballistic movements, whereby energy is progressively transferred from larger proximal segments to smaller distal segments to maximize endpoint velocity34–37. Within the futsal context, where shooting actions are frequently performed under reduced spatial and temporal constraints, greater preparatory knee flexion may support more compact and rapid force-generation strategies during instep shooting execution. By demonstrating significantly greater preparatory knee flexion in trained futsal athletes, the present study expands existing models of kicking mechanics by emphasizing the importance of the pre-swing phase in determining shot velocity.

In contrast, novice players displayed reduced knee flexion during the backswing, which may reflect less optimized movement patterns and limited ability to effectively utilize the preparatory phase of the kicking motion. Previous studies have shown that less experienced athletes often adopt mechanically simplified movement strategies to maintain balance and control, even at the expense of performance efficiency38–41. The “stiff-leg” kicking pattern observed in novice players may reflect less optimized movement patterns during shot execution, although neuromuscular mechanisms were not directly assessed in the present study. The lower peak knee angular velocity observed in novice players (469.5°/s ± 55.8°) may indicate reduced efficiency in transferring momentum from proximal to distal segments during the forward swing phase, which may partially explain their lower ball velocity outcomes. This constrained angular momentum generation is likely associated with their lower ball velocity outcomes.

Torso inclination at the moment of ball contact further differentiated trained athletes from novices and was associated with shot effectiveness. Greater forward torso inclination may enhance postural stability, facilitates pelvic alignment, and promotes efficient transfer of rotational energy from the trunk to the kicking limb42–44. In the present study, trained athletes demonstrated greater torso inclination at ball contact (83.9° ± 3.9°) compared with novice players (76.5° ± 5.6°), suggesting more effective trunk positioning during force transmission. In futsal-specific shooting situations characterized by limited preparation time and confined playing space, forward trunk positioning may assist players in maintaining movement compactness and directional control during rapid shooting execution. The present findings support the notion that trunk orientation plays a central role in directing force along the intended ball trajectory. Novice players, who exhibited less pronounced forward inclination, may experience disruptions in trunk–pelvis–limb coupling, thereby limiting the effective transfer of trunk-generated momentum despite adequate lower-limb movement. However, because the present study employed two-dimensional sagittal-plane analysis, interpretations related to whole-body kinetic-chain coordination should be interpreted cautiously, as transverse-plane trunk and pelvic mechanics were not directly quantified. These results reinforce the integrative role of the trunk within the kinetic chain during high-velocity futsal shooting actions.

Beyond lower-limb mechanics, upper-body kinematic positioning, particularly bilateral elbow alignment, may contribute to shooting performance by supporting trunk stability and overall movement control during the shot. Previous research in professional futsal has demonstrated that elite players rely heavily on precise whole-body coordination to optimize dominant-limb shooting accuracy and consistency44–46. Minimal inter-limb variability observed in high-level futsal players has been interpreted as reflecting refined movement organization and improved postural control during complex kicking actions. In the present study, trained athletes demonstrated greater left and right elbow flexion angles compared to novice players, which may indicate differences in upper-body movement organization during shooting execution rather than a direct mechanical contribution of elbow positioning itself. Given that the present study employed two-dimensional sagittal-plane analysis without direct assessment of upper-body kinetics or transverse-plane trunk rotation, the specific biomechanical role of elbow flexion in trunk stabilization or force transfer cannot be conclusively determined. Although upper-limb kinematics are often overlooked in kicking analyses, the present findings suggest that upper-body positioning may reflect broader differences in whole-body coordination and movement control between trained and novice futsal players.

Crucially, trained athletes demonstrated significantly higher angular velocities at the knee, hip, and torso, indicating more effective activation of the kinetic chain. Peak knee angular velocity was approximately 23% higher in trained athletes than in novice players, while peak hip angular velocity was approximately 21% greater, indicating more rapid segmental acceleration and more effective momentum transfer during the kicking sequence. These results are consistent with futsal and soccer biomechanics literature identifying joint angular velocity and foot speed as primary determinants of ball velocity5,47–49. Notably, regression analyses in soccer have shown that knee extension velocity alone can explain a substantial proportion of variance in ball speed7,47. The present regression analysis further demonstrated that knee angular velocity significantly predicted ball velocity (R² = 0.62), indicating that approximately 62% of the variance in shooting velocity was associated with knee angular velocity. This finding reinforces the biomechanical importance of rapid knee extension and effective segmental sequencing during high-velocity futsal shooting. The present findings corroborate and extend these observations by confirming that knee angular velocity remains a dominant predictor of ball velocity within the unique, time-constrained context of futsal, where rapid execution and reduced preparatory time place heightened demands on movement control and temporal precision, rather than directly measured neuromuscular efficiency.

Anthropometric and physical performance differences further illuminate the biomechanical advantages observed in trained players. Although both groups were comparable in stature and body mass index, trained athletes exhibited superior agility, explosive lower-limb power, core endurance, and flexibility. For example, vertical jump performance was approximately 28% greater in trained athletes, while agility performance was approximately 7% faster, suggesting enhanced lower-limb power generation and movement efficiency that may contribute to more effective shooting mechanics. These attributes are likely associated with more refined shooting mechanics by enabling stronger plant-leg forces, improved trunk control, and faster segmental sequencing. Previous studies have reported that professional futsal starters outperform non-starters in reactive strength index, countermovement jump height, and futsal-specific agility tests, with these qualities strongly associated with kicking speed and shooting effectiveness50–53. Collectively, these findings suggest that physical performance capacities may serve as foundational enablers of efficient biomechanical execution rather than isolated determinants of shooting success.

From a practical standpoint, the observed kinematic differences between groups may be modifiable through targeted coaching and training interventions. Coaching cues emphasizing preparatory knee flexion (“sink into your knee”), center-of-mass lowering, and forward torso inclination (“lean into the ball”) may help improve segmental alignment and momentum transfer in kicking-based tasks. Given the rapid and spatially constrained nature of futsal shooting, training strategies that emphasize compact preparatory mechanics and rapid lower-limb acceleration may be particularly beneficial for improving shooting execution in developing players. When combined with targeted strength development particularly eccentric lower-limb strength and core stability such cues may support the acquisition of efficient shooting mechanics in developing futsal players.

Despite these contributions, several methodological limitations should be acknowledged. The use of two-dimensional video analysis restricts the assessment to sagittal-plane kinematics and may introduce measurement error, thereby limiting insight into transverse and frontal-plane movements critical for rotational mechanics and hip–pelvis interactions. The absence of electromyography precludes direct assessment of neuromuscular activation patterns, while the lack of force-plate data constrains analysis of ground reaction forces and plant-foot kinetics. Additionally, the present analysis focused primarily on discrete kinematic variables and peak values, which may not fully capture temporal biomechanical variations across the entire kicking cycle. Waveform-based analytical approaches, such as Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM1D), may provide more comprehensive phase-specific insight into movement coordination and should be considered in future investigations.

In addition, the experimental protocol involved a controlled shooting task performed from a fixed position without defensive pressure, which does not fully reflect the dynamic and interactive nature of real match situations. In competitive futsal, shooting actions are influenced by opponent pressure, movement variability, and time constraints; therefore, the ecological validity of the present findings may be limited, and caution is warranted when generalizing the results to in-game performance. Furthermore, the selection of the five best trials for analysis may introduce selection bias and potentially overestimate performance outcomes, as it does not fully capture within-subject variability across all attempts. While this approach allows for the examination of optimal movement execution, it may not reflect typical performance consistency under real conditions. Future studies should incorporate three-dimensional motion capture, EMG, and force-platform technologies to provide a more comprehensive understanding of biomechanical and neuromuscular mechanisms underpinning effective futsal shooting. Longitudinal intervention studies are also warranted to establish causal links between targeted biomechanical training, improvements in kinematic efficiency, and enhanced ball-velocity outcomes.

Conclusion

This study compared futsal shooting mechanics between trained athletes and novice players, focusing on knee flexion, torso inclination, elbow positioning, segmental angular velocities, and ball velocity. Trained athletes generally demonstrated higher segmental angular velocities and greater ball velocity, patterns that are commonly associated with more effective shooting performance in futsal, likely reflecting differences in physical capacities and motor control strategies. These observations are descriptive and should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of biomechanical superiority. Interpretations should also consider methodological limitations, such as the 2D analysis and absence of EMG and force measurements. Consequently, training recommendations should be applied cautiously, and future studies incorporating kinetic and neuromuscular assessments are needed to clarify the functional significance of these biomechanical differences.
