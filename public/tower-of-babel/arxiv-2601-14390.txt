Entanglement scaling and dynamics in the Sauter-Schwinger effect
S. Mahesh Chandran, Karthik Rajeev

Abstract
In quantum field theory, entanglement entropy under spatial bipartitioning serves as a powerful information-theoretic probe of quantum correlations. In this work, we present the first comprehensive numerical study of the dynamical evolution and geometric scaling of entanglement entropy in a nonperturbative, strong-field QED setting — specifically, in the context of the Sauter–Schwinger effect. While the weak-field regime is dominated by area-law states, we show that the entanglement entropy undergoes a transition from area-law to a volume-law scaling for certain strong-field regimes in the pulse-profile parameter space — signaling a fundamental shift in the underlying correlation structure induced by nonperturbative pair production. For intermediate regimes, the scaling is a power-law that interpolates between area- and volume-law behavior. Finally, we provide interpretations based on the behavior of the low-energy pair-creation spectrum and discuss how these insights could inform future investigations of related phenomena.

Introduction

). Similarly, in quantum electrodynamics (QED), both in vacuum and under strong-field conditions, systematic studies of entanglement generated in physical processes are increasingly being explored

;
Fedida and Serafini 2023
;

;

;

;

Recent advances in high-intensity laser technology are creating new opportunities to probe nonperturbative quantum phenomena in extreme electromagnetic fields. Next-generation ultra-high-intensity lasers are expected to approach the critical Schwinger field strength, opening unprecedented possibilities to study strong-field QED effects, including vacuum pair production

Among various approaches that have proven useful for quantifying entanglement
, the entanglement entropy under spatial bipartition stands out as a particularly powerful tool for characterizing the quantum correlations of vacuum and excited states in quantum field theory (QFT). For local dynamics, it is well known that the leading contribution to the entanglement entropy of vacuum states typically scales with the area of the boundary separating the subsystems. Historically, this area law behavior was first identified in QFT

;
Srednicki 1993
;
Calabrese and Cardy 2004
;
Casini and Huerta 2009

While typicality is often formalized by averaging the entanglement entropy over all possible quantum states accessible under unitary evolution

;

;

;

;

;

poorly understood, with a systematic treatment still lacking.

In this work, we present the first comprehensive numerical investigation of the dynamical evolution
and geometric scaling of entanglement entropy in a nonperturbative strong-field QED setting — specifically, in the context of the Sauter–Schwinger effect. To this end, we consider the evolution of the vacuum state of scalar QED in the presence of homogeneous Sauter pulse of electric field, and
track the entanglement entropy of cylindrical subregions carved out by a spatial bipartition. We demonstrate that, for sufficiently strong background fields and large regions of the parameter space characterizing the pulse-profile, the entanglement entropy transitions from an area-law to volume-law scaling, signaling a
fundamental shift in the underlying quantum correlation structure indicative of typicality.

The paper is organized as follows — In

V

II
Primer on Gaussian states and entanglement

The dynamics of scalar-QED vacuum under the influence of a time-dependent electric field background can be fully described in terms of Gaussian states. The specific details of this description will be outlined in
III
. In this section, therefore, we provide a quick overview of some Gaussian-state essentials, including the basics of entanglement entropy under a bipartition of modes.

II.1
Multimode bosonic Gaussian states

A multimode bosonic system is associated with a tensor product Hilbert-space
{H}={H}_{\rm b}^{\otimes N}
, where
{H}_{\rm b}
is the Hilbert-space for a single bosonic oscillator. The system can be described by the quadrature field operators
\{\hat{\varphi}_{i},\hat{\pi}_{i}\}^{N}_{i=1}
that satisfy
[\hat{\varphi}_{i},\hat{\pi}_{j}]=i\delta_{ij}
and
[\hat{\varphi}_{i},\hat{\varphi}_{j}]=[\hat{\pi}_{i},\hat{\pi}_{j}]=0
. It is convenient to collect these operators into a
2N-
dimensional vector
\hat{\mathbf{Q}}
, with components
\hat{Q}_{i}=\hat{\varphi}_{i}
and
\hat{Q}_{i+N}=\hat{\pi}_{i}
.
The commutation relations for the quadrature field operators can then be represented concisely as follows:

[\hat{Q}_{i},\hat{Q}_{j}]=i\Omega_{ij}\,,\quad\mathbf{\Omega}=\begin{bmatrix}O&\mathbb{I}\\
-\mathbb{I}&O\end{bmatrix}\,,

(1)

where
\mathbf{\Omega}
is the symplectic matrix and
\mathbb{I}
is the
N\times N
identity matrix.

For any density matrix
\hat{\rho}
, one can define the Wigner characteristic function
Braunstein and van Loock 2005

\displaystyle{W}(\mathbf{q})={\rm Tr}\!\!\left[\!\hat{\rho}\,\exp\!\!\left(\!i\sum_{i,j=1}^{2N}\hat{Q}_{i}\,\Omega_{ij}\,q_{j}\!\right)\!\!\right]\,\,\,;\,\,\,\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N},

(2)

which provides an equivalent phase-space characterization of the quantum state described by
\hat{\rho}
. Gaussian states are defined as those whose Wigner characteristic function
\chi(\mathbf{q})
is Gaussian. For such states, the mean
\bar{\mathbf{Q}}={\rm Tr}[\mathbf{\hat{Q}}\hat{\rho}]
and the covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}
, defined as
Eisert et al. 2010
:

\Sigma_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}\left[\{\hat{Q}_{i}-\bar{Q}_{i},\hat{Q}_{j}-\bar{Q}_{j}\}\hat{\rho}\right]

(3)

contain the full information about the state.

It turns out that for the system we are about to consider in the paper
\bar{\mathbf{Q}}=\mathbf{0}
, so we shall be assuming that from here onward. This special class of
zero-mean
Gaussian states are fully characterized by just the covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}
, which can be conveniently organized into
Eisert et al. 2010
:

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\varphi\varphi}&\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\varphi\pi}\\
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\varphi\pi}^{T}&\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\pi\pi}\end{bmatrix}\,,

(4)

where the block matrices in
\mathbf{\Sigma}
are constructed from two-point correlation functions via

\displaystyle(\Sigma_{\varphi\varphi})_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\langle\{\hat{\varphi}_{i},\hat{\varphi}_{j}\}\rangle

(5a)

\displaystyle(\Sigma_{\varphi\pi})_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\langle\{\hat{\varphi}_{i},\hat{\pi}_{j}\}\rangle

(5b)

\displaystyle(\Sigma_{\pi\pi})_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\langle\{\hat{\pi}_{i},\hat{\pi}_{j}\}\rangle,

(5c)

where we use the notation
\left\langle...\right\rangle={\rm Tr}[...\hat{\rho}]
and we have used
\bar{\mathbf{Q}}=0
.

II.2
Entanglement entropy

For an arbitrary multimode
zero-mean
Gaussian state, the different modes of the system would generally be entangled. One approach to probe these inter-mode quantum correlations is to collect a set of modes, say
{D}_{in}=\{i|i\leq n<N\}
, to define an IN-subsystem. The idea is then to investigate the entanglement between the IN-subsystem and the rest of the system (OUT-subsystem).

The reduced density matrix
\hat{\rho}_{in}
of IN-subsystem will also be
zero-mean
Gaussian, characterized by the reduced covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}
, obtained simply from
3
by taking the
n\crossproduct n
sub-blocks of the
N\crossproduct N
matrices in
5
, such that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{\varphi\varphi}&\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{\varphi\pi}\\
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{\varphi\pi}^{T}&\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{\pi\pi}\end{bmatrix}\,,

(6a)

\displaystyle\left(\sigma_{\varphi\varphi},\sigma_{\pi\pi},\sigma_{\varphi\pi}\right)_{ij}

\displaystyle=\left(\Sigma_{\varphi\varphi},\Sigma_{\pi\pi},\Sigma_{\varphi\pi}\right)_{ij\in{D}_{in}}\,.

(6b)

The entanglement entropy
S
, which measures the entanglement between the IN- and the OUT-subsystems, is then defined as

\displaystyle S=-{\rm Tr}\left[\hat{\rho}_{in}\log(\hat{\rho}_{in})\right]\,.

(7)

The entanglement entropy can be computed from the symplectic eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}
, defined as the eigenvalues of the matrix
i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}}_{in}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}
, where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}}_{in}
is the symplectic matrix associated with the IN-subsystem. Specifically, consequent to Williamson’s theorem
Williamson 1936
, there exists a symplectic transformation
\mathbf{M}
such that

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}}\!=\!\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}\mathbf{M}^{T}\!=\!\begin{bmatrix}\textrm{diag}(\gamma_{1},..,\gamma_{n})&O\\
O&\textrm{diag}(\gamma_{1},..,\gamma_{n})\end{bmatrix},

(8)

where
{\rm diag}(...)
denotes a diagonal matrix and
\{\gamma_{k}\}
is the symplectic spectrum, obtained from the eigenvalues
\{\pm\gamma_{k}\}
of the matrix
i\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Omega}}_{in}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}
. Thus, the entanglement entropy finally reads

S=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Bigg[\left(\gamma_{k}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\log\left(\gamma_{k}+\frac{1}{2}\right)-\\
\left(\gamma_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\log\left(\gamma_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\Bigg]\,.

(9)

So far, our discussion has focused on systems with a finite number of bosonic modes. For applications to quantum field theory (QFT), the framework must be judiciously extended to encompass an infinite number of modes, i.e.,
N\rightarrow\infty
. In this continuum limit, modulo well-known caveats
Haag 1996
, the discrete mode indices
(i,j)
are naturally replaced by continuous field modes of the scalar field, characterized by the momentum vector (or, equivalently, by a set of angular momentum quantum numbers together with the radial energy). In the following section, we review the Sauter–Schwinger effect for a complex scalar field subject to a homogeneous Sauter pulse, working in a basis adapted to the cylindrical symmetry of the problem, and identify the natural mode labeling prescription that enables the application of the formalism outlined above.

III
Sauter-Schwinger effect and pairwise entanglement

We start by writing the Minkowski metric in the cylindrical coordinates as

\displaystyle ds^{2}=dt^{2}-dz^{2}-d\varrho^{2}-\varrho^{2}d\phi\,.

(10)

The Sauter-pulse background can then be described by the gauge potential

\displaystyle A(t)=-E_{0}\tau\left[1+\tanh{\left(\frac{t}{\tau}\right)}\right]dz

(11)

which gives rise to the electromagnetic field

\displaystyle F(t)=\frac{E_{0}}{\cosh^{2}\left(t/\tau\right)}dz\wedge dt

(12)

that describes a homogeneous electric field of magnitude
E_{0}
along the
z-
direction, and a temporal pulse width of the order
\tau
.

Before we move on to reviewing pair creation of a complex scalar field in this background, it is useful to briefly recall the dimensionless parameters that characterize the system. Denoting the mass and charge of the scalar particle by
\mu
and
e
, respectively, these parameters may be chosen as follows
Fedotov et al. 2023
:

•

Classical nonlinearity (intensity) parameter

\xi
:

\xi\equiv\frac{|eE_{0}|}{\mu\omega}=\frac{|eE_{0}|\tau}{\mu}\,.

(13)

The parameter
\xi
measures the work done by the electric field over a Compton wavelength in units of the characteristic field quantum energy.

•

Adiabaticity parameter

\eta
:

\eta\equiv\frac{\omega}{\mu}=\frac{1}{\mu\tau}\,.

(14)

This parameter quantifies how rapidly the external field varies compared to the Compton time. The adiabatic regime
\eta\ll 1
corresponds to slowly varying backgrounds, whereas
\eta\gtrsim 1
describes rapidly varying, nonadiabatic fields.

•

Quantum nonlinearity parameter

\chi
:

\chi=\frac{|eE_{0}|}{\mu^{2}}=\xi\,\eta\,.

(15)

In the adiabatic regime, the tunneling probability behaves as
P\propto e^{-\pi/\chi}
, being exponentially suppressed for
\chi\ll 1
and unsuppressed for
\chi\gtrsim 1
.

III.1
Mode functions and the asymptotic state

The Klein-Gordon equation for a complex scalar in an external electromagnetic background
A_{\mu}
takes the form

\displaystyle\left[\left(i\partial_{\mu}-eA_{\mu}\right)\left(i\partial^{\mu}-eA^{\mu}\right)-\mu^{2}\right]\Phi=0\,.

(16)

For the Sauter-pulse background in
11
, it is customary to choose a basis of solutions to the above equation parameterized by the spatial momentum
\mathbf{k}
. However, when studying the geometrical entanglement entropy, the symmetry of the system makes a cylindrical mode basis more appropriate.

To make the notation closer to that of the standard plane-wave basis, we shall use the short-hand
\mathbf{k}=(n,l,m)
and
\tilde{\mathbf{k}}\equiv(n,-l,-m)
, so that the positive/negative energy cylindrical modes, appropriate to asymptotic past, can be written as

\displaystyle{P}^{\pm}_{\mathbf{k}}(x)

\displaystyle={R}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}}(\mathbf{x})v^{\pm}_{\mathbf{k}}(t)\,,

(17a)

\displaystyle{R}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle\equiv{N}_{\mathbf{k}}e^{i\frac{2\pi l}{L}z}e^{im\phi}J_{m}\!\left(\!\frac{{{j}}_{mn}\varrho}{R}\!\right)\,,

(17b)

where
m,l\in\mathbb{Z}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
,
J_{m}(x)
is the Bessel function,
{{j}}_{mn}
denotes the
n^{\rm th}
root of
J_{m}(x)
, the normalization
{N}_{\mathbf{k}}=2\sqrt{\pi}/|J_{m+1}({{j}}_{mn})|
and
v^{\pm}_{\mathbf{k}}(t)
satisfies the time-dependent harmonic oscillator equation

\displaystyle\ddot{v}^{\pm}_{\mathbf{k}}(t)+\omega_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}(t)\,v^{\pm}_{\mathbf{k}}(t)=0\,,

(18)

with the frequency given by

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}(t)=\kappa_{nm}^{2}+\mu^{2}+\left(\frac{2\pi l}{L}+eA_{z}(t)\right)^{2}\,.

(19)

where,
\kappa_{nm}={{j}}_{mn}/R
. For the Sauter pulse, the modes functions appropriate to the asymptotic past — satisfying the boundary condition
v^{\pm}_{\mathbf{k}}(t\sim-\infty)\sim e^{\mp i\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(-\infty)t}
— can be solved for exactly and are given by
v^{+}_{\mathbf{k}}(t)=v_{\mathbf{k}}(t)
and
v^{-}_{\mathbf{k}}(t)=v^{*}_{\mathbf{k}}(t)
, with
Kim et al. 2008
:

v_{\mathbf{k}}=(1+e^{\frac{2t}{\tau}})^{\frac{1}{2}+i\lambda}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}}^{-},\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}}^{+};\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}};-e^{\frac{2t}{\tau}}\right)\frac{e^{-i\omega^{-}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}}t}}{\sqrt{2\omega^{-}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}}}},

(20)

where we define:

\displaystyle\omega^{\pm}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\rightarrow\pm\infty}\omega_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}}(t)

(21a)

\displaystyle\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}}^{\pm}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{i}{2}\left(\omega^{-}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}}\pm\omega^{+}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}}\right)\tau+i\lambda

(21b)

\displaystyle\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}}

\displaystyle=1-i\omega^{-}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}}\tau

(21c)

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\xi^{2}}{\eta^{2}}-\frac{1}{4}}\,.

(21d)

The modes satisfy appropriate completeness and orthonormality relations as described in Appendix
A
.

The complex scalar field operator can be expanded in the above basis to get

\displaystyle\hat{\Phi}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\left[b_{\mathbf{k}}{P}^{+}_{\mathbf{k}}(x)+d^{\dagger}_{\tilde{\mathbf{k}}}{P}^{-}_{\mathbf{k}}(x)\right]\,,

(22a)

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{{V}}\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{l=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\,,

(22b)

where
{V}=\pi R^{2}L
is the volume of the system. The creation/annihilation operators satisfy

\displaystyle[b_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}},b^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}}]

\displaystyle=[d_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}},d^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}}]={V}\delta_{mm^{\prime}}\delta_{ll^{\prime}}\delta_{nn^{\prime}}\equiv{V}\delta_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}

(23)

with all the other commutators vanishing. One then defines the
in-
vacuum
\ket{\rm in}
as the state annihilated by
\{b_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}},d_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}}\}
, for all choices of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}
.

\displaystyle b_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}}\ket{\rm in}=d_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}}\ket{\rm in}=0\quad,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}\,.

(24)

Having established the basic framework for quantizing a complex scalar field in a Sauter pulse background, we now turn to the discussion of pair creation and related aspects.

III.2
Particle Number

The scalar field can equivalently be expanded in terms of the asymptotic future modes,
{F}^{\pm}_{\mathbf{k}}(x)
, given by

\displaystyle{F}^{\pm}_{\mathbf{k}}(x)={R}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})\,u^{\pm}_{\mathbf{k}}(t)\,,

(25)

where
u^{\pm}_{\mathbf{k}}(t)
also solves
18
but with the boundary condition
u^{\pm}_{\mathbf{k}}(t\sim\infty)\sim e^{\mp i\omega^{+}_{\mathbf{k}}t}
. The scalar field operator then takes the equivalent form

\displaystyle\hat{\Phi}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\left[B_{\mathbf{k}}{F}^{+}_{\mathbf{k}}(x)+D^{\dagger}_{\tilde{\mathbf{k}}}{F}^{-}_{\mathbf{k}}(x)\right]

(26)

with the set of operators
\{B_{\mathbf{k}},B^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}},D_{\mathbf{k}},D^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}}\}
satisfying the same commutation relations as in
23
. The
out-
vacuum
\ket{\rm out}
as the state annihilated by
\{B_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}},D_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}}\}
, for all choices of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}
.

\displaystyle B_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}}\ket{\rm out}=D_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}}\ket{\rm in}=0\quad,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}\,.

(27)

Since both the past modes (
{P}^{\pm}_{\mathbf{k}}
) and future modes (
{F}^{\pm}_{\mathbf{k}}
) independently form a complete basis, they are linearly related, such that

\displaystyle{P}^{+}_{\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{\mathbf{k}}{F}^{+}_{\mathbf{k}}+\beta_{\mathbf{k}}^{*}{F}^{-}_{\mathbf{k}}

(28a)

\displaystyle{P}^{-}_{\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{\mathbf{k}}^{*}{F}^{-}_{\mathbf{k}}+\beta_{\mathbf{k}}{F}^{+}_{\mathbf{k}}\,,

(28b)

where

\displaystyle\alpha_{\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(-i\omega^{+}_{\mathbf{k}}\tau)\Gamma(1-i\omega^{-}_{\mathbf{k}}\tau)}{\Gamma(\gamma^{-}_{\mathbf{k}})\Gamma(\gamma_{\mathbf{k}}-\gamma^{+}_{\mathbf{k}})}\sqrt{\frac{\omega^{+}_{\mathbf{k}}}{\omega^{-}_{\mathbf{k}}}}\,,

(29a)

\displaystyle\beta_{\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(-i\omega^{+}_{\mathbf{k}}\tau)\Gamma(1+i\omega^{-}_{\mathbf{k}}\tau)}{\Gamma(\gamma^{+*}_{\mathbf{k}})\Gamma(\gamma^{*}_{\mathbf{k}}-\gamma^{-*}_{\mathbf{k}})}\sqrt{\frac{\omega^{+}_{\mathbf{k}}}{\omega^{-}_{\mathbf{k}}}}\,.

(29b)

The creation/annihilation operators are, in turn, related via

\displaystyle B_{\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{\mathbf{k}}b_{\mathbf{k}}+\beta_{\mathbf{k}}d_{\tilde{\mathbf{k}}}^{\dagger};\quad D_{\tilde{\mathbf{k}}}^{\dagger}=\alpha^{*}_{\mathbf{k}}d_{\tilde{\mathbf{k}}}^{\dagger}+\beta^{*}_{\mathbf{k}}b_{\mathbf{k}}\,.

(30)

With the
out-
particle/antiparticle number operators defined, respectively, as

\displaystyle N^{+}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}}B^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}}B_{\mathbf{k}}\quad;\quad N^{-}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}}D^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}}D_{\mathbf{k}}\,.

(31)

The average number of particles produced is then given by

\displaystyle\langle{\rm in}|N^{\pm}|{\rm in}\rangle={V}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}|\beta_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}\equiv{V}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}n^{\pm}_{\mathbf{k}}

(32)

For the Sauter pulse, the spectrum
n_{\mathbf{k}}
takes the form

n^{+}_{\mathbf{k}}=n^{-}_{\mathbf{\tilde{k}}}=\frac{\cosh{[\pi(\omega^{-}_{\mathbf{k}}-\omega^{+}_{\mathbf{k}} )\tau]}+\cosh[2\pi\lambda]}{2\sinh[\pi\omega^{-}_{\mathbf{k}}\tau]\sinh[\pi\omega^{+}_{\mathbf{k}}\tau]}\,.

(33)

It is well known that in the adiabatic regime (
\eta\rightarrow 0
) and the limit of small transverse momentum for the outgoing particles the above spectrum reduces to that of the Schwinger effect
Gavrilov and Gitman 1996
:

n_{\mathbf{k}}^{+}\sim\exp\left[-\frac{\pi}{\chi}\left(1+\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\right)\right]\,.

(34)

Several works have examined possible thermal interpretations of the above spectrum. For example, in the case
\kappa=0
, it can be viewed as the Boltzmann factor of particles with energy
\mu
and temperature
\beta^{-1}=eE/\pi\mu

Ritus 1981
;
Müller et al. 1977
;
Gavrilov and Gitman 1996
. Evidently, this does not apply generally for
\kappa\neq 0
.

It turns out that for interpreting some of the results presented below, it is useful to consider a different set of limits, focusing on the IR sector (
\omega^{+}_{\bf k}\approx\mu
). For ease of analysis, we note that the transverse
physical
momenta are
p_{z}^{-}=k_{z}+2\mu\xi
and
p_{z}^{+}=k_{z}
, at asymptotic past and future, respectively. Now, in the strong field
\mu\xi\gg\omega_{\mathbf{k}}^{+}
and nonadiabatic
\mu\eta\gg\omega_{\mathbf{k}}^{+}
regime, the spectrum reduces as follows:

\displaystyle n_{\mathbf{k}}^{+}\approx

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta\omega_{\mathbf{k}}^{+}}\,,

(35a)

\displaystyle\beta^{-1}=

\displaystyle\frac{\mu\eta}{2\pi}\bigg[\coth{\left(\frac{2\pi\xi}{\eta}\right)}

(35b)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\cosh{\left(2\pi\sqrt{\frac{\xi^2}{\eta^2}-\frac{1}{4}}\right)}\csch{\left(\frac{2\pi\xi}{\eta}\right)}\bigg].

Although the overall spectrum is non-thermal, we see that it coincides with the IR tail of a Planckian distribution in the aforementioned regime. One may interpret this as a near-thermal occupation of the IR modes of the final state corresponding to a temperature
\beta^{-1}
. Depending on how the strong-field limit is taken relative to the nonadiabatic limit, the spectrum further reduces to the following limiting forms:

n_{\mathbf{k}}^{+}\to\begin{cases}\frac{\mu\xi}{2\omega_{\mathbf{k}}^{+}}&1\leq\frac{\omega_{\mathbf{k}}^{+}}{\mu}\ll\xi\ll\eta\quad\text{Regime A}\\
\frac{\mu\eta}{\pi\omega_{\mathbf{k}}^{+}}&1\leq\frac{\omega_{\mathbf{k}}^{+}}{\mu}\ll\eta\ll\xi\quad\text{Regime B}\end{cases}

(36)

We observe that in Regime A the spectrum is independent of
\eta
, while in Regime B it is independent of
\xi
— implying that upon fixing any one of these parameters and tuning up the other, the particle content in the IR sector asymptotes to a constant value. This also results in an “L-shaped” symmetry for the low-energy spectrum along the
(\xi,\eta)
parameter space. To better visualize these regimes, let us consider an “overlap parameter”
\zeta
between
n_{\mathbf{k}}(\omega)
and an arbitrary spectrum
n_{X}(\omega)
:

\zeta[n_{\mathbf{k}},n_{X}]=\frac{4n_{\mathbf{k}}n_{X}}{(n_{\mathbf{k}}+n_{X})^{2}},

(37)

where
\zeta=1
indicates complete spectral overlap. In
1
, we see regions in the
(\xi,\eta)
parameter space where the IR mode (
\omega_{\mathbf{k}}^{+}=\mu
) of the Sauter-Schwinger spectrum overlaps with the limiting forms of
36
corresponding to Regimes A and B.

Let us first construct such a basis with the past-modes and the corresponding operators:

\displaystyle\sqrt{{V}}\,\mathbf{Q}_{{P}}

\displaystyle=\{\hat{{\Phi}}_{b,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}},\hat{{\Phi}}_{d,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tilde{k}}}},\hat{{\Pi}}_{b,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}},\hat{{\Pi}}_{d,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tilde{k}}}}\}

\displaystyle\frac{\hat{{\Phi}}_{b,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}}}{\sqrt{\omega^{+}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}}}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\varphi\varphi}&\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\varphi\pi}\\

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{b,\mathbf{k}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{d,\mathbf{\tilde{k}}}

\displaystyle\quad P

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\det{2\mathbfs{\Sigma}_{b,\vec{k}}}}=1

\displaystyle(\Sigma_{\pi\pi})_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}}{2}\left[\cosh{2r_{\vec{k}}}-\sinh{2r_{\vec{k}}}\cos{2\vartheta_{\vec{k}}}\right],

where the squeezing parameter (

\sinh{r_{\vec{k}}}=\sqrt{n_{\mathbf{k}}}\,;\quad 2\vartheta_{\mathbf{k}}=\arg{(\alpha_{\mathbf{k}}\beta_{\mathbf{k}}^{*})}-2\omega_{\mathbf{k}}^{+}t\,

\displaystyle\sqrt{{V}}\,\mathbf{Q}_{{F}}

\displaystyle=\{\hat{{\Phi}}_{B,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}},\hat{{\Phi}}_{D,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tilde{k}}}},\hat{{\Pi}}_{B,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}},\hat{{\Pi}}_{D,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tilde{k}}}}\}\,,

(47)

where

\displaystyle\frac{\hat{{\Phi}}_{B,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}}}{\sqrt{\omega^{+}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}}}}

\displaystyle=u_{\mathbf{k}}^{+}\hat{B}_{\mathbf{k}}+u_{\mathbf{k}}^{-}\hat{B}_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\,;\,\sqrt{\omega^{+}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}}}\,\hat{{\Pi}}_{B,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}}=\dot{u}_{\mathbf{k}}^{+}\hat{B}_{\mathbf{k}}+\dot{u}_{\mathbf{k}}^{-}\hat{B}_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle\frac{\hat{{\Phi}}_{D,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tilde{k}}}}}{\sqrt{\omega^{+}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}}}}

\displaystyle=u_{\mathbf{k}}^{+}\hat{D}_{\mathbf{\tilde{k}}}+u_{\mathbf{k}}^{-}\hat{D}_{\mathbf{\tilde{k}}}^{\dagger}\,;\,\sqrt{\omega^{+}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}}}\,\hat{{\Pi}}_{D,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tilde{k}}}}=\dot{u}_{\mathbf{k}}^{+}\hat{D}_{\mathbf{\tilde{k}}}+\dot{u}_{\mathbf{k}}^{-}\hat{D}_{\mathbf{\tilde{k}}}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{F}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}\cosh{2r_{\vec{k}}}&C_{\mathbf{k}}&0&\tilde{C}_{\mathbf{k}}\\

\displaystyle=\sinh{2r_{\vec{k}}}\cos{2\vartheta_{\vec{k}}};\,\,\tilde{C}_{\mathbf{k}}=\sinh{2r_{\vec{k}}}\sin{2\vartheta_{\vec{k}}}

Here, the particle (
B,\mathbf{k}

D,\mathbf{\tilde{k}}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{B,\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{D,\mathbf{\tilde{k}}}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}\cosh{2r_{\vec{k}}}&0\\

\displaystyle P

III.4
Real/Imaginary Partition

From the previous section it is clear that the calculation of entanglement depends on how we partition the two-mode system using different quadrature bases. Let us now consider a third basis, that serves to partition the real and imaginary components of the field, i.e.,
\hat{\Phi}_{\mathbf{k}}=(\hat{\varphi}_{1}+i\hat{\varphi}_{2})/\sqrt{2}
and
\hat{\Pi}_{\mathbf{k}}=(\hat{\pi}_{1}+i\hat{\pi}_{2})/\sqrt{2}
. However, to make the covariance matrix elements dimensionless, we will instead choose to rescale the operators with an arbitrary length scale
\epsilon
across all
\mathbf{k}
-sectors:

\displaystyle\sqrt{{V}}\mathbf{Q}

\displaystyle=\{\epsilon^{-1/2}\hat{\varphi}_{1},\epsilon^{-1/2}\hat{\varphi}_{2},\epsilon^{1/2}\hat{\pi}_{1},\epsilon^{1/2}\hat{\pi}_{2}\}

(51)

In this basis, the covariance matrix takes the GHDM form similar to
43
:

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{\varphi\varphi}&\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{\varphi\pi}\\
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{\varphi\pi}&\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{\pi\pi}\end{bmatrix},

(52)

where the elements are related to those of
43
as follows:

\displaystyle(\tilde{\Sigma}_{\varphi\varphi})_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\Sigma_{\varphi\varphi})_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}}{\omega_{\mathbf{k}}^{+}\epsilon};\quad\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{\varphi\pi}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\varphi\pi};

(53a)

\displaystyle\quad(\tilde{\Sigma}_{\pi\pi})_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\omega_{\mathbf{k}}^{+}\epsilon(\Sigma_{\pi\pi})_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}

(53b)

The real/imaginary (or R/I in short) partitions themselves factorize into pure states, and are each described by the reduced covariance matrix:

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{R,I}=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{\varphi\varphi}&\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{\varphi\pi}\\
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{\varphi\pi}&\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{\pi\pi}\end{bmatrix}\,.

(54)

The R/I sectors remain unentangled throughout the evolution. This basis can be utilized when a new length scale is introduced into the system, for instance in the form of lattice spacing that helps regulate divergent spatial correlations of the quantum field. While pairwise entanglement for the particles produced in the final state can be calculated in the basis defined in
47
, we will see in the next section that the basis defined in
51
becomes natural in the calculation of entanglement between spatial subregions of the field.

Building on the analysis of pair creation in a Sauter pulse and the associated momentum-space entanglement, we now focus on entanglement between spatial subregions. In this section, we present the formalism for quantifying spatial entanglement in scalar QED and provide explicit calculations. We begin with the action for a charged scalar field coupled to an electromagnetic field specified by a vector potential
A^{\mu}
:

{S}=\int d^{4}x\left[(D^{*\nu}\Phi^{*})(D_{\nu}\Phi)-\mu^{2}|\Phi|^{2}\right]

(55)

where
D_{\mu}=\partial_{\mu}+ieA_{\mu}
denotes the covariant derivative. For a Sauter-Schwinger pulse along the z-direction, i.e.,
A_{\mu}=\delta^{z}_{\mu}A_{z}(t)
, the Lagrangian reduces to:

{L}=\int d^{3}\mathbf{x}\Big[|\dot{\Phi}|^{2}-|\nabla\Phi|^{2}+ieA_{z}(t)\left(\Phi^{*}\partial_{z}\Phi-\Phi\partial_{z}\Phi^{*}\right)\\
-\left(\mu^{2}+e^{2}A_{z}^{2}(t)\right)|\Phi|^{2}\Big]

(56)

To perform lattice-regularization and implement the standard techniques for calculating real-space entanglement
Bombelli et al. 1986
;
Srednicki 1993
, we consider a series of decompositions tailored to the problem at hand.

First, given the cylindrical symmetry of the system, it is convenient to perform a Fourier decomposition along the z-direction as follows:

\Phi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\varrho}},z,t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{l}\,\Phi_{l}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\varrho}},t)e^{\frac{2i\pi l}{L}z},

(57)

which gives us the following Lagrangian where the transverse modes have been decoupled:

\displaystyle{L}=\sum_{l}\int d^{2}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\varrho}}\,\left[|\dot{\Phi}_{l}|^{2}-|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\varrho}}}\Phi_{l}|^{2}-\Omega^{2}|\Phi_{l}|^{2}\right]

(58a)

\displaystyle\Omega^{2}=\mu^{2}+\left(\frac{2\pi l}{L}+eA_{z}\right)^{2}\,,

(58b)

where
\Omega(t)
takes the form of a time-dependent effective mass.

Second, we decompose the real and imaginary components of the complex scalar field as
\Phi_{l}=(\varphi_{1,l}+i\varphi_{2,l})/\sqrt{2}
, which factorizes the Lagrangian as
{L}=\sum_{i=1,2}{L}^{(i)}
where:

{L}^{(i)}=\sum_{l}\int\frac{d^{2}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\varrho}}}{2}\left[\dot{\varphi}_{i,l}^{2}-(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\varrho}}}\varphi_{i,l})^{2}-\Omega^{2}\varphi_{i,l}^{2}\right]

(59)

Each component Lagrangian
{L}^{(i)}
thus describes a real scalar field with an effective time-dependent mass, and all components obey identical equations of motion. Consequently, we can omit the component index
i
and account for the total entanglement entropy by doubling the contribution from a single component.

Third, we perform an angular decomposition exploiting the axial symmetry of the system:

\displaystyle\varphi_{l}(\varrho,\theta,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\tilde{\varphi}_{lm}(\varrho,t)\cos{m\theta}}{\sqrt{\pi\varrho(1+\delta_{m,0})}}

(60a)

\displaystyle\quad\dot{\varphi}_{l}(\varrho,\theta,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\dot{\tilde{\varphi}}_{lm}(\varrho,t)\cos{m\theta}}{\sqrt{\pi\varrho(1+\delta_{m,0})}}

(60b)

Integrating out the
\theta
component, the Lagrangian reduces to:

{L}=\sum_{lm}\int\frac{d\varrho}{2}\left[\dot{\tilde{\varphi}}_{lm}^{2}-\varrho\left\{\partial_{\varrho}\left(\frac{\tilde{\varphi}_{lm}}{\sqrt{\varrho}}\right)\right\}^{2}-\Omega^{2}\tilde{\varphi}_{lm}^{2}\right]

(61)

This reduction transforms the original 3+1 dimensional problem into a set of independent 1+1 dimensional radial systems labeled by
(l,m)
, with each mode capturing the dynamics along the radial direction, and the angular dependence fully encoded in
m
(see
3
). Leveraging this simplification, we shall now construct a lattice formulation that is far simpler than a full 3+1 dimensional lattice, enabling faster numerical computation of entanglement entropy for cylindrical spatial bipartitions.

For each
l
and
m
-mode, the field degrees of freedom can be discretized along the radial direction via

\displaystyle\varrho

\displaystyle\rightarrow\varrho_{j}=j\epsilon\quad;\quad 1\leq j\leq N_{\varrho}\,,

(62a)

\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}_{lm}(\varrho)

\displaystyle\rightarrow\tilde{\varphi}_{lm,j}\equiv\tilde{\varphi}_{lm}(\varrho_{j})\,,

(62b)

where
\epsilon
is the lattice spacing and

N_{\varrho}
sets the total number of radial lattice points, to obtain the following Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\sum_{lmj}\Bigg[\tilde{\pi}_{lm,j}^{2}+\left(j+\frac{1}{2}\right)\left\{\frac{\tilde{\varphi}_{lm,j}}{\sqrt{j}}-\frac{\tilde{\varphi}_{lm,j+1}}{\sqrt{j+1}}\right\}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad-\left\{\epsilon^{2}\Omega^{2}+\frac{m^{2}}{j^{2}}\right\}\tilde{\varphi}_{lm,j}^{2}\Bigg]\,.

(63)

To simplify further, we perform a canonical transformation
\tilde{\pi}_{lm,j}=\epsilon^{1/2}\pi_{lm,j}
and
\tilde{\varphi}_{lm,j}=\epsilon^{-1/2}\varphi_{lm,j}
, to arrive at a Hamiltonian describing a system of harmonic oscillators with nearest-neighbor coupling:

H=\sum_{lmj}\Bigg[\frac{\pi_{lm,j}^{2}}{2}+\frac{\left(j+\frac{1}{2}\right)}{2\epsilon^{2}}\left\{\frac{\varphi_{lm,j}}{\sqrt{j}}-\frac{\varphi_{lm,j+1}}{\sqrt{j+1}}\right\}^{2}\\
-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\Omega^{2}+\frac{m^{2}}{j^{2}\epsilon^{2}}\right\}\tilde{\varphi}_{lm,j}^{2}\Bigg]\,.

(64)

Our aim now is to calculate the entanglement entropy from the above Hamiltonian.

Being a symplectic invariant measure, the entanglement entropy for spatial bipartitions is independent of the choice of canonical field variables, whether
\{\tilde{\pi}_{lmj},\tilde{\varphi}_{lmj}\}
or
\{\pi_{lmj},\varphi_{lmj}\}
, i.e.
Grain and Vennin 2020
;
Chandran and Shankaranarayanan 2020
,

H=\epsilon^{-1}\tilde{H}\implies S=\tilde{S}\,.

(65)

It is however convenient to work with the dimensionless Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}=\epsilon H
for a number of reasons — first, since the system
\tilde{H}
is fully characterized by dimensionless parameters which now also include:

\tilde{\mu}=\mu\epsilon;\quad\tilde{t}=\frac{t}{\epsilon};\quad\tilde{\tau}=\frac{\tau}{\epsilon}\,,

(66)

corresponding to the dimensionless mass, time coordinate, and pulse width, respectively. This formulation allows all results to be expressed independently of a specific choice of
\epsilon
. Second, the UV divergence arising from
\epsilon^{-1}\to\infty
is
folded
into a zero-mode divergence in the rescaled Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}

Mallayya et al. 2014
;
Chandran and Shankaranarayanan 2020
, making the numerical simulations easier to handle and ensuring that dominant contributions to entanglement are always confined to the IR sector. Lastly, the covariance matrix elements are automatically dimensionless, and there is no ambiguity in the choice of quadrature basis
\mathbf{Q}
.

We also note that while entanglement entropy is UV-sensitive, the scaling law as such is independent of the details of regularization — thus making it possible for us to numerically extract the correct leading order behavior from simply a finite number of modes. In fact, for dense enough lattice-points, the framework adopted here can resolve up to subleading terms obtained via the replica trick
Jain et al. 2021
.

IV.1
Boundary conditions and Axial modes

As mentioned earlier, the Hamiltonian
H
takes the form of a system of coupled oscillators. In particular, by decomposing the (rescaled) Hamiltonian into each
(lm)
sector,
\tilde{H}=\sum_{lm}\tilde{H}_{lm}
, we have

\tilde{H}_{lm}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\varrho}}\left[\tilde{\pi}_{i}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\varrho}}K_{ij}(l,m,\tilde{t})\tilde{\varphi}_{i}\tilde{\varphi}_{j}\right],

(67)

where
K_{ij}
is the coupling matrix and the
(lm)
indices of the field variables have been suppressed for readability. The coupling matrix, in turn, can be separated into time-independent and time-dependent matrices as follows:

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}(l,m,\tilde{t})=\bar{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}(m)+\epsilon^{2}\Omega^{2}(l,\tilde{t})\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mathbb{I}}}\,,

(68)

where
\mathbb{I}
is the identity matrix. We see that
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}
and
\bar{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}
are simultaneously diagonalizable.

To obtain a well-defined matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}
(or, equivalently,
\bar{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}
) the system must be supplemented with appropriate boundary conditions for the discretized field variables. By imposing the Dirichlet boundary conditions at
\varrho=0
and
\varrho=R=(N_{\varrho}+1)\epsilon
, we get the following non-zero entries for the coupling matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\bar{K}}}
:

\displaystyle\bar{K}_{jj}

\displaystyle=\frac{m^{2}}{j^{2}}+2\,,

(69a)

\displaystyle\bar{K}_{j,j+1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{j+\frac{1}{2}}{\sqrt{j(j+1)}}=\bar{K}_{j+1,j}\,.

(69b)

Following the standard procedure for coupled oscillators, the system can be diagonalized in the normal mode basis. To connect with the exact continuum case discussed in Sec.
III.1
, we retain the shorthand
\mathbf{k}=(n,l,m)
to also denote the normal mode frequencies
\tilde{\omega}_{\mathbf{k}}
of the discretized problem. A similar analysis then leads to

\displaystyle\tilde{\omega}_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}(\tilde{t})

\displaystyle=\tilde{\kappa}_{nm}^{2}+\tilde{p}_{l}^{2}(\tilde{t})+\tilde{\mu}^{2}\,,

(70)

where the adiabatic transverse momenta are defined as

\tilde{p}_{l}(\tilde{t})=\frac{2\pi l\epsilon}{L}-\tilde{\mu}\xi\left(1+\tanh{\frac{\tilde{t}}{\tilde{\tau}}}\right)\,.

(71)

The former equation is nothing but the direct analogue of
19
. Here,
\tilde{\kappa}_{nm}
is the
n
-th eigenvalue of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\bar{K}}}(m)
, and all the parameters with a tilde on top are dimensionless upon rescaling with
\epsilon
.

So far, our discussion of the discretization has closely paralleled the continuum analysis. We now turn to one of the first subtleties that arises specifically in the numerical implementation of the discretized problem. For the transverse tower of modes, recall that we have a discrete spectrum from fixing periodic boundary conditions at
z=\pm L/2
, indexed by
l\in\mathbb{Z}
. In practice, numerical computations necessitate a careful regularization of the formally infinite sum over these modes to a finite subset
\tilde{\mathbb{Z}}\subset\mathbb{Z}
. Moreover, due to the explicit time dependence of the system, this regularization may itself be time dependent in principle, i.e
\tilde{\mathbb{Z}}\rightarrow\tilde{\mathbb{Z}}_{\tilde{t}}
.

Our regularization strategy is guided by the following considerations. First, at each time slice, the infrared modes of the rescaled Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}
— which dominate the entanglement entropy — must always be retained in order to correctly extract the leading-order scaling behavior. A straightforward way to ensure this is to recenter the median value of the
regularized
set of adiabatic momenta
\{\tilde{p}_{l}(\tilde{t})|l\in\tilde{\mathbb{Z}}_{\tilde{t}}\}
to zero at each time slice, while keeping the cardinality of
|\tilde{\mathbb{Z}}_{\tilde{t}}|\equiv N_{z}+1
fixed for consistency across time — the same cardinality as that of the spatial transverse modes fixed via the boundary condition
L=N_{z}\epsilon
.
The momenta are therefore spaced apart by the IR cutoff as
\Delta p_{l}=2\pi/L
, and bandlimited by the UV cutoff as
|p_{l}|\leq\pi/\epsilon
. For the mode functions at a particular time-slice, we hence plug in the following set of adiabatic momenta
\tilde{p}_{l}(\tilde{t})
evolving from their respective initial values

\tilde{p}_{l}^{-}
as:

\displaystyle\tilde{p}_{l}(\tilde{t})

\displaystyle=\tilde{p}_{l}^{-}-\tilde{\mu}\xi\left[1+\tanh{\frac{\tilde{t}}{\tilde{\tau}}}\right]

(72a)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi l}{N_{z}};\quad l=0,\pm 1,\pm 2,..,\pm\frac{N_{z}}{2}\,.

(72b)

This ensures that at each time-slice the dominant contributions to entanglement entropy are counted, allowing us to numerically extract the correct leading order scaling law from a finite number of modes, otherwise only accessible in the continuum limit. Particularly for the final state, the above choice corresponds to
\tilde{p}_{l}^{+}=\tilde{p}_{l}^{-}-2\tilde{\mu}\xi=2\pi l/N_{z}
.

Lastly, the parameters are chosen such that the axial and radial extents of the system remain much larger than the Compton wavelength of the particle, i.e.,
L,R\gg\mu^{-1}
. This ensures adequate resolution of the long-range, nonlocal correlations emerging from pair creation. In terms of the dimensionless parameters, this condition translates to:

\tilde{\mu}^{-1}\ll N_{\varrho},\,N_{z}\,.

(73)

Accordingly, for the simulations in this paper, we choose
\tilde{\mu}=1
,
N_{\varrho}=300
and
N_{z}=100
unless otherwise specified. Although one could adopt a finer lattice (
\tilde{\mu}\ll 1
), doing so would require a substantial increase in
N_{z}
and
N_{\varrho}
to maintain
73
, significantly increasing the computational burden without affecting the dominant entanglement scaling behavior.

The quadratic form of the Hamiltonian

\tilde{t}\to-\infty

(76)

This way,

provides a complete description of the quantum dynamics of the vacuum. We now describe how to extract information about the correlations encoded in this state and, ultimately, compute the geometric entanglement entropy.

The Gaussian form of the wavefunction allows us to read off the
time-dependent
covariance matrix for each
\mathbf{k}
-mode subsystem:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}\langle\{\hat{\tilde{\varphi}}_{\mathbf{k}},\hat{\tilde{\varphi}}_{\mathbf{k}}\}\rangle&\langle\{\hat{\tilde{\varphi}}_{\mathbf{k}},\hat{\tilde{\pi}}_{\mathbf{k}}\}\rangle\\

\displaystyle(\tilde{\Sigma}_{\varphi\varphi})_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle(\tilde{\Sigma}_{\pi\pi})_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{\tilde{\omega}_{\mathbf{k}}^{-}}{2\Upsilon_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}}+\frac{\left(\partial_{\tilde{t}}\Upsilon_{\mathbf{k}}\right)^{2}}{2\tilde{\omega}_{\mathbf{k}}^{-}}\right]\delta_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}

(78)

It can be readily verified, using
76

54
, where the length scale introduced there now corresponds to the UV regulator
\epsilon
. In the asymptotic future
\tilde{t}\to\infty

53

\displaystyle(\tilde{\Sigma}_{\varphi\varphi})_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}}{2\tilde{\omega}_{\mathbf{k}}^{+}}\left[\cosh{2r_{\vec{k}}}+\sinh{2r_{\vec{k}}}\cos{2\vartheta_{\vec{k}}}\right]

\displaystyle(\tilde{\Sigma}_{\varphi\pi})_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}}{2}\sinh{2r_{\vec{k}}}\sin{2\vartheta_{\vec{k}}}

(79)

\displaystyle(\tilde{\Sigma}_{\pi\pi})_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{\omega}_{\mathbf{k}}^{+}\delta_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}}{2}\left[\cosh{2r_{\vec{k}}}-\sinh{2r_{\vec{k}}}\cos{2\vartheta_{\vec{k}}}\right]

This is unsurprising: it simply reflects that a canonical transformation of the standard quadrature variables yields the normal-mode oscillators of the system. The more challenging task, however, is to infer the nontrivial spatial correlations of the vacuum from the seemingly simple block-diagonal covariance matrix in the normal-mode basis, and to follow how these correlations evolve over time. We address this next.

The recipe for this translation involves the matrix
\mathbf{V}_{lm}
that transforms the discretized field variable from the real-space basis to the normal mode basis:

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\tilde{\varphi}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}}(\mathbf{V}_{lm})_{nj}=\tilde{\varphi}_{lm,j}\,\,;\,\,\mathbf{k}=(l,m,n)\,.

(80)

The matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{V}}_{lm}
has the defining property that it diagonalizes the coupling matrix as
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{V}}_{lm}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}(l,m,\tilde{t})\boldsymbol{\mathbf{V}}^{T}_{lm}=\diag\{\tilde{\omega}_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}(\tilde{t})\}
. Given this transformation matrix, the real-space covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{(lm)}
can then be constructed for each

-sector as:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{(lm)}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{V}}_{lm}^{T}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{\varphi\varphi}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{V}}_{lm}&\boldsymbol{\mathbf{V}}_{lm}^{T}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{\varphi\pi}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{V}}_{lm}\\

(81a)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\varphi\varphi}^{(lm)}&\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\varphi\pi}^{(lm)}\\
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\varphi\pi}^{(lm)}&\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\pi\pi}^{(lm)}\end{bmatrix}\,.

(81b)

In the exact continuum limit, the analogue of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{V}}_{lm}
is a kernel that may be viewed as a generalization of the Hankel transform to a finite disk
0<\varrho<R
. In the discretized setting,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{V}}_{lm}

Such nonlocal corrections are indicative of pair creation, particularly serving as smoking-gun signatures for Hawking radiation

The central idea is to bipartition the spatial region of the system into interior and exterior domains, following the construction outlined in
II

\displaystyle{D}_{\rm IN}=\{(\varrho,z,\phi)\quad|\quad 0\leq\varrho\leq r\}\quad;\quad r<R\,.

\displaystyle{D}_{\rm in}=\{j\quad|\quad 1\leq j\leq n_{\varrho}\}\quad;\quad n_{\varrho}\leq N_{\varrho}\,,

\displaystyle{D}_{\rm out}=\{j\quad|\quad N_{\varrho}\geq j>n_{\varrho}\}\quad;\quad n_{\varrho}\leq N_{\varrho}\,.

Recall that at each point in

(and similarly in

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{(lm)}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{\varphi\varphi}^{(lm)}&\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{\varphi\pi}^{(lm)}\\

V
Discussion and future outlook

In this work, we have carried out a detailed numerical investigation of the geometric scaling and dynamics of entanglement generated during nonperturbative vacuum decay in scalar QED, focusing on the Sauter-Schwinger effect. To this end, we have analyzed entanglement entropy under a cylindrical spatial bipartition and demonstrated that strong external electric fields can drive a fundamental change in entanglement scaling — from the area-law behavior characteristic of vacuum states to a more general power-law scaling — at late times. This transition provides a concrete information-theoretic signature of nonperturbative pair production and highlights entanglement entropy as a sensitive probe of strong-field quantum dynamics.

One of our key results is that this power-law scaling saturates to a volume-law behavior in appropriate strong-field regimes, particularly along an “L-shaped” region in the
(\xi,\eta)
parameter-space. We interpret this transition through the lens of the underlying particle spectrum, noting that the entanglement entropy in such regimes is dominated by a thermal-like distribution of low-energy pairs produced via nonperturbative dynamics. We argue that such a volume-law emerges when the probed range of subsystem sizes corresponds to the length scales where nonlocal partner-mode correlations can be sufficiently resolved. For subsystem probes beyond this range, the scaling law can drop sharply to sub-extensive values. In the intermediate regions of the pulse-profile parameter space — where a genuine power-law is observed — the scaling therefore indicates a competition between short-range vacuum correlations and the long-range correlations seeded by pair creation.

Our results also clarify the relationship between momentum-space and real-space entanglement in this setting. We observe that the entropy content of the pairwise entanglement between particle–antiparticle modes — completely characterized by the squeezing parameter and displaying the universal structure expected for quantum fields in time-dependent external backgrounds — is reflected in the scaling pre-factor of real-space entanglement. However, the latter captures additional information about how these partner-mode correlations evolve and reorganize in space. In fact, the volume-law entanglement observed in appropriate regimes is not simply a consequence of large pairwise entropy, but instead reflects a nontrivial redistribution of correlations across many spatial modes without obscuring the nonlocal signatures of pair creation. In this way, geometric entanglement probes collective features of the final state that are not manifestly captured by purely mode-localized measures.

From a broader perspective, the observed transition in entanglement scaling raises several conceptual questions. In ground states of local Hamiltonians, area-law behavior is often linked to locality and finite correlation length, whereas volume-law scaling is usually associated with highly excited or “typical” states
Page 1993
;
Bianchi et al. 2022
. Our findings show that unitary, nonperturbative time evolution in a relativistic quantum field theory can dynamically generate such highly entangled states starting from the vacuum, without invoking randomness or thermalization in the usual sense. This invites a closer examination of the role of effective ergodicity, mode mixing, and thermal-like spectra in strong-field QFT.

There are several directions in which this work can be extended. A natural next step is to generalize the analysis to fermionic QED
Kaushal 2025
, where Pauli blocking and spin degrees of freedom may qualitatively modify both pair production and entanglement scaling. It would also be interesting to explore more general pulse-profiles
Schützhold et al. 2008
, time-dependent bipartitions, or entanglement measures beyond the von Neumann entropy, such as logarithmic negativity, which can diagnose entanglement in Gaussian mixed states
Kranas et al. 2025
. The methods developed here could be directly applied to other instances of particle production in curved spacetime
Parker 1969
;
Hawking 1975
and their experimental analogues
Balbinot et al. 2008
;
Fedichev and Fischer 2004
, where entanglement plays a central role in revealing the quantum features associated with relativistic phenomena. Finally, another important direction would be to incorporate the effects of backreaction
Copinger et al. 2025
, and study its impact on the entanglement entropy.

Appendix A
Orthonormality and completeness of the cylindrical basis

In this section, we provide the details underlying the orthonormality and completeness of the cylindrical modes introduced in the main text. We begin by defining the positive- and negative-energy mode functions:

\displaystyle\varphi^{\pm}_{\kappa k_{z}m}(x)=\sqrt{2\pi}e^{ik_{z}z}e^{im\phi}J_{m}(\kappa\varrho)v^{\pm}_{\kappa k_{z}}(t)\,,

(90)

The Klein-Gordon inner product for two scalar fields
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
is defined by

\displaystyle(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2})=i\int d^{3}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}\left(\varphi^{*}_{1}\partial_{t}\varphi_{2}-\varphi_{2}\partial_{t}\varphi^{*}_{1}\right)

(91)

Applying this definition to the cylindrical modes, we find:

\displaystyle(\varphi^{+}_{k},\varphi^{+}_{k^{\prime}})=i\int d^{3}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}\left(\varphi^{*}_{k}\partial_{t}\varphi_{k^{\prime}}-\varphi_{k^{\prime}}\partial_{t}\varphi^{*}_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=i\left(v^{*}_{k}\dot{v}_{k^{\prime}}-v_{k^{\prime}}\dot{v}^{*}_{k}\right)\int_{0}^{\infty}\varrho d\varrho\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dz\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi

\displaystyle\qquad\times e^{i(m^{\prime}-m)\phi}e^{i(k^{\prime}_{z}-k_{z})z}(2\pi)J_{m}(\kappa\varrho)J_{m^{\prime}}(\kappa^{\prime}\varrho)

\displaystyle=i\left(v^{*}_{k}\dot{v}_{k^{\prime}}-v_{k^{\prime}}\dot{v}^{*}_{k}\right)(2\pi)^{3}\delta_{m,m^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\delta(k_{z}-k_{z}^{\prime})\frac{\delta(\kappa^{\prime}-\kappa)}{\kappa}

(92)

Here, the Wronskian
i\left(v_{k}^{*}\dot{v}_{k^{\prime}}-v_{k^{\prime}}\dot{v}_{k}^{*}\right)
is independent of time. By normalizing the
v_{k}
functions such that this Wronskian equals unity, we obtain the standard orthonormality relations

\displaystyle(\varphi^{\pm}_{k},\varphi^{\pm}_{k^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\pm(2\pi)^{3}\delta_{m,m^{\prime}}\delta(k_{z}-k_{z}^{\prime})\frac{\delta(\kappa^{\prime}-\kappa)}{\kappa}

(93)

\displaystyle(\varphi^{\pm}_{k},\varphi^{\mp}_{k^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=0

(94)

To verify completeness, one considers the matrix

\displaystyle-i\left[\varphi^{+}_{k}(t,\mathbf{x})\partial_{t}\varphi^{+*}_{k}(t,\mathbf{x}^{\prime})-\varphi^{-}_{k}(t,\mathbf{x})\partial_{t}\varphi_{k}^{-*}(t,\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle=e^{i(\phi-\phi^{\prime})m}\,e^{ik_{z}(z-z^{\prime})}(2\pi)J_{m}(\kappa\varrho)J_{m}(\kappa\varrho^{\prime})

(95)

and summing over all modes gives

\displaystyle\sum_{k}-i\left[\varphi^{+}_{k}(t,\mathbf{x})\partial_{t}\varphi^{+*}_{k}(t,\mathbf{x}^{\prime})-\varphi^{-}_{k}(t,\mathbf{x})\partial_{t}\varphi_{k}^{-*}(t,\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dk_{z}}{(2\pi)}\int\frac{\kappa d\kappa}{(2\pi)^{2}}e^{i(\phi-\phi^{\prime})m}\,e^{ik_{z}(z-z^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times(2\pi)J_{m}(\kappa\varrho)J_{m}(\kappa\varrho^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\delta(\phi-\phi^{\prime})\delta(z-z^{\prime})\frac{\delta(\varrho-\varrho^{\prime})}{\varrho},

(96)

confirming the completeness of the cylindrical basis.

A.1
Details of discrete modes

We now impose boundary conditions leading to discretized modes. Starting from the continuum expressions

\displaystyle\varphi^{\pm}_{\kappa k_{z}m}(x)=\sqrt{2\pi}e^{ik_{z}z}e^{im\phi}J_{m}(\kappa\varrho)v^{\pm}_{\kappa k_{z}}(t)\,,

(97)

and imposing periodic condition along the axis, we get

\displaystyle k_{z}L=2\pi l\Rightarrow k_{z,l}=\frac{2\pi l}{L}\,,

(98)

so that the modes become

\displaystyle\varphi^{\pm}_{\kappa k_{z}m}(x)

\displaystyle\rightarrow\varphi^{\pm}_{\kappa lm}(x)

(99)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\pi}e^{i\frac{2\pi l}{L}z}e^{im\phi}J_{m}(\kappa\varrho)v^{\pm}_{\kappa k_{z}}(t)\,.

Similarly, imposing a Dirichlet condition at the radial boundary
\varrho=R
leads to

\displaystyle J_{m}(\kappa R)=0\Rightarrow\kappa_{mn}=\frac{{{j}}_{mn}}{R}\,,

(100)

where
{{j}}_{mn}
denotes the
n^{\rm th}
zero of
J_{m}
. The normalized discrete modes then read

\displaystyle\varphi^{\pm}_{\kappa k_{z}m}(x)

\displaystyle\rightarrow\varphi^{\pm}_{nlm}(x)

(101)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\sqrt{\pi}e^{i\frac{2\pi l}{L}z}e^{im\phi}}{|J_{m+1}({{j}}_{mn})|}J_{m}\left(\frac{{{j}}_{mn}\varrho}{R}\right)v^{\pm}_{\kappa k_{z}}(t)\,.

The corresponding orthonormality relations for these discrete modes are

\displaystyle(\varphi^{\pm}_{k},\varphi^{\pm}_{k^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\pm{V}\delta_{m,m^{\prime}}\delta_{l,l^{\prime}}\delta_{n,n^{\prime}}\,,

(102)

\displaystyle(\varphi^{\pm}_{k},\varphi^{\mp}_{k^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=0\,,

(103)

where
{V}=\pi R^{2}L
the total volume. This motivates the discrete mode sum prescription

\displaystyle\sum_{k}=\frac{1}{{V}}\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{l=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}

(104)

which will be used in computations of mode sums, entanglement entropy, and related observables.
