AI-driven precision immunotherapy: emerging trends from AACR 2026
Authors: Yani Chen, Ming Chen, Yiqing Xi, Yun Xia, Siyuan Ma, Baoxiang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Artificial intelligence (AI) has become a pivotal driver of innovation in cancer immunotherapy through large-scale biological data modeling, multimodal information integration, and advanced machine learning approaches. At the AACR Annual Meeting 2026, AI-based frameworks demonstrated broad applications across the immunotherapy continuum, including response prediction, patient stratification, neoantigen identification, and therapeutic development. An array of studies has transcended conventional single-biomarker paradigms, leveraging multimodal clinical and molecular datasets to construct biologically interpretable representations of tumor–immune ecosystems. Taken together, these advances signal a shift from reductionist biomarker-based prediction toward integrative, data-driven precision immunotherapy, fundamentally reshaping therapeutic stratification and target discovery and establishing artificial intelligence as a central driver of the conceptual and practical transformation of cancer immunotherapy.

To the Editor

Reports from the AACR Annual Meeting 2026 highlight the rapidly expanding role of AI in cancer immunotherapy, spanning response prediction to therapeutic discovery and development. Although recent work has broadly summarized AI advances across oncology from the 2026 AACR Annual Meeting [1], in this narrative review, we focus specifically on the emerging role of AI in precision immunotherapy, from predicting treatment response to informing personalized therapeutic development.

AI-driven multimodal prediction of immunotherapy benefit

A major theme emerging from AACR 2026 was the shift from conventional biomarker-based prediction toward AI-driven frameworks capable of extracting clinically actionable information from high-dimensional biological data. Multiple studies extended this paradigm through large-scale biological representation learning, enhancing prediction of immunotherapy benefit via modeling of complex tumor–immune interactions (Table 1).

MutationProjector, a foundation model pretrained on genomic alterations from more than 30,000 tumors, accurately distinguished HPV status, squamous histology, and basal–luminal molecular subtypes without task-specific training, demonstrating robust zero-shot generalization [2]. The model achieved best-in-class performance for therapeutic prediction across cancer types; in anti-PD-1/PD-L1-treated NSCLC, predicted responders exhibited a one-year progression-free survival (PFS) rate of 39% versus 16% among predicted non-responders [2]. At the transcriptomic level, machine learning models trained on TIGER immunotherapy cohorts identified conserved response-associated programs characterized by interferon signaling, TNF–NF-κB activation, and inflammatory pathways. These models achieved robust predictive performance across multiple malignancies, with several cohorts reaching area under the curve (AUC) values approaching 0.9 [3]. Extending transcriptome-based prediction to single-cell resolution, foundation-model embeddings derived from peripheral blood single-cell RNA sequencing further enabled simultaneous prediction of treatment response and severe immune-related adverse events (irAEs), achieving AUC values approaching 0.8 and highlighting the potential of noninvasive immune monitoring [4].

Predictive modeling increasingly incorporated non-tumor and minimally invasive data sources. At the microbiome level, MiFM, the first microbiome foundation model applied to oncology, generated disease trajectories and risk clocks that outperformed conventional staging, identifying high-risk lesions in histologically normal mucosa and predicting immunotherapy resistance with AUCs reaching 0.95 in an independent NSCLC cohort [5]. Routine histopathology also emerged as a powerful source of predictive information. Path-IO extracted prognostic features directly from hematoxylin–eosin slides and consistently outperformed conventional PD-L1 assessment across institutional, multicenter, and phase III clinical trial cohorts. Integration with radiomic and clinical variables further improved predictive performance and linked high-risk scores to immune-cold tumor phenotypes [6]. LiquidTME reconstructed tumor microenvironment states from pretreatment plasma cell-free DNA, significantly separating patients with distinct PFS outcomes and identifying durable responders to immunotherapy. Notably, its predictive performance exceeded that of established tumor mutational burden (TMB), suggesting that circulating biomarkers may better capture immunotherapy-relevant biological states than conventional genomic metrics [7].

AI-enabled therapeutic design and personalized immunotherapy

A parallel theme at AACR 2026 was the growing application of AI to personalized immunotherapy development, encompassing antigen presentation prediction, neoantigen prioritization, vaccine design, and T-cell engineering (Table 2).

Accurate prediction of peptide–major histocompatibility complex (MHC) interactions remains fundamental to neoantigen discovery. JANUS integrated multi-allele immunopeptidomics data through iterative deconvolution and ensemble learning, maintaining or improving predictive performance compared with state-of-the-art predictors such as NetMHCpan and NetMHCIIpan, particularly across diverse MHC alleles [8].

Efforts to improve neoantigen prioritization also accelerated through AI-based modeling of immunogenicity. epiVIP was trained on 1.7 million HLA-I peptides from 254 tumor samples and accurately predicted epitope abundance, with AUC values exceeding 0.8 across held-out and independent datasets [9]. Importantly, predicted epitope abundance was associated with neoantigen immunogenicity and outperformed TMB in predicting response to immune checkpoint blockade [9]. NEMo incorporated evolutionary selection pressure from more than 25,000 mutations and achieved AUCs of 0.855 and 0.73 for predicting CD8⁺ and CD4⁺ neoantigen immunogenicity, respectively. In the TESLA consortium challenge, NEMo achieved 74% accuracy compared with 42% for the next best-performing method. By accounting for immune editing and HLA loss during tumor evolution, the model improved identification of clinically relevant neoantigens for personalized immunotherapy [10].

AI applications further extended to personalized vaccine development. EchoNeo extended AI applications from neoantigen prioritization to end-to-end vaccine design by integrating immunogenicity prediction with mRNA sequence optimization. Trained on approximately 3,700 experimentally validated peptides, the framework outperformed established prediction tools, including DeepImmuno and the IEDB Class I Immunogenicity Tool, and supported the development of personalized vaccines with favorable safety and antitumor activity [11]. Supporting evidence-based target selection, EpitopeMiner integrated more than 78,000 publications and 2.6 million curated epitopes. Compared with general-purpose large language models, it retrieved more experimentally supported immunological evidence while achieving 100% evidence coverage within the curated knowledge base in the benchmark analysis and complete citation traceability [12].

Taken together, AACR 2026 underscored the emergence of foundation models and multimodal AI systems as a new paradigm for precision immunotherapy. The incorporation of peripheral blood, microbiome, liquid biopsy, and pathology data is enabling more scalable and minimally invasive prediction strategies. Beyond clinical outcome prediction, AI is increasingly contributing to neoantigen discovery, vaccine development, and therapeutic design, extending its role across the immunotherapy development continuum.

Despite these advances, substantial barriers remain to clinical translation. Conference abstracts often present preliminary findings with limited methodological detail and may lack peer-reviewed validation and independent replication, leaving potential risks of overfitting and selective reporting. Prospective, multicenter and external validation will be essential to establish robustness and generalizability. Regulatory requirements, data privacy, workflow integration, cost, and infrastructure may further constrain implementation. Accordingly, these models should be regarded as promising translational tools rather than established clinical decision-making systems.
