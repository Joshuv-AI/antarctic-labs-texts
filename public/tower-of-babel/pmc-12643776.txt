Cervical Lymphatic Bypass for Alzheimer Disease: Toward Standardized Monitoring in a New Frontier of Supermicrosurgery
Authors: Lei Jin, Qingping Xie, Renpeng Fang, Ming Xiao, Maximilian Kueckelhaus
Published: 2025

Since the in-depth description of meningeal lymphatics in 2015,1 interventions targeting the brain’s lymphatic system to treat conditions such as Alzheimer disease have become a key research interest for neuroscientists and microsurgeons alike. Xie et al2 began applying the cervical lymphovenous anastomosis technique in 2020, gaining preliminary experience with the procedure.3 After its introduction, the procedure spread rapidly across China, with more than 400 hospitals reportedly adopting it. This rapid and largely unregulated expansion attracted international attention and provoked debate. In response, national health authorities introduced a policy to safeguard patient safety and ensure data integrity. The directive suspended routine clinical use, permitting continuation only within formal clinical trials. Among the first to be approved is our multicenter randomized controlled trial, CLEAN-AD (NCT07073066), developed with leading Chinese neurologists and trial methodologists.

The current main surgical targets are the deep cervical lymph nodes, which serve as a critical hub for lymphatic drainage from the brain. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that enhancing cervical lymphatic circulation can improve brain function, further supporting the rationale for targeting this anatomical site.4 The surgical technique introduced by Xie et al has undergone several iterations in pursuit of optimal outcomes. Initially, a direct lymphovenous anastomosis was performed, identifying poorly functioning lymphatic vessels with indocyanine green lymphography before anastomosis to an adjacent vein. This later evolved into lymph node–to-vein anastomosis, a modification based on the rationale that the lymph node is a more efficient collection point while providing opportunity for a larger caliber anastomosis with potentially superior long-term patency. Another recent optimization is the lymph–adipose flap anastomosis, where perinodal adipose tissue is preserved to protect delicate afferent and efferent vessels from iatrogenic injury during dissection (Fig. 1). Other teams in China have demonstrated considerable ingenuity, with some using vein interposition grafts to bridge lymph nodes to major veins, and others exploring vascularized lymph node transfer from sites such as the omentum to establish new drainage pathways.

This interest is now global, with pioneering work and clinical trials emerging in multiple countries. The current heterogenicity of surgical approaches and surgeons’ varying experience levels with such techniques may result in inconclusive outcomes. Therefore, we suggest a collaborative effort in designing trials that provide comparable outcome measures with sufficient sample size and statistical power. Consecutive meta-analyses with pooling of clinical trial data may be crucial to ultimately provide conclusive evidence of effectiveness.

Indeed, we have observed rapid functional improvements in many patients, often occurring within days of surgery, challenging the conventional view of neurodegeneration as a slow process driven by Aβ and tau. A potential explanation emerges from a recent landmark study in Cell, which demonstrated that dysfunctional meningeal lymphatics disrupt the excitatory/inhibitory balance of cortical neurons.4 To better understand this, we overcame considerable microsurgical challenges to establish a novel rat model, subsequently extending its application to Alzheimer disease mice to validate its utility for studying these fast-acting processes.5 For the time being, this effort remains highly translational while aiming to address one of the most urgent and unmet challenges in modern medicine: the treatment of neurodegenerative disease.
