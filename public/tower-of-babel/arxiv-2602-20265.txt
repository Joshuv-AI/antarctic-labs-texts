Qubit Noise Spectroscopy of Superconducting Dynamics in a Magnetic Field
Jiajie Cheng, Jaewon Kim, Oriana K. Diessel, Chong Zu, Shubhayu Chatterjee

Abstract
An applied magnetic field affects a superconductor in two ways — by promoting pairing fluctuations, and by inducing topological defects called vortices that carry quantized magnetic flux. A quantitative characterization of the resultant field-induced superconducting dynamics with spatio-temporal resolution remains challenging, particularly in two-dimensional materials. In this work, we analyze magnetic noise measured by the depolarization rate of a proximate single spin-qubit as a non-invasive probe of such dynamical fluctuations. We demonstrate that the temperature dependence of the magnetic noise spectrum near T c T_{c} deviates from predictions based on quasiparticle excitations due to critical superconducting fluctuations, which in turn are enhanced by a weak applied field. By analyzing the magnetic noise due to vortex dynamics, we further show that noise spectroscopy is not only able to distinguish between different vortex phases, but also extract key physical quantities of interest, such as oscillation frequencies of pinned vortices, phonon dispersion of vortex lattices and vortex diffusivity in a vortex liquid. Complementing recent work on noise magnetometry of quasiparticle excitations 13 and Berezinskii-Kosterlitz-Thouless transitions in two-dimensional superconductors without an applied field 15 , our work highlights the ability of noise spectroscopy to reveal a wealth of superconducting dynamical phenomena in an applied field.



I
Introduction

Recent advances in the experimental design of Van der Waals heterostructures
24
have led to a surge in the discovery of atomically thin two-dimensional (2D) superconductors
60
.
Remarkably, superconductivity in some of these materials can survive fairly strong out-of-plane magnetic fields
31
, or may emerge only in the presence of an applied field
71
.
However, unlike bulk superconductors, techniques to probe 2D superconductors with both momentum and energy resolution are limited.
An applied magnetic field introduces an additional layer of complication: scattering experiments typically use electrically charged and/or spin-ful probe particles that are deflected by the applied field or any field gradient, making it challenging to obtain momentum-resolved information.

Beyond affecting the nature and fluctuations of the pair condensate itself
44
, magnetic fields also induce vortices, topological defects in the superconducting order parameter carrying quantized fluxes
67
;
64
;
65
.
Since flux flow leads to a finite electrical resistance
8
, a detailed understanding of vortex dynamics would aid the design of superconducting materials.
However, studying vortex dynamics with simultaneous spatio-temporal resolution requires sensitivity at nanometer lengthscales over a wide range of temperatures, making such studies difficult for low-temperature micro-scale probes such as SQUIDs
36
.
Developing the capability to resolve in-field pairing fluctuations and vortex motion has the potential to shed light on questions of fundamental interest, e.g., the pairing mechanism in strong coupling superconductors, as well as issues of practical interest, e.g., the design of robust superconductors by impeding flux flow.

Isolated spin-qubits, occurring naturally as solid-state defects such as nitrogen vacancy (NV) centers in diamond
49
;
70
;
56
;
58
;
12
or boron vacancy (V
B
) centers in hexagonal boron nitride
27
;
26
;
66
;
25
, can detect fluctuating magnetic fields generated by low-dimensional materials with high sensitivity.
Recently, it has been proposed that characterizing the resultant magnetic noise measured by the spin-qubit can act as a non-invasive, wireless table-top probe of superconducting dynamics in 2D/thin film materials
13
;
18
;
15
;
17
;
35
.
To achieve this, we may optically initialize a spin-qubit in a polarized state in the proximity of the superconductor.
Magnetic field fluctuations at the qubit location, which may originate from Cooper pair fluctuations or the motion of vortices, induces depolarization of the qubit.
Studying this depolarization rate, denoted by
1/T_{1}
, as a function of experimental knobs such as temperature
T
, qubit-probe distance
z_{0}
, and probe frequency
\Omega
furnishes a wealth of valuable information about dynamical superconducting correlations.
For instance, previous works have argued that at zero external magnetic field, studying such dynamics can tell us about the pairing symmetry of the superconductor via the temperature dependence of the magnetic noise
13
;
18
, diagnose critical behavior, e.g., Berezinskii-Kosterlitz-Thouless (BKT) physics of vortex-unbinding via the distance dependence of noise
15
, or probe time-reversal symmetry-breaking in chiral superconductors
17
.
Indeed, some of these predictions have been verified in recent experiments, e.g., in thin film BSCCO
45

Notably, certain noise spectroscopy experiments have gone beyond zero-field measurements, and have also probed magnetic noise in the presence of an applied magnetic field
45
;
32
, leading to an important question:
what is the nature of magnetic noise due to a proximate superconductor in an external magnetic field?

In this work, we theoretically characterize the magnetic noise spectrum detected by an isolated spin-qubit placed near a superconductor.
To set the stage, we first study the noise at a thermal metal-insulator transition within the time-dependent Ginzburg Landau (TDGL) approach

;
43

16

61

Next, we extend our TDGL equations to include an applied magnetic field, and demonstrate how the magnetic noise can detect the enhancement of critical fluctuations in the presence of an external field.
Finally, we consider the magnetic noise due to superconducting vortices at finite fields.
We show that spin-qubit spectroscopy allows us to probe diverse aspects of vortex dynamics for both 2D and 3D superconductors, including Kelvin oscillations of isolated vortices

;
65
;
52

20
;
21
;
9

Our results complement recent works

;
57

;
6

;
18

II
Relating depolarization rate to source fluctuations

II.1
Experimental set-up

We begin by outlining the basic experimental set-up.
A single isolated spin-qubit, with quantization axis
\hat{{\bm{n}}}_{q}
and intrinsic splitting
\Omega
, is placed at
{\bm{r}}_{q}=(0,0,z_{0})
, i.e., at a distance
z_{0}
from the superconducting sample in the x-y plane [Fig.
1
].
The qubit couples to the background static magnetic field
\mu_{0}\bm{H}
as well as the local time-varying magnetic field
{\bm{B}}({\bm{r}}_{q},t)
generated by pairing fluctuations and/or vortex motion in the superconductor, and is therefore described by the Hamiltonian

\mathcal{H}_{q}=\frac{\hbar\Omega}{2}(\hat{{\bm{n}}}_{q}\cdot\bm{\sigma})+g\mu_{B}\big[\mu_{0}\bm{H}+{\bm{B}}({\bm{r}}_{q},t)\big]\cdot\bm{\sigma}.

(1)

The component of the static field
\bm{H}
along the qubit’s spin quantization axis
\hat{{\bm{n}}}_{q}
modifies the zero-field splitting
\Omega
.
On the other hand, fluctuations of the magnetic field
{\bm{B}}({\bm{r}}_{q},t)
, arising from current fluctuations and vortex motion in the sample, cause depolarization of a qubit when optically initialized in a fully polarized state.
The rate of depolarization
1/T_{1}
may be evaluated using Fermi’s Golden rule (e.g., see Refs.
4
;
14
), and relates to the magnetic noise tensor
\mathcal{N}_{\alpha\beta}(\Omega)
at the qubit location
{\bm{r}}_{q}
as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T_{1}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{g\mu_{B}}{\hbar}\right)^{2}\mathcal{N}_{+-}(\Omega),\text{ where }

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\alpha\beta}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\,\,e^{i\Omega t}\langle B_{\alpha}({\bm{r}}_{q},t)B_{\beta}({\bm{r}}_{q},0)\rangle_{T}\;.

(2)

In Eq. (
2
), we have used
\langle\ldots\rangle_{T}
to denote the thermal expectation value, and defined
B_{\pm}=B_{x^{\prime}}\pm iB_{y^{\prime}}
with
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},\hat{{\bm{n}}}_{q})
forming an orthonormal triad
1
1

1

We note that in the presence of quantum mechanical sources, the correlation function is promoted to an anti-commutator
.
Therefore, by orienting the qubit spin-quantization axis
\hat{{\bm{n}}}_{q}
appropriately, one may diagnose different components of the noise tensor
\mathcal{N}_{\alpha\beta}(\Omega)
as a function of the qubit splitting
\Omega

As discussed in Refs.
4
;
18
, in systems with additional rotational and mirror symmetries, the magnetic noise tensor is completely characterized by two scalars
2
2

2

Consider setting
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}_{q}=(\sin\theta,0,\cos\theta)
, the noise
\mathcal{N}=\left[\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{T}}\left(1+\cos^{2}\theta\right)/2+\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{L}}\sin^{2}\theta\right]
.

\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{T}}=\mathcal{N}_{xx}+\mathcal{N}_{yy}
, measured from the depolarization rate for
\hat{{\bm{n}}}_{q}=\hat{z}
, and (ii) the longitudinal noise
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{L}}=\mathcal{N}_{zz}
which may be detected by setting
\hat{{\bm{n}}}_{q}=\hat{x}
and probing the depolarization rate set by
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{L}}+\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{T}}/2

Therefore, the task at hand is to relate the magnetic noise tensor to the fluctuations of its source in the proximate superconducting sample.

II.2
Noise tensor and conductivity

In the vicinity of a thermal phase transition from a metal to a superconductor, the time-varying magnetic field
{\bm{B}}({\bm{r}}_{q},t)
originates from fluctuations of the current density
{\bm{j}}(\bm{\rho},t)
via the Biot-Savart law (defining
\bm{\rho}=(x,y)
to be the in-plane coordinates henceforth)
3
3

3

We neglect relativistic retardation effects since typical speeds in solid state systems are much smaller than the speed of light
c
.
Such current fluctuations are encoded in the non-local dynamical conductivity tensor
\sigma_{\mu\nu}
of the system, defined via the linear response to an electric field
\bm{E}(\bm{\rho},t)
that varies in space and time.

j_{\mu}(\bm{\rho},t)=\int dt\,\int d^{2}\bm{\rho}\,\sigma_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\rho}-\bm{\rho}^{\prime},t-t^{\prime})E_{\nu}(\bm{\rho}^{\prime},t^{\prime}).

(3)

To account for screening due to mobile charge carriers, it is useful to consider non-local conductivity in momentum space, and decompose it into longitudinal and transverse components (assuming translation and rotational invariance in plane):

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mu\nu}({\bm{q}},\Omega)

\displaystyle=\int dt\,\,e^{i\Omega t}\int d^{2}\bm{\rho}\,e^{-i{\bm{q}}\cdot\bm{\rho}}\sigma_{\mu\nu}({\bm{r}},t)

\displaystyle=\hat{q}_{\mu}\hat{q}_{\nu}\sigma_{\mathrm{L}}(q,\Omega)+(\delta_{\mu\nu}-\hat{q}_{\mu}\hat{q}_{\nu})\sigma_{\mathrm{T}}(q,\Omega).

(4)

As discussed in Ref.
4
, longitudinal current fluctuations create an imbalance in the charge density and get rapidly screened in the presence of mobile carriers, which results in its suppression by a factor of
(\Omega z_{0}/c)^{2}
.
For typical values of the qubit frequency
\Omega\approx 1
GHz, qubit-probe distance
z_{0}\approx 10
nm and
c=3\times 10^{8}
m/s,
(\Omega z_{0}/c)^{2}\approx 10^{-15}
, which is negligibly small.
Therefore, we will focus on transverse current fluctuations, that satisfy
\nabla\cdot{\bm{j}}(\bm{\rho},t)=0
or equivalently
{\bm{q}}\cdot{\bm{j}}_{\bm{q}}(t)=0
in momentum space, as the primary source of magnetic noise.
Such current fluctuations are encoded in the non-local transverse conductivity
\sigma_{\mathrm{T}}({\bm{q}},\Omega)
, which determines the transverse magnetic noise
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{T}}
in the experimentally relevant limit
\hbar\Omega\ll k_{B}T
as
4

\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{T}}(\Omega,z_{0})=\frac{\mu_{0}^{2}k_{B}T}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}dq\,q\,e^{-2qz_{0}}\,\text{Re}\left[\sigma_{\mathrm{T}}(q,\Omega)\right].

(5)

In the vicinity of the critical point
T\approx T_{c}
, the dominant contribution to
\sigma_{\mathrm{T}}({\bm{q}},\Omega)
comes from fluctuations of the superconducting order parameter
\psi
.
While this temperature window is narrow in 3D superconductors, it may be substantially wider due to enhanced order-parameter fluctuations in quasi-2D superconductors
45
.
Accordingly, we will focus on computing the fluctuation conductivity in the vicinity of the critical temperature
T_{c}
, both without and with an applied out-of-plane magnetic field
\bm{H}=H\hat{z}
.

We note that our calculation does not account for the contribution of Bogoliubov quasiparticle excitations to the magnetic noise: these were analyzed in Refs.
13
;
18
, and are suppressed relative to the noise originating from critical fluctuations when
T\approx T_{c}
.
Further, the most general expression for the transverse magnetic noise from a 2D sample is expressed in terms of the reflection coefficient
r_{s}({\bm{q}},\Omega)=\left[1+2iq/\left(\mu_{0}\Omega\sigma_{\mathrm{T}}({\bm{q}},\Omega)\right)\right]^{-1}
of s-polarized electromagnetic waves
4
.
However, the additional corrections are important only in the low-temperature limit when the superfluid density
n_{s}
is large
18
.
Thus, in the vicinity of the phase transition,
n_{s}\propto|\psi|^{2}\propto T_{c}-T
is small, Eq. (
5
) provides an accurate description of the magnetic noise.

II.3
Noise tensor from vortex dynamics

An alternate source of magnetic noise at the qubit location is the motion of vortices — topological defects in the superconducting order parameter
\psi
with integer winding of its phase around the vortex core.
Each vortex carries an integer number of magnetic flux quantum
\Phi_{0}=h/e^{*}
, with
e^{*}=2e
being the charge of the pair-condensate
67
.
In the absence of an applied magnetic field, the net vortex density remains zero, but the system can nucleate an equal number of vortices and anti-vortices satisfying this constraint.
In fact, the zero-field superconducting transition in two dimensions is described by a vortex-antivortex binding transition — known as a Berezinskii-Kosterlitz-Thouless (BKT) transition
7
;
38
;
37
. The signatures of such a transition in magnetic noise, as evidenced by a length-scale dependent vortex dielectric constant, were analyzed in Ref.
15
.

At non-zero applied magnetic field, one can instead enter a superconducting phase with a non-zero net vortex density in type-II superconducting materials.
The precise phase of the vortices depends on a complex interplay of thermal fluctuations and disorder
22
;
23
;
8
: the vortices may form a liquid, an Abrikosov vortex lattice or a vortex glass phase.
These phases are characterized by distinct vortex dynamics, which leads to tell-tale imprints of the phase on the magnetic noise detected by the qubit.
To characterize the noise due to vortex dynamics, we directly compute the correlations of the magnetic field
{\bm{B}}({\bm{r}}_{q},t)
at the location of the qubit due to vortices.

To this end, we first evaluate the magnetic field
{\bm{B}}_{0}({\bm{r}})
in the upper half space (
z>0
) due to a single vortex at the origin, i.e., at
{\bm{r}}_{\rm vortex}=(\bm{\rho},z)=(0,0,0)
in a 2D superconductor.
For analytical progress, we assume the extreme type-II limit, where
\kappa=\lambda_{L}/\xi\gg 1
, with
\lambda_{L}
being the bulk penetration depth and
\xi
being the order-parameter correlation length that sets the size of the vortex core.
For a 2D superconductor of thickness
d
, we need to solve the following Maxwell-London equation to find the magnetic field:

\nabla^{2}{\bm{B}}_{0}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{L}^{2}}\left[{\bm{B}}_{0}-\hat{z}\,\Phi_{0}\,\delta^{(2)}(\boldsymbol{\rho})\right],\quad-\dfrac{d}{2}<z<\dfrac{d}{2}\\
0,\qquad|z|>\dfrac{d}{2}\end{array}.\right.

(6)

Since all the currents are restricted to the 2D sample,
\nabla\times{\bm{B}}_{0}=0
for
z>0
, and accordingly we may write the field outside as the gradient of a scalar potential, i.e.,
{\bm{B}}_{0}=-\nabla\phi_{M}
.
Solving for
\phi_{M}
with appropriate boundary conditions (see Appendix
C
for details), we find that

\displaystyle\phi_{M}(\bm{\rho},z)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{2}{\bm{k}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}e^{i{\bm{k}}\cdot\bm{\rho}}e^{-kz}\phi_{M}^{\rm 2D}({\bm{k}}),~\text{ where }

\displaystyle\phi_{M}^{\rm 2D}({\bm{k}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\Phi_{0}}{k(1+\Lambda k)},\quad z>0

(7)

\Lambda=2\lambda_{L}^{2}/d
being the Pearl length
55
.
Using Eq. (
7
), we may directly calculate the field at the qubit location as
{\bm{B}}_{0}({\bm{r}}_{q})=-\nabla\phi_{M}|_{{\bm{r}}={\bm{r}}_{q}}
.

{\bm{B}}_{0}({\bm{r}}_{q})=\int\frac{d^{2}{\bm{k}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}e^{i{\bm{k}}\cdot\bm{\rho}_{q}}e^{-kz_{0}}(-i{\bm{k}},k)\frac{\Phi_{0}}{k(1+\Lambda k)}.

(8)

To contrast our results for magnetic noise with the noise originating from vortex dynamics in bulk superconductors occupying the lower half-space
(z<0)
, we also calculate the field of a line vortex along the
z
-axis.
In this case, the relevant Maxwell-London equations take the form

\nabla^{2}{\bm{B}}_{0}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{L}^{2}}\left[{\bm{B}}_{0}-\hat{z}\,\Phi_{0}\,\delta^{(2)}(\boldsymbol{\rho})\right],\quad z<0\\
0,\quad z>0,\end{array}\right.

(9)

where we have neglected possible variation of the superfluid density near the surface for simplicity.
Once again, the magnetic field outside can be characterized by a scalar potential
\phi_{M}^{\rm 3D}
as
{\bm{B}}_{0}=-\nabla\phi_{M}^{\rm 3D}
, with

\phi_{M}^{\rm 3D}({\bm{k}})=\frac{\Phi_{0}}{k(1+k^{2}\lambda_{L}^{2})},\quad z>0

(10)

implying that the field is modified as,

{\bm{B}}_{0}({\bm{r}}_{q})=\int\frac{d^{2}{\bm{k}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}e^{i{\bm{k}}\cdot\bm{\rho}_{q}}e^{-kz_{0}}(-i{\bm{k}},k)\frac{\Phi_{0}}{k(1+\lambda_{L}^{2}k^{2})}.

(11)

Therefore, in either case, we may find the field due to a collection of vortices at positions
\bm{\rho}_{i}
by the principle of superposition, in terms of the vortex density
n_{v}(\bm{\rho},t)=\sum_{i}\delta^{(2)}(\bm{\rho}-\bm{\rho}_{i}(t))
, as:

{\bm{B}}({\bm{r}}_{q},t)=\int d^{2}\bm{\rho}\,{\bm{B}}_{0}({\bm{r}}_{q}-\bm{\rho})n_{v}(\bm{\rho},t).

(12)

Thus, knowing the field created by a single vortex, we may directly derive the magnetic noise in terms of correlations of the vortex density

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\alpha\beta}(\Omega)=\int d^{2}\bm{\rho}\int d^{2}\bm{\rho}^{\prime}\,B_{0}^{\alpha}({\bm{r}}_{q}-\bm{\rho})B_{0}^{\beta}({\bm{r}}_{q}-\bm{\rho}^{\prime})C_{\bm{\rho},\bm{\rho}^{\prime}}(\Omega)~~~

\displaystyle\text{ where }C_{\bm{\rho},\bm{\rho}^{\prime}}(\Omega)=\int dt\,\,e^{i\Omega t}\langle n_{v}(\bm{\rho},t)n_{v}(\bm{\rho}^{\prime},0)\rangle_{T}

(13)

is the vortex density correlation function, and we have assumed that the vortices behave as classical point particles (rods) in 2D (3D).
For detecting the motion of isolated or pinned vortices, we can simply focus on the field created by a single vortex, while in a vortex solid or liquid phase,
one may use discrete or continuous translation symmetry to further simplify Eq. (
13
).
In the latter cases, the transverse noise
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{T}}
and the longitudinal noise
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{L}}
can extracted from the different components of the noise tensor, yielding useful information vortex dynamics.

III
Zero-field critical magnetic noise

To set the stage for evaluating magnetic noise near criticality at non-zero magnetic fields, we first calculate the magnetic noise arising from critical supercurrent fluctuations near the superconductor to metal phase transition at zero magnetic field.
Strictly speaking, this transition is described by BKT physics in two dimensions due to very weak screening of inter-vortex interactions
6
, and the magnetic noise originating for the resultant vortex-binding dynamics is discussed in Ref.
15
.
Nevertheless, in finite-sized samples or in layered quasi-2d materials, a Ginzburg-Landau (mean-field) approach can reasonably describe the conductivity at the onset of local pairing, and thus provide a useful approximation if the pairing temperature is not very distinct from
T_{\rm BKT}
where phase coherence sets in
19
.
Therefore, we resort to a calculation of the magnetic noise using a time-dependent Ginzburg-Landau approach, which also has the advantage of being straightforwardly generalizable to finite magnetic fields.

Our analysis shows that measurements of this magnetic noise provide direct access to physically relevant quantities, such as the characteristic lifetime of a critical supercurrent fluctuation, as well as providing an understanding as to how the magnetic noise scales as a function of the NV-sample distance
z_{0}
and coherence length
\xi
.

To make a quantitative analysis of the magnetic noise, our main objective is to find the non-local transverse conductivity
\sigma_{\mathrm{T}}({\bm{q}},\Omega)
, from which the magnetic noise can be obtained via Eq. (
5
).
We evaluate
\sigma_{\mathrm{T}}
with the classical fluctuation-dissipation theorem (FDT), through the transverse current-current correlator
41
.

\sigma_{\mathrm{T}}({\bm{q}},\Omega)=\frac{1}{2k_{B}T}\langle{\bm{j}}^{\mathrm{T}}_{{\bm{q}},\Omega}\cdot{\bm{j}}^{\mathrm{T}}_{-{\bm{q}},-\Omega}\rangle,

(14)

where the in-plane transverse current density is defined as
{\bm{j}}^{\mathrm{T}}_{\bm{q}}={\bm{j}}_{\bm{q}}-\hat{{\bm{q}}}(\hat{{\bm{q}}}\cdot{\bm{j}}_{\bm{q}})
, with
\hat{{\bm{q}}}
the unit momentum vector and
{\bm{j}}({\bm{q}},\Omega)
denotes the Fourier transform of the in-plane current density
{\bm{j}}(\bm{\rho},t)=[\hbar e^{*}/(2im^{*})]\big[\psi^{*}\nabla\psi-\psi(\nabla\psi^{*})\big]
,
\psi(\bm{\rho},t)
being the superconducting order parameter.

We work in the experimentally relevant regime
\hbar\Omega\ll k_{B}T
, and therefore set
\Omega=0
when computing the transverse conductivity.
To evaluate the transverse current correlator we use the time-dependent Ginzburg-Landau framework.
In this approach, the dynamics of the
\psi
are governed by the following Langevin equations
61
,

\hbar\partial_{t}\psi=-\Gamma\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta\psi^{*}}+\zeta,

(15)

with the Ginzburg-Landau Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
given by

\mathcal{H}=\int d^{2}\bm{\rho}\left(\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m^{*}}|\nabla\psi|^{2}+r|\psi|^{2}+\frac{u}{2}|\psi|^{4}\right).

(16)

r\propto T-T_{c}
is an effective parameter controlling the distance from the critical temperature, such that, at mean-field level,
r<0
describes the superconducting side, and
r>0
describes the metallic side.
m^{*}
denotes the effective mass of the Cooper pairs, and
u>0
controls the strength of the non-linearity of the order paramater.

\zeta
is a zero-mean Gaussian noise with correlations given by the FDT, with local temperature
T
,

\langle\zeta(\bm{\rho},t)\zeta(\bm{\rho}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\rangle=2\Gamma\hbar k_{B}T\,\delta(t-t^{\prime})\delta^{(2)}(\bm{\rho}-\bm{\rho}^{\prime})\,.

(17)

The dimensionless parameter
\Gamma
in Eq. (
15
) is determined by the coupling with the higher energy degrees of freedom, which act as a bath that generates the dissipative dynamics.

as the critical point is approached, the uniform (
q=0
) conductivity also exhibits a divergence, reflecting the critical slowing down of Cooper-pair fluctuations.

Applying Eq. (
21
) on Eq. (
5
), we arrive at the magnetic noise,

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\rm m}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{0}^{2}k_{B}T}{4\pi}\sigma_{\rm m}(0)\int_{0}^{\infty}dq\,q\,e^{-2qz_{0}}F_{\rm m}(q\xi_{\rm m}).

(22)

In Fig.
2
we plot the magnetic noise as a function of
T-T_{c}
, and also as a function of the sample-NV distance
z_{0}
.
However, the asymptotic limits can be readily understood by noting that in Eq. (
22
), the magnetic noise is simply a function of the dimensionless ratio
\tilde{z}=z_{0}/\xi_{\rm m}
.

Away from the critical regime, when
\xi_{\rm m}\ll z_{0}
, we may approximate
F_{{\rm m}}(q\xi_{\rm m})\approx F_{\rm m}(0)=1
and the integral in Eq. (
22
) scales as
1/z_{0}^{2}
, leading to the following expression for the magnetic noise:.

\mathcal{N}_{\rm m}=\frac{\mu_{0}^{2}k_{B}T}{16\pi z_{0}^{2}}\sigma_{\rm m}(0).

(23)

This expression highlights two distinct scaling behaviors.
First, since
\sigma_{\rm m}(0)\propto\tau_{\rm m}\propto(T-T_{c})^{-1}
, the noise diverges as
(T-T_{c})^{-1}
upon approaching the transition.
By fitting the slope
\mathcal{N}
, one can further determine to characteristic lifetime
\tau_{m}
of critical supercurrent fluctuations
45
.
Second, the prefactor reveals a spatial dependence, with the noise decaying as
z_{0}^{-2}
with the NV distance.
These trends are clearly reflected in Fig.
2
: panel (a) illustrates the temperature dependence, while panel (b) highlights the distance scaling.

As the temperature is lowered to approach the critical point, the coherence length
\xi_{\rm m}\propto(T-T_{c})^{-1/2}
becomes much larger than the NV–sample distance
z_{0}
.
In this regime, we may approximate
F_{\rm m}(q\xi_{\rm m})\approx(q\xi_{\rm m})^{-2}
in the integral in Eq. (
22
), leading to an integral that is independent of
z_{0}
(up to logarithmic corrections).
Consequently, the magnetic noise scales as

\mathcal{N}_{\rm m}\propto\frac{\mu_{0}^{2}k_{B}T}{\xi_{\rm m}^{2}}\sigma_{\rm m}(0)\ln\left(\frac{\xi_{\rm m}}{z_{0}}\right).

(24)

Since
\sigma_{\rm m}(0)\propto\tau_{\rm m}=(2m^{*}\Gamma/\hbar)\xi_{\rm m}^{2}
, the explicit dependence on
T-T_{c}
cancels out in
\sigma_{\rm m}(0)/\xi_{\rm m}^{2}
.
As a result, the noise no longer diverges algebraically with temperature, but instead saturates, up to a weak logarithmic dependence on either
z_{0}
or
T-T_{c}
.
This saturation is evident in Fig.
2
, appearing both in the
t\to 0
limit of panel (a) and in the
z_{0}\to 0
limit of panel (b).

III.2
Superconducting side

We next turn to investigate magnetic noise on the superconducting side of the transition.
While such a transition is typically a BKT transition as discussed previously, there can be a temperature window where order parameter fluctuations about the local pairing amplitude dominates magnetic noise.
Here, we consider such Gaussian fluctuations in a quasi-long-range ordered phase, and delineate the corresponding noise signatures — partly motivated by potentially similar observations in a recent experiment on a thin film high
T_{c}
cuprate
45
.
However, we carefully note that non-linear effects or vortex-antivortex fluctuations, as discussed in Ref.
15
, will determine the magnetic noise at temperatures very close to the transition.

We express the order parameter in the amplitude–phase representation as,

\displaystyle\psi(\bm{\rho},t)=\sqrt{M_{0}+\chi(\bm{\rho},t)}e^{i\theta(\bm{\rho},t)},

with
M_{0}=|r|/u
denoting the homogeneous average density evaluated at the mean-field level, and
\chi,\theta
the density and phase fluctuations, respectively.
Although the mean local pairing amplitude is set by
\sqrt{M_{0}}
, the order parameter correlations have a power law decay due to strong fluctuations of the phase
\theta(\bm{\rho},t)
.
In this representation, the current is given by,

{\bm{j}}(\bm{\rho})=(\hbar e^{*}/m^{*})\,\mathrm{Im}(\psi^{*}\nabla\psi)=(\hbar e^{*}/m^{*})[M_{0}+\chi(\bm{\rho})]\nabla\theta(\bm{\rho})
.
Converting to momentum space, we find the transverse current to be,

{\bm{j}}^{\mathrm{T}}_{{\bm{q}},\Omega}=\frac{i\hbar e^{*}}{m^{*}}\int_{{\bm{k}},\nu}{\bm{k}}_{\perp}\chi_{{\bm{q}}-{\bm{k}},\Omega-\nu}\theta_{{\bm{k}},\nu}.

(25)

This indicates that
both amplitude and phase fluctuations
are required for a non-zero transverse current density, as phase gradients are purely longitudinal.
Therefore, to compute the transverse conductivity, we first require the correlation functions of the amplitude and phase fluctuations,
\chi
and
\theta
.
To this end, we linearize Eq. (
15
) in these variables, upon which we obtain the following Langevin equations.

\begin{split}\hbar\dot{\chi}&=-\Gamma\Big(2uM_{0}\chi-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m^{*}}\nabla^{2}\chi\Big)+\zeta_{\chi},\\
\hbar\dot{\theta}&=\Gamma\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m^{*}}\nabla^{2}\theta+\zeta_{\theta},\end{split}

(26)

where
\zeta_{\chi}=2\sqrt{M_{0}}\operatorname{Re}\zeta
and
\zeta_{\theta}=\operatorname{Im}\zeta/\sqrt{M_{0}}
are zero-mean Gaussian noises with correlations:

\begin{split}\langle\zeta_{\chi}(\bm{\rho},t)\zeta_{\chi}(\bm{\rho}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\rangle&=4\hbar M_{0}\Gamma k_{B}T\delta(t-t^{\prime})\delta^{(2)}(\bm{\rho}-\bm{\rho}^{\prime}),\\
\langle\zeta_{\theta}(\bm{\rho},t)\zeta_{\theta}(\bm{\rho}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\rangle&=\frac{\hbar\Gamma k_{B}T}{M_{0}}\delta(t-t^{\prime})\delta^{(2)}(\bm{\rho}-\bm{\rho}^{\prime}).\end{split}

(27)

Crucially, we notice that
\chi
and
\theta
are decoupled, and
\theta
is a massless field (the would-be Goldstone mode in case of true spontaneous symmetry breaking), while the density fluctuations
\chi
are massive.
From Eq. (
26
), we find the correlation functions of
\chi
and
\theta
to be

\begin{split}C_{\chi\chi}({\bm{q}},t)&=\frac{2k_{B}TM_{0}}{\frac{\hbar^{2}{\bm{q}}^{2}}{2m^{*}}+2uM_{0}}e^{-\Gamma\big(\frac{\hbar^{2}{\bm{q}}^{2}}{2m^{*}}+2uM_{0}\big)|t|/\hbar},\\
C_{\theta\theta}({\bm{q}},t)&=\frac{k_{B}T}{\frac{\hbar^{2}{\bm{q}}^{2}}{m^{*}}M_{0}}e^{-\Gamma\frac{\hbar^{2}{\bm{q}}^{2}}{2m^{*}}|t|/\hbar}.\end{split}

(28)

Here, since
\chi
and
\theta
are decoupled in Eq. (
26
), their cross-correlations vanish.
Furthermore, Eq. (
28
) shows that the amplitude mode has a correlation length, i.e., superconducting coherence length,
\xi_{\rm sc}=\sqrt{\hbar^{2}/(4m^{*}uM_{0})}=\sqrt{\hbar^{2}/(4m^{*}|r|)}
, and that its lifetime (for
q=0
) is given by
\tau_{\rm sc}=\hbar/(2uM_{0}\Gamma)
.

From the transverse current-current correlator, we find that the transverse conductivity is given by,

\sigma_{\mathrm{T}}^{\rm sc}(q)=\frac{1}{2k_{B}T}\left(\frac{\hbar e^{*}}{m^{*}}\right)^{2}\int_{{\bm{k}},t}{\bm{k}}_{\perp}^{2}C_{\chi\chi}({\bm{k}}-{\bm{q}},t)C_{\theta\theta}({\bm{k}},t).

(29)

Applying Eq. (
28
) to Eq. (
29
) and evaluating the integral (see Appendix
A
), we finally arrive at the scaling form for the transverse conductivity on the superconducting side.

\begin{split}&\sigma_{\mathrm{T}}^{\rm sc}(q)=\sigma_{\rm sc}(0)F_{\rm sc}(q\xi_{\rm sc})\,,\textrm{ where }\\
&\sigma_{\rm sc}(0)=\frac{\ln 2}{\pi}\left(\frac{e^{*}}{\hbar}\right)^{2}k_{B}T\,\tau_{\rm sc}\\
&F_{\rm sc}(\tilde{q})=\frac{1}{\tilde{q}^{2}\ln 2}\left[\ln\left(4\frac{1+\tilde{q}^{2}}{2+\tilde{q}^{2}}\right)-\frac{\ln(2+\tilde{q}^2)}{1+\tilde{q}^{2}}\right].\end{split}

(30)

\sigma_{\rm sc}(0)
denotes the zero-momentum paramagnetic conductivity, and
F_{\rm sc}(\tilde{q})
stands for the scaling function
4
4

4

Note that the diverging conductance of superconductor come from the diamagnetic contribution to the conductance, and the paragmagnetic contribution
\sigma_{\rm sc}(0)
– driven by supercurrent fluctuations – is finite
.

Applying Eq. (
30
) to Eq. (
5
), we arrive at the magnetic noise on the superconducting side.
The results are plotted in Fig.
2
, where we plot the magnetic noise as a function of
|T-T_{c}|
and also as a function of the sample-NV distance
z_{0}
.
We find qualitatively similar behaviors to the metallic side of the transition.

Away from criticality, for coherence length
\xi_{\rm sc}\ll z_{0}
, we find the magnetic noise to scale as,

\mathcal{N}_{\rm sc}=\frac{\mu_{0}^{2}k_{B}T}{16\pi z_{0}^{2}}\sigma_{\rm sc}(0)\,.

(31)

Since
\sigma_{\rm sc}(0)\propto\tau_{\rm sc}
, the noise diverges as
(T_{c}-T)^{-1}
as the transition is approached, as shown in Fig.
2
(c).
Furthermore, the prefactor reveals that the noise decays as
z_{0}^{-2}
with the qubit-sample distance, as shown in Fig.
2
(d).

On the other hand, as we approach the critical point, and the coherence length
\xi_{\rm sc}\gg z_{0}
, the magnetic noise becomes approximately
z_{0}
independent and scales as,

\mathcal{N}_{\rm sc}\propto\frac{\mu_{0}^{2}k_{B}T}{\xi_{\rm sc}^{2}}\sigma_{\rm sc}(0)\ln\left(\frac{\xi_{\rm sc}}{z_{0}}\right)\,.

(32)

Since
\sigma_{\rm sc}(0)/\xi_{\rm sc}^{2}
has no explicit dependence on
T_{c}-T
, the noise does not diverge algebraically but instead saturates up to a logarithmic dependence, as seen in Fig.
2
.

IV
Magnetic noise at criticality in a field

IV.1
Metallic side

We now evaluate the magnetic noise near criticality in the presence of a finite magnetic field.
A background field
{\bm{H}}=H\hat{z}
not only enhances critical fluctuations and lowers
T_{c}
, but also introduces an additional lengthscale — the magnetic length
\ell=\sqrt{\hbar/(\mu_{0}e^{*}H)}
.
Our goal is to determine how the magnetic noise depends on the three lengthscales of the NV–sample distance
z_{0}
, the correlation length
\xi
, and the magnetic length
\ell
.
In the limit
H\to 0
, our results reduce smoothly to the zero-field expressions.
As we show below, the magnetic noise acquires a linear-in-
H
enhancement at small fields, reflecting an amplification of fluctuation-driven noise in the presence of a background magnetic field.

We model the dynamics of the order parameter by extending the time-dependent Ginzburg Landau approach in Ref.
53
.
To this end, we introduce the gauge-covariant derivative
D=\nabla-ie^{*}\mathbf{A}/\hbar
to the Hamiltonian of Eq. (
15
), with
\nabla\times\mathbf{A}=\mu_{0}{\bm{H}}
,

\mathcal{H}=\int d^{2}\bm{\rho}\left(\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m^{*}}|D\psi|^{2}+r|\psi|^{2}+\frac{u}{2}|\psi|^{4}\right).

The resulting Hamiltonian is invariant under local
U(1)
gauge transformations
\psi\to\psi e^{i\theta}
,
\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{A}+(\hbar/e^{*})\nabla\theta
.
In order to facilitate calculations, we work with the Landau gauge for the static background field,
\mathbf{A}=\mu_{0}H(0,x,0)
.

(34)

Note that in the limit of

.

IV.2
Superconducting side

Analogous to the metallic side, one can carry out a computation of the noise on the superconducting side arising from Gaussian order parameter fluctuations on top of the mean-field pairing amplitude
\langle|\psi_{0}|\rangle
.
However, 2D superconductors cannot effectively screen a three dimensional magnetic field via screening currents restricted to the plane.
Thus, at a finite magnetic field, the system necessarily nucleates vortices, whose dynamics introduce qualitatively new sources of magnetic noise that are not captured by the linearized time-dependent Ginzburg–Landau theory.
Therefore, to describe the experimentally relevant behavior for a 2D superconducting material in a magnetic field, we next develop a complementary framework that incorporates vortex motion and evaluates the magnetic noise arising from their dynamics.

V
Magnetic noise from vortex dynamics

We now consider type-II superconductors placed in an external field such that
H_{c1}<H<H_{c2}
, where the magnetic flux penetrates the superconducting material in quanta of
\Phi_{0}=h/e^{*}
via vortices in the superconducting order parameter.
As discussed previously, we will consider the vortices as classical point defects for analytical convenience.
Our approximation is accurate in the extreme type-II limit where the Ginzburg-Landau parameter
\kappa
— the ratio of the London penetration depth
\lambda_{L}
(Pearl length
\Lambda
in 2D
55
) to the superconducting coherence length
\xi
is large.
Within this limit, we will consider three distinct regimes of vortex dynamics: (i) a single pinned vortex due to impurities (e.g., in a vortex glass phase), (ii) collective phonon modes of an Abrikosov vortex lattice
3
;
2
, and (iii) diffusive motion of vortices in a vortex liquid phase obtained by melting the aforementioned vortex lattice.
In each case, we will demonstrate that the magnetic noise detected by the spin-qubit encodes unique signatures of the relevant dynamics, thereby making it possible to extract physical quantities such as the phonon dispersion and the vortex diffusivity from a careful characterization of the magnetic noise spectrum.

V.1
Fluctuations of a pinned vortex

To build intuition, we first consider magnetic noise due to a single vortex pinned by an impurity potential.
This is physically relevant in a vortex glass phase
23
;
22
when the qubit-sample distance
z_{0}
is much smaller than the inter-vortex spacing, so that the qubit-probe has only one pinned vortex within its field of view.
For simplicity, we will assume that the pinning potential is harmonic, i.e.,
V_{\rm pin}(\rho)=K\rho^{2}/2
, and that the vortex is in local thermal equilibrium with its surroundings.
Thermal fluctuations induce vortex motion, leading to a time-varying magnetic field directly above it and resulting in magnetic noise.
If the qubit-sample distance
z_{0}
is much smaller than the superconducting coherence length
\xi
, then these fluctuations are mostly sourced by metallic currents in the core and reflect the non-local conductivity of the metallic state, considered in Ref.
4
.
Therefore, we will focus on the opposite limit
z_{0}\gg\xi
, where the magnetic fluctuations are instead sourced by circulating super-currents that get displaced due to the motion of the vortex.

To evaluate the magnetic noise from the motion of a single vortex, we will make a few physically motivated assumptions.
First, we will assume that the strength of the pinning potential
K
is larger than the thermal energy scale
k_{B}T
.
Consequently, the amplitude of thermal oscillations of the vortex, set by
\sqrt{k_{B}T/K}

6
6

6

\lambda_{\rm scr}
(penetration depth
\lambda_{L}
or Pearl length
\Lambda
).
This allows us to recast the magnetic noise tensor in terms of correlations of the vortex displacement
{\bm{u}}_{t}
via a Taylor series expansion of the fluctuating magnetic field
{\bm{B}}({\bm{r}}_{q},t)
at the qubit location in the small parameter
{\bm{u}}_{t}/\lambda_{\rm scr}
.
Second, we will consider the spin qubit to be located directly above the equilibrium position of the vortex (assumed to be the origin), i.e.,
{\bm{r}}_{q}=(0,0,z_{0})
.
This leads to a different qualitative distance (
z_{0}
) dependence of

and
\mathcal{N}_{xx}

{\bm{u}}_{t}
does not depend on the
z
-coordinate.
This is true by definition for 2D Pearl vortices, and holds for rigid (rod-like) Abrikosov vortices when the
z
-axis elastic constant
\varepsilon_{l}
is large.
Later, we will study the consequences of relaxing this assumption by including undulations of the vortex axis.

Using these assumptions, we may write the time-varying magnetic field at the qubit as

\displaystyle{\bm{B}}({\bm{r}}_{q},t)

\displaystyle={\bm{B}}_{0}(|{\bm{r}}_{q}-{\bm{u}}_{t}|)

\displaystyle\simeq{\bm{B}}_{0}-(\partial_{\rho}B_{0}^{\rho})_{\rho=0}\;{\bm{u}}_{t}+\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{\rho}^{2}B_{0}^{z})_{\rho=0}\;|{\bm{u}}_{t}|^{2}\hat{z}\,,

where

\displaystyle\partial_{\rho}B_{0}^{\rho}|_{\rho=0}=\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{d^{2}{\bm{k}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,k^{2}e^{-kz_{0}}\phi_{M}({\bm{k}})\,,\text{ and }

\displaystyle\partial_{\rho}^{2}B_{0}^{z}|_{\rho=0}=\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{d^{2}{\bm{k}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,k^{3}e^{-kz_{0}}\phi_{M}({\bm{k}}).

(39)

In Eq. (
39
),
B_{0}^{\rho}
and
B_{0}^{z}
denote the in-plane and vertical components of the magnetic field respectively, defined for a Abrikosov (rigid 3D) or Pearl (2D) vortex via its magnetostatic potential
\phi_{M}
in Eq. (
10
) or Eq. (
7
) (See Appendix
C
for derivation).
Accordingly, the magnetic noise tensor components are given by

\begin{split}&\mathcal{N}_{zz}(\Omega)\simeq\frac{1}{4}(\partial_{\rho}^{2}B_{0}^{z})_{\rho=0}^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\,\ e^{i\Omega t}\langle|{\bm{u}}_{t}|^{2}|{\bm{u}}_{0}|^{2}\rangle\,,\\
&\mathcal{N}_{xx}(\Omega)=\mathcal{N}_{yy}(\Omega)\simeq\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{\rho}B_{0}^{\rho})_{\rho=0}^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\,\ e^{i\Omega t}\langle{\bm{u}}_{t}\cdot{\bm{u}}_{0}\rangle\,.\end{split}

(40)

The distance (
z_{0}
) dependence of the noise tensor is therefore determined by the radial derivatives of
{\bm{B}}_{0}
at the origin, while the frequency dependence encodes the dynamical correlations of the vortex displacement.

Next, we relax the assumption of rigid vortices in the 3D case, and solve the Maxwell-London equations in the bulk without assuming translation invariance in the
z
-direction.
Remarkably, we can still find an analytical expression for the fluctuating magnetic field due to an undulating vortex with
{\bm{u}}_{t}(z)={\bm{u}}_{t}\,e^{ik_{z}z}
as a power series (see Appendix
C
for a derivation):

\displaystyle{\bm{B}}({\bm{r}}_{q},t)=\int\frac{d^{2}{\bm{k}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}(-i{\bm{k}},k)e^{i{\bm{k}}\cdot\bm{\rho}_{q}}e^{-kz_{0}}\phi({\bm{k}},k_{z}),\text{ where }

\displaystyle\phi({\bm{k}},k_{z})=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{[-i{\bm{k}}\cdot{\bm{u}}_{t}(0)]^{m}}{m!}\frac{\Phi_{0}}{k\left(1+\lambda_{L}^{2}[k^{2}+(mk_{z})^{2}]\right)},

(41)

and
\bm{\rho}_{q}
is the in-plane coordinate of the qubit chosen as the
(0,0)
henceforth,
and we have chosen periodic boundary condition for the axial oscillations with wave-vector
k_{z}\in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}/d
, with
d\gg z_{0}
being the sample thickness.
When
k_{z}=0
, the series can be summed exactly, i.e.,
\phi({\bm{k}},0)=e^{-i{\bm{k}}\cdot{\bm{u}}_{t}}
, such that

\displaystyle{\bm{B}}({\bm{r}}_{q},t)

\displaystyle=\Phi_{0}\int\frac{d^{2}{\bm{k}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}(-i{\bm{k}},k)\frac{e^{i{\bm{k}}\cdot(\bm{\rho}_{q}-{\bm{u}}_{t})}e^{-kz_{0}}}{k(1+\lambda_{L}^{2}k^{2})}

\displaystyle={\bm{B}}_{0}(|{\bm{r}}_{q}-{\bm{u}}_{t}|),

(42)

recovering the magnetic field for rigid Abrikosov vortices.
Further, we note that the magnetic potential
\phi({\bm{k}},k_{z})
has Fourier components at precisely the harmonics of
k_{z}
, the oscillation wave-vector of the vortex in the
z
-direction.
These harmonics serve to suppress the magnetic field above the vortex, which may be intuitively understood as partial cancellation of the field arising from different segments of the vortex which are slightly misaligned along its axis.

Once again, to make analytical progress, we assume that we can expand in powers of
{\bm{u}}_{t}(z=0)/\lambda_{L}
, and consider only terms up to the lowest order time-dependent harmonics for
B^{\rho}
(
m
= 1) and
B^{z}
(
m
= 2),

\displaystyle{\bm{B}}({\bm{r}}_{q},t)={\bm{B}}_{0}({\bm{r}}_{q})-\left[\frac{\Phi_{0}}{2}\int\frac{d^{2}{\bm{k}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{k\,e^{-kz_{0}}}{1+\lambda_{L}^{2}(k^{2}+k_{z}^{2})}\right]{\bm{u}}_{t}(0)

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~+\hat{z}\left[\frac{\Phi_{0}}{4}\int\frac{d^{2}{\bm{k}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{k^{2}e^{-kz_{0}}}{1+\lambda_{L}^{2}(k^{2}+4k_{z}^{2})}\right]{\bm{u}}_{t}^{2}(0)+\ldots

\displaystyle\equiv{\bm{B}}_{0}({\bm{r}}_{q})-(\partial_{\rho}B^{\rho})_{\rho=0}\;{\bm{u}}_{t}(0)+\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{\rho}^{2}B^{z})_{\rho=0}\;|{\bm{u}}_{t}(0)|^{2}\hat{z}\,.

(43)

The first term in Eq. (
43
) is simply the static magnetic field of the rigid 3D vortex, while the latter two terms physically capture the effect of finite
k_{z}
by accounting for the suppression of
B^{\rho}
and
B^{z}
respectively.
The final expression for the magnetic field takes the same form as Eq. (
39
), where
{\bm{u}}_{t}(0)
now denotes the coordinate of the end-point of the vortex at the surface (
z=0
), with the caveat that we need to account for the suppression of the field at non-zero
k_{z}
, particularly if
k_{z}\lambda_{L}
is large.
Therefore, in both cases, to determine the magnetic noise due to the thermal motion of a pinned vortex, the task at hand is to calculate the correlation functions of its in-plane displacement
{\bm{u}}_{t}
, which we turn to next.

The Lagrangian for a point-like vortex with mass
\mu_{v}
(per unit length in 3D) is given by
8
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{v}}{2}(\dot{{\bm{u}}}_{t})^{2}+\bm{f}_{\rm M}\cdot{\bm{u}}_{t}-\mathcal{H},

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}_{\rm tension}+\mathcal{H}_{\rm pinning}=\frac{\varepsilon_{l}}{2}\left(\frac{\partial{\bm{u}}_{t}}{\partial z}\right)^{2}+\frac{K{\bm{u}}_{t}^{2}}{2},

(44)

where
\bm{f}_{\rm M}=n_{s}h(\dot{{\bm{u}}}_{t}\times\hat{z})
corresponds to the Magnus force on the vortex,
n_{s}
is the superfluid density,
\varepsilon_{l}
is the line tension of the vortex in the
z
-direction, (see Appendix
E.1
for a derivation of
\varepsilon_{l}
), and we have assumed that the spring constant
K
of the pinning potential is independent of
z
for simplicity.
One can subsequently derive the following equation of motion for the vortex displacement
{\bm{u}}_{t}(z)
:

\mu_{v}\ddot{{\bm{u}}}_{t}=n_{s}h(\dot{{\bm{u}}}_{t}\times\hat{z})-K{\bm{u}}_{t}+\varepsilon_{l}\partial_{z}^{2}{\bm{u}}_{t}-\eta\dot{{\bm{u}}}_{t}+\bm{\zeta}_{t}\,,

(45)

where we have added a phenomenological damping term characterized by the drag coefficient
\eta
.
In the Bardeen-Stephen model
5
which considers dissipation arising from the normal metallic core of a vortex in a fully gapped s-wave superconductor,
\eta=\Phi_{0}\mu_{0}H_{c2}/\rho_{n}
(where
\rho_{n}
is the normal-state resistivity), but here we will simply treat
\eta
as a phenomenological drag coefficient.
Since the dissipative drag would damp the motion of the vortex, we have also added a stochastic force
\bm{\zeta}_{t}
which provides thermal kicks to keep it in motion, as required for non-zero magnetic field fluctuations at long times.
For simplicity, we assume the stochastic force to be uncorrelated in time, with an amplitude determined by the FDT:

\langle{\zeta^{\alpha}_{t}\zeta^{\beta}_{t^{\prime}}\rangle}=2\eta k_{B}T\,\delta^{\alpha\beta}\,\delta(t-t^{\prime}).

(46)

In 3D, the stochastic force per unit length
\zeta_{t}(z)
depends on
z
.
In this case, we further assume that
\zeta_{t}(z)
is uncorrelated in the
z
-direction, implying that Eq. (
46
) gets modified to:

\langle{\zeta^{\alpha}_{t}(z)\zeta^{\beta}_{t’}(z^{\prime})\rangle}=2\eta k_{B}T\,\delta^{\alpha\beta}\,\left(\frac{\delta_{z,z^{\prime}}}{a_{c}}\right)\delta(t-t’),

(47)

where we have introduced the spacing
a_{c}
between superconducting planes in the
z
-direction, and used a lattice discretization of the Dirac-delta function
\delta_{z,z^{\prime}}=a_{c}\,\delta(z-z^{\prime})
such that Eq. (
47
) is dimensionally consistent.
A schematic of the vortex motion, with all the different forces acting on it, is shown in Fig.
4
(a).

To solve the equation of motion and find the desired correlations, we focus on the more general case of an Abrikosov (3D) vortex which can undulate in the z-direction.
Specifically, we consider the following Fourier-space ansatz for the displacement

{\bm{u}}_{t}(z)={\bm{u}}_{\Omega,k_{z}}e^{i(k_{z}z-\Omega t)}.

(48)

Eq. (
45
) can then be recast as a matrix equation

\displaystyle\left[M_{\Omega,k_{z}}^{-1}\right]_{\alpha\beta}u^{\beta}_{\Omega,k_{z}}=\zeta^{\beta}_{\Omega,k_{z}},\text{ where }M_{\Omega,k_{z}}^{-1}=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}-\mu_{v}\Omega^{2}-i\eta\Omega+\overline{K}(k_{z})&in_{s}h\Omega\\
-in_{s}h\Omega&-\mu_{v}\Omega^{2}-i\eta\Omega+\overline{K}(k_{z})\end{pmatrix},

(49)

and
\overline{K}(k_{z})=K+\varepsilon_{l}k_{z}^{2}
is the effective spring constant. Thereafter, we can write down the correlation function by inverting the matrix equation and using Eq. (
46
) (see Appendix
D
for details):

\langle u^{\alpha}_{\Omega,k_{z}}u^{\beta}_{-\Omega,-k_{z}}\rangle=2(\eta/a_{c})k_{B}T[M_{\Omega,k_{z}}M^{\dagger}_{\Omega,k_{z}}]^{\alpha\beta}.

(50)

Using Eqs. (
40
) and (
50
) and substituting
\partial_{\rho}B_{0}^{\rho}\to\partial_{\rho}B^{\rho}
to account for
k_{z}\neq 0
, we arrive at the following expression for
\mathcal{N}_{xx}
:

\mathcal{N}_{xx}(\Omega)=(\partial_{\rho}B^{\rho})_{\rho=0}^{2}\,(\eta/a_{c})k_{B}T\,\Tr[M_{\Omega,k_{z}}M^{\dagger}_{\Omega,k_{z}}].

(51)

Evaluating the trace leads to the following expression for
\mathcal{N}_{xx}(\Omega)
(see Appendix
D
for derivation):

\mathcal{N}_{xx}(\Omega)=\left(\partial_{\rho}B_{0}^{\rho}\right)_{\rho=0}^{2}\left(\frac{2\eta k_{B}T}{a_{c}}\right)\frac{[\overline{K}(k_{z})-\mu_{v}\Omega^{2}]^{2}+[\eta^{2}+(n_{s}h)^{2}]\Omega^{2}}{\left\{[\overline{K}(k_{z})-\mu_{v}\Omega^{2}]^{2}-[\eta^{2}+(n_{s}h)^{2}]\Omega^{2}\right\}^{2}+4\eta^{2}\Omega^{2}[\overline{K}(k_{z})-\mu_{v}\Omega^{2}]^{2}}.

(52)

Since the full expression for
\mathcal{N}_{xx}(\Omega)
is cumbersome to interpret, we next focus on certain simple physical limits [depicted in Fig.
4
(b)].

First, in the low-frequency limit
\Omega\to 0
or the nearly massless vortex limit
\mu_{v}\to 0
, the inertial
\mu_{v}\Omega^{2}
term may be neglected, and the Magnus force dominates vortex motion. Thus, the resonant modes are circularly polarized with
\Omega_{c}=\overline{K}(k_{z})/(n_{s}h)
for weak damping
\eta
.
Consequently,
\mathcal{N}_{xx}(\Omega)
takes the form of a Lorentzian peaked at
\Omega=\pm\Omega_{c}
, with a line-width set by the viscous damping
\eta
(which is assumed to be small),

\mathcal{N}_{xx}(\Omega)=(\partial_{\rho}B^{\rho})_{\rho=0}^{2}\,\left[\frac{2(\eta/a_{c})k_{B}T(\Omega_{c}^{2}+\Omega^{2})}{(n_{s}h)^{2}(\Omega^{2}-\Omega_{c}^{2})^{2}+4\eta^{2}\Omega^{2}\Omega_{c}^{2}}\right].

(53)

We note that as
\eta\to 0
, the Lorentzian approaches a Dirac-Delta function. Accordingly, the spin-qubit is able to find the resonant mode frequencies at different
k_{z}
, by showing a sharp response when its frequency
\Omega
approaches
\Omega_{c}
.
This information may be used to extract the strength
K
of the pinning potential, the vortex-line tension
\varepsilon_{l}
, and the superfluid density
n_{s}
.

Second, in the massive vortex limit or if the superfluid density
n_{s}
is low, we may neglect the Magnus force on the vortices, and keep only the inertial term.
Thus, the resonant mode is linearly polarized and features a distinct dispersion
\Omega_{l}=(\overline{K}(k_{z})/\mu_{v})^{\frac{1}{2}}
, and
\mathcal{N}_{xx}(\Omega)
remains Lorentzian but peaks at different resonance frequencies,

\mathcal{N}_{xx}(\Omega)=(\partial_{\rho}B^{\rho})_{\rho=0}^{2}\,\left[\frac{2(\eta/a_{c})k_{B}T}{\mu_{v}^{2}(\Omega^{2}-\Omega_{l}^{2})^{2}+\eta^{2}\Omega^{2}}\right].

(54)

In this case, the sharp increase in the relaxation rate of the spin-qubit on tuning its frequency
\Omega\to\Omega_{l}
may be used to identify the strength
K
of the pinning potential, the vortex-line tension
\varepsilon_{l}
, and the vortex mass
\mu_{v}
.

We now consider the limit when the viscous damping
\eta
is large, such that we may neglect both the Magnus force
n_{s}h\Omega
and the inertial term
\mu_{v}\Omega^{2}
.
In this limit,
\mathcal{N}_{xx}
takes a particularly simple form:

\mathcal{N}_{xx}(\Omega)=(\partial_{\rho}B^{\rho})_{\rho=0}^{2}\,\left[\frac{2(\eta/a_{c})k_{B}T}{\overline{K}(k_{z})^{2}+\eta^{2}\Omega^{2}}\right].

(55)

We note that the resonant peaks are washed out by strong viscous damping.
Nevertheless, even in this limit, we may use the magnetic noise to extract
K
and
\varepsilon_{l}
, provided one can excite different
k_{z}
modes.

We conclude our discussion of
\mathcal{N}_{xx}
for an isolated pinned vortex by noting that our results for a line vortex modulated by a single longitudinal mode
k_{z}
can be generalized straightforwardly to a superposition of modes.
Assuming periodic boundary conditions in the
z
-direction for simplicity, we expand the displacement
{\bm{u}}_{t}(z)
in terms of a complete set of eigenmodes
{\bm{u}}_{tM}
, where the
M^{\rm th}
mode has
k_{z}=2\pi M/d
:

{\bm{u}}_{t}(z)=\sum_{M}{\bm{u}}_{tM}e^{ik_{M}z}.

Then, to leading order in the displacement, we may write the deviation of the in-plane component of the magnetic field (which contributes to the transverse noise) from its equilibrium value as

\displaystyle\delta{\bm{B}}_{\|}({\bm{r}}_{q},t)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{M}\left[\frac{\Phi_{0}}{2}\int\frac{d^{2}{\bm{k}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{k\,e^{-kz_{0}}}{1+\lambda_{L}^{2}\left(k^{2}+k_{M}^{2}\right)}\right]{\bm{u}}_{tM}.

(56)

Within the Langevin dynamics described in Eqs. (
45
) and (
46
), the modes
{\bm{u}}_{tM}
are decoupled from one another for different
k_{M}
.
As a result,
\langle{u^{\alpha}_{tM}u^{\beta}_{t^{\prime}M^{\prime}}\rangle}\propto\delta_{MM^{\prime}}
.
The total magnetic noise therefore decomposes into a sum of independent modal contributions, with each mode contributing a noise spectrum given by Eq. (
52
).
In particular, this implies that
\mathcal{N}_{xx}(\Omega)
features a series of resonant peaks corresponding to the different
k_{z}
modes as the qubit frequency
\Omega
is tuned, provided the viscous damping
\eta
is weak [Fig.
4
(b), inset] (for further details, we refer the reader to Appendix
D
, specifically Eq. (
172
)).

So far, our discussion has focused primarily on the noise spectrum
\mathcal{N}_{xx}(\Omega)
.
Analogous information about vortex dynamics can also be extracted from
\mathcal{N}_{zz}(\Omega)
.
Unlike
\mathcal{N}_{xx}
, however,
\mathcal{N}_{zz}
depends on the correlation function of the squared vortex displacements,
\langle|{\bm{u}}_{t}|^{2}|{\bm{u}}_{0}|^{2}\rangle
according to Eq. (
40
).
This quantity is not independent: it can be expressed entirely in terms of the displacement correlators in Eq. (
50
).
Explicitly,

\langle|{\bm{u}}_{t}|^{2}|{\bm{u}}_{0}|^{2}\rangle=\langle|{\bm{u}}|^{2}\rangle^{2}+\sum_{\alpha,\beta}2\langle u^{\alpha}_{t}u^{\beta}_{0}\rangle^{2}\,.

(57)

In Eq. (
57
), the first term on the RHS is independent of time, and has no spectral weight at any non-zero frequency.
The second term, on the other hand, is the square of the two-point correlator and has spectral weight concentrated on twice the resonant frequency in the weak damping limit.
Thus,
\mathcal{N}_{zz}(\Omega)
probes the same vortex dynamics through higher-order correlations, yielding peaks at twice the resonant frequencies with approximately double the linewidth of the peaks of
\mathcal{N}_{xx}(\Omega)
.

To make this connection explicit, we again focus on three distinct analytically tractable regimes of
\mathcal{N}_{zz}(\Omega)
[Fig.
4
(c)] (for the full integral expression, see Eq. (
182
) in Appendix
D
).
We first consider the massless vortex limit (
\mu_{v}\to 0
).
In this regime, the magnetic noise takes the form

\mathcal{N}_{zz}(\Omega)=(\partial_{\rho}^{2}B^{z})_{\rho=0}^{2}\frac{2\eta}{\tilde{\eta}^{2}\overline{K}(k_{z})}\left(\frac{k_{B}T}{a_{c}}\right)^{2}\left[\frac{1}{(\Omega-2\Omega_{c})^{2}+\left(2\eta\overline{K}(k_{z})/\tilde{\eta}^{2}\right)^{2}}+\frac{1}{(\Omega+2\Omega_{c})^{2}+\left(2\eta\overline{K}(k_{z})/\tilde{\eta}^{2}\right)^{2}}\right]\,,

(58)

revealing resonant peaks at
\Omega=\pm 2\Omega_{c}
, where
\Omega_{c}=\overline{K}(k_{z})/(n_{s}h)
for weak damping (
\eta\ll n_{s}h
) denotes the resonant frequency of the circularly polarized vortex modes.

Second, we consider the massive vortex limit, corresponding to low superfluid density
n_{s}
, where the Magnus force may be neglected.
In this regime, the noise spectrum can be approximated by the following analytical expression with resonant features appearing at
\Omega=\pm 2\Omega_{l}
, where
\Omega_{l}=(\overline{K}(k_{z})/\mu_{v})^{\frac{1}{2}}
, in the limit of weak damping (
\eta\ll\mu_{v}\Omega_{l}
):

\mathcal{N}_{zz}(\Omega)\approx(\partial_{\rho}^{2}B^{z})_{\rho=0}^{2}\frac{\eta}{2\mu_{v}}\left(\frac{k_{B}T}{a_{c}\overline{K}(k_{z})}\right)^{2}\left[\frac{1}{\left(\Omega-2\Omega_{l}\right)^{2}+(\eta/\mu_{v})^{2}}+\frac{1}{\left(\Omega+2\Omega_{l}\right)^{2}+(\eta/\mu_{v})^{2}}+\frac{2}{\Omega^{2}+(\eta/\mu_{v})^{2}}\right].

(59)

Finally, in the damping dominated regime (
\eta\gg n_{s}h,\mu_{v}\Omega
), the noise spectrum simplifies to

\mathcal{N}_{zz}(\Omega)=(\partial_{\rho}^{2}B^{z})_{\rho=0}^{2}\frac{4}{\eta\overline{K}(k_{z})}\left(\frac{k_{B}T}{a_{c}}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{\Omega^{2}+(2\overline{K}(k_{z})/\eta)^{2}}\,,

(60)

which shows that in the limit of strong damping, the resonant peaks are washed out.

In analogy with
\mathcal{N}_{xx}(\Omega)
, we observe that the resonant mode frequencies and associated physical quantities, such as the strength of the pinning potential
K
, the vortex line tension
\varepsilon_{l}
, the vortex mass
\mu_{v}
and the superfluid density
n_{s}
may also be extracted from
\mathcal{N}_{zz}(\Omega)
in the weak damping regime.
We further note from
eqs.

58
,
59
and
60
that the explicit temperature dependence of
\mathcal{N}_{zz}
is markedly different from
\mathcal{N}_{xx}
, which may be used to isolate these two components.
This distinct temperature dependence arises from the fact that the
z
-component of the magnetic field is maximum directly above the vortex, where the spin qubit is placed.
As a result,
\mathcal{N}_{zz}
probes the correlations of the square of the vortex displacement
{\bm{u}}_{t}^{2}
, and scales as
\langle u^{2}\rangle^{2}\propto(k_{B}T)^{2}
via equipartition theorem.
Additionally, there are implicit temperature dependences via the drag
\eta
, the vortex line tension
\varepsilon_{l}
and possibly the pinning potential
K
, but these are expected to be relatively weak at low temperatures.

\mathcal{N}_{xx}

.

To conclude this section, we discuss the dependence of the noise on the qubit-sample distance
z_{0}
.
To this end, we explictly evaluate
\partial_{\rho}B_{0}^{\rho}
and
\partial_{\rho}^{2}B_{0}^{z}
at the coordinate origin
\bm{\rho}=0
.
For 2D (Pearl) vortices,
{\bm{B}}={\bm{B}}_{0}
by definition, as the vortex is a point defect with no notion of
k_{z}
.
The dependence of
\mathcal{N}_{xx}
and
\mathcal{N}_{zz}
on
z_{0}
can be found by evaluating the integrals in Eq. (
39
) and subsequently using Eq. (
40
): here we focus on two relevant limits (for the full expressions, see Appendix
C
).
If the qubit-sample distance
z_{0}
is much smaller than the Pearl length
\Lambda
, then

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{xx}(z_{0})

\displaystyle\propto(\partial_{\rho}B_{0}^{\rho})_{\rho=0}^{2}\xrightarrow{z_{0}/\Lambda\ll 1}\left(\frac{\Phi_{0}}{4\pi\Lambda}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{z_{0}^{4}},\text{ and }

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{zz}(z_{0})

\displaystyle\propto(\partial_{\rho}^{2}B_{0}^{z})_{\rho=0}^{2}\xrightarrow{z_{0}/\Lambda\ll 1}\left(\frac{\Phi_{0}}{2\pi\Lambda}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{z_{0}^{6}}.

(61)

By contrast, if the qubit-sample distance
z_{0}
is much larger than the Pearl length
\Lambda
, then

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{xx}(z_{0})

\displaystyle\propto(\partial_{\rho}B_{0}^{\rho})_{\rho=0}^{2}\xrightarrow{z_{0}/\Lambda\gg 1}\left(\frac{\Phi_{0}}{2\pi}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{z_{0}^{6}},\text{ and }

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{zz}(z_{0})

\displaystyle\propto(\partial_{\rho}^{2}B_{0}^{z})_{\rho=0}^{2}\xrightarrow{z_{0}/\Lambda\gg 1}\left(\frac{3\Phi_{0}}{2\pi}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{z_{0}^{8}}.

(62)

We note that the distinct
z_{0}
dependence of
\mathcal{N}_{xx}
and
\mathcal{N}_{zz}
can be used to detect an oscillating pinned vortex, even when it cannot be resolved using static magnetic field measurements by the qubit sensor.
Further,
\mathcal{N}_{xx}
decays more slowly with qubit-sample distance
z_{0}
than
\mathcal{N}_{zz}
in both the near-field and far-field scenarios, potentially making
\mathcal{N}_{xx}
easier to measure in experiments.
We confirm these asymptotic scalings by an exact numerical evaluation of the magnetic noise for a fluctuating Pearl vortex in Fig.
5
(a),(b).

Next, we consider Abrikosov vortices, which are line defects, and find the general expression for vortex undulations along its axis (
k_{z}\neq 0
).
Since the analytical expressions for the derivatives of the field in Eq. (
43
) are cumbersome, we relegate these expressions to Appendix
D
, and discuss the limiting cases for the noise tensor components.
If the qubit-sample distance
z_{0}
is much smaller than the London penetration depth
\lambda_{L}
, then

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{xx}(z_{0})

\displaystyle\propto(\partial_{\rho}B_{0}^{\rho})_{\rho=0}^{2}\xrightarrow{z_{0}/\lambda_{L}\ll 1}\left(\frac{\Phi_{0}}{4\pi\lambda_{L}^{2}}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{z_{0}^{2}},\text{ and }

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{zz}(z_{0})

\displaystyle\propto(\partial_{\rho}^{2}B_{0}^{z})_{\rho=0}^{2}\xrightarrow{z_{0}/\lambda_{L}\ll 1}\left(\frac{\Phi_{0}}{4\pi\lambda_{L}^{2}}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{z_{0}^{4}}.

(63)

On the other hand, if the qubit-sample distance
z_{0}
is much larger than the London penetration depth
\lambda_{L}
, then

\begin{split}\mathcal{N}_{xx}(z_{0})&\propto(\partial_{\rho}B_{0}^{\rho})_{\rho=0}^{2}\xrightarrow{z_{0}/\lambda_{L}\gg 1}\left(\frac{\Phi_{0}}{2\pi[1+(\lambda_{L}k_{z})^{2}]}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{z_{0}^{6}},\\
\mathcal{N}_{zz}(z_{0})&\propto(\partial_{\rho}^{2}B_{0}^{z})_{\rho=0}^{2}\xrightarrow{z_{0}/\lambda_{L}\gg 1}\left(\frac{3\Phi_{0}}{2\pi[1+(2\lambda_{L}k_{z})^{2}]}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{z_{0}^{8}}.\\
\end{split}

(64)

Similar to the 2D case,
\mathcal{N}_{zz}
is suppressed relative to
\mathcal{N}_{xx}
as a function of
z_{0}
, although the specific power laws differ in the near-field limit
z_{0}\ll\lambda_{L},\Lambda
owing to the weaker screening of electromagnetic fields in 2D.
Once again, these asymptotic scalings for a fluctuating Abrikosov vortex are confirmed via exact numerical evaluations of the noise in Fig.
5
(c),(d).

A few comments are in order.
First we note from Eq. (
63
) that the finite
k_{z}
effects are irrelevant when the qubit-sample distance
z_{0}
is much smaller than the London penetration depth
\lambda_{L}
.
This fits our expectations that electromagnetic screening effects do not play an important role in determining the magnetic noise when
z_{0}
is much smaller than
\lambda_{L}
, since the qubit approximately probes the field fluctuations within an area
\sim z_{0}^{2}
on the sample.
Second, we observe that in the far-field limit
z_{0}\gg\lambda_{L}
(Eq. (
64
)), non-zero
k_{z}
reduces the magnetic noise due to an overall reduction of the strength of the field, as intuitively expected.
This reduction is particularly important if
k_{z}\lambda_{L}\gg 1
, i.e., the vortex undulates several times along the
z
-axis within the penetration depth, leading to an efficient cancellation of the field from different segments of the vortex.
Finally, comparing Eqs. (
62
) and (
64
), we find that in the far-field limit, the magnetic noise is identical for Pearl vortices and rigid Abrikosov vortices (
k_{z}=0
), and does not depend on the electromagnetic screening length
\lambda_{\rm scr}
.
This happens because at distances
z_{0}\gg\lambda_{\rm scr}
, the magnetic field of a vortex resembles that of a point magnetic monopole at the origin, i.e,.
{\bm{B}}({\bm{r}})=\Phi_{0}\hat{{\bm{r}}}/(2\pi r^{2})
in both cases, so its variation as the vortex moves is simply set by this Coulomb-like magnetic field.
By contrast, comparing Eqs. (
61
) and (
63
) tells us that in the near-field limit
z_{0}\ll\lambda_{\rm scr}
, the field fluctuations scale differently for 2D and rigid Abrikosov vortices, leading to distinct
z_{0}
dependencies and potentially providing a route to identifying the dimensionality of the pinned vortex.

V.2
Collective modes: Vortex lattice phonons

Having discussed noise signatures of a single fluctuating vortex, we now consider the noise signatures of the collective modes of a vortex lattice — the vortex phonon modes.
To this end, we assume that the vortices form a two dimensional triangular lattice of lattice constant
a_{\Delta}
with primitive vectors
\{\bm{a}_{1},\bm{a}_{2}\}
, such that their equilibrium positions are given by
{\bm{R}}_{i}=m\bm{a}_{1}+n\bm{a}_{2}
(
m,n\in\mathbb{Z}
).
The low-energy fluctuations of the vortex lattice correspond to gapless Goldstone modes of the broken continuous translation symmetry.
To describe these modes, we write the time-dependent displacement of the vortex cores as
{\bm{r}}_{i}(t)={\bm{R}}_{i}+{\bm{u}}_{i}(t)
, where we again assume that
|{\bm{u}}_{i}|
is small compared to the lattice spacing
a_{\Delta}
.
These fluctuations of the vortex positions lead to a time-dependent magnetic field at the location of the qubit, and contribute to magnetic noise that leads to its depolarization.

Intuitively, we expect the magnetic noise to depend on the longitudinal fluctuations of vortices (i.e.,
{\bm{u}}_{\bm{k}}\,\|\,\hat{{\bm{k}}}
).
The simplest way to understand this dependence is to consider the limit of large qubit-sample distance
z_{0}\gg\Lambda,\lambda_{L},a_{\Delta}
, where the magnetic field
{\bm{B}}({\bm{r}})=\Phi_{0}\hat{{\bm{r}}}/(2\pi r^{2})
due to a vortex at the origin resembles that of a magnetic monopole (
z_{0}>0
).
At large distances, the qubit-sensor is not able to resolve individual vortices, so it senses a sheet of uniform
magnetic
sheet charge, which creates a uniform magnetic field (this is precisely the applied field
\bm{H}=H\hat{z}
).
Transverse vortex fluctuations with
{\bm{u}}_{\bm{k}}\perp\hat{{\bm{k}}}
do not change the
magnetic sheet charge density
on the surface of the superconductor.
As a result, the total magnetic field sensed by the qubit does not change with time, and there is no magnetic noise.
By contrast, longitudinal fluctuations with
{\bm{u}}_{\bm{k}}\,\|\,\hat{{\bm{k}}}
lead to local variations of the
magnetic charge density
and lead to a time-varying magnetic field.
Therefore, the magnetic noise should be determined solely by dynamical correlators of longitudinal vortex displacements.

To substantiate the above argument, we now explicitly compute the magnetic field due to the collective modes of a vortex lattice.
We assume that we are deep inside the superconducting phase, so that
z_{0}\gg\Lambda,\lambda_{L}
and further that the field
H
is large enough so that
z_{0}\gg\ell\sim a_{\Delta}
.
For simplicity, we choose the qubit to be at
{\bm{r}}_{q}=(0,0,z_{0})
, as we do not expect the transverse coordinates to affect the magnetic noise in the thermodynamic limit.
Then, the magnetic field (both for 2D Pearl vortices and rigid Abrikosov vortices, we will study the consequences of allowing
z
-axis undulations later) is simply given by the sum of Coulombic fields of each individual vortex:

\displaystyle{\bm{B}}({\bm{r}}_{q},t)=\frac{\Phi_{0}}{2\pi}\sum_{i}\frac{{\bm{r}}_{q}-{\bm{r}}_{i}(t)}{|{\bm{r}}_{q}-{\bm{r}}_{i}(t)|^{3}},

(65)

where the sum runs over all lattice sites
{\bm{R}}_{i}
.
To make analytical progress, we now expand
{\bm{B}}({\bm{r}}_{q},t)
to linear order in
{\bm{u}}_{i}(t)
, in exact analogy to our previous calculation for the single fluctuating vortex:

\displaystyle{\bm{B}}({\bm{r}}_{q},t)

\displaystyle={\bm{B}}_{0}({\bm{r}}_{q})+\delta B_{z}({\bm{r}}_{q},t)\hat{z}+\delta{\bm{B}}_{\|}({\bm{r}}_{q},t),\text{ where }

\displaystyle{\bm{B}}_{0}({\bm{r}}_{q})

\displaystyle=\frac{\Phi_{0}}{2\pi}\left(\sum_{i}\frac{z_{0}}{(z_{0}^{2}+R_{i}^{2})^{3/2}}\right)\hat{z},

\displaystyle\delta B_{z}({\bm{r}}_{q},t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\Phi_{0}}{2\pi}\sum_{i}\frac{3z_{0}{\bm{R}}_{i}\cdot{\bm{u}}_{i}(t)}{(z_{0}^{2}+R_{i}^{2})^{5/2}},\,\text{ and }

\displaystyle\delta{\bm{B}}_{\|}({\bm{r}}_{q},t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Phi_{0}}{2\pi}\sum_{i}\frac{3{\bm{R}}_{i}({\bm{R}}_{i}\cdot{\bm{u}}_{i}(t))-{\bm{u}}_{i}(t)\left(z_{0}^{2}+R_{i}^{2}\right)}{(z_{0}^{2}+R_{i}^{2})^{5/2}}.

(66)

The first term in Eq. (
66
) is the static field due to the vortex lattice.
When
z_{0}
is large, we may take the continuum limit
\sum_{i}\to n_{v}\int d^{2}\bm{\rho}
where
n_{v}=1/A_{\rm uc}=2/(\sqrt{3}a_{\Delta}^{2})
is the vortex density, and explicitly evaluate the integrals.
In this limit, we find that
{\bm{B}}_{0}({\bm{r}}_{q})=n_{v}\Phi_{0}\hat{z}=\mu_{0}{\bm{H}}
, precisely corresponding to the applied external field.

The second and third terms in Eq. (
66
) correspond to the out-of-plane and in-plane fluctuating fields respectively due to vortex motion (to linear order in displacement).
These can be conveniently written in Fourier space as (fixing
{\bm{r}}_{q}=(0,0,z_{0})
for clarity)

\displaystyle\delta B_{z}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{v}}}\sum_{\bm{k}}G_{z}^{\mu}({\bm{k}}){\bm{u}}_{\mu}({\bm{k}},t),

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~G_{z}^{\mu}({\bm{k}})\approx n_{v}\Phi_{0}(-ik_{\mu})e^{-kz_{0}},

\displaystyle[\delta{\bm{B}}_{\|}(t)]^{\mu}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{v}}}\sum_{\bm{k}}G_{\|}^{\mu\nu}({\bm{k}}){\bm{u}}_{\nu}({\bm{k}},t),

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~G_{\|}^{\mu\nu}({\bm{k}})\approx n_{v}\Phi_{0}\left(\frac{k_{\mu}k_{\nu}}{k}\right)e^{-kz_{0}},

(67)

where
N_{v}
is the total number of vortices, and the propagators
G_{\|}^{\mu\nu}({\bm{k}})
and
G_{z}^{\mu}({\bm{k}})
have again been evaluated in the same continuum approximation as
{\bm{B}}_{0}
(see Appendix
F
for a detailed derivation).
Crucially, Eq. (
67
) makes explicit our earlier claim:
\delta{\bm{B}}(t)
depends only on the longitudinal component of the displacement
\hat{{\bm{k}}}\cdot{\bm{u}}_{\bm{k}}
, i.e., transverse vortex motion does not contribute to magnetic noise.
We note that our approximation allows for analytical progress at the expense of periodicity in the Brillouin zone, i.e., the approximate expressions do not obey the constraint
G_{\|}^{\mu\nu}({\bm{k}})=G_{\|}^{\mu\nu}({\bm{k}}+\bm{b}_{\rm rl})
for any reciprocal lattice vector
\bm{b}_{\rm rl}
of the vortex lattice.
Nevertheless, at distances
z_{0}\gg a_{\Delta}
, the qubit-sensor probes low-momentum phonon modes with momenta
ka_{\Delta}\ll 1
: therefore, the deviation of our approximation from the exact value of the propagator at large momenta
|\bm{b}_{\rm rl}|\sim a_{\Delta}^{-1}
is not important.

From Eq. (
67
), one can simply compute the noise tensor components by Fourier transforming in time-domain.
Extending the summation over
{\bm{k}}
in the Brillouin Zone to an integral over all
{\bm{k}}
in the same spirit as the previous continuum approximations, and using translation invariance of the correlation function, we arrive at the following expression for
\mathcal{N}_{xx}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{xx}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\int dt\,\,e^{i\Omega t}\langle\delta B_{x}(t)\,\delta B_{x}(0)\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{v}}\sum_{\bm{k}}G^{x\mu}_{\|}({\bm{k}})G^{x\nu}_{\|}(-{\bm{k}})\int dt\,\,e^{i\Omega t}\langle u^{\mu}_{{\bm{k}}}(t)u^{\nu}_{-{\bm{k}}}(0)\rangle

\displaystyle\to\frac{n_{v}\Phi_{0}^{2}}{2}\int\frac{d^{2}{\bm{k}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\langle{\bm{k}}\cdot{\bm{u}}_{{\bm{k}},\Omega}\;{\bm{k}}\cdot{\bm{u}}_{-{\bm{k}},-\Omega}\rangle e^{-2kz_{0}}.

(68)

Similarly, we can write
\mathcal{N}_{zz}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{zz}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\int dt\,\,e^{i\Omega t}\langle\delta B_{z}(t)\,\delta B_{z}(0)\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{v}}\sum_{\bm{k}}G^{\mu}_{z}({\bm{k}})G^{\nu}_{z}(-{\bm{k}})\int dt\,\,e^{i\Omega t}\langle u^{\mu}_{{\bm{k}}}(t)u^{\nu}_{-{\bm{k}}}(0)\rangle

\displaystyle\to n_{v}\Phi_{0}^{2}\int\frac{d^{2}{\bm{k}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\langle{\bm{k}}\cdot{\bm{u}}_{{\bm{k}},\Omega}\;{\bm{k}}\cdot{\bm{u}}_{-{\bm{k}},-\Omega}\rangle e^{-2kz_{0}}.

(69)

Remarkably,
\mathcal{N}_{zz}=2\mathcal{N}_{xx}
, so it suffices to study the properties of
\mathcal{N}_{zz}
.
Just like the single fluctuating vortex discussed in the previous subsection, the frequency dependence of
\mathcal{N}_{zz}(\Omega)
is determined by the dynamical correlations of the collective vortex vibrations.
However, in contrast to single vortex dynamics, the distance dependence of
\mathcal{N}_{zz}(z_{0})
also depends on these dynamical correlations, which we turn to next.

The low-energy, long-wavelength Lagrangian density
\mathcal{L}
for the (2D/rigid 3D) vortex lattice is given by
8
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\sum_{\bm{k}}

\displaystyle\left\{n_{v}\left[\frac{\mu_{v}}{2}|\dot{{\bm{u}}}_{{\bm{k}}}|^{2}+n_{s}h(\dot{{\bm{u}}}_{{\bm{k}}}\times\hat{z})\cdot{\bm{u}}_{-{\bm{k}}}\right]\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\frac{1}{2}u^{\mu}_{\bm{k}}\phi_{\mu\nu}({\bm{k}})u^{\nu}_{-{\bm{k}}}\right\},

(70)

where
\phi_{\mu\nu}({\bm{k}})=k_{\mu}k_{\nu}c_{11}(k)+(k^{2}\delta_{\mu\nu}-k_{\mu}k_{\nu})c_{66}(k)
is the elastic matrix of the vortex lattice —
c_{11}(k)
being the compression modulus and
c_{66}(k)
being the shear modulus.
Subsequently, we derive the equation of motion for the collective displacement mode
{\bm{u}}_{\bm{k}}(t)
as

\mu_{v}\ddot{u}_{\bm{k}}^{\mu}=n_{s}h\,\varepsilon_{\mu\nu}\,\dot{u}_{{\bm{k}}}^{\nu}-n_{v}^{-1}\phi_{\mu\nu}({\bm{k}})u^{\nu}_{{\bm{k}}}-\eta\dot{u}_{{\bm{k}}}^{\mu}+\zeta^{\mu}_{{\bm{k}}},

(71)

where we have again added a phenomenological viscous drag coefficient
\eta

5
and a local stochastic force
\bm{\zeta}(t)
to account for scattering off high energy modes.
The stochastic force
\bm{\zeta}(t)
(per unit length in 3D) satisfies the FDT:

\langle\zeta^{\mu}_{{\bm{k}}}(t)\zeta^{\nu}_{{\bm{k}}^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\begin{cases}2\eta k_{B}T\delta^{\mu\nu}\,\delta(t-t^{\prime})\,\delta_{{\bm{k}},-{\bm{k}}^{\prime}},\text{ Pearl}\\
2\frac{\eta}{a_{c}}k_{B}T\delta^{\mu\nu}\delta(t-t^{\prime})\delta_{{\bm{k}},-{\bm{k}}^{\prime}}\text{ Abrikosov}\end{cases}.

(72)

To solve Eq. (
71
), we move to the frequency domain and recast the equation of motion as a matrix equation (in analogy with the pinned vortex scenario):

\displaystyle[M^{-1}_{{\bm{k}},\Omega}]_{\mu\nu}u^{\nu}_{{\bm{k}},\Omega}=\zeta^{\mu}_{{\bm{k}},\Omega},

\displaystyle M^{-1}_{{\bm{k}},\Omega}=

\displaystyle\,(c_{11}(k)k^{2}/n_{v}-\mu_{v}\Omega^{2}-i\eta\Omega)P_{\mathrm{L}}({\bm{k}})

\displaystyle+(c_{66}(k)k^{2}/n_{v}-\mu_{v}\Omega^{2}-i\eta\Omega)P_{\mathrm{T}}({\bm{k}})+i\Omega n_{s}h\varepsilon,

(73)

where
[P_{\mathrm{L}}({\bm{k}})]_{\mu\nu}=k_{\mu}k_{\nu}/k^{2}
is the longitudinal projector,
[P_{\mathrm{T}}({\bm{k}})]_{\mu\nu}=\delta_{\mu\nu}-k_{\mu}k_{\nu}/k^{2}
is the transverse projector, and
\varepsilon
is the fully antisymmetric rank-2 tensor in 2D.
The correlation function is obtained by inverting the matrix equation and using Eq. (
72
) (see Appendix
F
for details):

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{L}}({\bm{k}},\Omega)

\displaystyle\equiv\langle{\bm{k}}\cdot{\bm{u}}_{{\bm{k}},\Omega}\,~{\bm{k}}\cdot{\bm{u}}_{-{\bm{k}},-\Omega}\rangle=2\eta k_{B}Tk^{2}\Tr[P_{\mathrm{L}}({\bm{k}})M_{{\bm{k}},\Omega}M^{\dagger}_{{\bm{k}},\Omega}]

(74)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\eta k_{B}Tk^{2}\left[|c_{66}(k)k^{2}/n_{v}-\mu_{v}\Omega^{2}-i\eta\Omega|^{2}+(\Omega n_{s}h)^{2}\right]}{|(c_{11}(k)k^{2}/n_{v}-\mu_{v}\Omega^{2}-i\eta\Omega)(c_{66}(k)k^{2}/n_{v}-\mu_{v}\Omega^{2}-i\eta\Omega)-(\Omega n_{s}h)^{2}|^{2}},

where we have specified to Pearl vortices (for Abrikosov vortices, we have to divide the RHS of Eq. (
74
) by an additional factor of
a_{c}
, and note that
\eta
(
n_{s}
) denotes the viscous drag (superfluid density) per unit length).
In principle, we can find
\mathcal{N}_{zz}(\Omega)
by inserting Eq. (
74
) into Eq. (
69
), and performing a numerical integration over all momenta.
However, in analogy with our discussion of magnetic noise due to a single fluctuating vortex, we focus on simple physical limits.
In our expressions for
C_{\mathrm{L}}({\bm{k}},\Omega)
, we will often set the spacing between superconducting layers
a_{c}=1
for Abrikosov vortices, but we will restore them as appropriate while discussing the noise tensor
\mathcal{N}_{zz}(\Omega)
.

First, in the massless vortex limit, we can set
\mu_{v}=0
and neglect the inertial term
\mu_{v}\Omega^{2}
.
In this limit, the vortex phonons are circularly polarized as a consequence of the Magnus force, with a dispersion
\Omega_{\bm{k}}=\sqrt{c_{11}(k)c_{66}(k)}k^{2}/(n_{v}n_{s}h)

20
.
Therefore, the longitudinally projected correlation function takes the form of a Lorentzian with a peak at
\Omega=\Omega_{\bm{k}}
and a linewidth set by the viscous drag
\eta
and parametrized by the ratio of the drag to the Magnus force
\alpha_{\eta}\equiv\eta/(n_{s}h)
:

C_{\mathrm{L}}({\bm{k}},\Omega)=\frac{2\eta k_{B}Tk^{2}[(c_{66}k^{2}/n_{v}n_{s}h)^{2}+\tilde{\Omega}^{2}]}{(n_{s}h)^{2}[\Omega_{\bm{k}}^{2}-\tilde{\Omega}^{2}]^{2}+\alpha_{\eta}^{2}\Omega^{2}[(c_{11}+c_{66})/n_{v}]^{2}k^{4}},

(75)

where
\tilde{\Omega}=\Omega(1+\alpha_{\eta}^{2})^{1/2}
.
Crucially, the asympototic behavior of the compression modulus
c_{11}(k)
differs at small momenta for Pearl and Abrikosov vortices, leading to distinct dispersion for the phonon modes for 2D and 3D superconducting vortex lattices.
This, in turn, leads to a distinct dependence of
\mathcal{N}_{zz}(\Omega)
on the frequency
\Omega
in the resonant limit.

To find the field-dependence of
c_{11}(k)
and the resulting dispersion of the vortex lattice phonons, we consider intermediate values of the external field
H
, i.e., not too close to
H_{c2}
when the vortex cores strongly overlap, and not too close to
H_{c1}
when the superconductor is about to expel magnetic flux and enter the Meissner phase.
For a lattice of Pearl vortices, the strongly non-local interaction between vortices leads to a divergent Fourier space interaction
V_{\rm int}(k)\sim 1/k
at small
k
: in turn, the compression modulus
c_{11}(k)\approx n_{v}^{2}\,V_{\rm int}(k)
also diverges as
k\to 0
as a compression involves a change of vortex density (see Appendix
F
for a derivation).
At the same time, the shear modulus
c_{66}(k)
has a finite limit
c_{66}
as
k\to 0
as a shear does not change the vortex density, and the resulting energy change is dominated by lattice scale contributions.
More precisely, the elastic moduli for a lattice of Pearl vortices are given by
9

c_{11}(k)\approx\frac{(\mu_{0}H)^{2}}{\mu_{0}k(1+\Lambda k)},~~c_{66}(k)\approx\frac{(\mu_{0}H)\Phi_{0}}{16\mu_{0}\pi\Lambda}\ln\left(\frac{\Lambda}{a_{\Delta}}\right).

(76)

Therefore, the phonon dispersion has a non-analytic momentum dependence for a 2D vortex lattice:

\displaystyle\Omega_{\bm{k}}=\frac{\sqrt{c_{11}(k)c_{66}(k)}k^{2}}{n_{v}n_{s}h}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha k^{3/2}}{\sqrt{1+\Lambda k}},

\displaystyle\text{ where }\alpha

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\Phi_{0}^{3}H}{16\pi\mu_{0}\Lambda(n_{s}h)^{2}}\ln\left(\frac{\Lambda}{a_{\Delta}}\right)}.

(77)

By contrast, for a 3D vortex lattice, electromagnetic screening by the bulk superconductor is more effective: hence,
c_{11}(k)
approaches a finite limit as
k\to 0
while
c_{66}
remains approximately momentum independent
9
:

c_{11}(k)\approx\frac{(\mu_{0}H)^{2}}{\mu_{0}(1+k^{2}\lambda_{L}^{2})},~~c_{66}(k)\approx\frac{(\mu_{0}H)\Phi_{0}}{16\mu_{0}\pi\lambda_{L}^{2}}.

(78)

Hence, the low-energy dispersion for a 3D vortex lattice is quadratic in momentum
20
:

\Omega_{\bm{k}}=\frac{\sqrt{c_{11}(k)c_{66}(k)}k^{2}}{n_{v}n_{s}h}=\frac{\bar{\alpha}k^{2}}{\sqrt{1+k^{2}\lambda_{L}^{2}}},~~\bar{\alpha}=\frac{\sqrt{\pi\Phi_{0}^{3}(\mu_{0}H)}}{4\pi\mu_{0}\lambda_{L}n_{s}h}.

(79)

This difference in dispersion leads to a prominent distinction in the frequency dependence of the magnetic noise.
Such distinction is easiest to see in the resonant limit
(\eta\to 0)
, when the longitudinal projection of the correlator takes the form of a Dirac-delta function:

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{L}}({\bm{k}},\Omega)=\frac{2\pi k_{B}T|\Omega|n_{v}}{c_{11}(k)}\,\delta\left(\Omega_{\bm{k}}^{2}-\Omega^{2}\right).

(80)

The resultant magnetic noise is then simply obtained by using this correlator in Eq. (
69
).

For a lattice of Pearl vortices, in the limit of
\Lambda\ll z_{0}
such that
\Lambda\,k_{\rm typical}\sim\Lambda/z_{0}\ll 1
, we find that the magnetic noise in the resonant limit is given by

\mathcal{N}_{zz}(\Omega)=\frac{\mu_{0}k_{B}T\Omega}{3\alpha^{2}}\exp\left[-2\left(\frac{\Omega}{\alpha}\right)^{2/3}z_{0}\right].

(81)

We observe that resonant peak in
C_{\mathrm{L}}({\bm{k}},\Omega)
at the phonon frequency
\Omega_{\bm{k}}=\alpha k^{3/2}
translates to a noise peak at
\Omega_{c}^{\rm 2D}\sim\alpha z_{0}^{-3/2}
[Fig.
6
(a)], consistent with our intuition that the resonant modes with
k\sim z_{0}^{-1}
dominate the contribution.
The low-energy dispersion of the phonon modes of the Pearl vortex lattice can thus be extracted from the scaling of the noise peak frequency with
z_{0}
.

For rigid Abrikosov vortices, in the limit of
\lambda_{L}\ll z_{0}
such that
k\lambda_{L}\ll 1
, the magnetic noise is given by

\mathcal{N}_{zz}(\Omega)=\frac{\mu_{0}k_{B}T}{4a_{c}\bar{\alpha}}\exp[-2\left(\frac{\Omega}{\bar{\alpha}}\right)^{1/2}z_{0}].

(82)

In this case, the sharp resonance in
C_{\mathrm{L}}({\bm{k}},\Omega)
is smeared out in the magnetic noise by the the momentum integral in Eq. (
69
).
Nevertheless, the noise
\mathcal{N}_{zz}(\Omega)
has a pronounced frequency dependence [Fig.
6
(b)] that serves to distinguish between Pearl vortices and Abrikosov vortices.
Further, noting that
\alpha,\bar{\alpha}\sim\sqrt{H}
, the expressions for
\mathcal{N}_{zz}
explicitly show that the magnetic noise takes distinct non-analytic forms as a function of the applied field
H
, depending on the dimensionality: however, they both vanish as
H\to 0
.
Finally, while we assumed explicit expressions for the elastic constants
c_{11}
and
c_{66}
to deduce the field dependence, in principle we may extract these elastic moduli by fitting the magnetic noise data with our theoretical model.

Next, we consider the limit when the vortices are massive, i.e.,
\mu_{v}\Omega^{2}\gg n_{s}h\Omega
.
Neglecting the Magnus force, or equivalently setting
n_{s}h\Omega\to 0
in Eq. (
74
), we find that the dynamical correlation function
C_{\mathrm{L}}({\bm{k}},\Omega)
takes the following form:

C_{\mathrm{L}}({\bm{k}},\Omega)=\frac{2\eta k_{B}Tk^{2}}{(c_{11}(k)k^{2}/n_{v}-\mu_{v}\Omega^{2})^{2}+\eta^{2}\Omega^{2}}.

(83)

Once again, we focus on the resonant limit (
\eta\to 0
), such that the longitudinal correlator
C_{\mathrm{L}}
is given by

C_{\mathrm{L}}({\bm{k}},\Omega)=\frac{2\pi k_{B}Tk^{2}}{\mu_{v}|\Omega|}\;\delta\left(\Omega_{\bm{k}}^{2}-\Omega^{2}\right),\text{ where }\Omega_{\bm{k}}^{2}=\frac{c_{11}(k)k^{2}}{n_{v}\mu_{v}}.

(84)

Once again, the divergent form of the compression modulus
c_{11}(k)
at low momentum in two dimensions leads to distinct, dimension-dependent dispersions
\Omega_{\bm{k}}
for the longitudinal phonon mode.
For Pearl vortices, the longitudinal mode disperses as

\Omega_{{\bm{k}}}=\sqrt{\frac{\Phi_{0}Hk}{\mu_{v}(1+\Lambda k)}}\propto\sqrt{k}\,,\text{ for }k\Lambda\ll 1,

(85)

whereas for Abrikosov vortices.

\Omega_{{\bm{k}}}=\sqrt{\frac{\Phi_{0}Hk^{2}}{\mu_{v}(1+k^{2}\lambda_{L}^{2})}}\propto k\,,\text{ for }k\lambda_{L}\ll 1.

(86)

This distinction shows up in the magnetic noise spectrum.
For massive Pearl vortices, the noise in the resonant limit is given by

\mathcal{N}_{zz}(\Omega)=\mu_{0}k_{B}T\left(\frac{\mu_{v}}{\Phi_{0}H}\right)^{3}\Omega^{5}\exp\left[-\frac{2\mu_{v}\Omega^{2}z_{0}}{\Phi_{0}H}\right].

(87)

The noise exhibits a distinct peak at the qubit frequency
\Omega=\Omega_{l}^{\rm 2D}\sim\sqrt{\Phi_{0}H/\mu_{v}z_{0}}
, corresponding to the resonant mode frequency with
k\sim 1/z_{0}
[Fig.
6
(a)].
Further, the peak frequency scales as
1/\sqrt{z_{0}}
, reflecting the anomalous
\Omega_{\bm{k}}\sim\sqrt{k}
low-energy dispersion of longitudinal phonons in the Pearl vortex lattice.

By contrast, for massive Abrikosov vortices in the resonant limit, the magnetic noise is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{zz}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{(\mu_{0}H)^{3}k_{B}T\mu_{v}\Omega}{2a_{c}c_{11}^{2}(0)\Phi_{0}}\exp\left[-2\sqrt{\frac{n_{v}\mu_{v}}{c_{11}(0)}}\Omega z_{0}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{0}k_{B}T\mu_{v}\Omega}{2a_{c}H\Phi_{0}}\exp\left[-2\sqrt{\frac{\mu_{v}}{\Phi_{0}H}}\Omega z_{0}\right],

(88)

where the peak frequency
\Omega_{l}^{\rm 3D}\sim\sqrt{\Phi_{0}H/\mu_{v}z_{0}^{2}}
[Fig.
6
(b)] scales as
1/z_{0}
, reflecting the linear dispersion of the phonons (recall that
\mu_{v}
has the dimensions of mass per unit length in 3D).
Just like the massless limit, either the distinct frequency dependence of
\mathcal{N}_{zz}(\Omega)
or the distinct non-analytic field (
H
) dependence can be used to extract low-energy phonon dispersions and distinguish between Pearl vortices and Abrikosov vortices in the massive limit as well.
Further, only the longitudinal phonon mode, which is sharply defined in the massive vortex limit, contributes to the magnetic noise.
This implies that only the compressional elastic modulus
c_{11}
can be extracted from
\mathcal{N}_{zz}
in this limit.
Finally, we also note that the low density of states of vortex phonons implies that the magnetic noise is suppressed by powers of
\Omega
, which is typically the smallest energy scale in the set-up.
This suppression is particularly severe for Pearl vortices, owing to the sharp
\Omega_{\bm{k}}\sim\sqrt{k}
dispersion of the phonon modes.

Lastly, we consider the strongly dissipative limit of large viscous damping relative to the other kinetic terms, i.e.,
\eta\gg n_{s}h,\mu_{v}\Omega
.
The longitudinal correlator
C_{\mathrm{L}}({\bm{k}},\Omega)
in Eq. (
74
) now takes a simple form:

C_{\mathrm{L}}({\bm{k}},\Omega)=\frac{2\eta k_{B}Tk^{2}}{\left(c_{11}(k)k^{2}/n_{v}\right)^{2}+\eta^{2}\Omega^{2}}

(89)

For a lattice of Pearl vortices,
c_{11}(k)\sim k^{-1}
at small momentum, leading
C_{\mathrm{L}}({\bm{k}},\Omega)
to go approach a finite limit as
\Omega\to 0
.
Therefore, just like the overdamped fluctuating single vortex, we can simply take the
\Omega\to 0
limit in the magnetic noise:

\mathcal{N}_{zz}(\Omega\to 0)=\frac{\mu_{0}\eta k_{B}T}{4\pi H\Phi_{0}z_{0}^{2}}.

(90)

By contrast, for a lattice of rigid Abrikosov vortices, we cannot simply set the probe frequency
\Omega
to zero while evaluating the magnetic noise.
In this case,
c_{11}(k)
approaches a constant in the low momentum limit, and hence
C_{\mathrm{L}}({\bm{k}},\Omega\to 0)\sim k^{-2}
is strongly divergent: intuitively this divergence arises due to the enhanced density of states of vortex lattice phonons in 3D.
The qubit frequency
\Omega
naturally acts as an IR cutoff, and leads to a logarithmic dependence of the magnetic noise on both
\Omega
and
z_{0}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{zz}(\Omega)=\frac{\mu_{0}\eta k_{B}T}{2\pi a_{c}H\Phi_{0}}\ln\left(\frac{H\Phi_{0}}{\Omega\eta z_{0}^{2}}\right).

(91)

Remarkably, the magnetic noise is quite strong at weak magnetic fields in the overdamped limit, and the distinct qubit-sample distance dependence can be used to tell apart the vortex lattice dimensionality even when the viscous forces are strong.
This enhancement stems from the softening of the compressional elastic modulus
c_{11}
at weak magnetic fields, leading to enhanced low-energy fluctuations.
However, at sufficiently weak magnetic fields, the significant suppression of the elastic moduli can cause the vortex lattice to melt into a vortex liquid due to thermal fluctuations, thereby precluding an unphysical divergence in the magnetic noise as
H\to 0
.
The noise signatures of such a vortex liquid phase will be discussed in the next section.

To conclude this section, we discuss the effect of vertical distortions of Abrikosov vortices, i.e., a non-zero
k_{z}
, on our results for the 3D vortex lattice.
Drawing from the intuition built by incorporating
k_{z}\neq 0
for a single fluctuating vortex, we expect a weaker signal when the vortices are allowed to distort in the
z
-direction.
Additionally, we expect that the magnetic noise also depends on the elastic modulus
c_{44}

8
;
9
that controls such out-of-plane oscillations.
The precise expression for the magnetic noise that accounts for such modulations of line vortices is left for future work.

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\alpha\alpha}(\Omega)=\int\frac{d^{2}{\bm{k}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}|B^{\alpha}_{0}({\bm{k}})|^{2}\left[\frac{2k_{B}T\chi_{v}D_{v}k^{2}}{\Omega^{2}+(D_{v}k^{2})^{2}}\right].

A few comments are in order. First, we note that in the hierarchy of lengthscales assumed above, the noise depends very weakly on the frequency
\Omega
(via
\ell_{\Omega}
) and the sample-qubit distance
z_{0}
.
Intuitively, this is because the diffusive correlations scale as
1/k^{2}
, leading to a nearly scale-invariant integral for the noise, with a lower cutoff set by
\Omega
and an upper cutoff the qubit-sample distance
z_{0}
or the screening length
\lambda_{\rm scr}
.

Next, the field dependence of
\mathcal{N}_{zz}
contains information about the origin of viscous drag forces on the vortices.
In the dilute limit, the viscous force is dominated by dissipative electronic quasiparticles in the vortex core, which leads to a drag on the vortex in the classic Bardeen-Stephen picture of flux-flow
5
.
In this limit, inter-vortex scattering can be neglected and the diffusion constant
D_{v}
does not depend on the vortex density and hence on
H
.
On the other hand, the Einstein relation dictates that the vortex conductivity
\sigma_{v}=\chi_{v}D_{v}
, with
\chi_{v}
being the vortex compressibility.
For a dilute gas of vortices, the compressibility, given by
\chi_{v}=n_{v}/(k_{B}T)

34
, scales linearly with
H
, indicating that
\mathcal{N}_{zz}(\Omega)\propto n_{v}\propto H
.
On the other hand, in the dense limit, the interaction between the vortices are expected to be strong, and the viscous drag
\bar{\eta}
depends on inter-vortex collisions.
Further, if we assume, within a simple Drude-like model, that the vortex scattering time
\tau_{v}
is inversely proportional to its density
n_{v}
, then
\sigma_{v}\propto n_{v}\tau_{v}
is independent of
n_{v}
.
On the other hand, the vortex diffusion constant
D_{v}
will scale as
\langle v^{2}\rangle\tau_{v}\sim 1/n_{v}
, where we have assumed that the mean-squared vortex velocity
\langle v^{2}\rangle
is determined by the thermal energy
k_{B}T
.
Thus, in this limit, the magnetic noise scales as
D_{v}^{-2}\propto n_{v}^{2}\sim H^{2}
.
Note that in both limits, the field-scaling of
\mathcal{N}_{zz}
is distinct from the field scaling for vortex lattice phonons, and may be used to distinguish the two phases.

Next, we note that it is possible for the Pearl length
\Lambda
to exceed both
\ell_{\Omega}
and
z_{0}
in very thin samples.
This unscreened regime was considered in Ref.
15
: in this limit the noise scales as
\sigma_{v}\ell_{\Omega}^{2}/D_{v}^{2}\propto\sigma_{v}/D_{v}
.
Remarkably, for both the dilute and dense limits that we discussed previously, the noise scales linearly with
H
, with the linear scaling arising from
\sigma_{v}\propto H
in the dilute limit, and from
D_{v}^{-1}\propto H
in the dense limit.
However, a more complicated field-dependence of the inter-vortex scattering rate (that determines
D_{v}
) can potentially alter this scaling on increasing
H
to reach the dense limit.

Finally, we also note that for the dynamical vortex density correlations to be appropriately described by a diffusive process, we require the qubit to be able to probe low-energy and low momenta behavior.
Accordingly, we require the qubit-sample distance
z_{0}
to be larger than the ballistic lengthscale, and the qubit frequency
\Omega
to be much smaller than the typical vortex scattering rate.
Provided these assumptions hold, we summarize the scalings of magnetic noise for all possible length-scale hierarchies in Table.

and Fig.

.

VI
Conclusion and Outlook

In this work, we presented a comprehensive study of spin-qubit based spectroscopy of magnetic noise from a superconductor placed in an external magnetic field.
Specifically, we demonstrated how an applied field, known to lower the critical temperature for the superconducting transition, also significantly increases the magnetic noise near criticality due to enhanced pairing fluctuations.
Further, we showed that magnetic noise spectroscopy is able to detect fluctuations of superconducting vortices, characterize their low-energy dynamics, and diagnose key quantities of physical interest — such as the elastic moduli of vortex lattices and the associated phonon dispersion, as well as the diffusivity of vortices in a liquid phase obtained by melting of the vortex lattice.
Our results complement existing theoretical results on noise spectroscopy of superconductors
in the absence of an applied magnetic field

13
;
18
;
15
;
17
;
35
, and establishes the importance of this technique for interrogating pairing and vortex dynamics in a superconducting sample placed under a field.

On the experimental front, we note a couple of very recent experiments
45
;
32
investigating dynamical processes in superconductors via depolarization (
T_{1}
) and decoherence (
T_{2}
) noise spectroscopy with NV centers.
Ref.
45
found that the peak in the magnetic noise in the GHz range, detected by
T_{1}
spectroscopy, shifts to a lower temperature relative to zero-field transition temperature
T_{c}(H=0)
in a thin film cuprate superconductor (BSCCO).
Additionally, the peak noise spectral density increases linearly with
H
.
While Ref.
45
interpreted this
H
-linear increase of noise in terms of a vortex liquid state, our present results show that an alternate interpretation in terms of field-induced enhancement of order-parameter fluctuations can also lead to such a linear increase.
Future experiments that simultaneously measure transport along with magnetic noise can settle this issue: the vortex liquid phase is expected to have a finite resistivity.
At lower temperatures, both Ref.
45
and Ref.
32
uncover evidence of slow motion of vortices in a vortex glass phase, attributed to thermal fluctuation-induced depinning, in two distinct superconductors — thin film BSCCO and NbSe
2
.
Our results on single vortex dynamics can shed light on the nature of the pinning potential as well as the elastic line tension of vortices in such scenarios.

We conclude by discussing a few future directions of potential interest that are motivated by our results.
First, our results can be straightforwardly extended to decoherence-based (
T_{2}
) noise spectroscopy, which is able to detect and characterize noise at an even lower frequency scale (typically MHz for NV centers, as opposed to GHz-scale noise detected by depolarization-based spectroscopy discussed here).
While such methods necessarily lead to a broadened frequency filter function set by the pulse sequence
68
;
48
and a resultant smearing of the noise spectral density
\mathcal{N}(\Omega)
in frequency, they can prove useful to diagnose universal low-energy dynamics near superconducting phase transitions and slow motion of superconducting vortices, e.g., in a vortex glass phase.
Second, our theoretical approach for computing critical noise in an applied field can be extended by using non-linear corrections via a self-consistent calculation
19
.
Likewise, characterizing the noise signatures of vortex dynamics beyond our present considerations, such as thermally assisted flux creep in a vortex-glass phase
8
, can be useful to shed significant light on glassy dynamics of topological defects.
Third, given that the qubit sensor measures a stronger signal from dynamical fluctuations in the layers close to the sensor, it is naturally more sensitive to the surface than the bulk.
This makes it an ideal probe for surface superconductivity and interfacial superconductivity, predicted to occur in a variety of materials
29
;
28
;
10
;
63
.
Longer term, one can attempt to leverage the ability of the same qubit sensor to detect both magnetic fluctuations and electrical fluctuations
59
to shed light on superconductivity in low-density superconductors in putative critical ferroelectric materials
69
;
39
;
40
.

\begin{split}\sigma_{\mathrm{T}}^{\rm sc}(q)&=\frac{k_{B}T\hbar}{\Gamma}\left(\frac{\hbar e^{*}}{m^{*}}\right)^{2}\int_{{\bm{k}}}\frac{[{\bm{k}}-\hat{{\bm{q}}}(\hat{{\bm{q}}}\cdot{\bm{k}})]^{2}}{\Big(K_{m}({\bm{k}}-{\bm{q}})^{2}+2uM_{0}\Big)K_{m}{\bm{k}}^{2}\Big[K_{m}\big(({\bm{k}}-{\bm{q}})^{2}+{\bm{k}}^{2}\big)+2uM_{0}\Big]}\\
&=\frac{k_{B}T\hbar}{\Gamma K_{m}}\left(\frac{\hbar e^{*}}{m^{*}}\right)^{2}\int\frac{k\,dk\,d\theta}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{\sin^{2}\theta}{\Big(K_{m}(k^{2}-2kq\cos\theta+q^{2})+2uM_{0}\Big)\Big(K_{m}(2k^{2}-2kq\cos\theta+q^{2})+2uM_{0}\Big)}\\
&=\frac{k_{B}T\hbar}{\Gamma K_{m}^{2}(2uM_{0})}\left(\frac{\hbar e^{*}}{m^{*}}\right)^{2}\int\frac{\tilde{k}\,d\tilde{k}\,d\theta}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{\sin^{2}\theta}{(\tilde{k}^{2}-2\tilde{k}\tilde{q}\cos\theta+\tilde{q}^{2}+1)(2\tilde{k}^{2}-2\tilde{k}\tilde{q}\cos\theta+\tilde{q}^{2}+1)}\\
&=\frac{k_{B}T\hbar}{\Gamma K_{m}^{2}(2uM_{0})}\left(\frac{\hbar e^{*}}{m^{*}}\right)^{2}\int\frac{\tilde{k}\,d\tilde{k}}{2\pi}\frac{\sqrt{(1+2\tilde{k}^{2}+\tilde{q}^{2})^{2}-4\tilde{k}^{2}\tilde{q}^{2}}-\sqrt{(1+\tilde{k}^{2}+\tilde{q}^{2})^{2}-4\tilde{k}^{2}\tilde{q}^{2}}-\tilde{k}^{2}}{4\tilde{k}^{4}\tilde{q}^{2}}\\
&=\sigma_{\rm sc}(0)F_{\rm sc}(\tilde{q})\textrm{ where }\\
&\sigma_{\rm sc}(0)=\frac{\ln 2}{\pi}\left(\frac{e^{*}}{\hbar}\right)^{2}k_{B}T\frac{\hbar}{2uM_{0}\Gamma}\,,\ \ F_{\rm sc}(\tilde{q})=\frac{1}{\tilde{q}^{2}\ln 2}\left[\ln\left(4\frac{1+\tilde{q}^{2}}{2+\tilde{q}^{2}}\right)-\frac{\ln(2+\tilde{q}^2)}{1+\tilde{q}^{2}}\right].\end{split}

(105)

Here, we have followed the same procedure as on the metallic side and introduced the dimensionless momentum variable
\tilde{k}=k\xi_{\rm sc}
, where
\xi_{\rm sc}=\sqrt{K_{m}/(2|r|)}
denotes the coherence length.

Appendix B
Derivation of the transverse conductivity at finite-field

In this appendix, we present the details of the calculation of the transverse conductivity in the presence of a background magnetic field
{\bm{H}}
.
We first derive the order-parameter correlation function and effective correlation length
\xi_{H}
, followed by the transverse conductivity and its associated scaling function
F_{H}(q\xi_{H},\ell/\xi_{H})
.
Finally, we analyze the magnetic noise and elucidate how it differs from the zero–magnetic-field case.

B.1
Order-parameter correlation function

To begin with, under a finite magnetic field, the supercurrent
46
is rewritten as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{J}_{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{\hbar e^{*}}{2m^{*}i}\left(\psi^{*}\nabla\psi-\psi\nabla\psi^{*}\right)-\frac{\left(e^{*}\right)^{2}}{m^{*}}|\psi|^{2}\mathbf{A}=\frac{\hbar e^{*}}{2m^{*}i}\Big(\psi^{*}D\psi-\psi(D\psi)^{*}\Big),

(106)

where the differential operator
D_{\mu}=\partial_{\mu}-ie^{*}A_{\mu}/\hbar
. We may use Wick’s theorem to compute the current-current correlation function
\left\langle J_{\mu 1}J_{\nu 2}\right\rangle=\left(\frac{\hbar e^{*}}{2m^{*}i}\right)^{2}\left[\left(D_{\mu 1}-D_{\mu 3}^{*}\right)\left(D_{\nu 2}-D_{\nu 4}^{*}\right)\left\langle\psi_{1}^{*}\psi_{3}\psi_{2}^{*}\psi_{4}\right\rangle\right]_{3=1,4=2}
. We can then apply the classical fluctuation–dissipation theorem (FDT) Eq. (
14
) to compute the nonlocal conductivity
53
, which gives

\sigma_{\mu\nu}({\bm{q}})=\frac{-1}{2k_{B}T}\left(\frac{\hbar e^{*}}{2m^{*}}\right)^{2}\int\frac{d\Omega}{2\pi}\int_{\bm{\rho}_{1}-\bm{\rho}_{2}}d^{2}\left(\bm{\rho}_{1}-\bm{\rho}_{2}\right)e^{i{\bm{q}}\cdot\left(\bm{\rho}_{1}-\bm{\rho}_{2}\right)}\left(D_{\mu 1}-D_{\mu 3}^{*}\right)\left(D_{\nu 2}-D_{\nu 4}^{*}\right)C\left(\bm{\rho}_{2},\bm{\rho}_{3},\Omega\right)C\left(\bm{\rho}_{1},\bm{\rho}_{4},\Omega\right)\left.\right|_{3=1,4=2}.

(107)

The order-parameter correlation function
C
is related to the response function
\chi
by the classical FDT (
\hbar\Omega\ll k_{B}T
):

C\left(\bm{\rho},\bm{\rho}^{\prime};\Omega\right)=\int\frac{d\Omega}{2\pi}e^{-i\Omega\left(t-t^{\prime}\right)}\left\langle\psi(\bm{\rho},t)\psi^{*}\left(\bm{\rho}^{\prime},t^{\prime}\right)\right\rangle=\frac{2k_{B}T}{\Omega}\mathrm{Im}\,\chi\left(\bm{\rho},\bm{\rho}^{\prime};\Omega\right),

(108)

where
\chi\left(\bm{\rho},\bm{\rho}^{\prime};\Omega\right)
is the order-parameter response function, which is the solution of the linearized TDGL equations,

\displaystyle\hbar\Gamma^{-1}\partial_{t}\psi-K_{m}D^{2}\psi+r\psi

\displaystyle=\zeta,

(109a)

\displaystyle\left(-i\hbar\Gamma^{-1}\Omega-K_{m}D^{2}+r\right)\chi\left(\bm{\rho},\bm{\rho}^{\prime};\Omega\right)

\displaystyle=\delta^{(2)}\left(\bm{\rho}-\bm{\rho}^{\prime}\right).

(109b)

In the Landau gauge
\mathbf{A}=\mu_{0}H(0,x,0)
, the eigenfunctions of
-K_{m}D^{2}
are the standard Landau-level wavefunctions
u_{n}\left(x-x_{0}\right)
with guiding centers
x_{0}=k_{y}\ell^{2}
; the parameter
r(T)
merely shifts the energy eigenvalues
\epsilon_{n}
. The n-th energy level is
\epsilon_{n}=(n+1/2)\hbar\omega_{c}+r
, with the cyclotron frequency defined as
\omega_{c}=\mu_{0}e^{*}H/m^{*}
. Using the completeness relation
\delta\left(x_{1}-x_{2}\right)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}u_{n}\left(x_{1}-x_{0}\right)u_{n}\left(x_{2}-x_{0}\right)
, we obtain the correlation function

C\left(x_{1},x_{2};k_{y},\Omega\right)=\frac{2\hbar k_{B}T}{\Gamma}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{u_{n}\left(x_{1}-x_{0}\right)u_{n}\left(x_{2}-x_{0}\right)}{(\hbar\Omega/\Gamma)^{2}+\epsilon_{n}^{2}}.

(110)

After integration, we arrive at the correlation function in real space, as shown in Eq. (
33
). Here we have applied the wavefunctions of Landau levels, given by,

u_{n}(x)=\left(\frac{1}{2^{n}n!\sqrt{\pi}\ell}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2\ell^{2}}}H_{n}\left(\frac{x}{\ell}\right),

(111)

with magnetic length
\ell=\sqrt{\hbar/(\mu_{0}e^{*}H)}
and
H_{n}(x)
the Hermite polynomials
H_{n}(x)=(-1)^{n}e^{x^{2}}\frac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}e^{-x^{2}}
. We can then define a unit-length wavefunction
\tilde{u}_{n}(y)=\sqrt{\ell}\,u_{n}(y\ell)=e^{-y^{2}/2}H_{n}(y)/(2^{n}n!\sqrt{\pi})^{\frac{1}{2}}
.

B.2
Characteristic Lifetime & Correlation Length

We start by identifying the characteristic lifetime
\tau_{H}
and the correlation length
\xi_{H}
in the presence of a background magnetic field
{\bm{H}}
.
Fourier transforming Eq. (
110
), we arrive at the correlation function in the time domian

C\left(x_{1},x_{2};k_{y},t\right)=\frac{2\hbar k_{B}T}{\Gamma}\int\frac{d\Omega}{2\pi}e^{i\Omega t}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{u_{n}\left(x_{1}-x_{0}\right)u_{n}\left(x_{2}-x_{0}\right)}{(\hbar\Omega/\Gamma)^{2}+\epsilon_{n}^{2}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{k_{B}T}{\epsilon_{n}}e^{-\Gamma\epsilon_{n}|t|/\hbar}u_{n}\left(x_{1}-x_{0}\right)u_{n}\left(x_{2}-x_{0}\right),

(112)

From Eq. (
112
), we can read off the lifetime
\tau_{H}=\max(\tau_{n})=\hbar/(\Gamma\epsilon_{0})
, which reduces to Eq. (
34
),

\tau_{H}=\frac{\hbar}{\Gamma\left(r+\hbar\omega_{c}/2\right)}\,.

(113)

We note that in the limit of
{\bm{H}}\rightarrow 0
, we recover the lifetime at zero field, Eq. (
19
) of
\tau_{\rm m}=\hbar/(\Gamma r)
.

To obtain the correlation length, we now obtain the equal-time correlator in real space, by taking
t=0
, and integrating Eq. (
112
) over
k_{y}
:

\displaystyle C_{\bm{\rho}_{1},\bm{\rho}_{2}}

\displaystyle=k_{B}T\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\epsilon_{n}}\int\frac{dX}{2\pi\ell}\exp\left({ik_{y}(y_{1}-y_{2})}-\frac{(x_{1}-x_{0})^{2}+(x_{2}-x_{0})^{2}}{2\ell^{2}}\right)\frac{H_{n}\left(\frac{x_{1}-x_{0}}{\ell}\right)H_{n}\left(\frac{x_{2}-x_{0}}{\ell}\right)}{2^{n}n!\sqrt{\pi}\ell}.

(114)

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{B}T}{\sqrt{\pi}\ell^{2}}e^{-(X_{1}^{2}+X_{2}^{2})/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}dp\,\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-\epsilon_{n}p}\int\frac{dX}{2\pi}\exp\Big(-X^2 + X \big( X_1 + X_2 +i(Y_1 - Y_2) \big) \Big)\frac{H_{n}\left(X_{1}-X\right)H_{n}\left(X_{2}-X\right)}{2^{n}n!}

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{B}T}{\sqrt{\pi}\ell^{2}}e^{-(X_{1}^{2}+X_{2}^{2})/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}dp\,e^{-\epsilon_{0}p}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dX}{2\pi}\exp\Big(-X^2 + X \big( X_1 + X_2 +i(Y_1 - Y_2) \big) \Big)\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\varsigma^{2}}}\exp\left(\frac{2w_{1}w_{2}\varsigma-\left(w_{1}^{2}+w_{2}^{2}\right)\varsigma^{2}}{1-\varsigma^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{B}T}{2\pi\ell^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dp\,e^{-\epsilon_{0}p}\frac{1}{1-\varsigma}\exp\Big( i \Delta_y \frac{X_1+X_2}{2}-\frac{1+\varsigma}{4(1-\varsigma)}\left(\Delta_y^2 + \Delta_x^2\right)\Big)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{k_{B}T}{2\pi\ell^{2}}\frac{1}{\hbar\omega_{c}}\exp\left(i\Delta_{y}\frac{X_{1}+X_{2}}{2}\right)\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dp}{p}e^{-\epsilon_{0}p}e^{-\frac{\Delta^{2}}{2\hbar\omega_{c}p}}=\frac{k_{B}T}{\pi\ell^{2}}\frac{1}{\hbar\omega_{c}}\exp\left(i\Delta_{y}\frac{X_{1}+X_{2}}{2}\right)K_{0}\left(\sqrt{\frac{2\epsilon_{0}}{\hbar\omega_{c}}}\Delta\right).

In the second line, we introduced dimensionless coordinates
X=x_{0}/\ell
,
X_{i}=x_{i}/\ell
,
Y_{i}=y_{i}/\ell
, and rewrite
1/\epsilon_{n}=\int_{0}^{\infty}dp\,e^{-\epsilon_{n}p}
. In the third line, we extend the limits of integration to
(-\infty,+\infty)
, and apply the Mehler’s Hermite polynomial formula
50
,

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{H_{n}\left(w_{1}\right)H_{n}\left(w_{2}\right)}{n!}\left(\frac{\varsigma}{2}\right)^{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\varsigma^{2}}}\exp\left(\frac{2w_{1}w_{2}\varsigma-\left(w_{1}^{2}+w_{2}^{2}\right)\varsigma^{2}}{1-\varsigma^{2}}\right),

(115)

where
\varsigma=e^{-\hbar\omega_{c}p}
,
w_{i}=X_{i}-X
. In the fourth line of Eq. (
114
), the separation is defined as
\Delta=\sqrt{\Delta_{x}^{2}+\Delta_{y}^{2}}
,
\Delta_{x}=X_{1}-X_{2}
, and
\Delta_{y}=Y_{1}-Y_{2}
, the integral is dominated by small
p
(
p\ll 1
,
\varsigma\simeq 1-\hbar\omega_{c}p
), where we have used the identity
\int_{0}^{\infty}dp\,\left(e^{-ap-b/p}/p\right)=2K_{0}(2\sqrt{ab})
. For large separation
\Delta\gg 1
, modified Bessel function behaves like
K_{0}(x)\approx e^{-x}\sqrt{\pi/(2x)}
. The asymptotic form reveals the exponential decay
C_{\bm{\rho}_{1},\bm{\rho}_{2}}\sim e^{-\Delta\rho/\xi_{H}}
, which allows us to find the correlation length, as defined in Eq. (
34
),

\xi_{H}=\frac{\ell}{\sqrt{\frac{2\epsilon_{0}}{\hbar\omega_{c}}}}=\frac{\hbar}{\sqrt{2m^{*}(r+\frac{1}{2}\hbar\omega_{c})}},\quad s=\frac{\ell}{\xi_{H}}=\sqrt{\frac{2r}{\hbar\omega_{c}}+1}.

(116)

The phase factor
\exp\left(i\Delta_{y}\frac{X_{1}+X_{2}}{2}\right)
arises from the gauge choice. Since a physical correlator must be gauge invariant, we restore invariance by inserting the Peierls phase acquired along a straight‑line path between the two points:

C_{\mathrm{inv}}(\bm{\rho}_{1},\bm{\rho}_{2},\Omega)=\frac{2\hbar k_{B}T}{\Gamma}\exp\left(-i\frac{e^{*}}{\hbar}\int_{\bm{\rho}_{1}}^{\bm{\rho}_{2}}\mathbf{A}\cdot d\bm{\rho}\right)\int_{k_{y}}e^{ik_{y}(y_{1}-y_{2})}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{u_{n}(x_{1}-x_{0})u_{n}(x_{2}-x_{0})}{(\hbar\Omega/\Gamma)^{2}+\epsilon_{n}^{2}}.

(117)

B.3
Scaling function

The dimensionless energy levels
\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}
are obtained by scaling the energy levels with the cyclotron energy,

\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}=\frac{\epsilon_{n}}{\hbar\omega_{c}}=\frac{\hbar\omega_{c}(n+\frac{1}{2})+r}{\hbar\omega_{c}}=n+\frac{\ell^{2}}{2\xi_{H}^{2}}=n+\frac{s^{2}}{2}.

(118)

where the dimensionless ratio
s=\ell/\xi_{H}
, defined in Eq. (
116
), characterizes the magnetic field strength relative to the energy scale
r
. When the field is weak,
s\to+\infty
, and when the field is strong,
s\to 0^{+}
.

Similar to zero-field case, the transverse conductivity can be factorized into a zero‑momentum part
\sigma_{H}(0,s)
and a universal scaling function
F_{H}(q\xi_{H},s)
,

\sigma^{H}_{\mathrm{T}}(q,s)=\sigma_{H}(0,s)F_{H}(q\xi_{H},s).

(119)

Based on Eq. (
35
) and Eq (
107
), the scaling function is proportional to

F_{H}(\tilde{q},s)\propto\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\Omega}{\Gamma/\hbar}\frac{\left|\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx_{i}\,u_{m}^{*}(x_{i})(x_{i}e^{-iqx_{i}})u_{n}(x_{i})\right|^{2}}{\left[(\hbar\Omega/\Gamma)^{2}+\epsilon_{n}^{2}\right]\left[(\hbar\Omega/\Gamma)^{2}+\epsilon_{m}^{2}\right]}.

(120)

For the numerator of the scaling function
F_{H}(\tilde{q},s)
in Eq. (
120
), one can define a matrix element
A_{mn}(x)
, with
x_{i}=y\ell
,
\tilde{q}=q\xi_{H}
, satisfying
\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx_{i}\,u_{m}^{*}(x_{i})(x_{i}e^{-iqx_{i}})u_{n}(x_{i})=\ell\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dy\,\tilde{u}_{m}^{*}(y)\left(ye^{-i\tilde{q}sy}\right)\tilde{u}_{n}(y)=\xi_{H}sA_{mn}(\tilde{q}s)
. Note that the matrix element
A_{mn}(x)
can be written in its first quantization form:

\displaystyle A_{mn}(x)

\displaystyle=i\frac{d}{dx}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dy\,\tilde{u}_{m}^{*}(y)e^{-ixy}\tilde{u}_{n}(y)=i\frac{d}{dx}\langle m|e^{-ix\hat{y}}|n\rangle,

(121)

where
|n\rangle
is the
n
-th eigenvector of the unit-length wavefunction
\langle y|n\rangle=\tilde{u}_{n}(y)
, and
\hat{y}
is the position operator given by ladder operators,
\hat{y}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger})
.
The matrix element
\langle m|e^{ix\hat{y}}|n\rangle
can be evaluated following Refs.
47
;
11
, applying the generating function of the Hermite polynomials and the series expansion of the associated Laguerre polynomial
L^{(\alpha)}_{n}(x)
, and we find the explicit form of the matrix element,

\displaystyle A_{mn}(x)=i\left(\frac{2^{n}n!}{2^{m}m!}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{4}}(-ix)^{m-n}\left[\left(\frac{m-n}{x}-\frac{x}{2}\right)L_{n}^{(m-n)}\left(\frac{x^{2}}{2}\right)-xL_{n-1}^{(m-n+1)}\left(\frac{x^{2}}{2}\right)\right],\quad m\geqslant n

(122a)

\displaystyle|A_{mn}(x)|^{2}=\frac{\min(m,n)!}{\max(m,n)!}\left(\frac{x^{2}}{2}\right)^{|m-n|}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}\left[\left(\frac{|m-n|}{x}-\frac{x}{2}\right)L_{\min(m,n)}^{|m-n|}\left(\frac{x^{2}}{2}\right)-xL_{\min(m,n)-1}^{(|m-n|+1)}\left(\frac{x^{2}}{2}\right)\right]^{2},

(122b)

where the modulus square of
A_{mn}(x)
shown in Eq. (
122b
) is part of the numerator.

For the denominator of the scaling function Eq. (
120
), the integral is performed via the residue theorem,

(\Gamma/\hbar)^{-1}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\Omega}{\left[(\hbar\Omega/\Gamma)^{2}+\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}^{2}\right]\left[(\hbar\Omega/\Gamma)^{2}+\tilde{\epsilon}_{m}^{2}\right]}=\frac{\pi}{\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}\tilde{\epsilon}_{m}\left(\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}+\tilde{\epsilon}_{m}\right)}.

(123)

Using Eq. (
122b
) and Eq. (
123
), the normalized scaling function takes the form:

F_{H}(\tilde{q},s)=\frac{s^{2}}{g(s)}\sum_{n,m=0}^{+\infty}\frac{|A_{mn}(\tilde{q}s)|^{2}}{\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}(s)\tilde{\epsilon}_{m}(s)[\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}(s)+\tilde{\epsilon}_{m}(s)]},

(124)

where
g(s)
is a normalization factor to ensure
F_{H}(0,s)=1
, which can be evaluated by setting
\tilde{q}=0
,

\displaystyle\frac{g(s)}{s^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,m=0}^{+\infty}\frac{\left|A_{mn}(0)\right|^{2}}{\left(\frac{s^{2}}{2}+n\right)\left(\frac{s^{2}}{2}+m\right)\left(s^{2}+n+m\right)}=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{n+1}{(\frac{s^{2}}{2}+n)(\frac{s^{2}}{2}+n+1)(s^{2}+2n+1)}

(125)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\left(-\frac{\frac{s^{2}}{2}-1}{n+\frac{s^{2}}{2}}-\frac{\frac{s^{2}}{2}}{n+\frac{s^{2}}{2}+1}+\frac{4\frac{s^{2}}{2}-2}{2n+2\frac{s^{2}}{2}+1}\right)=1+\left(s^{2}-1\right)\left[\psi\left(\frac{s^{2}}{2}\right)-\psi\left(\frac{s^{2}+1}{2}\right)\right].

The normalization follows from the ladder-operator identity
\langle m|\hat{y}|n\rangle=\sqrt{\frac{n}{2}}\delta_{m,n-1}+\sqrt{\frac{n+1}{2}}\delta_{m,n+1}
, which gives
\left|A_{mn}(0)\right|^{2}=|i\partial_{x}\langle m|y|n\rangle|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\big((n+1)\delta_{m,n+1}+n\delta_{m,n-1}\big)
, which is equivalent to
(n+1)\delta_{m,n+1}
in the double sum. The sum is performed using the digamma‑function relations
\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{1}{n+C}=\psi(N+1+C)-\psi(C)
and
\psi(C+1)=\psi(C)+\frac{1}{C}
.
The function
g(s)
decreases monotonically from
\lim_{s\to 0}g(s)=2
,
\lim_{s\to+\infty}g(s)=\frac{1}{2}
as
s
increases.
The scaling function
F_{H}(\tilde{q},s)
thus obtained in plotted in Fig.
8
.

Scaling function

.

Next, we derive the limiting cases of the scaling function

(126)

As a
weak magnetic field
is turned on,
B\to 0^{+}
,
s\to+\infty
: we consider expansion of the scaling function near

.
A small-

A_{mn}(x)=\langle m|\hat{y}|n\rangle-ix\langle m|\hat{y}^{2}|n\rangle-\frac{1}{2}x^{2}\langle m|\hat{y}^{3}|n\rangle+\mathcal{O}\left(x^{3}\right),~x=\tilde{q}s

\displaystyle|A_{mn}(x)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\left(n+1\right)\delta_{m,n+1}+n\delta_{m,n-1}\right]

\displaystyle F_{H}(\tilde{q},s\to+\infty)

(128)

\displaystyle=1+\tilde{q}^{2}\left[-\frac{5}{24}-\frac{5}{24s^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{s^{4}}\right)\right]+\tilde{q}^{4}\left[\frac{11}{240}+\frac{11}{120s^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{s^{4}}\right)\right]+\mathcal{O}\left(\tilde{q}^{6}\right).

\displaystyle F_{H}(\tilde{q},s\to 0)

(129)

In general, the zero-momentum conductivity

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2k_{B}T}\left(\frac{2k_{B}T}{\Gamma}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{\hbar e^{*}}{2m^{*}}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{2\mu_{0}e^{*}H}{\hbar}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{(\hbar\omega_{c})^{3}}\frac{\hbar\pi\Gamma}{(2\pi)^{2}}\sum_{n,m=0}^{+\infty}\frac{\left|A_{mn}(0)\right|^{2}}{\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}(s)\tilde{\epsilon}_{m}(s)\left[\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}(s)+\tilde{\epsilon}_{m}(s)\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}k_{B}T\left(\frac{e^{*}}{\hbar}\right)^{2}\frac{m^{*}\xi_{H}^{2}}{\hbar\Gamma}g(s)=\frac{1}{4\pi}k_{B}T\left(\frac{e^{*}}{\hbar}\right)^{2}\frac{\hbar}{\Gamma(r+\frac{1}{2}\hbar\omega_{c})}g(s)\,,

B.4
Asymptotic behavior of noise from critical fluctuations

As discussed in Refs.
4
;
18
, the noise tensor of magnetic fluctuations can be related to the correlations of sources within the sample by solving Maxwell’s equations.
Neglecting retardation effects, most of the noise arises due to evanescent electromagnetic (EM) modes.
The transverse noise

(134)

(136)

Appendix C
Magnetic field from a single vortex

In this appendix, we determine the magnetic field generated by a single isolated vortex in several distinct settings.
We begin with the field produced by an Abrikosov vortex (three-dimensional line vortex), which is applicable when the film thickness
d
exceeds the penetration depth
\lambda_{L}
.
We first derive the magnetic field for a static line vortex and then generalize the result to the case of an undulating vortex line.
Finally, we derive the magnetic field generated by a Pearl vortex in two dimensions, which applies when the film thickness
d
is much smaller than the bulk penetration depth
\lambda_{L}

C.1
Abrikosov vortex

We first consider an isolated static vortex along the
z

We will further assume a type-II superconductor with

C.2
Undulating Abrikosov vortex

Now, we extend our results to include undulations of the vortex along its axis, i.e., we assume that the displacement
{\bm{u}}_{t}(z)
is a function of both time
t
and the vertical coordinate
z
.
In this case, we need to solve the following generalization of the Maxwell-London equation:

\nabla^{2}{\bm{B}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{L}^{2}}\left[{\bm{B}}-\hat{z}\,\Phi_{0}\,\delta\big(\boldsymbol{\rho}-{\bm{u}}_{t}(z)\big)\right],\quad z<0\\
0,\quad z>0\end{array}\right..

(145)

Similarly, we consider a Fourier representation of the field, that generalizes our calculation for the rigid vortex:

{\bm{B}}(\bm{\rho},z,t)=\sum_{n=0}^{N_{\ell}}\int\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\bm{b}_{n}(\bm{k},t)e^{i(\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\rho}+k_{n}z)},~~~~~z\leqslant 0,

(146)

where
k_{n}
denotes momenta along the
z
-direction, which is fixed by periodic boundary conditions to be
k_{n}=n(2\pi/d)
for a material of thickness
d
and inter-layer spacing
a_{c}
, i.e., number of layers
N_{\ell}=d/a_{c}
.
Plugging the ansatz in Eq. (
146
) into Eq. (
145
) and using the linear independence of the distinct Fourier modes, we find that
b_{n}(\bm{k},t)\equiv\hat{z}\cdot\bm{b}_{n}(\bm{k},t)
satisfies the Maxwell-London equation in momentum space:

\displaystyle\left[\lambda_{L}^{2}({\bm{k}}^{2}+k_{n}^{2})+1\right]b_{n}(\bm{k},t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Phi_{0}}{d}\int_{0}^{d}dz\,e^{-ik_{n}z}e^{-i{\bm{k}}\cdot{\bm{u}}_{t}(z)}

(147)

\displaystyle=\Phi_{0}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{[-i{\bm{k}}\cdot{\bm{u}}_{t}(0)]^{m}}{m!}\underbrace{\left(\frac{1}{d}\int_{0}^{d}dz\,e^{i(mk_{M}-k_{n})z}\right)}_{\delta_{k_{n},mk_{M}}}.

where we have assumed a fixed
k_{z}=k_{M}
for analytical progress, i.e.,
{\bm{u}}_{t}(z)={\bm{u}}_{t}(0)e^{ik_{M}z}
.
We note that only the Fourier modes
k_{n}=mk_{M}
which are the harmonics of the vortex undulation mode contribute to the magnetic field.
Accordingly, we can set
b_{n}=0
in Eq. (
147
) unless
k_{n}=mk_{M}
, and write the non-zero

as

b_{k_{n}=mk_{M}}({\bm{k}},t)=\frac{\Phi_{0}}{1+\lambda_{L}^{2}({\bm{k}}^{2}+(mk_{M})^{2})}\frac{[-i{\bm{k}}\cdot{\bm{u}}_{t}(0)]^{m}}{m!}.

(148)

Therefore, for an undulating vortex with
{\bm{u}}_{t}(z)={\bm{u}}_{t}(0)e^{ik_{M}z}
, the

{\bm{B}}_{0}
inside the superconductor is given by

B^{z}(\bm{\rho},z\leqslant 0,t)=\sum_{m}\int\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{\Phi_{0}}{1+\lambda_{L}^{2}({\bm{k}}^{2}+(mk_{M})^{2})}\frac{[-i{\bm{k}}\cdot{\bm{u}}_{t}(0)]^{m}}{m!}e^{i(\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\rho}+mk_{M}z)}.

(149)

At the same time, for
z>0
, we can simply use that

satisfies the Laplace equation outside the superconductor with boundary conditions set by
B^{z}(0^{-})
, to write

B^{z}(\bm{\rho},z\geqslant 0,t)=\int\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}B^{z}({\bm{k}},z=0,t)e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\rho}}e^{-kz}=\sum_{m}\int\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\left[\frac{\Phi_{0}}{1+\lambda_{L}^{2}({\bm{k}}^{2}+(mk_{z})^{2})}\frac{[-i{\bm{k}}\cdot{\bm{u}}_{t}(0)]^{m}}{m!}\right]e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\rho}}e^{-kz},

(150)

where we have made the replacement
k_{M}\to k_{z}
.
Since the magnetic field can be written as a gradient of a scalar potential in the upper half space (
z>0
), Eq. (
150
) implies that all components of the field may be obtained by generalizing the scalar potential
\phi_{M}({\bm{k}})
for the rigid vortex to

\phi({\bm{k}},k_{z})=\sum_{m}\frac{\Phi_{0}}{k(1+\lambda_{L}^{2}({\bm{k}}^{2}+(mk_{z})^{2}))}\frac{[-i{\bm{k}}\cdot{\bm{u}}_{t}(0)]^{m}}{m!},\implies{\bm{B}}(\bm{\rho},z\geqslant 0,t)=\int\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}(-i{\bm{k}},k)\phi({\bm{k}},k_{z})e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\rho}}e^{-kz},

(151)

as claimed in the Eq. (
V.1

C.3
Pearl vortex

Now, we consider the two-dimensional limit.
The Maxwell-London equation now only holds over the film thickness
d
which is assumed to be finite and much less than the bulk penetration depth
\lambda_{L}
(
d\ll\lambda_{L}
) :

\nabla^{2}{\bm{B}}_{0}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{L}^{2}}\left[{\bm{B}}_{0}-\hat{z}\,\Phi_{0}\,\delta^{(2)}(\boldsymbol{\rho})\right],\quad-\dfrac{d}{2}<z<\dfrac{d}{2}\\
0,\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad|z|>\dfrac{d}{2}\end{array}\right..

(152)

In the thin-film (
d\to 0
) limit, it is convenient to introduce a sheet-current density which depends only on the in-plane coordinate
\bm{\rho}=(x,y)
, by the assumption that the current density is uniformly distributed in the
z
-direction, i.e.,

{\bm{j}}(\bm{\rho},z)=\begin{cases}{\bm{K}}(\bm{\rho})/d,\quad-\dfrac{d}{2}\leqslant z\leqslant\dfrac{d}{2}\\
0,\qquad\qquad\quad|z|>\dfrac{d}{2}\end{cases}.

(153)

This will allow us to relate the magnetic field above and below the superconducting sheet.
We consider the
z
-component of the above equation, using vector identity
\nabla\times(\nabla\times{\bm{B}}_{0})=\nabla(\nabla\cdot{\bm{B}}_{0})-\nabla^{2}{\bm{B}}_{0}
, Maxwell equations
\nabla\times{\bm{B}}_{0}=\mu_{0}{\bm{j}}
and London equation Eq. (
152
), and integrate both sides over
z
from
-d/2
to
d/2
,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{\lambda_{L}^{2}}B_{0}^{z}-\frac{\Phi_{0}d}{\lambda_{L}^{2}}\delta^{(2)}(\bm{\rho})=-\mu_{0}\underbrace{\int_{-\frac{d}{2}}^{\frac{d}{2}}dz\,(\nabla\times{\bm{j}})_{z}}_{(\nabla\times{\bm{K}}(\bm{\rho}))_{z}}

\displaystyle=-\mu_{0}\left(\partial_{x}\int_{-\frac{d}{2}}^{\frac{d}{2}}dz\,j_{x}-\partial_{y}\int_{-\frac{d}{2}}^{\frac{d}{2}}dz\,j_{x}\right),

(154)

the boundary conditions for the in-plane field
{\bm{B}}_{0}^{\parallel}
and sheet-current density
{\bm{K}}
(taking the limit of small thickness, i.e.,
d\to 0
) are: (suppressing
\bm{\rho}
for clarity)

\displaystyle{\bm{B}}_{0}^{\parallel}(z\to 0^{+})-{\bm{B}}_{0}^{\parallel}(z\to 0^{-})=\mu_{0}\left({\bm{K}}\times\hat{z}\right),

(155a)

\displaystyle{\bm{B}}_{0}^{\parallel}(z\to 0^{+})=-{\bm{B}}_{0}^{\parallel}(z\to 0^{-})=\frac{\mu_{0}({\bm{K}}\times\hat{z})}{2},

(155b)

where Eq. (
155b
) is from the symmetry of the problem. Using the above result, we can express the curl of the sheet current density in terms of
B_{0}^{z}
,
\mu_{0}(\nabla\times{\bm{K}}(\bm{\rho}))_{z}=2[\nabla\times(\hat{z}\times{\bm{B}}_{0}^{\parallel}(0^{+}))]_{z}=-2\partial_{z}B_{0}^{z}(0^{+})
.
Finally, we can plug this result back into the
z
-integrated London equation to express everything in terms of
B_{0}^{z}
:

2\partial_{z}B_{0}^{z}=\frac{d}{\lambda_{L}^{2}}B_{0}^{z}-\frac{\Phi_{0}d}{\lambda_{L}^{2}}\delta^{(2)}(\bm{\rho})\implies B_{0}^{z}-\Lambda\partial_{z}B_{0}^{z}=\Phi_{0}\delta^{(2)}(\bm{\rho}),~~~z\to 0^{+},

(156)

where we have defined the Pearl length
\Lambda=2\lambda_{L}^{2}/d
.
Now, we are finally in a position to solve for the magnetic field.
To do so, we again use the scalar potential
\phi_{M}({\bm{r}})
for
z>0
, such that
{\bm{B}}_{0}({\bm{r}})=-\nabla\phi_{M}({\bm{r}})
, and impose
\nabla\cdot{\bm{B}}_{0}=0
to get
\nabla^{2}\phi_{M}=0
.
The general solution for a decaying solution
\sim e^{-kz}
for
z>0
can be adapted from Eq. (
140
),

\phi_{M}(\bm{\rho},z)=\int\frac{d^{2}{\bm{k}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}e^{i{\bm{k}}\cdot\bm{\rho}}e^{-kz}\phi_{M}({\bm{k}})\implies{\bm{B}}_{0}(\bm{\rho},z)=-\int\frac{d^{2}{\bm{k}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}e^{i{\bm{k}}\cdot\bm{\rho}}e^{-kz}(ik_{x},ik_{y},-k)\phi_{M}({\bm{k}}).

(157)

Applying Eq. (
157
) on Eq. (
156
), we can solve the scalar potential
\phi_{M}
,

\displaystyle B_{0}^{z}(\bm{\rho},z)=\int\frac{d^{2}{\bm{k}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}ke^{i{\bm{k}}\cdot\bm{\rho}}e^{-kz}\phi_{M}({\bm{k}})

\displaystyle\implies B_{0}^{z}-\Lambda\partial_{z}B_{0}^{z}=\int\frac{d^{2}{\bm{k}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}k(1+\Lambda k)e^{i{\bm{k}}\cdot\bm{\rho}}e^{-kz}\phi_{M}({\bm{k}})=\Phi_{0}\delta^{(2)}(\bm{\rho})

(158)

\displaystyle\implies\phi_{M}({\bm{k}})=\frac{\Phi_{0}}{k(1+\Lambda k)},

and find the scalar potential in real space,

\phi_{M}(\bm{\rho},z)=\Phi_{0}\int\frac{d^{2}{\bm{k}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{e^{i{\bm{k}}\cdot\bm{\rho}}e^{-kz}}{k(1+\Lambda k)}=\frac{\Phi_{0}}{2\pi\Lambda}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tilde{k}\frac{J_{0}\big(\tilde{k}z/\Lambda\big)e^{-\tilde{k}z/\Lambda}}{1+\tilde{k}},~z>0,

(159)

where we have taken advantage of radial symmetry to evaluate the angular integral, following a similar way of deriving Eq. (
142

\Lambda
to define a dimensionless
\tilde{k}=k\Lambda
.
We note now that all physics is sensitive to the Pearl length
\Lambda=2\lambda_{L}^{2}/d
, which can be much larger than the penetration depth in the thin film limit
d\ll\lambda_{L}
.
Accordingly, the magnetic fields are given by

{\bm{B}}_{0}({\bm{r}})=\frac{\Phi_{0}}{2\pi\Lambda^{2}}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tilde{k}\,\frac{\tilde{k}J_{1}\left(\rho\tilde{k}/\Lambda\right)e^{-z\tilde{k}/\Lambda}}{1+\tilde{k}}\right)\hat{\bm{\rho}}+\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tilde{k}\,\frac{\tilde{k}\,J_{0}\left(\rho\tilde{k}/\Lambda\right)e^{-z\tilde{k}/\Lambda}}{1+\tilde{k}}\right)\hat{z}\right].

(160)

We now investigate asymptotic limits of the potential in Eq. (
159
),

\phi_{M}(\,\rho\gg\Lambda\text{~or~}z\gg\Lambda)\approx\frac{\Phi_{0}}{2\pi\Lambda}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tilde{k}\,J_{0}\left(\tilde{k}\frac{\rho}{\Lambda}\right)e^{-\frac{\tilde{k}z}{\Lambda}}=\frac{\Phi_{0}}{2\pi r}\implies{\bm{B}}_{0}({\bm{r}})=\frac{\Phi_{0}}{2\pi r^{2}}\hat{r},~z>0.

(161)

For
\rho\gg\Lambda
or
z\gg\Lambda
, we have
r=\sqrt{\rho^{2}+z^{2}}\gg\Lambda

This is because far away from the vortex the internal structure becomes irrelevant and one essentially sees a
point magnetic monopole
.

For
\rho\ll\Lambda

Appendix D
Details on the pinned vortex / vortex dynamics

In this appendix, we provide details of the calculation of the magnetic noise generated by a pinned vortex, which were omitted in the main text.
Foremost, while the main text focused on the magnetic noise produced by a vortex undulating at a single wavevector, here we consider the more general case in which the vortex supports fluctuations at multiple wavevectors.
As we show below, the transverse magnetic noise contributions from different modes add independently, leading to a simple series summation over the undulation modes.
Second, we provide a detailed derivation that connects the displacement correlation function to the expressions for both the transverse
\left(\mathcal{N}_{xx}\right)
and longitudinal
\left(\mathcal{N}_{zz}\right)
magnetic noise.

D.1
Multiple undulating modes

We first discuss the magnetic noise produced by a pinned vortex undulating with multiple wavevectors, and show that the resulting transverse magnetic noise is a simple summation of the noise at all the different wavevectors.

To this end, we first determine the magnetic field generated by a vortex whose transverse displacement at depth
z
is given by,

{\bm{u}}_{t}(z)=\sum_{n}{\bm{u}}_{t,n}e^{ik_{n}z}~~~k_{n}=\frac{2\pi n}{d}\,.

(169)

Following Eq. (
146
), we introduce a Fourier representation of the magnetic field, and Taylor expand to leading order in the vortex displacement
u_{t}
Eq. (
147
).
We find,

\left[\lambda_{L}^{2}({\bm{k}}^{2}+k_{n}^{2})+1\right]b_{n}(\bm{k},t)\simeq\frac{\Phi_{0}}{d}\int_{0}^{d}dz\,e^{-ik_{n}z}(1-i{\bm{k}}\cdot{\bm{u}}_{t}(z)).

Inserting Eq. (
169
) and performing the
z
integration, we find that to leading order in
{\bm{u}}_{t,n}
,
b_{n}(k,t)
is given by,

b_{n}({\bm{k}},t)=\Phi_{0}\left[\frac{-i{\bm{k}}\cdot{\bm{u}}_{t,n}}{\lambda_{L}^{2}({\bm{k}}^{2}+k_{n}^{2})+1}\right].

(170)

Finally, the real-space magnetic field is then obtained by performing the inverse Fourier transform of Eq. (
170
).
Putting the qubit directly on top of the vortex at
{\bm{r}}_{q}=(\bm{\rho}_{q},z_{q})=(0,0,z_{0})
, we find that the time-dependence of the in-plane component of magnetic field
\delta{\bm{B}}_{\parallel}
is given by,

\delta{\bm{B}}_{\parallel}(t)=-\sum_{n}\left[\frac{\Phi_{0}}{2}\int\frac{d^{2}{\bm{k}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{k\,e^{-kz_{0}}}{(1+\lambda_{L}^{2}({\bm{k}}^{2}+k_{n}^{2}))}\right]{\bm{u}}_{t,n},

(171)

where we have used that the angular average
\langle\hat{k}_{\alpha}\hat{k}_{\beta}\rangle=\delta_{\alpha\beta}/2
inside the
{\bm{k}}
integral.

Finally, let us compute the transverse magnetic noise due to the vortex undulations.

\begin{split}\mathcal{N}_{\rm T}(\Omega)=\mathcal{N}_{xx}(\Omega)+\mathcal{N}_{yy}(\Omega)&=\int dt\,e^{i\Omega t}\langle\delta{\bm{B}}_{\parallel}(t)\cdot\delta{\bm{B}}_{\parallel}(0)\rangle\\
&=\int dt\,e^{i\Omega t}\sum_{n}\left[\frac{\Phi_{0}}{2}\int\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{k\,e^{-kz_{0}}}{(1+\lambda_{L}^{2}({\bm{k}}^{2}+k_{n}^{2}))}\right]^{2}\langle{\bm{u}}_{t,n}\cdot{\bm{u}}_{0,-n}\rangle.\end{split}

(172)

Here in the second line, we have used the fact that in the Langevin dynamics of Eq. (
45
) and (
47
), the modes with different wavevectors decouple from one another, and
\langle u^{\alpha}_{t,n}u^{\beta}_{t^{\prime},m}\rangle\propto\delta^{\alpha\beta}\delta_{n,-m}
.
Consequently, the transverse magnetic noise in the presence of multiple undulation modes reduces to a simple sum over contributions from individual modes.
For small drag coefficient
\eta
, each mode remains weakly damped, and the magnetic noise spectrum exhibits well-resolved peaks associated with the discrete mode frequencies.
In contrast, for large
\eta
, strong damping broadens the spectral features, causing the contributions from different modes to overlap and merge into a smooth continuum.

D.2
Displacement correlations from Langevin dynamics

We now provide a detailed derivation for the transverse and longitudinal magnetic noise.
Our primary focus is to derive the correlation functions of the vortex displacement
\langle{\bm{u}}_{t}{\bm{u}}_{0}\rangle
and
\langle|{\bm{u}}_{t}|^{2}|{\bm{u}}_{0}|^{2}\rangle
, since these correlation functions determine the magnetic noise through Eq.(
40
).

Recall that from Eq. (
45
), the Langevin equation for the undulating vortex is given by,

\mu_{v}\ddot{{\bm{u}}}_{t}=n_{s}h(\dot{{\bm{u}}}_{t}\times\hat{z})-K{\bm{u}}_{t}+\varepsilon_{l}\partial_{z}^{2}{\bm{u}}_{t}-\eta\dot{{\bm{u}}}_{t}+\bm{\zeta}_{t}\,.

(173)

Applying the Fourier-space ansatz for the displacement from Eq. (
48
),
{\bm{u}}_{t}(z)={\bm{u}}_{\Omega,k_{z}}e^{i(k_{z}z-\Omega t)}
, and restricting to a single longitudinal mode with wavevector
k_{z}
, the equation of motion simplify to Eq. (
V.1
),

\displaystyle M_{\Omega,k_{z}}^{-1}~{\bm{u}}_{\Omega,k_{z}}=\bm{\zeta}_{\Omega,k_{z}},\text{ where }~M_{\Omega,k_{z}}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}-\mu_{v}\Omega^{2}-i\eta\Omega+\overline{K}(k_{z})&in_{s}h\Omega\\
-in_{s}h\Omega&-\mu_{v}\Omega^{2}-i\eta\Omega+\overline{K}(k_{z})\end{pmatrix}\,.

(174)

Here, we have defined the effective spring constant
\overline{K}(k_{z})=K+\varepsilon_{l}k_{z}^{2}
.

Let us first find
\langle{\bm{u}}_{t}{\bm{u}}_{0}\rangle
, which determines the transverse magnetic noise.
Applying
{\bm{u}}=M\bm{\zeta}
on Eq. (
47
),

\langle u^{\alpha}_{\Omega,k_{z}}u^{\beta}_{-\Omega,-k_{z}}\rangle=2(\eta/a_{c})k_{B}T[M_{\Omega,k_{z}}M^{\dagger}_{\Omega,k_{z}}]^{\alpha\beta},~\implies~\left\langle{\bm{u}}_{\Omega,k_{z}}\cdot{\bm{u}}_{-\Omega,-k_{z}}\right\rangle=2(\eta/a_{c})k_{B}T\Tr\left[M_{\Omega,k_{z}}M^{\dagger}_{\Omega,k_{z}}\right],

(175)

where the trace of the matrix product
\Tr\left[MM^{\dagger}\right]
is determined from the inverse of the
2\times 2
matrix
M_{\Omega,k_{z}}^{-1}
,

\displaystyle\Tr\left[MM^{\dagger}\right]=\frac{\Tr\left[M^{-1}(M^{-1})^{\dagger}\right]}{\left|\det(M^{-1})\right|^{2}}=2\frac{K^{\prime 2}+(\eta\Omega)^{2}+\left(n_{s}h\Omega\right)^{2}}{\left|(K^{\prime}-i\eta\Omega)^{2}-\left(n_{s}h\Omega\right)^{2}\right|^{2}}

(176)

\displaystyle\implies\left\langle{\bm{u}}_{\Omega,k_{z}}\cdot{\bm{u}}_{-\Omega,-k_{z}}\right\rangle=4(\eta/a_{c})k_{B}T\frac{K^{\prime 2}+\tilde{\eta}^{2}\Omega^{2}}{(K^{\prime 2}-\Omega^{2}\tilde{\eta}^{2})^{2}+4\Omega^{2}\eta^{2}K^{\prime 2}}\,.

Here, we have defined
\tilde{\eta}=\sqrt{\eta^{2}+(n_{s}h)^{2}}
, and
K^{\prime}=K+\varepsilon_{l}k_{z}^{2}-\mu_{v}\Omega^{2}
for notational convenience.
Finally, applying Eq. (
176
) to Eq.(
40
), we determine the transverse magnetic noise to be,

\mathcal{N}_{xx}(\Omega)=\mathcal{N}_{yy}(\Omega)\simeq\left(\partial_{\rho}B_{0}^{\rho}\right)_{\rho=0}^{2}~\left(2\frac{\eta}{a_{c}}k_{B}T\right)\frac{K^{\prime 2}+\Omega^{2}\tilde{\eta}^{2}}{\left(K^{\prime 2}-\Omega^{2}\tilde{\eta}^{2}\right)^{2}+4\Omega^{2}\eta^{2}K^{\prime 2}}\,,

(177)

thus arriving at Eq. (
52
) of the main text.

We now turn to
\langle|{\bm{u}}_{t}|^{2}|{\bm{u}}_{0}|^{2}\rangle
, which determines the longitudinal magnetic noise.
To this end, we define a displacement correlation matrix
\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}
, with elements
\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}^{\alpha\beta}=\langle u_{\Omega}^{\alpha}u_{-\Omega}^{\beta}\rangle
obtained via Eq. (
175
) (we temporarily suppress the
k_{z}
index for clarity).
We note that by symmetry
\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}^{xx}=\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}^{yy}
, and
\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}^{xy}=-\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}^{yx}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}^{xx}=\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}^{yy}

\displaystyle=\frac{2(\eta/a_{c})k_{B}T\left(K^{\prime 2}+\Omega^{2}\tilde{\eta}^{2}\right)}{\left(K^{\prime 2}-\Omega^{2}\tilde{\eta}^{2}\right)^{2}+4\Omega^{2}\eta^{2}K^{\prime 2}},

(178)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}^{xy}=-\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}^{yx}

\displaystyle=-i\frac{4(\eta/a_{c})k_{B}T\cdot n_{s}hK^{\prime}\Omega}{(K^{\prime 2}-\Omega^{2}\tilde{\eta}^{2})^{2}+4\Omega^{2}\eta^{2}K^{\prime 2}}.

The four-point correlator can be decomposed into products of two-point correlators using Wick’s theorem,

\displaystyle\left\langle u^{\alpha}(t)^{2}u^{\beta}(0)^{2}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle u^{\alpha}(t)^{2}\right\rangle\left\langle u^{\beta}(0)^{2}\right\rangle+2\left\langle u^{\alpha}(t)u^{\beta}(0)\right\rangle^{2},

(179)

\displaystyle\langle|{\bm{u}}_{t}|^{2}|{\bm{u}}_{0}|^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\left[\left\langle u^{\alpha}(t)^{2}\right\rangle\left\langle u^{\beta}(0)^{2}\right\rangle+2~\mathcal{U}^{\alpha\beta}(t)^{2}\right].

After integrating over time,

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\,e^{i\Omega t}\langle\left|{\bm{u}}_{t}\right|^{2}\left|{\bm{u}}_{0}\right|^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\,e^{i\Omega t}\left[|2~\mathcal{U}^{xx}(0)|^{2}+2\left(2|\mathcal{U}^{xx}(t)|^{2}+2|\mathcal{U}^{xy}(t)|^{2}\right)\right]

(180)

\displaystyle=2\pi\delta(\Omega)|2~\mathcal{U}^{xx}(0)|^{2}+4\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\,e^{i\Omega t}\left[|\mathcal{U}^{xx}(t)|^{2}+|\mathcal{U}^{xy}(t)|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\pi}\delta(\Omega)\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega\,~\mathcal{U}_{\omega}^{xx}\right)^{2}+\frac{2}{\pi}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega\,~\mathcal{U}_{\omega}^{xx}~\mathcal{U}_{\Omega-\omega}^{xx}-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega\,~\mathcal{U}_{\omega}^{xy}~\mathcal{U}_{\Omega-\omega}^{xy}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\pi}\delta(\Omega)\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega\,~\mathcal{U}_{\omega}^{xx}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{\pi}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega~\mathcal{U}_{\omega}^{+}\mathcal{U}_{\Omega-\omega}^{+}+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega~\mathcal{U}_{\omega}^{-}\mathcal{U}_{\Omega-\omega}^{-}\right).

Here, in the last line, we have introduced the two-point correlators in circular basis
\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}^{\pm}=\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}^{xx}\pm i\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}^{xy}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}^{\pm}=\frac{2(\eta/a_{c})k_{B}T}{\left(K^{\prime}\mp\Omega n_{s}h\right)^{2}+\Omega^{2}\eta^{2}}.

(181)

Applying Eq. (
180
) back into Eq. (
40
), we determine the longitudinal magnetic noise.

\mathcal{N}_{zz}(\Omega)\simeq\left(\partial_{\rho}^{2}B^{z}_{0}\right)_{\rho=0}^{2}~\frac{1}{4\pi}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega~\mathcal{U}_{\omega}^{+}\mathcal{U}_{\Omega-\omega}^{+}+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega~\mathcal{U}_{\omega}^{-}\mathcal{U}_{\Omega-\omega}^{-}\right).

(182)

While the integral above is algebraically cumbersome, it simplifies significantly in several physically relevant limits.
We begin by considering the massless limit (
\mu_{v}=0
). In this limit, the two-point correlators simplify to,

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}^{\pm}=\frac{2(\eta/a_{c})k_{B}T}{\left(\overline{K}(k_{z})\mp\Omega n_{s}h\right)^{2}+\Omega^{2}\eta^{2}}=\frac{2(\eta/a_{c})k_{B}T}{\tilde{\eta}^{2}\left(\Omega\mp\Omega_{c}\right)^{2}+\left(\eta\overline{K}(k_{z})/\tilde{\eta}\right)^{2}},\quad\Omega_{c}=\frac{n_{s}h\overline{K}(k_{z})}{\tilde{\eta}^{2}}.

(183)

Convoluting this simplified correlator, we determine the correlator of the squared displacements to be,

\displaystyle\int dt\,\ e^{i\Omega t}\langle|{\bm{u}}_{t}|^{2}|{\bm{u}}_{0}|^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega~\mathcal{U}_{\omega}^{+}\mathcal{U}_{\Omega-\omega}^{+}+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega~\mathcal{U}_{\omega}^{-}\mathcal{U}_{\Omega-\omega}^{-}\right)

(184)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi\tilde{\eta}^{4}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega~\frac{2(\eta/a_{c})k_{B}T}{\left(\omega-\Omega_{c}\right)^{2}+\left(\eta\overline{K}(k_{z})/\tilde{\eta}^{2}\right)^{2}}\frac{2(\eta/a_{c})k_{B}T}{\left(\Omega-\omega-\Omega_{c}\right)^{2}+\left(\eta\overline{K}(k_{z})/\tilde{\eta}^{2}\right)^{2}}+\left(\Omega_{c}\rightarrow-\Omega_{c}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{8\eta}{\tilde{\eta}^{2}\overline{K}(k_{z})}\left(\frac{k_{B}T}{a_{c}}\right)^{2}\left[\frac{1}{(\Omega-2\Omega_{c})^{2}+\left(2\eta\overline{K}(k_{z})/\tilde{\eta}^{2}\right)^{2}}+\frac{1}{(\Omega+2\Omega_{c})^{2}+\left(2\eta\overline{K}(k_{z})/\tilde{\eta}^{2}\right)^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{16\eta}{\overline{K}(k_{z})}\left(\frac{k_{B}T}{a_{c}}\right)^{2}\frac{4\overline{K}(k_{z})^{2}+\Omega^{2}\tilde{\eta}^{2}}{\left(4\overline{K}(k_{z})^{2}+\Omega^{2}\tilde{\eta}^{2}\right)^{2}-16\Omega^{2}\left(n_{s}h\overline{K}(k_{z})\right)^{2}}.

Here, in the second-to-last line, we have used the convolution identity,

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega\,\frac{1}{(\omega-x)^{2}+\gamma^{2}}\frac{1}{(\Omega-\omega-y)^{2}+\gamma^{2}}=\frac{2\pi}{\gamma}\frac{1}{(\Omega-x-y)^{2}+(2\gamma)^{2}}.

(185)

Next, in the massive or high frequency limit, we can neglect the Magnus forces set by
n_{s}h
.
Here, the correlator
\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}^{\pm}
can be well-approximated by a sum of two Lorentzians centered at
\pm\Omega_{l}
, provided the damping is weak, i.e.,
\mu_{v}\Omega_{l}\gg\eta
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}^{\pm}

\displaystyle=\frac{2(\eta/a_{c})k_{B}T}{\left(\overline{K}(k_{z})-\mu_{v}\Omega^{2}\right)^{2}+\Omega^{2}\eta^{2}}=\frac{2(\eta/a_{c})k_{B}T}{\mu_{v}^{2}\left(\Omega^{2}-\Omega_{l}^{2}\right)^{2}+\eta^{2}\Omega^{2}},\quad\Omega_{l}=\sqrt{\frac{\overline{K}(k_{z})}{\mu_{v}}}

(186)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{2(\eta/a_{c})k_{B}T}{4\mu_{v}^{2}\Omega_{l}^{2}}\left[\frac{1}{\left(\Omega-\Omega_{l}\right)^{2}+(\eta/2\mu_{v})^{2}}+\frac{1}{\left(\Omega+\Omega_{l}\right)^{2}+(\eta/2\mu_{v})^{2}}\right],~\text{ for }\Omega\to\pm\Omega_{l}.

With this, the correlator of the squared displacements is given by,

\displaystyle\int dt\,\ e^{i\Omega t}\langle|{\bm{u}}_{t}|^{2}|{\bm{u}}_{0}|^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega~\mathcal{U}_{\omega}^{+}\mathcal{U}_{\Omega-\omega}^{+}+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega~\mathcal{U}_{\omega}^{-}\mathcal{U}_{\Omega-\omega}^{-}\right)

(187)

Appendix E
Energetics of vortices

E.1
Line tension of a single vortex

In this appendix, we will calculate the self-energy of a single static Abrikosov (line) vortex.
We will assume that the vortex axis is along the
z
-direction, and that the vortex is translation invariant in the
z
-direction, so we will only care about self-energy per unit length.
This quantity can also be interpreted as the line-tension
\varepsilon_{l}
of the vortex, as the energy required to stretch the vortex by unit length in the
z
-direction is given by
\varepsilon_{l}
.
Analytical results can be obtained in the extreme type-II limit, which is
\kappa=\lambda/\xi\gg 1
, i.e., the core of the vortex, where the order parameter varies in amplitude, is assumed to be very small.

We start with the full Ginzburg Landau free energy for a charged superfluid, which is given by

\mathcal{F}=\frac{1}{2m^{*}}\int d^{3}{\bm{r}}\,|(-i\hbar\nabla-e^{*}\bm{A})\psi|^{2}+\int d^{3}{\bm{r}}\,\left[\frac{|\nabla\times\bm{A}|^{2}}{2\mu_{0}}+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon_{0}}\bm{E}^{2}_{0}\right].

(189)

We will further assume that there is local charge density such that
\nabla\cdot\bm{E}_{0}=0
(one may think this is not true for the vortex core where the order-parameter amplitude is indeed changing, but that region has unpaired electrons that balance the positive charge background so that the system is electrically neutral everywhere), and that the magnetic field is static, such that
\nabla\times\bm{E}_{0}=0
, together implying that
\bm{E}=0
.
Finally, we note that the gauge-invariant superfluid velocity (where the order-parameter amplitude variation can be neglected) is given by

{\bm{j}}_{s}=n_{s}e^{*}{\bm{v}}_{s}=n_{s}e^{*}\left(\frac{\hbar\nabla\varphi-e^{*}\bm{A}}{m^{*}}\right).

(190)

where we used
\psi=|\psi|e^{i\varphi}
, and
|\psi|^{2}=n_{s}
is the superfluid density.
Hence, the first term in the GL free energy density can be interpreted as the kinetic energy density of the superfluid.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2m^{*}}|(-i\hbar\nabla-e^{*}\bm{A})\psi|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{|\psi|^{2}m^{*}}{2}\left|\frac{\hbar\nabla\varphi-e^{*}\bm{A}}{m^{*}}\right|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}n_{s}m^{*}{\bm{v}}_{s}^{2}

(191)

\displaystyle=\frac{m^{*}}{2n_{s}(e^{*})^{2}}{\bm{j}}_{s}^{2}=\frac{\mu_{0}}{2}\times\frac{m^{*}}{\mu_{0}n_{s}(e^{*})^{2}}{\bm{j}}_{s}^{2}=\frac{\mu_{0}\lambda_{L}^{2}}{2}{\bm{j}}_{s}^{2},

where we have used the definition of the London penetration depth
\lambda_{L}^{-2}=\mu_{0}n_{s}(e^{*})^{2}/m^{*}
.
Using this, and the Maxwell equation
\nabla\times{\bm{B}}=\mu_{0}{\bm{j}}_{s}
, the free energy can be written entirely in terms of the magnetic field
{\bm{B}}
as

\mathcal{F}=\frac{1}{2\mu_{0}}\left[\int d^{3}{\bm{r}}\,(\lambda_{L}\mu_{0}{\bm{j}}_{s})^{2}+\int d^{3}{\bm{r}}\,{\bm{B}}^{2}_{0}\right]=\frac{1}{2\mu_{0}}\left[\int d^{3}{\bm{r}}\,\left(\lambda_{L}^{2}|\nabla\times{\bm{B}}_{0}|^{2}+|{\bm{B}}_{0}|^{2}\right)\right].

(192)

Eq. (
192
) is the most convenient form to evaluate free energy.

The calculation of the line tension (
\varepsilon_{l}=\mathcal{F}/d
) can be done in (2d) momentum space, using our expression for
\phi_{M}({\bm{k}})
from Eq. (
C.1
), that gives the correct expression for the
{\bm{B}}_{0}
field as
z\to 0^{+}
in the half-space sample, and therefore by translation invariance at all
z
in the 3D sample with no boundaries.
In this case

{\bm{B}}_{0}={\bm{B}}_{0}(\bm{\rho})\hat{z}=-\partial_{z}\phi_{M}(\bm{\rho},z)|_{z\to 0^{+}}=\int\frac{d^{2}{\bm{k}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,e^{i{\bm{k}}\cdot\bm{\rho}}\,k\,\phi_{M}({\bm{k}}),\text{ such that we can define }{\bm{B}}_{0}({\bm{k}})=k\,\phi_{M}({\bm{k}})\,\hat{z}

(193)

has a component only in the
z
-direction, so the current in purely azimuthal. That gives line tension,

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{l}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda_{L}^{2}}{2\mu_{0}}\int d^{2}\bm{\rho}\,|\nabla\,\times{\bm{B}}_{0}|^{2}+\frac{1}{2\mu_{0}}\int d^{2}\bm{\rho}\,|{\bm{B}}_{0}|^{2}

(194)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda_{L}^{2}}{2\mu_{0}}\int\frac{d^{2}{\bm{k}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,|i{\bm{k}}\times{\bm{B}}_{0}({\bm{k}})|^{2}+\frac{1}{2\mu_{0}}\int\frac{d^{2}{\bm{k}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}|{\bm{B}}({\bm{k}})|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda_{L}^{2}}{2\mu_{0}}\int\frac{d^{2}{\bm{k}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,k^{4}|\phi_{M}({\bm{k}})|^{2}+\frac{1}{2\mu_{0}}\int\frac{d^{2}{\bm{k}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,k^{2}|\phi_{M}({\bm{k}})|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Phi_{0}^{2}}{2\mu_{0}}\int\frac{d^{2}{\bm{k}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{\lambda_{L}^{2}k^{2}}{(1+\lambda_{L}^{2}k^{2})^{2}}+\frac{\Phi_{0}^{2}}{2\mu_{0}}\int\frac{d^{2}{\bm{k}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{1}{(1+\lambda_{L}^{2}k^{2})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Phi_{0}^{2}}{2\mu_{0}}\frac{1}{2\pi\lambda_{L}^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tilde{k}\,\frac{\tilde{k}^{3}}{(1+\tilde{k}^{2})^{2}}+\frac{1}{2\mu_{0}}\frac{\Phi_{0}^{2}}{2\pi\lambda_{L}^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tilde{k}\frac{\tilde{k}}{(1+\tilde{k}^{2})^{2}}.

The first term of RHS is UV-divergent, so we need to regulate it by remembering that our vortex solution stops being valid for
\rho\lesssim\xi
, implying that we need to cut-off the integral at
k\approx\xi^{-1}
, i.e.,
\tilde{k}\approx\lambda_{L}/\xi=\kappa\gg 1

67
.
This yields

\frac{\Phi_{0}^{2}}{2\mu_{0}}\frac{1}{2\pi\lambda_{L}^{2}}\int_{0}^{\kappa}d\tilde{k}\,\frac{\tilde{k}^{3}}{(1+\tilde{k}^{2})^{2}}=\frac{1}{2\mu_{0}}\frac{\Phi_{0}^{2}}{2\pi\lambda_{L}^{2}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(1+\frac{\lambda_{L}^{2}}{\xi^{2}}\right)-\frac{1}{2(1+(\xi/\lambda_{L})^{2})}\right]\approx\frac{1}{2\mu_{0}}\frac{\Phi_{0}^{2}}{2\pi\lambda_{L}^{2}}\left[\ln\left(\frac{\lambda_{L}}{\xi}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\right].

(195)

For the second term, we find that there is no issue with UV divergence

\frac{1}{2\mu_{0}}\frac{\Phi_{0}^{2}}{2\pi\lambda_{L}^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tilde{k}\frac{\tilde{k}}{(1+\tilde{k}^{2})^{2}}=\frac{1}{2\mu_{0}}\frac{\Phi_{0}^{2}}{2\pi\lambda_{L}^{2}}\frac{1}{2}.

(196)

The magnetic field contribution exactly cancels off the subleading contribution due to current density, leading to a net line tension, i.e., free energy per unit length in the
z
-direction, given by

\varepsilon_{l}=\frac{1}{2\mu_{0}}\frac{\Phi_{0}^{2}}{2\pi\lambda_{L}^{2}}\ln\left(\frac{\lambda_{L}}{\xi}\right)=\frac{1}{2\mu_{0}}\underbrace{\left(\frac{\Phi_{0}}{\pi\lambda_{L}^{2}}\right)^{2}}_{\text{average magnetic field}}\times\underbrace{(\pi\lambda_{L}^{2})}_{\text{area where field is significant}}\times\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\frac{\lambda_{L}}{\xi}\right)}_{\text{U.V. physics}}.

(197)

E.2
Interaction energy of two vortices

We assume that the two vortices (labeled 1 and 2) are placed at a distance
\rho_{0}~(\gg\xi)
from each other, and that the total super-current density and the magnetic fields are the superpositions of those of the individual vortices.
Therefore, using Eq. (
192
), the total energy is given by

\mathcal{F}(\rho_{0})=\frac{1}{2\mu_{0}}\left[\int d^{3}{\bm{r}}\,(\lambda_{L}\mu_{0})^{2}({\bm{j}}_{s,1}+{\bm{j}}_{s,2})^{2}+\int d^{3}{\bm{r}}\,({\bm{B}}_{0,1}+{\bm{B}}_{0,2})^{2}\right]=\mathcal{F}_{1}+\mathcal{F}_{2}+V(\rho_{0}),

(198)

where
\mathcal{F}_{1}
and
\mathcal{F}_{2}
are the self-energies of the two vortices calculated in the previous section, and
V(\rho_{0})
is interaction energy between two vortices, given by

V(\rho_{0})=\frac{1}{\mu_{0}}\left[\int d^{3}{\bm{r}}\,(\lambda_{L}\mu_{0})^{2}({\bm{j}}_{s,1}\cdot{\bm{j}}_{s,2})+\int d^{3}{\bm{r}}\,{\bm{B}}_{0,1}\cdot{\bm{B}}_{0,2}\right].

(199)

More generally, in case of multiple vortices, we may write the most general interaction energy respecting translation invariance as

\mathcal{H}_{\rm interaction}=\frac{1}{2}\int d^{2}\bm{\rho}\int d^{2}\bm{\rho}^{\prime}\,n_{v}(\bm{\rho})V(\bm{\rho}-\bm{\rho}^{\prime})n_{v}(\bm{\rho}^{\prime}).

(200)

This reduces to the expression for two vortices in Eq. (
199
) when we consider two point vortices, such
n_{v}(\bm{\rho})=\delta^{(2)}(\bm{\rho})+\delta^{(2)}(\bm{\rho}-\bm{\rho_{0}})
.
Our goal is to find
V(\bm{\rho})=V(|\bm{\rho}|)
, and subsequently its Fourier transform
V(k)
.

E.2.1
Abrikosov vortex

In this case, we again assume translation invariance in the
z
-direction, and focus on the energy per unit length.

\displaystyle\frac{V(\rho_{0})}{d}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu_{0}}\left[\int d^{2}\bm{\rho}\,(\lambda_{L}\mu_{0})^{2}({\bm{j}}_{s,1}\cdot{\bm{j}}_{s,2})+\int d^{2}\bm{\rho}\,{\bm{B}}_{0,1}\cdot{\bm{B}}_{0,2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu_{0}}\left[\int\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}(\lambda_{L}\mu_{0})^{2}\,{\bm{j}}_{s}({\bm{k}})\cdot{\bm{j}}_{s}(-{\bm{k}})e^{i{\bm{k}}\cdot\bm{\rho_{0}}}+\int\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}{\bm{B}}({\bm{k}})\cdot{\bm{B}}(-{\bm{k}})e^{i{\bm{k}}\cdot\bm{\rho}_{0}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu_{0}}\int\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}(k^{2}\lambda_{L}^{2}+1)|B_{0}^{z}({\bm{k}})|^{2}e^{i{\bm{k}}\cdot\bm{\rho}_{0}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Phi_{0}^{2}}{\mu_{0}}\int\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{1}{1+k^{2}\lambda_{L}^{2}}e^{i{\bm{k}}\cdot\bm{\rho}_{0}}

(201)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Phi_{0}^{2}}{2\pi\mu_{0}\lambda_{L}^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tilde{k}\,\frac{\tilde{k}J_{0}(\tilde{k}\rho_{0}/\lambda_{L})}{1+\tilde{k}^{2}}=\frac{\Phi_{0}^{2}}{2\pi\mu_{0}\lambda_{L}^{2}}K_{0}\left(\frac{\rho_{0}}{\lambda_{L}}\right),

(202)

where we used
B_{0}^{z}({\bm{k}})=k\phi_{M}(k)=\Phi_{0}/(1+k^{2}\lambda_{L}^{2})
.
Remarkably, this has a similar analytic form as the single vortex self-energy, except that the modified Bessel function
K_{0}
has an argument that scales with the distance
\rho_{0}
between the vortices relative to the penetration depth
\lambda_{L}
.
Thus, we may use the asymptotic form of
K_{0}(x)
to determine the behavior is the small and large distance limits.
For small
x
we have
K_{0}(x)\sim-\ln(x)
, so the interaction scales as
\ln(\lambda_L/\rho_0)
for small
\rho_{0}/\lambda_{L}
, i.e, we recover the expected logarithmic interaction of unscreened vortices at lengthscales below the penetration depth
\lambda_{L}
.
For large
x
we have
K_{0}(x)\sim e^{-x}/\sqrt{x}
, so the interaction between vortices drop exponentially beyond the penetration depth
\lambda_{L}
.

Finally, from Eq. (
201
), we can also read off the Fourier space interaction (per unit length) for pairwise interaction between vortices:

\frac{V({\bm{k}})}{d}=\int d^{2}\bm{\rho}\,e^{-i{\bm{k}}\cdot\bm{\rho}}\frac{V(\rho)}{d}=\frac{\Phi_{0}^{2}}{\mu_{0}(1+k^{2}\lambda_{L}^{2})}.

(203)

E.2.2
Pearl vortex

The result follows directly from the self-energy calculation for the Pearl vortex and the interaction energy calculation for 3D vortices (except that we now calculate the total energy rather than the energy per unit length).

\displaystyle V(\rho_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu_{0}}\left[\int d^{2}\bm{\rho}\,(\lambda_{L}\mu_{0})^{2}({\bm{j}}_{s,1}\cdot{\bm{j}}_{s,2})+\int d^{2}\bm{\rho}\,{\bm{B}}_{0,1}\cdot{\bm{B}}_{0,2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{2\Lambda}{\mu_{0}}\int\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}|{\bm{B}}_{\parallel}({\bm{k}})|^{2}e^{i{\bm{k}}\cdot\bm{\rho}_{0}}+\frac{1}{\mu_{0}}\int\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{(|{\bm{B}}_{\parallel}({\bm{k}})|^{2}+|{\bm{B}}_{\perp}({\bm{k}})|^{2})e^{i{\bm{k}}\cdot\bm{\rho}_{0}}}{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\mu_{0}}\int\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,k(\Lambda k+1)|\phi_{M}({\bm{k}})|^{2}e^{i{\bm{k}}\cdot\bm{\rho}_{0}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\Phi_{0}^{2}}{\mu_{0}}\int\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\frac{1}{k(1+\Lambda k)}e^{i{\bm{k}}\cdot\bm{\rho}_{0}}

(204)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Phi_{0}^{2}}{\pi\mu_{0}\Lambda}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tilde{k}\frac{J_{0}(\tilde{k}\rho_{0}/\lambda_{L})}{1+\tilde{k}}=\frac{\Phi_{0}^{2}}{2\mu_{0}\Lambda}\left[H_{0}\left(\frac{\rho_{0}}{\Lambda}\right)-Y_{0}\left(\frac{\rho_{0}}{\Lambda}\right)\right],

(205)

where we have used that
\phi_{M}({\bm{k}})=\Phi_{0}/[k(1+\Lambda k)]
for Pearl vortices.
Once again, we may study the asymptotic limits — we have
H_{0}(x)-Y_{0}(x)\approx-\ln(x)
if
x\ll 1
, which leads to the expected unscreened logarithmic interaction between two Pearl vortices at short distances
\rho_{0}
compared to the Pearl length
\Lambda
.
For large
\rho_{0}/\Lambda
, we need the asymptotic form
H_{0}(x)-Y_{0}(x)\approx 1/x
for
x\gg 1
, which leads to
V(\rho_{0})\approx\Phi_{0}^{2}/(2\mu_{0}\rho_{0})
.
This is remarkably different from 3D vortices, and indicates a long range Coulomb-like
1/\rho
interaction between two pointlike Pearl vortices.
We will later show that this long-range interaction will have important consequences for the longitudinal elastic modulus of a lattice of Pearl vortices.

Finally, from Eq. (
204
), we can again read off the Fourier transform of the pair-interaction potential for Pearl vortices:

V({\bm{k}})=\frac{2\Phi_{0}^{2}}{\mu_{0}k(1+k\Lambda)}.

(206)

Appendix F
Vortex lattice phonon dispersion

In this appendix, we will first focus on the derivation of the propagators
G({\bm{k}})
that connect the in-plane displacements to the linear order expansion of the magnetic fields. From there, we can compute the vortex phonon noise, integrated from longitudinal correlation function
C_{\mathrm{L}}({\bm{k}},\Omega)=\langle{\bm{k}}\cdot{\bm{u}}_{{\bm{k}},\Omega}\,~{\bm{k}}\cdot{\bm{u}}_{-{\bm{k}},-\Omega}\rangle
.
We then determine the elastic moduli of the vortex lattice by vortex interactions, which together with
G({\bm{k}})
lead to the full expression of
C_{\mathrm{L}}
.

F.1
Propagators
G(\boldsymbol{k})

For
z\gg\lambda_{L}
, the magnetic field of a vortex behaves like that of a magnetic monopole, as discussed in Appendix
C
,

\phi_{M}(\bm{\rho},z\gg\lambda_{L})=\frac{\Phi_{0}}{2\pi r},\quad{\bm{B}}({\bm{r}})=\frac{\Phi_{0}}{2\pi r^{2}}\hat{r}~~~\text{ for }~r=\sqrt{\rho^{2}+z^{2}}\gg\lambda_{L}.

(207)

The sum of the magentic field at position
{\bm{r}}_{q}=(\boldsymbol{0},z_{0})
generated by each vortex in the lattice is

{\bm{B}}({\bm{r}}_{q},t)=\frac{\Phi_{0}}{2\pi}\sum_{i}\frac{{\bm{r}}_{q}-{\bm{r}}_{i}(t)}{\left|{\bm{r}}_{q}-{\bm{r}}_{i}(t)\right|^{3}}.

(208)

where
{\bm{r}}_{i}(t)=({\bm{R}}_{i}+{\bm{u}}_{i}(t),0)
,
{\bm{R}}_{i}
is the equilibrium position of the
i
-th vortex in the lattice, and
{\bm{u}}_{i}(t)
is its displacement from equilibrium. For small displacements
|{\bm{u}}_{i}(t)|\ll a_{\Delta}
, we can expand the magnetic field to linear order in
{\bm{u}}_{i}(t)
, by letting
\boldsymbol{\varrho}_{i}(t)={\bm{r}}_{q}-{\bm{r}}_{i}(t)={\bm{r}}_{q}-{\bm{R}}_{i}-{\bm{u}}_{i}(t)
,
\boldsymbol{\varrho}_{0i}={\bm{r}}_{q}-{\bm{R}}_{i}
,

\left|\boldsymbol{\varrho}_{i}(t)\right|^{-3}\approx\varrho_{0i}^{-3}\left[1+\frac{3\boldsymbol{\varrho}_{0i}\cdot{\bm{u}}_{i}}{\varrho_{0i}^{2}}\right]\implies\frac{\boldsymbol{\varrho}_{0i}-{\bm{u}}_{i}}{\left|\boldsymbol{\varrho}_{0i}-{\bm{u}}_{i}\right|^{3}}=(\boldsymbol{\varrho}_{0i}-{\bm{u}}_{i})\left|\boldsymbol{\varrho}_{i}(t)\right|^{-3}\approx\frac{\boldsymbol{\varrho}_{0i}}{\varrho_{0i}^{3}}+\frac{3\boldsymbol{\varrho}_{0i}\left(\boldsymbol{\varrho}_{0i}\cdot{\bm{u}}_{i}\right)}{\varrho_{0i}^{5}}-\frac{{\bm{u}}_{i}}{\varrho_{0i}^{3}},

(209)

that help us find the linear order expansion of the magnetic field,

\displaystyle{\bm{B}}\left({\bm{r}}_{q},t\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Phi_{0}}{2\pi}\sum_{i}\left[\frac{{\bm{r}}_{q}-{\bm{R}}_{i}}{\left|{\bm{r}}_{q}-{\bm{R}}_{i}\right|^{3}}+\frac{3\left({\bm{r}}_{q}-{\bm{R}}_{i}\right)\left[\left({\bm{r}}_{q}-{\bm{R}}_{i}\right)\cdot{\bm{u}}_{i}(t)\right]-{\bm{u}}_{i}(t)\left|{\bm{r}}_{q}-{\bm{R}}_{i}\right|^{2}}{\left|{\bm{r}}_{q}-{\bm{R}}_{i}\right|^{5}}\right].

(210)

Apply
\boldsymbol{\varrho}_{0i}={\bm{r}}_{q}-{\bm{R}}_{i}=(\boldsymbol{0}-{\bm{R}}_{i},z_{0})
,
\varrho_{0i}^{2}=R_{i}^{2}+z_{0}^{2}
,
\boldsymbol{\varrho}_{0i}\cdot{\bm{u}}_{i}=-{\bm{R}}_{i}\cdot{\bm{u}}_{i}

66

\displaystyle{\bm{B}}\left({\bm{r}}_{q},t\right)

\displaystyle={\bm{B}}_{0}\left({\bm{r}}_{q}\right)+\delta B_{z}\left({\bm{r}}_{q},t\right)\hat{z}+\delta{\bm{B}}_{\|}\left({\bm{r}}_{q},t\right),\text{ where }

(211)

\displaystyle{\bm{B}}_{0}\left({\bm{r}}_{q}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Phi_{0}}{2\pi}\left(\sum_{i}\frac{z_{0}}{\left(z_{0}^{2}+R_{i}^{2}\right)^{3/2}}\right)\hat{z},

\displaystyle\delta B_{z}\left({\bm{r}}_{q},t\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\Phi_{0}}{2\pi}\sum_{i}\frac{3z_{0}{\bm{R}}_{i}\cdot{\bm{u}}_{i}(t)}{\left(z_{0}^{2}+R_{i}^{2}\right)^{5/2}},\text{ and }

\displaystyle\delta{\bm{B}}_{\|}\left({\bm{r}}_{q},t\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Phi_{0}}{2\pi}\sum_{i}\frac{3{\bm{R}}_{i}\left({\bm{R}}_{i}\cdot{\bm{u}}_{i}(t)\right)-{\bm{u}}_{i}(t)\left(z_{0}^{2}+R_{i}^{2}\right)}{\left(z_{0}^{2}+R_{i}^{2}\right)^{5/2}}.

The first term is the static field from the vortex lattice, and the second and third terms are the fluctuating fields due to vortex motion (out-of-plane and in-plane components).

When
z_{0}\gg a_{\Delta}
, we can approximate the sum over the vortex lattice by an integral,
\sum_{i}\rightarrow n_{v}\int d^{2}R
,

\displaystyle{\bm{B}}_{0}\left({\bm{r}}_{q}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Phi_{0}n_{v}}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}d^{2}R\frac{z_{0}}{\left(R^{2}+z_{0}^{2}\right)^{3/2}}\hat{z}=\frac{\Phi_{0}n_{v}z_{0}}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\varphi\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{R\,dR}{\left(R^{2}+z_{0}^{2}\right)^{3/2}}\hat{z}=\Phi_{0}n_{v}\hat{z}

(212)

\displaystyle\delta B_{z}(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\Phi_{0}n_{v}}{2\pi}\int d^{2}R\frac{3z_{0}{\bm{R}}\cdot{\bm{u}}({\bm{R}},t)}{\left(R^{2}+z_{0}^{2}\right)^{5/2}},

\displaystyle\delta\boldsymbol{B}_{\|}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Phi_{0}n_{v}}{2\pi}\int d^{2}R\frac{3{\bm{R}}({\bm{R}}\cdot{\bm{u}}({\bm{R}},t))-{\bm{u}}({\bm{R}},t)\left(R^{2}+z_{0}^{2}\right)}{\left(R^{2}+z_{0}^{2}\right)^{5/2}}.

We could consider to write fluctuating fields in Fourier space for convenience, using the displacement field
{\bm{u}}_{i}(t)=N_{v}^{-1/2}\sum_{{\bm{k}}}{\bm{u}}({\bm{k}},t)e^{i{\bm{k}}\cdot{\bm{R}}_{i}}

N_{v}
is the total number of vortices in the lattice, for out-of-plane component,

\displaystyle\delta B_{z}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{v}}}\sum_{{\bm{k}}}G_{z}{}^{\mu}({\bm{k}})u_{\mu}({\bm{k}},t),

(213)

\displaystyle G_{z}{}^{\mu}({\bm{k}})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\Phi_{0}n_{v}}{2\pi}\int d^{2}R\frac{3z_{0}R^{\mu}}{\left(R^{2}+z_{0}^{2}\right)^{5/2}}e^{i{\bm{k}}\cdot{\bm{R}}}=i\frac{\Phi_{0}n_{v}}{2\pi}\cdot 3z_{0}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial k_{\mu}}\int d^{2}R\frac{e^{i{\bm{k}}\cdot{\bm{R}}}}{\left(R^{2}+z_{0}^{2}\right)^{5/2}}

\displaystyle=i\frac{\Phi_{0}n_{v}}{2\pi}\cdot 3z_{0}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial k_{\mu}}\frac{2\pi}{3z_{0}^{3}}\left(1+k_{\mu}z_{0}\right)e^{-kz_{0}}=\Phi_{0}n_{v}(-ik^{\mu})e^{-kz_{0}}=-n_{v}\Phi_{0}\frac{k_{\mu}k_{\nu}}{k}e^{-kz_{0}},

for in-plane component,

\displaystyle\delta B_{\|}{}^{\mu}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{v}}}\sum_{{\bm{k}}}G_{\|}{}^{\mu\nu}({\bm{k}})u_{\nu}({\bm{k}},t),

(214)

F.2
Elastic moduli of vortex lattices

In this section, we provide a brief derivation of the longitudinal elastic moduli
c_{11}(k)
of the vortex lattice for rigid Abrikosov vortices and Pearl vortices, and indicate how to compute the transverse modulus
c_{66}(k)
.
To this end, we will take a continuum approach, and assume that small lattice displacements can be quantified by a continuum displacement field
{\bm{u}}(\bm{\rho})
, where
\bm{\rho}=(x,y)
denotes the coordinate in the 2D plane.
Accordingly, the vortex number density will change as

\delta n_{v}(\bm{\rho})=-\bar{n}_{v}\bm{\nabla}\cdot{\bm{u}}(\bm{\rho}),

(216)

where we have used
\bar{n}_{v}
to denote the average density of vortices (and avoid confusion with the spatially fluctuating local density
n_{v}(\bm{\rho}
): in the main text this average density has been denoted simply by
n_{v}
).
Accordingly, in Fourier space, we can write the vortex density as
\delta n_{v}({\bm{k}})=-i\bar{n}_{v}{\bm{k}}\cdot{\bm{u}}({\bm{k}})=-i\bar{n}_{v}ku_{\mathrm{L}}({\bm{k}})
, which indicates that only longitudinal displacements
u_{\mathrm{L}}({\bm{k}})=\hat{{\bm{k}}}\cdot{\bm{u}}({\bm{k}})
change the vortex density.
Given the pairwise interaction potential
V({\bm{k}})
between vortices, the change in energy due to such a longitudinal displacement of the vortex lattice is given by

\delta E=\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}V({\bm{k}})\delta n_{v}({\bm{k}})\delta n_{v}(-{\bm{k}})=\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\bar{n}_{v}^{2}V({\bm{k}})k^{2}|u_{\mathrm{L}}({\bm{k}})|^{2}.

(217)

Comparing with the continuum elastic Hamiltonian, where
\delta E=\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}c_{11}({\bm{k}})k^{2}|u_{\mathrm{L}}({\bm{k}})|^{2}
for a longitudinal deformation (e.g., a compression), we find that:

c_{11}(k)=\bar{n}_{v}^{2}V(k).

(218)

Thus, the compression modulus is directly determined by the pair-wise interaction potential
V(k)
between vortices, as quoted in the main text.
The relevant interaction potentials for Abrikosov vortices (per unit length) and Pearl vortices were calculated in Appendix
E.2
.

Finally, we note that our results for the compression modulus can also be derived more formally by expanding the interaction Hamiltonian for the vortex lattice without appealing to the continuum limit.
We sketch the derivation here as it also leads naturally the shear modulus (which is zero in the continuum approximation).
The energy per unit area (per unit volume for Abrikosov vortices) due to a lattice displacement field
{\bm{u}}({\bm{k}})
can generally be written as

\frac{\mathcal{H}_{\rm int}}{A}=\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}u^{\alpha}({\bm{k}})\Phi_{\alpha\beta}({\bm{k}})u^{\beta}(-{\bm{k}}),\text{ with }\Phi_{\alpha\beta}({\bm{k}})=\sum_{\bm{G}}V_{\alpha\beta}^{\prime\prime}({\bm{G}})-V_{\alpha\beta}^{\prime\prime}({\bm{k}}+{\bm{G}}),

(219)

where
{\bm{G}}
denote the reciprocal vectors of the vortex lattice, and we have defined
V^{\prime\prime}_{\alpha\beta}({\bm{k}})
as the Fourier transform of
\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\beta}V(\rho)|_{\bm{\rho}={\bm{R}}_{i}}
, i.e.,

V^{\prime\prime}_{\alpha\beta}({\bm{k}})=\sum_{i}e^{-i{\bm{k}}\cdot{\bm{R}}_{i}}\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\beta}V(\rho)|_{\bm{\rho}={\bm{R}}_{i}}\xrightarrow{\rm{continuum~limit}}n_{v}\int d^{2}\bm{\rho}\,e^{-i{\bm{k}}\cdot\bm{\rho}}\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\beta}V(\rho).

(220)

If the sum on
{\bm{G}}
is convergent, we may further integrate by parts to write

V_{\alpha\beta}^{\prime\prime}({\bm{G}})-V_{\alpha\beta}^{\prime\prime}({\bm{k}}+{\bm{G}})=\left[({\bm{k}}+{\bm{G}})_{\alpha}({\bm{k}}+{\bm{G}})_{\beta}-{\bm{G}}_{\alpha}{\bm{G}}_{\beta}\right]V({\bm{k}}+{\bm{G}}).

(221)

In general, for a triangular lattice,
\Phi_{\alpha\beta}({\bm{k}})=k_{\alpha}k_{\beta}c_{11}(k)+(k^{2}\delta_{\alpha\beta}-k_{\alpha}k_{\beta})c_{66}(k)

where
c_{11}(k)
is the compression modulus and
c_{66}(k)
is the shear modulus.
We see that if we keep only the
{\bm{G}}=0
term to determine the elastic free energy, we get consistent results with the continuum theory.
Specifically, within this approximation, the compressional modes with
\nabla\cdot{\bm{u}}\neq 0
cost finite energy, while the shear modes cost zero energy, which is the characteristic of a vortex liquid phase.
Keeping the
{\bm{G}}\neq 0
terms gives a finite shear modulus which is approximately
{\bm{k}}
independent
8
;
9
.

Vortex phonon correlation function

In this section, we derive the longitudinal vortex phonon correlation function

This is easiest to see by writing the matrix
M^{-1}_{{\bm{k}},\Omega}
in the basis spanned by
\hat{{\bm{k}}}
and
\hat{z}\times\hat{{\bm{k}}}

M^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}a&c\\
-c&b\end{pmatrix}\implies M=\begin{pmatrix}b&-c\\
c&a\end{pmatrix},

(224)

where we have suppressed the
({\bm{k}},\Omega)

Therefore, the object of interest to calculate the longitudinal correlation function for vortex lattice phonons is given by

\Tr[P_{\mathrm{L}}MM^{\dagger}]=\frac{1}{|ab+c^{2}|^{2}}\Tr[P_{\mathrm{L}}(bP_{\mathrm{L}}+aP_{\mathrm{T}}-c\varepsilon)(b^{*}P_{\mathrm{L}}+a^{*}P_{\mathrm{T}}-c^{*}\varepsilon)]=\frac{|a|^{2}+|c|^{2}}{|ab+c^{2}|^{2}},

(225)

where we have used that
P_{\mathrm{L}}^{2}=P_{\mathrm{L}}

P_{\mathrm{T}}^{2}=P_{\mathrm{T}}

\Tr[P_{\mathrm{L}}\varepsilon]=0=\Tr[P_{\mathrm{T}}\varepsilon]

a_{{\bm{k}},\Omega}=c_{11}(k)k^{2}/n_{v}-\mu_{v}\Omega^{2}-i\eta\Omega
,
b_{{\bm{k}},\Omega}=c_{66}(k)k^{2}/n_{v}-\mu_{v}\Omega^{2}-i\eta\Omega

c_{{\bm{k}},\Omega}=i\Omega n_{s}h
from Eq. (

\Tr[P_{\mathrm{L}}({\bm{k}})M_{{\bm{k}},\Omega}M^{\dagger}_{{\bm{k}},\Omega}]=\frac{|c_{11}(k)k^{2}/n_{v}-\mu_{v}\Omega^{2}-i\eta\Omega|^{2}+(\Omega n_{s}h)^{2}}{|(c_{11}(k)k^{2}/n_{v}-\mu_{v}\Omega^{2}-i\eta\Omega)(c_{66}(k)k^{2}/n_{v}-\mu_{v}\Omega^{2}-i\eta\Omega)-(\Omega n_{s}h)^{2}|^{2}},

(226)

which leads to Eq. (

G.1
Derivation of diffusive vortex dynamics

In this appendix, we provide details on the magnetic noise calculations for the vortex liquid phase that was omitted in the main text.
To this end, we first derive the diffusion constant of the vortex liquid.
For this purpose, we shall derive the Fokker-Planck equations to the corresponding Langevin equations of (
93
).
The Fokker-Planck equation models the evolution of the probability distribution from the Langevin equations, and allows us to read off the diffusion constant directly.

Recall that the Langevin equations in the vortex liquid are given as,

M\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{t}u^{x}\\
\partial_{t}u^{y}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\bar{\zeta}^{x}\\
\bar{\zeta}^{y}\end{pmatrix},\textrm{ where }M=\begin{pmatrix}\bar{\eta}&-n_{s}h\\
n_{s}h&\bar{\eta}\end{pmatrix}

(227)

and
\bar{\zeta}
are Gaussian white noise with zero mean satisfying the FDT,

\langle{\bar{\zeta}^{\alpha}(t)\bar{\zeta}^{\beta}(t^{\prime})\rangle}=2\bar{\eta}k_{B}T\,\delta^{\alpha\beta}\,\delta(t-t^{\prime})\,.

(228)

Let us apply
M^{-1}
on both sides of Eq. (
227
).
We find

\partial_{t}u^{\alpha}=\tilde{\zeta}^{\alpha}\,,\textrm{ where }\ \tilde{\zeta}^{\alpha}=\frac{\bar{\eta}~\bar{\zeta}^{\alpha}+n_{s}h~\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}\bar{\zeta}^{\beta}}{\bar{\eta}^{2}+n_{s}^{2}h^{2}}.

(229)

It follows from Eq. (
228
) that
\langle{\tilde{\zeta}^{\alpha}(t)\rangle}=0
, and
\langle\tilde{\zeta}^{\alpha}(t)\tilde{\zeta}^{\beta}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\frac{2\bar{\eta}k_{B}T}{\bar{\eta}^{2}+(n_{s}h)^{2}}\delta^{\alpha\beta}\delta(t-t^{\prime})
.
This result has two important consequences.
First, the noise correlations remain diagonal despite the presence of the Magnus force, implying that the vortex dynamics can still be mapped onto effective Brownian motion.
Second, the absence of cross-correlations indicates that the
x
and
y
components of the displacement are dynamically decoupled and may be treated independently.

Having simplified the Langevin equations, we now derive the corresponding Fokker-Planck equation to model the evolution of the probability distribution due to the stochastic dynamics of Eq. (
227
)
33
.
To this end, let us consider a smooth function
h(u)
, and ask how the expectation value of
h(u(t))
evolves under a set of stochastic differential equations (such as Eqs. (
227
) and (
229
)).

\begin{split}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\langle h(u(t))\rangle&=\int du\,h(u)\partial_{t}P(u,t)\\
&=\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{\Delta t}\left[\int du\int dv\,h(u)P(u,t+\Delta t|v,t)P(v,t)-\int dv\,h(v)P(v,t)\right]\\
&=\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{\Delta t}\left[\int du\int dv\,\sum\frac{(u-v)^{n}}{n!}h^{(n)}(v)P(u,t+\Delta t|v,t)P(v,t)-\int dv\,h(v)P(v,t)\right]\,,\end{split}

(230)

where in the second line,
P(u,t+\Delta t|v,t)
denotes the conditional probability that a particle at position
u
at time
t
moves to position
v
at time
t+\Delta t
.
In the third line we have performed a Taylor expansion of
h(u)
about
u=v
.
Since
\int du\,P(u,t+\Delta t|v,t)=1
, we find that the zeroth order of the expansion cancels, and we have,

\begin{split}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\langle h(u(t))\rangle&=\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{\Delta t}\left[\int du\int dv\,h^{(1)}(v)\langle(u-v)\rangle+\frac{h^{(2)}(v)}{2}\langle(u-v)^{2}\rangle\right]\,,\end{split}

(231)

where
\langle(u-v)^{n}\rangle=\int dvP(v,t)(u-v)^{n}
.
Here, we have omitted the the higher than quadratic order in the Taylor expansion, which vanish in the limit of
\Delta t\rightarrow 0
, as we will show later.
Let us denote with

\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0}\frac{\langle(u-v)\rangle}{\Delta t}=D^{(1)}(v)
,
\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0}\frac{\langle(u-v)^{2}\rangle}{\Delta t}=D^{(2)}(v)
.
Integrating the right hand side of Eq. (
231
) by parts,
We finally arrive at,

\begin{split}\int du\,h(u)\partial_{t}P(u,t)&=\int du\,h(u)\left\{-\partial_{u}D^{(1)}(u)P(u,t)+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{u}^{2}D^{(2)}(u)P(u,t)\right\}\\
&\Rightarrow\partial_{t}P(u,t)=-\partial_{u}D^{(1)}P(u,t)+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{u}^{2}D^{(2)}(u).\end{split}

(232)

Consequently, to model the evolution of the probability distribution due to the stochastic differential equations of Eqs. (
227
), we simply need to find
D^{(1,2)}
.

From Eq. (
229
), we immediately find that
D^{(1)}
is zero.
To see this, let us find the change in
u
from time
t
to time
t+\Delta t
.
Since
\langle\partial_{t}u\rangle=\langle\zeta_{i}\rangle=0
, it follows that
D^{(1)}=\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0}\frac{\langle\Delta u\rangle}{\Delta t}=0
.
Now let us find
D^{(2)}
.

D^{(2)}=\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0}\frac{\langle\Delta u^{2}\rangle}{\Delta t}=\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0}\frac{\langle(\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t}dt^{\prime}\tilde{\zeta}_{t^{\prime}})^{2}\rangle}{\Delta t}=\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0}\frac{\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t}dt^{\prime}dt^{\prime\prime}\frac{2\bar{\eta}k_{B}T}{\bar{\eta}^{2}+(n_{s}h)^{2}}\delta(t^{\prime}-t^{\prime\prime})}{\Delta t}=\frac{2\bar{\eta}k_{B}T}{\bar{\eta}^{2}+(n_{s}h)^{2}}.

(233)

Putting this altogether, we consequently find that the Fokker-Planck equations are given by,

\partial_{t}P(u,t)=-\frac{2\bar{\eta}k_{B}T}{\bar{\eta}^{2}+(n_{s}h)^{2}}\partial_{u}^{2}P(u,t)\,.

(234)

Consequently, we find that despite the presence of Magnus force, the overall vortex dynamics is still diffusive.
Accordingly, the vortex density-density correlation function takes the following form,

C_{n_{v}n_{v}}({\bm{k}},\Omega)=\langle n_{v}({\bm{k}},\Omega)n_{v}(-{\bm{k}},-\Omega)\rangle=\frac{2k_{B}T\chi_{v}D_{v}k^{2}}{\Omega^{2}+(D_{v}k^{2})^{2}},

(235)

where
\chi_{v}=(\partial n_{v}/\partial\mu)_{T}
denotes the vortex compressibility.

G.2
Details on different noise regimes of the vortex liquid

We now discuss the various regimes of the magnetic noise in the vortex liquid phase.
We start with the expression for noise for a liquid of Pearl vortices in 2D:

\mathcal{N}_{zz}(\Omega)=\frac{k_{B}T\sigma_{v}\Phi_{0}^{2}}{\pi D_{v}^{2}}I_{2}(z_{0},\Lambda,\ell_{\Omega}).\text{ where }I_{2}(z_{0},\Lambda,\ell_{\Omega})=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tilde{k}\,\frac{\tilde{k}^{3}\,e^{-2\tilde{k}z_{0}/\Lambda}}{(1+\tilde{k})^{2}\left[\left(\frac{\Lambda}{\ell_{\Omega}}\right)^{4}+\tilde{k}^{4}\right]}.

(236)

We note that the integral
I_{2}(z_{0},\Lambda,\ell_{\Omega})
in Eq. (
241
) is determined by the hierarchy of three lengthscales —
z_{0}
,
\Lambda
and
\ell_{\Omega}=\sqrt{D_{v}/\Omega}
.
Hence, we evaluate the integral in these different limits, leading to the results presented in Table
1
in the main text.

First, if
\Lambda,z_{0}\ll\ell_{\Omega}
, then the integral can be approximated by:

I_{2}(z_{0},\Lambda,\ell_{\Omega})\approx\int_{\Lambda/\ell_{\Omega}}^{\infty}d\tilde{k}\,\frac{\,e^{-2\tilde{k}z_{0}/\Lambda}}{\tilde{k}(1+\tilde{k})^{2}}\approx\begin{cases}\displaystyle\int_{\Lambda/\ell_{\Omega}}^{\Lambda/z_{0}}\frac{d\tilde{k}}{\tilde{k}}=\ln\left(\frac{\ell_{\Omega}}{z_{0}}\right),\text{ if }z_{0}\gg\Lambda\\
\\
\displaystyle\int_{\Lambda/\ell_{\Omega}}^{1}\frac{d\tilde{k}}{\tilde{k}}+\int_{1}^{\Lambda/z_{0}}\frac{d\tilde{k}}{\tilde{k}^{3}}=\ln\left(\frac{\ell_{\Omega}}{\Lambda}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left[1-\left(\frac{z_{0}}{\Lambda}\right)^{2}\right]\approx\ln\left(\frac{\ell_{\Omega}}{\Lambda}\right),\text{ if }z_{0}\ll\Lambda\end{cases}.

(237)

Next, if
\Lambda,\ell_{\Omega}\ll z_{0}
, then the integral gets cut off at
\tilde{k}\sim\Lambda/z_{0}\ll 1
, so we may approximate:

I_{2}(z_{0},\Lambda,\ell_{\Omega})\approx\left(\frac{\ell_{\Omega}}{\Lambda}\right)^{4}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tilde{k}\,\frac{\tilde{k}^{3}\,e^{-2\tilde{k}z_{0}/\Lambda}}{\left[1+\left(\tilde{k}\ell_{\Omega}/\Lambda\right)^{4}\right]}\xrightarrow{\tilde{k}=\Lambda k}\ell_{\Omega}^{4}\int_{0}^{\infty}\,dk\,\frac{k^{3}e^{-2kz_{0}}}{1+(k\ell_{\Omega})^{4}}\approx\ell_{\Omega}^{4}\int_{0}^{\infty}\,dk\,k^{3}e^{-2kz_{0}}=\frac{3}{8}\left(\frac{\ell_{\Omega}}{z_{0}}\right)^{4}.

(238)

Finally, if
\ell_{\Omega},z_{0}\ll\Lambda
, then the answer depends on the hierarchy of
\ell_{\Omega}
and
z_{0}
.
If
\ell_{\Omega}\ll z_{0}
, then

I_{2}(z_{0},\Lambda,\ell_{\Omega})=\left(\frac{\ell_{\Omega}}{\Lambda}\right)^{4}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tilde{k}\,\frac{\tilde{k}^{3}\,e^{-2\tilde{k}z_{0}/\Lambda}}{(1+\tilde{k})^{2}\left[1+\left(\tilde{k}\ell_{\Omega}/\Lambda\right)^{4}\right]}\approx\left(\frac{\ell_{\Omega}}{\Lambda}\right)^{4}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tilde{k}\,\frac{\tilde{k}^{3}\,e^{-2\tilde{k}z_{0}/\Lambda}}{(1+\tilde{k})^{2}}\approx\left(\frac{\ell_{\Omega}}{\Lambda}\right)^{4}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tilde{k}\,\tilde{k}e^{-2\tilde{k}z_{0}/\Lambda}=\frac{\ell_{\Omega}^{4}}{4z_{0}^{2}\Lambda^{2}},

(239)

where we have used that the exponential cuts off the integral at
\tilde{k}\sim\Lambda/z_{0}\gg 1
(but still
\ll\Lambda/\ell_{\Omega}
where the
1+(\tilde{k}\ell_{\Omega}/\Lambda)^{4}
term in the denominator becomes important), while the
\tilde{k}^{3}
prefactor suppresses it close to zero, so the majority of the contribution comes from
\tilde{k}\gtrsim 1
such that we may approximate
(1+\tilde{k})^{2}\approx\tilde{k}^{2}
.

If
z_{0}\ll\ell_{\Omega}
, we can neglect the exponential factor and again approximate
(1+\tilde{k})^{2}\approx\tilde{k}^{2}

1\lesssim\tilde{k}\lesssim\Lambda/\ell_{\Omega}

Accordingly,

I_{2}(z_{0},\Lambda,\ell_{\Omega})\approx\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tilde{k}\,\frac{\tilde{k}}{\left[\left(\Lambda/\ell_{\Omega}\right)^{4}+\tilde{k}^{4}\right]}=\frac{1}{2(\Lambda/\ell_{\Omega})^{2}}\arctan\left(\frac{\tilde{k}^{2}\ell_{\Omega}^{2}}{\Lambda^{2}}\right)\bigg|_{0}^{\infty}=\frac{\pi}{4}\left(\frac{\ell_{\Omega}}{\Lambda}\right)^{2}.

(240)

Next, we consider rod-like Abrikosov vortices in 3D, where the noise in the vortex liquid phase is given by

\mathcal{N}_{zz}(\Omega)=\frac{k_{B}T\sigma_{v}\Phi_{0}^{2}}{\pi D_{v}^{2}}I_{3}(z_{0},\Lambda,\ell_{\Omega}).\text{ where }I_{3}(z_{0},\Lambda,\ell_{\Omega})=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tilde{k}\,\frac{\tilde{k}^{3}\,e^{-2\tilde{k}z_{0}/\Lambda}}{(1+\tilde{k}^{2})^{2}\left[\left(\frac{\Lambda}{\ell_{\Omega}}\right)^{4}+\tilde{k}^{4}\right]}.

(241)

We once again proceed case by case.
If
\lambda_{L},z_{0}\ll\ell_{\Omega}
, then the integral can be approximated by:

I_{3}(z_{0},\lambda_{L},\ell_{\Omega})\approx\int_{\lambda_{L}/\ell_{\Omega}}^{\infty}d\tilde{k}\,\frac{\,e^{-2\tilde{k}z_{0}/\lambda_{L}}}{\tilde{k}(1+\tilde{k}^{2})^{2}}\approx\begin{cases}\displaystyle\int_{\lambda_{L}/\ell_{\Omega}}^{\lambda_{L}/z_{0}}\frac{d\tilde{k}}{\tilde{k}}=\ln\left(\frac{\ell_{\Omega}}{z_{0}}\right),\text{ if }z_{0}\gg\lambda_{L}\\
\\
\displaystyle\int_{\lambda_{L}/\ell_{\Omega}}^{1}\frac{d\tilde{k}}{\tilde{k}}+\int_{1}^{\lambda_{L}/z_{0}}\frac{d\tilde{k}}{\tilde{k}^{5}}=\ln\left(\frac{\ell_{\Omega}}{\lambda_{L}}\right)+\frac{1}{4}\left[1-\left(\frac{z_{0}}{\lambda_{L}}\right)^{4}\right]\approx\ln\left(\frac{\ell_{\Omega}}{\lambda_{L}}\right),\text{ if }z_{0}\ll\lambda_{L}\end{cases}.

(242)

In the regime where
z_{0}\gg\lambda_{L}
, the vortices are essentially screened point objects.
Formally, the integrand in
I_{3}(z_{0},\Lambda,\ell_{\Omega})
is cut of at
\tilde{k}\sim\lambda_{L}/z_{0}\ll 1

I_{3}(z_{0},\lambda_{L},\ell_{\Omega})\approx\ell_{\Omega}^{4}\int_{0}^{\infty}\,dk\,k^{3}e^{-2kz_{0}}=\frac{3}{8}\left(\frac{\ell_{\Omega}}{z_{0}}\right)^{4}.

(243)

Finally, we consider the cases where
\lambda_{L}
is the largest length-scale.
For
\ell_{\Omega}\ll z_{0}\ll\lambda_{L}

I_{3}(z_{0},\lambda_{L},\ell_{\Omega})\approx\left(\frac{\ell_{\Omega}}{\lambda_{L}}\right)^{4}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tilde{k}\,\frac{\tilde{k}^{3}e^{-2\tilde{k}z_{0}/\lambda_{L}}}{(1+\tilde{k}^{2})^{2}}\approx\left(\frac{\ell_{\Omega}}{\lambda_{L}}\right)^{4}\int_{0}^{\lambda_{L}/z_{0}}d\tilde{k}\,\frac{\tilde{k}^{3}}{(1+\tilde{k}^{2})^{2}}\approx\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\ell_{\Omega}}{\lambda_{L}}\right)^{4}\ln\left(\frac{\lambda_{L}}{z_{0}}\right).

(244)

For

Hence, we may approximate

I_{3}(z_{0},\lambda_{L},\ell_{\Omega})\approx\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tilde{k}\,\frac{\tilde{k}^{3}}{(1+\tilde{k}^{2})^{2}\left[\left(\frac{\lambda_{L}}{\ell_{\Omega}}\right)^{4}+\tilde{k}^{4}\right]}=\frac{-1+\left(\frac{\lambda_{L}}{\ell_{\Omega}}\right)^{2}\left[\pi-\left(\frac{\lambda_{L}}{\ell_{\Omega}}\right)^{2}\right]+\left[\left(\frac{\lambda_{L}}{\ell_{\Omega}}\right)^{4}-1\right]\ln(\frac{\lambda_L}{\ell_\Omega})}{\left[1+\left(\frac{\lambda_{L}}{\ell_{\Omega}}\right)^{4}\right]^{2}}\approx\left(\frac{\ell_{\Omega}}{\lambda_{L}}\right)^{4}\ln\left(\frac{\lambda_{L}}{\ell_{\Omega}}\right).

(245)

Thus, we have derived all the scaling regimes presented in Table
1
in the main text.
