Evaluation of the effect of baseline predictors on the incidence of metabolic syndrome in middle-aged and elderly individuals: a population-based cohort study in Southwest Iran
Authors: Seyedeh Maryam Hashemi, Seyed Jalal Hashemi, Zahra Rahimi, Alireza Jahanshahi, Bahman Cheraghian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMetabolic syndrome (MetS) is a key predictor of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) and mortality, causing public health challenge worldwide. This study aimed to estimate the incidence rates and to assess the risk factors of MetS among adults in a prospective cohort study.MethodsThis population-based cohort study was conducted on 10,009 adult individuals in southwest Iran. After applying the exclusion criteria to the baseline cohort, 1,781 at-risk individuals were eligible and included for follow-up. Incidence rates (IRs) of metabolic syndrome were calculated by dividing the number of new events during the follow-up period by the person-years at risk. The effects of various explanatory variables on these incidence rates were assessed using Poisson regression models.ResultsThe overall incidence rate of metabolic syndrome (MetS) was 102.23 per 1,000 person-years, higher in females than males, and incidence was significantly associated with older age, female sex, marital status (married / divorced), higher BMI, history of cardiovascular disease, as well as abnormal FBS and elevated triglyceride levels (all p < 0.05).ConclusionTargeted public health strategies and lifestyle interventions are crucial for reducing the incidence of metabolic syndrome, particularly among high-risk groups such as women, older adults, and individuals with a history of cardiovascular disease.Keywords: Incidence, Metabolic syndrome, Cohort study, Iran

Introduction

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is defined by a combination of abdominal obesity, glucose intolerance, hypertriglyceridemia, low high-density lipoprotein (HDL), cholesterol, and hypertension [1, 2]. This condition is rapidly increasing worldwide, affecting both developed and developing countries [3, 4]. In Iran, over 30% of the population is estimated to be affected by MetS [5]. A baseline assessment within the Hoveyzeh Cohort Study (HCS) reported a prevalence of 39.1% [6].

A rapid increase in the incidence of MetS in the United States has been documented from 1980 to 2012, rising from 25% to 34.25% respectively, and a significant increase in incidence has also been reported in China [7]. Few studies have investigated the incidence of metabolic syndrome in Iran. A study conducted in Zahedan (southeastern Iran) indicated that the incidence of metabolic syndrome varied from 17.21% to 27.18%, depending on the criteria used [8]. A community-based study from central Iran, which enrolled individuals participating in the Yazd Healthy Heart Program (YHHP), estimated the incidence of MetS at 56.1 per 1,000 person-years for men and 58.7 per 1,000 person-years for women, using a modified version of the NCEP-ATP III criteria [9]. Another study conducted in Tehran reported an incidence of 55 per 1,000 person-years using the Joint Interim Statement criteria [10]. Additionally, metabolic syndrome imposes significant costs on the healthcare system and generally reduces the quality of life [11].

MetS is a cluster of conditions associated with abdominal obesity and increased risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) and mortality [12], type 2 diabetes [13], fatty liver disease, steatohepatitis and cirrhosis. Furthermore, MetS has been identified as a risk factor for a variety of disorders, including gastrointestinal, breast, and prostate cancers, polycystic ovary syndrome, and psychological conditions [14–17]. Additionally, MetS is a leading cause of mortality worldwide. Some studies have reported a twofold increase in overall mortality among patients with MetS compared to the control group [18].

Despite the significance of metabolic syndrome and its components in the incidence of common non-communicable diseases (NCDs), as well as the growing burden on the healthcare system, research on the incidence of this syndrome in Iran remains limited. Furthermore, since the factors contributing to metabolic syndrome can vary across different regions and demographic and socioeconomic groups, this study aims to estimate the 5-year incidence of metabolic syndrome and identify the factors influencing it in the Hoveyzeh cohort population.

Method

Study design and participants

The Hoveyzeh Cohort Study (HCS) is a population-based cohort study designed to assess NCDs in southwest Iran. During the enrollment phase of this prospective study, a total of 10,009 adults aged 35–70 years were recruited between May 2016 and August 2018 [19]. The re-assessment phases of this cohort aim to identify and document potential changes in the nature and extent of contact, exposures, and risk factors for NCDs among the study participants. These assessments are conducted at 5-year intervals following the enrollment phase. The first re-assessment phase was completed in 2022, during which a 30% representative sample of the original cohort participants was invited to the study center for comprehensive re-evaluation.

The re-evaluation study participants were systematically selected from the originally enrolled cohort through a multi-stage random sampling process. First, the initial cohort sample was stratified by urban and rural residence proportions. Subsequently, a systematic random sampling method was applied to select 3,019 individuals (from the total 10,009 enrolled participants) proportionally from both urban and rural participant lists.

The median follow-up period was 3.98 years. Following the exclusion of participants with baseline metabolic syndrome (n = 1,181), missing laboratory data (n = 8), pregnancy (n = 49), a final sample of 1,781 participants remained for the analysis (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of the study participants

The implementation process of the study during the re-assessment phase generally included registration, obtaining informed consent, recording the demographic information of the subjects, taking anthropometric measurements, collecting biological samples, and administering medical, general, and nutritional questionnaires. The supervisor and quality control officer were presented at the cohort center daily to continuously address any potential issues related to the study`s implementation and data collection.

Definition of metabolic syndrome (MetS)

The NCEP-ATP III criteria were utilized to diagnose MetS, which include: (1) abdominal obesity (waist circumference ≥ 102 cm in men and ≥ 88 cm in women), (2) elevated serum triglycerides (≥ 150 mg/dL) or the use of medications for hypertriglyceridemia, (3) low serum high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol (< 40 mg/dL in men and < 50 mg/dL in women) or the use of drug treatment for low HDL cholesterol, (4) high blood pressure (≥ 130/85 mmHg) or the use of antihypertensive medications, and (5) elevated fasting plasma glucose (FPG) (≥ 100 mg/dL) or the use of medications for hyperglycemia. A diagnosis of MetS is confirmed when three of the five criteria are identified (1).

Collection of biological samples

Participants in the study fasted for approximately10 to 12 h on the day of enrollment. Blood samples (15 mL) were obtained from each participant, consisting of a 9 mL EDTA tube and a 6 mL clot tube. After centrifugation of the clot tube, a serum sample (500 µL) was separated and transported to the laboratory for biochemical analysis. The laboratory procedures and quality control measures of the Hoveyzeh Cohort Study have been previously published [6].

Covariates

Demographic factors included sex (male/female), age groups (35–44, 45–54, 55–64, and 65–70 years), marital status (single, married, widowed, divorced), and education level (illiterate, primary school, secondary school, high school diploma, and university). Residential area was classified as urban or rural.

Physical activity was assessed using the Metabolic Equivalent Task (MET), with scores divided into quartiles (Q1-Q4). The wealth index was derived from household asset ownership (e.g., televisions, bicycles, cars, computers) and categorized into quintiles (poorest to richest). Body Mass Index (BMI) was classified as underweight, normal, overweight, and obese. Biochemical factors were assessed using fasting blood samples. Fasting Blood Sugar (FBS) was categorized as normal (< 126 mg/dL) or abnormal (≥ 126 mg/dL). Triglycerides (TG) were classified as normal (< 150 mg/dL) or abnormal (≥ 150 mg/dL). Low-Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol (LDL-C) was grouped as normal (< 160 mg/dL) or abnormal (≥ 160 mg/dL). High-Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol (HDL-C) was considered abnormal if < 40 mg/dL for men or < 50 mg/dL for women, and normal otherwise. Total Cholesterol was classified as normal (< 200 mg/dL) or abnormal (≥ 200 mg/dL). Smoking status and alcohol consumption were recorded as either yes or no. Sleep duration was categorized into ≤ 6, 7, 8, or ≥ 9 h. History of CVDs and a family history of diabetes were documented as yes/no.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated using the mean and standard deviation for quantitative variables, and incidence and percentage for categorical variables. Crude incidence rates (IRs) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) were calculated by dividing the number of metabolic syndrome events by the person-years at risk for the entire sample. Time at risk was defined as the number of years from the first visit in the baseline phase to the second visit in the reassessment phase for individuals who did not have metabolic syndrome. Generalized linear Poisson models were used to estimate the effects of explanatory variables on incidence rates. All reported p-values were derived from two-tailed tests and compared to a significance level of 0.05. All analyses were conducted using Stata software, version 15.

Results

A total of 1,781 individuals were evaluated. The mean age was 47.71 years (SD = 8.64; range: 35–70 years), with 907 (50.9%) being male. The median follow-up period was 3.98 years, with a total of 6,548.18 person-years of observation. During the follow-up period, 670 new cases of MetS were identified, resulting in an overall incidence rate of 102.23 per 1,000 person-years (95% CI: 94.72–110.37). This equates to an annual incidence of approximately 10.2% among at-risk participants.

Females had a higher incidence rate of MetS than males (122.86 vs. 83.66 per 1,000 person-years). Incidence generally increased with age, peaking in the 55–64 age group, before slightly decreasing in those aged ≥ 65 years. Widowed individuals had the highest incidence rate (140.69 per 1,000 person-years) compared to singles (49.68 per 1,000 person-years). Incidence rates decreased with higher education level, from 110.82 per 1,000 person-years among illiterate participants to 71.52 per 1,000 person-years among university graduates. Urban residents had a slightly higher incidence rate (102.99 per 1,000 person-years) than rural residents (101.19 per 1,000 person-years). Physical activity levels showed no consistent trend, although the highest activity quartile (Q4) had the lowest incidence (93.54 per 1,000 person-years). Wealth showed no clear pattern, although the highest rate was observed in the richest group (114.22 per 1,000 person-years). Non-smokers had a higher incidence rate than smokers (106.01 vs. 89.49 per 1,000 person-years). Alcohol consumption was associated with a lower incidence rate (92.14 per 1,000 person-years) than non-consumption (102.50 per 1,000 person-years), though the difference was not statistically significant. Sleep duration showed no clear association. Participants with a history of CVDs (134.72 per 1,000 person-years) and those with a family history of diabetes (113.53 per 1,000 person-years) had higher incidence rates than those without a family history of diabetes. In univariate Poisson regression, sex, age, marital status, education, BMI, FBS, history of CVDs, and family history of diabetes were significantly associated with MetS incidence (p < 0.05). However, area of residence, physical activity, wealth score, and lipid profiles (TG, LDL, HDL, and cholesterol), smoking, alcohol consumption, and sleep duration were not significantly associated (p > 0.05).

In multiple Poisson regression, variables with p < 0.25 in univariate analysis were included. Female sex remained a significant predictor (RR = 1.30, 95% CI: 1.05–1.61, p = 0.031). Participants aged 55–64 years had a higher incidence compared to the 35–39 age group, (RR = 1.37, 95% CI: 1.10–1.74, p = 0.006), while the difference for those aged ≥ 65 years was not significant (RR = 1.25, 95% CI: 0.82–1.92, p = 0.285). Married (RR = 1.83, 95% CI: 1.01–3.34), and divorced (RR = 2.30, 95% CI: 1.07–4.97) participants had higher risks than singles. Overweight (RR = 3.79, 95% CI: 1.40–10.21) and obesity (RR = 6.49, 95% CI: 2.40–17.54) were strongly associated with higher incidence. Biochemical parameters from the multivariable analysis revealed that fasting blood sugar (FBS) was significantly associated with the occurrence of metabolic syndrome. Participants with abnormal FBS had a higher risk of metabolic syndrome compared to those with normal FBS (adjusted RR: 1.77; 95% CI: 1.39–2.26; p < 0.001). Abnormal triglyceride (TG) levels were also significantly associated with an increased risk (adjusted RR: 1.27; 95% CI: 1.05–1.54; p = 0.012). In contrast, no significant associations were observed for total cholesterol, LDL, or HDL levels (p > 0.05). A history of CVDs (RR = 1.28, 95% CI: 1.03–1.61) was significant predictors (Table 2).

Discussion

The present study aimed to investigate the incidence of metabolic syndrome and its associated factors in the population aged 35 to 70 years participating in the Hoveyzeh Cohort Study (HCS). The incidence of MetS was found to be 102.23 per 1,000 person-years, which corresponds to an approximate annual incidence of 10.2% among at-risk participants. The findings indicated that age, gender, marital status, BMI, and history of CVDs were predictors for the incidence of MetS.

Additionally, our study demonstrated that abnormal FBS and elevated TG levels were the strongest predictors of metabolic syndrome, while LDL, total cholesterol, and HDL showed no significant associations after adjustment.

Our study indicated an overall incidence rate of 102.23 (95% CI: 94.72-110.37) per 1,000 person-years. In a study conducted in the southeast of Iran, the incidence rate of MetS was reported as 54.59 per 1,000 person-years [20]. Additionally, a systematic review estimated that the overall pooled incidence rate among the general population in Iran was 97.96 (95% CI: 75.98-131.48) per 1000 [21]. In a cohort study conducted in Tehran, the incidence rate was reported as 550.9/10,000 person-years. Several factors may explain this discrepancy. First, our participants had a higher mean age at baseline than those in most other Iranian studies, and age is a strong predictor of metabolic syndrome. Second, previous studies exhibited a high prevalence of risk factors, with over 75% reporting a sedentary lifestyle, more than 75% being overweight or obese [22], and over 90% having an abnormal WHR index [19]. Finally, differences in follow-up duration, diagnostic criteria of metabolic syndrome, and sampling methods may also have contributed to the observed variation.

The results showed that women were 26% more likely to develop MetS compared to men. In the baseline phase of the Hoveyzeh cohort, a higher risk of developing metabolic syndrome was observed in women compared to men. This finding was partly explained by lower levels of physical activity, more unfavorable lipid profiles, and larger waist circumferences among women in the sample [19]. Some previous studies supported our findings [23], while others had different results [24–26]. In a Portuguese cohort, the incidence rate was 47.2 per 1,000 person-years, with no significant gender difference [27]. The discrepancies seen in studies regarding the relationship between sex and the development of metabolic syndrome stem from a complex interplay of hormonal influences, variations in body composition, age-related factors, socioeconomic status, lifestyle choices like physical activity, and ethnic differences [28].

The results indicate that age is a significant risk factor for the development of metabolic syndrome. Other studies also suggest that age plays a crucial role in the development and progression of metabolic disorders. Aging is linked to a decrease in metabolic rate and changes in body composition, such as increased fat mass and decreased muscle mass. These changes contribute to insulin resistance and higher levels of inflammation, both of which are risk factors for MetS [29, 30]. Additionally, as individuals age, they tend to engage in less physical activity and make dietary changes. These can lead to enhanced weight and obesity, which in turn, increase the risk of developing MetS [29, 31]. Furthermore in women, specially post-menopausal women, hormonal changes play a significant role in the increased risk of MetS due to weight redistribution and enhanced fat accumulation around the abdomen [31].

Our results indicated that marital status was significantly associated with the incidence of metabolic syndrome, with divorced individuals showing the highest risk. Several mechanisms may explain this association. Marital transitions such as divorce can lead to psychosocial stress, reduced social support, and lifestyle changes, including poorer diet, decreased physical activity, and disrupted sleep patterns, all of which contribute to obesity, hypertension, and dysglycemia key components of metabolic syndrome. Additionally, divorced individuals may experience higher rates of depression and social isolation, which have been linked to metabolic and cardiovascular risk factors. Previous studies support these findings; for example, a cohort study in Tehran reported that marital status transitions were associated with increased risk of metabolic syndrome, particularly among women [32]. Similarly, a study in Korea found that divorced individuals had higher odds of developing metabolic syndrome compared to their married counterparts [33]. These observations underscore the importance of considering marital status as a social determinant of metabolic health in population-based studies.

The incidence of metabolic syndrome was significantly higher in patients with CVDs. Studies have shown that patients with CVDs are at greater risk of developing MetS due to several interconnected factors. These risk factors include hypertension [34], hyperglycemia (increased blood sugar) [35], dyslipidemia, and abdominal obesity [36], all of which are common in this population. Lifestyle factors such as a sedentary lifestyle, poor diet, and smoking worsen these risks [35, 36]. Additionally, age and gender play a role in this relationship, with older adults and postmenopausal women being more vulnerable [37, 38].

Elevated triglyceride (TG) levels were identified as major predictors of metabolic syndrome in our study. Similar to our results, several population-based studies have reported a strong and significant association between abnormal fasting blood sugar (FBS) and the risk of metabolic syndrome, highlighting impaired glucose regulation as a central component of the syndrome [39, 40]. In addition, the significant association we observed between abnormal TG levels and metabolic syndrome risk aligns with prior studies, which has frequently emphasized the role of hypertriglyceridemia in the development of metabolic and cardiovascular complications [41, 42]. In contrast, our study did not find significant associations between LDL, total cholesterol, or HDL levels and metabolic syndrome after adjustment for confounders. This finding is partly in agreement with some earlier studies, which have also reported inconsistent or nonsignificant associations between these lipid parameters and metabolic syndrome [43]. However, other investigations have demonstrated stronger relationships, particularly with low HDL levels [44]. These differences may be due to variations in population characteristics, lifestyle, and methodological approaches.

Limitations and strengths

Our study has several strengths. First, unlike previous studies that primarily examined the prevalence of metabolic syndrome, a cohort study design was employed in this study. This enabled us to observe the development of MetS over time and establish temporal relationships between risk factors and outcomes. Second, our study included large populations, increasing the statistical power of our results and reducing the possibility of random error. This led to more reliable incidence estimates. Third, the comprehensive data collection in the Hoveyzeh cohort study allowed us to examine associations between various health measures, lifestyle factors, and the full range of the association between multiple risk factors for metabolic syndrome. Fourth, we used standardized definitions for diagnosing metabolic syndrome and accurately measured its components using standard laboratory and anthropometric devices. This increased the comparability and validity of our findings across different studies. However, our study had limitations. First, the study population was limited to middle-aged and elderly individuals, which restricted the generalizability of the results. Second, the outcome was measured only in the re-assessment phase (approximately 5 years after the enrollment phase) and not during the follow-up period, so the exact timing of the outcome is not known.

Conclusion

The MetS incidence in the study population was approximately 10.2% per year, with higher rates in women, older adults, divorced individuals, those with lower education, and those with a history of cardiovascular disease. These findings underscore the need for targeted interventions focusing on high-risk groups to reduce the burden of MetS and its related complications.
