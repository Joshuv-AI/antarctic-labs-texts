Telomere-to-telomere gapless genome assembly of the giant grouper (Epinephelus lanceolatus)
Authors: Qian Zhou, Xuhui Liu, Yue Song, Ming Li, Guangyi Fan, Songlin Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe giant grouper (Epinephelus lanceolatus) is a large coral reef fish distributed in the Indian Ocean and the Pacific Ocean. With a high market value, this species can grow up to approximately 2.7 meters in length and weigh 440 kilograms. With the rapid development of bioinformatics, higher standards of genome analysis are now required compared to previous reference genomes. This study presents a gapless assembly of the giant grouper genome, which has a length of 1.03 Gb. The sequences were assembled onto 24 chromosomes with a coverage of over 99% (1.02 Gb), and telomeres were detected on 24 chromosomes. Analysis using Merqury indicated a high level of accuracy, with an average consensus quality value of 59.24. The ONT ultralong and PacBio HiFi data were aligned with the assembly using minimap2, resulting in a mapping rate of 99.9%. The study inferred 25,815 protein-coding genes. These results lay a foundation for exploring the evolution and biology of the giant grouper, and advancing molecular breeding techniques.Subject terms: Bioinformatics, Genomic analysis

Background & Summary

Groupers are a diverse group of commercially and ecologically important coral reef fishes found in tropical and subtropical waters worldwide. As currently understood, groupers comprise 165 species belonging to 16 genera, exhibiting significant variations in lifestyle, growth rate, and physical appearance1.The giant grouper is one of the most common artificially cultivated species, widely distributed in tropical and subtropical waters of the Pacific and Indian Oceans. The giant grouper is the largest grouper, reaching a maximum length of 270 centimetres. As a protogynous hermaphroditic species, all giant grouper individuals are born female and can later transform into males, undergoing 2–5 spawning seasons2. This kind of fish has a high commercial value because of its high nutritional value, delicious meat, and affordable price, and is favored by consumers. In recent years, decreasing diversity of giant groupers due to overfishing has clashed with rapidly increasing market demands3. Giant grouper, as the parent of hybrid grouper, occupies an important position in the large-scale culture of hybrid spot, which is gradually being cultured in the grouper industry due to its excellent traits and is the main grouper in the economic market. Despite the rapid growth of giant groupers, the scarcity of high-quality genetic resources severely hampers artificial breeding development. Previous research on giant groupers has primarily focused on immunology, cell line establishment, classification, and feed additives4–7. Revealing high-quality reference genomes can provide more insights and support advances in molecular biology, genetics, breeding, and conservation biology. To date, the genomes of several groupers, such as giant grouper (Epinephelus lanceolatus)4, Hong Kong Grouper (Epinephelus akaara)8 and Purple rockcod (Epinephelus cyanopodus)9 have been sequenced and assembled, however, they frequently contain substantial gaps, especially in repetitive regions, with telomeres and centromeres often lacking clear annotations. Long-read sequencing technologies like Pacific Biosciences’ HiFi and Oxford Nanopore Technologies’ ultra-long platforms offer a solution by utilizing longer reads to resolve complex chromosomal structures and assemble repetitive regions more effectively10. By employing diverse algorithms, we can strive to generate optimal assemblies. Recently, comprehensive T2T genomes devoid of gaps have been achieved in giant grouper through the integration of two state-of-the-art sequencing techniques and various assembly strategies11. A comprehensive T2T genome assembly without any gaps is crucial for enabling the detection and examination of all genomic variations12.

Here we combined Pacific Biosciences (PacBio) HiFi sequencing, Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) ultralong sequencing, and Hi-C technology to construct a high-fidelity T2T genome of the giant grouper. Our assembly represents a substantial enhancement over the two existing genome assemblies of this species, being nearly gap-free (Fig. 1). This T2T genome assembly will not only facilitate population genetic research and evolutionary analysis of giant grouper but also provide important resources for optimizing genetic breeding.

Methods

DNA sample collection, library construction, and sequencing

We extracted genomic DNA from the blood and muscle of a giant grouper (~2.1 kg, estimated age 2 years, female), provided by Laizhou Mingbo Aquatic Company. The PacBio HiFi sequencing technique included the construction of a standard SMRTbell library using the SMRTbell Express Template Prep Kit 2.0, following the prescribed guidelines from the manufacturer. Subsequently, the SMRTbell libraries underwent sequencing using a PacBio Sequel II system (Pacific Biosciences, CA, USA). For ONT ultralong sequencing13, high molecular weight genomic DNA (gDNA) was isolated from muscle tissue using a standard sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) extraction method for ONT ultralong sequencing. For PacBio HiFi sequencing, a Blood & Cell Culture DNA Kit (Qiagen 13323) was utilized to extract the gDNA. Tree methods were used for DNA quality and quantifcation testing, including (i) a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Termo Fisher Scientifc, USA), (ii) gel electrophoresis, and (iii) a Qubit fuorometer (Invitrogen, USA). Total DNA was purifed by AMPure PB beads (PacBio 100-265-900, USA). High-quality gDNA was prepared for the next step of library construction. A library was produced with the Oxford Nanopore SQK-ULK001 kit following the instructions provided by the manufacturer and then sequenced on a PromethION flow cell. As a result, 10.3 Gb (10×) of PacBio HiFi read data and 52.9 Gb (50×) of ONT ultralong read data were obtained.

Giant grouper Hic data were sourced from a previously published article4.

Genome assembly and telomere identification

With the ultralong ONT, PacBio HiFi, and Hi-C sequencing data described above, the contigs were assembled utilizing the initial values of Hifiasm(v0.19.8)14, Hi-C read assembly using single sample telomere-to-telomere in the Ultra-Long ONT Integration Module with parameters hifiasm -o -t32–ul–h1–h2 HiFi-reads.fq.gz. We obtained a gapless-level genome assembly of giant grouper, where the genome length was approximately 1.03 Gb and BUSCO analysis showed that 99.8% of the final assembly was complete BUSCOs15. (Table 1). The N50 was 44.38 Mb (Table 2). The 3D-DNA pipeline and Juicer-box16 (v2.17) were utilized to examine and visualize the interaction frequencies among different chromosomes. To assess the assembled telomere sequences in the giant grouper genome, we utilized the Telomere Identification quarTeT17 to identify occurrences of a 6 bp motif (TTAGGG) within the genome sequence. A total of 29 telomeres were identified, and telomeres were detected on both ends of 5 chromosomes (Fig. 1 and Table 3). We used blast comparison to compare the unmounted contig to the prokaryotic database with 0%, and to compare it to the previously published giant grouper genome with 99.99%. The samples were not contaminated and the data were reliable.

Repeat and noncoding RNA annotation

We utilized a combined approach involving de novo explorations and homologous alignments for the annotation of repeat elements. Homologue prediction was performed using RepeatMasker18 (v4.0.5) and RepeatProteinMask19 (v4.0.5) based on the Repbase library20 (v202101). Tandem Repeats Finder21 (v4.05) was utilized specifically for the detection of tandem repeats. RepeatModeler19 (v1.0.8) and LTR-Finder22 (v1.06) were employed for de novo prediction of repeat elements. The resultant predictions were merged to create a library utilized by RepeatMasker for the identification of repeat elements. The assembly results indicated that repeat sequences constituted approximately 50.25% of the genome. Among these repeats, long interspersed nuclear elements (LINEs), short interspersed nuclear elements (SINEs), and long terminal repeats (LTRs) accounted for 17.19%, 1.76%, and 8.73% of the genome, respectively (Fig. 2 and Table 4).

Noncoding RNAs, including rRNAs, snRNAs, miRNAs, and tRNAs, were identified by adopting INFERNAL (v1.1.2) through the Rfam database (release 13.0) for the giant grouper genome using BLASTN (E-value ≤ 1e−5)23–25. Transfer RNA was predicted using tRNAscan26 (v1.3.1) with default parameters for eukaryotes. Ribosome RNAs and their subunits were predicted using the RNAmmer27 (v1.2). For non-coding RNA annotation, a total of 824 miRNA, 3,094 tRNA, 3,043 rRNA, and 2,300 snRNA were identified (Table 5).

Gene prediction and annotation

Firstly, three strategies were used for gene structure prediction, including de novo prediction, homology-based, and RNA-seq data-based prediction. Augustus28 (v2.5.5), GlimmerHMM29 (v3.01), BGF30, Fgenesh31, and Genescan32, were used for de novo gene prediction with default settings. Protein sequences of giant grouper, black rockfish (Sebastes schlegelii), stickleback (Gasterosteus aculeatus), large yellow croaker (Larimichthys crocea), grass carp (Ctenopharyngodon idella), Japanese flounder (Paralichthys olivaceus), and red-spotted grouper were downloaded from Ensembl and NCBI databases. These sequences were aligned to the giant grouper genome with TBLASTN (E-value ≤ 10−5), and homologous genome sequences were then aligned against matching proteins by GeneWise33 (v2.4.0) to generate a gene structure based on the alignment. Furthermore, the RNA-seq data from different embryonic development stages were assembled using HISAT234 (v2.1.0) and mapped to the giant grouper genome by using the StringTie35 (v2.1.4). Gene prediction results from the methods above was merged to a consensus gene set using GeMoMa36 (v1.1.7). The functional annotation of the predicted genes of giant grouper was performed by alignment to the SwissProt37, NR38, KEGG39, Interpro40, GO41. A total of 25,815 protein-coding genes were predicted (Table 6), of which 25,222 (97.7%) genes were annotated (Fig. 3).

Technical Validation

To assess the completeness of the giant grouper genome assembly, we utilized BUSCO14 (v0.2.6) with the Actinopterygii database (actinopterygii_odb10) to identify conserved single-copy genes in the assembly. Of the 3,640 conserved genes searched, an impressive 99.75% were, identified as complete, indicating a high level of gene content preservation. Among these, 99.45% were both complete and present as single-copy genes, further emphasizing the quality of the assembly. Additionally, only 0.03% were fragmented, and 0.03% were missing from the assembly (Table 1). To ensure the quality and accuracy of the giant grouper assembly, we employed a two-step validation process. First, the assembly quality value (QV) was quantified using Merqury47 (v1.4.1), resulting in a QV score of 59.24, reflecting a high-quality assembly. Subsequently, we aligned the raw sequencing data to the assembly using minimap248 (v2.28). For PacBio HiFi and ONT ultralong sequencing, this alignment approach achieved mapping rates of 99.99% and 99.99%, respectively.
