Full-stack Physics-level model of cascaded entanglement links
J. Gabriel Richardson, Prajit Dhara, Abhishek Bhatt, Saikat Guha, Stefan Krastanov

Abstract
While the last few decades have seen a proliferation of experimental demonstrations of entanglement sources, practicality of deployment has been a secondary concern. Recently, the ZALM source Chen et al. 2023 was introduced, as a well-engineered functional device, easily integrated within a complete networking system. It addresses numerous concerns which make typical academic demonstrations less practical: reliable heralding signals, multiplexing across multiple dimensions, and efficient use of input power. We present a stack of tools for modeling mode-by-mode a ZALM source under realistic conditions, in isolation or as a part of a complete network testbed. Our modeling formalism builds upon a hybrid Gaussian and non-Gaussian representation, providing a flexible tradeoff between performance and accuracy, while also greatly simplifying the exact calculation of otherwise expensive scalar figures of merit. This toolkit, implemented in the python package called “genqo” , is integrated within the QuantumSavory full-stack simulator and the QuantumSymbolics computer algebra system. We use this software stack to demonstrate a number of complete networking protocols built upon the ZALM source.



I
Introduction

The gasoline to the engine of quantum networks is entanglement, particularly photonic entanglement that can be transmitted through existing optical fiber infrastructure or via free-space satellite links. This entanglement, generated by a source, may be used in its photonic format, but for many quantum computing and sensing applications it will be necessary to transduce it to some form of quantum memory, a qubit capable of universal quantum logic with enough coherence time for the specific application. The applications, such as distributed quantum computing
Fitzsimons 2017
;
Wehner et al. 2018
, entanglement enhanced long-baseline interferometry
Gottesman et al. 2012
;
Khabiboulline et al. 2019a
;
Khabiboulline et al. 2019b
, or entangled sensor networks
Van Milligen et al. 2024
;
Zhang and Zhuang 2021
are hungry for entanglement as distillation protocols generate entangled states in their most useful and pure form by combining received noisy Bell pairs. As such, it is necessary for entanglement sources to produce this resource at high rates and with high quality or fidelity.

Previous proposals for entanglement sources have a number of bottlenecks. Their fundamental mechanism for generating entanglement is via optical nonlinear processes, such as spontaneous parametric down conversion (SPDC). Colloquially called
“SPDC sources”

Kwiat et al. 1995
;
Kwiat et al. 1999
, these sources use engineered nonlinear optical crystals which are pumped by pulsed laser systems. The result is that with some probability, a single pair of entangled photons will be produced. The source does not know when these pairs are generated, and without quantum memories recognizing the previous entangled photon pairs amongst the noise is akin to finding a needle in a hay stack. Thus, ultimate entanglement generation rates of these sources are limited, and even in the presence of frequency multiplexing they suffer from a large resource overhead.

Thus, a different concept emerges: the cascaded-heralded entanglement source
Dhara et al. 2022
. This source is realized by utilizing two SPDC sources and performing a heralding Bell state measurement between two modes from each source. In this configuration, the cascaded source overcomes the challenge of the receiver not knowing when an entangled pair is produced. But, this heralding will reduce the rate of entanglement produced. Therefore, an extension of the cascaded source, called the zero-added loss multiplexing (ZALM) source
Chen et al. 2023
, proposes using frequency multiplexing to produce entangled photon pairs quasi-deterministically over some frequency band. Together with frequency mode conversion, this source overcomes the bottlenecks of other entanglement source proposals, drafting a blue print for one of the most important operating technologies of tomorrow’s quantum networks. Figure
1
shows from the perspective of operations on the optical modes how the cascaded (a.k.a. ZALM) source is constructed from two SPDC sources.

Figure 1:
A diagram showing the construction of both the SPDC source and the cascaded/ZALM source in terms of operations on optical modes. TMSV refers to two-mode squeezed vacuum state, SPDC refers to the SPDC source mentioned in the text. We give arbitrary indices to the modes, for referencing in the main text, however we do not impose any specific physical implementation of the source. The heralding mechanism achieved by the cascaded source via an entanglement swap between half of the modes from two SPDC sources is essential for ZALM operation. This is because it enables frequency multiplexing without introducing a large quantum memory overhead, as would be required for frequency multiplexing unheralded SPDC source. Cascading also enables source operation in a higher mean photon number regime as compared to the SPDC source, since the heralding mechanism essentially filters undesirable higher-photon-order terms.

The development and engineering of entangled photonic qubit sources based on ZALM is an active area of research with practical engineering challenges that need to be solved for practical implementations over realistic network links. Initial analyses of ZALM sources were limited in that they truncated the quantum state using a low mean photon number approximation
Shapiro et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2023
, thus limiting analysis of source performance to the regime where the pump laser power is low. A technique for overcoming this approximation by using a hybrid Gaussian/non-Gaussian source model was proposed in an article about cascaded sources
Dhara et al. 2022
; however, this technique was not pursued. A similar approach was recently used to analyze a ZALM source that utilizes phase-matched spectral islands
Shapiro et al. 2025
, providing further evidence to support the near-term realization of a ZALM source with entanglement rates necessary for high-demand applications.

In this paper, we present a detailed mathematical approach to modeling entanglement sources based on photonic nonlinearities. We analyze the SPDC source and the cascaded/ZALM source using this hybrid Gaussian/non-Gaussian approach, thereby removing problematic inaccuracies that limited the parameter regimes studied in previous work
Shapiro et al. 2024
. We include formalism for calculating both the photon-photon state of the source, in the presence of non-idealities such as loss and detector excess noise, as well as the spin-spin state, in the presence of the same non-idealities, following loading into idealized Duan-Kimble
Duan and Kimble 2004
style quantum memories. We also present an open-source toolbox,
“genqo”
, which implements this formalism in a Python modeling package.

The more accurate models presented here, uncover parameter regimes of interest, providing an opportunity for much higher overall rate and fidelity of distributing entangled pairs. As shown in this paper, at higher pump intensities, we have access to higher success probabilities that previous approximate models did not predict.

Next-generation quantum networks will not exist without high rates of entanglement generation, and ZALM is a proposed leading solution to this challenge. Our tools presented herein can enable ZALM architects to properly understand the source and its components in the context the system.

This paper is structured as follows. In
“Physical Principles”
, we give a schematic description of the entanglement source under consideration, we reiterate its advantages, and we present the experimental parameters that define the source and the critical figures of merit to evaluate. In
“Mathematical Principles”
, we provide a review of all of the techniques necessary for the successful high-fidelity modeling of the entanglement source. Then we apply these techniques to derive the aforementioned figures of merit. References to software implementations of each of these concepts (within the genqo framework) are provided. Then, in the
“Simulation Module”
, we present a few direct use cases of the genqo framework and demonstrate how to employ it for the study of entanglement sources. In the same section, we follow with a discussion and examples of how the genqo framework can be used from within the full-stack networking simulator QuantumSavory.jl or the computer algebra system QuantumSymbolics.jl.

II
Physical Principles

Spontaneous parametric down conversion (SPDC) is a nonlinear optical process that takes an input photon (colloquially called the pump) and produces two output photons (colloquially called the signal and idler)
Boyd 2003
. The photons satisfy conservation of energy and momentum by the relationships
Ou 2007

\displaystyle\omega_{P}=\omega_{S}+\omega_{I}

(1)

\displaystyle\mathbf{k}_{p}=\mathbf{k}_{S}+\mathbf{k}_{I}

(2)

We consider the action of a two-mode squeezing Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\rm TMS}
generated by a spontaneous parametric down conversion (SPDC) process on the signal and idler fields (labeled by the subscripts
S
and
I
)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\rm TMS}(\xi)=\iint\,d\omega_{S}\,d\omega_{I}\left(\xi\Psi(\omega_{S},\omega_{I})\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{S}(\omega_{S})\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{I}(\omega_{I})-\xi^{*}\Psi(\omega_{S},\omega_{I})^{*}\hat{a}_{S}(\omega_{S})\hat{a}_{I}(\omega_{I})\right)

(3)

where
\xi
governs the strength of the two-mode interaction, and
\Psi(\omega_{S},\omega_{I})
is the joint-spectral amplitude (JSA) function that governs the spectral correlations in the signal and idler beams.

Starting from the Hamiltonian for a down conversion process in terms of classical fields followed by quantizing the electric field
Grice and Walmsley 1997
, the joint spectral amplitude (JSA)
\Psi(\omega_{S},\omega_{I})
arises as a function of the properties of the pump field as well as the nonlinear crystal. It represents the probability amplitude of generating two photons; one at
\omega_{S}
and one at
\omega_{I}
, such that

\displaystyle\Psi(\omega_{S},\omega_{I})=\alpha(\omega_{S}+\omega_{I})\Phi(\omega_{S},\omega_{I})

(4)

where
\alpha(\omega_{P})
is called the spectral mode shape function, which the ZALM paper
Chen et al. 2023
considers as Gaussian having the form
\alpha(\omega_{P})=\exp(-(\omega_{P}-\omega_{P0})^{2}/2\sigma^{2}_{P})
where
\omega_{P0}
is the pump center frequency and
\sigma_{P}
is the pump linewidth;
\Phi(\omega_{S},\omega_{I})
is called the Phase Matching Function, which is dependent upon the physical characteristics of the nonlinear crystal. This description provides
“knobs”
for engineering the properties of the photons produced by the down conversion process, either by changing the characteristics of the pump or of the crystal. These
“knobs”
have been considered in previous studies, such as by Shapiro et al
Shapiro et al. 2024
.

In this paper, we present in full details the process of modeling individual modes of each of these sources. To model multimode attributes, such as those that occur as a result of the JSA and its corresponding filtering/modesorting mechanism, is an extension of the single mode analysis. We describe the details of this multimode analysis in our sister paper
Richardson et al. 2025
.

The SPDC process occurs in different types (0,I,II) depending on the group indices of the signal and idler photons. The frequency of the produced photons are either degenerate (the signal and idler have the same frequency) or non-degenerate (the signal and idler do not have the same frequency)
Schneeloch et al. 2019
. The quantum state produced by a non-degenerate spontaenous parametric down conversion is the two mode squeezed vacuum (TMSV)
Lvovsky 2016
, represented in the Fock basis is
Schumaker and Caves 1985

\displaystyle S(r,\theta)\ket{0}=(\cosh{r})^{-1}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-e^{2i\phi}\tanh{r})^{n}\ket{n}\ket{n}

(5)

Where
r
is a real number called the squeezing factor, and
\phi
is a phase angle. The
mean photon number per mode
,
N_{s}
is related to
r
by
N_{s}=\sinh(r)^{2}

Lvovsky 2016
. Since
\cosh^{2}(r)-\sinh^{2}(r)=1
,
\cosh(r)=\sqrt{1+\sinh^{2}(r)}=\sqrt{1+N_{s}}
. Also,
\tanh(r)=\frac{\sinh(r)}{\cosh(r)}=\sqrt{\frac{N_{s}}{1+N_{s}}}
. So, the two mode squeezed vacuum state can be written as

\displaystyle\ket{\text{TMSV}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+N_{s}}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(\frac{N_{s}}{1+N_{s}})^{n/2}\ket{n}\ket{n}

(6)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\frac{N_{s}^{n}}{(1+N_{s})^{n+1}}}\ket{n}\ket{n}

(7)

An SPDC source is constructed from two SPDC processes, or mathematically two, two-mode squeezed vacuum states with an idler mode swap. The first source of polarization entangled photons pairs was created using two non-linear optical crystals, rotated
90\deg
with respect to each other
Kwiat et al. 1995
;
Kwiat et al. 1999
. Today, a commonly used configuration of this source is the Sagnac configuration
Kim et al. 2006
;
Wong et al. 2006
.

II.0.1
The Heralding Process

As mentioned previously, the cascaded (i.e., ZALM) source is constructed from two SPDC sources, where a Bell state measurement is performed on a pair of modes (one from each SPDC source) to herald the generation of dual-rail entangled photons. The dual-rail Bell state which we seek to produce from these sources has the form
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{1,0;0,1}\pm\ket{0,1;1,0})
where the bases of the state might be two spatial modes, each with a horizontal or vertical polarizaiton (as is the case in the Sagnac configuration). The state produced by the SPDC source will have a vacuum component, as well as multi-photon pair terms
Dhara et al. 2022
. These multi-photon pair terms are generally not useful for the receivers, and are generally considered failure. Using the Bell state measurement, which in the dual-rail case would require click patterns where one horizontally polarized photon is measured and one vertically polarized photon is measured after beam splitter operations between the respective modes, we are able to essentially filter the multi-photon pair terms. The resulting state of the cascaded source is either the Bell state portion (as desired) or two photon terms
\ket{1,1;0,0}
or
\ket{0,0;1,1}
where both photons arrive at one receiver. This state is much more straightforward from a utility perspective, particularly in the presence of quantum memories.

Figure
1
shows the process of constructing the source from the perspective of operations on the optical modes. We begin with four TMSV states. An idler mode swap occurs between pairs of TMSVs, followed by a Bell state measurement between two modes from each resulting SPDC source which is accomplished by interfering the corresponding mode from that of the other source, and single photon detection.

II.0.2
Using Quantum Memories

Duan-Kimble style quantum memories
Duan and Kimble 2004
realize the operation of tranducing a dual-rail qubit into some atomic qubit basis state deterministically, such that

\displaystyle\alpha\ket{1,0}+\beta\ket{0,1}\rightarrow\alpha\ket{\mathbf{0}}+\beta\ket{\mathbf{1}}

(8)

Where
\ket{\mathbf{0/1}}
are the basis states of the atomic qubit. This is realized by: (1) a photon reflection off of the atom-cavity system, such that a phase is aquired if the photon is in one optical mode, but not in the other. In quantum computing terms, this is a CZ operation; (2) Interference of the two optical modes via a beam splitter; (3) single-photon detection in one of the optical modes. Using quantum memories, we can in theory herald the reception of a dual-rail Bell state from the entanglement source, and filter out the situations where two photons arrived at one receiver and not at the other.

II.0.3
Definition of Performance Metrics

In our modeling presented in this paper, we consider two primary performance metrics: (1)
P_{gen}
, the probability of generation, which for the cascaded source case is the probability that a successful Bell state measurement occurs. For the SPDC source, the probability of generation might be calculated in the presence of memories, and would be the probability that a Bell state is successfully loaded onto the quantum memories. The probability of generation is calculated simply by the trace of the un-normalized quantum state of the source in the specific instance of interest. (2)
F
, The fidelity which we assume is the Bell state fidelity, which mathematically is

\displaystyle F=\frac{\bra{\xi}\rho\ket{\xi}}{\text{Tr}(\rho)}

(9)

Where
\ket{\xi}
is the Bell state of interest. The Bell state produced by the cascaded source will be dependent upon the heralding click pattern. For example, if the source heralds the pattern
\ket{1,1,0,0}_{3,4,5,6}
(in the mode convention of figure
1
) then the Bell state produced is the
\ket{\psi^{-}}
state.

II.0.4
Physical Modeling Parameters

The central parameter of interest for modeling entanglement sources is the mean photon number per mode,
N_{s}
, which is described in the previous subsection in terms of how it relates to parameters such as the squeezing strengths.

We consider some physical non-idealities, including loss in the heralding process
\eta_{b}
, loss in the transmission of the state
\eta_{t}
, and detector specific loss
\eta_{d}
. We can also consider dark click probability,
P_{d}
, which is the probability that our photon detector registers a click despite a photon from our source not actually being present. Dark click probability can be used to model generic detector inefficiencies, as well as background noise.

Covariance Matrix of the Two Mode Squeezed Vacuum State

The nonlinear process of parametric down conversion is mathematically equivalent to the two mode squeezed vacuum (TMSV) state

III.1.3
Initial Covariance Matrix of the Cascaded/ZALM Source

The single-mode cascaded source and the single-mode ZALM source are equivalent, and hence the formulation of the covariance matrices in this section apply for both cases. \displaystyle V_{casc,i}=P_{3}(V_{spdc}\oplus V_{spdc})P_{3}^{T}

III.1.4
Bell State Measurement

To understand how these beam splitter operations will occur, consider for example the SPDC sources in the sagnac configuration. This means that the 8 modes of

\ket{H_{1a},V_{1a},H_{2a},V_{2a},H_{1b},V_{1b}H_{2b},V_{2b}}

and

III.2
From Gaussian to Density Matrix

In this section we describe the process of transforming a state represented as a covariance matrix into a density matrix representation via the K-function formalism.

III.2.1
K
-Function Formalism

These concepts are implemented in genqo through
calculate_k_function_matrix
which takes the covariance matrix and provides the K-function matrix, an intermediary step to the computation of the density matrix.

The K-function formalism
Gagatsos and Guha 2019
translates a covariance matrix into a density matrix in the coherent state basis. This translation allows non-Gaussian operations to be applied to the state.

Consider
N
-bosonic modes with canonical position and momentum operators
\hat{q}_{k},\hat{p}_{k};\;k\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}
. Define
N
-mode coherent states as
\ket{\vec{\alpha}}=\ket{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{N}};\;\alpha_{k}\in\mathbb{C}
. Each coherent amplitude can be expressed in terms of the canonical quadrature operators
\alpha_{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(q_{k}+ip_{k})
, i.e. the real and imaginary parts of
\alpha_{k}
correspond to displacements along the position and momentum quadratures of the
k
-th mode. An
N
-mode pure quantum state
\ket{\psi}
in
K
-function representation (i.e. projection on the coherent state resolution of the identity operator) is

\displaystyle\begin{split}\ket{\psi}&=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{N}}\int d^{2N}\vec{x}_{\alpha}\ket{\vec{\alpha}}\!\!\braket{\vec{\alpha}|\psi}=\int d^{2N}\vec{x}_{\alpha}K(\vec{x}_{\alpha})\ket{\vec{\alpha}}\end{split}

(41)

where
K(\vec{x}_{\alpha})=(2\pi)^{-N}\braket{\vec{\alpha}|\psi}
. If
\ket{\psi}
is a zero mean
N
-mode Gaussian state (i.e. the state is completely specified by a covariance matrix
V
), then
K(\vec{x_{\alpha}})
can be expressed as,

\displaystyle K(\vec{x}_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp{\left[-\frac{1}{2}\vec{x}_{\alpha}^{T}\mathcal{B}\vec{x}_{\alpha}\right]}}{(2\pi)^{N}(\det{\Gamma})^{1/4}},

(42)

where,

\displaystyle\Gamma

\displaystyle=V+I/2

(43a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}A+\frac{i}{2}(C+C^{T})&C-\frac{i}{2}(A-B)\\
C^{T}-\frac{i}{2}(A-B)&B-\frac{i}{2}(C+C^{T})\end{pmatrix}

(43b)

\displaystyle\vec{x}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=(\vec{q}_{\alpha},\vec{p}_{\alpha})=(q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{N},p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{N})

(43c)

\displaystyle\Gamma^{-1}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}A&C\\
C^{T}&B\end{pmatrix}

(43d)

III.2.2
Incorporating Loss

Loss can be incorporated into the quantum state in the K-function formalism using Kraus operators (
A_{k}
)
(
Dhara et al. 2022
)
, where

\displaystyle\hat{A_{k}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{(1-\eta)^{k}}{k!}}\sqrt{\eta}^{\hat{n}}\hat{a}^{k}

(44)

\displaystyle\hat{A_{k}}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{(1-\eta)^{k}}{k!}}(\hat{a}^{k})^{\dagger}\sqrt{\eta}^{\hat{n}}

(45)

These acts on coherent basis terms
\ket{\gamma}\!\!\bra{\delta}
, where
\gamma,\delta\in\mathbb{C}
as

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\hat{A_{k}}\ket{\gamma}\!\!\bra{\delta}\hat{A_{k}}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\sum\frac{(1-\eta)^{k}}{k!}\sqrt{\eta}^{\hat{n}}\hat{a}^{k}\ket{\gamma}\!\!\bra{\delta}\hat{a}^{\dagger k}\sqrt{\eta}^{\hat{n}}

\displaystyle=\sum\frac{(1-\eta)^{k}}{k!}(\gamma\delta^{*})^{k}

\displaystyle\exp{\left(-\frac{(|\gamma|^{2})+|\delta|^{2}))(1-\eta)}{2}\right)}\ket{\gamma\sqrt{\eta}}\bra{\delta\sqrt{\eta}}

\displaystyle=\exp{\left((\gamma\delta^{*})(1-\eta)-\frac{(|\gamma|^{2})+|\delta|^{2}))(1-\eta)}{2}\right)}

\displaystyle\ket{\gamma\sqrt{\eta}}\bra{\delta\sqrt{\eta}}

(46)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{G}(\gamma,\delta)\ket{\gamma\sqrt{\eta}}\bra{\delta\sqrt{\eta}}

(47)

Recalling that
\exp{(x)}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{x^{k}}/{k!}

III.3.3
Derivation of Wick’s Theorem

Consider the complex gaussian integral, which states that

\displaystyle\int d(\bar{z},z)e^{-\bar{z}wz}=\frac{\pi}{w}

(71)

Where
z=x+iy
,
\bar{z}
is the complex conjugate of of
z
,
\Re(w)>0
and
\int d(\bar{z},z)\equiv\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,dy
. This can be generalized to more than one dimension, by defining an
N
-component vector

\displaystyle\int d(\mathbf{v}^{\dagger},\mathbf{v})e^{-\mathbf{v}^{\dagger}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}},

(72)

where
\mathbf{A}
is a complex Hermitian matrix. To solve this integral, consider that
\mathbf{A}
can be diagonalized as
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{O}^{T}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{O}
. We can now define
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{O}\mathbf{v}
so that the integral becomes

\displaystyle\int d(\mathbf{u}^{\dagger},\mathbf{u})e^{-\mathbf{u}^{\dagger}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{u}}

(73)

Because
D
is diagonal and
\det(\mathbf{O})=1
this simplifies into a factor of complex gaussian integrals, such that

\displaystyle\int d(\mathbf{v}^{\dagger},\mathbf{v})e^{-\mathbf{v}^{\dagger}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}}=\frac{\pi^{N}}{\prod_{i=1}^{N}d_{i}}=\frac{\pi^{N}}{\det(\mathbf{D})}=\frac{\pi^{N}}{\det(\mathbf{A})}

(74)

This can be further generalized, such that

\displaystyle\int d(\mathbf{v}^{\dagger},\mathbf{v})e^{-\mathbf{v}^{\dagger}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{w}^{\dagger}\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{v}^{\dagger}\mathbf{w}^{\prime}}=\frac{\pi^{N}}{\det(\mathbf{A})}e^{\mathbf{w}^{\dagger}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{w}^{\prime}}

(75)

Where
\mathbf{A}^{-1}
is the inverse of the A matrix and
\mathbf{w}
and
\mathbf{w}^{\prime}
are independent complex vectors. This identity is resolved such that making the change of variables

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{w}^{\prime}

(76)

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}^{\dagger}+\mathbf{w}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{A}^{-1})^{\dagger}

(77)

If
\mathbf{A}^{-1}
is hermitian, then
(\mathbf{A}^{-1})^{\dagger}=\mathbf{A}^{-1}
, therefore

\displaystyle-\mathbf{v}^{\dagger}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{w}^{\dagger}\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{v}^{\dagger}\mathbf{w}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-(\mathbf{u}^{\dagger}+\mathbf{w}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{A}^{-1})^{\dagger})\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{w}^{\prime})+\mathbf{w}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{w}^{\prime})+(\mathbf{u}^{\dagger}+\mathbf{w}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{A}^{-1})^{\dagger})\mathbf{w}^{\prime}

(78)

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{u}^{\dagger}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}^{\dagger}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{w}^{\prime}-\mathbf{w}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{A}^{-1})^{\dagger}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{w}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{A}^{-1})^{\dagger}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{w}^{\prime}

\displaystyle+\mathbf{w}^{\dagger}\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{w}^{\dagger}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{w}^{\prime}+\mathbf{u}^{\dagger}\mathbf{w}^{\prime}+\mathbf{w}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{A}^{-1})^{\dagger}\mathbf{w}^{\prime}

(79)

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{u}^{\dagger}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}^{\dagger}\mathbf{w}^{\prime}-\mathbf{w}^{\dagger}\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{w}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{A}^{-1})^{\dagger}\mathbf{w}^{\prime}+\mathbf{w}^{\dagger}\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{w}^{\dagger}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{w}^{\prime}+\mathbf{u}^{\dagger}\mathbf{w}^{\prime}+\mathbf{w}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{A}^{-1})^{\dagger}\mathbf{w}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{u}^{\dagger}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{w}^{\dagger}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{w}^{\prime}

(80)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\int d(\mathbf{v}^{\dagger},\mathbf{v})e^{-\mathbf{v}^{\dagger}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{w}^{\dagger}\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{v}^{\dagger}\mathbf{w}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\int d(\mathbf{u}^{\dagger},\mathbf{u})e^{-\mathbf{u}^{\dagger}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{w}^{\prime\dagger}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{w}^{\prime}}

(81)

\displaystyle=\int d(\mathbf{u}^{\dagger},\mathbf{u})e^{-\mathbf{u}^{\dagger}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u}}e^{\mathbf{w}^{\prime\dagger}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{w}^{\prime}}

(82)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi^{N}}{\det(\mathbf{A})}e^{\mathbf{w}^{\dagger}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{w}^{\prime}}

(83)

The proof of this can be found here
Palkanoglou 2018
. Consider that

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial w_{m}}\frac{\partial}{\partial w^{\prime}_{n}}\int d(\mathbf{v}^{\dagger},\mathbf{v})e^{-\mathbf{v}^{\dagger}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{w}^{\dagger}\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{v}^{\dagger}\mathbf{w}^{\prime}}\right|_{\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{w}^{\prime}=0}

\displaystyle=\int d(\mathbf{v}^{\dagger},\mathbf{v})e^{-\mathbf{v}^{\dagger}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}}v_{m}\bar{v_{n}}

(84)

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{\pi^{N}}{\det(\mathbf{A})}e^{\mathbf{w}^{\dagger}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{w}^{\prime}}\right|_{\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{w}^{\prime}=0}

(85)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi^{N}}{\det(\mathbf{A})}A^{-1}_{mn}

(86)

These results are connected to the Gaussian moment, and explicitly

\displaystyle\langle v_{m}\bar{v_{n}}\rangle=A^{-1}_{mn}=\frac{\det(\mathbf{A})}{\pi^{N}}\int d(\mathbf{v}^{\dagger},\mathbf{v})e^{-\mathbf{v}^{\dagger}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}}v_{m}\bar{v_{n}}

(87)

Taking more partial derivatives we can calculate higher order moments, and by the chain rule we will get that

\displaystyle\langle\bar{v_{i_{1}}}\bar{v_{i_{2}}}...\bar{v_{i_{n}}}v_{j_{1}}v_{j_{2}}...v_{j_{n}}\rangle=\sum_{P}A^{-1}_{j_{1},i_{P1}}...A^{-1}_{j_{n},i_{Pn}}

(88)

Where
\sum_{P}
represents the sum over all permutations of
n
integers. This property is called Wick Coupling, and this formalism for calculating higher-order Gaussian moments is generally called Wick’s theorem, a technique most commonly used in quantum field theory
zee 2010
;
Zinn-Justin 2021
;
Altland and Simons 2010
.

Consider how things change slightly in the real case. Consider the Gaussian integral

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dxe^{-\frac{a}{2}x^{2}}=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{a}}

(89)

Where
\Re[{a}]>0
. This can be extended to multiple dimensions, considering
\int d\mathbf{v}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{v}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}}
where
\mathbf{A}
is a positive definite, real, symmetric,
N
-dimensional matrix, and
\mathbf{v}
is an
N
-component real vector. Because
\mathbf{A}
is symmetric, it can be diagonalized by orthogonal transformation,
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{O}^{T}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{O}
where the
\mathbf{O}
matrix is orthogonal and all elements of the diagonal matrix
\mathbf{D}
are positive. Absorbing
\mathbf{O}
in to the vector
\mathbf{v}
the Gaussian integral becomes diagonal, and is just a sequence of Gaussian integrals, therefore

\displaystyle\int d\mathbf{v}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{v}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}}=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{d_{i}}}=\sqrt{\frac{(2\pi)^{N}}{\det{\mathbf{D}}}}=\sqrt{\frac{(2\pi)^{N}}{\det{\mathbf{A}}}}

(90)

The Wick coupling will also change slightly. Following the same procedure as in the complex case, we find that

\displaystyle\langle x_{1}...x_{n}\rangle=\sum_{\text{all possible pairings P of }\{1,...,n\}}\langle x_{P_{1}}x_{P_{2}}\rangle...\langle x_{P_{n-1}}x_{P_{n}}\rangle

(91)

III.3.4
Application to hybrid Gaussian/Non-Gaussian modeling

For the purposes of doing hybrid Gaussian/Non-Gaussian modeling, we will need to solve integrals of the form

\displaystyle\int d^{4N}\vec{\mathbf{x}}Ce^{-\frac{1}{2}\vec{\mathbf{x}}^{T}\mathbf{A}\vec{\mathbf{x}}}

(92)

where
N
is the number of modes in a system,
\mathbf{x}
is a vector of all of the quadrature moment operators such that

becomes

\displaystyle\int d^{4N}\mathbf{x}Ce^{-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{x}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}}=\sqrt{\frac{(2\pi)^{4N}}{\det{\mathbf{A}}}}\mathbf{W}(A,C,\vec{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{(2\pi)^{2N}}{\sqrt{\det{\mathbf{A}}}}\mathbf{W}(A,C,\vec{x})

(93)

Where
\mathbf{W}(A,C,\vec{x})
is the wick coupling of
C=(x_{i}x_{j}...x_{l})
as defined for the real case (since we will operate in the phase-space basis), which is that

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}(A,C,\vec{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\text{all possible pairings P of }\{1,...,n\}}\langle x_{P_{1}}x_{P_{2}}\rangle...\langle x_{P_{n-1}}x_{P_{n}}\rangle

(94)

The W function is available in genqo under the routine
W
, which ingests the A matrix and the symbolic vector of quadrature variables, which selects which of the terms in the Gaussian moment integral is being considered.

III.3.5
Alternative method: Calculation via the Hafnian

The hafnian of an
nxn
symetric matrix
A=A^{T}
is defined as

\displaystyle\text{haf}(A)=\sum_{M\in\text{PMP}(n)}\prod_{(i,j)\in M}A_{i,j}

(95)

Where PMP
(n)
stands for the set of perfect matching permutations of n (even) objects. For example, for
n=4
,

\displaystyle\text{PMP}(4)=\{(0,1)(2,3),(0,2)(1,3),(0,3)(1,2)\}

(96)

In our notation and in terms of Wick’s theorem, the Hafnian calculation for
A
is

\displaystyle\text{haf}(A)=\mathbf{W}(A,\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i},\vec{x})

(97)

In other words, it is the Wick coupling calculation over all of the elements of the basis vector. In the next section, we will provide a specific example for how this calculation is performed by taking sub-matrices of the case-specific
\mathbf{A}
matrix.

III.5
Incorporating Duan-Kimble style Quantum Memories

Essential to this modeling is the considerations of quantum memories that store the arriving entangled bell pairs. The quantum memories are atomic qubits where the quantum state might be the spin state of an electron in a color center, but the Duan-Kimble protocol
Duan and Kimble 2004
is agnostic to the type of qubit and rather focuses on the fact that a cavity QED system enables light-matter coupling to realize a control-Z gate. In summary, loading the photon-photon entangled state into the quantum memory spin state via the Duan-Kimble protocol is achieved by

1.

The photonic state interacts with the spin state via a cavity interaction, resulting in a control-Z gate between the state of the spin and the state of the photon.

2.

The photonic state enters a polarizing beamsplitter, where each pair of modes interferes

3.

The photonic state is detected with photon detectors

For the purpose of our modeling we will treat these qubit basis states as bosonic modes. The interaction will be treated as a cross-Kerr unitary between the photonic source state and simulated atomic qubit, treated as a dual-rail bosonic mode.

III.5.1
Quantum Memory Interactions

We begin by describing the non-gaussian controlled-z operation between the bosonic modes generated by the entanglement distribution sources and the quantum memory simulated-bosonic modes. Recall that in the Schrodinger picture of quantum mechanics, a general unitary interaction can be applied to a quantum state by

\displaystyle\hat{U}(t)\ket{\Psi}=\exp(i\hat{H}t)\ket{\Psi}

(117)

Where
\hat{H}
is the Hamiltonian that represents the interaction. Recall that the series representation of an exponential is

\displaystyle\exp(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}=1+x+\frac{x^{2}}{2}+...

(118)

The series representation of the exponential can be used to apply the unitary operation to the quantum state. For example, recalling the fundamental operator relationships
10
, the phase operator is defined as

\displaystyle U_{\theta}=e^{i\theta\hat{N}}=e^{i\theta\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}}

(119)

When applied to the 0 and 1 fock states, this results in

\displaystyle U_{\theta}\ket{0}

\displaystyle=\ket{0}

(120)

\displaystyle U_{\theta}\ket{1}

\displaystyle=e^{i\theta}\ket{1}

(121)

When applied to a coherent state
\ket{\alpha}

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{\theta}\ket{\alpha}

\displaystyle=e^{i\theta\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}}\ket{\alpha}

\displaystyle=(1+(i\theta\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a})+\frac{(i\theta\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a})^{2}}{2}+...)\ket{\alpha}

\displaystyle=(1+(i\theta\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a})+\frac{(i\theta\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a})^{2}}{2}+...)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(\frac{e^{\frac{-|\alpha|^{2}}{2}}\alpha^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}})\ket{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(\frac{e^{\frac{-|\alpha|^{2}}{2}}\alpha^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}})(1+(i\theta\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a})+\frac{(i\theta\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a})^{2}}{2}+...)\ket{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(\frac{e^{\frac{-|\alpha|^{2}}{2}}\alpha^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}})(1+(i\theta n)+\frac{(i\theta n)^{2}}{2}+...)\ket{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(\frac{e^{\frac{-|\alpha|^{2}}{2}}\alpha^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}})e^{i\theta n}\ket{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(\frac{e^{\frac{-|\alpha|^{2}}{2}}(\alpha e^{i\theta})^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}})\ket{n}

\displaystyle=\ket{\alpha e^{i\theta}}

(122)

A control-z operation between two modes a and b can be performed using a cross-Kerr unitary
Imoto et al. 1985
where
\theta=\pi
of the form

\displaystyle U_{ck}=e^{i\pi\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}\hat{b}^{\dagger}\hat{b}}

(123)

As applied between a single-rail qubit and a coherent state, the result is

\displaystyle U_{ck}(\frac{\ket{0}+\ket{1}}{\sqrt{2}})\ket{\alpha}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{0}\ket{\alpha}+\ket{1}\ket{-\alpha})

(124)

This relation can be used to interact the source state, which using the K-function formalism is represented as an integral over coherent states, with the simulated atomic memories, which are represented as dual-rail qubits. First, define

\displaystyle\rho_{M}

\displaystyle=\ket{\phi}\bra{\phi}

(125)

\displaystyle\ket{\phi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{1;0}+\ket{1;0})

(126)

Therefore, the state of our system prior to interaction is

\displaystyle\rho=\rho_{zl}\otimes\rho_{M}\otimes\rho_{M}

(127)

Applying cross-Kerr unitaries as interactions of the source mode with the memory modes

\displaystyle\rho_{z,int}=U_{ck_{8,4}}U_{ck_{2,2}}\rho U^{\dagger}_{ck_{2,2}}U^{\dagger}_{ck_{8,4}}

(128)

Where

\displaystyle U_{ck_{i,j}}=e^{i\pi\hat{a}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{i}\hat{b}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{j}}

(129)

With
\hat{a}_{i}^{\dagger}
and
\hat{b}_{i}^{\dagger}
being the creation operators of the
i
th source mode and memory mode respectively. Therefore

\displaystyle\rho_{z,int}=\int d^{16}\vec{x_{\alpha}}d^{16}\vec{x_{\beta}}K(\vec{\alpha})K(\vec{\beta})^{*}\mathcal{G}(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta},\vec{\eta})\ket{\vec{\alpha}_{E}}\bra{\vec{\beta}_{E}}

(130)

Where

\displaystyle\ket{\vec{\alpha}_{E}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}U_{ck_{4,8}}U_{ck_{2,2}}\ket{\vec{\alpha}_{L}}(\ket{0,1}+\ket{1,0})(\ket{0,1}+\ket{1,0})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\ket{0,1}\ket{0,1}\bigotimes_{i=1}^{8}\begin{cases}\ket{-\alpha_{i}\sqrt{\eta_{i}}}\text{ for }i=2,8\\
\ket{\alpha_{i}\sqrt{\eta_{i}}}\text{ for }i=1,7\\
\ket{\alpha_{i}\sqrt{\eta_{i}}}\text{ for }i=3,4,5,6\end{cases}

\displaystyle+\ket{0,1}\ket{1,0}\bigotimes_{i=1}^{8}\begin{cases}\ket{-\alpha_{i}\sqrt{\eta_{i}}}\text{ for }i=2\\
\ket{\alpha_{i}\sqrt{\eta_{i}}}\text{ for }i=1,7,8\\
\ket{\alpha_{i}\sqrt{\eta_{i}}}\text{ for }i=3,4,5,6\end{cases}

\displaystyle+\ket{1,0}\ket{0,1}\bigotimes_{i=1}^{8}\begin{cases}\ket{-\alpha_{i}\sqrt{\eta_{i}}}\text{ for }i=8\\
\ket{\alpha_{i}\sqrt{\eta_{i}}}\text{ for }i=1,2,7\\
\ket{\alpha_{i}\sqrt{\eta_{i}}}\text{ for }i=3,4,5,6\end{cases}

\displaystyle+\ket{1,0}\ket{1,0}\bigotimes_{i=1}^{8}\begin{cases}\ket{\alpha_{i}\sqrt{\eta_{i}}}\text{ for }i=1,2,7,8\\
\ket{\alpha_{i}\sqrt{\eta_{i}}}\text{ for }i=3,4,5,6\end{cases})

(131)

Next, each pair of modes will pass through a polarizing beam splitter.

III.5.2
Interference to complete Encoding

Consider two coherent states
\ket{\alpha}
and
\ket{\beta}

\displaystyle\ket{\alpha}\ket{\beta}

\displaystyle=(e^{(-|\alpha|^{2})/2})\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\alpha)^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}}\ket{n})(e^{(-|\beta|^{2})/2})\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\beta)^{m}}{\sqrt{m!}}\ket{m})

\displaystyle=(e^{(-|\alpha|^{2})/2})\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\alpha)^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}}(\hat{a}^{n})\ket{0})(e^{(-|\beta|^{2})/2})\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\beta)^{m}}{\sqrt{m!}}(\hat{b}^{m})\ket{0})

\displaystyle=e^{(-|\alpha|^{2})/2}e^{(-|\beta|^{2})/2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\alpha)^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}}\frac{(\beta)^{m}}{\sqrt{m!}}(\hat{a}^{n})(\hat{b}^{m})\ket{0}\ket{0}

(132)

We know that the polarizing beam splitter will transform the creation operators such that

\displaystyle\hat{a}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\frac{\hat{a}+\hat{b}}{\sqrt{2}}

(133)

\displaystyle\hat{b}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\frac{\hat{a}-\hat{b}}{\sqrt{2}}

(134)

Therefore

\displaystyle U_{pbs}\ket{\alpha}\ket{\beta}

\displaystyle=e^{(-|\alpha|^{2})/2}e^{(-|\beta|^{2})/2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\alpha)^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}}\frac{(\beta)^{m}}{\sqrt{m!}}(\frac{\hat{a}+\hat{b}}{\sqrt{2}})^{n}(\frac{\hat{a}-\hat{b}}{\sqrt{2}})^{m}\ket{0}\ket{0}

(135)

\displaystyle=e^{(-|\alpha|^{2})/2}e^{(-|\beta|^{2})/2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\alpha)^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}}\frac{(\beta)^{m}}{\sqrt{m!}}\frac{1}{(\sqrt{2})^{n+m}}(\hat{a}+\hat{b})^{n}(\hat{a}-\hat{b})^{m}\ket{0}\ket{0}

(136)

Consider the binomial theorem

\displaystyle(x+y)^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}x^{n-k}y^{k}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}x^{k}y^{n-k}

(137)

So,

\displaystyle(\hat{a}+\hat{b})^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\hat{a}^{n-k}\hat{b}^{k}

(138)

\displaystyle(\hat{a}-\hat{b})^{m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{m}\binom{m}{l}\hat{a}^{m-l}(-1)^{l}\hat{b}^{l}

(139)

Therefore, our expression becomes

\displaystyle U_{pbs}\ket{\alpha}\ket{\beta}

\displaystyle=e^{(-|\alpha|^{2})/2}e^{(-|\beta|^{2})/2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\alpha)^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}}\frac{(\beta)^{m}}{\sqrt{m!}}\frac{1}{(\sqrt{2})^{n+m}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{l=0}^{m}\binom{n}{k}\binom{m}{l}(-1)^{l}\hat{a}^{n+m-k-l}\hat{b}^{k+l}\ket{0}\ket{0}

\displaystyle=e^{(-|\alpha|^{2})/2}e^{(-|\beta|^{2})/2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\alpha)^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}}\frac{(\beta)^{m}}{\sqrt{m!}}\frac{1}{(\sqrt{2})^{n+m}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{l=0}^{m}\binom{n}{k}\binom{m}{l}(-1)^{l}\ket{n+m-k-l}\ket{k+l}

(140)

This expression will simplify as we consider photon detection on these coherent state modes.

III.5.3
Detection

For modeling detection, it is desireable to include the non-idealities of loss and detector dark clicks. Detector loss can be incorporated using the same prescription as transmission loss (as described in
III.2.2
), just replace
\eta
with
\eta_{d}
. Detector dark clicks can be incorporated by calculating the probability of incorrect click patterns that would be accepted in the presence of detector dark clicks.

For the Bell state measurement modes, the desired click patterns are known exactly from
(
Dhara et al. 2022
)
. As for the remaining modes, heralded photons will collapse the state of the memories, and the click patterns for these detectors are known based on the encoding technique. For example, with Duan-Kimble memory encoding we know that acceptable click patterns are those where only one click is detected for each pair of source modes. For further details, see the examples that follow.

In many cases, the detection situation that will be considered are

•

\bra{1}\bra{0}U_{pbs}\ket{\alpha}\ket{\beta}

•

\bra{0}\bra{1}U_{pbs}\ket{\alpha}\ket{\beta}

•

\bra{0}\bra{0}U_{pbs}\ket{\alpha}\ket{\beta}

First, consider the most simple case

\displaystyle\bra{0}\bra{0}U_{pbs}\ket{\alpha}\ket{\beta}

\displaystyle=e^{(-|\alpha|^{2})/2}e^{(-|\beta|^{2})/2}

(142)

Next, for the single-photon detection patterns, we consider the overlap in products, which result in

\displaystyle n+m-l-k

\displaystyle=1

(143)

\displaystyle l+k

\displaystyle=0\rightarrow l=-k

(144)

Because
k
cannot be negative,
l=k=0
. So, therefore
n+m=1
, and so either
n=0,m=1
or
n=1,m=0

IV
Simulation Module

This work focuses on providing a highly accurate model of the ZALM cascaded entanglement source, and for the sake of reusability and reproducibility, a stack of open source software tools implementing the presented modeling methods is provided. These tools come with significant quality assurance measures, including proper continuous integration development processes, testing and verification suites, and ease of installation through popular software package indexes. In this section we discuss these tools, examining the low-level python implementation of these bespoke modeling techniques (the genqo Python package), its integration as one of the models available in the QuantumSymbolics.jl Julia computer algebra system, and its use in the full-stack networking simulator QuantumSavory.jl. We present the low-level ZALM-specific Python API, the visulization and exploration tools for studying the parameterization of the ZALM source among others within QuantumSavory, and examples of full-stack simulations. Lastly, as prescribed in the
“Software for Quantum Networks”
special issue, we provide a stand-alone
“mock hardware”
HTTP server which can be used as a universal API for this software, in addition to the already existing APIs.

IV.2
QuantumSymbolics.jl computer algebra system

The genqo model is integrated into QuantumSymbolics.jl, a computer algebra system (CAS) for quantum mechanics and quantum information. QuantumSymbolics.jl allows for the algebraic manipulation of quantum states and operators in a symbolic representation. A key feature of this CAS is its
express
API, which translates these symbolic objects into various numerical representations suitable for different simulation backends, such as state vectors, density matrices, or stabilizer tableaux.

Within this framework, the ZALM source is represented by the
GenqoMultiplexedCascadedBellPairW
struct. An instance of this struct, such as
s = GenqoMultiplexedCascadedBellPairW(
\eta^{b}
=1.0,
\eta^{d}
=1.0,
\eta^{t}
=0.8,
N
=0.1,
P^{d}
=1e-8)
, is a symbolic object that encapsulates the full, highly-parameterized physical model of the entanglement source. This object can be integrated into larger symbolic expressions representing more complex quantum information processing tasks.

Symbolic operations can be performed on these genqo-backed states before any numerical evaluation is triggered. For instance, to model the state after transmission through a lossy channel, one can define a symbolic representation of that channel
L
, and apply it to the source object:
s_final = L(0.5) * s
. This composition is handled purely at the symbolic level. The expensive numerical computation, which involves calling the ‘genqo‘ Python backend to generate the source’s density matrix, is deferred until the
express(s_final)
command is issued. At that point, QuantumSymbolics.jl resolves the entire expression: it first calls genqo to get the initial numerical state for the given parameters, and then applies a numerical representation of the loss channel to that state, caching intermediary results. This methodology allows for the seamless composition of a bespoke, high-accuracy model like genqo with the extensive library of general-purpose symbolic quantum operations available in QuantumSymbolics.jl, facilitating sophisticated, multi-component system modeling. Moreover, this approach permits symbolic objects to be expressed in a variety of different formalisms – the
express
function can convert not only to a state vector numerical object, but also to Gaussian quantum states, stabilizer states, or tensor networks, whenever feasible.

IV.3
QuantumSavory.jl full-stack network simulator

The genqo models, being fully integrated into QuantumSymbolics.jl, are immediately available for use within QuantumSavory.jl, a full-stack, discrete-event simulator for quantum networks. QuantumSavory.jl is designed to model the complex interplay of quantum state evolution, classical control messaging, and network topology, making it an ideal platform for assessing the system-level performance of quantum hardware. Because the simulator uses QuantumSymbolics.jl as its frontend for describing arbitrary quantum dynamics, any symbolic object, including the
GenqoMultiplexedCascadedBellPairW
state, can be seamlessly initialized in a network’s quantum registers.

To demonstrate this capability, we have developed a simulation of a first-generation quantum repeater chain. In this scenario, entanglement is first established between adjacent nodes of the network and then extended end-to-end via entanglement swapping. The simulation is parameterized by the choice of the initial entanglement source. By simply switching the state initialized at the source nodes – for example, from an idealized perfect Bell pair to the physically realistic
GenqoMultiplexedCascadedBellPairW
– a user can directly compare the performance of different hardware assumptions. The simulator tracks figures of merit such as end-to-end fidelity and entanglement generation rates, allowing for a direct assessment of how the non-ideal properties of the genqo source, such as multi-photon emission noise at high pump powers, impact the overall network performance. This enables a comprehensive, system-level analysis of the rate-versus-fidelity tradeoffs uncovered by the genqo model.

An interactive version of this simulation, allowing real-time parameter exploration, is available at
areweentangledyet.com/simplerepeaters/
.

IV.4
State Explorer visualization tool

To facilitate an intuitive understanding of the complex parameter space of the genqo sources and other sources available in QuantumSavory.jl, we provide an interactive visualization tool, the
“State Explorer”
. This web-based application, built on the Makie plotting library and integrated into QuantumSavory, allows for the real-time exploration of any two-qubit state, including the
GenqoMultiplexedCascadedBellPairW
.

Users can manipulate sliders corresponding to the source’s physical parameters, such as mean photon number and various coupling efficiencies. The tool dynamically recalculates and displays crucial figures of merit along multiple parameter dimensions, including generation probability and fidelity with respect to a perfect Bell pair. This provides a direct, visual confirmation of the trade-offs discussed in this work, such as the ability to recover high success probabilities at the cost of fidelity by increasing the pump power in lossy regimes.

The State Explorer is available at wthin the QuantumSavory code base and a live instance is provided at
areweentangledyet.com/state_explorer/
.

IV.5
JSON Server

To ensure the broadest possible accessibility and interoperability of our models, and in accordance with the guidelines for this special issue, we provide a standalone HTTP server that exposes the genqo model and other states available in QuantumSavory.jl through a universal, language-agnostic API. This server acts as a
“mock hardware”
interface, accepting requests with specified state parameters and state representation. Upon receiving a request, the server can provide the full density matrix of the requested state, its success probability, and other relevant figures of merit like fidelity and concurrence, all formatted in a standard data-interchange format like JSON. This allows researchers and developers to integrate our high-accuracy models into any third-party simulation or analysis tool capable of making standard web requests, without needing to directly engage with the underlying Julia or Python codebases. The tool is available at
areweentangledyet.com/states_rest_server/docs
.

V
Discussion and Conclusions

The ZALM source modeled in this work is a practical, deployable entanglement source overcoming multiplexing and heralding issues plaguing earlier blueprints. We provide a digital twin of such a source at an accuracy significantly surpassing previous approximate results, particularly in the high-photon-number regime, where new interesting tradeoffs are uncovered. Our model shows that drops in probability of generation due to loss can reliably be circumvented by increasing the mean photon number, a capability that was not uncovered in earlier models. While this newly interesting parameter regime suffers from lower entanglement fidelity, it poses interesting opportunities for entanglement distillation to be employed on top of a ZALM source to achieve unexpectedly high overall rates and fidelities – a prospect that deserves further exploration. Such exploration would be much easier thanks to the detailed, reproducible, tested, easy-to-install software models we provide.

The modular
“polyglot”
way in which a bespoke high-quality model (genqo) implemented in an arbitrary language is made seamlessly available in a holistic computer algebra system (QuantumSymbolics.jl) and incorporated in a full-stack quantum network simulator (QuantumSavory.jl) bodes well for the future of the modeling software ecosystem. It is a proof that the diverse tools developed in disconnected research labs can be brought together. Modern development practices (heavy use of package and dependency management, automated testing, static analysis, and continuous integration on all popular hardware platforms and operating systems) turn what used to be a difficult technical problem into a much simpler social problem of coordination, collaboration, and maintaining open access to results. What used to be a significant standardization effort and overhead of supporting such standardization is now a much simpler task thanks to the existence of engineering tools that interface different languages and numerical storage formats as well as scientific tools that convert between different formalisms (e.g. state vectors, Gaussian quantum formalism, stabilizer states, tensor networks, and more).
