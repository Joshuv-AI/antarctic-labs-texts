Fractional Integrals and Tangency Problems in Integral Geometry
B. Rubin

Abstract
. Many known Radon-type transforms of symmetric (radial or zonal) functions are represented by one-dimensional Riemann-Liouville fractional integrals or their modifications. The present article contains new examples of such transforms in the Euclidean, spherical, and hyperbolic settings, when integration is performed over lower-dimensional geodesic spheres or cross-sections, which are tangent to a given surface. Simple inversion formulas are obtained and admissible singularities at the tangency points are studied. Possible applications to the half-ball screening in mathematical tomography and some difficulties related to the general (not necessarily symmetric) case are discussed.

1.
Introduction

The Riemann-Liouville fractional integrals and derivatives have proved to be a powerful tool in the study of diverse Radon-type transforms in integral geometry; see, e.g.,
[
16
,
18
]
and references therein. The present article contains new examples of such transforms, when integration is performed over tangent manifolds, like spheres or chords of different dimensions and in different settings, provided that the integrated functions are invariant under the relevant group of transformations. The choice of the examples is motivated by potential application in mathematical tomography and the intimate connection to the Cauchy problem for the Euler-Poisson-Darboux equations on constant curvature spaces with the initial data on some characteristic surfaces.

In Section
2
, we recall some facts from fractional calculus. More information can be found in
[
16
,
18
,
19
]
. Further consideration includes integral transforms over geodesic spheres of different dimensions, which are tangent to a fixed sphere in a given constant curvature space. Specifically, Section
3
deals with spherical means of functions on the Euclidean ball and its exterior. Section
4
is devoted to integral transforms over cross-sections (or
k
-chords
) of the
n
-dimensional half-ball
{{B}}_{+}
by
k
-dimensional affine planes, which are tangent to the boundary of
{{B}}_{+}
. This consideration might be of interest from the point of view of medical tomography, when the examined region is located inside the half-ball. In Section
5
we consider the geodesic spherical means of functions on the unit sphere, when integration is performed over spherical sections tangent to the equator or, more generally, to a given parallel of latitude. Similar problems in the real hyperbolic space (for the hyperboloid model) are studied in Section
6
. Section
7
deals with spherical means of functions on the
n
-dimensional Euclidean half-space when integration is performed over
k
-dimensional spheres which are tangent to the boundary and lie in the relevant vertical affine plane.

A consideration of integral-geometric problems for tangent manifolds is not new; see, e.g. Gindikin
[
8
]
, Goncharov
[
10
]
, Palamodov
[
12
]
. To the best of our knowledge, known inversion formulas are pretty involved. They contain generalized functions and the corresponding integrals should be understood in the sense of regularization. This approach imposes inevitable restrictions on functions, which must be infinitely differentiable and vanishing (or rapidly decreasing) near the boundary.
On the other hand, the original spherical means are well defined on Lebesgue integrable functions, which may have singularities at the tangency points. The methods of the above works are inapplicable to such functions.

In the present article, we essentially restrict the setting of the problem by imposing additional invariance conditions on our functions. This restriction yields simple explicit inversion formulas in terms of one-dimensional Riemann-Liouville fractional integrals and derivatives. Such formulas might be of interest on their own right and useful in applications, e.g., in mathematical tomography. Our approach also allows one to obtain explicit weighted equalities for the relevant spherical means of arbitrary functions. These equalities give precise information about existence of the spherical means for specific classes of functions with prescribed behavior near the boundary.

The results of this article show that derivation of the explicit inversion formulas for tangent spherical means without additional restriction on the class of functions may be very complicated. As an example, we illustrate the complexity of this problem for tangent spherical means in the half-space (see Section
7.3
), when the Fourier transform technique can be applicable.

It is worth mentioning one more interesting class of spherical means, which occurs when the dimension of the boundary manifold is higher than the dimension of the tangent spheres over which the integration is performed. In this case, the tangent spheres can be oblique with respect to the tangent hyperplane to the boundary; cf. e.g., the half-space model or the Euclidean ball model. Consideration of such ‘oblique spherical means’ lies beyond the scope of the present paper.

2.
Some Facts from Fractional Calculus

Let
f
be an integrable function on a finite interval

[a,b]
. The Riemann-Liouville fractional integrals of order
\alpha>0
are defined by

(I^{\alpha}_{a+}f)(x)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int\limits^{x}_{a}\!\!\frac{f(y)\,dy}{(x\!-\!y)^{1-\alpha}},

(2.1)

(I^{\alpha}_{b-}f)(x)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int\limits^{b}_{x}\!\!\frac{f(y)\,dy}{(y\!-\!x)^{1-\alpha}}.

(2.2)

Here
a<x<b
,
\;\Gamma(\alpha)
is the Euler gamma function.
If
\,0<\alpha<1
, the corresponding fractional derivatives have the form

\displaystyle(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}_{a+}\varphi)(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\,\frac{d}{dx}\,\int\limits_{a}^{x}\frac{\varphi(y)\,dy}{(x-y)^{\alpha}},

(2.3)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}_{b-}\varphi)(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\,\frac{d}{dx}\,\int\limits_{x}^{b}\frac{\varphi(y)\,dy}{(y-x)^{\alpha}}.

(2.4)

In the general case
\alpha>0
, we set
\alpha=m+\alpha_{0}
, where
0\leq\alpha_{0}<1
,
m=[\alpha]
(the integer part of
\alpha
), and write

(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}_{a+}\varphi)(x)=(d/dx)^{m+1}(I^{1-\alpha_{0}}_{a+}\varphi)(x),

(2.5)

(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}_{b-}\varphi)(x)=(-d/dx)^{m+1}(I^{1-\alpha_{0}}_{b-}\varphi)(x).

(2.6)

Lemma 2.1
.

(See, e.g.
[
16
, Lemma 2.4]
)

If
f\in L^{1}(a,b),\;\alpha>0
, then

(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}_{a+}I^{\alpha}_{a+}f)(x)=f(x),\qquad(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}_{b-}I^{\alpha}_{b-}f)(x)=f(x)

(2.7)

for almost all
x\in[a,b]
.

3.
The Euclidean Ball and Its Exterior

3.1.
Preliminaries

Let
x\in{{R}}^{n}
,

{{B}}^{n}_{+}=\{x:0<|x|<1\},\quad{{B}}^{n}_{-}=\{x:|x|>1\},\quad{{S}}^{n-1}=\{x:|x|=1\}.

Given an integer
k
,
2\leq k\leq n
, consider the set of all
(k-1)
-dimensional spheres
\sigma
tangent to
{{S}}^{n-1}
.
Every such
\sigma
determines a unique
k
-dimensional plane
\xi(\sigma)
containing
\sigma
. If
k=n
, this plane coincides with
{{R}}^{n}
.
If
k<n
, one should distinguish the ‘
central tangency
’, when
\xi(\sigma)
is a linear subspace of
{{R}}^{n}
, and the ‘
oblique tangency
’, when
\xi(\sigma)
does not pass through the origin of
{{R}}^{n}
. We restrict our consideration to the first case and denote by
\Sigma_{n,k}
the set of all
(k-1)
-dimensional spheres, which are tangent to
{{S}}^{n-1}
and contained in some
k
-dimensional linear subspace of
{{R}}^{n}
.
The case of the oblique tangency represents an intriguing open problem.

Given a point
x\in{{R}}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
,
let
G_{n,k}^{x}
be the manifold of all
k
-dimensional linear subspaces of
{{R}}^{n}
containing
x
.
Every
\sigma\in\Sigma_{n,k}
, can be indexed by the pair
(x,\xi)
, where
x
is the center of the sphere
\sigma

and
\xi
is the subspace in
G_{n,k}^{x}
containing
\sigma
. We distinguish the cases of the interior and exterior tangency and denote

\Sigma^{\pm}_{n,k}=\{\sigma\equiv\sigma(x,\xi)\in\Sigma_{n,k}:x\in{{B}}^{n}_{\pm}\}.

We equip the sets
\Sigma^{\pm}_{n,k}
with the product measure, so that if
\sigma=\sigma(\theta,\xi)
, then
d\sigma=dxd_{*}\xi
, where
dx
is the standard Euclidean measure on
{{R}}^{n}
and
d_{*}\xi
is the Haar probability measure on
G_{n,k}^{x}
.

Our main concern is the spherical means

\displaystyle\!\!\!(Q_{+}f)(x,\xi)\!=\!\frac{1}{\sigma_{k-1}}\!\int\limits_{{S^{n-1}\cap\xi}}\!\!\!\!f(x\!+\!(1\!-\!|x|)\,\theta)\,d\theta,\quad|x|<1,\;\xi\in G_{n,k}^{x},\qquad

(3.1)

\displaystyle\!\!\!(Q_{-}f)(x,\xi)\!=\!\frac{1}{\sigma_{k-1}}\!\int\limits_{{S^{n-1}\cap\xi}}\!\!\!\!f(x\!+\!(|x|\!-\!1)\,\theta)\,d\theta,\quad|x|>1,\;\xi\in G_{n,k}^{x},\qquad

(3.2)

over the tangent spheres
\sigma(x,\xi)\in\Sigma^{\pm}_{n,k}
, respectively. The main question is how to reconstruct the function
f
from
Q_{\pm}f
in the respective domain

{{B}}^{n}_{\pm}
.

The setting of the problem is motivated in part by the intimate connection with the classical
Cauchy problem for the Darboux equation in PDE, when we are looking for a function
u(x,t)
satisfying

\Delta u-u_{tt}-\frac{n-1}{t}\,u_{t}\!=\!0,\qquad u(x,0)\!=\!f(x),\quad u_{t}(x,0)\!=\!0.

(3.3)

Here
x\in{{R}}^{n}
,
t>0
, and
f
is a given function. Let us consider the following inverse problem:
Given the traces of the solution of (
3.3
) on the characteristic conical surfaces

C_{+}\!=\!\{(x,t):|x|<1,\,t=1-|x|\},\quad C_{-}\!=\!\{(x,t):|x|>1,\,t=|x|-1\},

reconstruct the initial function
f(x)
.

If such an
f
is found, one can compute the solution of (
3.3
) in the entire cylinder
\{(x,t):|x|<1,\,t>0\}
and in its exterior by the formula

u(x,t)=\frac{1}{\sigma_{n-1}}\int\limits_{{{S}}^{n-1}}f(x+t\theta)\,d\theta,

(3.4)

where
d\theta
stands for the surface area measure on
{{S}}^{n-1}
;
see, e.g.,
[
5
, p. 699]
. Thus we arrive at the integral-geometric inversion problem for the operators
Q_{\pm}f
in the special case
k=n
.

The setting of the problem is not new and is known in a rather general context. Inversion problems for operators of integral geometry on real quadrics
were investigated by Gindikin
[
8
]
(see also
[
6
, p. 162]
) by making use of the kappa-operator and generalized functions. A more general case of tangent spheres to compact manifolds was studied by Goncharov
[
10
]
, who invoked the theory of
D
-modules. Another approach to spherical means over
(n-1)
-dimensional tangent spheres to a manifold in
{{R}}^{n}
was suggested by Palamodov
[
12
, Section 7.8]
, who considered the case of the interior tangency. In our notation, his inversion formula for the operator
Q_{+}
and
n
even can be written as follows:

f(x)=c\,\int\limits_{{{B}}^{n}_{+}}[q^{-n}]\,\frac{1-|x|^{2}-2(x\cdot y)+2|y||x|^{2}}{1-|y|}\,\frac{(Q_{+}f)(y)}{|y|}\,dy,

(3.5)

where
c={\hbox{\rm const}}
,
q=(1-|y|)^{2}-|y-x|^{2}
, and
[q^{-n}]
is the relevant distribution; see
[
12
, p. 13]
. If
n
is odd the inversion formula looks similarly, with
[q^{-n}]
replaced by the derivative of the delta distribution
\delta^{(n-1)}(q)
; see
[
12
, p. 133]
for details.

The afore-mentioned approaches are very important from the theoretical point of view. However, they inevitably impose very restrictive conditions on the functions
f
assuming them to be smooth and excessively good near the boundary. For instance, in
[
10
, Theorem 1.1]
it is assumed that
f
vanishes identically near the surface of tangency. Note also that the integral in (
3.5
) includes the distribution
[q^{-n}]
. It does not exist in the usual Lebesgue sense and must be interpreted in the framework of the distribution theory; cf., e.g.,
[
12
, Sections 1.3, 1.4]
,
[
7
]
. The corresponding explicit formula is pretty sophisticated, even on ‘good’ functions and is inapplicable if
f
is not differentiable.
It is natural to ask the following

Question:

Which minimal conditions should be imposed on the locally integrable function
f
to provide the existence of the integrals
Q_{\pm}f
in the a.e. sense and the relevant pointwise reconstruction formulas?

Questions of this kind are well investigated for totally geodesic Radon transforms on constant curvature spaces and for the horospherical transforms in the real hyperbolic space, where the unknown function
f

belongs to the corresponding
L^{p}
space; see, e.g.,
[
16
,
18
]
.

Remark 3.1
.

There is one more important issue to be mentioned, which was apparently missed in the previous publications.
In fact, the tangent spheres can be of two kinds, depending on whether their radius is less than
1/2
or greater than
1/2
. In the first case, the center of the ball
{{B}}^{n}_{+}
lies outside of the tangent sphere, while, in the second case, it is inside. If our goal is to reconstruct
f
on
{{B}}^{n}_{+}
, it suffices to consider only one of these cases. Moreover,
it is natural to expect that the separating sphere
|x|=1/2
may cause additional singularities of
Q_{+}f
.
We address this observation below.

Remark 3.2
.

The unit ball
|x|<1
can be replaced by any ball
|x|<a
,
a>0
. The limiting case
a=0
for the operator
Q_{-}
, corresponds to spheres through the origin. This case for
n=2

was first considered by Cormack
[
3
]
who obtained a formal inversion of the corresponding operator in terms of the Fourier series. Regarding further developments of this case (for
k=n\geq 2
) by Quinto
[
13
,
14
]
and other authors, see
[
16
, Sections 7.1, 7.4]
and references therein.

It is important to note that the operators
Q_{\pm}
are
O(n)
-equivariant. Using this fact, it will be shown that if
f
is a radial function, then
(Q_{\pm}f)(x,\xi)
are represented by one-dimensional fractional integrals, which can be explicitly inverted. The relevant support theorems then easily follow.

3.2.
The Interior Tangency

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
f(y)=f_{0}(|y|)
,
y\in{{B}}^{n}_{+}
,
f_{1}(s)=f_{0}(s)(1-s^{2})^{(k-3)/2}
,
2\leq k\leq n
. Then for all
|x|<1
and
\xi\in G_{n,k}^{x}
,

(Q_{+}f)(x,\xi)=\Phi_{+}(t),\qquad\Phi_{+}(t)=\frac{\Gamma(k/2)}{\pi^{1/2}}\,\left(\frac{1\!-\!t^{2}}{2}\right)^{2-k}\!\!F_{+}(t),

(3.6)

where
t=2||x|-1/2|\in[0,1)
,

F_{+}(t)=\frac{2}{\Gamma((k-1)/2)}\int\limits_{t}^{1}(s^{2}-t^{2})^{(k-3)/2}f_{1}(s)\,s\,ds.

(3.7)

It is assumed
that the integral (
3.7
) exists in the Lebesgue sense.

Proof.

Let
\rho=1-|x|
be the radius of the tangent sphere centered at the point
x
. By (
3.1
),

\displaystyle(Q_{+}f)(x,\xi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma_{k-1}}\int\limits_{{{S}}^{n-1}\cap\xi}f_{0}(|x+\rho\,\theta|)\,d\theta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma_{k-1}}\int\limits_{{{S}}^{n-1}\cap\xi}f_{0}(\sqrt{|x|^{2}+2\rho x\cdot\theta+\rho^{2}})\,d\theta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{k-2}}{\sigma_{k-1}}\int\limits_{-1}^{1}f_{0}(\sqrt{(1-\rho)^{2}+2\rho(1-\rho)t+\rho^{2}})\,(1-t^{2})^{(k-3)/2}\,dt

(cf.
[
16
, f. (1.12.13)]
). Changing variables
(1-\rho)^{2}+2\rho(1-\rho)t+\rho^{2}=s^{2}
, so that

t=\frac{s^{2}-\rho^{2}-(1-\rho)^{2}}{2\rho(1-\rho)},\quad 1-t^{2}=\frac{(1-s^{2})(s^{2}-(2\rho-1)^{2})}{(2\rho(1-\rho))^{2}},

we obtain

(Q_{+}f)(x,\xi)=\frac{\sigma_{k-2}}{\sigma_{k-1}}\int\limits_{|1-2\rho|}^{1}f_{0}(s)\,\frac{(1-s^{2})^{(k-3)/2}(s^{2}-(1-2\rho)^{2})^{(k-3)/2}}{(2\rho(1-\rho))^{k-3}}\,\frac{s\,ds}{\rho(1-\rho)}.

Setting
1-2\rho=2(|x|-1/2)
, we arrive at (
3.6
).
∎

Example 3.4
.

Let
f(x)=|x|^{1-k-2\beta}(1-|x|^{2})^{\beta+(1-k)/2}
,
\beta>0
. Then the right-hand side of (
3.6
) can be explicitly computed using tables of integrals and we have

(Q_{+}f)(x,\xi)\!=\!c\,\frac{(|x|(1\!-\!|x|))^{\beta+(1-k)/2}}{|\,|x|\!-\!1/2|^{2\beta}},\;c\!=\!\frac{\Gamma(\beta)\,\Gamma(k/2)}{2\pi^{1/2}\Gamma(\beta\!+\!(k\!-\!1)/2)}.

(3.8)

As we can see, the operator
Q_{+}
, which is symmetric with respect to the sphere
|x|=1/2
, induces a singularity of
Q_{+}f
on this sphere.

Corollary 3.5
.

The average
\tilde{\varphi}(\xi,x)=\int_{O(n)}\varphi(\gamma\xi,\gamma x)d\gamma
of the spherical mean
\varphi=Q_{+}f
is symmetric with respect to the sphere
|x|=1/2
and is represented by the fractional integral according to Lemma
3.3
.

Lemma
3.3
, along with the second formula in (
2.7
), yield the following inversion result.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
2\leq k\leq n
,
f(y)=f_{0}(|y|)
,
y\in{{B}}^{n}_{+}
, so that
(Q_{+}f)(x,\xi)=\Phi_{+}(t)
,
t=|2|x|-1|\in(0,1)
. We denote

H(v)=\frac{\pi^{1/2}}{\Gamma(k/2)}\,\left(\frac{1\!-\!v}{2}\right)^{k-2}\Phi_{+}(\sqrt{v}).

If

\int\limits_{\varepsilon}^{1}|f_{0}(s)|\,(1-s^{2})^{(k-3)/2}s\,ds<\infty\quad\text{for every}\quad\varepsilon\in(0,1),

(3.9)

then
f_{0}(s)
can be reconstructed for almost all
s\in(0,1)
by the formula

f_{0}(s)=(1-s^{2})^{(3-k)/2}(\mathcal{D}^{(k-1)/2}_{1-}H)(s^{2}),

where the fractional derivative
\mathcal{D}^{(k-1)/2}_{1-}H
is defined by (
2.6
).

Proof.

Setting
s^{2}=u
,
t^{2}=v
in (
3.7
) and denoting

h(u)=f_{1}(\sqrt{u}),\qquad H(v)=F_{+}(\sqrt{v}),

we obtain
I_{1-}^{(k-1)/2}h=H
. The assumption (
3.9
) makes it possible to apply Lemma
2.1
, which gives
h=\mathcal{D}^{(k-1)/2}_{1-}H
. It remains to return to the original notation.
∎

Remark 3.7
.

According to Lemma
3.3
and Theorem
7.2
, to reconstruct
f
, we do not need
(Q_{+}f)(x,\xi)
for
all

|x|<1
. It suffices to take either
|x|<1/2
(i.e.,
\rho=1-|x|>1/2
) or
1/2<|x|<1
(i.e.,
\rho=1-|x|<1/2

Moreover, the one-sided structure of fractional integrals immediately implies the following ‘support theorem’.

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
f(y)=f_{0}(|y|)
obey the condition (
3.9
). If
0<\delta<1
and
(Q_{+}f)(x,\xi)=0
for all
0<|x|<(1-\delta)/2
(or for all

. Then

for all
\delta<|y|<1
.

We conjecture that a similar theorem is true for arbitrary (not necessarily radial) functions.

The next lemma resembles duality relations for Radon transforms.

Lemma 3.9
.

Given a function
u_{0}(t)
,
t\in(0,1)
, let

\displaystyle u(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s^{1-k}u_{0}(|2s-1|),\qquad 0<s<1,

\displaystyle v(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathchar 1403\relax(s)\int\limits_{0}^{s}(s^{2}-t^{2})^{(k-3)/2}\,(1-t^{2})^{2-k}u_{0}(t)\,dt,

\mathchar 1403\relax(s)=\frac{2^{k-1}\Gamma(k/2)\,\sigma_{k-1}}{\pi^{1/2}\,\Gamma((k-1)/2)\,\sigma_{n-1}}\frac{(1-s^{2})^{(k-3)/2}}{s^{n-2}}.

is defined by (

).

.

u_{0}(t)=t^{\alpha}(1-t^{2})^{k-2}
,
Re\,\alpha>-1
, in Lemma

. Then

\displaystyle u(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s^{1-k}|2s-1|^{\alpha}(1-|2s-1|^{2})^{k-2}

\displaystyle 2^{\alpha+2k-4}s^{-1}|s-1/2|^{\alpha}(1-s)^{k-2};

\tilde{c}=\frac{\Gamma(k/2)\,\Gamma((\alpha+1)/2)\,\sigma_{k-1}}{2^{\alpha+k-2}\pi^{1/2}\,\Gamma((k+\alpha)/2)\,\sigma_{n-1}}.

Remark 3.11

\displaystyle=

(3.14)

3.3.
The Exterior Tangency

The next statement is an analogue of Lemma

Lemma 3.12

Let

It is assumed

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma_{k-1}}\int\limits_{{{S}}^{k-1}}f_{0}(|x+\rho\,\theta|)\,d\theta

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma_{k-1}}\int\limits_{{{S}}^{k-1}}f_{0}(\sqrt{|x|^{2}+2\rho x\cdot\theta+\rho^{2}})\,d\theta

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{k-2}}{\sigma_{k-1}}\int\limits_{-1}^{1}f_{0}(\sqrt{(1+\rho)^{2}+2\rho(1+\rho)t+\rho^{2}})\,(1-t^{2})^{(k-3)/2}\,dt.

4.
Tangent Chords of the Half-Ball

In this section, we slightly change the notation and write
{{B}}=\{x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in{{R}}^{n}:|x|<1\}
for an open unit ball in
{{R}}^{n}
and
{{B}}_{+}=\{x\in{{B}}:x_{n}>0\}
for an open half-ball;
n\geq 2
. Let also
{{S}}={{S}}^{n-1}
be the unit sphere in
{{R}}^{n}
with the north pole

, and let

be the corresponding open hemisphere.
The notation

is used for the geodesic distance on

.

Given an integer

the family of all cross-sections (or

-chords

by those

{{S}}^{n-2}=\{x\in{{S}}^{n-1}:x_{n}=0\}.

(4.1)

Question:

Can we reconstruct a function

on
{{B}}_{+}
from its integrals

(If)(h)=\int\limits_{h}f(y)\,d_{h}y

(4.2)

over tangent
k
-chords

, where

is the Lebesgue measure on
h
?

This question might be of interest from the point of view of the medical tomography, when the examined object is located inside the half-ball and the signals should not cross the bottom of this half-ball. In this section we show that if
f
is zonal, i.e., depends only on the last coordinate
x_{n}
,
the problem can be solved explicitly by making use of fractional differentiation.

Every
k
-chord

can be indexed by the pair
(\theta,\xi)
, where

\theta\in{{S}}_{+}
is the center of the
(k-1)
-dimensional geodesic sphere, which is the boundary of
h
, and
\xi
is a
k
-dimensional linear subspace of the
(n-1)
-dimensional subspace
\theta^{\perp}
orthogonal to
\theta
(and parallel to

). The Grassmann manifold of all such

will be denoted by
G_{k}(\theta^{\perp})
. Then
(If)(h)\equiv(If)(\theta,\xi)
can be explicitly written as

(If)(\theta,\xi)=(1-t^{2})^{k/2}\int\limits_{{{B}}\cap\xi}f(\sqrt{1\!-\!t^{2}}\,\eta+t\theta)\,d\eta,

(4.3)

\theta\in{{S}}_{+},\qquad\xi\in G_{k}(\theta^{\perp}),\qquad t=\sin d(e_{n},\theta)=\sqrt{1-\theta_{n}^{2}},

or (which is the same)

(If)(\theta,\xi)=\theta_{n}^{k}\int\limits_{{{B}}\cap\xi}f(\theta_{n}\eta+\sqrt{1-\theta_{n}^{2}}\,\theta)\,d\eta.

(4.4)

Remark 4.1
.

A simple geometric consideration
reveals two different classes of chords. We call them
A
-chords
and
B
-chords
.
The
A
-chords correspond to

(or

{{B}}_{+}
and we set

In contrast, the
B
-chords correspond to
0<\theta_{n}<1/\sqrt{2}
(or
\pi/4>d(e_{n},\theta)<\pi/2
). They do not meet the interior of the cone

C_{0}=\{x=(x^{\prime},x_{n})\in{{B}}_{+}:x^{\prime}\in{{R}}^{n-1},\,x_{n}<1-|x^{\prime}|\},

which is a ‘blind zone’, invisible for the signals along these chords. Thus, in the following we restrict to
\theta\in{{S}}_{A+}
and pay attention to the possible singularities on the border

.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\theta\in{{S}}_{A+}
,

,

that the integral in either side of (
4.5

We denote

to

\displaystyle(\tilde{I}f)(\theta,\xi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\limits_{{{B}}^{k}}\!f_{0}(\sqrt{1-t^{2}}\,(\eta\cdot\rho^{-1}_{\theta}e_{n})+t\theta_{n})\,d\eta

\displaystyle\int\limits_{0}^{1}r^{k-1}dr\int\limits_{{{S}}^{k-1}}f_{0}(\sqrt{1-t^{2}}\,(r\sigma\cdot\rho^{-1}_{\theta}e_{n})+t\theta_{n})\,d\sigma.

. We regard the north pole
e_{n}=(0,\ldots,0,1)
as
the origin of
{{S}}
and fix the parallel of latitude

{\mathcal{P}}_{\alpha}=\{\eta=(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{n})\in{{S}}:d(e_{n},\eta)=\alpha\},\qquad 0<\alpha<\pi,

which divides
{{S}}
in two parts:

{{S}}_{\alpha}^{+}\!=\!\{\eta\in{{S}}^{n-1}:d(e_{n},\eta)<\alpha\},\quad{{S}}_{\alpha}^{-}\!=\!\{\eta\in{{S}}^{n-1}:d(e_{n},\eta)>\alpha\}.

(5.1)

The case
\alpha=\pi/2
corresponds to the equator of
{{S}}
.

Let
k
be a fixed integer,
2\leq k\leq n-1
. We denote by
T_{\alpha}\equiv T_{\alpha,n,k}
the set of all
k
-dimensional affine planes
\tau
in
{{R}}^{n}
which are tangent to
{\mathcal{P}}_{\alpha}
and meet the interior of the unit ball

|x|<1
. The corresponding collections of ‘upper’ and ‘lower’
(k-1)
-dimensional cross-sections (spherical slices)
\gamma(\tau)={{S}}^{n-1}\cap\tau
are defined by

\Gamma_{\alpha}^{\pm}=\{\gamma(\tau)\subset\overline{{{S}}_{\alpha}^{\pm}}:\,\tau\in T_{\alpha},\},

where
\overline{{{S}}_{\alpha}^{\pm}}
denote the closures of
{{S}}_{\alpha}^{\pm}
, respectively. Every cross-section
\gamma(\tau)\in\Gamma_{\alpha}^{\pm}
is a
(k-1)
-dimensional geodesic sphere centered at some point
\theta\in{{S}}_{\alpha}^{\pm}
, so we can adopt the parametrization
\gamma(\tau)=\gamma(\theta,\xi)
, where
\xi\in G_{k}(\theta^{\perp})
, the Grassmann manifold of all
k
-dimensional linear subspaces of
\theta^{\perp}
.

The sets
\Gamma_{\alpha}^{+}
and
\Gamma_{\alpha}^{-}
can be thought of as the fiber bundles with the bases
{{S}}_{\alpha}^{+}
and
{{S}}_{\alpha}^{-}
, respectively, and the canonical projection
\pi:\gamma(\theta,\xi)\to\theta
. The fiber
\pi^{-1}\theta
over
the point
\theta
is the set of all
k
-dimensional linear subspaces of
\theta^{\perp}
.
If
\beta=d(\theta,{\mathcal{P}}_{\alpha})
, then, clearly,

d(e_{n+1},\theta)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\alpha-\beta\quad\mbox{\rm if }\quad\theta\in{{S}}_{\alpha}^{+},\\
\alpha+\beta\quad\mbox{\rm if }\quad\theta\in{{S}}_{\alpha}^{-}.\end{array}\right.

(5.2)

Our main objective is the Radon-type transforms

(R^{\pm}f)(\theta,\xi)=\int\limits_{\gamma(\theta,\xi)}f(\eta)\,d_{\theta,\xi}\eta,\qquad\gamma(\theta,\xi)\in\Gamma_{\alpha}^{\pm},

(5.3)

d_{\theta,\xi}\eta
being the normalized surface area measure on
\gamma(\theta,\xi)
. These operators
are intimately related to the spherical means on
{{S}}
defined by

(M_{\theta,\xi}f)(t)=\frac{1}{\sigma_{k-1}}\int\limits_{{{S}}\cap\xi}\!\!f(\sqrt{1-t^{2}}\,\eta+t\theta)\,d\eta,\quad\xi\in G_{k}(\theta^{\perp}),\;-1\leq t\leq 1;

(5.4)

cf., e.g.,
[
16
, p. 503, 537]
and references therein for
k=n-1
.

Clearly,
(M_{\theta,\xi}f)(\pm\,1)=f(\pm\,\theta)
if
f
is good enough, and

(R^{\pm}f)(\theta,\xi)=(M_{\theta,\xi}f)(t)\quad\text{\rm if}\quad t=\cos d(\theta,{\mathcal{P}}_{\alpha})\quad(\equiv\cos\beta).

(5.5)

Our main concern is the existence of the integrals (
5.3
) for
f
belonging to Lebesgue spaces, the singularities of
f
and
R^{\pm}f
at the boundary
{\mathcal{P}}_{\alpha}
and at the poles
\pm e_{n}
, the inversion formulas for the operators
R^{\pm}
. One should take into account that these operators are
O(n-1)
-equivariant, i.e. commute with orthogonal transformations preserving the last coordinate axis.

We mainly restrict to the case when
f
is zonal (i.e.
O(n-1)
-invariant). In this case,
f(\eta)\equiv f_{0}(\eta_{n})
and
(R^{\pm}f)(\theta,\xi)\equiv\varphi^{\pm}_{0}(\theta_{n})
, where

f_{0}
and
\varphi^{\pm}_{0}
are single-variable functions on the interval
(\cos\alpha,1)
or
(-1,\cos\alpha)
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
0<\alpha<\pi
,
a=\cos\alpha
,
\beta=d(\theta,{\mathcal{P}}_{\alpha})
,
2\leq k\leq n-1
. If
f(\eta)\equiv f_{0}(\eta_{n})
, then

(R^{\pm}f)(\theta,\xi)=u_{\pm}(\theta)\,\Phi_{\pm}(v_{\pm}(\theta)),

(5.6)

where

u_{\pm}(\theta)=\frac{\Gamma(k/2)}{\pi^{1/2}}\,(\sin\beta\,\sin(\alpha\mp\beta))^{2-k},\qquad v_{\pm}(\theta)=\cos(\alpha\mp 2\beta),

\Phi_{+}(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma((k\!-\!1)/2)}\int\limits_{a}^{t}\!\!f_{1}(s)\,(t-s)^{(k-3)/2}\,ds,\quad t\in(a,1),

\Phi_{-}(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma((k\!-\!1)/2)}\int\limits_{t}^{a}\!\!f_{1}(s)\,(s-t)^{(k-3)/2}\,ds,\quad t\in(-1,a),

f_{1}(s)=f_{0}(s)|s-a|^{(k-3)/2}.

Proof.

Owing to the
O(n-1)
-invariance, it suffices to assume that

\theta=(0,\ldots,0,\theta_{n-1},\theta_{n}),\qquad\theta_{n-1}>0.

(5.7)

Let
\rho_{\theta}\in O(n)
be an orthogonal transformation which maps
e_{n}
to
\theta
and the subspace
{{R}}^{k}={{R}}e_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus{{R}}e_{k}
to
\xi\subset\theta^{\perp}
. Denoting by
{{S}}^{k-1}
the unit sphere in
{{R}}^{k}
, for
f
zonal we obtain

\displaystyle(R^{\pm}f)(\theta,\xi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma_{k-1}}\int\limits_{{{S}}^{k-1}}f(\rho_{\theta}(\sqrt{1-t^{2}}\sigma+te_{n}))\,d\sigma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma_{k-1}}\int\limits_{{{S}}^{k-1}}f_{0}(\sqrt{1-t^{2}}(\sigma\cdot\rho^{-1}_{\theta}e_{n})+t\theta_{n})\,d\sigma,

t=\cos d(\theta,{\mathcal{P}}_{\alpha})=\cos\beta
; cf. (
5.5
).

Let
p_{\theta}
be the orthogonal projection of
\rho^{-1}_{\theta}e_{n}
onto
{{R}}^{k}
. Then

\sigma\cdot\rho^{-1}_{\theta}e_{n}=|p_{\theta}|\,\sigma\cdot p^{\prime}_{\theta},\quad p^{\prime}_{\theta}=\frac{p_{\theta}}{|p_{\theta}|}\in{{S}}^{k-1},\quad|p_{\theta}|=\sin d(e_{n},\theta),

and therefore (cf.
[
16
, f. (1.12.13)]
)

(R^{\pm}f)(\theta)=\frac{\sigma_{k-2}}{\sigma_{k-1}}\int\limits_{-1}^{1}f_{0}(u\sqrt{1-t^{2}}|p_{\theta}|+t\theta_{n})\,(1-u^{2})^{(k-3)/2}\,du.

We recall that

\alpha=d(e_{n},{\mathcal{P}}_{\alpha}),\quad\beta=d(\theta,{\mathcal{P}}_{\alpha}),\quad t=\cos\beta,\quad\sqrt{1\!-\!t^{2}}=\sin\beta

(see (
5.5
)). Then

\displaystyle|p_{\theta}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sin d(e_{n},\theta)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\sin(\alpha-\beta)\quad\mbox{\rm if }\quad\theta\in{{S}}_{\alpha}^{+},\\
\sin(\alpha+\beta)\quad\mbox{\rm if }\quad\theta\in{{S}}_{\alpha}^{-};\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\theta_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cos d(e_{n},\theta)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\cos(\alpha-\beta)\quad\mbox{\rm if }\quad\theta\in{{S}}_{\alpha}^{+},\\
\cos(\alpha+\beta)\quad\mbox{\rm if }\quad\theta\in{{S}}_{\alpha}^{-}.\end{array}\right.

Let
\theta\in\Sigma_{\alpha}^{+}
. Then

(R^{+}f)(\theta,\xi)=\frac{\sigma_{k-2}}{\sigma_{k-1}}\int\limits_{-1}^{1}f_{0}(u\sin\beta\,\sin(\alpha-\beta)+\cos\beta\cos(\alpha-\beta))\,(1-u^{2})^{(k-3)/2}\,du.

Setting

u\sin\beta\,\sin(\alpha-\beta)+\cos\beta\,\cos(\alpha-\beta)=s,\qquad u=\frac{s-\cos\beta\,\cos(\alpha-\beta)}{\sin\beta\,\sin(\alpha-\beta)},

and noting that

1-u^{2}=(1-u)(1+u)=\frac{(\cos(2\beta-\alpha)-s)(s-\cos\alpha)}{\sin^{2}\beta\,\sin^{2}(\alpha-\beta)},

we obtain

(R^{+}f)(\theta,\xi)=c\,(\sin\beta\,\sin(\alpha-\beta))^{2-k}\int\limits_{\cos\alpha}^{\cos(\alpha-2\beta)}f_{1}(s)\,(\cos(\alpha-2\beta)-s)^{(k-3)/2}\,ds,

(5.14)

c=\sigma_{k-2}/\sigma_{k-1},\qquad f_{1}(s)=f_{0}(s)(s-\cos\alpha)^{(k-3)/2}

(note that
\cos(\alpha-2\beta)>\cos\alpha
).
This gives (
5.6
).

If
\theta\in{{S}}_{\alpha}^{-}
, then

(R^{-}f)(\theta)=\frac{\sigma_{k-2}}{\sigma_{k-1}}\int\limits_{-1}^{1}f_{0}(u\sin\beta\,\sin(\alpha+\beta)+\cos\beta\cos(\alpha+\beta))\,(1-u^{2})^{(k-3)/2}\,du.

We observe that
\sin(\alpha+\beta)>0
because the assumption (
5.7
) yields
0<\alpha+\beta<\pi
.
Setting

u\sin\beta\,\sin(\alpha+\beta)+\cos\beta\,\cos(\alpha+\beta)=s,\qquad u=\frac{s-\cos\beta\,\cos(\alpha+\beta)}{\sin\beta\,\sin(\alpha+\beta)},

and noting that

1-u^{2}=\frac{(s-\cos(2\beta+a))(\cos\alpha-s)}{\sin^{2}\beta\,\sin^{2}(\alpha+\beta)},

we obtain

(R^{-}f)(\theta,\xi)=c\,(\sin\beta\,\sin(\alpha+\beta))^{2-k}\int\limits^{\cos\alpha}_{\cos(\alpha+2\beta)}f_{1}(s)\,(s-\cos(\alpha+2\beta))^{(k-3)/2}\,ds,

(5.15)

c=\sigma_{k-2}/\sigma_{k-1},\qquad f_{1}(s)=f_{0}(s)(\cos\alpha-s)^{(k-3)/2}.

This gives (
5.6
) (note that
\cos(\alpha+2\beta)<\cos a
).
∎

Corollary 5.2
.

Let
f(\eta)\equiv f_{0}(\eta_{n})
,
\eta\in{{S}}^{n-1}
.
If
\alpha=\pi/2
(the equatorial tangency), then (
5.6
) holds with

u_{\pm}(\theta)=\frac{2^{k-2}\Gamma(k/2)}{\pi^{1/2}}\,(\sin 2\beta)^{2-k},\qquad v_{\pm}(\theta)=\pm\sin 2\beta,

a=0
, and
f_{1}(s)=f_{0}(s)\,|s|^{(k-3)/2}
. Alternatively, setting

b=|\theta_{n}|\sqrt{1-\theta_{n}^{2}},\qquad\tilde{c}=\frac{\Gamma(k/2)}{\pi^{1/2}\Gamma((k\!-\!1)/2)},

(cf. Lemma
4.2
) we have

(R^{+}f)(\theta,\xi)=\tilde{c}\,(2b)^{2-k}\!\!\int\limits_{0}^{2b}\!\!f_{1}(s)\,(2b\!-\!s)^{(k-3)/2}\,ds,

(5.16)

(R^{-}f)(\theta,\xi)=\tilde{c}\,(2b)^{2-k}\!\!\int\limits^{0}_{-2b}\!\!f_{1}(s)\,(s\!+\!2b)^{(k-3)/2}\,ds.

(5.17)

Lemma
5.1
and Corollary
5.2
, in conjunction with the corresponding formulas for fractional derivatives from Section
2
, can be used for establishing explicit inversion formulas for the operators
R^{\pm}
and the relevant support theorems, as it was done in Sections
3
and
4
. We leave these exercises to the interested reader.

Example 5.3
.

Let

f(\eta)=\frac{\eta_{n}^{\beta+(1-k)/2}}{(1-\eta_{n})^{\beta+(k-1)/2}},\qquad 2\leq k\leq n-1,\qquad\beta>0.

Then a straightforward calculation in (
5.16
) yields

(R^{+}f)(\theta,\xi)=c\,\frac{(2\theta_{n}\sqrt{1-\theta_{n}^{2}})^{\beta+(1-k)/2}}{(1-2\theta_{n}\sqrt{1-\theta_{n}^{2}})^{\beta}},\quad c=\frac{\Gamma(k/2)\,\Gamma(\beta)}{\pi^{1/2}\Gamma(\beta+(k\!-\!1)/2)}.

(5.18)

In particular, if
\beta=(k-1)/2
, i.e.,
f(\eta)=(1-\eta_{n})^{1-k}
,
2\leq k\leq n-1
, we have

(R^{+}f)(\theta,\xi)=2^{2-k}\,(1-2\theta_{n}\sqrt{1-\theta_{n}^{2}})^{(1-k)/2}.

(5.19)

For
0<\theta_{n}<1
, the equality
1-2\theta_{n}\sqrt{1-\theta_{n}^{2}}=0
is equivalent to
\theta_{n}=1/\sqrt{2}
. Thus the singularity of
f
at the pole
e_{n}
yields the singularity of
R^{+}f
on the cross-section

d(e_{n},\theta)=\pi/4
. The same phenomenon occurs for
R^{-}
, when
d(-e_{n},\theta)=\pi/4
, and in the general case for
d(e_{n},\theta)=\alpha/2
and
d(-e_{n},\theta)=(\pi-\alpha)/2
; cf. Example
3.4
, where similar singularities occur for tangent spherical means in the Euclidean ball.

Remark 5.4
.

Taking into account the above example, we consider the following spherical zones:

\displaystyle{{S}}_{\alpha,A}^{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\theta\in{{S}}_{\alpha}^{+}:\,d(\theta,e_{n})<\alpha/2\},

\displaystyle{{S}}_{\alpha,B}^{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\theta\in{{S}}_{\alpha}^{+}:\,\alpha/2<d(\theta,e_{n})<\alpha\},

\displaystyle{{S}}_{\alpha,A}^{-}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\theta\in{{S}}_{\alpha}^{-}:\,(\pi-\alpha)/2<d(\theta,-e_{n})<(\pi-\alpha)/2\},

\displaystyle{{S}}_{\alpha,B}^{-}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\theta\in{{S}}_{\alpha}^{-}:\,(\pi-\alpha)/2<d(\theta,-e_{n})<\pi-\alpha\}.

Let

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\alpha,A}^{\pm}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\gamma(\theta,\xi):\,\theta\in{{S}}_{\alpha,A}^{\pm},\,\xi\in G_{k}(\theta^{\perp})\}\quad\text{\rm(the set of `A-slices')},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\alpha,B}^{\pm}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\gamma(\theta,\xi):\,\theta\in{{S}}_{\alpha,B}^{\pm},\,\xi\in G_{k}(\theta^{\perp})\}\quad\text{\rm(the set of `B-slices')}.

The radii of ‘A-slices’ are greater than the radii of ‘B-slices’. However, each collection covers the entire region
{{S}}_{\alpha}^{+}
or
{{S}}_{\alpha}^{-}
and might be sufficient for reconstruction of
f:{{S}}_{\alpha}^{\pm}\to{{C}}
from
R^{\pm}f
. The separating subspheres
d(\theta,e_{n})=\alpha/2
and
d(\theta,-e_{n})=(\pi-\alpha)/2
may contain singularities of
(R^{\pm}f)(\theta,\xi)
.

5.2.
Weighted equalities

Let, for simplicity,
\alpha=\pi/2
(the equatorial tangency) and denote

{{S}}^{\pm}={{S}}_{\pi/2}^{\pm},\qquad\Gamma^{\pm}=\Gamma_{\pi/2}^{\pm},

{{S}}_{B}^{\pm}=\{\theta\in{{S}}^{\pm}:\,|\theta_{n}|<1/\sqrt{2}\},\quad{{S}}_{A}^{\pm}=\{\theta\in{{S}}^{\pm}:\,1/\sqrt{2}<|\theta_{n}|<1\},

\Gamma_{B}^{\pm}=\{\gamma(\theta,\xi)\in\Gamma^{\pm}:\,\theta\in{{S}}_{B}^{\pm},\;\xi\in G_{k}(\theta^{\perp})\},

\Gamma_{A}^{\pm}=\{\gamma(\theta,\xi)\in\Gamma^{\pm}:\,\theta\in{{S}}_{A}^{\pm},\;\xi\in G_{k}(\theta^{\perp})\}.

The subbundles
\Gamma_{B}^{\pm}
consist of ‘smaller’ spherical slices of radius
<\pi/4
, while
\Gamma_{A}^{\pm}
are composed by slices of radius greater than
\pi/4
and less than
\pi/2
.
We equip
\Gamma_{B}^{\pm}
and
\Gamma_{A}^{\pm}
with the product measure
d\theta d_{*}\xi
, where
d\theta
is the usual surface area measure on
{{S}}_{B}^{\pm}
and

{{S}}_{A}^{\pm}
, respectively, and
d_{*}\xi
is the Haar probability measure on the Grassmannian
G_{k}(\theta^{\perp})
.

Clearly,

\gamma(\theta,\xi)\!\in\!\Gamma_{A}^{\pm}\Longleftrightarrow\gamma(-\theta,\xi)\!\in\!\Gamma_{A}^{\mp},\quad\gamma(\theta,\xi)\!\in\!\Gamma_{B}^{\pm}\Longleftrightarrow\gamma(-\theta,\xi)\!\in\!\Gamma_{B}^{\mp},

and therefore,

(R^{\pm}f)(\theta,\xi)=(R^{\mp}\check{f})(-\theta,\xi),\qquad\check{f}(\eta)=f(-\eta).

Thus we can restrict our consideration to the integrals
(R^{+}f)(\theta,\xi)
corresponding to
\theta\in{{S}}_{A}^{+}
or
\theta\in{{S}}_{B}^{+}
.

Lemma 5.5
.

Given a function
u_{0}(y)
,
y\in(0,1)
, let

\displaystyle u(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(2s)^{k-2}(1-s^{2})^{(k-n)/2}(1-2s^{2})\,u_{0}(2s\sqrt{1-s^{2}}),\qquad 0<s<1,

\displaystyle v(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathchar 1403\relax(s)\int\limits_{s}^{1}(y-s)^{(k-3)/2}\,u_{0}(y)\,dy,

(5.20)

where

\mathchar 1403\relax(s)=\frac{\Gamma(k/2)}{2\pi^{1/2}\Gamma((k\!-\!1)/2)}s^{(k-3)/2}(1-s^{2})^{(3-n)/2}.

(5.21)

If
X^{+}
denotes either
\Gamma_{A}^{+}
or
\Gamma_{B}^{+}
, then in both cases,

\int\limits_{X^{+}}(R^{+}f)(\theta,\xi)\,u(\theta_{n})\,d\theta d_{*}\xi=\int\limits_{{{S}}^{+}}f(\eta)\,v(\eta_{n})\,d\eta,

(5.22)

provided that either side of this equality exists in the Lebesgue sense.

Proof.

Suppose
X^{+}=\Gamma_{B}^{+}
and let
\rho\in O(n-1)
be an orthogonal transformation of
{{R}}^{n}
preserving the last coordinate axis. Denoting by
I
the left-hand side of (
5.22
) and changing variable
\theta\to\rho\theta
, after integration over all
\rho\in O(n-1)
we obtain

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\limits_{O(n-1)}\!\!d\rho\int\limits_{{{S}}_{B}^{+}}u(\theta_{n})\,d\theta\int\limits_{G_{k}(\rho\theta^{\perp})}\!\!(R^{+}f)(\rho\theta,\xi)\,d_{*}\xi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\limits_{{{S}}_{B}^{+}}u(\theta_{n})\,d\theta\int\limits_{G_{k}(\theta^{\perp})}\!\!\Big(R^{+}\Big[\int\limits_{O(n-1)}\!\!(f\circ\rho)\,d\rho\Big]\Big)(\theta,\xi)\,d_{*}\xi

An expression in the square brackets is an
O(n-1)
-average of the function
f(\eta)
,
\eta=(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{n})\in{{S}}^{+}
, the precise meaning of which is

\displaystyle\int\limits_{O(n-1)}\!\!f(\sqrt{1-\eta_{n}^{2}}\,\rho\,e_{n-1}+\eta_{n}e_{n})\,d\rho

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma_{n-2}}\int\limits_{S^{n-2}}f(\sqrt{1-\eta_{n}^{2}}\,\omega+\eta_{n}e_{n})\,d\omega

(5.23)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}

\displaystyle f_{0}(\eta_{n}).

Hence, by (
5.16
), the slice integration (see, e.g.,
[
16
, f. (1.12.13)]
) yields

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\limits_{{{S}}_{B}^{+}}u(\theta_{n})\,\Big[\tilde{c}\,\tau^{2-k}\!\!\int\limits_{0}^{\tau}\!\!f_{1}(s)\,(\tau\!-\!s)^{(k-3)/2}\,ds\Big]_{\tau=2\theta_{n}\sqrt{1-\theta_{n}^{2}}}\,d\theta

(5.24)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{c}\,\sigma_{n-2}\int\limits_{0}^{1/\sqrt{2}}(1-t^{2})^{(n-3)/2}\,u(t)\,(2t\sqrt{1-t^{2}})^{2-k}\,dt

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\int\limits_{0}^{2t\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}\!\!f_{1}(s)\,(2t\sqrt{1-t^{2}}-s)^{(k-3)/2}\,ds,\quad f_{1}(s)=f_{0}(s)\,s^{(k-3)/2}.

Changing variable
y=2t\sqrt{1-t^{2}}
, so that

\frac{dy}{dt}=\frac{2(1-2t^{2})}{\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}>0,\qquad t^{2}=\frac{1-\sqrt{1-y^{2}}}{2},

(5.25)

we obtain

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{c}\,\sigma_{n-2}}{2}\int\limits_{0}^{1}\Bigg(\frac{1+\sqrt{1-y^{2}}}{2}\Bigg)^{(n-2)/2}\,u\Bigg(\sqrt{\frac{1-\sqrt{1-y^{2}}}{2}}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\frac{y^{2-k}}{\sqrt{1-y^{2}}}\,dy\int\limits_{0}^{y}\!\!f_{1}(s)\,(y\!-\!s)^{(k-3)/2}\,ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma_{n-2}\int\limits_{0}^{1}f_{1}(s)A(s)\,ds,\quad A(s)=\frac{\tilde{c}}{2}\int\limits_{s}^{1}(y\!-\!s)^{(k-3)/2}\,u_{0}(y))\,dy,

u_{0}(y)=\Bigg(\frac{1+\sqrt{1-y^{2}}}{2}\Bigg)^{(n-2)/2}u\Bigg(\sqrt{\frac{1-\sqrt{1-y^{2}}}{2}}\Bigg)\,\frac{y^{2-k}}{\sqrt{1-y^{2}}}.

(5.26)

Hence, by (
5.23
),

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma_{n-2}\int\limits_{0}^{1}f_{0}(s)\,s^{(k-3)/2}A(s)\,ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\limits_{0}^{1}s^{(k-3)/2}A(s)\,ds\int\limits_{S^{n-2}}f(\sqrt{1-s^{2}}\,\omega+se_{n})\,d\omega

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\limits_{{{S}}^{+}}\!\!f(\eta)\,v(\eta_{n})\,d\eta,

where

\displaystyle v(\eta_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta_{n}^{(k-3)/2}(1\!-\!\eta_{n}^{2})^{(3-n)/2}A(\eta_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{c}}{2}\,\eta_{n}^{(k-3)/2}(1\!-\!\eta_{n}^{2})^{(3-n)/2}\int\limits_{\eta_{n}}^{1}(y\!-\!\eta_{n})^{(k-3)/2}\,u_{0}(y)\,dy.

It remains to note that (
5.26
) can be inverted as

u(s)=(2s)^{k-2}(1-s^{2})^{(k-n)/2}(1-2s^{2})\,u_{0}(2s\sqrt{1-s^{2}}),

and (
5.22
) follows.

If
X^{+}=\Gamma_{A}^{+}
, the reasoning follows the same lines. The only difference is that integration over
{{S}}_{B}^{+}
is replaced by integration over
{{S}}_{A}^{+}
, the integral in (
5.24
) is taken over
the interval
(1/\sqrt{2},1)
, and the derivative
dy/dt
in (
5.25
) is negative. All the rest remains unchanged.
∎

Example 5.6
.

Setting
u_{0}\equiv 1
in Lemma
5.5
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\Gamma_{B}^{+}}(R^{+}f)(\theta,\xi)\,\theta_{n}^{k-2}(1-\theta_{n}^{2})^{(k-n)/2}(1-2\theta_{n}^{2})\,d\theta d_{*}\xi

\displaystyle=c\int\limits_{{{S}}^{+}}f(\eta)\,\eta_{n}^{(k-3)/2}(1+\eta_{n})^{(3-n)/2}(1-\eta_{n})^{1+(k-n)/2}d\eta,

(5.27)

c=\frac{\Gamma(k/2)}{2^{k-1}\pi^{1/2}\Gamma((k\!+\!1)/2)}.

In particular, for equatorial tangent circles on the
2
-sphere in
{{R}}^{3}
we have

\int\limits_{{{S}}_{B}^{+}}\!(R^{+}f)(\theta)\,(1\!-\!\theta_{3}^{2})^{-1/2}(1\!-\!2\theta_{3}^{2})\,d\theta=\frac{1}{\pi}\int\limits_{{{S}}^{+}}\!\!f(\eta)\,\eta_{3}^{-1/2}(1\!-\!\eta_{3})^{1/2}d\eta.

(5.28)

These formulas remain true if
\Gamma_{B}^{+}
and
{{S}}_{B}^{+}
are replaced by
\Gamma_{A}^{+}
and
{{S}}_{A}^{+}
, respectively.

6.
Hyperbolic Slices

6.1.
Preliminaries

Let
{{E}}^{n,1}
,
n\geq 2
, be the pseudo-Euclidean space, which is an
(n+1)
-dimensional real vector space of points
in
{{R}}^{n+1}
with the inner product

[{\bf x},{\bf y}]=-x_{1}y_{1}-\ldots-x_{n}y_{n}+x_{n+1}y_{n+1}.

(6.1)

We denote by
\ e_{1},\ldots,e_{n+1}
the coordinate unit vectors in
{{E}}^{n,1}
. The
n
-dimensional real hyperbolic space
{{H}}^{n}
will be realized as the upper sheet of the two-sheeted hyperboloid in
{{E}}^{n,1}
, that is,

{{H}}^{n}=\{{\bf x}\in{{E}}^{n,1}:[{\bf x},{\bf x}]=1,\ x_{n+1}>0\}.

All results of this section can be reformulated for other models of
{{H}}^{n}
by making use of the corresponding transition formulas; see, e.g.,
[
2
]
.

In the following, the points of
{{H}}^{n}
will be denoted by the non-boldfaced letters, unlike the generic points in
{{E}}^{n,1}
.
The geodesic distance between the points
x,y\in{{H}}^{n}
is defined by
d(x,y)=\cosh^{-1}[x,y]
.
The point
x_{0}=(0,\ldots,0,1)\in{{H}}^{n}
, which is identified with the unit vector
e_{n+1}
, serves as the origin of
{{H}}^{n}
. The notation

G=SO_{0}(n,1)

is used for the identity component of the special pseudo-orthogonal group
SO(n,1)
preserving the bilinear form (
6.1
). We denote by
K\sim SO(n)
the subgroup of
G
, which consists of rotations about the
x_{n+1}
-axis.

Every point

x\in{{H}}^{n}
is represented in the hyperbolic coordinates
(\theta,r)\in S^{n-1}\times[0,\infty)
as

x=\theta\,\sinh r+e_{n+1}\,\cosh r.

(6.2)

A
G
-invariant measure
dx
on
{{H}}^{n}
has the following form in the coordinates (
6.2
):

dx=\sinh^{n-1}r\,drd\theta.

(6.3)

The Haar measure
dg
on
G
is accordingly normalized by the formula

\int\limits_{G}f(gx_{0})\,dg=\int\limits_{{{H}}^{n}}f(x)\,dx.

(6.4)

Given a point
x\in{{H}}^{n}
and a number
t>1
, consider the planar section (or
the hyperbolic slice
)

\gamma_{x}(t)=\{y\in{{H}}^{n}:[x,y]=t\},

(6.5)

which is a geodesic sphere in
{{H}}^{n}
of radius
r=\cosh^{-1}t
with center
at
x
. The corresponding spherical mean of a function
f
is defined by

(M_{x}f)(t)={(t^{2}-1)^{(1-n)/2}\over\sigma_{n-1}}\int\limits_{\gamma_{x}(t)}f(y)\,d\sigma(y),

(6.6)

where
d\sigma(y)

stands for the relevant induced Lebesgue measure.
More information about spherical means (
6.6
) can be found, e.g., in
[
16
, pp. 370, 443]
.

Let
\omega_{x}\in G
be a hyperbolic rotation which maps
e_{n+1}
to
x
. Changing variables and setting
f_{x}(y)=f(\omega_{x}y)
, we have

(M_{x}f)(t)=\int\limits_{{{S}}^{n-1}}f_{x}(\theta\,\sinh r+e_{n+1}\,\cosh r)\,d_{*}\theta,\quad t=\cosh r,

(6.7)

where
d_{*}\theta
stands for the probability measure on
{{S}}^{n-1}
.

Our main concern is the spherical means
(M_{x}f)(t)
over geodesic spheres which are tangent to a fixed horizontal cross-section

\Sigma_{\alpha}=\{x\in{{H}}^{n}:d(e_{n+1},x)=\alpha\},\qquad 0<\alpha<\infty.

(6.8)

The latter divides the hyperboloid
{{H}}^{n}
in two parts:

\Sigma_{\alpha}^{+}\!=\!\{x\in{{H}}^{n}:d(e_{n+1},x)<\alpha\},\quad\Sigma_{\alpha}^{-}\!=\!\{x\in{{H}}^{n}:d(e_{n+1},x)>\alpha\}.

The planar section
\gamma_{x}(t)
is tangent to
\Sigma_{\alpha}
if and only if
d(x,y)=d(x,\Sigma_{\alpha})
for all
y\in\gamma_{x}(t)
, that is,
t=\cosh d(x,\Sigma_{\alpha})
. In this case, we denote

(Mf)(x)=(M_{x}f)(\cosh d(x,\Sigma_{\alpha}))

(6.9)

and set

\gamma^{\pm}(x)=\Gamma_{x}(\cosh d(x,\Sigma_{\alpha}))\;\,\text{\rm if}\;\,x\in\Sigma_{\alpha}^{\pm},\,\;\text{\rm respectively.}

A natural lower dimensional analogue of the spherical mean
(Mf)(x)
can be defined as follows. Let
G_{n,k}
be the Grassmann manifold of all
k
-dimensional linear subspaces of
{{R}}^{n}=e_{n+1}^{\perp}
,
2\leq k\leq n
(if
k=1
, then, as we shall see below, the corresponding cross-sections of one-dimensional hyperboloids reduce to pairs of points). Given a subspace
\xi\in G_{n,k}
, consider the
k
-dimensional sub-hyperboloid

{{H}}^{k}_{\xi}={{H}}^{n}\cap\{\xi\oplus{{R}}e_{n+1}\}

(6.10)

and denote by

\Sigma_{\alpha,k}(\xi)=\Sigma_{\alpha}\cap{{H}}^{k}_{\xi},\qquad\Sigma_{\alpha,k}^{\pm}(\xi)=\Sigma_{\alpha}^{\pm}\cap{{H}}^{k}_{\xi}

(6.11)

the corresponding restricted domains. Given
x\in\Sigma_{\alpha,k}^{\pm}(\xi)
, we define

\gamma^{\pm}(\xi,x)=\{y\in{{H}}^{k}_{\xi}:[x,y]=\cosh d(x,\Sigma_{\alpha,k}(\xi))=\cosh d(x,\Sigma_{\alpha})\}.

The sets

\Gamma_{\alpha}^{\pm}=\{\gamma^{\pm}(\xi,x):\,\xi\in G_{n,k},\;x\in\Sigma_{\alpha,k}^{\pm}(\xi)\}

(6.12)

are the relevant collections of all
(k-1)
-dimensional
\Sigma_{\alpha}
-tangent slices in the respective domains
\Sigma_{\alpha}^{\pm}
.
They can be thought of as the fiber bundles with the base
G_{n,k}
and the canonical projections
\pi_{\pm}:\gamma^{\pm}(\xi,x)\to\xi
. The fibers
\pi_{\pm}^{-1}\xi
over
the point
\xi
are the sets of all
x\in\Sigma_{\alpha,k}^{\pm}(\xi)
, respectively.

Our main objective is the Radon-type slice transforms

({\mathcal{H}}^{\pm}f)(\xi,x)=\int\limits_{\gamma^{\pm}(\xi,x))}\!\!\!f(y)\,d_{\xi,x}y,

(6.13)

d_{\xi,x}y
being the corresponding surface area measure. The operators (
6.13
) are obviously
K
-equivariant, i.e.,

({\mathcal{H}}^{\pm}f)(\mathchar 1403\relax\xi,\mathchar 1403\relax x)=({\mathcal{H}}^{\pm}[f\circ\mathchar 1403\relax])(\xi,x)\quad\forall\mathchar 1403\relax\in K\sim SO(n).

(6.14)

6.2.
Slice Transforms of Zonal Functions

We restrict our consideration to the case when
f
is zonal (or
K
-invariant). In this case,
f(y)=f_{0}(y_{n+1})
for some single-variable function
f_{0}
on
[1,\infty)
and
({\mathcal{H}}^{\pm}f)(\xi,x)
are single-variable functions of
x_{n+1}
, i.e., the operators (
6.13
) are, in fact, one-dimensional. Our aim is to obtain explicit expressions for these operators and study their properties.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
0\leq\alpha<\infty
,
\beta=d(x,\Sigma_{\alpha})
,
a=\cosh\alpha
. If
f(y)=f_{0}(y_{n+1})
,
2\leq k\leq n
, then

({\mathcal{H}}^{\pm}f)(\xi,x)=u_{\pm}(x)\Phi_{\pm}(v_{\pm}(x)),

(6.15)

where

u_{\pm}(x)=\frac{\Gamma(k/2)}{\pi^{1/2}}\,[\,\sinh\beta\,\sinh(\alpha\mp\beta)]^{2-k},\qquad v_{\pm}(x)=\cosh(2\beta\mp\alpha),

\Phi_{+}(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma((k\!-\!1)/2)}\int\limits^{a}_{t}\!\!f_{+}(s)\,(s-t)^{(k-3)/2}\,ds,\quad t\in(1,a),

(6.16)

\Phi_{-}(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma((k\!-\!1)/2)}\int\limits_{a}^{t}\!\!f_{-}(s)\,(t-s)^{(k-3)/2}\,ds,\quad t\in(1,a),

(6.17)

f_{\pm}(s)=f_{0}(s)(a\mp s)^{(k-3)/2}.

It is assumed
that the respective integrals in (
6.16
) and (
6.17
) exist in the Lebesgue sense.

Proof.

Choose
\xi={{R}}^{k}={{R}}e_{n-k+1}\oplus\ldots\oplus{{R}}e_{n}
and consider the sub-hyperboloid

{{H}}^{k}={{H}}^{n}\cap\{{{R}}^{k}\oplus{{R}}e_{n+1}\}.

Let
\Sigma_{\alpha,k}=\Sigma_{\alpha}\cap{{H}}^{k}
,
\Sigma_{\alpha,k}^{\pm}=\Sigma_{\alpha}^{\pm}\cap{{H}}^{k}
; cf. (
6.10
), (
6.11
).
Given a
(k-1)
-slice
\gamma^{+}(\xi,x)
(or
\gamma^{-}(\xi,x)
), let
\mathchar 1403\relax_{\xi,x}\in K
be a rotation which maps
\xi\in G_{n,k}
to
{{R}}^{k}
and a point
x
in
\Sigma_{\alpha,k}^{+}(\xi)

(or in
\Sigma_{\alpha,k}^{-}(\xi)
) to some point

\tilde{x}=(0,\ldots,0,\tilde{x}_{n},x_{n+1})
in
\Sigma_{\alpha,k}^{+}
(or in
\Sigma_{\alpha,k}^{-}
) with
\tilde{x}_{n}>0
and unchanged last coordinate
x_{n+1}
. Then, by (
6.14
), for
f
zonal we obtain

({\mathcal{H}}^{+}f)(\xi,x)=({\mathcal{H}}^{+}f)({{R}}^{k},\tilde{x})\qquad\text{\rm(or $({\mathcal{H}}^{-}f)(\xi,x)=({\mathcal{H}}^{-}f)({{R}}^{k},\tilde{x})$)}.

Hence, as in (
6.7
), for
x\in\Sigma_{a,k}^{\pm}
we can write

\displaystyle({\mathcal{H}}^{\pm}f)(\xi,x)=\int\limits_{{{S}}^{k-1}}f(\mathchar 1403\relax_{\xi,x}(\theta\,\sinh\beta+e_{n+1}\cosh\beta))\,d_{*}\theta

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{{{S}}^{k-1}}\!\!\!f_{0}((\theta\cdot\mathchar 1403\relax_{\xi,x}^{-1}e_{n+1})\,\sinh\beta+x_{n+1}\,\cosh\beta)\,d_{*}\theta,

where
{{S}}^{k-1}
is the unit sphere in
{{R}}^{k}
,
\beta=d(\tilde{x},\Sigma_{\alpha})=d(x,\Sigma_{\alpha})
.

Let
p_{\xi,x}
be the orthogonal projection of
\mathchar 1403\relax_{\xi,x}^{-1}e_{n+1}
onto
{{R}}^{k}
. Then

\theta\cdot\mathchar 1403\relax_{\xi,x}^{-1}e_{n+1}=|p_{\xi,x}|\,\theta\cdot p^{\prime}_{\xi,x},\qquad p^{\prime}_{\xi,x}=\frac{p_{\xi,x}}{|p_{\xi,x}|}\in{{S}}^{k-1},

and therefore (cf.
[
16
, f. (1.12.13)]
)

({\mathcal{H}}^{\pm}f)(\xi,x)=\frac{\sigma_{k-2}}{\sigma_{k-1}}\int\limits_{-1}^{1}f_{0}(u\,|p_{\xi,x}|\,\sinh\beta+x_{n+1}\,\cosh\beta)\,(1\!-\!u^{2})^{(k-3)/2}\,du.

(6.18)

The expression
|p^{\prime}_{\xi,x}|
can be evaluated as

|p^{\prime}_{\xi,x}|\!=\sinh d(e_{n+1},x)\!=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\!\sinh(\alpha+\beta),\;\beta>0,\;\mbox{\rm if }\;x\in\Sigma_{\alpha}^{+},\\
\!\sinh(\alpha-\beta),\;0<\beta<\alpha,\;\mbox{\rm if }\;x\in\Sigma_{\alpha}^{-};\end{array}\right.\qquad

(6.19)

cf. the arcs of the hyperbola on the
(x_{n},x_{n+1})
-plane.

By (
6.18
)-(
6.19
),

\displaystyle({\mathcal{H}}^{+}f)(\xi,x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{k-2}}{\sigma_{k-1}}\int\limits_{-1}^{1}f_{0}(u\,\sinh\beta\,\sinh(\alpha-\beta)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\cosh\beta\cosh(\alpha-\beta))\,(1\!-\!u^{2})^{(k-3)/2}\,du.

We set

u\sinh\beta\,\sinh(\alpha-\beta)+\cosh\beta\cosh(\alpha-\beta)=s,

u=\frac{s-\cosh\beta\cosh(\alpha-\beta)}{\sinh\beta\,\sinh(\alpha-\beta)},\qquad 1-u^{2}=\frac{(s-\cosh(2\beta-\alpha))(\cosh\alpha-s)}{[\sinh\beta\,\sinh(\alpha-\beta)]^{2}}.

This gives

\displaystyle({\mathcal{H}}^{+}f)(\xi,x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{k-2}}{\sigma_{k-1}}\int\limits^{\cosh\alpha}_{\cosh(2\beta-\alpha)}\!\!\!f_{0}(s)[s-\cosh(2\beta-\alpha)]^{(k-3)/2}(\cosh\alpha-s)^{(k-3)/2}

(6.20)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\frac{ds}{[\,\sinh\beta\,\sinh(\alpha-\beta)]^{k-2}},

and (
6.15
) follows.

For
({\mathcal{H}}^{-}f)(\xi,x)
the reasoning is similar. In this case,

\displaystyle({\mathcal{H}}^{-}f)(\xi,x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{k-2}}{\sigma_{k-1}}\int\limits_{-1}^{1}f_{0}(u\,\sinh\beta\,\sinh(\alpha+\beta)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\cosh\beta\cosh(\alpha+\beta))\,(1\!-\!u^{2})^{(k-3)/2}\,du,

and the change of variables

u\sinh\beta\,\sinh(\alpha+\beta)+\cosh\beta\cosh(\alpha+\beta)=s

gives the desired result.
∎

Remark 6.2
.

We observe that in (
6.20
), we distinguish two cases for
\beta=d(x,\Sigma_{\alpha})
:

(a)
0<\beta<\alpha/2
, i.e.,
2\beta-\alpha<0
(small slices, which don’t reach the origin
e_{n+1}
), and

(b)
\alpha/2<\beta<a
, i.e.,
2\beta-\alpha>0
(big slices, which cut off some neighborhood of
e_{n+1}
).

In both cases the integral (
6.20
) has the same form, owing to the evenness of the
\cosh
-function.

The following corollary yields the result for slices through the origin (the case
\alpha=0
).

Corollary 6.3
.

If
f(y)=f_{0}(y_{n+1})
,
2\leq k\leq n
,
x\in{{H}}^{n}
,
\beta=d(x,e_{n+1})
,
\xi\in G_{n,k}
, then

({\mathcal{H}}^{-}f)(\xi,x)=\frac{c}{(\sinh r)^{2k-4}}\!\!\int\limits^{\cosh 2\beta}_{1}\!\!\!\!f_{-}(s)(\cosh 2\beta-s)^{(k-3)/2}ds,

(6.21)

c=\frac{\Gamma(k/2)}{\pi^{1/2}\,\Gamma((k-1)/2)},\qquad f_{-}(s)=f_{0}(s)\,(s+1)^{(k-3)/2},

provided that the integral on the right-hand side exists in the Lebesgue sense.

As in the previous sections, Theorem
6.1
yields explicit inversion formulas for the operators
{\mathcal{H}}^{\pm}
on zonal functions, as well as the relevant support theorems and weighted equalities. We leave these exercises to the interested reader. An analogue of Corollary
6.3
for zonal functions on the unit sphere
{{S}}^{n}
in
{{R}}^{n+1}
and
(n-1)
-dimensional spherical slices through the north pole was obtained by
Abouelaz and Daher
[
1
]
. The results from
[
1
]
were extended to arbitrary (not necessarily zonal) functions by Helgason
[
11
, p. 145]
(for
n=2
) and by the author
[
17
]
(for any
n
and
k
). Our consideration of the hyperbolic slices through the origin seems to be new.

7.
Tangent Spheres in the Half-Space

7.1.
Transition to Fractional Integrals

Let
{{R}}^{n}_{+}=\{x=(x^{\prime},x_{n}):x^{\prime}\in{{R}}^{n-1},\,x_{n}>0\}
. Given an integer
k
,
1\leq k\leq n-1
, let
\Omega
be a family of all
k
-dimensional spheres in the closed half-plane
\bar{{R}}^{n}_{+}={{R}}^{n}_{+}\cup\{x:x_{n}=0\}
, which are tangent to the boundary
x_{n}=0
and lie in some vertical
(k+1)
-dimensional affine plane. The corresponding spherical means are defined by

(Jf)(\omega)=\int\limits_{\omega}f(y)\,d_{\omega}y,\qquad\omega\in\Omega,

(7.1)

where
d_{\omega}y
is the normalized surface area measure on
\omega
. Our aim is to reconstruct a function
f
on
{{R}}^{n}_{+}
from its spherical means (
7.1
).

To parameterize the spheres
\omega\in\Omega
, let
x=(x^{\prime},x_{n})
in
{{R}}^{n}_{+}
be the center of
\omega
. Then
every vertical
(k+1)
-plane
\zeta
passing through
x
and containing
\omega
has the form

\zeta=(\xi+x^{\prime})\oplus{{R}}e_{n}
, where
\xi
belongs to the Grassmannian
G_{k}({{R}}^{n-1})
of
k
-dimensional linear subspaces of
{{R}}^{n-1}
. Thus we can write

\omega\equiv\omega(x,\xi)=\{y\in(\xi+x^{\prime})\oplus{{R}}e_{n}:\;|y-x|=x_{n}\},

and the integral (
7.1
) can be represented as

(Jf)(x,\xi)=\frac{1}{\sigma_{k}}\int\limits_{{{S}}^{k}_{\xi}}f(x+x_{n}\theta)\,d\theta,

(7.2)

x\in{{R}}^{n}_{+},\qquad\xi\in G_{k}({{R}}^{n-1}),\qquad{{S}}^{k}_{\xi}={{S}}^{n-1}\cap(\xi\oplus{{R}}e_{n}).

If
f
is invariant under horizontal translations, i.e.,
f(y)=f_{0}(y_{n})
for some single-variable function
f_{0}
, then

(Jf)(x,\xi)=\frac{1}{\sigma_{k}}\int\limits_{{{S}}^{k}_{\xi}}f_{0}(x_{n}+x_{n}\theta_{n})\,d\theta.

(7.3)

The next lemma represents (
7.3
) by the one-dimensional Riemann-Liouville fractional integral.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
f(y)=f_{0}(y_{n})
,
y\in{{R}}^{n}_{+}
,
f_{1}(s)=f_{0}(s)s^{(k-2)/2}
,
1\leq k\leq n-1
. Then for all
x\in{{R}}^{n}_{+}
and
\xi\in G_{k}({{R}}^{n-1})
,

(Jf)(x,\xi)=\Phi(x_{n}),\qquad\Phi(x_{n})=\frac{\Gamma((k+1)/2)}{\pi^{1/2}}\,x_{n}^{1-k}\,F(2x_{n}),

(7.4)

where

F(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(k/2)}\int\limits_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{(k-2)/2}f_{1}(s)\,ds=(I_{0+}^{k/2}f_{1})(t).

(7.5)

It is assumed
that the integral (
7.5
) exists in the Lebesgue sense.

Proof.

Owing to (
7.3
), the slice integration (see, e.g.,
[
16
, f. (1.12.13)]
) yields

\displaystyle(Jf)(x,\xi)=\frac{\sigma_{k-1}}{\sigma_{k}}\int\limits_{-1}^{1}f_{0}(x_{n}(1+t))\,(1\!-\!t^{2})^{(k-2)/2}dt.

Changing variable
x_{n}(1+t)=s
, we obtain

(Jf)(x,\xi)=\frac{\sigma_{k-1}\,x_{n}^{1-k}}{\sigma_{k}}\int\limits_{0}^{2x_{n}}f_{0}(s)\,s^{(k-2)/2}\,(2x_{n}-s)^{(k-2)/2}\,ds,

(7.6)

which gives the result.
∎

The above lemma yields the following inversion result, also containing information about the supports of
Jf
and
f
.

Theorem 7.2
.

Let
1\leq k\leq n-1
,
f(y)=f_{0}(y_{n})
,
y\in{{R}}^{n}_{+}
, so that
(Jf)(x,\xi)=\Phi_{+}(x_{n})
, and therefore

F(t)=\frac{\pi^{1/2}}{\Gamma((k+1)/2)}\,\left(\frac{t}{2}\right)^{k-1}\Phi(t/2).

If

\int\limits_{0}^{a}|f_{0}(s)|\,s^{(k-2)/2}\,ds<\infty\quad\text{for every}\quad a>0,

(7.7)

then

can be reconstructed for almost all

f_{0}(s)=s^{(2-k)/2}(\mathcal{D}^{k/2}_{0+}F)(s),

where the fractional derivative
\mathcal{D}^{k/2}_{0+}F
is defined by (

). If, moreover,
a>0
and

for all
0<x_{n}<a

f(y)=0
for all

.

7.2.
Weighted Equalities

The next statement of the duality type resembles Lemmas
3.9
,
3.16
,
5.5
and paves the way to the

in (

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
1\leq k\leq n-1
.
Given a function
u(t)
,
t>0
, we set

v(s)=c\,s^{(k-2)/2}\int\limits_{s/2}^{\infty}u(t)\,(t-s/2)^{(k-2)/2}\,\frac{dt}{t^{k-1}},\quad s>0,

(7.8)

c=2^{(k-2)/2}\sigma_{k-1}/\sigma_{k}
. Then

\int\limits_{{{R}}^{n}_{+}\times G_{k}({{R}}^{n-1})}\!\!\!(Jf)(x,\xi)\,u(x_{n})\,dxd_{*}\xi=\int\limits_{{{R}}^{n}_{+}}f(x)\,v(x_{n})\,dx,

(7.9)

provided that either side of this equality exists in the Lebesgue sense.

Proof.

We denote by
I
the left-hand side of (
7.8
) and write
(Jf)(x,\xi)=(Jf)(x^{\prime},x_{n};\xi)
. Then

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\limits_{O(n-1)}d\gamma\int\limits_{{{R}}^{n-1}}dx^{\prime}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}u(x_{n})\,(Jf)(x^{\prime},x_{n};\gamma{{R}}^{k})\,dx_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma_{k}}\int\limits_{O(n-1)}d\gamma\int\limits_{{{R}}^{n-1}}dx^{\prime}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}u(x_{n})dx_{n}\int\limits_{{{S}}^{k}_{\gamma{{R}}^{k}}}f(x^{\prime}+x_{n}\theta^{\prime},x_{n}(1+\theta_{n}))\,d\theta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma_{k}}\int\limits_{O(n-1)}d\gamma\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}u(x_{n})\,dx_{n}\!\!\int\limits_{{{S}}^{n-1}\cap(\gamma{{R}}^{k}\oplus{{R}}e_{n})}\!\!d\theta\int\limits_{{{R}}^{n-1}}f(y^{\prime},x_{n}(1+\theta_{n}))\,dy^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{k-1}}{\sigma_{k}}\int\limits_{{{R}}^{n-1}}dy^{\prime}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}u(x_{n})\,dx_{n}\int\limits_{-1}^{1}(1-t^{2})^{(k-2)/2}f(y^{\prime},x_{n}(1+t))\,dt.

Setting

and interchanging integrals, we obtain

I=\frac{2^{(k-2)/2}\sigma_{k-1}}{\sigma_{k}}\!\int\limits_{{{R}}^{n-1}}\!dy^{\prime}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}f(y^{\prime},s)\,s^{(k-2)/2}ds\int\limits_{s/2}^{\infty}u(x_{n})\,(x_{n}-s/2)^{(k-2)/2}\,\frac{dx_{n}}{x_{n}^{k-1}}.

It remains to change the notation, and we are done.
∎

Example 7.4

In particular, for

7.3.
The Fourier Transform Approach and Fractional Integrals with Bessel Functions in the Kernels

In this section we restrict to the case
k=n-1
, when
\xi={{R}}^{n-1}
and the integral (
7.2
) becomes

(Jf)(x)=\frac{1}{\sigma_{n-1}}\int\limits_{{{S}}^{n-1}}f(x^{\prime}+x_{n}\theta^{\prime},x_{n}(1+\theta_{n}))\,d\theta,\qquad\theta=(\theta^{\prime},\theta_{n}).

(7.12)

Because this operator commutes with translations in the
x^{\prime}
-variable, it might be natural to invoke the Fourier transform on
{{R}}^{n-1}
and compute

[f(\cdot,x_{n})]^{\wedge}(\eta)=\int\limits_{{{R}}^{n-1}}\!\!f(x^{\prime},x_{n})\,e^{ix^{\prime}\cdot\eta}\,dx^{\prime},\qquad\eta\in{{R}}^{n-1}.

Taking the corresponding inverse Fourier transform, we then reconstruct
f(x)
. Although implementation of the Fourier transform entails additional restrictions on the class of functions
f
, our reasoning might be instructive because it sheds some light on the complexity of the problem.

As we shall see below, this approach needs generalized fractional integrals of the form

(J_{\alpha,\lambda}\varphi)(t)=\int\limits_{0}^{t}\frac{(t-s)^{\alpha-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\,j_{\alpha-1}(\lambda\sqrt{s(t-s)})\,\varphi(s)\,ds,

(7.13)

(I_{\alpha,\lambda}\varphi)(t)=\int\limits_{0}^{t}\frac{(t-s)^{\alpha-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\,i_{\alpha-1}(\lambda\sqrt{t(t-s)})\,\varphi(s)\,ds,

(7.14)

where
\lambda\geq 0
is an additional parameter,
j_{\nu}(z)
and
i_{\nu}(z)
are the Bessel-Clifford functions defined by

j_{\nu}(z)=i_{\nu}(iz)=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\nu+1)}{\Gamma(\nu+1+k)}\,\frac{(-z^{2}/4)^{k}}{k!}.

These integrals were studied in
[
19
, Section 37.3]
, where the reader can find further references. If
\lambda=0
, then,
owing to the normalization
j_{\nu}(0)=i_{\nu}(0)=1
,
J_{\alpha,\lambda}\varphi
and
I_{\alpha,\lambda}\varphi
coincide with the Riemann-Liouville integrals
I^{\alpha}_{0+}\varphi
in (
2.1
).

Lemma 7.5
.

Let
f
be a function on
{{R}}^{n}_{+}
which is integrable on any

n
-dimensional layer

\Lambda_{b}=\{x=(x^{\prime},x_{n}):x^{\prime}\in{{R}}^{n-1},0<x_{n}<b\},\qquad 0<b<\infty,\quad n\geq 2.

If
f_{\eta}(s)=s^{(n-3)/2}[f(\cdot,s))]^{\wedge}(\eta)
,
\eta\in{{R}}^{n-1}
, then

[(Jf)(\cdot,x_{n})]^{\wedge}(\eta)=\frac{\Gamma(n/2)}{\pi^{1/2}}\,x_{n}^{2-n}\,(J_{(n-1)/2,|\eta|}f_{\eta})(2x_{n}).

(7.15)

Proof.

Changing the order of integration and using slice integration on
{{S}}^{n-1}
, we obtain

\displaystyle[(Jf)(\cdot,x_{n})]^{\wedge}(\eta)=\frac{1}{\sigma_{n-1}}\int\limits_{{{S}}^{n-1}}d\theta\int\limits_{{{R}}^{n-1}}\!\!\ f(x^{\prime}+x_{n}\theta^{\prime},x_{n}(1+\theta_{n}))\,e^{ix^{\prime}\cdot\eta}\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma_{n-1}}\int\limits_{{{S}}^{n-1}}e^{-ix_{n}\eta\cdot\theta^{\prime}}\,[f(\cdot,x_{n}(1+\theta_{n}))]^{\wedge}(\eta)\,d\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma_{n-1}}\int\limits_{-1}^{1}(1-t^{2})^{(n-3)/2}[f(\cdot,x_{n}(1+t))]^{\wedge}(\eta)\,dt\int\limits_{{{S}}^{n-2}}\!e^{-ix_{n}\sqrt{1-t^{2}}\,(\eta\cdot\omega)}d\omega.

The integral over
{{S}}^{n-2}
can be evaluated in terms of the Bessel function

J_{\nu}(z)=\frac{(z/2)^{\nu}}{\Gamma(\nu+1)}\,j_{\nu}(z),\qquad\nu=\frac{n-3}{2},\qquad z=x_{n}|\eta|\sqrt{1-t^{2}}.

Specifically,

\int\limits_{{{S}}^{n-2}}\!e^{-ix_{n}\sqrt{1-t^{2}}\,(\eta\cdot\omega)}d\omega=\sigma_{n-2}\,j_{(n-3)/2}(x_{n}|\eta|\sqrt{1-t^{2}});

cf., e.g.,
[
20
, p. 154]
. Thus

\displaystyle[(Jf)(\cdot,x_{n})]^{\wedge}(\eta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{n-2}}{\sigma_{n-1}}\int\limits_{-1}^{1}(1-t^{2})^{(n-3)/2}[f(\cdot,x_{n}(1+t))]^{\wedge}(\eta)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle j_{(n-3)/2}(x_{n}|\eta|\sqrt{1-t^{2}})\,dt.

Setting
x_{n}(1+t)=s
,
f_{\eta}(s)=s^{(n-3)/2}[f(\cdot,s))]^{\wedge}(\eta)
, we obtain

\displaystyle[(Jf)(\cdot,x_{n})]^{\wedge}(\eta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{n-2}\,x_{n}^{2-n}}{\sigma_{n-1}}\int\limits_{0}^{2x_{n}}(2x_{n}-s)^{(n-3)/2}f_{\eta}(s)

(7.16)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle j_{(n-3)/2}(|\eta|\sqrt{s(2x_{n}-s)})\,ds,

and (
7.15
) follows. Note that this equality formally agrees with the case
k=n-1
in
(\ref{lsoa})
if we set
\eta=0
and observe that
j_{(n-3)/2}(0)=1
.
∎

Setting
t=2x_{n}
in (
7.15
), we arrive at the following corollary.

Corollary 7.6
.

Let
(Jf)(x)=F(x)
,
x\in{{R}}^{n}_{+}
, where
f
obeys the conditions of Lemma
7.5
. If
f_{\eta}(s)=s^{(n-3)/2}[f(\cdot,s))]^{\wedge}(\eta)
, then

(J_{(n-1)/2,|\eta|}f_{\eta})(t)=F_{\eta}(t),\quad F_{\eta}(t)=\frac{\pi^{1/2}}{\Gamma(n/2)}\,\left(\frac{t}{2}\right)^{n-2}\![F(\cdot,t/2)]^{\wedge}(\eta),

(7.17)

for almost all
t>0
and all
\eta\in{{R}}^{n-1}
.

Corollary
7.6
reduces the equation
Jf=F
to the one-dimensional equation
J_{\alpha,\lambda}f_{\eta}=F_{\eta}
with
\alpha=(n-1)/2
and
\lambda=|\eta|
, provided that
\eta\in{{R}}^{n-1}
is fixed. The second equation can be explicitly solved using the following properties of the operators
J_{\alpha,\lambda}
, the proofs of which can be found in
[
19
, Section 37.3]
.

Proposition 7.7
.

(cf.
[
19
, Theorem 37.3]
)
Let
\varphi\in L^{p}(0,b)
,
1\leq p<\infty
,
0<b<\infty
.

(i)
If
\alpha>0
, then

J_{\alpha,\lambda}\varphi=I_{0+}^{\alpha}A\varphi,

(7.18)

where
I_{0+}^{\alpha}
is the
Riemann-Liouville operator (
2.1
) and
A
is a linear bounded operator in
L^{p}(0,b)
.

(ii)
If
\alpha>0
and
\beta>0
, then

I_{0+}^{\beta}J_{\alpha,\lambda}\varphi=J_{\alpha+\beta,\lambda}\varphi.

(7.19)

Proposition
7.7
makes it possible to investigate the operators
J_{\alpha,\lambda}
by making use of the case
\alpha=1
in conjunction with suitable facts for the usual Riemann-Liouville integrals and derivatives. In particular, we will need the following statement.

Proposition 7.8
.

(cf.
[
19
, Theorem 37.6]
)
Let
\psi(t)
be an absolutely continuous function on
[0,b]
. Then the equation

\frac{d}{dt}(J_{1,\lambda}\varphi)(t)=\psi(t)

(7.20)

has a unique solution of the form

\varphi(t)=\frac{1}{t}(I_{1,\lambda}\chi)(t),\qquad\chi(t)=\frac{d}{dt}\,[t\psi(t)],

where

(I_{1,\lambda}\chi)(t)=\int\limits_{0}^{t}i_{0}(\lambda\sqrt{t(t-s)})\,\chi(s)\,ds

(7.21)

(cf. (
7.14
) with
\alpha=1
).

To apply Proposition
7.8
to our case, we need to write (
7.17
) in the form (
7.20
) with
\varphi=f_{\eta}
and the suitable function
\psi
, which is determined by
F_{\eta}
. If
n\geq 3
, then
(
7.19
) yields

I_{0+}^{(n-3)/2}J_{1,|\eta|}f_{\eta}=F_{\eta}
, which gives
J_{1,|\eta|}f_{\eta}=\mathcal{D}_{0+}^{(n-3)/2}F_{\eta}
in accordance with Lemma
2.1
. Hence, we necessarily have

\frac{d}{dt}(J_{1,|\eta|}f_{\eta})(t)=\psi(t)=(\mathcal{D}_{0+}^{(n-1)/2}F_{\eta})(t),

provided that
F_{\eta}
belongs to the range
I_{0+}^{(n-1)/2}(L^{1}(0,b))
for any
b>0
. Moreover, once we follow Proposition
7.8
, we need to additionally assume that
\mathcal{D}_{0+}^{(n-1)/2}F_{\eta}
is absolutely continuous on
[0,b]
. It suffices, e.g., to assume that
F_{\eta}\in I_{0+}^{(n+1)/2}(L^{1}(0,b))
for any
b>0
.

If
n=2
, then (
7.17
) and (
7.19
) yield
J_{1,|\eta|}f_{\eta}=I_{0+}^{1/2}F_{\eta}
. In this case, we necessarily have

\frac{d}{dt}(J_{1,|\eta|}f_{\eta})(t)=\psi(t)=\frac{d}{dt}(I_{0+}^{1/2}F_{\eta})(t).

Moreover, to apply Proposition
7.8
, we need
(d/dt)(I_{0+}^{1/2}F_{\eta})(t)
to be absolutely continuous on
[0,b]
. It suffices, e.g., to assume that
F_{\eta}\in I_{0+}^{3/2}(L^{1}(0,b))
for any
b>0
.

Let us summarize the above reasoning. We denote by
[\cdot]^{\wedge}_{x^{\prime}\to\eta}
and
[\cdot]^{\vee}_{\eta\to x^{\prime}}
the Fourier transform and the inverse Fourier transform in the
x^{\prime}
-variable.

Theorem 7.9
.

Let
(Jf)(x)=F(x)
,
x=(x^{\prime},x_{n})\in{{R}}^{n}_{+}
,
n\geq 2
, where
f
is integrable on any

n
-dimensional layer
\Lambda_{b}
. We denote

\displaystyle F_{\eta}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi^{1/2}}{\Gamma(n/2)}\,\left(\frac{t}{2}\right)^{n-2}\![F(x^{\prime},t/2)]^{\wedge}_{x^{\prime}\to\eta},\qquad t>0;

\displaystyle\psi_{\eta}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(\mathcal{D}_{0+}^{(n-1)/2}F_{\eta})(t)\quad\mbox{\rm if }\quad n\geq 3,\\
\\
\frac{d}{dt}(I_{0+}^{1/2}F_{\eta})(t)\quad\mbox{\rm if }\quad n=2.\end{array}\right.

Then
f
can be formally reconstructed from
F
by the formula

f(x)\equiv f(x^{\prime},x_{n})=\left[x_{n}^{(1-n)/2}(I_{1,|\eta|}\chi_{\eta})(x_{n})\right]^{\vee}_{\eta^{\prime}\to x^{\prime}},

(7.26)

where
\chi_{\eta}(t)=(d/dt)[t\psi_{\eta})(t)]
and the operator
I_{1,|\eta|}
is defined by (
7.21
).

Here the word ‘formally’ means that an applicability of Proposition
7.8
and of the inverse Fourier transform must be carefully justified. The justification is simple if, for example,
f
is infinitely differentiable and compactly supported away from the boundary
x_{n}=0
.
