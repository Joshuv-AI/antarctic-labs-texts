Household-level WASH conditions and waterborne diseases among the Vantangiya Scheduled Tribe in India: A micro-scale spatial analysis
Authors: U Venkatesh, Margubur Rahaman, Arshad Ahmed, Hari Shanker Joshi, Anand Mohan Dixit, Ashoo Grover, Om Prakash Bera, P S Mohan Raj, Suneela Garg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite national progress in water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH), substantial disparities persist among Scheduled Tribe (ST) populations, including the Vantangiya community in Gorakhpur, India. This study assessed household-level WASH conditions and their association with waterborne diseases using a complete survey of 621 households across five villages conducted in 2024. Composite WASH indices were developed based on WHO/UNICEF Joint Monitoring Programme (JMP) indicators, including sanitation access, water source and treatment, and handwashing facilities; associations were analysed using Firth’s bias-reduced penalized logistic regression alongside household-level spatial analysis. Only 16% of households demonstrated good WASH conditions; 23% lacked toilet access, 32% relied on unimproved water sources, and 52% lacked handwashing facilities. Poor WASH conditions were associated with significantly higher odds of diarrhoea (aOR: 6.41, 95% CI: 2.08–10.11), typhoid (aOR: 8.91, 95% CI: 5.62-14.00), and any waterborne disease (aOR: 5.10, 95% CI: 1.48–9.89), compared with households with good WASH conditions. Spatial analysis identified statistically significant but very weak spatial autocorrelation, indicating localized heterogeneity rather than strong clustering. Notably, many existing toilets were poorly maintained, reflecting measurable behavioural gaps alongside infrastructure deficits. This finding provides context-specific evidence that WASH inequities remain closely linked to household disease risk. Integrating behavioural-focused interventions with infrastructure improvements may enhance effectiveness. Micro-scale spatial assessment further highlights localized high-risk households, supporting targeted, micro-area WASH interventions integrated within frontline public health delivery systems and aligned with Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 6 in underserved communities.Keywords: Vantangiya tribal health, Scheduled Tribe (ST), Waterborne diseases, WASH, Spatial epidemiology, Health disparities, IndiaSubject terms: Public health, Sustainability

Introduction

Access to safe drinking water, adequate sanitation, and proper hygiene, collectively referred to as WASH, is fundamental to public health, health equity, and sustainable development1. The WASH framework includes three interconnected pillars: water (W), sanitation (S), and hygiene (H). Together, these pillars reduce the transmission of infectious diseases such as diarrhoea, cholera, typhoid, dysentery, and hepatitis A2. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), inadequate WASH services contribute to the approximately 297,000 deaths among children under five each year3.

The adoption of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) in 2015 reaffirmed global commitment to WASH, particularly SDG 6, which aims to ensure universal access to safe drinking water and sanitation by 2030. These goals emphasize not only access but also service quality, equity, and behavioural dimensions, especially for marginalized populations such as women, children, and indigenous communities1,4.

Despite global progress, significant WASH disparities persist. In 2020, an estimated 2.8 billion people lacked safely managed sanitation, 1.6 billion lacked safe drinking water, and 1.9 billion had no basic handwashing facilities5,6. These gaps are concentrated in Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, where poverty, rapid urbanization, and environmental pressures intensify WASH vulnerabilities2,7. Young children remain disproportionately affected, with enteric infections accounting for 6.9 million DALYs globally in 20198.

India continues to face major challenges in achieving universal WASH coverage, particularly in rural and remote areas. National initiatives such as the Swachh Bharat Abhiyan have reduced open defecation from 74% in 2000 to 16% in 2019 and improved access to drinking water, increasing from 68% in 1993 to 96% in 20219,10. However, service quality and equity gaps remain. NFHS-5 (2019-21) reports that only 54% of households have safely managed sanitation and 44% have safely managed drinking water, while approximately 38% of rural households still lack basic handwashing facilities11.

These disparities are especially pronounced among Scheduled Tribes (STs) populations, who represent 8.6% of India’s population. Many ST communities face geographic isolation, socioeconomic disadvantage, and limited service access, which contribute to persistent WASH inequities12,13. Studies consistently show higher levels of open defecation, inadequate water infrastructure, and poor hygiene conditions among ST households14,15. Structural challenges, including difficult terrain and limited health service reach, further constrain progress11,15.

Among these groups, the Vantangiya community represents a particularly marginalized sub-population characterized by historical forest settlement, delayed administrative recognition, and limited access to basic infrastructure and public services. Unlike many other Scheduled Tribe groups, Vantangiya settlements were only recently incorporated into formal governance systems, resulting in persistent gaps in water, sanitation, and healthcare access. These context-specific vulnerabilities necessitate focused, community-level investigation.

Reliance on climate-sensitive livelihoods, low literacy, and socio-cultural barriers can hinder adoption of safe WASH practices in ST communities16. Additionally, limited health awareness and socio-cultural barriers such as taboos and traditional beliefs further impede behavioural adoption17,18. These structural and behavioural challenges have been linked to adverse health outcomes among ST populations, including high rates of undernutrition and diarrhoeal diseases19,20. Despite this burden, localized evidence examining household-level environmental and behavioural determinants of WASH-related disease, including water access, sanitation conditions, and hygiene practices, remains limited21.

Moreover, the absence of disaggregated WASH data for Scheduled Tribe (ST) populations further restricts targeted interventions planning22. While some spatial studies have identified regional diarrhoeal hotspots23, recent evidence in India has primarily focused on district- and state-level disparities, with limited application of fine-resolution household or village-level spatial epidemiological approaches in tribal settings24–26. This highlights the importance of localized, data-driven approaches to inform inclusive public health action. Such approaches are increasingly aligned with precision public health frameworks, which emphasize the use of fine-scale spatial data to identify localized risk heterogeneity and optimize targeted intervention delivery27.

To address this gap, the present study examines WASH inequalities in five Vantangiya tribal villages (a Scheduled Tribe community) of Gorakhpur district through micro-scale spatial assessment. Using a household-level geospatial approach, the study assesses WASH conditions, explores the prevalence of waterborne diseases, and examines their association using spatial analysis techniques such as univariate Local Indicators of Spatial Association (LISA). By integrating complete household survey data with geospatial modelling, the study aims to generate fine-resolution, context-specific insights that can inform equitable public health interventions. The findings are intended to support planning aligned with SDG 6 and India’s decentralized governance framework.

Beyond documenting WASH disparities, this study contributes methodologically by integrating complete household enumeration with fine-resolution geospatial analysis in a Scheduled Tribe community. Unlike aggregate or sample-based assessments, this approach enables household-level detection of localized vulnerability patterns and links environmental exposure, sanitation behaviour, and disease occurrence at fine spatial resolution. This micro-scale framework advances WASH-health research beyond descriptive case studies and supports precision targeting of interventions in underserved settings. Accordingly, this study was designed to: (1) quantify household-level WASH conditions using composite indicators aligned with global standards; (2) examine associations between WASH conditions and selected waterborne diseases; and (3) identify localized spatial patterns using household-level geospatial analysis. We hypothesized that poorer WASH conditions would be associated with increased household risk of waterborne disease and that these risks would exhibit localized spatial variation at the household level.

Methods

Study setting

The study was conducted in five Vantangiya tribal villages (a constitutionally recognized Scheduled Tribe community) located in the Gorakhpur sub-division of Uttar Pradesh, India. These selected villages Jangal Tinkoniya, Khale Tola, Aambagh, Rajahayi, and Chilbilva are situated approximately 10 km southwest of Gorakhpur city (26°43’30"N, 83°25’0"E) within a 110-hectare (1.1 km²) area in the fertile Terai region. The study area lies between 83°23’30” to 83°29’15” longitude and 26°40’15” to 26°48’15” latitude (Fig. 1). Geographically, the area is characterized by flat alluvial terrain with a monsoon climate. The Gorakhpur sub-division has a population of 4,440,895, of which Scheduled Tribes (STs) constitute approximately 0.4%28. The population is characterized by low income levels, high dependence on daily wage labour, and high illiteracy rates28. The selected villages represent the primary contiguous settlement of the Vantangiya tribal community in the region, enabling complete household enumeration within a defined geographic cluster. The study design intentionally focuses on a community-level micro-scale population rather than generating district-representative estimates. This approach facilitates high-resolution spatial analysis of WASH conditions and disease distribution within a historically underserved tribal population. All spatial analyses were conducted at the household point level, using Global Positioning System (GPS) coordinates collected during field data collection. Each household was treated as an independent spatial observation. For improved spatial interpretation, maps were developed incorporating both geocoded household locations and village boundary layers, where village boundaries serve as contextual reference units for visualization but not as analytical units for clustering. This fine-scale spatial design aligns with emerging precision public health frameworks, which emphasize the identification of localized heterogeneity and micro-level vulnerability patterns that may be obscured in aggregated administrative data.

Location of the study area, Gorakhpur, India.

Study population

The five villages are home to the Vantangiya tribal Community, a constitutionally recognized Scheduled tribe (ST) group that has resided in this region since 1922. The community derives its name from the word ‘van’ (forest) and was brought to the area during the British colonial period to undertake afforestation on government land. Over time, the community has sustained itself through forest-related livelihoods. The total population across these five villages is approximately 4,000.

Recently, the state revenue department, through an official notification, recognized these villages as revenue villages, facilitating improved access to government schemes and services. The selected villages fall within two Community Development (CD) Blocks: Chargawan and Pipraich. Chargawan block has a total population of 186,787, of which 658 individuals (0.35%) belong to STs, with a sex ratio of 988 females per 1,000 males. Pipraich block has a total population of 188,645, with an ST population of 895 (0.47%) and a sex ratio of 853 females per 1,000 males. In terms of literacy among Scheduled Tribes, Chargawan exhibits a literacy rate of 64.54% (male: 77.9%, female: 51.74%), reflecting a 26.16% male-female literacy gap. A total of 364 individuals (215 males and 149 females) are literate, while 294 individuals (116 males and178 females) are illiterate. In Pipraich, the literacy rate among 71.14% (male: 83.25%, female: 57.1%), with a 26.15% male-female literacy gap. A total 530 individuals (333 males and 197 females) are literate, whereas 365 individuals (150 males and 215 females) remain illiterate. These demographic characteristics highlight socio-economic vulnerabilities within the Scheduled Tribe population, highlighting the need for targeted WASH interventions28.

Study design

This study employed a community-based, cross-sectional study design to examine the association between household WASH conditions and waterborne diseases among a tribal population. Given the cross-sectional nature of the data, the analysis captures co-occurrence of exposure (WASH conditions) and outcomes (waterborne diseases) at a single point in time. Therefore, findings should be interpreted as associational rather than causal, and temporal directionality between WASH conditions and disease outcomes cannot be established. However, the integration of complete household enumeration with geocoded spatial data enables the identification of fine-scale spatial co-patterning of risk factors and disease outcomes, which is increasingly recognized as a key component of precision public health approaches. Such designs are particularly valuable in underserved settings where longitudinal surveillance data are limited, and where identifying localized vulnerability patterns can inform targeted interventions.

Data

A complete enumeration of all households in the five selected Vantangiya tribal villages was conducted between March and June 2024, corresponding to the pre-monsoon and early monsoon transition period in the study region. Individuals aged 18 years and above were considered eligible respondents from each household. A total of 621 households with complete information on WASH practices and waterborne disease history were included in the analysis. The dataset comprises household-level socio-demographic and economic characteristics, WASH conditions, and health status of household members. The complete enumeration design ensured that spatial analyses were conducted on the entire household population rather than sampled clusters, thereby enhancing the precision of localized risk detection. Waterborne disease outcomes were assessed using a 12-month recall period, which allows partial capture of seasonal variation in disease occurrence. However, the cross-sectional design does not permit explicit modelling of temporal or seasonal dynamics, and observed associations may reflect aggregated exposure across different seasons. These limitations have been acknowledged in the interpretation of results.

Survey tools and techniques

A structured questionnaire was developed covering four key domains: (i) household socio-demographic and WASH profile, (ii) clinical profile and healthcare-seeking behaviour, (iii) reproductive health of eligible couples, and (iv) child health. While multiple domains were included as part of a broader research framework, the present analysis focuses specifically on WASH conditions and waterborne disease outcomes. Variables related to reproductive and child health were not included in the current analysis but were collected for future research purposes.

The WASH-related components were aligned with WHO/UNICEF Joint Monitoring Programme (JMP) standards29, and disease classification followed WHO definitions. The questionnaire was pretested in a comparable population setting outside the study area to assess clarity, cultural appropriateness, and comprehension of disease-related terminology. Based on the pretest, minor revisions were made to improve question wording and response consistency. All field investigators underwent structured training and standardization prior to data collection, including instruction on questionnaire administration, probing for clinically diagnosed conditions, ethical considerations, and use of the Epicollect5 application. This process was designed to ensure consistency in data collection and to minimize interviewer-related bias.

Data collection was conducted using the Epicollect5 application, which enabled real-time data capture and integration of household-level geospatial coordinates, thereby reducing data entry errors and improving spatial accuracy. Informed consent procedures followed the approved ethical protocol. All participants received an information sheet and provided written informed consent prior to participation. For illiterate participants, the information sheet was read aloud in the local language in the presence of an impartial witness, and consent was documented accordingly. Data collection using digital tools was initiated only after completion of the consent process.

Measures in the study

Waterborne disease outcomes were measured at the household level based on respondents’ reports of clinically diagnosed illness among any household member during the 12 months preceding the survey. To improve reporting validity, respondents were explicitly asked to report only those disease episodes that were diagnosed by a healthcare provider (government or private), rather than self-diagnosed symptoms. In this low-resource tribal setting, formal medical records such as prescriptions, health cards, or laboratory reports was not consistently available for independent verification. Therefore, external validation of reported diagnoses was not feasible. However, multiple measures were implemented to minimize recall and misclassification bias: (i) restriction of reporting to provider-diagnosed cases; (ii) use of a structured questionnaire with standardized disease definitions; (iii) training of field investigators to probe specifically for confirmed diagnoses; (iv) conducting interviews in the local language to reduce misinterpretation; and (v) recording disease outcomes as binary household-level indicators, thereby minimizing over-reporting of repeated episodes. Although variation in healthcare access and utilization may influence disease reporting, this approach reflects standard practice in epidemiological studies conducted in similar resource-constrained settings and is considered appropriate for population-level risk assessment where clinical records are limited. The use of a 12-month recall period may introduce recall bias, particularly for mild or self-limiting illnesses. To minimize this, reporting was restricted to clinically diagnosed cases and recorded as binary household-level indicators (presence of at least one case), thereby reducing over-reporting of multiple episodes. However, mild cases that did not result in healthcare consultation may be underreported and should be considered when interpreting prevalence estimates. Cholera, diarrhoea, and typhoid were included based on standard WHO classifications of waterborne diseases. Food poisoning was included due to its local epidemiological association with faecal–oral transmission pathways in settings with unsafe water and hygiene conditions. We acknowledge that diagnostic certainty may vary across these conditions.

For analytical purposes within a WASH framework, food poisoning was grouped with waterborne diseases due to shared faecal-oral transmission pathways and its local epidemiological linkage to unsafe water use and hygiene practices. The Household Sanitation, Water, and Hygiene indices were constructed as composite measures based on key indicators aligned with WHO/UNICEF Joint Monitoring Programme (JMP) definitions. Equal weighting was applied across components to reflect their conceptual role as necessary and complementary conditions for interrupting faecal oral transmission pathways, rather than assigning differential importance based on statistical variance.

Alternative weighting approaches, such as principal component analysis (PCA), were considered. However, PCA-derived weights are data-driven and may not reflect epidemiological relevance, particularly in settings with a limited number of binary or ordinal indicators. Moreover, PCA-based weights may be sensitive to sample-specific variation and reduce interpretability in applied public health contexts. Therefore, an additive equal-weight approach was adopted to ensure conceptual clarity and comparability with established WASH monitoring frameworks. The sanitation, water, and hygiene indices were combined into a composite Household WASH Index to represent cumulative household-level exposure to multiple interrelated environmental risk factors. This approach reflects the understanding that water, sanitation, and hygiene function synergistically in determining disease risk, and that combined exposure provides a more realistic representation of vulnerability than isolated components.

While individual WASH components were examined descriptively, the composite index was used in regression analysis as a parsimonious indicator of overall household WASH conditions. We acknowledge that combining components may mask the independent contribution of specific domains; however, given the limited number of outcome events, disaggregated modelling could reduce statistical stability. The hygiene index included observable indicators such as the availability of a designated handwashing place and the use of cleansing agents (soap/detergent), which are widely used proxies in population-based WASH assessments. Behavioural aspects, including critical handwashing moments, were not assessed and represent an important limitation of the study.

Study variables

The key dependent variable was household-level self-reported, clinically diagnosed waterborne diseases among any household member, including cholera, diarrhoea, typhoid, and food poisoning within the previous 12 months. Detailed measurement procedures, including strategies to minimize recall and misclassification bias, are described in Sect. 2.6. Based on prior studies12,30,31, a range of spatial and non-spatial household characteristics were selected as explanatory variables. Statistical analyses

The present study employed descriptive, bivariate, and multivariable logistic regression analyses. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the socio-demographic background of study households. Fisher’s exact test was applied to assess bivariate associations. Penalized (Firth’s bias-reduced) multivariable logistic regression was conducted to examine the association between household WASH conditions and the risk of waterborne diseases, in order to address sparse outcomes and separation bias. All regression analyses were conducted using R statistical software, and Firth’s penalized logistic regression was implemented using the “logistf” package to address sparse outcome data and reduce small-sample bias.

Because households were nested within a small number of villages, the analysis focused on household-level estimation using penalized logistic regression rather than formal multilevel modelling. The limited number of clusters restricted stable estimation of cluster-level variance components. Therefore, results should be interpreted primarily as household-level associations.

The history of cholera, diarrhoea, typhoid, food poisoning, and any waterborne diseases were considered as dependent variables in five separate regression models. The Household WASH Index was the primary explanatory variable. Socioeconomic variables such as income, education, and occupation were available but were not included in the final models to avoid overfitting given the limited number of outcome events. Model specification prioritized proximal WASH-related exposures to maintain statistical stability. Multicollinearity among explanatory variables was assessed prior to modelling using variance inflation factors (VIF), and no significant multicollinearity was detected. In addition, other household-level factors, such as waste disposal, water disposal, child stool disposal, and housing conditions, were included to estimate the adjusted association between the Household WASH Index and vulnerability to waterborne disease. Separate regression models were estimated for each disease outcome because these outcomes are not mutually exclusive and may co-occur within households, making multinomial modelling inappropriate for the study design. Interaction terms between sanitation, water, and hygiene components were not modelled separately because these dimensions were intentionally integrated into a composite Household WASH Index representing cumulative exposure, and inclusion of interaction terms would introduce conceptual redundancy and reduce model stability given sparse outcome counts.

Global spatial autocorrelation was assessed using Univariate Moran’s I at the household point level, treating each geocoded household as an individual spatial unit. A distance-based spatial weights matrix was applied, where neighbouring households were defined based on geographic proximity within a specified threshold distance. Village boundaries were not used as analytical clustering units but served only as contextual frames for visualization and interpretation. A p-value below 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Because Moran’s I quantify the strength of global spatial dependence rather than the practical importance of clustering, statistically significant but low values were interpreted as evidence of weak spatial structure. In this context, Moran’s I was used to detect non-random spatial patterning rather than to imply strong clustering, and results were interpreted cautiously. LISA was applied to explore localized neighbourhood-level anomalies that may inform micro-scale intervention planning. This micro-scale analytical approach aligns with precision public health frameworks that emphasize fine-resolution spatial heterogeneity to detect localized vulnerability pockets that may be masked in aggregated analyses. Following Moran’s I assessment, Univariate LISA clustering maps were generated to identify localized pockets of elevated or reduced disease risk, rather than strong global clustering. Finally, spatial autocorrelation mapping examined the relationship between household WASH conditions and the spatial distribution of waterborne diseases. All adjusted estimates presented in the Results are based on Firth’s bias-reduced penalized logistic regression.

Result

Background characteristics of the study households

The mean age of the household head was 44 years. About 96% of the households were male-headed (Table 2). Most household heads were illiterate (73%), followed by those with primary education (15%). A nuclear family structure was typical among the study households. Semi-pucca houses (84%) and the use of dung or wood as cooking fuel (75%) were common. The mean household monthly income was INR 8,311, where 74% of household’s monthly income was INR ≤ 8,000. Approximately 65% of household heads were engaged in daily wage labour, followed by farmers (15%).

Household sanitation conditions

About 23% of households lacked toilet access, and among those with access, 17.4% used unimproved toilet facilities, often associated with increased health risks. Additionally, 62% of toilets were reported as unclean, indicating poor hygiene maintenance. This pattern suggests that access to sanitation facilities does not necessarily translate into hygienic usage or maintenance, potentially due to socioeconomic constraints, limited water availability, and behavioural factors. Based on the sanitation index, nearly one-third (31%) of households fell into the poor category, reflecting inadequate sanitation infrastructure and practices that may elevate exposure to faecal contamination and related diseases (Table 3).

Household water conditions

About 31% of households used unimproved water sources for non-drinking purposes (Table 4). Nearly one-third relied on unimproved sources for drinking water. Approximately one-tenth of households did not treat drinking water, indicating potential exposure to microbiological contamination. While water treatment was reported by the majority of households, the specific methods of treatment and water storage practices were not assessed, which may influence the effectiveness of household-level water safety. Based on the overall water score, 26% of households were in the good category, 69% were in the moderate category and 5% were in the poor category.

Household handwashing conditions

Regarding defined handwashing places, 52% of households had no such place for handwashing (Table 5), based on direct observation during household visits. For handwashing, 9% of households used only plain water, 8% used ash/mud/sand, and 28% reported using multiple elements such as soap in combination with water or other materials. The use of multiple elements reflects inconsistent hygiene practices, as effective handwashing is best achieved through the regular use of soap or detergent with water. The handwashing index shows that only one-quarter of households were in the good category, 58% in the moderate category, and 17% in the poor category.

Household-level composite WASH index

Figure 2 displays the composite WASH score across the study households. Only 16% of the households were classified as having good WASH conditions, 69% moderate, and 15% poor. The proportion of households with good WASH conditions (16%) is lower than national and state-level estimates of improved WASH coverage reported in large-scale surveys. This difference likely reflects the use of a composite household-level index integrating water, sanitation, and hygiene conditions, as well as the focus on a socioeconomically marginalized tribal population, where both infrastructure and behavioural constraints may limit effective WASH practices.

WASH index distribution among the study households.

Figure 3a shows the spatial autocorrelation of the WASH index using Moran’s I. The Moran’s I value (0.062), with a z-score of 3.03 and a p value of 0.002, indicates statistically significant but very weak spatial autocorrelation. The low magnitude suggests weak global spatial dependence, and therefore the result is interpreted as weak spatial structure rather than meaningful large-scale clustering. In this context, statistical significance is likely influenced by the high spatial resolution and dense household sampling, rather than strong clustering processes. Accordingly, the spatial distribution of WASH conditions among households is largely heterogeneous, with only limited non-random spatial patterning. Given this weak global spatial dependence, subsequent LISA results are interpreted as identifying localized neighbourhood-level variations (spatial anomalies) rather than evidence of strong or system-wide clustering. Figure 3b further illustrates localized spatial pockets, identifying hotspots and cold spots. Hotspot areas, characterized by relatively poor WASH status surrounded by similar households, were primarily located in the north-central part of the study area, while cold spots were observed to the west of these clusters.

Waterborne disease distribution among the study households

The overall prevalence of any waterborne disease was 8.2% (Fig. 4). Diarrhoea was the most prevalent waterborne disease, followed by typhoid (2.9%), food poisoning (2.25%), and cholera (0.48%) (Fig. 4). Given the extremely low prevalence of cholera, spatial patterns should be interpreted cautiously, and the analysis is presented primarily for exploratory comparison rather than definitive clustering inference. A sparse distribution of households with cholera is located in the western part of the study area (Fig. 5a).

Households with diarrhoea were primarily found in the north-central and eastern zones (Fig. 5b). Households with typhoid cases were located in the north-western portion of the study area (Fig. 5c). The distribution of households with food poisoning cases appeared spatially dispersed without a clear clustering pattern (Fig. 5d).

The Moran’s I result indicates statistically detectable but very weak positive spatial autocorrelation in the distribution of cholera across study households (Moran’s I 0.005, p 0.022). The z-score value (2.28) supports statistical non-randomness; however, the magnitude indicates weak spatial dependence rather than meaningful clustering (Fig. 6a). The sparse occurrence of cholera limits statistical stability, and hotspot patterns should be viewed as indicative rather than confirmatory. Similar patterns were observed for diarrhoea (Moran’s I 0.004, p ≤ 0.001), typhoid (Moran’s I 0.020, p ≤ 0.280), and food poisoning (Moran’s I 0.011, p ≤ 0.001) (Fig. 6b–d). Discussion

This study provides a multidimensional, household-level assessment of WASH conditions and their associations with waterborne diseases in the Vantangiya Scheduled Tribe community in Gorakhpur, India. A key contribution lies in integrating complete household enumeration with micro-scale geospatial analysis, enabling vulnerability patterns to be examined at a spatial resolution rarely achieved in tribal WASH research32,33. Unlike large-scale survey-based studies that rely on aggregated indicators, this GIS-integrated framework supports fine-resolution spatial assessment of environmental and behavioural risk in relation to disease occurrence, aligning with emerging approaches in precision public health. The findings demonstrate context-specific inequities in WASH access and hygiene practices and reveal measurable spatial heterogeneity within the study area33. However, these results should be interpreted as localized evidence rather than broadly generalizable conclusions.

Framed within a micro-scale public health perspective, the study demonstrates how fine-resolution spatial analysis can inform targeted intervention planning while contributing to the evolving framework for evaluating WASH-health linkage in underserved settings15,22. The sanitation profile indicates that infrastructure access alone does not guarantee effective hygienic use34. Although toilet availability has improved, maintenance and behavioural practices remain uneven. Similar sanitation-use gaps have been reported in other tribal settings, suggesting that functional utilization, rather than infrastructure coverage, is a critical determinant of health protection35. The localized spatial patterns observed in this study suggest that sanitation-related risks may operate at micro geographic scales, reinforcing the need for context-specific intervention strategies rather than uniform assumptions24,25.

Environmental factors may also contribute to these localized patterns. Conditions such as poor drainage, seasonal flooding, water stagnation, and proximity to contaminated water sources can influence exposure pathways for waterborne disease. Although these variables were not directly measured, their potential role should be considered when interpreting spatial clustering, and future studies should integrate environmental datasets to better explain such patterns. Water access patterns show mixed progress, where improved sources coexist with continued exposure to unsafe water handling practices34. These findings are consistent with broader evidence indicating that access to improved water sources does not necessarily ensure safe household-level water use9. The observed spatial proximity between disease occurrence and unsafe disposal practices suggests localized exposure pathways, although causal relationships cannot be established within the cross-sectional design14,15.

Household hygiene practices reveal behavioural gaps that extend beyond infrastructure availability. Variation in handwashing practices within small geographic areas suggests that hygiene behaviour is shaped by localized social and behavioural contexts34. While proxy indicators such as handwashing facilities and cleansing materials were included, detailed behavioural practices were not directly measured and represent an important limitation1,36. The observed disease patterns are broadly consistent with established links between WASH deprivation and waterborne illness34, although regional variation is expected. Differences from previous studies may reflect contextual epidemiological conditions, exposure environments, or recall-related factors. The localized concentration of certain disease outcomes suggests site-specific environmental and behavioural influences, warranting cautious interpretation2.

The socioeconomic characteristics of the study population high illiteracy and low household income reflect structural deprivation commonly documented among Scheduled Tribe communities12,13. While these factors provide important contextual understanding, they were not included in regression models due to limited outcome events. Therefore, regression results should be interpreted primarily as household-level environmental associations rather than fully adjusted causal relationships.

Methodologically, this study contributes to WASH research by demonstrating how micro-scale epidemiology can be applied to underserved populations. The integration of GIS with household-level data enables identification of localized vulnerability pockets that are often obscured in district- or state-level analysis15,25. Although composite WASH indices provide a useful summary of multidimensional conditions, they may mask domain-specific effects, which should be explored in future research. Overall, the study highlights the importance of combining spatial, environmental, and behavioural perspectives to better understand WASH-related health risks in marginalized settings and supports the development of targeted, evidence-based public health interventions20,37.

Policy implications

The findings of this study provide actionable insights for improving WASH outcomes in Scheduled Tribe (ST) settings through spatially targeted, micro-level intervention strategies. The identification of localized WASH and disease hotspots demonstrates that uniform, area-wide approaches may overlook high-risk clusters, and highlights the need for geographically prioritized interventions. In hotspot areas characterized by poor WASH conditions, targeted actions may include intensive sanitation reinforcement (toilet maintenance and usability), localized water safety interventions (household water treatment promotion and safe disposal practices), and focused hygiene behaviour-change campaigns. These interventions can be operationalized at the micro-scale by aligning hotspot locations with ASHA catchment areas, Anganwadi service zones, and Village Health, Sanitation and Nutrition Committee (VHSNC) jurisdictions, allowing frontline workers to prioritize high-risk households.

The study findings can be integrated with existing national programs such as the Swachh Bharat Mission (Gramin) and Jal Jeevan Mission, which primarily focus on infrastructure provision. The spatial evidence generated in this study complements these programs by identifying households and micro-areas where functional usage, maintenance, and behavioural gaps persist despite infrastructure availability. This enables a shift from coverage-based monitoring to risk-informed, outcome-oriented implementation. Feasible behaviour-change strategies emerging from the study include community-led demonstrations on toilet maintenance, structured handwashing promotion sessions, reinforcement of safe child stool disposal practices, and peer-supported hygiene education adapted to local cultural contexts. Embedding these activities within routine ASHA household visits and Anganwadi-led sessions enhances continuity, acceptability, and community engagement. Furthermore, hotspot-informed planning can support micro-level surveillance systems, such as targeted water quality monitoring and follow-up visits in high-risk clusters. This approach aligns with emerging precision public health frameworks that emphasize fine-resolution targeting of interventions based on localized risk patterns. Overall, the study demonstrates how household-level spatial evidence can be translated into practical, scalable, and context-sensitive WASH interventions. Linking hotspot identification with existing frontline delivery systems enhances feasibility, accountability, and sustainability, and offers a replicable model for improving WASH outcomes in other underserved settings.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that must be considered when interpreting the findings. First, its cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish temporal relationships or infer causality between WASH conditions and health outcomes. Second, waterborne disease outcomes were based on household self-report over a 12-month recall period. Although respondents referenced clinically diagnosed episodes, the absence of consistent medical documentation limited formal validation. This approach introduces potential recall error, misclassification, and reporting bias influenced by health awareness or care-seeking behaviour. Such biases may affect both prevalence estimates and regression associations, potentially attenuating or inflating observed relationships. Findings should therefore be interpreted as reflecting household-level patterns of reported illness rather than precise epidemiological incidence. Third, the composite WASH indices, while useful for summarizing multidimensional conditions, may introduce measurement bias due to equal weighting of components and reliance on proxy indicators. These indices may mask the relative contribution of individual WASH domains and should be interpreted as approximate measures of household exposure rather than precise constructs.

Fourth, the study was conducted in five villages belonging to a single Vantangiya Scheduled Tribe community and was designed as a focused community-level assessment rather than a district-representative survey. While this approach provides detailed insight into localized WASH conditions, findings should be interpreted as context-specific and may not fully represent tribal populations in other geographic or socio-environmental settings. Fifth, the extremely low prevalence of cholera introduces statistical instability in spatial and regression estimates. Cholera-related findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously and viewed as exploratory rather than definitive. Sixth, the clustered structure of households within villages could influence standard error estimation. Although households were geographically nested, the small number of village clusters limited the feasibility of multilevel or cluster-robust modelling. As a result, standard error estimates may be conservative or imprecise. Future studies with larger cluster structures could apply hierarchical modelling to better account for within-cluster correlation.

Seventh, the analysis may be subject to residual confounding, as socioeconomic variables such as income, education, and occupation, as well as detailed behavioural practices, were not included in the regression models due to limited outcome events. While these factors are described contextually, their exclusion may influence observed associations. Eighth, this study did not collect detailed environmental exposure information such as flooding patterns, drainage conditions, proximity to animals, or localized water stagnation. These factors may contribute to spatial variation in waterborne disease risk and could help explain localized clustering patterns. Their absence limits environmental interpretation of the spatial findings. Population density and environmental features (e.g., surface water bodies and drainage patterns) were not included as covariates due to limited variability and lack of compatible high-resolution data.

Finally, seasonality represents an important limitation. Waterborne diseases, particularly diarrhoeal illnesses, exhibit strong seasonal variation influenced by rainfall, temperature, and water availability. Because data were collected during a single time period, this study could not capture temporal fluctuations in exposure or disease occurrence. The absence of longitudinal or repeated seasonal measurements limits the ability to assess peak transmission periods and seasonal risk dynamics. Consequently, the reported prevalence and spatial patterns should be interpreted as a cross-sectional snapshot rather than a comprehensive representation of year-round disease burden. Overall, these limitations suggest that findings should be interpreted as indicative of localized patterns and associations rather than definitive causal relationships.

Conclusion

WASH remains a critical public health challenge among Scheduled Tribe (ST) communities. This study demonstrates an association between poor WASH practices and the prevalence of waterborne diseases, particularly diarrhoeal illness, often aggravated by unsafe stool disposal and reliance on unimproved water sources. Despite national initiatives like the Swachh Bharat Mission (Gramin) and Jal Jeevan Mission, important household-level gaps persist, especially in sanitation maintenance, hygiene practices, and water safety. A key contribution of this study lies in the application of micro-scale spatial analysis using complete household enumeration, which enables the identification of localized vulnerability patterns that are often obscured in aggregated datasets. This approach demonstrates the value of integrating geospatial methods with epidemiological analysis to inform precision public health planning in underserved settings. The findings highlight the need for context-sensitive, household-focused interventions that extend beyond infrastructure provision. Strengthening behaviour change communication, improving sanitation usability, and promoting safe water handling practices, supported by frontline health workers, are essential for improving local health outcomes. Future research should build on this approach by incorporating longitudinal study designs to capture seasonal dynamics, integrating high-resolution environmental datasets (e.g., flooding, drainage, and water quality), and applying advanced spatial modelling techniques to better understand the complex pathways linking WASH conditions and disease risk. Overall, this study supports the use of fine-resolution, spatially informed approaches to guide targeted interventions and reduce health inequities in marginalized populations.
