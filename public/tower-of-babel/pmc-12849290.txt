Risk factors for hypoglycaemia in adults with type 1 diabetes: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Qiang Zhang, Haojie Zhou, Ruonan Lin, Liping Hu, Xiaoli Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdult T1DM has a large population base, is insulin dependent, and has a high hazard of hypoglycaemia risk. Therefore, the objective of this systematic review is to evaluate the risk factors linked to the occurrence of hypoglycemia in adult patients with type 1 diabetes mellitus. This analysis aims to aid in the formulation of preventive strategies and support early-stage intervention measures.MethodsA computerised search of nine electronic databases, all with a timeframe of construction to December 2024, was conducted to find cross-sectional, cohort, and case-control studies on hypoglycaemia in patients with type 1 diabetes mellitus. The quality of the included studies was evaluated using the American Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) or the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS). The combined odds ratio (OR) and the corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated to evaluate the effect of the included risk factors on hypoglycaemia.ResultsA total of 31 studies including 54,634 participants were included. A total of 35 potentially relevant risk factors were identified. Ultimately, 18 risk factors were assessed for inclusion in the Meta-analysis, and 9 risk factors were statistically significant (P < 0.05), including disease duration (OR = 1.07, 95% CI: 1.01–1.13), history of hypoglycaemia (OR = 2.31, 95% CI: 1.70–3.15),smoking (OR = 1.17, 95% CI: 1.07–1.28), diabetic nephropathy (OR = 0.83, 95% CI: 0.71–0.98), multiple daily insulin injections (OR = 1.96, 95% CI: 1.46–2.61 ), insulin dose (OR = 1.14, 95% CI: 1.08–1.21), hypoglycaemic unawareness (OR = 5.18, 95% CI: 1.81–14.84), impaired hypoglycaemic awareness (OR = 5.99, 95% CI: 4.62–7.75), diabetic neuropathy (OR = 2.05, 95% CI: 1.12–3.76).ConclusionThis study identified 9 major risk factors affecting hypoglycaemia in adults with type 1 diabetes mellitus, including disease duration, history of hypoglycaemia, smoking, diabetic nephropathy (protective factor), multiple daily insulin injections, insulin dose, hypoglycaemic unawareness, impaired hypoglycaemic awareness and diabetic neuropathy.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12902-025-02122-9.

Background

Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus (T1DM) is defined by absolute insulin deficiency, necessitating lifelong reliance on exogenous insulin [1]. With the advancement of modern medicine, although insulin replacement therapy has significantly improved the prognosis of patients, hypoglycaemia, as the most common and intractable acute complication of T1DM, remains a major obstacle to the management of the disease, posing a serious threat to the safety of patients as well as a huge economic burden worldwide [2]. Although traditionally regarded as a pediatric disease, emerging epidemiological evidence [3] indicates that over half of new T1DM cases now occur in adults. This demographic shift presents significant diagnostic challenges: non-specific clinical manifestations often lead to delayed diagnosis or misclassification as T2DM. Findings from the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) 2021 report [4] demonstrate a global prevalence of approximately 9 million T1DM cases, with adults ≥ 30 years of age accounting for over 65% of diagnoses. Notably, this demographic subgroup exhibits a significant upward trend in incidence rates.

Hypoglycaemia is not only the most common acute complication of T1DM, but also a major obstacle to glycaemic management [5]. Epidemiological evidence reveals that nearly two-thirds of individuals with T1DM experience at least one severe hypoglycaemic episode annually [6]. Emerging research highlights that recurrent hypoglycaemia significantly increases risks for cognitive decline, cardiovascular morbidity, and sudden cardiac death [7]. Prolonged exposure to hypoglycaemic events further induces a state of hypoglycaemia unawareness, impairing the body’s compensatory responses and establishing a pathophysiological cycle of increased vulnerability [8].

Notwithstanding advancements in Continuous Glucose Monitoring (CGM) and novel insulin analogues, T1DM adults continue to face substantial residual hypoglycaemia risk [9]. Studies on hypoglycaemia risk factors have focused on children or T2DM [10], while the unique physiological, psychosocial and treatment adherence characteristics of adult T1DM have not been systematically evaluated.

Currently, clinical risk assessment for hypoglycemia primarily relies on subjective clinical judgment [11]. Although various risk screening tools exist, most of them lack validation evidence—such as small sample sizes, absence of internal/external validation, inappropriate variable selection methods, and incomplete handling of missing data—thereby increasing the risk of study bias. Additionally, while validated tools (GOLD score and Clarke score) exist for assessing hypoglycemic unawareness, their clinical application and generalizability remain limited [12, 13]. Notwithstanding the Chinese Guidelines for T1DM Management (2021) identifying key risk factors including insulin dosage, disease duration, comorbidities, and glycemic variability, standardized assessment protocols remain underdeveloped [14].

Thus, proactive identification of hypoglycaemia risk determinants in individuals with T1DM is critical for implementing preventive strategies and timely therapeutic interventions. Hypoglycaemia is recognized as a multifactorial disorder [15], for example disease duration, insulin injection status, diet, gender and age may influence the development of hypoglycaemia. However, methodological limitations in existing studies have hindered the identification of hypoglycaemia determinants. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to systematically evaluate risk factors for hypoglycaemia in individuals with T1DM, providing evidence-based insights for developing targeted prevention strategies.

Methods

Programme and registration

This meta-analysis conformed to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [16] (Supplementary File 1). The systematic review was prospectively registered in PROSPERO. Registry: CRD42024629573.

Search strategy

A systematic search was conducted across nine databases (PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, CINAHL, Embase, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, Wanfang Data, Chinese Scientific Journal Database, and China Biomedical Literature Service System) from inception to December 2024. The search strategy involved using speciffc keywords related to our topic of interest: “Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus”, “Hypoglycaemia”, “Risk Factors”, and related terms. Take the pubmed search as an example (Supplementary File 1). Furthermore, the bibliographies of the identified articles were manually scrutinized to identify additional relevant studies.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Two reviewers independently performed title/abstract screening followed by full-text evaluation. In cases of inter-rater disagreement, a third reviewer was consulted to reach consensus.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) studies involving individuals with T1DM ≥ 18 years; (2) hypoglycaemia occurrence as the primary outcome; (3) cross-sectional, cohort, or case-control designs; (4) peer-reviewed publications in English or Chinese.

Exclusion criteria encompassed: (1) unavailable full-text articles; (2) grey literature (e.g., dissertations, conference proceedings); (3) duplicate publications or review articles.

Data extraction and quality evaluation

Two independent reviewers extracted the following data: first author, publication year, country, study design, sample size, mean age, risk factors, and statistical values.

Cross-sectional studies were evaluated independently by 2 reviewers using the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ), and case-control and cohort studies were evaluated using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) [17, 18]. The AHRQ consists of 11 items with answers of “yes”, “no”, or “unclear”. If the answer is “no” or “unclear”, the score is 0, and if the answer is “yes”, a score of 1–3 indicates low quality, 4–7 indicated medium quality and 8–11 indicated high quality. The NOS included eight items in three blocks: subject selection, comparability between groups, and outcome measurement. The full score was 9, with 0–4 being low quality, 5–6 being moderate quality, and 7–9 being high quality. Any disagreement regarding data extraction or quality assessment was resolved through a full discussion with a third reviewer.

Strength of evidence

Evidence strength for each risk factor was prospectively graded using the validated Available Evidence Scale [19], defined as follows: (1) strong evidence: results from a collection of 3 or more studies, at least 2 of which are high-quality, homogeneous studies or a composite of multiple high-quality studies; (2) moderate evidence: results from 1 high-quality study and the combined results from 1 or more medium- and low-quality studies are statistically significant; (3) limited evidence: results from high-quality studies or a combination of 1 or more medium- and low-quality studies; (4) very limited evidence: no evidence: combined results from multiple studies are statistically significant and heterogeneous results are not related to quality.

Statistical analysis

Included studies were systematically categorized by risk factor type, and the same factors were presented using forest plots. To enhance the robustness of pooled effect estimates, meta-analyses were restricted to risk factors evaluated in at least 3 distinct studies. In Meta-analyses, to adhere to methodological standards for systematic reviews, mitigate selective reporting bias, and enhance result robustness, we quantitatively combined all risk factors in the included literature that reported effect sizes (e.g., risk ratios, odds ratios) even if the statistical tests of the individual original studies did not meet the significance thresholds (P ≥ 0.05). Data from ≤ 2 studies were summarized in tables without formal Meta-analytic synthesis.

Pooled odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were computed to evaluate the association between risk factors and hypoglycaemia occurrence. If OR values were not available, they were converted using software. Heterogeneity was quantified using the I² statistic, with thresholds defined as: low (I²≤25%), moderate (25%< I²≤50%), and high (I²>50%). Sensitivity analyses were conducted for high-heterogeneity results by sequentially removing individual studies to evaluate conclusion stability. Funnel plots were generated when ≥ 10 studies were included in the meta-analysis.

Results

Study selection

A total of 10,857 studies were retrieved from nine databases, reduced to 7,949 after duplicate removal, with 7,728 excluded following title/abstract screening. Exclusion criteria encompassed grey literature (reviews, dissertations, conference proceedings) and studies involving non-adult people with T1DM.

Following full-text evaluation of 221 eligible studies, 190 were excluded for the following reasons: (1) due to coexisting T2DM or undiagnosed diabetes mellitus (n = 47); (2) outcome metrics not met (n = 62); (3) full text could not be found (n = 21); and (4) inclusion of minors (n = 60). Thirty-one studies [20–50], all in English were finally included, with basic information shown in Table 1. The PRISMA flow chart is shown in Fig. 1.

Risk factors for hypoglycaemia

Collectively, 35 potential risk factors were identified from 31 included studies, including Age (years); Age at diagnosis (years); Alcohol; Blood glucose levels; Body mass index (BMI) ; C-peptide; Neuropathy; Co-prescribing; Coefficient of Variation (CV) of blood glucose levels; Diabetes duration; Duration of insulin therapy; Education levels; Fear of hypoglycaemia; Gender; Geographical area; Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c); History of hypoglycaemia; Income; Hypoglycaemia unawareness; Impaired hypoglycaemia Awareness, IHA; Insulin dose; Nephropathy; Private insurance; Self-reported eating speed; Serum angiotensin converting enzyme (ACE) activity ; Serum adiponectin; Smoking; Social status; Subjective intensity of the competition; The type 1 diabetes subtype1; Use of an insulin pump; Multiple daily injections; Bedtime intra-glycemic variability (IGV) ; Retinopathy; Microvascular complications; Macrovascular complications.

In this study, we first screened risk factors evaluated in at least three independent studies to ensure scientific validity. Then, we integrated considerations of the pathological mechanisms of diabetes and clinical guidelines. Finally, 18 risk factors were selected for the meta-analysis, among which 9 showed statistical significance: Disease duration; History of hypoglycaemia; Smoking; Diabetic nephropathy ; Multiple daily insulin injections; Insulin dose; Hypoglycaemic unawareness; Impaired hypoglycaemic awareness and Diabetic neuropathy. Diabetes duration

Eleven studies [21, 26, 31, 35, 36, 38, 39, 42, 44, 46, 48] analysed the effect of diabetes duration on hypoglycaemia in adults with T1DM, a total of 34,892 participants were evaluated, with evidence of a positive association identified between this factor and hypoglycaemia in adult individuals with T1DM. Pooled analysis demonstrated a significant association between diabetes duration and hypoglycaemia (OR = 1.07, 95% CI: 1.01–1.13, P = 0.03) (Fig. 2a).

However, sensitivity analyses were performed due to (I2 = 91%) high heterogeneity between studies. Excluding three studies [21, 35, 42] reduced heterogeneity to 53% while maintaining statistical significance (P < 0.05), whereas the findings remained statistically significant (P < 0.05) (Supplementary File 4: Supplementary Fig. 1a). These excluded studies were identified as potential sources of bias due to their non-prospective design, which could compromise the reliability of outcome assessments. Visual inspection of the funnel plot showed significant asymmetry (Fig. 3a), with all study sites clustered on the left side, suggesting a possible small-sample effect or publication bias.

History of hypoglycaemia

Thirteen studies [20, 23, 24, 30, 33, 35, 37, 38, 40–44] evaluated the association between the hypoglycaemia history and hypoglycaemia in adult individuals with T1DM, involving 29,800 participants and reporting a significant positive correlation. Pooled analysis demonstrated a significant association between prior hypoglycaemia history and hypoglycaemia risk (OR = 2.31, 95% CI: 1.70–3.15, P < 0.00001) (Fig. 2b). Further sensitivity analysis, involving the sequential exclusion of each of the 13 studies, revealed that the pooled odds ratios (ORs) ranged from 2.06 to 2.52, with 95% confidence intervals consistently excluding 1. This confirms the robustness of the study findings. Funnel plots showed almost bilateral symmetry and a low likelihood of being affected by publication bias (Fig. 3b).

Smoking

Four studies [20–22, 35] evaluated the association between smoking and hypoglycaemia in adult individuals with T1DM, involving 25,854 participants and reporting a significant correlation. A review of these studies indicates that none explicitly adjusted for potential confounders such as age, disease duration, or concurrent medications. Pooled analysis revealed a significant positive association between smoking and hypoglycaemia (OR = 1.17, 95% CI: 1.07–1.28, P < 0.0008, I²=42%) (Fig. 2c). The lack of adjustment for confounding factors in the original studies represents a limitation of the current meta-analysis, as unmeasured variables may influence the observed association.

Diabetic nephropathy

Three studies [20, 42, 43] evaluated the association between nephropathy and hypoglycaemia in adult individuals with T1DM, involving 2,344 participants and reporting a significant correlation. Pooled analysis revealed a protective association (OR = 0.83, 95% CI: 0.71–0.98, P = 0.03, I²=45%) (Fig. 2d).

Multiple daily insulin injections

Four studies [36, 38, 39, 41] evaluated the association between multiple daily insulin injections and hypoglycaemia in adult individuals with T1DM, involving 3,931 participants and reporting a significant correlation. Pooled analysis demonstrated a association (OR = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.67–0.87, P < 0.0001, I²=95%) (Fig. 2e). Given that the study by Giorda CB et al. [38] adopted a retrospective design and relied on patient self-reporting, which may introduce recall bias, its exclusion significantly reduced heterogeneity while maintaining statistical significance (OR = 1.96, 95% CI: 1.46–2.61, P < 0.00001, I²=51%). (Supplementary File 4: Supplementary Fig. 1b).

Insulin dose

Eight studies [21, 36, 39, 40, 43, 45, 46, 48] evaluated the association between insulin dose and hypoglycaemia in adult individuals with T1DM, involving 10,150 participants and reporting a significant correlation. Pooled analysis demonstrated a significant effect (OR = 1.14, 95% CI: 1.08–1.21, P < 0.00001, I²=53%) (Fig. 2f). Sensitivity analysis for insulin dose showed that the pooled OR fluctuated from 1.12 to 1.16 after sequentially excluding any of the eight studies, with 95% CIs consistently excluding 1 and I² remaining at 37% to 59%, suggesting that the results were not affected by individual studies, and the consistency of the overall association confirms the reliability of the conclusions.

Hypoglycaemic unawareness

Five studies [22, 31, 42, 43, 46] evaluated the association between hypoglycaemia unawareness and hypoglycaemia in adult individuals with T1DM, involving 3,207 participants and reporting a significant correlation. Pooled analysis demonstrated a strong association (OR = 5.18, 95% CI: 1.81–14.84, P = 0.002, I2 = 97%) (Fig. 2g). Given the small number of included studies (n = 5), meaningful subgroup analyses were not feasible. Instead, sensitivity analyses were conducted, which revealed that the pooled OR values remained within the range of 3.55 to 7.09, with I² values consistently high (94%–98%).

Impaired awareness of hypoglycaemia

Three studies [22, 31, 50] evaluated the association between impaired hypoglycaemia awareness and hypoglycaemia in adult individuals with T1DM, involving 1,436 participants and reporting a significant correlation. Pooled analysis demonstrated a strong association (OR = 5.99, 95% CI: 4.62–7.75, P < 0.00001, I²=0%) (Fig. 2h).

Diabetic neuropathy

Four studies [20, 38, 42, 45] evaluated the association between diabetic neuropathy and hypoglycaemia in adult individuals with T1DM, involving 2,557 participants and reporting a significant correlation. Pooled analysis demonstrated a significant association (OR = 2.05, 95% CI: 1.12–3.76, P = 0.02, I²=80%) (Fig. 2i). Given the small number of included studies (n = 4), meaningful subgroup analyses were not feasible. Heterogeneity tests showed a high degree of heterogeneity between studies (I²=80%), and sensitivity analyses (stepwise exclusion of individual studies) failed to significantly reduce heterogeneity.

Other risk factors

While several risk factors (e.g., educational level, economic income, insurance) have been linked to hypoglycaemia in adult people with T1DM in individual studies, these variables were evaluated in only 1–2 investigations or lacked sufficient statistical power, highlighting limited evidence for their association. Therefore, they did not perform Meta-analyses, but presented the data in tabular form (Supplementary File 5).

Discussion

Methodological rigor and strengths of study design

The study strictly followed the PRISMA guideline framework and was pre-registered on the PROSPERO platform (CRD42024629573), which ensured the transparency and reproducibility of the study process. The risk of literature omission was minimised through a systematic search and the development of a standardised search strategy to provide comprehensive coverage of relevant literature. Two reviewers independently conducted literature screening and data extraction, with discrepancies adjudicated by a third-party reviewer to minimize subjective bias. Methodological quality was evaluated using AHRQ and NOS scales, ensuring a critical appraisal of literature quality to establish a robust foundation for evidence grading. In addition, Meta-analysis only included risk factors validated by at least three independent studies to avoid small-sample bias, and dealt with heterogeneity through sensitivity analysis and random-effects model, resulting in high overall methodological rigour.

Sources of heterogeneity in meta-analysis

Despite the rigorous study design, some risk factors in the Meta-analysis showed significant heterogeneity. The main reasons for this analysis included (1) study design and population heterogeneity; of the 31 studies included, 23 were cross-sectional and 8 were cohort studies, and the two types of designs differed essentially in the temporal associations between exposure and outcome. In addition, there were significant differences in sample characteristics: the age span of participants ranged from 18 to 75 years, the disease duration from 1 to 40 years, the geographic coverage included multiple countries in Europe, America, and Asia, and the overall time span of the studies reached 26 years. Consequently, confounding from the baseline characteristics of the population was difficult to fully correct for through statistical modelling. (2) Inconsistent definitions and measurement criteria: the lack of uniform criteria for defining hypoglycaemia, the use of self-reporting in some studies, and the reliance of others on biochemical tests (blood glucose < 3.9 mmol/L) or clinical events resulted in heterogeneity of outcomes. (3) Inadequate adjustment for confounders: only a few studies corrected for key confounding variables such as insulin dose, treatment regimen, or complications, resulting in combined effect values that were confounded by residual confounding. (4) Limitations of the meta-analyses included the inability to evaluate several potentially critical variables, such as exercise, socioeconomic status, and T1DM subtype, owing to incomplete data reporting in primary studies or insufficient evidence from ≤ 2 investigations.

In summary, although heterogeneity was partially mitigated by random-effects models, its roots reflect the fragmented nature of existing studies. In the future, international multicentre collaboration needs to be promoted to standardise hypoglycaemia studies to enhance data comparability and provide an adequate information base for subgroup analysis.

Diabetes duration: a pivotal risk factor

Duration of diabetes mellitus was established as a significant risk factor in both current and prior investigations, the study by Italian researcher Giorda [38], who reported a threefold higher hypoglycaemia risk in patients with ≥ 20 years of disease duration compared to those with < 10 years. Another study further showed [26] that the risk of hypoglycaemia increased by 39% for each additional 10 years of disease duration. Global hypoglycaemia Assessment Tool (HAT) study [44] and US T1D Exchange study [21], alongside other investigations, demonstrated that diabetes duration is a significant risk factor, aligning with our findings. The effect of disease duration may be associated with beta cell failure, autonomic neuropathy and impaired hypoglycaemic awareness [31]. In addition, prolonged disease duration is often accompanied by autonomic neuropathy, which impairs the patient’s ability to perceive hypoglycaemia and creates asymptomatic hypoglycaemia [46]. For patients with ≥ 10 years of diabetes duration, glycemic target adjustment may be warranted to balance hypoglycaemia risk and long-term complication management [51]. In clinical practice, dynamic targets should be set taking into account patient age, comorbidities, and lifestyle. For patients with longer disease duration, education on hypoglycaemia recognition and coping should be strengthened, including symptom recognition, emergency management, and frequency of blood glucose monitoring.

Prior hypoglycemia history and risk of recurrence

Previous hypoglycaemia history serves as a key determinant of hypoglycaemia recurrence in adult individuals with T1DM, independent of factors including disease duration and glycemic control, as shown in current and prior research. The US DCCT/EDIC cohort study [23] identified prior severe hypoglycaemia as the strongest predictor of recurrent hypoglycaemia, with this association persisting independent of treatment intensity. The study also highlighted that a history of previous hypoglycaemia significantly increased the risk of follow-up even after 30 years of follow-up, suggesting its importance as a long-term predictor.

This study also found that impaired hypoglycaemic awareness was associated with an elevated risk even after excluding a history of hypoglycaemia, further supporting the cumulative effect of history. He Y et al. [44] conducted a large multinational study, which confirmed that a prior history of hypoglycemia was significantly associated with both non-severe and severe hypoglycemia, and remained independently associated after adjusting for HbA₁c, insulin dose, and other factors. Meanwhile, the history of hypoglycemia may create a vicious cycle by lowering the hypoglycemic threshold or impairing the autonomic response. A large cross-sectional study in Denmark [35] showed a dose-response relationship between the frequency of previous hypoglycemia and subsequent risk, and the predictive value of the history of hypoglycemia remained significant after adjusting for comorbidities. Therefore, patients with a history of previous hypoglycaemia should be included in the management of high-risk groups, with individualised glycaemic targets and priority given to the use of ambulatory glucose monitoring (CGM) to identify asymptomatic hypoglycaemia [52]. Currently, updated automated insulin delivery (AID) systems incorporating CGM technology include an automatic insulin suspension function to avoid hypoglycemia [53]. In patients with frequent hypoglycaemia, switching to insulin pumps or basal-meal insulin analogues is recommended to reduce glycaemic fluctuations [54]. At the same time, over-intensification of therapy should be avoided, especially at night when basal insulin dose needs to be carefully adjusted [55]. For patients with a previous history of hypoglycaemia, training in hypoglycaemia recognition and response is required, including symptom recognition, emergency management and frequency of blood glucose monitoring. Combined with psychological assessment tools, fearful behaviours should be identified and intervention strategies developed.

Insulin administration parameters and hypoglycemia risk

The frequency, dose and mode of insulin administration are core risk factors for hypoglycaemia in individuals with T1DM. Meta-analysis identified multiple daily insulin injections and insulin dose as independent risk factors for hypoglycaemia in adult individuals with T1DM. This finding is strongly aligned with results from multiple high-quality investigations. A landmark German prospective study [24] identified insulin doses ≥ 0.5 U/kg/d as an independent hypoglycaemia predictor. Subsequent global investigations, including a 2017 Polish cohort study [26], confirmed a 1.69-fold increased hypoglycaemia risk in insulin pump users versus traditional injection groups, likely due to continuous insulin infusion dynamics and patient-specific dose adjustment sensitivity. A 2023 Chinese multicenter study [36] reported that patients receiving ≥ 3 daily insulin injections or insulin pump therapy had significantly higher hypoglycaemia rates, thereby highlighting intensive insulin therapy as a risk factor. Furthermore, the investigation demonstrated that for every 0.1 U/kg/d increment in insulin dose, the risk of hypoglycaemia increased by 14%. This dose-dependent association may be related to decreased insulin sensitivity, declining β-cell function, and patients’ inadequate ability to cope with blood glucose fluctuations. Concurrently, the meta-analysis of multiple daily insulin injections (MDI) in this study revealed substantial heterogeneity. This variability may stem from several factors: at the study design level, the retrospective study by Giorda CB et al. [38], which relied on patient self-reporting, was prone to recall bias. After excluding this study, heterogeneity was reduced to 51% while statistical significance remained, indicating that differences in study types and data collection methods are key contributing factors. In terms of insulin treatment regimens, variations in MDI combinations, dose adjustment strategies, and patient compliance directly impact hypoglycaemia risk. Additionally, heterogeneity in patient baseline characteristics—including age, disease duration, HbA1c levels, comorbidities, and concomitant medications—may modulate the strength of the association between MDI and hypoglycaemia. Furthermore, regional differences in diagnostic and treatment guidelines, as well as disparities in medical resources, could influence the implementation of MDI and the monitoring of hypoglycaemia. Future studies should adopt prospective multicenter designs, incorporate standardized objective monitoring of hypoglycaemia, refine MDI treatment protocols, and integrate patient baseline characteristics to construct comprehensive risk models. This approach will enable accurate analysis of the underlying association mechanisms and optimize clinical intervention strategies.

In recent years, the use of insulin pumps has led to precise insulin dosing in patients, but an Italian exercise study [25] found a reduced risk of hypoglycaemia in insulin pump users during high-intensity exercise, but still a high incidence of asymptomatic hypoglycaemia on daily monitoring. The Global HAT study [28], on the other hand, noted that insulin pump use was significantly associated with non-severe hypoglycaemia, which may be related to patients’ lack of adaptation to pump therapy or untimely dose adjustment. Based on the above evidence, individualised insulin regimens should be developed for patients, and insulin doses should be adjusted cautiously in patients with long disease duration and low BMI. Preference should be given to basal-mealtime insulin regimens over multiple premixed injections to reduce glycaemic fluctuations [23]. Patient education and self-management also need to be strengthened, and for patients using insulin pumps, pump function and infusion sites should be assessed in a timely manner to avoid catheter blockage or abnormal insulin absorption [56]. In clinical practice, precise protocols should be formulated by taking into account individual patient characteristics, and risks should be reduced through technical assistance and enhanced education [57]. Future investigations should prioritize evaluating the safety profiles of novel insulin formulations and the clinical translation of smart management tools.

Adverse lifestyle factors: smoking and alcohol consumption

Adverse lifestyle habits have an equally important impact on the occurrence of hypoglycaemia. Meta-analysis in our study identified smoking as an independent risk factor for hypoglycaemia in adult individuals with T1DM, consistent with findings from multiple high-quality investigations. The US WESDR cohort study [20] reported current smokers had a 2.6-fold higher hypoglycaemia risk compared to non-smokers, independent of HbA1c and insulin dose. A recent Chinese multicenter investigation [36] further demonstrated a 1.45-fold increased hypoglycaemia incidence in smokers consuming ≥ 10 cigarettes/day, indicating a dose-dependent relationship. Despite the low strength of evidence for alcohol consumption, several studies suggest a potential association: the global HAT study [28] observed a 1.23-fold higher hypoglycaemia risk with ≥ 20 g/day alcohol intake, possibly due to alcohol-induced suppression of hepatic glycogenolysis. Hohendorff et al. [25] subsequently explained that alcohol consumption acutely increases insulin sensitivity by 30–40% within 6–8 h, heightening nocturnal hypoglycaemia risk [58]. Implement smoking cessation counselling for all diabetic patients who smoke, with recommendations for nicotine replacement therapy or pharmacological interventions such as bupropion. It is important to emphasize that smoking cessation reduces the risk of hypoglycaemia and improves cardiovascular outcomes [59]. Additionally, personalized plans for smoking cessation and alcohol restriction should be developed, incorporating nutritional education and exercise guidance [60]. Smoking cessation should be a priority intervention in clinical practice [61], with individualised glycaemic management for patients who drink alcohol. Future studies need to further clarify the safety thresholds for alcohol consumption and the impact of cultural differences to develop precise prevention strategies.

Diabetic complications and hypoglycemia risk

Diabetic complications, as critical determinants of disease progression, also exhibit significant associations with hypoglycemia risk. In this study, meta-analysis revealed that diabetic neuropathy constitutes a significant risk factor for hypoglycemia in adult individuals with T1DM. This finding is consistent with previous research. The study by Peng et al. [62] demonstrated that patients with peripheral neuropathy had a 0.94-fold increased risk of hypoglycemic unawareness. Additionally, a Chinese multicenter observational study conducted by Chen et al. [36] showed that the incidence of hypoglycemia in patients with neuropathy was 1.89 times higher than that in non-neuropathy controls, with nocturnal asymptomatic hypoglycemia accounting for 45% of all cases, thereby highlighting the clinical concern regarding nocturnal hypoglycemia. The mechanism underlying nocturnal asymptomatic hypoglycemia is linked to impaired compensatory responses to hypoglycemia and blunted symptom perception due to autonomic dysfunction [63]. Several other studies [20, 28] have also indicated that individuals with T1DM complicated by diabetic retinopathy and vasculopathy are more prone to developing hypoglycemia.

Among diabetic complications, diabetic nephropathy showed an inverse association with hypoglycemia risk in our meta-analysis. However, a 2017 study by Gubitosi-Klug et al. [23] reported that patients with chronic kidney disease (estimated glomerular filtration rate < 60 mL/min/1.73 m²) exhibited a 25–30% reduction in insulin clearance, leading to a significantly elevated risk of hypoglycemia. This discrepancy may be attributed to several factors: stricter clinical adjustment of insulin doses to compensate for renal dysfunction in patients with chronic kidney disease, the inclusion of early-stage kidney disease in the study population, and variations in diagnostic criteria. Limitations of the current study include insufficient adjustment for confounding factors such as insulin dose and HbA1c, selection bias inherent to the cross-sectional design, and measurement bias and publication bias in the diagnosis of hypoglycemia and kidney disease. Clinically, attention should be paid to individualized insulin dose adjustment in patients with kidney disease, and more prospective studies are warranted to clarify the causal relationship between diabetic nephropathy and hypoglycemia.

Hypoglycemia susceptibility: impaired awareness and unawareness

Impaired hypoglycaemia Awareness (IHA) and hypoglycaemia unawareness are strong evidential risk factors for hypoglycaemia in patients with T1DM, and the core mechanism involves autonomic dysfunction and beta-cell functional decline [64]. In this study, IHA was found to be an independent risk factor for hypoglycaemia in adult individuals with T1DM by Meta-analysis. Previous studies have confirmed that patients with longer disease duration and HbA1c < 7% have a higher chance of IHA [23, 25]. Meta-analysis of hypoglycaemic unawareness (defined as asymptomatic hypoglycaemia) showed a significantly higher risk, with existing studies [28] stating that hypoglycaemic unawareness occurs in up to 22% of patients with T1DM, which is 1.5-fold higher compared to patients with T2DM. Based on the above evidence, it is recommended that patients with ≥ 5 years of disease duration, insulin pumps or HbA1c < 7% should be assessed annually for hypoglycaemic awareness, using both the “Hypoglycaemia Awareness Questionnaire” [65] and the “Gold Hypoglycaemia Awareness Scale” [66]. In patients with impaired awareness [67], improving glucose targets and optimising insulin regimens can further aid patients in recognizing atypical symptoms such as fatigue and mood changes through simulated hypoglycaemia education, including video presentations. This study also found that IHA and Hypoglycaemia unawareness, which focus on early identification, mainly relied on questionnaires [68], with wide variations in the sensitivity of existing questionnaires, and the precision of these findings requires enhancement. Consequently, future investigations should focus on developing novel biomarkers such as heart rate variability assessment, while leveraging objective metrics to improve detection accuracy.

Unanalyzed risk factors and clinical considerations

Although this study included 9 risk factors through meta-analysis, certain variables could not be included in the analysis due to limited study availability or inability to extract relevant statistical data, but still need to be focused on based on clinical significance and available evidence. Previous studies have found [23, 24] that C-peptide levels can be used as a predictive biomarker of hypoglycaemia, especially in patients with a disease duration of ≥ 10 years, and that insulin dosage needs to be monitored and adjusted on a regular basis. A Chinese multicenter study [36] showed that HbA1c < 7% was significantly correlated with hypoglycaemia risk, yet the global HAT study failed to detect this association, probably due to differences in the study populations.It is recommended to intensify glucose monitoring in patients with HbA1c < 7%, especially at night and during exercise, to avoid overly tight control.

Previous studies [23] demonstrated a 2.5-fold elevated risk of hypoglycaemia in patients with pubertal onset (< 20 years of age), which may be associated with hormonal fluctuations and insulin resistance. Subsequent strategies should prioritize enhancing dynamic glucose monitoring in this population and formulating refined therapeutic regimens tailored to the patterns of hormonal fluctuations and insulin resistance, so as to mitigate the risk of hypoglycemia. Female gender was identified as a significant hypoglycaemia risk factor in multiple investigationss [28, 36], which may be related to oestrogen affecting insulin sensitivity. Education for young female patients needs to be strengthened to pay attention to the effect of the menstrual cycle on blood glucose and to adjust the insulin dose [69]. BMI and coefficient of variation (CV) of blood glucose are both associated with the occurrence of hypoglycaemia [70], but the number of studies is too small, and a larger study is expected to further validate it. There is also a need to focus on patients with lower socioeconomic status and advocate for society to provide free glucose monitoring devices and multilingual educational materials to reduce health inequalities [71]. Overall, these factors not included in the Meta-analysis have limited strength of evidence, but they need to be considered comprehensively in clinical practice, especially for young women, low BMI and patients with lower socioeconomic status, to implement individualised prevention strategies.

In the future, large-sample studies are needed to clarify their causal relationships and intervention effects, such as clarifying the threshold effect of C-peptide, the mechanism of gender differences, and the quantification of socioeconomic factors.

Strengthens and limitations

This study represents the first comprehensive and systematic review of risk factors for hypoglycaemia in adults individuals with T1DM. By pooling data from 31 studies involving 54,634 patients—of which 27 met high-quality standards—it provides a broad and representative sample base for exploring these risk factors. However, the study is not without limitations: first, the inclusion of only Chinese and English literature may introduce language bias and publication bias. Additionally, some studies exhibit high heterogeneity, which affects the consistency of results, and potential publication bias cannot be overlooked; second, most of the included studies adopted a cross-sectional design, limiting causal inference and readily introducing recall bias; third, the definition and measurement of hypoglycaemia lacked standardization, with some studies failing to distinguish between severe and non-severe events; fourth, insufficient data on key variables (such as C-peptide levels, continuous glucose monitoring, etc.) and the lack of standardization of relevant assessment tools hinder a comprehensive analysis of risk factors; fifth, the diversity in study designs, patient demographics, and treatment regimens further exacerbates the heterogeneity of the results. Future research should prioritize promoting international collaboration to establish standardized definitions and shared databases for hypoglycaemia research, while exploring the neural mechanisms and identifying novel biomarkers for impaired hypoglycaemia awareness. Additionally, conducting precision intervention studies to optimize preventive strategies, quantifying the impact of socioeconomic factors on hypoglycaemia risk, and investigating risk differences in special populations, such as adolescents, the elderly, and minority groups are essential. Moreover, exploring the potential of digital tools and developing predictive models based on biomarkers could enhance risk stratification and personalized management for individuals with T1DM.

Conclusion

In summary, while extensive research has identified numerous risk factors for hypoglycaemia in adult people with T1DM, this review consolidates nine robustly validated determinants: Diabetes duration; History of previous hypoglycaemia; Smoking; Renal disease; Multiple daily insulin injections; Insulin metering; Hypoglycaemic unconsciousness; Impaired hypoglycaemic awareness; Neuropathy. These nine risk factors could provide a basis for future hypoglycaemia management. However, limitations in study inclusion criteria and statistical power precluded meta-analyses for several variables. Consequently, we advocate large-scale multicenter original studies targeting these nine determinants to inform clinical risk stratification and prevent hypoglycaemia in adult people with T1DM.
