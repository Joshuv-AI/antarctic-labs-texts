Quantitation of Vaporized γ‐Aminobutyric Acid in Cigarette Smoke Extract From e‐Cigarettes by the Combination of HPLC‐Fluorescence Detection and Derivatization With DBD‐F
Authors: Mitsuhiro Wada, Haruna Onodera, Makoto Takada, Suzuna Saruwatari, Junpei Mutoh, Ken Tachibana, Tadashi Hayama, Naoya Kishikawa, Naotaka Kuroda
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGamma‐aminobutyric acid (GABA), which has attracted much attention as a bioactive ingredient, is used in functional foods. Recently, electronic cigarette (e‐cigarette) products for inhaling vaporized GABA have become commercially available. In this study, we developed a high‐performance liquid chromatography–fluorescence method for detecting GABA derivatized with 4‐(N,N‐dimethylaminosulfonyl)‐7‐fluoro‐2,1,3‐benzoxadiazole (DBD‐F) in cigarette smoke extract (CSE). The vaporized GABA captured in CSE was derivatized with DBD‐F under moderate conditions (80°C, 30 min). After chromatographic separation, the DBD‐GABA derivative was detected at 437 and 558 nm. The calibration curve of GABA ranging from 10 to 20,000 ng/mL showed good linearity (more than 0.999). The limit of detection at a signal‐to‐noise ratio of 3 was 1.1 ng/mL. The method was applied to the detection of GABA in e‐liquid and CSE for two kinds of e‐cigarette products, and the estimated collection efficiency of GABA was approximately 25%. Furthermore, the detection of minor components, such as glutamine, glutamic acid, and arginine, in the e‐liquid proved that the GABA used in the e‐cigarette was prepared by microbial fermentation.Keywords: γ‐aminobutyric acid (GABA), cigarette smoke extract, DBD‐F, e‐cigarette, HPLC‐fluorescence detection

Introduction

Gamma‐aminobutyric acid (GABA) is a non‐protein amino acid that is naturally and ubiquitously present in nature, including in animals, plants, and microorganisms [1]. GABA has attracted considerable attention due to its various physiological implications in humans. In particular, GABA plays a vital role in synaptic transmission as a major inhibitory neurotransmitter in the human nervous system; it regulates neuronal development and can improve sleep and mood [2, 3]. It is believed that GABA supplementation through foods can provide beneficial effects for human health, and the food industry has thus shown great interest in GABA‐enriched foods. However, the GABA levels in natural foods are generally low [4], so it is usually added exogenously. Currently, GABA is mainly produced through chemical synthesis [1] or microbial fermentation [5]. Notably, chemically synthesized GABA is not allowed to be used as a food additive in the food industry due to the possible toxicity of the reagents used [1]. It is possible to determine the origin of the GABA in GABA‐enriched foods, since the presence of glutamic acid, arginine, or glutamine, which are used in microbial fermentation, indicates that the GABA was produced by microbial fermentation [6, 7].

More recently, electronic cigarette (e‐cigarette) products for inhaling vaporized GABA have become commercially available and are commonly used. To inhale GABA, a battery, a heating coil, a reservoir or cartridge packed with e‐liquid containing GABA, an atomizer to convert the e‐liquid to an aerosol, and a mouthpiece for e‐cigarette products are required. Currently, two types of e‐cigarettes are commercially available: refill types with a separated instrument body and e‐liquid cartridge and disposable types that have an integrated e‐liquid cartridge in the body. Its appropriate use could be achieved by accumulation of basic data of the product. However, no quality evaluations of the e‐cigarette products for inhaling GABA have been performed, because there is no method for detecting and examining the vaporized GABA from e‐cigarettes. In our previous study, caffeine in e‐liquid and cigarette smoke extract (CSE) was detected and examined by a newly developed liquid chromatography (LC)–tandem mass spectrometry (MS) method [8, 9]. The presence of several other minor components, such as theophylline, theobromine, and paraxanthine, in the e‐liquid and CSE were revealed for the first time.

To detect GABA, several analytical methods, such as high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)‐fluorescence (FL) detection [10, 11, 12, 13] and LC–MS [14], have been developed. These methods require derivatization. For FL detection, GABA is reacted with o‐phthalaldehyde (OPA) and N‐acetyl‐L‐cysteine. These methods have been applied to detect GABA in brain tissue homogenates [10] and microdialysates [11], and they were sensitive enough for the detection of GABA in the samples. However, rapid measurement was required due to the instability of the derivative. Additionally, dansyl chloride [12], and napthalene‐2,3‐dicarboxaldehyde (NDA) [13] were used for determination of GABA. On the other hand, GABA derivatized with the succinimide reagent N‐α‐Boc‐l‐tryptophan hydroxysuccinimide ester could be detected by LC–MS, and this method was applied to detect GABA in microdialysates [14]. This method was sensitive and selective, but it required the use of expensive instruments. Recently, ELISA kit as a semi‐determination method was used for determination of GABA. That could be sensitively determined GABA in biological sample, but is expensive [15]. In our previous work, sulfur‐containing amino acids were derivatized with 4‐(N,N‐dimethylaminosulfonyl)‐7‐fluoro‐2,1,3‐benzoxadiazole (DBD‐F) and could be detected in biological samples with high sensitivity [16]. So sensitive determination of GABA with DBD‐F could be expected. In the present study, we developed an HPLC‐FL method to detect GABA that was derivatized with DBD‐F in the CSE from e‐cigarettes. We also validated the method, and examined the stability of the derivative (DBD‐GABA). Furthermore, we applied the method to detect GABA in commercially available e‐cigarette products, and attempted to determine the manufacturing process of the GABA by examining the minor components in the e‐liquid.

Experimental

Materials and Reagents

The GABA (99.4%), methanol (HPLC‐grade), acetonitrile (HPLC‐grade), and phosphoric acid were purchased from Fujifilm Wako Chemicals (Osaka, Japan). The DBD‐F was purchased from Tokyo Kasei Kogyo (Tokyo, Japan). L‐Arginine (Arg), L‐glutamine (Glu), L‐glutamic acid (Gln), and sodium dihydrogenphosphate dihydrate were purchased from Nacalai Tesque (Tokyo, Japan). Ultra‐pure water was prepared using a PURELAB flex‐UV from Organo (Tokyo, Japan). The other reagents used were of analytical reagent‐grade. Two commercially available e‐cigarette products for inhaling GABA (products A and B) were tested; product A was a refill type of e‐cigarette, and product B was a disposable type of e‐cigarette. The heating temperature was set to 200°C (wattage at 5 W) for both products. In addition to GABA, the e‐liquid contained propylene glycol and coconut flavoring, as indicated on the package insert.

CSE Sample Collection and Derivatization With DBD‐F

Preparation of the GABA CSE was performed according to our previous method [8]. Briefly, the cigarette smoke generation condition was set at 10 puffs per e‐cigarette using the puffing regimen parameters of the Cooperation Centre for Scientific Research Relative to Tobacco (CORESTA) approach (55 mL, 3 s, and 1 puff/30 s for the puff volume, duration, and interval, respectively). To obtain the smoke, a vacuum pump (Minipump MP‐Σ 100HN II, Shibata Scientific Technology Ltd., Saitama, Japan) was used for 3 s/puff at a constant flow rate of 1.1 L/min. The e‐cigarette smoke was passed through 5 mL of 50% acetonitrile.

To 100 μL of e‐liquid or CSE, 40 μL of 400 mmol/L borate buffer (pH 8.5) and 10 mmol/L DBD‐F in acetonitrile were added, and the sample was heated at 80°C for 30 min. After cooling in ice water, the reaction solution was filtered with a membrane (0.45 μm). The obtained filtrate was applied to HPLC analysis. Each e‐liquid was squeezed out from the liquid‐soaked cotton that was inside of the e‐cigarette cartridges. The volume of the obtained e‐liquid was measured, and then it was diluted with 50% acetonitrile aqueous solution. Triplicate measurements were performed to detect the GABA in the e‐liquid or CSE samples. To detect GABA in product A, the CSE was obtained by suctioning 10 consecutive puffs from one product. For product B, e‐liquid was squeezed out after the inhalation of 10 puffs, treat as well as product A.

HPLC System and Conditions

The HPLC system for the detection of GABA consisted of two LC‐20ad chromatographic pumps (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan), a DGU‐20A5R degasser (Shimadzu), an SIL‐20 AC autosampler (Shimadzu) set to an injection volume of 5 μL, an Imtakt Union UK‐C1 column (100 × 3.0 mm in diameter; 3 μm; Imtakt, Kyoto, Japan), a CTO‐20A column oven set at 35°C (Shimadzu), an RF‐20AXS spectrofluorometric detector (Shimadzu), and an LC workstation (LabSolutions/LC solution version 5.92; Shimadzu). In addition, 10 mmol/L phosphate buffer (pH 3.7 for GABA, and pH 5.0 for the minor components; solvent A) and MeOH (solvent B) were used as the mobile phase at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min. The gradient conditions for the detection of GABA were as follows: 0.0–1.0 min, 10% B; 1.1–4.0 min, 10%–50% B; 4.0–8.0 min, 50% B, 8.1–12.0 min, 99% B. For the identification of the minor components, the following conditions were used: 0.0–4.0 min, 10%–50% B; 4.0–8.0 min, 50% B, 8.1–12.0 min, 99% B. The column was re‐equilibrated with the initial ratio of B for 3 min. Detection wavelengths of λex = 437 nm and λem = 558 nm were used.

Method Validation

Detection of GABA in the e‐liquid and CSE from the e‐cigarette products was performed using a standard calibration curve. The GABA concentration range was 10 to 20,000 ng/mL. The limit of detection (LOD) and limit of quantification (LOQ) of GABA in the CSE were defined as the concentration at which the signal‐to‐noise (S/N) ratio was 3 and 10, respectively. The accuracy and the precision for intra‐day and inter‐day measurements were evaluated using quality control (QC) CSEs from product A spiked with three different concentrations of the standard (50, 2000, or 10,000 ng/mL). The accuracy percentage was expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), and precision was expressed as the relative SD (RSD) from triplicate measurements. The recovery of GABA was evaluated using the ratio of the slope of the spiked calibration curve prepared using the three QC solutions of the standard.

The stability of DBD‐GABA at 4°C and room temperature was evaluated. The CSE from product A was used and storage in autosampler. The GABA concentration is expressed as the mean ± SD (n = 3).

Results and Discussion

Establishment of the HPLC‐FL Method

To separate DBD‐GABA from interfering peaks, several kinds of columns were examined. Two micrograms per milliliters of GABA standard was used as sample. At first, octadecylsilica (ODS) columns such as XSelect HSS C18 (50 × 4.6 mm, 2.5 μm, Waters) and XBridge C18 (50 × 2.1 mm, 2.5 μm, Waters) were examined. The mixture of phosphate buffer and organic solvent (methanol or acetonitrile) was used as mobile phase. To retain DBD‐GABA, high water content in mobile phase (more than 70%) was needed due to low hydrophobicity of DBD‐GABA. Also, high water content in mobile phase caused decreasing fluorescence intensity of DBD‐GABA. To increase organic solvent content in mobile phase, Imtakt Unison UK‐C1 (100 × 3 mm, 3 μm) and Imtakt Unison UK‐C8 (100 × 3 mm, 3 μm) were examined. Other types of columns, Thermo Scientific Hypercarb (50 × 2.1 mm, 3 μm) and Develosil RPAQEOUS AR (150 × 2 mm, 3 μm), were also examined. In addition, several mobile phase compositions were used. The results indicated that DBD‐GABA could be easily separated from interfering peaks on the Imtakt Union UK‐C1 column with a gradient elution of phosphate buffer (10 mM, pH 3.7) and methanol. The retention time of DBD‐GABA was 6.8 min, and HPLC analysis, including re‐equilibration of the column, was completed within 15 min of runtime. Representative chromatograms are shown in Figure 1.

To determine sensitively, the effects of the DBD‐F concentration (1, 5, 10, 20, 50, or 100 mM), reaction temperature (50°C°C–100°C), and reaction time (10–90 min) on the peak area of GABA were examined (Figure 2) using the CSE from product A. DBD‐F at 10 mM or more gave a maximum and constant peak area (Figure 2a). In addition, a reaction time of more than 20 min gave a maximum and constant peak area (Figure 2b). Therefore, 30 min of reaction time was selected for the following experiments. A reaction temperature of 80°C gave the maximum peak area (Figure 2c).

Several collection solvents, such as H2O, 50% methanol, methanol, 50% acetonitrile, and acetonitrile, for the preparation of CSE were examined. We found that 50% acetonitrile gave the maximum peak area of DBD‐GABA (data not shown). Furthermore, the effects of the solvent volume on the peak area were examined (Figure 3). Method Validation

Using the established conditions, a calibration curve of GABA was prepared using 10 to 20,000 ng/mL of the standard solution with good linearity (more than 0.999, n = 3). The LOD and LOQ at a S/N ratio of 3 and 10 were 1.1 and 3.8 ng/mL, respectively. The accuracy as well as the precision for intra‐day and inter‐day measurements were evaluated using the QC CSEs (from product A) spiked with three concentrations of the standard (Table 1). The accuracy ranged from 92.0% to 101.7%, and the precision for intra‐day and inter‐day measurements was less than 2.6% and 3.8%, respectively. These findings indicated that the derivatization reaction and the HPLC analysis of the HPLC‐FL method were precise and reproducible. The recovery of GABA, which was evaluated using the ratio of the slope of the spiked calibration curve prepared using the three QC solutions of the standard was 97.3% ± 3.3% (n = 3). This indicated that the use of the standard calibration curve for the detection of GABA in CSE is reasonable. Furthermore, the stability of DBD‐GABA in the CSE from product A, which was stored in the autosampler (at 4°C or room temperature), was evaluated, as shown in Figure 4. After 24 h of reaction at both temperatures, the DBD‐GABA in the sample solutions remained stable. Thus, the HPLC‐FL method might be useful for multi‐sample processing.

Although the HPLC‐FL method was sensitive enough for the detection of GABA in CSE, the LOD (1.1 ng/mL) was sensitive than that of dansyl chloride method (0.35 μg/mL) [12], less sensitive than that of the conventional OPA method (0.1 ng/mL) [10], and NDA method (0.31 ng/mL) [13], and comparable to that of the LC–MS method (1.3 ng/mL) [14]. However, the GABA derivative that reacts with OPA is unstable, so HPLC analysis is needed within 10 min after derivatization. NDA derivatives was stable, but indicated toxicity [13]. Moreover, sensitivity of DBD derivatives could be enhanced by using chemiluminescence detection [17].

Quantitation of GABA in the e‐Liquid and CSE

The HPLC‐FL method was applied for the detection of GABA in the e‐liquid and CSE of two commercial e‐cigarette products (A and B), and the results are summarized in Tables 2 and 3. The GABA concentrations in the e‐liquid for products A and B were 3.05 and 3.33 mg/mL, respectively. The estimated inhalational GABA per 1 mL of cigarette smoke for products A and B was 60.0 ± 1.0 and 102.1 ± 15.5 ng/mL, respectively (n = 3; Table 2). Next, the GABA concentration in the CSE obtained by consecutive suctions was determined. The GABA concentration in products A and B were 1.47 ± 0.15 and 2.85 ± 0.08 μg/mL, respectively. The amount of extract per 1 mL of cigarette smoke for products A and B was 13.40 and 25.91 ng/mL, respectively (n = 3; Table 3). The collection efficiency of GABA was calculated, and the estimated inhalational GABA per 1 mL of cigarette smoke for products A and B was 22.3% and 25.4%, respectively, under the collection conditions used in this study. In our previous study, the collection efficiency of caffeine determined using the same conditions and instruments was 15.6% to 28.2% [8]. As shown in Figure 3, the GABA concentration in the CSE decreased as the collection solvent volume increased. This indicated that most of the absorbable GABA in the CSE could be capture by the collection solvent in our system. To improve the collection yield, absorbable GABA for collection solvent should be increased.

The GABA concentrations in the CSE from product A (refill type) varied (RSD%: 10.5%); in contrast, the concentration in the e‐liquid and CSE from product B (disposable type) did not vary. Appleton et al. have reported that variations in the sample content and e‐liquid volume accounted for the variations in the GABA concentration [18]. However, the e‐liquid volume and GABA contents did not vary in this study. The e‐liquid cartridge contains a heating coil and liquid GABA‐soaked cotton. In a previous study, the coil condition notably affected the aerosol mass from e‐cigarettes [19]. Therefore, the variation observed in the present study might be due to the coil condition in the e‐liquid cartridge.

To check the quality of the products, we monitored the GABA concentration in the CSE (per 10 puffs) obtained from consecutive suctions from product A (Figure 5). The GABA concentration rapidly decreased until the 50th puff, then continued to decrease gradually until the 260th puff (indicated number of inhalations on the e‐cigarette). The final GABA concentration was 18.4% ± 6.5% of the initial concentration (n = 3). There are no comparable data from previous studies to check the adequacy of our results.

Peak are of DBD‐GABA obtained by consecutive suction of e‐cigarette product A (n = 3). The initial peak area was taken as 100.

To identify the manufacturing process of the GABA, we examined the minor components in the e‐liquid of product A. Different elution conditions were used to separate the minor components from the GABA and interfering peaks. Representative chromatograms are shown in Figure 6. The retention times of the peaks on the chromatograms from the e‐liquid sample corresponded to Glu, Gln, and Arg. The retention times of the peaks for Glu, Gln, Arg, and GABA were 3.5, 4.1, 5.1, and 5.9 min, respectively. From these results, we determined that the GABA used in product A was prepared by microbial fermentation. Several microorganisms, such as Escherichia coli and lactic acid bacteria, are utilized for efficient GABA fermentative production [7]. These microorganisms have glutamate decarboxylase activity that converts Glu to GABA. Also, the presence of Arg [6] and Gln [7] was also found. Thus, the HPLC‐FL method is useful for determining the manufacturing process of GABA. However, these minor components could not be detected in the CSE by the HPLC‐FL method.

Limitations of This Study

The limitations of this study are as follows. Only two products were examined in the present study, since there are currently not many e‐cigarette products for inhaling vaporized GABA on the market. To improve the collection efficiency, the equipment used for preparing CSE should be improved. In addition, although our discussion on the variations in the GABA concentration of the CSE were based on three separate pieces of data (precise GABA concentrations in the e‐liquid of products A and B, and in the CSE of product B), further studies are required to clarify the reason for the variation.

Conclusions

We developed an HPLC‐FL method for the detection of GABA derivatized with DBD‐F in CSE. Using this method, we reported for the first time the detection of vaporized GABA generated from e‐cigarettes. From the validation parameters and the stability of the derivative, it is clear that sensitive and reproducible measurements can be obtained with this method. Furthermore, this method might be useful for obtaining basic information on e‐cigarette quality for estimating the risks to human health. The findings of this study also underscore the need for regulatory enforcement of manufacturing QC for e‐cigarettes.
