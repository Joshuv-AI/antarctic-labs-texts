Integrative machine learning and single-nucleus transcriptomics reveal MAPK-centered neuronal responses underlying the potential Alzheimer's disease risk of GenX
Authors: Zongtang Xu, Yao Yang, Kun Xiang, Linpei Zhuo, Zheyu Fang, Xiaoou Lin, Conghui Dai, Jiaming Liu, Jing Sun, Qiuling Tong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hexafluoropropylene oxide-dimer acid (GenX or HFPO-DA) is a novel per- and polyfluoroalkyl substance developed as a replacement for legacy compounds, yet its potential neurotoxic effects remain poorly understood. In this study, we combined bibliometric profiling, in silico target prediction, in vivo behavioral assessments, single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq), network biology, machine learning, molecular docking, and in vitro validation to explore the potential relevance of GenX in Alzheimer's disease (AD). Bibliometric analysis revealed increasing research attention to GenX-associated health hazards, including emerging concerns regarding brain-related effects. Target prediction identified 301 putative GenX-related genes, which were significantly enriched in AD-related pathways. Behavioral analyses demonstrated that chronic GenX exposure impaired recognition memory and spatial learning in mice. Analysis of human prefrontal cortex snRNA-seq data revealed pronounced transcriptional alterations in AD neurons and identified 200 AD-related neuronal DEGs. Integration with GenX targets yielded 10 overlapping genes, which were further prioritized through protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis. Machine learning further identified an 8-gene signature with robust diagnostic performance across training and external validation cohorts. Molecular docking showed favorable binding affinities between GenX and core target proteins. Finally, GenX exposure reduced SH-SY5Y cell viability, activated the RAS-RAF-MEK-ERK cascade, and promoted apoptosis-related alterations, supporting a MAPK-centered neurotoxic mechanism potentially relevant to AD-associated neuronal vulnerability. Collectively, this integrative multi-level analysis provides mechanistic insights into the potential neurotoxic effects of GenX and underscores its possible relevance to neurodegeneration-associated molecular processes in AD.

Introduction

Hexafluoropropylene oxide-dimer acid (GenX or HFPO-DA) is a short-chain per- and polyfluoroalkyl substance (PFAS) developed as a replacement for legacy long-chain compounds such as perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA) [1,2]. Compared with traditional long-chain PFAS, GenX was designed to exhibit improved physicochemical properties, including reduced bioaccumulation potential and enhanced industrial performance [3]. However, despite these intended advantages, GenX retains substantial environmental persistence and has been widely detected in surface water, groundwater, soil, and biological samples [1,[4], [5], [6]], raising increasing concerns regarding its environmental fate and potential health impacts. Although initially considered a safer alternative to long-chain PFAS, accumulating evidence suggests that GenX is not environmentally benign and may exert adverse biological effects.

Emerging toxicological studies have demonstrated that GenX exposure is associated with a range of adverse biological outcomes. Experimental and epidemiological evidence has linked GenX to hepatic toxicity, developmental impairment, immune dysregulation, and endocrine disruption, highlighting its potential systemic toxicity [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. However, despite the increasing attention to GenX-related health risks, studies investigating its effects on the nervous system remain limited. The nervous system is particularly susceptible to environmental contaminants due to its high metabolic demand, complex cellular organization, and limited regenerative capacity [[11], [12], [13]]. Recent studies have shown that GenX exposure induces oxidative stress and apoptosis in primary cortical neurons [14] and can also trigger inflammatory responses in glial cells, including microglia and astrocytes [15,16], suggesting that multiple neural cell types may be vulnerable to GenX exposure. Together, these findings have raised increasing concern that emerging environmental pollutants may contribute to neuronal dysfunction and long-term neurological disorders, underscoring the need to further investigate the neurotoxic potential of GenX.

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the most prevalent neurodegenerative disorder worldwide and a leading cause of dementia in the aging population [17]. It is pathologically characterized by progressive neuronal dysfunction, synaptic loss, and neuronal death, ultimately resulting in cognitive decline and memory impairment [18,19]. Although genetic factors play an important role in AD pathogenesis [20,21], they cannot fully explain disease onset and progression. Increasing evidence indicates that environmental exposures may act as important modifiers of AD risk by influencing neuronal survival, oxidative stress, and cell death-related signaling pathways [22,23]. Nevertheless, the molecular mechanisms by which environmental contaminants contribute to AD-associated neuronal pathology remain largely unresolved.

Despite increasing concern regarding the neurotoxic potential of emerging PFAS, the molecular links between GenX exposure and AD-related neuronal dysfunction remain poorly defined. In particular, whether GenX can perturb neuronal transcriptional programs, activate disease-relevant signaling pathways, or promote apoptosis-related processes associated with neurodegeneration has not been systematically investigated. Moreover, most existing studies rely on bulk-level analyses, limiting cell-type-specific mechanistic resolution. To address these knowledge gaps, we employed a publicly available AD single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) dataset as a disease-reference framework to identify neuronal genes and signaling pathways that may be susceptible to GenX exposure.

In this study, we integrated in silico target prediction, snRNA-seq, protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis, machine learning-based gene prioritization, molecular docking, and in vitro experimental validation to investigate the potential involvement of GenX in AD-related neuronal dysfunction. By focusing on neuronal molecular alterations in AD, this work aims to provide mechanistic insights into the potential neurotoxic effects of GenX and to elucidate its relevance to neurodegeneration-associated biological processes.

Materials and methods

Animal experiments and behavioral assessments

Eight-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were housed under standard laboratory conditions with free access to food and water. After acclimatization, mice were randomly assigned to control and GenX exposure groups. GenX was dissolved in water and administered at a dose of 2.5 mg/kg/day [24,25] for 3 months, while mice received an equivalent volume of water. A 3-month exposure was selected to establish a chronic exposure model, allowing sufficient time for the development of behavioral and molecular alterations associated with sustained GenX exposure.

Novel object recognition test

The novel object recognition (NOR) test was performed to assess recognition memory. During the training phase, mice were allowed to freely explore two identical objects in an open-field arena for 5 min. After a 24-h interval, one familiar object was replaced with a novel object of a different shape, and the mice were reintroduced into the arena. The time spent exploring the novel (TN) and familiar (TF) objects was recorded. The discrimination index (DI) was calculated as DI = TN/(TN + TF) × 100%.

Barnes maze test

The Barnes maze test was used to evaluate spatial learning and memory. The test was conducted over 6 days, including adaptation (day 1), training (days 2–5), and testing (day 6). During training, mice were placed at the center of the maze and allowed 3 min to locate the escape box, and escape latency was recorded. In the probe test, the escape box was removed, and the time spent in the target quadrant was measured to assess memory retention.

Chemical information of GenX and data sources

The 2D chemical structure of GenX was obtained from the PubChem database. Key physicochemical properties were predicted using MolViz and used for visualization. Literature data related to GenX were collected from PubMed (2015–2025) using the NCBI E-utilities interface via the rentrez package in R. Search terms included “HFPO-DA”, “GenX”, and major synonyms, and a hazard-focused dataset was generated by combining GenX terms with toxicity/health-risk keywords. Metadata (PMID, year, title, abstract, journal) were extracted, deduplicated, and used for publication trend analyses and organ/system keyword-based annotation.

Prediction of GenX-related target genes

Potential target genes of GenX were predicted using three complementary in silico tools, including SuperPred, PharmMapper, and TargetNet. The chemical structure of GenX was obtained and converted into an appropriate input format for each platform. SuperPred was used to predict potential targets based on structural similarity and pharmacophore features, PharmMapper was applied to identify candidate targets through reverse pharmacophore mapping, and TargetNet was employed to infer protein targets using machine learning-based models. The predicted target genes from the three databases were collected and merged.

Single-nucleus RNA sequencing analysis and identification of AD-related neuronal genes

snRNA-seq data of the human prefrontal cortex were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (GSE157827), including samples from AD patients and healthy controls (HC). Data preprocessing and downstream analyses were performed using the Seurat package (version 5.3.0).

Prior to downstream analysis, quality control was performed to remove low-quality nuclei. Nuclei with <300 or >6000 detected genes, <1000 total UMI counts, mitochondrial gene content >15%, or hemoglobin gene content >3 % were excluded from downstream analyses (Fig. S1). To reduce sample-specific batch effects, data integration across individuals was conducted using the Harmony algorithm with sample identity (orig.ident) as the integration variable. Harmony was implemented using the default parameters, resulting in improved cross-sample alignment (Fig. S2).

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on the highly variable genes for dimensionality reduction. Following Harmony integration, the first 15 principal components were used to construct a shared nearest-neighbor (SNN) graph, and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was applied for two-dimensional visualization. Cell clusters were identified using the Leiden graph-based clustering algorithm implemented in Seurat (FindClusters, resolution = 0.05) and annotated based on the expression of canonical marker genes [26,27]. neurons (NEU) were identified by the expression of SYT1 and RBFOX3, astrocytes (AST) by AQP4 and ADGRV1, microglia (MIC) by LRMDA and DOCK8, oligodendrocytes (ODC) by ST18 and PLP1, oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPCs) by CSPG4 and VCAN, and endothelial cells (END) by CLDN5 and ABCB1. Neuronal clusters were subsequently extracted for further analyses.

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between AD and control samples within neuronal populations were identified using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Genes with an absolute log2 fold change above a predefined threshold of 0.25 and an adjusted p-value (Benjamini-Hochberg correction for false discovery rate) below 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed. The identified neuronal DEGs were then intersected with AD-associated genes curated from the GeneCards and OMIM databases to obtain a set of AD-related neuronal genes for subsequent analyses.

Protein-protein interaction network and hub gene identification

PPI analysis was performed to explore the interactions among AD-related neuronal genes and predicted GenX-related target genes. The overlapping genes were imported into the STRING database to construct the PPI network, with the species limited to Homo sapiens. All parameters were set to the default values of the STRING database, and interactions with a minimum combined score of ≥0.400 were selected for downstream analysis. The resulting interaction network was visualized and further analyzed using Cytoscape software (version 3.7.1).

To identify hub genes within the PPI network, multiple topological algorithms were applied using the CytoHubba plugin in Cytoscape, including closeness centrality, maximal clique centrality (MCC), maximum neighborhood component (MNC), density of maximum neighborhood component (DMNC), and stress. Genes with high topological importance across these algorithms were considered hub genes and selected for subsequent analyses.

Machine learning model construction

Machine learning models were constructed to identify key genes associated with AD based on expression profiles of candidate genes. The primary cohort was used as the training dataset, while three independent GEO datasets (GSE109887, GSE173594, and GSE36980) served as external validation cohorts to evaluate model generalizability.

To comprehensively assess the predictive performance of different machine learning strategies, we employed 12 machine learning algorithms: Lasso, Ridge, Stepglm, XGBoost, Random Forest (RF), Elastic Net (Enet), Partial Least Squares Regression for Generalized Linear Models (plsRglm), Generalized Boosted Regression Modeling (GBM), NaiveBayes, Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA), Generalized Linear Model Boosting (glmBoost), and Support Vector Machine (SVM). A total of 113 combinations of these algorithms were systematically evaluated using the training dataset within a tenfold cross-validation framework. The performance of the model was subsequently validated using three independent GEO datasets (GSE109887, GSE173594, and GSE36980) as external test datasets.

Model performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). The optimal model was selected based on its predictive performance in the training cohort and its consistency across independent validation datasets. Additionally, calibration curves were generated to assess model calibration, and decision curve analysis was performed to evaluate the clinical net benefit across a range of threshold probabilities.

Molecular docking analysis

Molecular docking analysis was performed to explore the potential interactions between GenX and the identified hub proteins. The three-dimensional structure of GenX was obtained from public chemical databases and prepared through energy minimization before docking. For the protein receptors, experimentally determined X-ray structures were retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank for seven targets: HSPA1A (PDB: 6G3R, 1.40 Å), MAPK3 (PDB: 4QTB, 1.40 Å), PLA2G4A (PDB: 1CJY, 2.50 Å), CAT (PDB: 1DGF, 1.50 Å), KIT (PDB: 8PQD, 1.50 Å), IGF1 (PDB: 1IMX, 1.82 Å), and BCL2 (PDB: 8HTS, 1.25 Å). For STAT4, since no high-resolution experimental structure was available, we used the predicted model from the AlphaFold Database (entry AF-Q14765-F1-v6), generated with the AlphaFold v6 pipeline. The per-residue pLDDT confidence scores of STAT4 residues involved in the predicted GenX-binding interface, together with their corresponding hydrogen-bond interactions, are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Protein structures were preprocessed by removing water molecules and heteroatoms, followed by the addition of polar hydrogen atoms and assignment of partial charges. Molecular docking was carried out using AutoDock Vina (version 1.5.6). The docking grid was defined to cover the active or functional regions of each protein. Default parameters were used unless otherwise specified.

Docking results were ranked based on binding affinity scores, and the top-ranked binding conformations were selected for visualization and interaction analysis. Protein-ligand interactions, including hydrogen bonds, van der Waals forces, and hydrophobic interactions, were analyzed and visualized using PyMOL (version 3.1.5.1) and Discovery Studio (version 4.5.0).

Functional enrichment and network analysis

Functional enrichment analyses were conducted to investigate the biological functions and signaling pathways associated with the identified hub genes. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis, including biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF) categories, as well as Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis, was conducted using R-based bioinformatics tools. Pathway interaction networks were constructed to illustrate the relationships among enriched pathways and associated genes, facilitating the identification of core signaling pathways for subsequent experimental validation.

Cell culture and GenX exposure

SH-SY5Y human neuroblastoma cells were maintained under standard culture conditions at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. GenX was purchased from Macklin (No. A761751, Shanghai, China). GenX was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare a stock solution and was freshly diluted with complete culture medium immediately before use. Cells were treated with GenX at designated concentrations for 24 h, while vehicle-treated cells containing an equivalent concentration of DMSO served as controls.

Cell viability

SH-SY5Y cells were seeded in 96-well plates at an appropriate density and allowed to adhere overnight. Cells were then exposed to different concentrations of GenX for 24 h. Subsequently, Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8) reagent (C6005; NCM Biotech, Suzhou, China) was added to each well, followed by incubation for 1 h at 37 °C. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Multiskan Spectrum, PerkinElmer, MA, USA), and cell viability was calculated according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

qRT-PCR was performed as previously described [28]. Total RNA was extracted from SH-SY5Y cells using TRIzol reagent. Residual genomic DNA was removed before reverse transcription. cDNA was synthesized from total RNA using a reverse transcription kit, and qRT-PCR was subsequently performed using TB green Premix Ex Taq (Takara, Japan). qRT-PCR amplification was carried out under standard cycling conditions, and fluorescence signals were collected at each cycle. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the ΔΔCt method, with ACTIN used as the internal reference gene. The primer sequences used for qRT-PCR analysis are provided in Table S2.

Western blot

Western blot was performed as previously described in Ref. [29]. SH-SY5Y cells were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were quantified using a BCA assay (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). Equal amounts of protein samples were resolved by SDS-PAGE and subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% BSA, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against BCL2 (Bioworld, #bs1511), BAX (Bioworld, #bs2538), RAS (Cell Signaling, #3965), RAF (Affinity, #af6065), p-RAF (Affinity, #af3065), MEK (Affinity, #af6385), p-MEK (Affinity, #af8035), ERK (Bioworld, #ap0485), p-ERK (Bioworld, #ap0484), and β-actin (Huabio, #et1702-67). After washing, membranes were incubated with appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system and captured with a chemiluminescence imaging system. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software.

Immunofluorescence staining

SH-SY5Y cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature, followed by permeabilization using Triton X-100. After blocking with BSA, cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against neuronal and signaling markers, including NeuN, BCL2, and phosphorylated ERK (p-ERK). After incubation with appropriate fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies, cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Fluorescence images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope. Quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity and colocalization was performed using image analysis software.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software and GraphPad Prism. Experimental data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from three independent experiments. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using Student's t-test, while comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Bibliometric analysis of GenX-related studies published between 2015 and 2025 revealed a continuous increase in the total number of publications, with health hazard-focused research accounting for a substantial proportion (Fig. 1 C). Toxicity studies predominantly targeted the liver, followed by the developmental and immune systems, while brain-related studies, although fewer in number, were consistently reported (Fig. 1D). Notably, research attention toward brain-related effects showed a clear upward trend in recent years, indicating growing concern regarding the potential neurotoxic effects of GenX (Fig. 1 E).

To further explore the potential molecular basis of GenX-associated effects, GenX-related target genes were predicted using three complementary in silico platforms. Integration of predicted targets from TargetNet, SuperPred, and PharmMapper yielded a total of 301 nonredundant GenX-associated target genes (Fig. 1 F). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that these targets were significantly enriched in pathways related to Alzheimer's disease (Fig. 1 G).

To assess whether GenX exposure leads to functional neurological impairment, behavioral tests were conducted following chronic exposure. Representative movement trajectories from the NOR test and Barnes maze test are shown in Fig. 1 H. Quantitative analysis demonstrated that GenX-exposed mice exhibited a significantly reduced recognition index in the NOR test, indicating impaired recognition memory (Fig. 1 I). In addition, GenX-treated mice showed a decreased proportion of time and distance spent in the target quadrant in the Barnes maze test, reflecting deficits in spatial learning and memory (Fig. 1J and K).

Taken together, these findings indicate that GenX exposure induces cognitive impairment in vivo. Given that GenX-related targets were enriched in neurodegeneration-associated pathways, including the Alzheimer's disease pathway, we subsequently employed a human AD-derived snRNA-seq dataset as a disease-reference framework in a hypothesis-driven analysis to investigate neuronal transcriptional alterations and molecular pathways that may be susceptible to GenX exposure.

Differential gene expression analysis revealed widespread transcriptional alterations between AD and control samples across major cell types (Fig. 2 E). Given that AD is fundamentally characterized by progressive neuronal dysfunction, synaptic loss, and neurodegeneration, subsequent analyses focused specifically on neuronal populations to delineate AD-related molecular changes at higher resolution. Among all models, the LASSO combined with the stepwise regression (forward selection) model demonstrated robust and consistent performance, achieving favorable AUC values in both the training dataset and external validation cohorts. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis further confirmed the model's discriminative ability across all cohorts (Fig. 4 B). Model calibration analysis indicated good agreement between predicted and observed probabilities (Fig. 4 C), while decision curve analysis demonstrated a clear net clinical benefit over a wide range of threshold probabilities (Fig. 4 D).

Feature importance analysis based on the optimal model revealed that STAT4, IGF1, KIT, MAPK3, CAT, BCL2, HSPA1A, and PLA2G4A contributed differentially to model performance (Fig. 4 E). Notably, several of these genes, including MAPK3, BCL2, IGF1, and KIT, were also identified as key nodes in the PPI network analysis.

To characterize the expression patterns of the machine learning-prioritized hub genes in neuronal populations, we examined their expression in the snRNA-seq dataset. These hub genes exhibited differential expression between AD and control neurons, with elevated MAPK3 expression and reduced BCL2 expression observed in AD neurons (Fig. 4 F). Notably, among the major brain cell populations analyzed, significant differential expression of MAPK3 and BCL2 was detected only in neurons (Fig. S3 A). These expression profiles provide a biological context for the machine learning-prioritized hub genes in neuronal populations.

GO enrichment analysis further demonstrated that these genes were significantly associated with MAP kinase activity, phosphatase binding, and protease binding in the MF category. In contrast, CC terms were mainly associated with Bcl-2 family protein complexes and stress-associated subcellular structures. In the BP category, enriched terms included positive regulation of MAPK cascade, regulation of apoptotic signaling, and response to oxidative stress, indicating a close link between hub genes and apoptosis-related signaling (Fig. 6 B).

To further explore the relationships among enriched pathways, a KEGG pathway interaction network was constructed (Fig. 6 C), highlighting extensive crosstalk among MAPK-related and neurodegeneration-associated pathways. An integrated pathway-gene interaction network further demonstrated broad connections between hub genes and multiple signaling pathways, with MAPK3 occupying a central regulatory position (Fig. 6 D).

Based on these interactions, a core signaling network was extracted, revealing a MAPK-centered regulatory axis involving MAPK3, BCL2, IGF1, KIT, and PLA2G4A (Fig. 6 E). This network suggests that these genes may act coordinately to regulate neuronal survival, apoptosis, and stress response-related processes. Finally, qPCR analysis of the selected hub genes demonstrated significant upregulation of MAPK3 and HSPA1A, accompanied by downregulation of BCL2, KIT, STAT4, and IGF1, whereas CAT and PLA2G4A showed no significant changes following GenX exposure (Fig. 6 F). Overall, these expression trends were largely consistent with the neuronal gene expression patterns identified by snRNA-seq (Fig. 4 F).

As shown in Fig. 7 A, GenX exposure resulted in a concentration-dependent reduction in cell viability, with significant decreases observed at moderate to high concentrations, indicating that GenX exerts cytotoxic effects on neuronal cells. Based on protein-protein interaction analysis and machine learning-based prioritization, MAPK3 was consistently identified as a key AD-related neuronal hub gene. In addition, pathway enrichment and network analyses revealed significant involvement of the MAPK signaling pathway, with MAPK3 occupying a central regulatory position within the inferred signaling networks. Given that MAPK3 encodes ERK1, a core component of the canonical RAS-RAF-MEK-ERK cascade [30], we next examined whether GenX exposure activates MAPK/ERK signaling in neuronal cells. Western blot analysis showed that GenX treatment increased the protein levels of Ras and enhanced the phosphorylation of C-RAF, MEK, and ERK (Fig. 7 B). Quantitative analysis further confirmed significant activation of the RAS-RAF-MEK-ERK signaling cascade following GenX exposure (Fig. 7 C). Consistently, immunofluorescence staining revealed increased p-ERK signals in NeuN-positive neuronal cells in the GenX-treated group compared with controls (Fig. 7 F), which was further supported by quantitative analysis (Fig. 7 G).

Given the central role of MAPK/ERK signaling in regulating neuronal survival and the identification of BCL2 as both a hub gene and a machine-learning-prioritized target, we further examined apoptosis-related downstream markers. Western blot analysis demonstrated a marked reduction in BCL-2 expression accompanied by an increase in BAX levels in GenX-treated cells, resulting in a decreased BCL-2/BAX ratio (Fig. 7 D and E). In parallel, immunofluorescence analysis showed reduced BCL-2 staining intensity in NeuN-positive neuronal cells following GenX exposure (Fig. 7 H), which was quantitatively cells following GenX exposure (Fig. 7 I). Together, these results indicate that GenX exposure activates MAPK/ERK signaling and is accompanied by dysregulation of apoptosis-related pathways in neuronal cells.

Discussion

This study employed an integrative strategy combining single-nucleus transcriptomics, machine learning-based gene prioritization, network analysis, molecular docking, and in vitro validation to investigate the potential neurotoxic relevance of GenX in Alzheimer's disease. By focusing on AD-relevant neuronal molecular alterations, our findings suggest that GenX exposure may be associated with perturbations in neurodegeneration-related signaling processes and neuronal vulnerability. Importantly, this work expands current understanding of GenX toxicity by highlighting its potential relevance to neuronal dysfunction and neurodegeneration-related processes.

Although toxicological studies on GenX have predominantly focused on hepatic [[31], [32], [33]], developmental [34,35], and immune-related outcomes [36,37], emerging evidence suggests that short-chain PFAS may also exert effects on the nervous system. A recent pharmacokinetic study demonstrated that orally administered GenX was detectable in brain tissue following systemic exposure, supporting the biological plausibility that GenX can reach the central nervous system [38]. Recent experimental studies have begun to reveal that GenX can directly perturb glial and neural cell function through multiple inflammation- and stress-related pathways. In glial cells, GenX exposure has been shown to induce neuroinflammatory responses in BV2 microglial cells, accompanied by suppression of autophagy via mTOR signaling [15]. In parallel, proteomic analyses further demonstrated that GenX exposure leads to metabolic dysfunction and inflammatory alterations in astrocytes [16]. In neuronal models, GenX has been reported to activate oxidative stress-related signaling and promote apoptosis, providing direct evidence that neuronal survival can be compromised by GenX exposure [14]. Collectively, these findings suggest that GenX is capable of affecting multiple neural cell types and engaging molecular processes associated with inflammation, metabolic imbalance, oxidative stress, and cell death. Consistent with previous studies on PFAS neurotoxicity, oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and synaptic dysfunction have been recognized as major mechanisms underlying PFAS-induced neuronal injury [[39], [40], [41], [42]]. Moreover, several PFAS have been shown to cross the blood–brain barrier, accumulate in brain tissue, and induce cognitive impairment in experimental models [43,44]. Building upon these previous findings, our study further identifies MAPK-centered signaling as a potential molecular axis linking GenX exposure to neuronal dysfunction by integrating human snRNA-seq with machine learning and experimental validation.

AD is a multifactorial neurodegenerative disorder in which genetic susceptibility alone cannot fully account for disease onset and progression [45]. Increasing evidence indicates that environmental factors play an important modulatory role in AD pathogenesis [[46], [47], [48]]. At the pathological level, AD is characterized by progressive neuronal dysfunction, synaptic loss, and neuronal death, which precede and accompany cognitive decline [17,49]. Notably, neurons are considered particularly vulnerable to environmental insults due to their high metabolic demand, long lifespan, limited regenerative capacity, and reliance on tightly regulated intracellular signaling networks [50,51]. A growing body of neurotoxicological evidence has demonstrated that diverse environmental pollution, including air pollution, heavy metals, pesticides, and industrial chemicals, can preferentially disrupt neuronal function by including oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and apoptosis-related neurodegeneration [50,[52], [53], [54]].

MAPK signaling represents a central regulatory hub in neuronal stress responses, survival, and apoptosis, and its dysregulation has been widely implicated in AD pathogenesis [55,56]. In neurons, MAPK/ERK signaling integrates extracellular stress cues with intracellular programs governing synaptic plasticity, oxidative stress responses, and cell fate decisions. Aberrant activation of MAPK pathways has been associated with neuronal injury, tau phosphorylation, and neurodegenerative progression in AD, highlighting its relevance to disease-associated molecular dysfunction [[57], [58], [59]]. In the present study, integrative network and pathway analyses consistently highlighted MAPK-centered signaling among GenX-associated, AD-related neuronal pathways, suggesting that MAPK signaling may serve as a convergence point linking environmental exposure to neurodegeneration-related molecular processes. Notably, MAPK3 and its interaction partners, including apoptosis- and survival-related regulators, occupied central positions within the inferred signaling networks, supporting the potential involvement of MAPK-mediated regulatory axes in GenX-associated neuronal stress responses. Together with experimental evidence showing that GenX exposure activates apoptosis-related and stress-responsive signaling in neuronal cells, these findings suggest that perturbation of MAPK signaling represents a biologically plausible mechanism through which GenX contributes to neuronal vulnerability in the context of AD.

This study has several notable strengths. First, it integrates multiple complementary approaches, including in silico target prediction, single-nucleus transcriptomic analysis, PPI networks, machine learning-based gene prioritization, molecular docking, in vivo and in vitro validation, enabling a multi-layered investigation of GenX-associated neurotoxic mechanisms. Second, the use of snRNA-seq provides cell-type-specific resolution, enabling downstream analyses focused on neuronal populations relevant to AD. Third, the application of multiple machine learning models and external validation cohorts enhances the robustness and reproducibility of gene selection. Importantly, the AD single-nucleus RNA-seq dataset used in this study served as a disease-reference framework to identify neuronal genes and signaling pathways that may be susceptible to GenX exposure. Accordingly, our integrative analytical strategy was intended to explore potential mechanistic overlap between GenX-induced molecular alterations and AD-associated neuronal pathways, rather than to establish a direct causal relationship between GenX exposure and AD.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the computational analyses were based on publicly available transcriptomic datasets and in silico prediction methods. Although these approaches enabled systematic prioritization of candidate genes and pathways, the inferred molecular interactions require further experimental validation. The in vitro validation was conducted in a neuronal cell line, which cannot fully recapitulate the complexity of the human brain microenvironment or cell–cell interactions. Moreover, the use of a single neuronal cell line does not permit assessment of the cell-type specificity of the observed MAPK activation and apoptosis-related. In addition, the GenX exposure levels used in both the in vivo and in vitro experiments were selected to induce measurable toxicological responses and investigate potential mechanisms, but were higher than typical environmental exposure levels, which may limit direct extrapolation of our findings to real-world exposure scenarios. Furthermore, the present study did not evaluate the contribution of GenX metabolites or distinguish direct neurotoxic effects from indirect effects secondary to systemic toxicity, including hepatic injury. Although behavioral assessments in adult mice demonstrated that chronic GenX exposure impaired learning and memory, the current animal model does not recapitulate the pathological features of AD, and AD-related hallmarks such as amyloid-β and tau pathology were not evaluated. The present findings should be interpreted as evidence that GenX induces neurobehavioral impairment and engages molecular pathways associated with AD, rather than demonstrating that GenX directly causes AD. Future studies incorporating brain pharmacokinetic analyses, metabolite profiling, tissue-specific toxicity assessments, comparisons across neuronal and non-neuronal cell types, and aged or transgenic AD animal models with comprehensive characterization of AD pathology will be necessary to further validate the underlying mechanisms and extend the present findings.

In summary, this study provides integrative evidence that GenX may pose potential neurotoxic risks relevant to AD-associated molecular processes. By combining single-nucleus transcriptomic analysis, machine learning-based gene prioritization, network biology, molecular docking, and in vitro validation, we identified a set of neuronal targets and signaling pathways, with MAPK-centered signaling emerging as a key regulatory axis potentially linking GenX exposure to neuronal dysfunction and apoptosis. These findings highlight that GenX is not biologically inert with respect to the nervous system and may engage molecular pathways implicated in neurodegeneration. Although further in vivo and epidemiological studies are required to establish causal relationships and long-term outcomes, this work provides early mechanistic insights and a transferable analytical framework for evaluating the neurotoxic potential of emerging PFAS. Collectively, our results underscore the importance of incorporating neurodegeneration-relevant endpoints into the risk assessment of next-generation PFAS.
