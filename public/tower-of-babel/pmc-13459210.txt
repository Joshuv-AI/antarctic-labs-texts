Expression dynamics of starch and proanthocyanidin biosynthesis genes during Quercus glauca fruit development
Authors: Rui-Bin Cao, Qi Zhang, Gang-Biao Xu, Jia-Xiang Li, Huang-Zhuo Xiao, Jing-Ye Shi, He Li, Xiao-Long Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcorns are nutrient-rich fruits in which starch and proanthocyanidins (PAs) constitute the two dominant storage and defense metabolites. Transcriptomic studies in oaks have focused on stress responses, cork formation, and somatic embryogenesis, while omics and metabolic studies of acorn development remain scarce. We hypothesized that starch and PA biosynthesis in Quercus glauca fruits are governed by stage-specific transcriptional programs that drive their differential accumulation during fruit maturation.ResultsIn this study, we utilized transcriptome data to investigate the expression dynamics of genes associated with starch and PA biosynthesis during Quercus glauca fruit development. Starch content increased continuously from 5.0 to 9.5 mg/mg protein across the five developmental stages of Q. glauca fruit, whereas PA content fluctuated within 0.015–0.038 mg/mg protein, peaking at T3 and T5. Transcriptome sequencing identified 2,637 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), predominantly partitioned into the rapid fruit growth phase (T3-T5) and enriched in PA biosynthesis, sugar metabolism, seed germination, and cell-cycle processes. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) identified five modules significantly correlated with starch or PA content, within which 50 hub genes including QgPAL7 were identified. Mfuzz clustering showed PA biosynthesis genes were induced early and progressively repressed toward maturation, whereas starch biosynthesis genes were continuously up-regulated. WGCNA co-expression and metabolite-correlation analyses identified QgMYB33 as a stage-specific candidate associated with both PA and starch biosynthesis. The GPT family exhibited expansion through both tandem and segmental duplications. RT-qPCR validation of nine selected genes confirmed the RNA-seq expression patterns.ConclusionThis study characterizes a biphasic transcriptional program coordinating starch and PA biosynthesis during Q. glauca fruit development and identifies QgGPT7, QgGPT10, QgPAL7 and QgMYB33 as candidates, providing a molecular foundation for the future utilization and improvement of acorn resources.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09153-x.

Introduction

Quercus L. (Fagaceae), comprising approximately 450–500 species, is a genus of significant ecological and economic importance, dominating many temperate and subtropical ecosystems across the Northern Hemisphere [1, 2]. As a secondary center of diversity for the genus, China hosts four major sections of Quercus: sect. Cyclobalanopsis, sect. Cerris, sect. Ilex and sect. Quercus [3]. The fruits of oaks, known as acorns, are rich in nutrients, including starch, tannins (particularly proanthocyanidins, PAs), soluble sugars, oils, and fiber, which contribute to significant medicinal, edible, and health-promoting properties [4]. Starch constitutes the predominant macronutrient in acorns (~ 48–50%), making them a promising alternative resource for applications in food processing, biomaterials, and pharmaceuticals [5, 6]. Acorn oil, rich in unsaturated fatty acids such as oleic and linoleic acids, is nutritionally comparable to olive oil and offers various health benefits [7, 8]. Moreover, acorns contain abundant bioactive compounds, particularly flavonoids and tannins, which exhibit strong antioxidant capacity and diverse biological activities, including anti-tumor and anti-inflammatory effects [9, 10]. These properties support the expanding use of acorns in the food, cosmetic, and pharmaceutical industries.

Starch, the primary storage carbohydrate in plants, plays critical roles in energy storage, growth regulation, seed development, and environmental adaptation [11–13]. During fruit maturation, starch accumulation is essential for supporting development and ensuring adequate energy reserves for dispersal and germination [14, 15]. Structurally, starch consists mainly of amylose and amylopectin, which determine its physicochemical properties and functionality [16]. Its biosynthesis involves multiple enzymes, including glucose-6-phosphate translocator (GPT), phosphoglucomutase (PGM), ADP-glucose pyrophosphorylase (AGPase), soluble starch synthase (SS), granule-bound starch synthase (GBSS), branching enzyme (BE), isoamylase (ISA), and limit dextrinase (LDA) [17]. Although the core pathway and regulatory mechanisms of starch biosynthesis have been well characterized in major crops such as wheat [18], rice [17], and maize [19], the molecular mechanisms underlying starch biosynthesis during Q. glauca fruit development remain poorly understood.

Proanthocyanidins (PAs), or condensed tannins (CTs), are polyphenolic secondary metabolites widespread in plants, formed through the polymerization of flavan-3-ol units [20, 21]. PA biosynthesis is highly regulated, encompassing catalytic steps by multiple enzymes such as PAL, C4H, 4CL, CHS, CHI, F3H, F3’H, DFR, LDOX/ANS, LAR, and ANR, as well as transcriptional regulation by MYB, bHLH, and WD40 factors [22, 23]. In oaks, the distribution, content, and functional roles of PAs across various tissues have been documented [24–26]. Although acorn PAs possess valuable bioactive properties, such as antioxidant and anti-tumor activities, that support their use in health products and pharmaceuticals, they also contribute to astringency, which complicates processing and limits broader utilization [27, 28]. Therefore, understanding the molecular regulation of PA biosynthesis during acorn development is critical for enhancing their beneficial properties and reducing astringency.

Quercus glauca (section Cyclobalanopsis) is a widespread and ecologically significant oak species in Asia, producing acorns rich in starch and PAs [29]. Despite the ecological and economic importance of these compounds, transcriptomic studies in the genus Quercus have primarily focused on stress responses, cork formation, and somatic embryogenesis in species such as Q. robur, Q. suber, and Q. ilex [30]. Among studies addressing acorn development, Miguel et al. [31] provided the first transcriptomic characterization across five developmental stages of Q. suber, revealing enrichment of carbohydrate metabolism pathways during mid-to-late developmental stages. However, the expression dynamics of gene families governing the biosynthesis of two major storage metabolites (starch and PAs) throughout acorn development remain poorly understood, necessitating studies that integrate transcriptomic profiling with direct metabolite quantification.

To address this knowledge gap, we performed transcriptome sequencing of Q. glauca fruits across key developmental stages, along with quantification of starch and PA contents. These metabolites play crucial yet contrasting roles: starch serves as an essential energy reserve that supports embryo development and post-dispersal germination, whereas PAs act as a chemical defense against seed predators by reducing palatability and digestibility, thereby enhancing seed survival [29]. We hypothesize that starch and PA biosynthesis in Q. glauca acorns are governed by stage-specific transcriptional programs operating at distinct developmental phases, leading to differential accumulation patterns of these metabolites during fruit maturation. This study aims to: (I) Identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and conduct functional enrichment analysis; (II) Apply weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) and time-series expression profiling to identify modules and trends associated with starch and PA biosynthesis; (III) Systematically identify gene families involved in starch and PA pathways, analyze their expression profiles, and validate key expression dynamics using RT-qPCR. Our findings provide molecular insights into the regulation of storage metabolite accumulation in Q. glauca acorns, offering a foundation for future strategies aimed at improving the utilization and management of this valuable species.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

The fruit samples of wild Q. glauca were collected from Dashanchong Forest Park, Hunan Province, China (28°24’45’’N, 113°17’50’’E). As Q. glauca is a widely distributed species and is not listed under the national protection categories in China, no specific collection permits were required for this study. All sampling procedures were non-destructive and complied with the Regulations of the People’s Republic of China on the Protection of Wild Plants. The taxonomic identification of Q. glauca was authenticated by Professor Xiaolong Jiang of Central South University of Forestry and Technology. Voucher specimens have been deposited in the Herbarium of Central South University of Forestry and Technology under the accession numbers JXL175, JXL176, and JXL177. Sampling was conducted at 10-day intervals, beginning shortly after fertilization and initial embryo establishment and continuing through fruit and embryo maturation. These five successive time points were designated as T1 through T5 (Fig. 1) and were established based on our research group’s previous investigations into the fruit morphology and the dynamic changes in starch and tannin content across different developmental stages of Q. glauca. Morphological examination confirmed that the T1 stage represented post-fertilization embryo development, while the subsequent stages (T2–T5) reflected progressive expansion and maturation of both the fruit and embryo, thus covering key phases of Q. glauca fruit development. After collection, the outer shells of the fruits were removed, and the samples were immediately stored at − 80 °C. For each developmental stage, three independent biological replicates were prepared by pooling multiple fruits, yielding a total of 15 samples.

Determination of starch and PA content

The starch and PA contents (mg/mg prot) in Q. glauca kernels were determined using commercial kits (Starch Content Determination Kit, PA Content Determination Kit, and BCA Protein Content Determination Kit; Quanzhou RuiXin Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Fujian). Starch content was quantified by acid hydrolysis coupled with anthrone colorimetry, while PA content was measured using the sulfuric acid-vanillin colorimetric method. Total protein concentration was determined via the Bicinchoninic Acid (BCA) method. All metabolite concentrations were normalized to the total protein mass. The procedures and calculations were performed in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols.

RNA sequencing and data processing

Total RNA was extracted from each of the 15 frozen fruit samples. RNA integrity and purity were assessed, and high-quality samples (OD260/280 = 1.8–2.2, total amount > 1 µg) were used for library construction. Sequencing libraries were prepared using the Illumina TruseqTM RNA Sample Prep Kit. Briefly, poly(A)+ mRNA was enriched with Oligo(dT) magnetic beads and fragmented. The resulting double-stranded cDNA underwent end repair, adenylation, and adapter ligation. Fragments of suitable size were selected via gel electrophoresis, PCR-amplified, and validated. Finally, the libraries were sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform (Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) to generate 150-bp paired-end reads.

The raw read counts, total base numbers, GC content, and Q20 and Q30 values were calculated to ensure the suitability of the data for subsequent analyses (Table S1). Raw sequencing reads were processed to ensure data quality. Adapters and low-quality bases were trimmed using Trimmomatic v0.39 [32] with the parameters: LEADING:3, TRAILING:3, SLIDINGWINDOW:4:15, MINLEN:36, and HEADCROP:20. This process removed adapter sequences, leading/trailing bases with a Phred quality score below 3, and reads shorter than 36 bp after trimming. The quality of the filtered clean reads was assessed using FastQC v0.11.9 [33].

Read alignment and expression quantification

The high-quality clean reads from each sample were aligned to the published Q. glauca reference genome [34] using HISAT2 v2.2.1 [35]. The resulting Sequence Alignment Map (SAM) files were converted to Binary Alignment Map (BAM) format, sorted, and indexed using SAMtools v1.12 [36]. Gene expression levels were estimated by counting the reads mapped to each gene feature using featureCounts v2.0.1 [37] based on the genome annotation file. Expression abundance was normalized and reported as Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped fragments (FPKM) using Cufflinks v2.2.1 [38].

Differential expression analysis

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) among the five developmental stages (T1–T5) were identified using the edgeR package v3.36.0 [39] in R, with raw count data as input. After filtering low-expression genes and normalizing library sizes by the trimmed mean of M-values (TMM) method, all 10 pairwise comparisons among the five stages were performed using exact tests, with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction applied independently to each contrast. Genes with |log2 fold change| > 1 and FDR-adjusted p < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed. Shared and unique DEGs among different stage comparisons were analyzed using the UpSetR package v1.4.0 [40]. Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs was performed. Gene Ontology (GO) terms were assigned using eggNOG-mapper [41], and enriched terms were identified with the ClusterProfiler package v4.0.5 [42]. Metabolic pathways were annotated using the KEGG Automatic Annotation Server (KAAS) [43] and visualized with ggplot2 [44].

Expression pattern clustering analysis

To identify gene modules associated with fruit development, a weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed using the WGCNA package v1.72-1 [45] in R. The top 5,000 genes with the highest median absolute deviation (MAD) were used to construct a signed network. A soft-thresholding power of 9 was selected to achieve a scale-free topology (R² > 0.8). Modules were detected using a dynamic tree-cutting method (deepSplit = 2, minimum module size = 40, merge threshold = 0.2).

Biochemical measurements of starch and PA content were used as phenotypic traits. Module-trait associations were quantified using Pearson’s correlation between Module Eigengenes (MEs) and traits, with all p-values adjusted by the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR method. Five modules showing significant correlations with either PA or starch content (|r| ≥ 0.6, FDR-adjusted p < 0.05) were selected for hub gene identification and network analysis. Hub genes within each key module were identified based on module membership (|kME| ≥ 0.8) and gene significance (GS ≥ 0.2). The top 50 candidates per module, ranked by |kME|, were functionally annotated via KEGG pathway analysis. A co-expression subnetwork was constructed from the top 10 hub genes per module (50 nodes; TOM > 0.20) and visualized using the igraph [46] and ggraph [47] packages in R.

Functional enrichment analysis was performed on genes within key modules. Additionally, temporal expression patterns of all genes across the five stages were clustered using the Mfuzz package v2.56.0 [48] based on a fuzzy c-means algorithm to identify genes with similar expression trends during development.

Identification and expression analysis of key metabolite synthesis genes and transcription factors

Putative MYB transcription factors were identified from the Q. glauca protein sequences using the MYB annotator tool [49]. Genes involved in starch biosynthesis were identified by performing a BLASTp [50] search (E-value < 1e-10) against the Q. glauca proteome using known starch synthesis protein sequences from related species as queries. Candidate genes were further validated by checking for conserved domains using InterProScan [51] and confirming homology via NCBI BLAST [52]. For proanthocyanidin (PA) biosynthesis, gene families identified in a previous study were used [53]. Protein sequences of the identified starch biosynthesis genes and MYB transcription factors were aligned using MAFFT v7.515 [54]. Maximum-likelihood phylogenetic trees were constructed with FastTree v2.1.11 [55] using 1000 bootstrap replicates and visualized on the iTOL platform [56]. The expression profiles of these key gene families across the five developmental stages were visualized in heatmaps using the ggplot2 package [44]. Intra-genomic collinearity analysis was performed on the Q. glauca genome using MCScanX to characterize the duplication origins of the PA biosynthesis, starch biosynthesis, and MYB gene families, based on previously published genome-wide duplication analyses [53, 57].

Correlation analysis and real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis

Pearson correlation analysis was performed between the FPKM expression levels of PA biosynthesis genes, starch pathway genes, and MYB transcription factors and the corresponding measured PA and starch contents across the five developmental stages (T1–T5). Genes with a maximum FPKM < 1 across all stages were excluded. Pearson correlation coefficients (r) and p-values were calculated.

To validate the RNA-seq results, the expression of selected DEGs was analyzed by reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR). Total RNA was extracted from the same five developmental stage samples using an Omega plant RNA kit. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using the HiScript II Q RT SuperMix kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Gene-specific primers were designed using Primer-BLAST (listed in Table S4). RT-qPCR was performed on a CFX96 system (Bio-Rad) using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme), with the following cycling conditions: 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Prior to validation, four candidate reference genes (QgACT, QgEF1-α, QgCACs1, and QgCACs2) were tested for expression stability across the five developmental stages, and QgACT was selected as the internal control based on its lowest coefficient of variation. Statistical analysis was conducted using Origin software. For each of the nine validated genes, a linear regression was fitted between RT-qPCR-derived relative expression (2^(-ΔΔCt)) and the corresponding RNA-seq FPKM values across all biological replicates of the five developmental stages. The coefficient of determination (R2) and the two-tailed p-value of the regression were computed, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

RNA-seq analysis

Transcriptome sequencing of 15 Q. glauca fruit samples across five developmental stages (T1-T5) yielded 93.46 GB of raw data (Table S1). After quality control, 70.47 GB of high-quality clean reads were retained for subsequent analysis. All samples displayed Q20 > 97.12% and Q30 > 92.34%, indicating high base call accuracy. Sample correlation assessment revealed high reproducibility among biological replicates within each stage (Fig. 1A), supporting the robustness of the data.

Comparative analysis identified a total of 2,637 DEGs across the five developmental stages (T1-T5), comprising 1,418 upregulated and 1,219 downregulated genes (Fig. 1B, S1). The number of DEGs was substantially higher during the later stages of rapid fruit growth and maturation (T3-T5) compared to the early stage (T1-T2). Pairwise comparisons revealed maximal overlap of DEGs between the late stages T4 and T5 (Fig. S2), indicating persistent and stage-specific transcriptional reprogramming during maturation. Proanthocyanidin (PA) content varied from 0.015 to 0.038 mg/mg protein across the five stages, increasing gradually from T1 to T3, decreasing significantly at T4, and then rising again at T5 (Fig. 1C). Starch content increased continuously from 5.0 to 9.5 mg/mg protein across the five stages, showing a significant upward trend throughout fruit development (Fig. 1D).

GO enrichment analysis of upregulated DEGs revealed significant associations with processes related to secondary metabolism and fruit maturation, including phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (GO:0009699), monooxygenase activity (GO:0004497), quercetin glycosyltransferase activity (GO:0080043/GO:0080044), and oxidoreductase activity (GO:0016491) (Fig. S3A). Terms associated with light response and chloroplast organization were also enriched. Additional enriched processes included seed germination, abscisic acid response, and sugar metabolism. Conversely, downregulated DEGs were primarily associated with DNA metabolic process, cellular component organization, and hydrolytic enzyme activities (Fig. S3B).

We further identified genes exhibiting sustained upregulation or downregulation across the T4/T5 transition by intersecting genes differentially expressed in T4 (vs. T1-T3) and T5 (vs. T1-T4) to explore their functions (Fig. S4). The sustained activation genes at T4/T5 phase were enriched in sugar metabolism (GO:0006073), glyoxysomes (GO: 0005777), starch biosynthesis (GO:0019252), and glyoxylate cycle genes (GO:0009512) (Fig. S4A). The continuously suppressed genes exhibited enrichment in the process of cell cycle arrest (nuclear division GO:0000280 and meiosis GO:0140013), cytoskeletal inactivation (microtubule motor activity GO:0003777), and kinesin complex GO:0005871), and chromatin remodeling (chromosome part GO:0044427) (Fig. S4B).

KEGG pathway analysis further corroborated these findings. Upregulated genes were significantly enriched in phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthesis pathways and cytochrome P450-related genes (Fig. S5A), along with pathways related to sugar metabolism and phytohormone signaling. Downregulated genes were enriched in pathways such as pyruvate metabolism, photosynthesis, disease resistance, and hormone signaling (Fig. S5B). Collectively, enrichment analyses identified PA synthesis, sugar metabolism, phytohormone signaling, and seed development as core regulators of fruit maturation.

Expression pattern clustering analysis

To elucidate gene regulatory networks underlying fruit development, a WGCNA was performed. This analysis grouped the top 5,000 genes with MAD into 12 distinct co-expression modules (Fig. 2A) with sizes ranging from 95 (lightyellow) to 866 (turquoise). Module-trait correlation analysis identified several modules significantly associated with developmental stages and biochemical traits (Fig. 2B). The turquoise module showed significant positive correlations with both PA content (r = 0.92, p < 0.01) and starch content (r = 0.93, p < 0.01). The salmon (r = 0.89, p < 0.01), green (r = − 0.70, p < 0.05), and red (r = 0.74, p < 0.05) modules also showed significant correlations with PA content, while the blue module was significantly negatively correlated with starch content (r = − 0.84, p < 0.01).

The functional enrichment analysis of the key modules identified distinct biological processes (Fig. S6, S7). The turquoise module was enriched in cell wall organization, cellular carbohydrate and polysaccharide metabolism, and cellular response to hypoxia (Fig. S6). The red module was associated with starch biosynthetic and metabolic processes, while the blue module was enriched in plant morphogenesis and starch catabolic processes. KEGG pathway analysis revealed that the turquoise module was enriched in carbohydrate metabolism, amino acid metabolism, and biosynthesis of other secondary metabolites (Fig. S7).

Hub gene analysis identified 50 candidates across the five key modules, of which 26 were functionally annotated via KEGG (Fig. S8). In the green module, QgPAL7 was among the top hub genes (r = 0.87). The turquoise module included β-amylase (K01177) as a hub gene, and the lightcyan module hub genes were predominantly ribosomal proteins and rRNA-processing factors (RP-S25e, RP-L6e, RP-L36e, NOP58, BRX1, MRT4). Within the co-expression subnetwork, the turquoise module occupied a central position and showed the highest number of inter-module connections, most notably with the blue (n = 87) and red (n = 52) modules.

Time-series clustering of DEGs identified 10 distinct expression profiles (ranging from Cluster 1: n = 202 to Cluster 7: n = 351). Genes in Cluster 1 reached peak expression during rapid growth (T3-T4) and were enriched in PA biosynthesis (e.g., flavonoid and pigment metabolism) and fruit expansion (e.g., inositol and polyol transport), followed by a decline by T5 (Fig. 2C). In contrast, Cluster 6 was associated with late-stage accumulation of auxins, pigments, flavonoids, and starch (Fig. 2D), while Cluster 10 exhibited progressive upregulation in energy metabolism pathways, including pectin, carbohydrate/starch processing (Fig. 2E). Early-stage active clusters (3 and 7) showed transient DNA metabolic activity, suppressed during maturation, consistent with cell division cessation and expansion initiation (Fig. S9).

Expression analysis of key transcription factors and gene families involved in PA biosynthesis

We identified 46 putative QgMYB transcription factors (TFs) distributed across all 12 chromosomes (Table S2), with the highest density on chromosome 2 (n = 9) and single loci on chromosomes 5 and 11. Based on DNA-binding domains, these were classified into five 1R-MYB types, 30 R2R3-MYB types, five 3R-MYB types, and six pseudogenes. Phylogenetic analysis with 133 A. thaliana MYBs (Fig. S10) demonstrated conserved evolution of 1R- and 3R-MYBs between species, whereas pseudogenes clustered within R2R3-MYB clades, suggesting possible retention of partial function. Intra-genomic collinearity analysis identified 11 duplicate gene pairs within the QgMYB family, comprising 2 tandem pairs (QgMYB07/QgMYB08 and QgMYB37/QgMYB38) and 9 segmental pairs (Table S5).

Stage-specific expression profiling revealed distinct temporal regulation of MYB TFs and PA-biosynthetic genes. Low expression QgMYB genes were removed based on FPKM ≥ 2 at all developmental stages. Expression analysis showed that QgMYB15, QgMYB16, QgMYB30, QgMYB39, QgMYB42 and QgMYB43 were predominantly expressed at T1 and T2, while QgMYB19, QgMYB24, QgMYB27, and QgMYB28 maximized expression at T3. QgMYB13 and QgMYB22 peaked at T5 (Fig. 3A). Concurrently, PA-biosynthetic genes (e.g., QgPAL, QgF3H, QgF3’H) showed transcriptional activation at T3 (Fig. 3B), coinciding with peak proanthocyanidin accumulation. The Qg4CL gene family showed high expression in the T3, T4, and T5 stages of fruit development. Class II Qg4CL15 showed higher expression levels in T3 and T5 stages; Qg4CL2 and Qg4CL11 of Class I were putatively involved in lignin biosynthesis and exhibited elevated expression during the T5 stage.

QgC4H2, QgC4H3, and QgCHS3 showed low expression levels during fruit development but were expressed in stem, leaf, and inflorescence tissues (Fig. S12), suggesting roles in non-fruit metabolic processes. Genomic analysis identified Long Terminal Repeat Retrotransposon (LTR-RT) insertions in the promoter or coding regions of two phenylpropanoid synthesis genes (QgCHS1 and QgC4H3) and one MYB TF gene (QgMYB25; Fig. 3C). Gene structure and expression analyses revealed that the functional impact of LTR-RT insertion is location-dependent. Insertion within the QgC4H3 coding region was associated with suppressed expression, potentially leading to loss of function, whereas insertions in the promoters of QgCHS1 and QgMYB25 did not abolish expression. Notably, QgMYB25 and its homologous gene QgMYB23 showed different expression trends and significantly increased expression, likely due to the introduction of regulatory elements by the LTR-RT that promote transcription.

Starch synthesis during fruit development of Q. glauca

Co-expression analyses (WGCNA and Mfuzz) revealed significant enrichment for carbohydrate biosynthesis pathways. In Q. glauca, we identified major starch biosynthesis gene families, including 13 glucose-6-phosphate translocators (GPT), two phosphoglucomutases (PGM), five ADP-glucose pyrophosphorylases (AGPase), five starch synthases (SS), two granule-bound starch synthases (GBSS), three branching enzymes (BE), three isoamylases (ISA), and three limit dextrinases (LDA) (Table S3).

To elucidate evolutionary relationships, we constructed a maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree using protein sequences from 18 A. thaliana and 36 Q. glauca starch biosynthesis genes. Intra-genomic collinearity analysis further revealed that the QgGPT family expanded through both tandem duplication (QgGPT3/QgGPT4) and segmental duplication (QgGPT1/QgGPT9; QgGPT2/QgGPT12) (Table S5). Expression analysis showed high transcript levels of multiple genes from these families (e.g., GPT, PGM, AGPase, SS, GBSS, BE, ISA, LDA) during stages T3-T5, consistent with the directly measured continuous increase in starch content observed across the same developmental stage. Notably, specific isoforms displayed distinct temporal expression patterns; for instance, QgSS1, QgSS2, QgSS3, and QgSS4 peaked at stages T1, T2, T3, and T4, respectively. These findings suggest that expansion of starch biosynthesis genes, particularly in the GPT family, may facilitate stage-specific regulation of starch accumulation during fruit development in Q. glauca.

Validation of the expression patterns of selected DEGs by RT-qPCR

Pearson correlation analysis between gene expression levels and measured metabolite contents identified multiple significant associations among PA biosynthesis genes, starch pathway genes, and MYB transcription factors (Fig. S11). Among PA biosynthesis genes, QgCHI1 (r = 0.997, p < 0.001) and QgF3′H1 (r = 0.918, p < 0.05) showed the strongest correlations with PA content, while QgBE1 (r = 0.965, p < 0.01) and QgSS2 (r = 0.920, p < 0.05) were significantly correlated with starch content. Among MYB transcription factors, QgMYB33 showed significant positive correlations with both PA (r = 0.991, p < 0.01) and starch content (r = 0.923, p < 0.05).

To validate the transcriptomic data, the expression patterns of nine selected DEGs were analyzed using RT-qPCR (Fig. 4). These genes included four involved in PA biosynthesis genes (QgPAL7, QgCHS2, QgCHI1, QgF3’H1), two MYB TFs (QgMYB13, QgMYB33), and three involved in starch synthesis (QgGPT6, QgSS2, QgBE1). The results for PA biosynthesis genes showed that T3 reached the peak and then decreased. Expression levels of starch biosynthesis genes (QgSS2, QgBE1) showed a continuous increase throughout fruit development. Linear regression analysis demonstrated significant positive correlations (R2 = 0.62–0.87, p ≤ 0.018) between the RT-qPCR relative expression and RNA-seq FPKM values for all nine genes, indicating that the RNA-seq data reliably reflect the underlying transcript dynamics across the five developmental stages.

Discussion

Acorns are valuable resources rich in starch, tannins (including PAs), soluble sugars, oils, and fiber, components that confer notable medicinal, edible, and health-promoting properties with substantial market potential [4]. With increasing emphasis on the sustainable utilization of natural resources, the economic and ecological value of Q. glauca, a perennial evergreen oak species, has garnered growing interest. In this study, we employed transcriptome sequencing to analyze DEGs, co-expression networks, and functional enrichment across five developmental stages (T1–T5) of Q. glauca fruit. Our findings revealed asynchronous biosynthesis and transcriptional reprogramming of starch- and PA-related pathways, providing a molecular foundation for acorn breeding and utilization.

PA biosynthesis dynamics in the developmental biphasic expression pattern of acorns

Transcriptomic profiling revealed a distinct defense phase during the rapid fruit enlargement stage, characterized by the coordinated upregulation of PA synthesis genes such as PAL, CHI, F3’H, LAR, ANR (Fig. 3). The coordinated upregulation of upstream genes (QgF3’H, QgLDOX) and downstream genes (QgLAR, QgANR) at T3 supports the balanced production of flavanol monomers required for PA polymerization [58]. Such an early-stage activation of PA biosynthesis is consistent with a conserved developmental strategy among seed-bearing species, reflecting the need for chemical defense during vulnerable stages of fruit expansion [59–61]. The protein-precipitating and astringent properties of PAs deter herbivores and pathogens, whereas their polyphenolic nature confers antioxidant activity that protects developing embryos during periods of high metabolic and lipid-biosynthetic demand [29, 59]. During the mid-to-late developmental stages, the gradual decline in PA pathway gene expression suggested a metabolic shift, a pattern also reported in walnut and chestnut [61]. Once PA levels reached a threshold sufficient for defense, feedback-related mechanisms may attenuate PA biosynthesis gene expression, enabling a shift in metabolic priorities. This transition facilitates the redirection of carbon and energy from secondary metabolism toward the biosynthesis of storage reserves and structural components during fruit ripening [62, 63]. Class I Qg4CL genes were activated, indicating a shift from PA biosynthesis to lignin production, promoting seed-coat and pericarp lignification and enhancing mechanical protection and desiccation tolerance at maturity [53, 64–66]. Collectively, these findings revealed a biphasic transcriptional pattern in Q. glauca, characterized by early PA-mediated chemical defense followed by a transition to storage compound accumulation and structural reinforcement. These insights provide valuable guidance for optimizing harvest timing to balance nutritional quality and seed viability, thereby supporting value-added applications of acorns.

Developmental reprogramming mediates the shift from defense to storage metabolism

The temporal changes in DEGs abundance provided molecular evidence for a developmental shift from defense-oriented metabolism to storage accumulation. Pairwise comparisons showed relatively few DEGs between T1-T2 and T4-T5, indicating stable transcriptional programs during early embryogenesis and near maturity [31, 67]. In contrast, the marked increase in DEGs at intermediate stages highlighted T3-T5 as a key period of transcriptional reprogramming. DEGs with sustained repression or activation during acorn maturation stage were identified through the intersection of T4 (vs. T1-T3) and T5 (vs. T1-T4) comparisons (Fig. S4). Genes with sustained repression were enriched in GO terms related to cell cycle progression and chromatin organization. These processes were associated with conserved developmental regulators such as Cyclin-Dependent Kinase (CDK), Casein Kinase II (CK2), Kinesin (KIN13A), Fertilization-Independent Endosperm (FIE), and CURLY LEAF (CLF) [68–71]. Repression of this regulatory program may redirect cellular resources including ATP, amino acids and nucleotides from proliferative activity toward storage-compound synthesis. Conversely, the activated carbohydrate and starch biosynthesis pathways suggest a corresponding redirection of carbon flux toward reserve formation. These pathways involve coordinated activity of sucrose synthase, AGPase, and starch synthases [72, 73]. Enrichment of glyoxylate cycle genes (GO:0009512) further suggested increased conversion of lipid stores into soluble sugars, a process known to support germination in species [74–77]. These findings indicate that lipid and starch metabolism together form a complementary energy system that supports embryo maturation and enhances seed physiological readiness. Such coordinated metabolic reprogramming provides a mechanistic basis for improving acorn quality, developmental performance, and potential utilization in industrial and nutritional applications.

Functional diversification of QgMYB transcription factors during acorn development

Our integrated transcriptomic analyses identified MYB TFs as candidates associated with PA biosynthesis in Q. glauca, showing distinct expression and duplication patterns compared with model species. Comparative genomic analyses have indicated considerable evolutionary plasticity of MYB gene families across woody species, with gene number, structural features, and functional diversification shaped by life-history traits and ecological context [78]. The QgMYB family comprises 46 members, and intra-genomic collinearity analysis identified only 2 tandem pairs and 9 segmental pairs (Table S5), indicating limited duplication-driven expansion of this family in Q. glauca. Enrichment analysis of the QgMYB clades with limited expansion showed predominant involvement in environmental response pathways, consistent with long-term niche specialization and the retention of a streamlined core regulatory set [34]. Phylogenetic comparisons with functionally validated A. thaliana homologs revealed lineage-specific divergence in expression. QgMYB17 (orthologous to AtMYB11/12/111) and QgMYB09/25(orthologous to AtMYB113/114) clustered with known PA regulators, but exhibited constitutively low expression across all developmental stages [79–81]. This pattern suggests functional divergence, transcriptional silencing, or activation restricted to specific tissues or stress conditions. In addition to gene expression divergence, structural variation has been shown to play a crucial role in regulatory diversification [82]. Specifically, an LTR retrotransposon insertion upstream of QgMYB25 was associated with elevated expression during acorn maturation, a regulatory mode similar to LTR-mediated activation of MdMYB1 in apple [57, 83]. Such structural modifications may introduce novel cis-regulatory elements, enhancing transcription in specific developmental contexts. Notably, two MYB members, QgMYB13 and QgMYB33, showed sustained high expression during the T3-T5 transition. QgMYB33 was assigned to the WGCNA turquoise module associated with carbohydrate metabolism and showed significant positive correlations with both PA and starch content. This expression pattern suggests that QgMYB13 and QgMYB33 may function as bifunctional regulators mediating crosstalk between PA biosynthesis and storage carbohydrate metabolism during acorn maturation. Similar multifunctional MYB regulators have been reported in other species, where they mediate crosstalk between phenylpropanoid pathways and primary carbohydrate allocation [84–86]. Collectively, these results indicate that MYB TFs in Q. glauca have diversified through gene family contraction, lineage-specific expression reprogramming, and structural variation, providing candidate regulators potentially associated with both PA biosynthesis and primary carbohydrate metabolism during fruit development.

Progressive starch accumulation supports energy provisioning during acorn maturation

The shift from PA to starch biosynthesis was associated with a key transition in carbon allocation, with starch content steadily increasing from T3 to T5 in parallel with the upregulation of starch biosynthesis gene families (Fig. 5B). This progressive accumulation pattern aligns with the “stored TNC” strategy observed in woody perennials, in which carbon reserves accumulate gradually to buffer against long-term environmental variability [15]. Notably, intra-genomic collinearity analysis revealed that the QgGPT family expanded through both tandem and segmental duplications (Table S5), potentially enhancing metabolic flux and functional redundancy in starch biosynthesis. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that two highly expressed homologs, QgGPT7 and QgGPT10, clustered with AtGPT1 (At5g54800), which encodes a glucose-6-phosphate transporter essential for Arabidopsis seed development [87]. QgGPT7 and QgGPT10 exhibited significantly elevated expression during late-stage acorn maturation, suggesting a role in regulating carbon partitioning into storage tissues and aligning with the reported function of GPT homologs in Ipomoea batatas [88]. Collectively, the sustained high expression of GPT genes with evidence of duplication supports their potential role in maintaining energy reserves from acorn maturation through germination, consistent with carbon reserve strategies reported in woody perennials [15]. However, higher starch content may also increase predation risk. In acorns, PAs accumulating in the embryo have been reported to act as a chemical deterrent that reduces palatability and predation pressure with limited effect on germination [29]. Studies in other oak species have shown that acorns rich in PAs experience reduced predation rates, and even when partially consumed due to their astringency, they can still maintain normal germination [89, 90]. This dual strategy integrates rapid starch accumulation for energy provisioning with localized PA-mediated defense. In oaks, such integration enhances nutritional autonomy, predator resistance, and dispersal survival, a pattern that may reflect an integrated resource allocation mechanism in long-lived woody plants facing both biotic and abiotic variability.

Study limitation

Our integrated transcriptomic and biochemical evidence supports the central hypothesis that starch and PA biosynthesis in Q. glauca acorns are governed by stage-specific transcriptional programs operating during different developmental phases, resulting in differential accumulation patterns of these metabolites and concurrently delineating the scope of the present study. The early upregulation and progressive downregulation of PA biosynthesis genes, together with the continuous upregulation of starch biosynthesis genes, are consistent with the measured trajectories of PA and starch accumulation. However, transcriptomic data alone are insufficient to validate the underlying metabolic output and carbon allocation between the two pathways. The critical developmental stages and the metabolite baselines established in this study provide a temporal framework and quantitative reference for future enzyme activity assays, quantitative proteomics, and metabolic flux analyses. Several priority candidate genes emerged from these analyses, including the PA-pathway hub QgPAL7, the bifunctional regulator QgMYB33, and the starch-transporter genes QgGPT7 and QgGPT10. In follow-up studies, transgenic complementation and dual-luciferase reporter assays will be applied to test the regulatory roles of these candidates as a stable transformation system for Q. glauca is established. The present sampling spanned the rapid fruit-filling phase, and extending the time series from anthesis through late dormancy will further place these regulatory programs within the complete developmental cycle of acorn maturation. Such an extended sampling framework would also help to clarify how starch reserves and PA-mediated chemical defense are coordinated to support seed dormancy and post-dispersal germination in Q. glauca.

Conclusions

Transcriptome profiling of five developmental stages (T1-T5) in Q. glauca fruits identified 2,637 DEGs and characterized a biphasic transcriptional program coordinating starch and PA biosynthesis. Direct metabolite quantification confirmed continuous starch accumulation (5.0–9.5 mg/mg protein) and a fluctuating PA profile (0.015–0.038 mg/mg protein) peaking at T3 and T5, mirroring two contrasting transcriptional patterns: PA biosynthesis genes were induced early and progressively repressed toward maturation, whereas starch biosynthesis genes were continuously up-regulated. WGCNA further resolved five trait-associated modules and 50 hub gene candidates, while gene-metabolite correlation analysis identified QgMYB33 as a stage-specific candidate. Intra-genomic collinearity analysis revealed expansion of the QgGPT family through tandem and segmental duplications, with QgGPT7 and QgGPT10 emerging as candidate contributors to late-stage starch accumulation. RT-qPCR confirmed the reliability of the RNA-seq results. Collectively, this study characterizes the transcriptional dynamics underlying starch and PA biosynthesis during Q. glauca fruit development, providing insights into metabolic coordination in woody plant fruits.
