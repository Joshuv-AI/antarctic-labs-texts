Heritable variation drives rapid evolution of thermal performance curves in the protist Tetrahymena thermophila
Authors: Megan H. Liu, Ze-Yi Han, Yaning Yuan, Katrina DeWitt, Daniel J. Wieczynski, Kathryn M. Yammine, Andrea Yammine, Rebecca A. Zufall, Adam M. Siepielski, Douglas L. Chalker, Masayuki Onishi, Fabio A. Machado, Jean P. Gibert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Microbial respiration is a key biotic driver of climate change. Warming boosts microbial population growth, which increases biomass and respiration, potentially leading to more warming. This feedback might be disrupted by adaptation in thermal performance curves (TPCs) –whose shape describes how temperature drives growth. In this study, we uncover substantial genetic variation (G) in the intrinsic population growth rates (r) of the protist Tetrahymena thermophila, demonstrate a causal link between heritable variation in r and heritable variation in TPC shape, and show how this variation constrains predicted r-TPC shape evolution along specific evolutionary paths across temperatures. We also uncover Gene-by-Environment (G × E) variation in r, which results in specific signatures in TPC shape and predictable temperature-dependent TPC evolution that can erode heritable variation, thus reducing future evolutionary potential. Overall, we show how temperature-dependent evolution in microbial TPC shape—a linchpin of global ecosystem function—is determined by a combination of heritable and non-heritable variation in intrinsic growth rates.

Introduction

Microbes play a central role in regulating the global carbon (C) cycle that controls climate change1. Indeed, soil microbial respiration releases an estimated 94Pg/yr of C into the atmosphere2 while microalgae fix 30–50 Pg of C/yr globally3. While these estimates come with high uncertainty2, they remain five to ten times larger than the amounts of C emitted by human activity worldwide4, a testament to their importance in the broader global C cycle5. As global warming is expected to alter these microbial processes4, a deeper understanding of the biotic and abiotic factors influencing microbial respiration is essential in a warming world6,7.

A key factor is microbial population growth, which determines total standing biomass, and consequently, total or bulk microbial respiration8. The thermal performance curve (TPC) of intrinsic population growth rate (r) describes how microbial population growth responds to temperature (‘r-TPCs’ henceforth, Fig. 1). The shape of r-TPCs is governed by temperature-dependent metabolic rates: metabolic rates increase with temperature, and so does r, until an ‘optimal’ temperature (Topt) is reached (Fig. 1a), after which r declines as metabolic costs rise9 (Fig. 1a). The link between r-TPCs and metabolic rates can be extremely tight (e.g., in the genus Tetrahymena10), but not necessarily one-to-one –e.g., due to changes in carbon use efficiency with temperature11, or differences in Topt between r and metabolic rate TPCs12,13. While r-TPC shape varies across species14,15, they are typically unimodal16 and their shape can be described by parameters linked to thermal ecology: maximum growth rate (rpeak), minimum and maximum temperatures for population growth (CTmin and CTmax respectively), and rates of r-TPC increase/decrease with temperature (Ea, Ed respectively, Fig. 1b). Understanding how TPCs evolve in novel environments is a critical challenge, as their shape determines a species’ capacity to cope with environmental change. Thermal adaptation in turn depends on the evolution of intraspecific –heritable– genetic variation, and selection favoring genotypes suited to new conditions17. Quantifying heritable variation in r-TPC shape parameters is thus central to predicting r-TPC shape evolution and its effect on ecological dynamics18. To do this, we notice that the r-TPC represents the pattern of variation of r across temperatures –i.e., the reaction norm of r19. The question then becomes whether that reaction norm varies systematically across genotypes, and how, unlocking multiple tools from quantitative genetics.

Using quantitative genetics allows partitioning observed variation in r across temperatures and genotypes into (heritable) additive genetic variation (G), non-heritable environmental variation (E), and gene-by-environment interactions (GxE), if genotype-specific r-TPC data is available for a species. Connecting heritable variation in r across temperatures to heritable variation in r-TPC shape parameters would then further allow the estimation of heritability of r-TPC shape parameters, quantification of regimes of selection acting on those parameters, and predictions of shape parameter evolution under said selection. But what is the connection between heritable variation in r and r-TPC shape parameters?

In this study we address r-TPC shape evolution by developing and testing a conceptual model based on classic quantitative genetics of reaction norms to connect heritable r variation and heritable r-TPC variation explicitly, and do so by asking whether and how E, G and GxE variation in r explains variation in r-TPC shape parameters (Fig. 1c–e). First, environmental variation (E) in r, which is driven by differential expression of r across temperatures but not genotypes, manifests classically as completely overlapping reaction norms across genotypes (Fig. 1c, first row). Correspondingly, genotypic r-TPCs –which are the reaction norms of r extended over multiple temperatures– should also overlap fully (Fig. 1d, first row), and present no variation in shape parameters across genotypes (Fig. 1e, first row). In other words, E variation in r alone should not lead to nor explain variation in shape parameters.

Second, genetic variation (G) in r, which arises from differential expression of r across genotypes but not temperatures, leads to additive shifts in the intercepts of each genotypic reaction norm while slopes remain equal (Fig. 1c, second row). This translates to parallel r-TPCs (Fig. 1d, second row), that show variation in CTmin, CTmax, and rpeak among genotypes, but no variation in Topt, Ea, or Ed (Fig. 1e, second row). This inter-genotypic variation in CTmin, CTmax, and rpeak, is additive genetic variation, and, thus heritable. In other words, (heritable) genetic variation in r, should result in (heritable) genetic variation in CTmin, CTmax, and rpeak, only.

Finally, gene-by-environment (G × E) interactions in r, which alters both intercept and slope of reaction norms across genotypes and temperatures (Fig. 1c, third row), result in non-parallel r-TPCs (Fig. 1d, third row), and variation in all shape parameters among genotypes (Fig. 1e, third row). Traditionally, GxE variation was considered non-heritable, but some forms of GxE have been shown to be heritable20–22. So, it is possible for some fraction of this GxE variation in r, and resulting variation in r-TPCs, to also be at least partially heritable, even though it is classically assumed not to be. Overall, this conceptual model predicts that we should observe larger covariation between heritable genetic variation (G) in r and variation in CTmin, CTmax, and rpeak, compared to Topt, Ea, or Ed. A corollary of this prediction, is that CTmin, CTmax, and rpeak should be more heritable than Topt, Ea, or Ed. However, the latter might still show some level of heritable variation if some fraction of GxE in r is also heritable. Consequently, the model predicts that CTmin, CTmax, and rpeak are more likely to respond to selection than other aspects of the TPC, which imposes specific constraints on possible r-TPC evolutionary trajectories.

To understand how r-TPC shape might evolve across temperatures we quantified E, G, and G × E variation in r in the protist species Tetrahymena thermophila and tested predictions from our conceptual model, revealing substantial heritable variation in both r and shape parameters, and providing insights into the structure of genetic variation and covariation in r-TPCs. Using this information, we inferred selection and potential evolutionary trajectories of r-TPC shape parameters across temperatures. Last, we identified and tested a mechanism of rapid r-TPC evolution driven by G × E variation in r, which can lead to temperature-dependent selection on r-TPC shape but also erode heritable variation, potentially creating an evolutionary trap that constrains future microbial r-TPC evolution.

Results

Heritable variation in r leads to heritable variation in r-TPC shape parameters

Intrinsic growth rates (r) from 22 genetically distinct genotypes of T. thermophila (Supplementary Table 1) showed strong temperature responses within genotypes (F = 3092.70, Df = 6, Generalized η2 (GES) effect size = 0.964, p ≤ 0.001), significant variability across genotypes (F = 163.65, p ≤ 0.001, Df = 21, GES = 0.832), and significant G × E interactions (F = 29.7, p ≤ 0.001, Df = 122, GES = 0.840), leading to classically unimodal TPC curves that varied significantly among genotypes (Fig. 2a). Environmental variation (E) accounted for 71.7% of all observed variation in r, genetic variation (G) explained 6.2%, and Gene-by-Environment interactions (G × E) explained 11.7%, with 10.5% residual variation (Fig. 2b). These proportions are consistent with expectations for life history traits23. Also consistent with our framework’s predictions, shape parameter heritabilities were higher for CTmin and rpeak compared to Topt or Ea (Fig. 3). Consequences of G in r: selection and evolvability of r-TPC shape parameters

According to univariate models to quantify natural selection (see Methods, Supplementary Table 7), selection varied across shape parameters and was temperature dependent: rpeak showed no selection at low temperatures (<20 °C, Fig. 4a, Supplementary Table 8), no directional selection to weakly positive –but no significant curvature– at intermediate temperatures (between 20 and 30 °C, Fig. 4a, Supplementary Table 8), and strong positive directional selection at high temperatures (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Table 9). Ea followed a similar pattern with one difference: significant negative selection at low temperature was observed (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Table 9). CTmin was under negative selection at low/intermediate temperatures but not under selection at high temperatures (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Table 10). We found positive genetic covariances between shape parameters rpeak and Ea, CTmin and Ea, and CTmin and rpeak (Fig. 4), so selection on highly heritable parameters CTmin and rpeak could result in correlated evolution in more weakly heritable parameters Ea, and Topt. Accounting for the broader pattern of variation-covariation among shape parameters (see Methods), we confirmed differences in selection across shape parameters whose magnitude and direction shifted with temperature (Fig. 4f), leading to temperature-dependent expected evolutionary change (Fig. 4g). Specifically, a multivariate analysis (see Methods) suggested that selection would favor higher rpeak at high temperatures, low CTmin at low temperatures, and, possibly, higher Ea at high temperatures, but no directional selection on Topt (Fig. 4f), generally matching univariate predictions (Fig. 4a–d). Overall, the evolutionary responses (∆z) tracked those expected based on selection estimates (β, Fig. 4f, g), with a few exceptions that suggest indirect, correlated evolution. For example, strong positive response of Ea with temperature, despite comparatively weak selection, likely reflects correlated evolution with rpeak, CTmin or both (Fig. 4g). Despite differences in heritability, which impose constraints in r-TPC evolution, genetic correlations among shape parameters should enable temperature-dependent evolutionary responses in all of them except Topt, which appears to be under stabilizing selection (Fig. 4d).

Consequences of G × E in r: sorting of standing genetic variation across temperatures

We observed significant temperature-dependent differential selection among genotypes (Fig. 5e), consistent with theoretical predictions from a simple model of genetic evolution (Fig. 5f). This confirms the hypothesis that G × E in r could drive rapid r-TPC shape evolution through selection on standing genetic variation (Fig. 5a, b). Although there were quantitative discrepancies between observed and predicted frequencies—notably at 19 °C, where the model predicted a polymorphic population, but the data did not show that (Fig. 5e, f)—the model still correctly predicted qualitative changes in genetic frequencies across temperatures. The experimental results also aligned with our predicted responses to selection (cf. Fig. 4 and Fig. 5). At lower temperatures, the CU4106 genotype, which has lower Ea and CTmin compared to AXS, became more prevalent (Fig. 5d). Consequently, the average r-TPC of the population should reflect these traits, as predicted by our multivariate model. At higher temperatures, selection favored AXS, which has higher Ea, leading to a population with an average r-TPC with higher Ea (Fig. 5d). Finally, rising temperatures initially increased genetic variance—facilitating adaptation—but then reduced it as the CU4106 genotype became less prevalent (Fig. 5e, f), potentially constraining future adaptive evolution.

Discussion

Our study reveals considerable heritable genetic variation in Tetrahymena thermophila’s intrinsic population growth rates and several r-TPC shape parameters (Figs. 2, 3), thus enabling clear potential for thermal adaptation to new climates (Fig. 4). The observed intraspecific variation in r-TPC shape parameters sometimes rivals reported interspecific variation across multiple protist species15. We also found that most r-TPC shape parameters are under different selection regimes across temperatures (Fig. 4), with CTmin and rpeak being more likely to respond to selection than Ea and Topt. However, less heritable parameters can evolve through correlated evolution (Fig. 4). Specifically, colder temperatures select for lower CTmin, rpeak, and Ea, and warmer temperatures favor higher rpeak and Ea (Fig. 4). Finally, we demonstrated that G × E in r can drive rapid, predictable shifts in population genetic makeup across temperatures, facilitating r-TPC evolution (Fig. 5), consistent with a “plasticity drives adaptation” scenario24.

While the evolution of microbial TPCs is likely shaped by local adaptation25, how r-TPCs will adapt to rising temperatures remains uncertain. Several evolutionary paths have been proposed: (1) ‘Colder-is-Better’ (CIB), where warming reduces population growth, lowering rpeak, Topt, and Ea26; (2) ‘Warmer-Is-Better’ (WIB), where higher temperatures favor increased rpeak and Topt27; and, (3) Generalist-Specialist-Tradeoff (GST), where species either evolve towards rapid growth within a narrow temperature range –i.e., temperature specialists with higher rpeak, higher CTmin, and lower CTmax– or slower growth over a broader range –i.e., temperature generalists with lower rpeak, lower CTmin, and higher CTmax28. Tests of these hypotheses often rely on interspecies comparisons10,25, which would be technically capable of accounting for intraspecific variation within shape parameters and genetic associations that ultimately determine evolutionary trajectories within clades if such data was readily available. Our study provides precisely the type of data that has been missing, and shows that by accounting for genetic covariances, Ea evolution is likely driven by correlated evolution in response to positive selection on rpeak (Fig. 4f, g), despite no selection acting on Ea (Fig. 4d, f, g), thus contradicting WIB. Positive selection in rpeak (Fig. 4a, b) and the absence of positive selection in CTmin at higher temperatures (Fig. 4c), in turn contradicts CIB and GST. These results therefore suggest that neither WIB, CIB or GST, can explain predicted evolutionary responses in T. thermophila, emphasizing the importance of intraspecific variation and genetic associations to fully understand r-TPC evolution.

When G × E in r is prevalent, we showed that thermal adaptation can occur rapidly through temperature-dependent selection on r-TPC genotypes (Fig. 5), resulting in reduced genetic variation, which could slow down, or impede, future adaptation29. In other words, we show that phenotypic plasticity in r could lead to adaptive evolution in r-TPCs in novel climates, but that could come at the expense of genetic diversity. That this form of r-TPC evolution could be anticipated from simple models of genetic evolution, is striking, considering how little information beyond the r-TPC of each genotype the model accounts for. Yet, r-TPC adaptation in deep time has been suggested to occur gradually across six different Tetrahymena species10, directly countering our claim that r-TPCs could evolve rapidly through temperature-dependent selection. We argue that this form of rapid r-TPC evolution may only play out during fast-changing environmental conditions, not always resulting in long-term r-TPC change, or be masked by reversals in selection. In fact, selection often “erases its traces”30, and is strongest under novel conditions, weakening as populations adapt31—i.e., “the paradox of stasis”30.

The temperature-dependent loss of genetic diversity in adaptive r-TPC evolution could influence the survival of genetically depauperate species in a warming environment. However, whether this is the case in nature depends on quantifying the processes that generate diversity (mutation, gene flow, recombination) in r and r-TPC shape parameters in the wild, as well as evaluating when these processes might counter the loss of genetic diversity following episodes of thermally induced r-TPC evolution. T. thermophila has the lowest reported base substitution mutation rate of all species32 and limited gene flow33, but even weak dispersal or rare mutation events could contribute to r and r-TPC variation and rescue genetic diversity in very large, rapidly reproducing populations. The prevalence of sexual reproduction in nature is debated –e.g., ~50% of wild individual T. thermophila cannot reproduce sexually and some Tetrahymena lineages have lost this ability entirety34– but likely important, if present. In fact, T. thermophila possesses two separate genomes, a germinal line (in micronucleus) and an expressed genome (in macronucleus). Our study focuses on macronuclear variation, but asexual reproduction following sex results in random distribution of macronuclear alleles to daughter cells—i.e., phenotypic assortment—potentially generating novel variation in r and r-TPCs35. Moreover, seemingly lost genetic variation could persist in the population in other individual’s micronuclei and reemerge following sexual reproduction36. Fully understanding thermal adaptation in nature thus requires exploring these processes’ interplay, yet, for most organisms, they remain poorly understood.

We quantified TPCs without acclimation to each temperature before conducting growth assays. By definition, these TPCs must characterize an acute response. Because acclimated TPCs can differ from acute ones37,38, whether our TPCs meaningfully quantify change in performance across temperatures compared to fully acclimated TPCs needs to be addressed. Thermal acclimation occurs within a single generation in the ciliate Clyclidium glaucoma with regard to cell size dynamics39, which has been shown to influence population growth40,41. Knapp et al.42 further show rapid acclimation in less than 2 generations in the bacterium Escherichia coli. In T. thermophila, generation time varies from 2 to 5 h, and significant population growth occurs 6–24 h–but more commonly, 12–24 h– post seeding, and rarely before then (lag period)43. It is therefore possible that our TPCs show a pattern of growth for near-acclimated populations, but that cannot be directly determined by our data. However, shape parameter values from acclimated TPCs for T. thermophila closely match our own10,44 despite differences in experimental contexts, media, and procedures, which provides independent validation for our TPCs.

There is additional evidence suggesting that these TPCs meaningfully reflect performance, at least in protists. Wieczynski et al.15 used a very similar assay protocol to quantify the (acute) TPCs of 14 ciliate species–albeit with variation in the number of days elapsed between quantifying initial and final densities– then showed that their shape could be predicted by species traits (e.g., body size, shape, optical properties). In turn, these TPCs accurately predicted temperature-driven shifts in community dynamics, diversity, trait distributions, and ecosystem function. Moreover, Vasseur et al.45 showed that a TPC quantified using the same procedure as in Wieczynski et al.15 –the same used in the present study– also accurately predicts population growth, persistence, and extinction risk in Paramecium caudatum across temperatures. Together, these results suggest that our TPCs might be capturing more than the acute stress responses of unacclimated organisms, and clearly reflect ecologically relevant performance across temperatures, even though at temperatures much higher than 22 °C the individuals might be experiencing heightened thermal stress. This heightened thermal stress may ultimately impair their ability to survive and reproduce at rates higher than would be expected in a natural setting, which is a limitation of the present study.

While bodies of water in T. thermophila’s native range are warming significantly46 and experiencing more frequent and extreme heatwaves47, water temperature typically remains 2–5 °C cooler than air temperature48. Thus, some of the warmer temperatures in our study may be uncommon in T. thermophila’s native range. However, strong temperature effects observed at low temperatures suggest these patterns can still operate in many T. thermophila populations. Also, previous research showed that variation in r-TPC shape across species could be collapsed into a single dimension of variation49, suggesting that some of the covariation observed in this study between shape parameters could be spurious. However, if the r-TPCs collected here could in fact be collapsed into a single axis of variation, we would have expected strong and multiple covariations between most if not all shape parameters—which was not the case (Fig. 4e, f). Interestingly, most of the genetic variation in shape parameters was observed, in CTmin and rpeak, but these two parameters were at best weakly correlated to one another. So, perhaps intraspecific r-TPC variation buck the trend observed across species49, but more research is needed to clarify this point.

Overall, TPCs control the fate of populations50, ecological interactions51, food web dynamics41,52, and ecosystem processes53. Yet, TPC evolution in a rapidly warming world remains conspicuously unknown. Here, we show that r intraspecific variation can drive temperature-dependent evolution in microbial r-TPCs and have consequences for rapid shifts in population genetic makeup. Our study emphasizes the importance of temperature in mediating rapid microbial evolutionary change as we grapple with understanding and predicting organismal responses to an increasingly warmer world.

Methods

Study system and genotypes

Tetrahymena thermophila is a freshwater ciliate protist found across the Northeastern United States33 and part of a cosmopolitan genus54. Protists are unicellular Eukaryotes that dominate oceanic biomass and rank third in terrestrial biomass, collectively comprising twice the biomass of the Animal Kingdom55 and are key contributors to global ecosystem functioning56. While no single species is representative of the entire genus, studying thermal adaptation in T. thermophila might provide valuable insights into this important lineage.

We sourced 22 unique T. thermophila genotypes: 19 from the Washington University Tetrahymena Stock Center (https://sites.wustl.edu/tetrahymena/) and 3 from the Chalker lab at Washington University (Supplementary Table 1). These genotypes vary in geographic origin, exhibit well-documented genetic differences (Supplementary Table 1), and were selected to capture existing genetic variation rather than for their functional significance. While systematic biases in genetic distances between genotypes could influence analyses, to our knowledge, there is no genome-wide molecular divergence data for all genotypes used. To minimize the chance of inserting this form of bias, we aimed for a broad representation of genetic backgrounds, strains capable of both sexual and asexual reproduction, and both laboratory-generated and natural genotypes (Supplementary Table 1). Despite these efforts, most genotypes are artificial mutants, so our assemblage likely contains less variation than natural populations –which show significant amounts of genetic divergence even between neighboring populations33. Thus, our findings likely are only a conservative estimate of potential thermal adaptation in nature.

Quantifying r, r-TPCs, and TPC shape parameters

The Washington University Tetrahymena Stock Center maintains cultures frozen and thaws them up before sending. Upon reception, we transferred the genotypes from axenic Proteose Peptone growth medium to Timothy Hay growth medium inoculated with a bacterial community from Duke Forest Gate 9 pond/Wilbur pond (Lat 36.013914, Long −78.979720) –described elsewhere7– and a wheat kernel, as a Carbon source57. Cultures were allowed to grow to carrying capacity (~4 days) in Percival AL-22 growth chambers in a 12 h day-night cycle at 22 °C, prior to quantifying their TPCs. We quantified TPCs by conducting growth assays at seven temperatures (13, 19, 22, 25, 30, 32, 38 °C) in 3 cm diameter Petri dish microcosms containing 3 mL of growth medium. Cultures were transferred directly to their assay temperatures without an acclimation period. Each genotype-temperature combination was replicated six times, totaling 924 microcosms. These experimental temperatures span below and above the average growing season temperature in T. thermophila’s native range (~23 °C)58. Microcosms were initialized at densities of 1ind/mL in 3 mL Petri dishes, which results in a concentration that supports exponential growth for 1–2 days40,41,43. After 24 h, we conducted whole-population counts using a stereomicroscope (Leica M205C) and calculated r across temperatures as log(final density/initial density)/time, with time = 1 day15.

Link between heritable genetic variation in r, and r-TPC shape parameters

We quantified E, G and G × E variation in r using function gxeVarComps() in R package statgenGxE v1.0.5. on the observed r values for all genotypes across temperatures. The function fits a series of mixed effects models with r as the response variable, and temperature, genotype, and their interaction as fixed terms to calculate effect sizes and statistical significance, and then refits the model with those terms as random effects to estimate variance components (E, G, and G × E). The fraction of heritable genetic variation in r is classically defined as broad-sense heritability, H2 = G/(E + G + G × E). However, this expression does not account for inter-treatment and replicate variability, which inflates E relative to G, and underestimates H2,60,61. Other standard formulations for H2 exist that address these issues (Supplementary Table 2) and were calculated using package statgenGxE.

Next, we tested the predictions of our verbal model (Fig. 1) linking heritable variation (G) in r, to heritable variation in r-TPC shape parameters. Since each r-TPC yields a single set of shape parameters, only G variation in these parameters can be quantified. All other components of variation (e.g., E, G × E, residual variation and error) were thus treated as residual variance. We notice that this approach is conservative: it treats all unexplained variation (including any heritable G × E that may exist) as non-genetic, and hence, as part of the denominator, which underestimates heritability. To evaluate the model’s prediction that G variation in r results in greater G variation in rpeak and CTmin compared to Ea, and Topt (Fig. 1e), we tested whether rpeak and CTmin exhibited stronger covariance with r than did Ea, and Topt using a multivariate Bayesian Generalized Linear Mixed Model. The model includes mean-scaled values of r, Ea, rpeak, CTmin and Topt, as jointly modeled response variables, each with their own fixed mean, and Genotype as a random effect, which allowed us to estimate variances and covariances. The model was implemented in the R package MCMCglmm v.2.3662. Variance-covariance priors were obtained by bootstrapping r-TPC data 100 times within each genotype and re-fitting the Sharpe-Schoolfield model to each bootstrap replicate, generating a population of shape parameters for each genotype (Supplementary Fig. 6). Using non-informative inverse Wishart priors63 did not alter our results. Finally, if G variation in r results in greater G in rpeak and CTmin, these shape parameters also should exhibit greater heritability than Ea, and Topt. To test this, we calculated the broad-sense heritability in r-TPC parameters using the standard expression H2 = G/(G+residual variance) using 1000 posterior samples of G and the residual variance matrix from the MCMCglmm model.

Consequences of heritable variation: selection and evolutionary potential of r-TPC parameters

To understand how r-TPC shape parameters can evolve under selection, we quantified (1) the direction, form, and magnitude of selection on all four shape parameters, (2) whether and how temperature influenced selection, and (3) their potential evolutionary responses across temperatures, to said selection. We used two approaches: one that allows for the estimation of non-linear selection (e.g., quadratic, stabilizing selection), but neglects genetic correlations between parameters and thus cannot make predictions about possible evolutionary trajectories, and another that accounts for genetic correlations, does not account for non-linear selection, but enables predictions of evolutionary responses across temperatures. The combination of both gives us the most complete picture possible.

In the first approach, we quantified selection by characterizing the adaptive landscape as the relationship between shape parameter values and absolute fitness, assumed here to be a function of r64,65. This assumption is justified because r reflects average birth and death rates, two major fitness components66,67. We modeled this relationship for all shape parameters across genotypes using polynomial regression with r as the response. We obtained linear and quadratic effects (i.e., non-linear selection) for each mean-standardized shape parameter, as well as additive and interactive temperature effects (Supplementary Table 3). Quadratic regression coefficients were multiplied by two68. A positive relationship between r and a shape parameter indicates positive directional selection, a negative relationship indicates negative directional selection, and no relationship suggests no directional selection. Stabilizing selection produces a concave-down relationship, while disruptive selection manifests as concave-up, where extreme values have higher fitness66.

The second approach predicts r-TPC shape evolution across temperatures while accounting for genetic correlations among shape parameters. It uses the multivariate breeder’s65 and Price’s69 equations to estimate direct (i.e., acting on the focal parameter) and indirect selection (i.e., acting on genetically linked parameters70). This is achieved by estimating the response to selection of all shape parameters, Δz, as their covariance with fitness, i.e., the Price equation – and back-estimating selection gradients, β, via the multivariate breeder’s equation.

Operationally, this approach requires estimating a variance-covariance matrix Gzw, which includes the genetic variance-covariance matrix (G) of the focal traits (r-TPC shape parameters) as its first four rows/columns, and their genetic covariation with fitness (W) as the last row/column64,65,71. We conducted the analysis using either r as the measure of fitness, or the finite rate of increase, R = exp(r), the latter providing numerical stability in and around the points where r goes from negative to positive (both metrics led to similar results). From the Price equation, the last row/column of Gzw equals the vector of predicted trait change, Δz. To estimate β, we used the G and Δz components of Gzw in the multivariate Breeder’s equation, Δz = Gβ, where β is the selection gradient65, which can be rearranged as β = G−1 Δz, to estimate selection.

Positive/negative Δzi values indicate an increase/decrease in the i-th shape parameter, while positive/negative βi, indicates positive/negative selection operating on the i-th shape parameter. Alignment between Δzi and βi indicates direct responses to selection72, while misalignment suggest the effect of selective tradeoffs with other correlated traits.

To estimate Gzw for each temperature, we used subsets of r per genotype for each temperature. While shape parameters do not change across temperatures, r does, so the Gzw matrices only differed in their last row/column across temperatures, i.e., the covariances between shape parameters and fitness (Δz). For interpretability, Gzw was calculated as Gzw = SLS*(1/2 F) where L is a between-genotype covariance matrix, S is a diagonal matrix containing the inverse of each trait’s standard deviation, and F is the inbreeding coefficient, which equals 1 for clonal lineages73. This scaling ensures that the resulting β and Δz will be comparable across traits72.

Uncertainty in Gzw entries was quantified using a Bayesian posterior distribution of L matrices (and hence Gzw). We used a multivariate normal likelihood function and non-informative inverse regularized Wishart priors (nu=traits+1)63, implemented in evolqg v3.0 R package74. Using MCMCglmm instead of evolqg did not affect our results (Supplementary Fig. 7). We took 1000 posterior samples for Gzw to calculate Δz, G, and β, and 95% maximum density intervals for all estimates.

Consequences of G × E in r for rapid r-TPC evolution

Because G × E variation in r is predicted to result in non-parallel r-TPCs (Fig. 1), slight differences in intrinsic growth rates across temperatures can drive temperature-mediated differential selection among genotypes (Fig. 4a, b). Treating r as proxy for absolute fitness65, this selection should be strongest at temperatures where r-TPCs differ the most, i.e., the temperatures with the largest differences in relative fitness.

We empirically tested for temperature-dependent selection among genotypes by setting up an experimental evolution assay with two fluorescently marked strains (AXS and CU4106, Fig. 4c) with different r-TPCs and relative fitness (Fig. 4d, Fig. 4d inset) across six temperatures (19, 22, 25, 30, 32, 38 °C). Each temperature treatment was replicated seven times, and with two additional single-strain controls per temperature. The genotypes cannot mate because they belong to the same mating type (mating type VII, Supplementary Table 1), so only clonal reproduction was possible. We initialized our microcosms at equal densities (5 ind/mL in mixture treatments, 10ind/mL for single-genotype controls). After 48 h, we added Cadmium Chloride to induce fluorescence75, confirmed on a Leica Thunder Cell Culture inverted microscope equipped with an HC PL APO 63X/1.40 N.A. oil-immersion objective lens (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 8). Thirty minutes later, we cytometrically censused each sample (Novocyte 2000R). While fluorescently tagged genotypes can lose their ability to fluoresce over time, they carry a paromomycin resistance gene, allowing selection through paromomycin exposure (100 μg/mL) prior to counting and estimating relative frequencies based on fluorescence (Supplementary Fig. 9 and Supplementary Table 12). Antibiotics can negatively affect protists and the bacterial communities they feed on, so we replicated this experiment in paromomycin-free conditions, which did not qualitatively alter our results (Supplementary Fig. 1).
