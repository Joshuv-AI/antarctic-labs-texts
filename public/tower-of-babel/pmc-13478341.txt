Deciphering physiological mechanisms of biopolymeric seed coatings in oilseed crops
Authors: S. Vijaykumar, V. Sowmya, K. S. V. Poorna Chandrika, R. D. Prasad, M. Kavya, Praduman Yadav, M. Suresh, RK Mathur, B. Rajeswari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Oilseed crops experience substantial yield losses due to rapid seed deterioration, inconsistent moisture availability, early pathogen pressure, productivity and storability, particularly in smallholder systems. To meet these constraints, an eco-friendly, biopolymer-based double-layer seed coating containing multiple crop inputs, (fungicide, Trichoderma harzianum (Th4d), and a crop-specific biofertilizer (Azotobacter, Bradyrhizobium, and Rhizobium) for sesame, soybean and groundnut, respectively was developed. The layer-by-layer configuration ensured spatial separation and sequential release of chemical and biological inputs, enhancing compatibility, persistence, and functional delivery. Double-Layer Coating (DL-R) treatment was validated under laboratory, greenhouse, and field conditions in groundnut, while its efficacy under laboratory and greenhouse assays was confirmed in sesame and soybean. DL-R consistently retained the highest germination after storage (78.8–86.7%), with only 5–8% decline compared with 56–58% and > 12–23% decline in untreated seeds. Microbial viability remained superior with DL-R (2.86–2.94 Log CFU g⁻¹ for T. harzianum; 4.69–4.92 Log CFU g⁻¹ for biofertilizers), 0.3–0.5 Log units higher than control. Laboratory and greenhouse assays showed 60–75% lower seedling mortality than untreated seeds across all crops. In groundnut field trials, DL-R improved emergence (↑20–27%), reduced stem rot (↓80%), lowered pest load (↓50–70%), and achieved the highest yield (21.1 q ha⁻¹ vs. 12.9 q ha⁻¹), with the maximum B: C ratio (1:2.41). This study demonstrates a scalable double-layer coating platform that enhances seed protection, microbial symbiosis, and yield, providing a low-chemical pathway for resilient oilseed production.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56648-6.

Introduction

Oilseed crops are an essential segment of Indian agriculture, ranking next only to cereals in economic and nutritional importance. India is the fourth-largest producer of oilseeds globally, with a cultivated area of approximately 28–30 million hectares. In 2023–24, the country achieved a record oilseed production of 39.67 million tonnes, followed by 42.99 million tonnes in 2025–26 (IndiaStat, 2024–25). The major oilseeds grown include soybean, groundnut, sesame, mustard, sunflower, safflower, and niger (edible), and castor and linseed (non-edible). However, biotic and abiotic stresses severely constrain oilseed productivity. Biotic stresses—including fungal, bacterial, and viral pathogens, nematodes, and insect pests—are responsible for significant pre- and post-emergence seedling losses. Fungal pathogens, in particular, cause substantial damage to seed viability, vigour, and early crop establishment1,2. Simultaneously, abiotic stresses such as drought, salinity, high temperatures, and nutrient deficiencies negatively affect germination, metabolic activity, and seedling performance3,4. Together, these stressors can result in up to 30–50% yield reduction in oilseed crops under field conditions, depending on severity and crop variety5.

The widespread use of chemical seed protectants has been the traditional line of defense against biotic threats, but their overuse raises concerns of environmental contamination, human and animal health hazards, and development of pathogen resistance. Increasingly, the focus is shifting toward eco-friendly seed treatment technologies, particularly the use of biological control agents like Trichoderma harzianum and Bradyrhizobium spp., which offer dual benefits of disease suppression and plant growth promotion6,7.

Biological seed treatments can be delivered through coating technologies that enhance adhesion and protection of microbial inoculants. Biological seed coating refers to applying beneficial microorganisms or protective materials to seeds prior to sowing, aimed at suppressing pathogens, improving early vigor, and mitigating environmental stress effects (Moulahoum et al., 2026;8. However, a major bottleneck in the practical adoption of microbial seed treatments is the rapid decline in bioagent viability during storage, driven by poor shelf life, incompatibility with chemical inputs, and instability of consortia applied during seed processing rather than immediately before sowing9,10. As a result, farmers remain hesitant to use biological coatings due to concerns over their reliability and the absence of standardized application protocols11,12.

In this context, layer by layer-based seed coatings have emerged as an effective means to protect sensitive biological inputs and to deliver the incompatible multiple inputs during seed storage and germination (Chandrika et al., 2019,13,14. Biopolymers such as chitosan offer several advantages: they are biodegradable, possess plant bio-stimulant properties, and provide a microenvironment that enhances microbial survival and seed physiology8. Chitosan also acts as an elicitor of plant defense, adding a further layer of protection against biotic stress.

Advancing this concept, biopolymer-based double-layer seed coating technology—a strategic deposition of biopolymers and inputs in multiple discrete layers—has demonstrated significant potential (Vijay et al., 2024). In such a system, protective agents such as fungicides can be placed in an outer layer to offer immediate protection against soil pathogens, while beneficial microbes can be encapsulated in an inner biopolymeric matrix to ensure gradual release and enhanced survival15,16. This layer-by-layer strategy not only improves compatibility between bioagents and agrochemicals but also extends microbial shelf life, enhances stress tolerance, and ensures uniform seed germination and seedling vigor under both biotic and abiotic constraints.

Despite its promise, systematic evaluation of double-layer biopolymer seed coating under simulated storage and stress conditions remains limited for oilseed crops. Therefore, the present study investigates the effectiveness of biopolymer-based double-layer seed coatings containing a chemical fungicide (Penflufen + Trifloxystrobin), and biological agents (T. harzianum and crop-specific biofertilizers) in soybean, groundnut, and sesame. The study aims to assess their impact on seed quality, bioagent viability, grain biochemical properties, yield attributes and biotic stress suppression under greenhouse and field conditions, thereby contributing to climate-resilient, sustainable oilseed production17,18.

Materials and methods

Materials

Sesame (Var. Swetha), groundnut (Var. K-6) and soybean (Var. JS-335) seeds were obtained from the ICAR-IIOR Seed Unit and stored under cold conditions until use. Bioagents T. harzianum (Th4d) accession number: NAIMCC-F-02188; Bradyrhizobium arachidis- accession number: NAIMCC-B-00233; Azotobacter chroococcum accession number: NAIMCC-B-00061 and Rhizobium were sourced from the IIOR Pathology Unit19.

Preparation of chitosan-based seed coating biopolymer

The chitosan-based seed coating biopolymer was prepared following the method described in Vijay et al., 2024 and treatment details as per in the given Table 1. Briefly, chitosan was dissolved under mild acidic conditions (1% v/v acetic acid) to protonate amino groups and enhance solubility, following established protocols for agricultural film formation20,21. The selected temperature range (60–80 °C) and stirring conditions were optimized to ensure complete polymer dissolution and homogeneous film-forming properties without degradation. Later, chitosan and polyethylene glycol (PEG) were mixed in a ratio of 1:0.33% (w/v) and dissolved in distilled water under acidic conditions. Glycerol (0.66% w/v) was added as a plasticizer, and the mixture was stirred overnight at 60–80 °C (300 rpm) to obtain a homogeneous biopolymer solution.

The prepared biopolymer was impregnated with crop inputs including Trichoderma harzianum (1% w/v; 10¹⁰–10¹² CFU), Bradyrhizobium sp. (5% w/v; 10⁸–10¹⁰ CFU), and fungicide (EverGol Xtend; 10% w/v). The dispersions were agitated for 15 min at 150 rpm to ensure uniform distribution. Microbial inocula were standardized based on viable cell counts prior to application. T. harzianum inoculum was adjusted to 10¹⁰–10¹² CFU g⁻¹, while biofertilizers were standardized to 10⁸–10¹⁰ CFU g⁻¹ using serial dilution and plate count methods to ensure uniformity across treatments.

For evaluation of microbial entrapment, single-layer and double-layer chitosan films containing the respective inputs were prepared by casting the biopolymer mixture onto petri plates and allowing them to dry. Entrapment efficiency of the microbial agents was determined using serial dilution and plate count methods on selective media.

Seed coating was performed using single- and double-layer approaches with different concentrations of the inputs. In single-layer coating, sterilized seeds were mixed with the chitosan–input formulation and agitated for 15 min followed by air drying. In double-layer coating, seeds were first coated with a mixture of chitosan, fungicide, and T. harzianum, dried, and subsequently coated with a second layer containing chitosan and Bradyrhizobium sp., followed by air drying at room temperature.

Direct compatibility assays between fungicide and microbial inoculants, along with physicochemical characterization of the chitosan-based coating matrix, were not repeated in the present study, as these aspects have been comprehensively established in our previous work22. The current study utilizes this validated coating architecture to examine its biological performance, storage behaviour, and field-level functionality.

Laboratory evaluation of different seed treatments for groundnut, sesame and soyabean under storage

After the imposition of seed treatments, both treated and untreated seeds (control) of groundnut, sesame and soyabean were packed in 700-gauge polythene bags and stored under ambient conditions for a period of four months. The laboratory experiments were laid out in a completely randomized design (CRD) with three replications. Standard germination tests were conducted following ISTA guidelines (ISTA.,1999), using the rolled paper towel method. Seeds were incubated in a seed germinator maintained at 25 ± 1 °C temperature and 90 ± 5% relative humidity. Evaluation of different seed treatments for groundnut crop under greenhouse conditions

Pots of five kg capacity filled with sterilized soil were prior inoculated with test pathogen separately @ 4 g/kg of soil before one week of sowing. Ten seeds of groundnut (K-6) were sown in each pot and replicated five times. Data on effect of pathogens on seed germination, seedling vigour and disease incidence in groundnut were recorded after 15 days of sowing. The germination percentage, vigour index and disease incidence were recorded.

The soil used in pot experiments was a sterilized sandy loam, representative of the red soils of the Rajendranagar region, with near-neutral pH (6.8–7.2) and moderate fertility status. Soil sterilization was carried out by autoclaving at 121 °C for 30 min on two consecutive days. The pathogen inoculum (Sclerotium rolfsii) was mass multiplied on sterilized sorghum grains and standardized to approximately 10⁶ sclerotia g⁻¹ prior to soil incorporation.

Evaluation of different seed treatments for groundnut crop under field conditions

The study was carried out at the Rajendranagar experimental area of ICAR-Indian Institute of Oilseeds Research (IIOR) in Hyderabad, Telangana. The aim was to investigate the impact of single- and double-layer seed coatings using the biopolymer chitosan, fungicide, and bioagents on the plant biometrics, disease incidence and yield attributes of groundnut cultivar K-6 under field conditions. The treated seeds of groundnut cultivar K-6 were planted with a spacing of 30 × 10 cm in January 2022, following a randomized block design with five replications. Standard intercultural operations were conducted to ensure the growth of a healthy crop. Disease incidence observations were recorded at various crop growth stages, while plant biometrics, yield, and yield attributes were documented at the time of harvest.

The experimental field soil at ICAR-IIOR, Rajendranagar, Hyderabad, is classified as sandy loam (Alfisol) with near-neutral pH and moderate nutrient status, typical of the semi-arid tropics. Standard agronomic practices recommended for groundnut were followed uniformly across treatments.

Evaluation of seed biochemical parameters

After harvest, seeds from each treatment were biochemically characterized to determine oil content, fatty acid composition, and protein levels. Oil percentage was measured on a dry weight basis using a bench-top pulsed nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) analyser (MQC-5, Oxford Instruments, London, UK) with the pre-installed ‘Easy Cal’ calibration software, following the protocol outlined by Yadav and Murthy24. Fatty acid profiling was carried out with an Agilent 7860 A gas chromatograph (GC) equipped with a flame ionization detector (FID) and an autosampler. Peak identification and quantification were performed using EZChrom Elite Compact software, calculating the relative proportion of each fatty acid from its peak area. Protein content was assessed via the Kjeldahl method, in which total nitrogen was first determined and then converted to crude protein content using the factor 6.25, based on the assumption that proteins contain roughly 16% nitrogen.

Yield attributing traits for groundnut crop

The yield attributing traits procedure was mentioned in the supplementary file.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using ANOVA after verifying assumptions of normality (Shapiro–Wilk test) and homogeneity of variance (Levene’s test). Treatment means were compared using Tukey’s HSD test at P < 0.05. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the FactoMineR (v2.7) and factoextra (v1.0.7) packages in R. Variables were standardized prior to PCA to ensure comparability.

Results

Effect of storage on seed quality attributes under different seed treatments in oilseed crops

Among sesame, groundnut and soybean, the double-layer coating (DL-R) consistently retained the highest germination and vigour after 4 months of storage. In sesame, DL-R showed the maximum germination (81.4%) compared with the lowest in untreated seeds (57.8%) (Supplementary Table S1). In groundnut, DL-R recorded 86.7%, while untreated seeds showed 56.7% (Supplementary Table S4). Similarly, soybean seeds treated with DL-R (and DL-H2) showed the highest germination (78.8%) compared with untreated seeds (57.6%) (Supplementary Table S7).

Seedling vigour indices also exhibited the same trend. In sesame, DL-R produced the highest SVI-I (1351) and SVI-II (5.4) versus the lowest in untreated seeds (SVI-I: 481; SVI-II: 0.8) (Supplementary Table S1). In groundnut, DL-R recorded the maximum SVI-I (2575) and SVI-II (56.2), whereas untreated seeds showed the lowest (SVI-I: 1153; SVI-II: 18.4) (Supplementary Table S4). For soybean, DL-R maintained the highest SVI-I (3137) and SVI-II (41.1) compared with untreated seeds (SVI-I: 1292; SVI-II: 13.2) (Supplementary Table S7).

Overall, the DL-R treatment resulted in the highest germination and vigour in all three oilseeds, while untreated seeds consistently showed the lowest performance, confirming the superior storability and seedling performance of the double-layer coating using radar plots (Figs. 1 and 2).

Effect of storage on viability of bioagent and biofertilizers under different seed treatments in oilseed crops

Viability of T. harzianum (Th4d) on coated seeds

The viability of T. harzianum (Log CFU g⁻¹ seed) declined gradually from 0 to 4 months across all crops; however, DL-R consistently maintained the highest counts, while C2 showed the lowest (Supplementary Tables S2, S5, S7). In groundnut, DL-R recorded the highest viability (3.15 → 2.85 Log CFU g⁻¹), followed by DL-H2, whereas C2 showed the lowest value at 4 MAS (2.50 Log CFU g⁻¹). In sesame, DL-R and DL-H2 exhibited the highest initial counts (3.12 Log CFU g⁻¹), with DL-R retaining the maximum viability at 4 MAS (2.94 Log CFU g⁻¹); C2 recorded the lowest (2.66 Log CFU g⁻¹). In soybean, DL-R showed the highest initial viability (3.12 Log CFU g⁻¹) and maintained the maximum count at 4 MAS (2.86 Log CFU g⁻¹), while C2 again showed the lowest (2.30 Log CFU g⁻¹).

Overall, DL-R provided the most stable survival of T. harzianum during storage in all three oilseed crops (Fig. 3A, C, E).

Shelf life of crop specific biofertilizers on coated seeds

The persistence of crop-specific biofertilizers—Bradyrhizobium (groundnut), Azotobacter (sesame), and Rhizobium (soybean)—also declined with storage, but DL-R consistently supported the best population retention, whereas C3 showed the lowest survival (Supplementary Tables S3, S6, S9). In groundnut, DL-R and SL3 recorded the highest initial counts (5.05 Log CFU g⁻¹), with DL-R maintaining the maximum at 4 MAS (4.69 Log CFU g⁻¹); C3 showed the minimum (4.43 Log CFU g⁻¹). In sesame, DL-R had the strongest recovery (5.07 → 4.92 Log CFU g⁻¹), followed by DL-H2, while C3 had the lowest count (4.67 Log CFU g⁻¹). In soybean, DL-R sustained the highest viability (5.06 → 4.79 Log CFU g⁻¹), whereas C3 again recorded the lowest (4.23 Log CFU g⁻¹). Thus, DL-R markedly improved the long-term survivability of beneficial microbial inoculants on coated seeds (Fig. 3B, D, F).

Efficacy comparison between double-layer and single-layer seed coating approaches

Overall, treatments involving double-layer coating with chitosan and the sequential inclusion of fungicide, T. harzianum and biofertilizer (DL-R and DL-H2) demonstrated superior microbial viability across crops. The single bioagent-only treatments (SL2, SL3, C2 and C3) showed relatively reduced viability over time, emphasizing the protective role of biopolymer matrices and strategic layer positioning of microbial inputs (Fig. 3).

Efficacy studies of different seed treatments under laboratory and green house conditions in groundnut

Germination, seedling vigour and seed rot and seedling mortality (Paper Towel Method)

Under laboratory conditions, seed treatments combining chitosan, fungicide, T. harzianum (Th4d), and Bradyrhizobium significantly enhanced seed germination and vigour in groundnut compared with the untreated control (U). The double-layer treatment DL-R achieved the highest germination (94.0%), followed by DL-H2 (92.0%) and SL1 (90.0%), whereas U recorded the lowest (64.0%). A similar pattern was observed for Vigour Index-I and Vigour Index-II, with DL-R consistently exhibiting superior seedling growth. Seed rot and seedling mortality were also markedly reduced by DL-R (10.7%), followed by DL-H2 (18.9%) and SL1 (19.7%), while U recorded the highest mortality (75.2%), confirming the protective role of combined coating components against Sclerotium rolfsii (Fig. 4; Supplementary Table 10).

Pot culture studies for seed germination, seedling vigour and stem rot incidence

Under artificial inoculation with S. rolfsii (4 g kg⁻¹ seed), all coated treatments significantly improved early growth responses. DL-R maintained the highest germination (94%), followed by DL-H2 (92%) and SL1 (90%), while the untreated control (U) showed only 62%. Seed Vigour Index-I and II also recorded highest in DL-R (3207 and 94.2), with DL-H2 next (3063 and 86.0); the lowest values were observed in untreated control (1408.4 and 43.3). Stem rot incidence was markedly reduced in coated treatments, with DL-R recording the minimum disease (11.7%), followed by DL-H2 (13.4%) and SL1 (17.9%), compared to 86.7% in U (Fig. 5; Supplementary Table 11).

Efficacy studies of different seed treatments under field conditions in groundnut

Germination and emergence

Field germination trends aligned with pot studies. DL-R showed the highest germination (91.9%), followed by DL-H2 (90.7%) and DL-H1 (85%), while U recorded the lowest (70.9%). DL-R also showed maximum emergence index (84.8%), with DL-H2 (83.3%) and DL-H1 (78.1%) performing well; U remained lowest (64.8%) (Supplementary Table 12).

Growth and nodulation

Seed treatments significantly enhanced crop growth. DL-R plants were tallest (42.8 cm), followed by DL-H2 (39.8 cm) and DL-H1 (38.2 cm), whereas U plants remained shortest (27.1 cm). DL-R also recorded the highest nodulation (279 nodules plant⁻¹; 0.274 g dry weight), followed by DL-H2 (255.6 nodules; 0.256 g). The untreated control recorded the least (125 nodules; 0.102 g). DL-R also produced the maximum number of primary branches (9.36), with U recording the minimum (6.96) (Supplementary Table 12).

Disease progression (Stem Rot)

Stem rot incidence increased with plant age, peaking at 90 DAS. DL-R consistently registered the lowest incidence (5.2%) and the minimum AUDPC (102.8). In contrast, U showed the highest incidence (29.9%) and AUDPC (774.0). DL-H2 and DL-H1 also significantly suppressed disease progression (Fig. 6B; Supplementary Table 13).

Insect infestation

DL-R exhibited the lowest pest incidence—thrips (3.2), aphids (1.8), leafhoppers (1.2)—while U recorded the maximum (6.2, 5.8, 5.0, respectively). DL-H2 ranked second across all pests (Supplementary Table 13).

Yield attributes

DL-R showed clear superiority in yield parameters. It produced the highest number of pods (40.7), followed by DL-H2 (36.2) and DL-H1 (30.5), while U recorded the least (23.2). Seed yield plant⁻¹ was highest in DL-R (22.4 g), followed by DL-H2 (19.22 g) and DL-H1 (18.74 g), whereas U yielded only 10.84 g. Test weight was maximum in DL-R (38.5 g) and minimum in U (23.7 g).

At the crop level, DL-R recorded the highest seed yield (21.1 q ha⁻¹), closely followed by DL-H2 (20.9 q ha⁻¹), while U remained lowest (12.9 q ha⁻¹). Similar trends were observed for haulm yield, highest in DL-R (4760 kg ha⁻¹) and lowest in U (2865.5 kg ha⁻¹) (Supplementary Table 14).

Economic analysis

Double-layer treatments were economically superior. DL-R recorded the highest B: C ratio (1:2.41), followed by DL-H2 (1:2.40) and SL2 (1:2.39). The untreated control showed the lowest profitability (1:1.64), reaffirming the advantage of double-layer biopolymer coatings integrating fungicide, chitosan and bioagents under field conditions (Supplementary Table 14).

Principal component analysis (PCA) of agronomic and pest-related traits

Principal component analysis was conducted to explore multivariate relationships among yield-related, nodulation, emergence, and pest incidence parameters across treatments. The first principal component (PC1) accounted for the majority of the total variance (86.3%), while the second principal component (PC2) explained an additional 8.8%. Together, these two components captured over 95% of the total variation, justifying the use of PC1 and PC2 for describing and visualizing the overall data structure (Fig. 6C-F).

Variables such as yield per hectare (YpH), disease reduction percentage (DRP), field emergence index (FEI), germination percentage (GerP), haulm yield (HY), and number of nodules per plant (NpP) clustered together and pointed in the same direction on the biplot, indicating strong positive correlations with each other and with PC1. Longer vectors—particularly YpH and DRP—had a stronger influence on the principal components, with vectors pointing to the right on Dim1 (PC1) confirming their strong contribution to the first axis of variation. In contrast, sucking pest population per plant (SPPp) was oriented in the opposite direction (left), indicating a negative association with the primary group of beneficial traits and with PC1.

Sample distribution patterns reflected these associations: treatments plotted to the right of the biplot DL-R(5) and DL-H2(7) were characterized by high yield, greater nodulation, improved emergence, and reduced disease incidence. Conversely, treatments positioned to the left U(1), C3(10) and SL3(4) were associated with higher pest populations and lower values for beneficial agronomic traits. These results highlight the contrasting performance profiles of treatments, with DL-R consistently aligning with favourable trait clusters and the untreated control clustering with pest-dominated, low-performance profiles.

Biochemical analysis

The biochemical analysis revealed that seed coating treatments significantly influenced oil content, protein percentage, and fatty acid composition in groundnut seeds (Table 2). The highest oil content (52.85%) was recorded in treatment DL-R (DL-R: Chitosan + Fungicide + Th4d + Bradyrhizobium), followed by DL-H2 (49.46%) and SL3 (49.22%), indicating the positive impact of combined biopolymer and microbial treatments on oil biosynthesis. The untreated control (U) recorded the lowest oil content (46.83%). However, the variation was statistically non-significant (CD₀.₀₅ = NS).

Protein content varied significantly among treatments (CD₀.₀₅ = 2.223). The maximum protein was recorded in DL-R (23.6%), closely followed by DL-H1 and DL-H2 (23.4%), while the control (U) had the lowest protein content (20.1%). The enhancement in protein levels may be attributed to improved nutrient assimilation and rhizobial activity due to effective seed coating. The oleic acid content was highest in DL-R (46.71%), DL-H1 (46.78%), and SL3 (45.96%), indicating better lipid quality. A corresponding decrease in linoleic acid was noted in these treatments, with the lowest linoleic acid content in DL-R (34.74%) and DL-H1 (35.09%). Palmitic and stearic acid levels remained relatively stable across treatments, while linolenic acid content showed slight variation, with the lowest in DL-R (0.03%).

Discussion

The findings of the present study provide compelling evidence that biopolymer-based double-layer seed coating—comprising chitosan, fungicide, and beneficial microbes—plays a pivotal role in maintaining seed quality and improving crop performance under both storage and field conditions. A gradual decline in seed germination across all treatments over a four-month storage period was evident, primarily due to seed ageing, leading to depletion of stored reserves and reduced embryonic enzymatic activity. Temperature fluctuations during storage may have further accelerated this deterioration process, as similarly reported by Dheeraj et al.25,, Xing et al.26, and Rao et al.27,.

Notably, double-layer treatments, particularly DL-R and DL-H2 effectively delayed germination loss and preserved seed viability. The chitosan polymer likely formed a semi-permeable protective barrier, insulating seeds from microbial invasion and environmental stress while interacting synergistically with fungicides and bioagents. These findings are consistent with previous reports in groundnut and corn28; Vijay, 2024), corn29 and paddy30, where polymer-coated seeds exhibited higher germination rates and fewer abnormalities than untreated controls.

Enhanced seed vigour observed in coated treatments was a function of improved germination, seedling growth, and biomass accumulation. Bioagents such as T. harzianum release auxins, siderophores and organic acids that stimulate root branching, water uptake and cell elongation. Bradyrhizobium further contributes by supplying early nitrogen through rapid nodulation, accelerating chlorophyll synthesis and shoot growth. This aligns with earlier studies in soybean and legume crops where microbial inoculation and seed coating enhanced seedling vigour and nodulation31,32. Conversely, prolonged storage typically reduces vigour due to cumulative ageing stress (Kravic et al.,202533,34;, but in this study, the coating system effectively mitigated those effects.

Importantly, microbial viability during storage was better maintained in coated seeds. T. harzianum(Th4d) populations declined over time, but the double-layered treatments (DL-R and DL-H2) retained higher CFU counts compared to single-microbe treatments. The biopolymeric matrix seemingly supported microbial persistence by delaying desiccation and spore degradation—an observation in line with findings by Malavathu et al.35,, and by Prasad et al.14,, Akhil36and Jagadeesh et al.37, in pigeonpea, who reported extended shelf life of microbes in chitosan-PEG based formulations. The storage duration evaluated in this study (four months) represents short-term performance under ambient conditions. Extended storage evaluations across multiple crops and diverse environmental conditions are currently underway to further establish shelf-life stability and assess the broader applicability and commercial feasibility of the technology.

Bradyrhizobiumsp. survival also followed this trend. Despite natural declines due to nutrient depletion and physical breakdown of the biopolymer, double-layer treatments offered better protection and shelf life. This corroborates earlier reports by Kelly et al.38,, Akhil36, and Jagadeesh et al.37, indicating enhanced rhizobial persistence in coated legume seeds. Additionally, layered separation of bioagents and fungicides in the double-layer system likely minimized antagonistic interactions.

Field trials further reinforced the advantages of this technology. The double-layer treatment DL-R consistently outperformed others in germination, emergence, nodulation, plant height, and yield-related traits. These improvements are likely attributable to the controlled release of fungicide and prolonged microbial activity, which together enhanced nutrient uptake and suppressed Sclerotium rolfsii. Similar synergistic effects of polymer coatings combined with Trichoderma and rhizobial inoculants have been reported in groundnut, demonstrating improved disease suppression and yield enhancement38–40.

Reduction in disease incidence and pest pressure in the coated treatments further highlights the multifaceted benefit of this technology. Chitosan’s inherent antimicrobial and insect-repellent properties, combined with biocontrol agents, provided extended protection beyond the seedling phase. These results resonate with prior findings by Ganesan et al.39,, Singh and Singh40, and Arasu (2025), who emphasized the role of polymeric seed coatings in disease and pest management. These findings suggest that double-layer seed coatings, particularly DL-R can enhance oil and protein biosynthesis and favorably modulate the fatty acid profile in groundnut, possibly through improved microbial symbiosis and stress mitigation.

Lastly, the observed improvements in yield, pod number, seed weight, and benefit-cost ratio particularly under DL-R treatment demonstrate the economic viability of double-layer seed coating technology. Such advancements align with modern agricultural goals for climate-resilient, input-efficient, and sustainable crop production systems. The integration of biopolymer-based coatings with pest and nutrient management strategies represents a promising pathway toward environmentally friendly and economically sustainable groundnut cultivation.

While detailed physicochemical characterization and compatibility analyses were established in our earlier work22, the present study provides functional and biological validation of those mechanistic principles under storage, greenhouse, and field conditions. The superior performance of the double-layer system—reflected in enhanced microbial viability, reduced disease incidence, and improved yield—supports the role of spatial separation and controlled input delivery as key mechanistic drivers.

Thus, the findings bridge material-level design with biological outcomes, offering practical evidence for the functioning of biopolymer-mediated layered delivery systems. Further integration of advanced physicochemical analyses with long-term field validation would strengthen the mechanistic–translational continuum and support regulatory adoption.

The present study is based on a single location and season, and microbial assessment relied on CFU-based enumeration, which may not fully capture functional microbial activity. To address these aspects, multi-location and multi-season trials across different agro-climatic regions are currently in progress to establish broader applicability and performance stability.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrates that biopolymer-based double-layer seed coating represents a transformative advancement in seed enhancement technologies for oilseed production. By integrating chitosan, fungicides, and beneficial bioagents into a spatially structured delivery system, this approach not only improves immediate seed performance but also establishes a foundation for next-generation smart seed systems. The superior performance of the DL-R treatment highlights the potential of precision-designed coatings to simultaneously enhance microbial viability, strengthen plant resilience against biotic stresses, and optimize crop productivity under diverse agroecological conditions. Looking ahead, such multilayered coating platforms can be further engineered to incorporate targeted nutrient delivery, stress-responsive bioactives, and climate-resilient microbial consortia, paving the way for intelligent and adaptive seed technologies. The scalability and compatibility of this approach with existing seed processing infrastructure make it highly promising for large-scale adoption. Ultimately, the integration of these advanced coating systems into mainstream agriculture could play a crucial role in achieving sustainable intensification, reducing dependency on chemical inputs, and ensuring resilient oilseed production in the face of climate variability and evolving pest–pathogen dynamics.
