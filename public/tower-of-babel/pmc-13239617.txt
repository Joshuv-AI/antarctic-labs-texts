Access to Healthcare in Thailand During the COVID‐19 Pandemic: A Qualitative Interview Study of Migrants From Myanmar
Authors: Sarah H. Oung, Boram Kim, David Tanoko, Chengshi Shiu, Thin Nyein Nyein Aung, Saiyud Moolphate, Myo Nyein Aung, Min San Tun, Sai Htun Lin, Khin Moe Myint, Khine Myint Oo, Shu‐Sheng Lai, Rachel H. A. Arbing, Wei‐Ti Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAims and ObjectivesTo explore how the military coup, COVID‐19, and limited healthcare access affected the health and well‐being of Myanmar migrants.BackgroundMyanmar is among the poorest countries in Southeast Asia. The combined effects of COVID‐19, political instability, and civil conflict have created a humanitarian crisis, resulting in migrants having severely restricted access to food, water, healthcare, and basic rights.DesignA qualitative interview study.MethodsIn‐depth interviews were conducted with a purposive sample of 32 Myanmar migrants living in Thailand (M = 38.5 years, SD = 11.6). The study followed COREQ reporting guidelines.ResultsParticipants described their experiences with COVID‐19 prevention measures amid uncertainty, reporting substantial psychological distress and high levels of stress. Religion—particularly Buddhism—served as a central coping mechanism, helping them manage fear and emotional challenges. Many emphasised maintaining their cultural identity, expressed limited interest in acculturating to Thai society, and hoped to return to Myanmar once conditions stabilised. Some younger migrants sought support from local organisations for COVID‐19 information and resources. Overall, participants relied on personal resilience, spirituality, and informal support networks to manage their health and well‐being.ConclusionsMyanmar migrants often depend on spiritual beliefs, self‐reliance, and community connections to cope with adversity. Culturally sensitive interventions are needed to improve access to healthcare and support migrants experiencing ongoing instability, high levels of stress, and limited formal resources.Relevance to Clinical PracticeWithin the context of patient care, the findings contribute new knowledge on the status of Myanmar migrants who are facing intersecting stressors in resettlement areas of Thailand. Limited access to resources for migrants poses significant barriers to seeking professional medical help. More insight into why Myanmar migrants do not seek help despite experiencing stress and having knowledge of available resources in Thailand is needed.Patient or Public ContributionMembers of the migrant community were interviewed, and community advocates were involved in the study.Positionality StatementThe research team, with backgrounds in social work, counselling, public health, and nursing and with experience working with Southeast Asian migrant communities, recognised that this familiarity may shape interpretation. To minimise bias, we engaged in reflexive journaling, discussed assumptions in team meetings, and bracketed personal perspectives during data collection and analysis. As the researchers held no authority over the participants, the interviews emphasised respect, active listening, and minimised power differentials. These practices helped ensure that the findings reflected participants' voices rather than researchers' expectations.

Introduction

Myanmar has a difficult history that has affected its economic growth. Owing to instability within the government and the frequency of natural disasters such as cyclones, Myanmar has one of the lowest GDPs in Southeast Asia at $62.26 billion (USD) in 2022 (The World Bank 2025). From 2011 to 2019, Myanmar experienced high economic growth under the transitional military government known as the Aung San administration. Despite economic growth, Myanmar spent only 2% of its GDP on healthcare, and people were driven into poverty to afford it (Khaing et al. 2015). Since people cannot afford healthcare, it became easier for them to avoid debt by avoiding care altogether. For those who did access healthcare, many paid for it—the World Health Organization reported that 74% of healthcare spending came from out‐of‐pocket payments, and only 20% came from public spending (Myint et al. 2019). Compounded by years of low public expenditure on healthcare, the people of Myanmar have limited access to healthcare.

In 2020, the COVID‐19 pandemic struck, and the economy shrunk. From 2020 to 2023, government spending on health in Myanmar fell from 3.6% to 1.8% of GDP, and the people of Myanmar were further limited in terms of healthcare access (The World Bank 2025). To make matters worse, in February 2021, the Myanmar military staged a coup d'état that was followed by mass killings, torture, and arbitrary arrests against the people by junta security forces (Human Rights Watch 2023). In addition to physical harm, freedom of speech and assembly remained under restriction (Human Rights Watch 2023). The coup in Myanmar has led to the internal displacement of almost 1.5 million people, and an additional 70,000 people have sought refuge in neighbouring countries such as Thailand, where they stay in border towns such as Mae Sot (Human Rights Watch 2023). The mass influx of migrants has caused a crisis in Thailand, where migrants have limited access to food, water, and rights (Human Rights Watch 2023). Although it is common to see the terms “refugees” and “migrants” used incorrectly as synonyms, refugees are specifically all persons who are outside their country of origin for reasons of violence, persecution, or conflict. As a result, they require international protection and are covered by international law (Forte et al. 2018). To align the language that has been used in Thailand, we use “migrants” throughout this paper.

Background

Although effective tools are currently available for treating mental and physical illnesses, the accessibility of care remains a main challenge for both migrants and healthcare professionals (Due et al. 2020). For migrants, depleted social support in resettlements (Bellamy et al. 2015); navigation through unfamiliar health systems in new environments (Kasper et al. 2022); difficulty communicating and maintaining trusting relationships with care providers (Robertshaw et al. 2017); insufficient knowledge and skills about their illness, symptoms and self‐management (Shahin et al. 2021); and stigma all contribute to their suboptimal access to and engagement in care. On the other hand, barriers for healthcare professionals in delivering accessible care include inadequate training and guidance, poor cultural competency, subpar skills in communicating with migrants (Parajuli and Horey 2019), time constraints, and a shortage of organisational support, resources, and capacity (Robertshaw et al. 2017). Consequently, migrants suffer from more chronic conditions and fail to manage their chronic illnesses (Shahin et al. 2021). To overcome these limitations, different models have been explored and tested, with a handful of promising approaches, including multidisciplinary care teams, the use of interpreters or bilingual staff, cultural sensitivity training, no‐ or low‐cost services, outreach services, free transportation, longer clinic hours, care coordination, and patient advocacy (Joshi et al. 2013).

Currently, little is known about the health and well‐being of migrants, as well as the care available to them, in resettlements in low‐ and middle‐income countries. Most studies of migrants are conducted in resettlements in high‐income countries with established healthcare infrastructure in place, such as Canada, the United States, and EU countries. Given that most internationally displaced migrants seek asylum in nearby countries, the lack of understanding of the well‐being of migrants has prevented researchers from developing evidence‐based services for migrants from resource‐limited countries. In particular, insight into how traumatic geopolitics and COVID‐19 may have affected migrants' health and how health service providers may respond more effectively to their needs, while respecting local or cultural beliefs, is critical. The framework of culturally competent compassion is especially warranted for use in this population. The findings from this study will inform the future development of interventions to strengthen healthcare delivery infrastructure to better serve vulnerable populations under unstable political conditions during a public health crisis.

In this study, we aimed to focus on Myanmar migrants' access to healthcare resources in a resettlement area of Thailand during the COVID‐19 pandemic and how they coped with the discomfort they experienced while living as migrants. This research explored how Myanmar migrants experienced both direct and indirect impacts of the military coup and COVID‐19 in terms of their health and well‐being, providing a critical assessment of their healthcare needs while living along the Thailand‐Myanmar border. To our knowledge, few studies have explored how intersecting life stressors among migrants from Myanmar affect their health, well‐being, and access to healthcare near resettlement areas in Thailand. The perspectives taken in this paper include those from researchers and community collaborators in Thailand and Myanmar as well as from academics living in Thailand, Taiwan, Japan, and the United States.

Methods

Design

A purposive sampling technique was used to identify potential participants for this qualitative interview study. Semi‐structured in‐depth individual interviews were conducted with 32 migrants from Myanmar who were residing in or around a resettlement area along the Thailand‐Myanmar border. The study adhered to the reporting guidelines of the consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative studies (COREQ) (see File S1).

Theoretical Framework

Informed by the framework of culturally competent compassion (Apostolara et al. 2020), our study guides nurses to consider the suffering of others when they consider how their practice is perceived by migrant patients. Nurses in different countries understand and provide compassion in practice differently; leaders can create and uphold compassionate working environments only through understanding the notions of health and illness in the context of a patient's cultural background and life experiences.

Study Setting and Recruitment

From September to October 2022, 32 study participants were recruited from Thailand‐Myanmar migrant community schools and from a healthcare facility in Mae Sot, Tak Province, Thailand. Wana Zaw, an onsite collaborator, facilitated the identification of eligible participants who expressed a willingness to volunteer for the study. Migrant schoolteachers and individuals receiving care in Mae Sot and nearby areas were approached by fluent Burmese researchers, who shared their backgrounds and reasons for doing the research. The researchers who collected the data were not involved in employment or the provision of care to the migrants, thereby reducing the likelihood of coercion.

Inclusion and/or Exclusion Criteria

The eligibility criteria included being over 18 years old, residing on the Thailand‐Myanmar border, arriving in Thailand after 1 February 2021 (after the military coup), from Myanmar, and being able to understand and agree to participate in the study activities. The exclusion criteria were being less than 18 years old, not willing to participate in the study, and not being a migrant from Myanmar.

Data Collection/Measures

The study involved a single one‐on‐one in‐depth interview with each participant. Owing to Myanmar's conservative culture, a female researcher conducted the 19 female interviews, and a male researcher conducted the 13 male participants' interviews. One‐on‐one in‐depth interviews were conducted in Burmese, and the data were audio recorded for transcription. Each session, which lasted between 60 and 90 min, took place in a private setting. All the audio recordings were subsequently transcribed verbatim into Burmese, and certain representative quotations were translated into English for publication.

A semi‐structured interview guide was developed based on a review of the literature, input from community partners, and the research team's prior experience working with Myanmar populations. The interview discussion guide included open‐ended questions. The guide was reviewed and validated by two Burmese qualitative research experts and one community specialist to ensure cultural appropriateness, clarity, and content relevance. To ensure the credibility of the in‐depth interview questions, the interview guide was also pilot tested with two migrants. On the basis of the feedback and insights gathered, the interview guides were subsequently revised to better align with the cultural and contextual experiences of Myanmar migrants in Thailand (see Appendix S1).

The in‐depth interviews began with specific prompts, such as asking participants about their decision to leave Myanmar, their journey to their current location, their reasons for staying, their experiences during the COVID‐19 pandemic, their families in Myanmar postcoup, their planned duration of stay, and the measures taken to protect themselves and their families during the COVID‐19 pandemic.

Data Analysis

Inductive analysis of the in‐depth data was performed and the data were analysed by Atlas.ti (Scientific Software Development Version 8.0, 2019). The data were systematically coded to identify concept groupings, and code trees were used to establish relationships between health and migration processes (Hsieh and Shannon 2005). Three researchers individually reviewed the transcriptions and assigned codes based on predefined study themes. To ensure coding reliability, two transcripts were randomly selected for comparison, and any uncertainties or discrepancies in coding were resolved by the research team, contributing to the confirmability of the analysis. Descriptive quotations related to religion, acculturation, mental health, and local COVID‐19 policies were extracted from the transcriptions. To ensure its accuracy, external experts familiar with Myanmar's culture and migrant health issues along the Thailand‐Myanmar border reviewed the data collection, thematic process, and study findings. Data saturation was confirmed through the replication of information obtained from study participants (Guest et al. 2006).

Rigour and Reflexivity

Several steps were taken to ensure the quality of the methods used in this study. The interview questions were checked for clarity by members of the international team, and problems with translation were resolved. Moreover, the interview guide was piloted before being used in the study. Methodological rigour in the analysis was strengthened by involving several members of the research team in coding the raw data from two countries (Thailand and the United States) and by engaging in regular team discussions. To maintain neutrality during data collection and transcription, the research team used standardised interview procedures, reflexive journaling, and regular debriefing to minimise personal bias and ensure that participants' narratives were captured authentically. Transcriptions were completed verbatim, and cross‐checks by multiple Burmese team members helped prevent subjective interpretation.

Results

Thematic Analysis

Four major themes emerged from the interviews: COVID‐19 prevention measures, high levels of stress (mental health as a migrant in Thailand), self‐reliance and the value of religion as a coping mechanism, and a lack of acculturation.

Theme 1: COVID‐19 Preventative Measures

Since the coup d'état erupted while there was still a worldwide pandemic, COVID‐19 remained a prevalent issue among migrants during the study. By the summer of 2022, 22 out of the 32 participants had not been infected with COVID‐19 or did not know that they had been infected. One 31‐year‐old male teacher stated, “I did not get infected with COVID‐19… I did not experience much effect.” Many participants had similar experiences.

Among those who were infected and those who were not, only 6.25% (n = 2) of the participants used the free (a local healthcare facility) for their health care. One 35‐year‐old female farmer stated, “At (a local healthcare facility) where I met Dr. Ti Ti, she made contact with Burma Children Medical Fund (BCMF) and then referred me to (a local healthcare facility). I got treatment there. I could only go to the hospital with the support of (a local healthcare facility).” Another 34‐year‐old female teacher explained that she “got training from (a local healthcare facility) and Migrant Educational Coordination Center” on health education but did not actively seek health care from there.

Despite not actively seeking care, migrants in the border town of Mae Sot received help from organisations for COVID‐19 prevention. A 43‐year‐old female teacher revealed that they “received masks and hand gels… from Migrant Educational Coordination Center and BCMF”. Another explained that they “got supplies such as masks, hand gels, and soaps. We were afraid of COVID at first. Some people got financial support from organisations including teachers.” While they did not seek help, organisations educated them on prevention and provided supplies. A 35‐year‐old female teacher stated that “the healthcare personnel came to test us and we received COVID vaccination.” The organisations did not reach everyone, so this information spread through word‐of‐mouth throughout the migrant community. A 31‐year‐old male teacher stated, “The prevention materials were donated to our school. We then educated the villagers and the students. We trained the students. They again shared their knowledge with their families.”

In addition to medically advised prevention and protection methods against COVID‐19, many participants used herbal remedies. A 54‐year‐old construction worker spoke about how they “drank boiled water, practiced social distancing, and we used some traditional remedies.” Boiled water was supposed to help “kill” the spread of germs before consumption. Two participants (a 31‐year‐old male teacher and a 40‐year‐old male construction worker) mentioned boiling “Desmodium triquetrum DC,” a leaf with purported medicinal properties, to prevent COVID‐19 infection. It is seemingly a common plant in the village since one person explained how “People told [them] to take paracetamol and use home remedies such as Desmodium triquetrum DC.” In addition, a 19‐year‐old unemployed female participant stated, “I followed the traditional medicines found online. I also used turmeric as a home remedy.” Herbal remedies were shared through word‐of‐mouth and the internet, which made them a popular option among these individuals. Many participants used these herbal remedies and rituals as preventative measures against COVID‐19. As a 40‐year‐old male construction worker explained, “I let them take medicines and home remedies. And I also did some rituals such as lighting candles on the scissor. It was to overcome the pandemic.”

Theme 2: High Levels of Stress (Mental Health as a Migrant in Thailand)

Fleeing Myanmar to Thailand led migrants to experience high amounts of stress. All of them experienced stress from fleeing their homeland and being separated from their families. When they escaped Myanmar, many migrants travelled alone into a new foreign country. One 32‐year‐old male teacher explained, “I was alone when I arrived here. I did not have any friends. I have experienced stress, anxiety, and depression, but I tried to catch every opportunity I had.” Leaving their family behind in the violence added to the stress of being in a new country. One 26‐year‐old male teacher said, “It stressed me out… I did not want to come [to Thailand] as I had to leave my children.” Another 31‐year‐old male teacher said, “There was one situation in Myeik. The house of the people who were accused of label [sic] [being] a rebel against the army was shut down for no reason and the house owner's family was forcefully moved out… I am worried about my family.” Many participants talked about the dangerous conditions back in Myanmar, such as how “killing people is as easy as tearing paper for [the military government].” The 27‐year‐old female teacher continues to explain how they do not feel safe even in Thailand: “Even if we don't get arrested, we might be in danger. We are not safe. If the Thai government won't take care of us, we will be in great danger.”

In addition to violence, the COVID‐19 pandemic contributed to the stress faced by migrants when they travelled from Myanmar to Thailand. One 44‐year‐old female teacher explained, “It was hard to cross the border. If we went back home, we could not be returned easily. We had to do quarantine. The rules were strict before and people from Myanmar were not allowed to enter Thailand.” Transportation was affected by the pandemic, and a teacher described, “We could not go outside due to COVID and had to stay at home with limited food. Some villages were locked down. We could not go outside freely. Schools were closed and we had to go home to visit.” COVID‐19 also affected the job market in Thailand. One 40‐year‐old male construction worker commented that, “during COVID, I could not go outside and work. We were not allowed to visit nearby homes too. Job opportunity was rare.” Another 43‐year‐old male mason worker stated, “Before COVID, it was easy to get jobs. During COVID, we could not travel from place to place. We had restrictions. Our income and social life were impaired. We could not earn enough. After COVID, our lives become harder. Before that, my wife and I both worked and we were more than enough to support our livelihoods. But after COVID, we have to struggle a lot.” Those who knew they were infected with COVID‐19 had to deal with the stress of being ill with limited access to healthcare. A 32‐year‐old male teacher stated, “Some of my co‐workers passed away due to COVID. I was shocked. When I got omicron, I suffered from fever, cough and sputum, and headache.” A 31‐year‐old female teacher shared, “Public and private hospitals did not accept COVID patients… No hospital accepted patients with fever,” making it difficult for infected people to seek treatment.

Theme 3: Self‐Reliance and the Value of Religion as a Coping Mechanism

Despite the high levels of stress, the migrants did not seek help but instead relied on themselves. A 27‐year‐old female teacher clarified, “There are free online services such as Counseling Myanmar and paid services you can consult but I don't go there. I manage by myself.” Another 31‐year‐old male teacher revealed, “I don't want to share my stress with other people. I don't want other people to feel bad for me so I tend not to seek for [sic] help.” Another said, “I have made up my mind to overcome any situation I faced.” One 27‐year‐old female teacher even claimed to overcome disease through self‐empowerment. They claimed, “Mind power is the main point to overcome COVID…Health is depending upon our mind. If we decide to overcome the disease, we can make it.”

Religion played an essential role in overcoming challenges, and 29 out of the 32 participants identified as Buddhists. One 20‐year‐old female teacher was injured and explained how she used religion to overcome her difficulties. She disclosed, “I consulted with the doctor about my wound…As I am a Buddhist, I try to follow instructions. I pray to Buddha. I listen to the Buddha's teaching…Even if we have to illegally live here, at least we can survive compared to living in Myanmar with fear.” Another 32‐year‐old male teacher reported, “I sometimes listen to the Buddha's teaching. I don't drink. I try to stay calm.” Trying to make the best out of the situation, one 31‐year‐old male teacher recalled, “I listened to music or talked with my friends. I used to listen to music a lot and I listened to Buddha teaching at night.” Two participants focused their attention on what they could do for others. One 43‐year‐old male day laborer participant added, “I pay more attention to religion…According to our trust, it was due to karma. So, I try to do more good things.” One 31‐year‐old male teacher agreed, “I meditate, recite Patthana (Pa Htan), and pray for the well‐being of others. I pray that every living is free from the pain that I was suffering, I pray that there comes a time when we can live COVID free, I pray that justice will prevail and may righteousness win.”

Theme 4: Lack of Acculturation

The majority of the participants did not actively make an effort to assimilate into Thailand. Most of the participants did not speak the Thai language. One 20‐year‐old female teacher admitted, “The transportation and language barrier was the main issues for me. I could not understand their language at first. It was hard to communicate.” Another 41‐year‐old female teacher added, “I don't feel stressed easily. The one thing I encountered was the language barrier. At first, I did not understand their language much, and I had difficulty communicating with the owners. Some kind owners understood me and helped me.” In contrast, one migrant claimed that the “Thai owner and their wives tried to harm me when I could not understand their language.” Only one 44‐year‐old female teacher admitted, “My children have to transfer to a Thai School. At first, they went to a migrant school. If we were to stay in Thailand, they should learn the Thai language and education.”

The vast majority of the participants wanted to return to Myanmar once the country was stable. Many participants still referred to Myanmar as home. One 26‐year‐old male teacher stated, “I know it is just temporary. I believe that our country will be peaceful soon. It will end soon and our lives will become better.” One 68‐year‐old male farmer said, “I don't have the plan to move out to another place. I will go back home when the country becomes peaceful.” Many have family members back in Myanmar and held hope to reunite with them. One 48‐year‐old male farmer expressed, “If I were to become financially stable and my country is peaceful, I want to go back to my hometown and work there. I am saving as much as I can. I am trying and I miss my mother too.” One 47‐year‐old male farmer asserted, “When the uprising is over and my country is peaceful, I want to go back to my hometown and live there. I love my place. I will never give up. I can drive a car and earn my living there. I want to live closely with my family and relatives. I don't want to live in a foreign country for so long. I will go back one day. I have decided it.”

Discussion

In this study, we explored the issues that Myanmar migrants faced while living in Mae Sot, a border town between Thailand and Myanmar. The study participants shared their experiences with COVID‐19 prevention measures during the pandemic, described a mental health crisis with high levels of stress, shared how they valued their religion, did not want to acculturate to Thai society, and planned to return to Myanmar when the civil war ended.

Since the migrants did not have regular access to COVID‐19 testing through either a public clinic or at‐home test, they could have had COVID‐19 and did not know it, as evidenced by 22 of 32 participants stating that they did not know whether or not they had a COVID‐19 infection during the pandemic. Twenty‐four participants were uninsured, making the free Wana Zaw one of their only options for health care. Various problems acted as barriers to seeking healthcare, such as long travel times to clinics or the dominant belief in self‐reliance. More than one‐third of the study participants (n = 13) reported that their travel time to a clinic or hospital was between 1 and 3 h. Most (n = 20) reported that they did not have enough money to pay for health care. While educational organisations provided no‐cost preventative supplies to the teachers in the study sample, there may not have been enough for everyone to be fully protected, and other professionals did not reap these benefits.

With limited access to resources, migrants tended to rely mostly on themselves for physical and mental health treatment. Although none of the participants explicitly stated how boiling Desmodium triquetrum DC helps prevent COVID‐19, these leaves have been used throughout Asia as a treatment for colds, lung abscesses, rheumatic arthritis, and more (Tang et al. 2022). Boiling leaves and making tea are the most popular methods for consuming DC because of its medicinal properties (Thandar and Tun 2015). DC is reported to have a bitter taste and cooling properties, and it detoxifies, destroys phlegm, and excretes parasites from the body (Tang et al. 2022). A study reported that numerous traditional applications and pharmacological investigations have demonstrated a wide range of biological effects from different extracts of DC and its metabolites. These include, but are not limited to, anti‐inflammatory, anti‐microbial, anti‐hepatitis B virus, and anti‐hepatotoxicity effects as well as insecticidal properties (Tang et al. 2022).

In a similar example of resiliency, during the pandemic, a migrant camp in Kenya resorted to making their own soap and handwashing stations (Böhme and Schmitz 2022). The Kakuna camp was established in Kenya in 1992, and as of July 2020, it housed 196,666 refugees from more than 20 countries (Böhme and Schmitz 2022). During the pandemic, groups within the camp, such as the Kakuma News Reflector (KANERE), used multiple languages to inform residents about the virus and prevention measures by driving around the camp with loudspeakers (Böhme and Schmitz 2022). Another organisation called Dream Magical Studio mobilised the camp's entrepreneurial capacities by using camp tailors and designers to produce masks, soaps, and sanitisers for residents and frontline workers (Böhme and Schmitz 2022). Kakuma refugees demonstrated communal self‐organisation, leveraged local skills and leadership, and tapped into strong intracamp and transnational connections. Migrants/refugees in both Thailand and Kenya demonstrated self‐reliance in the face of adversity by taking COVID‐19 prevention into their own hands; both used herbal remedies as a technique for COVID‐19 prevention and word‐of‐mouth to disseminate preventive information.

One of the driving factors of poor help‐seeking behaviours for mental health support may be the lack of training for mental health staff within Myanmar. According to the United States Institute of Peace, only 1.4% of Myanmar's healthcare spending went toward mental health in 2019.11 Less than $0.05 was spent per person, supporting only 117 psychiatrists and 49 social workers nationwide (Sarmiento et al. 2024). With few resources available for mental health, mental health facilities cannot fully meet the needs of the entire population in Myanmar. The country has a population of 54 million people, but there are only two mental health hospitals in Myanmar with eight trained psychologists (Artingstoll 2023). In addition, the 1912 Lunacy Act discouraged people from seeking help. The legislation uses outdated language to describe how the government must provide funding only for the housing and care of in‐house “lunatics” (Government of Burma 1912). Nevertheless, it emphasises keeping these “dangerous” individuals from society rather than promoting recovery and rehabilitation. Given the mental health care system in Myanmar, it is not surprising that migrants are unlikely to seek help for their feelings. Only one participant was aware of Counselling Myanmar, a free counselling service; however, they did not use the service. Having a historically inadequate mental health system in Myanmar creates a self‐reliant population that may refuse to seek help. Self‐sufficiency is reinforced in the context of the myriad of challenges that migrants face in host countries, including no or reduced access to healthcare.

Instead of seeking professional help, many migrants talked among themselves to address their daily stressors. While peer support is not equivalent to professional help, many studies have shown positive effects of peer support on migrant populations. A scoping review revealed that peer support is among the most vital stress‐reducing strategies (Ho et al. 2022). These mutually beneficial relationships have the potential to help relieve traumatic experiences (Ho et al. 2022). In addition, these relationships can provide informal information about shelters, job opportunities, and COVID‐19 protective gear (Ho et al. 2022). As seen in this study, some participants were told of herbal remedies through word‐of‐mouth. A focus group study with mental health workers revealed a beneficial influence on an individual's journey toward recovery through the exchange of personal experiences and positive role modelling (Krumm et al. 2022). Peer support facilitates a more receptive environment for receiving help (Krumm et al. 2022). Overall, peer support can positively influence migrants' mental health and stress levels.

Migrants also turned to religion as a coping mechanism to address the high levels of stress that they experienced. Twenty‐nine out of the thirty‐two participants identified themselves as Buddhists. The fundamental belief of Buddhism is that life is an endless cycle of rebirth and that suffering stops only once Nirvana is achieved (National Geographic Society, n.d.). Nirvana represents enlightenment and can be achieved through physical and spiritual labor, meditation, and good behaviour (National Geographic Society, n.d.). This could also explain why these migrants would rather rely on themselves and their religion than seek professional help. Since life is full of suffering, they may not see the trauma from leaving a war‐torn country as a justifiable reason to seek help. Such challenges can be seen as one step to moving closer toward Nirvana. Many participants stated that they relied on their Buddhist teachings to get them through difficult times and used meditation, a common practice among Buddhists, to cope. Musical outlets were also used for comfort.

Despite coming from a nation torn apart by war, the majority of the migrants reported that they wanted to return home to Myanmar when things settled. Myanmar has been a politically unstable country for the last 60 years. After British colonial rule, this country lived under military rule until the parliamentary government was established, which lasted nearly 14 years (Maizland 2022). In 1962, a military coup under General Ne Win overtook the government and ruled for 26 years (Maizland 2022). Under Ne Win's government, there was a spike in corruption, drastic shifts in economic policy, and food shortages that led to mass protests. In 1988, the government retaliated against the protesters, which led to mass killings and displacements. Ne Win resigned, and a new military regime took charge and held complete power until 2007 (Maizland 2022). The Saffron Revolution, a series of anti‐government protests, forced the military junta to loosen its control until it dissolved in 2011. After that, a military‐dominated civilian parliament came to power and appointed Prime Minister Thein Sein (Maizland 2022). Sein advanced democratic reforms, and in 2015, the first multiparty democratic election led to the National League for Democracy taking power under the leadership of Aung San Suu Kyi. The most recent military coup, which saw Aung San Suu Kyi jailed, occurred in February 2021 (Maizland 2022).

Even with the long history of government instability, the migrants in this study hoped to return to Myanmar once the violence settled. However, not only does migrating to a new country cause significant stress to migrants, but stress is also felt by those remaining in Myanmar. A study conducted in October 2021 (9 months after the coup) revealed that 60.71% of the survey population in Myanmar had a probable combination of anxiety and depression, and although the study did not represent the entire population, the sample size of 7720 Burmese adults suggested that poor mental health is prevalent in Myanmar (Saw et al. 2023). The stress caused by the coup seemed not to deter the study participants' desire to return to their home country to reunite with family and resume their old way of life; this ideal served as a potential explanation for the limited desire to acculturate to the Thai culture that they reported. Only one participant mentioned sending her children to a Thai school to learn the language; however, she did not mention her own language training. Indeed, several participants complained about the language barrier in Thailand but did not talk about learning Thai to help them overcome it. A systematic review concerning suicidal behaviours among immigrants and ethnic minorities revealed that risk factors for suicidal behaviours included separation from family, worrying about family members who remained behind, and language barriers (Forte et al. 2018). While this study did not screen for suicidality, it would follow that participants were at heightened risk. Studies on acculturation and its impact on health risk behaviours and inequalities remain scarce in migration settings within Asian countries (Aung et al. 2020). Assimilation occurs when the characteristics of immigrant groups and host societies become similar (Brown and Bean 2006). Compared with Latin American migrants in the United States, migrants from Myanmar may have distinct experiences that warrant consideration. While some European‐origin populations have historically shown faster structural integration across generations, the trajectories of Latin American and Asian migrants can be more varied and shaped by differences in language, culture, migration context, and structural barriers (Brown and Bean 2006).

According to world systems theory, rich core capitalist societies succeed by exploiting poorer peripheral societies (Daniel 2015). Given that Thailand might not be viewed as a capitalist society, Myanmar migrants may not be tempted to stay in Thailand long term. In addition, none of the migrants in Thailand mentioned applying for legal status to remain there, despite benefits such as healthcare and job opportunities. While the participants could have filed an immigration claim without telling the interviewer, their legal status would most likely be temporary since most intended to return to Myanmar when the situation was safer. Living in a protracted state of limbo and uncertainty has been reported to be associated with decreases in resilience and increases in certain mental health problems, such as depression, indicating that access to mental health care is even more important for this population of Myanmar migrants.

Strengths and Limitations

This study population does not include all Myanmar migrants in Thailand. The study participants all resided in the Mae Sot area, which is only one border city in Thailand, and specific border towns might differ from this population. In addition, compared with the overall population of migrants in Thailand, the sample size is small. Another major limitation of this study is that 12 of the 32 participants were schoolteachers. This means that they were educated and are more likely to have access to more resources than migrants working in blue‐collar jobs such as factory workers or farmers are. As an example, unlike those working in fields or factories, many of these teachers received COVID‐19 protective equipment from educational organisations. Finally, close to one‐fifth of the study participants were HIV positive and therefore differed from healthier migrants. These participants need more medical attention than those without HIV do and face the stigma attached to the disease. Healthier migrants do not have the same lived experience as this population does. However, this study is among the first to collect data from Myanmar migrants for future intervention development.

Recommendations for Future Research

Future research should gather more data from migrant populations in low‐ and middle‐income countries, integrating the local environment's resources and cultural context to design feasible interventions that are culturally grounded. To gain more insight into why Myanmar migrants do not seek help despite experiencing high levels of stress and having knowledge of available resources in Thailand, more research is needed.

Implications for Policy and Practice

Thailand provides an important setting from which to study migrants from Myanmar that come from an emerging democratic country with traumatic geopolitics. In 2023, more than 1.3 million people were forced to flee their homes in Myanmar, and 2.6 million individuals remained displaced (UNHCR 2024). This study explores the impact of a constellation of stressors on migrant health and well‐being, advancing the field of migrant health research in resource‐limited settings. The study findings provide concrete directions for compassionate practice in supporting migrants who are experiencing intersecting stressors, potentially mitigating adverse health outcomes such as local pandemic containment efforts. Factors such as the degree of acculturation, mental health status, and the role of religion as a coping strategy should be incorporated into interventions to assist migrants in accessing essential health care. A culturally competent approach to compassion not only aims to alleviate suffering but also enhances mental health by creating a supportive environment that acknowledges migrants' emotional needs. By doing so, it reduces the societal burden of coping with migration‐related challenges and supports the global trend toward more patient‐centered care.

Conclusions

This study highlights the challenges faced by Myanmar migrants in Thailand, particularly regarding access to healthcare and coping mechanisms during the COVID‐19 pandemic. Limited access to resources, including healthcare facilities and financial means, poses significant barriers to seeking professional medical help. As a result, migrants often resort to self‐reliance, utilizing traditional herbal remedies and communal support networks to address physical and mental health needs. Moreover, religious beliefs, such as Buddhism, influence their approach to coping with adversity, further reinforcing self‐reliance. Despite facing mental health challenges exacerbated by the unstable political situation in Myanmar, many migrants express a desire to return home once conditions improve. However, the limitations of this study, including the small sample size and the overrepresentation of educated individuals and those with HIV, underscore the need for further research to better understand the diverse experiences of Myanmar migrants in Thailand and to inform targeted interventions to address their needs.

Relevance to Clinical Practice

The repercussions of prolonged neglect of mental well‐being mean that mental health must be integrated into formal health assessments of migrant populations. The strategies migrants use for managing high levels of stress, such as religious coping and communal support networks, can be leveraged to tailor services and improve patient outcomes in clinical settings. Healthcare providers serving migrants should be aware of how to provide better access to healthcare and migrants' potential coping mechanisms during stressful conditions. These findings highlight the importance of culturally competent compassion among all the actors involved in collaboration to address the health care needs of migrants.
