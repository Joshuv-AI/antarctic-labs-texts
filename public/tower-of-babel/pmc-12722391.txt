Intestinal LGR4 Signalling Alters Bacterial translocation, Intrahepatic Microbiota composition, and Type 1 Interferon Expression in Murine Liver Fibrosis
Authors: Junyan Gao, Bangtao Chen, Cheng Chen, Yanghui Bi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Liver fibrosis (LF) is linked to decreased type 1 interferon (IFN1) levels at steady-state, intestinal dysbacteriosis, leaky gut, and intestinal bacterial translocation. Liver leucine-rich repeats containing G protein-coupled receptor 4 (LGR4) signalling drives LF development, and intestinal LGR4 homeostasis maintains the intestinal stem cells. To explore the role of intestinal LGR4 signalling in LF and possible relationships with intrahepatic microbiota and IFN1, intraperitoneal injection of carbon tetrachloride (CCL4) was applied to induce LF in adult C57BL/6J mice with or without conditional knockout (cKO) of Lgr4 in intestinal epithelial cells (IECs). Fibrotic mice were simultaneously treated with intragastric bacteria as needed. Results showed that wild-type murine LF resulted in intestinal LGR4 reduction, increased intestinal permeability and bacterial translocation, decreased intrahepatic IFN1 levels, and decreased abundance of bacterial genera Lactobacillus, Dubosiella, and Bifidobacterium. Lgr4 cKO in IECs significantly promoted LF accompanied by increased intrahepatic abundance of genera Escherichia-Shigella, Klebsiella, Acinetobacter and decreased intrahepatic abundance of Lactobacillus, Dubosiella, and Bifidobacterium. In the absence of CCL4 challenge, Lgr4 cKO in IECs also resulted in increased intrahepatic Escherichia-Shigella and decreased Lactobacillus. In mice with Lgr4 cKO in IECs, intragastric administration of Lactobacillus significantly attenuated LF accompanied by significantly decreased intrahepatic Escherichia-Shigella, Klebsiella, and Acinetobacter along with increased intrahepatic Lactobacillus and IFN1 levels; however, deletion of the organismal IFN1 receptor abolished the Lactobacillus-mediated alleviation of LF. Co-culture of DC2.4 cells with Lactobacillus or its genomic DNA increased supernatant IFN-β levels. Overall, this study demonstrates that weakened intestinal LGR4 signalling facilitates LF by increasing intestinal permeability, altering intrahepatic microbiota composition, and decreasing liver IFN1 levels.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10753-025-02420-3.

Introduction

Liver fibrosis (LF), characterized by persistent hepatic stellate cell (HSC) activation and HSC-derived extracellular matrix over-deposition in normal liver architecture, is an inevitable but reversible pathological process that occurs during the progression of almost all chronic liver diseases (including virus-, drug-, parasite-, and alcohol-induced hepatitis and non-alcoholic fatty liver diseases) to liver cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Therefore, diseases involving LF pose a huge economic burden and significant health challenges to mankind, and developing novel therapeutic strategies based on the advancements in the understanding of its pathogenesis is key to optimizing the clinical management of LF [1].

Type 1 interferon (IFN1), including IFN-α/β/ω/κ/ε/δ/τ subtypes, is the most important innate immunity executor for monitoring viral infections and tumour immune escape. The anti-LF activity of IFN1 has been demonstrated not only in clinical practice where persistent IFN1 treatment delays the onset or progression of LF in patients with viral hepatitis, but also in in vivo and in vitro experiments using non-viruses-induced LF models [2, 3]. In the liver tissue, dendritic cell has the strongest ability to produce IFN1 [4]. Organismal IFN1 expression is orchestrated by beneficial gut bacteria such as Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium [5–7]. Gut dysbiosis plays an important role in LF progression, and correction of dysbacteriosis by various methods has been successfully applied in LF animal models and some patients with LF. The mechanisms through which gut microbiota regulate LF are multifaceted, involving bacteria themselves, metabolites, and the intestinal mucosal integrity barrier. Impaired intestinal barrier integrity and gut bacterial translocation are well-known characteristics that have been identified in clinical patients with LF or liver cirrhosis and murine LF models [8–10]; however, the profiles of translocated microbiota and related intestinal factors are poorly understood.

With advancements in technology for bacterial DNA (btDNA) enrichment from tissue samples and sequencing depth, some studies have reported the presence of microbiota in blood and solid tissues from patients and even healthy individuals [11–13]. For example, we previously profiled the circulating microbiota in patients with urticaria with vitamin D insufficiency and endometrium microbiota in patients with endometrial cancer [14, 15]. A leaky gut increases the risk of the influx of faecal microbiota and related metabolites into extraintestinal organs via circulation, and the liver is the most susceptible organ to bacterial deposition because of the presence of anatomic enterohepatic circulation. Clinical studies have indeed confirmed the presence of microbiota in portal blood and liver tissues, which is associated with disease severity possibly via regulating inflammation response [16].

Leucine-rich repeat-containing G protein-coupled receptor4 (LGR4) is a widely expressed membrane receptor that binds R-spondin, thereby potentiating the Wnt/β–Catenin signalling pathway [17]. We previously proved that LGR4 signalling in hepatocytes drives the development of HCC [18]. More importantly, intestinal LGR4 signalling is essential for the self-renewal of intestinal stem cells and maintaining intestinal barrier integrity [19, 20]. However, evidence regarding intestinal LGR4 expression and signalling in diseases involving LF are lacking.

In this study, we focused on characterizing the liver-resident microbiota in a carbon-tetrachloride (CCL4)-induced murine LF model with or without conditional knockout (cKO) of Lgr4 in intestinal epithelial cells (IEC). Further, we also evaluated alterations in LF phenotypes and liver IFN1 expression to elucidate the links between them.

Materials and Methods

Reagents

Olive oil (#O815210, Macklin, Shanghai, China), CCL4(#C805332, Macklin), Mouse direct PCR Kit (#B40015,Selleck, Shanghai, China), 2xPhanta Max Master Mix (Dye Plus, #P525-01,Vazyme, Wuhan, China), HiScript IV All-in-One Ultra RT SuperMix for qPCR(#R433-01, Vazyme), ChamQ Blue Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix(#Q312-02, Vazyme), QIAquick gel extraction kit (#28706 × 4, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), dual luciferase reporter assay kit(#DL101-01,Vazyme), 4% paraformaldehyde(#P885233, Macklin), paraffin(#P815701, Macklin), mouse LPS ELISA kit(#MM-0634M2,MEIMIAN, SUZHOU, China), mouse calprotectin ELISA kit(#MM-1180M2,MEIMIAN, SUZHOU, China), hydroxyproline (HYP) ELISA Kit (#E-BC-K062-M, Elabscience), mouse IFN-β ELISA Kit (#E-EL-M0033, Elabscience), hematoxylin and eosin (HE,#DH0020, Leagene, Beijing, China), Sirius red staining(#DC0041, Leagene), Masson staining(#DC0032, Leagene), HiPure Microbiome DNA Kit (#D3148-02, Magen, Guangzhou, China), Lactobacillus murinus (#Bio-03587, biobw), Lactobacillus intestinalis (#49335, ATCC, USA) DC2.4 cells (#M3-1401, Oricell), AML12 cells (#CL-0602, Pricella), bacterial genomic DNA extraction kit (#DP302, Tiangen), ZO-1 antibody (#GB11195,Servicebio) were purchased. Plasmids expressing pGM-IFN-β-RLuc or pGM-Fluc were kept in our laboratory previously [21].

Mice and LF Mouse Model

All experimental protocols involving mice were approved by the animal ethics committee of Changzhi Medical College (DW2024003) and were performed according to international and Chinese laws and guidelines on animal protection. Lgr4FLOX mice with the C57BL/6J background were kindly provided by Professor Weizhen Zhang (Peking University), and Vil1Cre mice (#C001243) were purchased from Chinese Cyagen Biosciences Inc (Suzhou, Jiangsu Province). Mice with cKO of Lgr4 in IEC (Lgr4kIEC mice) were generated by mating Lgr4FLOX and Vil1Cre mice. In addition, to block organismal IFN1 signalling in Lgr4kIEC mice, mice with knockout of IFN1 α receptor (IFNAR1) were crossed with Lgr4kIEC mice to obtain Ifnar1KO/Lgr4kIEC mice. All these mice were bred and housed in ventilated cages in the same housing unit (room temperature: 25 ± 2 °C, humidity: 50 ± 5%, light/dark: 12 h/12 h) in specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. As sex difference and housing in separate cages are inherent factors that cannot be ignored in microbial diversity, only female mice housed in the same cage were selected for this study. Female offspring (12 mice per group) aged 6–8 weeks and weighing 20 ± 2 g were intraperitoneally (i.p.) injected with CCL4 (1 mL/kg) diluted in olive oil (1:4, v/v, twice a week for 8–12 weeks) to induce LF development. An equal volume of olive oil was injected i.p. in the control mice.

Bacteria Culture and Supplementations

Lactobacillus murinus and Lactobacillus intestinalis cultures used in the study were the same as previously described [22, 23]. Chromosomal DNA extraction from live Lactobacillus murinus and Lactobacillus intestinalis was performed using a bacterial genomic DNA extraction kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To obtain heat-inactivated Lactobacillus, the bacterial precipitations were mixed with deionized water and then inactivated at 121 °C for 20 min. Female C57BL/6 mice aged 6–8 weeks were orally administered 1 × 109 CFU live Lactobacillus (murinus/intestinalis, 1:1) in 200 µL anaerobic saline daily during the establishment of LF model until the last dose of CCL4.

Cells Culture and Treated With Bacteria in Vitro

DC2.4 and AML12 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium and DMEM/F12 containing 10% FBS, respectively. Penicillin (100 U/mL) and streptomycin (100 µg/mL) were also added to the medium except when the cells were treated with bacteria or bacterial DNA. DC2.4 cells and AML12 cells were treated with inactivated bacteria or bacterial DNA, and culture supernatants were used to determine IFN-β levels. In addition, DC2.4 cells pre-transfected with pGM-IFN-β-RLuc and pGM-Fluc using lipotamine3000 were treated with live bacteria, and the cell lysates were used for the determination of IFN-β promoter activity.

Quantitative PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from the ileum tissues using an animal total RNA isolation kit. Extracted total RNA was dissolved in 50 µL RNase-free water. HiScript IV All-in-One Ultra RT SuperMix for qPCR was used for first-strand cDNA synthesis from total RNA, and ChamQ Blue Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix was applied to determine ZO-1 and Occludin mRNA levels using specific primers, with mouse β-actin used as an internal control (Table 1). The relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method.

Determination of btDNA

btDNA in serum or liver tissue was estimated using semi-quantitative reverse transription PCR. Briefly, microbiome DNA were isolated from 200 mg liver tissues or 50 µL serum using a HiPure Microbiome DNA Kit according to manufacturer’s instructions, and the DNA was eluted in a final volume of 100 µL. btDNA levels were determined using PCR via a 20-µL amplification reaction with universal eubacterial primers (Table 1) of a conserved region of the 16 S rDNA gene. btDNA fragments were extracted and purified using a QIAquick gel extraction kit, and the purified amplicons were dissolved in 100 µL of sterile water. A NanoDrop UV–Vis spectrometer (2000 C, Thermo Scientific, America) was used to quantify the btDNA amplicons. To rule out any contamination from reagents or environmental sources, six tubes of diethyl pyrocarbonate (DEPC) water were used as negative controls, and these tubes underwent the same processes of DNA extraction to btDNA fragment extraction as the serum and liver samples [24].

Liver btDNA Sequencing and Analysis

Fresh liver tissue samples (200 mg) were added to sterile EP tubes using sterile forceps in an ultra-clean laminar airflow chamber after sterilisation. The tubes carrying fresh samples were stored at − 80 °C and then sent to GENE DENOVO (Guangzhou, China) in dry ice for 16 S rDNA sequencing using bacterial universal primers V3–V4. Microbiome DNA was isolated from liver tissues using the HiPure Microbiome DNA Kit according to manufacturer’s instructions, and PCR and 16 S rDNA sequencing were performed using bacterial universal primers V3–V4. Bioinformatics analyses were performed as previously described, and P values were adjusted using false discovery rates (FDRs) [25]. The raw reads have been deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive database (Accession Number: PRJNA1268118).

Histological Staining

Fresh liver sections were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at least for 24 h and then embedded in paraffin. Paraffin-embedded liver tissues were sliced into 4-µm-thick sections, deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with hematein–eosin (HE) or Masson and Sirius red dyes to observe the morphology and inflammation infiltration or collagen deposition, respectively. All staining procedures followed previously described standard procedures [25]. In addition, the ileum was collected for immunofluorescence studies using a ZO-1 antibody following the method described by Hu et al. [22].

Biochemical Analysis

Serum LPS, liver HYP, and gut calprotectin levels were determined at 510 nm using commercial ELISA kits following manufacturer’s instructions. Liver total IFN1 levels were determined following a previously described method [14]. Briefly, HEK293 cells stably expressing IFN1 receptors were cultured in vitro and co-transfected with plasmids expressing ISRE-FLuc and RLuc using lipotamine3000. The supernatant of the homogenate of 200 mg liver tissue was added to the cell supernatant, and the cells were collected and lysed for the assessment of fluorescence intensity of firefly and renilla luciferases using a dual luciferase reporter assay kit. The IFN1 level is presented as the Fluc/RLuc ratio.

Statistical Analyses

In addition to the aforementioned analysis of tissue microbiota data, the continuous variables in this study are presented as mean (M) ± standard deviation (SD). Student’s t-test was used to test differences between two groups. One-way ANOVA and Bonferroni’s multiple comparisons post-tests were used in the analysis of the variables among three groups. A P-value < 0.05 (two sides) was used to determine statistical significance.

Results

Intestine LGR4 Reduction and Intrahepatic Microbiota Alterations in CCL4-induced LF

CCL4 administration for 12 weeks caused obvious liver pseudolobules and inflammatory cell infiltration, which were visualized using HE staining, and collagen deposition, which was visualized using Masson and Sirus red staining (Fig. 1A). Further, increased liver HYP levels were also noted (Supp. Figure 1A). At week 12, the weight gain from baseline and ratio of liver to body weight were significantly lower in the CCL4 group than in the mock group (Fig. 1B&C). In addition, serum LPS (Fig. 1D) and faecal calprotectin (Fig. 1E) levels were significantly higher, whereas liver IFN1 (Fig. 1F), ileum ZO-1 and Occludin mRNA levels (Fig. 1G) and ileum LGR4 protein levels (Fig. 1H) were lower in the CCL4 group. In addition, the reduction in ileum ZO-1 protein expression was also confirmed using immunofluorescence (Supp. Figure 2). btDNA loads in the serum and liver, estimated by amplification of microbial DNA using bacterial V3–V4 primers, were also remarkably higher in the CCL4 group (Fig. 1I). Total intrahepatic btDNA from seven and 11 mice from the mock and CCL4 groups, respectively, met the requirements for 16 S rDNA sequencing. Using bioinformatics analysis, we found that the reads involved 3191 operational taxonomic units (OTUs) including 35 phyla, 86 classes, 221 orders, 386 families, 780 genera, and 1213 species. There were 300 shared bacteria between the two groups at genus level, with 49 and 428 unique ones in the mock and CCL4 groups, respectively. At the OTU level, different clusters of the samples were displayed on principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) (R = 0.368, P = 0.009, Fig. 1J) between the two groups, and the CCL4 group had a higher microbial dysbacteriosis index (MDI) (− 1.16 ± 0.19 vs. 0.44 ± 0.29, P = 0.001). The relative abundance of the phylum Proteobacteria (9.19 ± 6.27 vs. 26.60 ± 14.63, P = 0.031) was markedly higher, whereas that of Actinobacteriota (13.50 ± 7.22 vs. 3.01 ± 1.26, P = 0.002, adjusted P = 0.035) and Verrucomicrobiota (1.85 ± 1.23 vs. 0.38 ± 0.50, P = 0.018) was remarkably lower in the CCL4 group than in the mock group. At the genus level (Fig. 1K), the CCL4 group displayed significantly increased levels of Lysinibacillus (0 ± 0 vs. 9.59 ± 12.79, P = 0.039) and Enterococcus (0.04 ± 0.04 vs. 2.16 ± 2.75, P = 0.011), decreased levels of members of the norank_f__Muribaculaceae (20.63 ± 7.91 vs. 9.54 ± 7.68, P = 0.018), Lactobacillus (24.50 ± 6.93 vs. 4.96 ± 1.99, P = 0.002), Dubosiella (12.39 ± 7.36 vs. 3.65 ± 6.82, P = 0.018), Bifidobacterium (10.08 ± 7.02 vs. 0.60 ± 0.62, P = 0.002), Allobaculum (2.53 ± 1.67 vs. 1.02 ± 1.71, P = 0.049), Akkermansia (1.81 ± 1.28 vs. 0.12 ± 0.14, P = 0.010), Coriobacteriaceae_UCG-002(1.52 ± 0.91 vs. 0.10 ± 0.13, P = 0.001), and Parasutterella (1.22 ± 1.01 vs. 0.27 ± 0.30, P = 0.023). At the species level, after excluding unclassified Lactobacillus, the abundance of the two major Lactobacillus (murinus + intestinalis) species in the mock group was 1.59 ± 0.31 times higher than that in the CCL4 group (P = 0.049). cKO of Lgr4 in IEC Aggravates LF and Influences Intrahepatic Microbiota

Compared with Lgr4FLOX mice that received CCL4 administration, Lgr4kIEC mice showed more obvious pseudolobules, greater inflammatory cell infiltration, higher HYP levels, and greater collagen deposition in the liver at week 8 (Fig. 2A, Supp. Figure 1B). Lgr4kIEC mice also showed reduced weight gain from baseline, a lower ratio of liver to body weight (Fig. 2B&C), and higher serum LPS levels (Fig. 2D). Further, the two groups had similar gut calprotectin levels (Fig. 2E), whereas liver IFN1 levels (Fig. 2F) were lower in Lgr4kIEC mice. The amplified btDNA loads from the serum were similar between the two groups, whereas those from the liver were significantly higher in Lgr4kIEC mice (Fig. 2G). The total intrahepatic btDNA from six Lgr4FLOX and 10 Lgr4kIEC mice, respectively, met the requirements for 16 S rDNA sequencing. The reads comprised 3040 OTUs, including 28 phyla, 80 classes, 205 orders, 351 families, 689 genera, and 1065 species. There were 249 shared bacterial genera with 152 and 274 unique genera in the Lgr4FLOX and Lgr4kIECmice, respectively. At the OTU level, different clusters of the samples were displayed on PCoA (R = 0.896, P = 0.001, Fig. 2H) between the two groups, with Lgr4kIEC mice having a higher MDI (−1.13 ± 0.23 vs. 0.69 ± 0.47, P = 0.000). In Lgr4kIEC mice, the relative abundance of the phylum Proteobacteria (10.28 ± 6.84 vs. 50.62 ± 25.08, P = 0.000, adjusted P = 0.009) was markedly increased, whereas that of Firmicutes (37.79 ± 11.72 vs. 21.63 ± 9.90, P = 0.008, adjusted P = 0.049), Bacteroidota (33.96 ± 11.66 vs. 13.70 ± 9.34, P = 0.000, adjusted P = 0.014), Campilobacterota (6.57 ± 3.96 vs. 1.13 ± 2.02, P = 0.002, adjusted P = 0.022), and Desulfobacterota (1.64 ± 1.36 vs. 0.39 ± 0.26, P = 0.008, adjusted P = 0.049) were remarkably decreased. At the genus level (Fig. 2I), Lgr4kIEC mice displayed a significant increase in Escherichia-Shigella (0.13 ± 0.21 vs. 30.77 ± 24.71, P = 0.000, adjusted P = 0.047), Klebsiella (0.15 ± 0.23 vs. 3.99 ± 5.63, P = 0.003), and Acinetobacter (0.41 ± 0.62 vs. 2.94 ± 4.49, P = 0.010), whereas members of the norank_f__Muribaculaceae (23.28 ± 9.51 vs. 4.98 ± 5.04, P = 0.000, adjusted P = 0.047), Lactobacillus (10.20 ± 2.89 vs. 4.01 ± 2.30, P = 0.000), Helicobacter (6.57 ± 3.96 vs. 1.13 ± 2.02, P = 0.002), Dubosiella (6.27 ± 7.33 vs. 0.73 ± 1.81, P = 0.008), and Bifidobacterium (2.84 ± 3.69 vs. 0.05 ± 0.15, P = 0.007) were decreased. At the species level, after excluding the unclassified Lactobacillus, the abundance of the two major Lactobacillus (murinus + intestinalis) in the Lgr4FLOX + CCL4 group was 1.58 ± 1.16 times greater than that in the Lgr4kIEC + CCL4 group (P = 0.209). cKO of Lgr4 in IEC Also Modifies Intrahepatic Microbiota and Decreases Liver IFN1 at steady-state

The weight of adult Lgr4flox and Lgr4kIEC mice that had not received CCL4 was monitored over 2 months. At week 8, there was no formation of pseudolobules, inflammation, collagen deposition, or altered HYP levels in the liver in Lgr4kIEC mice (Fig. 3A, Supp. Figure 1C). However, Lgr4kIEC mice showed reduced weight gain from baseline (Fig. 3B) but a similar ratio of liver to body weight (Fig. 3C), higher serum LPS (Fig. 3D) but similar gut calprotectin levels (Fig. 3E), higher liver IFN1 levels at steady-state (Fig. 3F), and amplified btDNA loads from the liver (Fig. 3G) compared with Lgr4FLOX mice. The total intrahepatic btDNA from seven Lgr4FLOX mice and eight Lgr4kIEC mice met the requirements for 16 S rDNA sequencing. The reads involved 2944 OTUs including 29 phyla, 79 classes, 207 orders, 356 families, 715 genera, and 1130 species. There were 225 shared bacterial genera with 178 and 305 unique genera in the Lgr4FLOX and Lgr4kIEC mice, respectively. At the OTU level, different clusters of the samples were displayed on PCoA (R = 0.904, P = 0.001, Fig. 3H) between the two groups. In Lgr4kIEC mice, phyla Proteobacteria (16.06 ± 8.79 vs. 28.35 ± 10.28, P = 0.045), Verrucomicrobiota (0.56 ± 0.60 vs. 2.89 ± 1.54, P = 0.004, adjusted P = 0.018), Cyanobacteria (0.68 ± 1.19 vs. 2.14 ± 2.51, P = 0.045), Planctomycetota (0.08 ± 0.13 vs. 2.30 ± 2.22, P = 0.002, adjusted P = 0.017), and Chloroflexi (0.04 ± 0.04 vs. 1.49 ± 1.18, P = 0.010) were markedly increased, whereas Firmicutes (53.49 ± 10.51 vs. 23.70 ± 9.70, P = 0.002, adjusted P = 0.017) was remarkably decreased. At the genus level, Lgr4kIEC mice displayed a significant increase in Escherichia-Shigella (0.07 ± 0.14 vs. 7.06 ± 2.86, P = 0.002), Bacteroides (0.40 ± 0.21 vs. 3.07 ± 1.82, P = 0.002), Klebsiella (0.25 ± 0.27 vs. 3.18 ± 2.30, P = 0.008), Prevotella (0.003 ± 0.004 vs. 2.77 ± 1.24, P = 0.002), Halomonas (0.71 ± 0.48 vs. 1.40 ± 0.63, P = 0.045), and Alloprevotella (0.08 ± 0.05 vs. 1.64 ± 0.48, P = 0.002), whereas Lactobacillus (31.52 ± 7.73 vs. 4.21 ± 0.65, P = 0.002), Pelomonas (5.38 ± 3.10 vs. 0.74 ± 0.60, P = 0.024), Lysinibacillus (3.29 ± 2.81 vs. 0.04 ± 0.08, P = 0.156), and Ralstonia (2.08 ± 1.34 vs. 0.16 ± 0.18, P = 0.002) were decreased (Fig. 3H). In Ifnar1KO/Lgr4kIEC mice that received Lactobacillus oral supplementation, the LF phenotype was similar to that in Lgr4kIEC mice that did not receive Lactobacillus supplementation (Fig. 4A, Supp. Figure 1D). In Lgr4kIEC mice, Lactobacillus supplementation significantly enhanced liver IFN1 levels (Fig. 4F) and amplified btDNA loads from liver (Fig. 4G). Discussion

This is the first study to attempt to delineate the intrahepatic microbiota signature and determine its role in CCL4-induced LF in female C57BL/6 mice with intestinal Lgr4 ablation. By conducting several comparisons, this study provides novel and important evidence on the association between intrahepatic microbiota (especially the changes in commensal bacteria), liver IFN1 expression, and intestinal LGR4 signalling in a murine LF model.

In 2004, Mazerbourg et al. reported the important role of LGR4 signalling in body weight gain and development of organs (including liver, kidney, and uterus) [26].The evidence that Lgr4 knockout impaired epithelial cell proliferation (50% decrease) and terminal differentiation of Paneth cells in intestinal crypts of postnatal mouse (80% reduction) highlighted the importance of LGR4 signalling in intestinal homeostasis [27], which may be related to intestinal mucosal permeability as the indispensability of the integrity of Paneth cells for the low permeability of intestinal mucosa in several types of diseases has been demonstrated [19, 27, 28]. Coincidentally, Liu et al. also showed that Lgr4 deficiency increased the susceptibility and severity of intestinal inflammation in mice challenged with oral dextran sodium sulphate via regulation of the Wnt/β–catenin signalling pathway [29]. However, these studies did not elucidate the role and mechanisms of organ-specific LGR4 signalling in diseases using Lgr4 cKO as it is widely expressed, especially highly expressed in the liver and kidney [30]. A series of studies conducted by Zhang et al. clarified the key role of liver LGR4 signalling in promoting liver injury, fibrosis, and liver cancer [31]. Recently, they found that bile duct ligation (BDL), another way to induce LF, downregulates hepatic Lgr4 mRNA [30].

In our previous study, up-regulated liver LGR4 expression was found in tumour tissues from patients with HCC and murine liver tumours induced by diethylnitrosamine + CCL4, and its level was positively associated with tumour malignancy [18]. CCL4-induced hepatic LGR4 expression may drive the progression of LF to carcinoma, and high expression of hepatic LGR4 may persist during tumour formation. Increases in the protein levels of liver LGR4 were verified in CCL4-induced LF in the current study, and it has been suggested that a high abundance of liver LGR4 promotes LF by potentiating intrahepatic R-Spondin signalling [32]. Moreover, we also showed the reductions in intestinal protein levels of LGR4 and mRNA levels of ZO-1 and Occludin in CCL4-induced LF. The increased intestinal mucosal permeability facilitates LF as circulating LPS level and btDNA loads were also significantly increased. In addition, the increased gut calprotectin levels imply the occurrence of intestinal inflammation during LF development, which has been previously verified. The roles of intestinal LGR4 in liver diseases are diverse. Liang et al. recently proposed a strategy for improving obesity and liver steatosis by targeting intestinal LGR4 as they observed that Lgr4 ablation in IEC unexpectedly protected mice from body weight gain and high fat diet (HFD)-induced liver steatosis, which was further proved to be related to intestinal LGR4 signalling maintaining enterocytes selective for absorption of long-chain fatty acid while decreasing carbohydrate-absorptive enterocytes via Wnt and Notch signalling pathways [20]. Interestingly, we also found that Lgr4 deficiency in IECs reduced body weight gain in mice fed a normal chow diet while rendering them susceptible to i.p. CCL4-induced liver injury and fibrosis. The difference in phenotypes largely lies in the different mechanisms of hepatotoxicity of CCL4 and HFD.

Specifically, intestinal LGR4 signalling may contribute to LF progression via controlling intrahepatic microbiota. In CCL4-induced LF models, the relative abundance of intrahepatic genera Lysinibacillus and Enterococcus was increased, whereas that of many taxa including genera Lactobacillus, Dubosiella, Bifidobacterium, Allobaculum, Akkermansia, and Parasutterella was significantly decreased. Among mice that received the CCL4 challenge, those with Lgr4 deletion in IECs exhibited increments in the abundance of intrahepatic genera Escherichia-Shigella, Klebsiella, and Acinetobacter and remarkable reductions in those Lactobacillus, Helicobacter, Dubosiella, and Bifidobacterium, compared with wild-type mice. The reduction in Lactobacillus, Dubosiella, and Bifidobacterium may be one of the reasons for the aggravation of LF mediated by Lgr4 ablation as these genera were also reduced in the livers of CCL4-treated mice compared with those wild-type mice of the mock group. Even after excluding the interference of CCL4 exposure, we found that intrahepatic Lactobacillus was reduced, whereas intrahepatic Escherichia-Shigella and Klebsiella were upregulated in mice with deletion of Lgr4 in IEC. These data imply that intrahepatic Lactobacillus reduction was the result of Lgr4 ablation in IEC independent of CCL4 i.p. injection. Oral Lactobacillus supplementation was unexpectedly found to relieve CCL4-induced LF, accompanied by increase in the abundance of intrahepatic Lactobacillus and Dubosiella and decrease in the abundance of Escherichia-Shigella, Klebsiella, Acinetobacter, Alloprevotella, and Bacteroides.

The presence of intrahepatic microbiota has been reported in clinical patients but not in animal models. In a study including 19 patients with HCC, members of phyla Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, and Firmicutes were found to be the dominant taxa both in the tumor tissue and the adjacent normal liver tissue, whereas the tumour was characterized by an increased abundance of the genera Rhodobacter/Megasphaera and the adjacent tissues by an increased abundance of Pseudochrobactrum. Moreover, enrichment of class Actinobacteria and order Actinomycetales has been observed in cirrhotic liver [33]. Intrahepatic microbiota may be derived from circulating microbiota that occur as a result of gut bacterial translocation; however, direct evidence is lacking. In comparison with the noncirrhotic non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, both the liver tissue and blood from patients with liver cirrhosis or HCC are enriched with the families Ruminococcaceae and Bacteroidaceae, which are present in high abundance in human faeces [34]. Gedgaudas et al. showed that several genera including Comamonas, Cnuella, Dialister, Escherichia/Shigella, and Prevotella are enriched, whereas Bradyrhizobium, Curvibacter, Diaphorobacter, Pseudarcicella, and Pseudomonas are lacking in circulating plasma or hepatic vein blood in patients with cirrhosis, and further analysis revealed that the enriched abundance of Escherichia/Shigella and Prevotella is positively correlated with serum IL-8 level and the degree of portal hypertension [16].

Our data showing high abundance of intrahepatic Escherichia-Shigella in Lgr4kIEC mice with or without CCL4 administration raises the question of liver infection because acute Escherichia-Shigella colonisation in the gut causes diarrhoea. Therefore, systemic inflammation biomarkers including tumour necrosis factor-α and interleukin-6 were assessed. Their levels were higher in the liver and serum of CCL4-challenged Lgr4kIEC mice than in CCL4-challenged Lgr4FLOX mice. However, without CCL4 challenge, their levels in the liver and serum remained similar in Lgr4kIEC and Lgr4FLOX mice (data not shown), implying that Escherichia-Shigella in the liver failed to induce an inflammation response, which is observed upon its acute gut colonisation, and the underlying reasons remain unknown. This study does not provide data about changes in gut microbiota, and whether Escherichia-Shigella in the liver is derived from the gut remains unknown. However, Vega-Magaña et al. found that the abundance of Escherichia-Shigella in the gut was significantly increased in mice with cholestatic LF; moreover, the presence of Escherichia-coli in mesenteric lymph nodes indicated bacterial translocation to the liver [35], which may indirectly confirm our findings.

The clinical correlation analysis found that the abundance of some circulating or intrahepatic bacteria is positively correlated with certain inflammatory mediators, which provides a reference for analysing the association of intrahepatic bacteria and liver IFN1 expression. Tanabe et al. revealed that the mRNA levels of murine transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β), basic fibroblast growth factor, collagen- 1A2 (COL1A2), and tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinase 1 in concanavalin A-induced liver injury (the initiating factors for LF formation) could be suppressed by exogenous IFN-β [3]. Inagaki et al. provided direct evidence that IFN-α could prevent progression of CCL4-induced LF via suppression of COL1A2 promoter activation in mice and repress COL1A2 transcription in TGF-β/Smad3-activated HSC by promoting the binding of phosphorylated Stat1 and p300/CBP, thus limiting the binding of p300/CBP and Smad3 [2]. In addition, blocking organismal IFN1 signalling at steady-state drives the severity of cholestasis- and hepatotoxin-induced LF [36]. Organismal IFN1 expression was recently found to be well-tuned by gut commensal bacteria in animal models with virus infections or tumour loads [37–39]. However, evidence of associations between tissue-resident bacteria and local IFN1 expression are currently missing. In the current study, CCL4-induced LF was accompanied by impairment in homeostasis of liver IFN1 expression, which may be associated with the reduction in intrahepatic Lactobacillus as it could trigger IFN1 production in dendritic cells in vitro, and the liver is rich in dendritic cells [36]. In this context, Lgr4 ablation in IEC caused a reduction in intrahepatic Lactobacillus and weak expression of liver IFN1, thereby aggravating LF severity. As Lgr4 deletion in IECs leads to leaky gut and increased bacterial translocation, active Lactobacillus was orally administered during CCL4 injection to supplement intrahepatic Lactobacillus in mice with Lgr4 ablation in IECs, which resulted in remarkably increased intrahepatic Lactobacillus abundance and IFN1 expression, thereby alleviating LF severity; however, IFN1 expression remained high, while LF severity could not be alleviated in mice with Ifnar1 knockout and Lgr4 ablation in IECs. Si et al. reported that Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG or its genomic DNA could induce IFN1 production in murine bone marrow-derived dendritic cells [40]. Our study further found that the live or heat-inactivated Lactobacillus (murinus + intestinalis) and their genomic DNA induced IFN-β production in DC2.4 cells (immortalized murine dendritic cells) but not in AML12 cells (murine hepatocyte). These data collectively demonstrate that intrahepatic Lactobacillus enrichment promoting liver IFN1 expression contributes to Lactobacillus oral administration-mediated delay in LF progression in mice with Lgr4 deletion in IECs.

An interesting phenomenon observed in this study was that the abundance of liver Escherichia-Shigella and Lactobacillus always opposed each other. Mechanisms from the perspective of IFN1 signalling were proposed to explain this. Approximately 30 years ago, researchers found that acute Shigella invasion could induce modest production of IFN1 in mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) [41]. However, in recent years, Dobbs et al. discovered that the Shigella effector protein IpaJ potently inhibited IFN1 production by blocking stimulator of interferon genes (STING) signalling in MEFs [42]. More recently, Alphonse et al. demonstrated that effector OspC1 and OspC3 of Shigella blocked signalling downstream of IFN from all three families in 293 T cells, and it was also proved that blocking IFN1 signalling facilitated Shigella colonisation of the murine intestine in vivo [43]. Therefore, Lactobacillus-mediated IFN1 upregulation may limit Escherichia-Shigella over-growth in the liver.

Our study had several limitations. First, whether intrahepatic bacteria in healthy mice of different ages without any treatments can be detected via 16 S rDNA sequencing needs to be further investigated, as some bacteria (including Lactobacillus, Pelomonas, Lysinibacillus, and Ralstonia) have been found in wild-type mice with olive oil injection or Lgr4FLOX mice. Second, if circulating and gut microbiota are simultaneously measured and the correlations among the microbiota compositions and abundance in gut, circulation, and liver are analysed, the sources of intrahepatic microbiota will be clear to a certain extent. Third, we could not rule out the possibility that the increment in liver IFN1 expression after Lactobacillus oral administration is a result of the increment in systemic IFN1 expression caused by intestinal Lactobacillus enrichment. Meanwhile, whether intrahepatic microbiota can act directly on HSC activation or microbiota metabolites serve as novel ligands for liver LGR4 require further investigation, and future studies should be performed using microbiota-depleted or germ-free mice to confirm whether ileum LGR4 reduction is a direct effect of CCL4 administration or secondary to inflammation or microbial shifts. In addition, temporal dynamics of the effects of Lactobacillus on changes in the liver and gut microbiota, liver IFN1 normalisation, and LF degree could help further clarify present conclusions. Finally, sex-specific effects should be further determined as only female mice were included in the current study.

Altogether, this study demonstrates that weakened intestinal LGR4 signalling facilitates LF by increasing intestinal permeability, altering intrahepatic microbiota composition, and decreasing liver IFN1 levels. Correcting intrahepatic microbiota and regulating liver IFN1 production via orchestrating gut permeability is a potential strategy for curbing LF.
