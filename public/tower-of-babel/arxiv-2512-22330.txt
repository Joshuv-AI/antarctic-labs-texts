Revisiting de Moivre–Laplace
Raphaël Cerf

Abstract
. We revisit the proof of the de Moivre–Laplace theorem, which is the ancestor of the central limit theorem for the binomial distribution. Our goal is to provide a proof that can be reasonably presented to undergraduate students within a basic course of probability theory. We follow the strategies presented in two classical references, the books of Breiman and Feller, but we replace the arguments involving series expansions of the logarithm or the exponential by the basic inequality exp ⁡ ( t ) ≥ 1 + t \exp(t)\geq 1+t . This way we avoid completely the use of uniform convergence and power series. We also avoid using Stirling’s formula, instead we use the exact formula for the Wallis integral. As a by product of the proof, we also obtain a non-asymptotic inequality linking the binomial and the Gaussian distributions.



1.
Introduction.

I am in charge of an introductory course on Probability at University Paris–Saclay,
for second-year undergraduate students.
The course focuses solely on discrete probability,
but my dream is to show the students the central limit theorem,
or rather a simplified version of it.
This would help them understand the need to develop the continuous side of
probability theory, and would serve as an excellent bridge before entering
the more advanced probability course involving distribution with densities,
and possibly measure theory.

B(n,\frac{1}{2})
,

{\mathcal{N}}(\frac{n}{2},\frac{n}{4})

for
n=1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8,20,40,50,100

Of course, it is out of the question to aim for the general central limit theorem.
A sensible goal would be to prove the de Moivre–Laplace theorem, which is the
ancestor of the central limit theorem for the binomial distribution.
It was obtained in the symmetric case
p=\frac{1}{2}
by Abraham de Moivre in 1733
and it appeared
in the second edition of The Doctrine of Chances,
published in 1738
[
4
]
.
It is illustrated in figure
1
, which presents
the histograms of the first binomial distributions.
Laplace
proved the result in 1812
for any value of
p
in
]0,1[

(see chapter III of
[
8
]
).
Here is the theorem, in all its splendor, presented in a modern form.

Theorem 1

(de Moivre–Laplace)
.

Let
p\in]0,1[
.
For each
n\geq 1
, let

X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be
n
independent Bernoulli random variables with parameter
p
, i.e.,

\forall i\in\{\,1,\dots,n\,\}\qquad P(X_{i}=0)\,=\,1-p\,,\qquad P(X_{i}=1)\,=\,p\,.

For any real numbers
a<b
, the following limit holds:

(1)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\,P\Big(a<\frac{X_{1}+\cdots+X_{n}-np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}<b\Big)\,=\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{a}^{b}e^{-\tfrac{s^{2}}{2}}ds\,.

So I searched the literature for an elementary exposition of
the de Moivre–Laplace theorem.
The Wikipedia page for the de Moivre–Laplace theorem presents the sketches of
two possible arguments.
The first one is based on a differential equation, which can
be made rigorous
(see
[
7
]
), but is not very intuitive.
The second one follows more the historical approach and relies on Stirling’s formula.
Amazingly enough, this theorem,
which is almost 300 years old,
is still the subject of recent papers
[
1
,
6
]
(these are technical refinements and they are
not suited for preparing an undergraduate lecture).

The best sources I found were in fact contained in two classical books
of probability.
Leo Breiman
[
3
]
presents a three pages proof in the section
entitled ”The bell-shaped curve enters”.
William Feller,
in his legendary book
[
5
]
, devotes a full chapter to the normal approximation
of the binomial distribution.
These are the most natural and obvious places to look, and indeed these two
references provide a full and elementary derivation. Both references present
first the specific case of the symmetric binomial distribution,
where the formulas are simpler and the computations easier.

So I wondered: would it be a pleasant and profitable experience for the students
to be exposed to these proofs ?
Profitable, certainly, they would learn a lot from it. Pleasant, I am not so sure,
because the proofs involve technical computations, which seem unavoidable.
Let us discuss the passages that are likely to leave
many of our students behind, however willing they may be.

\bullet

The most off-putting part of Breiman’s proof is the use of a uniform expansion of the logarithm.
Throughout the calculation, a residual term appears, denoted
successively by

\varepsilon_{j,n}
,

\varepsilon^{\prime}_{j,n}
,

\Delta_{j,n}
, where the index
j
varies in a set of integers
R_{n}
.
The point is that these error terms vanish uniformly over
j
in
R_{n}

as
n
goes to
\infty
.
The concern is that our students,
at the beginning of their second year, have not yet mastered uniform convergence.

\bullet

Let us look at Feller’s book,
chapter VII, section 2: ”Orientation: Symmetric distribution”.
The proof seems less technical than the one of Breiman, with apparently no nasty computations.
Yet, upon further examination, a crucial step is the approximation presented on formula (2.3) page 180 of
[
5
]
,

(2)

1+\frac{j}{\nu}\,=\,e^{\frac{j}{\nu}+\cdots}\,,

where it is said that “the dots indicate terms which add to less than

{j}/{\nu^{2}}
.”
The rigorous justification comes at the end of the section.
It relies on the series expansion of the logarithm,
which is compared with an adequate geometric
series, in order to
control
the error in (
2
), uniformly over
j
much smaller than
\nu
.
The problem is that our students
have not yet learned the series expansions of classical functions.
At this point, it seems that the experience would be very unpleasant,
not only
for the students but also for the teacher,
who would find himself explaining a delicate new tool in the middle of an
already complicated proof.
Hence, our question remains unresolved :
Is it reasonable and possible to present a proof of the de Moivre–Laplace theorem
in the symmetric case to students who do not know series expansion nor uniform convergence ?

Our purpose here is to show that the answer is in fact positive.
We present a
variant of the proofs appearing in the books of Breiman and Feller,
in which the series expansions are replaced by the basic
convexity inequality

(3)

\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}}\qquad\exp(t)\geq 1+t\,.

This is absolutely fine, at least in France,
where
the exponential function
is initially introduced to the students as
the only function which is equal to its derivative and whose
value at
0
is
1
;
inequality (
3
) can be easily derived from the identity

(4)

\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}}\qquad\exp(t)-1\,=\,\int_{0}^{t}\exp(s)\,ds\,.

To sum up, the prerequisites necessary to confidently tackle the proof
that follows
are basic knowledge of integral calculus and the
following elementary inequalities:

(5)

\displaystyle\forall t\in[-1,1[\qquad\frac{1}{1-t}\,\geq\,1+t\,,

(6)

\displaystyle\forall t\in[0,1/2]\qquad\frac{1}{1-t}\,\leq\,1+2t\,.

These inequalities are straightforward.
We simply multiply by
1-t
, develop and remove
1
from each side.
The first one reduces to

0\geq-t^{2}
.
The second one reduces to

0\leq t(1-2t)
, which holds for
0\leq t\leq 1/2
.

With this background knowledge, we will prove completely
theorem
1
.
We start with de Moivre theorem
in section
2
, which is substantially simpler than the
general case
p\in]0,1[
.
For an educational presentation in class, our recommendation
is to explain the proof of the upper bound for a symmetric interval.
This corresponds to the first part of
section
2
,
that is approximately two pages, knowing that
we have written a detailed proof with all the intermediate steps.
This should fit into a one-hour lecture.
If one wishes
to go for the full proof, then one could add the proof of the
lower bound, which nevertheless has the same flavor as the
upper bound. However, the lower bound is slightly more difficult,
the inequalities are less straightforward than for the upper bound.
Within a two hours lecture, I could quietly present the theorem and its history,
state the general version of the central limit theorem and perform the detailed proof
of the upper bound in the symmetric case, and the students
(at least some of them) looked quite happy!

Modern expositions of the de Moivre–Laplace theorem usually makes
appeal to the famous Stirling formula (this is the case of both
Breiman and Feller
[
3
,
5
]
).
If we incorporate Stirling’s formula in our initial luggage, we
arrive faster at the desired conclusion. However, Stirling’s
formula is not necessary at all.
In fact,
a simpler result,
namely the formula for the Wallis integral, is more than enough
to complete our proof.
As a by product, we
obtain the following non-asymptotic inequality linking
the binomial and the Gaussian distributions:

(7)

\forall x>0\quad\forall n\geq\max(4x^{2},2)\qquad\cr\Big|P\Big(\big|S_{n}-\frac{n}{2}\big|<x{\frac{\sqrt{n}}{2}}\Big)-\int_{-x}^{x}e^{-\tfrac{s^{2}}{2}}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{{2}{\pi}}}\Big|\,\leq\,e^{\tfrac{4x^{3}+8}{\sqrt{n}}}-1\,.

The literature abounds with more sophisticated
inequalities linking the binomial and the Gaussian distributions,
see for instance
[
1
,
6
,
2
]
.
One of the most powerful is Tusnady’s inequality
(see
[
9
]
and the references therein).
Hipp and Mattner
[
6
]
provide lower and upper bounds for
the difference of the
distribution functions of the symmetric binomial and the Gaussian law
of the form
c/\sqrt{n}
, with optimal values for the constant
c
.
Yet the advantage of the inequality (
7
) lies
in the simplicity of its proof.

The proof of the general case
p\in]0,1[
is more involved, it
is done in section
3
.
We would not recommend presenting it in the classroom.
We include it here, because it
is simpler than the proofs we found in the existing literature: it does
not require a series expansion, and it relies on the same elementary
inequalities than we used for de Moivre theorem.
However, the use of these inequalities is more tricky,
because in the symmetric case there is a magical cancellation
which leads to a considerable simplification of the formulas.
Even if this material does not seem suitable for a first presentation
in class, the proof of the general case would provide an excellent topic
for a homework assignment,
allowing students to deepen and generalize the argument of the symmetric case.

2.
de Moivre theorem.

We consider here the symmetric case
p=1/2
, and also
the case of a symmetric interval.
Let
x>0
and let us set

(8)

\forall n\geq 1\qquad P_{n}(x)\,=\,P\Big(\big|S_{n}-\frac{n}{2}\big|<x{\frac{\sqrt{n}}{2}}\Big)\,.

We deal first with the case of even integers
n
,
which makes the formulas better looking.
We fix
n\geq 1
and we start by
writing down the expression of
P_{2n}(x)
:

(9)

\displaystyle P_{2n}(x)

\displaystyle\,=\,P\Big(\big|S_{2n}-{n}\big|<x\sqrt{\frac{n}{2}}\Big)

(10)

\displaystyle\,=\,\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\{\,0,\dots,2n\,\}\\
|k-n|<x\sqrt{{n}/2}\end{subarray}}P\big(S_{2n}=k\big)\,=\,\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\{\,0,\dots,2n\,\}\\
|k-n|<x\sqrt{{n}/2}\end{subarray}}\binom{2n}{k}\frac{1}{2^{2n}}\,.

We make the change of variable
j=k-n
, so that (
9
) becomes

(11)

P_{2n}(x)\,=\,\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in\{\,-n,\dots,n\,\}\\
|j|<x\sqrt{{n}/2}\end{subarray}}\binom{2n}{n+j}\frac{1}{2^{2n}}\,.

We take apart the term for
j=0
, and we use the symmetry of the
binomial coefficient to rewrite (
11
) as

(12)

P_{2n}(x)\,=\,\frac{1}{2^{2n}}\binom{2n}{n}+2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in\{\,1,\dots,n\,\}\\
j<x\sqrt{{n}/2}\end{subarray}}\binom{2n}{n+j}\frac{1}{2^{2n}}\,.

This formula is the starting point of the computations.
For
j\geq 1
, we have

(13)

\displaystyle(n+j)!

\displaystyle\,=\,n!\,(n+1)\cdots(n+j)\,,

(14)

\displaystyle(n-j)!

\displaystyle\,=\,\frac{n!}{(n-j+1)\cdots n}\,,

As in the classical arguments of Breiman and Feller,
we use (
13
) and (
14
)
to rewrite the binomial coefficient

\binom{2n}{n+j}

with the help of the central binomial coefficient

\binom{2n}{n}

as follows:

(15)

\binom{2n}{n+j}\,=\,\frac{(2n)!}{{(n+j)!}{(n-j)!}}\,=\,\binom{2n}{n}\frac{(n-j+1)\cdots n}{(n+1)\cdots(n+j)}\,.

We introduce next a notation for the product appearing in (
15
),

(16)

\forall j\geq 1\qquad\pi(j,n)\,=\,\frac{(n-j+1)\cdots n}{(n+1)\cdots(n+j)}\,=\,\prod_{1\leq\ell\leq j}\frac{n+\ell-j}{n+\ell}\,.

We set also
\pi(0,n)=1
.
With this new notation, the formula (
12
) becomes

(17)

P_{2n}(x)\,=\,P(S_{2n}=n)\Bigg(1+2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in\{\,1,\dots,n\,\}\\
j<x\sqrt{{n}/2}\end{subarray}}\pi(j,n)\Bigg)\,.

2.1.
Upper bound.

Let us fix
j\in\{\,1,\dots,n\,\}
. To prepare for the upper bound, we rewrite

\pi(j,n)
as follows:

(18)

\pi(j,n)\,=\,\prod_{1\leq\ell\leq j}\frac{n+\ell-j}{n+\ell}\,=\,\prod_{1\leq\ell\leq j}\Big(1-\frac{j}{n+\ell}\Big)\,,

and we use inequality (
3
)
to deduce from (
18
) that

(19)

\pi(j,n)\,\leq\,\exp\Big(-\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq j}\frac{j}{n+\ell}\Big)\,\leq\,\exp\Big(-\frac{j^{2}}{n+j}\Big)\,.

Next, we use the inequality (
5
)
to obtain

(20)

\frac{j^{2}}{n+j}\,=\,\frac{j^{2}}{n}\frac{1}{\displaystyle 1+\frac{j}{n}}\,\geq\,\frac{j^{2}}{n}\Big(1-\frac{j}{n}\Big)\,.

Substituting successively (
20
) in (
19
)
and (
18
), we conclude that

(21)

\pi(j,n)\,\leq\,\exp\Big(-\frac{j^{2}}{n}+\frac{j^{3}}{n^{2}}\Big)\,.

With the upper bound of
\pi(j,n)
in hand, we compute
an upper bound on

P_{2n}(x)
. Let us fix
x>0
and let
n
be an integer
such that
n\geq x^{2}
. This ensures that

(22)

1\leq j<x\sqrt{\frac{n}{2}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad{j}\leq n\,,\quad\frac{j^{3}}{n^{2}}\leq\frac{x^{3}}{2\sqrt{2n}}\,.

For these values of
j
, the inequality (
21
)
holds, and we have, thanks to (
22
),

(23)

1\leq j<x\sqrt{\frac{n}{2}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\pi(j,n)\,\leq\,\exp\Big(-\frac{j^{2}}{n}+\frac{x^{3}}{2\sqrt{2n}}\Big)\,.

Substituting inequality (
23
)
into the sum appearing in (
17
), we get

(24)

P_{2n}(x)\,\leq\,P(S_{2n}=n)\Bigg(1+2e^{\frac{x^{3}}{2\sqrt{2n}}}\sum_{1\leq j<x\sqrt{{n}/2}}\exp\Big(-\frac{j^{2}}{n}\Big)\Bigg)\,.

Since the function
\exp(-t)
is decreasing on
[0,+\infty[
, then

(25)

\forall j\geq 1\qquad\exp\Big(-\frac{j^{2}}{n}\Big)\,\leq\,\int_{j-1}^{j}e^{-\tfrac{t^{2}}{n}}dt\,.

Substituting inequality (
25
)
into the sum appearing in (
24
), we get

(26)

\displaystyle P_{2n}(x)

\displaystyle\,\leq\,P(S_{2n}=n)\Bigg(1+2e^{\frac{x^{3}}{2\sqrt{2n}}}\sum_{1\leq j<x\sqrt{{n}/2}}\int_{j-1}^{j}e^{-\tfrac{t^{2}}{n}}dt\Bigg)

(27)

\displaystyle\,\leq\,P(S_{2n}=n)\Bigg(1+2e^{\frac{x^{3}}{2\sqrt{2n}}}\int_{0}^{x\sqrt{{n}/2}}e^{-\tfrac{t^{2}}{n}}dt\Bigg)\,.

We make the change of variable

s{\sqrt{{n}/2}}=t
in (
26
) and the inequality becomes

(28)

P_{2n}(x)\,\leq\,P(S_{2n}=n)\Bigg(1+\sqrt{2{n}}\,e^{\frac{x^{3}}{2\sqrt{2n}}}\int_{0}^{x}e^{-\tfrac{s^{2}}{2}}ds\Bigg)\,.

We finally factorize
\sqrt{n}
, we use the symmetry of
the integrand, and we
introduce the convenient normalizing factor
\sqrt{2\pi}
:

(29)

P_{2n}(x)\,\leq\,\sqrt{{n}{\pi}}P(S_{2n}=n)\Bigg(\frac{1}{\sqrt{{n}{\pi}}}+e^{\frac{x^{3}}{2\sqrt{2n}}}\int_{-x}^{x}e^{-\tfrac{s^{2}}{2}}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{{2}{\pi}}}\Bigg)\,.

2.2.
Lower bound.

Let us fix
j\in\{\,1,\dots,n/2\,\}
. To prepare for the lower bound, we rewrite

\pi(j,n)
as follows:

(30)

\pi(j,n)\,=\,\prod_{1\leq\ell\leq j}\frac{n+\ell-j}{n+\ell}\,=\,\prod_{1\leq\ell\leq j}\frac{1}{\displaystyle 1+\frac{j}{n+\ell-j}}\,.

Notice that

(31)

\forall\ell\in{\{\,1,\dots,j\,\}}\qquad\frac{j}{n+\ell-j}\,\leq\,\frac{j}{n+1-j}\,\leq\,\frac{n/2}{n/2+1}\,<\,1\,.

The inequality (
3
) readily implies that

(32)

\forall t\in[0,1]\qquad\frac{1}{1+t}\,\geq\,\exp(-t)\,,

and we use this inequality
to
deduce from (
31
) and (
30
) that

(33)

\pi(j,n)\,\geq\,\prod_{1\leq\ell\leq j}\exp\Big(-\frac{j}{n+\ell-j}\Big)\,=\,\exp\Big(-\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq j}\frac{j}{n+\ell-j}\Big)\,.

Next,
we bound crudely from above the argument of the exponential :

(34)

\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq j}\frac{j}{n+\ell-j}\,\leq\,\frac{j^{2}}{n-j}\,,

and we use the inequality (
6
)
to obtain

(35)

\frac{j^{2}}{n-j}\,=\,\frac{j^{2}}{n}\frac{1}{\displaystyle 1-\frac{j}{n}}\,\leq\,\frac{j^{2}}{n}\Big(1+2\frac{j}{n}\Big)\,.

Substituting successively (
35
) in (
34
)
and (
33
), we conclude that

(36)

\pi(j,n)\,\geq\,\exp\Big(-\frac{j^{2}}{n}-2\frac{j^{3}}{n^{2}}\Big)\,.

Notice that Feller obtained the better inequality where the
2
in (
36
)
is replaced by
1
(see
[
5
]
, page 180,
between formulas
(2.3)
and
(2.4)
).
However, his computation relies on a series
expansion and a comparison with an adequate geometric series.
For the upper bound, we obtained the inequality (
21
),
which is the same as the one of Feller.

With the lower bound of
\pi(j,n)
in hand, we compute
a lower bound on

P_{2n}(x)
. Let us fix
x>0
and let
n
be an integer
such that
n\geq 2x^{2}
. This ensures that

(37)

1\leq j<x\sqrt{\frac{n}{2}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad{j}\leq\frac{n}{2}\,,\quad\frac{j^{3}}{n^{2}}\leq\frac{x^{3}}{2\sqrt{2n}}\,.

Using the inequalities (
36
) and (
37
)
into the sum appearing in (
17
), we get

(38)

\displaystyle P_{2n}(x)

\displaystyle\,\geq\,P(S_{2n}=n)\Bigg(1+2\sum_{1\leq j<x\sqrt{{n}/2}}\exp\Big(-\frac{j^{2}}{n}-\frac{x^{3}}{\sqrt{2n}}\Big)\Bigg)

(39)

\displaystyle\,\geq\,P(S_{2n}=n)\Bigg(2e^{-\frac{x^{3}}{\sqrt{2n}}}\sum_{0\leq j<x\sqrt{{n}/2}}\exp\Big(-\frac{j^{2}}{n}\Big)-1\Bigg)\,.

Since the function
\exp(-t)
is decreasing on
[0,+\infty[
, then

(40)

\forall j\geq 0\qquad\exp\Big(-\frac{j^{2}}{n}\Big)\,\geq\,\int_{j}^{j+1}e^{-\tfrac{t^{2}}{n}}dt\,.

Substituting inequality (
40
)
into the sum appearing in (
38
), we get

(41)

\displaystyle P_{2n}(x)

\displaystyle\,\geq\,P(S_{2n}=n)\Bigg(2e^{-\frac{x^{3}}{\sqrt{2n}}}\sum_{0\leq j<x\sqrt{{n}/2}}\int_{j}^{j+1}e^{-\tfrac{t^{2}}{n}}dt-1\Bigg)

(42)

\displaystyle\,\geq\,P(S_{2n}=n)\Bigg(2e^{-\frac{x^{3}}{\sqrt{2n}}}\int_{0}^{x\sqrt{{n}/2}}e^{-\tfrac{t^{2}}{n}}dt-1\Bigg)

(43)

\displaystyle\,\geq\,\sqrt{{n}{\pi}}P(S_{2n}=n)\Bigg(e^{-\frac{x^{3}}{\sqrt{2n}}}\int_{-x}^{x}e^{-\tfrac{s^{2}}{2}}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{{2}{\pi}}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{{n}{\pi}}}\Bigg)\,,

where we have made the same final steps as in the upper bound
to get the last formula, namely
the change of variable

s{\sqrt{{n}/2}}=t
, the use of symmetry of the integrand and
factorization of
\sqrt{n\pi}
.

2.3.
Conclusion.

It follows from Stirling’s formula that

(44)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sqrt{{n}{\pi}}P(S_{2n}=n)\,=\,1\,.

Using (
44
) and passing to the limit in
the inequalities (
29
) and (
41
), we conclude that

(45)

\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{2n}(x)\,=\,\int_{-x}^{x}e^{-\tfrac{s^{2}}{2}}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{{2}{\pi}}}\,.

2.4.
The case of odd integers.

So far, we have dealt
only with the subsequence of the even integers.
For the odd integers, we could proceed as Breiman to show that
the infimum limit and the supremum limit of
the sequence
(P_{n}(x))_{n\geq 0}

coincide along the even and odd integers.
Or we could simply say that
the previous inequalities can be adapted with minor modifications
to handle the case of odd integers. However, this would be a bit unfair,
because there do exist differences between the two cases,
and the work has to be done if one really wishes to obtain the
correct inequalities. So we present next an accelerated version
of the derivation of these inequalities, which rests nevertheless
on the inequalities derived in the even case.

Let us fix
n\geq 0
. We have

(46)

P_{2n+1}(x)\,=\,P\Big(\big|S_{2n+1}-\frac{2n+1}{2}\big|<\frac{x}{2}\sqrt{2n+1}\Big)\cr\,=\,\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\{\,0,\dots,2n+1\,\}\\
|k-\frac{2n+1}{2}|<\frac{x}{2}\sqrt{2n+1}\end{subarray}}P\big(S_{2n+1}=k\big)\,=\,\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\{\,0,\dots,2n+1\,\}\\
|k-n-\frac{1}{2}|<\frac{x}{2}\sqrt{2n+1}\end{subarray}}\binom{2n+1}{k}\frac{1}{2^{2n+1}}\cr\,=\,\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\{\,0,\dots,n\,\}\\
n+\frac{1}{2}-k<\frac{x}{2}\sqrt{2n+1}\end{subarray}}\binom{2n+1}{k}\frac{1}{2^{2n+1}}\,+\,\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\{\,n+1,\dots,2n+1\,\}\\
k-n-\frac{1}{2}<\frac{x}{2}\sqrt{2n+1}\end{subarray}}\binom{2n+1}{k}\frac{1}{2^{2n+1}}\,.

We perform the change of variables
j=n-k
in the first sum and

j=k-n-1
in the second sum.
Using the symmetry of the
binomial coefficient, we see that the two sums are in fact equal and we get

(47)

P_{2n+1}(x)\,=\,2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in\{\,0,\dots,n\,\}\\
j+\frac{1}{2}<\frac{x}{2}\sqrt{2n+1}\end{subarray}}\binom{2n+1}{n-j}\frac{1}{2^{2n+1}}\,.

This formula is the analog for the odd case of formula (
12
). Notice that
we did not have to isolate the central term, because when
n
is even,
the symmetric binomial distribution
possesses two central terms, which are naturally incorporated in the sum.
We rewrite next the binomial coefficient

\smash{\binom{2n+1}{n-j}}

with the help of the central binomial coefficient

\smash{\binom{2n+1}{n}}

as follows:

(48)

\binom{2n+1}{n-j}\,=\,\frac{(2n+1)!}{(n-j)!{(n+1+j)!}}\,=\,\binom{2n+1}{n}\frac{(n-j+1)\cdots n}{(n+2)\cdots(n+j+1)}\,.

We define

(49)

\forall j\geq 0\qquad{\widetilde{\pi}}(j,n)\,=\,\frac{(n-j+1)\cdots n}{(n+2)\cdots(n+j+1)}\,=\,\prod_{1\leq\ell\leq j}\frac{n+\ell-j}{n+\ell+1}\,,

and the formula (
47
) becomes

(50)

P_{2n+1}(x)\,=\,2P(S_{2n+1}=n)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in\{\,0,\dots,n\,\}\\
j+\frac{1}{2}<\frac{x}{2}\sqrt{2n+1}\end{subarray}}{\widetilde{\pi}}(j,n)\,.

We could then proceed as in the even case to bound

{\widetilde{\pi}}(j,n)
from above and from below.
Instead, we notice that

(51)

\forall j\in\{\,0,\dots,n\,\}\qquad{\widetilde{\pi}}(j,n)\,=\,\frac{n+1}{n+j+1}\pi(j,n)\,,

and we take advantage of the work already done on
\pi(j,n)
:
the inequalities (
21
),(
36
)
and the identity (
51
) yield that,
for
j\in\{\,1,\dots,n/2\,\}
,

(52)

\frac{n+1}{n+j+1}\exp\Big(-\frac{j^{2}}{n}-2\frac{j^{3}}{n^{2}}\Big)\,\leq\,{\widetilde{\pi}}(j,n)\,\leq\,\exp\Big(-\frac{j^{2}}{n}+\frac{j^{3}}{n^{2}}\Big)\,.

Let us fix
x>0
and let
n
be an integer
such that
n\geq x^{2}
.
Setting

(53)

x_{n}\,=\,{\frac{x}{2}\sqrt{2n+1}}-\frac{1}{2}\,,

we have, thanks to the concavity of the square root function,

(54)

x_{n}\,\leq\,x\sqrt{\frac{n}{2}}\Big(1+\frac{1}{4n}\Big)-\frac{1}{2}\,\leq\,x\sqrt{\frac{n}{2}}+\frac{x}{\sqrt{32n}}-\frac{1}{2}\,<\,x\sqrt{\frac{n}{2}}\,.

It follows from (
53
) and (
54
) that

(55)

0<j+\frac{1}{2}<\frac{x}{2}\sqrt{2n+1}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad 0\leq j<x\sqrt{\frac{n}{2}}\,.

Thus we can use (
22
) to conclude from (
52
) that,
for the indices
j
appearing in the sum (
50
), we have

(56)

\frac{n}{n+x\sqrt{n}}\exp\Big(-\frac{j^{2}}{n}-\frac{x^{3}}{\sqrt{2n}}\Big)\,\leq\,{\widetilde{\pi}}(j,n)\,\leq\,\exp\Big(-\frac{j^{2}}{n}+\frac{x^{3}}{2\sqrt{2n}}\Big)\,.

Substituting inequalities (
56
)
into the sum (
50
),
and performing the same steps as for the even case, we get

(57)

e^{-\frac{x^{3}}{\sqrt{2n}}}\int_{0}^{x_{n}+1}e^{-\tfrac{t^{2}}{n}}dt\,\leq\,\frac{P_{2n+1}(x)}{2P(S_{2n+1}=n)}\,\leq\,1+e^{\frac{x^{3}}{2\sqrt{2n}}}\int_{0}^{x_{n}}e^{-\tfrac{t^{2}}{n}}dt\,.

We see from (
53
) that

x_{n}+1\geq x\sqrt{{n}/{2}}
.
We use also the inequality (
54
),
we make the change of variable

s{\sqrt{{n}/2}}=t
in (
57
) and we get

(58)

e^{-\frac{x^{3}}{\sqrt{2n}}}\sqrt{\frac{n}{2}}\int_{0}^{x}e^{-\tfrac{s^{2}}{2}}ds\,\leq\,\frac{P_{2n+1}(x)}{2P(S_{2n+1}=n)}\,\leq\,1+\sqrt{\frac{n}{2}}e^{\frac{x^{3}}{2\sqrt{2n}}}\int_{0}^{x}e^{-\tfrac{s^{2}}{2}}ds\,.

We finally factorize
\sqrt{n}
, we use the symmetry of
the integrand, and we
introduce the convenient normalizing factor
\sqrt{2\pi}
:

(59)

e^{-\frac{x^{3}}{\sqrt{2n}}}\int_{-x}^{x}e^{-\tfrac{s^{2}}{2}}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{{2}{\pi}}}ds\,\leq\,\frac{P_{2n+1}(x)}{{\sqrt{{n}{\pi}}}P(S_{2n+1}=n)}\,\leq\,\frac{2}{\sqrt{n}}+e^{\frac{x^{3}}{2\sqrt{2n}}}\int_{-x}^{x}e^{-\tfrac{s^{2}}{2}}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{{2}{\pi}}}\,.

The inequalities (
59
) readily imply that

(60)

\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{2n+1}(x)\,=\,\int_{-x}^{x}e^{-\tfrac{s^{2}}{2}}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{{2}{\pi}}}\,,

and it follows finally from (
45
) and (
60
) that

(61)

\lim_{n\to\infty}P\Big(\big|S_{n}-\frac{n}{2}\big|<x{\frac{\sqrt{n}}{2}}\Big)\,=\,\int_{-x}^{x}e^{-\tfrac{s^{2}}{2}}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{{2}{\pi}}}\,.

It is then an easy matter to obtain the result
for an arbitrary interval: just apply the previous result to two intervals

[-x,x]
and
[-y,y]
, with
x<y
,
subtract the two results and use the symmetry of
the binomial and Gaussian distributions.

2.5.
Avoiding Stirling’s formula.

Let us discuss now the role played by Stirling’s formula.
In the previous argument, it is used only to prove the limit (
44
).
However this limit can be proved directly with the help of
the Wallis integrals. These are defined as

(62)

\forall n\geq 0\qquad W_{n}\,=\,\int_{0}^{\pi/2}(\sin t)^{n}\,dt\,.

Let us recall some classical facts on the sequence
(W_{n})_{n\geq 0}
.
Since the sinus takes its values
in
[0,1]
on the interval
[0,\pi/2]
,
then

(63)

\forall n\geq 1\qquad W_{n+1}\,\leq\,W_{n}\,\leq\,W_{n-1}\,.

An integration by parts yields the classical recurrence formula

(64)

\forall n\geq 2\qquad nW_{n}\,=\,(n-1)W_{n-2}\,.

Multiplying (
64
) by
W_{n-1}
,
we obtained the remarkable fact that

(65)

\forall n\geq 2\qquad nW_{n}W_{n-1}\,=\,W_{1}W_{0}\,=\,\frac{\pi}{2}\,.

We can compute the exact values of the integrals
by iterating formula (
64
):

(66)

\displaystyle W_{2n}\,=\,\frac{(2n-1)\times\cdots\times 1}{(2n)\times\cdots\times 2}\frac{\pi}{2}\,,

(67)

\displaystyle W_{2n+1}\,=\,\frac{(2n)\times\cdots\times 2}{(2n+1)\times\cdots\times 3}\,.

It follows also from (
64
) that

W_{n+1}\sim W_{n-1}
as
n
goes to
\infty
. This fact,
combined with the inequalities (
63
), yields that

W_{n}\sim W_{n+1}
as
n
goes to
\infty
.
It follows then from (
65
) that

n(W_{n})^{2}\sim\pi/2
, whence

(68)

W_{n}\,\sim\,\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2n}}\quad\text{as}\quad n\to\infty\,.

The Wallis integrals are usually employed to prove
Stirling’s formula.
In a first step,
one proves that
n!\sim C(n/e)^{n}\sqrt{n}
, where
C

is a positive constant. In a second step, one inserts
this estimate in the formula for
W_{2n}
and uses the
estimate (
68
) to conclude that
C=\sqrt{2\pi}
.
However, if one only wishes to prove the
limit (
44
),
then these two steps,
and in fact all the asymptotic computations of the equivalents,
can be completely bypassed.
The trick consists in rewriting the formulas (
66
) and (
67
)
as product of factorials:

(69)

W_{2n}\,=\,\frac{(2n)!}{2^{2n}(n!)^{2}}\frac{\pi}{2}\,,\qquad W_{2n+1}\,=\,\frac{1}{2n+1}\frac{2^{2n}(n!)^{2}}{(2n)!}\,.

The central probability
P(S_{2n}=n)
pops up naturally and we see that

(70)

W_{2n}\,=\,P(S_{2n}=n)\frac{\pi}{2}\,,\qquad W_{2n+1}\,=\,\frac{1}{(2n+1)P(S_{2n}=n)}\,.

From there, we could easily deduce the limit (
44
)
from the asymptotic equivalent (
68
).
However, we can do it even more simply and efficiently. Indeed,
let us look
at the ratio

W_{2n}/W_{2n+1}
.
On one hand,
from the expressions (
70
), we have

(71)

\frac{W_{2n}}{W_{2n+1}}\,=\,\Big(n+\frac{1}{2}\Big)\pi\big(P(S_{2n}=n)\big)^{2}\,.

On the other hand, it follows from the inequalities (
63
)
and the recurrence relation (
64
) that

(72)

1\,\leq\,\frac{W_{2n}}{W_{2n+1}}\,\leq\,\frac{W_{2n-1}}{W_{2n+1}}\,=\,\frac{{2n+1}}{{2n}}\,=\,1+\frac{{1}}{{2n}}\,.

Combining (
71
) and (
72
), we conclude that,
for any
n\geq 1
,

(73)

1\,\leq\,\sqrt{\Big(n+\frac{1}{2}\Big)\pi}P(S_{2n}=n)\,\leq\,\sqrt{1+\frac{{1}}{{2n}}}\,,

which we rewrite in the more convenient form

(74)

\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{2n}}}\,\leq\,\sqrt{n\pi}P(S_{2n}=n)\,\leq\,1\,.

The inequalities (
74
) readily yield the desired limit (
44
).
For the odd integers,
we notice that

(75)

P(S_{2n+1}=n)\,=\,\frac{2n+1}{2n+2}P(S_{2n}=n)\,,

and we deduce from (
74
) that

(76)

\frac{1}{\Big({1+\frac{1}{2n}}\Big)^{3/2}}\,\leq\,\sqrt{n\pi}P(S_{2n+1}=n)\,\leq\,1\,.

Sending
n
to
\infty
in (
76
), we obtain
the counterpart
of the limit (
44
)
for the odd integers.

2.6.
Non-asymptotic inequalities.

One merit of our proof is that it produces interesting
non-asymptotic inequalities.
We restate next these inequalities, and we combine them with the
inequalities coming from the Wallis integrals.
Let
n\geq 1
and
x>0
be such that
n\geq x^{2}
.
Inequalities (
29
) and (
74
) yield

(77)

\displaystyle P\Big(\big|S_{2n}-{n}\big|<x\sqrt{\frac{n}{2}}\Big)

\displaystyle\,\leq\,e^{\frac{x^{3}}{2\sqrt{2n}}}\int_{-x}^{x}e^{-\tfrac{s^{2}}{2}}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{{2}{\pi}}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{{n\pi}}}

(78)

\displaystyle\,\leq\,\int_{-x}^{x}e^{-\tfrac{s^{2}}{2}}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{{2}{\pi}}}+e^{\frac{x^{3}}{2\sqrt{2n}}}-1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{{n\pi}}}\,,

because the integral is less than
1
.
Using inequality (
3
)
with
t=1/\sqrt{n\pi}
,
we have

(79)

e^{\frac{x^{3}}{2\sqrt{2n}}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{{n\pi}}}\,\leq\,e^{\frac{x^{3}}{2\sqrt{2n}}}\Big(1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{{n\pi}}}\Big)\,\leq\,e^{\frac{x^{3}}{2\sqrt{2n}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{{n\pi}}}}\,\leq\,e^{\frac{x^{3}+2}{2\sqrt{2n}}}\,.

Combining the inequalities (
77
) and (
79
),
we obtain

(80)

\forall x>0\quad\forall n\geq\max(x^{2},1)\qquad\cr P\Big(\big|S_{2n}-{n}\big|<x\sqrt{\frac{n}{2}}\Big)\,\leq\,\int_{-x}^{x}e^{-\tfrac{s^{2}}{2}}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{{2}{\pi}}}+e^{\frac{x^{3}+2}{2\sqrt{2n}}}-1\,.

We try now to derive a similar lower bound.
Let
n\geq 1
and
x>0
be such that
n\geq 2x^{2}
.
Inequalities (
41
) and (
74
) yield

(81)

\displaystyle P\Big(\big|S_{2n}-{n}\big|<x\sqrt{\frac{n}{2}}\Big)

\displaystyle\,\geq\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{2n}}}\Bigg(e^{-\frac{x^{3}}{\sqrt{2n}}}\int_{-x}^{x}e^{-\tfrac{s^{2}}{2}}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{{2}{\pi}}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{{n}{\pi}}}\Bigg)

(82)

\displaystyle\,\geq\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{2n}}}e^{-\frac{x^{3}}{\sqrt{2n}}}\int_{-x}^{x}e^{-\tfrac{s^{2}}{2}}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{{2}{\pi}}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{{2n}}}\,.

Applying the inequality (
3
)
with
t=1/(2n)
,
we have

(83)

\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{2n}}}e^{-\frac{x^{3}}{\sqrt{2n}}}\,\geq\,e^{-\frac{{1}}{{4n}}-\frac{x^{3}}{\sqrt{2n}}}\,.

Substituting (
83
) into (
81
),
and using the fact that
the integral is less than
1
,
we obtain

(84)

P\Big(\big|S_{2n}-{n}\big|<x\sqrt{\frac{n}{2}}\Big)\,\geq\,\int_{-x}^{x}e^{-\tfrac{s^{2}}{2}}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{{2}{\pi}}}+e^{-\frac{{1}}{{4n}}-\frac{x^{3}}{\sqrt{2n}}}-1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{{2n}}}\,.

Moreover, we have,
thanks to inequality (
3
) applied
with
t=1/\sqrt{2n}
,

(85)

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{{1}}{{4n}}-\frac{x^{3}}{\sqrt{2n}}}-1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{{2n}}}

\displaystyle\,\geq\,e^{-\frac{{1}}{{4n}}-\frac{x^{3}}{\sqrt{2n}}}-e^{\frac{1}{\sqrt{{2n}}}}

(86)

\displaystyle\,\geq\,1-e^{\frac{{1}}{{4n}}+\frac{x^{3}}{\sqrt{2n}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{{2n}}}}\,\geq\,1-e^{\frac{x^{3}+2}{\sqrt{n}}}\,.

We conclude from (
84
) and (
85
) that

(87)

\forall x>0\quad\forall n\geq\max(2x^{2},1)\qquad\cr P\Big(\big|S_{2n}-{n}\big|<x\sqrt{\frac{n}{2}}\Big)\,\geq\,\int_{-x}^{x}e^{-\tfrac{s^{2}}{2}}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{{2}{\pi}}}+1-e^{\frac{x^{3}+2}{\sqrt{n}}}\,.

We can finally combine
the inequalities (
80
)
and (
87
) into a single inequality:

(88)

\forall x>0\quad\forall n\geq\max(2x^{2},1)\qquad\cr\Big|P\Big(\big|S_{2n}-{n}\big|<x\sqrt{\frac{n}{2}}\Big)-\int_{-x}^{x}e^{-\tfrac{s^{2}}{2}}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{{2}{\pi}}}\Big|\,\leq\,e^{\tfrac{x^{3}+2}{\sqrt{n}}}-1\,.

For the odd integers,
with the help of the inequalities (
59
)
and (
76
),
we obtain the following similar inequality (the details are omitted):

(89)

\forall x>0\quad\forall n\geq\max(2x^{2},1)\qquad\cr\Big|P\Big(\big|S_{2n+1}-\frac{2n+1}{2}\big|<\frac{x}{2}\sqrt{2n+1}\Big)-\int_{-x}^{x}e^{-\tfrac{s^{2}}{2}}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{{2}{\pi}}}\Big|\,\leq\,e^{\tfrac{x^{3}+2}{\sqrt{n}}}-1\,.

Combining the inequalities (
88
) and (
89
),
we finally obtain the inequality (
7
) stated in the
introduction.

3.
de Moivre–Laplace theorem.

We consider
the general case
p\in]0,1[
.
The nice symmetries of the case
p=1/2
disappear.
To start the computations, we follow the path laid out
by Feller
(see
[
5
]
, section VII.3, pages 182 and 183).
First we set
q=1-p
, and we introduce the index
m
of the central
term of the binomial distribution
B(n,p)
as the unique
integer
m
such that

(90)

m=np+\delta\,,\qquad-q<\delta\leq p\,.

Let
x>0
and let us define

(91)

\forall n\geq 1\qquad P_{n}(x)\,=\,P\big(m\leq S_{n}<m+x{{\sqrt{npq}}}\big)\,.

We fix
n\geq 1
and we start by
writing down the expression of
P_{n}(x)
:

(92)

P_{n}(x)\,=\,\kern-10.0pt\sum_{{m\leq k<m+x\sqrt{{npq}}}}\kern-15.0ptP\big(S_{n}=k\big)\,=\,\kern-10.0pt\sum_{{m\leq k<m+x\sqrt{{npq}}}}\binom{n}{k}p^{k}q^{n-k}\,.

We make the change of variable
j=k-m
, so that (
92
) becomes

(93)

P_{n}(x)\,=\,\sum_{{0\leq j<x\sqrt{{npq}}}}\binom{n}{m+j}p^{m+j}q^{n-m-j}\,.

We proceed as in the symmetric case. The first step consists
in rewriting the binomial coefficient

\binom{n}{m+j}
as the product of

\binom{n}{m}
and a fraction.
For
j\geq 1
, we have

(94)

\displaystyle(m+j)!

\displaystyle\,=\,m!\,(m+1)\cdots(m+j)\,,

(95)

\displaystyle(n-m-j)!

\displaystyle\,=\,\frac{(n-m)!}{(n-m)\cdots(n-m-j+1)}\,,

whence

(96)

\binom{n}{m+j}\,=\,\frac{n!}{{(m+j)!}{(n-m-j)!}}\,=\,\binom{n}{m}\frac{(n-m)\cdots(n-m-j+1)}{(m+1)\cdots(m+j)}\,.

We introduce next a notation for the product appearing in (
96
),
and we incorporate as well the factor
(p/q)^{j}
coming from (
93
) :

(97)

\forall j\geq 1\qquad\pi(j)\,=\,\frac{(n-m)\cdots(n-m-j+1)}{(m+1)\cdots(m+j)}\Big(\frac{p}{q}\Big)^{j}\,=\,\prod_{1\leq\ell\leq j}\frac{(n-m-\ell+1)p}{(m+\ell)q}\,.

We set also
\pi(0)=1
.
With this notation, the formula (
93
) can be rewritten as

(98)

P_{n}(x)\,=\,P(S_{n}=m)\sum_{{0\leq j<x\sqrt{{npq}}}}\pi(j)\,.

We focus next on the product
\pi(j)
. Replacing
m
by
np+\delta
,
we obtain the following expression:

(99)

\forall j\geq 1\qquad\pi(j)\,=\,\prod_{1\leq\ell\leq j}\frac{npq-(\delta+\ell-1)p}{npq+(\delta+\ell)q}\,.

3.1.
Upper bound.

Let us fix
j\in\{\,0,\dots,n\,\}
. To prepare for the upper bound, we rewrite

\pi(j)
as follows:

(100)

\pi(j)\,=\,\prod_{1\leq\ell\leq j}\Big(1-\frac{\delta+\ell-p}{npq+(\delta+\ell)q}\Big)\,,

and we use inequality (
3
)
to deduce from (
100
) that

(101)

\pi(j)\,\leq\,\exp\Big(-\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq j}\frac{\delta+\ell-p}{npq+(\delta+\ell)q}\Big)\cr\,\leq\,\exp\Big(-\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq j}\frac{\ell-1}{npq+pq+jq}\Big)\,=\,\exp\Big(-\frac{j(j-1)}{2\big((n+1)pq+jq\big)}\Big)\,.

Let us remark that the previous computations are more complicated
than the ones we did in the symmetric case, not only because of the
presence of the parameters
p,q
, but because there is a specific
cancellation when
p=q
.
Indeed, suppose that we try to proceed exactly as in the symmetric case.
Instead of
formulas (
99
)
and (
100
), we would start from the
equivalent formula where
\ell

is changed into
j-\ell+1
in the numerator, and we would get:

(102)

\pi(j)\,=\,\prod_{1\leq\ell\leq j}\frac{npq-(\delta+j-\ell)p}{npq+(\delta+\ell)q}\,=\,\prod_{1\leq\ell\leq j}\Big(1-\frac{\delta+\ell(q-p)+jp}{npq+(\delta+\ell)q}\Big)\,.

In the symmetric case, the term
\ell(q-p)
in the last expression vanished,
and this leads to a simpler upper bound. In the non-symmetric case, one has
to go through an intermediate
exponential upper bound and perform the summation over
\ell

in the argument of the exponential, as we did in (
101
).
Moreover, these steps are more transparent when starting from
formula (
100
) than with formula (
102
), and to
tell the truth,
the product in the numerator of
formula (
99
) was
already written in reverse order
compared to (
16
).

Coming back to (
101
),
we use inequality (
5
)
to obtain that, for
0\leq j\leq(n+1)p
,

(103)

\frac{j(j-1)}{2\big((n+1)pq+jq\big)}\,=\,\frac{j(j-1)}{2(n+1)pq}\frac{1}{1+\frac{j}{(n+1)p}}\cr\,\geq\,\frac{j(j-1)}{2(n+1)pq}\Big(1-\frac{j}{(n+1)p}\Big)\,\geq\,\frac{(j-1)^{2}}{2(n+1)pq}-\frac{j^{3}}{2n^{2}p^{2}q}\,.

Substituting (
103
) in (
101
),
we conclude that

(104)

\forall j\,,\quad 0\leq j\leq(n+1)p\,,\qquad\pi(j)\,\leq\,\exp\Big(-\frac{(j-1)^{2}}{2(n+1)pq}+\frac{j^{3}}{2n^{2}p^{2}q}\Big)\,.

With the upper bound of
\pi(j)
in hand, we will compute
an upper bound on

P_{n}(x)
. Let us fix
x>0
and let
n
be an integer
such that
n\geq(q/p)x^{2}
. This ensures that

(105)

{{0\leq j<x\sqrt{{npq}}}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad{j}\leq np\,,\quad\frac{j^{3}}{2n^{2}p^{2}q}\leq\frac{x^{3}}{\sqrt{{pn}}}\,.

In particular, for these values of
j
, the inequality (
104
)
holds as well, whence

(106)

{{0\leq j<x\sqrt{{npq}}}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\pi(j)\,\leq\,\exp\Big(-\frac{(j-1)^{2}}{2(n+1)pq}+\frac{x^{3}}{\sqrt{{pn}}}\Big)\,.

Substituting inequality (
106
)
into the sum appearing in (
98
), we get

(107)

P_{n}(x)\,\leq\,P(S_{n}=m)\sum_{{0\leq j<x\sqrt{{npq}}}}\exp\Big(-\frac{(j-1)^{2}}{2(n+1)pq}+\frac{x^{3}}{\sqrt{{pn}}}\Big)\,.

Since the function
\exp(-t)
is decreasing on
[0,+\infty[
, then

(108)

\forall j\geq 2\qquad\exp\Big(-\frac{(j-1)^{2}}{2(n+1)pq}\Big)\,\leq\,\int_{j-2}^{j-1}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2(n+1)pq}}dt\,.

We sum the inequality (
108
) over
j

such that
0\leq j<x\sqrt{{npq}}
, and we bound
the first two terms by
1
:

(109)

\sum_{{0\leq j<x\sqrt{{npq}}}}\exp\Big(-\frac{(j-1)^{2}}{2(n+1)pq}\Big)\,\leq\,2+\int_{0}^{x\sqrt{{npq}}}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2(n+1)pq}}\,dt\,.

Substituting inequality (
109
)
into (
107
), we get

(110)

P_{n}(x)\,\leq\,P(S_{n}=m)\,e^{\frac{x^{3}}{\sqrt{{pn}}}}\Bigg(2+\int_{0}^{x\sqrt{{npq}}}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2(n+1)pq}}\,dt\Bigg)\,.

We make the change of variable

s{\sqrt{{(n+1)pq}}}=t
in (
110
) and the inequality becomes

(111)

P_{n}(x)\,\leq\,P(S_{n}=m)\,e^{\frac{x^{3}}{\sqrt{{pn}}}}\Bigg(2+\sqrt{{(n+1)pq}}\int_{0}^{x}e^{-\frac{s^{2}}{2}}\,ds\Bigg)\,.

We finally factorize

\sqrt{{npq}}
,
we
introduce the normalizing factor
\sqrt{2\pi}

and we use the inequalities

(112)

\sqrt{{1+\frac{1}{n}}}\,\leq\,1+\frac{1}{2n}\,\leq\,{e^{\frac{1}{n}}}\,\leq\,e^{\frac{1}{\sqrt{{pn}}}}

to get

(113)

P_{n}(x)\,\leq\,\sqrt{{2\pi npq}}\,P(S_{n}=m)\,e^{\frac{x^{3}+1}{\sqrt{{pn}}}}\Bigg(\frac{1}{\sqrt{{npq}}}+\int_{0}^{x}e^{-\frac{s^{2}}{2}}\,dt\frac{ds}{\sqrt{{2\pi}}}\Bigg)\,.

3.2.
Lower bound.

Let us fix
j\in\{\,0,\dots,n\,\}
. To prepare for the lower bound, we rewrite

\pi(j)
as follows:

(114)

\pi(j)\,=\,\prod_{1\leq\ell\leq j}\frac{npq-(\delta+\ell-1)p}{npq+(\delta+\ell)q}\,\geq\,\prod_{1\leq\ell\leq j}\frac{npq-\ell p+pq}{npq+\ell q+pq}\cr\,\geq\,\prod_{1\leq\ell\leq j}\frac{(n+1)pq-\ell p}{(n+1)pq+\ell q}\,\geq\,\prod_{1\leq\ell\leq j}\frac{1}{1+\frac{\ell}{(n+1)pq-\ell p}}\,.

We apply next the inequality (
32
).
For
j
such that

0\leq j\leq(n+1)pq/2
, we have

(115)

\displaystyle{(n+1)pq-jp}\,\geq\,(n+1)pq\Big(1-\frac{p}{2}\Big)\,\geq\,\frac{1}{2}(n+1)pq\,\geq\,0\,,

(116)

\displaystyle\frac{j}{(n+1)pq-jp}\,\leq\,1\,,

whence

(117)

\pi(j)\,\geq\,\exp\Big(-\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq j}\frac{\ell}{(n+1)pq-\ell p}\Big)\cr\,\geq\,\exp\Big(-\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq j}\frac{\ell}{(n+1)pq-jp}\Big)\,\geq\,\exp\Big(-\frac{j(j+1)}{2\big((n+1)pq-jp\big)}\Big)\,.

Next, we use the inequality (
6
)
to obtain that, for
0\leq j\leq(n+1)pq/2
,

(118)

\frac{j(j+1)}{2\big((n+1)pq-jp\big)}\,=\,\frac{j(j+1)}{2(n+1)pq}\frac{1}{1-\frac{j}{(n+1)q}}\cr\,\leq\,\frac{j(j+1)}{2(n+1)pq}\Big(1+\frac{2j}{(n+1)q}\Big)\,\leq\,\frac{(j+1)^{2}}{2npq}+\frac{(j+1)^{3}}{n^{2}pq^{2}}\,.

Substituting (
118
) in (
117
),
we conclude that

(119)

\forall j\,,\quad 0\leq j\leq\frac{1}{2}(n+1)pq\,,\qquad\pi(j)\,\geq\,\exp\Big(-\frac{(j+1)^{2}}{2npq}-\frac{(j+1)^{3}}{n^{2}pq^{2}}\Big)\,.

With the lower bound of
\pi(j)
in hand, we will compute
a lower bound on

P_{n}(x)
. Let us fix
x>0
and let
n
be an integer
such that

x\sqrt{{npq}}\geq\max(1,2x^{2})
.
This ensures that

(120)

{{0\leq j<x\sqrt{{npq}}}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad j\leq\frac{1}{2}(n+1)pq\,,\quad\frac{(j+1)^{3}}{n^{2}pq^{2}}\leq\frac{8x^{3}}{\sqrt{{qn}}}\,.

In particular, for these values of
j
, the inequality (
119
)
holds, thus

(121)

\pi(j)\,\geq\,\exp\Big(-\frac{(j+1)^{2}}{2npq}-\frac{8x^{3}}{\sqrt{{qn}}}\Big)\,\geq\,e^{-\frac{8x^{3}}{\sqrt{{qn}}}}\int_{j+1}^{j+2}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2npq}}\,dt\,.

Substituting inequality (
121
)
into the sum appearing in (
98
), we get

(122)

P_{n}(x)\,\geq\,P(S_{n}=m)\,e^{-\frac{8x^{3}}{\sqrt{{qn}}}}\int_{1}^{x\sqrt{{npq}}+1}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2npq}}\,dt\cr\,\geq\,P(S_{n}=m)\,e^{-\frac{8x^{3}}{\sqrt{{qn}}}}\Bigg(\int_{0}^{x\sqrt{{npq}}}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2npq}}\,dt-1\Bigg)\,.

We make the change of variable

s{\sqrt{{npq}}}=t
in (
122
) and we get

(123)

P_{n}(x)\,\geq\,P(S_{n}=m)\,e^{-\frac{8x^{3}}{\sqrt{{qn}}}}\Bigg(\sqrt{{npq}}\int_{0}^{x}e^{-\frac{s^{2}}{2}}\,ds-1\Bigg)\,.

We factorize

\sqrt{{npq}}
,
we
introduce the normalizing factor
\sqrt{2\pi}
, and we obtain

(124)

P_{n}(x)\,\geq\,\sqrt{{2\pi npq}}\,P(S_{n}=m)\,e^{-\frac{8x^{3}}{\sqrt{{qn}}}}\Bigg(\int_{0}^{x}e^{-\frac{s^{2}}{2}}\,dt\frac{ds}{\sqrt{{2\pi}}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{{npq}}}\Bigg)\,.

The two non-asymptotic inequalities (
113
) and (
124
) together imply the
limit of de Moivre–Laplace in the case where
a=0
and
b=x
.
Applying the same result
with the parameter
1-p
, and setting

(125)

\forall i\in\{\,1,\dots,n\,\}\qquad\smash{\widetilde{X}}_{i}=1-X_{i}\,,

we obtain

(126)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\,P\Big(0<\frac{n-\big(\smash{\widetilde{X}}_{1}+\cdots+\smash{\widetilde{X}}_{n}\big)-n(1-p)}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}<x\Big)\,=\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{a}^{b}e^{-\tfrac{s^{2}}{2}}ds\,.

Yet
\smash{\widetilde{X}}_{1},\dots,\smash{\widetilde{X}}_{n}
are i.i.d. Bernoulli with parameter
p
.
Rewriting the probability as

(127)

P\Big(0<\frac{-\big(\smash{\widetilde{X}}_{1}+\cdots+\smash{\widetilde{X}}_{n}\big)+np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}<x\Big)\,=\,P\Big(-x<\frac{\smash{\widetilde{X}}_{1}+\cdots+\smash{\widetilde{X}}_{n}-np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}<0\Big)

we see that
the limit (
126
) corresponds to the case

a=-x
and
b=0
.
Putting the two previous results together, we obtain the limit
for a symmetric interval.
As in the symmetric case, this yields
the limit for an arbitrary interval
[a,b]
.
