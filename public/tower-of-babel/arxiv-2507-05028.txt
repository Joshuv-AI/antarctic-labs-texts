The Myhill isomorphism theorem does not generalize much Thank you to Eike Neumann, Arno Pauly and Manlio Valenti for substantial discussions on this work and to Andrej Bauer for asking a motivating question and encouragements.
Cécilia Pradic

Abstract
The Myhill isomorphism is a variant of the Cantor-Bernstein theorem. It states that, from two injections that reduces two subsets of ℕ \mathbb{N} to each other, there exists a bijection ℕ → ℕ \mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N} that preserves them. This theorem can be proven constructively. We investigate to which extent the theorem can be extended to other infinite sets other than ℕ \mathbb{N} . We show that, assuming Markov’s principle, the theorem can be extended to the conatural numbers ℕ ∞ \mathbb{N}_{\infty} provided that we only require that bicomplemented sets are preserved by the bijection. This restriction is essential. Otherwise, the picture is overall negative: among other things, it is impossible to extend that result to either 2 × ℕ ∞ 2\times\mathbb{N}_{\infty} , ℕ + ℕ ∞ \mathbb{N}+\mathbb{N}_{\infty} , ℕ × ℕ ∞ \mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}_{\infty} , ℕ ∞ 2 \mathbb{N}_{\infty}^{2} , 2 ℕ {2^{\mathbb{N}}} or ℕ ℕ {\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}} .

1.2
The Myhill isomorphism theorem

We refer to the introduction for the key definitions; here we give a couple of
intuitions on how the usual Myhill isomorphism theorem is proven and a couple
of useful facts.

A first helpful intuition is that the sets
A
and
B
do not matter as data
when computing the bijection in the conclusion of the theorems. The relevant
property is enforced by making sure that the bijection only relates pairs which
belong to the transitive closure of the following bipartite graph, where

and
g
are injections witnessing

and
B\preceq_{1}^{X}A

respectively.

G_{f,g}=\{({\sf inl}(n),{\sf inl}(f(n)))\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}\cup\{({\sf inl}(g(m)),{\sf inr}(m))\mid m\in\mathbb{N}\}~\subseteq~(\mathbb{N}+\mathbb{N})^{2}

f
and
g
being reductions mean essentially that whenever we have an edge

(u,v)
in

, then
u

belongs to the subgraph induced by
A
and
B
(i.e.,
{\sf inl}(A)\cup{\sf inl}(B)
)
if and only if
v
does.

Definition 15
.

We say that

It is clear that the strong Myhill property implies the Myhill property for any

3
Weakening the Myhill property and one positive result

3.1
The modal Myhill property

For the rest of this paper, we will discuss
a weakening of the Myhill property, as well as a corresponding weakening of
the strong Myhill property. The basic idea is that, while we are still going to
ask explicitly for a bijection, we will relax our requirement on its correctness
so that we may no longer extract evidence telling us explicitly
which paths to follow in graphs such as those pictured
in Figures
1
and
2
.

To do this, we are going to use some modalities, taking some inspiration
from
[
17
,
10
,
19
]
.

Definition 29
.

A modality is a map
\bigcirc:\Omega\to\Omega
such that, for
every proposition
p\in\Omega
,
p\Rightarrow\bigcirc p
.
We call a proposition
p

\bigcirc
-stable if the converse

\bigcirc p\Rightarrow p
holds and, for any set
X
, we say that
A\subseteq X

is a
\bigcirc
-subset of
X
if, for every
x\in X
,
x\in A
is
\bigcirc
-modal.

Given two modalities
\bigcirc,\bigcirc^{\prime}\in\Omega
, we say that
\bigcirc\leq\bigcirc^{\prime}

if for every
p\in\Omega
,
\bigcirc p
implies
\bigcirc^{\prime}p
.

The double-negation map
\neg\neg:\Omega\to\Omega
is the main modality we are
going to use. In realizability models, double-negation erases the computational
content of propositions (that is, double-negated statement can always be trivially
witnessed). The other one, which we will use
to make a couple positive results a bit sharper, is defined as follows.

Definition 30

(The mindchange modality)
.

For any proposition
p\in\Omega
, define
\bigcirc_{{\sf LPO}}p
as

\exists x\in\mathbb{N}_{\infty}.\;\left(x\in\underline{\mathbb{N}}\vee x=\infty\right)\Rightarrow p

The basic intuition is that proving
\bigcirc_{{\sf LPO}}p
is the same as proving

p
after asking an
{\sf LPO}
question. The witness
x\in\mathbb{N}_{\infty}
indicates
that we want to ask whether
x=\underline{t}
for some
t
or whether
x=\infty

before we can prove
t
.

Lemma 31
.

We have
\bigcirc_{{\sf LPO}}\leq\neg\neg
.

Proof.

Assume that
\neg p
holds and
\bigcirc_{{\sf LPO}}p
, that is, we have some
x\in\mathbb{N}_{\infty}

and
x\in\underline{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{\infty\}
implies
p
.
Then we clearly have
x\not\in\underline{\mathbb{N}}
, so by induction we can
show that
x\geq\underline{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and thus that
x=\infty
.
But this is also a contradiction.
∎

Remark 32
.

The nomenclature
\bigcirc_{{\sf LPO}}
is chosen because
\bigcirc_{{\sf LPO}}
is an instance of the following
construction: to a map
r:S\to Q
, one may assign the following endomap
on truth values:

\begin{array}[]{llcl}\bigcirc_{r}:&\Omega&\longrightarrow&\Omega\\
&p&\mapsto&\exists q\in Q.\;\forall s\in S.\;r(s)=q~\Longrightarrow~p\end{array}

Regarding
r
as a problem with inputs
q\in Q
and solutions

, proving

\bigcirc_{r}p
means “we can prove
p
provided we can solve one instance of
F
”.
In particular “
r
has a section” proves that
\bigcirc_{r}
is the identity over
\Omega
.

The obvious map
\mathbb{N}+\{\infty\}\to\mathbb{N}_{\infty}
yields
\bigcirc_{\sf LPO}
, and this
map having a section is equivalent to
{\sf LPO}
.

Note that, unlike
\neg\neg
,
\bigcirc_{{\sf LPO}}
is
not
idempotent: to prove

\bigcirc_{{\sf LPO}}\bigcirc_{{\sf LPO}}p
, one is allowed to ask two
{\sf LPO}
questions
in succession.
To get a closure operators, one would need to allow an arbitrary finite number
of mindchanges, resulting in a modality better named
\bigcirc_{{\sf LPO}^{\diamond}}
or

\bigcirc_{\mathsf{C}_{\mathbb{N}}}
(see
[
15
, Proposition 10]
). Let us
define it now.

Definition 33

(The finite mindchange modality)
.

Define the modality
\bigcirc_{{\sf LPO}^{\diamond}}
as the map which takes
p\in\Omega
to

\exists s:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}.\;\text{$s$ is monotone, $\neg\left(\lim_{n\to+\infty}s(n)=+\infty\right)$ and }\left(\forall\ell\in\mathbb{N}.\;\lim_{n\to+\infty}s(n)=\ell~\Longrightarrow~p\right)

We now define the weakening of the (strong) Myhill property. In what follows,

\bigcirc
is an arbitrary modality.

Definition 34
.

We say that a set

has the
\bigcirc
strong Myhill property when, for
every pair of injections

, there is a bijection

such that, for every

\bigcirc\left(h(x)\in\bigcup_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}f\circ(g\circ f)^{m}\right)

We say that

has the
\bigcirc
Myhill property if, for any
\bigcirc
-subsets

, we have that

and

imply
that
A\simeq^{X}B
.

By unpacking the definitions and

one can
establish the following.

Proposition 35
.

Let
\bigcirc\leq\bigcirc^{\prime}
be two modalities and
X
be a set. The following holds:

•

If
X
has the
\bigcirc
(strong) Myhill property, then it also has the

\bigcirc^{\prime}
(strong) Myhill property.

•

If

has the
\bigcirc
strong Myhill property, then it has the
\bigcirc
Myhill property

To which extent those implications are strict is unclear, but we can note the
following when it comes to the

modality for certain sets and models.

Remark 36

If

is a modest set in a realizability model,

\neg\neg
Myhill property, then it necessarily has the

\neg\neg
strong Myhill property.

Proof.

Assume we have a realizer

for

\neg\neg

Up to trivial modifications, it takes input evidence for

and

\neg\neg
-stable,

and

and

reduce

to

Consider the families of sets

(A_{f,g,x})_{f,g,x}

(B_{f,g,x})_{f,g,x}

and

\begin{array}[]{l !\quad c !\quad l}z\in A_{f,g,x}&\Longleftrightarrow&\neg\neg z\in\bigcup_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}(g\circ f)^{m}(x)\\

This remark essentially applies to most concrete examples we examine in the

3.2
The conatural numbers have the
\bigcirc_{{\sf LPO}}
strong Myhill property

Theorem 37
.

Assuming
{\sf MP}
,
\mathbb{N}_{\infty}
has the
\bigcirc_{{\sf LPO}}
strong Myhill property.

For this whole subsection, let us thus fix some injections

f,g:\mathbb{N}_{\infty}\to\mathbb{N}_{\infty}
, from which we want to produce
some bijection
h\subseteq\bigcup_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}f\circ(g\circ f)^{m}
.

The gist of the proof is that one can attempt to build
h

over
\mathbb{N}_{\infty}\setminus\{\infty\}
using the same back-and-forth
construction as in the classical Myhill isomorphism theorem if we
optimistically assume that
f(\infty)=g(\infty)=\infty
.
Of course this is not something we can simply assume nor check, but we can
check that
\inf(f(\infty),g(\infty))
is “large enough” for a
given stage of the construction to not be wrong. If that quantity is always
large enough, this will mean that
f(\infty)=g(\infty)=\infty
and the
construction will naturally enforce that
h(\infty)=\infty
.
Otherwise, at some stage we will realize (via
Lemma

11
) that either

f(\infty)\neq\infty
or
g(\infty)\neq\infty
. In either case, we
can apply
Lemma

7
to deduce that

\mathbb{N}_{\infty}\cong\mathbb{N}
, which allows us to continue the back-and-forth as if

\mathbb{N}_{\infty}
was actually
\mathbb{N}
much like in the original proof of the Myhill
isomorphism theorem.

Now let us make all of that more precise. First, let us define what are
the approximations we are going to build. There are going to be two kinds,
those that are continuous, corresponding to the first optimistic phase.

Definition 38
.

A
continuous partial solution
is a finite subset
I
of
\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}

such that:

•

I
is a partial isomorphism: for all

(n,m)\in I~\text{and}~(n^{\prime},m^{\prime})\in I\qquad\text{if and only if}\qquad n=n^{\prime}\Longleftrightarrow m=m^{\prime}

•

the image of
I
by
(n,m)\mapsto(\underline{n},\underline{m})
is included in
\bigcup\limits_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}f\circ(g\circ f)^{m}

We say

has rank (at least)

if

•

for every
k<n
,

belongs to both the domain and codomain of
I

•

\inf(f(\infty),g(\infty))\geq\underline{n}
.

The second kind corresponds to the phase where we noticed

\inf(f(\infty),g(\infty))\neq\infty
and have
{\sf LPO}
available.

.

A

\mathbb{N}_{\infty}\times\mathbb{N}_{\infty}

•

I
is a partial isomorphism: for all

•

\infty
belongs to both the domain and codomain of

•

{\sf LPO}
holds

We say
I
has rank (at least)

if

•

for every
k<n
,
k
belongs to both the domain and codomain of
I

•

\infty
belongs to both the domain and codomain of

.

We say that a partial solution

discontinuous and the

by

is included in

I
. Now the first step towards proving

.

Assuming Markov’s principle, there exists a sequences of partial solutions

(I_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\left(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N})+\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N}_{\infty}\times\mathbb{N}_{\infty})\right)^{\mathbb{N}}

Lemma 41
.

There is a function that turns partial discontinuous solution of rank

.

Proof.

Since we are given a partial discontinuous solution, we know that
{\sf LPO}
holds.
Thus we can simply use the isomorphism
\mathbb{N}_{\infty}\cong\mathbb{N}
to extend the solution
as in the proof of
Theorem

16
.
∎

The more difficult part is thus to extend partial continuous solutions. In the
presence of
{\sf LPO}
, we can straightforwardly use

since we can then turn a partial continuous solution into a discontinuous one.

Lemma 42
.

There is a function that turns partial continuous solutions
I
of rank

into
a compatible partial discontinuous solutions

of rank
n
if

Proof.

First take the image of

With that in hand, it thus suffices to show the existence of a procedure that

•

Conclusion

The overall picture is rather bleak if one is hoping to naively and constructively
extend the Myhill isomorphism theorem to sets other than
\mathbb{N}
or subfinite
sets. While it is possible to get explicit
constructions for
\mathbb{N}_{\infty}
(at the cost of inserting some double-negations in
the correctness condition and accepting Markov’s principle), a number of natural examples including

2^{\mathbb{N}}
and
{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
fail to have the same property.

However, something that can be fun is to attempt to calibrate precisely how
hard this failure is in the style of constructive reverse mathematics.
For instance, for
\mathbb{N}+\mathbb{N}_{\infty}
,
\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}_{\infty}
and
\mathbb{N}_{\infty}^{2}

\neg\neg
Myhill property is equivalent to
{\sf LPO}
.
We could ask a similar question for
2^{\mathbb{N}}
and
{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
.

modalities in front of the existential statements (as in
59
),
and see what are the least powerful modalities that do the job.
