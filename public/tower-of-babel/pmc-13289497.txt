Explainable prediction of MDR/RR-TB in tuberculosis-diabetes mellitus multimorbidity: a machine learning model developed and validated in a dual-center study
Authors: Xinxin Zhong, Kuan Liu, Jiujin Zhang, Xinhua Tang, Tao Lu, Qiqi Chen, Jianzhi Pang, Rongjun Chen, Bingrou Li, Siyuan Ding, Libing Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTuberculosis-diabetes mellitus (TB-DM) multimorbidity significantly increases the risk of multidrug-resistant/rifampicin-resistant tuberculosis (MDR/RR-TB). Early risk stratification tools for this high-risk population remain lacking.ObjectiveTo develop and validate an interpretable machine learning (ML) model for predicting MDR/RR-TB in patients with TB-DM multimorbidity, and to identify key predictive factors using explainable artificial intelligence.MethodsThis dual-center retrospective study enrolled 245 patients with TB-DM multimorbidity from January 2019 to December 2022. Seven machine learning algorithms were constructed and validated with 10-fold cross-validation. Model performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC), accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, calibration curve, and decision curve analysis (DCA). SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) was applied to identify critical predictive factors.ResultsThe random forest (RF) model achieved the optimal performance, with an AUC-ROC of 0.818, accuracy of 0.806, precision of 0.688, recall of 0.611, and F1-score of 0.647; the moderate recall indicates a considerable false-negative rate (FNR) , supporting its use as a triage tool rather than a stand-alone diagnostic test. Calibration and DCA confirmed robust predictive reliability and substantial clinical net benefit within a clinically relevant threshold range of 0.06–0.80. SHAP analysis identified the symptom-to-diagnosis interval, tuberculosis (TB) treatment history, treatment adherence, pulmonary cavitation, and smoking history as the top five critical predictors.ConclusionThe interpretable RF model accurately and reliably predicts the risk of MDR/RR-TB in patients with TB-DM multimorbidity. The symptom-to-diagnosis interval is the most crucial risk factor. This model can assist clinical triage, early intervention, and personalized management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13480-7.Keywords: Tuberculosis–diabetes mellitus multimorbidity, Multidrug-resistant/rifampicin-resistant tuberculosis, Machine learning, Predictive model, SHAP analysis

Introduction

The multimorbidity [1] of tuberculosis and diabetes mellitus (TB-DM) presents a critical global public health challenge due to synergistic pathological mechanisms, including immune dysfunction [2], metabolic dysregulation [3], and imbalanced inflammatory responses [4]. These mechanisms collectively exacerbate progression of both tuberculosis and diabetes mellitus. Epidemiological studies report a global TB-DM multimorbidity prevalence of 13.73% [5], and people with diabetes mellitus face a 2–3-fold higher risk of tuberculosis infection compared to non-diabetic populations [6–8]. Notably, diabetes not only worsens tuberculosis symptomatology and diminishes therapeutic responses [9] but also significantly elevates the likelihood of progression to multidrug-resistant/rifampicin-resistant tuberculosis (MDR/RR-TB) [10–13]. Conversely, tuberculosis may precipitate the onset of dysglycemia and impair glycemic control in patients with preexisting diabetes. In addition, bidirectional pharmacokinetic interactions can occur between anti-tuberculosis drugs and glucose-lowering agents, potentially compromising treatment efficacy for both diseases [14]. However, conventional diagnostic systems exhibit limited capability for early detection of drug-resistant progression in TB-DM multimorbidity patients, underscoring the critical need for predictive models to enable risk-stratified management.

To address this gap, recent advances in artificial intelligence, particularly machine learning (ML), have introduced novel paradigms for clinical decision-making by uncovering latent patterns in high-dimensional data [15, 16]. ML algorithms excel at constructing data-driven predictive models for clinical tasks [17], offering a promising solution for early risk stratification. However, their “black-box” nature often impedes clinical translation due to a lack of interpretability [18]. SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) is a game-theory-based interpretability approach that quantifies the contribution of each input feature to the final prediction, visualizes the direction and magnitude of each variable’s influence, and supports both global model interpretation and individual case-level explanation [19]. By doing so, SHAP effectively resolves the “black-box” limitation of conventional machine learning models and enhances clinical interpretability and acceptance. This framework therefore addresses the key barrier to clinical translation by linking algorithmic outputs with transparent, clinically meaningful explanations [19].

This study aims to: (1) identify key predictors of MDR/RR-TB progression in TB-DM multimorbidity patients using SHAP-interpretable ML models; (2) validate model performance in risk stratification; and (3) elucidate the clinical significance of key risk factors through explainable analysis to guide clinical interventions. By balancing predictive accuracy with model transparency, this research advances the integration of interpretable artificial intelligence (AI) tools into drug-resistant tuberculosis control programs.

Methods

Study setting and participants

In this retrospective study, we initially identified 1,396 patients with TB-DM multimorbidity who were hospitalized at Red Cross Hospital of Yulin City and The Third People’s Hospital of Guilin between January 2019 and December 2022. Among these 1,396 patients, 1,126 (80.7%) had drug-susceptible TB (DS-TB), 83 (5.9%) had multidrug-resistant/rifampicin-resistant TB (MDR/RR-TB), and baseline and drug susceptibility data for the remaining 187 patients (13.4%) were unavailable from the routine tuberculosis surveillance system (Supplementary Table 1). Due to incomplete retrospective clinical records and missing key variables required for model development, complete data collection for the entire cohort could not be achieved. The final analytic cohort comprised 245 patients (73 with MDR/RR-TB and 172 with DS-TB) (Fig. 1). To assess potential selection bias, we compared baseline characteristics between included and excluded patients separately for DS-TB patients and MDR/RR-TB patients (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3).

Flowchart of patient enrollment and study design. A total of 1,396 patients with TB-DM multimorbidity were initially screened, and 245 were finally included after excluding those with incomplete data, missing key variables, or unavailable drug susceptibility test results. The included patients were assigned to training and validation cohorts, both containing DS-TB and MDR/RR-TB cases

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) confirmed diagnosis of both tuberculosis and diabetes mellitus; (2) age ≥ 18 years; (3) availability of complete DST results or Xpert MTB/RIF assay results. The exclusion criteria were: (1) incomplete clinical records or missing key variables required for model development; (2) loss to follow-up before diagnostic confirmation; (3) pregnancy or lactation.

Tuberculosis was diagnosed by standard laboratory methods including sputum smear microscopy for acid-fast bacilli(AFB)using Ziehl‑Neelsen staining, mycobacterial culture, and nucleic acid amplification tests. Bacteriological confirmation was based on positive culture for Mycobacterium tuberculosis or a positive nucleic acid amplification test [20]. DST for isoniazid and rifampicin was performed using the phenotypic proportion method on Lowenstein‑Jensen medium. Rifampicin resistance was also assessed using the Xpert MTB/RIF assay to detect rifampicin resistance‑associated mutations [21]. MDR-TB was defined as resistance to at least isoniazid and rifampicin [22], RR-TB as resistance to rifampicin [22], and DS-TB as bacteriologically confirmed or clinically diagnosed TB without rifampicin or isoniazid resistance [23]. New cases included treatment-naïve patients or those with < 1 month of irregular chemotherapy [22], previously treated cases denoted patients with ≥ 1 month of prior anti-TB treatment [24]. Medication adherence was defined as the extent to which patients took anti-TB medications in accordance with the prescribed interval and dose [25], which was assessed based on patient self-report and outpatient pharmacy refill records, consistent with the most common adherence identification methods [26]. Diabetes mellitus was diagnosed according to the 2006 WHO and International Diabetes Federation (IDF) criteria [27]: fasting plasma glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, or 2-hour plasma glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L after a 75-g oral glucose tolerance test. All analyses adhered to national and international TB guidelines.

Variable selection and data collection

Clinical data for all 245 TB-DM multimorbidity patients included demographics (age, sex, marital status, BMI, smoking history, alcohol abuse, substance abuse) and clinical manifestations (fever, cough, sputum production, hemoptysis, dyspnea, weight loss, anorexia, chest pain, chest tightness, fatigue, night sweats, incidental findings); TB-related characteristics (extrapulmonary TB, tuberculous pleurisy, bronchial TB); comorbidities (human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), autoimmune diseases, respiratory failure, malignancies); laboratory parameters (pretreatment AFB sputum smear and 2-month post-treatment AFB sputum smear, complete blood count (CBC) including white blood cell (WBC), neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, hemoglobin, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), serum albumin, C-reactive protein (CRP), fasting plasma glucose (FPG), 2-hour postprandial plasma glucose (2 h-PPG), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c)); chest CT findings (micronodules, cavitary lesions, consolidation, pleural involvement, affected lobes, bronchiectasis); and diagnostic/treatment characteristics (symptom-to-diagnosis interval, TB contact history, prior TB treatment, medication adherence, initial TB hospital presentation). Seven composite indices were derived from source laboratory parameters, including inflammatory-related composite indices: neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), C-reactive protein-to-albumin ratio (CAR), C-reactive protein-to-lymphocyte ratio (CLR), derived neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (dNLR), monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), systemic inflammation response index (SIRI); and a nutritional-related index: prognostic nutritional index (PNI). Binary variables including anemia (defined by hemoglobin < 120 g/L in females or < 130 g/L in males) [28] and hypoalbuminemia (defined by serum albumin < 35 g/L) [29] were also derived from the corresponding laboratory parameters.

Feature preprocessing and selection

The initial dataset contained 60 features (22 continuous, 38 categorical). Variables with missing values exceeding 20% were excluded prior to further preprocessing. Variable-level missingness in the final analytic dataset was minimal: among the 12 predictors selected for model development, only FPG had missing values, with a missing rate of 0.41% (1/245). The remaining 11 predictors were complete. Missing values were imputed using the k-nearest neighbors (k-NN) algorithm (k = 5) under the missing-at-random assumption. To assess the sensitivity of our results to this imputation assumption, we also performed a sensitivity analysis using median imputation (see sensitivity analyses section). Feature pairs with Spearman’s ρ > 0.9 were identified, and clinically less relevant variables were removed to mitigate multicollinearity [30]. The Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE; k = 5 neighbors) was applied to address the class imbalance (DS-TB: MDR/RR-TB = 172:73) [31]. Critically, SMOTE was applied after train-test splitting and only to the training data. The independent test set remained untouched by any oversampling procedure to ensure realistic performance evaluation. Continuous features underwent z-score normalization. Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression with L1 regularization (optimal α = 0.0356 via LassoCV) selected 12 predictive features.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were compared among groups using the chi-square test, or Fisher’s exact test when any expected cell count was less than 5, and results were presented as frequencies (percentages). Normally distributed continuous variables were analyzed using the independent two-sample t-test (with Levene’s test for equality of variances) for two-group comparisons and presented as means ± standard deviation (SD). For skewed continuous variables, the Mann-Whitney U test was used for two-group comparisons and the Kruskal-Wallis H test for multi-group comparisons; data were presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

ML modeling and evaluation

The modeling pipeline for identifying predictors of MDR/RR-TB was constructed as follows: first, data preprocessing (missing value imputation, multicollinearity removal, standardization); second, key feature selection via LASSO regression; third, training and comparison of seven machine learning models; fourth, selection of the optimal model based on receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC) and overall performance; finally, SHAP analysis was used to quantify feature importance and identify key predictors.

The cohort was randomly partitioned into training (75%, n = 183) and testing (25%, n = 62) sets with strict patient-level segregation (no overlap between training and testing sets to avoid data leakage). Seven algorithms were implemented: logistic regression (LR), random forest (RF), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), naive bayes (NB), support vector machine (SVM), decision tree (DT), and k-NN. Stratified 10-fold cross-validation reduced evaluation bias during training. Kruskal-Wallis H tests assessed statistical significance of performance metric differences across models.

Model performance was evaluated using accuracy, precision, AUC-ROC (primary metric), recall, and F1-score. Higher values of these metrics indicate better discriminative ability and predictive performance. In addition to discriminative performance, we evaluated model calibration using calibration curves, Brier score (lower = better calibration), calibration intercept, and calibration slope. DCA was performed to quantify the clinical net benefit of the model across a range of threshold probabilities, where a higher net benefit indicates greater clinical utility.

SHAP analysis was used for model interpretation. A high mean absolute SHAP value indicates a stronger contribution to predicting MDR/RR-TB. Positive SHAP values indicate an increased risk of MDR/RR-TB, whereas negative SHAP values indicate a protective effect. Features with non-zero SHAP values were regarded as significant contributors; values near zero were considered non-influential [32]. SHAP values interpreted the optimal model, quantifying global feature importance and feature-specific impacts on individual predictions using game theory principles. All analyses used Python 3.11.6 (64-bit). Hypothesis tests were two-tailed with significance at p < 0.05.

To evaluate model stability and address potential concerns regarding class imbalance, missing data imputation, and operating threshold dependency, three sets of sensitivity analyses were performed using identical train-test splits as the main analysis.

Resampling sensitivity (SMOTE impact): Models were constructed with and without the SMOTE to assess the influence of class imbalance correction. Critically, SMOTE was applied only to the training data after train-test splitting; the independent test set was never oversampled in either scenario, ruling out data leakage concerns.

Sensitivity analysis results

To assess the stability of the RF model to key preprocessing choices, three sensitivity analyses were performed following the same train-test split as the main analysis.

Resampling sensitivity (SMOTE impact): When SMOTE was omitted from the preprocessing pipeline, the RF model showed a marked decrease in recall from 0.611 to 0.389 and a corresponding increase in FNR from 0.389 to 0.611, while specificity increased from 0.864 to 0.955 (Table 3). Discrimination remained broadly similar (AUC-ROC 0.860 vs. 0.819 in the main analysis). These findings confirm that SMOTE played a substantive role in improving minority-class detection without artificially inflating performance through data leakage, as the independent test set was never oversampled.

Discussion

Despite global efforts to integrate screening, diagnosis, and collaborative management for TB-DM multimorbidity, early identification of individuals at high risk of progression to multidrug-resistant/rifampicin-resistant tuberculosis (MDR/RR-TB) remains a major unmet challenge in clinical practice and TB control programs [33–35]. Current strategies rely heavily on conventional laboratory-based diagnostic tools, which are often associated with long turnaround times and limited accessibility at the point-of-care, especially in resource-limited primary care settings [36]. Although the WHO has recommended near point-of-care rapid molecular tests to accelerate TB diagnosis, such tools remain underutilized in many high-burden regions, leading to persistent diagnostic delays, delayed treatment initiation, and further emergence of drug resistance, particularly among vulnerable populations including people with diabetes [37]. Moreover, existing clinical pathways lack standardized, risk-stratified approaches to prioritize high-risk TB-DM multimorbidity patients for enhanced monitoring and timely DST [35]. Most predictive models focus on either TB or DM alone, and few interpretable machine learning models have been specifically developed for MDR/RR-TB risk stratification in the TB-DM multimorbidity population [38, 39]. These gaps highlight an urgent need for a simple, objective, and clinically applicable tool to support early risk stratification in this high-risk group. Against this background, the present study aimed to develop and validate an interpretable machine learning model for triage and risk stratification of MDR/RR-TB in TB-DM multimorbidity patients using routinely available clinical and radiological features, with the goal of complementing existing diagnostic and care cascades.

In the present study, we developed and internally validated an interpretable machine learning model for MDR/RR-TB risk stratification and triage in patients with TB-DM multimorbidity using routinely available clinical and radiological features. Among seven algorithms tested, the RF model demonstrated strong overall performance, with a test-set AUC-ROC of 0.818, which was slightly lower than that of logistic regression (0.828) but significantly exceeded SVM and XGBoost. Although the logistic regression (LR) model achieved a marginally higher AUC-ROC (0.828) in the independent test set, the optimality of a clinical prediction model should not be judged solely by a single test-set AUC metric. The random forest (RF) model demonstrated excellent fitting ability during stratified 10‑fold cross‑validation, minimal performance fluctuation, higher overall prediction accuracy, and a well‑balanced F1‑score, making it more suitable for real‑world clinical scenarios. The model’s stability was confirmed by 10-fold cross-validation, sensitivity analyses (SMOTE, missing value imputation, threshold tuning), calibration, and decision curve analysis, all supporting reliable discrimination and clinical net benefit. However, the moderate recall (0.611) indicated potential limitations in detecting all high-risk cases, possibly due to unmeasured socioeconomic factors or microbial genomic variations. SHAP analysis identified the top five predictive features: symptom-to-diagnosis interval, TB treatment history, treatment adherence, pulmonary cavitation, and smoking history.

The superior performance of the RF model can be attributed to its high predictive accuracy, robust interpretability, and effectiveness in handling heterogeneous clinical data. It achieved an AUC-ROC of 0.818, with stable performance in 10-fold cross-validation and minimal overfitting. Comprehensive sensitivity analyses confirmed that SMOTE improved minority-class detection without data leakage, and missing value imputation strategies did not materially influence model outcomes. Threshold-dependent tuning supported flexible adjustment of sensitivity for clinical triage, while calibration and DCA verified reliable prediction and meaningful clinical net benefit. Furthermore, the RF model effectively integrated 12 heterogeneous features selected by LASSO regression and captured key non-linear relationships consistent with TB pathophysiology, including the association between prolonged diagnostic delay and MDR/RR-TB risk, as well as the protective effect of good treatment adherence.

This RF model has meaningful clinical value as a triage and risk stratification tool. First, it enables rapid identification of high-risk TB-DM patients using readily available clinical indicators, supporting targeted interventions such as prioritized rapid DST and intensified treatment regimens. Second, the key predictors derived from SHAP analysis provide actionable insights: shortening diagnostic delay through optimized care pathways and improving adherence via patient education or digital monitoring. Third, the model’s interpretability enhances clinical trust and facilitates integration into routine TB control programs, especially in resource-limited settings, without being constrained by delays of conventional DST. Nevertheless, cautious application is warranted because external validation in independent populations has not yet been performed.

A major strength of this study is that the model relies on routinely collected demographic, lifestyle, clinical, and imaging data, supporting its real-world implementation. Machine learning has been increasingly applied to MDR/RR-TB prediction. Li et al. used machine learning and radiomics to predict MDR/RR-TB in cavitary pulmonary tuberculosis, and their radiomics model (AUC-ROC = 0.844/0.829) outperformed clinical models [40]. Deelder et al. demonstrated accurate prediction of TB drug resistance from whole-genome sequencing data using gradient-boosted trees [41]. Similarly, Sharma et al. applied machine learning with WGS data to predict drug-resistant TB [42]. The present study contributes to the field by specifically focusing on the high-risk TB-DM multimorbidity subgroup using only routine clinical variables.

To enhance model interpretability, we employed SHAP analysis, which provides quantitative measures of feature contributions and enables visualization of prediction pathways, a recognized strength in clinical ML applications [43]. This advanced metric quantifies the contribution of individual features to model outputs, while SHAP decision plots offer intuitive visualizations of case-specific prediction pathways.

Our analysis revealed that patients with MDR/RR-TB in the TB-DM multimorbidity cohort had a significantly lower median age (47.5 years) than those with drug-susceptible TB (DS-TB) (51 years, p < 0.001). The relationship between age and MDR/RR-TB risk remains debated in the literature, with studies reporting higher risk in both older [44–46] and younger [47–50] populations. This discrepancy may reflect variations in study cohorts, regional epidemiology, or the unique impact of age-related immunological changes in the context of diabetic multimorbidity.

SHAP analysis identified the symptom-to-diagnosis interval (SHAP = 0.103) and TB treatment history (SHAP = 0.079) as the top risk factors for MDR/RR-TB. Prolonged diagnostic delays are consistently linked to increased drug resistance risk, as they allow for bacterial adaptation and accumulation of resistant mutations [51]. These delays often stem from limited diagnostic capacity in primary care (e.g., lack of rapid tests, necessitating referrals) [52–54], prolonging the infectious pre-diagnosis period, increasing transmission risk [50], and worsening outcomes [55]. A history of TB treatment, a well-documented risk factor, highlights how incomplete or non-standardized regimens can foster the selection and accumulation of drug-resistant strains [56, 57].

Treatment adherence (SHAP = 0.060) exhibited a protective effect, consistent with global TB control priorities [58]. However, adherence is particularly challenging for TB-DM multimorbidity patients due to high pill burden, adverse effects, and long treatment duration [35, 59–61], exacerbating poor outcomes and driving MDR/RR-TB risk.

Radiologically, pulmonary cavitation was predominant in MDR/RR-TB patients on CT scans, whereas nodular and consolidation opacities were more common in the DS-TB group—a finding consistent with previous studies [62, 63]. In TB-DM multimorbidity patients, the unique pathophysiological environment of cavities may facilitate bacterial persistence and transmission [63].

Malnutrition is an established risk factor for TB [64, 65]. In this study, hemoglobin levels showed an inverse association with MDR/RR-TB risk. To date, no clear pathological basis or consistent external evidence supports this observation. Therefore, this result should be regarded as an interesting preliminary finding that warrants further validation and mechanistic exploration in future studies, rather than a definitive protective effect.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, an unexpected inverse association between smoking history and MDR/RR-TB risk (β=-0.21, p = 0.03) was observed, which conflicts with some previous reports [49] and may reflect recall bias or residual confounding. Second, the high exclusion rate (1,151/1,396) due to incomplete retrospective data may introduce selection bias, even after sensitivity analyses. Third, the lack of external validation limits the generalizability of our findings. The present model was only developed and internally validated using data from two local hospitals; external validation in geographically and epidemiologically distinct populations is required before broad clinical application. Despite these limitations, our study provides valuable preliminary evidence that warrants verification in larger prospective cohorts.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the RF algorithm shows strong potential for predicting MDR/RR-TB in patients with TB-DM multimorbidity. By integrating routine clinical and radiological features, this interpretable model accurately identifies high-risk individuals. It can serve as a valuable clinical triage tool to prioritize patients for earlier drug susceptibility testing and timely intervention. Future research should focus on: (1) prospective trials evaluating the model’s impact on reducing MDR/RR-TB incidence; (2) developing user-friendly decision-support systems; and (3) translating and responsibly integrating such algorithms into routine clinical care while preserving model transparency and interpretability for frontline clinicians.
