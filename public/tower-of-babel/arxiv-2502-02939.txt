Combinatorial knot Floer homology and tangle decompositions
Hajime Kubota

Abstract
. We compute the top, next-to-top, and top–2 terms of the grid homology for diagonal knots, which are knots represented by grid diagrams with O-markings lying along the diagonal. This class includes many positive braid knots. Its next-to-top term detects the number of prime factors, and its top–2 term corresponds to the decompositions of the knot into two non-integer tangles. We compare diagonal knots with various classes of knots, including positive braid knots, fibered positive knots, and L L -space knots.

1.
Introduction

Grid homology is a combinatorial reconstruction of knot Floer homology for knots in
S^{3}

[
16
,
15
]
.
Sarkar
[
27
]
gave a combinatorial proof of the Milnor conjecture
[
17
]
using grid homology.
Sarkar observed that for positive torus knots, the tau invariant, a concordance invariant in knot Floer homology, can be easily calculated using grid diagrams as in Figure
1
.
Roughly speaking, this is because it is easy to see that the top term of the grid homology of a diagonal knot is one-dimensional.
In this paper, we call the top term the nontrivial homology with the highest Alexander grading.
We also define the next-to-top term, the top-2 term in a similar way.
Arndt-Jansen-McBurney-Vance
[
1
]
defined a
diagonal knot
as a knot represented by such a grid diagram.

While grid homology is a purely combinatorial theory and grid homology for any knot is computable in principle, it remains unclear which classes of knots are particularly well-suited to this framework.
In this paper, we compute the top, next-to-top, and top–2 terms of the grid homology for diagonal knots with coefficients in
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
(Theorem
1.6
).
Furthermore, we argue that the cycles representing the nontrivial homology classes in the next-to-top term correspond to prime factors of the knot, and that the cycles in the top–2 term reflect the decompositions of the knot into two non-integer tangles.
This suggests that diagonal knots are particularly amenable to analysis in the framework of grid homology.

1.2.
Main results

In this paper, we partially determine the grid homology of diagonal knots.

Grid homology
of a knot
K\subset S^{3}
is a finite-dimensional bigraded
\mathbb{F}
-vector space

\widehat{GH}(K)=\bigoplus_{d,s\in\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{GH}_{d}(K,s),

which is isomorphic to knot Floer homology
\widehat{HFK}(K)
as a bigraded vector space,
where
d
denotes the homological grading called the Maslov grading,
s
is called the Alexander grading, and
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
.
It is known that grid homology detects the genus of a knot, as

g(K)=\max\{s|\widehat{GH}_{*}(K,s)\neq 0\}.

Grid homology is a categorification of the Alexander polynomial
[
22
]
,

(1.1)

\Delta_{K}(t)=\sum_{d,s}(-1)^{d}\cdot\mathrm{dim}_{\mathbb{F}}\widehat{GH}_{d}(K,s)\cdot t^{s}.

See Section
2.2
for details of grid homology.

Theorem 1.6
.

If
K
is a prime diagonal knot other than the unknot, then we have

(1.2)

\widehat{GH}_{*}(K,g(K))\cong\begin{cases}\mathbb{F}&*=0\\
0&*\neq 0,\end{cases}

and

(1.3)

\widehat{GH}_{*}(K,g(K)-1)\cong\begin{cases}\mathbb{F}&*=-1\\
0&*\neq-1.\end{cases}

We can define the diagonal analogue of the arc index.
Recall that the
arc index

\alpha(K)
of a knot
K
is the minimal size of grid diagrams representing
K
.
The additivity of the arc index is known
[
6
]
, i.e.,

\alpha(K_{1}\#K_{2})=\alpha(K_{1})+\alpha(K_{2})-2
.
It is easy to see that the connected sums of diagonal knots are diagonal.
However, the converse is not obvious.
By exploring the next-to-top term of grid chain complexes, we obtain the following.

Definition 1.7
.

For a diagonal knot
K
, we define
diagonal arc index

\alpha_{\mathrm{diag}}(K)
as the minimal size of a diagonal grid diagram representing
K
.

Theorem 1.8
.

If the connected sum
K_{1}\#K_{2}
is diagonal, then both
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
are diagonal.

For two diagonal knots
K_{1},K_{2}
, then we have

\alpha_{\mathrm{diag}}(K_{1}\#K_{2})=\alpha_{\mathrm{diag}}(K_{1})+\alpha_{\mathrm{diag}}(K_{2})-2.

By definition we have
\alpha_{\mathrm{diag}}(K)\geq\alpha(K)
.
There is no known diagonal knot satisfying
\alpha_{\mathrm{diag}}(K)>\alpha(K)
.

Theorem 1.9
.

Let
K
be a prime diagonal knot other than a
(2,q)
torus knot or the unknot for any odd integer

.
Then, we have

(1.4)

\widehat{GH}_{*}(K,g(K)-2)\cong\begin{cases}\mathbb{F}^{m}&*=-1\\
0&*\neq-1,\end{cases}

for some integer

.
Furthermore,

admits at least

decompositions into two non-integer tangles.

Corollary 1.13

There are infinitely many prime knots that are positive but not diagonal.

Proof.

The counterexamples are the pretzel knots

As described in Question

It is well known that positive braid knots are fibered and positive

We give diagonal grid diagrams for some positive braid knots in Appendix

It is known that

2.1.
Grid diagrams

A
(toroidal) grid diagram

\mathbb{G}
is an
n\times n
grid of squares on the torus, some of which contain an
X
- or
O
-marking such that:

(i)

There is exactly one
O
-marking and
X
-marking on each row and column.

(ii)

O
-markings and
X
-markings do not share the same square.

A grid diagram determines an oriented link in
S^{3}
.
Drawing oriented segments from the
O
-markings to the
X
-markings in each row and from the
X
-markings to the
O
-markings in each column.
Assume that the vertical segments always cross above the horizontal segments.
Then we obtain an oriented link diagram.
We think that every toroidal diagram is oriented naturally.
The horizontal circles and vertical circles that separate the torus into
n\times n
squares are denoted by
\bm{\alpha}=\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
\bm{\beta}=\{\beta_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
respectively.

Any link can be represented by (toroidal) grid diagrams.
Two grid diagrams representing the same links are connected by a finite sequence of the following moves called grid moves
[
6
]
:

•

Commutation
(Figure
4
) permuting two adjacent columns (resp. rows) satisfying the following condition:
The projections of the two segments connecting two markings in each column (resp. row), into a single vertical circle
\beta_{j}
are either disjoint, or one is contained in the interior of the other.

•

(De-)stabilization
(Figure
5
) let
\mathbb{G}
be an
n\times n
grid diagram.
Then
\mathbb{G}^{\prime}
is called a stabilization of
\mathbb{G}
if it is an
(n+1)\times(n+1)
grid diagram obtained by the following procedure:
Choose a marked square in
\mathbb{G}
, and remove the marking in that square, in the other marked square in its row, and in the other marked square in its column.
Split the row and column into two.
There are four ways to put markings in the two new columns and rows in the
(n+1)\times(n+1)
grid to obtain a grid diagram.
There are four types of stabilizations when interchanging the roles of
X
’s and
O
’s.
The inverse of a stabilization is called a destabilization.

2.2.
Grid chain complexes

Let
\mathbb{G}
be an
n\times n
grid diagram.
A
state

\mathbf{x}
of
\mathbb{G}
is an
n
-tuple of points on the torus such that each horizontal circle
\alpha_{i}
has exactly one point and each vertical circle
\beta_{j}
has exactly one point of
\mathbf{x}
.
We denote by
\mathbf{S}(\mathbb{G})
the set of states of
\mathbb{G}
.
For
\mathbf{x,y}\in\mathbf{S}(\mathbb{G})
, a
domain

p
from
\mathbf{x}
to
\mathbf{y}
is a formal linear combination of the closures of the squares in
\mathbb{G}\setminus(\bm{\alpha}\cup\bm{\beta})
such that
\partial(\partial_{\alpha}p)=\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}
and
\partial(\partial_{\beta}p)=\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}
, where
\partial_{\alpha}p
is the portion of the boundary of
p
in the horizontal circles
\alpha_{1}\cup\dots\cup\alpha_{n}
and
\partial_{\beta}p
is the portion of the boundary of
p
in the vertical ones.
A domain
p
is
positive
if the coefficient of any square is nonnegative.
In this paper, we always consider positive domains such that there is at least one square whose coefficient is zero for each row and column.
We often call such a positive domain non-periodic.
Let
\pi^{+}(\mathbf{x,y})
denote the set of such domains from
\mathbf{x}
to
\mathbf{y}
.
For two states
\mathbf{x,y\in S}(\mathbb{G})
with
|\mathbf{x\cap y}|=n-2
, an
rectangle

r\in\pi^{+}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
is a domain such that
\partial r
is the union of four segments.
Let
\mathrm{Rect}(\mathbf{x,y})
be the set of rectangles from
\mathbf{x}
to
\mathbf{y}
.
A rectangle
r\in\mathrm{Rect}(\mathbf{x,y})
is
empty
if
\mathbf{x}\cap\mathrm{Int}(r)=\mathbf{y}\cap\mathrm{Int}(r)=\emptyset
.
Let
\mathrm{Rect}^{\circ}(\mathbf{x,y})
be the set of empty rectangles from
\mathbf{x}
to
\mathbf{y}
.
If
|\mathbf{x\cap y}|\neq n-2
, then we define
\mathrm{Rect}^{\circ}(\mathbf{x,y})=\emptyset
.
For two domains
p_{1}\in\pi(\mathbf{x,y})
and
p_{2}\in\pi(\mathbf{y,z})
, the
composite domain

p_{1}*p_{2}
is the domain from
\mathbf{x}
to
\mathbf{z}
such that the coefficient of each square is the sum of the coefficient of the square of
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
.
In this paper, a rectangle is called a square if its width and height are the same.

We denote the set of
O
-markings by
\mathbb{O}
and the set of
X
-markings by
\mathbb{X}
.
We will use the labeling of markings as
\{O_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
\{X_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
.

A
planar realization
of a toroidal diagram
\mathbb{G}
is a planar figure obtained by cutting it along some
\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{j}
and putting it on
[0,n)\times[0,n)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
in a natural way.

For two points
(a_{1},a_{2}),(b_{1},b_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we say
(a_{1},a_{2})<(b_{1},b_{2})
if
a_{1}<b_{1}
and
a_{2}<b_{2}
.
For two sets of finitely points
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, let
\mathcal{I}(A,B)
be the number of pairs
a\in A,b\in B
with
a<b
and let
\mathcal{J}(A,B)=(\mathcal{I}(A,B)+\mathcal{I}(B,A))/2
.
We consider that
n
points of states are on lattice point on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and each
O
- and
X
-marking is located at
(l+\frac{1}{2},m+\frac{1}{2})
for some
l,m\in\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}
.
We regard a state
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{S}(\mathbb{G})
as a set of
n
lattice points in
[0,n)\times[0,n)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Definition 2.1
.

Take a planar realization of
\mathbb{G}
.
For
\mathbf{x\in S}(\mathbb{G})
, the Maslov grading
M(\mathbf{x})
and the Alexander grading
A(\mathbf{x})
are defined by

(2.1)

\displaystyle M(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbb{O},\mathbf{x}-\mathbb{O})+1,

(2.2)

\displaystyle A(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{x},\mathbb{X}-\mathbb{O})+\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{O}+\mathbb{X},\mathbb{O}-\mathbb{X})-\frac{n-1}{2}.

Both the Maslov grading and the Alexander grading are well-defined as a toroidal diagram
[
16
, Lemma 2.4]
.

For a positive domain
D\in\pi^{+}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
and
i=1,\dots,n
, let
O_{i}(D)
be the coefficient of the square containing
O_{i}
and let

|D\cap\mathbb{O}|=\sum_{i=1}^{n}O_{i}(D).

We define
|D\cap\mathbb{X}|
in the same way.
The Maslov and Alexander gradings satisfy

(2.3)

M(\mathbf{x})-M(\mathbf{y})=1-2|r\cap\mathbb{O}|+2|\mathbf{x}\cap\mathrm{Int}(r)|,

and

(2.4)

A(\mathbf{x})-A(\mathbf{y})=|r\cap\mathbb{X}|-|r\cap\mathbb{O}|.

for any rectangle
r\in\mathrm{Rect}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
.
[
21
, Proposition 4.3.3]

In general, for a positive, non-periodic domain
D\in\pi^{+}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
, there is a sequence of states

\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{k}=\mathbf{y}
and rectangles
r_{i}\in\mathrm{Rect}(\mathbf{x}_{i-1},\mathbf{x}_{i})
such that
r_{1}*\dots*r_{k}=D
.
Then, we have

(2.5)

A(\mathbf{x})-A(\mathbf{y})=\sum(|r_{i}\cap\mathbb{X}|-|r_{i}\cap\mathbb{O}|)=|D\cap\mathbb{X}|-|D\cap\mathbb{O}|.

In the original construction, the minus version of grid homology is defined first, and the hat and tilde versions are then obtained from it
[
21
]
.
Since only the hat version of grid homology is used in the main results, we define the hat and tilde versions without introducing the minus version.
The hat version
\widehat{GH}(K)
is an invariant of knots, but the tilde version
\widetilde{GH}(K)
is not.
However,
\widehat{GH}(K)
is recovered from
\widetilde{GH}(K)
by applying a correction depending on the grid size.
Sometimes it is convenient to deal with the tilde version because the tilde grid complex is much simpler than the hat grid complex.
The precise definitions of the minus, hat, and tilde versions are described in
[
21
]
.

Definition 2.2
.

The grid chain complex
\widetilde{GC}(\mathbb{G})
is an
\mathbb{F}
-vector space generated by
\mathbf{S}(\mathbb{G})
, with the endmorphism defined by

\widetilde{\partial}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{S}(g)}\#\{r\in\mathrm{Rect}^{\circ}(\mathbf{x,y})|r\cap\mathbb{O}=r\cap\mathbb{X}=\emptyset\}\cdot\mathbf{y},

where
\#\{\cdot\}
counts rectangles modulo
2
.
The grid chain complex
\widetilde{GC}(\mathbb{G})
is a bigraded chain complex, i.e.,
\widetilde{GC}(\mathbb{G})
is a bigraded vector space with respect to the Maslov and Alexander grading, and the differential
\widetilde{\partial}
satisfies
\widetilde{\partial}\circ\widetilde{\partial}=0
and drops the Maslov grading by one, and preserves the Alexander grading.

The homology of
(\widetilde{GC}(\mathbb{G}),\widetilde{\partial})
is denoted by
\widetilde{GH}(\mathbb{G})
.

Let
W
be a two-dimensional graded vector space

.
For a bigraded
\mathbb{F}
-vector space
X
, the
shift
of
X
, denoted
X\llbracket a,b\rrbracket
, is the bigraded
\mathbb{F}
-vector space so that
X\llbracket a,b\rrbracket_{d,s}=X_{d+a,s+b}
.
Then, we have

X\otimes W\cong X\oplus X\llbracket 1,1\rrbracket.

Theorem 2.3

(
[

, Theorem 1.2, Proposition 2.15]

.

The bigraded vector space

5.2.
The complex of a planar grid

Let
\mathbb{E}
be an

planar
grid of squares such that there are
n

O
-markings from top left to bottom right and some

-markings.
Define the horizontal and vertical line
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n+1}

\{\beta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n+1}

(\alpha_{\sigma(1)}\cap\beta_{1},\dots,\alpha_{\sigma(n+1)}\cap\beta_{n+1})

In this subsection, we only consider the domains such that every coefficient is at most one.
We remark that, since
\mathbb{E}
is planar, a rectangle is a connected domain in the plane.
Let
\mathbf{x}_{0}=\mathbf{x}_{0}(\mathbb{E})
be the state
(\alpha_{n+1}\cap\beta_{1},\alpha_{n}\cap\beta_{2},\dots,\alpha_{1}\cap\beta_{n+1})

Let

be the positive domain whose coefficients of all squares equal one.
We denote by

the collection of domains

such that

is positive and
|(D_{\mathbb{E}}-D)\cap\mathbb{O}|=|(D_{\mathbb{E}}-D)\cap\mathbb{X}|=0
for some state
\mathbf{x}
.
The set

is a poset with respect to inclusion.

Definition 5.3
.

Let

be the span of
\pi^{+}(\mathbb{E})
and define the linear map
\partial_{\mathbb{E}}\colon C(\mathbb{E})\to C(\mathbb{E})
by

\partial_{\mathbb{E}}(D)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}D^{\prime}\in\pi^{+}(D_{\mathbb{E}})\\
D-D^{\prime}\text{ is a rectangle}\end{subarray}}D^{\prime}.

Then

is a graded
\mathbb{F}
-vector space with

for

.

Proposition 5.4
.

The vector space

is a graded chain complex, i.e.
C(\mathbb{E})=\bigoplus_{i=0}^{n-1}C_{i}(\mathbb{E})
and
\partial(C_{i}(\mathbb{E}))\subset C_{i-1}(\mathbb{E})
.

Proof.

The differential
\partial_{\mathbb{E}}
decreases the grading by one by definition.

Let
D^{\prime}
be one of the terms of
\partial_{\mathbb{E}}\circ\partial_{\mathbb{E}}(D)
.
Then the positive domain
D-D^{\prime}
is the composition of two rectangles, and moreover, it is a hexagon or two disjoint rectangles.
As in the proof that grid complexes are chain complexes (for example,
[
21
, Lemma 4.6.7]
), there are exactly two ways to decompose

into two rectangles.
Thus we have
\partial_{\mathbb{E}}\circ\partial_{\mathbb{E}}=0
.
∎

Proposition 5.5
.

(1)

If
\pi^{+}(\mathbb{E})=\{D_{\mathbb{E}}\}
, then
H(C(\mathbb{E}))
is one-dimensional.

(2)

If
\pi^{+}(\mathbb{E})\neq\{D_{\mathbb{E}}\}
and
\mathbb{E}
has no pair of
X
-markings symmetric with respect to the diagonal line from top left to bottom right, then

is acyclic.

Proof.

The case
\pi^{+}(\mathbb{E})=\{D_{\mathbb{E}}\}
is obvious.

Suppose that
\pi^{+}(\mathbb{E})\neq\{D_{\mathbb{E}}\}
.
For a domain
D\in\pi^{+}(\mathbb{E})
with
D\in\pi^{+}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{0})
, let

sw(D)=\{x_{i}\in\mathbf{x}|x_{i}\text{ is a southwest corner of }D\text{ and }x_{i}\notin\mathbf{x}_{0}\},

and

ne(D)=\{x_{i}\in\mathbf{x}|x_{i}\text{ is a northeast corner of }D\text{ and }x_{i}\notin\mathbf{x}_{0}\}.

Then we have
sw(D)+ne(D)+gr(D)=n+1
.
Choose a domain
D_{1}
that minimizes the grading.
Since the differential must increase either

or

by one, there is another domain
D_{2}
such that
\partial(D_{2})=D_{1}
.
Then the two-dimensional vector space
\mathbb{F}\{D_{1},D_{2}\}
is an acyclic subcomplex of

.
Replace
C(\mathbb{E})
with the quotient
C(\mathbb{E})/\mathbb{F}\{D_{1},D_{2}\}
.

Now, it is sufficient to show that
|\pi^{+}(\mathbb{E})|\in 2\mathbb{N}
.
Since
\mathbb{E}
has no pair of
X
-markings symmetric with respect to the diagonal, there is a point
x_{i}\in\mathbf{x}_{0}
such that exactly one of
\mathcal{I}(\{x_{i}\},\mathbb{X})
and
\mathcal{I}(\mathbb{X},\{x_{i}\})
is nonzero.
Then, there is a natural bijection between the set of domains one of whose corners is
x_{i}
and the set of other domains.
Thus, we have
|\pi^{+}(\mathbb{E})|\in 2\mathbb{N}
.
∎

Proposition 5.6
.

If
\mathbb{E}
has no

-marking, we have

H(C_{*}(\mathbb{E}))\cong\begin{cases}\mathbb{F}&*=0\\
0&*\neq 0.\end{cases}

Proof.

Add

-markings.
Then

inherits a filtration

where the filtration level of the domain

of

is

if

contains

The differential preserves this filtration as the filtration level decreases by

when the differential counts domains containing

We can observe that the lowest filtration level

In this section, we consider the grid homology at

for a prime diagonal knot

.
Again, we assume (

) and (

) in Section

.

By Lemma

and (
2.4
), for any state
\mathbf{x}
with
(M(\mathbf{x}),A(\mathbf{x}))=(-1,g(K)-2)

such that

This equation implies that such a state

determines a representation of

-tangles, as follows.

Definition 6.1
.

For a square domain
D
with
|D\cap\mathbb{O}|-|D\cap\mathbb{X}|=2
, define a
2
-tangle
T_{D}
obtained as follows:

•

If a row or column of
D
has both an
O
-marking and an
X
-marking, then connect them by an oriented segment.
The orientation is taken as in the case of grid diagrams.

•

If a row has an
O
-marking but no

-marking, then draw a ray from the
O
-marking to the right.

•

If a column has an
O
-marking but no
X
-marking, then draw a ray from the
O
-marking downward.

•

Finally, for each intersection, assume that the vertical segment (or ray) passes over the horizontal one.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a minimal grid diagram representing a prime diagonal knot.
Let
s<-1
.
For any state
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{S}_{s,g(K)-2}(\mathbb{G})
, the associated domain
D\in\pi^{+}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{0})
has coefficients at most one in each square.
If
D
is connected, then there are a state
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{x}_{-1,g(K)-2}(\mathbb{G})
and its associated square domain

such that
D
is contained in
D^{\prime}
and

|D\cap\mathbb{O}|=|D^{\prime}\cap\mathbb{O}|,|D\cap\mathbb{X}|=|D^{\prime}\cap\mathbb{X}|.

Proof.

First, we assume that the associated domain
D\in\pi^{+}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{0})
has coefficients at most one in each square.
Let
D^{\prime}
be the minimal square domain that contains
D
.
It is sufficient to show that
|(D-D^{\prime})\cap\mathbb{X}|=0
.
Since
D^{\prime}
is a square, we have
|D^{\prime}\cap\mathbb{O}|\geq|D^{\prime}\cap\mathbb{X}|
and hence
|(D^{\prime}-D)\cap\mathbb{X}|\leq 2
.
The primeness of

implies
|(D-D^{\prime})\cap\mathbb{X}|\neq 1
.
If
|(D^{\prime}-D)\cap\mathbb{X}|=2
, then we have

, which implies that

covers the entire

or that

represents a split link.

If
D
has coefficients more than one in some squares, let

be the domain obtained from

by setting all non-zero coefficients to one.
Then we have
|D^{\prime\prime}\cap\mathbb{O}|-|D^{\prime\prime}\cap\mathbb{X}|\leq|D\cap\mathbb{O}|-|D\cap\mathbb{X}|=2
.
By the same argument above, the minimal square domain
D^{\prime}
containing
D^{\prime\prime}
represents a nontrivial knot or split link component.
∎

Let

is an integer tangle if and only if

is not essential and contains no essential square domain.

Proof.

Suppose that

is an integer tangle and that

Let

Construct the

such that the upper-left and lower-right

\mathbb{G}
represents a

We remark that each of the upper-right and lower-left

\mathbb{G}
contains exactly two

\mathbb{G}
represents a knot.

Now Lemma

∎

The unknotting numbers of diagonal knots

In this section, we prove
u(K)=g(K)
for diagonal knots.

Proof of Theorem
1.18
.

The idea is that if we exchange an appropriate crossing of a diagonal knot
K
, we obtain a diagonal knot
K^{\prime}
such that
g(K^{\prime})=g(K)-1
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a minimal grid diagram representing a diagonal knot
K
.
Consider the diagram of
K
obtained from
\mathbb{G}
by drawing horizontal and vertical segments.
Let
c
be the number of crossings of the diagram.
Let
F
be the canonical Seifert surface of
K
obtained by Seifert’s algorithm and
d
be the number of Seifert circles.
By the proof of
[
24
, Theorem 1]
,
F
is the fiber of the fibration and thus
F
attains the minimal genus.
Since
\mathbb{G}
is diagonal, there is one Seifert circle that contains the others.

Choose one of the innermost Seifert circles
d_{i}
.
Let
m
be the number of half-twisted bands connecting to the circle
d_{i}
.
Since
\mathbb{G}
is minimal, we have
m>1
.
Since
\mathbb{G}
is diagonal, all bands are attached from the upper right or lower left of the disk
d_{i}
.
The minimality of
\mathbb{G}
ensures that there is a crossing of
K
whose distance from the northeast or southwest corner of
d_{i}
is
1/2
.
If we exchange the crossing, we can obtain a diagonal knot with the genus
g(K)-1
, as follows.

(1)

If
m>3
, the resulting diagonal knot has
c-2
crossings and
d
Seifert circles (top of Figure
15
).

(2)

If
m=3
, the resulting diagonal knot has
c-3
crossings and
d-1
Seifert circles (middle of Figure
15
).

(3)

If
m=2
, the resulting diagonal knot has
c-2
crossings and
d
Seifert circles (bottom of Figure
15
).

By repeating this procedure, we conclude that
u(K)=g(K)
.
∎
