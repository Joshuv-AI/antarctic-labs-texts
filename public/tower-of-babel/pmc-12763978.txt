Insights and implications: an analysis of the studies undertaken by Health Technology Assessment in India (HTAIn)
Authors: Varshini Neethi Mohan, V R Muraleedharan, Rekha Subramanian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHealth technology assessment sets explicit priorities to maximize health gains and support universal health coverage. Health Technology Assessment in India (HTAIn) was set up to ensure efficient allocation of public health expenditure impacting 1.4 billion Indians. We conducted an analysis to summarize the research undertaken by HTAIn thus far and recommend the way forward.MethodsWe shortlisted 33 of the 39 studies published by HTAIn on their website in October 2023. We analysed diseases treated, types of interventions evaluated, aspects of HTA addressed and whether the intervention was cost effective. The quality of each study was rated against Drummond’s checklist and the Indian Reference Case. Results were charted and tabulated where possible, and a narrative synthesis of findings was set down.ResultsOverall, 24.2% (n = 8) of the studies focused on interventions for maternal and neonatal disorders, 33.3% (n = 11) of the interventions were devices, and 45.5% (n = 15) were intended for screening. All of the studies addressed economic efficiency, 75.8% (n = 25) evaluated clinical efficacy, and 54.5% (n = 18) spoke of effectiveness; 45.5% (n = 15) addressed all three. Against the Indian Reference Case, six studies scored above 8, and the rest scored between 4.55 and 7.95. Equity and heterogeneity (population subgroup analysis) are the parameters that fared worst.ConclusionsA formal framework for choosing interventions for evaluation would make the decision-making process explicit. While difficult to evaluate, operational feasibility, equity and heterogeneity need to be appraised to ensure that policy-makers draw considered conclusions. We recommend that areas for disinvestment be identified, given health budget constraints.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12961-025-01422-9.Keywords: Health technology assessment, HTA, India, HTAIn, Economic evaluation

Introduction

Health technology assessment (HTA) aims to strengthen evidence-based decision making in healthcare. It helps prioritize public health spending, allowing policy-makers to allocate limited resources to the most cost–effective interventions and technologies [1] and to craft benefit packages for public reimbursement or insurance programmes, ensuring coverage of necessary medical services while preserving financial viability [2, 3]. HTA can also direct the development of national or state-level pricing plans for new medications, enabling fair access to cutting-edge therapies [4, 5], and of evidence-based clinical practice standards, improving the uniformity of healthcare delivery [6].

HTA has now been adopted by several countries across the world. According to the WHO’s Health Technology Assessment Survey 2020/2021, of the 121 countries that responded, 82% had a formalized process for decision-making with regard to health [7].

The Government of India aspires to achieve universal health coverage (UHC), providing accessible and affordable healthcare services to all of its 1.4 billion citizens [8]. In 2021, out-of-pocket spending stood at 49.8% of the total current health expenditure [9]. Already scarce health resources [10, 11] are further strained by the double burden caused by the increasing prevalence of noncommunicable diseases, while infectious diseases remain predominant [12]. This makes the optimal allocation and utilization of resources imperative. Towards these aims, the Planning Commission (now the National Institution for Transforming India (NITI) Aayog − a Government of India-run public policy think tank) convened a high-level expert group on UHC, which entrusted the mandate for a “Health Impact Assessment” to the Department of Health Research (DHR) within the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare (MoHFW) in the Twelfth Five-Year Plan (2012–2017) [13].

The National Health Policy (2017) committed to developing the capacity and the institutional framework for a health technology assessment [14]. Thereafter, Health Technology Assessment in India (HTAIn) was introduced to “design and institutionalize HTA that embodies modern best international practice which features transparent, inclusive, fair and evidence-based decisions”. HTAIn comprises an in-house Secretariat, a Board, a Technical Appraisal Committee (TAC) consisting of experts in the field, 16 Regional Resource Centres and 10 Technical Partners across the country [15]. HTAIn receives “topics” or interventions of interest from state and central government departments as well as from private agents. The TAC prioritizes these and allocates the topic to a suitable research centre for economic evaluation and assessment. Once the study is complete and approved by both the TAC and the Board, the HTAIn Board prepares a policy brief to advise the Government of India on the adoption or introduction of interventions.

HTAIn has carried out a number of economic evaluation studies since 2018 [16]. A detailed understanding of the scope, quality and rigour of these studies can help identify methodological gaps and, by recommending improvements, increase their role in decision-making processes [17, 18]. In the recent past, a few analyses have been carried out on economic evaluations in India. One study evaluated the quality of 29 pharmacoeconomic studies in India in 2012 [19] and another conducted a systematic review of 104 economic evaluations of healthcare interventions in India in 2015 [16]. There are several ways of assessing quality of economic evaluations for policy-making, including measuring adherence to nationally-defined guidelines and benchmarking against international standards [20].

The overarching objective of this paper is to analyse the studies conducted by HTAIn and make recommendations to improve the quality and relevance of these economic evaluations. Towards this end, the paper intends to (1) classify the studies according to disease addressed and (2) the type of the intervention; (3) examine whether efficacy, effectiveness and efficiency are considered; (4) analyse the quality of the studies by scoring them against both Drummond’s checklist and the Indian Reference Case; and (5) evaluate how many of the interventions considered were deemed cost–effective.

The “Introduction” section introduces HTA and HTAIn. The “Methods” section describes the methodology, with subsections “Study selection” and “Data extraction and synthesis”. The results are provided in the section titled “Results”, and the “Discussion” section discusses the current state of HTA research in India. The “Conclusions” section concludes this paper with recommendations for the way forward for HTAIn.

Methods

Study selection

The HTAIn website (https://htain.dhr.gov.in/) was accessed on 15 October 2023, and the 39 studies undertaken and published until then were considered in this study.

As we developed familiarity with the subject, inclusion and exclusion criteria were developed post hoc. We read all the studies in full and identified the relevant ones iteratively. We excluded studies that described protocols and did not assess any particular health technology (n = 2), as well as those that were summaries of other reports or were undertaken as a rapid HTA (n = 3). We also excluded a study that evaluated eight separate interventions (n = 1). Out of the 39 studies published by HTAIn, we included 33 in our analysis. Data extraction and synthesis

Two of the authors independently extracted the data by dividing the total studies into two sets towards the review objective. A standardized form for data extraction was devised to gather both general and methodological information from the selected studies. The form consisted of five sections: (1) study characteristics, including year of publication, collaborating institution, geographical area and disease addressed; (2) intervention characteristics; (3) whether the study addressed efficacy, effectiveness and efficiency; (4) quality parameters of the studies based on Drummond’s checklist [21] and the Indian Reference Case [22]; and (5) whether the study found the intervention cost–effective and the threshold used.

The top 20 causes for disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) lost in India according to the Global Burden of Disease (2019) initiative [23] were plotted against the number of studies undertaken that addressed these diseases.

Interventions were categorized by type – test, device, medicine, vaccine, procedure, programme or system; and by application – prevention, screening, diagnosis, treatment, rehabilitation and palliation.

A health technology assessment study typically asks three questions of the intervention evaluated: (1) Does it work? (2) Can it work? and (3) Is it worth it? The answers to these questions correspond to the efficacy, the effectiveness and the efficiency of the intervention, respectively [24]. Efficacy is generally established through randomized clinical trials (RCTs) [25] that ensure that the desired effect is attributable to the intervention [26]. Effectiveness measures how well the intervention works in real-world circumstances, usually measured by feasibility studies that consider aspects such as health-seeking behaviour, access, availability of resources, health provider actions and patient adherence [27]. Efficiency is measured by economic evaluations that can consider (a) cost minimization, (b) cost–effectiveness, (c) cost–utility and (d) cost–benefit [28]. We examined which of these aspects each study addressed.

A systematic literature review of 346 studies on use of checklists in reviewing health economic evaluations between 2010 and 2018 found that Drummond’s checklist was the most commonly used (117, 30%) [29]. We have used this as the most relevant checklist internationally. To evaluate the studies in the Indian context, we considered the HTA-Quality Assessment Checklist (HTA-QAC) [30] and the Indian Reference Case (IRC) [31], both developed and recommended as quality appraisal tools by HTAIn. We chose the IRC as the checklist to use over the HTA-QAC, as each criterion has been defined in more detail, and recommendations have been set down for each parameter in the former as compared with the latter, thus lending itself to the rating scale described in the following paragraphs. Evaluating against both the most widely-used checklist internationally and the most rigorous Indian one allows us to benchmark against international standards as well as check adherence with Indian standards.

Drummond’s checklist provides a framework to assess and critique economic evaluations by asking a set of 10 questions about whether: (1) the research question was clear, (2) competing alternatives were defined, (3) effectiveness was established, (4, 5 and 6) all costs and consequences were identified, evaluated correctly and in proper units, (7) discounting was carried out, (8) incremental analysis was performed, (9) uncertainty was accounted for and (10) the discussion included limitations and issues of concern [21]. Each item on the checklist is assigned a potential maximum score of 1, and the study’s aggregate score was calculated out of 10 [22].

The studies were also assessed against the Indian Reference Case [22], a set of standards developed specifically for economic evaluations for health technology assessments conducted in India. These address the following aspects: (1) decision problem, (2) comparator, (3) perspective, (4) effectiveness, (5) measuring costs, (6) health outcome, (7) time horizon, (8) discounting, (9) heterogeneity, (10) uncertainty analysis and (11) equity. The rating scale is presented in the Supplementary Material (Supplementary Table S2). The rating for each study was normalized to a scoring out of 10. The cumulative score for each standard was also normalized as a percentage to arrive at an adherence score for that parameter.

We used Google Sheets to work on data extraction collaboratively. Extracted data were categorized into subgroups and presented in charts and tables. Given the heterogeneity in outcomes, settings and study designs, a narrative review allows for a structured summary, facilitating analysis and critique [31]. Data synthesis, therefore, aimed to give a narrative review of interventions and results.

Interventions that addressed maternal and neonatal disorders represented the highest proportion of the studies undertaken, with 24.2% (n = 8) of the studies against a 10.0% disease burden by DALY among the top 20 causes. Out of the 33 studies, 12.1% (n = 4) were undertaken for interventions related to sense-organ diseases against a 2.9% DALY burden. Overall, 9.1% (n = 3) of the studies were undertaken for interventions related to cancer (neoplasms) against 5.9% of the disease burden. Interventions assessed by type and application

The types of interventions were classified as devices, procedures, programs, tests and drugs, and their applications were classified as prevention, screening, diagnosis, treatment, rehabilitation, palliation or a combination of these [33]. This is shown in Fig. 2.

Intervention types and applications

Among the 11 devices evaluated, 4 were for treating diseases, 3 for screening, 3 for diagnosis and 1 for prevention. Seven procedures were assessed, with six aimed at treating diseases and one at prevention. Seven tests were evaluated, with six meant for screening and one for diagnosis. All six programmes evaluated were intended to screen for diseases, with five of these also for diagnosis and two of these meant for treatment as well. Both drugs evaluated were intended for treatment.

Of the 33 interventions, 15 were for screening (5 of which were also for diagnosis, 2 of which were also for treatment), 12 were for treatment, 4 were for diagnosis and 2 were for prevention. None of the interventions addressed rehabilitation or palliation.

Aspects of HTA addressed

Among the 33 studies assessed, all (100%) performed an economic evaluation, as this was the key inclusion criterion. In total, 25 (75.8%) addressed clinical efficacy, generally through a literature review, either targeted or systematic, and 18 (54.5%) measured effectiveness in a real-world setting through primary studies, stakeholder interviews or literature reviews. Fifteen studies (45.5%) evaluated all three aspects. This is shown in Fig. 3.

Rating against Drummond’s checklist and the Indian Reference Case

We analysed the quality of economic evaluations by employing both Drummond’s checklist and that of the overall HTA using the Indian Reference Case (IRC) [34].

There were some limitations in valuing certain costs and consequences because of the lack of data specific to the Indian context, and the nonhomogeneous nature of the population and the conditions across the country.

Rated against the IRC, only 6 (18.2%) of the reports scored above 8, while 27 (81.8%) scored above 8 against Drummond’s checklist. The rest (81.8% and 18.2%, respectively) scored between 4.55 and 7.95 and between 5.5 and 7.5 against the IRC and Drummond’s checklist, respectively (Table 2). A Pearson’s correlation analysis demonstrated a statistically significant positive correlation between the scores against the two checklists (r = 0.680, p < 0.001).

The decision problem was clearly stated in almost all the studies, with a 95.6% guideline adherence. The comparator was also described sufficiently in almost all the studies, with a score of 94.9%.

It has been argued that the perspective, whether health system, patient or societal, needs to reflect the policy-makers’ requirements, and the role of the analyst is to state the perspective explicitly [35]. The IRC recommends the evaluation of health technology using an abridged societal perspective for both costs and outcomes – this includes all direct and indirect costs incurred by both the health system and the patient but does not include productivity loss [34, 36]. As recommended, the abridged societal perspective was adopted in 27 reports out of 33 and scored 86.8% overall. Twelve studies measured all direct and indirect costs, including out-of-pocket expenditures, and also incorporated wage loss. Sixteen considered all costs other than productivity loss, looking at them from an abridged societal perspective. The overall guideline adherence was 77.2% for this metric.

Discounting at 3% to adjust for the differing time periods in which costs and benefits accrue was sufficiently carried out in 27 out of 33 studies, achieving an overall score of 82.4%. The time horizon was long enough to accurately capture all costs and consequences in 25 out of 33 studies, with 78.7% adherence.

Health outcomes were measured appropriately, with quality of life assessed according to EuroQol-5 Dimensions-5 Levels (EQ-5D-5L) using an Indian set of values for four recent studies. In total, 24 studies assessed QALYs using international weights, and 3 used DALYs. Overall guideline adherence was 69.9%.

Uncertainty analyses with a one-way sensitivity analysis (OWSA) to calculate dependence on each input variable, a probability sensitivity analysis (PSA) to calculate how often the intervention is cost–effective on the cost–effectiveness plane and a threshold or a scenario analysis were conducted in six studies. In total, 22 performed either two (n = 16) or one (n = 6) of the uncertainty analyses, and 5 did not consider uncertainty. The overall score was 61.8%. The effectiveness of the intervention was assessed in 28 studies, of which 11 conducted comprehensive systematic reviews with meta-analyses, 7 conducted systematic reviews, 5 were observational trials and 5 performed targeted literature reviews. The adherence score for this metric was 58.8%. Equity was considered in 10 studies and scored 25.7% adherence. Heterogeneity, which considers how different subgroups of patients could respond differently to interventions, scored least out of all the parameters at 24.3%, with 10 studies identifying subgroups but only 6 performing subgroup analysis.

While the IRC does not consider budget impact analysis (BIA), national methodological guidelines were proposed in 2021 on how to conduct one [37]. Budget impact analysis was performed in 20 (60.6%) of the studies.

Cost–effectiveness

The intervention was cost–effective in 28 (84.8%) out of the 33 studies. Of these, 7 (21.2% of the total) were cost-saving interventions, with the intervention being cheaper than the comparator, and the remaining were cost–effective at a 1 × GDP per capita cost–effectiveness threshold.1 Of the remaining five studies, one established clinical effectiveness, accuracy and replicability, one identified the lowest-cost intervention and the other three (9.1%) found that the comparator (routine treatment) was more cost–effective than the intervention. Discussion

Our analysis of the studies undertaken by HTAIn throws up some interesting findings regarding topic selection, the aspects of HTA addressed, the quality of the evaluations and the threshold for cost–effectiveness.

The selection of interventions for study by HTAIn is decided by the Technical Advisory Committee (TAC), who consider requests from the central and state governments as well as private agencies, and other factors such as the burden of disease, the goals of the National Health Policy and alignment with the United Nations’ (UN) Sustainable Development Goals (SDG) [38]. That this selection is largely demand-driven explains the imbalance between the DALY burden and the proportion of studies assessing these diseases. Whether DALYs should be the primary metric used for priority-setting has been debated for decades [39]. It has been pointed out that they fail to capture broader social implications [40] and that they confound ideas of health and disability, implying that people with disabilities are less valuable than those without them [41]. Interventions evaluated, therefore, need not strictly be proportionate to the disease’s DALY burden. The priority-setting mechanism of the HTAIn could be evaluated, however, and formalized to indicate the parameters considered before undertaking a study, which should ideally include disease burden. In Thailand, for example, the national Health Intervention and Technology Assessment Program (HITAP) nominates and prioritizes topics for evaluation annually, in a process involving major stakeholders, including healthcare providers, payers, medical associations and academia [42]. Such processes would help HTAIn focus on interventions with the greatest impact.

In terms of the types and the applications of interventions assessed, the majority (n = 11, 33.3%) were devices. While 15 interventions were for screening for diseases, and 12 were for treatment, only 2 (6.0%) were aimed at prevention. Preventive interventions, in general, provide returns on investment in a longer timeframe when compared with treatment [43]. Perverse incentives might also come into play when increasing spending on treatment comes at the expense of interventions focussed on prevention. A Swedish study found that the willingness to pay (WTP) for prevention was higher than that for treatment, given the same outcomes [44]. The United States Preventive Services Task Force and the Partnership for Prevention assessed and found that several preventive measures provided excellent value [45]. The Productivity Commission estimated that Australia’s GDP could increase annually by $4 billion if chronic diseases were targeted with preventive measures [46]. There is a strong case to be made, then, for undertaking more preventive interventions for evaluation, with the caveats that the longer timeframes for the benefits to accrue must be properly accounted for, and uncertainty analysis must be conducted to allow for variations. None of the interventions addressed rehabilitative and palliative care. These could also be included for assessment.

Having discussed the choice of the interventions for which evaluations were undertaken, we now address the quality and comprehensiveness of the assessments themselves.

While 75.8% of the studies evaluated the clinical effectiveness of the intervention, only 54.5% addressed effectiveness or operational feasibility in real-world conditions. Less than half (45.5%) evaluated all three key aspects – efficacy, effectiveness and efficiency. Evaluating effectiveness is arguably an essential part of an HTA report, as policy-makers need to understand how the intervention can be integrated into the Indian health system. An implementation plan and issues regarding acceptability, adherence and adoption by the patient, provider and society at large are critical when deciding whether an intervention can work in that specific setting. This is even more important to evaluate considering the diverse and nonhomogeneous nature of India’s resources, health infrastructure, culture and population.

Against the Indian Reference Case, all the studies addressed the decision problem, the comparator, the perspective and the measured costs to some degree, scoring 70% and above on these metrics. Comprehensive uncertainty analyses, and systematic reviews with meta-analyses for clinical effectiveness were performed in 6 (18.2%) and 11 (33.3%) of the studies, respectively.

Equity and heterogeneity were not considered in 23 (69.7%) studies. According to the WHO, social determinants of health account for between 30% and 55% of health outcomes [47]. Addressing these would help reduce avoidable health inequities between groups divided socially, demographically and geographically. In India, 49.8% of health spending was out of pocket in 2021 [48]. High out-of-pocket expenditure (OOPE), in turn, pushes 55 million Indians into poverty every year [49]. Overall, 93.4% of the richest quintile had access to improved sanitation, while only 6% of the poorest quintile did in 2015–2016 [50]. Life expectancy for the richest quintile is 72.7 years, which is 7.6 years longer than the poorest quintile at 65.1 years. Health inequity is not limited to income levels. A study in Kerala showed that groups with lower socioeconomic status (SES) had a lower life expectancy (1.5–2 years shorter) than those belonging to a higher SES [51]. Gender also leads to inequities, with women faring worse in self-reported health [52]. Hospitalisation rate and mean health expenditure for women are lower despite higher morbidity across socioeconomic groups [53], and 91% of total health expenses incurred by the elderly are spent on men [54]. Considering that “minimizing inequality” is one of the three stated aims of HTAIn [15], equity has not been sufficiently measured in the studies included in this analysis. A distributional cost–effectiveness analysis can quantify equity impacts and trade-offs and make judgements on equity explicit [55]. The lack of disaggregated data poses a severe challenge. With the Cost of Health Services in India (CHSI) database and the new Indian EQ-5D-5L value set, we are only just beginning to value costs and outcomes for the Indian population. Data on different subgroups are even more difficult to gather and evaluate. However, equity and homogeneity remain important aspects to address while assessing health technologies, and distributional analysis for equity impact ought to be attempted.

The correlation between the scores against the Indian Reference Case and Drummond’s checklist was high, leading us to conclude that adherence to the Reference Case helps improve the quality of the economic evaluation against international standards as well.

All the studies that used a threshold to determine cost–effectiveness considered it as 1 × GDP per capita. The WHO Choosing Interventions that are Cost-Effective (WHO-CHOICE) project report in 2003 recommended that if the cost–effectiveness of an intervention was between 1 and 3 × times the GDP per capita per DALY, it can be deemed effective [56]. Several experts, including those at the WHO, have cautioned against using a GDP multiple as the sole threshold [57], as they do not consider opportunity costs or budgetary constraints. However, its ease of use means that it is still widely considered the CET [58]. Prinja et al. make the case that for a country such as India where public health expenditure is low, the threshold must be set according to the opportunity cost of current health spending, and should be used as a decision aid rather than as a rule [59].

The Department of Health and Family Welfare’s expenditure is budgeted to increase from the 77 625 crore (cr) (one crore equals ten million) rupees (Rs.) incurred in 2023–2024 to 87 657 cr Rs. in 2024–2025 [60]. While 28 of the 33 interventions were proposed as cost–effective, only 7 of them were cost-saving in the long run. Government funds would need to be allocated to these interventions, as is set down in the budget impact analyses. Even considering a 13% health expenditure increase as budgeted, this additional amount might not be allocated entirely to the interventions evaluated by HTAIn. The shortfall, then, needs to be disinvested from other, preferably less cost–effective interventions currently already in practice. A league table or the bookshelf method [61] might be drawn up to evaluate which these might be. While HTA helps decide on the introduction of new technologies, health technology reassessment (HTR) has been proposed to assess technologies already in use – “a structured, evidence-based assessment of the clinical, social, ethical, and economic effects of a technology currently used in the healthcare system, to inform optimal use of that technology in comparison to its alternatives” [62]. HTR is an iterative process to continuously reassess which interventions might be inefficient [63, 64] and therefore, might potentially be discontinued. Meaningful stakeholder engagement is essential, and the practical implications of disinvestment need careful consideration.

Limitations

We list here some of the limitations of our review – the type of intervention was classified according to the authors’ opinions. We chose two checklists among the several that exist for quality appraisal [Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS), Checklist for Health Economic Quality Evaluations (CHEQUE), Quality of Health Economic Studies (QHES), HTA-QAC, among others]. Evaluations against some of the parameters were subject to our interpretation. We have attempted to mitigate this by drawing up a rating scale, duplicating the data extraction process and reviewing any divergences. Other researchers might differ from our point of view, and reproducibility of these results might not be achievable. We analysed the outcome reports made available on the HTAIn website in October 2023; 38 more studies have since been undertaken and are in the pipeline, but these have not been made publicly available. The inclusion of these might result in different outcomes.

Conclusions

This analysis assessed the studies undertaken by HTAIn and provides a snapshot of the diseases covered and interventions employed and evaluates the quality of the economic evaluations performed. The evidence from the study leads us to the following recommendations for HTAIn – firstly, there is a need for an explicit framework for how interventions are selected for evaluation by HTAIn, considering burden of disease, SDG, National Health Policy, and central and state government priorities. Preventive interventions could be included for evaluation – as benefits accrue over longer timeframes, uncertainty analysis is key. Secondly, effectiveness and operational feasibility need to be evaluated for all interventions. Thirdly, adherence to the Indian Reference Case also improved quality of the study by international standards, so better adherence could strengthen the quality of economic evaluation in India. Distributional cost–effectiveness analyses to address equity and subgroup analyses to allow for heterogeneity could be included wherever disaggregated data are available. A cost–effectiveness threshold specific to the Indian scenario to aid decision-making should be arrived at, and lastly, given the limited resources available for health expenditure, health technology reassessment needs to be undertaken iteratively to identify potential areas for disinvestment – if we are unable to spend more, we must spend better.
