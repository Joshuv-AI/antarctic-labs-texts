Periodontal conditions and salivary microbiota are potential indicators to distinguish silicosis: an exploratory study
Authors: Shaoying Duan, Meiying Shao, Chenchen Zhang, Jialiang Zhao, Fangzhi Zhu, Nanyu Luo, Lei Lei, Ting Zhong, Tao Hu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSilicosis has always been a serious global occupational health problem. Oral microbiota plays important roles in the development of lung disease. However, few studies have investigated the relationship between periodontal conditions, oral bacteria and silicosis disease.MethodA single-center and cross-sectional study was conducted in 2019 in Sichuan Province, China, including a small sample of silicosis patient group and healthy control group. Demographic data and periodontal examinations measured by clinical attachment loss (CAL), bleeding on probing (BOP) and periodontal pocket (PD) were collected from each participant. Phenotypic changes were detected by histopathological staining. Next-generation sequencing targeting 16S ribosomal RNA was targeted to decipher the salivary microbiome of the two groups. Random forest, Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) logistic regression and multivariable logistic regression analysis were conducted to find potential indicators to distinguish silicosis.ResultsIn general, 29 male healthy controls and 24 male silicosis patients were included. The proportion of CAL ≥ 3 mm in silicosis group was greater than control group, while the proportion of BOP (+) and PD ≥ 4 mm was reduced in silicosis group. The α-smooth muscle actin and fibronectin expression increased in gingiva of patients. The composition of salivary microbiota exhibited significant differences between the two groups, with silicosis patients demonstrating a lower diversity of salivary microbiota. Genus of Aggregatibacter [odds ratio (OR) = 0.000, p = 0.003] and Catonella (OR = 0.000, p = 0.049) were identified as biomarkers to distinguish silicosis.ConclusionsThe silicosis group exhibited worse CAL, improved BOP and PD, which may be related to the gingival fibrosis found in this study. The composition of the oral microbiota underwent significant changes, accompanied by a decrease in diversity, in patients with silicosis. Our study indicates that respirable crystalline silica exposure affects oral health, and alterations of oral microbiota might be implicated in silicosis. We primarily identified Aggregatibacter and Catonella as the potential indicators to distinguish silicosis patients from healthy controls.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-024-03594-w.

Background

Silicosis is a progressive, incurable, chronic lung disease, which is globally prevalent in those who working in mining, stone and construction industry [1]. Even with better work environment protection, the occurrence of silicosis cannot be completely eradicated. In China, the prevalence of silicosis among coal miner is alarmingly as high as 6% [2]. Not only in developing countries or underdeveloped countries, but in developed countries, such as Israel, Australia, Spain and the United States, new silicosis cases have surged in recent years due to the proliferation of artificial stone [3]. According to the data from Global Burden of Disease Study 2019 [4], silicosis incident, prevalent cases, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) increased by 64.6%, 91.4%, and 20.8%, respectively. Silicosis has always been a serious global occupational health problem.

Silicosis is caused by occupational exposure to respirable crystalline silica [5], particulates typically measuring less than 5 μm in diameter. Crystalline silica instigates a persistent state of inflammation, tissue damage, and fibrosis, ultimately culminating in respiratory disability [6]. Though miners take precautionary measures during their work, the inadvertent inhalation of crystalline silica into mouth through mouth breathing may occur. A study shows that prolonged exposure fine particulate matter has been linked to an increased likelihood of developing periodontal disease [7]. There has been limited research on the periodontal health of coal mine workers, but the few studies available have indicated a poor periodontal status among this group [8, 9]. Nonetheless, studies investigating the periodontal health of patients with silicosis remain limited.

The human microbiome is potentially affected by the environmental changes [10]. Exposure fine particulate matter might impact oral microbiota [11]. Oral microbiota plays a critical role in the development of lung disease, constituting a potential “oral-lung axis” [12–14]. Research indicated that oral bacteria could affect the pulmonary microbiota via respiratory, micro-aspiration and mucosal dispersal, potentially impacting lung function and inflammation [15–17]. Saliva is a mixture of microorganisms that dislodged from different oral niches [17]. It is easy to collect, non-invasive, and does not require professional intervention, making it an ideal sample for studying oral microbiota [18]. A study has found that salivary microbiota are related to chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), especially with severe exacerbations [3]. Bacteria like Capnocytophaga and Veillonella in saliva are recognized as potential biomarkers for the identification and classification of lung cancer [19, 20]. Research indicates that the pathogenesis of silicosis, COPD, and lung cancer shares common pathogenetic characteristics, such as pyroptosis, chronic inflammation and the pivotal involvement of macrophages in disease onset and progression [21–23]. Hence, it is hypothesized that the occupational exposure to crystalline silica modifies the oral microbiota composition, potentially influencing the progression of silicosis diseases.

Thus, the purpose of the present study was to investigate the periodontal health of silicosis patients. A further objective was to perform 16S rRNA sequencing on the saliva samples obtained from both silicosis patients and healthy individuals to validate the distinction in oral microbiota composition. This study may be helpful for early distinguish and future treatment strategy of silicosis.

Methods

Study design and recruitment of participants

This study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of West China Hospital of Stomatology, Sichuan University (No. WCHSIRB-ST-2017-198). The study was registered at Clinical Trial Registration Center (No. ChiCTR1900021556). Written informed consent was obtained from each participant before enrollment. Participants include two groups: patients with silicosis, diagnosed according to the Diagnosis of Occupational Pneumoconiosis (GBZ 70–2015) clinical guideline; and healthy controls without occupational respirable crystalline silica exposure. The inclusion criteria for participants in this study were (1) adult and male (selected because the mining industry in China predominantly employs males, and the incidence of silicosis is significantly higher among them than among females) [24–26]), (2) have at least 20 teeth (excluding third molars), and (3) without medical history of tuberculosis. Participants were excluded if (1) antibiotics were used in the past 4 weeks or (2) periodontal scaling were treated within 6 months.

Questionnaire

Participants filled out questionnaires through one-on-one interview. The questionnaire includes demographic characteristics, smoking (No/Yes) and oral hygiene behaviors (i.e., frequency of tooth brushing, frequency of flossing). Demographic characteristics was recorded including age, body mass index (BMI) and education (“High”: “Tertiary or bachelor’s degree or above”, “Medium”: “Technical secondary school or high school”, “Low”: “Junior high school or below”).

All the questionnaire data were double entered for minimizing data entry errors. SPSS 22.0 software (IBM corporation, Armonk, NY, USA) was used to perform T-test, Chi-Square test, Rank sum test and One-Way ANOVA on different types of data, with the test level α = 0.05.

Oral examinations

Oral examinations were conducted by two trained dentists with cotton swabs, community periodontal index (CPI) probes, mirrors in accordance with the basic method of oral health survey recommended by World Health Organization [27–29]. Twenty-eight teeth except for the third molars were included into oral examination. The assessment encompassed the evaluation of gingival bleeding on probing (BOP), periodontal probing depth (PD), and clinical attachment loss (CAL). PD is quantified as the measurement from the gingival margin to the base of the periodontal pocket, while AL is defined as the measurement from the cementoenamel junction to the bottom of the periodontal pocket (Fig. 1A). Bleeding on probing positive ((BOP+), CAL greater than or equal to 3, and PD equal to or greater than 4 were tabulated for each group individually [30, 31]. Prior to the periodontal examination, two examiners underwent training by a standard examiner. Cohen’s kappa coefficient, which measures the consistency between or within examiners, was higher than 0.85 for the periodontal examination [27, 32]. All the examiners were blinded to the results of questionnaires. Data were entered into the EpiData 3.1 database using dual data entry.

Saliva collection

Before collecting saliva samples, the participants were informed to be fast for 2 h and not to brush their teeth in the morning. At least 3 mL saliva samples were collected using sterilized centrifuge tubes by the passive drooling method. The samples were immediately frozen in dry ice and stored at -80 °C.

Detection of phenotypic changes of gingival tissue

Gingival tissues were collected from impacted tooth extraction or dental implant surgery of 4 silicosis patients and 6 healthy volunteers. The samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 24 h, dehydrated in gradient alcohols, subsequently embedded with paraffin and sectioned according to routine method. The 5 μm slices were respectively subjected to hematoxylin-eosin staining (H&E), masson’s trichrome staining, and immunohistochemical staining. For immunohistochemical staining, the sliced samples were performed according to the manufacturer’s instruction. The primary antibodies used were anti-α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA; 1:300; Abcam, ab5694) and anti-fibronectin (FN; 1:200; Abcam, b2413).

DNA extraction, library preparation and 16S rRNA sequencing

The saliva samples were sent for 16S rRNA sequencing (V3-V4) to Novogene Corporation (Beijing, China). DNA was extracted from saliva samples using CTAB method according to protocol described in a previous study [33]. The purity and concentration of DNA were checked by agarose gel electrophoresis. The 16S rRNA was amplified using 1 ng/μL of DNA as a template with the barcoded primer (341F:5’-CCTAYGGGRBGCASCAG-3’, 806R:5’-GGACTACNNGGGTATCTAAT-3’) for saliva [34]. The PCR products were purified using a Qiagen Gel Extraction Kit (Qiagen, Germany).

Sequencing libraries of saliva samples were prepared using TruSeq DNA PCR-Free Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, United States) according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. After Library quality evaluated by Qubit 2.0 fluorometer (Thermo Scientific) and Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system, the library was sequenced on Illumina HiSeq 2500 PE250 platform.

Microbiome community analysis

After cutting off the barcode and primer sequences, paired-end reads were merged using FLASH (V1.2.11) [35]. For saliva samples, quality control, sequence denoising, and chimera removal were implemented in the QIIME2 package (version 2020.2) according to the default parameters using DADA2. Subsequently, the amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) feature tables were generated, and species annotation was assigned to each ASV utilizing the Silva database (version 138). Alpha diversity was used to examine biodiversity through Shannon and Simpson, which were computed using the ‘Vegan’ package in R version 4.0.5 (http://www.r-project.org/). Beta diversity was applied to reflecting the differences between the groups through principal coordinates analysis (PCoA). PCoA was performed by ‘ape’ package in R and displayed by R software based on Bray-Curtis Dissimilarity metric. Linear discriminant analysis Effect Size (LefSe) was used to measure the different abundance of bacteria at genus levels between the two groups. Microbial taxa with linear discriminant analysis (LDA) scores > 3 and a p-value < 0.05 were considered significantly different. That is, biological markers with statistical differences.

A random forest model was constructed using the random forest package to assess the differences between silicosis patients and controls the salivary microbiota [36], and the Gini index was used to assess the importance of genus. Package ‘glmnet’ was used to perform Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) logistic regression analysis on the identified silicosis-related genera. This method allows for the selection of the most relevant genera by shrinking the coefficients of less important variables to zero, thus enhancing the model’s distinguishing performance and interpretability. A 5-fold cross-validation was performed to choose optimal lambda for the LASSO logistic regression analysis to ensure the best balance between model complexity and distinguishing accuracy.

Then, multivariable logistic regression analysis with forward stepwise (conditional) was employed to build a promising diagnostic model, incorporating the genera selected in the LASSO regression model. The genera were evaluated as odds ratio (OR) having 95% confidence interval (CI) and as p-value < 0.05 (two sided). The constructed model was evaluated by drawing a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (ROC 1.15.0 package), and the area under the ROC curve (AUC) evaluated the distinguishing effect of the microbiota.

Periodontal health of participants

To investigate periodontal health of silicosis patients, we conducted a comparative analysis of the periodontal status between silicosis group and control group. Participants with CAL ≥ 3 mm and more than 2 teeth with PD ≥ 4 mm were diagnosed with periodontitis according to the 2018 World Symposium classification system [37, 38]. Based on this criterion, 7 participants in the silicosis group and 11 in the control group were diagnosed with periodontitis. It revealed no statistically significant difference between the two groups by Chi-square test (p = 0.569). However, the periodontal indexes of the two groups were significantly different (Fig. 1B-D). While the proportion of CAL ≥ 3 mm in silicosis group (24.4%) was greater than that of control group (6.8%). The proportion of PD ≥ 4 mm and BOP (+) in the silicosis group (7.3% and 8.1%, respectively) was much lower than that of the control group (10.6% and 16.9%, respectively). Patients with silicosis displayed heightened CAL (associated with hard tissue) yet presented milder BOP and PD (linked with soft tissue).

Increased expression of α-SMA and fibronectin in the gingiva from patients with silicosis

The gingival manifestations observed in silicosis group resembled those seen in individuals with systemic sclerosis, suggesting that gingival fibrosis could be responsible for this distinct pattern of periodontal presentation [39]. Then, histopathological staining was conducted. For patients with silicosis, inflammatory cells in the gingival interstitium were infiltrated obviously (Fig. 2A), and a large number of muscle fibers were formed in collagen fibers (Fig. 2B). High expression levels of α-SMA exhibited within the gingival interstitial blood vessels and in the non-vascular regions of the gingival stroma among the silicosis group compared with healthy volunteers (p = 0.0064) (Fig. 2C). Interstitial FN expression in silicosis patients was significantly higher than that in the control group (p = 0.0004) (Fig. 2D). The current findings suggest that individuals with silicosis exhibit gingival fibrosis, which could potentially obscure signs of periodontal inflammation symptoms.

Bacteria diversity of salivary microbiota

Periodontal inflammation is notably associated with in the dysbiosis of oral microbiota, and is intricately linked to lung function and lung diseases [40, 41]. Our subsequent aim was to investigate whether a relationship exists between oral microbiota and silicosis. The oral microbiota of saliva was assessed using 16S rRNA sequencing. A total of 275,401 high-quality 16S rRNA gene sequences were identified, with a median read count of 14,118 (ranging from 6,350 to 18,753). After the sequence denoising using DADA2, 3,870 ASVs were obtained from participants (Table S1). Rarefaction curve (Fig. 3A) of each sample demonstrated an approaching plateau, suggesting sufficient sequencing depth across all samples. Similarly, the relative bacterial evenness was assessed through rank abundance curves, revealing consistent patterns in all samples (Fig. 3B). Alpha diversity unveiled the differences in bacterial diversity between the silicosis patient and healthy control group (Table S2). Analyzed by Wilcoxon rank-sum test, the results showed that salivary microbial alpha-diversity, indexed by Simpson and Shannon, was decreased significantly in the silicosis group compared to healthy control group (Fig. 3C-D, p < 0.001). Venn diagram showing the shared and unique ASVs detected in saliva from two groups (Fig. 3E). The number of ASVs observed in control group is fewer than those in silicosis group and a total 1567 ASVs were shared between the two groups. The β-diversity similarity analyses were performed by PCoA analysis based on Bray-Curtis distances and PERMANOVA (Table S3). PCoA indicated a separated profile between silicosis and control, with 42.7% and 7.9% variation in PCo1 and PCo2, respectively (Fig. 3F, p = 0.001).

Phylogenetic profiles of salivary microbial communities

The average composition of bacterial communities at the phylum and genus levels between silicosis group and healthy control group are shown in Fig. 4A-D, respectively. At the phylum level, Proteobacteria, Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, Fusobacteria and Actinobacteria were the top five bacterial phyla in the two groups (Fig. 3A, Table S4). Proteobacteria was significantly higher, while Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes were significantly lower in the silicosis group (Fig. 3B). At the genus level, substantial distinctions were observed in the relative abundance of genus in relation to the top 20 species (Fig. 3C). The salivary microbiome of the silicosis group exhibited elevated abundances of Stenotrophomonas in contrast to the control group (Fig. 3D, Table S5).

To identify differentially abundant taxa in silicosis patient and healthy control group, we performed LEfSe on salivary microbiota composition between these two groups. The resulting LDA histogram (Fig. 4E) revealed that 14 genus showing distinct relative abundances between silicosis and control (LDA score > 3.0, p < 0.05). The increased abundance of genera Streptococcus, Neisseria, and Aggregatibacter was observed in control group, and the genera Stenotrophomonas, Sphingomonas and Corynebacterium was the representative bacterial genera in the in the salivary microbiota of silicosis group. The cladogram shows the salivary microbiota community structures and the differences in in the predominant bacterial taxa between the two groups (Fig. 4F).

Distinguishing model of silicosis with control group

Our data showed that the differential microbiota varied between silicosis patients with healthy control groups. To investigate the classification power of salivary bacteria markers for patient and healthy control at the genus level, a random forest model was constructed (Table S6). The mean decrease Gini index is used to evaluate the importance of each genus. The faster the Gini index decreases, the greater the importance of the species in classification between groups. For the random forest analysis, 23 signature genera were selected. (Fig. 5A, Table S5). The 5-fold cross-validation shows that the optimal classification model can be obtained when the Lambda value of the LASSO analysis regression coefficient is 0.08144623 and the number of bacteria selected is 7 (Fig. 5B-C). Seven signature genera were selected via LASSO algorithms, containing Actinobacillus, Aggregatibacter, Alloprevotella, Catonella, Haemophilus, Lactobacillus, Porphyromonas (Table S7). After employing the forward stepwise method for multivariable logistic regression analysis, two bacterial genera Aggregatibacter (OR = 0.000, 95%CI: 0.000–0.000) and Catonella (OR = 0.000, 95%CI: 0.000-0.167) were ultimately selected to be included in the final discriminant model (Table 2). The AUC of Aggregatibacter, Catonella and combined model for classifying silicosis patients was 0.925 (95%CI: 0.847-1.000), 0.861 (95%CI: 0.763–0.960) and 0.932 (95%CI: 0.862-1.000), respectively (Fig. 5D-F). The high AUC of the combined model suggests that the oral microbiota of silicosis patients could potentially aid in diagnosing silicosis to some degree.

Discussion

In this single-center and cross-sectional study, we explored periodontal health and oral microbiota in a small sample of silicosis and controls, and constructed a distinguishing model by the two genera (Aggregatibacte, Catonella) to recognise silicosis. We observed that the silicosis patients exhibited a unique pattern of periodontal manifestation, characterized by lower gingival bleeding index, lower PD while higher periodontal CAL in contrast to healthy individuals. The salivary microbiome diversity was lower in patients compared with healthy group, and microbiome composition was notably distinct between the two groups. Following the application of a random forest algorithm, LASSO regression analysis and multivariable logistic regression analysis, two bacteria in saliva at genus level, Aggregatibacter and Catonella, were identified as promising diagnostic indicators for diagnosing silicosis. This study established a connection between periodontal health, oral microbiota, and silicosis, contributing to further exploration of the “oral-lung axis”. The findings hold potential implications for the distinguish and future treatment strategy of silicosis.

Given that silicosis patients often belong to a lower socioeconomic stratum in China, and recognizing the linkage between oral health and socioeconomic factors, we recruited individuals with economic profiles akin to this group as control group. Our study indicated that the two groups included in our study exhibited uniformity in terms of oral hygiene practices. This consistency was characterized by no difference in the prevalence of periodontitis, low brushing frequencies and dental flossing frequencies across all groups. Moreover, no statistically significant disparities were observed in terms of smoking status between them. This suggests that disparities in periodontal health among the two groups may be related to silicosis.

In the context of periodontal examination, the silicosis group demonstrated a lower gingival bleeding index, lower PD and higher periodontal CAL in contrast to healthy individuals. This was in contrast to the periodontal conditions observed in patients afflicted with other lung conditions, such as asthma and COPD, who displayed deeper periodontal PD and a heightened incidence of BOP (+) [42, 43]. However, our findings align with researches on periodontal health among smokers and individuals with systemic sclerosis [39, 44, 45]. These results could be explained by gingival fibrosis [46]. The inhalation of crystalline silica into the lungs can induce fibrosis in pulmonary soft tissues. Meantime, these crystalline silica particles may adhere to oral soft tissues via mouth breathing when individuals are working in coal mining regions. It has been reported that oral submucous fibrosis occurs in Indian marble miners [47]. As the pathology of oral submucous fibrosis is characteristic with chronic inflammation and the presence of thick collagen bands and reduced blood vessels [48]. We speculate that gingiva fibrosis may occur in individuals with silicosis and mask signs of inflammation on periodontal soft tissue.

The pathogenesis of silicosis in lung discovered that silica can promote the apoptosis and activation of macrophages, thereby causing macrophages to release a variety of cytokines, leading to activation of fibroblasts into myofibroblasts. Afterwards, fibroblasts proliferate, migrate, and release collagen to induce fibrosis [49]. Alpha-SMA is considered to be a marker of fibroblast differentiation into myofibroblasts [50]. We observed that the expression of α-SMA in the gingival tissue of silicosis patients increased. Masson staining showed muscle fiber formation, suggesting that the molecular mechanism that silica dust stimulates the formation of pulmonary muscle fibrosis might also exist in the gum tissue. In addition, we realized that the expression of FN increased. Studies have reported that FN can recruit a large number of fibroblasts, induce fibroblast proliferation [51], participating in tissue fibrosis. The hallmark of silicosis is excessive extracellular matrix deposition produced by activated fibroblasts [52]. A major source of excessive extracellular matrix is the dysregulated matrix synthesis by activated fibroblasts, which differentiate into myofibroblasts. The dual effects of gingival inflammatory irritation and fibrosis may lead to increased gingival toughness in patients with silicosis and a decrease in bleeding during gingival probing.

Periodontal inflammation is notably associated with an imbalance in oral microbiota, and is intricately linked to lung function and lung diseases [40, 41]. Saliva serves as a reservoir of microbiota derived from various niches within the oral cavity, and it is a biological fluid with potentially rich diagnostic indicators for oral health and systemic diseases [53]. In this study, we found configuration and diversity alterations in salivary microbiota of silicosis and control group. Within the α-diversity of salivary microbiota, the silicosis exhibited significantly lower Simpson and Shannon indices. Higher α-diversity is generally considered healthier [54]. Research has identified a significant relationship between reduced microbiota diversity and diminished lung function [55]. The β-diversity analysis results showed that the silicosis and the control group were located in two different clusters. At genus level, we found an enrichment of Stenotrophomonas is the salivary of patient with silicosis. This bacterium is commonly linked with respiratory diseases such as pneumonia, COPD, and lung cancer, especially prevalent in the respiratory tracts of individuals diagnosed with cystic fibrosis [56]. Our investigation underscores Stenotrophomonas as a prominent bacterial group within the saliva of silicosis patients. This finding leads us to speculate that the oral cavity could potentially serve as a reservoir for lung-associated bacteria. Consequently, minimizing the inhalation of pathogenic bacteria originating from the oral cavity might prove crucial in preventing lung infections among patients diagnosed with silicosis.

It’s noteworthy that our study identified Aggregatibacter and Catonella as the potential indicators to distinguish silicosis patients from healthy controls and verified its distinguishing efficacy using AUC. The OR for Aggregatibacter and Catonella is close to 0, indicating that these two bacterial genera act as protective factors against silicosis. This is consistent with the observation that the relative abundance of these bacteria is higher in the control group compared to the silicosis. In a pilot case-controlled study, it was found that the abundance of Aggregatibacter was reduced in the oral microbiota of patients with multiple sclerosis [57]. A prospective study indicated that smoking might decrease the abundance of Catonella [58]. Additionally, another study revealed that, compared to the COPD group, the bacterial microbiota of patients in the bronchial asthma group was characterized by a decrease in both Aggregatibacter and Catonella [59]. However, there is limited literature investigating bacterial biomarkers in patients with silicosis. A study had examined the bacterial in the alveolar lavage fluid of silicosis patients, but lacked healthy controls [60]. Another study explored the differences of fecal microbiota from silicosis patients and healthy controls, but no further search for a biomarker for silicosis disease [61]. In contrast, our study suggested that the oral microbiota of silicosis patients could potentially aid in diagnosing silicosis.

Nevertheless, this study still has some limitations. Firstly, this observational study, not necessarily imply a potential causal relationship with the disease, compared the differences in periodontal health and oral microbiota composition between the two groups, without delving into the underlying mechanisms of silicosis. Secondly, our study was limited to a single center, whereas the composition and activity of oral microbiota can be influenced by various factors. Future research involving larger, multi-center, and longitudinal studies is needed to confirm and expand upon our findings. Dividing the participants into four groups, namely the control group, periodontitis group, silicosis group, and silicosis combined with periodontitis group in the future study, can better illustrate the lack of periodontitis interference with the research results. This study serves as an exploratory endeavor. Finally, while our study utilized saliva samples for oral microbiome analysis, other collection methods such as tongue coating, oral wash, and oral swabs could provide additional insights, potentially enhancing the comprehensiveness of our findings. In essence, this study forms a robust foundation for exploring the correlation between oral diseases and the onset and progression of silicosis. It also paves the way for innovative strategies for early monitoring or the future treatment strategy of silicosis.

Conclusion

In sum, our studies provide evidence for the periodontal conditions and salivary microbiomes of individuals related to silicosis. Silicosis patients exhibited higher periodontal CAL, but less bleeding on probing and lower PD, probably attributed to gingival fibrosis. Utilizing 16S rRNA analysis on saliva samples, we noted decreased α-diversity of salivary microbiota and increased expression of Stenotrophomonas among silicosis patients. Furthermore, we identified Aggregatibacter combined with Catonella as the potential model to distinguish silicosis patients from healthy controls. This study furnishes valuable insights and a theoretical framework for further exploration into the association between oral microbiotas and silicosis.
