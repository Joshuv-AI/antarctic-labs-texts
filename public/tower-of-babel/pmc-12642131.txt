Efficacy of human-derived fibrin sealant in preventing postoperative adverse events after gastric endoscopic submucosal dissection: a retrospective multicenter study
Authors: Meng Chen, Li Xu, Qin Lu, Yuan Ding, Zhengqing Xue, Hongxia Li, Cuie Cheng, Xilong Ou, Ruihua Shi, Chen Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe purpose of this study was to evaluate the efficacy of the human-derived fibrin sealant in mitigating adverse events subsequent to endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) for the treatment of gastric mucosal lesions.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on data from 661 patients who underwent ESD for gastric mucosal lesions with a resected specimen size ≥ 40 mm. These patients were categorized into the fibrin sealant group and the control group on the basis of postoperative wound management strategies. Comparisons were made between the groups regarding the incidence of adverse events and the postoperative wound healing rate at 1 month. Additionally, factors associated with delayed bleeding following ESD were analyzed.ResultsCompared with the control group, the fibrin sealant group had a significantly lower rate of delayed hemorrhage (1.7% vs. 6.9%; P < 0.001). Subgroup analysis confirmed the consistency of this effect across all the subgroups. Additionally, compared with the control group, the fibrin sealant group had a lower postoperative abdominal pain rate and a lower 24-h postoperative abdominal pain score (P < 0.001). One month after ESD, the fibrin sealant group had a higher wound healing rate (65.4% vs. 51.1%; P = 0.014). Multivariate analysis identified lesions located in the lower one-third of the stomach and specimen size ≥ 60 mm as risk factors for postoperative bleeding.ConclusionThe application of human-derived fibrin sealant to post-ESD wounds effectively decreased the risk of delayed postoperative hemorrhage, mitigated postoperative abdominal pain, and accelerated the healing process of artificial wounds.Keywords: Endoscopic mucosal resection, Adverse events, Hemorrhage, Stomach neoplasms

Introduction

Endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) is acknowledged as the first-line therapeutic approach for gastric mucosal lesions, encompassing early gastric cancer and precancerous conditions. Although ESD offers benefits such as rapid recovery and preservation of digestive tract integrity, the potential adverse events following ESD, including postoperative bleeding and perforation, cannot be ignored. Postoperative bleeding is a severe complication that can lead to hemorrhagic shock and seriously affect prognosis [1]. A retrospective study of 1192 gastric neoplasms demonstrated that specimen size > 40 mm was the sole independent risk factor for delayed bleeding [2]. Hashimoto et al. [3] reported resection size > 40 mm as an independent risk factor for rebleeding following successful hemostasis in patients with delayed bleeding after gastric ESD. The management of post-ESD wounds is a means to prevent postoperative bleeding. A retrospective study conducted at our center utilized the alternate mucosa-submucosa clip method for the closure of artificial wounds, which revealed a significant reduction in delayed bleeding to 2.1% with this technique [4]. Another approach for targeting post-ESD wounds is the endoscopic tissue shielding method. The application of fibrin glue to ulcers has been shown to promote hemostasis through blood clot formation and wound healing through tissue sealing. In a Japanese study, polyglycolic acid (PGA) sheets along with fibrin glue were applied to the wound after ESD in high-risk patients, which decreased the incidence of delayed hemorrhage to 6.7% [5]. However, comprehensive research assessing the efficacy of fibrin sealant alone in mitigating adverse events after ESD for gastric mucosal lesions exceeding 40 mm is lacking. This retrospective cohort study analyzed clinical data from patients who underwent ESD for gastric mucosal lesions to evaluate the efficacy of human-derived fibrin sealant in preventing postprocedural adverse events after ESD.

Methods

Study design

This multicenter retrospective study included 661 cases of gastric mucosal lesions treated with ESD across four medical centers in China. The study population comprised patients who underwent ESD procedures between March 2015 and June 2024; the case distribution was as follows: 377 patients from Zhongda Hospital Southeast University, 102 patients from Yancheng Sixth People’s Hospital, 96 patients from the Second Affiliated Hospital Zhejiang University School of Medicine, and 86 patients from the Affiliated Changshu Hospital of Nantong University.

Patients

The researchers conducted a retrospective study of all patients at designated medical centers. Data collection included meticulous examination of medical records and endoscopic images, including photographs and images of gastric mucosal lesions.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) diagnosis of gastric mucosal lesions, including low-grade and high-grade intraepithelial neoplasia, as well as gastric carcinoma within the expanded indications for endoscopic intervention, validated by gastroscopy prior to ESD and by pathology subsequent to ESD; (2) resected specimen size ≥ 40 mm; and (3) comprehensive clinical, endoscopic, and pathological data. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) insufficient data; (2) the presence of lymphovascular tumor embolus, lymph node metastases, or distant metastasis; and (3) a wound managed with clips or sprayed with hemostatic powder after ESD. All patients underwent magnified gastroscopy and ultrasonic endoscopy to evaluate lesion depth prior to ESD. Enhanced CT scans were performed to exclude lymph node or extragastric metastases. Patients were informed of ESD-related risks and signed an informed consent form. The gastric ESD procedures were performed by endoscopists with experience in more than 500 gastric lesion cases.

On the basis of the management of wounds subsequent to gastric ESD, the enrolled patients were categorized into two groups: the fibrin sealant group (postoperative wound sprayed with human-derived fibrin sealant) and the control group (postoperative wound not sprayed with human-derived fibrin sealant).

Equipment

A therapeutic endoscope (GIF-Q290J; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), an Olympus transparent cap (D-201–11804), an Alton injection needle (Alton Medical Instruments, Shanghai, China), an Olympus KD-650L knife, an Olympus KD-650Q knife, Olympus FD-412LR hemostatic forceps, and an Erbe VIO 300D high-frequency electrosurgical workstation (Erbe Elektromedizin, Tübingen, Germany) were used.

ESD procedure and application of fibrin sealant

The main steps were as follows: (1) Argon plasma coagulation was used to mark a point located 0.5 cm from the lesion’s edge. (2) A submucosal injection of 1:10,000 epinephrine saline, which contained methylene blue, was administered to adequately elevate the lesion. (3) The mucosa surrounding the lesion, along with the submucosa, was incised using the dual knife until ESD was completed. (4) Subsequent to the complete excision of the lesion, all the visible vessels on the ESD wound were routinely coagulated with hemostatic forceps (Fig. 1).

In the fibrin sealant group, a human fibrin sealant kit [Fibingluraas®, 2 mL; Shanghai Raas Medical Tech, Shanghai, China; National Medical Products Administration (NMPA), China number: S20030070)] served as a spray-type adhesive. Its primary constituents consist of 2 components: (1) fibrinogen, factor XIII, and phosphate buffer and (2) prothrombin and calcium chloride (CaCl2) solution. In the fibrin sealant group, after conservative coagulation, human fibrinogen was sprayed onto the wound via a spray tube inserted through the working channel. Upon mixing the above 2 components, a gel-like material forms immediately, covering the wound surface. Following the application of fibrin sealant, we endoscopically confirmed successful initial adhesion by observing the formation of a stable gel-like layer covering the ulcer base. For locations prone to displacement due to gravity (such as the gastric fundus or greater curvature), we adjusted the patient’s position, applied the sealant in layers, and maintained gentle pressure with the catheter tip to promote adherence. Only cases with confirmed initial adhesion under endoscopic visualization were included in the final analysis.

Control group: Following prophylactic electrocoagulation of the artificial wound utilizing hot biopsy forceps, the wound was not sprayed with fibrin sealant.

Postprocedural management

The patients fasted for 48 h after ESD and were regularly administered proton pump inhibitors such as omeprazole. The patients were carefully monitored for abdominal pain, hematemesis, and other adverse events following ESD. Abdominal radiography or computed tomography (CT) was performed when needed.

Definitions of terms

In this study, delayed hemorrhage was defined as bleeding occurring within a period ranging from 24 h to 30 days after ESD, fulfilling at least two of the following specified criteria: hematemesis, melena, dizziness, and other distressing symptoms; a reduction in hemoglobin levels exceeding 2.0 g/dL; a decrease in blood pressure greater than 20 mmHg or an increase in heart rate by more than 20 beats/min; and gastroscopic evidence of hemorrhage. Delayed perforation was characterized by the abrupt onset of postoperative acute abdominal pain, together with radiographs or CT scans suggesting the presence of free gas. Postoperative abdominal pain was evaluated utilizing a numeric rating scale ranging from 0 to 10 [6], as follows. 0 points: No pain. 1–3 points: Mild pain. Pain does not affect sleep, is tolerable, and has a minimal impact on daily life. 4–6 points: Moderate pain. Pain begins to disrupt sleep, is difficult to endure, requires attention and management, and may partially interfere with daily activities. 7–9 points: Severe pain. Intense pain prevents sleep and significantly impairs daily activities and appetite. 10 points: Unbearable agony, the worst excruciating pain the patient has ever experienced or can conceive of. Patients were asked to select one of 11 numbers to reflect their pain level, which was documented by the nurse in the nursing record sheet. For those who had multiple pain scores in one day, the highest score was chosen as the score for that day.

The wound healing rate was determined by assessing the proportion of ulcers that had reached the healing stage as observed endoscopically in comparison to the overall count of individuals who underwent a gastroscopic follow-up procedure. The endoscopic assessment of ulcer healing was based on the established Sakita–Miwa classification system for peptic ulcers [7]. The endoscopic classification of peptic ulcers comprises the active stage (A1, A2), healing stage (H1, H2), and scarring (S1, S2) stage. Within this system, the ‘white scar stage’ (S2 stage) indicates complete healing, characterized by a flat or slightly depressed area covered by regenerated mucosa that appears white because of the maturation of underlying fibrous tissue and a reduction in capillary density.

BEST-J Score is a clinical tool designed to quantitatively predict the risk of delayed bleeding after gastric ESD. This scoring system incorporates ten variables: warfarin or direct oral anticoagulants (4 points each), chronic kidney disease with dialysis (3 points), P2Y12 receptor antagonists or aspirin (2 points each), cilostazol, tumor size > 30 mm, tumor located in the lower third of the stomach, and multiple tumors (1 point each), while interruption of each kind of antithrombotic agent deducts 1 point. Based on the total score, patients are stratified into four risk categories: low-risk (0–1 points, bleeding rate ~ 2.8%), intermediate-risk (2 points, ~ 6.1%), high-risk (3–4 points, ~ 11.4%), and very high-risk (≥ 5 points, ~ 29.7%), thereby providing crucial guidance for individualized post-procedural management strategies [8].

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the incidence of delayed bleeding. The secondary outcomes were the incidence of delayed perforation, abdominal pain score, and wound healing rate at 1 month post-ESD.

Follow-up

Gastroscopy was conducted 1 month post-ESD to assess the healing of the artificial wounds. The endoscopic classification of peptic ulcers consists of the active stage (A1, A2), healing stage (H1, H2), and scarring (S1, S2) stage [7].

Statistical analyses

All the statistical analyses were conducted with SPSS software (version 27.0; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous data, assuming normal distributions, were represented as the means ± standard deviations and were analyzed via independent samples t tests for group comparisons. Skewed distribution quantitative data are represented as medians (interquartile ranges [IQRs]) and were evaluated using the Mann–Whitney U test for group comparisons. Categorical data are presented as numbers and frequencies (%) and were analyzed using the Pearson χ2 test, the corrected χ2 test, or Fisher’s exact probability method. Logistic regression analysis was employed for subgroup analysis. Logistic regression analysis was also used to assess secondary outcomes and the risk factors for postoperative bleeding following gastric ESD. Variables for inclusion in the multivariable logistic regression model for delayed bleeding were selected on the basis of a combination of clinical relevance and statistical significance. All variables with a P value < 0.1 in the univariable analysis, along with factors considered clinically important on the basis of the established literature (such as lesion location and specimen size), were initially included in the full model. A backward stepwise selection procedure was then employed, using a threshold of P < 0.05 for retention in the final model. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Characterization of endoscopic lesions and postoperative pathological features

Lesions in the fibrin sealant group were primarily located in the middle one-third of the stomach (36.9%), with a majority being depressed lesions (38.0%). In 37 patients (10.4%), ulcers or scars were observed on the lesion surface. Conversely, in the control group, 132 lesions (43.1%) were predominantly located in the upper one-third of the stomach, with a statistically significant difference in lesion distribution between the two groups (P < 0.001). The specimen sizes were comparable between the two groups (52 (47.0, 62.0) mm and 53 (45.0, 65.0) mm; P = 0.724). Postoperative pathological classifications in the fibrin sealant group included low-grade dysplasia in 111 (31.3%) patients, high-grade dysplasia in 72 (20.3%), and gastric carcinoma in 172 (48.5%), with lesions mainly infiltrating the epithelial layer (47.6%). Significant differences in lesion location, morphology, and depth of infiltration were noted between the groups (all P < 0.05). The presence of ulcers or scars on the lesion surface (P = 0.057) and margin-positive rates were not significantly different (P = 0.943) (Table 2).

The en bloc resection rate was 99.4% in the control group and 97.5% in the fibrin sealant group. Postoperative adverse events and follow-up

In the fibrin sealant group, 6 patients (1.7%) experienced delayed postoperative hemorrhage, which was significantly lower than that in the control group (21 [6.9%] patients) (P < 0.001). No intraoperative or postoperative perforations were noted in either group. Postoperative stenosis occurred in 3 patients (0.8%) in the fibrin sealant group and 1 patient (0.3%) in the control group, with no significant difference between the groups (P = 0.736). The postoperative abdominal pain rate was substantially lower in the fibrin sealant group than in the control group (35.5% vs. 50.7%, respectively; P < 0.001) (Table 4). The abdominal pain scores were significantly lower in the fibrin sealant group than in the control group 24 h after surgery (Fig. 2). After adjusting for confounding factors, including sex, history of diabetes mellitus, history of cardiovascular disease, history of dialysis, history of taking antithrombotic agents, lesion location, presence of surface ulceration or scarring, muscle layer injury, and specimen size, multivariable analysis confirmed that application of fibrin sealant was associated with a reduced rate of post-ESD abdominal pain (P = 0.002; OR = 0.60; 95% CI 0.44–0.83) (Table 5). However, there was no significant difference in abdominal pain scores between the two groups at 48 and 72 h postoperatively.

Risk factors for post-ESD delayed bleeding in gastric mucosal lesions

Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to assess risk factors for delayed postoperative bleeding following ESD for gastric mucosal lesions in the two groups. The risk factors identified in this study included lesions located in the lower one-third of the stomach (P = 0.002; odds ratio [OR] = 1.62; 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.21–2.42) and specimen size ≥ 60 mm (P < 0.001; OR = 2.57; 95% CI 1.26–4.42). The application of fibrin sealant was independently associated with reduced risk of delayed bleeding (P < 0.001; OR = 0.16; 95% CI 0.06–0.45) (Tables 7, 8).

Discussion

This multicenter retrospective study reported an easy-to-use method of manual wound management after gastric ESD. The results demonstrated that spraying human fibrin sealant on postoperative wounds could reduce postoperative adverse events, including delayed hemorrhage and abdominal pain.

Fibrin sealant is a biodegradable, absorbable biological agent with the core components of fibrinogen and thrombin, which are mixed to form a cross-linked fibrin mesh structure, leading to fibroblast proliferation and local accumulation of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) [9]. Fibrin sealant plays a hemostatic role by activating platelets to mimic the final step of the coagulation cascade, and its wound-covering effect promotes platelet aggregation, thereby accelerating wound healing. Previous studies have shown that the incidence of delayed hemorrhage after gastric ESD ranges from 1.8% to 15.6% [10–12]. Our study revealed that the postoperative bleeding rate decreased to 1.7% when fibrin sealant was sprayed on post-ESD wounds. Another randomized controlled trial employed fibrin glue specifically in high-risk patients with bleeding, and it was demonstrated that fibrin glue reduced overall bleeding events, especially acute bleeding [13], which is consistent with our results.

Notably, previous studies have focused on the combined use of fibrin sealant and polyglycolic acid (PGA) sheets such as NeoBeal and Neovare to increase mechanical coverage and prolong adhesion time. Kikuchi et al. [14] reported that autologous fibrin glue and the PGA sheet shielding method decreased delayed bleeding to 2.6% in the antithrombotic population. In another retrospective study, polyglycolic acid tablets combined with fibrin glue were used to cover gastric ESD wounds, and the incidence of post-ESD bleeding was 5.8% and 20.8% in the covered and control groups, respectively (P = 0.04) [10–12]. Future prospective studies comparing fibrin sealant alone versus PGA sheets (such as Neovare) would be valuable for determining the optimal strategy for post-ESD wound management.

Unlike previous studies [13], this study focused on the entire population rather than being limited to those with high bleeding risk. To further demonstrate the efficacy of fibrin glue in preventing postoperative bleeding in the whole population, we performed a subgroup analysis. The results revealed that the advantage of spraying fibrin sealant on the wound was consistent across subgroups, suggesting that this method is suitable for stomach lesions of different locations and sizes, as well as for different populations.

Abdominal pain is a common symptom following ESD. At present, studies on alleviating postoperative pain after gastric ESD are limited mainly to drug-related investigations. Choi et al. [15] reported that the application of a fentanyl patch could reduce acute postoperative pain on the second day after ESD. Our previous study demonstrated that after complete closure of the post-ESD wound, patients experienced significantly less abdominal pain on the postoperative day than patients in the unclosed group did (0 vs. 2, P < 0.001) [4]. In this study, spraying human fibrin sealant on the wound significantly reduced pain on the day of ESD without further need for analgesic medication. The reduction in pain may have occurred because the exposed muscle layers were protected from direct stimulation by acid and bile after the fibrin sealant was sprayed on the wound. However, for pain relief on the second postoperative day, the fibrin glue group did not show a significant advantage, which may be associated with the degradation of the fibrin glue substance. Although the fibrin sealant group demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in abdominal pain scores at 24 h post-ESD compared to the control group, the mean pain score in the control group was relatively low, indicating that most patients experienced only mild discomfort even without sealant application. These findings suggest that the absolute benefit of fibrin sealant in reducing pain may be modest in the general ESD population. However, even small reductions in pain can contribute to improved patient comfort and satisfaction during the early postoperative period.

Furthermore, in terms of postoperative wound healing, our study revealed that upon the application of fibrin sealant to the wound, the complete healing rate reached 65.4% at 1 month postoperative, surpassing that of the control group, which is in line with the findings of previous studies. The study by Becker et al. [9] reported that compared with those in the saline group, most of the lesions in the fibrin glue group were smaller, the number of proliferating cells at the ulcer margins was higher, and FGF-2 mRNA expression was significantly greater, which in turn positively regulated the healing of gastric ulcers. Another study conducted by Fukuda [16] et al. revealed that the adhesion time of fibrin glue ranged from 1 to 32 days. Fibrin glue has strong adhesion and good hemostatic effects, but it poses the risk of being dissolved by gastric acid, which may be one of the reasons for its variable effects. Intragastric pH affects the dissolution rate of fibrin glue in the stomach [17]. Fibrin glue is easily dissolved by gastric acid in an acidic environment, whereas its stability is significantly improved at near-neutral pH. Therefore, the selection of strong antacids may be effective in prolonging the time of fibrin glue dissolution by gastric acid.

Additionally, multivariable analysis further demonstrated that lesions located in the lower one-third of the stomach constitute a risk factor, which is potentially attributable to the artificial wound formed in this location after ESD. This type of wound is exposed to elevated concentrations of gastric acid and is stimulated by refluxed bile, thereby rendering artificial wounds more susceptible to postoperative hemorrhage. Previous studies have indicated that an increase in specimen diameter is correlated with an increased risk of postoperative bleeding, with artificial ulcers measuring > 30 mm [18], > 40 mm [2], and > 50 mm [18] identified as risk factors for postoperative bleeding after gastric ESD. Our study revealed that specimen size ≥ 60 mm was a risk factor.

Although this study utilized a fibrin sealant product manufactured in China (Fibingluraas®), it is important to note that similar fibrin sealants (e.g., Tisseel in the EU and US, Bolnial in Japan) are approved and routinely used in other regions. Given that the consistency in core components and mechanism of action are consistent among these products, the positive outcomes observed in our study are likely applicable to other fibrin adhesives available internationally.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, it was conducted retrospectively, and the management of wounds following ESD lacked randomization. Second, the duration of fibrin sealant coverage on gastric wounds was not confirmed by secondary endoscopy. Third, fewer than half of the patients in the fibrin sealant group underwent follow-up endoscopy 1 month after ESD, which may have affected the validity of the ulcer healing assessment and intergroup comparisons. Consequently, future multicenter, prospective clinical trials with large sample sizes are needed for further validation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the application of human-derived fibrin sealant to post-ESD defects could diminish the risk of postoperative hemorrhage, alleviate patient’s postoperative abdominal pain, and accelerate the healing process of artificial wounds. In addition, this method does not require any supplementary equipment and is easy to perform, rendering it a safe and practical method for post-ESD wound management that deserves to be promoted.
