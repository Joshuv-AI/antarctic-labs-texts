Harlequin syndrome following paravertebral block adjacent to a stretched pleura: a case report
Authors: Weisi Ding, Shang Li, Feng Pan, Jian Zhou, Yi Feng, Xue Tian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHarlequin syndrome is characterized by unilateral facial flushing and sweating induced by exercise, heat, and emotion, which is mainly caused by autonomic nervous system impairment.Case presentationA patient underwent thoracoscopic segmentectomy for a tumor located in the apicoposterior segment of the left upper lobe. Within two hours after surgery, the patient developed ipsilateral facial pallor accompanied by contralateral flushing, without any neurological deficits or hemodynamic instability. The symptoms persisted until late at night, and facial erythema resolved spontaneously by the following morning. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 4 without complications. Based on the clinical presentation and temporal association, the condition was diagnosed as Harlequin syndrome secondary to paravertebral nerve block at the corresponding vertebral level.ConclusionsWhen performing paravertebral block for pulmonary resection, the location of the tumor and the involvement of the pleura should be considered. If a patient’s preoperative computed tomography (CT) images are consistent with those of the patient described in this article, paravertebral block administration may induce Harlequin syndrome. Therefore, the nerve block procedure may be adjusted to avoid the corresponding segment to mitigate this risk.Keywords: Harlequin syndrome, Thoracic paravertebral block, Thoracoscopic segmentectomy

Introduction

Harlequin syndrome (HS) is a rare autonomic disorder characterized by unilateral facial pallor and contralateral flushing, with an estimated incidence remaining undefined due to its rarity [1, 2]. The nomenclature originates from the striking resemblance to the classic red-and-black mask of the European Harlequin archetype. HS primarily results from disrupted sympathetic innervation involving the T2-T4 ganglia, leading to impaired vasomotor control. Etiologies include central neurogenic lesions such as brainstem infarction, and superior mediastinal neurinoma, as well as peripheral neurogenic lesions like pancoast tumor, brachial plexus avulsion, internal carotid artery dissection, thoracic surgery, internal jugular vein catheterization, thyroid dysfunction, regional infections, or idiopathic origins [3]. It typically manifests as dysfunction of vasomotor and sudomotor sympathetic fiber activity inhibits the ability to flush on the affected side, causing the neurologically intact side to appear flushed [4]. We present this uncommon syndrome in a patient whose pleura at T4 level was stretched due to lung lesion, resulting in HS caused by paravertebral nerve block, and review the medical literature.

Case report

A 76-year-old female with a history of bronchial asthma (BMI 21.9 kg/m²) was scheduled for a thoracoscopic segmentectomy because of a pulmonary nodule. Preoperative evaluation revealed a mixed ground-glass nodule in the apicoposterior segment of the left upper lobe, measuring 2.6 × 1.9 cm on positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT). The lesion exhibited lobulated margins, pleural retraction, and mildly increased FDG uptake (SUV: 1.4) (Fig. 1A).

A Preoperative chest CT showing a mixed ground-glass nodule in the apicoposterior segment of the left upper lobe. The arrow indicates pleural indentation/retraction adjacent to the apical lesion. B Chest CT at 5 months postoperatively showed no significant abnormalities

Following arterial line placement and uneventful placement of a 35-Fr left-sided double-lumen endotracheal intubation, ultrasound-guided thoracic paravertebral block (TPVB) (0.4% ropivacaine, 15 mL each at T3/T7 levels) and serratus anterior plane block (0.4% ropivacaine, 10 mL) were performed in the right lateral decubitus position. Subpleural pressure monitoring confirmed adequate paravertebral blockade efficacy. Surgical duration was 60 min, and no additional abnormalities were observed throughout the operation. The patient was extubated in the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) and transferred to the ward. Notably, she developed unilateral left facial pallor with contralateral (right) flushing (Fig. 2) within 2 h postoperatively, without neurological deficits or hemodynamic instability and the symptoms lasted until late at night. The facial erythema resolved spontaneously the following morning, and she was discharged on postoperative day 4 without complications. Five months after surgery, reexaminations including chest computed tomography (CT) (Fig. 1B) and pulmonary function tests showed no significant abnormalities.

Postoperative Harlequin sign: left facial pallor with contralateral (right) facial flushing, observed approximately 2 h after surgery

Discussion

The case was initially attributed to HS resulting from TPVB. Subsequent detailed analysis of preoperative imaging by our radiology team revealed significant findings, including tumor-induced pleural retraction at the left lung apex adjacent to the spine, a distinct pleural indentation (Fig. 1A, arrow), and notable sympathetic trunk thickening. The absence of Horner’s syndrome symptoms (ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis) or vital sign abnormalities following TPVB suggested an alternative mechanism. We propose that the combination of pre-existing pleural invagination and sympathetic trunk thickening at the block level created a unique anatomical environment that facilitated delayed sympathetic blockade through altered local anesthetic spread patterns, explaining the delayed onset of the Harlequin sign. The local anesthetic in paravertebral block typically spreads cranio-caudally. In this case, the left pleura of the patient was pulled by the tumor, which may have resulted in the movement of the fluid towards the sympathetic nerve trunk.

We also considered the possibility of direct surgical injury to the sympathetic chain, such as that caused by electrocautery, as a differential diagnosis. However, a key distinction can be made based on the time course and nature of the event. Sympathetic nerve damage resulting from direct surgical trauma is generally associated with irreversible or long-lasting deficits. In contrast, our patient’s Harlequin sign—unilateral facial pallor with contralateral flushing—appeared postoperatively and resolved spontaneously within hours (by the following morning). This rapid, complete, and self-limiting nature is the hallmark of a transient, pharmacologically-induced sympathetic blockade, which is highly consistent with the eventual metabolism and clearance of the local anesthetic agent used in the TPVB. Therefore, the observed rapid reversibility is a key factor supporting our proposed mechanism of HS secondary to an altered spread of the local anesthetic solution, facilitated by unique pre-existing pleural pathology. A similar phenomenon has been observed in reported cases involving erector spinae plane blocks [5], thoracic epidural anesthesia [6]and intercostal blockade [7], intraspinal analgesia [8]. HS can be seen in pediatric patients [4, 9]. Variants of SCN10A may be associated with congenital Harlequin syndrome [10]. The treatment for HS should focus on addressing the underlying cause. Sympathectomy, stellate ganglion blocks and botulinum toxin [11] application have been recommended for patients who experience serious social embarrassment. Current evidence supports the efficacy of video-assisted thoracoscopic sympathectomy as a definitive treatment modality for refractory HS [12]. Secondary HS typically demonstrates spontaneous resolution without requiring specific therapeutic intervention.

This is the first reported case of HS secondary to TPVB in the setting of tumor-induced stretch of the pleura. We present this image of this rare, self-limiting condition to highlight the importance of comprehensive preprocedural imaging assessment to evaluate sympathetic chain anatomy and the need for extended postoperative monitoring when performing regional anesthesia in patients with pleural pathology. If a patient’s preoperative CT images are consistent with those of the patient described in this article, paravertebral block administration may potentially induce HS. Therefore, the nerve block procedure may be adjusted to avoid the corresponding segment to mitigate this risk.
