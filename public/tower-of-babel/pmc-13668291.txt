Triage models for surgical decision-making in patients with shoulder pain: an integrative review
Authors: Samah Hassan, Kenneth J. Faber, Joy C. MacDermid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Supplemental Digital Content is Available in the Text.Shoulder pain requires an effective triage model. This review underscores the need for a paradigm shift in shoulder pain triage that recognizes referral management as a multifactorial decision-making process.

1. Introduction

Shoulder pain is the leading cause of work absenteeism55 affecting 1 in 3 adults at some point during their lifetime.55,64 The majority of shoulder complaints stem from rotator cuff disease and joint inflammation.57,64

Evidence-based guidelines recommend nonsurgical management, including physical therapy, as the front-line treatment.20,57 However, approximately 40% of patients do not receive physical therapy.57 Instead, many are referred to orthopedic surgeons for potential surgical interventions, facing waiting times of up to 2 years without treatment.13,14,57,66

These prolonged delays not only postpone treatment but also impose significant direct and indirect costs, associated with decreased productivity, and prolonged physical and psychological suffering.66,68 In addition, many patients rely on opioids for pain relief, and 1 in 7 become chronic users while awaiting assessment and treatment.8,34,51

More importantly, a substantial number of patients referred for surgery are ultimately deemed ineligible and are redirected to start nonsurgical treatment after unnecessary delays.4 Even among those who undergo surgery, improvements are often modest and may not provide meaningful benefits.90

1.1. Knowledge-to-practice gap

Although progress has been made in establishing treatment recommendations for shoulder pain, the process of how to efficiently direct the tsunami of people referred from primary care toward the appropriate management remains unclear.20 The lack of a structured triage system has limited the implementation of evidence-based guidelines in clinical settings while contributing to inefficiencies in the referral process.20,53 Both surgical and nonsurgical candidates are referred to orthopedic surgeons, leading to long waiting lists and delaying care for others who might benefit from timely surgical intervention.103 These inefficiencies not only strain the health care system but also prolong patient suffering, increase costs, and result in suboptimal outcomes.103 In addition, the common practice of categorizing patients as surgical vs nonsurgical, without accounting for pain heterogeneity, is problematic. Substantial evidence demonstrates that pain is a multidimensional experience modulated by interacting biological, psychological, and social factors.37 Therefore, many patients may not fit neatly within this binary categorization (surgical vs nonsurgical) of the referral process, which may affect operative outcomes.37,91

1.2. Rationale

Shoulder-related disability imposes economic and societal burden and systemic innovation is needed to optimize patient care.68 A structured triage model could streamline referrals and assign patients to the most appropriate treatment pathways in a timely fashion while attending to the complex nature of pain.23,31 Such a model could optimize the management of nonoperative shoulder conditions while establishing clear thresholds for surgical referrals. A structured triage model has the potential to achieve the Quintuple Aim: better patient experience and clinical outcomes, cost-effectiveness, clinician experience, and equity.70,103

3. Methods

This review adopted an integrative approach through the synthesis and critique of the literature to generate new frameworks and perspectives.17,101 This approach enabled a comprehensive examination of referral model development, guidelines for shoulder pain management, surgical indications, and referral criteria, as well as their practical applicability. It also provided a broad understanding of key concepts and healthcare challenges by including diverse experimental, nonexperimental, theoretical, and empirical literature.17,101 This review was conducted in line with the 2020 guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA)102 as evidenced in the PRISMA 2020 checklist. After completion of the search strategy, the protocol for this review was registered on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) with the registration number CRD420250636294 on March 16, 2025.

3.1. Search strategy

A systematic search of the organizational literature up to October 2024 was conducted. Title, abstract, and possible keyword searches were used to query relevant databases including Medline and EMBASE via Ovid, CINHAL plus, and PubMed using combinations of shoulder pain-related terms (“Shoulder Injuries,” “Shoulder Pain,” “Shoulder,” “Shoulder Dislocation,” “Osteoarthritis, Shoulder,” “Shoulder Joint,” “Rotator Cuff Injuries”) and triage tools search terms(“Triage Tools,” “Referral and Consultation,” “Referral,” “Referral,” “Decision Making, Clinical,” “Decision Support Systems”). A medical librarian assisted with the search strategy. The search was complemented by searching the gray literature through Google Scholar, and ProQuest Dissertations & Theses web sites, hand searching of references of eligible full texts, and key journals in orthopedics and sports medicine. A regular current awareness search for newly published studies was set up and used to alert authors to new publications in the area. A detailed search strategy is included in Supplemental Materials (https://links.lww.com/PR9/A449).

3.2. Selection process

All search results were imported into EndNote software,35 and duplicates were removed. Titles and abstracts were screened to determine relevancy, and reviewers obtained and reviewed the full text of potentially eligible articles. Articles were excluded if they did not meet the eligibility criteria. A PRISMA flow diagram102 was created, documenting reasons for exclusion. For both abstracts and full-text selection stages, reviewers independently evaluated the eligibility of each of the identified studies in pairs. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or by third-reviewer adjudication.

Articles were considered eligible if they focused on adults (18 years and over) with shoulder pain because of any pathologies. Articles that discussed triage tools/models, guidelines for assessment, diagnosis, and treatment were considered eligible only if indications for surgical referral or surgical candidacy were included. Both experimental (eg, randomized trials, comparative cohort studies, pre-post/quasi designs) or nonexperimental (prospective or retrospective observational cohort studies, qualitative studies, cross-sectional surveys) studies were eligible. Outcomes included triage models, patient-reported outcomes (ie, improved patient satisfaction, pain reduction, improved function), and system-reported outcomes (ie, reduced waitlist, faster access to appropriate care, increased early interventions, reduced inappropriate referrals, reduced costs). There was no restriction on the publication date. Articles were excluded if they were conference abstracts, magazine articles, or protocol proposals. Articles without an available full text were also excluded.

3.3. Data collection

A customized data extraction tool was developed and used to extract details for each included study regarding article title, year of publication, study design, aims of the study, shoulder pathology, and details of interventions. Where available, data relating to the outcomes of the triage model/process described in each article were also captured. The consistency of data extraction was piloted before the main extraction on 3 randomly selected papers. These data extraction tool provided a standard and systematic approach to summarize the papers and extract all relevant information. Extracted data were then organized into thematic matrices to facilitate analysis.

3.4. Data synthesis

Data synthesis involved systematically organizing, summarizing, and interpreting findings from diverse sources to generate a comprehensive understanding of the triaging process or referral models under study.72 Thematic analysis was first conducted to identify recurring patterns, themes, and relationships among the different sources. The final synthesis involved a narrative analysis to generate a new perspective on the topic and insights across methodologies to create a cohesive narrative and to illustrate relationships among concepts. This approach allowed for a holistic understanding of the triage process, addressing the complexity of factors influencing referral models.72 The synthesized data were interpreted in relation to the review objectives, considering theoretical and practical implications. Critical appraisal of the findings was conducted to assess the robustness of conclusions, accounting for methodological limitations, potential biases, and contextual influences. This methodology supports a nuanced understanding of the topic, providing actionable insights and highlighting areas for future research in shoulder pain.72

3.5. Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included articles was assessed using the Mixed Method Appraisal Tool (MMAT) version 2018.41 The MMAT criteria were designed to concurrently appraise qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-method studies for large and complex systematic reviews and are well suited for the assessment of complex interventions that are context-dependent and process-oriented, such as triage models. In the MMAT, there are 2 generic items for all studies, in addition to 5 items specific to study types, including qualitative, quantitative (randomized, nonrandomized, descriptive) and mixed methods. Items were scored as yes = 1, unclear, or no = 0 (depending on whether the criteria were fully met, not met, or there was insufficient information to report). Mixed Method Appraisal Tool scores were included in Table 1 in the Supplemental Materials (https://links.lww.com/PR9/A449).

4. Results

The search identified 4326 potentially relevant articles. From these, 2674 unique articles were included for title and abstract screening. After screening, 378 articles were considered for eligibility. Of these eligible articles, 328 were removed because they were deemed irrelevant to the purposes of the review, and 50 articles were selected. A search of the gray literature obtained 184 articles, 21 of which were selected as additional articles specific to shoulder pain disorders. Thus, 71 articles in total were included for data extraction (Table 2 in Supplemental Materials, https://links.lww.com/PR9/A449). 4.1. General description

The review retrieved 37 articles describing a variety of models for managing shoulder issues (summarized in Table 3 in Supplemental Materials, https://links.lww.com/PR9/A449).

4.1.1. Diagnostic-based referral models

4.1.1.1. Guideline-based diagnostic models

Several diagnostic-based models to guide shoulder pain management, mainly for primary care providers, were identified. Although these models were originally developed to provide a structured approach to assess, diagnose, and manage shoulder pain, they also included either referral recommendations for surgical consultation or indications for surgical intervention. These models were developed mainly on the pathoanatomical concept, where they rely solely on pathoanatomical classifications to guide decision-making processes for either referrals or surgical candidacy.

Five articles discussed diagnostic decision models that set out straightforward guidelines to identify the patient's source of shoulder pain and initiate an appropriate management plan at the primary care level, and criteria for urgent and elective referral for specialist care.1,15,45,48,81 These models reviewed the 5 most common diagnoses (impingement syndrome, adhesive capsulitis, rotator cuff tears, and arthritis of the glenohumeral joint and acromioclavicular joint) responsible for shoulder pain, explained a simple method for making the correct diagnosis, reviewed the treatment options for each diagnosis, and provided indications to refer patients to an orthopedic surgeon.15,45,48,64,81

4.1.1.2. Consensus-based diagnostic models

The consensus process was repeatedly used to develop 7 diagnostic-based models, leveraging iterative expert feedback.18,19,25,26,36,99,100 One study developed a standardized clinical decision-making tool for shoulder pain in general.25 Six articles focused on the clinical pathway algorithm for diagnosing and managing rotator cuff pathology for adults.5,18,26,36,89,99 One study discussed a structured treatment algorithm specifically for elderly patients with rotator cuff tears,63 whereas another study produced a guideline for subacromial pain syndrome,19 and another achieved consensus for the first-time management of anterior glenohumeral dislocations.100 Another model was retrieved focusing on acute osseous Bankart lesions, classifying glenoid rim defects by 3D Computerized tomography scans and recommending conservative management for smaller lesions.94

4.1.1.3. Literature-based diagnostic models

Another 4 articles were retrieved that developed decision-making models based on literature reviews.33,59,64,73 These articles produced evidence-based decision models based on collecting data from mainly randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Two reviews proposed an evidence-based approach for treatment pathways using a simplified classification of shoulder problems.64,73 One review focused on rotator cuff surgery.59 Another review examined the literature regarding subscapular tear classification and treatment.33 This review also included a survey for shoulder surgeons to identify the approaches regarding surgical decision-making for these injuries.33

4.1.2. Physiotherapy-led triage model

Another identified type of decision-making model was the physiotherapist-led triage model. This model involved advanced practice physiotherapists using clinical reasoning skills to assess patients, request and interpret investigations, and undertake diagnostic procedures, as well as considering onward referral either to physiotherapy or surgery.71 Our review retrieved 13 articles discussing physiotherapy-led triage models.2,3,6,9,11,16,37,56,68,71,80,86,87 In these models, the referral process was streamlined by implementing criteria for surgical interventions, which were often based on findings from imaging and other investigations. For example, a study implemented a specialist soft tissue injury clinic where patients with recent shoulder trauma and an inability to raise their arm were referred to a physiotherapist, who assessed them and expedited surgery when necessary based on imaging.3 The Shoulder/Elbow Triage and Assessment model was another example that also used a physiotherapist-led triage process to assess patients on an orthopedic waitlist. This model was cost-effective, reduced the orthopedic clinic waitlists, treated patients nonoperatively when appropriate, and referred others for surgery if needed.9,16

4.1.3. Multicriteria decision-making models

The multicriteria decision-making (MCDM) is a model that uses mathematical analysis to quantify multiple clinical items. Unlike diagnostic-based models that rely solely on diagnosis, MCDM incorporates clinical information such as patient history, physical examination findings, and imaging results.97 By structuring this information into weighted criteria, MCDM prioritized key factors and guided treatment alternatives.28,97,98

Two MCDM models were retrieved based on the biopsychosocial framework.37,82 These models considered the biological, psychological, and social factors in supporting decision-making for shoulder pain. One model used the biopsychosocial model to identify phenotypes in individuals with acute and chronic rotator cuff-related shoulder pain.37 This model used algorithms and decision trees to prioritize patients for elective surgery, manage wait times, and allocate resources.37 The second model proposed a clinical framework integrating symptom characteristics, pain mechanisms and expectations, preferences and psychosocial factors to guide physiotherapists in conducting diagnostic triage and electing appropriate treatment pathways.82

4.1.4. Preference-based decision tools

Although we did not find any articles discussing patient prioritization for intake referrals, the review retrieved 2 studies incorporating patient preferences into the decision-making process for first-time anterior shoulder dislocations.27,42 These models demonstrated the shift towards structured, evidence-based, and patient-centred approaches in managing shoulder pain. They used the preference-based decision tool to guide patients toward treatment choices that were more closely aligned with evidence-based recommendations.27,42 By incorporating patient preferences, quantifying subjective data, and leveraging expert consensus, these models strived to tailor treatment recommendations to better align with individual needs, thus improving the quality and efficiency of care.42

Another preference-based decision model used a self-administered evaluation questionnaire form to appropriately triage new referrals with shoulder complaints.92 The questionnaire provided significantly more clinical information compared with traditional referrals. Together, they enabled appropriate patient triaging, ensuring that necessary investigations and initial treatments were initiated before formal orthopedic review.

4.1.5. Ultrasound-guided triage tool

Another retrieved triage model used ultrasound imaging as a diagnostic triage tool to improve management of patients with nonchronic shoulder pain and establish appropriate referrals.74,75 The model was developed as a pragmatic trial investigating the effect of using ultrasound imaging to target treatment to the specific underlying patho-anatomical disorders. One trial, however, showed no clinically significant difference in the primary outcome measure between the ultrasound-tailored treatment and usual care groups.74 Nevertheless, other studies suggested that shoulder sonography had a significant impact on clinical decision-making for shoulder pain, which goes beyond simply confirming or refuting a suspected diagnosis.12,29 One study found that 63% of patients had their treatment plans altered after undergoing shoulder sonography, likely because of sonography's ability to provide real-time imaging of the shoulder's soft tissues.29 Another study found that for patients or providers who often have a strong belief in the necessity of surgery to “fix” perceived mechanical problems in their shoulder, the sonographic findings might reinforce these beliefs or show the opposite, that there is no need for surgery, thus influencing patients' opinion towards surgical options and leading to more informed and appropriate treatment choices.12,29

4.2. Triage models key components

All models emphasized the importance of a thorough history, physical examination, and appropriate investigations to establish a diagnosis and inform a plan for either nonsurgical treatment or referral for surgical consultations. The key components of all shoulder pain decision-making models included the following.

4.2.1. Red flags

Regardless of the various methodologies used, all identified triage models emphasized the importance of identifying red flags as the first step in managing shoulder pain. These red flags mandate urgent referrals to emergency services once identified.5,27,48,81

4.2.2. Shoulder pain arising from the shoulder or elsewhere

The retrieved models discriminated between shoulder pain arising from the shoulder and shoulder pain originating from elsewhere.5,27,48,81 Shoulder pain arising from elsewhere included referred pain from the neck, myocardial ischemia, or referred diaphragmatic pain, polymyalgia rheumatica, or malignancy such as apical lung cancers and metastases. If shoulder pain arising from elsewhere was suspected, patients were recommended to be referred to the appropriate specialist, for example, a spine specialist, neurologist, cardiologist, or oncologist for consultation in a timely fashion to receive appropriate treatment for the underlying condition.5,27,48,81,82

4.2.3. Comprehensive history taking and physical examination

A thorough history was another key component in the retrieved triage models.5,27,48,81,82 It involved collecting detailed information about the patient's characteristics, including demographics (age, gender), occupation, sporting activities, dominant/nondominant hand, and hobbies. History taking also included questions regarding pain characteristics to clarify the patient's symptoms, including onset, history of trauma or instability, duration of symptoms, exacerbating and relieving factors, pain characteristics, involvement of other joints and systemic symptoms, and functional impact of pain. General medical history for comorbidities, current medications, and patient expectations was also listed.5,27,48,81,82

History taking was then followed by a physical examination which focused on identifying specific signs and symptoms associated with common shoulder conditions, including observation for any visible abnormalities, active and passive range of motion assessments to assess pain and function limitations, and levels of muscle strength.5,27,48,81,82

4.2.4. Differential diagnosis for shoulder pain

Based on clinical findings, the triage models then moved forward to a range of branching-logic questions to reach possible differential diagnosis for shoulder pain, including rotator cuff disorders (tendinopathy, impingement, bursitis, tears), glenohumeral disorders (adhesive capsulitis [frozen shoulder], arthritis), acromioclavicular joint problems, and traumatic and atraumatic instability.64 The branching questionnaire focused on instability, pain location, swelling, pain or limited active or passive motion, and pain with abduction of the thumb down and with resistance. The branching questions were also associated with specific tests and manoeuvres to assess for specific pathologies, such as impingement, labral tears, instability, and sensory and motor function loss.

4.2.5. Treatment offered

All retrieved models categorized treatments into 2 main categories for various shoulder pain conditions. These models suggested that treatment for shoulder pain may consist of (1) conservative management: analgesics, activity modification, patient education, steroid injections, and physiotherapy (including manual therapy and exercise) and (2) surgical interventions. Conservative treatment options were emphasized as the first line of management, whereas surgery was often recommended for only specific conditions or after the failure of the conservative approach. The models also highlighted that the conservative treatment needs to be conducted under the mentorship of a shoulder specialist and typically includes active rehabilitation under the guidance of a physiotherapist, supplemented by physiotherapy, exercises, and pain medication for effective conservative management.5,27,48,81,82

4.2.6. Clinical indications for surgical referrals

In addition to the retrieved triage models, our integrative review analyzed 17 articles comparing conservative treatments with surgical interventions for various shoulder pathologies and concluded a list of indications for surgery for specific clinical scenarios.7,21,30,38,40,43,46,47,54,67,69,79,83–85,88,96

Overall, the literature highlighted an ongoing debate regarding the optimal treatment approach for various shoulder injuries. Although the majority of articles demonstrated no significant difference in pain reduction between surgical interventions compared with conservative management,43,54,69 some articles suggested a benefit from surgical intervention, particularly in functional outcomes.67,79,83 For example, a systematic review published in 2017 concluded, based on the limited evidence available, that patients undergoing rotator cuff repair reported less pain and better function, particularly if surgery was performed less than 3 months from injury, compared with those operated on after 3 months.83 Similar conclusions were drawn from another large retrospective study investigating indications for rotator cuff repair, which found that symptom duration was a statistically significant factor that influenced the success of the surgery. The study also reported that a significant risk of retear existed if surgery was delayed beyond 2 years.79

Despite the controversy, our findings indicated that conservative treatment should always be the first option in the management of shoulder pain conditions.43,46,47,67,79,83 Nevertheless, articles outlined various scenarios where referral to an orthopedic surgeon for surgical consideration was indicated, categorized by shoulder condition, suggesting optimal repair times ranging from 3 weeks to 2 years.7,21,43,46,47,67,79,83,88,96 These scenarios where referral to an orthopedic surgeon is indicated are categorized by shoulder condition in Table 4 in Supplemental Materials (https://links.lww.com/PR9/A449). Despite these indications, however, the prioritization process was not explained in any of these articles.

4.3. General principles guiding referral decisions

Beyond specific conditions listed, the articles highlighted several key general principles that should guide decisions regarding referral for surgical consideration:

4.3.1. Shared decision-making

Shared decision-making was commonly emphasized throughout the literature as critical for the triage process. Articles emphasized the importance of engaging patients in open, collaborative discussions when determining treatment plans, including the consideration of surgical options.42,60,106 These discussions should include information about the risks and benefits of different treatment options, to facilitate informed decisions. In addition, all reviewed studies reached a consensus on the importance of a patient-centred approach to triage and treatment planning.42,60,106 This approach needs to include a comprehensive history that captures their occupational needs, functional goals, and desired activity levels. Considering these individual factors enables treatment decisions, particularly surgery, to be tailored to patients' expectations and preferences.42,60,106

4.3.2. Benefits of referral for orthopedic surgeons

The literature also showed that referral to an orthopedic surgeon does not ultimately mean going for surgery. Referral may have other benefits. It may include a clear discussion about the risks and benefits of surgical options.48,81,82 The authors also suggested that managing patient expectations regarding the recovery process and potential outcomes of surgical management is crucial. This can involve discussing a realistic timeline for surgery, explaining the duration of rehabilitation and how long it takes to restore function and reduce pain to a satisfactory level, and the possibility of retears or persistent symptoms.48,94,97,99

Referral may be necessary to access advanced imaging modalities like ultrasound or magnetic resonance imaging, particularly when the diagnosis remains uncertain or conservative treatment has not yielded satisfactory results.48

4.3.3. Factors affecting decision making

Ten articles exploring factors influencing decision-making regarding shoulder surgery and treatment choices for people with shoulder pain were identified and reviewed.29,42,50,58,60,61,65,105–107 These studies used various methodologies and examined both patients' and healthcare professionals' perspectives.

The articles showed that decision-making for shoulder surgery is influenced by a complex interplay of patient, provider, and systemic factors. Patient factors such as old age, comorbidities, fear of surgery, symptom severity, and failure of nonsurgical treatments to improve symptoms also influence decisions.65,105 Socioeconomic and cultural factors, patient beliefs, provider biases, and access to clear information were also found to significantly influence treatment choices.65 Patients with lower income, limited English fluency, and poor understanding of their condition were less likely to ask for surgery. In addition, structural factors like longer distances to the hospital and extended wait times were among the most common reasons for surgery cancellation.50

Many patients perceived surgery as an instant “fix” for a biomechanical issue, often trusting surgeons who may frequently emphasize structural abnormalities and imaging results in their recommendations.58 One study recommended the consideration of psychosocial factors alongside biomechanical explanations.60 Although another study stated that clinical reasoning among providers should mainly rely on history-taking, physical examination, and pattern recognition rather than specific diagnostic tests.61

Knowledge was another factor that influenced decision-making. Two studies investigated how a decision aid affected patients' intentions to have shoulder surgery.106,107 This decision aid provided unbiased information on the benefits and harms of different health care options and helped patients make informed treatment choices and potentially reduce unnecessary surgery.106 Although these decision aids improved knowledge, studies noted, however, that they had a limited effect on treatment intention, attitudes, informed choice, and decisional conflict. This suggests that other factors, such as trust in surgeon expertise, biomedical beliefs, and economic incentives, may be strong drivers for surgery.

4.3.4. Predictors for shoulder surgery

Our review identified 7 studies examining predictors for shoulder pain prognosis and the likelihood of surgical intervention.10,22,44,49,62,95,103 These studies encompassed various shoulder conditions and used different methodologies to identify key factors influencing treatment outcomes.

Several factors were identified as key predictors of the necessity of surgery for shoulder conditions. The patient's low expectation regarding the effectiveness of conservative treatment was the strongest predictor for patients being more likely to opt for surgery.44,103 In addition, higher activity levels and being a nonsmoker are also predictive of choosing surgery.22 For those with calcific tendinitis, a calcific lesion greater than 1 cm was associated with an increased likelihood of needing surgery.44 Furthermore, certain patient demographics, such as younger age, lower body mass index, and symptom duration of less than a year, are predictive of surgical treatment allocation for full-thickness rotator cuff tears.49,103 Finally, patients who have had an injury, previously saw an orthopedic provider, or previously underwent magnetic resonance imaging on the affected shoulder are more likely to undergo surgery. By contrast, structural factors, such as tear size and retraction, as well as pain levels and weakness, were not predictive in 3 studies.22,44,103

5. Discussion

Shoulder pain conditions are a common cause of disability, accounting for up to 30% of primary care consultations.64 Traditionally, many patients are referred from primary to specialized care for more comprehensive treatment. However, the lack of clear clinical guidelines and consensus on surgical indications has caused a continuous rise in referrals that can overwhelm surgeons with inappropriate referrals and increased waiting times.99 Therefore, there is a growing demand to review existing triage models used to streamline patient management.

Our review critically analyzed the literature, including peer-reviewed and gray literature, focusing on existing triage models, surgical indications, and factors influencing decision-making. We identified multiple triage models, including diagnostic-based, physiotherapy-led, preference-based, and multicriteria-based models (Table 3 in Supplemental Materials, https://links.lww.com/PR9/A449). Our findings highlighted 4 critical themes that must be considered for any triage model to be effective.

5.1. Complexity of the decision-making process

Although many of the retrieved articles focused on pathoanatomical-based decision making, our review findings highlighted the complexities surrounding triage decision making for conservative and surgical treatment of shoulder pain management. Multiple factors influence the decision-making process and the effectiveness of outcomes.48,94,97,99 These contributing factors may include condition-specific factors, patient-related characteristics, and provider-related factors.50,58,60,61,65 Each factor carries a specific weight in the decision-making process.97 Moreover, these factors tend to interact, providing a unique, individualized net impact on decision-making and its effectiveness on outcomes.97

Several articles underscored the impact of psychological factors on outcomes. Anxiety, depression, coping, fear of movement, catastrophizing, work satisfaction, psychological demands, and social support can delay recovery, increase the risk of chronic disability, and even impact a patient's preference for surgery.24,32 Therefore, it is essential to consider these factors during the triage process because they often indicate the need for psychologically informed interventions rather than solely biomechanical treatments. Considering such complexity, a biopsychosocial framework should be considered as a foundation for triage models to ensure appropriate treatment allocation.37

5.2. Shared decision-making and patient-centred care

The literature also indicated that patients' preferences and values play a crucial role in both the decision-making process and treatment effectiveness. Patients who strongly prefer surgery but are assigned conservative treatment often experience poor results, even when surgery is not clinically needed.42,107 In addition, patients' or providers' perspectives may focus on specific biological aspects, directing decision making towards certain pathways such as imaging or surgery, even when such interventions may not be necessary.42,107 These findings underscore the importance of aligning treatment plans with patient expectations to enhance adherence and satisfaction. Shared decision-making for shoulder pain triage models was recommended for optimizing treatment outcomes.27,42,107 This approach provides a transparent, 2-way conversation between the clinician and patient that addresses the patient's concerns, goals, and expectations. It also provides clear explanations of treatment options, the current evidence for various treatment approaches, the recovery timeline, the rehabilitation process, and potential risks associated with both conservative and surgical treatment, such as infections, retears, or persistent symptoms. Shared decision-making assists patients in making informed, personalized treatment choices consistent with their values. Shared decision-making also includes decision aids—such as educational tools, interactive models, and structured consultations—that can improve patient knowledge, reduce unrealistic expectations, and promote cost-effective resource use.27 By integrating shared decision-making, triage models can empower patients to make informed choices while supporting clinicians in delivering patient-centred care.27

5.3. Limitations of existing triage models

Although the retrieved decision-making models offered structured processes for shoulder pain management, the referral process and patient prioritization remained ambiguous.97 The models lacked clear guidance on how to navigate the complexity associated with determining whether a patient requires referral to an orthopedic surgeon or conservative treatment. Despite their potential, retrieved triage models presented several limitations that hindered their real-world implementation.10,44,97,103

5.3.1. Goals of triage models

Health care triage is a critical-thinking sorting process where an experienced clinician rapidly assesses referred patients, determines the severity of their condition, assigns them to a specific category (eg, emergent, urgent, nonurgent, or deferrable), and directs them toward appropriate care (ie, treatment allocation and patient prioritization).86,87 This decision-making process relies on both subjective and objective data to ensure alignment with clinical guidelines for shoulder pain assessment, diagnosis, and management.86,87 Notably, this review did not identify articles that explained the patient prioritization process or used specific algorithms or standardized triage rules for referral to orthopedic surgeons. Instead, the literature largely described pathoanatomic-based diagnosis as indications for surgical referral while listing potential patient, condition, and provider-related factors that may influence referral decisions, without integrating these elements into transparent or reproducible prioritization processes. The absence of concrete prioritization frameworks represents a critical gap in current referral pathways and directly motivates the need for future research focused on developing structured, multicriteria triage models that can support consistent, equitable, and patient-centred referral decisions.

Furthermore, although these triage models were able to reduce long waiting times, none of them showed any improvement in efficiency or timely access to the appropriate health care professional for optimal management. Reliable triage models, however, are expected to achieve the Quintuple Aims,70 including: 1. Better patient experience (expedited personalized treatment, reduced pain/disability), 2. Better population health (improved communication and transparent process, reduced wait times and disability burden), 3. Better clinician experience (streamlined process, reduced burnouts and workloads), 4. Better cost-effectiveness (reduced unemployment and work absenteeism, reduced/disability claims), and 5. Equity (fair triage and timely access to appropriate care).

5.3.2. Lack of generalizability

Although physiotherapy-led triage models showed promise in musculoskeletal conditions, including shoulder pain, and were associated with high patient and general practitioner satisfaction, evidence supporting their clinical effectiveness remained weak because of methodological limitations.71 Under this model, primary care providers send referrals to a central intake, where the nonurgent referrals are triaged to a Rapid Access Clinic (RAC)66 that is staffed by advanced practice clinicians (APCs), such as physiotherapists, who assess patients and determine whether surgical consultation is needed. Although APC assessments can improve care access and reduce unnecessary surgeon visits, the process is hampered by long waits, multiple handoffs, and delays from incomplete referrals. According to Ontario's Surgical Information Program, wait time 1a (from central intake to RAC) and wait time 1b (from RAC to surgeon consult) are major contributors to care delays, adding up to 46 days to patient wait times.66 Advanced practice clinicians face time constraints and workforce shortages can limit the consistent collection of patient data.66

None of the retrieved studies provided follow-up data on clinical outcomes after using physiotherapy-led triage. Another critical gap in these studies is the lack of robust evidence supporting the diagnostic accuracy of physiotherapists in triage settings compared with orthopedic surgeons.71 Predefined diagnostic criteria were often absent or inadequately defined, raising concerns about consistency and reliability in triage decision-making.71 Although many studies reported on the experience levels of physiotherapists, few provided detailed information on their training for diagnostic skills.

Most evaluations of physiotherapy-led triage models have focused on reducing waiting times and improving patient satisfaction as key success indicators. Although these are important outcomes, the success of triage models should also be measured by their ability to provide clear and feasible instructions for appropriate treatment allocation and patient prioritization, ensuring positive short- and long-term clinical outcomes.71

Physiotherapists also operate as independent providers requiring separate referrals, which complicates their integration into standard triage health care systems in some countries. They may not be covered by health insurance in some jurisdictions. This financial barrier can limit the generalizability and applicability of a physiotherapy-led triage model.

5.3.3. Overreliance on pathoanatomical classifications

Given the complexities of diagnosing and managing shoulder pain, relying solely on patho-anatomical classifications for referral and treatment decisions, as proposed in diagnostic-based models, may be insufficient.97 These models assumed that a diagnosis automatically leads to effective treatment. However, a shoulder pain diagnosis is often complex, and patients can present with overlapping symptoms or associated referred pain, making traditional diagnostic-based triage models impractical.52 For example, rotator cuff tendinopathy, impingement syndrome, and small rotator cuff tears can all manifest as pain during overhead activities.52,64 Frozen shoulder and glenohumeral arthritis can both lead to stiffness and restricted movement, particularly in the early stages. Referred pain from the cervical spine can mimic primary shoulder pain, making the rigid distinctions assumed by traditional guidelines inapplicable.104

Another key limitation of traditional guidelines was their dependence on evidence-based medicine, predominantly derived from RCTs. Although RCTs have significantly advanced evidence-based guidelines, they have limited real-world applicability. Randomized controlled trials are mainly designed to control external factors, which leads to failure to capture the complexity of real-world clinical scenarios, meaning study populations often do not fully reflect the diverse comorbidities and variability seen in real-world patients. Traditional guidelines attempt to standardize treatment pathways but may not account for individual patient variability, preferences, and contextual factors.27 Traditional guidelines frequently overlook the perspectives of both patients and healthcare providers. Most identified guidelines were based on expert opinions and lacked a patient-centred approach that integrates clinical evidence with shared decision making, which is essential to optimize outcomes.27

5.3.4. The pitfall of the binary approach

Despite all efforts toward a nuanced approach for the triage models, our review showed that all models to triage shoulder pain are adopting a predominant binary vision, for example, categorizing patients into 2 distinct groups, either “eligible” or “ineligible” for surgery or giving patients 1 of 2 treatment pathways, either surgery or conservative. This oversimplification contradicts what the same literature has highlighted regarding the complexity of the condition, the decision-making process, and the need to be more comprehensive and inclusive for all factors impacting the decision making, including patient preferences.

5.4. Multicriteria decision making: a comprehensive triage model

Although the apparent scarcity of biopsychosocial models may partly reflect the scope of our search rather than a true absence, a key finding of this review was the identification of the MCDM-based model for shoulder pain. This MCDM model represents a critical shift in the triage process from being based purely on a pathoanatomical-based approach.97 The retrieved MCDM-based model offered a more dynamic and nuanced framework for appropriate patient prioritization and treatment allocation, potentially enabling personalized and effective care tailored to individual needs.77,78 This model expanded the focus beyond physical evaluation to include physical examination findings and imaging results into weighted decision criteria. It also allowed for the adoption of the biopsychosocial framework for pain assessment and diagnosis, facilitating the consideration of psychological factors, financial status, and social factors in the decision-making process.37,82,97 By addressing both the physical and psychosocial components of shoulder pain, clinicians can develop more effective and personalized treatment strategies. Such a model would account for the heterogeneity in pain mechanisms, the psychosocial factors, and the patient's goals. Unlike other identified models that categorize patients into fixed pathoanatomic-based groups, MCDM model would recognize that symptoms and signs exist on a continuum where patients may exhibit criteria from multiple diagnostic categories. It could also identify various pain phenotypes within individuals, considering pathology and the efficiency of the body's own pain modulation systems (nociceptive, nocioplasty, and neuropathic), in addition to the complex interplay of biological, psychological, and social factors contributing to pain perception. In addition, MCDM-based models could also incorporate patients' goals, preferences, expectations, and predictors for treatment candidacy and outcomes.77,78 This approach encourages a shift from a purely biomechanical understanding of shoulder pain towards a more holistic model that mandates shared decision making and is rooted in patient-centred care principles.

The retrieved MCDM-based models represent a significant advancement for shoulder classification and can support novice and expert clinicians in the referral process.93 Clinicians can make more consistent and effective decisions with a systematic approach to integrating and quantifying subjective and objective clinical information, transforming complex decision-making into an ordered, transparent process.78 Most importantly, treatment recommendations and referrals are guided by patient and pain characteristics rather than presumed pathoanatomical diagnoses.104 Through a MCDM-based triage model (Fig. 2), clinicians can offer multiple treatment pathways simultaneously to address the complexity of individual cases and attend to various patients' presentations and needs. For example, a patient referred for surgery could receive conservative treatment and education for all available options while waiting for surgical consultation. However, patients who are clinically ineligible for surgery can be treated conservatively, while also referred for surgical consultation, albeit with lower urgency, if they believe in surgical interventions. Respecting patients' preferences can enhance patient autonomy and increase the effectiveness of conservative approaches.

New triage model framework.

A crucial component in the MCDM approach is the potential integration of psychological and social support in sequence with either conservative or surgical treatments. This component addresses underlying psychological issues such as fear of movement or depression, which may affect treatment effectiveness. Addressing these issues can improve treatment outcomes and enhance the effectiveness of conservative management.

The integration of patient education or decision aids is also critical to the MCDM approach. Providing comprehensive information and support empowers patients to make more informed decisions about their care or reconsider their preferences. This approach can potentially reduce unnecessary surgical referrals while improving patient satisfaction and reducing health care costs.

However, despite its clear benefits, integrating MCDM into routine clinical practice faces significant challenges. To apply MCDM models, primary care providers must navigate complex patient histories and varying communication styles, all of which can complicate clinical decision-making.97 In addition, existing barriers such as limited clinician training, time constraints, and the complexity of psychological assessment tools will hinder the widespread adoption of MCDM. Without addressing these barriers, triage models risk remaining subjective and inconsistent, leading to unnecessary referrals and imaging.97

Although we did not identify literature on the use of artificial intelligence (AI) in shoulder triage models, future research could explore its potential to support the integration and analysis of complex, multidimensional factors within decision-making frameworks. Artificial intelligence–powered machine learning algorithms can analyze clinical data beyond human capacity.97 These algorithms can quantify, weigh, and rank influential variables, improve the accuracy and consistency of treatment recommendations, and reduce bias in clinical decision-making.37 Despite these benefits, we have to acknowledge that key barriers include the need for high-quality, representative data; risks of algorithmic bias that may inadvertently reinforce existing health inequities; and issues related to clinician trust, transparency, and interpretability. In addition, a substantial gap persists between proof-of-concept models and real-world deployment, with regulatory, ethical, and system integration challenges limiting the routine use of AI in primary care settings. As such, AI should be considered a complementary and long-term opportunity, requiring rigorous evaluation, stakeholder engagement, and context-sensitive implementation before it can meaningfully support phenotype-informed triage approaches.

5.5. Strengths/limitations

This review is a systematic integrative analysis of the literature on existing triage models for shoulder pain management. By examining both peer-reviewed and gray literature, the review provided an integrative understanding of existing triage approaches, including diagnostic-based, physiotherapy-led, preference-based, and multicriteria-based models. The review identified key themes on triage models for shoulder pain: the goals of the triage process, the complexity of decision-making, shared decision-making and patient-centred care, and the limitations of existing models. These themes provided a structured framework for understanding the challenges and opportunities to develop an effective triage model.

There are several limitations associated with this review. We acknowledge that the protocol was registered after the completion of the search strategy, and that this may introduce potential risks related to methodological transparency and reporting bias. Although the use of both gray and peer-reviewed literature is considered a strength, the use of non–peer-reviewed literature has the potential for selection bias. Gray literature may lack the rigour of peer-reviewed studies, which can affect the robustness of the conclusions. We also have to acknowledge that the search strategy may have underrepresented psychosocial search terms and was not specifically designed to identify biopsychosocial decision-making models. Rather, the focus was on models related to shoulder pain more broadly, irrespective of whether conditions were acute or chronic. In addition, we did not include databases such as PsycINFO, which may have further limited the identification of relevant biopsychosocial frameworks. Additionally, since the review was limited to the English language, relevant articles may have been missed. The type of articles included in the review varied significantly in design, methodology, and outcomes measured and the heterogeneity made analysis challenging and limited the integration of qualitative and quantitative findings to provide strong, evidence-based recommendations. Moreover, although our proposed new approach has the potential to improve the triage process, it is a theoretical framework that requires evaluation in real-world practice to determine its effectiveness, feasibility, and applicability.

6. Conclusion

Shoulder pain triage is a complex process that requires a nuanced, multifactorial decision-making referral process that acknowledges the complexity of pain as a multifactorial condition according to the biopsychosocial framework and systematically incorporates patient preferences into the triage decision-making process while also attending to the feasibility of implementing this model within primary care settings.
