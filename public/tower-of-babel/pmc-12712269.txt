Comprehensive analysis based on spatial domains identifies CD44 as a potential target of puerarin
Authors: Song-yang Xi, Hui Zhang, Qi-juan Wang, Yuan-jie Liu, Shu-chen Chang, Chen-fu Shi, Dong Fang, Li-hui-ping Tao, Hai-bo Cheng, Xi Zou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMelanoma, a highly metastatic and treatment-resistant malignancy, urgently requires novel therapeutic strategies. Puerarin, a natural isoflavone with established anti-tumorigenic effects in diverse cancers, remains underexplored in melanoma despite its potential to modulate melanogenesis and oxidative stress. This study investigates puerarin’s spatial targeting mechanisms in melanoma to elucidate its therapeutic specificity.MethodsA multi-omics approach integrating single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), spatial transcriptomics, and structure-based docking plus normal-mode analysis was employed. Spatial domain analysis identified puerarin-responsive malignant cell clusters, while molecular docking and protein-ligand simulations prioritized CD44 as a candidate receptor. Functional validation included extracellular matrix (ECM) signaling pathway analysis, spatial colocalization studies, and in vitro experiments.ResultsSpatial analysis showed enrichment of puerarin-related signals in malignant-cell–dominated domains. ECM ligands (collagens, fibronectin, laminins) that signal through CD44/SDC1 were concentrated in these domains, where CD44 was overexpressed relative to surrounding compartments. Molecular modeling suggested CD44, a cell-surface receptor overexpressed in melanoma cells, as a putative mediator of puerarin’s effects on these pathways. In vitro experiments further supported CD44’s role in modulating puerarin-responsive domains.ConclusionThese observations are hypothesis-generating and provide a potential direction for future research on whether CD44 mediates puerarin’s spatial effects in melanoma.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00432-025-06389-2.Keywords: Spatial domain, Network pharmacology, Puerarin, Melanoma, CD44

Introduction

Malignant melanoma, a highly metastatic cutaneous malignancy, demonstrates a growing global incidence, particularly among Caucasian populations, with UV radiation and genetic susceptibility as key drivers (Ahmed et al. 2020). Despite representing < 5% of skin cancers, it accounts for >75% of skin cancer-related deaths due to its propensity for metastasis and therapeutic resistance. Standard care includes surgical excision for localized disease and systemic therapy for unresectable or metastatic disease. Systemic options include B-Raf proto-oncogene serine/threonine kinase (BRAF) inhibitors used with mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase 1/2 (MEK1/2) inhibitors in BRAF-mutant melanoma, and immune-checkpoint blockade targeting programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) or cytotoxic T-lymphocyte–associated protein 4 (CTLA-4). Radiotherapy is used in selected settings (for example, palliation or brain metastases) (Zaremba et al. 2020; Leonardi et al. 2018; Proietti et al. 2020). However, limitations such as acquired resistance to targeted therapies and immune-related adverse events persist (Schneider et al. 2021). Natural products, characterized by multi-modal mechanisms, offer complementary benefits, including modulation of oncogenic pathways, enhancement of immunogenic cell death, and attenuation of treatment-associated toxicity (Syed and Mukhtar 2011; Chinembiri et al. 2014). Compounds like resveratrol and berberine exemplify this potential, demonstrating synergistic effects with conventional agents while maintaining favorable safety profiles (Zhao et al. 2018; Palma et al. 2022).

Puerarin, a naturally derived isoflavone isolated from Pueraria lobata, has emerged as a promising candidate in oncological research due to its multifaceted anti-tumorigenic properties(Li et al. 2019). Preclinical studies demonstrate its capacity to modulate key carcinogenic pathways, including inhibition of cell proliferation, induction of apoptosis via mitochondrial dysfunction and caspase activation, and suppression of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). Recent preclinical studies report additive or synergistic effects with standard chemotherapeutics. The effects may involve downregulation of the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/AKT/mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway and nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB)–mediated inflammatory signaling (Zhou et al. 2014; Hu et al. 2024; Jia et al. 2019; Tao et al. 2020). Puerarin also shows antioxidant activity: it lowers reactive oxygen species (ROS), limits lipid peroxidation, and induces the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway (Lei et al. 2025; Liga and Paul 2024). Ongoing translational efforts focus on optimizing its bioavailability through nanoparticle formulations, with preliminary clinical data suggesting dose-dependent efficacy in hormone-responsive malignancies and hepatocellular carcinoma (Zhang et al. 2014; Ye et al. 2022). These findings underscore puerarin’s therapeutic versatility as both a standalone agent and adjuvant in precision oncology paradigms. Despite its emerging role in oncology, preclinical investigation of puerarin’s therapeutic potential in melanoma remains notably scarce, with limited mechanistic insights into its anti-tumor efficacy. While prior studies in other malignancies suggest potential pathways—such as modulation of apoptotic signaling or inhibition of angiogenesis—these mechanisms remain poorly characterized in the context of melanoma pathogenesis (Zeng et al. 2014; Pan et al. 2023). The scarcity of in vivo or clinical data specifically addressing puerarin’s impact on melanoma progression, metastatic cascades, or synergy with existing therapies underscores a critical knowledge gap requiring systematic exploration.

In this study, we integrated bulk, single-cell, and spatial transcriptomic data to explore how puerarin may act in melanoma. Puerarin-linked signals concentrated in spatial domains dominated by malignant cells. Domain-level communication highlighted collagen, fibronectin, and laminin pathways. From these domains, CD44 emerged as a candidate downstream target.

CD44 is a cell-surface receptor with a standard isoform (CD44s) and multiple variants (CD44v) generated by alternative splicing (Chen et al. 2018). It binds hyaluronan and other matrix ligands and connects through ezrin, radixin, and moesin (ERM) proteins to the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and PI3K/AKT pathways, which support adhesion, migration, and survival. CD44 is often high in tumors (Rudzki and Jothy 1997; Hassn Mesrati et al. 2021), including melanoma (Rünger et al. 1994), and is linked to invasive behavior (Voiculescu et al. 2025; Lah et al. 2020). Here, we test whether CD44 may mediate puerarin-related signals in melanoma using single-cell and spatial analyses, docking, and cell assays, suggesting a testable direction for future work.

Materials and methods

Puerarin-related targets acquisition

To predict the potential targets of puerarin, multiple computational tools were employed based on distinct theoretical frameworks. D3CARP (https://d3cart.pharmaceuticalinformatics.org/) (Shi et al. 2023) predicts potential protein targets for small molecules by employing a multi-scale computational framework that systematically integrates chemical structure, genomic similarity, and network pharmacology principles. OncoPubMiner (https://oncopubminer.chosenmedinfo.com/) (Xu et al. 2022) identifies and validates predicted drug-target interactions by harnessing a comprehensively curated knowledge base of published oncological literature, enabling evidence-based prioritization of high-confidence targets through advanced text mining. PharmMapper (Wang et al. 2017a, b) (http://www.lilab-ecust.cn/pharmmapper/) utilizes reverse molecular docking to identify potential binding targets by aligning the 3D structure of puerarin with a repository of pharmacophore models derived from known protein-ligand complexes. SwissTargetPrediction (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/) (Daina et al. 2019) combines 2D and 3D ligand similarity metrics, leveraging a curated database of bioactive compounds to infer targets through structural and physicochemical property comparisons. Similarity Ensemble Approach (SEA) (http://sea.bkslab.org/) (Keiser et al. 2007) predicts targets by calculating ligand similarity scores across large-scale pharmacological datasets, emphasizing shared molecular features between puerarin and annotated ligands. Search tool for interactions of chemicals (STITCH) (http://stitch.embl.de/) (version 5.0) (Kuhn et al. 2008) integrates experimental data, text-mining results, and structural homology to construct protein-chemical interaction networks, prioritizing targets based on multi-evidence confidence scores. SuperPred (https://prediction.charite.de/) (version 3.0) (Gallo et al. 2022) applies a machine learning framework trained on structural fingerprints and molecular descriptors to predict target associations through pattern recognition in chemical space. These complementary approaches—spanning pharmacophore matching, ligand similarity, and multi-evidence integration—collectively enhance the robustness of target prediction, addressing limitations inherent to single-method analyses. Finally, potential targets from the seven sources were pooled together for follow-up studies.

Enrichment and immune analysis

The targets associated with puerarin were subjected to functional enrichment analysis and immune analysis using the Biomarker Exploration for Solid Tumors (BEST) web tool (Liu et al. 2023), which was employed to perform Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) annotations, and immune-related correlations.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data sources and related processing

The scRNA-seq data involved in this study, including GSE148190 (Mahuron et al. 2020), GSE174401 (Emmons et al. 2023), and GSE215120 (Zhang et al. 2022a, b), were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus portal. We merged the three datasets and removed the batch effect using the “IntegrateLayers” function (Olst et al. 2025) in Seurat V5 (harmony method), and obtained the results including blood, brain, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), lymph node (LN), and skin tissues in a merged dataset.

For basal processing, the Seurat standard process was followed to calculate uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) coordinates for individual cells. Each cell was then manually annotated using recognized genetic markers and the “ggplot2” package (version 3.5.1.9) (Valero-Mora 2010) was used to visualize the clustering and annotation results. In order to compare the expression differences of puerarin targets in different cell groups, we used the six-algorithm including “viper”, “UCell”, “singscore”, “ssgsea”, “JASMINE”, and “AUCell” within “irGSEA” package (version 3.3.2) (Fan et al. 2024) to calculate the target scores and the “vlnplot” and “dotplot” functions for visualization. In addition, feature plots were generated using the “Nebulosa” package (version 1.14.0) (Alquicira-Hernandez and Powell 2021)for easy reading.

Spatial transcriptome (ST) data sources and related processing

The ST data involved in this study, including six melanoma samples (VISDS000241, VISDS000242, VISDS000243, VISDS000244, VISDS000459, and VISDS000460), were downloaded from the Comprehensive Repository of Spatial Transcriptomics (CROST) database (Wang et al. 2024).

We pre-processed the ST data by “Seurat” and “Semla” (version 1.3.0) packages (Larsson et al. 2023), and then the deconvolution was performed using the “SpaCET” package (version 1.2.0) (Ru et al. 2023) to annotate the proportion of cells in each spot. The annotation results were combined with the clustering results based on the “BayesSpace” package (version 1.14.0) (Zhao et al. 2021) with the help of the “SpaTopic” package (version 0.1.0) (Zhang et al. 2024) to classify all ST samples into different spatial domains (cell topics). Similar to the scRNA-seq scoring approach, we projected the expression of puerarin targets onto spatial organization to identify domains specifically targeted by puerarin.

To analyze domain-domain communications, we utilized the ligand-receptor information from the “CellChat” package (version 2.1.2) (Jin et al. 2021) and performed the associated analysis and visualization.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking was performed with PrankWeb (Jakubec et al. 2022), based on AutoDock Vina (version 1.2.7) (blind docking) (Trott and Olson 2010). Evolutionary conservation was included in the prediction model. The puerarin active ingredient structure was obtained from PubChem (Wang et al. 2017a, b), while CD44 (prediction infromation: https://prankweb.cz/api/v2/prediction/v4-alphafold-conservation-hmm/P16070/public/prankweb.zip) and SDC1 (prediction infromation: https://prankweb.cz/api/v2/prediction/v4-alphafold-conservation-hmm/P18827/public/prankweb.zip) protein structures were predicted by AlphaFold3 (Jumper et al. 2021). This allowed for the study of drug-protein interactions based on their structures.

Normal-mode analysis (NMA) of docked complexes

Docked protein–puerarin complexes were analyzed by normal-mode analysis (NMA) using the iMODS server (http://imods.chaconlab.org) (López-Blanco et al. 2014). The iMODS platform was utilized to characterize the structural dynamics and stability of docked complexes, analyzing deformability, B-factors, eigenvalues, variance, covariance maps, and elastic network properties. Input files consisted of docked Protein Data Bank (PDB) structures uploaded to the server with all analytical parameters retained at default settings. This dual approach enabled comprehensive assessment of ligand-induced conformational changes and dynamic stability within the protein-puerarin systems.

Cell culture

Human melanoma cell lines A375 (Cat: CL-0014, RRID: CVCL_0132) and SK-MEL-28 (Cat: CL-0717, RRID: CVCL_0526) were obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). A375 cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (Gibco, USA, Cat: C11995500BT) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, USA, Cat: 10099-141) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, USA, Cat: 15140-122), whereas SK-MEL-28 cells were cultured in minimum essential medium (MEM) (Procell Life Science & Technology, Wuhan, China, Cat: CM-0717) containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. All cultures were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2. Media were refreshed every 2–3 days, and cells were passaged upon reaching ~ 80% confluence using 0.25% trypsin–EDTA (Gibco, USA, Cat: 25200-072). Cell line identity was confirmed by short tandem repeat profiling, and mycoplasma contamination was routinely excluded by monthly PCR testing.

Cell viability assay (CCK-8)

Puerarin (purity ≥ 98%) (MedChemExpress Company, China, Cat: HY-N0145) was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (Sigma-Aldrich, USA, Cat: D2650-100) to create a stock solution. Cells were treated with various concentrations of puerarin (0, 5, 10, 20, 30, 40 µM) for 12, 24, 36, and 48 h. A DMSO-treated control group (0.1% final concentration) was included in each experiment to account for solvent effects. Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Dojindo Molecular Technologies, Japan, Cat: CK04). Following treatment, cells were incubated with 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent per well for 1 h at 37 °C, according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek, UK, FLx800) to determine cell viability.

TUNEL assay

Melanoma cells were cultured on coverslips, treated with puerarin or the positive control drug dacarbazine (MedChemExpress, USA, Cat: HY-B0078), and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosharp, China, Cat: BL539A). After permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Biofroxx, Germany, Cat: 143306), cells were incubated with the TUNEL reaction mixture (Beyotime, China, Cat: C1086) at 37 °C for 1 h. Nuclei were stained with DAPI (Beyotime, China, Cat: C1006). Apoptotic cells were identified as TUNEL-positive, exhibiting yellow fluorescence under an inverted fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Japan, Eclipse Ti).

Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)

CETSA experiments were performed according to the general CETSA protocol (Zhang et al. 2022a, b; Dai et al. 2019). Melanoma cells were seeded in 100-mm dishes and, at ~ 80% confluence, treated with puerarin (20 µM) or DMSO for 24 h. Cells were harvested and resuspended in PBS containing protease inhibitors. Samples were aliquoted into thin-walled PCR tubes and heated at 55–70 °C for 3 min in a thermal cycler, then cooled on ice. Cells were lysed by three liquid-nitrogen/heat-block freeze-thaw cycles. Lysates were cleared at 20,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. Supernatants were mixed with 4× Laemmli sample buffer (Biorad, Germany, Cat: 1610747) and heated at 95 °C for 5 min before Western blot (WB) assay.

Annexin V/propidium iodide (PI) flow cytometry

A375 and SK-MEL-28 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and grown to ~ 70% confluence. Cells were treated for 24 h with puerarin (0, 20, 30, 40 µM), or dacarbazine 30 µM (positive control). Cells were collected with 0.25% trypsin–EDTA, neutralized with medium, and washed twice with cold PBS. Pellets were resuspended in Annexin V binding buffer (from Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit, (Beyotime, China, Cat: C1062M). For each sample, 1 × 10⁵ cells were stained with Annexin V-FITC (5 µL) and PI (5 µL) in 100 µL binding buffer for 15 min at room temperature in the dark, then diluted with 400 µL binding buffer. Single-stain and unstained controls were prepared for compensation. Data were acquired within 1 h on a flow cytometer (Guilin Ulead Medical Electronics, URIT bf-730, China) and analyzed with FlowJo v10.X At least 10,000 events per sample were collected after debris exclusion and doublet discrimination (FSC-A/FSC-H). Quadrants were defined as live (Annexin V−/PI−), early apoptosis (Annexin V+/PI−), late apoptosis (Annexin V+/PI+), and necrosis (Annexin V−/PI+). Experiments were performed in triplicate.

Western blot (WB) assay

Melanoma cells were harvested and lysed in Radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) (Beyotime, China, Cat: P0013B) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentration was determined using the BCA protein assay (Abbkine, China, Cat: KTD3001). Equal amounts of protein (30 µg) were separated by 10% SurePAGE (GenScript, China, Cat: M00666) and transferred to PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature, followed by incubation with primary antibody overnight at 4 °C: CD44 (1:1000, Proteintech, USA, Cat: 15675-1-AP); cleaved caspase-3 (Asp175) (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA, Cat: 9661 S); caspase-3 (total) (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA, Cat: 9662 S); cleaved caspase-7 (Asp198) (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA, Cat: 9491 S); caspase-7 (total) (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA, Cat: 9492 S); cleaved PARP (Asp214) (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA, Cat: 9541 S); PARP (total) (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA, Cat: 9542 S); β-actin (1:5000 Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA, Cat: sc-58679) and GAPDH (1:5000, Proteintech, USA, Cat: 60004-1-Ig). Cleaved and total forms were detected on parallel membranes prepared from the same lysates with equal loading. After TBST washes, membranes were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with fluorescent, highly cross-adsorbed secondary antibodies (LI-COR, USA), as appropriate: goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (1:5000; Cat: 926-68070) and goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (1:5000; Cat: 926-68071). Incubations were performed in blocking buffer and protected from light. The protein bands were visualized using the Odyssey Fluorescence Imaging System (LI-COR Biosciences, Lincoln, NE, USA), and quantification was performed using Image Studio Software (LI-COR, version 5.2).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were repeated at least three times independently. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance between groups was assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Graphs were generated with GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software; RRID: SCR_002798).

Results

To obtain potential targets of puerarin, we compiled candidates from public resources: D3CARP, OncoPubMiner, PharmMapper, similarity ensemble approach (SEA), STITCH, SuperPred, and SwissTargetPrediction (Tables S1–S7). After normalization to UniProt entries and removal of duplicates, 700 non-redundant targets remained (Table S8).

To explore mechanism, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment using the candidate set. GO biological processes highlighted immune regulation, receptor-mediated signaling, and phagocytosis; GO molecular functions were enriched for ligand–receptor interaction and cell-surface receptor binding (Fig. 1C). KEGG pathways prioritized phagosome maturation, cytokine–receptor interaction, and cell-adhesion molecules (Fig. 1D).

We next related puerarin-associated target scores to immune-cell signatures and soluble mediators. Myeloid lineages—macrophages, monocytes, dendritic cells, and neutrophils—showed the highest correlations (Fig. 1E). We also observed broad correlations between the puerarin target score and multiple cytokines (Fig. 1F). These results suggest that puerarin may influence innate-immune polarization, antigen presentation, and inflammatory signaling in melanoma.

Identification of cell and tissue specificity of puerarin by scRNA-seq

Recent scRNA-seq advances allow high-resolution profiling of the tumor microenvironment (TME). We collected datasets from published studies, including 2 blood samples, 14 brain samples, 21 cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) samples, 2 lymph node (LN) samples, 8 skin metastasis samples, 7 primary acral melanoma (PAM) skin samples, and 3 primary cutaneous melanoma (PCM) skin samples. After strict quality control, 58,593 cells remained. Following batch-effect correction and unsupervised clustering, we identified seven major cell lineages—T cells, melanoma cells, natural killer (NK) cells, monocytes, B cells, endothelial cells, and fibroblasts—based on canonical markers (Fig. 2A). Cell-type proportions varied across tissues and across individual samples; in serial samples from the same patients, the TME also changed with therapy and disease stage (Figs. 2B-C). We show cell fractions for each dataset (Fig. 2D) and the top marker gene per lineage identified using Seurat’s FindAllMarkers (Fig. 2E).

Using six gene-set scoring methods, we mapped the puerarin target score onto the scRNA-seq data. As shown in Fig. 2F, the target score was lower in B cells and T cells and higher in other populations. Across sample types, scores were higher in PAM skin (Fig. 2G). These results indicate cell-type and tissue specificity of the puerarin-related signal.

Puerarin predominantly targets malignant cell-dominated spatial domains

Emerging data suggest that puerarin may act through inflammatory, oxidative-stress, and metabolic routes (Zhou et al. 2014, 2022). Reported receptor and pathway links include estrogen receptors, PPAR-γ, NF-κB, and MAPK (Gong et al. 2024; Yang et al. 2018; Wang et al. 2021), but context-specific actions remain unclear.

We downloaded six Visium slides from the CROST portal (VISDS000241, VISDS000242, VISDS000243, VISDS000244, VISDS000459, and VISDS000460). For each slide, we segmented tissue into tumor, stroma, and tumor–stroma interface regions based on gene-expression signatures (Figs. 3A–B and S1A–B). Considering the low-resolution properties of Visium (each spot contains multiple cells), we spatially clustered the CT samples using “BayesSpace” method and identified the cell proportions using “SpaCET” method, respectively. The two types of information were then integrated to categorize the spots into several cellular topics (spatial domains) with the help of “SpaTopic” method. As shown in Figs. 3C and S1C, 6 topics were identified each in VISDS000241, VISDS000243, VISDS000460, and VISDS000244, 5 topics in VISDS000242, and 7 topics in VISDS000459. These cellular topics effectively cover specific areas in the melanoma samples. We then projected potential targets of puerarin onto the spatial organization to examine the specific targeting domains of puerarin. Interestingly, we observed puerarin targeting specific domains in different samples (Topic1/3/5 in VISDS000241, Topic3/5 in VISDS000242, Topic3/5/6 in VISDS000243, Topic2/3/4/5 in VISDS000244, Topic1/5 in VISDS000459, and Topic4/5/6 in VISDS000460, Figs. 3D–G and S1D–E). By further dissecting the cellular composition of these specific domains, we note that malignant cells are a consistent and dominant member of them (Figs. 3H–K, S1F–G, and S2). Importantly, these specific domains were clustered together in each sample, suggesting that the spatial domains targeted by puerarin have similar transcriptional characteristics.

We next analyzed spatial proximity using neighborhood enrichment. Domains with high puerarin scores and malignant-cell predominance were spatially adjacent (Figs. 3L–M and S3), supporting the idea that puerarin may act on malignant cell-dominated domains with common features.

COLLAGEN, FN1, and LAMININ signaling pathways may be critical downstream targets of puerarin

Since different domains may represent different functional zones within the TME and form consistent spatial organizations, exploring the communication information among these domains will help to understand the overall or local regulation of the TME by the puerarin. We therefore inferred communication probability among or within domains by “CellChat” method. Surprisingly, we observed that the domains targeted by puerarin not only exhibit frequent communication within their respective domains but also potential strong interactions with other domains (Figs. 4A–D and S4A–B). On this basis, we further identified domain-specific signaling pathways, including intra-spatial domain communication and inter-spatial domain communication. Importantly, among all signaling exchanges associated with the puerarin-targeting domains, COLLAGEN, FN1, and LAMININ signaling received our attention because of the significant communication probabilities (both intra- and inter-domain) (Figs. 4E–F and S4C–D). Figures 4G–J and S4E–F illustrates the spatial flow of the three signals. In order to clarify the key role of these signaling in the communication network associated with puerarin-targeting domains, the network centrality scores for each domain were further calculated, and it was found that the puerarin-targeting domains were consistently the main senders and influencers of COLLAGEN, FN1, and LAMININ signaling in the 6 melanoma samples, especially VISDS000241, VISDS000242, VISDS000243, and VISDS000460 (Figs. 4K–N and S4G–H). This result implies a potential link between these 3 signaling and puerarin intervention.

Based on the three ECM-related signals, we looked for ligands or receptors shared across these pathways to identify candidate targets of puerarin. We used CellChat to score each ligand–receptor pair. CD44 and syndecan-1 (SDC1) were common co-receptors across all three pathways (Fig. S5). Prior studies link CD44 and SDC1 to immune control in tumors. For CD44, a collagen-CD44 axis marks an immune-suppressed, poor-prognosis subtype in gastric cancer (Yang et al. 2025); CD44+ cells in head and neck cancer show higher PD-L1 and reduce CD8+ T cell responses; and in glioblastoma (Zottel et al. 2025), CD44 overexpression associates with Treg infiltration and PD-L1 (Kong et al. 2020). For SDC1, single-cell analyses report collagen-SDC1 ligand-receptor pairs in immunosuppressive TME subtypes (Wei et al. 2023). We then examined their spatial patterns. In our data, the B-cell exhaustion score increased with distance from CD44/SDC1-high domains (Fig. S6). These observations suggest that CD44/SDC1 may be related to immunosuppression.

CD44 emerges as a candidate mediator of puerarin response

We next compared docking of puerarin to CD44 and SDC1. Using AutoDock Vina, puerarin showed a more favorable score for CD44 (− 8.545 kcal/mol) than for SDC1 (Fig. 5A). To examine the docked complexes further, we performed normal-mode analysis (NMA) using iMODS. Deformability profiles for the puerarin–CD44 and puerarin–SDC1 complexes highlighted hinge regions and rigid segments (Fig. 5B). B-factor–like plots from NMA showed regional stiffness differences (Fig. 5C). Eigenvalues associated with normal modes provided quantitative metrics for conformational energy requirements, where lower values correlate with enhanced deformability. The global eigenvalue was lower for puerarin-CD44 (2.892399 × 10−10) than for puerarin-SDC1 (1.953336 × 10−8), consistent with greater ease of collective motion in the former (Fig. 5D). An inverse relationship was observed between eigenvalue magnitude and variance, with cumulative (green bars) and individual (purple bars) variances depicted in Fig. 5E. Covariance maps (Fig. 5F) further resolved residue-pair interactions: red, white, and blue tones denote correlated, uncorrelated, and anti-correlated motions, respectively. Elastic network models corroborated these findings, with dark gray springs symbolizing rigid atomic connections within the complexes (Fig. 5G). Collectively, these analyses elucidate the conformational plasticity and interaction specificity of puerarin with CD44.

CD44 derived from melanoma may be the main target of puerarin

Guided by the docking (Fig. 5) and the receptor screen (Figs. S5–6) that nominated CD44, we examined its cellular context in melanoma tissues. In spatial transcriptomics, CD44 and the puerarin target score colocalized in malignant regions (Figs. 6A–D and S7). In single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), CD44 was highest in melanoma cells from leptomeningeal metastases (LMM) and was higher after immunotherapy in the available samples (Fig. S8). CD44 was detectable in most lineages except endothelial cells and was high in primary acral melanoma (PAM) samples. CD44 expression tracked with the puerarin target score, most clearly in melanoma cells (Fig. 6E). In bulk datasets, CD44 correlated with melanoma-cell infiltration (Figs. 6F, S9). These results indicate that melanoma-cell CD44 is a plausible context for puerarin; the evidence is associative.

Puerarin stabilizes CD44 in CETSA, consistent with direct binding

A CCK-8 assay was performed to assess the effect of puerarin on A375 and SK-MEL-28 cell viability. Cells were treated with 5–40 µM puerarin, and viability was measured at multiple time points. Puerarin significantly reduced cell proliferation in a dose- and time-dependent manner (Fig. 7A). Notably, 20 µM puerarin showed no significant effect at 24 h, and this condition was selected for subsequent mechanistic studies. Next, we studied cell-based thermal-shift experiments in vitro to verify the binding of puerarin and CD44. CETSA showed that puerarin (20 µM) increased the thermal stability of CD44 in A375 and SK-MEL-28 cells, consistent with direct binding (Fig. 7B). Western blot analysis revealed a dose-dependent decrease in CD44 protein levels after 24 h of puerarin treatment (Fig. 7C).

Puerarin induces apoptosis in melanoma cells

As a canonical stem-cell marker, CD44 has been linked to aggressive cancer traits, including invasive migration, therapy resistance, and EMT through interactions with the extracellular matrix (ECM) and pro-survival signaling (Hassn Mesrati et al. 2021). We performed GSEA on CD44-related genes and found significant associations with pigment granule localization, apoptosis, and DNA replication (Figs. 8A–C), which is consistent with prior reports. We then examined CD44 and apoptosis-related genes in melanoma cells and observed concordant expression patterns (Fig. 8D). In functional assays, TUNEL showed dose-dependent increases in apoptosis after puerarin treatment in A375 and SK-MEL-28 (Fig. 8E). Annexin V/propidium iodide (PI) flow cytometry confirmed a dose-dependent rise in early and late apoptosis at 20–40 µM, with dacarbazine (30 µM) as a positive control (Fig. 8F). WB assays further supported these findings: the cleaved/total ratios for caspase-3, caspase-7, and PARP increased with higher puerarin doses in both cell lines (Fig. 8G).

Discussion

Puerarin is a plant isoflavone with reported anti-proliferative and pro-apoptotic activity in several cancers, including colorectal and hepatocellular carcinoma (Deng et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2017). we highlight a possible mechanism by which puerarin may preferentially act in malignant-cell–dominated spatial domains while engaging ECM signaling. Notably, puerarin’s ability to target spatially defined malignant cell domains, rather than uniformly affecting all cellular subsets, represents a critical advancement. This spatial specificity may correlate with its reported inhibition of melanin deposition, a hallmark of melanoma progression, suggesting a dual mechanism of action: direct cytotoxicity and disruption of melanogenic pathways. Further investigation into the interplay between these mechanisms could elucidate synergistic therapeutic strategies.

We integrated single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) with spatial transcriptomics to map where the puerarin-related signal occurs in the tumor microenvironment (TME). scRNA-seq resolved major lineages and cell states. Spatial maps linked malignant regions to stromal context and extracellular-matrix (ECM) programs. Domain-level communication analysis highlighted collagen, fibronectin (FN1), and laminin routes, both within and across domains. These observations are consistent with reports that ECM remodeling and cell–matrix signaling can support therapeutic resistance and spread (Najafi et al. 2019).

Our study identifies CD44 as a potential receptor mediating puerarin’s intervention in melanoma, a hypothesis supported by spatial domain analysis, molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations, and in vitro experiments. CD44, a transmembrane glycoprotein overexpressed in aggressive melanoma subtypes, has been implicated in tumor-initiating cell maintenance, metastatic dissemination, and immunosuppressive TME remodeling (Weitzenböck et al. 2022). Recent studies have further highlighted its role in regulating hyaluronan-mediated signaling and PI3K/AKT pathway activation, both of which are critical for melanoma survival (Pasonen-Seppänen et al. 2012). In our data, CD44-related programs aligned with pigment-granule biology, apoptosis, and DNA replication, and CD44 tracked with apoptosis-associated genes. The spatial co-localization of CD44 with puerarin-responsive domains suggests a functional link, though the evidence is associative rather than causal. This interpretation is consistent with prior reports that CD44 contributes to drug resistance (Xie et al. 2008; Toole and Slomiany 2008), where its blockade enhances sensitivity to targeted therapies.

Despite these insights, several limitations warrant consideration. First, while our in-silico models and preliminary in vitro experiments provide compelling evidence for CD44-mediated mechanisms, in vitro validation using CRISPR-edited melanoma cell lines and in vivo xenograft models are essential to confirm causality. Second, the study’s focus on spatial domains precludes a comprehensive analysis of temporal dynamics, which may influence puerarin’s efficacy. Third, the lack of patient-derived organoid (PDO) data limits translational relevance, as PDOs better recapitulate intratumoral heterogeneity compared to traditional cell lines. Future studies should prioritize longitudinal spatial profiling and integration with clinical metadata to assess predictive biomarkers of response.

Conclusion

This study identifies puerarin as a promising therapeutic candidate for melanoma, demonstrating its ability to target malignant cell-enriched spatial domains and modulate CD44-mediated ECM signaling. While in vivo validation and clinical translation remain pending, our findings highlight puerarin’s precision oncology potential, particularly in CD44-overexpressing melanoma. Future work should focus on optimizing bioavailability and evaluating combinatorial regimens to enhance therapeutic efficacy.
