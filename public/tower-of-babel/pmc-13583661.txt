Use, Modality, and Reimbursement Patterns of Outpatient Psychotherapy Among Children With Commercial Insurance
Authors: Paul R. Shafer, Alex K. Gertner, Monica S. Aswani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This cross-sectional study examines trends in the use, modality, and reimbursement of outpatient psychotherapy among commercially insured children in the US from 2016 to 2023.

Introduction

Among commercially insured children in the US, who constitute more than half of those younger than 18 years,1 incidence and prevalence of documented mental health conditions rose by 29.4% and 34.6%, respectively, from 2012 to 2018.2 Yet during the 4 years preceding the COVID-19 pandemic, only 10.1% of children aged 3 to 17 years received counseling or other treatment.3 Most children receiving services do so in school or outpatient settings; however, only half with a treatable condition receive treatment.4 A contributing factor may be low reimbursement and resulting lack of insurance acceptance.5

The COVID-19 pandemic had a multifactorial influence on child mental health and access to care, with numerous federal and state policy changes that loosened restrictions on telehealth.6,7,8 Existing studies of the pandemic’s influence on pediatric mental health care use have focused principally on changes in emergency department visits, hospitalizations, and psychotropic medication prescriptions. Our objective is to describe trends in use, modality, and reimbursement of outpatient psychotherapy among commercially insured children before, during, and emerging from the COVID-19 pandemic.

Methods

For this cross-sectional study, we used the Merative MarketScan Commercial Database for 2016 through 2023, restricting our sample to children aged 0 to 17 years and including outpatient claims for Current Procedural Technology (CPT) codes 90832 (individual psychotherapy, 30 minutes), 90834 (individual psychotherapy, 45 minutes), 90837 (individual psychotherapy, 60 minutes), 90839 (crisis psychotherapy, first hour), 90840 (crisis psychotherapy, add-on for additional 30 minutes), 90846 (family psychotherapy, without the patient present), and 90847 (family psychotherapy, with the patient present). Additional details about the analytic sample are provided in the eMethods, eTables 1 and 2, and eFigure in Supplement 1.

We developed several outcomes to describe trends in use, modality, and reimbursement for outpatient psychotherapy among children. For outpatient psychotherapy use, we estimated a rate of any use and number of visits per 1000 children enrolled per state-year as well as composition by CPT code, telehealth, and in-network vs out-of-network visits. For reimbursement, we calculated the mean total payment per state-year-CPT-code and inflated to 2023 dollars using the Consumer Price Index for Medical Care. We assessed variation in means of all measures nationally over time and in any use across states in 2023.

Our study follows the STROBE guideline and was deemed exempt by the Boston University Medical Campus IRB because of our use of deidentified secondary data. Analyses were conducted from November 2024 through July 2026 using SAS, version 9.4 (SAS Institute) and Stata/MP, version 19 (StataCorp).

Results

Our analytic sample included 1 219 151 unique children (1 870 426 person-years) enrolled in commercial health insurance nationwide, capturing 17 161 192 outpatient psychotherapy visits. They were largely older, with ages 0 to 5, 6 to 12, and 13 to 17 years constituting 4.3%, 40.1%, and 55.6% of person-years, respectively. Girls constituted 55.0% of person-years, and boys 45.0%; 76.3% lived in a metropolitan statistical area; and 50.9% were in a preferred provider organization plan.

Any use per 1000 children enrolled increased by approximately 44% nationally from 2016 (41.8 [95% CI, 38.1-45.5] per 1000) to 2023 (60.0 [95% CI, 54.7-65.3] per 1000), while visits per 1000 grew by approximately 72% (from 367.8 [95% CI, 324.6-411.0] per 1000 in 2016 to 634.0 [95% CI, 560.7-707.3] per 1000 in 2023) (Table). Any use per 1000 varied across states in 2023, ranging from 28.4 in Florida to 109.1 in Rhode Island (Figure). The highest concentrations of any use were in the Midwest and New England. The percentage of telehealth visits rose from 0% in 2019 to a peak of 30.8% (95% CI, 30.7%-30.8%) in 2021 before falling to 20.5% (95% CI, 20.4%-20.5%) in 2023. The percentage of out-of-network visits fell from approximately 20% in 2016 through 2018 to 15.3% (95% CI, 15.3%-15.4%) by 2023, coinciding with growth in use. Use of individual (vs family) CPT codes increased during the study period, and there was a notable shift toward 60-minute individual visits (90837), from 38.3% (95% CI, 38.2%-38.4%) in 2016 to 63.0% (95% CI, 62.9%-63.0%) in 2023. Reimbursement increased for in-network visits during the study period for all codes but was mixed for out-of-network visits.

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study of a national sample of health insurance claims, we observed substantial increases in use and intensity of outpatient psychotherapy service among commercially insured children in the US. Coinciding with the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, we found large increases in the share of telehealth that peaked in 2021 and still constituted about 1 in 5 visits in 2023. Most of the growth in use appeared to be from in-network services. We also saw a compositional shift toward longer visits and individual psychotherapy alongside increases in reimbursement.

Limitations include the inability to observe psychotherapy delivered at school and paid for outside of insurance, delivered during evaluation and management visits, or delivered through nonparticipating and/or online subscription services. MarketScan is not a census or random sample of health plans, so results may not generalize to those derived from other data sources. Our telehealth indicator is derived from place of service codes reported on claims, which may be subject to measurement error due to misreporting.

Substantial variation in outpatient psychotherapy use and reimbursement across states, visit types, and network status suggests that access to pediatric mental health services remains uneven. Our study complements existing research examining trends in use and spending within specific pediatric mental health conditions.9
