A fluorescent counterstaining technique to differentiate microplastics from chitin using Nile red and calcofluor white in edible grasshoppers
Authors: Erika Tapia-Bahena, Gastón Contreras-Jiménez, Daniel Tapia-Maruri, Alex Córdoba-Aguilar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Microplastics (MPs) are emerging contaminants of increasing environmental concern, and their detection in terrestrial organisms is essential for evaluating their impact, exposure and dynamics within ecosystems. Although insects represent a biologically relevant group for environmental monitoring and for estimating potential trophic transfer routes to humans, no standardized method currently exists for identifying MPs in these organisms. We have developed a detection protocol using edible grasshoppers (Sphenarium purpurascens) as study subjects. This combines Nile red (NR) and calcofluor white (CW) staining to distinguish added MPs from residual biological material (chitin). When NR was applied at 0.01 mg/mL, it successfully stained high-density polyethylene (HDPE), acrylonitrile butadiene styrene (ABS), expanded polystyrene (EPS), and polypropylene (PP), exhibiting polymer-dependent green or red fluorescence. A low NR concentration (0.01 mg/mL) was sufficient to ensure reliable visibility of the added MPs, while CW selectively stained chitin, emitting blue fluorescence under ultraviolet light. Counterstaining with both dyes provided a clear separation between MPs and biological structures, minimizing false positives. Furthermore, infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) confirmed the chemical composition of the added MPs (PP, EPS, ABS), complementing the limitations of NR staining. Thus, our NR-CW counterstaining protocol proved effective even for particles < 50 μm, and is cost-effective, simple to implement, and suitable for environmental studies involving organisms with chitin structures. Introduction

Plastics comprise a wide range of products, primarily derived from synthetic polymeric materials such as polypropylene, polyvinyl chloride, polycarbonate, polystyrene, acrylonitrile butadiene styrene, and polyethylene, among others. These materials share several key characteristics that render them indispensable in our daily life (Batool & Qadir, 2022). Moreover, these plastics, undergo a gradual degradation (Erni-Cassola et al., 2017) that results in particles smaller than 5 mm in diameter, commonly referred to as microplastics (MPs; Eerkes-Medrano et al., 2015; Zhu et al., 2023. This degradation and plastic components pose a significant environmental threat due to their toxicity and persistence in natural ecosystems (Muhammad et al., 2021). For example, in terrestrial ecosystems, MPs can be ingested by a plethora of animals (Thrift et al., 2025), potentially leading to harmful effects (Li et al., 2024). One such case is that of insects whereby different studies have shown MPs present in different orders (e.g., Odonata, Diptera, Coleoptera; Lin et al., 2021; Maneechan & On Prommi, 2022; Zhu et al., 2023).

Despite studies of MPs presence in insects, there is no standardized and universally accepted method for detecting MPs in these animals. The choice of method depends on the type, quantity, and environmental matrix in which MPs are embedded, making a single technique generally unsuitable for most cases (Goveas et al., 2023). Thus, researchers adapt and combine different detection and quantification techniques—beyond simple visual identification, which can be challenging and subjective due to the diversity in shapes, colors, and the small size of MPs (Michelaraki et al., 2020; Prata et al., 2019). Related to this, fluorescence microscopy is one method that enhances MPs visualization by using fluorescent dye staining. This approach offers a simple, rapid, and accessible alternative to more expensive and less widely available spectrophotometric techniques (Tarafdar et al., 2022). In this context, calcofluor white stain (CW) is a fluorescent mix composed by calcofluor white and Evans Blue. Calcofluor white binds to chitin and cellulose and emits blue fluorescence under UV light exposure (excitation: 350–400 nm; emission: 450–500 nm), unlike Blue Evans does not bind specific biological material but it was used to diminishes the background fluorescence from biological material autofluorescence. Nile Red (NR) is a lipophilic fluorescent dye that preferentially binds to lipids and plastics such as polyethylene and polypropylene, emitting green, red, or yellow fluorescence depending on the excitation wavelength (Helmberger et al., 2020). NR has shown a staining efficiency of up to 80% for MPs, highlighting its value as a tool for their detection and identification (Ho et al., 2024).

However, NR can also stain biological materials. Therefore, an important step prior to sample staining is the removal of organic matter. It has been reported that staining natural samples with NR may generate false positives, making it necessary to distinguish between biological material and plastics particles (Villegas-Velázquez et al., 2025). This issue is particularly relevant for structures such as chitin, whose separation can be challenging due to its resistance to chemical digestion protocols used during the extraction of MPs from biological samples, which may lead to its misidentification as polymeric particles (Kallenbach et al., 2021).

The combined use of these two dyes, known as counterstaining, may allow for the identification of MPs in arthropods by clearly distinguishing between biological material and plastic particles reducing false positives (Helmberger et al., 2020). Here we show a protocol combining NR and CW stain to distinguish MPs from residual biological material (chitin), using the grasshopper Sphenarium purpurascens Charpentier (Orthoptera: Pyrgomorphidae). This species is widely consumed in Mexico: it is a generalist herbivorous insect that feeds on more than 20 plant species, including agriculturally important crops such as alfalfa, amaranth, and beans. In addition, S. purpurascens is distributed across central and southern Mexico, including Mexico City, State of Mexico, Tlaxcala, Oaxaca, and Puebla (Castellanos-Vargas & Cano-Santana, 2009). This feeding behavior, together with its broad distribution, high seasonal abundance, and direct interaction with plant surfaces, makes this species susceptible to ingesting plastic particles deposited on vegetation. Therefore, this species is suitable for monitoring MPs contamination due to its ecological characteristics. Finally, its importance in the human diet and its socioeconomic value confer significant relevance from a food safety perspective, which justifies the need to evaluate the presence of MPs in insects intended for human consumption.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

Edible specimens of S. purpurascens (collected in the locality of Huejotzingo, Puebla) were purchased at “Mercado de la Merced” in Mexico City between January and February 2024.

Sample preparation: lipid extraction

Since edible S. purpurascens are typically fried for sale and NR is known to stain lipids (Ho et al., 2024; Shim et al., 2016), a lipid extraction step was performed prior to staining. This step was necessary because residual lipids can act as contaminants and interfere with MPs detection (Ho et al., 2024). Grasshopper samples were first dehydrated in an oven at a constant weight at 50 °C for three days. The dried samples were then immersed in an anhydrous methanol solution (99.8% v/v). The mixture was agitated for 1 min and left to rest at room temperature (24 °C) for 2.5 h. After this period, the methanol solution was discarded, and the grasshoppers were dried in an oven at 40 °C for 8 h (Supplementary material Fig. S1).

Chemical digestion and filtration

The dried grasshopper samples were transferred to a porcelain mortar and finely to facilitate chemical digestion. The ground biomass was placed into amber glass jars containing 30 mL of 30% hydrogen peroxide (v/v) and incubated at 60 °C for 48 h. Hydrogen peroxide is an oxidizing agent commonly used to digest organic matter in biological samples (Ruggero et al., 2020). After digestion, the samples were diluted with ultrapure water (Milli-Q) and allowed to cool to room temperature (24 °C).

The digested biomass was then filtered using Milli-Q water through glass microfiber filters (Whatman, pore size 1 μm; 55 mm diameter), placed in clean glass Petri dishes.

Staining of MPs with NR

To determine the minimum concentration to distinguish plastic polymers from chitin via fluorescence, two NR solutions (N1142, Invitrogen) were prepared by dissolving the dye in anhydrous methanol (99.8% v/v) at concentrations of 0.01 mg/mL and 1 mg/mL. Both solutions were stored in amber glass vials at 4 °C. Four types of commercial white plastic polymers were used: high-density polyethylene (HDPE; granules, 34–50 μm, Sigma-Aldrich), acrylonitrile butadiene styrene (ABS; ∼1 mm fragments obtained from a commercial white ABS tube), expanded polystyrene (EPS; ∼1 mm microplastic fragments obtained from a commercial white polystyrene container), and polypropylene (PP; ∼1 mm fragments cut from transparent granules made of this polymer, INDELPO, Profax). MPs fragments were obtained by cutting plastic blocks with a sharp scalpel.

The polymeric composition of the commercial plastics used in this study was confirmed using Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FT-IR; IRAffinity-1, Shimadzu) in attenuated total reflectance (ATR) mode. Spectral data were acquired in the range of 4000–600 cm⁻1 with a resolution of 4 cm⁻1 and 60 scans per measurement. The spectra of the analyzed particles were compared against a spectral reference database. Particles with spectral match scores above 80% were classified as MPs (Supplementary material Figs. S2 and S3). These specific polymers were selected because they are widely used in manufacturing and are, therefore, among the most widely found in the environment (Erni-Cassola et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2021).

For NR staining, each plastic polymer was placed separately onto glass microfiber filters (Whatman). Specifically, both 0.01 mg/mL and 1 mg/mL NR solutions were applied to HDPE, while three or four drops of the 0.01 mg/mL NR solution were applied to ABS, EPS, and PP until their surface was completely covered. Each filter was placed inside a glass Petri dish, sealed with aluminum foil to prevent contamination, and incubated at 60 °C for 30 min in complete darkness.

Following incubation, the samples were rinsed with anhydrous methanol (99.8% v/v). The stained samples were then observed under a fluorescence microscope.

Counterstaining

CW was used to stain chitin and NR for MPs. This method relies on the ability of these dyes to selectively bind to their respective targets, enabling more accurate identification under fluorescence microscopy. In this stage, the filtered biomass (chitin) was mixed with each type of plastic polymer (HDPE, ABS, EPS, and PP) for subsequent analysis.

A sufficient volume of CW (1.0 g/L CW + 0.5 g/L Evans Blue, Sigma-Aldrich) was pipetted onto each microfiber glass filter containing the sample (chitin + MPs), ensuring full coverage of the surface. One drop of KOH (10%) was added, and the mixture was incubated for 10 min. Samples were then rinsed with Milli-Q water and allowed to dry completely at 24 °C before applying NR.

The NR solution was prepared by dissolving NR (N1142, Invitrogen) in anhydrous methanol (99.8% v/v) at a concentration of 0.01 mg/mL. The solution was stored in an amber glass vial at 4 °C. Then, three or four drops of the NR solution were applied to fully cover the surface of each sample on the glass microfiber filter. Filters were placed inside closed glass Petri dishes covered with aluminum foil to avoid contamination. Samples were incubated for 30 min at 60 °C in total darkness. After incubation, the samples were rinsed with anhydrous methanol (99.8% v/v). Note that during CW staining, filters were placed on a paper towel to reduce drying time.

Epifluorescence microscopy

Samples were mounted on glass microscope slides and covered with coverslips to prevent sample drift during observation. We initially assessed the autofluorescence of exoskeletal fragments (chitin) and then examined the fluorescence signal after staining only with CW stain. The fluorescence of HDPE staining, exclusively with NR, was also evaluated at two concentrations (0.01 mg/mL and 1 mg/mL), with the aim of assessing its efficiency and reducing the costs associated with NR. The fluorescence was determined by testing ABS, EPS, and PP staining at 0.01 mg/mL and HDPE staining at 1 mg/mL.

Microscopic images were acquired using an adapted Nikon AZ100M Multizoom Microscope (Macroview 0.5X to 40X; vertical stage; Jenoptik ProgRes c14 Plus CMOS color camera) and an inverted Nikon Eclipse Ti Microscope (motorized stage; ImagingSource DFK0423 CMOS color camera). We then analyzed the fluorescence of double staining with CW stain and NR (0.01 mg/mL) in samples where plastic polymers were mixed with chitin. Fluorescence filter cubes were employed throughout the fluorescence imaging: using three excitation/emission wavelength ranges: ultraviolet (excitation 350–400 nm, emission 450–500 nm), blue light (excitation 450–510 nm, emission 520–560 nm), and green light (excitation 520–565 nm, emission 580–650 nm). Mixed samples (chitin and MPs) were observed through the Nikon Eclipse Ti microscope. Upon identifying particles morphologically like the added MPs, we sequentially examined them under blue, green, and red emission fluorescence channels. The excitation sources (UV, blue, and green) were toggled to differentiate between particles stained only with CW stain, only with NR, or simultaneously using both dyes. Images were captured for further analysis of autofluorescence and specific fluorescence labeling. Note that since the aim of this study was to develop a qualitative fluorescence microscopy protocol to differentiate MPs from chitin, fluorescence intensity was not measured quantitatively. However, imaging parameters (exposure time and gain) were kept constant to ensure consistency across observations, but intensity values were not used to make quantitative comparisons.

Results and discussion

Fragments present in the filtered biomass displayed strong green autofluorescence under blue light (excitation: 450–510 nm, emission: 520–560 nm), and faint blue fluorescence under UV light excitation (excitation: 350–400 nm, emission: 450–500 nm) (Supplementary material Fig. S5), prior to CW application. These findings suggest that CW staining may be technically unnecessary for visualizing chitin in such samples, given its inherent autofluorescence under UV exposure. However, the low intensity of the blue autofluorescence signal from chitin limits the accuracy of particle identification. In this context, CW stain application becomes highly valuable for clear and reliable discrimination of biological material, as will be discussed below.

Upon applying CW stain to the biological material from the filtered biomass, the dye successfully stained the chitin entirely (Supplementary material Fig. S6). These fragments exhibited intense blue fluorescence under UV light (excitation: 350–400 nm, emission: 450–500 nm; see Supplementary material Fig. S6), in contrast to the weaker autofluorescence observed in the unstained sample (Supplementary material Fig. S5). These findings are consistent with those by Helmberger et al. (2020), who also documented the staining of insect exoskeleton using CW.

NR (9-diethylamino-5-benzo[a]phenoxazinone) is a lipophilic dye that has been widely and successfully used for the visualization and quantification of MPs through fluorescence microscopy (Wang et al., 2021). In our study, the application of NR at a concentration of 1 mg/mL induced strong green fluorescence in HDPE particles under blue light (excitation: 450–510 nm, emission: 520–560 nm; Supplementary material Fig. S7), whereas a weaker fluorescence response was observed at the lower concentration of 0.01 mg/mL. However, the concentration of 0.01 mg/mL still provided adequate visibility for observing the MPs.

We showed that the method of staining polymers with NR proved useful for visualizing and detecting MPs, as its application caused all plastic polymers (HDPE, ABS, EPS, and PP) to fluoresce. For example, NR-stained plastic particles were visible in different fluorescence colors. In this case, HDPE emitted intense green fluorescence (excitation: 450–510 nm, emission: 520–560 nm) and no detectable red fluorescence (excitation: 520–565 nm, emission: 580–650 nm; Fig. 1). In contrast, EPS showed intense green fluorescence and weak red fluorescence under the same excitation and emission conditions, whereas ABS displayed strong red fluorescence and weak green fluorescence. These results validate previous findings (Erni-Cassola et al., 2017; Helmberger et al., 2020; Shim et al., 2016) and demonstrate the usefulness of NR for staining and detecting various plastics. However, further testing is needed for plastic polymers with colors other than white. For example, in our study, PP exhibited weak green and red fluorescence, probably due to the transparent color of the polymer. These variations in fluorescence intensity and emission color may be associated with differences in the surface hydrophobicity of MPs, which can be influenced by factors such as environmental weathering of polymers, the presence of pigments or additives, particle morphology, and the excitation wavelength used (Prata et al., 2019; Shim et al., 2016; Shruti et al., 2022). No fluorescence was detected under UV light exposure (excitation: 350–400 nm, emission: 450–500 nm), so this wavelength was excluded from subsequent experiments. Overall, green emission fluorescence was best for detecting HDPE, EPS, and PP, while red emission fluorescence worked best for ABS. Both emissions were used for image analysis during counterstaining.

Furthermore, NR also allowed visualization of small-sized plastic particles, making it a very useful tool for identifying white MPs (HDPE, ABS, and EPS) and transparent MPs (i.e. PP), which may go unnoticed to the naked eye, especially MPs smaller than 1 mm (EPS, ABS, and PP), or even those in the micrometer range (HDPE < 50 μm) (Erni-Cassola et al., 2017; Shim et al., 2016).

NR staining was detected by emitting green fluorescence (excitation: 450–510 nm; emission: 520–560 nm) and red fluorescence (excitation: 520–565 nm; emission: 580–650 nm). Meanwhile, CW staining was detected by emitting blue fluorescence (excitation: 350–400 nm; emission: 450–500 nm). When observing mixed samples (MPs + chitin) through the Nikon Eclipse Ti microscope, simultaneous detection of CW and NR fluorescence (0.01 mg/mL) was not possible using the same filter cube. Therefore, when particles like the added MPs (HDPE, ABS, EPS, and PP) were found, fluorescence was shifted from green to blue.

Green fluorescence was chosen for HDPE, EPS, and PP, as they were more easily detected under this channel. For ABS, red fluorescence was used. In this context, particles emitting intense blue fluorescence under UV light were ruled out as biological material (chitin). Additionally, images captured in green, red (MPs) and blue (chitin) fluorescence were deconvolved and merged (clarify.ai module and Merge function) using Nikon NIS-Elements Advanced Research v6.4 software to visualize both fluorescence signals simultaneously. This was necessary because chitin exhibited strong fluorescence under blue light (excitation: 450–510 nm, emission: 520–560 nm), which overlaps with NR emission (Supplementary material Fig. S8), as demonstrated in previous studies (Erni-Cassola et al., 2017; Helmberger et al., 2020). This indicates that NR can stain and induce fluorescence in biological material, even after chemical digestion with hydrogen peroxide (Supplementary material Fig. S8) (Erni-Cassola et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2021). Thus, the use of CW stain is useful for distinguishing and discarding biological material during MPs detection, as it quenches the fluorescence of chitin induced by NR and helps to avoid false positives (Helmberger et al., 2020).

In counterstaining, the NR concentration of 0.01 mg/mL was sufficient to distinguish between chitin and MPs, even when MPs were underneath chitin fragments stained with CW stain (Fig. 2). This result is satisfactory as CW binds to chitin which reduces false positives caused by NR (Michelaraki et al., 2020). Therefore, fluorescence microscopy with dyes such as CW and NR enables the differentiation of MPs from chitin, even when removal of organic matter is incomplete or not feasible (Shruti et al., 2022). Note, that CW stain does not stain the four tested plastic polymers (HDPE, ABS, EPS, and PP), reducing the risk of misidentifying them as natural occurring material. In all four samples (HDPE, PP, ABS, and EPS), particles were selectively stained with NR on the glass microfiber filters, enabling clear visualization of plastics even after CW staining and among numerous chitin fragments that remained after chemical digestion with H₂O₂ in field samples (Shim et al., 2016) (Fig. 2).

Our study supports the findings of Helmberger et al., (2020), in which counterstaining resulted in blue fluorescence for chitin and green or red fluorescence for virgin MPs (HDPE and PP) and synthetic polymer products (EPS and ABS; see Fig. 2), allowing small MPs identification from chitin based on their fluorescence (Erni-Cassola et al., 2017; Prata et al., 2019). In samples lacking MPs, only CW-stained biological material would be expected to be observed, while no particles exhibiting the characteristic NR fluorescence associated with polymeric materials would be detected. Therefore, this approach reduces the likelihood of false positives.

Finally, we were able to determine the chemical composition of added particles (PP, EPS, ABS) using FTIR spectroscopy operated in ATR mode after counterstaining. This approach compensates the limitation given by NR staining (Supplementary material Fig. S4). However, detecting white plastic particles (< 50 μm), such as HDPE, using FTIR after counterstaining proved difficult due to the challenge of detecting and carefully separating tiny particles that blend into the white Whatman glass microfiber filter. Also, a complete separation of plastic particles from the biological matrix (chitin) is necessary, as chitin can produce signals and mask the weak FTIR signals of MPs (Ho et al., 2024).

Conclusion

Using edible S. purpurascens grasshoppers as study subjects, the application of CW onto the filtered biomass, fully stained the chitin, unlike what occurred to the unstained sample (autofluorescence). The concentration of 0.01 mg/mL provided good visibility for the four MPs types: HDPE, ABS, EPS, and PP. NR-stained plastic particles were visible in different fluorescence colors. In this case, HDPE and EPS emitted intense green fluorescence (excitation: 450–510 nm, emission: 520–560 nm), while ABS exhibited strong red fluorescence and weak green fluorescence. Counterstaining was also effective for MPs identification through epifluorescence microscopy. The use of CW helped to differentiate and discard chitin (which tends to remain after chemical digestion), while NR enabled marking and distinguishing MPs, avoiding false positives.

Therefore, the use of CW and NR dyes (0.01 mg/mL) proved to be a promising method, as it is reproducible, cost-effective (given the cost of consumables and compared to other techniques), accessible and less subjective for marking chitin and small MPs such as HDPE, PP, ABS, and EPS (34 μm ~ 1 mm), commonly found in manufactured products and plastic waste. For additional confirmation, this technique can be complemented with FTIR spectroscopy to determine the chemical composition of some plastic particles (Supplementary material Fig. S4).
