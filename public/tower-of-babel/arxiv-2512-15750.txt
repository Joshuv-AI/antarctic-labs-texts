Abstract
. Our paper focuses on investigating the existence and possible forms of solutions to the nonlinear differential equation f m + ( R ​ f ( k ) ) n = Q ​ e α , \displaystyle f^{m}+\big(Rf^{(k)}\big)^{n}=Qe^{\alpha}, where where k k , m m and n n are three positive integers, Q Q and R R are non-zero rational functions, and α \alpha is a polynomial. We examine this equation systematically for all positive integral values of k k , m m and n n , providing comprehensive conditions under which entire or meromorphic solutions may exist. Our results offer substantial improvements over those of Tang and Liao [ 16 ] and Han and Lü [ 7 ] , particularly with respect to the existence criteria and the explicit analytic forms of admissible solutions. These findings contribute meaningfully to the broader study of nonlinear differential polynomials and Fermat-type functional equations in complex analysis.

1.
Introduction

It is known that, Fermat’s last theorem states that the equation
x^{n}+y^{n}=1
when
n\geq 3
does not admit nontrivial rational solutions (see
[
17
,
20
]
). The equation
x^{2}+y^{2}=1
does admit nontrivial rational solutions. The following functional equation

\displaystyle f^{n}(z)+g^{n}(z)=1,

(1.1)

where
n
is a positive integer can be regarded as the Fermat diophantine equations
x^{n}+y^{n}=1
over function fields.
In 1927, Montel
[
14
]
proved that the equation (
1.1
) has no transcendental entire solutions for
n\geq 3
.
Also in Theorem 4
[
5
]
, we see that the equation (
1.1
) actually has no non-constant entire solutions when
n>2
.
Gross
[
4
, Theorem 3]
proved that equation (
1.1
) has no non-constant meromorphic solutions when
n>3
.
For
n=2
, Gross
[
5
, Theorem 4]
found that the equation (
1.1
) has entire solutions of the from
f(z)=\sin(h(z))
and
g(z)=\cos(h(z))
, where
h(z)
is an entire function.
In Theorem 1, Gross
[
4
]
proved that all meromorphic solutions of the equation
f^{2}(z)+g^{2}(z)=1
are the form

f(z)=\frac{1-\alpha^{2}(z)}{1+\alpha^{2}(z)}\;\;\text{and}\;\;g(z)=\frac{2\alpha(z)}{1+\alpha^{2}(z)},

where
\alpha(z)
is a non-constant meromorphic function. For
n=3
, Baker
[
1
, Theorem 1]
proved that the only non-constant meromorphic solutions of equation (
1.1
) are the functions

f=\frac{1}{2}\left\{1+\frac{\wp^{(1)}(u)}{\sqrt{3}}\right\}/\wp(u)\;\;\text{and}\;\;g=\frac{\eta}{2}\left\{1-\frac{\wp^{(1)}(u)}{\sqrt{3}}\right\}/\wp(u)

for a non-constant entire function
u
and a cubic root
\eta
of unity, where
\wp
denotes the Weierstrass function satisfying
(\wp^{(1)})^{2}=4\wp^{3}-1
.

In 1970, Yang
[
22
]
investigated the following Fermat-type equation

\displaystyle f^{m}(z)+g^{n}(z)=1

(1.2)

and obtained that equation (
1.2
) has no non-constant entire solutions when
\frac{1}{m}+\frac{1}{n}<1
(see the proof of Theorem 1). Therefore it is clear that the equation (
1.2
) has no non-constant entire solutions when
m>2
and
n>2
. However, for the case when
m=n=2
and
g=f^{(1)}
, many authors investigated the existence of solutions of the equation (
1.2
). As a result, successively several research papers were published (see
[
3
,
7
,
10
,
11
,
15
,
16
,
18
,
19
,
24
]
).

It is an interesting and quite difficult question to prove that a complex nonlinear differential equation has no meromorphic solution or to find out all the meromorphic solutions of a complex nonlinear differential equation if such solutions exist, even for a simple differential equation. In 2004, Yang and Li
[
24
]
study for the meromorphic solutions of the following type of the differential equation

f^{2}+b(L(f))^{2}=a,

where
L(f)
is a linear differential polynomial in
f
,
a
and
b
are small meromorphic functions of
f
. Actually they obtained the following results.

Theorem A
.

[
24
, Theorem 1]
Let
n(\geq 1)
be an integer and let
a,b_{0},b_{1},\ldots,b_{n-1}
be polynomials and
b_{n}
be a non-zero constant. Let
L(f)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}b_{k}f^{(k)}
. If
a\not\equiv 0
, then a transcendental meromorphic solution of the following equation:

f^{2}+(L(f))^{2}=a,

must have the form
f(z)=\frac{1}{2}\left(P(z)e^{R(z)}+Q(z)e^{-R(z)}\right)
, where
P
,
Q
and
R
are polynomials and
PQ=a
. If, furthermore, all
b_{k}
are constants, then
\deg(P)+\deg(Q)\leq n-1
. Moreover,
R(z)=\lambda
is a non-zero constant, which satisfies the following equations:

\sideset{}{{}_{k=0}^{n}}{\sum}b_{k}\lambda^{k}=\frac{1}{\iota},\;\;\sideset{}{{}_{k=j}^{n}}{\sum}b_{k}{}^{k}C_{j}\lambda^{k-j}=0,\;j=1,\ldots,p,

\sideset{}{{}_{k=0}^{n}}{\sum}b_{k}(-\lambda)^{k}=-\frac{1}{\iota},\;\;\sideset{}{{}_{k=j}^{n}}{\sum}b_{k}{}^{k}C_{j}(-\lambda)^{k-j}=0,\;j=1,\ldots,q.

In Theorem 2
[
24
]
, Yang and Li also proved that the differential equation
f^{2}+b_{1}^{2}(f^{(k)})^{2}=a
has no transcendental meromorphic solution provided that
b_{1}(\neq 0)
is a constant and
a
is a non-constant meromorphic small function of
f
.

Theorem B
.

[
24
, Theorem 3]
Let
a_{1}
,
a_{2}
and
a_{3}
be non-zero meromorphic functions. Then a necessary condition for the differential equation

\displaystyle a_{1}f^{2}+a_{2}(f^{(1)})^{2}=a_{3}

(1.3)

to have a transcendental meromorphic solution satisfying
T(r,a_{k})=S(r,f)
,
k=1,2,3
, is
\frac{a_{1}}{a_{3}}\equiv\text{constant}
.

Eq. (
1.3
) may have some entire solutions, when
a_{1}/a_{3}
is constant. For example, the function
f(z)=\frac{1}{2}(e^{z^{2}/{2\iota}}+e^{-z^{2}/{2\iota}})
satisfies
z^{2}f^{2}(z)+(f^{(1)}(z))^{2}=z^{2}
.

In the same paper, Yang and Li
[
24
]
also posed the following conjecture.

Conjecture A
.

[
24
, Conjecture 1]
Let
P_{1}
,
P_{2}
and
P_{3}
be non-zero polynomials. Then the equation
P_{1}f^{2}+P_{2}(f^{(1)})^{2}=P_{3}
has no transcendental meromorphic solution when

is a non-zero constant and

is not the square of any rational function.

By considering the following example, Tang and Liao
[

]
ensured that Conjecture A is not true in general.

Example A
.

[
16
]
Let
P_{1}f^{2}+P_{2}(f^{(1)})^{2}=P_{3}
, where
P_{1}\equiv 1
,
P_{2}(z)=4z
and
P_{3}\equiv 1
. Clearly
P_{1}/P_{3}
is a non-zero constant and
P_{2}/P_{3}
is not the square of any rational function. But,
f(z)=\cos(\sqrt{z})
is a transcendental entire solution of
P_{1}f^{2}+P_{2}(f^{(1)})^{2}=P_{3}
.

[

,

,

.

[

It follows easily from Theorem C that if either

or

1.4
) has no transcendental meromorphic solution.

Secondly, Tang and Liao
[
16
]
studied the following differential equation

\displaystyle f^{2}+\frac{1}{P^{2}}(f^{(k)})^{2}=Q,

(1.5)

for the existence of transcendental meromorphic solutions, where

In fact they got the following result.

Theorem D
.

[

If the Eq. (

) has a transcendental meromorphic solution

, then

k
is an odd integer and

, then

A^{2}=C

.

In 2019, Han and Lü
[
7
]
considered the following differential equation

\displaystyle f^{n}(z)+(f^{(1)}(z))^{n}=e^{\alpha z+\beta},

(1.6)

where
n(\geq 1)
is an integer,
\alpha
and
\beta
are constants and derived the following result.

Theorem E
.

[
7
, Theorem 2.1]
The meromorphic solutions
f
of the Eq. (
1.6
) must be entire functions and the following assertions hold.

(A)

For
n=1
, the general solutions of (
1.6
) are
f(z)=\frac{e^{\alpha z+\beta}}{\alpha+1}+ae^{-z}
for
\alpha\neq-1
, and
f(z)=ze^{-(z+\beta)}+ae^{-z}
.

(B)

For
n=2
, either
\alpha=0
and the general solutions of (
1.6
) are
f(z)=e^{\frac{\beta}{2}}\sin(z+b)
, or
f(z)=de^{\frac{\alpha z+\beta}{2}}
.

(C)

For
n\geq 3
, the general solutions of (
1.6
) are
f(z)=de^{\frac{\alpha z+\beta}{n}}
.

Here

with
d^{n}\left(1+\frac{\alpha}{n}\right)^{n}=1
for

.

In this paper, we assume that the reader is familiar with standard symbols and fundamental results of Nevanlinna Theory (see
[
8
,
23
]
). Throughout the paper, we denote by
\mu(f)
and
\rho(f)
the lower order of
f
and the order of
f
respectively.
As usual, the abbreviation CM means “counting multiplicities”, while IM means “ignoring multiplicities”. Let
f
and
g
be two non-constant meromorphic functions and
a\in\mathbb{C}
. If
g-a=0
whenever
f-a=0
, we write
f=a\Rightarrow g=a
.

Let
R=\frac{P}{Q}(\not\equiv 0)
be a rational function, where
P
and
Q
are relatively prime polynomials. We define the degree of
R
as
\deg(R)=\deg(P)-\deg(Q)
. If
R\equiv 0
, then we define
\deg(R)=-\infty
. If
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
are two rational functions, then

(i)

\deg(R_{1}R_{2})=\deg(R_{1})+\deg(R_{2})
,

(ii)

\deg\left(\frac{R_{1}}{R_{2}}\right)=\deg(R_{1})-\deg(R_{2})
,

(iii)

\deg(R^{(1)})\leq\deg(R)-1
,

(iv)

\deg\left(\frac{R^{(1)}}{R}\right)\leq-1
,

(v)

\deg(R_{1}+R_{2})\leq\max\{\deg(R_{1}),\deg(R_{2})\}
.

2.
Main results

In this section, we consider the following more general differential equation

\displaystyle f^{m}+\big(Rf^{(k)}\big)^{n}=Qe^{\alpha},

(2.1)

where
k
,
m
and
n
are three positive integers,
Q
and
R
are non-zero rational functions and
\alpha
is a polynomial.

It is easy to verify that the Eq. (
2.1
) has no rational solution provided that
\alpha
is non-constant. If
m=n=1
, it is easy to verify that
f(z)=e^{z^{2}}
is a solution of
f(z)+R(z)f^{(k)}(z)=Q(z)e^{\alpha(z)}
, where
k=2
,
R(z)=z
,
Q(z)=1+2z+4z^{3}
and
\alpha(z)=z^{2}
. Therefore we study the Eq. (
2.1
) for the existence of solutions for the case when
m+n>2
.

The following question is natural to pose:

Question 2.1
.

What can be concluded about the existence of non-constant meromorphic solution
f
of the Eq. (
2.1
) when
m+n>2
?

Our first objective is to provide a complete solution to Question
2.1
. But, here we give a partial answer of Question
2.1
under some conditions.
Now we state our first result.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
R(\not\equiv 0)
and
Q(\not\equiv 0)
be a rational functions,
\alpha
be polynomials and let
k
,
m
and
n
be three positive integers such that
m\neq n
and
m+n>2
. Let
f
be any transcendental meromorphic function such that one of the following holds:

(i)

\rho(f)=+\infty
;

(ii)

\rho(f)\leq+\infty
, when
\deg(\alpha)=0
;

(iii)

\deg(\alpha)<\mu(f)
, when
\deg(\alpha)>0
.

Then
f
can not be a solution of the Eq. (
2.1
),

(A)

if either
m>n>1
or
n>m>1
;

(B)

if either
m>n=1
or
n>m=1
and
n\geq k+1
.

When
\alpha
is a constant, we obtain the following corollary from Theorem
2.1
.

Corollary 2.1
.

Let
R(\not\equiv 0)
and
Q(\not\equiv 0)
be a rational functions and let
k
,
m
and
n
be three positive integers such that
m\neq n
and
m+n>2
. If the following Eq.

\displaystyle f^{m}+\big(Rf^{(k)}\big)^{n}=Q,

(2.2)

satisfies one of the following conditions:

(i)

m>n>1
or
n>m>1
;

(ii)

m>n=1
or
n>m=1
and
n\geq k+1
,

then the Eq. (
2.2
) does not have any transcendental meromorphic solution.

If
m=n
, then the Eq. (
2.1
) reduces to

\displaystyle f^{m}+\big(Rf^{(k)}\big)^{m}=Qe^{\alpha},

(2.3)

where
k(\geq 1)
and
m(\geq 2)
are two integers,
Q
and
R
are non-zero rational functions and
\alpha
is a polynomial.
We now state our next result.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
Q(\not\equiv 0)
and
R(\not\equiv 0)
be rational functions,
\alpha
be a polynomial and let
k(\geq 1)
and
m(\geq 2)
be integers. Then the Eq. (
2.3
) has no meromorphic solution of infinite order.

According to Theorem
2.2
, every solutions of the Eq. (
2.3
) must be of finite order.

Our second objective is to explore all possible characterizations of solutions to the Fermat-type functional equation (
2.1
) depending on the nature of
m(\geq 2)
,
R
,
Q
and
\alpha
.

When
R(\not\equiv 0)
is a polynomial and
Q\equiv 1
, we obtain the following result which includes complete list of solutions of the Eq. (
2.3
) for different values of
m(\geq 2)
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
R(\not\equiv 0)
and
\alpha
be polynomials and let
k(\geq 1)
and
m(\geq 2)
be two integers.
The meromorphic solutions
f
of the following Eq.

\displaystyle f^{m}+\big(Rf^{(k)}\big)^{m}=e^{\alpha},

(2.4)

must be entire functions,
R
reduces to a non-zero constant and the following assertions hold.

(A)

For
m=2
, the general solutions of (
2.4
) are one of the following:

(A1)

f(z)=\frac{d^{2}-1}{2\iota d}e^{\frac{az+b}{2}}
, where
a(\neq 0),b,d(\neq 0)
are constants such that
d^{2}(R(a/2)^{k}-\iota)=R(a/2)^{k}+\iota
;

(A2)

f(z)=e^{\frac{a_{1}z+a_{2}}{2}}\sin(b_{1}z+b_{2})
, where
a_{1},a_{2},b_{1}(\neq 0),b_{2}
are constants such that
R\left(\frac{a_{1}}{2}+\iota b_{1}\right)^{k}=\iota
and
R\left(\frac{a_{1}}{2}-\iota b_{1}\right)^{k}=-\iota
. Furthermore if
a_{1}=0
, then
k
is odd;

(B)

For
m\geq 3
, the general solutions of (
2.4
) are
f(z)=ce^{\frac{az+b}{m}}
, where
a(\neq 0),b,c(\neq 0)
are constants such that
c^{m}\left(R^{m}(a/m)^{km}+1\right)=1
.

Remark 2.1
.

Obviously the scope of Theorem E is extended in Theorem
2.3
by considering a larger class of functional equation (
2.4
) including the case when
k=1
,
R\equiv 1
and
\alpha(z)=az+b
which leads to the specific type of functional equation (
1.6
). Consequently Theorem
2.3
improves Theorem E. Also Theorem

Next, we intend to explore all possible characterizations of solutions to the Eq. (
2.3
) when
Q(\not\equiv 0)
and
R(\not\equiv 0)
are rational functions. Consequently, we obtain the following result.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
Q(\not\equiv 0)
and
R(\not\equiv 0)
be rational functions,
\alpha
be a polynomial and let
k(\geq 1)
and
m(\geq 2)
be two integers. Then the transcendental meromorphic solution of the Eq. (
2.3
) are characterized as follows:

(A)

f=R_{1}e^{\alpha/m}
, where
\alpha
is non-constant and
R_{1}(\not\equiv 0)
is a rational function such that
mk\deg(\alpha^{(1)})=\deg(Q-R_{1}^{m})-m\deg(RR_{1})

R_{1}^{m}+R^{m}e^{-\alpha}\left((R_{1}e^{\alpha/m})^{(k)}\right)^{m}=Q
;

(B)

f=\frac{d^{2}Q_{1}-Q_{2}}{2\iota d}e^{\alpha/2}
,
where
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
are rational functions and
d(\neq 0)
is a constant such that
Q_{1}Q_{2}=Q
and
d^{2}Q_{1}-Q_{2}\not\equiv 0
and
\alpha
is non-constant;

(C)

f(z)=\frac{Q_{1}e^{a_{1}z+b_{1}}-Q_{2}e^{a_{2}z+b_{2}}}{2\iota}
,
where
R\equiv A(\text{constant})
such that
Aa_{1}^{k}=\iota
,
Aa_{2}^{k}=-\iota
,
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
are constants such that
Q_{1}Q_{2}=Q
,
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
are constants such that
\alpha(z)=(a_{1}+a_{2})z+b_{1}+b_{2}
. Furthermore if
\alpha
is constant, i.e., if
a_{1}+a_{2}=0
, then
k
is odd;

(D)

f(z)=\frac{Q_{1}(z)e^{a_{1}z+b_{1}}-Q_{2}(z)e^{a_{2}z+b_{2}}}{2\iota}
,
where
Q\equiv B(\text{constant})
,
Q_{1}
,
Q_{2}
and
R
are non-constant rational functions such that
Q_{1}Q_{2}=B
,
\deg(R)=0
and
a_{1}(\neq 0),a_{2}(\neq 0)
,
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
are constants such that
a_{1}^{k}+a_{2}^{k}=0
and
\alpha(z)=(a_{1}+a_{2})z+b_{1}+b_{2}
. Furthermore if
\alpha
is a constant, then
k
is odd;

(E)

f(z)=\frac{Q_{1}(z)e^{tP(z)+c}-Q_{2}(z)e^{P(z)}}{2\iota}
,
where
P
is a non-constant polynomial,
Q_{1}
,
Q_{2}
and
R
are non-constant rational functions such that
k\deg\left(P^{(1)}\right)=-\deg(R)
,
Q_{1}Q_{2}=Q
,
\deg(R)<0
,
c
and
t
are constants such that
t^{k}=-1
and
(t+1)P^{(1)}=\alpha^{(1)}
. If
\alpha
is a constant, then
t=-1
and
k
is odd.
Furthermore if all the zeros of

have multiplicities at most

, then
Q_{1}
,
Q_{2}
and

Q_{1}
,
Q_{2}
and

.

.

Let

,

(3.2)

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle F^{m}+G^{n}=Q,

8.
Application:

The Fermat-type complex differential equation

\displaystyle f^{m}+\big(Rf^{(k)}\big)^{n}=Qe^{\alpha},

play an important role in the theory of nonlinear differential polynomials and the growth analysis of entire and meromorphic functions. Such equations naturally occur in the study of value-distribution problems, where they help characterize the interaction between a function and its higher-order derivatives. They also arise in complex dynamical systems, particularly in describing exponential forcing and growth-controlled trajectories. In mathematical physics, these equations appear as complex analogues of nonlinear balance laws governing oscillatory and wave-type phenomena. Moreover, they have applications in the geometric theory of holomorphic curves and complex differential geometry, where differential constraints of Fermat type determine curvature and immersion properties. Understanding the existence or non-existence of entire solutions therefore yields meaningful insight into the analytic structure, uniqueness behaviour, and asymptotic properties of complex differential systems.
