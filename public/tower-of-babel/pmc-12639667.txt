Trunk muscle morphology and lumbopelvic stability in adolescent soccer players with unilateral lumbar spondylolysis: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Toshiharu Tsutsui, Wataru Sakamaki, Suguru Torii
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLumbar spondylolysis (LS) is a common cause of low back injury in adolescent athletes; however, little is known about the relationship between trunk muscle morphology, lumbopelvic control, and LS. This study aimed to clarify trunk muscle morphology and clinical test characteristics in adolescent male soccer players with unilateral lumbar spondylolysis (uni-LS).MethodsIn total, 19 of the 107 adolescent male soccer players were enrolled and categorized into a uni-LS group, based on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and an age-, height-, and weight-matched control group. MRI-derived cross-sectional areas (CSAs) of the L4/5 vertebrae were extracted, and the psoas major (PM), multifidus (MF), and erector spinae (ES) muscles were analyzed. Active straight leg raise (ASLR) and hip extension (HE) tests were performed, and lumbopelvic depression was evaluated. Two-way analysis of variance was applied to determine trunk muscle CSA differences between groups, and the two-sided Fisher’s exact test was employed to evaluate the association between uni-LS and clinical findings.ResultsA significant main effect [F (1,35) = 15.1, p < 0.001] and interaction effect [F (1,35) = 4.1, p = 0.047] were found in the PM, indicating a smaller PM on the LS side in the uni-LS group. A significant association was also observed between uni-LS and ASLR on the LS side, with 13 of 16 players (81.2%) showing positive ASLR corresponding to the uni-LS group (p = 0.003).ConclusionA smaller PM and lumbopelvic depression during ASLR on the LS side characterize adolescent soccer players with uni-LS. These findings may provide useful information to help clinicians understand potential characteristics of uni-LS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13102-025-01387-w.

Introduction

Lumbar spondylolysis (LS), characterized by a pars articular defect, is caused by repeated lumbar spine extension and rotation [1] and is common in many sports [2]. The prevalence of LS in athletes is 2–5 times higher than that in the general population, where it is approximately 6% [3, 4]. LS typically occurs during adolescence, with reported risk factors including immature lumbar vertebrae, sagittal lumbosacral misalignment, and limited lower limb flexibility [5]. Once affected, athletes may need to restrict sports activities for extended periods, including wearing a corset for 2–3 months [6], which can reduce performance during adolescence and beyond. Given that approximately 80% of high school and college-aged soccer players with lower back pain have a history of LS [7], effective rehabilitation and prevention strategies during adolescence are essential.

The primary approach to LS management is conservative treatment, aiming for bony union if pseudoarthrosis has not developed. Rehabilitation typically includes trunk exercises, brace use, and stretching, achieving an 80%–90% bone union rate in early-stage LS [8–10]. Kasamasu et al. [11] reported that early and progressive-stage LS treated with brace wearing and trunk exercises achieved a 98.9% return-to-sport rate (mean return time: 4.7 ± 1.9 months), whereas pain management and athletic rehabilitation in terminal-stage LS achieved a 97.6% return rate (mean return time: 1.7 ± 1.8 months). These findings suggest that rest, spinal immobilization, and trunk exercises that enhance lumbar stability are vital components of return-to-sport protocols.

However, no studies have detailed trunk muscle morphology and lumbar stability function in athletes with LS, leaving optimal muscle-targeted treatment and prevention strategies unclear.

Identified factors inhibiting bone union in LS include pathology at L5, advanced LS stage, and contralateral progression [10]. Tatsumura et al. [12] speculated that unilateral LS (uni-LS) may increase mechanical stress on the contralateral side. Therefore, understanding the physical and clinical characteristics of uni-LS may help prevent LS progression and recurrence. The present study aimed to clarify the morphology of trunk muscles (i.e., the psoas major, multifidus, and erector spinae) and the characteristics of clinical tests in adolescent athletes with uni-LS. We assumed, in an exploratory manner, that adolescent athletes with uni-LS would exhibit trunk muscle atrophy and poor lumbopelvic control on the uni-LS side compared with the typical unilateral adaptations seen in sports.

Methods

Participants

In total, 107 male soccer players from the same competitive club were enrolled in this study, which was the same academy setting as our previous work [5, 13]. Among the 107 players who underwent MRI, 19 players with uni-LS were identified. To ensure comparability, 19 controls were selected using 1:1 propensity score matching. Of the 19 participants in each group, 18 had their right leg as the preferred kicking side. Players trained 2–3 h per day/per session, five days a week, after school and on weekends. Inclusion criteria required participants to be 12–14 years old at baseline. Players who had not participated in soccer activities for more than 6 months were excluded to avoid including individuals with prolonged absence due to severe injury or other health conditions that could affect muscle morphology and lumbopelvic function.

All measurements were performed at our institution in May, during a period of regular training. In addition, all measurements were performed on non-training session and match days. This study was approved by the ethics committee of the Waseda University (2021 − 218), and all participants and their parents provided signed informed consent.

Data collection and procedures

We measured body height and weight, assessed lumbar magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and conducted core stability clinical tests. MRI scans were obtained using a 3.0-Tesla whole-body MRI system (GE Healthcare, Waukesha, WI, USA) with two imaging modes: short-tau inversion recovery (STIR) and 3D LAVA. Scanning parameters for STIR were as follows: repetition time/echo time, 1800/60 ms; slice thickness, 2 mm; field of view, 24 cm; matrix, 320 × 224 pixels; inversion time, 150 ms. The parameters for 3D LAVA were as follows: repetition time/echo time, 4.5/2.1 ms; slice thickness, 0.80 mm; field of view, 24 cm; matrix, 384 × 384 pixels.

Cross-sectional area of the trunk muscle

Bilateral cross-sectional areas (CSAs) of the psoas major (PM), multifidus (MF), erector spinae (ES), as well as vertebral disc were measured using intermediate images of the L4/5 intervertebral disc (Fig. 1) based on the previous study [14]. Intra-rater reliability of MRI-derived cross-sectional areas was evaluated using ICC (3,1) with absolute agreement. A single trained examiner performed three repeated measurements per participant (n = 10) in randomized order. ICC (3,1) values (95% CI) were: psoas major, 0.87 (0.85–0.91); multifidus, 0.84 (0.80–0.88); erector spinae, 0.82 (0.80–0.84); and intervertebral disc, 0.89 (0.85–0.93).

Clinical testing

Active straight leg raise (ASLR) and hip extension (HE) tests were conducted to assess lumbopelvic stability during leg movement. ASLR and HE were selected because they are commonly used in clinical settings to evaluate lumbopelvic stability in a simple and practical manner [15]. ASLR involved raising one lower limb from a supine position, with subjective assessment of pelvic depression—defined as an apparent downward displacement of the pelvis caused by rotation in the horizontal plane of the body—on the raised side (i.e., pelvic rotation to the right during right leg flexion). The examiner palpated the anterior superior iliac spine to detect pelvic depression. Similarly, HE involved lifting one lower limb from a prone position, with evaluation of pelvic depression on the elevated side (i.e., left pelvic rotation during right leg extension). Both ASLR and HE were assessed as binary outcomes (positive/negative). ASLR targeted 30° of hip flexion, and HE targeted 15° of hip extension. All participants demonstrated sufficient mobility to perform these tests without restriction during screening. This definition of positivity was based on a previously validated procedure for assessing lumbosacral control [15]. The examiner who performed clinical testing performed independently, and no other examiner was present in the same booth during the assessment. In addition, passive flexibility of the iliopsoas, hamstrings, and quadriceps evaluated based on the previous study [5] which may potentially affect ASLR and HE did not differ significantly between groups (Supplementary Table 1). All clinical tests were conducted by a physical therapist (T.T.) with > 5 years of experience, in a setting blinded to MRI findings and group assignments.

LS determination

LS was diagnosed at the L5 level using both STIR and 3D LAVA MRI modes. In STIR, bone marrow edema (BME) at the pars articularis was evaluated (Fig. 2-A). In 3D LAVA imaging, fracture stages were determined as early (hairline fracture, Fig. 2-B), progressive (clear gap, Fig. 2-C), and terminal (pseudoarthrosis, Fig. 2-D). Participants were classified into the uni-LS group if findings were positive only on one side of the pars articularis. LS was diagnosed by an orthopaedic surgeon (S.T.) with > 40 years of experience.

Statistical analysis

SPSS Statistics 29 (SPSS, Inc., IBM, Japan) was used for all analyses. To form a control group matched to the uni-LS group, we selected participants with the same dominant/non-dominant leg (defined as the kicking/pivot leg in soccer kicking motion). In addition, this classification was based on the participants’ answers to a questionnaire regarding their dominant kicking side for shooting during regular play. Of the 74 players without LS, 70 proceeded to the next analysis step. Propensity scores were calculated using multiple logistic regression with age, height, and weight as covariates. The uni-LS and control (without LS) groups were matched 1:1 by propensity score (caliper 0.2) [16]. After matching, the balance between groups was confirmed by standardized mean differences (all < 0.2; small), indicating adequate covariate balance. For subsequent analyses, the LS side and non-LS side were assigned in the control group according to the dominant/non-dominant distribution observed in the uni-LS group (15 non-dominant, 4 dominant).

An unpaired t-test was used to compare fundamental characteristics between the groups. A two-way mixed-effects ANCOVA (Group: between-subjects; Side: within-subjects) was conducted to assess the effects of LS status (uni-LS vs. control) and side (LS vs. non-LS) on trunk muscle CSA for each muscle (PM, MF, and ES), with vertebral disc CSA included as a covariate to control for body size effects [17]. The analysis was performed using the General Linear Model. The 2 × 2 main effects and interaction were examined; if a significant interaction was detected, post hoc Bonferroni multiple comparisons were performed. No multiplicity adjustment was applied because each muscle (PM, MF, ES) was treated as a distinct outcome rather than repeated testing of the same variable. Partial η² was calculated to quantify effect size and interpreted as small (η² ≥ 0.01), medium (η² ≥ 0.06), and large (η² ≥ 0.14). To evaluate the relationship between LS and clinical test outcomes, we also used two-sided Fisher’s exact tests to analyze associations between uni-LS presence and binary ASLR and HE test results. Odds ratios (ORs) were estimated by conditional maximum likelihood, and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were obtained using exact conditional methods. A post-hoc power analysis was conducted based on the total sample size of 38 across four cells (2 × 2 ANOVA design), with α = 0.05 and assuming a large effect size (partial η² = 0.14, per Cohen’s guideline [18]). This indicated a statistical power of approximately 0.67.

Of the 19 soccer players with uni-LS, 15 and 4 had findings on the non-dominant and dominant sides, respectively. Among them, 4 were positive only for BME while 12, 3, and 0 were in the early, progressive, and terminal stages, respectively. Discussion

This study aimed to identify trunk muscle CSA characteristics and clinical test findings in adolescent male soccer players with uni-LS. Results revealed that the uni-LS group had a smaller PM CSA on the LS side and positive ASLR findings. These findings may offer preliminary insights for clinicians and athletic trainers when assessing adolescent athletes with uni-LS.

Few studies have reported the prevalence of uni-LS using MRI in athletic cohorts. We identified 19 adolescent soccer players (17.7%) with uni-LS. Prior studies have shown that unilateral lumbar bone stress injury detected by STIR mode only occurred in 40 (11.7%) of 342 adolescent athletes [5]. In our study, 3 T MRI was used to detect early-stage hairline fractures to terminal-stage pseudoarthrosis in the pars articularis, as well as BME, via the 3D T1 VIBE [19]. Therefore, the higher prevalence of uni-LS observed in our study may reflect the ability of this imaging method to detect both BME and fracture pathology [20]. Uni-LS is also more likely to occur on the nondominant side [21]. In our study, uni-LS was more frequently observed on the pivot side. Given that LS results from bony structural failure originating caudally in the pars articularis, repetitive stepping motions in soccer may create asymmetrical mechanical stress on the lumbar pars articularis.

Our results also showed reduced PM CSA on the LS side in the uni-LS group. Wagner et al. [22] reported that iliopsoas CSA distinguished patients with severe versus mild/moderate degenerative spondylolisthesis (1010.1 vs. 1178.6 mm²; 14.3% smaller in the severe group). In our study, the PM CSA on the LS side was 12.5% smaller than that of controls. Taken together, these findings suggest that the magnitude of morphological asymmetry observed in uni-LS athletes may therefore hold clinical relevance. The PM originates from the upper lateral and transverse processes of the first to fourth lumbar vertebrae and lies posterior to the lumbar flexion–extension axis [23]. The lower PM flexes the lower lumbar spine, whereas the upper PM extends the upper spine, highlighting its role as a potential stabilizer of lumbar lordosis [23, 24]. Additionally, adolescent athletes with LS often present with pronounced lumbar lordosis and sacral anteversion [5, 13, 25], which can increase compressive stress on the pars. Considering our L4/5 CSA measurements and prior research, the uni-LS group may have exhibited impaired lower lumbar flexion and reduced spinal stabilization on the LS side, which may be related to smaller PM. The PM also contributes to contralateral lumbar rotation [26]. Thus, a smaller PM on the LS side may be associated with increased lumbar rotation toward the LS side. Therefore, this morphological finding may suggest a potential role of PM in lumbopelvic stabilization, which warrants further investigation in prospective interventional studies. In contrast, no significant group differences were observed in the CSA of the MF and ES, with only small effect sizes (partial η² ≤ 0.05). Aoyagi et al. [14] reported that adolescent athletes with LS exhibited greater paraspinal muscle CSA compared to those with nonspecific low back pain, possibly reflecting an adaptive hypertrophy in response to repeated lumbar extension stress during sports activity. Furthermore, Tsuboi et al. [27] suggested that baseball players with terminal-stage LS may exhibit impaired recruitment of lower-threshold fast-twitch motor units in the MF and ES. These findings imply that the assessment of MF and ES in young athletes with LS may require not only morphological evaluation but also functional measures, such as electromyographic analysis or contraction rate assessments using ultrasound imaging.

ASLR is widely used to evaluate muscle strength and function around the lumbopelvic region [28]. Poor lumbopelvic stability during lower limb raising is often accompanied by pelvic rotation and excessive lumbar movement. Park et al. [29] found that controlled ASLR reduced lumbar spine rotation by activating abdominal muscles. Liebenson et al. [30] also supported ASLR as a screen for lumbar spine stability, especially in rotation control and abdominal function. These findings support our interpretation that athletes with uni-LS may have impaired lumbopelvic and abdominal control on the LS side. Furthermore, as previous reports suggest that athletes with uni-LS have greater anterior pelvic tilt asymmetry compared with controls, correcting side-to-side ASLR differences may help reduce mechanical stress on the lumbar pars articularis.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it had a cross-sectional design, making it unclear whether the smaller PM CSA and lumbopelvic depression observed in the uni-LS group were pre-existing or developed after onset. Second, trunk muscle behavior in the uni-LS group during movement remains unknown, as MRI was performed in a supine, resting position. Third, although individual player workload (e.g., playing position or training history) was not assessed, all players belonged to the same academy and followed a standardized training schedule, which may have minimized group differences. Future prospective studies are needed to clarify the causal relationship and temporal changes in lumbopelvic function (i.e., lumbopelvic kinematics, muscle activity during ASLR, objective trunk muscle strength by dynamometry, and and ultrasound-based evaluations) and PM morphology in adolescent athletes. Finally, the statistical power was moderate (0.67), which represents a major limitation of this study. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted with caution regarding potential Type I and II errors. Nevertheless, unilateral LS is a relatively uncommon condition, and the recruitment of 19 affected adolescent athletes from a single cohort constitutes a notable strength. When combined with carefully matched controls, these data provide valuable exploratory insights regarding trunk muscle morphology and lumbopelvic function in this population. Future intervention studies with larger samples that target reduced PM size and impaired lumbopelvic control are essential to highlight these associations.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that adolescent soccer players with uni-LS exhibited a smaller PM CSA and lumbopelvic depression during the ASLR, compared to matched controls. Although the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, these findings highlight the potential relevance of PM morphology and lumbopelvic control with uni-LS. While further longitudinal studies are needed, the ASLR test may serve as a useful screening tool for identifying altered lumbopelvic control associated with uni-LS, and PM-focused rehabilitation program may be warranted to support lumbar stability.
