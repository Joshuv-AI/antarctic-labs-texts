Beyond the price tag: a scoping review of the determinants of informal payments in health systems and strategies for curbing them
Authors: Faride Sadat Jalali, Najmeh Bordbar, Mohadeseh Ghanbari-Jahromi, Mohsen Bayati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInformal payments (IPs) remain a persistent challenge across health systems worldwide and may undermine equity, transparency, and public trust in healthcare delivery. Despite a growing body of research, evidence regarding the determinants of IPs and the strategies proposed to address them remains fragmented and lacks a comprehensive synthesis. This scoping review aimed to: (1) map and synthesize existing evidence on the key determinants of IPs in health systems globally; and (2) identify, categorize, and summarize the range of strategies proposed to curb them.MethodsFollowing the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology, we conducted a systematic search across five databases—PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, ProQuest, and ScienceDirect—from database inception through January 2025. Two reviewers independently screened all records against predefined eligibility criteria. Data from the included studies were extracted using a standardized form and thematically analyzed.ResultsThis review synthesized evidence from 68 studies. Determinants of IPs were categorized into five interconnected levels: (1) patient-level factors (e.g., fear of poor-quality care, desire for faster services, and limited knowledge); (2) structural/contextual factors (e.g., underfunding, weak regulation, and type of service); (3) provider-level factors (e.g., low salaries and shortages of supplies); (4) cultural/ethical factors (e.g., norms of gratitude and social acceptance of informal payments); and (5) organizational-level factors (e.g., corruption and lack of accountability). Proposed interventions were grouped into five strategic categories: patient empowerment (education and patient-rights mechanisms); cultural-normative strategies (shifting social norms and fostering trust); financial strategies (improving provider remuneration, formalizing payments, and expanding insurance coverage); structural strategies (strengthening health systems and improving service accessibility); and governance and regulatory strategies (legal frameworks, oversight mechanisms, and sanctions). An analytical framework was developed to illustrate the mechanisms of action and key contextual considerations associated with each strategy, highlighting that implementation processes and outcomes are highly context dependent.ConclusionsIPs are a multifactorial phenomenon rooted in systemic weaknesses, economic incentives, and sociocultural norms. Isolated interventions are unlikely to be effective. Addressing IPs requires integrated, multi-pronged strategies that reduce financial pressures on providers, strengthen governance and transparency, improve service quality and accessibility, empower patients, and reshape cultural norms. This review provides a foundational evidence map to support the design of context-sensitive, system-level policies aimed at reducing and ultimately eliminating informal payments. Future research should evaluate the effectiveness and sustainability of interventions across diverse health system contexts, particularly in underrepresented settings, and further examine the institutional and contextual factors that perpetuate informal payment practices.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14846-y.

Background

Informal payments (IPs) remain a persistent challenge across many health systems worldwide and have attracted increasing attention in health policy and health systems research [1]. Although the forms, drivers, and social meanings of these payments vary across contexts, IPs are widely recognized as practices that may undermine equity, transparency, accountability, and public trust in healthcare systems [2]. Existing evidence suggests that these practices are shaped by broader structural and governance-related factors, including resource constraints, weak regulatory oversight, and asymmetries in power and information between patients and providers. Despite the expanding body of research on IPs, important conceptual and contextual differences continue to complicate their interpretation and comparison across settings [3].

Informal payments (IPs) were defined as cash or in-kind contributions made by patients or their families outside formal payment mechanisms or approved tariffs for services to which they were officially entitled [4]. These payments take multiple forms, including cash payments for services that are formally free of charge, in-kind contributions offered in exchange for preferential treatment, and unofficial charges for essential medicines or supplies [5]. Their consequences are substantial, as they may expose households to catastrophic health expenditures, distort provider incentives, and divert resources away from formal health financing mechanisms [3, 6, 7]. Beyond their financial implications, IPs are embedded within broader sociocultural norms and often reflect governance weaknesses and structural dysfunctions within health systems [8, 9]. At the same time, their conceptual boundaries remain fluid. Practices described as “under-the-table payments,” “envelope payments,” or “gratuities” may exist along a continuum ranging from voluntary expressions of appreciation to coercive or implicitly expected payments associated with access to care, service quality, or timeliness of treatment [10, 11]. This conceptual variability further complicates cross-country comparisons and has important implications for both policy design and the interpretation of evidence [8].

IPs have been documented across a wide range of health systems, although reported prevalence varies considerably by country, type of service, and measurement approach, with estimates ranging from 2% to 80% in the reviewed literature [12]. Cross-national studies indicate that IPs remain prevalent in several Central and Eastern European countries, including Hungary, Bulgaria, and Ukraine, as well as across the EU-27 region [13, 14]. Similar practices have also been reported in countries such as Greece and Iran, alongside findings from comparative analyses conducted across multiple health systems [15–17]. Broader evidence syntheses and regional studies suggest that informal payments are driven by common structural and governance-related factors, although their manifestations and intensity differ across contexts [5, 9, 18]. In contrast, evidence from high-income countries such as the United States, the United Kingdom, and Canada appears relatively limited and less explicit in the literature. This may reflect both lower reported prevalence and differences in conceptual framing, terminology, measurement, and reporting practices [9].

Micro-level studies have highlighted the socioeconomic and cultural factors that shape patients’ decisions to provide or accept informal payments [5]. Qualitative research conducted across diverse settings has identified multiple drivers of IPs and proposed context-specific interventions to reduce these practices [10, 19, 20]. At the macro level, studies have demonstrated that structural reforms—such as market liberalization, privatization, and financial sector development—may reduce both the prevalence and frequency of informal payments [20]. More recent conceptual and policy-oriented research suggests that power imbalances, weak accountability mechanisms, and governance failures contribute to the persistence of these practices [21]. Complementary evidence from mixed-methods and participatory studies, including research conducted in Tanzania, further highlights the importance of engaging frontline health workers in identifying feasible and context-sensitive policy responses [22].

Despite this growing body of literature, the evidence remains fragmented and dispersed across different analytical perspectives, including policy recommendations, structural determinants, theoretical frameworks, and context-specific intervention designs. To date, no comprehensive synthesis has systematically mapped the multilevel determinants of IPs and linked them to the range of strategies proposed or implemented to address them. Such a synthesis is essential for informing holistic and evidence-informed policy responses. To address this gap, we conducted a scoping review with two objectives: [1] to map and synthesize the existing evidence on the key determinants of informal payments across health systems globally; and [2] to identify, categorize, and summarize the range of strategies, interventions, and policy approaches proposed in the literature to curb these payments. By integrating these findings, this review aims to provide a foundational resource for researchers and policymakers seeking to design context-sensitive, system-level interventions that promote equity, trust, and sustainability within health systems.

Materials and methods

This study employed a scoping review methodology based on the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) framework [23]. A five-stage process was undertaken to systematically identify, chart, and synthesize the existing evidence on the determinants of informal payments in health systems and the strategies proposed to address them. In this review, Informal payments (IPs) were defined as cash or in-kind contributions made by patients or their families outside formal payment mechanisms or approved tariffs for services to which they were officially entitled. This operational definition informed the eligibility criteria for study inclusion and was applied consistently throughout the screening and data extraction processes.

The review began with the formulation of a central research question designed to guide all stages of the study: “What are the key determinants of informal payments in health systems, and what strategies have been proposed to reduce them?” This overarching question served as the foundation for identifying and synthesizing relevant evidence across diverse settings. To further structure the review, a specific research question was developed using the PCC framework (Population, Concept, and Context). The population included all healthcare facilities, the concept encompassed factors influencing informal payments and strategies aimed at reducing them, and the context covered health systems across all countries worldwide. To enhance methodological transparency, the eligibility criteria were organized according to the PCC framework (Table 1).

To identify relevant literature, a systematic search was conducted across five electronic databases: PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, ProQuest, and ScienceDirect. The search covered publications from database inception through January 2025. To ensure comprehensive coverage, two complementary search strategies were developed: the first focused on the informal nature of payments, while the second addressed the financial or payment-related dimension. Keywords within each strategy were combined using the “OR” operator, and the two strategies were subsequently linked using the “AND” operator to identify studies addressing both the informal and financial dimensions of payments within health systems.

Detailed descriptions of the search strategies, including database-specific adaptations, are presented in Table 2 and Table S1 (Appendix). To improve the specificity and relevance of retrieved records, the search strategy primarily relied on title-based search terms. This approach was adopted because of the heterogeneous terminology used to describe informal payments in the literature and to facilitate the efficient identification of highly relevant studies while maintaining the feasibility of the screening process.

The search strategy

In this phase, predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied to guide study selection. In accordance with the PCC framework (Table 1), studies were included if they examined factors associated with informal payments—such as economic, sociocultural, institutional, or governance-related determinants—or reported strategies, interventions, or policy measures aimed at reducing informal payments across any healthcare setting or country context. Eligible studies included original quantitative, qualitative, and mixed-methods research published in English, with full texts available for data extraction.

Studies were excluded if they lacked a rigorous peer-review process or were categorized as book reviews, theoretical papers, review articles, or letters to the editor. Studies that did not explicitly address the determinants of informal payments or failed to report strategies or interventions aimed at reducing them were also excluded. The retrieved records were independently screened by two reviewers in three sequential stages: title screening, abstract screening, and full-text review. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion, and unresolved cases were referred to a third reviewer for final adjudication. Screening was conducted using EndNote version 21, which was used for reference management, organization of retrieved records, and removal of duplicate studies prior to screening.

Data were extracted using a standardized form developed in Microsoft Excel 2019 in accordance with the predefined research objectives. The extraction form captured key information, including the first author, article title, publication year, study setting, participant characteristics, determinants of informal payments, and strategies proposed to reduce them. In this review, any payments reported in the included studies that occurred outside formal payment channels or official tariffs were considered informal payments, consistent with the operational definition adopted in the study. Data extraction was independently performed by two reviewers, and the extracted data were subsequently verified by a third reviewer to ensure accuracy and consistency.

Consistent with scoping review methodology, no formal critical appraisal of the included studies was undertaken. However, studies with substantial reporting limitations or publication concerns (e.g., retracted articles or non-peer-reviewed sources) were excluded during the full-text review stage to ensure adequate quality and completeness of the extracted data.

In the final stage, the findings were compiled, summarized, and reported. One researcher conducted a comprehensive review of all included studies and prepared detailed analytical notes. To enhance the reliability of the synthesis, a second researcher independently reviewed the analysis and verified the findings. Relevant data were then systematically entered into the extraction form according to the predefined information categories. This collaborative approach helped ensure that the data were accurately captured and consistently organized, thereby providing a robust foundation for subsequent synthesis and interpretation.

Results

The initial search identified 1,702 records. After the removal of 718 duplicates, 984 unique records remained for title screening. Following title screening, 809 records were excluded due to irrelevance. The abstracts of the remaining 175 records were then assessed for eligibility, leading to the exclusion of 64 studies. Subsequently, 111 full-text articles were retrieved and evaluated against the predefined inclusion criteria. Of these, 43 articles were excluded for the following reasons: failure to report determinants or interventions related to informal payments (n = 18), not meeting the predefined PCC-based inclusion criteria (n = 9), insufficient information for data extraction—including retracted publications, duplicate datasets, and overlapping reports (n = 14)—and unavailability of the full text (n = 2). Ultimately, 68 studies met all eligibility criteria and were included in the final scoping review for data extraction and synthesis (Fig. 1).

Determinants of informal payments in health systems

The most extensively documented category is patient individual-level factors, which encompasses a wide range of demographic, socioeconomic, and psychosocial variables. Notably, fear of poor-quality care (frequency: 18) and the desire for faster access to care (frequency: 15) are the most frequently reported psychological drivers. Demographic determinants show heterogeneous associations: IPs are linked to both higher and lower income levels, varying levels of education, and mixed evidence regarding sex differences. Other important factors include limited awareness of entitlements to free services, health insurance status (which is generally protective), and place of residence.

At the structural and contextual level, systemic weaknesses emerge as key drivers. These include insufficient health financing, weak regulatory frameworks, unclear charging guidelines, and inadequate oversight mechanisms. The type of service is also a strong predictor of IPs, with surgical, inpatient, and emergency services showing the strongest associations.

Provider-level factors are primarily driven by economic pressures, with low salaries and wages being the most frequently cited determinant across the entire review. Additional factors include efforts to compensate for supply shortages and various motivational and ethical considerations among providers.

Cultural and ethical factors highlight the normative dimension of IPs. The most commonly reported factor in this category is the perception of informal payments as a form of gratitude for care, illustrating how social norms may normalize or legitimize such practices. Acceptance of IPs within social norms and lower trust in public institutions further reinforce their persistence.

Finally, organizational-level factors reflect institutional weaknesses, with corruption identified as the most frequently reported issue, followed by lack of accountability. Overall, this multi-level classification demonstrates that informal payments are a complex, multifactorial phenomenon, requiring comprehensive strategies that address all contributing dimensions simultaneously.

Patient empowerment

This is the most frequently cited intervention category and focuses on reducing information asymmetry while strengthening patients’ autonomy and decision-making capacity. The primary strategy is awareness and education (69.6% of interventions within this category), which includes clarifying patient entitlements, conducting public awareness campaigns on the negative consequences of IPs for equity and access, and improving communication skills. A secondary strategy is the establishment of formal patient rights and accountability mechanisms (17.4%), such as transparent complaint and grievance systems.

Cultural–normative interventions

This category addresses the social and ethical norms that sustain IPs. The most frequently reported strategy is shifting social norms (21.7%), which includes campaigns to distinguish gifts from bribes, de-normalize gratitude payments, and engage religious and community leaders. Additional strategies include strengthening the social contract (8.7%) and improving service quality and infrastructure (15.2%) as foundational measures to reduce the perceived need for informal payments.

Financial interventions

This category focuses on economic incentives. The most prominent strategy is providing provider incentives (45.7%), including increasing base salaries, introducing performance-based payment systems, and implementing alternative provider payment models. Another key strategy is formalizing payments (37.0%), aimed at channeling informal transactions into transparent and regulated systems. Additional approaches include systemic financing reforms (23.9%) and improving financial transparency and payment simplification (10.9%).

Structural interventions

These strategies target macro-level health system design. The most frequently cited intervention is expanding and strengthening health insurance coverage (47.8%), aimed at reducing out-of-pocket payments and uncertainty regarding entitlements. Other strategies include health system strengthening (21.7%), such as direct facility financing and expanding service availability, and reforms involving private sector engagement and new service delivery models (17.4%), including the implementation of gatekeeping and referral systems.

Governance and regulatory interventions

This category focuses on legal frameworks, oversight, and accountability mechanisms. The most frequently cited and emphatic strategy is establishing comprehensive legal and regulatory frameworks with sanctions (63.0%), including clear legislation, explicit bans on IPs, and strict enforcement mechanisms. Other strategies include enhancing transparency and accountability (26.1%), strengthening oversight and monitoring systems (23.9%), and broader governance reforms (13.0%), such as regulating or restricting dual practice between public and private sectors.

Overall, this classification highlights that a single-dimensional approach is insufficient to address informal payments. Effective policy responses require a coherent combination of interventions targeting provider financial incentives, structural weaknesses in the health system, governance and accountability gaps, patient awareness and behavior, and entrenched sociocultural norms.

Accordingly, the table is intended to inform the design of context-sensitive and multi-component policy responses, rather than to assess the effectiveness of specific interventions in reducing informal payments.

Discussion

This scoping review demonstrates that informal payments in health systems are complex and multidimensional phenomena arising from the interaction of patient behaviors, provider incentives, organizational arrangements, sociocultural norms, and broader governance structures. At the patient level, factors such as fear of receiving poor-quality care, expectations of expedited services, and limited awareness of entitlements underscore the importance of patient empowerment. Educational interventions, transparent information regarding service coverage and costs, and accessible communication channels may reduce the perceived necessity of informal payments and strengthen patients’ ability to navigate health systems effectively. Consistent with this interpretation, previous studies have shown that education, income, and other individual characteristics are significantly associated with both the prevalence and magnitude of informal payments [86, 87].

At the provider and organizational levels, low and inadequate remuneration, resource constraints, and weak oversight mechanisms create structural incentives that sustain informal payments. These findings suggest that interventions focused solely on patient education are insufficient to address the problem. Instead, system-level reforms—including formalizing payment mechanisms, expanding health insurance coverage, introducing performance-based remuneration systems, and strengthening transparency and accountability—are required to address the underlying economic and institutional drivers. Evidence from cross-national research by Incaltarau et al., covering 117 countries, supports this conclusion by demonstrating that institutional deficiencies are among the most important determinants of informal payments in health systems [35]. Moreover, informal payments are more prevalent in contexts characterized by low institutional trust and a misalignment between formal regulations and informal practices, highlighting the need to strengthen institutional frameworks as a central policy priority [25].

Cultural and ethical norms further contribute to the persistence of informal payments. Practices such as gift-giving and the social acceptance of unofficial payments can normalize these transactions and blur the boundary between gratitude and coercion. Accordingly, community-engaged interventions that promote professional ethics, strengthen social accountability, and foster trust between patients and providers are essential components of comprehensive prevention strategies. Review studies emphasize culturally oriented approaches, including increasing public awareness of insurance-covered services, medicines, cost-sharing limits, and available generic alternatives; informing patients about formal complaint and compensation mechanisms; and challenging the perception that informal payments are necessary to obtain quality care by reinforcing confidence in adequate public-sector remuneration [19, 44].

Importantly, no single determinant or intervention operates in isolation. Rather, informal payments emerge from a dynamic system in which financial pressures on providers are reinforced by weak governance structures, normalized through cultural narratives, and sustained by gaps in patient knowledge and information asymmetries. Therefore, the literature consistently highlights the need for multi-level strategies that simultaneously address patient empowerment, provider incentives, financial arrangements, governance capacity, and sociocultural norms. However, the effectiveness of these interventions is highly context dependent, as variations in social values, institutional capacity, and health system design shape both implementation processes and outcomes. Overall, this review underscores that informal payments cannot be effectively addressed through fragmented or isolated measures. Instead, integrated, system-wide approaches are essential to promote transparency, equity, and sustainability in health systems. Future research should move beyond descriptive analyses toward rigorous evaluation of intervention packages across diverse settings, with greater attention to underlying contextual mechanisms and long-term effects on health system performance and public trust.

The conceptual mapping presented in Fig. 2 further clarifies the relationships between determinants of informal payments and corresponding intervention strategies. It illustrates how different strategies operate through distinct mechanisms to address underlying drivers at multiple levels of the health system, while also highlighting cross-cutting effects across categories. This underscores that effective policy responses require strong alignment between identified determinants and targeted interventions, supporting the need for integrated, multi-level approaches rather than isolated measures. Notably, several strategies simultaneously address multiple determinants, reinforcing the importance of coordinated policy design and implementation.

Limitations

This review has several limitations. Restricting the search to English-language publications may have excluded relevant non-English evidence, particularly from high-prevalence settings. The geographical coverage is uneven, with underrepresentation of some high-income countries, while evidence is concentrated in Eastern Europe, Central Asia, parts of Africa, and Asia, where informal payments are more frequently reported or differently conceptualized. As a scoping review, no formal quality appraisal was conducted, limiting assessment of methodological rigor and introducing potential variability bias. Differences in definitions and conceptualization of informal payments across studies also complicated the synthesis. Grey literature may have been missed, and restricting the search to studies up to January 2025 may have excluded recent evidence. In addition, title-based searching, while increasing specificity, may have reduced sensitivity and led to the omission of relevant studies. Although data extraction and synthesis were independently conducted within the research team, the absence of external reviewers may have introduced interpretive bias. Finally, the review mapped strategies and mechanisms rather than assessing their effectiveness, so it cannot determine which interventions are most effective or sustainable, highlighting a key gap for future research. Despite these limitations, the study provides a comprehensive synthesis and a structured framework to inform policy and future research on informal payments in health systems.

Conclusions

This scoping review synthesizes evidence from 68 studies and demonstrates that informal payments (IPs) are a multifactorial phenomenon shaped by interconnected determinants at patient, structural-contextual, provider, cultural-ethical, and organizational levels. Correspondingly, effective responses encompass patient empowerment, cultural-normative change, financial reforms, structural improvements, and strengthened governance mechanisms. No single intervention is sufficient, as IPs persist due to systemic interdependencies that require integrated, multi-level strategies. Contextual sensitivity is essential, given that the effectiveness of interventions depends on institutional capacity, sociocultural values, and health system design. Overall, reducing and ultimately eliminating IPs requires comprehensive policy approaches that promote transparency, equity, and trust in health systems. However, evidence on the comparative effectiveness and long-term sustainability of interventions remains limited, particularly in underrepresented settings. Future research should therefore focus on evaluating which interventions or combinations are most effective across different contexts, while further exploring the institutional and sociocultural mechanisms that sustain informal payment practices.
