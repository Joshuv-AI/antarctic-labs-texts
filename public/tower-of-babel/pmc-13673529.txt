Misinformation beyond fact-checking: Definitional integration and the dual-path evolution model
Authors: Aoqi Chen, Yuchang Jin, Junxiu An, Peixuan Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Recently, misinformation has garnered wider attention. However, the ambiguous definition of misinformation hinders deeper and more integrated research across various fields concerning misinformation. Therefore, departing from fact-checking methods, we identify two receiver-based misinformation dimensions: public engagement level and gist misunderstanding. Furthermore, we integrate existing conceptualizations of misinformation to propose an evolutionary model that delineates two key pathways of misinformation development: (1) information distortion processes and (2) intra-group dissemination dynamics. This work is anticipated to (1) systematize the currently fragmented conceptualizations of misinformation through its dual-dimensional integration and (2) establish novel diagnostic metrics for disinformation identification in applied domains, such as health communication and public opinion governance, while (3) providing theoretical foundations for platform content moderation and media literacy education by elucidating misinformation’s source-level evolutionary mechanisms, ultimately contributing to more robust scientific frameworks and societal-level prevention systems.

Introduction

Misinformation erodes the foundations of democracy and science, and its harms to society escalate as it spreads. During the pandemic, health-related misinformation spread rapidly, while vaccine misinformation significantly impacted vaccination rates.1 Amplified by social media, misinformation continues to cause harm across multiple domains, including climate issues, public health, politics, and finance,2,3 which has prompted organizations such as the APA to call for combating this “infodemic” with interdisciplinary solutions.

Research on misinformation faces two main challenges: (1) the definition of misinformation is unclear, with varying criteria across studies,4 making integration difficult. Current definitions focus on factual inaccuracies and misleading intent, overlooking psychological differences.5 (2) There is insufficient research on the development of misinformation. Existing studies have focused on susceptibility, dissemination, and response strategies but have neglected the process by which valid information turns into misinformation, hindering efforts to address the issue at its source.6

It is imperative to expand and standardize the definition of misinformation; rigorously explore its origins, including unintended misinformation from credible sources7; and develop complex network models at a macro level to clarify its characteristics, consistent with psychological and communication research frameworks.8,9 To achieve this goal, this study first analyzed existing definitions of misinformation and then proposed two dimensions to integrate these definitions, ultimately constructing a theoretical model of misinformation development. The study not only bridges conceptual divides but also provides testable hypotheses for empirical validation, advancing the field beyond mere descriptive accounts.

Misinformation definition

To justify a definition of misinformation that transcends fact-checking, this study first briefly analyzes current concepts and highlights their limitations.(1)False information and disinformation: the former emphasizes fact-checking, while the latter focuses on the intent behind information dissemination.(2) Rumors: a rumor is defined as “an unverified, instrumental information statement that emerges in situations of ambiguity, danger, or potential threat.”10(3) Conspiracy theories: this concept emphasizes major events and is strongly associated with negative, confrontational stances against the public and authorities.(4) Fake news: this refers to the deliberate presentation of false or misleading claims as news, a deliberate “design” that conforms to certain characteristics of news production and dissemination.

A review of the prevailing definitions and empirical studies indicates that intent detection and fact-checking are the dominant dimensions. However, due to detection difficulties and existing blind spots, the research outcomes are often hard to implement. The analysis of cases such as political rumors, fake news, and pandemic-related rumors reveals that researchers typically focus on information that is likely to evoke negative emotions or harmful behaviors within groups. Therefore, this study proposes a redefinition of misinformation from two dimensions: public engagement level and gist misunderstanding. Therefore, misinformation is defined as a body of contradictory and conflicting information, characterized by assertive, emotive, and unsourced claims, that is sustained within specific communities (e.g., cancer misinformation targeting patients, food-related rumors concerning parents, or celebrity gossip among fan bases) around a particular theme.

The public engagement level serves as a key dimension for identifying significant misinformation issues, thereby directing attention to vulnerable populations and underscoring the essential connection between information and its participants. Gist misunderstanding emphasizes defining misinformation from the perspective of group cognition, allowing for the broader inclusion of information where facts are hard to verify and the dissemination intent is difficult to discern. Misinformation dual-path evolution model

Accurate information can be misinterpreted during group dissemination due to a lack of knowledge, as seen in the Mozart effect. Additionally, individuals may deliberately spread rumors for commercial or political purposes, such as election fake news. This shows that misinformation does not emerge suddenly or exist independently. We summarize its development in a dual-pathway interaction model, as shown in Figure 1B.

Path 1: Event and public engagement level

Typical information dissemination often requires a specific event as a trigger and is accompanied by a high degree of public engagement. Furthermore, considering the potential asymmetry in information dissemination and participation, we quantitatively define public engagement more specifically as the ratio of participants (those who actively seek information, demonstrate sharing intention, express opinions, or concretely verify information) to passive recipients. This study proposes the following related research indicators.

Event exposure volume

The frequency of event-related messages searched and viewed and the volume of associated content on the internet. This metric, when aligned with temporal event milestones, emphasizes analyzing the core narratives driving viral dissemination. It serves as a foundational indicator for assessing issue severity in communication studies.

Event sentiment perception

Event sentiment perception involves the emotional responses elicited by different framings of an event, facilitating the exploration of its positive or negative valence and identification of key audience segments. This metric draws on psychological research to understand affective dynamics in information processing.

Sharing intention

Sharing intention is the willingness of individuals to share specific information, highlighting its potential for further dissemination and evolution. This metric reflects psychosocial motivations underlying information propagation in communication and behavioral research.

Path 2: Gist misunderstanding and intergroup antagonism

Inspired by the noisy channel model, information is susceptible to various forms of noise during its transmission, processing, and reception stages. Factors such as entertainment-driven framing, emotional amplification, and fragmented reading contribute to gist misunderstanding, ultimately reducing shared consensus and increasing group polarization. This phenomenon is grounded in communication studies and psychological research on information distortion and social dynamics. This study proposes the following related research indicators about gist misunderstanding and intergroup antagonism.

Keyword proportion

Keyword extraction serves as an effective tool for critically analyzing a text for gist. Comparing the prevalence of keywords in user-generated content with original information reveals biases in user comprehension, particularly in content marked by phrases such as “I believe.” This metric aligns with communication studies’ focus on textual analysis and psychological research on cognitive bias.

Supplementary information

Beyond studying the dissemination of information itself, attention to ancillary elements such as identity, emotion, and prejudice embedded in the sharing process is crucial. These factors may illuminate directions of information distortion and group hostility, contributing to communication and social psychology research.

Identity alignment of information supporters

Distinct from macro-level analyses of user motivations, simple affirmations or rejections of information (“yes or no”) can delineate group affiliations. This binary categorization fuels group antagonism, a phenomenon underexplored in communication and psychological studies of social identity dynamics.

Future prospects

Prior research on misinformation has often lacked clear definitions and operational feasibility, with limited exploration of its evolutionary processes, hindering effective source-level mitigation. This study introduces “public engagement” and “gist misunderstanding” as key dimensions and proposes a model for misinformation evolution. Based on this framework, we offer the following recommendations for future misinformation research.(1)Event-driven dynamic databases: misinformation’s fleeting nature requires event-based data collection tied to major societal moments (e.g., health crises and elections), including tracking narrative shifts over time and systematically gathering and annotating open-source datasets using APIs from social media and fact-checking platforms to create traceable, scalable repositories for research.(2)Individual cognitive and behavioral focus: the focus should shift from fact-checking to studying individuals’ motivations, emotions, and sharing behaviors. Mixed methods (surveys and experiments) should be used to analyze susceptibility across age, education, and cultural groups, providing evidence for targeted prevention in communication and psychology research.(3)Technology-driven tools: cross-disciplinary efforts should be fostered to build lightweight social media simulations or use existing platform APIs (e.g., X and WeChat) for naturalistic studies. Monitoring tools should be embedded to capture real-time user engagement and dissemination data, boosting ecological validity and reproducibility.(4)Tiered interdisciplinary framework: a three-tier approach should be built as follows. (a)Data tier: use computer science for big data collection and AI-driven semantic analysis.(b)Model tier: integrate psychology (cognitive experiments) and communication (diffusion models).(c)Application tier: combine political science and sociology for governance policies and group interventions, creating a cohesive research-to-practice pipeline.

Conclusion

The lack of clear definitions in misinformation research hinders its practical application. This study proposes a novel definition, moving beyond hard-to-quantify fact-checking and intent detection by introducing public engagement and gist misunderstanding as core dimensions. To address the evolution of misinformation and the question of how information becomes erroneous, we propose a dual-path model: (1) events trigger heightened group engagement and information surges and (2) misinterpretations and group polarization escalate events, with these stages cyclically reinforcing each other.

To enhance practical impact, the model informs targeted interventions. For example, during early event stages, real-time browsing data can power early-warning systems on platforms such as X to flag emerging misinformation trends, enabling swift content moderation. In the sharing phase, policies targeting emotionally charged or identity-driven content can prioritize review of high-risk narratives.

However, methodological limitations persist. The absence of robust misinformation databases, untested applicability across languages and cultures, and the lack of longitudinal studies to validate long-term relevance are notable gaps. Additionally, the model does not fully account for algorithmic amplification, such as social media echo chambers, which may intensify both paths. Future research should address these through empirical validation, cross-linguistic studies, and modeling algorithmic effects to strengthen the framework’s applicability in communication and psychological research.
