Impact of Staged Rehabilitation Training on Urinary Incontinence Recovery After Radical Prostatectomy: A Retrospective Analysis
Authors: Yenuan Hu, Li Yao, Chuan Liu, Jing Tian, Aihong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo evaluate the clinical impact of staged rehabilitation training on the recovery of urinary incontinence (UI) after radical prostatectomy (RP) for prostate cancer and to identify factors associated with recovery outcomes and training adherence. In this retrospective cohort study, 169 patients who underwent robot-assisted RP at our hospital between June 2023 and December 2024 were included. Based on postoperative management with/without staged rehabilitation training, patients were assigned to a rehabilitation group or a conventional group. The rehabilitation group received a structured and staged pelvic floor muscle-training and behavioral-guidance protocol, whereas the conventional group received only basic health education. UI recovery rates and scores on the International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire–Short Form (ICIQ-SF) were compared at 1, 3, and 6 months postoperatively. Independent predictors of UI recovery were analyzed using a Cox proportional-hazards model. Within the rehabilitation group, factors associated with adherence were examined using logistic regression. The rehabilitation group showed significantly higher UI recovery rates at all postoperative time points and lower ICIQ-SF scores. Staged rehabilitation training was an independent protective factor for UI recovery (hazard ratio [HR] = 2.048), whereas older age (HR = 0.765) and higher Gleason score (HR = 0.551) were independent risk factors. Within the rehabilitation group, higher adherence was associated with better recovery and lower ICIQ-SF scores (all p < .05). Logistic regression further indicated that age was an independent predictor of adherence (odds ratio [OR] = 0.713). Staged rehabilitation training distinctly accelerates UI recovery after RP and serves as an independent facilitator of continence restoration.Keywords: radical prostatectomy, urinary incontinence, staged rehabilitation training, retrospective analysis

Introduction

Prostate cancer is one of the most common malignancies in men, ranking second in global incidence (Almeeri et al., 2024; Bray et al., 2018). With the advancement of prostate-specific antigen (PSA) screening and diagnostic techniques, radical prostatectomy (RP) has become the primary treatment for localized prostate cancer, leading to substantial improvements in oncologic control (Lepor, 2001; Perez-Ibave et al., 2018). However, postoperative urinary incontinence (UI), one of the most frequent complications following RP, seriously compromises patients’ quality of life (Sallami, 2017).

The pathophysiological mechanisms of postoperative UI are complex, involving urethral sphincter injury, neurovascular bundle damage, and detrusor overactivity (Zivkovic & Tamussino, 2001). This complication not only results in physical discomfort but also contributes to psychological problems such as anxiety, depression, and social withdrawal, thereby exerting multiple negative effects on postoperative recovery and overall quality of life (Manfredi et al., 2019). Consequently, identifying effective strategies to manage and expedite UI recovery has become a central challenge in the field of oncologic rehabilitation in urology.

Pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT) is widely recommended by both domestic and international guidelines as the first-line nonsurgical approach for preventing and treating UI after RP (Milios et al., 2019; Straczynska et al., 2019). PFMT aims to enhance the strength and coordination of the striated urethral sphincter, increase urethral closure pressure, and suppress detrusor overactivity, ultimately reconstructing lower urinary tract continence function (Rahnama’i et al., 2021). Traditional rehabilitation guidance often consists merely of simple, standardized verbal instructions or written health education provided postoperatively, lacking systematic, standardized, and sustained supervision and management (Feng et al., 2020). This conventional approach fails to account for the physiological course of tissue healing and neural recovery at different postoperative stages, as well as patients’ individualized functional status and psychological needs. As a result, poor adherence, improper technique, and insufficient or excessive training intensity are common, significantly diminishing the potential benefits of PFMT.

Against this backdrop, staged rehabilitation training has gained increasing attention. Grounded in modern rehabilitation medicine, this model emphasizes a progressive, goal-oriented rehabilitation plan tailored to the patient’s postoperative recovery trajectory. The rehabilitation pathway is divided into sequential phases, ranging from early pelvic floor awareness activation to mid-stage strength and endurance building, and finally to late-stage functional integration and daily-life application, which forms a comprehensive and continuous management system (Santos et al., 2024). This approach not only focuses on physiological recovery but also integrates psychological support and behavioral education throughout the process, thereby maximizing rehabilitation efficiency and facilitating the transition from “disease treatment” to “functional reconstruction” and “return to health”.

Although the concept of staged rehabilitation has been well established in fields such as orthopedics and neurological rehabilitation (Chen et al., 2022; McNeill et al., 2023), its application and systematic evaluation in postoperative prostate cancer rehabilitation, particularly within the domestic clinical setting, remain insufficient. Whether this model offers advantages over conventional care, especially in accelerating the recovery of UI, still requires validation through high-quality clinical research.

This retrospective cohort study aims to systematically examine the impact of staged rehabilitation training on the recovery process of UI after RP, with the goal of providing evidence-based guidance for optimizing clinical rehabilitation strategies and improving postoperative quality of life in patients with prostate cancer.

Materials and Methods

Baseline Characteristics

This study employed a retrospective cohort design, with all data obtained from the hospital electronic medical record system and routine follow-up records maintained by dedicated nurses. Consecutive patients who underwent RP for prostate cancer in the Department of Urology at the hospital between June 2023 and December 2024 were included. Based on previous clinical research, the expected incidence of UI after RP is approximately 80% (Sacco et al., 2006; Schepens et al., 2023). Considering potential loss to follow-up and missing data, a total of 169 patients who underwent robot-assisted RP were ultimately included. According to the postoperative rehabilitation management model, patients were divided into two groups: 85 in the rehabilitation group (received staged rehabilitation-training) and 84 in the conventional group. No significant differences were observed between groups (Table 1). The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the institutional ethics committee, with informed consent waived.

Inclusion criteria are as follows: (a) histopathologically confirmed prostate adenocarcinoma via transrectal prostate biopsy; (b) first-time RP; (c) normal urinary continence and no UI symptoms preoperatively; and (d) complete clinical data and ≥6 months of regular postoperative follow-up.

Exclusion criteria are as follows: (a) preoperative neurogenic bladder, bladder outlet obstruction, or urge UI affecting continence; (b) prior pelvic radiotherapy or transurethral prostate surgery; (c) severe dysfunction of major organs (heart, brain, lungs) that precluded participation in rehabilitation training; (d) postoperative positive surgical margins or immediate adjuvant endocrine therapy/radiotherapy due to lymph node metastasis; and (e) cognitive or psychiatric disorders impairing comprehension or execution of rehabilitation instructions.

Intervention Protocol

All surgeries were performed by the same team of senior urologists to minimize variability related to surgical technique.

In the conventional group, patients received standard postoperative care in urology, including verbal health education, a printed rehabilitation manual, and instructions at discharge to continue pelvic floor muscle contraction exercises at home. Patients were advised to return for outpatient visits at 1, 3, and 6 months postoperatively, during which physicians briefly assessed recovery status.

For patients in the rehabilitation group, they underwent a structured, staged rehabilitation-training program supervised by a professionally trained urologic rehabilitation therapist, in addition to routine care. The 6-month program followed individualized and progressive principles and consisted of four sequential phases: (a) Basic perception phase (Postoperative Week 1): bedside one-on-one instruction to ensure correct identification and low-intensity activation of pelvic floor muscles; for patients with difficulty perceiving contractions, digital rectal biofeedback was used. (b) Strength-building phase (Postoperative Weeks 2–4): standardized strength and endurance training, including instruction in The Knack technique to counteract episodes of increased intra-abdominal pressure. (c) Functional integration phase (Postoperative Months 1–3): integration of pelvic floor control into sitting, standing, and daily activities; bladder training was provided for patients with urge UI. (d) Return-and-consolidation phase (Postoperative Months 3–6): guidance on integrating pelvic floor control into moderate-intensity physical activity, lifestyle optimization, and psychosocial support to facilitate full return to daily life. The program included weekly telephone or video follow-ups and monthly outpatient assessments to ensure active supervision and adherence management. Details of the intervention protocol are provided in the supplementary material (Table S1).

Data Collection and Quality Control

Baseline characteristics (age, body mass index [BMI], PSA, etc.) were extracted by an independent researcher not involved in group assignment. All follow-up assessments were conducted by a dedicated research nurse blinded to group allocation. The rehabilitation therapist supervised all interventions and collected “rehabilitation diaries” for the staged rehabilitation-training group but did not participate in outcome assessment. However, since adherence to the rehabilitation protocol was assessed through “rehabilitation diaries” completed by patients, it is possible that patients could have inferred their assigned group, which may have influenced their self-reporting. While we aimed to maintain blinding in outcome assessment, we acknowledge this potential bias in adherence evaluation.

Outcome Measures and Measurement Tools

Primary outcome: Time to UI recovery, defined as the number of days postoperatively until patients used no pads or only one safety pad within 24 hours without leakage.

Secondary outcome: Scores on the International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire–Short Form (ICIQ-SF) at 1, 3, and 6 months postoperatively.

Exploratory analysis (adherence evaluation): Adherence in the rehabilitation group was retrospectively evaluated as follows: (a) High adherence: completion of ≥80% of scheduled outpatient rehabilitation sessions and ≥80% home-training completion based on the rehabilitation diary. (b) Low adherence: completion of <80% of outpatient or home-training requirements. Adherence evaluation was based on therapist records combined with rehabilitation diaries.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using independent-samples t-tests. Categorical variables were expressed as number (%) and compared using χ2 tests. Kaplan–Meier analysis was used to assess UI recovery, with the log-rank test for group comparisons. Multivariate Cox proportional-hazards regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of postoperative UI recovery after RP. Repeated-measures analysis of variance was used to evaluate changes in ICIQ-SF scores over time. In addition, for exploratory analyses, one-way analysis of variance was used to compare outcomes between high- and low-adherence subgroups within the rehabilitation group, and multivariate logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with adherence. A p-value <.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline Characteristics Analysis

A total of 169 patients were included in the final analysis, with 85 in the rehabilitation group and 84 in the conventional group. As shown in Table 1, there were no significant differences between groups in age, BMI, preoperative PSA, prostate volume, surgical approach, or Gleason score, indicating good baseline comparability.

UI Recovery Outcomes

Compared with the conventional group, patients receiving staged rehabilitation demonstrated significantly better recovery at all postoperative time points. As shown in Table 2, at 1, 3, and 6 months postoperatively, the recovery rates in the rehabilitation group were significantly higher [12 (14.1%) vs. 4 (4.8%), 33 (38.8%) vs. 20 (23.8%), and 63 (74.1%) vs. 40 (47.6%), respectively; all p < .05], with the intergroup differences progressively increasing over time. Similarly, the rehabilitation group showed significantly lower ICIQ-SF scores at 1, 3, and 6 months (all p < .05), indicating that staged rehabilitation effectively alleviated the severity of UI symptoms. Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed significantly different urinary continence recovery curves between the two groups (Log-rank test, P=0.00042) (Figure 1), with the rehabilitation group showing a faster recovery, indicating the benefit of rehabilitation intervention for post-operative continence recovery.

Cox Regression Analysis of Factors Associated With UI Recovery

Cox regression analysis (Tables 3 and 4) identified the nursing model, age, and Gleason score as independent predictors of postoperative UI recovery. Patients receiving staged rehabilitation had a 2.048-fold higher probability of short-term UI recovery compared with those receiving routine care (hazard ratio [HR] = 2.048, 95% CI: 1.326–3.164, p < .001). Age (HR = 0.765, 95% CI: 0.713–0.820, p < .001) and Gleason score (HR = 0.551, 95% CI: 0.401–0.757, p < .001) were negatively associated with recovery, suggesting lower recovery probability in older patients or those with higher Gleason scores. BMI, prostate volume, PSA-related parameters, operative time, surgical approach, and intraoperative blood loss were not independent prognostic factors (all p > .05).

Impact of Adherence on UI Recovery

Subgroup analysis within the rehabilitation group showed that adherence had a significant impact on postoperative UI recovery (Table 5). High-adherence patients had markedly higher recovery rates at 3 and 6 months compared with low-adherence patients [26 (54.2%) vs. 7 (18.9%), p = .001; 40 (83.3%) vs. 23 (62.2%), p = .027]. Their ICIQ-SF scores at 1, 3, and 6 months were also significantly lower (all p < .01). These findings indicate that higher adherence further enhances the beneficial effects of staged rehabilitation.

Multivariate Logistic Regression Analysis of Factors Associated With Adherence

Multivariate logistic regression analysis of the rehabilitation group (Table 6) showed that age was the only independent predictor of high adherence (OR = 0.713, 95% CI: 0.600–0.848, p < .001). This indicates that older patients were less likely to achieve high adherence. BMI, prostate volume, PSA-related parameters, operative variables, and Gleason score were not significantly associated with adherence (all p > .05).

Discussion

This retrospective study systematically evaluated the impact of staged rehabilitation training on the recovery of UI after RP. Three key findings emerged. First, staged rehabilitation training served as an independent and effective intervention that significantly improved recovery rates and accelerated symptom relief. Second, the effect of rehabilitation demonstrated a clear dose–response relationship: higher adherence was associated with greater clinical benefit. Third, age played a dual negative role, functioning both as an independent risk factor for UI recovery and as a major barrier to rehabilitation adherence. Together, these findings provide important theoretical and practical guidance for optimizing postoperative rehabilitation strategies.

Our results confirmed the beneficial role of staged rehabilitation in promoting post-RP UI recovery. Compared with the routine care group, patients in the rehabilitation group showed significantly higher recovery rates and lower ICIQ-SF scores at 1, 3, and 6 months. These outcomes correspond to the neuromuscular mechanisms underlying pelvic rehabilitation, including enhanced contraction of type II pelvic floor fibers, improved endurance of type I fibers, increased urethral closure pressure, and better α-motor neuron recruitment and neuromuscular control (Mehnert et al., 2009; Wang et al., 2024). Enhanced regional blood flow may further support neural repair and remodeling (Bo, 2024). These mechanisms collectively form the physiological foundation of continence restoration.

Multivariate Cox analysis demonstrated that staged rehabilitation was an independent protective factor for UI recovery even after adjustment for confounders such as age and tumor characteristics. Importantly, the between-group differences continued to widen over time, suggesting a sustained effect of rehabilitation mediated through long-term neuromuscular adaptation. Such adaptations may include improved motor unit recruitment, enhanced neuromuscular coordination (Del Vecchio et al., 2019), fiber-type transitions, and increased capillary density (Milios et al., 2019). Continued skill acquisition and reinforcement through repetitive training may also contribute. Staged rehabilitation should be regarded not merely as a short-term symptomatic intervention but as a long-term remodeling process of the neuromuscular continence system. These findings highlight the need to incorporate structured and sustained rehabilitation protocols into standard post-RP care.

Adherence emerged as an important determinant of recovery. Patients with high adherence achieved markedly superior UI outcomes, including an 83.3% recovery rate at 6 months. This dose-response pattern is consistent with previous findings indicating that adherence >80% can shorten continence recovery by approximately 30%. However, achieving satisfactory adherence remains a clinical challenge; previous studies indicate that only about 60% of patients complete their prescribed rehabilitation programs (Villa-Del-Pino et al., 2025), aligning with our observations.

Age demonstrated a complex and dual impact on recovery. Increasing age was not only an independent predictor of poorer UI outcomes but also the strongest determinant of reduced rehabilitation adherence. This pattern is consistent with findings by Frawley et al. (2015). From a physiological standpoint, aging is associated with reduced pelvic floor muscle mass, fatty infiltration, selective loss of type II fibers (de Carvalho Fusco et al., 2021), and diminished tissue repair capacity, collectively weakening urethral sphincter function and slowing postoperative recovery. From a behavioral perspective, older patients may face cognitive and motivational barriers that limit their ability to maintain long-term training. These physiological and behavioral mechanisms interact to produce a reinforcing negative cycle: impaired neuromuscular function slows recovery, while slow progress diminishes motivation, further reducing adherence. This dual mechanism underscores the need for age-tailored rehabilitation strategies.

We also identified Gleason score as an independent predictor of UI recovery. Higher-grade tumors may require wider resection, increasing the likelihood of neurovascular bundle injury and disruption of pelvic support structures. This finding has practical value: Gleason score should be incorporated into preoperative UI risk assessment to guide individualized surgical decisions and postoperative rehabilitation planning. Patients with high-grade disease may benefit from targeted strategies such as nerve-sparing techniques, preoperative pelvic floor training, and intensified early rehabilitation.

This study has limitations. First, its retrospective design carries risks of selection bias and residual confounding despite multivariate adjustment. Second, adherence assessment relied on self-report and medical documentation, which may introduce recall or desirability bias. Notably, adherence to pelvic floor exercises was not tracked in the conventional group, who received only passive written and verbal instruction without supervised follow-up; it is therefore possible that some of these patients engaged in little or no pelvic floor muscle training. This disparity in adherence ascertainment implies that the observed between-group difference reflects a comparison between a supervised rehabilitation program and routine passive care, rather than a strictly controlled comparison of two active training protocols with equivalent compliance. Given the significant role adherence plays in recovery, future studies should consider utilizing objective monitoring tools, such as electronic health records or wearable devices, to reduce the potential for these biases. Third, psychosocial determinants of adherence were not deeply explored. Fourth, the follow-up period was limited to 6 months, whereas continence recovery may continue for up to 1 to 2 years. Finally, as a single-center study, generalizability is limited; multicenter prospective trials are warranted to verify these findings and further refine rehabilitation protocols.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates the beneficial impact of staged rehabilitation training on UI recovery after RP, highlights the dose-response relationship between adherence and recovery, and elucidates the dual negative influence of age on both functional outcomes and adherence. These results provide robust evidence supporting individualized, risk-based rehabilitation strategies. Building a comprehensive intervention framework, including preoperative preventive training, staged postoperative rehabilitation, and long-term follow-up, while prioritizing the needs of older patients, may substantially improve postoperative quality of life and long-term functional recovery in patients undergoing RP.
