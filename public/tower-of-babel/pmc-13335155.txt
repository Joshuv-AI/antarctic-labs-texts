Detection of Pantoea ananatis in cerebrospinal fluid: a case report of central nervous system infection after craniotomy
Authors: Ziyi Zou, Jingyi Hu, Le Zhang, Ailin Li, Man Gao, Ying Xin, Xu Guo, Qin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPantoea ananatis(P. ananatis) is a facultative anaerobic gram-negative bacillus in the Enterobacteriaceae. It is commonly found in plants, soil, and water, rarely causing human infection. Here, we present the first cerebrospinal fluid infection case caused by P. ananatis in humans. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of central nervous system infection caused by P. ananatis. A 49-year-old male patient presented with acute traumatic left parietal subdural hematoma and subarachnoid hemorrhage, for which he underwent emergent intracranial hematoma evacuation and decompressive craniectomy at Tianjin Huanhu Hospital. Postoperatively, he developed intracranial infection with cerebrospinal fluid culture yielding Gram-negative bacteria. Through Matrix-assisted laser desorption ionisation-time of flight mass spectrometry and 16S rDNA gene sequencing, the pathogen was identified as P. ananatis. The bacterium exhibited high susceptibility to multiple antibiotics. Following treatment with Isepamicin, the patient recovered and was discharged from the hospital. Background

The genus Pantoea is a group of yellow-pigmented, Gram-negative rod in the Enterobacteriaceae. It can be isolated from water bodies, soil, and plant surfaces. The genus comprises approximately 20 species with diverse characteristics and applications, including Pantoea ananatis(P. ananatis), Pantoea agglomerans(P. agglomerans), Pantoea dispersa, Pantoea eucrina and Pantoea septica [1]. They hold significant applications in agriculture, industry, and novel drug development [2, 3]. As a rare pathogen in a clinical setting, Pantoea may cause soft tissue infections, suppurative arthritis, osteomyelitis, bloodstream and urinary tract infections [4–8]. Infection route is frequently associated with penetrating injuries caused by tools contaminated with soil or plant matter [9]. Infections may also related to contaminated venous catheters or foodborne transmission [10–12]. P. ananatis rarely cause human infection, with only a few of global cases reporting its isolation in blood cultures [11, 13, 14]. In central nervous system (CNS) infections, cases of infection caused by P. agglomerans have been reported [15, 16], but intracranial infections due to P. ananatis remain unreported. This paper describes a case of infection following intracranial hematoma evacuation and decompressive craniectomy in a patient with cerebral haemorrhage, where P. ananatis was detected in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) culture.

Case presentation

A 49-year-old male patient sustained a head injury following a blow to the head, resulting in transient loss of consciousness. Upon regaining consciousness, he experienced dizziness, headache, and repeated episodes of nausea and vomiting, prompting admission to a local hospital. A head Computed Tomography (CT) scan revealed left parietal subdural haemorrhage, for which symptomatic treatment was administered locally. Two days later, the patient developed somnolence, recurrent nausea and vomiting, leading to his transfer to Tianjin Huanhu Hospital on December 2nd, 2025. Hypertension and coronary artery disease were found as relevant background diseases. The patient denied history of infectious diseases, allergies, blood transfusions, or genetic disorders. He had not visited epidemic areas and reported no adverse habits such as smoking or alcohol consumption. The routine physical examination on admission included a temperature of 36.5 °C, a pulse rate of 68 beats/min, and a blood pressure of 125/80 mmHg. The patient was lethargic. Upon calling, the patient could open eyes voluntarily and respond, and could perform limb movements as instructed, with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 14. Muscle tone was normal throughout the limbs, with muscle strength graded as IV. Tendinous reflexes were brisk, and no pathological signs were elicited. The APACHE II score was 7. Chest CT revealed inflammation in the right lower lobe with right-sided pleural effusion, cardiac enlargement, and increased pulmonary markings. Follow-up head CT demonstrated intracranial haematomas were markedly increased compared to previous imaging (Fig. 1A). Preliminary diagnosis based on laboratory tests (Table 1) and imaging findings: mild closed craniocerebral injury, traumatic subdural haematoma, traumatic subarachnoid haemorrhage, pneumonia. The patient was immediately admitted for treatment (Fig. 2).

Clinical course of the patient

Under general anesthesia on admission, the patient underwent emergent decompressive craniectomy, evacuation of a 50 mL subdural hematoma, resection of necrotic contused brain tissue, with a drainage tube placed under the skin flap. Significant intraoperative haemorrhage necessitated transfusion of 3.5 units of packed red blood cells and 720 ml plasma. Empirical antimicrobial therapy commenced postoperatively with intravenous administration of 0.4 g of Isepamicin once daily. On postoperative day 8, the patient developed fever with decreased level of consciousness and linguistic function, with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 12. The head CT result indicated intracranial infection (Fig. 1B). Blood samples were immediately sent for laboratory testing, and a lumbar puncture was performed to collect CSF for culture and physical/chemical test. The CSF appeared cloudy with a pale red hue. The laboratory test results (Table 1) showed elevated levels of white blood cells, procalcitonin, CSF white blood cells, CSF protein, and CSF lactate in the patient. The CSF-to-serum glucose ratio was < 0.42, suggesting a possible CNS infection.

CSF aerobic and anaerobic culture bottles were incubated in the BACT/alert® microbial detection system (Merieux, France). The anaerobic bottle reported positive result within 72 h, whilst the aerobic bottle yielded negative result after five days of incubation. Pathogens from the culture bottles were subcultured onto blood agar plate (Autobio, China) and MacConkey agar plate(Autobio, China). After incubation at 37℃ for 24 h, blood agar plate yielded colonies with well-defined margins, smooth opaque surfaces, moist texture, and yellow pigment production (Fig. 3A). On MacConkey agar plate, growth appeared as circular, flat, and slightly reddish colonies (Fig. 3B). We simultaneously subcultured the strain onto nutrient agar plate (Autobio, China) to observe colony morphology, where it produced yellow, circular colonies (Fig. 3C). This also indicates that the growth of this bacterium does not require a high nutritional environment. Microscopic examination of CSF culture revealed typical Gram-negative rod-shaped bacteria (Fig. 3D). For preliminary microbial identification, we conducted an oxidase test (negative result) and a catalase test (positive result). For further strain characterisation, we employed Matrix-assisted laser desorption ionisation-time of flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS)(Autobio, China). MALDI-TOF MS analysis results were confirmed as P. ananatis with 95% confidence through database comparison (Fig. 3E). Concurrently, microbial sequencing was outsourced to Beijing RuiBio BioTech Co, where microbial identification was performed via 16S rDNA amplification and sequence comparison. Two primers were employed for this sequencing: 27 F (5’-AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG-3’) and 1492R (5’-GGTTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3’). The generating sequence has 99.62% similarity to the P. ananatis sequence (GenBank accession number OL794326.1) in the BLAST database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/BLAST). This sequence is presented in Supplementary Material 1. Both identification methods yielded results consistent with P. ananatis. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was conducted using the AutoMic-i600 fully automated antimicrobial susceptibility testing instrument (Autobio, China). The susceptibility report is presented in Supplementary Material 2. This bacterial strain is susceptible to all tested antibiotics. The patient continued treatment with 0.4 g of Isepamicin once daily. On postoperative day 11, follow-up CSF examination revealed a reduction in CSF WBC count to 140 × 106 /L and CSF protein to 1.07 g/L. And the patient’s body temperature had fallen, indicating effective anti-infective therapy. On day 14, the complete blood count showed a further decrease in WBC count to 8.79 × 109 /L, procalcitonin had also decreased to 0.048 ng/ml, and the patient’s body temperature decreased to 37.1 °C. On day 17, the complete blood count showed WBC at 5.63 × 109 /L with a neutrophil percentage of 77.1%. The patient’s symptoms of infection disappered and initiated hyperbaric oxygen therapy. The patient recovered and was discharged on day 29.

Discussion

P. ananatis is a rare pathogenic bacterium in CNS infection, with no relevant reports available to date. This case highlights that even rare human pathogens such as P. ananatis may cause CNS infection, particularly in immunocompromised state following craniotomy. Neurosurgical procedures, characterized by prolonged duration and significant invasiveness, carry an inherent risk of intracranial infections. Contributing factors include prolonged exposure of cerebral tissue to the ambient environment, intraoperative disruption of the blood-brain barrier, and the placement of drainage catheters, which collectively increase the vulnerability to microbial invasion [17]. CNS infection after craniotomy is a highly hazardous complication, which can lead to increased morbidity, mortality, reoperation, cost, and prolonged hospital stays postoperatively [18]. The incidence of CNS infection after craniotomy is as high as 10%. Factors such as CSF leakage, surgical duration > 4 h, use of glucocorticoids and age are all associated with the occurrence of CNS infection after craniotomy [19]. Common pathogenic bacteria include Staphylococcus aureus, Acinetobacter baumannii, Enterobacter cloacae, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa [20]. In CNS infections, cerebral abscesses have been reported follow in P.agglomerans infection in patients undergoing decompressive craniectomy for cerebral infarction [16]. However, human infections with P. ananatis are considerably rarer.

P. ananatis is a genus of Gram-negative bacilli in the Enterobacteriaceae, characterised by yellow pigment production, and widespread environmental distribution. It could be isolated from plant surfaces, soil, water bodies, and human faeces [21]. First discovered in 1928, which caused pineapples rot in the Philippines, it was initially regarded as a plant pathogen [1]. Subsequent research revealed that certain strains possess ice-nucleating activity, some of strains have antibacterial and antifungal properties, which enable P. ananatis to hold promise as biocontrol agent [22]. The antibacterial metabolites produced by P. ananatis also hold broad prospects in novel drug development [23]. Previous reports have documented P. ananatis-associated bloodstream infections. Additionally, P. ananatis DNA has been detected in synovial fluid samples from two arthritis patients [24]. However, isolation of P. ananatis from CSF remains unprecedented. A potential route of infection arises when patients come into contact with bacteria-contaminated medical devices, with the infection in such case potentially initiated via indwelling catheters.

The positivity rate for bacterial detection in CSF is low, particularly in specimens obtained after antimicrobial therapy. In this case, CSF cultures were performed eight days after antibiotic administration, which may explain why bacteria was detected only in anaerobic bottle while aerobic bottle yielded negative result. Laboratory results showed substantial red blood cells and WBCs in the CSF, the platelet count had also increased, consistent with intracranial infection following subarachnoid haemorrhage surgery. However, the patient exhibited mild infection symptoms, with only a modest elevation in WBC count on complete blood count and a slight increase in procalcitonin. The increase in body temperature was also not significant. Crucially, the CSF WBC count decreased rapidly within three days of infection onset. This may indicate that P. ananatis exhibits low virulence, and its high susceptibility to antibiotics facilitates its eradication. Even considering the relatively low blood-brain barrier permeability of aminoglycosides administered intravenously due to their large molecular weight [25], our antimicrobial therapy remained effective. Reports indicate that P. agglomerans exhibits low virulence even in immunocompromised hosts [11], and this case suggests the same condition for P. ananatis. Nevertheless, its infection risk remains significant, with reports of β-lactam antibiotic-resistant P. ananatis isolated from immunocompetent patients with bacteraemia [14]. Reports also indicate that Pantoea infections carry a fatal risk in children with pneumonia complications, particularly carbapenem-resistant strains [26]. Even in the faeces of healthy adults, Pantoea carrying β-lactamase genes can be isolated [27]. Therefore, heightened vigilance regarding Pantoea-induced intracranial infections is neccesery.

Previous reports indicate that the VITEK MS system misidentifies over 10% of clinical Pantoea isolates [28], suggesting clinical infections caused by Pantoea are likely more prevalent than currently recognised. Advancements in techniques such as MALDI-TOF MS or 16 S rDNA analysis may reveal further cases of infections caused by Pantoea. MALDI-TOF MS relies on detecting the mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) of bacterial ribosomal proteins, facilitating rapid generation of unique bacterial mass spectra [29]. These mass signals generate profile spectra, which consist of a series of peaks that remain conserved at the genus, species, and even subspecies levels [30].

Conclusion

In summary, we report the first isolation of P. ananatis from CSF specimen of a patient with CNS infection. This demonstrates that rare human pathogens can breach the blood-brain barrier to cause infection following neurosurgical procedures. The successful treatment of this case further underscores that early diagnosis of postoperative infections in neurosurgical patients is critical to overall prognosis. The selection of appropriate antimicrobial agents, administration routes, and treatment duration is paramount for clinical outcomes.
