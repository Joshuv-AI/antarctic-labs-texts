Effects of Cucurbita pepo seed extract on behavioral and neuroinflammatory changes in D-galactose-induced brain model
Authors: Minza Saher, Aisha Aziz, Saba Majeed, Arzoo Ajaz, Sabiha Gul, Somia Gul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAging is associated with progressive neuronal dysfunction driven by oxidative stress and neuroinflammation. Cucurbita pepo L. (pumpkin) is rich in bioactive compounds, including phenolics, flavonoids, carotenoids, tocopherols, and unsaturated fatty acids, with antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. These characteristics suggest that C. pepo may have a neuroprotective role against age-related neuronal damage. The aim of the present study was to evaluate the neuroprotective potential of ethanolic C. pepo seed extract in a D-galactose-induced brain aging model.MethodsHealthy adult Wistar albino male rats were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 6 each): control, D-galactose-induced disease, C. pepo seed extract treated, and donepezil-treated groups. Behavioral assessments were performed using light/dark, open field, and sucrose intake tests. Neuroinflammatory markers comprising tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) were evaluated using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays and PCR, followed by biochemical and histopathological analyses.ResultsD-galactose administration significantly impaired behavioral performance, reducing the time spent in the light compartment, open field activities (grooming and rearing), and sucrose intake (p < 0.001). Treatment with C. pepo L. seed extract significantly improved these behavioral parameters (p < 0.01). The extract also markedly reduced the TNF-α levels (0.20 ± 0.04, p < 0.001) and IL-6 expression (0.09 ± 0.02, p < 0.001), indicating attenuation of aging-associated neuroinflammation.ConclusionsIn this study, pumpkin seed extract was associated with modulation of neuroinflammatory markers and improvements in behavioral changes in an experimental model of brain aging.Keywords: Aging, Cucurbita pepo L., Neurodegeneration, Neuroinflammation, Oxidative stress

Introduction

Preclinical models remain an essential tool in biomedical research, providing controlled systems for investigating cellular, molecular, and physiological responses to bioactive compounds.1 Advanced platforms are emerging such as organ-on-a-chip and in silico models but they still cannot fully replicate the systemic complexity of whole-organism biology. Therefore, animal models remain the gold standard for evaluating pharmacological efficacy and toxicological safety in preclinical research.2 These models are crucial for bridging the gap between basic science and clinical translation because they facilitate integrated assessments of disease mechanisms and therapeutic responses under physiological relevant conditions.3

Aging is a multifactorial biological process characterized by the progressive deterioration of physiological functions and increased susceptibility to disease.4 The brain is particularly vulnerable to aging-related alterations, including oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, chronic inflammation, impaired cellular repair mechanisms, and apoptosis, which all contribute to cognitive decline and neurodegeneration.5 Reactive oxygen species (ROS), including the superoxide anion (·O2−) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), are continuously generated in cells and can damage lipids, proteins, and DNA, ultimately leading to neuronal dysfunction. Excessive ROS levels also stimulate inflammatory pathways, upregulating mediators such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), which exacerbate neuroinflammatory damage.6

Preclinical animal models play crucial roles in aging research by facilitating mechanistic studies of age-related biological alterations and evaluating therapeutic interventions under controlled conditions. These models are particularly important for designing translationally relevant studies that reflect aging-associated physiological decline, including conditions commonly noticed in older adults that resemble multimorbidity and polypharmacy. Furthermore, preclinical aging models can help minimize translational bias by providing a structured platform for assessing efficacy, safety, and mechanistic pathways before clinical application, thereby strengthening the predictive validity of experimental therapeutics for age-associated disorders.7, 8, 9

To experimentally model these processes, chronic D-galactose administration is widely used to induce an accelerated aging phenotype. This model closely mimics natural brain aging by inducing oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, synaptic dysfunction, and behavioral impairments. Due to its reproducibility and reliability, the D-galactose model is extensively used for evaluating antioxidant-based neuroprotective strategies.10 Previous studies have demonstrated that natural compounds with antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties can attenuate D-galactose-induced biochemical and behavioral alterations, supporting their therapeutic potential in aging-related neurodegenerative conditions.11

C. pepo L. (pumpkin) seeds are a rich source of bioactive phytochemicals, including phenolic compounds, flavonoids, carotenoids (e.g., lycopene), tocopherols, sterols, and unsaturated fatty acids. These compounds have potent antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and cytoprotective properties. In particular, lycopene is an abundant carotenoid in pumpkin seeds that is capable of scavenging reactive oxidative species through its highly conjugated polyene structure (Figure 1a), and it has also been demonstrated that cucurbitacin and related triterpenoids have anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory activities (Figure 1b). Together, these compounds may contribute to mitigating the oxidative stress-induced neuronal damage and inflammatory responses associated with aging.11

Several plant-derived antioxidants have been investigated in D-galactose-induced aging models, but most previous studies focused primarily on oxidative stress markers, with little integration of behavioral outcomes, neuroinflammatory cytokines, and structural brain changes.12,13 In addition, to the best of our knowledge, limited evidence is available regarding the effects of C. pepo seed extract on neuroinflammatory mediators such as TNF-α and IL-6, and behavioral and histopathological alterations, and thus further investigation is required to address this issue. Therefore, the aims of the present study were to evaluate the integrated effects of C. pepo seed extract on behavioral, molecular, and histopathological parameters in a validated experimental model of brain aging.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals and reagents

D-galactose, donepezil hydrochloride, ethanol, and other analytical grade reagents were purchased from standard commercial suppliers, including Sigma Aldrich , (St. Louis, MO, USA), BDH Laboratory Supplies (Poole, England, UK) and ATCO Laboratories (Karachi, Pakistan). All chemicals were analytical grade and used without further purification.

Collection and authentication of plant materials

C. pepo L. seeds were purchased from a local herbal store in Karachi, Pakistan, during 2021. The plant materials were submitted for taxonomic identification and authentication to the Department of Botany, Pakistan Council of Scientific and Industrial Research (PCSIR) where voucher specimen no. FMRC/Herb./0128/23 was assigned and deposited for future reference.

Following authentication, the seeds were thoroughly washed, cleaned, and air dried at room temperature. The dried seeds were then ground into a fine powder under controlled conditions using a mechanical grinder to prevent the degradation of heat-sensitive constituents.

Preparation of ethanolic extract

The powdered C. pepo seed extract was subjected to Soxhlet extraction using 95% ethanol (C2H5OH) as an extraction solvent. Extraction was continued until complete exhaustion of the plant material. The extract was concentrated under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator, and the resulting semisolid mass was stored at 2–8 °C in airtight, amber-colored containers to protect from light and moisture until further use. Prior to administration, the dried ethanolic extract was freshly reconstituted in 0.9% normal saline to obtain the required concentration for oral gavage. Donepezil was prepared in a similar manner. D-galactose was dissolved in 1% dimethyl sulfoxide for intraperitoneal administration. Oral therapies were given at a dose volume of 1 mg/kg body weight, and D-galactose was administered intraperitoneally at 0.5 mL/kg. The plant seed extract yield was 33.33% (w/w), and the comparable yields across batches indicated acceptable batch–batch consistency. Marker compounds were not quantified in the present study, but previous Fourier transform-infrared spectroscopy and gas chromatography–mass spectrometry analyses of C. pepo seed detected the presence of flavonoids, alkaloids, saponins, steroids, fatty acids, and other bioactive constituents.14

Stability was maintained by minimizing repeated freeze–thaw cycles and limiting exposure to environmental factors. The extract was used within an appropriate time frame under controlled storage conditions to preserve its integrity.

Experimental animals and treatments

Twenty-four healthy adult male Wistar albino rats weighing 200–250 g were obtained from the animal house facility of Jinnah University for Women, Karachi, Pakistan. Animals were housed under standard laboratory conditions (22 ± 2 °C, relative humidity 50–60%, and 12 h/12 h light/dark cycle) with free access to pellet diet and water ad libitum. Following an acclimatization period of 1 week, rats were randomly allocated to treatments using the random number method (n = 6 per group).15,16

•Group I (Control): Received 0.9% normal saline (2 mL/kg, oral gavage) once daily for 10 weeks.

Group I (Control): Received 0.9% normal saline (2 mL/kg, oral gavage) once daily for 10 weeks.

•Group II (D-galactose): Received D-galactose (200 mg/kg, intraperitoneally) once daily for 10 weeks to induce aging-related changes.

Group II (D-galactose): Received D-galactose (200 mg/kg, intraperitoneally) once daily for 10 weeks to induce aging-related changes.

•Group III (C. pepo + D-galactose): Received ethanolic C. pepo seed extract (100 mg/kg, oral gavage) and D-galactose (200 mg/kg, intraperitoneally) once daily for 10 weeks.

Group III (C. pepo + D-galactose): Received ethanolic C. pepo seed extract (100 mg/kg, oral gavage) and D-galactose (200 mg/kg, intraperitoneally) once daily for 10 weeks.

•Group IV (Donepezil + D-galactose): Received donepezil (2 mg/kg, oral gavage) and D-galactose (200 mg/kg, intraperitoneally) once daily for 10 weeks.

Group IV (Donepezil + D-galactose): Received donepezil (2 mg/kg, oral gavage) and D-galactose (200 mg/kg, intraperitoneally) once daily for 10 weeks.

After completing the 10-week treatment period, behavioral assessments were performed comprising light/dark box, open-field, and sucrose intake tests, before tissue collection and subsequent biochemical and molecular analyses.

All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals as described by Hubrecht and Kirkwood.17 The study was designed and reported in compliance with the ARRIVE (Animal Research Reporting of In Vivo Experiments) guidelines for animal research.18

Rationale for dose selection

The dose of D-galactose (200 mg/kg) was selected based on well-established experimental protocols for the induction of accelerated aging in rodent models.19,20 The dose of C. pepo seed extract (100 mg/kg) was selected based on previous studies that demonstrated its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory potential in experimental animal models.21 In addition, the selected dose was supported by preliminary pilot investigations and acute/chronic toxicity studies conducted in accordance with OECD (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development) Guidelines 425 (Guidelines for the Testing of Chemicals, Section 4 Test No. 425), which demonstrated acceptable safety and tolerability with no observed adverse effects at the test dose levels.22 Based on these findings, 100 mg/kg was selected as a safe and effective dose for evaluation in the present study. A positive control group received donepezil hydrochloride (2 mg/kg, oral gavage) in addition to D-galactose administration. Donepezil was selected as a reference standard due to its well-established acetylcholinesterase inhibitory, cognitive enhancing, and neuroprotective effects in experimental models of neurodegeneration.23, 24, 25, 26

Behavioral assessments

Behavioral tests were conducted during the light phase (09:00–14:00 h) under controlled environmental conditions. Animals were acclimatized to the testing room for at least 30 min prior to testing. All apparatus was cleaned with 70% C2H5OH between trials to eliminate olfactory cues. Behavioral observations were all performed by an investigator who was blinded to treatment allocation to minimize observer bias.

Light/dark box test

Each rat was placed in the light compartment and observed for 5 min. The times spent in the light and dark compartments were recorded.

Open field test

Each rat was placed in the center of the arena and observed for 5 min. Grooming and rearing behaviors were recorded as indices of exploratory and anxiety-related activities.

Sucrose intake test

Animals were habituated to 1% sucrose solution before testing. During the test period, rats were given free access to sucrose solution and water for 24 h.

Molecular analysis (RT-PCR)

Brain tissues were harvested following euthanasia for cytokine gene expression analysis. Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent and reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) with a Thermo Fisher Scientific reverse transcription kit according to the manufacturer's instructions.

The relative gene expression levels of TNF-α and IL-6 were analyzed by conventional PCR. β-actin was used as an internal housekeeping gene for normalization. Gene-specific primers were designed using NCBI databases (Table 1) and amplified with DreamTaq Green PCR Master Mix (2 × , Thermo Fisher Scientific).

PCR products were analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis, and band intensities were visualized under ultraviolet illumination. Densitometric band intensity analysis was performed using ImageJ software and the relative gene expression level was calculated as the ratio of the target gene band's intensity relative to the β-actin band's intensity. The primer set was based on previously published conventional RT-PCR protocols and generated amplicons of the expected size, including a product of approximately 896 bp for IL-6. Long amplicons are used less commonly in quantitative real-time PCR assays but they are acceptable for conventional end-point RT-PCR applications where the specificity of amplification is confirmed by the presence of a single discrete band with the expected size. The original uncropped gel images used for densitometric analysis are presented in the Supplementary material (Figures S1 and Figures S2).

To minimize analytical bias, tissue samples were coded prior to molecular analysis and group identifiers were disclosed only after completing data collection and analysis. All molecular experiments were performed in triplicate under identical experimental conditions to ensure the reproducibility and consistency of the findings.

Serum IL-6 estimation

Serum IL-6 levels were quantified using a commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Cloud-Clone, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Blood samples were centrifuged to obtain serum and stored at −40 °C until their analysis. Serum samples were added to microplates pre-coated with IL-6 antibodies, before incubating with biotin-conjugated detection antibodies and streptavidin–horseradish peroxidase. The optical density was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

IL-6 concentrations were calculated based on a standard calibration curve and expressed as pg/mL.

Histopathological analysis

Brain tissues, including the cortex and hippocampus, were fixed in 10% formalin, embedded in paraffin, sectioned to a thickness of 4 μm, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Sections were examined under a light microscope to assess histological changes. Representative images were captured for analysis.

Histopathological alterations were evaluated semi-quantitatively based on the neuronal density, cytoplasmic integrity, nuclear morphology, and tissue architecture. A neurodegeneration score ranging from 0 to 4 was assigned, where 0 = normal morphology with no detectable damage; 1 = minimal alterations; 2 = mild to moderate alterations; 3 = moderate to severe alterations; and 4 = severe alterations characterized by extensive neuronal damage, vacuolation, pyknosis, and tissue architecture disruption.

Statistical analysis

Before conducting statistical analyses, the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene's tests, respectively. After confirming that these assumptions were satisfied, differences between treatments were detected using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Given the relatively small sample size (n = 6 per group), data were also examined to detect potential outliers and ensure the robustness of the analysis. Data were analyzed using SPSS software (version 20, IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). All graphs and data visualizations were generated using GraphPad Prism (version 9.5.0, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Exact p-values were calculated and differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. The sample size (n = 6 per group) was selected based on previous studies that utilized D-galactose-induced aging models and in accordance with the principle of reduction in animal experimentation.27

Qualitative phytochemical screening of the ethanolic extract of C. pepo seeds confirmed the presence of multiple bioactive constituents, including carbohydrates, flavonoids, phenols, tannins, glycosides, terpenoids, and coumarins, whereas proteins, saponins, steroids, sterols, and quinones were absent (Table 2). The presence of flavonoids and phenolic compounds indicated that the extract contained bioactive constituents with known antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities.

Behavioral assessment

Anxiety-like behavior (light/dark box test)

One-way ANOVA detected significant differences among the experimental treatments (F = 43.799, p < 0.001). As shown in Table 3, rats treated with D-galactose spent less time in the light compartment (18.3 ± 2.40 s) compared with the control (126.0 ± 9.01 s; p < 0.001). Administering C. pepo seed extract (100 mg/kg) significantly increased the time spent in the light compartment (80.33 ± 5.09 s) compared with D-galactose-treated rats (p < 0.001). A similar increase was observed in the donepezil-treated rats (75.6 ± 6.67 s; p < 0.001) compared with D-galactose-treated rats. These findings are illustrated in Figure 2.

In the control rats, the hippocampal architecture was preserved with normal cellular morphology (Figure 9a). By contrast, marked histopathological alterations were found under D-galactose treatment, including neuronal shrinkage, pyknotic nuclei, vacuolation, and disrupted tissue organization (Figure 9b). Fewer histological abnormalities were observed in rats treated with C. pepo seed extract, and the neuronal structure and tissue organization were better preserved compared with D-galactose-treated rats (Figure 9c). A similar neuroprotection pattern was observed in donepezil-treated rats (Figure 9d).

Semi-quantitative histopathological scoring obtained neurodegeneration scores of 0, 4, 2, and 1 for the control rats and those treated with D-galactose, C. pepo, and donepezil, respectively (Table 9), indicating attenuation of D-galactose-induced structural damage following treatment.

Discussion

The effects of C. pepo seed extract in a D-galactose-induced model of brain aging were evaluated in the present study. The results demonstrated that D-galactose administration was associated with behavioral impairments, increased expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, elevated serum IL-6 levels, and histopathological alterations in hippocampal tissue, suggesting inflammation-associated neurobiological changes in this experimental model. Treatment with C. pepo seed extract was associated with improvements in anxiety-like and exploratory behaviors, lower TNF-α and IL-6 gene expression, reduced serum IL-6 levels, and neuronal architecture preservation. Collectively, these findings suggest that C. pepo seed extract may have protective roles against aging-associated neurobiological alterations.

D-galactose-induced aging is a widely used experimental model that reproduces several features of brain aging, including behavioral dysfunction, inflammation-related changes, oxidative stress, and structural neuronal damage.13,28 Inflammatory processes are increasingly recognized as major contributors to age-related neuronal dysfunction and neurobiological decline.29 Previous studies have shown that chronic D-galactose exposure is associated with increased oxidative stress, accumulation of advanced glycation end products, and activation of inflammatory pathways, ultimately contributing to neuronal dysfunction and behavioral abnormalities.30 Thus, the behavioral alterations observed in the present study are consistent with previous scientific investigations that utilized similar aging models.

Major findings obtained in this study were the improvement in anxiety-like behavior and enhanced exploratory activity following treatment with C. pepo seed extract. Animals treated with the extract spent more time in the light compartment and exhibited greater grooming and rearing activity compared with D-galactose-treated rats, thereby suggesting attenuation of anxiety-like behavior and partial restoration of exploratory activity in the experimental model of aging. However, it should be noted that these behavioral measures primarily reflect anxiety-related and locomotory/exploratory responses, and are not direct assessments of learning or memory. Similar findings were obtained in previous studies that investigated plant-derived extracts rich in antioxidant and anti-inflammatory phytochemicals, which were associated with improved behavioral performance in aging and neurodegenerative models.31, 32, 33, 34 The precise mechanisms associated with the extract were not investigated in the present study, but the behavioral improvements observed following C. pepo administration are consistent with its previously reported biological activities.

Reduced sucrose intake has been widely used as an indicator of anhedonia-like behavior in experimental models of aging and neurodegeneration.35,36 Consistent with previous studies, sucrose intake was markedly lower in D-galactose-treated animals than controls.37,38 C. pepo treatment improved several behavioral parameters but its effect on sucrose preference was less pronounced than that observed with donepezil, possibly because different behavioral domains responded differently to treatment, or the test dose and duration could have been insufficient to substantially influence reward-related behavioral pathways. It is also possible that higher doses or longer treatment durations may produce greater effects on sucrose intake, but this was not evaluated in the present study because only a single dose level was tested. Further investigations using multiple dose levels are required to clarify this effect.

Inflammatory processes are increasingly recognized as major contributors to age-related neuronal dysfunction and neurobiological decline. In particular, TNF-α and IL-6 play crucial roles in promoting inflammatory signaling and neuronal injury pathways.39,40 In the present study, D-galactose administration was associated with the increased expression of both cytokines, whereas treatment with C. pepo seed extract was associated with significantly lower TNF-α and IL-6 expression levels. In this study, neuroinflammatory status was inferred indirectly through hippocampal gene expression analysis and peripheral serum IL-6 levels rather than by directly quantifying brain tissue cytokine protein levels.41 Our findings are consistent with previous studies that found anti-inflammatory effects of plant-derived bioactive compounds in experimental models of neurological disorders.42 The underlying molecular mechanisms were not directly examined in the present study but the observed reduction in pro-inflammatory cytokine expression suggests that modulation of inflammatory processes may have contributed to the observed neuroprotective effects.

Analysis of the serum IL-6 levels also supported the molecular observations. Elevated IL-6 levels are associated with chronic low-grade inflammation and age-related pathological processes. Animals treated with C. pepo seed extract had lower serum IL-6 levels than those treated with D-galactose, indicating a potential systemic anti-inflammatory effect. Central cytokine levels were not directly assessed but circulating IL-6 may serve as an indirect indicator of systemic inflammatory status, and it is often associated with central neurobiological alterations in experimental models.43 Communication between peripheral and central inflammatory pathways has been implicated in neurodegeneration,44 so the reduced circulating IL-6 level may be relevant to the overall protective profile observed in this study, although further mechanistic studies are required to explore this relationship.

Histopathological examinations provided structural evidence that supported the behavioral and molecular findings. D-galactose administration was associated with marked neuronal damage in the hippocampal CA1 region, characterized by neuronal shrinkage, nuclear pyknosis, vacuolation, and tissue architecture disruption, consistent with previously reported models of accelerated brain aging.45,46 Treatment with C. pepo seed extract was associated with comparatively reduced histopathological alterations and improved hippocampal structure preservation, suggesting attenuation of D-galactose-induced neurodegenerative changes. These findings agree with those obtained in previous studies that reported neuroprotective effects of plant-derived extracts with antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties in experimental aging models.47 However, our observations are descriptive in nature and should be interpreted alongside molecular and behavioral findings to support the overall neuroprotective effects.

The observed effects may have been partly related to the phytochemical composition of C. pepo seed extract. Qualitative screening confirmed the presence of flavonoids, phenolic compounds, tannins, terpenoids, glycosides, and coumarins. These phytochemical classes have been extensively investigated because of their antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and cytoprotective properties.48,49 Previous studies have shown that phenolic-rich plant extracts may modulate inflammatory responses and support neuronal integrity under conditions of oxidative stress.50,51 The phytochemical profile identified in the present study is consistent with previous analyses of C. pepo seed extract, and it provides a plausible biological basis for the observed behavioral, molecular, and histopathological findings. However, individual compounds were not isolated or quantified, so the specific constituents responsible for the observed effects were not determined in the present study.

The present study had several notable strengths. In particular, a well-established D-galactose-induced aging model was employed to investigate neuroinflammation-associated behavioral and molecular alterations. A multi-level experimental approach was used by integrating behavioral assessments, analysis of pro-inflammatory cytokine gene expression (TNF-α and IL-6), serum cytokine evaluation, and histopathological examination of brain tissue. The inclusion of donepezil as a standard reference drug further strengthened the comparative and translational relevance of the findings. In addition, observer blinding and standardized experimental conditions were implemented to reduce bias in the behavioral assessments.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. A key limitation of this study was the lack of direct quantification of brain tissue cytokine protein levels (i.e., IL-6 and TNF- α), thereby limiting the ability to conclusively confirm central neuroinflammatory changes. In addition, the acetylcholinesterase activity in the brain was not assessed in the present study. Therefore, the contributions of cholinergic mechanisms to the observed neuroprotective effects could not be directly determined. Furthermore, evaluating only a single dose of C. pepo seed extract prevented the establishment of a dose–response relationship and optimal therapeutic range. Phytochemical analysis indicated the presence of flavonoids and phenolic compounds that could account for the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities. However, phytochemical profiling via HPLC, LC–MS, and quantifying the total phenolic and flavonoid contents may further facilitate identification of the bioactive constituents, and provide deeper mechanistic insights into the observed pharmacological effects.

The observed behavioral and histopathological improvements suggest that C. pepo seed extract has neuroprotective potential. Comprehensive future investigations including immunohistochemical evaluations using neuronal and glial markers (such as NeuN, GFAP, Iba-1, caspase-3, and synaptic proteins), as well as oxidative stress biomarkers (superoxide dismutase, catalase, glutathione peroxidase, ROS, and lipid peroxidation) and relevant neuroinflammatory signaling pathways would provide deeper mechanistic insights and strengthen the translational significance of these findings.

Histopathological examination provided evidence to support the observed behavioral and molecular findings, but the assessment was limited to hematoxylin and eosin staining, which primarily provides general morphological information and does not permit detailed neuronal ultrastructure evaluation. The present study was designed as an initial pharmacological evaluation of the neuroprotective potential of C. pepo extract. Gene expression analysis was performed using conventional end-point RT–PCR with densitometric quantification. In previous studies, including that by Almarfadi et al., conventional RT-PCR was successfully utilized to evaluate the expression levels of similar inflammatory gene markers.52 Molecular investigations using quantitative real-time PCR would further enhance the more precise quantification of gene expression and provide directions for future investigations to comprehensively validate the observed findings. Furthermore, the behavioral assessments conducted in this study primarily evaluated anxiety-like, exploratory, and anhedonia-like behaviors rather than learning and memory. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as evidence supporting the neuroprotective potential of C. pepo seeds rather than direct improvement of cognitive function. Our findings are consistent with results obtained by Aranarochana et al. after oral supplementation with plant-derived bioactive compounds in Prunus domestica extract against D-galactose-induced brain aging.53

Previous studies have demonstrated the protective effects of plant-derived bioactive compounds against D-galactose-induced tissue damage, which are comparable to most of our findings, highlighting the neuroprotective potential of C. pepo. In a previous study, D-limonene was shown to attenuate D-galactose-induced skin aging by substantially decreasing pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-6, supporting the similar anti-inflammatory potential of C. pepo seed extract for combating senescence-related damage.54 The phytochemical constituents of Rhus coriaria also have antioxidant activities, highlighting the therapeutic potential of phenolic compounds, as also found in C. pepo seed extract.55 In addition, chia seed oil extract promoted protection by activating the Nrf2/SLC7A11/GPX4 pathway against 5-fluorouracil-induced hepatotoxicity due to the abundant presence of linolenic acid, demonstrating that plant extracts can exert protective effects against oxidative stress-induced tissue damage.56 Moreover, Curcuma longa extract and cranberry extract both had protective effects against inflammation.57,58 These studies support our demonstration that a plant-derived extract exerted beneficial effects by enhancing antioxidant mechanisms, reducing oxidative damage, and preserving tissue integrity, thereby reinforcing its potential as a neuroprotective agent against age-related degenerative changes.

Conclusion

The findings obtained in this study indicate that C. pepo seed extract was associated with improvements in behavioral parameters and favorable modulation of selected neuroinflammatory markers in a D-galactose-induced model of brain aging. These results provide preliminary evidence supporting the potential role of C. pepo seed extract in modulating neuroinflammation-related processes.

In future research, it is necessary to evaluate multiple doses of C. pepo seed extract, conduct biochemical profiling, evaluate validated cognitive paradigms, and validate the molecular mechanism associated with the pharmacological activity using real-time PCR to elucidate the neuroprotective effects, and confirm its therapeutic potential in age-related neurodegenerative conditions.
