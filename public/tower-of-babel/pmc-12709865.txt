The role of deoxycholic acid in macrophage polarization and remodeling of the tumor microenvironment during colorectal adenoma-carcinoma transition
Authors: Yujing Liu, Jie Yin, Lu Lu, Jiashu Pan, Gaoxuan Shao, Yangxian Xu, Guang Ji, Hanchen Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDuring the progression from colorectal adenoma to colorectal cancer (CRC), the inflammatory state of the tumor microenvironment (TME) evolves in parallel with the epithelial‒mesenchymal transition (EMT) process. Among the key players, macrophages serve as pivotal mediators of inflammation-cancer transformation and are closely associated with these dynamic changes. Deoxycholic acid (DCA), a critical bile acid metabolite involved in CRC development, has been implicated in tumorigenesis; however, its role in macrophage polarization and the adenoma-carcinoma sequence remains unclear. In this study, we analyzed clinical cell types and DCA levels in colorectal adenomas and carcinomas to elucidate the correlation between DCA and macrophages in CRC. Furthermore, we investigated the mechanistic role of DCA in promoting M2-like polarization of tumor-associated macrophages and validated the impact of DCA on the EMT-associated Wnt/β-catenin pathway and inflammation-associated NF-κB signaling pathway in CRC model mice. Our findings may provide novel strategies for prognostic biomarkers and precise bile acid metabolism-based interventions in CRC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40170-025-00416-z.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, Deoxycholic acid, Epithelial‒mesenchymal transition, Tumor-associated macrophages

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a major global health threat. According to the latest statistics from the World Health Organization’s International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), the annual number of new CRC cases has exceeded 1.9 million, with approximately 930,000 deaths per year, ranking among the highest in both incidence and mortality among malignant tumors [1]. The pathogenesis of CRC involves a complex, multistep process involving diverse molecular pathways. While hereditary factors contribute substantially, 60–70% of clinical cases are sporadic, progressing slowly over years through the adenoma‒carcinoma sequence—the predominant pathway of carcinogenesis [2].

During this transformation process, the epithelial‒mesenchymal transition (EMT) mechanism plays a pivotal role. EMT drives the malignant progression of CRC by promoting the acquisition of aggressive, metastatic, and drug-resistant phenotypes by tumor cells [3]. This process is characterized by the downregulation of E-cadherin and the upregulation of mesenchymal markers such as N-cadherin and vimentin. In the adenoma-carcinoma model, sequential mutations in the wingless/integrated (WNT), epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), tumor protein 53 (TP53), and transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) signaling pathways over a period of years result in CRC progression [4]. Notably, these genetic mutations are typically related to inflammation during the adenoma–carcinoma sequence. In addition to activating EMT transcription, EMT promotes malignant progression in inflammation-cancer transformation, forming a positive feedback loop that exacerbates the inflammatory microenvironment [5].

The complex tumor microenvironment (TME) includes multiple immune cells. Among them, macrophages, as key immune cells, have emerged as a critical research focus in CRC. Macrophages exhibit significant dynamic changes and functional heterogeneity during different stages of tumor progression, with their phenotypes and functions adapting to the evolving TME [6]. In the adenoma/inflammatory stage, they predominantly display an M1-like (proinflammatory/antitumor) phenotype. During the carcinoma in situ/local invasion stage, a mixed M1/M2 phenotype is observed. In the tumor invasion/metastasis stage, the dominant phenotype shifts to the M2-like phenotype, which primarily exhibits immunosuppressive and premetastatic functions [7]. Given their pivotal role in CRC progression, targeting macrophage phenotypic plasticity has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy in oncology.

In recent years, the relationship between bile acid metabolism dysregulation and CRC development has garnered significant attention. Studies have shown that the level of deoxycholic acid (DCA), a major component of secondary bile acids, is markedly elevated in the CRC microenvironment [8]. Clinical research indicates that the fecal DCA concentration in individuals fed high-fat diets is 2–3 times greater than that in healthy controls and that its level is significantly positively correlated with CRC progression [9]. DCA can shape an immunosuppressive TME conducive to tumor progression by modulating the functions of various immune cells, including tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), T cells, dendritic cells (DCs), natural killer (NK) cells, and neutrophils [10]. Emerging evidence has established DCA as a crucial metabolic mediator that drives immunosuppressive TME remodeling in CRC.

However, the specific molecular mechanisms by which DCA promotes CRC metastasis through TME regulation remain incompletely understood. Therefore, this study aimed to systematically analyze DCA levels and macrophage functions in CRC samples at different disease stages, investigate the regulatory effects of DCA on macrophages, and further elucidate the impact of DCA on the EMT process in CRC. This research provides new theoretical insights into the “metabolism‒immunity” interplay in CRC development and identifies potential novel targets for clinical intervention.

Materials and methods

Patient population and inclusion/exclusion criteria

This study included 24 healthy controls, 30 patients with colorectal adenoma, and 30 patients with primary colorectal cancer. All patients were treated at the Gastrointestinal Surgery Department of Longhua Hospital, Affiliated with Shanghai University of Traditional Chinese Medicine, from 2015 to 2017. Prior to enrollment, the data and information of all patients were documented, and informed consent forms were signed. These data included patient name, sex, age, tumor location, histological type, clinical and pathological grade, preoperative radio chemotherapy, and differentiation level. The TNM staging system was utilized for tumor staging (details seen in Table S1). All tumor samples selected for this study were from primary CRC patients, and both their clinical data and medical records were complete. None of the patients underwent radiotherapy or chemotherapy prior to surgery.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: 1). Patients with colorectal adenoma or colorectal cancer, and adenocarcinoma diagnosed by pathology; 2). At least one definite measurable lesion according to RECIST 1.1 criteria; 3). Age ≥ 18 years old; 4). Patients agree to participate in the clinical study, voluntarily sign the informed consent form, and consent to follow the requirements of the research protocol for visits, examinations, and treatment. The exclusion criteria for patients were as follows: 1). Histological types of colorectal cancer other than adenocarcinoma; 2). Patients with mental disorders who are unable to cooperate with treatment and follow-up; 3). Other malignant diseases (except adequately treated basal cell carcinoma of the skin or carcinoma in situ of the cervix) diagnosed within 5 years before study entry; 4). Other conditions considered unsuitable for inclusion by the investigator. The work was carried out in accordance with the Code of Ethics of the World Medical Association (Declaration of Helsinki) for experiments involving humans.

Detection and analysis of tissue bile acid

Standard solutions and calibration curves

Stock solutions of single standard were prepared at 1 mg/mL in MeOH. Primary mixed standard stock solution(MSS)was prepared by mixing the single standard stock solution, and diluting with MeOH-water (1:1, v/v) to appropriate concentrations. Calibration curves were obtained by diluting the MSS with MeOH-water (1:1, v/v) to the following final concentrations: 100, 40, 16, 6.40, 2.56, 1.02, 0.41, 0.16, 0.07, 0.03, and 0.01 ng/mL. The mixed standard of 12.8ng/mL was selected as the quality control (QC) sample. All solutions and samples were stored at -80 °C until analysis.

UPLC-MS/MS analysis

The bile acids in tissues were analyzed using the following samples: matched tumor and adjacent non-tumor tissues from 30 colorectal cancer patients, and intestinal tissues from 24 healthy individuals. The chromatographic column used was a UPLC BEH C18 column (Waters Corporation). Bile acids were chromatographically separated via linear gradient elution at a flow rate of 0.45 mL/min. The mobile phase consisted of an aqueous solution containing 0.1% formic acid and acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid. The separation conditions were as follows: starting at 5% for 0.5 min, increasing to 25% by 1 min, further increasing to 28% by 3.5 min, reaching 45% by 6 min, 85% by 7 min, 100% by 8 min, maintaining until 9 min, and then returning to the initial gradient for 10 min and maintaining until 11.5 min. The mass spectrometry parameters were as follows: the ion source temperature was 550 °C, the curtain gas pressure was 30 psi, the nebulizer gas pressure was 50 psi, the auxiliary heating gas pressure was 50 psi, and the collision gas pressure was 8 psi. The ion spray voltage was − 4500 V, with entrance and exit voltages both set at -10 V. Multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode was used for qualitative and quantitative analysis. Instrument control and data collection were performed via AB Sciex Analyst software (version 1.6.3). Mass spectrometry data processing: Data analysis was conducted via MultiQuant software with default parameters. Integration, standard curve generation, and quantitative analysis were performed for each metabolite in both samples and calibration curves. The original sample content was calculated on the basis of the concentration values obtained from the sample runs. The results were analyzed to identify three different types of bile acids.

Mass cytometry (CyTOF) assay

CyTOF staining

An EP tube containing 2 mL of tissue storage solution was placed on ice. Freshly collected human colorectal tissue was placed in tissue storage medium during the collection process. The samples were washed once with 1x PBS, and a single-cell suspension was generated. Five minutes of staining with 100 µL of 250 nM cisplatin was performed on ice to exclude dead cells. The samples were incubated in Fc receptor blocking solution and then stained with a mixture of surface antibodies (see Table S2). The antibody mixture was incubated with the cell suspension on ice for 30 min. The cells were washed twice with FACS buffer (1x PBS + 0.5% BSA), and 200 µL of a solution containing Maxpar Fix and Perm Buffer with 250 nM 191/193Ir was added overnight to fix and permeabilize the cells in suspension. After fixation, the cells were washed once with FACS buffer and then with perm buffer, followed by staining with an intracellular antibody mixture for 30 min on ice. The cells were washed and resuspended in deionized water, 20% EQ calibration beads were added, and the results were obtained via mass spectrometry.

CyTOF data acquisition

Utilize a dual-filtering strategy with unique mass tags to remove data for each sample from the raw data. The data were manually gated via FlowJo software to exclude debris, dead cells, and doublets, leaving viable individual immune cells.

CyTOF analysis

Phenotype clustering algorithms are applied to all cells, which are divided into different phenotypes on the basis of marker expression levels. Each cluster’s cell type was annotated on the basis of the expression patterns of the markers in the cluster and heatmap. High-dimensional data are visualized in two dimensions via the t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) algorithm, which shows the distribution of each cluster and marker expression, as well as differences between groups or different sample types.

Cell culture

The normal intestinal epithelial cells NCM460, the human colorectal cancer cell lines LOVO, and HCT116 were purchased from the China Center for Type Culture Collection (Wuhan, China); All cell lines were cultured in DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% penicillin and streptomycin (Beyotime Biotech Co. Ltd., China) at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Human monocytic leukemia cells (THP-1) were passaged and cultured in our laboratory and maintained in RPMI 1640 (Gibco, USA) medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and antibiotics. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were extracted from human peripheral blood provided by Longhua Hospital, Shanghai University of Traditional Chinese Medicine. PBMCs were cultured in our laboratory using DMEM (Gibco, USA) medium supplemented with 10% and 1% penicillin and streptomycin. Cultures were maintained at 37 °C, 95% air humidity, and 5% CO2. Cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeats profiling and confirmed to be mycoplasma negative.

Isolation of human tumor-associated macrophages

Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) were extracted from human colorectal tumor adjacent tissues provided by Longhua Hospital, Shanghai University of Traditional Chinese Medicine. We isolated and extracted TAMs with a Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi #130-095-929). Human colon tumor tissues were minced into 2–4 mm pieces. The tumor tissues were transferred to a gentleMACS C Tube with ice-chilled enzyme solution and inverted onto the gentleMACS Dissociator sleeve. and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min with rotation under No.01 Program. Repeat dissociation using No.02 Program, followed by another 30 min rotation at 37 °C. After processing, the supernatant was discarded. The cell clusters were resuspended in RPMI 1640, filtered through a 70 μm strainer, rinsed with 20 mL of RPMI 1640, and centrifuged at 300 × g for 7 min. The supernatant was removed; purified TAMs were suspended, and cell viability was determined via Trypan blue. Sequencing was performed only if the viability exceeded 85%. Then, the TAMs were seeded in four-well chambers and cultured for 8 h until they adhered for further experiments.

Establishment of tumor and macrophage coculture models

Coculture of LOVO and HCT116 tumor cells: Tumor cells were preseeded in the lower layer of a coculture six-well plate 24 h in advance. THP-1 cells and PBMCs were seeded in six-well plates and stimulated with phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) at 10 ng/mL for 24 h to differentiate into M0 cells. M1 macrophages were generated by stimulating M0 cells with 10 ng/mL LPS and 20 ng/mL IFN-γ for three days. M2 macrophages were generated from M0 cells by stimulation with 10 ng/mL IL-4 and 10 ng/mL IL-13 for three days. M0, M1, and M2 macrophages were then stimulated with DCA for 48 h. M0, M0 + DCA, M1, and M1 + DCA cells were subsequently seeded onto the upper layer of the coculture six-well plate after adhesion. After 48 h of coculture, sublayer cells and supernatants from sublayer tumor cells were collected.

Coculture with NCM460 normal intestinal epithelial cells: Normal intestinal epithelial cells were preseeded in the lower layer of a coculture six-well plate 24 h in advance. M0, M0 + DCA, M1, and M1 + DCA cells were seeded onto the upper layer of the coculture six-well plate after adhesion. After 48 h of coculture, cells from the lower layer and supernatants from normal lower intestinal epithelial cells were collected.

Migration assay

Matrigel was thawed on ice and diluted with precooled serum-free medium (1 mg/ml). A 100 µL dilution was evenly spread on the surface of the upper chamber membrane of the Transwell chamber, incubated at 37 °C for 30 min until the gel solidified, and then the basement membrane was hydrated with serum-free medium.

The cells were digested with trypsin, centrifuged, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended in serum-free medium containing 0.1% BSA. The density was adjusted to 2–5 × 105cells/mL. 100 µL of cell suspension was added to the upper chamber and 600 µL of medium containing 20% FBS was added to the lower chamber. The cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The chambers were removed, washed with PBS, fixed with methanol for 30 min, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min. Nonmigrating cells in the upper chamber were brushed with a cotton swab, and the residual dye was rinsed with PBS. Five to nine fields (400 ×) were randomly selected under the microscope, and the attached cells on the lower chamber side were counted and the mean value was calculated.

Western blot detection

Total protein was extracted from the cells, and the protein content of the cells was measured. Polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, membrane transfer, immunoreaction (hybridization), and chemiluminescence were performed. Subsequently, ImageJ software was used for band analysis, and the grayscale values of each band were obtained for statistical analysis. The expression levels of TMT-related proteins in the human colorectal cancer tumor cell lines HCT116 and LOVO, as well as the expression levels of β-catenin, active β-catenin, P65, and P-P65 in the normal human intestinal epithelial cell line NCM460, were detected and analyzed. The information about the antibodies used can be found in Table S3.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) detection

The concentrations of cytokines were estimated for each experimental condition via ELISA via commercial kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The cytokine kits used included those for IL-1β (Abcam, ab242986), IL-10 (Abcam, ab242491), TNF-α (Mlbio, ml077385), and TGF-β (Multiscience, EK981). Positive controls were supplied with the kit.

Flow cytometry

The macrophages were processed into single-cell suspensions and incubated with antibodies (details in Table S3) for 1 h at 4 °C. The cells were then washed twice with 4 ml of flow buffer, centrifuged, and resuspended in 0.5 mL of flow buffer for analysis. Flow cytometry was performed via a FACS Calibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA). Flow cytometric analysis was performed with FlowJo software (FlowJo, USA).

qRT‒PCR detection

The total RNA in the cells was extracted via TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and then reverse transcribed to complementary DNA via a PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara, Shiga, Japan). The relative expression levels of genes were detected via the QuantStudio™ 7 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The resulting cDNA was used for quantifying gene expression with primers indicated in Table S4 and analyzed using the ΔΔCT method.

Immunofluorescence detection

Frozen sections were fixed, antigen retrieval was performed, BSA blocking was carried out, primary antibody was added, secondary antibody was introduced, DAPI staining was performed, nuclei were sealed, and images were observed under a confocal fluorescence microscope to assess the fluorescence levels of intracellular proteins.

Animal model establishment

C57BL/6 mice and APCmin+ mice (6–8 weeks, 18–22 g, male) were purchased from the Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc. (Shanghai, China). All the mice were housed in a specific-pathogen-free (SPF) environment with a controlled temperature of 20–22 °C, a humidity of 45 ± 5%, and a 12-h light/dark cycle. The mice had ad libitum access to standard chow (AIN93-G) and water. Experiments were performed on mice at Shanghai University of Traditional Chinese Medicine, and all procedures and manipulations were performed in accordance with the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee-approved protocol.

Twenty four C57BL/6 mice were acclimated for one week and were given one injection of 10 mg/kg azoxymethane/dextran sulfate sodium (AOM). This was followed by three cycles of 2.5% dextran sulfate sodium (DSS) administration: each cycle involved 5-days of 2.5% DSS in the drinking water and 16-days of normal water consumption. The intestinal samples from mice at weeks 0, 3, 6, and 12 were collected, respectively. The pathological identification was performed in the abnormal hyperplasia of the intestine to identify polyps or tumors. Count their number and obtain the ratio of tumor/polyp. The spleens of mice were collected and weighed to obtain the ratioof spleen/body. The tissue morphology of polyps was observed through HE staining, including cell structure, glandular arrangement, and infiltration of inflammatory cells. Polyps or tumor tissues from the colon of mice were processed for DCA detection. Simultaneously we observed whether the mice have lymph node metastasis and distant metastasis, and perform DCA detection on tumors with lymph node metastasis and distant metastasis.

Sixty-four C57BL/6 mice were acclimated for one week and were given one injection of 10 mg/kg azoxymethane/dextran sulfate sodium (AOM). This was followed by three cycles of 2.5% dextran sulfate sodium (DSS) administration: each cycle involved one week of 2.5% DSS in the drinking water and one week of normal water consumption. After 12 weeks of AOM + DSS induction, three mice were selected for PET‒CT scans to assess tumor burden. The mice were randomly divided into a group receiving regular drinking water and a group receiving drinking water containing 0.2% DCA. At the experimental endpoint (the 16th week), the mice were euthanized. The intestinal tracts were then harvested for analysis. We quantified the tumor number and volume, measured the colon length, and isolated tumor masses for subsequent HE staining and immunohistochemistry (IHC).

Sixty-four APCmin+ mice were acclimated for one week and divided into groups receiving regular drinking water and groups receiving drinking water containing 0.2% DCA. At the 12th week, eight mice from each group were assessed for tumor burden and macrophage expression level characteristics. Until the 16th week, the mice were euthanized, the intestines were collected, and the tumor masses were isolated for IHC and protein detection.

Small animal PET/CT scanning

C57BL/6 mice were fasted for 4–6 h prior to the administration of the radiotracer¹⁸F-FDG to reduce the degree of serum glucose competition. Following 1–3% isoflurane/oxygen mixture anesthesia induction and maintenance, the mice were positioned in the prone orientation on the scanning bed to ensure respiratory stability. Imaging acquisition began with CT scanning at a 50–80 kV tube voltage and 200–500 µA current for 1–5 min, followed by PET scanning for 10–30 min. Subsequent image reconstruction and fusion analysis were performed via Inveon Research Workplace software.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed via GraphPad Prism 8.0. Data conforming to a normal distribution are described as the mean ± standard deviation (x ± S), whereas data not conforming to a normal distribution are described as the median. For multiple group comparisons, data conforming to a normal distribution were analyzed via one-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA), followed by post hoc assessment via the least significant difference (LSD) method. Data not conforming to a normal distribution were analyzed via nonparametric tests. All tests were considered statistically significant if P < 0.05.

Results

The level of DCA is strongly associated with the development of colorectal neoplasms

To investigate bile acid metabolism and the development of colorectal neoplasms, intestinal tissue samples were obtained from a cohort comprising 30 healthy control (HC) individuals, 30 colorectal adenoma (CRA) patients, and 30 colorectal cancer (CRC) patients (Fig. 1A). All participants underwent comprehensive colonoscopy examinations, and subsequent tissue bile acid profiling was conducted to quantify and characterize the bile acid composition of the collected samples. Upon colonoscopy, polyp-like elevations were visible in the intestinal lumen of CRA patients compared with the smooth lumen of HCs. For the CRC sample, the mucosa in the lesion was dark red, and the boundary with the adjacent normal mucosa was not clear. HE staining revealed disordered and irregularly shaped glands with enlarged hyperchromatic nuclei in the CRA. In CRC, the glandular architecture is further disrupted, resulting in “back-to-back” arrangements and necrotic debris within glandular lumens (Fig. 1B). Through pathological analysis, we also detected the contents of the main bile acids (BAs) in the 3 groups. Fig. 1C shows that secondary BAs in CRC patients were significantly greater than those in HCs and CRAs. Specifically, the content of DCA, a major secondary BA, was markedly increased in CRC (Fig. 1D). In addition, CRC patients exhibited significantly higher DCA contents compared with CRA patients and HCs (Fig. 1E).

The AOM + DSS model is a classic experimental model that effectively recapitulates the pathological process of human chronic inflammation-related CRC. It is well established that colitis predisposes individuals to colorectal tumorigenesis. C57BL/6 mice were intravenously injected with 10 mg/kg AOM once and given 2.5% DSS in the drinking water for 5 days, followed by normal water for 16 days, for a total of 4 cycles (Fig. 1F). We found that the polyp started to arise from the epithelial lining of the colon of the mice at week 6. At the 12th week, the number of cauliflower-like polyps clearly increased, accompanied by localized thickening and stiffening of the intestinal wall (Fig. 1G). Histopathological analysis revealed that the 6-week-old mouse model developed CRA, whereas the 12-week-old mouse model progressed to CRC. Under microscopic examination, the proportion of neoplastic tissue numbers to polyp numbers is called as tumor/polyp ratio, which is a standard indicator used to assess the process of tumorigenesis. The spleen-body weight ratio serves as a key indicator for assessing systemic immune and inflammatory status in tumor models. During the 6th to 12th week, Throughout adenoma-carcinoma progression, polyp/tumor ratios and the spleen-body weight were significantly increased (Fig. 1H). This implies that rapid progression of inflammation is accompanied by the carcinomatous change of the polyp. Bile acid profiling of mouse intestinal tissues revealed a marked increase in absolute quantification of DCA at the 12th week (Fig. 1I). Additionally, we also checked the DCA content in the tumor tissues of CRC patients with lymph node metastasis (pN positive) and distant metastasis (pM positive). Comparing to groups of pM or pN negative, the DCA levels in pM or pN positive groups were markedly raised. (Fig. 1J and K). These findings indicate that DCA metabolism and the immune response are strongly correlated with the development of CRC.

M2-type macrophage infiltration is a prominent feature of CRC

There is a consensus that the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) plays a very complex role in the occurrence and development of tumors. To explore the changes in the TIME during the progression of CRC from CRA, we conducted CyTOF analysis on tumor samples from the HCs, patients with CRA, and patients with CRC mentioned above (Fig. 2A). The expression of 42 different surface markers was examined, and immune cell subpopulations in the tissues were identified (Fig. S1A). The main immune cell populations were manually gated and projected on a t-SNE plot (Fig. 2B). This finding revealed some differences in the cell subpopulations among HCs and CRA and CRC patients. Clustering of immune cell populations revealed that the identified cell populations included mainly B cells, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, NK cells, macrophages, granulocytes, and other cell types (Fig. 2C). Compared with HCs and CRA patients, CRC patients presented significant increases in the numbers of macrophages and CD4+ T cells, with the most pronounced increase observed in the number of macrophages (Fig. 2D). However, there were no statistically significant differences in the percentages of CD8+ T cells, B cells, NK cells, granulocytes, or other cell types (Fig. S1B).

Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) are among the most prominent tumor-infiltrating immune cell populations in the TIME. TAMs can polarize into M1 or M2 phenotypes in response to dynamic changes in the TME. The M2 phenotype is generally related to the poor prognosis of patients with CRC. To verify the characteristics of M2 macrophages during the development of CRC, IF staining for the M1-type marker CD86 and the M2-type marker CD206 was applied to tissue sections from HC, CRA, and CRC patients (Fig. 2E). CRC patients were further stratified into two groups, T1/2 and T3/4, according to clinical tumor stage. Confocal microscopy revealed that the expression of CD206 was significantly greater in CRC tissues than in HCs and CRA patients. Moreover, in CRC patients, CD206 expression was greater in the T3/4 subgroup than in the T1/2 subgroup (Fig. 2F). These results suggest that M2-type macrophage infiltration is a prominent feature of CRC and is associated with the metastasis of CRC. Given the parallel trends observed between M2-type macrophage infiltration and DCA levels during colorectal neoplasm progression, we performed the Spearman’s rank correlation between the concentrations of DCA and the intensity of CD206 in CRC tissues. Fig. 2G suggested that there is an interaction between DCA metabolism and M2 polarization that drives the progression from adenoma to carcinoma.

DCA promotes TAM polarization toward the M2 phenotype

Although bile acid metabolism can reportedly modulate intestinal inflammation and promote CRC development, the specific role of DCA, the secondary metabolite of bile acids, in TAMs has not yet been further investigated. Based on the findings mentioned above, we aimed to elucidate and investigate the mechanisms by which DCA influences M2 macrophage polarization and function. THP-1 monocytic cell lines were differentiated into human M0 macrophages by PMA. M0 macrophages were then stimulated into the M1 and M2 subtypes through the induction of LPS + IFN-γ and IL-4 + IL-13, respectively. To better mimic the complex conditions of TAMs, M0, M1, and M2 macrophages were separately exposed to 10 mM DCA or DMSO as a solvent control (Fig. 3A). ELISA revealed that DCA markedly enhanced the secretion of IL-10 and TGF-β in the supernatant of macrophages under all three conditions, but it had no effect on the levels of IL-1β and TNF-α (Fig. 3B). These findings indicate that DCA improved the polarization of macrophages to the M2 phenotype. To verify this finding, we performed IF staining of M0 and M1 macrophages. Fig. 3C and D respectively show that CD206 expression was greatly increased with DCA treatment in M0 and M1 macrophages. The Fig. 3E quantified CD206 relative fluorescence intensity under the two polarization statements mentioned above. In addition, the flow cytometry results demonstrated that DCA intervention significantly increased the expression of CD206 in M0 and M1 macrophages (Fig. 3F).

DCA increased the expression of the M2 markers MRC1 and ARG1 and decreased the expression of the M1 markers IL-6 and IL-1β. These results proved that DCA was able to polarize human macrophages toward the M2 phenotype and increase the capacity to secrete immunosuppressive cytokines (Fig. 3G). To further validate the effect of DCA on macrophage polarization, we isolated macrophages derived from human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and repeated all the experiments with this system. Both the ELISA and IF staining results were the same as those of the THP-1-derived macrophage system, confirming that DCA was an inducer of M2 macrophages (Fig. S2A-G). Furthermore, to obtain macrophages from the TME, we established an ex vivo TAM system by collecting fresh tumor tissues and adjacent tissues from CRC patients. TAMs were extracted through a series of dissociations and subjected to DCA intervention (Fig. 3H). IF staining revealed that the expression of CD206 in tumor tissues was significantly greater than that in adjacent tissues, and DCA treatment promoted the fluorescence expression of CD206 in adjacent tissues (Fig. 3I and J). These results further confirmed that DCA can promote the transformation of M0 and M1 macrophages into M2 macrophages.

DCA-mediated M2 macrophages facilitate the EMT of colorectal tumor cells

According to the clinic pathological analysis (Fig. 1B), tumor cells progressively lose epithelial characteristics and shift from localized growth to deep tissue invasion during the transition from CRA to CRC, This invasive behavior is recognized as a key indicator of EMT. To investigate whether DCA-mediated M2 macrophages promote EMT, we firstly established a co-culture system comprising colonic epithelial cells and macrophages to examine the role of DCA-conditioned macrophages in tumor invasion. We utilized a normal human intestinal epithelial cell line (NCM460) and two human CRC cell lines, HCT116 (derived from primary tumors) and LOVO (derived from peritoneal metastatic tumors), to model different stages of colorectal epithelial tumor progression. These epithelial cells were co-cultured with M0 (THP-1 treated by PMA) or M1 (induced by LPS + IFN-γ) macrophages that had been pre-treated with DCA or DMSO as control (Fig. 4A).

Invasion experiments on HCT116 and LOVO cells revealed that DCA-mediated polarized macrophages significantly enhanced the invasive ability of colorectal cancer cells (Fig. 4B and C). Having established that DCA-mediated macrophages promote invasion, we next sought to determine if this was associated with the induction of EMT program. We detected E-cadherin and Vimentin of HCT116 cells which were co-cultured with different macrophages. IF staining revealed that DCA-induced macrophage polarization promoted the downregulation of E-cadherin and the upregulation of Vimentin in HCT116 cells (Fig. 4D). Furthermore, coculture experiments confirmed that coculture with DCA-induced polarized macrophages promoted the downregulation of E-cadherin and the upregulation of Vimentin and N-cadherin protein expressions in HCT116 and LOVO cells (Fig. 4E-H). These results indicate that DCA-induced polarized macrophages can further promote the development of EMT in human CRC cells, thereby facilitating the progression of CRC.

DCA-mediated M2 macrophages activate the nuclear expression of β-catenin, p-ikbα, and p-p65 during EMT in CRC

To further investigate the mechanism of DCA-mediated macrophage polarization in the process of CRC, we focused on the expression of β-catenin, p-ikbα and p-p65. As shown in Fig. 5A, Wnt/β-catenin signaling promotes EMT by activating SNAIL, SLUG, and TWIST, which regulate E-cadherin and N-cadherin expression; phosphorylated p65 and IκBα activate the NF-κB signaling pathway, which is essential for EMT during inflammation and cancer development. The nuclear localization of β-catenin and p-p65 is direct evidence of the activation of EMT. Therefore, we extracted nuclear protein from tumor cells in the coculture system for subsequent detection (Fig. 5B). The Western blot detection and protein analysis in Fig. 5C and D show that DCA-treated macrophages significantly increased the nuclear expression of active β-catenin and promoted the nuclear accumulation of p-IκBα and p-p65 in NCM460 cells. The HCT116/LOVO cell lines were validated, and the same results were obtained following DCA exposure (Fig. 5E and F). In addition, the expression of active β-catenin, p-IκBα and p-p65 in HCT116/LOVO cells was obviously greater than that in NCM460 cells, which confirmed that EMT mainly occurs in the transitional stage from local invasion to distant metastasis. Therefore, we found that DCA-induced polarized macrophages promote colorectal cancer development by activating β-catenin and facilitating the nuclear translocation of p-IκBα and p-p65.

DCA exacerbates colorectal carcinogenesis in AOM + DSS mice

To further investigate the role of DCA in CRC carcinogenesis in vivo, we employed an AOM/DSS-induced model in C57BL/6 mice. The mice received a single intraperitoneal injection of AOM (10 mg/kg), followed by three cycles of 2.5% DSS in their drinking water (each cycle lasting 7 days). Based on this, vehicle control (AOM + DSS group) or 0.2% DCA (DCA group) was administered in the drinking water throughout the entire period. Tumor development was monitored via PET‒CT imaging at 12th and 16th weeks post initiation. The humane endpoint was the 16th week (Fig. 6A). Fig. 6B shows that tumor size was increased under DCA treatment. At the endpoint, the colons of the mice were isolated, and the tumor polyps were counted. Both AOM plus DSS and DCA caused tumor growth in the colon; in particular, mice with more than 10 intestinal tumors were mostly in the DCA group (Fig. 6C). In addition, as shown in Fig. 6D, the colon length of the DCA-treated group was shorter than that of the AOM plus DSS group at the 16th week. Mouse intestinal images and HE staining revealed that DCA intervention significantly increased the number of colon tumors in the mice, shortened the length of the small intestine, and disrupted the normal small intestinal glandular structure (Fig. 6E). To further explore TAM polarization/statement and mechanism in mice, F4/80 and Mannose were detected by IF staining. Compared with the control and AOM plus DSS models, DCA significantly increased the expression of F4/80 and Mannose (Fig. 6F and G). Moreover, we detected NF-KB P65 and pikas and found that DCA increased the pholysosis of P65 and pikas in CRC (Fig. 6H). These findings verified that DCA promotes an increase in M2 macrophages, the accumulation of p-IκBα and NF-κB p-p65 in the nucleus, and subsequent CRC development in vivo.

DCA promotes intestinal tumorigenesis in APCmin+ mice

Compared with AOM + DSS mice, APCmin/+ mice produce a truncated APC protein that fails to degrade β-catenin, causing cytoplasmic accumulation and nuclear translocation. This leads to persistent activation of β-catenin target genes, directly initiating intestinal adenoma formation. To explore the relationships between the development of CRC and the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, we repeated DCA administration in the APCmin/+ mouse model (Fig. 7A). As expected, DCA treatment significantly increased tumor numbers and tumor volumes, while reduced colon length (Fig. 7B). HE staining revealed that the tumor mass in the intestine shown in Fig. 7C indicated that DCA promoted rapid tumor growth and aggressive ability. Given the high expressions of Mannose and F4/80 in the DCA group, DCA promoted the M2 polarization of TAMs in APCmin/+ mice (Fig. 7D and E). IF staining and intensity quantification revealed that DCA treatment also increased the nuclear expression of p-IκBα and NF-κB p-p65 in the colon tumor tissue of the mice (Fig. 7F and G). These results all proved that DCA promotes intestinal tumorigenesis in APC min/+ mice.

Discussion

The adenoma‒carcinoma sequence involves stepwise progression from normal colonic epithelium to dysplastic adenoma and, ultimately, invasive CRC. A critical driver of this progression is DCA, a secondary bile acid generated by the gut microbial metabolism of primary bile acids. Elevated DCA levels—frequently associated with high-fat diets—promote colorectal carcinogenesis through multiple mechanisms, with chronic inflammation serving as a central mediator. DCA promotes a protumorigenic microenvironment by inducing genomic instability, aberrant cellular proliferation, and immune suppression [11]. Some clinical evidence has shown that CRC patients with high fecal DCA levels exhibit elevated IL-6 and STAT3 activation [12, 13], linking DCA to proinflammatory signaling. Elevated serum DCA correlates with tumor infiltration of CD163+ M2 macrophages [14], highlighting its role in polarizing immunosuppressive myeloid cells. These findings unequivocally position DCA as a key metabolic orchestrator of inflammation during the adenoma‒carcinoma transition, suggesting potential targets for therapeutic intervention.

Under the complex inflammatory landscape of the TME, DCA has emerged as a critical regulator of TAMs. DCA drives the polarization of macrophages toward a protumor, immunosuppressive M2-like phenotype through multiple mechanisms, including activation of the TGR5/STAT3 axis, NLRP3 inflammasome modulation, and suppression of NF-κB signaling. This reprogramming fosters a permissive TME that sustains chronic inflammation, promotes immune evasion, and accelerates cancer progression. Analysis of clinical samples revealed that macrophages exhibit the most pronounced alterations in patients with elevated DCA levels, underscoring their central role in DCA-mediated tumorigenesis. DCA activates the nuclear receptors TGR5 and the NF-κB pathway to polarize macrophages toward the M2 phenotype [15]. In addition, M2-like TAMs secrete the immunosuppressive factors IL-10 and TGF-β, inhibiting CD8+ T-cell function and promoting immune tolerance [16]. Evelina et al. [17] reported that DCA enhances Arg1 activity in macrophages, depleting arginine in the TME and further suppressing T-cell proliferation. While the DCA-TAM axis has been well characterized in CRC and hepatocellular carcinoma, its broader implications for tumor progression remain underexplored. This study addresses this gap by establishing a temporal framework for DCA-driven CRC development. On the basis of these findings, we determined that DCA shapes TAM phenotypes during the inflammation-to-cancer transition. These findings provide a foundation for future DCA-targeted diagnostics and therapies.

EMT drives colorectal cancer progression by conferring migratory, invasive, and metastatic capabilities through two major pathways: Wnt/β-catenin activation upregulates Snail family factors to suppress E-cadherin while inducing N-cadherin and vimentin [18], and inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α/IL-6/IL-1β) activate NF-κB/STAT3 signaling, where NF-κB serves as the central hub linking inflammation to EMT by degrading IκB, translocating to the nucleus, and upregulating Snail/Slug [19]. Our study demonstrated that DCA promotes CRC cell invasion and induces EMT through both direct activation of the NF-κB/Wnt pathway and indirect modulation of the tumor microenvironment, including the induction of M2 macrophage polarization. However, while we proved the DCA-mediated macrophage polarization promotes CRC cell invasion and induces an EMT phenotype, the limitations of this study include the unclear macrophage dependence of the EMT effects of DCA, the undefined contributions of macrophage-derived factors, and the incomplete elucidation of the temporal DCA-macrophage-invasion-EMT axis, highlighting critical areas for future investigations into this metabolic-inflammatory-EMT network in CRC progression.

In light of our findings that macrophage polarization is a key mechanism of DCA-accelerated carcinogenesis, it is important to consider that the overall in vivo effects likely represent a multifactorial process involving additional pathways. What is currently known is that, the biosynthesis of DCA is contingent upon the metabolic activities of the gut microbiota. Primary bile acids undergo transformation into secondary bile acids, specifically DCA, through the catalytic action of 7α-dehydroxylase, an enzyme produced by certain intestinal microbiota, including Clostridium scindens [20]. Notably, a high-fat dietary regimen has been demonstrated to promote the proliferation of specific bacterial taxa, such as Bacteroides and Firmicutes [21]. Recent findings have revealed a positive correlation between fecal DCA concentrations in CRC patients and the abundance of microbial species, such as Bacteroides fragilis [22]. Moreover, dysbiosis of the gut microbiota is intricately linked to alterations in the immune microenvironment. Consequently, strategies aimed at reducing DCA levels include the augmentation of probiotics, such as Lactobacillus species [23], and the exploration of farnesoid X receptor (FXR) agonists targeting the microbiota, exemplified by obeticholic acid, which is currently undergoing clinical evaluation for CRC and intestinal metabolic diseases [24, 25]. These potential pathways could be an important area for future investigation. Elucidating the relative contribution of these interconnected pathways, and how they synergize to drive tumor progression, represents a critical step toward effective combination therapies.

This study elucidates the central role of DCA as a molecular orchestrator of colorectal cancer progression, demonstrating its dual capacity to polarize tumor-associated macrophages and induce EMT through coordinated activation of the NF-κB and Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathways. Our mechanistic findings reveal how DCA simultaneously fosters an immunosuppressive TME via TAM reprogramming while promoting metastatic dissemination through EMT activation, thereby bridging metabolic dysregulation with malignant progression (Fig. 8). Although these results position DCA as a critical regulator of the inflammation‒EMT axis, key questions remain regarding its stage-specific contributions and translational applicability. These findings establish bile acid metabolism as a promising therapeutic target and provide a conceptual framework for the development of novel prognostic biomarkers and precision interventions for CRC management.
