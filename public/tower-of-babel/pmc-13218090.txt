Sino-Canadian Healthy Life Trajectories Initiative in China: an economic evaluation protocol

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionChildhood overweight and obesity (OWO) in China, especially in urban areas, has become a significant public health challenge. Recent studies indicate a high prevalence of OWO in both children and adults, exacerbated by urbanisation and economic development. This trend poses a threat to health outcomes, including neurocognitive functioning, socio-emotional development and risk of chronic conditions. Recognising the limitations of high-income country prevention models, the Sino-Canadian Healthy Life Trajectories Initiative (SCHeLTI) was established to evaluate a multifaceted intervention aimed at mitigating childhood OWO and related non-communicable disease risks. This protocol outlines the economic evaluation component of the SCHeLTI trial.Methods and analysisA within-trial economic evaluation and long-term model from both a healthcare/personal social services perspective and a broader societal perspective will be undertaken alongside the SCHeLTI study, comparing the multifaceted intervention to usual care. Costs will include intervention provision, implementation and household expenditures. Outcome measures span child and maternal health-related quality of life, growth indicators, cognitive and behavioural development, nutritional status and metabolic dysfunction indicators. Cost-effectiveness and cost-utility analyses will be performed, with incremental cost-effectiveness ratios calculated for primary outcomes and quality-adjusted life years. A decision-analytical model will also project long-term economic and health impacts. Equity impact analysis will assess the intervention’s effects across socioeconomic strata. Sensitivity analyses, including one-way and probabilistic approaches, will be conducted to ensure robust results.Ethics and disseminationThe study has been approved by the Medical Research Ethics Committees of the International Peace Maternity and Child Health Hospital in Shanghai, China (GKLW2017-01) and the CIUSSS de l’Estrie-CHUS in Sherbrooke, Canada (MP-31-2019–2967). Results will be disseminated through academic publications, policy briefs, stakeholder meetings and community engagement to inform early childhood development policy in China and similar settings globally.Trial registration numberChiCTR1800017773.

Introduction

The rising prevalence of childhood overweight and obesity (OWO) in China, particularly within urban epicentres like Shanghai, has transcended healthcare concerns to become a dramatic public health challenge. A seminal study leveraging the 2002 China National Nutrition and Health Survey database, which included a staggering 209 849 participants, revealed a combined OWO prevalence of 23.2%, with urbanisation and economic development cited as exacerbating factors.1 This trend is not just an urban phenomenon, but reflects a broader pattern of escalating obesity rates across various socioeconomic strata, with significant sex and age group differences.1 2

The gravity of this situation is further underscored by the latest national prevalence estimates indicating that OWO have become a widespread public health issue affecting both children and adults, with rates reaching as high as 34.3% and 16.4%, respectively.2 These statistics not only represent a threat to the health of the nation’s population,2 but also foreshadow a potential doubling of obesity rates as urbanisation progresses.1 2 The link between OWO and a spectrum of adverse health outcomes, including impaired neurocognitive functioning, socio-emotional developmental issues and an increased risk of chronic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular diseases, is well established.13 The economic implications of OWO in China are extensive and multifaceted, including elevated healthcare expenditures for managing obesity-related diseases, productivity losses attributable to absenteeism, presenteeism and premature mortality, as well as heightened economic inequality. Additionally, OWO contributes to diminished economic growth and escalated social welfare costs. Conversely, it presents opportunities within the wellness industry, while posing challenges for the food and beverage sector. Furthermore, there are significant costs associated with policies and interventions aimed at addressing the obesity epidemic.

The urgency of addressing OWO in China is heightened by the recognition that the traditional high-income country models of prevention may not be directly applicable to the unique socioeconomic context of low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), including China. The scarcity of research surrounding the collective roles of upstream and downstream factors influencing obesity in China underscores the need for multidisciplinary and transdisciplinary studies.2

In response to this evidence, the Sino-Canadian Healthy Life Trajectories Initiative (SCHeLTI) study emerges as one of four country trials within the Healthy Life Trajectories Initiative (HeLTI) consortium (Canada, China, India and South Africa). The economic evaluation component was subsequently incorporated in 2022 to assess cost-effectiveness to evaluate the efficacy and cost-effectiveness of a multifaceted community-family-mother-child intervention aimed at curbing childhood OWO and associated non-communicable disease (NCD) risks. The study is based on the Developmental Origins of Health and Disease (DOHaD) framework,4 which underscores the significance of early-life environmental factors on long-term health trajectories.

The SCHeLTI trial is a multicentre, cluster-randomised, parallel-group, superiority trial that rigorously compares the community-family-mother-child intervention with standard care.5 It employs a robust design that includes randomisation and blinding to ensure the validity of findings. The intervention was designed to initiate from the preconception phase, extending through pregnancy and into early childhood, with a focus on nutritional counselling, physical activity promotion and behavioural strategies to foster healthy lifestyle habits within families and communities.

The SCHeLTI study is poised to make a significant contribution to the field, offering insights into the prevention of childhood OWO in an LMIC context. Its findings will be instrumental in informing policy decisions and healthcare resource allocation, with the potential to substantially improve health outcomes and health-related quality of life (HRQoL) for children and mothers in China and similar settings.

Methods and analysis

Study setting and population

The SCHeLTI study is conducted in five districts of Shanghai, China. The trial recruited participants from 2018 to 2022, with intervention delivery continuing through 2027 and primary outcome assessment at child age 5 years scheduled for completion by 2029–2030. The economic evaluation module (cost and HRQoL instruments) was incorporated into the Chinese trial protocol in 2022, and the revised protocol was approved by the ethics committees on 20 June 2022. The study sites serving each district-level hospital and 42 Maternal Child Health Units (MCHUs) located across the five participating districts. MCHUs with the potential to deliver at least 100 study participants annually from their catchment area were selected for the trial. The trial includes 42 clusters (MCHUs) and plans to enrol approximately 3000 women/mother–child dyads, with follow-up to child age 5 years. To ensure balanced comparisons, MCHUs were stratified by district and randomly allocated to either the intervention or standard care group, with a ratio of 1:1 within each district.

The target population for this study comprises women who speak Mandarin aged 20–42 years, their partners and families residing in the service areas of the participating MCHUs. Participants were recruited during preconception or early pregnancy and are followed-up into early childhood, specifically focusing on interventions during preconception, pregnancy and the early years of the child’s life. Eligible pregnant women are identified and approached by research nurses or research assistants during their first presentation for routine preconception or prenatal care at participating hospitals or MCHUs.

Intervention description

The SCHeLTI intervention constitutes a comprehensive, integrated framework aimed at fostering healthy life trajectories from the preconception stage through pregnancy and into early childhood. This family-centred, personalised programme is crafted to assist families in adopting and sustaining healthy behaviours. It integrates multiple tailored components and behaviour change techniques, with the frequency of component delivery determined by families’ assigned intensity group (low-intensity or high-intensity) based on predefined risk factors.6

Healthy conversations (HC): As the foundational element of SCHeLTI, the HC approach is derived from the conceptual framework of the University of Southampton and is customised to align with the cultural and contextual requirements of the study population. Anchored in person-centred principles, HC employs active listening and open-ended ‘how’ and ‘what’ questions to facilitate an in-depth exploration of health issues by families. The primary objective is to support families in establishing SMARTER goals (Specific, Measurable, Action-oriented, Realistic, Timed, Evaluated, Reviewed) while formulating strategies, anticipating potential barriers and identifying resources necessary for goal attainment. Families are assigned to low-intensity or high-intensity groups based on risk factors; the presence of one risk factor qualifies a family for high-intensity. Low-intensity families are moved to high-intensity if new risk factors emerge, remaining there until re-evaluation at predefined time points (child’s birth, third birthday).

The intervention is administered by a dedicated team of trained nurses and community health providers. Families receive HC contacts at an intensity tailored by risk group: low-intensity families receive approximately 8–12 contacts, while high-intensity families receive approximately 20–30 contacts over the intervention period. In usual care, mother–child dyads receive approximately 5–8 routine contacts (low risk) and 8–12 contacts (high risk), while control clusters receive the standard care available through the existing healthcare system.

Measurement of health outcomes

The primary health outcome of the SCHeLTI trial is the assessment of childhood OWO at 5 years of age (binary outcome)5 (online supplemental table 1), defined as a body mass index (BMI) for age at or above the 85th percentile based on WHO sex-specific standards.5

Outcome data will be collected at multiple time points, including baseline and follow-ups primarily at 5 years of age for the primary outcome. The inclusion of these additional outcome measures will provide a more holistic view of the impact of the intervention on the overall health and well-being of both children and their mothers within the SCHeLTI trial.

Valuation of resource use:

To ensure comparability and accuracy, all cost data will be converted into international dollars using purchasing power parity exchange rates, adjusted for inflation and discounted annually at a rate of 5% in the base case analysis.10 Costs will be reported in 2022 Chinese yuan (price year). This approach accounts for the non-negligible amount of heterogeneity that exists within China and allows for proper comparison across studies.

Economic evaluation

The economic evaluation of the SCHeLTI study will be conducted to comprehensively assess the intervention’s financial implications and societal value (table 3). The primary economic evaluation is a within-trial cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA) against the trial’s primary endpoint (child overweight/obesity at age 5 years, binary). Secondary evaluations include within-trial cost-utility analysis (CUA) and modelled lifetime CUA. The evaluation will encompass multiple frameworks, including CEA, CUA and equity impact analysis. The results will inform policymakers and stakeholders about the economic feasibility and impact of scaling up the intervention.

Overview of health economic frameworks, outcome measures, perspective and format for presenting results

CEAC, cost-effectiveness acceptability curves; ICER, incremental cost-effectiveness ratio; NLP, natural language processing; QALY, quality-adjusted life year.

Cost-effectiveness analysis with trial data

Should statistically significant differences between study groups be identified in key outcomes, the CEA will quantify the incremental costs associated with improvements in these outcomes. This includes both primary outcomes, such as reductions in childhood OWO, BMI-z score and secondary outcomes like improvements in cognitive development, behavioural development and nutritional status. The analysis will be conducted from both the provider and societal perspectives, capturing direct and indirect costs.

The ICERs will be calculated by dividing the incremental costs by the incremental improvements in the outcomes of interest. For example, the ICER for improving BMI would be the additional cost per unit reduction in BMI z-score. The CEA results will be presented in the form of ICERs, cost-effectiveness planes and cost-effectiveness acceptability curves (CEAC), providing a comprehensive view of the intervention’s economic value compared with standard care.

Cost-utility analysis

The CUA will build on the CEA by focusing specifically on HRQoL through the use of QALYs. This analysis will also employ a robust decision-analytical model to project the long-term economic and health impacts of the intervention, incorporating the latest evidence on the relationship between childhood overweight and obesity and adult health outcomes.

The analysis will calculate the incremental cost per QALY gained. The decision-analytical model will simulate the progression of individuals from the trial cohort through different health states over their lifetime, considering both costs and benefits. The CUA results will be presented in the form of cost-utility ratios, net benefit analysis and sensitivity analysis, including probabilistic sensitivity analysis using Monte Carlo simulation.

While CEA cost-consequence analysis provides a broad assessment of the intervention’s economic value across multiple outcomes, CUA offers a more targeted evaluation focused on HRQoL through QALYs. By combining these two frameworks, policymakers and stakeholders can gain a comprehensive understanding of the intervention’s short- and long-term economic impacts, as well as its potential to improve overall health and well-being.

Equity impact analysis

The equity impact analysis will assess the intervention’s effects across different socioeconomic strata. Specifically, we will use five SES strata (quintiles) based on a composite asset-based wealth index using Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) methodology. The marginal mean difference in outcomes between the intervention and control arms will be calculated within quintiles of SES, measured using a composite asset index while adjusting for consumption expenditure. The report by the DHS programme provides a detailed methodology for constructing a wealth index using household asset data.11 It is widely used in LMICs to measure SES. Additionally, a Social Vulnerability Index will be used to provide a more nuanced understanding of need, considering both monetary and non-monetary dimensions of deprivation.12

The analysis will determine whether the intervention disproportionately benefits those with higher needs, informing discussions about targeting and scale-up. The results will be presented as equity impact ratios and a distribution of benefits and costs across socioeconomic groups.

Sensitivity analyses

To ensure the robustness of the economic evaluation results, both deterministic and stochastic sensitivity analyses will be performed. One-way sensitivity analysis will explore the impact of changes in key parameters, such as the discount rate, unit costs and utility weights, on the main economic evaluation results. Tornado diagrams will visualise the effect of percentage changes in each key model parameter on the ICERs.

Probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) will be conducted using Monte Carlo simulation with 5000 iterations. This will simultaneously model variation in all model parameters based on assumed distributions, generating a bootstrapped distribution of costs and QALYs. The PSA will produce CEAC to represent the probability of the intervention being cost-effective compared with standard care across a range of willingness-to-pay (WTP) thresholds (onefold, twofold and threefold the gross domestic product per capita).13 These thresholds are consistent with WHO guidelines, though we acknowledge ongoing debate regarding their appropriateness.

Affordability and budget impact analyses

Affordability and budget impact analyses will provide critical insights into the financial scalability of the intervention. The total provider costs will be used to estimate affordability thresholds, and the total cost of a fully scaled programme will be compared with current health spending in the context. These analyses will inform decision-makers about the fiscal space available for implementing the intervention at scale.

Reporting standards

The analysis and reporting of results will follow the updated Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS) statement to ensure transparency and rigour.14 The reporting will include a detailed description of the study design, data sources, methods and assumptions used in the economic evaluation.

Statistical analysis and handling missing data

Given the hierarchical structure of the cost and outcome data, appropriate statistical models will be employed to account for clustering effects and within-group and between-group variability. Missing data will be imputed using a multiple imputation procedure with chained equations, separately for each arm of the trial. Predictive mean matching will be used to handle non-normality in cost and outcome data. These methods follow established guidelines for economic evaluations with clustered data. An analysis of missing data patterns will be conducted to identify any systematic biases.13

Extrapolation beyond trial: lifetime cost-effectiveness

If the trial demonstrates differences in effectiveness, costs or cost-effectiveness between the treatment arms, the lifetime model will link childhood weight status at age 5 to subsequent risks of obesity-related cardiometabolic outcomes using published longitudinal evidence. Transition probabilities will be parameterised using external cohort data and/or validated risk models. The within-trial results will be extrapolated to the long-term using a behavioural Markov model. The model will be customised to incorporate specific challenges, such as the inclusion of sedentary behaviour, identification of ageing-specific health outcomes and choice of an appropriate time horizon. Monte Carlo simulation techniques will be used to account for uncertainty in the longer-term estimates.

Key assumptions for lifetime extrapolation: The lifetime model will link childhood weight status (overweight/obesity vs non-overweight/obesity at age 5 and/or BMI-z trajectory) to subsequent risks of obesity-related cardiometabolic outcomes in adolescence and adulthood using published longitudinal evidence and risk equations. We assume that (1) relative differences in child overweight/obesity attributable to the intervention at age 5 translate into differential long-term risk through established pathways; (2) transition probabilities between weight categories over time will be parameterised using external cohort data and/or validated risk models; and (3) health-state utilities and costs associated with long-term outcomes will be derived from published sources and local data where available. Model outputs will be assessed for face validity and calibrated to relevant epidemiological benchmarks when feasible. Parameter and structural uncertainty will be addressed through scenario analyses and probabilistic sensitivity analyses.

Ethics and dissemination

The study has been approved by the Medical Research Ethics Committee of the IPH in Shanghai, China (GKLW2017-01) and the CIUSSS de l’Estrie-CHUS in Sherbrooke, Canada (MP-31-2019-2967). The economic evaluation (EE) add-on protocol amendment in Chinese protocol (cost and HRQoL instruments and related procedures) was approved on 20 June 2022 (China; approval no. GKLW2022-08). Data sharing policies are consistent with the governance policy of the HeLTI consortium and government legislation. Results will be disseminated through academic publications, policy briefs, stakeholder meetings and other channels to inform early childhood development policy in China. Community members will be engaged in interpreting and disseminating findings locally.

Discussion

This protocol outlines the first economic evaluation of a comprehensive early childhood development intervention in urban China. Given the rapid urbanisation and economic development contributing to the increasing prevalence of OWO, this study stands to offer significant insights into the economic feasibility and impact of multifaceted, community-based interventions. Using a robust, multicentre, cluster-randomised design with a long-term follow-up, the SCHeLTI study provides a comprehensive economic analysis that captures both direct and indirect costs from provider and societal perspectives.

The SCHeLTI study distinguishes itself by including a wide array of outcome measures spanning growth indicators, HRQoL, cognitive and behavioural development and metabolic dysfunction. This approach ensures a holistic assessment of the intervention’s effectiveness. The integration of decision-analytical modelling further enhances the analysis by projecting long-term economic and health impacts, thereby providing valuable data for policymakers to make informed decisions regarding resource allocation for early childhood development programmes.

While previous studies, such as those reviewed by Palmer and colleagues,15 have evaluated the cost-effectiveness of interventions in similar contexts, there remains a paucity of economic evaluations that begin from preconception and extend through early childhood, especially in LMICs like China. The SCHeLTI study contributes to filling this gap by adopting a rigorous methodology to assess both short-term and long-term economic impacts. This is particularly important given that early childhood interventions have the potential to yield substantial long-term health and economic benefits, as indicated by the DOHaD framework.4

One of the notable strengths of the SCHeLTI study is its focus on health equity. By evaluating the intervention’s differential effects across socioeconomic strata, the study will provide critical insights into whether the benefits are equitably distributed among different demographic groups. This equity impact analysis is essential for ensuring that health interventions do not exacerbate existing disparities, thus supporting fair and just public health policies.

However, the study faces some limitations. First, power calculations for household cost data collection were not feasible due to the complexity of the required index and scarcity of the required data, potentially which could affect the precision of the cost estimates. Second, there is inherent uncertainty when extrapolating short-term outcomes to predict long-term effectiveness and cost-effectiveness. These limitations are common in economic evaluations of public health interventions,15 and rigorous sensitivity analyses, including one-way and probabilistic approaches, will be conducted to address these uncertainties, ensuring the robustness of the findings. Moreover, the original SCHeLTI design did not collect measures for EQ-5D-Y and EQ-5D-5L, which are essential for conducting CUA. To enable such analyses, we have supplemented the study with these measures, as well as the HuPs to be collected at 5-year old. The absence of these data in the initial design might have introduced bias into the results, but integrating them now allows for a more comprehensive and accurate economic evaluation. Additionally, while the longitudinal design of the study is a strength, it also presents challenges such as maintaining participant engagement over time and managing data attrition. These factors require careful consideration and innovative strategies to ensure the sustainability and quality of long-term data collection. Retention strategies include: (1) regular app engagement through personalised notifications; (2) festival greetings and birthday reminders; (3) flexible data collection modes; (4) community engagement activities; and (5) small incentives for completion of key assessments.

In summary, the SCHeLTI study is poised to make significant contributions to the field by providing comprehensive economic evidence on the effectiveness of early childhood interventions in mitigating OWO and associated NCDs. The findings will be disseminated through academic publications, policy briefs and community engagement activities, thereby informing early childhood development policies in China and similar settings globally. This study not only enhances our understanding of the economic implications of such interventions but also underscores the importance of adopting a holistic, equity-focused approach to public health policy.
