Data-driven dimensionally decomposed generalized polynomial chaos expansion for forward uncertainty quantification
Hojun Choi, Eunho Heo, Dongjin Lee

Abstract
Dimensionally decomposed generalized polynomial chaos expansion (DD-GPCE) efficiently performs forward uncertainty quantification (UQ) in complex engineering systems with high-dimensional random inputs of arbitrary distributions. However, constructing the measure-consistent orthonormal polynomial bases in DD-GPCE requires prior knowledge of input distributions, which is often unavailable in practice. This work introduces a data-driven DD-GPCE method that eliminates the need for such prior knowledge, extending its applicability to UQ with high-dimensional inputs. Input distributions are inferred directly from sample data using smoothed-bootstrap kernel density estimation (KDE), while the DD-GPCE framework enables KDE to handle high-dimensional inputs through low-dimensional marginal estimation. We then use the estimated input distributions to perform a whitening transformation via Monte Carlo Simulation, which enables generation of measure-consistent orthonormal basis functions. We demonstrate the accuracy of the proposed method in both mathematical examples and stochastic dynamic analysis for a practical three-dimensional mobility design involving twenty random inputs. The results indicate that the proposed method produces more accurate estimates of the output mean and variance compared to the conventional data-driven approach that assumes Gaussian input distributions.

Introduction

Uncertainty quantification (UQ) is a critical aspect of modeling and simulation in engineering and scientific disciplines.
It aims to understand and predict the impact of input uncertainties on responses or quantities of interest (QoIs) in complex systems.
Forward UQ, which propagates input uncertainties through a system to estimate the statistical moments or distributions of the QoI, is especially important in reliable engineering design
[
6
]
, risk assessment
[
1
]
, and reliability analysis
[
5
]
.
Despite advancements in computational power, standard Monte Carlo Simulation (MCS) remains computationally expensive due to its reliance on a large number of samples, often making it impractical for real-world applications involving high-dimensional or computationally expensive models
[
2
]
.
To alleviate such difficulties, we use surrogate modeling methods, such as polynomial chaos expansion (PCE)
[
22
,
3
,
21
]
, polynomial dimensional decomposition (PDD)
[
23
,
15
]
, spline chaos expansion (SCE)
[
16
]
, spline dimensional decomposition (SDD)
[
8
,
17
]
, among others
[
4
]
.
These methods approximate the response of complex systems by using simplified mathematical representations, such as orthonormal polynomial expansions, low-dimensional projections, or machine learning-based regression methods, allowing efficient prediction of system behavior.

A practical version of generalized PCE
[
11
]
, as the state-of-the art machine learning method, effectively estimates the mean, variance, and failure probability of complex systems under arbitrary input random variables. The GPCE uses a whitening transformation that numerically generates measure-consistent orthonormal basis functions, yet it suffers from the curse of dimensionality as the input number increases. Dimensionally decomposed GPCE (DD-GPCE)
[
12
]
alleviates the high-dimensional issue by truncating the GPCE’s basis functions in terms of the chosen maximum degree of interaction between input variables and the maximum polynomial order.
This method has been successfully applied to design optimization under uncertainty and reliability analysis in various engineering fields, such as civil engineering and mechanical engineering
[
9
,
12
]
. DD-GPCE has also been adapted into a multifidelity method that reduces computational cost by integrating both high-fidelity and low-fidelity simulation models
[
10
]
.

The DD-GPCE remains limited by its reliance on prior knowledge of the input distribution and its parameters (e.g., the mean and variance in the case of Gaussian inputs), which are often unavailable in real-world applications. This assumption restricts its applicability when input distributions are unknown or when only limited sample data are available. For this reason, we consider data-driven approaches that infer uncertainty directly from observed data without strong distributional assumptions. The work
[
13
]
uses association rule mining and information entropy to characterize uncertainty, validated through MCS without relying on prior distribution knowledge. A more recent work
[
7
]
provides closed-form estimates of uncertainties using kernel density estimation (KDE) to ensure scalability and robustness in large-scale, data-intensive applications. However, these methods still face challenges for robustness, as their performance can be sensitive to the characteristics and quality of the available dataset. In addition, KDE suffers from the curse of dimensionality, leading to inaccurate results as the number of input variables increases.

This work proposes a novel data-driven DD-GPCE method that eliminates the need for prior knowledge of input distributions.
The proposed method estimates input densities directly from sample data using KDE. To reduce the density estimator variance in limited or sparse data, KDE is combined with a smoothed-bootstrap resampling scheme. The resulting input densities informs a whitening transformation that numerically generates orthonormal bases consistent with the input measures. We then use these bases to construct a DD-GPCE for estimating statistical moments of QoI.
The DD-GPCE, which retains only low-order interaction terms among input variables, enables the KDE to use lower-dimensional marginals when estimating high-dimensional joint input densities.
This makes the data-driven DD-GPCE more suitable for high-dimensional UQ problems. The key contributions of this work are: (1) a data-driven DD-GPCE framework that eliminates the need for prior knowledge of input distributions; (2) improved input density estimation via smoothed-bootstrap KDE, enhancing DD-GPCE accuracy under limited data; and (3) the integration of DD-GPCE, which captures lower-order interactions among inputs, with KDE to efficiently solve UQ problems with high-dimensional inputs.

2.1
Preliminaries

Denote the sets of positive integers, non-negative integers, real numbers, and non-negative real numbers by
\mathbb{N}
,
\mathbb{N}_{0}
, , and
\real{}_{0}^{+}
, respectively. For a given positive integer
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathbb{A}^{N}\subseteq\real^{N}
denotes a sub-domain of
N
-dimensional real number
\mathbb{R}^{N}
that can either be bounded or unbounded.

2.2
Input random variables

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space, where
\Omega
represents the sample space,
\mathcal{F}
is a
\sigma
-algebra, and
\mathbb{P}
is a probability measure. The
N
-dimensional random vector
\mathbf{X}:=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N})^{\intercal}:\Omega\to\mathbb{A}^{N}
represents the uncertainties in a UQ problem. For instance,
X_{1}
can represent the elastic modulus of a material, which varies due to manufacturing inconsistencies, while
X_{2}
can represent the density of the same material, which varies due to impurities or environmental conditions.
The joint distribution function of
\mathbf{X}
is
F_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x}):=\mathbb{P}\big[\cap_{i=1}^{N}\{X_{i}\leq x_{i}\}\big]
, and its joint probability density function is
f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x}):=\frac{\partial^{N}F_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{1}\cdots\partial x_{N}}
. A realization
\mathbf{x}:=(x_{1},...,x_{N})^{\intercal}\in\mathbb{A}^{N}
represents a specific outcome of the random vector
\mathbf{X}
.
The image of
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
for
\mathbf{X}
thus forms the probability space
(\mathbb{A}^{N},\mathcal{B}^{N},f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})\text{d}\mathbf{x})
, where
\mathbb{A}^{N}
is the image of
\Omega
and
\mathcal{B}^{N}:=\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{A}^{N})
is the Borel
\sigma
-algebra.

2.3
Output random variables

For an input random vector
\mathbf{X}
, we define
y(\mathbf{X})
as a real-valued, square-integrable transformation on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
. The function
y:\mathbb{A}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
represents an output that an application engineer considers relevant for UQ. This study assumes that
y(\cdot)
is belong to the weighted
L^{2}
space as

\displaystyle\left\{y:\mathbb{A}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}:\int_{\mathbb{A}^{N}}|y(\mathbf{x})|^{2}f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})\text{d}\mathbf{x}<\infty\right\}

on the probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
.

2.4
Dimensionally decomposed generalized polynomial chaos expansion

Consider an input random vector
\mathbf{X}
with a probability density function
f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})
and its corresponding output random variable
y(\mathbf{X})
. The DD-GPCE surrogate propagates the statistical properties (e.g, mean, variance, and failure probability) of
y(\mathbf{X})
using orthonormal polynomial functions of
\mathbf{X}
that are measure consistent with
f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}
.

Let
\{1,\ldots,N\}
be an index set with
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Denote by
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\}
a subset, including the empty set
\emptyset
, with cardinality
0\leq|\mathcal{U}|\leq N
. Define the complement of
\mathcal{U}
as
\mathcal{U}^{\complement}:=\{1,\ldots,N\}\setminus\mathcal{U}
. For each
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
0\leq S\leq N
, we define the reduced multi-index set as

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{S,m}:=\left\{\mathbf{j}=(\mathbf{j}_{\mathcal{U}},\mathbf{0}_{\mathcal{U}^{\complement}})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{N}\,:\,\mathbf{j}_{\mathcal{U}}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{|\mathcal{U}|},\ |\mathbf{j}_{\mathcal{U}}|\leq m,\ 0\leq|\mathcal{U}|\leq S\right\},\quad|\mathbf{j}_{\mathcal{U}}|:=j_{i_{1}}+\cdots+j_{i_{|\mathcal{U}|}}.

Here, we arrange the elements as
\mathbf{j}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{j}^{(L_{N,S,m})},\penalty\ \mathbf{j}^{(1)}=\mathbf{0}
, according to a chosen monomial ordering. Also,
(\mathbf{j}_{\mathcal{U}},\mathbf{0}_{\mathcal{U}^{\complement}})
denotes an
N
-dimensional multi-index whose
i
th component is
j_{i}
if
i\in\mathcal{U}
and 0 if
\notin\mathcal{U}
. The
\mathcal{J}_{S,m}
has cardinality
L_{N,S,m}:=\left|\mathcal{J}_{S,m}\right|=1+\sum_{s=1}^{S}\binom{N}{s}\binom{m}{s}
.

Let
\hat{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{S,m}(\mathbf{x})=(\hat{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{1}(\mathbf{x}),\ldots,\hat{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{L_{N,S,m}}(\mathbf{x}))^{\intercal}
be an
L_{N,S,m}
-dimensional vector of multivariate orthonormal polynomials consistent with the probability measure
f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}
of
\mathbf{X}
. We determine the orthonormal polynomial vector through the following three steps
[
11
]
:

(1)

Create an
L_{N,S,m}
-dimensional column vector
of monomials
\mathbf{P}_{S,m}(\mathbf{x})
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{S,m}(\mathbf{x})=(\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{j}^{(1)}},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{j}^{(L_{N,S,m})}})^{\intercal},

(1)

where,
\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{j}}=\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{U}}^{\mathbf{j}_{\mathcal{U}}}\mathbf{0}_{\mathcal{U}^{\complement}}^{\mathbf{j}_{\mathcal{U}^{\complement}}}=\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{U}}^{\mathbf{j}_{\mathcal{U}}}
. The subvector
\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{U}}:=(x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{|\mathcal{U}|}})^{\intercal}
collects the components of
\mathbf{x}
indexed by
\mathcal{U}=\{i_{1}<\cdots<i_{\mathcal{U}}\}\subseteq\{1,\cdots,N\}
. The complementary subvector
\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{U}^{\complement}}
contains the remaining components.

(2)

Construct an
L_{N,S,m}\times L_{N,S,m}
monomial moment matrix
\mathbf{G}_{S,m}(\mathbf{X})
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{S,m}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\mathbf{P}_{S,m}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{P}_{S,m}^{\intercal}(\mathbf{x})f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.

(2)

This is estimated using MCS by sampling from the known or assumed input density
f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})
.

(3)

Select the
L_{N,S,m}\times L_{N,S,m}
whitening matrix
\mathbf{W}_{S,m}
from the Cholesky decomposition
[
14
]
of the monomial moment matrix
\mathbf{G}_{S,m}
, such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{S,m}^{-1}\mathbf{W}_{S,m}^{-\intercal}=\mathbf{G}_{S,m}.

(3)

Then, use the whitening transformation to generate measure-consistent multivariate orthonormal polynomials as

\displaystyle\hat{\Psi}_{S,m}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{W}_{S,m}\mathbf{P}_{S,m}(\mathbf{x}).

(4)

The
S
-variate,
m
th-order DD-GPCE approximation of
y(\mathbf{X})
is

\displaystyle y_{S,m}(\mathbf{X})=\sum_{i=1}^{L_{N,S,m}}c_{i}\hat{\Psi}_{i}(\mathbf{X})

(5)

with expansion coefficients

\displaystyle c_{i}:=\int_{\mathbb{A}^{N}}y(\mathbf{x})\hat{\Psi}_{i}(\mathbf{x})f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},\quad i=1,\ldots,L_{N,S,m}.

(6)

In this work, we determine these expansion coefficients using standard least squares (SLS). The truncation parameters
S
and
m
are chosen to satisfy
1\leq S\leq N
and
S\leq m<\infty
. Under these conditions, the DD-GPCE approximation
y_{S,m}(\mathbf{X})
converges to
y(\mathbf{X})
as
S\rightarrow N
and
m\rightarrow\infty
. For details on DD-GPCE, see
[
12
]
.

Problem 1
.

We consider a limited set of input samples with an unknown underlying probability distribution. For a nonlinear output
y(\cdot)
with finite variance and high-dimensional inputs, our goal is to accurately and efficiently estimate the mean and variance of
y(\mathbf{X})
using DD-GPCE.

2.5
Kernel density estimation

Given
M
input samples
\{\mathbf{x}^{(l)}\}_{l=1}^{M}
, where
\mathbf{x}^{(l)}=(x_{1}^{(l)},\ldots,x_{N}^{(l)})^{\intercal}
,
we estimate the probability density
f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{X})
using kernel density estimation, such that

\displaystyle f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})\approx\hat{f}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{H}):=\dfrac{1}{M|\mathbf{H}|^{1/2}}\sum_{i=1}^{M}K\left(\mathbf{H}^{-1/2}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{(i)})\right).

(7)

Here,
K(\cdot)
is a kernel function—an
N
-variate density that is symmetric and unimodal at the
N
-dimensional origin
\mathbf{0}
, and relies on the bandwidth
\mathbf{H}
which is a
N\times N
positive definite and symmetric matrix. The
\mathbf{H}
controls the smoothing of the kernel density estimate by determining the scale and shape of the kernel around each data point. This influences how closely the estimate fits the data, i.e., a smaller
\mathbf{H}
produces a more detailed but less smooth estimate, while a larger
\mathbf{H}
yields a smoother approximation.
For the multivariate kernel in this work, we use the Gaussian kernel
K(\mathbf{x})=(2\pi)^{-N/2}\exp\left({-(1/2)\mathbf{x}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}}\right)
. The transpose
\mathbf{x}^{\intercal}
of
\mathbf{x}
is to compute the squared Euclidean norm, i.e,
||\mathbf{x}||^{2}
of the vector
\mathbf{x}
. The Gaussian kernel is chosen over other types (e.g., polynomial or sigmoid) due to its broad applicability and prevalence in handling arbitrary data.

We interpret (
7
) as a data-driven mixture of probability densities for
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
. The kernel density estimation combines multiple kernel functions, with each function representing a localized probability distribution centered on a data point. The final density estimate is the sum of these individual kernel contributions.
We use the KDE estimate to approximate the distribution of
\mathbf{X}
and generate orthonormal polynomial functions consistent with the approximate probability measure
\hat{f}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{H})\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}
, as explained in Section
3.1
.

2.6
Bootstrapping

Bootstrapping is a statistical technique used to estimate the sampling distribution of an estimator (e.g, the mean or variance) by repeatedly resampling with replacement from the original dataset. In this process, each data point can be selected multiple times when generating a “bootstrap samples”, and multiple such samples are obtained by repeatedly resampling from the original dataset.

Given
M
input samples
\{\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{(M)}\}
where
\mathbf{x}^{(l)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, we construct a bootstrap sample
\bar{\mathbf{X}}^{(b)}
by drawing
M
points uniformly at random with replacement from the original dataset. This results in
B
independent bootstrap samples
\{\bar{\mathbf{X}}^{(1)},\ldots,\bar{\mathbf{X}}^{(B)}\}
, where each
\bar{\mathbf{X}}^{(b)}=\{\mathbf{x}^{(b_{1})},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{(b_{M})}\}
and multi-indices
\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{M}\}
are drawn independently and identically from the discrete uniform distribution over
\{1,\ldots,M\}
, i.e.,
b_{i}\sim\text{Unif}(\{1,\ldots,M\})
.
Through resampling with replacement from the original dataset, the bootstrapping method creates multiple datasets that simulate potential variations in the population, even when the true underlying distribution is unknown. By relying solely on the given data, bootstrapping provides robust estimates of statistical properties without assuming a specific distributional form.

2.7
Kernel density and smoothed bootstrap

The smoothed bootstrap
[
24
]
is an extension of the standard bootstrap technique and specifically improves kernel density estimation (KDE). While the standard bootstrap resamples the original dataset with replacement, it fails to capture the bias in the KDE estimator, particularly when the kernel involves higher-order derivatives of the target density.

We assume that the density
f_{\mathbf{X}}(\cdot)
(
7
) is sufficiently smooth and
K(\cdot)
is a kernel of order
L
. Then, the leading bias of the KDE at
\mathbf{x}
can be approximated by

\displaystyle\text{Bias}[\hat{f}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{H})]\approx\dfrac{1}{L!}\sum_{|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|=L}(\mathbf{H}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\cdot D^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x}))\mu_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(K),

where
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{N})
is a multi-index with the cardinality
|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|=L
,
D^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\partial^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}f_{\mathbf{X}}}{\partial x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\partial x_{N}^{\alpha_{N}}}(\mathbf{x})
,
\mu_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(K)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\mathbf{u}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}K(\mathbf{u})\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{u}}
, and
\mathbf{H}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=\prod_{i=1}^{N}H_{ii}^{\alpha_{i}}
when
\mathbf{H}
is diagonal. To estimate this bias without explicitly computing higher-order derivatives of
f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})
, the smoothed bootstrap introduces additional randomness into each bootstrap sample. Given a bootstrap sample
{\bar{\mathbf{X}}}^{(b)}=\{\mathbf{x}^{(b_{1})},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{(b_{M})}\}
, the smoothed bootstrap constructs a perturbed sample by adding random noise from the kernel, i.e.,

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{X}}^{+(b)}=\{\mathbf{x}^{+(b_{1})},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{+(b_{M})}\},\quad\text{with}\penalty\ \mathbf{x}^{+(b_{i})}=\mathbf{x}^{(b_{i})}+\mathbf{H}\mathbf{\epsilon}_{ib},

(8)

where each
\mathbf{\epsilon}_{ib}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is an independent random vector drawn from the kernel
K(\cdot)
.

A KDE is then constructed from each perturbed sample as

\displaystyle\tilde{f}^{(b)}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{H})=\dfrac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\dfrac{1}{|\mathbf{H}|}K(\mathbf{H}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{+(b_{i})})).

(9)

We now estimate the bias at point
\mathbf{x}
as the average deviation of the smoothed bootstrap estimators from the original KDE as

\displaystyle\tilde{B}(\mathbf{x})=\dfrac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\tilde{f}^{(b)}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{H})(\mathbf{x})-\hat{f}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{H}).

(10)

The final bias-corrected KDE is

\tilde{f}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{H})=\hat{f}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{H})-\tilde{B}(\mathbf{x}).

Fig.
1
shows the comparison of probability density estimates from different methods for a bivariate Gaussian distribution with mean
\boldsymbol{\mu}=[0,0]^{\intercal}
and covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0.7\\
0.7&1\end{bmatrix}
where 500 random samples are generated from this true distribution. The probability density is evaluated over a two-dimensional grid defined by
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
.
The left subplot presents the true probability density as a reference.
The middle subplot shows the density estimated using the standard KDE method applied directly to the original samples.
The right subplot shows the smoothed bootstrap KDE, where Gaussian noise scaled by the KDE bandwidth is added to the resampled points. The smoothed bootstrap method yields a smoother and more accurate density estimate than the standard KDE, especially in high-density regions.

We address the challenge of limited data by using a smoothed bootstrap KDE approach to improve the accuracy of data-driven DD-GPCE in predicting the mean and standard deviation of the system. The smoothed bootstrap generates multiple resampled datasets for bias correction, thereby enhancing the representativeness of the data and improving the robustness and accuracy of the DD-GPCE predictions.

3
Data-driven dimensionally decomposed generalized polynomial chaos expansion

This section introduces the data-driven DD-GPCE method, which enables the use of DD-GPCE when only a input dataset is available and its distribution is unknown, as described in Problem
1
—a common scenario in real applications. From this section onward, we refer to the DD-GPCE as the classic DD-GPCE to distinguish it from the data-driven DD-GPCE. The classic DD-GPCE, presented in Section
2.6
, fully assumes the input probability distributions. The conventional data-driven DD-GPCE assumes a Gaussian distribution type and estimates its parameters (e.g., mean and standard deviation) from the available data. In contrast, the proposed data-driven DD-GPCE does not require any assumption about the distribution type and its parameters, providing greater flexibility in representing arbitrary input distributions. Section
3.1
presents the data-driven method for constructing measure-consistent multivariate orthonormal polynomials. Section
3.2
explains the calculation of data-driven DD-GPCE coefficients, and Section
3.3
discusses the computational cost of the proposed data-driven method.

3.1
Proposed data-driven method for constructing measure-consistent multivariate orthonormal polynomials

The data-driven DD-GPCE method constructs multivariate orthonormal polynomials that are consistent with the probability measure directly from input samples. We achieve this using KDE with the smoothed bootstrap, as detailed in the following subsections.

3.1.1
Low-order KDE-based high-dimensional modeling in DD-GPCE

In UQ tasks involving high-dimensional inputs, KDE or its variants encounter the curse of dimensionality, where the sample size required for accurate estimation grows exponentially with the number of inputs. This makes the estimation of joint probability densities computationally expensive and statistically inefficient.

Consider an
N
-dimensional input vector
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N})^{\intercal}
, where
N
is high-dimensional (e.g.,
N\geq 10
in this work). Given observed samples
\{\mathbf{x}^{(l)}\}_{l=1}^{M}
, we use KDE to estimate the input densities. To address the issue arising in high-dimensional settings, we divide the inputs
\mathbf{X}
into
Q\in\mathbb{N}
groups.
For each group
q=1,\ldots,Q
, let

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{q}=\{i_{1}^{(q)},\ldots,i_{n_{q}}^{(q)}\}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\},\quad n_{q}=|\mathcal{I}_{q}|,

denote the corresponding multi-index set.
The sets
\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{Q}
are exhaustive, i.e.,
\cup_{q=1}^{Q}\mathcal{I}_{q}=\{1,\ldots,N\}
.
We then define the corresponding subvectors

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}_{\mathcal{I}_{q}}=(X_{i_{1}^{(q)}},\ldots,X_{i_{n_{q}}^{(q)}})^{\intercal},\quad q=1,\ldots,Q.

Here, each subvector
\mathbf{X}_{\mathcal{I}_{q}}
collects the components of
\mathbf{X}
indexed by
\mathcal{I}_{q}
.
The grouping of
\mathbf{X}
into
\mathbf{X}_{\mathcal{I}_{q}}

q=1,\ldots,Q,
follows one of the following three conditions:

(1)

If the input variables are mutually independent, we group each input variable individually.

(2)

If input variables are fully or partially correlated in a low-dimensional manner, we group them based on their correlation structure.

(3)

For correlated high-dimensional inputs, we group them based on components of the monomial moment matrix
\mathbf{G}_{S,m}
in (
15
), which includes only lower-order monomials under low-order DD-GPCE (e.g.,
S=1
or
S=2
). Each pair of monomial index sets
\mathcal{I}_{p}
and
\mathcal{I}_{q}
in
\mathbf{G}_{S,m,u,v}
defines a group by taking the union of their associated variables (see Step 4 in Section
3.1.3
).

For Condition (1), we apply KDE separately to independent variables, as their joint densities can be constructed by the product of marginal densities. For Condition (2), we apply KDE to estimate the joint densities, as this method effectively handles low-dimensional variables. Condition (3) addresses high-dimensional inputs with correlations. Since DD-GPCE, when constrained to low interaction orders (e.g.,
S=1
or
2
), captures only univariate or low-order effects, it requires only marginal or low-order joint densities.
This allows to use marginal or low-order KDE, as only partial joint densities need to be estimated instead of the full high-dimensional input density. The proposed approach thus alleviates the curse of dimensionality while preserving an accurate representation of input uncertainty.

We apply smoothed bootstrap resampling independently to each subvector
\mathbf{X}_{\mathcal{I}_{q}}
. This allows the sampling process to reduce estimator variance without requiring additional samples.

3.1.2
Smoothed bootstrap method for unbiased input density estimation

Let
(X_{i_{1}^{(q)}},\ldots,X_{i_{n_{q}}^{(q)}})^{\intercal}
be an
n_{q}
-dimensional subvector of
\mathbf{X}
corresponding to the index set
\mathcal{I}_{q}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\}
. The joint probability density of
\mathbf{X}_{\mathcal{I}_{q}}
is unknown. Given
M
samples
\{\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}_{q}}^{(l)}\}_{l=1}^{M}
of
\mathbf{X}_{\mathcal{I}_{q}}
, the KDE-based density estimate

\displaystyle\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}_{q}};\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}_{q}}):=\dfrac{1}{M|\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}_{q}}|^{1/2}}\sum_{i=1}^{M}{K}\left(\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}_{q}}^{-1/2}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}_{q}}-\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}_{q}}^{(i)})\right)

(11)

depends on the specific dataset and may exhibit significant variance. Here,
{K}(\cdot)
is a kernel function—an
n_{q}
-variate density that is symmetric and unimodal at the
n_{q}
-dimensional
\mathbf{0}
, and relies on the bandwidth

(2)

We determine the expansion coefficients

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[y(\mathbf{X})-\sum_{i=1}^{L_{N,S,m}}\tilde{c}_{i}\tilde{\Psi}_{i}(\mathbf{X})\right]^{2},

(18)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Ac}=\mathbf{b},

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}:=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\Psi}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{(1)})&\cdots&\tilde{\Psi}_{L_{N,S,m}}(\mathbf{x}^{(1)})\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\tilde{\Psi}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{(L)})&\cdots&\tilde{\Psi}_{L_{N,S,m}}(\mathbf{x}^{(L)})\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathbf{b}:=\begin{bmatrix}y(\mathbf{x}^{(1)})\\
\vdots\\
y(\mathbf{x}^{(L)})\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathbf{c}:=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{c}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\tilde{c}_{L_{N,S,m}}\end{bmatrix}.

3.3
The complete algorithm and its computational cost

Algorithm
1
presents the proposed data-driven DD-GPCE procedure for estimating the mean and variance of a QoI in complex mechanical systems.
The algorithm begins with input samples, which are divided into subvectors. KDE with
the smoothed bootstrap is then applied to generate bias-corrected input densities. Using these estimated densities, input samples are reconstructed, and measure-consistent orthonormal basis vectors are generated. The DD-GPCE coefficients are then computed via stochastic least-squares regression using the input-output data. Finally, the mean and variance of the model output are estimated from the obtained coefficients.

These samples are used to estimate the bias in the KDE-based input distribution. We standardized the data for each variable by subtracting its mean and dividing by its standard deviation, ensuring that all variables had zero mean and unit variance. The bandwidth matrix
\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}_{q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{q}\times n_{q}}
was selected using the multivariate Silverman’s rule of thumb
[
20
]
, expressed as
\sqrt{\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}_{q}}}=\left(\frac{4}{L(n_{q}+2)}\right)^{\tfrac{1}{n_{q}+4}}\mathbf{I}
, where
n_{q}
is the number of dimensions,
L
is the number of samples, and
\mathbf{I}
is the
n_{q}\times n_{q}
identity matrix.
The grid points for estimating the PDF were constructed independently for each dimension to ensure sufficient coverage of the distribution domain. For the
i
th variable (
i=1,\ldots,N
), the lower and upper bounds of the grid were defined as

x_{\mathrm{lower},i}=x_{{\min},i}-k\sigma_{i},\quad x_{\mathrm{upper},i}=x_{{\max},i}+k\sigma_{i}
, where
x_{{\min},i}
and
x_{{\max},i}
denote the minimum and maximum sample values of the
i
th variable,
\sigma_{i}
denotes its sample standard deviation, and
k
is a user-defined scaling factor controlling the range extension. In Examples 1–3, we set
k=1
.

We compare the proposed method with three methods: (1) data-driven DD-GPCE with Gaussian assumed inputs, (2) classic DD-GPCE that uses the true input distributions, and (3) crude MCS. The Gaussian-assumed data-driven approach estimates mean and covariance parameters from input data while assuming all inputs follow Gaussian distributions–a common practice in real-world applications. In contrast, classic DD-GPCE uses the true distributions, which is rarely feasible in real-world scenarios.

For the proposed data-driven DD-GPCE, we construct measure-consistent orthonormal basis functions through the five steps presented in Section

4.4.2
Results

Both data-driven methods demonstrate very close agreement with the MCS reference for the mean response. The proposed KDE-based approach achieves near-zero errors for the 1st through 6th natural frequencies, with an overall average relative error of 0.25%, which is comparable to the Gaussian-assumed approach’s 0.22% error.

For standard deviation, both data-driven methods underestimate variability, but the proposed KDE-based approach demonstrates better accuracy than the Gaussian-assumed method for the second through sixth frequencies. The KDE-based method yields an average relative error of
17.7\%
, while the Gaussian-assumed method shows a higher error of
21.9\%
. This improvement is attributed to the ability of KDE to capture the shape of the underlying distribution directly from the data, without restrictive assumptions.

For the classic method that uses the true lognormal distribution, when
m=3
,
S=1
, the polynomial expansion fails to fully capture the interaction effects present in the system. Although the input samples follow the true lognormal distribution, the restriction to univariate interaction terms limits the surrogate to model complex dependencies among input variables, leading to unexpectedly high errors.

When the interaction order increases to
S=2
with a reduced polynomial order
m=2
, a substantial improvement in accuracy is observed in standard deviation estimation (Table
3
b). This improvement highlights the importance of including higher-order interaction terms when modeling responses influenced by nonlinear, interdependent input effects. Despite the lower polynomial order, the inclusion of multivariate basis terms enhances the surrogate’s accuracy, particularly for capturing variance structure and tail behavior-critical elements in lognormal systems.

We remark that while the classic DD-GPCE using true distributions provides the most accurate capture of mean and standard deviation, this approach is often not feasible in many real-world scenarios. Therefore, the proposed DD-GPCE offers a practical solution that demonstrates better accuracy compared to its Gaussian-assumed counterpart.

Outlook

This paper presents a practical data-driven framework for constructing DD-GPCE surrogate models when only input samples are available. However, several challenges remain.

KDE suffers from the curse of dimensionality—its data requirements and computational cost grow exponentially with the number of input variables, making full-dimensional density estimation impractical. The smoothed bootstrap method further increases this cost by requiring grid generation for joint PDF evaluation.

DD-GPCE alleviates this issue by retaining univariate or low-order interaction terms (
S=1
), enabling scalable UQ in high-dimensional settings. This truncation limits the model’s ability to capture complex inter-variable dependencies. Future research should therefore explore advanced KDE-based or alternative density estimation methods capable of representing higher-order interactions efficiently.

6
Conclusion

This paper proposes a data-driven DD-GPCE method for efficient UQ in complex engineering systems with high-dimensional and arbitrarily distributed input variables, where only limited data are available.
The proposed method uses KDE to estimate the input distribution from available samples and a whitening transformation to construct orthonormal polynomial basis functions consistent with the estimated distribution. The smoothed bootstrap method enhances KDE’s robustness by estimating bias, thereby reducing the dependency on the given sample set.

Numerical examples demonstrated the effectiveness of the data-driven DD-GPCE method in accurately estimating statistical moments.
Compared to the classic approach, the data-driven method eliminates the need for prior knowledge of input distribution, making it applicable to real-world scenarios where only limited sample data are available.
The results showed that the data-driven DD-GPCE method achieves higher accuracy than the Gaussian-assumed approach, particularly when handling complex or unknown input distributions.

The data-driven DD-GPCE method provides a robust and reliable solution for UQ in engineering applications, paving the way for broader adoption in design and reliability assessments with limited input data.
