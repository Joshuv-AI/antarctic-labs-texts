Boundary values via reproducing kernels: The Julia-Carathéodory theorem
Frej Dahlin

Abstract
Given a reproducing kernel k k on a nonempty set X X , we define the reproductive boundary of X X with respect to k k . Furthermore, we generalize the well known nontangential and horocyclic approach regions of the unit circle to this new kind of boundary. We also introduce the concept of a composition factor of k k , an abstract analogue of analytic selfmaps of the unit disk. Using these notions, we obtain a far reaching generalization of the Julia-Carathéodory theorem, stated on an arbitrary set. We also prove Julia’s lemma in the abstract setting and give sufficient conditions for the convergence of iterates of some selfmaps. As an application we improve the classical theorem on the unit disk for contractive multipliers of standard weighted Dirichlet spaces, as well as Besov spaces on the unit ball. Many examples and questions are provided for these novel objects of study.

Introduction

In this paper
\mathbb{D}
denotes the unit disk in the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
and

\mathbb{T}
its boundary, the unit circle. We recall the classical Julia-Carathéodory
theorem.

Theorem

(Julia-Carathéodory)
.

Given a point

\zeta\in\mathbb{T}
and an analytic selfmap of the unit disk
\varphi:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
,
then the following are equivalent:

(i)

\liminf_{z\to\zeta}\frac{1-|\varphi(z)|^{2}}{1-|z|^{2}}=c<\infty.

(ii)

There is a
\lambda\in\mathbb{T}
such that the difference quotient

\frac{1-\varphi(z)\overline{\lambda}}{1-z\overline{\zeta}}

has nontangential limit
c
at
\zeta
.

(iii)

\varphi
and its derivative
\varphi^{\prime}
has nontangential limits equal to

\lambda\in\mathbb{T}
and
c\lambda\overline{\zeta}
at
\zeta
, respectively.

Furthermore, if any of the above hold, then
c>0
and
\varphi
has horocyclic limit
\lambda
at
\zeta
, which is the same in (ii)
and (iii), in the sense that
\varphi(z_{n})\to\lambda
along any sequence
z_{n}\to\zeta

constrained to a fixed set
E(M,\zeta)=\{z\in\mathbb{D}:|\zeta-z|^{2}\leq M(1-|z|^{2})\},\,M>0
.

The condition (i) was considered by Julia
[
18
]
in 1920, and his contribution
surrounding it is often referred to as

. Specifically, he proved that (i)
implies the existence of
\lambda\in\mathbb{T}
such that

(1)

\frac{|1-\varphi(z)\overline{\lambda}|^{2}}{1-|\varphi(z)|^{2}}\leq c\frac{|1-z\overline{\zeta}|^{2}}{1-|z|^{2}}.

In other words,
\varphi(E(M,\zeta))\subset E(cM,\lambda)
, which proves that
\varphi
has
horocyclic limit
\lambda
at
\zeta
. Moreover, if equality holds in
(
1
) for some
z\in\mathbb{D}
, then
\varphi
is an automorphism.

In 1929 Carathéodory
[
10
]
completed the above equivalences
showing that (iii),
\varphi
has an angular derivative. This classical
object is still being studied today, see for example the recent note

[
8
]
.

Generalizations of the Julia-Carathéodory theorem typically extend it to different
domains in several variables. For example, the unit ball
\mathbb{B}_{d}
in
\mathbb{C}^{d}

by Rudin
[
26
]
, and the polydisk

initiated by Jafari,
but completed by Abate
[
17
,
2
]

One should also mention the recent work in the bidisk by Agler, McCarthy, and Young, as

[
4

20

of this domain. Those methods yield results of considerable depth, but do not seem to
extend to higher dimensions.

The results mentioned have all been obtained using geometric considerations.
However, Sarason discovered a proof of the classical Julia-Carathéodory
theorem using the theory of Hilbert spaces that have a reproducing kernel

[
27
,
28
]
.

These are Hilbert spaces of
functions on some nonempty set
X
,
f:X\to\mathbb{C}
such that for every
x\in X
the point evaluation functional
f\mapsto f(x)
is bounded. By the Riesz
representation theorem there corresponds to every

kernel function

k_{x}
such
that
f(x)=\langle f,k_{x}\rangle
. The two variable function

k(x,y)=\langle k_{y},k_{x}\rangle
is called the

and we denote
its corresponding Hilbert space by
\mathcal{H}(k)
.
All of the required theory of reproducing kernels is recalled in §2.1.

We shall state Sarason’s version of the Julia-Carathéodory theorem,
but first note that for the Szegő kernel
k(z,w)=(1-z\overline{w})^{-1}
on

\mathbb{D}
and any analytic selfmap

\varphi:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
, then

k^{\varphi}(z,w)=\frac{1-\varphi(z)\overline{\varphi(w)}}{1-z\overline{w}}

defines a reproducing kernel on
\mathbb{D}
and its corresponding Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}(k^{\varphi})
,
are the well known de Branges-Rovnyak spaces.

Theorem

(Sarason)
.

Given a point
\zeta\in\mathbb{T}
and an analytic selfmap
\varphi
of
\mathbb{D}

\varphi:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
, then the following are equivalent:

(i)

\liminf_{z\to\zeta}\frac{1-|\varphi(z)|^{2}}{1-|z|^{2}}=c<\infty,

(ii)

There is a
\lambda\in\mathbb{T}
such that the function defined by

q_{\zeta}(z)=\frac{1-\varphi(z)\overline{\lambda}}{1-z\overline{\zeta}}

belongs to
\mathcal{H}(k^{\varphi})
.

(iii)

All functions in
\mathcal{H}(k^{\varphi})
have nontangential limits at
\zeta
.

Furthermore, if any of the above hold, then
\varphi
has horocyclic limit
\lambda
at
\zeta
and

\|q_{\zeta}\|^{2}=c
.

This theorem does not directly include the notion of angular derivatives. However,
one can prove using (ii) and (iii) combined that

has an angular derivative at

\zeta
, thus recovering the classical theorem.

The above was generalized to the case of the Drury-Arveson kernel on the
unit ball by Jury
[
19
]
. In this paper we obtain a
far reaching generalization of the Julia-Carathéodory theorem stated only in
terms of an arbitrary nonempty set and reproducing kernels on it. A central
difficulty that we have to address is to find abstract analogues of: the
boundary of
\mathbb{D}
, the nontangential approach regions of
\mathbb{T}
, and the
analytic selfmaps of
\mathbb{D}
.

Let
k
be a reproducing kernel on a nonempty set
X
.
The intuition behind a general notion of boundary is the identification of points

x\in X
with the kernel functions
k_{x}
together with the following simple
procedure that embeds

into an ‘extended’ set. More precisely, let
\widehat{X}

be the set of sequences
\hat{x}=(x_{n})
of

such that the sequence of kernel functions
(k_{x_{n}})

converge pointwise. Two sequences
\hat{x},\hat{y}\in\widehat{X}
are equivalent,

\hat{x}\sim\hat{y}
if and only if the corresponding sequences of functions
(k_{x_{n}})
and

(k_{y_{n}})

\widetilde{X}
denote the

\xi\in\widetilde{X}
we denote the above limiting function as

For these functions the following possibilities can occur:

(i)

k_{\xi}=k_{x}
for some

,

(ii)

k_{\xi}\in\mathcal{H}(k)
but (i) fails,

(iii)

k_{\xi}\notin\mathcal{H}(k)
.

We define the

of
X
with respect to

to be the
set of points
\xi\in\widetilde{X}
that satisfy (iii). We denote this set by
\partial_{k}X
,
and call their corresponding functions
boundary kernel functions
or

boundary functions
for short. For a sequence
(x_{n})\in\widehat{X}

we will write
x_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k}}{{\to}}\xi
to mean that
[(x_{n})]_{\sim}=\xi\in\widetilde{X}
, similarly
we write
x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k}}{{\to}}\xi
to mean
along any sequence
(x_{n})
such that
x_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k}}{{\to}}\xi
.
For more properties of the reproductive boundary and a discussion of (i) and (ii) see §2.3.

Now we are able to generalize the nontangential and horocyclic approach regions, respectively.
For
\xi\in\partial_{k}X
and
M>0
consider the family subsets of
X

(2)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{k}(M,\xi)

\displaystyle=\{x\in X:k(x,x)\leq M|k_{\xi}(x)|\},

(3)

\displaystyle E_{k}(M,\xi)

\displaystyle=\{x\in X:k(x,x)\leq M|k_{\xi}(x)|^{2}\}.

A set
\Gamma_{k}(M,\xi)

approaches

\xi
if there is a sequence
x_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k}}{{\to}}\xi

within this set, if along any such sequence the function
f:X\to\mathbb{C}
has a limit, then
we say that
f
has a
\Gamma_{k}
-limit at
\xi
. Of course similar definitions hold for
the
E_{k}
family of sets, see §2.4 for the complete definitions.

Supposing that
k
is nonzero, we shall generalize the notion of
analytic selfmaps of
\mathbb{D}
as the selfmaps
\varphi:X\to X
such that the function

(4)

x,y\mapsto\frac{k(x,y)}{k(\varphi(x),\varphi(y))},\qquad x,y\in X

is a reproducing kernel, in this case
\varphi
is a called a
composition factor
of
k
.
In general one can even allow for distinct kernels in the numerator and denominator,
see §2.2.

To illustrate these three object we give a list of many concrete examples in §2.5.

In order to state a variant of our main theorem we need to introduce the following (mild)
regularity assumptions on
k
. Details are deferred to §3.

(A)

For every
\xi\in\partial_{k}X
there exists a constant
a_{\xi}
such that

|k_{\xi}(x)|\leq a_{\xi}k(x,x),\forall x\in X.

(B)

There is a constant

such that
|k(x,y)|>b
for all
x,y\in X
.

(C)

Let
\xi\in\partial_{k}X
and
(x_{n})
be a sequence in
X
. If
|k_{\xi}(x_{n})|\to\infty
,
then
x_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k}}{{\to}}\xi
.

(D)

If for a sequence
(x_{n})
in
X

k(x_{n},x_{n})\to\infty

,
then there is a subsequence such that
x_{n_{j}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k}}{{\to}}\xi\in\partial_{k}X
.

With these assumptions we prove the following result, which actually is a special
case of our main theorem.

.

Let
k
be a reproducing kernel on a nonempty set

-
(D)
.
Let

.

\mathcal{H}(\frac{k}{k\circ\varphi})
.

\mathcal{H}(\frac{k}{k\circ\varphi})

-limit at

.

-limit

When

The paper is organized as follows. §2 serves a preliminary cause and
introduces the abstractions required. §2.1 briefly establishes the fundamental theory of
reproducing kernels. In §2.2 we introduce composition factors of reproducing
kernels. §2.3 discusses and prove some properties of the reproductive boundary.
The approach regions to the reproductive boundary are realized in §2.4.
In §2.5 a list of examples is given to illustrate these three concepts.
§3 is devoted to stating and proving our main theorem,
the general Julia-Carathéodory theorem for composition factors.
§4 contains applications of this theorem. §4.1 contains a general
version of Julia’s lemma and proves the uniqueness of some Denjoy-Wolff points
along with a condition for their existence. In §4.2 we prove Corollary 2 stated

concludes with §5, a list of questions in the circle of problems introduced in
the present paper.

Preliminaries

2.1
Reproducing kernels

The contents of this section is essentially included in
[
7
]
and
[
23
]
.
But we give a brief overview of the theory and state the theorems that we require.

Definition 1
.

Let
X
be a nonempty set. A function
k:X\times X\to\mathbb{C}
is a
reproducing kernel

if for every finite subset
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}\subset X
the matrix
[k(x_{i},x_{j})]_{i,j=1}^{n}

is positive semidefinite. We write
k\geq 0
or
k(x,y)\geq 0
to mean this.

If there is a a point
0_{X}\in X
such that
k(x,0_{X})\equiv 1
, then we say that
k
is
normalized at
0_{X}
.

By Moore’s theorem there exists a unique Hilbert space of functions
\mathcal{H}(k)
such
that each
kernel function

k_{y}(x)\coloneq x\mapsto k(x,y),\,x,y\in X
belong to

\mathcal{H}(k)
and they satisfy

f(y)=\langle f,k_{y}\rangle,

for every
f\in\mathcal{H}(k)
. It is an immediate consequence that
\|k_{y}\|^{2}=k(y,y)
.
Furthermore, a function
f:X\to\mathbb{C}
belongs to
\mathcal{H}(k)
if and only if there
is a constant
c>0
such that

(5)

c^{2}k(x,y)-f(x)\overline{f(y)}\geq 0,

and the least such constant is
\|f\|
, the norm of
f
.

Of particular interest is the following two classes of symbols, in §2.2 we shall
introduce a third class, which we will compare to these.

Definition 2
.

We call a function
\varphi:X\to\mathbb{C}

a
multiplier
of
\mathcal{H}(k)
if the function
x\mapsto\varphi(x)f(x)
belongs to

\mathcal{H}(k)
whenever
f\in\mathcal{H}(k)
, we write the set of these as
\Mult(k)
.

Using the closed graph theorem one can prove that the linear operator

f\mapsto\varphi f
is bounded on
\mathcal{H}(k)
. Furthermore
\varphi\in\Mult(k)
if and only
if there exists a
c>0
such that

(6)

c^{2}k(x,y)-\varphi(x)\overline{\varphi(y)}k(x,y)\geq 0,

and the least such constant is the norm of the induced operator. We will
write
\Mult_{1}(k)
to mean the set of contractive multipliers on
\mathcal{H}(k)
.
Often we are interested in excluding the unimodular constants
x\to\lambda,\,|\lambda|=1

from the set
\Mult_{1}(k)
, in this case we will write
\Mult_{1}(k)\setminus\mathbb{T}
.

Definition 3
.

We call a selfmap
\varphi:X\to X

a
composition symbol
of
\mathcal{H}(k)
if the linear operator
C_{\varphi}\coloneq f\mapsto f\circ\varphi

acts on
\mathcal{H}(k)
. The set of these are denoted by
\Comp(k)
.

\varphi\in\Comp(k)

if and only if there exists a constant
c>0
such that

(7)

c^{2}k(x,y)-k(\varphi(x),\varphi(y))\geq 0,

and the least such constant is
\|C_{\varphi}\|
. We will write
\Comp_{1}(k)
to mean the set
of contractive composition symbols.

Remark 1
.

The notation
\Comp(k)
is not standard, but we adopt it since we are concerned only
with the symbols themselves, not the operators that they induce.

We end this section by stating a few results that are essential to our methods.

Theorem

(Schur’s Product Theorem)
.

Let
k
and
t
be reproducing kernels on a nonempty set
X
, then their product

x,y\mapsto k(x,y)t(x,y)
is as well.

Proposition 1
.

Let
X
and
Y
be two nonempty sets and let
k
be a reproducing kernel on
X
.
For any map
\varphi:Y\to X
the composed kernel
k\circ\varphi=x,y\mapsto k(\varphi(x),\varphi(y))

is a reproducing kernel on
Y
.

The following fact, together with weak compactness will be used throughout the paper.
It is an immediate consequence of the density of the linear span of
\{k_{x}:x\in X\}
in

\mathcal{H}(k)
.

Proposition 2
.

A sequence
(f_{n})
in
\mathcal{H}(k)
converges weakly to a function
f\in\mathcal{H}(k)

if and only if
\|f_{n}\|
is bounded and
f_{n}\to f
pointwise.

2.2
Composition factors

Throughout this subsection fix
k
and
t
to be reproducing kernels on nonempty
sets
X
and
Y
, respectively. In addition, we suppose that
t
is
nonzero

i.e.
t(x,y)\neq 0
for all
x,y\in Y
.

Definition 4
.

Let
\varphi:X\to Y
be a map. If

(8)

\frac{k}{t\circ\varphi}\geq 0,

then we call
\varphi
a
composition factor
of
k
with
t
. We denote the
set of these as
\Fact(k,t)
.

In the special case that
k=t
then we simply say that
\varphi
is a composition
factor of
k
and write the set as
\Fact(k)
.

Proposition 3
.

Let
\varphi\in\Fact(k,t)
, then for every
f\in\mathcal{H}(\frac{k}{t\circ\varphi})
the
pair
(f,\varphi)
induces a bounded weighted composition operator from
\mathcal{H}(t)

into
\mathcal{H}(k)
of norm at most
\|f\|
. That is, the linear map defined by

(W_{f,\varphi})g(x)=f(x)g(\varphi(x))
is bounded from
\mathcal{H}(t)
into
\mathcal{H}(k)
.

In particular, if the constant function
x\mapsto 1
belongs to
\mathcal{H}(\frac{k}{t\circ\varphi})
,
then
\varphi\in\Comp(t,k)
and
if
x\mapsto 1\in\mathcal{H}(t\circ\varphi)
, then
\mathcal{H}(\frac{k}{t\circ\varphi})\subset\mathcal{H}(k)
.

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathcal{H}(\frac{k}{t\circ\varphi})
, this is equivalent to

\|f\|^{2}\frac{k(x,y)}{t\circ\varphi(x,y)}-f(x)\overline{f(y)}\geq 0.

Multiplying the above kernel with
t\circ\varphi
yields, by Schur’s Product Theorem,

\|f\|^{2}k(x,y)-f(x)\overline{f(y)}t\circ\varphi(x,y)\geq 0,

which is equivalent to our desired conclusion.
∎

Remark 2
.

Suppose that
k
and
t
are normalized at some points
0_{X}\in X
and
0_{Y}\in Y
,
respectively. If
\varphi\in\Fact(k,t)
with
\varphi(0_{X})=0_{Y}
, then the kernel

\frac{k}{t\circ\varphi}
is normalized at
0_{X}
. In this case,

Proposition

3
shows that
\varphi\in\Comp(k,t)
with norm at most 1.

Proposition 4
.

Let
r
be a nonzero reproducing kernel on a nonempty set
Z
.
If
\varphi\in\Fact(k,t)
and
\psi\in\Fact(t,r)
, then
\psi\circ\varphi\in\Fact(k,r)
.

Proof.

Compose the kernel
\frac{t}{r\circ\psi}
with
\varphi
and multiply with
\frac{k}{t\circ\varphi}
,
the result then follows by Schur’s Product Theorem.
∎

Remark 3
.

In the case that
t=k

Proposition

4
shows that
\Fact(k)
forms
a semigroup under composition.

Proposition 5
.

Let
k
be normalized at a point
0_{X}
. If for
\varphi\in\Fact(k)
there exists an automorphism

\psi
of
X

\psi(\varphi(0_{X}))=0_{X}

By

. Therefore

2.3
Reproductive boundary

Throughout this section, let
k
be a reproducing kernel on a nonempty set
X
.
Recall the definition of the reproductive boundary from the introduction.

Definition 5
.

Let
\widehat{X}
be the set of sequences
\hat{x}=(x_{n})
of
X
such that the sequence
of kernel functions
k_{x_{n}}
converge pointwise. Two sequences
\hat{x},\hat{y}\in\widehat{X}

are equivalent,
\hat{x}\sim\hat{y}
if and only if the corresponding sequences of functions

(k_{x_{n}})
and
(k_{y_{n}})
converge pointwise to the same function; we let
\widetilde{X}

denote the quotient set. For
\xi\in\widetilde{X}
we denote the above limiting function
as
k_{\xi}
. For these functions the following possibilities can occur:

(i)

k_{\xi}=k_{x}
for some
x\in X
,

(ii)

k_{\xi}\in\mathcal{H}(k)
but (i) is fails,

(iii)

k_{\xi}\notin\mathcal{H}(k)
.

We define the
reproductive boundary
to be the set of points
\xi\in\widetilde{X}

such that (iii), written as
\partial_{k}X
, and call their corresponding functions

boundary kernel functions
or
boundary functions
for short.

Similarly we define the
reproductive interior
to be the set of point
\xi\in\widetilde{X}

such that
either
(i) or (ii), written as
\Int_{k}X
, and call their corresponding
functions
interior kernel functions
or
interior functions
for short.

In any case, if the sequence of functions
k_{x_{n}}
tend pointwise to
k_{\xi}
,
then we say that the sequence is
k
-convergent and write
x_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k}}{{\to}}\xi
, in other words
the equivalence class
[(x_{n})]_{\sim}=\xi
. Similarly
we write
x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k}}{{\to}}\xi
to mean
along any sequence
(x_{n})
such that
x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k}}{{\to}}\xi
.

The reproductive boundary is our sole concern in this paper, we leave the discussion
of the reproductive interior as the following remark.

Remark 4
.

If (i), then we may freely identify
\xi
with a point
x\in X
, loosely speaking

\xi
is an interior point. However, if
k
does not separate points, then the point
x
might not be unique.
The possibility (ii) is never reached for the Szegő kernel, in a sense this kernel is
complete. Consider restricting the Szegő kernel
k^{*}(z,w)=(1-z\overline{w})^{-1}

to the punctured plane
\mathbb{D}^{*}=\mathbb{D}\setminus\{0\}
, in this case the constant function

z\mapsto 1
belongs to
\mathcal{H}(k^{*})
, but is not equal to
k^{*}_{w}
for any
w\in\mathbb{D}^{*}
.
In general we have the above trichotomy, but we can reduce it to a dichotomy by letting

\overline{k}(\xi,\upsilon)=\langle k_{\upsilon},k_{\xi}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}(k)},\,\xi,\upsilon\in\Int_{k}X

and by embedding
\mathcal{H}(k)
into the space
\mathcal{H}(\overline{k})
, i.e. we can extend every function
f\in\mathcal{H}(k)
to

\Int_{k}X
via the rule
f(\xi)=\langle f,k_{\xi}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}(k)}
.

Remark 5
.

In general,
\widetilde{X}
is not a topological space. One could let
\{k_{x}:x\in X\}

inherit the topology of pointwise convergence. But for the purposes of
this paper it is more convenient to work with sequences rather than nets.
Nonetheless, in the worked examples there will often be a clear correspondence
to a preexisting topology.

Another approach that might be of independent
interest is as follows. Consider the closure of
\{k_{x}:x\in X\}
in the topology
of uniform convergence on compact sets given by the metric, see
[
3
]

(9)

p_{k}(x,y)=\sqrt{1-\frac{|k(x,y)|^{2}}{k(x,x)^{\frac{1}{2}}k(y,y)^{\frac{1}{2}}}},\qquad x,y\in X.

This construction is similar to the Busemann boundary, see
[
9
]
.

When is this topology metrizable?
Let
(x_{n})
be a sequence in
X
and consider the properties:

(10)

if
k_{x_{n}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle w}}{{\to}}g\in\mathcal{H}(k)
, then
g=k_{x}
for some
x\in X
,

(11)

if
\|k_{x_{n}}\|\to\infty
, then
\hat{k}_{x_{n}}=\frac{k_{x_{n}}}{\|k_{x_{n}}\|}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle w}}{{\to}}0
,

(12)

if
k_{x_{n}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle w}}{{\to}}k_{x}
, then
k_{x_{n}}\to k_{x}
in norm.

If
k
satisfies the first two, then
X
is complete under the
p_{k}
metric.
If it also satisfies the third, then the closed balls centered around some
point
x\in X
given by

\overline{B(\epsilon,x)}=\{y\in X:p_{k}(x,y)\leq\epsilon\},\qquad 0<\epsilon<1

are compact. Clearly
\cup_{0<\epsilon<1}\overline{B(x,\epsilon)}=X
so
X
is
hemicompact with respect to
p_{k}
. Hence the closure of
\{k_{x}:x\}
in the
compact-open topology is metrizable.

The Szegő kernel
k(z,w)=(1-z\overline{w})^{-1}
on the unit disk satisfies

k(z,z)\to\infty
as
|z|\to 1^{-}
. Indeed, any kernel evaluated on the diagonal
diverges to
\infty
as the variable tends to the reproductive boundary.

Lemma 1
.

Let
x_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k}}{{\to}}\xi\in\partial_{k}X
, then
\|k_{x_{n}}\|^{2}=k(x_{n},x_{n})\to\infty
as

n\to\infty
.

Proof.

Suppose on the contrary that
k(x_{n},x_{n})
is bounded. By weak compactness,
passing to a subsequence if necessary, we can assume that
k_{x_{n}}
converges
weakly to a function
g\in\mathcal{H}(k)
. But

g(y)=\langle g,k_{y}\rangle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\langle k_{x_{n}},k_{y}\rangle=\lim_{n\to\infty}k_{x_{n}}(y)=k_{\xi}(y),

contradicting the fact that
k_{\xi}\notin\mathcal{H}(k)
.
∎

Remark 6
.

It appears that
Lemma

1
suggests an equivalent way to
define the boundary. Suppose conversely that for a sequence
(x_{n})
the
functions
k_{x_{n}}
converge pointwise to a function
k_{\xi}
and

k(x_{n},x_{n})\to\infty
, does this imply that
\xi\in\partial_{k}X
? The following
example shows that this fails in general. Observe the infinite matrix

\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1&\ldots\\
1&2&1&\ldots\\
1&1&3&\ldots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{pmatrix},

because it has strictly positive subdeterminants,
it can be regarded as a reproducing kernel on the set of natural numbers
\mathbb{N}
.
For any sequence
(j_{n})
in
\mathbb{N}
such that
j_{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
, then

k(j_{n},j_{n})=j_{n}\to\infty
. But the functions
i\mapsto k(i,j_{n})
tend pointwise
to
1
. In other words they tend pointwise to the function
i\mapsto k(i,0)
, represented
by the top row, which belongs to
\mathcal{H}(k)
.

It is a well known fact that functions
f\in H^{2}
, the Hardy space on the unit disk,
satisfy the growth restriction
f(z)=o((1-|z|^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}})
as
|z|\to 1^{-}
.
We have the following generalization to functions in
\mathcal{H}(k)
when the
variable tends to the reproductive boundary of
k
.

Lemma 2
.

If
x_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k}}{{\to}}\xi\in\partial_{k}X
, then the normalized kernel functions

\hat{k}_{x}=\frac{k_{x}}{k(x,x)^{\frac{1}{2}}}
tend weakly to
0
along this sequence,
that is, for every
f\in\mathcal{H}(k)

(13)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\langle f,\hat{k}_{x_{n}}\rangle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{f(x_{n})}{k(x_{n},x_{n})^{\frac{1}{2}}}=0.

Proof.

Since
\|\hat{k}_{x_{n}}\|=1
it is sufficient to prove that
\hat{k}_{x_{n}}
converge
pointwise to
0
. The sequence
k_{x_{n}}(y)
is convergent by assumption, hence
it is bounded, therefore

\hat{k}_{x_{n}}(y)=\frac{k_{x_{n}}(y)}{k(x_{n},x_{n})^{\frac{1}{2}}}

tend to
0
as
n\to\infty
by
Lemma

1
.
∎

2.4
Approach regions

Definition 6
.

Let
k
be a reproducing kernel on a nonempty set
X
.
For
\xi\in\partial_{k}X
and
M>0
consider the sets

(14)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{k}(M,\xi)

\displaystyle=\{x\in X:k(x,x)\leq M|k_{\xi}(x)|\},

(15)

\displaystyle E_{k}(M,\xi)

\displaystyle=\{x\in X:k(x,x)\leq M|k_{\xi}(x)|^{2}\}.

If a sequence
x_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k}}{{\to}}\xi
within a fixed set
\Gamma_{k}(M,\xi)
, then we say that

x_{n}
approaches
\xi

\Gamma_{k}
-wise and write
x_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Gamma_{k}}}{{\to}}\xi
. If
M
is
small, then there might be no sequence in
\Gamma_{k}(\xi,M)
that is
k
-convergent to
\xi
,
but if there is at least one such sequence, then we say that
\Gamma_{k}(M,\xi)

approaches

\xi
.

Let
f:X\to\mathbb{C}
, if for every sequence
x_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Gamma_{k}}}{{\to}}\xi
we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}f(x_{n})=\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, then we say that
f
has
\Gamma_{k}
-limit
\lambda

at
\xi
. Of course the same definition is valid for any metric space in place of
\mathbb{C}
.

Let
t
be a reproducing kernel on a nonempty set
Y
. For a map
\varphi:Y\to X
we will
similarly say that
\varphi
has a
\Gamma_{k}
-limit
\lambda\in Y\cup\partial_{t}Y
at
\xi
if

\varphi(x_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle t}}{{\to}}\lambda
as
n\to\infty
for every sequence
x_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k}}{{\to}}\xi
.

Remark 7
.

It might not be true that
\Gamma_{k}(M,\xi)\subset E_{k}(M,\xi)
however, for a
sequence
x_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k}}{{\to}}\xi
within
\Gamma_{k}(M,\xi)
we know that

k(x_{n},x_{n})\to\infty
by
Lemma

1
. So if
n
is big enough to guarantee
k(x_{n},x_{n})>M
,
then
|k_{\xi}(x_{n})|>1
and therefore, for these
n
we also have

x_{n}\in E_{k}(M,\xi)
. In conclusion, the notion of
E_{k}
-limit is stronger
than
\Gamma_{k}
-limit.

2.5
Examples

Here we list examples illustrating the reproductive boundary, approach regions and composition
factors of different reproducing kernels.

Example 1
.

Let
k(z,w)=(1-z\overline{w})^{-1}
be the Szegő kernel on
\mathbb{D}
corresponding to the
Hardy space
H^{2}
. Since
\frac{1}{1-z}
has
an isolated pole at
1
one easily sees that
\partial_{k}\mathbb{D}
can be identified with

\mathbb{T}
. Moreover, any sequence
(z_{n})
in
\mathbb{D}
converges to a point
\zeta\in\mathbb{T}

if and only if
z_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k}}{{\to}}\zeta
.

The approach regions
\Gamma_{k}
and
E_{k}
are seen to be the families of Stolz sectors
and horocycles, respectively, which in turn correspond to the notion of nontangential
and horocyclic boundary limits.

A selfmap
\varphi:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
belongs to
\Fact(k)
if and only if
\varphi
is a
contractive multiplier of
H^{2}
and is not a unimodular constant; the set of these are
precisely the analytic selfmaps of
\mathbb{D}
.
The analytic automorphism of the disk
\psi_{a}
that interchange
a
and
0
is
given by
\psi_{a}(z)=(a-z)/(1-z\overline{a})
and they
satisfy

(16)

1-\psi_{a}(z)\overline{\psi_{a}(w)}=\frac{(1-|a|^{2})(1-z\overline{w})}{(1-z\overline{a})(1-a\overline{w})}

A computation reveals that
\psi_{a}\in\Comp(k)
, therefore
Proposition

3

shows that
\Mult_{1}(k)\setminus\mathbb{T}=\Comp(k)=\Fact(k)
.

Example 2

(
Example

1
continued)
.

Let
k(z,w)=\frac{1}{(1-z\overline{w})^{\alpha}},\,\alpha>0
. For
\alpha<1
,
\alpha=1
, and

\alpha>1
, then
\mathcal{H}(k)
is a weighted Dirichlet space, see
Section

4.2
,

H^{2}
, and a standard weighted Bergman space, respectively.
The same considerations
Example

1
hold, and the approach regions with respect
to
k
are exactly the same for any
\alpha
. By (
16
) one
sees that each analytic disk automorphism belongs to
\Fact(k)
and also it is routine
to show that
\psi_{a}\in\Comp(k)
, hence
Proposition

3
proves
that
\Fact(k)\subset\Comp(k)
.

For
\alpha\geq 1
, then
\Fact(k)
are precisely the analytic selfmaps of
\mathbb{D}
.
While for
\alpha<1
, then they are strictly contained in this set. Letting
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}

and set
\varphi(z)=z^{2}
, then

\left(\frac{1-z^{2}\overline{w}^{2}}{1-z\overline{w}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=\sqrt{1+z\overline{w}},

which is not a reproducing kernel, since expressed as a power series in
z\overline{w}
it
has some negative coefficients. But we can say a few things. By
Proposition

4
,
each contractive multiplier of
\mathcal{H}(k)
belong to
\Fact(k)
, which in particular
includes the contractive multipliers of the classical Dirichlet space. There is another
class of functions that belong to
\Fact(k)
. For any analytic selfmap of
\mathbb{D}
we
have

\frac{1-\varphi(z)\overline{\varphi(w)}}{1-z\overline{w}}\geq 0.

Chu
[
12
]
classified exactly for which
\varphi
the above kernel
is complete Nevanlinna-Pick, see
[
3
]
, which is equivalent
to being able to write it as

\frac{1}{1-\langle u(z),u(w)\rangle},

where
u:X\to\ell^{2}
. Then by Schur’s Product Theorem and the power series representation
of
(1-x)^{-\alpha}
we obtain

\frac{k(z,w)}{k\circ\varphi(z,w)}=\left(\frac{1}{1-\langle u(z),u(w)\rangle}\right)^{\alpha}\geq 0.

But by no means should this condition be necessary.

Example 3
.

Let
k(z,w)=\frac{1}{z\overline{w}}\log(\frac{1}{1-z\overline{w}}),\,z,w\in\mathbb{D}
be the reproducing
kernel for
\mathcal{D}
, the Dirichlet space on the unit disk, see

[
24
]
. We have
\partial_{k}\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{T}
and for
\zeta\in\mathbb{T}

we have the family approach regions equivalent to the two inequalities

\displaystyle|\zeta-z|

\displaystyle\leq(1-|z|^{2})^{\frac{1}{M}},

\displaystyle|\zeta-z|

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\sqrt{\frac{1}{M}\log{\frac{1}{1-|z|^{2}}}}},

corresponding to
\Gamma_{k}
and
E_{k}
, respectively. Kinney
[
21
]

showed that every function in
\mathcal{D}
has
\Gamma_{k}
-limits almost
everywhere on
\mathbb{T}
. This was later improved by
Nagel, Rudin, and Shapiro
[
22
]
to the exponential approach
region defined by the inequality

|\zeta-z|\leq M\log\left(\frac{1}{1-|z|^{2}}\right)^{-1}.

In terms of reproducing kernels this compares the Szegő kernel and the Dirichlet kernel.

Example 4
.

Let
k(x,y)=\min(x,y)
be defined on
[0,\infty)
, this reproducing kernel corresponds
to the absolutely continuous functions
f:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
with
f(0)=0
such that

\int_{0}^{\infty}|f^{\prime}(x)|^{2}dx<\infty,

given the inner product

\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{0}^{\infty}f^{\prime}(x)\overline{g^{\prime}(x)}dx.

Let
x_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k}}{{\to}}\xi
, if the sequence
(x_{n})
is bounded, then the function
k_{\xi}
is
given by
k_{x}
for some
x\in[0,\infty)
. In the other case that
x_{n}\to\infty
, then

k_{x_{n}}
converges pointwise to the identity function
x\mapsto x
, which is not included
in
\mathcal{H}(k)
.

Therefore, the reproductive boundary is the singleton containing the identity map
\{x\mapsto x\}
,
which we can identify as a point at
\infty
. By
Lemma

2
each function

f\in\mathcal{H}(k)
satisfies
\frac{f(x)}{\sqrt{x}}\to 0
as
x\to\infty
.

Example 5
.

Let
k(z,w)=\frac{1-b(z)\overline{b(w)}}{1-z\overline{w}}
be the de Branges-Rovnyak space with

b(z)=\frac{z+1}{2}
. It is easily shown that if
w\to 1
, then the functions
k_{w}

converge pointwise to the constant function
z\mapsto\frac{1}{2}
, which belongs to
\mathcal{H}(k)
,
therefore
\partial_{k}\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{T}\setminus\{1\}
.

Example 6
.

Let
\zeta(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{-s},\,\Re{s}>1
be the Riemann zeta function and
consider its analytic continuation to
\mathbb{C}\setminus\{1\}
.
Set
\mathbb{C}_{\frac{1}{2}}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\Re{z}>\frac{1}{2}\}
and
let
k(z,w)=\zeta(z+\overline{w}),\,z,w\in\mathbb{C}_{\frac{1}{2}}
, this reproducing kernel
correspond to the Hardy space of Dirichlet series, see
[
16
]
.
In this case
\partial_{k}\mathbb{C}_{\frac{1}{2}}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\Re{z}=\frac{1}{2}\}
, the critical
line. We lack geometrical descriptions of the
\Gamma_{k}
and
E_{k}
approach regions,
see
Question

2
.

Example 7
.

By the power series expansion of
e^{x}
and Schur’s Product Theorem we have that

e^{k}\geq 0
. A selfmap
\varphi:X\to X
belongs to
\Fact(e^{k})
if and only if

\frac{e^{k}}{e^{k\circ\varphi}}=e^{k-k\circ\varphi}\geq 0.

Hence
\Fact(e^{k})
includes
\Comp_{1}(k)
.

Example 8
.

Let
k(z,w)=(1-\langle z,w\rangle)^{\alpha},\,\alpha>0
be a power of the Drury-Arveson
kernel on the unit ball
\mathbb{B}_{d}
of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, see
[
15
]
.
Then
\partial_{k}\mathbb{B}_{d}=\partial\mathbb{B}_{d}
and the
\Gamma_{k}
and
E_{k}
approach regions
are the Korányi regions and horospheres, respectively. See
Section

4.3
for
more details. Just as in
\mathbb{D}
one can prove that
\Fact(k)\subset\Comp(k)
.

Example 9
.

For
d>0
let

k(z,w)=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\frac{1}{1-z_{j}\overline{w}_{j}},\qquad z,w\in\mathbb{D}^{d},

be the reproducing kernel corresponding to the Hardy space on the polydisk
\mathbb{D}^{d}
.
The same considerations as in
Example

1
show that
\partial_{k}\mathbb{D}^{d}=\partial\mathbb{D}^{d}
,
the non distinguished boundary.
Suppose that
\varphi(z)=\sigma(\varphi_{1}(z_{1}),\ldots,\varphi_{d}(z_{d}))
, where each

\varphi_{j}:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
and
\sigma
is a permutation of
\{1,\ldots,d\}
.
Then
\varphi\in\Fact(k)
by Schur’s Product Theorem, since each
\varphi_{j}
satisfies

\frac{1-\varphi_{j}(z_{j})\overline{\varphi_{j}(w_{j})}}{1-z_{j}\overline{w}_{j}}\geq 0.

The analytic automorphisms of
\mathbb{D}^{d}
are given by
d
automorphisms of each separate
disk of
\mathbb{D}^{d}
followed by a permutation
[
25
]
. Hence they
belong to
\Fact(k)
. Furthermore, they are also included in
\Comp(k)
so therefore

\Fact(k)\subset\Comp(k)
, a fact noted by Chu in the bidisk
[
11
]
, but
he proved it more directly. The reverse inclusion is false, for example
z\mapsto(z_{1},z_{1})

belongs to
\Comp(k)
when
d=2
, but clearly

\frac{1-z_{1}\overline{w}_{1}}{1-z_{2}\overline{w}_{2}}\ngeq 0.

Example 10
.

If every nonconstant
\psi\in\Mult_{1}(k)
satisfies
|\psi(x)|<1
, for example
\mathcal{H}(k)
is a space
of analytic functions, then
\Mult_{1}(k)\setminus\mathbb{T}=\Fact(k,t)
where

is the Szegő kernel on
\mathbb{D}
.
Furthermore, in this case
Proposition

4
shows that if
\varphi\in\Fact(k)
,
then
\psi\circ\varphi\in\Mult_{1}(k)
.

Substituting
t
for the weighted Bergman kernel
t(z,w)=(1-z\overline{w})^{-n}
one can identify

\Fact(k,t)
as the
n
-contractive multipliers on
\mathcal{H}(k)
except
\mathbb{T}
, see
[
14
]
.

Example 11
.

If
\mathcal{H}(k)
is a space of analytic functions on a domain of
\mathbb{C}
that is shift invariant,
that is, the identity function
z\mapsto z
is in
\Mult(k)
, then
\Fact(k)\subset\Mult(k)
.

3
Main theorem

As previously mentioned, our main theorem requires some regularity assumptions. We restate
them here for the convenience of the reader.

Let
t
be a reproducing kernel on a nonempty set
Y
.

(A)

For every
\lambda\in\partial_{t}Y
there exists a constant
a_{\lambda}
such that

|t_{\lambda}(y)|\leq a_{\lambda}t(y,y),\,\forall y\in Y.

(B)

There is a constant
b>0
such that
|t(x,y)|>b
for all
x,y\in Y
.

(C)

Let
\lambda\in\partial_{t}Y
and
(y_{n})
be a sequence in
Y
. If
|t_{\lambda}(y_{n})|\to\infty
,
then
y_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle t}}{{\to}}\lambda
.

(D)

If for a sequence
(y_{n})
in
Y

t(y_{n},y_{n})\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
,
then there is a subsequence such that
y_{n_{j}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle t}}{{\to}}\lambda\in\partial_{t}Y
.

Remark 8
.

For every function
f\in\mathcal{H}(t)
we have the estimate
|f(y)|\leq\|f\|k(y,y)^{\frac{1}{2}}
,

(A)
is a weaker form of this estimate on the boundary.

(B)
of course implies that
t
is nonzero but also that

|t_{\lambda}(x)|>0
for all
x\in Y
and
\lambda\in\partial_{t}Y
, furthermore
it asserts that the constant function
x\mapsto 1
belongs to
\mathcal{H}(t)
.
The condition
(C)
is summarized as each boundary function
having an isolated singularity.
Lastly
(D)
should be viewed as a compactness condition, see the
example below.

Example 12
.

Let
t(z,w)=(1-z\overline{w})^{-1}
be the Szegő kernel on
\mathbb{D}
, then for

\lambda\in\mathbb{T}
we have
2|\lambda-z|\geq 1-|z|^{2}
, hence

(A)
is satisfied.
Clearly
|t(z,w)|>1
fulfilling
(B)
and each boundary function

t_{\lambda}
has an isolated singularity at
\lambda
, hence it satisfies

(C)
.
Lastly
t(y,y)
tends to
\infty
if and only if
|y|\to 1^{-}
so

(D)
follows by the compactness of
\mathbb{T}
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
k
and
t
be reproducing kernels on nonempty sets
X
and
Y
, respectively.
Suppose that
t
satisfies
(A)
-
(D)
.
For
\varphi\in\Fact(k,t)
and
\xi\in\partial_{k}X
, suppose that
\Gamma_{k}(M,\xi)

approaches
\xi
for some
M>0
, then the following are equivalent:

(i)

\liminf_{x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k}}{{\to}}\xi}\frac{k(x,x)}{t\circ\varphi(x,x)}=c<\infty.

(ii)

There is a
\lambda\in\partial_{t}Y
such that the function defined by

4.2
Standard weighted Dirichlet spaces on the unit disk

Throughout this section let
k(z,w)=(1-z\overline{w})^{-\alpha},\,0<\alpha<1
be defined
on
\mathbb{D}
. We shall the denote the corresponding space
\mathcal{H}(k)
as
\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}
.
It consists of analytic functions on
\mathbb{D}
such that

\|f\|^{2}\coloneq|f(0)|^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{D}}|f^{\prime}(z)|^{2}(1-|z|^{2})^{\alpha}dA(z)<\infty,

where
dA
is the normalized area measure on
\mathbb{D}
. As noted in
Example

2
,
we can identify
\partial_{k}\mathbb{D}=\{\zeta\in\mathbb{T}:z\mapsto(1-z\overline{\zeta})^{\alpha}\}
with
\mathbb{T}
,
and it is apparent that a sequence
(z_{n})
in
\mathbb{D}
converges to

\zeta\in\mathbb{T}
if and only if
z_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k}}{{\to}}\zeta
, so
k
-convergence overlaps with
the Euclidean metric.
The family of approach regions for
\zeta\in\mathbb{T}
given by
k
are

\displaystyle\Gamma_{k}(M,\zeta)

\displaystyle=\{z\in\mathbb{D}:|\zeta-z|\leq M(1-|z|^{2})\}

\displaystyle E_{k}(M,\zeta)

\displaystyle=\{z\in\mathbb{D}:|\zeta-z|^{2}\leq M(1-|z|^{2})\},

for
M>0
. They are the familiar nontangential and horocyclic approach regions, respectively.

The goal of this section is to prove the following. The condition (i) also appears in section
14 of
[
5
]
.

Corollary 2
.

Fix
\zeta\in\mathbb{T}
and let
\varphi
be a contractive multiplier of
\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}
, then
the following are equivalent:

(i)

\liminf_{z\to\zeta}\frac{1-|\varphi(z)|^{2}}{(1-|z|^{2})^{\alpha}}=c<\infty.

(ii)

There is a
\lambda\in\mathbb{T}
such that the difference quotient

q_{\zeta}=\frac{1-\varphi(z)\overline{\lambda}}{(1-z\overline{\zeta})^{\alpha}}

has a nontangential limit
c
at
\zeta
.

(iii)

The function
z\mapsto\varphi^{\prime}(z)(1-z\overline{\zeta})^{1-\alpha}
has nontangential
limit
c\lambda\overline{\zeta}\alpha
at
\zeta
.

Furthermore
c>0
and
\varphi
has horocyclic limit
\lambda
at
\zeta
.

The equivalence of
(i)
and
(ii)
are given by
Corollary

1

with the exception that
c
is replaced by
\|q_{\zeta}\|^{2}
. Their equality
is readily established as
\Gamma_{k}(M,\zeta)
approaches
\zeta
for every
M>\frac{1}{2}

and we can take
a_{\lambda}=2
in
(A)
for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{T}

where
t
is the Szegő kernel. Hence
\|q_{\zeta}\|^{2}\leq c\leq(2M\|q_{\zeta}\|)^{2}
and we prove
equality by letting
M\to\frac{1}{2}^{+}
. The equivalence of (ii) and (iii) is a matter of analysis
and we formulate it as a lemma.

Lemma 4
.

Let
\zeta\in\mathbb{T}
and let
f:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{C}
be an analytic function that has nontangential
boundary value
f(\zeta)
at
\zeta
. Fix a complex number
\sigma
, then the following are
equivalent:

(20)

\displaystyle\frac{f(\zeta)-f(z)}{(1-z\overline{\zeta})^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\to\sigma,

(21)

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(z)(1-z\overline{\zeta})^{1-\alpha}

\displaystyle\to\sigma\overline{\zeta}\alpha,

where the limits are taken as
z
tends to
\zeta
nontangentially.

Proof.

Suppose that (
20
) holds.
Let
R_{M}=\{z\in\mathbb{D}:|\zeta-z|\leq M(1-|z|)\}
be the typical nontangential approach regions,
note that
\Gamma_{k}(\frac{M}{2},\zeta)\subset R_{M}\subset\Gamma_{k}(M,\zeta)
, hence the respective
convergence notions are equivalent.
For
z\in R_{M}
consider the circle
\gamma_{z}
centered at
z
of radius
\frac{1}{2}(1-|z|)
;
a simple estimate shows that
\gamma_{z}\subset R_{2M+1}
. Because of
(
20
) we have

f(z)=f(\zeta)-\sigma(1-z\overline{\zeta})^{\alpha}-\beta(z)(1-z\overline{\zeta})^{\alpha},

where
\beta(z)\to 0
as
z\to\zeta
nontangentially. By the above and Cauchy’s formula

(22)

f^{\prime}(z)=\sigma\overline{\zeta}\alpha(1-z\overline{\zeta})^{\alpha-1}-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma_{z}}\frac{\beta(w)(1-w\overline{\zeta})^{\alpha}}{(z-w)^{2}}dw

For
w\in\gamma_{z}
we have

|1-w\overline{\zeta}|=|\zeta-w|\leq|\zeta-z|+|z-w|\leq(M+\frac{1}{2})(1-|z|)
,
therefore we can estimate the integrand as
(2M+1)(1-|z|)^{\alpha-2}\sup_{w\in\gamma_{z}}|\beta(w)|
.
Multiplying (
22
) by
(1-z\overline{\zeta})^{1-\alpha}
and the estimate
in the previous sentence yields

|\sigma\zeta\alpha-f^{\prime}(z)(1-z\overline{\zeta})^{1-\alpha}|\leq(2M+1)\sup_{w\in\gamma_{z}}|\beta(w)|,

which tends to
0
as
z\to\zeta
nontangentially.

Conversely suppose that (
21
) holds.
Let
\gamma(t)=z+t(\zeta-z),\,t\in[0,1]
, by the fundamental theorem of calculus we have

f(\zeta)-f(z)=\int_{0}^{1}f^{\prime}(\gamma(t))\gamma^{\prime}(t))dt=\int_{0}^{1}f^{\prime}(\gamma(t))(\zeta-z)dt.

Note of the identity
1-\gamma(t)\overline{\zeta}=(1-t)(1-z\overline{\zeta})
. Therefore,
upon dividing the above equation by
(1-z\overline{\zeta})^{\alpha}
we obtain

\frac{f(\zeta)-f(z)}{(1-z\overline{\zeta})^{\alpha}}=\zeta\int_{0}^{1}f^{\prime}(\gamma(t))(1-z\overline{\zeta})^{1-\alpha}\frac{dt}{(1-t)^{1-\alpha}}.

By assumption, as
z\to\zeta
nontangentially the right-hand side tends to

\sigma\alpha\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dt}{(1-t)^{1-\alpha}}=\sigma.

∎
